A Unified Lyapunov-IQC Framework for Uniform Stability of Smooth Quadratic First-Order Accelerated Optimizers
Don Li don@pdx.edu, Dacian Daescu daescu@pdx.edu

Abstract
We develop a unified Lyapunov–integral quadratic constraint (IQC) framework for establishing uniform stability of first-order accelerated optimization algorithms in the β \beta -smooth and γ \gamma -strongly convex regime. Classical analyses of uniform stability, such as the work of Hardt, Recht, and Singer for stochastic gradient descent (SGD), rely on direct coupling arguments and case-by-case control of iterate differences under random sampling. Extending such arguments to accelerated methods, such as Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG), is complicated by the presence of higher-order state dynamics induced by momentum. We first extend this classical approach with the use of Lyapunov functions to provide a uniform stability bound for smooth quadratic NAG, and supplement this result with small-scale numerical experiments. We then extend this framework by modeling first-order accelerated optimizers as Lur’e-type feedback interconnections between a linear dynamical system and a (non-linear) gradient operator. β \beta -Smoothness and γ \gamma -strong convexity are encoded a sector IQC inequality. Under this representation, uniform stability is certified via the existence of a quadratic Lyapunov function satisfying a finite-dimensional linear matrix inequality (LMI) in the form of a feasibility problem, which can be solved via semi-definite programming (SDP). We instantiate this framework for NAG and show how classical uniform stability bounds can be recovered via this framework. These results underscore a structural connection between optimization dynamics and robust control theory, providing a modular methodology for reliable and reproducible numerical certification of uniform stability and generalization behavior of first-order methods via convex optimization tools that is adaptable to increasingly complex optimization algorithms.

Introduction

Despite their efficacy with respect to solving complex, large-scale optimization problems, all convex optimization algorithms are susceptible to
overfitting
, i.e., being unable to generalize beyond its training dataset to new, unseen data. This is a major challenge for the application of said convex optimization algorithms to machine learning in practice. Such application of convex optimization algorithms to machine learning is framed in terms of the
empirical risk minimization
(ERM) paradigm, which is represented by the unconstrained minimization problem

\underset{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\text{min}}\quad f(w)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(w,z_{i}),

(1)

where
\ell(w,z_{i})
is the
loss function
with respect to parameter
w
at sample
z_{i}\in S=(z_{1},...,z_{n})
. The core task of any optimization algorithm is to minimize
f(w)
in the form of (1) by updating from
w_{t}
to each successive
w_{t+1}
at each iteration
t
. First-order optimization algorithms are the class of optimization algorithms that achieve this by using the gradient of the loss function,
\nabla\ell(w,z_{i})
, as its
oracle
to perform parameter updates. The most fundamental first-order optimization algorithm is (stochastic) gradient descent, whose update rule is

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\nabla\ell(w,z_{t}),

(2)

where
\eta
denotes the learning rate hyperparameter, which governs the magnitude of the parameter update via the gradient oracle.

It is a basic result in convex analysis that any strongly-convex function is guaranteed to have a global minimum, and our analysis here, while focused on generalization behavior, considers only strongly convex loss functions. We detail the relevant convexity properties in the following subsection.

1.1
Convexity Properties of the Smooth Quadratic Regime

We first formally define
\gamma
-strong convexity:

Definition 1

(
\gamma
-Strong Convexity)
.

The loss
\ell(w,z_{i})
is
\gamma
-strongly convex in
w
, i.e., for all
w,w^{\prime}
, there exists
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\ell(w^{\prime},z)\geq\ell(w,z)+\nabla(w,z)^{T}(w^{\prime}-w)+\frac{\gamma}{2}||w^{\prime}-w||^{2}
.

The strong convexity condition guarantees that the minimum curvature of the loss function is at least quadratic, the magnitude of which is governed by
\gamma
. Strong convexity of the loss function implies monotonicty of the gradient, i.e.,

Corollary 2

(Gradient Monotonicty)
.

Let
f
be
\gamma
-strongly convex. Then, for all
x,y\in\text{dom}(f)
,

\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\geq\gamma||x-y||^{2}
.

We likewise assume
\beta
-Lipschitzness of the gradient:

Definition 3

(
\beta
-Smoothness)
.

The loss
\ell(w,z)
has
\beta
-Lipschitz gradients
, or is
\beta
-smooth
, if, for all
w,w^{\prime},z,z^{\prime}
, there exists
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that

||\nabla\ell(w,z)-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime},z)||\leq\beta||w-w^{\prime}||
.

\beta
-smoothness ensures that the “growth” of the gradient is controlled by
\beta
. Consequently,
\beta
acts as the upper bound of the curvature of the loss function. We likewise assume boundedness of the gradient:

Definition 4

(Bounded Gradients)
.

The loss
\ell(w,z)
has
bounded gradients
, if, for all
w
, there exists
G\in\mathbb{R}
such that

||\nabla\ell(w,z)||\leq G
.

Since the gradient is uniformly bounded, i.e.,
||\nabla\ell(w,z)||\leq G
, we have the following corollary:

Corollary 5

(
G
-Lipschitz Loss Function)
.

Let
\ell(w,z)
denote the loss function, where
\ell(w,z)
is differentiable with respect to
w
over all
z
. Since the gradient is uniformly bounded where
||\nabla\ell(w,z)||\leq G
for all
w,z
, it follows that
|\ell(w,z)-\ell(w^{\prime},z)|\leq G||w-w^{\prime}||
.

As shorthand, we refer to these convexity conditions collectively as the
smooth quadratic regime
hereafter, and these convexity conditions are assumed for all following results in this paper.

1.2
Nesterov Accelerated Gradient

While our Lyapunov-IQC framework concerns a broader class of first-order optimization algorithms, we motivate the development of and apply this framework to Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG) in particular. Building upon the first accelerated optimizer (gradient descent with classical momentum, or the “heavy-ball” method, HB) introduced by
11
, Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG) was introduced in
9
, attaining, in the smooth general convex regime, a convergence rate
O(\frac{1}{T^{2}})
, an improvement over the
O(\frac{1}{T})
convergence rate achieved by SGD in the same regime. Accelerated optimizers such as NAG achieve such improved convergence rates via incorporating a “momentum” term in the parameter update. Doing so produces two update rules per iteration,

v_{t+1}=\mu v_{t}-\eta\nabla\ell(w_{t}+\mu v_{t}),

(3)

w_{t+1}=w_{t}+v_{t+1},

(4)

where
\mu\in[0,1)
is the momentum parameter and
v_{t}
is the momentum term. As
5
notes, the NAG update rules, specifically in the smooth quadratic regime, where, for
\kappa=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}
(where
\kappa
is the “condition number”), can be formulated as

v_{t+1}=w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t}),

(5)

w_{t+1}=(1+\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1})v_{t+1}-(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1})v_{t}.

(6)

Let
\theta:=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}
. Then equations 5 and 6 can likewise be written as

v_{t+1}=w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\nabla\ell(w_{t}),

(7)

w_{t+1}=(1+\theta)v_{t+1}-\theta v_{t}.

(8)

The execution of a single epoch of NAG (over
T\in\mathbb{N}
iterations) is detailed in the following pseudocode:

Initialization of
w_{t}
and
v_{t}
at
t=1
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
\eta
, and
\mu\in[0,1)

while

t\leq T

do

Compute
w_{t}+\mu v_{t}

\triangleright
The “look-ahead” position

Compute
\nabla\ell(w_{t}+\mu v_{t})

\triangleright

whilereturn

w_{T}

1.3
Algorithmic (Uniform) Stability

We analyze the generalization behavior of an optimization algorithm in terms of
uniform stability
as developed by
4
, which is defined formally as follows:

Definition 6

(
\epsilon_{n}
-Uniform Stability)
.

Let
A
be a particular stochastic optimization algorithm ran over
n
training iterations, let
S
be a dataset where
S^{\prime}
is
S
with a single data point
z_{j}
modified, and let
\xi=\{z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{n}\}
be a coupled random sampling of indices of
S
and
S^{\prime}
. Moreover, consider a loss function for
S
,
\ell(A(S,\xi),z)
, and the same loss function for
S^{\prime}
,
\ell(A(S^{\prime},\xi),z)
. Let
\epsilon_{n}
denote the (uniform) stability parameter of
A
, i.e., the smallest value such that replacing any single example in the training set (here,
z_{*}
) changes the loss on any test point by at most
\epsilon_{n}
. Then
A
is
uniformly stable
iff

\text{sup}_{S,S^{\prime},z}\mathbb{E}[|\ell(A(S,\xi),z)-\ell(A(S^{\prime},\xi),z)|]\leq\epsilon_{n}

Moreover, we say that
A
is stable iff
\epsilon_{n}=O(\frac{1}{n})
.

Informally, an algorithm is uniformly stable if, assuming a uniform distribution on a dataset
S
where
S^{\prime}
is
S
modified at a single data point
z_{j}
, the trajectories of the loss functions on each of
S
and
S^{\prime}
stay within
\epsilon
of one another. In this sense, uniform stability is a metric of how robust an optimization algorithm is to small changes in its input(s).

1.4
Lyapunov Functions

7
derived an
O(\frac{2G^{2}}{\gamma n})
uniform stability bound for smooth quadratic SGD (cf. Appendix A for their proof in comprehensive form). Recall that SGD is governed by the single update rule
w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\nabla\ell(w,z_{t})
. Hence, for SGD, there is only the parameter difference
w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}
to track, where
w_{t}
is the parameter value for the optimizer ran on
S
and
w_{t}^{\prime}
is the parameter value for the optimizer ran on
S^{\prime}
. The crux of the proof from
7
for the uniform stability of smooth quadratic SGD is to find the expected value of the parameter difference in the case where the differing sample
z_{j}
is not sampled (i.e.,
i_{t}\neq j
), then to do so for the case where
z_{j}
is sampled (
i_{t}=j
), and then to find the expected value of the parameter difference as a weighted average of both via the law of total expectation from probability theory. Let
A
denote the event where the differing sample is not selected, so
P(A)=1-\frac{1}{n}
, so
A^{c}
is the event where the differing sample is selected so
P(A^{c})=\frac{1}{n}
. Let
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{t+1}]=\mathbb{E}[w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}]
. Then by the law of total expectation we have

\mathbb{E}[\delta_{t+1}]=(1-\frac{1}{n})\mathbb{E}[||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}|||i_{t}\neq j]+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}|||i_{t}=j].

(9)

Refer to the full proof in Appendix A for the missing details. Eqn. (9), along with
G
-Lipschitzness of the loss function, yields the
O(\frac{2G^{2}}{\gamma n})
uniform stability bound as derived by
7
.

6
derived an
O(\frac{4\beta^{2}}{\gamma n}[1-(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}})^{T}])
uniform stability bound for smooth quadratic NAG. Recall that NAG has two update rules, one for “velocity”
v_{t}
and one for the parameter value
w_{t}
. Hence, case by case iterate difference analysis, as
7
did for SGD, is made cumbersome by having two iterate differences to track simultaneously:
\Delta w_{t}=w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}
and
\Delta v_{t}=v_{t}-v_{t}^{\prime}
. We sidestep the complications made by these higher-order dynamics via
Lyapunov functions
. Lyapunov functions have long been used in the theory of dynamical systems for proving stability results for (non-linear) dynamical systems, with the highly useful feature that doing so does not require explicitly solving ODE’s/PDE’s. Moreover, for our purposes, a Lyapunov function
\mathcal{V}(x)
has the additional useful property of taking vector/matrix inputs and producing scalar output, i.e.,
\mathcal{V}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Recall that NAG has two update rules per iteration, whereas SGD only has one, i.e., with
\theta:=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}
for
\kappa=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}
,

v_{t+1}=w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\nabla\ell(w_{t}),

(10)

w_{t+1}=(1+\theta)v_{t+1}-\theta v_{t}.

(11)

We consider the state vector
x_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta w_{t}\\
\Delta v_{t}\end{bmatrix}
, where
\Delta w_{t}:=w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}
and
\Delta v_{t}:=v_{t}-v_{t}^{\prime}
(where
w_{t},v_{t}
are the parameter iterate and velocity iterates from the original dataset
S
and
w^{\prime}_{t},v^{\prime}_{t}
are the parameter iterate and velocity iterates from the perturbed dataset
S^{\prime}
). Thus,
x_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta w_{t}\\
\Delta v_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}\\
v_{t}-v^{\prime}_{t}\end{bmatrix}
.
x_{t}
is the input of the Lyapunov function. In particular, we use a quadratic Lyapunov function, i.e.,

Definition 7

(Quadratic Lyapunov Function)
.

Consider a discrete linear time-invariant (LTI) system
\dot{x}=Ax
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, with
x^{*}=0
as the fixed point of the LTI. A
quadratic Lyapunov function
of such an LTI is a scalar function of the form

\mathcal{V}(x)=x^{T}Px,

(12)

where
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix, i.e.,
P=P^{T}\succ 0
.

Moreover,
\mathcal{V}(x)
is a valid Lyapunov function iff the following two conditions hold:

1.

Positive Definiteness

\forall x\neq 0
,
\mathcal{V}(x)=x^{T}Px>0
, and
\mathcal{V}(0)=0
,

2.

Negative Semi-Definiteness of the Derivative

\forall x\neq 0
,
\dot{V}(x)\leq 0
.

For discrete LTI systems, the matrix
P
can be obtained by solving the discrete-time Lyapunov equation

A^{T}PA-P=-Q

(13)

for some
Q\succ 0
. Moreover, we must have
P=P^{T}\succ 0
such that
A^{T}PA-P\prec 0
. This implies the following corollary:

Corollary 8
.

Let
\mathcal{V}(x)=x^{T}Px
. If
P=P^{T}\succ 0
, then
\mathcal{V}(x)=x^{T}Px
is a valid Lyapunov function.

Consider
\Delta v_{t+1}=v_{t+1}-v^{\prime}_{t+1}
. This implies
\Delta v_{t+1}=\Delta w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}(\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{t}))
. If
\Delta g_{t}:=\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{t})
, then
\Delta v_{t+1}=\Delta w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta g_{t}
. We next consider
\Delta w_{t+1}=w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}=(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t+1}-\theta\Delta v_{t}=(1+\theta)\Delta w_{t}-\theta\Delta v_{t}-(\frac{1+\theta}{\beta})\Delta g_{t}
. Thus, the subsequent state vector can be written as

x_{t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta w_{t+1}\\
\Delta v_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\theta)\Delta w_{t}-\theta\Delta v_{t}-(\frac{1+\theta}{\beta})\Delta g_{t}\\
\Delta w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta g_{t}\end{bmatrix}.

(14)

Before we construct our quadratic Lyapunov function, we take advantage of the properties of the smooth quadratic regime to derive some useful results, particularly with respect to the gradient difference
\Delta g_{t}
. To do so, consider the following lemma from
10
:

Lemma 9

(Integral Form of Taylor’s Theorem)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuously differentiable and let
\nabla f
be Lipschitz-continuous. Then for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)=(\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}f(y+t(x-y))dt)(x-y).

(15)

Proof.

Define the affine function
\phi(t)=\nabla f(y+t(x-y))
for
t\in[0,1]
. Then
\phi(0)=\nabla f(y)
and
\phi(1)=\nabla f(x)
. This implies
\phi(1)-\phi(0)=\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)
. We compute
\phi^{\prime}(t)
. By the chain rule,
\phi^{\prime}(t)=\nabla^{2}f(y+t(x-y))(x-y)
. Moreover, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus,
\phi(1)-\phi(0)=\int_{0}^{1}\phi^{\prime}(t)dt
. Then substituting
\phi(1)-\phi(0)=\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)
and
\phi^{\prime}(t)=\nabla^{2}f(y+t(x-y))(x-y)
as we derived yields

\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)=(\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}f(y+t(x-y))dt)(x-y)
.

∎

We use this lemma from
10
to derive the following relation and bound on
||H_{t}||
and
||\Delta g_{t}||
:

Theorem 10
.

Let
\ell(w_{t},z_{t})
,
\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{t}^{\prime})
where
z_{t}=z^{\prime}_{t}
be twice differentiable as well as
\gamma
-strongly convex and
\beta
-smooth. Then, for
H_{t}:=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}\ell(w_{t}^{\prime}-s(w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}),z_{t})ds
,

\Delta g_{t}=H_{t}\Delta w_{t}
,

with
||H_{t}||\leq\beta
and
||\Delta g_{t}||\leq\beta||\Delta w_{t}||
.

Proof.

Define the affine function
\phi(s):=\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t}+s(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}),z_{t})
for
s\in[0,1]
. By the Integral Form of Taylor’s Theorem, this implies

\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{t})=(\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}\ell(w_{t}^{\prime}+s(w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}),z_{t})ds)(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t})
.

But we already define
\Delta g_{t}=\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{t})
and
\Delta w_{t}=w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}
. Moreover, let
H_{t}:=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}\ell(w_{t}^{\prime}+s(w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}),z_{t})ds
. Then

\Delta g_{t}=H_{t}\Delta w_{t}
.

We next show that

. Since

is twice-differentiable,

-strongly convex, and

-smooth, it follows that

. We know that

are both symmetric matrices since

is twice-differentiable so all of the second-order derivatives contained in

), it follows that
\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}\ell(w^{\prime}_{t}-s(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}),z_{t})ds\preceq\beta I

. But recall

. This implies

. The norm of any symmetric matrix is equal to the absolute value of its largest eigenvalue. Let
\lambda_{\text{max}(H_{t})}
denote maximum eigenvalue of

(so
\lambda_{\text{max}(H_{t})}=||H_{t}||
). Since
H_{t}\preceq\beta I
, it follows that
\lambda_{\text{max}(H_{t})}\leq\beta
, which implies
||H_{t}||\leq\beta
.

We last show
||\Delta g_{t}||\leq\beta||\Delta w_{t}||
.
\ell(w_{t},z_{t})
is assumed to be
\beta
-smooth, i.e.,
||\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{t})||\leq\beta||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||
. But recall we define
\Delta g_{t}:=\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{t})
and
\Delta w_{t}:=w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}
. Then
||\Delta g_{t}||\leq\beta||\Delta w_{t}||
.
∎

Recall that the subsequent state vector can be written as

x_{t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta w_{t+1}\\
\Delta v_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\theta)\Delta w_{t}-\theta\Delta v_{t}-(\frac{1+\theta}{\beta})\Delta g_{t}\\

Direct Construction of NAG Quadratic Lyapunov Function

Here, we explicitly construct the quadratic Lyapunov function used to prove the uniform stability of smooth quadratic NAG.

Theorem 11

(Lyapunov Function for NAG Uniform Stability)
.

Assume that the loss
\ell(w,z)
is
\gamma
-strongly convex with
\beta
-Lipschitz (
\beta
-smooth) and
G
-bounded gradients for all
z
. Run NAG with constant step size
\eta\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
and fixed momentum parameter
0\leq\mu<1
for
T
iterations on a dataset
S
and
S^{\prime}
, the latter of which is
S
with a single arbitrary data point
z_{j_{t}}
modified. Let
x_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta w_{t}\\
\Delta v_{t}\end{bmatrix}
denote the state vector which contains
\Delta w_{t}=w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}
and
\Delta v_{t}=v_{t}-v^{\prime}_{t}
. Then we obtain the valid quadratic Lyapunov function

\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})=a||\Delta w_{t}||^{2}+b||\Delta v_{t}||^{2}+2c\langle\Delta w_{t},\Delta v_{t}\rangle,

(19)

where
a=1
,
b=(1+\theta)^{2}+\epsilon
, and
c=-(1+\theta)
,
\forall\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

We wish to find a quadratic Lyapunov function
\mathcal{V}(x)
for NAG. By definition, such a quadratic Lyapunov function will take the form
\mathcal{V}(x)=x^{T}Px
. Recall the update rules for NAG from
5
,

v_{t+1}=w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t}),

(20)

w_{t+1}=(1+\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1})v_{t+1}-(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1})v_{t}.

(21)

\kappa=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}
is the condition number. Let
\theta=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}
. Then the NAG update rules can be re-written as

v_{t+1}=w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t}),

(22)

w_{t+1}=(1+\theta)v_{t+1}-\theta v_{t}.

(23)

We next find the Lyapunov
\mathcal{V}(x_{t})=x_{t}^{T}Px_{t}
where
P=P^{T}\succ 0
. Let
P=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\
c&b\end{bmatrix}
. Clearly
P=P^{T}
. Consider
\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})=x_{t+1}^{T}Px_{t+1}
. Hence we have

\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})=x_{t+1}^{T}Px_{t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta w_{t+1}^{T}&\Delta v_{t+1}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\
c&b\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta w_{t+1}\\
\Delta v_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}.

(24)

Performing the needed matrix multiplication yields

\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})=a\Delta w_{t+1}\Delta w^{T}_{t+1}+b\Delta v_{t+1}\Delta v^{T}_{t+1}+c\Delta w^{T}_{t+1}\Delta v_{t+1}+c\Delta v_{t+1}^{T}\Delta w_{t+1}.

(25)

For any vector
u
,
||u||^{2}=u^{T}u
. Moreover, by the definition of the dot product,
c\Delta w^{T}_{t+1}\Delta v_{t+1}+c\Delta v_{t+1}^{T}\Delta w_{t+1}=2c\langle\Delta w_{t+1},\Delta v_{t+1}\rangle
. Thus,

\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})=a||\Delta w_{t+1}||^{2}+b||\Delta v_{t+1}||^{2}+2c\langle\Delta w_{t+1},\Delta v_{t+1}\rangle.

(26)

A Lyapunov function is non-increasing, i.e.,
\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})-\mathcal{V}(x_{t})\leq 0
. Recall
x_{t+1}=A_{\alpha}x_{t}
for
A_{\alpha}=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\theta)\alpha&-\theta\\
\alpha&0\end{bmatrix}
with
\alpha\in[0,1-\frac{\gamma}{\beta}]
, so we have
\mathcal{V}(x_{t})=x_{t}^{T}Px_{t}
and
\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})=x_{t}^{T}A_{\alpha}^{T}PA_{\alpha}x_{t}
. This implies

x_{t}^{T}(A_{\alpha}^{T}PA_{\alpha}-P)x_{t}\leq 0.

(27)

This implies the matrix inequality

A_{\alpha}^{T}PA_{\alpha}-P\preceq 0,

(28)

or
A_{\alpha}^{T}PA_{\alpha}\preceq P
. But this inequality does not guarantee the desired contraction from each
\mathcal{V}(x_{t})
to
\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})
. Instead, to guarantee strict contraction on the Lyapunov function, we enforce the condition

\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})\leq(1-\rho)\mathcal{V}(x_{t})

(29)

for
\rho>0
. This is analogous to what
7
did in their uniform stability proof of SGD, whereby they derived
||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||\leq(1-\eta\gamma)||w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}||
, i.e., the parameter iterate divergence in SGD ran on
S
and
S^{\prime}
strictly contracts by a factor of
1-\rho
where
\rho=\eta\gamma
(cf. Appendix A). Enforcing this strict contraction condition on our Lyapunov function yields the matrix inequality

A_{\alpha}^{T}PA_{\alpha}\preceq(1-\rho)P,

(30)

so we wish to find
P=P^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\
c&b\end{bmatrix}
that satisfies the above matrix inequality. This matrix inequality can likewise be written as
A_{\alpha}^{T}PA_{\alpha}-(1-\rho)P\preceq 0
. Let
M_{\alpha}:=A_{\alpha}^{T}PA_{\alpha}-(1-\rho)P
. Then by computation we obtain

M_{\alpha}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{2}[a(1+\theta)^{2}+2c(1+\theta)+b]-(1-\rho)a&-\alpha\theta(a(1+\theta)+c)-(1-\rho)c\\
-\alpha\theta(a(1+\theta)+c)-(1-\rho)c&a\theta^{2}-(1-\rho)b\end{bmatrix}\preceq 0,

(31)

for
\alpha\in[0,1-\frac{\gamma}{\beta}]
. Note
M_{\alpha}=M_{\alpha}^{T}
. Let
S:=a(1+\theta)^{2}+2c(1+\theta)+b
and
T:=a(1+\theta)+c
. Then we have

M_{\alpha}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{2}S-(1-\rho)a&-a\theta T-(1-\rho)c\\
a\theta T-(1-\rho)c&a\theta^{2}-(1-\rho)b\end{bmatrix}.

(32)

To ensure
M_{\alpha}\preceq 0
, we want
\alpha^{2}S-(1-\rho)a+a\theta^{2}-(1-\rho)b\leq 0
(i.e.,
\text{tr}(M_{\alpha})\leq 0
) and
(\alpha^{2}S-(1-\rho)a)(a\theta^{2}-(1-\rho)b)-(a\theta T-(1-\rho)c)^{2}\geq 0
(i.e.,
\text{det}(M_{\alpha})\geq 0)
. By virtue of the invariance of the Lyapunov inequality under positive scaling of
P
, we normalize by setting
a=1
. Moreover, by construction, let
T=0
, which implies
c=-(1+\theta)
. Thus, we now have

M_{\alpha}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{2}S-(1-\rho)&(1-\rho)(1+\theta)\\
(1-\rho)(1+\theta)&\theta^{2}-(1-\rho)b\end{bmatrix}.

(33)

Again by construction let
S=0
. This implies, with
a=1
and
c=-(1+\theta)
,
(1+\theta)^{2}-2(1+\theta)^{2}+b=0
, which implies
b=(1+\theta)^{2}
.

Thus, we have
P=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\
c&b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-(1+\theta)\\
-(1+\theta)&(1+\theta)^{2}\end{bmatrix}
. Note
\text{tr}(P)=1+(1+\theta)^{2}\geq 0
and
\text{det}(P)=(1+\theta)^{2}-[(-(1+\theta))(-(1+\theta))]=0\geq 0
. One can verify that the eigenvalues of
P
are
\lambda_{1}=0\geq 0
and
\lambda_{2}=1+(1+\theta)^{2}\geq 0
, so
P\succeq 0
.

With
a=1
,
b=(1+\theta)^{2}
, and
c=-(1+\theta)
, our Lyapunov function is

\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})=||\Delta w_{t+1}||^{2}+(1+\theta)^{2}||\Delta v_{t+1}||^{2}-2(1+\theta)\langle\Delta w_{t+1},\Delta v_{t+1}\rangle.

(34)

By completing the square, we likewise have

\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})=||\Delta w_{t+1}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t+1}||^{2}.

(35)

But
\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})=||\Delta w_{t+1}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t+1}||^{2}
does not suffice as a valid Lyapunov function since
P=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\
c&b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-(1+\theta)\\
-(1+\theta)&(1+\theta)^{2}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0
, i.e.,
P
is a positive semi-definite but not positive definite matrix as required (recall that one of the eigenvalues of our originally constructed
P
is
\lambda_{1}=0
). To rectify this, using a common control-theoretic technique, we employ
strictification
of
P
by adding
\epsilon>0
to the bottom-right entry of
P
, i.e.,

P_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-(1+\theta)\\
-(1+\theta)&(1+\theta)^{2}+\epsilon\end{bmatrix},

(36)

for
\epsilon>0
. Note
\text{det}(P_{\epsilon})=\epsilon>0
and
\text{tr}(P_{\epsilon})=1+(1+\theta)^{2}+\epsilon>0
. The eigenvalues of
P_{\epsilon}
are
\lambda_{1,2}=\frac{[(1+\theta)^{2}+\epsilon+1]\pm\sqrt{[(1+\theta)^{2}+\epsilon+1]^{2}-4\epsilon}}{2}
.
\lambda_{1,2}>0
for all
\epsilon>0
, so with strictification we have
P_{\epsilon}\succ 0
as desired. We now find our “new” Lyapunov function
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})=x_{t}^{T}P_{\epsilon}x_{t}
. With
P_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-(1+\theta)\\
-(1+\theta)&(1+\theta)^{2}+\epsilon\end{bmatrix}
, we obtain

\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})=||\Delta w_{t}||^{2}+[(1+\theta)^{2}+\epsilon]||\Delta v_{t}||^{2}-2(1+\theta)\langle\Delta w_{t},\Delta v_{t}\rangle.

(37)

Completing the square yields

\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})=||\Delta w_{t}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}||^{2}+\epsilon||\Delta v_{t}||^{2}.

(38)

∎

Remark
In convergence analyses, the standard
1-\rho
contraction rate for smooth quadratic NAG is
1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}
, i.e., with
\rho=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}
(cf.
8
). We show that our
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})
as constructed recovers this standard NAG contraction rate.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})=||\Delta w_{t}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}||^{2}+\epsilon||\Delta v_{t}||^{2}
where
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t+1})\leq(1-\rho)\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})
for
\rho>0
,
\epsilon>0
. Then
\rho=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}})
and
1-\rho=1-O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}})
.

Proof.

Let
y_{t}:=\Delta w_{t}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}
so
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})=||y_{t}||^{2}+\epsilon||\Delta v_{t}||^{2}
. We next find
y_{t+1}=\Delta w_{t+1}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t+1}
. Recall from our state dynamics that

\Delta v_{t+1}=\Delta w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}H_{t}\Delta w_{t},

(39)

\Delta w_{t+1}=(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t+1}-\theta\Delta v_{t}.

(40)

Then substitution yields

y_{t+1}=-\theta\Delta v_{t}.

(41)

Our strategy is to construct a lifted linear two-dimensional dynamical system in
(y_{t},\Delta v_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
state space to derive
\rho
. To do so, note that
y_{t}
is a function of
\Delta w_{t}
so we need to decouple
y_{t}
from
\Delta w_{t}
. Note
\Delta v_{t+1}=(I-\frac{1}{\beta}H_{t})\Delta w_{t}
. Let
\alpha_{t}:=I-\frac{1}{\beta}
. Then
\Delta v_{t+1}=\alpha_{t}\Delta w_{t}
and we obtain

\Delta v_{t+1}=\alpha_{t}y_{t}+(1+\theta)\alpha_{t}\Delta v_{t}.

(42)

Let
z_{t+1}:=\begin{bmatrix}y_{t+1}\\
\Delta v_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}
. Then
z_{t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}-\theta\Delta v_{t}\\
\alpha_{t}y_{t}+(1+\theta)\alpha_{t}\Delta v_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\theta\\
\alpha_{t}&(1+\theta)\alpha_{t}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}y_{t}\\
\Delta v_{t}\end{bmatrix}
. Let
\Gamma_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\theta\\
\alpha_{t}&(1+\theta)\alpha_{t}\end{bmatrix}
so the iteration evolution of
z_{t}
in this lifted state space is governed by
z_{t+1}=\Gamma_{t}z_{t}
. The characteristic equation of
\Gamma_{t}
is
\lambda^{2}-(1+\theta)\alpha_{t}\lambda+\theta\alpha_{t}=0
. For sufficiently large
\kappa
, we have
\theta\approx 1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}
, and we fix
\alpha_{t}=1-\frac{1}{\kappa}
corresponding to the worst-case eigen-direction. Then
\Gamma_{t}\approx\begin{bmatrix}0&-1+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\\
1-\frac{1}{\kappa}&(2-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}})(1-\frac{1}{\kappa})\end{bmatrix}
where
\text{tr}(\Gamma_{t})=2-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}
and
\text{det}(\Gamma_{t})=1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}
. Then the characteristic equation becomes

\lambda^{2}-(2-\frac{2}{\sqrt{k}})\lambda+(1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}})=0.

(43)

Solving for
\lambda_{1,2}
yields
\lambda_{1,2}\in[1,1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}]
. Then, for
\lambda_{2}=1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}
,
\rho=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}})
.
∎

For uniform stability results, we want to bound
||\Delta w_{t}||^{2}
in terms of
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})
.

Theorem 13

(Lyapunov Bound on
||\Delta w_{t}||^{2}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})=||\Delta w_{t}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}||^{2}+\epsilon||\Delta v_{t}||^{2}
for
\epsilon>0
. Then

||\Delta w_{t}||^{2}\leq C_{\epsilon}\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})

where
C_{\epsilon}=(1+\frac{(1+\theta)^{2}}{\epsilon})
.

Proof.

Note
\Delta w_{t}=(\Delta w_{t}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t})+(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}
. Let
a:=\Delta w_{t}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}
and
b:=(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}
. Then
||\Delta w_{t}||=||a+b||
. We now make use of the following lemma:

Lemma 14

(Young’s Inequality, Inner Product Form)
.

Let
a
and
b
be vectors. Then, for all
\zeta>0
,

||a+b||^{2}\leq(1+\zeta)||a||^{2}+(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})||b||^{2}
.

Therefore, by Young’s Inequality,

||\Delta w_{t}||^{2}\leq(1+\zeta)||\Delta w_{t}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}||^{2}+(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})||(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}||^{2}.

(44)

This is equivalent to

||\Delta w_{t}||^{2}\leq(1+\zeta)||\Delta w_{t}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}||^{2}+(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})(1+\theta)^{2}||\Delta v_{t}||^{2}.

(45)

Recall
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})=||\Delta w_{t}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}||^{2}+\epsilon||\Delta v_{t}||^{2}
. Then
\epsilon(1+\zeta)=(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})(1+\theta)^{2}
, which yields
\zeta=\frac{(1+\theta^{2})}{\epsilon}
. This gives us

||\Delta w_{t}||^{2}\leq(1+\frac{(1+\theta)^{2}}{\epsilon})\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t}).

(46)

Let
C_{\epsilon}=(1+\frac{(1+\theta)^{2}}{\epsilon})
. Then we have

||\Delta w_{t}||^{2}\leq C_{\epsilon}\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t}).

(47)

∎

This also gives the bound
||\Delta w_{t}||\leq\sqrt{C_{\epsilon}}\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})}
.

3
Uniform Stability of Smooth Quadratic NAG via Constructed Lyapunov

We are now in position to give a uniform stability bound for smooth quadratic NAG. Recall that
6
derived an
O(\frac{4\beta^{2}}{\gamma n}[1-(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}})^{T}])
uniform stability bound for smooth quadratic NAG. Via Lyapunov analysis, we derive our own uniform stability bound for smooth quadratic NAG as follows:

Theorem 15

(Uniform Stability of Smooth Quadratic NAG)
.

Assume that the loss
\ell(w_{t},z_{t})
is
\gamma
-strongly convex with
\beta
-Lipschitz/
\beta
-smooth and bounded gradients for all
z_{t}
. Run NAG with constant step size
\eta\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
with fixed momentum parameter
\mu\in[0,1)
for
T
total iterations. Then NAG in the smooth quadratic regime is uniformly stable with

\epsilon\leq O(\frac{4G\kappa^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\beta\sqrt{n}}[\sqrt{1-(1-\rho)^{T}}])

for condition number
\kappa=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}
and
\rho>0
.

Proof.

Let
S
and
S^{\prime}
be two samples of size
n
that differ only at a single point. We want to show that NAG is
\epsilon
-uniformly stable, i.e., by definition,

\text{sup}_{z}\mathbb{E}[\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}})]\leq\epsilon

for arbitrary point
z_{i_{t}}
and for
\epsilon>0
. Let NAG run over coupled indices over
S
and
S^{\prime}
, where
S=(z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{n})
and
S^{\prime}=(z_{1}^{\prime},z_{2}^{\prime},...,z_{n}^{\prime})
. We denote the index of the differing data point as
j\in\{1,2,...,n\}
, i.e.,
z_{j}\neq z_{j}^{\prime}
(so
z_{i}=z_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i\neq j
). Recall the update rules for
\beta
-smooth, quadratic NAG
5
:

v_{t+1}=w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t}),

(48)

w_{t+1}=(1+\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1})v_{t+1}-(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1})v_{t}.

(49)

Setting
\theta=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}
yields

v_{t+1}=w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{t}),

(50)

w_{t+1}=(1+\theta)v_{t+1}-\theta v_{t}.

(51)

Note that there are two possible cases, (1) where the differing point is not selected, i.e., where
i_{t}\neq j
, and (2) where the differing point is selected, i.e., where
i_{t}=j
. We prove the uniform stability of NAG via the quadratic Lyapunov function we constructed:

\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})=||\Delta w_{t}-(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t}||^{2}+\epsilon||\Delta v_{t}||^{2}.

(52)

Our proof strategy is to find the expected value of the Lyapunov function,
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})]
, over both cases. We do so via the law of total expectation from probability theory, i.e., for discrete random variable
X
with probability
P(A)
for event
A
, where
A^{c}
is the complement of
A
with probability
P(A^{c})
,

\mathbb{E}[X]=P(A)\mathbb{E}[X|A]+P(A^{c})\mathbb{E}[X|A^{c}].

(53)

Let
A
denote the event where the differing point is not selected, which we denote
i_{t}\neq j
, so
A^{c}
denotes the event where the differing point is selected (i.e.,
i_{t}=j
). We assume that the samples are uniformly distributed, which implies
P(A)=1-\frac{1}{n}
and
P(A^{c})=\frac{1}{n}
. Then the expected value of our Lyapunov function over both cases is

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})]=(1-\frac{1}{n})\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})|i_{t}\neq j]+(\frac{1}{n})\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})|i_{t}=j].

(54)

Thus, we prove by cases:

i_{t}\neq j
This is the case where the differing point is
not
selected. Let
\{z_{1},...,z_{n}\}
be uniformly distributed so
P[i_{t}\neq j]=1-\frac{1}{n}
. Note that
\Delta v_{t+1}=\Delta w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta g_{t}
. This implies
\Delta w_{t+1}=(1+\theta)(\Delta v_{t+1})-\theta(\Delta v_{t})=(1+\theta)(\Delta w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta g_{t})-\theta(\Delta v_{t})=(1+\theta)\Delta w_{t}-\theta(\Delta v_{t})-(\frac{1+\theta}{\beta})\Delta g_{t}
. Recall that we derived
\Delta g_{t}=H_{t}\Delta w_{t}
. Substitution and factoring yields

x_{t+1}=A(H_{t})x_{t},

(55)

where
A(H_{t})=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\theta)(I-\frac{H_{t}}{\beta})&-\theta I\\
I-\frac{H_{t}}{\beta}&0\end{bmatrix}
. Recall that our quadratic Lyapunov function takes the form
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t+1})=x_{t}^{T}P_{\epsilon}x_{t}
where
P_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-(1+\theta)\\
-(1+\theta)&(1+\theta)^{2}+\epsilon\end{bmatrix}=P_{\epsilon}^{T}\succ 0
. Our Lyapunov function satisfies
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t+1})\leq(1-\rho)x_{t}^{T}P_{\epsilon}x_{t}
so
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t+1})\leq(1-\rho)\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})
. Therefore, taking expectation in the event where
i_{t}\neq j
yields

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t+1})|i_{t}\neq j]\leq(1-\rho)\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t}).

(56)

i_{t}=j
This is the case where the differing point
is
selected. We keep the same uniform distribution assumption on
\{z_{1},...,z_{n}\}
so
P[i_{t}=j]=\frac{1}{n}
. Recall
\Delta g_{t}=\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{i_{t}})
. But here
i_{t}=j
so we have
\Delta g_{t}=\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{j})-\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j}^{\prime})
. Note that
\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{j})-\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j}^{\prime})=\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{j})-\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j})+\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j})-\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j}^{\prime})
. Thus, we have
\Delta g_{t}=H_{t}\Delta w_{t}+\Xi_{t}
for
\Xi_{t}:=\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j})-\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j}^{\prime})
.

We can bound
||\Xi_{t}||
via boundedness of the gradient. Note
||\Xi_{t}||=||\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j})-\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j}^{\prime})||
. Then by the Triangle Inequality
||\Xi_{t}||\leq||\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j})||+||\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j}^{\prime})||
. But by boundedness of the gradient,
||\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j})||\leq G
and
||\nabla\ell(w_{t}^{\prime},z_{j}^{\prime})||\leq G
. Thus,
||\Xi_{t}||\leq 2G
.

Recall that
\Delta v_{t+1}=\Delta w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta g_{t}
. Then substituting
\Delta g_{t}=H_{t}\Delta w_{t}+\Xi_{t}
yields
\Delta v_{t+1}=(I-\frac{H_{t}}{\beta})\Delta w_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\Xi_{t}
. Moreover, for
\Delta w_{t+1}=(1+\theta)\Delta v_{t+1}-\theta\Delta v_{t}
, this yields
\Delta w_{t+1}=(1+\theta)(I-\frac{H_{t}}{\beta})\Delta w_{t}-\theta\Delta v_{t}-(\frac{1+\theta}{\beta})\Xi_{t}
. Thus, the iteration evolution of the state vector
x_{t}
takes the form

x_{t+1}=A(H_{t})x_{t}+B\Xi_{t},

(57)

where
A(H_{t})=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\theta)(I-\frac{H_{t}}{\beta})&-\theta I\\
I-\frac{H_{t}}{\beta}&0\end{bmatrix}
and
B=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{(1+\theta)}{\beta}\\
-\frac{1}{\beta}\end{bmatrix}
. By change of metric from standard Euclidean
L^{2}
norm to the
P_{\epsilon}
norm, our Lyapunov function takes the form
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t+1})=||x_{t+1}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}
. Then
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t+1})=||A(H_{t})x_{t}+B\Xi_{t}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}
and by Young’s Inequality we have

||A(H_{t})x_{t}+B\Xi_{t}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}\leq(1+\zeta)||A(H_{t})x_{t}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}+(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})||B\Xi_{t}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}

(58)

for
\zeta>0
. Note that
||\Xi_{t}||\leq 2G
only in the
L^{2}
norm, so this bound is not guaranteed in the new
P_{\epsilon}
norm. We want to bound
||B\Xi_{t}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}
. By definition,
||B\Xi_{t}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}=(B\Xi_{t})^{T}P_{\epsilon}(B\Xi_{t})
. Computation yields
||B\Xi_{t}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}=(\frac{||\Xi_{t}||^{2}}{\beta^{2}})\epsilon\leq(\frac{(2G)^{2}}{\beta^{2}})(\epsilon)=\frac{4G^{2}\epsilon}{\beta^{2}}
. We next bound
||A(H_{t})x_{t}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}
. Note
||A(H_{t})x_{t}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}=x_{t}^{T}(A(H_{t})^{T}P_{\epsilon}A(H_{t}))x_{t}
. But by construction there exists
\epsilon>0
,
\rho>0
such that
A(H_{t})^{T}P_{\epsilon}A(H_{t})\preceq(1-\rho)P_{\epsilon}
. Then it follows that
||A(H_{t})x_{t}||^{2}_{P_{\epsilon}}\leq(1-\rho)\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})
. Thus, in the case where
i_{t}=j
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t+1})|i_{t}=j]\leq(1+\zeta)(1-\rho)\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})+(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})(\frac{4G^{2}\epsilon}{\beta^{2}}).

(59)

Then by the Law of Total Expectation we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t+1})]\leq(1-\frac{1}{n})(1-\rho)\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})+\frac{1}{n}[(1+\zeta)(1-\rho)\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})+(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})(\frac{4G^{2}\epsilon}{\beta^{2}})],

(60)

for
\zeta,\rho,\epsilon>0
. Algebraic manipulation yields

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t+1})]\leq(1-\rho)(1+\frac{\zeta}{n})\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})+\frac{(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})(4G^{2}\epsilon)}{n\beta^{2}}.

(61)

As
n\rightarrow\infty
,
(1-\rho)(1+\frac{\zeta}{n})\rightarrow(1-\rho)
. Let
\delta_{t}:=\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})
and let
C:=\frac{(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})(4G^{2}\epsilon)}{n\beta^{2}}
. Then we have a linear recurrence of the form

\delta_{t+1}\leq(1-\rho)\delta_{t}+C

(62)

for sufficiently large
n
. Since each of
S
and
S^{\prime}
are initialized identically, we have
\delta_{0}=0
so this linear recurrence is unrolled as

\delta_{T}\leq C\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}(1-\rho)^{k}.

(63)

|1-\rho|<1
so this geometric series converges. Thus, we have

\delta_{T}\leq C(\frac{1-(1-\rho)^{T}}{\rho}).

(64)

This yields
\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})\leq(\frac{(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})(4G^{2}\epsilon)}{n\beta^{2}})(\frac{1-(1-\rho)^{T}}{\rho})
. But we derived
||\Delta w_{T}||^{2}\leq C_{\epsilon}\mathcal{V}_{\epsilon}(x_{t})
where
C_{\epsilon}=1+\frac{(1+\theta)^{2}}{\epsilon}
. Then we have

\delta_{T}^{2}\leq(1+\frac{(1+\theta)^{2}}{\epsilon})(\frac{(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})(4G^{2}\epsilon)}{n\beta^{2}})(\frac{1-(1-\rho)^{T}}{\rho}).

(65)

Factoring and absorbing constants yields

\delta_{T}^{2}\leq(\frac{16G^{2}\sqrt{\kappa}}{n\beta^{2}})[1-(1-\rho)^{T}].

(66)

Then by
G
-Lipschitzness of the loss function and taking the square root we have

\delta_{T}\leq\frac{4G^{2}\kappa^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\beta\sqrt{n}}[\sqrt{1-(1-\rho)^{T}}].

(67)

Therefore, smooth quadratic NAG is uniformly stable with
\epsilon\leq O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
.
∎

Remark
Our uniform stability bound for smooth quadratic NAG is
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
, whereas the one derived by
6
is
O(\frac{1}{n})
. Moreover, as
T\rightarrow\infty
, we obtain a uniform stability bound
O(\frac{4G^{2}k^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\beta\sqrt{n}})
(i.e., with no consideration of “early stopping” as done in practice). Taking the same limit for the bound derived by
6
yields an
O(\frac{4\beta^{2}}{\gamma n})
bound.

5
Semi-Definite Programming for Automated Stability Certificates

Our method of deriving the Lyapunov function “by hand” to prove the uniform stability of smooth quadratic NAG was rather cumbersome. Here, we detail an approach that leverages the smooth quadratic regime to automate this method that can also be adapted to other first-order (accelerated) optimizers of similar structure.

8
show how first-order optimizers can be represented as
Lur’e
systems, i.e., a linear dynamical system of the form

\begin{cases}\zeta_{k+1}=A\zeta_{k}+Bu_{k},\\
y_{k}=C\zeta_{k}+Du_{k},\end{cases}

(68)

for inputs
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, outputs
y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and state vectors
\zeta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. All first-order optimizers use the gradient as its oracle, and introducing the gradient (operator) consequently introduces non-linearity into the system. As
8
show, let
\phi(y):=\nabla f(y)
. Then a first-order optimizer with non-linearity
\phi
takes the form

\begin{cases}\zeta_{k+1}=A\zeta_{k}+Bu_{k},\\
y_{k}=C\zeta_{k}+Du_{k},\\
u_{k}=\phi(y_{k}).\end{cases}

(69)

NAG can be formulated as a Lur’e system in this framework from
8
. Let
x_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}w_{t}\\
w_{t-1}\end{bmatrix}
. Then smooth quadratic NAG in Lur’e form is

\begin{cases}x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t},\\
y_{t}=Cx_{t}+Du_{t},\\
u_{t}=\phi(y_{t}),\end{cases}

(70)

with
A=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\theta)I&-\theta I\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}
,
B=\begin{bmatrix}-\eta I\\
0\end{bmatrix}
,
C=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\theta)I&-\theta I\end{bmatrix}
, and
D=0
.

Note that, in the smooth quadratic regime,
\gamma
-strong convexity and
\beta
-smoothness of the loss
\ell(w_{t},z_{t})
both hold. Since smooth quadratic NAG in Lur’e form is representable in an affine space, finding
P\succ 0
is achievable via semi-definite programming (SDP) (cf. Lecture 4 of
3
for a comprehensive overview of SDP). Recall
x_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta w_{t}\\
\Delta v_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}\\
v_{t}-v_{t}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}
and that we prove the uniform stability of smooth quadratic NAG via the quadratic Lyapunov function
\mathcal{V}(x_{t})=x_{t}^{T}Px_{t}
. We construct an SDP that finds
P\succ 0
,
\lambda>0
such that
\mathcal{V}(x_{t})=x_{t}^{T}Px_{t}
satisfies

\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})-\mathcal{V}(x_{t})\leq-\lambda||x_{t}||^{2}

(71)

for any
S
,
S^{\prime}
. Let
\Delta V:=\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})-\mathcal{V}(x_{t})
. Then our SDP finds
P\succ 0
,
\lambda>0
such that

\Delta V\leq-\lambda||x_{t}||^{2}.

(72)

Recall that, in Lur’e form,
x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t}
, which implies
\mathcal{V}(x_{t+1})=(Ax_{t}+Bu_{t})^{T}P(Ax_{t}+Bu_{t})=x_{t}^{T}A^{T}PAx_{t}+2x_{t}^{T}A^{T}PBu_{t}+u_{t}^{T}B^{T}PBu_{t}
. This implies

\Delta V=x_{t}^{T}(A^{T}PA-P)x_{t}+2x_{t}^{T}A^{T}PBu_{t}+u_{t}^{T}B^{T}PBu_{t}.

(73)

Let
z_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
u_{t}\end{bmatrix}
. Then we have

\Delta V=z_{t}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}A^{T}PA-P&A^{T}PB\\
B^{T}PA&B^{T}PB\end{bmatrix}z_{t}

(74)

with
M:=\begin{bmatrix}A^{T}PA-P&A^{T}PB\\
B^{T}PA&B^{T}PB\end{bmatrix}
. Recall that the condition for our SDP is
\Delta V\leq-\lambda||x_{t}||^{2}
for
\lambda>0
. Note that
-\lambda||x_{t}||^{2}=-z_{t}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda I&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}z_{t}
. Thus, we have

z_{t}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}A^{T}PA-P+\lambda I&A^{T}PB\\
B^{T}PA&B^{T}PB\end{bmatrix}z_{t}\leq 0.

(75)

Let
M^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}A^{T}PA-P+\lambda I&A^{T}PB\\
B^{T}PA&B^{T}PB\end{bmatrix}
. Then this condition takes the form

z_{t}^{T}M^{\prime}z_{t}\leq 0.

(76)

Recall
z_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
u_{t}\end{bmatrix}
with
u_{t}=\phi(y_{t})=\nabla f(y)
by our Lur’e construction. Since we are in the smooth quadratic regime, we know
\gamma I\preceq\nabla^{2}f\preceq\beta I
, which thus constrains the set of all possible
z_{t}
via the following
sector inequality/IQC
:

Theorem 16

(Smooth Quadratic Gradient Sector IQC)
.

Let
f
be both
\gamma
-strongly convex and
\beta
-smooth, and let
\phi(y_{t})=\nabla\ell(y_{t},z_{t})
. Let
\Delta y_{t}:=y_{t}-y_{t}^{\prime}
,
\Delta u_{t}:=\phi(y_{t})-\phi(y_{t}^{\prime})
, and
\xi_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta y_{t}\\
\Delta u_{t}\end{bmatrix}
. Then any admissible trajectory of
(\Delta y_{t},\Delta u_{t})
must satisfy

\xi_{t}^{T}(\tau_{1}\Pi_{1}+\tau_{2}\Pi_{2})\xi_{t}\geq 0

for
\Pi_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}-\gamma I&\frac{1}{2}I\\
\frac{1}{2}I&0\end{bmatrix}
,
\Pi_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2}I\\
\frac{1}{2}I&-\frac{1}{\beta}I\end{bmatrix}
, and
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\geq 0
.

Proof.

f
is both
\gamma
-strongly convex and
\beta
-smooth, so we make use of the following result from
2
:

Lemma 17

(Baillon-Haddad Theorem)
.

Let
f
be convex, continuously differentiable, and defined everywhere on a Hilbert space with
\beta
-Lipschitz gradients. Then
\forall x,y\in\text{dom}(f)
,

\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\beta}||\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)||^{2}
.

The Baillon-Haddad Theorem is also known as
co-coercivity of the gradient
. Since
\Delta y_{t}:=y_{t}-y_{t}^{\prime}
and
\Delta u_{t}:=\phi(y_{t})-\phi(y_{t}^{\prime})
, we have

\langle\Delta u_{t},\Delta y_{t}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\beta}||\Delta u_{t}||^{2}.

(77)

Moreover, since
f
is
\gamma
-strongly convex, by corollary
f
also satisfies monotonicity of the gradient, i.e.,
\forall x,y\in\text{dom}(f)
,
\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\geq\gamma||x-y||^{2}
. Consequently, we also have

\langle\Delta u_{t},\Delta y_{t}\rangle\geq\gamma||\Delta y_{t}||^{2}.

(78)

This implies

\langle\Delta u_{t},\Delta y_{t}\rangle-\gamma||\Delta y_{t}||^{2}\geq 0.

(79)

Let
\xi_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta y_{t}\\
\Delta u_{t}\end{bmatrix}
. Then we have the IQC for the
\gamma
-strong convexity condition, i.e.,

\xi_{t}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}-\gamma I&\frac{1}{2}I\\
\frac{1}{2}I&0\end{bmatrix}\xi_{t}\geq 0,

(80)

where
\Pi_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}-\gamma I&\frac{1}{2}I\\
\frac{1}{2}I&0\end{bmatrix}
. Recall
\langle\Delta u_{t},\Delta y_{t}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\beta}||\Delta u_{t}||^{2}
by the Baillon-Haddad Theorem. This implies

\langle\Delta u_{t},\Delta y_{t}\rangle-\frac{1}{\beta}||\Delta u_{t}||^{2}\geq 0.

(81)

Then the IQC for
\beta
-smoothness condition is

\xi_{t}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2}I\\
\frac{1}{2}I&-\frac{1}{\beta}I\end{bmatrix}\xi_{t}\geq 0,

(82)

with
\Pi_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2}I\\
\frac{1}{2}I&-\frac{1}{\beta}I\end{bmatrix}
. Any admissible trajectory of
(\Delta y_{t},\Delta u_{t})
must satisfy each IQC, so any admissible trajectory of
(\Delta y_{t},\Delta u_{t})
likewise satisfies any non-negative combination of these IQC’s, i.e., for
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\geq 0
,

\xi_{t}^{T}(\tau_{1}\Pi_{1}+\tau_{2}\Pi_{2})\xi_{t}\geq 0.

(83)

Let
\Pi:=\tau_{1}\Pi_{1}+\tau_{2}\Pi_{2}
. Then any admissible trajectory of
(\Delta y_{t},\Delta u_{t})
satisfies

\xi_{t}^{T}\Pi\xi_{t}\geq 0.

(84)

∎

In Lur’e form,
y_{t}=Cx_{t}+Du_{t}
so
\Delta y_{t}=C\Delta x_{t}+D\Delta u_{t}
. But recall
z_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
u_{t}\end{bmatrix}
and
\xi_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta y_{t}\\
\Delta u_{t}\end{bmatrix}
. This implies
\xi_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}C&D\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}z_{t}
. Let
J:=\begin{bmatrix}C&D\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}
so
\xi_{t}=Jz_{t}
. Consequently, the IQC takes the form

\xi_{t}^{T}J^{T}\Pi J\xi_{t}\geq 0.

(85)

Our Lyapunov strict contraction condition takes the form
z_{t}^{T}M^{\prime}z_{t}\leq 0
, i.e., as a certificate of uniform stability, we show that
z_{t}^{T}M^{\prime}z_{t}\leq 0
for all trajectories that satisfy
\xi_{t}^{T}J^{T}\Pi J\xi_{t}\geq 0
, i.e., if
\xi_{t}^{T}J^{T}\Pi J\xi_{t}\geq 0
, then
z_{t}^{T}M^{\prime}z_{t}\leq 0
. This implication holds if the
S-lemma
holds. The original result is due to
12
.

Theorem 18

(S-Lemma, Lur’e/IQC Formulation)
.

Let
Q:=J^{T}\Pi J
. If there exist
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\geq 0
such that

M^{\prime}+Q\preceq 0
,

then
z_{t}^{T}Qz_{t}\geq 0
implies
z_{t}^{T}M^{\prime}z_{t}\leq 0
for all
z_{t}
.

With the S-Lemma, we can now state the SDP:

find

\displaystyle P\succ 0,\lambda>0,\tau_{1}\geq 0,\tau_{2}\geq 0

(86)

s.t.

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}A^{T}PA-P+\lambda I&A^{T}PB\\
B^{T}PA&B^{T}PB\end{bmatrix}+J^{T}(\tau_{1}\Pi_{1}+\tau_{2}\Pi_{2})J\preceq 0

(87)

In the case of smooth quadratic NAG,
\Pi_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}-\gamma I&\frac{1}{2}I\\
\frac{1}{2}I&0\end{bmatrix}
,
\Pi_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2}I\\
\frac{1}{2}I&-\frac{1}{\beta}I\end{bmatrix}
, and
J=\begin{bmatrix}C&D\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}
.

6
Discussion

We have shown how Lyapunov functions can be extended from convergence to stability analysis of first-order optimization algorithms, thus permitting the case-by-case iterate uniform stability arguments made by
7
for stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to be extended to accelerated optimizers and any other first-order optimizer with higher-order dynamics expressible as a Lur’e system. Instantiating this framework for Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG), we derived an
O(\frac{4G^{2}k^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\beta\sqrt{n}}[\sqrt{1-(1-\rho)^{T}}])
uniform stability bound for smooth quadratic NAG, roughly matching the uniform stability bound for NAG derived by
6
in the same regime. Moreover, we show how our Lyapunov construction for deriving uniform stability bounds for first-order (accelerated) optimizers lends itself to an IQC formulation that allows for the automation of deriving such uniform stability bounds via semi-definite programming (SDP).

Since the Lypapunov-IQC framework detailed here lends to numerical verification of algorithmic stability, a clear research direction is work towards actual software implementation of such SDP’s (e.g., with integration in to CVPXY, MOSEK, etc.). Moreover, another clear future research direction is to extend such analysis to the smooth general convex case, or even the non-convex setting. However, as
1
note, this is complicated by not having a fixed
H_{t}
over the execution of the algorithm over each of
S
and
S^{\prime}
.

Appendix A
Uniform Stability of Smooth Quadratic SGD

7
originally proved the uniform stability of stochastic gradient descent (SGD) in the smooth quadratic regime. We present the proof here for reference as follows:

Theorem 19
.

Assume that the loss
\ell(w,z)
is
\gamma
-strongly convex and
\beta
-smooth for all
z
. Run SGD with constant
\eta\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
for
T
iterations. Then SGD is uniformly stable with

\epsilon\leq\frac{2G^{2}}{\gamma n}
.

Proof.

Let
S
and
S^{\prime}
be two samples of size
n
that differ only at a single point. We want to show that SGD is
\epsilon
-uniformly stable, i.e., by definition,

\text{sup}_{z}\mathbb{E}[\ell(A(S),z)-\ell(A(S^{\prime}),z)]\leq\epsilon

for arbitrary point
z
. Let SGD run over coupled indices over
S
and
S^{\prime}
, where
S=(z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{n})
and
S^{\prime}=(z_{1}^{\prime},z_{2}^{\prime},...,z_{n}^{\prime})
. We denote the index of the differing data point as
j\in\{1,2,...,n\}
, i.e.,
z_{j}\neq z_{j}^{\prime}
(so
z_{i}=z_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i\neq j
).

Recall the update rule for SGD for some arbitrary parameter
x_{t}
, i.e.,

x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\eta\nabla f(x_{t})
,

where
\eta
is the learning rate/step size and
\nabla f(x_{t})
is the gradient at
x_{t}
. Run SGD over both
S
and
S^{\prime}
. In doing so, we obtain two separate update rules:

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\nabla f(w_{t}),

(88)

w^{\prime}_{t+1}=w^{\prime}_{t}-\eta\nabla f(w^{\prime}_{t}),

(89)

We now define an iterate divergence term,

\delta_{t}=\mathbb{E}[||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||].

(90)

By the definition of uniform stability, SGD is uniformly stable iff
\delta_{t}=O(\frac{1}{n})
. Note that there are two possible cases, (1) where the differing point is
not
selected, i.e., where
i_{t}\neq j
, and (2) where the differing point
is
selected, i.e., where
i_{t}=j
. Thus, we use proof by cases.

{
i_{t}\neq j
}
Let
\{z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{n}\}
be uniformly distributed, so
\mathbb{P}[i_{t}\neq j]=1-\frac{1}{n}
. Then we obtain Eqns. 3 & 4 as the SGD update rules with the loss function and gradient values. Thus,
\delta_{t+1}=||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||=||(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t})-\eta(\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}}))||
. This implies
||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||^{2}=||(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t})-\eta(\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}}))||^{2}=||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||^{2}-2\eta\langle w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t},\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}}\rangle+\eta^{2}||\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}})||^{2}
, i.e.,

||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||^{2}=||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||^{2}-2\eta\langle w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t},\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}}\rangle+\eta^{2}||\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}})||^{2}.

(91)

We bound the middle term,
-2\eta\langle w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t},\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}}\rangle
, which we do via gradient monotonicity. Doing so yields

-2\eta\langle w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t},\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}})\rangle\leq-2\eta\gamma||w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}||^{2}.

(92)

We can now bound the last term,
\eta^{2}||\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}})||^{2}
, which we do via
\beta
-smoothness. Directly applying the definition of
\beta
-smoothness yields

\eta^{2}||\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}})||^{2}\leq\eta^{2}\beta^{2}||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||^{2}.

(93)

These inequalities thus yield

||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||^{2}\leq||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||^{2}-2\eta\gamma||w_{t}-w_{t}^{\prime}||^{2}+\eta^{2}\beta^{2}||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||^{2}.

(94)

Factoring
||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||^{2}
on the right-hand side of the inequality yields

||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||^{2}\leq(1-2\eta\gamma+\eta^{2}\beta^{2})||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||^{2}.

(95)

Recall that we select a constant learning rate such that
\eta\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
. This implies
\eta^{2}\beta^{2}\leq\eta\beta
. Moreover, we know
\gamma\leq\beta
, so
1-2\eta\gamma+\eta^{2}\beta^{2}\leq 1-\eta\gamma
. This implies

||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||^{2}\leq(1-\eta\gamma)||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||^{2}.

(96)

Then taking the square root yields

||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||\leq(1-\eta\gamma)||w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t}||.

(97)

Thus, in the case where
\{i_{t}\neq j\}
, the iterate divergence decreases from one iteration to the next at a factor of
1-\eta\gamma
.

\{i_{t}=j\}
We keep the same uniform distribution assumption for both
S
and
S^{\prime}
, where SGD is ran over a coupled set of indices over both
S
and
S^{\prime}
, so
\mathbb{P}[i_{t}=j]=\frac{1}{n}
. Recall
\delta_{t+1}=||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||=||(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t})-\eta(\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}}))||
. Applying the triangle inequality implies

||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||\leq||(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t})||+\eta(\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}}))||.

(98)

We can then apply the triangle inequality again to the
(\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})-\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}}))||
, which yields

||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||\leq||(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t})||+\eta||\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})||+\eta||\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}}))||.

(99)

By boundedness of the gradient, we can infer
||\nabla\ell(w_{t},z_{i_{t}})||\leq G
and
||\nabla\ell(w^{\prime}_{t},z^{\prime}_{i_{t}}))||\leq G
, which implies

||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}||\leq||(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t})||+\eta G+\eta G=||(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t})||+2\eta G.

(100)

Let
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{t}]=||(w_{t}-w^{\prime}_{t})||
. Taking expectation over (15) yields

\mathbb{E}[||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}|||i_{t}=j]\leq\delta_{t}+2\eta G.

(101)

Moreover, we let
\delta_{t+1}=\mathbb{E}[||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}|||i_{t}=j]
. Thus, for
i_{t}=j
,

\delta_{t+1}\leq\delta_{t}+2\eta G.

(102)

Recall the law of total expectation for a discrete random variable
X
with probability
P(A)
for event
A
, where
A^{c}
is the complement of
A
with
P(X^{c})=P(A^{c})
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[X]=P(A)\mathbb{E}[X|A]+P(A^{c})\mathbb{E}[X|A^{c}]
. Here,
A
is the event where
i_{t}\neq j
, so
A^{c}
is the event where
i_{t}=j
. Thus, applying the law of total expectation yields

\delta_{t+1}=(1-\frac{1}{n})\mathbb{E}[||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}|||i_{t}\neq j]+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}|||i_{t}=j].

(103)

We derived
\mathbb{E}[||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}|||i_{t}\neq j]\leq(1-\eta\gamma)\delta_{t}
and
\mathbb{E}[||w_{t+1}-w^{\prime}_{t+1}|||i_{t}=j]\leq\delta_{t}+2\eta G
. Substituting this yields

\delta_{t+1}\leq(1-\frac{1}{n})(1-\eta\gamma)\delta_{t}+(\frac{1}{n})(\delta_{t}+2\eta G).

(104)

Expansion of the right-hand side yields

\delta_{t+1}\leq(1-\eta\gamma-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\eta\gamma}{n})\delta_{t}+(\frac{1}{n})(\delta_{t}+2\eta G),

(105)

\delta_{t+1}\leq(1-\eta\gamma-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\eta\gamma}{n})\delta_{t}+\frac{1}{n}\delta_{t}+\frac{2\eta G}{n},

(106)

\delta_{t+1}\leq\delta_{t}(1-n\gamma-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\eta\gamma}{n}+\frac{1}{n})+\frac{2\eta G}{n},

(107)

\delta_{t+1}\leq(1-\eta\gamma)\delta_{t}+\frac{2\eta G}{n}.

(108)

This inequality expresses a linear recurrence of the form
\delta_{t}\leq a\delta_{t}+b
with
\delta_{0}=0
, where
a=1-\eta\gamma
and
b=\frac{2\eta G}{n}
. Expressed as a summation for
\delta_{T}
, this linear recurrence is

\delta_{T}\leq a^{T}\delta_{0}+b\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}a^{k}.

(109)

\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}a^{k}
is a geometric series, so
\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}a^{k}=\frac{1-a^{T}}{1-a}
. Moreover, since
0<a<1
,
a^{T}\rightarrow 0
as
T\rightarrow\infty
. This implies

\delta_{T}\leq b(\frac{1}{1-a}).

(110)

Recall
a=1-\eta\gamma
and
b=\frac{2\eta G}{n}
. This implies

\delta_{T}\leq\frac{2\eta G}{n}(\frac{1}{\eta\gamma})=\frac{2G}{\gamma n}.

(111)

Since the loss function is
G
-Lipschitz, we know
|\ell(w,z)-\ell(w^{\prime},z)|\leq G||w-w^{\prime}||
. Thus,
|\ell(w,z)_{T}-\ell(w^{\prime},z)_{T}|\leq G\delta_{T}\leq G(\frac{2G}{\gamma n})=\frac{2G^{2}}{\gamma n}
. Thus,
\delta_{T}\leq\frac{2G^{2}}{\gamma n}
. Moreover, since
\delta_{T}\leq\epsilon=O(\frac{1}{n})
, it follows that SGD under
\gamma
-strong convexity and
\beta
-smoothness, with constant
\eta\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
ran over
T
iterations, is uniformly stable.
∎
