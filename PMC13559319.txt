Glycyrrhizin alleviates Mycoplasma pneumoniae-induced acute lung injury in mice by regulating ferroptosis via the GPX4/ACSL4 axis
Authors: Yingjie Hu, Jiaxing Xu, Xiaowen Chen, Kangkang Zhang, Sixiang Tang, Yinghui Peng, Yuneng Lin, Zhanhang Huang, Wei Xia, Dehui Chen, Shangzhi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is a major cause of community-acquired pneumonia in children and can induce severe acute lung injury (ALI). Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, remains poorly understood in MP-induced ALI. Glycyrrhizin possesses anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, but its potential to alleviate MP-associated ALI via ferroptosis modulation has not yet been established. This study investigated the role of ferroptosis in MP-induced ALI and evaluated the therapeutic efficacy and underlying molecular mechanisms of glycyrrhizin in a murine model.MethodsForty specific pathogen-free male BALB/c (Bagg albino substrain c) mice (4–5 weeks old, 20±2 g) were randomly assigned to five experimental groups (n=8 per group): control, MP-low, MP-high, MP-high + ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, a specific ferroptosis inhibitor), and MP-high + glycyrrhizin. The MP infection model was established via intranasal instillation for three consecutive days. On day 7 post-instillation, lung histopathology, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) inflammatory profiles, pro-inflammatory cytokines [interleukin (IL)-6, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-1β, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ)], oxidative stress markers [malondialdehyde (MDA), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), reduced glutathione to oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG) ratio], tissue iron content, and ferroptosis-regulatory proteins (GPX4, ACSL4, HMGB1) were systematically evaluated.ResultsThe MP-low group induced only mild pulmonary changes with no statistically significant alterations in inflammatory or ferroptotic biomarkers compared to the control group (P>0.05). Conversely, the MP-high group caused severe ALI, characterized by markedly elevated BALF inflammatory cell infiltration and cytokine secretion (2.3- to 3.8-fold), increased tissue concentrations of MDA, 4-HNE, and ferrous iron (Fe2+; 1.8- to 2.5-fold), a 61% reduction in the GSH/GSSG ratio, a 62% downregulation of GPX4, and a 2.1-fold upregulation of ACSL4 (all P<0.05 versus the control group). Glycyrrhizin intervention significantly attenuated these pathological changes: the histopathological lung injury score decreased by 54%, pro-inflammatory cytokine levels fell by 42% to 67%, lipid peroxidation markers decreased by 45% to 52%, the GSH/GSSG ratio increased 1.6-fold, GPX4 expression was rescued 1.7-fold, and ACSL4 expression was suppressed by 58% (all P<0.05 versus the vehicle-treated MP-high group). Crucially, the tissue-protective and anti-ferroptotic effects of glycyrrhizin were comparable to those of the positive control, Fer-1 (P>0.05).ConclusionsFerroptosis serves as a substantial pathological driver of MP-induced ALI. Glycyrrhizin effectively mitigates severe lung injury by inhibiting pulmonary ferroptosis through the modulation of the GPX4/ACSL4 axis and by suppressing secondary inflammatory cascades. These findings provide a novel mechanistic insight and an experimental therapeutic rationale for treating severe MP-associated respiratory complications in pediatric clinical settings.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Our study demonstrates that ferroptosis is a critical pathological driver of Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP)-induced acute lung injury (ALI) in mice. High-titer MP infection downregulates the ferroptosis suppressor GPX4 and upregulates the pro-ferroptotic enzyme ACSL4 in lung tissues, leading to ferrous iron overload, excessive lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic alveolar epithelial cell death, which further drives secondary inflammatory cascade amplification and aggravates pulmonary tissue damage. Glycyrrhizin effectively alleviates MP-induced severe ALI by inhibiting pulmonary ferroptosis via modulation of the GPX4/ACSL4 axis, and simultaneously exerts direct anti-inflammatory activity by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. Its tissue-protective and anti-ferroptotic efficacy is comparable to that of the specific ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1, supporting ferroptosis inhibition as the primary mechanism underlying its pulmonary protective effect. These findings offer novel mechanistic insight into MP-induced ALI pathogenesis and provide experimental support for repurposing glycyrrhizin as a potential therapeutic agent for severe pediatric MP pneumonia.

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is a leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia in pediatric populations, often precipitating severe intrapulmonary and extrapulmonary complications. Among these, acute lung injury (ALI) represents one of the most critical sequelae of severe M. pneumoniae pneumonia (SMPP) (1). The pathological hallmarks of MP-induced ALI include the disruption of the alveolar epithelial and capillary endothelial barriers, robust inflammatory cell infiltration, unchecked pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and profound oxidative stress imbalance. In severe cases, this cascade can rapidly progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), substantially escalating mortality rates and clinical burdens within pediatric intensive care units (2,3). Despite extensive investigation into the immunopathological mechanisms of MP infection over recent years, the precise molecular pathways driving ALI induction—particularly the exact modalities of programmed cell death involved—remain to be fully elucidated (4).

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated, iron-dependent cell death characterized by the lethal accumulation of intracellular iron and lipid peroxides (5). Accumulating evidence indicates that ferroptosis plays a pivotal role in various acute organ injuries, including ischemia-reperfusion injury and sepsis-related ALI (6,7). Nonetheless, its specific involvement in MP-associated ALI remains poorly understood. While ferroptosis-modulating strategies have been explored in other pulmonary injury contexts including natural bioactive compounds that attenuate lipopolysaccharide-induced ALI via targeting ferroptotic signaling pathways (8), and Netrin-1 that mediates alveolar type 2 cell survival and neonatal lung injury repair through the UNC5B receptor axis (9) systematic quantification of ferroptosis biomarkers and functional validation with specific ferroptosis inhibitors have not been established in the setting of MP-induced lung injury. Given the immature immune systems and relatively constrained antioxidant capacities characteristic of pediatric patients, ferroptosis may possess heightened pathophysiological relevance in lung injury secondary to MP infection (10).

Glycyrrhizin, the primary bioactive triterpenoid saponin extracted from licorice root (Glycyrrhiza glabra), exhibits a diverse range of pharmacological properties, including potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory activities (11). Previous studies have demonstrated that glycyrrhizin exerts a protective effect in adult models of ALI by suppressing the release of high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), downregulating the nuclear factor-kappaB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, and mitigating oxidative damage (12,13). Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that glycyrrhizin can inhibit ferroptosis during myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury by modulating key ferroptosis regulators, such as glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) (14). However, whether glycyrrhizin alleviates MP-induced ALI by targeting the ferroptosis pathway has not yet been reported. This study aims to systematically investigate the pathophysiological role of ferroptosis in MP-induced ALI using a murine infection model, evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of glycyrrhizin against pulmonary damage, and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms with a focus on the GPX4/ACSL4 regulatory axis. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1480/rc).

Methods

Experimental animals

Forty specific-pathogen-free (SPF) male BALB/c (Bagg albino substrain c) mice, aged 4 to 5 weeks and weighing 20±2 g, were obtained from Guangzhou Ruige Biotechnology Co., Ltd. [Experimental Animal Use License No. SYXK (Yue) 2023-0183]. The mice were acclimated for 3 days prior to experimentation in an SPF facility maintained under controlled environmental conditions: temperature of 23±2 degrees Celsius, relative humidity of 40% to 70%, and a strict 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on at 08:00 and off at 20:00). All animal experiments were performed under a project license (No. K2025-01-218-197) granted by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Guangdong Academy of Sciences, in compliance with the 3Rs principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) for the care and use of animals.

Animal grouping and treatment

Following the 3-day acclimation period, the mice were allocated into five experimental groups (n=8 per group) using a computer-generated random number table:

Control group: mice received intranasal instillation of an equal volume of sterile mycoplasma culture medium, supplemented with daily intragastric administration of normal saline.

Control group: mice received intranasal instillation of an equal volume of sterile mycoplasma culture medium, supplemented with daily intragastric administration of normal saline.

MP-low group: mice received intranasal instillation of 100 microliters of 1×107 CCU/mL M. pneumoniae suspension for 3 consecutive days, supplemented with daily intragastric administration of normal saline.

MP-low group: mice received intranasal instillation of 100 microliters of 1×107 CCU/mL M. pneumoniae suspension for 3 consecutive days, supplemented with daily intragastric administration of normal saline.

MP-high group: mice received intranasal instillation of 100 microliters of 1×1010 CCU/mL M. pneumoniae suspension for 3 consecutive days, supplemented with daily intragastric administration of normal saline.

MP-high group: mice received intranasal instillation of 100 microliters of 1×1010 CCU/mL M. pneumoniae suspension for 3 consecutive days, supplemented with daily intragastric administration of normal saline.

MP-high + ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) group: mice received a tail vein injection of the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (5 mg/kg, dissolved in 1% dimethyl sulfoxide) 1 hour prior to model induction, with all subsequent treatments identical to the MP-high group.

MP-high + ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) group: mice received a tail vein injection of the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (5 mg/kg, dissolved in 1% dimethyl sulfoxide) 1 hour prior to model induction, with all subsequent treatments identical to the MP-high group.

MP-high + glycyrrhizin group: mice received an initial intragastric dose of glycyrrhizin solution (50 mg/kg, prepared at 5 mg/mL in normal saline) 2 hours post-intranasal MP instillation, followed by daily gavage treatments until the conclusion of the study.

MP-high + glycyrrhizin group: mice received an initial intragastric dose of glycyrrhizin solution (50 mg/kg, prepared at 5 mg/mL in normal saline) 2 hours post-intranasal MP instillation, followed by daily gavage treatments until the conclusion of the study.

The clinical status of the mice was monitored hourly following model induction, and predefined humane endpoints were strictly enforced. Animals were immediately euthanized via cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia induced with 5% and maintained with 2% isoflurane if they displayed any of the following criteria: body weight loss exceeding 20% of their baseline weight, severe dyspnea, cyanosis or apnea, loss of righting reflex, inability to access food or water independently for more than 24 hours, severe hemorrhage, ulceration, limb necrosis, or a drop in core rectal temperature below 34 degrees Celsius that failed to respond to external heat support. Mice exhibiting a clinical health status score of 3 received supportive care consisting of a subcutaneous injection of 0.5 mL warm normal saline and external warming. If clinical scores failed to improve or continued to deteriorate within 2 hours post-intervention, immediate euthanasia was performed. On day 7 post-modeling, all surviving mice were humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation under deep isoflurane anesthesia to ensure a rapid, painless, and distress-free termination. No unexpected or accidental animal deaths occurred during the study. All mice underwent tissue harvesting on day 7 following a 4-hour period of food and water deprivation.

MP strain culture and model establishment

The M. pneumoniae reference strain (ATCC 15531) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured in PPLO liquid medium at 37 degrees Celsius in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The culture was continuously passaged upon shifting from red to yellow, indicating a change in pH, and the third passage was utilized for all experimental procedures. The concentration of M. pneumoniae was determined via the color-changing unit (CCU) method, and serial dilutions were performed to prepare suspensions at final concentrations of 1×107 and 1×1010 CCU/mL.

The two inoculation dosages were selected based on well-characterized BALB/c mouse models of pulmonary MP infection established in prior published studies. The foundational study by Hardy et al. (15) first demonstrated a positive correlation between instillation dose, intrapulmonary bacterial colonization load, and lung injury severity: low-dose inoculation elicits only mild interstitial inflammatory infiltration without overt acute parenchymal damage, whereas high-dose inoculation induces pronounced pneumonia with stable and reproducible pathological features. This dose-dependent pathogenic pattern has been further corroborated by subsequent studies adopting repeated intranasal instillation regimens consistent with our 3-day protocol (15,16).

To establish the infection model, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of avertin (30 microliters/g) and positioned with their heads tilted backward at an angle of 40 degrees. A 100-microliter aliquot of the M. pneumoniae suspension was slowly instilled into both nostrils, and the animals were returned to their cages after confirming that the suspension had entered the lower respiratory tract via spontaneous respiration. Control mice received an equal volume of sterile mycoplasma culture medium via intranasal instillation.

Detection indicators and methods

Pulmonary computed tomography (CT) examination

On day 7 post-modeling, mice were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation, and chest micro-CT scans were performed using a SkyScan 1176 scanner (Bruker, Belgium). The acquisition parameters were configured as follows: tube voltage of 90 kV, tube current of 80 microamperes, and slice thickness of 72 micrometers.

Lung histopathological observation and scoring

The upper lobe of the right lung was excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours. The fixed tissues were routinely dehydrated, cleared, embedded in paraffin wax, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 micrometers for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Semi-quantitative evaluation of lung injury was performed according to the Smith scoring method, assessing five distinct pathological features on a scale of 0 to 4: pulmonary edema, alveolar and interstitial inflammation, hemorrhage, atelectasis, and hyaline membrane formation. Five random fields of view per mouse were examined under high-power magnification (200×), and the mean score was calculated to determine the total lung injury score.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) collection and quantification

Following anesthesia, the trachea was surgically isolated, and a 22G indwelling catheter was inserted and secured. The right lung was slowly lavaged twice with 0.8 mL of ice-cold 0.9% normal saline, allowing a retention time of 1 minute prior to aspiration. The average fluid recovery rate ranged between 75% and 88%. The collected BALF was centrifuged at 1,200 g for 10 minutes at 4 degrees Celsius, and the resulting supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80 degrees Celsius for downstream analyses. The cell pellet was resuspended, and a 10-microliter aliquot was subjected to Wright-Giemsa staining to determine the total cell count and the percentage of neutrophils.

The total protein concentration in the BALF supernatant was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, P0010). The levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6; ABclonal, Wuhan, China, RK00008), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α; ABclonal, RK00027), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β; ABclonal, RK00006), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ; ABclonal, RK00019) were determined using commercial ELISA kits in strict accordance with the manufacturer instructions.

Lung wet-to-dry (W/D) ratio measurement

The intact left lung was taken, blotted dry of surface moisture, and weighed (wet weight). It was then dried in an oven at 65 ℃ for 72 h to constant weight, and the dry weight was recorded. The W/D ratio was calculated to evaluate the degree of pulmonary edema.

Assessment of oxidative stress biomarkers and iron content

Approximately 50 mg of the middle lobe of the right lung was homogenized to prepare a 10% tissue homogenate. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 degrees Celsius, and the supernatant was collected. Commercial assay kits were utilized to quantify the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan, M496), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE; Elabscience Biotechnology, Wuhan, China, E-EL-0128), reduced glutathione-to-oxidized glutathione ratio (GSH/GSSG; Dojindo, G263), and ferrous iron (Fe2+) content (Leagene Biotechnology, Beijing, China, TC1015).

Western blot analysis

Approximately 100 mg of the lower lobe of the right lung was homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) using a tissue grinder. Homogonezation was performed three times for 30 seconds each with 1-minute intervals, followed by lysis on ice for 20 minutes. After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 20 minutes at 4 degrees Celsius, the supernatant was isolated, and protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA method. Equal amounts of protein (30 micrograms per lane) were resolved via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA, IPVH00010).

The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 hour and incubated overnight at 4 degrees Celsius with primary antibodies against GPX4 (Abcam plc, Cambridge, UK, ab125066, 1:1,000), ACSL4 (Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China, 15817-1-AP, 1:1,000), HMGB1 (CST, Danvers, MA, USA, 3935S, 1:1,000), receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE; CST, 6996S, 1:1,000), and beta-actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig, 1:5,000). After three washes with Tris Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000) at room temperature for 1 hour. Protein bands were visualized using an electrochemiluminescence (ECL) chemiluminescence substrate kit, and the densitometric gray values were analyzed using ImageJ 2.0 software. The relative expression of target proteins was normalized to beta-actin as the internal control.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.2). Quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (mean ± SEM). Multi-group comparisons were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey post hoc tests. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the unpaired Student t-test. A P value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The in vivo experiment was conducted as a single independent study, whereas all downstream biochemical and molecular assays were performed with at least three independent replicates.

Results

MP infection aggravates ALI and ferroptosis in a dose-dependent manner

Compared with the control group, the low-dose MP group exhibited only mild pulmonary edema and sparse inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, the high-dose MP group demonstrated severe disruption of the pulmonary architecture, characterized by thickened alveolar septa, pronounced edema, and dense infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages, accompanied by alveolar hemorrhage and atelectasis (Figure 1A). Quantitative analysis revealed that the lung histopathological injury score, lung W/D weight ratio, and pulmonary consolidation area increased significantly in a dose-dependent manner with increasing MP titers (Figure 1B-1D).

Analysis of BALF showed that the total cell count, neutrophil count, and total protein concentration in the high-dose MP group were elevated 3.2-, 4.5-, and 2.8-fold, respectively, relative to the control group (all P<0.001, Figure 1E). Concurrently, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, increased 2.3- to 3.8-fold in response to high-titer MP infection (all P<0.01, Figure 2A).

Assessment of ferroptotic biomarkers revealed that the concentrations of the lipid peroxidation products 4-HNE and MDA, as well as Fe2+ content, in the lung tissues of the high-dose MP group were increased 2.5-, 2.1-, and 1.8-fold, respectively (all P<0.001, Figure 2B-2D). Conversely, the antioxidant GSH/GSSG ratio was reduced by 61% (P<0.001, Figure 2E). Western blot analysis demonstrated that the expression of the key ferroptosis suppressor protein GPX4 was downregulated by 62%, whereas the pro-ferroptotic enzyme ACSL4 was upregulated 2.1-fold in the high-dose MP group (all P<0.001, Figure 2F). Furthermore, the expression levels of HMGB1 and its receptor RAGE were markedly increased following high-titer MP exposure (P<0.001, Figure 2F). This graded pathological phenotype is underpinned by dose-dependent differences in intrapulmonary MP colonization burden. Previous quantitative microbiological studies employing equivalent infection protocols have consistently shown that on day 7 post-inoculation, pulmonary MP titers reach 107–108 CCU per gram of lung tissue in the high-dose group, compared with approximately 104 CCU/g in the low-dose group—a nearly 1,000-fold difference in pathogen load (15,16). This substantial disparity in intrapulmonary microbial burden directly drives the stepwise escalation of inflammatory cascade activation and ferroptotic tissue damage observed in the present study. Collectively, these findings indicate that MP infection triggers severe pulmonary inflammation and ferroptosis in a dose-dependent manner. Based on these results, an MP suspension concentration of 1×1010 CCU/mL was selected to establish the ALI model in subsequent experiments.

Glycyrrhizin treatment alleviates MP-induced ALI

Compared with the vehicle-treated high-dose MP group, mice receiving glycyrrhizin intervention demonstrated a significantly reduced pulmonary consolidation area, preserved alveolar architecture, attenuated interstitial edema, and diminished inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 3A). Specifically, glycyrrhizin administration led to a 54% reduction in the lung histopathological injury score and a 32% decrease in the lung W/D ratio (both P<0.001, Figure 3B,3C).

In line with the histological improvements, BALF analysis indicated that total cell counts, neutrophil counts, and total protein concentration in the glycyrrhizin group were reduced by 48%, 57%, and 42%, respectively, compared with the untreated high-dose MP cohort (all P<0.01, Figure 3D). Furthermore, the concentrations of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ decreased by 42% to 67% following glycyrrhizin intervention (all P<0.05, Figure 3E). These results indicate that glycyrrhizin effectively mitigates MP-induced histopathological lung damage and inflammatory responses in vivo.

Glycyrrhizin inhibits MP-induced oxidative stress and pulmonary ferroptosis

Oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation-driven ferroptosis represent cardinal pathological features of MP-induced ALI. Compared with the vehicle-treated high-dose MP group, the tissue levels of 4-HNE and MDA in the glycyrrhizin intervention group were decreased by 52% and 45%, respectively (both P<0.001, Figure 4A,4B). Moreover, glycyrrhizin administration resulted in a 41% reduction in Fe2+ accumulation (P<0.001, Figure 4C) and a corresponding 1.6-fold increase in the GSH/GSSG ratio (P<0.01, Figure 4D).

At the molecular level, Western blot analysis revealed that glycyrrhizin treatment significantly rescued GPX4 protein expression, yielding a 1.7-fold increase relative to the untreated model group (P<0.001). This was accompanied by a concurrent 58% downregulation of the pro-ferroptotic protein ACSL4 (P<0.001, Figure 4E). These data strongly suggest that glycyrrhizin protects against MP-induced ALI by restoring oxidative balance and suppressing the ferroptotic pathway.

The specific ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 phenocopies the protective effects of glycyrrhizin

To functionally validate the causal role of ferroptosis in MP-induced ALI and verify that suppression of ferroptosis is a primary mechanism underlying the lung-protective effect of glycyrrhizin, the specific ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 was employed as a positive control. As an established functional benchmark for verifying ferroptosis dependency, Fer-1 selectively blocks lipid peroxidation-driven ferroptotic cell death without interfering with general non-specific oxidative stress or other regulated cell death modalities. Prophylactic treatment with Fer-1 markedly attenuated MP-induced histopathological lung injury, reduced inflammatory cell counts and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in BALF, inhibited lipid peroxidation, and prevented ferrous iron accumulation. It also upregulated GPX4 expression and downregulated ACSL4 expression (Figure 5). The robust phenotypic rescue achieved by Fer-1 directly confirms that the observed lung injury and lipid peroxidation phenotype are ferroptosis-dependent rather than mediated by non-specific oxidative stress.

Quantitative comparative analysis of BALF inflammatory profiles was further conducted to delineate the contribution of ferroptosis to the inflammatory cascade. Specific blockade of ferroptosis by Fer-1 reduced the concentrations of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β and IFN-γ by 38–55% relative to the vehicle-treated MP-high group, indicating that a substantial fraction of the inflammatory response in MP-induced ALI is driven by ferroptotic cell death and subsequent secondary inflammation. In parallel, glycyrrhizin intervention decreased the aforementioned pro-inflammatory cytokines by 42–67%, exhibiting a moderately stronger anti-inflammatory efficacy. The overlapping magnitude of cytokine reduction between the two groups supports that ferroptosis inhibition accounts for a major component of glycyrrhizin’s anti-inflammatory action, while the additional cytokine-lowering effect beyond Fer-1’s efficacy likely arises from the direct immunomodulatory activity of glycyrrhizin independent of ferroptosis suppression.

Direct intergroup comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences between the glycyrrhizin treatment group and the Fer-1 group in lung histopathological scores, BALF inflammatory profiles, or core ferroptotic markers, including GPX4, ACSL4, Fe2+, and MDA (all P>0.05, Figure 5). These results demonstrate that the therapeutic efficacy of glycyrrhizin in alleviating MP-induced ALI is comparable to that of a dedicated ferroptosis inhibitor, further supporting that glycyrrhizin exerts pulmonary protection primarily via ferroptosis inhibition.

Discussion

In this study, we established murine models of ALI induced by varying titers of MP to systematically investigate the pathological involvement of ferroptosis. Our findings demonstrate that ferroptosis is critically involved in the pathogenesis of MP-associated ALI, and we show that glycyrrhizin significantly mitigates this injury. Specifically, glycyrrhizin suppresses ferroptosis by modulating the GPX4/ACSL4 axis while concurrently dampening the pulmonary inflammatory response, thereby exerting a robust protective effect. These insights offer a promising therapeutic direction and provide a mechanistic framework for managing SMPP in pediatric contexts.

The execution of ferroptosis fundamentally depends on the iron-mediated accumulation of lethal lipid peroxides. GPX4 serves as the primary and indispensable antioxidant enzyme responsible for clearing lipid peroxides, and its functional inactivation is universally recognized as a defining hallmark of ferroptosis execution (5,17). Conversely, ACSL4 dictates cellular ferroptosis sensitivity by shaping the intracellular polyunsaturated fatty acid pool and generating susceptible lipid peroxidation substrates, acting as a core pro-ferroptotic regulatory node (18). In the present study, high-titer MP infection markedly downregulated GPX4 expression and upregulated ACSL4 expression in murine lung tissue. This was accompanied by a massive accumulation of Fe2+ and lipid peroxidation products, suggesting that MP infection drives lung epithelial cell ferroptosis by disrupting cellular iron homeostasis and lipid metabolism. This phenotype aligns with observations previously reported in models of sepsis-induced ALI (6). Notably, previous investigations have only addressed related mechanisms in non-MP pulmonary injury models: one study demonstrated the anti-ferroptotic efficacy of a natural bioactive compound in lipopolysaccharide-induced lung injury (8), and another identified the alveolar epithelial protective function of the Netrin-1/UNC5B axis in neonatal pulmonary disease (9). However, neither focused on MP-induced pulmonary pathology nor conducted systematic quantification of core ferroptotic biomarkers. Our study addresses this critical gap by providing comprehensive quantification of canonical ferroptosis hallmarks and includes parallel validation using the specific ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1, thereby definitively establishing the pathophysiological role of ferroptosis in MP-induced ALI.

Glycyrrhizin is a widely utilized clinical anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective agent with an established safety profile in pediatric medicine. We observed that glycyrrhizin intervention effectively rescued MP-induced GPX4 downregulation, suppressed ACSL4 upregulation, and substantially reduced intracellular iron and lipid peroxide accumulation. Remarkably, these therapeutic effects were comparable to those achieved with Fer-1, confirming that the inhibition of ferroptosis is a principal mechanism underlying the pulmonary protection conferred by glycyrrhizin. Previous literature indicates that glycyrrhizin can alleviate oxidative injury by activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway (13). Because Nrf2 directly upregulates GPX4 at the transcriptional level, this pathway may represent the upstream mechanism through which glycyrrhizin modulates ferroptosis, a hypothesis that warrants further validation in future studies.

Intriguingly, our study also demonstrated that glycyrrhizin significantly suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1 beta, and TNF-α, in BALF, highlighting its dual anti-inflammatory and anti-ferroptotic activities. A complex, bidirectional regulatory feedback loop exists between ferroptosis and inflammation. On one hand, ferroptotic cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), which activate the TLR4/NF-κB pathway to amplify the inflammatory cascade (19). On the other hand, pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α can accelerate ferroptosis by inducing lipid peroxidation and altering iron homeostasis (20).

By comparing the anti-inflammatory efficacies of Fer-1 and glycyrrhizin, we further dissected ferroptosis-dependent and -independent inflammatory regulatory pathways in this model. The observation that selective ferroptosis inhibition by Fer-1 reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels by 38–55% indicates that ferroptosis-mediated cell death and subsequent DAMP release serve as important drivers of pulmonary inflammation in MP-induced ALI. This aligns with the established concept that ferroptosis, as a form of lytic regulated cell death, promotes inflammatory responses by releasing intracellular DAMPs into the extracellular microenvironment (21). Consistent with previous findings in infectious ALI models, Fer-1-mediated ferroptosis blockade can substantially dampen neutrophil activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion by interrupting this lytic cell death-driven inflammatory cascade (6). Compared with Fer-1, glycyrrhizin exerted a more pronounced anti-inflammatory effect, which can be attributed to its dual mechanism of action: it not only suppresses ferroptosis and thereby reduces secondary inflammatory damage, but also directly inhibits pro-inflammatory signaling in a ferroptosis-independent manner. As a well-characterized HMGB1 inhibitor, glycyrrhizin directly binds to HMGB1 and suppresses its pro-inflammatory activity, blocking downstream NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades independent of ferroptosis regulation (12,22).

Our results demonstrate that glycyrrhizin simultaneously inhibits ferroptosis and HMGB1 release, suggesting that it exerts multi-target protection by disrupting this self-amplifying ferroptosis-HMGB1-inflammation vicious cycle. This is consistent with previous findings by Gu et al., which demonstrated that glycyrrhizin blocks the HMGB1/TLR9 pathway (12).

From a clinical translation perspective, MP-induced ALI in children is characterized by an acute onset, rapid progression, and a propensity for multisystem complications. Clinical management currently relies heavily on macrolide antibiotics and corticosteroids; however, the rising prevalence of drug-resistant strains and the systemic adverse effects of hormones present substantial clinical challenges (23). Our study demonstrates that glycyrrhizin remains highly effective even when administered post-infection, offering the dual advantages of anti-inflammatory and anti-ferroptotic actions. Compared with Fer-1, which solely targets ferroptosis, glycyrrhizin may provide a more comprehensive intervention for infectious pathologies, underscoring its strong potential for clinical translation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The BALB/c murine model, despite its widespread utility in preclinical pulmonary infection research, cannot fully recapitulate the immunological and physiological complexity of the pediatric human lung. Accordingly, further validation in humanized mouse models or human clinical specimens is required to support the translational relevance of the present findings.

Another notable constraint is that all mechanistic interrogations were conducted exclusively in the in vivo murine model, without complementary validation in an in vitro system of MP-challenged alveolar epithelial cells. Alveolar epithelial cells represent the primary cellular targets of MP infection and the core effectors mediating ferroptosis-driven pulmonary injury (24); a cell culture system would enable precise dissection of cell-autonomous regulatory mechanisms while eliminating confounding influences from the in vivo immune microenvironment, circulating inflammatory mediators, and systemic metabolic perturbations. At present, it remains unresolved whether glycyrrhizin modulates the GPX4/ACSL4 axis through direct action on alveolar epithelial cells, or via indirect effects secondary to the ameliorated pulmonary microenvironment and suppressed systemic inflammation. Defining this cell-autonomous causal relationship is critical to further strengthening the mechanistic rigor of our conclusions, as highlighted in prior studies on regulated cell death in infectious lung disease (25). Nevertheless, the in vivo murine model employed herein more faithfully recapitulates the full pathophysiological cascade of MP-induced ALI, including epithelial barrier disruption, immune cell infiltration, and inflammatory cascade amplification, thereby conferring robust pathophysiological relevance and translational value to our findings. The establishment of MP-infected alveolar epithelial cell models will be prioritized in our follow-up work to delineate the direct intracellular regulatory mechanisms of glycyrrhizin.

Regarding ferroptosis phenotypic confirmation, activation of ferroptosis in the present work was inferred primarily through quantification of biochemical biomarkers and Fer-1-mediated functional rescue experiments. Classic morphological gold-standard evidence, such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM) visualization of the characteristic subcellular alterations of ferroptosis, as well as high-precision detection of lipid reactive oxygen species and targeted lipidomic profiling of oxidized polyunsaturated fatty acids, were not included in the current experimental design. These assays would further improve the specificity of ferroptosis identification and more rigorously exclude potential interference from non-specific oxidative stress. Morphological validation and lipidomic characterization of ferroptosis will be prioritized in subsequent investigations to further consolidate the conclusions of this study.

In parallel, definitive functional validation of the GPX4/ACSL4 regulatory axis remains to be supplemented. Our current inference that this axis mediates the anti-ferroptotic effect of glycyrrhizin is derived primarily from the concordant changes in target protein expression and ferroptosis phenotype, supported by the consistent rescue effect of the ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Fer-1. However, to formally establish the functional necessity of the GPX4/ACSL4 axis in glycyrrhizin-mediated protection, targeted loss-of-function experiments are required—for instance, pharmacological inhibition of GPX4 with RSL3 or ACSL4 with triacsin C—to examine whether the protective efficacy of glycyrrhizin is abrogated upon disruption of this axis. Advancing the conclusion from expression correlation to mechanistic causality will substantially enhance the rigor of the study. Pharmacological and genetic validation of the GPX4/ACSL4 axis will constitute the core content of our follow-up mechanistic investigations.

Our investigation focused primarily on the core ferroptosis effector proteins GPX4 and ACSL4, while upstream regulatory factors such as SLC7A11 and Nrf2 were not examined, which limits the ability to fully delineate the complete signaling cascade.

Furthermore, a dose-response gradient for glycyrrhizin was not established in this study, such that the optimal minimum effective dose remains to be determined. The direct antimicrobial activity of glycyrrhizin against MP was also not evaluated, meaning a potential contribution from direct antibacterial effects cannot be completely ruled out.

From a clinical translational perspective, the current investigation is confined to the acute injury phase of MP infection (day 7 post-inoculation), and does not address long-term pulmonary sequelae including interstitial fibrosis, alveolar remodeling, and post-infectious bronchiolitis obliterans—well-documented persistent complications in children following SMPP (26). Emerging evidence indicates that ferroptosis of alveolar epithelial cells and subsequent aberrant tissue repair play pivotal roles in the initiation and progression of pulmonary fibrosis (27), implying that ferroptosis inhibition may confer sustained protection against chronic lung remodeling beyond the acute phase. Accordingly, systematic investigation into the long-term efficacy of glycyrrhizin against post-infectious pulmonary fibrosis and alveolar architectural remodeling represents a high-priority direction for future research, which would further expand the clinical application value of this therapeutic strategy.

In summary, this study highlights the critical role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of MP-induced ALI. We demonstrate for the first time that glycyrrhizin suppresses ferroptosis and mitigates the pulmonary inflammatory response by modulating the GPX4/ACSL4 axis. These findings offer a fresh perspective on the molecular mechanisms driving MP-associated ALI and provide an experimental foundation supporting the clinical translation of glycyrrhizin in pediatric respiratory medicine.

Conclusions

Ferroptosis serves as a substantial pathological driver of MP-induced ALI in mice. High-titer MP infection disrupts pulmonary iron homeostasis and lipid metabolism, triggering excessive lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic alveolar epithelial cell death, which further drives secondary inflammatory cascade amplification and aggravates tissue damage. Glycyrrhizin effectively mitigates MP-induced severe ALI by inhibiting pulmonary ferroptosis through modulation of the GPX4/ACSL4 regulatory axis, and concurrently exerts direct anti-inflammatory effects. Its tissue-protective and anti-ferroptotic efficacy is comparable to that of the specific ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1, supporting ferroptosis inhibition as the primary mechanism underlying its pulmonary protective effect.

These findings provide a novel mechanistic framework for understanding MP-associated ALI pathogenesis, and establish an experimental rationale for repurposing glycyrrhizin as a potential therapeutic strategy for severe MP pneumonia in pediatric populations.
