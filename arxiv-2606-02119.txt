How Hard Can It Be? Hardness-Aware Multi-Objective Unlearning
Jiangwei Chen, Xinyuan Niu, Rachael Hwee Ling Sim, Zhengyuan Liu, Nancy F. Chen, Bryan Kian Hsiang Low

Abstract
Machine unlearning aims to remove the influence of specific forget training data due to privacy, copyright or bias concerns while maintaining the model performance on the remaining retain data. Existing unlearning algorithms, such as optimizing a weighted combination of losses, have tried to achieve these objectives of improving forget quality and maintaining retain utility. However, they do not guarantee that these objectives can be improved by a specified extent for all forget and retain data. In this work, we address this limitation with a novel and theoretically-grounded approach from a constrained optimization perspective. Firstly, we identify that the hardness of reconciling both objectives can be quantified by the similarity between the forget data and the retain data. Next, we derive an unlearning algorithm (HAMU) with the overall goal of guaranteeing a specified improvement in forget quality while minimizing the retain utility cost/degradation by updating the model weights based on our hardness measure. Our hardness measure also informs users when retain utility degradation is unavoidable, i.e., both objectives cannot be improved simultaneously, and stopping should be considered. Our algorithm is applicable to non-convex models and is easily parallelizable, making it readily deployable in real-world scenarios. We empirically demonstrate HAMU’s superior performance over baselines on both image and text datasets using large models. Our code is available at https://github.com/aoi3142/HAMU .

Introduction

Machine unlearning
(
Cao & Yang 2015
)
presents an appealing paradigm to combat the data issues arising from the prevalent adoption of large
machine learning
(ML) models
(
Raza et al. 2025
;
Khan et al. 2025
)
,
such as privacy leakage
(
Pawelczyk et al. 2024
)
, copyright infringement
(
Eldan & Russinovich 2023
)
and harmful bias
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
.
Machine unlearning aims to fulfill both objectives:

(1)
improve
forget quality
by removing the influence of some forget data, from the ML models;
while
(2)
maintaining
retain utility
, the ML model performance on the remaining retain data.
Unlearning usually achieves these two objectives by increasing the loss on the forget data
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
or approximating the retrained model which minimizes the loss on the retain data
(
Neel et al. 2021
)
.

Most existing unlearning algorithms have neglected the possible conflict between both objectives.
To achieve
(1)
, unlearning may incur some
cost of forgetting
, which is the negative impact on the ML model performance (retain utility).
For example, it may lead to catastrophic forgetting where the unlearned model performs considerably worse than the retrained model
(
Nguyen et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2025
)
or more generally retain utility degradation where the ML model performance or knowledge of the remaining retain data worsens, sacrificing
(2)
.
We term this phenomenon of improving forget quality causing retain utility degradation as
collateral forgetting
.
Existing works have considered multi-objective unlearning
(
Jin et al. 2025
)
that attempts to simultaneously improve both objectives, however, it may not be feasible to do so for all possible forget and retain data.
An extreme and intuitive example is that collateral forgetting is unavoidable when the forget and retain data are identical.
This conflict oversight raises the following fundamental questions:

How much is the
cost of forgetting
(in terms of retain utility degradation) to improve forget quality by a specified extent?
How hard is it to reconcile both objectives in machine unlearning?

We answer the above questions by quantifying the hardness of unlearning,
\kappa
, as a model-dependent continuous measure that depends on the similarity between the forget and retain data.
Then, we establish a theoretical connection which shows that the cost of forgetting, in terms of retain utility degradation, generally increases with the hardness measure (Fig.
1
).
For example, the cost is the highest in the hardest scenario where the forget and retain data are identical.
Moreover, we identify a hardness threshold
\kappa_{2}
(Fig.
1
), whose exceedance informs the model owner prior to unlearning that
local collateral forgetting is unavoidable and small updates to improve forget quality will degrade retain utility (i.e., the cost of forgetting is positive).

Next, how can the model owner ensure that the forget quality improves by a specified extent and reduces the cost of forgetting, even for non-convex
1

Methods with theoretical guarantees usually require strong assumptions such as strongly-convex loss functions
(
Ullah et al. 2021

Allouah et al. 2025

ML models such as
neural networks
(NNs)?

At first glance, one solution would be to use a carefully tuned weighted combination of the forget and retain losses.
However, this simple solution does not allow the model owner to guarantee that the forget quality would increase by a specified extent
(
Kurmanji et al. 2023
;
Duan et al. 2025
)
.
In contrast, we formulate unlearning in each iteration as a constrained optimization problem where the goal is to minimize the cost of forgetting (the first-order approximation of the retain utility degradation) while constraining that the linear approximation of the forget quality must improve by the specified extent.
Our hardness-aware multi-objective unlearning (HAMU) algorithm decides on the unlearning weight update based on the hardness measure (Fig.

).
For harder scenarios, our HAMU algorithm uses the rectified update which incorporates the forget data’s gradients to guarantee forget quality improvement.
HAMU is efficient but applicable to non-convex models as we consider the first-order approximation of the loss function limited to a small local radius.
The linear and layer-wise independence nature of HAMU at each iteration also makes it scalable and parallelizable for use with
large language models
(LLMs).
We empirically show that HAMU outperforms other baselines in terms of unlearning performance and reduces the cost of forgetting through extensive experiments.

To summarize, our key contributions include:

\bullet

We introduce a new unlearning perspective — the goal is to improve an objective (e.g., forget quality) by a specified extent while optimizing the other objective (e.g., minimizing the cost of forgetting/retain utility degradation) (Sec.

\bullet

We quantify the hardness of unlearning and establish a
theoretical connection
between hardness and cost of forgetting (Sec.
4.1

4.2
) and when
collateral forgetting
occurs (Sec.
4.3
).

\bullet

We adapt HAMU to efficiently and effectively work for large, non-convex ML models (Sec.
5
).

\bullet

We demonstrate HAMU consistently outperforms baselines across datasets and model architectures through extensive experiments (Sec.
6

2
Multi-Objective Machine Unlearning

We consider a supervised learning task on the dataset
D\triangleq\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
y_{i}\in[K]\triangleq\{1,\ldots,K\}
represents classes or vocabulary tokens.
A model (e.g., LLM) parameterized by
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is trained or evaluated using the cross-entropy loss
L({\bm{p}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{w}}),y)\triangleq-\log p_{y}({\bm{x}};{\bm{w}})
for each sample
({\bm{x}},y)
,
where
{\bm{p}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{w}})\in\Delta^{K-1}
denotes the predicted class probabilities (for classification) or distribution over the next token (for next token prediction).
In machine unlearning, the training data
D
is partitioned into the forget data
D_{f}\subseteq D
and the retain data
D_{r}\triangleq D\setminus D_{f}
.
Let
{\bm{w}}_{0}
denote the weights of the original model that is trained to minimize the averaged cross-entropy loss on
D
.
At iteration
t
(starting with
t=0
), the unlearning algorithm updates the model weights
{\bm{w}}_{t}
using information from
D_{f}
or
D_{r}
, or combination thereof, depending on the objectives, to obtain
{\bm{w}}_{t+1}
.

One common objective in machine unlearning is to approximate the retrained model
(
Georgiev et al. 2025
;
Chen & Yang 2023
)
,
i.e., the model trained from scratch on
D_{r}
.
This is usually achieved by minimizing the retain loss via fine-tuning (e.g., gradient descent on
D_{r}
) the original model on the retain data
(
Neel et al. 2021
;
Bui et al. 2026
)
, which helps to maintain retain utility.
Due to the inadequacy of this objective to ensure forget quality
(
Block et al. 2025
)
, the objective of increasing forget loss (e.g., gradient ascent on
D_{f}
) has been proposed
(
Halimi et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
.
However, these gradient-ascent methods may induce retain utility degradation
(
Mavrothalassitis et al. 2025
)
, making increasing forget loss insufficient as the sole objective
(
Fan et al. 2025
)
.

Hence, instead of solely approximating the retrained model or increasing the forget loss, we focus on the multi-objective aspect of unlearning.
Existing multi-objective unlearning methods typically optimize a weighted combination of the objectives
(
Kurmanji et al. 2023
;
Duan et al. 2025
)
.
However, there is no guarantee that the desired performance can be achieved for any of the objectives.
Furthermore, they have not considered the inherent statistical dependence between the forget and retain data, hence there is no attempt to reconcile the possibly conflicting objectives.
The two objectives of ensuring forget quality and maintaining retain utility are not the same for non-convex models.
In fact, we show that depending on the relationship between
D_{f}
and
D_{r}
, these two objectives can be either conflicting or compatible.

3
Conflict Analysis via Learning Dynamics

We begin by analyzing how taking a single gradient descent step on a sample
{\bm{z}}^{\prime}=({\bm{x}}^{\prime},y^{\prime})
during unlearning affects the loss on another sample
{\bm{z}}=({\bm{x}},y)
.
Let
L_{t}({\bm{z}})\triangleq L({\bm{p}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{w}}_{t}),y)
denote the loss on
{\bm{x}}
at iteration
t
.
We are interested in the quantity
\Delta_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}}L_{t}({\bm{z}})\triangleq L_{t+1}({\bm{z}})-L_{t}({\bm{z}})
for different choices of
{\bm{z}}^{\prime}
and
{\bm{z}}
,
where
\Delta_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}}
means the change in loss is induced by taking a single step on the sample
{\bm{z}}^{\prime}
.
We first consider the simplified case of fine-tuning on a single sample from the retain data.
Using first-order approximation
2
2

2

We defer the detailed derivation and discussion on why first-order approximation is sufficient to App.
B.1
.
, for any
{\bm{z}}_{r}=({\bm{x}}_{r},y_{r})\in D_{r}
,

\Delta_{{\bm{z}}_{r}}L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})=L_{t+1}({\bm{z}}_{r})-L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})\approx-\eta\cdot\left\lVert\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})\right\rVert^{2},

where
\eta
is the learning rate, and
\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}
denotes the gradient with respect to the model parameters
{\bm{w}}
.
Hence, unlearning by fine-tuning on a sample from the retain data reduces the loss on that sample.
Now consider how fine-tuning on the same sample impacts the loss on a sample from the forget data.
For any
{\bm{z}}_{f}=({\bm{x}}_{f},y_{f})\in D_{f}
we have

\Delta_{{\bm{z}}_{r}}L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{f})\approx-\eta\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{f})\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r}).

Unlike the previous scenario,
\Delta_{{\bm{z}}_{r}}L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{f})
is not always negative, but depends on the alignment between the two gradients
\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{f})
and
\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})
.
When the two
gradients are aligned
(positive dot product), the losses on the forget and retain samples both decrease.
When the
gradients are misaligned
(negative dot product), the loss on the forget sample increases as desired.
The analysis naturally extends to batch gradients
L_{t}(D)\triangleq 1/|D|\sum_{{\bm{z}}\in D}L_{t}({\bm{z}})
.
When taking a single gradient step
-\eta\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}L_{t}(D_{r})
to improve retain utility, the change in the batch forget loss and quality depends on the dot product
\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}L_{t}(D_{f})\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}L_{t}(D_{r})
.
Negative and positive gradient dot product leads to better and worse forget quality, respectively.
Intuitively, the dot product fundamentally determines how hard it is to reconcile both objectives of improving retain utility and forget quality in unlearning.
In the next section, we will show that
the gradient dot product also monotonically determines the minimum cost of forgetting, i.e., cost in retain utility to improve forget quality by a specified extent.

4
First-Order Convex Formulation

Since optimizing for smaller
L_{t}(D_{r})
and larger
L_{t}(D_{f})
simultaneously can be conflicting and provides no guarantee for satisfying either objective, we consider the problem of optimizing for the best retain utility while stipulating a minimum requirement on the forget quality,
i.e., minimize
L_{t}(D_{r})
subject to
\Delta L_{t}(D_{f})>Q
,
where
Q>0
is the per-iteration threshold for the forget quality requirement.
Following the first-order approximation in Sec.
3
, we focus on the objectives in each iteration.
Specifically, at iteration
t
, the retain utility degradation (i.e., cost of forgetting)
\Delta L_{t}(D_{r})\approx\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}
and the improvement in forget quality
\Delta L_{t}(D_{f})\approx\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}
,
where
\bm{g_{f}}\triangleq\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}L_{t}(D_{f})
,
\bm{g_{r}}\triangleq\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}L_{t}(D_{r})
are the averaged gradients,
and
\Delta{\bm{w}}\triangleq{\bm{w}}_{t+1}-{\bm{w}}_{t}
is the weight update at iteration
t
.
To ensure accurate approximation (see App.
B.1
), we restrict the per-iteration update to a local neighborhood,
i.e.,
\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{w}}\right\rVert\leq R
for some small
R>0
.
Therefore, it is equivalent to solve the following optimization problem
3
3

3

Unlike multi-objective optimization problems where all objectives are
minimized
, our formulation only minimizes the retain utility degradation and improves the forget quality (i.e.,
minimizing
retain loss while
increasing
forget loss) during unlearning.
:

\displaystyle\underset{\Delta{\bm{w}}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\geq Q,\quad\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{w}}\right\rVert\leq R.

Our formulation is general and allows the use of alternative loss functions such as the NPO loss
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
.
We state our main results below and defer the full derivations to App.
B
.

4.1
Hardness of Unlearning

Problem (
1
) is feasible when
Q\leq R\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert
, under this feasible condition we can solve the problem analytically.
The following theorem provides the closed-form expression for the optimal cost of forgetting in terms of retain utility degradation given a minimum forget quality requirement.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\kappa\triangleq\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}
be the gradient dot product
and
{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\triangleq\bm{g_{r}}-\frac{\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}
be the component of
\bm{g_{r}}
orthogonal to
\bm{g_{f}}
.
Assume
4
4

4

This assumption is typically satisfied in practice (Sec.
6
).

Q\leq R\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert
, the optimal value to Problem (
1
) and the minimum cost of forgetting
F_{r}^{*}\triangleq\min\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}
is given by

\begin{cases}-R\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert&\text{if }\kappa\leq\kappa_{1}\\
\frac{\kappa Q}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}-\left\lVert{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2)

where
\kappa_{1}\triangleq-Q\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert/R
.

As prior hardness measures are largely heuristic and unable to predict unlearning performance for a given instance
(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
, Theorem
4.1
provides
the first theoretical connection between a measurable quantity
\kappa
and unlearning objective performance
, quantified by the minimum retain utility degradation subject to a sufficient forget quality improvement.
Since
\left\lVert{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert
also depends on
\kappa
, the precise relationship between
F_{r}^{*}
and
\kappa
is not immediately apparent.
We formalize this relationship below.

Corollary 4.2

(Hardness Measure)
.

The minimum cost of forgetting
F_{r}^{*}
is monotonically non-decreasing with
\kappa
for fixed
\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert
.

Consequently,
\kappa
naturally serves as a hardness measure for unlearning.
This makes intuitive sense as a larger
\kappa
implies
\bm{g_{r}}
and
\bm{g_{f}}
are more aligned, and
D_{r}
and
D_{f}
are more similar.
Hence, it is more difficult to reconcile both objectives,
i.e., minimizing retain loss while ensuring forget quality,
resulting in a higher cost of forgetting and retain utility degradation
F_{r}^{*}
.
Consider the extreme example when
D_{r}=D_{f}
, it becomes impossible to maintain retain utility while improving forget quality, as the losses on the forget and retain data are identical, making it a particularly hard instance.
Note that the hardness measure
\kappa
depends not only on the similarity between
D_{r}
and
D_{f}
, but also on the model itself.
This is intuitive, as reconciling the objectives can become more difficult after unlearning the same forget data
D_{f}
for several iterations.

4.2
Hardness-Aware Unlearning Update

Interestingly, the hardness measure
\kappa
not only indicates the difficulty of the unlearning instance and predicts the cost of forgetting, but also informs the optimal unlearning update, as detailed in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3

(Optimal Unlearning Update)
.

Assume
Q\leq R\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert
, the minimum cost of forgetting
F_{r}^{*}
is achieved via the optimal unlearning update
\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}
, given by

\begin{cases}-\frac{R}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}\cdot\bm{g_{r}}&\text{if }\kappa\leq\kappa_{1}\\
\frac{Q}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}-\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\cdot\frac{{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}}{\left\lVert{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3)

The threshold
\kappa_{1}
determines the level of hardness and governs which type of unlearning update should be applied.
When
\kappa\leq\kappa_{1}
, the objectives are compatible and unlearning is relatively “easy”.
It is sufficient to apply the
direct
update in the same direction as
-\bm{g_{r}}
to improve retain utility without violating the constraint on forget quality since
\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}\geq Q
for
\kappa\leq\kappa_{1}
.
Conversely, the objectives become conflicting when
\kappa>\kappa_{1}
.
If we still apply the direct update, the forget quality constraint would be violated as

\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\left(-\frac{R}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}\cdot\bm{g_{r}}\right)=-\frac{\kappa R}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}<-\frac{R}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}\cdot\frac{-Q\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}{R}=Q.

Thus, when it is “hard” to reconcile the two unlearning objectives, we can no longer move freely in the direction of
-\bm{g_{r}}
, but must instead apply the
rectified
update, which incorporates the direction of
\bm{g_{f}}
to ensure the forget quality improves by the specified extent
Q
.

4.3
Unavoidable Collateral Forgetting

Problem (
1
) only imposes a constraint on the forget quality, and optimizes for the lowest cost of forgetting.
However, it is possible that
F_{r}^{*}>0
and retain utility degrades.
This inadvertent erasure of knowledge is also observed in other unlearning works
(
Nguyen et al. 2020
;
Tian et al. 2024
)
.
We term this phenomenon of improving forget quality causing retain utility degradation as
collateral forgetting
.
To identify the per-iteration condition under which collateral forgetting is unavoidable, we investigate an alternative formulation that also imposes a constraint on the retain utility:

\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\leq 0,\quad\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\geq Q\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{w}}\right\rVert\leq R.

(4)

Local collateral forgetting is unavoidable when it is infeasible to find a solution
\Delta{\bm{w}}
to Problem (
4
).
The following theorem establishes the exact boundary for this infeasibility.

Theorem 4.4

(Local Collateral Forgetting Condition)
.

Let
\kappa_{2}\triangleq\sqrt{\left(\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert\right)^{2}-Q^{2}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}/{R^{2}}}
.
Problem (
4
) admits no feasible solution for
\Delta{\bm{w}}
if and only if
\kappa>\kappa_{2}
, implying that local collateral forgetting is unavoidable.

The infeasibility condition is again governed by the hardness measure
\kappa
.
When the problem is too hard (
\kappa>\kappa_{2}
), it becomes impossible to satisfy the forget quality constraint without compromising the retain utility.
By leveraging this condition, model owners can identify unavoidable local collateral forgetting prior to the unlearning process.
To better understand this condition, we revisit the extreme example when
D_{r}=D_{f}
, in which collateral forgetting is clearly unavoidable.
Theorem
4.4
precisely captures this as the hardness
\kappa
exceeds the threshold:

\kappa=\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert>\sqrt{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}\left(\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)}=\kappa_{2}.

The threshold
\kappa_{2}
provides a clear interpretation.
The maximum dot product
\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert
represents the maximum hardness of the problem.
The condition implies that if the actual hardness is too close to this maximum, then Problem (
4
) becomes infeasible.
The term
-Q^{2}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}/{R^{2}}
serves as a safety margin:
a more negative value results in a lower threshold
\kappa_{2}
that is more easily exceeded by
\kappa
.
For example, when
Q
is large, we place a stricter requirement on the forget quality, which makes it harder to satisfy both forget and retain constraints simultaneously.
The thresholds
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
partition the unlearning problem into distinct regimes of hardness, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

5
The HAMU Algorithm

Building on the theoretical analysis in Sec.
4
, we propose the
hardness-aware multi-objective unlearning
(HAMU) algorithm.
The core of HAMU relies on Proposition
4.3
for the unlearning update and Theorem
4.4
for the stopping criterion.
These results imply that after
T
iterations, our HAMU algorithm can improve the constraint by at least
TQ
, as long as linear approximation holds.

Stochastic Update.

As computing the full gradients
\bm{g_{r}}
and
\bm{g_{f}}
can be computationally expensive, we sample a batch of examples from
D_{r}
and
D_{f}
at each iteration and use the batch gradient estimates
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{r}}
and
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}
to update the weights.
The hardness measure (
\kappa
) and thresholds (
\kappa_{1}
,
\kappa_{2}
) are estimated using these batch gradients for the remainder of this section.
Based on Proposition
4.3
, at each iteration, we first estimate the hardness
\kappa
, and then apply either the direct update or the rectified update based on the threshold
\kappa_{1}
.
Following convention in the literature, we use the learning rate
\eta
as our hyperparameter.
Specifically, we set
R=\eta\left\lVert\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{r}}\right\rVert
, under this choice of
R
, the direct update coincides with stochastic gradient descent on
D_{r}
.
We also investigate the effects of varying batch size in App.
G.3
and observe that our method is relatively robust to batch size.

Stopping Criterion.

Before applying the unlearning update, we first compare the hardness
\kappa
to the threshold
\kappa_{2}
, and terminate the algorithm if the local collateral forgetting condition is met (
\kappa>\kappa_{2}
).
Theorem
4.4
suggests that the terminated algorithm cannot improve the approximate forget quality by
Q
without degrading the approximate retain utility.
When
Q=0
, a local approximate Pareto optimum is reached.
This convergence to local optima is inherent to first-order methods and is commonly observed in deep learning optimization
(
Lee et al. 2016
)
.
These local minima are typically easy to find and well-performing
(
Choromanska et al. 2015
)
, as demonstrated by our experiment results.
We discuss practical adaptations of our stopping criterion in App.
C
.

Objective Variants.

We propose the HAMU-Q and the HAMU-U algorithms based on two constrained optimization formulations.
The former is based on Problem (
1
) while the latter is based on Problem (
16
) where we optimize for the best forget quality while requiring the improvement on the retain utility to exceed
U
(App.
B.6
).
The pseudocode for HAMU-Q and HAMU-U are provided in Alg.
1
and Alg.
2
, respectively.
Here,
Q
or
U
are user-chosen parameters depending on their minimum requirements on forget quality or retain utility, and the only hyperparameter of our algorithms is the learning rate
\eta
.
We provide guidance on how to select
Q
and
U
in Sec.
6.1
.

Practical Applicability.

In App.
B.1
, we discuss in mathematical details when HAMU first-order approximations are applicable to complex non-convex models.
The approximation error is negligible when the learning rate and the maximum Hessian eigenvalue are small.
In App.
B.5
, we also discuss the second-order formulation which requires a convexity assumption and computation of the Hessian and may be impractical for large ML models.
Furthermore, HAMU redoes the approximation at each iteration,
thereby ensuring consistent approximation along the unlearning trajectory
.
HAMU only introduces minimal computational overhead (gradient dot product calculation) beyond standard training and is highly parallelizable due to its layer-wise constraint independence (Sec.
5.1
).
We report the GPU memory and computational cost in App.
E.3
.

5.1
Parallel and Practical Implementation

Our algorithm scales efficiently to large models by exploiting layer-wise independence, enabling straightforward multi-GPU parallelization.
We decompose the flattened weights
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
\ell
components corresponding to the layers of the model, such that
{\bm{w}}=[{\bm{w}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{w}}^{(\ell)}]
, where
{\bm{w}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
denotes the weights of layer
i
.
The gradients
\bm{g_{f}}
,
\bm{g_{r}}
and the weight update
\Delta{\bm{w}}
are partitioned similarly.
We distribute the global forget quality constraint
Q
into layer-specific constraints
(Q_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}Q_{i}=Q
.
Enforcing the local condition
\bm{g_{f}}^{(i)}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{(i)}\geq Q_{i}
guarantees compliance with the global constraint:

\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bm{g_{f}}^{(i)}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{(i)}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}Q_{i}=Q.

This allows us to perform layer-wise weight update
\Delta{\bm{w}}^{(i)}
in Eq. (
3
) using layer-specific gradients
\bm{g_{f}}^{(i)}
and
\bm{g_{r}}^{(i)}
.
Hence, our algorithm can be efficiently parallelized by assigning different layers to different GPUs.
More details about the parallelized implementation can be found in App.
G.1
.
We also discuss how to improve the efficiency of HAMU by using optimizers and practical compatibility of HAMU with modern deep learning frameworks in App.
G
.

Practitioners have observed that different layers in the same model encode qualitatively distinct information,
both for
convolutional neural networks
(CNNs) and LLMs
(
Raghu et al. 2017
;
Skean et al. 2025
)
.
Consequently, these layers exhibit varying sensitivities to the same data.
To leverage this insight, instead of assigning each layer the same uniform constraint
Q/\ell
, we set the layer-wise constraint
Q_{i}
to be proportional to the product of the gradient norms,

Q_{i}\triangleq\frac{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}^{(i)}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}^{(i)}\right\rVert}{\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}^{(j)}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}^{(j)}\right\rVert}\cdot Q.

Intuitively, a layer
i
with larger gradient norms is more sensitive to optimization objectives and can contribute more to improving the forget quality, thus, should be assigned a higher
Q_{i}
.
We demonstrate how this technique further improves unlearning performance in Sec.
6.1
.

Varying hardness by changing similarity mixing ratio.

7
Conclusion

We introduce HAMU, a novel hardness-aware multi-objective unlearning algorithm that adjusts updates based on per-iteration hardness and terminates when collateral forgetting is reached.
Through theoretical analysis of a constrained optimization problem, HAMU optimally reduces the cost of forgetting while satisfying the forget quality constraint.
Naturally arising from the analysis, we propose using the gradient dot product as the hardness measure to quantify the difficulty of reconciling the unlearning objectives.
This proposed measure theoretically predicts the unlearning performance, formalizing the intuition that more similar data are harder to unlearn.
Our experiments demonstrate HAMU’s superior performance compared to existing baselines, and its applicability and scalability to large, non-convex models.
We believe HAMU represents a significant step toward developing unlearning algorithms that are both practical and theoretically-grounded, enabling broader adoption of unlearning in real-world scenarios.
We discuss the relevant literature in App.
A
.

Appendix A
Related Work

One common objective of machine unlearning is to approximate the retrained model
(
Georgiev et al. 2025
;
Nguyen et al. 2022
)
, which is equivalent to minimizing the retain loss
(
Neel et al. 2021
;
Bourtoule et al. 2021
)
.
Another prevalent objective is to increase the forget loss
(
Halimi et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
.
As neither of these objectives alone suffices to ensure a model with both strong forget and retain performance
(
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Fan et al. 2025
)
, multi-objective unlearning
(
Jin et al. 2025
)
aims to optimize both objectives simultaneously.

One straightforward approach to multi-objective unlearning is to directly optimize a weighted combination of the loss functions
(
Kurmanji et al. 2023
;
Duan et al. 2025
)
.
Other approaches such as GDR-GMA
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
and GRU
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
combine the retain gradient with the forget gradient to compute the unlearning update.
However, neither approach guarantees that a certain objective is achieved, nor do they adjust the update based on the hardness to optimally reduce the cost of forgetting.

Reconciling the retain and forget objectives in machine unlearning can be viewed as a specific instance of multi-objective optimization, and therefore multi-task learning approaches such as PCGrad
(
Yu et al. 2020
)
and CAGrad
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
can be adapted for multi-objective unlearning.
Our approach is closely related to the classical
\varepsilon
-constraint method
(
Haimes et al. 1971
)
but differs from standard approaches in that, instead of running gradient descent on the global constrained optimization problem, we derive a per-iteration, first-order update rule that enforces a minimum improvement in forget quality while minimizing retain utility degradation.
The novelty lies in this local, gradient-level formulation (Sec.
4
), as well as in the hardness characterization and its interpretation, which directly inform the design of our algorithm (Sec.
5
) and enable efficient application to large-scale, non-convex models.

Unlearning methods with theoretical guarantees
(
Guo et al. 2020
;
Ullah et al. 2021
)
typically require strong assumptions, such as strongly convex loss functions and the existence of a unique minimizer
(
Wu et al. 2020
;
Allouah et al. 2025
)
, limiting their practicality.
Existing works relevant to unlearning hardness
(
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Sarvmaili et al. 2024
)
are largely heuristic-based and do not establish a formal connection between the proposed hardness measures and unlearning performance.

Appendix B
Proofs and Derivations

B.1
First-Order Loss Approximation

For any
{\bm{z}}_{r}=({\bm{x}}_{r},y_{r})\in D_{r}
, applying Taylor expansion to
L_{t+1}({\bm{z}}_{r})
, we have

L_{t+1}({\bm{z}}_{r})=L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})+\Delta{\bm{w}}^{\top}\nabla L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\Delta{\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{E},

where
E
is the approximation error term, and
{\bm{H}}=\nabla^{2}L({\bm{p}}({\bm{x}}_{r};{\bm{w}}^{\prime}),y_{r})
is the Hessian at some intermediate model
{\bm{w}}^{\prime}={\bm{w}}_{t}+c\Delta{\bm{w}}
for some scalar
c\in(0,1)
.
As the model update
\Delta{\bm{w}}
is induced by a single gradient descent step on
{\bm{z}}_{r}
, substituting
\Delta{\bm{w}}=-\eta\nabla L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})
gives

\Delta_{{\bm{z}}_{r}}L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})=L_{t+1}({\bm{z}}_{r})-L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})=-\eta\left\lVert\nabla L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})\right\rVert^{2}+E.

The approximation quality can be measured by the ratio between the absolute values of the error term and linear term:

\frac{|E|}{\left\lvert\Delta{\bm{w}}^{\top}\nabla L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})\right\rvert}=\left|\frac{\frac{1}{2}\Delta{\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}\Delta{\bm{w}}}{-\eta\left\lVert\nabla L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})\right\rVert^{2}}\right|\leq\left|\frac{\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\left\lVert\nabla L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})\right\rVert^{2}\lambda_{\text{max}}}{-\eta\left\lVert\nabla L_{t}({\bm{z}}_{r})\right\rVert^{2}}\right|=\frac{1}{2}\eta\lambda_{\text{max}},

where
\lambda_{\text{max}}
is the maximum eigenvalue of
{\bm{H}}
.
Empirical evidence
(
Santurkar et al. 2018
;
Bui et al. 2026
)
has shown that the eigenvalues of Hessian typically falls below 200 for neural networks on standard datasets such as CIFAR-10.
Combined with a small learning rate of
1\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
used in our experiments (Sec.
6
), the error ratio falls below 1%.
Therefore, the second-order derivative component is negligible in practice and using the first-order derivative component is sufficient in each iteration.
Furthermore, we are redoing the approximation at each iteration, thereby ensuring consistent approximation along the unlearning trajectory.
Since our formulation in Problem (
1
) relies on approximation, the derived algorithms can be applied to any non-convex model as long as a sufficiently small learning rate is chosen to ensure approximation quality, the theoretical results also only hold up to the extent of approximation.
Despite this, our methods still perform well on non-convex models as demonstrated in our experiments (Sec.
6
).

B.2
The Forget-Constrained Problem

Theorem
4.1
and Proposition
4.3
are proven below by solving a convex optimization problem using KKT conditions.

As Problem (
1
) enforces a constraint on the forget quality, we refer to it as the forget-constrained problem.
Alternatively, we can enforce a constraint on the retain utility, we discuss this alternative retain-constrained problem in App.
B.6
.
To solve Problem (
1
), first note that it is feasible when
Q\leq R\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert
.
We assume this feasibility condition holds henceforth.
The Lagrangian of Problem (
1
) is given by

\mathcal{L}(\Delta{\bm{w}},\alpha,\beta)=\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}+\alpha(Q-\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}})+\beta(\Delta{\bm{w}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}-R^{2}).

Since Problem (
1
) is a convex optimization problem with convex inequality constraints, we can find its optimal value by solving the KKT conditions:

\displaystyle\textbf{Stationarity Condition}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Delta{\bm{w}}}=\bm{g_{r}}-\alpha\bm{g_{f}}+2\beta\Delta{\bm{w}}={\bm{0}}

\displaystyle\textbf{Complementary Slackness}

\displaystyle\alpha(Q-\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}})=0

\displaystyle\beta(\Delta{\bm{w}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}-R^{2})=0

\displaystyle\textbf{Primal Feasibility}

\displaystyle Q-\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\leq 0

\displaystyle\Delta{\bm{w}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}-R^{2}\leq 0

\displaystyle\textbf{Dual Feasibility}

\displaystyle\alpha\geq 0,\beta\geq 0.

We discuss all possible cases corresponding to different values of the dual variables
\alpha
and
\beta
.
Note that when the dual variables are positive, the corresponding primal feasibility conditions must bind.
Specifically, when
\alpha>0
,
\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}=Q
; when
\beta>0
,
\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{w}}\right\rVert=R
.

Case 1
:
\alpha=0,\beta>0
.

When
\alpha=0
, the stationarity condition simplifies to

\bm{g_{r}}+2\beta\Delta{\bm{w}}={\bm{0}},

which implies

\Delta{\bm{w}}=-\frac{1}{2\beta}\cdot\bm{g_{r}}.

(5)

To find the value of
\beta
, we equate the norm of the above expression to
R
since
\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{w}}\right\rVert=R
when
\beta>0
:

\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{w}}\right\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{4\beta^{2}}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}=R^{2}.

Solving for
\beta
, we obtain

\beta=\frac{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}{2R}.

Substitute the value of
\beta
into Eq. (
5
), we obtain the direct update

\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}=-\frac{R}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}\cdot\bm{g_{r}},

which gives the optimal value

F_{r}^{*}=\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}=-R\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert.

To show that the direct update corresponds to the case when
\kappa\leq\kappa_{1}
, we check that
\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}
should satisfy primal feasibility:

\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}\geq Q\implies\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}\leq-\frac{Q\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}{R},

which is exactly
\kappa\leq\kappa_{1}
.

Case 2
:
\alpha>0,\beta>0
.

We first decompose
\bm{g_{r}}
into components that are parallel and orthogonal to
\bm{g_{f}}
.
Let
\hat{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}\triangleq\bm{g_{f}}/\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert
be the unit vector of
\bm{g_{f}}
,
then
\bm{g_{r}}=x\cdot\hat{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}+{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}
,
where
x=\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\hat{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}
is the magnitude of the parallel component,
and
{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\perp\bm{g_{f}}
is the orthogonal component.
Similarly we can decompose
\Delta{\bm{w}}
into
\Delta{\bm{w}}=y\cdot\hat{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}+{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}
, where
{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}\perp\bm{g_{f}}

When
\alpha>0
,
\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}=Q
, substitute the decomposed
\Delta{\bm{w}}
we obtain

\bm{g_{f}}\cdot(y\cdot\hat{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}+{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp})=Q,

which gives the magnitude of
\Delta{\bm{w}}
’s parallel component to
\bm{g_{f}}
,

y=\frac{Q}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert}.

By Pythagorean theorem, the magnitude of
\Delta{\bm{w}}
’s orthogonal component is

\left\lVert{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{w}}\right\rVert^{2}-y^{2}=R^{2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}.

From the stationarity condition we know
\Delta{\bm{w}}\in\operatorname{span}\{\bm{g_{r}},\bm{g_{f}}\}

{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}
is parallel to
{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}

As we want to minimize

{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}

{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}

{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}\cdot{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}<0

Hence, the rectified update is

\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}=\frac{Q}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}-\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\cdot\frac{{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}}{\left\lVert{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert},

(6)

where
{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}=\bm{g_{r}}-x\cdot\hat{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}=\bm{g_{r}}-\frac{\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}
.
Thus, the optimal value
F_{r}^{*}
is

\displaystyle\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{Q}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\left(\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}\right)-\left\lVert{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}}

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\left(\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}\right)-\sqrt{\left(\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{(\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}})^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\right)\left(R^{2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\right)}.

Next, we will show how
Case 2 corresponds precisely to the scenario in which the boundary condition fails
,
i.e., the rectified update is applied when
\kappa>\kappa_{1}
,
thus
justifying our use of hardness measure to inform the unlearning update
.

By decomposing the stationarity condition in the direction parallel to
\bm{g_{f}}
and the direction orthogonal to
\bm{g_{f}}

x-\alpha\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert+2\beta y=0\quad\text{and}\quad{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}+2\beta{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}={\bm{0}},

we obtain the expressions for the dual variables:

From
\alpha>0

\beta

\displaystyle x+2\beta y

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle x+2\cdot\frac{\left\lVert{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert}{2\left\lVert{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert}\cdot y

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle x\left\lVert{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert+y\left\lVert{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert

\displaystyle>0.

(8)

If
\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}>0

\kappa=\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}>-Q\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert/R=\kappa_{1}

If
\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}<0

x
,
y

\left\lVert{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert
,
\left\lVert{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert
into Ineq. (
8

\displaystyle y\left\lVert{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert

\displaystyle>-x\left\lVert{\Delta{\bm{w}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\frac{Q}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert}\sqrt{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{(\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}})^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}}

\displaystyle>-\frac{\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert}\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}}

\displaystyle\frac{Q^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\left(\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{(\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}})^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle>\frac{(\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}})^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\left(R^{2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle Q^{2}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}

For Cases 3 and 4, we first solve for the optimal values and updates, then show they are degenerate cases of Case 2.

Relationship Between Unlearning Performance and the Hardness Measure

The proof of Corollary

is given below.

Proof.

When
\kappa\leq\kappa_{1}
, the function is constant.
When
\kappa>\kappa_{1}

B.5
Unavoidable Collateral Forgetting (Second-Order)

In this section, we discuss the local collateral forgetting condition in the second-order formulation.
Identifying this condition requires solving the second-order formulation of Problem (
1
),
which also yields an update rule but remains impractical due to assumptions and computational cost involving the Hessian.
We find that it is also impossible to avoid collateral forgetting in the second-order formulation under certain scenarios.
In fact, the hardness measure again plays an important role in determining whether we can avoid local collateral forgetting in machine unlearning.
We show that
when the problem becomes too hard (
\kappa=\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}
exceeds a certain threshold), collateral forgetting is unavoidable
.

We formulate the problem similar to Problem (
4
), but better approximates
\Delta L_{t}(D_{r})
with a second-order approximation, while keeping first-order approximation for
\Delta L_{t}(D_{f})
to ensure we can still obtain the solution in analytical form.
Assume the Hessian on the retain data
\bm{H_{r}}\triangleq\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}^{2}L_{t}(D_{r})
is positive definite, we wish (or prove impossible) to find
\Delta{\bm{w}}
such that the following constraints are simultaneously satisfied:

\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta{\bm{w}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}\Delta{\bm{w}}\leq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\geq Q.

(11)

The first constraint requires the retain utility to not drop after the update, while the second constraint requires the forget quality to exceed a threshold
Q>0
.
Note that unlike in Problem (
4
), we do not constrain the maximum norm of the weight update, so if the problem is infeasible, then collateral forgetting is unavoidable for all possible weight updates
\Delta{\bm{w}}
.
To find the infeasibility condition, we consider the following convex optimization problem, which is the second-order formulation of Problem (
1
):

\displaystyle\underset{\Delta{\bm{w}}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle F(\Delta{\bm{w}})\triangleq\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta{\bm{w}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}\Delta{\bm{w}}

(12)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\geq Q.

If the constraint in Problem (
12
) is satisfied, and the minimum objective value
F(\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*})>0
, then Problem (
11
) is infeasible.
This establishes the local collateral forgetting condition in the second-order formulation.
The Lagrangian of Problem (
12
) is given by

\mathcal{L}(\Delta{\bm{w}},\gamma)=\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta{\bm{w}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}\Delta{\bm{w}}+\gamma\left(Q-\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\right).

Since Problem (
12
) is a convex optimization problem with convex inequality constraints, we can find its optimal value by solving the KKT conditions:

\displaystyle\textbf{Stationarity Condition}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Delta{\bm{w}}}=\bm{g_{r}}+\bm{H_{r}}\Delta{\bm{w}}-\gamma\bm{g_{f}}={\bm{0}}

\displaystyle\textbf{Complementary Slackness}

\displaystyle\gamma(Q-\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}})=0

\displaystyle\textbf{Primal Feasibility}

\displaystyle Q-\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\leq 0

\displaystyle\textbf{Dual Feasibility}

\displaystyle\gamma\geq 0.

From the stationarity condition we have

\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}=\gamma\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{f}}-\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}.

(13)

Case 1
:
\gamma=0
.

Then
\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}=-\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}
.
We can verify that
\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*\top}\bm{H_{r}}\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}\leq 0
is always true:

\displaystyle\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*\top}\bm{H_{r}}\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\left(-\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}\right)^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}\left(-\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\bm{g_{r}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{g_{r}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{H_{r}}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\bm{g_{r}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle 0,

where we use
\left(\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\right)^{\top}=\left(\bm{H_{r}}^{\top}\right)^{-1}=\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}
because
\bm{H_{r}}
is symmetric, and the inequality is due to
\bm{H_{r}}\succ 0
.
Therefore, Problem (
11
) is feasible if
\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}
additionally satisfies
\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}\geq Q
.
To simplify discussion, we first assume a spherical loss landscape,
i.e.,
\bm{H_{r}}=\lambda{\bm{I}}
for some
\lambda>0
,
and discuss the general case afterwards.
Problem (
11
) is infeasible if

\displaystyle\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}

\displaystyle<Q

\displaystyle\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\left(-\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}\right)

\displaystyle<Q

\displaystyle-\bm{g_{f}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}

\displaystyle<Q

\displaystyle-\bm{g_{f}}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}{\bm{I}}\right)\bm{g_{r}}

\displaystyle<Q

\displaystyle\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}

\displaystyle>-\lambda Q.

For the infeasibility condition of general
\bm{H_{r}}\!\succ\!0
, we first establish a lower bound for
\bm{g_{f}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}
with the following lemma:

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\lambda_{\text{min}}
,
\lambda_{\text{max}}
be the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix
{\bm{A}}_{d\times d}
, and
{\bm{u}}
,
{\bm{v}}
are vectors of dimension
d
, then
{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{v}}\geq\lambda_{\text{min}}{\bm{u}}\cdot{\bm{v}}-\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}\right)\left\lVert{\bm{u}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bm{v}}\right\rVert
.

Proof.

Decompose
{\bm{A}}
into
{\bm{A}}=\lambda_{\text{min}}{\bm{I}}+\left({\bm{A}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}{\bm{I}}\right)
and denote
{\bm{B}}\triangleq{\bm{A}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}{\bm{I}}
.
Then the spectral norm of
{\bm{B}}
is
\left\lVert{\bm{B}}\right\rVert_{2}=\lambda_{\text{max}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}
.
We have

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}^{\top}\left(\lambda_{\text{min}}{\bm{I}}\right){\bm{v}}+{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{B}}{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\text{min}}{\bm{u}}\cdot{\bm{v}}-\left\lVert{\bm{u}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bm{B}}{\bm{v}}\right\rVert

(
Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\text{min}}{\bm{u}}\cdot{\bm{v}}-\left\lVert{\bm{u}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bm{B}}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert{\bm{v}}\right\rVert

(
Definition of spectral norm
)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\text{min}}{\bm{u}}\cdot{\bm{v}}-\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}\right)\left\lVert{\bm{u}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bm{v}}\right\rVert.

∎

Problem (
11
) is infeasible if
-\bm{g_{f}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}<Q
, by applying Lemma
B.1
we obtain the infeasibility condition:

\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}>\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{min}}}\left[\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}\right)\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-Q\right],

where
\lambda_{\text{min}}
,
\lambda_{\text{max}}
are the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}
, respectively.

Case 2
:
\gamma>0
.

Primal feasibility must bind so
\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}=Q
.
Substituting the expression for
\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}
in Eq. (
13
) and solving for
\gamma
,

\gamma=\frac{Q+\bm{g_{f}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}}{\bm{g_{f}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{f}}}.

To simplify notation, let
A=\bm{g_{f}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{f}}
,
B=\bm{g_{r}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}
,
C=\bm{g_{r}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{f}}=\bm{g_{f}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}
, then
\gamma=\frac{Q+C}{A}
, and

\displaystyle F(\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*})

\displaystyle=\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\left(\gamma\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{f}}-\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{f}}-\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}\right)^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}\left(\gamma\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{f}}-\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{r}}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma C-B+\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma^{2}A-2\gamma C+B\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}A-\frac{1}{2}B

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{Q+C}{A}\right)^{2}A-\frac{1}{2}B

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{(Q+C)^{2}}{A}-\frac{1}{2}B.

Problem (
11
) is infeasible if
F(\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*})>0
, which is equivalent to
(Q+C)^{2}>AB
.
Note that
\frac{Q+C}{A}=\gamma>0
and
A>0
since
\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\succ 0
(assuming
\bm{g_{f}}\neq{\bm{0}}
),
we know that
Q+C>0
.
The infeasibility condition then becomes

Q+C>\sqrt{AB}.

(14)

If we again assume that
\bm{H_{r}}=\lambda{\bm{I}}
for some
\lambda>0
, then
A=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}
,
B=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}
,
C=\frac{1}{\lambda}\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle Q+\frac{1}{\lambda}\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}

\displaystyle>\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda}\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}}

\displaystyle\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}

\displaystyle>\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-\lambda Q.

(15)

For the general case, again let
\lambda_{\text{min}}
,
\lambda_{\text{max}}
be the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}
, by applying Lemma
B.1
,

C=\bm{g_{r}}^{\top}\bm{H_{r}}^{-1}\bm{g_{f}}\geq\lambda_{\text{min}}\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}-\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}\right)\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert.

By the Rayleigh quotient bound,
A\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}
,
B\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}
, thus

\sqrt{AB}-Q\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}\cdot\lambda_{\text{max}}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}-Q=\lambda_{\text{max}}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-Q.

From Ineq. (
14
) we know the infeasibility condition is

C>\sqrt{AB}-Q.

Therefore, the problem is infeasible if the lower bound of
C
is strictly greater than the upper bound of
\sqrt{AB}-Q
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{min}}\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}-\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}\right)\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle>\lambda_{\text{max}}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-Q

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{min}}\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}

\displaystyle>2\lambda_{\text{max}}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-\lambda_{\text{min}}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-Q

\displaystyle\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{min}}}\left[\left(2\lambda_{\text{max}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}\right)\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-Q\right].

Observe that
we can combine both cases
since the condition when
\lambda=0
is subsumed by the condition when
\lambda>0
:

\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{min}}}\left[\left(2\lambda_{\text{max}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}\right)\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-Q\right]\geq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{min}}}\left[\left(\lambda_{\text{max}}-\lambda_{\text{min}}\right)\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-Q\right],

and

\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-\lambda Q\geq-\lambda Q.

We interpret the local collateral forgetting condition under the assumption of a spherical loss landscape (Ineq. (
15
)).
The maximum dot product
\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert
describes the maximum hardness of the problem, the condition says that if the actual hardness of the problem is too close to this maximum, then collateral forgetting is unavoidable.
The term
-\lambda Q
acts as a safety margin, lowering which lowers the threshold and it is easier for the problem to become infeasible.
If
\lambda
is large, the loss surface is steep, it is harder to bound the decrease in retain utility.
If
Q
is large, we are putting a stricter demand on the forget quality, which makes it harder to satisfy both constraints simultaneously.
In the extreme case when
\lambda Q
is large enough, it is possible that
\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert-\lambda Q<0
, and even a negative
\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}
could theoretically be infeasible.

When the problem is feasible, i.e., both constraints are satisfied, Eq. (
13
) provides an unlearning update that does not induce collateral forgetting.
However, it requires computing and inverting the Hessian, which is prohibitively expensive.
It also requires the additional assumption that the Hessian is positive definite, hence limiting its applicability to non-convex models, whereas our first-order algorithm can be applied to any non-convex model as long as a suitable
R
is chosen for approximation quality.

B.6
The Retain-Constrained Problem

The retain-constrained problem provides an alternative formulation to Problem (
1
).
In Problem (
16
), we optimize for the best forget quality improvement while also enforcing a constraint on the minimum retain utility improvement.
The retain-constrained problem is formulated below for
U>0
,
R>0
:

\displaystyle\underset{\Delta{\bm{w}}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}

(16)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\leq-U,\quad\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{w}}\right\rVert\leq R.

which is the same as

\displaystyle\underset{\Delta{\bm{w}}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle-\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle-\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\geq U,\quad\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{w}}\right\rVert\leq R.

This is structurally equivalent to Problem (
1
), and is only feasible when
U\leq R\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert
.
We can derive the solution to Problem (
16
) by substituting
\bm{g_{r}}\leftarrow-\bm{g_{f}},\bm{g_{f}}\leftarrow-\bm{g_{r}},Q\leftarrow U
into the solution to Problem (
1
), and then negating the optimal value.
Note that the hardness measure
\kappa=\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}
is preserved under this substitution:

\displaystyle\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{R}{\left\lVert-\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert}\cdot(-\bm{g_{f}})\quad&\text{if }\kappa\leq-\frac{U\left\lVert-\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert}{R}\\
\frac{U}{\left\lVert-\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}\cdot(-\bm{g_{r}})-\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{U^{2}}{\left\lVert-\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\cdot\frac{-{\bm{g_{f}}}_{\perp}}{\left\lVert-{\bm{g_{f}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert}\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{R}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}\quad&\text{if }\kappa\leq\kappa_{3}\\
\mathrlap{-\frac{U}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}\cdot\bm{g_{r}}+\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{U^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\cdot\frac{{\bm{g_{f}}}_{\perp}}{\left\lVert{\bm{g_{f}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert}}\hphantom{\frac{U}{\left\lVert-\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}\cdot(-\bm{g_{r}})-\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{U^{2}}{\left\lVert-\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\cdot\frac{-{\bm{g_{f}}}_{\perp}}{\left\lVert-{\bm{g_{f}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert}}\quad&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
{\bm{g_{f}}}_{\perp}\triangleq\bm{g_{f}}-\frac{\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}\cdot\bm{g_{r}}
and
\kappa_{3}\triangleq-U\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert/{R}
.
Similarly, we have

\min(-\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}})=\begin{cases}-R\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert\quad&\text{if }\kappa\leq\kappa_{3}\\
\frac{\kappa U}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}-\sqrt{\left(\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}\right)\left(R^{2}-\frac{U^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}\right)}\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The optimal value of the original problem is then its negation:

\max\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}=\begin{cases}R\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert\quad&\text{if }\kappa\leq\kappa_{3}\\
-\frac{\kappa U}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}+\sqrt{\left(\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}\right)\left(R^{2}-\frac{U^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}\right)}\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This also implies that
\max\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}
monotonically decreases with
\kappa
for fixed
\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert
, given
F_{r}^{*}=\min\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}
monotonically increases with
\kappa
for fixed
\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert
, and the fact that hardness
\kappa
is preserved under our substitution.

The direct update (

) in the retain-constrained problem corresponds to gradient ascent on the forget data if we set

.
To find the stopping criterion of HAMU-U, we find the infeasibility condition to the following problem:

\bm{g_{f}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\geq 0,\quad\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\Delta{\bm{w}}\leq-U\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{w}}\right\rVert\leq R.

(17)

Appendix C
Adapting Stopping Criterion for Layer-Specific Constraints

In Alg.
1
, the stopping criterion is specified to be

\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}=\kappa>\kappa_{2}=\sqrt{\left(\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert\right)^{2}-\frac{Q^{2}}{R^{2}}\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}}.

This inequality is equivalent to

Q>\frac{R}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}\sqrt{\left(\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert\right)^{2}-\left(\bm{g_{r}}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}\right)^{2}}.

(18)

As described in Sec.
5.1
, the global constraint
Q
can be distributed into layer-wise constraints
\left(Q_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{\ell}
, and the stopping criterion has to be adapted accordingly.
One possible way of terminating the unlearning process could be to replace all variable in Ineq. (
18
) with their layer-wise counterparts,

Q_{i}>\frac{R}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}^{(i)}\right\rVert}\sqrt{\left(\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}^{(i)}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}^{(i)}\right\rVert\right)^{2}-\left(\bm{g_{r}}^{(i)}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}^{(i)}\right)^{2}},

(19)

such that unlearning terminates when this layer-wise stopping criterion is met for any layer
i
in the model.

However, as described in Sec.
5.1
, different layers in the model can exhibit varying sensitivities to the same data.
It might be harder to unlearn at certain layers compared to other layers, such that the layer-wise stopping criterion in Ineq. (
19
) is triggered in an earlier unlearning epoch for layers that are harder to unlearn.
Terminating when any layer meets this criterion would result in an undesirably early termination of unlearning, when other layers have not yet met the criterion.

Instead, considering the layer-wise constraints are distributed with
\sum_{i=1}^{l}Q_{i}=Q
, we opt to terminate when this aggregate condition across all layers is met,

Q=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}Q_{i}>\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{R}{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}^{(i)}\right\rVert}\sqrt{\left(\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}^{(i)}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}^{(i)}\right\rVert\right)^{2}-\left(\bm{g_{r}}^{(i)}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}^{(i)}\right)^{2}}.

(20)

Using Ineq. (
20
) as the stopping criterion allows layers that are easier to unlearn to compensate for layers that are harder to unlearn, such that unlearning only terminates when the required forget quality threshold exceeds the value that can be sustained with the aggregate of all layers in the model.
This is the stopping criterion that we have used in Fig.
9
.

Appendix D
Unlearning Algorithms

In this paper, we compare HAMU against the following loss-based and gradient-based unlearning algorithms.
We use the same learning rate of
1\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
for all unlearning algorithms in our experiments.

Gradient ascent (GA)

The gradient ascent unlearning algorithm
(
Jang et al. 2023
)
only trains on the forget data, and uses a negated loss function to increase the loss on the forget data.

Fine-tune (FT)

The fine-tune unlearning algorithm trains the model using only the retain data, to decrease the loss on the retain data.

Gradient difference (GDiff)

The gradient difference unlearning algorithm
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
combines GA and FT, with gradient ascent on the forget data and gradient descent on the retain data.

KL minimization (KL)

The KL minimization algorithm
(
Maini et al. 2024
)
combines gradient ascent on the forget data with minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the prediction of the model being unlearned and the original model before unlearning on the retain data.

SCRUB

The SCRUB algorithm
(
Kurmanji et al. 2023
)
uses a weighted combination of different losses, by maximizing the KL divergence between the predictions of the unlearned model and original model on the forget data, while minimizing the loss and KL divergence of the predictions on the retain data.
Following the source code
6
6

6

https://github.com/meghdadk/SCRUB
of SCRUB, we set
\alpha=0.001
and
\gamma=0.99
.

Gradient Rectified Unlearning (GRU)

The Gradient Rectified Unlearning (GRU) algorithm
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
projects the gradient on the forget data,
\bm{g_{f}}
, such that the new vector is orthogonal to the gradient on the retain data,
\bm{g_{r}}
, when the angle between the two gradients is obtuse.

Projecting Conflicting Gradients (PCGrad)

We adapt the projecting conflicting gradients algorithm
(
Yu et al. 2020
)
to our unlearning setting.
If the angle between
\bm{g_{f}}
and
\bm{g_{r}}
is obtuse,
\bm{g_{f}}
is projected to be orthogonal to
\bm{g_{r}}
and vice versa for
\bm{g_{r}}
, and the mean of the two projected gradients is used for the weight update.

HAMU

For our unlearning algorithm HAMU, we perform weight updates according to Alg.
1
and Alg.
2
.

D.1
Implementation Details

For unlearning algorithms that use both the retain and forget data (HAMU, GDiff, KL, SCRUB), we duplicate the forget data to match the size of the retain data.
For unlearning algorithms which only use the retain data (FT) or the forget data (GA), we duplicated smaller unlearning datasets to match the size of the dataset used when using both retain and forget data (HAMU, GDiff, KL, SCRUB).
For fair comparison, all algorithms were re-implemented with the same training code, with only the specific weight update step swapped out for each unlearning algorithm.
The same GPU optimizations described in Sec.
G.1
were used for all methods which require gradients of both the forget and retain data (GDiff, KL, SCRUB, GRU, PCGrad, HAMU).
This provides an additional advantage for other unlearning methods, and might result in lower resource requirements than those reported in the original papers.
In our experiments, we ran all unlearning algorithms using the same learning rate for the same number of unlearning steps on the same hardware and number of GPUs.
Unless otherwise stated, we use
42
as the random seed in all experiments.

Appendix G
Practical Considerations with Modern Deep Learning Frameworks

G.1
Parallelized Computation of Forget and Retain Gradients

As our proposed unlearning algorithm requires the use of and comparison between the gradients of the retain samples
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{r}}
and gradients of the forget samples
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}
, both copies of the gradients are required to be available on the GPU at each iteration.
A naive approach at each step would be to perform the forward and backward pass on the retain samples, store the gradients separately, then perform the forward and backward pass on the forget samples, and finally compare the two gradients.
This incurs a high memory cost on the GPU, as memory has to be allocated for both
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{r}}
and
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}
.
As such, we would only be able to process half the number of samples at each iteration as compared to if we only needed one copy of the gradients based on the memory available on the GPU.
This is also true even if this naïve approach is used with multi-GPU model parallelism, where each GPU still has to allocate memory for two copies of gradients.

To perform this efficiently, we implemented a parallelized unlearning script, which allocates the forget and retain samples to alternate GPUs when ran on an even number of GPUs.
This allows half of the GPUs to perform computation on the retain samples while the other half of the GPUs perform computation on the forget samples at the same time.
Each half of the GPUs would only require memory to store either
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{r}}
or
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}
(but not both), allowing us to make full use of compute and memory resource on all GPUs.
As each GPU only needs enough memory to store a single copy of its own gradients, we would effectively be able to process double the batch size with the same memory available, as compared to the naïve implementation (or enable HAMU to be used when two copies of the gradients cannot fit within the memory of a single GPU).

To implement this, we made use of the integration between the SFT Trainer and
accelerate
library
(
Gugger et al. 2022
)
for a multi-GPU setup.
During the data preparation step, we interleave the forget and retain samples into the unlearning dataset according to the batch size of each GPU, such that the dataloader would send forget samples to one half of the GPUs and retain sample to the other GPUs.
During the backward pass, instead of simply aggregating the gradients from all GPUs as in a typical multi-GPU setup, we instead aggregate the gradients from alternate GPUs responsible for the forget and retain samples separately.
When comparison of
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{r}}
and
\bar{\bm{g}}_{\bm{f}}
are required, each layer’s gradient is iteratively sent over to the other GPUs as needed, such that only a small amount of additional memory is required to store only the single layer’s worth of gradients.
These gradients are then passed into the weight update function which computes the final weight update according to the unlearning algorithm.
Exact implementation is in our provided code.

G.2
Using HAMU with Optimizers

In the main paper, our experiments are set up in a way to clearly demonstrate the theoretical formulation in Problem (
1
) and Problem (
16
).
However, when deploying unlearning algorithms in practice on real world ML models, there are often other practical constraints and requirements, as well as practical optimizations that could be made to improve the overall performance.
For practical constraints, the model owner might be limited to a small number of unlearning steps (e.g., only
1
epoch) such that the computational cost of unlearning remains small relative to retraining.
For practical optimizations, the model owner typically implements momentum-based optimizers (e.g., AdamW
(
Loshchilov & Hutter 2019
)
) to accelerate the convergence of the optimization process.

Optimizers such as AdamW
(
Loshchilov & Hutter 2019
)
speed up the optimization process by incorporating the weight updates in previous iterations to the update in the current iteration.
When the gradients do not change significantly between iterations, the step size taken by each weight update gradually increases.
This speeds up the unlearning process, and the model is able to achieve a similar or better forget quality and retain utility with fewer number of unlearning steps.

To allow our weight update to work with momentum-based optimizers, instead of directly performing weight update with
{\bm{w}}_{t+1}={\bm{w}}_{t}+\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}
, we instead compute the equivalent gradient which would have resulted in a gradient descent weight update,
\bm{g}^{*}=-\frac{\Delta{\bm{w}}^{*}}{\eta}
.
This equivalent gradient
\bm{g}^{*}
is then passed to the momentum-based optimizers.
When we set
R=\eta\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert
, the equivalent gradient for HAMU-Q is

\bm{g}^{*}=\begin{cases}\bm{g_{r}}\quad&\text{if }\kappa\leq\kappa_{1}\\
-\frac{Q\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}{R\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert}\cdot\bm{g_{f}}+\sqrt{\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert^{2}-\left(\frac{Q\left\lVert\bm{g_{r}}\right\rVert}{R\left\lVert\bm{g_{f}}\right\rVert}\right)^{2}}\cdot\frac{{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}}{\left\lVert{\bm{g_{r}}}_{\perp}\right\rVert}\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(21)

Using AdamW with only
1
epoch achieves a higher
\Delta L_{f}
and
-\Delta L_{r}
than vanilla SGD in

G.3
Sensitivity analysis for unlearning batch size

In our experimental setup, we used a large batch size that could still fit within the available memory in the GPU.
However, there will be situations where a smaller batch size has to be used, such as when a GPU with less memory is used, the model being unlearned is large with more parameters, or text training data for LLMs has longer sequence lengths.
A smaller batch size can result in several possible issues, such as poorer approximation and high variance between iterations of batch gradient estimates when updating the weights.

To resolve these issues, we have implemented several possible solutions in our code.
Firstly, our code can be run with more than 2 GPUs, such that alternate GPUs handle the gradient computation of the retain and forget data respectively.
When more GPUs are available, it results in a larger effective batch size.
Secondly, our code is also fully compatible with gradient accumulation, where gradient values are collated across multiple iterations of the unlearning loop before the weight update is performed, similarly achieving a larger effective batch size.
Lastly, to address the issue of fluctuations in
\bar{\kappa}
prematurely triggering the stopping criterion, practical adjustments can be made to the stopping condition, such as implementing a moving average of
\kappa/\kappa_{2}
, or only stop when the stopping criterion is met for a certain number of times.

To evaluate the effect of different batch size on HAMU, we conducted ablation experiments, varying the batch size used in the CV experiments.
Table
2
shows the unlearning performance of HAMU-Q with batch size between
128
and
2048
.
We observe that smaller batch sizes tend to achieve better forget quality, likely due to more frequent weight updates for a fixed number of training samples (a smaller batch size will have more weight update steps within a single epoch).
Retain utility is relatively robust to batch size, but very small batches (128) degrade the retain utility, and increase runtime due to poor GPU utilization.
Under this experimental setting, batch size 512 provides a good balance.
