Diagnostic Performance of the AlzoSure Predict Assay and Its Association With Alzheimer's Disease Biomarkers and Imaging Findings
Authors: Ali Rajabpour‐Sanati, Hamide Nasiri, Farbod Khosravi, Farhang Ghahrieh, Alireza Asemanrafat, Alireza Keshavarz Bahaqiqat, Parsa Saberian, Rozhin Bakhshi, Faranak Rastegari, Ramin Ahangar‐Sirous, Amirmohammad Shahidzadehasadi, Hadis Seif, Maryam Ghahremani, Faeze Gandomi‐Nasrabadi, Zahra Azizan, Mahsa Mayeli, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative*
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEarly diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease (AD) is critical for improving patient outcomes. The laboratory‐developed blood test of AlzoSure measures the unfolded conformational variant of p53 (U‐p53AZ) in plasma and has shown promise as a screening tool for AD risk. We aimed to evaluate the association between U‐p53AZ with established cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and neuroimaging measures, and to determine its diagnostic performance in distinguishing cognitively normal (CN) individuals from those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI).MethodsParticipants included CN and MCI individuals aged 55–90 years with complete baseline and 24‐month follow‐up assessments. Associations between U‐p53AZ, CSF biomarkers, standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) of glucose measured by fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG‐PET), and cognition were examined with multivariable regression models adjusted for age, sex, and APOE ε4 status. Diagnostic performance was assessed with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsAt baseline, no significant group differences were observed in plasma U‐p53AZ, FDG SUVR, or CSF biomarkers between CN and MCI. Longitudinally, FDG SUVR significantly declined in MCI (p = 0.040), while CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181 increased in both groups (all p < 0.05). Higher U‐p53AZ levels were independently associated with elevated CSF t‐tau (β = 0.38; p = 0.033) and p‐tau181 (β = 0.37; p = 0.033) at baseline, and these associations persisted at follow‐up (β range 0.43–0.48; all p < 0.02). No significant associations were found with FDG SUVR or cognitive scores. The discriminative ability of U‐p53AZ to distinguish CN from MCI was modest (AUC = 0.617, 95% CI 0.518–0.716).ConclusionAlzoSure measurements are significantly associated with CSF tau values but demonstrate limited utility in differentiating CN from MCI. Although promising as a marker of tau‐related neurodegeneration, AlzoSure has a modest diagnostic performance as a stand‐alone assessment.

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia and the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, constituting approximately 60%–70% or more of dementia cases globally [1, 2]. As of the latest estimates, over 55 million individuals worldwide are affected by dementia, with projections suggesting this burden will nearly double every two decades, reaching approximately 82 million by 2030 and 152 million by 2050 [2, 3, 4, 5].

Early diagnosis of AD is crucial for better patient outcomes, as it allows for early intervention and symptomatic management [6, 7, 8]. Extensive research has been dedicated to the identification of early AD markers [9, 10]. Recent advancements in understanding AD have identified biomarkers including imaging, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), or blood markers, shifting the diagnosis from the late dementia stage to the early stages [11]. Positron emission tomography (PET) utilizing 2‐[18F]fluoro‐2‐deoxy‐d‐glucose (FDG) has been widely used to monitor AD‐related brain alterations, providing insights into synaptic functioning and density through the measurement of the cerebral metabolic rate of glucose (CMRglc) [12]. Disruptions in glucose metabolism have been identified as significant underlying factors in the initiation and progression of AD, with implications for oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal dysfunction [13, 14].

Over the past decade, a spectrum of biomarker modalities has emerged to improve early detection of AD. Among these, plasma phosphorylated tau at threonine‐217 (p‐tau217) has demonstrated exceptional diagnostic and prognostic utility. In large multicohort meta‐analyses, plasma p‐tau217 distinguished AD from other dementias with high accuracy (e.g., AUC > 0.90) and showed strong concordance with established CSF and PET biomarkers [15, 16, 17]. In one systematic review, blood‐based p‐tau217 levels were found to increase progressively from normal cognition to MCI to AD, outperforming p‐tau181 and p‐tau231 in sensitivity and specificity [18]. Furthermore, p‐tau217 has shown robust prognostic value = in predicting future cognitive decline in cognitively unimpaired individuals over 10‐year follow‐up horizons [19].

Beyond tau, other blood biomarkers have garnered attention: neurofilament light (NfL) reflects neuroaxonal damage and is elevated in AD, though its specificity is modest [20]. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) indicates astroglial activation and has shown strong diagnostic performance (e.g., serum GFAP AUC ~ 0.93, distinguishing AD from MCI and controls) [21]. Additional promising biomarkers include amyloid‐β ratio (Aβ42/40) as a blood‐based marker representing amyloid pathology [22], and extracellular vesicles (EVs), particularly neuron‐derived EVs carrying Aβ and tau proteins, which show diagnostic and prognostic potential in early and preclinical stages [23].

Despite these advances, obstacles remain: assay heterogeneity across platforms, cohort variability, and limited longitudinal validation constrain widespread clinical translation. Standardization and head‐to‐head comparisons across biomarker assays are urgently required to establish robust, generalizable tools for early AD detection.

AD is diagnosed clinically, with potential confirmation testing using CSF biomarkers like total tau (T‐tau), phosphorylated tau at threonine‐181 (p‐tau181, as used in this study), amyloid‐β (Aβ), imaging modalities like magnetic‐resonance imaging (MRI), 18F‐FDG PET, and amyloid PET [24, 25]. Imaging techniques offer essential insights into the pathophysiological changes related to AD, while CSF‐tau exhibits high sensitivity and specificity in differentiating AD from normal aging [26, 27]. It is therefore important to note that AD diagnosis requires a multimodal approach that combines clinical assessment, cognitive testing, and biomarker evidence for accuracy. The novel AlzoSure assay investigated in the present study, was suggested to help diagnose AD dementia up to 6 years before a clinical diagnosis in the prodromal and preclinical stages [28].

We aimed to investigated the associations between this new assay and FDG‐PET, as the gold standard of brain hypometabolism and an early marker of prodromal and preclinical stages, in addition to other common AD biomarkers [13, 29, 30, 31].

Moreover, instead of proposing a stand‐alone predictive model, we integrated the AlzoSure Predict assay with other established diagnostic biomarkers including CSF biomarkers, demographic data, APOEε4 allele status, and neuroimaging findings, to evaluate their combined ability to differentiate MCI from AD.

Methods

Participants and Study Population

This cross‐sectional study analyzed data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI; http://adni.loni.usc.edu/), a public–private partnership launched in 2003 under the leadership of Principal Investigator Dr. Michael W. Weiner. ADNI aims to determine whether serial MRI, positron emission tomography (PET), fluid biomarkers, and comprehensive clinical and neuropsychological assessments can reliably track the progression of MCI and early AD.

Participants aged 55–90 years were eligible if they had completed neuroimaging, lumbar puncture, and longitudinal clinical evaluation. Key exclusion criteria included a Hachinski Ischemic Score > 4, recent or unapproved medication changes, a Geriatric Depression Scale score ≥ 6, and fewer than 6 years of formal education or equivalent occupational history. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria have been detailed previously [32].

MCI diagnosis was established according to one or more of the following criteria: (i) a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of 24–30, (ii) a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0.5 with a memory box score ≥ 0.5, or (iii) a Wechsler Logical Memory II score below education‐adjusted thresholds. All participants met the diagnostic standards outlined by the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association.

For the present analysis, only cognitively normal (CN) individuals and participants with MCI at baseline were included, provided complete data were available for FDG‐PET indices, U‐p53AZ levels, and CSF biomarkers. The data cleaning and selection workflow is illustrated in Figure 1.

Ethical Approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All ADNI data used were de‐identified, and the use of these data for research purposes was approved by the respective institutional review boards. Ethical approval and data use permission were obtained from ADNI and relevant bodies before analysis.

Consent to Participate

As this study utilized pre‐existing datasets obtained from an online repository using previously conducted research, direct informed consent from participants was not applicable. However, informed consent had been obtained during the original data collection, and all data were anonymized and deidentified prior to use in this study.

Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Assessments

A total of 416 CSF baseline samples from the ADNI cohort were analyzed for Aβ1 – 42, T‐tau, and p‐tau181 concentrations using the multiplex xMAP Luminex platform (Luminex Corp, Austin, TX). Quantification employed reagents from the INNO‐BIA AlzBio3 immunoassay kit (Innogenetics, Ghent, Belgium), specifically developed for research applications. This assay combines capture monoclonal antibodies and detector antibodies targeting each biomarker, conjugated to distinct color‐coded beads. Calibration curves were generated using buffered standards of known biomarker concentrations to ensure assay accuracy.

Assay reliability was confirmed through an interlaboratory validation study prior to analysis of ADNI and autopsy‐derived CSF samples, demonstrating coefficients of variation below 10% for CSF pool samples and under 7% for quality control samples. Additional validation using test–retest analysis of 29 randomly selected samples showed high reproducibility, with correlation coefficients (r 2) of 0.98 for t‐tau, 0.90 for Aβ1 – 42, and 0.85 for p‐tau181 [33, 34]. Further methodological details are available at http://www.adni-info.org/.

Alzosure Predict Plasma Biomarker Assessment

AlzoSure Predict is a validated in vitro diagnostic assay for quantifying U‐p53AZ in human plasma. The assay combines immunoprecipitation with the 2D3A8 monoclonal antibody and subsequent liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) detection. It is intended for use in individuals aged ≥ 50 years to identify those at risk of progression to AD.

Plasma samples were analyzed by Diadem SpA across eight sessions conducted between June and July 2022. Each session included ADNI test samples and three quality control (QC) samples obtained from the Australian Imaging, Biomarkers and Lifestyle (AIBL) biobank. Nine samples were excluded due to repeated assay failure.

Quantification relied on detection of the AZ284 peptide using a TSQ Vantage LC‐MS/MS system, with a standard curve generated from spiked calibrants ranging from 0.08 to 5 fmol/10 µL. Only results meeting predefined quality control criteria were retained, including: coefficient of determination (R 2) > 0.99 for calibration curves, > 90% recovery of synthetic U‐p53AZ, coefficient of variation (CV) < 10% for internal standard signals, and matrix effect deviation < 15%.

Repeatability and concordance of QC sample measurements complied with U.S. FDA bioanalytical method validation guidelines, confirming the assay's high precision and reliability across all testing sessions.

We selected data from the ADNI‐GO and ADNI‐2 cohorts because U‐p53AZ (AlzoSure Predict) measurements were available only in these phases, not in ADNI‐1 or ADNI‐3 (see ADNI: http://adni.loni.usc.edu). Based on the discovery of the unfolded conformation variant of p53 (U‐p53AZ) peptide in plasma, Diadem's AlzoSure, an FDA‐ and CE‐IVD‐approved blood test, has demonstrated promise in the early detection of AD [35].

FDG‐PET Meta ROI Definition and Processing

To identify hypometabolic regions indicative of pathological change in MCI and AD, we developed a set of meta‐regions of interest (Meta ROIs) based on prior research. These ROIs included Left/Right Angular Gyrus, Bilateral Posterior Cingulate, Left/Right Inferior Temporal Gyrus, which were binarized and combined into a single composite region for analysis. PET images were normalized in SPM to the MNI template, and mean FDG uptake within the composite MetaROI was intensity‐normalized by the mean of the top 50% of voxels in a hand‐drawn pons/cerebellar vermis reference region [36, 37].

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.11) using pandas (v2.2) for data management, scipy (v1.13) and statsmodels (v0.14) for statistical testing, and scikit‐learn (v1.5) for model fitting. Continuous variables were assessed for outliers within diagnostic groups using the interquartile range (IQR) method, with values exceeding 1.5 × IQR below the first quartile or above the third quartile excluded prior to analysis. Group comparisons between CN and MCI participants were performed using independent‐samples t tests for normally distributed variables and Mann–Whitney U tests for non‐normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using χ 2 tests. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For longitudinal analyses, within‐group changes from baseline to 24 months in FDG‐PET, Aβ1 – 42, t‐tau, p‐tau181, and ADAS‐13 scores were assessed separately for CN and MCI participants. Paired t‐tests were applied when both baseline and follow‐up distributions satisfied normality assumptions; otherwise, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used. Longitudinal results are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Associations between U‐p53AZ levels and neuroimaging, CSF biomarkers, and cognitive measures were evaluated using multivariable linear regression models stratified by diagnostic group. All models were adjusted for age, sex, and APOE ɛ4 carrier status. Model assumptions, including normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity, were systematically assessed. To account for multiple testing, p values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) approach (Benjamini–Hochberg procedure). The diagnostic performance of U‐p53AZ for distinguishing CN from MCI was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, and APOE ɛ4 carrier status. Discrimination was quantified with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves derived from the fitted logistic regression model. The area under the curve (AUC) was estimated with 95% confidence intervals obtained from 1000 bootstrap resamples. The optimal decision threshold was identified using Youden's index, with corresponding sensitivity and specificity reported. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Longitudinal within‐group comparisons revealed distinct patterns between CN and MCI participants (Figure 2). In the CN group, FDG‐PET SUVR showed a trend toward reduction over time (p = 0.051), while no significant change was observed in CSF Aβ1−42 (p = 0.171). By contrast, both CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181 significantly increased from baseline to follow‐up (t‐tau: p = 0.013; p‐tau181: p = 0.006). Cognitive performance improved modestly, with lower ADAS‐Cog 13 scores at follow‐up (p = 0.003). In the MCI group, FDG‐PET SUVR significantly declined over time (p = 0.040), accompanied by significant increases in CSF t‐tau (p = 0.005) and p‐tau181 (p < 0.001). No significant change was observed in CSF Aβ1−42 (p = 0.086) or ADAS‐Cog 13 (p = 0.506).

The discriminative ability of U‐p53AZ, adjusted for age, sex, and APOE ɛ4 status, to differentiate CN from MCI was assessed using ROC curve analysis (Figure 3). The model yielded an AUC of 0.617 (95% CI: 0.518–0.716), indicating modest diagnostic performance.

Discussion

We assessed the performance of the AlzoSure Predict assay in differentiating HC and MCI and found that higher AlzoSure values were associated with elevated CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181 at both baseline and follow‐up. In contrast, these values showed no association with CSF Aβ1–42, FDG‐PET, or cognition. AlzoSure's ability to discriminate CN from MCI was modest (AUC ≈ 0.62), characterized by high sensitivity but low specificity. Longitudinal analyses mirrored these patterns, with tau markers increasing over time, hypometabolism, Aβ1–42 remaining stable, and cognition showing slight improvement in CN but no change in MCI.

Previous work has reported promising prognostic performance for U‐p53AZ (or related conformational p53 measures), including multi‐year prediction of symptomatic AD in observational cohorts [28]. Our results partly agree with those findings, showing consistent associations with tau biomarkers. However, we could not reproduce strong diagnostic/prognostic utility in CN/MCI once standard covariates were controlled. Differences could reflect cohort composition (ADNI vs. AIBL and single‐center studies), or assay platform and batch structure.

Our FDG‐PET trajectories (decline over time in both groups) match the well‐described progressive hypometabolism in AD [38] and the classical link between FDG uptake and synaptic density [12]. However, U‐p53AZ did not correlate with FDG SUVR, implying that U‐p53AZ is not a surrogate for synaptic metabolic dysfunction captured by FDG imaging. This dissociation is consistent with models in which FDG‐PET reflects network‐level synaptic activity while blood p53 conformers reflect systemic cellular stress pathways.

Finally, our observation that CSF Aβ1–42 is comparatively stable while tau markers rise is consistent with the staging literature in which amyloid change plateaus earlier, whereas tauopathy and neurodegeneration continue to track clinical progression [24, 39]. Parallel evidence from plasma p‐tau studies underscores that tau‐related blood biomarkers (p‐tau181/217) show robust diagnostic and prognostic accuracy, often outperforming other plasma markers and approaching CSF and tau‐PET performance [40, 41, 42]. Together, these data suggest that if U‐p53AZ is used clinically, it may be most informative when combined with tau‐anchored biomarkers rather than used alone.

Practically, our results do not support using U‐p53AZ as a stand‐alone screening or prognostic test in CN/MCI. A more promising role may be in multi‐marker panels, for example, pairing U‐p53AZ with plasma p‐tau181/217 and simple clinical measures to refine enrichment for trials or risk stratification in memory clinics. Such an approach is aligned with current biomarker roadmaps emphasizing stepwise validation and clinical utility [24].

Strengths and Limitations

Several limitations merit caution: (i) modest sample size after strict completeness and outlier exclusions; (ii) limited longitudinal coverage for U‐p53AZ; (iii) potential batch/session effects despite QC; (iv) absence of tau‐PET to localize tau pathology; (v) residual confounding not captured by available covariates; and (vi) the small number of clinical converters (n = 21) limits statistical power for prognostic analyses and may have contributed to the modest AUC estimates.

Future Directions

Future studies should include larger longitudinal cohorts with serial assessments of U‐p53AZ and tau‐PET imaging to validate these findings. In addition, comprehensive multimarker models integrating U‐p53AZ with established plasma biomarkers, such as p‐tau181, p‐tau217, and NfL, along with relevant clinical variables, may improve the early diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease. The incremental value of U‐p53AZ within these models should be evaluated using measures of calibration, decision curve analysis, and net reclassification. Finally, further investigation into the mechanistic relationship between U‐p53AZ and tau phosphorylation and clearance pathways may provide additional insights into its role in Alzheimer's disease pathogenesis.

Conclusion

We showed that plasma U‐p53AZ was consistently associated with CSF tau but not with amyloid, FDG‐PET, or cognitive outcomes. Its modest ability to distinguish CN from MCI participants indicates limited value as an isolated biomarker. These findings suggest that U‐p53AZ may primarily reflect tau‐related processes and could be more informative when considered alongside established biomarkers. Additional multicenter studies with larger samples, longer follow‐up, and direct comparisons to current blood‐based measures are warranted to further clarify its role in AD research.
