 ABSTRACT
 A set of novel fuel concepts has been proposed for use in advanced low-
enriched uranium (LEU) systems that utilize powder metallurgical methods for
fabrication of the fuel forms. Preliminary tests demonstrated the ability to produce
powder but did not yield the desired quality to be used as feedstock in these
applications. This study seeks to establish an improved powder production method
for uranium-based alloys and evaluate the parameters required to refine the
particles produced by size and morphology. Powder samples were fabricated via
atomization and analyzed for particle morphology utilizing sieving methods and
scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Surrogate testing displayed improved
performance in producing small (<250 µm) spherical particles, and initial tests
demonstrated the initial capability to atomize uranium. Further work is required to
refine the atomization process to produce high quality uranium microspheres.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Advanced fuel concepts utilizing low-enriched uranium (LEU) have been of increasing interest for test
and power-producing reactors. To achieve higher power densities and reduced reactor core sizes using
traditional fuels such as UO2, higher levels of fuel enrichment are necessary, which creates proliferation
concerns. However, by pursuing fuel materials with higher densities of U atoms, high-power, compact-core
designs may be realized while still utilizing LEU. Such high-U-density fuel forms would be valuable for
both the current reactor fleet as well as next-generation small modular reactors and microreactors.
 Programs such as the Reduced Enrichment for Research and Test Reactors (RERTR) and High-
Performance Research Reactor (HPRR) programs have developed dispersion fuel concepts incorporating
fuel (e.g. U-xMo alloys, x=7, 10 wt% Mo) into a non-fissile matrix (e.g. aluminum) [1,2]. Fuel kernels of
approximately 150-250 µm in diameter were dispersed into the matrix at approximately 50% by volume
fuel meat [3-6]. Alternative matrix materials that can operate at higher temperatures, such as zirconium, or
contribute to the fissile material content, such as U22Zr20Nb58, may make these fuel systems a viable option
for power-producing reactors that operate at temperatures greater than 300°C [7-9].
 Fuel concepts utilizing the UN system have also been developed as accident tolerant fuel candidates
for commercial reactors due to the higher uranium density and thermal conductivity compared to UO2 [8].
The hydride-dehydride-nitriding process can utilize bulk metal uranium to synthesize UN powder. Then,
by incorporating a powder metallurgy process, monolithic UN specimens can be fabricated. However, some
studies have preferentially utilized atomized uranium feedstocks in attempt to improve the purity and
density of the final UN product [10].
 Advancements in additive manufacturing (AM) coupled with new, groundbreaking heat exchanger
(HX) technologies, hold the potential to significantly advance the state of the art in nuclear fuel fabrication
[11]. All AM methods applicable to nuclear fuel require high quality powder with a stringent size
distribution. Across industry, atomization is almost exclusively used to fabricate this powder feedstock.
Establishing uranium atomization capabilities is essential to enabling this new frontier of nuclear fuel
development.
 Additionally, powder metallurgy hot isostatic pressing (PM-HIP) shows promise for fabricating
complex geometries and consolidating multiple fuel systems of interest. Like the other manufacturing
methods noted here, PM-HIP requires consistent, quality powder feedstock to enable repeatable fabrication
results. Again, the industry standard for producing this powder is atomization and establishing uranium
capable atomizers is required for this manufacturing method to be successfully implemented.
 Ultimately, a refined process to produce powder of the desired size and shape is required for the
development of a fabrication method for these advanced fuel concepts. High quality powder feedstock
impacts the ability of the materials to consolidate, leading to improved mechanical and irradiation
properties. Previous work, discussed in Section 2, was unable to produce powder of the desired quality and
efficiency due to the limitations of the available rotating electrode system (RES) [12]. A custom RES
system was developed to enable finer control over the process parameters, such as power, feed speed, and
rotational speed. This custom system will produce smaller molten droplets during atomization and better
accommodate materials with varying thermal properties. This report describes the design of a vertical RES
and operational test results of the vertical RES with surrogate and uranium specimens.

 2. BACKGROUND
 The bulk material properties of the proposed fissile matrix (U22Zr20Nb58, U-43.28Nb-14.65Zr, wt%)
were studied previously under the Advanced LEU (ALEU) program [9]. Preliminary studies sought to
evaluate the properties (including microstructure, particle size, and geometry) of the matrix (U-Nb-Zr) and
fuel (U-xMo) alloys in the powder form [12].
 Arc-cast rods of each alloy were atomized into powder using a RES, previously designed as part of the
RERTR program, with a catch pan approximately 15” in diameter in an argon atmosphere glovebox, shown
in Figure 1 [13]. The atomized powders were sieved to >250 µm and <250 µm. Preliminary powder
production tests resulted in a low yield (15-30%) of powder <250 µm when compared to the starting pin
mass. A variety of particle shapes, both spherical and irregularly shaped, were retrieved, as shown in Figure
2 for U-xMo particles [12].

 The preliminary tests also showed a variation in both particle size and shape with different applied
power used during the atomization process. This was tested on the U-Nb-Zr alloy at 3 different current
settings, with resulting particle size distribution and particle morphology shown in Figures 3 and 4,
respectively. While the highest current setting did produce a higher yield of particles <250 µm, the
distribution as well as sphericity of the particles were also impacted to yield less desirable results, as the
smaller, more spherical particles are desirable for consolidation methods [12].

 A RES was also developed at Texas A&M University (TAMU), shown in Figure 5, [14] using a

Additional design configurations were incorporated to optimize the process for fabrication of higher melting
temperature uranium alloys (i.e. U-xZr). This design was used as the basis for an INL horizontal RES.

 3. EXPERIMENTAL
 3.1 Development of the INL Horizontal RES
3.1.1 System Design
 The configuration of the horizontal RES atomizer was designed to minimize the footprint for use inside
of a radiological glovebox, as well as be able to move in and out of the glovebox transfer ports with minimal
assembly/disassembly required. The main improvement factored in this design was the ability to pre-set
atomization parameters, such as current, feed speed, and rotational speed. Fine control of the process would
offer reproducibility that was not achievable with its predecessor. Other design considerations include
compatibility with varying feedstock rod sizes, ease of feedstock loading and powder collection, and
rotational speed requirements. The CAD rendering of the final configuration fabricated at INL is shown in
Figure 6.

 4

 Figure 6. CAD rendering of new lab-scale atomizer fabricated at INL.

 This lab-scale atomizer consists of an Air Turbine Motors 740XP air turbine mounted to a stepper-
driven linear slide. An adjustable collet was attached to the air turbine shaft in a horizontal orientation. A
tungsten inert gas (TIG) welding electrode is configured in a horizontal orientation on the door of the
collection chamber, in line with the pin to be atomized, and powered by a Miller Maxstar 800 GTAW power
supply. A solenoid, also mounted on the door of the collection chamber, actuates the electrode to establish
an arc between the electrode and pin. Jogging of the linear slide, solenoid actuation, welding power, and
the atomization process are controlled externally via a three-button pendant and foot pedal. These settings
can be adjusted using the touchscreen of the control box (timing settings), the potentiometer located inside
the control box (linear feed speed), and the welding power supply interface. Following atomization, cooled
particles would fall to the bottom of the atomization chamber into a removable catch cup.

3.1.2 Benchtop Operational Testing
 Prior to installation of the RES atomizer into a glovebox, operational testing was performed using the
benchtop configuration shown in Figure 7. Plexiglass shields were placed around the system to prevent
contact with electrical components and contain any particles that may escape the catch chamber.
Atomization was performed using copper rod of varying dimensions. Two diameters were tested, 0.25in
and 0.375in. Multiple lengths were tested, with 2 in. being the final rod length based on fabrication
limitations with uranium use.

rotational speed of 40000 rpm was selected and anticipated to be able to yield particle sizes <75 μm [17].

The resulting powder batches were sieved into ranges suitable for the various manufacturing methods of
choice. Benchtop testing was concluded when optimal settings were determined for each variable with

 3.2 RES Installation and Atomization in Glovebox
3.2.1 Installation of the RES
 The new RES atomizer was installed into an Ar atmosphere glovebox, which requires shared water-
cooled power feedthrough between the atomizer and a collocated Centorr single arc furnace. As the coolant
is required for the arc furnace but not the atomizer, the atomizer leads clamps can be easily
connected/disconnected at the inner feedthrough interface. Due to this configuration, these two systems
cannot be operated simultaneously. Additional gas feedthroughs were installed to supply argon to the air
turbine of the atomizer. This configuration is shown in Figure 8. While the system was designed to fit into
the glovebox transfer port, due to the complexity of the previous system to be removed and installation of
feedthroughs, the window of the glovebox was removed and replaced after installation.

3.2.2 Preliminary Atomization Tests in Ar Glovebox
 Copper rods were used as a surrogate test material for the atomizer configuration described in Section
3.2.1, and using the variable settings determined during the benchtop testing, described in Section 3.1.2.
The parameters were adjusted as needed to account for differences introduced by operation in an argon
atmosphere versus air. Once the atomizer operation was demonstrated with copper, development of a
process for uranium atomization was able to proceed.
 Uranium rods were cast via arc-melting in the Centorr 5SA single arc furnace. Depleted uranium rods
two inches in length and either 0.25in or 0.375in in diameter were fabricated via arc-melting in the Centorr
5SA single arc furnace. These rods were atomized per the parameters listed in Table 2. Due to the physical
restrictions and administrative requirements to working with radiological material in a glovebox, fewer

 3.3 Characterization Techniques
 Particle morphology was evaluated via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with back-scattered
electron imaging (BSE). Samples of copper powder were sieved placed on an adhesive mount, and analyzed
using a JOEL IT-500HR SEM. Uranium powders were sieved to <250um, sampled to de minimis quantities
in an argon glovebox, and placed on an adhesive mount, and analyzed using a Phenom XL SEM.
 Particle size distribution was determined for select runs by manual sieving the atomized product using
sieve mesh sizes of 60, 100, 140, 200, and 400 μm. The mass was measured of the powders remaining at
each mesh size. Atomization yields per feedstock rod were evaluated by percent of powder in the desired
particle size range, defined here as <250 μm, to the total powder produced (percent yield of atomized) and
to the total initial pin mass (percent yield of total pin).

 4. RESULTS
 4.1 Benchtop Testing of the Horizontal RES
 The first set of operational tests were performed using 0.375” diameter copper feedstock rods, feed
rates between 0.077-0.187 in/s, and current settings of 60-100A. A sufficient melt pool upon initialization
was unable to be formed and runs were terminated to prevent the feedstock rods from running into the
tungsten electrode. A ‘spindle delay’ was programmed following initiation of rotation and the arc to allow
the copper to heat and melt across the entire cross-section and reach full rotational speed before advancing
the feedstock forward. A delay of 1000 ms produced a good melt pool using 90A by the time the feed
started.
 Next, the feed rate needed to be balanced with the power applied such that a stable melt pool could be
maintained and atomized at the same rate the feedstock was advanced. A feed rate of 0.077 in/s was too

 7

slow and created too large a gap between the rod and the electrode. At 0.134 in/s, the melt pool became
unstable, forming large melt ligaments and film disintegration and causing the arc to fail. This improved
with a speed of 0.100 in/s, but a significant amount of copper splatter was observed on the back wall of the
chamber, as pictured in Figure 9, indicating the atomization was occurring too far back in the chamber, and
forming large melt ligaments in the process. The tungsten tip was progressively shortened from 0.82” to
0.6” to shift the position, improving but not eliminating the formation of these large deposits.

 Figure 9. Atomization chamber during operation (left) and immediately following
 operation (right).

 Fewer large particles were produced upon increasing the current to 100A, but this also required the feed
rate to be increased. At 0.173 in/s, the feed rate was high enough that the melt pool went inside the feed
piece and "cupped" it. The arc continued to melt and atomize until the entire tungsten tip was nearly inside
the copper, at which point the run was stopped. The current was increased to 110A and yielded the first
complete atomization run. While some scrap was still stuck to the back and sides of the chamber, much of
the material appeared to be fine particulates, as pictured in Figure 10. A second test was run with these
parameters with similar but further improved results.

 Figure 10. Copper powder produced from atomization

 Copper rods of 0.25” diameter were also tested for atomization. The current was reduced to 70A to
accommodate the smaller surface area and prevent it from melting too quickly. Copper plating observed on
the chamber and components behind the chamber also reduced with the decreasing power. At settings of
60A and 0.173 in/s, the melt rate and feed rate appeared to be well balanced and yielded fine powder with
little residue on the chamber walls. Additional runs were performed at 60A to examine reproducibility, as
well as a trial run using current of 65A with a high frequency start function versus lift-arc.
 The resulting parameters of the successful atomization runs and resulting yields are shown in Table 3,
and the particle size distribution of the atomized product is shown in Figure 11. The yield of <250 um
particles improved with the use of smaller diameter feedstock pin, which is consistent with observations of
the improved stability of the melt pool across a smaller area versus larger. There was also a significant shift
in the particle size distribution as more fine particles were produced using the smaller feedstock rod, which
is more desirable for many manufacturing techniques.
 Upon comparing runs performed with consistent parameters, atomization yields were repeatable within
+/- 5.0%. The overall yield when compared to the mass of the feedstock pin may be further improved with
the use of a longer feedstock, a capability introduced in the horizontal configuration from the vertical. A
longer copper pin (0.25” diameter, 4” long) was also tested, but began to bend upon rotation. This behavior
may improve with stronger materials but may also present as a limitation of the system.

 4.2 Initial Glovebox Testing of the Horizontal RES
 Atomization results inside the glovebox varied significantly from the benchtop results for the same
input variables. This is largely attributed to the difference in atmosphere based on the differences in thermal
conductivity between argon and air. In addition, the differences in the welding power supply lead path from
the benchtop to the glovebox had an unexpectedly large impact on performance. For a given amperage and
voltage, less power appeared to be delivered to the rod in the glovebox. The suspected cause is the addition
of glovebox feedthroughs, increased length of the leads, and the increased complexity of the current path
due to the arc melter’s lines. Atomization parameters were adjusted to match glovebox copper yields to
benchtop copper yields, resulting in an increase in current to 160A, then the switch in feedstock was made
to depleted uranium.

 Figure 13. Atomized copper product from atomization testing in an Ar glovebox.

 Uranium rods were able to be atomized for the full length of the available rod, pictured in Figure 14,
with current and linear feed rate settings varying between 120-160A and 0.173-0.187 in/s, respectively.
While the atomization was complete, these tests did not produce a significant yield of particles <250 μm.
A small selection of particles were imaged via SEM, shown in Figure 15. Some particles exhibited a high
degree of sphericity, but many retained a tail. This feature may be formed when a particle resolidifies too
quickly during or after release from the melt pool as it does not have time to fully form to a sphere while
still molten and in-flight. This behavior is also likely related to the formation of larger particles caused by
a higher surface tension of uranium versus copper [18, 19].

 5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
 Initial atomization tests of the horizontal RES atomizer using copper and uranium rods demonstrated
operational capabilities of the new configuration. While the new system did produce higher yields of
particles than observed with its predecessor, the use of a copper surrogate in this study versus uranium does
not allow for a direct comparison. The main performance improvement of this design observed during
operational testing was reproducibility. Preliminary atomization yields using a copper surrogate were
repeatable within +/- 5.0% and more consistency in the particle size distribution. The consistency observed
with finer control over parameters enables the development of processes to tailor production of particle
sizes based on desirability for different fabrication processes.
 Atomization in the glovebox environment was notably different from the benchtop, and thus further
development was required to find the correct variable settings. The main difference is the atmosphere —
argon in the glovebox and air on the benchtop. The thermal conductivity of argon is significantly lower
than that of air, which in turn significantly slows the cooling rate of the atomized material. This changes
how the rods melt, eject from the rod, break apart in-flight, and solidifiy before contacting the containment
walls. While spherical particles of uranium were produced, atomization parameters need to be optimized to
refine the particle size and geometry. However, due to failure of the glovebox containing the atomizer, a
parametric study was unable to be performed.
 This atomizer is in the process of relocation to an independent glovebox, which will introduce the
capability of running in inert atmospheres of Ar, He, or Ar/He mixtures. The increase in thermal
conductivity of helium compared to argon offers faster cooling rates of particles in-flight and may result in
a higher yield of fine, spherical particles of higher melting point materials. In addition, the glovebox will
be configured with an independent arc-melting furnace, such that the systems may be operated
simultaneously for more efficient casting and atomizing of materials. This will reduce scheduling impacts
from other ongoing activities. Upon relocation of the atomizer, a parametric study will be performed with
the goal of demonstrating similar trends in performance for uranium as observed during operation with the
copper surrogate. An optimization of the process parameters may be performed with various uranium alloys
to develop a more effective cooling and solidification process and yield more consistent and spherical
particles.
