Clinical and Demographic Characteristics in Schizophrenia Patients with and without a History of Taking Long-acting Injectable Antipsychotics: A Comparative Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Seyyed Javad Boskabadi, Mehran Zarghami, Arghavan Fariborzifar, Mahmood Moosazadeh, Narjes Hendouei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Poor adherence to oral antipsychotics is an important challenge in schizophrenia, highlighting long-acting injectables (LAIs) as an alternative. This study aimed to compare the clinical and demographic profiles of patients with schizophrenia with prior LAI exposure versus those maintained exclusively on oral antipsychotics.Methods:This cross-sectional, observational study enrolled eligible schizophrenia inpatients aged 18–65 diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition criteria from June 2022 to October 2023. Participants were divided into two groups: those with prior LAI exposure and those who had maintained only oral antipsychotics. Demographic data, clinical history, and baseline laboratory parameters were collected. The primary outcome was the severity of psychotic symptoms, assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) and Clinical Global Impression Severity (CGI-S) Scale upon admission.Findings:A total of 70 patients were included in the final analysis, with 36 patients having a history of LAI and 34 on only oral treatment. Demographically, both groups were comparable across most characteristics. Baseline PANSS score and its subscales, CGI-S scores, and laboratory markers showed no statistical difference. However, the LAI group showed a significantly longer duration of illness (P = 0.017), more hospitalizations (P < 0.001), and greater prior exposure to first-generation antipsychotics (P < 0.001) with higher chlorpromazine equivalent doses (P < 0.001) compared to the oral-only group.Conclusion:This study highlights treatment patterns among Iranian schizophrenia patients, showing that patients on LAIs generally have a history of longer illness durations, more hospitalizations, and more expose to first generation antipsychotics. Further studies should be conducted to investigate the efficacy of early versus delayed initiation of LAI and their long-term outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Schizophrenia is a severe, chronic, and disabling mental disorder affecting about 1% of the world’s population. Its persistent nature requires long-term treatment with antipsychotic medication, which appears effective in preventing relapse.[1]

Antipsychotic medications are available in two main forms: oral antipsychotics and long-acting injectable formulations (LAIs). Oral antipsychotics provide greater flexibility in dose adjustment and easier discontinuation, making them appropriate for early treatment stages or patients with good insight.[23] However, medication adherence is often significantly poor among individuals with schizophrenia. Nonadherence to treatment, observed in both early and later stages of the illness, poses a major challenge in managing schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders.[4]

Adherence to medication in schizophrenia depends on various factors, including personal and social variables (such as age, gender, and education), disease-specific factors, the patient’s perception and attitude towards the illness, treatment-related aspects, and the patient’s overall health status.[56] Consistently, poor adherence to oral antipsychotics is linked to higher relapse rates and more hospital stays.[7]

LAIs have emerged as a critical intervention to address adherence barriers in patients with poor compliance and insight. Compared to oral antipsychotics, LAIs provide more stable plasma drug levels through scheduled injections and eliminate the requirement for daily dosing, thereby markedly enhancing treatment adherence.[8] Some evidence suggests that LAI use is associated with reduced re-hospitalization rates, improved long-term outcomes, particularly among patients with poor insight.[910]

The efficacy of LAIs in reducing relapse rates has been reported;[1112] however, some studies show no significant difference between LAIs and oral medications in preventing relapse.[13] Understanding the clinical status of patients with a history of LAI use compared to those who have never received this treatment, especially during an acute relapse, requires careful examination. Since previous treatment history (LAI vs. oral) may indicate underlying differences in illness severity, treatment adherence, or clinician decision processes, it is crucial to isolate the effect of this variable from established predictors of severity, such as total duration of disorder or cumulative antipsychotic exposure.

This cross-sectional study aims to assess the association between previous treatments and baseline symptom severity between two distinct groups (the prior LAI exposure and the oral-only history). Furthermore, we explored the role of treatment history as an important covariate in this initial evaluation and determined the effect of the frequency of treatment switching on overall clinical outcomes.

METHODS

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted among patients diagnosed with schizophrenia from June 2022 to October 2023. This study was a branch of a cohort study that was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Mazandaran University of Medical Science with ethical number IR.MAZUMS.REC.1401.225. Participants were recruited from inpatient psychiatric units at Zare Hospital in Sari, northern Iran.

Inclusion criteria included age between 18 and 65 years, schizophrenia diagnosis confirmed by at least two psychiatrists according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria[14], available comprehensive information of their disease history and antipsychotic treatments, and have received either oral antipsychotic medications or LAIs in the 12 months prior to the study. Exclusion criteria include having other psychiatric illnesses in addition to schizophrenia, current substance dependence, or substance use in the last three months before this hospitalization, or a positive urine screen for substances during the current hospitalization (except for nicotine agents).

Demographic and clinical characteristics were recorded, including age, gender, marital status, age at initial diagnosis, family history of psychiatric disorders, and total duration of psychotic illness. Detailed information regarding treatment history, such as the number and type of antipsychotic medications currently prescribed, was also recorded. The required information was obtained through interviews with the patients’ families and then matched with the previous medical records.

The duration of antipsychotic treatment was based on the total duration of therapy with any antipsychotic from the start of the disorder. The dosage of the last received antipsychotic was expressed in chlorpromazine equivalents.

Medication adherence is connected to the social and treatment factors of a patient.[15] Patient adherence was evaluated based on the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale,[16] family reports, and a review of previous hospitalization records, and then based on the results from these three sources, they were classified into four categories based on their medication adherence.

(1) Adequate adherence: Patients who voluntarily take over 70% of their daily medications without any pressure. (2) Compulsion: Patients who receive medication forcibly from care centers or their families. (3) Relative acceptance: Patients who take <70% of their medications voluntarily, sometimes requiring coercion and facing challenges. (4) No adherence: Patients who stop taking medications entirely shortly after hospital discharge, despite family efforts to persuade them.

To assess the metabolic status of the participants, baseline laboratory investigations were performed on all subjects within 24 h after hospitalization. These included fasting blood glucose levels, complete blood count, and lipid profile measurements (total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, and triglycerides). All laboratory procedures were conducted in the hospital’s central laboratory using standardized biochemical methods under quality-controlled conditions.

Patients were categorized into two main groups according to their history of using LAIs.

Oral-only history group: Patients who have never received any LAIs before or during the current hospitalizationLAI exposure group: Patients who have previously received at least one course of treatment with LAI prior to their current hospitalization.

The primary outcome was the severity of psychotic symptoms, which were assessed using validated rating scales: the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)[1718] to measure the intensity of positive, negative, and general psychopathology symptoms, and the Clinical Global Impression Severity (CGI-S) scale[19] to evaluate overall illness severity. Symptom severity was assessed on the 1st day of hospitalization by a psychiatrist and a clinical pharmacist. Furthermore, laboratory sampling was conducted on the same day to reduce variability caused by time-related factors. The secondary outcome was the frequency and number of different antipsychotics, the pattern of antipsychotic use during the current hospitalization (oral or LAI), any possible link to previous treatment history, and the association between how often antipsychotics were switched and overall clinical outcomes.

The sampling method for this cross-sectional study was census-based. The sample size was based on the availability and eligibility of the population who were admitted to the psychiatric hospital, and it was not possible to calculate the sample size based on the power of the test.

Collected data were entered into a structured database and statistically analyzed using SPSS Software (version 25; IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of all data. Qualitative variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while quantitative variables were reported as means ± standard deviation (SD). The independent sample t-test was utilized to compare the PANSS score and its subscales, metabolic parameters, and other parametric data. Categorical variables, including CGI-S scores, were compared using the Chi-square. P values below 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients

We evaluated 152 patients who met the eligibility criteria. We excluded 63 patients who did not fully comply with the inclusion criteria (e.g., other diagnosis rather than schizophrenia, older age). Additionally, 11 patients were excluded from the study due to a lack of historical treatment data, eight patients had acute use of substances, and the final participants were 70 patients.

The demographic and clinical features of both groups at baseline were compared. Table 1 summarizes these comparisons. No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups regarding current age, sex, residence, education, weight, marital status, or age at schizophrenia diagnosis. However, the LAI group showed a significantly longer duration of schizophrenia and more previous hospitalizations (P = 0.017). Medication adherence showed a trend toward significance. Specifically, the LAI group had a lower rate of “adequate adherence” (2.8%) and a higher rate of “no adherence” (77.8%).

Treatment history

The LAI group showed a higher total number of antipsychotic medications received, and greater prior exposure to first-generation antipsychotics (FGA), with a higher average chlorpromazine equivalent. In the LAI history group, 66.7% of patients had received more than four different antipsychotics in the past.

Risperidone, perphenazine, and haloperidol were the most commonly prescribed antipsychotics received by our patients, respectively. At least 80% of total patients received risperidone. Fluphenazine decanoate was prescribed slightly more often than flupentixol [Table 4].

Pattern of antipsychotic classes and current treatment switching

Table 5 displays the most recent treatment and the current treatment. Regarding the current pharmacotherapy initiated during this admission, no significant difference in the pattern of FGAs versus second-generation antipsychotics (SGA) use was found between patients with and without prior LAI exposure (P = 0.241). In patients with a history of LAI exposure, 50% were receiving only FGAs; in contrast, 58.8% of those in the oral-only group. Overall, only 25% of the study population started treatment with SGAs during this hospitalization [Table 5]. This higher proportion of FGA probably reflects historical prescribing habits rather than a clear clinical preference or differences in effectiveness. LAIs have traditionally been dominated by FGAs, such as flupentixol and fluphenazine decanoate, which may explain the numerical difference.

DISCUSSION

Several researchers have examined the patterns of LAIs across different populations.[2021] To our knowledge, there is only one study on the use of LAIs in a clinical sample of Iranian schizophrenia patients.[22] The novelty of our study is that it presents the first comprehensive study highlighting the patterns related to LAIs and oral treatments among a sample of Iranian schizophrenia patients admitted to a psychiatric hospital. This approach helps us examine the relationship between previous LAI exposure, treatment history, and the initial severity of symptoms.

There were no differences between the two groups (oral vs. LAI treatment) in terms of demographic characteristics. Ahmadkhaniha et al., in a cohort study, assessed the relapse rate among an Iranian sample of schizophrenia patients treated with oral atypical antipsychotics and LAI typical antipsychotics. There was no statistically significant difference between the study groups in terms of age, sex, family history, marital status, or substance abuse.[22] We observed that the male-to-female ratio was 1.9, indicating a higher hospitalization rate among men. The gender differences in schizophrenia vary across different areas.[2324] Safiri et al. conducted a systematic review to assess the prevalence of schizophrenia, considering factors such as sex, age, and socio-demographics in the Middle East and North Africa from 1990 to 2019. Patients aged 35–39 years had the highest prevalence, with males generally showing a higher prevalence across most age groups.[25] Substance use during their lifetime was reported in 44.3% of all our patients. Methamphetamine, morphine, and cannabis were the most frequently used substances. In a recent study conducted at our center, substance use among patients admitted to the psychiatric emergency department was examined. The results indicate that the most frequently used substances by patients were morphine, methamphetamine, methadone, and cannabis.[26]

The LAI group experienced a significantly longer duration of schizophrenia and reported more previous hospitalizations. This pattern of complexity is well-supported by data on prior medication histories. Some previous research showed that patients on LAIs tended to be older and had experienced the disease for a longer period.[27] Furthermore, our findings align with those from Asian countries. Chao Tian Tang examined LAI usage patterns across 15 Asian nations and discovered that patients on LAI were significantly older, experienced more hospitalizations, and often received multiple antipsychotics compared to those on oral medications.[28]

Many of our patients in the LAIs group had taken various antipsychotics, with 66.7% having received more than four different antipsychotics (P < 0.001). They also had greater exposure to the entire class of FGA. It should be noted that the medication history is based on the patient’s overall medical history. Therefore, the patient may have taken one medication during one period and a different medication at another time. Reasons for switching antipsychotics may include side effects, ineffectiveness, relapse, or preferences of the patient and family.[2930] Frequent switching of antipsychotics, regardless of patient compliance, response, or side effects, can lead to higher healthcare resource utilization and increased costs,[31] raising the risk of relapse,[32] and more hospitalization (relapse) is linked to treatment resistance.[33]

Our results include assessing the severity of psychotic symptoms at baseline. We measured the severity of psychotic symptoms using the PANSS and the CGI-S scale. This design enables us to determine the relationship between prior LAI’s exposure history and the initial severity level of symptoms. The two groups demonstrated comparable overall symptom severity. However, the LAI group tended to present with numerically higher symptoms. This finding is important because it suggests that, at the time of assessment, the acute clinical need, as measured by standardized symptom scales, was similar in both groups. Haya Ascher-Svanum et al. evaluated treatment approaches and clinical features in schizophrenia patients starting on LAI typical antipsychotics. They found no significant differences in symptom severity based on PANSS scores and their subscales between the LAI and oral groups.[34] This indicates that the advantages of LAIs might be more noticeable in particular patient groups or real-world clinical environments, and that baseline symptom severity may not differ significantly among treatment groups.

Several LAI antipsychotics are approved for schizophrenia treatment, but their availability differs by region. Typically, FGAs available in our country include fluphenazine, flupentixol, and, to a lesser extent, haloperidol decanoate, while limited supplies of the second-generation long-acting risperidone have recently become available. Regarding current pharmacotherapy initiated during this admission, no significant difference in the pattern of antipsychotic class use (FGA vs. SGA) was found. The high prevalence of FGA use in the current regimen (50% in the LAI group) likely reflects historical prescribing habits, as LAIs like flupentixol and fluphenazine decanoate have traditionally been FGA-based. Furthermore, 27.8% of the LAI group received an oral antipsychotic combination during this hospitalization. Recent research from Australia observed that about 40% of schizophrenia patients received combination oral and LAI therapy.[35]

One of the important findings of our study was that 35.3% of the oral-only group (no history of LAIs) transitioned to an LAI during this acute episode (new LAI initiators), indicating an active re-evaluation of treatment strategy during hospitalization, likely prompted by the complexity suggested by their history. Conversely, over half (52.8%) of those with a prior LAI history had not started an LAI by the time of this assessment.

It is important to decide the appropriate timing for introducing LAIs into schizophrenia treatment.[36] The decision to initiate an LAI during the current hospitalization in our patients was not associated with their previous treatment history or disease severity. The choice to switch to or begin LAI therapy during a single admission is influenced more by immediate clinical needs than by the patient’s entire treatment history. Physicians often select LAIs based on the underlying factors such as the patient’s adherence, the patient or their family’s requests, and the possible adverse events profile. Moreover, logistical and pharmacological factors like injection schedules, fear or hesitation about the injections, ease of accessing clinics, LAI cost, and insurance coverage are considered when deciding to switch from oral medications to LAIs.[373839] Possible side effects are important barriers because long-acting medications cannot be quickly removed if adverse effects suddenly develop.[40] However, a recent study showed that physicians who used LAIs more frequently tended to be more familiar with their associated adverse events.[37]

Studies showed that clinicians should consider offering LAIs early in the illness course, especially when adherence is uncertain, or risk factors for relapse are present.[41] A patient who has previously been noncompliant with medication is more likely to experience the same issue again later. Previous research indicated that many patients still have adequate decision-making ability regarding their treatment.[42] Typically, LAIs were prescribed for patients with poor treatment adherence. Studies suggest that LAIs can be initiated during the first episode of the disease.[43] A study found that first-time hospitalized patients with schizophrenia who started LAI treatment had a lower risk of readmission when they continued therapy (Hazard ratio = 0.88); however, early discontinuation of injectable treatment was linked to an increased in-hospital use of LAIS and an increased readmission risk in patients with first-admission.[44] In another study, treatment-naïve schizophrenia patients received either oral haloperidol or LAI for 12 weeks. The baseline severity of symptoms based on PANSS scores was similar, while the LAI group showed higher adherence and significantly better quality of life compared to the oral group.[45] These findings highlight the significance of continuing LAI therapy, since stopping it early may result in a quicker relapse.[46]

This study has important limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, no follow-up was performed for these patients after initiation or continuation of treatment; therefore, this research does not assess treatment efficacy but instead focuses on the correlation between treatment history and the patient’s clinical status at the time of acute presentation. We can only demonstrate association, not causality. For instance, the longer illness duration in the LAI group may be a confounder that needs to be addressed in future research. Second, we were unable to verify the reasons for prescribing LAIs or oral antipsychotics or for stopping each medication in previous episodes for each patient (personal decision of the psychiatrist, patient desire, side effect, etc.), and its implications for interpretation. On the other hand, some drugs were unavailable at certain times because of sanctions and access restrictions. Consequently, it was not possible to determine if these restrictions influenced the decision to switch to other antipsychotics for individual patients. Third, our patients only had access to first-generation LAIs, so we could not compare them with second-generation injectable drugs. Fourth, our sample size was relatively small, so drawing broad, definitive results must be approached with caution.

CONCLUSION

This study assessed treatment characteristics and clinical profiles of Iranian schizophrenia patients receiving either LAI or oral antipsychotics. The results revealed no differences in demographic factors or baseline symptom severity; however, LAI users had a longer illness duration, more hospitalizations, and greater prior exposure to various antipsychotics. These findings suggest that LAIs are often introduced later in the disease progression, usually for patients with complex or long-term treatment histories. While this study was descriptive and cross-sectional, the link found between starting LAI therapy and a history of complex pharmacological management, rather than acute symptom severity, sets an important benchmark for clinical practice in Iran. This indicates that current decision-making processes might be delayed in adopting LAI treatments for suitable patients. The results of this study could impact clinical practices and support a more evidence-based application of LAI antipsychotics.

Future prospective long-term research, with a larger sample size, should address the impact of LAI treatment across different healthcare environments. In particular, determining causal relationships, it would be interesting to assess clinical outcomes like starting in early episodes, long-term mortality, suicide attempts, the change in the number of hospitalizations before and after starting LAI treatment, change in symptom severity, and psychotic relapses compared to patients receiving oral antipsychotics.
