Bilateral Impaction of Mandibular Second and Third Molars With Lingual Cortical Plate Perforation: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Yahya Fayaz, Muhammad Jalal Ud Din Torani, Mustafa Sherzai, Ahmad Seyar Sharifi, Mir Hamza Zaland, Shahab Uddin Ahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBilateral impaction of mandibular second and third molars with severe lingual displacement is exceptionally rare. CBCT is essential for accurately assessing tooth orientation, lingual cortical plate perforation, and proximity to the inferior alveolar canal, thereby facilitating diagnosis, surgical planning, and risk assessment.Keywords: bilateral impaction, cone beam computed tomography, cortical plate perforation, linguoversion, mandibular second molar, mandibular third molar

Introduction

Tooth eruption is the physiological movement of a tooth from its developmental position within the jaw to its functional position in the oral cavity. Disturbances of this process may result from systemic or local factors and can lead to ectopic eruption, retention, or impaction. Impaction occurs when eruption is prevented by a physical obstacle or an abnormal eruption pathway and may be associated with factors such as odontomas, supernumerary teeth, crowding, or ectopic tooth position [1].

Mandibular second molar impaction is an uncommon eruption disturbance, with a reported prevalence of 0.65%–2.0%. It occurs more frequently in the mandible than the maxilla, is more common in males, and typically presents as a unilateral mesioangular impaction [1]. Simultaneous bilateral impaction of the mandibular second and third molars is exceptionally rare. To the best of the authors' knowledge, only three comparable cases have been reported in the literature [1, 2, 3].

Impacted mandibular second molars may result in complications affecting adjacent teeth and supporting structures, making early diagnosis important for timely intervention [4]. Because treatment depends on factors such as the patient's age, tooth position, and severity of impaction, management should be individualized, and no single treatment approach is universally applicable [1].

The present report describes an unusual case of bilateral impaction of mandibular second and third molars evaluated using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), highlighting the three‐dimensional characteristics of impactions and their relationship to adjacent anatomical structures that influenced surgical planning.

Case Report

A 23‐year‐old Afghan male was referred to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at the National Curative and Specialized Stomatology Hospital, Kabul, for evaluation and management of bilaterally impacted mandibular second and third molars. The patient was medically fit and had no history of significant systemic disease or syndromic conditions associated with eruption disturbances.

The patient was asymptomatic at presentation, and the impactions were detected incidentally during orthodontic assessment. At the time of referral, he was undergoing active orthodontic treatment in the maxillary arch for spacing in the anterior segment.

Extraoral examination revealed no facial asymmetry or swelling. Intraoral examination confirmed that the mandibular second and third molars were not clinically visible. No significant crowding or space deficiency was observed in the mandibular arch.

A panoramic radiograph obtained before referral (Figure 1) demonstrated bilateral impaction of the mandibular second and third molars (teeth 37, 38, 47, and 48). To further evaluate the three‐dimensional orientation of the impacted teeth and their relationship to adjacent anatomical structures, CBCT was performed using a VATECH CBCT unit with a field of view of 110 × 90 mm, 0.3‐mm isotropic voxel size, 84 kVp, 7.8 mA, and a dose –area product of 4.57 dGy·cm2. Images were reconstructed and analyzed using Ez3D‐I software (version 5.2.6.1; EWOOSOFT Co. Ltd., Korea).

Panaromic view of the patient.

CBCT evaluation demonstrated bilateral severe lingual displacement of the mandibular second molars. On the right side, tooth 47 exhibited marked linguoversion and was positioned inferior to tooth 48, with its crown directed lingually. Tooth 48 was horizontally impacted with a near‐transverse orientation, the crown positioned lingually and roots directed buccally. The position of tooth 48 appeared to mechanically obstruct the eruption pathway of tooth 47 (Figures 2 and 3). On the left side, tooth 37 was also severely lingually inclined and positioned slightly inferior to tooth 38, which was horizontally impacted with mesial crown angulation, contributing to obstruction of the eruption path of tooth 37 (Figures 4 and 3).

Sagittal view of the right side mandible showing the lingoversion second molar (red circle) with lingual cortical palate perforation (white arrow).

Axial view of the mandible showing the perforation of bilateral lingual cortex (white arrows).

Sagittal view of the left side mandible showing the lingoversion second molar (red circle) with inferior alveolar canal attached to the rooth of second molar (white arrow).

CBCT further revealed clinically significant anatomical relationships. On the left side, the roots of tooth 37 were in intimate contact with the inferior alveolar canal, with loss of the intervening corticated bony boundary (Figure 4). In addition, disruption of the lingual cortical plate was observed bilaterally: on the right side, both tooth 47 and the lingually positioned cusp of tooth 48 were associated with cortical perforation, while on the left side, lingual cortical perforation was present in relation to tooth 37, whereas the cortical plate adjacent to tooth 38 remained intact (Figure 3).

Management

After comprehensive clinical and radiographic evaluation, the available options, including orthodontic uprighting combined with surgical exposure and surgical extraction, were discussed with the patient. Because of the patient's age, the severe lingual displacement of the impacted teeth, the anticipated complexity and prolonged duration of treatment, and the associated financial burden, the patient declined this option and elected surgical management. After obtaining informed consent, a two‐stage surgical approach was planned. In the first stage, teeth 47 and 48 were surgically removed. The third molar (48) required sectioning to facilitate extraction, whereas the second molar (47) was removed without sectioning. No intraoperative complications were encountered.

Postoperative healing was uneventful, with no reported complications. The patient was scheduled for removal of the contralateral impacted molars (37 and 38) following adequate healing and based on patient convenience.

Discussion

This paper reports a very rare case of bilateral impaction of both second and third mandibular molars. Such a finding is uncommon in otherwise healthy individuals with no history of systemic disorders or syndromic associations.

Mandibular second molar impaction has a reported prevalence of 0.8%–3.6% [5]. Its etiology is multifactorial and includes arch length deficiency, ectopic eruption, ankylosis, physical obstruction, skeletal factors, and the potential influence of the developing third molar [6, 7]. Recent evidence suggests that impaction may occur despite adequate posterior space and even before development of the third molar germ, indicating that the third molar is not always the primary etiological factor [6]. In the present case, the absence of significant mandibular crowding or space deficiency, together with the severe buccolingual inclination observed on CBCT, suggests that an ectopic developmental position of the mandibular second molar germs may have played a more important role in the eruption disturbance. Although the impacted third molars appeared to obstruct the eruption pathway mechanically, the sequence of events cannot be determined retrospectively.

The contribution of the mandibular third molar to second molar impaction remains controversial. Although third molars are frequently associated with impacted second molars, current evidence suggests that they are more likely to contribute to, rather than initiate, the eruption disturbance [6, 7]. In the present case, both third molars were positioned superior to the impacted second molars and appeared to mechanically obstruct their eruption pathway. However, because the sequence of tooth development cannot be determined retrospectively, it is not possible to conclude that the third molars were the primary cause of impaction. Instead, they most likely acted as secondary contributing factors.

Mandibular second molars are most commonly impacted in a mesioangular position, although vertical and distal angulations have also been reported [8]. In contrast, both second molars in the present case exhibited severe lingual displacement, an orientation that has rarely been described in the literature. This abnormal position was associated with perforation of the lingual cortical plate, further emphasizing the unusual nature of the impaction. Clinically, such findings are important because they may increase the risk of lingual plate fracture, soft tissue injury, and surgical difficulty during extraction. The bilateral occurrence of this distinctive impaction pattern further underscores the rarity of the present case.

CBCT was indispensable for the evaluation and management of this case because it provided an accurate three‐dimensional assessment of tooth orientation, lingual cortical plate integrity, and the relationship of the impacted teeth to the inferior alveolar canal. These findings could not be reliably appreciated on panoramic radiography alone. In particular, CBCT demonstrated severe lingual displacement of both second molars, perforation of the lingual cortical plate, and intimate contact between the roots of tooth 37 and the inferior alveolar canal. This information was essential for surgical planning, risk assessment, and minimizing the potential for intraoperative complications. Similar advantages of CBCT have been reported in the evaluation of complex impacted mandibular molars [7].

Only three comparable cases of bilateral impaction involving both mandibular second and third molars have previously been reported [1, 2, 3]. However, the impaction patterns differed substantially from the present case. Reported cases have described horizontal, transverse, or bilateral kissing molar configurations, whereas the current case was characterized by severe bilateral lingual displacement of the second molars associated with lingual cortical plate perforation. These distinctive anatomical features further highlight the rarity and clinical significance of the present case.

Early diagnosis of mandibular second molar impaction is important because treatment options are broader during adolescence, and the optimal period for intervention has been suggested to coincide with the normal eruption period of the mandibular second molar (11–14 years of age) [1, 3, 7, 9]. However, treatment should be individualized according to patient age, tooth position, root development, and the severity of impaction. In the present case, the patient was 23 years old, and the impacted molars exhibited severe lingual displacement, lingual cortical plate perforation, and unfavorable spatial relationships with the adjacent third molars. These findings reduced the predictability of conservative treatment approaches and supported surgical extraction as a reasonable management option.

Orthodontic uprighting combined with surgical exposure was discussed with the patient; however, this approach would have required prolonged treatment, multiple follow‐up visits, and substantially greater financial cost. After discussing the advantages and limitations of each option, the patient elected staged surgical extraction. Thus, the final treatment plan reflected both the clinical complexity of the case and the patient's informed preferences.

This case highlights that severe buccolingual displacement of impacted mandibular molars may not be adequately assessed using panoramic radiography alone. In such complex impactions, CBCT is essential for accurate diagnosis, risk assessment, and safe surgical planning.

Conclusion

Bilateral impaction of mandibular second and third molars is an extremely uncommon finding. CBCT is invaluable for evaluating the three‐dimensional position of impacted teeth, cortical plate integrity, and proximity to the inferior alveolar canal. Early diagnosis is important because delayed detection may limit treatment options and increase surgical complexity.
