Chapter 1

Introduction

1.1 Purpose of the project
The PIP-II Superconductive Linac is a linear accelerator that provides the first stage of beam
acceleration. The velocity increase is achieved through a sequence of different cryomodules1 , where all
the cavities are aligned to form a continuous string. The cryomodule team is currently developing a
spare for the HWR cryomodule2 . This spare will not be an exact replica of the existing one, but rather
will be based on the SSR f650 cryomodule design created for PIP-II.

 Figure 1.2: Design of the Strongback assembly.
Figure 1.1: Assembly of the string and the Strong-
back.

Each cavity, magnet, and coupler form a unit, with a total of eight units per string. These components,
together with additional equipment (such as piping, the thermal shield, and beamline interconnections),
are supported by the Strongback.
The aim of this project is to develop a brand-new Strongback design for HWR cavities, and to perform
the related analyses and calculations in order to meet the required specifications.
 1
 Each optimized for a specific velocity range.
 2
 The first cryomodule houses a half-wave resonator (HWR), an accelerating structure typically employed in the early
stages of particle acceleration. This cryomodule has already been designed and manufactured.

 1

1.2 Project requirements
Mounting process: to simplify the assembly and to minimize the displacement of beamline compo-
nents during cooldown, a full-length Strongback is used to support the entire cold mass. The Strongback
is designed to slide into the vacuum vessel during cryomodule assembly, while remaining at room
temperature when the system is in operation.
Once the assembly is completed, a central pin prevents the cold mass from sliding along the rails.

 Figure 1.4: View of the Strongback fully inserted.

Figure 1.3: Insertion phase of the previous designs.

The rails used will be the same as SSR ones, so their location in respect to the vessel and the Strongback,
is a fixed constraint. Along the axis of the vessel there will be 8 bushings per side, each one located at
the cavity hole position.

Stiffness: the primary analysis concerns the feasibility of sliding the cold mass into the vacuum vessel;
the Strongback must be sufficiently stiff to ensure that the rail edge properly engages with each bushing
during installation. In this phase, any misalignment (or deformation due to the applied load on the free
end) may lead to binding, locking the component in place and preventing further sliding.
The maximum allowable deformation of the Strongback is specified by the bushing manufacturer (Pillow
Block PN20 1.25" Plain Linear Bearing – Open): the bearings tolerate up to 0.5◦ of misalignment from
the centerline (1◦ in total). With a bushing length of approximately 65 mm, this corresponds to a
maximum permissible end deformation of about 0.58 mm.

 2

 Figure 1.6: Bushing specifications.
 Figure 1.5: Bushing disposition.

To meet this requirement, several static analyses will be performed, considering the Strongback and all
applied loads3 in the most critical configuration during the mounting phase.

Envelope: a major challenge of this project is to enhance stiffness while also accounting for the space
occupied by the component. The presence of couplers, which must be inserted inside the Strongback
casing holes, together with the external vacuum vessel, the magnetic shield, and the rail system, imposes
strict dimensional constraints. As a result, the component must be designed to be both stiff and
compact.

 Figure 1.7: Maximum envelope available.

Mass: the Strongback must also be lightweight to facilitate handling during assembly. Mass mini-
mization is further required to reduce costs as much as possible.
Moreover, the manufacturing process relies on an Aluminum starting plate. Increasing its thickness
would not only raise costs but also lead to higher material waste during machining. An optimization
process is therefore required to properly balance stiffness, envelope, and mass.

Thermal flux: the Strongback must remain close to room temperature (set as 293 K) during both
the cooldown process and the operational phase of the system.
A steady-state thermal analysis will therefore be conducted, accounting for both conductive and radiative
heat loads from surrounding components.

Chapter 3

Design change

As shown in the previous simulations, the stiffness of the Strongback must be improved to reduce the
total deformation at the critical end.
The analysis highlighted that bending is the predominant source of deformation. To mitigate this effect,
the overall section height should be increased by adding material either at the bottom or at the top of
the component. These design modifications are, however, constrained by the presence of surrounding
components and by the thickness of the starting plate1 .

3.1 New design
Once again, considering the component as a beam, sectional stiffness can be increased either by raising
the overall height (given that the maximum starting plate thickness is limited to 3.5′′ ) or by modifying
the shape of the Strongback ’s lateral sides (which must not interfere with the thermal shield).
The maximum allowable height is constrained by the presence of the Coupler at the bottom and the
Magnetic Shield at the top (where the surface must remain flat to allow its straightforward installation).
As a result, the overall body thickness cannot exceed 80 mm.
Possible modifications for the new design include:

 1. Increase in height: the most effective way to
 increase the moment of inertia of the section is
 to add material as far as possible from the neu-
 tral axis (which, in this case, passes through
 the centroid of the section);

 2. Reinforcing the vertical walls: increasing the
 thickness of the vertical walls (beneath the
 upper plate) can positively affect stiffness;

 3. Adding material to the sides: unused space on
 both sides along the longitudinal direction of
 the Strongback can be exploited.

4.1 Topology optimization
In additionANSYS Topology Level Set Based Optimization 1 can be used to determine wether the
optimized design is best or can be improved.
This tool removes material from areas where it does not contribute to structural resistance. The software
performs an iterative process, removing unloaded elements from the mesh at each cycle.
The considered model corresponds to the Strongback ’s end of Design 2. The results can be used to
evaluate whether the optimized geometry of Design 2 was efficient. Some surfaces were excluded from
the analysis, since they consist of boundary conditions (such as the flat upper surface or the holes for

Chapter 6

Thermal Analysis

The operating temperature of the Strongback must remain as close as possible to the ambient value
in order to minimize heat transfer from the vessel to the cold mass. Avoiding a temperature gradient
helps ensure a more uniform thermal contraction across the component, thereby reducing deformations
and misalignments between the cavities. To estimate the equilibrium temperature of the Strongback, a
thermal analysis was performed considering three main effects:

  1. Radiation: between the lower Strongback surfaces and the Vessel;

  2. Heat flux: to represent radiation between the upper surface and the thermal shield ;

  3. Conduction: through the contact surfaces between the Strongback and the posts.

All thermal properties used in this study are defined in previous analyses and collected in Sec. 7.4.

6.1 Analysis without thermal straps
The reference model includes the Strongback, the vessel, and all posts. As in previous simulations, a
symmetry plane was introduced to reduce the computational domain by half.
A radiation condition was imposed between the Strongback and the vessel with the following settings:
