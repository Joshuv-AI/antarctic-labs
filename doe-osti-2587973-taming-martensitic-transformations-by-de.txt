1. Abstract
This project explores new classes of shape memory alloys (SMAs), through integration of
novel processing, manufacturing, and composition to modulate the SMA
thermomechanical response. The key focus areas are: (1) oxide dispersions in an additively
manufactured NiTi alloy and characterization of the structure and thermomechanical
response; (2) advancement of spherical indentation theory, simulation, and experimental
implementation to determine features of the phase transformation in SMAs; (3)
characterization of unique herringbone-like microstructures within extended twin
boundaries of cold-rolled B2 NiTi SMA and their role in enabling “strain glass” type
reversible deformation; and (4) experimental and computational studies of the eQects of
ion implanted nm-scale amorphous clusters.

For focus area (1), the addition of yttrium in additively manufactured NiTi was observed to
impart desirable features, where the load to induce the martensitic phase transformation
decreases with increasing temperature, in contrast to a Clausius-Clapeyron eQect, while
imparting resistance to permanent (plastic deformation). For focus area (2), advances in
the modeling and interpretation of nanoindentation data enable us to distinguish between
ﬂag-shaped versus linearized shape memory alloy response down to spatial length scales
~10 nm. For focus area (3), the strain-glass response of cold-rolled B2 NiTi SMA is
correlated with unique microstructures that impart strain glass response yet enhance
cyclically stable recoverable strains. For focus area (4), studies of ion implanted NiTi shape
memory alloy showcase how nano scale distributions of amorphous clusters can impart
extraordinary SMA properties, including increased transformation stress, reduced
hysteresis, and reduced plasticity. Overall, this work combines novel processing,
microstructural characterization, computational methods, and thermal mechanical
nanoindentation-based methods to explore how the austenite-to-martensite
transformation can be tamed to potentially enhance SMA design and performance.

2. Oxide Dispersion Strengthened NiTi and NiTiY Additively Manufactured SMAs
Laser powder-bed fusion (LPBF) additive manufacturing was employed to incorporate
Oxide Dispersion Strengthening (ODS) into conventional binary NiTi SMA systems, through
a collaboration with NASA-Glenn Research Center. The addition of nm-scale oxide
dispersions can pin dislocations and therefore stabilize dislocation motion, potentially at
elevated temperature. The scientiﬁc issues center on the crystal structure of the oxide(s)
produced by extreme spatial and temporal heating and cooling rates during LPBF and the
eQect of nanoscale oxide dispersions on stress and/or temperature-induced phase
transformations. This theme builds on prior DOE-BES support, in which we explored the
coupling of nm-scale dislocation substructures produced by the austenite-to-martensite
phase transformation.

homogenization of the material. In contrast, the NiTi alloy exhibits an increase and then
decrease in the h-intercept with thermal excursions, and also a decrease then increase in
peak indentation load. The scatter in peak load also increases (Figure 2 a-c).

The NiTiY alloy has a markedly diQerence response at 120 C. Compared to the 25 C results,
there is more recovery upon unloading, consistent with a reverse martensite-to-austenite

transformation. This is especially pronounced in the NiTi alloy for both the ﬁrst and second

The eQect of yttrium on the 25 C indentation response is summarized in Figure 2 (g-i). The
average maximum load (Figure 2g) decreases and then increases for the NiTi alloy but is
noticeably uniform for the NiTiY alloy. The average remnant depth upon unloading (Figure

martensite-austenite mixture. In contrast, the NiTi alloy exhibits a sharp reduction in
remnant depth after the second cycle, suggesting the stabilization of austenite and a large
increase in reverse transformation upon unloading. Finally, the load hysteresis on the
second indentation cycle (Figure 2i) decreases dramatically after the ﬁrst thermal
sequence and increases – dramatically for the NiTi alloy.

The eQect of yttrium on indentation response at several temperatures during the second
thermal excursion is summarized in Figure 3 (e, f). For both the NiTi and NiTiY alloys, the
average maximum load (Figure 3e) decreases with temperature between 100 C and 180 C.
A Clausius-Clapeyron eQect would predict the opposite trend and thus the increase in
temperature is inducing other eQects, such as plasticity or morphological evolution. At 200
C, the NiTi alloy exhibits a precipitous decrease in peak load, indicative of morphological
instability or the onset of plastic deformation. In this respect, the NiTiY alloy is more stable.
The trend in average remnant depth (h-intercept) suggests that thermal excursions impart
remarkable properties to the NiTiY alloy. The average remnant depth increases between
100 C and 120 C but then decreases between 160 C and 200 C. This is consistent with an
increase in the reverse martensite-to-austenite transformation upon unloading. In
contrast, the NiTi alloy appears to exhibit an instability where the remnant depth spikes.

In total, the LPBF NiTiY alloy exhibits more stable nanoindentation response to thermal
excursions. The prevailing hypothesis is that yttrium oxides can stabilize dislocation
substructure, particularly at elevated temperature, so that plastic deformation and creep
are suppressed at elevated temperature. This is substantiated by the relatively stable
response with increasing temperature, compared to the instabilities in peak load and
remnant displacement exhibited by the NiTi alloy. However, the NiTiY alloy has an intriguing
combination of features that are not yet understood: the decrease in both average
maximum load and average remnant depth between 160 C and 200 C (Figure 3f) is
unconventional. In the absence of plasticity, a Clausius-Clapeyron eQect would increase
(not decrease) the peak load and potentially decrease the remnant depth (as observed).
The decrease in peak load suggests that the yttrium addition may potentially aid the

3. Advances in Nanoindentation to Characterize SMAs
The project seeks to tame the martensitic transformation via defect engineering, which can
be diQicult to measure at micron to nanometer length scales with conventional methods.

 9/18

This eQort develops a novel analysis that enables diQerentiation of stress-strain response
through spherical nanoindentation at nanometer length scales. Indentation provides a
high-throughput avenue for analysis of materials, requiring relatively simple surface
preparation and delivering high spatial resolution with the ability to probe nm-scale regions
of material. Prior eQorts in indentation literature have calculated modulus and yield stress,
suQicient for analysis of conventional metals, but they lack an ability to deduce shape
memory stress-strain response, which depends on the elastic moduli of austenite and
martensite and critical stresses for transformation and plasticity. This research aimed to
understand how SMA stress-strain response can be determined from spherical
nanoindentation measurements. Mastering this capability opens new avenues for
characterizing the stress-strain response of shape memory alloys from nm to mm scales.

Spherical indentation introduces a characteristic strain ~ h/R, the indentation
depth/indenter radius. The gradients in strain are smaller compared to Berkovich
indentation, for example, and the available range of h/R during indentation permits probing
of the elastic, phase-transformation, detwinning, and plastic regimes of SMA response.
Based on pioneering work by Fleck et al., the indentation load (P)-depth (h) loading
response for a Holloman type material can be modeled as P = C*ℎ!!"# . The resulting value
of indentation exponent mind(h/R) and coeQicient C can be correlated with stress-strain
constitutive properties.

The characteristic SMA ﬂag-shaped stress-strain curve arises from the nonlinear, hysteretic
behavior of the forward and reverse transformations. For example, cold-rolling of NiTi SMA
can reduce hysteresis and linearize the SS curve, thus producing a “strain-glass” type SMA
with recoverable strains exceeding 1%. Identifying microstructures that can induce
linearization can be diQicult and can require characterization at sub-micron length scales.
Thus, a goal of the approach to extract mind and C from nanoindentation tests is to
determine whether nanoindentation can diQerentiate between linear, strain-glass response
versus ﬂag-shaped response.

Finite Element simulations and subsequent extraction of mind(h/R) during loading shows
signatures that mirroring the deformation regimes in the SMA stress-strain curve. Figure 6
(a-b) shows a proof-of-concept in which the Hollomon hardening exponent n correlates to
the indentation exponent mind. Figure 6 (c-d) demonstrates that mind(h/R) varies with elastic-
only, elastic-plastic, and ﬂag shaped SMA curves. Figure 6 (e-f) applies the mind method to
experimental spherical indentation of a solution-annealed 50.5 at% Ni-Ti binary SMA and
demonstrates the signature features of the mind(h/R) response for a ﬂag-shaped SMA, over
the range hmax/R ~ 0.04 (hmax = 200nm, R = 5000 nm).

A key result of this novel indentation analysis is identiﬁcation of the transition points
between elastic, transformation, and yield by examining mind(h/R). The correlation with
stress-strain response is derived from a relation mind = 1+ n/2 between the Hollomon
hardening exponent n and mind, as proposed by Fleck et al. This relation is validated by
analysis of ﬁnite element simulations in Figure 6 (a-b). In particular, stress-strain curves are
generated by selecting values of n and using the Holloman relation σ = Kε" , where σ is
stress, K is the strengthening coeQicient, ε is strain, and n is the Hollomon exponent. Then
the spherical indentation (P-h) response is simulated, and mind(h/R) is deduced by ﬁtting
the response to P = C*ℎ!!"# . The ﬁtting process is nontrivial and involves a least-squares ﬁt
of the ﬁnite element to this equation in the elastic regime and a local ﬁt of the ﬁnite
element response to deduce mind(h/R) outside the elastic regime.

In principle, the Holloman exponent n in the stress-strain response can be deduced from
mind obtained from indentation tests. Indeed, asymptotic values of mind(h/R) = 1.5, 1.4, and
1.3 are obtained from simulations with n = 1.0, 0.8, and 0.6, validating the result that
mind = 1+ n/2. However, for n = 0.4, the corresponding mind(h/R) response approaches the
predicted value of 1.2 but then decreases below this at larger h/R. For n = 0.2, mind(h/R)
does not approach 1.1 as predicted. This is attributed to insuQicient mesh and/or stress-
strain curve reﬁnement, resulting in overestimations or sections of the curve that appear to
skew the response.

Figure 6 (c-d) demonstrates that mind(h/R) signatures (d) correlate with diQerent stress-
strain curves (c) including a ﬂag-shape SMA. For the linear-elastic case, the mind(h/R)
response is a horizontal line at 1.5. For the elastic-plastic case, mind(h/R) = 1.5 up to
(h/R)yield, beyond which mind(h/R) decreases. This decrease is rationalized in terms of the
decrease in slope of the stress-strain response at yield; more abrupt decreases correspond
to smaller values of Holloman exponent n, where n = 1 is characteristic of an elastic-
perfectly-plastic material. In principle, (h/R)yield correlates with the yield strain εyield.

For the SMA case in Figure 6 (c-d), an elastic regime (I) exists where mind(h/R) = 1.5, but the
regime is too small to discern, since the strain εT-start at the onset of transformation is small
and thus (h/R)T-start signifying the end of the elastic regime is too small. This stems from the
resolution of the ﬁnite element mesh and simulation step size. The onset of the
transformation regime (II) at (h/R)T-start introduces a sharp decrease in stress-strain slope
and thus mind(h/R) decreases, similar to the elastic-plastic case. A minimum in mind(h/R)
occurs at (h/R)T-end corresponding to the end of the transformation regime. Beyond this,
martensite elasticity (regime III) intervenes, and the stress-strain slope increases

 12/18

commensurate with the martensite elastic modulus. mind(h/R) increases until martensite
yield (or detwinning) commences at (h/R)yield. At larger h/R, mind(h/R) monotonically
decreases and in principle, it could asymptote to a value inﬂuenced by the plastic
hardening exponent.

The analysis of spherical indentation response for SMAs thus oQers a potential approach to
invert mind(h/R) responses with characteristic features into SMA stress-strain features.
Extensions include calculation of C(h/R) in the relation P = C*ℎ!!"# and processing
features of the unloading indentation (P-h) response and (h/R)remnant value at complete
unloading. Machine learning approaches, including regression analyses, can be trained on
data from ﬁnite element simulations to map indentation features into stress-strain
features.

4. Novel Strain Glass Structures in Cold Rolled SMAs
Plastic deformation signiﬁcantly alters the microstructure and properties of NiTi shape
memory alloys. We have investigated the formation of a unique herringbone-like sub-grain
structure with extended twin boundaries in the B2 phase of a cold-rolled TiNi50.8 (at. %)
and its impact on the superelasticity of the alloy. Detailed electron backscatter diQraction
(EBSD) analysis reveals abundant sub-grain areas in diQerent orientations distinct from the
matrix grain after multiple passes in cold-rolling deformation. Notably, unique band and
herringbone structures emerge within the sub-grain areas (Figure 7), with all the
boundaries identiﬁed as coincident site lattice (CSL) boundaries. Further analysis reveals
spontaneous CSL boundary generation following periodic <011> B2 and <001> B2 tilt axes
perpendicular to the normal direction of the deformed surface in [100] B2 oriented grains,
forming a characteristic quadruple junction within the herringbone structure, distinct from
the single [011]B2 tilt axis identiﬁed in the band structure.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) identiﬁes B2 twin relations corresponding to the
special CSL Σ boundaries, while dark-ﬁeld and high-angle annular dark-ﬁeld (HAADF)
images show B19′ martensitic nanodomains decorating the twin boundaries (Figure 8).
This extended twin boundary structure enhances drastically cyclically stable
superelasticity characterized by much more limited functional fatigue, 27 % lower modulus
and 76 % larger recoverable strain as compared to the solution-treated sample after 100
training cycles. Our study provides new insights into the microstructure evolution and
enhancement of the superelastic properties of NiTi SMAs by cold-rolling, oQering a novel
approach to optimize the functionality of SMAs by introducing special sub-grain structures
with martensitic-nanodomains-nested B2 twins through simple cold rolling process.

5. Ion Implanted NiTi SMAs
We have collaborated with the Center for Integrated Nanotechnologies at Los Alamos
National Labs (N. Li and K. Hattar) a hypothesis that implantation of NiTi (Ni50.5Ti49.5)
wires with a <111> axial texture as (Figure 9a) can tame the autocatalytic nature of the
stress-induced austenite-to-martensite phase transformation. The underlying reason is
that implantation is expected to create nanoscale defects that can alter the nucleation and
growth of the phase transformation.

Figure 9: a) Ni6+ ion implantation of Ni50.5Ti49.5 and nanoindents at positions z below the
implantation surface; (b) nanoindentation response at z = 3.6 μm (red) and in unimplanted (black)
material; (c) variation in peak indentation load with z; (d) high resolution STEM-HAADF near z = 3.6
μm showing amorphous clusters; (e) phase ﬁeld simulations showing constrained phase
transformation arising from amorphous clusters; (f) molecular dynamics simulations showing
constrained phase transformation arising from vacancy clusters

Following implantation with 30 MeV Ni6+ ions to a ﬂuence of 5 x 1013/cm2 (Tandem
Accelerator Facility at Sandia National Labs-CINT), the resulting pseudoelastic response
was probed using Berkovich indentation on a polished side surface of the sample (inset,
Figure 9a), thereby sampling the material at diQerent positions z below the implanted
surface (Hysitron TI950 TriboIndenter at Los Alamos National Laboratory-CINT). Figure 10
shows varying amounts of amorphization and change in ordering arising from the depth-
dependent (z-coordinate) implantation damage, which includes nano scale amorphous
clusters inﬂuencing indentation response. Figure 10b shows that at z ≈ 3.6 μm, the material
is ~ 50% harder, yet the recoverable displacement is comparable. The second indentation

 15/18

response at the same site (cycle 2) is nearly reversible with negligible hysteresis and ~ 96%
recovery of mechanical work upon unloading compared to ~ 74% for unimplanted material.
This magnitude of recoverable work is extraordinary; for example, cyclic stress-strain data
gives ~ 51% for unimplanted NiTi and ~94% for cold rolled (27% strain). However, such
values are achieved only after 10 or more training cycles.

Figure 10c shows that hardness and other properties vary smoothly with position below the
implanted surface. The origin of this response is attributed to amorphous clusters ~5 to 10
nm in diameter (Figure 10d). These observations motivated phase ﬁeld simulations (Figure
10e) and a collaboration with Janelle Wharry on molecular dynamics simulations (Figure
10f). The simulations demonstrate that amorphous clusters and even vacancy clusters can
spatially constrain the autocatalytic austenite-to-martensite phase transformation,
thereby rationalizing the increased hardness and increased work recovery. The results
suggest that the isolated, nanoscale amorphous clusters observed here expand the

performance space for NiTi, relative to conventional training methods based on
