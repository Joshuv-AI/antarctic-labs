ABSTRACT:
This report describes an initial investigation into a proposed solution to the important problem of
performing a detailed evaluation of heliostat optical performance, in situ in a heliostat field. Our
approach is to place digital cameras in a position near the receiver where they look back toward
the heliostat mirrors. The pixels of each camera sensor identify a set of small cells on the mirror
surface, each corresponding to a “mixel.” By either passing reflected sunbeam over the camera
or passing the camera through the reflected sunbeam, the cameras intercept sunlight reflected
from each mixel. We then analyze the recorded video data to determine times when each mixel
transitions from dark to light, and then back to dark. We then use these transitions to construct
vectors from the camera to the mixel, and then from the mixel to the edge of the Sun at that
moment. We then compute the surface normal at the mixel, which bisects the angle between
these vectors. Performing this analysis for all mixels in the mirror yields a high-resolution map
of slope across the mirror surface. We have implemented most of this process, successfully
collecting data for an example heliostat facet and computing a preliminary estimated slope map.
However, more work remains to complete this calculation, since certain factors and
transformations are not yet included. Our observations so far support our hypothesis that such a
system is possible, but we have not yet completed our quantitative evaluation of the concept.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY:
An initial experimental concern that is critical to the fundamental operating principle of this
work is managing the amount of light that is collected by the imaging system. Concentrating
solar optics are by design intended to increase the intensity of light at their focal point. This
general principle applies for single facets, an ensemble of facets that make up a heliostat, and
ensembles of heliostats that comprise a field. Therefore, the first set of experiments utilized
existing imaging equipment within the Concentrating Solar Optics Laboratory (CSOL) and a
range of available neutral density filters to safely image the sun directly. This test utilized two
Nikon D3300 cameras with telephoto lenses and accomplished two goals. First, a rapid
determination was made that direct sun imaging was not immediately possible without saturating
or potentially damaging the camera sensors while maintaining data quality. Therefore, additional
neutral density filters with higher optical density were sourced. Second, the rapid tests
highlighted a few key findings for further experimental work such as necessary equipment,
possible adjustments that could be made on the camera systems, and an understanding of what
the output data would look like. Two optical filters were selected because of this testing. First, an
absorptive neutral density filter with coatings to reduce ultraviolet and infrared wavelengths
manufactured for professional photographers, specifically marketed for direct sun imaging, was
sourced. Second, a wedge shaped reflective neutral density filter typically used for laser optics
was sourced with an adaptor to fit on existing camera equipment. Details of these filters and a
sample of initial direct sun imaging tests can be found on slides 46-47.

After these initial tests, another rapid experimental test was conducted in-situ to fine tune single
facet aiming at the camera systems and derisk further data collection. A single mirror facet
connected to a mobile dolly system was used to direct our concentrated beam. This dolly system
controls azimuthal angle with manual positioning and a simple kickstand establishes elevation
angle. Future data collection efforts will utilize a mirror positioning cart with higher stability and
accuracy. A series of initial exposures and review of the data captured with a static camera
system produced one of the most impactful findings of this project. During the review of data
and notes it was noted that the dynamic nature of the proposed system could be drastically
simplified in a few ways. First, the stability of the kickstand system or total manual positioning
of the dolly system was difficult to reliably aim. Second, the dolly system did not have relative
positional indicators or data acquisition to describe its motion during tests. Both issues could be
solved by locking the support structure of the facet in place. A fixed facet could leverage the
rotation of the Earth to produce a concentrated beam of light that traverses across the camera
setup. This decreased the degrees of freedom of the dolly system and decreased the need for a
complex camera traversal system early on. A static mirror and static camera system enabled
further detailed data collection.

Two detailed data collection efforts were completed. First, a static facet static camera data
collection used the sun as the optical target and iterated through camera settings and capture
modes during eight total tests. Data captured during this series of tests was utilized to produce
results discussed below and provided on slides 56-63. Second, a static heliostat static camera
setup used the Moon as the optical target, during a full moon, and similarly iterated through
settings for a total of eleven tests using an entire heliostat. The full heliostat moonlight testing
provided key information for future work. These tests were conducted without any optical filters

 2

on the cameras. Moonlight testing allowed for a full heliostat’s concentrated beam on the camera
systems without damage, due to the drastically reduced power compared to concentrated
sunlight. Moonlight testing revealed the need for multiple camera perspectives to capture the
entire heliostat, due to the wider range of optical errors in the heliostat. Imperfections in the
aiming of the heliostat, for various reasons, showed that sections of the heliostat were revealed in
one camera perspective but not the other while some sections of the heliostat were never
illuminated. Areas of a facet that are not illuminated at all during data capture indicate either the
facet is physically pointing to a different location, or more likely the small aperture of the camera
lens relative to the concentrated spot size is insufficient to capture all locations. A multi-camera
wand was proposed to address this shortcoming by sampling the reflected beam in more
locations. Additional details about the preliminary design and testing of a camera wand that
would transit through a static beam and a static wand that would necessitate a beam transit across
its location are shown in slides 31-32.

A separate test to use multiple full heliostats with moonlight was performed with limited success,
and the pursuit of multi-heliostat testing was paused for future work. Successful data capture was
achieved during both detailed tests in the form of video files and images, but processing,
analysis, and results are only presented for the sunlight data collection due to time constraints.

Analysis of the data, which are primarily video files, included the following steps: First,
extracting each image frame from the video and selecting the frames that comprise the actual test
data. Video recording started well before actual data acquisition to allow for a safety buffer for
the camera operator. Second, each pixel of the selected frames is compared with a user defined
threshold percentage of the maximum possible pixel response based on the bit depth of the
image. A series of binary arrays are created for each frame based on the user defined thresholds.
Third, a composite image is created by compiling every pixel that exceeded the threshold value
at any point during the test. This composite is meant to visualize the percentage of the mirror that
provided signal for the camera. Fourth, the binary arrays are compared in sequence to locate the
specific video frames at which a transition occurred, either light-to-dark or dark-to-light. Fifth,
these transitions are collected to determine the start and end time of the illumination caused by
the celestial target, accounting for time offsets between camera recorded time and noted test
time. Sixth, at the known start and end time, the location of the celestial body is calculated with a
high accuracy computational astronomy database, created and hosted by NASA Jet Propulsion
Laboratory, and an open-source python package to reference that database [20-21]. Vectors from
the celestial body and the mirror location are created within a unit sphere centered on the mirror
location. Seventh, the projected areas of the celestial object on the unit sphere at the start and end
time are checked for intersection(s). A perfect data set would have the two projected areas of the
celestial object perfectly tangent to one another on the unit sphere. Finally, the new vector(s)
created from points of intersection are combined with the known vector between the mirror and
camera system to estimate the slope of the mirror at the camera pixel.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION:
This project was successful in the sense that our work confirmed the basic hypothesis that swept-
lookback techniques could construct a high-resolution mirror slope map, without requiring a
screen. Previous methods for constructing a slope map in most cases required a screen,

 3

complicating their use outdoors and for large heliostats [4, 8, 10]. Further, prior methods were
constrained by other limitations, such as measurement only at heliostat angles that were not
characteristic of tracking conditions [9]. Our work is closely related to other tower lookback
approaches, but employs a data collection scheme that does not require real-time coordination of
heliostat motion and camera data collection [16]. Further, it returns a detailed map of slope
across the mirror, which some other methods do not [18]. It is closely related to other methods
that use a tower lookback or drone-based approach, but our Earth-rotation data collection scheme
is much simpler mechanically than other methods [11, 12, 13, 14, 17]. After implementing the
planned extension to measure pointing accuracy for multiple heliostats simultaneously, we
anticipate the ability to calibrate heliostat fields faster than previous methods [6, 7]. (This aspect
of the approach was out of scope for this proposal.)
We also successfully collected laboratory data for four sample facets, providing a solid basis for
data comparison for this lookback technology and also other systems. The figure below shows
one example; see Addendum slides 34-42 for background information and further examples.

We conceived and executed improved lookback data collection procedures, and also improved
data processing algorithms employing advanced representations and computational geometry.
Along the way, we developed several new tools and capabilities:
 • New technologies for medium input flux imaging, successfully tested.
 • Potential technologies identified for capturing image data within high flux.
 • OpenCSP support for precise solar position identification.
 • Geometric analysis on the surface of a unit sphere.
 • Video stream analysis, finding dark/light and light/dark transitions for each pixel.
 • Novel algorithms for computing geometric relationships and constraints resulting from
 observing the beginning and end of reflected transits of the Sun. This algorithm can be
 readily extended to cover Moon reflected transits.

But our current software implementation is incomplete. For example, full orthorectification code
is not yet implemented, and the image above still reflects the perspective distortion resulting
from an oblique viewpoint. (It turns out that ordinary image rectification transforms are not the
most effective choice for this data. Instead, a data-driven interpolation/rectification scheme is
preferred.)
Further, while our current data analysis implementation correctly computes the mirror-to-sun and
camera-to-mirror vectors to compute a surface normal estimate, it makes the simplifying
assumption that the mirror is a point, which omits the spatial extent of the mirror in this vector
calculation. We hypothesize that this is a key cause of the discrepancy between our computed
lookback and SOFAST slope maps. This clearly needs to be addressed. Therefore, our
computed slope maps were not suitable for comparison to SOFAST, and we could not evaluate
our proposed success criterion of ±0.25 mrad agreement with the SOFAST data. See Addendum
slides 65-67.
However, we note that after the Sun/Moon reflected transit analysis identifies points on the
projected sphere and then translates these into per-mixel slope estimates, the corresponding data
representation is closely related to an intermediate representation used in the SOFAST code.
Further, the geometric semantics of the situation are fundamentally the same.
This led us to observe a promising approach to adjust our code and data collection to produce
input to the SOFAST back-end code. This would take advantage of SOFAST’s mature data
processing algorithms, and it’s well-developed analysis rendering capabilities. This may yield
slope maps closely matching the measured SOFAST data, but derived from an entirely different,
screenless, outdoor data collection scheme.
It is also worth noting that what we learned during the course of the project improved our
concept. Our initial concept envisioned a two-sweep motion by an active camera wand, using
geometric analysis of beams projected onto the target in two directions to estimate a spot
position on the target plane, from which we would then estimate the spot center and use that to
infer the mixel surface normal. But now we instead utilize a system with no moving parts (or in

 5

the future a single sweep to accelerate speed), which employs direct calculations of astronomical
geometric constraints to infer mixel slope, with both simpler data collection and fewer
calculation transforms. We view these simplifications as a significant advance, which both
increase numerical precision and reduce system cost for both the field monitoring and prototype
development scenarios.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:
In the short term, we intend to continue this investigation, and implement the algorithmic details
that we have not yet been able to develop. This will be supported by funding from the Heliostat
Consortium, a project funded by the DOE Solar Energy Technologies Office (DOE SETO).
SETO has expressed interest in this technology. In addition, a commercial heliostat developer
has expressed interested, and requested a DOE voucher program to have us test this technology
against a prototype heliostat at their site.
We plan to publish this work in a forthcoming CSP conference. Candidate venues include
SolarPACES 2026, the ASME Energy and Sustainability 2026 conference (Concentrating Solar
Track 1), or the SPIE Heliostat conference. Choice among these will depend on timing of
progress, availability of travel funds, and also foreign travel approval outlook.
Regarding intellectual property, we have filed a copyright assertion request, to release the code
and related data to OpenCSP, as proposed (see Addendum slide 23). We received Sandia Legal
approval for this copyright assertion on July 16, 2025. OpenCSP is a collaborative development
platform supporting the dissemination of CSP results from Sandia and others [22]. OpenCSP
includes both code and data components; these have been accessed over 2,932 times, by users in
at least 43 countries. We chose to release our lookback analysis code to this venue in order to
maximize its positive impact.
If this technology ultimately achieves it technical goals, it will enable several impacts:
 1. Improved commissioning and operation of full-scale heliostat fields:
 a. Accelerated heliostat field calibration.
 b. Higher fidelity heliostat field diagnosis and maintenance.
 This technology is designed to provide higher fidelity, faster in situ heliostat diagnosis.
 2. Support for new heliostat development:
 a. Earlier, more impactful support for high-quality heliostat prototype development.
 b. Increased cycles of learning enabled by low-cost, on-site availability.
 This technology makes it possible for heliostat developers to access high-fidelity heliostat
 metrology at low cost, and with readily available infrastructure requirements (the great
 outdoors).
 3. Sandia national security applications:
 a. This technology is designed to enable the Sandia National Solar Thermal Test
 Facility (NSTTF) to improve the performance of its heliostat field, and provide
 increased high-temperature testing capabilities for its various customers.

CONCLUSION:

This project has taken key first steps toward developing a system for measuring detailed slope
maps and pointing information for heliostats in situ in an operating heliostat field. Our lookback
approach avoids the large optical screen that complicates other approaches. It has both a
minimal configuration requiring only a few cameras and the mirror of interest, suitable for
development situations, and a higher-performance configuration including a few cameras,
thermal/flux protection, an optional single-degree-of-freedom wand, and the mirror of interest,
for field evaluation situations involving multiple heliostats. When complete, the envisioned
system is designed to address a key unsolved problem in concentrating solar optical metrology,
enabling accelerated field calibration and high-fidelity in-field monitoring of heliostat optical
performance. As a bonus, this also produces a solution enabling heliostat developers to obtain
high-quality optical measurements of their mirror prototypes, with very low cost and
infrastructure requirements.
The lookback approach we studied enabled us to measure high-resolution mirror slope maps
using the Sun and Moon as optical targets. By exploiting the Earth’s rotation, we were able to
collect the required data with only a single camera on a tripod, and our data collections are both
very precise and low cost. We successfully collected data for an NSTTF heliostat facet, and
found that both the data collection procedure and the resulting video stream matched our
expectations. We also collected companion data from the same heliostat using the SOFAST
deflectometry system. We then implemented software that utilized a novel data analysis
algorithm to compute a slope map from the resulting video data. The resulting slope map is
preliminary because our current software implementation is incomplete, which prevented us
from evaluating whether our result met the project’s goal criterion of agreement with the
SOFAST data with at most ±0.25 mrad discrepancy for 95% of the mirror surface. While we
were not able to perform this final quantitative evaluation, the results obtained along the way are
all consistent with our expected hypothesis, and we are encouraged by these results. We look
forward to completing our implementation and evaluating this criterion in future work.
