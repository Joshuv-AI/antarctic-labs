Abstract
Although there has been widespread deployment of wind farms in the United States, and plans to
continue deployment into the future, the complete effects of wind farms on Earth systems are not
well understood. The work performed here has incorporated wind farm models into the Energy
Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM) capable of simulating the effects of extracting momentum
from the atmospheric flow field using power generating wind farms. This new capability will allow
scientists to quantify the impacts of wind farm induced changes on Earth systems by exploiting
E3SM’s ability to couple atmospheric, oceanic, and biogeochemical (BGC) models on a global
scale and monitor precipitation levels, extreme weather events, soil moisture content and jet stream
location over decades-long time periods. This tool will be used to inform decision making on
wind farm citing and will contribute to the Lab’s ability to assess energy technology impacts on
the environment and evaluate the trade-offs between energy infrastructure investments and their
impacts on natural systems.

1. INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS

Wind turbines are large structures, reaching up to 250 meters in height, with a rotor diameter of 200
meters[1] and when placed together in large arrays these turbines act as significant momentum sinks
and as sources of atmospheric turbulence on the air current, which can alter the local environment.
Coastal models have shown that off-shore wind farms influence the local shear driven oceanic
currents[2, 3]. A similar study, which Sandia collaborated on, showed the effect of offshore
wind farms on the oceanic upwelling[4] responsible for distribution of nutrients and oxygen at the
bottom of the food chain. Empirical evidence has shown an increase in land surface temperature
and wind turbine installations[5, 6] and these results have been supported by computational models
performed by Miller[7] who noted that regional models will under-predict the global-scale climatic
response.
Beyond these regional studies, the far-reaching and long-term effects of wind farms on atmospheric
currents and other wind-driven earth systems are not well understood. Fitch[8] has performed the
most comprehensive study on the global impact of wind farms using CAM5, showing changes
in wind speed and wind direction, however this investigation looked only at global temperature
impacts, and neglected any oceanic effects, studying only twelve land-based wind farms worldwide,
at just two North American sites.
The work presented here enables the investigation of large-scale wind turbine deployments, and
their impact on the global climate. This effort incorporates wind farm representation into the
atmospheric model of E3SM to simulate the effects of extracting energy from the flow field, to
quantify the climate impacts of atmospheric changes. By incorporating this model into E3SM,
future studies will be able to investigate long term, global effects on precipitation levels, extreme
weather events, soil moisture content and jet stream location by exploiting E3SM’s ability to couple
atmospheric, oceanic, and biogeochemical models into one Earth systems model.
Compatibility with the wind turbine model in the Weather Research & Forecasting (WRF) Model
input files has been maintained so that researchers can utilize common configuration files for
the two models. Conceptual offshore turbine layouts, provided by the Bureau of Ocean Energy
Management (BOEM) ant the Schatz Energy Research Center at California State Polytechnic
University, Humboldt, which were recently used in recent regional atmospheric studies [4] provide
validation data for this study, and qualitative comparisons are shown below in the Results section.
One-to-one comparisons were not be made between E3SM and WRF, since WRF utilizes real
historic wind conditions for boundary conditions and E3SM simulates a representative climate
from post-industrial surface conditions. However, qualitative comparison of the results are shown
in Chapter 3 and indicate that the large scale phenomena of turbine wake speed and temperature
differences are being captured by the newly implemented E3SM model, and are consistent with
results predicted by WRF models.

2.4. Simulation configuration

Testing of the implemented module was performed to verify resonable output was being produced.
Year-long E3SM simulations were run of global atmospheric conditions both without and with
turbine influenced on the air flow. Both simulations consisted of the F2010 E3SM compset, run on
the Regionally Refined CONUS mesh (E3SM grid name: conusx4v1pg2_r05_IcoswISC30E3r5)
which is 4X resolved from the standard mesh around the United States of America. This baseline
control simulation was run using the predefined compset simulation parameters, and the second
simulation was run identically other than the inclusion of the wind farms modeled off the western
coast of the United States, as depicted by red dots in 2-1. The layout consisted of 215 turbines off
the coast of Brookings, Oregon, 90 turbines off the coast of Coos Bay, Oregon, 214 off the coast of
Humboldt County, California, and 453 in Morro Bay, California. As shown by the circled red dots
in Figure 2-1, the turbines from each location were grouped into a single “type”, which allows for

the power production of each wind farm to be totaled independently during output analysis. The
conceptual layouts used here were provided by the Schatz Energy Research Center at California
State Polytechnic University, Humboldt, as lease areas identified for potential future wind farm sites.
To ensure realistic values were used to represent the wind farms, turbine physical characteristics,
including height, diameter, and performance coefficients were taken from the NREL report, The
cost of floating offshore wind energy in California between 2019 and 2032 [1]. The baseline and
the test simulations were differentiated to quantify the effects of the wind farms on the atmospheric
flow conditions.

Due to the inherent chaotic nature of Earth systems, small changes in the local flow can have a far
reaching effect, and early tests identified that even though wind turbine effects were only added
to a specific region off the coast of California, the two simulations showed differences globally.
Therefore, subtracting the two solutions resulted in a significant amount of noise over the entire
domain, from which the signal was difficult to isolate. This necessitated the implementation of
“nudging”[14, 15] into the wind farm E3SM simulation. Nudging relaxes a defined subsection of
the computational domain back to a baseline solution. In this case, nudging was use to restore all
far-reaching effects of the test study towards the solution obtained in the baseline control study.
This allowed us to effectively create boundary conditions which isolated the turbine effects to their
local regions for evaluation against the baseline without the additional chaotic weather effects. The
color contours in Figure 2-2 show how nudging was able to relax the far-field domain of the turbine
study towards the control solution, such that when the difference was taken, we see a clear velocity
wake behind the turbine locations.

 11

Note that nudging was done solely to isolate the turbine effect for the purpose of model compar-
ison and validation. By utilizing the nudging feature, far reaching, global effects are effectively
eliminated from the solution. A full Earth system study aimed at evaluating the long term and far
reaching effects of such turbines should not use E3SM’s nudging capabilities, but rather should
look at the changes in the statistical parameters of interest over long time periods between control
and test scenarios.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The results of the simulations performed are shown here. To show the velocity wake of the wind
farms, a baseline case was run without turbines and compared to a similar case modified only
with the turbine influence. Figure 3-1 shows the baseline simulation with velocity streamlines on
the left and the computed velocity difference between the baseline atmospheric simulation and the
simulation with the turbine module on the right. The image on the left indicates an overall south
easterly wind off the western coast, and the image on the right shows an atmospheric velocity deficit
located at the turbine sites and the expected wake extending from the wind farms in the direction
of the wind.

For comparison, these results are shown in Figure 3-2 beside Weather Research and Forecasting
(WRF) model results previously performed by Sandia and published in Raghukumar et al. [4].
The WRF simulations were run using identical turbine parameterizations and WRF’s Fitch model

It is important to note that these results are strictly qualitative. and are shown to demonstrate that
the new E3SM wind farm module is producing results consistent with those of the WRF model.
The plotted WRF results show the average velocity difference over a real year simulated using
historical wind forcing conditions, and the E3SM image shows average results over a single month
representative of a post-industrial climate, without any historical forcings. This means that the
differences between the models could be attributed to different weather conditions, however the
similar trends shown between the two are promising.

4. ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS

This small project LDRD was limited in scope to the implementation of the new module into E3SM.
As such, it did not result in any publications or conference presentations. However, should future
opportunities become available, the author would be very interested in demonstrating the outcomes
of this project at a future E3SM workshop, or professional conference such as AGU or AMS.

Although wind resources promise vast amounts of energy available to be converted to electrical
power, the side effects of capturing this energy are still not fully understood, in part because the
tools have not been developed to study these effects. The work shown here has documented the
first step in quantifying the complete effects of wind farms using a global climate model, setting
the stage for long term simulation analysis to be performed in the future. Now researchers have the
tools available to study whether wind farm effects extend beyond the immediate area of influence
and to quantify their accumulation over multi-decadal time periods.

Preliminary results developed as part of the wind farm module development demonstrate the ability
of E3SM to capture not only the wake effect on the atmospheric velocity, but also the cooling effect
at the ocean surface in the wake of the turbines. Both NOAA and BOEM have expressed interest in
working with Sandia to expand these simulations to fully coupled Earth systems models including
oceanic and BGC effects, and the three organizations are currently exploring the options available
to continue this research. Impacts on oceanic circulation have been predicted by Raghukumar
et al. using WRF simulations as input conditions to regional oceanic models. These were the first
simulations of this kind and were limited to one-way coupling on a very localized scale. The E3SM
module developed here means these kinds of simulations can now be run on a global domain using
full coupling between oceanic models and all of the other E3SM Earth system models (ice, soil
moisture, precipitation, riverine, biomass, etc), using native E3SM interfacing.

Global oceanic effects will be studied to better understand the impacts on current upwelling and
recirculation of nutrient-rich waters at the ecosystem it feeds. These model will provide the
input conditions to biogeochemical models that predict algae and plankton grown for a better
understanding of the oceanic ecosystem. Wind farm wake effects can now be studied over long
time scales and coupled to E3SM’s biogeochemical (BGC) models to investigate any impacts
off-shore wind farms may have on plankton grown or nitrogen distribution throughout the water
column.

The ability of E3SM to couple atmospheric results to multiple other Earth system models will allow
the investigation of wind farm array’s secondary and tertiary impacts on Earth systems. Results
will be used to inform wind farm citing decisions based on power production potential, mitigating
undesirable environmental impacts, or leveraging cooling effects to reduce evaporation around
aqueducts and prevention of soil moisture loss in areas of crop growth.

 19

All of these simulations will result in a more complete understanding of the direct atmospheric
effects of wind farm deployment as well as the indirect environmental effects. This will provide de-
cision making support for responsible deployment of future wind farm facilities, while maximizing
clean energy production.

 20

5. CONCLUSION

A new module has been developed here using documented momentum theory which allows the
investigation of wind farm effects within the E3SM framework, which incorporates all aspects of
Earth systems including precipitation, soil, riverine, oceanic and atmospheric models. Preliminary
results were investigated using the module developed here and were shown to be consistent with
results from the widely used and validated WRF model. The module was implemented in a manner
which activates the wind turbine module simply by placing the configuration files in the E3SM run
directory, eliminating the need to edit namelist files or configure run scripts. Compatibility has
been maintained with the WRF implementation allowing users to directly utilize existing turbine
configuration files in the E3SM framework.
Not only will this work allow for predictive research to be performed before planning wind farm
deployments, but this module will also allow for more accurate simulations of current weather
systems. There are over 60,000 utility-scale wind turbines deployed in the United States [16], and
current weather and climate models have so far neglected their influence. The new capability added
in this effort will allow scientists to represent wind farm effects in E3SM for informed decision
and policy making about citing locations and lease areas on and off shore, as well as more robust
modeling of current weather conditions due to existing deployments.

APPENDIX B. User Manual

B.1. Model Input

To use the newly added windfarm module in E3SM, at least two input files are required: windturbines.txt
and wind-turbine-1.tbl. If these file are present in the E3SM run directory, the wind farm
module will be activated. These files represent the turbine location file and the turbine coefficient
files, respectively, as shown in Figure B-1.

Figure B-1. Example of the turbine location file, windturbines.txt (left), and the turbine coefficient
 curve definition file, wind-turbine-1.tbl (right).

On the left of Figure B-1 is an example of the turbine location file, which is simply an ascii file
containing 3 columns. Each turbine is defined by a single row in the file, which specifies its latitude,
longitude, and turbine type (or group) number. There must be at least one turbine type, but there
is no upper limit on how many types are defined. For each turbine type, a file must exist named
wind-turbine-X.tbl, where X is substituted with the turbine type number. This file defines the
specifics of each turbine type to be modeled.
The format of this file is as follows. The first line contains just the number of rows in the velocity
tables. The second line contains:
 • Turbine hub height

 27

 • Turbine rotor diameter
 • Standing thrust coefficient
 • Nominal power output
 • Standing turbulence coefficient
The velocity-dependent coefficients are defined starting at the third line of the file, where each each
row contains:
 • Velocity
 • Thrust Coefficient (𝐶𝑇 )
 • Power Coefficient (𝐶𝑃 )
 • Turbulence Coefficient (𝐶𝑡𝑘𝑒 )
As noted in Section 2.3, the final column of wind-turbine-XX.tbl is an optional field allowing
the user to manually specify a value for 𝐶𝑡𝑘𝑒 . When this column is omitted, the module will default
to the Fitch model for turbulent production where 𝐶𝑡𝑘𝑒 = 𝐶𝑇 − 𝐶𝑃 .

B.2. Model Output

The output of the wind farm module is in the for of entries in the NetCDF files generated by the
software. Specifically, the atmospheric model in E3SM will output history files with a naming
convention similar to “CASENAME.RESOLUTION.COMPSET.eam.h0.YYYY-MM.nc”. When the
wind turbine module is active, there will be additional output values written to this file. Of the
additional variables, the ones of most relevance will be:
 • PTWT## - Average Power output, by numerical column, of all turbines of type ## since the
 last output
 • PowWT## - Instantaneous power output, by numerical column, of a single turbine of type ##
 at the current output time
 • FXWT## and FYWT## - Instantaneous thrust force vector component, by numerical column,
 on a single wind turbine of type ##
Each of these variables will be written out for each type of turbine defined in the input files, with
its type number in place of ##, allowing the user to discern power produced by an arbitrary number
of turbine groups.
