The Drosophila CEBPG homolog Irbp18 partners with crc (ATF4) to mediate the integrated stress response in degenerative disease models
Authors: Sahana Mitra, Huai-Wei Huang, Rhihab Faruk, Arissa Low, Adam I Yeo, Hyung Don Ryoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe integrated stress response (ISR) coordinates cellular adaptation to diverse stress conditions. In Drosophila, two bZIP transcription factors, Xrp1 and crc (ATF4 homolog), are induced during ISR. Crc protein can dimerize with two CEBP factors in vitro, but the in vivo relevance of those interactions remained unknown. Here, we report that the CEBPG homolog, Irbp18, is an essential partner of crc during ISR. Specifically, Irbp18 is broadly required for the transcriptional induction of ISR target genes in the photoreceptors of ninaEG69D, a Drosophila model of retinitis pigmentosa. Moreover, CUT&RUN analysis indicates that Irbp18 loss reduces or abolishes crc binding to target DNAs in photoreceptors and impairs crc’s ability to induce target transcripts upon overexpression. Functionally, Irbp18 loss causes retinal degeneration and suppresses ISR signaling in parkin mutants, a model of Parkinson’s disease. Together, these findings identify Irbp18 as a cofactor for crc, impacting pathological outcomes in Drosophila models of degeneration.

INTRODUCTION

Metazoan cells can respond to various cellular stresses through adaptive signaling pathways. One such pathway is the integrated stress response (ISR), which is activated by diverse conditions, including endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, amino acid deprivation, and mitochondrial stress. The ISR is initiated by stress-induced eIF2α phosphorylation, leading to the induction of downstream transcription factors that drive gene expression programs for cellular adaptation.1 ISR broadly impacts adaptation to stress, and consistently, there is an increasing list of metabolic and degenerative disorders associated with abnormal ISR signaling.2-5

Perk is an eIF2α kinase activated by ER stress, and this specific ISR pathway is also referred to as the perk-branch of the unfolded protein response (UPR).6,7 In mammals, ATF4 and CHOP are among the best characterized transcription factors that mediate ISR signaling downstream of eIF2α phosphorylation.8-12 Drosophila also encodes two transcription factors, Xrp1 and cryptocephal (crc), which are induced as part of ISR signaling and regulate distinct downstream target genes.13-18 crc encodes the Drosophila homolog of ATF4, which plays essential roles in several developing tissues and adult photoreceptors.14,19,20

ISR is implicated in multiple human diseases that can be studied with Drosophila models. One such example is retinitis pigmentosa, a genetic disorder of age-related retinal degeneration most frequently caused by missense alleles of the rhodopsin gene.21,22 ninaEG69D is an analogous Drosophila allele, which encodes a misfolding-prone rhodopsin-1 protein that imposes ER stress, activates multiple branches of the UPR that include perk, and dominantly causes age-related retinal degeneration.13,23,24 In another disease model, perk becomes active after the loss of Pink1 or parkin, whose human homologs are associated with rare forms of Parkinson’s Disease.25

Both Xrp1 and crc (ATF4) are members of the bZIP family of transcription factors, which form dimers through their leucine zipper helices to bind to DNA. ATF4 does not form stable homodimers in vitro and in cells,26,27 but high affinity heterodimer partners of ATF4 include members of the CAAT/enhancer binding protein (C/EBP) family, a subclass of bZIP proteins.26-29 The Drosophila genome encodes three C/EBP family factors. Noc1, a CEBPZ homolog; slobo (slbo), homologous to CEBPA; and Irbp18, a homolog of CEBPG. An unbiased in vitro physical interaction profiling study reported that crc stably interacts with only two other Drosophila bZIP family proteins, slbo and Irbp18.26 Irbp18 is an established heterodimerization partner of Xrp1,30-33 but the in vivo roles of crc’s putative heterodimerization partners in ISR remain unknown.

Here, we investigated the role of Irbp18 in stress response signaling. Specifically, gene expression profiling of ninaEG69D/+ adult photoreceptors revealed a broader role of Irbp18 in stress-inducible gene expression. We found that Irbp18 plays a role beyond Xrp1-mediated gene expression, being required for the induction of most crc target genes in adult photoreceptors. Our adult photoreceptor-specific CUT&RUN analysis indicated that most crc-target binding requires Irbp18. On the other hand, slbo did not affect the expression of a crc/Irbp18 target gene. Loss of Irbp18 caused age-related retinal degeneration and modulated the parkin loss-of-function phenotype. These findings indicate that Irbp18 acts as a shared cofactor for both Xrp1 and crc at an essential node of ISR signaling to influence pathological outcomes in Drosophila models of human diseases.

RESULTS

Irbp18 is broadly required for stress-responsive gene induction in a Drosophila model for retinitis pigmentosa

To profile gene expression changes specifically in Drosophila photoreceptors carrying the ninaEG69D mutation, we expressed the nuclear envelope-localizing EGFP::Msp300KASH (henceforth KASH-GFP) in adult R1 to R6 photoreceptors through the Rh1-Gal4/UAS system34,35 (Figure 1A). The EGFP-labeled photoreceptor nuclei were purified from 0- to 1-day-old adult flies for RNA-seq analysis (Figures 1A and 1B; STAR Methods). The quality metrics of this dataset are detailed in Table S1. The differential gene expression analysis identified 216 transcripts that were significantly induced (p-adjusted <0.05) in ninaEG69D/+ photoreceptors compared to ninaE wild type controls (Table S2). Consistent with ninaEG69D encoding a misfolding-prone protein synthesized in the ER, the top 10 enriched GO terms for ninaEG69D-induced genes in terms of fold enrichment included “response to unfolded protein (GO:0006986)” and “endo-plasmic reticulum unfolded protein response (GO:0030968)” (Figure 1C; Table S3). Other top GO terms included tRNA aminoacylation (GO:0043039), amino acid activation (GO:0043038), and tRNA aminoacylation for protein translation (GO:0006418) (Figure 1C; Table S3), which are frequently associated with ATF4 signaling.1 Among the specific genes induced was the well-established crc-target gene Thor14,36 (Table S2). The principal-component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation of controls (ninaE wild type) and the ninaEG69D/+ group (Figure 1D).

To determine the role of Irbp18 in this model, we employed an established null allele with a PiggyBac element inserted in the coding sequence of the leucine zipper domain.30 Notably, 128 out of the 216 ninaEG69D-inducible transcripts, including Thor, were significantly reduced in ninaEG69D/+ photoreceptors in the Irbp18f05006 homozygous background (Figure 1E; Table S4). There was a smaller subset of ninaEG69D-inducible transcripts that remained unaffected by Irbp18 loss (e.g., Yp1, Yp2, Yp3, Gp93, and Erp60).

Because of the broad effect of Irbp18f05006, we corroborated the genotype of our samples by examining the RNA-seq reads. In the Irbp18f05006 samples, we found a paradoxical increase in the Irbp18 reads 5′ to the PiggyBac insertion site, perhaps due to genetic compensation or transcriptional adaptation known to occur in response to gene mutations.37-39 Notably, there was an abrupt discontinuity in the amount of RNA-seq reads 3′ to this insertion site, with the PiggyBac insertion predicted to truncate the transcript and the encoded protein (Figure S1). Approximately half of the ninaE sequence reads contained the C to T change corresponding to the G69D allele (Figure S1). These results corroborate the genotype and reveal a broad requirement of Irbp18 for ninaEG69D-induced changes in photoreceptor gene expression.

Irbp18 is required for the induction of an Xrp1 target reporter, gstD-GFP

Irbp18 is a well-established heterodimerization partner of Xrp1.30 Consistently, the position frequency matrix of Xrp1 derived from a previous ChIP-seq dataset contains a putative C/EBP half-site (Figure 2A).40 To investigate Xrp1’s ability to induce target genes, we used a reporter with three putative Xrp1 binding sites from the gstD1 locus we had previously characterized (Figure 2B). ER stress-induced induction of this gstD-GFP reporter requires Xrp1 but not crc.15 To confirm the requirement of Irbp18 in this Xrp1-dependent gstD1-GFP reporter expression, we examined eye imaginal discs mis-expressing ninaEG69D (Rh1G69D) through the GMR-Gal4 driver, which robustly activates the ISR.13,15,23 While control imaginal discs showed no detectable gstD-GFP expression, Rh1G69D-expressing discs displayed robust reporter induction (Figure 2C, left and center panels). In contrast, the equivalent expression of Rh1G69D in the Irbp18f05006 homozygous background failed to induce gstD-GFP expression (Figures 2C and 2D). These results confirm that Irbp18 is required for the induction of Xrp1 target genes.

CUT&RUN profiling reveals Irbp18’s requirement for crc binding to its target DNA

The broader than expected requirement of Irbp18 in ninaEG69D/+ photoreceptors prompted us to test if Irbp18 is required for other transcription factors activated by ninaEG69D. We considered crc a candidate based on a previously reported in vitro interaction with recombinant Irbp18,26 and because crc protein is induced by ninaEG69D.13 To profile crc-DNA interaction in vivo, we expressed a 3xHA-tagged crc transgene (crc-3xHA) together with KASH-GFP in the adult photoreceptors using Rh1-Gal4. The KASH-GFP-labeled photoreceptor nuclei from 0 to 3-days old adult flies were first purified, followed by anti-HA CUT&RUN to profile crc binding sites in the genome (Figure 3A; also, STAR Methods). The quality metrics of the sequencing results are described in Table S1. 3,975 anti-HA consensus peaks were called in both of our replicates with the criteria of FDR <0.01 (Table S5).

We further examined the genes associated with anti-HA peaks by filtering the dataset with different peak score cutoffs. When those with peak scores above 5000 were analyzed, the top two GO terms with the highest log2 fold enrichment were “ISR (GO:0140467)” and “perk-mediated unfolded protein response (GO:0036499) (Figure 3B; Table S6). We consider this group of binding sites high confidence hits because both GO terms refer to the crc (ATF4)-mediated signaling response. On the other hand, GO terms associated with crc (ATF4) signaling were not among the top enriched categories when we applied the relaxed peak score cutoff of above 500 (Figure S2).

The CUT&RUN data also showed a notable correlation with ninaEG69D/+ RNA-seq results, especially with the ones with high peak scores in Table S5. For example, 8 out of the 10 genes (80%) with the highest crc-binding peak scores (peak scores >19,500) in the CUT&RUN dataset were ninaEG69D-inducible genes. Expanding the analysis to the top 30 genes with the highest peak scores (peak scores >12,500), 53% of them were ninaEG69D-inducible genes. Further extending the analysis to the top 131 genes (peak scores >5000), 20% overlapped with the ninaEG69D-inducible genes list, showing a moderate correlation of r = 0.32 (Figure 3C; Table S7). Given that ninaEG69D also activates crc-independent UPR signaling pathways such as Ire1-Xbp1,23 the degree of correlation between the two datasets appears highly significant. The genes with the highest peak scores in the CUT&RUN analysis likely represent direct crc targets in this ninaEG69D disease model.

We next examined crc-3xHA binding peaks in Irbp18f05006 homozygous adult fly photoreceptors in an otherwise equivalent experimental setup. There was a significant reduction in crc-3xHA-DNA interaction under these conditions, as visualized in a heatmap (Figure 3D). Irbp18’s requirement in crc-3xHA binding was also evident in the genomic tracks of representative crc target genes. For example, HisRS, CG2017 (GTPBP2 homolog), and Thor loci lost crc-3xHA peaks almost completely in the Irbp18f05006 homozygous background (Figure 3E). Together, these results support the requirement of Irbp18 for crc-DNA interaction in vivo.

Photoreceptor-specific gene expression profiling supports an essential requirement of Irbp18 for crc target gene induction

Since UPR-associated transcription factors other than crc also contribute to differential gene expression in ninaEG69D photoreceptors,23 we specifically examined gene expression changes in crc overexpressing photoreceptors to directly test Irbp18’s role as a partner of crc. The external morphology of adult eyes expressing crc with Rh1-Gal4 appeared normal. Moreover, crc overexpression did not disrupt the expression of a photoreceptor specific marker, ninaE-GFP (Figure S3). As in the ninaEG69D-inducible gene analysis (Figure 1), we co-expressed KASH-GFP with Rh1-Gal4 to specifically mark the adult photoreceptor nuclear membranes prior to purification for RNA-seq. The libraries were sequenced, and their quality metrics are detailed in Table S1. After filtering the data for those with p-adjusted values below 0.05, we found 272 transcripts significantly induced in Rh1-Gal4 > UAS-crc photoreceptors (Table S8). Of those 272 crc-inducible transcripts, 164 were dependent on Irbp18 as their transcripts were significantly reduced (padj <0.05) in the Irbp18f05006 homozygous background (Figure 4A; Table S9). PCA revealed a clear separation among the three genotypes examined (Figure 4B).

Because artificially high levels of crc could induce non-physiological crc targets, and because ninaEG69D activates other UPR-associated transcription factors in addition to crc, we turned our attention to the transcripts commonly induced in ninaEG69D/+ and Rh1-Gal4 > UAS-crc photoreceptors. There were 36 overlapping transcripts from the two datasets (Figure 4C), indicating that crc is necessary and sufficient to induce approximately 17% of the ninaEG69D-inducible genes. Most of this group (91%) required Irbp18 for induction (Figure 4D).

Independently, we assessed the physiological significance of Irbp18 by examining the external adult eye morphology. The Drosophila compound eye is composed of a regular array of hexagonal ommatidia and interommatidial bristles, which can be visualized through scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Control GMR-Gal4/+ eyes maintained this regular ommatidial array, but crc overexpression during eye development through GMR-Gal4 disrupted this regular ommatidial organization and resulted in significant bristle loss (Figure 4E, compare the left two panels). Strikingly, these phenotypes were suppressed when crc was overexpressed in the Irbp18f05006 homozygous background (Figure 4E, third panel from left). By contrast, Irbp18f05006 did not modify the external eye phenotype caused by overexpressing the active isoform of the UPR transcription factor Xbp1 (not be confused with Xrp1), which stimulates a signaling branch independent of crc (Figure S4). Overexpression of Irbp18 through GMR-Gal4 did not disrupt external eye morphology (Figure 4E, right panel), and this may be because Irbp18 heterodimerization partners are not present at significant levels during normal eye development. These findings indicate that Irbp18 is necessary for crc function once it is expressed.

Induction of dgp-1-DsRed, a crc reporter, requires Irbp18

To validate the results from RNA-seq and CUT&RUN, and to examine if crc is sufficient to regulate target genes in heterologous tissues, we examined the expression of crc target reporter transgenes in the larval eye imaginal discs. We focused on dgp-1 because the CUT&RUN data showed an Irbp18-dependent crc-3xHA binding near its first intron (Figure 5A), and because dgp-1 is commonly induced by ninaEG69D and Rh1-Gal4 > UAS-crc (Table S2; Figure S8). Moreover, dgp-1 had been independently reported as a Perk-crc inducible gene by others,24,41 and dgp-1’s mammalian homolog GTPBP1 is also induced by the ISR in a mouse model for neurodegeneration.42,43 We specifically generated a dgp-1 expression reporter by placing a putative regulatory sequence, between the first exons of the isoforms A and B, upstream of DsRed (Figure 5B; see STAR Methods). Consistent with our CUT&RUN results, this region contains at least two sequences with high crc binding scores calculated based on its position weight matrix (Figures 5C and 5D). The crc position weight matrix (Jaspar Matrix ID MA2208.1) includes a potential C/EBP binding half-site (Figure 5C). When crc was overexpressed in eye imaginal discs with the GMR-Gal4 driver, dgp-1-DsRed reporter was robustly induced. Such induction was completely abolished in the Irbp18f05006 background (Figures 5E-5H). The loss of dgp-1-DsRed reporter in the Irbp18f05006 mutant disc was rescued by driving the expression of the UAS-Irbp18 transgene (Figures 5I, 5J, and 5L), further corroborating the requirement of Irbp18 in crc-induced dgp-1-DsRed expression.

In addition to testing Irbp18, we examined slbo, the other C/EBP factor that can bind to crc in vitro.26 First, we assessed slbo’s ability to rescue the Irbp18 loss-of-function phenotype in GMR-Gal4 > UAS-crc eye discs. UAS-slbo expression was unable to restore the loss of dgp-1-DsRed in these eye discs (Figures 5K and 5L). Furthermore, dgp-1-DsRed induction caused by GMR-Gal4 UAS-crc was not abolished in the slbo01310/slbory7 trans-heterozygotes (Figure S5). These results indicate that Irbp18, but not slbo, is specifically required for this crc target gene induction.

Irbp18 is required for the expression of the crc-responsive reporter, Thorintron-DsRed

Thor is another well-established crc target gene, but unlike dgp-1, crc expression in the eye is only necessary but not sufficient to induce Thor expression (Figures 1 and 4). In the late 3rd instar larval stage, Thor is strongly induced in the larval fat body by endogenous crc due to physiological stress associated with normal development. Thor mRNA levels decrease when crc is knocked down in the larval fat body.14 We used this developmental stage to test the requirement of Irbp18 in Thor expression. We specifically performed qRT-PCR for Thor transcripts, which showed a significant reduction of this crc target transcript in the Irbp18f05006 larvae as compared to control w1118 larvae (Figure 6A). To independently validate the results, we employed a Thor expression reporter, Thorintron-DsRed, containing its intron regulatory sequence with ATF4 binding sites that drive DsRed (Figure 6B). This reporter shows physiological crc signaling in the late third instar larval fat body, which is lost in the Irbp18f05006 homozygous background (Figures 6C and 6D). Knockdown of Irbp18 in the larval fat body using RNAi similarly caused a marked reduction in Thorintron-DsRed signals (Figure S6). By contrast, the slbo01310 homozygous larvae showed Thorintron-DsRed signals similar to control larvae (Figures 6E and 6F). These results further validate that this crc target reporter specifically requires Irbp18.

Irbp18 affects retinal degeneration and survival of parkin mutants

ATF4 loss causes eye abnormalities in mice, and hypomorphic crc mutants in Drosophila exhibit age-dependent retinal degeneration.20,44,45 Therefore, we tested whether the loss of an essential crc partner also shows retinal degeneration. The retinal integrity could be readily assessed in live flies with Rh1-GFP, which generates a trapezoidal pattern of fluorescent pseudopupils that disappear with retinal degeneration.46 This assay revealed that by day 32, all Irbp18f05006 homozygotes lost pseudo-pupils, while most control flies maintained them at this age (p < 0.0001) (Figures 7A and 7B). We independently validated the role of Irbp18 by specifically knocking down the gene in photoreceptors using Rh1-Gal4 > UAS-Irbp18 RNAi. Labeling the retina with phalloidin showed a regular pattern of ommatidia in 35-days-old control flies, with each ommatidium containing seven rhabdomeres arranged in a trapezoidal pattern (Figures S7A and S7B). This rhabdomere pattern was disrupted in the equivalent aged Irbp18 RNAi retina (Figure S7C), indicating an essential requirement of Irbp18 in photoreceptors.

We next examined parkin (Park) mutants, which activate the ISR.25 Consistently, Parkd21/Park25 transheterozygotes strongly induce the Thorintron-DsRed reporter expression.47 Strikingly, the Thorintron-DsRed signal was substantially reduced in Parkd21/Park25 mutant larvae in the Irbp18f05006 homozygous background (Figure 7C). We followed the development of these larvae by assessing their adult eclosion rates. When reared in vials containing 20 larvae each, approximately 78% of the Parkd21/Park25 flies and 76% of the Irbp18f05006 single mutants successfully eclosed as adults. However, only 5% of the Parkd21, Irbp18f05006/Park25, Irbp18f05006 double mutants reached the adult stage under equivalent conditions (Figure 7D), indicating an essential role of Irbp18 in the survival of Park mutants. As in the larval stage, the adult Parkd21/Park25 mutants exhibited strong Thorintron-DsRed expression, which was suppressed in the few surviving Parkd21, Irbp18f05006/Park25, Irbp18f05006 double mutant adults (Figure 7E). These results suggest that Irbp18 mediates ISR signaling in Park mutants and contributes to their survival.

DISCUSSION

Crc and Xrp1 are established transcription factors of the ISR. These bZIP transcription factors bind target DNAs as dimers, but the identity of crc’s heterodimerization partner had remained unclear. Here, we provided genetic and genomic evidence supporting an essential role of Irbp18 in crc-mediated ISR signaling.

Previous studies established Irbp18 as a heterodimerization partner of Xrp1.30 Consistently, the loss of Irbp18 gives rise to phenotypes similar to Xrp1 loss in various biological contexts, including hybrid dysgenesis,30 Ribosomal protein (Rp) mutant-induced cell competition,31,33 and the cellular response to spliceosome impairment.32 Our results with the Xrp1 reporter, gstD-GFP, further support Irbp18’s role in Xrp1 target gene induction.

In the current study, we specifically examined young ninaEG69D adult photoreceptors (0 to 1-day after adult eclosion), during which photoreceptors induce target genes with a clear ISR signature as indicated by the enriched GO terms. Of note, our previous gene expression profiling study with older adult photoreceptors (5-days-old) did not show enrichment of GO terms associated with ISR.48 It is now well established that ISR signaling promotes a negative feedback loop that dampens the signaling pathway over time,49,50 which may contribute to the age-related loss of the ISR gene expression signature in older photoreceptors.

Through gene expression profiling at an early time point, we asked whether Irbp18 is required for the function of other transcription factors associated with ninaEG69D. We considered crc as a candidate based on a previous in vitro study indicating that Irbp18 can form stable heterodimers with only two bZIP transcription factors—Xrp1 and crc.26 Yet, the in vivo significance of the crc-Irbp18 association had remained unknown. For example, in the ribosomal subunit mutant cell competition model, Irbp18 loss phenocopied Xrp1 loss, but not crc loss.31 Our data, which combines cell type-specific gene expression profiling, CUT&RUN, and reporter analysis, now provide strong evidence that Irbp18 is broadly required for crc target gene expression.

We could gain insights into the specificity of Irbp18 function by comparing different datasets. One can see in the PCA plots that the Irbp18+ and Irbp18f05006 samples are more clearly separated in the Rh1-Gal4/UAS-crc dataset (Figure 4B), but clustered together in the ninaEG69D data (Figure 1D). The clustering of Irbp18+ and Irbp18f05006 PCA plots in the ninaEG69D background may be because ninaEG69D stimulates additional UPR pathways that include the Ire1-Xbp1 pathway,23 and targets of those other pathways are not necessarily affected by the loss of Irbp18. Supporting this view, the loss of Irbp18 did not suppress the active Xbp1 (not to be confused with Xrp1) overexpression phenotype (Figure S4). We also note that the bZIP dimerization properties have been systematically profiled in vitro, but Xbp1-Irbp18 interaction was not observed.26

While there is little evidence to support Irbp18 as a dimerization partner of Xbp1, some of the downstream target genes could be under the combinatorial control of multiple stress-response pathways. This would explain why a well-established Xbp1 target gene, like Hsc-3,23 is also reduced in our Irbp18 mutant photoreceptor dataset. It also explains why crc overexpression is sufficient to induce only a fraction of the genes that appear to be crc/Irbp18 targets. The target genes that require crc/Irbp18, but not sufficient to be induced by crc overexpression, could be under a combinatorial control together with other transcription factors associated with the UPR.

Several studies have examined the roles of mammalian C/EBP family members in ATF4-mediated gene expression, but the results have been inconsistent. Some studies suggest ATF4 pre-dominantly partners with CEBPB,29,51-53 others point to CEBPG,54 and still others report ATF4 forming heterodimers with CREB3L2.55 Our current study in Drosophila identifies Irbp18 as the primary C/EBP member required for ATF4 (crc) signaling.

In vitro studies had shown that crc can also form a stable heterodimer with the bZIP factor, slbo.26 We found that the loss of slbo did not block the induction of the crc target reporter, dgp-1-DsRed, in eye imaginal discs. Because this negative result could be related to the low levels of endogenous slbo transcripts, we also overexpressed UAS-slbo and examined if it could substitute for Irbp18 loss. While we found no evidence for slbo in partnering with crc in eye imaginal discs, we cannot exclude roles of slbo in other tissues and in other target genes, particularly since slbo and ISR are implicated in border cell migration in the Drosophila ovary.56

We have used two Drosophila disease models to explore potential pathological roles of Irbp18. In the photoreceptors, the loss of Irbp18 caused an age-related retinal degeneration, consistent with previous findings that crc loss causes retinal degeneration.20 In addition, Irbp18 was required for the proper development of parkin mutants, which serves as a Parkinson’s disease model.

In conclusion, our results indicate that Irbp18 has a role beyond regulating Xrp1 target genes. Our gene expression profiling, CUT&RUN, and reporter analyses identify Irbp18 as a critical cofactor of crc in the ISR-mediated gene expression program. As a critical partner of both Xrp1 and crc, Irbp18 appears to function at a critical node of ISR signaling. Given the expanding list of physiological and pathological contexts involving ISR, these findings provide important mechanistic insights into the transcriptional control of this pathway.

Limitations of the study

A few different Gal4 drivers were used to conduct experiments in different tissues, and we highlight the common outcomes in different experimental setups. Regarding the Irbp18 loss-of-function study, a majority of the experiments were conducted in the Irbp18f05006 whole body mutants. While we have complemented the analysis with a few tissue-specific Irbp18 RNAi experiments, we could not fully rule out possible non-autonomous and systemic effects in some of the Irbp18f05006 phenotypes.

METHOD DETAILS

Molecular cloning and dgp-1-DsRed generation

To use as the 3′ UTR for reporter transgene, the SV40 3′ UTR was PCR’d from the pUAST plasmid using the oligos listed in the key resources table, and subcloned into the NotI XbaI sites of the pattB vector. Into the BamHI and NotI sites of this plasmid, we subcloned the dgp-1-DsRed DNA, which had the 982 bp dgp-1 regulatory DNA followed by the DsRed coding sequence. The 982 bp dgp-1 regulatory sequence in this DNA starts immediately after 1st exon of dgp-1 RA isoform, includes the 976bp full intron sequence, and stretches an additional 15 bases into the first exon and dgp-1 RB isoform. The starting and ending sequence of this regulatory DNA is listed in the key resources table. The resulting dgp-1-DsRed-SV40 3′UTR-pattB construct was injected into the VK37 line (BestGene #9752) to generate transgenic flies.

Immunohistochemistry, phalloidin labeling, and imaging

Tissues were dissected in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (043368.9 M, Thermo Scientific Chemicals) for 25 min at room temperature. For imaginal disc immunohistochemistry, we followed a standard immuno-labeling protocol, using PBS with 0.2% Triton X-100 for antibody incubation and washing steps. For adult retina immunohistochemistry, PBS containing 0.4% Triton X-100 was used. For phalloidin labeling, fixed retinas were incubated with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated phalloidin (1:200 dilution) for 3 h at room temperature, followed by three additional washes with PBS containing 0.4% Triton X-100. Samples were mounted in 50% glycerol, and imaging was done using Zeiss 700 and 800 microscopes (Oberko-chen, Germany). Fiji (Schindelin et al., 2019) was used for image analysis. Whole larvae and fly images were taken with a SMZ1500 (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) microscope and NIS Elements BR camera (Nikon). DsRed signals from whole larvae were taken with 300 ms exposure time. The fluorescence signal intensities were quantified using the average pixel intensity (Mean Gray Value) measurement function available in ImageJ. Mean Gray Value is defined as the sum of gray values within a selection divided by the number of pixels.

Scanning electron microscopy

For scanning electron microscopy imaging, adult flies were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde, followed by dehydration and drying in HMDS solvent. The samples were sputter coated with gold-palladium and imaged at the NYU Langone Microscopy Laboratory.

Photoreceptor-specific nuclear RNA extraction and gene expression profiling

To isolate adult photoreceptor nuclei for gene expression profiling, we tagged those nuclear membranes with KASH-GFP using Rh1-Gal4>UAS-EGFP::Msp-300KASH flies.35,48 Approximately 500 adult fly heads (males and females combined, within 1 day of eclosion) per genotype were lysed in ice-cold nuclear isolation buffer (10 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.5; 2.5 mM MgCl2; 10 mM KCl) with a Dounce homogenizer. The homogenate was filtered through a 40μm Flowmi cell strainer (VWR, cat. #BAH136800040), followed by incubation with anti-EGFP-coupled protein G Dynabeads (Invitrogen, cat. #10003D) for 1 h at 4°C. The beads were collected using a magnetic microcentrifuge tube holder (Sigma, cat. #Z740155). Following washes (with PBS, pH 7.4; 2.5mM MgCl2), the beads were resuspended in a final volume of 150μL of wash buffer. The post-isolation nuclei were suspended in 1mL of Trizol reagent (Life Technologies, cat. #15596018) for RNA extraction following standard procedures. Prior to RNA precipitation with isopropanol, 0.3 M sodium acetate and glycogen were added to facilitate visualization of the RNA pellet. We then suspended the pellet in RNAse-free water and purified it using a Qiagen RNeasy MinElute cleanup kit (Qiagen, cat. #74204) following standard protocols. For the ninaEG69D/+ gene expression profiling, 2 biological replicates for each genotype were analyzed. For the Rh1-Gal4 > UAS-crc profiling, 3 biological replicates per genotype were examined.

RNA-seq and data processing

The NYU Genome Technology Center performed library preparation and RNA sequencing. For ribodepletion, we utilized a custom Drosophila Nugen Ovation Trio low-input library preparation kit (Tecan Genomics), using approximately 20 ng total RNA per sample. For sequencing, we performed paired-end 50bp sequencing of samples on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, cat. #20012850) using half of a 100 cycle SP flow cell (Illumina, cat. #20027464). To process the fastq files, we used the Seq-N-Slide automated workflow developed by Igor Dolgalev (https://github.com/igordot/sns). For read mapping, we used the alignment program STAR (v2.6.1 d) to map reads of each sample to the Drosophila melanogaster reference genome dm6, and for quality control we used the application Fastq Screen (v0.13.0) to check for contaminating sequences. We employed featureCounts (Subread package v1.6.3) to generate matrices of read counts for annotated genomic features. For differential gene expression analysis between groups of samples, we used the DESeq2 package (R v3.6.1).

CUT&RUN

For CUT&RUN from adult Drosophila photoreceptors, we expressed crc-3xHA (from FlyORF) using the Rh1-Gal4 driver, in either Irbp18+ or Irbp18f05006 genetic background. We used approximately 800 fly heads per genotype (males and females combined, within 3 days of eclosion). These flies also had UAS-KASH-GFP, generating GFP-tagged nuclei for photoreceptor nuclei purification. After isolating these nuclei with anti-GFP beads (see above), the EpiCypher CUTANA protocol was followed to perform CUT&RUN. In brief, the nuclei were permeabilized with the dig-wash buffer containing 0.025% of digitonin. Rabbit anti-HA (Abcam 9110; dilution 1:100) was used to recognize crc-3xHA, and a control IgG (Abcam 171870; dilution 1:50) was used as a negative control. The antibodies were incubated with the nuclei overnight at 4°C. After washing with the digitonin-wash buffer, the nuclei were incubated with Protein A/G-MNase fusion protein at 4°C for 1 h. After washes with the digitonin wash buffer, the MNase was activated by the addition of CaCl2. The MNase digestion was performed at 4°C for 4 h before stopping the reaction and isolating the DNA fragments for sequencing (NovaSeq 6000).

For the bioinformatic processing of the reads, we followed version 3.2 of the nfcore/cutandrun pipeline,64 implemented via Nextflow 24.10.3.65 Additionally, we referenced the Henikoff lab CUT&Tag tutorial (https://doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.bjk2kkye). In brief, we used Bowtie266 to align the sequence reads to the Drosophila melanogaster reference genome dm6. Normalization mode for the tracks was set to CPM, and normalization bin size was set to 50. The CPM-normalized bigwig files were generated using deepTools.67 Peak calling was performed using MACS268 with an FDR threshold below 0.01. Consensus peaks were generated via bedtools69 and filtered to include only those present in both biological replicates. We used HOMER70 to annotate consensus peaks. As represented in Figure 3E, the values from independent IgG CUT&RUN (negative control) samples were subtracted from each replicate via deepTools bigWigCompare. After subtraction, the replicates in each condition were visualized using Integrated Genomics Viewer (IGV).71 We generated the heatmap using deepTools, specifically using the computerMatrix, plotProfile, and plotHeatmap functions. Two biological replicates for each condition were examined. Quality metrics were recorded via MultiQC.72

RT-PCR

RT-PCR was performed using standard protocols, using Power SYBR Green PCR master mix (Life Technologies, cat #4367659). The mRNA fold change was estimated using the ΔΔCt method. The oligos used for RT-PCR are listed in the key resources table, listed in 5′ to 3′ orientation.

Retinal degeneration assay

All retinal degeneration assays were performed with mixed sex flies in the cn, bw −/− background to eliminate eye pigments that otherwise affect the course of retinal degeneration. The Irbp18f05006 flies were backcrossed to a control w1118 strain (Bloomington Stock Center #5905) for five generations and incubated in the 25°C incubator with 1000 lux of light. For retinal degeneration assays under constant darkness, the flies were reared in an enclosed cardboard box in the 25°C incubator. Retinal degeneration was assessed based on fluorescent pseudopupils originating from the Rh1-GFP transgene. Those with clear trapezoidal pseudopupils were marked as intact photoreceptors.

Adult eclosion rate analysis

All flies had NP1-Gal4, Thorintron-dsRed/+ on the 2nd chromosome, which was used to assess crc activity. To reduce the effect of background mutations, the Parkind21 chromosome was backcrossed for 3 generations to w1118, and Parkind21, Irbp18f05006 was backcrossed to a control Irbp18f05006 line for 2 generations. This Irbp18f05006 control was itself backcrossed to w1118 for 5 generations. 20 larvae (2nd or early 3rd instar stage) were moved to individual vials, and the adults eclosing from these vials were counted to calculate the adult eclosion rate (percentage).
