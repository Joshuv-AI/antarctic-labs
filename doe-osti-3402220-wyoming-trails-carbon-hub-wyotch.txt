DE-FE0032347 Final Technical Report ii

 Executive Summary
 The Wyoming Trails Carbon Hub (WyoTCH) project completed a front-end engineering
 and design (FEED) study for a commercial-scale, open-access carbon dioxide (CO2)
 transport pipeline in Wyoming under U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Award DE-
 FE0032347, funded through the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law Carbon Capture
 Technology Program and administered by the National Energy Technology Laboratory.
 The project’s approach of designing a multi-source, multi-destination pipeline, rather
 than a dedicated line serving a single project, would lower the barrier to entry for
 individual CO2 projects. The projects would leverage Wyoming's concentrated industrial
 and power generation CO2 sources, its existing CO2 pipeline infrastructure, and its
 extensive CO2 storage and utilization capacity.
 The FEED study finalized a pipeline network extending approximately 386 miles across
 Natrona, Converse, Platte, Laramie, Niobrara, and Goshen counties, sized to carry up to
 9.6 million metric tons of CO2 per year (MtCO2/yr). The project is designed to be
 optionally completed in two construction phases, with an initial build-out of roughly 5.4
 MtCO2/yr followed by an additional 4.2 MtCO2/yr to support varying CO2 project
 timelines. The network is designed with capacity to serve a coal-fired power generating
 station along with several smaller industrial sources along the route and offtake from
 two existing CO2 pipelines. The project delivers CO2 to a storage complex near Glenrock,
 Wyoming and to a planned CO2 utilization facility in Niobrara County.
 The project produced a complete FEED package including regulatory, economic, and
 environmental analyses. The project scope included six tasks: project management,
 engineering design, regulatory plan, community benefits plan (CBP), business case
 analysis, and environmental safety & health (ES&H). Work on the community benefits
 task was not completed per DOE memorandum "Cease All Activities Associated with
 DEI and CBP" dated January 28, 2025.
 Four findings shape the project's outlook. First, the pipeline is technically feasible as
 designed. Second, the great majority of the route crosses private property, which makes
 voluntary easement negotiation a critical factor influencing project schedule, cost, and
 overall viability. Third, at least a portion of the pipeline network is likely to be
 economically viable, with two of the four segments performing at or above project
 financial targets. Finally, full system economic viability will depend on willingness to pay
 higher tariffs to transport CO2 to the CO2 utilization facility and/or favorable financial
 conditions such as increased duration of the federal 45Q tax credit and the availability of
 long-term, low-rate financing.

 SECTION 1

 Introduction
 1.1 Project Background
 The Wyoming Trails Carbon Hub (WyoTCH) project was undertaken in accordance with
 the requirements of the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE) DE-FOA-0002730 “Bipartisan
 Infrastructure Law (BIL): Carbon Capture Technology Program, Front-End Engineering
 and Design for Carbon Dioxide (CO2) Transport.” In accordance with the 42 USC 16292,
 BIL Section 40303, the objective of DE-FOA-0002730 was to fund the completion of
 front-end engineering and design (FEED) studies to support and accelerate commercial-
 scale CO2 transport projects for anthropogenic CO2 disposition capable of contributing
 to economies of scale, supporting transport volume growth, improving system
 efficiency, and maximizing optionality and connectivity among CO2 source, conversion,
 and storage locations.
 The FOA's Area of Interest, FEED Studies for CO2 Transport Infrastructure (Onshore and
 Offshore), required that proposed systems be capable of moving a minimum of one
 million metric tons of anthropogenic CO2 annually (MtCO2/yr), from two or more capture
 sources to one or more carbon sinks. Permissible CO2 sources included point-source
 capture at industrial or power generation facilities and CO2 obtained from removal
 approaches. Permissible carbon sinks included secure geologic storage locations, CO2
 conversion locations, or a combination of both. The scope of the FOA project covered
 the FEED study for the transport system only; it did not include FEED studies or other
 work on the capture and sink elements of the system.
 The WyoTCH project seeks to accelerate growth of Wyoming’s carbon capture,
 utilization, and storage (CCUS) industry by developing a commercial-scale, open-
 access CO2 transport pipeline. The pipeline will connect the state’s concentrated
 industrial and power generation CO2 sources, existing CO2 pipelines, and considerable
 storage capacity and utilization opportunities. By designing WyoTCH as an open-access,
 multi-source, multi-sink network rather than a pipeline dedicated to a single capture
 project, WyoTCH aims to lower the barrier to entry for individual entities seeking to
 transport CO2. The open-access pipeline will be accessible to and support all industries
 within the state with demand for CO2 transportation. The project will include excess
 capacity, preserving room for future expansion to additional existing and emerging
 demand for CO2 transportation and opportunities for connection with neighboring
 states.

DE-FE0032347 Final Technical Report 1

 The WyoTCH project capitalizes on Wyoming’s distinctive set of advantages for CCUS
 development. First, Wyoming has a concentrated mix of large stationary CO2 sources
 including multiple coal-fired power plants required to evaluate carbon capture under the
 state’s Reliable and Dispatchable Low Carbon Energy Standards law. In addition to
 abundant CO2 sources, Wyoming has extensive, high-quality storage and utilization
 capacity positions throughout the state. Second, the Wyoming Department of
 Environmental Quality (WDEQ) was granted primacy for Class VI underground injection
 control permitting in 2020. This allows Wyoming project developers to avoid backlogs
 associated with the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA) permitting process.
 Third, Wyoming has extensive experience and sustained investment in its CCUS industry
 from private industry, state government, and the U.S. DOE. Wyoming is home to the
 world's largest carbon capture project, ExxonMobil’s Shute Creek Facility, along with the
 ExxonMobil/Contango-owned Greencore CO2 pipeline extending from the southwest
 corner to the northeast of the State. U.S. DOE investments have included the
 CarbonSAFE Phase I, II, and III storage characterization project supporting Basin
 Electric's Dry Fork Station near Gillette, a companion commercial-scale FEED study for
 a membrane-based CO2 capture system at the same facility, and a CarbonSAFE Phase I
 storage project in the Rock Springs Uplift in southwest Wyoming. Finally, Wyoming has
 laid the groundwork for a statewide transport system through the Wyoming Pipeline
 Corridor Initiative (WPCI), a multi-million-dollar, decade-long state investment to
 designate corridors for CO2 pipeline development that culminated in a record of
 decision signed by the U.S. Secretary of the Interior.

 1.3 Pipeline Network Overview
 The final WyoTCH CO2 transport pipeline design extends approximately 386 miles across
 southeastern Wyoming, traversing Natrona, Converse, Platte, Laramie, Niobrara, and
 Goshen counties, as shown in Figure 1. The pipeline is designed to connect multiple
 anthropogenic CO2 sources to permanent geologic storage and utilization capacity
 within the state. As finalized through this FEED study, the base network is sized to carry
 up to 9.6 MtCO2/yr, built in two phases, an initial build-out of approximately 5.4 MtCO2/yr
 followed by an additional 4.2 MtCO2/yr of capacity as further CO2 demand materializes,
 with Laramie River Station, a coal-fired power generating facility, as its principal source
 alongside several smaller industrial sources along the route. The network delivers CO2 to
 an anchor sink complex near Glenrock, Wyoming, referred to throughout this report as
 the Powder River Basin (PRB) Gateway, and, via a lateral connection, to a planned CO2
 utilization facility, ANGH, in Niobrara County, while interconnecting with two existing
 CO2 pipelines traversing the state, the ExxonMobil/Contango-owned Greencore pipeline
 and the Tallgrass-owned Trailblazer pipeline.

 SECTION 2

 Project Management
 and Planning
 3.1 Project Management Plan
 The WyoTCH management team developed and maintained a project management plan
 governing task coordination, budget and schedule management, and risk oversight for
 the duration of the WyoTCH Pipeline project, submitting an initial version within the first
 month of the period of performance and updating it as necessary to reflect changes in
 technical, cost, or schedule baselines. Management of the project and DOE-required
 meetings and reporting were also completed under this task. The majority of the task
 addressed project governance rather than technical approaches and technical
 deliverables. During the project, the project’s technical approach supported the
 project’s ability to manage risks to the project associated with changes in the project
 team and project environment. The technical approach and its role in the project’s risk
 management approach is described here as a lesson learned.
 Lesson Learned: The Role of Technical Approach in Project Risk Management
 The project's technical approach followed a three-stage route refinement and
 deliverable development process with a concurrent concept study evaluating future
 expansion potential. Each stage included development of business, regulatory, and
 technical analysis and route refinement to incorporate the new information derived from
 those analyses. The process began with the initial route from the project proposal. This
 served as the starting point for the first route refinement and deliverable development
 stage that included an updated feasibility study, initial business case, and regulatory
 assessment. The updated feasibility study incorporated new information about CCUS
 developments in Wyoming and changes in the project environment and re-evaluated the
 WyoTCH pipeline network including CO2 storage and utilization locations and CO2
 sources, possible iterations of the route, and potential local and regional expansion
 opportunities and potential routing for additional pipelines. The project team used the
 findings from the feasibility study, initial business case, and initial regulatory
 assessment to update the route before continuing to more detailed engineering work. In
 the second stage of route refinement and deliverable development, the updated route
 then underwent a detailed engineering route evaluation and hydraulic analysis. The
 team then updated and finalized the route based on insights from these deliverables.
 The finalized route served as the basis for the third final phase of development of

 engineering deliverables and final business case, regulatory, and ES&H planning
 documents. Alongside the route refinement and deliverable development process, the
 team developed and refined a concept study evaluating future potential network
 expansions.

 Figure 3: Flowchart showing project technical approach.

 Prior to project start and throughout the course of the project, a number of changes
 among project stakeholders and in the project environment occurred impacting the
 project that necessitated changes to the pipeline network’s route to ensure the pipeline
 would be technically and economically viable. Examples of changes impacting the
 project included changes in priorities for project commercial partners, changes in some
 sources’ CCUS plans, and development of other CCUS and energy infrastructure
 projects in Wyoming.
 From a project management perspective, the inclusion of an early-stage feasibility study
 to reevaluate the proposal under conditions present at project start combined with the
 staged approach to route refinement and deliverable development was critical to
 managing risk to the project associated with changes impacting the project. The
 Feasibility Plan, conducted early in the project, provided the opportunity to assess
 changes in the project environment that arose between proposal submittal and project
 start and adjust the pipeline route as needed to accommodate these changes without
 disrupting the broader project schedule. Additionally, because the Feasibility Plan
 considered a broad set of potential sources, sinks, and routing alternatives, the project
 had a good starting point to assess routing adjustments required to accommodate new
 information as the project continued to evolve. The multi-stage process for route
 refinement and deliverable development provided the project with the ability to
 incorporate changes while minimizing rework.
 Future FEED-stage CO2 transport projects that develop over an extended time can
 benefit from a similar technical approach. Because of the nature of CO2 transport
 projects depending on multiple, often independent individual capture, storage, and
 utilization projects, it is likely that the projects would experience changes impacting the
 transport project’s route. These projects are subject to independent stakeholder
 priorities and timelines for financing, permitting, and development that can introduce
 delays or withdrawals not directly controlled by the transport project developers. The
 WyoTCH project’s technical approach can offer similar transport projects improved
 ability to adapt to changes with the projects’ sources and sinks, needs of project
 partners, and the project environment to ensure the project’s economic and technical
 viability.

 3.2 Workforce Readiness Plan
 The project team developed a workforce readiness plan describing the skillsets required
 for the future commercialization and deployment of CO2 pipeline transport projects,
 assess the availability of the required workforce, and identify the apprenticeships,
 certifications, and academic training pathways currently available to support that
 workforce. The plan found that the majority of direct pipeline construction and
 operations and maintenance (O&M) positions require a high school diploma or
 equivalent paired with position-specific technical training or certification. Specialized
 roles in engineering, architecture, project management, and environmental and
 regulatory compliance require a bachelor's degree or higher. Wyoming's workforce,
 given the state's extensive history in oil, gas, and coal, is better positioned to supply this
 labor than the national workforce at large. In addition to direct workforce needs, the plan
 discussed indirect workforce needs to support the CCUS infrastructure connected by
 the pipeline. Examples of these types of roles include specialized capture-facility
 operating roles and storage-site characterization, drilling, and monitoring positions.
 Workforce availability for these roles, while not directly required for the pipeline,
 impacts the pipeline's viability due to its interdependencies with the capture, storage,
 and utilization facilities it connects.
 Regarding training infrastructure to support the workforce, the plan identified extensive
 opportunities available in Wyoming to support both direct and indirect workforce needs.
 The University of Wyoming offers an undergraduate certificate program in CCUS.
 Additionally, training and certification resources are offered directly by project partners
 across the capture, transport, and storage segments of the value chain. The Wyoming
 Workforce Development Council coordinates sector strategies, career pathways, and
 apprenticeships statewide. The plan did not expect new specialized programs to be
 needed to support the WyoTCH project’s workforce needs. The plan did note the
 likelihood for apprenticeship programs or opportunities to be created given the
 apprenticeship requirement under Section 45Q of the Internal Revenue Code, which
 conditions eligibility for the full carbon oxide sequestration tax credit on capture facility
 operators dedicating at least 15 percent of labor hours to apprenticeships.

 SECTION 3

 Engineering Design
 3.1 Project Parameters Outline
 The project parameters outline deliverables establish the foundational criteria for the
 WyoTCH pipeline that underlie subsequent engineering work. These deliverables
 captured site characteristics and ambient conditions in the project area, defined CO2
 product gas composition specifications from identified capture sources, identified
 applicable codes and standards (including 49 Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) Part
 195 and relevant American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) and American
 National Standards Institute (ANSI) standards), and established the project’s
 environmental, safety, and health (ES&H) framework. Deliverables included
 environmental and regulatory reports organized by pipeline segment and a product gas
 composition specification.
 Environmental and Regulatory Reports
 The WyoTCH project team conducted analysis of environmental, biological, and cultural
 resources within or adjacent to each segment's footprint and identified the regulatory
 permitting agencies and associated authorizations required for each pipeline segment.
 The project team evaluated a 300-foot buffer centered on each route for environmental
 and biological features using USGS topographic, hydrologic, and hydrography (NHD)
 data, USFWS National Wetland Inventory (NWI) and critical habitat data, Wyoming
 Natural Diversity Database sensitive species data, Natural Resources Conservation
 Service (NRCS) soil survey data, Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) flood
 hazard data, and WGFD sage grouse core and connectivity data. A cultural resources
 contractor separately identified resources within a 2,000-foot buffer through review of
 the National Register of Historic Places (NRHP) and Wyoming SHPO records.
 Site Characterization
 Habitat: The WyoTCH pipeline corridors collectively span the Powder River Basin and
 Casper Arch Level IV ecoregions of the Northwestern Great Plains Level III Ecoregion,
 the Foothill Shrublands Level IV ecoregion of the Southern Rockies Level III Ecoregion,
 and, within the High Plains Level III Ecoregion, the Moderate Relief Rangeland, Flat to
 Rolling Cropland, Platte River Valley and Terraces, Pine Bluffs, and Sandy and Silty
 Tablelands Level IV ecoregions.
 Topography: A review of USGS 7.5-Minute Quadrangle maps covering each corridor
 showed elevation ranges and alignment features specific to each segment. Along the
 Contango-PRB Gateway segment, elevation ranges between ±5,050 ft. and ±5,900 ft.

 Along the Junction-ANGH segment, elevation ranges between ±4,505 ft. and ±5,500 ft.
 Along the LRS-PRB Gateway segment, elevation ranges between ±4,450 ft. and ±5,525
 ft. Along the TSS-PRB Gateway segment, elevation ranges between ±4,450 ft. and ±5,905
 ft.
 Soils: Review of the NRCS mapped soil units showed that the WyoTCH pipeline route
 crosses a broad and largely non-hydric mix of loams, sandy loams, and complex soil
 associations. The Junction-ANGH segment does not cross any hydric soils. The
 Contango-PRB Gateway segment crosses one single hydric soil, the Aquic Ustifluvents,
 saline-Orpha complex associated with Sand Spring Creek in Natrona County. The TSS-
 PRB Gateway segment crosses multiple hydric soils: the Fluvaquentic Endoaquolls-
 Whetsoon complex associated with Cottonwood Creek in Platte County, the Aeric
 Fluvaquents-Oxyaquic Torrifluvents complex associated with a stock pond check dam
 wetland and Dry Creek in Converse County, the Merden variant silty clay loam
 associated with Muddy Creek and its North Fork, the Merden silty clay loam associated
 with Bear Creek, and the partially hydric Haverdad-Clarkelen-Kovich complex
 associated with Little Bear Creek. The LRS-PRB Gateway segment also crosses the
 Fluvaquentic Endoaquolls-Whetsoon complex and the Aeric Fluvaquents-Oxyaquic
 Torrifluvents complex identified with the TSS-PRB Gateway segment.
 Hydrology: Each segment's hydrology assessment identified the sub-basins, named
 waterways, and FEMA flood zones crossed by its corridor. The Junction-ANGH segment
 sub-basins include the Niobrara Headwaters, Lance, and Glendo Reservoir. The
 proposed route will cross six named waterways/tributaries and one FEMA Flood Zone A
 feature. The Contango-PRB Gateway segment sub-basins include the Salt and Middle
 North Platte-Casper. The proposed route will cross five named waterways/tributaries
 and one FEMA Flood Zone A feature. The TSS-PRB Gateway segment sub-basins include
 the Middle North Platte-Scotts Bluff, Lower Laramie, Horse, Upper Lodgepole, Middle
 North Platte-Casper, and Glendo Reservoir. The proposed route will cross 31 named
 waterways/tributaries and 27 FEMA Flood Zone A features. The LRS-PRB Gateway
 segment crosses a subset of the sub-basins (Lower Laramie, Middle North Platte-
 Casper, and Glendo Reservoir), named waterways (23), and FEMA Flood Zone A features
 (7) crossed by the TSS-PRB Gateway segment.
 Wetlands and Waters
 The WyoTCH pipeline route crosses multiple wetlands and waters mapped by the
 USFWS NWI and streams mapped by the USGS NHD. These include palustrine aquatic
 bed, palustrine emergent, palustrine scrub shrub, palustrine forested, palustrine
 unconsolidated shore habitats and lower perennial, upper perennial, riverine
 intermittent, and unknown perennial stream beds. The environmental and regulatory
 reports identify 85 crossing locations along the Contango-PRB Gateway segment, 81
 along the Junction-ANGH segment, 191 along the TSS-PRB Gateway segment, and 145
 along the LRS-PRB Gateway segment.

 Operating Philosophy
 The project team developed an operating philosophy document to establish the control
 basis for the WyoTCH CO2 transport system. Each pump station is designed for N+1
 redundancy using variable-speed booster and mainline pump trains under a low-select
 control scheme between suction and discharge pressure. Centralized supervisory
 control is hosted at the PRB Gateway Control Center, but local programmable logic
 controllers retain autonomy over sequencing, trips, and interlocks in the event of a
 communications loss. Normal operation maintains continuous, steady-state dense-
 phase flow through automated discharge pressure control. Start-up follows a sequential
 line-pack and pressurization procedure and shutdown proceeds upstream to
 downstream with controlled depressurization as discussed in the commissioning
 specifications section of this report. A hierarchical emergency shutdown structure
 governs abnormal conditions, escalating from local equipment-level trips through
 station-level isolation to system-wide shutdown. A cause-and-effect matrix defines
 automated responses to high or low pressure, temperature, vibration, and fire or gas
 detection events. The document also addresses coordinated inter-station scenarios

 facilities, planned block valve closures, and wide-area blackstart recovery. The

 3.2.5 Preliminary Hazard and Operability Analysis (HAZOP)
 The project team conducted a Hazard and Operability (HAZOP) Analysis for the WyoTCH
 pipeline in order to identify hazards and potential hazard escalation scenarios, to rank
 these scenarios according to consequence and likelihood, and to develop
 recommendations based on HAZOP findings. The analysis was performed by a
 multidisciplinary team comprising representatives of the project owner, the FEED
 engineering contractor, and an independent process safety facilitation firm. The scope
 of the review included each of the four pipeline segments and associated facilities and
 equipment. Facilities included transfer at CO2 source and destination sites, pump
 stations, and mainline valve sites. Equipment included 12 pig launchers, 12 pig
 receivers, 3 custody transfer meter skids, 8 leak detection meter skids, 24 CO2 booster
 pumps, and 16 pump aftercoolers. The review was organized around three defined
 nodes: the pipeline and mainline valve systems, the CO2 source custody transfer
 metering, and the pump station equipment.
 The HAZOP methodology applied the standard structured technique of pairing process
 parameters, including flow, pressure, and temperature, with guidewords such as "no,"
 "more," and "less" to generate credible process deviations from design intent. For each
 deviation, the team identified credible causes, evaluated potential consequences,
 recorded existing engineering and administrative safeguards, and developed
 recommendations where warranted. The team operated under a defined set of ground
 rules and applied a defined set of guidewords for technical considerations throughout
 the review to ensure consistency and rigor.

 The team evaluated risk using an industry-accepted Risk Assessment Matrix, calibrated
 specifically for the WyoTCH Project. The matrix scores each deviation on a severity scale
 against a probability scale. The severity scale ranged from 0 (no impact) to 5 (extremely
 severe consequence) across four consequence categories: people, assets,
 environment, and reputation. The probability scale ranged from A (never before
 observed in the industry) to E (occurs several times per year at a given location). The
 combination of severity and probability scores yielded an overall Low, Medium, or High
 risk ranking for each identified deviation.
 Applying the calibrated Risk Assessment Matrix to the evaluated deviations indicated
 that, with credited engineering and administrative controls in place, all ranked scenarios
 fell within the Low risk category, including higher-severity scenarios such as inadvertent
 additional pump operation and pipeline line strike. The HAZOP resulted in several
 recommendations to address deviations identified during the analysis. The team
 recommended that a surge analysis be performed during detailed design to confirm that
 transient pressure events associated with valve closure, pump trip, or flow interruption
 are adequately characterized and mitigated, and that a fracture control study together
 with a CO2 dispersion analysis study be conducted to support threat mitigation planning
 and mainline valve spacing tied to loss of containment scenarios along the pipeline
 segments. At the CO2 source transfer locations, the team recommended evaluating the
 need for an ASME Class 1500 ESD valve rating contingent on the final CO2 source facility
 design, and, at both the CO2 source and pump station nodes, implementing a valid flow
 path control scheme within the programmable logic controller (PLC) programming to
 prevent misdirected or bypassed flow paths, such as inadvertent meter bypass through
 valve misalignment, from going undetected during operation. The team further

 additional items were captured as broader project considerations rather than deviation-

 3.2.6 Investigation into Possible Conversion of Service Lines
 The project team evaluated existing pipeline infrastructure within the WyoTCH corridor
 for potential conversion to CO2 service. No existing infrastructure was identified that
 was both 1) available for conversion and 2) aligned with the project's intended route.
 However, the project team prepared a memo with considerations and requirements for
 CO2 service conversion should it become relevant in later stages of the project.
 Converting existing carbon steel pipelines from natural gas or oil service to CO2 service
 requires a technical, regulatory, and economic evaluation to confirm that legacy
 infrastructure can safely support CO2 transport. Initial suitability rests on three factors:
 shippers' volume and pressure requirements, the pipeline's operation and maintenance

DE-FE0032347 Final Technical Report 21

 history and design basis, and applicable regulatory considerations. If a pipeline is a
 viable candidate for conversion based on those factors, detailed evaluation is required
 for regulatory considerations, hydraulic requirements, corrosion and material
 compatibility, and economic viability.
 Regulatory Considerations: Existing pipelines fall under either 49 CFR Part 195
 (hazardous liquids) or Part 192 (natural and other gas). Because supercritical CO2 is
 classified as a hazardous liquid, a pipeline already under Part 195 requires only a
 technical review rather than a formal conversion, while a Part 192 pipeline must follow
 Part 195 conversion of service provisions. Since neither part directly addresses vapor-
 phase CO2 Part 195 compliance is recommended given the similarity of CO2's properties
 across phases. Confirming regulatory readiness requires a due diligence review
 benchmarked against PHMSA Part 195 audit protocols, gathering material test reports,
 inline inspection reports, anomaly and repair records, flow composition history,
 coatings and corrosion control data, depth-of-cover survey results, and new CO2
 dispersion analyses to identify potentially impacted HCAs.
 Hydraulic Requirements: CO2 transport must remain consistently in either the
 supercritical/liquid phase or the gas phase to avoid damage. Supercritical or liquid CO2
 requires operation above its critical pressure of roughly 1,060 to 1,200 psig, typically
 demanding ANSI 900 or higher ratings that exceed the ANSI 600 ratings common to
 existing oil and gas facilities; upgrades or retesting may be necessary. Velocity limits of
 15 ft/s for liquid flow and 40 ft/s for gas phase flow, along with a minimum temperature
 of approximately 34°F, guard against ice and CO2 hydrate formation.
 Corrosion and Material Compatibility: CO2 combined with water forms carbonic acid,
 posing a significant corrosion risk to carbon steel. Low temperature service must be
 evaluated per 49 CFR 195.102(b), since rapid depressurization during commissioning
 can drive temperatures into a damaging range. A chemical assay of the CO2 stream is
 also required to confirm compatibility with non-metallic components such as gaskets
 and valve seals.
 Economic Viability: Conversion economics weigh capital costs for pipeline
 modification, added infrastructure, and safety systems against ongoing operational and
 maintenance costs, with viability determined by comparing projected transport revenue
 and tax incentives against these costs over the pipeline's remaining lifecycle.
 Repurposing existing infrastructure offers potential cost savings over new construction,
 provided lifespan and depreciation are factored into long-term planning. However, if
 significant modification is required for the conversion, conversion may be less
 economical than new construction.

 Dispersion Modeling and HCA Evaluation
 The project team performed CO2 dispersion modeling for all four segments of the
 WyoTCH pipeline. The modeling simulated full-bore rupture releases along each pipeline
 to characterize the potential atmospheric extent of a CO2 release and to support a
 subsequent HCA analysis identifying where a release could affect sensitive receptors.
 The results files for the dispersion analysis and geospatial files showing the HCA
 evaluation results will be publicly available in NETL EDX after the release restriction
 date.
 The dispersion analysis relied on two linked models. A consequence analysis model
 characterized the source term for each rupture scenario, calculating the proportion of
 liquid and vapor released along with the time-varying release rate and overall release
 duration. A dense gas dispersion model, developed for the U.S. Environmental
 Protection Agency and widely used in the public domain, then simulated the
 atmospheric dispersion of the released vapor as a dense gas under summer and winter
 meteorological conditions. Inputs to this model included the mass release rate, release
 duration, and initial liquid mass fraction calculated during source-term characterization,
 together with regional meteorological data and land cover-derived surface roughness
 values.
 The modeling methodology was designed to be conservative at each step in order to
 produce a worst-case estimate of potential impact distances. Releases were assumed
 to consist of 100 percent pure CO2, and a pressure of 2,220 psig was applied, above the
 design basis maximum operating pressure of 2,183 psig. Atmospheric dispersion was
 simulated under Pasquill stability Class F, the most stable and least dispersive
 condition, and each release was modeled as a surface-level vertical jet, which
 maximizes the predicted impact area. The team drew meteorological inputs from three
 regional meteorological stations nearest the pipeline corridors and averaged by season.
 Because each pipeline includes motor operated valves (MOVs) capable of isolating a
 ruptured segment, the modeling effort developed scenarios representing the range of
 pipeline lengths between valves rather than a single worst-case pipeline length. An
 initial set of 26 full-bore rupture scenarios was developed across the four segments,
 varying in segment length, flow rate, and pipeline diameter. Following receipt of a revised
 valve layout containing 62 total valve segments, each new segment was matched to the
 existing scenario whose modeled length most closely approximated it, and the LRS-PRB
 Gateway segment, which received eight additional valves, was reassessed accordingly.

 Four concentration thresholds were applied to the modeling results, each tied to a
 specific exposure duration. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA)
 Permissible Exposure Limit (PEL) of 5,000 parts per million (ppm) represents an 8-hour
 time-weighted average. The OSHA Short-Term Exposure Limit (STEL) of 30,000 ppm
 applies to a 15-minute exposure. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and
 Health (NIOSH) Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health (IDLH) threshold of 40,000 ppm
 applies to instantaneous exposure. A fourth threshold of 105,000 ppm, drawn from
 published guidance on major hazard assessment, reflects a 1 percent probability of
 fatality for an exposure exceeding one minute.
 Across all scenarios, larger pipeline diameters and longer isolable segment lengths
 consistently produced greater downwind impact distances, since both factors increase
 the volume of CO2 available for release before valve isolation is complete. The smaller-
 diameter Junction-ANGH segment produced shorter exceedance distances than the
 three 12-inch segments. For nearly all scenarios, the STEL threshold produced the
 largest maximum impact distance, ranging from 10 feet for a short, small-diameter
 segment to 3,590 feet for a longer, larger-diameter segment under winter conditions.
 Winter conditions generally produced the greatest exceedance distances across
 thresholds, a result attributed to higher regional wind speeds increasing downwind
 dispersion, while the IDLH threshold showed comparatively similar results between
 seasons because it does not incorporate a time-averaging period.
 HCA Evaluation
 The team used the modeling results to evaluate potential impacts to High Consequence
 Areas (HCAs) as defined in 49 CFR § 195.450 and 49 CFR § 195.6, spanning five
 categories: commercially navigable waterways, high population areas, other populated
 areas (OPA), drinking water resources (DW), and ecological unusually sensitive areas
 (ECO). No commercially navigable waterways or coastal ecological unusually sensitive
 areas were present within the WyoTCH study area. Each segment was divided into 100-
 meter lengths and evaluated individually against the maximum impact distance, across
 all seasons and thresholds, to determine whether an HCA fell within the potential
 impact buffer. The team then classified impacts as direct, where the pipeline physically
 intersects the HCA, or indirect, where a release from the pipeline segment has the
 potential to reach the HCA without crossing it directly.
 The Junction-ANGH segment was found to indirectly affect two OPA HCAs, with no
 direct impacts identified. The Contango-PRB Gateway segment directly affected one
 DW and one ECO HCA and indirectly affected an additional DW, ECO, and OPA. The
 LRS-PRB Gateway segment directly affected four DW and one OPA HCA and indirectly
 affected four DW and one OPA HCA. The TSS-PRB Gateway segment, the longest of the
 four segments, directly affected four DW and three OPA HCAs and indirectly affected
 five DW and three OPA HCAs. No high population area impacts were identified for any of
 the four segments.
 The dispersion modeling results represent a conservative, worst-case characterization
 of potential CO2 release impacts rather than a probabilistic estimate of likely outcomes,

DE-FE0032347 Final Technical Report 24

 since the simultaneous occurrence of every conservative assumption applied in the
 analysis is considered unlikely. The majority of identified HCA impacts were associated
 with direct pipeline crossings, with most indirect impacts occurring immediately
 adjacent to these same crossings rather than at isolated locations along the route. More
 than half of the indirect HCA impacts involved drinking water resources, which are not
 expected to experience severe consequences from CO2 exposure given the nature of the
 hazard. The analysis concluded that additional valve placement would provide limited
 benefit in reducing the identified HCA impacts, as existing valves are already positioned
 near most affected HCAs or the indirect impacts occur immediately adjacent to
 unavoidable direct crossings.
 Crack Arrestors (Fracture Propagation Control)
 Dense-phase CO2's two-phase decompression behavior increases the risk of running
 ductile fracture in CO2 pipelines, requiring additional design emphasis on wall thickness
 and material toughness to arrest potential fracture propagation. The two primary
 mitigation measures are improving pipe toughness (through increased wall thickness
 and Charpy impact toughness) and installing external crack arrestors at specified
 positions and intervals along the pipeline. The team deferred specification of pipe
 toughness and crack arrestor locations to detailed design to incorporate additional
 information not available at the FEED study phase. During detailed design, a fracture
 control plan is required to ensure three objectives: preventing brittle fracture under
 normal operating conditions, providing adequate resistance to fracture initiation, and
 arresting fast ductile tearing fracture in the unlikely event of a rupture.
 Emergency Shutdown Capabilities
 Emergency shutdown valves are provided on the incoming and outgoing sides of the
 gathering and distribution hub. In the event of a low-pressure situation that would be
 indicative of a catastrophic pipeline leak or rupture, the emergency shutdown valves
 close at a predetermined set point. For the WyoTCH CO2 Pipeline System, the minimum
 set point should be within a range of 10 percent above the critical pressure of the flowing
 CO2 stream and below the normal operating pressure to be determined. Actuation on
 the emergency shutdown valve shall have a closing time of one second per inch
 diameter.

 the air purge while controlling piping vibration, and monitor and record pressure
 throughout the operation until notified that purging is complete. At the outlet site,
 personnel must install and monitor nitrogen or CO2 gas sampling equipment, record the
 time of first gas detection at the venting point, communicate rising pressure back to the
 inlet site, and continue monitoring gas content until 100 percent nitrogen or CO2 is
 confirmed and the vent is closed. At control and intermediate sites, personnel must
 confirm valves are in the correct operating position, monitor discharging gas for color
 and piping vibration, conduct leak tests, obtain permissions for loading, and maintain
 communications and documentation throughout the operation.
 The commissioning plan also addresses the specific commissioning procedure covering
 the sequence for purging the pipeline and facilities with a nitrogen fill and for
 subsequently filling with dense-phase CO2. Because dense-phase CO2 and nitrogen do
 not mix well, the plan directs use of a separation pig to physically separate the two
 media. Low CO2 temperature presents a particular risk during the first fill, and the plan
 directs that inlet temperature be monitored continuously and that filling be discontinued
 if temperature drops below -20°F with consideration in the throttling process given to
 avoiding the formation of solid CO2. The plan recommends that, as nitrogen pressure at
 each downstream valve reaches a predetermined upper limit, the downstream control
 valve is opened to pressurize the next pipeline segment, and the pipeline valve is then
 opened once pressure is balanced across it, allowing the separation pig to advance and
 progressively fill each subsequent segment with high-pressure dense-phase CO2 in
 sequence.

 The WyoTCH project team conducted an in-depth evaluation of the permitting and other
 regulatory requirements applicable to the siting, construction, and operation of a

 including the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE), the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service

 Wyoming Game and Fish Department (WGFD), OSLI, and the Wyoming State Historic

 5.1 Business Case Analysis
 The WyoTCH project team prepared a business case analysis addressing a technical
 overview of the project, project financial analysis including pro-formas for the base case
 and alternative scenarios, market analyses, deployment projections, and project
 benefits. The Excel pro-forma tool is publicly available in NETL EDX ; the pro-forma
 analyses for the base case and alternation scenarios will be publicly available after the
 release restriction date. The introduction content mirrors the information presented in
 the introduction section of this report. Details of the remainder of the business case
 analysis are discussed further below.
 Base Case Scenario
 The business case employs a spreadsheet-based pro forma model evaluating project
 economics across the 50-year life of the project. The Full System Base case models the
 complete network, comprising the TSS-PRB Gateway, LRS-PRB Gateway, Junction-
 ANGH, and Contango-PRB Gateway segments, under a fixed per-tonne tariff with annual
 cost escalation, a conventional debt-equity capital structure with defined interest and
 equity costs over ten-year terms, a federal tax rate, a maintenance reserve, and an asset
 retirement obligation target. The case conservatively assumes that CO2 destined for
 storage generates revenue for twelve years, corresponding to the duration of the 45Q tax
 credit, and that CO2 destined for utilization generates revenue for thirty years,
 corresponding to the expected life of the utilization facility. Under these assumptions
 the full system is not likely to be profitable, showing a levered equity IRR under the
 project’s target and a payback period longer than the project’s target.
 Additional Scenarios
 The business case analysis evaluated an additional 36 scenarios spanning four
 dimensions of sensitivity: full system vs. independent segments, operating time horizon,
 financing structure, and capital cost sensitivity. First, the team varied system scope
 between the full system and each individual segment operating on a stand-alone basis.
 Second, the team extended the operating horizon from the twelve-year base case
 through the thirty-year and fifty-year cases. Third, the team varied financing structure
 across debt-equity ratio, interest rate, debt term, and an all-equity configuration. Finally,

DE-FE0032347 Final Technical Report 47

 the team tested capital cost sensitivity at reductions and increases relative to the base
 estimate to represent P-10, P-50, P-90 costs. For each scenario the model calculated
 the minimum tariff required to achieve breakeven and to achieve the project’s equity
 return targets.
 Extending the revenue-generating period improves project economics for both the full
 system and the individual segments, with the move from twelve to thirty years
 converting the full system from not profitable to profitable and the further extension to
 fifty years yielding only marginal additional improvement. Financing assumptions
 materially influence equity returns with the strongest financial results achieved with
 long-term, low-rate financing (consistent with terms for subsidized financing). All-equity
 financing configurations uniformly produce the weakest equity returns and the highest
 breakeven tariffs, which reinforces the importance of debt leverage in meeting the
 project’ equity return target.
 While increasing or decreasing capital costs affected returns and project payback
 periods, the changes did not affect overall conclusions about potential profitability of
 the WyoTCH pipeline system or individual components.
 The four standalone segments show markedly different economic performance. The
 Contango-PRB Gateway segment shows strong performance on all scenarios evaluated.
 The LRS-PRB Gateway segment likewise performs at or above project targets in most
 scenarios. The TSS-PRB Gateway segment showed the weakest economics of the four
 segments despite carrying the largest CO2 volume. In order to meet financial
 performance targets, substantially higher tariffs, significant cost reductions, cost-
 sharing with other segments, or grant and subsidy support would be required. The
 Junction-ANGH segment represents a special case because it serves a utilization facility
 that may support higher tariff rates. At assumed tariff rates the segment would meet
 economic targets only in the most optimistic scenarios. In addition to direct tariffs for
 this segment, CO2 entering the segment would additionally bear the TSS trunkline tariff.
 Economic viability of this segment would depend on the utilization facility’s willingness
 to pay for CO2.
 3.0 Market Analysis
 Wyoming presents a strong prospective market for CO2 capture and storage, containing
 clustered emission sources, high-quality storage potential distributed throughout the
 state, and access to significant mileage of existing CO2 pipeline infrastructure. In
 determining the optimal route, the commercial team examined all potential sources in
 and around the state, grouped them into clusters, and evaluated each cluster against
 the concentration and volume of CO2 within each sector, the cost of capture, the
 pipeline length to the nearest potential sinks, and the presence or absence of
 competing CO2 transport infrastructure. The route was subsequently refined to
 accommodate business developments within the state and engineering design
 considerations that arose as engineering deliverable development progressed.

DE-FE0032347 Final Technical Report 48

 The finalized design connects the two major existing CO2 pipeline systems serving
 Wyoming, with the Greencore pipeline extending from southwestern to northeastern
 Wyoming and the Trailblazer pipeline running from Nebraska into southeastern Wyoming
 and northern Colorado. The network anticipates receiving offtake from both systems,
 and interconnection provides additional optionality to source CO2 from other parts of
 Wyoming and from neighboring states. The final route includes seven existing CO2
 sources capable of providing approximately 6.6 MtCO2/yr located directly along or within
 7.5 miles of the alignment. The largest sources along the route are Laramie River Station
 and the Dave Johnston Power Plant, and for purposes of analysis the study assumed
 that only one unit at each facility would be selected for carbon capture and storage
 (CCS), an approach intended to establish the minimum volumes likely to render the
 pipeline viable, with a supplemental model run demonstrating the economies of scale
 available if two units were captured at Laramie River Station. Volume from a single unit
 at Laramie River Station was estimated from capacity factors before and after CCS
 installation, the parasitic load of the capture facility, and an assumed capture rate, and
 because of the longer anticipated timeline for that project, the pipeline is planned to be
 built in phases with the associated volume added in a parallel segment when the facility
 comes online.
 The analysis further identifies announced data center projects along the route as a
 significant source of future demand, noting that many of these projects plan to use co-
 located natural gas combustion as a sole or primary source of electricity. Limited public
 information on power demand and intended power source precludes precise emissions
 estimation, so the team estimated capturable emissions using a typical Wyoming gas
 power combustion emission factor. Beyond CO2 volumes, the presence of these
 facilities creates opportunities for co-location of transmission and fiber-optic lines to
 support offsite power and data transmission needs, and the commercial team is
 investigating co-siting opportunities.
 On the disposition side, multiple Wyoming storage basins offer moderate to high
 capacity, and the state has received Class VI primacy, creating favorable conditions for
 storage developers, with multiple permits issued and additional applications under
 review. The project will deliver CO2 to storage sinks near Glenrock, where a Class VI
 permit application for the initial well is under review, and will also support a utilization
 facility in Niobrara County requiring approximately 1.0 MtCO2/yr for synthetic fuel
 production, which creates important diversification in CO2 disposition and revenue
 generation.
 The pipeline system is not eligible to directly claim tax credits, and its revenue model is
 instead indirectly tied to monetization by the capture facilities using the pipeline for
 transport, with revenues derived from tariffs charged under long-term contracts with
 CO2 source operators whose willingness and ability to pay are contingent on capturing
 economic value from 45Q credits. Alternative monetization pathways, including
 voluntary carbon markets, emerging carbon removal procurement programs, and
 bilateral offtake agreements with corporations, are not currently expected to be primary
 revenue drivers, as their application to point-source industrial capture remains limited

 and they provide less revenue certainty than the 45Q credit. The business model is not
 exclusively dependent on capture monetization, however, because deliveries to the
 utilization facility create willingness to pay based on the price commanded for goods
 produced from CO2, and further utilization opportunities may shift demand from
 transport for storage toward transport for utilization. The assumed tariff rate is based on
 rates observed at other existing CO2 pipelines, and the project would be developed by
 the commercial consortium through a combination of equity and debt financing,
 supplemented where available by federal grant or loan programs, with the range of

 Future Deployment Projection
 The final design substantially improves connectivity for CO2 sources and for storage and
 utilization providers by joining the two major existing CO2 pipelines that enter the state,
 which creates substantial opportunity to expand the network to serve CO2 sources in
 Wyoming and surrounding states. Drawing on the Concept Study, the analysis reports
 that Wyoming and neighboring states hold a substantial and diverse inventory of
 capturable CO2 emissions distributed across hundreds of facilities and dozens of
 industrial sectors, and that although capture cost renders adoption uneconomic for
 many of these facilities, a large subset remains available at moderate average capture
 costs. The principal barrier to expansion is the coordination of storage and utilization
 facility deployment and capture installation at CO2 sources with pipeline deployment,
 though the extensive connectivity introduced by the network offers flexibility to match
 source and sink partners with compatible timelines. Permitting and right-of-way
 acquisition present a further challenge, mitigated in Wyoming by a relatively favorable
 permitting environment demonstrated through the Wyoming Pipeline Corridor Initiative
 (WPCI) and by landowner familiarity with pipeline easements.
 Potential Project Benefits
 The final section concludes that the network would deliver modest benefits in the form
 of emissions reductions, tax revenue, and job creation. Although CO2 transportation
 does not directly reduce emissions, it functions as a critical enabler of reductions at
 point sources, and at full capacity the network would facilitate reductions of 9.6
 MtCO2/yr from facilities in and around Wyoming. Deployment would generate modest
 federal income tax revenue and modest state property tax, license fee, and utility tax
 revenue. Construction would create between 250 and 750 temporary construction and
 inspection positions, in addition to permanent positions for pipeline operations and
 maintenance staff.

 5.4 Concept Study
 The WyoTCH project team developed a concept study evaluating possibilities for
 expanding the WyoTCH base network, which carries up to 9.6 MtCO2/yr as described in
 the Feasibility Plan, into a regional CO2 pipeline network connecting emission sources
 across Wyoming and neighboring states to the state's CO2 storage and utilization sites.
 The study served as input to the Business Case Analysis and was intended to inform
 future network expansion decisions. The concept study report will be publicly available
 in NETL EDX. The team developed the analysis using SimCCSPRO, a mixed-integer linear
 programming optimization tool that identifies cost-effective CO2 capture, transport, and
 storage strategies by determining the optimal set of emission sources, capture volumes,
 transport network configuration, and storage injection locations and volumes needed to
 minimize total annual capital and operating cost, while accounting for terrain-based
 routing challenges, right-of-way constraints, and storage capacity limitations.
 The study's area of interest (AOI) encompasses Wyoming and the neighboring states of
 Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, Colorado, and Utah, along with
 portions of Idaho and Minnesota, within which CO2NCORD software identified 563
 facilities across 30 industry sectors, comprising 699 individual CO2 streams and a
 combined capturable volume of 279.99 MtCO2/yr at an average capture cost of $59.34
 per tonne. Coal-fired power plants represent the largest single source category by far,
 accounting for 182.21 MtCO2/yr across 61 facilities, or 65 percent of total AOI emissions,
 followed by ethanol production at 22.34 MtCO2/yr across 70 facilities, gas-fired power
 plants at 17.41 MtCO2/yr, oil and gas operations at 15.20 MtCO2/yr, and refineries at
 11.14 MtCO2/yr, with the remaining 25 sectors collectively contributing approximately 31
 MtCO2/yr; average capture costs vary considerably by sector, ranging from lower-cost
 chemicals, petrochemicals, and ammonia sources to higher-cost cement, hydrogen,
 and manufacturing sources, with the dominant coal-fired sector carrying a below-
 average capture cost of $45.18 per tonne.
 The study evaluated two network scenarios, each analyzed under two variations.
 Scenario 1, Direct, connects AOI emission sources via direct lateral pipelines to one of
 five candidate connection points along the WyoTCH trunkline route, leveraging the
 existing trunkline corridor as the primary transport backbone to the PRB Gateway sink
 complex, while Scenario 2, Selected, connects sources instead to a set of
 geographically distributed potential storage sites across Wyoming ranging from active

DE-FE0032347 Final Technical Report 52

 CarbonSAFE projects to Class VI permitted sites, allowing each source to route to
 whichever connected sink offers the most economical connection. Each scenario was
 evaluated first under a full-capture baseline connecting all 279.99 MtCO2/yr of AOI
 emissions simultaneously to establish the maximum extent of pipeline network
 required, and second under a ramping capture analysis stepping capture volume
 upward through eleven discrete levels from 25 to 279.99 MtCO2/yr, incorporating
 sources in ascending order of average capture cost at each step to reflect a realistic
 development sequence favoring the most economically attractive sources first.
 Under full capture, both scenarios connect all 563 AOI facilities and extend the network
 across Wyoming into Montana, North Dakota, Nebraska, Colorado, and other
 neighboring states, though the resulting network shapes differ: Scenario 1 produces a
 heavily interconnected hub-and-spoke pattern anchored at the WyoTCH base trunkline,
 while Scenario 2 produces a more distributed set of networks serving proximally located
 storage sites with limited interconnection between them, and in both cases lateral
 connection density is greatest in eastern Nebraska, where coal-fired power plant and
 ethanol facilities cluster most densely. The ramping capture analyses show a similar
 cost progression under both scenarios: at the lowest 25 MtCO2/yr level, networks
 concentrate near the WyoTCH trunkline corridor connecting low-cost chemicals, oil and
 gas, and natural gas processing sources at average costs near $34 to $35 per tonne;
 through the 50 to 175 MtCO2/yr range, networks expand progressively as ethanol,
 mining, and additional oil and gas and power plant sources are incorporated at
 comparable average costs across both scenarios, though Scenario 2's distributed sinks
 produce different routing patterns and a less interconnected network than Scenario 1 at
 equivalent capture levels; and above 175 to 200 MtCO2/yr, the incorporation of large,
 more isolated coal-fired power plants and, at the highest levels, cement, hydrogen, and
 manufacturing sources drives a sustained increase in both network extent and average
 cost, with both scenarios converging on the same full-capture average of $59.34 per
       tonne at 279.99 MtCO2/yr.
 The concept study concludes that Wyoming and its neighboring states hold a
 substantial and diverse inventory of capturable CO2 emissions, and that both the Direct
 and Selected scenarios demonstrate that a network on the order of 100 to 150 MtCO2/yr
 could be developed using relatively low-cost sources averaging $34 to $42 per tonne,
 indicating viable opportunities to expand the WyoTCH base network and de-risk the
 project by incorporating sources beyond those originally planned, while also
 establishing a longer-term vision for broader expansion should market conditions make
 higher-cost sources more economically viable.

 6.2 Hazardous Materials and Pollutant Release Study

 6.6 Emergency Preparedness and Response Plan
 The WyoTCH project team developed an emergency preparedness and response plan
 (EPRP) establishing the framework the pipeline operator will use to prepare for and
 respond to a CO2 release, tailored to the pipeline's specific locations, high-
 consequence area (HCA) determinations, corridor topography, and the proximity of local
 emergency response resources. The EPRP follows the guidelines established in the
 American Petroleum Institute (API) and Liquid Energy Pipeline Association Carbon
 Dioxide Emergency Response Tactical Guidance Document, developed with input from
 the National Association of State Fire Marshals, and is grounded in the pipeline safety
 requirements of 49 CFR § 195.402, the hazardous waste operations standard at 29 CFR
 § 1910.120, and API Recommended Practice 1174. Its purpose is to establish the
 preparedness elements the operator must put in place, including community and
 stakeholder outreach, response drills and exercises, training, Incident Command
 System (ICS) establishment, notification protocols, reportable release thresholds, and
 public safety notifications, while its scope provides the operator the foundation from
 which to develop site-specific, facility-specific emergency procedures for the WyoTCH
 pipeline. At minimum, the EPRP addresses the physical characteristics and hazards of
 CO2, dispersion modeling, leak detection procedures, notification protocols,
 information to be provided to first responders, response actions, responder safety and
 isolation strategies, real-time plume prediction and surveillance, air monitoring, and
 incident management, and it will be reviewed at intervals not exceeding fifteen months
 and at least once per calendar year.
 The EPRP characterizes the physical hazards distinguishing a CO2 pipeline release from
 a liquid, hazardous liquid, or natural gas release. Because CO2 is transported in the
 dense phase at pressures above 1,070 psi, a rupture causes the fluid to expand rapidly
 into a dense gas phase, producing a highly endothermic effect that freezes ambient
 water vapor and vegetation and that can extend the affected area from hundreds of feet
 to miles depending on the nature of the rupture and the pipeline's operating pressure.
 Because CO2 is approximately 1.53 times as dense as air, a release can displace
 breathable air in low-lying areas such as valleys, canals, and ditches, and the resulting
 vapor cloud reduces visibility, particularly at night, while under low-humidity conditions
 a release may disperse without forming a visible cloud at all. Human exposure limits
 established by the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists set the

DE-FE0032347 Final Technical Report 59

 threshold limit value at 5,000 ppm for an eight-hour time-weighted average and 30,000
 ppm as the short-term exposure limit for exposures of up to fifteen minutes, while the
 immediately-dangerous-to-life-or-health level is set at 40,000 ppm; oxygen
 displacement below 19.5 percent begins to impair coordination and mental acuity, with
 levels below 6 percent causing cessation of breathing, convulsions, cardiac arrest, and
 death.
 The EPRP's preparedness planning element directs the operator to conduct outreach to
 populations, emergency managers, public officials, fire and police departments, Local
 Emergency Planning Committees, hazardous materials response teams, and emergency
 response contractors within the HCAs confirmed through the field reconnaissance
 discussed in the Engineering Design chapter of this report, and to identify public
 gathering locations such as hospitals, schools, community centers, and churches
 within each HCA's potential impact radius. The operator's plan must address
 communication and continuing public education, resources and personal protective
 equipment for safely evacuating affected populations, low-oxygen-capable evacuation
 vehicles, and the effectiveness of shelter-in-place measures under conditions of
 reduced visibility and potential vehicle stalling within a dense CO2 vapor cloud. The
 operator must also maintain pipeline line markers meeting 49 CFR § 195.410 along the
 corridor and at public rights-of-way and railroad crossings, conduct emergency
 response drills and exercises adapted from PHMSA's National Preparedness and
 Response Exercise Program guidelines and tailored to HCA-specific scenarios, and
 implement an annual training program addressing CO2 release properties and hazards,
 corrective actions, and impact minimization, alongside minimum certifications in
 Hazardous Waste Operations and Emergency Response, the Incident Command
 System, air monitoring equipment, CO2-specific personal protective equipment,
 respiratory protection, and first aid and CPR.

 Because the potential impact radius for a CO2 release varies with topography and
 weather, the EPRP relies on the pipeline rupture and air dispersion modeling conducted
 during the initial pipeline safety evaluation to provide emergency responders with maps
 illustrating the potential impact radius for each corridor zone, incorporating pipeline
 segment characteristics, isolation valve locations and timing, and weather conditions,
 and calling for overland spread analysis in areas where topography or elevation change
 would extend the plume beyond standard dispersion predictions. Leak detection
 combines physical identification, including visual cues such as dense white cloud or ice
 formation, indirect signs such as airborne dust or bubbling water, characteristic hissing
 sounds, and a musty odor accompanying the otherwise odorless gas, with remote
 identification through SCADA monitoring of pressure and flow data, under which
 controllers are expected to identify a rupture and actuate isolation valve closure within
 thirty minutes of initial identification. Periodic right-of-way patrol surveys,
 supplemented by the additional assessment procedures required within HCAs under 49
 CFR § 195.452, monitor for conditions including vandalism, evidence of leaks, third-
 party excavation, erosion or subsidence, exposed pipe, and excessive vegetation cover.

 The EPRP establishes internal and external notification procedures, including a
 standardized internal reporting protocol supported by a dedicated hotline and chain-of-
 communication flowcharts, and requires notification of the National Response Center
 within one hour of confirmed discovery of a release meeting defined thresholds, among
 them any incident causing death or hospitalization, fire or explosion, property damage
 exceeding $50,000, discharge to a water body, road closure, evacuation or shelter-in-
 place, or public CO2 exposure. The operator must also notify the applicable 9-1-1 Public
 Safety Answering Point, maintaining documented direct-dial numbers for dispatch
 centers along the route to ensure prompt contact regardless of a caller's location, and
 must provide first responders with information on the release's origin, location,
 dispersion characteristics, suspected path, estimated volume, and recommended

 suspected release, with controllers trained to gather relevant details and to instruct
 callers on protective measures such as sheltering in place or evacuating to an upwind

 The EPRP directs the operator to develop a formal Emergency Response Plan under 49

 Key findings reported include the presence of six federally listed species within the
 corridor, including piping plover, whooping crane, pallid sturgeon, Preble's meadow
 jumping mouse, Ute ladies'-tresses, and western prairie fringed orchid, with no
 designated critical habitat affected but potential short-term, limited impacts to Greater
 Sage-Grouse lek core areas; all applicable criteria pollutants in the project area are in
 attainment with National Ambient Air Quality Standards, though new source review
 permits will be required for the pump stations and any associated combustion
 equipment as new emissions sources. The project is not expected to generate process
 or sanitary wastewater, with the only anticipated discharge arising from hydrostatic
 pipeline testing, requiring an NPDES permit and stormwater BMPs consistent with the
 Regulatory Plan's WYPDES discussion. Hazardous materials identified for use include
 petroleum-based hydraulic oils, fusion-bonded epoxy coating, and diesel and gasoline
 fuels, managed through secondary containment and spill prevention measures, with
 solid and hazardous waste volumes and disposal handled through the waste
 management and hazardous waste minimization plans described above. Segments of
 the Oregon Trail along the route are listed on the NRHP, and additional potentially
 eligible historical roads, trails, railroads, and waterways along the route will require
 survey to verify eligibility, with State Historic Preservation Office coordination planned
 but not yet initiated.
