Enhancing Lipid Nanoparticle-Mediated Circular RNA and mRNA Expression in the Placenta through Inhibition of IFNAR-JAK-STAT Signaling
Authors: Hannah C. Safford, Hannah C. Geisler, Ajay S. Thatte, Kuo-Chieh Liao, Alexandre Poirier, Marshall Padilla, Hannah M. Yamagata, Anushka Agrawal, Amanda M. Murray, Alex G. Hamilton, Kelsey L. Swingle, Emily Fitzgerald, Ori Chalom, Yue Wan, Michael J. Mitchell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The placenta has emerged as a promising target for RNA lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-based therapies to treat obstetric complications, yet efficient extrahepatic RNA transfection remains a challenge. Here, we identify innate immune signaling as a regulator of placental RNA translation and demonstrate that inhibition of IFN-α/β receptor (IFNAR) and JAK-STAT signaling enhances LNP-mediated transgene expression in the placenta for both messenger RNA (mRNA) and circular RNA (circRNA). While a placenta-tropic LNP enabled robust and durable circRNA expression in trophoblasts in vitro, circRNA translation was substantially decreased in vivo compared to mRNA in pregnant mice. Inhibition of IFNAR-JAK-STAT signaling enhanced circRNA translation up to 12-fold in maternal organs and increased circRNA and mRNA translation in the placenta up to 17.5- and 4-fold, respectively. JAK-STAT inhibition also enhanced translation of therapeutically relevant VEGF-encoding circRNA and mRNA in pregnant mice, suggesting innate immune modulation as a broadly applicable strategy to improve RNA therapeutics during pregnancy.

Following the success of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccines, ionizable lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have emerged as the leading nonviral platform for nucleic acid delivery. To date, LNPs have been largely developed for messenger RNA (mRNA) delivery in applications including vaccines, cancer immunotherapy, and gene editing. , However, a key limitation of mRNA LNPs is their inherently transient nature. Their relatively short half-life and limited storage stability constrain their durability and may necessitate repeated dosing to achieve a therapeutic effect. −

Reducing dosing frequency is important across many therapeutic applications but is particularly critical during pregnancy, where placental disorders, such as preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction, can persist for months throughout gestation. Placental disorders are often characterized by dysregulated expression of specific pathologic proteins, making them well-suited for RNA LNP therapeutics. However, LNPs primarily accumulate in the liver following systemic administration, , making extrahepatic delivery to the placenta a significant challenge. To overcome this barrier, several groups, including our own, have developed placenta-tropic LNP formulations for mRNA delivery to the placenta during pregnancy. − In particular, we have previously demonstrated that LNP-mediated delivery of mRNA encoding vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) ameliorates disease symptoms in a mouse model of preeclampsia. Despite these promising advances, the limited stability and duration of expression of linear mRNA motivate the exploration of more stable RNA modalities.

Circular RNA (circRNA) is an emerging nucleic acid cargo characterized by its single-stranded, covalently closed structure, which lacks free 5′ and 3′ ends. This unique architecture confers enhanced stability and resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation, enabling more durable transgene expression compared to linear mRNA. − Unlike linear mRNA, circRNAs initiate translation through a cap-independent mechanism mediated by an internal ribosome entry site (IRES), which recruits ribosomes to initiate translation and drive protein synthesis. ,

One additional advantage of circRNA is that purified circRNA typically exhibits inherently low immunogenicity even with canonical nucleosides, although immune activation can still occur for in vitro transcribed circRNA. − Specifically, exogenous RNAs can trigger endosomal and cytosolic pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I), thereby activating downstream innate immune signaling cascades and driving the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. ,,, These pathways can, in turn, elicit interferon (IFN)-mediated antiviral immune responses that signal through the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway, ultimately suppressing RNA translation. −

Overall, the potential for sustained protein production from circRNA makes it an attractive platform for placental applications, where a long-lasting therapeutic effect following a single LNP administration may enable dose-sparing effects and reduce risks of inflammation resulting from repeated RNA LNP dosing. , Despite these advantages, the delivery of circRNA to the placenta and its efficacy during pregnancy have remained largely unexplored.

In this work, we screen a library of established placenta-tropic LNPs to identify a lead candidate for circRNA delivery to placental trophoblast cells. In vitro, our lead formulation, LNP 55 encapsulating circRNA, exhibited durable reporter gene expression for up to 6 days and increased cumulative protein production 2.4-fold relative to its mRNA LNP control. In vivo, LNP 55 and the FDA-approved SM-102 LNP formulation, both encapsulating circRNA, drove sustained transgene expression in pregnant mice; however, peak protein expression from circRNA LNPs was markedly reduced compared to mRNA LNPs in the maternal organs and placentas. To investigate the mechanisms underlying this disparity, we evaluated the role of innate immune signaling in regulating RNA translation during pregnancy. Inhibition of the interferon (IFN)-α/β receptor (IFNAR) and JAK-STAT signaling rescued circRNA translation up to 12-fold in the maternal organs and up to 17.5-fold in the placentas, restoring expression to levels comparable to mRNA LNP-treated mice. Notably, IFNAR-JAK-STAT signaling inhibition did not enhance mRNA translation in maternal organs but increased placental mRNA translation by 2.5–4-fold, suggesting that RNA translation may be broadly suppressed in the placental microenvironment during pregnancy. Finally, JAK-STAT inhibition also enhanced serum VEGF levels in mice treated with VEGF-encoding circRNA and mRNA LNPs, demonstrating that JAK-STAT inhibition can improve the translation of therapeutically relevant RNA cargos. Together, these findings identify immune signaling pathways as a key barrier to circRNA translation in pregnancy and highlight immunomodulation as a strategy to enhance circRNA and mRNA LNP therapeutic efficacy in the placenta.

LNP 55 Effectively Encapsulates and Delivers circRNA to Trophoblasts In Vitro

Prior studies have demonstrated successful encapsulation of circRNA within existing mRNA LNP formulations. − Building on this, we evaluated whether placenta-tropic LNPs developed for mRNA delivery could be leveraged for circRNA delivery. We selected four published placenta-tropic LNP formulations developed by our groupA4, C5, 55, and Sito − alongside two previously reported placenta-tropic LNPs developed by other groups306Oi10 and A10to evaluate in this study. , Additionally, we reformulated three placenta-tropic ionizable lipids (C12-494, C14-494, and 306Oi10) into a design of experiments (DOE) excipient-optimized formulation previously shown to enhance circRNA delivery to immune cells. These excipient-optimized LNPs were formulated with increased molar ratios of ionizable lipid and cholesterol, an increased lipid:RNA weight ratio of 20:1, and a full substitution of the phospholipid with SOPC. Together, the nine formulations were benchmarked against four established clinical or industry-standard LNPs: C12-200, SM-102, MC3, or ALC-0315. All LNPs were formulated via microfluidic mixing to encapsulate circRNA encoding NanoLuc, an engineered luciferase protein. All LNPs exhibited comparable physicochemical properties, including diameters between 70 and 104 nm, low polydispersity (PDI < 0.35), and circRNA encapsulation efficiencies exceeding 80% (Table S1).

We then transfected BeWo b30 cells, an immortalized human placental trophoblast cell line, with our circRNA LNP library and quantified luminescence 24 h post-LNP treatment. Across the library, placenta-tropic LNPs demonstrated enhanced NanoLuc expression compared to clinical LNP controls, namely, MC3 and ALC-0315; however, only two formulations, LNP 55 and C12-494 DOE LNPs, were able to significantly improve circRNA delivery and translation compared to all LNP controls (Figure A). Dose–response studies confirmed dose-dependent behavior across all LNP formulations, with LNP 55 and SM-102 LNPs emerging as the most potent formulations, consistently outperforming C12-494 DOE and C12-200 LNPs across multiple doses (Figure B).

circRNA LNPs Drive Durable Transgene Expression In Vitro but Exhibit Attenuated Translation In Vivo

Given the enhanced stability of circRNA relative to linear mRNA, we first sought to evaluate and compare the expression kinetics of circRNA and mRNA LNPs in trophoblasts in vitro. BeWo b30 cells were treated with LNP 55, C12-494 DOE, SM-102, or C12-200 LNPs encapsulating either NanoLuc circRNA or mRNA, and NanoLuc expression was measured over 10 days. Across all formulations, circRNA LNPs demonstrated greater and more sustained NanoLuc expression than mRNA controls (Figure C–F). Area-under-the-curve (AUC) analysis showed at least a 2-fold increase in cumulative NanoLuc expression for all circRNA formulations compared to mRNA controls (Figure G). We also normalized luminescence for each group to their respective day 1 signals, confirming that circRNA LNPs maintained stable expression over 6 days, whereas mRNA LNPs exhibited a ∼50% decrease in signal by day 5 (Figure S1). Based on its strong in vitro performance, we selected LNP 55 as the lead candidate for circRNA delivery to trophoblasts, with SM-102 as a clinically established control.

We next evaluated the in vivo expression kinetics of circRNA LNPs in pregnant mice. Here, LNP 55 and SM-102 LNPs encapsulating either NanoLuc circRNA or mRNA were administered intravenously to pregnant mice on gestational day E11, and whole-body bioluminescence imaging was performed over 7 days (Figure ). In contrast to the in vitro findings, mRNA LNPs produced higher peak bioluminescence signal compared to the circRNA-treated mice (Figure A,D–G). Additionally, we observed more potent NanoLuc expression for the SM-102 LNP-treated mice compared to those treated with LNP 55.

Despite reduced peak expression, circRNA LNPs exhibited modestly improved expression kinetics in vivo. By day 3, 52% and 61% of peak bioluminescence signal remained for mice treated with circRNA LNP 55 and circRNA SM-102 LNPs, respectively, compared to more rapid signal decay for their mRNA counterparts, which retained 46% and 34% of peak signal by day 3 (Figure C). However, this increased persistence was insufficient to offset the substantially lower initial protein production, as evident by AUC analysis (Figure B). Together, these results demonstrate that while circRNA enables durable protein expression in vitro and modestly prolonged expression in vivo, it is limited by reduced translation efficiency in pregnant mice, motivating us to investigate the mechanisms underlying these differences and identify strategies capable of enhancing circRNA translation during pregnancy.

circRNA LNPs Demonstrate Order-of-Magnitude Reduced RNA Expression Compared to mRNA LNPs in Maternal Liver, Spleen, and Placenta

We next evaluated the organ-specific LNP biodistribution and RNA translation in pregnant mice. Here, we formulated LNP 55 and SM-102 LNPs with either NanoLuc circRNA or mRNA and labeled LNPs with the fluorescent DiD dye. LNPs were administered intravenously on gestational day E11, and maternal organs, placentas, and fetuses were collected 24 h later for fluorescent and luminescent imaging.

Strong LNP accumulation was observed in the maternal livers and spleens across all formulations, with modest accumulation in the lungs (Figures A,C,D, S2, S3). In the liver, circRNA LNPs showed significantly greater accumulation compared to their mRNA counterparts, while splenic accumulation was comparable across all four LNP formulations. In the placenta, a modest increase in LNP accumulation was observed for circRNA LNP 55-treated mice, and no significant differences in DiD fluorescence were detected in the fetuses across any LNP treatment group (Figures B,E,F and S4). Overall, we observed comparable LNP accumulation across all formulations, suggesting that cargo identity (circRNA versus mRNA) does not substantially alter LNP biodistribution in vivo.

Despite comparable tissue accumulation, striking differences in ex vivo organ luminescence were observed between circRNA- and mRNA-treated mice. In the liver, mice treated with mRNA LNP 55 or mRNA SM-102 LNPs displayed a significantly higher bioluminescence signal than those receiving the corresponding circRNA LNPs, despite greater hepatic LNP accumulation for circRNA LNP groups (Figures G,I and S5). A similar trend was observed in the maternal spleen, where mRNA LNP-treated mice again exhibited greater bioluminescence signal relative to circRNA LNP-treated mice, although the magnitude of this difference was less pronounced (Figures G,J and S5). A low bioluminescence signal was also detected in the maternal heart, lung, and kidneys for mRNA LNP-treated mice (Figure S6).

This pattern of reduced circRNA translation was observed again in the placenta. Mice treated with mRNA LNPs displayed markedly higher placental bioluminescence signal compared to those receiving circRNA LNPs (Figures H,K and S7). Specifically, mRNA LNP 55 mediated 23-fold higher placental NanoLuc expression relative to circRNA LNP 55, while mRNA SM-102 LNPs showed a 60-fold higher bioluminescence signal in the placenta compared to circRNA SM-102 LNPs. Notably, SM-102 has not, to our knowledge, been previously evaluated for mRNA delivery to the placenta, yet mRNA SM-102 LNPs mediated significantly higher placental bioluminescence than the placenta-tropic LNP 55 formulation. Finally, no bioluminescence signal was detected in fetuses across any LNP formulation, as these LNPs are not expected to cross the placental barrier and transfect fetal cells (Figure L).

Inhibition of IFNAR-JAK-STAT Signaling Enhances circRNA Translation in Maternal Organs of Pregnant Mice

Having established that circRNA and mRNA LNPs accumulated comparably across tissues, we hypothesized that the reduced bioluminescence observed in circRNA LNP-treated mice reflected differences in RNA translation rather than LNP biodistribution. While exogenous circRNA is generally considered to exhibit reduced recognition by the innate immune system compared to linear mRNA, , exogenous RNA can activate innate immune signaling cascades and elicit IFN-mediated antiviral immune responses that signal through the JAK-STAT pathway, which may potentially lead to the suppressed circRNA translation observed here. ,,−

Several studies have explored the use of immunomodulatory small molecules and antibodies to attenuate innate immune responses to other immunogenic nucleic acid therapeutics, including self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) and DNA. In particular, FDA-approved JAK inhibitors, including baricitinib, ruxolitinib, and peficitinib, have been shown to reduce inflammation and enhance transgene expression for saRNA and DNA LNPs. − Additionally, pretreatment with an anti-IFN-α/β receptor (IFNAR) blocking antibody has been shown to improve tolerability and enhance transgene expression following DNA LNP administration in mice. We therefore sought to apply these strategies to our circRNA LNP platform to determine whether pretreatment with a JAK inhibitor or anti-IFNAR antibody could rescue circRNA transgene expression in vivo.

LNP 55 and SM-102 LNPs were again formulated to encapsulate either NanoLuc circRNA or mRNA. One hour prior to LNP administration, gestational day E11 pregnant mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 200 μg of baricitinib, an FDA-approved JAK1/2 inhibitor, or an anti-mouse IFNAR blocking antibody. LNPs were then administered intravenously, and maternal organs, placentas, and fetuses were collected for bioluminescence imaging 24 h post-LNP administration.

For mice treated with both circRNA LNP 55 and circRNA SM-102 LNPs, baricitinib and anti-IFNAR pretreatment noticeably improved bioluminescence signal in the maternal livers and spleens (Figures A,B and S8). Mice pretreated with the anti-IFNAR antibody prior to circRNA LNP 55 administration exhibited significantly enhanced hepatic and splenic NanoLuc expression compared to mice receiving circRNA LNP 55 alone, corresponding to approximately 12- and 7-fold increases in bioluminescence signal in the liver and spleen, respectively (Figure C,D,M). Although baricitinib pretreatment did not yield statistically significant improvements in transfection for circRNA LNP 55-treated mice, a 4- and 5-fold improvement in hepatic and splenic bioluminescence signal was observed (Figure C,D,M). For circRNA SM-102 LNP-treated mice, baricitinib pretreatment increased bioluminescence signal in the liver and spleen 2- and 8-fold, respectively, while anti-IFNAR pretreatment increased bioluminescence signal approximately 5-fold in both organs (Figure E,F,O). Pretreatment with either inhibitor broadly increased NanoLuc expression in the heart, lungs, and kidneys for mice treated with either circRNA LNP formulation, suggesting that JAK-STAT and IFNAR pathway inhibition may enhance circRNA translation broadly across multiple organ systems (Figure S9).

We next examined whether baricitinib or anti-IFNAR pretreatment would similarly affect mRNA translation. Given the extensive work demonstrating that chemical modifications to mRNA, such as N1-methylpseudouridine substitution, substantially reduce innate immune sensing of exogenous mRNA, , we hypothesized that mRNA translation would be largely unaffected by these inhibitors. As expected, no significant differences in bioluminescence signal were observed in the maternal livers or spleens of mice treated with mRNA LNP 55 or mRNA SM-102 LNPs with or without baricitinib or anti-IFNAR pretreatment (Figures G–L and S10). Modest increases in hepatic and splenic bioluminescence signal were observed for mice receiving mRNA LNP 55 or mRNA SM-102 LNPs with baricitinib pretreatment, 1.8- and 1.6-fold, respectively, but these differences were not statistically significant (Figure N,P). Finally, small increases in bioluminescence signal were also observed in the heart and lungs of mRNA LNP-treated mice receiving baricitinib pretreatment, suggesting JAK inhibition may enhance mRNA translation beyond the liver and spleen (Figure S9).

We next assessed whether differences in translation observed between circRNA and mRNA LNPs were specific to pregnancy or would be recapitulated in healthy, nonpregnant mice (Figure S11). In nonpregnant mice, NanoLuc mRNA LNP 55 mediated approximately an order of magnitude higher hepatic bioluminescence signal compared to NanoLuc circRNA LNP 55, consistent with findings in pregnant mice. Baricitinib pretreatment significantly increased circRNA translation in the liver and spleen, whereas anti-IFNAR pretreatment did not significantly enhance circRNA expression in either organ. In contrast, no differences in bioluminescence signal were observed between circRNA and mRNA SM-102 LNP-treated nonpregnant mice regardless of inhibitor pretreatment. These formulation-dependent differences in translation may reflect variability in the intrinsic immunogenicity of the individual LNP components. Different ionizable lipid excipients have been shown to stimulate TLRs and exacerbate inflammatory responses through endosomal disruption, , suggesting that excipient composition may also contribute to variability in the immune-mediated suppression of circRNA translation observed here. Furthermore, pregnancy is known to substantially remodel the maternal immune landscape, − which may also amplify differences in circRNA versus mRNA translation efficiency in vivo.

Inhibition of IFNAR-JAK-STAT Signaling Enhances Both circRNA and mRNA Translation in the Placenta of Pregnant Mice

Having characterized the effect of inhibitor pretreatment on maternal organ RNA translation, we next examined NanoLuc circRNA and mRNA translation in the placentas and fetuses of pregnant mice following baricitinib or anti-IFNAR pretreatment. Consistent with the trends observed in the maternal livers and spleens, pretreatment with either inhibitor enhanced placental circRNA translation in mice treated with both LNP 55 and SM-102 LNPs (Figures A and S12). Baricitinib pretreatment yielded a significant 8-fold increase in placental bioluminescence in circRNA LNP 55-treated mice compared to those receiving circRNA LNP 55 alone, while anti-IFNAR pretreatment produced an approximately 4-fold increase (Figure C,G). For circRNA SM-102 LNP-treated mice, baricitinib pretreatment mediated a significant 17.5-fold improvement in placental bioluminescence compared to circRNA SM-102 LNPs alone, while anti-IFNAR pretreatment yielded an approximately 4-fold improvement in placental bioluminescence (Figure D,G).

In contrast to what was observed in the maternal organs, baricitinib and anti-IFNAR pretreatment also enhanced mRNA translation in the placentas of mRNA LNP-treated mice (Figures B and S13). For mRNA LNP 55-treated mice, both baricitinib and anti-IFNAR pretreatments increased placental bioluminescence by approximately 4-fold (Figure E,G). For mRNA SM-102 LNP-treated mice, baricitinib pretreatment improved placental bioluminescence by 2.5-fold, while anti-IFNAR pretreatment yielded a significant 4-fold increase in placental bioluminescence (Figure F,G). No bioluminescence signal was detected in the fetuses across any LNP treatment group with or without inhibitor pretreatment, indicating that neither baricitinib nor anti-IFNAR administration promoted fetal transfection (Figure S14).

Notably, pretreatment with either inhibitor enhanced placental bioluminescence in both circRNA and mRNA LNP-treated mice, suggesting that innate immune-mediated suppression of RNA translation may be particularly pronounced in the placental microenvironment regardless of RNA cargo. The placenta harbors a unique immune environment that balances tolerance of the semiallogeneic fetus with defense against pathogens. − For example, trophoblasts are known to express TLR-3, -7, -8, and -9 and secrete type I interferons, such as IFN-β, throughout pregnancy and particularly in response to viral infections. ,, It is plausible that the unique immunological environment of the placenta renders trophoblasts and other placental cells more sensitive to innate immune activation following RNA LNP delivery, which may contribute to suppression of transgene expression in the placenta for both circRNA and mRNA LNPs relative to what was observed in the maternal organs.

The placental immune environment also evolves dynamically throughout gestation, transitioning from pro-inflammatory states in early and late pregnancy to a tolerogenic state midgestation. ,, As these experiments were performed on gestational day E11 in mice, corresponding roughly to the end of the first trimester in humans, we next examined whether inhibition of the IFNAR-JAK-STAT pathway would similarly enhance placental RNA translation at a later gestational time point.

To this end, we examined LNP 55-mediated NanoLuc circRNA translation in gestational day E15 pregnant mice following baricitinib pretreatment. While baricitinib pretreatment did not improve circRNA translation in the maternal organs at E15, placental circRNA translation was enhanced 10-fold, consistent with findings at E11 (Figure S15). Future studies should also examine the effect of IFNAR-JAK-STAT inhibition on mRNA translation at later gestational time points to further establish the generalizability of this immunomodulatory approach. Together, these findings demonstrate that modulation of the IFNAR-JAK-STAT pathway may broadly enhance LNP-mediated circRNA translation in the placenta across multiple gestational days during pregnancy.

Inhibition of JAK-STAT Signaling Enhances VEGF-Encoding circRNA and mRNA Translation in Pregnant Mice

Finally, we evaluated whether this immunomodulatory strategy enhances translation of a therapeutically relevant RNA cargo, VEGF, which has been previously explored for the treatment of preeclampsia. Here, LNP 55 was formulated to encapsulate either VEGF circRNA or mRNA and intravenously administered to gestational day E11 pregnant mice following either no inhibitor pretreatment or pretreatment with baricitinib or anti-IFNAR. For both RNA cargos, serum VEGF levels were significantly increased with baricitinib pretreatment compared to mice that received no inhibitor pretreatment or anti-IFNAR pretreatment (Figure A,C). Notably, anti-IFNAR pretreatment had little to no effect on serum VEGF levels.

We next characterized circulating cytokine levels following inhibitor pretreatment to assess downstream effects on interferon signaling and the maternal immune response to the inhibitors and RNA LNPs. Specifically, we measured serum levels of IFN-β, IFN-γ, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, and IL-10 (Figure B,D). Broadly, VEGF mRNA LNP-treated mice had lower cytokine levels than VEGF circRNA LNP-treated mice, suggesting that mRNA formulations may be less immunostimulatory.

circRNA LNPs without inhibitor pretreatment induced higher serum IFN-β levels compared to mRNA LNP-treated mice. Baricitinib pretreatment reduced IFN-β levels for both circRNA and mRNA LNPs, whereas anti-IFNAR pretreatment had no effect on IFN-β levels (Figure E). This difference may reflect the fact that JAK-STAT inhibition disrupts the JAK-mediated transcriptional feedback loop that drives IFN-β production, in contrast to anti-IFNAR blockade, which only prevents signaling through the interferon receptor. Notably, reduced IFN-β levels with baricitinib pretreatment corresponded with greater serum VEGF levels for both RNA cargos, suggesting that reducing type I interferon secretion may enhance RNA translation in pregnant mice.

Inhibitor pretreatment had no effect on IFN-γ levels, though circRNA LNP-treated mice had overall higher IFN-γ levels compared to mRNA LNP-treated mice (Figure F). Similarly, TNF-α and IL-6 levels were generally higher in circRNA LNP-treated mice relative to mRNA LNP-treated mice (Figure G,H). Of note, the only cytokines significantly elevated compared to PBS-treated mice were IFN-β in circRNA LNP-treated mice without inhibitor or anti-IFNAR pretreatment and TNF-α in circRNA LNP-treated mice with anti-IFNAR pretreatment, suggesting that the overall inflammatory responses to both the inhibitors and the RNA LNPs remain relatively modest (Figure E,G). Finally, IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine secreted in response to type I interferons, , was elevated in both RNA LNP groups without inhibitor pretreatment and was modestly reduced following inhibitor pretreatment (Figure I). Anti-IFNAR pretreatment produced the lowest IL-10 levels, with multiple samples below the limit of detection. Together, these findings highlight the greater immunostimulatory nature of circRNA LNPs relative to mRNA LNPs and suggest that JAK-STAT inhibition modulates IFN-β secretion in association with enhanced RNA translation.

Together, this work suggests that innate immune signaling acts as a key barrier to efficient RNA translation in both maternal and placental tissues during pregnancy. While circRNA LNPs hold promise as a therapeutic RNA strategy, our findings establish that circRNA translation is substantially attenuated during pregnancy across both maternal organs and the placenta, likely driven by innate immune responses to the circRNA cargo. Notably, inhibition of IFNAR-JAK-STAT signaling, using either the clinically approved JAK inhibitor baricitinib or an anti-IFNAR blocking antibody, rescued circRNA translation in maternal organs across different LNP formulations. However, the most consistent and marked improvements were observed in the placenta, where both circRNA and mRNA translation were enhanced with inhibitor pretreatment. Importantly, baricitinib pretreatment also enhanced serum VEGF levels following delivery of VEGF circRNA and mRNA LNPs, underscoring the potential of immunomodulation as a broadly applicable strategy to improve RNA therapeutic efficacy in the placenta during pregnancy.

Several important directions remain for both mechanistic understanding and improvement of circRNA and mRNA translation during pregnancy. First, the specific cell types mediating type I interferon secretion and innate immune suppression of RNA translation in both the placenta and maternal organs should be investigated using approaches such as flow cytometry to delineate the cellular sources of IFN-β in these tissues. Second, while our findings demonstrate that inhibition of IFNAR-JAK-STAT signaling enhances circRNA and mRNA translation, the specific downstream antiviral mechanisms responsible for this effect have yet to be elucidated, as type I interferon signaling can suppress exogenous RNA expression through both translational inhibition and RNA degradation pathways. − Third, a thorough safety evaluation of IFNAR-JAK-STAT inhibitor pretreatment and RNA LNP administration during pregnancy will be critical for clinical translation, including assessment of placental structural integrity, fetal survival and development, and the potential long-term effects of these inhibitors on maternal immune health. Finally, additional strategies to mitigate innate immune sensing of circRNA should be explored, including nucleoside modifications or alternative IRES elements, which have been shown to reduce immunogenicity and enhance tissue-specific regulation of circRNA translation, respectively, ,− as well as optimization of inhibitor dosing regimens. Taken together, this work establishes a framework for understanding and overcoming barriers to RNA translation in pregnancy and motivates future studies evaluating circRNA LNPs for the treatment of obstetric conditions such as preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction.
