Executive Summary

 While internal combustion engines fueled by natural gas are the industry standard
for combined heat and power (CHP) genset systems, several customers are exploring
utilization of alternative energy sources, such as renewable hydrogen, in the interest of
reducing the carbon footprint of their operations and developing the ability to store excess
renewable energy as hydrogen and converting it back to power and thermal energy when
needed. Through a nationally competitive process, Caterpillar team won an award from the
US Dept. of Energy (DOE) in Jan 2021 to design, develop, and demonstrate a cost-effective
and scalable, unique-to-the-industry 2.0 MWe fuel-flexible G3516 CHP genset that can run
on 100% hydrogen, 100% natural gas, and up to 25% blends of hydrogen in natural gas
(volume basis). We started working on this CHPH2 program in March 2021 after receiving
pre-approval from the DOE to do so. This was accomplished based on over two years of
extensive R&D work done on a single-cylinder engine for developing innovative hydrogen
fueling, combustion, air, and electronic control systems design and strategies. Findings
from the single-cylinder engine work were scaled up and applied for modifications of and
lab testing and development of a 2.0 MWe 16-cylinder G3516 fuel-flexible engine-genset
and electronic controls systems. During this testing and development in the engine lab, the
genset was run on 100% hydrogen, 100% natural gas, and up to 25% blends of hydrogen in
natural gas (volume basis) and its steady-state, transient, and emission were documented.

 After the lab testing work, the fully developed flex-fuel genset was containerized,
along with a CHP skid to recover waste heat from the engine system, in a standardized
factory-assembled solution to minimize complexity and infrastructure requirements and
was integrated with various hydrogen safety features, advanced engine controls, and
Caterpillar Energy Control System (ECS). The containerized genset was installed at and
integrated with renewably fueled District Energy St Paul (DESP) plant in downtown St Paul,
Minnesota. The DESP is a leader in community energy, providing the Saint Paul area with
sustainable district heating and cooling services for 41 years. Besides various site
development permits, the hydrogen system permits, and site safety permits were obtained
by the project team from the local authorities having jurisdiction. Detailed hydrogen safety
analysis and quantitative risk analysis were also performed to enable safe operation and
demonstration of the CHP system at the DESP site.

 To fully assess every aspect of the hydrogen system under real-world operating
conditions, electric power and thermal waste heat recovered from the integrated flex-fuel
CHP genset system were fed into DESP’s active distribution system. The CHP genset was
run using 100% natural gas fuel and 100% hydrogen fuel for 200+ hours with each fuel type

                                                                              Page 13/122

during the demonstration at DESP and extensive amount of performance and emission
data was collected. Although CHP genset performance on hydrogen during startup, steady-
state and transient operations were very close to the performance on natural gas, the
hydrogen engine exhaust contained only water vapor, no carbon dioxide, and 90% less NOx
emission than a natural gas engine exhaust. A detailed technoeconomic analysis showed
that diesel and natural gas genset scenarios offer the most cost-effective solutions for
power generation, which makes sense considering the technology readiness level, low fuel
costs, and low capital intensity of these scenarios. All 100% hydrogen fueled scenarios are
costly compared to the natural gas and diesel options largely due to the high costs of
delivered liquid or gaseous hydrogen, hydrogen production, and relative technology
readiness levels. The greenhouse gas (GHG) analysis showed that replacing diesel gensets
with renewably generated hydrogen powered genset has the potential to reduce the GHG
emissions and provide fuel flexibility to a site. The diesel scenario produces just under
1000 g/kWh of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e), while the best hydrogen scenario, “onsite
gaseous hydrogen generation from PEM water electrolysis using onsite wind energy and H2
genset” produces just over 100 g/kWh CO2e. Biodiesel and renewable natural gas are
interesting options that could significantly lower emissions while using infrastructure
already in place today for distribution and storage.

 Several interested officials from the local government bodies and industries, and
Caterpillar dealers and customers witnessed this demonstration at DESP. Supported and
partially funded by the U.S. DOE Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy (EERE),
the project was led by Caterpillar in collaboration with DESP and the National Laboratory of
the Rockies (formerly National Renewable Energy Laboratory or NREL). This project is a key
step forward in our journey toward providing sustainable integrated products for our
customers who are considering reducing their carbon footprint, and to well position
Caterpillar to serve potential global markets as the timing and dynamics of renewable
hydrogen production, distribution and storage gain speed.

 1. Introduction

 Internal combustion engine customers worldwide are becoming increasingly
focused on decarbonization and sustainable solutions. Investors are also evaluating
companies on their Environment, Social and Corporate Governance (ESG) progress.
Renewable Hydrogen is one of several fuels gas engine customers are considering helping
reduce their carbon footprint. Hydrogen production from renewable sources solves
supply/demand mismatch and yields decarbonized energy. Caterpillar is also experiencing
pull across all industries for solutions to improve customers’ sustainability goals. However,
many factors influence if and when hydrogen will achieve critical mass and help generate
sales of hydrogen based internal combustion engines: infrastructure, cost, regulations,
safety, storage, packaging, governmental policy and incentives, etc. Caterpillar continues
to invest in hydrogen technology and is well positioned to serve customers as the timing
and dynamics of renewable hydrogen production, distribution and storage gain speed. We
expect future improvements will be made in hydrogen infrastructure, storage, and
transportation to lower project costs such as for distributed power generation. Work done
in this project helped Caterpillar –

 1. Expedite development of hydrogen capable flex-fuel gensets that can provide
 100% hydrogen capability with fallback capability to partial or full natural gas
 fueling in times when hydrogen supply is not adequate or not available
 2. Develop and test cost competitive electronics and controls for easy “plug-n-
 play” flexible CHP system integration with a variety of Distributed Energy power
 systems
 3. Gain and document understanding of how a hydrogen power profile would
 compare to that of traditional fuels (e.g., diesel, natural gas) and futuristic power
 systems (e.g., fuel cell, battery) in terms of power density, efficiency, transient
 response, GHG emissions profile, and TCO

 The project team used advanced simulation, analysis, development, and testing
capabilities at Caterpillar to develop a new state of the art fuel flexible CHP system for
integration with an existing district energy system. Extensive 1D and 3D simulation and
single-cylinder engine testing and development work were carried out to develop the
combustion recipe for achieving flex-fuel capability. A baseline Caterpillar spark ignited
natural gas serial production engine generator or genset system was optimally modified to
enable flex-fuel operation on natural gas, hydrogen, or mixtures thereof while maximizing
performance and response and minimizing emissions. This system was containerized with

                                                                                Page 16/122

a CHP module and then integrated with and demonstrated at a renewably fuel district
energy site in St Paul, MN.

 Caterpillar is an industry leader in continuous, prime, and standby electric power
generation systems, including CHP applications. Caterpillar has also been offering hybrid
energy solutions, including distributed energy storage and electric power conversion and
controls, since 2014. These systems are currently installed in multiple locations around the
world, providing Caterpillar customers with cost effective and reliable power while helping
to lower the carbon footprint of their operations. This project for development of a flex-fuel
CHP genset system leveraged Caterpillar’s technical experts in combustion, engine design,
hydrogen chemistry power conversion and systems integration to demonstrate a robust,
sustainable, efficient alternative energy profile for the stationary distributed power
systems. With a successful demonstration and a sound commercialization plan for this
CHP market, Caterpillar can leverage this solution into other distributed energy markets it
serves that have similar requirements. Caterpillar will be able to serve these markets and
customers through its global product development and manufacturing operations very
quickly. Caterpillar’s main distribution channel is through its Dealer Network. This
extensive network, employing 160,000 people, is unrivalled in its global reach (~ 200
countries) and is a key competitive advantage for the company. In addition to the dealer
network, Cat Financial offers financing that is complemented by a deep understanding of
this business and industry.

 2. Background

 This project sought to design, develop, and demonstrate a cost-effective and
scalable natural gas/hydrogen powered flexible CHP genset and advanced engine, genset
and microgrid controls. The goals included development of a power system that will meet
baseload requirements, automatically and seamlessly respond to variations in electric
power generation by renewable generation resources within a district energy system and be
able to serve as a rapidly dispatchable generation asset for grid support during peak
demand times. As a result, in this program, a containerized 2.0 MWe fuel-flexible CHP
genset system was developed and demonstrated at a renewably fueled municipal district
energy facility (i.e., at DESP). The key project objectives of this development and
demonstration project are as follows –

                                                                               Page 17/122

 1. Development of a 2.0 MWe fuel-flexible spark-ignited internal combustion gas
 engine capable of operating on 100% natural gas, 100% hydrogen, and mixture
 of these fuels.

 3. Results and Discussion

 The project team used advanced simulation, analysis, development, and testing
capabilities at Caterpillar to develop a new state of the art fuel flexible CHP system for
integration with an existing district energy system. Extensive 1D and 3D simulation and
single-cylinder engine testing and development work was carried out to develop the
combustion recipe for achieving fuel flexible capability. A baseline Caterpillar spark ignited
natural gas serial production engine generator or genset system was optimally modified to
enable fuel flexible operation on natural gas, hydrogen, or mixtures thereof while
maximizing performance and transient response and minimizing engine tailpipe emissions.
This genset system was containerized along with a CHP module and then integrated with
and demonstrated at a renewably fuel district energy site in St Paul, MN. Following sections
provide details of various steps involved in the fuel flexible CHP genset system
development (the R&D work) and demonstration at DESP.

  3.3 Simulation Work (1D and 3D)
 Preliminary 1D simulations were carried out to understand the impacts of utilizing
pipeline natural gas and pure hydrogen, with differing compression ratios and Miller
amounts, on the brake thermal efficiency (BTE) and brake mean effective pressure (BMEP).
The maximum achievable BMEP from pure hydrogen was limited by the peak cylinder
pressure limit and the air system capability, whereas the natural gas baseline engine did
not reach its hardware limitations. More extensive 1D/3D simulations were conducted to
optimize compression ratio and turbulence intensity in the combustion chamber and
select an optimum piston and camshaft design. The design and development of both port
fuel injection system and the pre-mixed hydrogen compatible (up to 25% hydrogen in
natural gas, by vol.) fuel system were carried out using 1D and 3D simulation models, some
1-cyl engine test data, and based on detailed discussion with suppliers to build these
systems using components that are compatible with hydrogen. Using data from the single
cylinder tests, the simulation models were updated and improved. The new and improved
models were used for sizing and selecting components for the multi-cylinder G3516
prototype engine such as combustion system hardware, air-system, etc.

  3.4 Development of a Port Fuel Injection (PFI) System for Hydrogen Fuel
 Based on 1-cyl engine testing and 1D and 3D simulation models, a hydrogen port
fuel injection system was designed, fuel-train and gas-train components/parts procured,
and complete fuel system built and installed on the prototype G3516 flex-fuel engine.
These engine and fuel system designs are shown in Figure 8 (a) and (b), respectively - (a)
hydrogen compatible port fuel injection system colored green, and (b) hydrogen
compatible fumigated or pre-mixed fueling system colored green for use with 25%
hydrogen blends with natural gas. The properties (lower density and ignition energy) of
hydrogen fuel negatively impact the pumping work and is a backfire risk. Port fuel injection
(PFI) helps by injecting larger quantities of hydrogen quickly at higher pressures and
minimizes the amount of hydrogen in the intake system. Optimization of the fuel delivery
near the inlet valve opening and timing/duration of injection were found to be critical to
obtain good mixing with PFI. Also, the end of injection timing was found to be critical due to
fuel transport delay from injector to the intake valve. Performance of both fuel systems was
evaluated during G3516 testing and development work at SwRI.

  3.5 Development of Engine Controls for the Fuel Flexible Gas Engine
 Ignition timing is important for both performance (e.g. efficiency) and hardware life
(e.g. knocking). It is also a critical degree of freedom used to control combustion due to
changes in fuel quality/fuel type as characterized by methane number (MN). In this work
where fuel will shift from natural gas to hydrogen or mixtures thereof and thereby affecting
the MN, we leveraged an existing ignition timing control algorithm, coupled with in-cylinder
pressure sensing to detect and prevent knocking, and optimized it for this flex-fuel
application so that both maximized fuel efficiency and reduced impact of knocking could
be realized during operation on various fuel types. Cylinder-to-cylinder variation and
abnormal combustion control were managed. An initial version of controls for the flex-fuel
engine system, that included both a fumigated (pre-compressor fuel injection) as well as a
dedicated port fuel injection system, was developed to handle different fuel mixtures and
proportions in response to types of fuel availability as well as load demand and optimize
combustion. These systems make use of in-cylinder pressure sensing, power distribution,
cylinder balancers, pre-ignition identification, pre-ignition control, and air fuel ratio control.
The engine control system architecture team did a proper vetting of different software
features needed to meet the requirements for this program with controls and performance
experts.
 Co-simulation models in Software-In-The-Loop mode, and in Hardware in the Loop
(HIL) mode were used for various controls logic debug, development, and validation prior
to use of these engine controls on the G3516 prototype engine in the engine test cell. These
techniques also helped us to understand performance tuning options as well as to further
develop some value-added features that may allow customers to increase their up-time by

                                                                                  Page 26/122

avoiding engine shutdowns due to pre-ignition or other combustion-related phenomena.
Individual pre-ignition identification and mitigation strategies were shown to be effective on
the single cylinder test engine and are shown in Figure 9. These features were
implemented into multi-cylinder engine software. Control I/O was developed, based on
sensors, and ranges thereof, and wiring harness pinouts were properly defined.

  3.6 Multi-cylinder G3516 Prototype Engine Hardware Preparation and
 Procurement
 Several engine hardware changes to the baseline natural gas engine were necessary
to enable operation on 100% hydrogen, while maintaining the capability to also operate on
natural gas and natural gas / hydrogen blends. Based on the 1D/3D simulation, analysis,
and single-cylinder engine testing work we identified the combustion recipe and other
hardware for a flex-fuel engine. We worked with our supplier base to procure
hardware/components for this flex-fuel gas engine prototype for lab development work.
These components included hydrogen port fuel injection system and pre-mixed fueling
system (for natural gas and blends) and associated off-engine gas trains, turbos, pistons,
cylinder heads, camshafts, ECMs, wirings and sensors, etc. Additionally, respective off-
engine gas trains were also developed that consisted of a gas filter, dual gas shut off valves
with proof of closure, and a knock down regulator (Figure 10). The turbocharger/air system
is a key piece of the flexible natural gas/hydrogen genset system. Typically, the air system
on a natural gas industrial genset is highly optimized for a narrow operating range.
Compared to natural gas air mixtures, significantly higher compressor work is required for

                                                                               Page 27/122

hydrogen air mixtures due to their lower density, which could result in higher turbine work
and pumping loss. This required additional optimization of tradeoffs in both design and
operation as extremely lean or dilute conditions were needed for hydrogen combustion. To
mitigate this risk, the team identified the limits of the air systems with pure hydrogen
combustion and optimized the air system to be able to provide optimum performance
across the full range of fuel types. The new turbocharger enabled induction of low-density
hydrogen, while preserving the full power capability of the engine operating on natural gas/
hydrogen blend.

  3.8 Containerized Flex-fuel CHP Genset System Development
 The key components of a containerized Caterpillar standard natural gas spark-
ignited gas genset CHP solution are shown in Figure 13 for example. Separate fuel trains
exist for natural gas and hydrogen within the container, and each fuel uses a different
injection system at the engine. As such, there is no piping system or instruments common
to these two fuels. A CHP skid is used to extract heat from the engine jacket water and
exhaust stream using a plate-and-frame heat exchanger to produce warm or hot water up
to 120° C, depending on the engine and mechanical constraints. As part of the water loop
there is a radiator for cooling the jacket water prior to returning to the engine if CHP mode is
not used. A 3-way valve located in the return line from the heat exchanger is used to divert
water to the radiator. The incoming gas temperature from the exhaust stack of a natural gas
engine typically ranges between 400°C – 500°C, which is hot enough to convert water
coming into the heat exchanger into steam. A 2-way bypass valve located in the exhaust
stack in front of the heat exchanger is used to divert stack heat away from the heat
exchanger if, for example, some customer’s water temperature setpoint is reached, or the
heat exchanger needs to be taken out of service. The CHP skid also includes water pumps
for jacket water and after cooler water circuit. A paralleling switchgear, genset and CHP
controls, a control room, gas fuel train, engine and generator, and a robust ventilation
system are also part of this containerized CHP power plant.

 3.9.2 Hydrogen Genset – Fueling System Venting/Purging Procedures

 We added a purge line and a GSOV to the off-engine hydrogen fuel train (Figure 19).
This was added to vent the piping from the hydrogen supply line shutoff valve up to our
main supply shut off valves. This was done to vent the lines of pressurized hydrogen all the
way to just 0.5 kPa above the atmospheric pressure (i.e., only extremely small quantity of
H2 fuel left in the lines). The Hydrogen vent line goes through the enclosure wall and runs
up to a support post and is vented at a location 6 feet above the enclosure per NFPA2. The

                                                                                  Page 37/122

genset enclosure is designed according to applicable requirements of NFPA 2, although it
is not classified as a hydrogen equipment enclosure. The enclosure incorporates a variety
of safety features, including a robust ventilation system, gas/LEL detection system,
automatic depressurization and purge of the hydrogen fuel train, and other safety
measures as prescribed by NFPA 2. Downstream of the genset enclosure isolation valves,
gaseous hydrogen enters the enclosure and the genset fuel train at nominally 116 psig (8
barg). The fuel train consists of fuel filter, two pneumatically actuated isolation valves, and
pressure regulator. Downstream of the fuel train, the hydrogen is mixed with air on its way
to the fuel header. Additionally, a vent connection operated by a separate pneumatically
actuated isolation valve is present upstream of the fuel train’s double block. This vent valve
is used for automatic depressurization of the fuel line during engine trip/shutdown.
 Ambient air—containing 21% oxygen—enters in piping systems and/or equipment
following initial construction, maintenance activities (involving pipe/tubing/component
disassembly), and extended shutdowns. The goal of purging is to displace air (or hydrogen)
in fuel lines with an inert gas to prevent the formation of an explosive mixture upon
introduction of hydrogen. Nitrogen from a bank of high-pressure cylinders was used as
inert gas.

 3.10 The Crankcase Ventilation System
 The natural gas engine requires a negative pressure crankcase. The Hydrogen gas
engine crankcase requires a positive pressure crankcase. We devised a way to use a fan
blower to serve both functions. We added additional piping that can be swapped to either
configure for natural gas engine use or hydrogen engine use. The rerouting of the lines can
pull air from engine room and push (positive pressure or open crankcase ventilation OCV
system for H2) the air through the crankcase and vent outside. Or it can pull the fresh air
through the crankcase (negative pressure or closed crankcase ventilation CCV system for
NG) and vent outside (Figure 20). The crankcase vent line goes through the enclosure wall
and runs up to a support post and is vented at a location 6 feet above the enclosure per
NFPA2.

 3.16.1 Development of process risk management approach:

 Caterpillar formed an overall strategy to risk management for the project. This
strategy was composed of four main phases: Hazard Identification, Assessment,
Mitigation, and Verification. Hazard identification was accomplished through pre-PHA and
final/design phase PHA reviews. Additionally, Caterpillar employed a Facility Siting Study
(FSS) and Quantitative Risk Assessment (QRA) to better characterize the risk profile for this
demonstration. Following calculation of the risk associated with each identified hazard,
mitigation strategies including additional safeguards were proposed.

 3.16.2 Completion of Piping and Instrumentation Diagrams (P&IDs):

 Safety reviews such as PHA/HAZOP, Facility Siting Study (FSS), and Quantitative
Risk Assessment (QRA) rely on detailed engineering documents and drawings to aid the
identification and assessment of potential hazards. Essential to all these studies/analyses
is the P&ID which describes in detail the equipment, piping, instrumentation, and other

                                                                               Page 56/122

components and the integration of these pieces into a complete system. Completion of
these documents now allows us to accelerate completion of the remaining safety reviews
such as PHA/HAZOP, FSS, and QRA.

 3.16.3 Pre-PHA:

 The pre-PHA was conducted across three virtual meetings in October – November
2022. Caterpillar led the meeting with NLR providing facilitation and scribe services. We
used What If methodology for this pre-PHA with questions guided by the performance
criteria set forth in NFPA 2 Hydrogen Technologies Code (2020). Participants from all other
project stakeholders including McKinstry and DESP were present at these meetings.
 The selected What-If prompts were sufficient to capture the high-level safety
vulnerabilities associated with hazardous material (e.g., hydrogen). These safety
vulnerabilities included hydrogen release, fire, explosion, loss of ventilation leading to a
hazard, co-located hazards, and loss of power. Additional performance criteria were
considered in evaluation of electrical systems (e.g. Voltage too high/low, etc.). The What Ifs
provided a guide to identify potential hazards and to describe existing and potential
additional safeguards for each. A total of 16 recommendations came out of the pre-PHA
analysis. These recommendations are actively being worked to closure by the time of final
PHA. At this pre-PHA stage, hazards and corresponding safeguards were only identified.

 3.16.4 Final PHA/HAZOP Analysis or Design Phase PHA:

 The design phase PHA meeting was convened August 21st through 23rd, 2023 with
engineering and design at a more mature stage. The 38 project team members who
assembled for this meeting covered a diverse range of competencies and experiences in
engineering, safety, project management, and others. All project partners were represented
including McKinstry, DESP / Ever-Green Energy, Certarus, the National Laboratory of the
Rockies (NLR), and the 3rd party Hydrogen Safety Panel by American Institute of Chemical
Engineering (AIChE). The goals of this design phase PHA were to –
 ➢ expand upon the risks to key performance criteria examined in the pre-PHA by
 taking an in-depth look at the system design, operating parameters, materials of
 construction, etc. and at any unintended interaction among the various
 equipment systems,
 ➢ rank identified hazards by severity and probability/frequency of event
 occurrence,
 ➢ identify existing and additional risk mitigation strategies, equipment,
 policies/procedures, etc.,
 ➢ assess if the selected set of mitigations provides for a tolerable residual risk.

 In accordance with these goals, the final PHA used the well-established Hazard and
Operability (HAZOP) methodology. HAZOP relies on the use of guided words (e.g. high

semi-quantitative approach in ranking the risks associated with each identified hazard.

 3.16.5 Pre-Startup Safety Review (PSSR):

 Caterpillar conducted Pre-Startup Safety Reviews (PSSRs) as part of the initial
commissioning of the process and for each MOC resulting in significant modification
enough to require a change in the process safety information thereafter. In general, the
PSSR was conducted after construction, as part of commissioning of the site prior to first
startup. For this project, two PSSR activities were carried out; one prior to the unit initially
starting up on natural gas (“NG-PSSR”) and the second prior to the starting up on hydrogen
fuel (“H2-PSSR”).
 Each PSSR confirmed, prior to the introduction or re-introduction of a highly
hazardous chemical into the process, that:

 ➢ Construction and equipment were in accordance with design specifications,
 ➢ Safety, operating, maintenance, and emergency procedures were in place and
 were adequate.
 ➢ Training of each employee involved in operating a process has been completed.
 ➢ A process hazard analysis had been performed, and recommendations had
 been resolved or implemented before startup; and modified facilities met the
 requirements contained in management of change.
 ➢ Any modifications have been processed through Management of Change (MOC)
 as appropriate and all requirements are met.

 The PSSR methodology relied on the use of a comprehensive checklist which was
reviewed by a wide team of experts involved in the engineering, construction,
commissioning, and start-up of the system. PSSR activities included reviews of the plant
installation against design drawings, quality control documentation, PHA / HAZOP action
item close out, safety system testing (e.g., alarms and interlocks), purge completion, and
tracking of open items. For this project, Caterpillar contracted an independent third party
BakerRisk to facilitate both PSSRs.

  3.20 Techno-economic Analysis (TEA)
 Caterpillar has vast experience in large-scale distributed power generation projects
utilizing variety of fuels and in conducting total cost of ownership analysis for these
projects over long periods of time using appropriate techno-economic analysis tools. Our
project partners at National Lab of the Rockies or NLR, have also conducted vast research
in hydrogen infrastructure and grid integration to determine technical feasibility and
compare cost factors. By leveraging Caterpillar's and NLR's past experiences and using key
inputs from other project partners as well as relevant test and performance data from this
pilot scale field demonstration, the project team conducted detailed analysis of specific
scenarios with design details and estimates for capital costs and operating expenses. To
help advance the CHP system adoption in various DE installations, detailed techno-
economic analysis of overall power generation pathways in various selected applications is
critical and was done by including site-based design details, data analytics, estimates for
capital costs, operating expenses, and system reliability and availability factors. With NLR’s
expertise, we used data analytics and forecasting tools and used renewable generation
capabilities, site heating and electrical load needs, fuel type availability, and market prices
for electricity and fuels, to develop various scenarios for CHP operation.

                                                                                Page 74/122

 For this project, Hydrogen Financial Analysis Scenario Tool (H2FAST) was used to
complete the Techno-economic Analysis (TEA), which compared twelve different power
generation scenarios designed for prime power leveraged at a local district energy site
(District Energy Saint Paul, DESP). The H2FAST is a downloadable tool from the NLR and
was used for in-depth financial analysis of hydrogen and non-hydrogen systems [1]. The
H2FAST enables side-by-side scenario analysis and is a proven framework for hydrogen
systems. The main H2FAST output studied for this project was the Levelized Cost of Energy
(LCOE) in $/kWh, which allows for easy comparison of different system technologies and
scenarios. The H2FAST also allows for variation of parameters based on expected
technological advancement or commodity costs (e.g., cost of natural gas, hydrogen, or
other feedstocks). This enabled the comparison of current and future costs based on an
anticipated technological improvement, allowing for short- and long-term cost
comparison.
 Each representative system and scenario was designed to deliver continuous power

 The LCOE provides results in $/kWh, allowing for clear comparison between each
scenario. The side-by-side LCOE with a cost breakdown by subsystem and category is
shown in Figure 45.

 The overall costs include contributions from the generator system, storage,
installation, fuel/feedstocks, O&M, other financial costs, and potential value added by the
CHP component. Other financial costs are a combination of financial inflows and outflows.
More explicitly, these other costs cover financial items such as dividends paid (payments
back to capital investments), reserve cash on hand (reserved for O&M and unexpected
events), debt repayment (payment back to bank-type debts), and interest expenses
(accrued on borrowed money). For the scoping-level TEA, “Other Costs” can generally be
thought of as an indicator of “capital intensity” given by the select scenario. This means the
more upfront, capital intensive a project, the higher the incurred “Other Costs” component
will be. Specific financial incentives and metrics can be included in real-world
deployments beyond the scope of this project as needed. The contribution of the CHP
value essentially is considered as a by-product “sold” by the system. This allows it to play
into the LCOE as a way to reduce the overall cost of operating the system.
 Current costs demonstrate that diesel and natural gas FFGS scenarios offer the
most cost-effective solutions, which makes sense considering the technology readiness
level, low fuel costs, and low capital intensity of these scenarios. Also contributing to the
lower costs is the availability of the fuel delivered via established delivery network by truck
or integrated pipelines. The FFGS utilizing a natural gas blend with hydrogen is reasonably

                                                                                Page 77/122

cost-effective but has added cost due to the consumption of the more expensive hydrogen
fuel relative to diesel or natural gas.
 All 100% hydrogen fueled scenarios are costly compared to the natural gas and
diesel options largely due to the high costs of delivered liquid or gaseous hydrogen,
hydrogen production, and relative technology readiness levels. Below are broken out cost
contributors and main takeaways for each contributing factor.

 • Onsite storage is a large cost hurdle when operating under prime power type
 conditions, where large quantities are needed to be stored to meet energy demands
 at this scale. Liquid hydrogen offers cost savings due to lower assumed delivered

 3.20.4 TEA Discussion & Other Considerations

 A TEA analysis was performed for several power generation scenarios and
technologies using NLR’s H2FAST modeling tool. Comparison of these LCOE results can
help inform stakeholders of variable generator systems that can integrate into various
applications of prime or ancillary power generation.
 Diesel gen sets and the Caterpillar Flex Fuel Gen Set operating on 100% natural gas
offer the lowest LCOE due to their low barriers of entry and low overall fuel costs relative to
the more expensive hydrogen (gaseous or liquid) alternative. The 100% hydrogen FFGS has
the highest LCOE out of all compared scenarios but has an opportunity to achieve cost
parity with other options assuming delivered hydrogen costs and FFGS system efficiencies
improve in the near- and long-term. Overall, the largest contributing factors for all
scenarios (where applicable) relate to the fuel cost and the fuel storage system cost.
Gaseous hydrogen storage at this scale adds a significant cost to hydrogen fuel cell
applications. Moving towards liquid hydrogen provides cost reductions and a smaller
footprint; however, these technologies are much more nascent and need further

                                                                                 Page 84/122

demonstrations to be proven out. Systems that can optimize and improve storage can
reduce overall LCOE to compete with current state-of-the-art technologies.
 For PEM fuel cells, in all scenarios, the HDV PEM fuel cell is a much more cost-
effective option compared to the stationary PEM fuel cells. While typically durability
concerns have been associated with automotive designed fuel cells, future productions of
these stacks are designed to achieve longer lifespans with improved durability. Currently,
durability targets are for “1 million miles” or approximately 25,000 hours of runtime. If these
targets are achieved, it can help shift PEM fuel cells towards the cheaper HDV fuel cell cost
projections.
 Other key considerations are site specific and can help reduce overall LCOE. Co-
location of resources, other hydrogen end uses, and further renewable energy integration
can help drive feedstock costs in a favorable direction.

 3.21.1 GREET Life Cycle Model

 The GHG analysis was done using R&D GREET.NET V1.3.0.13857 Life-Cycle model
which is a publicly available tool from ANL.[15] The GREET tool is undergoing continual
development and improvement to provide the most up to date parameters for
stakeholders. GREET has preset emission inputs for some technologies while other
nonstandard technologies require more manual input. This is a well-to-wheel analysis tool
designed to capture the GHG emissions associated with the full lifecycle of a process,
which we applied to our CHP scenario analysis.
 In GREET, the emissions pathway for each scenario is built using a series of
conversion steps as shown in Figure 50. The efficiency, emission, and other specifications
for each process step is adjustable to match a specific system of interest. To keep the
results system agnostic the published efficiencies and emissions were utilized when
available, not project specific values. The emissions results from this analysis are given in
GHG-100. ANL’s “GHG emissions calculation combines carbon dioxide, methane, and
nitrous oxide with their global warming potentials (1, 25, and 298, respectively, based on a
100-year time window)” as outlined in ANL’s GREET documentation.[15]

Figure 50. Example of the lifecycle steps captured in GREET for offsite liquid hydrogen
generation from renewables.

 3.21.3 GHG Results

 The results of the analysis, shown in Figure 51 and Figure 52, were broken down into
seven categories. 1) Fuel production and refining, 2) SMR/Grid electricity production, 3)
Electric transmission and distribution, 4) Liquefaction, 5) Compression, 6) Transportation,
7) Power production (generation). The categories are designed to differentiate the different
parts of the process including production, transportation, and generation and clearly

                                                                               Page 87/122

highlight the differences between processes. The scenarios in Figure 51 and Figure 52 from
left to right align with the scenarios’ descriptions, 1-19 above.

Figure 51: Results from the well-to-wheel greenhouse gas emissions analysis utilizing
different fuels and power generation scenarios (Electric Only)

Figure 52: Results from the well-to-wheel greenhouse gas emissions analysis utilizing
different fuels and power generation scenarios (CHP).

 The first three scenarios are the traditional fuel scenarios. Most of the emissions
associated with first and third scenarios are linked to electricity generation due to the
combustion of diesel or natural gas in the generator. In the Biodiesel scenario (2) the
emissions are drastically reduced since the emissions associated with the power
production portion of the process are natural environmental emissions. The Biodiesel

                                                                              Page 88/122

scenario had the third lowest GHG emissions of the 19 scenarios analyzed when not
including emissions due to land-use change to grow products for biofuel production. When
including methodology from GREET for the effect of Induced Land-Use Change the
GHG100 emissions for biodiesel are increased by 87.91 g/kWh electricity of carbon dioxide
equivalent in the electric only scenario and increased by 87.91 g/kWh electricity of carbon
dioxide equivalent in the CHP scenario. This makes it still one of the better scenarios, but
understanding the effects of land-use changes due to biofuel production is an important
aspect to also consider.
 Scenarios 4-11 are the offsite hydrogen production scenarios where the
transportation and hydrogen generation methods are varied. Figure 51 (electric) and Figure
52 (CHP) show that gaseous hydrogen scenarios have less associated greenhouse gas
emissions than their liquid hydrogen counterpart due to the emissions associated with
liquefaction. Liquefaction of hydrogen is a very energy intensive process and accounts for
the largest GHG emission in these offsite hydrogen production scenarios (4-11). This is one
of the most significant contributors to GHG emissions of the analyzed scenarios. However,
greenhouse gas emissions associated with the gaseous hydrogen tube trailer
transportation are significantly higher than liquid hydrogen tube trailer transportation due
to the greater transport capacity of a liquid hydrogen tube trailer (4X more). Additionally, a
liquid system does not require a compressor. Comparing LH2 to GH2, considering
liquefaction, compression, and transportation, the electric only LH2 scenario contributes
anywhere from 120-390 g/kWh electricity of carbon dioxide equivalent more than the GH2
scenario, while the similar CHP scenarios are about half as much. This highlights a need to
take in all aspects of the site and expected use cases.
 Another significant contributor to GHG emissions is SMR. Based on the analysis
SMR with CCS with GH2 delivery is comparable to other technologies in both electric only
and CHP scenarios. Carbon capture technology significantly reduces the GHG emissions
associated with the SMR scenarios (8 and 9). The error bars for those scenarios show that
without CCS the SMR with GHG emissions would increase by about 362 g/kWh electricity
of carbon dioxide. Scenario 10 highlights a different renewable pathway, using renewable
natural gas produced from wastewater sludge to reduce emissions. Like the Biodiesel
discussion above, the use of renewable natural gas can significantly reduce the lifecycle
emissions when compared to a natural gas or diesel generator but is not impacted by the
potential for emissions from induced land-use change from biofuel production. Scenario 7,
which includes renewable offsite hydrogen generation and pipeline delivery, has one of the
lowest greenhouse gas emissions overall and is a likely future scenario with expansion of
regional hydrogen generation centers. Using the GREET model the emissions associated
with a pipeline are lower than liquid hydrogen tube trailer delivery for transport of an

                                                                               Page 89/122

equivalent length. These H2 pipeline routes do not currently exist but show the potential
value of expansion of the hydrogen pipeline network.
 The final category of scenarios (11, 15-19) are the onsite hydrogen production
methods. The primary difference in these scenarios is in how the electricity supplied to the
electrolyzer is generated. As expected, onsite gaseous hydrogen generation using
renewable energy (wind) is the scenario with the lowest GHG emissions. The final five
scenarios demonstrate that the GHG emissions associated with hydrogen production via
onsite electrolysis are highly dependent on the electricity generation technology for the site
or the states grid electricity mix. This highlights the importance of pairing hydrogen
generation systems with clean electricity resources. Grids heavily reliant on fossil fuels
could have significant GHG emissions associated with hydrogen production. Whereas
grids with a significant penetration of renewable energy generation have emissions, due to
hydrogen generation, that are competitive with some of the offsite hydrogen production
and transportation scenarios. Looking at the Wyoming grid mix 73.7% is coal but 19.4% is
from wind. In this scenario if the hydrogen generating equipment is operated in an area or
at times where there is a high penetration of renewable wind energy the greenhouse gas
emissions associated with the system could decrease significantly.
 The emissions contribution of each major category in the well-to-watts analysis is
broken down in Table 9. This breakdown highlights where there are significant emissions in
the fuel lifecycle and clearly shows area for improvement. For example, creating electricity
with a hydrogen fuel cell does not create any GHG emissions but compression of the gas
for long duration storage comes at an emission cost. Similarly long-distance transportation
is a significant contribution to the total emissions of the hydrogen-based scenarios.
Reduction of the transportation emissions could be accomplished through reduction of the
transport distance or alternative powered trucks (e.g., fuel cell electric vehicles).
Distributed hydrogen generation like DOE’s hydrogen hubs could significantly reduce both
the distance from the supplier to end us and the overall cost of the hydrogen fuel.

 3.21.4 GHG Conclusions

 Replacing diesel gensets with renewably generated hydrogen powered Genset has
the potential to reduce the GHG emissions and provide fuel flexibility to a site. The diesel
scenario produces just under 1000 g/kWh, while the best hydrogen scenario, “onsite
gaseous hydrogen generation from PEM water electrolysis using onsite wind energy and H2
Genset” produces just over 100 g/kWh. Biodiesel and renewable natural gas are interesting
options that could significantly lower emissions while using infrastructure already in place
today for distribution and storage. These results when paired with the TEA can help industry
make informed decisions regarding the cost and potential GHG emissions associated with
technology selection.

 4. Key Achievements
 The project team used advanced simulation, analysis, development, and testing
capabilities at Caterpillar to develop a new state of the art fuel flexible CHP system for
integration with an existing district energy system. This project enables leveraging diverse
domestic energy resources in U.S. industries, while strengthening environmental
stewardship and manufacturing workforce by supporting innovative technologies and
practices. As a result of this work Caterpillar now, additionally, has hydrogen-based power
generation offerings like - Caterpillar G3516H DTO for 100% hydrogen fuel for 50 or 60 Hz
applications, gas gensets configured for 25% hydrogen blended with natural gas, and
retrofit kits to enable hydrogen fuel usage on existing natural gas engines.
 At the beginning of this project, to carry out single-cylinder (or 1-cyl) gas engine R&D
work, an engine test cell at the engine development facility of Caterpillar at Technical
Center, Mossville, IL was upgraded with a brand new 1-cyl engine (4.9 liter, 135 bkW),
gaseous hydrogen supply system from a remote mounted tube trailer, hydrogen safeties, a
port fuel injection system, and a hydrogen concentration measurement system to measure
the hydrogen concentration in the intake manifold, engine exhaust, and crankcase.
 We conducted single-cylinder engine testing to develop combustion recipes for
100% hydrogen, 100% NG, and hydrogen + natural gas fuel blends. This work included
testing concept hardware such as pistons, ignition, and port fuel injection systems.
 The 1D simulations were carried out to understand the impacts of utilizing pipeline
natural gas and pure hydrogen, with differing compression ratios and Miller amounts, on
the brake thermal efficiency (BTE) and brake mean effective pressure (BMEP). And, for
selecting components for multi-cylinder G3516 prototype engine such as air-system, etc.
 During CHP genset system demonstration at DESP, while supplying both electricity
and waste heat recovered from the engine to the district energy plant, we achieved up to
41.5% electrical and 85% overall fuel efficiency from this flex-fuel genset. Although system
performance on hydrogen during startup and steady-state and transient operation is very
similar to that on natural gas, the hydrogen engine exhaust contained only water vapors,
zero CO2, and 90% less NOx emission than that from natural gas engine exhaust. Summary
of achievements –

 ➢ Islanded mode operation of the genset (grid paralleling not possible due to local
 utility constraints)
 ➢ NG: ~266 run hours, 1.75MWe max genset load tested (~13% derate due to gas
 quality), 1.0 g/bhp-hr engine tailpipe NOx,
 ➢ 100% H2: ~206 run hours, 1.0MWe max genset load tested, ~0.1 g/bhp-hr engine
 tailpipe NOx, Near zero GHG or CO2 in engine exhaust
 ➢ Up to 1.32MWe electrical load from DESP plant was put on the genset, and up to
 1.85 MW-thermal engine WHR was supplied to DESP plant

 7. Conclusions

 This project successfully developed a state of the art two-megawatt fuel-flexible
CHP genset system capable of running on 100% NG, 100% H2, and up to 25% blends of H2
in NG (vol. basis) and integrated it with a renewably fueled district energy plant and
successfully completed the demonstration involving utilization of electricity and engine
waste heat recovery by the plant. After extensive R&D work involving simulation, single-
cylinder engine R&D, and multicylinder-engine R&D, the 2MW genset was containerized
and shipped to the site. After an initial feasibility study at the demonstration site, the team
progressed to develop detailed engineering plan and filed and received required permits
from the local AHJ, navigating a changing regulatory landscape to secure the necessary
permissions for on-site hydrogen storage and power generation.
                                                                                Page 96/122

 The site development work followed, involving the integration of 2MW flex-fuel CHP
genset system with DESP plant for delivery of electric power and engine waste heat
recovery, natural gas supply from an existing pipe network of the local utility and hydrogen
supply from the compressed hydrogen tube-trailers, and supporting infrastructure.
Detailed hydrogen safety analysis and quantitative risk analysis were also performed to
enable safe operation and demonstration of the CHP system at the DESP site. Rigorous site
safety protocols were implemented throughout, especially concerning the handling and
containment of hydrogen, a highly flammable fuel.
 After installation and integration exercises, all cross-functional teams from
Caterpillar, McKinstry, DESP, Ziegler Power Systems, NLR, Certarus, BakerRisk, and
Hydrogen Safety Panel worked collaboratively on commissioning and debugging of the
entire system to ensure safe and smooth operation during demonstration and testing. To
fully assess every aspect of the hydrogen system under real-world operating conditions,
electric power and thermal waste heat recovered from the integrated flex-fuel CHP genset
system were fed into DESP’s active distribution system. The CHP genset was run using
100% natural gas fuel and 100% hydrogen fuel for 200+ hours with each fuel type during the
demonstration and extensive performance and emission data was collected. Although
CHP genset performance on hydrogen during startups and steady-state and transient
operations was very close to the performance on natural gas, the hydrogen engine exhaust
contained only water vapors, no carbon dioxide, and 90% less NOx emission than that from
a natural gas engine exhaust. A technoeconomic analysis found diesel and natural gas
generators to be the most cost-effective power generation options due to their technology
readiness, low fuel costs, and capital intensity, while 100% hydrogen-fueled scenarios are
more expensive because of high hydrogen costs and technology maturity. The greenhouse
gas emissions analysis showed that replacing diesel generators with hydrogen-powered
ones using onsite renewable energy could reduce emissions from nearly 1000 g/kWh CO2e
to just over 100 g/kWh CO2e, with biodiesel and renewable natural gas also offering
significant emission reductions using existing infrastructure.
 This hands-on demonstration not only confirmed the technical viability of hydrogen
and natural gas-based flex-fuel CHP genset as a supplemental electric power and thermal
energy source at a real-world application district energy site but also yielded valuable
insights into system development, integration, safety best practices, and the future
scalability of such solutions for many other applications.

 8. Future Work/Recommendations

 ➢ Based on our discussion with some Caterpillar customers who visited the
 demonstration site and who owned chlore-alkali plant where hydrogen is produced
 as by-product of the chemical processes used in the plant, some government

 subsidies on installation of hydrogen-based gas gensets would help and encourage

 ➢ Green or low carbon intensity hydrogen production sites, using excess renewable
 electricity to produce hydrogen, are other possible early adopters of hydrogen-
 based gas gensets.

 ➢ Further R&D on port-fuel injected SI hydrogen engine/genset to help reduce power
 derate compared to natural gas operation and increase break thermal efficiency.

 ➢ Further R&D to investigate pros and cons of using low/medium pressure direct

 ➢ Transportation of compressed hydrogen in tube-trailers to the point-of-use site for
 hydrogen gensets significantly increases the cost of hydrogen fuel. A network of
 pipes to deliver this hydrogen is required.

 nitrogen purging of the H2 fuel-train after the shutdown.

 Appendix C: Techno-economic Analysis (TEA) Assumptions

 The Caterpillar CHP Fuel Flexible Gen Set (FFGS) assumptions were based on
demonstrated data and quotes made available to the analysis team. The FFGS is capable
of running on three different fuel types: 1) 100% natural gas combustion, 2) a 75% natural
gas / 25% hydrogen blend, and 3) 100% hydrogen combustion. Each scenario is designed
for combined heat and power (CHP) availability and was captured as a by-product in the
analysis. An assumed yearly operation & maintenance (O&M) cost was agreed upon by the
team, and installation costs (% of Capital Expenditure) were incorporated to ensure
consistent bookkeeping when compared to the other scenarios. Table 12 below details the
assumptions made for the FFGS.

2) Biodiesel generator:

Biodiesel has many of the same strengths as diesel fuel but is not as readily available.
Since biodiesel is made using carbon that is recovered from natural processes the

extracted from soybeans and undergoes transesterification, where it is reacted with an
alcohol to produce biodiesel. For biodiesel production there is included an induced land
use change in the emissions of 68 g CO2/gal biodiesel due to land (forest, grassland, young
forest, cropland pasture) transitioned to wide-scale fuel productions.[15]

4) 25% Hydrogen/75% NG Blend Pipeline to H2 Genset

explore the benefits of a flexible fuel generator that can run on pure NG, H2/NG blends up to
30% and 100% H2. In a mature hydrogen economy, it represents the chance that a site
might purchase inexpensive hydrogen to be stored onsite and then used to either for cost
