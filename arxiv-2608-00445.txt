Exact detection threshold of the packing test
Tuan Pham

Abstract
Using Poisson approximation techniques, we derive the detection threshold of the packing test in Cai et al. [2013] when testing spherical uniformity under high-dimensional Fisher–von Mises–Langevin (FvML) and Watson alternatives. Our result rigorously confirms the empirical observation that the packing test is strictly suboptimal for testing uniformity in these two popular models. In the high-dimensional FvML model, its detection threshold is precisely κ = Θ ⁡ ( p 3 / 4 / ( log ⁡ n ) 1 / 4 ) \kappa=\Theta\left(p^{3/4}/(\log n)^{1/4}\right) . In the high-dimensional Watson model, its detection threshold is p − 2 ​ κ = Θ ⁡ ( p ​ log ⁡ n ) p-2\kappa=\Theta(\sqrt{p\log n}) , or equivalently κ = p / 2 − Θ ⁡ ( p ​ log ⁡ n ) \kappa=p/2-\Theta(\sqrt{p\log n}) . We show that the limiting scalings of the largest squared inner product undergo a discontinuous phase transition in the Watson model, whereas no analogous phenomenon occurs in the FvML model.



1
Introduction

Assessing uniformity is a classical goodness-of-fit problem in directional statistics. Arguably one of the most fundamental questions in the field, uniformity testing has attracted considerable attention over the past few decades. Let us briefly describe the setting. Let
\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n}
be observations with
\bm{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. We assume that the variables
\bm{X}_{i}
are drawn independently from an unknown distribution
\mu
supported on the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
. Denote the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
by
\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
. The uniformity testing problem can then be formulated as

\displaystyle H_{0}:\ \mu=\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_{1}:\ \mu\neq\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1}).

(1)

In fixed dimensions, particularly for
p=2
and
p=3
, uniformity testing plays an important role in applications such as geology, paleomagnetism, and cosmology. For detailed background and examples, we refer to the monographs
Fisher et al. [1987]
;
Ley and Verdebout [2017]
;
Mardia and Jupp [2000]
. Recent tests that apply in arbitrary but fixed dimensions include
García-Portugués et al. [2023]
;
Fernández-de-Marcos and García-Portugués [2023]
;
García-Portugués et al. [2021]
(see also the references therein). We also refer to the survey papers
Portugués and Verdebout [2018]
;
Pewsey and García-Portugués [2021]
for an overview of recent progress and additional testing procedures.

More recently, the testing problem (
1
) has found new applications in high-dimensional statistics and machine learning. The situation in high dimensions is more interesting than its fixed-dimensional counterpart because the sphere suffers from the curse of dimensionality in high dimensions, and a test can be suboptimal in terms of sample size, dimension, or both. Let us briefly highlight some applications below.

Testing spherical symmetry
.
It is well known that a random vector
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is spherically symmetric if and only if
\|\bm{X}\|
is independent of
\bm{X}/\|\bm{X}\|
, and
\bm{X}/\|\bm{X}\|
is uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
. Therefore, spherical symmetry can be assessed by combining an independence test with a uniformity test. In practice, departures from spherical symmetry often manifest as violations of uniformity for the projected directions (see
Cutting et al. [2017]
for details).

Contrastive learning
.
Contrastive learning is a form of representation learning in which one seeks to learn a map
\phi
, given data
\{\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n}\}
, such that
\phi
extracts useful features for a downstream task, which may range from estimation and classification to clustering.

More precisely, for each
i\in[1,n]
, one generates two augmented views
\left(\bm{X}^{(1)}_{i},\bm{X}^{(2)}_{i}\right)
of
\bm{X}_{i}
using random transformations (one may think of
\bm{X}_{i}
as an image and of the random transformations as random cropping, blurring, or color distortion). An ideal
\phi
should align the paired views and separate the unpaired views. One way to separate the unpaired views is to require that the normalized transformed data

\bm{z}^{(j)}_{i}:=\frac{\phi\left(\bm{x}^{(j)}_{i}\right)}{\left\|\phi\left(\bm{x}^{(j)}_{i}\right)\,\right\|};\qquad j=1,2

satisfy

\bm{z}^{(1)}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mbox{Unif}\left(\mathbb{S}^{p-1}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bm{z}^{(2)}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}^{(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mbox{Unif}\left(\mathbb{S}^{p-1}\right).

A high-dimensional version of Ajne’s test
Ajne [1968]
for uniformity was used in
Fürst et al. [2022]
to assess the quality of the learned map
\phi
; see also
Wang and Isola [2020]
;
Jing et al. [2021]
and the references therein.

Regularization of neural networks
.

In overparameterized neural networks, regularization is crucial for preventing overfitting and improving generalization.
Given
n
neuron weight vectors
\bm{w}_{1},\bm{w}_{2},\ldots,\bm{w}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, a regularization term typically takes the form

\displaystyle\underbrace{\lambda_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\bm{w}_{i})}_{\text{magnitude regularization}}+\underbrace{\lambda_{2}\,g(\bm{w}_{1},\bm{w}_{2},\ldots,\bm{w}_{n})}_{\text{structure regularization}}.

The penalty function
h
typically regularizes the magnitudes of the neurons, for example through
\ell_{1}
or
\ell_{2}
norms, analogous to the Lasso or ridge penalties in high-dimensional statistics.
In contrast, the term
g
aims to promote specific structures among the neurons.
A line of empirical work
Lin et al. [2020]
;
Liu et al. [2018]
;
Xie et al. [2017]
has demonstrated that when
g
is designed to encourage
hyperspherical uniformity
among the neurons, it can lead to improved generalization.
Uniformly distributed neurons have also been shown to help avoid spurious local minima during training
Xie et al. [2017]
.
Connections between this perspective and testing hyperspherical uniformity have been discussed in
Liu et al. [2021]
.

Despite the abundance of existing tests in fixed- and low-dimensional settings, uniformity testing in high dimensions remains comparatively underexplored.
To the best of the author’s knowledge, only a few high-dimensional tests have been investigated in the literature.
We provide a brief overview of these tests below.

1.

Rayleigh test

Cutting et al. [2017]
;
Paindaveine and Verdebout [2016]
.
This test can be formulated as a U-statistic of the data points with the inner-product kernel:

\displaystyle R_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sqrt{2p}}{n}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}.

(2)

2.

Bingham test

Cutting et al. [2022]
;
Zou et al. [2014]
;
Paindaveine and Verdebout [2016]
.
This test is also based on a U-statistic of the data points, but with a quadratic inner-product kernel:

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{p}{n}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left[\bigl(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}\bigr)^{2}-\frac{1}{p}\right].

(3)

3.

Packing test

Cai et al. [2013]
.
This test is based on the smallest pairwise angle, or equivalently on the largest absolute inner product:

\displaystyle P_{n}

\displaystyle:=p\cdot\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\bigl(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}\bigr)^{2}-4\log n+\log\log n.

(4)

4.

High-dimensional Sobolev tests

Ebner et al. [2025]
.
This is a family of Sobolev-based tests that generalizes the classical tests proposed in
Gine [1975]
.

Among the tests above, the packing test (
4
) remains comparatively less understood in terms of sharp detection rates and consistency against standard local parametric alternatives.
Empirically, it does not appear to be optimal against common classes of alternatives, although it is provably consistent against certain types of non-local alternatives
Jiang and Pham [2025a]
.
For the Rayleigh and Bingham tests, several detection-rate and optimality results have already been established in the literature. The Rayleigh test (
2
) is known to be optimal against the FvML distributions (see (
5
) below for a precise definition) and blind to the Watson distributions (see (
6
) below for a precise definition)
Cutting et al. [2017]
.
The Bingham test exhibits the opposite behavior: it is optimal against the Watson distributions but suboptimal against the FvML distributions
Cutting et al. [2022]
.

The goal of the present article is to rigorously derive and justify the detection rates of the packing test under the classes of FvML and Watson distributions using Poisson approximation techniques; see, for example,
Arratia et al. [1989]
and the references therein. Our result shows that the detection threshold for the packing test is
\Theta\left(p^{3/4}/\left(\log n\right)^{1/4}\right)
under the FvML model and
p/2-\Theta\left(\sqrt{p\log n}\right)
under the Watson model.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The models and known results are presented in Section
2
. The main results can be found in Sections
3
and
4
, respectively. Section
5
contains some remarks and discussion. The proofs are given in Sections
7
,
8
,
9
, and
10
. Technical results are provided in the appendix.

2
Model and known results

Recall that the data are
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{n}
, which are i.i.d. points on the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
with distribution
\mu
.
Under the null, that is,
\mu\equiv\mbox{Uni}\left(\mathbb{S}^{p-1}\right)
, the asymptotic distribution of
P_{n}
is well known to be the Gumbel distribution
Cai et al. [2013]
. Under nonuniform alternatives, however, the limiting distribution is unknown. Since the packing test is of extreme-value type, its analysis requires techniques different from those used for U-statistic-type tests.

In what follows, we describe two common parametric models in directional statistics: the Fisher–von Mises–Langevin (FvML) distributions and the Watson distributions. These models consist of densities that have the form

\displaystyle x\;\longmapsto\;c^{\rm FvML}_{p,\kappa}\,\exp\!\left(\kappa\,\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\mu}\right),\qquad x\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1},

(5)

and

\displaystyle x\;\longmapsto\;c^{\rm Wat}_{p,\kappa}\,\exp\!\left(\kappa\,(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\mu})^{2}\right),\qquad x\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1},

(6)

respectively. Here
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the concentration parameter,
\bm{\mu}
is the unknown location (nuisance) parameter, and
c^{\rm FvML}_{p,\kappa},c^{\rm Wat}_{p,\kappa}
are normalizing constants.

The optimal detection rates in these two models have been studied in
Cutting et al. [2017]
;
Cutting et al. [2022]
;
Jiang and Pham [2025b]
. It was shown in
Cutting et al. [2017]
that the optimal detection rate for the FvML model is
\kappa\sim p^{3/4}/\sqrt{n}
and is achieved by the Rayleigh test (
2
). Moreover, the Rayleigh test is asymptotically optimal among all rotationally invariant tests. The optimal detection rate for the Watson model (
6
) is more interesting and complicated: it exhibits a phase transition between the low- and high-dimensional regimes. It was shown in
Cutting et al. [2022]
that when
p\ll n
, the optimal detection rate is
\kappa\sim p^{3/2}/\sqrt{n}
, whereas it was shown in
Jiang and Pham [2025b]
that when
p\gg n
, the optimal detection rate is
p/2-\kappa\sim\sqrt{pn}
.

Using Le Cam’s third lemma, the authors of
Jiang and Pham [2025a]
show that the packing test (
4
) is asymptotically blind at the optimal detection threshold in the FvML model (
5
): if one sets
\kappa=\tau p^{3/4}/\sqrt{n}
, then regardless of how large
\tau
is, the packing test has asymptotic power equal to its size. Extending this result to the Watson model (
6
) is highly nontrivial because a high-dimensional LAN result is unavailable in this model.

Using Le Cam’s third lemma, the authors of
Ebner et al. [2025]
further establish a somewhat surprising result: within a general class of Sobolev-based tests that includes the Rayleigh and Bingham tests as special cases, the Rayleigh test is the only one that achieves the optimal detection rate under the FvML model.
In particular, any linear combination of
R_{n}
and
B_{n}
in (
2
) and (
3
), respectively, that is not proportional to
R_{n}
fails to achieve the optimal detection rate.

It is also clear that the packing test (
4
) should not be blind in these two models: when the concentration parameter
\kappa
is large enough, the maximum of squared inner products is approximately one with overwhelming probability, and thus
P_{n}\to\infty
in probability. This motivates the problem of precisely identifying the detection thresholds of
P_{n}
in these two models. We do so by deriving the non-null asymptotic distribution of
M_{n}
in (
7
) below under the two models (
5
) and (
6
).

. We will also write

3
Detection rate and limiting distribution under the FvML model

Recall the FvML model in (
5
). Our first main result gives a non-null asymptotic distribution of
P_{n}
under this model.

Theorem 1
.

Recall
P_{n}
in (
4
). Suppose
p/(\log n)^{2}\to\infty
and

\kappa_{n}=\tau\cdot\frac{p^{3/4}}{\left(\log n\right)^{1/4}}

for some
\tau\geq 0
. Then, under the FvML model with concentration parameter
\kappa_{n}
,

P_{n}-2\log\cosh\left(2\tau^{2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}G

where
G
is the Gumbel law with distribution function

\mathbb{P}(G\leq y)=\exp\{-(8\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-y/2}\}.

Theorem
1
states that the asymptotic distribution of
P_{n}
under the class of FvML distributions is a shifted Gumbel distribution, with a deterministic shift. Note that we recover the null asymptotic distribution by setting
\tau\equiv 0
.
As a direct consequence of Theorem
1
, the packing test
P_{n}
has detection rate of order

\frac{p^{3/4}}{(\log n)^{1/4}}.

Comparing this rate with the known minimax rate
p^{3/4}/\sqrt{n}
from
Cutting et al. [2017]
, we observe an interesting phenomenon: the packing test achieves the optimal scaling in terms of the dimension dependence, namely
p^{3/4}
, but is suboptimal in its sample-size dependence, with
(\log n)^{-1/4}
replacing the optimal scale
n^{-1/2}
. This helps explain its low empirical power at the minimax scale.

As a consequence of Theorem
1
, we can calculate the power function of
P_{n}
explicitly.
Let
q_{\alpha}
denote the asymptotic level-
\alpha
critical value under the null, defined by

q_{\alpha}=-2\log\!\left[\sqrt{8\pi}\,\left(-\log(1-\alpha)\right)\right].

Suppose that the FvML concentration parameter satisfies

\frac{\kappa_{n}(\log n)^{1/4}}{p^{3/4}}\to\tau\in[0,\infty).

Theorem
1
then gives the asymptotic power of the level-
\alpha
packing test to be

\displaystyle\beta_{\rm FvML}(\kappa_{n},\bm{\mu}_{n})

\displaystyle:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\kappa_{n},\bm{\mu}_{n}}\!\left(P_{n}>q_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{q_{\alpha}-2\log\cosh(2\tau^{2})}{2}\right)\right\}=1-(1-\alpha)^{\cosh(2\tau^{2})}.

The proof of Theorem
1
is based on Poisson approximation techniques
Arratia et al. [1989]
(see also
Feng et al. [2026]
;
Jammalamadaka and Janson [2015]
and the references therein).
The deviation from the Gumbel law in Theorem
1
comes from the mean shift of the inner product, which makes the FvML model easier to analyze than the Watson model, which is symmetric and deviates from the null through its variance.

4
Detection rate and limiting distribution under the Watson model

Recall the Watson model in (
6
). We will assume that
\kappa_{n}<p/2
and

\displaystyle\rho_{n}:=\frac{\Delta_{n}}{\sqrt{p\log n}}\to\rho\in(0,\infty),\qquad\Delta_{n}:=\frac{p-2\kappa_{n}}{2}.

Recall
M_{n}
from (
7
). We will investigate the asymptotic distribution of
M_{n}
in three regimes:
\rho\in(1,\infty)
,
\rho\in(0,1)
, and
\rho=1
. The regime
\rho\in(1,\infty)
will be called
local
because the packing test has nontrivial asymptotic power (strictly less than one) in this regime. The regime
\rho\in(0,1)
will be called
non-local
since the packing test always has asymptotic power one in this regime. The regime
\rho=1
will be called
critical
since its second-order approximation differs from those in the other regimes; see Section
4.3
below for more details.

It turns out that although
M_{n}
is asymptotically Gumbel after appropriate normalization in all three regimes, the corresponding asymptotic scalings undergo a subtle and interesting phase transition in the second-order approximation of the asymptotic mean, with a surprisingly discontinuous transition at
\rho=1
.

4.1
The local regime
\rho\in(1,\infty)

Our result is as follows.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
p/\left(\log n\right)^{2}\to\infty
and
\rho_{n}\to\rho>1
. For every fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\kappa_{n},\bm{\mu}_{n}}\left(P_{n}\leq x\right){\to}\exp\left\{-(8\pi)^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{-2}}}\cdot e^{-x/2}\right\},\qquad

\displaystyle\text{if $\rho\in(1,\infty)$}.

Here
P_{n}
is defined as in (
4
).

Theorem
2
states that when
\rho>1
,
P_{n}
converges to a shifted Gumbel law

P_{n}+\log(1-\rho^{-2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}G,\qquad\mathbb{P}(G\leq y)=\exp\left\{-(8\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-y/2}\right\}

under the Watson alternatives, with deterministic shift
\log\left(1-\rho^{-2}\right)
. Thus the detection rate achieved by the packing test in this case is of order

\kappa_{n}=\frac{p}{2}-\rho\sqrt{p\log n}\cdot\left(1+o(1)\right).

In other words, the detection rate of the packing test in the Watson model is

\kappa_{\rm packing}=p/2-\Theta\left(\sqrt{p\log n}\right).

Let us compare this detection rate to the minimax rate in the Watson model
Jiang and Pham [2025b]
;
Cutting et al. [2022]

\kappa_{\rm minimax}=\begin{cases}\Theta\left(\frac{p^{3/2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right),\qquad&\text{if $p\ll n$}\\
\frac{p}{2}-\Theta\left(\sqrt{pn}\right),&\text{if $p\gg n$}.\end{cases}

Comparing
\kappa_{\rm packing}
and
\kappa_{\rm minimax}
, we see that the level-
\alpha
packing test is suboptimal in both dimension and sample size in the low-dimensional regime
p=o(n)
. Interestingly, in the high-dimensional regime, it correctly captures the dimensional dependence but is suboptimal in terms of sample-size scaling.

As a direct consequence of Theorem
2
, we can calculate the asymptotic power function of the packing test against the Watson alternatives as follows. Let
q_{\alpha}
denote the asymptotic level-
\alpha
critical value under
the null, defined by

q_{\alpha}=-2\log\left[-\sqrt{8\pi}\log(1-\alpha)\right].

Suppose that the Watson concentration parameter satisfies

\frac{\Delta_{n}}{\sqrt{p\log n}}\to\rho\in[0,\infty).

Thus the asymptotic power function is

\displaystyle\beta_{\rm Wat}(\kappa_{n},\bm{\mu}_{n})=\begin{cases}1-(1-\alpha)^{(1-\rho^{-2})^{-1/2}},&\rho>1,\\[4.0pt]
1,&0\leq\rho\leq 1.\end{cases}

(12)

The assertion of asymptotic power one in the regime
0\leq\rho\leq 1
is a consequence of Theorems
3
and
4
below. Note that although the case
\rho=1
does not exactly follow from Theorem
4
, its proof requires only minor modifications (and is even simpler because we only need to prove consistency). The needed modifications can be found in Appendix
B
.

From the asymptotic power formula above, as
\rho\downarrow 1
, the exponent

(1-\rho^{-2})^{-1/2}
diverges and the power tends to one. On the other
hand, as
\rho\to\infty
, the exponent tends to one and the limiting power
approaches the size
\alpha
. This is consistent with the interpretation
that the packing test has nontrivial asymptotic power only when

\Delta_{n}=\frac{p}{2}-\kappa_{n}=\Theta\left(\sqrt{p\log n}\right),

or equivalently

\kappa_{n}=\frac{p}{2}-\Theta\left(\sqrt{p\log n}\right).

4.2
The non-local regime
\rho\in(0,1)

In the non-local regime
\rho\in(0,1)
, the scaling limit of
M_{n}
in (
7
) becomes
\rho
-dependent. This dependence arises because the distribution of
M_{n}
is also affected by the pairwise products of the cosine components (see the representations (
15
) and (
39
)), rather than solely by the inner products of the uniform components. Before stating our result, define

\displaystyle A(\rho):=\sqrt{2(1+\rho^{2})};\qquad a(\rho):=2\left[\sqrt{2(1+\rho^{2})}-\rho\right];

(13)

\displaystyle K(\rho):=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot A(\rho)\cdot\sqrt{\left(A(\rho)-\rho\right)\cdot\left(A(\rho)-2\rho\right)}}.

(14)

Our main result in the non-local regime
\rho<1
is as follows.

Theorem 3
.

Assume that

\frac{p}{\left(\log n\right)^{3}}\to\infty;\qquad\rho_{n}=\frac{\Delta_{n}}{\sqrt{p\log n}}\to\rho\in(0,1).

With
M_{n}
defined as in (
7
) and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

p\cdot M_{n}^{2}-a\left(\rho_{n}\right)^{2}\cdot\log n+\frac{a\left(\rho_{n}\right)}{A\left(\rho_{n}\right)}\cdot\log\log n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}G_{\rho}

where
G_{\rho}
is a Gumbel distribution with CDF

F_{\rho}(x):=\exp\left[-K(\rho)\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{A(\rho)}{2a(\rho)}\cdot x\right)\right].

Let us compare the results of Theorem
3
and Theorem
2
. Although both limiting distributions are of Gumbel type, their scalings are markedly different: Theorem
2
implies that

M_{n}^{2}=\frac{4\log n}{p}-\frac{\log\log n}{p}+o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\log\log n}{p}\right)

In particular, the first- and second-order approximations of
M_{n}^{2}
are independent of the value of
\rho
as long as
\rho>1
. This scaling is also the same as under the null (uniformity). In contrast, Theorem
3
gives

\displaystyle M_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{4\left[\sqrt{2(1+\rho^{2})}-\rho\right]^{2}+o(1)\right\}\cdot\frac{\log n}{p}-\left[\frac{2\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}-\sqrt{2}\rho}{\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}}+o\left(1\right)\right]\cdot\frac{\log\log n}{p}

\displaystyle+o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\log\log n}{p}\right)

for which the first- and second-order approximations depend on the value of
\rho
when
\rho<1
. Here the
o(1)
term is due to
\rho_{n}\to\rho
. Interestingly, the transition from the local regime to the non-local regime is continuous but not smooth.

We note that in this non-local regime, we require a slightly stronger condition on the relative growth of the dimension and sample size: we need
p/(\log n)^{3}\to\infty
instead of
p/(\log n)^{2}\to\infty
. This is a technical condition that arises in the large-deviation analysis. It might be possible to get a sharper condition by a more careful asymptotic analysis. However, since our goal is to highlight the phenomenon rather than to investigate the sharpest possible conditions, we will not pursue this technical extension in the current paper.

4.3
The critical regime
\rho=1

We now investigate the critical regime
\rho=1
. Interestingly, the scaling in this regime is discontinuous at the second-order approximation.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that

\frac{p}{\left(\log n\right)^{3}}\to\infty;\qquad\sqrt{\log n}\left(\rho_{n}-1\right)\to\Delta\in\mathbb{R}.

Define

K_{\rm cr}\left(\Delta\right):=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\Delta y^{2}-\frac{y^{4}}{4}\right\}\,dy.

With
M_{n}
defined as in (
7
) and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

5
Conclusion and discussion

In this paper, we derive the asymptotic distribution of the packing test for uniformity under the high-dimensional FvML and Watson models.
As a consequence, we precisely characterize the detection thresholds of the packing test under these two models.
In the high-dimensional FvML model, we find that the detection threshold of the packing test is

\Theta\!\left(\frac{p^{3/4}}{(\log n)^{1/4}}\right),

which has the optimal dependence on the dimension but is suboptimal in terms of the sample size.
In contrast, under the Watson model, the detection threshold is of the form

\frac{p}{2}-\Theta\!\left(\sqrt{p\log n}\right),

which differs from both the FvML threshold and the known minimax rates.

Our method is based on Poisson approximation and truncation techniques to prove asymptotic independence. We precisely identify the local regime under the Watson model in which the packing test has strictly nontrivial asymptotic power (strictly between the size
\alpha
and one).
Outside the local regime, we identify a non-local regime in which the scaling of the largest squared inner product undergoes a discontinuous phase transition, and we show that the packing test is asymptotically powerful throughout this regime.

We believe the framework developed in the current paper can be useful for other models as well. A natural extension is to consider the class of monotone, rotationally invariant distributions in
Cutting et al. [2017]
, which generalizes the two models considered in the present paper. However, extending the framework to those settings requires a new analysis to address the loss of strong convexity, for which Laplace’s method might no longer be effective.

6
Proof idea

We give a brief heuristic derivation of the three regimes in the Watson model.
The same type of derivation can also be adapted to the FvML model, where it is simpler because the deviation from uniformity comes from the mean, whereas in the Watson model it comes from the variance.
By the tangent–normal decomposition (
39
), we have the heuristic representation

\left\{\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}_{j}\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\approx}}\left\{T_{i}T_{j}+\sqrt{1-T_{i}^{2}}\sqrt{1-T_{j}^{2}}\,W_{ij}\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n},

where the variables
W_{ij}
are approximately independent normal random variables with variance
1/p
, and the
T_{i}
are i.i.d. with density (
40
).

Let us first consider the case
\rho_{n}\to\rho>1
. In this regime, the limiting distribution can be guessed from a point-process heuristic. Since the
T_{i}
are small, we make the approximation

\left\{\sqrt{p}\,\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}_{j}\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\approx}}\left\{\sqrt{p}\,T_{i}T_{j}+\sqrt{p}\,W_{ij}\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}.

This approximation should be understood in the point-process sense. It can be justified rigorously, although the proof below does not rely on such a point-process result. Since

\sqrt{p-2\kappa_{n}}\,T_{i}\approx Z_{i},\qquad Z_{i}\sim N(0,1),

we can rewrite the preceding approximation as

\left\{\sqrt{p}\,\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}_{j}\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\approx}}\left\{\frac{\sqrt{p}}{p-2\kappa_{n}}\,Z_{i}Z_{j}+\sqrt{p}\,W_{ij}\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n},

where the
Z_{i}
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables.

It is well known that, for
\widetilde{a}_{n}\sim 2\sqrt{\log n}
,

\left\{\widetilde{a}_{n}\left(\sqrt{p}\,W_{ij}-\widetilde{a}_{n}\right)\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}

converges in distribution to a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}
with intensity measure
e^{-x}\,dx
. Therefore, heuristically,

\left\{\widetilde{a}_{n}\left(\sqrt{p}\,\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}_{j}-\widetilde{a}_{n}\right)\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\approx}}\left\{\frac{\sqrt{p}\cdot\widetilde{a}_{n}}{p-2\kappa_{n}}\,\widetilde{Z}_{i}+\Xi_{i}\right\}_{i\geq 1},

where
\{\Xi_{i}:i\geq 1\}
are the points of a Poisson point process with intensity
e^{-x}\,dx
, and the
\widetilde{Z}_{i}
are i.i.d. copies of the product of two independent standard normal random variables, independent of the
\Xi_{i}
.

The last approximation is again in the point-process sense. In this heuristic derivation, we have ignored the weak dependence among the products
Z_{i}Z_{j}
, which does not affect the first-order extreme-value behavior. Since

\frac{\sqrt{p}\cdot\widetilde{a}_{n}}{p-2\kappa_{n}}\to\rho^{-1},

the limiting point process is the image of the marked point process

\left\{\left(\widetilde{Z}_{i},\Xi_{i}\right):i\geq 1\right\}

under the map

(z,\xi)\longmapsto\rho^{-1}z+\xi.

Thus we expect the limiting distribution of the maximum to satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{a}_{n}\left(\sqrt{p}\cdot\max_{i<j}\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}_{j}-\widetilde{a}_{n}\right)\leq x\right)

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{P}\left(\text{no pair }(\widetilde{Z}_{i},\Xi_{i})\text{ satisfies }\rho^{-1}\widetilde{Z}_{i}+\Xi_{i}>x\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\iint_{\{(\xi,z):\,\rho^{-1}z+\xi>x\}}e^{-\xi}\,d\xi\,dF_{\widetilde{Z}}(z)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-e^{-x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(\frac{z}{\rho}\right)\,dF_{\widetilde{Z}}(z)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-e^{-x}\,\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\rho^{-1}\widetilde{Z}_{1}\right)\right\}=\exp\left\{-\frac{e^{-x}}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{-2}}}\right\}.

Here
F_{\widetilde{Z}}
denotes the law of
\widetilde{Z}_{1}
, where
\widetilde{Z}_{1}
is the product of two independent standard normal random variables. The last equality follows from the identity

\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\lambda Z_{1}Z_{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}},\qquad|\lambda|<1,

where
Z_{1},Z_{2}
are independent standard normal random variables, applied with
\lambda=\rho^{-1}
.

The preceding limiting distribution makes sense only when
\rho>1
. This is precisely the local regime of Theorem
2
, and a different approach is needed for the case
\rho\leq 1
. The same calculation also gives the scaling for the largest squared inner product: squaring doubles the edge fluctuation, so in the last display we replace
x
by
x/2
to obtain the limiting distribution of the largest squared inner product. We emphasize that this is only a heuristic point-process derivation and the rigorous proof below uses Poisson approximation rather than point-process convergence.

The point-process heuristic also suggests why the dependence structure must be handled carefully. In the Watson model, the perturbation involves products
Z_{i}Z_{j}
. Pairs such as
Z_{i}Z_{j}
and
Z_{i}Z_{k}
share the common factor
Z_{i}
, and this shared factor can increase the probability of simultaneous exceedances. This is why the correlation term
b_{2}
in the Chen–Stein bound requires a more delicate argument. In the rigorous proof, we handle this issue by removing a collection of rare events
\mathcal{A}_{ij}
; see Section
8.2
for details. The analogous derivation for the FvML model is almost identical, except that the product
Z_{1}Z_{2}
is replaced by
Z_{1}+Z_{2}
. Since
Z_{1}+Z_{2}
is exactly Gaussian, the FvML case is easier than the Watson case.

We next explain the scaling in the remaining regimes. The same heuristic can be used to identify the correct first-order scale for all values of
\rho
. We look for a constant
c
such that

n^{2}\,\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{p}\,S_{12}\geq c\sqrt{\log n}\right)\asymp 1,

where
S_{12}
is defined in (
7
). Since

\sqrt{p}\max_{i<j}W_{ij}

is of order
\sqrt{\log n}
, the product term
\sqrt{p}\,T_{1}T_{2}
must be put on the same scale. To do this, we make the change of variables

T_{i}=\alpha_{i}\left(\frac{\log n}{p}\right)^{1/4},\qquad i=1,2.

The purpose of this change of variables is that
\sqrt{p}\,T_{1}T_{2}
is then on the scale
\sqrt{\log n}
. A direct computation gives the following approximation for the density of
\alpha_{i}
:

g_{n}(\alpha_{i})\approx\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{n}\log n}{\pi}}\,\exp\left\{-\log n\left(\rho_{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{i}^{4}}{4}\right)\right\},

where
\rho_{n}
is defined in Theorem
3
.

Thus the exceedance probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{p}\,S_{12}\geq c\sqrt{\log n}\right)

is approximately

\displaystyle\iint\exp\left\{-\log n\left[\rho_{n}(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2})+\frac{\alpha_{1}^{4}+\alpha_{2}^{4}}{4}+\frac{(c-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})_{+}^{2}}{2}\right]\right\}\,d\alpha_{1}\,d\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\approx\exp\left\{-\log n\cdot\inf_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\left[\rho_{n}(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2})+\frac{\alpha_{1}^{4}+\alpha_{2}^{4}}{4}+\frac{(c-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})_{+}^{2}}{2}\right]\right\},

where the last approximation follows from Laplace’s method, and
x_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
. A direct calculation yields

\inf_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\left[\rho_{n}(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2})+\frac{\alpha_{1}^{4}+\alpha_{2}^{4}}{4}+\frac{(c-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})_{+}^{2}}{2}\right]=\begin{cases}\frac{c^{2}}{2},&\text{if }c\leq 2\rho_{n},\\[3.99994pt]
\frac{c^{2}}{4}+\rho_{n}c-\rho_{n}^{2},&\text{if }c>2\rho_{n}.\end{cases}

Suppose
\rho_{n}\to\rho
. To make

\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{p}\,S_{12}\geq c\sqrt{\log n}\right)

of order
\Theta(n^{-2})
, we need

c=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if }\rho\geq 1,\\[3.99994pt]
2\left[\sqrt{2(1+\rho^{2})}-\rho\right],&\text{if }\rho<1.\end{cases}

Thus the tail probability changes behavior depending on whether
\rho_{n}\to\rho<1
or
\rho_{n}\to\rho\geq 1
. This explains why the scaling changes between the local and non-local regimes in the Watson model.

The proof of asymptotic independence in the Poisson approximation, specifically the treatment of the term
b_{2}
in Lemma
5
, proceeds by truncating to small neighborhoods of the minimizers of the variational problem above. These neighborhoods contain the main contribution to the relevant joint exceedance probabilities.
The minimizers are different in the two regimes. When
\rho\geq 1
, there is a unique minimizer near
(0,0)
. When
\rho<1
, for the right-tail problem there are two minimizers near

\left(\sqrt{A(\rho)-2\rho},\sqrt{A(\rho)-2\rho}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left(-\sqrt{A(\rho)-2\rho},-\sqrt{A(\rho)-2\rho}\right),

where
A(\rho)
is defined in (
13
). The change in the saddle-point structure, together with the loss of uniform strong convexity near the critical regime, makes the proof in the non-local regime more involved than the proof in the local regime.

Proof of Proposition

Let

Note that

so the density of

By the Brascamp–Lieb inequality [see equation (1.9) in

This proves the second claim in (

We now prove the sub-Gaussian bound (

Hence the tilted law has density proportional to

Observe that

Therefore, another application of the Brascamp–Lieb inequality under the tilted law

Now define

A direct calculation yields

Moreover,

\psi(0)=0

and

\psi^{\prime}(0)=\mathbb{E}T-m=0.

Integrating the second-derivative bound twice gives, for all

For

\psi(\lambda)=\int_{\lambda}^{0}(s-\lambda)\psi^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds\leq\frac{1}{p-3}\int_{\lambda}^{0}(s-\lambda)\,ds=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(p-3)}.

Therefore, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

which is (

\displaystyle\exp\left[\frac{\kappa_{n}}{2\Delta_{n}}\cdot t^{2}-\frac{p-3}{2}\cdot\frac{t^{2}}{2\Delta_{n}}-\frac{p-3}{16\Delta_{n}^{2}}\cdot t^{4}+O\left(\frac{p}{\Delta_{n}^{3}}\right)\right]\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\left\{|t|\leq\sqrt{2\Delta_{n}}\right\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+o(1)\right]\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\left\{|t|\leq\sqrt{2\Delta_{n}}\right\}}

where the
o(1)

Moreover, we have the upper bound

\exp\left[\frac{\kappa_{n}}{p-2\kappa_{n}}\cdot t^{2}+\frac{p-3}{2}\cdot\log\left(1-\frac{t^{2}}{p-2\kappa_{n}}\right)\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{p-3-2\kappa_{n}}{2(p-2\kappa_{n})}\cdot t^{2}-\frac{p-3}{4\left(p-2\kappa_{n}\right)^{2}}\cdot t^{4}\right).

Therefore, the dominated convergence theorem gives (i).

Proof of (ii)
.
Observe that

. Moreover, by part (i),

where

are independent standard normal random variables. Hence

Suppose (
42
) has been proved. Then the preceding display, (
42
), and Vitali’s theorem give

\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\pm u_{n}\sqrt{p}\,\widetilde{T}_{1}\widetilde{T}_{2}\right\}\to\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\pm\rho^{-1}Z_{1}Z_{2}\right\}=\left(1-\rho^{-2}\right)^{-1/2}

which is (
41
).

Therefore, it suffices to prove
(
42
)
. First note that, for every
fixed
a<1/2
,

Indeed, this follows from the density bound in the proof of (i), since the
normalizing constants of the densities of

are bounded away from zero.

Now fix

Thus, for all sufficiently large

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{n\geq 1}\left\{\mathbb{E}\exp\left[\frac{(1+\delta)u_{n}\sqrt{p}}{2}\cdot\left(\widetilde{T}_{1}^{2}+\widetilde{T}_{2}^{2}\right)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{n\geq 1}\left[\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\frac{(1+\delta)u_{n}\sqrt{p}}{4\Delta_{n}}\cdot(p-2\kappa_{n})\cdot\widetilde{T}_{n}^{2}\right\}\right]^{2}<\infty.

uniformly for

(51)

for all sufficiently small

Combining this normalization with (
50
), we obtain the first claim. To finish the proof of the second claim, let us derive a lower bound on
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
.
Recall that

\mathcal{Z}_{n}=\int_{-1}^{1}\exp\left(\kappa_{n}z^{2}\right)\cdot(1-z^{2})^{(p-3)/2}\,dz.

Restricting the integral above to
|z|\leq(p\log n)^{-1/4}
and using the estimate
\log(1-y)\geq-y-y^{2}

for all sufficiently small
y\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}z^{2}+\frac{p-3}{2}\log(1-z^{2})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{p}{2}-\Delta_{n}\right)z^{2}-\frac{p-3}{2}(z^{2}+z^{4})

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{n}z^{2}+\frac{3}{2}z^{2}-\frac{p-3}{2}z^{4}.

Since
|z|\leq(p\log n)^{-1/4}
,

\Delta_{n}z^{2}\lesssim\sqrt{p\log n}\cdot(p\log n)^{-1/2}=O(1);\qquad pz^{4}\lesssim\frac{1}{\log n}=o(1).

Therefore, on the interval
|z|\leq(p\log n)^{-1/4}
, the exponent is
bounded below by a constant. Consequently,

\mathcal{Z}_{n}\geq c(p\log n)^{-1/4}

for some constant
c>0
. Together with (
51
), the preceding display yields (
49
). This completes the proof.

\hfill\square

We will also use the following uniform version of Laplace’s method. We first describe the setting. We are interested in the asymptotic behavior of the sequence of integrals

\int_{B(\bm{x}_{n},r)}h_{n}(\bm{x})\exp\{-L_{n}\cdot H_{n}(\bm{x})\}\,d\bm{x}.

Here
L=L_{n}
is a sequence such that
L_{n}\to\infty
, and
B(\bm{x}_{n},r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a sequence of open balls centered at
\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with fixed radius
r>0
. We assume that
H_{n}
is three times continuously differentiable on

B(\bm{x}_{n},r)
and
h_{n}
is positive on
B(\bm{x}_{n},r)
. Assume further that the following regularity conditions hold:

(i)

\nabla H_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})=0.

(ii)

The eigenvalues of
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}:=\nabla^{2}H_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})
are bounded away from
0
and uniformly bounded above, uniformly in
n
, and

H_{n}(\bm{x})\geq H_{n}\left(\bm{x}_{n}\right)+c\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{n}\|^{2},\qquad\bm{x}\in B\left(\bm{x}_{n},r\right)

for some fixed constants
c,r>0
.

(iii)

As
n\to\infty
,

\sup_{\|\bm{x}\|\leq\log L_{n}}\left|\frac{h_{n}(\bm{x}_{n}+\frac{\bm{x}}{\sqrt{L_{n}}})}{h_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})}-1\right|\to 0.

(iv)

For some constant
C>0
,

\sup_{\bm{x}\in B(\bm{x}_{n},r)}\frac{h_{n}(\bm{x})}{h_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})}\leq L_{n}^{C}

and

\sup_{\|\bm{y}\|\leq\log L_{n}}\left|L_{n}\left[H_{n}\left(\bm{x}_{n}+\frac{\bm{y}}{\sqrt{L_{n}}}\right)-H_{n}\left(\bm{x}_{n}\right)\right]-\frac{\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\bm{y}}{2}\right|\to 0.

Lemma 2
.

If conditions
(i)-(iv)
above hold, then

\displaystyle\int_{B(\bm{x}_{n},r)}h_{n}(\bm{x})\exp\Big[-L_{n}\cdot H_{n}(\bm{x})\Big]\,d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{L_{n}}\cdot\frac{h_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})}{\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{n})^{1/2}}\cdot\exp\Big[-L_{n}\cdot H_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})\Big]\cdot\left(1+o(1)\right)

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Write
m_{n}:=H_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})
and
R_{n}:=\log L_{n}
. Since
R_{n}/\sqrt{L_{n}}\to 0
, the ball
B(\bm{x}_{n},R_{n}/\sqrt{L_{n}})
is contained in
B(\bm{x}_{n},r)
for all sufficiently large
n
.
By conditions (iii) and (iv), uniformly for
\|\bm{y}\|\leq R_{n}
,

\displaystyle h_{n}\left(\bm{x}_{n}+\frac{\bm{y}}{\sqrt{L_{n}}}\right)\exp\left\{-L_{n}\left[H_{n}\left(\bm{x}_{n}+\frac{\bm{y}}{\sqrt{L_{n}}}\right)-m_{n}\right]\right\}=h_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\bm{y}\right\}\{1+o(1)\}.

Using the change of variables

\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{n}+\frac{\bm{y}}{\sqrt{L_{n}}},\qquad d\bm{x}=L_{n}^{-1}\,d\bm{y},

we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{n}\|\leq R_{n}/\sqrt{L_{n}}}h_{n}(\bm{x})\cdot e^{-L_{n}H_{n}(\bm{x})}\,d\bm{x}=\frac{h_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})e^{-L_{n}m_{n}}}{L_{n}}\cdot\int_{\|\bm{y}\|\leq R_{n}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\bm{y}\right\}\,d\bm{y}\cdot\{1+o(1)\}.

By condition (ii),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\pi}{\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{n})^{1/2}}-\int_{\|\bm{y}\|\leq R_{n}}e^{-\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\bm{y}/2}\,d\bm{y}\leq\int_{\|\bm{y}\|>R_{n}}\exp\left\{-\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\right)\cdot\frac{\|\bm{y}\|^{2}}{2}\right\}\,d\bm{y}=o(1).

Since
\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{n})
is uniformly bounded above and bounded
away from zero,

\int_{\|\bm{y}\|\leq R_{n}}e^{-\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\bm{y}/2}\,d\bm{y}=\frac{2\pi}{\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{n})^{1/2}}\{1+o(1)\}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{n}\|\leq R_{n}/\sqrt{L_{n}}}h_{n}(\bm{x})e^{-L_{n}H_{n}(\bm{x})}\,d\bm{x}=\frac{2\pi}{L_{n}}\cdot\frac{h_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})}{\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{n})^{1/2}}e^{-L_{n}m_{n}}\cdot\{1+o(1)\}.

It remains to control the annular region. By conditions (ii) and (iv),

\displaystyle\int_{\{R_{n}/\sqrt{L_{n}}<\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{n}\|<r\}}h_{n}(\bm{x})\cdot e^{-L_{n}H_{n}(\bm{x})}\,d\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leq h_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})\cdot L_{n}^{C}\cdot e^{-L_{n}m_{n}}\cdot\int_{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{n}\|>R_{n}/\sqrt{L_{n}}}e^{-cL_{n}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{n}\|^{2}}\,d\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})e^{-L_{n}m_{n}}}{L_{n}}\cdot L_{n}^{C}\cdot\int_{\|\bm{y}\|>R_{n}}e^{-c\|\bm{y}\|^{2}}\,d\bm{y}.

Since
R_{n}=\log L_{n}
,

L_{n}^{C}\cdot\int_{\|\bm{y}\|>\log L_{n}}e^{-c\|\bm{y}\|^{2}}\,d\bm{y}=o(1).

Hence the annular contribution is

o\left(\frac{h_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})e^{-L_{n}m_{n}}}{L_{n}}\right),

This completes the proof.

\hfill\square

Proposition 6

We start with a useful tail bound.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose
d\sim p
and
p/(\log n)^{2}\to\infty

•

With

as in

•

Suppose

for some constant

.

72
) follows directly from the standard tail asymptotics of the beta distribution. Given (
72
), (
73
) follows by applying (
72

\Psi_{d}(u_{n}-a)
and
\Psi(u_{n})

74
) and (
75

Lemma 4
.

Let
R_{m}
denote the first coordinate of a uniform point on

\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, and put
M_{m}(a):=\mathbb{E}e^{aR_{m}}
. Uniformly for

|a|=o(m)
,

\log M_{m}(a)=\frac{a^{2}}{2m}+O\left(\frac{a^{4}}{m^{3}}\right).

(76)

Proof of Lemma
4
.

We use the exact moments of a uniform spherical coordinate. By equation (9.6.3) in
Mardia and Jupp [2000]
, we have

\mathbb{E}U_{1}^{2k}=\frac{(1/2)_{k}}{(m/2)_{k}}=\frac{(2k-1)!!}{m(m+2)\cdots(m+2k-2)}

with the convention that the empty product equals
1
.

The monotone convergence theorem then yields

\displaystyle M_{m}(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{2k}}{(2k)!}\cdot\mathbb{E}U_{1}^{2k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{2k}}{(2k)!}\cdot\frac{(2k-1)!!}{m(m+2)\cdots(m+2k-2)}.

Since

(2k-1)!!=\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!},

we get

M_{m}(a)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{2m}\right)^{k}\cdot\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left(1+\frac{2\ell}{m}\right)^{-1}.

Put

\theta:=\frac{a^{2}}{2m},\qquad w_{k,m}:=\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left(1+\frac{2\ell}{m}\right)^{-1}.

Then

M_{m}(a)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{k}}{k!}\cdot w_{k,m}
.
Let
N\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\theta)
and note that, since

\mathbb{P}(N=k)=e^{-\theta}\cdot\theta^{k}/k!
, we may rewrite the preceding display as

M_{m}(a)=e^{\theta}\cdot\mathbb{E}w_{N,m}.

Now define

w_{k,m}=e^{-S_{k,m}},\qquad S_{k,m}:=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\log\left(1+\frac{2\ell}{m}\right).

Using the bounds
\log(1+x)\geq 0
and
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x\geq 0
, we have

0\leq S_{k,m}\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{2\ell}{m}=\frac{k(k-1)}{m}.

Consequently,

0\leq S_{N,m}\leq\frac{N(N-1)}{m}.

On the other hand, Jensen’s inequality gives

\log\mathbb{E}e^{-S_{N,m}}\geq-\mathbb{E}S_{N,m}.

Combining the two bounds above with
\mathbb{E}[N(N-1)]=\theta^{2}
, we obtain

\frac{-\theta^{2}}{m}=\frac{-\mathbb{E}\left[N(N-1)\right]}{m}\leq-\mathbb{E}S_{N,m}\leq\log\mathbb{E}e^{-S_{N,m}}\leq 0.

Consequently,

\log M_{m}(a)=\theta+O\left(\frac{\theta^{2}}{m}\right)=\frac{a^{2}}{2m}+O\left(\frac{a^{4}}{m^{3}}\right).

This completes the proof.
\square

The following Poisson approximation result will be used throughout the paper. It gives a total variation bound between a sum of Bernoulli random variables and a Poisson distribution with the same mean. The bound is expressed in terms of the dependency graph of the Bernoulli variables. Readers are referred to the paper
Arratia et al. [1989]
for a proof.

Lemma 5

(
Arratia et al. [1989]
)
.

Let
\{I_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}
be Bernoulli variables with a
dependency graph:
I_{\alpha}
is independent of

\{I_{\beta}:\beta\notin B_{\alpha}\}
. Let

W=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}I_{\alpha}
and
\lambda=\mathbb{E}W
. Define

b_{1}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{\beta\in B_{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}I_{\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}I_{\beta},\qquad b_{2}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in B_{\alpha}\\
\beta\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}(I_{\alpha}I_{\beta}).

Then

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{L}(W),\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)\right)\leq\min(1,\lambda^{-1})(b_{1}+b_{2}).

Lemma 6
.

There exists a universal constant
c>0
such that

\inf_{\varepsilon\in\left[0,\frac{1}{10}\right],q\geq 0}\left\{\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)\left[\sqrt{2}q+\frac{q^{2}}{2\sqrt{2}}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\left(2-q-\varepsilon\right)_{+}^{2}\right\}>\frac{3+c}{2}.

Proof of Lemma
6
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in[0,1/10]
. Consider the case
q\geq 2-\varepsilon
. Then

\displaystyle\inf_{q\geq 0}\left\{\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)\left[\sqrt{2}q+\frac{q^{2}}{2\sqrt{2}}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\left(2-q-\varepsilon\right)_{+}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{q\geq 2-\varepsilon}\left\{\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)\left[\sqrt{2}q+\frac{q^{2}}{2\sqrt{2}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)\cdot\left[\sqrt{2}q+\frac{q^{2}}{2\sqrt{2}}\right]\Bigg|_{q=2-\varepsilon}>3/2

for all
\varepsilon<=1/10
.

If

Recall that a function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mu
-strongly convex if
F(\bm{x})-\frac{\mu}{2}\cdot\|\bm{x}\|^{2}
is a convex function. The following elementary bound is used repeatedly in the proofs.

Lemma 7
.

Suppose
G:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function such that there exists a nonnegative,
\mu
-strongly convex function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following condition:

G\left(\bm{x}\right)\geq\max\left\{a,F(\bm{x})\right\},\qquad\text{for all $\bm{x}\in\Omega$}.

Assume that this condition holds for some constant
a>0
. Then, for any
\lambda>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\int_{\Omega}\exp\left[-\lambda\cdot G\left(\bm{x}\right)\right]\,d\bm{x}\leq\exp\left[-\lambda(1-\varepsilon)a\right]\cdot\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda\varepsilon\mu}\right)^{d/2}.

Proof of Lemma
7
.

Let
\bm{x}_{\rm min}
be the unique global minimizer of
F
. By strong convexity and the nonnegativity of
F
, for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

F\left(\bm{x}\right)\geq\left(\mu/2\right)\cdot\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\rm min}\|^{2}.

Note that
G(\bm{x})\geq(1-\varepsilon)a+\varepsilon F(\bm{x})
. Therefore,

\int_{\Omega}\exp\left[-\lambda\cdot G\left(\bm{x}\right)\right]\,d\bm{x}\leq e^{-\lambda(1-\varepsilon)a}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left\{\frac{-\lambda\varepsilon\mu}{2}\cdot\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\rm min}\|^{2}\right\}\,d\bm{x}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda\varepsilon\mu}\right)^{d/2}e^{-\lambda(1-\varepsilon)a}.\qquad

\square
