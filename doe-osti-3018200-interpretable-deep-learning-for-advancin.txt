 Executive Summary
The goal of this Early Career project is to advance the in-depth understandings on the
field-dipole effects and promote electrostatic catalysis by developing an interpretable,
physics-informed deep active learning approach (Figure 1).
The hypotheses of this project: (1) The local electric fields will be strengthened by greater
than 10-fold at the points with low coordination number; (2) The dipole moments of
adsorption systems are linearly correlated to the partial charge of chemical species and
their surface coverages; (3) The field-dipole interactions will remarkably alter the
mechanism, lower the energetics and consequently, enhance the overall rates of
ammonia cracking and synthesis.
To achieve the overall goal and access the hypotheses of this Project, PI Che focuses on
a study case of carbon-neutral hydrogen production and utilization from ammonia
cracking and synthesis over earth-abundant, non-critical metal catalysts. In this project,
PI Che group integrates their expertise in quantum chemistry, field-enhanced chemistry,
multi-scale kinetic modeling, and machine learning, with three key pillars of research
innovation:
Aim 1: Probe the local electric fields of metallic nanoparticles under working conditions.
Aim 2: Predict field-driven adsorption energetics with deep learning.

Aim 3: Discover the optimal field-dipole effects on catalysis actively.
Developing advanced theory, modeling, and data science approaches for understanding
field-enhanced catalysis has extensive applications in renewable energy technologies,
particularly for plasma catalysis, electrostatic catalysis, zeolite catalysis, interfacial
electrocatalysis, programmed catalysis, and fuel cells. The research goals and activities
are in resonant with the central component of the DOE Catalysis Science Program: (1)
elucidating catalytic reaction mechanisms in diverse chemical environments and the
structure-reactivity relationships of solid and molecular catalysts, (2) promoting carbon-
neutral H2 production and utilization, (3) investigating thermal or electro-catalysis
mediated by earth-abundant, non-critical metals, and (4) developing advanced theory,
modeling, data-science and machine learning approaches to mechanism identification,
catalyst discovery. The knowledge and open-source tools derived from the project will
advance the basic understanding of structure-reactivity relationships of heterogeneous
catalysts under electric fields for energy applications. The integration of ab-initio modeling
and machine learning algorithms offers exceptional possibilities to facilitate non-critical
materials development and create the opportunity to enter a new paradigm in catalyst
design based on fundamentals and materials data rather than trial-and-error.

Aim 1: Probe the local electric fields of metallic nanoparticles under working
conditions.
Major Activity 1 – Developing and Validating Local Electric Field (LEF) Mapping. We
developed and validated methods for mapping Local Electric Fields (LEFs) on Ni surfaces
and nanoparticles (NPs) using two complementary techniques: Vibrational Stark Effect
(VSE) with adsorbed CO as a probe and the electrostatic Potential Difference (PD)
approach (Figure 2a-c). A strong linear relationship between PD- and VSE-derived LEFs
(Figures 2d, 2e) was established using a 790-point DFT database. The corrected LEF
distribution on the Ni(100) x-y plane (Figure 2f) confirmed that at 0.5 V/Å EEF, the LEF
at the top site was 0.51 V/Å, validating our assumptions. This approach accurately maps
site-specific LEFs on Ni surfaces and NPs with efficiency comparable to direct VSE
calculations.

Major Activity 2 – Impact of Nanoparticle (NP) Geometry on LEFs. Figure 3 illustrates
how NP size and generalized coordination number (GCN) influence LEFs under a 0.5 V/Å
EEF. While Ni(111) shows an LEF of ~0.45 V/Å at top sites (GCN = 7.5), the Ni 43 cluster
(GCN = 3.08) reaches ~1.7 V/Å at its tip—four times higher than the flat surface. This
trend indicates that as NP size (and GCN) increases, LEF decreases, primarily due to
charge distribution differences. In slabs, GCN and LEFs are strongly correlated, but in
clusters, GCN alone does not fully capture LEF variations (Figure 3a-e). Principal
Component Analysis (PCA, Figure 3f) further highlights how corners and smaller clusters
concentrate LEFs, emphasizing the importance of structural features in catalyst design
for field-enhanced reactions.

Aim 2: Predict field-driven adsorption energetics with deep learning.

Major Activity 3 – Local Electric Field Prediction and Adsorption Energy Modelling. To
efficiently predict adsorption energy shifts under EEFs, we developed a streamlined
machine learning (ML) workflow (Figure 4a) with three key steps:

1. ML estimates LEFs using NP size, GCN, and EEF as inputs. ML-predicted LEFs closely
match DFT values (MAE ~0.03 V/Å) while reducing computational cost by over four orders
of magnitude (Figure 4b).

2. A data-driven ML model predicts CO adsorption energies under varying EEFs using
GCN, NP size, and zero-field adsorption energies as inputs (Figure 4c). While effective
(MAE ~0.03 eV), this method requires a large training set across multiple EEFs.

3. To improve accuracy and reduce data demands, we incorporated a Taylor expansion
physics rule as an additional feature. This physics-guided approach achieves lower MAEs
(~0.0081 eV, Figure 4d) with fewer training samples and strong transferability across
metals and adsorbates (e.g., CH, Figure 4e).

Major Activity 4 – Developing CN-based Group Additivity Framework for Predicting the
NH3 Decomposition Species on the Metal Cluster. We used NH₃ decomposition over
Ru45 cluster as a case study and have developed a framework based on coordination
number (CN) of the active site and group additivity (GA) to predict the correlations
between catalytic activity, cluster sites, and EEF. Figure 5a demonstrates the workflow
of the framework which includes database generation, GA model development, and
predictions. Figure 5b illustrates the key concepts in the CN-based GA approach
development for metal clusters: i) each exposed metal atom is a Group; ii) the Group is
defined by the CN of itself and surrounding first-nearest neighbors; iii) the Groups are
applied to describe the surface sites; and iv) adsorption energy is the sum of contributions
from all Groups. Our results in Figure 5c show that the CN-GA framework accurately
predicts NH₃ physisorption with an average MAE of 0.03 eV, from 100 iterations of 5-fold
cross-validation. This method can also be extended to predict species-cluster interactions
under EEFs via the physics-enhanced ML techniques, described in Major Activity 3. More
importantly, electron accumulation at edge sites strengthens nitrogen adsorption (subplot
in Figure 5d), underscoring the crucial role of electrostatic effects in altering species-site
interaction strength and, consequently, impacting catalytic reactions.
Aim 3: Discover the optimal field-dipole effects on catalysis actively.

Major Activity 5 – DFT Calculations and Microkinetic Modelling of Ammonia Cracking.
Before evaluating electric field effects on ammonia cracking, we performed DFT
calculations without EEFs. Results (Figure 6) confirm that the Brønsted-Evans-Polanyi
(BEP) relationship holds across all ammonia-cracking steps, demonstrating a linear
correlation between reaction energies and activation barriers. We also developed a
microkinetic model (MKM) for ammonia decomposition under standard conditions. MKM
calculations at 400 °C and 1 atm (Figure 7a) revealed a volcano-type correlation between
turnover frequency (TOF) and nitrogen adsorption energy (E N). Ru exhibited the highest
TOF (2.13 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹), aligning with experiments (Figure 7b-c), confirming EN as a key
activity descriptor.

Major Activity 6 – Multi-Scale Simulation for Predicting Field-Dipole Effects on Ammonia
Cracking over Ru. We then integrated the DFT and MKM to assess the performance of
Ru under various EEFs and temperature. Our results reveal that Ru(1013) exhibits
superior catalytic activity compared to Ru(0001), due to the presence of active low-
coordinated B5 sites (Figure 8a-b). A negative electric field enhances electron density at
these B5 sites, thereby reducing the activation barriers for NH dehydrogenation (Figure
8c-d), which is the rate-limiting step in ammonia cracking over Ru(1013) (Figure 8e). This
enhancement facilitates a reduction in operating temperature from 750 K (0 V/Å) to 586
K (-1 V/Å), while maintaining TOF of 5 s⁻¹. Furthermore, within the temperature range of
673 K to 823 K, the negative electric field increases the TOF by four orders of magnitude
relative to zero-field conditions (Figure 8f). This multi-scale modeling approach provides
a deeper understanding of field-enhanced catalysis, offering crucial insights for the
development of more efficient hydrogen production via onboard ammonia cracking.
Major Activity 7 – Interpretable ML-Guided Discovery of Optimal Earth-Abundant, Non-
Critical Catalysts for Ammonia Cracking. Leveraging our MKM findings, we employed an

interpretable machine learning approach (Figures 9a) to identify optimal bimetallic
catalysts for ammonia decomposition. Using a dataset comprising over 3,300 bimetallic
surfaces (Figures 9b), we trained machine learning models (Figures 9c) to predict EN
values with high accuracy (mean absolute error ~0.16 eV) (Figures 9d-f). The screening
process identified several earth-abundant alloys, including Fe3Cu, Ni3Mo, Ni7Cu, and
Fe15Ni, as promising candidates for ammonia decomposition. SHapley Additive
exPlanations (SHAP) analysis (Figure 9e) revealed that d-band filling is the most
significant feature influencing EN predictions. This interpretable framework enables
rational catalyst selection based on fundamental electronic structure descriptors,
minimizing reliance on high-throughput DFT calculations.

Major Activity 8 - Inverse Design of Bimetallic Alloys for Ammonia Cracking. We
developed an inverse design approach combining a diffusion model with predictive ML to
discover earth-abundant bimetallic alloys for ammonia cracking (Figure 10a).
Starting with a bimetallic alloy database of 2,000+ species, we used DFT simulations to
compute key surface properties, including d-state information, and EN. To accelerate
discovery, we trained an ML model to predict EN based on composition and site properties,
achieving 0.16 eV accuracy (Figure 10b). The generative diffusion model introduces
Gaussian noise into the alloy database and refines new alloys targeting an optimal EN of
-0.9 eV. Physics-based constraints ensure validity, while the ML model guides the
diffusion model by rewarding alloys with improved catalytic performance (Figures 10c-
d). This inverse design framework successfully identified promising bimetallic alloys,
which will undergo further DFT validation and experimental testing. The workflow provides
a computationally efficient approach to discovering high-performance catalysts for
targeted reactions.
 Overall Conclusions

This project demonstrates that electric field–dipole interactions provide a powerful and
tunable lever for controlling catalytic activity. By combining DFT, microkinetic modeling,
and interpretable machine learning, we established predictive relationships between
catalyst structure, local electric fields, adsorption energetics, and reaction rates.
The findings confirm that low-coordination sites amplify electric fields, that field-modulated
adsorption energetics govern reaction kinetics, and that physics-informed ML can
efficiently guide catalyst discovery. Together, these advances lay the scientific foundation
for a new, data-driven paradigm in catalyst design based on fundamental electronic
structure principles rather than empirical trial-and-error approaches.
