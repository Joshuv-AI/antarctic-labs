Adaptively Optimized Combination of Phased‐Array MR Spectroscopy Data Without Noise Correlation Estimation: A Joint Diagonalization Method

Liang Fang, Minjie Wu, Howard J Aizenstein, Anand Kumar, Shaolin Yang

Abstract

The adaptively optimized combination (AOC) method optimally combines multichannel magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) data by incorporating noise correlations among receive coil elements/channels. These correlations are typically estimated from noise samples extracted from the fully decayed tails of acquired free induction decays (FIDs). To eliminate the need for FID noise tails and the corresponding interchannel noise correlation estimation, we propose a novel coil‐combination method, termed AOC‐JD, that exploits the intrinsic multichannel noise information contained in the differences between individual repetitions and the repetition‐averaged spectroscopic data. The proposed method is based on joint diagonalization (JD) of two correlation matrices constructed from the individual repetitions and the repetition‐averaged multichannel MRS data, thereby circumventing the conventional interchannel noise correlation estimation procedure. The performance of AOC‐JD was evaluated using both computer simulations and in vivo human brain experiments. The results demonstrate that, when multichannel FIDs do not contain valid or sufficiently long noise tails, AOC‐JD significantly improves the signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) of the combined spectra compared with the conventional singular value decomposition (SVD) method. Moreover, its performance is comparable to that of the original AOC method, which is optimized to maximize the SNR of the combined spectrum, when applicable. These findings demonstrate that AOC‐JD provides a theoretically grounded and reliable approach for combining multichannel MRS data when FID noise tails are unavailable or insufficient.
A novel method for adaptively optimized combination (AOC) of multichannel magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) data was developed that requires neither noise samples nor estimation of noise correlations across coil elements/channels. The method employs joint diagonalization (JD) of two correlation matrices: one derived from the individual repetitions of the multichannel spectroscopic data and the other from the multichannel spectroscopic data averaged across repetitions. This proposed AOC‐JD method achieves SNR improvement comparable to that of the original AOC method and significantly outperforms the conventional SVD method.

INTRODUCTION

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) provides a noninvasive means to probe in vivo metabolism through quantification of endogenous metabolite concentrations [, , ]. However, the inherently low MR sensitivity, combined with low metabolite concentrations, results in a relatively low signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR). This low SNR represents a significant limitation for the clinical applications of MRS []. Advances in MRI hardware, including multichannel phased‐array receive coils, have enabled phased‐array MRS to achieve higher SNR through optimized coil‐combination methods [, , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ].

Methods for constructive combination of multichannel MR spectroscopic data differ primarily in the weighting coefficients assigned to individual coil elements/channels and can be broadly classified into two categories. One category determines the weighting coefficients of coil elements/channels without accounting for multichannel noise characteristics [, , , , ]. The singular value decomposition (SVD) method [], a representative method in this category, decomposes a matrix constructed from the received multichannel spectroscopic data to extract the principal component, which serves as the weighting coefficients of coil elements/channels. The other category uses noise samples from the received multichannel spectroscopic data to estimate the weighting coefficients of coil elements/channels [, , , , , , , , , , , ]. Note that in 1H MRS, noise samples are often obtained from the later, decayed portion of the received free induction decays (FIDs). Selecting noise samples from the spectral domain can be problematic because the non‐flat spectral baseline induced by different sources such as macromolecules, lipids, and residual water signal can bias noise samples in the spectral domain. Within this category, the adaptively optimized combination (AOC) method [], developed by our group, incorporates the inverse of the full noise correlation matrix across coil elements/channels into the weighting coefficients. It effectively suppresses nearly all types of noise, thereby improving the SNR of the combined spectrum in an adaptive and robust manner. The AOC method has been shown to outperform other existing methods [, , , , , , , , , , , , , ] in terms of SNR enhancement and preserving the combined spectrum from distortion [, ]. However, the AOC method requires sufficient noise samples to accurately estimate the noise correlation among coil elements/channels. This requirement may not be met when noise samples are unavailable or insufficient in the acquired multichannel spectroscopic data. For example, in ultrashort TR and TE 1H FID magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (FID‐MRSI) [, ], the readout duration is significantly reduced by decreasing the number of sampling points and using a shorter dwell time. As a result, the later, decayed portion of the FID, where the noise samples are typically obtained, may not be acquired at all or may be sampled insufficiently. Moreover, noise samples obtained from a separate prescan may not accurately reflect the noise characteristics of the subsequently acquired multichannel spectroscopic data, because multichannel noise may be nonstationary, owing to factors such as subject motion and changes in coil loading []. Under these circumstances, the AOC method, which relies on noise correlation estimation, is not feasible for achieving an optimal coil combination. In contrast, the SVD method, which does not utilize noise samples, is commonly applied but results in a suboptimal combination of multichannel spectroscopic data []. This raises an important question: Is there a universally applicable coil‐combination method that remains optimal even when noise samples are unavailable or insufficient in the acquired multichannel spectroscopic data?

It has been shown that incorporating multichannel noise characteristics into the weighting coefficients of coil elements/channels is critical for achieving optimal coil combination [, ]. In the absence of explicit or pure noise samples that are typically obtained from the later, decayed portion of received FIDs in 1H MRS, multichannel noise can alternatively be estimated from the noise components inherently embedded in the acquired multichannel spectroscopic data. In routine MRS applications, an ensemble of multichannel spectroscopic data is typically acquired through repeated measurements []. Spectroscopic data from individual coil elements/channels are often averaged across these repetitions prior to coil combination, yielding averaged multichannel spectroscopic data with improved SNR. This SNR improvement arises because metabolite resonance signal adds coherently across repetitions, whereas noise tends to add incoherently and cancel out. Accordingly, compared with the raw multichannel spectroscopic data from individual repetitions, the averaged data across these repetitions preserve the coherent signal component while reducing noise variance in inverse proportion to the number of repetitions []. As a result, the difference between the raw and averaged multichannel spectroscopic data contains valuable information about the underlying multichannel noise. However, this noise information has largely been overlooked in MRS, as coil combination is typically performed using only the averaged multichannel spectroscopic data [, , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ]. To fully exploit the underlying multichannel noise for optimal coil combination, it is therefore necessary to use both the raw multichannel spectroscopic data across the repetitions and the corresponding averaged multichannel spectroscopic data.

In the present study, we developed a novel method for adaptively optimized combination of multichannel MRS data that does not require noise correlation estimation. This method employs joint diagonalization (JD) of two correlation matrices: one calculated from the raw multichannel spectroscopic data from individual repetitions and the other from the corresponding multichannel spectroscopic data averaged across repetitions. The performance of the proposed method was evaluated using computer simulations and in vivo human brain experiments conducted on a 3 T MRI scanner with an 8‐channel phased‐array head coil. Compared with the conventional SVD method [], the proposed method yielded significantly higher SNR in the combined spectrum. Furthermore, compared with the original AOC method [], it achieved comparable SNR enhancement without requiring noise correlation estimation.

Simulation‐Based Evaluation of AOC‐JD

A high‐SNR FID with a relatively long decayed tail (hereafter referred to as the “reference spectroscopic data”) was acquired from a voxel encompassing the rostral anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) of a participant (see the in vivo spectroscopic data acquisition in the following subsection). The reference spectroscopic data were used to generate a set of simulated 8‐channel spectroscopic data. Each 8‐channel dataset, comprising 128 repetitions, was synthesized by scaling and phase‐shifting the reference spectroscopic data according to predefined amplitudes and phases assigned to the individual coil elements/channels, followed by repeated addition of simulated stochastic multichannel noise. The simulated multichannel noise matrix was modeled as

where A is an M×M matrix whose nonzero off‐diagonal elements represent the noise correlation between coil elements/channels, B is an M×M diagonal matrix whose diagonal elements represent the simulated noise variances, and N~ is an M×P matrix containing M uncorrelated, identically distributed Gaussian noises, each consisting of P sampled points. Two multichannel noise scenarios were simulated: (1) uncorrelated noise with identical variances across coil elements/channels (A=0,B=IM), and (2) correlated noise with unequal variances across coil elements/channels (A≠0,B≠IM). Accordingly, two 8‐channel spectroscopic datasets with distinct noise characteristics were generated. Each dataset was then combined using three different methods: the conventional SVD method [], the AOC method [], and the proposed AOC‐JD method. For the SVD and AOC methods, the weighting coefficients of coil elements/channels were estimated solely from the multichannel spectroscopic data averaged across repetitions. In contrast, the proposed AOC‐JD method estimated the weighting coefficients using both the raw individual repetitions of multichannel spectroscopic data and the multichannel spectroscopic data averaged across repetitions.

A third 8‐channel spectroscopic dataset, comprising 128 repetitions and correlated noise with unequal variances across coil elements/channels, was synthesized using a resampled version of the reference spectroscopic data with a substantially shorter decay tail. The resampled reference spectroscopic data were generated by decimating the original reference spectroscopic data by a factor of 2 and retaining the first 512 complex data points. This configuration mimics spectroscopic data acquired with an echo‐planar readout in echo‐planar spectroscopic imaging (EPSI) [, ], where the spectral bandwidth and the number of readout gradient lobes are limited. The synthesized dataset was then combined using the conventional SVD method [] and the proposed AOC‐JD method. The AOC method [] was not applicable in this scenario because accurate estimation of noise correlation between coil elements/channels requires a sufficient number of noise samples from the long FID decay tail, which were unavailable in the shortened dataset.

Evaluation of AOC‐JD in Human Brain Experiments

Human brain experiments were conducted in 12 healthy participants to evaluate the performance of the proposed AOC‐JD method. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants under a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board. 1H‐MRS scans were performed on a Philips Achieva 3 T MRI scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Best, the Netherlands) using an 8‐channel phased‐array head coil (SENSE‐Head‐8). Single‐voxel spectra were acquired using a PRESS sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 3000/35 ms, 2048 complex data points, spectral bandwidth = 2000 Hz, 128 signal averages with 16‐step phase cycling, and an unsuppressed water acquisition at the beginning for signal scaling and eddy‐current correction. Spectroscopic voxels were placed in the rostral ACC (2 × 2 × 2 cm3), the left and right dorsolateral frontal white matter (FWM; 2 × 1 × 2 cm3), and a subcortical region encompassing the head of the left caudate nucleus (Caud; 1 × 2 × 2 cm3) in each participant (see Figure ).

The raw multichannel in vivo spectroscopic data were processed offline in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). The data were combined using the conventional SVD method [], the original AOC method [], and the proposed AOC‐JD method. The performance of each coil‐combination method was subsequently evaluated using the SNR metric described above.

Statistical analyses were performed on the MRS data acquired from the 12 participants. For each participant, differences in SNR among the three methods (SVD, AOC, and AOC‐JD) were calculated within participant for each region (ACC, left FWM, right FWM, and left Caud). Summary statistics are presented as mean, standard deviation (SD), and median for each method and region. Pairwise comparisons between methods (AOC vs. SVD, AOC‐JD vs. SVD, and AOC‐JD vs. AOC) were performed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, and the corresponding percentage increase in SNR from one method to the other method and the p‐values were reported for each region.

Discussion

We proposed a novel method for adaptively optimized combination of multichannel MR spectroscopic data acquired with a phased‐array coil that does not require explicit estimation of noise correlations between coil elements/channels. The proposed AOC‐JD method employs joint diagonalization of two correlation matrices: one derived from the individual repetitions of multichannel spectroscopic data and the other from the spectroscopic data averaged across repetitions. Compared with the original AOC method [] and the conventional SVD method [], the proposed AOC‐JD method achieved performance comparable to that of the AOC method and significantly outperformed the SVD method in enhancing the SNR of the combined spectrum.

The proposed AOC‐JD method is formulated by solving an optimization problem that maximizes the SNR of the combined spectrum, as defined in Equation (), consistent with the optimization strategy underlying the original AOC method []. This theoretical consistency explains why the AOC‐JD method achieved SNR performance comparable to the AOC method, as demonstrated in Figures , , and .

In the AOC method, the optimal weighting coefficients for coil elements/channels are directly derived from Equation () using estimates of coil sensitivities, spatial phase shifts, and noise correlations between coil elements/channels. In contrast, the proposed AOC‐JD method determines the optimal weighting coefficients through joint diagonalization of two correlation matrices: one calculated from the individual repetitions of multichannel spectroscopic data and the other from the multichannel spectroscopic data averaged across repetitions, as shown in Equations () and (). A key assumption underlying the AOC‐JD method is that averaging preserves the spatial correlation structures of both the noise and the metabolite resonance signal, as described in Equations () and (). In practical MRS acquisitions, this assumption is reasonable when the spatial correlation structures of the multichannel noise and metabolite resonance signal remain stationary across repetitions, as demonstrated in Figures and .

Based on the established relationship between joint diagonalization and generalized eigendecomposition (see Appendix ), the AOC‐JD method can be implemented as generalized eigendecomposition of the two correlation matrices (Equation ()). This formulation can be interpreted as a form of principal component analysis [] designed to maximize the power ratio of the multichannel spectroscopic data averaged across repetitions to the individual repetitions of multichannel spectroscopic data.

In clinical MRS application, in vivo spectroscopic data are typically acquired with multiple repetitions (e.g., 32, 64, 128, or 256) to compensate for the inherently low SNR. The differences between the individual repetitions and the averaged multichannel spectroscopic data contains valuable information about the underlying multichannel noise. The AOC‐JD method explicitly leverages this information to achieve optimal coil combination. Consequently, it does not require explicit noise samples or knowledge of the noise correlations between coil elements/channels. The proposed AOC‐JD method therefore overcomes a key limitation of the original AOC method, enabling optimized combination of multichannel MRS data even when noise samples are unavailable or insufficient.

This scenario is particularly relevant to fast spectroscopic imaging approaches such as FID‐MRSI, in which the acquired FID is short and the number of repetitions may also be limited. Although single‐voxel MR spectroscopy is typically performed with longer TRs and may therefore permit longer FID acquisitions, obtaining sufficiently clean noise‐only samples or accurately estimating the noise correlation matrix across coil elements/channels can still be challenging in practice. This is especially true when the FID or portions of the spectrum are contaminated by residual water or lipid signals, eddy‐current effects, out‐of‐voxel echoes, baseline distortions, or other artifacts. In addition, physiological and instrumental factors, including subject motion, respiration, pulsation, and scanner instabilities such as fluctuations in receiver gain or coil loading during acquisition, may further complicate noise correlation estimation. Under these conditions, the AOC‐JD method may offer a practical advantage by reducing reliance on explicit noise estimation and thereby limiting the influence of these confounding factors. However, the specific performance and underlying behavior of the method under these different conditions remain to be elucidated through further investigation, as the present study primarily focused on establishing the concept and mathematical foundation of the AOC‐JD method.

Simulation studies demonstrated that the AOC‐JD method consistently achieved SNR performance comparable to that of the original AOC method across different multichannel noise scenarios while outperforming the conventional SVD method, particularly in the presence of correlated noise with unequal variances across coil elements/channels (Figure ). Importantly, when noise samples were unavailable or insufficient, as illustrated in Figure , the AOC method became infeasible because of its reliance on accurate estimation of noise correlations. In contrast, the AOC‐JD method maintained robust performance under correlated noise with unequal variances across coil elements/channels by exploiting intrinsic information about multichannel noise contained in the repeated acquisitions, resulting in improved SNR under these challenging conditions.

In addition to the simulation studies, the AOC‐JD method was evaluated using in vivo human brain data acquired from four voxels at different locations and of different sizes. As shown in Figures and and Tables and , the proposed AOC‐JD method consistently achieved SNRs comparable to those of the AOC method and higher than those of the SVD method across all voxels, further demonstrating its robustness and practical utility.

Conclusions

We introduced a novel method for adaptively optimized combination of multichannel MRS data acquired with phased‐array coils that does not require explicit noise samples or knowledge of noise correlations between coil elements/channels. Both computer simulations and in vivo human brain experiments demonstrated that the proposed AOC‐JD method achieves SNR performance comparable to that of the AOC method while outperforming the conventional SVD method. These findings establish the proposed AOC‐JD method as a theoretically grounded and practically robust approach for multichannel MRS coil combination, particularly when noise samples are unavailable or insufficient in the acquired spectroscopic data.
