1. Executive Summary
Green Era Educational NFP constructed and commissioned the Renewable Energy &
Urban Farming Campus, a commercial-scale anaerobic digestion (AD) and renewable
natural gas (RNG) facility located in the Auburn Gresham neighborhood on Chicago's
South Side. Funded in part by DOE Award DE-EE0010714, the project addresses a gap
in the existing literature and field practice by demonstrating that integrated food-waste-
to-energy and nutrient recovery technology can be successfully deployed and operated
at commercially meaningful scale within a dense urban, historically underserved
community. Prior to this project, commercial-scale urban AD deployments of this type
were largely absent from the Midwest, and the technical and operational feasibility of
siting, permitting, and commissioning such a system in a complex urban environment
was unproven at this throughput level.

The 35,000-square-foot processing building and associated infrastructure on an 8.9-
acre site are designed to accept up to 80,000 wet tons of food waste annually. Green
Era's feedstock stream consists primarily of commercial and institutional food waste
from regional grocery, food distribution, institutional, commercial, and hauling partners.
Organic feedstocks are preprocessed through a Wackerbauer separation mill rated at
10 tons/hour, then fed into a 1.68-million-gallon digester with a hydraulic retention time
exceeding 30 days. Biogas is upgraded by a Guild Associates pressure-swing
adsorption (PSA) system to pipeline-quality biomethane (minimum 970 BTU/SCF) and
injected into the Peoples Gas distribution system. The facility has demonstrated
technical effectiveness at commercial scale, processing nearly 55,000 wet tons of
organic waste in 2025 (almost double 2024 volumes) while achieving pipeline injection,
regulatory compliance, and consistent operations. Economically, the facility generates
revenue through tipping fees, RNG sales, and nutrient recovery, supporting a financially
sustainable operating model that does not rely on ongoing subsidy and is designed for
long-term viability.

The project delivers direct public benefit through three primary channels. First, it diverts
large volumes of organic waste from landfill disposal, reducing associated fugitive
methane emissions and contributing to domestic greenhouse gas reduction goals.
Second, it produces domestic renewable natural gas and nutrient-rich digestate that
supports agricultural production, advancing national goals for energy self-sufficiency
and domestic fertilizer supply. Third, it returns a long vacant brownfield industrial site to
productive use, creates approximately 15 permanent jobs drawn from the surrounding
historically underserved community, and provides a replicable implementation model for
peer cities, policymakers, and DOE program partners seeking to deploy similar
infrastructure at scale.

2. Background
2.1 Project Context and Community Need
Auburn Gresham is a historically underserved neighborhood on Chicago's South Side
with longstanding economic disinvestment and limited access to clean energy
infrastructure and quality employment. Green Era Educational NFP was founded with a
mission to transform a long vacant brownfield industrial site into a productive community
asset that generates domestic clean energy, produces agricultural nutrients, and
creates sustainable local employment.

Chicago alone generates an estimated 451,500 tons of food waste annually, the
majority of which is landfilled, contributing to fugitive methane emissions and
representing a significant lost resource opportunity [7]. Green Era's facility addresses
this gap by providing infrastructure to receive, preprocess, and digest commercial and
institutional food waste within city limits, converting a waste liability into domestic
renewable energy and valuable nutrient outputs.

2.2 Campus Overview and Scope
The Green Era Renewable Energy & Urban Farming Campus is organized around three
interconnected components: (1) a commercial-scale, closed-loop anaerobic digestion
system converting food waste into renewable natural gas and nutrient-rich digestate; (2)
a local farming and food production education program supporting community
engagement in sustainable local food systems; and (3) a community education center
supporting workforce development, environmental education, and community
engagement activities.

The DOE-supported work focused on deployment and operationalization of the organics
processing, renewable energy production, and nutrient recovery systems at the core of
the campus. The project is sited at 650 W. 83rd Street, Chicago, IL 60620, on an 8.905-
acre parcel zoned for manufacturing use in the Auburn Gresham neighborhood. The
processing building measures approximately 35,000 square feet under roof with a 43-
foot eave height. Key facility elements include:

 • Enclosed receiving area for non-pumpable (solid) organics with 90-cubic-yard
 live-bottom bunker
 • Liquid tanker unloading pad and underground storage for pumpable food waste
 and fats, oils, and grease (FOG)
 • Separation mill room housing the Wackerbauer preprocessing system
 • 1.68-million-gallon bolted-steel anaerobic digester tank (75.66 ft diameter x 53 ft
 sidewall)
 • 320,000-gallon feedstock equalization/biomass tank (33.63 ft diameter x 53 ft
 sidewall)
 • Biogas upgrading skid, compression equipment, and Peoples Gas pipeline
 interconnection vault
 • Charter 2-meter belt press dewatering system and polymer dosing equipment
 • SCADA/control room with Allen-Bradley ControlLogix PLC and FactoryTalk HMI

The facility operates 24 hours per day, 7 days per week, with approximately 15 direct
employees drawn from Auburn Gresham and surrounding communities. Sound levels

2.3 DOE Award Overview
DOE Award DE-EE0010714 was awarded under the Fiscal Year 2023 Community

2.4 Relationship to Current State of the Field
Anaerobic digestion and renewable natural gas production from organic waste are well-
established technical pathways for reducing landfill disposal, capturing methane
emissions, generating renewable energy, and recovering nutrients [1][3]. Current
research and industry development efforts increasingly focus on improving operational
reliability, feedstock preprocessing effectiveness, contaminant management, gas
upgrading efficiency, and economically viable deployment at commercial scale within
diverse urban settings [1][2].

Recent peer-reviewed literature identifies food waste diversion and anaerobic digestion
as critical components of broader decarbonization and circular economy strategies. A
2024 review published in Results in Engineering identified anaerobic digestion as an
effective pathway for transforming food waste into renewable energy while reducing
landfill utilization and associated greenhouse gas emissions [1]. Additional reviews
similarly emphasize the importance of feedstock preparation, contamination reduction,
and operational optimization in improving biogas system performance and
commercialization outcomes [2][3].

A key challenge highlighted in the current literature and directly addressed by this
project is the preprocessing of heterogeneous commercial food waste containing
packaging contaminants and variable organic compositions [2][6]. Reliable depackaging
and preprocessing infrastructure is widely recognized as operationally critical for
achieving consistent throughput and contamination management at commercial scale.
The Green Era facility addresses this through the Wackerbauer wet mechanical
separation mill, which simultaneously shreds, mixes, de-bags, and separates inorganic
contaminants from the organic fraction at 10 tons/hour.

The project also reflects the life-cycle assessment (LCA) and techno-economic literature
emphasizing that commercially viable anaerobic digestion systems depend not solely on
biological digestion performance, but on effective integration of preprocessing, gas

 Page 7 of 37

handling, emissions compliance, utility coordination, and operational logistics [7][8][9].
Rather than developing novel biological processes, the Green Era project applied
commercially proven technologies within a complex urban redevelopment environment
where site constraints, utility coordination, and proximity to residential neighborhoods
present distinct deployment challenges.

At the federal level, EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin's September 2025 launch of Feed It
Onward, a national initiative focused on reducing food waste and strengthening food
security, reinforces the growing policy alignment with food waste diversion infrastructure
like Green Era's. The Green Era Campus contributes directly to these objectives by
capturing organic waste that would otherwise reach landfill and converting it into
domestic renewable energy and nutrient-rich outputs that support agricultural
production.

In summary, the published literature and field practice are similar to this project in
validating anaerobic digestion, PSA gas upgrading, and mechanical preprocessing as
proven technical pathways for food waste-to-energy conversion. They differ from this
project in that peer-reviewed literature and prior deployments have largely focused on
purpose-built rural or industrial-park installations, laboratory-scale investigations, or
single-technology demonstrations rather than fully integrated commercial-scale systems
deployed within dense urban, historically underserved communities with complex
permitting, utility, and operational environments. The Green Era project fully leveraged
this published knowledge base to inform technology selection (Wackerbauer
preprocessing, anaerobic digestion, Guild PSA upgrading), feedstock characterization
approaches (total solids targeting, contamination management), and operational
sequencing, accelerating the implementation timeline and improving system design
quality relative to what would have been possible without the existing body of applied
research.

Significantly, the Green Era Campus integrates organics diversion and renewable
energy generation within a broader model that also encompasses nutrient recovery for
agricultural use, local food production education, workforce development, and
brownfield revitalization. This integrated approach reflects increasing national interest in
circular economy infrastructure capable of simultaneously addressing environmental,
economic, and community development objectives.

3. Project Objectives
The primary objective of the Green Era Renewable Energy & Urban Farming Campus
project was deployment and operationalization of anaerobic digestion and organics
processing infrastructure capable of diverting large-scale food waste streams from
landfill disposal while supporting renewable natural gas production, nutrient recovery,
and long-term expansion of regional circular economy infrastructure. Additional
objectives included:

 • Construct and commission a commercial-scale AD and RNG production facility in
 an historically underserved urban community
 • Demonstrate the technical and economic feasibility of processing commercial
 and institutional food waste into pipeline-quality biomethane
 • Deploy preprocessing and material handling infrastructure capable of managing
 heterogeneous urban food waste streams at meaningful throughput
 • Implement gas management and emissions control systems necessary for safe
 and compliant operation
 • Create approximately 15 permanent, quality jobs drawn from Auburn Gresham
 and surrounding communities
 • Produce nutrient-rich digestate to support composting operations and domestic
 food production
 • Support educational programming in local food production and sustainable
 resource recovery
 • Establish a replicable model for urban food-waste-to-energy and nutrient
 recovery projects in underserved communities

3.1 National Clean Energy and Economic Impact
A distinguishing contribution of this project is demonstrating that commercial-scale food-
waste-to-RNG and nutrient recovery infrastructure is viable within a dense urban
environment, not only at rural or industrial-park sites. By operating adjacent to
residential neighborhoods on Chicago's South Side, Green Era expands the geographic
range of communities that can host organics-to-energy facilities, directly supporting
DOE's goals for equitable distribution of clean energy infrastructure. The facility's
injection of pipeline-quality biomethane into the Peoples Gas distribution system
displaces fossil natural gas and contributes to domestic renewable energy supply at a
time when U.S. energy self-sufficiency is a national priority.

Equally important, the nutrient-rich digestate produced by the facility supports
composting operations that contribute to domestic fertilizer supply. As the U.S. seeks to
reduce dependence on imported synthetic fertilizers and strengthen domestic
agricultural supply chains, the Green Era model demonstrates how urban food waste
can be converted into a locally produced, cost-effective nutrient resource for agricultural
use. This connection between urban waste management and rural food production
reflects the full circular economy potential of commercial anaerobic digestion
infrastructure.

3.2 Significance, Innovation, and Advancement of the Work
The significance of the Green Era project lies in the successful deployment and
operationalization of integrated organics processing, renewable energy production, and
nutrient recovery infrastructure at commercially meaningful scale in an historically
underserved urban community. The project demonstrates how established anaerobic
digestion principles can be integrated with mechanical preprocessing, PSA gas
upgrading, belt press dewatering, and direct pipeline injection within a complex real-
world operating environment on Chicago's South Side.

The project's innovative aspects derive primarily from the integration of multiple
operational systems into a single infrastructure platform combining food waste
diversion, renewable energy generation, nutrient recovery, educational food production
programming, and community redevelopment functions. The facility's location adjacent
to residential neighborhoods required design solutions including enclosed processing
areas, negative-pressure odor control, vehicle-sensor-controlled doors, and an activated
carbon exhaust treatment system that contribute implementation knowledge for future
urban AD deployments.

4.3 Technical Discussion of Results

4.3.1 Feedstock Receipt and Characterization
Green Era's feedstock stream consists primarily of commercial and institutional food
waste, including bulk food scraps, packaged and expired food products, packaged

                                                Page 12 of 37

beverages, liquid food waste, grocery waste, and other non-glass organic materials
suitable for anaerobic digestion. Feedstock is sourced from a mix of regional grocery,
food distribution, institutional, commercial, and hauling partners that aggregate organic
waste streams from across the Chicago area.

Incoming materials arrive in several forms, including roll-off containers, tankers, Gaylord
boxes, palletized packaged food, and depackaged organic material. The facility's
preprocessing and material handling systems are designed to manage these varied
streams by separating organic material from packaging and preparing the recovered
organics for digestion, while residual packaging materials are managed for appropriate
recycling or disposal pathways.

The facility is designed to accept up to 80,000 wet tons of food waste annually across
both non-pumpable (solid) and pumpable categories. Non-pumpable organics arrive by
collection truck at 8-16 loads per day (average 12 tons/load) and are tipped directly into
the 90-cubic-yard receiving pit/live-bottom bunker. Pumpable materials arrive via liquid
tanker at 5-8 deliveries per day (average 13 tons/truck). The combined feedstock blend
is designed to achieve 12-14% total solids and 86-88% moisture content, with average
non-digestible contamination of approximately 7% by weight.

In 2025, Green Era processed nearly 55,000 wet tons of organic waste, representing
almost double the volume processed in 2024 and demonstrating strong year-over-year
growth. 2026 year-to-date volumes are continuing to trend higher, with average monthly
tonnage running ahead of 2025 levels. The facility is scaling toward its full design
throughput in a deliberate and phased manner, prioritizing operational stability and
process quality across all system components.

4.3.2 Preprocessing: Separation Mill and Grit Removal
Non-pumpable organics are preprocessed through a Wackerbauer separation mill
(manufactured in Germany), which simultaneously shreds, mixes, de-bags, and
separates inorganic contaminants (plastic packaging, metals) from the organic fraction.
The mill is driven by a 100 HP motor and is rated at 10 tons of material per hour
(approximately 25 cubic yards per hour). Operating 16 hours per day, the mill can process
up to 192 tons per day. Output slurry exits at approximately 14% total solids with residual
contamination up to 2.0% by weight.

Following the separation mill, a grit separation system removes dense inorganic fines. A
3,000-gallon sloped-bottom receiving hopper captures settled grit, which is removed by
an inclined screw conveyor for disposal. This two-stage preprocessing approach protects
downstream pumps, heat exchangers, and the digester agitator from abrasion damage.

4.3.3 Feedstock Equalization and Biomass Tank
Preprocessed solid slurry and pumpable food waste are combined in a 320,000-gallon
equalization tank (33.63 ft diameter x 53 ft sidewall) maintained at approximately 113
degrees F (45 degrees C) via four internal heating circuits totaling 1,200 linear feet of
corrugated stainless-steel tubing with approximately 1.25 MMBTU/hour combined

                                        Page 13 of 37

capacity. Variable-speed Seepex Smart Stator progressive cavity pumps with NBR
elastomer stators transfer feedstock to the digester at controlled rates, with mag-flow
meters on each circuit.

4.3.4 Anaerobic Digester
The anaerobic digester operates with the following specifications:

 Working volume 1,680,000 gallons (1.68 million gallons)
 Tank diameter 75.66 feet
 Tank height 53 ft sidewall; 62 ft to peak
 Construction Bolted steel panels, white powder-coat finish; white aluminum
 standing-seam insulation cladding
 Hydraulic retention time >30 days
 (HRT)
 Agitation Single slow-turning top-mounted mixer
 Internal heating 5 circuits, 1,150 linear ft of 2-inch corrugated stainless tubing;
 approximately 1 MMBTU/h capacity

System operation provides stable, long-term digestion of the heterogeneous commercial
feedstock blend at manageable operating temperatures. The long HRT (>30 days)
ensures thorough digestion and consistent biogas production.

4.3.5 Post-Digestion Screening and Dewatering
Digester effluent passes through a Huber Strainpress (Model S-205) post-digestion
screen, capturing solid particles larger than 3/16 inch. Screened digestate flows to a
30,000-gallon in-ground storage tank (T-205) before dewatering. A single Charter 2-meter
belt press dewatering system processes feedstock following cationic polyacrylamide
polymer conditioning, producing approximately 30 cubic yards per day of dewatered cake.
Dewatered cake is transferred to composting operations that support domestic food
production.

Filtrate from dewatering is discharged to the Metropolitan Water Reclamation District
(MWRD) Stickney Water Reclamation Facility under a permitted industrial pretreatment
agreement. Filtrate characteristically contains total Kjeldahl nitrogen (TKN) of 2,000-5,000
mg/L and chemical oxygen demand (COD) of 3,000-7,500 mg/L.

4.3.6 Biogas Upgrading and RNG Injection
Biogas at approximately 60% methane is upgraded using a Guild Associates pressure-
swing adsorption (PSA) system: 400 SCFM raw biogas in, 216 SCFM biomethane out
at minimum 970 BTU/SCF. Product gas is compressed to 150 PSIG, then boosted to
175 PSIG and injected into the Peoples Gas local distribution system at 160 PSIG.
Pipeline interconnection required coordination with Peoples Gas for metering, pressure
regulation, odorization verification, and gas quality certification.

Project was officially placed in service on 10/2/23. The final interconnection agreement
with Peoples Gas Light and Coke Company was signed December 22, 2023. First
measured fuel pushed to the Peoples Gas grid was achieved on 6/24/24.

4.3.7 Flare System
A dedicated enclosed flare system provides safety and compliance management during
startup, planned maintenance, and any periods when the PSA upgrading system is
offline. The flare is sized for maximum digester biogas output (500 SCFM) and is
equipped with a dedicated flame management controller and manual override capability.
Work included mechanical and electrical engineering, foundations, installation and
related control panels.

4.4 Methodologies Utilized
Project implementation was grounded in a rigorous engineering basis of design
developed by Green Arrow Engineering, LLC. This document established process
design parameters, equipment selection criteria, feedstock characterization
assumptions, mass and energy balances, and control system requirements for all major
facility systems. Equipment selection emphasized commercially proven technologies
with established service records in food waste anaerobic digestion applications,
minimizing technical risk associated with the urban deployment context.

Feedstock characterization informed key design parameters including total solids
targets (12-14% TS blend), non-digestible contamination assumptions (approximately
7%), and hydraulic loading rates to the digester. Progressive cavity pump selection
(Seepex Smart Stator with NBR stators) was based on the viscosity and abrasion
characteristics of preprocessed slurry. System operation was designed to maximize
operational stability and long-term system reliability for the heterogeneous commercial
feedstock blend.

The Guild Associates PSA biogas upgrading technology was selected based on its
ability to produce pipeline-quality biomethane (>=970 BTU/SCF) at 400 SCFM
throughput with demonstrated commercial reliability. Commissioning followed a
sequential system-by-system integration approach: preprocessing systems were
brought online first, followed by the equalization tank and transfer pumps, digester
inoculation and heat-up, biogas system integration, and finally PSA upgrading and
pipeline injection. Performance was tracked through the SCADA system's one-minute
data logging, with operational adjustments made based on real-time process monitoring
and periodic operational data review.

Emissions compliance methodology followed applicable federal, state, and local
regulatory requirements. Filtrate management methodology involved characterization of
TKN and COD loads to ensure compliance with MWRD pretreatment permit limits prior
to discharge to the Stickney Water Reclamation Facility.

4.5 Challenges Encountered and Resolution Measures
Project implementation encountered several infrastructure and coordination challenges
common to deployment of complex organics processing systems within active urban
redevelopment environments.

                                        Page 16 of 37

One of the most significant challenges involved the permitting process. As a first-of-its-
kind commercial food waste-to-RNG facility of this scale in an urban environment,
Green Era's project presented novel regulatory questions that required extended review
and coordination across multiple agencies. Permit timelines exceeded initial projections
as regulators worked to evaluate a system type with limited precedent in dense urban
settings. Green Era worked proactively with permitting authorities to provide technical
documentation, process descriptions, and operational data to support thorough review
and ultimately obtain all required approvals. This experience highlights the importance
of early agency engagement and technical transparency for innovative infrastructure
projects and contributes practical knowledge for future developers navigating similar
permitting processes.

The most significant infrastructure adjustment involved the originally contemplated
ClearSpan compost structure. As final operational layout planning advanced, the
originally proposed location became incompatible with final site configuration and
operational scale and grading requirements. As a result, the associated capital
resources were reallocated toward higher-priority AD infrastructure and commissioning
activities.

Water tower removal activities were impacted by utility coordination requirements and
logistical constraints involving overhead power lines and on-going coordination with
ComED, the public utility. These activities were excluded from the final DOE-funded
scope while the project prioritized operational deployment of the core organics-to-
energy and nutrient recovery systems.

In certain cases, equipment rental approaches were utilized in place of outright
equipment purchases, supporting more efficient reallocation of project resources toward
more critical operational objectives.

Despite these adjustments, the project successfully achieved all primary technical and
operational milestones through completion and commissioning of the anaerobic
digestion and organics processing systems.

4.6 Lessons Learned
Several actionable lessons emerged from project implementation that are directly
applicable to future commercial-scale urban anaerobic digestion deployments. First,
early and sustained engagement with permitting authorities is critical for first-of-its-kind
systems. Regulatory agencies benefit from substantial upfront technical documentation
and direct dialogue; investing in this early reduces delays and builds the institutional
knowledge base for future projects. Second, site layout finalization must occur early in
the project timeline, before detailed infrastructure planning is locked in – spatial
constraints in urban brownfield settings can force late-stage adjustments that affect cost
and schedule. Third, operational commissioning sequencing matters: bringing
preprocessing and digester systems online before biogas upgrading and pipeline
injection systems allowed the team to validate biological performance and feedstock
handling independent of gas system constraints. Fourth, SCADA integration from the

                                        Page 17 of 37

outset of commissioning provides critical real-time operational data that supports faster
performance optimization. Finally, maintaining operational and financial flexibility –
including through targeted equipment rental strategies – can improve resource
efficiency and resilience in complex project environments where scope prioritization is
required.

6.1 Remaining Technical Risks and Commercialization Barriers
The primary remaining technical risk is feedstock supply chain concentration: the
facility's financial and operational performance depends on maintaining reliable, high-

                                        Page 21 of 37

volume relationships with food waste generators and hauling partners. Diversifying the
feedstock supply base (including grocery, food and beverage manufacturing,
institutional food service, and distribution partners) is a critical risk mitigation priority. A
secondary technical risk involves preprocessing optimization for the widest possible
range of incoming material types; improvements to contamination management
workflows will be required to maximize throughput reliability across varying feedstock
compositions. Additionally, digester biological stability at high throughput requires
continued monitoring and adaptive management as organic loading rates increase
toward design capacity.

The primary commercialization barrier is the regulatory environment for first-of-its-kind
systems: as Green Era's permitting experience demonstrated, novel urban AD
deployments face extended regulatory review timelines that are difficult to predict and
budget. Future projects of this type will benefit from the precedent Green Era has
established, but each new jurisdiction is likely to require significant upfront regulatory
investment. Market-side barriers include RNG price volatility and the dependency of
project economics on renewable natural gas market conditions and renewable fuel
standard program structures. Addressing these risks through feedstock diversification,
operational optimization, and continued stakeholder engagement with regulators and
policymakers remains central to Green Era's path toward fully commercialized
operations.
