Comparative evaluation of seven severity grading systems in pediatric oral food challenges: Distinctions between anaphylactic and non-anaphylactic reactions☆
Authors: Taha Yasin Akın, Yusuf Ziya Varlı, Nagihan İskender, Nefise Nezihe Uluç, İsmail Özanlı, Mebrure Yazıcı, Döndü Ceren Kaval, Mehmet Serkan Erkan, Muhammet Taha Demirci, Sibel Balci, Müjde Tuba Çöğürlü, Işıl Eser Şimşek, Metin Aydoğan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple severity scoring systems exist for classifying reactions during OFCs; however, the lack of uniformity among them results in inconsistent identification and management of anaphylaxis.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics and severity of anaphylactic and non-anaphylactic reactions during oral food challenges (OFCs) in children and to compare the concordance and discrepancies among 7 different severity scoring systems.MethodsIn this single-center, retrospective, and observational study, positive OFCs were retrospectively graded using 7 different severity classification systems. The concordance and discrepancies among the classification systems, including the World Allergy Organization (WAO) 2024 criteria, the International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD-11), and the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) classifications, were evaluated.ResultsOf the 1901 OFCs performed on 855 patients, 344 (18.1%) were positive, and among these, 61 (17.7%) were classified as anaphylactic reactions. Patients with anaphylactic reactions were significantly older than those with non-anaphylactic reactions (median age: 27 months vs. 15 months; p < 0.001). Using the WAO2024 criteria as the reference standard, both ICD-11 and EAACI classifications showed 100% sensitivity, while specificity for all systems ranged from 86.2% to 99.3%. Discrepancies among severity scoring systems were primarily due to differences in the interpretation of gastrointestinal manifestations. EAACI showed the most favorable balance between sensitivity and specificity, whereas CoFAR and Dribin demonstrated the highest specificity.ConclusionThe findings emphasize the need for a harmonized severity classification system that is applicable in both clinical practice and research. Such standardization would enhance diagnostic accuracy and improve the management of food allergies in children.

Introduction

Oral food challenges (OFCs) are used to verify food allergy diagnoses, assess tolerance in individuals with confirmed allergies, and determine the threshold dose of an allergenic food. Immediate hypersensitivity reactions during OFCs range from localized urticaria to severe and life-threatening anaphylaxis. However, the current literature shows variability in the reported clinical characteristics and frequencies of anaphylactic versus non-anaphylactic reactions, as well as their associations with specific food groups.1, 2, 3, 4 Accordingly, a systematic assessment of reactions observed in diverse age groups and food categories is essential to improve clinical prediction and achieve standardization of risk stratification.

Accurate grading of allergic reaction severity is critical for identifying anaphylaxis and guiding appropriate management. International guidelines are consistent regarding the clinical criteria for diagnosing anaphylaxis, defining it as a potentially life-threatening, severe, and rapidly evolving systemic hypersensitivity reaction.5,6 However, there is no universally accepted severity classification system, and multiple grading scales are still in use. The World Allergy Organization (WAO) published its anaphylaxis guidelines in 2020,7 which were later updated in 20248 through a new publication introducing a revised grading system. In addition, several other severity classifications are frequently recommended, including the Consortium of Food Allergy Research (CoFAR) v3 scale,9 the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) grading system,10 the 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11),11 the Delphi consensus–based classification proposed by Dribin et al.,12 and the scoring model developed by Blazowski et al.13 Considerable differences exist among these systems, and there is a need to evaluate the clinical relevance of such discrepancies using real-life data. Furthermore, greater emphasis should be placed on practice-based evidence to highlight the need for a unified and standardized classification system.

The primary aim of this study was to apply 7 different classification systems to all patients who developed reactions during oral food challenges (OFCs) and analyze the concordance and discrepancies among severity scoring systems using real-life data. In addition, we aimed to determine the clinical characteristics of children with positive OFC reactions and identify risk factors for anaphylaxis.

Methods

Study design

Our study was a single-center, retrospective, observational study conducted at the Pediatric Allergy and Immunology Department of University Hospital. The study included children admitted to our outpatient clinic with suspected food allergies who underwent open OFC for diagnostic purposes, as well as children who were previously diagnosed with food allergies and underwent OFC for tolerance evaluation. The study was approved by the University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Project number: 2025/256. Document number: E-80418770-020-782461).

Data collection and documentation

Since 2017, patient data have been collected in a standardized and structured format aligned with international OFC protocols,14 following principles consistent with prospective study design. For each OFC, detailed information was recorded, including whether a reaction occurred, the dose level at which it was triggered, the cumulative amount of food consumed prior to the reaction, the latency period between the final dose and onset of symptoms, the type and severity of the reaction, treatments administered, and the response to those treatments. All OFC procedures were routinely performed by an experienced healthcare team consisting of 1 physician and 2 nurses experienced in OFC. Patients who did not experience a reaction during the OFC were contacted by phone 1 week later to gather information on whether any reaction had developed. As a center adhering to this rigorous protocol, we retrospectively reviewed all OFCs conducted between 2017 and 2025 and systematically analyzed the data collected.

Patient demographic information (age and sex), specific food used in the OFC, and test outcomes were recorded. All data from medical records were obtained in cases where a reaction developed.

OFC procedure

All patients underwent OFCs according to a standardized, unified protocol. For liquid food items, the OFC was conducted in 6 incremental dose steps: 1, 3, 10, 30, 50, and 106 mL of liquid. For solid foods, the OFC was administered in 4 escalating dose steps corresponding to 1/16, 2/16, 4/16, and 9/16 of the total cumulative doses. Prior to the initiation of testing, at 15-min intervals following each dose, and 2 h after the final exposure, all patients underwent comprehensive monitoring, including assessment of vital signs (pulse, oxygen saturation, and blood pressure), symptom inquiry, and physical examination. If a patient exhibited equivocal symptoms, a repeat OFC was scheduled using the same food item under the same conditions. In such cases, a single-dose OFC was performed the following day, and the outcome of the single-dose OFC was documented.

OFCs were classified as positive if a reaction consistent with an immediate hypersensitivity reaction was detected. In the absence of any clinical symptoms during the observation period, the OFC result was considered negative. OFCs that could not be completed because the patient left before finishing the protocol or observation period were categorized as inconclusive cases. For patients who did not exhibit a reaction during the OFC, post-test follow-up data regarding any immediate reactions at home were obtained through structured telephone interviews with caregivers. In cases where no reaction was observed during the OFC, but the patient subsequently experienced an immediate-type hypersensitivity reaction upon consuming the same food at home, the result was classified as a false negative. All data were systematically documented in our department's corporate information system throughout the test implementation process. The data acquired for this study was sourced from our corporate database.

Severity scoring of reactions

The criteria for each system were carefully applied to real-life clinical documentation, and OFCs were classified by 2 independent reviewers blinded to the original clinical interpretation to ensure consistency. Discrepancies were resolved by a third, senior allergist.

The diagnostic criteria recommended by the WAO anaphylaxis guideline published in 20207 were used for the primary classification of reactions into “anaphylaxis” and “non-anaphylaxis”. Specifically, patients were considered to have experienced anaphylaxis if they fulfilled at least 1 of the amended WAO diagnostic criteria, which included rapid-onset symptoms with multisystem involvement or isolated cardiovascular or respiratory compromise after exposure to a known or suspected allergen. Conversely, reactions not meeting these criteria -particularly those with isolated skin and mucosa, gastrointestinal involvement-were classified as “non-anaphylaxis.”

At the time of testing, no predefined anaphylaxis severity grading system was used to categorize clinical reactions observed during OFCs. However, during the retrospective phase of the study, we sought to systematically classify the severity of all documented reactions; therefore, we applied 7 established severity-scoring systems to our dataset for comparative evaluation. All reactions during OFC recorded in our dataset were retrospectively re-evaluated and scored using 7 established anaphylaxis severity grading systems: CoFAR v3 scale,9 EAACI grading,10 Dribin grading,12 Blazowski grading,13 ICD- 11 grading,11 the grading recommended in the WAO anaphylaxis guideline published in 20207 (WAO2020) and the severity classification updated by WAO in 20248 (WAO2024). In a 5-grade classification systems (WAO2020, WAO2024, CoFAR, Dribin), grades 3 or higher are regarded as the threshold for diagnosing anaphylaxis. In classification systems with 3 (EAACI) and 4 grades (Blazowski, ICD-11), grades 2 or higher serve as the threshold for diagnosing anaphylaxis. Concordance between the different classification systems was evaluated separately, first using the WAO2024 classification and subsequently the ICD-11 classification as the reference standard.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS for Windows version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc Statistical Software version 22.026 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium). Kolmogorov-Smirnov's test was used to assess the normality assumption. Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) because the normality assumption did not hold. Categorical variables are presented as numbers of observations and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Associations between categorical variables were examined using the chi-square test with Bonferroni correction. The odds ratio (OR) was calculated using a binary logistic regression model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses and area under the curve (AUC) comparisons were performed. Pairwise comparisons of the AUCs were performed using the DeLong test. Sensitivity, specificity, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were also calculated. AUC values were interpreted according to conventional thresholds: excellent (≥0.90), good (0.80–0.89), fair (0.70–0.79), poor (0.60–0.69), and fail (<0.60). Agreement between WAO2024 and the other classification systems for anaphylaxis categorization was additionally evaluated using Cohen's kappa coefficient after dichotomizing severity grades into anaphylaxis and non-anaphylaxis categories according to each classification system's predefined threshold (grade ≥3 for 5-grade systems and grade ≥2 for 3- or 4-grade systems). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 2019 OFCs were performed during the study period. Of these, 58 tests were inconclusive and excluded from the analysis. In addition, 60 tests were deemed equivocal based on clinical observation and were followed by a single-dose re-administration of the same food the next day. Of these, 5 patients exhibited positive reactions, whereas 55 exhibited negative outcomes. These control tests were included in the final analysis and were not counted as separate challenges. After excluding inconclusive tests, 1901 OFCs from 855 patients were included in the final analysis; 351 patients were female, and 504 were male. The median age (IQR) was 15 (11–28) months. 1901 OFCs were evaluated, of which 344 (18.1%) were positive and 1557 (81.9%) were negative. Among the 344 patients who exhibited positive reactions during OFCs, 283 (82.3%) were classified as non-anaphylactic and 61 (17.7%) as anaphylactic. Four patients (0.26%) who exhibited no reaction during the OFC subsequently reported immediate hypersensitivity reactions at home. Three of these reactions occurred after cow's milk challenges and 1 after egg challenge. No cofactors such as infection, exercise, or NSAID exposure were identified in these patients. These were considered false negatives and were not included in the final analysis because their reactions were not observed. When the WAO2024 classification was used as a reference, the specificity of the classification systems ranged from 87.6% to 99.3%. ROC curve analysis demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy for all classification systems, with AUC values of 0.973 for EAACI, 0.964 for CoFAR, 0.938 for Blazowski, 0.931 for ICD-11, and 0.882 for Dribin classification. Comparative analyses further revealed statistically significant differences between the AUC of WAO and those of EAACI (p < 0.001), ICD-11 (p < 0.001), Blazowski (p < 0.001), Dribin (p < 0.001), and CoFAR (p = 0.025), thereby underscoring the heterogeneity across currently available classification systems. When ICD-11 was used as the reference standard, the specificities ranged from 82% to 90%, with EAACI and Blazowski exhibiting the highest values (87% and 90%, respectively). The highest sensitivities were observed for Dribin (98%) and CoFAR (97%), respectively. ROC curve analysis confirmed high diagnostic accuracy across all systems, with AUC values of 0.839 for Blazowski, 0.802 for EAACI, 0.781 for CoFAR, and 0.738 for Dribin classification.

Agreement analyses for anaphylaxis categorization demonstrated substantial to almost perfect concordance between WAO2024 and the other classification systems. Cohen's kappa coefficients were 0.909 for CoFAR, 0.854 for EAACI, 0.837 for Dribin, 0.706 for Blazowski, and 0.680 for ICD-11 (all p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table VII).

Sources of discrepancy between classification systems

Among patients evaluated for anaphylaxis according to WAO2024, 57 (93.4%) patients were classified as grade 3, and 4 patients (6.6%) were classified as grade 4. One patient who was classified differently under WAO2020 had treatment-responsive hypotension and was assigned grade 4 rather than grade 5 according to the WAO2024 update. While discrepancies in classification were observed for 44 patients between the WAO2020 and EAACI systems, this number decreased to 12 when the WAO2024 classification was compared with the EAACI system. When examining 44 OFCs classified as grade 2 in the WAO classification and grade 1 in the EAACI classification, it was found that 8 patients had isolated mild gastrointestinal symptoms, 12 patients had mild gastrointestinal symptoms along with other systemic findings, and 24 patients had multiple mild systemic symptoms without gastrointestinal involvement. Among the 12 patients responsible for the discrepancies between the WAO2024 and EAACI classifications, 8 had moderate cutaneous reactions and 4 had moderate cutaneous reactions accompanied by mild gastrointestinal symptoms (Table S III, Table S IV, Table S V).

Discussion

There is a wide array of anaphylaxis severity classifications, each with different criteria and interpretative nuances, underscoring the need for validation against real-life data. Our study revealed significant variability across existing systems, with gastrointestinal manifestation interpretation being a key factor in the discrepancies. However, the EAACI and ICD-11 classifications demonstrated the highest sensitivity in identifying anaphylaxis, closely aligning with the WAO criteria. Our findings highlight the need for a harmonized severity classification applicable to both clinical and research settings.

Despite the integrative approach proposed by the WAO in 20207 and updated in 2024,8 a universally accepted classification system is still lacking. Bouderbala et al reported inconsistencies when different severity classification methods were applied to OFC outcomes, comparing the EAACI, CoFAR, Blazowski, and Dribin systems using ICD-11 as a reference. Their analyses showed the EAACI classification aligned closely with ICD-11, with 100% sensitivity and 93.5% specificity.15 In our study, using ICD-11 as the reference, the EAACI system showed 86% sensitivity and 87% specificity. The discrepancy with results from Bouderbala et al may be due to gaps in the ICD-11 classification. Unlike other systems, which examine each organ system and define severity, ICD-11 lacks comprehensive guidance. Notably, it lacks classifications for isolated severe respiratory or cardiovascular findings, causing variability in classification between studies. Güvenir et al compared the WAO2020 and EAACI classifications in 492 patients undergoing OFCs, finding 94.1% met the anaphylaxis definition by both criteria, but 5.3% were diagnosed only by WAO criteria.16 In our study, using WAO as the reference, sensitivity was 100% and specificity was 94.7%. These findings highlight that different severity grading systems applied to the same OFC dataset can yield different outcomes. Collectively, these findings highlight that applying different severity grading systems to the same OFC dataset may lead to substantial variability in severity categorization and anaphylaxis classification.

Our study identified uncertainty in grading gastrointestinal symptoms as a significant issue in classifying patients with and without anaphylaxis. This inconsistency poses a critical challenge, potentially contributing to diagnostic uncertainty in identifying anaphylaxis. Severity grading differences have been addressed in many studies. In 1 study of 23 severity classification systems, including the EAACI classification used from 1959 to 2018, GI symptoms included subjective (OAS, nausea, abdominal pain) and objective (vomiting, diarrhea) findings. Some systems prioritize these symptoms, while others do not, leading to scoring inconsistencies.17 Blazowski et al noted that gastrointestinal symptoms are ambiguous in predicting anaphylaxis severity.13 The WAO anaphylaxis guidelines also noted differences in classifying gastrointestinal complaints, with regional and guideline disagreements on considering gastrointestinal symptoms in defining anaphylactic symptoms. Their previous guidelines replaced “persistent” with “severe” GI symptoms for anaphylaxis diagnosis in 2020.7 The WAO2024 update aimed to reduce ambiguity in interpreting gastrointestinal symptoms described in the 2020 guideline by clearly defining phrases like “abdominal pain lasting more than 20 min and not distractible” and “vomiting not due to gag reflex or taste aversion”.8 This emphasis by the WAO is appropriate and has met many needs; however, our study shows the definition of severe GI symptoms should be more objective. Greater clarification is especially needed regarding the clinical significance of the number of vomiting episodes, the severity of a single vomiting episode, the intensity and duration of abdominal pain, and whether isolated vomiting should be interpreted differently from vomiting accompanied by abdominal pain or diarrhea.

Another limitation of existing classification systems is grading non-anaphylactic reactions. Stafford et al noted many tools generalize mild symptoms by placing them in the same severity category.18 Non-anaphylactic reactions are often limited to 1 or 2 severity categories, making it hard to distinguish different severities. This issue was evident in the non-anaphylactic group, especially with systems like EAACI, Blazowski, and ICD-11. Despite 5 grading levels, the 2024 WAO classification also grouped most non-anaphylactic cases as grade 1. While 44 patients were grade 1 according to EAACI, they were grade 2 in WAO2020, decreasing to 12 in WAO2024. Separating anaphylactic and non-anaphylactic reactions—evaluating non-anaphylactic cases separately and reserving a category for anaphylaxis—might help. In assessing non-anaphylactic reactions, main factors causing differences among systems were classifying mild gastrointestinal symptoms and categorizing OFCs with multiple mild systemic findings.

There are studies in the literature reporting that the severity of reactions increases with age 19, 20, 21. Kennedy et al found fewer severe symptoms in children under 12 months.22 In our study, anaphylaxis was detected in less than 1 in 10 patients under the age of 1, whereas in those over the age of 5, it was observed in 1 out of every 3 patients. These age-related differences may reflect the natural course of food allergy. Transient phenotypes tend to resolve with the development of tolerance in early life, whereas more persistent and resistant phenotypes may continue into later childhood and adolescence, often presenting with more severe clinical reactions.

Data obtained from Europe show that the rate of adrenaline administration in anaphylactic cases is around 27%; this rate is 19.6% in Germany, while it can vary up to 66.7% in Greece, France, and Spain.23 In our study, the rate of adrenaline administration was 73.8%, which is relatively high. This can be attributed to the evaluation of cases at a referral allergy center and the close clinical monitoring of patients, particularly during OFCs. In addition, this high rate may have contributed to the complete absence of patients requiring a second dose of adrenaline or resulting in mortality. Our findings are consistent with observations in the literature that delayed or absent adrenaline treatment is the most important determinant of the risk of fatal outcomes in food-induced anaphylaxis.24,25 Additionally, in our study, most patients who did not receive adrenaline presented with isolated lower respiratory tract symptoms without skin or mucosal findings. This indicates that the diagnostic challenges in cases of isolated lower respiratory tract involvement, which are particularly emphasized in the WAO guidelines,7 are also reflected in real-life data and continue to persist in daily clinical practice.

False-negative OFCs have been reported in previous studies, with rates of approximately 3% depending on the study population and challenge protocol.26,27 One proposed explanation is that administration of very small divided doses during OFC may transiently increase the reaction threshold, resulting in tolerance during the challenge but reactions after subsequent full-dose exposure (“rapid desensitization” phenomenon).27 In our study, the relatively low false-negative rate may partly be explained by our institutional protocol, in which patients with equivocal symptoms routinely undergo next-day single-dose re-exposure under medical supervision.

This study provides valuable insights into the clinical characteristics and severity classification of anaphylactic and non-anaphylactic reactions in pediatric OFCs. A comparison of different severity grading systems revealed significant variability, particularly in the classification of gastrointestinal manifestations. One of the main strengths of our study is the large number of oral food challenges performed. Furthermore, although the study is retrospective, the documentation was carried out prospectively and with a standardized approach, ensuring high data quality and consistency. Our study had some limitations. As this was a retrospective study, some data may have been missing from certain patients' medical records. Additionally, despite their ease of use and validity in daily practice, the choice of WAO and ICD-11 classifications as references could potentially introduce bias. Furthermore, the limited number of patients with neurological symptoms prevented a more detailed examination of the inconsistencies in the severity scoring of these symptoms. Finally, as our study had no fatal outcomes, we could not contribute to the debate on whether death should be classified as a separate severity level.

Our study demonstrated that the incidence of anaphylaxis increased with age, highlighting the need for caution during OFCs in older children. When the WAO reference classification was applied, the EAACI system exhibited the highest specificity for identifying anaphylaxis. Nevertheless, there were considerable differences among severity classifications, and our real-life data specifically underscored the role of gastrointestinal symptoms in these discrepancies. Future research should focus on developing a standardized global severity classification system that addresses the current inconsistencies in grading gastrointestinal involvement, enhances diagnostic accuracy, and guides appropriate therapeutic strategies for the management of food allergies.
