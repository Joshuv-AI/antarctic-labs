 1 INTRODUCTION

 The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) has been actively developing and advancing the
 concept of automated mobility districts (AMDs) as a pathway to the future of transportation mobility within
 dense urban settings and major activity centers (Young & Lott 2024). As a U.S. Department of Energy research
 and development center, NREL studies the mobility trends by which the convergence of automation,
 electrification, and on-demand services will enable resilient and productive urban environments through the
 concept of AMDs.

 Central to the AMD concept is the operation of multiple autonomous vehicle (AV) technologies with
 vehicles of different sizes deployed to provide different service modes, all within the integrated operation of
 the multiple fleets of different vehicles. Figure 1 illustrates this range of vehicle technologies and operating
 modes the authors believe will flourish in the next five to ten years as AMDs come into existence. In this
 scenario, fleets small and medium-sized vehicles provide on-demand service for single-travel party “private
 rides,” and medium to large vehicles are dispatched on-demand to service multiple travel party “shared rides”
 (i.e., shared with individual strangers or two more parties unknown to each other). These demand-responsive
 service modes will be combined into a comprehensive transportation mobility system that will align with fixed-
 route bus service and regional high-capacity transit service, as well as pedestrian and micromobility (e.g.,
 bicycle, scooter, etc.) modes. Within the AMD or at its perimeter, all services will typically converge for
 regional access and first-/last-mile intermodal connections at designated mobility hubs.

 This comprehensive automated mobility transportation environment, comprising a “system of
 systems” – that is a balanced and integrated approached with various vehicle fleets, vehicle sizes, service
 modes, and technologies - will provide a high service level throughout the urban district or other major activity
 centers, such as university campuses, airport terminal complexes, or large medical complexes. And when
 combined with the dominant trend toward deploying AV fleets with electric propulsion vehicles, the energy
 efficiency benefits and reduced environmental impacts can offer a highly resilient transportation model.

 The authors are studying the factors that will determine the nature and configuration of the command-
 and-control systems and the associated facilities for the range of types, sizes, and complexities of AMDs they
 envision. This work is part of NREL’s Phase IV research of the anticipated implementation challenges for
 large-scale, multiple-fleet, multimodal AMDs in the coming years. The concepts discussed in this paper explore
 the authors’ initial investigations, currently underway, which will frame the ongoing study of human
 supervision of and interactions with fully automated systems comprising future AMDs.

 2 HUMAN SUPERVISION OF AUTOMATED SYSTEM OPERATIONS

 The authors have, through early research, advanced a view of automated fleet operational deployment
 that has compared the control system’s functional subsystems with those of automated guideway transit
 technology utilizing communications-based train control (CBTC) (Lott et al. 2021). These functional control
 subsystems (represented in Figure 2), comprising the combination of vehicular onboard controls, roadway
 infrastructure control elements, and the operations control center command and control functions, are
 integrated into the complete fleet operations control system identified in the diagram as the Automatic Fleet
 Operations System. These subsystems include both on-vehicle and off-vehicle hardware and software, with
 communications systems tying everything together.

 As indicated in the Figure 2, the focus of this paper is the Automatic Supervisory Dispatch and Control
 (ASDC) subsystem. This corresponds to the Automatic Train Supervision subsystem in the classic CBTC
 automated train control system.

 2

 Figure 2. Definition of AV automated fleet operations control system and subsystems based on the
 functionally equivalent automatic train control subsystems for a fully automated transit system with
 driverless and unattended trains.

 In technical literature and press coverage a large amount of attention is paid to the onboard automated
 driving system (ADS), which aims to replace a human driver with its array of sensors, machine intelligence,
 and means of knowing precisely where it is in the roadway network. But as essential as the ADS is, by which
 most of the functional aspects of driving and safety are performed in each vehicle, the complete automated
 fleet operations (AFO) system must also include the human-to-machine interface with operations personnel in
 the facility that is referred herein as the operations control center (OCC).

 Figure 3 illustrates how the automatic ASDC subsystem incorporates a fully integrated OCC. Within
 this OCC, operations personnel continually monitor and occasionally interact with various elements within the
 automatic fleet operations control system as individual vehicles are dispatched in response to passenger
 activity. This is referred to as management by exception, in that personnel are present to address issues that
 arise that are outside the fully automated systems boundaries — whether derived from anomalies of
 environments, failures of equipment, or needs of riders. Of particular importance is the role of OCC personnel
 providing a human interface with passengers by responding to passenger inquiries via intercom or video links
 within stations and in the vehicles. Both aspects of human supervision and oversight of the fully automated
 passenger transport system are discussed below.

 The continuous monitoring of AV fleet operations is typically facilitated by other automated features
 that continuously report key performance indicators for the fleet to OCC operations personnel. This monitoring
 is also applicable for individual vehicles when conditions warrant closer scrutiny, such as when an OCC
 operator makes an inquiry for this information at an operations workstation or when preset thresholds or
 conditions create a warning flag for operations to address or when a rider requests assistance. When judgment
 is made by operations personnel that some type of change should be initiated within the normal parameters
 affecting vehicle dispatch, the operator’s control functions typically allow their adjustment of a selected set of
 command-and-control functions. Examples of this intervention would be an action to hold a particular vehicle
 at a given location for a period, or a decision made by OCC personnel to remove a specific vehicle from service
 and dispatch it to a maintenance facility for special attention.

 Other aspects of human intervention may involve actions taken by personnel within the OCC to
 resolve critical operational issues of a specific AV in real time. These interactions are called “remote
 assistance” and are discussed below in further detail.

 3 STRATEGIC LOCATION OF OPERATIONS CONTROL CENTER FACILITIES
 The placement of the OCC facilities is traditionally in a location convenient to the main service area
 of the AMD. This is an important consideration when preparing for the ultimate expansion of the AMD to a
 large scale, since the OCC involves the management tasks of the primary supervisory functions of the fully
 automated fleet operations. However, there have been conditions in the early R&D and demonstration pilot
 phases of relatively small-scale AV transport system deployments in which the primary OCC has been at a
 location remote from the actual deployment site, perhaps located in a distant city in which one OCC manages
 multiple AMD deployment through integrated electronic communications. Typically, the circumstances under
 which remote locations have been incorporated involved a relatively small fleet size of a dozen or fewer AVs
 in service and the presence of operations personnel onboard each of the vehicles (as a safety driver or attendant)
 as the vehicles were engaged in passenger service.
 An example of a remotely located OCC was during the deployment of the EasyMile shuttles on the
 Texas Southern University (TSU) campus during 2019–2020. The single shuttle shown in Figure 4 was
 monitored from the EasyMile headquarters in France, with only the local operations staff on-site at the TSU
 campus onboard each vehicle as it was in operation along Tiger Walk within the TSU campus in Houston,
 Texas.
 In contrast with the remote OCC approach described previously, Figure 5 shows the placement of the
 Autonomous Innovation Center facility for the new Jacksonville Transportation Authority’s (JTA’s) Ultimate
 Urban Circulator (U2C). The location of this OCC facility is in the center of the operational service area, as the
 complete U2C system will ultimately be implemented−the scale of which is depicted in the figure graphic.
 Personnel at this facility are currently monitoring and acting to adjust the system performance and operations
 for the Phase 1 Bay Street Innovation Corridor, called the NAVI transport service, which initiated automated
 passenger service in June 2025.

 With reference to these two examples, the NREL AMD research is investigating the factors
 determining the OCC location. The possible combination of both on-site and remote OCC locations is
 anticipated under some circumstances, when factors such as the economics of skilled labor cost and availability
 at a given site are considered.
 If the actions taken by the OCC personnel do not require a physical human presence, human assistance
 to the automated systems could be effectively and efficiently executed by activating automated processes

 and/or notifying other personnel located on-site, who could directly intervene. The authors have encountered
 examples of these factors in traffic management operations centers and from fixed guideway OCCs. Many
 traffic management centers are co-located with emergency dispatch for coordination and communications
 within the region of which they serve, though during off-peak, overnight periods it is common for these
 functions to shift to a state-wide center. As the ‘feet-on-the-ground’ response is handled through either
 emergency personnel or mobile operations personnel, remote location is not uncommon. For traditional
 automated people mover systems, typically rail based and frequently encountered at large airports, the OCC is
 local, allowing personnel to quickly travel to location of any anomaly to assist with passenger management
 and system recovery procedures. Current trends in the AV industry indicate remote OCCs for large AMDs
 could be common going forward because of the prevalence of high-speed, reliable telecommunications
 networks, although on-site OCC has the advantage of human presence when absolutely required. The balance
 of remote versus on-site OCC facilities has yet to be determined.

 4 CO-LOCATION OF OCC FOR DIFFERENT AV TECHNOLOGY FLEETS IN A SHARED FACILITY
 AT THE AMD LOCAL SITE
 Co-located OCCs of various fleets, each with different AV technology and different passenger service
 modes, into a single facility with oversight by the AMD management authority, is an approach proposed in
 prior published papers (Lott & Young 2021). This is similar to traffic management centers, 911 call centers,
 and emergency dispatch having co-located centers within a metropolitan area—where the impacts of any one
 situation may impact many subsystems, the co-location of central oversight management presents synergistic
 benefits, and initial incident detection from any of the sub-systems provides direct input to the others.
 An integrated OCC would encompass different vehicle technologies of different sizes providing an
 interconnected mobility service (e.g., one fleet for shared-ride on-demand service connecting at specific
 locations with a different technology fleet providing larger capacity fixed route service). The AMD
 management authority may host a single district-level management entity to oversee the multi-fleet operations,
 or the facility could be provided for each of the different fleet operators to co-locate their OCCs at a single
 location. In such a co-located OCC facility, there could still be other operations offices within the larger district
 that operate specifically under the auspices of the individual fleet operators. This is consistent with the model
 found in the placement of the primary OCCs in large, automated fixed-guideway systems, which are located
 within the service area of the system but also complemented with small operations command centers/offices
 that are in proximity to different localized areas of service within the AMD.
 For example, within a large medical district that encompasses a dozen major hospitals and outpatient
 centers, several geographical areas at opposite ends of the large campus may have field support offices from
 which operations personally can stage and be prepared to respond to the need of any individual vehicle
 operating in the immediate area, when a presence of a representative is necessary. Such an operations office
 could be effective for the fleet operator of a given small AV technology providing on-demand service for single
 party travelers. Operations personnel could be quickly dispatched either to observe and interpret the conditions
 during problems or incidents, or to physically intervene in the automated operations to take manual control of
 a vehicle to drive it out of complex conditions in which it is unable to operate under automated or remote
 operation.
 The direct involvement of the AMD management authority in the large-scale operations across all
 fleets may also be a factor in the day-to-day affairs of the shared OCC—including police and/or fire emergency
 operations or short-term construction activities impacting normal fleet operations. Emergency situations to be
 considered include events within the AMD’s operational domains (such as a failure of a vehicle blocking a
 critically important arterial street, or a fire at power substation) or events completely external to the AMD (such
 as major weather events that impact many vehicles, whether automated or otherwise). When these types of
 conditions occur, coordination with traditional emergency response will require an urgent and strategic
 response. This level of coordination across multiple public and private entities is best facilitated through a
 common operations center. Examples of this sharing of transportation operations centers with emergency
 management personnel are commonly found in roadway traffic management centers, such as the Houston
 Transtar facility managed by Texas Department of Transportation—a major traffic operations management
 center serving the entire metropolitan area of Houston, Texas. 1

 5 ROLE OF OCC PERSONNEL PROVIDING REMOTE ASSISTANCE TO AVS
 One of the key roles of operations personnel working in the fleet operations OCC is to perform human
 interactions with specific autonomous vehicles using the vehicle communications and control functions
 provided by the system-wide AFO system. As shown in Figure 3, the AFO is the umbrella command and
 control system for all subsystems and vehicles operating within the entire fleet for each independent vehicle

 7

 technology in service, including the human interfaces that occur within the automated supervisory and dispatch
 control functions.
 Within the AFO, the ASDC subsystem has the capability to provide remote assistance to fleet vehicles
 requiring assistance to navigate situations outside of their operational design domain. Through this human-
 facilitated assistance, the vehicle is allowed to maneuver through or around a roadway environment that would
 otherwise be against the safe driving rules and/or protocols of a specific technology’s ADS.
 An example of this permitted violation of the programmed operational criteria would be for an AV to
 move into the adjacent reverse directional flow lane to drive around an object of vehicle blocking progress
 through the normal travel lane of the AV fleet operations. The Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) Industry
 Technology Consortia, Automated Vehicle Safety Consortium (AVSC) has prepared a Best Practice industry
 guideline (SAE International 2023) that describes this human interaction:
 Remote assistance “is one approach that can help to address the challenges for operating ADS in
 such scenarios. The ability to provide timely remote support to vehicles can help overcome some
 situational challenges for the ADS to continue the trip, such as handling ambiguous scenarios.”
 One of the authors experienced this kind of operational incident while riding within an uncrewed AV
 operated by Cruise while in Austin, Texas, on June 16, 2023. The incident and the remote response of the OCC
 personnel to provide remote assistance to the stalled vehicle is documented in a published technical paper (Zhu,
 Lott & Young 2025). The successful remote assistance by OCC personnel provided to an AV stopped in an
 active traffic lane behind a line of illegally parked vehicles. The vehicle was “permitted” by the human
 intervention to move into the opposing directional traffic lane when the vehicle sensors detected there were no
 vehicles approaching from the opposite direction. It traveled several hundred feet around the blocking vehicles
 before moving back into the correct travel lane to continue its assigned trip.
 This example illustrates that even sophisticated AV technologies must have design provisions for
 human interaction with the vehicle by remote assistance that provides the vehicle permission to take exception
 to the programmed rules. There are indications from AV developers that different approaches to providing this
 remote assistance can be found within the ADS of the various AV technologies. One is to allow operations
 personnel in a remote OCC to take control of the vehicle and remotely “drive” it around the problem area or
 incident—a methodology often referred to a “tele-operation.” Another approach that has been described is to
 have the OCC personnel give the vehicle a series of “breadcrumbs,” as points along a permitted travel path by
 which the stalled vehicle can circumvent the conditions that have caused it to stop. As the industry matures,
 these various approaches and methodologies of providing remote assistance to individual AVs will be tested
 and refined. This vetting of the various means and methods of assistance will determine the criticality of other
 factors—such as command and control communications latency and the sufficiency of strictly the vehicle’s
 own sensing and perception equipment without other supporting systems such as intelligent roadway
 infrastructure.
 Another scenario that would typically involve human intervention would be a failure of a given
 vehicle’s ADS equipment or other supporting control system elements that bring the vehicle to a complete stop.
 These types of failures would require operations personnel to physically travel to a stalled vehicle, though these
 will likely be rare occurrences. However, with the expansion and scaling of fleet sizes in large-scale AMD
 deployments, these incidents could frequently occur, possibly even daily. When this type of failure occurs in
 an active travel lane, the fleet operations person would often need to manually move the AV to an appropriate
 stopping point that is out of the flow of traffic. For automated fixed guideway transit systems, this type of
 operations personnel is typically called “recovery personnel.” They are able to travel to any location within the
 automated transport system’s service area to address a stalled vehicle and move it if needed.

 5 PASSENGER COMMUNICATIONS FROM OCC
 Passenger communication in manned systems is typically handled by the driver or vehicle attendant.
 Use of electronic systems for announcing stops and other general travel information is also common, but any
 customized or ad hoc communication has typically been handled by local personnel—typically the driver. As
 AV systems remove the on-board presence of the driver, the communications function with the passenger,
 particularly for ad hoc communications, will become necessary in response to the passenger needs, or in
 situations where normal transport is disrupted. In these situations, prompt and clear communication with the
 passenger(s) is critical for safety and comfort.

 8

 Further, when multiple parties are riding within the same vehicle, the driver or attendant has also
 served as the authority figure for maintaining safety and security, as well as resolving any issues that may arise
 from multiparty travel. Addressing these functions in the absence of a physical driver or attendance is under
 the domain of the ASDC within the OCC. The functions that address these concerns may be digitally operated,
 or even remote, but the responsibility for oversight (ensuring it is carried out properly) remains within the
 ASDC purview.
 Communications functions are discussed in three areas—traveler initiated, operations induced, and
 multiparty oversight.
 Traveler-Initiated Ad Hoc Communication
 Traveler-initiated ad hoc communication may be as simple as the traveler asking for assistance with
 HVAC controls or advice on restaurants or as serious as notification of critical medical conditions (e.g., heart
 attack or stroke) or detection of malfunction (e.g., the smell of smoke). The simpler communication requests
 may be handled through customer service call buttons or even AI assistants (both of which are already
 appearing in industry practice).
 The need for direct emergency response is noted in other forms of automation such as elevators— in
 which it is standard practice and embedded in standards that some type of direct line-of-contact is required
 with someone capable of dispatching emergency response. So, too, in an autonomous vehicle the need for
 travelers to request and obtain direct support from operations personnel would be expected. The interface may
 start with a standard customer support call but would need to be escalated if the traveler indicated the issue
 was life threatening. Such support would ultimately be managed through the ASDC OCC personnel, as well
 as the necessary interaction with emergency response personnel (emergency medical personnel, firefighters,
 police) to address the situation.
 System-Induced Ad Hoc Communication
 OCC personnel are also responsible for ad hoc communication in response to system-induced changes.
 These may be as simple as a rerouting the vehicle because of an accident to as complex and urgent as needing
 to direct passengers to safety in the event of an imminent weather threat, fire along the route, or even large
 crowds, mobs, or active shooters. As with traveler-induced ad hoc communication, a portion of this may be
 managed through normal customer service or AI capabilities, but, particularly for any health-threatening
 emergency, direct contact with human personnel for direction is essential and falls to the ASDC OCC personnel
 responsibility.
 Multiparty Oversight
 Multiparty oversight refers to the discomfort and potential threat imposed when strangers share a
 confined space for an extended period. This first came to prominence when elevators operators were removed,
 and parties began to share building elevators, unsupervised, and without attendants. As a result, a study of
 proxemics ensued to characterize expectations and behaviors of unacquainted humans in proximity (Hall
 1966). The general observations of proxemics include:
 • Humans are comfortable with family and friends within 18 inches to 4 feet.
 • Casual and professional relationships are comfortable from 4 feet to 10 feet.
 • Typically, strangers are comfortable 10 feet or greater in proximity.
 Elevator rides, which are typically 1 minute or less, require unacquainted people to be in close
 proximity for short periods of times. In the case of elevators, even though riders may not be acquainted, being
 in a common building implies some form of shared purpose—either a common employer, residence, shopping
 preference, or generally need to access similar services—all of which lessens the aspect of a “complete
 stranger.”
 The advent of completely unattended robotic mobility services will further push the time boundaries
 of sharing a space among complete strangers with multi-party rides. Current micro-transit rides typically
 average 3 miles, so within urban areas, shared ridership (compulsory with most micro-transit services) typically
 requires strangers to share a common vehicle for 5 to 10 minutes on average. In these circumstances, the driver
 (or attendant) provides oversight and management of unfamiliar parties. On larger vehicles, such as buses and
 train cars, the presence of multiple parties (3 or more) also provides a form of “safety in crowds.” At the time
 of writing, no AV taxi or microtransit shuttle operation with unattended service has offered multiparty service
 on a large scale that provides significant experience with this aspect of human interaction for multiparty
 oversight functions.

 9

 The Morgantown, West Virginia, personal rapid transit (PRT) system, which began service in the late
 1970s, was one of the first to deal with the issue of personal safety. Its vehicles, which hold a maximum of 21
 people (including persons standing), are equivalent in size to a large van or short bus. As a PRT system, the
 vehicles transport individuals and small groups of travelers (typically students at West Virginia University)
 from one of its five stations to another with no intervening stop—functionally the essence of PRT. This mode
 of operation maximizes throughput during peak demand and minimizes user wait time. However, during off-
 peak periods, the operations of the system are changed for safety and comfort reasons. If the PRT operations
 mode were continued into the evening hours, a rider may be on the PRT vehicle with a stranger for a prolonged
 period—possibly presenting a threat. For this reason, soon after opening, the operation during off-peak hours
 was changed to function as traditional shuttle operations in which the vehicle stopped at each station,
 minimizing unsupervised time with a possible stranger, and providing opportunity to disembark at every
 station.
 Methods to provide security and oversight for multiparty rides are yet to be fully established, but
 methods (available from parallel disciplines) include:
 • Noncompulsory ride-sharing
 • Larger, roomier vehicles
 • Physical partitions, separating seating areas within vehicles
 • Social profiles created by riders for digital trust similar to social media provides (unprecedented for
 public transit modes)
 • Education—use of escape/control button/mechanism to end ride early
 • Digital third-party presence or virtual third-party presence
 • Conspicuous monitoring through video and audio (“You are being watched”)
 • Multiple party service—encourage multiparty (>2) ride-sharing (safety in a crowd)
 o Two parties may be uncomfortable, three or more provide “crowd safety”
 • Additional AI solutions that project both surveillance as well as security.
 6 CONCLUSIONS
 Advances in AV technology and expanded operations by early developers set the stage for AMD
 deployment in major global cities in the near to medium term. NREL’s AMD research has reached the
 following conclusions about the systems, essential functions, and optimal configuration of supervisory systems
 and facilities needed to support an AMD, and in particular the role of humans in large AV fleet operations and
 passenger communications.
 1. Future automated transport fleets operating within AMDs will need on-site or near-site
 command and control facilities. The authors envision that OCCs will manage various vehicle
 types, sizes, and operational modes, including on-demand, fixed route service, connecting to
 large transport vehicle services by which these AMDs are accessible from the edges of the
 surrounding metropolitan area.
 2. An OCC serves as an integrated command and control framework through which vehicle-fleet
 systems operating in the AMD are managed. Within the OCC, human operators can monitor a
 range of situations from overall fleet activity at the system level to the automated dispatch of
 individual vehicles in response to passenger demands. It is this facility where the main interface
 between human operators, the automated system, and passengers occurs, as well as between
 fleets of various automated vehicles. OCC operators can provide remote assistance to individual
 AVs, which become “stuck” within unexpected traffic operating situations to provide either
 human driving of the vehicle or human guidance to the ADS through or around the problem.
 When co-located within an operational area, an OCC can coordinate ASDC subsystem functions
 to enable real-time human oversight and better coordination of transport services.
 3. Direct involvement of the AMD management authority in the daily multifleet OCC operations
 helps integrate roadway traffic management (i.e., both AV and conventional nonautomated
 vehicle operations), as well as construction management, inclement weather response, and
 emergency responder/police support services.
 4. Operations personnel within the OCC are also responsible for providing audio and video
 communications with transport system passengers onboard vehicles to assist them in ways
 previously accomplished by the human driver or attendant onboard the transport vehicle.
 Typical conditions warranting these operator-to-passenger communications can be characterized

 10

 as (1) traveler-initiated ad hoc communications, (2) system-induced ad hoc communications, (3)
 multi-party oversight (ensuring passengers feel safe and secure).
 Societal norms for multi-party large capacity unattended vehicle operation exist, having precedent in train-
 based automated people mover systems, however multi-party small-vehicle automation has little precedent
 short of elevator operations which are typically limited in time duration with multiple unacquainted parties
 within a common building. This latter area remains a new frontier for society and will require additional
 focus in future research. Future AMD research will monitor and report best practice with respect to the
 functional implementations of OCCs, as well as passenger interfaces. Future research will characterize
 critical data exchange between AV fleets and broader municipal systems for safe and efficient AMD
 operation.
