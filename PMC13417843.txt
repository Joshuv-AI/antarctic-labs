Thrombosis in rare bleeding disorders: a bleeding phenotype does not fully preclude thrombotic risk- frequency, clinical contexts, and management in a large Iranian cohort
Authors: Majid Naderi, Saeedeh Yaghoubi, Sina Rajaee, Ilia Mirzaei, Shadi Tabibian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBleeding is the dominant clinical feature of rare bleeding disorders (RBDs), yet thrombotic events have occasionally been reported. Available evidence is limited and mostly based on case reports. Thrombotic events tend to occur in high-risk clinical contexts and may be influenced by replacement therapy and coexisting risk factors. We aimed to estimate the frequency of imaging-confirmed thrombosis and describe associated clinical contexts and potential predisposing factors in an unselected RBD cohort.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study was conducted on all patients with confirmed diagnoses of RBDs, including rare coagulation factor deficiencies, von Willebrand disease, and inherited platelet function disorders, under care at our center between January and December 2025. Data were extracted from medical and laboratory records and imaging archives. Variables included age, sex, type and severity of deficiency, comorbidities, prothrombotic triggers (e.g., pregnancy, surgery, infection, and immobility), exposure to replacement therapy, and thrombophilia profile. Thrombotic events were verified through imaging.ResultsAmong 1,251 patients (682 FXIII, 127 FVII, 130 FV, 23 FX, 12 FII, 28 afibrinogenemia, 131 von Willebrand disease, 62 Glanzmann thrombasthenia, 26 FXI deficiency, 19 combined FV and FVIII deficiency, and 11 Bernard-Soulier syndrome), 12 (0.96%; 95% CI, 0.50–1.67%) had at least one lifetime imaging-confirmed thrombotic event. Events were exclusively venous. Events occurred mainly as deep vein thrombosis (50%) or cerebral venous thrombosis (41.7%), with other sites less common, most often in high-risk contexts such as pregnancy (4 cases), surgery, infection, or exposure to prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC). Thrombosis was more frequent in afibrinogenemia (7.1%) and FII deficiency (8.3%) than in FXIII deficiency (0.7%), although interpretation is limited by small numbers. In a sensitivity analysis excluding events associated with pregnancy, surgery, and exposure PCC, the proportion decreased to 0.48% (6/1,251; 95% CI, 0.18–1.04%). Thrombotic events were treated with therapeutic anticoagulation, most commonly initial low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) with transition to a Direct Oral Anticoagulant (DOAC) in selected non-pregnant adult patients; when replacement therapy was required, dosing was individualized to maintain minimum effective hemostatic levels and avoid full factor normalization.ConclusionsThrombosis in RBDs was rare and largely associated with major transient high-risk contexts and/or treatment exposure. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to improve the evidence base for clinical decision making regarding thrombotic risks in this vulnerable population.

Introduction

Rare bleeding disorders (RBDs) comprise a heterogeneous group of inherited hemostatic defects, including rare coagulation factor deficiencies (such as quantitative or qualitative fibrinogen disorders and deficiencies of prothrombin [FII] and factors (F) V, combined FV and FVIII, VII, X, XI, and XIII), as well as von Willebrand disease (VWD) and inherited platelet function disorders. Although VWD, particularly type 1, is common in population-based estimates (prevalence up to 1%), its prevalence in referral-based and tertiary care settings is substantially lower, ranging from 0.3 to 16.5 per 100,000, which is comparable to other rare bleeding disorders. Our cohort, derived from a specialized referral center, reflects this selected population and includes all three VWD subtypes (type 1, n = 84; type 2, n = 28; type 3, n = 19), with type 1 patients representing individuals with clinically significant bleeding requiring specialist care. Collectively, RBDs are far less prevalent than hemophilia A or B, with reported prevalence estimates ranging from approximately 1:500,000 to 1:2,000,000 [1, 2]. Most RBDs follow an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern and are more frequently encountered in populations with higher rates of consanguinity [3–5]. In contrast, fibrinogen disorders show heterogeneous inheritance: afibrinogenemia and hypofibrinogenemia are usually autosomal recessive, whereas dysfibrinogenemia is most often autosomal dominant. FXI deficiency is classically autosomal recessive, with variable penetrance; bleeding may also occur in heterozygotes. Bleeding manifestations in RBDs span a wide clinical spectrum, ranging from mild mucocutaneous bleeding to life-threatening events such as intracranial hemorrhage, depending on the specific defect and residual factor activity level [4, 6]. Despite bleeding being the hallmark of RBDs, sporadic thrombotic events, more commonly venous than arterial, have been reported, particularly in afibrinogenemia and, less consistently, in FXI or FVII deficiency. Thrombosis in RBDs appears to occur predominantly in recognizable high-risk settings, including pregnancy and the postpartum period, surgery, infection, immobility, and exposure to replacement products such as prothrombin complex concentrate or recombinant activated factor VII [7, 8]. However, available evidence is largely restricted to case reports and small series, and no large unselected cohort has systematically estimated the frequency of objectively confirmed thrombotic events across multiple RBD subtypes or described the associated predisposing factors. As a result, the true frequency of thrombosis in routine RBD care and the extent to which events cluster in these high-risk settings remain poorly characterized [9, 10]. We therefore aimed to estimate the proportion of patients with imaging-confirmed thrombosis in a large unselected RBD cohort, and to describe the associated high-risk settings, potential predisposing factors, and exposure to replacement therapy. This study presents descriptive data on the frequency and clinical characteristics of thrombotic events in RBDs.

Materials and methods

Study design and diagnostic criteria

This single-center retrospective observational study was conducted at a referral center in Zahedan, southeastern Iran, from 1 January to 31 December 2025, corresponding to the period of data abstraction and record review from the institutional registry and archives. Our institution serves as the provincial referral center for inherited bleeding disorders in Sistan and Baluchestan located in Southeast of Iran, and the cohort therefore reflects an unselected referral population from this region. Data were extracted exclusively from medical records, laboratory databases, and imaging archives; no patient interviews or self-reported histories were used at any stage, substantially reducing classical sources of recall bias by design. Thrombotic events were identified based on the complete available clinical history irrespective of time of occurrence, and confirmed by objective imaging, specifically compression Doppler ultrasound for deep vein thrombosis and hepatic vein thrombosis, and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)/Magnetic Resonance Venography (MRV) for cerebral venous thrombosis. Accordingly, the reported proportion of 0.96% represents a cumulative lifetime prevalence of imaging-confirmed thrombosis among registered patients, not an incidence rate limited to the 2025 data abstraction period.

Coagulation factors were measured using one-stage clotting assays on the Sysmex CS-2500 system. Functional fibrinogen was assessed by the Clauss method, and FXIII activity by chromogenic assay. VWD was diagnosed based on VWF antigen level and ristocetin cofactor activity. Platelet function disorders were diagnosed by platelet aggregation studies using multiple agonists, including ristocetin, arachidonic acid, ADP, and collagen, confirmed by flow cytometry; abnormal results were repeated when clinically indicated. Genetic testing was used as supportive evidence where available; in FXIII deficiency, genetic confirmation was available in the majority of patients; facilitated by the high prevalence of the regional founder variant (F13A Trp187Arg) [11]. Deficiency severity was classified as severe (< 1%), moderate (1–5%), and mild (> 5%); afibrinogenemia was defined as undetectable functional fibrinogen by the Clauss method. For FXIII deficiency specifically, severity was further classified as severe (< 1%), moderate (1–30%), and mild (> 30%), consistent with published criteria [12].

Participants

All 1,251 patients with confirmed RBDs registered and followed at our center were identified through the institutional clinical database. We included all consecutively followed patients during the study period, regardless of whether they required active treatment. Patients managed exclusively at other institutions without referral to our center may not have been captured in our records, representing a potential source of underestimation of thrombotic events. Furthermore, patients included in the cohort who experienced thrombosis but were treated at external facilities may have been misclassified as non-thrombotic if such events were not documented in our institutional records. Medical records and imaging archives were reviewed to identify objectively imaging-confirmed thrombotic events. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Zahedan University of Medical Sciences. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were a confirmed diagnosis of RBDs defined by at least one coagulation factor activity level below the assay-specific range on a validated assay or abnormal platelet aggregation tests confirmed by flow cytometry, with supportive genetic testing when available. All patients across the full spectrum of deficiency severity (mild, moderate, and severe) were included and systematically screened for thrombotic events regardless of severity or treatment intensity. Patients with acquired coagulopathies (e.g., liver failure, vitamin K antagonists, and disseminated intravascular coagulation) and acquired factor deficiencies were excluded. Concomitant medication use, including oral contraceptive pills (OCP) or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), was not used as an exclusion criterion; however, none of the 12 patients with thrombotic events had a documented history of such use at the time of their event.

Exposures and covariates

From medical records we extracted age, sex, RBD type, factor activity level, comorbidities (e.g., epilepsy, varicose veins), and potential prothrombotic contexts (pregnancy/puerperium, surgery/immobility, acute infection, obesity, estrogen exposure), as well as exposure to replacement therapy and thrombophilia testing results. Thrombophilia screening was performed in all 12 patients with thrombotic events and included factor V Leiden mutation, prothrombin G20210A mutation, protein C deficiency, protein S deficiency, and antithrombin deficiency. For patients with thrombosis, we additionally collected event type and site, management strategy, and outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Thrombotic events were summarized as counts and percentages, overall and by RBD type, with 95% confidence intervals calculated using the exact binomial method. Continuous variables are presented as median (IQR) or mean (SD), depending on distribution. Given the rarity of thrombotic events (n = 12), formal statistical comparisons between subgroups were not performed due to insufficient statistical power; analyses were therefore exclusively descriptive. A sensitivity analysis excluded events occurring in major transient high-risk contexts (pregnancy, surgery, and PCC exposure) to estimate the proportion of less-provoked events. Because follow-up time was not uniformly available across all registered patients, we report the proportion with imaging-confirmed thrombosis rather than an incidence rate per patient-year. Analyses were performed using R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [13].

Results

Among the 1,251 patients with confirmed RBDs, 682 had FXIII deficiency (54.5%; severe 75%, moderate 15%, mild 10%), 131 had von Willebrand disease (10.5%; type 1: n = 84, type 2: n = 28, type 3: n = 19), 130 had FV deficiency (10.4%; severe 40%, moderate 30%, mild 30%), 127 had FVII deficiency (10.2%; severe 19.7%, moderate 19.7%, mild 60.6%), 62 had Glanzmann thrombasthenia (5.0%), 28 had afibrinogenemia (2.2%), 26 had FXI deficiency (2.1%; severe 30.8%, moderate 30.8%, mild 38.5%), 23 had FX deficiency (1.8%; severe 52.2%, moderate 30.4%, mild 17.4%), 19 had combined FV and FVIII deficiency (1.5%), 12 had FII deficiency (1.0%; severe 66.7%, moderate 16.7%, mild 16.7%), and 11 had Bernard-Soulier syndrome (0.9%). The cohort comprised 738 male (59%) and 513 female (41%) patients, with a mean age of 15.9 ± 11.6 years and a median age of 13 years (range 1–63 years). All patients with thrombosis had severe deficiency of their respective coagulation factor, with the exception of the patient with FVII deficiency (activity 12%) and the patient with FII deficiency (activity 15%), who had moderate deficiency levels.

Within this cohort, 12 patients (0.96%; 95% CI, 0.50–1.67%) had experienced at least one thrombotic event. Ages of thrombotic patients ranged from 3 months to 52 years; eight were female and four were male. No arterial thrombotic events were observed, and one patient experienced two thrombotic episodes. Thrombotic events occurred in 5 of 682 patients with FXIII deficiency (0.73%), 2 of 130 with FV deficiency (1.54%), 2 of 28 with afibrinogenemia (7.14%), 1 of 23 with FX deficiency (4.35%), 1 of 12 with FII deficiency (8.33%), and 1 of 127 with FVII deficiency (0.79%). The proportion of patients with thrombosis was higher in afibrinogenemia and FII deficiency than in FXIII deficiency; however, these observations rely on small numbers and may not reflect true differences in risk. Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) occurred in six cases (50.0%), cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT) in five cases (41.7%), and hepatic vein thrombosis (HVT) in one case (8.3%). Among patients with thrombosis, comorbidities included seizure disorder/epilepsy in five patients and varicose veins in two patients; the remaining patients had no recorded comorbidity. Pregnancy-associated DVTs occurred in three women with FXIII deficiency, all in the second trimester. A pregnancy-associated CVT occurred in a woman with FV deficiency in the third trimester. In a 2-year-old child with afibrinogenemia, hepatic vein thrombosis developed following gastroenteritis. One CVT occurred shortly after exposure to PCC in a patient with FX deficiency, suggesting a possible temporal association between PCC exposure and thrombosis in this high-risk clinical setting. One patient with FXIII deficiency and varicose veins developed DVT following surgery (Table 1).

In a sensitivity analysis excluding pregnancy-associated events (n = 4), surgery-associated events (n = 1), and the single PCC-temporally related event (n = 1), the lifetime proportion of thrombosis decreased to 0.48% (6/1,251; 95% CI, 0.18–1.04%). Patients with FXIII deficiency were receiving regular prophylactic FXIII concentrate (Fibrogammin®) at the time of their thrombotic event, consistent with standard prophylaxis practice for severe FXIII deficiency. All other patients received episodic replacement therapy administered in response to the thrombotic event or in the context of the associated high-risk situation rather than as scheduled prophylaxis. Anticoagulation was initiated with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) during the acute phase, with transition to a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) in selected non-pregnant adult patients, for a total duration of 3–9 months depending on the individual clinical context. Replacement therapy was administered conservatively, targeting minimum effective hemostatic levels while avoiding full factor normalization, based on institutional protocol and available published evidence. Factor levels were monitored using one-stage clotting assays for most deficiencies, with the exception of FXIII activity which was measured by chromogenic assay. Monitoring was performed at baseline, within 30–60 min post-infusion (peak), and at trough (immediately before the next dose), with frequency adjusted according to clinical context and deficiency type. Individualized targets for each deficiency are detailed in Table 1. Short-term outcomes, including recurrent thrombosis, major bleeding, and death, were assessed until completion of the anticoagulation course. All 12 patients completed their planned anticoagulation course without interruption, achieved full clinical recovery, and experienced no recurrent thrombotic events, no major bleeding complications, and no deaths. These observations should be interpreted cautiously given the small numbers in several subgroups and the absence of formal statistical comparisons. Discussion

In this large unselected cohort of 1,251 patients with confirmed rare bleeding disorders, objectively confirmed thrombotic events were uncommon, occurring in 12 patients (0.96%; 95% CI 0.50–1.67%). All events were venous; no arterial thromboses were identified, consistent with previously published reviews demonstrating a predominance of venous over arterial thrombotic events in rare congenital bleeding disorders [7, 8]. DVT and CVT predominated, accounting for 50% and 41.7% of events respectively. The majority of events occurred in identifiable high-risk contexts, including pregnancy, surgery, acute infection, and exposure to PCC. In the sensitivity analysis, exclusion of events associated with pregnancy, surgery, or PCC exposure reduced the proportion of patients with thrombosis from 0.96% to 0.48%. These findings suggest that thrombosis in RBDs is rare and predominantly associated with well-recognized general VTE risk factors rather than with the bleeding disorder phenotype per se. Our retrospective data cannot determine whether RBD itself or replacement therapy adds to the thrombotic risk associated with these standard risk factors. Prospective controlled studies are needed to clarify this [14–17]. An important interpretive question is whether the 0.48% thrombosis proportion remaining after excluding major high-risk contexts is higher, lower, or comparable to the background thrombotic risk in the general population. Direct numerical comparison is not straightforward, as our estimate is a lifetime proportion from a retrospective cohort without uniform person-time follow-up, whereas general population data are typically expressed as incidence rates per 1,000 person-years (approximately 1–2 per 1,000 person-years overall). A qualitative comparison suggests that the overall thrombosis burden in our relatively young cohort does not appear substantially higher than general population expectations, raising the possibility that RBD status alone, in the absence of major triggers, may not meaningfully elevate thrombotic risk. However, this interpretation must remain cautious: our study lacks a non-RBD age-matched control group, which is particularly relevant given the relatively young median age of our cohort.

Among the 6 patients without a recorded pregnancy, surgical, or PCC-related trigger, events did not occur in the complete absence of identifiable risk factors, 3 had seizure disorder and 1 had varicose veins, both recognized contributors to thrombotic risk [14, 15]. Whether these conditions alone were sufficient, or whether an RBD-related mechanism, such as dysregulated thrombin generation or impaired fibrinolysis, contributed independently, cannot be determined from our retrospective data. Importantly, the triggers identified across our cohort, pregnancy, surgery, acute infection, and replacement product exposure, represent well-established VTE risk factors in the general population and are not specific to RBDs. Whether RBD patients are inherently more susceptible to these triggers than non-RBD individuals cannot be determined, as we lack a concurrent control group. It is possible that underlying coagulation imbalances lower the threshold at which standard triggers produce clinically evident thrombosis; however, this hypothesis remains speculative and is not directly testable from our cohort-level data. Prospective studies comparing RBD patients and non-RBD controls exposed to similar high-risk contexts would be required to address this question.

Five of the 12 patients with thrombosis had FXIII deficiency, reflecting the distinctive regional epidemiology of southeast Iran. Sistan and Baluchestan province has the highest documented prevalence of FXIII deficiency worldwide, driven by high rates of consanguineous marriages and a founder effect attributable to the Trp187Arg mutation (F13A c.562T > C). According to World Federation of Hemophilia (WFH) annual reporting data, 682 of the 876 known FXIII-deficient patients in Iran (78%) are followed at our center, confirming that our cohort represents the large majority of the known national FXIII-deficient population rather than the national or international RBD distribution [18].

The predominance of FXIII deficiency in our cohort, reflecting the unique regional epidemiology of southeastern Iran, limits the generalizability of our overall thrombosis proportion to other RBD populations. Centers serving predominantly VWD, FVII, or FV-deficient patients may observe different thrombotic frequencies and patterns. Our cohort-level estimate of 0.96% should therefore be interpreted in the context of this specific referral population rather than as a universal estimate applicable to all RBD cohorts.

The published evidence on thrombosis in FXIII deficiency remains largely limited to case reports. Almeida et al. reported DVT as a complicating event during management of intracranial hemorrhage in a 3-year-old with severe FXIII deficiency, highlighting the clinical complexity of managing concurrent hemostatic and thrombotic risk in a single patient [19]. Bounaix et al. described recurrent VTE managed with rivaroxaban combined with individualized FXIII replacement targeting trough levels of 50% during loading and 30% during maintenance, with no bleeding complications [20]. Similarly, van Vulpen et al. reported DVT followed by pulmonary embolism (PE) managed with apixaban alongside intensified FXIII prophylaxis, supporting a case-by-case approach balancing anticoagulation with replacement therapy [21]. In contrast to these published case reports describing recurrent VTE, the FXIII-associated events in our cohort mostly occurred in the presence of clear provoking factors, most notably pregnancy [22]. Based on the observed pattern, acknowledging the small number of events and absence of formal statistical testing, thrombosis in FXIII deficiency appeared to occur more frequently in the presence of recognizable co-triggers rather than as an isolated phenotype; this observation is hypothesis-generating only and requires confirmation in larger prospective studies [11, 23, 24].

Beyond FXIII deficiency, afibrinogenemia represents another RBD in which thrombosis can occur despite a severe bleeding tendency. Most available evidence comes from case reports, and publication bias is possible; however, reported events include both venous and arterial thromboses and often arise in recognizable high-risk situations or after exposure to replacement products. Cohort and case-based literature has described venous thromboses involving portal, renal, adrenal, and deep veins, often in association with cryoprecipitate therapy, surgical procedures, or trauma [25]. Mukaddam et al. reported extensive splanchnic thrombosis in a patient with afibrinogenemia carrying a homozygous FGB G434D missense mutation, with potential contributing factors including the specific fibrinogen variant and prior cryoprecipitate/FFP exposure [26]. Le Quellec et al. described afibrinogenemia with concomitant antithrombin deficiency and recurrent arterial and venous events, underscoring how coinherited thrombophilia can further amplify thrombotic risk in a bleeding disorder [27]. Hepatic vein thrombosis in afibrinogenemia, including pediatric Budd-Chiari syndrome, has also been reported [28, 29].

Mechanistically, the thrombotic paradox in afibrinogenemia is thought to arise primarily from impaired thrombin clearance: in the absence of fibrinogen, thrombin generated during coagulation cannot be incorporated into a fibrin clot and accumulates in the circulation, potentially activating platelets and driving further procoagulant activity. However, these mechanisms are generally insufficient to cause clinically evident thrombosis without a transient prothrombotic trigger — such as acute infection, surgery, pregnancy, or exposure to replacement products, that further amplifies the hemostatic imbalance [30]. Our cohort finding of hepatic vein thrombosis following gastroenteritis in an infant with afibrinogenemia, managed successfully with LMWH followed by a DOAC alongside conservative fibrinogen replacement targeting 50–100 mg/dL, illustrates that anticoagulation is feasible when replacement is carefully titrated to the minimum effective hemostatic level, targeting 50–100 mg/dL while avoiding supraphysiological fibrinogen levels, consistent with published recommendations for fibrinogen replacement in afibrinogenemia [25].

Congenital FVII deficiency is primarily recognized as a bleeding disorder, but published case reports demonstrate that thrombosis can occur even at very low FVII levels. Ramdass et al. described bilateral DVT and pulmonary embolism (PE) following hip replacement surgery in a patient with severe FVII deficiency (< 5%) who received perioperative rFVIIa, with no prior significant bleeding history [31]. Singh et al. reported an apparently unprovoked PE in a FVII-deficient patient without identifiable precipitating factors or replacement therapy exposure [32]. Kang et al. described a catastrophic postpartum PE, and Shyam et al. reported thrombotic complications in a patient with infective endocarditis and coinherited factor V Leiden, illustrating how acute illness and thrombophilia can outweigh the baseline bleeding tendency [33, 34]. In our cohort, thrombosis in FVII deficiency was uncommon (1/127; 0.79%), occurring in a 3-month-old infant without a clearly identified trigger. Together, these observations suggest that thrombotic events can occur in FVII deficiency, particularly when additional risk factors are present, and that the baseline bleeding phenotype should not be assumed to confer thrombotic protection.

A similar trigger-associated pattern was observed in FV and FII deficiency. In FV deficiency, Castoldi et al. described recurrent venous thromboses in severe FV deficiency with markedly increased thrombin generation and extreme APC resistance despite low FV levels, illustrating that FV deficiency can, in rare situations, coexist with a net prothrombotic profile [35]. Yap et al. described PE during pregnancy in a patient with combined FV and FVIII deficiency, highlighting pregnancy as a strong prothrombotic state even in the presence of baseline bleeding risk [36]. In FII deficiency, Watson et al. reported an unprovoked spontaneous femoral vein thrombosis in a woman with congenital FII deficiency without identifiable provoking factors or replacement therapy exposure, while Ding et al. described dysprothrombinaemia with thrombotic episodes in pregnancy and the postpartum period [37, 38]. In our cohort, thrombosis was uncommon in both FV deficiency (2/130; 1.54%) and FII deficiency (1/12; 8.33%), and events occurred predominantly in the presence of identifiable co-triggers. These observations are hypothesis-generating only and should not be used to support specific thromboprophylaxis recommendations; prospective studies are needed to determine whether RBD patients face meaningfully elevated thrombotic risk during high-risk periods.

In the absence of specific guidelines for thrombosis management in RBDs, the 12 patients in our cohort were treated using a pragmatic, case-by-case approach informed by the underlying factor deficiency, clinical context, and available evidence from published case reports. Anticoagulation was initiated with LMWH during the acute phase and transitioned to a DOAC for a total duration of 6–9 months in selected non-pregnant adult patients. Replacement therapy was administered conservatively, targeting the minimum hemostatic level required for safety rather than full factor normalization, to avoid creating an iatrogenic prothrombotic state, with individualized targets for each deficiency type as detailed in Table 1. The absence of major bleeding complications supports the feasibility of this approach, although the small number of treated patients and retrospective design preclude any formal efficacy or safety conclusions.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the retrospective single-center design precludes causal inference and true incidence rate calculation, as standardized follow-up time was not uniformly available; follow-up data were available only from the date of registration, meaning pre-registration thrombotic events may not have been captured. Second, thrombotic events were rare, limiting statistical precision and subgroup comparisons; wide confidence intervals for smaller subgroups should be interpreted with caution. Third, some events managed entirely outside our referral pathway may have been missed, as no systematic cross-checking with external hospitals was performed and inclusion was restricted to imaging-confirmed events; collectively, these factors support interpreting our estimate of 0.96% as a conservative lower bound. Fourth, replacement product dosing and timing details were not uniformly available across all patients. Thrombophilia testing was performed in all 12 patients with thrombotic events; however, it was not systematically performed in the non-thrombotic patient population, which limits comparison, leaving potential for incomplete data and limited data availability to affect interpretation. Fifth, information on concomitant medications potentially affecting hemostasis, such as oral contraceptives or NSAIDs, was not systematically recorded, representing a potential source of residual confounding; however, none of the 12 thrombotic patients had a documented history of such use at the time of their event. It should be noted that over-the-counter medication use, such as NSAIDS, may not be routinely captured in medical records, and therefore this finding should be interpreted with caution. Sixth, as data collection relied exclusively on documented records, recall bias was unlikely; any residual information bias would more likely result in underestimation of thrombotic events. Finally, the frequency of high-risk contexts among the 1,239 non-thrombotic patients was not systematically recorded, preventing calculation of context-specific thrombotic rates and limiting the strength of conclusions regarding the role of high-risk situations in driving thrombosis in RBDs.

Our findings provide a useful cohort-level estimate and a clear clinical signal: in this large unselected RBD cohort, objectively confirmed thrombosis was uncommon (0.96%) and exclusively venous, with DVT and CVT predominating. Many events occurred in identifiable high-risk contexts, supported by a lower estimate after excluding pregnancy-, surgery-, and PCC-associated events (0.48%; 95% CI 0.18–1.04%). These results demonstrate that thrombotic events in RBDs are rare and predominantly associated with well-recognized general VTE risk factors. While it is tempting to attribute these events to an interaction between RBD status and precipitating triggers, our data cannot exclude the alternative explanation that standard triggers alone were sufficient and that the RBD was coincidental. This distinction cannot be resolved from our retrospective observational data alone, and prospective multicenter studies with appropriate control groups would be required to determine whether RBD status independently modifies thrombotic risk and to inform management approaches in this population.
