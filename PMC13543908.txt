Assessing the pandemic potential of Nipah virus: From molecular pathogenesis to ecological spillover risks
Authors: Qikai Yin, Kai Nie, Shiwen Wang, Huanyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Nipah virus is a deadly zoonotic pathogen with pandemic potential.•Malaysian strain spreads via pigs; Bangladesh strain spreads directly between people.•Deforestation and climate change drive bats closer to human communities.•One Health approach integrates human, animal, and environmental health.•No licensed vaccines or treatments exist; global preparedness is urgent.

Introduction

Since its first documented outbreak in Malaysia and Singapore in 1998, Nipah virus (NiV) has emerged as one of the most lethal zoonotic pathogens known to humanity, with case‑fatality rates (CFRs) ranging from 40% to over 75% in subsequent outbreaks.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 As a member of the Henipavirus genus within the Paramyxoviridae family,6 NiV is classified as a biosafety level 4 (BSL‑4) agent and is designated by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a priority pathogen in its research and development (R&D) Blueprint.7 The virus’s capacity to induce severe respiratory distress and fatal necrotizing encephalitis,8, 9, 10 combined with the absence of licensed vaccines or therapeutics, underscores its profound threat to global health security.11

The natural reservoirs of NiV are fruit bats of the genus Pteropus,12 which harbor the virus in a subclinical state.13 Unlike many other paramyxoviruses, NiV exhibits an unusually broad host range, infecting a variety of mammals including pigs, horses, and humans.1,14 Cross‑species transmission is mediated by the highly conserved cellular receptors Ephrin‑B2 and Ephrin‑B3,15 which are abundantly expressed in vascular endothelial cells and neurons. This widespread receptor distribution accounts for the virus’s multi‑organ tropism and the devastating clinical manifestations observed in human infections.16,17

Epidemiological patterns of NiV have evolved markedly over the past two decades. The initial Malaysian outbreak (NiV‑M) was driven by land‑use changes and intermediate amplifying hosts (pigs),13,18 whereas subsequent outbreaks in Bangladesh and India (NiV‑B) have demonstrated a more direct spillover pathway via contaminated food sources,19,20 such as raw date‑palm sap.21,22 More alarmingly, NiV‑B strains have sustained human‑to‑human transmission, primarily through respiratory droplets and close contact.9 Under intensifying pressures from climate change and habitat fragmentation, spillover events at the human–animal interface are predicted to increase as bats migrate closer to human settlements.

Despite substantial advances in our understanding of NiV’s molecular biology including high‑resolution insights into its polymerase complex, a critical gap remains in translating basic research into effective clinical interventions and predictive risk models.23 This review synthesizes recent findings on NiV’s genomic architecture and molecular pathogenesis, analyzes the ecological drivers behind its expanding epidemiological footprint, and proposes a robust framework for assessing the risk of escalation from localized clusters to a global pandemic. By adopting a “One Health” perspective, the review underscores the necessity of integrated surveillance and the urgent development of medical countermeasures to avert a future NiV‑driven public‑health crisis. A thorough understanding of NiV’s transmission dynamics across diverse ecological and social contexts is therefore paramount. The following section delineates the distinct epidemiological patterns that have characterized NiV outbreaks across time and space.

Epidemiology and transmission dynamics

The epidemiology of NiV is defined by intricate zoonotic cycles and marked regional variation in transmission pathways.8 While the virus’s natural history is tightly coupled to its reservoir—Pteropus fruit bats-spillover mechanisms into human population exhibit remarkable flexibility.12,13

Natural reservoir and geographic distribution

Fruit bats (genus Pteropus), commonly known as flying foxes, are the definitive natural hosts of NiV.12,13 Serological and molecular evidence has identified NiV or NiV-like viruses in bat populations spanning from Southeast Asia to the South Pacific and as far west as the African coastline,24, 25, 26 indicating a potential risk zone far exceeding the areas of known human outbreaks.27, 28, 29, 30 In these hosts, the infection is typically subclinical, with viral shedding occurring intermittently through saliva, urine, and birthing fluids.31

The Malaysian model: intermediate host amplification

The 1998–1999 Malaysia outbreak established the classic “amplifier host” model of NiV transmission.1, 2, 3 In this scenario, land-use changes and the proximity of pig farms to fruit orchards facilitated the initial spillover from bats to domestic pigs.1 Pigs served as highly efficient amplifying hosts,32 developing severe respiratory illness and excreting high viral loads, which subsequently led to occupational exposure among farmworkers and abattoir personnel.1,9,13 Notably, human-to-human transmission was virtually absent during this period,33, 34, 35, 36 suggesting that NiV-M strains were primarily adapted for pig-to-human spillover.

The Bangladesh-India model: direct spillover and human-to-human transmission

Environmental and ecological drivers as risk amplifiers

The escalating frequency of NiV spillover is inextricably linked to anthropogenic environmental pressures that amplify both the probability and the geographic scope of cross-species transmission.42 Deforestation and El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO)-induced fruiting failures force Pteropus bats to forage in human-dominated landscapes, including cultivated fruit groves and peri‑urban areas. This landscape-scale shift in bat behavior increases contact frequency with humans and domestic animals,43 but more critically, it restructures the spatiotemporal risk landscape.14 These ecological perturbations do more than elevate spillover frequency; they fundamentally restructure the risk matrix through which NiV’s intrinsic pandemic potential is expressed44 (Table 2). Historical overview of human outbreaks

Since the first documented outbreak in Malaysia and Singapore in 1998–1999,1, 5 NiV has caused sporadic but highly lethal human infections across South and Southeast Asia.46 The initial outbreak strain, subsequently classified as NiV-Malaysia (NiV-M), has not re-emerged except for a distinct 2014 cluster in the Philippines, where 17 cases (9 deaths; CFR 53%) were linked to horse-to-human transmission with limited person-to-person spread.33, 41

All subsequent outbreaks have been attributed to the NiV-Bangladesh (NiV-B) lineage. Since 2001, near-annual outbreaks have occurred in Bangladesh and India,47, 48 resulting in hundreds of infections with case-fatality rates ranging from 40% to 100% (Table 3).21, 22, 49, 50 The first Indian outbreak in Siliguri (2001) remains the largest to date (66 cases, 49 deaths; CFR 74%).39 Notably, outbreaks have expanded geographically from West Bengal to Kerala since 2018—approximately 2000 km from the eastern focus—suggesting long-distance viral introduction or independent spillover events.51, 52, 53, 54 Epidemiological data consistently implicate Pteropus bats as the reservoir,12, Molecular pathogenesis

Genomic architecture

The virulence of NiV is rooted in its 18.2 kb single‑stranded, negative‑sense RNA genome,6 which adheres to the “rule of six” characteristic of the Paramyxoviridae family.56 The genome encodes six core structural proteins in the canonical order 3′‑N‑P‑M‑F‑G‑L‑5′.57 NiV’s high replication efficiency, coupled with sophisticated mechanisms of host‑cell entry and immune evasion, underpins its remarkable pathogenicity.58, 59, 60

Receptor recognition and cell entry

NiV entry is a coordinated, two‑step process involving the attachment glycoprotein (G) and the fusion protein (F).17 Unlike most paramyxoviruses that exploit sialic acid moieties, Nipah virus attachment glycoprotein specifically engages the highly conserved ephrin ligands Ephrin‑B2 and Ephrin‑B3 which are key regulators of cell‑cell signaling.15, 61, 62 Ephrin-B2 is predominantly expressed on vascular endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells,61, 62 while Ephrin-B3 is found in the central nervous system, particularly in the brainstem and hippocampus.15 This receptor distribution explains the systemic vasculitis and severe neurological manifestations observed clinically.16 Ligand binding induces a conformational change in Nipah virus attachment glycoprotein that activates the pH‑independent membrane fusion activity of Nipah virus fusion glycoprotein a process that requires proteolytic cleavage by the host protease Cathepsin L.

Immune evasion

A hallmark of NiV is its ability to subvert the host innate immune response via products of the P gene. Through RNA editing, the P gene encodes the phosphoprotein (P) and three non‑structural proteins: V, W, and C, each of which contributes to immune evasion through distinct mechanisms.63, 64 The V and W proteins act as potent interferon (IFN) antagonists, sequestering signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 2 (STAT2) transcription factors and thereby blocking both Type I and Type II IFN signaling pathways.65, 66, 67 In addition to this STAT‑mediated blockade, the W protein further suppresses innate immunity by disrupting toll‑like receptor 3 (TLR3)-mediated signaling, thereby impairing the host’s ability to sense viral double‑stranded RNA.68 Moreover, the P protein itself directly inhibits IFN signaling by interfering with the activation of IFN‑regulatory factors, independent of the STAT‑blocking activity of V/W.65 This multi‑layered and coordinated blockade prevents establishment of an antiviral state, permitting rapid viral replication and high‑level viremia.69, 70

Genomic evolution and its implications for pandemic potential

Phylogenetic analyses have resolved two principal NiV lineages: Malaysian (NiV-M) and Bangladeshi (NiV-B), which exhibit an approximate 8–10% nucleotide divergence across the entire genome (Fig. 1). This genetic bifurcation aligns with distinct epidemiological patterns: NiV-M outbreaks generally necessitate amplifying swine hosts and are characterized by minimal human‑to‑human transmission,41 whereas NiV-B strains spillover directly from bats to humans and frequently generate sustained person‑to‑person clusters.14, 40 Molecular‑clock estimates date this divergence to the early 20th century, implying that both genotypes have circulated independently in bat reservoirs for decades prior to the first documented human cases.73Fig. 1Maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree of Nipah virus complete genomes.Fig 1 dummy alt textNote: Phylogenetic tree of representative NiV complete genome sequences, showing distinct NiV-M and NiV-B clades. From a pandemic‑risk perspective, the persistent divergence between lineages poses critical questions regarding countermeasure efficacy. Current vaccine candidates such as mRNA‑1215 and ChAdOx1 NiV are built on NiV-M glycoprotein backbones and require validation against heterologous NiV-B challenge. Positive‑selection signals identified within the G attachment protein, particularly at residues that discriminate between NiV-M and NiV-B, may modify receptor affinity and alter transmission efficiency.16 Consequently, genomic surveillance must fulfill a dual mandate: tracking viral diversification that could compromise vaccine cross‑protection, and providing early warning of adaptive changes, such as enhanced respiratory transmissibility, that could elevate NiV’s pandemic potential.27, 28, 29, 30, 74 Integrating phylogenetic data with ecological and clinical insights remains essential for refining risk assessments and guiding the development of broadly protective interventions.23

Pandemic potential

Intrinsic viral risk factors

NiV’s pandemic potential is rooted in its genomic plasticity, high virulence, and documented human‑to‑human transmission—particularly by the Bangladesh lineage (NiV‑B).21, 22 Although the basic reproduction number (R₀) for most outbreaks remains below 1.0, superspreading events in health‑care settings demonstrate that efficient transmission can occur under favorable conditions, exposing systemic weaknesses.31 The virus causes severe respiratory distress and fatal encephalitis,8, 9 with case‑fatality rates of 40–75% that impose a heavy clinical burden.10, 49, 50 Moreover, approximately 20% of survivors suffer long‑term neurological sequelae, adding to the public health impact.75, 76, 77, 78, 79 Genomic analyses reveal ongoing positive selection in the G attachment protein, especially at residues that distinguish NiV‑M from NiV‑B; such adaptive changes could increase receptor affinity and transmission efficiency, potentially elevating the virus’s pandemic threshold.74

Ecological amplifiers of spillover risk

Intrinsic viral risk is not static; it is dynamically amplified by ecological drivers that reshape the human–animal interface. As detailed in Section 2.4, deforestation, climate‑induced fruiting failures, and habitat fragmentation force Pteropus bats to forage in human‑dominated landscapes, thereby increasing contact frequency with people and domestic animals.43 These perturbations do more than raise spillover probability—they fundamentally alter the risk matrix. Spatially, bat foraging ranges expand into peri‑urban and agricultural zones, bringing the virus closer to susceptible populations. Temporally, climate‑driven phenological shifts extend the bat–human overlap from seasonal to near‑year‑round, widening the spillover window. Pathway diversification—through contamination of date‑palm sap, fruit orchards, and livestock feed—creates multiple indirect transmission routes.37, 80 Modeling studies indicate that deforestation rates exceeding 5% per decade are associated with a two‑ to three‑fold increase in spillover probability in Bangladesh.22 These ecological amplifiers transform a sporadic zoonosis into a persistent, geographically expanding threat.

Geographic expansion of risk zones

The geographic footprint of NiV risk extends far beyond the regions where human outbreaks have been recorded. Serological and molecular evidence of henipavirus infection has been found in Pteropus bats across South Asia, Southeast Asia, Oceania, and coastal Africa,24, 25, 26, 81 indicating a vast potential risk zone. While human cases remain confined to South and Southeast Asia, the presence of susceptible reservoir hosts in China—combined with large‑scale pig farming (a highly efficient amplifying host) in regions where bat and human activities overlap—constitutes a tangible, albeit currently unrealized, domestic risk.82, 83 Serological data also suggest circulation of NiV or related viruses in African Eidolon bats,24, 81, 84 further expanding the conceivable risk map. The emergence of NiV in Kerala, a region geographically distant from the historical epicenter, highlights the risk of viral dissemination over large distances or local spillover from bat reservoirs.52, 53, 54, 85Thus, the convergence of ecological disruption, reservoir host distribution, and agricultural practices collectively shapes an expanding global risk landscape for NiV emergence.

The pandemic potential of NiV is therefore not a static parameter to be measured, but a dynamic trajectory that must be monitored—one that evolves at the intersection of viral adaptation, ecological change, and human vulnerability.27, 28, 29, 30

Countermeasures

Laboratory biosafety

Work with NiV carries substantial risk. Manipulation of live virus or un‑inactivated clinical material must be performed under BSL‑4 containment. Inactivation of diagnostic specimens can be carried out in biosafety level 3 (BSL‑3) laboratories, with downstream molecular assays conducted at lower containment levels only after rigorous validation and adherence to standard operating procedures. Primary laboratory hazards include percutaneous exposure (e.g., through sharps or open wounds) and aerosol generation during centrifugation, pipetting, or vortexing. Comprehensive biosafety measures are mandatory: Appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE), strict waste decontamination (e.g., autoclaving, chemical disinfection with 0.5% sodium hypochlorite), and ongoing medical surveillance of staff. Personnel who are febrile, immunosuppressed, or pregnant should be excluded from high‑containment activities. The inherent dangers of NiV, in both the natural environment and the laboratory,42 underscore the non‑negotiable requirement for effective medical countermeasures.86 Fortunately, the portfolio of vaccines and therapeutics is expanding, providing tools that could alter the trajectory of future outbreaks.

Diagnostic technologies

Accurate and rapid diagnosis is the linchpin of NiV outbreak control, enabling timely isolation and therapeutic intervention. Because the prodromal symptoms of NiV infection are non‑specific and often mimic influenza, dengue, or other common illnesses, and because outbreaks frequently occur in resource‑constrained settings, the development of reliable diagnostics is paramount. Current strategies encompass pathogen detection, serology, and emerging platforms.87, 88

Conventional laboratory diagnostic methods

Virus isolation and nucleic acid detection

Virus isolation remains the diagnostic “gold standard” but is time-consuming, poses significant risks, and must be performed in BSL-4 facilities, rendering it unsuitable for routine use. Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is the primary method for confirming early infection. Real-time RT-PCR targeting the N, P, or L genes offers high sensitivity and specificity, capable of detecting viral RNA from blood, cerebrospinal fluid, or throat swabs in the initial stages of illness.88 Beyond acute-phase diagnosis, molecular detection has also revealed unexpected sites of viral persistence, as demonstrated by the detection of Nipah virus RNA in the semen of a convalescent patient.89

Serological assays

These detect virus-specific IgM and IgG antibodies and are useful for diagnosing later stages of infection or for serosurveys.90, 91, 92 Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and serum neutralization test (SNT) are commonly used.93, 94, 95, 96 While RT-PCR remains the cornerstone for early detection, serological assays are indispensable for identifying past exposure and for epidemiological surveillance. The serum neutralization test, although the reference serological standard,97 is limited by its biosafety requirements and is therefore rarely used outside specialized reference laboratories.96, 98, 99

Emerging and point-of-care rapid diagnostic technologies

To obviate the dependence on high‑containment laboratories that characterizes conventional methods, researchers are developing rapid diagnostics that can be deployed directly at outbreak sites or in routine clinical settings.87

Isothermal amplification technologies

Techniques such as loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) and recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) can rapidly amplify viral nucleic acids at a constant temperature without the need for complex thermocyclers,100 showing great potential for field use in primary care settings.101

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-cas systems

Detection platforms based on Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats-Cas12a/Cas13 combine specific recognition with signal amplification, enabling highly sensitive and specific visual readouts of nucleic acids (e.g., via lateral flow strips). This promises “sample-in, answer-out” bedside diagnostics.98, 102, 103

Antigen rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs)

Developing monoclonal antibodies against the NiV nucleoprotein (N) or glycoprotein (G) for use in colloidal gold-based immunochromatographic strips can provide results within 15–30 min. While the sensitivity of RDTs is generally lower than that of nucleic‑acid amplification, their rapid turnaround and ease of use make them invaluable for first‑line surveillance in resource‑constrained environments.98, 102

Therapeutic strategies

Monoclonal antibodies

The human monoclonal antibody m102.4 targets the receptor-binding domain of the G protein. Antiviral drugs

The nucleoside analog Remdesivir (GS-5734) has shown 100% efficacy as post-exposure prophylaxis in non-human primate models of NiV infection.115, 116, 117, 118, 119 The broad-spectrum polymerase inhibitor Favipiravir has also shown promise in reducing viral load in preclinical studies.120, 121, 122, 123 These countermeasures collectively represent a rapidly evolving therapeutic arsenal that could transform the clinical and public‑health response to future NiV outbreaks (Table 6).

Clinical management and infection control

NiV infection is managed supportively, focusing on treating respiratory, neurological, and systemic complications.9 Patients are cared for in negative‑pressure isolation rooms with strict contact‑ and droplet‑precaution (powered air-purifying respirators or N95 respirators, fluid‑resistant gowns, double gloves, eye protection). Severe cases may need ICU care (mechanical ventilation, extra corporeal membrane oxygenation, renal replacement therapy)75, 76; adjunctive treatments (steroids, anticonvulsants, plasma‑exchange) are used only when evidence supports them. Experimental therapies such as m102.4 monoclonal antibody, remdesivir, and favipiravir, can be administered under compassionate‑use or outbreak‑study protocols, with dosing guided by emerging safety data. Early recognition, rapid isolation, and multidisciplinary supportive care remain the foundation of clinical management while ongoing trials refine evidence‑based treatments.23

Challenges in deployment

Despite significant technological advances, the translation of diagnostics, vaccines, and therapeutics from research laboratories into field-ready tools for outbreak control faces formidable and interconnected hurdles. These challenges span the entire development-to-deployment continuum and are exacerbated by the sporadic nature of NiV outbreaks and the high-containment requirements for virus research.86, 88

Diagnostic development and deployment challenges

NiV diagnostics must contend with the genetic diversity of the virus—two major lineages (NiV‑M, NiV‑B) and emerging sublineages—necessitating assays with broad strain coverage that target conserved genomic regions (e.g., the N gene). Molecular tests (RT-PCR) require periodic revalidation against evolving sequences, while serological assays face cross‑reactivity with other henipaviruses and the BSL‑4 constraint for live virus neutralization tests, limiting their availability in endemic regions.124, 125, 126, 127, 128 For point‑of‑care rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), three critical barriers remain: Ensuring thermostability without cold chain reliance, achieving sensitivity comparable to PCR during acute infection, and enabling mass production at costs affordable to low‑resource health systems.87, 129 Future efforts should prioritize the development of multiplex pathogen detection panels capable of simultaneously identifying NiV and other common febrile illnesses (dengue, Japanese encephalitis), alongside artificial intelligence‑assisted imaging diagnostic systems to detect characteristic brain lesions, thereby constructing an integrated diagnostic network spanning pathogen detection to clinical evaluation.23

Vaccine and therapeutic development challenges

The sporadic, unpredictable nature of NiV outbreaks makes traditional large‑scale Phase III efficacy trials nearly impossible,86 forcing reliance on alternative regulatory pathways such as the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)’s “Animal Rule,” which requires well‑controlled animal model studies combined with human safety and immunogenicity data. This pathway introduces uncertainties in dose extrapolation and demands validated animal models that faithfully recapitulate human disease. The BSL‑4 requirement for live virus research further constrains the global research and development enterprise: Fewer than two dozen laboratories worldwide possess BSL‑4 capacity, creating geographic inequities and limiting essential neutralization and efficacy studies.114

Vaccine development faces additional complexities. The immunological correlates of protection remain incompletely defined—while neutralizing antibodies against the G glycoprotein are strongly associated with protection, the role of T‑cell immunity is increasingly recognized, complicating vaccine evaluation and bridging studies. Current lead candidates (mRNA‑1215, chimpanzee adenovirus Oxford 1 [ChAdOx1] NiV, Hendra virus soluble glycoprotein [HeV‑sG]) are based on NiV‑M sequences and require validation against heterologous NiV‑B and emerging Indian strains; the 8–10% nucleotide divergence and positive selection signals in the G protein raise legitimate questions about cross‑protection durability.130 Moreover, defining an optimal immunization strategy is challenging—sporadic outbreaks suggest ring vaccination of contacts and high‑risk groups may be more appropriate than mass immunization, demanding vaccines with rapid onset, thermostability, and acceptable safety profiles for vulnerable populations. Platform selection (mRNA, viral vector, subunit) must balance immunogenicity, thermostability, manufacturing scalability, and cost, particularly for low‑resource endemic countries.131

Overcoming these deployment challenges requires coordinated action: establishing regional BSL‑3/4 research networks, developing “universal” henipavirus vaccines targeting conserved epitopes, creating global stockpile mechanisms with clear governance, and ensuring seamless integration of rapid diagnostics with countermeasure deployment.131 Only through such synergistic, system‑level strategies can bench‑side innovation be translated into bedside protection against NiV.

Conclusion and future perspectives: towards an integrated defense system against NiV

NiV has progressed from a regionally confined zoonosis to a persistent threat to global health security, a pathogen whose sporadic outbreaks belie its potential for broader catastrophe. This review has systematically examined the virus through an integrated framework, encompassing the epidemiological divergence of the Malaysian (NiV‑M) and Bangladesh‑India (NiV‑B) lineages, the sophisticated receptor‑binding and immune‑evasion strategies that underlie its high pathogenicity, and the ecological amplifiers reshaping spillover risk.58 Our findings align with previous assessments that NiV’s pandemic potential is dynamic,27 but unlike earlier reviews,7, 46 we explicitly link ecological amplifiers (e.g., deforestation thresholds) to quantifiable spillover probability. The key insight is that NiV’s pandemic potential is not a static property of the virus alone but a dynamic product of viral evolution, climate‑driven habitat change, and the expanding interface between reservoir hosts and human populations.27, 28, 29, 30

Although the basic reproduction number (R₀) of NiV in most outbreaks remains below 1.0, this metric can be misleading. Superspreading events in health‑care settings and the emergence of more transmissible Bangladeshi sublineages demonstrate that human‑to‑human transmission is fluid.34, 35, 36 When coupled with ecological perturbations—spatial expansion of bat foraging, temporal extension of exposure windows, and diversification of transmission routes—the likelihood of a more transmissible variant arising in densely populated, ecologically primed hotspots becomes tangible. The absence of licensed vaccines and therapeutics, combined with the formidable challenges in deploying existing countermeasures, further exacerbates this vulnerability.103, 132 In contrast to earlier views that basic infection control could contain NiV,29 accumulating evidence shows sustained human‑to‑human transmission is possible under favorable conditions,21, 22, 39 reshaping global preparedness priorities.131

Nevertheless, this review also highlights several limitations that must be addressed in future work. First, epidemiological data are largely derived from Bangladesh and India, leaving the status of NiV in other Pteropus‑endemic regions such as Southeast Asia, Oceania, Africa) under‑characterized. Second, many molecular mechanisms are inferred from in vitro or animal studies and require validation in human‑relevant systems. Third, phylogenetic analyses are constrained by limited publicly available sequences, potentially biasing evolutionary interpretations. Finally, socioeconomic and behavioral determinants that modulate spillover risk have not been fully explored, yet they are essential for designing culturally sensitive interventions.

Over the past two decades, significant advances have been made—from high‑resolution structures of the NiV polymerase complex to exhaustive deep‑mutational scans of the G protein—yet the translation of these insights into deployable interventions remains sluggish. The gaps highlighted in this review are not merely academic; they expose blind spots in our global preparedness architecture.103, 132 Our framework explicitly incorporates environmental monitoring as a core pillar of early warning systems, providing actionable metrics (e.g., deforestation rates, bat foraging range expansion) to operationalize a “One Health” approach.

These shortcomings underscore the necessity of a true “One Health” approach. Confronting NiV demands the harmonisation of human, animal, and environmental health.42 No single discipline, sector, or nation can tackle this threat in isolation. Ecological amplifiers—spatial expansion of bat foraging driven by habitat loss, climate change, and intensified agriculture—set the stage for spillover. Viral evolution unfolds silently within bat reservoirs before crossing into humans.14 Clinical outcomes depend on both pathogen biology and the readiness of health systems, while the effectiveness of countermeasures hinges on community engagement, equitable access, and robust global governance.52

Therefore, we call upon the international community, research institutions, and public health organizations to collaboratively commit to building a proactive, multi-layered, “One Health”-informed global defense system. Drawing directly from the analyses in this review, such a system should rest upon four interlocking pillars.

Intelligent forecasting and proactive surveillance networks

Integrate satellite remote sensing, climate projections, animal‑movement datasets, and machine‑learning algorithms to produce high‑resolution predictive maps of spillover risk. Operationalise the ecological amplifiers identified throughout this review, and strengthen cross‑species genomic surveillance in identified hotspots to shift from a “passive response” model to an “active early‑warning” paradigm.

A repository of broad-spectrum and rapidly deployable medical countermeasures

Prioritise investment in “universal” vaccine platforms and broadly neutralising monoclonal antibodies that retain efficacy across henipavirus lineages. Concurrently, ensure equitable, rapid deployment to frontline settings during outbreaks through technology transfer and expansion of global manufacturing capacity.

Grassroots empowerment and diagnostic democratization

Accelerate the development and pre‑positioning of field‑deployable rapid diagnostic kits suitable for primary‑care environments. These tools constitute the first line of defence, breaking transmission chains and enabling early isolation while addressing genetic diversity and guaranteeing thermostability and affordability in low‑resource settings.

Bridging basic research with clinical and ecological practice

Create multinational cohorts to investigate long‑term neurological sequelae and their mechanisms,77, 78, 79 translating molecular insights into enhanced patient care. Simultaneously, fund pilot ecology‑based interventions—bat‑habitat protection, safe sap‑harvesting practices—to reduce spillover at its source.12, 22

Investing in NiV preparedness is not merely a reaction to a single pathogen; it strengthens collective resilience against future, unknown threats. The actions we take—closing surveillance gaps, stockpiling the right tools, empowering local health systems, and tackling root ecological drivers—will determine whether we remain locked in a cycle of crisis response or achieve robust, pre‑emptive defenses.133, 134, 135, 136 Nipah has issued a warning; the question is whether we heed it, and whether we respond as an integrated One‑Health coalition rather than as isolated sectors.
