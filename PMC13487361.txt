Caffeic Acid Intervention Slows Cancer Cell Growth While Reversing the Th2‐Biased Immune Balance in Human Colorectal Cancer HT‐29 Cell‐Bearing Mice
Authors: Hsuan‐Yin Chen, Chang‐Lin Lin, Chou‐Chen Chen, Jin‐Yuarn Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe effects of caffeic acid (CA) intervention on the relationship between human colorectal cancer (CRC) HT‐29 cell growth and Th1/Th2 immune balance in vivo are not fully understood. To unravel the perplexity, this study examined the impact of CA intervention on the relationship between tumor growth and the Th1/Th2 immune balance in CRC‐bearing mice for 35 days using an established xenograft animal model. The BALB/c nude mice were subcutaneously xenografted with HT‐29 cells and received three different CA doses daily, including 6 mg (low‐dose), 30 mg (medium‐dose), and 60 mg CA/kg AIN‐93 M feed (high‐dose). The results showed that CA intervention slightly decreased tumor volumes and weights in CRC‐bearing mice. Meanwhile, the CRC‐bearing mice tended to have a Th2‐biased immune balance, as indicated by the cytokine secretion profile of splenocytes. Notably, CA intervention dose‐dependently increased Concanavalin A‐stimulated IFN‐γ secretion and the IFN‐γ (Th1)/IL‐10 (Th2) secretion ratio. There is a negative correlation between IFN‐γ/IL‐10 secretion ratios and tumor weights, suggesting that higher doses of CA intervention may reverse the immune balance toward a Th1‐dominant immune response in CRC‐bearing mice. CA showed modest trends toward reduced tumor burden under the experimental conditions tested, while reversing the Th2‐biased immune balance in CRC‐bearing mice.Keywords: caffeic acid, human colorectal cancer HT‐29 cells, Th1/Th2 immune balance

Introduction

Cancers have been increasing in the world and topping the death list for many years. Deaths due to colorectal cancers (CRCs) have ranked in the top three in Taiwan and the USA among diverse cancers [1]. Most CRCs develop from polyps and are classified into four types: adenocarcinoma, colorectal lymphoma, neuroendocrine tumor, and gastrointestinal stromal tumor; however, most CRCs are adenocarcinomas. Many methods are involved in cancer treatment, but adverse side effects, such as drug resistance, result in significant challenges to date. Recently, cancer immunotherapy has been applied to treat cancers by integrating immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and chemotherapy to inhibit cancer cell growth. To improve immunotherapy effectiveness and reduce adverse side effects in cancer treatment, natural phytochemicals, including caffeic acid (CA) derivatives, have been screened and suggested as auxiliary treatments for chronic inflammation‐related diseases, such as CRCs [2, 3].

The immune system comprises innate and adaptive immunity [4]. Among innate immunity, the immune cells mainly comprise neutrophils, eosinophils, natural killer cells (NKCs), macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), and mast cells. These immune cells may combat infections and cancers through phagocytosis, cytotoxicity, activation of the complement system, release of acute‐phase proteins, and production of inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines and chemokines [4, 5]. Immune cells, including lymphocytes, DCs, and macrophages found in the tumor microenvironment (TME), play an essential role in helping cancer cells to penetrate the vascular barrier by producing growth factors, cytokines, and proteases, and promoting angiogenesis and tumor invasion [6, 7].

Differential T‐ and B‐lymphocyte contributions in adaptive immunity may further promote cancer development and progression. Depending on the environment, a naïve helper T cell (Th0) may further differentiate into either a type 1 helper T cell (Th1) or a type 2 helper T cell (Th2). Th1 cells mainly defend against intracellular pathogens and secrete interferon (IFN)‐γ and interleukin (IL)‐2, which further help activate macrophages and CD8+ T cells. In contrast, Th2 cells primarily defend against parasitic infections and allergen exposure, producing IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐10, and IL‐13 that can activate mast cells and promote B cells to produce antibodies [8]. The Th1/Th2 immune balance may influence the cancer progression in the body; Th1 tends to inhibit tumors, while Th2 promotes tumor growth [9, 10]. Helper T cells (Th) are closely related to the polarization of macrophages. IFN‐γ produced by Th1 cells polarizes macrophages toward type 1 macrophages (M1). M1 produces pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α, to activate Th1 immune responses and CD8+ T lymphocytes to kill tumor cells, enhancing antitumor immunity. Th2‐type cytokines, including IL‐4, IL‐10, and IL‐13, may induce macrophages to differentiate into type 2 macrophages (M2). M2 cells produce anti‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐10, IL‐13, and transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β, to stimulate Th2 immune responses and tumor development. Potential phytochemicals such as CA may enhance tumor immunotherapy by targeting tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) in CRC or regulating the Th1/Th2 immune balance in TME [11, 12, 13].

CA, namely 3,4‐dihydroxycinnamic acid, which is a common polyphenol compound, is rich in coffee, fruits, grains, and vegetables. CA has been found to possess the potential for antioxidant, antibacterial, anti‐inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and neuroprotective effects to improve chronic metabolic diseases [14]. CA may alleviate atherosclerosis and Alzheimer's disease by reducing serum TNF‐α and IL‐6 levels [15]. CA may inhibit skin cancer by targeting and inhibiting extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) 1 and ERK2 [16, 17]. CA improves non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease by inhibiting imbalanced intestinal flora and their lipopolysaccharide (LPS) release [18]. However, CA's impact on CRCs through cancer immunotherapy is still not fully understood. Recently, CA has been shown to have anti‐inflammatory potential and to inhibit the growth of human colorectal cancer HT‐29 cells by triggering the Bax/Bcl‐2 apoptotic pathway in vitro [3, 19]. Most recently, CA has been found to improve M1‐polarized immune balance in HT‐29 cell‐bearing nude mice [20]. However, the effects of CA intervention on Th1/Th2 immune balance in the CRC‐bearing mice are still unclear. To unravel the perplexity, this experiment primarily investigated the effects of CA intervention on changes in the Th1/Th2 immune balance with an established human colorectal cancer HT‐29 cell‐bearing mouse model [20].

Materials and Methods

Materials

Caffeic acid (CA, C9H8O4, Sigma, C0625, St. Louis, MO, USA) at the available highest purity (>98.0%) was used for the experiment.

HT‐29 Cell Culture

The HT‐29 cell line (ATCC HTB‐38), a human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line having an epithelial cell morphology, was initially isolated from the primary tumor of a female Caucasian patient. HT‐29 cells were cultured for a xenograft colorectal cancer mouse model in the present experiment and performed as described previously [19]. After appropriate subculturing procedure, the HT‐29 cells were collected to adjust to a fixed cell density of 1 ×107 cells/mL phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, 1.5 mM KH2PO4 (Sigma, P‐5379, St. Louis, MO, USA), 8.1 mM Na2HPO4 (Sigma, S7907, St. Louis, MO, USA), 137 mM NaCl (Wako, 191–01655, Osaka, Japan), 2.7 mM KCl (Wako, 163–03545, Osaka, Japan), pH 7.4, 0.22 µm filtered) by a trypan blue (Corning, 25‐900‐C1, Manassas, VA, USA) exclusion method aided with a hemocytometer for establishing a xenograft CRC animal model [21].

CA Intervention Effects on Cancer Cell Growth and Immune Responses Using a Xenograft CRC‐Bearing BALB/c Nude Mouse Model: Experimental Mice and Grouping

BALB/cAnN.Cg‐Foxnlnu/CrlNarl female mice at 7 weeks old were purchased from the National Institutes of Applied Research, National Center for Biomodels, Taipei, Taiwan, for the in vivo experiment. The adopted CA doses and animal study model were performed as described previously [3, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. At 8 weeks old, the experiment mice were grouped randomly into seven groups (n = 12), including non‐treatment control (NTC, mice without any treatment), vehicle control (VC, mice just received Matrigel without HT‐29 cells), dietary control (DC, mice received Matrigel with HT‐29 cells), caffeic acid low‐dose group (CALD, mice received Matrigel with HT‐29 cells and 6 mg CA/kg AIN‐93 M feed), caffeic acid medium‐dose group (CAMD, mice received Matrigel with HT‐29 cells and 30 mg CA/kg AIN‐93 M feed), caffeic acid high‐dose group (CAHD, mice received Matrigel with HT‐29 cells and 60 mg CA/kg AIN‐93 M feed), and positive control (PC, mice received Matrigel with HT‐29 cells and paclitaxel treatment). NTC, VC, DC, and PC groups were fed just AIN‐93 M feed powder without CA. Four mice in the same group were allocated to a cage, marked with ear holes, and allowed free access to different formulas using AIN‐93 M feed (Table S1) and water after grouping (day 0) [24]. Additionally, CA was appropriately incorporated into the AIN‐93 M feed powder to ensure a homogeneous formulation of the diets and different dosages of the test compound for the experimental animals (Table S1). The AIN‐93 M powder feed was free of phenolic compounds based on known ingredients in the formula. According to the surface area of the animal body, the equivalent dose for humans can be computed. The equivalent dose of CA in a 60 kg person would be 4.374 mg per day in the CALD group, 21.87 mg per day in the CAMD group, and 43.74 mg per day in the CAHD group [20].

Establishment of a Xenograft CRC Animal Model

The established xenograft CRC animal model using HT‐29 cells was performed and measured as described previously [20]. All experimental mice were humanely killed on day 35 after they were fasted for about 10 h. The experimental mice's blood, sera, splenocytes, tumors, and organs were harvested for analyses. The absolute tissue weights (ATW, g) and relative tissue weights (RTW, %) of the organs, including the heart, lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys, and adrenal glands, were measured. RTW (%) was calculated with the equation: RTW (%) = [organ weight (g)/animal body weight (g)] × 100. The tumor weights and volumes were measured. The tumor volume was calculated using the equation: tumor volume (cm3) = tumor length (cm) × tumor width2 (cm2)/2. Tumor tissue pathological analyses were performed. A trained pathologist performed the evaluation. The scoring was blinded and used predefined criteria. Moreover, the experimental mice's body weight, feed intake, energy intake, and feed and energy efficiencies were calculated. Serum CA levels in the CA‐administered mice were measured. Levels of IL‐2, IFN‐γ (Th1), IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐10 (Th2) cytokines secreted by spleen cells from the experimental mice were analyzed. The protocol adopted in the animal study was examined and approved (IACUC No.: 111–168R2) by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), National Chung Hsing University, Taichung, Taiwan. The animal study results in this experiment were observed, recorded, and reported under Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines [25].

Serum Collection and Analyses: Serum Preparation

The blood from the experimental mice was harvested into a 2.0 mL microcentrifuge tube. The tube was allowed to stand for 2 h at room temperature. Then, the tube was centrifuged for 30 min at 4°C, 9500× g. The supernatant (serum) was harvested into a 2.0 mL microcentrifuge tube and stored at −80°C for determining CA content.

Analysis of Serum CA Contents by High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC): Sample Preparation

An aliquot of 100 µL serum sample was pipetted into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, and 500 µL methanol (Wako, 137–01823, Osaka, Japan) was added to the tube. The mixture was mixed thoroughly and centrifuged for 15 min at 4°C, 12 000× g. The supernatant was collected into another 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. The harvested supernatant was filtered with a 0.22 µm filter (Merck Millipore Ltd., Tullagreen, Carrigtwohill, Co. Cork, Ireland), kept in another clean microcentrifuge tube, and allowed to be placed in an 80°C water bath to evaporate the methanol residue. An aliquot of 25 µL of methanol was added to re‐dissolve the sample, and the mixture was centrifuged at 4°C and 12 000× g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected and stored at −30°C for HPLC analysis.

CA Analyses Using HPLC

An aliquot of 1 mg CA was dissolved in 1 mL of methanol to prepare a 1000 µg/mL CA stock solution and filtered with a 0.22 µm filter. The CA stock solution was appropriately diluted with methanol to prepare standards at the indicated concentrations of 3.9, 7.8, 15.6, 31.2, and 62.5 µg/mL for assay and calibration. Mobile phase solvents were filtered with a 0.45 µm filter before use. All standards, sample solutions, and HPLC reagents were ultrasonically degassed before being subjected to HPLC operation. Autosampler (Hitachi, L‐2200, Tokyo, Japan), chromatographic separation column (Mightysil RP‐C18 GP250‐4.6, 4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 µm, Kanto Chemicals, 25415–96), and UV–Visible detector (Hitachi, L‐2400, Tokyo, Japan) were equipped and used in the HPLC machine. The mobile phase A was composed of ddH2O: tetrahydrofuran (ECHO, AH‐2301, HPLC grade, Taiwan): trifluoroacetic acid (Sigma, T‐6508, St. Louis, MO, USA) at the ratio of 98:2:0.1 by volume. The mobile phase B was acetonitrile (Fisher Scientific Korea Ltd., A988‐4, HPLC grade, Seoul, Korea). This experiment used a mobile phase pump (Hitachi, model L‐2130, Tokyo, Japan) to control the ratio of mobile phase A and mobile phase B as well as the gradient condition at 83:17 (0–7 min), 75:25 (7–11 min), 50:50 (11–18 min), 0:100 (18–25 min), and 83:17 (25–30 min). The mobile phase was maintained at a 1.0 mL/min flow rate. The UV detection wavelength was set at 370 nm. Using the autosampler, each analysis was loaded with an aliquot of 10 µL sample solution. Data acquisition was collected using accompanying software (Hitachi, model D‐2000 Elite Chromatography Data Station Software). The known concentrations of the CA standard and their corresponding peak areas were used to draw a standard curve. Based on the CA standard curve, the sample peak areas obtained at the same retention time (RT) were compared to calculate the CA concentration [26].

CA Intervention Effects on Splenocytes’ Numbers, Proliferation, and Th1/Th2 Cytokine Secretion by the HT‐29‐Bearing Mice: Isolation of Mouse Primary Splenocytes

The mouse peritoneum was opened with sterile scissors under aseptic conditions. The spleen was cut, weighed, and placed in a 3.5 cm Petri dish containing 3 mL tissue culture medium (TCM, a defined commercial serum replacement) [20]. The splenocytes were isolated from the spleen with the flat surface of a sterile syringe shaft to release splenocytes as described previously [24]. The splenocyte cell density was counted with a hemacytometer using the trypan blue (Corning, 25‐900‐C1, Manassas, VA, USA) exclusion method and adjusted to 1 × 107 cells/mL TCM medium for the subsequent culture experiment [24].

Culture of Mouse Primary Splenocytes

Aliquots of harvested splenocyte suspension (1 × 107 cells/mL, 0.5 mL/well) were added to the wells in a 24‐well plate. TCM medium (negative control, 0.5 mL/well) and Concanavalin A (Con A, Sigma, C2010, St. Louis, MO, USA, positive control, 5.0 µg/mL, 0.5 mL/well) were added to the wells. The plate was incubated for 72 h in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator, and then centrifuged for 10 min at 400× g. The supernatant was harvested into a 2.0 microcentrifuge tube. The tube was stored in a −40°C refrigerator. Th1 (IL‐2 and IFN‐γ) and Th2 (IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐10) cytokine levels in the cell culture supernatant were measured using an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [25].

Determination of Th1/Th2 Cytokine Secretion Levels With an ELISA

Th1/Th2 cytokine levels were determined with sandwich ELISA kits. IL‐2 (Biolegend, ELISA MAX Deluxe Set Mouse IL‐2, Cat. No. 431004, San Diego, CA, USA), IFN‐γ (R&D, Mouse IFN‐γ DuoSet ELISA DY485‐05, Minneapolis, MN, USA), IL‐4 (R&D, Mouse IL‐4 DuoSet ELISA DY404‐05, Minneapolis, MN, USA), IL‐5 (Biolegend, ELISA MAX Deluxe Set Mouse IL‐5, Cat. No. 431204, San Diego, CA, USA), and IL‐10 (Biolegend, ELISA MAX Deluxe Set Mouse IL‐10, Cat. No. 431414, San Diego, CA, USA) were measured with sandwich ELISA kits and performed as manufacturers’ instruction [27, 28].

Statistical Analyses

The experiment used the mean ± standarderror of the mean (SEM) to present the results. Data were first analyzed using one‐way ANOVA to determine if p < 0.05 in the same group and to test whether normality and homogeneity of variance were maintained. Data within a 95% confidence interval in the same group were further selected to perform Duncan's new multiple range test among groups, which is a post hoc test. Associations between different parameters were assayed by the Pearson product‐moment correlation coefficient (r). A value of p < 0.05 is considered a significant difference among groups. IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 software was adopted to perform statistical analyses.

Results And Discussions

CA Intervention Effects on Body Weight, Food Intake, and Organs in CRC‐Bearing Mice

Figure 1 shows the effects of CA intervention on the body weights of CRC‐bearing mice for 35 days. Notably, CA intervention slightly (p > 0.05) improved the weight loss phenomenon. A significant difference in body weight among groups was observed on the 17th day. The NTC group was significantly (p < 0.05) higher than the DC group, evidencing that the tumor growth of HT‐29 cells in the experimental mice in the middle experiment period resulted in apparent weight loss or cachexia [29]. From the 10th day onwards, the body weight of the CALD and CAMD groups had a slightly (p > 0.05) higher trend than that of the DC group. We speculated that low and medium doses of CA intervention can slow down the weight loss phenomenon caused by tumor growth. However, no significant difference in body weights among groups was found at the time of sacrifice. Increased tumor weights in the body might compensate for the weight loss in the experimental mice at a later stage.

Table 1 shows the effects of CA intervention on initial and final body weights, feed intakes, and feed and energy efficiencies of CRC‐bearing mice for 35 days. The results showed no significant difference among groups in the experimental mice's initial and final body weights. All of the final body weights, except for the PC group, increased slightly. However, the PC group showed negative body weight gain and feed and energy efficiencies. We hypothesized that the PC group was administered paclitaxel by intraperitoneal injection on five different days, resulting in a stressful situation for the experimental mice. Unfortunately, the stressful situation for the experimental mice was not reproduced in the other experimental groups with the corresponding i.p. intervention of saline. This weakness might influence the results. The feed and energy intakes were further adopted to calculate feed and energy efficiencies. The results showed no significant differences between the groups fed with CA and the DC group, confirming that CA intervention incorporated into feed would not affect the experimental mice's feed intakes and energy efficiencies. However, this study found that CRC‐bearing mice (DC group) had a lower feed efficiency than normal mice (VC group) (Table 1), which is speculated to be due to metabolic abnormalities caused by tumor injection [30].

Table 2 shows the effects of CA intervention on absolute and relative organ weights of CRC‐bearing mice for 35 days. No significant differences between the VC and DC groups in terms of absolute organ weights were observed, indicating that tumor injection in this experimental model does not affect organ weights. There were no significant differences between the CA‐administered and DC groups, suggesting that long‐term CA feeding would not significantly affect the absolute organ weights. However, the relative liver weight in the DC group slightly (p > 0.05) increased as compared with the VC group, suggesting that a small number of HT‐29 cancer cells might metastasize to the liver. The most common metastatic site of colorectal cancer is the liver, followed by the lungs and abdominal cavity, which is related to a poor prognosis [31]. The staining of liver tissue sections in experimental mice may be measured to judge whether cancer cells have metastasized to the liver. Noticeably, the CA‐administered groups had a lower relative liver weight than the DC group. We hypothesized that CA may potentially inhibit cancer cell metastasis. However, the suggestion that relative liver weight differences indicate metastatic spread remains speculative without histopathological confirmation. It deserves to be explored further in the future. In addition, no significant difference in the spleen weights between the CA‐administered and DC groups was observed. Since the spleen is the largest immune organ in the body, its primary function is to maintain immune cells and collect blood antigens for initiating adaptive immunity. Our results indicated that CA feeding would not significantly cause spleen inflammation or toxicity in vivo.

Effect of CA Intervention on Serum CA Contents in CRC‐Bearing Mice

HPLC was used to evaluate CA metabolism in vivo and analyze serum CA contents in CRC‐bearing mice for 35 days. Figure S1 shows the HPLC chromatograms and the corresponding standard curve of CA. As seen in Figure S1A–E, the RT of the CA standard is 7.24 min. Different concentrations of CA and their corresponding peak area at 7.24 min RT were depicted in a standard curve (Figure S1F). Figure S2 is the HPLC spectra of serum CA contents of CRC‐bearing mice fed with CA for 35 days. When measuring the CA content in the serum, no peaks were found at the CA's RT in all groups (Figure S2); however, six unknown peaks, with RT at 2.66, 2.87, 3.53, 12.7, 14.7, and 21.5 min, appeared in the sera of the CA‐administered groups (Figure S2 and Table 3). Based on the HPLC chromatogram profiles, we hypothesized that the six unknown peaks were not CA's metabolites because there were no dose‐dependent changes among CA‐administered groups (Table 3). Therefore, our results evidence that CA may be quickly metabolized and its metabolites cannot be stored in the body. Unfortunately, circulating parent CA was not detectable under the current analytical conditions, likely reflecting rapid metabolism and/or methodological detection limitations. Regarding the measurement of CA in the serum, the procedure for preparing the serum samples for analysis may not be the most appropriate, as most phenolic compounds, such as CA, are thermolabile, and heating the serum samples at 80°C to remove the extract solvent (ethanol) for an undetermined time may influence the circulating metabolites. The serum samples may be further determined by liquid chromatography (LC)–mass spectrometry (MS), which may be sensitive enough to detect the low concentrations of circulating metabolites. Unfortunately, there is still no information on the actual time elapsed between the last meal and blood collection when the blood samples were obtained, as each experimental mouse had free access to feed and fasted for over 10 h. The formation of CA metabolism is crucial for considering the low bioavailability and short half‐lives of phenolic metabolites in circulation. An experiment to further explore CA metabolism is suggested, although there is existing literature on CA metabolism.

In this experiment, no significant difference in body weights between the CA‐administered groups and the DC group was found (Figure 1). It is speculated that CA may be rapidly metabolized and its metabolites cannot be accumulated in the body (Table 3), resulting in no apparent toxicity to the experimental mice. CA, namely, 3, 4‐dihydroxycinnamic acid, is one of the typical phenols in nature. Literature indicates that CA in a free form in the feed may be rapidly absorbed by the stomach and intestines within two hours and could be detected in the gastric mucosa, blood, and urine in a rat experimental model, suggesting that CA has a high absorption rate and a high metabolic rate [32, 33, 34]. After being absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, CA can be quickly metabolized into highly water‐soluble products, such as ferulic acid, isoferulic acid, caffeic acid 3‐O‐glucuronide, and so forth, through dehydroxylation, demethylation, hydrogenation, O‐methylation, sulfation, and glucuronidation in the liver [32, 33, 34]. Then, the CA's metabolites were quickly removed from the blood and excreted in the urine within hours; therefore, the six unknown peaks in the serum from the sacrificed experimental mice were not CA's metabolites (Table 3). Additionally, CA is known to have limited stability as a free parent compound in vivo due to rapid absorption, extensive phase II metabolism, and short systemic persistence. CA's catechol structure makes it chemically susceptible to oxidation and degradation during sample handling, particularly under heating conditions. Accordingly, failure to detect parent CA in the serum might not be unexpected and should not be interpreted as absence of biological exposure or used to support safety conclusions. The observed findings may reflect rapid metabolism, analytical limitations, or compound instability during sample preparation.

Effects of CA Intervention on the Cell Numbers and Th1/Th2 Immune Balance in the Spleen of CRC‐Bearing Mice

The experiment used BALB/c nude mice, which are immunodeficient due to a lack of a mature thymus, resulting in an inability to produce sufficient qualified T cells. However, the thymus and T cells were still found in the experimental nude mice. There are still many immune cells, including T cells, that exist in the spleen and the blood of the experimental nude mice [20]. Therefore, the splenocytes were isolated from the experimental mice to measure their inclination toward Th1/Th2 immune balance in this study. Figure 4 shows the effect of CA intervention on splenocyte numbers from CRC‐bearing mice for 35 days. The results showed that injecting cancer cells increased the splenocyte numbers, indicating inflammation (DC vs. VC groups). Compared with the cancer cell injection (DC) group, the CAMD, CAHD, and PC groups tended to have lower splenocyte numbers. They were similar to the VC group, indicating that feeding medium and high doses of CA and the paclitaxel treatment i.p. may reduce the inflammatory status in the body of CRC‐bearing mice for 35 days.

Table 5 shows the effect of CA intervention on the secretion of Th1/Th2 cytokines by splenocytes of CRC‐bearing mice. In the spontaneous mode, the DC group had a slightly (p > 0.05) lower IL‐2/IL‐10 cytokine secretion ratio as compared with that of the VC group, indicating that injection of HT‐29 cancer cells slightly decreased the Th1 immune balance that might hinder the cytotoxic ability to cancer cells in vivo. Notably, CAMD, CAHD, and PC groups reversed this immunodeficient phenomenon by increasing the higher secretion ratio of IL‐2/IL‐10 cytokines compared to that of the DC group, evidencing that CA intervention and the paclitaxel treatment i.p. may slightly improve adaptive immunity and Th1/Th2 immune balance in CRC‐bearing mice for 35 days. Con A is a specific T‐cell mitogen; therefore, we chose it to stimulate splenocytes to enhance the response of T cells in the experiment. In the Con A stimulation mode, CALD, CAMD, and CAHD groups dose‐dependently increased the secretion ratios of IFN‐γ/IL‐10 cytokines as compared to that of the DC group, evidencing that CA intervention may regulate Th1/Th2 immune balance toward a Th1‐inclined immune response in the experimental mice. The PC group had a significantly (p < 0.05) higher IFN‐γ/IL‐10 cytokine secretion ratio compared to that of the DC group, evidencing that the paclitaxel treatment i.p. also regulated Th1/Th2 immune balance toward a Th1‐inclined immune response in the experimental mice. Our results verify that CA intervention and the paclitaxel treatment i.p. may improve adaptive immunity and increase Th1‐inclined immune balance in the experimental mice. CA may influence immune‐associated pathways consistent with a shift toward a more Th1‐like cytokine profile. The Th1‐inclined immune balance helps kill cancer cells in the body. Our results evidenced that CA intervention improved Th1‐immune balance in the spleen of CRC‐bearing mice; however, circulating cytokines had not been analyzed in this study. This should be studied in the future.

The Relationship Between Th1/Th2 Immune Balance and Tumor Size in the Experimental Mice

To understand whether CA intervention can regulate the immune balance and thus influence the tumor size (tumor weights and volumes), this experiment analyzed the relationship between cytokines secreted by splenocytes and the tumor size using Pearson's product‐moment correlation coefficient (r). The absolute value of the correlation coefficient (r) was compared in each analysis. It was a low correlation if the absolute r value was less than 0.3. It was a moderate correlation if the absolute value of r fell between 0.3 and 0.7. There was a high correlation when the absolute value was greater than 0.7.

Figure S6 shows the relationship between CA intervention and Th1/Th2 cytokines secreted by splenocytes of CRC‐bearing mice. The results showed that CA intervention was low and positively correlated with the spontaneous IL‐2/IL‐10 secretion ratio (r = 0.056, p = 0.804) (Figure S6A). CA intervention was low and positively correlated with the IFN‐γ secretion (r = 0.247, p = 0.268) and IFN‐γ/IL‐10 ratio (r = 0.184, p = 0.412) by splenocytes under Con A stimulation (Figure S6B,C). The results indicate that CA intervention may dose‐dependently induce a Th1‐inclined immune balance in the CRC‐bearing mice. Unfortunately, the association was not significant (p > 0.05).

Figures S6 and S7 show the relationship between Th1/Th2 secreted levels and tumor sizes in CRC‐bearing mice. The results showed that IFN‐γ/IL‐10 secretion ratios under Con A stimulation had a low and negative correlation with tumor weights (r = −0.213, p = 0.226) (Figure S6C) and volumes (r = −0.139, p = 0.434) (Figure S7C); however, the association was not significant (p > 0.05). Based on the results, CA intervention increased the IFN‐γ/IL‐10 ratio (Table 5), causing the experimental mice to shift toward a Th1 immune balance, thereby reducing the tumor weight and volume (Figures S6 and S7). Literature indicates that IFN‐γ could stimulate major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC I) and MHC II in tumor cells and activate antigen‐presenting cells (APCs), consequently enhancing the production of IL‐12 by APCs, promoting Th1 cell polarization, activating T cells and NK cells through Th1 cytokines produced in the cancer microenvironment, and thereby inducing apoptosis of cancer cells [39]. Th1 cells and IFN‐γ infiltration into the colorectal cancer microenvironment are correlated with a good prognosis [40].

Common methods, such as the fecal occult blood test, colonoscopy, sigmoidoscopy, and barium enema, have been included in the routine of CRC detection. CRC treatments have included surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapeutic drug therapy, depending on the cancer development stage, tumor location, and clinical condition. Chemotherapeutic drugs 5‐fluorouracil and oxaliplatin have been found to inhibit HT‐29 cells; therefore, they are standard treatment options for CRC. Recently, monoclonal antibodies for inhibiting epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) have been included as commonly targeted drugs in CRC treatment. However, these treatments, except immunotherapy, can cause adverse side effects, including fatigue, decreased appetite, and gastrointestinal symptoms [41]. Therefore, immunotherapy that aims to avoid the immune escape of cancer cells so that the body's immune system can effectively eliminate them is now being used to mitigate side effects. Specific antibody drugs are currently used to treat various cancers [42, 43]. Pembrolizumab and nivolumab are used clinically to inhibit programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1) by helping to unlock the ability of the immune system to fight cancers. Ipilimumab boosts the immune system by inhibiting cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA‐4) [42, 43]. Recently, CA and its derivatives have been found to have the potential to treat diverse inflammation‐related diseases, such as neurological diseases, breast cancers, gastric cancers, and colon cancers, through intervening in cancer signaling [2, 3, 16, 19, 32, 44, 45]. This study aims to inhibit HT‐29 cells in vivo through immunotherapy by dietary CA. CA has been found to possess strong potential for anti‐inflammation and inhibition of human colorectal cancer HT‐29 cells in vitro and in vivo [3, 19, 20]. Our results evidenced that CA administration can inhibit cancer cell growth while restoring M1‐polarized immune balance but lowering serum IgG and IgM levels in HT‐29 cell‐bearing nude mice, suggesting CA's anti‐CRC potential through immunotherapy in vivo [20]. Subsequently, the immune status in the tumor microenvironment can be further explored by detecting the distribution of immune cells. The metabolic pathway, overcoming resistance in cancer therapy, and adjuvant properties of CA in the body should be analyzed, with the hope of unraveling the immunotherapy of colorectal cancer in the future [16, 46]. Unfortunately, there is no deep information concerning the underlying action mechanisms of CA on colorectal cancer HT‐29 cells, which further compromises the potential application of CA.

The present experiment has achieved several scientific merits; however, a few drawbacks remain to be further unraveled. First, the study uses BALB/c nude mice (T‐cell immunodeficient) while aiming to explore Th1/Th2 immune balance, which presents a fundamental logical contradiction. The severely impaired T‐cell function in nude mice makes it difficult to accurately and fully reflect changes in the Th1/Th2 balance. Lacking a fully functional T‐cell compartment substantially weakens the central conclusions regarding Th1/Th2 immune balance. Thus, immunological interpretations are not fully supported by the animal model employed. However, residual immune activity in BALB/c nude mice may persist, which is fair, but this biological limitation should be more explicitly reflected in the interpretation. Although this does not invalidate the experimental observations, it does limit the strength of mechanistic conclusions. In the present experiment, the observed cytokine secretion patterns suggest altered immune‐associated signaling. The findings may indicate immunomodulatory tendencies, but the restoration of adaptive Th1 immunity is not appropriate in the established animal model. It is recommended to switch to an immunocompetent mouse model (e.g., C57BL/6) and establish a syngeneic tumor model in the future. However, BALB/c nude mice may still partially retain immunity and are suitable for a xenograft tumor mouse model [20]. Because HPLC failed to detect CA in sera, serious flaws in CA metabolism and detection methods might exist in this in vivo experiment. This phenomenon was attributed to CA's rapid metabolism in vivo; however, it did not use LC‐MS/MS to confirm possible metabolites or provide pharmacokinetic parameters (e.g., half‐life, bioavailability). Additionally, heating serum samples at 80°C may cause CA degradation, necessitating method optimization. Only cytokines secreted by splenocytes in vitro were detected in the present experiment; however, there was no examination of immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment (e.g., T cell and macrophage phenotypes), cytokine levels within tumor tissues, or dynamic changes in peripheral blood cytokines, making it difficult to assess immunomodulatory effects comprehensively. The study did not explore the molecular mechanisms by which CA regulates the Th1/Th2 balance (e.g., whether through influencing transcription factors such as T‐bet/GATA3, or signaling pathways like JAK‐STAT and NF‐κB). Additionally, it did not investigate its effects on tumor cell migration, although CA has been found to decrease HT‐29 cell growth through the Bax/Bcl‐2 pathway in an immunotherapy manner in vitro [19]. As a result, the profound implications of CA in inhibiting colorectal cancer growth remain unclear. It is recommended to incorporate research on the molecular mechanisms underlying the signaling pathways through which CA exerts its anticolorectal‐cancer effects. There was a poor positive control, because paclitaxel was administered via intraperitoneal injection, which differs from the oral intervention route of CA. The positive control group was paclitaxel‐treated animals that underwent repeated intraperitoneal intervention, whereas comparator groups did not receive equivalent sham injections. This is an important limitation because procedural stress may influence body weight, metabolism, and immune parameters; therefore, comparisons involving the positive control should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, paclitaxel itself has strong immunomodulatory effects, making direct comparison inappropriate. A positive control with the same route of administration (e.g., oral 5‐fluorouracil) should be established in the future. Other limitations in the present experiment include: the use of a single colorectal cancer cell line, subcutaneous xenograft rather than orthotopic disease, and immunocompromised host biology. These are standard limitations in preclinical oncology and do not diminish the exploratory value of the work, but appropriately bound translational interpretation should be kept in mind. There were insufficient sample sizes and non‐rigorous statistical methods due to a reduced amount of animal use, bringing in sample sizes in some groups were only 3–6, and Duncan's multiple range test was used without correction for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni). Only descriptive scoring of tumor tissues was performed (Table 4); however, immunohistochemical staining (e.g., for Ki‐67, CD3, CD8, and CD68) to quantify tumor proliferation and immune cell infiltration was not conducted. Finally, the present experiment used Duncan's multiple range test for analysis. The statistical analysis appears acceptable for exploratory preclinical work, but several limitations should be openly acknowledged. Duncan's multiple range test is less conservative than more modern post hoc approaches, such as Tukey's HSD or Holm‐adjusted pairwise comparisons. In Duncan's multiple range test, multiple endpoints are assessed without formal multiplicity correction, leading to interpretation of weak/non‐significant correlations. Although this does not necessarily invalidate the results, it affects confidence in the strength of inference, particularly for the correlation analyses. Therefore, the interpretation of the results using Duncan's multiple range test should be approached with caution. Biological discussion of directional trends is acceptable, but mechanistic inference from weak and non‐significant associations should be avoided.

Conclusions

This study adopted an established xenograft HT‐29 cell animal model to evaluate the effects of CA intervention on cancer cell growth and Th1/Th2 immune balance in vivo. The results showed that HT‐29 cell‐bearing mice tended to have a Th2‐biased immune balance. After CA intervention, splenocytes tended to increase IFN‐γ secretion under Con A stimulation, which moved the experimental mice toward a relatively Th1‐inclined immune balance. CA showed modest trends toward reduced tumor burden under the experimental conditions tested, while reversing the Th2‐biased immune balance in CRC‐bearing mice.
