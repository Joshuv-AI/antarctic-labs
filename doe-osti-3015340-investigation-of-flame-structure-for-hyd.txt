EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 We have performed an investigation of flame structure and dynamics for gas
turbine combustion with hydrogen (H2) and another hydrogen-based fuel, ammonia
(NH3), and with mixtures of these fuels with natural gas (NG). The objective of the
research was to explore processes such as flame stabilization, ignition, and
flashback, and to characterize combustion efficiency and pollutant emissions under
combustion conditions characteristic of commercial aeroderivative and heavy-duty
F- and H-class gas-turbine systems. Our research focused on a multi-stage, multi-
tube micromixing (M3) injector with straight channels to carry the heated air and
featuring staged transverse jet injection of fuels to vary the degree of premixing
at the channel exit. The system was configured so that any of the three fuels, H2,
NH3 or NG, or their blends, could be injected at any of the multiple injection
locations.
 A test rig configured for combustion dynamics characterization with well-
defined acoustic boundaries and high-frequency pressure instrumentation was
used for the M3 injector experiments. Optical access downstream of the injector
was attained by using thin windows to contain the flame gases. Experiments
were performed at pressures of approximately 10 bar and the combustion
dynamics of different fuel mixtures were investigated for a very wide range of
fuel mixture compositions. The flame structure and dynamics were investigated
using several different laser diagnostics including, ultrahigh-speed planar laser-
induced fluorescence (PLIF) of OH and NO, in addition to high-speed
chemiluminescence. The results of our measurements have been published in a
number of archival journal articles and conference papers and there are at least
three groups at other universities who are performing advanced numerical
simulations of our experiment.

 i. 𝐌 𝟑 Injector Sizing
 The residence time of combustion gas in the combustor is designed to be 10-15 ms to
 match typical gas turbine combustors. Considering the acoustic speed of the
 combustion gas and the resulting combustor length, an acoustic frequency of 600 Hz
 was determined. An injector Mach number of 0.24 was also targeted to ensure enough
 flow velocity to prevent flashback.
 The injector is designed around a baseline test case with an air mass flow rate of 1 lb/s,
 an adiabatic flame temperature of 3100 °F, a natural gas to ammonia/hydrogen ratio
 (X) of 0.5, and an ammonia decomposition efficiency (𝜂) of 0.5. The final two
 parameters prescribe the fuel composition through the following expression.

 ii. 𝐌 𝟑 Injector Acoustic Design
 The Generalized combustion Instability Model (GIM) utilizes the linearized Euler
 equations to study the acoustic response of a system in 1D with given boundary

 5

conditions [1]. The analysis divides the test article geometry into four domains. Three
domains correspond to regions of fuel/air mixing in the injector. The upstream most
injector domain is modeled as air and ammonia. An addition of natural gas occurs in
the second domain, and a mixture of hydrogen and nitrogen in the third. The fourth
domain is the combustor, which interfaces with the third injector domain at the burner
face. This analysis assumes choked boundary conditions, and considers mean flow
effects.
The result of the GIM analysis is provided in Figure 1. In order to promote pressure
coupled response at the entrance to the combustion chamber, a pressure antinode is
located at the burner face. The total injector length is 17”, and the combustor length is
27.5”.
A similar analysis was conducted using an acoustics solver in ANSYS Workbench.
This solver uses the same governing equations as GIM, but is not limited to 1D,
axisymmetric geometries. Figure 2 illustrates the acoustic mode shape in the 3-
dimensional injector and combustor.

iii. 𝐌 𝟑 Injector Mechanical Design
The M3 injector is partitioned into three sections which are adjoined via flanges and
sealing surfaces. This design allows for individual modulation of each of the three fuel
injection schemes to tune injector dynamics. Each fuel injection component consists of
tubes equally spaced in a hexagonal pattern, as shown in Figure 3. Each tube has two
fuel injection holes diametrically opposed. These tubes are inserted into a “spool”
assembly of two custom flanges adjoined by a section of pipe. The tubes are welded to
the outer edge of each flange, resulting in a sealed volume between the outer diameter
of the tubes and the pipe. This volume serves as the manifold to supply fuel to each
injector element. Figure 4 depicts a cross-section view of one “spool” with the element
tubes highlighted.
The ammonia and natural gas spools share the same design and only vary geometrically
in length and axial fuel injection location. The hydrogen/nitrogen however, requires a
unique geometry to interface with the optically accessible combustion chamber. The
upstream flange, known as the interface flange, serves as the connection point and
sealing surface between M3 and the combustion chamber. Figure 5 shows a section
view of the hydrogen/nitrogen spool. Part of the fuel flow path is machined into the
flange.
Figure 6 and Figure 7 illustrate the processes required to assemble and install the M3
injector into the combustion chamber. The hydrogen/nitrogen spool is placed face
down and the natural gas spool is bolted to it from the top. Then, the ammonia spool is
bolted to the upstream end of the natural gas spool. The assembled injector is then
positioned in the combustor chamber and bolted to its upstream end. Finally, the venturi
transition duct and choke plate are bolted to the upstream end of the M 3 injector.

Subtask 2.2: Develop Modular Combustion Chamber. (Completed)
 The injector was installed into a new combustion chamber with a matching rectangular
 cross-section. The combustion chamber was developed with quartz windows to provide
 optical access to the flame. A combination of water-cooling and film-cooling was
 implemented to manage the high thermal loads, based on successful designs from
 previous test articles. Changes in the combustor length were enabled with a modular
 mechanical design, such that the bulk residence time of the reacting flow can be tuned
 between 5 and 15 milliseconds. Two exit geometries were developed: 1) an open exit
 discharge to enable emissions sampling at a specific residence time and 2) a choked
 exit discharge to simulate the acoustic reflection from a turbine nozzle guide vane for
 the study of combustion instability.

 i. High Pressure Feed System and Ammonia Vaporizer
 Within the design envelope, the largest ammonia flowrate occurs at large values of 𝑋
 and small values of 𝜂. It was determined that 30 gallons of bulk ammonia storage is
 sufficient to operate COMRAD at these conditions.
 The high-pressure ammonia supply consists of four 7.5-gallon piston accumulators.
 The accumulators are filled individually, then configured together as a combined 30-
 gallon volume. The necessary valving to operate tanks individually or as a combined

 10

volume is shown in Figure 8. Regulated nitrogen gas was used as the system pressurant.
This pressurant is supplied through the existing facility nitrogen system, and is capable
of 413 bar. A CAD rendering of the ammonia feed system is shown in Figure 9.
Immediately downstream of the high-pressure ammonia supply is the ammonia
vaporizer. Figure 10 illustrates the thermodynamic state of ammonia on a pressure
enthalpy diagram as it passes through the vaporizer. By supplying ammonia to the
vaporizer at 138 bar or greater, transition through the vapor dome is avoided. It is
desirable to circumvent the vapor dome due to the instabilities associated with phase
change. The supercritical ammonia exiting the heater is expanded down to the target
experiment conditions using a venturi.
The power required to vaporize ammonia for various fuel blends is shown in Figure
11. At an overall pressure ratio (OPR) of ~10 (174 psia), a 54kW heater exceeds the
requirement for all fuel blends within the design envelope. At OPR ~30 (435 psia), a
catalytic decomposition efficiency of 71% can be simulated with fuel made up entirely
of 𝑁𝐻3 and 𝐻2 . An existing 54 kW fuel heater has been demonstrated in COMRAD to
heat FT-SPK (a jet-A surrogate) up to 570K at 50 bar. An image of this fuel heater is
shown in Figure 12. This fuel heater served as the ammonia vaporizer for this work.

 A water-cooled probe was used to extract exhaust samples for composition analysis in

 Lean Blowout (LBO) conditions were experimentally determined for some range of
 operating parameters by varying the fuel flow rate while all other flow conditions were
 held constant. Optical emission, or rather the cessation of optical emission, was used
 to detect the point of LBO.

Subtask 4.2: Develop Parametric Stability Map with Baseline Instrumentation.

 During the FY2022Q3 Reporting period, 43 hot fire tests were conducted with the
 dynamics configuration to characterize influence of fuel composition on self-excited
 combustion instabilities. A mean chamber pressure of 12 bar was targeted, with a total
 reactant flowrate of 0.3 kg/s. A preliminary stability map was generated with hydrogen
 (with nitrogen addition to simulate ammonia decomposition) and natural gas at flame
 temperatures of 2750, 3000, and 3250 F. Ignition was achieved using a laser induced
 spark with a single-element operating with premixed hydrogen-air. A combustor
 pressure field was characterized with an array of high frequency pressure transducers
 as seen in Figure 30. High speed broadband chemiluminescence imaging was used with
 a spatial resolution of 768 x 480 at a framerate of 60 kHz.

 During the FY2022Q4 reporting period, the data from the natural gas and hydrogen
tests was analyzed to understand the sensitivity to fuel composition on the combustion
instabilities in the experiment. The mean pressure fluctuation amplitude (p’) is evaluated
from the pressure time history within the interrogation window for each condition. Figure
39 portrays a map of global flame stability as a function of flame temperature and hydrogen
addition. For all target flame temperatures, an inverse correlation between instability
amplitude and hydrogen addition is observed. Pressure fluctuation amplitudes as large as
35% of the mean chamber pressure are present in cases where hydrogen fraction is less
than 70%. Conversely, p’ amplitudes are less than 5% in all conditions where hydrogen
fraction is 80% or larger.
 Combustion instabilities result from a resonant coupling of the acoustic field in the
combustor with the heat release from the flame. The time-scales of the flame are based on
the mixing and kinetic rates of the fuel and air, which for natural gas and hydrogen are
significantly different. As the hydrogen content in the fuel is increased, the ignition delay
of the fuel mixture decreases. Similarly, hydrogen has a higher diffusivity allowing faster
mixing with air, even though it is injected last in the fuel injection scheme of the M 3
injector. Disparate chemical time-scales between natural gas and hydrogen can result in a
phase-relationship between flame heat release and acoustic wave of the combustor such
that resonant couplings at different distinct frequencies are attenuated to different extents.
 Chemical kinetic modeling of the fuel composition flame speed was performed to
further illustrate the effects of fuel composition on self-excited combustion instabilities.
Laminar flame speeds for reactant mixtures of air with H2, natural gas, and nitrogen are
calculated using GRI-Mech 3.0. Flame speeds were calculated at 10.7 bar using a 755 K
reactant inlet temperature and a varying equivalence ratio that provided an adiabatic flame

 25

temperature of 1922 K. It should be noted that the studied flow field is highly turbulent,
but laminar flame speed still acts as a reasonable indicator of chemical reaction rates and
their sensitivity to operating condition. Figure 40 shows the laminar flame speed as a
function of hydrogen fraction for the parameterized fuel blend used in this work. Also
depicted are the flame speeds for H2 and natural gas, which serve as the upper and lower
boundaries for the fuel blend flame speed. The rate of change of flame speed shows a strong
dependence on 𝜒, with the largest change in SL occurring between a hydrogen fraction of
0.6 and 1.0. This also designates the stability limit of the combustor at a flame temperature
of 1900 K, furthering the hypothesis of flame speed playing a role in combustion dynamics
sensitivity.
 The sensitivity of hydrogen content in the fuel to combustion instabilities appears to
change at different flame temperatures. For T CC,eq greater than 1900 K, the pressure
fluctuation amplitude is observed below 5% for hydrogen fractions greater than 60%. At
flame temperatures near 1750 K however, strong combustion instabilities persist until a
hydrogen fraction of 70% or greater. This does not appear to be a monotonic trend, as only
a 10% p’/pcc is present in the 𝜒 = 0.5, TCC,eq = 1750 K case. At lower flame temperatures,
the reduction in equivalence ratio would increase the preheating timescale of reactants.
This preheating process is susceptible to pressure and temperature oscillations in the
combustor, which can affect the reaction rate of the local reactant mixture based on the
modified Arrhenius equation. Based on the phase of the oscillation, the resulting change in
heat release oscillation can strengthen or attenuate instabilities.
 Spectral Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (SPOD) is a frequency domain form of
proper orthogonal decomposition that yields dynamic modes of statistically stationary flow
data. The decomposition of chemiluminescence intensity fluctuations yields energy ranked
spatially orthogonal modes corresponding to single frequencies of these fluctuations. The
modes can be isolated and reconstructed in space and time, which can be used to
differentiate flame dynamics between operating conditions. For each condition, intensity
fluctuations are decomposed over 300 cycles of the 2L acoustic mode within the limit cycle
region of the pressure time history. A hamming window with a window size of 150 cycles
at the 2L frequency and a 50% window overlap is utilized.

 It is understood from first principles that the resonant coupling between heat release
rate fluctuations and acoustic pressure fluctuations drives thermoacoustic instabilities as
described in the Rayleigh criterion. Consequently, the observed changes in dynamics
caused by changes in fuel composition and flame temperature must be altering the phase
relationship between q' and p' to lead to growth or attenuation of instability amplitude. To
investigate this, one dimensional, freely propagating premixed laminar flame simulations
were performed in Cantera. Reactions of air with hydrogen, natural gas, and nitrogen are
performed using CEU-NH3-Mech 1.1. Reactions proceed at 10.7 bar using a 755 K
reactant inlet temperature and a varying equivalence ratio to produce a 𝑇𝑎𝑑 of 1750 K. It
should be noted that while the studied flow field is highly turbulent, analysis of laminar
flame modeling can still illustrate the operating condition sensitivity to chemical reaction
rates. The evolution of gas temperature and heat release rate across the length of the flame
is illustrated in Figure 45 for a representative test condition with 𝜒=50. For each simulation,
the location of preheat onset is defined when the gas temperature rise first exceeds 1% of
the total temperature rise. The end of the preheat zone and beginning of the flame zone is
delineated by a heat release rate increase that first exceeds 1% of the maximum. The end
of the flame zone is prescribed at the location where the gas temperature reaches 99% of
the total temperature rise.

 During the FY2024Q1 reporting period, further analysis and processing of dynamics
sensitivity to fuel composition was performed. Investigation of the in-phase coupling
between pressure fluctuations and heat release oscillations provides insight into the
sensitivity of the system dynamics to fuel composition. Pressure fluctuations in the
chamber are decomposed using multivariate singular spectrum analysis (mSSA). Singular
spectrum analysis is a signal processing technique that performs eigenvalue decomposition
on a lagged-covariance matrix generated from time series data. The projection of the time
series data on the resulting eigenvectors yields the principal components which act as
filtered components of the original time series. mSSA allows for phase synchronization
analysis of a system of coupled oscillators with no prior phase knowledge required. A
multivariate system composed of 100 ms time samples of two pressure sensors in the
injector, and either two or three injectors in the combustion chamber was utilized as the
input signal. Each probe is then reconstructed for the fifteen most dominant modes of the
system. Figure 50 depicts the percent variance of each mode, along with the cumulative
variance up to a given mode. Across all unstable cases, the first fifteen modes consistently
captures ~80% of the total variance.
 For each reconstructed mode, a one-dimensional spatial distribution of p’ is generated
using the computed mode frequency and equilibrium acoustic speed in the chamber. A
pressure anti-node boundary condition is enforced at the exit of the nozzle plate. P’
amplitude as a function of axial chamber length is computed using a least-square fit of a
sinusoidal wave. The individual 1D profiles are then superposed, yielding a spatial
distribution of p’ corresponding to the fifteen highest energy modes identified in the mSSA
decomposition. A representative p’ distribution is depicted on Figure 51. Spatial p'
reconstruction computed for rank 1-15 modes identified in mSSA decomposition. The
largest contribution to amplitude is the 2L mode, which is observed as a full wave in the
chamber. Due to the enforced pressure anti-node at the exit nozzle, this results in a pressure
anti-node at the injector dump plane where the flame stabilizes.

reconstruction uses these two sensors along with an additional sensor located between the

TASK 5.0 – Perform Advanced Diagnostics of Flow-Flame Structure and Dynamics
  The multi-parameter maps generated in Tasks 3 and 4 were used to select cases for detailed
  characterization with advanced diagnostics. These tests began with imaging of the flow
  and flame structure using hydroxyl (OH) planar laser induced fluorescence (PLIF).

 Subtask 5.1: Perform Simultaneous High-Speed PIV and OH PLIF Measurements.

 The initial OH PLIF measurements were acquired at a rate of 10 kHz and captured a large
 field of view to resolve the large-scale structure and dynamics of the flow and flame.

 Subtask 5.2: Perform Ultrahigh-Speed PIV and OH-PLIF Measurements.

 i. Diagnostic Setup
 The sensitivity of dynamics over a wide range of fuel compositions through pressure
 and chemiluminescence measurements provides us with a good understanding of
 directional sensitivities. However, the underlying physical mechanisms that result in
 significant changes to flame structure with small changes in fuel composition are still
 unclear. To address this, spatially and temporally resolved OH-PLIF measurements at 100
 kHz with variations in ammonia and natural gas were performed during the FY2024Q3
 reporting period.
 The OH PLIF system consisted of a flash-lamp pumped, Nd:YAG burst-mode laser
 (BML) (Spectral energies QuasiModo) and an optical parametric oscillator (OPO). The
 BML produced high-energy pulse bursts at 100 kHz with a pulse width of 10 ns. The
 duration of the burst was selected to adequately capture the timescales that govern the
 behavior of the flame. Due to pulse energy drop off across the burst duration, a 2 ms
 window was used for all diagnostics, which corresponds to 200 laser pulses.
 A diagnostic diagram of the BML and OPO system is shown in Figure 60. The OPO
 was pumped in a double-pass configuration by the frequency-tripled 355 nm output of the
 BML. Two uncoated, Type-I 𝛽-barium-borate (BBO) crystals were used to manually tune
 the output wavelength to ∼609 nm. An external cavity diode laser (Sacher, Lion) was set
 to an idler wavelength of ∼851 nm to injection seed the cavity and produce a narrow signal
 line width. Only the signal beam was resonant in the cavity. The frequency-doubled 532
 nm output from the BML was mixed with the OPO output beam using a BBO crystal cut
 to allow the sum frequency generation (SFG) of ∼284 nm light. The resulting pulse energy
 was ≈1.5 mJ/pulse.
 Due to collisional broadening effects expected under elevated pressure conditions, a
 narrow bandwidth absorption line was not selected. Rather, the OPO system was tuned to
 an output of 284.007 nm between the 𝑄1(9) and 𝑄1(8) lines within the 𝐴2Σ+-𝑋2Π(1-0)
 band of OH. This frequency has been successfully demonstrated in previous work to detect
 the OH PLIF signal at around 5 bar. Under elevated pressure conditions, collisional
 broadening causes the 𝑄1(9) and 𝑄1(8) lines to broaden into one peak with increased
 bandwidth, allowing greater certainty in line tuning. A wavelength meter (HighFinesse
 WS6-200) was used to measure the wavelength of the BML and idler laser, thus ensuring
 that the proper excitation wavelength was achieved for each test.
 A negative and positive cylindrical lens pair was used to form a collimated laser sheet
 about 40 mm tall from the 284 nm beam. The sheet was focused in the test section using
 cylindrical lenses with f = 150 mm, which entered the experiment through a fused quartz

 46

window. A 320 nm band pass filter with a 40 nm bandwidth (Semrock 320/40 Brightline
Bandpass) was used to suppress scattered light and collect OH fluorescence in the 𝐴2Σ+-
𝑋2Π(1-1) and 𝐴2Σ+-𝑋2Π(0-0) bands. Signal was collected with an f = 100 mm UV grade
objective lens with f/2.8 (Cerco). OH PLIF images were recorded using a Phantom TMX
7510 camera coupled to a Lambert HiCATT 25 intensifier with a 1:1 relay lens. A 9.7 µs
exposure was used with an image size of 1280 by 512 pixels, resulting in a spatial
resolution of 12.4 pixels/mm.
 Just downstream of the first focus lens, a UV beam sampler (Thorlabs BSF20-UV) was
installed to reflect 8% incident light on a N-BK7 glass diffuser (Thorlabs DG20-600-MD).
A Phantom v411 camera equipped with a Nikkor f = 50 mm lens with f/1.4 (Nikon), and a
0.1 OD neutral density filter (Thorlabs ND2R01A) was used to capture the fluorescence
produced on the glass. Images were captured with a 512 by 64 pixel window and a 9.36 µs
exposure time, resulting in a spatial resolution of 6.9 pixels/mm. Laser sheet imaging was
performed synchronously with the OH PLIF camera at 100 kHz. Each row of pixels was
summed to produce a 1D profile of the laser sheet intensity.
 During the FY2024Q4 period, a sheet correction algorithm was developed to account
for spatial and temporal non-uniformity of the laser pulse energy, which must be accounted
for, as the fluorescence signal linearly increases with excitation energy. To correct this
variation, 1D normalized laser sheet intensity profiles were first scaled and interpolated to
match the resolution of the PLIF images. Then, each pixel column of the PLIF image was
divided by the corresponding laser sheet intensity value. The sheet correction process is
shown in Figure 61. In Figure 61b, the regions near the injector face and at the downstream
end of the FOV are dark due to lower laser intensity. This signal variation is consistent
with the 1D laser sheet intensity profile shown in Figure 61a. The result of the sheet
correction in Figure 61c shows that the extent of the OH regions become clearer, providing
a more representative illustration of the distribution of OH. To account for the drop off of
pulse energy in time, each corrected frame is multiplied by the corresponding integrated
sheet intensity value. This allows for a more faithful comparison of the PLIF images at
different times. Unless otherwise specified, all subsequent results will involve analysis of
corrected images.

 During the FY2025Q2 reporting period, additional analysis of the OH-PLIF results was
performed. Extraction of the OH edge front plays a crucial role in quantitative analysis of
OH-PLIF images, as the classical understanding of flame chemistry indicates the sharpest
gradients in OH concentration occur when heat release is maximized. Traditional methods
for edge detection often involve filtering, thresholding, and subsequent binarization.
Previous studies have successfully leveraged these segmentation methods, but key
limitations to these methods remain. The broad range of length scales observed in highly
turbulent flames leads to a large dynamic range of OH signal intensity. Additional effects
such as laser sheet absorption and non-uniformity in the sheet profile contribute further to
spatial variations in the OH intensity gradient. Thus, specific thresholding parameters for
binarization require careful tuning, and a single set of parameters is often unsatisfactory
over large datasets spanning disparate fuel compositions. With recent advancements in
deep learning, convolutional neural network (CNN) approaches to image classification
provide an alternative to the conventional OH-PLIF edge tracking methods.
 The model developed for this study utilizes a Deeplabv3+ semantic segmentation
architecture with a ResNet-101 image classification network backbone. This architecture
remains on the leading edge of deep learning methods for image segmentation and has been
previously applied to OH-PLIF images, successfully identifying the attached and lifted
flame regions of a liquid-fueled swirl stabilized flame. The training dataset in the present
study consisted of 152 ground truth images labeled manually corresponding to the full
range of fuel compositions and heat release structures studied in this work. A separate set
of 20 images were also manually labeled and utilized as the validation dataset. Typical
edge detection methods classify each pixel as either OH signal or background, allowing for
total OH area to be determined from the binarized image. This approach however requires
distinction between signal and background pixels on both the reactant-to-OH and OH-to-
product interfaces, which see dissimilar OH gradients. The reactant-to-OH surface exhibits
a stronger gradient than the OH-to-product surface. Thus, each pixel is instead classified
as reactant or background. This method of segmentation provides greater consistency in
the spatial features the model is trained to track at the cost of no longer being able to extract
the total OH area. To enhance the training dataset, a data augmentation pipeline involving
random distortions of the ground truth images was implemented. This augmentation
pipeline consisted of horizontal and vertical reflections, elastic transformations, grid
distortions, and brightness distortions that are applied randomly to each image of the
training dataset.
 Training of the model utilizes stochastic gradient descent with Nesterov momentum. A
dynamic learning rate of 0.01 is utilized with a plateauing scheduler that reduces the
learning rate by half after no decrease in training loss is observed over 5 consecutive
iterations (epochs). A weight decay of 5𝐸4 is implemented to reduce susceptibility to
overfitting through penalization of large model parameters. Training occurs over 30 epochs
with a batch size of two. This epoch number was determined to be sufficient by an observed
increase in validation loss and simultaneous decrease in test loss after the 12𝑡ℎ epoch,
indicating model overfitting. This analysis utilizes the model parameters that produce a

 54

local minimum in validation loss. Another meaningful performance parameter is the
intersect over union (IoU), defined as the ratio of area overlap between prediction and
ground truth to the total area encompassed by both. The best performing model parameters
in this work yielded a mean IoU of 85%. A representative OH-PLIF image and
corresponding output of the CNN are depicted on Figure 75.

the shear layer. As this hydrogen is substituted for methane, the decrease in reaction rates
shifts the structure of this heat release further downstream. Slower reaction kinetics acts to
increase reactant residence time. This increase allows unburned gas to persist into the inter-

 55

flame regions where, as seen in regions (ii) and (iii) of Figure 76, the resulting heat release
occurs over a longer period supporting the half-wave mode of the combustor.

 During the FY2025Q3 reporting period, additional analysis of the OH-PLIF edge
tracking results was performed. Calculations of the flame curvature were performed in
order to highlight changes in the local flame structure due to various fuel compositions.
To compute curvature, instantaneous flame edge contours are first parameterized by the
contour arc-length (s). The direction of increasing s (𝑠̂ ) traces each curve such that 𝑠̂ × 𝑛̂ =
 𝑧̂ , where 𝑛̂ is the unit normal vector that points in the direction of flame propagation. The
local radius of curvature is then calculated. In conjunction with the described trace notation,
the curvature formulation indicates the presence of positive (negative) curvature when the
flame surface is convex (concave) to the reactants. To mitigate digitization from affecting
the curvature computation, the coordinates of each edge were re-sampled over a ∼0.8 mm
inner cutoff scale, which is determined from the circle method. Cubic spline curves are
then fit to the resampled coordinates, from which the curvature is then computed.
 Figure 78 depicts the probability density function (PDF) of R for cases of varying
ammonia addition (η). As H2 is replaced with NH3, the distributions are observed to narrow
and shift slightly towards smaller magnitudes. This is linked to a disparity of surface
dilatation observed near the flame base between these cases. As seen on Figure 78, the
flame surface density in this region is quite compact, suggesting a relatively flat flame
element with low curvature. To investigate this, additional PDFs of R are computed for
flame edges between X = 5 mm and X = 10 mm, which are illustrated on Figure 79. The
A1 case, which has greatest hydrogen fuel mole fraction, sees a shorter and more
symmetric distribution than the A3 or A6 case, which contain more ammonia. Recent direct
numerical simulations are consistent with this finding, highlighting the base of a turbulent
H2-air jet flame producing little curvature, and flame surface production being largely
governed by strain effects. The resulting region is characterized by high heat release rate
and a balance of surface production and surface annihilation, consistent with the relatively
constant flame brush thickness observed from X = 5 mm to x = 8 mm on Figure 78. A flat
flame structure caused by the enhancement of thermo-diffusive instabilities juxtaposes the
traditional visualization of cellular flame structures arising from hydrogen flame
propagating in quiescent flow. Such contrast highlights the inherent link between these
flame structures and the surrounding high-pressure turbulent flow field. This finding also
contrasts similar jet flame structures at lower turbulence intensities, which observed
positively curved bulges with hydrogen addition to methane near the flame base. The
enhancement of kinematic restoration in this region would also result in broader heat
release response in the frequency domain, which could impact the excitation of resonant
acoustic modes in the combustor. It is possible this region corresponds to one of net
positive stretch, leading to rapid heat release rate and flame surface annihilation. This is
consistent with the relatively constant flame brush thickness observed from X = 5 mm to x
= 8 mm on Figure 78. Enhancement of kinematic restoration in this region would also result
in broader heat release response in the frequency domain, which could impact the
excitation of resonant acoustic modes in the combustor.
 Returning to Figure 79, a bias towards negative curvature is observed in all cases. This
is because near the flame tip, negative curvature becomes dominant and the formation of
cusps is observed. This bias is also more pronounced for the A1 case than the A6 case,
correlating this disparity to the prevalence of hydrogen in the fuel mixture. This is
attributed to the difference in flame propagation behavior near the jet tip. In this region,

 56

 thermo-diffusive instabilities characteristic of hydrogen flames enhance the effect of
 kinematic restoration and flame-flame interactions, forming strong cusps before rapid
 surface annihilation. To illustrate this further, PDFs of R are generated for flame edges
 between X = 14 mm and X = 30 mm and depicted in Figure 79. The negative curvature
 bias in the A1 case is even more pronounced here, confirming a large contribution is due
 to cusps near the jet tip being enhanced through hydrogen addition.

 Subtask 5.3: Perform NO PLIF Measurements. (Completed)
 PLIF measurements of NO were performed in a 20-30mm wide region immediately
 downstream of the injector exit.

 i. Diagnostic Setup

 65

 The coupled flame structure and thermoacoustic behaviors were investigated using the
OH-PLIF diagnostic technique. Here, the structure of NO formation in the flame is studied
using PLIF of the NO molecule at 100 kHz, which allows for a spatially and temporally
resolved understanding of emissions formation in the flame.
 The NO PLIF system was developed during the FY2025 Q3 period, and is similar to
the OH PLIF system, consisting of the same laser and OPO setup as described previously.
A diagnostic diagram of the BML and OPO system is shown in Figure 80. The OPO was
pumped in a double-pass configuration by the frequency-tripled 355 nm output of the
BML, and two un-coated, Type-I 𝛽-barium-borate (BBO) crystals were used to manually
tune the output wavelength to ≈622 nm. An external cavity diode laser (Sacher, Lion) was
set to an idler wavelength of ≈827 nm to injection seed the cavity and produce a narrow
signal line-width. Only the signal beam was resonant in the cavity. A portion of the 355
nm pump was split from the BML output and mixed with the OPO output beam using a
BBO crystal cut to enable sum frequency generation (SFG). The OPO system was then
tuned to 226.03 nm to excite the P1(23.5) rotational transition of the A-X(0,0) of NO. This
transition has been used in previous literature, and was shown to have both high signal
intensities and low interference with molecular oxygen LIF at elevated pressures and
temperatures. The resulting pulse energy was ≈1 mJ/pulse.
 A set of cylindrical lenses was used to form a collimated laser sheet about 46 mm tall
from the 226 nm beam. The sheet was focused into the test section using cylindrical lenses
with f = 750 mm, which entered the experiment through a fused-silica window. The
combination of a 244 nm longpass filter (Semrock LP02-244RS) and a 265 nm short-pass
filter (Asahi Spectra ZUS0265) was used to suppress scattered light and collect
fluorescence of NO at the X(0,2) and A-X(0,3) bands. Signal was collected with a f =
100mm UV grade objective lens with f/2.8 (Cerco), and NO PLIF images were recorded
on a Phantom v2512 high-speed CMOS camera coupled to a Lambert HiCATT 25
intensifier with a 1:1 relay lens. A 15 𝜇s exposure was used with an image size of 640x432
pixels, resulting in a spatial resolution of 0.20 mm/pixel. The beam sampling and sheet
correction algorithm developed during the FY2024 Q4 period for the OH-PLIF results was
similarly employed for processing of the NO-PLIF images.

ii. Results
Experiments were conducted using a constant air flow rate of 0.454 ± 0.003 kg/s (∼ 1 lb/s),
an inlet temperature of 755 ± 3 K, and a chamber pressure of 10.8 ± 0.2 bar. An equivalence
ratio(𝜙) of 0.55 ± 0.02 was chosen for all tests. This value allowed for a wide flame stability
region and maintained (𝑇𝑎𝑑) close to 1920K (∼3000 F) for the range of fuel blends
considered. The fuel composition is defined using equation (1) where the hydrogen fuel is
considered to be derived from the decomposition of ammonia, which yields a 3:1 ratio of
hydrogen to nitrogen. The fraction of hydrogen-nitrogen to ammonia is defined by 𝜂, which
is the ammonia decomposition efficiency, such that a value of 0 corresponds to operation
with pure ammonia.
 Table 4 summarizes the test conditions considered for the present study. The value of
𝜂 was varied from around 0.9 to 0.4, which corresponds to ammonia mole fractions of
around 5% to 42%. For all reported test conditions, the maximum computed uncertainty
for chamber pressure and inlet air temperature measurements is 0.1% and 0.75%,
respectively. The resulting maximum computed uncertainties for 𝜙 and 𝜂 are 1.0% and
0.7%, respectively.

 66

 Table 4: Summary of test conditions.

 The effect of changing 𝜂 is explored to understand how various fuel compositions
affect the structure of NO formation. To serve as a point of reference for the NO PLIF
images, a 1D freely propagating flame model was developed in Cantera. The CEU-NH3-
Mech 1.1 mechanism was implemented as it has been experimentally validated for H2–
NH3 and CH4–NH3 mixtures at elevated pressures under both lean and rich conditions.
Figure 81 shows profiles of the simulated NO𝑥 species production at 𝜙 = 0.55 for varying
values of 𝜂 and combustor pressure. Across all conditions, NO remains the predominant
nitrogen oxide, followed by N2O, and finally NO2. NO concentration is higher for H2–
NH3 mixtures compared to pure NH3, as the presence of H2 increases the available oxygen
radicals that promote NO formation. At higher pressure, the NO concentration is
significantly decreased, and the fuel mixture producing maximum NO shifts from around
𝜂 = 0.6 to a more hydrogen-rich mixture near 𝜂 = 0.8, which is a trend consistent with
recent literature studying high-pressure H2–NH3 flames. A range of 𝜂 values was chosen
based on the trends of the kinetic model to investigate the dominant impacts on NO
concentration with changing ammonia addition.

 The NO PLIF image data is compared to OH PLIF data from experiments in the same
combustor to ground observations in relation to the flame front. Figure 82 shows
representative instantaneous OH PLIF (left) and NO PLIF (right) images collected at
condition A2. The OH PLIF image highlights the predominant features of the micromixer
flame: low-intensity regions define the reactant cores and are surrounded by compact, high-
intensity zones of heat release along the jet shear layer. The flame exhibits high surface
curvature due to the intense turbulence and presence of thermodiffusive instabilities as
driven by the hydrogen-rich fuel. Further downstream, surface curvature increases and the
reaction layers merge, forming intermittent reactant pockets and zones of high OH signal.
The signal in these regions eventually decays, resulting in a relatively low global OH signal
at the downstream end of the FOV. In the NO PLIF image, the overall shape of the reactant
cores is similar, and high-curvature flame features can be made out; however, multiple
differences are apparent. A steep, positive gradient is seen near the edges of the reactant
cores, indicating rapid NO production. Further downstream, the fluorescence intensity
appears relatively uniform and constant, even in the regions where high-intensity OH
pockets may occur.
 The observed NO gradients appear to be closely located to the high heat release regions
indicated in the OH signal. A spatial overlap of OH and NO is expected, as the fuel-NO𝑥
formation pathways require the presence of oxygen radicals. However, while OH can
provide a reliable marker of the flame zone, the profile of NO along the reaction front
depends on the reactant mixture and the operating conditions. To further investigate this
spatial overlap, the evolution of NO mole fraction, OH mole fraction, and heat release rate
along the flame is computed using the 1D Cantera model. Normalized profiles are shown

 67

in Figure 83 at a pressure of 1 bar and 10 bar for conditions A1 (𝜂 = 0.89) and A6 (𝜂 =
0.41). With both low and high ammonia fractions, the rise in NO mole fraction sits close
to the peak in heat release rate and the rise in OH mole fraction. This overlap is improved
significantly at higher pressure, as the reaction zone is more compact, which suggests that
the sharp increase of NO observed in the PLIF images can be assumed to occur across the
reaction layer. It is important to note that this model assumes a laminar 1D flame, while
the true flame behavior is highly turbulent and three-dimensional. However, it is expected
that the thin flamelet assumption remains valid at the experimental conditions, so that the
implications discussed for Figure 83 can be applied to the real flame.
 Despite the effectiveness of the present NO PLIF technique, there are multiple
challenges regarding the diagnostic setup and fluorescence behavior that lead to a reduced
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) compared to the OH PLIF image. Primarily, the NO
fluorescence quantum yield is significantly lower compared to OH due to high rates of
collisional quenching that are further amplified under high-pressure conditions. Absorption
of hot O2 and H2O is also a concern in the deep ultraviolet (UV) range, and is further
exacerbated at high-pressure, leading to attenuation of laser power and therefore
fluorescence intensity along the −𝑦-direction. Significant fluorescence on the fused quartz
tube also occurs due to the deep UV wavelength of the laser sheet, which results in a ≈25%
reduction in pulse energy. Spurious laser sheet reflections also occur, as is observed near
𝑦 = −20 mm in the NO PLIF image of Figure 82. The UV absorption of ammonia should
also be considered at this wavelength, which would increase the magnitude of laser sheet
attenuation. However, because of the fuel lean conditions and uniformity in the attenuation
behavior along the 𝑥-direction, this effect is expected to be minimal.
 In Figure 84, time-averaged and normalized images of the OH* chemiluminescence
(a1-a6) and NO PLIF (b1-b6) are shown to highlight the global changes in heat release
distribution and NO formation with varying ammonia addition. At condition A1, the OH*
CL image (a1) shows the compact conical flame structure typical in a hydrogen-rich
micromixer. The signal intensity is highest in the shear layer regions of the flame, and
promptly decreases downstream of the flame zone. In contrast, the NO PLIF image (b1)
exhibits a severely low SNR due to a globally low signal. The reactant core regions can
still be made out and appear uniformly conical, indicating high fuel reactivity and flame
speed. At condition A2, the mean NO PLIF signal (b2) has a significantly higher SNR,
indicating a drastic increase in NO concentration. The distribution of OH* within the flame
zone (a2) also appears broader, and the relative signal intensity downstream of the flame
zone remains much higher compared to (a1). This behavior is expected, as ammonia
addition reduces the flame temperature and mixture reactivity, resulting in less production
of oxygen radicals in the flame zone and slower consumption in the post-flame region due
to reduced rates for recombination reactions.
 As the ammonia content is increased further from condition A3 to A6, the flame zone
becomes increasingly broad (a3-a6), resulting in a further increase to the downstream OH*
signal intensity. Correspondingly, the mean NO distribution follows the flame front, which
transitions from a short conical shape to a longer and more cylindrical shape, indicating a
reduction in flame speed. A discrepancy in the mean length of the reactant cores can also
be observed, particularly in (b3). Previous numerical simulations of this combustor, along
with experiments employing OH PLIF, have shown that this behavior can be explained by
the intermittent influence of recirculation zones due to flame-flame and flame-wall
interactions. Across all conditions, the NO signal intensity in the post-flame region remains
generally constant within the FOV along the 𝑥-direction. This trend is characteristic of lean

 68

premixed, single-stage H2–NH3 flames, as the lack of intermediate fuel radicals such as
NH and NH2 leads to minimal NO consumption through De-NO𝑥 reaction pathways, even
as residence time increases. It should be noted that attenuation in the NO signal intensity
is more prominent at the highest ammonia conditions (b5-b6), and is attributed to a lower
NO concentration that is more greatly affected by the laser absorption and power loss
mechanisms discussed previously.
 Changes in the flame heat release profile with ammonia addition are further
emphasized in Figure 85, which shows 𝑦-direction summed profiles of normalized, time-
averaged OH* CL. From condition A1 to A2, only ≈5% addition of ammonia occurs by
mole, while the point of maximum intensity moves downstream by roughly 10 mm. A
drastic increase in intensity is also observed in the post-flame region, as seen in image (a2)
of Figure 84. In contrast, between conditions A2 and A6, the most significant change is the
broadening of the flame zone, while the location of maximum OH* intensity moves more
gradually. Clearly, only a small amount of ammonia addition is necessary to significantly
alter the global heat release profile at hydrogen-rich conditions.
 This sensitivity is investigated for the NO formation using spatiotemporal averages of
the NO PLIF image time series at each condition. Spatial averaging is performed in a
window around the top flame element to minimize the effect of signal attenuation and
quartz tube fluorescence on the average. The resulting global NO fluorescence intensity is
plotted against 𝜂 in Figure 86 and is compared to the normalized results of the kinetic
model. To justify this comparison, it should be noted that all tests were performed using
consistent settings for all detection systems and a consistent laser alignment. A similar
overall trend is apparent between the experiment and model, where NO first reaches a
maximum with a small amount of ammonia addition and decreases more gradually as
further ammonia is added. However, the measured NO intensity at condition A1 is
significantly less than the model prediction, and the drop off in intensity at higher ammonia
conditions appears more rapid in the measured data. This discrepancy indicates that the
kinetic model may under-predict the sensitivity of NO formation to ammonia content at
high-pressure conditions. The low signal intensity measured at condition A1 also suggests
that for the current experiment, slightly higher than predicted amounts of ammonia (5-10%
addition by mole) may be necessary to realize a drastic increase in NO formation. It then
follows that smaller ammonia fractions (<5%) may not be sufficient to activate the fuel-
NO𝑥 reaction pathways in this case.

 Figure 87 displays the NO PLIF image time series at condition A2, which reveals the
time-resolved evolution of NO formation structures in the high-pressure micromixer
flames. In the upper flame at 𝑡 = 0 μs, highly curved surface features can be made out near
the end of the reactant cores. Moving forward in time, these features convect downstream
and propagate normal to themselves. By 𝑡 = 40 μs, the surface features have been largely
consumed, resulting in a rapid increase in the local NO intensity. At 𝑡 = 50 μs, surface
wrinkling forms a pocket of reactants that is rapidly consumed by 𝑡 = 70 μs, resulting in
further NO production. These observations as a whole, along with the comparisons
discussed from Figure 82, indicate that the formation of NO follows the evolving flame
front, despite the highly stretched nature of the flame under high-pressure, hydrogen-rich
conditions.
 The NO formation behavior with increased ammonia addition is seen in Figure 88 with
an image time series of the upper flame element at condition A5. Despite the lower SNR,
the general structure of the evolving flame front can be surmised. At 𝑡 = 10 μs, the

 69

 formation of a large corrugation is observed at the upper flame surface near 𝑥 = 20 mm.
 Compared to the surface curvature in Figure 87, this feature is larger in size and appears to
 grow and roll up more slowly as it convects downstream. The feature eventually diffuses
 into the post flame region by 𝑡 = 70 μs, resulting in a more uniform NO intensity at 𝑥 = 20
 mm. Collectively, the image time series indicates that the amplitude of surface corrugation
 is larger compared to condition A2. This change has been investigated in previous studies
 using 100 kHz OH PLIF, and can be attributed to a more prominent influence of
 hydrodynamic instabilities, lower fuel reactivity, and a weaker impact from
 thermodiffusive instabilities. These effects reduce the degree of surface curvature
 destruction and promote stronger flame wrinkling.
 Larger amplitude surface fluctuations also explain the shallower gradients in NO
 intensity observed from the mean NO PLIF images in Figure 84 at conditions of higher
 ammonia content (b4-b6). The stronger fluctuations lead to a broader distribution of NO
 when time-averaged, giving the impression that the rate of NO production may be more
 gradual along the flame front. However, this is not the case, as the images in Figure 88
 show comparably strong NO gradients compared to Figure 87. This shows that even with
 higher ammonia content, NO formation along the flame front remains rapid. While the
 flame thickness is expected to increase due to ammonia addition, as seen in Figure 83, this
 change is much smaller than what can be captured with the spatial resolution of the current
 imaging setup.

The results of this project have been published in numerous archival journal articles and
conference papers. The M3 experiments are being modeled by at least three different
university groups. The technology and design for our ammonia supply system has been
shared with numerous other groups including NETL.

Not applicable.

The project has provided outstanding training opportunities for graduate students. Dr.

project.

infrastructure?
The demonstration of the M3 injector technology provides a basis for extending these
results to another DOE-funded project and probably to another project funded by a
power-generating gas turbine manufacturer. The ammonia supply technology will be
used in future projects.
