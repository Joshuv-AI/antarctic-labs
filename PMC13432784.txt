Industrial Hydrocarbons Exposure Deteriorates Sperm Integrity and Reproductive Function
Authors: Madhan Kumar Pichandi, Babujanarthanam Ranganathanan, Doraiah Abilash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOccupational exposure to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) or metal fumes in the workplace may be detrimental to male fertility. The present study compared sperm concentration, total motility, strict morphology and oxidative stress and SDF levels between 81 men, controls (n = 17) and workers from plastic industry workers (n = 18), steel EAF process (n = 15), waste incineration plants (n = 16) or aluminium factories smelting in the same plant for all workplaces involved in this cross‐sectional research. In all treated groups, the sperm concentration, motility, normal morphology, acrosome integrity and membrane function dropped significantly compared with those in controls (p < 0.001). Elevated ROS, MDA and low SOD and CAT activities confirmed the presence of severe oxidoreductive imbalance, particularly in Aluminium and incineration workers. DNA breaks assayed by SCD and comet showed a clear positive exposure‐response relationship with the highest OTM in aluminium workers. Network pharmacologic work showed that 67 shared targets were regulated by PAH exposure and male infertility. The hub gene analysis pointed at EGFR, ESR1, RELA, PTGS2, MMP2 and MMP9 as the core hub genes while KEGG enrichment suggested that the HIF‐1 and TNF‐related inflammatory pathway, along with the endocrine disruption side of stress activating signalling, was involved. Inflammatory signal activation and endocrine disturbance together seemed to end up with really severe damage to sperm DNA, and it also turned on a bunch of mechanisms through which these chemical carcinogens can exert effect. ROS and MDA were negatively correlated with motility as well as sperm concentration significantly (p < 0.05), whereas antioxidant enzymes showed a positive correlation. These findings show how PAH‐related industrial poisoning disrupts important reproductive output through ROS‐mediated genetic instability and suggest the relevance of protection measures in high‐risk industries.

Introduction

One common health issue caused by a malfunction with the male or female reproductive system is infertility. It is characterised having the inability to conceive after 12 months or more of consistent sexual activity without the use of birth control [1]. Globally, it affects 1 in 6 couples, a total of 48 million couples, along with 186 million individuals; more than half of the cases are caused by male causes [2]. Infertility has become more prevalent in recent years due to a number of factors, including: an interruption in the appearance of the first pregnancy; alterations in semen quality triggered by behaviours like smoking or consuming alcohol; or the consumption of hazardous substances in the atmosphere like polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), which affect seminal quality as well as male reproductive function [3]. Insufficient combustion of wood, biomass, oil, gas, as well as petroleum releases polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) into the atmosphere as a complex combination of molecules or other chemical compounds [4]. PAHs are a class of hazardous, lipophilic, and endocrine‐ disrupting compounds that are found in large quantities in the environment. PAHs can cause oxidative & nitrosative stress as one of their modes of action [5]. According to recent research, PAHs can seriously harm sperm DNA and induce oxidative stress in seminal plasma. Because of the quantity of membrane unsaturated fatty acids and the absence of cytoplasmic antioxidant enzymes, spermatozoa are extremely vulnerable to oxidation, which can result in major alterations to their number, concentration, motility & shape. Male fertility would be compromised as a result of the overall decline in sperm quality [6].

Employees of the plastic industry, steel in the Electric Arc Furnaces (EAF), waste incinerators and those working during aluminium production or smelting are easily potential targets for high concentration exposure to Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs) resulting from incomplete burning of organic materials and thermal industrial processes. Emission of PAH‐ containing vapour occurs in processing polymer heated and melted during plastics production, and from stocks of scrap metal melting and carbon electrode‐combustion in steel‐industry EAF operations [7]. Workers with skill trades in waste incineration are exposed to complex mixtures of PAHs derived from the combustion of municipal refuse and while handling contaminated ash residues; and aluminium smelting, particularly in potrooms, produces some of the highest exposures to carcinogenic PAHs, including coke and pitch fumes. They accumulate in the body and can cause oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, endocrine disruption, mitochondrial harm and sperm DNA damage [8]. There is increasing evidence that long‐term exposure to occupational PAH may be endangering different aspects of male reproductive health, such as a reduction in sperm concentration, motility, viability and normal morphology; acrosome damage; and DNA fragmentation. Nonetheless, few data are available on these four major high‐exposure occupational groups [9]. Therefore, more research about PAH‐induced reproductive effects in the workers for plastic production, EAF steel production and waste incinerators, as well as aluminium smelters, is necessary to identify exposure–response relations and test biomarkers of impaired spermatogenesis in high‐risk groups of men [3].

Heavy metal and PAH exposure during employment decreases semen quality by producing ROS. The byproducts of cellular and physiological metabolism are called reactive oxygen species (ROS). This aerobic metabolism occurs via phosphorylation in the mitochondria. ROS is a transitional phase for testicular gene regulation. The development of mature sperm cells, capacitation, the acrosome response, and the fusion of sperm with the egg all depend on regular standard amounts of ROS [10]. Human telomere length is shortened as a result of exposure to hazardous metals, which reduces their ability for conception [11]. The present study is among the few of its kind to combine mechanistic oxidative stress, functional sperm parameters violations in motility, morphology alterations, acrosome abnormalities and sensitive genotoxicity assays into such an integrated analysis.

In the present cross‐sectional study, semen quality, oxidative stress and sperm DNA integrity were examined among four occupational categories characterised by different levels of exposure to PAH: workers in a plastic factory; workers in the steel industry electric arc furnace, workers on the waste incineration line and aluminium producers/smelters. This study aims to comprehensively examine the impact of occupational PAH exposure on male reproductive health in four separate industrial groups with increasing burden of PAH measurement through dysfunction in sperm.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Sigma‐Aldrich (Merck, India) supplied all of the study's supplies, including chemicals like sulphuric acid (H2SO4), methanol, ferric chloride (FeCl3), potassium ferritinate, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), sodium chloride (NaCl), ascorbic acid, potassium dihydrogen phosphate, ethanol, sodium bicarbonate, Folin‐Cyclocalcium reagent, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ninhydrin and 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), and nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT).

Ethical Approval

The current student were approved by the appropriate institutional ethics committee of Sri Narayani Hospital and Research Centre for collecting human semen samples. Written informed consent was given by patients to take part in this clinical study. The sample donor's name, address, and background are all noted and kept confidential.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The adult male workers were aged 20–50 years and had worked at least for 1 year in plastic manufacturing, steel production (electric arc furnace units), waste incineration plants or aluminium smelting facilities and also the individuals who reached the WHO (semen parameter) normal range were chosen as the control group, while the remaining individuals were categorised as infertile. If selected, volunteers had to be free from known reproductive problems and willing to contribute semen, blood, and exposure history data. For men, samples were provided after having abstained from ejaculation for 2 to 7 days. Subjects were not included in the study if they had chronic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disease, renal function impairment and cardiovascular disease; or with hormonal therapy, chemotherapy or radiotherapy history at present; also, those who took medicine which could affect reproductive hormones or spermatogenesis. Workers who had a history of cryptorchidism, varicocele operation, testicular trauma, urogenital infection or sexually transmitted disease were also excluded. Furthermore, subjects who had been recently exposed to pesticides, radiation or other occupational hazards, rather than PAHs, were excluded, as well as smokers of more than 10 cigarettes/day and alcohol users with a heavy chronic consumption in order to minimise important confounding factors for oxidative stress and semen quality. The final analysis retained only the subjects matching all inclusion criteria and contributing with the entire set of measurements.

Study Design and Study Population

This cross‐sectional analytical investigation was carried out among male workers from 4 PAH‐polluted industrial settings, such as plants producing plastic, steel with the electric arc furnace technology, waste incineration and aluminium production. 64 workers between the ages of 20 and 50 years were enroled by the workplace for voluntary recruitment. All subjects were at the same time mandatory to have worked for ≥ 1 year. Subgroup comparison was performed with normozoospermic men (n = 17) classified according to WHO criteria 2021 semen reference standards. Patients were asked to collect the sample for 3 days after abstinence to the collecting area by manual pressing into a large sterile wide container. The samples were allowed to melt at 37° C for 20–30 min upon collection. General aspects of microscopic examination were followed according to WHO (2021) laboratory manual protocol [12]. Cells and motility and morphology is counted using a makler counting chamber. Spermatozoa were examined in five different readings after a coverslip had been applied onto 10 µl of the semen on a microscope slide.

NBT Analysis for ROS Assessment

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels should be examined, according to Ranganathan et al. (2024). After suspending 20 µl of sperm in 1 mL of 0.2% Nitroblue Tetrazolium (NBT) solution, the mixture was incubated for 15 min at 20°C. Following incubation, centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 3 min was followed by the addition of 1 mL of 1X PBS. The pellets (200 µl) were collected, washed twice, and then spread out on a sterile glass slide to air dry. After rinsing the slide for 1 min with 100% methanol & double‐distilled water, it was examined under a microscope at 40× magnification. The maximum cell count is 100, and the ROS values were given as percentages (ROS %) [13].

Morphology Analysis

Sperm viability was evaluated using the eosin‐nigrosin staining technique. A drop of sperm (1 × 106 sperm/ml) was mixed with a dye solution made of 10% blue nigrosin stain and 5% red eosin. After spreading the mixture out on a slide, it was set to air dry at ambient temperature. A bright‐field optical microscope with a 100x magnification was used to examine the slides through oil immersion [14].

Hypo Osmotic Swelling Test

Impact ROS mostly through lipid peroxidation in order to assess the functional integrity of the sperm cell membrane. The Hypo Osmotic Swelling test reagent is made by adding 1.351 g of fructose and 0.735 g of sodium citrate dehydrate to 100 mL of distilled water. 0.1 mL of semen and 1 mL of HOS reagent were combined, and the mixture was then incubated for 30 min at 37°C. A 40x magnification microscope should be used to view the drop of semen sample after it has been placed on a glass slide and covered with a coverslip. 55% of the expanding tail indicates a fertile sample [15].

Sperm Chromatin Condensation Analysis

The samples were taken from the Narayani Hospital and Research Centre following the semen analysis. After the seminal plasma was separated, fresh semen samples were centrifuged twice at 2000 rpm for 10 min using a 1x phosphate salt solution. After centrifugation, semen pellets were spread out on sanitised slides. After air drying, 3% glutaraldehyde was used to fix the slides. After that, the samples were submerged in a solution of 4% glacial acetic acid and 5% aniline blue. After staining, the slides were made and examined at 1000x magnification using a light microscope (Olympus CX‐31). For analysis, a minimum of 200 sperm were counted from each slide [16].

Lipid Peroxidation Assay

Lipid peroxidation in seminal plasma and sperm cells (10 × 106/0.2 mL) was determined by the reaction of thio barbituric acid (TBA) with MDA. TBARS reagent is 0.67 g of TBA dissolved in 100 mL distilled water. The solution with 0.5 G NaOH and make it acid, after the manner of (g), With 100 mL glacial acetic acid. Mix well, store the reagent in an amber bottle at 4 °C prior to use. Dilute a sample of semen with the same volume as distilled water. One mL of the resulting TBARS reagent is added and the mixture is vigorously mixed before being placed in a boiling water bath for 15–20 min. After the MDA‐ TBA adduct is formed, centrifuge supernatant at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The measurements are determined at 532 nm with a UV‐Visible Spectrophotometer. The colour intensity of malondialdehyde (MDA) was determined at 535 nm and expressed as μM MDA with tetra‐ethoxypropane as a standard [17].

Measurement of Enzyme Activity

After adding 100 µl of pyrogallol and 200 µl of the semen sample, the mixture was established as different. The absorbance at 450 nm was immediately measured for 5 min. Marklund & Marklund's methodology was followed when conducting the SOD test. Aebi's standard techniques were used to conduct the catalase test. The solution was well mixed with 50 µl of seminal plasma. By monitoring the decrease in absorption at 240 nm every 50 s for 5 min, the apparent breakdown of H2O2 was observed [18, 19].

Acrosome Integrity

Giemsa staining was used to assess spermatozoa's acrosomal integrity. Semen was dripped over a glass object to carry out the staining procedure. A warming plate was used to prepare the smear and heat it to 37°Celsius. After fixing the mixture with methanol for 10 min, it was washed under running water. It was stained for 3 h by submerging it in a staining jar filled with a mixture of three millilitres of 100% Giemsa, two millilitres of PBS, and thirty‐five millilitres of distilled water. After rinsing under running water, it was dried once more. A light microscope with a 400x magnification was used to study 100 cells [20].

Metal Concentration Assessment in Seminal Plasma By AAS

Samples of semen were centrifuged at 4°C for 10 min at 800 rpm. The supernatant was moved to the Eppendrorf tube to totally eliminate debris. Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS) was then used to estimate the concentrations of Pb, Cd, Cr, Ni, As, Hg, AI, Cu, and Zn. The standard curve was plotted using Hi‐Media. The content of Pb, Cd, Cr, Ni, As, Hg, AI, Cu, and Zn was assessed after the test solutions were diluted, if required, using Millipore distilled water. Graph Pad Prism version 8.0 was used for all graphs and statistical analysis [21].

Sperm DNA Fragmentation Analysis

Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

Slides were gently submerged in cold 2.5 M NaCl lysing buffer containing 100 mM N2‐EDTA, 10 mM Tris (pH 10), 1.0% Triton X‐100, & 1% lauryl sarcosine after the gels had solidified and the coverslips had been carefully removed. The slides were then incubated for an hour at 4 C. After that, the slides were placed in a second 2.5 M NaCl buffer to be treated with 5 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 0.5% lauryl sarcosine, & 10 g/mL RNase A. They were then incubated at 37 C for 4 h. After that, the slides were incubated for 15 h at 37 C with 1 mg/mL proteinase K in 2.5 M NaCl & 5 mM Tris (pH 10). Further incubating for 1 h at the ambient temperature in a 1.0% Beta‐mercaptoethanol solution with 2.5 M NaCl was employed to remove additional proteins from the DNA. After that, slides were evenly arranged in a horizontal electrophoresis tank and given time to settle in electrophoresis buffer 45 mL of 10 N NaOH and 30 mL of N2‐EDTA, pH 10, were brought to 1.5 L with twice‐distilled water for 20 min to facilitate unravelling of DNA. For 15 min, electrophoresis was carried out. After neutralising the slides in 0.4 M Tris (pH 7.4) at 20 V, an average of 10 min until cells were analysed [22].

Network Pharmacology and Protein–Protein Interaction Network Analysis

Possible target genes of PAHs were mined from databases (Gene Cards, and PubChem), and genes associated with male infertility were gained by data mining databases (Gene Cards and DisGeNET). Comparing possible target genes of PAHs between male infertility associated genes through Venny 2.1 online tool and chose common target genes. In addition, common target genes were imported into STRING database (https://string-db.org) to construct PPI network, and defined the species as Homo sapiens and score confidence as > 0.90. The resulting network were illustrated by Cytoscape (version 3.10.2), and hub genes were detected by degree centrality using the plugin of CytoHubba [23].

Gene Ontology KEGG Pathway Mapping Analysis

GO (Biological Process [BP], Cellular Component [CC], and Molecular Function [MF]) enrichment analysis, in addition to KEGG pathway enrichment for each module, was conducted using the DAVID (version 6.8) and ShinyGO databases. We considered pathways and GO terms with p < 0.05 significantly enriched. The enrichment results were visualised through the Bioinformatics Online Analysis Platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn). KEGG Mapper was performed to show the distribution of hub genes in significantly enriched KEGG pathways. HIF‐1 signalling pathway, TNF signalling pathway, IL‐17 signalling pathway and chemical carcinogenesis–receptor activation were powerful pathways that associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, endocrine regulation and reproductive dysfunction [24].

Comet Assay of Sperm Fragmentation

Agarose solutions containing normal as well as low gel points were prepared at 0.5% and 0.7%, respectively, for slide production. The tubes were heated for one to 2 min after the agarose powder and DPBS were mixed together. To keep both agarose combinations liquid until they were used, they were kept at 37 C. Slides were prepared by first putting a thin layer of the 0.5% normal gel point agarose on appropriately frosted slides to insure adhesion of following layers. A 50‐L drop of the agarose was spread throughout the entire length of the slides via a coverslip by wiping off the excess, along with the slides were permitted to air dry. 5 µL of the cleaned sperm (approximately 106–107 sperm/mL) in DPBS/PVA was carefully mixed with 95 µL of 0.7% low gel point agarose for the second agarose layer. 75 µL of this sperm with agarose mixture was transferred onto a 24 × 50 mm glass and the first layer of agarose on each slide. A coverslip was placed over each sperm‐agarose drop to facilitate spreading. The agarose has been allowed to gel at 4°C for 10–15 min. A third layer of 75 µL of 0.7% low gel point agarose was pipetted onto the slides after the coverslips were carefully removed. Coverslips were placed as before, and the gel was allowed to solidify for ten to 15 min at 4°C [18, 25].

Fragmentation Analysis Using Flow Cytometry

Sperm DNA fragmentation was quantified with the acridine orange (AO) test using a flow cytometer Beckman‐Coulter CytoFLEX. The sperm was washed by PBS twice after the semen pellet was centrifuged at 800 r/min 10 min, and resuspended in PBS to a concentration of 1 × 106 sperm/mL. Partial DNA denaturation was induced by the addition of 200 µL of 0.1 M HCl for 30 s at room temperature. The pellet was resuspended in 100 µL PBS, and permeabilisation was induced by adding 100 µL of 0.1% Triton ×100 in 0.1% sodium citrate and incubation on ice for 2 min. The cells were collected by centrifugation and were washed twice with 1% PBS to remove the permeabilisation buffer. The pellet was resuspended in 50 µl AO solution and incubated at 37°C for 15 min in the dark with gentle vortexing every 10 min for a total of 1 h. Stained cells were washed twice in PBS/1%BSA before analysing by flow cytometry, suspended in PBS/0.1% BSA (200 ul) prior to acquiring data. One drop of AO was added to 45 samples before being analysed by FCM. The green one's indicative of integrity native DNA (AO) binding monitored under 530 ± 30 nm band‐pass filter and red ones, indicative of the single‐strand DNA and fragmented DNA [26].

Halo Sperm Technique Analysis (SCD)

The Halo‐sperm kit (Halotech DNA) was used for the Halosperm test, which is based on the SCD technique for assessing sperm DNA fragmentation, in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. In short, melted agarose was combined with a diluted sample of semen and placed on a slide. The sample was submerged in HCl solution for 7 min after the coverslip was removed. The sample was submerged in lysing solution for 25 min prior to being cleaned with water and ethanol (70%, 90%, and 100%) [27], Bright‐field microscopy with a ×40 objective was used to view the halos of scattered DNA loops.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and data were expressed as mean ± SD. All the data were interpreted using Graph Pad Prism 8.0.2. One‐way ANOVA, followed by Turkey's Post‐hoc test for multiple comparisons, was used to determine to observe the groups' statistically significant (p < 0.05) differences and the correlation coefficient were calculated to determine the differences.

Result

The study was carried out total (n = 81) male workers with different professions and degree of exposure to PAHs. The age distribution showed that the numbers of 30–35, 20–30 years and > 40 years of workers were relatively small, indicating fairly young or middle‐aged staff. The subjects whose mean BMI was within upper limits of normal or overweight, only a significant group belonged to weight categories between 20.5 and 25.0 kg/m2 and 25.1–30.0 kg/m2, respectively. Lifestyle factors: Over half of the workers were never smokers, with a significant minority reporting occasional cigarette smoking and alcohol drinking. Educational levels were split in the cohort; nearly half of the participants did not have a matriculation certificate or only one; also, 43% and 42% had a level degree education (Table 1). The patients had at least one child, indicating some previous normal reproductive capacity. Together, these findings offer the sample of working men with diverse sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics that were considered in subsequent semen quality and oxidative stress analyses.

Semen Parameter Analysis

The semen quality of the four occupational groups was significantly different with respect to all major parameters. Normozoospermic controls had the highest semen quality parameters: average sperm concentration of 68.4 ± 12.7 million/mL, total motility of 58.2 ± 8.9%, progressive motility of 46.7 ± 7.5%, and normal morphology of 12.4 ± 2.1%. Sperm function was significantly diminished in all exposure groups compared to controls. The greatest decrease was pointed out in the EAF steel plant workers, who had mean sperm concentration 41.3 ± 10.5 million/mL, total motility 39.2 ± 7.1%, progressive motility with real progression velocity > 0 μ/s a replaced by 28.6 ± 5.4, and normal morphology 7.9 ± 8.0%. The workers employed in the plastic industry had a moderate decrease (52.6 ± 11.4 million/mL; progressive motility 34.1 ± 6.2%) and so did municipal's incinerators‐manufacturer's workers (45.8 ± 10.9 million/mL, progressive motility 29.4 ± 6.1%). Other Aluminium smelter workers had lower concentration (48.3 ± 9.6 million/mL), and progressive motility (31.7 ± 5.9%) concentration than controls, also showed lower concentrations of sperm cells (Table 2). Between groups, both motility and morphology were significantly reduced (p < 0.05), suggesting an overall semen impairment in correlation with occupational PAH exposure.

Comparison of Enzyme Activity and Oxidative Stress Across Occupational Groups

The evaluation of the oxidative stress biomarkers showed significant changes in all the occupational groups with respect to controls. MDA concentration an index of lipid peroxidation were the least in control group (2.31 ± 0.42 nmol/mL) and gradually increased with the exposure groups. The EAF steel workers (5.81 ± 0.67 nmol/mL) were the group with the highest levels of TP, followed by aluminium smelter workers (5.36 ± 0.54 nmol/mL), municipality incineration workers (4.73 ± 0.49 nmol/mL), and plastic industry workers (4.12 ± 0.46) effects in p < 0.001. On the other hand, activities of antioxidant enzymes decreased as the exposed group increased. The SOD was the highest in control subjects (6.72 ± 0.51 U/mL) and significantly reduced in aluminium (3.54 ± 0.39) and EAF workers (3.81 ± 0.42 U/mL). Likewise, CAT activity significantly reduced in EAF (2.53 ± 0.31 U/mL) and aluminium workers (2.74 ± 0.28 U/mL) as compared with the control value (4.86 ± 0.48 U/mL), (p < 0.001). Collectively, these results suggest a severe oxidative unbalance in PAH‐exposed workers (Table 3).

Correlation of Biomarkers Vs Quality Parameters

Correlation between oxidative stress and semen quality were positive and strongly significant in all other occupational categories. Otherwise, The ROS levels were negatively correlated with sperm concentration (r = −0.68; p = 0.001), indicating that increased ROS generation was directly proportional to the decline in functional integrity of the spermatozoa. The concentration of MDA had a strong negative correlation with the progressive motility (r = −0.67, p = 0.031) as well, implying that increased lipid peroxidation may damage the structure of the membrane required for sperm motility. However, some antioxidant biomarkers had a positive skewing relationship with semen performance. The significantly positive relationship of SOD activity with sperm concentration (r = +0.54, p = 0.045) and progressive motility (r = +0.59, p = 0.008), and CAT activity with sperm concentration (p = +2.1, p = 0.014) confirms their protective action from oxidative attack. These results (Figure 1) prove that an imbalance of oxidative stress at least partly determines the quality of semen.

ROS Levels and Acrosome Integrity Across Occupational Groups

A significant variation in the level of ROS and acrosomal integrity between occupational groups was observed (p < 0.001). ROS was detected in descending order from the control group (2.12 ± 0.38 RLU/sec/106 sperm) to plastic workers (11.6 ± 2.7), steel EAF workers (15.9 ± 3.3), municipal incineration workers (21.7 ± 4.2), and to aluminium smelting workers, in which the highest activity was recorded with an exposure level of 26.4 ± 4.8 RLU. In contrast, both acrosome integrity and acrosome reacted were decreasing in controls (84.1 ± 6.2%), plastic (70.4 ± 7.1%) mice that fell progressively down to steel (63.8 ± 6.5%), incineration (57.2 ± 7.0%) and aluminium workers respectively with these values being lowest in the last category of studied population; as mentioned, some meaningful differences occurred between them (Table 4) (Figure 2). This suggests that sperm functional quality decreases gradually with increasing levels of occupational exposure intensity.

Discussion

The present study illustrates in a comprehensive manner that occupation‐exposure to PAHs, industrial metals, and combustion‐related emissions exerts far‐reaching and multi‐level effects on the male reproductive health systems interfering with process from early sperm physiology to complex chromatin structure/chromosomal integrity. For all the investigated sperm parameters, workers from plastic manufacturing, steel electric arc furnace processing, municipal waste incineration and aluminium smelting showed a marked worsening in sperm function compared with controls, which indicated a strong exposure–effect correlation that resulted from an accumulated toxic load. Concomitant with the deterioration of traditional semen parameters, i.e., concentration, motility and morphology, appear basic dysfunctions in spermatogenesis and post‐testicular maturation; both these processes are likely to be extremely susceptible to environmental attack as they interact with the reproductive axis [28]. According to Peña‐García et al. (2024) there was no detrimental effect on the workers' sperm cellularity after a year of exposure to PAHs in a solar thermal plant, either quantitatively (sperm count) or qualitatively contain motility, cellular DNA fragmentation [6]. On the other hand, seminal plasma leucocyte infiltration and reduced antioxidant ability are linked to PAH exposure. Madeen and Williams (2017) claim that the observed changes are equivalent to a year of occupational exposure [29]. The observed deterioration in semen quality across exposed groups is consistent with earlier reports linking occupational PAH exposure to oxidative stress–mediated reproductive toxicity. Our study confirms that long‐term exposure to PAHs has negative effects on male fertility [29].

Notably, the severe loss of motility among the exposed group is evident due to the fact that it was found to be one of the first sperm functions to deteriorate when sperm are subjected to oxidative and chemical stress; a trend consistent with reported oxidative stress markers taken at the current investigation [30]. Increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), as evidenced through NBT staining and associated biochemical estimations, indicate prolonged oxidative stress experienced by industrial workers due to chronic exposure of PAH‐containing fumes along with airborne metal particulates, inducing formation of various free radicals and in turn aggravating redox equilibrium [3]. According to Abilash et al. (2025), these biochemistry changes showed similar trend to the NBT test with large formazan accumulation on sterile sample, indicating potential significant intracellular ROS explosion inducing the mitochondrial failure. Nuclear DNA integrity, flagellar activity and plasma membrane fluidity are some of the key structural components of sperm, which is also influenced by oxidative stress [15]. Spermatozoa are particularly sensitive to lipid peroxidation because of their relatively high complement of polyunsaturated fatty acids peroxidizable, low cellular oxygen radical scavenging potential and deficit in cytoplasmic antioxidants focusing on the common functional defects as Acrosome reaction, membrane integrity (HOST) and mitocondrial‐dependent movability [31].

The chronic oxidative stress also impaired the antioxidant enzymatic defences, as shown by low activities of SOD, CAT; thus, suggesting that the endogenous antioxidants were overwhelmed and not capable to scavenge such a massive amount of ROS generated in these industrial workplaces. Morphological studies indicated more frequent abnormalities in the cadmium‐exposed groups and greater deviation from normal morphology in the aluminium workers. These morphological abnormalities not only lower fertilising potential but reflect deeper changes in morphology that impact upon overall sperm viability [32].

According to Jeng et al. (2018), there was no correlation between the percentages of normal morphology and the quantities of ROS and MDA. The oxidation‐induced membrane degradation seen in this study could not be severe enough to have a major impact on sperm morphological traits [4]. The observation that MDA content was associated with sperm DNA decondensation as opposed to DNA fragmentation and that MDA correlated with a number of peroxidation products, such as lipid hydroperoxides, glutathione, along with protein hydrogen peroxide, yet did not correlate with DNA fragmentation outcomes along with antioxidant concentration, confirmed the findings of Montano et al. (2025) [3],

The low‐ hypo osmotic swelling test (HOST) also demonstrated that damaged membrane integrity was accompanied at least by the industrial exposure group, suggesting the participation of industrial toxicants in modulating permeability and composition of membrane through ion channel function‐permeability or peroxidation process [17]. According to Simon et al. (2018), standard clinical fertility evaluations should incorporate DNA fragmentation analysis. A recent study assessed the link between HOST shape and DNA fragmentation using the Halosperm assay [33].

The molecular mechanisms underlying occupationally acquired PAH induced reproductive toxicity, we performed network pharmacology along with enrichment analyses. In other words, because we identified 67 shared targets between PAH exposure and male infertility, there seems to be an intimate molecular relationship between exposure to environmental toxicants and impairment of male reproductive function, although the link itself is somewhat unclear. Hub gene analysis, RELA, PTGS2, EGFR, ESR1, ESR2, AR, MMP2, MMP9, PPARA and EP300 are identified to be central regulatory nodes within the interaction network [23]. Typically, activation of NF‐B signalling pathways may induce production of excessive amounts of ROS, which subsequently leads to the development of mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation and induction of apoptotic signalling cascades; these cascade events would feed into impairments in sperm function. Such hypotheses appear to be supported by observed high levels of ROS and MDA and decreased antioxidant enzyme activities in occupationally exposed subjects [24].

The chemical carcinogenesis–receptor activation pathway is supportive evidence of the association between sperm DNA damage and PAH exposure. PAHs are metabolised in a process driven by the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, producing reactive intermediates that can give rise to excessive ROS and DNA adducts. Such events may produce chromatin instability, DNA breaks and apoptosis. The activation of the EGFR, RELA and PTGS2 genes in this pathway supports the massive DNA fragmentation measured by SCD assay, flow cytometry, agarose gel electrophoresis and comet analysis [34].

In addition, the enrichment of oestrogen signalling and endocrine‐related pathways associated with both ESR1, ESR2 and AR indicates that PAHs may have an endocrine‐disrupting effect on male reproductive physiology [23]. Changes in steroid hormone signalling may disrupt spermatogenesis, sperm maturation and chromatin packaging, which collectively lead to poor semen quality. The predictions from this study on network pharmacology nearly unanimously find good agreement with several experimental findings of elevated oxidative stress, reduced antioxidant defence, and dysfunctional acrosome integrity/de‐condensation of sperm chromatin, leading to excess sperm DNA fragmentation.

The chromatin decondensation and onset of DNA‐damage response is markedly more pronounced in the aluminium employees suggesting that aluminium represents a highly relevant potency‐reproductive toxicant, creating an increased oxidative burden and instability to the DNA structure. DNA fragmentation is one of the most important clinical parameters in male infertility because fragmented DNA affects embryo development, fertilisation rates and pregnancy results [35]. The concomitant combination of high DNA fragmentation and strong chromatin condensation defect reflects thereby a mechanistic pathway by which work‐related toxicants will impair protamation and nucleoprotamines, therefore leading conditions for sperm‐DNAs to be damaged by oxidation into free‐damaged site nicks and strand breaks [5]. According to Gualtieri et al. (2021), ROS can rupture the sperm DNA chain via directly oxidising sperm DNA bases or by covalently binding lipid peroxidation products along with DNA. This may result in damage to the cellular structure of sperm or DNA damage, along with aberrant sperm chromatin assembly can cause sperm DNA double‐strand break. Sperm chromatin assembly abnormalities, improper sperm cell death, and excessive oxidative stress are the primary causes of sperm damage [36].

Additionally, some research has shown connections between DNA fragmentation, phosphatidylserine externalisation, and caspase activation. The quantity of halos created when nuclear proteins are eliminated is measured indirectly using the Halosperm test, which has a direct correlation to single‐stranded DNA damage [27]. Additionally, the test‐analysed sperm cannot be used, particularly for intracytoplasmic sperm injection. Comets indicate a clear exposure–response relationship. The tail comet length, DNA in the tail and OTM were gradually increased from the EAF, aluminium group according to oxidative genotoxic stress raise. The substantially increased OTM and %DNA‐in‐tail in the incineration‐ and aluminium‐exposed workers indicate high oxidative stress with production of reactive oxygen species in addition to other direct genotoxicants, such as PAHs and co‐emitted metals that attacked protamine‐compacted chromatin, resulting in single‐ and double‐strand breaks [18]. The higher levels of Al, Cu, Pb and Cr in the exposed groups obviously present convincing data to suggest that metal‐induced toxicity could play a role in PAH‐induced oxidative stress. Metals like aluminium induce ROS generation and affect mitochondrial function, leading to oxidative damages; they can also bind to the phosphate group of DNA strands, resulting in an enhancement of genotoxic and oxidative effect [21]. According to Saad et al. (2019), PAHs' interactive metabolites may enter the testicles and epididymis and interact with sperm DNA to create adducts. The substances produced by PAH oxidation have the capacity to enter oxidation cycles, which raise the ROS composition and harm sperm DNA [9].

The combined oxidative, structural, biochemical and genomic data demonstrate the soundness of our findings and highlight a systemic basis for occupational reproductive toxicity. Furthermore, that oxidative stress markers are significantly associated with reproductive outcomes indicates that anti‐oxidant treatment may have a protective effect, which should be assessed in more detail to identify the best approach [4, 5]. On the whole, the current investigation not only adds new information to occupational toxicology but also clearly indicates important paradigms responsible for industrial exposures‐induced damage of male reproductive function, highlighting the need for measures preventing and regulating against negative influences on the reproductive health of industrial workers.

Study Limitation

There are some limitations in our study. First, the cross‐sectional design of our study prevented us from drawing causal conclusions about the relation between occupational PAH exposure and sperm functional impairment. Longitudinal studies were required to further confirm this temporal association. Second, workforces were well characterised in occupational groups, but neither personal air monitoring nor biological monitoring for PM (with biomarker‐based PAH metabolites) was conducted and exposure misclassification may have occurred. Third, other confounders (dietary habits) and lifestyle variation may not be fully controlled; no adjustment was made for concomitant exposure to additional environmental pollutants either. The sample is large but may be limited for subgroup stratification analyses. Finally, there was no read out of the functional fertility end‐points such as fertilisation or pregnancy rates. However, the consistency of effects across end points (semen quality, oxidative stress and DNA damage) in separate datasets tends to indicate the biological relevance of our results.

Conclusion

The current study clearly demonstrated that chronic occupational exposure to PAHs and industrial toxicants can contribute significantly to the resultant oxidative stress‐mediated sperm dysfunction with concomitant decreased parameters of semen as well as affected acrosomal and plasma membrane integrity, coupled together with extensive DNA damage in particular among waste incineration and aluminium workers in men's reproductive health is seriously endangered by pollution from industries. This study highlights the impact of ROS concentrations on oxidative DNA damage & sperm genomic integrity. Compared with controls, exposed workers exhibited a marked elevation in seminal ROS levels (2.12 ± 0.38 in controls vs. 21.7 ± 4.2 and 26.4 ± 4.8 in waste incineration and aluminium workers, respectively; p < 0.001), accompanied by a significant decline in acrosome integrity (84.1 ± 6.2% in controls vs. 57.2 ± 7.0% and 48.9 ± 7.6%; p < 0.001). Network pharmacology analysis suggested that HIF‐1‐, TNF‐, and IL‐17‐related pathways, together with hub genes involving EGFR, ESR1, RELA, PTGS2, MMP2 and MMP9 may connect PAH exposure to oxidative stress, inflammation, endocrine disruption, sperm genomic damage. The biological mechanism that most likely contributed to the association between exposure to PAHs and reduced sperm quality is oxidative stress. The findings of this study improve our understanding of how exposure to PAHs affects male reproductive health.
