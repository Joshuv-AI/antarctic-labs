EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 This report provides an overview of the engineering and experimental efforts to measure
the effects of water on hydrogen uptake in nickel plated Zr-4 alloy getters (NPG) used in tritium
producing absorber rods (TPBAR)s. The system developed here allows for the introduction of
hydrogen gas, acting as a surrogate for tritium, blended with contaminant gases (CO2, CO, CH4)
in dry and humid environments at relevant conditions for their use in pressurized water reactors
(PWR); approximately 350 °C and 20 torr partial pressure of hydrogen.

 We established a combined magnetic suspension balance with precision gas flow controls
enabling continuous flow of gas into a sample chamber while measuring hydrogen uptake over
multiple days, with the added capability of directly introducing water vapor into the chamber.
This enables a direct measurement of the effects of water on hydrogen uptake when combined
with potential contaminant gas mixtures.

 The major findings of this work are that hydrogen uptake is significantly reduced by the
introduction of CO, CO2 and methane, even while maintaining the same partial pressure of
hydrogen. In pure hydrogen atmospheres, hydrogen uptake is rapid 7 mg / day, however this rate
collapses to be much slower 0.68 mg/day representing a decrease in observed H2 uptake rate with
the introduction of water vapor representing a 91 % decrease in H2 uptake rate. When small
amounts of carbon monoxide or carbon dioxide are added the rate also shows a significant
decrease in H2 uptake rates. When these contaminant gases are combined with water, a reversible
sorption of water onto the surface of the NPG is measured, but hydrogen uptake becomes
negligible (less than 200 ug/day). The combination of water vapor with a contaminant gas, either
CO2, CO, or a mixture of CH4, CO and CO2 were observed to consistently eliminate the ability
of the NPG to uptake hydrogen at appreciable scales.

 Abstract
 A Tritium Producing Burnable Absorber Rod (TPBAR) is a specialized component used
in commercial nuclear reactors to produce tritium (3H ). TPBARs include a zirconium alloy
(Zircaloy-4, Zr-4) getter tube to bind tritium as a metal hydride with a nickel (Ni) barrier coating
to protect the Zr-4 getter from oxidation. Since zircaloy is susceptible to surface reactions with
contaminant gases and surface impurities (e.g. CO, CO2, CH4 and H2O) that can form unwanted
surface oxide and carbon (coke), the Ni coating acts as a protective barrier to prevent getter
poisoning effects. Herein, we used a custom high resolution gravimetric sorption (HRGS) system
developed at Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) to benchmark getter rate changes
resulting from contaminant species (e.g. CO, CO2, CH4) and water vapor, exposure at 20 torr
total pressure using hydrogen (H2) as a tritium surrogate at 350 ⸰C. The HRGS system can
operate under high pressure, vacuum, and corrosive gas conditions within a wide temperature
range (-196 ⸰C to 400 ⸰C). Using an in-situ exposure approach we tracked changes in hydrogen
uptake performance in real-time at 20 and 70 torr partial pressure of hydrogen and 350 ⸰C;
relevant conditions for the gettering of hydrogen pressurized water reactors. Scanning electron
microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and BET surface area and pore size analysis were
used for post-exposure sample investigations.

 Hydrogen uptake measurements reveal that the custom HRGS system is good for NPG
getter performance evaluation. Herein we demonstrate sample reproducibility and a detailed
workflow for co-exposure of hydrogen gas blended with contaminant gases and water vapor.
This enables direct measurement of the compounding effects of water vapor and contaminant
gases (CO, CO2, CH4) on hydrogen uptake. Hydrogen mixed with CO, CO2 and a combination of
CH4, CO and CO2 in dry conditions were all observed to greatly reduce the hydrogen uptake
compared to pure hydrogen despite equivalent partial pressures of H2 in each system. This
suggests competitive sorption on the Ni surface potentially reduces diffusion of hydrogen into
the Zr-4 layer. Adding water vapor into the system further reduces hydrogen uptake in high dew
point environments. The water suppression effect was overcome by increasing the partial
pressure of hydrogen in each experiment from 20 torr to 70 torr.

 Introduction
 A Tritium Producing Burnable Absorber Rod (TPBAR) is a specialized component used
in commercial nuclear reactors to produce tritium (3H). TPBARs include a zirconium alloy
(Zircaloy-4) getter tube to bind tritium as a metal hydride with a nickel (Ni) protective barrier
coating. Since Zircaloy is susceptible to surface reactions with contaminant gases and surface
impurities (e.g. CO, CO2, CH4 and H2O) that form unwanted surface oxide and carbon (Coke),
the Ni coating acts as a protective barrier to prevent getter poisoning effects.1

 From previous post-irradiation examination (PIE) campaigns, surface carbon deposits
have been observed on multiple TPBAR components with unknown effects on performance.2, 3
Thus, fundamental studies to understand carbon transport and deposition mechanisms are
needed. While the carbon source and transfer mechanism during TPBAR operation are under
investigation, gaseous sources such as CO2, CO, CH4 have been previously observed to react at
metallic surfaces under relevant temperature conditions 350 ⸰C.4-6 Thus, gaseous and surface
contaminant species are likely a contributing source to the observed carbon deposits that must be
better understood to accurately predict TPBAR performance and reliability.

 For Nickel-plated getters (NPG), the role of Ni is of particular interest due to known
competitive catalysis mechanisms at Ni surfaces. Accessible catalytic reactions for Ni at
relatively low temperature (350 ⸰C) include reverse water-gas shift or water-gas shift, CO2 or CO
methanation, and methane reforming reactions which can result in surface C(coke) deposition
under high carbon, hydrogen lean, and dry conditions.7-9 Surface C or coke formation occurs
when carbon surface species (CO2, CO, CH4) cannot be removed by hydrogenation or
gasification reactions on a metal surface. Instead surface bound carbon species can follow
disproportionation pathways that result in C(coke) deposition.10 Surface absorbed species can
also lead to changes in Ni surface oxidation state and surface morphology resulting in defects
that can reduce the fidelity of the Ni-barrier protection of the zircalloy getter.

 In our previous FY25 study, we built a custom high-resolution-gravimetric sorption
(HRGS) system with in-line mass spectrometry (MS).11 We used this system to show that surface
Ni chemistry is playing a role in slowing getter uptake rates under CO and methane
environments. While the getter sample capacity was unaffected, reduction in getter H2 uptake
rate kinetics were observed and accompanied by Ni-surface defects after CO and methane
exposure via SEM imaging.

 Herein, we describe co-exposure studies for gas mixtures of hydrogen, CO2, CO, CH4 in
dry and humid environments. Where mixed gases are introduced together to further understand

 Methods & Experimental Approach
 In this section, we describe the components of LANL developed high resolution
gravimetric sorption (HRGS) capability that uses an in-line universal gas analyzer (UGS) mass
spectrometer to benchmark getter rate and uptake performance.12 Contaminant exposure was
introduced by charging the HRGS cell with 20 torr of contaminant gas (CO2, CO, CH4) mixtures
in dry and humid environments. SEM-EDX was used to characterize getters after exposure
studies.

 Custom High Resolution Gravimetric Sorption Capability

 The custom HRGS system was designed to evaluate getter performance under isothermal
and isobaric conditions by modifying a commercial IsoSORP Rubotherm (1 µg or 10 µg
resolution). The commercial system uses a unique magnetic suspension balance system that
magnetically suspends the sample holder assembly and measures the mass with an external
electromagnet attached to a microbalance. This design allows hermetic separation of the balance
from the reaction chamber enabling gravimetric sorption experiments over a wide range of
pressures and temperatures and enabling the use of toxic or corrosive reaction gases such as H2
and CO. LANL-engineering upgrades incorporated a flow-through gas feed system to allow for
constant flow measurements for both dry and humid environments that can interface with a
variety of in-line gas analysis tools and spectroscopy, the integrated system is shown in Figure
2.

 Custom Rubotherm Configuration for Dry and Humid Gas Exposure Experiments

 The custom LANL HRGS was designed to facilitate isobaric pure- and mixed-gas
experiments over pressures ranging from 1 mTorr to 1000 Torr, see system configuration in
Figure 2. LANL set up two similar systems, with either 1 µg or 10 µg resolution, inside of air-
free gloveboxes for parallel operation in a controlled environment. These systems rely on a
unique magnetic suspension balance feature to allow for periodic zero-point (ZP) weight
correction to compensate for electronic drift over the course of an experiment. This eliminates
background fluctuations resulting from changes in ambient conditions including temperature,
pressure, and humidity. In addition, the automatic sample decoupling (ASD) feature tares the
balance during experiments to provide long-term baseline stability to achieve µg-level
resolution.

 For the analysis conditions used in this report, high purity gas (99.999%) is supplied
through a 500-sccm upstream mass flow controller (MFC) (GM50A013502, MKS Instruments)
at a set flow rate (10 sccm), and the set pressure (20 Torr) is maintained using a downstream
dual-diaphragm pump (MV 2NT, vacuubrand) and a 500-sccm pressure control valve (0248A-
00500SV, MKS Instruments) controlled using a vacuum system controller (946, MKS
Instruments). Temperature control of the magnetic assembly is supplied using a recirculating

chiller (F25, Julabo). Sample chamber temperature conditions are maintained using a flexible
resistive heater and Barnett Co. temperature controller. Both HRGS are housed in gloveboxes
(PureLab HE 2GB, Inert Technology) The HRGS can utilize either dry gas streams or
humidified gas streams. An inline gas bubbler thermostatted with a chiller enables controlled

provides a measure of the water vapor concentration. The water is then trapped in a –40 °C

 Gas Physisorption Analysis (BET & pore structure characterization)

 The specific surface area and pore characteristics were determined by N2 adsorption-
desorption isotherms at 77.35 K using an Anton Paar Nova 800 BET surface area analyzer. Prior
to measurement, samples were degassed at 110˚C for ~12 h to remove any adsorbed species. The
BET surface area was calculated from the adsorption data in the relative pressure range of 0.05-
0.30. Pore size distributions and pore volumes were determined using the Density Functional
Theory (DFT) method applied to the full isotherm (P/P0 = 0-1). Total pore volume was estimated
from the amount adsorbed at P/P0 = 0.99.

 Getter Rate Sample Preparation for Rubotherm Sorption

 Getter samples were provided by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL)
(LOT#431147-210802A). The material was prepared, cleaned, and activated by PNNL. LANL
cut as received getter samples into 0.5-inch sample lengths for loading into alumina sample
holder basket. We incorporated a one-hour thermal treatment step in air (400 ⸰C) to remove
additional organic contaminants that may have been introduced during the second cuts made to
the samples.

 Experimental Approach

 Getter Gas Exposure Workflow

 Initial and final mass measurements under He were necessary to verify the
experimentally measured gas uptake through the Δmass observed in He and H2. The buoyancy
factor, determined by the specific gas and sampling pressure, affects the measured sample weight
in the HRGS system and must be accounted for. The use of these distinct measurement
conditions ultimately enables a double check of the final Δmass and, correspondingly, the extent
of uptake observed.

Baseline H2 uptake experiment with no contaminant gas exposure

 Getter samples (0.5 inch in length) were thermally pre-treated under flowing air at 10
sccm and 20 Torr (21% O2 bal N2) at 400°C for 1 h for removal of water and organic
contaminants. Next, the sample chamber was cooled to 350 ⸰C under He for initial mass
sampling and ensure balance stabilization. Subsequently, the system was switched over to H2.
The sample was allowed to react to completion (i.e. no measurable uptake observed over a 24-h
period). After completion, the system was switched back to He to obtain the Δmass measured in
He.

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 3-3

Dry gas exposure and getter performance experiment

 Getter samples (0.5 inch in length) were thermally pre-treated under flowing air at 10
sccm and 20 Torr (21% O2 bal N2) at 400°C for 1 h for removal of water and organic
contaminants. Next, the sample chamber was cooled to 350 ⸰C under He for initial mass
sampling and ensure balance stabilization. Subsequently, the system was switched over to the
target dry gas. The sample was allowed to react for up to 2 days or approximately half H2 wt %
loading. Experimental run times varied due to changes in uptake rates. After completion, the
system was switched back to He to obtain the Δmass measured in He.

Humid gas exposure and getter performance experiment

 Getter samples (0.5 inch in length) were thermally pre-treated under flowing air at 10
sccm and 20 Torr (21% O2 bal N2) at 400°C for 1 h for removal of water and organic
contaminants. Next, the sample chamber was cooled to 350 ⸰C under He for initial mass
sampling and ensure balance stabilization. Separately, the water bubbler was conditioned with
He gas before flowing the humid He over the sample for routine leak and system checks.
Afterward, the system was switched over to the target humid gas. The sample was allowed to
react for up to 2 days or approximately half H2 wt % loading. Experimental run times varied due
to changes in uptake rates. After completion, the system was switched back to He to obtain the
Δmass measured in He.

 For all mixed gas experiments, the partial pressure of H2 was kept constant at 20 torr but

recorded. Future plans include upgrading the water introduction mechanism on the HRGS with a
variable leak valve that can control the flow of humidified gas at controlled leak rates from 1.00
x10-9 torr L/s to max flow rate of 6.00 L/min.

 Summary of different gas exposures

 Since H2 uptake is governed by surface mediated dissociation and transport process at the
Ni-layer the presence of additional gas species (CO, CO2, CH4) may interfere, compete or
change active Ni-layer sites. The influence of mixed carbon containing H2 gas mixtures and that
of water was evaluated by comparing H2 uptake rates under dry and humid CO/H2, CO2/H2O,
and mixed CH4/CO/CO2 environments. These paired experiments were designed to distinguish
the effects of water from inhibitory effects associated with the carbon containing gas mixtures.

 To evaluate whether pressure alters the inhibitory effects of water and the carbon
containing gas mixtures, H2 uptake rates were compared at partial pressures of 20 and 70 torr of
H2 (Figure 10). The increasing partial pressure of H2 may increase the surface sorption
concentration and drive higher gas transfer rates across the Ni-layer mitigating kinetic limitations
associated with water derived surface species or competitive adsorption by carbon containing
gases. This comparison gives insight into whether the observed H2 suppression is sensitive to H2
concentration or remains dominated by persistent surface inhibition.

 For all carbon containing mixed gas conditions evaluated at 70 torr partial pressure
higher H2 uptake rates for both dry and humid environments were observed. For example, multi
component gas mixture CH4/CO/CO2 showed a H2 uptake rate of 962 µg/day and 1233 µg/day
for corresponding humid and dry conditions at 70 torr that dropped to 163 µg/day and 564
µg/day at 20 torr partial pressure of H2. Increasing the pressure from 20 to 70 torr resulted in
higher uptake under humid H2 conditions, but not for dry. This contrasting behavior could be due
to competing uptake and embrittlement effects on the NPG where embrittlement phenomena may
become accelerated at higher pressures resulting in a larger error in the measurement due to
sample loss. Another possibility is the transport resistive influence of water may be sensitive to
changes in pressure, and this pressure sensitivity is removed under dry conditions. Further
pressure relationship testing needs to be investigated to better understand this deviation for dry
H2 environments.

 Getter Surface Changes after Gas Exposure

 SEM & XRD of Ni Phase after Dry vs Humid Gas Exposure

 Scanning electron microscopy is a powerful imaging technique that was used here for
 material characterization of the Ni surface post-gas exposure. The focus of these methods was to
 characterize surface morphology changes and couple these with potential observed differences in
 Ni phases via XRD. The changes in surface morphology can be used to understand surface
 corrosion effects that may result from the surface exposure to different gaseous environments.

Figure 11. SEM images of (a) fresh NPG sample and (b) after air and He thermal treatment steps

 As shown in Figure 11, the staring morphology of the NPG sample shows many surface
 defects including scratches and pinholes. After thermal treatment in air and He, the surface
 defects appear less pronounced in some regions; suggesting that the Ni may undergo a thermal
 annealing step during the activation of the NPG. The XRD peaks from both the as received fresh
 NPG and air-He thermal treated NPG samples are dominated by reflections from crystalline
 metallic Ni, indicating that the Ni coating retains its primary phase after thermal treatment
 (Figure 12). No distinct reflections corresponding to NiO are observed, suggesting that the air-
 He treatment does not produce detectable bulk phase transformation, but does not exclude the
 possibility of very thin layer formation or amorphous surface layer formation below the detection
 limit of XRD.

 SEM imaging revealed no clear or systematic changes in surface morphology following
 subsequent exposures to various dry and humid gas conditions, shown in Figure 13. Across all
 conditions, the sample retained the primary microstructural features observed before exposure
 including pinhole defect and rough scratches. Although minor local variations in surface features
 were present, these changes could not be confidently attributed to specific gas composition
 effects. No obvious evidence of extensive surface degradation, cracking or morphology changes
 were consistent with a specific gas composition. These results suggest that the gas exposures
 used in this study did not produce substantial morphological changes on the micron-scale. These

 NPG Physisorption Measurements and Pore Characteristics

 BET gas sorption and surface analysis were performed on NPG exposed to H2, mixed
gas, and He both with and without water. Key findings show that both gas composition and water
exposure can affect pore characteristics, with water having the dominant effect on surface area
and pore volume reduction. Figure 14a shows pore volumes for dry samples under different gas
conditions including an NPG with no gas exposure. NPG samples exposed to H2 and mixed gas
showed an increase in pore volume when compared with an NPG sample exposed to inert He
with no gas exposure. Figure 14b shows the pore volumes for NPG after mixed gas or H2
exposure, followed by water. There was a significant drop in the pore volume for the mixed gas
exposed sample at the highest dew point tested.
 Overall, NPG samples exposed to water showed more variation in pore volume,
particularly in the 10 - 20 nm range, indicating that water had a measurable effect on pore
volume. Both hydrogenated NPG samples exposed to water and mixed gas/water exposed
samples at a dew point = 1˚C had lower pore width (<25 nm) than all other wet and dry samples
(Figure 14).
 All pore volumes, widths and surface areas for each sample are reported in Table 3.

 Conclusions
 This study set out to establish a new capability for measuring the effect of water on H2
uptake rates for carbon containing gas mixtures on NPG materials at 350 °C and 20 torr
conditions relevant to PWRs. Herein, we successfully established a high-resolution gravimetric
sorption (HRGS) system in a flow through configuration that can measure sample mass changes
with high resolution (1 µg or 10 µg) over long experiment times (days), high temperature, and
varying pressures and in humid environments. The system developed here allows for direct real
time uptake measurements of NPG in mixed gas environments with the capability of introducing
water vapor into the system over several days. The results reported here are preliminary and are
limited by a small sampling size per contaminant species and dry or humid gas exposure.

 The findings of our study suggest that the introduction of water has the most detrimental
effects on getter H2 uptake performance resulting in over 90% decrease in rate compared to ideal
dry H2 environments. Since H2 permeation through the nickel layer is mediated by surface
sorption, dissociation, and transport for storage as a metal hydride in the Zr-4 getter material the
presence of other gases may modify or alter these steps preventing or slowing H2 transfer and
storage. Due to the significant H2 uptake suppression observed, water may inhibit H2 transfer by
competing with H2 for active Ni surface sorption sites or changing the nickel surface chemistry
resulting in significant suppression in H2 uptake rates.

 For all carbon containing gas mixtures, CO/H2, CO2/H2, or multi-component
CH4/CO/CO2, H2 uptake rates decreased by 89 - 93% relative to ideal dry H2 only conditions.
The addition of humidity led to further H2 uptake rate suppression with observed rate decrease
by 69.2%, 79.7% and 71.1% with the addition of water to the respective CO/H2, CO2/H2, and
mixed gas environments. Humid carbon containing mixed gas environment led to the largest

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 5-11

observed rate decrease of 98% relative to ideal dry H2 conditions. For carbon containing gas
mixtures, the water effect on H2 uptake rate suppression could be overcome at higher 70 torr
partial pressures of H2.

 SEM and XRD, were used to understand changes in NPG morphology and phase,
changes after gas exposures under dry and humid conditions. SEM did not show any distinct
systematic changes in surface morphology following gas exposure. While minor local variations
were observed, these changes could not be confidently attributed to a specific gas combination.
Similarly, XRD did not show new Ni phase reflections after exposure, suggesting that these air
treatments do not produce detectable phase transformations but does not exclude their possibility
due to the detection limit of XRD. The only exception is for the double water exposed sample
that underwent humid He before introducing humid CO/H2. Due to the prolonged exposure of
the sample, it is difficult to decouple the gas composition effects with that of water, but due to
the lack of similar new Ni reflections observed for similar gas composition, these new phases are
likely a result of long water exposure.

 From surface sorption studies, BET and pore characteristics for exposed NPG samples
found that both carbon containing gas mixtures and water effect pore characteristics, with water
having the dominate effect on surface area and pore volume reduction. Overall, NPG samples
exposed to water showed more variation in pore volume, particularly in the 10 - 20 nm range,
and surface area indicating that water had a measurable effect on pore volume. Among humid
exposed samples, there was no significant difference in pore volume or surface area between H2
and mixed gas, showing that gas composition does not cause significant surface changes and
water likely plays a more dominate role in the observed surface changes. Dry NPG samples
exposed to H2 and mixed gas showed an increase in surface area compared to dry He and as
received NPG samples.

 Taken together, these results demonstrate that H2 uptake by NPG materials is governed
not only by gas composition but more strongly by the water content of the gas mixture. While
carbon containing gases substantially inhibited uptake relative to H2 alone, the addition of water
imposed further limitations across the gas mixtures examined in this work. Increasing partial
pressure of H2 was shown to be an effective strategy in overcoming the water-induced H2 uptake
suppression for dry or humid carbon containing gas mixtures. Further surface-sensitive
characterization and kinetic studies are needed to identify the role of absorbed species or changes
in nickel surface to understand the effect on H2 transport processes responsible for H2 uptake
suppression.
