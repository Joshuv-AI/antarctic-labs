Effects and safety of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation on cognitive outcomes in mild cognitive impairment: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Hua Ren, Jian Jiao, Xinru Lan, Chunyan Tan, Ziqi Wang, Haonan Pei, Dezhong Yao, Tiejun Liu, Li Dong

Abstract

BackgroundRepetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique that has been increasingly investigated as a potential treatment for mild cognitive impairment (MCI). However, its clinical efficacy remains uncertain due to heterogeneous findings across studies.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials comparing active rTMS with sham stimulation or matched control interventions was conducted. Electronic databases were searched from inception. Eligible studies included patients with MCI and reported cognitive outcomes using validated scales, including the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), or Rivermead Behavioral Memory Test (RBMT).ResultsFourteen studies contributing 15 datasets were included. Compared with sham or control interventions, rTMS was associated with higher post-intervention scores on the MoCA, MMSE, and RBMT. For the primary MoCA outcome, the pooled effect estimate was significant but accompanied by substantial between-study heterogeneity (Hedges’ g = 1.46, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.94; p < 0.001; I2 = 86.0%). Exploratory subgroup analyses showed significant between-subgroup differences according to stimulation frequency (p = 0.021) and stimulation site (p < 0.001), whereas the difference according to risk of bias was not statistically significant (p = 0.114). Among the 10 datasets reporting quantitative safety outcomes, no significant increase in adverse events was observed compared with sham stimulation (RR = 1.25, 95% CI 0.76 to 2.04; p = 0.374).ConclusionrTMS may improve cognitive test performance in patients with MCI. However, substantial between-study heterogeneity limits the precision and generalizability of the estimated treatment effect. Although exploratory subgroup analyses suggested variation according to stimulation frequency and site, these findings do not establish definitive effect modifiers or optimal stimulation parameters. Further well-designed, adequately powered trials with rigorous blinding, standardized protocols, and clinically meaningful outcomes are needed to clarify the magnitude and clinical relevance of rTMS effects in MCI.

Introduction

As global population ageing deepens, cognitive impairment has emerged as a major public-health concern that compromises the well-being of older adults and places an increasingly heavy burden on medical and social care systems (). Cognitive impairment spans a continuum of decline from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to overt dementia, characterized by deficits across multiple cognitive domains – most notably memory, executive function, attention and language (; ). Such deficits substantially erode patients’ functional independence and quality of life, while imposing long-term caregiving pressure on families and on society at large (). Timely identification of individuals with MCI and early intervention may delay or even halt the progression to dementia, representing a critical stage in dementia prevention and management (; ).

Currently, clinical interventions for cognitive impairment remain limited in scope. Pharmacological treatments offer only modest benefits, are indicated for narrow patient populations, and are frequently constrained by tolerability and long-term adherence concerns (). Conventional non-pharmacological interventions, although generally safe, are often criticized for their slow onset of action, variable adherence, and inconsistent effectiveness, and consequently fall short of the clinical demand for an efficient, safe and scalable cognitive intervention (). Exploring novel interventions that are safe, non-invasive, well-tolerated, and capable of improving cognition and slowing disease progression has therefore become a priority research direction in the field of cognitive disorders (; ).

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique that has been widely applied to a range of neurological and psychiatric disorders (), owing to its ease of use, favorable safety profile and generally mild side-effect profile. A growing body of clinical research indicates that rTMS can meaningfully improve core cognitive domains – including memory, executive function and attention – in patients with cognitive impairment, thereby raising overall cognitive performance and providing a promising non-pharmacological therapeutic option (; ). Multiple primary trials and previous meta-analyses have reported that rTMS improves global cognitive function and activities of daily living to varying degrees, with demonstrable benefit across populations diverse in etiology and severity (; ).

Despite this encouraging signal, existing evidence is characterized by significant heterogeneity: stimulation protocols vary widely across studies, outcome-assessment frameworks lack standardization, and the resulting effect estimates are heterogeneous (; ; ). In clinical practice, there is still no clear, evidence-based guidance on the overall efficacy of rTMS, the populations most likely to benefit, or the optimal stimulation parameters, making it difficult to support rational, standardized clinical decision-making (; ). The present systematic review and meta-analysis therefore synthesizes the current randomized controlled evidence, with the twin aims of (i) quantifying the effect of rTMS on cognitive performance, with Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores as the primary outcome, and (ii) evaluating its clinical applicability and safety profile in patients with MCI.

Materials and methods

Protocol and reporting standards

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement (; ,). A comprehensive literature search was performed across both English- and Chinese-language electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CNKI and Wanfang Data, from database inception through March 27, 2025. Reference lists of eligible trials and previous relevant systematic reviews were hand-screened to identify additional records. The search combined MeSH terms and free-text keywords related to the intervention (“repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation,” “rTMS,” “transcranial magnetic stimulation”) and the population (“cognitive impairment,” and “mild cognitive impairment”). No language restriction was applied at the search stage; language eligibility was assessed at the full-text screening stage.

Inclusion criteria for the selection of studies

Studies were included if they met all of the following criteria: (i) randomized controlled trials comparing active rTMS with a sham control or evaluating rTMS as an add-on intervention (e.g., rTMS plus co-intervention versus the same co-intervention alone); (ii) participants with clinical diagnosis of MCI established by recognized diagnostic criteria (e.g., Petersen criteria, DSM-5); (iii) reporting of at least one of the pre-specified cognitive outcomes [MoCA, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and Rivermead Behavioral Memory Test (RBMT)] at post-intervention assessment with extractable means, standard deviations and sample sizes; (iv) publication in English or Chinese. Studies were excluded if they were non-randomized, single-arm, or lacked an eligible comparator; used a region- or volume-of-interest neuroimaging design incompatible with the present outcome synthesis; reported samples that overlapped with an already-included trial; lacked extractable between-group outcome data; enrolled participants with comorbid Parkinson’s disease or other confounding neurological conditions; or were published in a language other than English or Chinese.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria; subsequently, potentially relevant records underwent full-text assessment. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, where necessary, adjudication by a third reviewer. A standardized data-extraction form was employed to extract the following data for each included trial: sample size per arm, participant characteristics, TMS protocol, outcome measures and time points, and reported adverse events. Data were cross-checked by a second reviewer and validated against the authoritative primary dataset maintained by the data custodian. For crossover trials (), each treatment period was treated as a separate comparison. Because the within-subject correlation required to account for the paired design was unavailable, the two periods were analyzed as independent observations. This approach does not fully account for the dependence between repeated observations from the same participants and may therefore underestimate the variance of the pooled estimate.

Statistical analysis

Effect-size calculation

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Treatment effects were estimated using post-intervention cognitive scores. For each study, effect sizes were calculated as the between-group difference in post-treatment means comparing the active rTMS group with the sham or control group for pre-specified outcomes, including the MoCA, MMSE, and RBMT. Continuous cognitive outcomes were synthesized using Hedges’ g with 95% confidence intervals. Hedges’ g was used as the standardized effect-size measure because it incorporates a correction for the upward bias of standardized mean differences in small samples. Because the primary analysis was based on post-intervention values, this approach assumes baseline comparability between groups through randomization. Potential baseline imbalances were considered when interpreting the findings. Positive effect sizes indicated better cognitive performance in the active rTMS group. For add-on intervention trials, the estimated effect size was interpreted as the incremental benefit of rTMS beyond the co-intervention.

Data synthesis and heterogeneity

Effect sizes were pooled using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic and the I2 index, with I2 values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively ().

Publication bias

Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test when at least 10 independent studies were available, with p < 0.05 indicating evidence of small-study effects (). For outcomes with fewer than 10 studies, funnel plots were presented for descriptive purposes only. For the MoCA analysis, the crossover study by was excluded because its two comparisons were not statistically independent. When asymmetry or small-study effects were detected, trim-and-fill analysis was applied as a sensitivity analysis ().

Subgroup analyses

Seven subgroup analyses were performed on the primary MoCA outcome to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, stratified by: (i) overall risk of bias (low vs. high/unclear); (ii) stimulation modality (rTMS alone vs. rTMS combined with an adjunctive intervention); (iii) diagnostic criteria (Petersen vs. non-Petersen vs. unspecified); (iv) stimulation frequency (1 Hz vs. ≥ 5 Hz); (v) stimulation intensity [< 100% vs. ≥ 100% of resting motor threshold (RMT)]; (vi) cumulative pulse exposure (< 30,000 vs. ≥ 30,000 pulses); and (vii) stimulation site (left Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex (DLPFC) vs. bilateral DLPFC vs. right DLPFC vs. other). Each subgroup-specific pooled estimate was computed using the same random-effects model, and between-subgroup differences were tested using a fixed-effect Cochrane Q-test for interaction. Subgroup classifications were drawn directly from the pre-coded categorical variables curated in the primary data file to ensure consistency with the original trial-level coding.

Meta-regression analyses

To further explore potential sources of heterogeneity in the primary MoCA analysis, exploratory univariable meta-regression analyses were conducted using 12 independent studies. Mean age, baseline MoCA score, number of treatment sessions, cumulative pulse exposure, and treatment duration were examined separately as continuous study-level moderators. Between-study variance was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood, with the Knapp–Hartung modification applied for statistical inference ().

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary MoCA findings. First, a leave-one-out analysis was performed by sequentially omitting each study and recalculating the pooled effect size to examine whether the overall results were driven by any single trial. Second, the stability of the pooled estimates was evaluated by comparing results obtained from the random-effects model with those from a fixed-effect model. Third, influence diagnostics were assessed using a Baujat plot to identify studies that contributed disproportionately to between-study heterogeneity and to the overall pooled effect. In addition, because the two treatment periods of the crossover trial () were analyzed as independent comparisons, a separate sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding both comparisons from this study and recalculating the primary MoCA pooled estimate.

Adverse-event synthesis

For each trial reporting quantitative safety data, the number of participants experiencing at least one adverse event was extracted for the active and sham arms. A Mantel–Haenszel pooled risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence interval was calculated across trials with non-zero events; trials reporting zero events in both arms were included using a 0.5 continuity correction where necessary. Reported event types, severity, attribution and impact on trial retention were additionally summarized narratively. Trials providing no usable quantitative safety information were explicitly flagged as contributing incomplete safety evidence.

Risk-of-bias assessment

The methodological quality of each included trial was evaluated using an adapted risk-of-bias framework based on the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 1.0). Six prespecified domains were assessed: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, incomplete outcome data, selective outcome reporting, and other sources of bias (). Each domain was assessed as having a low, unclear, or high risk of bias by two independent reviewers, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Domain-level and study-level judgements were summarized in . Risk-of-bias assessments were also used to examine whether methodological quality was associated with variation in the estimated treatment effects, with trials categorized as having low versus high/unclear overall risk of bias for subgroup analysis.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The study selection process is summarized in . A total of 2,948 records were identified through database searching. After removing 800 duplicates, 2,148 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 1,980 were excluded. A total of 168 reports were sought for retrieval, and 163 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility after excluding 5 unretrieved reports. Of the 163 full-text reports assessed for eligibility, 149 were excluded: 88 did not use an eligible randomized controlled design or comparator, 51 did not provide sufficient or extractable between-group cognitive outcome data, 7 were conference abstracts without sufficient full-text information, and 3 were study protocols without outcome data. Finally, 14 studies were included in the meta-analysis, comprising 6 English-language and 8 Chinese-language publications (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). One crossover trial was included as two independent comparisons, resulting in a total of 15 datasets in the quantitative synthesis (). Fourteen datasets contributed to the primary MoCA analysis, five to MMSE, and three to RBMT. The included datasets comprised 706 participants (rTMS n = 367; sham n = 339). Participant characteristics and study details are summarized in , with stimulation parameters provided in . Participants were aged approximately 60–75 years and diagnosed with MCI. Most studies used Petersen criteria, while others applied alternative or unspecified diagnostic frameworks.

All studies used figure-of-eight coils. Stimulation frequency ranged from 1 to 20 Hz, with the majority applying high-frequency protocols (≥ 5 Hz), and stimulation intensity ranged from 80 to 110% of resting motor threshold. The left DLPFC was the most common target, followed by the bilateral or right DLPFC and other regions. Treatment duration, number of sessions, and total pulse exposure varied substantially across studies. Most studies (11/14) applied rTMS as a stand-alone intervention, whereas a minority combined it with adjunctive therapies.

Effects of rTMS on cognitive performance

Fourteen datasets (n = 672; rTMS n = 352, sham n = 320) reported post-intervention MoCA scores (). Random-effects pooling showed higher post-intervention MoCA scores in the rTMS group than in the sham group (Hedges’ g = 1.46, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.94; p < 0.001). However, substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 86.0%, 95% CI 57.5% to 93.2%). Most point estimates favored active rTMS; however, the pooled estimate should be interpreted as an average across heterogeneous studies rather than as a precise estimate of the expected treatment effect. Five datasets contributed to the MMSE analysis (). rTMS was associated with a significant improvement compared with sham (g = 1.08, 95% CI 0.61 to 1.54; p < 0.001), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 57.6%, 95% CI 0.0% to 85.0%). Three datasets assessed RBMT outcomes (), showing a significant benefit of rTMS (g = 0.99, 95% CI 0.24 to 1.75; p = 0.010), despite substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 76.9%, 95% CI 0.0% to 93.7%).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity in MoCA outcomes (–). According to risk of bias, the pooled effect was numerically smaller in the low-risk subgroup than in the high/unclear-risk subgroup; however, the between-subgroup difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.114) (). Notably, the low-risk subgroup comprised only two datasets derived from a single crossover trial. No significant subgroup differences were observed according to adjunctive interventions (p = 0.681) or diagnostic criteria (p = 0.935) (, , respectively). Among stimulation parameters, a significant subgroup difference was observed for stimulation frequency (p = 0.021), with high-frequency stimulation associated with a larger pooled effect than low-frequency stimulation (). In contrast, the between-subgroup differences according to stimulation intensity (p = 0.099) and cumulative pulse exposure (p = 0.143) were not statistically significant (, ). A significant subgroup difference was also observed according to stimulation site (p < 0.001), with bilateral DLPFC stimulation showing the largest pooled effect, followed by left DLPFC stimulation, whereas right DLPFC and other targets showed smaller or less consistent effects ().

Meta-regression analysis

Exploratory univariable meta-regression analyses did not identify significant associations between the MoCA effect size and mean age (β = −0.105, 95% CI −0.105, 95% CI d m = 0.171), baseline MoCA score (β = −0.045, 95% CI −0.710 to 0.621; p = 0.884), number of treatment sessions (β = −0.005, 95% CI −0.069 to 0.058; p = 0.856), treatment duration (β = 0.028, 95% CI −0.214 to 0.271; p = 0.800), or cumulative pulse exposure per 10,000 pulses (β = −0.019, 95% CI −0.085 to 0.047; p = 0.539). Substantial residual heterogeneity remained across all models (residual I2 = 84.96%–85.86%), indicating that these continuous study-level moderators did not adequately explain the observed between-study variability.

Sensitivity analysis of MoCA

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary MoCA findings. In the leave-one-out analysis, sequential omission of each dataset yielded consistently positive pooled effect estimates, with Hedges’ g values remaining within a relatively narrow range of approximately 1.24–1.46. All corresponding 95% confidence intervals excluded zero (). No individual dataset materially altered the direction or statistical significance of the pooled association, indicating that the primary MoCA finding was not driven by any single dataset. To specifically assess the influence of the crossover trial, a separate sensitivity analysis excluding both comparisons from yielded a pooled effect of Hedges’ g = 1.65 (95% CI 1.16 to 2.13; p < 0.001), with substantial heterogeneity remaining (I2 = 84.5%, 95% CI 47.2% to 92.7%) (). Thus, exclusion of the crossover trial did not materially alter the direction or statistical significance of the primary association. Results were also consistent across fixed-effect and random-effects models. Influence diagnostics identified several datasets contributing to heterogeneity, although their exclusion did not materially alter the direction of the pooled association.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed for the MoCA outcome using 12 independent studies after exclusion of the crossover study by . For the MMSE and RBMT outcomes, funnel plots were provided for descriptive purposes only because of the limited number of studies; no formal statistical tests for small-study effects were performed. For the MoCA outcome, visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested some asymmetry (), and Egger’s regression test indicated significant small-study effects (bias = 5.39, SE = 2.07, 95% CI 0.78 to 10.00; p = 0.026). A trim-and-fill sensitivity analysis () imputed no potentially missing studies, and the pooled effect estimate remained unchanged (Hedges’ g = 1.69, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.22). The MMSE and RBMT funnel plots are shown in , and are interpreted descriptively only.

Risk-of-bias assessment in individual studies

Risk-of-bias assessments are summarized in . Across the 14 unique trials, random sequence generation was rated as low risk in 8 trials and unclear risk in 6, whereas allocation concealment was rated as low risk in 2 and unclear risk in 12. Blinding of participants and personnel represented the main methodological concern, with 5 trials rated as low risk, 2 as unclear risk, and 7 as high risk. Incomplete outcome data were rated as low risk in 13 trials and unclear risk in 1, while selective reporting was rated as low risk in 13 trials and high risk in 1. Other bias was rated as low risk in 5 trials and unclear risk in 9. Overall, 2 trials were classified as low risk of bias, 4 as unclear risk, and 8 as high risk. Two trials, and , were judged to be at low risk of bias across all domains. Study-level risk-of-bias classifications are summarized in .

Adverse events

Ten of the 15 included datasets (67%) reported quantitative adverse-event data, while the remaining five provided no usable safety information. Among datasets with available data, adverse events occurred in 29/243 participants (11.9%) in the rTMS group and 21/231 (9.1%) in the sham group. The most commonly reported events were transient headache and dizziness. No serious adverse events attributable to rTMS were reported. Pooled analysis showed no significant difference in adverse-event risk between groups (RR = 1.25, 95% CI 0.76 to 2.04; p = 0.374).

Discussion

This meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found that rTMS was associated with higher post-intervention scores on measures of global cognitive function and memory in patients with MCI. Although the direction of effect generally favored rTMS across the MoCA, MMSE, and RBMT outcomes, the large pooled effect for MoCA was accompanied by substantial heterogeneity, indicating considerable uncertainty in the magnitude and generalizability of the estimated benefit. This overall direction of the finding is consistent with previous studies (; ; ). However, an earlier systematic review reported more modest improvements in MoCA scores, with a standardized mean difference of approximately 0.3–0.5 (), further supporting cautious interpretation. This caution is reinforced by the overall risk-of-bias profile: only 2 of the 14 trials were classified as having a low overall risk of bias, whereas 8 were rated as high risk and 4 as unclear, with blinding representing a particular methodological concern. Although the between-subgroup difference according to risk of bias was not statistically significant (p = 0.114), the numerically larger pooled effect among high/unclear-risk trials and the limited amount of low-risk evidence further support cautious interpretation of the relatively large pooled MoCA effect. Moreover, statistically significant improvements in cognitive test performance should not be assumed to represent clinically meaningful functional benefits, because the included studies did not consistently assess clinically anchored response thresholds or patient-centered functional outcomes.

The clinical interpretation of these findings also depends on the characteristics of the cognitive measures used. The MoCA, MMSE, and RBMT are commonly used tools in the field of cognitive disorders. The MMSE demonstrates limited sensitivity for early MCI due to a ceiling effect, whereas the MoCA shows greater sensitivity to deficits in executive function and visuospatial abilities (). The RBMT focuses on episodic memory and aligns more closely with everyday memory functioning, but it may overlook impairments in other cognitive domains, such as executive function, attention (). In addition, domain-specific outcomes involving executive function, attention, language, and visuospatial abilities were inconsistently reported and assessed using heterogeneous instruments across studies, precluding reliable domain-specific meta-analyses. Therefore, although statistically significant pooled effects were observed across the available cognitive measures, it remains uncertain whether these changes translate into meaningful improvements in daily functioning, independence, or quality of life. Future trials should combine standardized cognitive measures with broader neuropsychological assessments and clinically meaningful functional outcomes to better establish the clinical relevance of rTMS in MCI.

Substantial heterogeneity remained a central feature of the present analysis. Previous studies have identified several potential sources of variability in rTMS trials, including differences in stimulation frequency (high-frequency vs. low-frequency) (; ), intensity, stimulation targets such as the DLPFC, parietal-hippocampal regions (; ; ), and coil type (; ). In the present subgroup analyses, statistically significant between-subgroup differences were observed for stimulation frequency (p = 0.021) and stimulation site (p < 0.001), whereas differences according to risk of bias (p = 0.114), adjunctive interventions (p = 0.681), diagnostic criteria (p = 0.935), stimulation intensity (p = 0.099), and cumulative pulse exposure (p = 0.143) were not statistically significant. These study-level comparisons should nevertheless be interpreted as exploratory rather than as evidence of causal or definitive effect modification, particularly because several stimulation and study characteristics were interrelated and some subgroups contained relatively few studies. Exploratory meta-regression analyses likewise did not identify significant associations between the MoCA effect size and mean age, baseline MoCA score, number of treatment sessions, cumulative pulse exposure, or treatment duration. However, substantial residual heterogeneity remained, suggesting that the available study-level characteristics did not adequately account for the observed between-study variability. Given the limited number of independent studies, these null meta-regression findings should not be interpreted as evidence that these factors are unrelated to treatment response. Thus, the pooled effect should be regarded as an average across clinically and methodologically heterogeneous rTMS protocols rather than as the expected effect of a single standardized intervention.

The risk-of-bias subgroup analysis showed a numerically larger pooled effect among high/unclear-risk trials than among low-risk trials, but the between-subgroup difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.114). Moreover, the low-risk subgroup comprised only two datasets derived from a single crossover trial, substantially limiting the precision of this comparison. Accordingly, the present data do not establish study quality as a definitive effect modifier. Nevertheless, methodological quality remains important when interpreting the relatively large pooled effect. Blinding represents a particular challenge in non-invasive brain stimulation studies (), because even when sham procedures such as coil tilting or shielding pads are used, participants may distinguish treatment allocation through differences in scalp sensations, auditory cues, or coil positioning. Such inadvertent unblinding could increase expectancy or placebo-related effects and potentially inflate estimated treatment effects. The overall pooled estimate should therefore continue to be interpreted cautiously, although the present subgroup analysis does not provide statistical evidence that risk of bias significantly modified the treatment effect.

In contrast to the findings for frequency and stimulation site, the between-subgroup differences according to stimulation intensity (p = 0.099) and cumulative pulse exposure (p = 0.143) were not statistically significant. Therefore, the present results do not support a clear dose-response relationship or an optimal intensity or pulse dose. Although stimulation intensity and cumulative pulse exposure may influence neuroplastic responses under some experimental conditions (; ), higher stimulation doses do not necessarily translate into greater clinical benefit. Moreover, treatment-related variables are often correlated, making it difficult to isolate the independent contribution of intensity or pulse exposure in study-level subgroup analyses. Direct comparative trials that systematically vary individual stimulation parameters are needed before conclusions regarding dose optimization can be made.

Regarding stimulation site, the bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) stimulation was associated with a larger pooled effect in the subgroup analysis. However, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that bilateral DLPFC stimulation is superior, given the small number of studies in some subgroups and potential confounding by study quality and other protocol characteristics. The DLPFC nevertheless remains a biologically plausible target because of its role in cognitive control, working memory, and executive function, as well as its functional connections with memory-related brain regions such as the hippocampus (; ; ). Stimulation of the DLPFC may therefore influence cognition not only through local modulation of cortical excitability but also through distributed network-level effects (; ; ). More broadly, rTMS is one of several non-invasive brain-stimulation approaches investigated for cognitive enhancement. Studies in healthy adults have suggested that prefrontal, particularly DLPFC-targeted, rTMS can modulate attention, working memory, executive function, and other cognitive processes, although the magnitude and direction of these effects vary according to stimulation protocol, cognitive task, and baseline brain state (). In addition, differences in total sessions and outcome assessment time points across studies are important factors contributing to heterogeneity (). Regarding concomitant medication, some studies suggest that combining rTMS with neurotransmitter-targeting drugs, including donepezil, may influence cognitive and network-level outcomes (; ), but most studies did not control for this variable. These protocol and clinical factors further complicate the interpretation of target-specific effects.

Several complementary biological mechanisms may contribute to the cognitive effects of rTMS. At the synaptic level, repeated stimulation may induce long-term potentiation- or long-term depression-like plasticity and influence metaplasticity, thereby altering the responsiveness of cortical circuits to subsequent stimulation (). Activity-dependent modulation of neurotrophic factors, particularly brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), may further support synaptic remodeling and plasticity (). rTMS may also modify glutamatergic and GABAergic neurotransmission and influence dopaminergic signaling relevant to attention, learning, and executive control (; ). At the systems level, prefrontal stimulation may propagate beyond the stimulated cortex and reorganize connectivity within distributed cognitive networks, including the frontoparietal and default mode networks, both of which are implicated in cognitive dysfunction in MCI (). rTMS may additionally alter regional cerebral blood flow and metabolic activity, potentially modifying metabolic support within cognition-related networks (). These mechanisms are likely to interact across synaptic, neurochemical, network, and vascular levels; however, their specific contributions to the cognitive effects observed in MCI remain to be established.

The present findings can also be viewed in the broader context of rTMS applications in major depressive disorder (MDD), where DLPFC-targeted rTMS has an established therapeutic role and cognitive improvement is generally considered a secondary outcome. Although MCI and MDD differ in their primary clinical targets, both may involve modulation of prefrontal networks and neuroplasticity. Research in depression has also explored individualized prediction of rTMS response using time-frequency EEG, non-linear signal processing, reinforcement-learning approaches, and machine-learning models (; , , ; ). These approaches may provide a useful methodological framework for future MCI studies integrating neurophysiological, cognitive, and clinical features to improve patient selection. However, protocols and predictive models developed in MDD cannot be directly extrapolated to MCI, for which the current evidence remains limited and heterogeneous.

No statistically significant between-subgroup differences were observed according to diagnostic criteria or adjunctive interventions. However, these null subgroup tests should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence across diagnostic frameworks or treatment contexts. Diagnostic criteria varied across the included studies, and many studies either did not clearly differentiate etiological subtypes or lacked sufficient statistical power for such comparisons. Furthermore, concomitant medication use was incompletely and inconsistently reported, precluding reliable assessment of whether background pharmacotherapy modified the effects of rTMS. Medication status may therefore represent an additional source of between-study heterogeneity. Therefore, the absence of significant subgroup differences should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect modification, and future trials should improve diagnostic stratification and reporting of concomitant medications and baseline characteristics.

Sensitivity analyses showed that the main findings were generally robust. The overall effect size remained statistically significant after the sequential exclusion of individual studies, and the results were consistent across different statistical models. These findings indicate that the observed association between rTMS and cognitive improvement was not driven by any single study or by the choice of statistical model, thereby supporting the stability of the overall direction of effect. Importantly, exclusion of both comparisons from the crossover trial by yielded a pooled effect of Hedges’ g = 1.65 (95% CI 1.16 to 2.13; p < 0.001), with I2 = 84.5%. Thus, inclusion of the crossover trial did not materially alter the direction or statistical significance of the overall association. Nevertheless, treating the two treatment periods as independent observations does not account for their within-subject correlation and may underestimate the variance of the pooled estimate.

Potential small-study effects provide an additional reason for caution. Egger’s regression test indicated significant small-study effects for the MoCA outcome, although the trim-and-fill analysis imputed no missing studies and did not materially change the pooled estimate. Given the substantial between-study heterogeneity, funnel-plot asymmetry cannot be attributed solely to publication bias and may also reflect differences in sample size, study quality, stimulation protocols, or other study characteristics. For the MMSE and RBMT outcomes, the small numbers of available studies precluded reliable statistical assessment of funnel-plot asymmetry, and the corresponding funnel plots should therefore be interpreted descriptively only.

Among the datasets reporting quantitative safety outcomes, no statistically significant increase in reported adverse events was observed with rTMS compared with sham stimulation. Reported adverse events were predominantly mild and transient, and no serious adverse events attributable to rTMS were reported. However, quantitative adverse-event data were available for only 10 of the 15 datasets, limiting the completeness of the safety evidence. In particular, the available data primarily reflect short-term adverse events and do not permit firm conclusions regarding long-term tolerability or rare adverse events. This limitation is clinically relevant in MCI, which predominantly affects older adults who may have greater medical comorbidity, polypharmacy, and vulnerability to treatment-related adverse effects. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant increase in reported adverse events should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of safety in routine clinical practice. Previous studies have also often reported tolerability without systematically documenting the incidence, severity, causal relationship to treatment, and outcomes of adverse events (). More comprehensive and standardized safety reporting is therefore needed to characterize the long-term safety profile of rTMS in this population.

From a clinical perspective, the available evidence suggests that rTMS may improve cognitive test performance in MCI, but it does not yet permit reliable identification of patients most likely to benefit or establish an optimal stimulation protocol. Although significant between-subgroup differences were observed for stimulation frequency and site, the findings remain exploratory and should not be interpreted as demonstrating that high-frequency or bilateral DLPFC stimulation is superior. Evidence is also insufficient to define the optimal stimulation intensity, cumulative pulse exposure, treatment duration, expected time course or durability of benefit, or the need for maintenance sessions. Future trials should directly compare prespecified stimulation protocols and evaluate longer-term outcomes, maintenance strategies, and integration with pharmacological or cognitive interventions ().

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the limited number of trials within several subgroups reduced statistical power and the precision of subgroup estimates. Second, domain-specific cognitive outcomes were inconsistently reported, preventing quantitative assessment of whether rTMS preferentially affects particular cognitive domains. Third, substantial residual heterogeneity remained after the subgroup and meta-regression analyses, limiting the precision and generalizability of the overall pooled effect. Although exploratory meta-regression analyses examined mean age, baseline MoCA score, number of treatment sessions, cumulative pulse exposure, and treatment duration, none adequately explained the observed heterogeneity. Other potentially relevant sources of variability, including target-localization method, sham methodology, and outcome assessment timing, could not be formally evaluated because of limited and inconsistent reporting. In addition, correlations among study and stimulation characteristics made it difficult to isolate the independent contribution of any single factor. The subgroup and meta-regression findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory rather than as evidence of definitive effect modifiers. Because multiple exploratory subgroup comparisons were performed without adjustment for multiplicity and some subgroups contained very few studies, nominally significant subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously. Fourth, because the primary analyses were based on post-intervention scores, residual baseline imbalances in small trials may have influenced individual effect estimates despite randomization. Fifth, due to the absence of within-subject correlation data, one crossover trial was analyzed as two independent comparisons, a methodological approach that may have contributed to imprecision in the pooled estimates. However, exclusion of both comparisons from this crossover trial yielded a similar overall conclusion, suggesting that inclusion of this study did not materially affect the direction or statistical significance of the primary finding. Finally, although potential small-study effects were formally assessed for the primary MoCA outcome, the small numbers of studies contributing to the MMSE and RBMT analyses precluded reliable statistical assessment of funnel-plot asymmetry. The funnel plots for these secondary outcomes should therefore be regarded as descriptive only, and publication bias or small-study effects cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Overall, the available randomized evidence suggests that rTMS may improve cognitive test performance in patients with MCI. However, substantial between-study heterogeneity limits the precision and generalizability of the pooled effect. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested variation according to stimulation frequency and site, but these findings do not establish definitive effect modifiers or optimal stimulation parameters. Other examined subgroup factors, including risk of bias, did not show statistically significant between-subgroup differences. No significant increase in reported short-term adverse events was observed, although safety reporting was incomplete. Well-designed, adequately powered trials with rigorous blinding, standardized stimulation protocols, clinically meaningful outcome measures, and longer follow-up are needed to clarify the magnitude, durability, and clinical relevance of rTMS effects in MCI.
