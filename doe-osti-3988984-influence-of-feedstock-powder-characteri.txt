 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 Powder metallurgy hot isostatic pressing (PM-HIP) offers a near-net-shape
manufacturing route for producing large and complex nuclear components while
reducing material waste and machining/welding requirements. However, powder-
derived oxides reduce the creep and creep–fatigue performance of PM-HIP
316H, creating a challenge for code qualification and high-temperature
deployment. This study investigated the effects of powder feedstock
characteristics, particularly oxygen content, on the microstructure and
mechanical properties of PM-HIP 316/316H. Three feedstocks were evaluated
which include virgin powder with 0.028 wt.% O, recycled powder with 0.047
wt.% O, and recycled powder with 0.034 wt.% O. Recycled powder refers to
powder recovered after laser powder bed fusion. The fabricated PM-HIP billets
were denoted as large HIP can (LHC)-01, LHC-02, and LHC-03, respectively.
Characterization of the powder feedstock revealed differences in powder
morphology, microstructure, and surface oxide films. The three as-HIPed billets
exhibited equiaxed austenitic grain structures but differed substantially in grain
size and oxide distribution. LHC-03 had the finest grain structure, whereas LHC-
02 had the coarsest grains. Heat treatment produced moderate grain growth (by
~40%) in LHC-01 and pronounced grain growth (by ~74%) in LHC-03, while
LHC-02 showed no appreciable change. Larger oxide particles or clusters
developed after heat treatment, particularly in LHC-03. These observations
demonstrate that microstructural evolution is governed by the size, distribution,
and chemistry of powder-derived oxides rather than bulk oxygen content alone.
All three billets exceeded applicable ASME room-temperature tensile
requirements in both as-HIPed and heat-treated conditions. Solution annealing
reduced tensile strength, with the greatest reduction occurring in LHC-03
because of its greater grain growth. Creep testing showed that post-HIP heat
treatment influenced deformation and rupture behavior, through the coarsening
and redistribution of powder-derived oxides. These findings demonstrate the
need for powder-conditioning strategies to reduce oxide-related damage and
improve the creep performance of PM-HIP 316/316H stainless steel.

 Influence of Feedstock Powder Characteristics
 on the Microstructure and Mechanical
 Performance of PM-HIP 316H
 1. INTRODUCTION
 Qualification of a structural material for nuclear service depends not only on alloy designation but
also on the manufacturing route. This distinction is important for advanced manufacturing processes such
as powder metallurgy-hot isostatic pressing (PM-HIP). Type 316/316H stainless steel has an extensive
high-temperature database and is permitted for elevated-temperature construction under ASME Boiler
and Pressure Vessel Code Section III, Division 5. However, the existing design was developed from
wrought products. Applying the same nominal chemistry through PM-HIP introduces a different
microstructural history and a different population of features that may affect long-term (creep and creep-
fatigue) properties. Qualification of PM-HIP 316H requires evidence that the process produces
reproducible properties and that its process-specific microstructures are adequately understood. PM-HIP
can consolidate powder directly into dense, near-net-shape components through temperature and isostatic
gas pressure. Complex geometries can be produced with high material utilization, fewer machining
operations, and fewer large weldments than many conventional fabrication routes [1]. These capabilities
can reduce production time and expand the supply chain for advanced-reactor components. Reflecting this
potential, the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) includes PM-HIP among several advanced
manufacturing technologies under evaluation for nuclear deployment [2, 3]. The qualification challenge is
that the PM-HIP material can provide predictable performance over the full range of nuclear conditions.
 PM-HIP consolidates powders which may have nano-scale oxide films whose thickness and
chemistry depend on alloy composition, particle size, atomization conditions, handling, storage, and reuse
history. During HIP, neighboring particles deform and bond, but stable surface oxides are not necessarily
dissolved. They may fragment or react with oxygen-active alloying elements and become incorporated
into the billet as oxide particles along prior particle boundaries. Carbon and nitrogen further affect carbide
or nitride precipitation, solid-solution strengthening, and grain-boundary mobility. Consequently,
powders satisfying the same nominal Type 316 composition can generate different grain sizes, inclusion
populations, and prior particle boundary structures after HIP. This issue is not fully captured by bulk
composition alone. Oxygen concentration measures the total oxygen inventory but does not define oxide-
film continuity, local thickness, chemistry, or surface coverage. Powder particle-size distribution also
changes the total surface area and influences packing, recrystallization, and retention of prior particle
boundaries. Studies of HIPed 316 have shown that increasing powder oxygen content increased oxide-
inclusion density, while powder-size distribution affected inclusion topology and microstructural
homogeneity [4, 5]. Other work has demonstrated that oxide size and spacing can determine whether
particles have little effect on tensile strength or accelerate void formation and fracture [6, 7]. Powder
reuse adds another variable because exposure to prior processing, sieving, handling, and storage may
modify surface oxides.
 PM-HIP 316H can exhibit tensile properties and low-cycle fatigue properties comparable to wrought
material [8, 9]. In contrast, creep and creep-fatigue studies have reported substantially reduced properties
for PM-HIP 316H [10-14]. Studies also reported reduced creep and cyclic properties in PM-HIP nickel
alloys [15-17]. During sustained high-temperature loading, oxide-matrix interfaces and decorated particle
boundaries can promote stress concentration, interfacial separation, and creep-cavity nucleation [10].
Cavities may then grow and link along susceptible boundaries, causing premature rupture. This contrast
makes creep resistance a central obstacle to code qualification. A systematic feedstock comparison is
needed to understand the influence of powder chemistry on mechanical properties. Such understanding is
essential for establishing powder acceptance criteria and developing powder conditioning strategies to

 1

obtain mechanical properties comparable to the wrought product.
 This work evaluated three commercially available Type 316 powder feedstocks with different oxygen
contents ranging from 0.028 to 0.047 wt.%. All powders were consolidated using the same HIP cycle at
the same time. The resulting billets were examined in the as-HIPed and solution-annealed conditions.
Powder characterization, microscopy, grain-size analysis, tensile testing, and creep testing were used to
connect feedstock conditions with billet microstructure and mechanical response. The study provides a
basis for defining powder-process-microstructure-property relationships to support powder conditioning
for improved elevated-temperature mechanical properties of PM-HIP 316/316H.
 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
 2.1. Powder Feedstock and HIP Processing
 Three commercially produced Type 316 stainless steel powder feedstocks were evaluated to
determine the effects of powder source, oxygen content, and reuse history on the microstructure and
mechanical properties of PM-HIP material. The feedstocks included virgin Sandvik powder, recycled
powder supplied by PAC, and recycled Praxair powder. In this report, recycled powder refers to powder
remaining after previous laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) processing, whereas virgin powder refers to
powder that had not previously been used in any form. The corresponding consolidated billets by PM-HIP
were designated as large HIP can (LHC) -1 (Sandvik), LHC-2 (PAC), and LHC-3 (Praxair), respectively.
The three feedstocks were selected primarily to investigate how differences in powder oxygen content
influence oxide formation and the creep properties of PM-HIP 316/316H.
 The measured compositions of the powders are summarized in Table 1. The recycled PAC powder
contained the lowest carbon concentration (0.040 wt.%) but the highest oxygen and nitrogen
concentrations, at 0.047 and 0.068 wt.%, respectively. The Sandvik powder had the lowest oxygen
content (0.028 wt.%), while the recycled Praxair powder had the intermediate oxygen content (0.034
wt.%). Variations in carbon were also considered when interpreting the resulting microstructures and
mechanical behavior.

 Each powder feedstock was loaded into a separate 316L stainless steel HIP canister with a diameter
of 65 mm and length of 150 mm. Powder loading was performed with intermittent vibration to improve

 2

particle packing and minimize large internal voids. After filling, the canisters were evacuated to remove
entrapped gas. The evacuation tubes were subsequently sealed by welding to produce hermetic assemblies
before consolidation.
 All three canisters were consolidated at the same time in a single HIP cycle to isolate the influence of
feedstock characteristics from variations in processing conditions. HIP was performed at 1160°C under an
argon pressure of 103 MPa, with a 4-hour hold at the target temperature and pressure. A heating rate of
10°C/min was used. The simultaneous application of elevated temperature and isostatic pressure
promoted particle deformation, interparticle diffusion, and pore closure, producing near-fully dense PM-
HIP billets. After completion of the cycle, the billets were cooled with a cooling rate of ~10°C/min and
removed for subsequent specimen extraction, microstructural characterization, and mechanical testing.
Figure 1a shows the images of the LHC-01, LHC-02, and LHC-03. Figure 1b shows the history of
temperature and pressure in consolidating the three billets.

 Following HIP, each billet was sectioned transversely into two equal portions (Figure 1a). A slice was
removed from the center of each billet for microstructural characterization. The remaining billet sections
were used for machining mechanical testing specimens. As shown in Figure 1a, cylindrical blanks were
extracted from the three billets for tensile, creep, and creep-fatigue testing. The extraction plan was
displayed in Figure 2a. Four cylindrical blanks were extracted from each billet for tensile testing
including both room-temperature (RT) tensile testing and elevated-temperature (ET, 650°C) tensile

testing. The tensile specimens were evaluated in both as-HIPed condition and heat-treated condition.
Before machining heat-treated specimens, solution annealing (SA1) at 1150°C with a 4h hold and water
quenching (WQ) was performed on two tensile cylindrical blanks, one creep blank, and two creep-fatigue
blanks. Figure 2b shows the dimensions of the tensile/creep specimens.
 2.2. Mechanical Testing
 Four tensile specimens were prepared from each billet. Two specimens were tested in the as-HIPed
condition (one tested at RT and the other at 650°C). The two SA1 specimens were tested at RT and
650°C. This test matrix enabled comparison of powder feedstock and post-HIP heat-treatment effects on
the RT and ET tensile properties. The tests were performed using a Zwick KAPPA 250 DS tensile
machine equipped with a 100-kN load cell. During testing, initial deformation was controlled at a
crosshead displacement rate of 0.5 mm/min with gauge-section strain measured using an extensometer.
After the engineering strain reached 5%, the extensometer was removed, and testing continued under a
crosshead displacement rate of 1.5 mm/min until specimen fractured. Engineering stress-strain curves
were generated from the recorded data. The yield strength (YS), ultimate tensile strength (UTS), and total
elongation were obtained for each specimen.
 Creep testing was performed in air at 650°C under an applied stress of 160 MPa. During heating, a
preload corresponding to approximately 15 MPa was applied to maintain specimen alignment. After
reaching the target temperature, each specimen was held for 3 hours to establish thermal equilibrium
before the load was increased to the target stress. The specimens were then tested until rupture.

 2.3. Microstructure Characterization
 Microstructural characterization was conducted on the powder feedstocks and sintered materials from
the three PM-HIP billets in both as-HIPed and solution-annealed conditions. Metallographic specimens
were extracted from the excess material remaining in the cylindrical blanks used to machine the
corresponding mechanical test specimens. Detailed characterization of the powder-derived oxides
associated with the different feedstock oxygen levels was performed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL) as part of the assigned project scope. The Idaho National Laboratory (INL) characterization
therefore focused on the overall grain structure, residual porosity, prior particle boundaries, and
microstructural features needed to correlate the billet conditions with their tensile, creep, and creep-
fatigue behavior. Metallographic samples were mounted and prepared using conventional grinding and
polishing procedures. Final preparation was performed in a vibratory polisher using a 0.02µm colloidal
silica suspension for ~4 hours. The polished samples were examined using an FEI Quanta 650 scanning
electron microscope (SEM). Secondary-electron (SE) and backscattered-electron (BSE) imaging were
used to examine residual pores, prior particle boundaries, and the morphology and distribution of
secondary phases. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was used to characterize grain morphology
and grain size. The EBSD data were processed using Orientation Imaging Microscopy analysis software,
with data points having confidence indices below 0.1 excluded from the analysis.

 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
 3.1. Characterization of Powder Feedstocks
 Figure 3 compares representative cross-sectional microstructures and near-surface elemental
distributions of the three powder feedstocks. All three powders exhibited a rapidly solidified cellular and
dendritic characteristic of gas-atomized stainless-steel powder. However, noticeable differences were
observed in the scale and uniformity of the internal solidification structures, the population of particle-
scale defects, and the morphology of the surface oxide layers. The virgin Sandvik powder displayed a
comparatively uniform cellular structure across the particle cross section (Figures 3a1 and 3a2). The cells
were generally equiaxed, although elongated solidification features were present near portions of the
particle surface. A limited number of small dark features, confirmed as oxides of Mo, Mn, and Si, were
observed within the particle. High-resolution characterization revealed a thin (<10 nm in thickness) and
relatively continuous surface layer enriched in O, Si, and Mn on Sandvik powders (Figure 3a3). The
recycled PAC powder exhibited a more heterogeneous internal microstructure (Figures 3b1 and 3b2). The
particle contained irregular cellular and dendritic regions, with a comparatively high population of fine
oxides. Satellite particles and surface irregularities were also visible along the particle perimeter. The
near-surface elemental maps showed an oxide layer with a thickness of ~10 nm (Figure 3b3). Compared
with the Sandvik powder, the PAC surface layer appeared rougher. The elevated oxygen layer of the PAC
feedstock may be related to the powder nature or its previous use in LPBF.
 The recycled Praxair powder also contained a heterogeneous solidification structure, including
cellular regions, elongated features, and isolated internal defects (Figures 3c1 and 3c2). The powder had
surface oxides with a thickness of ~60 nm, despite its intermediate bulk oxygen content of 0.034 wt.%.
Therefore, bulk oxygen concentration does not necessarily describe the local thickness, continuity, or
chemistry of the oxide film on individual powder feedstock. The present characterization provided a
qualitative comparison of the three feedstocks. Detailed analysis of powder morphology, particle-size
distribution, oxide thickness, oxide chemistry, and surface-oxide coverage will be documented in a future
milestone report led by ORNL.

 3.2. Microstructure of PM-HIP 316/316H
 Figure 4 shows the BSE images of the three PM-HIP billets in the as-HIPed and solution-annealed
conditions (SA1). All three billets exhibited consolidated austenitic microstructures composed primarily
of equiaxed grains. No continuous network of macroscopic porosity or unconsolidated powder-particle
interfaces was evident, indicating effective densification during HIP processing. The as-HIPed LHC-01,
produced from the virgin Sandvik powder with the lowest oxygen content, exhibited a heterogeneous
equiaxed grain structure with isolated dark features distributed within grains and along grain boundaries
(Figure 4a1). Most of these features were spatially separated. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) analysis on a previous smaller PM-HIP billet (65 mm diameter and 100 mm height) from the same
batch of Sandvik powder reported in our previous report [10] revealed that two inclusions were present in
the as-HIPed microstructure. These inclusions contained refined particles with enrichment in Mn and O,
while several large inclusions were enriched in Mn, Mo, Si, and O. Following SA1 at 1150°C, the overall
grain morphology remained similar (Figure 4a2). Numerous large dark features remained within the
microstructure at grain boundaries, as shown in Figure 4a2. These dark features were not pores, but oxide
particles as evidenced by the SE images shown in Figures 5a1 and 5a2. These oxides were enriched in
Mo, Si, and O. The SA1 treatment did not completely dissolve the precipitates in the As-HIPed
microstructure but enlarged the stable oxides at grain boundaries. During SA1, migrating grain
boundaries may sweep or redistribute fine powder-derived oxide particles, concentrating them at newly

 6

formed grain boundaries and triple junctions. The increased proximity of these particles can promote
agglomeration or coarsening, producing fewer but larger oxides.
 LHC-02, fabricated from the recycled PAC powder with the highest measured oxygen content (0.047
wt.%), contained a comparatively high population of fine dark features in the as-HIPed condition (Figure
4b1). Many of these features were arranged along grain boundaries and curved interparticle traces,
producing locally decorated boundary networks. This morphology is consistent with the retention of
surface-derived constituents at prior particle boundaries during solid-state HIP consolidation. Because
PM-HIP is a solid-state consolidation process, these surface oxides were retained at prior particle
boundaries, contributing to the comparatively high population of fine boundary-associated oxides
observed in LHC-02. After SA1, the boundary-associated oxides appeared to be discontinued slightly
(Figure 4b2), although some oxides were still observed at grain boundaries. Several large oxides were
also observed as indicated in the SE images in Figures 5b1 and 5b2.
 As shown in Figure 4c1, the as-HIPed LHC-03 produced from Praxair powder also exhibited
relatively fine oxides along grain boundaries similar to the as-HIPed microstructure of LHC-02.
Following SA1, the LHC-03 exhibited many large and irregular oxides at grain boundaries (Figures 4c2,
5c1, and 5c2). Figure 4c2 also shows many localized fine oxides in the sample after SA1. Since the LHC-
03 was fabricated from recycled Praxair powder containing a high O impurity and thick oxide film (~60
nm) on powder surfaces, large, accumulated oxides were observed in the sintered billet. It can be
observed from Figures 4 and 5 that the LHC-02 fabricated from the powder with the highest oxygen
concentration exhibited the most pronounced population of fine boundary-associated oxides. This
observation is consistent with increased retention of powder-surface oxides at higher feedstock oxygen
levels. The LHC-01 produced from virgin powder with the lowest oxygen concentration has the lowest
level of oxide population (Figure 4a1). In terms of heat treatment, SA at 1150°C resulted in limited
change of grain structure but significant coarsening of the oxides.
 Figure 6 shows the EBSD inverse pole figure (IPF) maps and corresponding grain size distribution
of the as-HIPed LHC-01 (Figures 6a1, 6a2), LHC-02 (Figures 6b1, 6b2), and LHC-03 (Figures 6c1, 6c2).
Compared with the wrought 316H which had a grain size of 39.9 ± 38.1 µm [10], the as-HIPed billets had
much refined grains due to the fine powders and HIP processing nature. Among the three powder
feedstocks, the LHC-03 had the smallest grain size while the LHC-02 had the largest grain size. The
differences in grain size among the three billets could result from the combined effects of powder
chemistry and secondary-phase distribution in addition to oxygen and carbon concentration. LHC-03,
which had the highest carbon content (0.081 wt.%), may have developed a comparatively high density of
fine carbides that restricted grain-boundary migration through pinning, resulting in the smallest average
grain size. Conversely, the lower carbon content of LHC-02 (0.040 wt.%) may have reduced carbide
pinning and permitted greater grain growth. Detailed characterization of carbide and oxide size, number
density, and spatial distribution through transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atom probe
tomography (APT) is helpful to further confirm these mechanisms. In addition to chemical composition,
differences in the initial powder particle-size distribution and intraparticle grain structure may have
contributed to the grain-size variation among the HIPed billets. Smaller atomized particles generally
experience higher cooling rates and consequently develop finer internal microstructures. Their greater
surface area also increases the density of prior particle boundaries and surface-derived oxides, which may
restrict grain-boundary migration during HIP processing.

 Figure 7 shows the EBSD IPF maps and grain size statistics of the SA1 LHC-01 (Figures 7a1, 7a2),
LHC-02 (Figures 7b1, 7b2), and LHC-03 (Figures 7c1, 7c2). The three billets exhibited markedly
different responses to SA. The average grain size of LHC-03 exhibited a ~74% growth after SA1,
whereas LHC-01 exhibited a ~40% increase in grain size after SA1. In contrast, LHC-02 showed no
measurable grain growth after SA1. This behavior reflects competition between the curvature-driven
force for grain growth and particle-induced pinning. The initially fine-grained LHC-03 (7.2 ± 6.4 µm) had
the highest grain-boundary energy and the strongest driving force for coarsening. The high carbon content
may have produced carbides that initially restricted grain growth in the as-HIPed material, but the

 10

dissolution of these carbides during SA1 reduced the pinning effect, which resulted in substantial grain
growth. LHC-01 had a lower initial driving force and consequently exhibited more moderate growth.
Conversely, LHC-02 began with the coarsest grain structure due to the lowest carbon content. However,
due to the highest powder oxygen concentration, the LHC-02 was expected to have a high density of
thermally stable, boundary-associated oxides, which could restrict grain-boundary migration.

 3.3. Tensile Behavior
 Figure 8 presents the RT engineering stress-strain curves for LHC-01, LHC-02, and LHC-03 in the
as-HIPed and SA1 conditions. The response of wrought 316H was included as a reference. The YS, UTS,
and total elongation of the as-HIPed materials are summarized in Figures 9a and 9b, while the
corresponding properties after SA1 are presented in Figures 9c and 9d. Figure 10 directly compares the
properties before and after SA1 for each billet.
 Despite the differences in powder source, reuse history, and oxygen content, the three as-HIPed
billets exhibited very similar tensile strengths. The YS ranged from 278 MPa for LHC-02 to 286 MPa for
LHC-01, representing a variation of less than 3% among the billets. These values were 8-10% lower than
the YS of wrought 316H (310 MPa). The UTS varied from 637 MPa for LHC-03 to 647 MPa for LHC-
01, a difference of less than 2%, and was comparable to the wrought-material value of 635 MPa. These
results indicated that the powder-feedstock differences examined in this study had only a limited effect on
the RT tensile strength of the as-HIPed materials. The as-HIPed billets also retained excellent tensile
ductility. Total elongation ranged from 64.2 to 66.0%, exceeding the 56.6% measured for wrought 316H.
All three as-HIPed billets exceeded the applicable ASME minimum RT tensile properties for 316H. The
combination of comparable UTS and greater elongation demonstrated that the powder-derived oxides and
other microstructural heterogeneities did not produce substantial embrittlement under RT tensile loading.
Although oxide particles may act as localized stress concentrators, the high elongations suggested that
they were sufficiently discrete and that plastic deformation remained governed primarily by the ductile
austenitic matrix. This behavior differs from long-duration creep, during which oxide-matrix interfaces
may provide sufficient time for cavity nucleation and growth [10].

 As shown in Figure 10, SA reduced both YS and UTS in all three billets. The magnitude of strength
reduction was qualitatively correlated with grain-growth behavior. LHC-03 experienced the largest
increase in average grain size, from 7.2 to 12.5 µm (74% growth) as shown in Figures 6 and 7. It also
exhibited the largest reductions in YS and UTS, ~12 and ~8%, respectively, as shown in Figures 9 and 10.
This response is consistent with a reduction in Hall-Petch grain-boundary strengthening, potentially
combined with the dissolution of fine carbide particles during solution annealing. LHC-01 underwent
more moderate grain growth, from 10.8 to 15.1 µm, and correspondingly showed a smaller reduction in
strength. LHC-02 retained an essentially unchanged average grain size but still softened after SA1,
indicating that dissolution of precipitates and secondary-phase evolution were important contributors to
its tensile response.
 The effect of solution annealing on ductility differed among the billets. Total elongation decreased for
LHC-01 and LHC-03 but increased slightly for LHC-02. The greatest reduction was observed for LHC-
03, which also contained relatively large oxides after SA (Figures 4c2 and 4c1). These large oxide
agglomerates could have promoted local strain concentration, particle-matrix decohesion, and earlier
crack initiation, resulting in the sharp reduction of elongation in LHC-03. A similar, although less
pronounced, mechanism may have contributed to the reduction in elongation of LHC-01. In contrast, the
persistence of a fine distributed particle population in LHC-02 may have limited the formation of large
crack-initiation sites, allowing its elongation to remain stable or increase slightly.

 Powder carbon content revealed a stronger relationship with the heat-treatment response than with the
absolute tensile strength of the as-HIPed materials. In the as-HIPed condition, the low-carbon LHC-02
billet exhibited the lowest YS, while LHC-01 exhibited the highest YS and UTS. However, LHC-03,

 13

which contained the highest carbon concentration, did not exhibit the highest strength. The absence of a
monotonic relationship indicates that RT tensile strength was not controlled by carbon content alone.
Differences in grain size, nitrogen and oxygen concentrations also contributed to the measured behavior.
A clearer carbon-related trend was observed during solution annealing. The higher-carbon LHC-01 and
LHC-03 materials exhibited ~40% and ~74% grain growth, respectively, whereas the low-carbon LHC-02
material showed no measurable grain growth. Fine carbides may have restricted grain-boundary migration
in the as-HIPed high-carbon materials. Their dissolution during solution annealing can reduce this
pinning effect and permit grain growth, particularly in the initially fine-grained LHC-03. The resulting
grain coarsening contributed to the reductions in YS and UTS after SA1. In contrast, grain boundary
migration in LHC-02 may have remained restricted by its comparatively high population of thermally
stable powder-derived oxides. These results suggest that carbon affected the tensile behavior primarily
through carbide evolution and its influence on grain-size stability rather than through direct carbon
strengthening. Elevated-temperature tensile testing is ongoing, and the resulting data will be used to
evaluate the effects of powder feedstock and chemistry on elevated-temperature tensile behavior.

 3.4. Creep Behavior
 Figure 11 presents the creep strain-time responses of PM-HIP 316H specimens tested at 650°C and
160 MPa. In this study, an as-HIPed creep specimen from the previously fabricated small HIP can (SHC,
65 mm x 100 mm) [10] was also tested and included in Figure 11. Detailed microstructure of the SHC can
be found in [10]. The LHC-01 specimen was machined from a LHC (65 mm x 150 mm) produced from
the same Sandvik powder and was subjected to SA1 before creep testing. As shown in Figure 11, the as-
HIPed SHC specimen exhibited the longest rupture life and greatest creep ductility, reaching a creep life
of 511 hours and creep strain of 46.2% before fracture. Although more creep data points are needed, the
creep testing results indicated that SA at 1150°C reduced both creep life and rupture ductility. The creep
curve of SHC SA1 remained similar to that of SHC As-HIPed specimen from the beginning of the test to
~350 hours, but the tertiary creep began earlier in the SA1 specimen. This behavior indicated that the heat
treatment had a greater effect on damage accumulation and strain localization. As reported earlier [10],
increasing the SA temperature to 1300°C (SA2) produced a much larger degradation in creep
performance. The observed reduction in rupture life after SA indicates that heat-treatment-induced
changes in the powder-derived oxide population were more influential than the potential benefit of grain
growth. After heat treatment, thermodynamically stable oxides remained and accumulated due to the
grain boundary migration. Larger oxide particles can generate local stress concentrations and provide
preferential sites for oxide-matrix decohesion and creep-cavity nucleation [10]. Subsequent cavity growth

 14

and coalescence can promote early tertiary creep and reduce rupture ductility, consistent with the short
life and low strain of the SA specimen. The root cause of accelerated creep damage was discussed in [10].
 As shown in Figure 11, the comparison between the SHC-SA1 and LHC-01-SA1 specimens, which
were produced from the same Sandvik virgin powder and subjected to the same SA treatment, revealed
similar creep strain but slightly different creep life. The LHC-01 SA1 specimen revealed reduced creep
life compared to SHC SA1. LHC-01 SA1 had a grain size 15.1 ± 9.7 µm which is similar to SHC-SA1
(12.6 ± 8.6 µm). Because only one specimen was tested from each billet, the observed difference may fall
within the normal statistical scatter of creep-rupture behavior and cannot be attributed conclusively to
HIP-can size. Creep testing on LHC-02 and LHC-03 specimens was in progress during the preparation of
this report. Therefore, the present results establish a baseline for Sandvik-powder billets, while ongoing
testing of LHC-02 and LHC-03 will expand the dataset for assessing feedstock-dependent creep behavior.

 4. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
 This study evaluated the effects of powder source, reuse history, and oxygen content on the
microstructure and mechanical behavior of PM-HIP Type 316/316H stainless steel. Three feedstocks with
oxygen content of 0.028 wt.%, 0.034 wt.%, and 0.047 Wt%. were investigated. The conclusions were
summarized below.
1. All three gas-atomized powders contained surface oxide layers enriched in O, Si, and Mn. The virgin
 Sandvik powder exhibited a thin (<10 nm) and relatively continuous oxide film, whereas the recycled
 PAC powder showed a rougher and more developed surface layer. The Praxair powder contained a
 more spatially heterogeneous oxide distribution with a much thicker oxide layer of ~60 nm.
2. The as-HIPed billets exhibited distinct grain structures despite being consolidated in a single HIP
 cycle. The average grain sizes were 10.8 ± 8.7 µm for LHC-01, 16.7 ± 12.3 µm for LHC-02, and 7.2
 ± 6.4 µm for LHC-03. Grain size did not vary monotonically with feedstock oxygen content,

 15

 indicating that grain development was influenced by the combined effects of powder microstructure,
 particle-size distribution, carbon concentrations, and the size and spatial distribution of oxides.
3. Following SA at 1150°C, the average grain size increased by ~40% in LHC-01 and ~74% in LHC-03
 but remained unchanged in LHC-02. The initially fine-grained LHC-03 had the greatest driving force
 for grain growth, and dissolution of soluble carbides during SA may have reduced particle pinning.
 SA did not eliminate the fine oxides in the microstructure but increased the size of the oxides due to
 grain boundary migration.
4. Powder-feedstock differences had only a limited effect on the RT tensile properties of the as-HIPed
 billets. All three billets exceeded the ASME minimum RT requirements. SA reduced the YS and UTS
 of all three billets. All solution-annealed materials continued to satisfy the applicable ASME tensile
 property requirements.
5. Creep testing demonstrated that increasing the SA temperature progressively reduced rupture life and
 ductility. The degradation following SA indicated that the redistribution or coarsening of stable
 powder-derived oxides had promoted oxide-matrix decohesion, cavity nucleation, and early
 localization of creep damage. The LHC-01 SA1 specimen exhibited 366 hours of rupture life and
 26.4% rupture strain. Creep testing on LHC-02 and LHC-03 specimens was in progress to evaluate
 the effect of different powder feedstocks on creep properties.
 Future work will focus on completing creep testing of LHC-02 and LHC-03. Post-creep
characterization will include fractographic examination and analysis of longitudinal specimen sections to
identify cavity-nucleation sites, crack-propagation paths, and dominant fracture modes. These damage
characteristics will be correlated with the microstructure and chemistry of the corresponding powder
feedstocks. The combined powder-characterization, microstructural, tensile, and creep datasets will be
used to establish quantitative powder-process-microstructure-property relationships. Future studies will
also investigate hydrogen treatment as a powder rejuvenation strategy to reduce or modify surface oxides
before consolidation. The treated powders will be consolidated by PM-HIP, and the resulting billets will
be evaluated through tensile, creep, and creep-fatigue testing. These studies will validate the hypothesis
that powder rejuvenation combined with optimized post-HIP heat treatment can suppress oxide-assisted
creep damage and improve the long-term performance of PM-HIP 316H, thereby supporting code
qualification and eventual deployment of PM-HIP technology.
