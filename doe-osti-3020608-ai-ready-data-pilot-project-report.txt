 Abstract
 The proliferation of artificial intelligence in scientific research has created an urgent need to
 define "AI-ready data" for researchers and, more importantly, provide resources to help them
 produce AI-ready data. At Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, we conducted a pilot study
 with three data scientists evaluating three CSV datasets from different scientific domains,
 followed by semi-structured interviews capturing assessment practices. Our findings reveal that
 AI-readiness evaluation is intuition-based, with practitioners asking "How fast can I go from raw
 data to my machine learning pipeline?" Data scientists consistently prioritized workflow
 efficiency, human interpretability, and quality stewardship signals. From these insights, we
 developed a practical evaluation framework comprising data requirements, metadata standards,
 and validation tests that provides actionable criteria for producing and curating AI-ready
 datasets, addressing the gap between theoretical understanding and practical implementation.

 1.0 Introduction
 The increase in artificial intelligence applications across scientific research has left a vacuum
 when it comes to defining what constitutes "AI-ready data." While best practices abound, there
 is no standardized definition or evaluation framework to validate these claims. This ambiguity
 creates significant challenges for researchers seeking to publish data that is multi-purpose –
 supporting their scientific claims while also acting as a source for AI/ML applications.
 At Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL), this challenge is particularly present given the
 laboratory's diverse research portfolio spanning multiple scientific domains. While the lab has a
 goal to publish more AI-ready data, we don’t have a working definition of what that would look
 like. Researchers regularly publish datasets with varying levels of documentation, formatting,
 and structural consistency, which would lead to substantial time investments in data preparation
 and validation before any AI work can begin.
 The Data Stewardship Subcommittee recognized that while theoretical frameworks for AI-ready
 data exist, there was a critical gap between theory and practice. Domain experts and AI
 practitioners may have different perspectives on what makes data truly "AI-ready," and these
 differences needed to be understood and reconciled to develop practical, actionable guidance
 for the laboratory's research community.
 Additionally, the absence of standardized evaluation criteria creates inefficiencies in data
 sharing and reuse. Curators have no standard to rely on when evaluating data, and what AI-
 ready means for one project or dataset may not be applicable to another dataset.

 2.0 Project Goals
 This pilot project was designed to address the theory-practice gap in AI-ready data definition
 through direct engagement with practicing data scientists. The primary objectives were
 structured around four key areas:
 Validate Theoretical Framework Through Practice
 By engaging practicing data scientists in actual dataset evaluation, the project aimed to test
 what characteristics aligned with practical requirements and identify any gaps or misalignments
 between theory and practice.
 Develop Standardized Evaluation Process
 We also wanted to create a practical, standardized evaluation process that could be used by
 researchers and data curators across PNNL. This process needed to be accessible to
 individuals who may not be AI experts but are responsible for preparing or curating datasets.
 The evaluation framework would serve as both a checklist for data producers and a validation
 tool for data consumers.
 Inform Tool and Resource Development
 Understanding the practical challenges faced by data scientists when evaluating AI-readiness
 could inform the development of tools and resources for both data producers and consumers.
 This included identifying opportunities for automation, determining documentation requirements,
 and understanding workflow needs that could streamline the data preparation process.
 Enable Data Catalog Enhancement
 The project also aimed to establish criteria for labeling datasets as "AI-ready" within PNNL's
 data catalog, Atlas. Datasets that met the criteria could have a badge labeling them as AI-ready
 attached, enabling other researchers to efficiently identify and reuse data for their own AI
 processes that came with a predetermined base level of quality.
 By achieving these objectives, the project would contribute to the lab-wide promotion of AI-
 ready data products as a new and important scientific output at PNNL, ultimately enhancing the
 laboratory's capacity for AI-driven scientific research and improving the efficiency of data
 sharing and reuse.

 3.0 Methodology
 Participant Selection

 For the pilot project, we recruited three data scientists at different career stages and levels of AI
 expertise. The variation in experience levels was needed to investigate whether AI-readiness
 assessment approaches differed based on experience or whether standardized evaluation could
 work for different skill levels.

 All participants had hands-on experience with AI applications, so they could provide informed
 assessments based on actual implementation challenges rather than theoretical knowledge
 alone.

 Participants were also selected based on their availability during the project timeline. To prevent
 bias, participants were informed about the general project objectives but were not provided with
 details about the specific evaluation tasks.

 Dataset Selection

 The dataset selection strategy was designed to include datasets with suspected varying levels
 of AI-readiness. This approach enabled review of not only what qualities made a well-prepared
 AI ready dataset, but also what red flags arose from poorly prepared data.

 For ease of analysis, the datasets were all the same format – CSVs. Other than that, the
 characteristics of the datasets differed. The file structures varied between datasets, as did the
 amount of documentation. The datasets were also all from different domains. This diversity was
 essential for understanding how format and structural characteristics influence AI-readiness
 assessment. All datasets provided to the data scientists are available publicly:
       • https://doi.org/10.15485/1960313
       • https://doi.org/10.25584/2229733
 • https://data.pnnl.gov/group/nodes/dataset/33553

 Evaluation Process

 Participants were intentionally provided with minimal upfront guidance about evaluation criteria
 or expected outcomes. They were given eight hours to review all three datasets. This approach
 was designed to capture authentic, unbiased assessment practices. The goal was to
 understand how practitioners naturally approach AI-readiness evaluation rather than how they
 might respond to prescribed criteria.

 Interview Structure and Protocol

 Following their independent evaluation, each participant engaged in a semi-structured interview
 lasting approximately 60-90 minutes. The interview included standardized questions designed to
 elicit consistent information across participants while allowing flexibility for domain-specific
 insights and unexpected observations. A full list of questions can be found in the appendix. Key
 question areas included:

 4.0 Findings
 Our interviews revealed that AI-readiness assessment is more gut-feeling than checklist-driven,
 with researchers relying on their experience rather than formal evaluation criteria. Participants
 approached datasets asking themselves: "How fast can I go from raw data to my machine
 learning pipeline?" This practical mindset dominated their evaluations—they weren't looking to
 tick boxes but rather to spot anything that would bog down their workflow.
 Adequate context and trustworthy data emerged as prerequisites for AI applications. If there is
 enough information contained within the data (headers, measurements) and documentation
 (methods, readmes, provenance), a user can make a solid judgement call about using the data
 or not. Our interviewees kept coming back to the need for domain experts, even when datasets
 looked technically solid, suggesting that AI-readiness isn't binary but exists on a sliding scale
 depending on how much specialized knowledge you need. On the other hand, if data contains
 questionable null values, inconsistencies, and no information on previous data transformations,
 trust in the data is eroded.
 Human understanding emerged as a prerequisite for AI applications. As one participant noted:
 "…if I don't know what it means, maybe the AI does. But then how do I know the AI is giving me
 correct answers…?" his really drives home that AI-ready data isn't just about making machines
 happy—humans need to understand it too. Even if the machine can understand the technical
 formatting, human understanding is valued to bolster trust. Our interviewees kept coming back
 to the need for domain experts, even when datasets looked technically solid, suggesting that AI-
 readiness isn't binary but exists on a sliding scale depending on how much specialized
 knowledge you need.
 Documentation emerged as a classic "Goldilocks problem"—researchers wanted information
 that was just right. Too little left them guessing about what they were looking at, while too much
 had them drowning in background details when they just needed the essentials. But
 documentation wasn't just about information transfer; it was also a quality signal. Clean, well-
 organized docs suggested someone had taken care with the data, while messy documentation
 raised red flags about whether the underlying data could be trusted.
 These quality signals went beyond just the written documentation. Consistent naming
 conventions suggested systematic data management, clear file organization implied careful
 curation, and comprehensive but focused documentation indicated purposeful preparation for
 reuse. These stewardship signals shaped how confident researchers felt about using the data
 for AI, showing that evaluation isn't just technical—it's also about assessing whether someone
 cared enough to do things properly.

 5.0 Limitations and Future Work
 Study Limitations
 This pilot study provides valuable initial insights, but several limitations constrain the scope and
 generalizability of our findings. Working with only three researchers evaluating three datasets
 limits our ability to capture the full range of evaluation approaches across different practitioner
 backgrounds and experience levels. While we successfully tested our theoretical framework in
 practice, the small sample size means some evaluation patterns or criteria may not have
 emerged that would be apparent in a larger study.
 Our focus on tabular data represents only one subset of data types commonly used in AI
 applications. Scientific research increasingly involves images, time series, text, and multi-modal
 datasets that likely have different AI-readiness requirements. The evaluation criteria we
 identified may not translate directly to these other data formats, limiting the broader applicability
 of our findings.
 The PNNL context also affects how broadly these results can be applied. The scientific domains
 represented in our dataset selection reflect PNNL's research portfolio and may not encompass
 the full range of challenges encountered elsewhere.
 Areas for Further Research
 Future work should go beyond tabular data to investigate AI-readiness for images, video, audio,
 and text. Each data type likely has unique technical requirements and quality assessment
 challenges that would require different evaluation approaches and tools.
 Another interesting avenue of research would be to implement and test more datasets with the
 AI-ready data checklist. Using the checklist, we could select or curate datasets that would
 supposedly be “AI-ready”, then have computer scientists assess or use the datasets in AI
 workflows.
 It would also be interesting to apply these AI-ready criteria to a broader range of scientific
 domains to understand how disciplinary context influences evaluation criteria. Fields like
 astronomy, social sciences, and clinical research may have fundamentally different data
 characteristics and quality standards that would expand or modify our current framework.

Limitations and Future Work 6

 6.0 Conclusion
 This pilot project revealed that no standard evaluation currently exists to evaluate data for AI-
 readiness. However, there are commonalities in how practitioners assess datasets that can form
 the basis for practical evaluation criteria. Data scientists consistently prioritized workflow
 efficiency, human interpretability, and quality stewardship signals.

 Our proposed framework addresses the current gap between theoretical understanding and
 practical implementation by translating practitioner insights into actionable criteria. This starter
 checklist can serve as both a preparation guide for data producers and an evaluation tool for
 data consumers, providing PNNL researchers with clear standards for what constitutes AI-ready
 data.

 The path forward involves testing and refining this checklist with additional researchers and
 datasets to ensure it reliably identifies truly AI-ready data while remaining practical for everyday
 use across PNNL's diverse research portfolio.

 Appendix A – AI-ready data checklist items
 This checklist was created by Juliane Schneider and Shannon Sheridan, who analyzed the
 scripts of the interviews with the data scientists, and compiled their observations of what they
 think AI-ready data is. This analysis was, in part, done with the help of AI.

 Data Requirements
 • File Format
 – CSV or similar text-based tabular format
 – UTF-8 encoding
 – Consistent delimiter usage
 – Refrain from nested formatting structure
 – The data files can be easily opened
 • Column Structure
 – Descriptive column headers (no "Column A", "Column B")
 – Headers in first row (not buried in data)
 – One data type per column
 – Consistent units within each column
 – Relationships between columns are explicit
 • Data Quality
 – Missing values clearly marked (e.g., "NaN", "NULL", or empty)
 – No mixed units in same column (all Celsius OR all Fahrenheit)
 – No embedded calculations or formulas
 – Consistent data formatting within columns
 – If the data is located in more than one file, column headers and values are consistent
 • Identifiers
 – Unique identifier column for each row/sample
 – Clear relationship between related data files

 Required Metadata
 • Data Dictionary
 – Definition for each column
 – Units of measurement specified
 – Data type (numerical, categorical, etc.)
 – Valid ranges or categories listed

Appendix A A.1

 • Dataset Overview
 – Brief description of what the data represents
 – Collection methodology summary
 – Sample size and scope
 – Any known limitations or biases
 – Representative set of the data for initial evaluation
 • Usage Information
 – Suggested input/output variables for AI tasks
 – Domain context that affects interpretation
 – Any preprocessing already applied
 • Ready-to-Use Structure
 – Data can be loaded directly into pandas/similar tools
 – No additional parsing required
 – Clear separation of features and targets (if applicable)
 • Sufficient Volume
 – Dataset size appropriate for intended AI applications
 – Balanced representation (if classification data)
 • Scientific Context
 – Variable relationships explained
 – Measurement conditions documented
 – Quality control procedures noted
 – Provenance recorded
 • Interpretability
 – Column names meaningful to domain experts
 – Units and scales familiar to field
 – Cross-references to relevant standards/protocols

 Quick Validation Tests
 • Technical Test: Can the file be loaded into a standard data analysis tool without errors?
 • AI Test: Can a simple AI task be performed (e.g., "find the most common value in column
 X")?
 • Domain Test: Can a domain scientist understand what each variable represents without
 external documentation?

 Red Flags (Automatic Disqualifiers)

Appendix A A.2

 • Binary or proprietary file formats
 • Mixed data types in single columns
 • Missing or unclear column definitions
 • Inconsistent units within columns
 • No unique identifiers for samples

 Appendix B – Interview Questions
 1. Walk me through the steps you used to evaluate the datasets for AI readiness.
 2. What criteria did you actually use to assess AI readiness, and what were the triggers you
 noted that determined the dataset was lacking readiness?
 3. Where did you spend the most time and why?
 4. How did your approach change as you worked through the datasets?
 5. What would make this evaluation process better/faster/more reliable?
 6. Based on this experience, what could a standardized evaluation process (or set of
 characteristics/framework) look like?
 7. After this exercise, what does “AI-ready data” mean to you? What characteristics do you
 expect of a dataset labeled "AI-ready"?
 8. Knowing what you know now, how would you design/create AI-ready data?
