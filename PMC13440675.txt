Alzheimer neuropathological change, α-synuclein and transactive response DNA-binding protein of 43 kDa expression in children and young adult brains from forensic autopsies: Value for early measurement of abnormal protein expression prevalence in neurodegenerative diseases
Authors: Lilian Calderón-Garcidueñas, Ozkan Ufuk Nalbantoglu, Angélica González-Maciel, Ricardo Torres-Jardón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer disease (AD) neuropathologic change (ADNPC), Parkinson disease (PD) α-synuclein (α-Syn), and TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) pathology overlap in a continuum in fine particulate matter (PM2.5)-exposed Metropolitan Mexico City (MMC) children and young adult forensic autopsy brains. This report focuses on a forensic targeted immunohistochemistry protocol to assess ADNPC, α-Syn and TDP-43 in ≤40y subjects, and to define their relationship with cumulative PM2.5 (CPM) exposures. We proposed an early measurement of abnormal protein expression to evaluate neurodegenerative disease prevalence in exposed PM2.5 urban young populations. We studied 189 autopsies average age 26±10y, including 179 MMC ≤40y olds and 10 low pollution controls. Among MMC adults 18-40y, 11.3% exhibited ADNPC alone; 50% had ADNPC + PD, 32.0% had ADNPC + PD + TDP-43 and 6.7% had ADNPC + TDP-43 pathology. In 37 children (13.0±4.8y), 24.3% had ADNPC, 37.8% had ADNPC + PD, 32.4% had ADNPC + PD + TDP-43; 5.4% had ADNPC + TDP-43 pathology. The overlapping children’s neuropathology was documented under low CPM. We suggest that measurements of abnormal protein expression to evaluate neurodegenerative disease in young PM2.5-exposed young urban populations in US autopsies will define the prevalence and overlap of early neurodegenerative biological markers. This information guide preventive medicine, health services, environmental PM2.5 emission control and early neuroprotection from potentially preventable air pollution-associated neurodegenerative diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer disease (AD) neuropathologic change (ADNPC), Parkinson disease (PD), frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) are defined by their biology and neuropathology. ADNPC, PD, FTLD, and/or ALS exist in a continuum and they overlap.1–6 Overlapping neuropathology is a major driver of heterogeneity in the clinical presentation and extent of neurodegeneration in AD patients.7 The description of AD hallmarks in children is not new; Braak et al described them in 2011.8

We have reported the development of AD, PD, FTLD, and ALS neuropathology in Metropolitan Mexico City (MMC) pediatric and young adult forensic autopsies and emphasized that their early neuropathology overlap is not different from that of US elderly cognitively unimpaired and impaired subjects.1 Moreover, the neuropathologic burden leads to the appearance and progression of impairment of cognitive function, (along with cortical first parietal and fronto-parietal, precentral gyrus, hippocampi, basal ganglia, thalamus, amygdala and cerebellar atrophy), and disturbances of olfaction, audition, sleep, gait and balance in the absence of comorbidities or known risk factors.9–20 MMC children exhibit more numerous MRI white matter hyperintense lesions with enlargement of perivascular spaces, spectroscopic abnormal brain metabolites, low CSF concentrations of Aβ1 to 42 and brain-derived neurotrophic factor than low pollution controls.9–11,16 We have identified ADNPC, hyperphosphorylated tau Braak staging acceleration, a high risk of suicide and IQ decreases if they carry one Apolipoprotein E 4 allele.8–11,14,16 Pathophysiologic mechanisms involved very early in the disease processes include damage to the neurovascular unit, oxidative stress and neuroinflammation correlating with PM2.5, ultrafine particulate matter (UFPM) and magnetic nanoparticle (NP) exposure.20–26

MMC is a megacity with unrestrained urban growth and complex, severe environmental pollution.27–31 Key to this work is the NP distribution in MMC: Caudillo et al described three NPs primary emission modes during morning peak traffic hours along a secondary mode coinciding with the highest photochemical activity.31 MMC residents walking along high traffic avenues are exposed to NPs 49 ± 20 nm, composed of ∼60% Fe, Pb, and Zn. The MMC subway represents a serious health hazard with PM2.5 exposures 34 to 93 μg/m−3, including NP number concentrations up to 50 300 ± 10 600 (# cm−3) with an average size of 38 ± 16 nm, predominantly composed of Fe, Cu, Ni, Cr, and Mn.26,28–31

Exposure to PM2.5 in MMC has been associated with cardio- and neurovascular mortality along with non-accidental, broad-category mortality outcomes including digestive and genitourinary diseases.32,33

We have three aims for this work, based on a population exposed to PM2.5 concentrations above the current US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) annual standard (9 µg/m3) for decades. The aims are: (1) to report the prevalence of neurodegenerative disease hallmarks of AD, PD, and TDP-43 in MMC cases using a short protocol focused on forensic ≤40-year autopsies; (2) to measure the cumulative concentrations of PM2.5 (CPM2.5) above the US EPA annual standard for each individual and to establish a relationship with age and the neuropathology. The key question is to determine whether there is a CPM threshold for the development of the biological markers of fatal neurodegenerative diseases in MMC populations, and (3) to propose a short forensic neuropathology protocol targeting cortical and subcortical regions exhibiting abnormal proteins (p-Tau, Aβ42, α-Syn, TDP-43).

The MMC results are based on the principle that AD is defined by its biology, making it clear we need to establish the steps for early diagnosis in exposed megacity residents. The current revised criteria for diagnosis and staging of Alzheimer disease by the Alzheimer’s Association Workgroup (NIA-AA framework) is not applicable to populations with biological AD plus overlapping pathologies in pediatric and young adulthood subjects.34

Neuropathologists across the world need to be aware that living in a polluted environment is strongly associated with early AD, PD, FTLD, and ALS biological diseases and more importantly, fatal neurodegenerative diseases associated with air pollution ought to be preventable. Walking outdoors for AD is not recommended in polluted environments.28–31,35,36

The “State of the Air” 2025 American Lung Association report37 finds 46% of Americans—156.1 million people—are living in places that get failing grades for unhealthy levels of ozone or particle pollution.37 Nearly 25 million more people are breathing unhealthy air compared to the 2024 report, and more than in any other “State of the Air” report in the last decade. In the USA, nervous system disorders, including conditions with the greatest collective disability ie, AD and other dementias, affected 180.3/332.7 million in 2021.38 We propose an exploratory research study on forensic autopsies to define the prevalence and overlap of neurodegenerative biological hallmarks across the US.

METHODS

Study cities and air quality

Metropolitan Mexico City (MMC) has >24 million residents involuntarily exposed to outdoor atmospheric pollutants; its public transportation system is a well-known source of PM2.5 and NP high emissions.27–33 MMC ferrimagnetic minerals in road dust contain heavy metals including Cr, Zn, Cu, and Pb.36 Control cities had criteria pollutants below current US EPA standard, including PM2.5.

Brain samples

In this study of MMC children and adult forensic 179 autopsies ages 11 months to 40 years, we also included 10 residents from cities representing typical provincial areas (Hermosillo, Veracruz) with air pollution emissions below current standards. All cases were included consecutively in this forensic study if their sudden death was attributed to accidents, homicides and suicides and did not involve the brain and if there were no significant extra neural pathologies in the general autopsy (Table 1). We also had access to the general autopsy samples and examined targeted organs (lungs, heart, liver, pancreas, stomach, small bowel, kidneys).

The forensic neuropathology protocol (Table S1) was aimed to study individuals ≤40 years. Samples included the superior and middle frontal cortices (Brodmann Areas 9/46); primary motor cortex (Brodmann 4), mid-uncal level through medial temporal lobe including hippocampal formation, parahippocampal gyrus and transentorhinal region. The brainstem was sectioned from the midbrain at the level of the superior colliculi to cervical level one. Brainstem motor nuclei of cranial nerves XII-X, VII, V, spinal cord α-motoneurons and sections of the midbrain at the level of the substantia nigra and red nucleus and the upper dorsolateral pons at the level of the locus coeruleus were examined.39–41

Brain tissues were blindly investigated to assess AD, PD, and TDP-43 pathology by a US Board certified neuropathologist. Early subcortical and cortical lesions, the amyloid-β protein phases, p-Tau, alpha-synuclein and TDP-43 pathology were investigated.8,9,38–46 Neuropathology evaluation (Table S1) included the identification of hyperphosphorylated tau cortical and subcortical: 0 = absent, 1 = pretangle stages a-c, 2 = pretangle stages 1a, 1 b, 3 = NFT stages I-II, 4 = NFT stages III-IV, 5 = NFT stages V-VI; β-amyloid phases43 0 = absent, 1 = neocortex, 2 = allocortex, limbic areas, 3 = diencephalon, basal ganglia, 4 = brainstem, midbrain; α-synuclein41,42 0 = negative, 1 = positive, α synuclein aggregates in the somatodendritic compartment, Lewy neurites, Lewy bodies, and TDP-4339,40 0 = negative, 1 = positive, dash-like IR particles in the vicinity of the cell nucleus, with or without complete loss of nuclear TDP-43 expression, somatic skein inclusions and glial pathology.

The main purpose of the AD, PD, and TDP-43 groups was to characterize them based on the aberrant proteins (biological diagnosis) in the selected brain regions and to emphasize key early lesions in young individuals. Likewise, overlapping proteinopathies are important at early stages because a similar overlapping pattern is seen 6 decades later.1

Statistical analysis

Our sample comprised 179 MMC brains and 10 low-pollution controls, with sample size determined a priori by autopsy availability and logistics over a 5-year period.22 We summarized continuous variables (eg, age and CPM) as mean ± SD and categorical variables (aberrant protein IHC and age bands) as counts and percentages.

To assess the relationship between CPM and age, we calculated Pearson product–moment correlation coefficients (r) with 2-sided P-values and 95% confidence intervals for the overall MMC cohort and stratified by age group (children ≤17 years vs adults ≥18 years). Differences in CPM across aberrant proteins associated with neurodegenerative diseases (AD alone, AD+PD, AD+TDP, AD+PD+TDP) while adjusting for age were evaluated using linear regression/ANCOVA models of the form CPM ∼ age + aberrant protein group with robust (heteroskedasticity-consistent) standard errors.

To explore potential non-linear and distribution-level patterns in CPM versus age, we generated scatterplots with locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) and fitted quantile regression models of CPM on age at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles (q = 0.25, 0.50, 0.75). All tests were 2-sided and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and graphics were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), Python (pandas, SciPy, statsmodels), and Microsoft Excel.

RESULTS

Air pollution

Metropolitan Mexico City is a prime example of uncontrolled urban growth and unsuccessfully controlled environmental pollution for the last 3 decades.27–33 MMC residents have been continually exposed to PM2.5 above 9 μg/m3 and to nanoparticles starting in utero.30,31 Figure 1 illustrates the sustained high concentrations of PM2.5 above the US EPA annual standard for MMC residents in the last 2 decades. The CPMs were estimated based on several criteria: (1) the residence of the deceased subject, (2) the age of the subject plus 9 months pregnancy, (3) the CPM obtained from the closest air quality monitoring station to the residency of each subject considering the age. The CPM was calculated as the sum of the annual average PM2.5 concentration in excess for each monitoring station to the PM2.5 annual average US EPA air quality standard along the life of each subject. CPM is included for each case in Table S2.

Brain in MMC versus controls

Autopsies were performed 4.1 ± 1.7 h after death and samples were collected weekdays, weekends and holidays during the 5-year study period. Macro- and microscopic examination of extra-neural organs was unremarkable. Brains were examined macroscopically; vascular and cortical examination was extensive; and brain sections were selected based on pertinent literature39–46 using a protocol work-up that included olfactory bulb, brainstem, cortical and subcortical regions.

Abnormal proteins were identified with IHC (Table S2) starting as early as 11 months and covering the spectrum of AD, PD, and TDP-43 neuropathological markers with the control cases being mostly negative (2 females ages 45 and 68 displayed isolated p-Tau and amyloid plaques). DISCUSSION

There is serious concern about the increased incidence and prevalence of fatal neurodegenerative diseases in our elderly populations and the fact that diseases such as AD are contributing to a significant health burden across the world.47–51 Most studies are focused on older adults and their comorbidities, which no doubt contribute to the complex neuropathology in affected subjects.47–55

MMC children are showing overlapping aberrant proteins in targeted brain areas with relatively low CPM values following the same exposure–accumulation trajectory seen later in adulthood. This observation is relevant for the identification of early cognition and olfactory deficits, auditory, gait and equilibrium abnormalities, fall risk, sleep disorders and brain MRI structural and volumetric alterations in MMC young seemingly healthy cohorts.9–13,15,17–21

It is clear to US neuropathologists and clinicians that we are facing a complex heterogeneity in AD. An overlap of aberrant proteins, race, ethnicity and socioeconomic status affect the risks for mild cognitive impairment and dementia.1,7,47,48,50,52 Latin American studies also document complex exposomes correlating with lower cognitive and functional performance, higher neuropsychiatric symptoms and brain structural and connectivity alterations in frontal-temporal-limbic and cerebellar regions. These exposomes include food insecurity, financial and socioeconomic status and access to healthcare.49

Early life conditions and exposures are relevant to cognition and risk of dementia.53–57 Cockell et al listed depression and learning problems during childhood associated with incident impairment, relative to normal cognition, while childhood headaches/migraines had 1.43 times higher risk of incidence of dementia in their follow-up of the US Health and Retirement Study (normal baseline cognition; n = 16 509; 2008 to 2018 waves).53 Aiello et al measured the Cardiovascular Risk Factors, Aging, and Incidence of Dementia (CAIDE) score (age, education, sex, systolic blood pressure, body mass index, cholesterol, physical activity and apolipoprotein E ε4 allele status), at age 28 with each 1-point increase in CAIDE was associated with a 0.03 standard deviation lower backward digit span score.54 They concluded that AD risk is linked to cognitive function as early as ages 24 to 44.54

Canning et al. acknowledged the probable effects of exposure early in life, with higher exposures to nitrogen dioxide, nitrogen oxides, and coarse particulate matter in midlife to older age associated with poorer cognition, processing speed, and brain structural outcomes.55 The Nun Study strengthened evidence linking epidemiological factors, cognitive function, and brain pathology.56

Our neuropathology findings in young MMC subjects with lifelong exposures to a myriad of neurotoxicants, including PM2.5, are not a surprise, particularly because the cognition and brain MRI findings are strongly statistically significant when compared to clean air controls.9,10,12,17,18

Our proposed protocol for the detection of abnormal proteins associated with fatal neurodegenerative diseases is focused on young individuals with sudden deaths.1–8,41–46 The MMC findings are key for raising awareness among clinicians and for informing public health and environmental authorities that the earliest biological markers of fatal neurodegenerative diseases start in pediatric ages and that environmental pollution is likely a key risk factor. MMC residents in industrial areas and/or those close to high traffic heavy vehicle roads are exposed to higher concentrations of PM2.5, diesel nanoparticles, street dust heavy metal pollution, magnetic NPs, and experience higher cause-specific mortality outcomes including hypertensive disease, acute ischemic heart disease, and hemorrhagic stroke.32,33 Violence, particularly homicides, is a serious public health concern in MMC that includes highly polluted municipalities from the Mexico State.36,58 Within states with higher violence indices, a heterogeneous profile of marginalization and poverty was identified by Lopez-Ortiz et al.58

In the US, residence in mobile homes is the most influential factor predicting AD prevalence in 19.9% of US counties; this is followed by normalized difference vegetation index (17.4%), physical inactivity (12.9%), households with no vehicle (11.3%), and particulate matter (10.4%).59 Of utmost importance, as Aiello et al have shown, the key risk factors for AD are linked to cognitive function as early as ages 24 to 44, highlighting the need for early prevention in the US.54 Wong et al documented self-reported cognitive disability increasing across the US, with age, race, socioeconomic status and regional disparities playing key roles.60

It is important for MMC health workers to identify subjects at higher risk and to neuroprotect them. Regarding early screening for AD, Chatanaka et al recently concluded that, “… After careful consideration, and despite the recent significant biological, diagnostic and therapeutic advances for AD, screening does not seem to be justified at present, due to numerous reasons, such as lack of trained professionals and specialized clinics to handle the anticipated highly increased workload, the huge cost, the ineffectiveness and side effects of current therapy, the lack of long-term therapy studies, and the disagreement among experts as to whom to test and treat and when (at either asymptomatic or early symptomatic stages).”61

We believe that this assertion is invalid for MMC residents. MMC residents have been chronically exposed to sustained concentrations of PM2.5 for decades; we know the emission sources, and how to control emissions. Thus, all neurodegeneration in progress in potentially 24 million residents can be halted and avoided if we control anthropogenic combustion emissions, UFPM/NPs in ambient air and their industrial sources. High quality education, good nutrition, access to health care, preventive medicine and environmental management are important considerations if we are to prevent adverse effects in early pregnancies and all through neurodevelopmental stages.59,62 Fe, Ti, and Al alloys, Hg, Cu, Ca, Sn, and Si nanoparticles in maternal and fetal placenta compartments and brain strongly support the placental barrier does not limit the access of environmental NPs at any stage in pregnancy in exposed PM2.5 mothers, with all neurological consequences in prenatal and postnatal health.24

We need non-invasive, affordable, patient-friendly, easy, quick, and robust biomarkers for early AD detection and we should not have to wait for the late stages of fatal neurodegenerative diseases to make a diagnosis, nor avoid what we are supposed to do as physicians ie, preventive medicine.63

In the US, we have the Consortium of Forensic Science Organizations (CFSO) listing the majority of forensic science labs in the USA.64 There are >400 forensic US science labs and we should be able to integrate brain examination into forensic cases under a neuropathology research protocol incorporating major neurodegenerative diseases that impact our US populations. We need a representative number of cases in cities and rural areas and we will be in the position as forensic pathologists and neuropathologists to evaluate the extent of the neurodegenerative early changes in our young populations. The information will be very useful for health federal authorities.

This MMC forensic neuropathology study has limitations. Most of our cases were male, as expected in large cities everywhere, and all were Mexican, limiting the generalizability to other regions and/or ethnic groups. The high aberrant protein heterogeneity and overlap in young populations, without comorbidities, reflects the complexity of the neurodegeneration pathomechanisms, the genetics of the population and the significant differences in pollutants’ profiles across the city and modifiable potential risk factors, all variables we could easily follow in the clinical setting in seemingly healthy populations.

Identifying the forensic prevalence of AD, PD, and TDP-43 aberrant proteins in children and young adults remains essential to guide clinical research, health service organization, and effective environmental control of PM2.5 and UFPM/NPs emissions.65 Understanding typical urban and rural pollution scenarios (including exposures to wild forest fires, wood and pellet burning, occupational high-risk jobs, etc.) may support healthcare professionals in educating the affected populations, informing local clinicians about the expected distribution of neurodegenerative diseases and offering a foundation for future research aiming to report forensic neuropathology across young ages, ethnic groups and causes of death. Breaking the silence is needed, forensic neuropathology is an accurate, early window to overlapping fatal neurodegenerative diseases.
