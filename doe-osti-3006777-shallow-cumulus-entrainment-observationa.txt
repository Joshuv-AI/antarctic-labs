 Executive summary

This document constitutes a final technical report for DE-SC0020083, a research grant
awarded by the US Department of Energy Office of Science, to Prof. Daniel Kirshbaum
at McGill University. The original research proposal, entitled “Shallow cumulus entrain-
ment: observational retrieval, physical interpretation, and climate impacts”, defined four
scientific objectives that synthesized ARM observations with turbulence-permitting large-
eddy simulations (LES) to quantify and understand the process of cumulus entrainment,
whereby a cumulus cloud ingests surrounding air through its lateral edges. These objec-
tives included developing observational climatologies of cumulus bulk entrainment rate at
two ARM observatories, physical interpretation of these findings using LES, quantification
of the impacts of subcloud circulations on entrainment, and the climate impacts of flow-
dependent entrainment variations. In the six years that have elapsed since the grant was
awarded (three funded years and three no-cost annual extensions), all four of the objectives
were successfully addressed. Four personnel were trained (one B.Sc., one M.Sc. to comple-
tion, five years of Ph.D. training, and 2.5 years of postdoctoral training), eight peer-reviewed
articles were published in high-impact journals, and 20 presentations were delivered (three
invited).

 Review of project activities

This document reports the scientific advancements made under the aforementioned DoE/ASR
grant. It is organized according to the four principal scientific objectives of the successful
grant proposal from 2019. The original project was funded for three years, but it was ex-
tended for an additional three years via three consecutive no-cost extensions.

1 Addressing the scientific objectives

1.1 Objective 1: Construct multiyear observational climatologies of shallow-cumulus en-
 trainment at two ARM observatories: the continental Southern Great Plains (SGP)
 site and the maritime Eastern North Atlantic (ENA) site

This objective was addressed by Kirshbaum and Lamer (2021), who presented observational
climatologies of nonprecipitating shallow-cumulus bulk entrainment rates () at the SGP
and ENA sites over a four-year period. Two different methods were used to retrieve  using
ground-based remote data, one developed by Jensen and Del Genio (2006) and the other by
my group at McGill (Drueke et al., 2019). The former uses a combination of thermodynamic
profiles (from radiosonde and AERI) and Ka-band radar measurements of cloud-top height
to estimate a bulk  for each cloud, and the latter uses a theoretical scaling of the turbulent-
kinetic-energy (TKE) budget to estimate a bulk  for a mesoscale cloud ensemble. The latter,
termed the “TKE method”, requires retrievals of cloud-base mass flux, convective available
potential energy (CAPE), and cloud-layer depth, which were obtained using a combination
of Doppler Lidar, radiosonde, AERI, Raman Lidar, and Ka-band cloud radar.
Values of  were estimated for thousands of clouds at each site. To determine the physical
parameters regulating and/or co-varying with , various environmental and cloud-related pa-
rameters were also estimated for each cloud or cloud ensemble. These parameters included,

 1

among others, cloud-layer humidity, sensible heat flux, boundary layer vertical velocity vari-
ance, cloud vertical velocity, cloud width, cloud depth, cloud base height, and cloud liquid
water path. Of these, the most prominent sensitivity of  was the location (continental vs
maritime). On average, both retrievals showed a near-doubling of  at the maritime ENA
site compared to the continental SGP site (Fig. 1). This study marks the first observational
evidence of a robust land–ocean contrast in , which has major implications for climate
modelling and prediction. Interestingly, a similarly contrast had been used in versions of
the Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory (GFLD) climate model (Zhao et al., 2009), but
with no physical justification—it simply served to improve precipitation verification against
observations. Thus, our study provides a retroactive observational justification for parameter
choices that have improved climate-model performance.

Among various other significant correlations between  and the above-mentioned parameters,
a robust positive trend between  and cloud-layer relative humidity (RH) was found. This
finding directly contradicts the model inter-comparison study of Derbyshire et al. (2004),
which argued that  varies negatively with RH. This latter idea gained traction because larger
 tends to suppress cumuli (for all else being equal), which is nominally consistent with the
empirical finding that cumuli grow deeper in moister atmospheres. Its widespread adoption
in the community was reflected by Bechtold et al. (2008), who used this hypothesized inverse
-RH relation to parameterize  in the ECMWF global weather model (Bechtold et al.,
2008). Our finding, by contrast, emphasizes the importance of both  and the properties
of the entrained air. While increased  implies stronger dilution, the moister entrained air
induces far less buoyancy loss per unit of entrainment, which more than offsets the increased
. Furthermore,  was found to vary inversely with cloud-chord width, consistent with recent
findings from large-eddy simulations (e.g., Khairoutdinov and Randall, 2006; Rousseau-Rizzi
et al., 2017).

1.2 Physically interpret the observational sensitivities of cumulus entrainment found in
 Objective 1 using large-eddy simulations (LES)

This objective was addressed by the two-part study of Drueke et al. (2020) and Drueke et al.
(2021), who quantified  in systematic LES experiments of shallow cumulus convection. Al-
though these articles both appeared before the publication of Kirshbaum and Lamer (2021),
they were informed by the emerging findings from that observational analysis.
Drueke et al. (2020) examined the thermodynamic controls on simulated  in shallow cumu-
lus convection, using LES configurations from recent model inter-comparison studies. These
included two studies of diurnally forced cumuli at the SGP site (Brown et al., 2002; Vogel-
mann et al., 2012) and two studies of shallow convection over the Caribbean (Siebesma et al.,
2003; vanZanten et al., 2011). Four environmental parameters were varied, including conti-
nentality (land or ocean), cloud-layer relative humidity (RH), cloud-base height, and cloud
layer depth. While  was largely insensitive to the latter two parameters, it was strongly
sensitive to the other two, which were both identified in the aforementioned observations of
Kirshbaum and Lamer (2021).
The simulated sensitivity of  to continentality in Drueke et al. (2020), which was consistent
with the observational result in Fig. 1, was interpreted using the TKE-budget framework of
Grant and Brown (1999) and Grant and Lock (2004). Increased daytime sensible heat fluxes
over land energize boundary-layer turbulence and generate stronger cloud-base mass fluxes.
Although the total cloud entrainment flux increases in response, it cannot keep pace with
the increased mb because it is also constrained by CAPE. Thus, the stronger boundary-layer
forcing over land gives rise to more efficient cumulus convection with smaller , where a
larger fraction of available potential energy is converted to cloud kinetic energy.
The sensitivity of simulated  to RH was also consistent with the observations, with larger
RH associated with larger . This finding was interpreted using the buoyancy-sorting hy-
pothesis of Taylor and Baker (1991). In drier cloud layers, mixing between clouds and their
surroundings produce more negatively buoyant mixtures that are likely to be expelled from
the cloud, causing detrainment (δ) to greatly exceed entrainment (Fig. 2). As a result, the
clouds rapidly decay with height. In moister cloud layers, by contrast, the mixing produces
more positively buoyant mixtures that remain inside the cloud, causing  to increase and
δ to decrease, favoring enhanced cloud mass flux. Thus, consistent with the observational
conclusion reached in section 1.1, dilution is relatively strong in moist layers but the effects
on cloud buoyancy per unit of entrainment are greatly reduced, allowing the clouds to retain
more mass and vigor with height.
In the second part of this study, Drueke et al. (2021) examined the influence of the vertical
wind profile on . We conducted a suite of LES experiments that were similar to those
of Drueke et al. (2020) except for varying the wind profile rather than thermodynamic
aspects of the flow. Of particular interest were the impacts of the background wind speed
and vertical shear in both the cloud and subcloud layers. This work identified two factors
that empirically controlled  in the experiments: the length of time that buoyant cores
were exposed to environmental air within the cloud layer (the “core-exposure” effect) and
the humidity of the entrained air (the “cloud-shell dilution” effect). Whereas increases in
the former tended to enhance , increases in the latter tended to weaken . Both effects
varied inversely with the core updraft speed, but their offsetting effects did not cancel.

Increased cloud-layer vertical wind shear enhanced the core-exposure effect more than the
cloud-shell dilution effect, leading to increased . In contrast, increased mean wind speed
did the opposite and led to decreased . Ultimately, we showed that  could be adequately
parameterized based on a combination of the core updraft speed and the core-shell mixing
fraction (Fig. 3).

1.3 Objective 3: Quantify the impact of subcloud forcing on cloud-layer entrainment using
 ARM observations and LES

This objective was addressed through four publications, two numerical (Kirshbaum, 2022;
Kirshbaum et al., 2024) and the other two observational (Liu et al., 2023; Kirshbaum et al.,
2025). The former two studies evaluated the impacts of subcloud circulations on  and deep-
convection initiation (DCI). They both found that larger-scale subcloud circulations give
rise to wider and more intense cumulus clouds that are more likely to undergo deep ascent.
Kirshbaum (2022) investigated the response of  and DCI to variations in low-amplitude
surface terrain. The prescribed terrain variations followed different spectral slopes, ranging
from k 1 (uniformly distributed), to k −5/3 , to k −3 , where k is the horizontal wavenumber.

 4

series of LES shallow-cumulus experiments. The parameterization is a power-law relation using
only cloud-core vertical velocity and cloud-shell mixing fraction. From Drueke et al. (2021).

For more negative slopes, the energy was more concentrated in larger-scale variations at
the expense of the smaller scales. Using cloud-tracking analyses, we found that circulations
induced by larger-scale terrain initiated wider and longer-lived clouds than those forced
by smaller-scale terrain. These spatiotemporally larger updrafts exhibited smaller  and δ
(Fig. 4), larger buoyancies and vertical velocities, and less interference from terrain-induced
downdrafts during their life cycles. As a result, DCI occurred earlier and more vigorously
when forced by larger-scale terrain.

Figure 4: Conditionally averaged vertical profiles of  and δ over all cores in varying bins of cloud
cross-sectional area at the level of free convection (ALFC ), in a simulation with a k −3 terrain spectral
slope. The bin edges are shown in the legend (units: m2 ). From Kirshbaum (2022).

Kirshbaum et al. (2024) examined the role of cloud cross-sectional area on  and δ more
closely, performing a series of LES experiments that systematically varied the radius (R)
of an axisymmetric surface heat source. This surface-based heating gave rise to thermal
circulations that effectively initiated cumulus clouds. Consistent with other findings reported
above, both  and δ decreased with increasing R. However, various measures of cloud vigor,
including vertical velocity, buoyancy, liquid-water content, and mass flux also varied inversely

 5

with R, leaving cause and effect unclear. Did decreased  and δ, in response to increasing R,
explain the trends in the cloud-vigor metrics, or was the decrease in  and δ itself a response
to the variations in cloud vigor? Although a conclusive answer to this question was not
provided, sensitivity tests supported the notion that cloud width largely regulated  while
cloud vertical velocity and buoyancy largely regulated δ.
Another advancement in Kirshbaum et al. (2024) was the development of new, approximate
methods of estimated direct  and δ in LES. These quantities were traditionally inferred
in “bulk” from profiles of conserved variables inside clouds, but they made questionable
assumptions that limited their accuracy (e.g., Siebesma and Cuijpers, 1995). Romps (2010)
and Dawe and Austin (2011) developed new methods for estimating  and δ directly, which
broke new ground but also demanded major computational expense. We developed two
methods that accurately estimated direct  and δ with far less computational exigencies
than the existing direct methods. Thus, in addition to providing physical insight into the
controls on  and δ, this study developed methods that can benefit the cloud-modelling
community as a whole.
Onto the observation studies, Liu et al. (2023) performed a climatology of boundary-layer
convergence lines (CLs) at the SGP, using a combination of ARM instruments. The main
objective of this study was to understand why these convergence lines are particularly effec-
tive at deep-convection initiation (DCI), as has been shown by decades of observations (e.g.,
Wilson and Schreiber, 1986). Two methods were developed to retrieve convergence line prop-
erties, including their amplitude, width and depth. The first method, or the “radar method”
used vertical cross-sections of Doppler radial velocity from the C-SAPR2 radar in northern
OK along azimuths perpendicular to the convergence line. Under the 2D assumption in
the line-normal plane, the three properties were readily estimated. The second method, or
the “surface method”, used only surface observations from the SGP facility, combined with
information on squall-line orientation available from the C-SAPR2, to infer the line-normal
wind variations during CL passage. Both methods gave consistent estimates for common CL
properties over 60-70 CLs observed at each location (the C-SAPR2 location and the SGP
facility about 40 km to the south).
The findings of Liu et al. (2023) indicate that, in general, the CLs are slightly wider than the
corresponding boundary-layer depth (zi ) and slightly deeper than 0.5zi , the latter consistent
with an updraft that peaks in the middle boundary layer and decays to nearly zero at zi . Liu
et al. (2023) also used Doppler Lidar to quantify the vertical CL structure and compare CL
updrafts with turbulent boundary-layer updrafts preceding CL arrival. We found that the CL
updrafts are slightly (25-50%) wider and stronger than those in the “pre-CL” environment,
leading to a nearly 100% enhancement of updraft mass flux (Me ) in the middle portion of
the boundary layer (Fig. 5). These results suggest that CLs produce stronger forcing for
cumulus convection than ordinary turbulent clouds, but by a smaller margin than we had
hypothesized. Thus, the factors responsible for the tendency of DCI to initiate along CLs
was not fully clarified. Further research to provide a more definitive answer to this question
is ongoing.
The observational study of Kirshbaum et al. (2025) focused on the relationship between
subcloud forcing and squall-line maintenance at the ARM SGP observatory. It did not
address cumulus entrainment or detrainment specifically, leaving it slightly outside the scope
of this proposal. Nevertheless, it is relevant for its use of ARM observations to provide novel

or RKW88) to real-world squall lines. RKW88 postulated that a balance between baroclinic
vorticity generation by the system cold pool and environmental vorticity (owing to vertical
shear of the line-normal wind) favors more upright subcloud updrafts that promote stronger
and more sustained squall lines.
Kirshbaum et al. (2025) used ARM data to estimate two parameters central to RKW88:
the cold-pool intensity (c) and the line-normal shear (∆Vn ). The strongest systems are
expected to form in environments with c/∆Vn ≈ 1. AERI data was used to estimate c, and
soundings from ARM SGP and/or profiles from NCEP’s RAP model were used to estimate
∆Vn . To verify the theory, c/∆Vn was correlated with different measures of storm structure
and intensity, including subcloud tilts estimated from Doppler Lidar (Fig. 6) and cloud-
layer updraft tilts estimated from operational WSR-88D data, and the updraft strength and
solidity (also estimated from WSR-88D radar). These correlations suggested that the theory
of RKW has some predictive power in estimating updraft tilts and system solidity, but other
measures of storm intensity (maximum reflectivity and maximum height of 40 dBZ echoes)
were more closely related to traditional convective parameters like CAPE and CIN. Thus,
the theory should be interpreted as a model of storm intensity for a given thermodynamic
state, not as an absolute measure of system strength.

1.4 Objective 4: Evaluate the impacts of flow-dependent cumulus entrainment on large-scale
 climate patterns in global climate models

Objectives 1-2 both examined the land–ocean contrast in  and noted its potential climate
implications. To investigate these implications, we performed a set of global simulations of

the current climate with the GFDL model at 50-km grid spacing over a 21-year period. One
experiment (L100O100) used the same  over land and ocean, another (L75O100) reduced 
by 25% over land, and another (L75O75) reduced  by 25% over both land and ocean. The
effect of reduced  over land was quantified by comparing L75O100 against L100O100, which
showed averaged precipitation rates increasing by 1-2 mm day−1 (up to 40%) over tropical
land regions, particularly the Amazon rainforest (Figs. 7a-b). This increase stemmed from a
direct uptick in convective precipitation owing to the reduced . This increased precipitation
moistened the soil and increased surface latent-heat fluxes, which led to increased CAPE
in the equilibrium state. Thus, rather than more efficiently stabilizing the atmosphere,
reduced  over land tended to further destabilize land regions, representing a positive feedback
process. This enhanced deep convection over land modestly strengthened the Pacific Walker
circulation (by 3-4%) and also strengthened the Hadley circulation (by 4%) in the northern
hemisphere while reducing it by a similar amount in the southern hemisphere.
On the other hand, comparison of L75O75 with L75O100 indicates the impacts of reduced
 over the ocean. In this scenario, deep convection in the tropical Pacific Ocean shifted
eastward, with a reduction over the west Pacific warm pool and an increase in the central
Pacific (Fig. 7c). In this case, the reduced  over the central Pacific promoted an upstream
(relative to the easterly tropical flow) shift in the location of heavy oceanic precipitation.
The resulting upper-level heating gave rise to large-scale uplift in the central Pacific and
compensating descent downstream over the western Pacific. This modification projected
more strongly on the Walker circulation, inducing a 10-15% weakening. Also, in direct
contrast to the effects of reducing  over land, the Hadley circulation strengthened by 4% in
