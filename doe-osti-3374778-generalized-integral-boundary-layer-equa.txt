ABSTRACT
The momentum–energy integral technique (MEIT) is an advanced integral boundary-layer (IBL)
method for predicting wall heat transfer and skin friction on hypersonic vehicles. Presentations of
MEIT associated with legacy models are limited to planar or axisymmetric flows and geometries in
which boundary layer quantities of interest are solved for along individual streamlines as ordinary
differential equations. In this report, we derive a formulation of the core MEIT thickness and
streamline-metric equations in streamline curvilinear coordinates that is applicable to general three-
dimensional surface geometries, accommodates vortical inviscid flows and wall mass blowing, and
allows for an arbitrary definition of the edge state. The formulation includes a generalized streamline
metric that captures streamline convergence/divergence in the boundary layer and greatly improves
treatment of fully three-dimensional, non-axisymmetric surface flows. Finally, the curvilinear
system of equations are extended to a surface framework by rewriting streamwise derivatives
as surface advection operators suitable for scalable solution by methods for partial differential
equations.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Background

Integral boundary layer (IBL) methods provide engineering-fidelity solutions of the thin-layer
Navier-Stokes equations. The momentum energy integral technique (MEIT) is an advanced IBL
model for high-Mach aerothermodynamics and ablation modeling. When joined with an inviscid
flow model, such as Newtonian-type panel methods or the Euler equations, MEIT offers a tradeoff
of accuracy and generality for significant cost savings relative to solving averaged forms of the
Navier-Stokes equations.
The historical basis of IBL methods is the von Karman momentum integral equation and its broad
usage in low-speed aerodynamics (see Thwaites [1949], Head [1957], Cooke [1959], Drela and
Giles [1987]). Throughout the past decades a variety of IBL and flat plate boundary layer methods
have been developed for high-Mach flow and ablation modeling. Early methods for high-speed flow
were grounded in classical boundary layer modeling and the work of Eckert and Drewitz [1943],
Eckert [1960], Lees [1956], Lees and Kubota [1957], Van Driest [1956], Cooke [1959], Fay and
Riddell [1958], and many others. IBL and related methods have been established as critical design
and analysis tools via codes such as CBAERO (Kinney [2004, 2007]), UNLATCH3 (Hamilton
et al. [2014]), HI-Mach (Goates et al. [2025], Freeman et al. [2025]), ASCC (Dahm et al. [1976]),
as well as in a variety of SNL software packages (Polansky [1990], Delgado et al. [2024], Murray
[2026]).
Methods for lower-fidelity high-Mach aerothermodynamics rely on splitting inviscid shock layers
and viscous boundary layers (sometimes in an iterative fashion). Such boundary layer models
require solutions to differential equations on the outer surface of a vehicle. Finally, boundary layer
closures for skin friction, convective heating, turbulent transition, surface roughness, and more are
required to enable broad and/or accurate application. High-Mach IBL models can be categorized
into three types, all of which have been demonstrated as capable of modeling friction and heating
on three-dimensional vehicle geometries. We describe these three types of models as follows.
Flat plate techniques employ streamwise distance (arc length) to generalize simpler closures
 from flat plate theory. These models solve for streamwise distance, use an appropriate
 Reynolds number to compute skin friction, and incorporate a Reynolds analogy factor to
 produce a Stanton number and heating predictions.
Momentum integral techniques solve streamwise differential equations for various forms of the
 momentum thickness and tend to use more advanced closures than flat plate models.
Momentum-energy integral techniques solve distinct momentum thickness and energy thick-
 ness equations with distinct heating and shear closures. Heating and shear can be coupled
 through transition prediction and more advanced closure models.

 13

Under this taxonomy, CBAERO (Kinney [2004]) and HI-Mach (Goates et al. [2025], Freeman et al.
[2025]) are flat plate techniques, UNLATCH3 and its predecessors (Hamilton et al. [2014], Zoby
et al. [1981]) are momentum integral techniques, and ASCC and SNL-based codes employ MEIT
(Dahm et al. [1976], Polansky [1990], Delgado et al. [2024], Murray [2026]).

1.2. Overview

The objective of this report is to detail the integral boundary layer formulation at the core of MEIT.
We also aim to relate MEIT to the other two types of models listed above. To the extent possible,
our intention is not to muddy the waters with implementation details or the myriad closure models
available to IBL codes.
This document is broken up as follows.
 • Section 2 presents a split inviscid-viscous formulation, briefly discusses inviscid models rele-
 vant to MEIT, and derives the corresponding integral boundary-layer relations in streamline-
 oriented ordinary differential equation (ODE) form. The derivation extends two previous
 presentations of the MEIT equations:
 – Kirsch et al. [2023], which derives MEIT equations specifically for axisymmetric ge-
 ometries with non-vortical inviscid flows.
 – Anderson [1976], which presents a derivation of the momentum thickness equation
 for vortical inviscid flows over axisymmetric or flat-plate geometries with a wall-based
 edge state.
 We extend both perspectives to a unified curvilinear-surface formulation that (1) is not
 restricted to flat-plate or axisymmetric geometries, and (2) allows for an arbitrary definition
 of the edge state (i.e., edge properties that are not necessarily identified with the inviscid
 solution evaluated at the slip wall). As in Anderson [1976], the resulting framework also
 accommodates vortical inviscid flows (e.g., induced by curved shocks) and wall mass blowing.
 For completeness, a simplified derivation for non-vortical inviscid flows is also summarized.
 • Section 3 presents an extension of the MEIT equations to a system of surface partial differ-
 ential equations (PDEs).
Several viable MEIT equation sets are provided (only the core integral boundary layer relations
without any closure models). These include the momentum thickness equation, energy thickness
equation, and in some cases a streamline metric equation. The ODE forms are appropriate for
codes where inviscid streamlines are explicitly traced along the surface. The PDE forms are ready
for PDE solvers on meshed surfaces.

2. DERIVATION OF MEIT ODES IN STREAMLINE COORDINATES

2.1. Split Inviscid-Viscous Construction

Before we may derive meaningful integral boundary layer models, we must address the splitting
of the viscous and inviscid flow into distinct models. Our intention with this document is to
support a broadly compatible viscous boundary layer formulation that consistently admits simple
and advanced inviscid flow models.

We consider wall-bounded, high-Mach viscous flows spanning an unperturbed freestream region
down to the wall surface of interest. Such flows consist of large outer inviscid layers surrounding
an inner viscous boundary layer around the wall. We presume validity of the continuum assump-
tion, meaning that a higher-fidelity, scale-resolving model of the fluid motion is the Navier-Stokes
equations augmented by various energy and chemical species equations. Nothing within this report
presumes perfect gas or thermochemical equilibrium behavior, although we are principally con-
cerned with skin friction and heat transfer as characterized by the total energy equation. Additional
(novel) developments would be necessary to deploy such integral methods to nonequilibrium flows,
and this is well outside of our current scope.

 Figure 2-1. Relevant boundary layer profiles

The viscous flow is described by a velocity vector, 𝒖(𝒙), and a complete, unique thermochemical
state description denoted by a vector, 𝑸(𝒙). This state vector may simply contain temperature
and pressure supplemented by equilibrium assumptions (a divariant gas model), or it may be
equipped with species composition and internal temperatures to more fully describe thermochemical
nonequilibrium. The presence of wall mass blowing results from surface ablation and/or offgassing
from internal material decomposition. Mass blowing results in the viscous velocity field adhering
to a no-slip condition and a nonzero wall-normal velocity at the wall.

 17

We make several assumptions about the boundary layer, further simplifying matters in accordance
with high-Mach formulations in practice. We presume two-dimensional curvilinear boundary layer
behavior, neglecting crossflow primarily. The pressure is assumed to be fixed in the wall normal
direction within the viscous layer. Streamwise variation of the viscous stresses and total viscous
energy flux are neglected, following thin shear layer approximations.

Alongside the viscous flow we consider a companion inviscid flow extending all the way through
the viscous boundary layer to a slip wall boundary condition (see Figure 2-1). This inviscid flow has
velocity vector 𝒖𝑖 (𝒙) and thermochemical state vector 𝑸 𝑖 (𝒙). Wall mass blowing is not considered
in the companion inviscid flow - the inviscid velocity at the wall boundary is entirely tangent to
the wall surface. All closures associated with mass blowing are left to the viscous boundary layer
model. As shown in Figure 2-1, the viscous and inviscid velocity vectors differ within the boundary
layer and converge at a finite distance, 𝛿, away from the wall.

This setup of a viscous flow and its companion inviscid flow will produce a general structure of an
integral boundary layer formulation, into which practical inviscid models and IBL closures may be
deployed. The structure presented herein allows the IBL to consistently function with simple and
advanced inviscid models, as well as model curved shock effects and rotational inviscid flows. The
extent to which inviscid models conform to this full structure will vary.

2.2. Inviscid Model Considerations

Figure 2-2 describes the predictions required of an inviscid model when using an IBL of the form
presented in this report. The role of the inviscid model is to predict these quantities given the
velocity and thermochemical state of the freestream fluid, denoted by an ∞ subscript. Necessary
inviscid quantities are the velocity and thermochemical state vector at the wall, 𝒖𝑖,𝑤 and 𝑸 𝑖,𝑤 ,
respectively, as well as the same at the outer edge of the boundary layer, 𝒖 𝑒 and 𝑸 𝑒 , respectively.
The edge quantities may be computed from the inviscid model given viscous flow quantities,
suggesting either monolithic or iterative inviscid-viscous coupling. The boundary layer edge is
discussed further in Section 2.6.

Figure 2-2. Interface for inviscid-viscous model coupling. The dashed lines indicate options for iterative
 coupling through displacement thickness, boundary layer thickness, and entrainment.

Relevant inviscid models are simplistic non-differential approaches such as panel methods, shock
integral methods as presented by Dahm et al. [1976], and numerical solutions to the Euler equations.
Next we separately discuss several inviscid flow models as they fit into the paradigm of Figure 2-2.
This is not intended to recommend any particular inviscid model for any particular application.

2.2.1. Modified Newtonian Aerodynamics

Modified Newtonian Aerodynamics (MNA) is an extremely simple inviscid flow model that requires
only the freestream conditions and the local surface inclination. MNA improves upon the classical
Newtonian theory by accounting for Mach number dependence of the normal shock structure Lees
and Kubota [1957]. At 𝛾 → 1 and Ma∞ → ∞ MNA reduces to the classical Newtonian result,
with the essential difference between the two simply being the local pressure coefficient, 𝐶 𝑝 , and its
dependence on the local inclination angle, Θ. Newtonian methods such as these do not distinguish
between the inviscid quantities at the wall and the edge. Nevertheless they are surprisingly useful
as a simple inviscid model.

MNA is a three-step thermodynamic process that can model real gas effects only through an
assumption of thermochemical equilibrium. Exact relations for these processes are available in
textbooks for perfect gases. Numerical determination of real gas composition requires thermody-
namic potential minimization outside of our current scope.

 19

Equation (2.1) shows how MNA produces the edge and inviscid wall properties needed by an IBL
viscous model. Note again that the inviscid wall and boundary layer edge are coincident, and that
there is no apparent room for inviscid-viscous coupling. It is entirely conceivable that one could

2.2.2. Euler Equations

A higher-fidelity inviscid model than MNA is directly solving the Euler equations, numerically
solving for the companion inviscid flow discussed earlier. Solutions to the Euler equations accom-
modate accurate shock shape prediction, complex flow features, nonequilibrium processes in the
inviscid shock layer, and meaningful distinction between the slip wall and boundary layer edge.
An important aspect of high-Mach aerothermodynamics is the presence of curved shock effects
such as entropy gradients and regions of high vorticity in the inviscid shock layer. Figure 2-3
presents a qualitative picture of inviscid streamlines over a curved body, colored by their post-
shock entropy. The inviscid streamline passing through the normal shock along the stagnation line
acquires the large normal shock entropy, while less central streamlines pass through the shock at
lower shock angles and correspondingly acquire lower post-shock entropy. This inviscid entropy
gradient plays a significant role in heat transfer calculations around regions of high curvature and
in turbulent boundary layers [Anderson, 1989]. While the inviscid flow is isentropic, consumption
of entropy within the viscous layer means that the normal shock entropy associated with the slip
wall conditions makes for a poor choice of boundary layer edge characterization. As discussed by
Zoby et al. [1981], Hamilton et al. [2014], and Polansky [1990], coupling IBL methods to Euler
solutions may involve iterative determination of the boundary layer edge properties.
An additional complexity in solving the Euler equations is the option of offsetting the slip wall
of the inviscid flow domain based on the displacement thickness. This corrects the overall mass
flux of the inviscid model (see Figure 2-1) but still involves the solution of inviscid flow inside
the boundary layer. While this coupling can be strong and essential to capture for subsonic and
transonic flows (see Giles et al. [1985], Drela [1990], Youngren [1988]), it has apparently not found
such importance in high-Mach aerothermodynamics. Perhaps such finer coupling between inviscid
and viscous models will increase in importance with future efforts to deploy truly three-dimensional
boundary layer treatments to high-Mach flows.

A variety of Euler-IBL coupling schemes may be considered. The simplest such scheme is to use
the Euler slip wall prediction as the edge, resulting in 𝒖 𝑒 = 𝒖𝑖,𝑤 and 𝑸 𝑒 = 𝑸 𝑖,𝑤 . This scheme, along
with MNA as discussed previously, falls to the error of the normal shock entropy being preserved
in the inviscid wall state. Rectifying this error due to entropy gradients in turbulent boundary
layers requires looking outward in the Euler solution. Two attractive options have been assessed in
literature, which respectively match the predicted boundary layer thickness or the entrained mass
flux.
 1. Interpolate the Euler solution to a point 𝛿 away from the wall (the IBL boundary layer
 thickness), producing a local 𝒖 𝑒 and 𝑸 𝑒 .
 2. Integrate the streamwise mass flux of the Euler solution until matching the entrainment
 prediction of the IBL model, and interpolate to find a local 𝒖 𝑒 and 𝑸 𝑒 .
Both of these options require iteration of the IBL model, as 𝛿 or 𝐹shape (the entrainment shape factor)
will vary with 𝑸 𝑒 . Both schemes still use the slip wall data from the Euler solution for the inviscid
wall properties as expected. In practice, Zoby et al. [1981] in particular have concluded that the
distance-based edge properties perform well and with less complication than the entrainment-based
properties. The IBL formulation presented in this report is agnostic to this choice.
It appears that any of these schemes could be deployed with Euler solutions that solve for the
flow extending to the displacement thickness instead of the wall. Figure 2-2 shows both options,
with boundary layer thickness or mass entrainment determining edge properties and displacement
thickness informing slip wall properties. A subtlety of likely little practical importance is that the
artificial slip surface at 𝛿∗ would require a slight rotation of the 𝒖𝑖,𝑤 to maintain tangency to the
wall. We do not entertain such schemes further here, as we have not observed such Euler solutions
used for high-Mach flows.

2.2.3. Shock Integral Method

The final inviscid model of interest to this report is the integral method for axisymmetric surfaces
presented by Dahm et al. [1976]. We highlight this approach as an intermediate inviscid model
between MNA and Euler solutions, but which also is able to fill the distinct wall and edge data
available to the IBL formulation presented here.

This model incorporates both MNA and surface pressure correlations to determine pressure at
the wall and extending up to the boundary layer edge. Given surface pressures, oblique shock
relations, and a presumption of linear inviscid velocity and pressure variation in the shock, an
integral method computes 𝒖𝑖 and 𝑸 𝑖 in the shock layer. This method presumes a slip wall boundary
condition without mass blowing similarly to our companion inviscid flow. It is important to mention
that Dahm et al. [1976] only apply this method for axisymmetric flows, meaning that the direction
of flow at the slip wall, 𝒖𝑖,𝑤 /∥𝒖𝑖,𝑤 ∥, is known from the surface shape alone.

Finally, Figure 2-6 shows a control volume designed to compute 𝒖 𝑒 and 𝑸 𝑒 in this approach. The
mass flow entrained by the boundary layer, computed with the IBL solution and entrainment shape
factor 𝐹shape , is matched to that of the freestream at a given height, effectively building a closed
“top” of the control volume by following an inviscid streamline outside of the boundary layer.
From this height and the integral shock method, the shock angle and oblique shock properties may
be computed. Rather than looking outwards a distance 𝛿 to interpolate from an inviscid solution,
this approach looks backwards along a streamtube. While not made entirely clear in the reference
material, one might use the post-shock state to compute entropy which remains constant up to the
edge state. From that entropy, the surface pressure, and assumption of thermochemical equilibrium
(enforcing a certain elemental composition of the mixture), one may compute 𝑸 𝑒 similarly to MNA
but with a more accurate entropy than that of the normal shock. The edge speed, ∥𝒖 𝑒 ∥, falls out the
same way as in MNA.

such that 𝜓𝑖 (𝑠, 𝑠3∗ ) = 𝜓(𝑠, 𝑠3 ) (see Figure 2-6).
The inviscid velocity at the wall is identified as 𝒖𝑖,𝑤 = 𝒖𝑖 (𝑠, 𝜓𝑖 = 0). In the presence of vortical
inviscid flows induced by curved shocks, 𝜓𝑖 = 0 therefore identifies the inviscid streamline that
carries normal-shock entropy. Away from the wall, and in the absence of mass blowing, 𝜓 ≥ 0, and
there exists an 𝑠3∗ such that 𝜓𝑖 (𝑠, 𝑠3∗ ) = 𝜓(𝑠, 𝑠3 ). However, with viscous mass blowing at the wall,
there are viscous streamlines where 𝜓 < 0 with no inviscid counterparts, i.e., there is no value of
𝑠3∗ such that 𝜓𝑖 (𝑠, 𝑠3∗ ) < 0 (see Figure 2-6). Thus the streamline 𝜓 = 0 is elevated off the wall. To
remedy the analysis in this case, we define the inviscid mapping for 𝜓 < 0 by holding the inviscid
state fixed at its wall value, i.e., 𝒖𝑖 (𝑠, 𝑠3∗ (𝑠, 𝜓 < 0)) = 𝒖𝑖 (𝑠, 𝑠3∗ (𝑠, 𝜓 = 0)). That is, we pair all
viscous streamlines with 𝜓 ≤ 0 to the inviscid streamline 𝜓𝑖 = 0. Similar extensions are required
when the inviscid solution is only available on a truncated domain and does not span the full range
of boundary layer streamlines, e.g., MNA only provides the single inviscid streamline 𝜓𝑖 = 0.

3. MEIT SURFACE PDE FORMULATION

3.1. Multi-Domain Formulation

The ODE formulation of MEIT presented above requires the tracing of inviscid streamlines on the
wall surface and the determination of initial conditions at the stagnation point on each streamline.
A subtle but essential detail of the thickness equations is that they are not well-posed arbitrarily
close to a stagnation point with zero edge speed. As 𝑢 𝑒 → 0 the streamwise flux of momentum
and energy degrades to zero and leaves degenerate thicknesses. In the first MEIT codes this was
addressed through a nontrivial series expansion (see Dahm et al. [1976]) around the stagnation
point and explicit correlations for stagnation point heating.
In constructing an MEIT solver built atop finite volume methods for surface-based partial differential
equations, we’ve addressed the well-posedness issues of MEIT through a multi-domain formulation.
A similar approach can be used to solve for turbulent quantities which only evolve after a transition
locus. Figure 3-1 shows a notional finite stagnation region, Ω, surrounded by a streamline region,
Λ, ultimately bounded by an outflow boundary condition along 𝜕Λ𝑒 . Having partitioned the
surface into stagnation and streamline regions, we define governing equations on each region and
compatibility conditions on the interface.
On the stagnation region one may enforce stagnation heating/friction correlations or series expan-
sions. These will be implicit, nonlinear expressions of an algebraic nature. For example, matching
the heat flux computed from a correlation constrains the stagnation region energy thickness, 𝜙 𝑠 , to
satisfy
                             𝑞 stag − 𝑞 𝑤 (𝜙 𝑠 ; 𝜌 𝑒 , 𝑢 𝑒 , 𝐻𝑒 , . . .) = 0, on Ω.           (3.1)
Such an expression may present analytical solution for 𝜙 𝑠 if heating closure models are sufficiently
simple. In general, closure models are complex and it is worthwhile to support any implicit,
nonlinear, algebraic constraint in the stagnation region.

4. CONCLUSION

In this report we developed a generalized MEIT equation set intended for use on realistic three-
dimensional vehicle surfaces and within modern finite-volume CFD workflows.

 • General curvilinear MEIT derivation: We derived the core MEIT momentum- and energy-
 thickness equations in an orthogonal streamline curvilinear coordinate system built from the
 inviscid wall streamline direction and the surface-normal direction.
 • Vortical inviscid flows and wall blowing: By introducing viscous and inviscid stream func-
 tions, the formulation remains valid when the companion inviscid flow is vortical (e.g., due
 to curved shocks/entropy gradients) and when the wall has mass blowing.
 • Edge-state generalization: We showed how the boundary-layer edge state can be defined on
 an arbitrary inviscid streamline, rather than being tied to the inviscid slip-wall state, enabling
 consistent use with IBL formulations that distinguish between wall and edge conditions.
 • Generalized streamline metric: We derived a streamline metric evolution equation that re-
 places the axisymmetric radius factor seen in legacy derivations and captures streamline con-
 vergence/divergence, improving applicability to fully three-dimensional, non-axisymmetric
 surface flows. Formulations without explicit dependence on the streamline metric were also
 derived that avoid complexities introduced with solving the metric evolution equation.
 • Surface-PDE forms: We rewrote the streamline ODE system as surface PDEs suitable for
 scalable solutions on general three-dimensional surface meshes with finite-volume solvers,
 avoiding explicit streamline tracing.
