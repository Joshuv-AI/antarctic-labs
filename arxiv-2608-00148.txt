Sharp bounds for non-adaptive randomized approximation of high-dimensional noisy vectors
Robert J. Kunsch, Marcin Wnuk

Abstract
We study the complexity of approximating the finite-dimensional vector space embedding ℓ p m ↪ ℓ q m \ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m} for 2 ≤ p < q ≤ ∞ 2\leq p<q\leq\infty based on non-adaptive randomized algorithms that use up to n n arbitrary linear functionals as information on a problem instance 𝒙 ∈ ℝ m \boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m} , where n ≪ m n\ll m . We prove lower bounds on the non-adaptive randomized approximation error with a joint dependence on ( n , m ) (n,m) matching previously known upper bounds.



Keywords:
Monte Carlo, information-based complexity, lower bounds,
linear information

1
Introduction

We investigate the approximation of the embedding
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}

for
2\leq p<q\leq\infty
by means of non-adaptive randomized algorithms,
where
\ell_{p}^{m}
is
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
equipped with the
\ell_{p}
-norm.
The article is a follow-up to the papers
[
4
,
6
,
5
]
, yet self-contained.
The problem of approximating the embeddings of finite-dimensional spaces is of interest,
mainly due to two factors:
on the one hand, approximating those embeddings is a building block
for approximating more complicated function space embeddings; see, e.g.
[
3
,
8
]
.
On the other hand, finite-dimensional embeddings constitute a ‘minimal working example’
where one can observe interesting theoretical phenomena,
such as the fact that randomized adaptive algorithms can be much better
than randomized non-adaptive algorithms
[
4
,
6
,
5
]
.

We start by describing admissible algorithms.
A
deterministic non-adaptive algorithm
for the problem
\ell_{p}^{m}\to\ell_{q}^{m}

is any mapping of the form

A=\varphi\circ N,

where

•

N:\ell^{m}_{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear mapping called
information mapping
,
and

•

\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}
,
called the
reconstruction mapping
,
can be any mapping.

Thus,
A(\boldsymbol{x})
first applies
n
linear functionals to
\boldsymbol{x}
(it ‘collects information’)
and then transforms this information in some way to obtain the output.
One calls
n
the
cardinality (or information cost)
of
A
and writes

\card A=n.

A
randomized non-adaptive algorithm
is a collection
A=(A^{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}

of deterministic non-adaptive algorithms indexed by the elements of the sample space
of some probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
,
where the mapping
\Omega\ni\omega\mapsto\card A^{\omega}
is a random variable
and, for a fixed
\boldsymbol{x}\in\ell^{m}_{p}
,
the mapping
\Omega\ni\omega\mapsto A^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})

is also a random variable, usually written as
A(\boldsymbol{x})
.
For technical reasons, we also assume that the mapping
(\omega,\boldsymbol{x})\mapsto A^{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})

is measurable with respect to the Borel-
\sigma
-algebra on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

and the product
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
We regard deterministic non-adaptive algorithms as a special instance of randomized algorithms,
in particular, we assume Borel-measurability.
Since in the rest of the paper we deal mainly with non-adaptive algorithms,
we will often simply speak of algorithms.

Given a randomized algorithm
A
, we define its
error

when approximating the embedding
\ell^{m}_{p}\hookrightarrow\ell^{m}_{q}
as

e(A,\ell^{m}_{p}\hookrightarrow\ell^{m}_{q}):=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\colon\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-A(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{q}

where
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is the classical
\ell_{p}
-norm.
The cardinality of a randomized algorithm is given by the worst case cost,

\card A:=\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}\card A^{\omega}.

We are interested in the
n
-th minimal error

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell^{m}_{p}\hookrightarrow\ell^{m}_{q}):=\inf_{A_{n}}e(A_{n},\ell^{m}_{p}\hookrightarrow\ell^{m}_{q}),

where the infimum is taken over all randomized algorithms
A_{n}
with cardinality at most
n
.
For
\varepsilon>0
, we also consider the dual notion of the
\varepsilon
-complexity
,

n^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(\varepsilon,\ell^{m}_{p}\hookrightarrow\ell^{m}_{q}):=\inf\{\card A\colon e(A,\ell^{m}_{p}\hookrightarrow\ell^{m}_{q})\leq\varepsilon\},

where the infimum is taken over randomized non-adaptive algorithms.

In this article,
we focus on the situation where the dimension of the problem
m

is much larger than the cardinality
n
of algorithms,
n\ll m
.
The main result states that, in the case
2\leq p<q\leq\infty
, one has

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell^{m}_{p}\hookrightarrow\ell^{m}_{q})\asymp\min\left\{1,\,\left(\frac{m^{1-2/p}\cdot\log m}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(1-p/q)}\right\},

(1)

see Theorem
3.1
.
The upper error bounds are already known from
[
5
, Thm 4.4]

and were obtained by means of denoising a linear randomized algorithm.
The contribution of this paper is a proof of the matching lower bounds.
For this we use Bakhvalov’s trick,
where we switch from the randomized setting
to an average case setting for deterministic algorithms.
The average case setting is studied in Section
2
.
The results of Section
2

are used to prove a randomized lower bound in Section
3
.

Upper bounds are known for the non-adaptive randomized approximation of
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}

for the general parameter range
1\leq p<q\leq\infty
, see
[
5
]
.
We conjecture that these algorithms are asymptotically optimal also for
1\leq p<2
.
The paper
[
4
]
contains a lower bound for non-adaptive randomized approximation
which holds for the whole parameter range of
p
and
q
, in particular
\ell_{1}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}^{m}
,
but it only shows that the error will be larger than a constant
\varepsilon_{0}=\frac{1}{100}

if
n
is too small compared to
m
.
This paper now proves lower bounds with joint dependence on
m
and
n
,
but only for
p\geq 2
.
For
p<2
the suitable average case setting appears to be more difficult to analyse.
We note that from the information-based complexity point of view,
the approximation problem is much harder for
p>2
because the error rates and complexity
exhibit a factor that is polynomially large in
m

while for
p\in[1,2]
the dependence on
m
is only logarithmic.
Upper bounds are also known for the adaptive randomized setting
where information functionals may be chosen depending on previous measurements,
see
[
6
,
5
]
.
In these bounds, instead of a
\log m
-dependence,
we find a
\log\log m
-dependence;
still, for
p>2
a polynomial dependence on
m
remains.
Proving lower bounds for the adaptive setting is yet another challenge, especially for
p<2
.

A short note why we call the vectors from the unit ball of
\ell_{p}^{m}
with
p>2
‘noisy vectors’:
Randomized algorithms for
\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}

aim to recover the largest entries (in absolute value) of
\boldsymbol{x}
.
In randomized measurements,
the smaller entries of
\boldsymbol{x}
act as noise that scales with the
\ell_{2}
-norm,
which can be large for
p>2
,
namely: If
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
then
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\leq m^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}
is a sharp estimate.
This gives an intuitive explanation for the factor
m^{1-2/p}

occuring in the error bounds.
The lower bound proof shows that this factor is indeed unavoidable,
at least if we restrict ourselves to non-adaptive algorithms.

Asymptotic notation

We use asymptotic notation to compare functions
f
and
g

that depend on variables
(\varepsilon,m)
or
(n,m)
,
writing
f\preceq g
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
f\leq Cg
holds.

Weak asymptotic equivalence

f\asymp g
means
f\preceq g\preceq f
.
We often state such asymptotic relations with restrictions on the variables,
e.g., for sufficiently large
m\geq m_{0}
and
n\leq m^{\alpha}
or
\varepsilon\geq m^{-\beta}
, respectively,
meaning that the inequality
f\leq Cg
only holds for pairs
(\varepsilon,m)
or
(n,m)

that satisfy these restrictions.
The implicit constant
C
may depend on other parameters such as
p
and
q
.

2
Average case analysis

We use Bakhvalov’s technique (also known as Yao’s principle)
to prove lower bounds for randomized methods
by switching to an average case setting.
For a (sub-)probability measure
\mu
on
\ell_{p}^{m}
,
the
\mu
-average error for a deterministic algorithm
A\colon\ell_{p}^{m}\to\ell_{q}^{m}

is defined as follows:

e^{\mu}(A,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}):=\int\|A(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}\|_{q}\,d\mu(\boldsymbol{x}).

(2)

Given a (sub-)probability measure
\mu
with support in the
\ell_{p}
-unit ball of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
Bakhvalov’s technique states

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\geq\inf_{A_{n}}e^{\mu}(A_{n},\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m}),

(3)

where the infimum for the
\mu
-average error
is taken over non-adaptive deterministic algorithms
A_{n}

that use at most
n
pieces of linear information.
Note that here, we consider the randomized error for algorithms with strict cardinality bound
n
.
For the sake of simplicity,
in this paper we ignore randomized algorithms with expected cardinality bound,
but there are variations of Bakhvalov’s technique for such settings as well
[
7
, Sec 1.3]
.

2.1
Truncated Gaussian mixture

The notation introduced in this subsection will be used throughout Section
2
.
We start by defining the measure
\mu
on the
\ell_{p}
-unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
In addition to
p\in[2,\infty)
, this measure depends on a parameter
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
k\ll m
.
Let

W=W_{k}^{m}:=\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{m}\colon\boldsymbol{w}\ \text{with exactly $k$ non-zero entries}\}

(4)

with
M:=\#W=\binom{m}{k}2^{k}

and let
W=\{\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{M}\}
be an arbitrary enumeration of the elements of
W
;
we will write
[M]=\{1,\ldots,M\}
for the index set.
If two elements
\boldsymbol{w}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\in W
differ in at least
k
entries,
then they have an
\ell_{p}
-distance bounded by
\|\boldsymbol{w}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\|_{p}\geq k^{1/p}
.
Rescaling to
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}:=\frac{1}{2}\cdot k^{-1/p}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
,
we then have
\|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\|_{p}=\frac{1}{2}
and

\|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}-\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\|_{q}\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot k^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}
.
Consider the random vector

\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{u}_{I}+\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\,,

(5)

where
I\sim\Uniform[M]
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\sim\Normal(\boldsymbol{0},\id_{m})
are independent random variables
and
\sigma=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{p}}\,m^{-1/p}>0
is a scaling factor.
The probability measure
\widetilde{\mu}:={\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{X}}

is not supported on the
\ell_{p}
-unit ball of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
so we introduce a truncation by the event

\Omega^{\prime}:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega\colon\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}(\omega)\|_{p}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\right\},

defining the sub-probability measure

\mu(\,\cdot\,):={\mathbb{P}}\left(\Omega^{\prime}\cap\{\boldsymbol{X}\in\,\cdot\,\}\right).

Indeed, on
\Omega^{\prime}
we have

\|\boldsymbol{X}\|_{p}=\|\boldsymbol{u}_{I}+\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{p}+\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}}}{{\leq}}\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}=1.

By Lemma
A.2
,
our choice of the scaling factor,
\sigma=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{p}}\,m^{-1/p}
,
implies that the truncation probability vanishes in the dimension limit:

\delta={\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}>\frac{1}{2}\right)<\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2/p}}{\pi}\right)\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}0.

(6)

For this truncated measure,
finding a lower bound for the
\mu
-average error of an algorithm
A\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

as defined in (
2
)
reduces to the problem of an approximate reconstruction of the random centres
\boldsymbol{u}_{I}
under noise,
for
\varepsilon>0
we have:

\displaystyle e^{\mu}(A,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|A(\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{X}\|_{q}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\|A(\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}-\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{q}\right)\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|A(\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Omega^{\prime}}\right]-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{q}

\displaystyle\geq\bigl[{\mathbb{P}}(\|A(\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}\geq\varepsilon)-\delta\bigr]\cdot\varepsilon-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{q}.

(7)

We will choose
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{4}k^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}

and show that
{\mathbb{P}}(\|A(\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}\geq\varepsilon)>\frac{1}{16}
.
The term
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{q}
turns out to be considerably smaller than
\varepsilon
for
k\ll m
,
eventually proving a
\mu
-average error of order
k^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}
,
see Lemma
2.2
.

2.2
Reconstructing the centers from linear information

In view of (
7
),
we now focus on the probability of a large reconstruction error
\|A(\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}

for a deterministic algorithm
A=\varphi\circ N\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

with non-adaptive linear information mapping
N\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
We identify the information mapping with a matrix
N\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
.
Without loss of generality,
the rows of
N
are orthogonal with
\ell_{2}
-norm
\sigma^{-1}

such that
\boldsymbol{Z}:=N(\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m})\sim\Normal(\boldsymbol{0},\id_{n})
.
Hence,
the information
N(\boldsymbol{X})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
can be represented as a random vector

\boldsymbol{Y}:=N(\boldsymbol{X})=\boldsymbol{v}_{I}+\boldsymbol{Z}

(8)

with
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}:=N\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
and independent random variables

I\sim\Uniform[M]
and
\boldsymbol{Z}\sim\Normal(\boldsymbol{0},\id_{n})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Consider
\boldsymbol{Y}
as in (
8
) with
n\geq 16
, and for
\varepsilon>0
define

K:=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\#\{i\in[M]\colon\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\|_{q}<\varepsilon\}.

(9)

Let
R>0
and define
\mathcal{I}:=\{i\in[M]\colon\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{2}\leq R\}
.
Assume that
R
satisfies
\#\mathcal{I}\geq\frac{M}{2}
and
2R^{2}\leq\log\frac{M}{8K}
.

Then, for any reconstruction mapping
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
one has

{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(\|\varphi(\boldsymbol{Y)}-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}\geq\varepsilon\bigr)\geq\frac{1}{16}\,.

Proof.

The random variable
\boldsymbol{Y}
is distributed as a Gaussian mixture. Its density function is given by

f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}f_{j},

where
f_{j}
is the density of a standard Gaussian random variable
centred at
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
. For
i\in[M]
we define the super-level sets

L_{i,t}:=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon f_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})\geq t\}=\left\{\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|^{2}}{2}\right)\geq t\right\}.

We extend this definition to
i=0
with
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}:=\boldsymbol{0}

and write
L_{t}:=L_{0,t}
for the super-level sets of a standard Gaussian random variable
centred around the origin.
For
t\in\left(0,(2\pi)^{-n/2}\right)
,
these super-level sets are Euclidean balls
B_{r(t)}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i})

of radius
r(t):=\sqrt{-2\log\left((2\pi)^{n/2}t\right)}

centred around
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
.
For any measurable
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

we can represent the following probability by an integral
over level sets of the probability density
f_{i}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{Y}\in D\,\wedge\,I=i)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\int_{D}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})\,d\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathds{1}[\boldsymbol{y}\in D]\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}[f_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})\geq t]\,dt\,d\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathds{1}_{D\cap L_{i,t}}(\boldsymbol{y})\,d\boldsymbol{y}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}(D\cap L_{i,t})\,dt.

(10)

We define the regions of approximate detection of
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
by the reconstruction
\varphi
,

D_{i}:=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon\|\varphi(\boldsymbol{y})-\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\|_{q}<\varepsilon\},

The definition of
D_{i}
and
K
implies that, for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\#\{i\in[M]\colon\boldsymbol{y}\in D_{i}\}\leq K.

(11)

We use these sets
D_{i}
to represent the probability of a bad approximation
in the style of (
10
):

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(\|\varphi(\boldsymbol{Y})-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}\geq\varepsilon\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}(D_{i}^{c}\cap L_{i,t})\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}(D_{i}^{c}\cap L_{i,t})\,dt.

(12)

As a short-hand notation, we write
B_{\rho}:=B_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{0})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Let
V_{n}:=\lambda^{n}(B_{1})
denote the volume of the
n
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball.
If we set the radius to
\rho(t):=\sqrt{r(t)^{2}+R^{2}}
,
then for
i\in\mathcal{I}
the ball
B_{\rho(t)}

covers at least half of the volume of the super-level set
L_{i,t}
,
see Figure
1
.
In detail,
considering the half space

H_{i}:=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle\leq\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{i},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle\},

we find

\lambda^{n}(L_{i,t}\cap B_{\rho(t)})\geq\lambda^{n}\left(L_{i,t}\cap H_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,\lambda^{n}(L_{i,t})=\frac{1}{2}\,r(t)^{n}\,V_{n}.

(13)

Indeed: Elements
\boldsymbol{y}\in L_{i,t}\cap H_{i}
can be written as orthogonal decomposition

\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{w}

with

\boldsymbol{u}=\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle}{\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{i},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
.
In the case of

\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle}{\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{i},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle}\leq 0

we have the estimate

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}+\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{2}
.
Otherwise, since
\boldsymbol{y}\in H_{i}
, we have

\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle}{\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{i},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rangle}\in[0,1]

which implies
\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{2}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2},\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\}+\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq R^{2}+r(t)^{2}=\rho(t)^{2},

which shows
L_{i,t}\cap H_{i}\subset L_{i,t}\cap B_{\rho(t)}
.

Now we can estimate the sum that occurs in (

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda^{n}(D_{i}^{c}\cap L_{i,t})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda^{n}(D_{i}^{c}\cap L_{i,t}\cap B_{\rho(t)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda^{n}(L_{i,t}\cap B_{\rho(t)})-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda^{n}(D_{i}\cap L_{i,t}\cap B_{\rho(t)})

\displaystyle[\eqref{eq:yinDi}\text{ and }\eqref{eq:vol(LcapBrho)}]\qquad

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{n}(L_{i,t})-K\cdot\lambda^{n}(B_{\rho(t)})

,

,
and using the assumption

, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\rho(t)}{r(t)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}}{r(t)^{2}}}\leq 1+\frac{R^{2}}{2r(t)^{2}}\leq 1+\frac{2R^{2}}{n}\leq 1+\frac{\log\frac{M}{8K}}{n}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{\log\frac{M}{8K}}{n}\right)=\left(\frac{M}{8K}\right)^{1/n}.

In that case, (

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda^{n}(D_{i}^{c}\cap L_{i,t})\geq\frac{M}{8}\cdot r(t)^{n}\cdot V_{n}\,.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(\|\varphi(\boldsymbol{Y})-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}\geq\varepsilon\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}(D_{i}^{c}\cap L_{i,t})\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}\int_{0}^{\infty}r(t)^{n}\,V_{n}\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\left(r(t)^{n}-r(T_{n})^{n}\right)V_{n}\,dt

\displaystyle\left[r(T_{n})=\tfrac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\right]\qquad

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\lambda^{n}\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\right\}\cap L_{t}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\left[\boldsymbol{Z}\sim\Normal(\boldsymbol{0},\id_{n})\right]\qquad

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:P(|Z|>sqrt(n)/2}}}{{\geq}}\frac{1}{16}\,.

This finishes the proof.

2.3
Lower bound for the average case error

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
2\leq p<q\leq\infty
. Let
m,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
m\geq 4^{p}
and

10\leq k\leq\begin{cases}c_{p,q}\cdot m&\text{for }q<\infty,\\
c_{p,\infty}\cdot\frac{m}{(\log m)^{p/2}}&\text{for }q=\infty,\end{cases}

with positive constants

c_{p,q}=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{64}\sqrt{\frac{p}{q}}\right)^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}&\text{for }q<\infty,\\
\left(\frac{\sqrt{p}}{64e}\right)^{p}&\text{for }q=\infty.\end{cases}

Then for any non-adaptive deterministic algorithm
A
that uses
n
pieces of information
where

16\leq n\leq\frac{1}{54p}\cdot k^{2/p}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\log\frac{m}{k},

we have the following lower bound for the
\mu
-average error
for the sub-probability measure as defined in Section
2.1
:

e^{\mu}(A,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})>\tfrac{1}{256}\,k^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}.

Proof.

We start by showing that our assumptions on
m
and
k
imply

m>64^{2}\,k=4096\,k.

(15)

For
q<\infty
, this follows directly if we bound
c_{p,q}
from above
using
\frac{p}{q}<1
and
\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)<\frac{1}{2}
.
For
q=\infty
we use
m\geq 4^{p}
and obtain

k\leq c_{p,\infty}\cdot\frac{m}{(\log m)^{p/2}}\leq\left(\frac{\sqrt{p}}{64e}\right)^{p}\cdot\frac{m}{(p\log 4)^{p/2}}=\left(\frac{1}{64e\sqrt{\log 4}}\right)^{p}\cdot m<\frac{1}{64^{2}}\cdot m.

We put
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{4}\,k^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}
.
For
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
consider the set

J(\boldsymbol{x}):=\left\{i\in[M]\colon\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\|_{q}<\varepsilon\right\}.

If
J(\boldsymbol{x})\neq\emptyset
,
for
j,i\in J(\boldsymbol{x})
we have
\|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}-\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\|_{q}<2\varepsilon
,
hence, for any fixed
j\in J(\boldsymbol{x})
we obtain the inclusion

J(\boldsymbol{x})\subseteq\left\{i\in[M]\colon\|\boldsymbol{u}_{j}-\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\|_{q}<2\varepsilon\right\}.

Recalling
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}k^{-1/p}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
, we can apply Corollary
B.3

(the prerequisite
m\geq 3k
is covered by (
15
)) and obtain

\displaystyle\#J(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\leq\#\left\{i\in[M]\colon\|\boldsymbol{u}_{j}-\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\|_{q}<2\varepsilon\right\}

\displaystyle=\#\left\{i\in[M]\colon\|\boldsymbol{w}_{j}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\|_{q}<\tfrac{1}{2}\,k^{1/q}\right\}

\displaystyle<\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)^{k/10}\cdot\#W\,.

Hence, with
K=\sup\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\#J(\boldsymbol{x})
as defined in (
9
)
and
M=\#W
, we find

\frac{M}{K}>\left(\frac{m}{k}\right)^{k/10}.

(16)

The prerequisite
2R^{2}\leq\log\frac{M}{8K}
from Lemma
2.1

will be met if

R^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{k}{10}\log\frac{m}{k}-\log 8\right).

Since
k\geq 10
and
\frac{m}{k}>4096
, see (
15
),
the following is an even stricter requirement:

R^{2}\leq\frac{k}{27}\log\frac{m}{k}\,.

(17)

Recall
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=N\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\,k^{-1/p}\cdot N\boldsymbol{w}_{i}

from (
5
) and (
8
),
where in the context of Lemma
2.1
we assumed that
the information matrix
N=(N_{ij})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
has orthogonal rows
with
\ell_{2}
-norm
\sigma^{-1}=2\sqrt{p}\,m^{1/p}
each.
The coordinates of
\boldsymbol{w}_{I}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})

take values in
\{-1,0,1\}
and are pairwise uncorrelated,
hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{I}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\,k^{-2/p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{ij}X_{j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\,k^{-2/p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{ij}^{2}\underbrace{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}X_{j}^{2}}_{=\frac{k}{m}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\,k^{-2/p}\cdot\frac{k}{m}\cdot n\cdot 4p\,m^{2/p}

\displaystyle=pn\cdot\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}.

Putting
R:=\sqrt{2pn}\cdot\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}
,
Markov’s inequality gives
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{v}_{I}\|_{2}^{2}\leq R^{2}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}
,
in other words,
the set
\mathcal{I}
as defined in Lemma
2.1

satisfies
\#\mathcal{I}\geq\frac{M}{2}
.
For this value of
R
, the requirement (
17
) reads

2pn\cdot\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\leq\frac{k}{27}\log\frac{m}{k}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad n\leq\frac{1}{54p}\cdot k^{2/p}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\log\frac{m}{k}\,.

With this, the assumptions of Lemma
2.1
are thus satisfied,
which implies the probability bound
{\mathbb{P}}(\|A(\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}\geq\varepsilon)\geq\frac{1}{16}
.
Now, we can finally evaluate the lower bound (
7
) for the
\mu
-average error:

e^{\mu}(A,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\geq\bigl[{\mathbb{P}}(\|A(\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}\geq\varepsilon)-\delta\bigr]\cdot\varepsilon-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{q}.

For
m\geq 4^{p}>\left(\pi\log(32)\right)^{p/2}

we have

\delta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:deltalim}}}{{<}}\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2/p}}{\pi}\right)<\frac{1}{32}
,
hence,

\bigl[{\mathbb{P}}(\|A(\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{u}_{I}\|_{q}\geq\varepsilon)-\delta\bigr]\cdot\varepsilon>\left[\tfrac{1}{16}-\tfrac{1}{32}\right]\cdot\tfrac{1}{4}\,k^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}=\tfrac{1}{128}\,k^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}.

From (
27
) and (
28
),
which can be used since
m\geq 3
,
we have

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{q}\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{q}{p}}\cdot m^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}&\text{for }q<\infty,\\
\frac{e}{4}\sqrt{\frac{\log m}{p}}\cdot m^{-1/p}&\text{for }q=\infty.\end{cases}

Restricting the range of
k
relative to
m
as stated in the assumptions of the lemma
ensures

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{q}\leq\frac{1}{256}\,k^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}
,
hence,

e^{\mu}(A,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})>\tfrac{1}{256}\,k^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}.

This is the statement of the lemma.
∎

3
Main result

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
2\leq p<q\leq\infty
and
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
where
m>m_{0}(p,q)
is sufficiently large.
Restricting to
n\leq m^{\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha<1
,
we obtain non-adaptive randomized error rates

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\asymp\min\left\{1,\;\left(\frac{m^{1-2/p}\cdot\log m}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)}\right\}.

Conversely, let
0<\beta<\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}
.
Then there exists a constant
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)

such that for
\varepsilon\in[m^{-\beta},\varepsilon_{0}]
,
we have the non-adaptive randomized asymptotic complexity

n^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(\varepsilon,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\asymp\varepsilon^{\left.-\frac{2}{p}\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log m.

The implicit constants may depend on
p
,
q
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
,
but not on
m
and
n
or
\varepsilon
, respectively.

Proof.

The upper error and complexity bounds can be found in
[
5
, Thm 4.4]
.

We start with the lower bound for the complexity.
We switch to the average case setting with

k:=\left\lfloor(256\,\varepsilon)^{-\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right\rfloor\asymp\varepsilon^{-\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}.

We have
k\geq 10

if we assume
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{256}\cdot 10^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}=:\varepsilon_{1}
.
Restricting to

\varepsilon\geq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{q}{p}}\cdot m^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}&\text{for }q<\infty,\\
\frac{e}{4\sqrt{p}}\cdot m^{-1/p}\sqrt{\log m}&\text{for }q=\infty,\end{cases}

(18)

the conditions for
k\ll m
from Lemma
2.2
are met.
For
\varepsilon\geq m^{-\beta}
and sufficiently large
m
,
the prerequisite (
18
) is fulfilled.
If we take

\displaystyle 16\leq n

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{54p}\cdot k^{2/p}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log\frac{m}{k}

(19)

\displaystyle\asymp\varepsilon^{-\left.\frac{2}{p}\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log\left(m\cdot\varepsilon^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right),

then Bakhvalov’s technique (
3
) and Lemma
2.2
imply

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\geq\inf_{A_{n}}e^{\mu}(A_{n},\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})>\varepsilon,

where the infimum is taken over non-adaptive deterministic algorithms
A_{n}

that use at most
n
pieces of linear information.
This argument only works if there exists
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
that fulfils (
19
).
Since
\frac{m}{k}\geq 4096
for
k
satisfying the conditions of Lemma
2.2
,
see (
15
), we have

\frac{1}{54p}\cdot k^{2/p}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log\frac{m}{k}\geq\frac{2\log 2}{9p}\cdot 4096^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot k.

The right-hand side is larger than
16
for

k\geq k_{0}:=\left\lceil\frac{72p}{\log 2}\cdot 4096^{-\left(1-\frac{2}{p}\right)}\right\rceil
.
Assuming
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{256}\,k_{0}^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}=:\varepsilon_{2}
,
we have
k\geq k_{0}
.
Altogether,
\varepsilon_{0}:=\min\{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\}
is a suitable upper limit for
\varepsilon
.

The conclusion of Lemma
2.2
then is
that if we want to achieve an error at most
\varepsilon
,
we need to take more measurements than in (
19
),
that means

n^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(\varepsilon,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\succeq\varepsilon^{-\left.\frac{2}{p}\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log\left(m\cdot\varepsilon^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right).

In summary, there exists a constant
c=c(p,q)>0

such that for
\varepsilon
satisfying (
18
) and
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,
we have

n^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(\varepsilon,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})>c\cdot\varepsilon^{-\left.\frac{2}{p}\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log\left(m\cdot\varepsilon^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right)=:\nu(\varepsilon).

(20)

Define

n_{0}:=\lfloor\nu(\varepsilon_{0})\rfloor\succeq m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log m.

Due to monotonicity of the
n
-th minimal error, for
n\leq n_{0}
we have

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})>e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n_{0},\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\geq\varepsilon_{0}\asymp 1.

For
n>n_{0}
, put

e(n):=\left(\frac{cp}{2}\cdot\frac{m^{1-2/p}\cdot\log\frac{m}{n}}{n}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)},

(21)

which is chosen such that,
for
m\geq e^{2/(cp)}\cdot n\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\frac{cp}{2}\cdot\log\frac{m}{n}\geq 1
,
we have

\nu(e(n))=\frac{n}{\log\frac{m}{n}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{cp}{2}\cdot\frac{m\log\frac{m}{n}}{n}\right)\geq n.

(22)

Assuming

m\geq\max\{10,k_{0}\}\cdot 256^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot e^{1/c}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;c\cdot\log\left(m\cdot\varepsilon_{0}^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right)\geq 1
,
and using the fact that the expression
e(n)
defined in (
21
)
is monotonically decaying in
n
on its natural domain
(0,m)
,
for
n\geq\nu(\varepsilon_{0})
we have

\displaystyle e(n)

\displaystyle\leq e(\nu(\varepsilon_{0}))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\cdot\left(\log\left(\frac{m\cdot\varepsilon_{0}^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}}{\left(c\cdot\log\left(m\cdot\varepsilon_{0}^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right)\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{\log\left(m\cdot\varepsilon_{0}^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right)}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

Furthermore, assuming
m\geq en\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\log\frac{m}{n}\geq 1
,
the expression (
21
) is lower bounded by

e(n)\geq\left(\frac{cp}{2}\cdot\frac{m}{n}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}\cdot m^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}.

Hence, we can guarantee that
\varepsilon=e(n)
satisfies the lower constraint (
18
)
if we further assume

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{cp}\left(\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{q}{p}}\right)^{\left.\frac{2}{p}\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot n

\displaystyle\text{for }q<\infty,

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{m}{\log m}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{2}}{8cp^{2}}\cdot n

\displaystyle\text{for }q=\infty.

(24)

Restricting to
n\leq m^{\alpha}
with some
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
these conditions are indeed satisfied for large
m
.
Then, by (
20
) and the definition of
e(n)
, for sufficiently large
m
, we thus have

n^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(e(n),\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})>\nu(e(n))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:nu(e(n))}}}{{\geq}}n.

Hence,

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})>e(n)\asymp\left(\frac{m^{1-2/p}\cdot\log\frac{m}{n}}{n}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}.

(25)

Finally, restricting ourselves to
\varepsilon\geq m^{-\beta}
, we have

\left(1-\frac{\beta}{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\right)\log m\leq\log\left(m\cdot\varepsilon^{\left.1\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\right)\leq\log m,

hence, the logarithmic term in (
20
) is asymptotically equivalent to
\log m
.
Analogously, for
n\leq m^{\alpha}
with some
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
we have
\log\frac{m}{n}\asymp\log m
, thus simplifying (
25
).
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Compared to the upper bound results from
[
5
, Thm 4.4]
,
the range of parameters for which we prove the lower bounds
is more constrained here.
However, these restrictions are quite natural.

Assume, for example, that we had
a very small value
\varepsilon\preceq m^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}
.
Then the expression of the complexity bound would evaluate to

\varepsilon^{\left.-\frac{2}{p}\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log m\succeq m\cdot\log m,

but
n=m
already means that we have complete information about
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
allowing for exact recovery.

For
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{0}
the expression for the complexity bound would give a value

\varepsilon^{\left.-\frac{2}{p}\middle/\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right.}\cdot m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log m\preceq m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log m.

Restricting to such small values
n\preceq m^{1-\frac{2}{p}}\cdot\log m
,
the error bound gives

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\asymp 1,

meaning that we are essentially not improving over the initial error

e^{\textup{ran},\textup{nonada}}(0,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})=1.

4
Conclusions and outlook

Previously known asymptotic lower bounds due to Heinrich
[
3
]

state that for
m\geq 2n
and
n\geq 2
we have

e^{\textup{ran}}(n,\ell_{p}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{q}^{m})\preceq\begin{cases}n^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}&\text{for }1\leq p<q<\infty,\\
n^{-1/p}\cdot\sqrt{\log n}&\text{for }1\leq p<q=\infty.\end{cases}

(26)

These bounds were obtained by studying the average case setting
with a truncated Gaussian measure centered around the origin.
By studying an average case setting
for a truncated Gaussian mixture with centers around
k
-sparse vectors,
we now obtained lower bounds that not only exhibit a dependence on
m
,
but also match the improved upper bounds
from our recent paper
[
6
]
.
The older
m
-independent bounds (
26
)
hold for the entire parameter range
1\leq p<q\leq\infty

and general randomized algorithms, including adaptive ones,
while our new
m
-dependent bounds
only cover the case of non-adaptive randomized methods for
p\geq 2
.

We believe that the same average case setting with Gaussian mixtures
can be used to prove lower bounds for adaptive randomized methods
in the regime
2\leq p<q\leq\infty
,
namely by employing techniques from information theory in the spirit of
[
10
]

(a paper that studies lower bounds for adaptive randomized methods
in the related field of sparse recovery).
However, if we want to cover the case
1\leq p<2
,
we expect that a more complicated Gaussian measure is needed.
In detail, instead of a random vector
\boldsymbol{X}
as defined in (
5
),
we consider a random vector of the shape

\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{u}_{I}+\sigma P\boldsymbol{Z}_{m},

where
P
is a random projection onto
2n
coordinates independent of
I
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}
.
(Without such a random projection, for
p<2
the scaling factor
\sigma
would need to be too small
in order to control the truncation probability.)
A measure like this has been studied in
[
4
]
, yet only for
1
-sparse vectors
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
.

Appendix A
Gaussian measures in high dimensions

Throughout this section
let
\boldsymbol{Z}
denote a standard Gaussian random vector with values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
For
1\leq p<\infty
we have

\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot m^{1/p}\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}\leq\sqrt{p}\cdot m^{1/p},

(27)

see Pisier
[
9
, Lem 4.14]
for the initial result and
[
7
, Lem A.9]
for explicit constants.
With
p=\log m
, for
m\geq e
, we obtain

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{\infty}\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}\leq e\cdot\sqrt{\log m}

(28)

The following lemma is about concentration of Gaussian vectors around the expectation of their norm,
see
[
9
, pp. 180/181]
, or
[
1
, Lem 2.1.6]

for a more general statement.
Here, we state it specifically applied to the
\ell_{p}
-norm with
p\geq 2
.

Lemma A.1
.

For
2\leq p<\infty
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}>(1+t)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}m^{2/p}}{\pi}\right),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}<(1-t)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}m^{2/p}}{\pi}\right).

Proof.

The result from
[
9
, pp. 180/181]
applied to the embedding
\ell_{2}^{m}\hookrightarrow\ell_{p}^{m}
states

{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}>(1+t)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}\bigr)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}\varrho^{2}}{2}\right),

where

\varrho:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}/\|\id\|_{\ell_{2}^{m}\to\ell_{p}^{m}}
.
The same bound holds for the second probability.
For
p\geq 2
we have the embedding constant
\|\id\|_{\ell_{2}^{m}\to\ell_{p}^{m}}=1
,
so by the first inequality of (
27
)
we have
\rho\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot m^{1/p}
,
which gives

\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}\varrho^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}m^{2/p}}{\pi}\right).

This bounds the right-hand side of both inequalities as stated.
∎

In particular, for
p=2
and
t=1-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\sqrt{m}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{2}<(1-t)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\pi}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{2}<(1-t)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}m}{\pi}\right)\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}1.

(29)

Exploiting that the last expression in (
29
)
is monotone in
m
, we find

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{2}\geq\tfrac{\sqrt{m}}{2}\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\qquad\text{for }m\geq 16.

(30)

This estimate will be crucial in the proof of Lemma
2.1
.
The next case is important to bound the truncation probability of the measure
we define in Section
2.1
.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
2\leq p<\infty
.
For
\epsilon>0
put
\sigma:=\sigma(\epsilon)=\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{p}}\cdot m^{-1/p}
.
Then

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}>\epsilon\right)<\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2/p}}{\pi}\right).

Proof.

We apply Lemma
A.1
with
t=1
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\sigma\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}>2\sqrt{p}\,m^{1/p}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:GaussianExBound}}}{{\leq}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}>2\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}\|_{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2/p}}{\pi}\right).\qed

Appendix B
Some coding theory

We apply a result from coding theory about the existence of codes
known as Gilbert-Varshamov bounds, compare
[
2
, Lem 3.1, Sec A]
.
For a general introduction to coding theory,
see, for instance
[
11
]
.
For words
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in[q]^{k}
of length
k
written in an alphabet of length
q
,
their
Hamming distance
is defined as

\Delta(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\#\{i\in[k]\colon u_{i}\neq v_{i}\}.

For a subset
T\subseteq[q]^{k}
, called a
codebook
,
we define its
minimal Hamming distance
by

\Delta(T):=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in T\\
\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{v}\end{subarray}}\Delta(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}).

The following lemma is standard in coding theory.
It is based on a special case of
[
2
, Claim A.1]
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
q\geq 6
and
k
be natural numbers.
Then there exists a codebook
T\subseteq[q]^{k}

with minimal Hamming distance
\Delta(T)\geq\frac{k}{2}
and size

\#T>q^{k/10}.

Proof.

We define
r:=\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor
.
For
\boldsymbol{u}\in[q]^{k}
we define the Hamming ball of radius
r
by

B_{r}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in[q]^{k}\colon\Delta(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\leq r\}\,.

By the definition of
r
,
for
\boldsymbol{v}\in[q]^{k}\setminus B_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})
we have
\Delta(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\geq\frac{k}{2}
.
Assume that we have a codebook
T\subseteq[q]^{k}
with
\Delta(T)\geq\frac{k}{2}
that satisfies

\#T<q^{k}/\#B_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})
,
then there exists
\boldsymbol{v}\in[q]^{k}\setminus\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in T}B_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})

and the larger codebook still satisfies
\Delta(T\cup\{\boldsymbol{v}\})\geq\frac{k}{2}
.
This packing argument implies
that there exist
T\subseteq[q]^{k}
with
\Delta(T)\geq\frac{k}{2}
and

\#T\geq\frac{q^{k}}{\#B_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})}\,.

(31)

We estimate the cardinality of a single Hamming ball in
[q]^{k}
:

\displaystyle\#B_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{k}{j}(q-1)^{j}\leq(q-1)^{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{k}{j}\leq(q-1)^{r}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}}_{=(1+1)^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq q^{k/2}\cdot 2^{k}.

Together with (
31
) we have

\#T\geq q^{k/2}\cdot 2^{-k}=q^{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\log_{q}2\right)k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle[q\geq 6]}}{{>}}q^{k/10}.\qed

We use this result to prove a lower for a packing number in
W=W_{k}^{m}
as defined in (
4
).
The following is adapted from
[
2
, Lem 3.1]
.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
k,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
m\geq 3k
and
p\in[1,\infty]
. Then there exists a set
S\subseteq W_{k}^{m}
with cardinality

\#S\geq\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{m}{k}\right\rfloor\right)^{k/10}>\left(\frac{m}{k}\right)^{k/10}.

that has a minimal
\ell_{p}
-separation of

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\in S\\
\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{v^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\|_{p}\geq k^{1/p}.

Proof.

Consider the alphabet

A=\{\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{s},-\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,-\boldsymbol{e}_{s}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{s}

for
s:=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{k}\right\rfloor

with
q:=\#A=2s\geq 6
letters.
An element
\boldsymbol{u}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in A^{k}
corresponds to a concatenated vector

\boldsymbol{v}=f(\boldsymbol{u}):=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{m-ks})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},

where
f\colon A^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is injective.
Note that
\boldsymbol{v}
has exactly
k
non-zero entries,
and if two codewords
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in A^{k}
satisfy
\Delta(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\geq\frac{k}{2}

then the corresponding vectors
f(\boldsymbol{u}),f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

satisfy
\|f(\boldsymbol{u})-f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\|_{1}\geq k
.
Indeed, if two entries of
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}
differ,
a_{j}\neq a_{j}^{\prime}
,
then there are two possibilities for the related coordinates
i\in I_{j}:=[js]\setminus[(j-1)s]

of
\boldsymbol{v}=f(\boldsymbol{u})
and
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})
:

•

There exists
i^{\ast}\in I_{j}
with
|v_{i^{\ast}}-v_{i^{\ast}}^{\prime}|=2
,
and
v_{i}=v_{i}^{\prime}=0
for all
i\in I_{j}\setminus\{i^{\ast}\}
.

•

There are two indices
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
in
I_{j}
where
|v_{i_{1}}|=|v_{i_{2}}^{\prime}|=1
,
further,
v_{i}=0
for all
i\in I_{j}\setminus\{i_{1}\}
,
as well as
v_{i}^{\prime}=0
for all
i\in I_{j}\setminus\{i_{2}\}
.

Along the same lines,
one can prove
\|f(\boldsymbol{u})-f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\|_{p}^{p}\geq\frac{k}{2}\cdot\min\{2^{p},1^{p}+1^{p}\}=k

for
p\in[1,\infty)
.
For
p=\infty
, the bound
\|f(\boldsymbol{u})-f(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\geq 1

follows directly from a single differing entry.
By Lemma
B.1
, there exists a set
T\subseteq A^{k}

with minimal Hamming distance
\frac{k}{2}
and
\#T>q^{k/10}
.
The set
S:=f(T)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
of the corresponding vectors with entries in
\{-1,0,1\}

then satisfies the assertion.
∎

Corollary B.3
.

Let
k,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
m\geq 3k
and consider the set
W_{k}^{m}
as in (
4
).
For
\boldsymbol{v}\in W_{k}^{m}
and
p\in[1,\infty]
define the set

U(\boldsymbol{v}):=\left\{\boldsymbol{w}\in W_{k}^{m}\colon\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p}<\tfrac{1}{2}\,k^{1/p}\right\}.

Then it holds

\frac{\#W_{k}^{m}}{\#U(\boldsymbol{v})}>\left(\frac{m}{k}\right)^{k/10}.

Proof.

All sets
U(\boldsymbol{v})
for
\boldsymbol{v}\in W_{k}^{m}
have the same cardinality.
Let
S\subset W_{k}^{m}
be a set with minimal
\ell_{p}
-separation
k^{1/p}

as in Lemma
B.2
.
For this we obtain the following disjoint union:

\bigsqcup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in S}U(\boldsymbol{v})\subseteq W_{m}^{k}.

Hence,
\#S\cdot\#U(\boldsymbol{v})\leq\#W_{m}^{k}
for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in W
.
Rearranging for
\#S
and using Lemma
B.2
proves the assertion.
∎

Remark B.4
.

For
p=\infty
, the corollary above simplifies with
\#U_{\boldsymbol{v}}=1
,
hence, only an estimate for the cardinality of
W_{k}^{m}
is required.
Classical bounds give

\left(\frac{2m}{k}\right)^{k}\leq\#W_{k}^{m}=\binom{m}{k}2^{k}\leq\left(\frac{2em}{k}\right)^{k}.
