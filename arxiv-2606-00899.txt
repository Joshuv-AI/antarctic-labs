Well-posedness of a first-order formulation for fractionally damped nonlinear acoustics
Pascal Lehner, Mostafa Meliani

Abstract
In this work, we study the well-posedness of a quasilinear first-order-in-time system with memory arising in nonlinear acoustics. The model features a memory kernel describing the fractional damping and covers, as special cases, first-order formulations of Kuznetsov- and Westervelt-type equations. For completely monotone convolution kernels, we prove that the Westervelt-type system admits unique local-in-time solutions in bounded domains for space dimensions d ≤ 3 d\leq 3 , under homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, suitable regularity, and smallness assumptions. The analysis is based on energy estimates exploiting novel nonlinear coercivity properties of the memory terms. Under additional assumptions on the resolvent of the kernel, we show that the smallness conditions can be significantly relaxed. For finite sums of exponential kernels our results generalize to the Kuznetsov-type system. Additionally, we show that the inviscid case (absence of memory kernel) in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} can be treated by standard hyperbolic arguments in the case of the Kuznetsov-type system.

Introduction

We study an initial boundary value problem for a quasilinear hyperbolic system with damping memory effects and establish well-posedness for special cases of the system modeling fractionally damped nonlinear acoustic waves. The general system of interest in this paper is

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}A_{0}u_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}(u,x)u_{x_{j}}-\mathcal{J}\mathbin{\ast}\Delta u=g&\mathrm{on}\qquad(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
u(0)=u_{0}&\mathrm{on}\qquad\Omega,\\
u=0&\mathrm{on}\qquad(0,T)\times\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

posed on the space–time cylinder
(0,T)\times\Omega
, where
T>0
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open, sufficiently smooth domain. The unknown is
u=(p,v)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
, with scalar pressure
p
and velocity vector

v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The matrix
A_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{(1+d)\times(1+d)}
is symmetric positive definite and independent of space and time, and the flux is encoded by

A_{j}(u,x)=A_{j}(p,v,x)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\vartheta v_{j}&(1+\alpha(x)+\lambda p)e_{j}^{\top}\\
(1+\beta(x)+\mu p)e_{j}&\kappa e_{j}v^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(1+d)\times(1+d)},

(1.2)

where
e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the canonical
j
-th unit vectors for
1\leq j\leq d
. The parameters
\kappa,\lambda,\mu,\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lambda\neq\vartheta
govern the nonlinear couplings, while
\alpha,\beta
are space-dependent coefficients. A physical choice of the parameters is described in Appendix
A
in (
A.28
). The source
g:(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
and the initial datum
u_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
are given. The operator
\mathbin{\ast}
denotes convolution in time, i.e.,
(\mathcal{J}\mathbin{\ast}\Delta u)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{J}(t-s)\,\Delta u(s)\,ds
, with
\mathcal{J}\in L^{1}(0,T)^{1+d}
a given memory kernel acting componentwise.
For simplicity, we impose homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
u=0
on
(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
.

Observe that the matrix in (
1.2
) is generally not symmetric, a property typically required for proving well-posedness of first-order hyperbolic systems, see
[
29
]
. We overcome this difficulty by symmetrizing the system (
1.1
) using a suitably chosen multiplicative matrix
S
; see Section
3.2
for details.
This symmetrization is crucial for our results when
\mathcal{J}\in L^{1}(0,T)^{1+d}
.
In contrast, when
\mathcal{J}=\delta_{0}
(with
\delta_{0}
denoting the Dirac measure at
t=0
) well-posedness of the general system (
1.1
) is shown in
[
23
]
and relies on the parabolicity of the system exploiting regularity from the Laplacian
-\Delta
. For a general kernel
\mathcal{J}\in L^{1}(0,T)^{1+d}
, the difficulties in establishing the well-posedness of (
1.1
) stem from the fact that less regularity can be inferred from the non-local damping term. More details are given in Section
3
.

Within the above framework, we establish two types of well-posedness results.

•

Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
, valid for the class of fluxes with
\mu=\kappa=0
and for completely monotone kernels
\mathcal{J}
, state that the problem (
1.1
) is well posed in a bounded domain for

under natural regularity assumptions together with a smallness condition on the data. This is the main part of the paper and the proofs use novel energy estimates (see Theorem
3.1
) and nonlinear coercivity estimates for
\mathcal{J}
(see Lemmas
1.1
,
1.2
).

•

Theorem
1.5
covers the inviscid case
\mathcal{J}=0
with
\vartheta=\kappa
on the whole space
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for arbitrary dimensions

. It establishes local-in-time well-posedness relying on standard hyperbolic techniques developed in
[
29
]
.

The general system (
1.1
) is linked to several classical models in (nonlinear) acoustics. Setting
\alpha=\beta=0
yields a first-order-in-time formulation of Kuznetsov’s equation; see
[
23
, Rem. 2.4]
. If, in addition,
\mu=\kappa=0
, the system reduces to a Westervelt-type equation in first-order-in-time form, see Appendix
A
for details. Moreover, taking
\lambda=\vartheta=0
gives a linear fractionally damped wave equation. Finally, setting
\mathcal{J}=0
leads to the first-order-in-time formulation of linear, lossless acoustic wave propagation

\left\{\begin{aligned} p_{t}+\nabla\cdot v&=g_{p},\\
v_{t}+\nabla p&=g_{v}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.3)

In many applications, the acoustic damping and nonlinear coefficients are typically small but generally not negligible; see, e.g.,
[
17
]
for a review of nonlinear acoustic wave propagation and the physical relevance of strongly damped nonlinear models such as Kuznetsov’s or Westerwelt’s equations.

1.1
Motivation of the model

Classical nonlinear acoustics models are typically derived from the Navier–Stokes equations, complemented with constitutive relations such as a Newtonian fluid stress tensor for the acoustic medium and Fourier’s law of heat conduction; see, e.g.,
[
17
,
39
]
. However, these assumptions imply infinite propagation speed and predict viscous losses that increase quadratically with frequency, which is inconsistent with observations in medical ultrasound
[
19
, §5.1]
. To address these shortcomings, two approaches have been developed. The first, introduced by Gurtin and Pipkin
[
16
]
, modifies the heat flux, while the second incorporates power laws into the viscoelastic stress tensor; see, e.g.,
[
19
]
.
These approaches lead to second-order nonlinear acoustic models featuring fractional damping terms as derived in, e.g.,
[
18
,
41
]
.

Motivated by the suitability of first-order systems for the development of efficient numerical schemes, we consider the recently proposed model

\left\{\begin{aligned} p_{t}+(1+\alpha+\lambda p)\nabla\cdot v-\mathcal{K}*\Delta p+\nabla p\cdot v&=0,\\
v_{t}+(1+\beta)\nabla p-\mathcal{V}*\Delta v+\frac{1}{2}k\nabla(|v|^{2}-p^{2})&=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.4)

with
k\in\{0,1\}
,
which is derived and analyzed in
[
23
]
for the case
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{V}=\delta_{0}
. The system (
1.4
) is in dimensionless form and can be obtained from the general system (
1.1
) by setting
\kappa=\mu=k
and
\vartheta=1
.
A derivation of (
1.4
) with
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{V}\neq\delta_{0}
is provided in Appendix
A
.
The model incorporates fractional damping through stress-strain relations and heat flux laws using separate memory kernels in the mathematical formulation. The kernel
\mathcal{K}
corresponds to the heat conduction law, while
\mathcal{V}
represents a generalized stress-strain relation modeling viscoelasticity.

Related mathematical literature

The well-posedness analysis of the fractionally damped Westervelt equation in pressure form (which can be derived from (

p_{tt}-\Delta p-\mathcal{K}*\Delta p_{t}=\lambda(p^{2})_{tt}

Here,

is a nonlinearity parameter. In
[

, a limiting analysis as

is performed, recovering the lossless Westervelt model. Fractional versions of Kuznetsov and Blackstock were studied in
[

]
.

Another related model is the fractional Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson equation, arising from incorporating thermally relaxed heat flux laws of Maxwell–Cattaneo and Compte–Metzler type
[

,
22
]
. These models were first derived in
[

]
and their well-posedness and singular-limit analyses have been carried out in
[

,
26
,

]
. A global existence result has been recently established in
[
33
]
exploiting the concept of strongly positive kernels similarly to
[
5
,
38
]
.

In the broader acoustics and viscoelastic wave literature, much work has been done in the study of hyperbolic (second-order and third-order) equations with smooth integrable kernels;
we refer the reader to, e.g.,
[
8
,
11
,
30
]
.
In contrast, the present work relies on complete monotonicity assumptions (see Assumptions
1
–
2
below), enabling us to cover large families of relevant kernels for applications; see Section

.

While the aforementioned well-posedness results for fractionally damped nonlinear acoustics models concern second-order-in-time (e.g., Westervelt or Kuznetsov) and third-order-in-time (Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson) equations, fewer results exist for first-order formulations.
To the best of our knowledge, the only such result is due to
[
9
]
, where well-posedness is established for a space-fractionally damped first-order nonlinear acoustic model.
Our work seeks to fill this gap in the analysis for first-order time-fractional models.

1.3
Examples of relevant kernels

Although the Abel kernel of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\mathcal{J}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\quad\mathcal{J}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}t^{\alpha-1},

(1.6)

is our main example of interest as it is used in the definition of Caputo–Djrabashyan fractional derivatives, see
[
40
]
, the assumptions we impose on the kernels (see Assumptions
1
–
2
below) are sufficiently general to include a broad class of kernels. In particular, they cover kernels arising in the modeling of acoustic and viscoelastic phenomena such as

•

the exponential kernel

\mathcal{J}(t)=\beta\mathrm{e}^{-\beta t},\qquad\beta>0.

(1.7)

•

the exponentially regularized Abel kernel, along the lines of that found in
[
35
]
,

\mathcal{J}(t)=\frac{t^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta t}}{\Gamma(\alpha)},\qquad 0<\alpha<1,\,\beta>0.

(1.8)

•

the fractional Mittag-Leffler kernels, encountered in the study of fractional second order wave equations in complex media in
[
24
]
,

\mathcal{J}(t)=\frac{t^{\beta-1}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}E_{\alpha,\,\beta}\left(-t^{\alpha}\right),\qquad 0<\alpha\,{\leq}\,\beta<1,

(1.9)

where
E_{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)}
is the two-parametric Mittag-Leffler function.

•

the polynomially decaying kernel

\mathcal{J}(t)=\frac{1}{(1+t)^{q}},\qquad q>0

(1.10)

arising in viscoelasticity, see, e.g.,
[
36
]
.

Here,
\Gamma:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\,t\mapsto\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{t-1}\mathrm{e}^{-s}\,\,\textup{d}{s}
stands for the Gamma function. Note that all kernels should be scaled in a physically meaningful way.

1.4
Main results

We state our well-posedness results under the hypothesis that the vector-valued memory kernel
\mathcal{J}
is completely monotone on
(0,T)
in the sense that

(-1)^{n}\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\mathcal{J}(t)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\text{ and a.e.\ }t\in(0,T),

(1.11)

where the inequality is understood componentwise and derivatives may be taken in the distributional sense. Informally, complete monotonicity means that
\mathcal{J}
is non-negative and non-increasing, decaying rapidly at short times and more slowly at large times.

We formulate two nested sets of assumptions, leading to two distinct well-posedness theorems. Under the first (weaker) assumption, well-posedness is obtained at the expense of stronger smallness conditions on the data. The second (stronger) assumption strictly refines the first and allows us to relax these smallness requirements. The assumptions are as follows.

Assumption 1
.

The kernel
\mathcal{J}:(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
in (
1.1
) lies in
L^{1}(0,T)^{1+d}
, is completely monotone, and non-constant or
\mathcal{J}=0
.

Assumption 2
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. In addition if
\mathcal{J}\neq 0
, suppose that there exist
T>0
and
q>1
such that the resolvent of first kind of
\mathcal{J}
defined by

(\mathfrak{r}*\mathcal{J})(t)=1\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }t\in(0,T)

(1.12)

lies in
L^{q}(0,T)^{1+d}
.

We note here that the resolvent
\mathfrak{r}
of a completely monotone kernel is in general a Radon measure, see e.g.,
[
14
, Ch. 5]
and the discussion in Appendix
C
. The assumption on the regularity of the resolvent associated with the kernel ensures that
\mathcal{J}
possesses a sufficiently strong (yet integrable) singularity at
t=0
.

Note that the before mentioned kernels (
1.6
) to (
1.10
) and
\mathcal{J}=0
all fulfill Assumption
1
within the given parameter range. For further details, see
[
20
, Cor. 3.2]
concerning the two parametric Mittag-Leffler functions. Assumption
2
is satisfied for the (regularized) Abel and Mittag-Leffler kernels (
1.6
), (
1.8
), and (
1.9
). It does, however, not hold for the exponential (
1.7
) or polynomially decaying (
1.10
) kernels, since their resolvents contain a Dirac pulse
\delta_{0}
due to the absence of singularity at
0
; see
[
14
, Ch. 5, Thm. 5.4]
.

For the well-posedness analysis, we rely on nonlinear coercivity of
\mathcal{J}\in L^{1}(0,T)^{1+d}
in the following sense. We denote as
\lambda_{\min}
the smallest eigenvalue of a matrix.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
V\in W^{1,\infty}\!\left(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{(1+d)\times(1+d)})\right)
be a diagonal and positive definite matrix. Assume that there exists a constant

, possibly depending on

\|\partial_{t}V\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{(1+d)\times(1+d)}))}<C_{K}\inf_{{s\in(0,T),\,x\in\Omega}}\lambda_{min}(V)(s,x).

(1.13)

If the kernel

satisfies Assumption

, then for every

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(V(s)\big(\mathcal{J}*y\big)(s),y(s))_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{1+d})}\,\textup{d}{s}\geq C_{\mathcal{J}}\int_{0}^{t}\|\big(\mathcal{J}*y\big)(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{1+d})}\,\textup{d}{s}

(1.14)

holds, where

depends on
\Omega,T,V,
and
\mathcal{J}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
C.1
.

Lemma 2.2

Let

The first equation is a consequence of Green’s formula; see

(2.7)

Our analysis is based on fixed-point iterations. The next lemma provides a general form of Banach’s fixed point theorem tailored to situations where contractivity holds only in a weaker norm than the one controlling the a priori bounds. Such a formulation is standard in hyperbolic problems; see, for instance,
[

]
and

We denote by

the (topological) dual of a Banach space

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

be Banach spaces such that
X\hookrightarrow Y
and assume there exist Banach spaces

such that

,

, with

separable.

Let

be non-empty, bounded in
X
, and weak-
\star
closed in
X
.
If
\mathfrak{T}:B\to B
is a contraction with respect to the
Y
-norm, i.e., there exists
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that

\|\mathfrak{T}(x)-\mathfrak{T}(y)\|_{Y}\leq\gamma\|x-y\|_{Y}\qquad\forall\,x,y\in B,

(2.13)

then
\mathfrak{T}
has a unique fixed point in
B
.

Proof.

We intend to apply Banach’s fixed point theorem to the metric space
M=(B,\|\cdot\|_{Y})
. Since
\mathfrak{T}
maps
B
into itself and (

) gives contractivity in

, it only remains to prove that

is complete.

Let

be Cauchy with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{Y}
. As
Y
is complete, there exists
y\in Y
such that

in
Y
. Since
B
is bounded in

and

is separable, the Banach–Alaoglu theorem (see
[
4
, Thm. 3.2.1]
) yields a subsequence

and an

such that

x_{n_{k}}\rightharpoonup^{\star}x\text{ in }X.

Because
B
is weak-
\star
closed in

, we have

.

On the other hand, norm convergence in

yields the weak-
\star
convergence

x_{n_{k}}\rightharpoonup^{\star}y
in

. Thus, since

, uniqueness of weak-
\star
limits gives

.
∎

3
Well-posedness analysis

In the special case
\mathcal{J}=\delta_{0}
, the well-posedness analysis of (
1.1
) is enabled through higher-order parabolic estimates. The higher regularity is needed to control the nonlinear terms via the Sobolev embeddings (
2.11
) valid for
d\leq 3
.
Working with the solution norm

E(u)=\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}^{2},

(3.1)

the ‘linearized’ nonlinear term
\|A_{j}(z,x)\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq E(z)\|\Delta u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}
, is absorbed for
E(z)
small enough; see
[
23
]
for details.

For general kernels
\mathcal{J}
, the situation changes, since non-local behavior of the damping term is introduced. Performing the same steps as in the parabolic case and assuming coercivity of
\mathcal{J}
in the form of (
1.14
), one obtains the solution norm

F(u)=\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\mathcal{J}*u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}^{2},

(3.2)

which fails to provide control over
\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}
needed to treat the nonlinear terms.

Kernels with sufficiently strong singularities can recover time regularity. For instance, in the case of Abel kernels (
1.6
), one gains control of
\|u\|_{H^{-\alpha/2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}
if
F
is controlled, where
H^{-\alpha/2}(0,T)
is the dual space of the fractional space
H^{\alpha/2}(0,T)
; see, e.g.,
[
12
, Lemma 2.3]
. However, we do not pursue this approach, as our goal is to establish well-posedness for a broader class of kernels. This leads to additional difficulties, since for very smooth kernels, no time regularity in
H^{2}(\Omega)
is expected. Consequently, we must work with higher-order energy estimates that do not rely on the regularity induced by the Laplacian, thus placing (
1.1
) in a hyperbolic framework.

For the Westervelt-type system, assuming
\mu=\kappa=0
, symmetrization of (
1.1
) is achieved by multiplying (
1.1
) with the diagonal matrix

S_{W}(u)=S_{W}(p,v)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{1+\alpha+\lambda p}{1+\beta}I_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(1+d)\times(1+d)},

(3.3)

while for the Kuznetsov-type system we refer to Section
3.2
for details. The symmetrization process introduces nonlinearity in front of the Laplacian operator when
\mathcal{J}\neq 0
. This difficulty is overcome by the nonlinear coercivity result (Lemma
1.1
) under suitable assumptions on
S
.

Note that while
\vartheta=\kappa
is physically motivated for (
1.4
), the parabolic case allows for a wider range of parameters; see
[
23
]
.

Before turning to the proofs, we briefly note that high regularity of solutions is standard in the theory of quasilinear symmetric hyperbolic systems; see, e.g.,
[
29
]
. In the present work, we assume
H^{4}
regularity of the initial data. This choice ensures sufficient regularity of the solution to control the nonlinearities and facilitates the cancellation of boundary terms under Dirichlet conditions; see, e.g., (
3.25
).

3.1
Well-posedness of Westervelt-type equation

In this section, we establish the well-posedness of the nonlinear problem (
1.1
) when
\mu=\kappa=0
. To this end, our strategy is as follows.
We linearize (
1.1
) at a sufficiently regular

3.1.1

Motivated by multiplication of (

4
Conclusion

In this work, we establish well-posedness for a first-order formulation of fractionally damped nonlinear acoustics. Since our analysis does not rely on regularity from damping terms, we symmetrize system (
1.1
) to apply energy estimates that exploit the resulting hyperbolic structure. The nonlinearities introduced by this symmetrization in the non-local damping terms are handled by carefully designed nonlinear coercivity lemmas. For the Westervelt-type system on bounded domains, our results hold locally in time for small data assuming completely monotone memory kernels, while for the Kuznetsov-type system we prove well-posedness in the inviscid case in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Future work will be concerned with relaxing the assumptions underlying Lemma
1.1
.
One direction is to weaken Assumption
1
, which is currently formulated for completely monotone kernels (
1.11
). We expect that the analysis of (
1.1
) can be extended to a broader class of completely positive kernels
[
6
, Def. 1]
, provided that the associated resolvents exhibit sufficient regularity (possibly after suitable regularization) to establish Lemma
C.1
.

A second direction concerns the diagonal matrix assumption in Lemma
1.1
. It remains open whether this assumption is necessary for singular kernels. A positive answer would enable well-posedness for the Kuznetsov-type system using the methods developed here.

Another direction is to study the limiting behavior of (
1.1
) as the memory kernels vanish
\mathcal{J}\to 0
.

Additionally, the study of system (
1.1
) with more general boundary conditions is of interest. In the case
\mathcal{J}=\delta_{0}
, well-posedness of inhomogeneous Dirichlet and slip boundary conditions is shown in
[
31
]
.

In summary, this work takes a first step toward first-order fractional-in-time models for nonlinear acoustics. It lays a foundation for future research on more detailed mathematical analysis and provides a solid basis for efficient numerical schemes, with potential applications such as parameter identification for (
1.1
).

[
39

13

\partial_{t}\varepsilon&=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\nabla}v+\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\top}v\bigr),\\

]

C.4.1
Counter example for coefficient with large derivative

The assumption that the time derivative of

To illustrate this choose

Thus, choosing

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}V(s)(J*y)(s)y(s)\,\textup{d}{s}=\int_{0}^{t}(6s+\frac{1}{2})(-7e^{-s}-6s+7)(1-6s)\,\textup{d}{s}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{e}^{-t}\left(\left(108t^{4}-180t^{3}+18t^{2}+7t+959\right){e}^{t}-504t^{2}-966t-959\right).

We show that there exists a

C.4.2
Counter example of (
C.9
) for non-diagonal coefficient

Even for positive definite
V
, (
C.9
) can fail if
V
is not diagonal. A counterexample is

k(t)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-t}\\
e^{-t}\end{pmatrix}\quad V=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\
-1&2\end{pmatrix}\quad v(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1-t\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Then, on the one hand, we have

Vv(t)\cdot(k*v_{t})(t)=(2-2t)\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}(-1)\,\textup{d}{s}=2(1-t)(e^{-t}-1)

(C.32)

and on the other hand,

\frac{1}{2}V\mathbf{1}\cdot(k*\partial_{t}(v\odot v))(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}(s-1)\,\textup{d}{s}=t-2+2e^{-t}.

(C.33)

Thus, inequality (
C.9
) holds if

2(1-t)(e^{-t}-1)\geq t-2+2e^{-t},

which simplifies to

-2te^{-t}+t\geq 0
, and ultimately gives
t\geq\log(2)
. Therefore,
the inequality fails for
t\in(0,\log(2)).

We note that this does not mean that Lemma
1.1
never holds for non-diagonal coefficient matrices, but it shows that it cannot be proven with the use of (
C.9
).
