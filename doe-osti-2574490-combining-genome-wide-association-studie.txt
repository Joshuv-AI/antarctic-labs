 1. An overview of the original project objectives and their completion within the
 project

(i). To deploy a large-scale experimental drought trial for up to 1000 unique genotypes of
Populus equipping the sites with controlled irrigation and drought treatments that are fully

COMPLETED

 2. Summary of scientific achievements within each task

Task 1 Identification of genetic loci controlling drought response using computational
approaching linking phenotype to genotypes – Genome Wide Association Study (GWAS).

A major part of this task was the establishment of a new field site, that went significantly beyond
what was described in the proposal, with additional tree genotypes, instrumentation and
monitoring. This field site was a significant achievement and was completed in the depths of the
COVID pandemic. As written in the proposal, six blocks were established, three that were
subjected to drought and three that were held at full irrigation. a 6.1-ha plot at the UC Davis
Plant Science Field Facility in Davis, CA (38°32′47.4″N, 121°47′32.7″W) (Fig. 1a), as described
previously (Taylor et al., 2024- see outputs below). The experimental layout of the whole site
consisted of drought and control treatments across adjacent fields divided into three distinct
blocks arranged in an incomplete randomized block design. The Populus trichocarpa Torr. &
Gray population used in this study was sourced from a range of latitudes exhibiting diverse
climate and rainfall patterns, representative of most of the species' habitats (38.9–54.3°N, 116–
128.7°W), and included a total of 1382 genotypes (Fig. 1a). P. trichocarpa cuttings were
received and planted in a glasshouse on February 1, 2020, a few weeks prior to the completed
lock-down of the USA and with no hope that the ‘planting team’ could arrive from ORNL. On
March 30, 2020, trees were relocated to a lath house to harden. Subsequently, 7628 trees were
transplanted to the field on April 10, 2020, with 782 unique genotypes in the drought field site
and 1382 unique genotypes in the control field site, reflecting the availability of viable cuttings. A
single row of P. trichocarpa border trees (not used for measurements) was established around
the experimental trees in each replicate block to reduce the effect of undesired edge effects.
This whole planting of over 8,000 trees (with border trees) was undertaken in a COVID-
compliant way, using a local field crew who worked in PPE throughout, with no COVID
transmission and successful establishment of the site.

A second part of the task was to identify and measure a suite of leaf traits that were
representative of drought tolerance, identified from earlier research. Eleven traits and two
indices were identified in the original proposal. However this was significantly extended such
that 30 traits and two indices were assessed as trait measurements were repeated to gain extra
rich data sets in Y2 of the project.

Details of the phenotyping campaigns are published in the Masters and PhD thesis listed below
in 3., and in the peer-review paper, Klein et al., (2025).

Task 1 was also concerned with identifying the GWAS hits from the extensive phenotypic data
set and this has also been fully achieved. Trait values and genotype means were transformed
using a rank-based inverse normal transformation (INT). Genome-wide associations were
conducted for all traits using genotype means for drought, control treatments, and their
plasticity. Genomic variation, including SNPs and small insertions/deletions (InDels), was
obtained from previous research (Tuskan et al., 2006; Evans et al., 2014). Associations between
genome-wide genetic variants and phenotypic traits were analyzed using Gemma (v.0.98.4),
incorporating a kinship matrix as a random covariate to account for population structure. Each
genetic variant was modeled using the equation y = Xβ + g + ɛ, where y is the vector of trait
values for each individual, X is the matrix of fixed effects (variants), β is the vector of fixed-effect
coefficients, g represents the random genetic effect due to population structure and relatedness,
and ɛ is the vector of residual errors. The statistical significance of allele–trait associations was
determined by the P-value of β. Variants were filtered to include only those with a minor allele

frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05. To identify putative GWAS peaks and address redundancies caused by
linkage disequilibrium (LD), alleles were clustered using PLINK with parameters: --clump-p1 1, -
-clump-p2 1, --clump-r2 0.05, and --clump-kb 50. The 50-kb clumping threshold was informed by
the genome-wide LD decay rate, which reached equilibrium at an r2 of 0.05 within a 50-kb
window. This process identified the most statistically significant allele for each LD group.

For visualization, clumped alleles were initially filtered to retain those with P < 1 × 10−4. Further
refinement was performed based on a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (P ≤ 0.05)
using a chi-squared test to evaluate the significance of all linked alleles within each clump
against a uniform P-value distribution. Genes located within 20 kb of the most significant variant
in each clump were selected for downstream analyses.

Task 2 Identification of cis- and trans-regulatory mechanisms via eQTN mapping

Task 2 was concerned with the utilization of RNASeq analysis to achieve wide-scale expression
patterns in a very large sample set. This approach has already been deployed by PI and Co-Is
and the group has extensive experience in the P. trichocarpa and P. nigra for field sampling and
RNA seq analysis. We were able to utilize ‘ early leads data’ from CBI that constituted over
3,000 samples from a different site with a 40% reduction in irrigation. RNA was extracted from
the samples and has been used as an important ‘proof of concept’ to cross check the GWAS
analysis from the UC Davis site. For example, in Klein et al., (2025) and Baliey-Bale et al., (in
prep) we are validating our gene targets from UC Davis, using eQTN data from this early-leads
site.

In addition to this, a further 3,000 leaf samples for RNA Seq was collected for the Y2
phenotyping campaign in September of that year. These data are in a pipeline for sequencing
with JGI following a team effort to collect, grind and extract the tissue form the whole population
of unique genotypes in control and drought conditions. Overall, our RNA efforts constitute a
tremendous amount data achieved towards our overall aim to identify GWAS-eQTN targets that
has already been successful, as shown below.

Using this approach we identified a key trait for adaptation to climate – increased drought and
temperature, to be stomatal size. Following this, we identified a strong GWAS hit in the
population on chromosome 10, This allele was selected based on its significant association with
stomatal size and the climate of origin, suggesting its potential responsiveness to future climatic
shifts. We utilized downscaled future climate projections (10-min spatial resolution) from the
Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6), available through WorldClim v.2.1,
which, as described (https://www.worldclim.org/data/cmip6/cmip6climate.html), underwent
both downscaling and calibration (bias correction) against the baseline climate provided
by WorldClim v.2.1 Our analysis encompassed data from 10 global climate models (GCMs),
specifically: ACCESS-CM2, CMCC-ESM2, EC-Earth3-Veg, GISS-E2-1-G, INM-CM5-0, IPSL-
CM6A-LR, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MRI-ESM2-0, and UKESM1-0-LL. We evaluated these
models across four Shared Socio-economic Pathways (SSPs): SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0,
and SSP5-8.5, at four future time intervals: 2021–2040, 2041–2060, 2061–2080, and 2081–
2100.

Initially, we compared future climate predictions to current climatic conditions (baseline) using
bioclimatic variables from WorldClim, the geographic locations of P. trichocarpa genotypes. We
performed t-tests, using the t-test statistic as a unitless measure of the severity of change for
each bioclimatic variable. This preliminary step established an understanding of the extent of
future climatic changes potentially facing P. trichocarpa.

To predict future allele frequencies, we adopted a machine learning approach using RF models
in R. This involved analyzing the SNP with the strongest correlation to stomatal size within the
identified locus on chromosome 10. Given the imbalanced genotype frequencies, we first
balanced the dataset through random downsampling. From this balanced dataset, we trained a
RF model to predict genotype frequencies based on bioclimatic variables. This model was
applied to both the current climate baseline and all future climate scenarios for each

Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from the RNA Seq dataset.
Evaluating GWAS results across traits after variant clumping and considering multiple testing
revealed a notable locus on chromosome 10. This region was significantly associated with
stomatal size in both drought and control treatments, and for measurements in both June and
September, as well as for abaxial contact angle, a proxy measure of cuticle wax. We found that
alleles in this locus explained 15.9% of the variance in stomatal size across all measured plants,
irrespective of treatment. This region contains a cassette of tandem 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase
(KCS) family genes, with KCS genes being previously linked to both cuticular wax and stomatal
traits.

Task 3

Validation of transcriptional regulatory mechanisms and construction of regulatory
networks.

Validation of GWAS hits was further analyzed by the construction of transcriptional regulatory
networks in the ORNL partner laboratory. Although we have shown the power of GWAS analysis
in identifying target traits and SNPs, GWAS can be limited in statistical power. We have
addressed this here in two ways (i) by improving the accuracy of phenotyping data (ii) by
developing an apporach using the construction of regulatory networks that utilizes RNA Seq
transcription data, phenotyping and GWAS hits. Some of this research is still being analyzed
and assess and prepared for publication. For improving the accuracy, speed, reliability and
reducing cost, we have employed UAVs. Remote sensing provides a fast, reliable, and cost-
effective trait measurement and biomass estimation alternative, enabling large-scale—also
known as High-Throughput Phenotyping (HTP) across an unprecedented number of genotypes,
thereby increasing the probability of detecting an appropriate ideotype for breeding and
selection and also targeting the genetic basis of traits. Although most HTP platforms were
initially developed for indoor use, the limited correlation between plant performance in
greenhouse versus real-world conditions has driven the widespread adoption of Unmanned
Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), equipped with cameras and sensors, for collecting phenotypic data
directly in field trials. The most common types of cameras and sensors for HTP are RGB,
Multispectral, Hyperspectral, Thermal cameras, and LiDAR, each suitable for a specific
purpose.

UAS-based Light Detection And Ranging (LiDAR) offers a reliable and accurate alternative
solution to extract physical properties of trees, providing a three-dimensional point cloud. LiDAR
is an active sensor, where a laser pulse is emitted toward an object, and the distances of
individual elements, e.g. leaves within canopy, are determined based on the backscattered
echoes from the surface and, if penetrable, from the inner layers. This technology has been
widely employed for biomass estimation in diverse settings, including forests, orchards, and row
crops. The accuracy of using LiDAR in these settings is usually remarkable. However, in poplar
HTP, where the goal is to measure the biomass of individual trees with maximum accuracy,
separating each tree from others is challenging due to the dense arrangement of commercial

 plantations. To improve the accuracy of height and volume measurements for individual trees,
 we introduced a new processing technique based on the Seam Carving method that can be
 used to accurately segment dense trees. Seam carving is an image processing technique
 initially introduced for content-aware image resizing, where a horizontal or vertical string of
 pixels can be identified based on an energy function with minimal effect on the content (the least
 noticeable path of pixels to remove). By corroborating LiDAR-derived data with traditional
 manual measurements as ground truth, we applied a genome-wide association study (GWAS)
 pipeline to analyze phenotypes captured through this high-throughput technique.

 These highly accurate LiDAR data have been used in GWAS analysis with multiple hits and
 gene targets for both UAV and manual yield targets used. Phenotypes were analyzed in multiple
 formats: raw individual block values; as best linear unbiased estimates, calculated using a
 mixed-effects model with block as a fixed effect and genotype as a random effect; as absolute
 and relative growth rates; and as drought response indices (DRI and YSI). Single nucleotide
 polymorphisms (SNPs) were filtered to retain those with minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05.
 GWAS analysis was conducted using GEMMA, a univariate linear mixed model incorporating a
 centered kinship matrix to account for population structure and relatedness among genotypes,
 which is used as a correction factor to control for background genetic effects. Quantile–quantile
 (Q–Q) plots were used to assess deviations from the expected null distribution of P-values. We
 established significant SNP-trait associations using a P-value threshold of P < 10-6, based on the
 Bonferroni correction method for multiple testing, P ≤ 1/N, where N is the total number of
 genome-wide SNPs. We selected a significance threshold of P < 10-6 to reduce false positives,

while still allowing the detection of meaningful associations across the diverse set of phenotypic
traits. The Quantitative Trait Loci (QTL) intervals were defined as 10 kb upstream and
downstream of each genome-wide significant SNP. The SNP with the lowest P-value within
each interval was identified as the peak SNP. This pipeline has been used extensively to identify
gene targets linked to traits in this population. Table 1 highlights a set of key gene targets and
their functional modules that are being taken forward for the regulatory network analysis. This
research is at advanced draft stage for manuscript submissions Jafarbiglu et al (In prep).

Task 4 Validation of key genes in drought response via transgenics.

As outlined in the proposal, early leads and targets for drought tolerance from on-going
research of WATBIO and CBI were taken forward during the initiation of this project and these
have proved to be a highly fruitful source of material to develop transgenic approaches. Medium
to long-term tree transgenic experiments are extremely challenging but despite this, during the
course of this project, in December 2023, a field trial (T1) was planted at UC Davis (Figure 6),
using the 717-1b4 hybrid was sued in collaboration with CBI researchers at ORNL, who
supplied material and University of Georgia who generated the constructs. Beyond the life of
this project, the second field trial of transgenic poplar trees is in preparation to be planted in
Sept 2025 (T2), that utilizes leads developed in the project. Both T1 and T2 and fully
randomized and blocked field experiments with full irrigation control, enabling the impact of
drought to be quantified and performance in water-limited conditions to be assessed. Beyond
the scope of this project, we have been joined by CBI researchers at two additional field sites in
Oregon and Maryland to enable us to test the genotype x environment interactions to be fully
explored. From our detailed knowledge of transgenic tree experiment, we believe this to be a
significant and impactful experiment, rarely matched and should produce highly innovative
outcomes in terms of papers and validation of targets from the GWAS analysis. For each of the
edited targets, both Knock-Out and Over Expression (OE) lines were generated, and three
independent transformation events for each edit was made, alongside empty vector control
trees. In T1, at least three replicates of each edit were planted, with guard trees. For T2,
material is still being produced but at least three replicates will be available for the trial. Early
assessments have shown that phenology traits (such as bud flush) and functional traits (such as

stomatal conductance) different between control and edited lines, suggesting that the material
may be better suited to water-limited environments although this remains to be fully elucidated
over the coming years.
