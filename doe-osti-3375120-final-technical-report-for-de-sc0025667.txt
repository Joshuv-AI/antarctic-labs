PROJECT OVERVIEW

The objective of this project is to understand how irradiation enables deformation‐induced
phase transformations in fcc metallic alloys. Low‐temperature deformation of face centered cubic
(fcc) metals and alloys can occur through several modes, one of which is martensitic
transformation, wherein fcc γ‐Fe austenite reverts to hexagonal close packed (hcp) ε‐ martensite
or body centered cubic (bcc) α’‐martensite. Irradiation is believed to enhance the tendency for this
transformation to occur, considering that irradiation similarly enhances other low‐temperature
deformation modes such as dislocation channeling and deformation twinning. However, the
mechanisms underlying the irradiation enhancement of martensitic transformations remain
unknown. Most studies on deformation of irradiated fcc alloys have focused on deformation
twinning rather than on the martensitic transformation, and have tended to simply ascribe any
martensites to the twinning associated with irradiation‐induced dislocation loops. Recently,
however, porosity has been shown to enhance the martensitic transformation in shape memory
alloys through surface energy effects. Thus, the role of both interstitial‐type (e.g. loops) and
vacancy‐type (e.g. cavities) irradiation defects on the phase transformation must be understood.
An altered tendency to phase transform can have severe implications on the performance of load‐
bearing materials exposed to irradiation, such as nuclear reactor structural materials. More
scientifically, using irradiation to tailor the austenite‐to‐martensite phase transformation can
facilitate materials design and innovation across numerous energy generation applications, through
microstructure optimization, thermal conductivity, lightweighting, electromagnetics, biocell
control, and geological exploration. Thus, there is a critical need to understand exactly how
irradiation affects and enables the deformation‐induced martensitic phase transformation.

This critical need will be addressed with an overarching hypothesis that the type (i.e. interstitial or
vacancy), morphology, and distributions of irradiation defects enable precise tuning of the
deformation mechanism. This project resolves the hypothesis through three scientific questions:

 1. How does irradiation enable the deformation mechanism to evolve from dislocation‐
 mediated or twinning, to martensitic transformation?
 2. How do the relative populations of irradiation‐induced vacancy‐ and interstitial‐type
 defects modulate the martensitic transformation pathway?
 3. How does the stacking fault energy (SFE) vary locally at irradiation‐induced defects, and
 how does this variation affect the deformation pathway?

The hypothesis will be tested using a multiscale approach that combines novel small‐scale
mechanical testing and in situ electron microscopy investigations with mechanics models that will
help us interpret and expand the parametric space of our experiments. Work will focus on alloys
irradiated with neutrons and/or charged particles intentionally selected to create tailored
microstructures that are dominated either by vacancy‐type or interstitial‐type defects, or some
combination of these extremes. We will utilize small‐scale mechanical testing techniques to
introduce deformation, which will then be characterized using transmission electron microscopy
(TEM). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations will provide complementary insight into
deformation mechanisms in the proximity of irradiation-induced defects.

ACCOMPLISHMENTS

1. IRREVERSIBLE TRANSFORMATION MECHANISMS

We conducted a series of irradiations on nominally Ni-20Cr (Alloy 690) and nominally Ni-20Cr-
10(Mo,Nb) (Alloy 625) to produce cavity-dominated (He ion irradiation), loop-dominated
(proton irradiation), or loop+cavity (neutron irradiation) microstructures. These irradiations – as
well as the unirradiated and irradiated deformation mechanisms – were presented in the DE-
SC0020150 Final Report, and are summarized in Figure 1.

  Figure 1: Summary of deformation-irradiation microstructures obtained by high resolution
scanning/transmission electron microscopy (HR-STEM) from Ni-20Cr-10(Mo,Nb) (top row) and
  Ni-20Cr (bottom row) for unirradiated, loop and cavity microstructures (neutron irradiated),
loop-dominated microstructures (proton irradiated), and cavity-dominated microstructures (He
 ion irradiated).

For a deformation-induced phase transformation, the energy landscape between the parent and
transformed states can be depicted along a transformation strain (horizontal axis), Figure 2. In the
initial unirradiated but mechanically strained conditions, the free energy of the Ni-20Cr-10(Mo,Nb)
parent (fcc) phase is slightly higher than that of the martensite phase. This indicates the favorability
of forming martensite in the unirradiated material as long as sufficient mechanical energy is
provided to overcome the kinetic barrier of frictional resistance. All irradiation types produce
defects that increase the free energy of the parent phase, which effectively raises the left side of
the free energy hump in Figure 2. While this would seemingly make the martensite phase even
more energetically favorable, the irradiation-induced defects also increase the frictional resistance,
making it more difficult to overcome the kinetic barrier for transformation. Loop and cavity
microstructures (neutron irradiation) provide greater free energy increase, while cavity-dominated
microstructures (He ion irradiation) provide greater frictional resistance. Consequently, in Ni-

 DE-SC0025667 Final Report – pp. 3

20Cr-10(Mo,Nb), the martensitic transformation is more mature in the neutron irradiated specimen
than in the He irradiated specimen under the same loading conditions. On the other hand, in Ni-
20Cr, the unirradiated material has free energy of the parent phase lower than that of the martensite
phase, hence the fcc phase is stable and martensite does not form. Upon irradiation, free energy of
the parent phase increases, making the martensite phase more energetically favorable. Although
frictional resistance also increases, the loading conditions must be sufficient to overcome this
kinetic barrier. Hence, the martensitic transformation becomes increasingly favorable in Ni-20Cr
under proton, He, then neutron irradiation.

Through this proposed mechanism, irradiation-induced increases in free energy compete with
irradiation-induced hardening to control the extent of the deformation-induced martensitic phase
transformation. Tailoring the type of irradiation-induced microstructural features present can
consequently influence the transformation. That is, since cavities contribute more toward free
energy than they contribute toward hardening, the presence of cavities can make the transformation
more favorable at constant load. Conversely, loops contribute more toward hardening than toward
free energy, which raises the kinetic barrier and thus hinders activation of the transformation. The
relative populations of loops and cavities in an irradiated microstructure will thus govern whether
the transformation is enhanced or suppressed at a fixed mechanical load.

 Figure 2: Pictorial explanation of free energy and hardening competition mechanism for
 deformation-induced martensitic transformation. Different irradiating particle types have
competing effects on increasing free energy of parent (fcc) phase and increasing frictional stress
 (Efr), which can change the energetic favorability of the martensite phase during deformation.

2. EXTENSION TO REVERSIBLE TRANSFORMATIONS

The generalized expression for the favorability of DIMT requires that the sum of the chemical free
energy change and interaction energy must exceed the critical free energy for transformation.
However, correlating the interaction energy (i.e., the external mechanical driving force) to the
propensity of DIMT is valid only for the stable α’-martensite phase. While the hcp ε-martensite
phase is inherently metastable, and thus does not follow this same relationship to DIMT, it
nevertheless plays a critical role in the DIMT pathway of irradiated materials. We therefore extend

the previous work to explore the development and stability of ε-martensites in the presence of
irradiation-induced defects.

2.1 Irradiation-Induced Pseudoelsticity Driven by Reversible Transformations

We characterized the nanoindented microstructure in a neutron irradiated (loop and cavity
microstructure) 316L stainless steel, Figure 3. Shear bands below the indent contain no evidence
of deformation-induced α’-martensite laths, but instead contain metastable ε-martensite. The ε-
martensites are often found in close vicinity of irradiation-induced dislocation loops (dashed
circles, Figure 3), suggesting loops may be initiation sites for ε-martensite. Geometric phase
analysis (GPA) maps reveal relative strain distributions in an HR-STEM image. The directions of
compressive strain at dislocation loops are parallel to or aligned with the longitudinal directions of
the ε-martensite laths associated with those loops. However, along these same strain directions, a
relatively large magnitude of tensile strain occurs between the ε-martensite laths.

The mechanism of loop-initiated ε-martensite formation begins with compressive strain at
dislocation loops, which provide the transformation strain required to initiate ε-martensite. The
direction of this strain (e.g., xx, xy, or yy) determines the growth direction of these ε-martensites.
To enable further growth of ε-martensite laths, a tensile strain along the growth direction is
necessary. Some ε-martensite laths subsequently participate in the reverse ε → γ transformation,
utilizing these existing tensile strains as a mechanical driving force. The reverse transformation
relaxes strains. Differences in reversibility of the transformation may be associated with the
orientation of the martensite lath relative to the loading direction or the size or orientation of the
initiating dislocation loop.

The practical implication of loop-initiated, deformation-induced transformation to metastable ε-
martensite transformation is the introduction of pseudoelasticity. This phenomenon is similar to
the reversible γ ↔ ε transformations responsible for the shape memory effect and pseuodoelasticity
in Fe-Mn-Si and Fe-Ni-Mn. The neutron irradiated 316L specimen herein shows ~37% recovery
of displacement; by comparison, polycrystalline multi-component Fe-based shape memory alloys
have room temperature recovery strains of only ~0.7–13%. That said, shape memory effects are
typically measured using uniaxial tensile testing rather than nanoindentation like in the present
study. In addition, near-complete pseudoelasticity requires formation of just a single ε-martensite
variant. However, activation of multiple partial dislocations, indicated by deformation-induced ε-
martensite on multiple {111} planes, reduces shape-memory effects. This suppresses the reverse
ε → γ transformation by causing stress concentrations at the intersection of ε-martensite laths.
Multiple equivalent Shockley partial dislocations may be activated to varying degrees during
reverse transformation, which consequently restricts the preferential motion of a specific type of
partial dislocation that is necessary to attain complete shape memory effect.

2.2 Toward a Unifying Mechanism

A defect map – compiled from data reported in the archival literature together with results from
the current project – may provide some guidance on the deformation-induced phase transformation
pathway that can be expected in fcc structures given an irradiation-induced population of cavities
and dislocation loops, Figure 4. The map shows deformation twinning at low defect densities, with

the propensity for phase transformation to increase along with defect density. Dislocation loops
alone tend to facilitate the γ → ε transformation, and with increasing loop density, the γ → ε → α՛
transformation. Increasing both the loop and cavity density, however, appear necessary to promote
the direct γ → α՛ transformation. Hence, a moderate population of dislocation loop-type defects is
necessary to facilitate γ → ε without permanent α’ transformation, which is a precursor to achieve
pseudoelasticity in fcc structures not conventionally known to exhibit shape-memory effects.

3. ROLE OF CAVITY INTERNAL PRESSURE ON TRANSFORMATIONS

3.1 Summary of 4D-STEM Measurements

The role of cavity gas pressure has been studied using four-dimensional scanning transmission
electron microscopy (4D-STEM). This targeted investigation focuses on the Ni-20Cr-10(Mo,Nb)
alloy irradiated with He ions and/or with deformation introduced through nanoindentation. Three
specific specimens are studied:
 • Unirradiated, deformation through nanoindentation
 • He irradiated, no deformation
 • He irradiated, deformation through nanoindentation

Before irradiation, 〈101〉 oriented grains were identified using scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) with electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), then marked with fiducial marks for tracking.
Nanoindentations were made on the identified grains either before or after irradiation.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) lamellae were then extracted from the grains/indents of
interest. Advanced nanostructural characterization was conducted using 4D-STEM at the
Molecular Foundry, to compute full-field lattice strain maps (εxx, εyy, εxy, and orientation Θ)
surrounding localized defect regions of interest, Figures 5-7.

A summary of the 4D-STEM analysis of unirradiated indented Ni-20Cr-10(Mo,Nb) is shown in
Figure 5. An area of interest containing two planar-like parallel features is selected for closer
investigation, Figure 5(a). The corresponding strain maps, Figure 5(b), present the relative
principal strains εxx, εyy, εxy, and orientation Θ – note that these strains are relative within the field-
of-view or mapped area. Strain maps show a distribution of both compressive (negative sign) and
tensile (positive sign) strains throughout the field-of-view. There is relatively little strain in
between the two planar features, with higher relative strain “above” and “below” these features.
The planar features themselves contain high compressive strain, but this strain appears in spots
along these features, rather than consistently across the entire feature. In the upper right corner of
the field-of-view is another high compressive strain feature. Masks are applied to streaks in
diffraction patterns, Figure 5(c), which reveal that the two central parallel features are twins or
stacking faults, while the upper right corner feature is an ε-martensite. These results suggest that
the unirradiated material deforms through dense networks of planar faults through compressive
strains (unsurprising since indentation is a compressive stress mode).

Nanoindentation load-displacement curves from the He irradiated Ni-20Cr-10(Mo,Nb) require
lower load to reach the same displacement as the unirradiated Ni-20Cr-10(Mo,Nb), suggesting that
He irradiation may lead to a softening effect. A summary of the 4D-STEM analysis of He irradiated
non-indented Ni-20Cr-10(Mo,Nb) is shown in Figure 6. Even without indentation, planar
intersecting stacking faults are observed (Figure 6(a)), which is consistent with the idea of
irradiation softening – i.e., cavities alone are sufficient to induce these plastic planar fault features.
The corresponding strain maps, Figure 6(b), show compressive principal strains εxx, εyy surrounding
the planar faults, with relative tensile strain in the surrounding matrix. The εxy strain and orientation
Θ, however, are both nearly negligible or nil across the entire field-of-view. Simulated nanobeam

diffraction patterns after masking streaks in the diffraction patterns confirms the planar features
are indeed stacking faults, Figure 6(c).

Upon nanoindentation deformation, this same He irradiated Ni-20Cr-10(Mo,Nb) specimen shows
no evidence of streaks within the 4D-STEM diffraction patterns. The relative strains are
heterogeneous, and there is no evidence of deformation-induced formation of twins, stacking faults,
or ε-martensite. Rather, the irradiated materials deforms primarily through localized dislocation
plasticity, as shown through the 4D-STEM results summarized in Figure 7.

3.2 Role of Implanted Gas Atoms

The cavities identified are He bubbles, containing a He/vacancy ratio of ~7-10. These cavities form
during low-energy irradiation (i.e., implantation), and act as sinks that facilitate trap mutation of
additional He during subsequent high-energy implantations. These clusters self-organize along
⟨111⟩ directions within stacking faults. The large internal pressure generated by the He-vacancy
clusters pushes interstitial atoms from close-packed planes into adjacent octahedral sites, which
changes the localized stacking fault energy (SFE) of the region. The combination of this localized
SFE change, together with the internal cavity pressure, enables stacking fault formation. These
stacking faults further act as preferential sinks or sites for accumulation of He-vacancy clusters
formed through further implantation, providing stabilization for this He-vac cluster–SF–He-vac
cluster configuration. This bubble-induced compressive strain directly counteracts and cancels out
the tensile strains required to propagate twinning and martensitic transformations, effectively
reversing the stacking faults during external mechanical loading.

SOCIETAL IMPACTS

Research accomplishments enabled by this project have shed light on the fundamental nano/micro-
scale mechanisms that enable irradiation defects to activate deformation-induced martensitic
transformations and other novel deformation mechanisms. Scientifically, this knowledge can now
allow researchers to pioneer new directions in irradiation-enabled materials design, i.e., creating
irradiated microstructures that enable precise tuning or tailoring of mechanical properties. Such
irradiation-tailored materials will become evermore critical amidst the growing need to deploy
nuclear fission and fusion energy to support artificial intelligence (AI), advanced computing, and
the energy-water-food-technology nexus. Moreover, results herein are directly relevant to
advanced alloys as well as ubiquitously-used materials such as steels and Ni-based alloys. The
advancements herein provide guidance to enhance the strength, ductility, and mechanical integrity
of such materials, thus having broad societal relevance to extending the safe service lifetime and
efficiency of advanced energy structures.
