 Title: Spatio-Temporal Dynamics of CO2 Capture by Sorbents: Multimodal, In-Situ and
 Operando Measurements

1-What are the major goals of the project?
This work will provide a mechanistic understanding of spatio-temporal changes in high-
performance MOFs during CO2 DAC conditions using a synergistic state-of-the-art in-
situ/operando experimental and computational approach. We assert that such an approach will
provide design criteria for DAC anchored in mechanistic insights. Here, we investigate changes
on two distinct timescales: 1) within a single capture-release cycle to identify critical features that
impart selectivity, capacity, and govern kinetics, and 2) over a high number of capture-release
cycles to identify processes that lead to reduced capacity and selectivity. We use multimodal in
situ spectroscopy, X-ray crystallography, electron microscopy and synchrotron-based analyses
coupled with computational modeling. We aim to investigate interactions underlying selective CO2
capture in MOFs and how they evolve during DAC operation as they undergo mechanical changes,
develop heterogeneity, yield unwanted chemical reactions, or accumulate impurities.
Our program consists of three objectives:
 1. To investigate, using operando methods, the chemical and structural changes of CO2 DAC
 sorbents during adsorption and desorption processes.
 2. To employ operando characterization methods to understand failure mechanisms of state-
 of-the-art MOF-based DAC sorbents and establish failure-specific accelerated aging
 protocols.
 3. To establish design rules for MOFs with high selectivity, capacity, and longevity for DAC.
This program will provide a mechanistic understanding of the chemical and structural changes that
occur in MOF-based DAC sorbents, shedding light on the design of robust sorbents that can
operate in challenging environments. The success of this project depends on the selection of a team
with a diverse background on porous materials, state-of-the-art in situ characterization, and
molecular modeling.

2- What was accomplished under these goals?
Through several intergroup collaborative projects, which have resulted in 30 publications over the
lifetime of the funding period, we have explored the stability and feasibility of several MOF
systems including: SIFSIX-3-Ni, CALF-20, Mg2dobpdc and Nb-OFFIVE. In addition to
demonstrating the relative viability of these sorbents fundamental understanding has been learned
about the competitive adsorption of CO2 and H2O in these systems. This understanding has been
furthered by the implantation and development of novel characterization techniques both in and
ex situ modalities. Synchrotron sources were used to study the in situ evolution of the framework
with exposure to multiple gases. Analytical electron microscopy was utilized to determine the
effect of local order and defects on the adsorption of greenhouse gases. Molecular dynamics
simulations were performed to explain fundamental guest host interactions in the frameworks.

Tailoring Hydrophobicity and Pore Environment in Physisorbents for Improved Carbon
Dioxide Capture under High Humidity:
 In this work, the incorporation of methyl groups into the triazolate linkers was
demonstrated to afford robust materials better tailored than the related material CALF-20 (without
methyl groups) to capture CO2 from post-combustion flue gas in the presence of water (Figure 1).
Specifically, three new MOFs were synthesized, denoted as NU-220, CALF-20M-w, and CALF-
20M-e, from the same metal salt and organic linker precursors but from different solvents. NU-
220 features a different topology than that of CALF-20, whereas CALF-20M-w and CALF-20M-
e are isostructural to CALF-20. In all three cases, the presence of methyl groups resulted in
significant modifications of the pore size and pore environment. Molecular simulations suggest
that the methyl groups sterically hinder the formation of hydrogen bonding networks by water
molecules and further repel water molecules from approaching framework Zn−N bonds relative to
the non-methylated analogs, supporting the experimentally observed increase in stability in the

presence of water for these new MOFs. Humid CO2 capture experiments confirm that
incorporating the methyl group results in a significant improvement in CO2 uptake at high RH
values, as CALF-20M-w and CALF-20M-e maintain about 20% of their initial CO2 uptake at 80%
RH, whereas CALF-20 loses all of its initial CO2 uptake at this same RH. As a result, CALF-20M-
w and CALF-20M-e show promise for CO2 capture in post-combustion flue gas environments,
while NU-220 excels among this series for CO2 capture in dry or low RH conditions. Overall, this
simple design strategy has afforded new adsorbent materials better suited for practical CO2 capture
conditions from humid post-combustion flue gas streams than CALF-20, overcoming a significant
drawback for this CO2 adsorbent and increasing the potential success of this class of physisorbents
in carbon capture technologies.

 The many-body (MB) potential energy function was implemented to investigate 1) the
effects of unit cell volume on the thermodynamic and dynamical properties of CO2 within CALF-
20, and 2) the co-sorption of water and CO2 within CALF-20, focusing on competitive adsorption
effects on their thermodynamic and dynamic properties. These computational studies provide
crucial guidance for the rational design of MOFs for carbon capture and provide insights that are
challenging to obtain through experimental approaches but can be systematically explored through
simulations.

 Molecular insights into CO2 confinement in different phases of the CALF-20 framework
were determined. Since the framework maintains the same stoichiometry across phases, the
differences in the thermodynamic and dynamical properties of CO2 can be directly correlated to
the structural differences of the framework. It was demonstrated that a smaller unit cell volume
leads to higher CO2 affinity and slower translational mobility within the framework. Unlike
experimental approaches where phase interchanges can occur, simulations allow these conclusions
to be drawn without ambiguity.

 Real-world applications of direct atmospheric CO2 capture using MOFs must account for
competition from other air molecules, notably water, due to its abundance and physical and
chemical properties. However, it is experimentally challenging to control the concentration of each
species, making it difficult to study their concentration-dependent thermodynamic and dynamical
properties. Nonetheless, concentrations can be easily manipulated in simulations, allowing the
exploration of the interplay among water, CO2, and the framework. The competition origin
between water and CO2 within the CALF-20 framework was elucidated by showing that both
species share preferential void regions and similar energetic affinities to the framework. As models
incorporate permanent electrostatic and polarization effects, strategies to increase CO2 affinity
while reducing water affinity can be inferred. Additionally, the structural rearrangement of water
and CO2 under the framework's confining effects was demonstrated, resembling their bulk
structures at high pressure. The presence of water is energetically favorable but entropically
unfavorable for CO2 capture. Moreover, water leads to faster reorientation rates and lower
translational mobility of CO2, while CO2 results in slower reorientation rates and lower
translational mobility of water.

Probing Structural Transformations and Degradation Mechanisms by Direct Observation
in SIFSIX-3-Ni for Direct Air Capture:
 By growing the largest single crystals of SIFSIX-3-Ni to date, we were able to determine
the correct symmetry of this crystal through SCXRD as well as determine how H2O and CO2 gases
can occupy the structure during competitive gas adsorption (Figure 2). Based on the herein
reported I4/mcm crystalline structure, the rotational dynamics of the pyrazine ligands are modeled
accurately, while the previous structural model did not sufficiently describe this rotation. Our
description of the crystalline lattice enabled the analysis of structural transformation on the long-
and short-range order of the framework. Structural envelopes derived from the DED analysis and
the LCA applied on diffraction data collected under a dry CO2 atmosphere revealed a significant
desorption of CO2 from the framework pores at 150 °C. We found that upon aging, SiF62– building
units are being displaced into the pores of the material, blocking the CO2 binding site and reducing
overall CO2 uptake. Water within the structure is hydrogen-bonded to the SiF62– building units and
coordinated strongly with metal sites and as a result is very difficult to remove from the structure.
However, with sufficient time and heat, the material can be nearly fully recovered. The material
cannot be fully recovered after being exposed to accelerated humid conditions, as the porous
framework partially degrades to small single layer nanosheets of edge-sharing nickel oxide
octahedra. Thus, the resilience of the material against humidity is to be reevaluated. It is possible
that this material could be post synthetically modified or used under different conditions in low
relative humidities. These findings provide fruitful insights into the development of more efficient
DAC sorbents under practical conditions, allowing for a step toward a more sustainable future.

Unveiling the Mechanisms of Abnormal Sorption Behaviors Triggered by Sorbent
Molecules:
This study explores the unusual structural dynamics and flexibility of the CALF-20 family of
metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), which are well-known CO2 sorbents, and the mechanism
through which these lead to highly unusual water adsorption isotherms. While typical water
isotherms are L-shaped and S-shaped, the methylated CALF-20 derivative reported by our group,
CALF-20M-w, exhibits an atypical, stepped S-shaped isotherm (Figure 12), which suggests the
coupling of a multi-step structure transition to the water adsorption. Unlike rigid MOFs, for which
adsorption isotherms are categorized into Type I-VI by IUPAC, flexible MOFs can exhibit more
complex emergent behavior during adsorption due to the cooperative interplay of the framework
and guest molecules. These guest-framework interactions can trigger phenomena such as gate-
opening, breathing, and swing effects. The isotherm observed for CALF-20M-w in Figure 12
isotherm is unconventional and deviates from the standard IUPAC classifications, showing a
unique, history dependent adsorption-desorption that arises from the specific interactions between
the MOF and the adsorbate.

Figure 12: Water sorption isotherms at 298 K and structural transformation of CALF-20M-w.
 Our ongoing research aims to elucidate the fundamental phenomena that underlie the abnormal
sorption behavior seen in CALF-20M-w at different levels of relative humidity. This will yield a
new perspectives on the interplay between water molecules and MOF structures. Using advanced
characterization methods, including in situ/ex situ single-crystal and powder X-ray diffraction
(XRD) analysis, along with adsorption isotherm analysis, we initially documented the structural
transformations of MOFs across different hydration phases and found that the topological
connectivity is retained during the transition without undergoing irreversible changes or
degradation. Initially, the water isotherm is Type I, but as the water vapor dosing increases, the
relative pressure surprisingly gives a negative increment after equilibration for an extended period
of time. This behavior implies a water molecule-triggered phase transition that cannot be assigned
to any standard isotherm classification. We hypothesize that identifying the mechanism through
which the guest molecule triggers the structural transformations is the critical step to understanding
highly unusual isotherm shapes, which deviate significantly from established classical adsorption
models.
 Our initial data demonstrate that the structural dynamics of MOFs during water isotherms are
both tunable and reversible by controlling humidity and activation temperature. The specific
reversibility and mechanism are heavily dependent on the methylated triazole linker, which allows
water to trigger the linker rotation during water sorption and stabilize the structure after
overcoming the energy barrier by heating process. Notably, this behavior was not observed in the
original CALF-20 case, highlighting how a small change in the triazolate linker structure affords
unique properties to the resulting MOF.

 The sorption behavior of samples with different activation conditions (Figure 13) indicates that
the dynamic vacuum activation at high temperature (>120 °C) results in the phase I structure,
while the phase IV structure can be obtained when activated under lower temperature (<100 °C),
showing the typical Type I water isotherm (blue line). Notably, phase IV demonstrates a 50%
improvement in CO2 uptake, increasing from 1.8 to 2.6 mmol/g at 15% CO2 concentration. We
hypothesize that the heating below a threshold temperature fails to overcome the energy barrier
required for the phase transition, which involves the rotation of the triazole linkers connect two
adjacent Zn centers. Despite significant efforts to collect each state crystal structure (I-IV), we still
face challenges in obtaining the intermediate structures when suspending the water isotherms at
turning points (between phases II & III). Conventional ex situ SCXRD measurements are not
able to identify the structural information of the transition state. We believe that combining in situ
structural analyses and adsorption isotherm measurements are needed to elucidate the mechanism
of this transformation.

Challenges and Opportunities: Metal–Organic Frameworks for Direct Air Capture:

Figure 14: Direct air capture (DAC) by metal-organic frameworks.
 In this perspective, we evaluated the development of sorbents used for CO2 DAC with a
particular focus on MOF-based sorbents, which have begun to show promise for this application.
In general, sorbents used in DAC are functionalized with amine groups because of the need for a
strong nucleophile to capture CO2 from extremely low concentrations (400 ppm). In addition to
amine-functionalized MOFs, researchers have also investigated the use of OMS-MOF that are also
capable of CO2 chemisorption, as well as MOFs that capture CO2 through predominately
physisorption interactions, such as ultramicroporous MOFs or flexible MOFs that undergo
structural changes upon contact with CO2. The majority of the reported MOFs for DAC are post-
synthetically functionalized (appended) with amine groups, while the remaining portion of studies
incorporated amines into MOFs by using amine-containing linkers during synthesis. Alkyl amine-
appended Mg-MOF-74 variants show promising CO2 capture performance under DAC settings,
and we highlighted the unique structure of Mg-MOF-74 and its cooperative CO2 capture
mechanism. We also evaluated MIL-101-Cr and its amine functionalized derivative, and similar
to Mg-MOF-74, the amine groups are attached to the OMS of MIL-101-Cr. Given the
mesoporosity and high surface area of MIL-101-Cr, the polyethyleneimine- and
tetraethylenepentamine-appended derivatives of MIL-101-Cr exhibit remarkable CO2 uptake,

especially at low CO2 concentrations. Similar to other liquid and solid amine-based sorbents,
amine-functionalized MOFs also face challenges of high-energy regenerations, urea formation,
and oxidative degradation. Amine-grafted MOFs are found to be more stable than the amine-
impregnated MOFs, similar to the case in other types of amine-based solid sorbents such as porous
silica and porous polymers. As a potential alternative to amine-based MOF sorbents, MOFs that
feature other nucleophiles such as hydroxyl groups show promise for CO2 chemisorption. In this
work, we only probed a small number of papers in the literature studying OH-functionalization in
MOFs for CO2 capture, and the performance of these materials is still inferior to that of amine-
based analogs (Figure 14).
 We have also discussed the potential of physisorbent MOFs for DAC. One of the highlighted
features of MOFs is their excellent ability to control the pore structure and functionalization. The
amenability of the pore design enables some MOFs to perform well under DAC settings. Several
MOFs, including the inorganic anion-based (SiF6) SIFSIX-Ni and SIFSIX-Cu, have demonstrated
high CO2 capture capacity in ultra-dilute CO2 environments. Results suggest that a combination of
ultra-micropores (<4 Å) and the presence of fluoride sites that exhibit high affinities for CO2 result
in extremely high CO2 capture capacities at low concentrations. We also highlighted a similar
physisorption-based MOF, NbOFFIVE-1-Ni, which shows very high CO2 capture capacities due
to the (NbOF5)2− building blocks and the small pore apertures. Generally, physisorbent MOFs
demonstrate very high stability over many CO2 adsorption-desorption cycles compared to that of
amine-based MOFs. Moreover, relatively lower regeneration temperatures are required due to the
reversible CO2 sorption properties. However, water competes with CO2 at the adsorption sites in
these MOFs, and under humid conditions that will be encountered in real-life DAC settings, these
materials experience significant decreases in CO2 uptake capacities. Nevertheless, employing
these MOFs in low humidity conditions, such as at lower temperatures, could mitigate this CO2
capacity loss. Understanding the CO2 adsorption mechanism for MOFs under humid conditions
provides valuable insight for future sorbent design.

Expanding the Library of Ions for Moisture-Swing Carbon Capture:
Moisture-swing carbon capture, a methodology for DAC utilizing ion-exchange resins, is a
promising approach for lowering energy use in DAC processes. The investigation then turned to
exploring other materials that can be leveraged for moisture-swing capture. A range of
carbonaceous and metal oxide nanomaterials was selected for their potential dual-functional use
in DAC (capture and conversion) as well as ease of synthesis or broad availability. These materials
were tested for thermodynamic and kinetic moisture-swing performance in a closed-loop setup for
testing carbon capture under changing humidity conditions.

To tease out the kinetics of this moisture-swing process, two approaches were developed: analysis
of the hysteresis curve of capture and release of CO2 from the sorbents, and quantification of the
continuous phase lag between the H2O and CO2 levels in the setup. The effect of loading carbonate
ions—the “active species” for moisture-swing capture—on the swing capacity was determined,
alongside correlating the surface area and pore distributions of the platform materials with their
moisture-swing performance.

This research in moisture-swing carbon capture has synergistic benefits with the broader thrust of
research: methodologies for quantifying and modeling DAC performance can be cross-applied for
MOF-based sorbents, providing insights into the effect of humidity on nanomaterials more

broadly, as well as potential mechanisms for exploiting humidity-driven capture and release in
MOFs.

Previous studies reported NbOFFIVE-Ni-1 as an exceptional material to capture CO2 even in dilute
concentrations. However, to be considered for industrial applications the material needs to be
stable over multiple adsorption-desorption cycles which has been a challenge for similar
framework materials in the past. To evaluate the long-term stability of NbOFFIVE-Ni-1 the
framework was exposed to 70 °C and 80% RH for several days.

Figure 18. Comparison of a) PXRD and b) PDF data of the pristine and aged NbOFFIVE-Ni-1 .
A comparison of the diffraction data of the pristine and aged material (Figure 18Figure 18 a)
reveals no significant changes in relative peak intensities or peak positions which indicates that

the crystalline structure was retained. Furthermore, the PDF data shows no noteworthy changes in
peak positions or amplitudes, verifying that the local structure was not affected by the humid aging
procedure. PDF and PXRD analyses indicate that NbOFFIVE-Ni-1 is significantly more stable
than the previously studied SIFSIX-3-Ni framework.

Behavior of potential DAC sorbents under accelerated aging conditions
We developed a standardized accelerated aging protocol in which we placed MOF sorbents in a
humidity oven at 80 °C with 100 % RH for 7 d, then probed their stability through quantitative
PXRD by using silicon as an internal standard to probe losses in crystallinity, N2 or CO2 sorption
isotherms to probe porosity, and SEM imaging. We investigated the promising CO2 sorbents
CALF-20, MFU-4l-OH, mmen-Mg-dobpdc, NbOFFIVE-1-Ni, MOF-808-X (X = TFA, AA, and
OH), after exposure to accelerated aging conditions described above and found that CALF-20,
NbOFFIVE-1-Ni, and MOF-808-TFA exhibit minimal structural changes under these conditions.

Computationally probing the mechanism of water adsorption in NU-1500-Cr
Commun. Chem. 2023, 6, 70.
We analyzed the mechanism of water adsorption in NU-1500-Cr using a combination of molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations and diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy
(DRIFTS) spectroscopy. We were able to monitor, at the molecular level, the development of
hydrogen-bond networks inside the MOF pores as a function of relative humidity using both
experimental and computational DRIFTS spectroscopy. Based on our simulations, we determined
fundamental principles to modulate water adsorption in MOFs, which is key to improving the CO2
adsorption capacity of MOFs in humid environments.

Investigating unexpected CO2 dynamics in MOF-808-X
J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2023, 145, 11195.
MOF-808 was originally identified in our studies as a platform to investigate sorption mechanisms
because we could install several different modulators to afford MOF-808-X (X = formate, acetate,
or trifluoroacetate) and tune the pore environment to yield structure-property relationships.
Through a combination of in situ DRIFTS experiments, differential envelope densities obtained
from synchrotron PXRD experiments, NMR spectroscopic analysis, and molecular dynamics
simulations, we found that the modulator identity plays a significant role in CO2 sorption, and CO2
chemisorbs by inserting between the modulator and the node, providing insight for the first time
into this adsorption mechanism for CO2.

Clarifying the mechanism of CO2 capture in a nanographene MOF
J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2023, 14, 4334.
In an external collaboration, DRIFTS data were gathered to determine the mechanism by which
photoreactive MOFs capture CO2 and to determine whether this led to the formation of carbonate.
These spectroscopic studies suggested that carbonate did not form in the gas phase but could
potentially form in the liquid phase.

Elucidating Phase Changes in a MOF with Exceptional CO2 Affinity
In this project, we investigated CO2 adsorption in CALF-20. We found that CALF-20 (denoted α-
CALF-20) undergoes a structural transformation following exposure to humid environments to
generate a new polymorph, β-CALF-20, identified through combined synchrotron powder X-ray

diffraction and pair distribution function (PDF) analysis. We have also synthesized a derivative of
CALF-20 with a methylated linker, denoted as CALF-20-M, which is robust and does not appear
to undergo structural changes upon exposure to dry or humid CO2, making it an excellent candidate
for DAC applications.

MD investigation into the mechanism of water adsorption in UiO-66 variants
In an external collaboration with the group of Prof. Martina Lessio (The University of New South
Wales), we gained computational insights into the adsorption of water in UiO-66 and its
functionalized derivatives as function of RH, revealing the role played by different adsorption sites
and functional groups in the adsorption mechanism. This study identified key interactions that
determine water adsorption in MOFs at various RH values, which is key to the optimization of
framework with higher CO2 capture capacity in humid conditions.

Novel analysis of moisture-swing sorbent materials
Another component of our work builds on the promising moisture-swing modality for CO2 DAC
by introducing several new anions into ion exchange resins, allowing for the cyclable capture of
CO2 under dry conditions and its release under wet conditions. This work not only sheds light on
ways to improve DAC, but also provides insights into the interplay between CO2 and H2O in
sorbent materials.

Reduced Si window thickness in EM allowing more detailed mechanistic studies
One of the limitations of in-situ gas phase electron microscopy has been the method of introducing
gas into the sample. The Dravid group was able to reduce the thickness of the SiN window from
50 nm to 10 nm, which enables the acquisition of much more spectroscopic and real space
information. With this new development, the ultrathin membrane will allow for dynamic kinetic
and in situ mechanistic MOF studies for CO2 DAC applications.

Improved insight into MOFs by EELS optimization
Electron microscopy of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have more recently shown strides in
minimizing electron beam damage through a variety of lower dose techniques. Electron energy
loss spectroscopy (EELS) is one often overlooked technique that contains rich information with
minimal dose. We examined the near edge structure of the EELS spectra to determine
compositional alterations in the bonding environment of the MOF SIF-SIX-3-Ni before and after
exposure to humidity, which has been demonstrated to change the occupancy of adsorbed gasses.
The changes in the Si edge fine structure point to the mechanism of degradation in SIF-SIX-3-Ni
as the bonding environment of the Si is changing from SiF6 to SiF4-O.

Exploration of MOFs with porous melanin coatings
We developed a robust coating procedure create synthetic allomelanin (AM) coatings from 1,8-
dihydroxylnaphthalne (1,8-DHN) precursors on a microporous and water-stable MOF, UiO-66.
We also extended this coating procedure to the borderline-mesoporous MOF-808. We found that
tuning the coating thickness affords control over the sieving separation behavior of the MOF
composite, which has direct implications for the development of robust sorbents for CO2 DAC
under practical conditions.

Evaluation of electron beam damage in ZIF-8

We have recently focused on understanding the nucleation and growth of a variety of MOFs in
situ using liquid cell TEM, where we can trigger the formation of MOF crystallites by mixing
precursor solutions and subsequently beginning to heat the mixture. We examined the effects of 2
different common liquid cell imaging modes, in two different beam conditions of ZIF-8 in 3
different solvent/media. These findings showed that irradiation of the MOFs leads to degradation
of the crystalline structure of the MOF prior to observing the “etching” or deterioration of the
crystallite morphology via S/TEM imaging and illustrate the importance of optimizing electron
beam flux (imaging) conditions towards studying these beam sensitive structures.

Improved understanding of structural degradation mechanisms using LCTEM
We have recently made efforts in attempt to study common phase transformation of DAC relevant
MOFs in the presence of humidity. One such MOF we chose to study via liquid cell TEM is
SIFSIX-3-Ni due to its nanoscale size and complementary experimental studies. We have not yet
been able to observe the phase transition or morphology change of this MOF in situ, but we have
managed to determine by postmortem TEM that upon evaporation of the solvent layer, SIFSIX-3-
Ni had undergone the phase transition. We aim to examine this and other similar phenomena
further by attempting to evaporate and capture water vapor within the liquid cell to monitor the
phase transition using a combination of liquid cell TEM and 4DSTEM, then apply to other relevant
MOFs.

Leveraging high-throughput sampling to accelerate understanding of sorbents
High-throughput characterization techniques have been recognized as efficient and cost-effective
methods for the facilitation of materials discovery. The high-throughput TEM sampling robot
SciTEM, combined with automated TEM imaging and electron diffraction, could be a
straightforward method to screen stability of MOFs after treating them under different conditions.
However, we have found that this workflow only applies to nano-sized MOFs with high degree of
completion, and most of the micron-sized MOFs studied in the accelerated aging project do not fit
this methodology very well. We are developing a variant of this technique amenable to micron-
sized materials.

Investigating structural changes in chemically modified MOFs
To understand the chemical and structural changes that DAC sorbents undergo during the
adsorption and desorption processes in relevant conditions, we selected diamine-appended
Mg2(dobpdc) and employed real time thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) coupled with Fourier
transformed infrared (FTIR) and gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GCMS). We did not
observe any amine loss below the regeneration temperature of 200 °C under humid and dry
conditions, indicating the stability of this MOF. We found that the optimal regeneration conditions
for this MOF after dry CO2 adsorption involve flowing N2 at 150 °C, which leads to 90% capacity
retention and almost no change in capacity after 15-20 cycles.

6- What is the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project?
The impact of this work will aid future work towards development of advanced electron
microscopy experiments. We hope to work further with students in the Dravid and Farha groups
to enable the nanoscale synthesis of MOFs for CO2 uptake, and then for further analysis on the
changes to the crystal structure (through 4D-STEM and diffraction) and the detection of water
vapor and/or carbon dioxide via spectral detection (through EELS).

These studies have provided invaluable molecular insights into the design of metal-organic
frameworks for carbon capture. By illuminating clearer pathways, our research suggests that
synthesizing new materials tailored for carbon capture could potentially be more efficient.
Moreover, while our focus has been on water, CO2, and their mixtures in these studies, our

research methodologies validate the success of the many-body formalism, which incorporates
fundamental laws of physics.

Our data-driven many-body MB-nrg models have become the reference molecular models for
molecular fluids, including water and carbon dioxide, as demonstrated by the increasing number
of citations of our original studies. This has effectively opened the door to realistic simulations of
CO2 and H2O adsorption in porous materials which enable direct comparison with various
experimental measurements, including heat of adsorption, vibrational spectra, and diffusion.

7- What is the impact on other disciplines?
The X-ray and electron microscopy methods developed here to characterize DAC processes can
be broadly applied to other framework, hybrid, and porous crystalline materials, including electron
beam-sensitive materials such as polymers, proteins, COFs, and hydrogels. The in situ X-ray and
electron beam techniques we develop will benefit separation and catalysis studies where
understanding long-term sorbent and catalyst stability is crucial, and the methodology for EBSD
on soft materials can be extended to a wide array of crystalline organic materials. Our experimental
findings can also assist in correlation with computational studies across groups.

Our many-body formalism provides a general platform for accurate representations of molecular
interactions, relevant to molecular adsorption, storage, diffusion, and sensing, fuel cell
technologies, batteries, drug design, and biomolecular recognition, with quantitative
characterization of CO2 and H2O adsorption being particularly key to efficient carbon capture
materials.

More broadly, this work is relevant to industries aiming to implement large-scale carbon capture,
as the molecular insights gained can help optimize material performance and efficiency. These
combined technologies represent a significant steppingstone for materials science in the design
and application of novel materials.

9- What is the impact on physical, institutional, and information resources that form
infrastructure?
This work has allowed for closer collaboration between a variety of groups, including PhD students
with the skilled microscopists working at NUANCE. Their collaboration has pushed the
capabilities of the PhD students involved with these projects.

The development of our MBX software (https://github.com/paesanilab/MBX) for many-body
molecular dynamics simulations involves close interactions with the San Diego Supercomputer
Center (SDSC). Our group is an active participant and contributor to The Triton Shared Computing

Cluster (TSCC) housed at SDSC, which provides central colocation and systems administration
for our cluster nodes (“condo cluster”) as well as a hotel service for UC San Diego groups with
temporary or burst high-performance computing (HPC) needs.

10- What is the impact on technology transfer?
Our data-driven many-body models are made publicly available through our MBX software
(https://github.com/paesanilab/MBX). MBX is interfaced with LAMMPS
(https://lammps.sandia.gov) and i-PI (http://ipi-code.org), and enables computer simulations of
generic molecular systems (including molecular fluids) in different environments (including
MOFs) and across different thermodynamic states, with unprecedented accuracy.

11- What is the impact on society beyond science and technology?
The continued development of our data-driven many-body methodology provides the community
with a unique integrated software platform for molecular simulations with chemical accuracy. This
will eventually enable the rational design of new materials for energy applications and
desalination, in silico screening and discovery of new drugs, and fundamental studies of molecular
systems of relevance for climate research. This progress is key to building a sustainable society
with an improved quality of life, which uses energy resources more carefully and takes care of our
planet.
