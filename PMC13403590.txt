Emergency superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery bypass under dual antiplatelet therapy: an extreme salvage procedure for a misleading moyamoya case. Illustrative case

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDSurgical revascularization is the cornerstone of treatment for moyamoya angiopathy (MMA), yet postoperative contralateral progression and the timing of surgery after acute ischemia remain controversial. The role of surgical cerebral revascularization as the mainstay therapy for moyamoya angiopathy (MMA) is widely accepted and supported by robust evidence.1,2 However, the postoperative course of unilateral (or strikingly asymmetric) MMA and the issue of follow-up are poorly addressed in the current literature. Moreover, current guidelines suggest respecting a 6- or 12-week minimum time interval from an acute cerebrovascular event before revascularization.1 Nonetheless, the emerging role of salvage bypass surgery as a viable treatment in acute ischemia allows the neurosurgeon to challenge this recommendation in the case of rapidly progressive clinical worsening.3 Hence, we hereby present a case of contralateral ischemia following cerebral revascularization in unilaterally presenting MMA treated with a salvage superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) bypass while the patient was on dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT). Although this illustrative case does not represent the typical postoperative course and management of MMA, it addresses controversial issues that may be faced in case of rare, yet critical, presentations. Besides, it underlines the need to further characterize unsettled topics in the management of MMA.

Illustrative Case

A 26-year-old otherwise healthy woman presented to our neurological department with recurrent transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), manifesting as transient paresthesias in her right hand and thigh, followed 2 months later by transient right-sided hemiparesis and aphasia. A multimodal instrumental assessment was performed (Fig. 1A– F) that documented a severe steno-occlusive internal carotid artery (ICA)–MCA disease with MMA features, left hemispheric hypoperfusion and cerebrovascular reserve impairment; on the right, only a very slight reduction of the distal ICA caliber was noted (Fig. 1E). A standard left microsurgical hemispheric revascularization via STA-MCA bypass was performed. The postoperative course was uneventful, and our postsurgical radiological protocol confirmed the patency of bypass, with a satisfactory flow seen on NOVA MRI (145 ml/min) (Fig. 2). Three weeks after discharge, she started to experience episodes of transient left upper limb weakness, related to physical effort and orthostatism. Repeated radiological assessment with brain MRI and digital subtraction angiography, revealed new ischemic lesions in her right hemisphere and severe stenosis of the right ICA apex–MCA with vessel wall enhancement (Fig. 3A– D). This atypical and rapidly progressive contralateral course, together with the evidence of vessel wall enhancement, raised the suspicion of an inflammatory arteriopathy, such as primary angiitis of the CNS or a postsurgical inflammatory vasoconstrictive response. CSF analysis was positive for a low viral load of herpes simplex virus 2 (HSV-2) DNA, without laboratory or clinical signs of immunosuppression. Laboratory tests for autoimmune and thrombophilic conditions revealed a heterozygous factor V Leiden mutation. Whole-body FDG-PET findings were negative. Antiviral therapy with acyclovir along with empirical intravenous high-dose steroids (methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 6 days, then tapered) were administered. Nonetheless, in the following 10 days the patient experienced further repeated stereotypical, left-sided sensory-motor symptoms, triggered by physical efforts and orthostatic position, despite the intensive intravenous fluid loading, DAPT, statins, and compression garments. Ischemic symptoms, initially transient and of brief duration (minutes), became fixed and an urgent MRI study showed occlusion of the right ICA-MCA axis with new right-sided watershed infarctions. An emergency right STA-MCA bypass under DAPT was performed without complication and resulted in immediate symptom resolution. Early and 1-month postoperative radiological assessment confirmed bypass patency with high bypass flow (120 ml/min) and adequate hemodynamic compensation (Fig. 4A–C). At the 6-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated excellent clinical and neurological recovery, and her ischemic symptoms had completely disappeared after bilateral STA-MCA revascularization. Discussion

Observations

This case illustrates the clinical and pathophysiological complexity of intracranial steno-occlusive disease and especially of MMA in young adults, highlighting the limitations of current classification systems and the challenges associated with rapidly progressive ischemic symptoms. The initial diagnosis of left unilateral MMA was supported by the typical angiographic features, including stenosis of the distal ICA and MCA and the presence of collateral vessels with a cigarette smoke appearance.4,5 Ischemic symptoms along with the MMD diagnosis led to prompt STA-MCA bypass on the patient’s left side. However, the rapid contralateral progression that occurred in the weeks following the first revascularization surgery raised concerns for a superimposed or alternative vasculopathy. This doubt was reinforced by the concentric wall enhancement seen on contrast-enhanced vessel wall (CE-vW) MRI, which has been reported during the “active stenosing” course of MMA and can also be associated with coexisting inflammatory arteriopathies, such as primary angiitis of the CNS and HSV-2 vasculitis.6–11 Hence, although the viral load of CSF HSV-2 positivity was low, there were no other specific clinical signs or symptoms, and the patient was immunocompetent, intravenous antiviral therapy and high-dose steroids were administrated, albeit without any benefit. A putative role of postinfectious immune response in the initiation or bilateral progression of MMA is suggested by the finding that ring finger protein 213, whose mutation is considered a predisposing factor for developing MMA, also plays a role in the immune system as an antimicrobial agent.12,13 Sussman et al. highlighted the possibility of contralateral strokes immediately after a bypass procedure through a hemodynamic mechanism, but a contralateral rapid progression in a few weeks was never documented.14 Notably, 2 patients in their series (of the 3 specifically tested for thrombophilia) had a factor V Leiden mutation, like our patient. Both recent bypass and factor V Leiden mutation might represent factors predisposing to the contralateral disease progression that we observed and that led to the rescue surgery.

This case shows that rapid contralateral disease progression after STA-MCA bypass for cerebrovascular insufficiency may occur, and these patients require strict postoperative follow-up in a facility where an urgent contralateral revascularization can be performed, if needed.

In our experience, in fact, ongoing therapy with DAPT is not an absolute contraindication to urgent surgery. We manage the DAPT by preoperative platelet administration and perform the bypass through a small, tailored craniotomy centered on the recipient branch. The use of an internal protocol of multimodal radiological assessment emerged as a decisive instrument for assessing the hemodynamic compromise and guiding surgical and medical management. In fact, preoperative identification of impaired cerebrovascular reserve supports the indication for bypass surgery, whereas intraoperative indocyanine green videoangiography and postoperative NOVA MRI flow quantification validate bypass patency and its adequate hemodynamic contribution. The growing use of dynamic perfusion studies and vessel wall imaging in differentiating intracranial vasculopathies has been emphasized in recent literature, highlighting their role in refining the diagnostic process and treatment planning.1,15,16

Finally, this case acts as a reminder that a clear-cut pathogenetic classification of cerebrovascular steno-occlusive diseases is still lacking and that different diseases with unpredictable clinical course still fall under the umbrella of an MMA diagnosis. Yet, regardless of terminology and classifications, revascularization surgery must be considered and may be warranted in rapidly progressive cases, when medical treatment fails. Thus, a multidisciplinary approach in the management of complex intracranial vasculopathies is crucial. Particular attention must be paid to warning signs such as crescendo TIAs, necessitating urgent revascularization procedures. This case also underlines the emerging role of CE-vW MRI in stratifying risks and identifying MMA patients prone to rapid disease progression, who may benefit from closer clinical and radiological monitoring and prompt revascularization before irreversible stroke occurs.

Lessons

MMA can present with a rapidly evolving clinical course and asymmetrically, shifting fast from a unilateral to bilateral progression, and this may be accelerated by surgical revascularization. Thus, the timing and rigor of postoperative follow-up cannot be understated. The role of possible predisposing factors such as coexisting thrombophilic and pro-inflammatory, including post- or parainfectious, conditions remains to be elucidated. The presented case shows that prompt surgical revascularization plays a key role when medical treatment fails, even under DAPT. Hence, salvage surgical revascularization is a viable option worth considering in cases of acute and progressive ischemia in the setting of MMA.
