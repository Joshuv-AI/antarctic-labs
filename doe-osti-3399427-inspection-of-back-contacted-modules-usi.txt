 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This work will focus on analysis of defects in modules fabricated using a conductive backsheet.
 While most defect types are known, reasonable methods for easily detecting manufactured
 defects are still relatively unknown.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Energy, Assists laboratory in achieving programmatic scope
 • Uses the laboratory’s core competencies

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Project Management

 Contractor and Participant to participate in project management meetings to discuss project
 status as and when required.

 Joint meetings were successfully held between NLR and Silfab collaborators where sample
 fabrication, test designs, and resulting data was discussed.

 Task 2: Test Sample Creation

 Participant will produce various sets of sample modules with deliberately generated defects. The
 nature of the defects and the number of samples will be determined collaboratively with the
 contractor. Samples will be manufactured, serialized, and cataloged (to associate the artificial
 defect with test sample).

 Silfab created test samples containing varying extents of intentional defects in the electrical
 contact design. The samples were provided to NLR in a “blind test” in order to find what
 methodology can be used to identify the extent of under-performance and existence of
 processing defects.

 Task 3: Test Sample Analysis

 Contractor and Participant will discuss the best measurement techniques for evaluating contact
 resistance as well as correlated measurements. Contractor will execute those measurement
 techniques on samples received from Participant. Once finished Contractor will ship samples to
 Participant’s test facility.

 2

 Measurement techniques were executed to characterize possible processing defects in the
 electrical contact design, including current-voltage (IV) sweeps, injection-dependent
 electroluminescence (EL), photoluminescence (PL), and dark lock-in thermography (DLIT). A
 summary of the test data is provided below. The outcomes are identification of characteristic
 signatures for over/under-welding of contacts, investigating correlation of these defects to series
 resistance or shunt resistance, and identification of data acquisition parameters for EL images
 that can be used for screening defects in Silfab’s processing line.

 Figure 1 shows the initial current-voltage (IV) curves for a set of silicon photovoltaic (PV) mini-
 modules that were fabricated with intentional processing defects. The variation in power output
 (Pmax) from the IV curves largely stems from changes in fill factor (FF). There is minimal
 variation in open-circuit voltage (Voc). Short-circuit current (Isc) loss is only significant for
 Sample 04. The variations in IV metrics are further presented in Figure 3.

 Figure 2 shows the electro-optical images of the four-cell mini-modules with the novel
 interconnection strategy utilizing a conductive backsheet. The images show spatial defects
 associated with the intentional processing issues. The images are arranged from low Pmax on the
 left to higher Pmax on the right.

 3

 Figure 2. Different characterization methods applied to identify intentional processing defects.
 Samples are arranged from low maximum power output (Pmax) on the left toward higher Pmax on
 the right.

 The first two rows of Figure 2 show the electroluminescence (EL) and dark lock-in
 thermography (DLIT) images collected in forward-bias equivalent to Isc. Because these images
 are collected at relatively high current density, these images demonstrate the sample features
 expected to arise from impacts of series resistance (Rs) on the FF. The third row of Figure 2
 gives low-current EL at 0.01xIsc injection in forward-bias. These images are expected to show
 impacts of low shunt resistance (Rsh) but should not be significantly impacted by Rs due to the
 low current density. For the analysis, we collected an automated series of 10 images with current
 injection ranging from 0.01x to 1x Isc. The photoluminescence (PL) images in the fourth row of
 Figure 2 are used to identify the material quality of the silicon and any impacts of nonradiative
 recombination. PL images are acquired under light excitation and thus are not significantly
 impacted by the electrical current-carrying pathways that cause changes in FF.

 We identified several types of initial processing defects from the images in Figure 2. For
 example, Sample 08 shows high contrast in the EL(Isc) and DLIT images. Here, we observe
 bright EL and hot DLIT signal around only a few local contact points. These bright regions
 indicate current crowding pathways. A high-contrast result such as Sample 08 suggests many
 points of under-welding at the contacts in dark EL/DLIT areas, such that current cannot easily
 flow into these dark regions due to poor contact quality.

 In Sample 08, the uniform PL images suggest that the silicon wafer quality is in general not a
 problem, although there is a scratch on the upper right cell causing a region of high
 recombination. In the EL(0.01xIsc) image, the scratched cell has low intensity due to carrier
 motion to this high recombination region at the low injection condition. A more subtle feature in
 Sample 08 includes local darkening around a few contact points observed in the EL(0.01xIsc)
 and PL images. This local darkening corresponds to contact points having high intensity in the
 EL(Isc) and DLIT images. The local darkening in EL(0.01xIsc) and PL images likely
 corresponds to points that were over-welded, causing higher carrier recombination due to some

 4

 damage to the silicon. It appears that the over-welded points in this sample are still making good
 contact for current flow and thus contribute as locations of current crowding.

 Samples 04, 05, and 07 also show signs of under-welded contacts resulting in nonuniformity in
 the EL(Isc) and DLIT images. However, the overall contrast in the EL(Isc) and DLIT images is
 less severe than in Sample 08 due to a lower number of under-welded points. In these samples, a
 larger area of the cell is well-connected and thus less current crowding occurs.

 Samples 09, 11, 12, 13, and 14 contain examples of over-welded contacts. The bright point-like
 spots in the DLIT images suggest heating from high local current flow at over-welded contacts.
 These bright DLIT spots are also dark in the EL(Isc) and PL images, suggesting damage to the
 silicon from over-welding. The low-current EL(0.01xIsc) images of these samples are dark. This
 is likely because the small amount of injected current flows through shunt pathways and/or
 regions of high non-radiative recombination at the over-welded regions instead of generating EL
 signal.

 Sample 06 is an example of minimal severity of under- or over- welding, but there is some
 performance loss due to scratches and cracks on the cells. The EL(0.01xIsc) image demonstrates
 the impacts of cracks, where high recombination at un-passivated edges of the cracks cause
 darkening of the cells at low-injection conditions.

 Task 4: Model Derivation

 Contractor will use data from Task 3 to build a quantitative model that can be used to absolutely
 or relatively quantify the series resistance of each individual interconnection on a module. An
 ideal solution would involve a direct measurement of a correlated measurement (i.e. DLIT to EL
 intensity) that gives a full module map with resolution fine enough to inspect individual
 interconnect resistances.

 NLR analyzed the IV data using quantitative fitting of a diode model to extract series and shunt
 resistance. In general, the fitting implied high series resistance as the source of low fill factor for
 the samples identified as under-welded in the image analysis. However, we note that the diode
 model for PV cells did not fit the data very well, likely due to the assumptions of the model that
 simplify physical phenomena making it work best for ideal conditions which may not exist in
 scaled-up samples.

 Task 5: Accelerated Aging

 Participant will run samples through their environmental test chambers to perform accelerated
 testing. Standard practices will be used and standard measurements will be made as part of
 those standard practices. Afterwards, Participant will ship aged samples back to Contractor.

 Accelerated aging was carried out at NLR in environmental test chambers. The test included
 thermal cycling, with 100 temperature cycles from -40 to 85 C and current injection in forward
 bias equivalent to the short-circuit current (10.8A). The purpose of the aging was to induce
 thermomechanical fatigue on the contact points to observe if the identified processing defects
 caused risk of long-term degradation.

 After initial characterization, the samples underwent an accelerated aging test to identify whether
 pre-existing defects cause further degradation. We used TC100, defined as thermal cycling for
 100 temperature cycles from -40 C to 85 C with current injection in forward bias equivalent to
 Isc (10.8A). Figure 3 shows the performance changes as well as the initial values of Pmax, FF,
 Voc, and Isc. The samples are ranked left to right by the initial Pmax value.

 Figure 3. Changes in performance metrics before and after thermal cycling accelerated stress
 testing. This includes maximum power (Pmax), fill factor (FF), open-circuit voltage (Voc) and
 short-circuit current (Isc).

 6

 We find that the most performance loss after TC100 occurs for the samples exhibiting the initial
 processing defect of under-welding identified from the image analysis. This includes Samples
 04, 05, 07, and 08. The loss in Pmax largely correlates with further loss in FF. By fitting the IV
 curves with a single diode model, we find that the loss is mainly attributable to increasing Rs.
 However, we note that the single diode model does not always provide a good fit, especially after
 degradation. Voc and Isc are minimally affected by the TC100 test.

 Thermal cycling stresses the electrical contacts due to expansion and contraction of various
 materials in the mini-module with different thermal properties. Therefore, the result of more
 substantial loss in Pmax and FF for the under-welded samples is not surprising. The under-
 welding likely caused weak initial contact points that were more susceptible to thermal fatigue
 and disconnection. We also note that the under-welded samples generally had lower initial
 performance, but the DPmax did not correlate directly with the initial Pmax. This is likely
 because samples starting with very poor performance already have fully disconnected contact
 points and therefore cannot become much worse (e.g. Sample 08). The greatest risk for loss in
 performance with aging is for samples with a mild extent of under-welding. For example, in
 Samples 04, 05, and 07, the defect may be more subtle to identify with initial screening in the
 processing line, but the contact points are weak enough that they brake under environmental
 stress.

 Task 6: Model Validation

 Contractor will repeat measurement methodologies from Task 3. Final outcomes will be 2-fold:
 a working model that can predict or map contact series resistance across a module and
 identification of what interconnect defects are critical to the lifetime of the module.

 The characterization methodologies were repeated after the accelerated aging, showing that some
 of the samples degraded more than others. Analysis showed that the under-welded samples
 degraded more than over-welded samples or samples that showed no signs of over/under-
 welding. The degradation mode led to decreasing fill factor mainly due to higher series
 resistance.

 Figure 4 shows the EL(Isc) images of these samples before and after TC100. We note that the
 DLIT images followed similar changes, while the EL(0.01xIsc) and PL images showed minimal
 change before and after TC100. The changes in EL images further support the decrease in FF
 from increasing Rs in Samples 04, 05, 07, and 08. That is, after TC100, these samples have an
 increase in nonuniformity with greater current crowding around local contact points.

 7

 Figure 4. Comparison of EL(Isc) images before and after thermal cycling (TC100).

 Finally, we compared the results of the mini-modules with full commercial-sized modules that
 were produced using the same conductive foil interconnection but using electrically conductive
 paste instead of welding and no intentional processing defects. See Reference [1]. Figure 5
 shows the IV performance and electro-optical images before and after a sequence of thermal
 cycling for 50 cycles (TC50) and 2x humidity-freeze for 10 cycles (HF10). Here, we observe
 only -0.6% degradation in Pmax after the thermomechanical stress sequence, demonstrating high
 durability of foil interconnection strategies in a commercial module when processing is
 optimized.

 Figure 5. Current-voltage (IV) curves along with imaging with photoluminescence (PL),
 electroluminescence (EL) and dark lock-in thermography (DLIT) demonstrating minimal
 degradation for a full commercial-sized module using the novel interconnection method.
