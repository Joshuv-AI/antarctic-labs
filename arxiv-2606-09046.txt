Decoy-Calibrated Failure Audits for Language Models
Vyzantinos Repantis, Ameya Gawde, Harshvardhan Singh

Abstract
Useful audits reveal not only how often a model fails, but also where its failures concentrate. An auditor may test many candidate explanations: long inputs, indirect questions, distracting evidence, or combinations of these factors. The risk is selection. The largest observed effect may reflect a real failure mode, or it may simply be the best result among many tried. We introduce Janus , a procedure for deciding when a proposed error explanation is credible enough to report. The goal is not to generate new explanations, but to decide which ones hold up. The auditor starts with a fixed model, a labeled evaluation set, and a frozen list of candidate explanations, which we call descriptors . Janus scores each descriptor by its error-rate lift, then compares real descriptors with fake ones that have the same frequencies but are randomly assigned to examples. A descriptor is confirmed only if it beats this decoy floor on the data used for discovery and then repeats on separate held-out data. In a controlled audit of multi-table lookup tasks, Janus identifies the planted failure, confirming long-chain descriptors and their interactions. The LLM often stops partway through the lookup chain instead of reaching the final answer. On two public benchmarks, MuSiQue and LongBench v2, the SliceLine baseline flags plausible high-error pockets, but Janus confirms none of them. Ablations show why both safeguards matter. On LongBench v2, an uncalibrated fixed threshold reports 20 descriptors, the decoy floor leaves one, and the holdout check rejects the last one after its lift shrinks from 0.36 to 0.05. The resulting principle separates proposing explanations from reporting them. Candidates may come from any source, but only those that beat decoys and replicate on fresh data become audit findings.



1
Introduction

Consider a controlled audit with 160 multi-table lookup tasks. The LLM answers 102 correctly and misses 58. This accuracy number establishes that errors occurred, but not where they concentrate.

One candidate explanation is
long_chain
: reaching the answer requires chaining several lookups across linked tables, rather than a single direct lookup. Errors are much more common on examples with this property. Yet the audit also includes indirect questions, late evidence, distractor collisions, flattened formatting, and interactions among these factors. A large observed lift is, therefore, not sufficient on its own: it may reflect a real failure mode, or it may be the most favorable result among the candidates examined.

This is the reporting problem we study. Model audits rarely stop at accuracy. Once errors are observed, auditors want to know where they concentrate and which explanations are credible enough to report. Slice-discovery methods search large spaces of subgroups and return coherent, interpretable error slices

. But a wide search makes reporting harder. An explanation found after many tries must be checked more carefully than one named in advance.

Janus
makes this check concrete with fake competitors. It begins with a frozen library of
descriptors
: interpretable true/false properties of an example, such as
long_chain
. Each descriptor is scored by error-rate lift.
Janus
then scores fake descriptors in the same way, using them to define an empirical decoy floor. A descriptor becomes an audit finding only if it clears that floor during discovery and repeats on held-out data.

This separation is intentional. Candidate descriptors may come from domain knowledge, slice-mining systems, or LLM-assisted debugging.
Janus
does not generate explanations. It decides which proposed explanations are credible enough to report.

Contributions.

First, we formulate the reporting problem for model failure audits, the task of deciding which of many candidate descriptors to report. Second, we introduce
Janus
, a decoy-calibrated procedure that compares descriptor lift with fake descriptors of the same frequency and requires replication on held-out data. Third, we evaluate
Janus
on one controlled audit and two public benchmarks, showing that it recovers a planted long-chain failure while refusing plausible but unconfirmed findings on MuSiQue and LongBench v2.

Formally, the audit records an error indicator for each example. Let
f
be a fixed model and let
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a labeled evaluation set of
n
examples, where
x_{i}
is the input and
y_{i}
is the ground-truth label. We define

e_{i}=\mathbf{1}[f(x_{i})\neq y_{i}],

so
e_{i}=1
when the model is wrong, and
e_{i}=0
otherwise. In our experiments, this binary correctness indicator is the audit outcome. More generally, the procedure only requires a per-example audit outcome, so regression or graded tasks can use a bounded loss or a thresholded error event.

A
descriptor
is an interpretable yes/no property of an example, such as
long_chain
,
answer_para_late
,
support_nonadjacent
, or
difficulty=hard
. The candidate library
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{L}\}
is a set of
L
descriptors, fixed before the discovery split is scored. Numeric metadata can be converted into thresholded descriptors, and categorical metadata into one descriptor per category.

We only evaluate descriptors with enough data on both sides of the comparison. A descriptor must be true often enough to estimate the slice error rate, and false often enough to estimate the complement error rate. We enforce this requirement with minimum support on each side and a
prevalence window
, such as 10–90%. A descriptor’s prevalence is the fraction of examples where it is true, so the window excludes descriptors that are true for almost none or almost all examples.

For a descriptor
g
, the discovery lift is

\mathrm{lift}(g)=\mathrm{err}_{\rm dis}(g=1)-\mathrm{err}_{\rm dis}(g=0),

where
\mathrm{err}_{\rm dis}(g=1)
is the error rate among discovery examples where
g
is true, and
\mathrm{err}_{\rm dis}(g=0)
is the error rate among discovery examples where
g
is false. Positive lift means the descriptor marks examples with higher error, and negative lift means it marks examples with lower error. We rank descriptors by
|\mathrm{lift}(g)|
.

2.1
Decoys

A decoy is a fake descriptor scored in the same way as a real one. To construct one,
Janus
takes a real descriptor that already clears the support and prevalence filters and permutes its true/false values across the discovery examples. If a real descriptor such as
indirect_query
is true for 30% of the examples, its decoy is also true for 30%, but on randomly chosen examples (Figure
2
). The decoy, therefore, preserves the descriptor’s prevalence but removes its intended connection to the model’s errors.

The decoys then answer a simple calibration question. Under the same sample size, prevalence limits, and scoring rule, how large can a fake descriptor look? We use
K=100
decoys for the MuSiQue and LongBench v2 main audits and
K=200
for the controlled-docs audit and the
Janus
-lite ablation. Other decoy constructions are possible, such as hash-based controls or knockoffs.

The decoys are a practical calibration device, not a proof that real and fake descriptors behave identically under the null. They preserve prevalence, but not all relationships among descriptors. For example, if
long_chain
and
indirect_query
often co-occur, permuting one descriptor at a time breaks that dependence. The decoy floor should, therefore, be read narrowly: it calibrates individual-descriptor lift under the same support and prevalence constraints, but it is not a complete null model for correlated metadata.

When descriptor correlation is central to an audit, the decoys can be made more structured, for example, by permuting related descriptors together or by resampling descriptors conditional on a feature model.

2.2
Procedure A: setting the decoy floor

For any lift threshold
\tau
, let
R(\tau)
be the number of real descriptors whose discovery lift has magnitude at least
\tau
, and let
D(\tau)
be the corresponding number of decoys:

R(\tau)=\#\{g\in\mathcal{G}:|\mathrm{lift}(g)|\geq\tau\},\qquad D(\tau)=\#\{d\in\mathcal{D}:|\mathrm{lift}(d)|\geq\tau\}.

Here,
L=|\mathcal{G}|
is the number of real descriptors and
K=|\mathcal{D}|
is the number of decoys. At threshold
\tau
, Procedure A estimates the false-discovery proportion by

\widehat{\mathrm{FDP}}(\tau)=\frac{(L/K)D(\tau)}{\max(1,R(\tau))}.

The hat indicates that
\widehat{\mathrm{FDP}}(\tau)
is an estimate, not the unknown true false-discovery proportion.

The ratio has a direct interpretation. At threshold
\tau
,
R(\tau)
counts how many real descriptors would survive, and
D(\tau)
counts how many decoys would survive. If many decoys clear the same threshold, the threshold is not very selective, since fake descriptors can achieve comparable lift under the same scoring rule.

Because the decoy library may not have the same size as the real descriptor library, the factor
L/K
rescales the decoy count to the real-library scale. The numerator
(L/K)D(\tau)
is, therefore, an estimate of how many real survivors could be attributed to decoy-level chance effects. Dividing by
\max(1,R(\tau))
converts this count into an estimated false-discovery proportion. The maximum only avoids division by zero when no real descriptor survives.

Given this estimate, Procedure A scans candidate thresholds and chooses the most permissive one whose estimated FDP is at most
q
. In our main experiments below, we use
q=0.10
.

Procedure A should be read as an empirical decoy screen, not as a finite-sample false discovery rate (FDR) guarantee. Its role is to decide which descriptors are strong enough to pass from discovery to holdout. The final reported object is not the discovery survivor set, but the set confirmed on the holdout.

Why not formal FDR control?

Formal FDR procedures require structure that this audit does not assume. Benjamini–Hochberg requires valid
p
-values for a fixed hypothesis family
(
3
)
. Knockoff filters require valid knockoff statistics
(
2
;
4
)
. Finite-sample target-decoy rules require the decoy and real statistics to be exchangeable under the null, which our single-descriptor permutation does not ensure. In our setting, candidate descriptors may come from heterogeneous sources, including metadata thresholds, slice-mining systems, or LLM-assisted hypothesis generation. The natural audit statistic is lift, not a parametric
p
-value.

Janus
, therefore, uses decoys as an empirical calibration device, estimating the lift that fake descriptors can attain under the same sample size, support constraints, prevalence constraints, and scoring rule. This screen does not certify the discovery survivors as an FDR-controlled set. Instead, it filters candidates for a separate holdout gate, where each survivor must replicate on fresh examples. We use the target-decoy ratio above for this screen.

2.3
Holdout confirmation

After discovery, the survivor set is fixed. On the holdout split, we recompute each survivor’s lift. A survivor is confirmed if it still has sufficient support, its absolute holdout lift exceeds a pre-specified minimum (we use
|\mathrm{lift}_{\rm hld}|\geq 0.10
unless otherwise stated), and its lift has the same sign as in discovery. The rule is intentionally conservative. An explanation selected on one split must retain its direction and strength on another.

3
Experiments

The experiments test two properties of the reporting rule. First, when a genuine and interpretable failure mode is present,
Janus
should recover it. Second, when plausible explanations do not survive calibration and replication,
Janus
should return no findings. We, therefore, use one controlled audit with planted structure and two public benchmarks where plausible metadata descriptors are available but not assumed to be valid explanations.

All three audits use the same evaluated model, Claude Haiku 4.5 (API identifier
claude-haiku-4-5-20251001
), with constrained short-answer or multiple-choice outputs. Descriptors are computed from document and query metadata, not from model internals. We report only the descriptors confirmed on the holdout.

3.1
Controlled document packets: planted structure

The controlled audit asks the model to find a renewal code in a synthetic operations packet. Every example contains all facts needed to answer. Each packet consists of relational tables linked by shared keys: a
STAFF DIRECTORY
(badge, name, division), an
ASSIGNMENT LEDGER
(name, division, project), and one or more registries mapping projects to renewal codes. Answering requires following a key chain across tables.

The audit serves as a recovery check. Each example is built by turning five interpretable properties on or off, namely whether the answer requires a long key chain across tables, whether the query is indirect, whether distractor collisions are present, whether the target rows occur late, and whether the formatting is flattened. These five properties are the base descriptors for the audit.

Three of the five base descriptors vary the packet without changing the lookup path. Target-late placement moves the rows holding the needed facts to the end of their tables. Flat formatting concatenates the tables into running text rather than separated, labeled blocks.

target_late

Qualitatively, many errors follow the same pattern. The model begins the key chain correctly but stops early, returning an intermediate vendor or subsidiary code instead of the final renewal code. The confirmed descriptors, therefore, point to a specific failure. The difficulty is not table reading in general, but completing a multi-table key chain. Janus
recovers the planted failure mode under its decoy and holdout rule, not that the failure mode was unknown.

3.2
Natural benchmarks: when plausible explanations do not hold up

The next two audits test restraint. In MuSiQue
(
19
)
and LongBench v2
(
1
)
, the available descriptors suggest plausible explanations of error, and slice search can find high-error subgroups. The question is whether any of these explanations survives the same decoy calibration and holdout confirmation used in the controlled audit.

The candidate descriptors differ by benchmark. In MuSiQue, they include hop count, support-paragraph distance, whether supporting paragraphs are nonadjacent, answer position, distractor count, and question length. In LongBench v2, they include context size, question type, the similarity and context overlap among answer options, entity and number densities, and dataset metadata such as domain, subdomain, and difficulty.

MuSiQue illustrates the value of a reporting rule that can return no findings. The model is evaluated with all supporting context present and an objective answer criterion, answering 84 of 150 examples correctly. Some descriptors are too imbalanced to estimate reliably. For example,
support_nonadjacent
is true for 141 of 150 examples, leaving only nine examples in the off-slice group, so it is removed by the support and prevalence filter. The remaining descriptors, including hop-count and support-distance descriptors, do not beat the decoy floor.
Janus
, therefore, reports no confirmed descriptors.

LongBench v2 shows the same restraint in a different setting. We evaluate 115 feasible examples, namely those that pass the scoring and formatting constraints described in Appendix
C
. The model answers 56 of 115 correctly. Slice-style baselines can find high-error pockets, but
Janus
again reports no confirmed descriptor. This is a meaningful null, not a sign that the model’s errors are random. Among the descriptors we tested, none supported a reportable explanation after decoy calibration and holdout replication.

3.4
Why the decoy floor and holdout gate are both essential

Table
3
shows what happens when parts of the rule are removed. For this ablation, we use a simplified variant of
Janus
, which we call
Janus
-lite, so that the contribution of each safeguard is easy to see.
Janus
-lite uses a fixed decoy floor. A descriptor must have discovery lift above the 95th percentile of decoy lifts, and must then replicate on the holdout split. In other words, it must beat almost every fake descriptor scored under the same rule.

We use this lighter rule because Procedure A folds the decoy comparison and the threshold choice into a single false-discovery estimate, which makes it hard to attribute a change to one safeguard. With a fixed floor, we can remove the decoy step or the holdout step separately, so each safeguard’s effect is visible on its own. The main results instead use Procedure A, which scans candidate thresholds and chooses the most permissive one whose estimated false-discovery proportion remains at or below the target level
q
.

The ablation separates the two safeguards. A fixed lift threshold can over-report because it ignores how large fake effects can become after the same search. The decoy floor adds this comparison with fake descriptors. The holdout gate then asks whether each survivor repeats on fresh examples.

The controlled audit confirms that these safeguards do not erase real signal. All six decoy-surviving descriptors replicate. In MuSiQue, the decoy floor rejects weak natural patterns before holdout. On LongBench v2, the full sequence is visible. A fixed lift threshold reports 20 descriptors. The decoy floor leaves one. The holdout check rejects the last one after its lift shrinks from 0.36 to 0.05. This drop is the
winner’s curse
, a form of regression to the mean. When we select the most extreme descriptor among many noisy candidates, part of its apparent strength is luck specific to the discovery split, and the holdout check exposes this shrinkage. A genuine failure mode should keep substantial same-sign lift, whereas a lucky artifact should shrink toward zero.

4
Related work

The most relevant prior work falls into four groups. Slice-discovery methods search for high-error subgroups. Fairness-auditing methods test whether errors or performance disparities concentrate on subpopulations. Knockoff and target-decoy methods calibrate discoveries against manufactured competitors. Behavioral-testing work for NLP and LLMs creates, organizes, or expands targeted test cases.
Janus
draws on all four lines, but its target is narrower. Given a fixed audit and a frozen library of proposed error descriptors, it decides which descriptors are credible enough to report.

Slice discovery.

Slice-discovery methods search for subsets of examples on which a model performs poorly. SliceFinder introduced automated search over high-error feature conjunctions and used alpha-investing for false-discovery control during search
(
7
)
. SliceLine made exact enumeration scalable with linear algebra and returns top-
K
high-error slices
(
17
)
. Later methods improve the coherence or semantics of discovered slices using learned representations. Domino uses cross-modal embeddings and an error-aware mixture model
(
11
)
. Spotlight searches for high-error regions in representation space
(
9
)
. InfEmbed clusters influence embeddings
(
20
)
. MCSD discovers error slices that are compact on the data manifold
(
21
)
. Language- and attribute-driven systems such as LADDER and HiBug2 generate hypotheses or visual attributes before slice enumeration
(
12
;
5
)
. These methods are natural sources of candidate descriptors.
Janus
addresses the next question. Once candidates have been proposed and scored, which ones are credible enough to report as audit findings?

Fairness and subgroup auditing.

Fairness auditing is statistically close to our setting because it also asks whether model errors or performance disparities concentrate on subpopulations. Cherian and Candès formulate model-agnostic fairness auditing as a multiple-testing problem over subpopulation classes, using bootstrap calibration to provide simultaneous statistical guarantees
(
6
)
. AFISP identifies worst-performing clinical subsets and then extracts interpretable patient phenotypes with potential performance disparities
(
18
)
. FairTree audits subgroup performance with permutation and fluctuation-test variants, decomposes performance disparities into systematic bias and variance, and compares directly with SliceLine
(
8
)
.
Janus
shares the auditing motivation, but the reported object is different. It reports named error descriptors after a manufactured-decoy screen and a held-out replication check.

Manufactured nulls and multiple testing.

The statistical precedent for
Janus
is the idea that observed discoveries should be judged against manufactured or chance baselines. Knockoff methods create synthetic variables for feature selection and, under their assumptions, control the false discovery rate
(
2
;
4
)
. Target-decoy search in proteomics searches target and decoy databases with the same spectra and search machinery, using decoy hits to estimate false identifications in large identification sets
(
10
)
. A related chance-corrected idea appears in retrieval evaluation, where selectivity is measured against a random baseline
(
14
)
. Classical multiple-testing procedures such as Benjamini–Hochberg control the false discovery rate for a fixed family of valid
p
-values under standard assumptions
(
3
)
.
Janus
borrows the manufactured-null intuition, not the formal guarantees of these settings. Its decoys provide an empirical noise floor for descriptor lift, and the final reported set is additionally filtered by holdout replication.

LLM and behavioral testing.

Behavioral testing methods make model evaluation more systematic by creating, organizing, or expanding targeted test cases. CheckList introduced a task-agnostic methodology for behavioral testing of NLP systems
(
16
)
, and AdaTest made test generation iterative and model-in-the-loop
(
15
)
. Long-context analyses and benchmarks, including Lost in the Middle and LongBench v2, show that access to long context is not the same as reliable use of that context
(
13
;
1
)
.
Janus
does not replace these benchmarks or test generators. It provides a reporting rule for deciding which human-readable explanations of observed benchmark errors survive a multiple-descriptor audit.

5
Limitations

Janus
is deliberately narrow. It is a reporting rule for proposed failure descriptors, not a complete explanation engine. Its limitations follow from that scope.

Candidate quality.

Janus
can only confirm descriptors that are present in the candidate library. If a real failure mode is never proposed, the procedure cannot recover it. Candidate descriptors may come from domain knowledge, slice-mining systems, or LLM-assisted debugging, but the library must be frozen before the discovery split is scored.

Association, not causation.

A confirmed descriptor is associated with model errors. It does not by itself prove that the descriptor causes the errors. The controlled document audit is useful because the factors are designed, but in natural data a descriptor may be a proxy for another unobserved property.

Power for rare slices.

The support and prevalence filters protect against unstable rare-slice claims, but they also reduce power for genuinely rare failures. Audits focused on rare or protected groups may need larger evaluation sets, targeted sampling, or a statistic designed for the subgroup of interest.

No finite-sample FDR guarantee.

As discussed in Section
2
, Procedure A is an empirical target-decoy screen rather than a procedure with a finite-sample FDR guarantee. The final safeguard on reported findings is holdout replication. Audits that require finite-sample FDR control can replace the discovery floor with a valid-
p
-value procedure or a knockoff procedure, while keeping the holdout gate.

Model and benchmark scope.

The experiments use one evaluated model and modest sample sizes. They demonstrate the audit workflow and its restraint, not a universal claim about all language models or all long-context failures. The natural-benchmark audits are deliberately small calibration checks. Larger audits may reveal confirmed descriptors in MuSiQue, LongBench v2, or other benchmarks. The present result is more limited:
Janus
does not report weak descriptors merely because slice search can name them.

Absolute versus relative lift.

We use an absolute holdout-lift threshold because it is easy to interpret at the error rates studied here. For very high-accuracy systems, where the baseline error rate is only a few percent, an absolute threshold can become too stringent. In that regime, a relative-risk threshold may be more appropriate.

Beyond error rate.

The procedure is not tied to binary error rate. The same decoy-and-holdout logic can be applied to other scalar descriptor scores, such as bounded-loss lift or subgroup disparity, provided real and decoy descriptors are scored identically.

6
Conclusion

A plausible error slice is not necessarily a reportable audit finding. When many candidate explanations are examined, the largest observed effect may partly reflect the search itself.
Janus
addresses this reporting problem by comparing proposed descriptors with decoys of the same frequency and then requiring replication on held-out data. In the controlled audit, this rule recovers the planted long-chain failure family. In the natural-benchmark audits, it reports no findings when plausible explanations do not survive calibration and replication.

The central distinction is between proposing explanations and reporting them. Candidate descriptors may come from domain knowledge, slice-mining systems, or LLM-assisted debugging, but they become audit findings only when they beat decoys and replicate on fresh data.

Appendix C
Implementation notes

All experiments use deterministic splits. For the controlled-docs audit, descriptors are filtered by prevalence in
[0.10,0.90]
and by a minimum support of eight examples on both the descriptor and its complement. For MuSiQue, descriptors are filtered by prevalence in
[0.10,0.90]
. For LongBench v2, whose feasible population has only 115 examples, the main
Janus
run uses a support rule of at least three on-slice examples in each split and at least eight total on-slice examples. The
Janus
-lite ablation uses a common stricter filter across datasets. Both the descriptor and its complement must have at least eight examples on the relevant split and prevalence must lie in
[0.10,0.90]
. Decoys are generated on the discovery split by permuting values of eligible descriptors. The main
Janus
runs use Procedure A at
q=0.10
, with
K=100
decoys for MuSiQue and LongBench v2 and
K=200
decoys for controlled docs. The
Janus
-lite ablation uses
K=200
decoys and the 95th percentile of absolute decoy lift as a simplified floor.

For reproducibility, the submitted code retains the original API-generation cells and appends offline re-analysis cells that recompute the reported MuSiQue and LongBench v2 Procedure-A results from the frozen JSONL outputs. These appended Procedure-A cells are the source used to compute the main natural-benchmark
Janus
results reported here.

As an offline robustness check, we repeated the Procedure-A discovery/holdout analysis over 200 random splits using the frozen JSONL outputs, with both-sides support at least eight for controlled docs and at least three on-slice examples per split for MuSiQue and LongBench v2. The controlled-docs audit returned a nonempty confirmed set in all 200 splits, with the six main descriptors recurring frequently, each confirmed in 65–100% of splits. In contrast, MuSiQue and LongBench v2 remained empty in 197/200 and 194/200 splits, respectively. The few nonempty natural-benchmark splits involved isolated descriptors with confirmation frequencies at most 3%.

Cost of decoys.

Decoy calibration adds little overhead relative to model evaluation. Each decoy is a length-
n
Boolean vector obtained by shuffling an eligible descriptor, and its lift is computed by two empirical error rates on the discovery split. The additional work is
O(Kn)
after the candidate descriptors and error vector have been computed. In these audits, the computational cost is dominated by model evaluation and descriptor construction, not by decoy scoring.
