An unusually small pituitary microadenoma causing severe Cushing disease in a male patient: lessons from early Radiosurgical management
Authors: Bayar A Qasim, Halder J Abozait, Rende S A Kochary, Luay E Alsaoor, Saleem K Musalah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCushing disease is a rare endocrine disorder caused by an ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma. We report a 27-year-old male with severe hypercortisolism due to a 1.2-mm pituitary microadenoma. He presented with facial plethora, centripetal obesity, violaceous striae, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and fatigue. Biochemical testing confirmed ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism, and 3 T MRI identified the lesion. Owing to its minute size and the anticipated difficulty of surgical localization, transsphenoidal surgery was deferred and Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKRS) was performed as first-line therapy. After 5 months, biochemical hypercortisolism persisted, with elevated cortisol and ACTH levels. Ketoconazole 200 mg daily was subsequently initiated, resulting in normalization of serum cortisol within four weeks, ACTH levels remained elevated. Mild transient ALT elevation was observed during treatment. This case highlights that very small adenomas can cause severe disease and underscores the delayed response to GKRS, necessitating early adjunctive medical therapy to control hypercortisolism and reduce morbidity.

Introduction

Cushing disease, a specific form of Cushing syndrome, results from chronic ACTH hypersecretion by a pituitary corticotroph adenoma, leading to adrenal overproduction of cortisol [1]. The annual incidence is around 1.6 cases per million [2]. Untreated disease is associated with significant morbidity and mortality due to metabolic, cardiovascular, and infectious complications [3]. Transsphenoidal pituitary surgery is the treatment of choice, with reported remission rates of 42%–95% for Cushing disease [4]. For patients who are poor surgical candidates or those with persistent or recurrent disease, medical therapies such as ketoconazole, metyrapone, or mitotane may be used to inhibit steroidogenesis [5]. Stereotactic radiosurgery, including Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKRS), is another treatment modality, offering a non-invasive, targeted approach to pituitary tumors [6]. However, the onset of GKRS efficacy is often delayed, with biochemical remission occurring months to years after treatment, and some patients fail to achieve remission altogether [7]. Herein, we report a patient with ACTH-dependent Cushing disease who remained biochemically active five months after GKRS, necessitating ketoconazole therapy.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old male presented with a two-year history of progressive facial swelling, facial erythema, and marked weight gain. This was followed by progressive abdominal distension. Over time, large reddish-purple striae appeared on the abdomen and flanks. He reported significant fatigue with minimal exertion, bilateral lower limb pain, excessive sweating (especially at night), hypersomnolence with non-restorative sleep, intermittent blurred vision, particularly in low light, and mild headaches occurring one to two times per week. Appetite was increased, and he gained approximately 15 kg over the course of one year. The patient also reported low mood and reduced libido. His past medical history was unremarkable until he was diagnosed with hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia a year prior to the presentation. His medications included perindopril 10 mg once daily, indapamide 2.5 mg once daily, amlodipine 10 mg once daily, metformin 1000 mg once daily, sitagliptin 100 mg once daily, and glimepiride 3 mg once daily. The patient denied alcohol consumtion and illicit drug use. He had been smoking approximately half a pack of cigarettes daily for over 10 years. His family history was notable for hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus among first-degree relatives, but no known endocrine or genetic disorders.

On physical examination, the patient had a rounded, erythematous, and plethoric face. Mild scleral erythema was noted, without conjunctival pallor or jaundice. Oral hygiene was poor. There were supraclavicular fullness and a prominent dorsocervical fat pad. The skin was thin, with large violaceous striae over the lower thoracic cage and across the abdomen. Palmar erythema was present, with mild swelling of the fingers. Abdominal examination revealed distension with multiple large violaceous striae under the axilla and across the abdomen (Fig. 1). There was no tenderness, palpable masses, or organomegaly. No neurological deficits were identified.

Initial biochemical evaluation demonstrated an evening serum cortisol level of 574 nmol/l (reference range: 64–327 nmol/l) and plasma ACTH of 151 pg/ml (reference range: 7.2–63.3 pg/ml), confirming ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism. The overnight and low-dose dexamethasone suppression tests showed failure of cortisol suppression, while the high-dose dexamethasone suppression test demonstrated > 50% suppression, consistent with ACTH-dependent Cushing’s disease due to a pituitary adenoma. Serum electrolytes were as follows: sodium 140 mmol/l (reference range: 136–146 mmol/l), potassium: 3.39 mmol/l (reference range: 3.5–5.5 mmol/l), chloride 103 mmol/l (96–106 mmol/l). Thyroid function tests were normal. Pituitary MRI confirmed the presence of a 1.2 mm microadenoma (Fig. 2). The imaging was performed using a 3.0 Tesla (3 T) high-field MRI scanner with thin-slice (≤2 mm) dynamic contrast-enhanced sequences, which markedly improve spatial resolution and lesion-to-gland contrast. These high-resolution protocols enable visualization of pituitary microadenomas as small as 1–2 mm [8]. Inferior petrosal sinus sampling was not performed, as the imaging findings were considered concordant with the biochemical findings. Chest and abdominal imaging were unremarkable.

MRI of the pituitary gland using a 3.0 tesla (3 T), showing 1.2 mm focal hypointense lesion at the left side of pituitary gland (arrow) consistent with small microadenoma.

The standard first line of treatment of Cushing disease is transsphenoidal resection. However, following neurosurgical evaluation, surgical intervention was deferred because of concerns regarding the extremely small size of the lesion (1.2 mm) and the expected difficulty of accurate intraoperative localization. Thus, the patient was initially managed by GKRS, targeting the pituitary adenoma. After GKRS, laboratory results showed persistently elevated morning cortisol (1240 nmol/l after one month, 879 nmol/l after two months (reference range: 171–537 nmol/l)) and ACTH (178 pg/ml after one month, 142 pg/ml after two months), with high LDL-cholesterol (171 mg/dl after one month, 118 mg/dl after two months (reference range: < 100 mg/dl) and concomitant poor glycemic control (HbA1c 9.4%). Consequently, insulin therapy and rosuvastatin were initiated. At five months’ follow-up, there was persistent biochemical evidence of hypercortisolism with evening serum cortisol at 637 nmol/l and ACTH at 192 pg/mL. The patient continued to experience fatigue and weight gain. He was prescribed ketoconazole 200 mg once daily. Four weeks after starting ketoconazole therapy, morning serum cortisol had normalized at 500 nmol/l, ACTH was 193 pg/mL, and HbA1c 8.1%. Liver function showed normal AST (24 IU/l, reference range: < 40 IU/l), but mildly elevated ALT (53 IU/L, reference range: < 40 IU/l). He was advised to return for follow-up after 8–12 weeks (Table 1).

Discussion

Cushing disease, first described by Harvey Cushing in 1932, results from an ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma that drives excess cortisol production by the adrenal glands [9]. While most cases occur in women, males represent less than 10%–15% of patients, and their disease course is often more aggressive with delayed diagnosis and higher morbidity [10]. This case is therefore noteworthy for its occurrence in a young male and the presence of severe hypercortisolism arising from an exceptionally small pituitary microadenoma measuring only 1.2 mm.

The case underscores several unusual and educational aspects. First, the tiny adenoma size presented a major diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma. Although transsphenoidal resection remains the first-line treatment for Cushing disease, surgical removal of such a minute lesion can be technically difficult due to challenges in intraoperative localization. In this patient, the neurosurgical team therefore opted for Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKRS) as a non-invasive alternative.

Second, the case illustrates the limitations of early biochemical response following GKRS for the management of Cushing disease. Although GKRS was selected because localization and surgical resection of the 1.2-mm lesion were considered technically challenging, clinicians should recognize that radiosurgery does not provide immediate biochemical remission. Published data indicate that remission after GKRS may take anywhere from six months to two years, with reported remission rates varying between 28% and 73%. [11, 12] The persistence of elevated cortisol and ACTH levels in this patient during the early post-radiosurgical period is therefore not necessarily indicative of treatment failure but highlights the importance of close biochemical monitoring and timely institution of adjunctive medical therapy during the latency period following GKRS. Predictors of delayed remission include high baseline cortisol, larger tumor volume, and interindividual radiosensitivity [12].

Third, the addition of ketoconazole effectively normalized serum cortisol within four weeks, yet ACTH levels remained elevated. This biochemical dissociation reflects the drug’s mechanism—suppression of adrenal steroidogenesis without directly affecting pituitary ACTH secretion [5]. Persistently elevated ACTH levels despite normalized cortisol concentrations emphasises the complexity of hormonal feedback dynamics in the post-treatment phase and supports the need for long-term biochemical monitoring.

This case further illustrates the extensive metabolic burden associated with chronic hypercortisolism. The coexistence of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and weight gain underscores the multisystemic impact of cortisol excess. Even after biochemical improvement, these comorbidities can persist, predisposing to cardiovascular morbidity [3, 13]. Mildly elevated liver enzymes may indicate either metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) secondary to Cushing’s metabolic effects or early ketoconazole-related hepatotoxicity, both warranting periodic liver function monitoring [14].

This case is remarkable for several reasons: (1) male sex—Cushing disease remains up to eight times more common in females [10]; (2) minute pituitary microadenoma (1.2 mm) causing florid clinical Cushing syndrome; (3) primary GKRS employed as first-line therapy due to surgical inaccessibility; and (4) persistent ACTH elevation despite normalized cortisol after medical therapy. Together, these features make this case both diagnostically and therapeutically instructive, underscoring the importance of individualized, multidisciplinary management in complex endocrine disorders.

Conclusions

This case highlights that severe Cushing disease can arise from a minute pituitary microadenoma, particularly in male patients, a demographic less commonly affected. Gamma Knife radiosurgery offered a non-invasive alternative when surgery was not feasible, but remission was delayed, necessitating adjunctive ketoconazole therapy for biochemical control. Persistent ACTH elevation despite normalized cortisol levels demonstrates the intricate feedback disruption in treated Cushing disease. Ongoing surveillance and multidisciplinary care remain essential to optimise outcomes, manage comorbidities, and monitor for therapy-related adverse effects.
