Stratification by a polygenic risk score of common variation aids in Alzheimer's disease rare variant discovery
Authors: Oluwatosin Olayinka, John J. Farrell, Congcong Zhu, Zainab Khurshid, Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project, Eden R. Martin, William S. Bush, Margaret A. Pericak‐Vance, Li‐San Wang, Gerard D. Schellenberg, Jonathan L. Haines, Kathryn L. Lunetta, Xiaoling Zhang, Lindsay A. Farrer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe utilized an Alzheimer's disease (AD) polygenic risk score (PRS) to discover associations with novel rare variants (RVs).METHODSPRSs for European ancestry (EA) participants of the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project were calculated using summary statistics from a large genome‐wide association study. Participants were classified into high (n = 5738) and low (n = 5324) PRS groups based on the median PRS and on the lower and upper 35% of the PRS distribution.RESULTSRisk variants were disproportionately enriched in the low‐PRS group, while protective ones were disproportionately enriched in the high‐PRS group. Genome‐wide significant (GWS) associations for increased AD risk were identified with RVs spanning a 3.5‐Mb region on chromosome 14. GWS protective variants in ALDH9A1, BICC1, and PAN3 were identified in the upper 35% PRS group.CONCLUSIONOur findings provide unique opportunities to study RVs whose effects are opposite to the risk conferred by the genetic background.

INTRODUCTION

Polygenic risk scores (PRSs) are quantitative measures of genetic liability for diseases or traits that are derived from aggregated effects of individual genetic variants and can be useful tools for clinicians and biologists in disease and phenotype prediction. 1 Typically, a PRS is calculated using effect sizes for common genetic variants that have a minimum minor allele frequency (MAF) between 1% and 5%. 2 Importantly, although a common‐variant PRS captures much of the phenotypic variance for many traits, individuals who have phenotypes that deviate substantially from PRS prediction may harbor high‐impact rare variants (RVs) that account for the observed phenotype. For example, a UK Biobank (UKBB) study showed that a large portion of individuals whose height was much lower than that predicted by their PRS had RVs linked to monogenic forms of short stature. 3 Similarly, individuals with low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) levels that deviated from their PRS‐predicted LDL level were enriched for rare loss of function variants in known LDL genes. 3 An analogous pattern was observed in a study of five common diseases among UKBB participants that showed that rare pathogenic variants were more prevalent among affected individuals with a low PRS. 4

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by extracellular deposits of misfolded amyloid protein and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein and is the leading cause of dementia worldwide. 5 A PRS with the APOE region included has been shown to explain as much as 24% of the AD phenotypic variance, 6 but much of the heritability of AD is still “missing.” 7 RVs in many genes have been implicated in AD, 8 but the evidence for most of these findings is not robust due largely to study samples that are too small to demonstrate association with variants having a frequency less than 0.5%.

To discover novel associations of AD with rare (MAF < 1%) and low‐frequency (1% ≤ MAF < 5%) variants (RLVs), we conducted a genome‐wide association study (GWAS) stratified by an AD PRS constructed from common variants using whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) data from the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project (ADSP). 9 We hypothesized that we would detect associations not only with RLVs conferring increased risk of AD among individuals with a low PRS but also with RLVs that are protective among individuals with a high genetic predisposition to AD.

METHODS

The study design is depicted in Figure S1.

Study participants

Study participants were selected from WGS Release 4 dataset assembled by the ADSP. The pre‐filtered dataset included 35,014 individuals who self‐identified as African American (n = 5414), Caribbean Hispanic (n = 5601), Hispanic (n = 5174), South Asian (n = 2983), and European ancestry (EA, n = 15,842). Details of studies that contributed DNA specimens and phenotype data associated with the ADSP participants are available at the National Institute on Aging Genetics of Alzheimer's Disease Data Storage site. 9 An AD diagnosis was assigned to individuals who met National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association criteria. 10 Controls lacked evidence of cognitive impairment or other neurodegenerative disease. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Boston University Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. H‐28168).

Genotyping calling, data processing, and quality control (QC)

Genetic data were processed by the Genome Center for Alzheimer's Disease (GCAD) and included in the ADSP Release 4 (R4) dataset. 9 Details about the generation of these data are reported elsewhere. 9 Briefly, data were processed using the Variant Calling Pipeline and data management tool (VCPA) that was developed by GCAD for processing sequence data and joint calling of variants. The Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) implemented in VCPA was used for generating genomic Variant Call Format (gVCF) files and for generating project‐level VCF (pVCF) files based on the human GRCh38/hg38 reference genome. 11 Additional details of data filtering and quality control are reported elsewhere. 9 , 12 An additional QC step was implemented to reduce the effect of variant calling errors in RLVs by removing variants that had a “FisherStrand” value greater than 4.963, a non‐zero MQRankSum, and a QD value less than 10.

Genetic relationship and population substructure analyses

A genetic relationship matrix (GRM) was calculated for each individual using 146,964 variants that were linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruned by Hail software using the SNPRELATE function “snpgdsIBDKING.” 13 Global principal components (PCs) of ancestry were computed for unrelated individuals and projected onto related individuals using the principal component analysis in relatedness (PC‐AIR) method. 14 Individuals were clustered into population ancestry groups using a Gaussian mixture model applied to several K‐means and subsequently assigned to eight clusters that are described elsewhere. 15 A GRM and PCs were recalculated for each cluster using PC‐AiR. Subsequent analyses of RLVs were focused on the EA group due to small sample sizes of the other population clusters that are relatively small for association tests with RVs.

PRS calculation

A PRS was calculated for each ADSP participant in the EA group using summary statistics from a large AD GWAS of EA cohorts in which an AD diagnosis was established using the same criteria described above. 16 Although the AD GWAS conducted by Bellenguez et al. represents a much larger and potentially more powerful dataset for summary statistics used for PRS calculations, the predictive power of the PRS derived from the Kunkle et al. GWAS summary statistics (odds ratio [OR] = 1.16, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.15 to 1.17, p = 1.00 × 10−322) was much greater than the PRS based on the Bellenguez et al. data (OR = 1.05, 95% CI = 1.04 to 1.06, p = 3.45 × 10−32). The reduced predictive power of the PRS derived from the larger dataset may be explained by the AD diagnosis for several constituent datasets, which was based on informant report and the use of a “proxy AD” diagnosis in the UKBB dataset, which was assigned if either parent was reported to have had dementia. Genomic coordinates were converted from GRCh37 to GRCh38 using liftOver. 17 Summary statistics for variants were assessed for allele flips by comparing the reference and alternative allele base pairs with the GRCh38 reference genome. The effect direction was reversed for single‐nucleotide variants whose reference allele did not match the reference genome. The PRS was constructed with the PRSice2 software, 18 including variants with MAF ≥ 5% but excluding chromosome 19 variants to minimize the effects of the APOE ε2 and ε4 alleles compared to the rest of the genome. The best model was derived using a permutation procedure in PRSice2 that tests the inclusion of various numbers of single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) for a range of p value thresholds and subsequently identifies the model yielding the largest Nagelkerke's R 2 value. The optimal PRS emerging from this procedure was adjusted for population substructure within the EA cluster by fitting a linear regression model using the first 20 PCs.

Association analysis methods

The association of AD with each RLV having MAF < 5% and minor allele count (MAC) ≥ 10 was tested in the total sample and separately in subgroups of individuals with low and high PRS using generalized linear mixed models implemented in GENESIS with the rare allele coded as the effect allele and including terms for GRM, sex, age, APOE ε2 and ε4 alleles, and the first six PCs. P values were calculated using a score test. Stratification of individuals into low and high common variant risk groups was done in two ways. In one approach, individuals were assigned to low‐ and high‐risk groups based on the median PRS value. Low‐ and high‐risk groups were also constructed after removing individuals with PRS between the 35th and 65th percentiles, respectively. Associations were treated as genome‐wide significant (GWS) if the p value was less than 5.0 × 10−8. A significance threshold for suggestive associations was set at p < 1.0 × 10−6.

RESULTS

Distribution of AD cases and controls in the low‐ and high‐PRS groups

After excluding individuals who did not meet criteria for AD or control or were missing covariates essential for analysis, a total of 11,062 individuals aligned with the EA cluster by population substructure analysis. In this group, 5324 (2232 AD cases and 3092 controls) had PRS values below the median for the entire ADSP sample and were assigned to the low‐PRS group and 5738 (4063 AD cases and 1675 controls) with PRS values above the median were assigned to the high‐PRS group (Figure S1). Associations were evaluated with approximately 7.44 million variants in the low‐PRS group and 7.77 million variants in the high‐PRS group. After applying stricter cutoffs of the PRS distribution (lower and upper 35th percentiles), there were 3759 individuals (1393 AD cases and 2366 controls) in the low‐PRS group and 4158 individuals (3180 AD cases and 978 controls) in the high‐PRS group. Associations were tested with approximately 6.21 million and 6.58 million variants in these groups, respectively. Characteristics of individuals in the total sample and within PRS groups are shown in Table S1.

Association of AD with RLVs in the low‐ and high‐PRS groups

There was little evidence of genomic inflation in genome‐wide analyses (λ = 1.011 to 1.021) conducted in the total sample (Figure S2A) or separately within low‐ or high‐PRS groups (Figure S3). No GWS associations were detected in the total sample (Figure S2B). In the low‐PRS group, GWS associations for increased AD risk were observed, with eight RVs spanning a 3.5‐Mb region on chromosome 14 that was approximately 49 Mb from PSEN1 and encompassed several genes including rs5709101195 located in the NYNRIN 3’UTR (OR = 30.5, p = 1.60 × 10−8), a 1‐bp insertion variant in LINC02286 (rs1019362505, OR = 35.0, p = 3.02 × 10−9), and rs960000673 located 99 kb from BNIP3P1 (OR = 108.1, p = 7.59 × 10−9) (Figures 1 and 2, Table 1). There were suggestive associations with RLVs at other loci on chromosomes 1, 2, 6, and 8. In the high‐PRS group, a near GWS association was identified with rs150913764 located between SMNDC1 and LOC105378482 (OR = 2.63, p = 5.84 × 10−8), noting that the effect was deleterious rather than protective (Table 1). Additional associations with protective variants were identified in the more extremely defined high‐PRS risk group (Figures 1 and 3, Table 2), including ones at the GWS level with rs539602997 in ALDH9A1 on chromosome 1 (OR = 0.02, p = 8.94 × 10−9), rs571829077 in BICC1 on chromosome 10 (OR = 0.03, p = 2.78 × 10−8) and rs17086376 in PAN3 on chromosome 13 (OR = 0.21, p = 2.78 × 10−8), and suggestive associations with variants at 11 loci on chromosomes 1, 4, 6, 8, 10, 11, 13, and 14 including rs140189521 in S100A8, which was nearly GWS (p = 5.23 × 10−8) (Table 2). More extensive details for an expanded set of GWS and suggestive RLV associations are presented in Table S2 for the sample stratified at the median and in Table S3 for the more strictly defined upper and lower PRS groups.

Distinct effect directions of RLVs associated with AD in low‐ and high‐PRS groups

To examine the general hypothesis that RVs associated with increased AD risk are more prevalent in individuals with a low PRS based on common variants and protective RVs are more likely to be observed in individuals with a high PRS, we evaluated the relationship between association test p values with the proportion of variants with a positive effect direction (i.e., increasing risk of AD) within the low‐ and high‐PRS groups. We observed that the proportion of variants showing a positive association with AD risk progressively increased with statistical significance in the low‐PRS group, whereas this proportion progressively , , decreased with increasing statistical significance in the high‐PRS group (Figure 4). These patterns were very similar in the PRS groups stratified at the median and at the lower 35th and upper 65th percentiles of the PRS distribution.

The thresholds for high and low‐PRS group membership are the sample median (left panel) and lower and upper 35th percentile (right panel).

RLV associations evident primarily in the Amish

Further scrutiny of the significant findings revealed that several associations, especially the GWS ones on chromosome 14 and several other suggestive loci in the low‐PRS group, are accounted for primarily by an Amish cohort (Tables S2 and S3), which has a genetic profile distinct from other EA individuals (Figure 5). This observation is consistent with frequency information about these variants in the Genome Aggregation Database 19 showing that they are 10 to 100 times more common in the Amish compared to non‐Amish Europeans and other population ancestries. Notably, the Amish cohort did not contribute substantially to any of the protective RV associations, with the possible exception of the PAN3 rs17086376 SNP, which had a MAC of 32 among Amish individuals and 118 in other EA individuals (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

Previous studies identified GWS associations of AD with aggregated sets of RVs in whole‐genome sequence data using several approaches, 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 but GWS associations with individual RVs have been detected only in established AD genes (e.g., PSEN1, SORL1). 21 , 24 Because of the extremely large sample requirement to detect associations with individual RVs, we applied a strategy to increase the signal of risk and protective RVs by grouping individuals with a relatively low or high risk of AD, respectively, based on a PRS constructed from aggregated common variants. Using this approach, we identified novel GWS associations of increased AD risk with many RVs located in a region on chromosome 14 spanning multiple genes located approximately 45 Mb from PSEN1. These findings were specific to individuals with a common variant‐derived AD PRS below the median for the sample median and accounted for primarily by their enrichment in the Amish, noting that Amish were split nearly evenly into the low‐PRS (52.4%) and high‐PRS (47.6%) groups. Evidence of suggestive associations for increased AD risk was also observed in the low‐PRS group with variants in seven additional loci, three of which were specific to the Amish. In contrast, GWS associations of reduced AD risk were detected with RVs in ALDH9A1, BICC1, and PAN3 among study participants with a high AD PRS (>65th percentile), who were predominantly non‐Amish. These findings are perhaps more robust because they were observed in the much larger non‐Amish sample and, thus, based on a larger number of RVs at each locus. There were also many suggestive associations with protective variants in the high‐PRS group, including one in S100A8 (rs140189521, p = 5.23 × 10−8) and another located between SMNDC1 and LOC105378482 (rs150913764, p = 5.84 × 10−8) that were borderline GWS.

Generally, significant associations were found with RLVs that increased AD among those with a low PRS, whereas most significant RLVs in the high‐PRS group were protective. None of the top‐ranked findings were evident in the other corresponding PRS group or in the total sample, suggesting that considering the contribution of common variants to AD risk can aid in RLV discovery. Although the benefit of searching for rare risk variants for common disease among low‐PRS individuals was previously shown, 3 , 4 to our knowledge this is the first demonstration that rare protective variants can be found among those with a high disease risk derived from aggregated common variants.

The 17 GWS and suggestive variants in the low‐PRS group on chromosome 14 are highly correlated (r 2 > 0.2, Figure 2) and likely tag one functional variant or mechanism. They span a 5.7‐Mb region encompassing many protein coding genes of known and unknown function and non‐coding RNAs. Four borderline GWS variants (p = 6.4 × 10−8) are in PRKD1, which encodes a serine/threonine protein kinase (PKD1) involved in many cellular processes, including Golgi body membrane integrity and transport, cell migration, and differentiation. PKD1 has been shown to be neuroprotective in a mouse model of AD. 25 The GWS 1‐bp insertion variant we identified at this locus (rs1019362505) is 94 kb upstream of the gene encoding syntaxin binding protein 6 (STXBP6). A recent study of Parkinson's disease (PD) dementia showed that STXBP6 was significantly differentially expressed in a two‐sample Mendelian randomization and transcriptome analysis involving data obtained from a GWAS of PD dementia and single‐cell data derived from macaques with and without PD‐like symptoms. 26 Expression of another gene at this locus, NYNRIN, is associated with LDL‐C level in liver cells. 27 NYNRIN could modulate AD risk through this mechanism as high LDL‐C is a risk factor for AD. 28 Another GWS RV in this chromosome 14 locus, rs960000673, is located 99 kb from BNIP3P1, a pseudogene of BNIP3, which encodes a mitochondrial protein (BCL2 interacting protein). Overexpression of BNIP3 was associated with neuronal death in the frontal cortex in a mouse model of brain ischemia, a cerebrovascular disease that often progresses to AD‐type dementia. 29

Among the GWS associated genes in the high‐PRS group, there is little evidence for the role of ALDH9A1 in AD pathogenesis. However, MGST3, a homolog of MGST1 located 6 kb downstream of ALDH9A1, has been linked to AD using genetic and bioinformatic approaches. For instance, an association of MGST3 with hippocampal size has been identified by comparative analysis using human GWAS data of hippocampal volume and quantitative trait locus (QTL) interval mapping data for hippocampal weight in BXD mice and subsequently demonstrated that it is significantly co‐expressed with genes in a KEGG AD pathway. 30 The MGST family of genes encodes an enzyme (microsomal glutathione S‐transferase) that catalyzes the conjugation of leukotriene A4 and reduced glutathione to produce leukotriene C4, a provocateur of inflammation. 31 Alterations in the BicC family RNA‐binding protein 1 encoded by BICC1 have been associated with major depressive disorder, and levels of this protein are significantly increased in the serum of individuals with recurrent depression and other mood disorders. 32 , 33 Depression is a risk factor for AD, and depressive disorder occurs in approximately 15% of those with AD. 34 , 35

Several of the suggestively associated genes have plausible roles in AD. A recent large multi‐ancestry GWAS for AD identified GWS association with a common variant in LRRC4C, which has a potential role in enhancer regulation in tissues and cell types related to AD pathogenesis. 36 DOCK2 expression has been localized to microglia in human and mouse brains, 37 and its absence leads to reduced amyloid burden in APPswe‐PS1Δe9 mice. 38 Eukaryotic elongation factor 2 (encoded by EEF2K) signaling has a functional role in the regulation and dysregulation of synaptic plasticity and cognitive function and has been proposed as a potential novel therapeutic approach for AD. 39 S100A8 encodes a member of the S100 family of proteins containing two EF‐hand calcium‐binding motifs. Increasing evidence links AD to S100 proteins, which have intra‐ and extracellular functions related to the regulation of several processes and are important modulators of inflammatory responses. 40 A proteomics study showed that AD patients had a lower level of S100A8 measured in plasma‐derived extracellular vesicles, which act as mediators for intercellular transfer of Aβ and tau proteins. 41 S100A8 has been proposed as a novel AD biomarker 41 and potential therapeutic target. 42 A GWS association was identified by GWAS for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)‐defined brain infarcts with a LINC00539 SNP located in the same intron less than 1200 base pairs from rs563318254. 43 a variant associated with lower risk of AD in the high‐PRS group.

Suggestive associations were also identified in the low‐PRS group with four variants in high LD spanning four genes on chromosome 10. Among these genes, the protein encoded by SFNX2, sideroflexin, is involved in mitochondrial transmembrane transport, and CNNM2 is a cyclin and CBS domain divalent metal cation transport mediator. Variants in both genes have been associated with schizophrenia. 44 A trans‐ancestry GWAS identified a GWS association of CNNM2 variants with brain arterial diameters, which are novel imaging biomarkers of cerebrovascular disease, cognitive decline, and dementia. 45

Our study has several notable limitations. The sample size was relatively small for detecting associations with RVs, especially within the stratified low‐ and high‐PRS subgroups. The reported association findings should be confirmed in a sufficiently large independent sample. However, independent AD GWAS datasets are not well suited for replication because the imputation quality for RVs (MAF < 0.5%) is suboptimal, largely due to their low frequency in haplotype reference panels. Subsequent WGS data releases by the ADSP will enlarge the sample to detect associations with smaller effect variants and in other population ancestries. Application of this approach to non‐European populations should utilize a PRS that influences AD risk in those ancestries comparable to the PRS applied in this study. 46

We also acknowledge that a PRS‐stratified GWAS is vulnerable to collider/selection bias because AD status is a collider between PRS and RVs, and the case‐control sampling already conditions strongly on AD. Because the PRS was built from common variants and the RVs are independent (no LD or inclusion in the PRS), direct overlap bias is small. However, we recognize that indirect correlations via shared biological pathways, ancestry, or selection can still induce some bias. Our analyses including PCs of ancestry reduced this concern. The mean ages of AD cases and controls are similar across PRS groups (Table S1). The proportion of females among controls is also not different across PRS groups; however, the proportion of females is slightly higher among AD cases in the high‐PRS group (p = 0.014). In addition, the MAF distributions of common variants (MAF > 0.05) not used in the calculation of the PRS were similar between AD cases and controls analyzed separately in the low‐ and high‐PRS groups. We also tested for induced correlations between PRS and RV burden. As shown in Figure S4, there was no correlation between the PRS and burden of RLVs. A related concern is that 2410 individuals in the ADSP R4 dataset used for RV association analysis (21.8% of the total sample) were included in the Kunkle et al. GWAS. Although this overlap could lead to inflated PRSs for those who were present in the base GWAS 47 and thus result in PRS group misclassification of some individuals, it would likely bias the results toward the null hypothesis rather than induce false positives.

Another potential limitation of our findings is the choice of the median value as the cutoff for low‐ and high‐PRS groups, which was arbitrary, so misclassification could adversely impact the results. To address this concern, we repeated our analyses using more extreme cutoffs for each group, which reduced power but strengthened some association signals that were not detected using the median score. Another concern is that evidence for several top‐ranked findings was derived from an Amish cohort in which the frequency of the variants was substantially greater than among other EA individuals, likely due to a founder effect. Thus, the statistical support for these specific findings may be inflated, and the results should be considered tentative until replicated in an independent sample and may not generalize broadly to other populations. However, population substructure related to the Amish subsample was accounted for at least in part by the PCs included in the analysis model. 48 The relatively recent founding of the Amish and high consanguinity rate in this population likely explain the large region on chromosome 14 harboring significantly associated variants, making it difficult to pinpoint the exact origin of the signal. Finally, ORs for many of the significantly associated variants, particularly those with MAF < 0.003 (i.e., MAC < 35), are extreme and have wide confidence intervals and so are unlikely representative of their effects in the general population.

In summary, our findings highlight the utility of a common variant PRS for detecting associations with and assessing effects of RVs related to AD and other complex disorders. Future studies delineating the functional effects of these variants and performing similar analyses in non‐European populations are warranted.
