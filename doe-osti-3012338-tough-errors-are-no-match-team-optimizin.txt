1. Abstract

 This report summarizes our contributions to the Department of Energy’s Tough Errors are no
Match (TEAM) project (DE-SC0020377) under Thrust 2: Quantum Programming and
Compilation. The central outcomes of this work included a novel efficient quantum compiling
algorithm which works without requiring the quantum computer to exactly invert its operations,
answering a longstanding open problem in quantum compiling. Additional results include the
implementation of zero-noise extrapolation error mitigation in collaboration with the Unitary
Fund, as well as novel quantum algorithms for entanglement detection and pseudorandomness.

2. Technical Description

 The central goal of this project was to develop and implement novel quantum compilation
algorithms for addressing and mitigating noise in prototype quantum computers. We made
progress on this goal in multiple ways.
 First, PI Adam Bouland and PhD student Tudor Giurgica-Tiron developed a novel quantum
compilation algorithm which removed the need for quantum devices to exactly invert their
operations (publication A, presentations A,B). This solved an open problem in the theory of
quantum compilation going back to the 1990’s.
 Our work generalized the Solovay-Kitaev Theorem which is at the core of quantum
computing. The theorem states it is possible to efficiently compile quantum programs from any
gate set to another other. In particular it says that if a quantum gate set is universal i.e. capable of
applying any quantum operation or unitary, it can also apply any unitary efficiently. More
formally, if one wishes to apply a unitary U to error epsilon, then one can efficiently compile U
using merely polylog(epsilon-1) gates from any universal gate set. Moreover, this compilation
algorithm is algorithmically efficient.
 The Solovay-Kitaev Theorem is a foundational result in quantum computing. For example, it
implies that the runtime of quantum algorithms is independent of the choice of quantum
architecture, up to small logarithmic corrections. This means, for example, that the runtime of
Shor’s factoring algorithm does not depend (asymptotically up to log factors) on whether it is
implemented on a trapped ion quantum computer or a superconducting qubit quantum computer.
So long as a quantum computer admits a set of universal operations, it can apply any quantum
program efficiently, and one can efficiently convert quantum programs from any architecture into
any other. Given the centrality of this algorithm to quantum computing, it has been improved in
myriad ways, for example to find shorter gate sequences for particular gate sets related to fault-
tolerant architectures.
 However, Solovay and Kitaev’s compilation algorithm requires that the quantum computer
be able to exactly invert its operations. In other words, if one can apply an operation g, the
device must also be capable of applying g-1 exactly. This is unrealistic on a noisy quantum
device, and inverse operations would inherently be noisy. It was a longstanding problem if
efficient quantum compilation was possible in this setting without inverses – for example this

problem is mentioned in Dawson and Nielsen’s 2005 survey on the Solovay-Kitaev algorithm, as
well as subsequent papers of Kuperberg. Even special cases of this problem – such as inverse-
free compilation on a qubit – remained open.
 Our work (publication A) gave the first efficient general-purpose quantum compiler without
using inverses – which we call an “inverse-free Solovay-Kiteav theorem.” This means one can
efficiently compile quantum algorithms between arbitrary architectures, even those without the
ability to invert operations. Our algorithm is completely general and works in arbitrary
dimensions d. The main new ingredient of our work was carefully designed pulse sequences that
“self-correct” their errors. More formally, we showed that certain specially chosen sequences of
Pauli operations are robust to errors, in the sense that applying each Pauli with error epsilon,
results in a net error at the end of the sequence of merely O(epsilon2) – in other word the error
improves by applying the sequence. We showed such sequences can be used to approximately
invert arbitrary gate operations, building on prior work of Sardharwalla, Cubitt, Harrow and
Linden and Bouland and Ozols. Our work appeared as a contributed talk at QIP (presentation A),
the top venue in quantum computing theory, as well as an invited talk at TQC (presentation B).
 Our work opened several new directions in quantum compilation. For example, in further
unpublished work with Tudor Giurgica-Tiron, we discovered that our pulse sequences are related
to robust forms of dynamical decoupling algorithms, which were developed for noise mitigation.
Also, it inspired further work of Kuperberg to further improve the runtime of the Solovay-Kitaev
with inverses. Additionally, MS student Victor Shyaka and PhD student Chenyi Zhang have since
worked to improve the runtime of this compiler to make it more practically efficient. We have
work in progress showing this is possible, which Victor has continued as an hourly contributor
after completing his MS. We anticipate a further publication of inverse-free quantum compilation
algorithms in the future based on Victor and Chenyi’s work. PhD student Jordan Docter also
performed work on algebraic quantum compilation algorithms as well, which we hope will
improve the efficiency of this algorithm in the future.
 Second, PhD student Tudor Giurgica-Tiron and MS student Yousef Hindy, in collaboration
with the Unitary Fund, implemented a novel error mitigation algorithm known as zero-noise
extrapolation (publication B). This error mitigation technique allows one to perform non-scalable
noise mitigation on near term quantum computers. Their work showed order of magnitude
improvements in the noise rate when applied to small systems. This algorithm was implemented
by the Unitary Fund in their software product Mitiq as well. Additionally MS student Victor
Shyaka later completed a remote project with the Unitary Fund on error mitigation as well.
 The techniques developed in these projects yielded several additional research products in
quantum algorithms. For example, a fundamental question in quantum algorithms is to learn
which qubits of a quantum state are entangled with one another. This could be useful for
diagnosing errors in quantum devices – for example it could reveal if the device is producing
unwanted entanglement between qubits. Our projects produced a series of works (publications
D,E,F,G,I) with PI Adam Bouland and PhD students Tudor Giurgica-Tiron, Chenyi Zhang, and
Jack Zhou showing when and where entanglement can be efficiently diagnosed, and where this is

not possible efficiently. For example, we gave the first efficient algorithm which learns which
qubits are entangled/unentangled with one another (publication I), exponentially improving on
prior work of Harrow, Lin and Montanaro showing this is information-theoretically possible. Our
algorithm used a novel quantum state variant of the hidden subgroup to efficiently learn the
entanglement structure of an unknown quantum state. Our work appeared as a plenary at QIP as
well as STOC. Our algorithm, however, requires the unentangled qubits to be perfectly
unentangled with one another. In the other direction, we showed a series of results showing that
if the qubits are slightly entangled, then efficient entanglement detection is not possible. We did
this by constructing “pseudo-entangled” quantum states (publication C) which are not very
entangled but nevertheless appear highly entangled to all efficient quantum algorithms. We
generalized this work with many constructions (publications E,F,G,H) showing that
entanglement is difficult to measure in a variety of settings. We expect future results with PhD
student Jessica Yeh in this direction as well. Our results nearly characterize when entanglement is
vs is not possible to efficiently diagnose in quantum devices.
 We also made progress on the theory of quantum advantage experiments, such as random
circuit sampling and BosonSampling, which are used to benchmark near term quantum devices
and assess their noise rates (publications J,K). Our work gave further evidence that these
benchmark tests are indeed verifying the quantum computer is truly performing a quantum task
which cannot be efficiently reproduced classically. We additionally made progress in quantum
algorithms, for example producing the most efficient quantum algorithms for unitary t-designs to
date (publication C) with PI Bouland and PhD student Jordan Docter, novel algorithms for zero-
sum games (publication L) with PI Bouland, and novel investigations of axions with PhD student
Tudor Giurgica-Tiron (publication M).
 In summary our work made progress on core problems in quantum compiling theory,
provided new quantum algorithms and hardness results for diagnosing which qubits are
entangled vs. unentangled in quantum computers, and yielded several further insights into
quantum computer science more broadly.
