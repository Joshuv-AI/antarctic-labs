Ultrasensitive detection of lung inflammation in male mice using immuno-magnetic particle imaging
Authors: Swannie Pedron, Sara Martinez de Lizarrondo, Laurent D’Arco, Jolan Malherbe, Sophie Trébel, Jurie Tashkandi, Karen Alt, Bertrand Galy, Pierre-Simon Bellaye, Jonathan Vigne, Denis Vivien, Maxime Gauberti, Thomas Bonnard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTimely and sensitive detection of inflammatory and infectious lung diseases remains a major clinical challenge. Current diagnostic approaches often identify pathology only at advanced stages, leaving gaps in acute triage, fibrotic activity assessment, transplant rejection monitoring and inflammation follow-up. Magnetic Particle Imaging (MPI) offers a sensitive, background-free, whole-body imaging modality without ionizing radiation. Here, we present immuno-Magnetic Particle Imaging (immuno-MPI) using micron-sized iron oxide particles targeted to VCAM-1, a key marker of endothelial inflammation. In three lung inflammation models in male mice, MPIO@αVCAM-1 generates high-contrast images within 3 minutes following intravenous injection at 1 mg/kg, with rapid accumulation at the inflamed endothelium and fast clearance of unbound particles. Predominantly confined to the vasculature, these particles selectively target VCAM-1 and discriminate inflamed from healthy lungs at doses as low as 0.18 mg/kg (7.5 µg iron). Specificity for VCAM-1 is confirmed by the absence of signal using control antibodies (MPIO@IgG) and competitive blocking with free αVCAM-1 antibodies. Overall, immuno-MPI provides a non-invasive approach that avoids ionizing radiation and supports pulmonary inflammation monitoring.Subject terms: Respiratory distress syndrome, Imaging the immune system, Preclinical research

Introduction

Inflammatory and infectious pulmonary diseases are associated with high morbidity and mortality worldwide. Chronic respiratory diseases are responsible for around 4 million premature deaths per year, mainly from chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases and asthma1. Pneumonia caused by pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumonia as well as viral infections like influenza or SARS-CoV-2, also contribute significantly to this burden. COVID-19 may have been responsible for over 10 million deaths over 2 years, mainly from respiratory complications2.

In this context, prompt diagnosis is key to enabling early treatment and minimizing the burden of these pathologies. Current diagnosis of lung diseases is first established from apparent respiratory symptoms and physical examination, followed by pulmonary function testing such as spirometry, pulse oximetry, or blood gas analysis, which help to identify low breath volume, airflow limitation, or hypoxemia³. The eventual presence of pathogens may be characterized by a sputum test or pleural fluid culture. Thoracic imaging, mainly performed with computed tomography or chest CT, provides crucial observation of anatomical abnormalities such as lesions, nodules, or opacifications3. However, despite the clear input of these techniques, they only reveal advanced anatomical consequences of the pathologies, and their prognostic value is limited.

Inflammation plays a central role in the pathophysiological mechanisms of many respiratory diseases. In asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, inflammation primarily involves the bronchial airways, whereas in interstitial lung diseases such as autoimmune or infectious pneumonias, it predominantly affects the alveolar tissue. Vascular inflammation is also key in conditions like thromboembolic disease or pulmonary hypertension. In many of these contexts, the inflammatory response involves increased vascular permeability and massive immune cell infiltration, contributing to edema formation, respiratory distress, and severe hypoxemia4. For this reason, anti-inflammatory treatments are often considered to counter the damages caused by hyper-inflammation and fasten the recovery of injured alveoli5,6. They constitute, for instance, the main pharmacological treatment of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) in COVID-19 patients7,8. Yet optimal dosage and timing are difficult to establish, mainly because no precise readout of the degree of lung inflammation is currently available. Importantly, such a readout is not intended to distinguish between different inflammatory etiologies but rather to provide an objective measure of inflammatory burden. In this context, a non-invasive imaging method capable of quantitatively monitoring the degree of pulmonary inflammation would represent a major clinical asset to better tailor anti-inflammatory therapies by informing both treatment timing and dosage.

Positron emission tomography (PET) with a tracer of inflammatory processes is considered for this specific purpose9. In immuno-PET, radiolabeled antibodies or small molecules specifically bind to inflammation-associated targets, such as cell adhesion molecules or activated immune cells like CD-4510. However, the widespread adoption of immuno-PET is limited by logistical constraints, such as the need for specialized facilities to handle radioactive isotopes, high costs, and concerns about radiation exposure, particularly for repeated imaging.

Magnetic particle imaging (MPI) is an emerging imaging technology that enables the detection of the non-linear magnetization signal specifically produced by exogenously injected superparamagnetic iron oxide particles11,12. It offers a unique combination of attractive features compared to the other imaging modalities. It enables a significantly more sensitive and quantitative detection of iron oxide contrast agent than magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with a similar temporal and spatial resolution to PET, without any ionizing radiation, and in a very easy-to-implement and cost-effective manner.

Vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) is overexpressed by endothelial cells under inflammatory conditions. It mediates the adhesion of leukocytes to the activated endothelium via its specific recognition of the very late antigen-4 (VLA-4) motif13. Being upregulated in the early onset of the immune mechanism and the most numerously expressed cell adhesion molecule, VCAM-1 constitutes an ideal biomarker for sensitive and early diagnosis of vascular inflammation14. For instance, revealing VCAM-1 with single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) enables quantitative monitoring of lung inflammation in breast cancer mouse models15. To date, targeting activated endothelial cells to detect lung inflammation using MPI has been proposed with iron oxide nanoparticles presenting hydrodynamic diameters ranging from 100 nm, and no specificity for the target could be demonstrated16. The use of micrometric rather than nanometric particles is supported by previous studies showing that the margination of micrometric particles is superior to nanometric by several orders of magnitude17.

In the present study, we first compared two different approaches for lung inflammation imaging by MPI, using anti-VCAM-1 monoclonal antibodies conjugated to either small nanoparticles of iron oxide (100 nm, SPIO) or larger, micron-sized particles of iron oxide (1 µm, MPIO). In a model of infectious respiratory disease induced by intranasal administration of bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS), we showed that only the use of MPIO allows for differentiation between inflamed and control lungs by MPI. We studied the effect of anti-inflammatory treatment in those experimental settings in order to assess the ability of immuno-MPI to quantitatively monitor treatment response. Our results demonstrate both higher sensitivity (with a strong signal in inflamed lungs) and higher specificity (no signal in control lungs) of MPIO for detecting lung inflammation by MPI, compared to SPIO. Using this diagnostic approach, termed “immuno-MPI,” we were also able to detect lung inflammation and monitor whole-body inflammation in several kinds of inflammation-related disease models, including sepsis and bleomycin models. The immuno-MPI technique is a promising diagnostic method that could enable rapid and quantitative assessment of lung inflammation in a simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective manner.

Results

Comparison of nano- and micron-sized probes for immuno-MPI

We synthesized two different types of targeted MPI probes by conjugating anti-VCAM-1 monoclonal antibodies to either small (SPIO) or micron-sized (MPIO) iron oxide particles (Fig. 1a), and we first compared in vitro abilities to generate a magnetic signal. SPIO@αVCAM-1 was produced using a coprecipitation method using FeCl₃ and FeCl₂18. The purified nanocrystals were then incubated with dopamine, which provides reactive functional groups for antibody conjugation. Afterward, dialysis against purified water was performed to remove excess dopamine, and the SPIOs were incubated with anti-VCAM-1 antibodies for direct bioconjugation. Following final filtration and sonication steps, SPIO@αVCAM-1 with a mean diameter of 183.5 ± 57,2 nm and a zeta potential of −15.8 ± 0.62 mV (n = 3) were obtained (Fig. 1b, Fig. S1). For MPIO@αVCAM-1, we used commercial iron oxide microparticles (Dynabeads®), consisting of an iron oxide core encapsulated in a polystyrene polymer and coated with a polyurethane layer. Their ability to generate an MPI signal is directly linked to the presence of ~8 nm maghemite nanoparticle clusters embedded within a non-superparamagnetic matrix19. Conjugation with anti-VCAM-1 antibodies was performed using a well-established protocol20. Highly uniform microparticles were obtained (Fig. S2) with a mean diameter of 908.7 ± 148.5 nm and a zeta potential of −8.7 ± 0.64 mV (n = 3) (Fig. 1b, Fig. S1). Flow cytometry analysis of the SPIO@αVCAM-1 and MPIO@αVCAM-1 particles incubated with secondary anti-rat fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) antibodies confirmed the presence of rat anti-mouse VCAM-1 (Fig. S3). Dose-response curves comparing SPIO@αVCAM-1 and MPIO@αVCAM-1 showed linear MPI signals with increasing iron concentrations, validating their potential as MPI tracers (Fig. 1c, d). At 0.3 mg/mL iron concentration, both particles exhibited equivalent MPI signals, indicating consistent iron doses and allowing for specific investigation into the effect of particle size on MPI sensitivity. A 3D-printed device with varying gap sizes was used to visually compare the spatial resolution of SPIO@αVCAM-1 and MPIO@αVCAM-1 to that of Vivotrax®, a tracer specifically developed for MPI (Fig. 1e). The MPI relax mode yielded point spread functions (PSFs) whose FWHM values were used to estimate spatial resolution based on the applied field gradient (5.7 T/m). The resulting estimated spatial resolution values were 4.21 mm for SPIO@αVCAM-1, 3.64 mm for MPIO@αVCAM-1, and 1.42 mm for Vivotrax (Fig. 1f). Although SPIO@αVCAM-1 and MPIO@αVCAM-1 exhibited lower spatial resolution, they still provided sufficient signal for MPI imaging.

LPS-induced pulmonary inflammation model

To assess the ability of the immuno-MPI technique to monitor lung inflammation and quantify its severity, we used a mouse model of infectious respiratory disease induced by intranasal administration of Escherichia coli lipopolysaccharides (3 mg/kg, LPS i.n.) (Fig. 2a). Histological analysis of lung sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin was performed to characterize this model. At 24 h post-induction, LPS i.n. mice exhibited alveolar damage, including alveolar edema, intra-alveolar hemorrhages, and peribronchovascular inflammatory infiltrates (Fig. S4A, B). Immunohistochemical staining revealed a significantly higher number of LY6G+ cells in the peribronchovascular regions and lung parenchyma of LPS i.n. mice (Fig. S4C, D). These histopathological findings are consistent with infectious respiratory disease features, confirming the validity of this model. Then, we validated the relevance of targeting endothelial VCAM-1 to detect lung inflammation. Immunohistochemical staining showed a significantly higher VCAM-1/VWF ratio in LPS i.n. mice compared to naive controls (0.805 ± 0.034 vs. 0.065 ± 0.017) and ELISA analysis of harvested lungs further confirmed an overexpression of VCAM-1 protein in LPS i.n. mice compared to naive controls (861.6 ± 119.4 vs. 542.6 ± 49.1) (Fig. S5A, D). Other CAM proteins, such as P-Selectin, ICAM-1, and PECAM, were also evaluated but did not exhibit such overexpression under inflammatory conditions (Fig. S5E).

Particle size effect on pulmonary immuno-MPI

Whole-body immuno-MPI was performed 24 h after intranasal LPS administration in both LPS-treated and naive mice. Mice received an intravenous injection of 1 mg/kg of either SPIO@αVCAM-1 or MPIO@αVCAM-1, and 2D imaging was initiated 10 or 3 min later, respectively, according to the plasma half-life of magnetic particles (Fig. 2a). Since MPI exclusively detects magnetic tracers, images were acquired only after intravenous injection. Although background signals from feces or hemorrhagic areas have been described in the literature, they were considered negligible in our experimental setting. Moreover, MPI provides a linear and tracer-specific signal (Fig. 1c), allowing quantification to be performed by measuring the mean intensity within defined regions of interest directly on the reconstructed images. Following injection of SPIO@αVCAM-1, no significant pulmonary signal was detected in either LPS i.n.-treated or naive mice (31.4 ± 5.3 vs. 36.0 ± 3.1, n = 4, p < 0.05). In contrast, a significantly higher lung signal was detected in LPS i.n.-treated mice compared to naive controls when larger particles, MPIO@αVCAM-1, were used (51.2 ± 6.2 vs. 16.9 ± 1.5, n = 4, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2b, c). The lung signal observed with MPIO@αVCAM-1 reflects the ability of micron-sized targeted particles to rapidly bind to VCAM-1 expressed on inflamed pulmonary endothelium, while the unbound fraction is efficiently cleared by the liver. To improve the assessment of lung inflammation, we used the lung-to-liver MPI signal ratio to account for hepatic clearance and normalize tracer accumulation (Fig. 2d). This ratio was close to 1 in LPS i.n.-treated mice injected with MPIO@αVCAM-1, indicating that the pulmonary signal was nearly equivalent to the hepatic signal. A significantly higher ratio was observed in LPS i.n.-treated mice compared to naive controls with MPIO@αVCAM-1, but not with SPIO@ αVCAM-1. Finally, to confirm the source of the signal and ensure more accurate quantification, the lungs were harvested after imaging and placed in the MPI scanner for ex vivo 2D acquisition. These analyses confirmed the presence of a magnetic signal in the lungs of LPS i.n.-treated mice that received MPIO@αVCAM-1 (15.24 ± 2.02 vs. 5.45 ± 1.01, n = 5, p < 0.05), while no significant difference was observed in the ex vivo lung signal of mice that received SPIO@αVCAM-1 (4.3 ± 1.53 vs 5.69 ± 0.68) (Fig. 2e, f). Taken together, these preliminary experiments suggest that micron-sized particles are better suited for immuno-imaging of pulmonary inflammation by MPI.

Detection of VCAM-1 expression by immuno-MPI

We investigated the signal specificity of MPIO@αVCAM-1 for detecting lung inflammation using 3D MPI acquisition, which allows precise spatial localization of magnetic tracers throughout the entire volume (Fig. 3a). In LPS i.n.-treated mice, the magnetic signal was predominantly localized in the lungs, whereas in naive mice, it was mainly detected in the liver (1.28 ± 0.12 vs. 0.73 ± 0.07 in the lungs; 0.96 ± 0.05 vs. 1.41 ± 0.07 in the liver, n = 4, p < 0.05, Fig. 3b). These findings highlight the relevance of MPI for characterizing the plasma kinetics of targeted micron-sized particles, with rapid accumulation in the pulmonary vasculature under inflammatory conditions and no significant binding in naive animals (Fig. 3c). The localization of the magnetic signal was confirmed using CT-scan imaging, as shown in coronal and axial sections (Fig. 3d). In naive mice, the magnetic signal was observed in the liver and sometimes in the spleen, two key organs of the mononuclear phagocyte system responsible for clearing circulating large particles. CD68 immunostaining of liver sections in naive mice confirmed that MPIO@αVCAM-1 accumulated in hepatic macrophages (Fig. S6), thereby confirming that the liver signal corresponds to passive accumulation in the macrophages. To confirm the specificity of the signal to VCAM-1 expression, MPIO@IgG was injected in LPS i.n.-treated mice, and no significant lung signal was detected (0.82 ± 0.006 vs. 1.29 ± 0.11, n = 4, p < 0.05, Fig. 3e, f), in line with a free flow through the microcirculation and an absence of binding. Instead, a strong liver signal was observed, confirming that the liver signal is due to the normal physiological clearance of the particles and not to VCAM-1 expression. In addition, these findings are consistent with one of our recent studies, in which we more precisely characterized the elimination kinetics of similar particles using whole-body dynamic PET imaging, revealing rapid clearance predominantly by the liver and spleen21. Together, these results demonstrate that immuno-MPI using MPIO@αVCAM-1 is both sensitive and specific for detecting VCAM-1 overexpression in the pulmonary vasculature.

Immuno-MPI for monitoring therapeutic response

To evaluate the potential of immuno-MPI as a tool for monitoring therapeutic efficacy, we investigated its ability to quantify the resolution of pulmonary inflammation following anti-inflammatory therapy. Mice received dexamethasone at 6, 24, and 32 h after LPS intranasal administration, and imaging was performed 48 h later (Fig. 4a). Immuno-MPI imaging demonstrated a clear and marked reduction of lung signal in dexamethasone-treated animals compared with untreated LPS i.n. mice (Fig. 4b). 2D in vivo acquisitions used for quantification confirmed this visual decrease (Fig. 4c), with a significant reduction in total lung MPI signal in treated mice (20.04 ± 1.50 vs. 36.57 ± 2.92, n = 4, p < 0.05, Fig. 4d). These results were supported by quantitative analysis of ex vivo imaging performed on harvested lungs (4.70 ± 0.48 vs. 15.83 ± 1.62, n = 4, p < 0.05, Fig. 4e, f). This ability to non-invasively monitor dynamic changes in lung inflammation highlights the translational potential of immuno-MPI as a tool for treatment stratification and therapeutic response assessment in inflammatory lung diseases. Such an approach may provide objective imaging biomarkers to support multidisciplinary decision-making in clinical practice.

Immuno-MPI of systemic inflammatory response

Then we applied immuno-MPI in a murine model of experimental sepsis induced by intraperitoneal injection of LPS (5 mg/kg, LPS i.p.) to assess its ability to detect and quantify lung inflammation resulting from a systemic inflammatory response. ELISA analysis of harvested lungs confirmed increased VCAM-1 protein expression in LPS i.p.-treated mice, supporting the relevance of this molecular target for imaging lung inflammation in this model (Fig. S5C). Importantly, the ELISA quantification of VCAM-1 protein in harvested lungs from naive, LPS i.n. and LPS i.p. indicated a proportional increase in VCAM-1 expression (Fig. S5D) that was consistent with the corresponding lung MPI signal intensity, supporting a close relationship between ex vivo protein quantification and in vivo molecular imaging.

Despite the absence of visible morphological changes on CT scans (Fig. S7), MPI/CT imaging revealed a predominant magnetic signal in the lungs of LPS i.p.-treated mice following MPIO@αVCAM-1 injection. In contrast, naive animals showed higher signal intensity in the liver and spleen, consistent with nonspecific clearance (Fig. 5a–d). Kinetic studies in the sepsis model at 30 min, 3 h, 6 h, and 24 h after injection showed that the signal in LPS i.p.-treated mice peaked immediately after injection and gradually decreased in the lungs over time. Conversely, the liver signal progressively increased, confirming the detachment of MPIO@αVCAM-1 and their uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system (Fig. S8).

In some LPS-treated mice, a magnetic signal was detected in the kidneys in addition to the signal present in the lungs (Fig. 5b, c). No significant difference was found in vivo in the kidneys or in the heart, but some heterogeneity in the signal was noted (1.1 ± 0.3 vs 0.5 ± 0.1, n = 4, p = 0.2 for the kidneys and 1.6 ± 0.2 vs 0.7 ± 0.1 for the heart) (Fig. 5d). It is important to note that, for the heart, in vivo identification of this signal remains difficult in this model due to its proximity to the lungs. Ex vivo MPI studies on organs from LPS i.p.-treated and naive mice confirmed the presence of MPIO@αVCAM-1 magnetic signal in the lungs, the kidneys, and the heart of LPS i.p. mice. These results are linked to the model of intraperitoneal LPS injection, which causes systemic inflammation, including vascular inflammation in the heart and kidney medulla. In naive mice, the magnetic signal in the liver was significantly higher due to the clearance of MPIOs from the circulation by the mononuclear phagocyte system (Fig. 5e, f).

To further evaluate the relevance of immuno-MPI in a more clinically representative context, we applied the same imaging protocol to the cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model of polymicrobial sepsis (Fig. S9A). This model induces a progressive and sustained inflammatory response that more closely mimics human sepsis. Immuno-MPI revealed a clear pulmonary signal in a subset of CLP mice at 24 and 72 h after CLP, as well as a systemic reaction (Fig. S9B, C). Overall, however, quantitative analysis did not show a significant intergroup difference, possibly due to the substantial inter-individual variability inherent to the CLP model.

To ensure that the observed MPI signal was specifically due to the recognition of MPIO@αVCAM-1 by activated endothelium, control experiments were also conducted in the sepsis model with another comparison between MPIO@αVCAM-1 and MPIO functionalized with a control isotype (MPIO@IgG). While MPIO@αVCAM-1 induced a clear magnetic signal in the lungs 24 h after intraperitoneal injection of LPS, no lung signal was observed in LPS i.p.-treated mice that received MPIO@IgG (Fig. 6a, b, 1,5 ± 0.14 vs 0.5 ± 0.07, n = 4–5, p < 0.05). Moreover, when LPS i.p.-treated mice were pre-treated intravenously with αVCAM-1 monoclonal antibodies to block the MPIO@αVCAM-1 binding sites, no significant binding of MPIO@αVCAM-1 was observed in the lungs, and the entire signal was detected in the liver, confirming the specificity of the MPIO@αVCAM-1 signal in this sepsis model (Fig. 6c–e, 0.5 ± 0.07 vs 2.1 ± 0,07 for the signal in the lungs and 1.8 ± 0.05 vs 1.14 ± 0.12 for the signal in the liver, n = 4, p < 0.05). Histological observation further confirmed the presence of MPIO@αVCAM-1 in the inflamed vascular beds of the lungs in LPS i.p.-treated mice (Fig. 6f). We examined the histological sections thoroughly, and particles were exclusively found bound to VCAM- 1-positive endothelium confined to the vascular compartment (Fig. S10).

We also confirmed that the MPIO@αVCAM-1 accumulated efficiently in the inflamed lungs of LPS i.p.-treated mice by ex vivo MRI (Fig. S11). No hyposignal could be detected in the lungs by in vivo MRI, as they already appear black on T2*-weighted sequences prior to MPIO injection due to the air trapped in the alveoli. MPI appears to be a promising alternative technique for detecting magnetic signals in the lungs without background interference.

Detection threshold and dose-response in immuno-MPI

Dose-escalation experiments were conducted in LPS i.p.-treated and naive mice to evaluate the sensitivity of immuno-MPI for detecting pulmonary inflammation in severe sepsis (Fig. 7a). In LPS i.p.-treated mice, an iron dose as low as 7.5 µg of MPIO@αVCAM-1 (0.18 mg/kg) was sufficient to generate a distinct pulmonary signal, clearly distinguishable from the background hepatic signal (Fig. 7b). This dose was also sufficient to significantly differentiate lung signal between LPS i.p.-treated and naive mice (Fig. 6c). These results underscore the high sensitivity of immuno-MPI, as even minimal tracer doses can reveal pulmonary inflammation. They also point to the strong translational potential of immuno-MPI, as signal detection may be even more favorable in humans due to larger organ volumes. Such performance achieved in a small-animal model suggests strong potential for clinical translation with imaging using low iron exposure.

To further characterize the sensitivity of immuno-MPI and assess its ability to reflect the severity of inflammation, we next investigated whether the targeted MPIO signal correlated with the degree of pulmonary inflammation. We administered different intraperitoneal doses of LPS (0, 2.5, 5, or 10 mg/kg), followed 24 h later by intravenous injection of MPIO@αVCAM-1 (1 mg/kg) and whole-body immuno-MPI. Almost no magnetic signal was observed in the lungs of naive mice and those receiving 2.5 mg/kg of LPS. Starting from the dose of 5 mg/kg of LPS, significant pulmonary uptake was detected, with signal intensity increasing in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 7d, e, 0.78 ± 0.13 for naive, 0.86 ± 0.04 for 2.5 mg/kg, 1.43 ± 0.03 for 5 mg/kg and 2.39 ± 0,19 for 10 mg/kg, n = 4, p < 0.05). This dose-response experiment allowed us to determine the threshold of inflammation required for reliable detection by immuno-MPI and to demonstrate its capacity to quantify varying degrees of inflammatory burden.

Detection of early inflammatory changes in lung fibrosis

To determine whether immuno-MPI can detect lung inflammation in a more chronic setting, we applied the technique to a bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model (Fig. 8). Mice were imaged longitudinally by whole-body immuno-MPI/CT at days 4, 10, and 18 after bleomycin administration, followed by [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 injection at day 18. Whole-body immuno-MPI/CT and [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET/MRI were also performed on naive mice to provide baseline signal comparison. Immuno-MPI imaging revealed a significant increase in pulmonary MPI signal at day 4 compared with naive mice, indicating early inflammatory activation (Fig. 8a, b, 34.84 ± 4.21 at D4 vs 15.90 ± 0.42 for naive, n = 4, p < 0.05). This signal decreased at days 10 and 18, while liver signal remained unchanged, confirming lung-specific variations (Fig. 8a, b, 23.7 ± 1.85 at D10 and 18.85 ± 0.92 at D18). These results are confirmed by ex vivo MPI (Fig. 8c, d). In contrast, CT imaging showed a progressive increase in lung density following bleomycin exposure, with significant changes observed at days 10 and 18 (Fig. 8e, f). Longitudinal analysis demonstrated that the MPI signal peaked early and declined thereafter, whereas the CT-derived lung density increased over time, reflecting the transition from inflammation to fibrosis (Fig. 8g). At day 18, [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 PET/MRI showed significantly higher pulmonary uptake in bleomycin-treated mice compared with naive animals, consistent with fibroblast activation (Fig. 8h, i). Histological analysis confirmed the presence of fibrotic lesions in bleomycin-exposed lungs (Fig. 8j). Together, these results indicate that immuno-MPI enables early detection of pulmonary inflammation preceding fibrotic remodeling and provides complementary information to CT and FAPI-PET imaging.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that pulmonary inflammation can be detected by Magnetic Particle Imaging (MPI) in both an infectious respiratory disease and a sepsis model, using large particles of iron oxide. Unlike conventional MPI tracers, these micrometer-sized particles (MPIO@αVCAM-1) enable the visualization of inflammation with excellent sensitivity and specificity immediately after injection.

One of the key strengths of our approach lies in the use of micrometer-sized iron oxide particles, which offer several advantages over smaller nanoparticles commonly used in molecular imaging. Due to their larger size, these particles remain confined to the vascular compartment, preventing nonspecific extravasation into surrounding tissues. The second interesting aspect of large particles for immuno-MPI is their improved “margination”. Margination refers to the movement of particles along the endothelial walls when present in a flow, such as blood flow22. Larger particles are more easily pushed toward the vessel walls by circulating red blood cells, while smaller particles (less than 100 nm) tend to circulate more centrally in the vessel23,24. Thus, in an inflammatory context, MPIO@αVCAM-1 particles are more likely to interact with the VCAM-1 proteins overexpressed on the luminal side of the vascular wall. Moreover, as described by Fonnum et al. the MPIOs are made of clusters of ~8 nm maghemite nanoparticles embedded within a non-superparamagnetic polymer matrix, a configuration that provides favorable magnetic properties for MPI signal generation19. In our study, this structure proved particularly advantageous for targeting vascular inflammation.

In contrast, smaller superparamagnetic iron oxide particles (SPIOs and USPIOs) have demonstrated more limited efficacy for molecular imaging of VCAM-1. A recent paper used SPIOs conjugated to a peptide targeting the VCAM-1 protein17, but this approach lacks specificity, as previous studies have shown that USPIOs coupled with anti-VCAM-1 peptides or antibodies exhibit limited sensitivity24. Although MPI is a promising tool to overcome this lack of specificity, the authors did not verify whether the signal obtained was truly specific to VCAM-1 expression and not related to nonspecific nanoparticle accumulation. Moreover, the authors did not show whole-body images, and the transversal sections included do not enable comparison of the signal with the liver uptake. It is probable that using nanosized iron oxide particles, no specific lung signal could be obtained. Indeed, we have compared the efficacy of small particles of iron oxide (SPIO) presenting a mean diameter of 150 nm with a 1 µm microparticle of iron oxide (MPIO) and demonstrated that the larger diameter is a requirement to yield efficient targeting of the inflamed lung.

In our study, we verified signal specificity in both inflammatory models using iron oxide microparticles conjugated to an isotype control. We also performed competition experiments with pre-injection of a VCAM-1 antibody to block VCAM-1 sites and demonstrated that the signal decreased in the absence of specific binding. Together, these results confirm that the MPI signal obtained is indeed specific to the recognition of MPIO@αVCAM-1 on overexpressed VCAM-1 proteins. We demonstrated, in three models showing overexpression of the VCAM-1 protein in the lungs, that the immuno-MPI strategy using MPIO@αVCAM-1 allows the detection of pulmonary inflammation compared to control animals.

The signal obtained with MPIO@αVCAM-1 reflects the immune response, hence the name “immuno-MPI” for this technique. Similar to immuno-PET or the more recently developed immuno-MRI, an antibody is conjugated to a signal-generating particle, and the resulting signal reflects the expression of a specific immune-related molecule. In this study, we confirmed through ex vivo ELISA performed on extracted lungs that VCAM-1 is overexpressed under inflammatory conditions, making it a valuable target for inflammation detection.

The sepsis model induced by intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharides (LPS i.p.) is frequently used in the literature, particularly in immuno-MRI, to study renal25 or intestinal inflammation26. This model triggers a systemic response in animals, causing damage to multiple organs (kidneys, intestines, but also heart and brain), mimicking widespread inflammation throughout the body. The immuno-MPI method allows us to assess this multisystemic involvement and revealed significant pulmonary inflammation in this model, which was previously undetected by in vivo MRI due to the low proton density in the lungs, limiting MRI studies using superparamagnetic contrast agents (Fig. S8). With immuno-MPI, we demonstrated that the inflammation signal in the lung from the sepsis animals on 2D in vivo acquisitions was found significantly different from the healthy lung of the naive group from a dose as low as 7.5 µg of iron (corresponding to 0.18 mg Fe/kg), which underlines the high sensitivity of the immuno-MPI technique. Consistent with this observation, recent work using an intraperitoneal LPS-induced sepsis model in mice demonstrated that inflammatory and endothelial responses are not uniformly distributed across organs but are predominantly localized in the lungs, which exhibit the most pronounced vascular inflammation, whereas other highly vascularized organs display a more limited or heterogeneous response27.

The infectious respiratory disease model, induced by intranasal administration of LPS (LPS i.n.), is comparable to a bacterial pneumonia model. Less severe than the sepsis model, it demonstrates that immuno-MPI can detect lower levels of inflammation. Interestingly, in this model, the difference in signal between the lungs and liver is less pronounced than in the sepsis model, suggesting that VCAM-1 sites in the lungs are saturated with MPIO@αVCAM-1, with the remaining microparticles accumulating in the liver and the spleen.

To further validate our findings, we applied immuno-MPI to a bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model. This model combines an acute inflammatory phase with subsequent fibrotic remodeling, thereby representing a more complex disease process. Immuno-MPI revealed a marked increase in pulmonary signal at an early time, which decreased as the fibrotic phase progressed. In contrast, CT imaging detected structural changes only at later stages. These results demonstrate that immuno-MPI is sensitive to inflammatory events that precede fibrotic remodeling, providing temporal information that complements conventional imaging modalities. Importantly, in fibrotic diseases, the ability to visualize and quantify active pulmonary inflammation could be highly valuable to identify patients with ongoing inflammatory activity who may benefit from immunosuppressive therapy. Together with the sepsis and infectious respiratory disease models, the bleomycin model illustrates that immuno-MPI can detect both acute and evolving inflammatory processes.

The most attractive feature of MPI over PET is that instead of radioactive tracers, it uses magnetic particles, which are non-ionizing for patients, easier to handle, and stable over time with a spatial resolution approaching that of PET, making it a highly promising modality for molecular imaging in humans. Moreover, as a tomographic imaging technique, it enables whole-body imaging and provides rapid multiple-organ assessment, which is particularly relevant to assessing inflammation in systemic disease such as sepsis. Finally, for moderate iron concentrations, as presented in this article, the MPI signal is largely proportional to the iron concentration, allowing for quantitative and sensitive imaging.

Several limitations of the present study should nevertheless be acknowledged. In particular, some experiments that would further strengthen the translational characterization of immuno-MPI, including larger cohorts in complex models such as CLP-induced sepsis, extended longitudinal evaluations, and additional comparisons with other molecular imaging approaches, were beyond the scope and timeline of the present study. These aspects will constitute important directions for future work.

Despite advances in clinical imaging, the management of pulmonary inflammation remains constrained by the limitations of current diagnostic tools. Most imaging modalities—such as chest X-ray, CT, or even functional imaging techniques—primarily detect anatomical or physiological abnormalities that appear only at relatively advanced stages of disease. As a result, there is currently no reliable imaging method to reflect inflammatory activity at an early or subclinical stage, when therapeutic intervention would be most effective. This lack of sensitivity delays diagnosis and limits the ability to monitor treatment response in real time. Our results suggest that molecular imaging of endothelial inflammatory markers such as VCAM-1 could overcome these limitations by providing a direct and quantitative readout of pulmonary inflammatory activity. In clinical settings where anti-inflammatory therapies are considered—such as ARDS or severe pneumonia28,29—this approach could help identify patients with a predominant inflammatory phenotype who are most likely to benefit from treatment. Moreover, longitudinal imaging of inflammation could support therapy monitoring and adjustment by objectively tracking the resolution or persistence of inflammatory burden over time. Regarding interstitial lung diseases (ILD), this issue is particularly critical. Immunosuppressive therapies have demonstrated benefit when ILD is associated with autoimmune diseases driven by pathological inflammatory processes, which represent a substantial proportion of ILD cases (7.5–34.8%)30. Conversely, in predominantly fibrotic ILD without active inflammation, such therapies may be harmful, as strikingly demonstrated by the PANTHER trial31, which showed increased mortality under immunosuppressive treatment in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. In routine practice, distinguishing inflammatory from purely fibrotic disease remains challenging, as clinical and radiological patterns frequently overlap, and PET-CT has not proven sufficiently informative for this purpose. Therapeutic decisions therefore rely on multidisciplinary discussion integrating clinical, biological, and imaging data, highlighting the urgent need for a reliable, non-invasive imaging technology capable of directly assessing inflammatory activity within the lung parenchyma.

In this context, immuno-MPI emerges as a highly promising solution. It would provide longitudinal monitoring of inflammation, helping to determine whether fibrotic lesions are still active or whether a transplanted lung is undergoing immune rejection. It may also allow for better characterization of lesions with prognostic value based on the inflammatory status measured, thereby guiding clinical decisions more accurately. Importantly, all of this can be achieved without exposing patients to ionizing radiation. No other imaging modality offers this combination of molecular specificity, safety, sensitivity, and practicality. PET is sensitive but involves ionizing radiation and, most importantly, is not widely accessible and suitable for bedside use, as critically ill patients cannot easily be transported to a nuclear medicine facility. MRI offers excellent resolution but is technically difficult to apply to the lungs, and CT lacks the sensitivity and molecular specificity required. Immuno-MPI stands out as a uniquely suited technique to fill this gap.

Overall, this study demonstrated the efficacy of immuno-MPI for the diagnosis of pulmonary inflammation, offering potential clinical applications. MPI systems for clinical use are currently under development. Additionally, it will be necessary to develop contrast agents similar to MPIO@αVCAM-1 that can be used in humans. A biocompatible and biodegradable version has recently been proposed18. Nonetheless, this article establishes the proof of concept for the feasibility of this approach in detecting whole-body pulmonary inflammation.

Methods

Production of SPIO@αVCAM-1

SPIO@αVCAM-1 were obtained through a synthesis previously described in the literature18. First, SPIOs were produced through a coprecipitation process in an alkaline buffer. 540 mg of FeCl₃·6H₂O and 198.8 mg of FeCl₂·4H₂O were dissolved in 5.7 ml of distilled water by vortexing, resulting in a homogeneous yellow solution. Under continuous stirring at room temperature, 6.3 ml of a 13% ammonia solution was gradually added at a rate of 0.2 ml/min. The solution transitioned from yellow to brown and ultimately to a deep black color, indicating the formation of magnetite. The precipitate was washed five times with distilled water via magnetic separation. The purified nanocrystals were then incubated with dopamine to introduce reactive functional groups for antibody conjugation. Excess dopamine was removed by dialysis (10-kDa cutoff) and a subsequent filtration step through a 1.2 µm filter. Finally, the dopamine-functionalized SPIOs were incubated with anti-VCAM-1 antibodies in a 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8.5) for direct bioconjugation. The dopamine coating presents surface-exposed amine groups that can interact covalently with electrophilic sites naturally available on the antibody under mildly alkaline conditions. This leads to the formation of stable chemical linkages between the dopamine-functionalized SPIO surface and lysine-associated groups on the antibody, yielding a robust SPIO@αVCAM-1 conjugate. After conjugation, the SPIO particles were washed in 0.3 M mannitol containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to block any remaining active groups, then rinsed in 0.3 M mannitol containing 0.1% BSA and subjected to a low-intensity sonication for 20 s to ensure proper dispersion. Unbound antibodies were eliminated through several cycles of magnetic purification, and the final product was resuspended in an isotonic mannitol buffer and briefly sonicated to disperse any aggregates.

Production of MPIO@αVCAM-1

MPIO@αVCAM-1 were produced using commercial iron oxide microparticles (Dynabeads®) conjugated to purified monoclonal rat anti-mouse VCAM-1 antibodies, following a well-established protocol. The conjugation process involved incubating the MPIOs with the antibodies (clone A(429); BD Biosciences) in borate buffer (pH 9.5) at 37 °C for 48 h, using 40 μg of antibody per 1 mg of reactive MPIOs. Under these alkaline conditions, primary amine groups on lysine residues of the antibody become more nucleophilic and react with the tosyl-activated surface of the MPIOs. This nucleophilic substitution forms stable covalent bonds between the antibody and the particle surface, ensuring robust and long-lasting conjugation. After conjugation, the particles were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to block any remaining active groups. The MPIOs were then rinsed in PBS (0.1% BSA) and subjected to a low-intensity sonication for 60 s to ensure proper dispersion. Finally, the MPIO@αVCAM-1 was stored at 4 °C in PBS under constant agitation to prevent aggregation. For control experiments, MPIO@IgG was prepared by replacing the anti-VCAM-1 antibody with control rat IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch) using the same conjugation conditions. Additional control particles (MPIO@BSA) were prepared following the same protocol, except that no antibody was added. Through the study, fresh batches of antibody-functionalized particles (SPIO@VCAM-1, MPIO@VCAM-1, and MPIO@IgG) were prepared for every experiment and were not kept for longer than 2 weeks. Before every set of experiments, particles were concentrated on the side with a magnet, and the protein content of the supernatant (2 μL) was checked via UV-Vis light absorption (280 nm, NanoDrop Spectrophotometer) to verify the absence of detectable antibody release from the particles. We then added 2 μL of buffer to compensate for the volume difference and resuspended the particle suspension via strong vortexing before use.

Verification of antibody conjugation by Flow Cytometry

The efficacy of antibody conjugation was verified by flow cytometry analysis. SPIO@αVCAM-1, SPIO@BSA, MPIO@αVCAM-1, and MPIO@BSA were washed twice with a 0.3 M mannitol solution and incubated with secondary anti-rat fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) secondary antibodies to reveal the presence of rat anti-mouse VCAM-1 (or anti-rabbit FITC for isotype mimicking control condition for 30 min at room temperature in the dark). After incubation, the particles were washed twice and resuspended in a buffer containing 0.3 M mannitol and 2% BSA before analysis by flow cytometry (FACSVerse cytometer, BD Biosciences). The gating strategy first excluded debris based on forward and side scatter parameters, followed by selection of the main particle population according to FSC/SSC characteristics. FITC-positive events were then quantified relative to control particles incubated without primary antibody. The results were analyzed using FlowJo version 7.6.5 (BD Biosciences).

Size and zeta potential measurement

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were performed using a Zeta sizer Ultra (Malvern Panalytical®) equipped with a 633 nm laser at a fixed scattering angle of 173°. SPIO@αVCAM-1 and MPIO@αVCAM-1 suspension was diluted 1/100 (v/v) in distilled water. Zeta potential measurements were obtained after dispersing the samples in 10⁻³ M KCl, using the same equipment with a DTS 1070 cell at 25 °C. The zeta potential was calculated from the electrophoretic mobility using the Smoluchowski equation. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

In vitro magnetic particle imaging

Magnetic particle imaging was performed using an MPI scanner (Momentum CT, Magnetic Insight, Inc., Alameda, CA, USA) with a magnetic gradient strength of 5.7 T/m. In vitro dose-response experiments were conducted using a 2D imaging protocol on 3 µL of various diluted concentrations of SPIO@αVCAM-1 or MPIO@αVCAM-1. Samples were digested in acid, and the resulting solutions were analyzed to quantify iron content based on its isotopic signature. Relaxometry scans were performed using the relax module on 100 µL of SPIO@αVCAM-1, MPIO@αVCAM-1, or 3 µL of Vivotrax in individual PCR tubes. These scans yielded point spread functions (PSFs) expressed in units of magnetic field (mT). The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of each PSF was first determined in mT. An estimate of the corresponding spatial resolution (in mm) was then obtained by dividing the FWHM value by the applied magnetic field gradient (5.7 T/m). This approach provides an indirect estimate of spatial resolution and does not account for image reconstruction or system-specific imaging effects32.

TEM observation of MPIO@αVCAM-1

To observe MPIO@αVCAM-1 in suspension using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), a droplet of the particle suspension was deposited and dried on a glow-discharged (hydrophilized) 200-mesh formvar/carbon grid. Images of MPIO@αVCAM-1 were captured using an ORIUS 200 digital camera (GATAN) mounted on a JEOL 1011 transmission electron microscope. Sample preparation and image acquisition were performed at CMAbio3 (Centre de Microscopie Appliquée à la Biologie, Biological Microscopy Facility, UFR des Sciences, US EMerode, University of Caen Normandy).

Animals

All experiments were performed on 8-week-old male Swiss mice (CURB, Centre Universitaire de Ressources Biologiques, Basse-Normandie, France). The animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at CURB and had free access to food and tap water. Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled room (21 °C), with 55% humidity and 100 lux brightness, under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. They were kept in small stable social groups (5 per cage) with minimal enrichment (shredded paper strips and compressed cotton squares) and handled only by trained staff. All experimental procedures were approved by the local ethical committee of Normandy (CENOMEXA – comité d’éthique normand en matière d’expérimentation animale, APAFIS#44363, APAFIS#57314). For each intravenous administration of the particles, mice were deeply anesthetized with 4% isoflurane. A catheter was then inserted into the tail vein while anesthesia was maintained with 1.5–2.0% isoflurane. During the entire procedure, body temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C using a feedback-regulated heating system with a rectal probe.

Infectious respiratory disease model (LPS i.n.)

LPS from E. coli (5 mg/mL, 0111:B4, Sigma-Aldrich, Lisle d’Abeau, France) dissolved in saline was administered intranasally (i.n.). Swiss mice were deeply anesthetized with 4% isoflurane and maintained under anesthesia with 2.0% isoflurane. A total volume of 50 μL of LPS solution was then administered intranasally by placing the solution into the nostrils, allowing inhalation.

Treatment response evaluation

To evaluate the anti-inflammatory response, mice received dexamethasone (1 mg/kg, i.p.) at 6, 24, and 32 h following LPS inhalation.

LPS-induced systemic inflammation model (LPS i.p.)

Swiss mice received an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of LPS from E. coli (2.5–10 mg/kg, 0111:B4, Sigma-Aldrich, Lisle d’Abeau, France).

CLP systemic inflammation model

Clinically relevant polymicrobial sepsis was induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP). Mice were deeply anesthetized with 4% isoflurane and maintained under anesthesia with 2.5–3.0% isoflurane. To prevent postoperative pain, a subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine (Buprecare®, 0.1 mg/kg) was administered prior to surgery. The cecum was accessed by a short median laparotomy, ligated at its external third, and punctured through and through with a 23-G needle to allow the release of fecal material. All mice received an intraperitoneal injection of sterile saline for fluid resuscitation immediately post-surgery.

Bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis model

Mice were deeply anesthetized with 4% isoflurane and maintained under anesthesia with 2.0% isoflurane. They were suspended by their incisors on an inclined platform, and the nasal passage was temporarily occluded to ensure oropharyngeal respiration. Bleomycin (2 mg/kg, B053, TOKU-E, Bellingham, WA) was delivered in a total volume of 50 µL using a syringe fitted with a flexible catheter. Correct tracheal placement was confirmed by transillumination before injection. Mice were monitored for 15 s post-procedure to ensure no reflux.

In vivo magnetic particle imaging

In vivo Magnetic Particle Imaging was performed 24 h after intranasal or intraperitoneal LPS administration. Anesthetized mice were placed in the MPI/CT bed with the temperature maintained at 37 °C and received an intravenous injection of 1 mg/kg (equivalent Fe) of SPIO@αVCAM-1, MPIO@αVCAM-1, or MPIO@IgG. Imaging was performed 10 min after administration of SPIO@αVCAM-1, and 3 min after administration of either MPIO@αVCAM-1 or MPIO@IgG, in accordance with their respective plasma half-lives. The total scan time per mouse was approximately 3 min for 2D imaging and between 18 and 23 min for 3D imaging. The scanning mode selected was multichannel, and 3D MPI images were acquired with 21 projections and a field of view of 10 × 6 × 6 cm (slice thickness of 0.25 mm).

Competition experiments

For the competition experiments, mice received an intravenous injection of 100 µg of either control immunoglobulin or free anti-VCAM-monoclonal antibodies 15 min before imaging and injection of conjugated MPIO@αVCAM-1.

MPIO@αVCAM-1 dose-response experiments

For the competition experiments, mice received successive injections of MPIO@αVCAM-1 with 2D imaging between each injected dose: 0 µg, 7.5 µg, 15 µg, 30 µg, or 43,5 µg (equivalent Fe).

MPIO@αVCAM-1 kinetics experiments

For the MPIO@αVCAM-1 kinetics study, the mice were awakened after the first scan (time = 30 min), then re-anesthetized and placed back into the MPI scanner at 3 h, 6 h, and 24 h after the initial scan.

Ex vivo study

For ex vivo imaging, animals were first euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia, after which the organs of interest were immediately harvested without perfusion and placed on Parafilm inside the MPI scanner. Two-dimensional images of each organ were acquired. This approach allows for detailed visualization of the biodistribution of SPIO@αVCAM-1 or MPIO@αVCAM-1 within the tissues.

MPI signal analyses

The MPI data analysis was performed using ImageJ v1.52 (National Institutes of Health). For in vivo MPI signal quantification, a region of interest (ROI) was delineated on the CT image and transferred to the MPI image. The mean MPI signal within the ROI was then measured for quantitative analysis. While most of the analysis was conducted on 3D MPI images, some was performed on 2D images to reduce total acquisition time. The ex vivo MPI signal was quantified on 2D images and expressed as a percentage of the injected dose (%ID) by normalizing the total signal of each organ to the total body MPI signal.

Automated radiosynthesis of [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46

The FAPI-46 precursor and non-radioactive reference standard were supplied by SOFIE Bioscience. Gallium-68 was obtained from a pharmaceutical-grade 68Ge/68Ga generator (GALLIAD®, IRE Elit). Automated radiosynthesis of [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46 was performed using a GAIA® synthesis module (Elysia-Raytest) equipped with single-use fluidic cassettes and reagent kits supplied by ABX Pharmaceuticals. Radiolabeling was carried out by reacting 50 µg of FAPI-46 with generator-eluted 68Ga in 0.8 M sodium acetate buffer in the presence of ascorbic acid (70 mM) as an antioxidant at 98 °C for approximately 8 min. The crude reaction mixture was purified using a custom-made C18 cartridge (Macherey-Nagel). The radiolabeled product was subsequently formulated in an ethanol/saline solution containing ascorbic acid as a stabilizing agent and terminally sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 µm sterile filter. Radiochemical purity was determined by reverse-phase HPLC with UV and radioactivity detection, and by radio-TLC using iTLC-SG strips.

In vivo PET-MRI with FAPI

Experiments were carried out on a small-animal 7 T PET-MRI system (Bruker, Germany). 18 days after bleomycin treatment, anesthetized mice (isoflurane 1.5–2.0%) received an intravenous injection of 5–20 MBq of 68[Ga]Ga-FAPI-46. For anatomical MRI reference, T1_Fisp_3D scans were performed, including 3 stitched volumes, allowing for obtaining a whole-body image of a mouse, with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) 5.5 ms, echo time (TE) 2.6 ms, number of averages (NA) 4, and voxel spacing of 0.16 × 0.16 × 0.30 mm. PET list-mode data were acquired for 10 min and 15 min after injection of ⁶⁸Ga-Ga-FAPI-46.

In vivo MRI of the lungs

In vivo MRI of the lungs was carried out on a Pharmascan 7-T/12-cm system using surface coils (Bruker, Germany). Anesthetized mice (isoflurane 1.5–2.0%) were placed in the MRI bed with the temperature maintained at 37 °C and received an intravenous injection of 1 mg/kg (equivalent Fe) of MPIO@αVCAM-1. Scans were performed using a 2D protocol (TE/TR: 2.5/42 ms, an in-plane resolution of 150 µm × 150 µm and a slice thickness of 0.8 mm).

Ex vivo MRI of the lungs

The lungs were perfused with heparinized saline to flush the vascular compartment and reduce deoxyhemoglobin interference. To this end, mice were deeply anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and received a subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine (Buprecare®, 0.1 mg/kg) 20 min prior to the perfusion. After vascular perfusion, the trachea was incised and cannulated with a 23-gauge catheter. The lungs were inflated in situ with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), ligated before extraction and placed in a 15 mL Falcon tube with 0.01% azide PBS. MRI was carried out on a Pharmascan 7 T/12 cm system using surface coils (Bruker, Germany) with a volume transmit/receive coil and a 3D T2*-weighted GEFC sequence (TE/TR: 8.6 ms/50 ms, resolution: 128 µm × 128 µm × 150 µm, flip angle: 15°).

Histology

Lungs were perfused with heparinized saline and inflated in situ with 4% PFA as described above. The lungs were then harvested and post-fixed overnight in 4% PFA at 4 °C and washed two times in 20% sucrose in phosphate-buffered saline. Finally, the tissues were embedded in Cryomatrix (Tissue-Tek OCT) before cryostat sectioning. Cryostat-cut sections (thickness, 10 μm) were collected on polylysine slides and stored at –80 °C before processing.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Slides were stained by sequential immersion in filtered hematoxylin, acid alcohol, and eosin, with rinsing between each step. They were then dehydrated in ethanol baths of increasing concentration (70%, 95%, 100%) and mounted with a coverslip for analysis. Images were further acquired with a VS120 Virtual Slide Microscope (Olympus) with VS-AW-L100 software and processed using QuPath (v0.5.1) and ImageJ (v1.52).

Sirius red

To evaluate collagen deposition in bleomycin-treated lungs, slides were stained with Picro-Sirius Red using a commercial kit (Diapath). Briefly, sections were stained with the Sirius Red solution for 1 h and then rapidly rinsed in acidified water to remove unbound dye and then dehydrated through a series of ethanol baths of increasing concentration. Finally, sections were cleared in toluene and mounted with a coverslip. Images were further acquired with a VS120 Virtual Slide Microscope (Olympus) with VS-AW-L100 software and processed using QuPath (v0.5.1) and ImageJ (v1.52).

Immunofluorescence

Slides were incubated overnight with rat anti-mouse Ly6G antibody (1:200; BioLegend, clone 1A8), rabbit anti-mouse VWF (1:1000; Dako, A0082), rat anti-mouse VCAM-1 (1:500; BD Biosciences, 553330) or rabbit anti-rat CD68 (1:500; Abcam, ab125212). Primary antibodies were revealed by using Fab′2 fragments of anti-rabbit linked to Cy3 and anti-rat linked to 647 Alexa Fluor or 488 Alexa Fluor (1:500; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, USA). The Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rat secondary antibody was also used to detect the rat anti-VCAM-1 antibodies conjugated to the MPIOs (MPIO@αVCAM-1). Washed sections were coverslipped with antifade medium containing DAPI. Epifluorescence images were digitally captured using a Leica DM6000 epifluorescence microscope coupled CoolSNAP camera, visualized with Leica MM AF 2.2.0 software (Molecular Devices, USA) and further processed using ImageJ (v1.52).

Labeling of MPIO@αVCAM-1 suspension

MPIO@αVCAM-1 was incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rat secondary antibody in borate buffer for 20 min, followed by magnetic washing and sonication. This allowed the visualization of MPIO@αVCAM-1 in suspension using both fluorescence and brightfield microscopy. The distinct spherical black structures of MPIO@αVCAM-1 observed in vitro served as a reference to identify these same structures on lung histological sections under identical brightfield imaging conditions.

VCAM-1 ELISA dosage

Mice were deeply anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and received a subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine (Buprecare®, 0.1 mg/kg) 20 min prior to the perfusion. Perfusion was performed using heparinized saline, and the lungs were then harvested and cut into small pieces before being frozen at –80 °C. The samples were ground using a Potter-type tissue grinder with 1 µL of lysis buffer (Pierce RIPA Buffer, Thermo Scientific) for 0.1 mg of tissue. A dissociation of the cells was then carried out using syringes, and the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min. Protein assay using a BSA range was then performed. VCAM-1 proteins were quantified using commercially available kits (MVC00, R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Values represent the mean of 2 consecutive measurements. VCAM-1 ELISA was performed on both LPS i.n. and LPS i.p. models. A single group of naive mice served as a control for both models to minimize the number of animals used. Mean ELISA and MPI values obtained for each experimental group were used to assess the relationship between these two parameters. A linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate proportionality between variables, and the coefficient of determination (R²) was calculated. Correlation analysis (Pearson or Spearman, as appropriate) was performed for descriptive purposes.

Statistical analyses

Given the small sample sizes (maximum n = 4 per group), non-parametric statistical analyses were systematically performed. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. When more than two groups were included, pairwise comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests between relevant time points.
