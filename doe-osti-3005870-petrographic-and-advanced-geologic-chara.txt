EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Lively Grove #1 (LG1 API number 1218924947) well was drilled to a depth of 5,758 feet, from
the Glen Dean Limestone to the top of the Precambrian unit. In total, 49 thin sections were taken
from Rotary Sidewall Core (RSWC), and one thin section was taken from Whole Core (2,918 feet,
New Albany Shale) from 1,700 to 5,872 feet for this report.
This report details specifically thin section point-counting analysis that includes mineralogical and
pore space analysis, including grain size analysis annotated thin section photomicrographs,
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with Energy Dispersive x-ray Spectroscopy (EDS), X-ray
Diffraction (XRD), statistics of grain size analysis on St. Peter Sandstone thin sections, and Argon-
Argon (Ar-Ar) dating on Precambrian samples from LG1. Characterized units include the Salem
Limestone, New Albany Shale, Trenton Group, Joachim Dolomite, St. Peter Sandstone, Everton
Formation, Eminence Formation, Davis Member, Eau Claire Formation, and Precambrian
Basement.

METHODOLOGY
All 50 thin sections were digitized using a Zeiss Axioscan with a 10x objective lens, capturing one
channel of Plain Polarized Light (PPL), six channels of Cross Polarized Light (XPL) at 0°, 15°,
30°, 45°, 60°, and 75°, and one channel of Circular Polarized Light (CPL).
Point counting analysis was performed on nine thin sections of St. Peter Sandstone, with a Petrog
automated stage installed on a Zeiss AxioImager Mkm.2 microscope. Each slide was analyzed
with 400 points for mineralogy and 100 points for texture analysis.
SEM analysis was conducted using a Hitachi Flex SEM SU1000 equipped with a Brucker SCU
Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) unit.
X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted by Martin Pentrak of the Illinois State Geological
Survey, employing a Brucker XRD D8 in thin section mode on selected thin sections.
Geochemical analysis for argon dating was performed at the Oregon State University Argon
Geochronology Lab by Dr. Daniel Miggins.

PETROGRAPHIC DESCRIPTIONS
Salem Limestone
Formation Depth: 1,363 to 1,736 feet
Description: The Salem Limestone is almost 400 feet thick in Lively Grove 1. Only one thin
section was collected from the bottom of the formation at 1,700 feet. Geophysical logging shows
this group has varying mineralogy from pure calcite to up to 80% dolomite, 10% quartz and 1-2%
illite. This thin section is by no means representative of the average mineralogy or lithology of the
entire 400 feet formation. Rather, it provides information for the variation inside this formation,
and some are indicative to its paleo environment. The siliceous siltstone in this thin section
resembles features seen in the Borden siltstone that belongs to the same Mammoth Cave Limestone
Megagroup as Salem Limestone, which Borden was also used to name part of the formation in
earlier interpretation of this well.
This Rotary Sidewall Core (RSWC) thin section of Salem Limestone can be easily separated into
three facies: calcareous on the top, siliceous in the middle, and dolomitic at the bottom (Figure
1.a). Inside each facies, the grain size varies greatly from mudstone to limestone/dolomite/wacke.
The calcareous face is fining upward, where it transforms from calcareous mudstone to limestone
(Figure 1.b, Figure 1.c). The siliceous face is coarsening upward. The top contains coarse chert
grains (> 2mm) and chert ooid (Figure 1.d and Figure 1.a) in the wacke, and the bottom is matrix
supported siliceous siltstone, with chert and monocrystalline quartz grain that are smaller in size
(< 500μm). The dolomite face is fining upward, with the top of dolomitic mudstone and the bottom
fossiliferous dolomite (Figure 1.e).

New Albany Shale
Formation Depth: 2,225 to 2,237 feet
Description: New Albany Shale is known as the top producer for petroleum in the Illinois Basin.
In LG1, New Albany Shale covers 12 feet of the stratigraphic column. Lithology is uniform from
wire logging, that it has around 40% illite, 25% quartz,15% calcite, and 15% kerogen. One thin
section was taken at 2,235 feet from RSWC, and 1 thin section was taken from 2,918 feet from
Whole Core (WC).
The Shale color varies from light to deep brown (Figure 2.a), the pink color is from the dyed epoxy
that sealed the fractured sample), depending on the quartz content. These microfacies are parallel
laminated with mild turbation. The reddish brown colored illite indicates an oxidized iron content
in the new Albany shales. The amorphous opaques in the shale are likely kerogens (Figure 2.b, red
arrow). Minor phases seen in this formation include organics like tasmanite (Figure 2.b, blue
arrows), which is closely related to oil bearing nature of the New Albany Shale.
It is worth noting that at 2,918 feet, the New Albany Shale contains up to 30% volume of rounded
authigenic pyrite forming within the shale and ferroan-rich calcite phase. (stained purple, Figure
2.c). Under SEM, it is further found that some of these pyrites are framboidal, and sometimes
replacing the organics mold (Figure 2.d, same area as Figure 2.c).

Trenton Group
Formation Depth: 2,962 to 3,066 feet
Description: Trenton group, also known as the Galena group, is about 100 feet thick in LG1. One
thin section was taken from the bottom of this formation at 3,065 feet. In the elemental log analysis
(ELAN), Trenton is mostly limestone, but there’s an increasing amount of quartz and illite to the
bottom of Trenton group.
In the thin section of 3,065 feet, where the top of the thin section is coarser with angular coarse
silt sized calcite (Figure 3.a, blue arrows) and small amount of subrounded quartz grains (Figure
3.a, red arrows) suspended in the clay matrix, as well as euhedral pyrite crystals seen alongside
with these grains. Trenton is also known as a fossiliferous limestone, shell fragments of varying
sizes from 50 to 1000 μm can be seen spottily through the thin section (Figure 3.a).
The bottom of the slide has less silt sized particles. Millimeter sized lenticular lamination are
commonly seen at a dipping angle of 10 to 30 degrees (Figure 3.a).

Joachim Dolomite
Formation Depth: 3,456 to 3,589 feet
Description: Joachim Dolomite is about 130 feet thick in LG1. Three thin sections were taken from
this formation at 3,460 feet, 3,514 feet, and 3,565 feet. The Joachim Dolomite, situated above the
St. Peter Sandstone, plays a crucial role in the geological structure. As the injection zone is located
within the St. Peter Sandstone, the overlying Joachim Dolomite effectively serves as a seal for the
reservoir.
From ELAN, the top 15 feet of the Joachim Dolomite contains up to 90% anhydrite. Anhydrite is
less prevalent in the lower part of Joachim, where the dominant amount of dolomite decreases with
depth (from 80% to 40%), and a corresponding increase in quartz content occurs with depth (from
10% to 60%).
At 3,460 feet shows this high anhydrite content (Error! Reference source not found..a) in the
top of the formation. The anhydrite is interbedded with argillaceous dolomite. Soft sediment
deformation is seen throughout the thin section, with very distinctive fabric (Error! Reference
source not found..b and Error! Reference source not found..c) resulted from the drying process
of sea water that formed the very impermeable layers of anhydrite.
At 3,514 feet, the thin section is almost completely micritic dolomite, except to some thin layers
(<200 μm) of terrigenous clay lamina (Error! Reference source not found..d). A vertical micro-
fault that goes perpendicular through the slide dipping at an unknown angle is found in the lower
part of the thin section. Where euhedral dolomite formed and sealed the pore space of the fault
(Error! Reference source not found..e, the blue color is from the epoxy indicating pore space).
At 3,565 feet, a very homogenous phase of argillaceous dolomite with sub angular to surrounded
fine sandy quartz grains (Error! Reference source not found..f, blue arrows), and similar sized
micritic dolomite grains (Error! Reference source not found..f, red arrows).
All the thin sections from Joachim dolomite show almost no porosity, and layers of anhydrite on
top and micritic dolomite shale to the bottom would provide very good seal for the injection zone
below. The micro-fault seen at 3,514 feet has been partially sealed by calcite and may need
additional testing.

St. Peter Sandstone
Formation Depth: 3,589 to 3,708 feet
Description: St. Peter Sandstone is about 120 feet thick in LG1. Ten thin sections were taken from
this formation, spaced at roughly 10 feet intervals. The St. Peter Sandstone, which serves as the
injection zone, is of particular interest in this report. Point counting is a quantitative tool to
objectively analyze the crucial properties of mineralogy and texture of each thin section. Point
counting analysis has been done on nine out of ten thin sections taken from the formation (One
sample was rejected for quality assurance and uniformity due to the lack of blue epoxy on that
slide).
The point counting analysis shows St. Peter Sandstone is moderately well sorted (average σ Trask =
1.279), subrounded to rounded, fine to medium grained (mean major grain length = 0.21mm) (

Table 1), quartz arenite (average detrital Grains Quartz = 99.9%) (Table 2).
The routine analysis by W.D. Von Gonten Laboratories at St. Peter Sandstone is not aligned with
where the thin sections were taken, but from two of the closely sampled points at 3,665 feet (point
counting) and 3,664.35 feet (routine analysis), the difference are within 17% (point counting often
seen undercount the pore space due to the sampling size), which assures the quality of point
counting (Figure 5).

Figure 5. Comparison of pointing counting and routine core analysis data points in the St Peter Sandstone sample.

Over 90% authigenic mineral are quartz overgrowth (Table 2). Most typical quartz overgrowth at
St. Peter Sandstone doesn’t go more than 20 μm (3,665 feet, Figure 6.a, red arrows). However, in
extreme scenarios, the quartz overgrowth can reach up to 100 μm (3,623 feet, Figure 6.b, blue
arrows). This is to a certain degree, depending on the accommodation, such as secondary porosity.
It is also seen that some quartz grains are sutured together by pressure dissolution (Figure 6.c, red
arrows) and quartz overgrowth (Figure 6.c, blue arrows).
Illite and other authigenic clay minerals only make up the other 10% of the authigenic minerals.
The illite are often pore lining on the quartz grains, and occasionally surrounds moldic porosity,
the result of a completely dissolved feldspar grain (Figure 6.d, red arrow). The illite also are found
to be pore filling in rare cases, and sometimes forming bridges between quartz grains, and blocking
the pore throats (Figure 6.e red arrows). Illite is not a major factor controlling porosity for its
relatively negligible quantity. It plays a mixed role of preserving the porosity (seal the quartz grain
from overgrowth at later stage and preserve pore space, and sometimes blocks the pore spaces and
reduce porosity). Pore filling clays are rather rarely seen in St. Peter Sandstone. At 3,702 feet, the
pore filling clay appeared to be a mixture of two types of clay minerals, suspected to be dickite
and smectite for its low birefringence (Figure 6.f).

Albite on the ELAN, it shows the St. Peter Sandstone is very homogenous in its pure quartz

the entire thin section (Figure 7.a, red color from staining, showing the calcite is non-ferroan). This

At 3,710, the contact with the lower formation, Everton Dolomite, where calcite is cementing the
bottom of the St. Peter Sandstone. Pore space and quartz overgrowth are not completely cemented
by the calcite even to the bottom of the formation (Figure 7.b).
In the St. Peter Sandstone, intergranular pore spaces are more prevalent than apparent secondary
pore spaces. This report classified three types of intergranular pore spaces, based on their size
relative to the average grain size. Pore spaces that fall outside the range of 1st and 3rd quartile of
the grain size at that depth are considered either undersized or oversized.
Undersized porosity, including pore throats, are common to the upper part of the formation. As
seen in 3,611 feet (Figure 7.c), where the sandstone has been compacted, and most of the pore
spaces here are undersized. Oversized porosity on the other end, seen in the middle of the formation
(3,702 feet, Figure 7.d). This type of pore space is mostly secondary in nature, as such pores would
not survive through the compaction of early-stage deposition. With oversized, moldic, and partial
dissolution all considered, these secondary porosity makes around 14% to 38% of the total
porosity, thus it can be concluded that primary porosity is the dominating type of porosity in St.
Peter Sandstone.

Everton Formation
Formation Depth : 3,708 to 3,869 feet
Description: Everton Dolomite belongs to the Knox Dolomite Group. It is about 160 feet thick in
LG1. Ten thin sections were taken from this formation, evenly spaced at 15 feet intervals. ELAN
shows Everton has two distinctive lithology, a dolomitic top (~75 feet) and siliceous bottom (~80
feet).
The top of Everton Formation is calcareous mudstone that consists roughly 5% to 10% (by volume)
of quartz grains (3720 feet, Figure 8.a, blue arrows), and calcite veins (Figure 8.a, red arrows,
Figure 8.b) can be found throughout the thin section cross cutting the limestone. At 3780 feet, clay
lamina crosscut the calcareous mudstone. Organic tasmanite is seen beneath these laminas (Figure
8.c).
The bottom of Everton Formation is an 80 feet interval of quartz arenite highly similar to the St.
Peter Sandstone. At 3,790 feet, a couple of calcite veins of 500 μm thick runs horizontally through
the sandstone (Figure 8.d, red arrows) and reduces horizontal permeability. Compared to St. Peter
Sandstone, Everton has more quartz overgrowth, as seen at 3,789 feet, some of the grains are 100%
covered by the quartz overgrowth (Figure 8.e, red circle).

Eminence Formation
Formation Depth: 4,715 to 4,869 feet
Description: Eminence Formation is about 150 feet thick in LG1. One thin section was taken from
this formation at 4755 feet, closer to the top of the formation. From ELAN, Eminence formation
has a varying content of dolomite (20% to 90%) and quartz (0 to 80%).
At 4,755 feet, it is a fossiliferous siliceous mudstone layered between dolomitic mudstone top and
bottom (Figure 9.a, red arrow point to the top dolomitic mudstone). This siliceous mudstone has
been through multiple phases of replacement, dissolution, and reprecipitation. Vug formed by the
replacement and dissolution of organic skeletons, which are lined with inward-growing authigenic
quartz and chalcedony, are commonly observed throughout the thin section (Figure 9.b).
Potosi dolomite below the eminence formation was not sampled for thin sections. Other
professional reports from the project are recommended for understanding the nature of Potosi in
LG1.

Davis Member
Formation Depth: 5,404 to 5,481 feet
Description: Davis Member is about 80 feet thick in LG1. One thin section was taken from this
formation at 5,428 feet, closer to the top of the formation. From ELAN, Davis Member has two
distinctive lithology, the top 30 feet are siliceous dominated with 20% clay minerals and 10 %
calcite and bottom 50 feet have equal part of quartz and dolomite in addition to 10% clay minerals.
At 5,428 feet, calcite cement clusters throughout the thin section. The cluster normally forms layers
that leave little to no porosity (Figure 10.a, red arrow). Glauconite makes up to 20% of the grains.
Authigenic calcite is seen throughout this thin section. Plagioclase and albite are seen spottily
(Figure 10.b).

Eau Claire Formation
Formation Depth: 5,481 to 5,759 feet
Description: Eau Claire Formation is about 280 feet thick in LG1. Two thin sections were taken
from this formation at 5,483 feet and 5,750 feet. In the ELAN, the top 150 feet of the formation
are homogeneous, consisting almost entirely of dolomite, with an increasing amount of clay
minerals up to 20% towards the bottom of the formation.
At depth 5,483.5 feet, pores of varying sizes from 500 μm to 1mm are seen spottily in the dolomite
groundmass. Authigenic euhedral dolomite crystals grow inside these open cavities (Figure 11.c,
clear epoxy pointed by red arrow).
At depth 5,750 feet, to the bottom of the Eau Claire, the calcite groundmass has millimeter-sized
crystal grains (Figure 11.b), and a noticeable amount of detrital clay (~20% at this depth).

Precambrian Basement
Formation Depth: 5,759 feet to unknown
Description: The lowest RSWC sample taken from the Precambrian basement was from 5,872 feet.
Ten thin sections were taken from this 113-foot thick interval. This is a highly heterogenous
formation. No obvious trends of transition between different lithologies can be interpreted from
the limited number of thin sections collected.
The shallowest depth at 5,763 feet is horizontally layered of interbedded fine crystalline K-feldspar
(grey to light pink) and clay lamina (dark brown) (Figure 12.a).
Lithology of 5,799 feet should be considered syenite for its high orthoclase content (60-80% by
visual estimate) (Figure 12.b, Figure 12.c, blue arrows). The other phases seen from the optical
microscopy are light colored chlorite, and high birefringence muscovite (Figure 12.c, red arrows).
Part of the amorphous opaque minerals are hematite.
5,810 feet has significantly more plagioclase than orthoclase (Figure 12.d), that it should be
classified as gabbro. Elongated plagioclase and orthoclase are clustered into groups and chlorite
are seen in the middle of these clusters.
5,818 feet are brecciated syenite by dolomite intrusions (Figure 12.e). These dolomite veins are
bimodal from euhedral to micritic crystals, indicative of different cooling processes. In both types
of calcite veins, dark specs of opaque mineral less than 5 μm were seen throughout the calcite
(Figure 13.a) (more so in the euhedral ones). EDS analysis was used to identify this mineral,
focusing on a larger grain with the same brightness/contrast. It is found that this opaque mineral is
magnetite (Figure 13.b, The calculation was done by taken the atom%, and remove the oxide
content of adjacent minerals, where Fe: O is roughly 4:3, Figure 12.b).
5,830 feet and beyond are homogenous in that they are mostly gabbro or monzonite (Figure 12.f),
with variation in alkali feldspar content.

GEOCHEMICAL ANALYSIS
Two samples of Precambrian LG1 at 5,818 feet and 5,850 feet were sent to the lab and have been
crushed and separated by mineralogy, and FCT-NM (Fish Canyon Tuff Neutron Monitor) and
Atmospheric Argon were used as standard for reference.
Two plagioclase samples from 5,850 feet show same low temperature age at around 400 Ma
(Figure 14.a, Figure 14.b), and different High Temperature age at 1,040 Ma and 1,168 Ma,
suggesting a thermal reset event around 400 Ma during the end of Lower Devonian, that may or
may not correlate to the Acadian orogeny during the same time and affected part of the Illinois
Basin.
The Amphibole samples of 5,850 feet show closure temperature close to that of Plagioclase. Both
plateaued around 1.255 Ga (Figure 15.a, Figure 15.b) which predates the formation of plagioclase
at the same depth as expected due to the higher closure temperature of amphibole.
K-feldspar samples from 5,818 feet have an upper age of 667 Ma and 650 Ma (Figure 16.a, Figure
16.b), which could either be ruled by the same event as for the thermal reset of plagioclase in lower
depth at 400 Ma due to different closure temperatures between K-feldspar and Plagioclase, or due
to a different event in late Precambrian.
