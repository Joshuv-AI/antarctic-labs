ABSTRACT
The Dynamic Networks (DN) Experiment for FY24 (DNE2) is an experiment within DN with the
goal of quantitatively evaluating the effectiveness of solutions developed so far by various
researchers under the Low Yield Nuclear Monitoring (LYNM) program using a shared set of metrics
and datasets. A key component of this experiment is the mimicking of a signature processing
pipeline, and comparing currently accepted and standard-use processing methods to more state-of-
the-art processes developed under DN. In this work, we focus specifically on the Event
Characterization (EC) Focus Area (FA) of the pipeline, where a seismic event’s magnitude, yield and
class are identified. We use Deep Learning (DL) to classify the type of events being processed as
either earthquakes (EQs) or explosions (EXs) for three iterations of experiment datasets. The model
is noticeably more confident and accurate in classifying explosions than earthquakes, reflecting a
known shortcoming of the model, that being of a bias towards predicting explosions over
earthquakes in the west coast due to training data biases.

1. INTRODUCTION
The Dynamic Networks (DN) venture under the Low Yield Nuclear Monitoring (LYNM) aims to
develop explosion-monitoring capabilities that operate at significantly lower detection thresholds
than is currently possible. The DN Experiment for FY24 (DNE2) is an experiment within DN with
the goal of quantitatively evaluating the effectiveness of solutions developed so far by various
researchers under the LYNM program using a shared set of metrics and datasets (Berg et al., 2025).
A key component of this experiment is the mimicking of a signature processing pipeline, and
comparing currently accepted and standard-use processing methods to more state-of-the-art
processes developed under DN. There are two data pipelines to consider: an operationally
representative one (Op Rep) which comprises of methods that are currently deployed and in use,
and an optimal pipeline which will comprise of modern algorithms and will serve as a benchmark for
the return on technical investments to date. See the DNE2 Experiment Execution Plan (Berg, 2023)
and DNE2 seminal paper (Berg et al., 2025) for more details.
There are six Focus Areas (FAs) representing each of the steps in the data processing pipeline. In
this work, we focus specifically on the Event Characterization (EC) FA of the pipeline, where a
seismic event’s magnitude, yield and class are identified. We use Deep Learning (DL) to classify the
type of events being processed as either earthquakes (EQs) or explosions (EXs) for both the Op
Rep and Optimal datasets built, as well as a Ground Truth dataset, which are the real events that are
meant to be recovered by the other steps in the experiment pipeline, to have an understanding of
the model’s behavior using the absolute truth data and compare to the performance of the pipeline-
built ones.

1.1. Model
The Supervised Learning Continental US (SLCONUS) model is an ensemble of models, consisting
of various Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) based discrimination models that are trained using
Event-Based learning (EBL) presented by Linville (2022). Each individual model takes in
spectrograms of waveforms as inputs, and outputs a probability of being either an earthquake
(negative class) or an explosion (positive class). At a high level, training the individual models of the
ensemble involves creating batches of events, with all the spectrograms of an event existing within
the same batch. A model will output a classification for each individual spectrogram, and by using
EBL as a regularization term we can group these into a singular prediction for the event at large and
enforce consistent labels across the samples that comprise an event. In the case of perfect and
complete labelling EBL is redundant and unlikely to be helpful. In the case of unknown quality or
incomplete labelling, EBL, when balanced with the use of labels through cross-entropy, may induce
model behaviors that are expected from the domain and advantageous for performance. More
details in the training of this model can be seen in Linville et al. (2025).
As SLCONUS is an ensemble, many of individual models are trained and used for inference. They
vary in the training data set used, which creates a diversity in how features are represented, and it is
this diversity that allows for a useful interpretation of uncertainty of classifications. If the various
models agree on a class, this will naturally result in a confident prediction. If models are confident in
their own predictions but disagree between each other, then the aggregate classification of that event
is uncertain.

2. DATA

2.1.1. Training Data
Initial training and testing data for the model utilizes four datasets of earthquakes and explosions.
The training datasets are two U.S. scale catalogs. The first U.S. scale dataset we use is a catalog from
Dickey at al. (2020) that uses the events built by the Array Network Facility using stations from the
Transportable Array (TA), totaling 51,460 observations from 7,073 events. This dataset mostly
contains earthquake samples in the west coast, with some explosions spread throughout the
continental U.S. The second U.S. scale catalog we use is from the National Earthquake Information
Center (NEIC). This dataset includes earthquakes excluding the Western U.S, and non-earthquake
sources (includes both explosive sources as well as exotic events such as landslides, bolides,
rockfalls, and ice-quakes.) for the entire U.S. The 18,455 events in the NEIC catalog (15,404 only
considering EQ and EX) contain a class balance between earthquake and non-earthquakes (exotics
are not considered in training but used exclusively to evaluate uncertainty and model behavior).
NEIC sources are heterogeneously distributed, and critically contain events in abundance in regions
that are poorly represented in the TA dataset.
The University of Utah Seismograph Stations catalog (Linville, 2021) and the Unconstrained Utah
Event Bulletin or UUEB from (Linville et al., 2019) served as the test sets for final model selection
and evaluation. Both the UUEB and the UUSS datasets contain events in and around the U.S. state
of Utah. The UUSS dataset has a roughly balanced number of earthquakes and surface explosions
and a total dataset size of roughly 16,000 events from October 2012 through October 2020. The
UUEB dataset comes from the same geographic area as the UUSS dataset but from a distinct time (a
14-day period preceding the UUSS catalog by almost two years). The UUEB catalog (approximately
7700 events) was also developed manually by an analyst unconstrained by real-time processing and
therefore contains more observations per event.

2.1.2. Experiment Data
Data from the experiment comes from the area of the Nevada National Security Site (NNSS),
obtained from the University of Nevada network, Advanced National Seismic System (ANSS)
sensors, and local and regional International Monitoring System (IMS) sensors (Price et al., 2025)
that were augmented with additional injected explosions (Carmichael et al., 2025). Since the
experiment was to be carried in a manner to simulate a data processing pipeline, the event tables
being used for grouping and classifying seismic data is highly dependent on previous algorithms in
the pipeline, the associator in particular as that is the one in charge of building event candidates.
This results in an issue when determining what the ground truth labels for events are to determine
the model’s performance, as the pipeline may not recover a true event and end up creating a
completely new one. As such, we limit the study of performance to those events which we can
match to a true and known event within the Ground Truth dataset. Another issue is the set of
waveform samples comprising an event are critical for the resulting classification, as the model takes
in waveform data and aggregates the decision of each sample within an event. If an associated event
is composed of different waveforms across the different datasets, it is possible to get differing
classification results from the model.
We test the model against the three iterations of experiment datasets: the Op Rep dataset consisting
of 16,699 samples, here spectrograms of waveform windows (Linville et al., 2022), across 1,394
events, the Optimal dataset of 13,646 samples across 1,017, and the Ground Truth dataset of 5,179
samples across 720 events.

The balanced accuracy represents the average accuracy of the two classes (explosions and
earthquakes), weighted by their respective population sizes. The number of correctly predicted
positive samples (positive in this case being explosion) is represented as TP, or true positives.
Similarly, TN is the number of true negatives (earthquakes). Explosions incorrectly classified as an
earthquake are labeled FN for false negatives, and vice versa as FP for false positives. Precision
represents the fraction of correctly identified explosions out of all events predicted to be explosions,
which can be interpreted as a metric of how prone the model is to false alarms. Recall is the fraction
of correctly identified explosions out of all events that are truly labeled explosions, to which we must
include a new category of predictions representing the number of explosions that were missed not
because they were incorrect but because they were not assigned a label during inference UP, that is
Uncertain Positives. Likewise, UN represents the number of earthquakes not predicted for, or
Uncertain Negatives. We then define a new metric, the Balanced Uncertain Rate, which is the
average rate of uncertain predictions for both classes. To distinguish the impact on recall from
certain and uncertain predictions, I also define a Certain Recall metric that no longer considers UP.
Bar charts reporting these performance metrics on the various event catalogs of the DNE2
experiment are shown in Figure 1. The overall balanced accuracy seen in the ground truth data set is
of approximately 72.0%, with a precision of 73.5% and a recall of 72.0% as well. Immediately
noticeable is the leap in the certain recall to 90.5%, indicating that the model’s failure to identify
explosions is largely due to abstaining on predictions rather than making mistakes. The
experimentally generated tables showcase an even more impressive result 99.4% and 99.5% certain
recall, resulting in a minimal number of misclassified explosions. A balanced uncertain rate of 29.9%
is on par with the error rate of the model (the complement of balanced accuracy, so 28.0%) and thus
the model is equally likely to predict erroneously on an event as to not predict for it at all. Slight
variations of these numbers are seen for the Op Rep and Optimal data sets, but the overall story
largely remains the same.

4. CONCLUSION
In this report, we discussed the steps taken and results of using the SLCONUS model on classifying
experiment data from DNE2. The model is noticeably more confident and accurate in classifying
explosions than earthquakes. This considerable contrast in performance between the two classes
reflects a known shortcoming of the model, that being of a bias towards predicting explosions over
earthquakes in the west coast. The original training data set for this model included a large and
balanced number of explosions and earthquakes in the east coast, but the west coast presented a
considerably larger number of explosions than earthquakes. This results in the model learning to
expect more explosions in the region in which the experiment takes place in, and the model is likely
able to pick up on distinctive features that identify the individual waveforms as west coast data and
thus predominantly tends to classify samples as explosions. Retraining of the model with a more
balanced dataset or balanced sample weighing would be necessary to correct this behavior.
