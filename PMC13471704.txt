Evolutionary diversity of the pfmdr1 hinge region and association with antimalarial drug resistance mutations post ACT introduction
Authors: Emma Filtenborg Hocke, Helle Hansson, Ana Chopo-Pizarro, Louise Wellmann, Gauthier Mesia Kahunu, Hypolite Muhindo Mavoko, Papy Mandoko Nkoli, Eric Mukomena Sompwe, Destin Mbongi, Patrick Mitashi, Innocent Mbulli Ali, Akindeh Nji, Wilfred Mbacham, Azizath Moussiliou, Codjo Dandonougbo, Augustin Kpemasse, Cyriaque Affoukou, Martin Akogbeto, Corine Ngufor, Khalid Beshir, Andria Mousa, Cally Roper, Colin J Sutherland, Michael Alifrangis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Plasmodium falciparum multidrug resistance-1 (pfmdr1) gene, which encodes the ABC P-glycoprotein homolog PgH1, accumulates mutations that result in amino acid changes, namely single-nucleotide polymorphisms at codons N86Y, Y184F, and D1246Y, associated with altered parasite susceptibility to multiple antimalarial drugs. Among these antimalarials are compounds used in artemisinin-based combination (ACT) therapies, which combine a fast-acting but rapidly clearing artemisinin with a longer-lasting partner drug. The in vitro susceptibility of each of these drugs varies with the pfmdr1 genotype of the treated parasites.This study characterized sequence diversity within the pfmdr1 hinge domain and the association with pfmdr1 point mutations at codons 86, 184 and 1246 in population samples from Cameroon, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and Benin, two decades after the introduction of ACTs. The data were evaluated and compared with similar data reported before or during the early implementation of ACT-based treatment strategies.Among 780 successfully sequenced isolates, a high diversity of Arginine-rich repeats in the hinge domain was observed. A significant correlation between the (Asn-Asp-Asn) 7-2-9 variant and both the 86Y and the 1246Y mutation was observed (p < 0.005, OR = 12.6, respectively). We also uncovered, when examining haplotypes across the N86Y, Y184F, and D1246Y codons, that an accumulation of mutations was inversely correlated with diversity in the hinge region. The data suggest patterns consistent with selective pressure and/or linkage with reduced diversity as a potential result of ACT implementation.Keywords: Plasmodium falciparum, Drug resistance, Linker, Genetic diversity, Evolution

Introduction

The Plasmodium falciparum multidrug resistance-1 (pfmdr1) gene, encoding the ABC (ATP-binding cassette) P-glycoprotein homolog PgH1, accumulates mutations that are associated with altered parasite susceptibility to multiple antimalarial drugs (Foote et al., 1990; Gil and Krishna, 2017; Sharom, 2011). Increased pfmdr1 copy number can also lead to enhanced parasite survival following antimalarial treatment (Gadalla et al., 2011; Price et al., 2004; Wilson et al., 1989). Although the precise biological function of pfmdr1 remains unknown, evidence indicates that it acts as an ATP-dependent small molecule transporter located in the membrane of the parasite's digestive vacuole, the site of action for several antimalarial drugs (Cowman et al., 1991). Associations between single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in pfmdr1 and drug susceptibility are complex, either reducing or increasing the effect of different antimalarials (Cui et al., 2015; Reed et al., 2000; Veiga et al., 2016). In sub-Saharan Africa, three codons in pfmdr1 are implicated in reduced drug susceptibility, namely N86Y, Y184F, and D1246Y (Flueck et al., 2000; Folarin et al., 2011; Humphreys et al., 2007; Sisowath et al., 2005; Tinto et al., 2008). Parasites harbouring the 86Y and 1246Y SNPs have been shown to be less susceptible to amodiaquine (Okell et al., 2018), while the 86Y mutation has historically been associated with reduced chloroquine sensitivity (Mu et al., 2003). Furthermore, transgenic parasites carrying the 184F and 1246Y variants demonstrate reduced chloroquine susceptibility in vitro (Reed et al., 2000). Consequently, the prolonged use of chloroquine as a first-line treatment for malaria led to these alleles becoming widespread across endemic regions/areas.

For nearly two decades, artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) have been recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the first- and second-line treatment for uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria (WHO, 2024). ACTs combine fast-acting but rapidly clearing artemisinins with a longer-lasting partner drug. The most used partner drugs are lumefantrine (combined with artemether), mefloquine (paired with artesunate), amodiaquine (paired with artesunate), and piperaquine (paired with dihydroartemisinin) (WHO, 2024). The measured in vitro susceptibility of these drug compounds differs depending on the pfmdr1 genotype of the targeted parasites (Veiga et al., 2016). Several studies have reported that parasites harbouring the 86Y and 1246Y variants have increased susceptibility to lumefantrine, mefloquine, and artemisinins, and are, in fact, selected against in populations exposed to artemether/lumefantrine (Conrad et al., 2014; Cui et al., 2015; Humphreys et al., 2007; Okell et al., 2018; Sisowath et al., 2005; Some et al., 2010; Tumwebaze et al., 2015; Zongo et al., 2007). As a result, the 86Y and 1246Y variants have become less common across the continent (Beshir et al., 2023). On other continents, additional pfmdr1 mutations have shown to be associated with drug resistance. In Southeast Asia mutations in codons E130K, I185 K/N, V1225L have been observed (Zhao et al., 2019) and in South America, additional mutations S1034C and N1042D have been reported (Sidhu et al., 2005). However, although very rare, mutation S1034C and N1042D have been reported in Sub-Saharan Africa (Veiga et al., 2016).

Another feature of the pfmdr1 locus where variation has been well-described is the asparagine-rich (Asn-R) polymorphic repeat region located at codons 1412-1426 in the predicted 4th cytoplasmic loop of the protein (Gil and Krishna, 2017). This poly-asparaginated “hinge” region displays varying length according to the number of asparagine and aspartic acid residues encoded.

Although the exact function of the hinge region in pfmdr1 is unknown, other taxa show Asn-R domains in homologous ABC transporters that have the function to mediate ubiquitination and protein turnover (Gil and Krishna, 2017). Early studies investigating the hinge region in association with resistance mutations to explore the relatedness of P. falciparum populations have suggested that specific configurations of the Asn-R hinge domain are linked with pfmdr1 resistance mutations 86Y, 184F in West Africa and 1246Y in Kenya (Basco et al., 1995; Duraisingh et al., 2000; Okombo et al., 2013). However, the broader evolutionary dynamics of the pfmdr1 hinge region remain to be investigated.

This cross-sectional study, therefore, aimed to descriptively characterise sequence diversity within the pfmdr1 hinge domain in P. falciparum populations from three sub-Saharan African countries: Cameroon, the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), and Benin, two decades after the introduction of ACTs. Furthermore, associations between hinge domain sequences and Sub-Saharan resistance-associated point mutations at codons 86, 184, and 1246 were evaluated and compared with similar data reported before or during the early implementation of ACT-based treatment strategies.

Methods

Samples from P. falciparum-positive participants

For samples from Cameroon: P. falciparum positive samples were collected as dried blood spots on filter paper from assented/consented febrile children and adults suspected of malaria in seven public health facilities between 2018 and 2022 (n = 412).

From DRC, P. falciparum positive samples from febrile children aged 6-59 months from two sites in the equatorial zone were included: Rutshuru and Mikalayi. The samples were collected in 2020-2021 as part of therapeutic efficacy studies in sentinel sites in the DRC. The pfmdr1 resistance haplotype profile was previously published by Mesia Kahunu et al. (Mesia Kahunu et al., 2024) (n = 367). Samples from Benin were collected in 2023 by the National Malaria Elimination Programme in sentinel sites of Collines, Atakora and Littoral (n = 237).

Amplification of pfmdr1 Fragments

Three regions of the pfmdr1 gene were amplified by nested PCR on extracted DNA samples as previously described: two amplicons covering codons 86 and 184 (∼533 bp), and codon 1246 (∼532 bp), respectively (Nag et al., 2017, 2019) plus an amplicon representing the hinge region (∼372 bp) (Duraisingh et al., 2000) (Fig. 1) Primers for amplification are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Regions selected for amplification of known pfmdr1 resistance mutations and the hinge region.

Target amplicon sequencing

For sequencing of the pfmdr1 resistance mutations and the hinge regions, the samples were dual-indexed with non-annealing overhangs in the nested PCR, and specific 8-base pair sequences were attached in an index PCR step for unique barcoding of each sample, as described (Nag et al., 2017, 2019). After the index step, library preparation consisted of bead purification and dilution to a final concentration of 4 nM. A 5% PhiX library (Illumina, California, United States) was spiked in, to counteract the low nucleotide diversity caused by the high AT content in the P. falciparum genome. Sequencing by synthesis was then performed using the Illumina MiSeq V3 kit (Illumina, California, United States).

Quality control and analysis of pfmdr1

For quality control and analysis, the same parameters were used as developed by Nag et al. (2017). For initial quality control, sequences were handled using the online software Galaxy (https://usegalaxy.org/). The FastQ files were quality-controlled using FastQC (Andrews), and the QC results were then aggregated using MultiQC (Ewels et al., 2016). Sequences were then trimmed using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al., 2014) with the following parameters: the average Phred score across 4 bases had to be above 20 (base-calling probability above 99). The trimmed sequences were then combined using the concatenate datasets function in Galaxy.

The trimmed and combined fastq files were aligned to the 3D7 reference genome using assimpler, a Python program to compare reads with a custom database. The following conditions were further applied: only SNPs with more than 50 reads were accepted, and a cutoff was set to exclude mixed infections. Infections were classified as mixed if the minor variant was greater than 20%. So, for example, if a SNP in a particular sample had 74% of reads in base A and 26% of reads in base T, it was considered a mixed infection with the genotype A/T. Mixed infections were excluded from the analysis of associations to ensure that only singular haplotypes were compared to their hinge variants.

Assembly of the pfmdr1 hinge region

For closer inspection of the hinge region, a consensus sequence approach was developed by Hocke et al. (2025). The trimmed FastQ files from the Quality Control step were aligned using the Rsubread package to a pfmdr1 reference gene (Gene ID: 813045, retrieved from https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/813045). A consensus sequence was then constructed for each isolate using QuasiTools, using a majority-base consensus. Sequences were visualised and inspected using MEGA X (Tamura et al., 2021) and aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm (Edgar, 2004). The combination in the hinge region was assigned corresponding to the number of asparagine-aspartic acid-asparagine repeats in the region (e.g., 7-2-9), as first described by Basco et al. (1995).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (R Core Team, 2025), with a significance level set at 5%. Chi-square and Fisher's exact tests were employed to evaluate significant differences between qualitative variables of genotypes. Odds ratios and confidence intervals were additionally calculated for the genotypes.

Phylogenetic analysis

For investigating diversity, several phylogenetic analyses were performed; for similarity between pfmdr1 mutations + hinge region and sites in each country, Euclidean pairwise distance was calculated using the R package ‘Ape’ and a hierarchical clustering was performed using Ward's method. The tree was polished and finalised using Biorender (https://www.biorender.com/).

For phylogenetic investigation between haplotypes and hinge combination, a sequence alignment of the haplotypes’ hinge region was performed using the MUSCLE algorithm in MEGA X. A UPGMA tree was constructed using Number of differences as a model and Uniform rates among sites. The tree was transformed into a circular cladogram and colour-coded according to country using Figtree (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/), and then additionally colored in response to hinge combination using Biorender. Haplotypes with <5 observations were excluded from analysis.

Results

Sample sequencing

Out of the 1016 samples from Benin (n = 237), DRC (n = 367), and Cameroon (n = 412), 780 (77%) samples yielded a positive sequencing signal in at least one codon of known pfmdr1 resistance codons (N86Y, Y184F, or D1246Y) and hinge region. All hinge variants that can be observed are listed in Supplementary Table 2. No mutations were observed in codons 1034 and 1042, and they were therefore excluded from the data analysis.

Moreover, samples that were polyclonal in one or more codons were omitted from further analysis. From Benin, 193 of 237 isolates provided a positive sequencing signal in at least one of the three pfmdr1 codons, while 97 complete haplotype profiles (all three pfmdr1 codons + hinge sequence) were obtained. Of 367 isolates from DRC, 321 yielded a positive sequencing signal, with 207 complete haplotype profiles; from Cameroon, 266 of 412 isolates yielded sequence data, with 140 complete haplotype profiles.

The frequency of pfmdr1 genotypes and haplotypes at codons 86, 184 and 1246 in samples from Cameroon, DRC and Benin

The 86Y and 184F mutations were detected in all three countries, with 184F being the most prevalent. Its frequency was highest in Cameroon (54%, n = 95), followed by Benin (39%, n = 66) and the DRC (38.8%, n = 81). In contrast, 86Y occurred at lower frequencies: 13.9% (n = 21) in Cameroon, 5.3% (n = 4) in Benin, and 15.6% (n = 40) in the DRC. The 1246Y mutation was mainly observed in DRC (10.5%, n = 25) and occurred once in Cameroon (0.6%) (Table 1).

Consistent with these findings, the NYD and NFD haplotypes were the most common across all three countries. In Cameroon, their frequencies were 37.9% (n = 53) and 48.6% (n = 68), respectively; in Benin, 31.9% (n = 31) and 63.9% (n = 62); and in the DRC, 45.9% (n = 95) and 32.8% (n = 68).

By contrast, the YYY and NYY haplotypes were rare and observed only in Cameroon and the DRC. NYY was detected in 4.8% (n = 10) of samples from the DRC and 0.7% (n = 1) from Cameroon, while YYY occurred at 6.28% (n = 13) in the DRC and 0.7% (n = 1) in Cameroon.

The polymorphic distribution of Asn-R repeats in the hinge region of pfmdr1

Diversity of Asn-R repeats was observed across samples with a general pattern of four to ten Asn codons followed by one to four Asp codons and between zero and eleven Asn codons (Fig. 2). The repeat 7-2-9 was by far the most abundant across all countries, accounting for 63.9% (n = 170), 74.8% (n = 240) and 57.0% (n = 110) of the samples from Cameroon, DRC and Benin, respectively. The second most abundant hinge combination varied between countries; in Cameroon and Benin, the 7-2-8 combination was the second most abundant, whereas in DRC, it was the 7-2-7 combination. Interestingly, some samples had no Asp region and consisted of only asparagine in varying lengths (n = 24, labelled ‘x’-0-0 where x indicates the number of asparagines, Fig. 2). These variants were found across all countries, but were most prevalent in the DRC, where the most abundant combination, 9-0-0, was observed 13 times. In Cameroon, variants containing a serine in the hinge region were identified, and the most abundant variant was the 6-S-1-10 (Asp-Ser-Asn-Asp), observed six times. To the authors' knowledge, this variant has never been reported before.

Geographical relationships of the combined pfmdr1 resistance-associated haplotypes and hinge region

A phylogenetic analysis explored relatedness between constructed pfmdr1-haplotypes/hinge region in samples from the various study sites within the three countries. Countries clustered together based on geography; Sequences from Cameroon and Benin, both West-African countries, clustered together across countries' sites, while sequences from the two DRC sites clustered together in a separate clade. Association of pfmdr1 hinge region variants with resistance-associated mutations

A significant association was observed between carriage of the 7-2-9 hinge variant and the 86Y mutation (Chi Square, OR = 3.54, 95% CI: 1.65 to 7.59, p < 0.005), and likewise a significant correlation of 7-2-9 with 1246Y was observed (Fisher's exact, OR = 12.6, 95% CI: 1.70 to 93.55, p < 0.0005). No hinge combination was significantly associated with the 184F mutation (Chi Square, OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.52 to 1.12, p > 0.05).

For further analysis, pfmdr1 hinge combinations were combined with pfmdr1 86-184-1246 constructed haplotypes. Consistent with the observed linkage, haplotypes with 86Y and/or 1246Y, although lower in numbers, generally showed less hinge diversity compared to wildtypes and were predominantly the 7-2-9 variant (Fig. 4).

Barplot showing count of pfmdr1 hinge combination according to resistance haplotype combined for three sub-Saharan African countries: Cameroon, DRC and Benin. Haplotypes with <2 observations, and hinge combinations observed <5 times were omitted from analysis.

Geographic distribution of pfmdr1 hinge and resistance haplotype diversity

Phylogenetic tools were used to assess the diversity of combined resistance haplotype and hinge variants, stratified by geographic origin. The wildtype haplotype NYD and the haplotype with the 184F mutation NFD showed a generally high diversity of hinge combinations scattered across all countries and sites. The NYD haplotype was found with 20 different hinge combinations, and the NFD haplotype with 24 (Fig. 5). The 6-S-1-10 Cameroon variant was only observed with the NFD haplotype. Less diversity was observed in the hinge region in isolates with two or three resistance-associated mutations, but these were also more scarce in the dataset than the NYD and NFD haplotypes. For example, the YYY haplotype was predominantly observed in DRC (n = 13) and Cameroon (n = 1) and only with the 7-2-9 hinge variant.

Discussion

The present study investigated variation within the pfmdr1 hinge region and its relatedness with resistance-associated pfmdr1 mutations at codons 86, 184 and 1246 across three sub-Saharan African countries.

A markedly high prevalence of the 7-2-9 pattern was observed, accounting for 57–75% of isolates across the three countries. The prevalence observed here exceeds that reported in earlier studies (Basco et al., 1995; Duraisingh et al., 2000; Okombo et al., 2013). These previous publications on the pfmdr1 hinge region additionally found an association of 8-2-8/9/10 with 86Y and 184F (Duraisingh et al., 2000; Okombo et al., 2013), which was not observed in this study. Furthermore, the 7-2-10 variant, previously associated with the YYY haplotype in Kenya (Okombo et al., 2013), was absent from Benin and the DRC and was only detected in a small number of samples from Cameroon (n = 6), none of which carried either 86Y or 1246Y. Consistent with previous reports, the data in this study suggest that the quinoline resistance-associated pfmdr1 mutations 86Y and 1246Y have become uncommon in African parasite populations following the widespread implementation of ACTs (Beshir et al., 2023; Conrad et al., 2014). The variants were observed at low frequencies in Cameroon and the DRC (Table 1), both countries that currently are using AL or AS + AQ as first-line treatment (WHO, 2024). Similarly, in Benin, which uses AL as first-line treatment, the 86Y was similarly observed at low frequencies (WHO, 2024). However, the 1246Y was absent.

A notable observation was that parasites carrying the combination of the 86Y and 1246Y mutations (YYY haplotype) occurred exclusively with the 7-2-9 hinge variant. The reduced hinge diversity observed among isolates carrying 86Y and/or 1246Y, together with the predominance of the 7-2-9 variant, is consistent with selective pressure and/or linkage with reduced genetic diversity at this locus. One possible explanation is that historical changes in antimalarial drug use, including chloroquine withdrawal and subsequent ACT implementation, altered the frequencies of pfmdr1 variants within African parasite populations. All three Sub-Saharan African countries included in this study have implemented ACT as first-line treatment in the early 2000s. Cameroon and Benin switched to ACTs in 2004 (Basco et al., 2006; Group et al., 2017) and DRC in 2005 (Akilimali et al., 2022).

However, the present study does not directly assess selection, and alternative explanations, including linkage to other pfmdr1 variants or broader population-level processes, cannot be excluded. Furthermore, although the association was statistically significant, the frequencies of the aminoquinoline-resistant haplotypes were low, and the biological significance of the observed co-inheritance remains uncertain.

The low abundance of the YYY haplotype was similarly observed in a study by Beshir et al., who reported a similar low prevalence of the YYY haplotype (0.0%-5.84%) in areas across seven African countries implementing seasonal malaria chemoprevention (Beshir et al., 2023). Although the YYY haplotype has previously been associated with reduced sensitivity to amodiaquine (Okell et al., 2018) its low prevalence suggests it is unlikely to represent a major current threat to treatment efficacy.

A previously unreported hinge variant, 6-S-1-10, was identified in six isolates from Cameroon. This variant was detected exclusively in parasites carrying the NFD haplotype, a known marker associated with lumefantrine selection (Gadalla et al., 2011). Additionally, truncated variants lacking aspartic acid (X-0-0) and newly uncovered variants with serine present were observed. These observations indicate that variation within the pfmdr1 hinge region remains dynamic and continues to evolve within contemporary parasite populations.

The biological function of the pfmdr1 hinge region in P. falciparum remains unclear. The human mdr1 linker region is composed of serine/threonine repeats and has been shown, when phosphorylated, to aid in modulating ATPase activity and interacting with the transport of certain drugs (Szabo et al., 1997). Except for the few hinge variants that harbored a single serine, the pfmdr1 linker region does not harbor serine or threonine repeats, making phosphorylation non-viable. Although speculative, the pfmdr1 hinge region might serve a similar function, controlled by other post-translational modifications. Previous structural and functional studies have suggested that the Pfmdr1 polymorphisms, including 86, 184 and 1246, may influence drug susceptibility through conformational effects on transporter function and substrate transport (Calcada et al., 2020; Ferreira et al., 2011). Although no association between hinge variants and 184F was observed in the present dataset (but some significance with 86Y and 1246Y), variation within the hinge region could still be biologically relevant if it contributes to transporter dynamics or occurs in linkage with functionally important pfmdr1 haplotypes.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, mixed infections were excluded from the analysis, which may have reduced the observed diversity and limited the ability to detect co-circulating hinge variants within individual infections. Second, consensus sequences were generated by merging Illumina reads within each sample. While this approach facilitated population-level analyses, it may have obscured low-frequency variants and thereby underestimated true genetic diversity. Consequently, within-host variation could not be assessed, limiting the ability to detect minority hinge variants or mixed parasite populations that may contribute to the overall diversity of this locus. Third, no phenotypic drug susceptibility data were available, preventing direct assessment of whether the observed hinge variants influence antimalarial susceptibility. Fourth, the analysis focused on a specific region of pfmdr1 and did not evaluate variation across the entire gene. Consequently, it cannot be excluded that the observed associations reflect linkage with other pfmdr1 variants not captured in this study. Finally, non-resistance-related impacts might explain the change in hinge diversity, such as population structure and demographic processes, and thus these potential selective effects cannot be addressed using the present dataset.

To summarise, the findings provide new information on the diversity and distribution of pfmdr1 hinge region variants in African P. falciparum populations. The predominance of the 7-2-9 variant and its association with specific pfmdr1 haplotypes highlight patterns that may be relevant for understanding parasite population dynamics following changes in antimalarial drug use. While functional consequences remain unknown, characterization of hinge region diversity may complement existing molecular surveillance efforts and contribute to future investigations of pfmdr1 evolution and antimalarial drug susceptibility.
