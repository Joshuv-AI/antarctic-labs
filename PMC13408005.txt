Widespread complex enameloid microstructure in teeth and rostral denticles of Cretaceous sclerorhynchoid skates (Rajiformes: †Sclerorhynchoidei)
Authors: Endy Chateauneuf‐Gautier, Guillaume Guinot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnameloid is a type of hard mineralised tissue covering teeth and dermal denticles of extant and extinct chondrichthyans. Understanding the diversity of enameloid microstructures across chondrichthyan clades is key to highlight potential phylogenetic or adaptive signals provided by histological characters. Among chondrichthyans, the enameloid microstructure of teeth and odontodes of batomorphs (skates and rays) has been understudied despite the high extant and past taxonomic and morphological diversities of this clade. Here, we focus on the sclerorhynchoid rays, a speciose extinct (Cretaceous) clade of skates that developed rostra bearing enlarged denticles in most taxa. We explore the enameloid microstructure of both teeth and rostral denticles of 13 species representing a wide range of morphologies. Our results show that the tooth enameloid microstructure of sclerorhynchoid skates achieved a complex arrangement, with a high diversity of bundled components. This microstructure is furthermore complexified by a strong degree of apico‐basal variation in the organisation of the enameloid components. This organisation is the most complex known so far among batomorph teeth and probably elasmobranchs in general. Our results further confirm the presence of a similarly complex enameloid microstructure in the dermal denticles covering the elongated rostrum of these extinct skates. Most importantly, we provide evidence for the pervasiveness of this complex enameloid across rostral denticles of all studied taxa, despite the wide range of taxa and morphologies represented. The common enameloid organisation reported in both teeth and rostral denticles is, from base to the apex: a Single‐Crystallite Enameloid (SCE) layer passing into Radial Bundled Enameloid (RBE) sometimes through a phase of protobundles as the enameloid thickens slightly higher in the crown, with apico‐basal Parallel‐Bundled Enameloid (PBE) appearing mixed with RBE bundles higher up at the base of the cusp, followed by the classical three‐layered enameloid including SCE, PBE and Tangled‐Bundled Enameloid (TBE) in the apex, crossed by rare Thick Radial Bundles (TRB). The presence of protobundles and RBE forming the entire enameloid thickness in the basalmost crown enameloid of teeth and rostral denticles analysed here was so far restricted to placoid scales and teeth of some hybodonts and batomorphs. Our study identified protobundles as transitional organisation between SCE and structured bundles and the RBE as an organisational stage preceding the PBE. We demonstrate that TRB are isolated RBE bundles and that both RBE and PBE bundles can participate in circumferential bundles whose development is linked with the size of the cutting edges. We found no evidence for a clear phylogenetic signal provided by the distribution of enameloid microstructure characters neither within teeth, nor among rostral denticles sampled here. Instead, we propose a possible link with geometric constraints (height, width), with SCE and RBE (with or without protobundles) being restricted to thin enameloid layers. Taken together, these results have strong implications for the understanding of enameloid character distribution in elasmobranch odontodes.Keywords: Batomorphii, Chondrichthyes, Elasmobranchii, fossils, histology

INTRODUCTION

Teeth and dermal denticles of cartilaginous fishes (chondrichthyans) are mineralised structures composed of an attachment tissue and a crown, the latter being formed by a hypermineralised tissue called enameloid (Poole, 1971) covering an internal dentine core (Ørvig, 1951; Reif, 1973; Sire & Huysseune, 2003). Enameloid is a hard mineralised tissue composed of hydroxyfluorapatite microcrytals (Chen et al., 2014; Francillon‐Vieillot et al., 1990) referred to as ‘crystallites’. These crystallites can have a large range of different arrangements, known as ‘microstructures’, and both the developmental origins and the microstructural diversity of the chondrichthyan enameloid have been the focus of intense research over the past decades (see Cuny et al., 2017). In particular, biologists and palaeontologists have focused on the enameloid microstructure of teeth mostly because the chondrichthyan fossil record is mainly represented by isolated teeth. As teeth provide characters of a high taxonomic value and display a wide range of morphologies interpreted as reflecting ecological and morpho‐functional adaptations (Cappetta, 2012), their enameloid microstructure was expected to bring further taxonomic and/or adaptive signal.

Extant chondrichthyans comprise two sister clades: the Elasmobranchii (including extant sharks, skates and rays and their fossil relatives) and the Holocephali (including extant chimeras and their fossil relatives). Modern sharks (Selachimorpha) are sister to rays and skates (Batomorphii) and form together the Neoselachii. It has long been thought that the tooth enameloid of all non‐neoselachian chondrichthyans was composed of a single layer of individualised crystallites without preferential orientation, called SCE (‘Single Crystallite Enameloid’). Conversely, neoselachians were characterised by a triple‐layered enameloid comprising the external SCE and an inner unit where crystallites are associated into bundles, referred to as BCE (‘Bundled Crystallite Enameloid’). This BCE unit comprises the PBE component (‘Parallel‐Bundled Enameloid’) and the inner TBE component (‘Tangled‐Bundled Enameloid’), which can be crossed by bundles of RBE (‘Radial Bundled Enameloid’). This complex microstructure was thought to be secondarily simplified among batomorphs (Enault et al., 2013, 2015; Reif, 1977). Subsequently, more recent works found complex tooth enameloid microstructures in some Palaeozoic non‐neoselachian chondrichthyans (Guinot et al., 2013), hybodonts (sister to neoselachians) (Cuny et al., 2017) and batomorphs (Enault et al., 2013, 2015; Manzanares et al., 2016). These have refuted the former simplistic view of enameloid character distribution and questioned the relevance of tooth enameloid features for taxonomic purposes (Enault et al., 2015).

Much of the uncertainty in the understanding of the distribution of tooth enameloid features among chondrichthyans relates to the weak taxonomic sampling compared to the high extinct and extant diversity of this clade, an issue that is exemplified by batomorphs (Enault et al., 2015). The tooth enameloid microstructure of the few batomorph taxa studied so far turned out to be much more diverse than what is known in sharks, despite the comparatively much smaller number of taxa analysed (Cappetta, 2012; Enault et al., 2013, 2015; Manzanares et al., 2016, 2018). Similarly, the diversity of the histology of chondrichthyan dermal denticles (placoid scales) has received little attention, especially since their crown enameloid was thought to be composed of a single SCE or TBE layer (Cuny et al., 2017). Again, recent studies have provided new information suggesting some degree of organisation within the enameloid of dermal scales. Loosely organised RBE was reported from the crown of some large ‘button‐like’ fossil hybodont dermal denticles (Enault et al., 2015) and of small placoid scales of various extant taxa (Manzanares et al., 2014). Complex and well‐differentiated bundled enameloid was later demonstrated to compose both the crown of Valanginian (Cretaceous) isolated neoselachian placoid scales (Feichtinger et al., 2020) and the large rostral scales that line up along the enlarged rostrum of two species of the rajiform clade †Sclerorhynchoidea (Cook et al., 2022, 2026).

Sclerorhynchoids are an extinct monophyletic clade closely related to modern skates (Rajiformes) (Underwood et al., 2016; Villalobos‐Segura et al., 2019; Villalobos‐Segura, Kriwet, et al., 2021; Villalobos‐Segura, Underwood, et al., 2021; Wueringer et al., 2009). Sclerorhynchoids are characterised by an elongate rostrum, most often equipped with rostral denticles. This rostrum is a character that appeared convergently with sawfish (Pristidae) and sawsharks (Pristiophoridae) (Cappetta, 1974, 2012; Underwood et al., 2016; Welten et al., 2015). Sclerorhynchoids represent one of the most successful and diverse clades of extinct neoselachians (Underwood, 2006) and exhibit a tremendous diversity of skeletal, dental and rostral morphologies. This group reached a cosmopolitan distribution in the tropical to subtropical, shallow marine and coastal environments of the Cretaceous and even colonised some freshwater settings (Kriwet & Kussius, 2001; Underwood et al., 2016; Wueringer et al., 2009) from the Barremian (Lower Cretaceous) until their extinction during the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event (Cappetta, 2012; Guinot & Condamine, 2023). There are about 50 named sclerorhynchoid species, divided into 26 genera (Cappetta, 2012; Guinot & Condamine, 2023). This group comprises three families: †Sclerorhynchidae, †Onchopristidae and †Ptychotrygonidae, the latter being characterised by their rostrum devoid of enlarged denticles. The sclerorhynchoid fossil record is mainly represented by isolated hypermineralised elements (teeth and rostral denticles), but a substantial number of sclerorhynchoid genera are represented by articulated specimens (Cappetta, 1980, 2012; Underwood et al., 2016), which helped provide a phylogenetic framework (Villalobos‐Segura et al., 2019; Villalobos‐Segura, Kriwet, et al., 2021; Villalobos‐Segura, Underwood, et al., 2021). Considering the abundant fossil record and high taxonomic richness coupled with the tremendous diversity and disparity in tooth and rostral denticle morphologies reached by sclerorhynchoid skates, this clade is highly relevant to analyse the distribution of enameloid microstructures among dental and dermal elements.

The histology of the teeth and rostral denticles of sclerorhynchoids has been little studied to date. Cappetta (2012) used the types of dentine forming the rostral denticles of non‐ptychotrygonids to separate the taxa whose rostral teeth include osteodentine, which were included in Type A (†Onchosaurus, †Pucapristis, †Schizorhiza), from the orthodonts (included in Type B), which include all other sclerorhynchids and the subsequently named †Onchopristidae [i.e., †Onchopristis and possibly †Ischyrhiza, see Villalobos‐Segura, Kriwet, et al., 2021]. However, the type of dentine of the rostral denticles (and teeth) of a large number of genera remains unknown. Knowledge on the enameloid microstructure of teeth and rostral denticles of sclerorhynchoids is even more limited: only the rostral denticle histology of †Ischyrhiza mira (Cook et al., 2022) and †Onchopristis numida (Cook et al., 2026) along with the tooth enameloid microstructure of the ptychotrygonid genus †Ptychotrygon (Enault et al., 2015) have been studied. These studies pointed to the presence of BCE in teeth of †Ptychotrygon, and the shark‐like enameloid complexity displayed by the studied rostral denticle suggested that these odontodes are able to evolve structures histologically similar to teeth outside the oropharyngeal cavity (Cook et al., 2022).

Here, we explore the distribution of enameloid features in teeth and rostral denticles of 13 sclerorhynchoid species and genera, which represent both the taxonomic diversity and the wide range of dental and rostral morphologies realised by this clade. Based on extensive sections and surface analyses, we provide a comprehensive overview of the histological similarities between chondrichthyan teeth and odontodes. We demonstrate that the tooth enameloid microstructure of sclerorhynchoid skates is among the most complex among elasmobranchs and highlight that the highly derived organisation in the enameloid microstructure of their rostral denticles is widespread in this clade. Our study brings additional information about potential parameters affecting the enameloid microstructure of chondrichthyan teeth and odontodes with a possible effect of geometric constraints (height, width, thickness), whereas we found no clear link with phylogenetic relationships.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Taxon sampling and specimens analysed

Species analysed here were selected to represent the wide range of tooth and denticle morphologies evolved by sclerorhynchoids as well as the taxonomic richness of the clade (families, subfamilies) and to match the taxa sampled in previous phylogenetic analyses (Villalobos‐Segura et al., 2019; Villalobos‐Segura, Kriwet, et al., 2021; Villalobos‐Segura, Underwood, et al., 2021). Consequently, the fossil material sampled here consists of isolated sclerorhynchoid teeth representing 13 species and genera originating from the Upper Cretaceous (Cenomanian‐Maastrichtian) of a range of localities worldwide (Figures 1 and 2; Tables 1 and S1). These taxa represent the three families (†Sclerorhynchidae, †Ptychotrygonidae, †Onchopristidae) recognised in the literature (Villalobos‐Segura, Kriwet, et al., 2021), along with (sub‐)families of dubious validity (e.g., †Schizorhiza stromeri for †Schizorhizinae, †Ischyrhiza for †Ischyrhizinae). The teeth sampled here cover a large range of sizes, crown ornament (smooth, strong ridges, fine folds) and morphologies (from strongly cuspidate to low‐crowned) (Figure 1). Among the 13 species selected, two (†Ptychotrygon, †Ptychotrygonoides) belong to the ptychotrygonids, whose rostra were shown to be devoid of enlarged denticles (Villalobos‐Segura, Underwood, et al., 2021). For the sampled †Sclerorhynchidae and †Onchopristidae, we aimed at exploring the rostral denticle enameloid microstructure of the same species as those selected for tooth enameloid analysis. We therefore analysed rostral denticles of 10 out of the 11 sampled taxa representing sclerorhynchoids with denticle‐bearing rostra (Figure 2; Table 1), with †Micropristis solomonis being the only species not represented by rostral denticles here due to the lack of available specimens. Sampled rostral denticles also cover the large range of sizes (millimetre to centimetre‐scale), crown ornament (smooth, fine wrinkles, folds) and morphologies (low‐crowned and triangular, elongate with sharp cutting edges, developed posterior barbs) (Figure 2). All rostral denticles analysed originate from the same locality as the teeth they are conspecific with, and in the vast majority of cases (except †Dalpiazia stromeri and †Ganopristis leptodon), from the same horizon. Most species selected (10/13) correspond to the type species of their genus (except †Onchopristis dunklei, †Onchosaurus pharao and †Sclerorhynchus sp.) and, when possible, specimens come from the type locality of the species they represent (†Ptychotrygonoides pouiti, †Pucapristis barnisi, †Libanopristis hiram, †Micropristis solomonis). All specimens come from localities with published geographical and stratigraphical settings (Table 1) and all tooth‐rostral denticle associations considered here follow published taxonomic schemes and are monospecific (Cappetta, 2012). The sole exception to this is for specimens attributed to †O. pharao, which originate from the bivalve‐rich white limestone of the lower Turonian Aït Ben Ali Formation (Andreu et al., 1995) near Boulemane (Middle Atlas, Morocco). The two named †Onchosaurus species were so far known exclusively from isolated rostral denticles (Cappetta, 2012). In the Turonian locality of Aït Ben Ali, rostral denticles of †O. pharao (Figure 2D,E) are associated with sclerorhynchiform teeth (Figure 1S,T) that do not correspond to previously published taxa and are here assigned to †O. pharao. Considering the small number of specimens available for some taxa and the destructive methodology for observing enameloid microstructure, some specimens analysed were also scanned/illustrated whereas some analysed specimens were poorly preserved and better‐preserved specimens were illustrated (Table 1).

Methods

All teeth and some rostral denticles were scanned using the EasyTom 150 microCT of the Institute of Evolutionary Science of Montpellier (ISEM) (Table 1). Following the methods described in literature (Enault et al., 2015; Guinot & Cappetta, 2011), specimens were first embedded in a transparent epoxy resin. Rostral denticles were transversely sectioned on the upper half of the cap and longitudinally sectioned when the material permitted it (Table 1). Bigger specimens were first sectioned with a diamond circular saw. All denticles were sectioned and polished using 2400‐ and 4000‐grit silicon carbide abrasive papers. Teeth were observed with apicobasal labiolingual sections and polished on 1200‐, 2400‐ and 4000‐grit silicon carbide abrasive papers. All fossils were etched in diluted 10% HCl for 1, 3 or 5 s (depending on the width of the enameloid layer). Surface etching was also made in diluted 10% HCl for 1, 5 and 15 s. The material was carbon coated and observed on the Scanning Electron Microscope, Zeiss Sigma 300 (5 kV) and Hitachi S4800 (5 kV) of the European Institute for Membrane of Montpellier (IEM) (Table S1).

The terminology used here follows that of Enault et al. (2015).

Tooth (Figure 3E–J). The dentine is compact (orthodentine) and a pulp cavity is visible but filled with sediment. The enameloid is very thin in the basal lingual and labial crown faces (5–60 μm), but thickens towards the apex (80–100 μm). The enameloid–dentine boundary is clear and regular except in the apex where a few dentine tubules penetrate the enameloid (Figure 3E). At the base of the labial and lingual faces, the enameloid is composed of small and short crystallites with no organisation or orientation (SCE) (Figure 3F). Higher on the apron (labially) and the uvula (lingually), the enameloid thickens and the crystallites display a rudimentary organisation that is intermediate between the basal SCE and the RBE found higher up in the crown and referred to as ‘protobundles’ here. These protobundles are made of short crystallites that are aggregated and mostly oriented radially (Figure 3G), as seen in some hybodont and batomorph teeth (Cuny et al., 2017; Enault et al., 2015). At mid‐height of both crown faces, the enameloid arranges into radial bundles forming the RBE, with bundles directed towards the apex (PBE) being present (Figure 3H). Near the base of the main cusp, the radial bundles become less frequent, whereas bundles of the PBE dominate the enameloid's thickness (Figure 3I). The apex is mainly composed of bundles of the PBE along with scarce bundles organised into thick radial bundles (TRB) on the lingual and labial edges. The PBE bundles of both crown faces do not connect at the tip of the apex, possibly due to wear functionally. The inner part of the enameloid forming the apex is composed of bundles with no preferred orientation as well as bundles transverse to the section, which form the TBE (Figure 3J).

Rostral denticles (Figure 4). The pulp cavity is thin and very elongated anteroposteriorly. The enameloid layer is thin with a clear and well‐defined enamel–dentine junction. The orthodentine is compact (Figure 4A,D). In cross‐section (Figure 4A–C), only one anterior cutting edge can be observed. The enameloid of the posterior half is thinner (25–30 μm) than the anterior half (40–80 μm), which reaches its maximum thickness at the cutting edge (80 μm). The enameloid is crossed by numerous cracks, with the largest being filled with resin. In the inner one‐third of the enameloid thickness, the crystallites are individualised without preferential orientation. In the outer two‐thirds, the crystallites are organised into poorly defined ‘protobundles’ oriented perpendicular to the surface of the crown (Figure 4B). In the cutting edges, poorly structured bundles are found with no preferred orientation, especially in the inner part (Figure 4C). In longitudinal section, the enameloid at the basalmost anterior region of the denticle is thin and composed of SCE, which can be worn and sometimes absent at the base of the anterior edge of the crown (Figure 4D,E), possibly due to wear. As the enameloid thickens at the level of the ventral and dorsal flanges of the crown (Figure 4F), poorly defined bundles compose the inner two‐thirds of the enameloid, while the external part is made of individual crystallites with no dominant orientation. Some poorly structured tangled bundles forming a TBE‐like unit develop at the zone of maximum enameloid thickness, in the marginal flanges of the crown (Figure 4F). The enameloid forming the concavity between the marginal crown flanges and the main cusp is thin and composed of poorly defined radially oriented protobundles in the inner half and individual crystallites in the outer part (Figure 4G). The enameloid forming the base of the cusp is composed of RBE bundles (Figure 4H). The enameloid of the anterior and posterior cutting edges is made of poorly structured bundles oriented perpendicular to the axis of the cutting edges (PBE), as observed on the surface‐etched specimen (Figure 4I,K). The surface of the denticle is covered by individual crystallites with no preferred orientation (SCE) (Figure 4J), which were not observed in sections.

DISCUSSION

Diversity of tooth enameloid microstructures among batomorphs

Historically, hypotheses based on weak taxon sampling suggested that the enameloid of batomorph teeth lacked the ‘triple layer’ present in shark teeth (Enault et al., 2013; Manzanares et al., 2016; Reif, 1977). Their enameloid was originally thought to consist solely of either TBE (Gillis & Donoghue, 2007; Reif, 1977) or SCE (Cuny et al., 2009; Thies, 1983). More recent evidence points to the widespread presence of a unique SCE layer in teeth of most batomorph clades, including extant Rajoidei (Rajiformes), Rhinobatidae (Rhinopristiformes), Plathyrhinidae, Torpedinidae (Torpediniformes) and Myliobatoidei (Myliobatiformes) (Manzanares et al., 2016). A similar condition was found in some fossil batomorph taxa such as †Hypsobatis (probable Rajiformes) as well as †Rhombodus and †Leidybatis (Myliobatiformes) (Enault et al., 2015). However, some recent studies have demonstrated that several batomorph taxa actually achieved more complex, diverse and structured enameloid organisations than previously assumed. Members of the stem‐batomorph †Apolithabatiformes (e.g., †Belemnobatis) and extinct taxa possibly related to Rhinopristiformes (†Parapalaeobates) display teeth with a more complex microstructure including poorly differentiated BCE bundles (PBE and RBE‐like), along with isolated crystallites (Enault et al., 2015). A complex enameloid organisation was also reported from teeth of the Early Jurassic Toarcibatidae—the oldest batomorphs analysed so far – where all main enameloid components (SCE, PBE, TBE, RBE) were identified (Cuny et al., 2017; Manzanares et al., 2018). A similar complex organisation was also reported from teeth of the extant Rhina ancylostoma (Rhinidae, Rhinopristiformes) (Manzanares et al., 2016). Consequently, although all batomorph orders comprise taxa with tooth enameloid composed of a single SCE layer, some taxa achieved more complex microstructures. The only sclerorhynchoid tooth analysed so far (†Ptychotrygon sp.) was reported to be mostly composed of RBE bundles crossed by PBE‐like bundles, along with what was identified as SCE close to the occlusal surface (Enault et al., 2015). However, the presence of PBE bundles was not clearly established.

Our results clearly support the presence of complex enameloid microstructure in teeth of all 13 analysed species belonging to 13 genera, which represent all known named Sclerorhynchoidei families (†Sclerorhynchidae, †Ptychotrygonidae, †Onchopristidae) as well as (sub‐)families of dubious validity (†Schizorhizidae, †Ischyrhizidae; see Greenfield, 2021). Strikingly, all the major enameloid components reported from shark teeth (SCE, PBE, RBE, TBE) and related microstructures are represented in the sclerorhynchoid teeth analysed here. Many of them also display ‘protobundles’ as an intermediary organisation between the SCE at the base of the crown and the more structured RBE higher up in the crown (†O. dunklei, †P. triangularis, †P. pouiti, †P. branisi and †C. nougareti). These protobundles have so far only been identified in some hybodonts, a clade sister to neoselachians (Cuny et al., 2017). Despite marked morphological differences between the analysed teeth, all analysed taxa display a shared general tooth enameloid organisation, which is the most complex known so far among batomorphs—and probably among elasmobranchs in general. Most display, from base to the apex: a single SCE layer which passes into RBE sometimes through a phase of protobundles as the enameloid thickens slightly higher in the crown, with apico‐basal PBE appearing mixed with RBE higher up at the base of the cusp, followed by the classical three‐layered enameloid (SCE, PBE, TBE) in the apex, with rare TRB. This highly complex but shared tooth enameloid microstructure does not lend support for either a phylogenetic signal nor a morpho‐adaptative signal provided by enameloid characters across sclerorhynchoid taxa. However, we identified variations in the microstructure of crown ornament, which can be composed of PBE with an external SCE (e.g., †C. nougareti) or only SCE (e.g., †Sclerorhynchus sp.), as in shark tooth enameloid. We further report new microstructure associations among elasmobranchs in the tooth ornament of some taxa such as RBE with PBE (†D. stromeri and †P. pouiti), SCE, RBE with TBE (†L. hiram) and PBE, RBE with TBE (also possibly SCE) (†O. pharao, †P. branisi, †P. triangularis and †G. leptodon). These features are unknown in shark tooth enameloid and point to an extremely high diversity of microstructures achieved by sclerorhynchoids compared to other elasmobranch clades analysed so far. These variations in ornament microstructure, however, do not seem to fit the systematics of sclerorhynchoids (Cappetta, 2012; Villalobos‐Segura, Kriwet, et al., 2021; Villalobos‐Segura, Underwood, et al., 2021) and most probably carry little phylogenetic signal.

Widespread complex enameloid microstructures among batomorph rostral denticles

The enameloid microstructure of odontodes of extant and extinct elasmobranchs has been little studied and available information about the distribution of enameloid characters within their dermal skeleton is even less known than for their teeth (Cuny et al., 2017; Manzanares et al., 2014). Manzanares et al. (2014) found that the microstructure of placoid scales sampled from various taxa representing †Hybodontiformes as well as extant batomorphs and sharks is mainly composed of SCE. However, they reported some degree of organisation (e.g., weakly oriented crystallites, poorly arranged parallel RBE‐like crystallites oriented perpendicular to the crown surface) in most samples. Cuny et al. (2017) reported similar poorly structured RBE ‘protobundles’ oriented perpendicular to the crown surface from a ‘button’‐shaped hybodont dermal denticle and noted the striking similarity with the microstructure found in the teeth of some hybodont taxa (Cuny et al., 2017; Enault et al., 2015). This weak organisation found in placoid scales was proposed to occur as a consequence of self‐organising processes, possibly linked with constraints imposed by the presence of dentine tubules invading the inner parts of the enameloid (Manzanares et al., 2014). An even more complex enameloid microstructure was reported from isolated fossil chondrichthyan dermal scales from the Valanginian (Lower Cretaceous) of Austria (Feichtinger et al., 2020). The latter demonstrated that bundled enameloid differentiated into TBE and PBE was present in the minute placoid scales of some elasmobranch taxa, whereas such components were so far restricted to elasmobranch teeth. Recent studies have further demonstrated the presence of complex enameloid microstructure in the rostral denticles of the sclerorhynchoid species †Ischyrhiza mira and †Onchopristis numida (Cook et al., 2022, 2026). These two onchopristid species display highly derived enameloid microstructures in the apical region of the enameloid covering their rostral denticles.

Our study confirms the presence of such a complex organisation in rostral denticle enameloid of members of the family †Onchopristidae (Cook et al., 2022, 2026) and brings strong evidence for a widespread presence of complex organisation across most members of the Sclerorhynchoidei sampled so far. Our results allow the identification of a typical sclerorhynchoid rostral denticle enameloid microstructure that is strikingly similar to that of their teeth, namely (from base of crown to apex): SCE passing to RBE (sometimes with a more or less developed protobundle structure), then PBE mixed with RBE and an apex formed with PBE, TBE and TRB. This structure was observed in most taxa whose complete rostral denticle(s) could be analysed in longitudinal section and varies only at the base of the crown where the SCE/protobundles can be lacking in some taxa (†S. stromeri, †Sclerorhynchus sp. and †D. stromeri). In the latter, the enameloid forming the base of the crown is composed of RBE. All available taxa display a similar microstructure in the apex (not observable in †C. nougareti), which is identical to the microstructure observed in teeth. This similarity is even more striking among rostral denticles considering the extremely wide range of sizes and morphologies represented by the specimens selected for this study.

We observed variations in the microstructure of denticles' cutting edges, which are either composed of: (1) RBE only (†L. hiram, Figure 12E); (2) RBE with bundles oriented perpendicular to the cutting edge's axis and inner TBE (†P. branisi, Figure 16C; †L. hiram, Figure 12F); (3) inner RBE crossed by apico‐basal PBE bundles and outer PBE perpendicular to the axis (†I. mira, Figure 10K,L); (4) only PBE with the outer part being oriented perpendicular to the cutting‐edge's axis (†G. leptodon, Figure 8F); and (5) inner TBE and external PBE perpendicular to the axis (†O. dunklei, Figure 3D; †C. nougareti, Figure 4F,K; †D. stromeri, Figure 6F; †Sclerorhynchus sp., Figure 13I; †S. stromeri, Figure 14D; †O. pharao, Figure 15I), often with TRB. This variation is very likely due to slight differences in the height at which the transverse sections were made and to differences in the morphology and size of the cutting edges. Since we demonstrated that only the uppermost part of the cusp is composed of PBE/TBE, whereas the rest of the crown is made of RBE, minor differences in the relative position of the section can result in either observing only RBE and no PBE, a mix of the two, or PBE/TBE with TRB (reminiscent of the RBE). Most denticles analysed were either devoid of crown ornament or the latter was not observable, but we identified that the ridges situated near the posterior cutting edges in the denticle of †G. leptodon are composed of SCE.

Overall, the enameloid microstructure identified in the rostral denticles analysed compares well with previous descriptions of denticles from †I. mira (Cook et al., 2022) and †O. numida (Cook et al., 2026). Cook et al. (2026) noted that the enameloid microstructure of rostral denticles of †O. numida differs from that of †I. mira in the presence of ‘circumferential bundles’ on the outer surface of the dorsal and ventral faces of the apical crown region. Circumferential bundles (see Akimoto et al., 2025) refer to crystallite bundles oriented perpendicular to the axis of the cutting edges, thus enveloping the inner enameloid layer. The variety of sections and surface observations made on the large sample of taxa and morphologies represented here allows the identification of the typical presence of this structure across most analysed rostral denticles, as in teeth. Our observations indicate that circumferential bundles are mostly PBE bundles that orient perpendicular to (and make up) the cutting edges or labial crown folds, as in teeth of †D. stromeri. However, in some instance circumferential bundles originate from RBE bundles as in denticles of †D. stromeri (Figure 6C), †G. leptodon (Figures 8C–F) and †L. hiram (Figure 12D), as tentatively suggested earlier (Cook et al., 2026; see also Akimoto et al., 2025). The presence of circumferential bundles seems related to the size and development of the cutting‐edges or crown folds and not necessarily to force constraints (contra Cook et al., 2026). Denticles (and teeth) with large and developed cutting edges display external PBE bundles oriented perpendicular to the cutting‐edge axis well away from the cutting edge (†C. nougareti, †Sclerorhynchus sp., †O. pharao, †P. branisi) and sometimes as distant as in the middle of the crown face (†S. stromeri). This is similar to the condition in sclerorhynchoid teeth (see surface observations herein) and in other elasmobranch teeth composed of a BCE unit and cutting edges (Guinot & Cappetta, 2011). The slight differences in enameloid microstructure at the base of the crown between †O. numida (RBE) and †I. mira (SCE) are most probably due to observation bias based on non‐continuous sections. Therefore, the apparent absence of circumferential bundles in denticles of †I. mira is likely related to their reduced cutting edges, but our analyses show that it is present in the cutting edges.

As concluded for teeth, we could not identify variations in the enameloid microstructure of rostral denticles that could represent taxonomic criteria and these variations do not seem to reflect similar morphologies either.

Implications for the understanding of enameloid character distribution in elasmobranch odontodes

Our enameloid microstructure analysis of a broad taxonomic and morphological range of oral and dermal elements of an extinct batomorph clade provides important clues to the understanding of the distribution and evolution of the enameloid organisation in elasmobranchs. One major outcome of our observations is that virtually all enameloid units and components previously recognised separately in various elasmobranch clades (stem elasmobranchs, hybodonts, sharks, batomorphs) are realised within teeth as well as within rostral denticles of sclerorhynchoids. In addition, our results support continuity between each components/units. Perhaps the most interesting in this regard concerns the RBE component. This component combines multiple structures that have different characteristics but share the feature of being oriented perpendicular to the surface of the crown (Cuny et al., 2017). This includes Loose Radial Bundles (LRB) and Thick Radial Bundles (TRB) that are common in shark teeth, but can also make up the whole enameloid thickness. The latter was only rarely identified in some hybodont and batomorph teeth, as well as in placoid scales (Cuny et al., 2017; Enault et al., 2015; Manzanares et al., 2014). Our results show that this RBE making up the whole enameloid layer is always present at the base of the crown of all sclerorhynchoid teeth and denticles analysed. They further highlight that the RBE layer is a transitional microstructure between SCE and PBE and that it can be composed of well‐individualised bundles or by poorly defined ‘protobundles’. This RBE is very similar to the enameloid microstructure observed at the base of the crown of the neoselachian shark †Synechodus sp. (Guinot & Cappetta, 2011; Figure 7E), where the RBE is crossed by packs of radially oriented PBE bundles. A similar RBE was reported (as SLE) at the very base of the crown of †Paraorthacodus sp. (Guinot & Cappetta, 2011; Figure 5). Similarly, our observations allow the identification of protobundles – i.e., poorly structured (disorganised) bundles (see Cuny et al., 2017; Enault et al., 2015) – as an intermediate microstructure between individualised crystallites (SCE) and well‐defined bundles. Consequently, our results indicate that some of the few features (RBE, protobundles) that were considered characteristic of some taxa (e.g., hybodonts) is realised as intermediate organisations between other components in teeth and rostral denticles of sclerorhynchoids.

Enameloid microstructure exploration using a combination of surface observations, transverse sections and longitudinal sections allowed the identification of tight relationships between different components of the BCE unit. We have underlined above that the RBE can participate in the circumferential bundles linked with cutting edges, but we also identified that bundles forming the RBE can curve apico‐basally to participate in the PBE at the level of the transition from RBE to PBE (base of the apex in teeth and denticles) (Figures 6I, 7B, 10D,E). Furthermore, TRB bundles present in the well‐differentiated enameloid of the crown apex are simple reminiscences of RBE bundles that become scarce and are replaced by apico‐basally oriented PBE bundles. The enameloid of the rostral denticle of Sclerorhynchus sp. further supports a continuity between circumferential bundles of the PBE and TRB crossing the inner, apico‐basally oriented PBE (Figure 13H). Similar evidence is found in C. nougareti teeth where TRB bundles originate from outer apico‐basally oriented PBE bundles (Figure 5C). It was clearly demonstrated that the PBE and TBE components are composed of the same bundles and that bundles of RBE can participate in the TBE (Cuny et al., 2017; Enault et al., 2015). Our observations provide strong arguments to conclude that PBE and RBE (including TRB) can also be composed of the same bundles of changing orientation. This relationship is not unexpected considering that all these components (PBE, RBE, TRB and TBE) are included in the same BCE unit. Yet, it has been reported that bundles forming the TRB originate from the external SCE unit in shark teeth (Fellah et al., 2021; Guinot & Cappetta, 2011), and this seems to be also the case in sclerorhynchoid odontodes. However, this observation may be incongruent with recent analysis of calcification processes of extant lamnid shark teeth proposing that RBE bundles differ from PBE‐TBE in being constructed by the calcification of collagen and non‐collagenous organic fibrils, respectively (Akimoto et al., 2025).

We found no evidence for a clear phylogenetic signal provided by the distribution of enameloid microstructure characters neither in teeth, nor among rostral denticles sampled here. Similarly, we found no evidence for a link between odontode morphology and enameloid organisation. Instead, our observations point to a possible link with geometrical constraints of the crown enameloid layer such as height and width. The SCE is only present in the lowermost part of the crown of denticles and teeth, where the enameloid layer is very thin. This component is absent from odontodes whose basal crown edge is thick (e.g., denticles of †D. stromeri). Similarly, the presence of RBE (either composed of protobundles or bundles) making up the entire enameloid thickness is restricted to crown region below the main cusp/cap, but is reduced to rare TRB higher in the crown, where the enameloid thickens and comprises TBE, PBE and SCE. The enameloid microstructure of rostral denticles of †C. nougareti supports this hypothesis. Denticles of this species are only composed of SCE and RBE with protobundles as in the lower crown region of most teeth and denticles of other sclerorhynchoid taxa along the elongate crown analysed. Although none of the specimens studied has a complete apex, which may have a more complex structure, the absence of highly structured enameloid in these denticles is very likely related to their extremely thin (30–40 μm) crown. A similar RBE occupying a substantial part of the crown's height where the enameloid is thin is present in other elongate rostral denticles (e.g., †G. leptodon, †I. mira, †P. branisi). It is interesting to note that this area of the crown where the enameloid is entirely composed of RBE is always affected by several fissures oriented apico‐basally (Figure 2), which suggests that this component does not prevent enameloid cracks.

Finally, although the enameloid microstructure of teeth and denticles is highly similar among sclerorhynchoid rays, we did not find a clear link between the histological organisation of teeth and rostral denticles within the same species. Rostral denticles of some species are composed of poorly defined bundles, whereas teeth are composed of well‐structured bundles forming all known components (e.g., †C. nougareti). Similarly, although most species sampled here have both their teeth and rostral denticles composed of orthodentine, our results support osteondont denticles but orthodont teeth in †P. branisi. Other analysed species (†O. pharao and †S. stromeri) were reported to have rostral denticles composed of osteodentine (Cappetta, 2012), with corresponding teeth being orthodont. However, only orthodentine was identified in the transverse sections made in the rostral denticles of †O. pharao and †S. stromeri. It is possible that these rostral denticles are pseudo‐osteodont and that the transverse sections were made too high in the cusp to reach the inner osteodentine.

Overall, although our results support previous hypotheses that some elasmobranch dermal odontodes can evolve histologically complex microstructures similar to those found in teeth (Cook et al., 2022), the developmental, evolutionary and mechanical processes behind those microstructures and their potential links remain to be explored. Nevertheless, our results on tooth enameloid microstructure would lend support for an ancestral three‐layered enameloid microstructure in neoselachian teeth, with the single‐layered microstructures and other simplified organisations observed in several batomorph clades being derived (Manzanares et al., 2018; Underwood, 2006). This would cast doubt on the taxonomic identifications made on the basis of tooth enameloid microstructure (Manzanares et al., 2018). Considering the number of understudied batomorph (and shark) clades so far, knowledge on the distribution of enameloid characters across extant and extinct elasmobranch clades is too weak to draw conclusions about the evolution of enameloid characters.
