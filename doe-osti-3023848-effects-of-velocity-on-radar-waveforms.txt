 Effects of Velocity on Radar Waveforms
 Armin W. Doerry

 Abstract
A common assumption in pulse-Doppler radar is that when interrogating a point, or point-like
target, the received echo waveform is an attenuated and time-delayed, but otherwise faithful
facsimile of the transmitted waveform. The effects of a relative line-of-sight velocity between
radar and target is often presumed to manifest only as a pulse-to-pulse time delay, usually
simplified as a phase shift. However, a significant line-of-sight velocity will also in fact
substantially perturb the received waveform even for a single pulse, and may need to be
accounted for, and compensated during radar signal processing.

1 Introduction and Background
A common assumption for radar is that the waveform that is transmitted is essentially the same
waveform that is received. This implies a distortionless system, both radar components and
signal channel. That is, the received waveform is merely delayed and attenuated version of the
transmitted waveform. However, this is idealistic, and in fact wrong, on several fronts.

First, radar components in the signal path have non-ideal frequency responses. The best we can
often do is to design for acceptable performance in the signal passband, and occasionally employ
equalization techniques to improve performance. This is usually good enough, but not always.

Second, a distortionless channel requires that a reflecting target itself also offers constant group
delay, which in turn implies infinite bandwidth with constant spectral density and linear phase.
While this is clearly unrealistic, we can nevertheless get close with many targets that exhibit
nearly constant spectral density and linear phase over a limited bandwidth employed by the
radar. Many canonical test targets can serve in this manner, such as corner reflectors, tophats,
and spheres, when suitably sized.1

Third, the transmission medium through which the radar signal propagates is generally neither
free space, nor even homogeneous.† Consequently, it too may exhibit frequency-dependent
behavior, even in the microwave region. There is little that we can do except to perhaps roughly
estimate bulk transmission parameters, or perhaps make relative measurements.

Fourth, and finally, we observe that even if we could otherwise build and operate an acceptable
distortionless system, the usual presumption is that the system is distortionless for a target at a
constant range. That is, we often presume that for a waveform that spans some temporal width
or duration, the signal channel remains constant for the entirety of the duration of the radar
waveform. In short, a relative Line-of-Sight (LOS) velocity between radar and target is
presumed zero for the duration of the waveform.

In a pulse-Doppler radar system, we will often allow for velocity effects between pulses, which
manifest as pulse-to-pulse time delays, or phase shifts, and therewith estimate LOS velocity.
However, velocity effects within or across any one pulse itself are often ignored. This
characterizes the so-called “Stop and Go” model for radar echoes, a common simplification,
perhaps an oversimplification.

However, radar systems are often operated under conditions where the LOS velocity’s effects
even within a pulse are problematic. In such cases, the effects of velocity need to be understood
and compensated for optimum radar performance. This is the subject of this report. In
particular, herein we delve into the details of the radar echo characteristics for a general
waveform, and high LOS velocity.

2.1 Line-of-Sight Velocity Effects
We now acknowledge that the point target echo delay is time varying, and assume that it itself
varies linearly with time. This might be due to radar motion, to target motion, or both. Figure 1
illustrates relative motion due to radar motion, as is presumed by Synthetic Aperture Radar
(SAR). Figure 2 illustrates relative motion due to target motion for a stationary radar, as for a
ground-based Moving Target Indicator (MTI) radar.‡

‡
  Although radar motion may of course involve higher-order time dependence, we justify the assumption that the
point target echo delay varies linearly with time as follows. Within a pulse, typically on the order of tens or
hundreds of microseconds, we generally expect acceleration and higher-order motion to not manifest significantly.
For radar modes like SAR, pulses are ultimately placed or interpolated to spatial positions, so accelerations are thus

non-linear part of the target’s oscillatory motion. Nevertheless, if significant accellerations are expected, then due
dilligence would require reassessing this constant velocity model. We leave this case analysis as a homework
problem for the reader.

2.5 Doppler Tolerance
Recall that for the preliminary processing previously discussed, we presumed to know the rate of
change of echo delay time ts . This led to Eq. (16) and drove our subsequent processing.
However, this is problematic on two fronts. First, it is often rather presumptive to know a priori
the relative velocity between radar and unknown target. Second, we may have multiple targets,
indeed with perhaps a spectrum of velocities, that all need to be simultaneously detected. This is
the case for SAR imaging with an antenna footprint on the ground that yields a Doppler
bandwidth due to different radar/target velocities in different parts of the antenna beam, i.e., a
spatially variant velocity spectrum. Recall Figure 1.

If we make but a single representative guess at this parameter, then a good choice is the rate of
change of echo delay time to some overall reference point. For SAR, this might be the center of
the antenna illumination footprint on the ground. For MTI, this might correspond to some
anticipated range and range-rate. An important question then becomes “How good is our guess
at a representative target point?”

distortion of the received pulse [from intra-pulse Doppler scaling] is negligible.”6 We interpret
this to mean that for this application, across the antenna’s illumination footprint, the intra-pulse
Doppler scaling is adequately represented by that at the center of the beam.

3 Comments and Conclusions
The essence of this analysis is that the shape of the waveform that is transmitted is not
necessarily the shape of the waveform that is received, especially for high radar velocities and
extreme squint angles. Nevertheless, the echo signals can still be effectively processed.
Furthermore, they can be processed via any of several mechanisms, including with hardware,
firmware, and/or software. Nevertheless, the needs as well as choosing among processing
options should be done as part of a general system design, and not left as an afterthought.

We repeat several specific observations.

  A line-of-sight velocity between radar and target implies that the signal that was
 transmitted is not the same signal that is received, delay and attenuation notwithstanding.
 In addition to a time delay due to range, there will also be a compression or dilation in
 time, which is equivalent to a scaling of frequency.

  Doppler will manifest both as a phase change from pulse to pulse, as well as a frequency
 scaling within a pulse.

  For a SAR, even within a single scene, different spatial locations will exhibit different
 intra-pulse frequency scaling.

  The intra-pulse frequency scaling will be exacerbated for SAR by high radar velocities
 and extreme squint angles.

  For a SAR, it is usually sufficient to compensate the radar echoes to the scene reference
 point. Other locations within the target scene will often be compensated ‘good enough’
 with this.

  For an MTI radar, different velocity bands may require suitable adjustments to their
 correlation kernels.

  This effect will need to be compensated during radar signal processing.
