 Executive Summary
 The Delaware Valley Regional Planning Commission (DVRPC), through a U.S. Department of
 Energy (DOE) Energy to Communities (E2C) partnership, is leading the planning and launch of
 a regional center to coordinate strategies that advance energy affordability, resilience, and
 reliability across Bucks, Chester, Delaware, and Montgomery counties in southeast
 Pennsylvania.

 This report documents a housing stock analysis of the study area led by the National Laboratory
 of the Rockies (NLR), a project team member, as part of the planning phase of the effort. The
 housing stock analysis combines public ResStock™ data with project-specific utility rates and
 retrofit measure costs to characterize the study area’s single-family housing stock and to estimate
 baseline site energy use, emissions, energy utility bills, and energy affordability as well as the
 potential changes to these metrics that result from residential retrofit measures. The intent is to
 provide a consistent analytical foundation that supports the implementation phase of the project,
 particularly efforts that seek to improve coordination, ease of access, and delivery of income-
 qualifying home repair, weatherization, and related whole-home retrofit pathways.

 The baseline housing stock analysis shows that there are about 1 million housing units in the four
 counties—56% single-family detached, 21% single-family attached, 22% multifamily, and 1%
 mobile homes. These housing units, as modeled by ResStock, use about 34 billion kWh of site
 energy annually—41% of that being natural gas, 35% electricity, 19% fuel oil, and 4%
 propane—with 62% of that site energy used for heating, followed by water heating (11%), plug
 loads (7%), and cooling (5%). The analysis also shows that about 30% of single-family houses
 heat primarily with either fuel oil or propane.

 stock analysis, including the number of single-family houses by county and vintage, heating fuel,
 and income, from the ResStock 2024 Release 2 dataset.

 For the retrofit potential analysis of the housing stock, seven different retrofit packages were
 considered. Of these seven packages considered, the “light touch” envelope retrofit package
 reduces modeled site energy consumption from each fuel countywide, showing energy utility bill
 reductions for all occupied single-family houses, and a positive modeled net present value (NPV)
 in 45% of occupied single-family houses.

 The other six retrofit measure packages all include heat pumps, and their results are more
 nuanced. All six of these measure packages increase electricity consumption and decrease site
 consumption of the other four fuels, while decreasing site energy overall. These packages range
 from showing 8%–56% of occupied houses with increases in modeled annual energy utility
 bills—with bill increases being much more common with lower-efficiency heat pumps, an
 original heating fuel of natural gas, and without existing full-house cooling, as shown in Figure
 ES-2. The maximum portion of positive modeled NPV for these six measure packages is 20%,
 for the high-efficiency heat pump-only measure. Overall, housing subsets with most of the
 positive modeled NPV include houses with an original heating fuel of electricity, and houses
 with both existing central air conditioning and an original heating fuel of fuel oil or propane.
 Positive modeled NPV for original heating fuel of natural gas rounds to 0% for these six measure
 packages.

 The heat pumps provide both a new load, and a new energy service, for houses that do not
 already have whole-house cooling, such as those with no air conditioning (AC) or with room

 modeled situation. This impact is due to both the energy use of different types of AC and the

 1 Introduction
 This report presents the housing stock analysis of the Energy to Communities (E2C) Delaware
 Valley project. The analysis covers the housing stock of Bucks, Chester, Delaware, and
 Montgomery counties in southeast Pennsylvania. It focuses primarily on single-family houses,
 including their existing characteristics and energy use as well as the potential impact of various
 retrofit measure packages applied to them. This analysis was a collaborative effort among the
 project team, including national lab researchers and local organizations. It derives primarily from
 results of modeling tools from the national labs, especially the National Laboratory of the
 Rockies’ (NLR’s) ResStock, supplemented by key information from the study area.

 1.1 Report Organization
 This report examines the existing single-family housing stock within the E2C Delaware Valley
 study area and evaluates the potential of seven residential energy retrofits, including impacts on
 energy use, emissions, energy utility bills, and energy affordability. Accordingly, the report is
 organized into two main sections: Section 2 characterizes the existing housing stock and
 establishes baseline conditions, and Section 3 evaluates retrofit measure packages and their
 potential impacts. These are preceded by an introduction in Section 1 and followed by a
 summary of results in Section 4 and next steps in Section 5.

 1.2 The Energy to Communities Delaware Valley Project
 The Delaware Valley Regional Planning Commission (DVRPC), through a U.S. Department of
 Energy (DOE) Energy to Communities (E2C) partnership, is leading the planning and launch of
 a regional center to coordinate strategies that advance energy affordability, resilience, and
 reliability across Bucks, Chester, Delaware, and Montgomery counties in southeast
 Pennsylvania.

 The Delaware Valley E2C effort is structured in two phases. Phase 1 (Planning) focuses on
 research, analysis, relationship-building, and planning to establish the regional center’s vision,
 goals, and implementation strategies. Phase 2 (Implementation) focuses on carrying out those
 strategies through three focus areas: facilitating collaboration with home retrofit programs to
 reduce residential utility costs, expanding the capacity of local governments, and advancing
 regional visioning and convening.

 Phase 1 work was carried out by a project team that includes the following: DVRPC; home
 repair and retrofit program administrators (community action agencies, community-based
 organizations, and PECO); the four county governments of southeast Pennsylvania (Bucks,
 Montgomery, Chester, and Delaware); and nonprofit organizations serving the region (Green
 Building United, New Ecology, Inc., Pennsylvania Utility Law Project, and the Smart Energy
 Initiative of Southeastern Pennsylvania). The project team also included collaboration from DOE
 national laboratory teams—NLR, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), and Oak
 Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL)—providing technical analysis and project support. Private
 technical experts advised specific elements of the work, including modeling and analysis,
 stakeholder engagement, workforce development, and legal/regulatory issues (including
 AECOM, Connect the Dots, and eco(n)law). Additionally, the Philadelphia Energy Authority
 and the Energy Coordinating Agency advised on overall strategy and replicability.

 1

 Phase 1 activities include stakeholder and resident engagement; baseline conditions and utility
 rate analysis; energy affordability analysis; electric grid and regulatory considerations; home
 retrofit program and supply chain research; workforce and training analysis; funding and
 financing analysis; local government energy management planning; and governance analysis for
 the regional center—culminating in the Regional Operations Plan.

 This report documents the Phase 1 housing stock analysis. It uses ResStock modeling outputs—
 supplemented with local utility rate data and cost inputs—to characterize the study area’s single-
 family housing stock and to estimate baseline and retrofit scenario potential for site energy use,
 emissions, energy utility bills, and energy affordability. The intent is to provide a consistent
 analytical foundation that supports Phase 2 strategy design, particularly efforts that seek to
 improve coordination, ease of access, and delivery of income-qualifying home repair,
 weatherization, and related whole-home retrofit pathways.

 1.3 ResStock
 ResStock is a building stock energy model developed by NLR and funded by DOE that
 represents the U.S. housing stock circa 2018 (Reyna et al. 2025). ResStock datasets provide
 credible information about the housing stock and its energy use as it is, as well as its technical
 potential with retrofit measures applied. ResStock datasets include hundreds of thousands of
 housing unit models that represent the variety of the U.S. housing stock as accurately as possible
 using the best available data. The housing unit models’ site energy use and associated emissions
 and energy utility bills are modeled using OpenStudio® and EnergyPlus™. The resulting data are
 published as ResStock datasets. These datasets can be used to analyze the housing stock as a
 whole or specific segments of housing, such as a specific geography, housing type, or vintage.
 4,097 of the models from the ResStock 2024 Release 2 dataset (White et al. 2024) are in the
 four-county study area and form the basis for this housing stock analysis.

 ResStock models the application of retrofit measure packages to the housing stock using an “if-
 then” logic-based approach. For example, a measure package could be specified replacing all
 incandescent lighting with high-efficacy LEDs, with no changes to any other lighting. Any
 housing unit models with any characteristics change under the specified logic have their energy
 use re-simulated. ResStock models retrofit measures that its if-then logic determines are possible,
 regardless of whether they are a good idea for a particular house, and results must be evaluated
 together with information such as costs and alternative opportunities.

 The result of ResStock retrofit measure analysis is the overnight technical potential of a specific
 retrofit measure package, including the modeled impacts on site energy use, energy utility bills,
 and emissions. These results can be used directly or as inputs to other analyses such as adoption
 modeling or technology turnover studies. The overnight technical potential is a theoretical
 maximum impact, without consideration of economics, workforce capacity, or other practical
 aspects.

 2 Existing Housing Stock
 This section characterizes the existing housing stock in the study area, with a focus on the single-
 family housing stock. It describes the modeling approach and data inputs, followed by results
 and discussion covering housing characteristics, energy use, emissions, energy utility bills, and
 energy affordability across income groups. This characterization of the existing housing stock
 provides valuable information on its own as well as serving as the foundation for the retrofit
 analysis presented in Section 3.

 2.1 Methodology
 This section describes how the results of this analysis were developed.

 2.1.1 Data, Inputs, and Equations

 2.1.1.1 Study Area and Geographic Resolution
 The study area for this housing stock analysis is the counties of Bucks, Chester, Delaware, and
 Montgomery that border Philadelphia in southeast Pennsylvania.

 The study area for DVRPC’s project includes the portions of these four counties that are within
 PECO electric service territory because of the significant overlap of these two geographies. The
 project’s NLR housing stock analysis team found that the portions of the counties that were
 outside PECO’s electric service territory were not easily separated because they did not align
 with the sub-county geographic resolution options in ResStock datasets (i.e., Public Use
 Microdata Area [PUMA] from the U.S. Census) and that they did not disproportionately impact
 results because they were generally areas lower in population density. Based on these
 observations and limitations, the project team agreed to use the full four counties as the study
 area for the housing stock analysis.

 2.1.1.2 Weather Year
 The analysis uses the Actual Meteorological Year (AMY) 2018 weather data results in the
 ResStock 2024 Release 2 dataset. Using the AMY2018 results instead of the Typical
 Meteorological Year 3 (TMY3) results ensures that the weather data are aligned across
 geographies 1 and allows comparisons between the results of the existing housing stock analysis
 and 2018 data from utilities or other sources.

 2.1.1.3 Housing Type

 2.1.1.6 Household Income
 The household income data comes from ResStock 2024 Release 2, which in turn is based on U.S.
 Census data from 2015–2019 (Public Use Microdata Sample 5-year data). These income values
 are used to assign each household an income category: “low and moderate income” represents
 income up to 80% of the area median income (AMI), “medium income” represents 80%–150%
 AMI, and “high income” represents over 150% AMI. Unoccupied housing units do not have an
 associated income in ResStock.

 2.1.1.7 Energy Affordability Ratio
 The NLR team calculated the energy affordability ratio for each occupied ResStock housing unit
 model and measure package combination as the ratio of the household energy utility bill,
 calculated using the analysis-specific utility rates, to the household’s modeled income from
 ResStock. Higher energy affordability ratios are therefore associated with higher utility bills or
 lower household incomes. Using income circa 2018 with utility rates from 2025 for this
 calculation means the energy affordability ratio results likely indicate that energy bills account
 for a higher portion of household income than is actually the case. This inconsistency occurs due
 to different data availability for the incomes versus the utility rates and is a limitation of this
 work.

 The energy affordability ratio category is then assigned based on the ratio. If the ratio is over 0.1,
 the energy affordability ratio category is severe. Over 0.06 but under 0.1 is high. Ratios below
 0.06 are not categorized as either severe or high. These threshold values were determined by the
 project team after conducting a review of thresholds used in other similar analyses.

   2.1.1.8 First Costs
 Retrofit measures have associated costs. These include “first costs,” meaning material costs,
 equipment costs, and labor costs for installation. An NLR subcontractor, ICF, developed retrofit
 measure first cost models for this project relying primarily on data from RSMeans (Gordian
 2025). ICF provided two sets of costs, one for Doylestown, PA, which the NLR team used for
 Bucks and Montgomery Counties, and one for West Chester, PA, which the team used for
 Chester and Delaware Counties. The cost data, equations, and information they provided,
 including their methodology documentation, was made available to the full project team (Taylor
 et al. 2025).3 The NLR team used these equations to calculate first costs by providing ResStock-
 generated building characteristics to the cost models, such as the size of the housing model’s
 attic or capacity of the water heater.

 These costs represent the total first costs of each measure package, reflecting the costs if the

 In addition to first costs, costs associated with retrofit measures can also include ongoing costs

 2.1.2 Additional Modeling Notes, Limitations, and Uncertainties
 The modeling and analysis approaches used in this study, like all modeling and analysis
 approaches, have some important nuances, limitations, and uncertainties. Those most relevant for
 this analysis are included in this section, whether inherent in ResStock or specific to this
 analysis. However, this is not an exhaustive list.

   2.1.2.1 Time Frame
 Modeled energy use, emissions, and energy utility bills are presented over a time frame of one
 year.
 7

   2.1.2.2 Fuels
 Modeled on-site use of electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, and propane are included, together with
 their impacts (e.g., energy utility bills). Other on-site energy use (e.g., wood) is not included. The
 on-site use is often called “site energy”4 and is the basis for the calculation of energy utility bills
 that residential utility customers pay. On-site solar generation is not included in the analysis.
 Total use of site energy is reported, not net.

 2.1.2.3 Units of Measure
 All energy use is presented in units of kWh, including on-site use of natural gas, fuel oil, and
 propane, which are not traditionally shown in kWh.

 2.1.2.4 Correlation Is Not Causation
 Differences in results between populations of houses cannot be attributed to a single housing
 characteristic. For example, a figure showing a difference in results between homes with and
 without wall insulation may seem to indicate that adding wall insulation will cause a house to
 experience that difference in results. However, there may be a correlation between wall
 insulation and another characteristic (e.g., heating fuel, air leakage) that is the primary driver of
 the difference.

 2.1.2.5 Geographic Granularity
 Geographic variation is captured only to the extent possible with the best available data. For
 some characteristics ResStock data capture sub-county information (e.g., number of housing
 units, heating fuel). For other characteristics, data are at the state, regional, or national level, and
 only vary within that geography due to variation in other characteristics. For example, water
 heater fuel data are incorporated into ResStock at the state level, but are dependent on housing
 type and heating fuel. This means the same proportion—85%—of single-family detached homes
 with natural gas heating in Pennsylvania are assigned natural gas water heating in ResStock
 modeling, regardless of the location within Pennsylvania, and similarly 4% of single-family
 detached homes that have electric heating will be assigned natural gas water heating. Differences
 in the proportion of homes with natural gas water heating between locations in Pennsylvania are
 due to differences in the proportions of housing type or heating fuel.

 2.1.2.6 Shared Energy Use in Multifamily Buildings
 Energy use that is not typically metered at a dwelling unit, such as elevators in multifamily
 buildings, is not included in the modeled energy usage—with the exception that the portion of
 shared HVAC and water heating energy use associated with a specific dwelling unit is
 apportioned back to that dwelling unit. The Shared HVAC Systems appendix (Appendix A. ) has
 information on the modeled prevalence of shared HVAC systems in the multifamily housing
 stock in the study area.

 2.1.2.7 Heat Pumps Always Provide Whole-Home Cooling
 In the existing housing stock analysis, every house with a heat pump is modeled as using it for
 whole-home cooling. Similarly, in the retrofit analysis the measures that include heat pumps
 result in whole-home cooling for every house. This provides a new load, and a new energy

 2.1.2.8 Condition of Housing
 ResStock models do not account for broken equipment or other housing conditions requiring
 remediation (e.g., mold and moisture, broken windows), which may be prevalent in some

 The first (leftmost) subfigure in Figure 35 shows that wood frame walls and brick walls are both

 R-value is a measure of insulation’s ability to resist heat traveling through it. The higher the R-
 value the better the thermal performance of the insulation. Types of insulation differ, but values
 of R-6 or R-7 per inch of insulation are common. IECC 2009, a widely used residential building
 energy code for construction, specifies an R-value of R-13 or higher in wood framed walls in the
 relevant climate zone (4A) (International Code Council 2009). Less than a quarter of single-
 family houses with wood frame walls likely have levels of wall insulation that meet IECC 2009-
 specified levels.

 The second (second-left) subfigure in Figure 35 shows the types of windows that are likely in the
 single-family housing stock in the four-county study area. Double-pane windows are likely most
 common (three-quarters of houses), but single-pane windows are also common (almost a quarter
 of houses). Single-pane windows tend to be older and therefore also leakier than other window
 types (e.g., between the window and the wall) (Ridley et al. 2003).

 The third (center) subfigure in Figure 35 shows the infiltration levels that are likely in the single-
 family housing stock in the four-county study area. Infiltration is a measure of the amount of
 outdoor air that enters a house through uncontrolled means (e.g., around doors and windows)
 (U.S. EPA 2025), generally increasing the amount of energy needed to heat or cool a house.
 ACH50 is a common unit of measuring infiltration. It is the number of air changes per hour a
 house experiences when pressurized to 50 pascals during a blower door test. IECC 2009
 specifies less than 7 ACH50 when testing with a standard blower door test (International Code
   Council 2009).

 Figure 35 shows that most houses are likely leaky. The IECC 2009-specified level of less than 7
 ACH50 would only be met by 8% of single-family homes according to ResStock model data.

 3 Housing Stock Retrofit Potential
 Building on the existing housing stock analysis, this section evaluates the applicability and
 impacts of residential retrofit measure packages. Results include modeled changes in site energy
 use, emissions, energy utility bills, and energy affordability, as well as modeled first costs and
 NPV. This section is intended to support understanding of the scale and distribution of potential
 retrofit benefits and trade-offs across the housing stock.

 3.1 Methodology
 This section describes how the results of the housing stock retrofit analysis were developed.

 3.1.1 Data Sources and Equations
 All approaches described in Section 2.1.1 were used for both the baseline (existing) housing
 stock and for the housing stock with retrofit measures applied. In most cases the post-retrofit
 results were used directly. In a few cases, the displayed result is a calculated difference between
 the post-retrofit and baseline result.

 Not all measure packages apply to all houses. If a measure package is not applicable, the house
 will have the same results for the baseline and the retrofit measure. Most figures include all
 houses, regardless of measure applicability. Exceptions are indicated.

 3.1.2 Retrofit Measure Packages
 The NLR and DVRPC teams considered two of the more recent ResStock datasets (ResStock
 2024 Release 1 and ResStock 2024 Release 2) in the context of project goals and priorities,
 including discussions of available timescales, weather data used for modeling, and available
 measure packages. Based on these discussions a set of seven measure packages from the
 ResStock 2024 Release 2 dataset was selected as a proposal for use in the analysis. This set of
 packages was presented to the entire project team for review and comment, and it was ultimately
 selected for the analysis.

 The seven measure packages range from basic weatherization to more comprehensive retrofits.
 They include the envelope; water heating; heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC);
 and appliance measure components described in this section, assembled into the measure
 packages shown in Table 6.

 3.2 Results and Discussion
 This section presents and discusses results of the housing stock retrofit potential analysis in the
 following order. First, Section 3.2.1 describes the modeled applicability of retrofit measure
 packages across the housing stock, exploring which homes are candidates for different retrofit
 measures and providing context for other results. Sections 3.2.2 through 3.2.4 then quantify the
 modeled impacts of retrofit packages on site energy use, emissions, utility bills, and energy
 affordability. Cost-related results including first costs and NPV are presented in Sections 3.2.5
 and 3.2.6. Finally, Section 3.2.7 disaggregates retrofit impacts by specific end uses to highlight
 how different end uses contribute to overall results.

 3.2.1 Applicability of Retrofit Measure Packages
 The applicability criteria for each measure package are outlined in Section 3.1.2. Figure 42
 shows how many houses meet those criteria for the respective measure package across the study
 area.

 Houses that already have attic insulation and infiltration rates at or better than the levels in the
 envelope-only package—about 11% of those in the study area—do not receive the envelope-only
 retrofit measure package. Houses that already have electric heating, or that have certain other
 specific combinations of HVAC (e.g., boiler and central AC)—about 25% of those in the study
 area—do not receive the ENERGY STAR heat pump with existing heating as backup retrofit
 measure packages. The other packages are applicable, or at least one component of them is
 applicable, to nearly all houses.

 Applying a measure package changes a dwelling unit’s modeled results. If a dwelling unit does
 not meet the applicability criteria for a specific measure package, the modeled results will be the
 same as the baseline results. This is true for all results: energy use, emissions, and energy utility
 bills.

 • All packages show decreases in annual site energy use and in annual energy utility bills
 on average for single-family houses in the study area.

 • The other six retrofit measure packages, all of which include heat pumps, showed more
 nuanced results.
 o All six of these measure packages increase site electricity use and decrease

 5 Next Steps
 This section discusses how this housing stock analysis is currently being used and how it is
 planned to be used in the future, as well as plans for future related work.

 The E2C DVRPC team is currently using the results of this analysis—and the feedback received
 from the home retrofit providers—to refine and address additional research questions for
 inclusion in their Regional Operations Plan for the center, which will support coordination
 between weatherization, whole home repair, and other residential assistance programs in
 southeast Pennsylvania. The first of these additional research questions is the identification of
 specific communities with housing likely to achieve a positive NPV and related questions aiming
 at using this housing stock analysis to identify houses that are good candidates for retrofits.
 Using insights from the housing stock analyses, the E2C DVRPC project team intends to define
 regional research questions that may require additional analysis. The project team intends to
 research the suitability of certain measures to be included in home retrofit scopes in the study
 area. An example would be evaluating the availability of attic space to support R-60 insulation.
 Additionally, the project team intends to research the impact of bulk procurement on reducing
 the first costs of specific retrofit measures, such as heat pumps. The project team will also use
 the results of the housing stock analysis to inform other areas of the workplan, including analyses
 of funding needs, workforce capacity, and the region’s supply chain.

 Appendix C. Residential Heating Rate Participation
 The team looked at FERC Form 1 for PECO for the end of 20217 (PECO Energy Company
 2022). Table ED. 12-95 shows 193,629 residential heating (RH) rate customers (of 1,513,214
 total customers), which is 12.8%, as shown in the first row of Table C-1.

 The team compared this information to the ResStock 2024 Release 2 data for the equivalent
 housing stock, including the five counties of Bucks, Chester, Delaware, Montgomery, and
 Philadelphia, and all housing types, which showed 24.1% with electric heat and therefore eligible
 for the RH rate. This works out to 53% of eligible households on the RH rate in this housing
 stock.

 The study area’s housing stock differs in two ways from this. First, it does not include
 Philadelphia (which has a higher portion of electric heating), and second, it includes only single-
 family housing, both detached and attached (e.g., townhouses). With these differences, the
 ResStock data for the single-family housing stock of the study area show 12.8% with electric
 heat. However, data on the percent of this set of households that is on the RH rate is not
 available.
