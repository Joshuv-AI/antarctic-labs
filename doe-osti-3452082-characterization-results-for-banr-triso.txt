 ABSTRACT

Tri-structural isotropic (TRISO) particles were fabricated by BWX Technologies (BWXT) for their
BWXT Advanced Nuclear Reactor (BANR) project. These particles have some differences in geometry
compared to standard TRISO particles, based on modeling done by BWXT to optimize TRISO
performance. A representative sample of these particles was riffled at BWXT and sent to Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) for characterization using methods developed and applied in the Advanced
Gas Reactor Fuel Development and Qualification (AGR) Program. Results of the BANR fuel
characterization are reported herein.

 1. CHARACTERIZATION OVERVIEW

provides the sampling plan included in PIP-35 for the BWLEU01 material. Subsamples were created by

 2. DATA REPORT FORMS

Data Report Forms (DRFs) for individual characterization methods are produced herein to provide further
details on specific properties beyond those captured in IRF-35. All DRFs are attached to ORNL’s internal
procedures for particle fuels characterization, referred to as Data Acquisition Methods (DAMs). Each
DAM provides detailed instructions on the performance of specific characterization methods and includes
quality assurance controls as well as training documentation for operators. Any deviations or oddities

 4

observed in the performance of a DAM are noted by the operator in the Comments section of the
corresponding DRF.

Average particle weight for the BWLEU01 composite was measured on a total of ten sublots, each riffled
out from the initial sublot BWLEU01-B. Each sublot consisted of approximately 150 particles, which
were weighed and then counted using a digital assistant tool. The average particle weight for each sublot
was then calculated in the DRF, and the mean average particle weight was calculated for all sublots as
shown in Figure 4 and Figure 5. As would be expected, the standard deviation in average particle weight
was quite small, as the sample size of each sublot was sufficient to obtain a consistent average particle
weight despite the natural distribution of particle weight. The only oddity noted in this measurement was
a single doublet particle that was identified in BWLEU01-B04; this was counted as 2 particles.

Approximately 800 BWLEU01 particles were measured for size and shape by shadow imaging. Backlit
images of a monolayer of particles were processed to identify the boundary points around the outer
circumference of each particle, and these points were used for calculation of all subsequent parameters for
this method. The first tab of the shadow imaging DRF (Figure 6) provides the imaging conditions and
microscope calibration information. The second tab of the shadow imaging DRF (Figure 7) shows the
distribution of average particle diameter, which was normally distributed about the mean average particle
diameter of 790.5 µm. The third tab of the shadow imaging DRF (Figure 8) shows the distribution of
particle aspect ratio, which was calculated as the ratio of the maximum diameter to the minimum
diameter. This parameter has historically been used in TRISO specifications to control for particle
asphericity; however, this is based on the historical experience with external gelation kernel synthesis,
which sometimes led to oblate spheroid kernels whose asphericity would propagate through the coating
layers and be reasonably well captured by measurement of aspect ratio. More modern internal gelation
kernel synthesis does not typically result in oblate spheroid kernels, though faceted and fractured kernels
may be present. These aspherical kernels and variations in sphericity following the buffer deposition are
generally transmitted through the outer three coating layers but are not well captured by aspect ratio.

The fourth tab of the shadow imaging DRF (Figure 9) gives an estimate for particle volume based on the
shadow imaging data. This estimate is not fully accurate because it is based only on the two-dimensional
measurement of each particle; however, it is more accurate than simply using mean particle diameter to
calculate equivalent volume due to the statistical effect of cubing the mean diameter. The fifth (Figure 10)
and sixth (Figure 11) tabs of the shadow imaging DRF show the distribution of local minimum/maximum
curvature for the particles. As mentioned previously, aspect ratio does not capture well the asphericities
common in TRISO particles fabricated with internal gelation synthesized kernels. Measurement of local
Gaussian curvature is a more local measure, which better captures the impact of faceted or fractured
kernels and asphericities created by buffer deposition. There is no formal basis or historical precedent for

 6

specification of minimum or maximum curvature, although areas with low curvature tend to have locally
thin structural layers and areas with high curvature may act as stress concentration points.

Measurement of the density of TRISO coating layers typically requires either interrupted coating runs or
hot sampling for the buffer and IPyC layers because these layers cannot be readily separated from the
others. However, the SiC layer density and the OPyC layer density can be measured from complete
TRISO particles by crushing particles, removing fragments of OPyC for measurement, and removing
fragments of SiC with attached carbonaceous layers for a burn step to remove other layers. The resulting
SiC and OPyC fragments can then be floated in linear density columns in which a gradient of density in
the correct range is produced by careful mixing and pumping of two liquids of appropriate density in
varying ratios. The columns are calibrated based on floats of known density, and based on that calibration

the density of each fragment can be assessed based on its position within the column. Results for the
density of the SiC and OPyC layers of BWLEU01 are given in Figure 20 and Figure 21.

TRISO particles with a thin or porous IPyC layer may allow chlorine, which is a byproduct of the SiC
layer deposition, to infiltrate into the buffer layer. Once there, this chlorine can interact with the kernel

 21

during high-temperature heat treatment such as is used in compacting, resulting in uranium dispersion
into the buffer layer. Particles from BWLEU01 were mixed with graphite powder and heat-treated for one
hour at 1800°C to simulate compacting conditions, then placed in monolayers in tape mounts for
radiography imaging to identify any particles with uranium dispersion indicating potential defective IPyC.
As shown in Figure 22, two particles out of 63,522 were identified as such, resulting in an observed rate
of 3.15E-5 and a 95% confidence limit of 9.9E-5 for the true defect rate of the population.

When analyzing radiographs of such a large number of particles, other anomalies may be identified.
These are also catalogued in Figure 22 even though these anomalies are not generally given specified
limits. Most notably, 69 particles were identified as having more than one kernel, typically full doublets.
