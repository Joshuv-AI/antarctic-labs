 Abstract

 useful in identifying buried transuranic waste. The generation rate of these xenon isotopes
 depends on the amount of 240Pu present and the cumulative fission yield (fission fraction) for
 each isotope. Published measurements of the cumulative yields for masses 133 and 135 were
 not consistent with one another. The uncertainties obtained from the variation in the literature
 are on the order of 13% and 10% for masses 133 and 135, respectively. The cumulative fission

 Summary
 Radioactive xenon isotopes (133Xe and 135Xe) generated by the spontaneous fission of 240Pu are
 useful in identifying buried transuranic waste. The generation rate of these xenon isotopes
 depends on the amount of 240Pu present and the cumulative fission yield (fission fraction) for
 each isotope. While fission yields have been measured and critically evaluated for neutron-
 induced fission of 239Pu, there is significantly less knowledge regarding the yields from
 spontaneous fission of 240Pu. Published measurements of the cumulative yields for 133I and 135I
 (which undergo beta decay to form 133Xe and 135Xe, respectively) are in poor agreement with
 one another. This leads to large uncertainties (on the order of 10-13%) in the necessary xenon
 cumulative yields.

 This project aims to measure the cumulative fission yields of 133Xe and 135Xe from 240Pu
 spontaneous fission with the goal of reducing the uncertainty in these quantities below 5%. This
 is accomplished by sampling the headspace gases from cylinders containing well-characterized
 plutonium nitrate solutions. The gases within the cylinder are allowed to come to secular and
 thermodynamic equilibrium, and then the headspace is flushed into a receptacle. Gamma
 spectrometry is used to quantify the radioxenon collected from the headspace. Reducing the
 uncertainty in the final values requires repeated sampling, and 24 samples are used to calculate
 the 133Xe fission yield, and 29 samples are used for 135Xe.

 The uncertainty in the measured fission yields depends on several factors. Each individual
 measurement results in an estimated spontaneous fission yield with approximately 11%
 uncertainty. The uncertainties arise from xenon quantification and the amount of plutonium
 present in the cylinder. Systematic effects from other sources of xenon (spontaneous fission of
 238Pu and 242Pu, neutron-induced fission of 239Pu), and sampling/collection efficiency are

 accounted for in the analysis. Uncertainty from the systematic corrections is included in the final
 analysis. The uncertainty for each individual headspace gas sample is quantified by Monte
 Carlo analysis. The final cumulative fission yields are (6.6 ± 0.2)% for 133Xe and (5.8 ± 0.2)% for
 135Xe where the reported uncertainties are the standard errors of the mean and account for the

 effect of collecting multiple measurements. The uncertainties represent 3% of the final
 quantities. This achieves the goal of reducing the uncertainty in the measured fission yields
 below 5% of the total.

 This report explains the motivation for using the gas sampling technique, the quantification of
 plutonium in the cylinders, estimation of neutron-induced fission effects, the apparatus design
 (including engineering steps taken to maximize reproducibility of the measurements), and the
 gamma spectrometry analysis. Control experiments to establish the sampling efficiency are
 discussed and analyzed. The measured headspace activities are reported and used to calculate
 the cumulative fission yield of each isotope for each measurement. Finally, the calculated
 cumulative fission yields and associated uncertainties are discussed. The 133Xe yield agrees
 with the literature, but the 135Xe yield is anomalously low. Explanations for this result are
 explored with the likeliest being a small holdup of xenon in the solution phase that alters the
 partitioning of the short-lived 135Xe while having minimal effect on the longer-lived 133Xe
 partitioning.

 1.0 Introduction
 Fission events create a spectrum of daughter nuclei with probability distributions that depend on
 multiple factors. For a given nucleus undergoing fission, the probability distribution will vary with
 the nature of the fission process (neutron-induced or spontaneous) and, if appropriate, the
 energy of the incident neutron. (Lestone, 2011) While products of neutron-induced fission (IF)
 for important nuclei (e.g., 235U, 239Pu) have been studied extensively, the results of spontaneous
 fission (SF) have seen less investigation and are much harder to measure. Measuring
 radioxenon as generated by SF has been demonstrated for underground sources containing
 238U (Hayes et al., 2013) or 240Pu (Dresel and Waichler, 2004) The latter work would have

 benefited from published data on xenon production from 240Pu – a lack of published data led
 them to assume that the SF yields were equivalent to the thermal neutron fission yields from
 239Pu. This report details measurements of the cumulative fission yields for xenon isotopes

 generated by the SF of 240Pu using repeated gas samples collected above plutonium nitrate
 solutions.

 1.1 Background/Relevance
 The distribution of nuclei produced in fission (characterized by the mass and atomic numbers)
 can be described using various definitions. Independent fission yields are the fraction of fission
 events that directly produce a specific daughter nucleus. Cumulative fission yields are often
 reported for a given mass number and combine all the independent yields for nuclei with same
 mass. Like the latter definition, the cumulative fission yield for a specific isotope is fraction of
 fission events that lead directly or indirectly (by subsequent decay chains) to its production. In
 this report, the terms “fission fraction” and “fission yield” are used interchangeably.

 This study presents measurements of the 133Xe and 135Xe cumulative fission yields from 240Pu
 SF using a gas sampling method. Solutions of plutonium nitrate are allowed to come to secular
 and thermodynamic equilibrium with a closed headspace. Subsequently, the headspace gas is
 sampled and the radioxenon is quantified. Using more than twenty headspace measurements,
 the cumulative fission yield of each isotope is calculated. The uncertainty for each experiment is
 assessed using Monte Carlo techniques, and the standard error of the mean is reported.

 1.2 Solubility Model
 The experimental apparatus was designed to facilitate mass transfer of volatile species (i.e.,
 xenon) from the solution into the headspace (Apparatus design). This allows thermodynamic
 equilibrium to govern the vapor/liquid partitioning of the xenon while secular equilibrium controls
 the amount of each xenon isotope in the cylinder. These assumptions guided the four-week
 separation between samples from a given cylinder.

 1.3 Uncertainty
 The contributions to the uncertainty budget are summarized in this section. The overall
 uncertainty includes contributions from the following categories: experimental apparatus and
 sampling efficiency, plutonium quantification and SF rate uncertainty, xenon quantification by
 gamma spectrometry. Each category is briefly described below along with relative contributions
 to the overall uncertainty. The experimental uncertainty is presented along with results below
 (Results).

 1.3.1 Experimental apparatus and sampling efficiency

 The sampling efficiency is affected by several aspects of the experimental apparatus design,
 each of which are physical phenomena that could contribute to Xe loss: adsorption of Xe to the
 walls and outlet HEPA filter, incomplete flushing of the headspace during sample collection,
 dead space within the apparatus, and potential leaks in the apparatus. These are systematic
 losses, the aggregate of which can be determined experimentally via control experiments where
 the headspace of a cylinder is filled with xenon gas and then sampled to determine the fraction
 recovered. The sampling efficiency is expected to be the greatest source of uncertainty related
 to the apparatus design. The experimental design was adapted to minimize the contribution
 from each of these sources of uncertainty, as described in greater detail below (Design
 Considerations and Implementation of Experimental Design). The sampling efficiency was
 experimentally measured at approximately (100 ± 13)% (Evaluation of the Sampling Efficiency).

 1.3.2 Plutonium quantification and spontaneous fission rate

 This uncertainty contribution is comprised of the uncertainty in the amount of Pu in each
 cylinder, the isotopic composition of the Pu in each cylinder, the rate of IF of 239Pu from both
 neutrons within the sample and neutrons in the external environment, and the SF constant for
 240Pu. The rate of IF is estimated at 2-5% of total fissions and therefore represents a negligible

 contribution to the measured radioxenon. The MCNP model used to calculate the estimated IF
 is discussed in further detail below (see Induced fission—neutron monitor and MCNP modeling).
 The 240Pu per cylinder ranges from 50-60 mg, with 2-3.5% uncertainty (see Plutonium
 Quantification Results).

 2.0 Experimental Design
 The experimental design, including apparatus and choice of materials, aimed to minimize the
 sources of uncertainty discussed previously (Uncertainty). Specifically, the Pu materials were
 carefully chosen and quantified to minimize confounding influences from 238Pu and 242Pu SF.
 Models were constructed to estimate the systematic influence of IF of 239Pu, and a neutron
 detector was used to monitor the external neutron background throughout the experiments.
 Finally, the experimental apparatus was designed with the goal of eliminating as many
 systematic uncertainties as possible.

 2.1 Pu materials
 Three sources of 240Pu were needed to supply sufficient material for measurement of SF
 products from 240Pu. These materials were sourced from in-house supply. They underwent
 characterization to verify major Pu isotopes and quantify minor isotopes that had not been
 previously analyzed.

 2.1.1 Characterization of Pu materials

 The characterization of the ingoing materials is key to reducing the uncertainty in the quantity of
 240Pu present in each sample, as well as avoiding confounding factors that would negatively

 impact data quality. This section discusses methods used for material characterization. Three
 parent sources of plutonium were prepared and characterized. They will henceforth be
 referenced by their PNNL Radiological Material Tracking (RMT) numbers: RMT 48, RMT 12962,
      and RMT 125001.

 In general, larger quantities of daughters, e.g., 241Am, were observed in RMT 48 and RMT
 12962, while minimal quantities were observed in RMT 125001 which had recently undergone
 ion exchange separations, although the difference was insufficient to impact contributions from
 IF.

 2.1.1.1 Material characterization overview

 Quantification of the ingoing 240Pu was important as an input parameter in the calculation of the
 SF yields. Additionally, quantification of the minor isotopes 238Pu and 242Pu is particularly
 important as these isotopes also have significant SF decay rates that may contribute excess
 fission products that increase uncertainty in the measured values. Likewise, knowledge of 239Pu,
 241Am, and 241Pu are used to estimate IF rates caused by the sample-generated and

 environmental neutron fields.

 The characterized Pu sources were selected for low 238Pu and low 242Pu content relative to
 240Pu. Two of the sources, RMT 48 and RMT 125001, were already available in nitric acid

 solution, 5 M and 0.35 M respectively. The third Pu source, RMT 12962, was a solid containing
 3.126 g of Pu, which was dissolved in a glove box with 20 mL concentrated nitric acid plus
 60 μL concentrated HF, for a final HF concentration of 0.015 M.

 Each Pu source was quantified to determine both total Pu concentration and isotopic
 composition using a range of techniques that provide complementary information about the
 sample. These techniques include ultraviolet-visible spectrometry (UV-Vis), gamma energy
 analysis (GEA), alpha energy analysis (AEA), liquid scintillation counting (LSC), and thermal

 2.1.2 Conflicting sources of fission

 Any additional contributions to fission will produce an excess of radioxenon isotopes that are
 then collected with the headspace gas samples. This section considers sources of radioxenon
 fission products that are not derived from SF of 240Pu.

 2.1.2.1 Spontaneous fission isotopes

 Among Pu isotopes, 240Pu, 238Pu, and 242Pu are known for their high cross-sections for SF.
 Analysis of the ingoing Pu material by TIMS for characterization of Pu isotopics is discussed
 above (Section 2.1.1.3). The AEA analysis provides an additional screen for non-Pu isotopes
 with high cross-sections for SF: 252Cf and 244Cm. No peaks were detected in regions associated
 with these isotopes so they can reasonably be excluded from consideration when interpreting
 the data.

 2.1.2.2 Induced fission—neutron monitor and MCNP modeling

 While this experiment focused on measuring the fission yield of radioxenon isotopes from 240Pu,
 the samples used were primarily composed of 239Pu and 241Pu, which both have large IF cross

Experimental Design 11

 sections. To assess potential interferences from radioxenon arising from IF, a set of Monte
 Carlo N-Particle (MCNP) Version 6.2 (Werner et al. 2018) simulations were produced.

 The first of these simulations considered neutrons generated within the samples from SF and
 (α,n) reactions on oxygen and fluorine in the solution. The neutron emissions from each of the
 three samples were calculated using the SOURCES4C code (Shores et al. 2002). The primary
 source of sample-induced neutrons were O(α,n) reactions comprising ~65-70% of the neutron
 production depending on the sample. Spontaneous fission generated most of the remainder,
 with F(α,n) contributing less than 2%. The neutron energy spectra for the three sample types
 were virtually identical, differing only in total neutron output. The geometry in the MCNP
 simulation consisted of the nine stainless steel sample vials embedded in the aluminum block,
 as shown in Figure 1. The output of the simulation was the ratio of the IF to SF rate in each
 sample vial assuming no external source of neutrons. The ratios ranged from 0.1% to 0.3% with
 dependence upon sample isotopics and position (i.e. samples near the edge had lower IF
 rates). Table 11 contains the results of this simulation along with the SF rate for each sample.

 Figure 1: MCNP simulation geometry

 The other two simulations address the issue of neutrons from an external source. There are
 standard background neutrons that are associated with cosmic radiation, however this
 experiment took place in the PNNL Radiochemical Processing Laboratory, where neutron
 backgrounds may be significantly higher due to a variety of potential sources. To track the
 neutron count rate a Ludlum Model 42-38 WENDI-2 (Wide Energy Neutron Detection
 Instrument) neutron detector along with a ratemeter with a 0-10V output (Figure 2) was installed
 next to the experimental apparatus. Neutron count rates were continuously logged throughout
 the entire experiment. A pair of simulations were developed to associate the measured neutron
 count rate with an IF rate. One simulation used the geometry shown in Figure 1, but with an
 external source of 252Cf SF spectrum neutrons. The other was a validated model of the WENDI-
 2 detectors taken from Garcia-Fernandez et al. (2018) with the same source of 252Cf SF
 spectrum neutrons. The combined output of these simulations was the ratio of the IF rate to the
 SF rate in each sample per count per second measured by the WENDI-2 detector. The neutron
 detector count rate was generally between 2-4 cps, with occasional short periods where the
 ratemeter would saturate (>10 cps). Based upon these values, it is expected that the xenon
 produced in the samples is 2-5% higher than it would be from SF alone (Table 11).

 The design of the apparatus has the potential to significantly impact safety and data quality.
 This section describes the goals of the design process as well as the features of the
 implemented design. The P&ID for the system is given in Appendix A.

 2.2.1 Design Considerations and Implementation of Experimental Design

 2.2.1.1 Design: Purpose & Considerations
 The primary objective of this project was to develop an automatic sampling unit capable of
 delivering consistent test conditions across multiple samples with integrated safety features to
 prevent spread of contamination during the experiment. This system aims to optimize yield
 accuracy by significantly reducing measurement uncertainties and enhancing the efficient
 extraction of xenon from waste tanks. Achieving these objectives required integrating features
 such as automated control mechanisms, the ability to handle multiple test cylinders, precise
 temperature regulation, strict adherence to volumetric constraints, and automated flow control
 and totalization processes. Additionally, the design incorporates sample agitation to encourage
 release of xenon into the headspace above each sample, thereby minimizing xenon holdup in
 solution.

Experimental Design 13

 The physical design, shown in Figure 3, includes nine sample collection cylinders and three
 control cylinders, all arranged within a solid aluminum heated cylinder block that maintains a
 stable thermal environment. A robust orbital shaker, with a three-quarter-inch orbital rotation at
 30 revolutions per minute, ensures uniform agitation across all 12 cylinders. The integration of
 14 thermocouple probes and four pressure transducers, coupled with detailed volumetric
 regulation of piping and manifolds, provides comprehensive oversight and control over the
 entire system. The system is monitored and operated by a Linux-based logging and control
 server (LCS) (page 17).

 2.2.1.2 Design and Engineering

 Temperature Control
 The aluminum block (Figure 4) provides a stable thermal environment for the samples. The
 temperature is regulated to 37°C by four 48-watt cartridge heaters, with the internal temperature
 continuously monitored and logged by 14 strategically positioned thermocouple probes for
 reliable temperature readout across the aluminum block (Figure 5). The temperature of 37°C
 was selected to be slightly above room temperature to mitigate the effects of variable
 temperature in the building. The effects of evaporation at this temperature in a closed vessel
 were calculated to result in a loss of 0.1% of the liquid volume, which is considered negligible.

 Electrical system

 The electrical control design was made and built from modules that are well understood due to
 their previous use on other projects. Some cost savings were realized by repurposing used and
 excess modules from past projects. The assembly was focused on a design customized and

 tailored for this need, but utilized Commercial Off-the-Shelf (COTS) items for ease of future
 maintenance.

 In order to satisfy PNNL electrical Authority Having Jurisdiction while maintaining serviceability,
 the control system was constructed in a way where high voltage was segregated from the low
 voltage. This allows non-electrical-trained personnel more access to open and troubleshoot
 operational issues in the low voltage electrical control box.

 For safety of operation, a manual enable circuit was implemented such that there is no way for
 software or a remote user to enable the power for opening the valves. While software or a
 remote user can disable and lock the valves, an operator must locally enable operation of the
 valves.

 Logging and control software
 The LCS provides high-level monitoring and control for the equipment. It serves two primary
 functions: automate the sampling procedures and save the system state to file regularly. It also
 enables remote monitoring so that staff can check the system status without having to travel to
 the system site. The code—written in C++ for use with a Linux operating system—uses software
 developed at PNNL over a period of more than 20 years. Roughly 90% of the code was used
 unaltered for standard operations (e.g., opening/closing valves, setting flow controllers). The
 remaining ~10% was customized for just this system (e.g., the sequence and timing for sample
 extraction). This server automates various controls, including those for solenoids, mass flow
 controllers, heaters, over-temperature protection, and vacuum pumps. Interlocks are embedded
 to ensure the correct operation of the apparatus while significantly reducing the potential for
 human error.

 Safety
 The design of the assembly maintained a consistent focus on safety. All components, including
 solenoids, are engineered to be suitable for hazardous environments. Solenoids remain closed
 when unpowered to isolate the plutonium sample solution from users, and outlets pass through
 HEPA filters, enabling sample collection in non-radiological conditions. Despite the system's
 considerable weight exceeding 750 pounds, low-friction, lockable casters allow it to be
 maneuvered by a single individual. Additionally, bundled protected wiring reduces wear on
 components due to the orbital motion of the shaker, while well-organized electrical cabinets
 minimize the risk of electrical shock during maintenance. The entire system underwent
 commissioning and inspection by various subject matter experts prior to introducing radioactive
 materials.

 Updates and Required Changes
 The design process was iterative, undergoing revisions along the way to adapt to performance
 observations and new information. Some situations requiring adaptation are summarized here:
 • The apparatus needed to fit within a walk-in fume hood measuring 3 feet by 5 feet by 30
 inches, accommodating the entire assembly, including the drip tray, beneath a table.
 • The diameter of the gas lines was reduced from 1/4-inch lines to 1/8-inch lines, driven by
 volumetric constraints essential for maintaining the desired xenon flow.
 • Output lines were configured into a helical pattern to maintain consistent length and
 internal volume, with each line precisely three feet long.

 • Attaching the entire system to the shaker table alleviated line strain and eliminated

 2.2.1.3 Testing and Verification
 Before deployment, each component was subjected to rigorous testing for leaks and operational
 readiness, with leak tests conducted during fabrication, initial testing, and post-deployment.
 Pressure testing followed an identical protocol. Comprehensive inspections were performed by
 PNNL subject matter experts encompassing pressure, radiological, and electrical expertise. To
 bolster operational readiness, numerous verification samples were captured using a residual
 gas analyzer (RGA), with multiple test extractions performed at the fabrication site and post-
 deployment, using a krypton standard as an analog for xenon due to their chemical similarities.
 The leak testing of the gas sampling apparatus had two phases. The cylinder and solenoid
 assembly was pressurized with nitrogen and submerged in water to find gross leaks.
 Additionally, all connections were tested with Snoop leak detection fluid while the system was
 pressurized with nitrogen. After all physical leak checks and repairs were made, full system high
 pressure and vacuums holds were done. All the lines were sufficiently leak tight before system
 operation validation was done with Krypton gas.

 The system was validated for leak tightness and operation using Krypton gas and an RGA. The
 normal sample collection container was replaced with a sample collector with a 1/16 in
 Swagelok fitting on the back for direct connection to the RGA. With this setup, we were able to
 see the sample transfer to the collection cylinder in real time. All the cylinders were filled with
 30ml of water to allow for the proper amount of headspace in each cylinder.
 The 3 control cylinders were loaded with Kr gas using the injection operation on the LCS which
 consisted of ten minutes of Kr gas pulled through the system using the purge pump and then 10
 minutes of nitrogen gas purge of the sample lines using the installed bypass. After loading the
 test cylinders, a normal extraction operation was used to test each cylinder.

 Before loading the Pu samples, the nine sample cylinders were also injected with Kr gas using a
 manual operation similar to the control cylinders. The main vacuum pump was used to pull the
 Kr through the selected sample cylinder for 10 minutes, manually opening solenoids and MFC’s.
 The main sample cylinders do not possess bypasses to allow for nitrogen purge, so the line was
 evacuated with a vacuum pump for 10 minutes instead.

 The RGA data showed a gradual increase in amount of Kr gas to a plateau corresponding with
 end of the sample extraction (Figure 8). The rise of Kr on the RGA data was much higher than
 nitrogen carrier gas indicating a full sample extraction of the headspace. A full atomic mass unit
 sweep was done after each sample extraction to look for ingress of atmospheric gases.

 2.2.1.4 Sample Extraction
 Sample extraction is initiated through the LCS, which precisely opens and closes solenoid
 valves, modulates flow rates via mass flow controllers and vacuum, and provides uniform
 sample extraction across all cylinders. The nitrogen carrier gas is introduced across all cylinders
 through dual manifolds. Nitrogen flow is regulated by a nitrogen injection solenoid valve and a
 mass flow controller. The LCS manages flow rate control and logs the cumulative gas
 throughput during the process.

 Figure 9 provides a schematic representation of cylinder with solenoid (left) and the flow path of
 nitrogen through the sample headspace (right). Upon entering through the input solenoid valve
 (red), nitrogen gas passes through the snorkel tube (blue), effectively clearing the gas
 accumulated in the headspace (yellow) of the cylinder. Flow modeling demonstrated a 98%
 removal of headspace accumulation with the use of the snorkel tube.

Experimental Design 19

 Figure 9. (Left) Schematic representation of the sample cylinder and solenoid
 assembly, identifying key components, snorkel tube geometry, and the controlled flow path
 through the system. (Right) Flow diagram illustrating nitrogen carrier gas injection through dual
 manifolds, snorkel-assisted headspace purge (~98% removal efficiency), and the controlled
 extraction pathway to downstream manifolds for xenon recovery.

 The sample holder is connected to the system by a dual-end quick shut-off that automatically
 seals when disconnected to prevent loss of sample. Each sample holder has a capacity of 250
 mL. Manual valve operations further curtail sample loss by isolating unused portions of the
 manifold. Closure of unused outlet manifolds effectively mitigates volume and sample loss while
 reducing the risk of withdrawing from the incorrect bank of sample cylinders.

 The operation sequence for a normal sample extraction starts with evacuation of the back end
 of the system, comprising the sample holder and all the lines downstream of the mass flow
 controllers. A pump pulls a vacuum for 5 minutes then the LCS holds for 5 minutes to test for
 leaks before sample collection begins. If the vacuum hold is successful (i.e., no pressure
 increase is observed), the solenoids open, nitrogen carrier gas begins to flow, and the mass
 flow controller (MFC) slowly ramps up to 30mL/min to minimize sample turbulence. The totalizer
 on the MFC resets before every system operation; the sample extraction is complete when 240
 mL of gas moves through the sample MFC. After passing through mass flow controllers,
 samples pass through HEPA filters and are drawn into the sample collection cylinder (aka
 “pancake”) by residual vacuum. The HEPA filters provide compliance with radiological controls,
 permitting the free flow of xenon isotopes while capturing particulate contaminants, e.g., Pu,
 ensuring safe, efficient operation, and sample extraction.

 Totalizer data for both the carrier gas and sample MFC’s is compared after the operation and
 any errors are recorded in the system health files by the LCS. The final pressure of the sample
 container is recorded and then the sample is disconnected from the system and transferred to a
 HPGE detector for counting. The LCS additionally records parameters such as pressure, time,
 and flow for each sample.

 2.3 Sampling protocols for control sample
 Incorporated within the system are three control cylinders filled with nitric acid, which undergo
 xenon standard injection to serve as experimental controls. Internally, these cylinders contain
 the same solution matrix as the plutonium samples. The xenon injection process initiates with
 the cylinder being charged with a known quantity xenon standard, which is then pulled through
 the cylinder until headspace is fully occupied by the xenon standard. Post-charging, solenoids
 seal the system, and lines are purged with nitrogen to remove any residual xenon. The
 extraction procedure mirrors that of the other nine cylinders, executed automatically via the
 LCS.

 2.4 Detection
 Headspace gas samples were collected in an aluminum cylinder with a 4.45-cm inner diameter,
 a 4.03-cm depth, and a 0.32-cm wall thickness on its side and bottom. The sample cylinder was
 then transferred to the counting laboratory where it was placed directly on the face of a Ortec
 GEM coaxial high-purity germanium (HPGe) detector enclosed in a thick lead shield (Figure 10).
 The samples were typically measured for three days minimum with some samples measured for
 approximately a week. The process of transferring the sample to the count lab and beginning
 the measurement usually took less than an hour. Cumulative spectra were saved every four
 hours, since the optimal count time is different for the two xenon isotopes detected in the
 samples. Statistical uncertainty is optimized for 135Xe after approximately 24 hours, while 133Xe
 continues to improve but only minimally after 3 days of counting (Figure 11). Each of three
 empty sampling cylinders were measured for 2-days count time prior to the beginning of the set
 of sampling experiments and showed no indication of background peaks that would interfere
 with radioxenon measurements. One of the three sampling cylinders was shipped to Eckert &
 Zeigler Analytics for conversion into a NIST-traceable gas simulant mixed gamma standard
 (link) that was used to efficiency calibrate the detector system.

 3.0 Results
 Headspace gas samples were collected between October 2024 and March 2026. The
 experiment had nine cylinders holding plutonium nitrate solution. Each cylinder’s headspace
 was sampled twice. After the initial set of headspace samples had been collected, the measured
 activities were reviewed according to the following criteria:

 1. (reproducible) Were the measured activities from a cylinder consistent?

 2. (credible) Did the measured xenon activities agree with the amounts expected based on
 the broad range of published cumulative SF yields?

 The cumulative SF yields from 240Pu for mass 133 and mass 135 range from 6-8.19% and 6.39-
 8.12%, respectively. (Ladler and Brown, 1962; Bushuev et al., 2007; Toraskar and Melkonian,
 1971; Demattè et al. 1997). Cylinders with initial activity measurements below 1 Bq for both
 133Xe and 135Xe were rejected on the grounds of credibility since these would have cumulative

 yields of 4% or less. These measurements likely suffered from sampling issues such as
 headspace leaks or gas flow. The initial hypothesis for the low activity measurements involved
 issues with the plutonium solution (e.g., precipitation or crud formation) in the cylinder. This was
 disproven upon visual inspection when the cylinders were emptied after the sampling campaign.

 From the initial analysis, three of the nine cylinders gave reasonable headspace activity
 measurements. One cylinder was disregarded because it contained significantly less plutonium
 than the others and the resulting xenon activities were too small for reliable measurement. Five
 cylinders (including all three from one manifold) had issues with reproducibility and credibility.

 Three additional considerations were used to reject measurements from the reliable cylinders.

 3. Was an issue reported during sample collection (e.g., valves not properly configured)?

 4. Was the interval between samples too short to allow 133Xe to reach secular equilibrium?

 5. Was there evidence of sample contamination from other sampling activities?

 The short interval arises for a sequence of measurements taken at one-week intervals from the
 same cylinder using 135Xe as a fiducial to establish reproducibility of the system. This sampling
 cadence allowed for 135Xe (but not 133Xe) to reach secular equilibrium. Thus, the 135Xe activity
 measurements for each of these samples is reliable while the 133Xe must be rejected. The
 contamination issue arose for samples collected shortly after attempts to inject control samples
 spiked with 133Xe to measure the sampling efficiency. These samples showed no increase in
 135Xe compared to other measurements, but the elevated 133Xe was explained by the

 contamination from the tracer gas.

 After applying these criteria to evaluate the data, there were 24 acceptable 133Xe measurements
 and 29 acceptable 135Xe measurements.

 3.2 Control samples
 Control experiments were performed to establish the sampling efficiency (𝜖𝜖) of the apparatus.
 This quantity is the fraction of xenon in the headspace that winds up being collected in the
 sample cylinder and measured in the analysis. The control experiment methodology is
 described in Section 2.3. In short, gas with a known xenon concentration is injected into the
 headspace of a cylinder containing a nitric acid solution that mirrors the solutions in the
 experimental cylinders but does not contain plutonium or other fissile materials. The injected
 gas is then extracted from the headspace using the same sampling procedure as the routine
 headspace sampling. The injected gas had a nominal composition of 15:85 Xe:N2 (as measured
 in mole percent).

 Four control experiments were performed, and three of these were accepted. The first
 measurement had an abnormally high injection pressure, so the experiment was modified
 slightly to first purge the lines to the control cylinder with N2 prior to injecting the sample. This
 resulted in three experiments with consistent injection and extraction pressures.

 3.3 Fission fraction calculated for individual samples
 Calculating the fission fraction (cumulative fission yield) requires evaluating Equation 8 for each
 acceptable activity measurement. The uncertainty for each calculated fission fraction is
 determined using Monte Carlo simulation over the possible values of the parameters. Each
 parameter in Equation 8 is assigned a distribution (either uniform or normal) with the appropriate
 breadth (uniform) or standard deviation (normal). The Monte Carlo simulation then generates
 10,000 sets of input parameters for each experiment, and the resulting distribution is used to
 evaluate the sample standard deviation. The sample standard deviations are then used to
 estimate the standard error of the mean (i.e., the uncertainty around the average fission
 fraction). The sample standard deviation is taken to be the average of the standard deviations
 calculated for each experiment. These values tend to cluster around a similar value with smaller
 values arising from the consistency of the activity measurements.

 3.4.1 Assessment of potential systematic sources of error

 The major question related to these results is how to explain the 135Xe results. There are a few
 reasons that this quantity may be systematically lower than expected. Earlier analysis of the
 sampling efficiency (Section 3.2) indicated that there wasn’t a systematic issue that would lower
 the measured xenon activities. Therefore, other explanations are required. In the existing
 literature, the mass 135 yield is usually higher than the mass 133 yield. (Bushuev et al. 2007;
 Toraskar and Melkonian, 1971; Demattè et al. 1997) The only exception was the data from
 Laidler and Brown (1962). Likewise, cumulative yields for 239Pu IF are higher for mass 135 than
 mass 133. (Tsubikihara et al, 2021)

 The most likely explanation for the 135Xe yield being lower than the 133Xe yield and range of
 reported values is that mass transfer in the system is not as rapid as originally thought. If an
 average of two hours passed from the time a xenon atom was generated until it reached the
 headspace, then 14% of the 135Xe would decay in transit while only 1.1% of the 133Xe would be
 lost in the same manner. A quick accounting for transport losses suggests that approximately
 two hours holdup is sufficient to make the two cumulative fission yields equal and to bring the
 135Xe into the experimental range while having a statistically insignificant impact on the 133Xe

 yield. In these experiments, the solution was heated and swirled to encourage mass transport.
 However, the solution may simply have been too deep to eliminate the effects of mass
 transport.

 A second source of error arises from the attempt to separate the contribution of IF from SF. The
 MCNP simulations discussed previously (Induced fission—neutron monitor and MCNP
 modeling) attempted to quantify the amount of IF arising from internal and external sources.
 Without knowing the energy spectrum of the neutrons incident on the system and while keeping
 the MCNP model relatively simple, the contribution was potentially too large. However,
 removing IF from the model only raises the final fission yields by around 0.003. This is
 insufficient to explain the apparently low SF yield of 135Xe.

 Despite the low value for the 135Xe yield, the 133Xe yield looks reasonable. Additionally, the
 technique showed that reproducible measurements were achievable once the behavior of the

Results 33

 apparatus was understood and characterized. In the end, uncertainties of 3% were achieved for
 the measured fission yields. This matches the project’s goal of reducing the uncertainty on
 these measurements below 5%. Therefore, a value of 0.066 ± 0.002 for the cumulative fission
 yield of 133Xe appears reasonable.

 4.0 Conclusions
 The project aimed to measure the 133Xe and 135Xe cumulative fission yields (fission fractions)
 from 240Pu SF with uncertainty of less than 5%. The final values are (6.6 ± 0.2)% for 133Xe and
 (5.8 ± 0.2)% for 135Xe. The final uncertainties are 3% of the measured value for 133Xe and 135Xe.
 Thus, the measurement goal of less than 5% uncertainty was achieved.

 The uncertainty analysis included systematic corrections for the sampling efficiency and for
 confounding IF and SF events. The final uncertainty included the uncertainty in these systematic
 contributions plus the uncertainties arising from radioxenon quantification, sample composition,
 and thermodynamic parameters. The average uncertainty for an individual measurement was
 14.6% for 133Xe and 15.3% for 135Xe. The standard error of the mean for each cumulative fission
 yield was tempered by the number of measurements used.

 The final cumulative fission yield for 133Xe agrees with corresponding 133I yield measurements in
 the literature, and it provides a value with a reasonable uncertainty. However, the cumulative
 fission yield for 135Xe is significantly lower (5.8 ± 0.2)% than any of the previous literature reports
 (6.39-8.12)%. This disagreement likely results from a small, but nonzero, transport time for
 xenon in the solution. Due to the short half-life of 135Xe (9.14 hours), holdup of approximately 2
 hours in solution would significantly impact its partitioning between the liquid and vapor phases
 while having a negligible impact on the 133Xe partitioning. Therefore, the cumulative fission yield
 of 135Xe should be used with caution.

 The technique of headspace sampling appears to offer advantages when investigating volatile
 isotopes with significant fission yields and sufficiently long half-lives. While the headspace
 samples were only able to provide quantitative 133Xe and 135Xe yields, this technique could be
 adjusted to measure the 131mXe cumulative yield or to isolate other species of interest. With
 thoughtful engineering and repeated measurements, it is possible to measure cumulative fission
 yields with a reasonable uncertainty.
