Lessons in balancing function with oncological control in a destructive lumbosacral tumor of uncertain pathology: illustrative case

Undifferentiated (unclassified) sarcoma of the spine is exceptionally rare and diagnostically fraught, particularly in the sacrum, where overlapping pathologies and complex neurovascular anatomy confer well-known management challenges.1 The astute surgeon should maintain all pathologies on the differential, including, but not limited to, chordoma, lymphoma/myeloma, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, metastatic malignancies including melanoma, and in particular giant cell–rich mimics such as tenosynovial giant cell tumor (TGCT)—­notably, all of which can confound the same frozen-section interpretation.2,3 The terms “unclassified sarcoma” and “undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma” (UPS) both denote sarcomas lacking definitive lineage assignment despite modern immunohistochemical and molecular evaluation, with differences reflecting histological appearance rather than distinct biology; such diagnoses remain uncommon in axial sites.4 Molecular reclassification has also shrunk the pool of undifferentiated entities as CIC-rearranged and NTRK-rearranged sarcomas are now recognized. At present, such advances underscore the need for broad panels before committing to irreversible surgery.5,6

Within the contemporary spinal literature, pediatric sacral unclassified sarcoma is documented, but adult sacral primaries are exceedingly rare. In 2004, Ohata et al. reported the case of a 13-year-old with primary sacral unclassified sarcoma treated by extended sacrectomy.7 Subsequent case reports have tended to feature definable genetic alterations in some of these undifferentiated neoplasms, including a cervical/thoracic epidural CIC-DUX4 undifferentiated sarcoma5 in a child and a lumbosacral UPS6 harboring NTRK1 fusion with targeted-therapy implications.

Sacral UPS reports illustrate anatomy-driven management challenges rather than truly unclassified disease. Maa et al. described sacral UPS managed with stereotactic radiosurgery facilitated by temporary visceral displacement using Foley balloons to provide an instructive example of technique enabling dose delivery adjacent to the bowel.8 Radiation-associated sarcoma arising in the pelvis/sacrum also enters the differential. Shibahara et al. documented UPS9 after palliative S2 irradiation, while larger series have highlighted the poor-prognosis phenotype of radiation-associated sacropelvic sarcomas.10 At the sacral location, these reports emphasize how prior radiation therapy and histological ambiguity complicate early decisions about margins versus function.

Contemporary spine-adjacent UPS case work beyond the sacrum tends to underscore the heterogeneity of presentation and the premium on multidisciplinary planning. Cariboni et al. detailed a paravertebral UPS invading the aortic wall and T9 vertebra managed with staged aortic resection and triple en bloc vertebrectomy.11 Additional single-patient reports across diverse anatomical sites (e.g., inflammatory UPS with fever of unknown origin, rare meningeal unclassified sarcoma) reinforce the variability of systemic features and the diagnostic exclusion process required before labeling a tumor “unclassified.”12,13

As it stands, ambiguous destructive lumbosacral tumors with intractable pain and neurological compromise present a dilemma between timely decompression and oncological adequacy. In this case, we report an exceedingly rare case of sacral unclassified sarcoma to add practical contributions to this sparse literature.7 Under diagnostic uncertainty—and in contrast to this earlier pediatric example—this case explores the balance of urgent, oncologically minded resection with the modern, anticipatory strategy of root-sparing decompression and definitive spinopelvic stabilization to preserve options for adjuvant therapy once permanent pathological analysis excludes look-alikes.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 59-year-old woman with no significant past medical or surgical history presented with excruciating low back pain and bilateral sciatica (left worse than right). Neurological examination showed intact lower extremity strength and preserved continence, but severe radicular pain limiting ambulation. Cross-sectional imaging demonstrated a giant destructive lumbosacral mass centered in the sacrum and extending from L5 through S4 with circumferential cauda equina compression. Systemic staging revealed no metastatic disease. Laboratory studies suggested a possible plasma cell dyscrasia, but multiple myeloma was not confirmed.

A CT-guided biopsy at the referring institution was nondiagnostic. The working differential there included amelanotic melanoma versus sarcoma; chordoma, plasmacytoma/myeloma, lymphoma, and metastatic carcinoma were believed to be unlikely. On presentation, her pain was intolerable despite scheduled acetaminophen and oxycodone 10 mg every 6 hours (approximately 120 morphine milligram equivalents (MME) over the first 2 hospital days) and required intravenous hydromorphone 0.5 mg every 2 hours for breakthrough (3.5 mg administered, approximately 70 MME). While maintaining an oncological mindset amid diagnostic uncertainty, this severity, as well as further evidence of progressive, high-grade canal compromise risking neurological decline, prompted us to proceed urgently with preoperative embolization followed by decompression and stabilization.

Imaging

Preoperative 3T MRI of the lumbar spine showed a centrally located, multilobulated, avidly enhancing T1-hypointense/T2-hyperintense mass centered in the sacrum with a necrotic component measuring 9.8 × 6.6 × 10.0 cm on the limited field of view (Fig. 1). The lesion invaded the right lamina and spinous process of L4, completely replaced the posterior elements of L5 with extension into the right L5 body, and nearly replaced the S1 body. There was encasement of the exiting right S1 root and compression of the left S1 root. Partial replacement of S2 and invasion of paraspinal musculature were present. Endovascular Management

Two days preoperatively, diagnostic catheter angiography mapped arterial supply from a hypertrophied right L4 segmental branch, bilateral iliolumbar arteries, and contributions from the left superior gluteal artery. Superselective microcatheterization confirmed robust tumor blush without opacification of the anterior spinal artery. Particle embolization of distal branches of both iliolumbar arteries (250–350 μm of polyvinyl alcohol particles on the left, 300–500 μm of Embospheres on the right) resulted in a approximately 50% reduction in tumor blush with preserved flow in parent internal iliac circulation (Fig. 2). Operation

The patient underwent a single-stage posterior operation. Exposure proceeded from L1 to the coccyx. L3–4 pedicle screws were placed bilaterally. We completed L4–S4 laminectomies and L5–S1 complete facetectomies, exposing severe dorsal compression. As anticipated, the tumor was hypervascular and infiltrative with extension through the pedicles into the bodies of S1–3 (≥ 70% involvement at each level) abutting or encasing multiple sacral roots. Intralesional frozen sections were read as “atypical epithelioid and spindle cell neoplasm with giant cells,” most consistent intraoperatively with TGCT. In that context—and given the patient’s severe pain and intact motor function and the functional cost of high sacral root sacrifice required for an en bloc resection—we pursued the maximal safe, nerve root–sparing decompression.

We proceeded with bilateral transpedicular corpectomies at S1–3 to amputate the dorsal tumor through the L5–S3 pedicles and meticulously dissect tumor from the thecal sac and L5–S5 roots. Although the sacroiliac joints were not completely resected, the tumor abutted the posterior sacroiliac joints bilaterally, and the extensive S1–3 resection, including ≥ 70% vertebral body removal at each level, was expected to compromise the sacral side of the sacroiliac articulation and destabilize the lumbopelvic junction. Accordingly, spinopelvic stabilization consisted of bilateral L3–4 pedicle screws, stacked bilateral titanium iliac screws to maximize distal pelvic fixation strength,14,15 bilateral double-rod titanium constructs to the pelvis, and a transverse cross-connector to augment construct rigidity.16,17 Posterolateral fusion from L3 to the sacral ala was then performed with thorough bilateral decortication of the L3–5 transverse processes and sacral ala, followed by placement of demineralized bone matrix bilaterally (Fig. 3). Hemostasis was challenging throughout despite prior embolization, with approximately 2000 mL of blood loss and a total of 4 units of packed red blood cells transfused. Neuromonitoring remained stable aside from transient limited fluctuations in the right tibialis anterior/gastrocnemius motor evoked potential. Reconstruction and Plastic Closure

The plastic surgery team then performed bilateral paraspinous advancement flaps with a Strattice acellular dermal matrix biological mesh onlay secured to surrounding muscle fascia and periosteum to separate the hardware and thecal sac from subcutaneous tissues and bridge the residual midline soft tissue defect (Fig. 3). Given the exposed critical structures, the possibility of adjuvant radiation therapy, and our institution’s established multidisciplinary approach to high-risk spinal oncology closure, this reconstructive strategy was selected to optimize durable soft tissue coverage.18–20 Two closed-suction drains were placed, and an incisional wound vacuum system was applied.

Pathology

As noted, the intraoperative frozen-section analysis demonstrated an atypical epithelioid and spindle cell neoplasm with multinucleated giant cells favoring TGCT. Permanent histological sections disclosed a neoplasm composed of sheets of epithelioid and spindle cells with a variable amount of bright eosinophilic cytoplasm and large, atypical nuclei with open vesicular chromatin and prominent nucleoli infiltrating the vertebral bones and paravertebral soft tissue. There was minimal intervening stroma. Some tumor foci demonstrated a lesser degree of cytological atypia with frequent multinucleated giant cells and intense lymphohistiocytic infiltrates. Mitoses numbered up to 19 per high-power field, and tumor necrosis (approximately 20%) was present (Fig. 4). On immunohistochemical analysis, the tumor cells were positive for SMA (multifocal), S100 (multifocal), and CD68 and were negative for keratin AE1/AE3, CAM5.2, SOX10, GFAP, desmin, HMB45, CD4, brachyury, WT1, and BRAF V600E. H3K27me3 demonstrated retained nuclear expression. A comprehensive solid-tumor fusion next-generation sequencing panel detected no gene fusions. Based on the nonspecific morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecular findings, the tumor was ultimately characterized as a malignant epithelioid and spindle cell neoplasm, best regarded as an intermediate-grade unclassified sarcoma. Postoperative Course and Early Outcome

Postoperative radiographs demonstrated intact alignment and hardware positioning of the lumbopelvic construct; however, MRI of the sacrum with contrast showed nodular enhancing foci within the right sacral ala at S1–2 consistent with residual disease (Fig. 5). Additionally, there was nonspecific enhancement along bilateral sacral roots, greatest at the left S1. The patient’s pain improved immediately; she ambulated independently with preserved strength and continence. Drains were removed as an outpatient, and the wound healed uneventfully. She completed adjuvant external-beam radiation therapy to the sacrum starting 2 months postoperatively (70 Gy in 35 fractions) without unexpected toxicity or treatment breaks. Ongoing metastatic evaluation has included biopsy of a suspicious lymph node, which was negative for malignancy. A lung nodule remains under evaluation with planned biopsy for possible solitary metastasis. Should metastasis be confirmed, systemic therapy will be individualized at the sarcoma tumor board review. Discussion

Destructive lumbosacral tumors can remain diagnostically ambiguous despite high-resolution imaging and intraoperative frozen-section analysis. In this case, a giant cell–rich epithelioid/spindle lesion mimicked TGCT on frozen-section analysis; however, permanent-section analysis demonstrated high mitotic activity, necrosis, and a molecular profile incompatible with common mimics to ultimately support a diagnosis of unclassified sarcoma. Early outcomes in this patient have been favorable, including immediate analgesia (which was most pressing) and preserved continence and ambulation without assistive devices. Unfortunately, however, there was a small residual enhancement, and this case harbors several high-risk features, thus rendering a guarded prognosis. In sacral sarcomas, local control is most influenced by margin status, tumor grade, and the ability to deliver adjuvant radiotherapy.21,22 Unclassified sarcomas (defined by the absence of fusion drivers and lineage-defining markers) behave variably, but typically resemble high-grade soft tissue sarcomas prone to local recurrence and metastasis.23 Dose-escalated radiation therapy will be central to local control, with a low threshold to consider early interventions for isolated recurrence. Most likely, anthracycline-based systemic therapy would follow should her workup reveal metastasis.

Observations

As said, the clinical urgency in this presentation with excruciating pain and progressive cauda equina compromise necessitated decompression and stabilization before definitive lineage assignment. In such cases, we deliberately engineer constructs to preserve oncological options and work to avoid gross contamination, provide durable lumbopelvic fixation, and plan closure. A timely preoperative embolization moderated blood loss and enabled a resection that might not have been technically feasible otherwise. A double-rod lumbopelvic construct restored stability across a resected sacrum, and the use of highly vascularized flap closure with biological barrier created a wound bed suitable for adjuvant radiation therapy. As a single case with early follow-up and intralesional margins, definitive oncological durability cannot be assessed—and is inherently limited by the tumor’s biology—so these conclusions remain illustrative rather than generalizable.

Lessons

When histology is uncertain, sacral tumors should be approached with an oncological mindset while still prioritizing neurological rescue and function. Irreversible decisions cannot rely on frozen-section analysis alone, and molecular testing is often required to avoid misclassification. When en bloc resection would necessitate high sacral root sacrifice with major disability, maximal safe, root-sparing decompression with definitive lumbopelvic stabilization is a reasonable first step provided the construct and closure are built to withstand adjuvant therapy or re-resection. Preoperative embolization, rigid multirod fixation, thorough decortication with graft, and early plastic surgery involvement help control perioperative risk and preserve the ability to deliver dose-escalated radiation therapy.

Diagnostic ambiguity persists even with modern imaging and intraoperative pathology, and urgent cases may require decompression before a final diagnosis. Here, frozen-section analysis suggested a benign mimic and supported a nerve root–sparing strategy; despite residual malignant disease, the patient achieved immediate pain relief and preserved continence and ambulation. This illustrates how thoughtful prioritization of function, stability, and future oncological options can yield meaningful outcomes when definitive classification is not yet available.
