When LLMs Stop Following Steps: A Diagnostic Study of Arithmetic Procedural Execution in Language Models
Sailesh Panda, Pritam Kadasi, Abhishek Upperwal, Mayank Singh

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) often achieve strong performance on reasoning benchmarks, but final-answer accuracy alone does not show whether they faithfully execute the procedure specified in a prompt. We introduce a controlled diagnostic benchmark for procedural execution , where models are given a step-wise arithmetic procedure and two numeric inputs, and must return the final computed value. Complexity is varied through procedure length and look-back dependencies over intermediate variables. Average first-answer accuracy drops from 63% on 5-step procedures to 20% on 95-step procedures. Generation-level analysis shows that failures often involve missing answers, premature answers, self-correction after an initial error and under-executed traces. These findings suggest that apparent reasoning ability can mask substantial weaknesses in faithful long-horizon procedural execution.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have shown strong performance on many reasoning tasks, including arithmetic reasoning, symbolic manipulation, multi-step question answering, and code generation
(
Wei et al. 2022b
;
Wei et al. 2022a
;
Weng et al. 2023a
;
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2022
)
. Recent progress has been driven by scale, instruction tuning, test-time reasoning, and reinforcement learning-based methods that encourage models to generate longer reasoning traces before producing an answer
(
Guo et al. 2025
;
Shao et al. 2024
)
.

However, strong final-answer performance does not necessarily reflect faithful execution of the intended procedure. This limitation is especially important for procedure-following tasks, where models must execute explicitly specified multi-step procedures while tracking intermediate states, applying operations in order, and terminating at the intended final output. Prior work has shown that LLMs may rely on shortcut heuristics, shallow patterns, or unstable intermediate computations, and can fail even when the complete procedure is provided in the prompt
(
Elazar et al. 2022
;
Shojaee et al. 2025a
;
Yang et al. 2025b
;
Xu et al. 2025a
)
. However, existing studies often focus on broad reasoning benchmarks or final-answer outcomes, making it difficult to isolate how procedural execution breaks down when the procedure is fully specified, the elementary operations are simple, and complexity increases primarily through procedure length.

We study
arithmetic procedural execution
: the ability of an LLM to carry out an explicitly specified arithmetic procedure and return the correct final value. We introduce a controlled diagnostic benchmark in which each example provides a step-wise arithmetic procedure and two numeric inputs. The procedure defines a sequence of intermediate variables using basic arithmetic operations, and the model must execute the steps in order to return the final value. Since the reference output is deterministic, this setup isolates procedural failures from ambiguity in task interpretation.

Our benchmark increases complexity along two axes. First, we vary procedure length, testing whether model accuracy is maintained as the number of required steps increases. Second, we introduce look-back dependencies, where a current step may require retrieving and using intermediate variables produced several steps earlier. This stresses procedural state tracking rather than only elementary arithmetic. Across selected combinations of input ranges, data types, operation variants, and look-back settings, the benchmark consists of 55 synthetic datasets, totaling 55,000 examples (see Section
4.1
). We evaluate 15 language models across multiple scales and report both answer-level and generation-level metrics, including FAA,
Correct@Any
, answer position, and step-execution behavior. This paper addresses the following research questions:

•

RQ1:
How does model accuracy change as arithmetic procedures become longer and require deeper look-back dependencies?

•

RQ2:
What generation-level failure modes emerge during arithmetic procedural execution, and how do these failures relate to final-answer accuracy?

Empirically, we find that the average first-answer accuracy (FAA) decreases from
63%
at 5 steps to
20%
at 95 steps. Increasing the dependency depth from using only the immediately preceding intermediate variable to using variables from up to seven previous steps further reduces average accuracy by
23.85
% points, indicating that models struggle not only with longer procedures but also with retrieving and combining non-local intermediate states. Our contributions are: (1) we introduce a controlled synthetic benchmark for evaluating long-horizon arithmetic procedural execution in LLMs, (2) we conduct more than 55,000 model evaluations across 15 models to analyze the effects of procedure length and look-back dependency on execution accuracy, and (3) our generation-level analysis reveals that a substantial fraction of failures are not caused by arithmetic inaccuracies alone, but by the model’s inability to reliably maintain and update procedural state across multi-step execution. Our benchmark, evaluation code, and generated datasets will be publicly released.

Related Work

LLM reasoning has been improved through prompting, scaling, self-verification, and reinforcement learning-based training methods
(
Wei et al. 2022b
;
Wei et al. 2022a
;
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Weng et al. 2023b
;
Guo et al. 2025
;
Shao et al. 2024
)
. We focus on work most related to procedural execution along four directions.

2.1
Algorithmic Execution

Prior work has studied whether neural models can learn and execute algorithmic procedures such as sorting, graph traversal, arithmetic, and program-like computation
(
Veličković and Blundell 2021
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Schwarzschild et al. 2021
;
Weiss et al. 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Abbe et al. 2023
;
Kazemnejad et al. 2023
)
. These studies often focus on whether models can learn an algorithm from data and generalize to longer or harder inputs. More recent work evaluates whether reasoning-oriented LLMs can execute explicitly specified procedures at inference time.
Shojaee et al. 2025b
show that models can fail on controllable puzzle tasks even when the full solution procedure is provided, while follow-up work finds that performance varies substantially with task design and complexity
Varela et al. 2025
. Our work follows this line but studies a simpler and more controlled setting: arithmetic procedures with deterministic ground truth and systematically varied execution length and dependency depth.

2.2
Synthetic Diagnostics

Synthetic diagnostic benchmarks provide controlled settings for testing specific reasoning abilities, such as question answering, compositional instruction following, relational reasoning, and semantic parsing
(
Weston et al. 2015
;
Lake and Baroni 2018
;
Sinha et al. 2019
;
Kim and Linzen 2020
)
. Recent diagnostic studies further show that strong benchmark performance can hide brittle reasoning behavior under small perturbations or increasing complexity
(
Mirzadeh et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Kohli et al. 2025
)
. Our benchmark has a similar diagnostic goal, but focuses specifically on procedural execution: the model is given the full procedure in the prompt, and complexity is controlled through procedure length, input type, operation type, and look-back dependency.

2.3
State Tracking

Many reasoning tasks require models to maintain and update intermediate states. Prior work has studied state tracking in procedural text, action-state reasoning, dialogue, and other structured settings
(
Dalvi et al. 2018
;
Tandon et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2021
)
. Recent work also examines whether LLMs encode intermediate reasoning states or rely on shortcut associations instead
(
Yang et al. 2025c
;
Huang et al. 2026
;
Lu et al. 2025
)
. Our task provides a controlled form of state tracking: procedures define intermediate variables
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3},\ldots
, and later steps may depend on variables produced several steps earlier. By varying look-back depth, we test how well models retrieve and combine non-local intermediate states during execution.

2.4
Compositional Generalization

Compositional generalization studies whether models can recombine familiar parts in new or harder configurations
(
Lake and Baroni 2018
;
Kim and Linzen 2020
;
Keysers et al. 2020
;
Hupkes et al. 2020
)
. For Transformers, prior work shows that length generalization and arithmetic generalization depend strongly on task structure and architectural choices such as positional encoding
(
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Kazemnejad et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2025b
)
. Our work is related because long procedures require repeated composition of simple operations. However, unlike training-time generalization studies, we test instruction-tuned and reasoning-oriented LLMs at inference time, where the full procedure is explicitly provided. Failures in this setting therefore point to weaknesses in execution, state tracking, or generation behavior rather than lack of access to the rule.

Task Formulation

We study whether large language models can faithfully execute an explicitly specified multi-step procedure when the full procedure is provided in the prompt. To isolate procedural execution from broader linguistic ambiguity, we consider a controlled arithmetic setting.

Each example consists of a step-wise natural-language procedure and two numeric inputs,
x
and
y
. The procedure initializes
S_{1}=x
and
S_{2}=y
, then defines subsequent variables through arithmetic operations over previously computed variables. The model must execute the procedure exactly as written and return only the final numeric result. We vary procedure length to control task difficulty. Formally, for each step
t\geq 3
,

S_{t}=S_{i}\;o_{t}\;S_{j},

where
o_{t}\in\{+,-,\times,\div\}
denotes the operation at step
t
, and
S_{i},S_{j}
are selected from a bounded history of previously computed variables. The size of this accessible history is controlled by the look-back parameter
k
, which determines how many earlier intermediate variables remain available for future computation. The final target output is the value computed in the last step of the procedure. Figure
1
shows a representative example of a generated procedure.

This setup gives us full control over procedural complexity while keeping the underlying computation simple and verifiable. Since the correct answer is deterministically computable, the benchmark allows us to distinguish between final-answer success and faithful procedure execution.

4
Experimental Setup

We describe the dataset construction, evaluated models, inference procedure, and evaluation metrics used to study procedural execution under increasing complexity.

4.1
Dataset Construction

We construct a controlled benchmark of step-wise arithmetic execution tasks following Section
3
. Each instance consists of two numeric inputs,
x
and
y
, an explicitly defined arithmetic procedure, and a deterministic reference output. We restrict the benchmark to two input variables to isolate procedural execution and intermediate state tracking from additional complexity due to larger input spaces or variable-management overhead. Models must execute the procedure exactly and return the value from the final step.

The benchmark is designed so that each elementary operation is simple, while the procedural burden increases through procedure length and dependency structure. This allows us to stress-test sustained instruction execution without introducing ambiguity about the correct answer.

Input ranges and data types.

We generate inputs from three numeric ranges: [0,1], [1,10] and [10,100].
For the ranges
[1,10]
and
[10,100]
, we include both integer and floating-point inputs. For the range
[0,1]
, we use floating-point inputs only. This yields five input settings in total: one setting for
[0,1]
and two settings each for
[1,10]
and
[10,100]
. Inputs are sampled uniformly at random. Integer values are drawn from the corresponding discrete interval with replacement, while floating-point values are sampled from a continuous interval and rounded to three decimal places.

Operation variants.

We construct both single-operation and mixed-operation procedure. In the mixed-operation setting, each procedure may contain all four arithmetic operations. In the single-operation setting, all steps in an procedure use the same operation type. We create four single-operation variants: addition-only, subtraction-only, multiplication-only, and division-only. This design lets us compare homogeneous operation sequences with heterogeneous sequences that require switching between different arithmetic operations. For mixed-operation procedure at each step, arithmetic operators are sampled uniformly from the selected operation set. For each input setting we have five operation variants, four from single and one from mixed.

Procedure Horizons.

We vary the number of steps from 5 to 95 in increments of 10 in order to study progressively longer procedural horizons while keeping generation cost and trace analysis computationally manageable. For each execution horizon, we generate 100 examples. Thus, each dataset contains 1,000 examples.

Look-back dependencies.

In addition to varying the number of steps, we introduce look-back dependencies to increase the amount of intermediate state the model must track. In the simplest setting, each new variable depends on the immediately preceding intermediate output. In more complex settings, the current step may depend on outputs produced two or three steps earlier.
For a look-back setting
k
, let

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{(k)}=\{S_{j}\mid\max(1,t-k)\leq j<t\}

denote the set of accessible intermediate variables at step
t
. Two distinct operands,

S_{i},S_{j}\sim\mathcal{H}_{t}^{(k)},

are sampled uniformly without replacement, and the next state is computed as

S_{t}=S_{i}\;o_{t}\;S_{j},

where
o_{t}\in\{+,-,\times,\div\}
.
We evaluate look-back settings up to
k=7
in order to study progressively longer dependency chains while keeping the benchmark computationally tractable and the generated procedures interpretable.

These settings introduce increasingly non-local dependencies while keeping the ground-truth computation deterministic.

Across the selected combinations of input range, data type, operation variant, and look-back setting, the benchmark contains 55 datasets and 55,000 examples

Reference outputs.

For each sample, we compute the reference output using an external deterministic executor. The executor parses the procedure, execute each step in order, and returns a list containing intermediate values of each step and the final output. We intentionally retain edge cases arising during execution, including division-by-zero operations, near-zero numerical instabilities, infinities, and NaN values, in order to evaluate whether models correctly propagate or report such behaviors. The implementation of the executor is provided in Appendix
A
.

4.3
Inference Procedure

For each model and benchmark variant, we perform inference on all examples using a fixed prompt format. The prompt contains the system instruction, the full step-wise procedure, and the two input values. The model is instructed to execute the procedure exactly and return the final numeric answer enclosed within
<answer>
and
</answer>
tags. The full prompt template is provided in Appendix
B
.

After generation, we extract all answer spans matching the required tag format. If the model produces multiple tagged answers, we record both the first extracted answer and the set of all extracted answers. If the output contains no valid tagged answer, the prediction is marked as
null
. All extracted answers are compared against the deterministic reference output after rounding to three decimal places.

All experiments were conducted on NVIDIA H200 140GB GPUs using vLLM
(
Kwon et al. 2023
)
for efficient batched inference. All models were evaluated using the same answer extraction and scoring pipeline. Each configuration was evaluated using a single inference run. The complete evaluation required approximately 300 GPU hours.

Decoding configuration.

We use
temperature = 0
,
top-p = 1.0
, and a maximum generation length of 32,768 tokens. Generation is terminated upon emitting the model’s end-of-sequence (EOS) token or reaching the maximum token limit. For cases where the model reaches the maximum generation length without producing a correct answer, we rerun inference with the model’s maximum supported context length.

4.4
Evaluation Metrics

We, evaluate five complementary metrics: First-Answer Accuracy (FAA), Correct@Any (CAA), Parseable Answer Rate (PAR), Answer Position (AP), and Step-Following Behavior (SFB).

Let
Y_{n}
denote the reference output for example
n
, and let
\hat{Y}_{n}^{(i)}
denote the
i
-th answer produced by the model during iterative re-evaluation within a single generation. We define
r(x)=\mathrm{round}(x,3)
, and let
\mathbf{1}[\cdot]
denote the indicator function, which evaluates to 1 when the condition is true and 0 otherwise.

First-answer accuracy (FAA).

FAA measures whether the first extracted answer is correct:

\mathrm{FAA}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\left[r(\hat{Y}_{n}^{(1)})=r(Y_{n})\right]

Correct@Any (CAA).

CAA marks an example as correct if any extracted answer matches the reference output:

\mathrm{CAA}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\left[\exists i\;r(\hat{Y}_{n}^{(i)},3)=r(Y_{n},3)\right].

Parseable Answer Rate (PAR).

PAR measures the percentage of examples containing at least one parseable
<answer>
span extracted using regular-expression-based parsing.:

\mathrm{PAR}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\left[|\mathcal{A}_{n}|>0\right],

where
\mathcal{A}_{n}
denotes the set of extracted answer spans for example
n
.

Answer Position (AP).

AP measures the normalized position of the first parseable
<answer>
span in the generated output. Let
p_{n}
denote the token index of the first parseable answer span and
L_{n}
denote the total output length. We compute:

\mathrm{AP}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{p_{n}}{L_{n}}\times 100.

Step-Following Behavior (SFB).

For outputs containing visible reasoning traces, we extract generated step counts by parsing occurrences of patterns of the form
Step k
. Let
G_{n}
denote the number of parsed generated steps and
T_{n}
denote the expected number of steps. We classify outputs as under-execution when
G_{n}<T_{n}
, exact execution when
G_{n}=T_{n}
, and over-execution when
G_{n}>T_{n}
. We report the percentage of examples in each category.

5.1
Performance Degradation with Procedural Complexity

5.1.1
Effect of Execution Horizon

We first analyze how FAA changes as the procedure length increases from 5 to 95. Across models and experimental settings, we observe a consistent decline in FAA as the number of steps increases (see Figure
2
). Averaged across models, the difference between 5-step and 95-step procedures is approximately 43%. NM-30, DS-7, QW-4, KK-1T, OA-120, and DS-14 exhibit the largest degradation, each dropping by more than 55 percentage points. In contrast, MI-7 and OL-7 show minimal decline; however, both models also maintain very low FAA across all procedure lengths, leaving limited room for further degradation. These results indicate that even though each individual operation is elementary, models struggle to sustain correct execution over longer procedures.

Figure
14
in Appendix
D
further compares FAA and CAA across models. We observe a mean difference of
3.35\times 10^{-4}
between the two metrics across models and procedure lengths, indicating that models rarely self-correct after producing an incorrect first answer. In most cases, incorrect initial predictions remain incorrect throughout the generation.

5.3.1
Parseable Answer Rate

Table

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we evaluated reasoning models on a controlled benchmark for multi-step procedural execution with varying procedure lengths and look-back dependencies. We find that performance degrades consistently as procedural complexity increases, indicating difficulties in maintaining reliable long-horizon execution and intermediate-state tracking. Future work includes extending the benchmark to branching procedures, symbolic transformations, natural-language workflows, and tool-augmented agents to better understand faithful multi-step instruction execution beyond arithmetic settings.

7
Limitations

Our study is limited to synthetic arithmetic tasks and may not fully generalize to broader reasoning domains. The benchmark does not include branching logic, semantic reasoning, external tool use, or interactive decision-making, and we do not evaluate commercial frontier models. While we observe a strong relationship between procedural complexity and performance degradation, extending the analysis to more diverse structured reasoning tasks remains an important direction for future work.

8
Ethics considerations

Our work evaluates language models on a fully synthetic benchmark for procedural execution using automatically generated arithmetic tasks. Since the dataset is programmatically constructed, it does not contain personally identifiable information (PII), human annotations, or user generated content. All evaluated models and resources used in this study are publicly available and used in accordance with their respective licenses. While the benchmark is designed to isolate procedural reasoning and intermediate state tracking in a controlled setting, it is limited to arithmetic execution and may not fully reflect broader real world reasoning tasks. We hope this work contributes toward more reliable and transparent evaluation of long horizon instruction following behavior in language models.
