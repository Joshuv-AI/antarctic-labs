STL‐DeepBDC: A Robust Few‐Shot Learning Framework for Multiclass Ovarian Tumor Classification in Ultrasound Outperforms Conventional Transfer Learning
Authors: Ning Zhao, Chuanxi Guan, Liwei Zhang, Qian Lv, Fei Teng, Xiaoqiu Dong, Siming Wang, Yingtao Zhang, Kuo Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAccurate preoperative pathological classification of malignant and borderline ovarian tumors (OMTs) can support surgical planning, fertility preservation, and prognosis, but ultrasound‐based subtype assessment remains difficult because imaging phenotypes are heterogeneous, class imbalance is common, and interpretation varies among operators. This study developed and evaluated an exploratory deep learning framework for multiclass ovarian tumor classification under small‐sample conditions.MethodsThis single‐center retrospective study used ultrasound images from patients with surgically and pathologically confirmed OMTs treated between August 2017 and October 2022. Six pathological types were included. After quality control, preprocessing, and augmentation, the dataset was split at the patient level into training, validation, and test sets at a 5:3:2 ratio. An optimized ConvNeXt model served as the baseline. STL‐DeepBDC combined stratified transfer learning with a Deep Bilinear Discriminative Covariance module. Performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, macro‐F1 score, AUC, AUPRC, and Grad‐CAM.ResultsSTL‐DeepBDC outperformed the optimized ConvNeXt baseline. Accuracy increased from 0.51 to 0.67, macro‐average sensitivity from 0.53 to 0.68, specificity from 0.87 to 0.92, and macro‐F1 score from 0.52 to 0.66. Macro‐average AUC improved from 0.75 to 0.87, and macro‐average AUPRC from 0.49 to 0.63. Category‐level AUCs ranged from 0.76 to 0.94 and AUPRCs from 0.51 to 0.78, suggesting more stable discrimination across six pathological types under class imbalance. Ablation analysis supported the contributions of STL and DeepBDC. Grad‐CAM highlighted clinically relevant regions, including thickened cyst walls, papillary projections, septations, and solid components.ConclusionSTL‐DeepBDC provides an exploratory, interpretable framework for ultrasound‐based multiclass ovarian tumor classification in a limited‐sample setting. Larger multicenter studies with external validation are required before clinical implementation.Keywords: deep learning, grad‐CAM, malignant and borderline ovarian tumor, multiclass classification, small‐sample learning, ultrasound imaging

Introduction

Ovarian tumors are pathologically heterogeneous, and their ultrasound phenotypes are closely associated with histological subtype. Accurate preoperative subtype classification can inform the extent of surgery, staging strategy, fertility preservation, and long‐term prognosis [1, 2]. Ultrasound remains a first‐line modality for ovarian and adnexal tumor evaluation because it is radiation‐free, widely available, dynamic, and capable of high‐resolution soft‐tissue assessment [3]. Conventional ultrasound assessment relies mainly on expert interpretation, the International Ovarian Tumor Analysis (IOTA) rules, and the Ovarian‐Adnexal Reporting and Data System (O‐RADS). However, diagnostic overlap and observer dependence persist in complex lesions, borderline ovarian tumors (BOTs), and different malignant subtypes [4, 5]. Radiomics can provide quantitative texture features, but it usually requires manual region‐of‐interest (ROI) delineation and handcrafted feature engineering, and its generalizability across devices and centers remains limited. More automated, robust, and pathology‐oriented methods are therefore needed for gynecologic ultrasound analysis.

Deep learning (DL), particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and vision Transformers (ViTs), has advanced rapidly in medical imaging and has achieved promising results in breast ultrasound, mammography, brain tumor MRI classification, and colorectal cancer pathology image analysis [6, 7, 8]. Previous studies have shown that CNN architectures such as InceptionV3, DenseNet169, and ResNet50 can perform well in breast ultrasound classification [9]. Lightweight networks, including EfficientNet, EfficientNetV2, and RexNet‐200, may further improve computational efficiency while maintaining competitive classification performance [10, 11]. Transformer‐CNN hybrid architectures have also strengthened global context modeling, multi‐scale feature fusion, and complex boundary recognition [12, 13]. Nevertheless, high performance on standardized datasets often depends on large annotated cohorts. Stable learning and domain transfer under small‐sample conditions remain major challenges in medical imaging AI [7, 8].

In ovarian tumor ultrasound AI research, most existing studies have addressed binary benign‐versus‐malignant classification or three‐class benign‐borderline‐malignant tasks, whereas fine‐grained classification of multiple malignant pathological subtypes remains less explored [4]. BOTs and malignant subtypes can share overlapping ultrasound appearances, which increases the risk of misclassification by both subjective interpretation and conventional models [5]. Real‐world ultrasound images also vary in device settings, acquisition quality, noise level, and residual annotations, further complicating model training and external generalization [14]. To mitigate small sample size and class imbalance, recent studies have explored data augmentation, transfer learning, self‐distillation, and covariance feature modeling to improve robustness and generalizability [12]. In parallel, multicenter external validation and human‐AI comparison have become increasingly important for improving clinical credibility and transferability [15, 16].

Accurate discrimination among ovarian pathological types has practical clinical implications for surgical extent, lymph node management, staging strategy, fertility‐sparing surgery (FSS), and long‐term follow‐up [17, 18]. For BOTs, several studies have reported acceptable oncological safety of FSS under appropriate indications, although recurrence risk is related to surgical procedure and pathological type [19, 20, 21]. Therefore, improved preoperative pathological subtype prediction based on routine ultrasound may provide auxiliary evidence for individualized surgical planning, fertility preservation, and postoperative management. However, many AI studies still emphasize overall accuracy, whereas stability under small‐sample conditions, model interpretability, and real‐world clinical usability remain insufficiently evaluated [16].

Against this background, this study focused on multiclass ultrasound classification of multiple ovarian tumor pathological subtypes and developed a DL framework for small‐sample and class‐imbalanced scenarios. The proposed STL‐DeepBDC model integrates stratified transfer learning (STL) with a Deep Bilinear Discriminative Covariance (DeepBDC) module. By combining stratified feature transfer with higher‐order covariance feature modeling, the framework was designed to improve feature stability and multiclass discrimination when training data are limited. The study was positioned as an exploratory methodological investigation in a real‐world small‐sample ultrasound setting, rather than as a finalized clinical diagnostic system or a comprehensive benchmark against all state‐of‐the‐art models.

The main contributions of this study were as follows: (1) a six‐class ultrasound DL framework was constructed for ovarian tumor classification under small‐sample and class‐imbalanced conditions; (2) STL was combined with DeepBDC‐based higher‐order covariance feature modeling to improve fine‐grained discrimination with limited samples; and (3) ablation experiments and multidimensional statistical analyses were performed to evaluate the independent contributions of the proposed modules.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This single‐center retrospective study included patients with surgically and pathologically confirmed malignant and borderline ovarian tumors (OMTs) who underwent treatment at the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University between August 2017 and October 2022.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a definitive postoperative pathological diagnosis; (2) preoperative transvaginal or transabdominal ultrasound images that met the analytical quality requirements; and (3) images that clearly displayed lesion morphology, margins, and internal echogenicity. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) absent or incomplete postoperative pathological reports; (2) ultrasound images with conspicuous measurement scales, text, or image labels; (3) poor image quality, including severe noise, artifacts, or blurred lesion boundaries; and (4) inconsistency between the pathological site and ultrasound annotation. After screening, 83 patients and 425 ultrasound images that met the quality criteria were retained. The patient selection workflow and final image composition are shown in Figure 1.

Patient selection workflow and final dataset composition. A total of 138 patients (587 ultrasound images) were initially screened. Based on predefined exclusion criteria, including lack of pathological confirmation, missing or poor‐quality ultrasound images, incomplete lesion visualization, artifact interference, and discrepancies between pathological findings and ultrasound annotations, 83 patients and 425 ultrasound images were ultimately included in the final analysis.

Pathological classification was based on postoperative paraffin‐embedded sections and included six categories: serous cystadenocarcinoma, BOT, granulosa cell tumor, mucinous cystadenocarcinoma, metastatic carcinoma, and endometrioid carcinoma. All cases were independently reviewed by two senior pathologists with associate chief physician qualifications or higher. Disagreements were resolved by a third expert, who made the final diagnosis.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (No.: 2023‐SCILLSC‐14). Because this was a retrospective, non‐interventional study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

To support analysis of model generalizability across clinical characteristics, patient age, maximum tumor diameter, and surgical approach were recorded during data curation. Subgroup analyses were performed by age (< 40 years vs. ≥ 40 years) and tumor diameter (< 8 cm vs. ≥ 8 cm) to evaluate the stability of model performance across different clinical strata.

Instrumentation and Equipment

Original ultrasound images were acquired using six mainstream diagnostic ultrasound systems to reflect device diversity and improve the applicability of the findings. The devices included the Voluson E6, Voluson E8, and LOGIQ E9 systems from GE Healthcare; the WS80A from Samsung Healthcare; the Rap2102LM from SuperSonic Imagine; and the MyLab Twice from Esaote. All devices were color Doppler ultrasound systems equipped with two‐dimensional B‐mode grayscale imaging and power Doppler functionality.

The frequency ranges of the transvaginal probes were 5.0–9.0 MHz for the Voluson E6/E8/LOGIQ E9, 3.0–12.0 MHz for the WS80A, and 3.0–9.0 MHz for the Rap2102LM and MyLab Twice. To maintain imaging consistency, key parameters were preset according to standardized gynecological protocols: gain was maintained at 55–65 dB, dynamic range at 65–75 dB, frame rate at ≥ 30 frames/s, and the focal point at the deepest or central portion of the lesion. High‐frequency transvaginal scanning was used whenever feasible, with transabdominal scanning added when necessary to capture the complete lesion.

All images were acquired by physicians with at least 5 years of experience in gynecological ultrasound. Each device underwent daily self‐checks and grayscale uniformity testing. Dedicated engineers performed weekly beam‐focusing and probe‐linearity calibration to maintain comparability of imaging parameters across devices. Images were exported in JPEG format at a resolution of at least 1024 × 768 pixels and archived using a unified numbering system.

Ultrasound Examination Procedures and Image Quality Control

All ultrasound examinations were independently performed by three ultrasound physicians, each with at least 5 years of experience in gynecological imaging. The examination workflow followed the IOTA Simple Rules and referred to the imaging acquisition and grading recommendations of the ACR O‐RADS system. Before examination, patients were instructed to empty the bladder and assume the lithotomy position. Standard planes were obtained using a high‐frequency transvaginal probe (5.0–9.0 MHz or 3.0–12.0 MHz), including longitudinal, transverse, and maximum‐diameter views of the lesion. For large or high‐positioned lesions, transabdominal scanning was performed as a supplement. For each lesion, at least three to five grayscale still images from different angles and one color Doppler video clip of 5–10 s were acquired.

To maintain imaging consistency, the following parameters were standardized: overall gain, 60 ± 5 dB; dynamic range, 70 ± 5 dB; time gain compensation, linear adjustment; focal zone, middle or lower margin of the lesion; and depth, adjusted so that the lesion margin was approximately 0.5 cm from the lower edge of the image. After image acquisition, the primary examiner immediately reviewed image quality. Images with artifacts, overexposure, or insufficient signal‐to‐noise ratio were reacquired. All images were stored in JPEG format in the hospital ultrasound workstation at a resolution of at least 1024 × 768 pixels and were exported to an independent database for analysis.

For quality control, all original images were first manually screened to exclude images with conspicuous measurement scales, text annotations, or equipment parameter bars; incomplete lesion boundaries or resolution < 720 p; obvious motion artifacts or noise interference; and inconsistency between the pathological report and the imaged lesion site. Approximately 86.5% of the original images passed the initial screening. Two senior imaging physicians then independently performed blinded secondary quality control, and uncertain images were jointly reviewed. The agreement rate was 92.4%. Only high‐quality images with quality scores ≥ 4 on a five‐point scale and complete lesion visualization were included for model construction. ROI selection and cropping were performed under unified criteria by experienced gynecologic ultrasound physicians and were reviewed by another senior imaging physician to minimize bias. Because this study focused on feasibility verification of pathological classification under small‐sample conditions, a semi‐automated ROI‐based analytical strategy was used instead of a fully end‐to‐end lesion detection‐classification framework. Residual instrument information, patient identifiers, and parameter bars at the lower image margin were removed using a uniform cropping strategy (upper and lower margins ≤ 5% of the original image height) to reduce the influence of non‐lesion information on feature extraction.

Preprocessing of Ultrasound Images

All raw ultrasound images underwent standardized preprocessing before model training. Image resizing was performed using OpenCV 4.8 and torchvision.transforms in a Python 3.9 environment. All images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels while preserving the original aspect ratio, and the remaining background was completed by center cropping. Each training image underwent one to two randomly selected augmentation operations per epoch, sampled uniformly from the augmentation pool. Images in the validation and test sets were not augmented and underwent only standardized preprocessing to preserve their original distributions. After augmentation, the effective size of the training set increased to approximately five times the original size (n ≈ 2100 images), while class proportions were preserved to avoid distributional shift.

To avoid potential data leakage, dataset partitioning was strictly performed at the patient level rather than at the image level. All ultrasound images from the same patient were assigned exclusively to one subset (training, validation, or testing), ensuring that no patient appeared in more than one subset. All preprocessing and augmentation procedures were performed on a unified computational platform (Ubuntu 20.04, CUDA 11.8, and PyTorch 1.13), with script versions and random seed configurations documented for reproducibility.

Construction of ConvNeXt Model

A ConvNeXt model was implemented on the PyTorch 1.13.1 platform (Python 3.9, CUDA 11.8) to classify six pathological ovarian tumor categories: serous cystadenocarcinoma, BOT, granulosa cell tumor, mucinous cystadenocarcinoma, metastatic carcinoma, and endometrioid carcinoma. ConvNeXt‐Tiny was used as the backbone network. It consists of 27 layers and four stages with channel dimensions of 96, 192, 384, and 768, a patch size of 4 × 4, and a convolution kernel size of 7 × 7. The four stages contain 3, 3, 9, and 3 ConvNeXt blocks, respectively. Each block includes depthwise convolution, LayerNorm, pointwise linear projection, and a GELU activation function. The classification head contains a global average pooling layer, a dropout layer (p = 0.2), and a fully connected layer with six output dimensions corresponding to the six pathological categories.

The model was initialized with ImageNet‐22K pretrained weights and then fine‐tuned using a transfer learning strategy. Parameters in the first two layers (stem + Stage 1) were frozen for 10 epochs to preserve low‐level texture features. The network was then progressively unfrozen and jointly trained to adapt to ultrasound‐domain features. A class‐weighted cross‐entropy loss was used, with class weights calculated from the inverse frequency of each pathological type in the training set to reduce the influence of class imbalance. The optimizer was AdamW with momentum parameters (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999) and weight decay = 5 × 10−2. Learning rate, batch size, dropout rate, and weight decay were optimized by grid search based on validation‐set performance. The final initial learning rate was 5 × 10−4, and a cosine annealing scheduler was used for dynamic learning‐rate decay.

The batch size was 16, and the total number of training epochs was 50. Accuracy and loss were calculated on the validation set after each epoch. Model weights were saved every 5 epochs, and the checkpoint with the best validation performance was automatically recorded. To reduce overfitting, early stopping (patience = 10), L2 regularization, and Mixup augmentation (α = 0.2) were used. Class‐balanced sampling was also applied during training to reduce the dominance of majority classes in gradient updates.

Model training was performed on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB of memory. Each training epoch required approximately 58 s, and the total training time was approximately 48 min. To reduce the influence of random initialization and small‐sample variability, all experiments were repeated five times using different random seeds (2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, and 2025), and final performance was reported as the mean across runs. Model logs and weight files were retained for reproducibility. The final model weights were selected using a combined criterion of macro‐F1 score and macro‐AUC on the validation set and were then evaluated and visualized on the test set.

Construction of the Few‐Shot Learning Model

To improve generalization under small‐sample conditions, a few‐shot learning model was developed using a feature‐level STL framework combined with the Deep Bilinear Discriminative Covariance (DeepBDC) module. This design enables higher‐order statistical relationships and within‐class covariance structures to be modeled from limited samples, thereby improving feature discrimination and stability.

Training of the STL‐DeepBDC model consisted of two stages: pretraining and self‐distillation.

Pretraining stage: the model was initialized on the classical few‐shot learning benchmark dataset mini‐ImageNet. The dataset was organized in a 6‐way, 10‐shot setting, with 10 samples per class used to construct support and query sets. ResNet12 was used as the feature extraction backbone. Its output feature vectors were passed through global average pooling and then input into the DeepBDC covariance module. The DeepBDC module computes the within‐class covariance matrix C=1/N−1*X−X¯X−X¯T, and processes it through a feature transformation layer followed by a Log‐Euclidean mapping to obtain a second‐order statistical embedding. This stage was designed to learn stable feature distributions and provide priors for subsequent cross‐domain transfer.

Self‐distillation stage: the pretrained model was transferred to the ovarian tumor ultrasound classification task. ConvNeXt‐Tiny was used as the teacher model, and ResNet12‐DeepBDC was used as the student model. Parameter optimization was performed through self‐distillation. Specifically, soft labels predicted by the teacher model were used as pseudo‐supervision signals, and Kullback–Leibler divergence constrained output consistency between the teacher and student models. At the feature level, MSE loss was introduced to preserve stable transfer of intermediate representations. The self‐distillation process was iterated for three rounds, with early stopping determined by validation‐set performance in each round (patience = 5). Final model weights were obtained by weighted averaging of the teacher and student network parameters.

Within the STL framework, transfer learning was performed in a stratified manner. Lower convolutional blocks were first frozen to preserve general edge‐level and texture features, whereas higher network layers were fine‐tuned for different pathological types to improve robustness on the imbalanced dataset. This two‐stage strategy was designed to improve classification stability under very limited sample conditions. The main training parameters are listed in Table S1.

In this study, STL‐DeepBDC was used to examine the feasibility of stratified transfer learning and covariance feature modeling for small‐sample medical ultrasound classification. The optimized ConvNeXt baseline was selected as the principal comparator to evaluate the performance gain of STL‐DeepBDC under limited‐sample and class‐imbalanced conditions. Classical few‐shot and meta‐learning frameworks, such as Prototypical Networks, Matching Networks, and Relation Networks, were not included in a unified benchmark. Future studies should compare different few‐shot learning strategies for ovarian ultrasound image classification within a standardized experimental framework.

To evaluate the independent contributions of the STL strategy and the DeepBDC module, an ablation study was conducted. Under the same data split, training epochs, and optimization parameters, four model variants were compared: (1) a basic ResNet12 model; (2) ResNet12 + DeepBDC; (3) an STL‐only model; and (4) the complete STL‐DeepBDC model. All ablation experiments were repeated five times using the same random‐seed settings. Macro‐F1 score, macro‐AUC, and overall accuracy were used as the primary evaluation metrics. Performance changes across these module combinations were compared to clarify the specific contributions of STL and DeepBDC to small‐sample ovarian ultrasound classification.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the stats, pROC, epiR, and ggplot2 packages. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage (n, %). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene's or Bartlett's test. For variables satisfying both normality and homogeneity assumptions, between‐group comparisons were performed using two‐sided independent‐samples t‐tests. Variables that did not meet these assumptions were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum test or the Mann–Whitney U test. All tests were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model diagnostic performance was evaluated using postoperative pathological findings as the reference standard. True positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) were calculated to derive accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1 score. Confusion matrices were generated for each pathological type. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using a one‐vs‐rest strategy, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was calculated. Precision–recall (P–R) curves were constructed using the same strategy, and the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) was obtained.

For model comparison, DeLong tests were used to compare paired AUCs between ConvNeXt and STL‐DeepBDC at the overall level and for each pathological type. AUPRC differences were evaluated using a nonparametric bootstrap procedure with 1000 resamples to estimate the 95% CI of between‐model differences. A CI that did not cross zero was considered statistically significant. For paired accuracy comparisons based on predictions from the same subjects, McNemar's test was used as a complementary sensitivity analysis when appropriate. Unless otherwise specified, all p values were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Preprocessing procedures comprised 224 × 224 resampling, z‐score normalization, and data augmentation. Two modeling pipelines were developed: (1) an optimized ConvNeXt baseline trained with the AdamW optimizer, class‐weighted loss, class‐balanced sampling, and Mixup augmentation; and (2) the proposed STL‐DeepBDC framework, which integrates ResNet12 with DeepBDC covariance modeling and knowledge distillation. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, F1 score, AUC, AUPRC, confusion matrices, subgroup analyses, and ablation experiments. Interpretability was evaluated using Grad‐CAM heatmaps and lesion‐focused attention maps.

Results

General Characteristics of Patients With OMTs

This study included 83 patients with surgically and pathologically confirmed OMTs and 425 ultrasound images that met the quality criteria. The distribution of patients and images across the six pathological types was as follows: serous cystadenocarcinoma, 30 patients (186 images); BOT, 30 patients (146 images); granulosa cell tumor, 5 patients (18 images); mucinous cystadenocarcinoma, 5 patients (30 images); metastatic carcinoma, 8 patients (27 images); and endometrioid carcinoma, 5 patients (18 images). Image counts differed substantially among pathological types, indicating class imbalance that could influence subsequent model sensitivity and stability.

The overall mean patient age was 51.11 ± 12.77 years. Age did not differ significantly among pathological types (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.128). Patients with metastatic carcinoma were the oldest on average (55.25 ± 6.45 years), whereas patients with granulosa cell tumor were the youngest (42.80 ± 13.22 years). The mean maximum tumor diameter was 10.60 ± 4.91 cm. Differences in tumor diameter among groups approached statistical significance (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.053). Pairwise comparisons showed that tumor diameter was larger in the BOT group (12.02 ± 6.12 cm) than in the serous cystadenocarcinoma group (9.32 ± 3.42 cm; Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.04). No significant differences were observed among the other groups (all p > 0.05), as shown in Table S2.

Serous cystadenocarcinoma and BOT were the two most common pathological types, whereas granulosa cell tumor and endometrioid carcinoma were relatively rare. Because image counts varied substantially across pathological types, class imbalance was considered in model training and was specifically evaluated using confusion matrices and P–R curve analyses.

Diagnostic Performance of ConvNeXt and STL‐DeepBDC Models

In the overall performance comparison for six‐class ovarian tumor classification, the optimized ConvNeXt baseline achieved moderate performance, with an overall accuracy of 0.51, macro‐average sensitivity of 0.53 (95% CI: 0.47–0.59), specificity of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.83–0.91), and macro‐average F1 score of 0.52. STL‐DeepBDC showed higher performance, with an overall accuracy of 0.67, macro‐average sensitivity of 0.68 (95% CI: 0.62–0.74), specificity of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.88–0.95), and macro‐average F1 score of 0.66. The difference in overall accuracy was statistically significant by the paired McNemar test (p = 0.008), indicating improved classification stability under small‐sample and class‐imbalanced conditions. Detailed results are provided in Table S3.

The diagnostic performance of ConvNeXt varied across the six pathological categories. Category‐level AUC ranged from 0.61 to 0.84, AUPRC from 0.38 to 0.60, sensitivity from 0.35 to 0.74, specificity from 0.71 to 0.94, and F1 score from 0.32 to 0.71 (Table S4). For the two categories with larger sample sizes, serous cystadenocarcinoma (n = 30) and BOT (n = 30), ConvNeXt achieved AUCs of 0.72 and 0.68, respectively, suggesting limited‐to‐moderate discriminative ability. In minority classes such as granulosa cell tumor (n = 5) and endometrioid carcinoma (n = 5), some metrics appeared relatively high, but the corresponding 95% CIs were wide, reflecting statistical instability caused by limited sample size. Bootstrap estimation with 1000 resamples showed that the AUC 95% CIs for some pathological types approached 0.5, indicating limited generalization stability in this complex multiclass task. The P–R curves also showed that STL‐DeepBDC maintained relatively higher precision across recall ranges, indicating more stable predictive performance under class imbalance (Figure 5A,B). Overall, STL‐DeepBDC improved diagnostic performance in the six‐class ovarian tumor classification task and showed better robustness and generalization potential than the optimized ConvNeXt baseline (Figure 6A,B).

Interpretability Analysis of the Model

Grad‐CAM was used to visualize the feature extraction regions of STL‐DeepBDC across the six ovarian tumor categories (Figure 7). The model showed relatively stable and clinically plausible attention patterns. In serous cystadenocarcinoma and BOT, high‐attention regions were mainly located in focal cyst wall thickening, papillary projections, or hyperechoic areas, which correspond to ultrasound features commonly considered during lesion assessment. For granulosa cell tumors and endometrioid carcinomas, the main regions identified by the model included solid components, discontinuous interfaces, and focal hyperechoic points, suggesting sensitivity to imaging features of solid tumor components.

Statistical Significance Comparison of Performance Differences Between Models

To further assess the performance difference between STL‐DeepBDC and the optimized ConvNeXt baseline, multidimensional statistical tests were performed using overall and class‐specific metrics. STL‐DeepBDC achieved higher overall accuracy than ConvNeXt (0.67 vs. 0.51; McNemar test, p = 0.008), indicating that the combination of stratified transfer learning and covariance feature modeling improved classification performance under small‐sample and class‐imbalanced conditions (Figure 8A). The macro‐average AUC was also higher for STL‐DeepBDC than for ConvNeXt (0.87 vs. 0.75; DeLong test, p = 0.018; Figure 8B). Under imbalanced data conditions, macro‐average AUPRC further supported the advantage of STL‐DeepBDC for minority‐class recognition (0.63 vs. 0.49; bootstrap test, p = 0.021) (Figure 8C).

(B) Macro‐average AUC comparison, showing better performance of STL‐DeepBDC (DeLong p = 0.018). (C) Macro‐average AUPRC comparison, indicating an advantage of STL‐DeepBDC under imbalanced‐category recognition (bootstrap p = 0.021). (D) Sensitivity comparison across the six pathological types, with more evident improvements in serous cystadenocarcinoma and endometrioid carcinoma. (E) Specificity comparison, showing that STL‐DeepBDC maintained relatively high values in most categories. Overall, the results indicate that STL‐DeepBDC improved global performance, minority‐class recognition, and cross‐category stability compared with the optimized ConvNeXt architecture.

Across the six pathological types, STL‐DeepBDC showed varying degrees of sensitivity improvement in serous cystadenocarcinoma, BOT, mucinous cystadenocarcinoma, and endometrioid carcinoma, with the most evident increases in serous cystadenocarcinoma and endometrioid carcinoma (Figure 8D). In specificity comparisons, STL‐DeepBDC maintained relatively high values in most categories, especially serous cystadenocarcinoma, BOT, and endometrioid carcinoma (Figure 8E). Macro‐average metrics also supported stable performance differences between the two models: STL‐DeepBDC achieved higher macro‐average accuracy, F1 score, AUC, and AUPRC than the optimized ConvNeXt baseline (Figure 8F).

After re‐optimization of the ConvNeXt baseline, STL‐DeepBDC still maintained better overall classification performance and stronger performance in several pathological subtypes, indicating greater stability in small‐sample multiclass medical imaging tasks.

As an additional conventional transfer‐learning comparator, ResNet34 achieved an overall accuracy of 0.34, a macro‐F1 score of 0.37, a macro‐average AUC of 0.81, and a macro‐average AUPRC of 0.67 on the held‐out test set. ResNet34 contained 21.29 million trainable parameters and required 3.66 GFLOPs for a 224 × 224 input, whereas ConvNeXt‐Tiny contained 27.82 million trainable parameters and required 4.46 GFLOPs under the same input‐size setting. The ResNet34 rerun required 310.6 s for eight epochs, corresponding to 38.8 s per epoch, with a mean inference latency of 27.7 ms per image. These findings provide an additional descriptive comparison of classification performance and computational characteristics (Table S7).

Performance Differences Between ConvNeXt and STL‐DeepBDC Across Clinical Subgroups

To evaluate model stability across different patient characteristics, subgroup analyses were conducted according to age and tumor diameter. Among patients aged < 40 years, STL‐DeepBDC achieved higher accuracy than the optimized ConvNeXt baseline (0.69 vs. 0.54), with 95% CIs of 0.60–0.78 and 0.45–0.63, respectively. In patients aged ≥ 40 years, STL‐DeepBDC maintained an accuracy advantage (0.65 vs. 0.50), with corresponding 95% CIs of 0.58–0.72 and 0.42–0.58. In the tumor diameter stratification, STL‐DeepBDC achieved an accuracy of 0.68 (95% CI: 0.59–0.76) in the < 8 cm subgroup, exceeding ConvNeXt (0.52; 95% CI: 0.43–0.61). A similar trend was observed in the ≥ 8 cm subgroup (0.64 vs. 0.49; Figure 9A).

Ablation Analysis of the STL Strategy and DeepBDC Module

To evaluate the independent contributions of the STL strategy and the DeepBDC module, ablation analyses were performed. Under the same data split, training epochs, and optimization parameters, four models were compared: basic ResNet12, ResNet12 + DeepBDC, STL‐only, and complete STL‐DeepBDC. The results are summarized in Table S6.

The basic ResNet12 model showed limited performance in the six‐class task, with an overall accuracy of 0.55, macro‐AUC of 0.78, and macro‐F1 score of 0.56. After introduction of the DeepBDC module, overall performance improved, with accuracy increasing to 0.61 and macro‐AUC to 0.83, suggesting that covariance feature modeling enhanced discrimination among fine‐grained pathological types. The STL‐only model also improved performance, with an overall accuracy of 0.63 and macro‐AUC of 0.85, indicating that stratified transfer learning improved the stability and generalizability of feature transfer under small‐sample conditions.

The complete STL‐DeepBDC model, which incorporated both the STL strategy and the DeepBDC module, achieved the best performance, with an overall accuracy of 0.67, macro‐AUC of 0.87, and macro‐F1 score of 0.66.

These results indicate that both STL and DeepBDC independently contributed to performance improvement, and their combined use further enhanced classification stability and discriminative capacity in small‐sample ovarian ultrasound multiclass classification.

Discussion

This study developed and evaluated STL‐DeepBDC, an ultrasound‐based deep learning framework for multiclass classification of ovarian malignant and borderline tumor subtypes under small‐sample and class‐imbalanced conditions. Compared with the optimized ConvNeXt baseline, STL‐DeepBDC achieved higher overall accuracy, macro‐average sensitivity, specificity, macro‐F1 score, macro‐average AUC, and AUPRC. It also showed more stable category‐level performance and lesion‐focused Grad‐CAM attention patterns. These findings suggest that stratified transfer learning combined with DeepBDC covariance feature modeling may improve feature stability and fine‐grained subtype discrimination in limited‐sample gynecologic ultrasound datasets.

Recent evidence supports the increasing use of artificial intelligence in ovarian and adnexal tumor ultrasound assessment. Systematic reviews have shown promising diagnostic performance for AI‐assisted ultrasound models, but external validation, methodological consistency, and clinical applicability remain insufficient [22, 23]. Most current studies focus on benign‐versus‐malignant discrimination or benign‐borderline‐malignant three‐class classification. A recent ultrasound‐based deep learning radiomics study differentiated benign, borderline, and malignant ovarian tumors [4]. Another study showed that a deep learning model could assist malignancy prediction in O‐RADS US category 4 ovarian lesions [24]. These studies support the feasibility of ultrasound‐based AI for ovarian tumor stratification. The present study extends this direction to six pathological categories, including serous cystadenocarcinoma, borderline ovarian tumor, granulosa cell tumor, mucinous cystadenocarcinoma, metastatic carcinoma, and endometrioid carcinoma. This task is more difficult because several subtypes share overlapping ultrasound features and uneven sample distributions. It is also clinically relevant because pathological subtype may affect surgical extent, fertility‐sparing decisions, referral strategy, and follow‐up planning.

Recent multicenter studies further highlight the need for external validation and data diversity. An international multicenter validation study using more than 17,000 ultrasound images from more than 3600 patients showed that AI‐driven ultrasound models can detect ovarian cancer across different centers, ultrasound systems, histological diagnoses, and patient age groups [16]. A separate multicenter study showed that ultrasound‐based deep learning can assist clinicians in ovarian tumor diagnosis [25]. These findings support the clinical potential of ultrasound AI, while also emphasizing that model reliability depends on dataset scale, center diversity, and clinician‐supervised evaluation. Compared with these large‐scale studies, the present study used a smaller single‐center dataset. Its contribution is not to provide a ready‐to‐use diagnostic system, but to explore a small‐sample learning strategy for fine‐grained ovarian tumor subtype classification.

The improved performance of STL‐DeepBDC may be explained by three methodological features. Stratified transfer learning preserved low‐level ultrasound texture features and adapted high‐level representations to pathological subtype differences. This staged feature transfer may reduce overfitting when training data are limited. DeepBDC introduced second‐order covariance feature modeling, which can capture within‐class feature correlations and subtle interclass differences. This is relevant for ovarian tumor ultrasound images, where cyst wall thickening, papillary projections, septations, solid components, and mixed echogenic areas may differ only subtly across subtypes. Class‐weighted loss, class‐balanced sampling, data augmentation, and repeated training reduced the influence of class imbalance. The ablation analysis supported these findings. Both STL and DeepBDC improved model performance, and their combination achieved the best results.

Recent work also supports the value of transfer learning, radiomics, and multimodal modeling in ovarian ultrasound AI. An ultrasound image‐based nomogram combining clinical, radiomics, and deep transfer learning features has been used for O‐RADS‐based ovarian mass classification [26]. Another nomogram combining clinical features, O‐RADS US, and radiomics showed value for ovarian cancer diagnosis [27]. Multimodal deep learning based on ultrasound images and clinical information also improved ovarian cancer diagnosis [28]. A multimodal ultrasound model integrating clinical variables, radiomics, and deep learning features improved differential diagnosis of O‐RADS 4–5 adnexal masses [29]. These studies indicate that image‐only models may be strengthened by adding clinical variables, O‐RADS descriptors, radiomics, Doppler information, contrast‐enhanced ultrasound, or laboratory markers. The current STL‐DeepBDC model was designed as an image‐based small‐sample framework. Future models may combine multimodal inputs with the feature‐stability advantages of STL and DeepBDC.

Grad‐CAM interpretability supported the plausibility of STL‐DeepBDC. The model mainly attended to clinically meaningful lesion regions, including cyst wall thickening, papillary projections, septal structures, solid components, irregular interfaces, and mixed echogenic areas. These regions are consistent with features commonly assessed in gynecologic ultrasound. This lesion‐focused attention pattern reduces the concern that the classifier mainly relied on background artifacts, cyst fluid, or irrelevant image information. Grad‐CAM remains qualitative. Future studies should include expert pixel‐level annotations or quantitative attention‐consistency metrics to test whether highlighted regions match clinically validated diagnostic areas.

The clinical value of this study is exploratory. STL‐DeepBDC may provide Supporting Information for preoperative ovarian tumor subtype assessment, especially when rare subtypes and limited datasets make conventional model training difficult. More accurate identification of borderline ovarian tumors may support fertility‐sparing surgical planning. Recognition of metastatic carcinoma or highly suspicious malignant subtypes may influence surgical strategy and referral decisions. The current model should not be considered an independent diagnostic tool. Its performance is not sufficient for direct clinical decision‐making. Its current role is better defined as a methodological framework or auxiliary analytical tool. This positioning is consistent with recent evidence showing that ultrasound AI requires external validation, transparent reporting, and clinician‐supervised workflows before clinical use [16, 23, 25].

From an implementation perspective, the computational cost of such an image‐based framework should be interpreted as model development and inference cost rather than patient‐level monetary cost. Because the input is routine two‐dimensional ultrasound, no additional CT, MRI, contrast agent, or laboratory test is required for image acquisition. Nevertheless, deployment would require software integration, quality control, hardware maintenance, data security management, and clinician‐supervised review, which were not monetized in this retrospective study.

The dataset was acquired using six ultrasound systems, which introduced a degree of device heterogeneity. However, all data came from one institution, and single‐center multi‐device data cannot substitute for external cross‐center or formal leave‐one‐device‐out validation. Future work should test cross‐device transfer, operator variability, and protocol differences in larger multicenter cohorts.

The modular design of STL‐DeepBDC may allow extension to additional pathological categories or more complex clinical workflows. Potential extensions include automatic lesion detection or segmentation, ultrasound video, Doppler features, structured O‐RADS descriptors, serum biomarkers, and clinical variables. A practical workflow could connect image export from the ultrasound workstation or picture archiving and communication system with standardized preprocessing, model inference, probability output, and Grad‐CAM visualization for clinician review.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single‐center retrospective study with a limited sample size. Some pathological categories, including granulosa cell tumor, mucinous cystadenocarcinoma, and endometrioid carcinoma, contained very few cases, resulting in wide confidence intervals and limited statistical power. Patient‐level splitting, repeated training, bootstrap estimation, AUPRC evaluation, and ablation analysis improved methodological reliability, but they cannot replace external validation. Larger multicenter cohorts with more balanced class distributions are needed to confirm the robustness and generalizability of STL‐DeepBDC. Second, the model was based on manually selected ultrasound images and did not include automatic lesion detection or segmentation. Manual ROI selection may reduce background noise, but it can introduce operator‐dependent bias. Recent studies on automatic segmentation of ovarian or adnexal masses suggest that end‐to‐end segmentation–classification workflows may reduce manual intervention and improve reproducibility [30, 31, 32]. Third, classical few‐shot and meta‐learning baselines, such as Prototypical Networks, Matching Networks, and Relation Networks, were not systematically compared under the same experimental framework. Future studies should compare these models using standardized data splits and external test cohorts. Finally, the current model used static images and did not incorporate ultrasound videos, Doppler features, contrast‐enhanced ultrasound, clinical biomarkers, or structured O‐RADS descriptors. These data may improve clinically relevant subtype prediction.

In addition, true long‐term operation stability has not been evaluated because the framework has not yet been prospectively deployed in a real clinical environment. The present reliability evidence is therefore limited to retrospective repeated training, bootstrap estimation, subgroup analysis, multi‐device image acquisition, and representative Grad‐CAM case visualization. Prospective deployment studies should monitor inference latency, failure rate, memory use, prediction drift, clinician‐AI interaction, and quality‐control alarms over time.

In summary, STL‐DeepBDC improved multiclass ovarian tumor subtype classification compared with the optimized ConvNeXt baseline and showed interpretable lesion‐focused attention patterns. This study supports the potential value of stratified transfer learning and covariance feature modeling for small‐sample gynecologic ultrasound AI. The findings remain exploratory. Future multicenter validation, multimodal fusion, automated lesion segmentation, and comparison with additional few‐shot learning frameworks are needed before clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study proposed and evaluated STL‐DeepBDC, a multiclass DL model for ultrasound‐based ovarian tumor classification. By integrating stratified transfer learning with DeepBDC covariance feature modeling, the model improved classification performance under small‐sample and class‐imbalanced conditions and outperformed the optimized ConvNeXt baseline across six common pathological types. The model performed particularly well in categories such as serous cystadenocarcinoma, BOT, and mucinous cystadenocarcinoma, suggesting sensitivity to diagnostically relevant ultrasound structures and discriminative imaging patterns. Grad‐CAM analysis further showed that the model attended to lesion regions consistent with clinical diagnostic features, supporting its transparency and interpretability.

As an exploratory small‐sample DL framework based on routine ultrasound images, STL‐DeepBDC showed potential for pathological subtype recognition in multiclass ovarian tumor classification and demonstrated relatively favorable stability and interpretability under class imbalance. These findings suggest that combining stratified transfer learning with covariance feature modeling may be useful for medical imaging tasks with limited samples. However, because of the limited sample size and the small number of cases in several pathological subtypes, the current model is not sufficient for direct use as an independent clinical diagnostic tool. Its statistical reliability and clinical generalizability should be confirmed in larger, multicenter, and externally validated datasets.
