1. Executive Summary

All the 1ω campaign’s performance objectives were achieved and all the ignition-quality, EYC laser
performance requirements were met (see tables in appendix) for the entire bundle 32, which includes both
Q32T and Q32B. The demonstration also included significant margins and robustness against potential aging
and degradation of the ML small signal gain.

The EYC project aims to enable NIF operations at 2.6 MJ in the UV and maximize the yield available for
stockpile stewardship applications. Since the EYC operating point is significantly higher than the initial NIF
requirement of 1.8 MJ –and 2.05-2.2 MJ current operations– as shown in Figure 1, a risk reduction
campaign is a worthwhile endeavor.
The high-level objective for EYC Performance Bundle (IR)/Quad (UV) Campaign was to perform a
quantitative assessment of the performance, risk and cost of NIF operation at 2.6 MJ 450 TW. To meet this
goal, four nominal beamlines from a single quad were configured as close as possible to the EYC optical
baseline configuration as described in [1]. The pulse shape chosen for the campaign was defined by A.
Kritcher (along with suitable energy/power scaling relationships) to be relevant for EYC target physics
experiments. While a Quad campaign at 2.6 MJ, 480 TW (higher operating power compared to current EYC
design point) was already conducted in 2017 [see NIF-1003081972-AA], the campaign this year will focus
on deploying more amplifier slabs in the main laser (ML) –adding margins for small signal gain
degradation– as well as applying some recent learnings to mitigate focusing lens thick edge filamentary
damage [see MRT 8845 Filamentation milestone memo].

Consequently, some specific objectives of the present campaign are:
− Demonstrate processes are in place to install and commission the laser at EYC-level operations, esp. in
 the IR,
− Deploy and validate the 11-7 1 amplifier ML configuration,
− At the EYC operating point, measure the 1ω/3ω laser performance, and compare with model
 expectations and EYC requirements,
− Evaluate optics damage rate and extrapolate to the Optic Recycle Loop Model.

This report describes the successful completion of the ML re-configuration, the detailed 1ω performance
results and readiness for the next step. This final phase of the overall campaign – conducted on Q32B–
assessed the 3ω laser performance and optics resilience results is documented in two companion reports
[2, 3]. As a side note, a follow-up campaign will conduct a series of 3ω performance and optics resilience
tests on Q32T in FY27.

2. Bundle 32 Facility Re-configuration

The EYC’s 11-7 re-configuration populated bundle 32 PA1 and PA2 slots of the main laser (ML) cavity with
laser slabs (a total of 16 slabs were added for the bundle, 2 more slabs for each of the 8 beamlines). A spare
bank module (BM) was also installed –it was already used in 2003 for 11-7 demonstration experiments on
Bu31– in the so-called “position 9” to generate population inversion in the PA1-PA2 Nd:glass slabs. Spare
flashlamps, which are required to pump these new laser slabs, were first installed and commissioned.
Figure 2 shows the BM/flashlamp output from the new install and from two existing ones on NIF. As shown
by the excellent pulse overlap in Figure 2, the new install has the same 460 µs output pulse width
(measured at 10% of the peak amplitude) as the existing NIF units and consistent with the requirements.
The level of the output currents and the waveform accuracy from the spare module/flashlamps were found
to be similar to the modules/flashlamps currently used for NIF routine operations.

3. 1ω Main Laser (ML) Performance

3.1 Verification of parasitics-free operations

Because EYC’s 11-7 configuration provides significantly more laser gain than the current 11-5
configuration, any parasitic effects such as scattering or unintended residual back-reflection can
potentially cause laser instability, oscillations that could deplete the gain in an unattended way and
potentially create damage. Therefore, “parasitics” laser shots were first performed to ensure the lack of
such instability. Such laser shots utilize minimal input for the ML to examine amplified spontaneous
emission (ASE) behavior. Tests were performed at 16%, 18% and 19% explosion fractions. Figure 5 shows
relative backward ASE measurements at the 19% flashlamp explosion fraction. For both laser quads, the
ASE follows a quadratic fit and no output clamping is observed nor any irregularities in the curves. This
behavior indicates that ML is free of parasitic lasing and laser system shots can proceed and will deliver
the expected gain. The remaining laser shots for the first phase of the campaign were taken at 18%
flashlamp explosion fraction.

3.3 Near field shaping performance

Achieving and maintaining good spatial beam quality was an important part of this campaign. Each
preamplifier module in the NIF front-end is equipped, at a quad-level, with a Programmable Spatial Shaper
(PSS) system containing an optically addressed liquid-crystal light-valve. The PSS is used to sculpt the
spatial fluence profile injected into the four beams in each quad. One route is to flatten the output beam
profile by pre-compensating for the laser gain spatial nonuniformity in the flash-lamp-pumped main
amplifiers (referred to as PSS beam flattening). The process and results were described in a 2019 Nuclear
Fusion article [6]. In Figure 7, B454 is used to illustrate the performance improvements obtained, resulting
in a drastic reduction of the hot tail of the fluence histogram, hence minimizing damage initiation in the UV
optics [7]. The optimum transmission map for the PSS is derived from the near-field fluence profiles
measured at the output of the ML on low-energy (unsaturated) shots. When this optimum transmission
map (a compromise over the four beams in the quad) is applied, the output beam quality (fluence contrast)
is improved by ~2x at low energy (Figure 7, top row). The same optimum transmission map also improves
beam quality at high energy. Figure 7 (bottom row) compares the B454 near-field fluence profile measured
during the 1ω energy ramp portion of the campaign (shot N161115-001-999) with the measured fluence
profile from the same beam on shot N120705-002-999, taken at similar energy and power but without PSS
beam flattening. In this case the fluence contrast was improved from 8.6% to 5%.

Figure 7: Examples of near-field beam quality obtained on beam B454 with (right) and without (left) PSS
beam flattening. Top and bottom rows show low and high energy operation, respectively [3].

Figure 8 shows the near-fields of Q32B 1ω beams with PSS application. The spot pattern on the near-fields
shows beam spot blockers, which will be addressed in detail in the next section. Q32T beams have similar
performance (right panel). The fluence contrast for B326-328 is 7-8% and excellent for the large number

3.4 Wavefront correction

While PSS performs beam shaping aiming for a flat near field (with the application of spot blockers) for the
amplitude (fluence) portion of the optical field, it is also important to control the phase (wavefront) of the
optical field to ensure overall good beam quality and focusability. Phase distortions arise not only from the
inherent optical surface qualities of laser slabs, but also the thermal distortions of the laser slabs when
pumped by flashlamps. Consequently, as our code predicted, the magnitude of wavefront pre-
compensation via the NIF deformable mirror (DM) increased when more amplifier slabs are added. Here
we measure the aggregate phase distortions on the NIF Shack-Hartman sensor during laser shot. The
aberrations magnitude and shape depend on how many slabs are pumped, their configuration and the
associated flashlamp explosion fraction. Measurements are first made on the low energy laser shots and
pre-figures are applied to the laser beam via the end-cavity DM. Because the flashlamp operating point is
fixed at 18% regardless of output energy in the first phase of the experiment, the thermal distortion at low
energy is representative of those experienced by the high energy shots. Figure 9 shows the wavefront from
Q32B. All four beams see a P-V distortion under 2 waves after correction. This outcome is satisfactory for
subsequent 3ω testing. Q32T has a similar wavefront performance along with all the other NIF beams
during ignition experiments.

3.5 Spot blockers validation

 7

A second important function of the PSS is to create shadows (“spot blockers”) at specific locations in the
beam to mask defects or damage spots in the final optics. Spot blocker diameter and roll-off parameters
are designed to achieve the best balance for preserving beam area (keep them small) while avoiding
damage risk from nonlinear-optical modulations (but not too small/sharp) at SF4 (exit lens of the transport
spatial filter) and WFL/GDS planes. For 2.2 MJ full NIF equivalent (FNE), the optimum spot blocker has 2.4
cm diameter and a softened edge (complementary erf function width of 1.1cm). For EYC operation at 2.6
MJ FNE, the increased level of amplifier saturation and total B-integral associated with higher energy
operation changes this optimum, requiring larger spot blockers to maintain the same machine safety
margins. VBL simulations determined that a diameter of 2.6 cm for the spot blockers was this new optimum
and the edges are further softened to 1.2 cm. Interestingly enough, while the size and roll-off of the blockers
are slightly different, the overall loss of beam area is essentially the same due to nonlinear effects, which is
a very good news for 2.6 MJ operations.

The number of spot blockers (20 total: 18 black and 2 gray) deployed during this test was set to mimic the
total obscuration anticipated during EYC operations when a beam-based PSS capability is assumed –to be
delivered by NIF Sustainment. Adding the adequate number of spot blockers and delivering 2.6 MJ FNE
ensured that the 3ω fluence tests would be fair and relevant to true NIF EYC operating conditions. Note
that the spot blockers were positioned along a grid pattern for convenience as they were not obscuring any
actual flaw in the 3ω final optics as the experiments were confined to the 1ω section of the laser.

The new spot blocker specifications were verified by performing shots to the output of the ML
corresponding to 2.6 MJ 3ω TCC Full NIF Equivalent, with the Output Sensor Package (OSP) near-field
camera imaged at SF4 - the optical component furthest out of relay. Figure 10 shows the measured time
averaged spot blocker profile. A lineout through this time-integrated fluence profile is smooth and does
not exceed machine safety limits at SF4. However, further modeling shows that as B-integral accumulates
throughout the pulse, nonlinear modulation starts to produce enhanced contrast at a few spatial locations.
While operating at EYC energy levels is still considered safe, further energy scale will need to account for
this enhanced nonlinear modulation.

For the beams in Q32B, their pre-pulse powers have historically been above average for the 11-5

approximately another factor of 1.6, the pre-pulse magnitude will increase further by this ratio for EYC
operation. Initially, the measured 1ω pre-pulse level at OSP is ~3 GW for the EYC shots (see N260211-002-
999, N260401-002-999 and N260506-001-999), clearly unacceptable when projected to 192-beam
operations. Consequently, an effort was undertaken to improve this pre-pulse level. The investigation
found that this elevated level was due to excessive amplified spontaneous emission at the input of the main
laser. Therefore, the seed had to be increased at the pre-amp stage, which included both the regenerative
amplifier and the multi-pass pre-amplifier. Fortuitously, recent improvements from the fiber-optic master
oscillator enabled such stronger seeding. Additionally, work on the regenerative amplifier was performed
to improve the spatial overlap between the pumped region and the extraction beam. These improvements
were instrumental in meeting the pre-pulse requirement for the EYC 11-7 configuration. Figure 11 shows
the OSP power sensor measurement for B326. Shot N260506-001-999 is an older EYC shot that had an
elevated pre-pulse level, while the latter Shot N260609-001-999 incorporated the improvement from the
master oscillator and its pre-pulse performance met the NIF laser requirements with margin. The pre-pulse
performance for the other three beams in Q32B is comparable to that of B326. This proves that the current
NIF laser can meet, with margin, the pre-pulse requirement at the elevated main laser gain, therefore from
the more challenging EYC 11-7 configuration.

3.7 Demonstration of ML gain margins with fx=19% operations

1. Executive Summary

The 3ω campaign’s objectives were achieved and all the ignition-quality, EYC performance requirements were
met (see tables in Appendix 6.3) for the quad 32B laser delivery. This campaign –totaling 10 system shots–
successfully demonstrated repeated, reliable and precise delivery of 54 kJ UV per quad (the level required to
deliver 2.6 MJ using the entire NIF). These demonstrations were performed with the EYC pulse shape on Q32B
and showed that all laser performance requirements were met with ignition-quality deliveries.

The EYC project aims to enable NIF operations at 2.6 MJ in the UV and maximize the yield available for
stockpile stewardship applications. Since the EYC operating point is significantly higher than the initial NIF
requirement of 1.8 MJ –and 2.05-2.2 MJ current operations– as shown in Figure 1, a risk reduction
campaign is a worthwhile endeavor.

The high-level objective for EYC Performance Bundle (IR)/Quad (UV) Campaign was to perform a
quantitative assessment of the performance, risk and cost of NIF operation at 2.6 MJ and 450 TW. To meet
this goal, four nominal beamlines from a single quad were configured as close as possible to the EYC optical
baseline configuration as described in [1]. The pulse shape chosen for the campaign was defined by
A. Kritcher (along with suitable energy/power scaling relationships) to be relevant for optimal ICF
implosions at EYC conditions. While a Quad campaign at 2.6 MJ, 480 TW (higher operating power compared
to current EYC design point) was already conducted in 2017 [see NIF-1003081972-AA], the campaign this
year focused on deploying more amplifier slabs in the main laser (ML) –adding margins for small signal
gain degradation– as well as applying some recent learnings to mitigate focusing lens thick edge
filamentary damage [2].

Consequently, some specific objectives of the present campaign are:
− Demonstrate processes are in place to install and commission the laser at EYC-level operations, esp. in
 the IR,
− Deploy and validate the 11-7 1 amplifier ML configuration,
− At the EYC operating point, measure the 1ω/3ω laser performance, and compare with model
 expectations and EYC requirements,
− Evaluate optics damage rate and extrapolate to the Optic Recycle Loop Model.

This report describes the successful validation of the 3ω laser models, and its associated configuration
changes to evaluate both 3ω laser/optics and the optic recycle loop. This campaign also successfully
demonstrated repeated, reliable and precise delivery of 54 kJ UV with the EYC pulse shape on Q32B, also
dubbed as EYC 2.6 MJ Full NIF Equivalent (FNE). While 1ω IR reconfiguration and performance
demonstration have been successfully completed for both Q32B and Q32T (results documented in [3]),
only Q32B was utilized for 3ω evaluation during this campaign and will be the subject of the present report.
The 3ω optics resilience and performance results are documented in a subsequent report [4]. As a side

3. 3ω Laser Configuration Updates

A few changes and updates have been made to the 3ω laser and laser beam path in Q32B for the EYC
campaign because of higher 3ω operating fluence (see details in [1], [2]). These include:
 1. Updated spot blocker size and shape (deployed in the front-end),
 2. Two newly refurbished beam dumps,
           3. Blue Blockers in B326/B328 and 1ωDS in B325/B327,
 4. Low diffraction efficiency (DE) grating debris shield (GDS),
 5. Tab3 wedge focusing lens (WFL),
 6. New (“so called 100% transmission” or “Tx1” at times) Borofloat disposable debris
 shields (DDS).

Spot blockers: Presently a Quad-based Programmable Spatial Shaper (PSS) creates shadows (“spot
blockers”) at specific locations in the beam to mask defects or damage spots in the final optics [5]. Note
that a future beam-based device will be fielded by NIF Sustainment, minimizing the area loss created on 3
beams when masking the defects measured on one beamline in the quad. This device will be critical for
EYC operations and is part of its laser architecture baseline [1]. Additionally, for EYC operation at 2.6 MJ
FNE, larger spot blockers are needed to maintain the same machine safety margins. VBL simulations
determined that a diameter of 2.6 cm and an edge of 1.2 cm was the new optimum for the spot blockers.
Experimental evidence at 1ω ML showed that these larger spot blockers do not lead to nonlinear Poisson
spots at SF4 [3]. For the EYC campaign, we restricted total obscuration to 18 fully black blockers, compared
to 25 spot blockers permitted during current 2.2 MJ operations. This reduction accounts for the anticipated
benefits of the future Beam-Based Programmable Spatial Shaper being delivered by the Sustainment
program. For the various shots, the obscuration was implemented using a combination of black and gray
spot blockers, where two gray spot blockers provide equivalent obscured area to one black blocker.
While the EYC blockers have slightly different size and roll-off characteristics, the overall loss of beam area
is essentially equivalent to that in 2.2 MJ operations due to saturation effects and the reduced blocker count.
This represents positive news for EYC operations. Calculations examining nonlinear optical fluence
intensifications around the spot blockers indicate that 3ω damage is not expected from the EYC spot
blocker parameters [6]. These calculations have been experimentally validated by the absence of any rim-
shape damage in the optics.

Beam dumps: Both beam dumps (capture the ghosts coming back from the IOMs) in Q32B were replaced
with new ones to minimize potential debris generation from older beam dumps [2].

Blue blockers: Both blue blockers and 1ωDS have been installed to assess blue blocker performance and
resilience under EYC conditions. For more context, a blue blocker is a cerium-doped fused silica optic that
transmits IR light but blocks UV wavelength. This property enables blue blockers to block UV back-
reflections from TCC while transmitting the 1ω light from the main laser. This will be a key element to
mitigate LM8 damage due to SBS backscatter in the current 2.2 MJ NIF operations and even more critical
for EYC. In the EYC configuration, where all the 192 beams will be equipped with a blue blocker optic as
described in the EYC laser architecture baseline [1].

Grating Debris Shields: Low-DE GDS ensures that the diagnostics do not experience excessive light

4. Low Fluence Conversion Crystals Tuning and Conditioning Shots

As a standard NIF process, rod-shots were performed prior to the EYC 2.6MJ system shots at 3ω to optimize
the crystal angular tuning and maximize conversion efficiency. This so-called “rod shot rocking” sequence
uses 88 ps impulses with about 0.5 J of injected energy, without energizing the main amplifiers. The UV
light (a few hundreds of pJ) is collected by the Target Alignment Sensor, acting like a UV calorimeter at TCC.
Multiple rod shots were taken (total of 12), rocking sequentially the doubler and then the tripler crystals.

A rocking curve was generated using the output of these rod shots for each of the frequency doubling
(Figure 2) and tripling (Figure 3) stages. UV laser performance was optimized based on these rocking
curves. Each point on the curves represents a rod shot experiment that could be taken much faster than
system shots. The x-axis represents the angular axis for the crystal tuning, resp. SHG and THG. The y-axis
represents the normalized 3ω energy measured for a given crystal angle. The best phase matching angle is
then automatically extracted by LPOM via a curve fitting algorithm and future alignment of the crystals will
take these results into account to ensure the maximal conversion efficiency when system shots are taken.

5. 3ω Laser Performance results at 2.6 MJ FNE

As planned, the final stage of the EYC campaign was concluded with four experiments using all the Q32B
beams at 2.6 MJ, 450 TW with the EYC ignition pulse shapes. To allow some turn-around time between
experiments as well as to provide a more realistic use profile to study damage growth for the multi-shot
optics –potential initiation at EYC level followed by growth with repeated lower energy shots– the last four
experiments were spaced one month apart. The first of the series happened in April, followed by
experiments in May, June and finally concluded with the last experiment in July.

In all cases, the 3ω laser performed according to expectations. Beam quality was excellent, and the
delivered performance was consistent with the requested performance up to 13.5 kJ UV per beam, the
maximum tested and consistent with EYC performance. Agreement between 3ω delivered and requested
energy was better than 5% on a beam-by-beam basis, and better than 1.8% at the quad level. The
agreement between delivered and requested TCC pulse shape met ignition experiment requirements
(Figure 5). To quantify this power accuracy (e.g., with the last shot of the campaign), we utilize quad-
averaged power curve. The quad average is first time-aligned with the requested power curve. It is then
divided into bins and the deviation between the measured and requested for each bin is recorded. Figure
6 shows such a power accuracy report. The deviations for picket, trough, Shock3, Rise-to-peak and at-peak
are 1.8%, 6.5%, 2.5%, 0.4%, 0.3%, respectively. This power accuracy is excellent especially since repeated
3ω optimization (process known as “loop3” typically applied for ignition experiments) was not performed
in real-time prior to the system shot.

The NIF 3ω pre-pulse requirement is detailed in Appendix 6.3. Because the 3ω pre-pulse is too low to
measure accurately, NIF performs experimental verification at the output of the 1ω laser. Previously the
allowable 1ω power of 1.5 GW/beam was derived using a mix of nonlinear crystal configurations. Because
the EYC 11-7 configuration substantially increases the laser small signal gain, pre-pulse performance is
expected to worsen. In fact, the first EYC shots did not satisfy the pre-pulse requirement. Improvements
were made at the input of the 1ω laser to minimize spontaneous emission background. The improvement
was successful and the last two EYC shots (N260609-001-999, and N260715-003-999) easily met the pre-
pulse requirement with margin as shown in Figure 7. The measured 1ω pre-pulse is ~2x below the
required 1.5 GW/beam. Because of the cubic dependence of IR to UV nonlinear conversion [8], if all the
beams had a similar 1ω pre-pulse level for an EYC experiment, then the pre-pulse laser intensity delivered
to the target over 192 beams amounts to ~107 W/cm2, nearly an order-of-magnitude lower than the
requirement.

SUBJECT: FY26 Bu32 Enhanced Yield Capability (EYC) Performance Quad
Campaign: Optics Damage Risk Reduction

1. Executive Summary
The FY26 Bu32 EYC Performance Quad Campaign showed that mid-life optics (i.e., not just
new optics, as were used in the FY17 Performance Quad Campaign) in an average NIF quad
have acceptable damage-initiation and growth rates under 2.6 MJ-equivalent conditions when
used in conjunction with pre-shot laser cleaning and 18 blockers per quad. These results
represent a significant reduction in risk toward the EYC goal of supporting routine 2.6 MJ
shots. Future performance campaigns, incorporating additional optics process mitigations
(such as SiO2 surface degradation mitigation, increased RAM cone damage resistance, and
improved mirror and crystal conditioning) and evaluating additional NIF quads, will provide
input to the optics system loop model and help determine the rate at which routine 2.6 MJ shots
can be supported.

2. Summary
The number of 2.6 MJ shots that NIF can execute per year is expected to be governed by
damage initiation and growth in the transport mirrors and specific optics in the Final Optics
Assembly (FOA), namely the Wedge Focus Lens (WFL), Grating Debris Shield (GDS), and
Third Harmonic Generator (THG). Historically, NIF's operational tempo has been limited by
the damage-initiation rate in the final SiO2 optics, the WFL and GDS. The initiation rate on
SiO2 surfaces imposes a fundamental limit on the energy levels at which these optics can be
operated and hence is one of the largest optics damage risks to routine 2.6 MJ operation. The
FY26 Bundle 32 EYC (FY26B32) Performance Campaign was planned and executed using
the four NIF beamlines in Bundle 32 (Quad 32B) configured as close as practical to the EYC
final-optics configuration to perform a quantitative risk assessment of fundamental laser-
induced damage initiation and growth during NIF operation at 2.6 MJ / 450 TW in typical NIF
beams. An emphasis was placed on separating the fundamental limits of WFL and GDS
damage from other failure modes (such as Gen 1 RAM-cone failures and debris from Gen 1
beam dumps that were not evaluated in this campaign). The details of the 1ω and 3ω laser
performance during this campaign are summarized elsewhere [1, 2], and this memo focuses on
optics performance.
In summary (see the details in the following sections), the FY26B32 performance campaign
demonstrated an average damage initiation rate on the SiO2 optics approximately 30% lower

per LG than the rate observed during 2.2 MJ operation (although significantly higher than the
2017 P&E test [3], which used new and nearly new optics). The growth rates and LG life of
the GDS and WFLs observed during the FY26B32 Campaign were statistically identical to
those observed during 2.2 MJ operation between 2/1/24 and 2/1/25. These damage initiation
and growth observations indicate that recycled, mid-life SiO2 optics in typical NIF beamlines
can support routine 2.6 MJ operation with 18 blockers and the use of laser cleaning shots,
provided that operation is not limited by other, separable failure modes.
The effects of 2.6 MJ shots on LM8 and the THGs were inconclusive due to the relatively long
life of these optic types compared to the duration of the campaign. However, no noticeable
differences were observed in LM8 life during the campaign relative to 2.2 MJ operation. Some
large sites on the THGs appeared to grow approximately 50% faster than during the limited
campaign observations than the average site growth rate during 2.2 MJ operation; however,
the faster growth rate is likely attributable to defects on the LM7 mirrors which were not
representative of the full EYC configuration. The slope of the THG life metric appears
unchanged during the 2.6 MJ shots, suggesting that while THG life is strongly influenced by
the condition of the laser mirrors, it is not directly affected by 2.6 MJ shots.
Although not the focus of this campaign, the usage rate of the FSDS optic was monitored and
found to be indistinguishable from that during 2.2 MJ operation. DDS usage could not be
evaluated because debris from detonated targets is the primary life-limiting factor for the DDS,
and all the 2.6 MJ shots were non-target shots.
The 2.6 MJ shot rate that NIF can sustain will instead likely be governed by the availability of
beam-based blockers and the accrual rate of permanent blockers arising from next-generation
RAM cone failures, cluster damage, THG damage, and LM damage, as well as any tail beams
that exhibit significantly worse-than-average damage performance. Each of these failure
modes is independent of the fundamental damage limitations of the GDS and WFL, and each
is the subject of ongoing EYC optic-process mitigations, the success of which will determine
the sustainable rate and cost-effectiveness of 2.6 MJ operation.

3. Optics Configuration
The FY26B32 Campaign has taken an important step toward emulating an EYC configuration
that includes: 1) two additional amplifier slabs in the power amplifier in each beamline; 2) a
reduced-aperture mask for the blastshield in the power amplifier; 3) the addition of blue
blockers or 1ωDS in front of the TCVW; 4) the use of TAB 3 (thinnest) WFLs in all beamlines;
5) low-diffraction-efficiency GDS; 6) calibrated DRD and new WL1 lens; 7) no Grey Edge
Blockers; and 8) a reduction in spot blockers from 25 to 18 per quad, with a new, optimized
blocker size for 2.6 MJ shots. These changes are also illustrated in Figure 1. Some of the
mitigations of the full EYC configuration are not yet present on this test quad; these include:
1) second-generation beam dumps; 2) Blue Blockers and IOM refurbishment on all four
beamlines; 3) beam-based (vs. quad-based) blockers; 4) next-generation, more damage-
resistant RAM cones; 5) nonstick LM coatings; and 6) SiO2 degradation mitigations. Each of
these gaps has an independent failure mode that contributes to the collective effect on the rate
at which NIF can be expected to execute 2.6 MJ shots (e.g., LM damage rate and RAM cone
failure rate are both expected to be overestimated relative to full EYC because mitigations for

these failure modes are in development), and the memo's comparison of the FY17 P&E test
and 2.2 MJ operation is framed accordingly.

5. Optics installation and microscopy

Damage initiation and growth are expressed in the metrics of damage site initiation density,
probability of growth, and growth rate. All three metrics are most cleanly evaluated by high-
resolution microscopy before installation and after removal from NIF, with a single 2.6 MJ
shot in between. Although this approach provides low-noise information on the fundamental
laser-induced damage initiation and growth limitations under 2.6 MJ conditions, it has limited
utility for extrapolation to NIF EYC operation, wherein optics would essentially never be
exposed to only a single exposure during an installation period. Single-shot exposures fail to

account for several environmental factors, such as debris generation from laser-induced
damage on other exposures (not limited to 2.6 MJ shots), debris from targets, debris from beam
dumps, and 3ω silica surface degradation.

In order to account for these real operating conditions while also collecting low-noise
information, the WFL and GDS experimental methodology included optics exposed to both
single-shot and multi-shot installation periods between offline inspections by automated
microscopes. Two beams in the 32B quad (Beams 5 and 6) were designated as single-shot
beamlines. In these beamlines, the WFL and GDS were installed for the 2.6 MJ exposure only
and removed immediately afterward. This was accomplished by maintaining two complete sets
of optics for these two beamlines: while one set was installed, the second set was inspected
and processed through a standard NIF recycle loop, including damage mitigation and a coating
strip-and-recoat cycle. The optics from these two beams thus provide fundamental initiation
and growth values for 2.6 MJ shots without other factors affecting the results. The remaining
two beams (7 and 8) were designated as multi-shot beamlines. In these beamlines, the WFLs
and GDSs were installed before the beginning of the campaign and remained installed
throughout, being removed only when required by optic performance or after completion of
the campaign. These optics provide cumulative initiation and growth data spanning not only
multiple 2.6 MJ exposures but also a range of ongoing NIF operations and the commensurate
environmental effects on laser-induced damage and growth.

By including these two different methods of optic use and inspection during the campaign, we
were able to compare the fundamental damage behavior observed during 2.6 MJ operation to
that measured for other NIF operating points, for which NIF's use of 192 beams over a year
provides much greater statistical certainty. The 2.6 MJ multi-shot beams then offer a direct
comparison with the extrapolation discussed in Section 7 below.

initiation, the number of new sites on an optic between inspections, and damage growth, the

7. Implications of damage on routine 2.6 MJ operation

The fundamental requirement for NIF's GDS and WFL optics to support 2.6 MJ operation is
that damage initiation per 2.6 MJ shot be low enough that optics can be mitigated in the recycle
loop. The loop rate model [5,6] shows that an initiation rate on the order of 200 sites or less is
required to allow the recycle loop to function under its current concept of operations. Figure
2 shows that most WFLs and GDSs had initiation rates on the order of 20 to 200 sites per 2.6
MJ shot, with the exception of the second shot on the WFL in Beam 326, which had no damage
initiation.

The LG life of the WFL and GDS optics is a measure of how long the optics last on NIF, and
it will ultimately determine the rate and cost-effectiveness of 2.6 MJ shots. LG life is not a
fundamental property of the optics but is an empirical observable [6] which is determined by
the rate at which sites initiate, the probability that they will grow, and the rate at which they
grow large enough to require a blocker and the number of blockers available. The loop rate
model uses this empirical data on individual optics' behavior to accurately predict the number
of optics exchanges needed to support NIF operation. As of this writing, only four of the 18
optics used in the FY26B32 Campaign had reached the damage state requiring recycling. Of
these four optics, three were of E10 recycle pedigree, which is representative of EYC
operations; the fourth was of E20 recycle pedigree, used to collect growth rate and probability-
of-growth data. The LG exposure experienced by these optics (Table 2) gives us early insight
into expected optics life during 2.6 MJ operation but is necessarily biased toward the short-life
side of the distribution.

The rate at which optics will fall out of the recycle loop and need to be replaced will depend
on the rate at which they accrue obscuration and permanent blockers from repaired and
unrepairable damage, respectively. The obscuration accrual rate is available for two GDS
optics and one WFL in Table 2, all of which are similar to or lower than those observed during
routine 2.2 MJ operation. As stated above, these optics were the first to reach the state requiring
mitigation and are therefore in the lowest quartile of longevity, giving damage sites less time
to grow than those on optics with an average installation life — consistent with a smaller
increase in obscuration. Over the course of the campaign, these three optics accrued two
permanent blockers, both of which are attributed to failure modes expected to be significantly
reduced, if not eliminated, in typical NIF beams under full EYC. The permanent blocker
accrual rate during full EYC will be determined by the success of the ongoing EYC Optics
Process Mitigation project.
The duration of the campaign was considered too short to provide significant insight into
expected THG performance. There was no statistically significant change in the Tripler Life
Metric (Table 2 and Figure 4) during the campaign, which could indicate no change in
expected THG life or more likely, the installation period was too short to detect a change. THG
performance will be evaluated in future campaigns.
Evaluation of the two LMs removed for microscopy showed insufficient growth to estimate
changes in LM life. In addition, damage initiation on the LMs was dominated by debris-

induced damage that is not expected to be relevant under EYC conditions. LM performance
will therefore be evaluated in future campaigns.

8. New Finding: Debris-Driven Damage

Following the conditioning ramp for the FY26B32 Campaign, inspection of the silica optics
revealed damage initiation levels in excess of expectations, with one WFL accumulating more
than 15,000 total initiation sites over the three- to four-shot (beam line dependent) conditioning
sequence. Across all WFLs on the conditioning ramp, the average was approximately 2,000
damage sites per shot (see Figure 2). The spatial distribution of this damage (Figure 5) was
equally unexpected: rather than the roughly uniform pattern with a slight centerline bias typical
of NIF beamlines, damage was concentrated at the top-center and bottom-center on the top and
bottom beam pairs, respectively.

The damage morphology (Figure 5) was also inconsistent with typical pristine-surface damage,
instead matching the signature of extrinsic, debris-based initiation observed in prior online and
offline experiments [5]. A follow-up inspection, which removed the IOM optics on one
beamline, found no identifiable debris source proximal to the damage.

Figure 5. WFL damage morphology and spatial distribution during the EYC 32B conditioning
ramp as compared to the 2.6 MJ 32B loop rate campaign and 2.2 MJ operation.

Despite the inability to identify a debris source, the combination of damage morphology and
spatial pattern provides strong support for extrinsic debris-based initiation as the dominant
cause of the anomalous conditioning-ramp damage. To move forward with the 2.6 MJ
campaign, mid-life optics (instead of EOL optics) were installed and a laser-cleaning approach
was adopted, drawing on its demonstrated (if statistically limited) effectiveness in reducing
damage initiation by about an order of magnitude during the FY17 P&E Campaign [3]. A small
validation experiment — two 2.6 MJ exposures bracketing a single low-fluence cleaning shot
— resulted in damage initiation ranging from tens to hundreds of sites. It was concluded that
laser cleaning resulted acceptable levels of damage for the 2.6 MJ shots and was subsequently
adopted as a standard pre-shot step for the remainder of the campaign.

One important caveat tempers this conclusion: because the underlying debris source was never
positively identified, it remains unclear how much of the improvement seen during the
campaign is actually attributable to laser cleaning versus other unknown factors. A plausible
alternative or contributing mechanism is self-contamination — debris ejected from growing,
unblocked damage sites on the end-of-life optics used during the conditioning ramp, a
phenomenon known to occur on upward-facing optics such as those in 32B. However, this
hypothesis does not account for the observed global spatial pattern, which points instead to an
extrinsic source. As a result, no definitive root cause has been established for this damage event.
What can be said is that it has not recurred since laser cleaning was implemented, whether
because of genuine mitigation or because the original cause did not recur.
