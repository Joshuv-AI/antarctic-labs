1 INTRODUCTION
This progress report (Level 3 Milestone Number M3SF-25LL010301052) summarizes research
conducted at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) within the Argillite Host Rock
Properties & Processes Work Package SF-25LL01030105. The focus of this milestone is to create
an initial SUPCRTNE database and extensively test the SUPCRTNE code. We prepared a draft
manuscript describing our workflow and approach for developing a next-generation thermodynamic
database for the Spent Fuel and High Level Waste Disposition campaign. The goal is to ease any
future formal software qualification effort as was done on the Yucca Mountain Project for codes
including SUPCRT92 and EQ3/6. We are attempting to extend the SUPCRTNE database to include
more species, mainly from the NEA volumes. We can also take advantage of thermodynamic
database activities that are ongoing at Thermochimie and as part of the EURADII program. However,
our effort is limited by the funds available and the changes to program scope that was initiated in
mid-FY25.

A second effort focused on integration of both surface complexation and ion exchange optimization
capabilities with a particular focus on clay minerals relevant to argillite rock. High throughput
automated workflows were developed using the CrunchFlow geochemical modeling engine (replacing
PHREEQC) to model sorption of U(VI) to montmorillonite. This effort is leading us from workflows that
focus on single radionuclide-mineral pairs to more comprehensive modeling workflows that will more
readily support downstream performance assessment models. Both the thermodynamics and
sorption efforts will be presented at the annual EURADII workshop and the Migration2025
conference. Summaries of reports and draft manuscripts prepared as part of this effort are
summarized below.

INTRODUCTION

Chemical thermodynamic data are foundational for geochemical modeling and reactive transport
simulations, with applications ranging from earth and planetary sciences to industrial processes. The
reliability of such modeling depends on the internal consistency of the underlying thermodynamic
databases, which are often compiled from diverse sources. Inconsistencies may arise due to
differences in experimental data, calculation methods, or the selection of key reference species. This
paper addresses the challenge of combining two large thermodynamic datasets, each of which is
internally consistent: the OECD/Nuclear Energy Agency (NEA) database, which is based on the
CODATA international standard, and the mineral dataset of Holland and Powell (2011, HP11), which
is widely used in petrology and geochemistry but lacks explicit key reference data for certain species.

The NEA dataset is recognized for its rigorous methodology and international peer review, making it a
preferred standard for regulatory applications such as nuclear waste disposal. However, it offers
limited coverage of rock-forming minerals, a gap filled by the HP11 dataset, which contains data for
210 such minerals. The goal of this work is to develop a methodology for adjusting the HP11 data to
maximize consistency with the NEA standard, thereby enabling the creation of a comprehensive and
internally consistent thermodynamic database suitable for a wide range of geochemical applications.

METHODS

The methodology centers on identifying and correcting differences in the standard thermodynamic
properties (enthalpy and entropy) of key reference species between the NEA and HP11 datasets. Key
reference species are typically elemental forms and simple binary compounds (e.g., H2O, CO2, SiO2,
Al2O3, MgO, CaO), which serve as anchors for the thermodynamic properties of more complex
species. The approach assumes each dataset is internally consistent, meaning the data for non-key
species are coherent with the chosen key data.

The adjustment process involves calculating the differences in enthalpy and entropy values for key
reference species present in both datasets. These differences define adjustment coefficients that
are then applied linearly to adjust the HP11 data for more complex minerals and compounds. Where
direct comparison is not possible due to missing data, adjustments may be inferred using alternative
reference species, related reactions, or auxiliary data from aqueous models. The methodology
ensures that reaction properties calculated with the adjusted HP11 data remain consistent with
those calculated using the original dataset, preserving the internal relationships established by
Holland and Powell.

Special attention is given to the treatment of water properties, as the NEA dataset uses the IAPWS-
95 model, while HP11 employs its own equation of state. Adjustments are made to ensure
consistency at standard temperature and pressure. The methodology also considers the limitations
and uncertainties inherent in both datasets, comparing the magnitude of adjustments to reported
uncertainties.

RESULTS

Application of the adjustment methodology reveals that the differences between the NEA and HP11
datasets for most key reference species are small, generally less than the corresponding 2σ
uncertainties. This suggests both datasets are largely derived from a common pool of vetted
experimental data. Adjustments are straightforward for oxides, silicates, aluminosilicates,
carbonates, and chlorides, which constitute the bulk of rock-forming minerals. For species lacking

August 15, 2025                                                                                       4

direct NEA data (e.g., MnO, Cr2O3, Na2O, K2O), the HP11 values are retained or inferred using related
aqueous species, though these inferred adjustments are treated with caution.

The process preserves the calculated properties of reactions among minerals, demonstrating that
the adjustment does not alter the internal consistency of the HP11 dataset. Notably, the
methodology can be extended to other internally consistent databases, facilitating the construction
of large, comprehensive thermodynamic datasets without introducing inconsistencies from differing
key reference data.

The adjusted HP11 dataset, combined with the NEA database, provides a robust foundation for
modeling geochemical processes relevant to nuclear waste disposal, planetary science, and
industrial applications. The approach also highlights areas where further refinement or additional
data may be needed, particularly for species with limited experimental coverage.

SUMMARY

This work presents a practical and systematic methodology for combining two large, internally
consistent thermodynamic databases by adjusting one (HP11) to match the key reference data of
the other (NEA/CODATA). The adjustment process is based on differences in enthalpy and entropy
values for key reference species and is applied linearly to more complex minerals and compounds.
Where direct data are unavailable, adjustments are inferred using related reactions or auxiliary data,
though such inferences are made cautiously.
The results show that the magnitude of required adjustments is generally smaller than 2σ
uncertainties, indicating strong underlying consistency between the datasets. The methodology
preserves the internal relationships within the HP11 dataset, ensuring that reaction properties
remain unchanged after adjustment. This enables the creation of a combined thermodynamic
database that is both internally consistent and aligned with international standards, suitable for a
wide range of geochemical modeling applications.
The approach is flexible and can be applied to other datasets believed to be internally consistent,
supporting future database development and integration efforts. While the methodology is not
perfect and some limitations remain, particularly for species with sparse data, it represents a
significant advance in the construction of reliable thermodynamic databases for geochemical and
industrial applications.
In conclusion, the combination of the NEA and adjusted HP11 datasets offers a strong starting point
for future work in geochemical modeling, with the potential to support complex scenarios in nuclear
waste isolation, rock-water interactions, and beyond. The adjustment methodology ensures that
inconsistencies arising from differing key reference data are minimized, enhancing the reliability and
applicability of thermodynamic models in earth and planetary sciences.

SUMMARY
In support of nuclear waste repository science, effective parameterization of sorption processes over
the wide range of conditions that may be present in nuclear waste repositories requires a robust and
comprehensive database of experimentally derived sorption data. The Lawrence Livermore National
Laboratory-Surface Complexation/Ion Exchange (L-SCIE) database is a recent effort to unify
community sorption experiments and metadata in a findable, accessible, interoperable, and
reusable (FAIR) format. To date, it has mined 10’s of thousands of raw sorption data from the
literature and provides a platform to test novel approaches to surface complexation/ion exchange
modelling and database development. The database was recently enhanced to include sorption data
compiled by the Paul Scherrer Institute and the sorption database from the Japanese Atomic Energy
Agency (JAEA). We present an update on the L-SCIE database and its application to both traditional
surface complexation/ion exchange modelling and hybrid machine learning (ML) that achieve high-
quality predictions of radionuclide sorption to mineral surfaces using this community data approach.
These efforts have identified the need for more robust systems for exchanging and sharing data,
standard formats for reporting such data, and automated workflows that allow for continual updating
of models and associated reaction databases that support nuclear waste repository science and
performance goals.
INTRODUCTION
In support of nuclear waste repository science, effective representation of radionuclide sorption and
retardation processes in reactive transport and performance assessment models is essential.
Effective parameterization of sorption processes over the wide range of conditions that may be
present in nuclear waste repositories requires a robust and comprehensive database of
experimentally derived sorption data. The Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory-Surface
Complexation/Ion Exchange (L-SCIE) database is a recent effort to unify community sorption
experimental data and metadata in a findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable (FAIR) format
[1]. To date, it has mined 10’s of thousands of raw sorption data from the literature and provides a
platform to test novel approaches to surface complexation/ion exchange modelling and database
development. Briefly, L-SCIE mines sorption data (Kd, % sorbed, surface excess) and dataset

August 15, 2025                                                                                        6

experimental conditions (background electrolyte, mineral surface area, gas composition, etc.) from
journal manuscripts and loads them into a database. All the sorption data undergo a series of unit
conversions to yield a unified database which includes propagated conversion errors from the
original extracted data. The database can then be filtered for a metal-mineral pair of interest in order
to display a corresponding experimental dataset. The database is linked to the PHREEQC and
CrunchFlow geochemical modelling codes that can be used to identify reactions and associated
reaction constants that effectively reproduce the available community data. A detailed description of
the L-SCIE database and modelling framework was reported in Zavarin et al. [2].
In this short summary, we present an update on the L-SCIE database and its application to both
traditional surface complexation/ion exchange modelling and hybrid machine learning (ML) that
achieve high-quality predictions of radionuclide sorption to mineral surfaces using this community
data approach. We demonstrate both approaches and describe the next steps in the evolution of
LLNL’s community FAIR data effort and its application to radioactive waste management.
THE STATE OF THE L-SCIE DATABASE
The L-SCIE database is composed of a series of linked tables that are stored in a Microsoft Access
relational database. The details regarding the structure and content of the database are beyond the
scope of this summary. However, it is important to note some key properties that represent the basis
for the collection of data in the database:
• The data in L-SCIE are, at present, focused entirely on synthetic and mined mineral phases with a
 strong emphasis in simple binary sorption data.
• The database is composed of original data extracted from journal manuscripts, “grey literature”
 reports, or other digital databases available in the community.
• Data are stored in their original form (no unit conversions performed) and directly linked to the
 manuscript and the associated tables and figures from which the data were extracted.
• The data are not screened through any type of data quality process
• All data processing is performed external to the database via R and Python codes and associated
 workflows
• Links to geochemical modelling codes and optimization algorithms used to develop models
 based on L-SCIE data are integrated with but external to the database.
• All L-SCIE database products are freely available, follow the FAIR principles, and adhere to the
 Open Science philosophy.
Today, the L-SCIE database contains nearly 73,000 data (Figure 1). The recent major additions to
this database, beyond the manual extraction of data from the literature, was the inclusion of ~5000
clay sorption data that were provided through a collaboration with the ClaySor group at the Paul
Scherrer Institute [3], 40,000 data (out of 70,000 total) that were extracted from the online sorption
database supported by the Japanese Atomic Energy Agency [4], and small numbers of data (100’s
out of ~4700 total) that were provided through our collaboration with the SOREDA/RES3T team at
the Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf [5, 6]. It should be noted that a large fraction of the
JAEA sorption data are related to rocks and engineered materials that are not the focus of the L-SCIE
effort and were not carried over into L-SCIE. Nevertheless, the workflows and python code necessary
to transform all JAEA data into L-SCIE are available.

U(VI) Sorption to Quartz – Machine Learning Application
Following the demonstration of the L-SCIE database and surface complexation modelling approach,
Chang et al. [8] investigated how machine learning may be applied if large sorption datasets are
available to train these models. Machine learning models are implemented most effectively when
large datasets are available for testing and training. Thus, the L-SCIE database provides an
opportunity to test these approaches and compare them to traditional geochemical models.
Attempts to build a purely AI-driven model were found to be lacking because integrating a ML-based
sorption model with reactive transport was not straightforward. However, a hybrid approach held

August 15, 2025                                                                                      9

significant appeal because the ML algorithm could be developed within the context of geochemical
modelling principles. For example, the ML model could be trained using “features” that are readily
produced during geochemical model simulation (e.g aqueous species concentrations, pH, sorption
site concentrations, etc.). This hybrid approach took the form presented in Figure 3A where a simple
Random Forest ML model was trained using species concentrations that were simulated using the
PHREEQC geochemical model. The approach was tested for U(VI) sorption to quartz and
montmorillonite and found to be equally effective in simulating the available sorption data as the
explicit surface complexation modelling approach. Interestingly, only three features were needed to
effectively simulate the full sorption dataset (Figure 3B). This ML example, which is one of many now
being tested in the literature, suggests that ML approaches may have a place in geochemical
modelling once sufficient training data are made available.

Protolysis Reactions on Iron Oxide Minerals
Data contained in L-SCIE include mineral titration data that can be used to model the surface charge
properties of mineral surfaces. Indeed, L-SCIE includes nearly 10,000 data associated with mineral
titration experiments. These data can be used to simulate surface protolysis reactions using a
community data approach. As shown by Han et al. [9], protolysis constants for a series of iron oxides
were modelled using community data (Table 1). Importantly, the weighted confidence intervals for
the protolysis constants suggest significant variability in the protolysis behaviour of solid phases
synthesize or purchased from a variety of sources. Indeed, the analysis suggests that uncertainty in
surface protolysis may play an important role in limiting the accuracy of predicted radionuclide
transport behaviour. Covariances between protolysis constants and radionuclide surface
complexation constants need to be acknowledged and accounted for in models.

Next Steps in Database and Sorption Model Development
In our efforts to develop a sorption database based on FAIR principles, key gaps and bottlenecks
have been identified. First, the collection of historical data into a structured database that is
amenable to automated workflows and modelling efforts is time consuming. Developing international
community standards in data collection, reporting, sharing, and digitization is essential. Second, the
surface complexation/ion exchange modelling approaches are evolving and AI and hybrid
approaches are being explored. As such, databases and their associated workflows must incorporate
automation principles to ensure that models and associated databases are readily updateable and
do not become “out of date”. Finally, we acknowledge that sorption data available in the literature
may not cover all conditions and relevant processes for nuclear waste repository science. Large
numbers of literature data remain untapped [2]. However, as sorption databases grow, they can be
used, in conjunction with performance assessment models, to direct research towards important
data gaps.
CONCLUSIONS
Today, the L-SCIE database contains nearly 73,000 data. A number of organizations around the
globe are actively developing sorption databases to support their programs (e.g. [3-5]). The
development of such databases is leading to a resurgence in surface complexation model
development efforts and will lead to breakthroughs in reactive transport modelling and performance
assessment. International collaboration and coordination are essential to maintaining the
momentum towards a paradigm shift in the science of interface geochemical modelling and full
integration of traditional thermodynamics with surface complexation and ion exchange. The science
is leading towards more robust and defensible development and acceleration of performance
assessment models for disposition of nuclear waste. Accelerating the safe disposition of nuclear
waste has an essential role to play in the resurgent interest in global nuclear power production.

Application of Large Community Sorption Datasets to Surface Complexation and Ion Exchange Model
Development: Uranium Sorption to Montmorillonite

Luis Eguiguren1, Haruko Wainwright2, and Mavrik Zavarin1

1Glenn T. Seaborg Institute, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Livermore, California 94550,

United States (zavarin1@llnl.gov, le0766@princeton.edu)
2Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, Massachusetts 02139, United States

(hmwainw@mit.edu)

INTRODUCTION

The management and long-term disposal of nuclear waste is a critical challenge due to the
persistence and mobility of radionuclides, particularly uranium (U) isotopes, in groundwater. Deep
geological repositories are being developed globally to safely contain nuclear waste. A key aspect of
repository safety assessment is the accurate prediction of radionuclide transport, which depends
heavily on understanding and modeling the sorption (retardation) of radionuclides onto subsurface
minerals. Traditionally, sorption has been described by empirical linear distribution coefficients
(Kds), but this approach fails to account for dynamic solution chemistry, competitive sorption, and
surface site saturation.

Modern approaches employ mechanistic models—specifically, surface complexation (SC) and ion
exchange (IE) models—to describe the chemical reactions governing radionuclide sorption. These
models require reliable reaction constants, but the literature contains a wide variety of model types,
assumptions, and parameterizations, making it difficult to develop comprehensive and transferable
databases of reaction constants. In contrast to the well-established consensus and accessibility of
aqueous thermodynamic databases, SC/IE reaction constant databases remain fragmented and
inconsistent.

To address this gap, the present study leverages the FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and
Reusable) data principles and open science workflows to aggregate, standardize, and apply large
community datasets of sorption data. The focus is on U(VI) sorption to montmorillonite, a clay
mineral with high cation exchange capacity and surface area, and a key component of bentonite
backfill in nuclear waste repositories. The study aims to optimize SC/IE reaction constants by
integrating data from diverse sources, assessing the robustness of existing models, and developing
a more universally applicable set of reaction constants for U(VI)-montmorillonite sorption.

METHODS

The study begins by examining the Bradbury and Baeyens (2005) (BB05) model, a widely cited SC/IE
model for U(VI) sorption to montmorillonite. The BB05 model, developed for the Swiss nuclear waste
program, employs a non-electrostatic SC approach combined with a Gaines-Thomas IE model, and is
based on a limited dataset of uranium sorption experiments with montmorillonite from different
sources. The reaction constants, mineral properties (surface area, site density, cation exchange
capacity), and experimental conditions are carefully digitized and used as a baseline for further
analysis.

A major methodological advance in this work is the use of the LLNL Surface Complexation/Ion
Exchange (L-SCIE) sorption database, which contains nearly 76,000 sorption data from the literature,
including about 2,500 clay sorption data from the ClaySor group, 35,000 from the Japanese Atomic
Energy Agency (JAEA), and additional data from other international efforts. For this study, the focus is

narrowed to 21 references involving purified montmorillonite or bentonite, resulting in a curated
dataset of 2,585 batch U(VI) sorption data points after quality screening. The data are standardized
using R scripts to ensure uniform units and metadata, and then formatted for input into the
CrunchFlow geochemical modeling engine. CrunchFlow, coupled with the PEST parameter estimation
tool, is used to iteratively optimize SC/IE reaction constants by minimizing the weighted residuals
between modeled and observed aqueous uranium concentrations. The modeling workflow allows for
the inclusion of various SC and IE reaction stoichiometries, enabling systematic testing of model
sensitivity to reaction selection and parameterization.

The initial approach tests the performance of the original BB05 model on both its own dataset and
the larger, global dataset. Subsequently, the study explores whether a reduced set of reaction
constants can adequately describe the global data by sequentially eliminating reactions with minimal
impact on model fit. The robustness of the optimized constants is evaluated by comparing global fits
to fits obtained from individual references and by assessing the effect of montmorillonite source
material on the fitted parameters.

RESULTS

The analysis reveals several key findings. First, when the original BB05 model is applied to the much
larger global dataset without parameter optimization, its performance is poor, highlighting the
limitations of using reaction constants derived from limited data. However, when the BB05 model is
refitted to the global dataset using PEST, the model’s performance improves substantially, and the
optimized reaction constants remain close to the original values, suggesting some degree of
transferability.

Through systematic model reduction, the study finds that only four reaction constants—three for
surface complexation (one strong site and two weak site reactions) and one for ion exchange—are
necessary to achieve a high-quality fit to the global dataset. Notably, the inclusion of ternary uranyl-
carbonate complexes does not improve model performance, indicating that such complexes are not
significant for U(VI) sorption to montmorillonite under the conditions studied, at least within the non-
electrostatic SC framework.

Comparisons between global fits and per-reference fits show that the optimized reaction constants
are statistically similar, with the global fit producing tighter confidence intervals due to the larger
volume of data. The weighted average of per-reference fits exhibits greater variability, reflecting the
influence of individual datasets. Importantly, the optimized reaction constants are not significantly
affected by the source of montmorillonite (e.g., Wyoming vs. Arizona), despite differences in mineral
properties such as cation exchange capacity and surface area. This suggests that fixing these
properties in the model is a reasonable approach when modeling a broad range of data.

The study also examines the impact of removing data points with large weighted residuals (potential
outliers or data with high uncertainty). While the overall model fit improves as these points are
excluded, the optimized reaction constants remain stable within two standard deviations,
underscoring the robustness of the parameterization. Overall, the results demonstrate that a
reduced, four-reaction SC/IE model can effectively simulate U(VI) sorption to montmorillonite across
a wide range of experimental conditions and literature sources. The approach also validates the use
of large, community-aggregated datasets and automated workflows for developing reliable,
transferable reaction constants for use in reactive transport modeling.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that integrating large, community-sourced sorption datasets using FAIR
data principles and automated modeling workflows enables the development of robust, transferable
surface complexation and ion exchange models for uranium sorption to montmorillonite. By
optimizing reaction constants across more than 2,500 data points from 21 references, the authors
show that a reduced set of four reaction constants can accurately simulate U(VI) sorption under
diverse conditions, independent of montmorillonite source material. The findings highlight the value
of open data and collaborative modeling in advancing the reliability of reactive transport models for
nuclear waste repository safety assessment. Future work should extend this approach to other
radionuclides and mineral systems to build comprehensive, self-consistent sorption databases for
environmental risk assessment and management.

5 PLANNED FY26 EFFORTS
Workscope for FY26 is being evaluated based on a shift in R&D program priorities that is now
underway. As such, we are anticipating that the FY26 effort will need to be adjusted based on these
program priorities. In the interim, LLNL is considering an effort to determine data gaps and needs in
both thermodynamics and sorption with regards to hot repository conditions and in coordination with
EURAD-II activities (DITUSC and RAMPEC). The US is presently considering the direct disposal of dual
purpose canisters in deep geologic repositories (DGRs). This path for radioactive waste disposal is
based on a “hot repository” concept which has not been investigated in as much detail as the “cold
repository” concepts moving forward in Europe. The “hot repository” concept will require a more
rigorous assessment of the knowledge gaps regarding the thermodynamic behavior of rocks,
minerals, and waste packages at temperature. LLNL and other national labs are presently engaged
in several EURAD-II efforts as Associate Partners. Two activities (DITUSC and RAMPEC) are directly
related to the assessment of thermodynamic database and experimental data limitations with
regards to addressing radionuclide reactive transport under “perturbed conditions”. In parallel, LLNL
has been the leading US national laboratory for development of thermodynamic and sorption
databases for nuclear waste repository modeling. Over the past several years, we have been
developing a workflow to improve the US thermodynamic modeling capability and develop a next
generation thermodynamic database based on international and publicly available data resources
(e.g. NEA-TDB, Thermochimie, PSI-DB, etc.). In FY26, we propose to collaborate and take advantage
of ongoing effort in the EURAD-II program and assess gaps in data availability and understanding for
radionuclide thermodynamics within the context of a “hot repository” concept. We will assess the
gaps in thermodynamic data availability and provide an assessment of their potential impacts on
repository concepts and performance.
