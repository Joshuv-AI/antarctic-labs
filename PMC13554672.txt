Mental Health and Smoking Cessation: Anxiety and Depression Among Participants in a Public Control Program in Southern Brazil
Authors: Kelser de Souza Kock, Maria Luiza Ataide Cardoso, Victória Pietsch, João Vithor Martins Dias, Cíntia dos Santos Fagundes Cardoso, João Felipe Demeneck Belen, Rodrigo Vasquez da Silva, Daniella Da Rosa Colares, Solange de Souza Castelini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmoking cessation programs are designed to support individuals in quitting tobacco and improving their health. Psychological factors, including anxiety and depression symptoms, can affect cessation outcomes, emphasizing the need to investigate their influence in public program settings. Objective is to assess how anxiety and depression symptoms influence therapeutic success in a public smoking cessation program and to identify associated clinical and behavioral factors.MethodsA longitudinal study was conducted from January to April 2025 with 80 participants enrolled in the Smoking Cessation Program of the municipality of Tubarão, Santa Catarina. The Fagerström test for nicotine dependence, Beck Anxiety Inventory, and Beck Depression Inventory were administered at baseline, 30, and 90 days. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and association tests (p < 0.05).ResultsThe participants showed a cessation rate of 40%; 26.3% of them dropped out, while 33.8% did not quit. The non-quitters were older (58.6±11.4 years) and had a higher tobacco exposure (47.7±25.3 pack years; p < 0.01) than the quitters (49.0±10.2 years; 29.5±18.6 pack years). The mean Fagerström score decreased among the quitters (0.05±0.2 vs. 2.5±1.1; p < 0.001). Higher anxiety at 90 days was associated with cessation failure (10.2 vs. 3.7; p < 0.001), whereas depression showed a similar but nonsignificant trend (p = 0.08).ConclusionAnxiety negatively affected cessation success, whereas depression showed a comparable pattern. Older age and greater tobacco exposure were associated with increased vulnerability. Thus, psychological assessments and multidisciplinary support in public cessation programs are essential.Keywords: Anxiety, Depression, Mental health, Smoking cessation, Tobacco

INTRODUCTION

The consumption of tobacco-derived products represents one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the current health scenario in Brazil [1]. Analyzing 2019 data from the National Cancer Institute (INCA), it is observed that smoking accounts for about 13% of all deaths occurring annually in the country [2]. In this context, 161,853 deaths were identified as directly caused by smoking, excluding indirect causes such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart disease, lung cancer, and passive smoking [2]. Concurrently, there is a substantial economic burden on the health system, including R$ 50.289 billion in direct medical costs (equivalent to 7.8% of total health expenditures), R$ 42.452 billion in indirect costs related to productivity losses due to premature death and disability, and R$ 32.400 billion associated with the care provided by family members and close individuals [2]. In this regard, it becomes evident that smoking, beyond being a public health issue, also affects the psychosocial and economic spheres of the country [3].

The consolidation or remission of an addiction is influenced both by objective aspects, such as the environment in which the individual is inserted, and by psychic aspects, such as socioemotional factors [3,4]. In Brazil, there are public policies that encourage smoking cessation, such as the National Smoking Cessation Program, implemented through Article 14 of the World Health Organization Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, which promotes treatment at various levels of health care, including pharmacological and psychotherapeutic approaches [5].

However, despite a relative increase in the demand for smoking cessation treatment in 2021, with 100,891 people seeking treatment according to DATASUS data, this initial decision depends on several variables for treatment success, such as psychological, motivational, and subjective individual factors [6,7]. This implies that the smoking habit is perceived as an escape valve for internal conflicts, as reported in the correlation between mental health and addiction to licit and illicit drugs in the scientific literature [7,8].

The understanding of dependence mechanisms within neural circuits—particularly in the limbic system and, more specifically, the nucleus accumbens—is linked to short- and long-term pleasure and reward, which considerably affect habit consolidation [7,9]. It is reported that patients diagnosed with anxiety and depressive disorders, as defined by diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders, fifth edition criteria, present alterations in this circuit, including in synaptic monoamines, through mechanisms that remain unknown [10,11]. Similarly, nicotine addiction also causes changes in the brain’s reward system [9]. Currently, Brazil presents high prevalence rates of anxiety and depressive disorders, which represent a significant burden in terms of disability and public health impact [12]. In this context, this may represent a variable that interferes with the success of treatment among patients seeking smoking cessation [13].

Clinical and psychometric instruments, such as the Fagerström test and Beck scales, have been recommended for assessing the degree of nicotine dependence and the presence of depressive and anxious symptoms in smokers [14-16]. The use of these tools as a screening method can improve the identification of individual vulnerabilities and optimize personalized therapeutic strategies, contributing to increased adherence rates and reduced relapse during the smoking cessation process.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A prospective cohort was conducted to evaluate the sociodemographic, clinical, and psychological profile of participants in the Smoking Cessation Program of the municipality of Tubarão, Santa Catarina, and to analyze the relationship between anxiety and depression symptoms and success in smoking cessation.

1. Procedures

All program participants, enrolled in the municipality’s primary health care units between January and April 2025, were contacted at the time of program enrollment. Initially, they received information about the program and signed the informed consent form. The program lasted 90 days, structured in weekly meetings in small groups. During these sessions, behavioral and pharmacological approaches were carried out, following the guidelines of the INCA and the Ministry of Health. Pharmacological therapy included nicotine gum and patches, in addition to bupropion, according to clinical evaluation by the multidisciplinary team.

Participants were followed in three stages: stage 1 – beginning of the program; stage 2 – 30 days after initiation; stage 3 – at the end of 90 days. At each stage, the same assessment instruments were applied, allowing observation of the individual evolution of psychological symptoms and success in smoking cessation.

The sampling was by convenience, composed of 80 participants. Patients aged 18 years or older, enrolled in the program, and who agreed to participate in the study were included. Participants who completed the questionnaires incompletely or presented missing data in more than 25% of the variables were excluded.

2. Measures

Stage 1 consisted of four instruments: (1) sociodemographic and clinical data (age, sex, use of medications, frequency of meetings, tobacco load, type of cigarette used, and date of smoking cessation in successful cases); (2) Fagerström test to assess nicotine dependence, categorized as mild (0-4), moderate (5-7), and severe (8-10); (3) Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) and Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), both with 21 items, classifying symptoms as minimal/mild or moderate/severe; (4) a qualitative questionnaire developed by the authors, addressing motivation for smoking cessation and the most commonly used type of cigarette.

Stages 2 and 3 consisted of reapplying the same instruments, allowing the follow-up of the evolution of psychological symptoms and smoking behavior throughout the program.

3. Statistical analysis

Data were entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft 365) and exported to the IBM SPSS 20.0 program (IBM Corp.). They were described as relative and absolute frequency or measures of central tendency and dispersion. To verify the occurrence of statistically significant differences in the association between variables, the Chi-square test was used for the comparison of proportions, and Student’s t-test or a nonparametric equivalent, as appropriate to the data, was used for differences in means. A significance level of 5% was considered.

4. Ethical statement

The project was approved by the National Research Ethics Commission, following the recommendations of CNS Resolutions No. 466/12 and/or 510/16, under protocol number 7.285.526.

RESULTS

The study analyzed data from 80 patients, with a higher participation of females (62.5%) and a mean age of 51.6 (±13.1) years. The most frequent comorbidities were psychiatric (42.5%), followed by cardiovascular (38.8%), respiratory (15.0%), and oncological (5.0%). The mean tobacco load was 39.5 (±25.4) pack-years. DISCUSSION

In summary, the study revealed that older age (p < 0.05) and higher tobacco load (p < 0.01) were predictors of a lower likelihood of smoking cessation. Although no initial difference was found in anxiety and depression indicators, by the third month, patients with higher anxiety levels showed lower success rates (p < 0.001). Depressive symptoms showed a similar trend but without statistical significance. These results suggest that clinical and emotional factors substantially influence adherence and therapeutic success.

These findings corroborate recent literature, which recognizes anxiety and depression as important negative predictors of smoking cessation. Longitudinal studies show that individuals with anxious symptoms have higher relapse rates, especially in the first weeks of abstinence, a period in which psychological and physiological discomfort is most intense [4,17,18]. Evidence indicates that anxiety is associated with lower smoking cessation success, even among individuals receiving structured follow-up [18].

The present study reinforces this association by demonstrating that patients with higher BAI scores had significantly lower cessation rates after three months of treatment (p < 0.001). This pattern suggests that anxiety not only interferes with initial motivation but also with the process of maintaining abstinence, possibly by intensifying craving symptoms and physiological stress responses [19]. Additional studies have observed a positive correlation between higher baseline anxiety levels and increased tobacco relapse, supporting the role of emotional self-regulation in therapeutic effectiveness [20].

Although depressive symptoms did not reach statistical significance in our study, a clear trend of poorer outcomes was observed among participants with higher BDI scores. This finding is consistent with literature identifying depression as a vulnerability factor, interfering with the persistence of abstinence and the management of nicotine withdrawal syndrome [21]. Nicotine, by modulating mesolimbic dopaminergic circuits, exerts a transient mood-regulating effect, and its withdrawal may exacerbate pre-existing depressive symptoms [9,11]. Thus, combined strategies involving pharmacotherapy and psychotherapeutic follow-up become essential to optimize adherence and reduce relapse [7,20].

The influence of age and tobacco load was also significant. Older patients with higher cumulative exposure (mean = 47.7 pack-years) had significantly lower cessation rates than younger participants (mean = 29.5 pack-years; p < 0.01). This finding is consistent with epidemiological evidence showing that chronic smokers develop more intense nicotine dependence and greater behavioral resistance to change [22]. Studies suggest that aging is associated with more persistent addictive behavior patterns and a reduced response to cessation interventions [22].

Another relevant result was the marked reduction in the mean Fagerström score among patients who quit smoking, especially at the end of the third month (0.05±0.2 vs. 2.5±1.1; p < 0.001), demonstrating the program’s effectiveness in modulating the level of nicotine dependence. This difference aligns with evidence indicating that the greatest decline in dependence occurs in the early phase of abstinence, during which continuous psychological support plays a decisive role in consolidating abstinent behavior [20].

The predominance of psychiatric comorbidities (42.5%) among participants also deserves attention, as it reinforces the vulnerability profile of this population. Studies demonstrate that smokers with psychiatric disorders have lower cessation rates and a higher propensity for early relapse [4]. The finding that anxiety was the main emotional factor associated with therapeutic failure suggests the importance of early psychological screening and multiprofessional follow-up throughout the process.

The reasons reported for attempting to quit smoking, health (31.25%), family and emotional factors (13.7% each), reflect motivational determinants widely described in the literature. Intrinsic motivation, such as concern for health, is more strongly associated with sustained abstinence than extrinsic reasons, such as social pressure [23]. This finding reinforces the role of medical counseling and educational campaigns in initial engagement, although emotional factors appear decisive for maintenance.

The integrated analysis of clinical and psychological data in this study reinforces the concept that the success of smoking cessation depends on the interaction between biological and emotional factors. Anxiety, acting as both a behavioral and physiological trigger, intensifies craving and reduces inhibitory control, perpetuating the cycle of dependence [18,19]. This bidirectional pattern between emotional symptoms and nicotine consumption is widely recognized, and interventions addressing both domains simultaneously have demonstrated superior results [4,21].

1. Limitations

Our study presents some limitations that should be considered. First, the sample size (n = 80) may have reduced statistical power to identify differences in subgroups, especially regarding depression. The follow-up period (three months) limits the analysis of late relapses. In addition, the use of self-report scales (BAI and BDI) may have introduced individual perception bias. Finally, the observational design does not allow causality to be established between anxiety, depression, and smoking cessation. Despite these limitations, the results provide consistent evidence convergent with recent studies.

2. Conclusion

In summary, our results demonstrate that high anxiety levels are significantly associated with lower smoking cessation success (p < 0.001), while depression shows a similar trend. Older age, higher tobacco load, and the presence of psychiatric comorbidities reinforce the clinical and emotional vulnerability profile of this population. These findings are particularly relevant within the context of Brazil’s Unified Health System, since public smoking cessation programs constitute a priority strategy for the prevention of chronic diseases. The integration of psychological interventions, with systematic screening for anxiety and depression and multiprofessional therapeutic support, can substantially increase the effectiveness of these programs, reduce healthcare costs, and strengthen tobacco control policies as a central component of primary health care.
