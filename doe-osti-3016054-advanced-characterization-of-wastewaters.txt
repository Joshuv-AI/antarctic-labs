1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This project, funded by the U.S. Department of Energy under award DE-FE0032457 and conducted at the
University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, focused on advancing the characterization of coal
combustion residual (CCR) effluents. The primary objectives were to develop analytical methods for
detecting major cations, anions, trace metals, and rare earth elements (REEs) in CCR wastewater, assess
environmental impacts, and explore opportunities for resource recovery. The project advanced the
development of a "One-Shot" analysis system designed to simultaneously analyze multiple analytes in
CCR effluents. Significant progress was made in integrating a sample separation and enrichment (SE)
system with in-line inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) analysis
enabling effective separation and concentration of target elements, particularly REEs. Ion
chromatography methods were established for anion detection, and hardware and software controls
were seamlessly integrated into the developed platform to enable unified operation. This configuration
facilitated high-resolution analysis and enrichment of multiple analytes in complex CCR effluent
matrices.

In parallel, fluorescence sensor arrays were developed for deployable, on-site REE detection using
reagents that respond to the presence of REEs, such as 8-hydroxyquinoline sulfate (HQS), which
demonstrated excellent detection limits for several REEs. Additionally, carbon dots (CDs) were
synthesized and characterized, showing tunable photoluminescence and selective responses to various
REEs under different conditions. Synthetic CCR effluent samples were prepared to simulate real
wastewater for method validation, supporting the robustness of the analytical approaches during
method development.

The project was successfully managed through ongoing collaboration with DOE project managers and
industry partners, ensuring coordinated progress and effective outreach. Although the project was
terminated early in October 2025 due to funding rescindment solely as a result of shifting DOE priorities,
the accomplishments achieved provide a strong foundation for future research in CCR effluent
characterization and treatment. The integrated SE and ICP-OES system, combined with fluorescence-
based REE detection techniques and carbon dot sensors, offers promising tools for environmental
monitoring and resource recovery from coal combustion wastewater. These advancements contribute
valuable knowledge and technology toward sustainable management of CCR effluents. It should also be
noted that this technology could also be translated to investigation of other materials, such as mining
wastewater, landfill leachates, and other aqueous materials where the potential for environmental
impact and/or resource recoveries are unknown.

2. PROJECT OUTLINE
a. Background
DE-FOA-0002796, titled “Water Research and Development for Oil and Gas Produced Water and Coal
Combustion Residuals Wastewater Associated with Coal Power Plants,” was made publicly available in
February 2023.

The funding opportunity outlined a potential for developing new technologies to economically extract
critical minerals and elements from waste streams produced in the generation of electrical power from
fossil fuels, mitigate the environmental impacts of wastewater, support job growth and equity, and
advance manufacturing capabilities and supply chains within the United States.

 4

Specifically, the following key research areas are addressed as areas of interest (AOI):
 • AOI 1: Characterizing, Managing, and Treating Oil/Natural Gas Produced Water – developing
 technologies for critical mineral and rare earth element recovery from produced water, including
 developing an understanding of the composition of the produced water to enable resource
 recovery.
 • AOI 2: Field Operation of Oil and Natural Gas Produced Water Consortium – supporting field
 operations to treat and reuse produced water.
 • AOI 3: Characterization and Treatment of Coal Combustion Residuals Wastewater – addresses
 technological development for critical mineral and rare earth element recovery and reduction of
 environmental impacts specifically from wastewater associated with coal power plants.

b. Major goals
Pursuant to the funding opportunity’s areas of interest, this project had two main objectives: (1) to
advance the characterization of coal combustion residual (CCR) effluents, and (2) illustrate the use of such
characterization to determine environmental impact and resource recovery.
The project goal is to advance the characterization of CCR effluents through:
 • Development of a one-shot analysis for the major cations, anions, trace metals, and rare earth
 elements in CCR effluents.
 • Development of a fluorimetry technique for rare earth elements prospecting in CCR effluents.
 • Development of a fluorescence sensor array for detecting and discriminating toxic metals in CCR
 effluents.
 • Evaluation of total x-ray fluorescence for multielement detection in CCR effluents.
 • Demonstration of the utility of these methods may be attained through:
 o Modeling the environmental impact of characterized CCR effluents on ground and surface
 water.
 o Examining design options for effluent treatment and resource recovery.

Goal 1 supports AOI 3 of DE-FOA-0002796: Characterization and Treatment of Coal Combustion Residuals
Wastewater Associated with Coal Power Generation for Groundwater Impact and Rare Earth Elements
(REEs), Critical Minerals (CMs), and Elements of Interest (EOIs). Goal 2 demonstrates the development’s
relevance to commercial applications.

c. Project termination
Cooperative Agreement DE-FE0032457 for this project between the Department of Energy (DOE) and the
University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign (the University) was established on September 1, 2024, and
terminated on October 10, 2025, in a unilateral decision from the DOE. The University was in good
standing with the DOE immediately preceding the termination, advancing all agreed-upon objectives in
accordance with the project’s stated objectives and timelines. All reporting had been completed with
punctuality. One week prior to termination, the University received positive feedback regarding the
project’s progress at the first annual review meeting wherein the University discussed all technical
progress made in the first year since award receipt.

Let it be so reflected that the rationale for termination was stated to be that “this project does not

Work to progress tasks 5.0 (one-shot analysis) and 6.0 (sample separation/enrichment) involved the
utilization of elemental standards of known, fixed composition to develop applicable methods. The
purpose of the synthetic CCR effluents is to be used to test the processes as they would be used for real

Task 5.0 One-Shot Analysis

 10

Instrumentation to be used in the one-shot analysis protocol includes an inductively coupled plasma
optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES), an ion chromatography (IC) system, and a standard fraction
collector.

The purpose of this task is to develop a “One-Shot” panel of instrumentation to facilitate a single, cost-
effective, comprehensive analysis of complex samples with both high- and low-level concentrations of
target analytes compared to current practices which involve a time-intensive and worker-demanding set
of sequential analyses using multiple analyses over multiple instruments. The “One-Shot” system will
include an IC system to measure anion concentrations, an ICP-OES to measure cations, and the separation
system defined in Task 6.0 will be used to selectively concentrate low-level rare earth elements to
concentrations that can be more accurately measured.

As a whole, design considerations were implored to account for both known and potential analytical
requirements from real or synthetic CCR wastewaters pertaining to the relative expected analyte
concentrations, whether the analytes will be further enriched in the SE system, and the analytical order
required for success in the “One-Shot” analysis system. The proposed schematic is depicted in Figure 1
below. The proposed schematic covers a wide range of possible analytical requirements including direct
sample analyses both in- and off-line, in-line dilution, separation and enrichment, off-line fraction
collection, and in-line analysis of the generated fractions with at-will use determination.

Figure 1. Updated “One-Shot” analysis system schematic proposal to include all targeted equipment and
 instruments.

Exploration into the precise mechanism of inter-software communication was also initiated, probing
potential avenues for integration of the various operations into a unified control suite. Contact with the
manufacturing company of the analytical instruments was made to gain further insight.

ICP-OES analysis of multi-element and single-element standards was performed to identify ideal
wavelengths and detection limit determinations for elements of interest. The ICP-OES was also utilized to
progress the characterization of the ion-exchange columns utilized in the separation/enrichment system

 11

detailed in Task 6.0. Additional work with the ICP-OES included establishing inter-element corrections
(IEC) for analytes which have interfering signals. The purpose of the separation/enrichment system is to
separate analytes into fractions and concentrate them to a detectable level, though, complete separation
of all interfering elements from each other is not feasible in complex samples. Applying the integrated
software mathematical corrections to potentially conflicting signals allows for prevention of false positive
or negative concentrations for analyte analysis. A series of calibrations and single-element standards have
been evaluated to apply these corrections for optimal analysis when analyzing complex mixtures of
analytes, such as coal combustion residues.

Integration of the separation and enrichment system (task 6.0) with in-line ICP-OES analysis as intended
to comprise one-half of the larger “One-Shot” analysis system goal was successful through simultaneous
control via LabVIEW programming.

Investigative work into the function and requirements of the ion chromatography (IC) system – the other
half of the “One-Shot” analysis system – was initiated to best determine an effective means of integration
with the separation/enrichment (SE) and ICP-OES analysis. Specifically, method development to
determine the optimal analytical conditions for anion determination of analytes anticipated to be within
coal combustion residuals (CCR) wastewater was performed. Linear dynamic ranges were determined for
seven analytes: fluoride, chloride, nitrite, bromide, sulfate, nitrate, and phosphate. The linear dynamic
range represents the range of concentration at which analysis would be both accurate and reliable. Since
the anion fraction of the CCR wastewater is not concentrated in the SE system, understanding the
instrumental limits is essential to determine any in-line dilution or sequential analyses which may need to
be implemented for accurate quantification of the target analytes, as well as inform the exact order of
analytical steps in the “One-Shot” analysis system. Specific considerations to the reported concentration
of certain analytes from the EPRI CPINFO database were made, and method adjustments were
investigated to maintain the analytical integrity of each analyte with scaling concentrations. Specifically,
some analytes are anticipated to be in a range of concentrations much higher than other analytes, and
the method design must ensure that the analytes do not interfere with each other as well as fall within
the linear dynamic range for accurate results.

Continuation of this task would require further development of the IC method to secure the analysis of
anions at target concentration ranges, obtaining and implementing the required number of valves and
pumps, integrating the additional hardware into the LabVIEW control program, and establishing inter-
software communication.

Task 6.0 Sample Separation/Enrichment
The purpose of this task is to develop a protocol to selectively concentrate target analytes within the
samples to achieve a detectable level, while removing any potentially interfering elements. An established
system of ion-exchange resin columns proven to be effective in the separation of lanthanide metals from
transition metals, alkali metals, and alkaline earth elements were selected for further study and
improvement. However, explicit documentation reporting the specific use applications of these columns
is limited to selective concentration of few target analytes of historical interest. Given the wide scope of
interest in compositional data of CCR effluent samples and the lack of availability of data on the
concentration columns separation capacity for the entire range of elements of interest in this project,
reference materials were utilized to determine separation capacities of these columns prior to their
incorporation in the “one-shot” analysis scheme.

 12

A series of 6-port valves and pumps were utilized to control sample and eluent flow through the system
of ion-exchange resin columns. LabVIEW was implemented to program all of the components of the
system. Full control of the separation and enrichment system was successfully demonstrated, and
enrichment factors were reported to the DOE project manager to verify milestone completion on August
31, 2025. Continued improvements to the LabVIEW code to control all of the components were made to
allow for a more flexible application during the method development.

The separation/enrichment (SE) system successfully proved capacities for both effective separation and
enrichment of target elements. A series of eluents is used within the SE system to generate collectable
“fractions” containing a specific selection of elements depending on their interactions with the separating
columns and eluents themselves. The elements which are not retained by the first separation column are
not concentrated and therefore exhibit a relatively lower enrichment factor (less than 2.0), as seen in Table
4. In some cases, the elements are not eluted – either in part or in whole – and require special processes
to remove them, or that the column be replaced over time to prevent negative consequences on the
desired separation and enrichment outcomes. This may result in inconsistent EFs for these elements
compared to effectively recovered elements since elements which do not fully elute may exhibit
concentration to a lesser extent than elements which readily elute from the columns, but still more than
elements which are not retained for concentration at all.

The EFs were seen to be inconsistent between elements, as a result of several additional factors, but likely
can largely be attributed to the manner in which the EFs were calculated by utilizing the analytical
timepoint of maximum measured concentration during the on-line analysis relative to the concentration
of the feed solution measured before concentration. Due to differences in the interactions between the
analytes, eluents, and column material, some elements elute from the column over a longer period of
time which reduces the overall concentration effect as it will take a larger volume of eluent to fully remove
the element and therefore have a lower relative concentration maximum compared to elements which
elute more readily. An example of this behavior can be seen in Figure 4 below, with Aluminum reflected
as a lower, broader peak which extends over a longer period of time than the Zinc or Copper which have
higher EFs. Collection and measurement of individual fractions containing the entirety of the concentrated
target elements for off-line analysis would mitigate this but may also result in lower overall EFs due to a
potentially larger fraction volume required for collection to encompass all target elements compared to
the minimized volume per analytical measurement of the on-line analysis.

Another source of potential discrepancies of the EFs relates to the analytical complications of quantifying
elements in multiple different matrices using a single external calibration curve with the current
configuration of the analytical instrument not having the capacity to apply the necessary corrections, and
potential for an apparently heightened concentration of common contaminating elements originating
from within the system or in the preparation of the eluents.

The next step of this task would be to apply the SE system to a panel of standards of known concentrations
to establish the enrichment capacity for varying loads of elements, as well as to establish the system’s
capacity for consistent and reproducible enrichments. Future investigations into possible improvements
to EFs will also need to be conducted to ensure that analytes are concentrated as effectively as possible.

Task 9.0 REE Prospecting in CCR Effluents
The objective of this task is to develop a fluorimetry-based technique for rare earth element (REE)
prospecting in coal combustion residual (CCR) effluents. Fluorescent metal sensors typically consist of two
key components: a metal-chelating or binding moiety and at least one fluorophore capable of absorbing
and emitting light. For a sensor to function effectively, metal binding must induce changes in either the
electronic or molecular structure of the sensor. Alterations in electronic structure can result in shifts in
light absorption or emission intensity or wavelength, while structural changes may affect the distance or
orientation between fluorophores acting as a donor–acceptor pair. Two common mechanisms by which
metals modulate fluorescence are energy transfer and electron transfer between the metal ion and the
photoexcited fluorophore. These interactions can result in either a “turn-off” or “turn-on” fluorescence
response, depending on whether fluorescence is quenched or enhanced. Selecting an appropriate
fluorescent sensor is a critical first step in the detection and quantification of metal ions. Such sensors may
function as integrated receptor-signaling systems or as two-component assemblies comprising separate
chelating and sensing units.

Spectrofluorimetric methods are customarily restricted to analyzing one element at a time. However, if a
fluorophore with an affinity to a class of elements is used, then a “class-specific sensor” is realized. The
compound 8-hydroxyquinoline-5-sulfonic acid (HQS) is known to form a complex with numerous metals
while at the same time producing strong fluorescence response during the chelation. Given the
properties of HQS, this compound was selected as the first fluorescent probe to explore as a REE class
sensor at 1 mM concentration.

Sixteen different REEs were complexed with HQS under separate pH environments ranging from 4-10 to
determine the optimal pH, since fluorescent responses of fluorophore-REE complex can vary with pH of
the solution. These 16 elements included: Scandium (Sc), Yttrium (Y), Lanthanum (La), Cerium (Ce),
Praseodymium (Pr), Neodymium (Nd), Samarium (Sm), Europium (Eu), Gadolinium (Gd), Terbium (Tb),
Dysprosium (Dy), Holmium (Ho), Erbium (Er), Thulium (Tm), Ytterbium (Yb), and Lutetium (Lu). Of the 16
REE tested, 8 produced readily detectable fluorescent complexes with HQS at a high concentration (100
ppm).

Since the pH environment has a significant impact on the fluorescence response of HQS complexes, the
interferents were also tested over the same range as REE experiments. Ideally, there could be a pH
where the REE complex is maximized while the range of interference could be minimized. Strontium,
Rubidium, Manganese, and Calcium exhibited a minimal fluorescence response when complexed with
HQS at pH 6, the optimal fluorescence response for REEs. However, Magnesium, Aluminum, and Zinc
exhibited relatively high fluorescence responses at pH 6. The maximum signal intensity at the optimal pH
is seen with Aluminum, with an observed excitation at 360 nm and emission at 493 nm. These results
suggest that interfering elements could pose a potential risk for competitive complexing with REEs at the
optimal pH and must be considered when developing further rapid tests for field samples. However, it
should be noted that the concentration used to test for aluminum interference far exceeded what is
typically observed in CCR effluents (average 0.58 mg/L, max. 8.5 mg/L). This was also the case with
Magnesium and Zinc. In further development work with HQS, the analysis of interference will be
evaluated at concentrations that are environmentally relevant to CCR effluents. The ion-exchange resin
used to separate lanthanides from the other constituents could also aid in overcoming this challenge
(Task 6.0).

Standard preparation methods for HQS and REE complexes were developed. Stock solutions of REEs
were prepared at concentrations of 4 ppb, 400 ppb, and 40 ppm. 2.0 mM HQS was prepared so the
resulting concentration after dilution would be 1.0 mM. The HQS-REE mixture is optimized to pH 6
before the addition of DI water to the final volume. HQS-REE triplicates were prepared at a range of 1-
1000 ppb, then analyzed by the Aqualog 2D spectra within 30 minutes of preparation and evaluated at
their intensity at 500 nm.

The as-synthesized CDs consisted of a mixture of multiple species exhibiting distinct excitation-emission
profiles. CDs_1-CDs_8 and CDs_14 showed excitation-dependent emission behavior, while CDs_9-CDs_13,

detection from CCR effluents, separation of the desired CDs species is necessary. Prior to purification, the

as REE sensing materials.

was adjusted using either acetate buffer solution or HEPES buffer solution. The photoluminescence (PL) of
