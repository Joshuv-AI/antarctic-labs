ABSTRACT
This report presents analysis from Sandia National Laboratories predicting contributions to risk
associated with the use of hydrogen technology for rail. Event sequence diagrams are used to
describe possible accident scenarios and progressions. Initiating event frequencies and branch event
probabilities for each scenario are quantified with uncertainty using distributions fit to Federal
Railroad Administration and U.S. Department of Transportation Pipeline and Hazardous Materials
Safety Administration data on applicable accidents from 2000 to 2020. Uncertainty is propagated
through the event sequence diagram to estimate the frequency and conditional probability of
accident end states. The analysis identifies four scenarios with significant contributions to risk from
hydrogen that are predicted to occur relatively frequently, which may inform priorities for reducing
uncertainty. These scenarios are 1) overpressure events resulting from collisions with hydrogen
release due to mechanical damage and delayed ignition, 2) jet fire events resulting from collisions
with hydrogen release due to mechanical damage and immediate ignition, 3) jet fires resulting from
fire or explosion initiating events involving the hydrogen tank and correct operation of the
thermally-activated pressure relief device (TPRD) subsequent to the thermal insult, and 4) pressure
burst resulting from fire or explosion initiating events involving the hydrogen tank and failure of the
TPRD. Delayed and immediate hydrogen ignition probabilities are identified as being highly
uncertain and potential candidates for reducing conservatism in the predicted frequencies for these
two scenarios.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
There are unknown risks with the use of hydrogen technology for rail which require further study to
inform safety recommendations. This report documents analysis of some of the potential
contributions to risk from accidents during the operation of hydrogen powered rail vehicles.
Because there are significant uncertainties in this analysis, the results are useful for prioritizing
research gaps. The risk results are dominated by ignition frequency uncertainty, so this research
identifies a need for additional research around ignition. Only contributions from hydrogen as the
fuel source for trains are considered; contributions from hydrogen freight transportation as a
commodity are not included in the analysis.
The first step in assessing hydrogen risk for rail was to determine the data quality and availability to
inform risk calculations. A data analysis was performed which identified data in an FRA database
(Federal Railroad Administration Office of Safety Analysis, 2021) and PHMSA (U.S. Department of
Transportation Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration, 2022) database of high
quality and completeness to inform many of the factors in risk calculations. The data used in this
analysis cover the time period from 2000 to 2020. Some additional factors for risk calculations were
established in previous work from smaller data sets (Ehrhart, Brooks, Muna, & LaFleur, 2020).
The data analysis was also used to identify the most common types of accidents for freight and
passenger rail. Accidents were grouped into two types: accidents initiated by collisions and accidents
initiated by fire or explosion events. Three categories of accidents were identified for the collision
accidents: derailments, train-train collisions, and train-other object collisions. Derailments are the
most common accident type for freight rail, whereas train-other collisions are the most common
accident type for passenger rail.
Event sequence diagrams (ESDs) were constructed to describe the possible progression of rail
accidents to consequences that could be expected from hydrogen trains. Risk calculations were then
performed using these ESDs to assess the relative frequencies of different hydrogen-induced
consequences. The risk calculations were performed as part of an uncertainty analysis, in which
simple random sampling (sample size of 100,000) was used to propagate uncertainty in the initiating
event frequencies and event probabilities through the ESDs. This approach incorporates uncertainty
into the risk estimates.
The uncertainty distributions that were sampled for this uncertainty analysis were either fit to data
from the FRA database or the PHMSA database or were taken directly from previous studies based
on data in the literature. These different data sets vary in applicability, quality, and completeness.
The FRA database, which was used to derive distributions on initiating event frequencies, is the
most applicable and complete data set, so there is higher confidence in those distributions. The
distribution derived from the PHMSA database represents the probability that the hydrogen tank is
involved given there is a fire. However, data for precisely this quantity was not available, so the
distribution was approximated from data on fire events with information on release. Distributions
obtained from previous work on the delayed or immediate ignition probabilities and the thermally-
activated pressure relief device (TPRD) operation probability are derived from lower quality data
with high uncertainty.
The results are presented using empirical cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) to describe
scenario and end state frequencies or probabilities, assuming the initiating event has occurred.
Comparison between scenarios for collision initiating events indicates that the most likely accident
progression does not include significant contribution to consequences from hydrogen. However,

there are two accident sequences with significant contributions to consequences from hydrogen that
are also estimated to occur with a higher relative frequency. These are 1) overpressure events
resulting from collisions with hydrogen release due to mechanical damage and delayed ignition, and
2) jet fire events resulting from collisions with hydrogen release due to mechanical damage and
immediate ignition. Two accident progressions with significant contributions to risk from hydrogen
for fire or explosion initiating event scenarios are also estimated to occur with a higher relative
frequency. These are 1) jet fires resulting from fire or explosion initiating events involving the
hydrogen tank and correct operation of the TPRD subsequent to the thermal insult, and 2) pressure
burst resulting from fire or explosion initiating events involving the hydrogen tank and failure of the
TPRD. The immediate and delayed ignition probabilities are significant factors for these collision
scenarios and are highly uncertain.
This analysis identified scenarios that may warrant further investigation into risks related to
hydrogen rail operation. The risks were calculated incorporating high uncertainty in some factors
affecting accident progression. Reducing uncertainty in these factors may change the relative
significance of hydrogen consequence events or reduce their estimated frequency. Frequency results
reported here are based on the full population of trains and incidents, which means the frequency
values would imply that every single train in the country was using hydrogen. This is an extremely
conservative assumption based on the overall train incident population, and would not apply to early
stage introduction of hydrogen trains in which there would be few hydrogen trains.
This analysis focused on the contribution to risk due to the presence of hydrogen as a fuel, but this
does not translate directly into increased risk to the public. End states with little or no contribution
to risk due to hydrogen may still be highly dangerous or damaging, such as a train crash with the
locomotive not involved. Conversely, the same hydrogen-involved end state may have variable
effects based on the direction of the leak and what people or buildings are nearby. A jet fire pointed
straight up into the air may harm no one, while the same jet fire directed at an occupied building
may be highly dangerous. These outcomes in which hydrogen does not contribute to risk can be
highly specific to individual crash scenarios and incidents, and so are not considered here.
The most likely end state for fire or explosion events is a jet fire and the least likely end state is a
pressure burst. If every train was powered by hydrogen and incidents were to continue at the same
rate, jet fires are expected to occur multiple times a year and pressure bursts are expected to occur
once every ten years, based on the distribution medians. This is an extremely conservative
assumption based on the overall train incident population, and would not apply to early stage
introduction of hydrogen trains in which there would only be a small number. The probabilities for
a jet fire outcome given a fire or explosion are fairly high. Further refinement of the tank
involvement probability and the TPRD failure probability may be needed to clarify if these high
probabilities are due to conservative assumptions or not. Operating data or component testing could
be used to evaluate whether the current TPRD failure probabilities are reasonable or overly
conservative. Operating data could clarify whether jet fires or overpressures are more likely to result
from hydrogen ignition. Requirements to improve crashworthiness and reduce the frequency of
crashes in the first place would also reduce the risk from hydrogen fuels.
This analysis utilized existing data, none of which was derived from hydrogen locomotive operation.
As a result, some of the probabilities in the risk calculations are proxies, which are likely
conservative but inaccurate. More information about hydrogen locomotive designs and operation
can inform whether the conservative assumptions made here are warranted or not.

1. INTRODUCTION
This report presents research by Sandia National Laboratories conducted from January 2021 to
September 2023 for the Federal Railroad Administration (FRA) to assess possible post-crash
outcomes for rail accidents involving hydrogen-fueled trains. This report summarizes accident
scenario development, data analysis, uncertainty quantification, and estimation of scenario risks for
hydrogen-fueled trains. The results of the analysis identify research priorities for reducing
uncertainty around additional risks related to implementation of hydrogen trains. In particular,
hydrogen ignition probabilities were identified as an important research gap.

1.1. Background
There are unknown risks to the use of hydrogen technology for rail which require further study to
inform safety recommendations. FRA has contracted with Sandia National Laboratories to support
in developing knowledge and understanding on the use of hydrogen fuel in rail environments.

1.2. Objectives
The objective of the project is to investigate the safety and performance of advanced energy
technologies used for rail. This includes identification of gaps in existing knowledge and safety
standards with recommendation for resolving these gaps. The task documented in this report is the
analysis of crash scenarios and assessment of post-crash outcomes for passenger and freight. The
task objectives were to:
 • Develop an event sequence diagram (ESD) with uncertainty quantification for release of
 hydrogen for both freight and passenger rail
 • Focus analysis on the additional hazard (if any) from gaseous or liquid hydrogen fuel

1.3. Overall Approach
Accident scenarios were developed using ESD informed by FRA data and previous research on
hydrogen contributions to risk for cars in tunnels (Glover, Baird, & LaFleur, 2020; Ehrhart, Brooks,
Muna, & LaFleur, 2020). Scenarios were further defined based on incident information in the FRA
database (Federal Railroad Administration Office of Safety Analysis, 2021). The FRA database was
analyzed to develop uncertainty distributions for the probability of each event in the event sequence
diagrams. Events not covered by the database were assigned distributions based on either a PHMSA
database (U.S. Department of Transportation Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety
Administration, 2022) or literature values (Ehrhart, Brooks, Muna, & LaFleur, 2020). Once all events
were populated with probability distributions, simple random samples were taken for each event
probability and multiplied for each scenario, resulting in an uncertainty distribution on the
probability of the scenario assuming an accident. Accident frequency distributions were also
obtained via analysis of the FRA database and these distributions were multiplied by the scenario
probability distributions to get distributions on the scenario frequencies. Comparison of these
distributions across scenarios demonstrates the overall contribution to risk from hydrogen. Leak
scenarios for different leak sizes and hydrogen states were modeled to compare potential unignited
and ignited scenario outcomes.

 15

1.4. Scope
The analysis documented in this report considers freight and passenger rail. The types of accidents
included were:
 • Derailments on mainlines
 • Collisions between trains
 • Collisions between trains and other objects (e.g., automobiles)
 • Fires or explosions on any track type

1.5. Organization of the Report
This report first presents analysis of data that were used to further define the problem, scope and
structure of the analysis in Section 2. The construction of the risk model and risk calculation details
are provided in Section 3. Uncertainty was incorporated in the risk calculation using uncertainty
distributions for each factor in the calculation. These distributions and the data informing them are
presented in Section 4. Section 5 presents the resulting risk calculations and analysis by scenario
(Section 5.1) and by end state (Section 5.2). Together, these analyses identify the most concerning
accident progressions for hydrogen trains and the risk factors that may warrant further
investigations. These conclusions are discussed in Section 5.1.8 and 5.2.12. Modeling comparisons of
potential unignited and ignited end states are provided in Section 7. Overall conclusions are
presented in Section 8.

2. DATA ANALYSIS
Risk quantification relies on the amount and quality of available data for describing the frequencies
of key events that define consequential scenarios. The first challenge in quantifying this risk was to
explore the available data to see which events could be quantified and which events presented
knowledge gaps. This activity co-occurred with development of the ESDs (Section 3) which describe
the progression of potential accidents. The level of detail in the data determines the level of
refinement in the ESD. Two databases were used to assess the state-of-knowledge, one from the
FRA and one from the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA). This
section discusses each of the databases and presents summary statistics of the information from the
databases that was used to create and populate the ESDs for risk quantification.

2.1. FRA Database
The FRA maintains a rich database of rail data (Federal Railroad Administration Office of Safety
Analysis, 2021), which informed the ESD structure, accident frequencies, and many of the
probabilities for the ESD. Analysis of the data showed a general trend toward decreasing accidents
over many decades for most accident types. Because of this downward trend, using all years of data
in the database (since 1975) would be highly conservative and potentially misleading. Instead, data
from 2000 to 2020 was used. This provides enough data points to estimate annual frequencies
without being overly conservative. There is still a trend towards decreasing accidents within this time
period, so the frequencies are likely still somewhat conservative for predicting future events.
The numbers of freight rail accidents are plotted in Figure 2-1 and the numbers of passenger rail
accidents for these years are plotted in Figure 2-2. The accidents are categorized by type using the
accident types described in the database. Derailments were kept as a single category, whereas the
other accident categories are groupings of accident types. Train-Train collisions were defined to
include all accidents described as head-on, rear-end, side, raking, or broken train. Train-other
collisions were defined to include all accidents described as highway-rail crossing, railroad grade
crossing, obstruction, or other impacts. Fire or explosion accidents were defined to include all
accidents described as explosive-detonation or fire/violent rupture. These categories were chosen to
group accidents together that, up to the resolution of the data, would be expected to have similar
outcomes.

2.2. PHMSA Database
The FRA database contains sufficient information to inform most of the ESD and event
frequencies. However, the FRA database does not have enough information on hazardous materials
release to estimate the probability that a fire involves the hydrogen tank. This is a critical component
in the analysis because the safety concern with hydrogen is its potential release and flammability;
thermal exposure necessitating operation of the thermally-activated pressure relief device (TPRD) is
one potential release mechanism.
The most applicable data found to inform hydrogen tank involvement given a fire was collected by
PHMSA. PHMSA collects data on the incidence of accidents, including rail accidents, involving
hazardous materials. Though this data is not specific to hydrogen, it includes a broad range of
hazardous materials (e.g., methanol, liquefied petroleum gas, hydrochloric acid, petroleum products)
and could be down selected to rail. Hydrogen tends to be contained in thick-walled pressure vessels
(for gaseous hydrogen) or vacuum-insulated tanks (for liquid hydrogen). This means the data may
lead to estimates for hydrogen release that are conservative or contain high uncertainty. Data from
this database for the years between 2000 and 2020 were used, from which yearly data on fire or heat
events and fire and heat releases were extracted.
The probability of a hydrogen tank being involved in a fire event was estimated by the number of
events in which fire or heat caused a release divided by the total number of fire and heat events. This
is not precisely the correct probability because there may have been fire events that affected the
tanks but did not cause a release. However, given lack of data specifically on whether fires resulted
in thermal insults to hazardous materials tanks and high reliability of TPRDs, this is a reasonable
proxy; a fire certainly resulted in a thermal insult to a hazardous material tank if it resulted in a
release.

3. RISK MODEL
The risk model for this analysis is implemented as an ESD. ESDs describe the sequences of
dependent events that define different accident progressions. Each accident progression results in an
end state, which is the consequence environment for that scenario. Events are included in the event
sequence diagram if they contribute to defining this consequence environment.
The ESDs for this analysis are based on previous work done on the hydrogen contribution to risk
for automobile accidents in tunnels (Glover, Baird, & LaFleur, 2020; Ehrhart, Brooks, Muna, &
LaFleur, 2020). One of the aims of the data analysis (Section 2) was to explore how the ESD defined
in that previous work could be modified to describe rail accidents. Iteration between the data
analysis and risk model construction resulted in the ESDs in Figure 3-1 and Figure 3-2. Figure 3-1
describes accidents that involve a mechanical impact such as derailment, collision between two
trains, or collisions between a train and other objects. Figure 3-2 describes accidents that initiate
with a fire or explosion rather than impact.

The ESDs are read from left to right, following the chain of events that lead to each end state. For
example, a collision that involves the locomotive, where the hydrogen is used but does not release
hydrogen via mechanical insult and does not cause a fire is mapped to end state A0 through the ESD
in Figure 3-1. This is the accident progression following the top branch at each event in the ESD
from left to right. The end states are colored by the hydrogen contribution to their consequences.
Green (superscript 0) indicates no increased risk due to the presence of hydrogen, yellow (*)
indicates a jet fire risk due to the presence of hydrogen, and red (**) indicates a pressure burst or
overpressure event risk due to the presence of hydrogen.
Accidents resulting in end states A0 (no release), C0 (unignited dispersion), F0 (no release), G0 (no
release), or J0 (no release) see no increased risk from hydrogen. Accidents resulting in end states E*
(jet fire), H* (jet fire), or K* (jet fire) see a moderately increased consequence from hydrogen.
Accidents resulting in end states D** (overpressure), I** (pressure burst), or L** (pressure burst) are
the most concerning because these are the events that pose the highest potential consequences from
hydrogen.
This analysis focused on the contribution to risk due to the presence of hydrogen as a fuel used in
the locomotive, but this does not translate directly into increased risk to the public. End states with
little or no contribution to risk due to hydrogen may still be highly dangerous or damaging, such as a
train crash with the locomotive not involved. Conversely, the same hydrogen-involved end state may
have variable effects based on the direction of the leak and what people or buildings are nearby. A
jet fire pointed straight up into the air may harm no one, while the same jet fire directed at an
occupied building may be highly dangerous. These outcomes can be highly specific to individual
crash scenarios and incidents, and so are not considered here.
Figure 3-1 contains two ESDs. This is because end state B (external fire) is the only progression in
this ESD for which the collision results in a fire, as opposed to end states D** (overpressure) and
E* (jet fire), in which the collision itself does not cause a fire but released hydrogen ignites. This
progression can only be mapped to a consequence by including additional events which are not
relevant for end states A0 (no release), C0 (unignited dispersion), D** (overpressure), E* (jet fire),
and F0 (no release). For simplicity, end state B is a transfer gate; frequencies or probabilities from
end state B are the initiating frequencies or probabilities for the second ESD in Figure 3-1. This is
mathematically equivalent to including hydrogen tank involvement and TPRD operation in the top
ESD, but significantly simplifies the ESD visually.
Figure 3-2 contains one simple ESD for events that initiate with a fire. These events follow the same
progression as events in Figure 3-1 that involve fire given a collision. The difference is in the
initiating frequency. The initiating frequency for Figure 3-2 is the frequency of fire/explosion events.

Because the initiating event frequencies and probabilities in the ESDs are not precisely known, the
frequencies and probabilities of the end states were calculated incorporating uncertainty. This was
done by defining uncertainty distributions for each event in the ESD (Section 4). Each of these
distributions was sampled independently using simple random sampling and the calculations for
each end state were repeated for each sample. By repeating the end state calculations many times
with different values for the likelihood of each event, a distribution of end state frequencies is
obtained. This describes the range of possible frequencies based on uncertainty or variation in the
likelihood of each event in the ESD. These results are presented in Section 5.

4. UNCERTAINTY QUANTIFICATION
As stated in Section 3, the frequencies and probabilities for the ESD events are not precisely known,
so uncertainty distributions were used to propagate the uncertainty in these values through to the
end states. All applicable events from the data sources were counted for each year from 2000 to
2020. For frequencies, these annual counts are the data points. For probabilities, the data point for
that year is calculated as the number of applicable events for that year divided by the total number of
events for that year. For example, if there were 100 derailment events in a year and 20 of those
events involved a locomotive, then the data point from that year for the frequency of derailment
events would be 100. The data point from that year for the probability of locomotive involvement
given a derailment would be 20/100 = 0.2.
The uncertainty distributions in this analysis describe the current state-of-knowledge for each split in
the ESD based on existing and available data. Some splits, such as whether the locomotive is
involved in the accident, are not expected to differ for hydrogen rail unless the number of
locomotives required for hydrogen rail is different. Other splits in the ESD, such as the probability
of a mechanical release of hydrogen, are being estimated with data points that are not hydrogen-
specific. Gaseous hydrogen is stored in thick-walled pressure vessels, while liquid hydrogen is stored
in vacuum-jacked tank cars. These proxy uncertainty distributions are likely conservative compared
to what would be expected from hydrogen rail, but cannot be estimated more accurately with
currently available data.
A notional distribution for a probability is shown in Figure 4-1. The probability on the horizontal
axis is the true probability of the event, which is not known precisely. However, there is enough
evidence about the probability to describe what values it could have, and which of those values are
most likely. The curve describes this distribution of possible values using the concept of subjective
probability. The cumulative probability on the vertical axis is the subjective probability, given the
available data, that the true value of the event probability is less than the corresponding horizontal
axis value. It is a statement about which values of the event probability are most supported by the
evidence. For example, the point at (0.001, 0.5) is the median of this distribution; that means the
cumulative (subjective) probability that the event probability is less than 0.001 is 0.5. This point can
be interpreted as saying that half of the data supports a value of the event probability less than 0.001
and half of the data supports a value of the event probability greater than 0.001. The 5th and 95th
percentiles are also shown in the plot. These bound the region of the distribution supported by most
of the evidence.

There was one event probability in the ESD for which a different method of distribution fitting was
required. There was applicable data for the probability of a fire given a passenger train-train

is not effective for this case. Instead, a beta(0.5, 0.5) distribution was assumed for the prior and
updated with the total number of passenger train-train collisions (89) and the total number of
passenger train-train collisions resulting in a fire (0) using the formula in (Siu & Kelly, 1998) (see
Section 4.4.5). The beta(0.5, 0.5) distribution, also called the Jeffrey’s prior, is a commonly used
uninformed prior distribution because it assumes no knowledge outside of the data used to fit the
distribution.

presented for each of the analyzed scenarios.
The initiating events in the ESDs are quantified using distributions of frequencies; the events which
occur after the initiating event are quantified as distributions of probabilities dependent upon the
previous events in the ESD. Because many continuous distributions are defined on (−∞, 0) or
(−∞, ∞), which include unrealistic frequencies, the fitted distributions for frequency were truncated
below by the maximum taken between the lowest data point and 0.001. This lower limit was chosen
based on the highest precision that could be obtained from any annual estimate in the FRA
database. Generally, the data were on the order of hundreds to thousands of applicable data points
or less, so 0.001 is the highest precision any data point could have. However, there were some years
for which the estimated probability of an event was zero. In order to use these data points without
artificially giving them higher precision than is warranted, the lower truncation limit of 0.001 is used,
even if there are data points at zero. Similarly, an upper truncation limit of 1000 was used. This is
well above the highest estimated frequency in the data and avoids sampling far in the upper tails of
distributions with infinite support. This constrains the frequencies to reasonable values.
Similarly, probabilities are only defined on the interval [0, 1] whereas most distributions can be
defined including values outside this interval. These distributions obtained from FRA or PHMSA
data were similarly truncated below by 0.001 and were truncated above by 1.0. The distributions
obtained from previous work (Section 4.5 and Section 4.7) were not truncated, since they already
limit sampling to (0, 1).
Because truncation can affect the distribution fit, plots are provided comparing the empirical
distribution of the data with the fitted distribution for each quantity in the ESDs.

4.2. Locomotive Involvement
This section presents the fitted distributions for the probability that a locomotive is involved given
that an accident has occurred. These are scenario-dependent probabilities, meaning that the
probability of locomotive involvement depends on the type of accident. Additionally, lower and
upper probabilities were defined for locomotive involvement. The position of the first car involved
in an accident is provided in the FRA database by counting from and including the first locomotive
unit. The FRA database also provides the number of head-end, mid-end, and rear-end locomotives.
Therefore, if the position of the first involved car is smaller than the number of head-end
locomotives, it is assumed that at least one locomotive is involved in the accident. When the
position of first involved car is larger than the number of head-end locomotives, it is uncertain
whether a mid-end or rear-end locomotive is involved as it is not specified in the FRA database. To
provide a conservative upper bound, the number of accidents for which the position of the first
involved car is larger than the number of head-end locomotives is counted as accidents with a
locomotive involved. Results using this assumption are referred to as upper estimates; results
without this assumption are referred to as lower estimates. For each year and each scenario, the
number of accidents with locomotives involved is divided by the total number of accidents to
calculate the probability of locomotive involvement for that year.
There were event reports in the FRA database for which no locomotives were involved. This is
because a train-train collision may have two records in the database, one for each train, and these
records were not combined into a single data point. For example, if a train with one head-end
locomotive impacted a train without a rear-end locomotive, the record for the first train would
count as a record with locomotive involvement and the record for the second train would count as a
record without locomotive involvement.

4.3. Hydrogen Release from Mechanical Impact
The fitted distribution for hydrogen release from a mechanical impact is shown in Figure 4-24. This
is the dependent probability assuming that an accident with an impact has occurred but is not
dependent on the specific scenario.
The hydrogen release data is based on statistics collected with incident report form 5800 by PHMSA
during 2000-2020 for hazardous material rail transportation. The data field “quantity released” was
used to identify reports with releases and the data field “cause of failure” was used to filter accidents
with mechanical impact. The probability of hydrogen release due to mechanical insult was estimated
as the number of reports with releases divided by the number of reports with mechanical insults (all
of which were assumed to affect the hazmat car).
A report was counted as including a release when the “quantity released” value for that accident was
larger than zero. A report was counted as including mechanical insult when at least one cause of
failure for the accident was “derailment”, “rollover accident”, or “vehicular crash or accident
damage” (the three options for mechanical causes in Form 5800).
The incident statistics for hazmat transportation were used for hydrogen release because of the
limited fuel release statistics for trains with traditional fuel or alternative fuel. This assumes that the
probability of hazmat release when a hazmat car is involved in an impact is a reasonable analogue
for the probability of hydrogen release when a locomotive is involved in an impact. Because the
PHMSA database only provides information on whether there was a release, but not whether the
cause was the mechanical insult, the hydrogen release estimates are likely conservative.
The probability of hydrogen release from mechanical impact is not dependent upon the specific
scenario because scenario-specific information was not available in the PHMSA database for this
event. This distribution is a Weibull distribution with parameters 𝐴 = 0.98 and 𝐵 = 39.69.

4.4. Fire Caused by Collision
This section presents fitted distributions for the probability of a fire given an accident. These
probabilities are scenario dependent. Distributions are not presented here for the fire/explosion

5. RISK RESULTS
Risk results are presented in this section using empirical cumulative distribution functions (CDFs)
calculated from the end state frequencies and probabilities obtained by propagating uncertainties
(Section 4) through each event in the ESD. Simple random sampling was used for the input
uncertainties and the sample size was 100,000. This was a sufficient sample size to reach converged
results; increasing the sample size further did significantly change the distributions on end state
probabilities or frequencies.
Some of the discussions in Section 5 refer to the number of incidents expected annually. These
discussions are based on the medians of the CDFs on frequency. The median is where the
cumulative probability is equal to 0.5 and is a measure of the center of the distribution. One way to
interpret the median is as follows: half of the evidence supports an annual frequency below the
median and half of the evidence supports an annual frequency above the median.
For example, consider if the end state frequency at a cumulative probability of 0.5 is 10-1. If taken as
a point estimate, this means the end state is expected to occur 1/10 times per year. Instead of
conceptualizing that as a fractional event, it is more intuitive to express this as an expected 1 event
every 10 years. The median is used as a point estimate here because the distributions in this analysis
are not normal distributions; the mean can be an extremely unlikely value in the upper tail of the
distribution, whereas the median is always the center of the distribution.
Example results are shown in Figure 5-1 and Figure 5-2. Figure 5-1 shows the CDFs on the end
state frequencies for three different end states. The horizontal axis describes the predicted annual
frequencies of the different end states. The vertical axis describes the subjective probability
distribution of those frequencies. For example, the median for the Example End State 3 distribution
occurs at the cumulative probability of 0.5. The median is at about 10-1 events per year and the
interpretation of the cumulative probability is that the annual frequency for this end state is equally
likely to be above or below 10-1 events per year. This distribution reaches a cumulative probability
close to 1 at around 10 events per year, so the interpretation is that Example End State 3 is expected
to occur no more than 10 times per year. Similarly, the distribution reaches a cumulative probability
close to 0 around 10-6, so the interpretation is that the annual frequency is expected to be higher
than 10-6 per year. Plotting the different end states on the same axis also allows us to draw
conclusions about the relative frequencies, which is even more important for an analysis like this in
which there is high uncertainty. The distribution for Example End State 3 overlaps significantly with
Example End State 1 and Example End State 2, so these end states cannot be concluded to
definitely have different annual frequencies, given all of the uncertainty. However, on average,
Example End State 3 is expected to occur less frequently than Example End State 1 and Example
End State 2. This type of comparison allows analysts to prioritize different scenarios and accident
progressions relative to each other based on their overall expected contribution to risk. In this
example, Example End State 2 has the highest end state frequency on average, so it is expected to
occur most often.

5.1.8. Scenario Risk Results Summary
Across all collision scenarios, the most likely end state (either C0 (unignited dispersion) or F0 (no
release) depending on the scenario) does not see a contribution to consequence from hydrogen.
However, end states D** (overpressure) and E* (jet fire) are within the four most likely end states
for each scenario, representing potentially significant contributions to risk. The importance of these
end states across scenarios suggests that the dominant factors contributing to these accident
progressions are not currently estimated as scenario dependent. These factors are the probability of
hydrogen release given that a collision has occurred, the probability of immediate ignition, and the
probability of delayed ignition.
The immediate and delayed ignition probabilities, in particular, warrant significant consideration
because their evidence base is fairly low (Ehrhart, Brooks, Muna, & LaFleur, 2020) and the
probabilities are likely conservative.
The most likely end state for fire/explosion events is a jet fire and the least likely end state is a
pressure burst. If every train was powered by hydrogen and incidents were to continue at the same
rate, jet fires are expected to occur multiple times a year and pressure bursts are expected to occur
once every ten years, based on the distribution medians. This is an extremely conservative
assumption based on the overall train incident population, and would not apply to early stage

 63

introduction of hydrogen trains in which there would only be a small number. The probabilities for
these events given a fire/explosion are fairly high. Further refinement of the hydrogen tank
involvement probability and the TPRD failure probability may be needed to clarify if these high
probabilities are due to conservative assumptions or not. Operating data or tank/component testing
could be used to evaluate whether the current TPRD failure probabilities are reasonable or overly
conservative. Operating data could clarify whether jet fires or overpressures are more likely to result
from hydrogen ignition. Requirements to improve crashworthiness and reduce the frequency of
crashes in the first place would also reduce the risk from hydrogen fuels.

5.2.12. End State Risk Results Summary
The most concerning end states for hydrogen with collision scenarios are end state D**
(overpressure) and E* (jet fire). The scenarios with the highest expected frequency for these end
states are freight train-other collisions, freight derailments, passenger train-other collisions, and
freight train-train collisions. When the initiating event frequencies for these scenarios are set equal to
one, the probability of reaching this end state is estimated to be between 0.01 and 0.1 based on the
medians. This relatively high probability exists regardless of whether the upper or lower probability
of locomotive involvement is used, so other factors in the ESD may warrant further refinement to
improve estimates. In particular, the immediate and delayed ignition probabilities are significant
factors for these end states and their distributions reflect high uncertainty. Increasing the state-of-
knowledge on these probabilities or reducing conservatisms in the distributions may significantly
change the estimate frequencies of these higher-consequence events.
Freight derailments, despite being the most common accidents (Section 2), do not dominate all of
the risk results. This is because the probability of locomotive involvement (Section 4.2) and the
probability of fire caused by collision (Section 4.4) are lower for this scenario than for other
scenarios.

6. SENSITIVITY STUDY ON HYDROGEN RELEASE FROM
 MECHANICAL IMPACT
The probability of mechanical hydrogen release due to collision is a significant contributor to overall
hydrogen risk. However, this probability distribution is not derived from hydrogen data (see Section
4.3). To illustrate sensitivity of the overall risk problem to this distribution, the beta distribution
derived from data was replaced with a uniform distribution between 0 and 1. This represents just
how unknown this probability of hydrogen release due to a collision is, as all values of this
probability are now equally likely. The risk calculations were repeated with this change. This
comparison shows that some of the risk results and conclusions are sensitive to this distribution.
Results in this section are discussed for the freight derailment and freight train-other collisions;
results for the other collision scenarios are provided in Appendix A. Fire and explosion scenarios are
not included in the sensitivity study because these scenarios are not affected by the probability of
mechanical release. Only the distributions on probability are shown; the purpose of the analysis is to
demonstrate how much of an effect the mechanical release probability distribution has, which can be
done with the probability plots.
The plots in Figure 6-1 compare the end state probabilities for the original analysis (top) and the
sensitivity case (bottom) for freight derailments. The change in the mechanical release distribution
increased the estimated probabilities of end states A0 (no release), G0 (no release), H* (jet fire), and
I** (pressure burst). It decreased the estimated probabilities of end states C0 (unignited dispersion),
D** (overpressure), and E* (jet fire). The probability for end state F0 did not substantially change.
This is expected since the change to a uniform distribution decreases the average mechanical release
probability, so the end states corresponding to mechanical release have a decreased probability.
Additionally, the distributions corresponding to these outcomes related to mechanical release show
more uncertainty (wider distributions). Decreasing the average mechanical release probability also,
correspondingly, increases the probability of no mechanical release, leading to an overall increased
proportion of accident sequences progressing along the branch in the ESD from a collision with no
hydrogen release to an external fire. This is why the probabilities of H* (jet fire) and I** (pressure
burst) increased. The most notable change is the increase in the probability of end state A0 (no
release). In the original analysis, this was the 5th most likely scenario; in the sensitivity case it is the
second most likely scenario, now more likely than E* (jet fire) and D** (overpressure).

(no release). Because these end states are not affected by hydrogen, the most significant change

The original mechanical release distribution (as described in Section 4.3) is likely conservative
because it was derived from data points that are not hydrogen-specific and do not account for the
more robust design of a hydrogen vessel to withstand mechanical insult. This sensitivity analysis
demonstrated how even a moderate decrease in the average mechanical release probability changed
risk results. Further reduction in conservatism could significantly change risk estimates, particularly
reducing estimates for end states D** (overpressure) and E* (jet fire). The sensitivity case assumed a
uniform distribution, which incorporates maximum uncertainty, so a distribution fit to hydrogen-
specific data would also be expected to narrow the distributions on end state probabilities (i.e.,
reduce uncertainty).

7. HYDROGEN LEAK OUTCOME MODELING
Different leak sizes for the same end-state can result in significantly different magnitudes of
outcomes. A release of hydrogen that then ignites can affect nearby people, structures, or materials,
but this can depend greatly on what is nearby. A smaller release of hydrogen that is directed at
exposed persons or an occupied building can potentially be more harmful than a larger release of
hydrogen directed into an open, unoccupied area. Some of the end states discussed above can
potentially result in a hydrogen release, but the analysis above does not determine the size of the leak
nor what might be nearby. However, some leak outcome modeling is useful to illustrate potential
extents of harmful conditions away from a potential leak point. This is done for different release
conditions and leak sizes in order to provide an illustration of example outcomes, but it should be
emphasized that the actual consequences of a leak will depend on the specifics of the release
scenario.
Hydrogen may be present within the locomotive or tender car at different pressures or phases
depending upon the system design, and during an accident a leak could occur at components that
contain hydrogen at one of those states. Hydrogen states considered within this modeling analysis
include 10,152 psi (700 bar) gaseous hydrogen, 5,076 psi (350 bar) gaseous hydrogen, and 290 psi
(20 bar) liquid hydrogen. Leaks modeled within this study assumed a horizontal release angle and
ignored the effect of wind conditions. Leaks resulting from accident scenarios could occur at any
release angle and ambient conditions such as wind direction, wind magnitude, and the relative
humidity could impact leak outcomes. The impact of such factors will affect potential damage to
people or structures nearby.

7.1. Flammable Cloud Outcomes
To compare potential unignited end-state outcomes for different leak sizes, spatial contours of the
resulting hydrogen jet’s lower flammability limit are overlaid. The lower flammability limit of
hydrogen in air is a mole or volume fraction of 4% hydrogen. The lower flammability limit
represents the bounds of a hydrogen jet within which the jet could be ignited, potentially resulting in
overpressure or thermal hazards. Figure 7-1 shows the lower flammability limit contours for leaks
originating from a component containing 10,152 psi (700 bar) gaseous hydrogen while Figure 7-2
and Figure 7-3 show the equivalent results for components containing 5,076 psi (350 bar) gaseous
hydrogen and 290 psi (20 bar) liquid hydrogen, respectively. These results were made using the
HyRAM+ software, which considers both the momentum of the pressurized release as well as
buoyancy effects. The coordinates are relative to the leak point; i.e., the leak is shown at the origin
(0,0) and directed in the horizontal x direction. As such, these leaks are indicative of ground-level
horizontal leaks at the given conditions.

8. CONCLUSION
ESDs were constructed to describe the possible progression of rail incidents to consequences that
could be expected from hydrogen-fueled trains. These calculations were repeated for eight scenarios,
derived using initiating event frequencies for different types of incidents described in the FRA
database: train-train collisions, train-other object collisions, and derailments for passenger rail and
for freight rail. Scenarios initiated with fire/explosion events rather than collisions were also
assessed for passenger and freight rail. Simple random sampling was used to propagate uncertainty
in the initiating event frequencies and event probabilities in the ESDs. The uncertainty distributions
that were sampled for this analysis were fit to data from the FRA database, the PHMSA database,
and data from previous vehicle crash studies. These different data sets vary in applicability, quality,
and completeness. Some of the uncertainty distributions were derived from data points that are not
hydrogen-specific due to lack of hydrogen-specific data. In these cases, the uncertainty distributions
are proxies for hydrogen-specific information and are likely conservative.
The results were presented using CDFs to describe scenario and end state frequencies or
probabilities (assuming the initiating event has occurred). Comparison between scenarios indicates
that the most likely end state does not see significant contribution to consequences from hydrogen.
However, there are two scenario progressions with potentially significant contributions to
consequences from hydrogen that are also estimated to occur with a higher relative frequency. These
are overpressure events resulting from collisions with hydrogen release and delayed ignition, and jet
fire events resulting from collisions with hydrogen release and immediate ignition. Analogous results
were obtained for scenarios resulting from a fire/explosion event rather than a collision. The
ignition probabilities are highly uncertain and affect these estimations.
This analysis identified scenarios that may warrant further investigation into risks related to
hydrogen rail operation. The risks were calculated incorporating high uncertainty in some factors for
the event sequence diagrams. Reducing uncertainty in these factors may change the relative
significance of hydrogen consequence events or reduce their estimated frequency. Additionally, this
analysis focused on the potential risk of hydrogen as a fuel; some scenarios with little or no
contribution to risk due to hydrogen may still be highly dangerous or damaging. Conversely, the
same hydrogen-involved scenario progression may have variable effects based on the direction of
the leak and what is nearby.
Modeling of potential unignited and ignited leak scenarios resulting from crashes illustrated the
impact of hydrogen state and leak size on potential outcomes. Unignited leaks could ignite and cause
thermal or overpressure hazards to the surrounding area. Ignited leaks could cause jet fires resulting
in dangerous heat fluxes to surroundings. The distances at which those potential consequences
could occur were shown to scale with leak size and pressure. The uncertainty distribution on
mechanical release of hydrogen given a collision was derived from non-hydrogen trains. Because
hydrogen fueled trains will be more robust than the generic data set, this probability is conservative.
The level of conservatism cannot be determined without hydrogen data or modeling and simulation.
The most likely end state for fire/explosion events is a jet fire and the least likely end state is a
pressure burst. If every train was powered by hydrogen and incidents were to continue at the same
rate, jet fires are expected to occur multiple times a year and pressure bursts are expected to occur
once every ten years, based on the distribution medians. This is an extremely conservative
assumption based on the overall train incident population, and would not apply to early stage
introduction of hydrogen trains in which there would only be a small number. The probabilities for
these events given a fire/explosion are fairly high. Further refinement of the hydrogen tank

 95

involvement probability and the TPRD failure probability may be needed to clarify if these high
probabilities are due to conservative assumptions or not. Operating data or tank/component testing
could be used to evaluate whether the current TPRD failure probabilities are reasonable or overly
conservative. Operating data could clarify whether jet fires or overpressures are more likely to result
from hydrogen ignition. Requirements to improve crashworthiness and reduce the frequency of
crashes in the first place would also reduce the risk from hydrogen fuels.
