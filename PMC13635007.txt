A multi-site, multi-year labeled polygon dataset for agricultural land use and land cover mapping in central and northern Benin
Authors: Sabi Bruno Bio Nikki Sarè, Raffaele Gaetano, Yvon-Carmen Hountondji, Comlan Hervé Sossou, Laurent Gazull, Gérard Nounagnon Gouwakinnou, Nestor René Ahoyo Adjovi, Simon Madec, Agnès Bégué, Naboua Abdelkader Kouhoundji, Omoto Aurelle Christelle Sedegnan, Mikhaïl Jean De Dieu Dotou Padonou, Coômlan Charles Hounton, Séverin Biaou, Jaurès Kakpo, Samirath Chabi Yoruba, Nicanor Kouton, Bénoît Adinafo Clément Bessan, Biaou Denis Olou, Jean Yarou Koto, Amah Akodewou, Benoit Mertens, Antoine Denis, Bernard Tychon, Jonathan Peereman, Valentine Lebourgeois, Roberto Interdonato, Jean Huchon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate land use and land cover (LULC) mapping in West African agricultural landscapes is severely constrained by the scarcity of high-quality, geo-referenced reference data. In the central and northern regions of Benin, this limitation is particularly acute due to the high spatial heterogeneity of the landscape, the small size of agricultural plots, the diversity of farming practices, and the logistical challenges of conducting field surveys in remote agro-pastoral areas. This data article addresses this gap by describing a multi-site, multi-annual dataset of geo-referenced labeled polygons for agricultural LULC mapping, produced within the framework of the EU DeSIRA OBSYDYA Project.The dataset integrates complementary sources of reference information across six pilot sites in central and northern Benin, each covering a 50×50 km2 observation window. Annual field campaigns conducted between 2022 and 2024 provided the primary reference information for annual crops, while a dedicated field campaign documented areas occupied by natural formations. Additional polygons were delineated by direct visual interpretation of SPOT 6/7 pansharpened imagery and, for built-up surfaces, complemented with OpenStreetMap (OSM) data. All polygons were subsequently subjected to expert visual validation and geometric and topological quality control. The nomenclature comprises 13 land cover classes adapted to the local agricultural context, including annual crop types (leguminous/oleaginous crops, cereals, cotton, roots/tubers, and other crops), perennial agroforestry systems (fruit tree crops and other tree crops), natural formations (dense forest, open forest, and shrubby savanna), and non-agricultural classes (built-up surfaces, bare soil, and water).The dataset contains 33 065 labeled polygons representing 18 189.32 ha of labeled surface across 18 site-year combinations, with a growing spatial coverage over the three years (9 027 polygons in 2022, 9 932 in 2023, and 14 106 in 2024).The dataset constitutes a solid and unique reference base for central and northern Benin, serving three complementary purposes: the operational production of annual agricultural LULC maps within the OBSYDYA project using the MORINGA processing chain [1]; the design, training, and evaluation of deep learning models for LULC classification; and, more broadly, the benchmarking of supervised classification approaches for the monitoring of agricultural dynamics in Sudanian West Africa.Field reference data are openly available under a CC-BY licence in the CIRAD GeoDE repository at https://doi.org/10.18167/GEODE/9446 [2].Keywords: Labeled dataset, Smallholder agriculture, Sentinel-2, SPOT, West Africa, Northern Benin, LULC mapping, Deep learning

Background

Food security is a growing challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, where rapid population growth, agricultural expansion, and increasing climate variability are placing mounting pressure on rainfed smallholder farming systems [5], [6], [7]. In central and northern Benin, agricultural landscapes are undergoing rapid transformations driven by demographic growth, the expansion of commercial crops, and land-use intensification, making accurate and regularly updated land use and land cover (LULC) information essential for agricultural monitoring and evidence-based decision making.

The increasing availability of freely accessible Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 imagery, combined with recent advances in machine learning and deep learning, has opened new opportunities for large-scale agricultural LULC mapping. However, the effectiveness of these supervised methods depends critically on the availability of high-quality, representative reference datasets [8]. Despite recent efforts, publicly available reference data remain scarce for West African smallholder systems, limiting the reproducibility and transferability of remote sensing approaches [6], [8].

It is within this context that the OBSYDYA Project1 was established to develop an operational satellite-based monitoring system for agricultural landscapes in Benin. To address the identified data gap, we present a multi-site, multi-year labeled polygon dataset covering six study sites and three growing seasons (2022–2024), intended to support supervised classification, model training, validation, and benchmarking. Although other datasets exist for other agricultural systems in the West-African region [9], to the best of our knowledge, no publicly available dataset currently provides multi-site, multi-year, field-referenced agricultural polygons specifically designed for LULC mapping in Benin.

Data Description

The dataset consists of geo-referenced labeled polygon files, available in vector GIS format, covering six agricultural sites in central and northern Benin across three annual campaigns (2022–2024). It contains a total of 33 065 labeled polygons annotated according to a 13-class local LULC nomenclature (Table 1), representing approximately 18 189.32 ha of labeled surface area. Field reference data are openly available under a CC-BY licence in the CIRAD GeoDE repository at https://doi.org/10.18167/GEODE/9446 [2].

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Study area

The dataset covers six agricultural sites located in central and northern Benin (Fig. 2), each corresponding to a 50×50 km2 observation window centered on a research and development site of Benin’s National Agricultural Research Institute (INRAB2), monitored annually within the OBSYDYA project. The region is characterized by a unimodal Sudanese tropical climate, with a dry season from October to April and a rainy season from May to September during which all agricultural activities take place. Annual rainfall ranges from 900 to 1 200 mm and temperatures remain between 25 and 32×C. The landscape is dominated by smallholder farming mosaics, with plot sizes typically between 0.25 and 1 ha, organized in a mosaic of cultivated fields, fallow vegetation within shrubby savanna, woody savannas, and agroforestry plantations.

The six sites encompass diverse agricultural landscapes shaped by contrasting climatic, edaphic, and socio-economic conditions (Table 7). Awanla (Zone IV, Atacora West) is characterized by cereal and tuber-based systems on shallow soils. Bagou (Zone II, Northern Cotton Basin) represents cotton and cereal-based systems with intensive cattle farming. Soaodou and Ouenou (Zone III, South Borgou) both reflect typical agropastoral dynamics: Soaodou combines subsistence crops with cotton and cashew production, while Ouenou features residual forest cover within a diversifying landscape (soybean, cotton, food crops, cashew). Parakou (Zone V, peri-urban) combines market gardening with cereals and cash crops. Finally, Gbanlin (Zone V, Central Cotton Basin) presents the highest crop diversity, with cereals, legumes, cotton, tubers, and perennial plantations.

Reference imagery: SPOT 6/7 pansharpened products

SPOT 6/7 pansharpened images at 1.5 m spatial resolution were acquired for each site a few days before each annual field campaign to ensure temporal proximity with field conditions. These products were generated by fusing the 1.5 m panchromatic band with the four 6 m multispectral bands (blue, green, red, near-infrared) using the Bayesian data fusion method [10] implemented in Orfeo ToolBox [11]. SPOT 6/7 images were used exclusively as a basemap for field survey support and polygon delineation, and are not distributed as part of this dataset.

Field survey protocol

Field campaigns were conducted annually between October and November, corresponding to the late growing season when the dominant annual crops (cereals, legumes, cotton, and root crops) have reached advanced phenological stages and can be reliably identified in the field. Surveys followed pre-defined transects established prior to each campaign to ensure systematic and representative spatial coverage of all land cover types present at each site, with particular attention to classes that are frequently confused due to similar spectral or structural characteristics (e.g., fallow vs. degraded natural vegetation, cashew vs. mixed agroforestry, young cotton vs. other annual crops).

Field observations were collected using Samsung Galaxy Tab S7 FE tablets running QField3, with site-specific projects incorporating SPOT 6/7 pansharpened imagery at 1.5 m as the reference basemap. The integrated GPS was used primarily to position and navigate the field teams along predefined transects, rather than as a survey-grade GNSS instrument. The collection protocol allow points to be recorded as GPS offsets by identifying the extent and type of nearby surfaces on the VHR satellite image base map; therefore, no survey-level positional accuracy is assigned to the recorded points. Four field teams, each composed of four members and supervised by a team leader, participated in the campaigns. The teams brought together complementary expertise in agronomy, geomatics, and computer science. For each observation, the geographic coordinates and land-cover class according to the local nomenclature (Table 1) were recorded, together with descriptive information when relevant.

Polygon delineation

Following the field campaigns, the recorded observation points were used as spatial references for the delineation of homogeneous land-cover units. For each observation point, the corresponding land-cover class was first reviewed and the surrounding area was interpreted on the SPOT 6/7 pansharpened imagery in QGIS. A polygon was then delineated around the most spatially homogeneous and visually identifiable area corresponding to the observed class. Homogeneity was assessed from the visual consistency of the land-cover unit in terms of texture, spectral appearance, and vegetation structure. Several rules were applied during polygon delineation to ensure thematic and geometric consistency. A polygon was not allowed to intersect a road or to contain distinct land-cover classes associated with different ground observations. Polygons were also required to be non-overlapping. When several ground observations of the same class were located close to each other within a single visually homogeneous area, they could be represented by a single polygon. Conversely, observations of the same class were not aggregated when they were separated by visually heterogeneous areas. Ground observations located very close to one another but associated with different land-cover classes were excluded from polygonization when their spatial configuration would have resulted in very small or mixed polygons.

The resulting polygons therefore represent spatially homogeneous labeled land-cover units rather than direct buffers around individual field observation points. This approach combines the thematic information collected in the field with the finer spatial detail available from the SPOT 6/7 imagery and provides polygon boundaries suitable for subsequent spatial analysis and supervised classification.

Complementary annotation and temporal updating

The reference database integrates several complementary sources of annotation according to the type and temporal stability of the land-cover classes. Annual field campaigns constitute the primary source for annual crops, whose spatial distribution and land-cover status may change substantially between agricultural seasons. Consequently, new field observations were collected during each annual campaign to document the current distribution of these classes. Natural vegetation formations were supported by a dedicated field campaign. Because these land-cover types, along with tree crops, are generally more spatially persistent than annual crops, their reference polygons can be retained across successive campaigns and re-evaluated annually against the available imagery and field information. This applies in particular to natural formations and perennial plantations such as teak and cashew. Polygons were retained only when their thematic identity and spatial homogeneity remained sufficiently consistent over time. Additional polygons were generated through direct visual interpretation of the SPOT 6/7 imagery, particularly for land-cover features that can be identified reliably from very-high-resolution imagery, such as bare-soil surfaces and selected built-up and water features. Built-up areas were also complemented using OpenStreetMap (OSM) data.

Regardless of their original source, all candidate polygons were subjected to expert visual interpretation using the SPOT 6/7 pansharpened imagery. Thus, the annotation source records the provenance of the original reference information, whereas the final polygon geometry and thematic label result from the common delineation and validation procedure described in this section. The original annotation source is explicitly recorded in the Source attribute of the released dataset.

Quality control and thematic validation

Quality control was conducted after polygon delineation and included geometric, topological, and thematic checks. The geometric review focused on the spatial consistency of polygon boundaries with the interpreted land-cover units and on the absence of visible digitizing artefacts. Particular attention was given to the delineation rules described above, including the exclusion of polygons intersecting roads, polygons containing distinct land-cover classes, and overlapping or spatially ambiguous polygons. A systematic topological check was subsequently performed in QGIS using the Topology Checker. The final database was checked for multipart geometries, internal holes, duplicate features, invalid geometries, and polygon overlaps. These checks ensured that each retained feature corresponded to a valid and spatially coherent polygon suitable for GIS-based analysis.

In addition, thematic validation was performed through expert visual interpretation of the SPOT 6/7 imagery, with the original field observations and available ancillary information used as complementary evidence. For annual crops, non-representative portions of an observed area, such as portions affected by tree cover, were removed when they could be identified from the imagery. For natural environments, polygons showing strong internal heterogeneity in vegetation density or structure were excluded rather than assigning a potentially ambiguous label to the entire area. These procedures were intended to retain only polygons for which the assigned land-cover class could be interpreted with sufficient confidence and spatial homogeneity.

The resulting database therefore combines source-specific reference information with a common expert validation and quality-control framework. The numbers of individual polygons corrected, removed, or merged during the original production process were not systematically recorded; consequently, no retrospective quantitative estimate of these operations is reported.

Annual LULC map production using the MORINGA processing chain

The labeled polygon dataset has also been used as reference data for the operational production of annual agricultural LULC maps within the OBSYDYA project, using the MORINGA (Automatic object-based land cover classification of tropical agrosystems using multi-sensor satellite imagery) processing chain [1]. MORINGA follows an object-based supervised classification approach that combines very-high-resolution SPOT 6/7 imagery for object delineation with multi-temporal Sentinel-2 observations and a Random Forest classifier. In the present application, the Sentinel-2 time series was generated from atmospherically corrected THEIA products [12], using ten spectral bands resampled to a common 10 m spatial resolution and temporally gap-filled on a 15-day interval [13]. The reference polygons were intersected with the image objects to generate training and validation samples, and a stratified five-fold cross-validation grouped by polygon was used to avoid spatial leakage. The resulting maps were produced at 1.5 m spatial resolution according to the Level-1 nomenclature of the dataset.

The MORINGA workflow is summarized in Fig. 3. Its use with the present dataset illustrates how the labeled polygons can support the transition from field-referenced observations to spatially continuous annual LULC products over the 50×50 km2 observation windows. The processing chain has previously been applied to several tropical agricultural landscapes [14], [15], [16], and its source code and a ready-to-use Docker image are openly available at https://forge.inrae.fr/raffaele.gaetano/moringa.

Limitations

The temporal coverage of the dataset is uneven across sites: for annual crop types, three annual campaigns (2022–2024) are available for Parakou, Ouenou, and Bagou, while only two campaigns (2023–2024) are available for Soaodou, Awanla, and Gbanlin. This limits the interannual analysis that can be conducted for the less-covered sites. Furthermore, since crop rotations are frequent in this region, annotations from one year cannot be directly reused for another campaign, and each year’s reference data must be treated as independent. A last field campaign has been performed in November 2025, which will soon enable the update of this dataset with the addition of a further year on all the six sites.

The heterogeneity of farming practices in the study area, combined with the predominance of smallholder plots typically below 0.25 ha, results in a large proportion of small polygons potentially introducing mixed-pixel effects at class boundaries. In addition, the dataset exhibits a marked class imbalance reflecting the actual land cover distribution of the surveyed landscapes: perennial classes such as Fruit Trees and Shrubby Savanna dominate in spatial extent at several sites, whereas classes such as Water, Bare Soil, Dense Forest, and Other Crops are considerably less represented in the reference data.

Finally, field surveys were conducted exclusively during October and November. While this period is favorable for identifying most annual crops at their advanced phenological stage, some land cover types such as early-season crops, wetland surfaces at peak extent, or certain fallow dynamics may be underrepresented or misclassified in the dataset.
