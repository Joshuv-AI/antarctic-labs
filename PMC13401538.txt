Robotic guidance for ultrasound-guided percutaneous procedures: a prospective multispecialty feasibility study
Authors: Abhishek Singh, Shreyans Jain, Kunal Kukreja, Abhijit Patil, Arvind Ganpule, Ravindra Sabnis, Mahesh Desai, Mihir Desai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the safety, feasibility, and technical performance of a handheld robotic guidance system for ultrasound-guided percutaneous procedures across multiple procedural domains.MethodsA prospective, single-center, open-label feasibility study evaluated the Focalist™ handheld robotic guidance system (Mendaera Inc., USA) across multiple ultrasound-guided percutaneous procedures was performed between December 3 and December 11, 2025. Patients undergoing clinically indicated organ access, vascular access, or pain management injection procedures for urologic conditions were prospectively enrolled. Primary endpoints were safety and technical success. Secondary endpoints included procedural performance metrics and operator-reported user experience.ResultsA total of 61 ultrasound-guided percutaneous procedures were performed in 46 patients by 11 operators. Procedure categories included 20 organ access procedures, 31 pain management injections, and 10 vascular access procedures, including 15 specific procedures that had never previously been performed using the system. Technical success was achieved in 60 of 61 procedures (98.4%), with no device-related adverse events. First-attempt success occurred in 45 procedures (74%), with a mean of 1.4 needle attempts per procedure. Mean robotic procedure time (sterile scan start to needle at target) was 5.6 min. Operator survey responses indicated agreement that the system was easy to use (mean 3.7/5) and enhanced confidence during procedures (mean 3.7/5).ConclusionIn this prospective feasibility study, a novel handheld robotic ultrasound guidance system was used successfully across a diverse range of ultrasound-guided percutaneous procedures. High technical success and absence of device-related adverse events support consideration of its inclusion in standard of care workflows.CTRI registrationCTRI/2025/11/098124.ClinicalTrials.gov identifierNCT07358351.Keywords: Ultrasound guidance, Robotic surgery, Percutaneous procedures, Nerve block, Endourology, Vascular access

Introduction

Ultrasound-guided (USG) needle placement is widely used across multiple clinical specialties, including urology, anesthesiology, nephrology, and interventional radiology. These techniques enable percutaneous access to organs, vessels, and peri-neural structures for diagnostic and therapeutic indications. Despite widespread applicability, ultrasound-guided needle placement remains technically challenging. Accurate targeting requires continuous needle tip visualization and alignment of the needle trajectory with the ultrasound imaging plane and anatomic target, which can be technically challenging even for experienced clinicians [1].

Existing technologies, such as static clip-on needle guides, have been developed to assist with ultrasound-guided targeting, but have shown mixed results regarding improvements in targeting accuracy and procedural efficiency [2, 3]. These technical demands contribute to variability in procedural success and may limit adoption of ultrasound-guided techniques in several clinical contexts.

Organ access

Urologist-obtained renal access prior to PCNL has several benefits, such as reduced hospital stay, higher stone-free rates, and fewer major complications, but only 11–20% of urologists obtain their own access instead of relying on other specialists; lack of skill and comfort with this portion of the procedure has been cited as a limiting factor [4–7]. Among those urologists who do obtain their own access, fluoroscopy is more commonly used than ultrasound, introducing ionizing radiation exposure and potentially contributing increased cost compared to ultrasound [8, 9]. Ultrasound guidance during suprapubic tube (SPT) placement has also been recommended to enhance safety by identifying interposed bowel and minimizing the risk of visceral injury, but ultrasound is not routinely incorporated into standard SPT placement practice today [10]. Similarly, biopsy of small renal masses remains underutilized, despite comparable safety outcomes being demonstrated between urologists and radiologists when performing renal mass biopsy (RMB), and many urologists reporting a desire to learn ultrasound-guided biopsy [11, 12].

Despite numerous examples of the utility of ultrasound-guidance in urology, 57% of surveyed practicing urologists in the United States report using no ultrasound in their practice or only performing prostate ultrasound, at least in part due to inadequate training [13].

A Randomized, Single-Blind Clinical Trial Comparing Robotic-Assisted Fluoroscopic-Guided with Ultrasound-Guided Renal Access for Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy by Taguchi k et al.. showed that the mean number of needle punctures was significantly fewer, and the median duration of needle puncture was shorter in the RAF group (1.83 vs. 2.51 times, p = 0.025; 5.5 vs. 8.0 min, p = 0.049, respectively). Multivariate analysis revealed that RAF guidance reduced the mean number of needle punctures by 0.73 times (p = 0.021). It concluded that robotic assistance may provide more accurate and faster percutaneous access, which could potentially reduce the perioperative complication rate. With their promising features, robotic-assisted platforms will be a beneficial innovation in percutaneous procedures [14].

Pain management, vascular access

Regional nerve blocks are increasingly used to reduce perioperative opioid consumption and improve postoperative pain outcomes [15, 16], including blocks alongside arterio-venous fistula creation in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [17, 18] and other urologic surgeries to control post-operative pain, though utilization remains limited [19–21]. Vascular access for monitoring and rapid medication delivery as well as central venous cannulation for patients requiring hemodialysis is common, though use of ultrasound remains technically challenging despite its association with lower complication rate [1]. A 2016 survey found that while most physicians report using ultrasound for routine access, 59% are not comfortable with real-time needle tracking, particularly at more challenging sites such as for subclavian access [22].

Subject device and present study

Focalist™ (Mendaera Inc., USA) is a handheld robotic guidance system designed to support ultrasound-guided instrument placement. Focalist is 510(k) cleared in the USA (K250524). The system assists with precise instrument placement by aligning instruments to a real-time ultrasound image.

At the time of this study, Focalist had previously only been used in PCNL procedures for safe and effective renal access. Amenyogbe et al. describe the safe and effective use of Focalist from the first 10 cases in the world, a non-hand-selected series at a major academic institution in the United States. Eight of ten accesses were successful, with six of eight being first-attempt-successes and seven of eight being papillary accesses. Operators reported smooth workflow integration and highlighted the value of continuous needle-tip tracking and real-time trajectory adjustment, while noting limitations related to probe field-of-view and image quality [23].

The study reported here builds on this early clinical experience. The objective of this study was to evaluate the safety, feasibility, and technical performance of the Focalist robotic guidance system across multiple ultrasound-guided percutaneous procedures, including PCNL and several other “first-in-world” procedures.

Methods

Study design and oversight

This was a prospective, single-center, open-label, single-arm feasibility study conducted to evaluate the safety and performance of the Focalist handheld robotic guidance system (Mendaera Inc., USA) across a broad range of ultrasound-guided percutaneous procedures. The study was conducted at Muljibhai Patel Urological Hospital (MPUH), Nadiad, Gujarat, India, between December 3 and December 11, 2025. Procedures were scheduled within existing clinical operating lists during a dedicated study period. As a multispecialty feasibility evaluation, procedures were performed by experienced attending physicians to establish initial safety and feasibility and a technical performance baseline prior to broader evaluation in trainees and less experienced operators.

The protocol was approved by the MPUH Ethics Committee and registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI). The study was monitored by an independent contract research organization (CRO). All patients provided written informed consent prior to participation. All data were de-identified prior to analysis. Patients were compensated for travel expenses if required. There were no protocol deviations.

Patient population

Patients were prospectively enrolled into one of three procedural categories: organ access (OA), vascular access (VA), or pain management (PM). The protocol specified enrollment of up to 60 subjects, with a minimum of 10 procedures performed in each category. All procedures were clinically indicated independent of study participation.

Procedural categories included:

Organ Access (OA): Procedures such as renal access for percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) or nephrostomy tube placement, percutaneous biopsies, and percutaneous drainages.

Device and procedural workflow

The handheld robotic device attaches directly to the ultrasound probe and guides instruments to an operator-selected target on the touchscreen. After tapping a target on the touchscreen, the robotic arm moves to establish an angle of puncture, which can further be optimized by the operator. Once the user is satisfied with the target and angle, they advance the needle manually, and the system displays a real-time graphical overlay representing the tip of the needle as it moves toward the target.

Typical Focalist workflow. A A scan is performed over the region of interest to confirm adequate visualization with ultrasound, and places robotic arm in contact with patient skin. B User taps desired target on touchscreen. Robot automatically aligns itself to the target. User can make further optimizations to the approach angle on the touchscreen. User optionally marks entry site and makes skin incision. C User manually advances needle by pushing on the hub of the instrument. The needle is visualized in real-time on the ultrasound. The system displays the anticipated trajectory and tracks the distance-to-target and displays an indicator for the estimated instrument tip position (arrow). D The indicators change color when the instrument reaches the selected target. They change color again as a caution if the instrument tip goes beyond the selected target

All procedures were performed using the Focalist handheld robotic guidance system integrated with the Kosmos ultrasound platform (EchoNous Inc., USA). Lexsa or Torso-One ultrasound probes were used as clinically appropriate. Sterile draping was used in all cases.

Focalist was used for the entire needle placement process in all procedures, including trajectory planning and needle guidance. Operators retained manual override capability at all times. The system accommodates a universally compatible needle adapter supporting instruments ranging from 14 to 25 gauge and 3.5–20 cm in length; therefore, instrumentation was selected per institutional standard of care.

Endpoints

Primary endpoints were safety and technical success. Safety was measured as occurrence and severity of serious adverse events related to device use, as assessed intra-procedurally and during a 12-hour follow-up period, which was selected to evaluate acute peri-procedural safety and immediate device-related adverse events, consistent with the feasibility-focused objectives of this early-stage investigation.

Technical success was measured by successful instrument placement using the Focalist system for the indicated procedure, confirmed by standard clinical criteria for each procedure type (e.g., ultrasound visualization of needle tip at target, aspiration of intended fluid, or expected clinical effect after injection).

Secondary endpoints included procedural performance metrics and user experience. Standardized time points were recorded, including sterile scan start, touchscreen trajectory selection (“tap on screen”), and needle at target. Intervals between these time points were calculated to characterize the various phases of the procedure. User experience metrics were captured immediately following each procedure; operators completed a non-anonymous survey assessing agreement (five-point Likert scale from [1] strongly disagree to [5] strongly agree, with scores > 3 indicating agreement) with statements regarding ease of system use, enhancement of operator confidence in needle placement, and preference for the system compared to standard of care.

Operators

Eleven operators participated in the study. Operators were urologists, anesthesiologists, and nephrologists, and performed procedures within their respective specialties. All operators had prior experience with ultrasound-guided procedures. Prior to first clinical use, each operator underwent a standardized 45-minute hands-on training session with the system. Training included system setup, workflow familiarization, and needle targeting practice using a phantom ultrasound model. Operators practiced trajectory planning and device manipulation using a phantom model prior to clinical use.

Pooled and category-level results are reported in Table 2. There were no adverse events reported in any procedures. Technical success was achieved in 60/61 procedures (98.4%). The single unsuccessful case in which Focalist alone was unsuccessful occurred during an organ access procedure, and a combination of fluoroscopy and ultrasound was used to complete the access. First-attempt success was achieved in 74% of procedures, with a mean of 1.4 needle attempts per procedure. Mean robotic procedure time (from sterile scan start to needle at target) was 5.6 min. User experience scores related to ease of use, procedure confidence, and preference over standard of care were 3.7, 3.7, and 3.5 respectively.

In the OA cohort, operators had a technical success rate of 95% and first-attempt success rate of 70%, with a mean of 1.5 attempts per procedure. Overall mean procedure time was 6.8 min. User experience scores for ease of use, procedure confidence, and preference over standard of care were highest in this group (3.8, 3.8. and 3.9 respectively).

In the PM and VA cohorts, operators had a technical success rate of 100%, with first-attempt success rates of 77% and 80% respectively. Both groups had a mean of 1.4 attempts per procedure. Overall mean procedure time was 5.3 and 4.4 min for PM and VA respectively.

Truncal plane blocks (TAP, ESP, QL), which were performed post-laparoscopic/robotic urological surgery, were analyzed as an exploratory descriptive subgroup. Given the small sample size and heterogeneity of block types, these findings are descriptive and hypothesis-generating only. Among the 8 blocks in this cohort, technical success was 100%. First-attempt success was 63%, and mean number of attempts was 1.2 (SD 0.4). Mean completion time was 4.0 min (SD 4.3) and average user rating for ease of use, confidence in needle placement, and preference over standard of care were 3.9 (SD 0.4), 3.9 (SD 0.4), and 3.3 (SD 0.5) respectively.

Qualitative feedback

Operators most frequently highlighted three themes: (1) real-time trajectory planning with dynamic angle adjustment, (2) needle tip depth tracking, and (3) ergonomics constraints in anatomically restricted workspaces. The use of the touchscreen to tap anatomic targets on the live ultrasound image and robotically aim was noted to be particularly intuitive. The flexibility of the entry site compared to a fixed needle guide was also a helpful feature because it allowed operators greater control over a path that avoids obstacles. Operators reported that depth tracking was particularly useful when acoustic shadowing or artifacts limited direct visualization of the needle tip, supporting fewer redirections in some cases, including deeper renal procedures and truncal plane blocks. Some operators also reported that the system beneficially preserved tactile feedback during needle advancement. Ergonomics constraints were noted in certain patient positions and anatomically restricted regions (e.g., very lateral access in supine cases), where the system footprint could lead to interference with the bed, though patient positioning adjustments were effective at mitigating this. One urologist noted that improved grayscale homogeneity could further enhance visualization of fine anatomic structures. Field-of-view was not observed as a challenge or concern.

Discussion

In this prospective feasibility study, use of Focalist resulted in a high technical success rate (98%) with no device-related adverse events. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first prospective clinical evaluations of a robotic ultrasound guidance platform across multiple procedural domains and operator specialties.

Operators were able to successfully employ the system for procedures they are familiar with within clinically acceptable time parameters following a single training session. This study captured the first clinical use of the system by 11 unique operators, suggesting that the system can be successfully adopted following a brief introductory training session. Highlighted features, such as the ability to dynamically adjust instrument angle and instrument depth tracking, have the potential to help experienced and inexperienced operators perform image-guided percutaneous procedures. While user experience metrics should be interpreted cautiously, as they reflect subjective operator perceptions in a small cohort and were not collected using validated instruments, the scores and qualitative feedback suggest applicability across a range of procedures and operator backgrounds.

Organ access

All but one organ access procedure was successful using Focalist alone; the one unsuccessful case was a PCNL, for which even non-robotic ultrasound was inadequate, and a combined fluoroscopic and ultrasound approach was required. The success across the cohort suggests the system may facilitate ultrasound-guided procedures across multiple clinical contexts, including PCNL, PNT, renal mass biopsies, and cyst aspirations, and non-renal procedures such as pelvic fluid drainage and suprapubic tubes. The high user experience scores for ease of use, confidence in needle placement, and comparison to standard-of-care support these findings. To augment the trajectory pre-planning and depth tracking features already present on the device, urologist operators noted that future improvements could include additional automation and intelligence, such as advanced motion/deformation compensation.

Pain management

Four anesthesiologists were successfully able to complete nine unique types of blocks with confirmed high-quality analgesic effects following medication injection. Qualitative commentary provided following the study indicated that while the system performed well across all blocks, it provided particular value for deeper targets such as TAP, QL, and ESP blocks, where the depth tracking feature helped operators understand needle tip position and avoid the “guess-and-check” methodology commonly required with the non-robotic approach. Notably, although all operators had substantial prior experience performing ultrasound-guided procedures and demonstrated proficiency with freehand techniques, three of four operators indicated a preference for the system over the current standard of care. One anesthesiologist reported that although initial cases required additional time for system setup, by the final cases, procedure times were felt to be comparable to, or slightly shorter than, the non-robotic workflow.

Vascular access

All ten central venous access procedures were completed successfully by nephrologist operators. This group reported the greatest number of limitations, chiefly related to the system size and constrained anatomic workspace, which contributed to comparatively lower qualitative scores. Both nephrologists noted that although they personally do not routinely encounter challenges in their own practice that would necessitate ongoing use of the system, they perceived potential utility for less experienced operators, which should be evaluated directly in cohorts that include trainees and less experienced users.

Limitations and future directions

This study did not include a contemporaneous control group using conventional freehand ultrasound guidance, limiting direct comparison of procedural performance outcomes. All operators were attendings and were extensively experienced with free-hand non-robotic ultrasound-guided needle placement. Future evaluations should include larger cohorts of trainees, residents, and other users who are earlier in their ultrasound-guided procedural experience, as well as comparative studies with conventional freehand ultrasound. Enrollment also occurred over a short time window at a single site, which may limit generalizability and precluded formal assessment of learning effects over time.

While this study highlighted the versatility of the Focalist system across a broad range of procedures within and adjacent to urology, to further substantiate the generalizable nature of the platform, future evaluations should aim to utilize Focalist in the many procedures that were not included in this study,

Conclusion

In this single-center feasibility study, the Focalist robotic guidance system was successfully used across a diverse range of ultrasound-guided percutaneous procedures. Technical success was achieved in 98% of cases with no device-related adverse events. Operators reported high ease-of-use scores and were able to use the system effectively following minimal training. These findings support use of robotic guidance systems as a potential tool to improve the accessibility, consistency, and utilization of ultrasound-guided procedures.
