Introduction
We conducted a long-wave FTIR (Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy) portable spectrometer collection
at Oak Ridge National Laboratory campus. Our objective is to collect long-wave radiance spectra (upwelling
and downwelling) to calculate emissivity and apparent temperature of several prepared and in situ samples.
What we expect to collect can be viewed from NIST Webbook as an exemplar where the blue box denotes
around 2-16um, our collection range:

 Figure 1. Example long-wave spectrum.

The creation of these emissive products is created to show that the recently purchased spectrometer is
functioning properly, to learn how to best produce such products from sourcing materials, planning
collections, and analyzing results, and also to show the effectiveness of such collections to potentially benefit
other projects at the lab.

The general locations were the east patio of bldg. 5800 because it’s not far away to carry things, no shadows,
places to sit, not a crazy amount of people traffic nor cars as well as the firefighter training location on the
west end of ORNL campus. The collection team consisted of David Hughes (Sensor Operator), Shannon
Meade (Sample Coordinator), and Abbie Faxon (Data Logger).

In taking measurements, we:

 Figure 3. The D&P 102 fully assembled.

 (a) Picked days where there is consistent cloud cover (preferably no or little cloud cover). We ended up
 having very good atmospherics early in the first collect but some stray clouds did come into our region
 later in the collect. On the second collect (PFAS), we had near-perfect, blue-sky conditions.
 (b) To avoid temperature variations and high heat, we collected early in the mornings on both collects.
 (c) To avoid drifting in the device we warmed up the entire night before stabilizing; all devices differ in
 that stabilization time.
 (d) We took measurements around 1 meter between the sample/calibration panel and the device. This
 gives a good sampling from field of view (FOV).

 Figure 4. The D&P viewing scope fore optic.

 5

(e) We set the cold blackbody about 10 degrees C above the sample temperature (but not below the dew
 point Temperature).

 Figure 5. The cold/hot blackbody fore optic for the D&P.

(f) We set the warm blackbody to just above the highest expected sample temperature expected which
    was 40 C (104 F).
(g) We endeavored to take the blackbody calibration for every target or at least every 10 minutes.
(h) When collecting the spectra, we kept pedestrians and shadows away as well as no moving near the
 sample of people (stand still during collection and keep distances and number of people equal for all
 collections). Also stay clear of buildings and cars.
(i) For LN,
 a. For our early morning collections, I obtained 3 liters of LN the afternoon before (1pm) for the
 first collect and early in the morning prior to the second collect. There was some off gassing,
 but I had plenty for the collect in the morning from both collection days/times using our dewar.
 b. The detector usually stabilizes at 77 K in about 5-10 minutes after filling.
 c. I filled 3-4 funnels full and waited for it to cool, then filled it up. This will keep it from overfilling
 or spewing.

Collection
We picked materials that would exhibit the capabilities of the spectrometer to identify materials and show
value beyond visible, near IR, short-wave IR capabilities.

Minerals

 (1) A silicate-bearing material like playsand (distinctive Si-O absorption feature). One can find this long-
 wave spectrum at NIST webbook: Dioxosilane
 (2) A carbonate such as concrete (carbonate absorption feature).

Plastics

 (3) High density (recycling 2) and Low density (recycling 4) Polypropylene tarp (HDPE/LDPE). Plan to show
 the spectral difference between polyethylene and polypropylene.
 (4) I have two tarps from USCG (VNS captured already) that are HDPE (99% sure).
 (5) Used bubble wrap for a LDPE example.
 (6) Ethene, homopolymer (differences between HDPE and LDPE, see diagram in folder are around 6-7um).
 SWIR requires high resolution (10 wavenumbers) in the 2.3-4.3um region which I’m unaware of any
 sensor having that. Also, SWIR is very noising in the 2.5-3um region from what I have seen.
 (7) See Figure 8 (spongy, and Figure 24): This is neither polyethylene nor polypropylene. It appears to be
 polyolefin foam. Between 8 and 14 microns, polyolefin foams display relatively weak intrinsic mid-
 infrared absorption and high transmission/reflection. Overall apparent emission is heavily modified by
 internal cellular scattering. Small absorbance bands related to amorphous/crystalline methylene
 rocking modes ~ 13.7 μm.

Vegetation

Our initial interest is volatile organic compounds (VOCs) detection in situ regarding vegetation.
This is the least likely to work due to minimum detectable quantity (MDQ) for detection of isoprene (one of
such compounds) but we took the signatures to investigate. We considered performing this observation of
oak or willow leaves in bulk on the tree but highly unlikely to detect isoprene off-gas. When told of the
collection, Jack Orebaugh said, “any vegetation would be great [to collect]”. The first and second vegetation
measurements were taken so as to have a high difference in thermal load on the plant. We thought by taking
them with varying environmental temperatures, we might see a spectral difference in the vegetation. It might
be good to engage the APPL lab for future efforts on this topic.

PFAS Collect
PFAS stands for per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances. PFAS are a large group of man-made chemicals used to
make products resist water, grease, and heat so are highly non-reactive. They are termed forever chemicals
because they do not break down easily in nature or the human body. Taylor Frye (fryetb@ornl.gov) reached
out to me to see if we could provide long-wave spectra of locations where they know aqueous film-forming
foam (AFFF) was sprayed. AFFF is a specialized liquid concentrate mixed with water and used by firefighters to
put out difficult fires involving substances such as petrol, oil, and jet fuel. Most importantly, AFFF is composed
of PFAS. PFAS can occur as monofluorinated or polyfluorinated compounds. Spectrally in the long wave,
monofluorinated compounds show one very strong, prominent single stretching band roughly between 9.0
µm and 10.0 µm. In the same region, polyfluorinated compounds feature stretching peaks creating a sharp,
highly intense doublet or cluster within the 7.35 µm to 8.33 µm range. This region can be difficult to measure
due to strong water absorption regions from 7 to 8 µm. Since the samples were taken at very rapid succession
and it is terrestrial observation (sensor close to sample) we minimize the effect of such water absorption
effects. In addition, bending and skeletal modes add additional intense, sharp emission signals at longer
wavelengths such as 12.8 µm to 14.1 µm. The four locations we surveyed for PFAS (asphalt #1, concrete #2,
asphalt #3, concrete #4) are shown in figures 15, 17-19. Taylor instructed us to collect at those locations in
that PFAS should be present at asphalt #1, it may have PFAS on concrete #2, and it should not have PFAS on
asphalt #3 or concrete #4. In Figure 30 we see that the polyfluorinated PFAS is exhibiting in the two regions it
would be expected. AFFF is composed of such poly- PFAS and not mono- PFAS which was not detected.

Conclusion and Next Steps

There are multiple applications that the long-wave, hyperspectral, portable spectrometer can service as
outlined in 1-6 below. This white paper shows definitive proof that each of these has been accomplished with
this shake-out exercise.

 (1) Additives detection: calcite in HDPE plastic
 (2) Building material characterization (e.g. age): cement aging
 (3) Gas detection: Dustoff detection
 (4) Minerology characterization: silicate and carbonate signatures as an initial proof.
 (5) Plastics differentiation and characterization: polypropylene, polyethylene, and others detected.
 (6) Environmental monitoring (e.g. PFAS detection): successful detection of PFAS.

Next steps would be:

 (a) Get truth on what was sprayed, when, and exactly where at the PFAS site.
 (b) A few years back, Chris Tate and I took an ASD (visible to SWIR) measurement of some concrete at
 ORNL, and we can look to attach what we collected then together with the long-wave to have a full-
 characterization. I would suggest the creation of spectral libraries that are full-spectrum (visible –
 near IR – Shortwave IR – Midwave IR – Longwave IR) which are now possible with both the ASD and
 D&P spectrometers covering both the reflective and emissive regions.
 (c) It is interesting to note that ORNL launches national cement and concrete innovation hub (ACCENT)!
 We may send them our signatures of concrete to see if they are interested in such.
 (d) Joe Pyle has brought forward the idea of characterizing various roof tiles with the spectrometer so we
 can provide to the built and defense/intel communities libraries of roof tiles. This would be an easy
 trip to Lowes to buy various types and shoot them.
