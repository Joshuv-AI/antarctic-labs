Linear convergence of iterative contour integral-based eigensolvers for nonlinear eigenvalue problems
Daniel Kressner, Yuqi Liu, Jose E. Roman, Meiyue Shao, Nian Shao

Abstract
Solving nonlinear eigenvalue problems is an important and challenging task in scientific computing. Contour integral-based approaches are attractive for such eigenvalue problems because they reliably target all eigenvalues in a prescribed domain. However, unlike in the linear case, many traditional methods of this type, such as Beyn’s method, lack an inherent iterative refinement mechanism. Consequently, achieving high accuracy requires high-quality quadrature rules for approximating the contour integral, which often leads to prohibitive computational costs. A notable exception is the so-called NLFEAST algorithm, which combines contour integral techniques with a nonlinear Rayleigh–Ritz extraction step. In this work, we propose a general framework of iterative contour integral-based methods for nonlinear eigenvalue problems that includes NLFEAST. This allows us to prove linear convergence of NLFEAST under mild assumptions and also explains why certain nonlinear eigensolvers do not combine well with iterative methods. Numerical experiments confirm our theoretical findings; in particular that NLFEAST can achieve high accuracy even with a limited number of quadrature nodes, significantly outperforming Beyn’s method on challenging problems.

In this paper, we focus on the first type, commonly referred to as

Let

(3)

For standard eigenvalue problems, it is well known how to turn contour integral-based eigensolvers into iterative methods. For instance, FEAST

For NEPs, the nonlinear FEAST method (NLFEAST)

In this paper, we propose a framework for solving NEPs with iterative methods and establish local linear convergence under mild assumptions. In particular, our analysis clarifies the convergence behavior of NLFEAST.

Theorem 1

Suppose that a holomorphic matrix-valued function

The following lemma evaluates the contour integral in

Theorem 2
.

Consequently, quadrature introduces an error into Beyn’s method, as well as the (block) SS method for similar reasons, that cannot be mitigated by iterative refinement.

The next method we consider is a kind of discretization of

in

(

To understand this from a theoretical point of view, we look into the asymptotic behavior of one simple eigenpair

as follows:

\lim_{\rho\to\lambda}F_{\mathsf{it}}(\rho)u=\lim_{\rho\to\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}T(\xi_{j})^{-1}T^{\prime}(\rho)u=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}T(\xi_{j})^{-1}T^{\prime}(\lambda)u=:F_{N}T^{\prime}(\lambda)u.

Note that

is typically a nonzero vector independent of

For a generic NEP, the matrix

varies with

, making it unlikely that

holds for some prescribed

.
Hence,
\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}}
is unlikely to be a fixed point of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{it}}
, which implies that the scheme above is not an iterative method.

3.3
A sufficient condition for linear convergence

In this section, we derive one of the main results of this work,
Theorem

6
, which establishes local linear convergence provided that a sufficiently good filter and extraction mechanism is used. For this purpose, we will first introduce
Section

3.3.1
on extraction and link it to a suitable notion of eigenpair accuracy. As our result will be asymptotic with respect to filter quality, we will need to consider a whole filter family that contains increasingly good filters.

Throughout this section, we make the following assumption on
T(\cdot)
for the sake of simplicity in our analysis.

Assumption 3
.

Assume that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{m}
in
\mathcal{D}
are simple, and their corresponding (unit) eigenvectors
u_{1},\dotsc,u_{m}
are linearly independent.

Under
Section

3.3

\mathcal{D}
satisfies
m\leq n
.
Moreover, since all eigenvalues are simple, then the eigenvectors of
T(\cdot)
are unique up to scaling. If we choose the right and left eigenvectors in accordance with
Keldysh’s theorem (that is, the eigenvectors are columns of the matrices
U_{\mathcal{D}}
and
W_{\mathcal{D}}
in

1

u_{i}w_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}T(\xi)^{-1}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{1}{w_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}T^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})u_{i}}u_{i}w_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad w_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}T^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})u_{i}=1,

where
\mathcal{C}_{i}
is a small contour that only encloses one eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
.

Remark 1
.

Section

3.3
excludes certain challenging scenarios. Nevertheless, with a careful implementation,
Algorithm

1
can still be applied and achieves linear convergence even when
Section

3.3
is violated; see
Section

4.4
for a numerical demonstration.
From a theoretical standpoint,
Section

3.3
could be relaxed by invoking the concept of invariant pairs
Kressner2009
.

3.3.1
Assumption on eigenpair extraction

To establish a convergence result, we also need the following assumption on the quality of approximate eigenpairs extracted in line
1
of
Algorithm

1
.

Assumption 4
.

Given
Section

3.3
, we assume that there exists a sufficiently small constant
0<\epsilon_{T}<\pi/2
depending only on
T(\cdot)\colon\mathcal{D}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, such that the following holds. For any
m
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfying
\epsilon:=\angle(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}})\leq\epsilon_{T}
for
\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}}=\Span\{u_{1},\dotsc,u_{m}\}
, the eigenpairs
(\rho_{i},x_{i})
extracted from
\mathcal{X}
in line
1
of
Algorithm

1
are
\order(\epsilon)
-approximations to the (appropriately ordered) exact eigenpairs
(\lambda_{i},u_{i})
of
T(\cdot)
in
\mathcal{D}
:

\lvert\rho_{i}-\lambda_{i}\rvert=\order(\epsilon)\quad\text{and}\quad\angle(x_{i},u_{i})=\order(\epsilon),\quad i=1,\ldots,m.

Here, the constants in
\order(\cdot)
are independent of the choice of
\mathcal{X}
.

Section

3.3.1
essentially says that, once the subspace
\mathcal{X}^{(k)}
is a good approximation to
\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}}
, then we can extract equally good approximate eigenpairs from it. In particular, for the exact
\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}}
, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are recovered exactly by the extraction.

Remark 2
.

For standard eigenvalue problems, the standard Rayleigh–Ritz procedure
satisfies
Section

3.3.1
.
For NEPs, the nonlinear Rayleigh–Ritz procedure used in
Algorithm

2
is
usually
observed to satisfy
Section

3.3.1
, but it may fail for certain
T(\cdot)
’s because the compressed NEP may be
\epsilon
-close to singular; see
(
shao2026stabilizing
, Ex. 3)
for example.
The
randomized Rayleigh–Ritz
procedure recently introduced in
shao2026stabilizing
satisfies the convergence guarantee required in
Section

3.3.1
with high probability.

3.3.2
Decomposition of approximate eigenvectors

Recalling
U_{\mathcal{D}}=[u_{1},\dotsc,u_{m}]
, we denote
U_{\mathsf{out}}
as an orthonormal basis of the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}}=\range(U_{\mathcal{D}})
and
U=[U_{\mathcal{D}},U_{\mathsf{out}}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
Let us consider an arbitrary
m
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}}
on the Grassmann manifold. Let
\{(\rho_{i},x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
be the approximate eigenpairs of
T(\cdot)
extracted from
\mathcal{X}
in line
1
of
Algorithm

1
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
x_{i}
is normalized by
e_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}U^{-1}x_{i}=1
, and hence, it admits the following decomposition:

x_{i}=u_{i}+U_{-i}y_{i}+U_{\mathsf{out}}z_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\dotsc,m,

(8)

where
U_{-i}=[u_{1},\dotsc,u_{i-1},u_{i+1},\dotsc,u_{m}]
.
Even though
U
is not an orthogonal matrix, it is invertible and fixed with respect to
i
; hence,
\lVert y_{i}\rVert
and
\lVert z_{i}\rVert
are still indicative of how closely
x_{i}
approximates
u_{i}
.
The following proposition shows that
\angle(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}})
is a good measurement for the accuracy of all approximate eigenpairs.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\epsilon:=\angle(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}})\leq\epsilon_{T}
. Consider the extracted eigenpairs
(\rho_{i},x_{i})
of
T(\cdot)
from the subspace
\mathcal{X}
, where
x_{i}
admits the decomposition
Equation

8
.
Under
Section

3.3.1
, it holds that

\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\max\Bigl\{\lvert\rho_{i}-\lambda_{i}\rvert,\lVert y_{i}\rVert,\lVert z_{i}\rVert\Bigr\}=\order(\epsilon).

Proof.

The property
\lvert\rho_{i}-\lambda_{i}\rvert=\order(\epsilon)
follows directly from the convergence of Ritz values in
Section

3.3.1
. The remaining terms follow from the convergence of Ritz vectors:

\lVert z_{i}\rVert^{2}+\lVert y_{i}\rVert^{2}=\tan^{2}\angle(U^{-1}x_{i},e_{i})=\order\bigl(\tan^{2}\angle(x_{i},u_{i})\bigr)=\order(\epsilon^{2}),

where we used that
U
is invertible and a constant with respect to
\epsilon
.
∎

3.3.3
Effectiveness of a filter family

We now consider a family of filters
F_{\mathsf{it}}\equiv F_{\mathsf{it}}^{[N]}
parametrized by a positive integer
N
.
Let
\widehat{x}_{i}:=F_{\mathsf{it}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})x_{i}
, and consider a decomposition analogous to (
8
):

\widehat{x}_{i}=\widehat{\alpha}_{i}u_{i}+U_{-i}\widehat{y}_{i}+U_{\mathsf{out}}\widehat{z}_{i}.

(9)

While the variables
\widehat{\alpha}_{i}
,
\widehat{y}_{i}
and
\widehat{z}_{i}
of course depend on
N
, we omit the superscript
\cdot^{[N]}
for notational clarity.

We require the filter family to be effective in the sense that components in
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{out}}
are reduced by a factor in every step, while components in
\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}}
are nearly preserved.
Specifically, we assume that

\bigl\lvert\widehat{\alpha}_{i}-1\bigr\rvert=\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon),\quad\lVert\widehat{y}_{i}\rVert=\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon)\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\widehat{z}_{i}\rVert=\order(\gamma_{N}\epsilon),\quad i=1,\ldots,m,

(10)

where
\epsilon=\angle(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}})
as before.
The quantity
\gamma_{N}\geq 0
quantifies the quality of the filter family and we assume that
\gamma_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.
In
Section

3.4
, we show that the filter
Equation

6
used in NLFEAST defines such a family; it satisfies
Equation

10
with the parameter
N
representing the number of quadrature nodes.

3.3.4
Linear convergence of
Algorithm

1

We are now ready to formulate our first main result on the convergence of
Algorithm

1
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
T(\cdot)
be an NEP satisfying
Section

3.3
for a domain
\mathcal{D}
. Using the notation of
Section

3.3.1
, consider an
m
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{X}
such that
\epsilon:=\angle(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}})\leq\epsilon_{T}
, and let
(\rho_{i},x_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, denote approximate eigenpairs of
T(\cdot)
extracted from
\mathcal{X}
satisfying
Section

3.3.1
.
Assume that there exists a family of filters
F_{\mathsf{it}}^{[N]}(\cdot)
such that
\widehat{x}_{i}:=F_{\mathsf{it}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})x_{i}
satisfies
Equation

10
with
\gamma_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.
Then the updated subspace
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}=\Span\{\widehat{x}_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{x}_{m}\}
satisfies

\widehat{\epsilon}:=\angle(\widehat{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}})=\order(\gamma_{N}\epsilon).

Here, the constants in
\order(\cdot)
are independent of the choice of
\mathcal{X}
.

Theorem

6
shows—under the stated assumptions—that one step of
Algorithm

1
effects a linear contraction of
\angle(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}})
.
By applying
Theorem

6
recursively, it follows that, for sufficiently large
N
,
Algorithm

1
exhibits local linear convergence with a rate proportional to
\gamma_{N}
.

Proof of
Theorem

6
.

Let
X=[x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m}]
and
\widehat{X}=[\widehat{x}_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{x}_{m}]
. Since

\widehat{\epsilon}=\angle(\widehat{X},U_{\mathcal{D}})=\order\bigl(\angle(U^{-1}\widehat{X},E_{m})\bigr)=\order\bigl(\tan\angle(U^{-1}\widehat{X},E_{m})\bigr),

where
E_{m}=[e_{1},\dotsc,e_{m}]
.
To establish a linear convergence, it suffices to show

\tan\angle\bigl(U^{-1}\widehat{X},E_{m}\bigr)=\order(\gamma_{N}\epsilon).

(11)

Let
\widehat{Y}=[\widehat{y}_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{y}_{m}]
and
\widehat{Z}=[\widehat{z}_{1},\dotsc,\widehat{z}_{m}]
.
According to
Equation

10
and the triangle inequality, we have

\lVert U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}\widehat{X}\rVert=\lVert\widehat{Z}\rVert=\order(\gamma_{N}\epsilon)\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert U_{\mathcal{D}}^{\dagger}\widehat{X}-I\rVert\leq\lVert\widehat{Y}\rVert+\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\lvert\widehat{\alpha}_{i}-1\rvert=\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon).

As a consequence, we know that
\lVert(U_{\mathcal{D}}^{\dagger}\widehat{X})^{-1}\rVert=\order(1)
.
Then
Equation

11
is obtained by a representation of the tangent of principal angles in
(
Zhu2013
, Thm. 3.1)
and the submultiplicativity of the spectral norm:

\tan\angle\bigl(U^{-1}\widehat{X},E_{m}\bigr)=\lVert U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}\widehat{X}(U_{\mathcal{D}}^{\dagger}\widehat{X})^{-1}\rVert\leq\lVert U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}\widehat{X}\rVert\lVert(U_{\mathcal{D}}^{\dagger}\widehat{X})^{-1}\rVert=\order(\gamma_{N}\epsilon),

and in turn, the theorem is proved.
∎

As we have already seen in
Section

1
and
Section

3.2
, some contour integral discretizations do not combine well with
the iterative refinement performed by
Algorithm

1
.
To gain further theoretical insight, let us consider the convergence to an individual eigenpair, w.l.o.g., the first eigenpair
(\lambda_{1},u_{1})
. For the filter family
F_{\mathsf{it}}^{[N]}
to satisfy
Equation

10
we essentially need to ensure that

F_{\mathsf{it}}^{[N]}(\rho_{1})=U\begin{bmatrix}1+\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon)&\order(1)&\order(1)\\
\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon)&\order(1)&\order(1)\\
\order(\gamma_{N}\epsilon)&\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon)&\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon)\end{bmatrix}U^{-1}

(12)

holds, where the block partitioning is according to
Equation

8
and we recall from
Section

3.3.2
that
\lvert\rho_{1}-\lambda_{1}\rvert=\order(\epsilon)
.
The most challenging, but nevertheless important part is to ensure the asymptotics for the
(3,1)
block.
Given the contour integral-based operator
F_{\mathsf{ci}}
,
Theorem

2
yields

\lim_{\rho_{1}\to\lambda_{1}}F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{1})=U\begin{bmatrix}1&\order(1)&\order(1)\\
0&\order(1)&\order(1)\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}U^{-1}.

Both discretizing the contour integration and changing the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
to an approximate eigenvalue
\rho_{1}
will perturb the eigenvectors of
F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\lambda_{1})
.
Unless particular attention is paid, as is done in NLFEAST, this can be expected to introduce
an error proportional to
\gamma_{N}+\lvert\rho_{1}-\lambda_{1}\rvert
into the
(3,1)
block of
F_{\mathsf{it}}^{[N]}(\rho_{1})
. As a consequence,
condition (
12
) is not satisfied.
Also,
\lim_{\rho_{1}\to\lambda_{1}}F_{\mathsf{it}}^{[N]}(\rho_{1})u_{1}
is unlikely to be contained in
\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}}

and hence,
\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}}
is
not
the fixed point of the operator
\lim_{\rho_{1}\to\lambda_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{it}}^{[N]}(\rho_{1})
.
Thus, some contour integral-based nonlinear iterative eigensolvers fail to improve their accuracy via performing iterations.

3.4
Linear convergence of NLFEAST

In this section, we aim at showing that the filter family
F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}
, defined in
Equation

6
and used in NLFEAST, is effective in the sense of
Equation

10
.
Since
F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho)
is a bounded operator and the integrand is holomorphic on some domain that contains the closure of
\mathcal{D}
, we can assume that our numerical quadrature converges exponentially
(
Trefethen2013
, Thm. 19.3)
, that is, there exists a
\gamma_{N}=C_{T}\exp(-c_{T}N)
, where
c_{T},C_{T}>0
depend only on
T\colon\mathcal{D}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, such that

\max\Bigl\{\Bigl\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\omega_{j}}{\xi_{j}-\rho}H(\xi_{j})-H(\rho)\Bigr\rVert,\bigl\lVert F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho)-F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho)\bigr\rVert\Bigr\}\leq\gamma_{N}\quad\text{for all}\quad\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{0},

(13)

where
H(\cdot)
is the holomorphic matrix-valued function in
Theorem

1

and
\mathcal{D}_{0}\subset\mathcal{D}
is a compact set containing small neighborhoods of the desired eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}
. In the following theorem, we show that
Equation

13
suffices to ensure the effectiveness of the filter family in NLFEAST.

Theorem 7
.

Let
T(\cdot)
be an NEP satisfying
Section

3.3
.
Consider an arbitrary approximate eigenpair
(\rho_{i},x_{i})
and let
\widehat{x}_{i}:=F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})x_{i}
, where
x_{i}
and
\widehat{x}_{i}
admit the decomposition
Equations

8
and
9
, respectively, and
1\leq i\leq m
.
Assume that the accuracy of quadrature in
F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\cdot)
satisfies
Equation

13
, and

\epsilon:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\max\Bigl\{\lvert\rho_{i}-\lambda_{i}\rvert,\lVert y_{i}\rVert,\lVert z_{i}\rVert\Bigr\}

is sufficiently small, then the effectiveness condition
Equation

10
holds for all
(\rho_{i},x_{i})
.

Proof.

Since all eigenvalues of
T(\cdot)
in
\mathcal{D}
are simple,
\rho_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
for
i\neq j
whenever
\epsilon
is sufficiently small. In the following, we will treat two different situations:
\rho_{i}=\lambda_{i}
or
\rho_{i}
is not an eigenvalue of
T(\cdot)
.

Let
[v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n}]=U^{-\mathsf{H}}
and
V_{-i}=[v_{1},\dotsc,v_{i-1},v_{i+1},\dotsc,v_{m}]
.
Using
Equation

13
, we know that

\lvert\widehat{\alpha}_{i}-1\rvert=\bigl\lvert v_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})x_{i}-1\bigr\rvert=\bigl\lvert v_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{i})x_{i}-1\bigr\rvert+\order(\gamma_{N})=\bigl\lvert v_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{i})u_{i}-1\bigr\rvert+\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon).

When
\rho_{i}=\lambda_{i}
, we have
T(\rho_{i})u_{i}=0
. As a consequence,

v_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{i})u_{i}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{D}}\frac{1}{\xi-\rho_{i}}v_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}\bigl(I-T(\xi)^{-1}T(\rho_{i})\bigr)u_{i}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{D}}\frac{v_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}u_{i}}{\xi-\rho_{i}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\xi=1.

Otherwise, when
\rho_{i}\not=\lambda_{i}
, recall that
J_{\mathcal{D}}=\diag\{\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{m}\}
.
Theorem

2
yields that

F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{i})=U_{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{i}I-J_{\mathcal{D}})^{-1}W_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{H}}T(\rho_{i}).

Using the facts that
T(\lambda_{i})u_{i}=0
and
w_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}T^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})u_{i}=1
, we have

\displaystyle v_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{i})u_{i}

\displaystyle=v_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{i}I-J_{\mathcal{D}})^{-1}W_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{H}}T(\rho_{i})u_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}T(\rho_{i})u_{i}}{\rho_{i}-\lambda_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}\bigl(T(\rho_{i})-T(\lambda_{i})\bigr)u_{i}}{\rho_{i}-\lambda_{i}}

\displaystyle=w_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}T^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})u_{i}+\order(\lvert\rho_{i}-\lambda_{i}\rvert)

\displaystyle=1+\order(\epsilon).

Thus,
\lvert\widehat{\alpha}_{i}-1\rvert=\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon)
holds for both situations.

With a similar calculation, it follows that

\lVert\widehat{y}_{i}\rVert=\lVert V_{-i}^{\mathsf{H}}F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})x_{i}\rVert=\lVert V_{-i}^{\mathsf{H}}F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{i})u_{i}\rVert+\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon)=\order(\gamma_{N}+\epsilon),

where, in the last equality, we used the facts that
V_{-i}^{\mathsf{H}}u_{i}=0
and

\displaystyle v_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{i})u_{i}

\displaystyle=v_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{i}I-J_{\mathcal{D}})^{-1}W_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{H}}T(\rho_{i})u_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}T(\rho_{i})u_{i}}{\rho_{i}-\lambda_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\bigl(T(\rho_{i})-T(\lambda_{i})\bigr)u_{i}}{\rho_{i}-\lambda_{k}}

\displaystyle=\order(\epsilon)

for any
1\leq i\neq k\leq m
and
\rho_{i}
not being an eigenvalue of
T(\cdot)
in
\mathcal{D}
.
When
\rho_{i}=\lambda_{i}
, the desired estimate for
\lVert\widehat{y}_{i}\rVert
immediately follows from

V_{-i}^{\mathsf{H}}F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\lambda_{i})u_{i}=V_{-i}^{\mathsf{H}}u_{i}=0
.

It remains to bound
\lVert\widehat{z}_{i}\rVert
. For this purpose, we first note that
U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{i})=0
and
\lVert x_{i}-u_{i}\rVert=\order(\epsilon)
imply

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{z}_{i}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})x_{i}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})u_{i}\rVert+\lVert U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})(x_{i}-u_{i})\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})u_{i}\rVert+\lVert U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}\bigl(F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})-F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{i})\bigr)(x_{i}-u_{i})\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})u_{i}\rVert+\order(\gamma_{N}\epsilon).

Now it suffices to show that
\lVert U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})u_{i}\rVert=\order(\gamma_{N}\epsilon)
.
When
\rho_{i}=\lambda_{i}
, this term vanishes because

U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\lambda_{i})u_{i}=U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\omega_{j}}{\xi_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\Bigl(I-T(\xi_{j})^{-1}T(\lambda_{i})\Bigr)u_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\omega_{j}}{\xi_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\cdot U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}u_{i}=0,

where we used
U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}u_{i}=0
in the last equality.
Otherwise, by
Theorem

2
and
U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}U_{\mathcal{D}}=0
, we know that

U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}H(\rho_{i})T(\rho_{i})u_{i}=U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}(I-F_{\mathsf{ci}}(\rho_{i}))u_{i}=U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}\bigl(I-U_{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{i}I-J_{\mathcal{D}})^{-1}W_{\mathcal{D}}T(\rho_{i})\bigr)u_{i}=0.

Using Keldysh’s theorem, we derive

\displaystyle U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\rho_{i})u_{i}

\displaystyle=-U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\omega_{j}}{\xi_{j}-\rho_{i}}T(\xi_{j})^{-1}T(\rho_{i})u_{i}

\displaystyle=-U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\omega_{j}}{\xi_{j}-\rho_{i}}\bigl(U_{\mathcal{D}}(\xi_{j}I-J_{\mathcal{D}})^{-1}W_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{H}}+H(\xi_{j})\bigr)T(\rho_{i})u_{i}

\displaystyle=-U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\omega_{j}}{\xi_{j}-\rho_{i}}H(\xi_{j})T(\rho_{i})u_{i}

\displaystyle=U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}\Bigl(H(\rho_{i})-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\omega_{j}}{\xi_{j}-\rho_{i}}H(\xi_{j})\Bigr)\bigl(T(\rho_{i})-T(\lambda_{i})\bigr)u_{i}

\displaystyle=\order(\gamma_{N}\epsilon).

Here, we used
U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}U_{\mathcal{D}}=0
in the third equality,

U_{\mathsf{out}}^{\dagger}H(\rho_{i})T(\rho_{i})u_{i}=0
and
T(\lambda_{i})u_{i}=0
in the fourth equality,
as well as
Equation

13
in the last equality.
∎

Plugging
Theorem

7
into
Theorem

6
and using
Section

3.3.2
, we obtain the convergence of NLFEAST.

Corollary 8
.

Consider NLFEAST with the filter
F_{\mathsf{nl}}^{[N]}(\cdot)
applied to an NEP
T(\cdot)
satisfying
Section

3.3
.
Under
Section

3.3.1
, there exist constants
\epsilon_{\mathsf{nl}}>0
and
c_{\mathsf{nl}}>0
depending only on
T(\cdot)\colon\mathcal{D}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that the following statements hold.

Assume that the accuracy of the numerical quadrature in
Equation

13
satisfies
\gamma_{N}<1/c_{\mathsf{nl}}
, then

\angle(\mathcal{X}^{(k)},\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}})\leq(c_{\mathsf{nl}}\gamma_{N})^{k}\angle(\mathcal{X}^{(0)},\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}})

holds for any initial subspace
\mathcal{X}^{(0)}
satisfying
\angle(\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{X}^{(0)})\leq\epsilon_{\mathsf{nl}}
. Here,
\gamma_{N}=C_{T}\exp(-c_{T}N)
for some constants
c_{T}
and
C_{T}
depending only on
T\colon\mathcal{D}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.

Remark 3
.

The constant
c_{\mathsf{nl}}
in
Section

3.4
may depend on the eigenvalue and eigenvector condition numbers of
T(\cdot)
.
This is different from subspace iteration or FEAST for linear (non-Hermitian) eigenvalue problems since in NLFEAST, we have to perform extraction in each iteration. However, since
\gamma_{N}
can be made arbitrarily close to zero by using a sufficiently large number of quadrature nodes, NLFEAST can always be made (linearly) convergent.

To conclude this section, we stress that although the convergence of NLFEAST also requires a sufficiently accurate quadrature rule in
Equation

13
, this requirement is fundamentally different from the “failure” of being an iterative method. The “failure” refers to the fact that, for any inexact quadrature rule, the eigenpairs produced by those methods, such as Beyn’s method, are generically
not
eigenpairs of
T(\cdot)
.
In contrast, for NLFEAST, given an NEP satisfying
Section

3.3
, there exists a
\gamma_{N}
such that for any quadrature rule satisfying
Equation

13
, the computed eigenpairs (linearly) converge to the exact eigenpairs of
T(\cdot)
as the iterations proceed.
Moreover, for a prescribed
\gamma_{N}
, only a finite number of quadrature nodes is required to meet
Equation

13
.

4.3
Comparison between NLFEAST and Beyn’s method

The purpose of this section is to highlight the benefits of using a contour integral-based nonlinear eigensolver that allows for iterative refinement, such as NLFEAST.

It is important to recall that the linear systems arising in NLFEAST involve matrices
T(\xi_{j})
,
j=1,\dotsc,N
, that remain the same throughout the iterations.
Thus, we just need to compute (sparse) LU factorizations of these
N
matrices only once, during the first iteration, and can reuse these factorizations in later iterations.

In contrast, the only way to improve the accuracy of Beyn’s method is to increase the number of quadrature nodes.
A practical inconvenience is that one usually does not know in advance how many quadrature nodes are needed to reach a specified accuracy.
Even if
N
can be (tightly) estimated, achieving higher accuracy inevitably needs more quadrature nodes.
This leads to linear systems involving a larger number of distinct matrices and, in turn, requires computing a larger number of matrix factorizations.
Since matrix factorizations are usually more expensive than subsequent (triangular) linear solves, one expects NLFEAST to be faster than Beyn’s method when desiring highly accurate eigenpair approximations.

In this numerical experiment, we select the three test problems
acoustic_wave_2d
(QEP),
photonics
(REP), and
gun
(NEP) from
Table

1
,
and compare the performance of NLFEAST with Beyn’s method in
Figure

4
.
For NLFEAST, we fix the number of quadrature nodes to
N=16
,
32
, or
48
, and plot the residual vs. the accumulated computational time after every iteration. For Beyn’s method, the residual vs. the computational time is plotted when choosing
N=16+kd
quadrature nodes for
k=1,2,\dotsc
, where the step sizes
d
are set to
8
,
64
, and
16
for the three problems, respectively. Note that NLFEAST with
N=16
fails to converge on
photonics

As shown in
Figure

4
, except when very few quadrature nodes are used (such as
N=16
for
photonics
), NLFEAST consistently outperforms Beyn’s method, usually achieving the small residuals within less time. Furthermore, the convergence rate of NLFEAST is much more favorable, because its cost for sufficiently many iterations is governed primarily by the cost of triangular solves rather than matrix factorizations.

Concluding remarks

In this paper, we proposed a general framework for iterative contour integral-based methods for nonlinear eigenvalue problems. Under arguably mild assumptions, we establish linear convergence for methods from this framework, including NLFEAST.
One promising direction for future research is to explore this framework further and design novel iterative nonlinear eigensolvers with improved properties.

Appendix A
Some variants of NLFEAST

A natural question is whether there exist algorithms other than NLFEAST (
Algorithm

2
) that fit
the framework of
Algorithm

1
.
For linear eigenvalue problems, a family of such algorithms can be constructed by choosing a different
filter
F_{\mathsf{it}}
, including, for example, subspace iteration, FEAST, and Riemannian gradient descent.
In contrast, for nonlinear eigenvalue problems, constructing a filter
F_{\mathsf{it}}
that satisfies the
convergence conditions established in
Section

3.3
is rather challenging,
as can already be seen from the analysis presented in
Section

3.4
.
To the best of our knowledge, apart from NLFEAST, no other iterative refinement algorithms are currently known to conform to the framework of
Algorithm

1

For exploratory purposes, we include
Algorithm

3

Algorithm

1
but chooses the filter adaptively (and differently) in every iteration.
The basic idea of

is motivated by the observation that,
in each iteration of NLFEAST, it obtains the updated vector

from a fixed linear combination of

\Span\{x_{i}^{(k)},P_{i}^{(k)}x_{i}^{(k)}\}

To this end, we attempt to approximate a Newton-like descent direction
