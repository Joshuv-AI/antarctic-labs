Operationalizing the EU AI Act in a comprehensive cancer center through an institutional governance framework
Authors: W Gehin, JC Faivre, JE Bibault, Y Thiery, S Deon, E Desandes, A Lambert, A Baudin, M Schoumacker, P Eschwege, S Supiot, T Perennec, D Peiffert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe EU AI Act subjects high-risk clinical AI systems to deployer obligations whose institutional operationalization remains undefined. Drawing on experience at a French comprehensive cancer center, we present a governance framework combining three novel components: a dual-axis model for institutional risk stratification, a five-category AI-related adverse event taxonomy inspired by pharmacovigilance, and structured proficiency testing against simulated AI errors. Successful AI integration depends on institutional readiness, not algorithmic performance.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Medical research, Scientific community

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated substantial technical progress across multiple domains of oncology, including radiotherapy planning, imaging analysis, clinical decision support, and predictive modeling1,2. In controlled research environments, many AI systems achieve high task-specific accuracy, fostering expectations that AI could enhance efficiency, standardization, and quality of cancer care. However, despite this expanding body of technically validated systems, real-world clinical adoption remains limited. Systematic reviews indicate that only 15–25% of departments have successfully integrated AI tools into routine clinical workflows, while ~60% of AI initiatives fail to progress beyond pilot or proof-of-concept phases3–5. Growing evidence suggests that this implementation gap is not primarily driven by insufficient algorithmic performance. Instead, qualitative and mixed-methods studies consistently highlight organizational, infrastructural, regulatory, and human factors as critical determinants of successful deployment6–10. Model performance degradation in external environments, limited interoperability with existing clinical information systems, and insufficient lifecycle monitoring further constrain safe translation into routine practice5,11,12.

Comprehensive cancer centers (CCC) represent particularly complex socio-technical environments for AI deployment. In such contexts, AI systems are not neutral technological add-ons; they actively reshape clinical reasoning, workflow dynamics, professional roles, and institutional responsibility1,6–8,13. Consequently, safe and scalable AI integration requires coordinated governance mechanisms that address risk management, human oversight, data governance, infrastructure readiness, workforce development, and continuous monitoring simultaneously7,9,12,14,15. In radiology and radiotherapy, where AI tools have been among the earliest to enter clinical use, prospective evaluations have consistently demonstrated that integration into workflows requires structured oversight well beyond retrospective technical validation1,9,11,12,16.

Despite increasing recognition of these challenges, there remains a notable gap in the literature regarding operational models that describe how CCCs can concretely structure governance, oversight, and safety monitoring for high-risk clinical AI systems in alignment with emerging regulatory frameworks. Most published studies focus either on technical validation of individual AI tools or on qualitative exploration of barriers and facilitators, with limited guidance on institutional architecture, accountability mechanisms, and regulatory operationalization6–9.

Within this context, the Institut de Cancérologie de Lorraine (ICL), a French CCC within the Unicancer network, has developed an institutional governance framework for high-risk clinical AI aligned with EU AI Act deployer obligations17. The framework emerged from late 2024 onwards through iterative regulatory and institutional deliberation, informed by regulatory analysis of MDR/AI Act interactions18,19 and targeted review of published AI governance models7,20 and adjacent safety paradigms drawn from pharmacovigilance and materiovigilance21,22, and was formalized in 2025 with the establishment, under executive mandate, of a multidisciplinary Data/AI Steering Committee. Rather than isolated algorithm validation, we propose a transversal institutional architecture integrating risk stratification, lifecycle-based monitoring, a structured AI-related adverse event (AI-AE) reporting taxonomy, and competency development with proficiency testing against simulated AI errors.

Regulatory framework: the EU AI Act and clinical AI

The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) establishes the first comprehensive legal framework governing the development and deployment of AI systems. It adopts a risk-based classification approach, categorizing AI systems into four tiers: unacceptable, high, limited, and minimal risk17,23. Under Art. 6(1)(b), AI systems are classified as high-risk when they are, or are safety components of, products required to undergo third-party conformity assessment under Union harmonisation legislation listed in Annex I. AI-based clinical software supporting diagnosis, therapeutic decision-making, or patient triage typically meets this condition through the Medical Device Regulation (MDR, EU 2017/745), which under Rule 11 classifies most such software as Class IIa or higher17,24. Annex III does not list healthcare as a general high-risk domain; only narrower uses fall within its scope, namely eligibility assessment by public authorities for essential public benefits and services including healthcare (point 5(a)) and emergency healthcare patient triage (point 5(d)). High-risk clinical AI systems are therefore subject to both the EU AI Act and the MDR, with conformity assessment conducted through a single notified body19,24,25.

The EU AI Act imposes on providers structured obligations covering risk management, data governance, technical documentation, transparency, human oversight, and cybersecurity (Art. 9–15). Deployers face specific obligations for system monitoring, effective human oversight, and transparency toward individuals subject to AI-based decisions (Art. 26). Post-market monitoring (Art. 72) and serious incident reporting (Art. 73) are primarily provider obligations but impose corresponding monitoring and escalation duties on deployers under Art. 26(5). A transversal AI literacy obligation applies to all staff operating AI systems (Art. 4).

The EU AI Act introduces AI-specific obligations without direct equivalents in the MDR, particularly regarding data governance, transparency, human oversight mechanisms, and AI literacy. For CCCs as deployers of clinical AI, this dual regulatory framework translates into concrete operational obligations: establishing governance structures, ensuring staff competency, monitoring deployed systems, and reporting incidents through formalized channels.

The regulatory timetable has since been refined by the Digital Omnibus on AI. Proposed by the European Commission in November 202526 and followed by a provisional political agreement between the European Parliament and the Council in May 2026, the package aims to simplify implementation while preserving the core risk-based architecture of the AI Act. As of June 2026, pending formal adoption of the legislative act, the expected application timeline for high-risk AI rules is deferred to 2 December 2027 for stand-alone high-risk AI systems (primarily under Annex III) and to 2 August 2028 for high-risk AI systems embedded in, or governed by, Union product-safety harmonisation legislation listed in Annex I27. This second timeline is particularly relevant to AI-based medical-device software classified under MDR Rule 11. In parallel, on 19 May 2026 the Commission issued, under Art. 6(5), draft guidelines on high-risk classification (comprising general principles and dedicated treatments of the Annex I product-safety and Annex III use-case routes) for stakeholder consultation closing 23 June 202628; although not legally binding, these guidelines reflect the Commission’s interpretation and are expected to inform implementation and enforcement. These developments do not reduce the need for hospital-level governance. Rather, they create a preparation window during which clinical deployers should build the inventories, risk-triage processes, AI literacy programs, monitoring infrastructures, and incident-reporting pathways required before full enforcement.

Despite these detailed requirements, most healthcare institutions, including CCCs, lack the governance architectures, data management frameworks, workforce competencies, and monitoring systems to meet them7,8. The EU AI Act defines what institutions must achieve but offers limited operational guidance for complex clinical environments (Table 1).

Institutional governance architecture

AI deployment in CCCs cannot be approached as a purely technical or departmental initiative. It requires an institutional governance architecture capable of simultaneously addressing clinical complexity, regulatory obligations, and organizational readiness. Figure 1 presents the proposed architecture, integrating an institutional AI doctrine, a multidisciplinary Data/AI Steering Committee, and three operational pillars (project intake and risk stratification, lifecycle management and AI-AE reporting, and human oversight and competency development) each detailed in a dedicated section below.

Institutional governance architecture for the responsible deployment of high-risk clinical AI systems.

The framework integrates a foundational AI doctrine, a multidisciplinary Data/AI Steering Committee, and three operational pillars. EU AI Act article references (right margin) indicate regulatory alignment as detailed in Table 1.

An explicit institutional AI doctrine establishes the shared reference for the framework. Its core principles are the primacy of human clinical judgment over algorithmic output, the positioning of AI strictly as a decision-support technology, proportionality between expected benefits and associated risks, and alignment with regulatory and ethical requirements. Rather than prescribing specific tools, this doctrine establishes boundary conditions applicable across clinical, research, and administrative functions. It is a living document, refined iteratively from operational feedback, emerging use cases, and regulatory developments7,29.

Under the doctrine, a Data/AI Steering Committee serves as the central coordination and decision-making body for AI- and data-related initiatives. Its multidisciplinary composition brings together clinical departments, information systems, data protection, project management, and institutional leadership, covering both strategic (institutional priorities, risk–benefit assessment, regulatory compliance) and technical (feasibility, data availability, interoperability, resources) dimensions. The committee oversees the full AI project lifecycle through formalized decision gates (approval, deferral, reformulation, rejection) and maintains a registry of all deployed or candidate AI tools using a standardized registration form (Supplementary Table S1).

Data governance is treated as a foundational prerequisite: a structuring principle distinguishes health from non-health data along one axis, and the proximity of each use to clinical decision-making along a second (Fig. 2), informing the risk stratification approach developed below. Patient transparency is a core component; at first contact, patients are informed that their data may be used for AI-based applications, with a formal opt-out mechanism ensuring compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation30. Technical infrastructure separates a controlled internal environment for development and testing from cloud-based certified hosting for production deployment.

Dual-axis framework for institutional AI risk stratification.

The horizontal axis distinguishes non-health from health data; the vertical axis reflects the proximity of the AI use to clinical decision-making. The four quadrants map each use case to an operational category and its corresponding MDR/EU AI Act regulatory pathway, indicated within each quadrant. Risk stratification and use case prioritization

The dual-axis framework introduced above (Fig. 2) serves not only as a data governance tool but as the primary instrument for institutional risk stratification and use case prioritization. By crossing the nature of data involved (health versus non-health) with the degree of influence on clinical decision-making, the framework generates a gradient of regulatory exposure and governance requirements that informs how the committee evaluates, sequences, and authorizes AI initiatives.

The first axis (data nature) determines baseline data governance obligations; GDPR for all health data, MDR when data inform clinical decisions. The second axis (proximity to clinical decision-making) determines regulatory exposure under the MDR and, by extension, under the EU AI Act: systems directly influencing clinical outcomes generally qualify as Class IIa or higher medical devices under MDR Rule 11 and fall within the high-risk category under Art. 6(1)(b), triggering the full deployer obligations detailed in the Regulatory Framework section. This stratification ensures that governance intensity is proportionate to actual risk rather than applied uniformly. Crucially, use cases are positioned by the proximity of their function to the clinical decision, not by the professional category of the user: a documentation tool remains low-proximity even when used by a care professional such as a clinical pharmacist, whereas a tool informing therapeutic choice is high-proximity irrespective of who operates it. This resolves the otherwise ambiguous classification of support tasks performed within care professions.

Three operational categories of AI use cases structure the institutional prioritization logic. The first two categories involve tools with limited direct clinical impact. Transversal applications supporting documentation, administrative workflows, and communication use non-health or anonymized data, present low regulatory risk, and are prioritized for early deployment to build institutional experience. Clinical-organizational tools (patient pathway coordination, activity reporting, quality monitoring, cohort identification for research) may involve health data but do not directly alter individual clinical decisions, placing them at intermediate risk conditional on adequate data governance, interoperability, and committee review, without triggering the full MDR compliance pathway.

The third category comprises high-risk clinical AI systems supporting diagnostic reasoning, therapeutic stratification, or treatment adaptation, under the dual EU AI Act/MDR framework. Deployment is conditional on governance maturity: validated data readiness, prospective workflow integration assessment, and formal lifecycle monitoring (detailed below). Applications threatening professional autonomy, lacking a safe integration pathway, or not amenable to meaningful human oversight are excluded; not as Art. 5 prohibition, but as an institutional determination that Art. 14 (human oversight) and Art. 9 (risk management) obligations cannot yet be satisfied.

Lifecycle management and AI-related adverse event reporting

AI systems deployed in clinical settings are not static: performance evolves with data distribution, workflows, user behavior, and software updates31,32, requiring lifecycle-oriented governance analogous to the total product lifecycle framework advocated by regulators33. Within this architecture, AI tools undergo staged oversight across pre-deployment assessment, controlled deployment, and continuous post-deployment monitoring.

Before any exposure to routine clinical environments, AI projects undergo structured pre-deployment evaluation including prototyping, controlled testing on non-clinical data, and assessment of feasibility, usability, and workflow integration, consistent with the DECIDE-AI guideline16. Formalized decision gates, managed by the Data/AI Steering Committee, determine progression between phases. Only systems demonstrating acceptable technical performance, safe workflow integration, and governance readiness proceed to broader deployment.

Once deployed, AI tools are monitored through quantitative and qualitative indicators: adoption and usage patterns, concordance between AI outputs and clinical decisions, user-reported usability, and performance drift detection via statistical monitoring of output distributions. Predefined conditions trigger recalibration, suspension, or withdrawal when performance degrades beyond acceptable thresholds or when contextual changes alter validation assumptions. This continuous monitoring operationalizes the post-market surveillance obligations defined in Art. 72 of the EU AI Act and aligns with the post-market clinical follow-up requirements of the MDR, as recently emphasized by the European Society of Radiology consensus recommendations on AI medical device surveillance34.

Algorithmovigilance, introduced by Embi35 and systematically compared with pharmacovigilance principles by Balendran et al.21, provides a conceptual foundation for structured post-market safety monitoring22. No operational equivalent exists for clinical AI; recent analyses of the FDA MAUDE database show existing medical device reporting systems fail to capture AI-specific failure modes36.

We propose an institutional AI-related adverse event (AI-AE) reporting system structured around five event categories (Table 2): performance failure, automation bias, workflow disruption, data-related incident, and human–AI interaction failure. Each category is graded by severity and by attribution confidence, the latter adapted from the WHO-UMC causality assessment system used in pharmacovigilance21,22.

Human oversight and competency development

The EU AI Act imposes a transversal AI literacy obligation (Art. 4) and requires human oversight of high-risk systems by adequately trained and competent individuals (Art. 14), reframing AI competency as a regulatory requirement with direct implications for institutional compliance and patient safety.

AI competencies are treated as a collective institutional responsibility: the objective is not to transform clinicians into data scientists but to ensure every professional interacting with an AI tool can use it in an informed, critical, and accountable manner. Three training levels are distinguished by degree of involvement37,38: (i) end users—intended use, known limitations, expected failure modes, output uncertainty, principles of human–AI interaction; (ii) clinical professionals involved in AI project design, evaluation, or governance—data governance, regulatory constraints, lifecycle monitoring; (iii) institutional leaders and Steering Committee members—strategic, ethical, and regulatory dimensions of AI governance, risk–benefit evaluation, and compliance oversight.

However, conventional training approaches (lectures, e-learning, conceptual workshops) address knowledge but do not reliably develop the operational vigilance required to detect AI errors in practice. Evidence from radiology shows that incorrect AI outputs increase both false-positive and false-negative rates among clinicians, with less experienced practitioners particularly susceptible to automation bias39,40. These findings indicate that the ability to override erroneous AI recommendations is not an innate clinical skill; it must be trained and periodically assessed.

We therefore propose that AI competency assessment incorporate structured proficiency testing, analogous to external quality assessment in laboratory medicine or test-case reviews in screening mammography. Test cases are curated from three complementary sources: de-identified scenarios drawn from the institutional case archive, vendor-supplied challenge sets where available, and synthetically perturbed outputs generated by modifying validated AI results. Deliberate errors are authored by a small multidisciplinary panel pairing domain clinicians with the tool’s clinical lead, and are designed against an explicit failure-mode or threat model derived from the five AI-AE categories defined in the Lifecycle Management and AI-Related Adverse Event Reporting section; they span plausible false negatives, false positives, miscalibrated confidence, and edge cases known to challenge the specific system. The assessment unit is not a binary pass/fail result but a calibrated error-detection rate, complemented by scoring of the appropriate downstream action: detection, correction or override, escalation, and initiation of the AI-AE reporting pathway (see the Lifecycle Management and AI-Related Adverse Event Reporting section).

Two illustrative examples convey the approach. For an auto-contouring assistant in radiotherapy, a test case may deliberately omit part of the brachial plexus, shift an organ-at-risk contour by a few millimetres, or assign a contour to the wrong laterality; the user is assessed on whether the deviation is detected, corrected, escalated, and documented before plan validation. For a clinical documentation tool based on a large language model, a test case may insert an incorrect chemotherapy line or hallucinate a molecular marker into a generated clinical summary; the user is assessed on whether the error is identified before the note is signed in the medical record.

For non-deterministic and generative systems, where identical inputs may yield different outputs, a single fixed answer key is insufficient: assessment shifts from pass/fail on a fixed set toward a detection rate measured over sampled outputs, and quality control relies on ongoing sampling rather than one-time validation, linking competency assessment to the lifecycle monitoring described in the Lifecycle Management and AI-Related Adverse Event Reporting section. Proficiency testing is thus conceived not as one-time certification but as a recurring component of continuing professional development, embedded within institutional quality frameworks such as professional practice evaluation, with individual and aggregate results fed back to users and retained as institutional evidence of oversight effectiveness. By periodically exposing users to realistic failure scenarios, this approach maintains clinical vigilance against automation complacency, reinforces AI as decision support rather than decision authority, and directly supports compliance with Art. 14.

Early implementation experience

The framework has already entered early operational use within ICL. Following an executive-level decision to structure AI governance, the Data/AI Steering Committee was formally established in 2025 and has convened in structured sessions since autumn 2025, marking the transition from informal experimentation to coordinated institutional steering. It operates as a single point of entry for Data/AI initiatives, bringing together institutional leadership, information systems, the project office, the data protection officer, and clinical and medico-technical functions. Over its first six months, three formal sessions, supplemented by intermediate meetings, reviewed approximately ten projects. Only a subset progressed to pilot implementation; others were deferred or reformulated because of insufficient data-governance readiness, uncertain regulatory qualification, limited interoperability, resource constraints, or misalignment with available funding.

These projects spanned all operational categories: predominantly transversal-productivity, clinical-organizational, and data-infrastructure initiatives, including report generation, document analysis, digital assistants, clinical research, and predictive modeling, with a smaller number of clinical decision-support applications, for example in radiology and radiation therapy. Each project was risk-stratified using the dual-axis model (see the Risk Stratification and Use Case Prioritization section). In line with this risk-proportionate logic, lower-risk transversal and clinical-organizational use cases were prioritized for early piloting. Beyond project triage, the committee produced foundational institutional instruments, including an AI-systems usage charter, a formalized project-intake process, and a Data/AI roadmap, operationalizing the doctrine described in the Institutional Governance Architecture section.

Early implementation also surfaced recurring challenges: clarifying roles across information-systems, data, and clinical functions; recruiting scarce profiles combining clinical and data-science expertise; aligning project timelines with external funding cycles; and addressing the scale of the acculturation and change-management effort required. The AI-AE reporting taxonomy and proficiency-testing program have been designed and embedded in the governance framework; however, no formal AI-AE report has yet been filed through the taxonomy, and no proficiency-testing assessment data have yet been generated. This early experience supports the feasibility of a governance-first approach, while prospective evaluation will be required to assess its impact on deployment efficiency, safety monitoring, and clinical outcomes.

Discussion

Current approaches focusing primarily on algorithmic performance are insufficient to ensure safe clinical integration; without structured governance, even high-performing AI systems are at risk of failing in real-world deployment. We present an operational governance framework for deploying high-risk clinical AI in a CCC, aligned with EU AI Act requirements; not isolated AI projects or abstract principles, but an integrated institutional architecture bridging regulatory and operational practice across the full AI lifecycle.

Several elements of this framework address the gaps identified in the current literature. Stetson et al.7 recently described responsible AI governance at a major North American cancer center in a US regulatory context, which does not address the specific requirements of the EU AI Act or the dual regulatory layer imposed by the MDR on AI-based medical devices. Our framework explicitly operationalizes this European regulatory environment at the institutional level, providing a concrete model for how deployer obligations under Art. 9, 14, 26, and 72 can be translated into governance structures, decision processes, and monitoring mechanisms (Table 1). Similarly, Balendran et al.21 established a rigorous conceptual parallel between pharmacovigilance and algorithmovigilance, but their work remains at the level of principles and comparative analysis. The AI-AE reporting system proposed here operationalizes these concepts at the institutional level, with a five-category event taxonomy, a severity grading system, and a reporting workflow integrated within existing governance structures. To our knowledge, this is among the first institutional algorithmovigilance systems with operational specificity, complementing the system-level monitoring infrastructure recently described at Vanderbilt University Medical Center20. The integration of proficiency testing into AI competency assessment, analogous to external quality assessment in laboratory medicine, extends current AI literacy frameworks37,38 beyond knowledge-based training toward the active development and verification of error detection capabilities, addressing the well-documented risks of automation bias in clinical AI use39,40.

Although developed in a comprehensive cancer center, the framework is not oncology-specific in design: its components (risk stratification, algorithmovigilance, and competency assessment) operate on the regulatory and organizational properties of clinical AI rather than on disease-specific features, and are intended to be transferable to general and university hospitals. Oncology nonetheless constitutes a demanding test environment, concentrating within a single institution a broad and high-density spectrum of clinical AI use cases (radiotherapy planning, diagnostic imaging, digital pathology, molecular biology, multidisciplinary tumor-board decision support, and clinical-trial matching) across data of widely varying sensitivity and clinical proximity. A comprehensive cancer center therefore functions as an institutional stress-test for AI governance rather than a narrowing of scope.

Several limitations merit acknowledgment. The framework is operationalized within a single French CCC and has not yet accumulated sufficient duration for prospective evaluation of its impact on clinical outcomes, safety events, or implementation efficiency. The AI-AE reporting system, while conceptually grounded, has not yet generated sufficient operational data to characterize reporting rates or the sensitivity of the proposed taxonomy; under-reporting and attribution uncertainty are anticipated challenges requiring iterative refinement, as in early pharmacovigilance systems. Likewise, the proficiency-testing program has been designed but not yet evaluated for its effect on error-detection performance. The regulatory landscape itself remains in transition: the Digital Omnibus on AI had reached provisional political agreement at the time of revision but still required formal adoption, while Commission guidance on high-risk AI classification and harmonized standards for AI Act compliance were still under development. The framework should therefore be interpreted as an operational readiness model rather than as a definitive legal compliance checklist. Transferability across healthcare systems and organizational cultures, while supported by the framework’s modular and disease-agnostic structure, remains to be demonstrated empirically beyond the originating institution.

Broader questions of cloud computing dependency, data localization, and the extraterritorial reach of foreign legislation (notably the US CLOUD Act) raise unresolved digital sovereignty challenges for European healthcare institutions that warrant dedicated analysis beyond this framework.

Patient-facing generative AI also increasingly shapes clinical encounters, as patients arrive with AI-generated interpretations of their condition or prognosis. How this reshapes clinician–patient interactions, trust, and shared decision-making is an emerging priority that governance frameworks must address.

Conclusion

The durable integration of AI into comprehensive cancer centers depends less on algorithmic performance than on institutional readiness and governance capacity. The EU AI Act, while imposing substantial compliance obligations, simultaneously provides a structuring opportunity for healthcare institutions to formalize oversight, accountability, and safety mechanisms that were previously absent or fragmented. Beyond the single-institution experience described here, collaborative efforts are needed to standardize AI governance across cancer centers and other clinical institutions, to develop shared algorithmovigilance databases enabling cross-institutional signal detection, and to establish validated competency assessment tools for clinical AI users. Early operational experience at our institution illustrates the feasibility of a governance-driven approach and the value of progressive, structured adoption over rapid, uncoordinated deployment. As AI becomes embedded in routine oncology practice, the question is no longer whether institutions can afford to build governance; it is whether they can afford not to.
