Finite-Frequency Fluctuation-Response Bounds for Open Quantum Systems
Jie Gu, Kangqiao Liu

Abstract
We derive a finite-frequency fluctuation-response inequality for Markovian open quantum systems in an input-output setting. For any downstream measurement of the emitted field, the measured lock-in response-to-noise matrix is bounded by the output-field quantum Fisher information rate. For dissipative amplitude modulation with vacuum inputs, this information rate is further bounded by a frequency-independent signal-channel activity, which reduces for kinetic modulation to the stationary channel fluxes. The result is detector-facing but unraveling-independent: it applies after choosing a measurement record, while the information ceiling is set by the quantum field before any detection scheme or trajectory representation is selected. We formulate the bound for multiple signal channels and real finite-frequency quadratures, and illustrate it with a single-sided cavity, resonance fluorescence, and a truncated Kerr-parametric cat resonator.



I
Introduction

Weak periodic signals are commonly detected through the fields emitted by an open quantum device. This is the case in quantum optics, circuit QED, optomechanics, resonance fluorescence, and mesoscopic transport, where the experimentally recorded object is typically an output current: a homodyne or heterodyne photocurrent, a photon-counting trace, or a filtered electrical current
9
;
4
;
42
. At a fixed analysis frequency, the central quantities are therefore not equal-time variances but spectra and lock-in response coefficients
13
. A natural question is then how large a coherent response at frequency
\omega
can be, relative to the spontaneous fluctuations seen in the same detector record and relative to the physical activity through which the signal is injected.

At thermal equilibrium, the fluctuation-dissipation theorem answers this type of question by relating linear response to equilibrium fluctuations
7
;
27
;
28
. Away from equilibrium, the equality is generally lost, but response and fluctuations remain constrained by information-theoretic principles
33
;
2
;
40
. Classical fluctuation-response inequalities express this idea through the Cramér–Rao bound or relative-entropy bounds on stochastic path measures: a large response requires either large fluctuations in the measured observable or large distinguishability between the unperturbed and perturbed dynamics
38
;
10
;
12
;
35
;
16
;
1
;
17
;
36
. Finite-frequency versions of this principle have recently been developed for classical Markovian dynamics, including jump processes and Langevin systems
15
;
43
. Related spectral bounds constrain deviations from equilibrium-style fluctuation-dissipation predictions in nonequilibrium Markov processes
23
;
24
;
14
;
22
. These results provide a well-defined classical framework for response precision at finite frequency.

Open quantum systems add a further layer that is essential for detector-facing bounds. Before any classical trajectory record is obtained, the system has emitted a quantum output field. Different detection schemes correspond to different positive-operator-valued measurements on this field. Photon counting, homodyne detection, heterodyne detection, and more general filtered measurements are therefore not merely different coarse grainings of a single classical trajectory; they are different downstream measurements of the same emitted quantum field
8
;
11
;
5
. A bound applied after choosing a particular quantum trajectory is operational for that detector, but it is not independent of the unraveling
41
. Conversely, a bound written only in terms of internal noncommutative correlation functions need not correspond directly to the spectra and response coefficients displayed by a laboratory receiver.

This paper formulates a finite-frequency fluctuation-response bound at the quantum input-output level. The emitted field is treated as the measurement-independent carrier of information, while the observed detector current is a classical record obtained from that field by a chosen measurement and subsequent filtering. The result is therefore detector-facing but unraveling-independent: its left-hand side contains only measurable output-current spectra and lock-in responses, while the upper limit is imposed before selecting photon counting, homodyne detection, heterodyne detection, or any other downstream measurement.

The central inequality is

\mathsf{R}^{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)\left[\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\right]^{+}\mathsf{R}(\omega)\preceq\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\preceq\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}.

(1)

Here
\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)
is the covariance matrix of the measured real lock-in current modes,
\mathsf{R}(\omega)
is the corresponding finite-frequency response matrix, and
\mathsf{M}^{+}
denotes the Moore–Penrose inverse of a matrix
\mathsf{M}
. The intermediate quantity
\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)
is the quantum Fisher information rate carried by the emitted output field at frequency
\omega
. The matrix
\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}
is the signal-channel activity associated with calibrated dissipative amplitude modulation of the Markovian coupling operators, and the factor
\mathbb{I}_{2}
acts on the cosine and sine components of the sinusoidal perturbation. Equation (
1
) states that no output detector can display a response-to-noise matrix larger than the information available in the emitted field, and that this field information is itself limited by the activity of the channels through which the signal enters.

The formulation is intended to match how finite-frequency sensing is performed in quantum technologies. The response matrix and the noise spectrum can be extracted from the same detector record using the same lock-in convention. The activity matrix is fixed independently by the calibrated signal-bearing coupling channels. Thus Eq. (
1
) gives a consistency relation between measured spectral signal-to-noise and the physical resources used to encode the signal. It applies to arbitrary downstream measurements of the output field and to any fixed-frequency linear lock-in filter, provided the open-system dynamics is Markovian, the perturbation is weak and sinusoidal, and the relevant signal channels are properly accounted for.

The result also clarifies the relation to response kinetic uncertainty relations. Response KURs and their quantum open-system extensions bound response precision by a Fisher-information/activity mechanism
31
;
32
. The present work places that mechanism at the level of the emitted field rather than at the level of a chosen measurement record. This change is crucial in quantum input-output settings, because different detectors access incompatible aspects of the same output field. The bound therefore gives a common information ceiling for homodyne, heterodyne, photon-counting, and more general detection architectures.

Beyond the general inequality, the paper develops a multiparameter matrix formulation for several modulated signal channels and for the two real quadratures of a periodic drive. It gives explicit detector-level response-to-noise bounds for output currents, connects the activity term to the calibrated dissipative coupling tangents, and works out representative examples.
The examples include a Gaussian coherent-input benchmark based on a single-sided cavity, a finite-dimensional resonance-fluorescence example for dissipative coupling modulation, and a truncated Kerr-parametric resonator illustrating the matrix activity bound.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
introduces the Markovian input-output model and states the main finite-frequency bounds. Section
III
discusses their operational interpretation. Section
IV
presents the three examples. Section
V
relates the result to classical finite-frequency fluctuation-response inequalities, spectral fluctuation-dissipation-response bounds, response KURs, quantum Cramér–Rao theory, input-output theory, and quantum trajectories. Section
VI
summarizes the limitations and possible extensions. The detailed derivation is given in the Appendix.

II
Main results

We consider a finite-dimensional quantum system coupled to Markovian bosonic input fields through channels indexed by
\mu=1,\ldots,n_{c}
. Writing

\mathcal{D}[L]\rho=L\rho L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\rho\},

(2)

the unperturbed reduced state obeys the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad equation
20
;
30

\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}\rho=-\mathrm{i}[H,\rho]+\sum_{\mu=1}^{n_{c}}\mathcal{D}[L_{\mu}]\rho.

(3)

We assume throughout the main theorems that the finite-dimensional
Markov semigroup is exponentially mixing. More precisely, let

\mathfrak{T}_{0}=\{X:\operatorname{Tr}X=0\}

be the traceless subspace, and assume that there is a constant

\gamma_{\rm mix}>0
such that

\operatorname{spec}\!\left(\mathcal{L}|_{\mathfrak{T}_{0}}\right)\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\leq-\gamma_{\rm mix}\}.

(4)

This assumption implies uniqueness of the stationary state

\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}
and exponential convergence to it. It is slightly
stronger than mere uniqueness of a stationary state, but it is the
natural condition under which the stationary output spectra, finite-time
lock-in limits, and Liouvillian resolvents used below are unambiguous.
In particular, for every real
\omega
the operator

-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L}

is invertible on
\mathfrak{T}_{0}
. All unperturbed spectra and
responses are evaluated in the stationary state
\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}
.
With
L_{\mu}(t)
denoting the Heisenberg-picture coupling operator, the
input and output fields satisfy
18
;
19

b_{\mu,\mathrm{out}}(t)=b_{\mu,\mathrm{in}}(t)+L_{\mu}(t).

(5)

Unless stated otherwise, the incoming fields are in vacuum.

A downstream detector performs a measurement on the output field and returns real currents

\mathbf{I}(t)=(I_{1}(t),\ldots,I_{m}(t))^{\mathrm{T}}.

(6)

This notation includes homodyne, heterodyne, counting, inefficient, and adaptive measurements. The resulting classical process need not be Markovian.

The signal is a weak time-dependent modulation of the coupling amplitudes. For
n_{p}
signal parameters, write
\epsilon(t)=(\epsilon_{1}(t),\ldots,\epsilon_{n_{p}}(t))
and

L_{\mu}^{(\epsilon)}(t)=L_{\mu}+\sum_{q=1}^{n_{p}}\epsilon_{q}(t)M_{\mu q}+O(\|\epsilon\|^{2}),

(7)

where
M_{\mu q}
is the tangent of channel
\mu
with respect to
parameter
q
. The activity theorem below is stated for purely
dissipative amplitude tangents. In the fixed input-output
representation used here, this means that the first-order tangent does
not contain an additional Hamiltonian-like Stinespring component:

\sum_{\mu}\left(L_{\mu}^{\dagger}M_{\mu q}-M_{\mu q}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}\right)=0,\qquad q=1,\ldots,n_{p}.

(8)

If this condition is not imposed, a coherent channel tangent appears in
the sequential channel Fisher information. Such a term is not bounded
by the jump activity alone and should be treated as a Hamiltonian signal
contribution.

The kinetic modulation used in several examples is

L_{\mu}^{(\epsilon)}(t)=\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q}b_{\mu q}\epsilon_{q}(t)\right]L_{\mu},

(9)

with real calibration coefficients
b_{\mu q}
. In this case

M_{\mu q}=b_{\mu q}L_{\mu}/2
, and the purely dissipative condition
(
8
) is automatically satisfied.

At a fixed nonzero frequency
\omega
, we use real unit-RMS envelopes

\phi_{c}(t)=\sqrt{2}\cos\omega t,\qquad\phi_{s}(t)=\sqrt{2}\sin\omega t,

(10)

for which
T^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}\phi_{\alpha}(t)\phi_{\beta}(t)\mathrm{d}t\to\delta_{\alpha\beta}
with
\alpha,\beta\in\{c,s\}
. The local finite-frequency signal is

\epsilon_{q}(t)=\eta_{q,c}\phi_{c}(t)+\eta_{q,s}\phi_{s}(t),

(11)

and the real parameter vector is ordered as

\eta=(\eta_{1,c},\eta_{1,s},\ldots,\eta_{n_{p},c},\eta_{n_{p},s})^{\mathrm{T}}.

(12)

For a one-dimensional signal direction we write
\eta=\epsilon\vartheta
, where
\vartheta
is a fixed real vector in this same space.

For each measured current component, define the finite-time positive-frequency mode

\tilde{I}_{a,T}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}t\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\left[I_{a}(t)-\langle I_{a}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}\right].

(13)

The real lock-in vector is

\mathsf{X}_{T}(\omega)=\left(\sqrt{2}\operatorname{Re}\tilde{I}_{1,T},\sqrt{2}\operatorname{Im}\tilde{I}_{1,T},\ldots,\sqrt{2}\operatorname{Re}\tilde{I}_{m,T},\sqrt{2}\operatorname{Im}\tilde{I}_{m,T}\right)^{\mathrm{T}},

(14)

with all Fourier modes evaluated at the same
\omega
. Its stationary noise matrix is

\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{Cov}\,\mathsf{X}_{T}(\omega).

(15)

The finite-frequency response matrix
\mathsf{R}(\omega)
is defined by

\delta\langle\mathsf{X}_{T}(\omega)\rangle=\sqrt{T}\,\mathsf{R}(\omega)\eta+o(\sqrt{T}\|\eta\|).

(16)

All matrix inequalities below use this real lock-in normalization. Other Fourier-amplitude conventions require the corresponding rescaling of both response and Fisher-information rates.

The measured response-to-noise matrix is

\mathsf{J}_{\rm meas}(\omega)=\mathsf{R}^{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)\left[\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\right]^{+}\mathsf{R}(\omega),

(17)

where the Moore-Penrose inverse acts on the support of
\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}
. For a direction
\vartheta
, the scalar
\vartheta^{\mathrm{T}}\mathsf{J}_{\rm meas}(\omega)\vartheta
is the largest response-to-noise ratio per unit time obtainable from a real linear combination of the measured lock-in components.

Let
\varrho_{\mathrm{out},\eta}^{T}(\omega)
be the output-field state over

[0,T]
generated by the local model (
11
). Its
symmetric-logarithmic-derivative quantum Fisher information matrix with
respect to
\eta
at
\eta=0
is denoted by

F_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q,T}(\omega)

25
;
26
;
6
.

We define the frequency-resolved output-field QFI rate directionally.
For every real direction

\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n_{p}}
, set

\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega;\vartheta)=\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\vartheta^{\mathrm{T}}F_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q,T}(\omega)\vartheta.

(18)

This definition avoids taking a matrix
\limsup
, which is not a
canonical operation in the Loewner order. Whenever the ordinary matrix
limit exists, for example in operator norm,

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega)=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}F_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q,T}(\omega),

(19)

then

\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega;\vartheta)=\vartheta^{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega)\vartheta.

Thus all statements below are first formulated as directional quadratic
inequalities; the corresponding matrix inequalities follow whenever the
matrix QFI-rate limit exists.
The derivation is given in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 1

(Output-field data-processing bound)
.

Under the mixing assumption (
4
), for any
downstream measurement of the output field producing currents

\mathbf{I}(t)
, and for any weak sinusoidal signal at a fixed nonzero
frequency
\omega
, the measured response-to-noise matrix satisfies

\vartheta^{\mathrm{T}}\mathsf{R}^{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)\left[\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\right]^{+}\mathsf{R}(\omega)\vartheta\leq\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega;\vartheta)

(20)

for every real signal direction
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n_{p}}
.
If the matrix limit (
19
) exists, this is
equivalently the Loewner inequality

\mathsf{R}^{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)\left[\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\right]^{+}\mathsf{R}(\omega)\preceq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega).

(21)

Theorem
1
is independent of the chosen detector.
Photon counting, homodyne detection, heterodyne detection, inefficient
detection, and adaptive detection merely correspond to different
classical channels or POVMs applied to the same output field, and none
can increase the quantum Fisher information of that field.

For the dissipative tangent (
7
), define the
signal-activity matrix

(\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig})_{qr}=4\operatorname{Re}\sum_{\mu}\operatorname{Tr}\left[M_{\mu q}^{\dagger}M_{\mu r}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\right].

(22)

For the kinetic modulation (
9
), this reduces to

(\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig})_{qr}=\sum_{\mu}b_{\mu q}b_{\mu r}\operatorname{Tr}\left[L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\right].

(23)

The diagonal element

\operatorname{Tr}(L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}})
is the stationary photon flux,
jump rate, or tunneling rate in channel
\mu
.

Theorem 2

(Input-output activity bound)
.

For the purely dissipative amplitude modulation
(
7
) satisfying
(
8
), with vacuum Markov inputs and stationary
state
\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}
, the output-field quantum Fisher information rate
satisfies, for every fixed nonzero frequency
\omega
and every real
signal direction
\vartheta
,

\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega;\vartheta)\leq\vartheta^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}\right)\vartheta.

(24)

If the matrix limit (
19
) exists, then

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega)\preceq\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}.

(25)

For the kinetic modulation (
9
), the right-hand side is
the channel activity matrix (
23
).

Combining Theorems
1
and
2

gives the finite-frequency input-output fluctuation-response inequality
in directional form:

{\vartheta^{\mathrm{T}}\mathsf{R}^{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)\left[\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\right]^{+}\mathsf{R}(\omega)\vartheta\leq\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega;\vartheta)\leq\vartheta^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}\right)\vartheta.}

(26)

Since this holds for every
\vartheta
, it also implies the
detector-facing matrix inequality

{\mathsf{R}^{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)\left[\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\right]^{+}\mathsf{R}(\omega)\preceq\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}.}

(27)

When the output-field QFI-rate matrix exists, the sharper chain

{\mathsf{R}^{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)\left[\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\right]^{+}\mathsf{R}(\omega)\preceq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega)\preceq\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}}

(28)

is recovered.

In the usual scalar complex notation for one measured current and one
signal direction, the same statement is written as

{\frac{|R_{\rm cplx}(\omega)|^{2}}{S^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)}\leq\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q,{\rm scalar}}(\omega)\leq\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig},}

(29)

where
R_{\rm cplx}
packages the two real lock-in response components
and

\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q,{\rm scalar}}(\omega)
is the corresponding
directional QFI-rate upper limit in the real two-quadrature convention.

There is a closely related coherent-input version. Suppose the signal is a weak coherent displacement of an incoming vacuum field,

b_{\mu,\mathrm{in}}(t)\rightarrow b_{\mu,\mathrm{in}}(t)+\epsilon f(t),

(30)

rather than a modulation of
L_{\mu}
. The input coherent state already carries a quantum Fisher information rate equal to
4
for the real displacement amplitude in the normalization used here (details given in Appendix
B
). The joint input-system-output evolution is unitary, and tracing out unobserved degrees of freedom cannot increase quantum Fisher information. Hence

\mathsf{R}^{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)\left[\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\right]^{+}\mathsf{R}(\omega)\preceq 4\mathbb{I}_{2},

(31)

or in scalar form

{\frac{|R_{\rm cplx}(\omega)|^{2}}{S^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)}\leq 4.}

(32)

This version is useful when the experimentally applied signal is an incoming microwave or optical tone rather than a modulated decay rate.

III
Remarks on the main results

Eq. (
28
) is not an operator fluctuation-dissipation theorem. It does not assert that a retarded commutator is bounded by a symmetrized internal spectrum of the same operator. Instead, it refers to detector records. The matrix
\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)
is the covariance matrix of the real lock-in components of currents actually measured outside the system. The response matrix
\mathsf{R}(\omega)
is obtained by applying a weak sinusoidal signal and measuring the coherent lock-in output at the same frequency. Both quantities can be obtained without reconstructing the system density matrix.

The inequality is not tied to one unraveling. Photon counting, homodyne detection, heterodyne detection, and adaptive measurements are different measurements on the same output field. The first inequality in Eq. (
28
) says that every such measurement produces a classical Fisher information no larger than the quantum Fisher information of the output field. A fixed quantum-jump trajectory gives a valid classical record, but it is only one possible readout.

The third point concerns the role of noncommutativity. Noncommuting system operators enter through the Lindblad evolution and through the input-output relation. In a driven qubit, for example, a homodyne current measures a field quadrature proportional to a dipole quadrature of the atom. Its spectrum contains Rabi oscillations and phase-sensitive correlations. These effects are absent in a classical jump process with the same average jump rate. The bound is therefore not purely classical, even though the final measured current is a classical time series.

The input-output formulation is essential for experimental interpretation. Internal system observables are not always directly measured. Detectors see fields leaving the system, and the detected current contains both system radiation and input noise. The relation
b_{\mathrm{out}}=b_{\mathrm{in}}+L
provides the link between Lindblad dynamics and observed spectra
18
;
19
;
9
. This is why Eq. (
28
) is formulated in terms of
\mathsf{R}(\omega)
and
\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)
, rather than in terms of abstract internal correlation functions. It is also why homodyne or heterodyne spectra can display coherent quantum features, such as those in resonance fluorescence
34
, while still being constrained by a channel activity. The distinctive feature of the present inequality is therefore not frequency dependence alone, but the placement of an output-field QFI between experimentally measured spectra and a calibrated signal activity.
The right-hand side is deliberately chosen to be experimentally calibratable. For kinetic coupling modulation,
\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}
is built from steady channel fluxes. In a fluorescence experiment this is the photon emission rate. In a transport experiment this is a tunneling rate. In a lossy cavity this is the photon loss flux through a specified port. This is less abstract than a Kubo-Mori or symmetric-logarithmic-derivative correlation spectrum of an internal system operator.

IV
Examples

IV.1
Single-sided cavity

As a preliminary Gaussian benchmark, we consider a passive single-sided cavity. Since its Hilbert space is infinite-dimensional, this example is used only for the coherent-input bound (
31
), not for the finite-dimensional activity theorem.
Consider a passive single-port cavity with annihilation operator
a
, Hamiltonian

H=\Delta a^{\dagger}a,

(33)

and input-output coupling

L=\sqrt{\kappa}\,a.

(34)

The Langevin equation is

\dot{a}(t)=-\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}\Delta\right)a(t)-\sqrt{\kappa}\,b_{\mathrm{in}}(t),

(35)

and, with the convention of Eq. (
5
),

b_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=b_{\mathrm{in}}(t)+\sqrt{\kappa}\,a(t).

(36)

Equation (
35
) is the standard single-port
Markovian input-output Langevin equation
18
,
obtained by applying the Heisenberg equation with coupling operator

L=\sqrt{\kappa}a
. Solving it in the frequency domain gives

a(\omega)=-\sqrt{\kappa}\,\chi_{c}(\omega)b_{\mathrm{in}}(\omega),\qquad\chi_{c}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}(\Delta-\omega)}.

(37)

Therefore

b_{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)=s(\omega)b_{\mathrm{in}}(\omega),\qquad s(\omega)=1-\kappa\chi_{c}(\omega).

(38)

For a lossless single-sided cavity,

s(\omega)=\frac{-\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}(\Delta-\omega)}{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\mathrm{i}(\Delta-\omega)},\qquad|s(\omega)|=1.

(39)

For an ideal homodyne measurement of the output quadrature,

I_{\theta}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}b_{\mathrm{out}}(t)+e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}b_{\mathrm{out}}^{\dagger}(t),

(40)

the unperturbed input is vacuum. Since the single-port scattering
relation is passive and lossless,
|s(\omega)|=1
, the output
quadrature is again a vacuum quadrature. In the real lock-in
normalization of Eq. (
15
), its shot-noise spectrum is
therefore

S^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)=1.

(41)

Now consider a weak coherent displacement of the incoming vacuum field,

b_{\mathrm{in}}(t)\mapsto b_{\mathrm{in}}(t)+\epsilon f(t).

(42)

In the scalar coherent-input notation of Eq. (
32
),

R_{\rm cplx}
is the coefficient of the linear change in the
phase-matched homodyne mean. The displacement is scattered as

\delta\langle b_{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\rangle=s(\omega)\epsilon f(\omega).

(43)

Hence

\delta\langle I_{\theta}(\omega)\rangle=2\epsilon f(\omega)\,\operatorname{Re}\!\left[e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}s(\omega)\right].

(44)

Optimizing over the homodyne phase,
\theta=\arg s(\omega)
, gives

|R_{\rm cplx}(\omega)|=\max_{\theta}2\left|\operatorname{Re}\!\left[e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}s(\omega)\right]\right|=2|s(\omega)|.

(45)

For the lossless single-sided cavity,
|s(\omega)|=1
. Therefore

\frac{|R_{\rm cplx}(\omega)|^{2}}{S^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)}=4,

(46)

which saturates the coherent-input data-processing bound (
32
). The cavity
can reshape and phase-shift the signal, but it cannot create information
about a displacement that entered only through the input field.

IV.2
Resonance fluorescence

Let
\sigma_{x}
,
\sigma_{y}
, and
\sigma_{z}
be Pauli operators, and let

\sigma_{-}=|g\rangle\langle e|,\qquad\sigma_{+}=\sigma_{-}^{\dagger}.

(47)

We consider a driven two-level atom with one measured radiative channel,

H=\frac{\Delta}{2}\sigma_{z}+\frac{\Omega}{2}\sigma_{x},\qquad L=\sqrt{\kappa}\,\sigma_{-}.

(48)

The output field and ideal homodyne current are

b_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=b_{\mathrm{in}}(t)+\sqrt{\kappa}\,\sigma_{-}(t),\qquad I_{\theta}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}b_{\mathrm{out}}(t)+e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}b_{\mathrm{out}}^{\dagger}(t).

(49)

The corresponding system quadrature is

X_{\theta}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}L+e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}L^{\dagger}=\sqrt{\kappa}\left(\cos\theta\,\sigma_{x}-\sin\theta\,\sigma_{y}\right).

(50)

We modulate the radiative coupling amplitude as

L^{(\epsilon)}(t)=e^{\epsilon(t)/2}L.

(51)

Since this is the kinetic modulation of Eq. (
9
), the activity appearing on the right-hand side of the main bound is the steady fluorescence flux,

\mathcal{A}=\operatorname{Tr}[L^{\dagger}L\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}]=\kappa\langle\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}.

(52)

We now verify the main inequality explicitly for the resonant case
\Delta=0
. Define

D=\kappa^{2}+2\Omega^{2},\qquad p_{e}=\langle\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{D},\qquad\mathcal{A}=\kappa p_{e}.

(53)

The steady-state Bloch components are

\langle\sigma_{x}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=0,\qquad\langle\sigma_{y}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=\frac{2\Omega\kappa}{D},\qquad\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{D}.

(54)

With the real unit-RMS lock-in convention of Sec.
II
, the coupling modulation produces no linear response in the
\sigma_{x}
quadrature. Therefore

R_{\theta}(\omega)=-\sin\theta\,R_{y}(\omega),\qquad S_{\theta}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)=\cos^{2}\theta\,S_{x}(\omega)+\sin^{2}\theta\,S_{y}(\omega).

(55)

Let

d(\omega)=\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}-\mathrm{i}\omega\right)\left(\kappa-\mathrm{i}\omega\right)+\Omega^{2}.

(56)

The phase-quadrature response is

R_{y}(\omega)=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}\,\Omega\kappa}{D}\frac{3\Omega^{2}-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}-\omega^{2}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\kappa\omega}{2}}{d(\omega)}.

(57)

This expression contains both the dynamical response of the atom and the direct input-output contribution from the explicit dependence of
L^{(\epsilon)}
on
\epsilon
. The two quadrature spectra are

S_{x}(\omega)=1+\frac{2\kappa^{2}\Omega^{2}}{D\left[(\kappa/2)^{2}+\omega^{2}\right]},

(58)

and

S_{y}(\omega)=1-\frac{4\kappa\Omega^{2}}{D^{2}}\operatorname{Re}\frac{\kappa(\kappa^{2}-4\Omega^{2})-\mathrm{i}\omega(\kappa^{2}-2\Omega^{2})}{d(\omega)}.

(59)

The leading term
1
in each spectrum is the vacuum shot-noise contribution.

This distinction is also important relative to quantum-trajectory fluctuation-response bounds

The bound is therefore unraveling-independent at the field level, while still reducing to classical or trajectory-level activity bounds in the appropriate monitored jump limits.

VI
Discussion and conclusion

The main message of Eq. (
28
) is that finite-frequency response precision in an output measurement is limited before an unraveling is chosen. The measured side of the inequality is classical spectral data: the response matrix
\mathsf{R}(\omega)
and the output-current spectrum
\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)
. The intermediate object is the QFI rate of the emitted quantum field, which bounds the information available to any downstream detector. For dissipative amplitude modulation of Markovian coupling channels, this QFI rate is further bounded by the signal activity
\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}
. The result therefore occupies a middle ground between two extremes. It is more quantum than applying a classical fluctuation-response inequality to a fixed trajectory record, but more operational than a bound written only in terms of internal noncommutative correlation functions.

The inequality is most useful as a detector-facing diagnostic bound. A violation of Eq. (
28
) would point to missing physics or inconsistent calibration. Possible causes include unaccounted signal paths, non-Markovian filtering, detector nonlinearities, unmodelled gain, or an incorrect normalization of the measured response.

The bound is a linear-response result. The response matrix is defined at
\epsilon=0
, and the noise spectrum is that of the unperturbed steady state. Nonlinear response can be treated by higher-order information inequalities
3
, but Eq. (
28
) is a first-order finite-frequency statement.

The frequency independence of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}
follows from the Markovian nature of the signal channel. A Markovian bath has no memory, and a local change in the coupling injects distinguishability at a rate fixed by the instantaneous activity. If the bath is non-Markovian, or if the signal is filtered before reaching the system, the right-hand side generally becomes frequency dependent.

The examples illustrate different roles of the bound. The single-sided cavity gives a Gaussian benchmark in which the coherent-input version can be saturated because the output field is related to the input field by a linear scattering transformation. The resonance-fluorescence example shows how a finite-dimensional quantum emitter can display phase-sensitive homodyne response while still obeying the same output-field information constraint. The truncated Kerr-parametric cat resonator provides a non-Gaussian matrix validation in a larger Hilbert space, where the response and noise spectra are no longer reducible to a simple passive scattering problem. Taken together, these examples show that the inequality is not tied to photon counting, classical jump trajectories, or zero-frequency observables.

The tightness of the bound depends strongly on the measurement architecture and on the amount of unobserved information loss. Linear passive systems driven and measured through the relevant port can saturate the coherent-input version. Unobserved loss channels, thermal noise, inefficient detection, and internal nonlinear dynamics generally make the inequality strict. Inefficient detection can be modelled by inserting beam splitters before ideal detectors; this can only decrease the measured signal-to-noise ratio, so the bound remains valid, but the achievable precision moves farther below the activity limit. Nonlinear quantum systems may approach the bound near resonant features and for optimized quadrature measurements, but exact saturation should not be expected generically.

Several assumptions should be kept explicit. The derivation assumes Markovian input fields, weak sinusoidal signals, stationary operation, and calibrated accounting of the relevant output channels. If the bath has memory, the information injected by the signal may become frequency dependent, and a frequency-independent activity bound need not hold in the present form. If the signal changes the stationary state strongly or drives the system into a nonlinear-response regime, higher-order response terms are required. If some output channels are unobserved or if the detector chain contains nontrivial filtering, the measured spectra must be interpreted as spectra of the accessible field after those transformations, not as spectra of the ideal output field itself.

The multiparameter case also raises a genuine quantum issue. The scalar inequality along any fixed signal direction follows from the ordinary single-parameter QFI bound. For several signal quadratures or several modulated channels, however, the SLD-QFI matrix need not be jointly attainable by a single measurement when the corresponding parameters are incompatible. The matrix inequality in Eq. (
28
) should therefore be read as an information upper bound on all downstream measurements, not automatically as a jointly saturable estimation bound. A finite-frequency Holevo-type formulation is the natural next step when simultaneous optimal estimation of incompatible signal components is required
26
.

Another important extension is the Hamiltonian signal case. If the perturbation is

H(t)=H_{0}-\epsilon f(t)B,

(87)

then the signal is not injected through a jump activity. The corresponding information cost should instead be controlled by a quantum Fisher strength associated with the Hamiltonian tangent vector generated by
B
. Such a result would be closer to a noncommutative operator-level FRI and would generally not reduce to a simple channel flux. The input-output data-processing part of Eq. (
28
) should still apply, but the simple activity expression (
23
) is special to dissipative amplitude modulation of Markovian coupling operators.

The most direct experimental settings for testing the bound are platforms where input-output theory and calibrated detection are already standard, including resonance fluorescence, circuit QED, optomechanics, and mesoscopic transport. Homodyne and heterodyne detection are particularly useful because they access coherent quadrature response that may be invisible in simple counting statistics. In such settings the inequality provides a direct consistency relation between measured finite-frequency signal-to-noise ratio and independently calibrated signal-channel activity.

In summary, we have formulated a finite-frequency fluctuation-response inequality for open quantum systems in an input-output setting. At each frequency, the optimal linear response-to-noise ratio of measured output currents is bounded by the QFI rate of the emitted field, and for dissipative amplitude modulation of Markovian coupling channels this QFI rate is bounded by a signal activity. The central structural feature is detector-facing but unraveling-independent: different measurements are treated as different POVMs on the same output field, while the bound itself is imposed before any of them is selected. Extending this structure to non-Markovian environments, Hamiltonian perturbations, nonlinear response, and multiparameter incompatible estimation would clarify how broadly finite-frequency response precision in quantum open systems is constrained by information injected into the output field.

contributes only to the
O(\Delta t^{2})
remainder, while the jump
derivatives give the leading
O(\Delta t)
term.

For the full time interval, concatenate the bin isometries and retain
the final system and all field bins. The actual observed output field
is obtained from this enlarged state by tracing out the final system and
any unobserved fields, so monotonicity of QFI allows us to bound the
output-field QFI by the QFI of the enlarged sequential state. Applying
the amortized channel-QFI chain rule to the sequence of bins gives

F_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q,T}(\omega;\vartheta)\leq 4\sum_{k}\sum_{\mu}\operatorname{Tr}\left[N_{\mu}^{\vartheta}(t_{k})^{\dagger}N_{\mu}^{\vartheta}(t_{k})\rho_{0}(t_{k})\right]\Delta t+O(T\Delta t),

(107)

where
\rho_{0}(t_{k})
is the unperturbed pre-bin system state. The

O(T\Delta t)
term vanishes in the continuous-time limit at fixed

T
. Since the unperturbed process is initialized in the stationary
state,
\rho_{0}(t_{k})=\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}
. Passing to the continuous-time
limit therefore yields

F_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q,T}(\omega;\vartheta)\leq 4\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}t\,\sum_{\mu}\operatorname{Tr}\left[N_{\mu}^{\vartheta}(t)^{\dagger}N_{\mu}^{\vartheta}(t)\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\right]+o(T).

(108)

Substituting the definition (
97
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}F_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q,T}(\omega;\vartheta)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q,r}\sum_{a,b\in\{c,s\}}\vartheta_{q,a}\vartheta_{r,b}\left[\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\phi_{a}(t)\phi_{b}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right]4\operatorname{Re}\sum_{\mu}\operatorname{Tr}\left[M_{\mu q}^{\dagger}M_{\mu r}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\right]+o(1).

(109)

For fixed nonzero
\omega
, the real unit-RMS modes satisfy

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\phi_{a}(t)\phi_{b}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\longrightarrow\delta_{ab}.

(110)

Taking the upper long-time limit gives

\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega;\vartheta)\leq\sum_{q,r}\sum_{a\in\{c,s\}}\vartheta_{q,a}\vartheta_{r,a}(\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig})_{qr}=\vartheta^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}\right)\vartheta.

(111)

This proves Theorem
2
. If the matrix QFI-rate limit
(
19
) exists, the same directional inequality
for all
\vartheta
is equivalent to

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega)\preceq\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}.

For the kinetic modulation
M_{\mu q}=b_{\mu q}L_{\mu}/2
, the activity
matrix becomes

(\mathcal{A}_{\rm sig})_{qr}=\sum_{\mu}b_{\mu q}b_{\mu r}\operatorname{Tr}\left[L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\right],

(112)

which is Eq. (
23
). The right-hand side is the
steady event flux in the modulated channels, weighted by the calibrated
signal-coupling coefficients.

Appendix B
Coherent input drive bound

For a coherent input displacement,

b_{\mathrm{in}}(t)\rightarrow b_{\mathrm{in}}(t)+\epsilon f(t),

(113)

the parameter is encoded in the incoming field before the field interacts with the system. Let

f(t)=u\,\phi_{a}(t),\qquad a\in\{c,s\},

(114)

where
u
is a fixed input-channel vector and the real frequency modes are normalized as

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\phi_{a}(t)\phi_{b}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\longrightarrow\delta_{ab}.

(115)

For each real mode define

B_{u,a}=T^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{T}\phi_{a}(t)\,u^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}B_{\mathrm{in}}(t),\qquad[B_{u,a},B_{u,a}^{\dagger}]=1,

(116)

assuming
u^{\dagger}u=1
. The incoming vacuum displaced by a small real amplitude
\epsilon
is therefore, in this mode,

\ket{\psi_{\epsilon}}=\exp\!\left[\epsilon\sqrt{T}(B_{u,a}^{\dagger}-B_{u,a})\right]\ket{0}.

(117)

Using the pure-state formula

F_{Q}=4\left(\braket{\partial_{\epsilon}\psi_{\epsilon}|\partial_{\epsilon}\psi_{\epsilon}}-|\braket{\psi_{\epsilon}|\partial_{\epsilon}\psi_{\epsilon}}|^{2}\right),

(118)

one obtains
F_{Q}^{T}=4T
, and hence a quantum Fisher information rate equal to
4
for each unit-RMS real input quadrature. The cross term between the cosine and sine modes vanishes in the long-time limit by their orthogonality, so the input-field QFI rate is
4\mathbb{I}_{2}
in the two-dimensional real quadrature space.

The subsequent system-field evolution is unitary on the joint input, system, and output degrees of freedom. Discarding the system or unobserved output channels is a quantum channel, and therefore cannot increase quantum Fisher information. Hence, for every real input-quadrature direction
\vartheta
,

\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega;\vartheta)\leq 4\,\vartheta^{\mathrm{T}}\vartheta.

(119)

If the corresponding matrix QFI-rate limit exists, this is equivalently

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{out}}^{Q}(\omega)\preceq 4\mathbb{I}_{2}.

Together with Theorem
1
, this gives
Eq. (
31
).

Appendix C
Liouvillian formulae for response and spectra

For completeness, we spell out the Liouvillian formulae used in the examples. The purpose of this subsection is not to introduce an additional assumption, but to fix the precise convention behind the resolvents, the homodyne insertion superoperator, and the direct input-output term.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the unperturbed Lindblad generator,

\mathcal{L}\rho=-\mathrm{i}[H,\rho]+\sum_{\mu}\mathcal{D}[L_{\mu}]\rho,

(120)

and let
\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}
be its stationary state,

\mathcal{L}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}=0,\qquad\operatorname{Tr}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}=1.

(121)

We use the projection

\mathcal{Q}Y=Y-\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\operatorname{Tr}Y,

(122)

which removes the stationary trace component. On the traceless subspace, the mixing assumption implies that

-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L}

is invertible for every real nonzero
\omega
. Equivalently, throughout this subsection one may read

(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L})^{-1}
as the reduced resolvent acting on the
\mathcal{Q}
-projected subspace.

Suppose first that the system generator is weakly perturbed as

\dot{\rho}(t)=\mathcal{L}\rho(t)+\epsilon(t)\mathcal{V}\rho(t)+O(\epsilon^{2}),

(123)

where
\mathcal{V}=\partial_{\epsilon}\mathcal{L}^{(\epsilon)}|_{\epsilon=0}
is the first-order perturbation superoperator. Linearizing around the stationary state,

\rho(t)=\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}+\delta\rho(t)+O(\epsilon^{2}),

(124)

gives

\delta\dot{\rho}(t)=\mathcal{L}\delta\rho(t)+\epsilon(t)\mathcal{V}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}.

(125)

For a positive-frequency perturbation

\epsilon(t)=\epsilon_{\omega}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t},\qquad\delta\rho(t)=\epsilon_{\omega}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\delta\rho(\omega),

(126)

Eq. (
125
) gives

(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L})\delta\rho(\omega)=\mathcal{V}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}.

(127)

Thus

\delta\rho(\omega)=(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{V}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}.

(128)

Since
\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{V}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}]=0
for a trace-preserving perturbation of the generator, the source term lies in the traceless subspace, where the reduced resolvent is well defined.

Now let the measured output current have the form

I_{a}(t)=\xi_{a}(t)+X_{a}(t),

(129)

where
\xi_{a}(t)
denotes the vacuum input noise contribution and
X_{a}
is the system operator appearing in the corresponding output quadrature. For homodyne detection of one output channel with coupling operator
L
and local-oscillator phase
\theta
,

I_{\theta}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}b_{\mathrm{out}}(t)+e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}b_{\mathrm{out}}^{\dagger}(t)=\xi_{\theta}(t)+X_{\theta}(t),

(130)

with

\xi_{\theta}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}b_{\mathrm{in}}(t)+e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}b_{\mathrm{in}}^{\dagger}(t),\qquad X_{\theta}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}L+e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}L^{\dagger}.

(131)

The response of the mean current has two contributions. The first is the indirect response caused by the perturbation of the density matrix. Using Eq. (
128
), this contribution is

R_{a}^{\rm ind}(\omega)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{a}(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{V}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\right].

(132)

The second is a direct response, present only if the measured output operator itself depends explicitly on the signal. If

X_{a}^{(\epsilon)}=X_{a}+\epsilon\,\dot{X}_{a}+O(\epsilon^{2}),\qquad\dot{X}_{a}=\partial_{\epsilon}X_{a}^{(\epsilon)}|_{\epsilon=0},

(133)

then the direct contribution is

R_{a}^{\rm dir}=\operatorname{Tr}[\dot{X}_{a}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}].

(134)

Therefore the full complex response coefficient is

R_{a}(\omega)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{a}(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{V}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\right]+R_{a}^{\rm dir}.

(135)

For several signal parameters
\epsilon_{q}
, one replaces
\mathcal{V}
by
\mathcal{V}_{q}
and
\dot{X}_{a}
by
\dot{X}_{a,q}
:

R_{a,q}(\omega)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{a}(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{V}_{q}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\right]+\operatorname{Tr}[\dot{X}_{a,q}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}].

(136)

In the truncated cat-resonator example, the monitored external coupling is modulated as

L_{{\rm ex},N}^{(\epsilon_{\rm ex})}=e^{\epsilon_{\rm ex}/2}L_{{\rm ex},N}=L_{{\rm ex},N}+\frac{\epsilon_{\rm ex}}{2}L_{{\rm ex},N}+O(\epsilon_{\rm ex}^{2}).

(137)

Hence

X_{\theta,N}^{(\epsilon_{\rm ex})}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}L_{{\rm ex},N}^{(\epsilon_{\rm ex})}+e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}L_{{\rm ex},N}^{(\epsilon_{\rm ex})\dagger}=X_{\theta,N}+\frac{\epsilon_{\rm ex}}{2}X_{\theta,N}+O(\epsilon_{\rm ex}^{2}).

(138)

Thus

\dot{X}_{\theta,N;{\rm ex}}=\frac{1}{2}X_{\theta,N},\qquad\dot{X}_{\theta,N;{\rm in}}=0,

(139)

because the internal loss channel is not the monitored output port. Therefore

\operatorname{Tr}[\dot{X}_{\theta,N;q}\rho_{\mathrm{ss},N}]=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{q,{\rm ex}}\operatorname{Tr}[X_{\theta,N}\rho_{\mathrm{ss},N}],

(140)

which gives

R_{\theta,q,N}(\omega)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{\theta,N}(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L}_{N})^{-1}\mathcal{V}_{q,N}\rho_{\mathrm{ss},N}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{q,{\rm ex}}\operatorname{Tr}[X_{\theta,N}\rho_{\mathrm{ss},N}].

(
72
)

We next derive the homodyne spectrum. For a stationary current, define the connected correlation function

C_{ab}(\tau)=\langle\delta I_{a}(t+\tau)\delta I_{b}(t)\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}},\qquad\delta I_{a}(t)=I_{a}(t)-\langle I_{a}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}.

(141)

The output spectrum is the Fourier transform

S_{ab}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}C_{ab}(\tau).

(142)

The vacuum input part of the homodyne current gives the white shot-noise contribution. For a single ideal homodyne current this contribution is
1
in the real lock-in normalization used in the main text.

The system-dependent part is obtained from the quantum regression theorem. For the homodyne current in Eq. (
131
), define the current-insertion superoperator

\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}L\rho+e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\rho L^{\dagger}.

(143)

This superoperator represents the effect of inserting the earlier output-current operator into a two-time correlation function.
To see this insertion rule, consider an infinitesimal output time bin
and write the homodyne increment as

dY_{\theta}(t)=e^{-i\theta}dB_{\rm out}(t)+e^{i\theta}dB_{\rm out}^{\dagger}(t).

For a system state
\rho
, the one-bin Stinespring map gives, to the
order needed here, field coherences

V\rho V^{\dagger}=\cdots+L\rho\,\sqrt{dt}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 0|+\rho L^{\dagger}\sqrt{dt}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 1|+\cdots.

Using

dB_{\rm out}\simeq\sqrt{dt}\,|0\rangle\langle 1|
and

dB_{\rm out}^{\dagger}\simeq\sqrt{dt}\,|1\rangle\langle 0|
,
and tracing over the output bin, one obtains

\operatorname{Tr}_{\rm bin}\!\left[(\mathbf{1}\otimes dY_{\theta})V\rho V^{\dagger}\right]=\left(e^{-i\theta}L\rho+e^{i\theta}\rho L^{\dagger}\right)dt=B_{\theta}\rho\,dt.

Thus insertion of the earlier homodyne increment prepares the
unnormalized system operator
B_{\theta}\rho\,dt
. Propagation for a
time
\tau>0
is then given by the unperturbed semigroup

e^{\mathcal{L}\tau}
, and the later homodyne mean is

\operatorname{Tr}[X_{\theta}(\cdot)]dt
. Therefore

\langle dY_{\theta}(t+\tau)dY_{\theta}(t)\rangle_{\rm ss}^{\rm sys}=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{\theta}e^{\mathcal{L}\tau}B_{\theta}\rho_{\rm ss}\right]dt^{2}.

Dividing by
dt^{2}
implies, for
\tau>0
,

\langle I_{\theta}(t+\tau)I_{\theta}(t)\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}^{\rm sys}=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{\theta}e^{\mathcal{L}\tau}\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\right],

(144)

where the superscript “sys” indicates the part beyond the instantaneous vacuum noise. The disconnected contribution is removed by replacing
\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}
by its projected version

\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}})=\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}-\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}].

(145)

Since

\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}]=\operatorname{Tr}[X_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}}],

(146)

this subtraction is precisely the subtraction of

\langle I_{\theta}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}^{2}
.

For
\tau>0
the connected system part is therefore

C_{\theta}^{\rm sys}(\tau)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{\theta}e^{\mathcal{L}\tau}\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}})\right].

(147)

The one-sided Fourier-Laplace transform gives the reduced resolvent:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}C_{\theta}^{\rm sys}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{\theta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{(\mathcal{L}+\mathrm{i}\omega)\tau}\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}})\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{\theta}(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}})\right].

(148)

For a real homodyne current, the negative-time part of the correlation function gives the complex conjugate of Eq. (
148
). Hence the full two-sided spectrum is

S_{\theta}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)=1+2\operatorname{Re}\,\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{\theta}(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}})\right].

(149)

This is the formula used in the homodyne examples.

For multiple real output currents, the same argument gives a matrix spectrum. If

I_{a}(t)=\xi_{a}(t)+X_{a}(t),

(150)

and if
\mathcal{B}_{a}
denotes the corresponding current-insertion superoperator, then

\displaystyle S_{ab}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=D_{ab}+\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{a}(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{B}_{b}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{b}(\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{B}_{a}\rho_{\mathrm{ss}})\right],

(151)

where
D_{ab}
is the white-noise covariance of the input vacuum noises
\xi_{a},\xi_{b}
. For a single ideal homodyne current,
D_{\theta\theta}=1
, and Eq. (
151
) reduces to Eq. (
149
).

Specializing Eq. (
149
) to the truncated cat-resonator notation gives

S_{\theta,N}^{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)=1+2\operatorname{Re}\,\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[X_{\theta,N}(-\mathrm{i}\omega-\mathcal{L}_{N})^{-1}\mathcal{Q}_{N}(\mathcal{B}_{\theta,N}\rho_{\mathrm{ss},N})\right],

(
71
)

with

X_{\theta,N}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}L_{{\rm ex},N}+e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}L_{{\rm ex},N}^{\dagger},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{\theta,N}\rho=e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}L_{{\rm ex},N}\rho+e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\rho L_{{\rm ex},N}^{\dagger}.

(152)

Thus the constant term in Eq. (
71
) is the vacuum shot noise, while the resolvent term is the finite-frequency transform of the connected system-emission correlation function.
