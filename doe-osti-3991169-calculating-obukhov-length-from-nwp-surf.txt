 Abstract
 This document derives a working equation for the Monin–Obukhov length (L) from standard
NWP surface output fields. The derivation explicitly represents both sensible and latent heat
flux contributions to the surface buoyancy flux, extending the conventional sensible-heat-only
formulation. While the worked example uses HRRR fields, the resulting equation applies to
any NWP surface output containing 2 m temperature, 2 m specific humidity, surface pressure,
friction velocity, sensible heat flux, and latent heat flux.

 1

Overview
 The Obukhov length is calculated from the balance between mechanical turbulence and buoy-
ancy (Obukhov, 1946; Foken, 2006). In this formulation, both sensible heat and moisture transport
contribute to the buoyancy flux. The document included in the Appendix was helpful in generating
this formulation.
 Throughout this document, the term NWP surface output refers generically to the standard
surface-layer diagnostic fields, 2 m temperature, 2 m specific humidity, surface pressure, friction
velocity (u∗ ), sensible heat flux (H), and latent heat flux (LE), distributed in GRIB2 format by
NOAA numerical weather prediction models such as HRRR, NAM, and GFS.1 While the worked
example in this document uses HRRR fields, the derivation and final working equation apply equally
to any NWP surface output containing these variables.
 The Obukhov length (L) is not a direct output of these NWP models. Instead, it must be
calculated from the available surface-layer diagnostic fields. Although L is used internally within the
surface-layer and planetary boundary-layer (PBL) parameterizations of these models to determine
stability-dependent similarity functions, it is not retained as an output variable in the standard
GRIB2 product suite. As a result, users requiring L for downstream applications, such as particle
dispersion modeling or turbulence parameterization, must reconstruct it from the available mean-
state and flux variables. This document provides the derivation and working equations necessary
to calculate L directly from standard NWP surface output. The resulting formulation explicitly
represents both the sensible and latent heat flux contributions to surface-layer buoyancy.

Why This Matters for Particle Dispersion
 Accurate estimation of L is particularly important for particle dispersion models and schemes
that use Monin–Obukhov similarity theory to parameterize atmospheric stability and turbulence. In
these applications, L characterizes the relative importance of buoyancy and mechanical turbulence
and is used to determine stability-dependent corrections and turbulence scales. These parameters
affect the representation of atmospheric mixing and, consequently, the predicted transport and
dispersion of particles.
 Over agricultural areas, latent heat flux can represent a substantial component of the surface
energy balance during the growing season. For example, Hiestand et al. (2023) documented sub-
stantial seasonal and spatial variability in latent heat flux over cropland in the U.S. Corn Belt,
demonstrating the importance of evapotranspiration in the partitioning of available surface energy.
Forest ecosystems can likewise partition a substantial fraction of available energy into latent heat
flux. Measurements above a ponderosa pine plantation in the Sierra Nevada demonstrated that
latent heat flux was an important component of the surface energy balance and varied substantially
with climate conditions (Goldstein et al., 2000). Measurements over a subalpine coniferous forest
similarly showed summer Bowen ratios between approximately 0.7 and 2.5, indicating that latent
heat flux can represent a substantial component of the forest surface energy balance (Turnipseed
et al., 2002).
 Including both sensible and latent heat fluxes provides a more complete representation of the
surface buoyancy flux and may improve the physical consistency of atmospheric stability estimates
used in particle-dispersion applications.

 2. Potential Temperature (q)

 a. Static Stability
Potential temperature is the temperature that a parcel of air at pressure P and temperature
T would have if it were adiabatically brought to a reference pressure Po. The potential
temperature helps determine the buoyancy of a dry displaced fluid parcel relative to its
surroundings. For a static fluid, when heavier fluid lies below lighter fluid, we say that it
is stably stratified (since tilting of a density surface will result in a restoring force). When
lighter fluid lies below heavier fluid the equilibrium is unstable and small tilting of the
density surface will grow and lead to convective motions. From the equation of state, for
an adiabatic system, we know that a change in pressure results in a change in
Temperature. This change must be accounted for when comparing displaced fluid
parcels.
