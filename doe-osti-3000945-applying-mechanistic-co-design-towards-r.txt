Abstract: The next generation of qubits, the building blocks of quantum technologies, are
designed using molecules. Molecular qubits have natural advantages over solid-state equivalents
in biological and chemical sensing. Chemistry allows the properties of molecular qubits to be
fine-tuned, such as the ability of multiple qubits to work together using qubit-qubit coupling.
Molecules and structures containing multiple qubits, made from both inorganic and organic
elements, are developed in this work to bring quantum technologies closer to room-temperature
operation. The goal is achieved by understanding how the core of the qubit, known as spin,
interacts with the vibrating parts of the molecule, other spins, and the surrounding environment.
A combined approach involving theory, spectroscopy, and synthesis is used to achieve the design
goal.

Executive Summary: We develop the mechanistic design rules needed to realize next-generation
optical, all-organic, and microwave qubit sensors with improved properties over solid-state
equivalents. We achieve our goal by understanding fundamental spin-phonon, spin-spin, and spin-
environment mechanisms. A combined theory/spectroscopy/synthesis co-design process is used.
The strategy allowed Phase I of the grant to produce record room-temperature T1/T2 coherent
microwave qubits, multi-metallic qubits with tunable qubit-qubit interactions, and the first
theoretical route to all-organic optical qubits. In the Phase II grant renewal, we use these novel
systems as molecular building blocks. We explore adding optical functionality by controlling spin-
phonon coupling in the ground and excited state. We explore how to tune the sensitivity of the
multi-metallic and nanographene-tethered microwave qubits to local environmental interactions
and macroscopic E/B fields. This includes novel capabilities in detecting small analytes through
charge or spin-specific transfer. The product of the proposal will be the first routes to creating room
temperature, optically addressable molecular qubits and optimizing molecular spin interactions for
new sensing scenarios.
The fundamental science needed to achieve these products are broken into two co-design focused
thrusts. The thrusts build on the Phase 1 molecular outcomes and technical advances in
spectroscopy and theory to accelerate progress. Thrust 1 focuses on creating design rules for new
S ≥ 1 inorganic and all-organic optical qubits. The primary goal is to determine how intersystem
crossings and excited state spin-phonon coupling change spin dephasing mechanisms compared to
microwave S = ½ qubits. The Phase I theoretical design guidelines for all-organic molecular qubits
are experimentally explored for comparison with the conventional inorganic qubits. Thrust 2
focuses on creating design rules for different sensing applications using multiple-center or
nanographene-tethered molecular qubits. The primary goal is to determine how spin-environment
interactions can be balanced with intrinsic spin-phonon mechanisms to optimize E/B field
detection versus local analyte-target detection. The goal includes studying how spin-spin
interactions can be tuned in multi-metallic versus nanographene-tethered systems for field
anisotropy measurements, increased sensitivity, or multiplexed analyte detection.
Molecular qubits have intrinsic advantages in biochemical and chemical sensing because of their
size, biocompatibility, and exquisite control of bonding, vibrations, and local environments.
Molecular qubits can easily tune the qubit-qubit coupling, environmental dephasing, and spin-
phonon-photon transduction required for most quantum applications. However, molecular qubits
still need significant research before these functionalities can be realized and compete with solid-
state qubits. The grant’s primary impact is a set of fundamentally derived and experimentally
realized design rules toward achieving this goal. The design rules will be based on a new
understanding of quantum mechanisms including spin-phonon, spin-spin, and spin-environment
interactions that are as relevant to solid-state systems as molecules. The grant outcome is,
therefore, beneficial for the broader quantum materials and photonics technologies in development
for quantum networks, quantum information, quantum sensing, and quantum computing.

Accomplishments:
Fundamentals of Molecular Qubit Systems
S = 1 transition metal complexes continue to serve as ideal model platforms for understanding how
electronic structure (e.g., ligand field excited states and zero-field splittings) and molecular
vibrations/phonons govern spin coherence and relaxation in optically addressable qubits. Yet,
direct experimental information on the phonons that couple to electronic spins remains limited –
and in fact also rare for molecular systems in general. Over the past year, we have advanced this
frontier by applying inelastic neutron scattering (INS) to characterize the low-energy phonon
spectra of S = 1 transition metal complexes, focusing initially on Ni(II) species. A preliminary
beamtime at the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL, a
key DOE Federal Research Facility) yielded the first low-energy INS spectra of several Ni(II)
qubit candidates with distinct counterions, revealing rich variations in the phonon features below
100 cm⁻¹. These findings establish a foundation for correlating specific lattice vibrations with
variable-temperature, variable-field T₁ (VTVH-T1) spin relaxation measurements, offering a new
path toward quantifying spin-phonon coupling. This work represents the first experimental access
to the phonon modes responsible for spin-lattice relaxation in S = 1 optically addressable qubits,
marking a milestone in the field.1

Complementary to these efforts, the team has focused on S = ½ Cu(II) and Co(II) qubit candidates
coordinated by the novel MesN₆ ligand framework.2 Combined spectroscopic and theoretical
analysis revealed how equatorial and axial ligand field strength govern spin relaxation dynamics
and their sensitivities to perturbations in the local coordination environment.4 In Cu(II), removing
Cu–X axial coordination bonds elevates d-d excited states (quantified by magnetic circular
dichroism (MCD) spectroscopy and time-dependent DFT (TDDFT) calculations) and suppresses
orbital angular momentum, prolonging T1. This result represents the strongest ligand field yet
measured in an MCD-to-T1 correlation. While in Co(II), stronger axial donors increase T₁ by
increasing ligand field excited-state energies. These results establish molecular-level design
principles for controlling spin-phonon interactions via ligand-field and ground-state wavefunction
tuning. Together, these collaborative advances provide critical mechanistic insight and quantitative
experimental benchmarks for the rational synthetic design of highly coherent molecular qubits.

We have also calculated using DFT the weak exchange interaction in various bimetallic transition
metal complexes3–6, including Cu(MesN6)Cl2 dimers with terphenyl or naphthalene linkers of
different length and twist, [Cu(MesN6)(OTf)2]2Pyrazine and two M(II)-V(IV) (M=Cu, Co)
heterometallic complexes. From the result for Cu(MesN6)Cl2 dimers, strong dependence of J on the
linker length and the relative orientation between the two MesN6 planes were revealed.
We have calculated the potential energy surfaces (PES), at the CASSCF+QD-NEVPT2 level, and
the SOC matrix elements between states on the PES for a carbene molecule as a candidate of
ODMR qubit. The results show consistency with those from the semiempirical model developed,
confirming its validity while providing more accurate details of the electronic structure. The same
kind of calculations is also being performed for another carbene molecule, the results of which
will help determine parameters of a PPP-like semiempirical model for general carbenes of C2
symmetry.

Next Generation Molecular Qubits
Bimolecular Qubits Molecular quantum bits and sensors provide an exciting pathway towards the
next generation of quantum materials. Previously, we synthesized and reported a new rigid ligand,
Mes
 N6, which acts as a host for a room temperature coherent Cu(II) molecular quantum bit. A
distinct advantage of MesN6 compared to phthalocyanines and porphyrins is the neutral charge of
the ligand. This has now allowed for the synthesis of a family of Cu(II) and Co(II) complexes with
anions of varying coordinating strengths. A new Cu(MesN6)(SiF)2 [SiF = PhSi(O2C6F12)2] complex
with improved T1 relative to Cu(MesN6)(OTf)2 is synthesized in addition to a Co(MesN6)(Cl)2
complex with the longest reported T1 for low spin Co(II). These compounds increase the structural
diversity of our original report and will likely find utility outside the molecular qubit field.
Using our previously reported MesN6 synthetic pathway, a modified ligand synthesis is used to
generate three complexes with metal-metal distances of about 12, 16, and 20 Å. Electron
paramagnetic resonance (EPR) experiments reveal a larger ground state spin for the shortest
distance compared to the other compounds alongside an increase in spin lattice relaxation rate.
Additionally, the use of a Cu(II) homodimer instead of V(IV) which is employed for studies with
porphyrin dimers, allows for an increase in isotropic exchange (J) at longer distances.

Through a modified synthetic strategy, we have also been able to make an asymmetric bimetallic
through a new ligand (MesN6Phen). The asymmetric coordination environments around the
individual Cu(II) metals allows them to maintain their individual spectral addressability despite
their exchange interaction. Further, the differences in coordination environment result in different
in the spin lattice-relaxation rate which mirror that of the parent monometallic, demonstrating that
despite the exchange the individual metals retain some of their original identity. However, during
a phase coherence measurement, we find that the entangled species is limited by the fastest relaxing
molecular qubit.
Nanographene Qubits Additionally, the discovery of an efficient post-modification method for
nanographenes has been made. We first explored the “click chemistry” approach to tether a
nanographene (NG) with the VOPc (vanadium complex). While we are able to successfully
prepare the azide-tethered NG and the alkyn-tethered VOPc, the Cu-catalyzed click reaction
proved to be extremely challenging, likely owing to the poor solubility of NG and the poor stability
of VOPc under the reaction conditions. We next focused on developing a post-modification
approach using thiodiazole as a 1,2-diamine precursor to connect a qubit to NG. While reduction
of simple benzothiadiazoles to 1,2-diaminobenzene has been established in small molecule
systems, a suitable reaction condition for polyaromatic substrates, such as NGs, remained
unexplored. Here we found that the use of excess LiAlH4 as the reductant with an extended period
of the time, the desired 1,2-diamino-NG was successfully obtained. This is an exciting result
because the 1,2-diamino moiety can serve as a sticky group to not only offer easy connection with
another molecule but also enable forming a fully conjugated linkage. To examine the feasibility of
this idea, we first examined the condensation between 1,2-diamino-NG and 9,10-
phenanthrenedione, which gave almost quantitative yield. In addition, the condensation between
,2-diamino-NG and 1,10-phenanthroline-5,6-dione, though less efficient, was also achieved, which
potentially can serve as a ligand to construct metal complexes. This is a direction that can be further
explored in the future. Moreover, we have spent a large amount of effort on the synthesis of a
diketone-containing VOPc complex. The condensation between 1,2-diamino-NG and the
diketone-Cu complex prepared by the Agapie group at Caltech proved to be successful.

Organic Qubits We innovated the theoretical and computational design of new classes of
luminescent organic molecules with high-spin ground state electronic configuration7–9. These
molecules can be exploited to carry out optically-detected magnetic resonance (ODMR) and serve

 7

as analogues of nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond, and therefore, as quantum sensors (for
magnetic fields, temperature, etc.), but designed from a bottom-up molecular approach.
We showed that by connecting two trityl radicals along the para configuration and ensuring steric
hindrance, one ensures no coupling between the radicals in the ground state (and therefore,
degenerate singlet-triplet energy levels), yet strong spin-orbit (SO) interactions in the excited states
mediated by torsion between the radicals. The key innovation in this work is to recognize that next-
nearest neighbor SO couplings can substantially enhance the ground-state spin polarization, giving
rise to much larger optically-detected magnetic resonance signals than what could be achieved
with previous designs. Similarly, we explored the possibility of triplet carbenes (which are
predicted to have record-high spin-orbit couplings due to the same carbon hosting two twisted and
half-filled p orbitals) of carrying out similar feats, demonstrating that vibronically mediated
interactions play a key role in their ODMR mechanism.
Finally, we designed a new photoredox catalysis scheme10 that, for the first time, exploits nontrivial
spin photophysics to give rise to unprecedented chemical interactions that can lead to
enantiopurification of racemic mixtures of organic substrates. Our scheme leverages previously
underappreciated coherent spin interactions that despite their low-energy nature can lead to
macroscopic reactivity effects.

New Spectroscopy for Molecular Qubits
A nano-FTIR (scattering-type scanning near-field optical microscopy, s-SNOM) methodology to
was implemented to quantitatively determine molecular orientation within single nano-crystalline
at nanometer-scale spatial resolution. This technique employs the s-SNOM finite-dipole model to
correlate the measured near-field amplitude and phase with the complex dielectric function of the
sample, establishing a direct link between local optical response and molecular orientation. The
measurements are important for understanding how molecular qubits orient when functionalized
on a surface to understand the new or potential dephasing routes.

Using metallic AFM tips illuminated by a tunable infrared source, we acquired spatially resolved
nano-FTIR spectra from crystalline regions of metal–organic compounds – a potential candidate

transport and structure–property relationships in low-dimensional quantum molecular materials.

Additionally, new deep UV entangled photon sources were created using LGBO, which are closer
aligned with the wavelengths needed for entangled photon spectroscopy of molecular qubit excited
states.11 The new nonlinear material was advanced using periodic poling. #LGBO has a
transparency window into the deep UV, and the periodic poling was successfully performed for
the first time to create entangled photon pairs at 532 nm. The wavelength of signal and idler can
be tuned using temperature to cover various transitions. The source was then being applied to
entangled pump probe and entangled time resolved fluorescence measurements of the optical
molecular qubits being developed.

Molecular Quantum Sensors
Quantum sensing of microenvironments using molecular quantum sensors provides potential for
unmatched sensitivity and spatial resolution through quantum entanglement of multiple spins. The
functionalization of the previously reported MesN6 ligand scaffold allows for the incorporation of
an ion trap. A crown ether provides a binding site with high affinity towards biologically relevant
cations like Li+ and K+. Preliminary data demonstrate an increase in T1 and Tm after the addition
of both Li+ and K+ salts to Cu(MesN6-crown)(OTf)2, with almost no change in T1/Tm for the parent
Cu(MesN6)(OTf)2. Previously, organic radicals in MOFs have been used to sense Li+ in solution,

sensing of nuclear spin inactive ions like K+ is therefore rare.
