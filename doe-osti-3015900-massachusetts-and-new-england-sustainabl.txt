 Foreword
 The Massachusetts Port Authority (Massport) funded the National Laboratory of the Rockies to
 assess New England biomass feedstock availability and the potential to produce sustainable
 aviation fuel (SAF) in the region, along with an evaluation of jet fuel logistics.

 Key Takeaways
 • A resource assessment was conducted for ten New England Airports to quantify biomass
 feedstocks that could be used to produce biofuels including SAF.
 • Municipal solid waste, woody biomass, and food waste were found to be the most widely
 available resources.
 • The volume and type of biomass resources available near each airport vary significantly
 depending on the collection radius and local conditions such as population and land use.
 • A techno-economic analysis was conducted on total New England state (and select New
 York Counties) feedstocks to estimate a theoretical maximum total biofuel potential of
 850 million gallons of gasoline equivalent per year.1,2 The purpose was to show that New
 England has sufficient feedstock to support SAF production facilities.
 • The analysis for bioenergy production potential for ten New England airports reveals
 strong opportunities for localized renewable fuel generation.
 • SAF produced outside of New England is expected to be pre-blended with Jet A in other
 markets such as New York Harbor and the Gulf Coast and delivered to New England via
 the same infrastructure that delivers Jet A today.
 • Enabling SAF produced in New England requires more consideration and planning due to
 infrastructure constraints. For example, the terminal supplying the highest volume of jet
 fuel can only receive fuel by barge or tanker, not by truck.
 • SAF produced in New England would be blended at a nearby terminal that can
 accommodate both blending and off-loading to a barge or tanker.
 • Many New England terminal owners expressed a willingness to work with future SAF
 producers to enable blending and delivery to airports within the region. Future regional
 SAF producers should work with the terminals identified in this report.

 Executive Summary
 In 2022, Massachusetts Port Authority (Massport) launched “Net Zero by 2031,” outlining plans
 to reduce Massport-controlled sources of emissions through operational improvements and
 sustainable infrastructure (Massport 2025b). They plan to achieve these goals through energy
 conservation and efficiency, enabling renewable energy resources, and alternative ground
 transportation. Massport established 2019 data as their baseline year, at which time Boston
 Logan International Airport (BOS) represented roughly 92% of the 877,947 metric tons of
 carbon dioxide equivalent (Massport 2025c).

 Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) is a commercially available drop-in fuel made from biomass and
 waste feedstocks and is chemically identical to and meets the same fuel quality specifications as
 Jet A. SAF can be used in the same infrastructure and aircraft as Jet A. SAF can reduce
 greenhouse gas emissions by up to 90% and is a key and is a key near-term technology for
 aviation to reduce emissions (Prussi et al. 2021). This study assesses the technical and economic
 potential for sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) production and use across New England.

 Massport launched a Massachusetts and New England Regional SAF Hub in June 2025 to unite
 industries and sectors for the purpose of developing strategies, partnerships, and policy
 frameworks to accelerate SAF adoption (Massport 2025a). The working group for this effort
 released a report Recommendations to the Governor on Accelerating the Adoption of Sustainable
 Aviation Fuels in Massachusetts and New England, which established a plan to incentivize the
 use of SAF as well as develop a regional SAF industry (Massport 2025a). As a part of this effort,
 Massport funded the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) to prepare a SAF study. This
 study evaluated the potential SAF production in New England and a logistics assessment for
 using SAF in the region regardless of where it is produced.

 A resource assessment was completed to determine SAF feedstock availability within a 50- and
 100-mile radius of 10 New England airports selected by Massport. These airports include BOS,
 Laurence G. Hanscom Field (BED), and Worcester Regional Airport (ORH), all owned by
 Massport, as well as Bradley International Airport (BDL), Martha’s Vineyard Airport (MVY),
 Portland International Jetport (PWM), Portsmouth International Airport at Pease (PSM), Rhode
 Island T.F. Green International Airport (PVD), Manchester-Boston Regional Airport (MHT), and
 Westfield-Barnes Regional Airport (BAF). The biomass resource assessment evaluated vegetable
 and waste oils, wet organic waste, municipal solid waste (MSW), and lignocellulosic biomass
 (woody biomass and crop residues). Results indicate that MSW and food waste are the most
 abundant resources within both 50- and 100-mile radii, reflecting the airports’ locations in
 densely populated urban areas. Some overlap in resource availability occurs due to the proximity
 of the airports. Vegetable oils and related byproducts are not locally available but could be
 sourced from other regions, while sources of manure (livestock operations) were not identified
 near the selected airports.
 A techno-economic analysis (TEA) was conducted to evaluate the feasibility of producing SAF
 from various biomass feedstocks. This analysis utilized data derived from a comprehensive
 resource assessment of potential feedstocks available within a specific region. BAF and ORH
 were selected as the primary sites for the TEA modeling, chosen for proximity to feedstock and
 as a comparison between a location in Western Massachusetts and another location closer to

 vi

 Boston. The airports’ infrastructure and accessibility were deemed conducive to the
 establishment of a SAF production facility. Exceptions were made for woody biomass, where
 PWM was selected as the TEA location due to its strategic proximity to Maine’s robust and well-
 established wood industry and its existing infrastructure for handling and transporting wood
 products. This site selection optimized access to feedstock and facilitated the assessment of
 relevant processing pathways.

 The analysis of bioenergy production potentials around ten New England airports reveal strong
 opportunities for localized renewable fuel generation. BDL and BAF airports show the highest
 total potentials—519 and 436 MMgge/yr3, respectively—while Massachusetts airports dominate
 overall. Across all locations, MSW (up to 2,311 MMgge/yr) and FOG (up to 375 MMgge/yr)
 represent nearly 80% of total resources, highlighting urban waste streams as key feedstocks.
 Spatially, most bioenergy potential is captured within 5–50 miles of each airport nonlinearly,
 indicating that local collection systems are both efficient and sustainable. Regional patterns show
 MSW and FOG dominance in urban areas and woody biomass strength in Maine. These findings
 suggest that developing airport-centered biorefineries—particularly near BDL, BAF, ORH, and
 BOS—could enhance sustainable aviation fuel production, leverage waste recovery
 infrastructure, and support New England’s transition toward a circular, low-carbon energy
 economy.

 NLR contacted all New England terminal owners to identify opportunities and challenges for the
 receipt of SAF, storage, and blending. SAF produced outside of New England is expected to be
 delivered pre-blended with Jet A due to existing production volumes, terminal space constraints,
 infrastructure availability, and limitations in the ability of some terminals to receive fuel by
 multiple modes. Deliveries of SAF/Jet A blends results in business as usual for New England,
 with the infrastructure investment to handle SAF occurring upstream of New England. It is
 expected that a pre-blended SAF/Jet A fuel would either be blended in New York Harbor or
 along the Gulf Coast and delivered to New York Harbor via the Colonial Pipeline. Patrick Leahy
 Burlington International Airport (BTV) received their first shipment of SAF/Jet A blend in April
 2025, and their fixed-base operator confirmed that the fuel was blended in Texas, delivered to a
 New York Harbor-area terminal, and delivered by truck to BTV (Business Air News 2025).

 SAF produced in New England requires more consideration. A SAF biorefinery is not expected
 along the coast due to the high value of land. Therefore, local SAF production is expected to be
 delivered to terminals by truck or barge. The Energy Transfer terminal in East Boston, which

 viii

 supplies the highest volume of Jet A in the region, cannot receive fuel by truck, only by barge,
 and space for additional tanks and associated infrastructure is limited. Therefore, other terminals
 were evaluated for their potential to handle locally produced SAF. A preferred terminal for
 blending SAF produced in New England would have the following attributes to reduce costs:

 • Located near a SAF production facility.
 • Currently stores jet fuel due to existing dedicated infrastructure to reduce capital costs.
 • Existing infrastructure to receive fuel by truck.
 • Existing infrastructure to offload fuel by both truck and marine.
 While the potential location of a regional SAF facility is unknown, Sprague Energy’s South
 Portland, Maine, and Quincy, Massachusetts, terminals both meet the other criteria. With
 additional equipment to offload to marine, Sunoco in South Portland, Maine would also be a
 suitable location.

 There are other terminals storing transportation fuels throughout New England that could present
 an opportunity to accommodate SAF and Jet A storage and blending. Terminals with space could
 consider adding truck offloading infrastructure to receive SAF produced nearby. The following
 terminal operators expressed a willingness to consider receipt, storage, and blending of locally
 produced SAF at their terminals: Global Partners, Inland Fuel Terminals, Shell, Sprague Energy,
 and Sunoco Logistics. Section 3 and Appendix C provide details on terminal tanks, capacity, and
 fuel receiving and offloading modes.

 Any terminal will require a capital investment for storing and blending SAF and Jet A. The
 investment amount will vary based on infrastructure availability and if different fuel receipt and
 off-loading modes are needed to accommodate the fuel. Terminals will need to assess space to
 accommodate the infrastructure necessary to accommodate SAF and blending activities.
 Terminals expressed that adding off-loading fuel infrastructure to marine is generally not
 difficult to accommodate the addition of dedicated lines, pumps, and associated equipment that
 requires little additional space.

 Introduction
 In 2022, Massachusetts Port Authority (Massport) launched “Net Zero by 2031,” outlining plans
 to reduce Massport-controlled sources of emissions through operational improvements and
 efficient infrastructure (Massport 2025b). They plan to achieve these goals through energy
 conservation and efficiency, enabling renewable energy resources, and alternative ground
 transportation. Massport established 2019 data as their baseline year, at which time Boston
 Logan International Airport (BOS) represented roughly 92% of the 877,947 metric tons of
 carbon dioxide (CO2) equivalent (Massport 2025c). SAF is a commercially available drop-in fuel
 made from biomass and waste feedstocks and is chemically identical to and meets the same fuel
 quality specifications as Jet A. Use of SAF at Massport-owned airports is one method to meet
 their goals. SAF can reduce greenhouse gas emissions by up to 90% dependent upon both the
 feedstock and technology pathway (Prussi et al. 2021).

 Massport launched a Massachusetts and New England Regional SAF Hub in June 2025 to unite
 industries and sectors for the purpose of developing strategies, partnerships, and policy
 frameworks to accelerate SAF adoption (Massport 2025a). The working group of this effort
 released a report Recommendations to the Governor on Accelerating the Adoption of Sustainable
 Aviation Fuels in Massachusetts and New England, which established a plan to incentivize the
 use of SAF as well as to develop a regional SAF industry (Massport 2025a). As a part of this
 effort, Massport funded the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) to prepare a SAF study.

 This study evaluated the potential SAF production in New England and a logistics assessment for
 using SAF in the region regardless of where it is produced. The study includes background
 information on national and regional jet fuel use, SAF production technologies and plants, a
 biomass resource assessment for 10 selected airports (Table 1), a techno-economic analysis
 (TEA) of a New England-based SAF biorefinery, and a review of regional jet fuel and SAF
 logistics. These airports were selected to represent diverse geographies, fuel volumes, and
 facility types.

 This report refers to both jet fuel and Jet A. Jet fuel is a broader term that includes both Jet A and
 other types of jet fuel used by the military, because there are terminals in New England solely
 providing jet fuel to the military. Jet A is used by commercial and general aviation aircraft and is
 blended with SAF for use in the same aircraft. At the time this study was prepared, the military is
 using Jet A for commercial-type aircraft in its fleet but does not use SAF blends currently.

 1.2 SAF Production and Plants
 SAF is produced from non-petroleum-based renewable feedstocks. Current commercial
 production uses fats, oils, and greases (FOG), as well as waste. Other anticipated feedstocks
 include the organic fraction of municipal solid waste (MSW), woody biomass, alcohol, and other
 biogenic resources. ASTM International is a voluntary consensus standards organization who
 creates, maintains, and updates jet fuel quality specifications and test methods. ASTM D7566,
 Standard Specification for Aviation Turbine Fuel Containing Synthesized Hydrocarbons, dictates
 fuel quality standards for non-petroleum-based jet fuel and outlines approved SAF-based fuel
 production pathways and the percentage allowable in a blend with Jet A.6 Currently, there are
 eight approved pathways in ASTM D7566 (Table 4). ASTM D1655, Standard Specification for
 Aviation Turbine Fuels, allows coprocessing of non-petroleum feedstocks at petroleum refineries
 at a rate of up to 5% and up to 10% for the biointermediates hydroprocessed esters and fatty
 acids (HEFA). SAF must be blended with conventional jet fuel (from 10% to 50% depending on
 the annex, shown in Table 4).

 Existing commercial SAF plants all use the HEFA pathway, which is very similar to the
 technology used at petroleum refineries. In addition to SAF, HEFA plants produce renewable
 diesel and other renewable fuels and chemicals. Alcohol-to-jet (ATJ) commercial facilities are
 expected to be the next commercialized pathway.

 SAF has been commercially available since 2016, with substantial new production capacity
 coming online in late 2024 and early 2025 (Table 5). There are six domestic commercial plants
 and one international producer supplying the U.S. market. Three of the plants are conversions of
 petroleum refineries—World Energy, Montana Renewables, and Phillips 66. Diamond Green
 Diesel was a conversion and expansion of an existing diesel plant while LanzaJet and New Rise
 Renewables are new plants. Neste, an international producer, has plants in Finland, the
 Netherlands, and Singapore. Neste began exporting to the United States in 2020 with deliveries
 to a Northern California terminal, and annual production capacity was 515 million gallons (or
   34,000 barrels/day) as of May 2025 (Neste 2020, 2025).

 Total domestic SAF consumption in 2025 was 281 million gallons (Figure 2, EPA 2025). The
 new plants contributed to a significant increase in in consumption from domestically produced
 SAF increasing from 39 million gallons in 2024 to 241 million gallons in 2025 (EPA 2025). The
 International Air Transit Association (IATA) estimates 2025 global production of 627 million
 gallons with domestic production representing about 38% of the total (IATA 2025a, EPA 2025).
 Imports from Neste supplied 65% of U.S. SAF in 2024 but dropped to 14% in the 2025 (Figure
 3, EPA 2025). Two recent changes are impacting SAF markets, including implementation of the

 5

 Inflation Reduction Act 45Z Clean Fuel Production Tax Credit and the European Union’s
 ReFuelEU SAF requirement of 2% in 2025 growing to 70% by 2050 (EASA 2025; IRS 2025).

 Future production of SAF will depend on multiple factors including competition for feedstock,
 the advancement of technology pathways, and policies. The International Air Transit Agency
 conducted a study to identify potential SAF production (IATA 2025b). The study assumed 2050
 global biomass of 12,000 million tonnes (Mt) and that 35% of this would be available for
 bioenergy and biofuels. After accounting for other bioenergy and biofuels use of biomass, the
 study assumed 1,580 Mt would be available to produce approximately 300 Mt (99 billion
 gallons) of biomass-based SAF (IATA 2025b). The study also evaluated power-to-liquids (PtL)
 potential and estimated 2050 availability of 1,000 Mt of biogenic carbon dioxide could be
 available to produce about 200 Mt (66 billion gallons) of electric-based SAF (IATA 2025).
 SkyNRG’s SAF Market Outlook report estimates about 18 Mt (6 billion gallons) of production
 capacity by 2035 based on analysis of plant announcements and their likelihood of success
 (SkyNRG 2025). The study suggests other methods to scale by 2035 through increasing co-
 processing at petroleum refineries could add 2 Mt (700 million gallons) and conversion of
 renewable diesel plants could add 4 Mt (1.3 billion gallons).

 1.3 SAF Market
 There are several financial mechanisms enabling the SAF market. Book-and-claim decouples the
 physical location of where SAF is used from its environmental value. The Massachusetts
 Institute of Technology’s Center for Transportation & Logistics in partnership with the Smart
 Freight Centre developed guidelines to enable book-and-claim (Massachusetts Institute of
 Technology 2021). The “book” refers to SAF producers’ generation of certificates (also referred
 to as credits) documenting a volume of SAF that was produced and consumed. The “claim”
 refers to the purchasers of certificates such as airlines or corporations who claim the emissions
 reductions.

 SAF qualifies as an advanced biofuel under the Renewable Fuel Standard, and obligated parties
 must meet volume requirements. There are values attached to the renewable identification
 number system used to track compliance. States have introduced various mechanisms to enable
 SAF markets. SAF qualifies for tradeable credit values in states with Low Carbon Fuel
 Standards, including California, New Mexico, Oregon, and Washington. The following states
 provide SAF-specific incentives:

 • Illinois: $1.50/gallon for SAF purchased or used in the state by airline common carriers
 effective through Dec. 31, 2032 (Illinois Department of Revenue 2023).
 • Minnesota: $1.50/gallon for SAF produced or blended in the state and used for flights
 departing Minnesota effective through July 1, 2030. There is also a sales exemption for
 SAF facility construction materials through July 1, 2034 (Minnesota Department of
           Revenue 2025).
 • Nebraska: $0.75/gallon (up to an additional $0.50/gallon for emissions reductions
 exceeding 50%) effective through Jan. 1, 2035 (Nebraska Farm Bureau 2024).
 • Washington: The following incentive is triggered if in-state SAF production reaches 20
 million gallons/year and is effective for 10 years (Port of Seattle 2023): $1.00/gallon (up
 to $2.00/gallon for emissions reductions exceeding 50%) for SAF produced or blended
 in-state for flights departing Washington.

 1.4 E-Fuel SAF Technology
 PtL is an emerging technology where electricity and/or low-carbon hydrogen is used to convert
 CO2 into intermediate products such as syngas or alcohols (Figure 4). These intermediate
 products can be converted to SAF using ASTM-approved pathways including FT to convert
 syngas to SAF and ATJ converting methanol or other intermediate alcohols to SAF. The
 motivation for SAF via PtL technology is the potential to significantly reduce emissions by using
 CO2 as a feedstock. For PtL to find success in the marketplace, a plant needs access to low-cost
 and low-carbon electricity, a reliable source of CO2, and a source of low-carbon hydrogen.
 Research is focused on electrolyzers—devices that split water into hydrogen and oxygen.
 Electrolyzer research is focused on reducing capital costs, improving efficiency, and increasing
 the useful life to greater than 70,000 hours. One example is Lydian, a Massachusetts-based
 company operating a PtL plant in North Carolina, demonstration the region’s potential leadership
 in next-generation SAF innovation (Lydian 2025).

 An analysis of the potential for regional SAF production was conducted. A feedstock evaluation
 quantified available resources at a defined radius for each of the 10 airports selected by
 Massport. A TEA was conducted to estimate the volumes of renewable fuel and SAF production

 are located near abundant feedstocks. There are multiple potential feedstocks and technology
 pathways. As production grows, certain feedstocks and pathways may provide economic
 advantages.

 The following resources are considered in this study:

 • Vegetable oils and byproducts include soybean oil and distillers corn oil (DCO), a

 chips, and sawdust), other forest processing waste, and orchard prunings. Crop residues

 2.2 Techno-Economic Analysis
 NLR develops and maintains TEA models that describe the process and production economics of
 conceptual biochemical conversion pathways to biofuels and bioproducts. Data were generated
 with Aspen Plus process simulation using a set of conversion parameters, material and energy
 balances, and flow rates. These data were used to size and cost process equipment and compute
 raw material and other operating costs. Based on a discounted cash flow rate of return, the
 analysis determined the minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) required to obtain a net present
 value of zero for a 10% internal rate of return.

 It should be emphasized that TEA outputs carry some uncertainty related to the assumptions
 made about capital and raw material costs. Without a detailed understanding of the basis behind
 it, the absolute computed cost values have limited relevance. Any new plant could have different
 capital and input costs that would impact the overall costs and economics that determine whether
 a plant is viable. Also, all available feedstocks would likely not go to a single plant; the TEA
 shows the potential production and MFSP. The TEA outputs are best used to compare different
 pathways and feedstocks against one another.

 The economic analysis includes a conceptual process design that enables development of a
 detailed process flow diagram (based on research or commercial data), rigorous material and
 energy balance calculations (via a commercial simulation tool, Aspen Plus), capital and project
 cost estimations (via an in-house model using spreadsheets), a discounted cash flow economic
 model, and the calculation of an MFSP (Humbird et al. 2011; Tao, Markham, et al. 2017). The
 operating expense calculation for the designed facility is based on material and energy balance
 calculations using Aspen Plus process simulations. All costs are adjusted to 2020 U.S. dollars
 using the Plant Cost Index from Chemical Engineering, the Industrial Inorganic Chemical Index
 from SRI Consulting, and the labor indices provided by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics
 (Chemical Engineering 2021; Bureau of Labor Statistics 2021).

 The overall process efficiency (or on-stream factor) is assumed to be 90% (7,884 operating hours
 per year), consistent with other TEA design reports (Humbird et al. 2011; Dutta et al. 2011;
 Davis et al. 2014).8 Salaries for personnel are inflated to $2023. Sixty percent of the total salaries
 is added for labor burden, and 2.0% of the total installed capital was designated for maintenance
 (which includes cleaning expenses) (Davis et al. 2014). Property insurance and taxes account for
 1.5% of the total capital investment (TCI) (Davis et al. 2014). The federal corporate tax rate used
 in our analysis is 35%, and U.S. income tax was averaged over the plant life, calculated on a per-
 gallon basis. The amount of income tax to be paid by a potential fuel producer varies annually
 due to changes in the volume of product produced and the allowable depreciation deduction.

 After the TCI, variable operating costs, and fixed operating costs are determined, a discounted
 cash flow rate of return analysis is typically used to determine MFSP. The discounted cash flow
 analysis was calculated by iterating the selling cost of the product until the net present value of
 the project is zero with a 10% internal rate of return. The analysis requires that the discount rate,
 depreciation method, income tax rates, plant life, and construction startup duration be specified.
 The discounted cash flow assumes 40% equity financing with a loan interest at 8% for 10 years.
 Working capital is assumed to be 5% of the fixed capital investment. The plant is assumed to

 2.2.1 Oils, Fats, Yellow Grease, Brown Grease, and Animal Fats
 Although feedstocks for HEFA processes include natural oils derived from plants, animal fats,
 postconsumer wastes (e.g., yellow grease), and aquatic microorganisms such as algae and
 cyanobacteria, the generic process concept is very similar. A representative process flow diagram
 is shown in Figure 14, including the processes of hydrogenation, propane cleave, hydrocracking
 and hydroisomerization, and product fractionation.

 Figure 14. Schematic HEFA process flow diagram.
 Source: Tao, Milbrandt, et al. (2017)

 Oils. Bio-oils are sent to the hydro processing facility, fundamentally with three reaction steps—
 hydrogenation, propane cleave, and decarboxylation (Tao, Milbrandt, et al. 2017). First, catalytic
 hydrogenation could be used to convert liquid-phase unsaturated free fatty acids or glycerides

 19

 into saturated with the addition of hydrogen (H2). The second step is to cleave the propane and
 produce three moles of free fatty acids (Pearlson 2011) per mole of triglycerides. The glycerol
 portion of the triglyceride molecule is converted into propane by adding H2. The third reaction is
 to remove the oxygen from the fatty acids (Pearlson 2011). There are three pathways occurring
 in this stage: decarboxylation, decarbonylation, and hydrodeoxygenation. The decarboxylation
 pathway removes oxygen in the form of CO2, decarbonylation removes oxygen in the form of
 carbon monoxide (CO), and hydrodeoxygenation removes oxygen in the form of H2O.
 Hydrogenation takes place to saturate the double bonds in the unsaturated triglycerides.

 The hydroisomerization and hydrocracking processes are followed by a fractionation process to
 separate the mixtures into paraffinic kerosene, paraffinic diesel, naphtha, and light gases. The
 hydrocracking reaction is exothermic and results in the production of lighter liquid and gas
 products. They are relatively slow reactions; thus, most of the hydrocracking takes place in the
 last section of the reactor. The hydrocracking reactions primarily involve cracking and saturation
 of paraffins. This pathway is based on the models developed previously (Tao, Milbrandt, et al.
 2017), with all values reported in $2023.

 Yellow Grease. Yellow grease could be processed via the HEFA pathway to produce HC.
 Feedstock cost is assumed to be $550/ton ($0.61/kg) (Milbrandt and Badgett 2024).

 Availability of yellow grease varies significantly between 50 and 100 miles near BAF, and both
 have small volumes to support a biorefinery facility converting yellow grease using the HEFA
 pathway concept. Assuming a yellow grease price of $0.61/kg, the MFSP of the hydrocarbon
 (HC) via the HEFA-SPK pathway is estimated at approximately $6.40/gge (Table 9) for 50 miles
 and $4.49/gge for feedstocks from a 100-mile collection radius. This means that HC could be
 more than twice as expensive as compared to conventional jet fuel with yellow grease. The
 conversion cost without considering feedstock cost would be about $3.9–$2.0/gge. Annual HC
 production is estimated to be 3.0–15.9 million gge (MMgge).

 Yellow grease alone is not a viable option as a HC feedstock due to its high feedstock cost and
 limited availability within 50–100 miles of BAF. Yellow grease and other FOGs have a
 relatively high selling price due to commoditization for bio-oils and other industries. Select FOG
 sources may have resources to offset costs, as well as benefiting from economies of scale,
 typically in areas with organic waste disposal bans, high population densities, and high landfill
 tipping fees. It is recommended to take advantage of these programs if they are available within
 50–100 miles of BAF. These programs, paired with a larger radius, would ensure economic
 viability for yellow grease as a HC feedstock.

 2.2.2 Wet Wastes to Renewable Natural Gas Production Estimates
 Anaerobic digestion (AD) includes four biological steps as shown in Figure 16 (Chen and
   Nebling 2014):

 1. Hydrolysis breaks down insoluble organic matter into simple monomers via hydrolytic
 microorganisms. Proteins are converted to amino acids, lipids into fatty acids, starch to
 glucose, and carbohydrates to monomeric sugars.
 2. Acidogenesis converts simple sugars and acids to volatile fatty acids and alcohols via
 acidogens. This is the fastest step in the AD process, which involves complex consortia
 of bacteria, including bacteriocides, Clostridia, Bifidobacteria, Streptococci, and
 Enterobacteriaceae (Weiland 2010).
 3. Acetogenesis further converts volatile fatty acids to acetate, CO2, and H2.
 4. Methanogenesis converts acetate and H2 to methane (CH4) and CO2 via methanogenic
 bacteria.

 23

 The consortium of microbial populations needs to be maintained to stabilize the AD process and
 increase biogas production efficiency.

 Figure 16. Steps in the AD process.
 Source: Bhatt and Tao (2020)

 The AD technology facilitates organic decomposition and reduces inorganic matter in the
 absence of oxygen (Global Methane Initiative 2016). These organic materials are converted to
 final products, which are mainly biogas, digestate (liquid form in most cases), and a combination
 of solid and liquid effluents derived from digestate treatments (Chen and Nebling 2014). Biogas
 can be used to produce electricity and heat, while the effluent liquid, rich in crop nutrients, is
 used as agricultural fertilizer, depending on the amount of nutrient loading, especially nitrogen
 (Chen and Nebling 2014). The fraction from solid/liquid separation of the digestate can be used
 as dairy bedding or converted to potting soil mixes. Generally, the feed to the digester is
 pretreated using different physical operations to reduce maintenance issues. For example, the
 solids from wastewater primarily include primary and secondary sludge, which are grinded,
 shredded, or screened for efficient operations. Also, accumulation of grits inside the tanks
 reduces the working volume, which affects biogas production and increases maintenance and
 cleaning (Water Environment Federation 2017). Therefore, degritting is applied to prevent
 accumulation, which helps to improve process efficiency as well. Similarly, plastics, stones, and
 metals are removed from food waste during pretreatment depending on the collection methods.

 For TEA, the minimum biogas selling price (MBSP) is reported with capital intensity,
 production of biomass, and MBSP without feedstock cost.

 2.2.3 Woody and Select MSW Biomass HC Production Estimates
 Woody type of biomass (woody biomass and select MSW) is dried to a moisture content of 10
 wt % and fed to an indirect gasifier, where biomass deconstructs to tars, chars, and raw syngas
 (CO2, CO, and H2). Char is combusted to provide heat for the gasifier. The clean syngas stream
 is then converted to fuels via FT synthesis and separated into gasoline-, jet-, and diesel-range
 products via distillation. Light end products from FT synthesis are recycled, and either
 combusted for process fuel or converted back to syngas in the tar reformer. A more detailed
 description of the process design is provided elsewhere (Dutta and Phillips 2009; Swanson et al.
 2010; Dutta et al. 2012, 2014; Tan et al. 2015), and products are often called FT-SPK.

 In the FT-SPK pathway (ASTM D7566 Annex A1), woody biomass is converted to syngas (from
 biologic or petrochemical sources) using gasification, then to jet fuel components using the FT
 synthesis reaction. Feedstocks for this pathway are from various sources of renewable biomass,
 with a focus mainly on woody biomass such as MSW, agricultural and forest wastes, wood, and
 energy crops. This pathway was approved by ASTM in June 2009 with a 50% blend limit.

 Figure 18 illustrates this process concept. The overall yield of the process is 71 gge/dry ton from
 woody biomass feedstock to hydrocarbon fuels. Feedstock composition for the MSW material
 considered here (paper/cardboard, yard trimmings, and clean wood) are similar to woody
 biomass feedstocks, but the TEA model accounts for about 10% yield loss when using MSW.
 The yield of the process is 64 gge/dry ton from select MSW feedstock to hydrocarbon fuels.

 2.2.4 Crop Residue HC Production Estimates
 This pathway is based on a corn stover report case ethanol-to-jet model (Uddin et al, 2025) with
 all values converted to $2023. This process uses a biochemical conversion pathway that converts
 herbaceous biomass into ethanol via anaerobic fermentation and then upgrades the ethanol to HC
 fuels. The corn stover ethanol design uses dilute acid pretreatment followed by enzymatic
 hydrolysis and co-fermentation with recombinant Zymomonas mobilis. The corn stover is first
 treated with a dilute sulfuric acid catalyst at a high temperature (190°C) for a short time (2–10
 minutes), liberating the hemicellulose sugars and other compounds. A purchased cellulase
 enzyme is added to the hydrolysate at an optimized temperature for enzyme activity. Several
 days (3–7 days) are required to convert most of the cellulose and xylose to ethanol. The “beer”
 liquor with approximately 4–8 wt % ethanol is then sent to recovery and purification, which uses
 standard adsorption technology. The solids after fermentation are separated and combusted in a
 fluidized bed combustor to produce high-pressure steam for electricity credits and process heat.
 The schematic process flow diagram is illustrated in Figure 20. The overall yield of the process
 is 79 gal ethanol/dry ton of corn stover.

 Figure 20. Process block diagram for the cellulosic-biomass-to-ethanol process.
 Sources: Humbird et al. (2011); Tao, Markham, et al. (2017)

 In this TEA, operating expenses are calculated based on material and energy balance calculations

 It is crucial to acknowledge that the TEA outputs inherently carry a degree of uncertainty. These
 uncertainties are primarily attributed to the assumptions made regarding capital costs, including
 construction, equipment, and permitting fees, as well as the variable costs associated with raw
 materials. Factors such as fluctuating market prices, advancements in technology, and location-
 specific conditions can significantly impact both capital expenditures and input costs.
 Consequently, any future plant’s actual capital and input costs may differ substantially from the
 assumptions within the TEA, potentially altering the overall costs and economic viability of the
 HC production facility.

 Therefore, the TEA outputs are best utilized as a comparative tool, enabling a direct comparison
 between different HC production pathways and evaluating the economic potential of different
 feedstock options. The technology pathways modeled within the TEA were carefully selected
 and tailored to the specific characteristics and processing requirements of each feedstock type,
 ensuring a comprehensive and insightful assessment of the various HC production routes. The
 study’s primary goal is to identify the most promising and cost-effective pathways for HC
 production within the targeted region, considering the available resources and economic factors.

 3 New England Jet A and SAF Logistics
 3.1 General SAF and Jet A Logistic Considerations
 The method of moving fuels throughout the country depends on the location of production, fuel
 type, and volume. The modes of fuel transport include barge, tanker (ship), pipeline, rail, and
 truck (Figure 21). Each mode operates under regulations and standards that protect quality and
 safety. Pipelines carry multiple fuels, barges can transport several fuel types with multiple tanks,
 and the same railcar type is used for biofuels and petroleum products, but they are generally
 leased and used for a single product over the period of that lease. Where pipelines are not
 available, dedicated trucks are used to move Jet A from terminals or refineries to airports where
 pipelines are not available.9

 There is also the consideration of how and where to blend SAF and Jet A. Previous studies
 identified terminals as the optimal location for blending due to existing tanks, associated
 equipment, blending software, trained staff, insurance, and permits covering these activities.
 Today, nearly all SAF is blended with Jet A at terminals (Moriarty, Milbrandt, and Tao 2021).10
 Section 3.2 evaluates New England considerations on receipt, storage, and blending of SAF and
 Jet A.

 SAF produced at a stand-alone plant meets ASTM D7566 and is delivered to terminals tanks for
 certification testing. SAF is then blended with Jet A into a third tank, tested, and certified as
 ASTM D1655. The blended fuel is then delivered to airports by the same trucks and pipelines
 used today (Figure 22). Another potential production and fuel receipt method would be if a
 petroleum refinery coprocessed SAF. In this instance, SAF would be coprocessed alongside Jet
 A, certified as ASTM D1655 at the refinery, and delivered through the supply chain as Jet A is
 today.

 fuels by more modes. A previous study funded by the Port Authority of New York and
 New Jersey identified several options for blending SAF and Jet A (Moriarty, Milbrandt,
 and Tao 2021). Appendix B provides details on New York Harbor terminals storing Jet
 A.
 If SAF production significantly increases, there would be potential to deliver SAF and Jet A
 separately by barge or tanker to New England terminals for blending and delivery to airports.
 Shell’s Providence terminal can receive fuel by unit-car train. If SAF were produced in the
 Midwest, it could be delivered by rail to Providence, where it could be offloaded to trucks or
 barges/tankers with additional equipment. There is potential to evaluate existing but unused rail
 at other terminals. While fuels deliveries by rail are limited in New England, Massport receives
 renewable diesel that is delivered by rail to Broco Energy—while they do not handle jet fuel at
 any of their facilities, it could be considered.

 SAF Produced in New England
 NLR contacted all terminal companies in the region to discuss their fuel receiving and offloading
 modes and their potential to accommodate SAF, Jet A, and associated blending infrastructure.

 Movements, storage, and blending of SAF produced in New England requires more
 consideration compared with receipt of a SAF/Jet A blend from New York Harbor. A SAF
 biorefinery is not expected along the coast due to the high value of land. Therefore, local SAF
 production is expected to be delivered to terminals by truck or barge. The Energy Transfer
 terminal, which supplies the highest volume of Jet A in the region, cannot receive fuel by truck,
 only by barge, and space for additional tanks and associated infrastructure is limited. Therefore,
 other terminals were evaluated for their potential to handle locally produced SAF. A preferred
 terminal for blending SAF produced in New England would have the following attributes to
 reduce costs:

 • Located near a SAF production facility.
 • Currently stores jet fuel due to existing dedicated infrastructure to reduce capital costs.
 • Existing infrastructure to receive fuel by truck.
 • Existing infrastructure to offload fuel by both truck and marine.
 It is generally preferred to blend SAF at terminals already storing jet fuel, as there is dedicated
 infrastructure, and the investment would generally be lower than adding jet fuel to a terminal not
 currently handling it. A SAF plant located near a terminal would benefit from lower trucking
 costs. Sprague Energy’s South Portland, Maine, and Quincy, Massachusetts, terminals both meet
 these criteria. Currently, the Quincy location stores jet fuel solely for military use. The primary
 consideration for offloading by ship is for delivery to Energy Transfer’s East Boston terminal for
 delivery to BOS—the airport with the highest Jet A consumption. Terminal owners stated that it
 would not be difficult to add offloading marine equipment at locations that receive fuel by ship.

 36

 There is the potential for future siting analysis to identify unused rail infrastructure for fuel
 delivery. Table 23 shows terminals existing fuel receiving and offloading modes.

 There are other terminals storing transportation fuels throughout New England that could present
 an opportunity to accommodate SAF and Jet A storage and blending. The following terminal
 operators expressed a willingness to consider receipt, storage, and blending of locally produced
 SAF at their terminals: Buckeye Partners, Global Partners, Inland Fuel Terminals, Shell, Sprague
 Energy, and Sunoco Logistics. Several terminal companies mentioned that they would be more
 likely to recommend their terminals that currently store kerosene due to the similar properties
 with Jet A. Tank and capacity data for New England terminals not currently storing jet fuel are
 provided in Appendix C.

 Nearly all terminals lease tanks and associated equipment to third parties for storage and
 blending activities. Any terminal storing and blending SAF would require a capital investment to
 accommodate these activities. It is common to incorporate these investment costs into the lease
 agreement, or there is the option for the entity leasing the tanks to pay the capital investment
 upfront.

 Most tanks at terminals are in use, and available tanks could be cleaned and repurposed for SAF.
 Not all tanks are suitable for jet fuel storage, as a fixed roof is required, and not all distillate
 tanks are equipped with this roof type. It is preferred to use existing tanks due to lower costs;
 however, there is potential to build new tanks with the awareness that the permitting process
 could take years. A SAF facility would work directly with fuel terminals or through a fuel
 marketer to identify available tank capacity at preferred terminals. The data and information
 provided in this report could serve as a guide to help regional SAF producers identify potential
 terminals.

 4 Conclusions and Potential Future Work
 This report evaluated SAF potential production and use in New England. The results are
 intended to help New England states prepare for future SAF availability and to highlight
 opportunities for regional production. This report evaluated the potential for local production due
 to the potential positive economic impacts of converting nearby waste and other feedstocks into
 SAF for use at New England airports.

 The resource assessment found that MSW and food waste are the most abundant resources
 within 50- and 100-mile radii, reflecting the airports’ locations in densely populated urban areas.
 Woody biomass was highest near PWM given Maine’s well established wood products industry.
 While the focus of this study was SAF, the resource assessment is useful for production of other
 biofuels, biochemicals, and bioenergy.

 A TEA was conducted and BAF and ORH were selected the selected airports due to proximity to
 feedstock and to represent locations in Western Massachusetts and near Boston. The highest
 biofuel production potential was using select MSW within a 100-mile radii resulting in estimated
 annual production of 336 million gallons (BAF) and 278 million gallons (ORH). The woody
 biomass TEA was conducted for PWM with potential production of 53 million gallons per year.

 It takes several years to build a new biofuels plant. Therefore, for the foreseeable future, SAF
 produced outside of New England is expected to be delivered pre-blended due to existing
 production volumes, terminal space constraints, infrastructure availability, and limitations in the
 ability of some terminals to receive fuel by multiple modes. Deliveries of SAF/Jet A blends
 results in business as usual for New England jet fuel infrastructure, and the investment to handle
 SAF would occur upstream of New England.

 In the longer term, enabling SAF produced in New England requires more consideration and
 planning due to infrastructure constraints. For example, the terminal with the highest jet fuel
 volume that serves BOS and other airports is limited in space and can only receive fuels by barge
 or tanker, not truck.

 It is recommended that future regional SAF producers should work with terminals identified in
 this report that can receive fuel by truck and offload by barge or tanker to ensure supply to BOS,
 the largest consumer of Jet A in New England. Many terminal owners expressed willingness to
 work with future SAF producers to enable blending and delivery to airports within the region.

 Future potential work could include a detailed siting analysis to identify ideal sites for production
 evaluating closed industrial sites, brownfield sites, and similar. Such an analysis could consider
 market competition to identify the most viable locations for SAF development. An analysis of
 the potential for PtL fuels and SAF within New England could also be conducted.
