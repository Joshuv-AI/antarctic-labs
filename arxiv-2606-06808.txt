Information Rate Decomposition for Noisy Nanopore Channels with Geometric Duplication
Brendon McBain, Emanuele Viterbo

Abstract
This paper studies information rates of noisy duplication channels with memory, motivated by nanopore DNA sequencing. In nanopore sequencing, the measured signal is affected by both inter-symbol interference (ISI), caused by multiple DNA bases residing in the pore, and random sample duplications, where variable translocation speed causes each base to generate a random number of samples. These two effects make direct theoretical analysis difficult. To address this, we derive a new decomposition of the information rate into two interpretable terms: one associated with the intrinsic memory of an auxiliary ISI channel, and another that captures the uncertainty in the segment boundaries caused by random duplications. This decomposition separates the dominant channel distortions and replaces the direct analysis of the full channel with two more readily interpretable components. We then study the second term through a soft alignment functional closely related to Soft-DTW, which enables strong AEP results and an alternative proof of the Markov-constrained coding theorem based on strong information stability. Finally, we develop a lower bound on the information rate that depends on the distribution of jump distances between adjacent nanopore levels. This bound gives a simple geometric explanation of channel synchronisability and provides a tractable framework for computing achievable rates of Oxford nanopore sequencers.



I
Introduction

DNA-based data storage has emerged as a promising technology for archival
information storage in synthetic DNA molecules, and has motivated a growing
body of work at the interface of information theory, coding, and molecular
communications
[
2
,
3
]
. A central component of any
DNA storage system is the sequencing device used during data retrieval. Among
current sequencing technologies, the nanopore sequencer developed by Oxford
Nanopore Technologies (ONT) is particularly attractive since it combines
real-time readout, ultra-long reads, and portable low-cost hardware

[
4
]
. However, from an information-theoretic perspective, the nanopore
sequencer presents a challenging read channel
[
5
]
. Firstly, the
measured current signal is influenced by a number of
bases
residing inside the pore, which can be thought of as the
nanopore state
, such that the input bases experience inter-symbol interference (ISI). Secondly, the
translocation speed
of the bases through the pore is variable, resulting in duplications of the nanopore state at the sample level. Therefore, the nanopore sequencer is
naturally modelled as a
noisy duplication channel with memory

[
6
,
7
]
, following earlier signal-level modelling approaches for nanopore sequencers
[
8
]
. After basecalling, the induced sequence-level errors may alternatively be approximated by an insertion-deletion-substitution (IDS) channel with memory
[
9
,
10
]
. Here, we study the underlying signal-level channel. Understanding achievable information rates over
this channel is therefore a key step toward characterising the limits of
nanopore-based DNA storage systems.

This paper extends the theoretical foundations of a class of noisy duplication
channels that includes the nanopore sequencer as an important special case,
namely the noisy nanopore (duplication) channel (NNC)

[
6
,
7
]
. In this model, an input state sequence evolves
according to a Markov source on a de Bruijn state-space, representing the
action of shifting bases into the nanopore, where the bases inside the nanopore
uniquely specify the channel state. Due to the random translocation speed, each
channel state is duplicated for a randomly distributed number of samples. The
duplicated channel states are mapped to reference current levels via a
pore
model
, and are then corrupted by additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN). The
channel output is therefore a noisy piecewise-constant signal that is stretched
by random sample duplications. Additive noise blurs the
jumps
between
successive levels, while the random duplications make the level-segment
boundaries unknown. Consequently, there are two principal challenges in
analysing achievable information rates for such channels: channel memory and
random sample duplications. Removing either distortion leads to either an ISI
channel or a duplication channel, both of which are already challenging in
their own right. The results developed in this paper therefore focus on
characterising how these two distortions interact in the information rate.

Previous work established achievable information rates and coding theorems for
noisy nanopore channels under ergodic Markov inputs, showing that the
Markov-constrained capacity is the maximum Shannon mutual information rate over
all ergodic Markov sources
[
11
]
. This theoretical framework was
later used to empirically investigate information rates for the nanopore
simulator Scrappie
[
7
]
, and was validated on ONT sequencing
data through estimation of achievable rates under mismatched decoding

[
12
]
. Capacity bounds for more general noisy nanopore
channels were subsequently developed in
[
13
]
; these bounds are
particularly useful in high-sampling-rate or low-noise regimes. Nevertheless,
the theoretical analysis of information rates remains difficult, particularly
when seeking accurate performance evaluations for modern nanopore systems.

The main contribution of this paper is a new decomposition of the information
rate for noisy duplication channels with geometric duplication. The
decomposition separates the effect of channel memory from the synchronisation
uncertainty caused by random sample duplications. Specifically, we show that
the information rate can be expressed as the difference between two
interpretable terms. The first term is the achievable information rate of an auxiliary ISI channel,
which captures the memory of the nanopore signal at the sample level, while
the effect of sample duplications is accounted for separately. The second term
is a
soft alignment penalty
that captures uncertainty in the unknown boundaries
between segments, where each segment is a consecutive run of duplicated
observations generated from the same underlying current level and channel
state. This reformulation
replaces the direct analysis of the full noisy duplication channel with two
more interpretable components: a hidden-Markov entropy rate term

[
14
]
and a Soft-DTW-type alignment functional

[
15
]
.

The specific contributions of this paper are as follows.

•

We derive a new information rate decomposition for noisy duplication channels with geometric duplication. The decomposition separates the channel memory induced by the nanopore state sequence from the synchronisation uncertainty induced by random sample duplications.

•

We introduce a soft alignment functional, closely related to the
Soft-DTW loss, whose normalised limit characterises the soft alignment
penalty in the decomposition. We prove a strong-AEP-type result for this
functional. Since the auxiliary ISI term is already known to satisfy the
strong AEP
[
11
]
, this establishes strong information
stability for noisy duplication channels with ergodic inputs and yields an
alternative proof of the Markov-constrained coding theorem.

•

We show that the decomposition leads to a more tractable Monte Carlo method for estimating information rates. The auxiliary ISI term can be
evaluated using the forward algorithm, while the soft alignment penalty can
be computed using a Soft-DTW-type dynamic program. This improves upon the
computational procedures used in
[
7
]
and

[
12
]
under the geometric duplication model.

•

We derive an upper bound on the soft alignment penalty in terms of
the jump distance distribution between adjacent nanopore levels. This gives a
lower bound on the information rate, which we call the jump-reliability
bound, and provides a geometric explanation of nanopore synchronisability.

•

We apply the resulting bound to estimate achievable rates for a range
of ONT nanopore sequencers released over the years, enabling comparisons
that were not feasible using previous techniques.

The jump-reliability bound is the central practical consequence of the decomposition. It formalises the empirical intuition that larger and more distinguishable jumps between adjacent nanopore levels improve synchronisation, and therefore improve achievable information rates
[
6
]
. In this way, the proposed framework connects information-theoretic performance directly to the geometry of the pore model and the translocation dynamics of the sequencer. The resulting lower bound is especially useful in sequencing regimes of practical interest, where direct information rate estimation is computationally demanding. This is especially important for modern pore models such as R10.4.1, whose large state space makes direct information rate estimation computationally prohibitive. The jump-reliability bound reduces this analysis to the geometry of the nanopore level mapping, through statistics of adjacent level jumps.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. In Section
II
, we introduce the soft alignment
functionals used throughout the paper and establish the shift-subadditivity property needed later for the strong-AEP analysis. In
Section
III
, we define the noisy geometric duplication channel and its sample-level Markov representation. In
Section
IV
, we introduce the relevant information and soft alignment rates, and prove the strong AEP for the soft alignment rate. In Section
V
, we derive the information rate decomposition, which expresses the achievable information rate as an auxiliary ISI channel term minus a segmentation penalty. In Section
VI
, we use this decomposition to develop computable lower bounds, including the jump-reliability bound. Finally, in Section
VII
, we evaluate achievable rates for noisy nanopore channels with parameters based on ONT sequencers.

II
Soft Alignment Functionals

Soft-DTW is a smooth relaxation of dynamic time warping (DTW), which compares two sequences while allowing for local misalignment.
DTW minimises cost over alignment paths that include local repetition, making it well suited to settings in which repeated observations of the same state of the system arise naturally. In our application, this is particularly appropriate because duplication in the channel produces outputs whose lengths and local alignments need not coincide with those of the input.

The key idea of Soft-DTW is to replace the minimum over alignment costs in DTW by a soft minimum over all admissible paths. Consequently, the resulting cost depends not only on the single minimum-cost alignment, but on all admissible alignments, with lower-cost paths weighted more heavily. The temperature parameter
\gamma
controls this interpolation, with the hard DTW limit recovered as
\gamma\to 0^{+}
. For the duplication-only model considered here, the temperature is set to the noise level and the admissible paths are in one-to-one correspondence with admissible segmentations of the observations.

Given sequences
x^{m}
and
y^{t_{m}}
, an
alignment path
is a sequence

\displaystyle\pi=\{(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2}),\ldots,(i_{L},j_{L})\}

(1)

satisfying
(i_{1},j_{1})=(1,1)
,
(i_{L},j_{L})=(m,t_{m})
, and

\displaystyle(i_{r+1},j_{r+1})-(i_{r},j_{r})\in\{(1,0),\cancel{(0,1)},(1,1)\}.

(2)

That is, it is a path from initial point
(1,1)
to termination point
(m,L)=(m,t_{m})
that hits
L
coordinates in a two-dimensional lattice. The set of all such paths is denoted by
\Pi_{m,t_{m}}
. Relative to the standard Soft-DTW formulation, we slightly restrict the admissible paths by excluding the vertical transition
(0,1)
. Thus, each input symbol must be aligned to at least one output sample, while repeated observations in the output samples are represented through horizontal moves, and therefore
L=t_{m}
.

The cost of a path is

\displaystyle C(\pi)=\sum_{r=1}^{L}c(x_{i_{r}},y_{j_{r}}),

(3)

where
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is the local cost.

Definition 1

(Alignment functionals
[
15
]
)
.

The Soft-DTW soft alignment functional is

\displaystyle\Psi_{\gamma}(x^{m},y^{t_{m}})=-\gamma\ln\left(\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{m,t_{m}}}e^{-C(\pi)/\gamma}\right).

(4)

for
\gamma>0
. At
\gamma=0
, we have the DTW hard alignment functional

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(x^{m},y^{t_{m}})=\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Psi_{\gamma}(x^{m},y^{t_{m}})=\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{m,t_{m}}}C(\pi).

(5)

For squared Euclidean cost, the Soft-DTW functional is differentiable, although in general it is neither convex nor concave. In contrast, the low-temperature limit DTW is non-differentiable. This smoother behaviour has motivated the use of Soft-DTW in the machine learning literature
[
15
]
. Fig.
1
illustrates the computation of Soft-DTW using a dynamic-programming matrix.

The following lemma quantifies the gap between the soft alignment functional
\Psi_{\gamma}
and its hard-alignment limit
\Psi_{0}
.

Lemma 1

(Soft-min inequality
[
15
]
)
.

For every
\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(x^{m},y^{t_{m}})-\gamma\ln|\Pi_{m,t_{m}}|\leq\Psi_{\gamma}(x^{m},y^{t_{m}})\leq\Psi_{0}(x^{m},y^{t_{m}}).

(6)

Hence
\Psi_{\gamma}(x^{m},y^{t_{m}})\to\Psi_{0}(x^{m},y^{t_{m}})
as
\gamma\to 0^{+}
.

A key property of alignment functionals is that they are subadditive, up to shifts in the indices of the input sequences. This leads to the following shift-subadditive lemma.

Lemma 2

(Shift-subadditivity)
.

For every
m_{1},m_{2},t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
t_{1}\geq m_{1}
and
t_{2}\geq m_{2}
, and every
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the alignment functional
\Psi_{\gamma}
is shift-subadditive in the sense that

\displaystyle\Psi_{\gamma}(x^{m_{1}+m_{2}},y^{t_{1}+t_{2}})\leq\Psi_{\gamma}(x^{m_{1}},y^{t_{1}})+\Psi_{\gamma}(x_{m_{1}+1}^{m_{1}+m_{2}},y_{t_{1}+1}^{\,t_{1}+t_{2}}).

(7)

Proof.

We first consider the case
\gamma>0
. Let

\Pi^{(m_{1},t_{1})}_{m_{1}+m_{2},t_{1}+t_{2}}=\{\pi\in\Pi_{m_{1}+m_{2},t_{1}+t_{2}}:(m_{1},t_{1})\in\pi\}

denote the subset of paths passing through
(m_{1},t_{1})
. Any path in
\Pi^{(m_{1},t_{1})}_{m_{1}+m_{2},t_{1}+t_{2}}
decomposes uniquely into a path in
\Pi_{m_{1},t_{1}}
followed, after shifting indices, by a path in
\Pi_{m_{2},t_{2}}
. Moreover, the path cost is additive under this decomposition. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{m_{1}+m_{2},t_{1}+t_{2}}}e^{-C(\pi)/\gamma}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\pi\in\Pi^{(m_{1},t_{1})}_{m_{1}+m_{2},t_{1}+t_{2}}}e^{-C(\pi)/\gamma}

(8)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\pi_{1}\in\Pi_{m_{1},t_{1}}}e^{-C(\pi_{1})/\gamma}\right)\left(\sum_{\pi_{2}\in\Pi_{m_{2},t_{2}}}e^{-C(\pi_{2})/\gamma}\right).

(9)

Applying
-\gamma\ln(\cdot)
to both sides, and using that
-\gamma\ln(\cdot)
is decreasing, yields the inequality.

For
\gamma=0
, the same inequality follows by taking the limit
\gamma\to 0^{+}
, or directly from the fact that restricting the minimum over
\Pi_{m_{1}+m_{2},t_{1}+t_{2}}
to the subset of paths passing through
(m_{1},t_{1})
can only increase its value.
∎

Although noisy duplication sequences are not ergodic in the usual sense, the shift-subadditive lemma is setting up the key structural property needed to apply Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem
[
16
]
when the input sequences are stochastic later in the paper.

III
Noisy Geometric Duplication Channels

Consider an arbitrary channel input
B^{m}
on alphabet
\mathcal{B}
, which uniquely specifies the channel/source state sequence
S^{m}
on the state-space
\Omega
with
|\Omega|=|\mathcal{B}|^{\tau}
for memory constraint
\tau
(i.e., the channel input/source depends on the current input and the previous
\tau-1
inputs). The set of admissible state sequences is denoted by
\mathcal{S}^{(m)}
. The initial state
S_{0}
has distribution
q_{0}(s_{0})
for all
s_{0}\in\Omega
.

The states in a sequence
S^{m}
are duplicated according to the sample durations
K^{m}=(K_{1},\ldots,K_{m})
to give the duplicated states, or sample states, as

\displaystyle Z^{T_{m}}=(\underbrace{S_{1},\ldots,S_{1}}_{K_{1}},\underbrace{S_{2},\ldots,S_{2}}_{K_{2}},\ldots,\underbrace{S_{m},\ldots,S_{m}}_{K_{m}}),

(10)

which retains the support
\Omega
. The durations
K_{\ell}
are i.i.d. geometric random variables
{\rm Geom}(1/\mu)
on
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
with mean
\mu
. The number of duplication samples is
T_{m}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}K_{\ell}
, and the number of duplications
T_{m}-m
is the negative binomial random variable
{\rm NegBin}(m,1/\mu)
with mean
m\mu
and variance
m\mu(\mu-1)
. The support of
K^{m}
is given by the set of admissible
segmentations
as
\mathcal{K}_{m,T_{m}}=\{k^{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}_{\geq 1}:\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}k_{\ell}=T_{m}\}
. For convenience, we define the cumulative output-index sequence

T^{m}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{m})
by

T_{\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}K_{i},\qquad\ell=1,\ldots,m.

Thus,
T_{\ell}
denotes the cumulative number of output samples produced up to and including
input position
\ell
, or equivalently the index of the final output sample
generated by the first
\ell
input symbols.

The duplicated states
Z^{T_{m}}
are mapped to real numbers according to the level mapping
f
as
f(z)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
z\in\Omega
, giving the level sequence
f(Z^{T_{m}})=(f(Z_{1}),f(Z_{2}),\ldots,f(Z_{T_{m}}))
. This level mapping captures the inter-symbol interference between bases through
the dependence of each level on the channel state.
Finally, the measured levels are corrupted by additive measurement noise,
giving the channel output

\displaystyle Y^{T_{m}}=f(Z^{T_{m}})+N^{T_{m}},

(11)

where the
N_{t}
are independent
\mathsf{Normal}(0,\sigma^{2})
random variables,
for
t=1,\ldots,T_{m}
.

Therefore, the noisy geometric duplication channel is concisely described as in the following definition.

Definition 2

(Noisy geometric duplication channel
[
12
]
)
.

The noisy duplication channel with AWGN and geometric duplications is
W^{(m)}:\mathcal{S}^{(m)}\rightarrow\cup_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq m}}\mathbb{R}^{t}
with channel transition probabilities

\displaystyle W^{(m)}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{s})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{m}\sum_{s_{0}\in\Omega}q_{0}(s_{0})\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}_{m,t_{m}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}||\mathbf{y}-f(\mathbf{s}^{\mathbf{k}})||^{2}}

(12)

with
\alpha_{m}=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-\frac{t_{m}}{2}}(1-\frac{1}{\mu})^{t_{m}-m}(\frac{1}{\mu})^{m}
, for all inputs
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}^{(m)}
and all channel outputs
\mathbf{y}\in\cup_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq m}}\mathbb{R}^{t}
.

In the following, we exclusively consider time-homogeneous Markov inputs
S^{m}
on
\Omega
with transition matrix
Q
, whose
(s^{\prime},s)
-entry is
P_{S\mid S^{-}}(s\mid s^{\prime})
. For such sources,
(S_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
is a Markov process,
(Z^{T_{m}})_{m\geq 1}
is a randomly indexed semi-Markov process, and
(Y^{T_{m}})_{m\geq 1}
is a randomly indexed hidden semi-Markov process
[
17
]
. Importantly, the indexing process
(T_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
is dependent on
Z^{T_{m}}
, since it is the counting process corresponding to the number of samples produced after
\ell
input symbols.

Remark 1

(Noisy nanopore channel)
.

Choosing the state space as the de Bruijn state space
\Omega=\{\mathsf{A},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{G}\}^{\tau}
, with transitions defined by shifting by one symbol and appending the next input symbol from the DNA alphabet, recovers the noisy nanopore channel in the geometric-duplication setting
[
7
]
. More generally, allowing the duration process
(K_{\ell})
and noise process
(N_{t})
to be arbitrary yields a general noisy nanopore channel model
[
13
]
.

In addition, we will need the sample-level duplicated state process
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 1}
, which is a semi-Markov process, and the sample-level output process
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 1}
, which is a hidden semi-Markov process. Under geometric duplications, however, the sample-level duplicated state process reduces to a Markov process with transition matrix
R
, whose
(z^{\prime},z)
-entry is
P_{Z\mid Z^{-}}(z\mid z^{\prime})
. Since

P_{Z\mid Z^{-}}(z\mid z^{\prime})=P(Z_{t+1}=z\mid Z_{t}=z^{\prime})=\left(1-\frac{1}{\mu}\right)\mathds{1}[z=z^{\prime}]+\frac{1}{\mu}P_{S\mid S^{-}}(z\mid z^{\prime}),

it follows that

\displaystyle R=\left(1-\frac{1}{\mu}\right)I+\frac{1}{\mu}Q,

(13)

where
I
denotes the
|\Omega|\times|\Omega|
identity matrix.

IV
Information and Soft Alignment Rates

IV-A

Information Rates

In this section, we recall the entropy and mutual information rates used to characterise noisy geometric duplication channels.
For a random process
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots)
, the classical entropy rate is
defined as

\displaystyle\overline{H}(Y)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}H(Y^{t}),

(14)

when the limit exists. This definition normalises the entropy of the first

t
output symbols by the number of output symbols. However, for channels
with synchronisation errors, the number of output symbols is generally not
equal to the number of input symbols. Therefore, an entropy rate definition
normalised per input symbol is required.

For the noisy duplication channel, the output produced by
m
channel inputs
is
Y^{T_{m}}
, where
T_{m}
is the random cumulative number of output samples produced up to and including the
m
-th input symbol. Hence, to obtain a quantity with an operational meaning per channel input, the output entropy should be
normalised by
m
, not by the random output length
T_{m}
. Equivalently,
this can be viewed as randomly indexing the output process

Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots)
according to the indexing sequence

T=(T_{1},T_{2},\ldots)
, as in
stopped random walks

[
18
]
. We therefore define the
T
-indexed
entropy rate of

Y
as

\displaystyle H_{T}(Y)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}H(Y^{T_{m}}),

(15)

when the limit exists.

It is important to note that
\overline{H}(Y)
and
H_{T}(Y)
are not
necessarily identical. The classical entropy rate
\overline{H}(Y)
fixes the
number of observed output symbols
t
, but does not reveal how many input
symbols, or equivalently how many nanopore states, produced those observations.
In contrast,
H_{T}(Y)
considers the output generated by exactly
m
input
symbols, while the corresponding output length
T_{m}
is random. Throughout
the paper, we use this
T
-indexed normalisation analogously for the entropy
rates of other processes that depend on
T
, such as
H_{T}(K|Y,S)
.

When
S^{m}
is a Markov process, all
T
-indexed entropy rates exist by subadditivity
[
11
]
, and they satisfy the usual basic properties of entropy rates. Furthermore, when
Y
is a hidden Markov or hidden semi-Markov process, as is the case for Markov sources,
\overline{H}(Y)
and
H_{T}(Y)
differ only by a scaling factor converting bits per sample to bits per symbol. This relationship was first established in
[
11
]
. Here, we give an alternative proof, adapted from
[
19
]
, which also yields a strong AEP for
H_{T}(Y)
using the strong AEP for
\overline{H}(Y)
. This is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(AEP for output entropy)
.

For an ergodic Markov source,
then

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{m}\log P(Y^{T_{m}})\to\mu\overline{H}(Y)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(16)

as
m\to\infty
, and

\displaystyle H_{T}(Y)=\mu\overline{H}(Y).

(17)

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{m}\log P(Y^{T_{m}},T_{m})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{m}\log P(T_{m})-\frac{1}{m}\log P(Y^{T_{m}}\mid T_{m})

(18)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{m}\log P(T_{m})+\frac{T_{m}}{m}\left(-\frac{1}{T_{m}}\log P(Y^{T_{m}}\mid T_{m})\right).

(19)

Since the AEP for the ergodic process
Y^{t}
gives

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{t}\log P(Y^{t})\to\overline{H}(Y),\qquad\text{a.s.},

(20)

due to the Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem, the same convergence holds along the random subsequence
t=T_{m}
, because
T_{m}\to\infty
almost surely. Hence

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{T_{m}}\log P(Y^{T_{m}}\mid T_{m})\to\overline{H}(Y)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(21)

Combining this with
\frac{T_{m}}{m}\rightarrow\mu
, due to the strong law of large numbers, we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{m}\log P(Y^{T_{m}}\mid T_{m})\to\mu\overline{H}(Y)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(22)

Moreover, by Lemma
A1
, the first term in (
19
) related to the length vanishes, i.e.,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{m}\log P(T_{m})\to 0\qquad\text{a.s.},

(23)

which proves the result.
∎

For the sample-level Markov process
Z
, the analogous identity
H_{T}(Z)=\mu\overline{H}(Z)
follows directly from Wald’s identity
[
18
, Theorem 5.3]
. The above theorem can therefore be viewed as an extension of this relation to the hidden Markov process
Y
. In contrast, no corresponding extension is available after conditioning on the Markov source
S^{m}
, as is required in the definition of mutual information rates. Part of the motivation for the results developed in this paper is to identify the closest analogous formulations in that setting.

The mutual information rate of the noisy geometric duplication channel is defined, whenever the limit exists, by

\displaystyle I_{T}(S;Y)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}I(S^{m};Y^{T_{m}}).

(24)

Thus,
I_{T}(S;Y)
represents the asymptotic mutual information per input symbol between the input process and the random-length output process. For ergodic Markov sources, this limit exists and yields an achievable information rate
[
11
]
. This leads to the following coding theorem under Markov input constraints.

Theorem 2

(Markov-constrained capacity
[
11
, Theorem 4]
)
.

The capacity of the noisy geometric duplication channel constrained to ergodic Markov sources exists and is given by

\displaystyle C_{\rm Markov}=\sup_{\text{ergodic }P_{S|S^{-}}}I_{T}(S;Y).

(25)

Thus,
C_{\rm Markov}
is the supremum of achievable information rates over ergodic Markov inputs. The proof in
[
11
]
establishes information stability using only Markov’s inequality and the Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem, and was later presented in a slightly refined form in
[
19
]
. In this paper, we provide an alternative proof that in part relies on the strong AEP for the output entropy rate in Theorem
1
, together with the strong AEP-type result for soft alignment rates and the information rate decomposition developed in the subsequent sections. This upgrades the weak AEP results in
[
11
]
, which were established with convergence in probability, to strong AEP results with almost sure convergence.

If arbitrary input sources are permitted, the unconstrained channel capacity is

\displaystyle C=\sup_{\{P_{S^{m}}\}}I_{T}(S;Y),

(26)

and therefore
C_{\rm Markov}\leq C
.

IV-B

Soft Alignment Rates

We now introduce soft alignment rates, which quantify the asymptotic
contribution, per input symbol, of alignment uncertainty between the input
sequence and the variable-length output. These rates are obtained by normalising the expected Soft-DTW alignment functional by the number of input symbols. They play a central role in the subsequent information rate analysis through their
relationship with
H_{T}(K|Y,S)
.

Moreover,
H_{T}(K|Y,S)
admits a physical interpretation: it asymptotically
quantifies the logarithmic size of the set of high-probability segmentations compatible with a
typical
state sequence and channel output. In the
following sections, we show that the soft alignment rate appears naturally in an information rate decomposition, where it captures the penalty associated with synchronisation uncertainty and separates this effect from the ordinary
output entropy rate. It is interesting to note that, if there were only a single admissible segmentation, e.g., under the restriction
K_{\ell}=K
for all
\ell
, the penalty would be zero.

Definition 3

(Soft alignment rate)
.

The soft alignment rate is defined as

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m\mu\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\Psi_{2\sigma^{2}}(X^{m},Y^{T_{m}})]~.

(27)

In the following lemma, we show that the soft alignment rate has a natural information-theoretic interpretation, since it is directly related to the conditional entropy rate
H_{T}(K|Y,S)
.

Lemma 3

(Soft alignment entropy relation)
.

The soft alignment rate
\overline{\psi}
relates to entropy rate
H_{T}(K|Y,S)
as

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}=1-\frac{\ln(4)H_{T}(K|Y,S)}{\mu}~.

(28)

Proof.

Denote by
\mathbf{S}^{\mathbf{k}}
the duplicated state sequence
S^{m}
according to segmentation
\mathbf{k}
. Observe that
\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y^{T_{m}}-f(Z^{T_{m}})\|^{2}\right]=\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[T_{m}]=\sigma^{2}m\mu
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}H(K^{m}|Y^{T_{m}},S^{m})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|Y^{T_{m}}-f(Z^{T_{m}})\|^{2}}}{\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}_{m,T_{m}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|Y^{T_{m}}-f(\mathbf{S}^{\mathbf{k}})\|^{2}}}\right]

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{\ln(4)}-\frac{1}{m\sigma^{2}\ln(4)}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Psi_{2\sigma^{2}}\big(X^{m},Y^{T_{m}}\big)\right].

(30)

∎

Corollary 1
.

The soft alignment rate is bounded as

\displaystyle 0\leq\overline{\psi}\leq 1~.

(31)

Proof.

The upper bound follows from non-negativity of the conditional entropy. Indeed, Lemma
3
gives

\displaystyle H_{T}(K\mid Y,S)=\frac{\mu}{\ln(4)}\left(1-\overline{\psi}\right)\geq 0,

(32)

and therefore
\overline{\psi}\leq 1
. The lower bound follows directly from non-negativity of the soft alignment functional.

Alternatively, the upper bound can be obtained directly from the alignment functional itself. For arbitrary inputs, the soft-min inequality in Lemma
1
gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{\Psi}_{2\sigma^{2}}(X^{m},Y^{T_{m}})\leq\mathrm{\Psi}_{0}(X^{m},Y^{T_{m}})~.

(33)

Moreover,
\mathrm{\Psi}_{0}(X^{m},Y^{T_{m}})
is the minimum mean-square alignment cost over all admissible segmentations. In particular, evaluating this cost on the true segmentation
K^{m}
gives mean-square error
\sigma^{2}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m\mu}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{\Psi}_{0}(X^{m},Y^{T_{m}})\right]\leq\sigma^{2}~.

(34)

By monotonicity of expectation, this proves the result.
∎

The next important result shows that soft alignment rates satisfy an analogue of the strong AEP for information rates: the normalised soft alignment functional converges almost surely to the soft alignment rate. The proof is essentially an application of Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem
[
16
]
, which extends the classical Birkhoff ergodic theorem from additive time averages to normalised subadditive functionals, combined with properties of soft alignment functionals established in Section
II
.

Theorem 3

(Strong AEP for the soft alignment rate)
.

For an ergodic Markov source, the normalised soft alignment functional satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m\mu\sigma^{2}}\Psi_{2\sigma^{2}}(X^{m},Y^{T_{m}})\to\overline{\psi}\qquad\text{a.s.}

(35)

as
m\to\infty
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\Lambda_{m}=\Psi_{2\sigma^{2}}(X^{m},Y^{T_{m}}),\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}.

(36)

By Lemma
2
, for every
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{m_{1}+m_{2}}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{2\sigma^{2}}(X^{m_{1}+m_{2}},Y^{T_{m_{1}+m_{2}}})

(37)

\displaystyle\leq\Psi_{2\sigma^{2}}(X^{m_{1}},Y^{T_{m_{1}}})+\Psi_{2\sigma^{2}}(X_{m_{1}+1}^{m_{1}+m_{2}},Y_{T_{m_{1}}+1}^{T_{m_{1}+m_{2}}}).

(38)

Since the source is ergodic Markov, the joint process of the input sequence
X^{m}=f(S^{m})
and the corresponding output
Y^{T_{m}}
is stationary and ergodic under shifts in the input index. Hence

\displaystyle\Psi_{2\sigma^{2}}(X_{m_{1}+1}^{m_{1}+m_{2}},Y_{T_{m_{1}}+1}^{T_{m_{1}+m_{2}}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Psi_{2\sigma^{2}}(X^{m_{2}},Y^{T_{m_{2}}}),

(39)

and
\{\Lambda_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}
is a subadditive process. Therefore, by Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem
[
16
]
,

\displaystyle\frac{\Lambda_{m}}{m}\to c\qquad\text{a.s.}

(40)

for some deterministic constant
c
, and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Lambda_{m}\right]\to c.

(41)

Thus, dividing by
\mu\sigma^{2}
yields the result.
∎

In light of the soft alignment entropy relation in Lemma
3
, Theorem
3
also yields a strong AEP for
H_{T}(K\mid Y,S)
. Combined with the decomposition developed in the following section, which expresses
I_{T}(S;Y)
in terms of
\overline{H}(Y)
and
\overline{\psi}
, this completes the alternative proof of Theorem
2
.

We briefly note that Theorem
3
is analogous to limit results for random path-sum models, such as directed polymers in random environments from the statistical physics literature on disordered systems
[
20
]
. This connection suggests that tools from that literature may be useful for analysing soft alignment rates beyond the geometric setting
considered here.

V
Information Rate Decomposition

In this section, we introduce the decomposition theorem for noisy geometric duplication channels. This theorem shows that
I_{T}(S;Y)
can be written in terms of an ISI channel, with states evolving according to the sample-level Markov process, and a penalty term proportional to the soft alignment rate.

Theorem 4
.

The information rate has decomposition

\displaystyle I_{T}(S;Y)=\mu(I_{\rm ISI}-R_{\rm seg}),

(42)

where

\displaystyle I_{\rm ISI}

\displaystyle=\overline{H}(Y)-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma^{2}),

(43)

\displaystyle R_{\rm seg}

\displaystyle=h_{b}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\right)-\frac{1}{\ln(4)}\left(1-\overline{\psi}\right).

(44)

This decomposition has a particularly appealing structure, since both terms can be evaluated using dynamic programming at the sample level (indexed by
t
), rather than at the segment level (indexed by
m
). In the ISI term
I_{\rm ISI}
, the quantity
\overline{H}(Y)
denotes the entropy rate of a hidden Markov process. While the duplication events influence this term through
\mu
, they do not directly enter into its computation. In the
segmentation penalty

R_{\rm seg}
, the conditional entropy rate
H_{T}(K|Y,S)
is represented in terms of the soft alignment rate
\overline{\psi}
, which is advantageous since Soft-DTW is computed at the sample level. Since the AEP extends to soft alignment rates, Monte Carlo estimation of
R_{\rm seg}
does not require explicit marginalisation over the state process. This avoids the significant computational burden that typically arises when marginalising both the state evolution and the duplication events.

We now present the direct proof of Theorem
4
. An alternative proof, which is less informative but is shorter, is provided in Appendix
B
. The key idea behind this proof is applying the chain rule to an auxiliary channel
(S^{m},K^{m})\mapsto Y^{T_{m}}
with respect to the inputs, allowing the separation of the state information from the segmentation information. Subsequently, we show how this decomposition leads to simple yet effective lower bounds.

V-A

Direct Proof

Since
Z^{T_{m}}
is a deterministic function of
(S^{m},K^{m})
, and the channel
output depends on
(S^{m},K^{m})
only through
Z^{T_{m}}
, the channel is represented
by the Markov chain
(S^{m},K^{m})\rightarrow Z^{T_{m}}\rightarrow Y^{T_{m}}
. Hence, by conditional independence,

I(S^{m},K^{m};Y^{T_{m}}\mid Z^{T_{m}})=0.

Moreover, since
Z^{T_{m}}
is a deterministic function of
(S^{m},K^{m})
,

I(S^{m},K^{m};Y^{T_{m}})=I(S^{m},K^{m},Z^{T_{m}};Y^{T_{m}}).

Therefore, by the chain rule with respect to
Z^{T_{m}}
,

\displaystyle I(S^{m},K^{m};Y^{T_{m}})

\displaystyle=I(Z^{T_{m}};Y^{T_{m}})+I(S^{m},K^{m};Y^{T_{m}}\mid Z^{T_{m}})

(45)

\displaystyle=I(Z^{T_{m}};Y^{T_{m}}).

(46)

Now, applying the chain rule with respect to
(S^{m},K^{m})
gives

\displaystyle I(S^{m},K^{m};Y^{T_{m}})

\displaystyle=I(S^{m};Y^{T_{m}})+I(K^{m};Y^{T_{m}}\mid S^{m}).

(47)

Rearranging and using the identity above, we obtain

\displaystyle I(S^{m};Y^{T_{m}})

\displaystyle=I(S^{m},K^{m};Y^{T_{m}})-I(K^{m};Y^{T_{m}}\mid S^{m})

(48)

\displaystyle=I(Z^{T_{m}};Y^{T_{m}})-I(K^{m};Y^{T_{m}}\mid S^{m}).

(49)

Observe that
I(Z^{T_{m}};Y^{T_{m}})=H(Y^{T_{m}})-H(Y^{T_{m}}|Z^{T_{m}})
. In Theorem
1
, it was shown that
H_{T}(Y)=\mu\overline{H}(Y)
.
In addition, we have

\displaystyle H_{T}(Y|Z)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{m}H(Y^{T_{m}}|Z^{T_{m}})

\displaystyle=\left(\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{T_{m}}{m}\right)H(Y|Z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma^{2}).

(50)

Hence, the first term in the decomposition gives

\displaystyle I_{T}(Z;Y)=\mu I_{\rm ISI},

(51)

and the second term in the decomposition gives

\displaystyle I_{T}(K;Y|S)

\displaystyle=H(K)-H_{T}(K|Y,S).

(52)

The first term in (
52
) is the entropy of a geometric random variable, given by

\displaystyle H(K)=\mu h_{b}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\right),

(53)

and the second term in (
52
) can be represented in terms of
\overline{\psi}
using Lemma
3
to give the final rate decomposition.

VI
Information Rate Bounds via Decomposition

VI-A

Elementary Bounds

The rate decomposition immediately yields simple bounds on
I_{T}(S;Y)
by using only coarse bounds on the residual segmentation term. In particular, from Corollary
1
, or equivalently from the entropy bounds

\displaystyle 0\leq I_{T}(K;Y|S)\leq H(K),

(54)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mu I_{\rm ISI}-H(K)\leq I_{T}(S;Y)\leq\mu I_{\rm ISI}.

(55)

These bounds are useful as a first benchmark, since they isolate the contribution of the associated ISI channel and require no detailed analysis of the segmentation term. In particular, removing the segmentation penalty altogether yields the simple upper bound
\mu I_{\rm ISI}
, whereas replacing the residual uncertainty by its maximum possible value
H(K)
yields the corresponding lower bound.

However, the lower bound in (
55
) can be quite loose. The reason is that even in the absence of additive noise, the channel output need not reveal every level transition. This occurs whenever two consecutive states can produce the same observed level, i.e., when
f(S_{\ell})=f(S_{\ell+1})
for some admissible transition, such as a self-loop in the state-space or a transition between distinct states with the same level mapping under
f
. In such cases, the noiseless output still contains an intrinsic ambiguity, and hence the source entropy cannot in general be achieved even when
\sigma=0
.

This observation suggests using the segmentation term evaluated in the noiseless setting to sharpen the elementary lower bound uniformly over all noise levels. This motivates the following result.

Theorem 5

(Uniform zero-noise bound)
.

Denote by
R_{\rm seg}(0)
the segmentation rate
R_{\rm seg}
term at
\sigma=0
. The information rate is bounded below as

\displaystyle I_{T}(S;Y)\geq\mu(I_{\rm ISI}-R_{\rm seg}(0)),

(56)

and

\displaystyle R_{\rm seg}(0)=H(Z_{2}|Z_{1})-\frac{1}{\mu}I(S;Z).

(57)

Proof.

For the lower bound on
I_{T}(S;Y)
in (
42
), replace the segmentation term in the rate decomposition by its noiseless value
R_{\rm seg}(0)
, which gives a valid lower bound uniformly over the noise level.

Regarding
R_{\rm seg}(0)
, observe that when
\sigma=0
we have
Y=f(Z)
, and therefore
I(S;Y)=I(S;Z)
. In addition, in the noiseless setting, the associated ISI channel information rate reduces to
I_{\rm ISI}=H(Z_{2}|Z_{1})
. Applying the rate decomposition in the noiseless case yields

\displaystyle I(S;Z)=\mu\bigl(H(Z_{2}|Z_{1})-R_{\rm seg}(0)\bigr),

(58)

and rearranging gives
R_{\rm seg}(0)
in (
57
).
∎

Theorem
5
is useful because it replaces the crude entropy penalty
H(K)
in (
55
) by the sharper quantity
R_{\rm seg}(0)
, which captures only the ambiguity that remains even in the absence of observation noise. Hence, it accounts for the structural non-identifiability induced by duplications of repeated identical output levels, rather than coarsely penalising by the duration entropy
H(K)
. In this sense, it gives a substantially more informative baseline lower bound than (
55
). Tighter bounds for the case of
f(S_{\ell})\neq f(S_{\ell+1})
will be developed in the next section.

Another advantage of this bound is that
R_{\rm seg}(0)
is computable. Indeed, the theorem shows that it suffices to compute
I(S;Z)
in the noiseless model. This can be done using techniques recently developed in
[
13
]
. By contrast, extending this refinement directly to the noisy case would require computation of entropy rates for hidden Markov processes, which is generally intractable and is a classical obstacle in the computation of information rates for channels with memory
[
14
,
21
]
. Thus, although the bound is based on the zero-noise model, it remains practically useful because it is both computable and uniform over all noise levels.

Remark 2
.

For the special case of a binary input alphabet with distinguishable levels
\pm 1
, the noiseless information rate
I(S;Z)
reduces to the information rate of the binary sticky channel with geometric duplications
[
22
]
, which we denote by
I_{\rm sticky}
. This quantity was directly used as a bound on
I_{T}(S;Y)
in
[
11
]
. For i.i.d. equiprobable binary inputs, Theorem
5
gives

\displaystyle R_{\rm seg}(0)=h_{b}\!\left(\frac{1}{2\mu}\right)-\frac{1}{\mu}I_{\rm sticky}.

(59)

VI-B

Jump-reliability Bound

The following bound is expressed in terms of the
jump distance
, a quantity introduced in
[
6
,
7
]
as a basic measure of synchronisability. For two adjacent states
s^{\prime},s\in\Omega
with
P_{S|S^{-}}(s|s^{\prime})>0
, define

\displaystyle J(s^{\prime},s)=\bigl|f(s)-f(s^{\prime})\bigr|.

(60)

Intuitively, larger jump distances make neighbouring segments easier to distinguish in noise, and hence reduce the uncertainty in the associated jump time. This intuition is formalised in the following theorem.

Theorem 6

(Jump-reliability lower bound)
.

Let

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{\rm seg}=I(K_{1};Y^{T_{2}}\mid S_{1},S_{2},T_{2}).

(61)

Then

\displaystyle I_{T}(S;Y)\geq\mu\bigl(I_{\rm ISI}-\overline{R}_{\rm seg}\bigr).

(62)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{\rm seg}=h_{b}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\right)-\frac{1}{\ln 2}\left(1-\sum_{s^{\prime},s\in\Omega}q_{0}(s^{\prime})P_{S|S^{-}}(s\mid s^{\prime})\overline{\psi}(s^{\prime},s)\right),

(63)

with

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}(s^{\prime},s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mu\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Psi_{2\sigma^{2}}\bigl(f(s^{\prime},s),Y^{T_{2}}\bigr)\,\middle|\,S_{1}=s^{\prime},\,S_{2}=s\right].

(64)

Proof.

Starting from the chain rule,

\displaystyle I(K^{m};Y^{T_{m}}\mid S^{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}I(K_{\ell};Y^{T_{m}}\mid S^{m},K^{\ell-1})

(65)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}I(K_{\ell};Y_{T_{\ell-1}}^{T_{m}}\mid S^{m},T_{\ell-1}).

(66)

For each
\ell
, we reveal the neighbouring jump time

T_{\ell+1}
, thereby restricting the observations
Y^{T_{m}}_{T_{\ell-1}}
to the two segments
Y^{T_{\ell+1}}_{T_{\ell-1}}
associated with states
S_{\ell}
and
S_{\ell+1}
. Since additional side information can only increase the conditional mutual information, we have

\displaystyle I(K^{m};Y^{T_{m}}\mid S^{m})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}I(K_{\ell};Y_{T_{\ell-1}}^{T_{\ell+1}}\mid S^{m},T_{\ell-1},T_{\ell+1})+I(K_{m};Y^{T_{m}}\mid S^{m},T_{m-1},T_{m})

(67)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}I(K_{\ell};Y_{T_{\ell-1}}^{T_{\ell+1}}\mid S^{m},T_{\ell-1},T_{\ell+1})

(68)

\displaystyle=(m-1)\,I(K_{1};Y^{T_{2}}\mid S_{1},S_{2},T_{2})

(69)

\displaystyle=(m-1)\overline{R}_{\rm seg},

(70)

where we used stationarity under shifts in the segment index and
I(K_{m};Y^{T_{m}}\mid S^{m},T_{m-1},T_{m})=0
. Observe that

\displaystyle I_{T}(K;Y|S)\leq\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m-1}{m}\overline{R}_{\rm seg}=\overline{R}_{\rm seg}.

(71)

Substituting this upper bound on the segmentation term into the rate decomposition yields (
62
).

For the expression of
\overline{R}_{\rm seg}
, we have

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{\rm seg}

\displaystyle=I(K_{1};Y^{T_{2}}\mid S_{1},S_{2},T_{2})

(72)

\displaystyle=H(K)-H(K_{1}\mid Y^{T_{2}},S_{1},S_{2},T_{2}).

(73)

Conditioning further on
(S_{1},S_{2})=(s^{\prime},s)
gives

\displaystyle H(K_{1}\mid Y^{T_{2}},S_{1}=s^{\prime},S_{2}=s,T_{2})=\frac{\mu}{\ln 2}\bigl(1-\overline{\psi}(s^{\prime},s)\bigr),

(74)

and averaging over
(S_{1},S_{2})
completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2

(Dependence on jump distance)
.

The quantity
\overline{\psi}(s^{\prime},s)
depends on the state pair
(s^{\prime},s)
only through the jump distance
J(s^{\prime},s)
. That is, there exists a scalar function, again denoted by
\overline{\psi}
, such that

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}(s^{\prime},s)=\overline{\psi}\bigl(J(s^{\prime},s)\bigr).

(75)

Consequently, the bound in Theorem
6
depends on the channel only through the jump matrix

\displaystyle J=[J(s^{\prime},s)]_{s^{\prime},s\in\Omega}.

(76)

Proof.

Let
a=f(s^{\prime})
and
b=f(s)
. Conditioned on
(S_{1},S_{2})=(s^{\prime},s)
, the observation
Y^{T_{2}}
is generated by a two-segment signal with levels
(a,b)
and AWGN, so
\overline{\psi}(s^{\prime},s)
depends on
(s^{\prime},s)
only through
(a,b)
. Since the squared Euclidean local alignment cost is invariant under the common translation
(a,b)\mapsto(a+c,b+c)
and
Y^{T_{2}}\mapsto Y^{T_{2}}+c
, this dependence is only through
b-a
. Moreover, the global alignment cost (the sum of local alignment costs over all alignment paths) is invariant under the common permutation
(a,b)\mapsto(b,a)
and
(Y^{T_{1}},Y_{T_{1}+1}^{T_{2}})\mapsto(Y_{T_{1}+1}^{T_{2}},Y^{T_{1}})
, since any alignment path under this permutation is still valid and with identical cost, so the dependence is even in
b-a
.
∎

Theorem
6
admits a natural genie-aided interpretation. The quantity
\overline{R}_{\rm seg}
is a first-order upper bound on the segmentation-rate term obtained by restricting the soft alignment decoder to a two-segment observation model and revealing the neighbouring jump times as side information. More precisely, the soft alignment decoder estimates each duplication count
K_{\ell}
from the two-segment observation block spanning segments
\ell
and
\ell+1
, conditioned on the adjacent jump times that delimit this block. Since conditioning on additional side information cannot increase the residual uncertainty,
\overline{R}_{\rm seg}
upper bounds the true segmentation penalty.

Although
\overline{R}_{\rm seg}
does not generally admit a closed-form expression, it is substantially more tractable than the full segmentation-rate term. In particular, its evaluation reduces to averaging a two-segment quantity over state pairs
(s^{\prime},s)\in\Omega^{2}
, rather than analysing the full global alignment problem over an unbounded sequence of segments. Consequently, the bound can be computed accurately by spatial averaging with relatively modest complexity.

The main structural consequence of the theorem is given by Corollary
2
, which identifies the jump distance
J(s^{\prime},s)
as the sole state-dependent parameter governing the local reliability term. Specifically, the corollary shows that
\overline{\psi}(s^{\prime},s)=\overline{\psi}\bigl(J(s^{\prime},s)\bigr)
, so the first-order segmentation penalty depends on the state pair
(s^{\prime},s)
only through the corresponding entry of the jump matrix. Table
I
illustrates this dependence for selected jump distances and noise levels. This establishes a direct relation between the achievable information rate and the geometry induced by the level mapping
f
.

The first-order bound naturally extends to a sequence of higher-order bounds whose tightness can, in principle, be increased arbitrarily by enlarging the observation block. One may enlarge the observation block and withhold a larger set of neighbouring jump times from the genie, thereby capturing a greater portion of the global segmentation uncertainty. Such extensions may yield tighter bounds, but at the cost of substantially increased computational complexity and reduced structural transparency. By contrast, the first-order bound remains computationally feasible and depends on a single explicitly identifiable geometric parameter, namely the jump distance.

Numerical Results

VII-A

Binary-Input AWGN Channel with Geometric Duplications

Firstly, we demonstrate the zero-noise bound for a noisy duplication channel where runs of identical levels is possible. In particular, we consider a binary-input noisy geometric channel with state-space
\Omega=\{0,1\}
, binary signalling with
f(0)=-1
and
f(1)=1
, and duplication probability
0.1

VIII
Conclusion

This paper developed an information rate decomposition for noisy geometric duplication channels, motivated by nanopore DNA sequencing. The decomposition separates the achievable rate into an auxiliary ISI channel term and a soft alignment penalty, thereby isolating the effects of channel memory and synchronisation uncertainty. We showed that the soft alignment term satisfies the strong AEP and is directly related to the uncertainty in the unknown segmentation.

The main practical consequence is the jump-reliability lower bound, which links achievable rate to the distribution of level jumps between adjacent nanopore states. This bound provides a tractable geometric proxy for information rate analysis and enables theoretical comparisons of ONT pore models that would be difficult using direct information rate computation alone. In particular, it allows modern R10.4.1 pore models, with state spaces of size
4^{9}
, to be compared alongside earlier R9 models. The numerical results show that the bound is tight in SNR regimes relevant to nanopore sequencing and reveal a geometric mechanism for synchronisability: larger and more frequent level jumps reduce the alignment penalty and increase achievable rate.

Overall, the proposed framework turns the basic intuition that larger nanopore level jumps improve synchronisation into an information-theoretic principle, linking the geometry of the nanopore level mapping directly to achievable rates for DNA data storage readout.

Random-Length Entropy Lemma

Lemma A1
.

Suppose
K_{1},K_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d. geometric random variables with mean
\mu<\infty
, and let

\displaystyle T_{m}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}K_{\ell}.

(79)

Then

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}H(T_{m})=0.

(80)

Proof.

The random variable
T_{m}
is negative binomial with mean
\mu m
and variance
m\,\mu(\mu-1)
. For any integer-valued random variable
X
with variance
\mathrm{Var}(X)
, we have
[
24
]

\displaystyle H(X)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\!\bigl(2\pi e(\mathrm{Var}(X)+1/12)\bigr).

(81)

Applying this to

gives

\displaystyle H(T_{m})\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\!\bigl(2\pi e(m\,\mu(\mu-1)+1/12)\bigr)=O(\log m).

(82)

Dividing by
m
and letting
m\to\infty
yields the result.
