Dyadic Martingale Transforms and Weighted Walsh-Carleson Operators
Ushangi Goginava and Farrukh Mukhamedov

Abstract
. We study weighted Walsh–Carleson maximal operators arising from dyadic martingale transforms associated with Walsh–Fourier partial sums. For weights satisfying a uniform dyadic variation condition and a uniform bound at the top dyadic scale, we prove weak type ( 1 , 1 ) (1,1) estimates for the corresponding maximal operators along subsequences. We also give divergence criteria in terms of the behavior of the weights near the top dyadic scale and, under suitable admissibility assumptions, relate these criteria to explicit ratio conditions. As applications, we obtain results on matrix transforms of Walsh–Fourier partial sums, including de la Vallée Poussin means, Cesàro means with varying parameters, Nörlund logarithmic means, and general Nörlund means. In particular, we prove a Walsh–Paley analogue of the Leindler–Tandori theorem and establish everywhere divergence results for several summability methods.

Introduction

It is well known that almost everywhere convergence of sequences of
operators is intimately related to the weak-type behavior of the
corresponding maximal operators (see, for example, Stein
[
45

). In
the context of Fourier analysis, establishing weak-type inequalities for
such maximal operators is typically a delicate matter, largely due to their
singular nature. A prototypical example is provided by the Carleson–Hunt
theorem, which asserts the almost everywhere convergence of trigonometric
Fourier series for functions in
L_{p}([-\pi,\pi])
with
p>1
, and whose
proof fundamentally relies on a weak-type estimate for the Carleson maximal
operator

(1)

A^{\ast}(f)(x):=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x-t)\,\frac{e^{int}}{t}\,dt\right|.

In the framework of Walsh systems, questions of almost everywhere
convergence and divergence are closely connected with martingale
transformations.
The partial sums of the Walsh–Fourier series of an integrable
f
can be written in the form

S_{n}(f)=w_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}(n)\,\mathcal{E}_{k}(fw_{n}),

where
E_{k}
denotes conditional expectation with respect to the
k
-th
dyadic
\sigma
-algebra and

\mathcal{E}_{k}f:=E_{k+1}f-E_{k}f

is the corresponding dyadic martingale difference operator. In this formula,

\varepsilon_{k}(n)
denotes the
k
-th Walsh digit of
n
, and
w_{n}
is
the
n
-th Walsh function. Thus
S_{n}(f)
can be represented as a martingale
transform.

Let
\{\Omega_{k}(n):k,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a positive family such that,
for each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
\{\Omega_{k}(n)\}_{k\geq 0}

is nondecreasing in
k
, that is,

0<\Omega_{k-1}(n)\leq\Omega_{k}(n),\qquad k,n\in\mathbb{N}.

We introduce the operator

M_{n}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})f:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}(n)\,\Omega_{k}(n)\,\mathcal{E}_{k}(fw_{n}),

which represents a martingale transform. Then we have

(2)

S_{n}(f)=w_{n}\,M_{n}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})f,

where
\Omega_{k}(n)\equiv 1
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Equality (
2
) emphasizes the close relationship between martingale
transforms and the partial sums of Walsh–Fourier series. Detailed accounts
of martingale transforms and their connections with Walsh dyadic analysis can
be found in
[
49
,
48
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
23
,
10
]
.

Based on this definition, we consider the corresponding weighted
Walsh–Carleson maximal operator

(3)

W_{C}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})f:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}(n)\,\Omega_{k}(n)\,\mathcal{E}_{k}(fw_{n})\right|.

We also introduce the weighted Walsh–Carleson maximal operator along a
subsequence
\{n_{a}:a\in\mathbb{N}\}
, defined by

W_{C}(\boldsymbol{\Omega},\{n_{a}\})f:=\sup_{a\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}(n_{a})\,\Omega_{k}(n_{a})\,\mathcal{E}_{k}(fw_{n_{a}})\right|.

Next, we define the kernel

P_{n}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}(n)\,\Omega_{k}(n)\,r_{k}D_{2^{k}},

where
r_{k}
is the
k
-th Rademacher function and
D_{2^{k}}
is the
Walsh–Dirichlet kernel of order
2^{k}
. It is straightforward to verify
that
M_{n}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
admits the convolution representation

(4)

M_{n}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})f=(fw_{n})\ast P_{n}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})=w_{n}\bigl(f\ast(w_{n}P_{n}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}))\bigr),

where
\ast
denotes convolution on the dyadic group.

We observe that, for the classical Walsh–Carleson maximal operator, one takes

(5)

\Omega_{k}(n)=1,\qquad 0\leq k\leq|n|,

with the convention that the terms with
k>|n|
do not contribute because

\varepsilon_{k}(n)=0
. With this choice, the operator in (
3
) reduces to
the classical Walsh–Carleson maximal operator.

Thus, the operator (
3
) can be regarded as a weighted generalization
of the Walsh–Carleson operator. As will be shown below, it is also closely
connected with classical summability problems for Walsh–Fourier series.

Definition 1
.

Let
\bigl(\Omega_{k}(n)\bigr)_{k,n\geq 1}
be a family of
weights, and let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\bigl(\gamma(n)\bigr)_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of positive integers. We say that
\gamma
is a
sequence of divergence with respect to

\bigl(\Omega_{k}(n)\bigr)_{k,n\geq 1}
if the following conditions hold:

(1)

\gamma:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
;

(2)

the sequence tends to infinity uniformly with respect to the dyadic
order, that is,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\inf\{\gamma(n):n\in\mathbb{P},\ |n|\geq N\}=\infty;

(3)

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{P}}\frac{\gamma(n)}{|n|}<1;

(4)

there exists a constant
c>0
such that

(6)

\Omega_{|n|-\gamma(n)}(n)\,\geq\,c\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{P}.

The purpose of the present paper is to obtain sharp conditions on the weight
system
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
under which the maximal operator
W_{C}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
is of weak type
(1,1)
. Our main result is the
following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\bigl(\Omega_{k}(n)\bigr)_{k,n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a system of weights, and let
\left(n_{a}\right)_{a\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{P}
be a sequence of indices.

(a) Boundedness.
Assume that the weights along
the sequence
\left(n_{a}\right)_{a\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy the uniform
summability condition

(7)

\sup_{a\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{|n_{a}|}\bigl|\varepsilon_{k-1}(n_{a})-\varepsilon_{k}(n_{a})\bigr|\,\Omega_{k}(n_{a})<\infty.

Assume also the top-scale bound along the same sequence,

(8)

\sup_{a\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{|n_{a}|}(n_{a})<\infty.

Then the maximal operator
W_{C}(\boldsymbol{\Omega},\{n_{a}\})
is of weak
type
(1,1)
; that is, equivalently,

(9)

\|W_{C}(\boldsymbol{\Omega},\{n_{a}\})\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{I})\rightarrow L_{1,\infty}(\mathbb{I})}<\infty.

(b) Divergence.
If there exists a sequence of
divergence with respect to
\bigl(\Omega_{k}(n)\bigr)_{n=1}^{\infty}
,
then the full maximal operator
W_{C}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
is not of weak
type
(1,1)
; that is,

\|W_{C}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{I})\rightarrow L_{1,\infty}(\mathbb{I})}=\infty.

Remark 1
.

In part (b) of Theorem
1
the problem can be formulated in a more
general setting. Let
\left\{n_{a}:a\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
be a
subsequence of integers, and suppose that condition (
7
) fails along
the sequence
\left\{n_{a}:a\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
. The question is
whether this failure necessarily implies that

\|W_{C}(\mathbf{\Omega,}\left\{n_{a}\right\})f\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{I})\rightarrow L_{1,\infty}(\mathbb{I})}=\infty.

In general, this problem has a negative answer. Indeed, in his solution of
the Balashov problem, Konyagin
[
27
]
proved that, in the case of partial sums (see
(
5
)), there exists a sequence
\left\{n_{a}:a\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

for which condition (
7
) does not hold, while condition (
9
) is nevertheless satisfied.

In the general setting, however, this problem remains open.

Moreover, even in the special case of partial sums (
5
), the problem
of finding necessary and sufficient conditions on a subsequence
\left\{n_{a}:a\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
that guarantee condition (
9
) is
still unresolved. This problem was posed by Konyagin
[
26
]
in 2006,
at the
International Congress of Mathematicians
, and a partial
solution was obtained in a paper by the first author jointly with Oniani

[
20
]
.

Since the divergence part of Theorem
1
is formulated in terms of
the existence of a sequence of divergence with respect to
\bigl(\Omega_{k}(n)\bigr)_{n=1}^{\infty}
, this criterion is not, at first sight, easy to
verify in concrete applications. This naturally leads to the following two
questions:

(1)

Can one provide simple, verifiable criteria for the existence of a
sequence of divergence?

(2)

What is the precise relationship between the existence of a sequence
of divergence and the stronger condition

(10)

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{|n|}\Omega_{k}(n)=\infty\,?

First we observe that, in general, condition (
10
) does
not guarantee the existence of a sequence of divergence. Indeed, consider
the family of weights

\Omega_{k}(n):=\frac{1}{|n|-k+1},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\ 0\leq k\leq|n|.

For each fixed
n
we have

\sum_{k=0}^{|n|}\Omega_{k}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{|n|}\frac{1}{|n|-k+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{|n|+1}\frac{1}{j},

so that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{|n|}\Omega_{k}(n)=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{j}=\infty,

and hence condition (
10
) is satisfied.

On the other hand, for any integer
\gamma
with
0\leq\gamma\leq|n|
we
have

\Omega_{|n|-\gamma}(n)=\frac{1}{|n|-(|n|-\gamma)+1}=\frac{1}{\gamma+1}.

In particular, for any choice of integers
\gamma(n)\rightarrow\infty

with
0\leq\gamma(n)<|n|
, we obtain

\Omega_{|n|-\gamma(n)}(n)=\frac{1}{\gamma(n)+1}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Consequently, there is no sequence
\gamma:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}

with
\gamma(n)\rightarrow\infty
for which there exists a constant
c>0

satisfying

\Omega_{|n|-\gamma(n)}(n)\geq c\quad\text{for all }n.

As we shall see below, the underlying obstruction in this example is the
behaviour of the double ratio limit

(11)

\lim_{k,n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Omega_{k}(n)}{\Omega_{k-1}(n)},

which, for the weights considered above, does not exist. Indeed, we have

\frac{\Omega_{k}(n)}{\Omega_{k-1}(n)}=1+\frac{1}{|n|-k+1}.

If we take
k=|n|
, then
|n|-k=0
, and hence

\frac{\Omega_{|n|}(n)}{\Omega_{|n|-1}(n)}=2.

In contrast, along any sequence
(k_{j},n_{j})
in the triangular index
region
\{(k,n):1\leq k<|n|\}
for which
|n_{j}|-k_{j}\rightarrow\infty
,
we have

\frac{\Omega_{k_{j}}(n_{j})}{\Omega_{k_{j}-1}(n_{j})}=1+\frac{1}{|n_{j}|-k_{j}+1}\longrightarrow 1.

Thus the limit in (
11
) fails to exist.

To this end, we first introduce the notion of the limit of a double sequence
over a cone. First, we define the following cone: there exists a constant
\kappa\in(0,1)
such that

\Delta_{\kappa}:=\bigl\{(k,n)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{P}:\kappa|n|<k\leq|n|\bigr\}.

We now introduce a sharper cone defined as follows. Let
\omega=(\omega_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence of positive integers satisfying the
following conditions:

(M)

\omega_{n}\nearrow\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{|n|}{\omega_{n}}\nearrow\infty.

Define

\Delta_{\omega}:=\bigl\{(k,n)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{P}:|n|-\omega_{n}<k\leq|n|\bigr\}.

We notice that
\Delta_{\omega}\subset\Delta_{\kappa}
. For the sake of shortness, we write
\Delta
to denote either
\Delta_{\kappa}
or
\Delta_{\omega}
. Given a family
\left(a_{k}(n)\right)_{(k,n)\in\Delta}
and
L\in\mathbb{C}
, we say that

\lim_{(k,n)_{\Delta}\rightarrow\infty}a_{k}(n)=L

if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

|a_{k}(n)-L|<\varepsilon\quad\text{whenever }(k,n)\in\Delta\text{ and }|n|\geq N_{0}.

A sequence
\{\Omega_{k}(n)\}
is called
\Delta
-admissible
, if it satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

there exists a finite limit
L
such
that

(12)

\lim_{(k,n)_{\Delta}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Omega_{k}(n)}{\Omega_{k-1}(n)}=L.

(b)

The sequence
\{\Omega_{|n|}(n)\}
is uniformly bounded above and
below, that is, there exist constants
0<c\leq C<\infty
such that

(13)

c\leq\Omega_{|n|}(n)\leq C\qquad(n\in\mathbb{P}).

Equivalently,
\Omega_{|n|}(n)\asymp 1
.

Now we answer Question 1 formulated above.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\{\Omega_{k}(n)\}
be a
\Delta
-admissible sequence. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The triangular limit satisfies

(14)

\lim_{(k,n)_{\Delta}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Omega_{k}(n)}{\Omega_{k-1}(n)}=1;

(ii)

There exists a sequence of divergence with respect to
\bigl(\Omega_{k}(n)\bigr)_{k,n\geq 1}
.

Next we address Question 2 formulated above and clarify the relationship
between
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is a
sequence of divergence with
respect to

\bigl(\Omega_{k}(n)\bigr)_{k,n\geq 1}
and (
10
).

Theorem 3
.

Let
\kappa\in(0,1)
be fixed, and assume that

\{\Omega_{k}(n)\}
is
\Delta_{\kappa}
-admissible. Then the following two
conditions are equivalent:

(i)

(15)

\lim_{(k,n)_{\Delta_{\kappa}}\to\infty}\frac{\Omega_{k}(n)}{\Omega_{k-1}(n)}=1;

(ii)

One has

(16)

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{|n|}\Omega_{k}(n)=\infty.

Moreover, if
\{\Omega_{k}(n)\}
is
\Delta_{\omega}
-admissible and

(17)

\lim_{(k,n)_{\Delta_{\omega}}\to\infty}\frac{\Omega_{k}(n)}{\Omega_{k-1}(n)}=1,

then (

) follows. This sufficient condition is not necessary in

Remark 2
.

The boundedness of maximal operators associated with partial sums is one of
the central topics in Fourier analysis. Theorems
1
,
2
and
3
show that, in the Walsh setting, the weak-type
(1,1)
boundedness
of the weighted Walsh–Carleson maximal operator reduces to the simple and
explicit condition
(
14
)
on the weight
\mathbf{\Omega}
.

We next discuss everywhere divergence and almost everywhere convergence for sequences of operators associated with the matrix

\mathbb{T}:=(t_{k,n})_{k,n\in\mathbb{P}}
which enjoys
the following assumptions:

(1)

t_{k,n}\geq 0
for all
k,n\in\mathbb{P}
;

(2)

t_{k,n}\leq t_{k+1,n}
for all
k,n\in\mathbb{P}
(rowwise
monotonicity);

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}t_{k,n}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{P}
.

By means of
\mathbb{T}
, the
n
th triangular matrix transform of the Walsh–Fourier series of
f
is
defined by

(18)

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}(f;x):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}t_{k,n}\,S_{k}(f;x),\qquad n\in\mathbb{P}.

Theorem 4
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

Uniform boundedness implies pointwise
convergence.

Let
\{n_{a}:a\in\mathbb{N}\}
be any increasing sequence of natural
numbers. If

(19)

\sup_{a\in\mathbb{N}}\bigl\|\mathcal{T}_{n_{a}}^{\mathbb{T}}\bigr\|_{H_{1}(\mathbb{I})\to L_{1}(\mathbb{I})}<\infty,

then for every
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})
one has

\mathcal{T}_{n_{a}}^{\mathbb{T}}(f;x)\longrightarrow f(x)\quad\text{as }a\to\infty,

for almost every
x\in\mathbb{I}
.

(2)

Everywhere divergence.

If

(20)

\lim_{(k,n)_{\Delta_{\omega}}\to\infty}\frac{\widetilde{T}_{2^{k},n}}{\widetilde{T}_{2^{k-1},n}}=1,

then there exists a function
f_{0}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}(f_{0};x)\right|=\infty

at every point
x\in\mathbb{I}
.

To establish this result, we use the kernel decomposition recalled in
Appendix A and apply Theorem
1
to the weights

\Omega_{s}(n)=\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n},\qquad 0\leq s\leq|n|.

Remark 3
.

If, in addition, the weights satisfy

\lim_{(k,n)_{\Delta_{\kappa}}\to\infty}\frac{\widetilde{T}_{2^{k},n}}{\widetilde{T}_{2^{k-1},n}}=1

for some
\kappa\in(0,1)
, then, by Theorem
2
, condition (
20
)
in Theorem
4
may be equivalently replaced by

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigl\|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}\bigr\|_{H_{1}(\mathbb{I})\to L_{1}(\mathbb{I})}=\infty.

(1)
of Theorem

Theorem
4
can be applied to recover many classical results,
as well as to obtain new ones, for the Walsh–Paley system.

1.1.
Divergence of partial sums of Walsh-Fourier series

Let

t_{k,n}:=\mathds{1}_{\{n\}}(k),n\in\mathbb{N}.

Then the matrix
\mathbb{S}:=\{t_{k,n}:k,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
generates the
sequence of partial sum operators
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{S}}
, that is,

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{S}}(f;x)=S_{n}(f;x).

In this case it is easy to see that

\lim_{(k,n)_{\Delta_{\kappa}}\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\widetilde{T}_{2^{k},n}}{\widetilde{T}_{2^{k-1},n}}\right)=1.

Therefore, by Theorem
1
,

(21)

\Bigl\|\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{S}}|\Bigr\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{I})\rightarrow L_{1,\infty}(\mathbb{I})}=\infty.

On the other hand, invoking Theorem
4
in this particular
situation, we recover the classical result of Stein
[
45
]
, which
asserts that there exists an integrable function
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})

whose Walsh–Fourier series has divergent partial sums at every point
x\in\mathbb{I}
.

1.2.
Almost everywhere convergence of the de la Vallée Poussin means
of Walsh–Fourier series

Let
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a nondecreasing sequence of integers satisfying
1\leq\lambda_{n}\leq n
. We define the de la Vallée Poussin means by

(22)

V_{n}^{(\lambda)}(f;x):=\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}+1}\sum_{k=n-\lambda_{n}}^{n}S_{k}(f;x),

where
S_{k}(f;x)
denotes the
k
-th partial sum of the Fourier series of
f
. Leindler
[
31
]
proved that if

(23)

n=O(\lambda_{n})\qquad(n\to\infty),

then
V_{n}^{(\lambda)}(f;x)\to f(x)
as
n\to\infty
almost everywhere for
every integrable function
f
. He also raised the question whether condition (
23
) is essential. Tandori
[
46
]
solved Leindler’s problem
and showed that condition (
23
) is indeed necessary.

The analogous problem for the Walsh–Paley system has remained open. Theorem
4
provides the Walsh–Paley counterpart of the
Leindler–Tandori theorem and yields a complete solution of this problem.

Theorem 5
.

Leindler–Tandori theorem for the Walsh–Paley system

(a)

If condition (
23
) holds, then for every
integrable function
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})
the de la Vallée Poussin
means (
22
) with respect to the Walsh–Paley system converge almost
everywhere to
f
, i.e.

V_{n}^{(\lambda)}(f;x)\longrightarrow f(x)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

for almost every
x\in\mathbb{I}
.

(b)

If

(24)

\frac{n}{\lambda_{n}}\longrightarrow\infty\qquad(n\to\infty),

then there exists an integrable function
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})
for which
the sequence
\{V_{n}^{(\lambda)}(f;x)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
diverges everywhere
on
\mathbb{I}
.

We also note that significant results on divergence phenomena for sequences
obtained by matrix transformations of partial sums of the trigonometric
system were obtained by Totik
[
47
]
. We further emphasize the
recent paper of Gát
[
11
]
, where he answered a question posed by
Zalcwasser
[
50
]
in 1936 concerning almost everywhere
convergence of arithmetic means of subsequences of partial sums of Fourier
series.

1.3.
Almost everywhere convergence of the Cesàro means of
Walsh-Fourier series

Let

\mathbb{A}:=\left\{t_{k,n}:=\frac{A_{n-k}^{\alpha_{n}-1}}{A_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}}:k=0,\dots,n,\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

where
\{\alpha_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset(0,1]
and

A_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}:=\frac{(\alpha_{n}+1)\cdots(\alpha_{n}+n)}{n!},\qquad A_{0}^{\alpha_{n}}:=1.

We have

\frac{\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}}{\widetilde{T}_{2^{s-1},n}}=\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{n}}{2^{s-1}}\right)\cdots\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{n}}{2^{s}-1}\right).

Hence

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}}{\widetilde{T}_{2^{s-1},n}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=2^{s-1}}^{2^{s}-1}\ln\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{n}}{k}\right)=\sum\limits_{k=2^{s-1}}^{2^{s}-1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{n}}{k}+O\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}\sum\limits_{k=2^{s-1}}^{2^{s}-1}\frac{1}{k}+O\left(\frac{1}{2^{s}}\right)=\alpha_{n}\ln\left(2\right)+o\left(1\right)\text{ \ }\left(s\rightarrow\infty\right).

We consider two cases:

Case (a):

\alpha_{n}\equiv\alpha\in(0,1]
is
constant. In this situation one has

Case (b):

1.4.
Almost everywhere divergence of the Nörlund logarithmic means of
Walsh-Fourier series

Consider the Nörlund logarithmic means generated by the matrix

\mathbb{L}:=\left\{t_{k,n}:=\frac{1}{\ln(n)}\frac{1}{n-k}:k=0,\dots,n-1,\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

where

\ln(n):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}

denotes the
n
th harmonic number. A straightforward calculation shows that

\lim_{(s,n)_{\Delta_{\kappa}}\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}}{\widetilde{T}_{2^{s-1},n}}\right)=\lim_{(s,n)_{\Delta_{\kappa}}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{s\ln\left(2\right)+\gamma+2^{-s-1}+O\left(2^{-2s}\right)}{\left(s-1\right)\ln\left(2\right)+\gamma+2^{-s}+O\left(2^{-2s}\right)}=1.

Hence the maximal operator

f\longmapsto\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{L}}f|

does
not
satisfy a weak type
(1,1)
inequality; that is,

\Bigl\|\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{L}}|\Bigr\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{I})\rightarrow L_{1,\infty}(\mathbb{I})}=\infty.

Applying Theorem
4
, we deduce the existence of an integrable
function
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})
whose Nörlund logarithmic means (with
respect to the Walsh system) diverge everywhere. Almost-everywhere
divergence for
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{L}}
was proved by the first author
together with Gát in
[
12
]
. More recently, this phenomenon
was extended to general orthogonal systems, yielding in particular a Nörlund-logarithmic analogue of Bochkarev’s classical theorem in
[
18
]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
E^{0}\subset\mathbb{I}
be a measurable set with
|E^{0}|=0
, and let
\{\varepsilon_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a sequence of positive
numbers such that
\varepsilon_{j}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. Then there exists
an increasing sequence
\{b_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
with
b_{j}\uparrow\infty
as
j\to\infty
. Moreover, the choice of
b_{j}

depends on
\varepsilon_{j}
and can be made arbitrarily large by taking
\varepsilon_{j}
sufficiently small.

In addition, there exists a sequence
\{A_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
of
measurable subsets of
\mathbb{I}
and a sequence of Walsh polynomials
\{W_{b_{j}}^{0}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that:

(1)

E^{0}\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}A_{j}
;

(2)

each
x\in E^{0}
belongs to infinitely many of the sets
A_{j}
;

(3)

|A_{j}|<\varepsilon_{j}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
;

(4)

\operatorname{sp}(W_{b_{j}}^{0})\subset[2^{b_{j}},2^{b_{j}+1})
for
all
j\in\mathbb{N}
;

(5)

for all
x\in A_{j},\ j\in\mathbb{N};

\bigl|\mathcal{T}_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{\mathbb{T}}(W_{b_{j}}^{0};x)\bigr|\geq 2^{b_{j}/2};\qquad

(6)

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|W_{b_{j}}^{0}\|_{1}<\infty.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

The existence of a family
\{A_{j}\}
satisfying properties (1)-(3) is a
standard consequence of the fact that
|E^{0}|=0
(see, for example,
[
42
]
). More precisely, there exists a sequence of dyadic intervals
\{I_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that

E^{0}\subset\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{k},\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|I_{k}|<1,

and each
x\in E^{0}
belongs to infinitely many of the intervals
I_{k}
.
We may represent each
A_{j}
as a finite union of these intervals:

A_{j}:=\bigcup_{k=n_{j}}^{n_{j+1}-1}I_{k},

for a suitably chosen increasing sequence of indices
\{n_{j}\}
.

Since
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|I_{k}|<1
, we can choose
n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots

such that

|A_{j}|=\sum_{k=n_{j}}^{n_{j+1}-1}|I_{k}|\leq\sum_{k=n_{j}}^{\infty}|I_{k}|<\varepsilon_{j},\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}.

This yields property (3). Property (1) follows from
E^{0}\subset\bigcup_{k}I_{k}
and the fact that
\{n_{j}\}
partitions the index set
\mathbb{N}
into disjoint blocks, while property (2) follows from the
assumption that each
x\in E^{0}
belongs to infinitely many of the
intervals
I_{k}
.

Next we define the sequence
\{b_{j}\}
. Set

(46)

2^{b_{j}}:=\max\Bigl\{\frac{1}{|I_{k}|}:n_{j}\leq k<n_{j+1}\Bigr\}.

Since
|I_{k}|\to 0
as
k\to\infty
(because
\sum_{k}|I_{k}|<\infty
),
equality (
46
) implies that
b_{j}\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
. Moreover,
by refining the choice of the indices
\{n_{j}\}
(equivalently, by taking
\varepsilon_{j}
smaller), we can make
b_{j}
arbitrarily large, which
establishes the claimed dependence of
b_{j}
on
\varepsilon_{j}
.

We now turn to the construction of the polynomials
W_{j}^{0}
. Define

\widetilde{W}_{b_{j}}^{0}(x):=\alpha_{j}\,r_{b_{j}}(x)\,\mathds{1}_{A_{j}}(x)=\sum_{k=2^{b_{j}}}^{2^{b_{j}+1}-1}C_{k}^{(j)}w_{k}(x),

where
\alpha_{j}>0
will be chosen later,
r_{b_{j}}
denotes the
b_{j}
-th Rademacher function, and
w_{k}
is the
k
-th Walsh function. Thus
\widetilde{W}_{j}^{0}
is a Walsh polynomial, and its Walsh spectrum is
contained in
[2^{b_{j}},2^{b_{j}+1})
.

We define the desired polynomial by

W_{b_{j}}^{0}(x):=\sum_{k=2^{b_{j}}}^{2^{b_{j}+1}-1}\frac{C_{k}^{(j)}}{T_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{\left(k+1\right)}}\,w_{k}(x).

The denominator is uniformly bounded below. Indeed, since each row of

\mathbb{T}
is nondecreasing and has sum one, the mass of the last

n-k
terms is at least
(n-k)/(n+1)
. With
n=2^{b_{j}+2}
and

2^{b_{j}}\leq k<2^{b_{j}+1}
, this gives

T_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{(k+1)}=\sum_{l=k+1}^{2^{b_{j}+2}}t_{l,2^{b_{j}+2}}\geq\frac{2^{b_{j}+2}-k}{2^{b_{j}+2}+1}\geq c>0,

where
c
is an absolute constant independent of
j
and
k
. Hence

\Bigl\|W_{b_{j}}^{0}\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=2^{b_{j}}}^{2^{b_{j}+1}-1}\Bigl(\frac{C_{k}^{(j)}}{T_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{\left(k+1\right)}}\Bigr)^{2}\lesssim\sum_{k=2^{b_{j}}}^{2^{b_{j}+1}-1}\bigl(C_{k}^{(j)}\bigr)^{2}=\Bigl\|\widetilde{W}_{b_{j}}^{0}\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}.

By Parseval’s identity and the definition of
\widetilde{W}_{b_{j}}^{0}
,

\Bigl\|\widetilde{W}_{b_{j}}^{0}\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}=\alpha_{j}^{2}|A_{j}|,

so that

\|W_{b_{j}}^{0}\|_{1}\leq\|W_{b_{j}}^{0}\|_{2}\lesssim\alpha_{j}\,|A_{j}|^{1/2}.

The sequence
\{a_{j}\}
of positive integers was constructed according to
conditions (
37
)-(
39
), and we define

\varepsilon_{j}:=2^{-2a_{j}},\qquad\alpha_{j}:=2^{a_{j}/2}

and hence

\alpha_{j}\,|A_{j}|^{1/2}\leq 2^{a_{j}/2}\cdot 2^{-a_{j}}=2^{-a_{j}/2}.

Thus

\|W_{b_{j}}^{0}\|_{1}\leq\|W_{b_{j}}^{0}\|_{2}\lesssim 2^{-a_{j}/2}

and

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|W_{b_{j}}^{0}\|_{1}<\infty,

which proves property (6).

It remains to verify property (5). Using the definition of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}
and the fact that
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}
acts
diagonally on Walsh polynomials, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{\mathbb{T}}(W_{b_{j}}^{0};x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{b_{j}+2}}t_{k,2^{b_{j}+2}}\,S_{k}(W_{b_{j}}^{0};x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=2^{b_{j}}}^{2^{b_{j}+1}-1}\frac{C_{l}^{(j)}}{T_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{\left(l+1\right)}}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{b_{j}+2}}t_{k,2^{b_{j}+2}}S_{k}(w_{l};x).

Since
S_{k}(w_{l};x)=0
for
k\leq l
and
S_{k}(w_{l};x)=w_{l}(x)
for
k>l
, we obtain

\sum_{k=0}^{2^{b_{j}+2}}t_{k,2^{b_{j}+2}}S_{k}(w_{l};x)=\Bigl(\sum_{k=l+1}^{2^{b_{j}+2}}t_{k,2^{b_{j}+2}}\Bigr)w_{l}(x)=T_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{\left(l+1\right)}\,w_{l}(x).

Hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{\mathbb{T}}(W_{j}^{0};x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=2^{b_{j}}}^{2^{b_{j}+1}-1}\frac{C_{l}^{(j)}}{T_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{\left(l+1\right)}}T_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{\left(l+1\right)}\,w_{l}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=2^{b_{j}}}^{2^{b_{j}+1}-1}C_{l}^{(j)}w_{l}(x)=\widetilde{W}_{b_{j}}^{0}(x)=\alpha_{j}\,r_{b_{j}}(x)\,\mathds{1}_{A_{j}}(x)

\displaystyle=2^{a_{j}/2}r_{b_{j}}(x)\,\mathds{1}_{A_{j}}(x).

In particular, for
x\in A_{j}
we have
|r_{b_{j}}(x)|=1
, and therefore

\bigl|\mathcal{T}_{2^{b_{j}+2}}^{\mathbb{T}}(W_{b_{j}}^{0};x)\bigr|=2^{a_{j}/2}\geq 2^{b_{j}/2},\qquad x\in A_{j},

which is property (5).

This completes the proof of Lemma
2
.

Proof of Theorem
5

Define the matrix
\mathbb{V}=\{t_{k,n}\}
by

t_{k,n}:=\begin{cases}0,&k=0,\dots,n-\lambda_{n}-1,\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{n}+1},&k=n-\lambda_{n},\dots,n,\end{cases}\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Then the corresponding matrix means
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{V}}
coincide
with the de la Vallée Poussin means

in (
22
).

For this matrix one checks (using the definition of
\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}
)
that

(47)

\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}\lesssim\begin{cases}\dfrac{2^{s}}{\lambda_{n}+1},&s=0,1,\dots,|\lambda_{n}|,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda_{n}+1},&s=|\lambda_{n}|+1,\dots,|n|.\end{cases}

Proof of part (a)

Assume that condition (
23
) holds, i.e.

n=O(\lambda_{n})\quad(n\to\infty).

Summing the bounds in (
47
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{|n|}\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}+1}\sum_{s=0}^{|\lambda_{n}|}2^{s}\;+\;\sum_{s=|\lambda_{n}|+1}^{|n|}\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda_{n}+1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{2^{|\lambda_{n}|+1}}{\lambda_{n}+1}\;+\;(|n|-|\lambda_{n}|)\,\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda_{n}+1}.

Since
2^{|\lambda_{n}|}\simeq\lambda_{n}
and
\lambda_{n}/(\lambda_{n}+1)\leq 1
,
this yields

\sum_{s=0}^{|n|}\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}\lesssim 1+|n|-|\lambda_{n}|.

Moreover,
|n|\simeq\log_{2}n
and
|\lambda_{n}|\simeq\log_{2}\lambda_{n}
, so

|n|-|\lambda_{n}|\lesssim\log\frac{n}{\lambda_{n}}.

By (
23
), there exists
C\geq 1
such that
n/\lambda_{n}\leq C
for all
sufficiently large
n
, and hence

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{s=0}^{|n|}\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}<\infty.

Thus the uniform boundedness condition in Theorem
4
(1) is
satisfied for the matrix
\mathbb{V}
. Consequently, for every
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})
one has

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{V}}(f;x)=V_{n}^{(\lambda)}(f;x)\longrightarrow f(x)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

for almost every
x\in\mathbb{I}
. This proves part (a) of Theorem
5
.

Proof of part (b)

Now assume that

(48)

\frac{n}{\lambda_{n}}\longrightarrow\infty\quad(n\rightarrow\infty),

Set

\omega_{n}:=\left\lfloor\frac{|n|-|\lambda_{n}|}{2}\right\rfloor.

By (
48
), we have
\omega_{n}\to\infty
and

\omega_{n}/|n|\to 0
. Moreover, if
(s,n)\in\Delta_{\omega}
, then, for all
large
n
,
s>|\lambda_{n}|
; hence the second line of (
47
)
applies

\lim_{(s,n)_{\Delta_{\omega}}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}}{\widetilde{T}_{2^{s-1},n}}=1.

In other words, the triangular ratio condition (
20
) of Theorem
4
(2) is satisfied in this case.

Therefore, by Theorem
4
(2) there exists an integrable
function
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bigl|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{V}}(f;x)\bigr|=\infty

at every point
x\in\mathbb{I}
. Since
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{V}}(f;x)=V_{n}^{(\lambda)}(f;x)
, this is precisely the statement of part (b) of Theorem
5
.

The proof of Theorem
5
is complete.

7.
Proof of Theorem
6

We now observe that the triangular limit condition (
11
) is not required in the special case when
the weights
\Omega_{k}(n)
originate from a single nondecreasing
sequence
\{Q_{2^{k}}\}_{k\geq 0}
. Indeed, suppose that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we define

(49)

\Omega_{k}(n):=\frac{Q_{2^{k}}}{Q_{2^{|n|}}},\qquad 0\leq k\leq|n|,

where the sequence
\{Q_{2^{k}}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfies

0\leq Q_{2^{k}}\leq Q_{2^{k+1}}\qquad(k\geq 0).

In order to prove Theorem
6
by Theorems
1
and
4
it is sufficient to show that there exists a sequence of divergence
\left(\gamma_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
a_{k}:=Q_{2^{k}}
and
A_{k}:=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}
.
Assume that

(50)

\sup_{n\geq 0}\frac{A_{n}}{a_{n}}=\infty.

Then there are a constant
c\in(0,1)
, an increasing sequence
n_{j}\uparrow\infty
, and integers
\gamma_{j}\uparrow\infty
such that

a_{\,n_{j}-\gamma_{j}}\;\geq\;c\,a_{\,n_{j}}\qquad(j\in\mathbb{N}).

Proof of Proposition
1
.

We first assume that

(51)

\forall m\in\mathbb{N},\ \text{there exist infinitely many }n\ \text{such
that }\ a_{n-m}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\,a_{n}.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and choose
n_{m}
so large that
n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots

and
a_{n_{m}-m}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\,a_{n_{m}}
. Setting
\gamma_{m}:=m
, we obtain

\gamma_{m}\uparrow\infty
and the desired estimate along the subsequence
\{n_{m}\}
.

Next we show that (
51
) must hold. Assume to the contrary
that it fails. Then its negation is

(52)

\exists\,m\in\mathbb{N}\ \text{such that only finitely many }n\ \text{satisfy}\ a_{n-m}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\,a_{n}.

Thus there exist integers
m
and
N
such that

(53)

\forall n>N:\quad a_{\,n-m}<\tfrac{1}{2}\,a_{\,n}.

We now show that (
53
) contradicts (
50
).

Decomposing the sum
A_{n}
into
m
arithmetic progressions
modulo
m
, for any
n
we may write

(54)

A_{n}\;\leq\;C+\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\ \sum_{0\leq j\leq(n-l)/m}a_{\,n-l-jm},

where
C:=\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_{k}
.

Iterating (
53
), for all
j\geq 0
with
n-l-jm>N
we have

a_{\,n-l-jm}\;\leq\;\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{j}a_{\,n-l}\;\leq\;\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{j}a_{n}.

Substituting into (
54
) yields

A_{n}\;\leq\;C+a_{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{j}\;=\;C+2m\,a_{n}.

Consequently,

\sup_{n}\frac{A_{n}}{a_{n}}\;\leq\;\frac{C}{a_{n}}+2m\;\leq\;\frac{C}{a_{N}}+2m\;<\infty,

contradicting (
50
). Thus (
52
) and hence (
53
) are
impossible. Therefore (
51
) holds, and the proposition follows.

∎

Completion of the proof of Theorem
6
.

Part (a1) is precisely the uniform boundedness criterion for
Nörlund means in the Walsh setting proved in
[
17
]
. Indeed, for

\mathbb{Q}
one has, up to absolute constants,

\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}=\frac{Q_{2^{s}}}{Q_{2^{|n|}}},\qquad 0\leq s\leq|n|,

and therefore (
25
) is equivalent to the uniform boundedness of the
corresponding
H_{1}(\mathbb{I})\to L_{1}(\mathbb{I})
norms. The pointwise
convergence then follows from Theorem
4
(1).

Assume now that (
26
) holds. With
a_{s}:=Q_{2^{s}}
and

A_{m}:=\sum_{s=0}^{m}a_{s}
, condition (
26
) is exactly

\sup_{m}\frac{A_{m}}{a_{m}}=\infty.

By Proposition
1
, there are
c>0
, integers
n_{j}\uparrow\infty
,
and
\gamma_{j}\uparrow\infty
such that

Q_{2^{n_{j}-\gamma_{j}}}\geq cQ_{2^{n_{j}}}.

Consequently, for the weights in (
49
),

\Omega_{n_{j}-\gamma_{j}}(2^{n_{j}})=\frac{Q_{2^{n_{j}-\gamma_{j}}}}{Q_{2^{n_{j}}}}\geq c.

This supplies the subsequential divergence blocks required in the proof of
Theorem
4
. Applying the construction there to the dyadic
subsequence
2^{n_{j}}
yields an
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})
for which the Nörlund
means
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{Q}}(f;x)
diverge at every point

x\in\mathbb{I}
. This proves part (b1).
∎

8.
Appendix A: Background Material

Let
\mathbb{P}:=\{1,2,\dots\}
,
\mathbb{N}:=\mathbb{P}\cup\{0\},\mathbb{I}:=[0,1)
.

We denote by
L_{1}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)
the space of all (Lebesgue)
measurable functions
f:\mathbb{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\left\|f\right\|_{1}:=\int\limits_{\mathbb{I}}\left|f\right|<\infty.

The weak
L_{1}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)
space, denoted by
L_{1,\infty}\left(\mathbb{I}\right),
consists of all measurable functions
f:\mathbb{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for which

\left\|f\right\|_{1,\infty}:=\sup\lambda\left|\left\{\left|f\right|>\lambda\right\}\right|<\infty.

Let
\{w_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
denote the Walsh-Paley orthonormal system on

\mathbb{I}:=[0,1)
, and let
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})
. We denote by
S_{n}(f;x)
the
n
th partial sum of the Walsh–Fourier series of
f
,

S_{n}(f;x):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\widehat{f}(k)\,w_{k}(x),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

The operator
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}
can be written as a convolution
operator,

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}(f;x)=(f\ast V_{n}^{\mathbb{T}})(x),

where

V_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}(u):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}t_{k,n}D_{k}(u),\qquad D_{k}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}w_{j}(x)

is the
k
th Walsh–Dirichlet kernel. The corresponding weighted Lebesgue
function is defined by

\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}:=\|V_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}\|_{1}.

Let
(f_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a martingale with respect to the dyadic
filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{I}
, where each
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is generated by the dyadic intervals of length
2^{-n}
. We
recall the standard dyadic maximal function and square function:

S(f)(x):=\Biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f_{n}(x)-f_{n-1}(x)|^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}.

The
dyadic Hardy space

H_{1}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)
is defined by

H_{1}\left(\mathbb{I}\right):=\{f:S(f)\in L_{1}(\mathbb{I})\},\qquad\|f\|_{H_{1}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)}:=\|S(f)\|_{1}.

We next record some notation related to the binary expansion of integers and
to the matrix
\mathbb{T}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write its dyadic
expansion as

(55)

n=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{j}(n)\,2^{j},\qquad\varepsilon_{j}(n)\in\{0,1\}.

Set

\left|n\right|:=\max\left\{s:\varepsilon_{s}\left(n\right)=1\right\}.

Then (
55
) can be rewritten as

n=\sum_{j=0}^{\left|n\right|}\varepsilon_{j}(n)\,2^{j}.

For
s\in\mathbb{N}
we set

n^{(s)}:=\sum_{j=s}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{j}(n)\,2^{j},\qquad n(s):=\sum_{j=0}^{s}\varepsilon_{j}(n)\,2^{j}.

Furthermore, for
0\leq m\leq n
we define

T_{n}^{(m)}:=\sum_{l=m}^{n}t_{l,n}.

A useful decomposition of the kernel
V_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}
was obtained in
[
19
]
. It can be written in the form

V_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}=\sum_{s=0}^{|n|}\varepsilon_{s}(n)\,w_{n^{(s+1)}}\,\sum_{k=0}^{2^{s}-1}T_{n}^{\bigl(k+1+n^{(s+1)}\bigr)}\,w_{k},\qquad n\in\mathbb{P}.

Suppose that
\varepsilon_{s}(n)=1
for some
s\in\{0,1,\dots,|n|\}
.
Using the identity
2^{s}-1-k=(2^{s}-1)\oplus k
(where
\oplus
denotes
bitwise addition modulo
2
) and the relation
w_{n\oplus m}=w_{n}w_{m}
(see
[
42
,
21
]
),
and applying Abel’s transformation, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{2^{s}-1}T_{n}^{\bigl(n^{(s)}-(2^{s}-1-k)\bigr)}\,w_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{s}-1}T_{n}^{\bigl(n^{(s)}-k\bigr)}\,w_{(2^{s}-1)\oplus k}

\displaystyle=w_{2^{s}-1}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{s}}T_{n}^{\bigl(n^{(s)}-k+1\bigr)}\,w_{k-1}

\displaystyle=w_{2^{s}-1}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{s}-1}\Bigl(T_{n}^{\bigl(n^{(s)}-k+1\bigr)}-T_{n}^{\bigl(n^{(s)}-k\bigr)}\Bigr)D_{k}+w_{2^{s}-1}T_{n}^{\bigl(n^{(s+1)}+1\bigr)}D_{2^{s}}

\displaystyle=-\,w_{2^{s}-1}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{s}-1}t_{n^{(s)}-k,n}\,D_{k}+w_{2^{s}-1}T_{n}^{\bigl(n^{(s+1)}+1\bigr)}D_{2^{s}}

\displaystyle=-\,w_{2^{s}-1}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{s}-2}\bigl(t_{n^{(s)}-k,n}-t_{n^{(s)}-k-1,n}\bigr)\,kK_{k}

\displaystyle\quad-\,w_{2^{s}-1}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{s}-1}t_{n^{(s+1)}+1,n}\,(2^{s}-1)K_{2^{s}-1}+w_{2^{s}-1}T_{n}^{\bigl(n^{(s+1)}+1\bigr)}D_{2^{s}},

where
K_{k}
denotes the
k
th Walsh–Fejér kernel and
D_{k}
the
Walsh–Dirichlet kernel.

Since
w_{n}w_{n^{(s+1)}}=w_{n(s)}
and
D_{2^{s}}\,w_{2^{s}-1}w_{n(s)}=w_{2^{s}}D_{2^{s}}
, we arrive at the decomposition

(56)

\displaystyle w_{n}V_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\sum_{s=0}^{|n|}\varepsilon_{s}(n)\,w_{n(s)}w_{2^{s}-1}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{s}-2}\bigl(t_{n^{(s)}-k,n}-t_{n^{(s)}-k-1,n}\bigr)\,kK_{k}}_{=:V_{n,1}^{\mathbb{T}}}

\displaystyle\quad\underbrace{-\sum_{s=0}^{|n|}\varepsilon_{s}(n)\,w_{n(s)}w_{2^{s}-1}\,t_{n^{(s+1)}+1,n}\,(2^{s}-1)K_{2^{s}-1}}_{=:V_{n,2}^{\mathbb{T}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\sum_{s=0}^{|n|}\varepsilon_{s}(n)\,T_{n}^{\bigl(n^{(s+1)}+1\bigr)}\,w_{2^{s}}D_{2^{s}}}_{=:V_{n,3}^{\mathbb{T}}}.

Thus the kernel
w_{n}V_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}
decomposes naturally into three
components
V_{n,1}^{\mathbb{T}}
,
V_{n,2}^{\mathbb{T}}
and
V_{n,3}^{\mathbb{T}}
, which will play distinct roles in the proof of Theorem
4
.

We introduce the quantities

\widetilde{T}_{n(s),n}:=\sum_{l=0}^{n(s)-1}t_{n-l,n}.

It is straightforward to check that

T_{n}^{\bigl(n^{(s+1)}+1\bigr)}=\sum_{l=n^{(s+1)}+1}^{n}t_{l,n}=\sum_{l=0}^{n(s)-1}t_{n-l,n}=:\widetilde{T}_{n(s),n}.

When
\varepsilon_{s}\left(n\right)=1
then

\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}\leq\widetilde{T}_{n(s),n}\leq\widetilde{T}_{2^{s+1},n}\leq 2\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}.

We define

\Omega_{s}\left(n\right):=\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n},\text{ \ \ \ \ }0\leq s\leq|n|.

We assume that the following limit exists:

(57)

\lim_{(s,n)_{\Delta}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Omega_{s}\left(n\right)}{\Omega_{s-1}\left(n\right)}=\lim_{(s,n)_{\Delta}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}}{\widetilde{T}_{2^{s-1},n}}=L\geq 1.

It is easy to see that

P_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right)=\sum\limits_{s=0}^{|n|}\varepsilon_{s}\left(n\right)\widetilde{T}_{2^{s},n}w_{2^{s}}D_{2^{s}}

and the martingale transform (
4
) can be written in the form

(58)

M_{n}\left(\Omega\right)f=w_{n}\left(f\ast\left(w_{n}P_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right)\right)\right)=\left(fw_{n}\right)\ast P_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right).

From (
56
) we can write

(59)

\displaystyle f\ast V_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{n}\left(fw_{n}\right)\ast\left(w_{n}V_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}\right)=w_{n}\left(fw_{n}\right)\ast V_{n,1}^{\mathbb{T}}

\displaystyle+w_{n}\left(fw_{n}\right)\ast V_{n,2}^{\mathbb{T}}+w_{n}\left(fw_{n}\right)\ast P_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right).

Throughout the paper we work on the dyadic group
\mathbb{I}:=[0,1)

equipped with the dyadic expansion

x=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{j}2^{-(j+1)},\qquad x_{j}\in\{0,1\}.

For each integer
m\geq 1
and any choice of digits
t_{0},\dots,t_{m-1}\in\{0,1\}
, we define the dyadic interval

I_{m}(t_{0},\dots,t_{m-1}):=\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{I}:x_{j}=t_{j}\ \text{for }j=0,\dots,m-1\bigr\}.

Equivalently,

I_{m}(t_{0},\dots,t_{m-1})=\Biggl[\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}t_{j}2^{-(j+1)},\ \sum_{j=0}^{m-1}t_{j}2^{-(j+1)}+2^{-m}\Biggr).

In particular, in the notation

I_{a}\bigl(t_{0},\dots,t_{a-\gamma(a)-1},t_{a-2\gamma(a)-1},\dots,t_{a-\gamma(a)-1}\bigr)

we mean the dyadic interval of rank
a
whose dyadic digits coincide with
the specified values at the corresponding positions; the remaining digits
are irrelevant for the expressions in which this notation appears.
