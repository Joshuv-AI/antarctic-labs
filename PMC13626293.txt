Goal Location Beta Oscillations and Sharp Wave–Ripples Are Complementary Markers of Learning-Related Changes in Hippocampal–Neocortical Networks

Sameera Shridhar, Zachary M Leveroni, Nan Zhou, Ellie Quattrocchi, Audrey E Kaye, Jaquelin Gutierrez, Amanda Rodríguez León, Leslie M Kay, Jai Y Yu

Abstract

The hippocampus and neocortex are thought to undergo reorganization to incorporate new information during learning. How can we identify network states that indicate learning is occurring? In the hippocampus, one network signature with these properties is the sharp wave–ripple (SWR), which occurs more frequently during initial learning. However, it remains unclear which neural signatures track changes in neocortical networks during learning. We therefore recorded local field potentials simultaneously from the dorsal hippocampus (CA1) and prefrontal cortex (PFC) as male rats learned spatial navigation tasks. After animals reached goal locations, we found beta frequency (∼20 Hz) oscillations in PFC that increased in power with improvements in performance across days of training. In contrast, the rate and size of hippocampal SWRs decreased over the same period. Furthermore, goal-entry beta in PFC but not in CA1 was correlated with reward likelihood. Our results suggest that neocortical beta oscillations develop gradually as learning occurs, which stands in contrast to the reduction in hippocampal SWRs as the task becomes familiar. We hypothesize that PFC beta oscillations and hippocampal SWRs are complementary markers of experience-dependent change in hippocampal–neocortical networks during learning.

Significance Statement

Hippocampal and neocortical circuits are crucial for learning. The network states associated with the learning process remain challenging to identify. It remains unclear how to distinguish states associated with active reorganization during learning versus states of stability after learning has taken place. We report that properties of neocortical beta oscillations and hippocampal sharp wave–ripples show an inverse relationship across training. Our results support the hypothesis that these network signatures are complementary learning- and experience-related markers in hippocampal–neocortical networks.

Introduction

Learning involves incorporating new information from experience into existing knowledge structures. This process is hypothesized to rely on the complementary contributions of the hippocampus and neocortex (; ). Hippocampal networks show rapid activity changes under novel conditions to capture new information, whereas neocortical networks are gradually reorganized through coordination with the hippocampus to form enduring memories of new experiences. Whether learning is occurring or has occurred can be assessed by observing changes in performance. At the neural level, however, the network states associated with the learning process remain challenging to identify. How can we determine if these networks are in a state of reorganization associated with learning as opposed to being in a state of stability after learning has taken place?

Network signatures associated with learning can be found in the hippocampus. Goal location hippocampal sharp wave–ripples (SWRs) become more frequent in novel environments (; ; ; ; ) or when a change occurs in a familiar environment or task (; ; ; ). SWRs are hypothesized to support learning by initiating the reorganization of extrahippocampal networks during awake and sleep states to incorporate new and relevant information (, , ; ). In contrast, it remains unclear which neocortical network markers can signal learning-related changes during the waking state.

Several lines of evidence point to beta oscillations (15–30 Hz) as a candidate neocortical network marker for learning-related changes. First, in olfactory learning, beta oscillations in the piriform cortex and olfactory bulb (OB) during odor sampling develop with experience in a task (; ; ; ; ; ). Second, across human and nonhuman animal models, neocortical beta oscillations are strongly linked to reward processing, which is critical for learning through reinforcement (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ,, ; ). Frontal cortical beta oscillations have been observed before arrival at the goal () or after the outcome at the goal has been revealed (; ; ; ; ; ; ), which supports the hypothesis that beta oscillations are network states supporting reward outcome processing to guide reinforcement learning (). Third, we recently found learning-related changes in the power of prefrontal cortical beta oscillations at goal locations in spatial navigation tasks ().

The dynamics of beta during initial task acquisition remain poorly understood. Furthermore, it is unknown how neocortical beta dynamics during initial task acquisition relate to hippocampal markers of learning. We therefore examined hippocampal–prefrontal cortical beta during initial task acquisition and its relationship with changes in hippocampal SWRs. To connect learning-related dynamics with known reward processing correlates of beta, we then determined how hippocampal–neocortical beta relates to task-specific variables such as reward outcome and goal choice.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Long–Evans rats (Charles River Laboratories, 12 males, 7–12 months) were used for the study. All procedures were approved by the university’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, according to the guidelines of the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care.

Pretraining

To accustom animals to exploring and drinking liquid reward (70% evaporated milk, Carnation, with 5% sucrose), we first conducted open field training with droplets scattered in the field to encourage exploration. This was done for 3 d, with each session lasting 10–20 min. Animals were then food-restricted to maintain 85–90% of their baseline weight and trained to run on an elevated linear track for reward for three 10 min sessions per day over 3 d.

Maze apparatus

All tracks and mazes were constructed from acrylic (black DP-9, 8 cm wide, 4 cm tall walls, 76 cm above floor level). Reward delivery in all tasks was automated. Infrared sensors were located at the ends of the maze arms to detect the rat’s arrival and to trigger the reward. On rewarded trials, reward delivery was triggered at the time of goal entry. Reward was delivered by a syringe pump (100 μl at 20 ml/min, NE-500, New Era Pump Systems). The length of each arm of the Y maze was 84 cm (center to goal).

Neural implants

Animals were chronically implanted with neural recording devices () with up to 32 adjustable tetrodes. Up to 16 tetrodes targeted the CA1 region of the left hippocampus (AP −3.8, ML −2.75 relative to bregma), and up to 16 tetrodes targeted both hemispheres of the prefrontal cortex (PFC) (AP +3.5, ML ±1.5 relative to bregma and −2.5 to −3.5 DV), which corresponds to the prelimbic region (). The PFC bundle was angled at 15° toward the midline to avoid the superior sagittal sinus. A ground screw was placed in the skull above the right cerebellum. Tetrodes were gradually adjusted into the target regions over 2–3 weeks after surgery, based on local field potential (LFP) markers and estimated depth of the tetrodes. Once the tetrodes were within the target brain regions, small adjustments of 25 μm, when necessary, were made to isolate single cells. Tetrode locations were verified at the end of the experiment. To mark the tip of the tetrode, electrolytic lesions were created for each tetrode (100 μA, 2 s). Nissl staining was used to verify the location of the lesion was within the intended area.

One male animal was implanted with electrodes (100 μm, formvar-coated stainless steel) targeting the left OB and PFC. Two electrodes (separated by 1.5 mm) were implanted in the OB (+8.5 anterior to bregma, 1.5 mm lateral to midline and 4 mm from dorsal surface) and two (separated by 1 mm) in PFC (+3.5 anterior to bregma, 1.5 lateral and −2.5 to −3.5 deep).

Retraining

Five days prior to the main experiment, animals were food-restricted to 85–90% of their baseline weight and retrained on the linear track for 2 d.

Maze tasks

We trained two groups of animals on Y-shaped mazes. One group (n = 5) was trained to perform a continuous alternation task, while the other group (n = 6) was trained to perform a binary multiarmed bandit task, which we refer to as the probability task. Animals were trained over 5 consecutive days, with four maze sessions (10 min each) per day. Maze sessions were separated by 45 min rests.

For the continuous alternation task, the animal needed to visit goal locations in a specific sequence to be rewarded on every trial. There were three goal locations in each maze, which were wells at the end of the arms. The animal needed to alternate visits between the left and right goals (L and R) via the center goal (C), i.e. C, L, C, R, C, L, C, R, ..., consistent with previously described spatial alternation tasks (; ; ). For example, if the rat was at the center goal, it would be rewarded next at the left goal only if it had previously visited the right goal. Similarly, if the rat was at the center goal, it would be rewarded next at the right goal only if it had previously visited the left goal. If the rat was at the left or right goals, it would be rewarded only by going to the center goal. For the probability task, each of the three goals of the Y maze had a different likelihood of delivering reward when visited (25, 50, and 75%). Once the animal had visited a goal, it had to choose between the two other goals. Repeated visits to the same goal did not yield further opportunities to claim a reward.

Data recording

Data were collected using the Trodes data acquisition system (SpikeGadgets). Neural data were recorded using a 128-channel digitizing headstage. The headstage was tethered via a commutator to the main data acquisition system. The commutator and tether were supported by a passive suspension system. Raw data were recorded at 30 kHz. LFP (<300 Hz) was extracted at 1,500 Hz. The recording system also controlled reward delivery with its scripting function.

Behavioral analysis

Performance on the alternation task was calculated as the mean proportion of goal visits that followed the correct alternation pattern. For the probability task, we calculated the proportion of visits to the goal with higher reward probability on the next trial. For example, if the animal had previously visited the 25% goal, the next choice is counted as correct if the animal visited the 75% goal rather than the 50% goal.

Spectral analysis

We computed beta power for each brain region using a continuous wavelet transform (CWT). For the LFP from each tetrode, we first applied CWT using the generalized Morse wavelet (γ = 4, β = 7.5) to extract time-resolved spectral power for 0.5–130 Hz in 1 Hz steps. This was done using the GhostiPy package (). The power in the beta band (15–30 Hz) isolated from the CWT spectrogram was then calculated. We found a consistent transient increase in beta power occurring after goal entry. Goal entry was defined as the time when the animal triggered the infrared sensor at the goal. To calculate the burst of beta power after goal entry, we identified a 500 ms window centered on the time of the peak of the beta burst for each day for each animal. We calculated the mean power in the 500 ms window, and to control for changes in baseline beta power, we subtracted the beta power of a baseline period, defined as the 500 ms before goal entry.

SWR detection

SWRs were identified based on previous methods (github.com/Eden-Kramer-Lab/ripple_detection; ). We first filtered out artifacts due to LFP noise by excluding time intervals in which the LFP signal magnitude exceeded 10 standard deviations of values across all tetrodes. LFP from tetrodes in the dorsal CA1 region (oriens and pyramidale layers) was filtered in the ripple frequency band (150–250 Hz). The median of the smoothed envelope (Gaussian kernel σ = 4 ms) was calculated. We used a threshold of 2 standard deviations for detecting SWRs, where the peak value of the ripple-frequency-filtered signal needed to exceed 2 standard deviations to be classified as a ripple. In addition, a detected event needed to have a minimum duration of 15 ms and separation of 50 ms. Only SWR events occurring at speed <4 cm/s and at the goal locations were included. The SWR size was calculated as the mean standard deviation above the mean ripple-frequency-band-filtered signal for the duration of the SWR. The SWR rate was calculated for the first 10 s after goal entry. Goal visits shorter than this duration were excluded from the calculation. The 10 s interval is based on the behavior of the animal at the goal, where it typically remains immobile to consume the reward.

Experiment design and statistical analyses

We used linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) to account for variance explained by fixed as well as random effects (from individual animals). We report the marginal R2 value, which is the proportion of variance explained by the fixed effect, and the conditional R2 value, which is the proportion of variance explained by the fixed and random effects (). Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. We used Python packages for statistics: SciPy (scipy.org) and statsmodels (statsmodels.org) for LMMs. We used pingouin (pingouin-stats.org) for ANOVA. Plots were made using Matplotlib (matplotlib.org).

Day averages are mean values (beta power, SWR rate or size, reward probability, or goal visit probability) of all trials on each day. Session averages are mean values of all trials within each of the four sessions per day. To normalize per-trial values within each day, we converted values for each trial into a z score by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation of all trials of the day. The trial values could then be grouped by goal location or sessions. This within-day normalization still allows us to identify differences between goal locations or across sessions within the same day while accounting for changes between days.

Results

Spatial navigation tasks with distinct rules

To characterize neocortical beta dynamics during initial task acquisition, we chose spatial learning tasks because rats require minimal shaping or habituation for navigating space. This also complements our understanding of neocortical beta dynamics from operant conditioning tasks, which have been the task of choice in prior studies (; ,, ). We trained two groups of rats over 5 d, with four 10 min sessions per day separated by 45 min rests, on either a Y maze with a deterministic or a probabilistic reward rule. We recorded local field potentials simultaneously in dorsal CA1 of the hippocampus and prelimbic region (referred to as PFC). By comparing the two groups, we aimed to dissect which aspects of the dynamics could be attributed to general learning-related changes versus rule-specific differences.

Given that prior studies have shown strong correlates of frontal cortical beta with reward likelihood and outcome (; ; ; ; ; ; ), we designed our tasks to vary along these dimensions. The deterministic and probabilistic reward rules were chosen to dissociate the animal's certainty about the optimal goal visit pattern from the certainty of reward delivery for each goal visit. One group (n = 5) was trained on a continuous alternation task in which the animals learned to visit the three goals of the Y maze in an alternation sequence (C to L to C to R and repeat...; , left). In this deterministic reward task, each visit was rewarded with certainty if the animal learned the sequence of goal visits; thus, the maximum reward rate was 100% of all goal visits. The second group (n = 6) was trained on a binary multiarmed bandit task, where each of the three goals of the Y maze had a different probability of delivering reward (25, 50, and 75%; , right). In this task, the animals had to learn to visit, back and forth, the two goals with the highest probabilities to maximize reward (50% to 75% to 50% and repeat...). However, even if the animal followed this pattern, the outcome of each goal visit remained uncertain, and the maximum reward rate for the probability task was 62.5% of all goal visits.

Over the 5 d of training, animals in both groups learned their respective tasks. Across days of training, animals showed an increase in the proportion of expected visit patterns for each rule (). As expected, the two tasks had distinct reward yields. In the alternation task, 77 ± 3.5% of trials were rewarded in the last days of training, whereas in the probability task, 56 ± 1.8% of trials were rewarded (Wilcoxon rank-sum test p = 0.0062). Animals in both groups showed choices that maximized reward rate over days of training. In the alternation task, the animals visited the center arm of the Y maze at approximately twice the rate of the side arms (, left). For the probability task, the animals learned over days to visit the two goals with the highest reward probabilities (50 and 75%) while avoiding the goal with the lowest reward probability (25%; , right). In each task, the proportion of reward received at each goal differed, remaining stable across days for the alternation task, while converging toward higher proportions for the two high-likelihood goals in the probability task (). Over the days of training, the goal choices in both tasks became more closely matched to the proportion of reward received at each goal (). The animals became more certain about their choice of goals to visit in both tasks over time, despite inherent differences in certainty about the reward outcome of each goal visit between the tasks.

Goal-entry beta power increases with training in PFC but not CA1

We next examined beta oscillations when the animal reached the goal, given the importance of goal outcome processing in learning and the prevalence of both beta oscillations and SWRs during this period. We characterized beta dynamics in PFC, since less is known about goal-related beta dynamics in this region of the rodent frontal cortex despite its importance in reward learning across task domains (; ; ). Prior work on reward-related frontal cortical beta oscillations has primarily investigated the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC; ,, ) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC; ).

We previously found that beta oscillations occurred as animals visited goal locations in spatial navigation tasks (). Consistent with our previous findings, we saw increases in beta frequency power in PFC and CA1 LFP relative to goal entry. The increase in beta power is not explained by gross movement of the animal since the animal remains immobile after goal entry (, speed profile). However, we noticed a strong experience-related pattern for the PFC burst that occurred immediately after goal entry. On the first day of training, there was low PFC power in the beta frequency range within the first second after goal entry (). However, on Day 5 of training, we saw a clear burst of beta (). We quantified the increase in beta power after goal entry relative to a 500 ms window before goal entry. This normalization accounts for changes in the baseline beta power on each tetrode across sessions or days. The goal-entry PFC beta power increased over days (; Extended Data ) of training and with improved performance (), for both the alternation and probability tasks. CA1 beta power did not show a significant change across days (; Extended Data ) and was negatively correlated with performance for the alternation task but not the probability task ().

We performed control analyses to verify PFC beta could not be explained by volume conduction from the OB or harmonics from lower frequencies. Strong beta oscillations occur in the OB (; ), raising the possibility that electrodes in PFC, which are ∼4–5 mm posterior to the OB, could detect volume conducted beta from the OB. In simultaneous recordings from OB and PFC, we found beta bursts can occur independently in each region (Extended Data ), and beta power was correlated within each region but not between regions (Extended Data ). Furthermore, we quantified the power on PFC tetrodes based on their location along the anterior–posterior axis, with the anterior-most tetrodes being closest to the OB. If PFC beta is volume conducted from OB, we would find a consistent decrease in power toward the posterior PFC electrodes. We found beta power was not correlated with distance along the anterior–posterior axis (Extended Data ). We next excluded the possibility of theta harmonics contributing to PFC and CA1 goal-entry beta power. Beta oscillations span the 15–30 Hz range, and bandpass filtering the LFP signal could include the first harmonic of theta oscillations (∼7–8 Hz). To ensure our beta quantification was not detecting the theta harmonic, we computed the power spectral density for periods when the animal was moving on the maze or immobile at the goal. As expected, we found movement periods showed higher power in the theta frequency range whereas the immobile periods had lower power (Extended Data , on maze or at goal). Importantly, LFP from goal periods in PFC showed a prominent peak at ∼20 Hz without a peak at the frequency for the harmonic of theta (∼14–16 Hz; Extended Data , 10–30 Hz panels).

Complementary training-dependent changes in PFC and CA1

Hippocampal SWRs occur when animals visit goal locations and are more frequent during early stages of learning or when exposed to a new environment or task (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). Consistent with previous findings, we found that CA1 SWR rate () and size () decreased over days of training on both tasks, and CA1 SWR rate and size were comparable between the tasks (one-way ANOVA: SWR rate F(1,50) = 0.398, p = 0.531; SWR size F(1,50) = 0.400, p = 0.528). This decrease in CA1 SWR rate and size stands in contrast to the increase in PFC goal-entry beta power (). We confirmed this inverse relationship by correlating CA1 SWR rate () and size () with goal-entry PFC beta power for each day, which showed negative correlations for both the alternation and probability tasks. This relationship was unique to PFC, as we did not find a negative correlation between CA1 goal-entry beta power and SWR properties (). Despite differences in the reward scheme between the two tasks, the inverse correlation between PFC beta power and CA1 SWR properties was found in both tasks, which suggests these markers correspond to general, but complementary, experience-dependent changes in hippocampal–neocortical networks across learning.

The across-day changes in PFC beta power and CA1 SWR properties could reflect familiarity or experience-dependent adaptations on the timescale of days. Alternatively, changes in beta and SWR properties could be related to performance improvements observable within each day and across days. To dissociate these possibilities, we examined the relationship between PFC beta power or CA1 SWR properties and performance across the sessions on each day. Task performance and PFC beta power increased across sessions on some days but not others (). When analyzed within each day, beta power was significantly correlated with performance for both tasks (). However, within-day SWR rate and size were not correlated with performance (). These results suggest beta power reflects learning-related changes over sessions and days, whereas SWR properties reflect experience-dependent changes across days.

We next determined the relationship between PFC beta and SWR properties within each day. One hypothesis is that the inverse relationship between PFC beta and hippocampal SWR properties across days () could reflect gradual changes in each network across learning. Alternatively, the power of PFC beta may actively regulate the occurrence of hippocampal SWRs or vice versa, which would lead to the inverse relationship across sessions within the same day. We can test these hypotheses by removing the across-day effects we found earlier (). If the inverse relationship remains after accounting for across-day changes, then PFC beta and SWRs may be regulated on a faster timescale as well as over days. We therefore performed the correlation across sessions on a given day before (Extended Data ) and after (Extended Data ) normalizing beta power and CA1 SWR property values within the day. This within-day normalization eliminated the negative correlation between PFC beta power and CA1 SWR properties (Extended Data , compare A, B and E, F), which is consistent with our within-day analysis showing SWR properties were not related to performance within each day. Overall, PFC goal-entry beta and CA1 SWRs are likely independent markers for gradual experience-dependent changes on the timescale of sessions and days.

PFC goal-entry beta power does not signal outcome at the goal

In addition to identifying experience-dependent changes in mean beta power, we asked whether goal-entry beta in PFC and CA1 was correlated with other task variables. In the frontal cortex, reward period beta power correlates with reward outcome (; ; ; ; ), which supports the hypothesis that beta frequency activity is a network signature for reward outcome processing. However, it is unknown whether rat PFC beta power reflects reward outcome as rat PFC spiking shows weaker reward outcome correlates compared with OFC (; ). We therefore compared beta burst power between rewarded and unrewarded goal visits. In our experiment, the reward was triggered by the goal-entry event to immediately reveal the outcome for that goal visit. If PFC beta power was correlated with goal outcome, we would expect to find a difference in goal-entry PFC beta power between rewarded and unrewarded goal visits. Comparing the two tasks allows us to separate the two potentially confounded task variables: outcome and choice. In the alternation task, the outcome is deterministic: a correct choice to visit a goal guarantees a reward, so the outcome and the choice are linked and cannot be distinguished. In the probability task, outcome is stochastic: a correct choice to visit the highest-probability goal does not guarantee reward, so the outcome is dissociated from the choice. The probability task can test whether goal-entry beta power tracks outcome. In PFC and CA1, goal-entry beta power was similar irrespective of goal visit outcome. Although PFC beta on rewarded trials was significantly higher than on unrewarded trials when all trials were analyzed each day (, right panel), controlling for goal location identity eliminated this difference (Extended Data , right panels). This result is likely explained by differences between goals rather than outcome, which we examine in the next section. Thus, PFC beta after goal entry is unlikely to reflect the outcome at the goal.

We further found that goal-entry beta power did not correlate with the history of goal visit outcomes. We compared beta power on goal visits based on the history of visits, whether previous visits were rewarded or not. We ensured the values were normalized to the power distribution for each goal. We did not find strong relationships between beta power in either PFC or CA1 and subsequent visits after rewarded or unrewarded visits, including after consecutive rewarded or unrewarded visits (Extended Data ). Thus, goal-entry beta power in both CA1 and PFC is unlikely to reflect the history of goal visit outcomes.

PFC and CA1 goal-entry beta correspond to distinct task variables

Rat orbitofrontal cortical beta power is correlated with the probability of reward for a given choice (). We asked how PFC or CA1 goal-entry beta power relates to reward probability, which would indicate the involvement of this network state in reward processing.

To characterize the relationship between goal-entry beta power and reward probability, we correlated the mean beta power at each goal location with the empirical reward probability based on the animal's behavior, defined as the proportion of rewarded visits to the goal location. In both of our tasks, the proportion of rewarded visits at each goal differed based on the animal's experience. We found PFC beta power was correlated with reward likelihood (), whereas CA1 beta power was not correlated (). This pattern was consistent for both tasks, despite differences in task rules. This result explains why, in the probability task, we found rewarded trials had higher beta power than unrewarded trials when all trials were combined but not when separated by location (, right; Extended Data , right): the higher-probability goals had more rewarded trials, which increased the average power value; this is controlled for with goal-specific comparisons.

Interestingly, goal-entry PFC beta power was positively correlated with the proportion of visits to the goal but only for the probability task, such that more frequently visited goal locations showed higher beta power (). In contrast, goal-entry CA1 beta power was not correlated with goal visit proportion (). Our results suggest PFC goal-entry beta could reflect an estimate of a goal's reward likelihood () rather than the outcome of that visit ().

Discussion

Using spatial navigation tasks with distinct reward rules, we found complementary learning-related changes in the properties of PFC beta oscillations and CA1 SWRs at goal locations. Consistent with prior findings in spatial tasks, the rate of CA1 SWRs decreased over days as the animals learned. In contrast, we found that bursts of beta in PFC occurring after the animals reached the goal showed an increase in power over days as the animals learned. We propose that the inverse relationship between these network signatures reflects gradual complementary changes in hippocampal and neocortical networks over the course of learning.

The inverse pattern sheds light on the complementary contribution of the hippocampus and neocortex to learning. Although several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the function of hippocampal SWRs, one idea is that an increase in the occurrence of SWRs reflects a response to new information that is worth capturing (), which is more prevalent at the start of learning when there is novelty. This increase in SWRs is thought to initiate reorganization in connected brain areas, including the neocortex, to incorporate new information into existing networks. As familiarity increases during learning, the amount of new information decreases, and we see a corresponding decrease in the rate of SWRs. In contrast to hippocampal SWRs, our results suggest beta power may signal complementary changes taking place in the neocortex during learning. During initial learning when the environment is novel, we did not observe strong goal-entry beta, but instead, beta power increased as familiarity increased. This inverse pattern mirrors what we saw with hippocampal SWRs. Low beta power may indicate an active learning state that favors the addition of new information, whereas high beta power may correspond to a postlearning state in which the availability of information has plateaued and neocortical networks have established stability.

We found that goal location beta in CA1 and PFC showed distinct correlations with task variables. In CA1, we saw beta power at goal entry was uncorrelated with reward probability for both tasks. By comparing results from a deterministic and a probabilistic reward task, we showed that PFC goal-entry beta power was correlated with reward probability and not reward outcome at each goal. This pattern shares similarity with beta dynamics in frontal cortical regions but also points to distinctions. Goal-related beta oscillations in other frontal cortical regions, such as the OFC (; ) or ACC (), show reward probability correlates. Notably, we found PFC beta dynamics did not correlate with the outcome at the goal. Our results provide support for regional specialization within frontal cortical regions (; ; ). Goal location spiking in PFC has stronger correlates with choice, whereas OFC spiking has stronger correlates with the reward outcome (; ; ). Furthermore, inactivation of PFC and OFC has distinct effects on reversal learning (; ; ). Although we have described differences in goal location beta dynamics between PFC and CA1 (), future research is needed to determine the contribution of the goal-entry beta burst in PFC to goal-directed behaviors. Our finding that similar PFC goal-entry beta burst power occurs for rewarded and unrewarded outcomes argues for a role in expectation of the outcome rather than outcome evaluation.

Our work shows experience-dependent changes in beta dynamics in PFC; these patterns are consistent with the acquisition of task rules. These changes are complementary to SWRs in the hippocampus, pointing to coordinated changes in hippocampal–cortical networks in response to new information that warrants reorganization of existing networks (). The function of beta oscillations in cognition is less well understood than that of other neural oscillations (; ; ; ; ). Our work raises questions about the mechanism that coordinates these complementary network changes during learning. One hypothesis is that a common novelty-driven signal may downregulate PFC beta oscillations while increasing the likelihood of hippocampal SWRs, especially during initial task acquisition. As the animal learns, the novelty signal will likely decrease, which results in stronger PFC beta oscillations and less frequent hippocampal SWRs. Alternatively, the neocortex and hippocampus may have intrinsic mechanisms for regulating learning-related changes.

Our results provide evidence that beta dynamics in hippocampal–neocortical networks at goals are distinct from known beta-modulated states in these networks. Prior work examining beta dynamics in the neocortex and hippocampus focused on cue sampling during olfactory discrimination tasks (; ; ; ). During this period, there is strong coherence within the network at the beta frequency. Our results show goal-entry PFC beta is more prominent than in CA1 and has task correlates distinct from those in CA1. Thus, the goal-related beta dynamics point to a hippocampal–neocortical state that is mechanistically distinct from cue sampling. These networks are operating to subserve distinct functions for cue sampling and goal attainment.

Drawing on findings from motor control and sensory processing, our work suggests that neocortical beta may signal a stable postlearning state. In the motor cortex, beta oscillations have been associated with the state of inaction or status quo (; ). Similarly, for olfactory processing, beta power during odor sampling increases with experience (; ; ; ; ) and the transfer of learned rules (). Extrapolating from these concepts, we suggest that learning-dependent changes in neocortical beta oscillations signal the development of a new, postlearning status quo. Taken together, these findings provide evidence for beta oscillations being important signatures for experience-dependent changes in neocortical networks.

Synthesis

The present study reports the observation of beta oscillations in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) during reward periods. Based on electrophysiological recordings performed across different tasks, the study provides important evidence linking prefrontal beta oscillations to reward processing and learning in rodents, and how they relate to hippocampal sharp-wave ripples. The reviewers found the work interesting and potentially suitable for publication at eNeuro. However, they raised several concerns that must be addressed to warrant publication of the work.

1) While beta oscillations have been extensively studied in PFC in humans and non-human primates, most prior rodent studies have examined beta during olfactory tasks, often showing coherent beta activity between CA1 and PFC (e.g., Jayachandran et al., 2023; Allen et al., 2016; Igarashi et al., 2014; Symanski et al., 2022). In contrast, the current study reports robust PFC beta during reward delivery, which appears largely absent in CA1 (Figs. 1A-B, 5A-B) and correlates with reward-port visits and experience. This suggests that the observed PFC beta may be functionally and mechanistically distinct from previously described odor- or sniffing-related beta. A discussion explicitly addressing this distinction would clarify the novelty and broader significance of the findings.

2) Along these lines, it should be made clear whether the observed beta oscillations are locally generated (or at least are locally relevant). Specifically, given the proximity of the olfactory bulb (OB), which generates large beta field potentials (e.g. Kay et al. 2010) it is possible these reach the mPFC through volume conduction? Showing spike-field relationships, and/or a spatial profile of beta power as a function of distance to the OB could help address this important issue.

3) In Figs. 2, S1, 5, and S3, beta-band activity is quantified using raw power (μV2), whereas Figs. 6 and S4 use z-scored power normalized by daily mean and standard deviation. The rationale for switching between these approaches is not explained and should be clarified.

More importantly, comparing raw LFP power across days is problematic, as absolute power can vary due to electrode drift or impedance changes. Without within-day normalization, it is difficult to exclude the possibility that the observed increase in PFC beta reflects recording variability rather than physiological modulation.

This concern is particularly relevant for the reported enhancement in the 350-850 ms window following goal entry. Normalizing beta power within this window to a pre-goal baseline period within the same session is recommended. Reanalyzing key effects using a consistent within-day normalization procedure would substantially strengthen the conclusions.

4) In Fig. S1, beyond magnitude changes, beta enhancement appears to begin earlier across days in both tasks. Although not quantified, the time-frequency plots suggest a progressive shift in beta onset relative to goal entry. If accurate, this would be an important finding, potentially reflecting learning-related changes in anticipatory or preparatory dynamics rather than amplitude modulation alone. First, we quantified beta power for each tetrode and plotted the power based on the relative position on anterior-posterior (AP) axis, as proposed by the reviewer.

Tetrodes in our recording drives are individually placed in polyimide tubes (210μm diameter) that are then bundled using a mold with a length of approximately 2.3mm (Fig. 2-2 C). We used the spatial separation between the tetrodes along the AP axis to determine whether there is a distance-dependent decrease in beta power. We assigned 0 to the anterior most tetrode and 1 to the posterior most tetrode. If there is volume conduction, we expect beta power to drop along the AP axis. We found the power remained constant. The results are reported in Fig. 2-2 D-E.

Second, we did not have high-quality single units from this dataset to perform spike-field analysis as proposed by the reviewer; instead we implanted a male rat with two electrodes in the OB and two electrodes in PFC to determine if there was volume conduction between these regions. 2-2A) and the beta power is most strongly correlated within each region (Fig. 2-2B).
