Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

Hydrogen (H₂) is gaining attention as a versatile energy carrier with potential applications across
industrial processes, power generation, and transportation. However, its practical deployment,
particularly in large-scale distribution systems, faces significant infrastructure challenges.
Transporting hydrogen through dedicated pipelines or blending it into existing natural gas
networks can lead to serious issues, such as leakage due to the small size of hydrogen molecules
and material degradation in pipelines through embrittlement. These technical risks raise safety
concerns and could limit the integration of hydrogen into current energy infrastructures.
Additionally, using hydrogen-enriched gas mixtures in combustion systems like turbines and
engines introduces new performance and compatibility challenges that must be resolved before
widespread use becomes feasible.

Reliable hydrogen detection is a critical challenge that must be addressed for hydrogen
technologies to reach industrial-scale targets. This project introduces a novel computational
framework that combines the power of high-performance computing (HPC) and artificial
intelligence (AI) tools to accelerate the discovery of materials suitable for hydrogen sensing. Our
focus is on sensors based on metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) targeting the detection of
hydrogen leaks at extremely low concentrations, therefore capable of timely monitoring H 2
concentration in pipeline systems.

The proposed approach relies on a robust HPC workflow designed for scalability and efficiency.
Thousands of MOF candidates are initially screened using cost-effective, semi-empirical methods,
selecting structures that align with known experimental data and basic structural parameters.
From this reduced set, materials are further evaluated based on geometric features and
adsorption characteristics to identify those most likely to exhibit favorable hydrogen sensing
behavior. These promising candidates are then refined using high-accuracy ab initio simulations
with meta-GGA density functional theory (DFT). The resulting structural and dynamical data are
used to fine-tune machine-learned interatomic potentials (ML-IPs), which replicate the accuracy
of quantum mechanical calculations at a much lower computational cost. These ML-IPs enable
the rapid exploration and inverse design of new MOF structures tailored to specific performance
targets.

This flexible and extensible computational framework acts as a black-box materials discovery
engine, capable of screening, predicting, and designing advanced materials with minimal manual
intervention. While developed for hydrogen sensing, the approach can be readily adapted to
other materials challenges, offering a powerful tool for data-driven materials innovation.

Summary of Research Results:

The accurate modeling of gas behavior in MOFs is limited by the lack of reliable interatomic
potentials and the high computational cost associated with running ab initio molecular dynamics
simulations on MOF supercells. As a result, most computational studies have been restricted to
high-throughput screening or machine learning model development for identifying MOFs with
favorable gas adsorption behavior. However, most MOFs possess complex structures with
multiple adsorption sites that significantly influence gas interactions, adsorption, and dynamics

within the framework. Moreover, these gases are typically present as mixtures, commonly with
N₂, O₂, or water, which further complicates gas capture and storage in MOFs. Therefore, it is
important to understand the long-timescale behavior of gases such as H₂ in MOFs, including their
interactions and traversal through these complex structures. This can be achieved through Monte
Carlo (MC) or molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. For instance, we employed MC-based
Widom insertion methods to compute ensemble-averaged adsorption energy, heat of
adsorption, and Henry’s coefficient, and used multiscale MD simulations to study the diffusion
and intermolecular interactions of H₂ in MOFs (Fig 1). This was made possible by our efforts in
generating a sufficiently large dataset to fine-tune pre-train ML-IP foundational models. These
models make it feasible to run MD simulations with near-quantum accuracy, something that
would be intractable with ab initio MD. Our results show that H₂ diffuses an order of magnitude
faster than water in typical MOFs such as Cu-squarate, and that H₂ interacts more strongly with
carbon and oxygen atoms than with copper or other oxygens, primarily because the latter are
buried deeper in the structure. Overall, this project has opened new avenues for the successful
modeling of MOF-gas systems, and we anticipate that our methodology will gain increasing
attention as more papers emerge from simulations using our developed ML models.

Figure 1. (A) Schematic illustration of the Cu(Squarate) MOF supercell with H₂, used to study both microscopic and
macroscopic properties of H₂ within the MOF. (B) Self-diffusion coefficient of H₂ in the MOF, obtained using machine
learning-based molecular dynamics simulations. (C) Radial distribution functions and coordination numbers of H₂
with various atoms in the MOF.

The computational workflow we implemented provided a systematic path to filter, refine, and
generate high-quality structural data for hydrogen absorption in MOFs. Applying this
computational workflow (outlined in the implementation section), we systematically refined the
initial set of ~1,000 MOF structures to identify configurations relevant for hydrogen absorption.
The workflow, combining rapid structural optimization, experimental validation, and targeted
hydrogen placement, resulted in ~200 MOF-H₂ configurations with favorable adsorption
energies. These selected structures were then employed to generate high-level B97M-V
molecular dynamics simulations at multiple temperatures (300 K, 325 K, and 350 K), capturing
both equilibrium geometries and thermal fluctuations relevant to practical adsorption
conditions.

The resulting dataset comprises approximately 38,000 structural frames, which were partitioned
into ~30,000 for training, ~4,000 for validation, and ~4,000 for testing, including both in-
distribution and out-of-distribution. This workflow not only ensured that the dataset is physically

representative and meaningful but also maximized computational efficiency by focusing high-
level simulations on the most relevant structures. Overall, the approach demonstrates a scalable
and automated strategy for generating high-quality structural data for fine-tuning machine
learning interatomic potentials.

Training data was then used to fine-tune the MACE-MP-0 foundation model. The use of a pre-
trained model has been shown to reduce data requirements for ML-IP training. Specifically, we
used various MACE-derived architectures including "MACE-DAC", "MACE-MPA-0", and "MACE-
MP-0b3" as starting points for fine-tuning. Our fine-tuning used a reduced learning rate of
0.0001, 20 epochs, and atom energies computed from a linear fit. Energy/force coefficients on
the MLIP loss function were 1:10, respectively.

Baker Hughes worked with computational chemists at Lawrence Berkeley National Lab (LBNL) to
assess a library of MOFs for their interactions with H 2 molecules. Nearly 15,000 MOFs were
initially studied, with this being reduced to a library of ca. 200 MOF structures with high potential
for H2 adsorption at low partial pressures. This list contained several well-known MOF variants,
including MFM-300, MOF-303, and BUT-66. Additionally, some of the structures identified
included benchmark H2 storage materials, including UMCM-152, NU-200 and PCN-200. Finally,
this list also included a number of MOFs which Baker Hughes is currently commercializing for H 2
related applications. These included Ni-MOF-74, Fe-MOF-74, and Cu-MOF-74. Due to commercial
availability, Ni-MOF-74 was chosen for development for sensing related applications.

Baker Hughes fabricated and tested the Ni-MOF-74 based sensors for H 2 sensing applications.
Two sensing methodologies were deployed during testing: QCM and impedance-based sensors.
We successfully deposited MOF thin films on each sensor and tested under a H 2 environment
using a custom-built test setup. During impedance testing, the sensor display negligible response
under H2 atmosphere. The natural insulator properties of the MOF, combined with low H2 uptake
can be attributed to the limited response observed. The vast majority of the observed response
can be attributed to environmental fluctuations during testing. During QCM testing, significant
initial response was observed upon exposure to H2, however, this response slowly tapered off as
the test cell returned to equilibrium conditions. Due to the test setup, it was difficult to determine
how much of the response was related to H2 adsorption, and how much was related to
fluctuations in the test chamber atmosphere. For future testing, a dedicated flow cell will be
utilized in testing the exposed sensors to consistent conditions with low variability. Overall, while
the MOF materials display good affinity for H2 under isothermal conditions, this performance was
not found to translate to a strong response under sensor testing conditions.

The fine-tuning approach used in this project was sufficient for exploratory simulations, but
could be improved. Specifically, force errors with respect to our out-of-distribution test set
remained higher than is desired. This could have been due to the fact that MACE models tested
are somewhat old and were only pre-trained on solid-state materials systems. Fine-tuning may
work better with the released Universal Model of Atoms, which is pre-trained on a far larger
and more diverse set of data, including data at multiple different levels of theory. While MACE
models were previously successfully fine-tuned for MOFs, we are using a higher level of theory

here, and H2 adsorption may be a particularly challenging context for fine-tuning given the
small forces involved. The combination of the small forces and our different level of theory may
also require changes to the fine-tuning procedure or hyperparameters for optimal
performance.

Beyond better fine-tuning, this project revealed the need for more robust benchmarking of
simulations against experimental MOF data. The clear target for this would be comparing
simulated and experimental isotherms. We now have an effort underway involving a
collaboration with a group that has developed a GPU-accelerated grand canonical Monte Carlo
code which is ideally suited for MLIP-based simulation of isotherms, and we will be benchmarking
multiple different pre-trained MLIPs and potentially also the fine-tuned MLIP from this effort
against experimental isotherms covering a variety of different MOF-adsorbate configurations. A
better understanding of the predictive power of our models will allow us to more confidently
inform on a MOF’s ability to function effectively under sensor conditions in future collaborations.
Finally, the computational approach that we developed in this project is not limited to H2 or even
to MOFs, but could be applied to any application space involving the uptake of gas by a material
via a physisorption mechanism. Further, the approach will become even faster and more effective
as better pre-trained models are released and diverse fine-tuning procedures are established.
