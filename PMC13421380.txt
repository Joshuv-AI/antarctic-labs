Canonical Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl complexes as confocal-compatible stains for lignified secondary-wall domains in Arabidopsis thaliana stems
Authors: Ignacio Fuentes, Juan A. Fuentes, Alexander Carreño, Allan Cortés, Pablo Sepúlveda-Orellana, Susana Saez-Aguayo, Rubén Polanco, Evys Ancede-Gallardo, Ana Gabriel Suárez, Carolina Otero, Luis Leyva-Parra, Angel A. Martí, Adrián A. Moreno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluorescent stains for lignified plant walls must operate in chemically heterogeneous, autofluorescent matrices while remaining compatible with confocal multiplexing. Here, we evaluated two canonical Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl luminophores, Ru1 ([Ru(deeb)3]2+) and Ru2 ([Ru(phen)3]2+), as non-derivatizing stains for fixed Arabidopsis thaliana stem sections. In situ spectral profiling defined practical 405-nm confocal detection windows, and both probes produced reproducible wall-associated photoluminescence enriched in secondary-wall-rich vascular domains, especially xylem vessels and interfascicular fibers. Their anatomical distribution showed qualitative concordance with Wiesner/Mäule lignin histochemistry and condition-validated Safranin O maps, supporting their use as spatial reporters of matrix-associated enrichment within anatomically defined lignified secondary-wall territories. The molecular determinants of this enrichment, including the relative contribution of lignin and other wall polymers, remain to be resolved. Sequential co-staining with Calcofluor White separated broad β-glucan-rich wall architecture from Ru-enriched secondary-wall domains, while spectral-overlap analysis identified far-red Alexa Fluor 647 excitation at 638 nm as the most orthogonal tested third-label configuration. Ligand-comparative DFT descriptors provided structure–property fingerprints summarizing differences in π-surface continuity and electrostatic anisotropy. Overall, these results position canonical Ru(ii) polypyridyl luminophores as confocal-compatible, chemically tractable scaffolds for anatomical imaging of lignified plant-wall territories.

Introduction

Plant cell walls are chemically heterogeneous biopolymer matrices whose mechanical, hydraulic, and defensive functions emerge from the spatial organization of cellulose, hemicelluloses, pectins, lignin, and associated wall components. Primary walls are generally enriched in cellulose, hemicelluloses, and pectins, whereas specialized cell types reinforce their walls through the deposition of lignin and the formation of thick, patterned secondary walls that support long-distance water transport and mechanical strength, particularly in xylem vessels and fibers.1–4 Lignification is therefore not simply a binary trait but a spatially patterned developmental and physiological process whose subcellular and tissue-level distribution provides important information on plant development, biomechanics, stress responses, and biomass properties.1,2,5

Because lignified secondary-wall domains are central to plant structure and function, there is a sustained need for imaging strategies that can delineate these domains rapidly, reproducibly, and with sufficient compatibility for modern fluorescence workflows. Current approaches to lignified-wall imaging span several complementary levels. Label-free strategies exploit the intrinsic autofluorescence of lignin and related phenolic polymers, including intensity-based fluorescence imaging and fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy, while vibrational approaches such as Raman, CARS, and SRS microscopy provide chemically informative maps of lignin and other wall polymers.6–10 These methods offer important chemical or label-free information, but they generally require specialized acquisition platforms, spectral interpretation, or advanced instrumentation.9,10 A second set of approaches relies on exogenous fluorescent stains and chemically designed probes.11,12 Widely used wall stains, including Basic Fuchsin, Safranin O, Auramine O, Calcofluor White, Congo Red, Direct Red, Direct Yellow, Fluorol Yellow, and Nile Red, have enabled practical visualization of lignified, suberized, lipid-rich, and polysaccharide-rich wall domains across diverse plant tissues.11–16 In parallel, fluorescence-tagged monolignols and click-compatible monolignol analogues have provided more chemically interpretable routes to visualize lignification dynamics and monolignol incorporation in living or metabolically active systems.17–21 Together, these advances have substantially expanded the toolkit for plant wall imaging, but they also illustrate a persistent trade-off between chemical interpretability, workflow simplicity, sample preparation requirements, spectral compatibility, and broad usability in fixed histological sections.

In this context, classical histochemical and fluorescent benchmarks remain valuable, provided that their interpretive limits are made explicit. The Wiesner/phloroglucinol–HCl reaction preferentially reports cinnamaldehyde end groups rather than total lignin, whereas Mäule-based staining is commonly interpreted as an indicator of syringyl-enriched lignin features rather than a direct quantitative map of all lignin substructures.3,22–24 Safranin O is a powerful and widely used fluorescent reporter of lignified secondary walls, including in ratiometric confocal workflows, but its signal intensity, wall-resolved contrast, and background are shaped by wall chemistry, dye concentration, washing, section thickness, imaging depth, embedding medium, and acquisition settings.11,14,15,25 Thus, in the present study, these methods serve as condition-validated anatomical benchmarks rather than as absolute chemical standards.

This landscape creates a methodological opportunity for drop-in photoluminescent stains that can be applied directly to fixed sections, operate under standard confocal configurations, and provide empirically controlled multichannel compatibility in autofluorescent plant matrices. From a dye-chemistry perspective, Ru(ii) polypyridyl complexes are attractive scaffolds because ligand identity can tune sterics, electrostatics, redox properties, absorption, emission, and excited-state behavior while preserving a robust octahedral Ru(ii) tris-diimine core.26–31 Their broad metal-to-ligand charge-transfer absorption bands, large Stokes shifts, photostability, visible emission, and long-lived triplet MLCT excited states have supported extensive development as luminophores for biological imaging and sensing.26–29,32,33

Ru(ii) polypyridyl complexes have therefore been applied previously as biological luminophores, including classical [Ru(phen)3]2+ and [Ru(bpy)3]2+ probes evaluated in animal and plant cells as oxygen-sensitive imaging agents.34 Related Ru(ii) complexes have also shown ligand-dependent fluorescent staining patterns in walled-cell models such as yeasts.35 However, these precedents address cellular imaging, oxygen sensing, or general probe localization, rather than the use of canonical Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl luminophores as rapid, non-derivatizing stains for lignified secondary-wall territories in fixed plant tissue sections. The specific gap addressed here is therefore not whether Ru(ii) polypyridyl complexes can emit in biological systems, but whether two established members of this family can reproducibly report lignified plant-wall territories under defined histological, confocal, and multiplexing conditions.

A chemically plausible rationale for testing cationic Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl luminophores in lignified walls is that lignified secondary-wall domains present aromatic surfaces, heterogeneous oxygenated functionalities, and composite polymer architectures that may favor enrichment of cationic aromatic probes through dispersive, π-associated, electrostatic, hydrogen-bonding, and possibly cation-π contributions.36–39 In such a chemically heterogeneous matrix, probe enrichment should initially be interpreted as matrix-associated partitioning or anatomical reporting, not as direct evidence of specificity for individual lignin substructures. Accordingly, the relevant readout in this study is the reproducible spatial enrichment of Ru photoluminescence in wall domains independently recognized as lignified secondary-wall territories, while the molecular determinants of this enrichment remain to be resolved.

Here, we evaluate two archetypal Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl luminophores, Ru1 = [Ru(deeb)3]2+ and Ru2 = [Ru(phen)3]2+, as a minimal ligand-contrast pair for non-derivatizing photoluminescent staining of lignified secondary-wall regions in fixed Arabidopsis thaliana stem sections. The two complexes share the same general dicationic Ru(ii) tris-diimine architecture, but differ in ligand periphery: Ru1 contains ester-substituted 2,2′-bipyridine ligands that introduce a more polar and electrostatically heterogeneous surface, whereas Ru2 contains the more rigid, fused aromatic 1,10-phenanthroline scaffold, which provides a more contiguous π-surface. We therefore asked whether these ligand-encoded differences would influence wall-associated enrichment, spectral behavior, or compatibility with lignified-wall imaging workflows. We define practical confocal excitation and detection conditions, benchmark the resulting staining patterns against established lignin-associated anatomical readouts, evaluate compatibility with Calcofluor White and representative red/far-red fluorophores, and use ligand-comparative computational descriptors to summarize intrinsic electronic differences between the two probes. We frame the Ru-associated signal as a qualitative spatial readout of matrix-associated enrichment in anatomically defined lignified secondary-wall territories, rather than as proof of lignin-substructure specificity or direct lignin binding. Accordingly, this work is positioned as an application-specific methodological evaluation of established Ru(ii) luminophores in a lignified plant-wall context.

Results

Synthesis and verification of Ru1 and Ru2

The homoleptic Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl complexes Ru1, [Ru(deeb)3]2+, and Ru2, [Ru(phen)3]2+, were prepared as PF6− salts using established tris-chelation procedures from RuCl3·H2O and the corresponding N^N ligands. The isolated orange/red-orange solids were characterized by ATR-FTIR and 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S1–S4), confirming the expected ligand coordination patterns and the absence of major detectable starting-ligand impurities under the analytical conditions used. The spectroscopic features were consistent with reported data for related [Ru(deeb)3]2+ and [Ru(phen)3]2+ complexes.40–42

Ru1 and Ru2 (Fig. 1) were selected not as new chemical entities, but as a deliberately simple ligand-contrast pair within a common Ru(ii) tris-diimine scaffold. This design keeps several major variables constant, including formal charge, octahedral tris-chelation, metal center, and general MLCT-based photoluminescence, while varying the ligand surface presented to the wall matrix. Ru1 contains the ester-substituted 2,2′-bipyridine ligand deeb, in which electron-withdrawing ethoxycarbonyl groups introduce peripheral oxygenated functionality, increased electrostatic heterogeneity, and potential hydrogen-bond accepting sites. Ru2 contains the rigid fused aromatic ligand 1,10-phenanthroline, which provides a more compact and contiguous π-surface. Because lignified secondary walls are chemically heterogeneous matrices containing aromatic lignin domains, oxygenated functionalities, and polysaccharide-rich components,2,4,36 we reasoned that these ligand-level differences could influence probe enrichment, local photophysical response, or wall-resolved contrast. Thus, the comparison was designed to test whether lignified wall staining is dominated by the shared cationic Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl architecture or whether it is detectably modulated by ligand-encoded differences in polarity, π-surface continuity, and electrostatic anisotropy.

Structures of the Ru(ii) polypyridyl luminophores evaluated in this study. Ru1 = [Ru(deeb)3]2+, where deeb = 4,4′-di(ethoxycarbonyl)-2,2′-bipyridine. Ru2 = [Ru(phen)3]2+, where phen = 1,10-phenanthroline. Both compounds are octahedral Ru(ii) tris-diimine complexes composed of three bidentate N^N ligands coordinated to a Ru2+ center.

In situ spectral windowing and staining standardization

To establish robust confocal imaging conditions for Ru1 and Ru2 in plant tissue sections, we first defined practical excitation and emission windows and then standardized a short staining workflow compatible with wall imaging. Because plant tissues are intrinsically autofluorescent and wall-associated dyes can exhibit matrix-dependent spectral behavior, solution spectra alone are insufficient to define reliable acquisition settings. We therefore combined solution fluorometry with in situ confocal λ-scans of stained Arabidopsis thaliana stem cross-sections to anchor detection windows to the probes' behavior within the tissue matrix. This strategy is consistent with recommended practice for quantitative multicolor confocal imaging, where single-label controls, reference spectra, sequential acquisition, and, when necessary, spectral imaging or linear unmixing are required to minimize cross-talk and misassignment of fluorescence signals.43–46

Solution fluorometry showed that both Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl complexes were compatible with 405-nm excitation and produced broad visible emission in the yellow-to-red region, consistent with the general photophysical behavior of Ru(ii) polypyridyl luminophores.26–29 Under these conditions, Ru1 and Ru2 displayed partially distinct emission profiles (Fig. 2A and B). To determine whether these differences could be translated into practical confocal acquisition settings, we performed λ-scans directly on Ru-stained stem sections. The in situ spectra confirmed red-shifted wall-associated emission and supported the use of spectrally separated detection windows for subsequent imaging: 600–660 nm for Ru1 and 550–600 nm for Ru2 (Fig. 2C). These windows were selected to preserve probe signal while reducing emission overlap and while limiting the collection of shorter-wavelength plant autofluorescence.

We next evaluated whether the staining pattern was robust to common aqueous diluents used in plant imaging. Ru1 was used as the primary probe for this buffer survey, given the similar staining workflow and related photophysical behavior of both complexes, and Ru2 was subsequently evaluated under the selected conditions, but Ru2 was not tested across all of them. Because Ru(ii) polypyridyl luminescence can be influenced by microenvironmental factors, including polarity and quenching effects,26–29 this experiment was interpreted as a qualitative robustness screen rather than as a quantitative comparison of emission efficiency. Across water, TBS, PBS, and HEPES, Ru1 produced comparable wall-associated fluorescence patterns in basal stem sections (Fig. S9), indicating that the staining response was not strongly dependent on buffer composition under the conditions tested. Based on these results and on compatibility with downstream multichannel imaging, we selected 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 40 µM probe concentration, and 10 min incubation followed by gentle buffer rinses as the standard staining protocol for both Ru1 and Ru2.

Under these standardized conditions, Ru1 and Ru2 produced spatially restricted photoluminescence associated with cell-wall domains across the section. This observation established a reproducible optical and staining framework for comparing the two probes in situ and for interpreting the Ru-associated signal in a stepwise manner. At this stage, Ru photoluminescence was treated operationally as a spatial staining pattern whose biological interpretation was subsequently anchored to independent lignin-associated anatomical benchmarks. Defining the detection windows also constrained the multiplex imaging space explored later, particularly by identifying which fluorophores could be combined with Ru probes under 405-nm excitation and which configurations would require sequential acquisition, spectral imaging, or linear unmixing to avoid artifactual co-localization.43–46 The 405-nm line was selected not as a universally optimal excitation wavelength for Ru(ii) polypyridyl complexes, but as a practical confocal excitation condition that enabled Ru detection while preserving a multiplex-aware workflow. In particular, this choice supported Calcofluor/Ru dual imaging under a common violet excitation regime and avoided relying on 488- or 552-nm excitation for the Ru readout, conditions that more strongly engage common orange/red fluorophores such as Safranin O and Alexa Fluor 555.

Ru1 and Ru2 staining highlights lignified secondary-wall domains in Arabidopsis thaliana stems

To determine whether Ru-associated photoluminescence spatially coincides with lignified secondary-wall territories, we first compared Ru1 staining against classical lignin histochemistry in serial basal stem cross-sections from 6-week-old Arabidopsis thaliana. Adjacent sections were analyzed using the Wiesner/phloroglucinol–HCl and Mäule reactions. The Wiesner reaction is widely used as a lignin histochemical readout and primarily reports hydroxycinnamyl aldehyde structures, particularly coniferyl and sinapyl aldehyde end groups.22 The Mäule reaction is commonly used to reveal syringyl-enriched lignin domains by characteristic red color development.23,24 In parallel, no-dye controls were imaged under the same confocal settings to assess tissue autofluorescence and background signal under 405-nm excitation.44,45

In the imaged cross-sections, Ru1 fluorescence was strongly enriched in the vascular ring, outlining secondary-wall-rich tissues, with the highest signal associated with xylem vessels and interfascicular fiber regions and comparatively weak labeling in the largely parenchymatous cortex and pith (Fig. 3A and B). This tissue-level distribution is consistent with the established localization of lignified secondary walls in Arabidopsis stem tracheary elements, xylem fibers, and interfascicular fibers.47–50Ru1 fluorescence qualitatively matched the Wiesner-positive domains and aligned with Mäule-positive territories within secondary xylem and interfascicular fibers (Fig. 3C and D). Thus, Ru1 staining showed qualitative anatomical concordance with independent lignin-associated histochemical readouts. Because Wiesner and Mäule report specific lignin-associated chemical features rather than total lignin content, this concordance should be interpreted as evidence that Ru1 reports lignified secondary-wall territories, not as proof of binding specificity for lignin or for individual lignin substructures.22–24Fig. 3E provides a schematic anatomical guide to the expected distribution of cellulose-rich primary-wall tissues and lignified secondary-wall domains in the Arabidopsis stem cross-section.

Having established qualitative concordance between Ru1 staining and classical lignin histochemistry, we next benchmarked both Ru1 and Ru2 against Safranin O, a long-standing dye for plant anatomy whose fluorescent readout is widely used as a practical reporter of lignified secondary-wall domains and has been adapted to confocal and ratiometric workflows for evaluating wall lignification in situ.14,51 Serial basal stem cross-sections of A. thaliana were stained under the standardized Ru-probe conditions defined above and compared with Safranin O staining. Under our FV-1000 confocal settings, Safranin O staining of LR White-embedded sections produced strong, diffuse resin-associated fluorescence that compromised wall-resolved contrast (Fig. S10). Under these specific LR White embedding and acquisition conditions, Safranin O fluorescence did not provide sufficient wall-resolved contrast, so the Safranin O confocal comparison shown in Fig. 4 was performed on Paraplast paraffin sections and interpreted using no-dye controls, single-label controls, and sequential acquisition practices.14,25,44,45

Across whole-section overviews, Ru1, Ru2, and Safranin O produced qualitatively concordant, wall-enriched fluorescence patterns concentrated in the vascular ring (Fig. 4A–C). A comparison between stained and unstained Paraplast paraffin sections acquired under the same settings used for Fig. 4 showed negligible background/autofluorescence in the corresponding detection channels (Fig. S11). At higher magnification, all three fluorophores delineated secondary-wall-rich territories, with the strongest emission associated with xylem vessels and interfascicular fiber domains and comparatively weak signal in largely non-lignified parenchymatous tissues (Fig. 4D–F). Bright-field images of the same regions confirmed that the detected fluorescence tracked anatomically defined secondary-wall territories within the vascular ring rather than a diffuse tissue-wide signal (Fig. 4G–I). These observations support the interpretation that, under the defined staining and confocal acquisition conditions, Ru1 and Ru2 report the spatial distribution of wall microenvironments that are anatomically and histochemically consistent with lignified secondary-wall territories. The signal is therefore best framed as matrix-associated anatomical enrichment, with its molecular basis requiring future analysis.

Occasional Ru1 fluorescence at the outermost cell layers may reflect distinct wall-associated microenvironments, such as cuticle- or suberin-adjacent boundaries, other phenolic-rich interfaces, and/or section-edge accessibility effects (Fig. 4A and D). Discriminating among these possibilities would require targeted controls, including section-edge exclusion, permeability or solvent controls, and orthogonal counterstains for cutin/suberin-rich domains, and is therefore beyond the scope of the present anatomical comparison.11,13 Because Safranin-based fluorescence is also informative but not strictly lignin-specific and can be influenced by local wall chemistry, staining accessibility, and sample preparation, this peripheral Ru1 signal is best interpreted conservatively as additional wall-associated labeling that requires separate mechanistic resolution.14,25

Multiplexing compatibility and spectral cross-talk analysis of Ru1 and Ru2

To determine whether Ru1 and Ru2 can be incorporated into multichannel plant cell-wall imaging workflows, we first evaluated their compatibility with Calcofluor White, a widely used fluorochrome that labels weakly substituted β-glucans, including cellulose and callose, and is commonly used to visualize overall wall architecture in plant tissues.3,11,52 Because Calcofluor White is not chemically exclusive for cellulose, we used it here as a broad β-glucan-rich wall counterstain rather than as a cellulose-specific probe, consistent with current recommendations for interpreting fluorescent wall stains in complex plant matrices.11,52,53

In sequentially co-stained basal stem cross-sections, Calcofluor White produced a pervasive wall-outline signal across the section, delineating primary and secondary wall architecture at both whole-section and higher-magnification views (Fig. 5A and B; Calcofluor panels). In the same specimens, Ru1 and Ru2 retained the vascular-ring-enriched, wall-associated photoluminescence observed in single-stain experiments and in the Safranin O comparison (Fig. 3 and 4), with signal preferentially concentrated in xylem vessels and interfascicular fiber regions and comparatively weak fluorescence in surrounding parenchymatous tissues (Fig. 5A and B; Ru panels and merged images). Sequential acquisition under 405-nm excitation, combined with separated detection windows for Calcofluor and Ru emission, yielded merged images without visually detectable cross-channel bleed-through under the acquisition settings used. These results support a straightforward two-channel workflow in which Calcofluor White provides a broad β-glucan-rich wall reference, and Ru1 or Ru2 highlights secondary-wall-enriched vascular domains. Partial overlap between Calcofluor and Ru signals is expected on biological grounds, since lignified secondary walls remain cellulose-based composite structures.4,47,48 Consistent with this interpretation, pixel-intensity correlation analysis using JACoP showed partial positive association between the Calcofluor White channel and the Ru-associated channels, with Pearson's correlation coefficients of r = 0.708 for Calcofluor White-Ru1 and r = 0.701 for Calcofluor White-Ru2 (Fig. S12). These values support spatial co-occurrence between the broad β-glucan-rich wall signal and Ru-enriched secondary-wall domains, but they do not by themselves establish molecular co-binding or chemical specificity. Therefore, signal co-occurrence in merged images should not be interpreted automatically as spectral contamination, particularly under the sequential acquisition and separated detection windows used here.

To extend this analysis beyond dual staining, we next evaluated whether Ru1 and Ru2 could be accommodated in more complex multicolor configurations. Multichannel confocal microscopy is frequently limited by spectral cross-talk, which can arise from cross-excitation, where a fluorophore is excited by a laser line intended for another probe, and from emission bleed-through, where overlapping emission bands are detected in the wrong channel. These effects are major sources of signal misassignment and artifactual colocalization in fluorescence microscopy and are usually mitigated by careful laser/detector selection, single-label controls, sequential acquisition, spectral imaging, and, when required, linear unmixing.43,44,54

We therefore recorded emission spectra for Ru1, Ru2, Safranin O, Calcofluor White, Alexa Fluor 555, and Alexa Fluor 647 under excitation at 405, 488, 552, and 638 nm, matching the laser lines available in our confocal configuration and commonly used in multichannel fluorescence imaging. Alexa Fluor dyes were included as representative orange-red and far-red labels with well-characterized spectral properties.55,56 Because dye spectra and plant autofluorescence can shift depending on tissue chemistry, embedding, local microenvironment, and detector configuration, these solution spectra were used as first-pass spectral fingerprints and interpreted together with the in situ λ-scan-anchored Ru detection windows defined above.7,43–45,57

Under 405-nm excitation, Calcofluor White showed a short-wavelength blue emission clearly separated from the longer-wavelength emissions of Ru1, Ru2, and Safranin O (Fig. 6A). This spectral separation supports the practical co-detection of Calcofluor with either Ru probe using independent blue and orange/red detection windows. In contrast, Ru1, Ru2, and Safranin O displayed extensive overlap in the orange-to-red region, indicating that these three labels are intrinsically difficult to combine in the same specimen without stringent sequential acquisition, narrow detector windows, spectral imaging, or linear unmixing.43,44,54 Thus, Ru1 and Ru2 should be treated as red-region alternatives to Safranin O in panel design, rather than as spectrally orthogonal partners for simultaneous red-channel co-staining.

Excitation at 488 nm produced substantial emission overlap among Alexa Fluor 555, Safranin O, and Ru1 (Fig. 6B). This indicates that 488-nm excitation, although common in multicolor confocal workflows, is not an optimal configuration for samples containing Ru probes together with orange/red-emitting dyes unless cross-excitation and emission bleed-through are explicitly controlled. Under 552-nm excitation, Alexa Fluor 555 and Safranin O dominated the orange-red emission region, while Alexa Fluor 647 showed measurable far-red emission under the tested conditions, consistent with partial excitation through its shorter-wavelength absorption tail (Fig. 6C).55,56 This finding indicates that combinations involving 552/561-nm excitation and far-red labels require conservative detector windowing and sequential acquisition to avoid misassignment of signal.

By contrast, 638-nm excitation provided the cleanest separation among the tested conditions. Alexa Fluor 647 was selectively excited and emitted strongly in the far-red region, whereas Ru1, Ru2, Safranin O, Calcofluor White, and Alexa Fluor 555 showed minimal emission under this excitation (Fig. 6D). Therefore, under the tested conditions, Alexa Fluor 647 represents the most spectrally favorable third-label option for multiplex configurations built around Calcofluor White and Ru-probe staining. This conclusion is operational rather than universal: channel compatibility should be revalidated when changing tissue type, sample preparation, fluorophore concentration, microscope configuration, or acquisition parameters.11,43,44

Collectively, the dual-staining experiment and spectral-overlap analysis define an operational multiplexing space for Ru1 and Ru2 in plant cell-wall imaging (Fig. 5, 6 and Table S1). Calcofluor White and either Ru probe provide a robust two-channel workflow under 405-nm excitation, separating broad β-glucan-rich wall architecture from Ru-enriched secondary-wall domains. In contrast, Ru probes, Safranin O, and Alexa Fluor 555 occupy overlapping orange/red spectral regions and should not be combined without rigorous spectral controls. For three-color configurations, a far-red Alexa Fluor 647-class label excited at 638 nm provides the most orthogonal option among the fluorophores tested. These results position Ru1 and Ru2 as practical violet-excited probes for multiplex-aware confocal imaging of lignified wall territories, while emphasizing that acquisition windows and cross-talk controls must be empirically validated for each experimental configuration.

Conceptual DFT and density-based descriptors of Ru1 and Ru2

Electron-density-based descriptors were used to compare intrinsic ligand-dependent differences in charge redistribution, surface electrostatics, weak intramolecular contacts, and π-electron delocalization in Ru1 and Ru2. Given the heterogeneous and aromatic nature of lignified cell walls36 and the known ability of lignin-rich matrices to engage aromatic/cationic molecules through weak noncovalent interactions,37–39 these descriptors provide probe-centered structure–property fingerprints that may inform hypotheses about matrix-associated enrichment. However, because the calculations were performed on isolated Ru(ii) complexes and did not include lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, model wall polymers, or a wall-mimetic environment, they should not be interpreted as direct models of probe-wall binding. Thus, the DFT analysis provides a qualitative, hypothesis-generating framework, but it does not establish binding modes, interaction strengths, or molecular selectivity in lignified cell walls.

Fukui functions58–62 were analyzed to identify regions of ligand-centered electronic responsiveness in Ru1 and Ru2. Both complexes showed predominantly ligand-localized reactivity patterns, with ligand-dependent differences in the spatial distribution of the response. Ru1 displayed a more heterogeneous distribution associated with its ester-functionalized bipyridyl framework, whereas Ru2 showed a more delocalized response consistent with its extended phenanthroline π-system (Fig. S5). These differences describe intrinsic electronic responsiveness in the isolated probes and may be useful for future explicit probe–polymer interaction models, but they do not by themselves demonstrate preferential binding to lignin or to any specific wall component.

To complement this analysis, molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) surfaces were examined.63,64Ru1 showed a more heterogeneous electrostatic profile, with localized neutral and negative regions associated with oxygenated functionalities, whereas Ru2 presented a more uniform and predominantly positive surface over its compact polypyridyl framework (Fig. S6). These results indicate that ligand identity modulates the surface electrostatic anisotropy of the isolated complexes, a feature that could influence matrix partitioning in chemically heterogeneous environments but remains to be tested experimentally.

Weak intramolecular contacts were then examined using the NCI/RDG framework.65,66 Both Ru1 and Ru2 showed dense low-RDG regions around sign(λ2)ρ ≈ 0, consistent with predominantly weak dispersive interactions within the complexes (Fig. S7). The corresponding 3D NCI isosurfaces revealed localized steric regions in congested zones together with limited attractive patches, indicating an intramolecular landscape dominated by weak, spatially distributed interactions. Because this analysis was performed only on the isolated probes, it reflects internal preorganization and exposed surface features rather than intermolecular interactions with lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, or other wall polymers.

Finally, π-electron delocalization was evaluated using complementary EDDB and MICD descriptors.67,68 Both complexes retained significant multicenter π-delocalization upon coordination; however, Ru2 exhibited a more extended and continuous π-framework, as reflected in its higher delocalized-electron population and stronger diatropic response (Fig. S8). This result indicates that Ru2 presents a more contiguous aromatic platform than Ru1 in the isolated complex. Such a feature could be relevant to future studies of partitioning in aromatic wall microenvironments, but the present calculations do not test this directly. In contrast, Ru1 combines a less continuous π-system with a more heterogeneous electrostatic surface, suggesting a distinct ligand-encoded physicochemical profile whose consequences for wall enrichment remain unresolved.

Overall, these descriptors define intrinsic ligand-dependent differences between the two isolated Ru(ii) probes: Ru2 presents a more contiguous aromatic platform, whereas Ru1 displays greater electrostatic heterogeneity. These differences provide a chemically plausible hypothesis space for future probe–polymer modeling, binding assays, partitioning studies, or lifetime-resolved imaging, but they do not by themselves explain the observed staining pattern or establish specific probe–lignin interactions.

Discussion

This study evaluates two classical Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl luminophores, Ru1 and Ru2, as confocal-compatible, non-derivatizing photoluminescent stains for delineating lignified secondary-wall territories in fixed Arabidopsis thaliana stem sections. Under a short staining protocol and 405-nm excitation, both complexes produced wall-associated emission enriched in xylem vessels and interfascicular fibers, with qualitative anatomical concordance with Wiesner/Mäule lignin histochemistry and Safranin O-based confocal imaging. In addition, in situ λ-scans and solution spectral-overlap analyses defined practical detection windows and multiplexing constraints, supporting a two-channel workflow with Calcofluor White and identifying 638-nm-excited Alexa Fluor 647 as the most spectrally favorable third-label option among the fluorophores tested. The main contribution is therefore not the introduction of Ru(ii) polypyridyl complexes as biological probes, nor the development of a chemically specific lignin assay, but the application-specific benchmarking of two established Ru(ii) luminophores for lignified plant-wall imaging. In live-cell Ru(ii) bioimaging, ligand hydrophobicity, lipophilicity, and aromatic surface area can influence cellular uptake, intracellular trafficking, and luminescence signal intensity.26,29,69–71 However, the present assay was performed on fixed plant sections and therefore probes matrix-associated enrichment rather than membrane uptake or subcellular localization.

The broadly similar anatomical staining patterns produced by Ru1 and Ru2 are an important outcome of this comparison. The two probes were not selected with the expectation that one would necessarily outperform the other, but to determine whether a controlled change in ligand periphery would produce detectable differences in lignified-wall staining under the standardized conditions used here. The absence of a major divergence at the tissue-domain level suggests that the shared dicationic Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl architecture is sufficient to drive enrichment in lignified secondary-wall territories, whereas the ligand differences between deeb and phen do not generate strongly distinct anatomical maps in this fixed-section assay. This result does not exclude subtler ligand-dependent effects on partitioning, emission lifetime, microenvironment sensitivity, or wall-polymer affinity; rather, it indicates that such effects are not resolved by the qualitative anatomical readouts used in the present study. Future work should therefore include quantitative log D/log P measurements, polymer-partitioning assays, probe-wall binding studies, and lifetime-resolved imaging to determine whether ligand tuning can be translated into stronger matrix selectivity or improved photophysical performance.

Both complexes retain significant multicenter π-delocalization; however, Ru2 exhibits a more extended π-framework than Ru1. This intrinsic feature could be relevant to future studies of partitioning in aromatic wall microenvironments, but the present calculations do not test this directly because no explicit lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, or lignocellulose fragments were modeled. Consistent with the broadly similar staining patterns observed experimentally, the shared dicationic and aromatic Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl scaffold likely dominates the tissue-level staining behavior, whereas ligand-dependent differences may contribute only subtly or remain unresolved under the qualitative anatomical readouts used here.

The preferential enrichment of Ru1 and Ru2 in secondary-wall-rich vascular domains is chemically plausible in the context of the contrast between lignified and polysaccharide-rich wall environments. Lignin is a heterogeneous aromatic polymer derived from oxidative coupling of monolignols, generating a spatially variable network of H, G, and S units connected through ether and carbon–carbon linkages.2,72 Such lignified domains present aromatic surfaces and oxygenated functionalities capable of supporting multiple weak association modes with cationic aromatic dyes, including dispersive, π-associated, electrostatic, hydrogen-bonding, and cation-π contributions.36–39 By contrast, primary-wall-dominated regions are comparatively enriched in polysaccharide matrices, where β-(1,4)-d-glucan chains assemble into semi-crystalline cellulose microfibrils embedded within hemicellulose- and pectin-containing networks, and are therefore less likely to provide extended aromatic surfaces for π-associated interactions.4,73–76 This compositional difference provides a reasonable physicochemical basis for the observed preferential enrichment of cationic Ru(ii) polypyridyl luminophores in lignified secondary-wall microdomains rather than in primary-wall-dominated parenchymatous tissues. However, the present data do not establish a single binding mechanism or molecular specificity for lignin substructures. Thus, the probes report wall domains in which the local physicochemical environment favors Ru-probe enrichment, and these domains coincide spatially with lignified secondary-wall territories. This interpretation frames the Ru signal as matrix-associated anatomical enrichment while leaving the molecular basis of probe partitioning to future binding and wall-polymer interaction studies.

This distinction is important because the anatomical concordance observed here relies on complementary but inherently qualitative benchmarks. The Wiesner reaction primarily reports cinnamaldehyde-related lignin end groups, whereas the Mäule reaction is commonly used to reveal syringyl-enriched lignin features.22–24 Similarly, Safranin O is a highly useful fluorescent reporter of lignified secondary walls and has been developed into ratiometric approaches for in muro lignification assessment, but its fluorescence depends on wall chemistry, sample preparation, and local microenvironment.11,14,77 Therefore, the agreement among Ru staining, Wiesner/Mäule histochemistry, and Safranin O imaging supports the use of Ru1 and Ru2 as anatomical reporters of lignified secondary-wall territories, but it does not demonstrate quantitative lignin content, lignin monomer composition, or selective binding to specific lignin motifs. This conservative interpretation is strengthened by the observation that occasional peripheral Ru1 signal at the outermost cell layers may reflect cuticle- or suberin-adjacent interfaces, other phenolic-rich wall environments, or section-edge accessibility effects rather than lignified secondary walls per se.11,13 Resolving these peripheral signals would require targeted controls, including section-edge exclusion, solvent/accessibility tests, and orthogonal markers for cutin- or suberin-rich domains.

A major practical advantage of the Ru-based workflow is its compatibility with multiplex-aware confocal imaging. Calcofluor White produced a broad wall-outline signal across the stem section, consistent with its use as a β-glucan-rich wall counterstain rather than a cellulose-specific probe.3,11,52,53 In the same specimens, Ru1 and Ru2 retained vascular-ring-enriched photoluminescence, enabling a simple two-channel configuration in which Calcofluor defines general wall architecture and Ru emission highlights secondary-wall-enriched vascular domains. Partial spatial overlap between Calcofluor and Ru signals is biologically expected because lignified secondary walls remain cellulose-based composite structures.4,47,48 Thus, co-occurrence in merged images should not be interpreted automatically as spectral bleed-through. Instead, the relevant conclusion is that separated detection windows under 405-nm excitation can distinguish a broad β-glucan-rich wall channel from a Ru-enriched secondary-wall channel under the acquisition conditions used here.

The spectral-overlap analysis further defines the practical limits of Ru1 and Ru2 in multicolor panel design. Under 405-nm excitation, Calcofluor White emission is largely confined to the blue region, whereas Ru1, Ru2, and Safranin O emit in longer-wavelength orange/red regions. This supports Calcofluor/Ru dual labeling but also indicates that Ru probes and Safranin O should generally be treated as red-region alternatives rather than as spectrally orthogonal partners. Their overlapping emission profiles would make simultaneous interpretation difficult without stringent detector windowing, single-label controls, sequential acquisition, spectral imaging, or linear unmixing.43,44,54,57,78 Similarly, 488-nm excitation produced substantial overlap among orange/red-emitting fluorophores, including Alexa Fluor 555, Safranin O, and Ru1, making this a suboptimal configuration for Ru-containing multiplex panels unless cross-excitation and emission bleed-through are explicitly controlled. By contrast, 638-nm excitation selectively favored Alexa Fluor 647 emission with minimal contribution from the other fluorophores tested, making a far-red Alexa Fluor 647-class label the most operationally favorable third-label option in the present configuration. This conclusion is instrument- and condition-dependent and should be revalidated when changing tissue type, fixation/embedding procedure, fluorophore concentration, detector settings, or microscope platform.11,43–45

The present results also highlight the importance of sample preparation for fluorescent plant-wall imaging. In our workflow, Safranin O staining of LR White-embedded sections generated diffuse resin-associated fluorescence that compromised wall-resolved contrast, whereas paraffin sections provided a more reliable platform for Safranin-based benchmarking. This observation reinforces the need to validate each dye within the exact fixation, embedding, sectioning, mounting, and imaging conditions used.11,14,25,45 More broadly, plant tissues are challenging fluorescent matrices because endogenous autofluorescence is abundant, chemically heterogeneous, and spectrally broad, with lignin and other phenolic components contributing signals that may be useful for label-free imaging but problematic for multichannel experiments.6,7 The Ru workflow described here mitigates part of this problem by anchoring detector windows to in situ λ-scans and by using no-dye and single-label controls, in line with best practices for quantitative confocal and plant fluorescence imaging.44,45 However, spectral windowing alone cannot eliminate all autofluorescence or matrix-dependent spectral shifts. The long-lived triplet MLCT emission characteristic of many Ru(ii) polypyridyl luminophores therefore represents an attractive future opportunity for time-gated or lifetime-resolved imaging, which could improve contrast against nanosecond-scale plant autofluorescence without changing the staining chemistry.6,7,26–29,32,33 Together, these observations emphasize that fluorescent benchmarks for lignified-wall imaging are most informative when their contrast, background, and wall specificity are validated within the same preparation and acquisition workflow used for comparative analysis.

From a dye-chemistry perspective, the main significance of this work is that it connects a classical Ru(ii) polypyridyl scaffold with a biologically relevant, aromatic, and optically complex polymer matrix. Compared with established lignin-imaging approaches, including Safranin O-based fluorescence and bioorthogonal monolignol labeling strategies,14,20,21,77Ru1 and Ru2 offer a complementary route based on operational simplicity, 405-nm excitation compatibility, ligand modularity, and empirically defined multiplexing constraints. The approach does not replace ratiometric Safranin O methods for semi-quantitative lignification assessment or click-chemistry reporters for mapping active lignification dynamics. Instead, it provides a rapid and chemically tractable staining platform for anatomical visualization of lignified secondary-wall territories, with the added advantage that ligand modification may eventually be used to tune spectral behavior, wall partitioning, and microenvironment sensitivity.

Several limitations should guide the interpretation and future development of this strategy. First, the present study establishes qualitative anatomical concordance between Ru photoluminescence and lignified secondary-wall territories, but it does not determine binding constants, partition coefficients, or probe affinity toward purified lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, or model wall polymers. Second, although this study establishes practical excitation/emission windows and in situ staining behavior, a more complete photophysical characterization of Ru1 and Ru2 in wall-like matrices remains necessary, including quantum yields, emission lifetimes, photobleaching kinetics, oxygen sensitivity, and concentration-dependent aggregation behavior. Third, the experiments were performed in fixed A. thaliana stem sections, so the generality of the workflow across plant species, organs, developmental stages, lignin compositions, and sample-preparation pipelines remains to be established. Finally, while the computational descriptors provide a useful ligand-comparative framework, explicit modeling of probe interactions with realistic lignin or lignocellulose fragments would be required to move from structure–property correlation toward mechanistic binding hypotheses.

Future work should therefore combine systematic ligand tuning with quantitative photophysics, controlled polymer-partitioning assays, and broader plant-imaging validation. Ru(ii) polypyridyl chemistry offers a large design space for shifting absorption/emission profiles, modulating charge distribution, changing π-surface continuity, and introducing functional groups that may bias partitioning toward specific wall microenvironments.26–31 In parallel, time-resolved imaging, FLIM, or time-gated detection could exploit Ru(ii) excited-state lifetimes to improve contrast in highly autofluorescent plant tissues.6,7,32,33 Direct comparison with ratiometric Safranin O imaging, lignin autofluorescence lifetime measurements, chemical lignin quantification, and bioorthogonal monolignol reporters would help define when Ru staining should be used as a purely anatomical reporter and when it may support more quantitative or chemically informative contrasts.14,20,21,77 Overall, the present work establishes Ru1 and Ru2 as practical starting points for a ligand-controlled family of photoluminescent stains for lignified plant wall imaging and provides the spectral and methodological framework needed for their rational development in complex biological matrices.

Materials and methods

Materials and instruments

The reagents used for the synthesis were commercially available by Aldrich, and they were used without further purification. 1H NMR for Ru1 and Ru2 were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE 400 spectrometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) at 25 °C and are reported in ppm in deuterated DMSO.

Synthesis and characterization of tris-Ru(ii) complexes

Ru1 and Ru2 were synthesized from RuCl3·H2O and the corresponding ligands (deeb or phen) in a 1 : 3 ratio under reflux in ethanol, using hydroquinone as a reducing agent, following reported procedures.40–42 After completion, PF6− salts were obtained by counter-ion exchange and purified by recrystallization (ethanol/water, 2 : 1) followed by diethyl ether treatment. Identity and purity were confirmed by ATR-FTIR and 1H NMR (400 MHz, DMSO-d6), consistent with literature reports for Ru1 and Ru2.40–42

Computational methods

To support the interpretation of ligand-dependent electronic features in Ru1 and Ru2, both complexes were analyzed using density functional theory (DFT). Ground-state geometries were optimized with ORCA version 5.0 at the BP86/def2-TZVP level of theory.79–82 Scalar relativistic effects for the ruthenium center were treated using the Zeroth-Order Regular Approximation (ZORA),83–85 together with Segmented All-electron Relativistically Contracted (SARC) basis sets for Ru.86 The optimized geometries and corresponding electron-density information were subsequently used for conceptual DFT and density-based descriptor analyses.

Conceptual DFT descriptors were calculated to compare the electronic responsiveness of Ru1 and Ru2. Fukui-function maps were generated from the optimized structures to differentiate the intrinsic reactivity patterns of Ru1 and Ru2. Subsequent operations on cube files and visualization of Fukui-function distributions were performed using Chemcraft version 1.8.

Molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) surfaces were generated from single-point calculations performed with Gaussian 16, Revision C.01, using the ORCA-optimized geometries.63,64,87 MEP values were mapped onto outer electron-density isosurfaces to compare the surface electrostatic anisotropy of Ru1 and Ru2.

Noncovalent interaction (NCI) analyses were performed using the wavefunction files obtained from the ORCA calculations. Reduced density gradient (RDG) analyses as a function of sign(λ2)ρ were carried out with Multiwfn version 3.8,88 and the resulting NCI isosurfaces were visualized with VMD version 1.9.4.89 These analyses were used to assess weak intramolecular contacts and internal preorganization of the isolated Ru(ii) probes. They were not intended to model explicit interactions between the probes and lignin or other plant cell-wall polymers.

Aromaticity and π-electron delocalization were evaluated using complementary electronic and magnetic descriptors. Electron density of delocalized bonds (EDDB) calculations were performed using EDDBRun scripts to quantify π-electron delocalization in selected polypyridyl fragments.67,90 Magnetically induced current density (MICD) analyses were carried out with the GIMIC program68,91 using GIAO wavefunctions.92 Current-density profiles were computed across planes placed perpendicular to the selected ligand fragments, and ring-current strengths were obtained by numerical integration using two-dimensional Gauss–Lobatto quadrature. Current-density pathways were visualized using ParaView version 5.10.0. EDDB and MICD analyses were performed using the same optimized molecular geometries to allow an internally consistent comparison between Ru1 and Ru2.

Plant material and growth conditions

The Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia-0 (Col-0) was used in all experiments. Seeds were stratified for 48 h at 4 °C in darkness prior to sowing. Plants were grown in soil (Top Crop™) supplemented with fertilizer (Top Crop™) and maintained in a controlled growth chamber under long-day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark). Illumination was provided by LED lamps at a light intensity of 120 µmol m−2 s−1. Temperature ranged between 19 and 24 °C, and relative humidity was maintained at 65%. Samples were collected at 6 weeks after sowing. A minimum of three biological replicates was used for all experiments.

Fixation, embedding, and sectioning of Arabidopsis thaliana tissues

Stems (second internode) from 6-week-old Arabidopsis thaliana plants were fixed in FAA (50% absolute ethanol, 5% glacial acetic acid, 10% formaldehyde, 35% distilled water; v/v) for ≥24 h at room temperature. Samples were dehydrated through an ethanol series (50%, 70%, 85%, 95%, and 3 × 100%; ≥1 h per step).93–95

For paraffin embedding, tissues were cleared in ethanol : Neo-Clear (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 2 : 1, 1 : 1, and 1 : 2 (v/v; ≥1 h each), followed by three changes of 100% Neo-Clear (≥1 h each). Infiltration proceeded in Neo-Clear : Paraplast (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 1 : 1 (v/v; 1 h) and then three changes in molten Paraplast at 60 °C (≥3 h total).96–98 Blocks were cast and sectioned at 5 µm using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2125 RT; Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany).98

For resin embedding, dehydrated samples were infiltrated with LR White ((1-methylethylidene)bis(4,1-phenyleneoxy-2,1-ethanediyl) bismethacrylate, 2-propenoic acid, 2-methyl-, C12-16-alkyl esters; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) using graded ethanol : resin mixtures (e.g., 3 : 1, 1 : 1, 1 : 3; v/v; ≥1 h each) before three changes in 100% resin, following standard graded-transition protocols reported for plant tissues, and then polymerized at ∼60 °C for ∼24 h. Semi-thin sections (≈1.5 µm) were cut on an ultramicrotome and collected for light microscopy.25,99,100

Histochemical staining

The mounted sections were subjected to lignin histochemistry. For the Wiesner (phloroglucinol–HCl) reaction, the reagent was prepared as 1% (w/v) phloroglucinol (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in 99.5% ethanol mixed 1 : 1 (v/v) with 12 M HCl (final 0.5% phloroglucinol in 6 M HCl) and applied at room temperature; staining reached a plateau after ∼2 min and images were acquired promptly to limit signal decay.101,102 For the Mäule test, sections were sequentially treated with 1% (w/v) KMnO4 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 5 min, rinsed three times in distilled water, incubated in 1 N HCl (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 30 min, rinsed, and the color was developed with 28% aqueous NH3 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in the stabilized variant, 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 8; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used instead of NH3 to generate the color. Observations were performed shortly after development; when applicable, fluorescence was recorded under blue excitation (460–495 nm) with emission >510 nm. Unless otherwise stated, all steps were performed at room temperature.23,103

Fluorophore emission spectra measurement

Excitation and emission spectra were recorded using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). For Fig. 2, excitation and emission spectra of the ruthenium complexes Ru1 and Ru2 (20 µM) were measured in solution to define practical excitation and emission detection conditions for the confocal workflow. Excitation spectra were acquired in PBS by scanning 350–600 nm in 10 nm increments while monitoring emission at 640 nm, whereas emission spectra were recorded from 440–800 nm under 405 nm excitation. For Fig. 6, emission spectra of Ru1, Ru2, Safranin O, Calcofluor White, Alexa Fluor 555, and Alexa Fluor 647 were obtained under excitation at 405, 488, 552, and 638 nm, corresponding to common confocal laser lines, to evaluate spectral overlap and fluorophore compatibility in multiplex imaging experiments.

Fluorescent staining protocols

Safranin O

Paraffin or resin sections were rehydrated and incubated in 0.01% (w/v) Safranin O (aqueous; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 5–10 min at room temperature, gently rinsed in buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.0) and mounted. Safranin O was evaluated on both LR White resin and Paraplast paraffin sections; the Fig. 4 benchmarking was performed on paraffin sections because LR White produced diffuse resin-associated fluorescence under our confocal settings.

Ruthenium probes (Ru1, Ru2)

Stock solutions of [Ru(deeb)3]2+ (Ru1) and [Ru(phen)3]2+ (Ru2) were prepared at 10 mM in DMSO, stored protected from light at −20 °C, and diluted immediately before use. Working solutions (40 µM) were prepared in 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.0 (final DMSO ≤0.5% v/v). Buffer dependence was surveyed for Ru1 in HEPES, PBS, TBS, and water; Ru2 was evaluated only under the selected HEPES condition unless stated otherwise. Sections were incubated 10 min at room temperature, followed by 2 × gentle rinses (2 min each) in the same buffer before imaging.

Calcofluor and dual-staining

For cell-wall counterstaining, sections were incubated in Calcofluor White M2R at 0.01% (w/v) for 5 min at room temperature (optionally with a few drops of 1 N NaOH as in classic protocols), rinsed in water or buffer, and imaged. For dual staining (Ru + Calcofluor), incubations were performed sequentially (Ru first, Calcofluor second), with intermediate rinses, and imaged.

Epifluorescence microscopy

Epifluorescence microscopy was used only for the qualitative Ru1 buffer-tolerance survey shown in Fig. S9. Images were acquired on an Olympus IX73 microscope equipped with a broad-spectrum mercury lamp and a CCD camera (MicroPublisher 3.3 RTV, Q-Imaging). The objectives used were 10×/0.30, 20×/0.50, and 40×/0.75 from the UPlanFL N series. Acquisition was controlled with Q-capture-Pro 7 (Q-Imaging); images were recorded at 16-bit depth with 1 × 1 binning, and exposure times were adjusted to avoid saturation. Ru1-stained sections were imaged using a TRITC/Rodamine filter cube (Ex ∼ 520–550 nm, Em ∼ 570–620 nm),71 which captured Ru1 emission sufficiently for this preliminary qualitative screen. These epifluorescence settings were not used for the main confocal imaging experiments. All main-text Ru-probe images were acquired by confocal microscopy under the standardized conditions described in Section 4.10, using 405-nm excitation and emission detection windows of 600–660 nm for Ru1 and 550–600 nm for Ru2.

Confocal microscopy

Confocal imaging was performed on an Olympus FV-1000 (Olympus, Japan) equipped with variable spectral detectors. Unless stated otherwise, images were acquired with UPlanFL N 40×/1.30 oil (high-resolution) or UPlanFL N 20×/0.75 (surveys), the pinhole set to 1 Airy unit, scan speed 4–8 µs per pixel, 2–4× line averaging, and the lowest laser power that achieved adequate signal while minimizing photobleaching.44

Excitation/emission settings. Based on preliminary λ-scans and the optimization experiments described above, Ru1 and Ru2 were excited at 405 nm. Emission was collected at 600–660 nm (Ru1) and 550–600 nm (Ru2), while Calcofluor was collected at ∼412–490 nm under 405-nm excitation. For Safranin O, images were acquired in the range of ∼570–600 nm under 488-nm excitation.

Spectral (λ) scanning and channel separation. Reference emission profiles for each fluorophore in plant tissues were obtained with the FV-1000 λ-scan mode (step 5 nm, typical range 430–750 nm), and used to confirm window placement and, when needed, to perform linear unmixing. This workflow (λ-scan, unmixing, sequential acquisition) follows established practice for spectral imaging in plants on the FV-1000.46 To prevent bleed-through in dual experiments (Calcofluor + Ru complexes), sequential scanning (line- or frame-sequential) was used, as recommended for quantitative confocal imaging.44

Z-stacks were acquired with a Nyquist-sampled step (typically 0.5–0.8 µm for the 40×/1.30 oil objective and our emission wavelengths). Stack limits were set to encompass the entire section thickness while avoiding saturation in bright lignified regions.44

For each section, an autofluorescence control (no dye) was recorded in all channels to assess background from lignin and other wall components; blue/UV-excited lignin autofluorescence was used to guide exposure and avoid bleed-through into Ru channels.7 Flat-field correction and background subtraction were applied in FIJI/ImageJ before quantitative analyses. Channel selection for Calcofluor and red-emitting dyes followed published plant cell-wall imaging protocols to ensure spectral separation in multi-label experiments.13

In all cases, detector gain/offset were adjusted to keep intensities within the linear range; identical settings were maintained within each experimental batch. The FV-1000 spectral detector/λ-scan implementation and workflow have been widely used for plant fluorophores and cell-wall dyes, including separation of overlapping signals in living plant cells.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the canonical Ru(ii) tris-polypyridyl complexes Ru1 and Ru2 can be used as practical, non-derivatizing photoluminescent stains for confocal visualization of lignified secondary-wall territories in fixed Arabidopsis thaliana stem sections. Under a short, buffer-compatible staining protocol and 405-nm excitation, both complexes produced reproducible wall-associated emission enriched in xylem vessels and interfascicular fibers, showing qualitative anatomical concordance with Wiesner/Mäule histochemistry and Safranin O fluorescence. Importantly, the Ru-associated signal provides a reproducible spatial readout of matrix-associated enrichment within anatomically defined lignified secondary-wall domains. The molecular basis of this enrichment, including the relative contribution of lignin and other wall polymers, remains to be resolved by future binding and partitioning assays. In situ λ-scans and solution spectral-overlap analyses defined practical detection windows for Ru1 and Ru2 and established their compatibility with Calcofluor White as a β-glucan-rich wall counterstain, while identifying far-red Alexa Fluor 647 excitation at 638 nm as the most spectrally orthogonal third-label configuration among the fluorophores tested. Complementary DFT-based descriptors provided a ligand-comparative, hypothesis-generating framework by summarizing intrinsic differences in π-surface continuity, electrostatic anisotropy, and ligand-centered electronic responsiveness between Ru1 and Ru2. Overall, this work positions classical Ru(ii) polypyridyl luminophores as chemically tractable, confocal-compatible starting points for the development of ligand-tuned stains for plant wall imaging and provides an operational basis for future optimization of Ru-based colorants with improved spectral orthogonality, quantitative photophysical performance, and matrix-selective staining behavior.
