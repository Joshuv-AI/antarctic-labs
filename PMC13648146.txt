Psychometric validation of brief scales for loneliness at work in Korea: the Three-Item Scale for Loneliness at Work (SLAW-3) and Single-Item Scale for Loneliness at Work (SLAW-1)
Authors: Ganguk Lee, Chunhui Suh, Seongdong Lee, Norito Kawakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWorkplace loneliness is an emerging psychosocial risk factor in the occupational health context, but comprehensive assessment instruments are often impractical if large workforce surveys are planned. This study evaluated the measurement properties of two brief workplace loneliness indicators: the Single-Item Scale for Loneliness at Work (SLAW-1) and the Three-Item Scale for Loneliness at Work (SLAW-3).MethodsData were obtained from a nationwide online survey of Korean employees (n = 1,000), with proportional sampling by sex, age, and occupation. A subsample (n = 200) underwent 4-week follow-up to assess test–retest reliability. Internal consistency and confirmatory factor analysis were examined for SLAW-3, whereas test–retest reliability was assessed for SLAW-1. Criterion validity was tested against a single-item measure of general loneliness. Convergent validity was examined by deriving correlations with occupational stress and psychological distress, work performance, work engagement, and well-being. ResultsSLAW-3 demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.883) and was consistent with a single-factor interpretation. SLAW-1 exhibited moderate 4-week test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.603). Both scales were associated with workplace loneliness and related psychological and occupational variables, in the expected directions. SLAW-1 was more strongly related to general loneliness, whereas SLAW-3 evidenced stronger associations with workplace loneliness. ConclusionsSLAW-1 and SLAW-3 appear to be practical brief indicators of workplace loneliness. SLAW-1 may be appropriate for rapid screening, whereas SLAW-3 offers a more context-specific assessment in terms of occupational health research and monitoring.Keywords: Brief loneliness measurement, Psychometric validation, Workplace loneliness

BACKGROUND

Workplace loneliness refers to “the distress caused by the perceived lack of good quality interpersonal relationships between employees in a work environment.”1 Empirical evidence implies that such loneliness is linked to poor mental health, including burnout.2 Workplace loneliness is also associated with poorer job performance and diminished interpersonal effectiveness, underscoring the relevance thereof to organizational outcomes.3 Measuring workplace loneliness is important when planning organizational interventions and for early identification of lonely employees.

During large-scale workforce surveys and repeated assessments focused on psychosocial risk monitoring, measurement feasibility is a critical methodological issue because an excessive response burden can undermine both data quality and the participation rate, particularly in applied and longitudinal research settings.4 To date, quantitative research on workplace loneliness has primarily relied on validated self-report instruments, most commonly the Loneliness at Work Scale (LAWS) and adapted versions of the UCLA Loneliness Scale.5,6 The methodological literature suggests that, in large-scale surveys where administrative time and respondent burdens constrain questionnaire lengths, brief indicators such as single-item measures with well-defined constructs may be appropriate for screening or monitoring.7 Specifically, a validation study using representative population samples found that a single-item loneliness question may serve as a brief screening measure for population-based assessments, supporting the construct validity thereof via the expected associations with mental health outcomes and sociodemographic risk factors.8 Nevertheless, accepted methodological guidelines advise that single-item measures might not perform as well as multi-item scales in various empirical contexts, emphasizing the necessity for rigorous validation when using concise measures.9

Despite the practical appeal of brief scales, evidence on the psychometric performance of such scales in working populations remains comparatively limited, particularly in large-scale survey settings. Therefore, further validation studies are needed to determine whether brief workplace loneliness measures can serve as useful tools for occupational health surveillance and repeated monitoring. With a focus on this gap, the present study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of brief workplace loneliness measures in a nationwide sample of Korean employees. Specifically, we examined the Single-Item Scale for Loneliness at Work (SLAW-1) and the Three-Item Scale for Loneliness at Work (SLAW-3) that were originally developed in Japan.10 We evaluated the reliability and validity thereof in large-scale and repeated occupational surveys.

METHODS

Study design and subjects

This psychometric analysis used data from a nationwide online survey of workplace loneliness in Korea. The study design and the procedures used have been previously documented by the team who validated the Korean version of the Loneliness at Work Scale (LAWS-K).11 Baseline data were collected in November 2024 from full-time employees aged 18–59 years who had been employed for a minimum of 1 year and worked over 35 hours per week. Individuals who were unemployed, self-employed, on military service, or on parental or sick leave were excluded. Participants were proportionally allocated by sex, age group, and occupational type, resulting in a final sample of 1,000 respondents. A random subsample of 200 participants completed a follow-up survey 4 weeks later to evaluate test–retest reliability.

Measurements

This study concentrated on two brief measures of workplace loneliness: the SLAW-1 and the SLAW-3.10 SLAW-1 directly evaluated workplace loneliness via a single question “How often do you feel lonely while you are at work?” This question was adapted from a general loneliness item12 by adding the phrase “at work.”10 SLAW-3 comprised three items that assessed three fundamental aspects of loneliness: perceived lack of companionship, feelings of alienation, and a sense of social isolation. These items were derived from the Three-Item Loneliness Scale13 and were tailored to fit the workplace setting.10 Participants responded using a five-point Likert scale ranging from (1) never, (2) hardly ever, (3) occasionally, (4) some of the time, and (5) always/often. The total score was calculated by adding each item’s score, which was assigned according to frequency. For both SLAW-1 and SLAW-3, higher scores indicated greater workplace loneliness.

When developing the Korean version of SLAW-3, our research team reviewed the Korean wording of the UCLA-based items used in three previous Korean studies that employed the UCLA Loneliness Scale version 3,14 the Revised UCLA Loneliness Scale,15 and a Korean short-form loneliness measure based on the UCLA loneliness items.16 Although these studies did not directly validate the identicality of the Korean workplace-adapted SLAW-3, they did include conceptually corresponding items. The Korean version of SLAW-1 was a direct translation of the original English SLAW-1 version because no Korean validation of the identical, direct, single-item loneliness measure was available. The final Korean language items were chosen via discussion among researchers. It was sought to both ensure semantic consistency with the original items and contextual appropriateness for Korean employees.

Data analysis

The internal consistency of SLAW-3 was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and item–total correlations, whereas the test–retest reliability of SLAW-1 was examined using the intraclass correlation coefficient. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to assess the unidimensional structure of SLAW-3. Given that the one-factor model with three indicators is just-identified, global goodness-of-fit indices were not informative.17 Consequently, interpretation of the CFA results was mainly based on the magnitude and statistical significance of the standardized factor loadings.

Criterion validity was examined using the LAWS-K, a previously validated multi-item measure of workplace loneliness,11 as well as a single-item measure of general loneliness in a non-workplace context.12 For these measures, higher scores indicate greater loneliness. To assess convergent validity, additional constructs theoretically related to workplace loneliness—occupational stress and psychological distress, work performance, work engagement, and well-being—were evaluated using the Korean Occupational Stress Scale (KOSS®19),18 Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6),19 World Health Organization Health and Work Performance Questionnaire (WHO-HPQ),20 Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES),21 and WHO-5 Well-Being Index (WHO-5)22 instruments. Higher scores on the KOSS®19 and K6 indicate greater occupational stress and psychological distress, respectively, whereas elevated scores on the WHO-HPQ, UWES, and WHO-5 indicate more favorable states (i.e., better work performance, higher work engagement, and greater well-being). Criterion and convergent validity were assessed by deriving Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Subgroup analyses were conducted by sex and age. SLAW-3 and SLAW-1 descriptive statistics, and Pearson’s correlation coefficients between these parameters and the validation measures were calculated separately for each subgroup. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Inje University Busan Paik Hospital (approval no. BPIRB 2024-10-062), including a waiver of informed consent. Participants were informed of the study purpose and of the voluntary, anonymous nature of their involvement. Only those who agreed to participate proceeded to the online questionnaire.

RESULTS

In total, 1,000 participants were included in the baseline survey, and 200 completed the follow-up survey. The sex distribution was balanced at baseline (50.0% male, 50.0% female) and was similar in the follow-up group (48.5% male, 51.5% female). The mean age was 39.75 years (standard deviation [SD]: 10.52) at baseline and 42.65 years (SD: 10.24) at follow-up. At baseline, service workers constituted the largest occupational group (22.7%), followed by clerks (13.7%), and professionals and related workers (12.9%). In the follow-up sample, professionals and related workers accounted for the largest proportion (21.0%). Most participants lived with others (73.4%), and nearly half were married (47.6%) (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the psychometric properties of two brief measures of workplace loneliness (the SLAW-1 and SLAW-3) in a large, nationally representative sample of Korean employees. The findings reveal that both the SLAW-1 and SLAW-3 exhibited acceptable reliability and validity.

The SLAW-3 demonstrated psychometric properties consistent with a single-factor structure of workplace loneliness. The minimal change in internal consistency when individual items were deleted supports the retention of all items to preserve adequate content coverage, particularly given the brevity of the scale. These findings are consistent with the original Japanese validation study,10 which likewise reported strong internal consistency and a unidimensional structure of the SLAW-3.

The observed moderate test–retest reliability over a 4-week interval for SLAW-1 was acceptable. This suggests that workplace loneliness, as captured by a single-item indicator, is sufficiently stable while remaining responsive to short-term changes in workplace social experiences. While the original study examined only the cross-sectional construct validity of the SLAW-1,10 the present findings extend this by offering evidence of temporal stability.

To assess criterion validity, this study examined the correlations of the SLAW-1 and SLAW-3 scores with general loneliness, building on the work of a previous study11 that reported correlations with workplace-specific loneliness (LAWS-K). This allowed a more direct comparison of how the two brief measures related to both general and workplace-specific loneliness. The SLAW-1 scores correlated more strongly with general loneliness, likely reflecting the similarities in the construct focus and measurement formats of the two measures. In contrast, the SLAW-3 scores correlated more strongly with the LAWS-K, which may reflect their shared emphasis on workplace-specific relational experiences. In a study conducted in Japan, both SLAW-1 and SLAW-3 exhibited strong correlations (r = 0.75) with general loneliness. However, SLAW-3 demonstrated a more pronounced association with social companionship, one component of workplace-specific loneliness.

Recent evidence suggests a low concordance between workplace and non-workplace loneliness (κ = 0.36), indicating that loneliness experienced in the workplace may constitute a distinct contextual construct.23 This broader context supports the interpretation that the two brief measures may differ in the extent to which they reflect general versus workplace-specific loneliness. Accordingly, brief workplace loneliness indicators should not be considered interchangeable, but rather understood as instruments that capture different aspects of loneliness.

In terms of convergent validity, the correlations of SLAW-1 and SLAW-3 with psychological distress and well-being were consistent with those reported in the original study,10 thereby supporting the stability of these relationships across different samples. Furthermore, the overall pattern of correlations—including with occupational stress, psychological distress, work performance, work engagement, and well-being—was very similar to that observed for LAWS-K.11 Although the magnitudes were generally smaller than those of the multi-item scale, this would be anticipated given the narrower content coverage of brief measures. Collectively, our findings endorse the use of SLAW-1 and SLAW-3 as effective indicators of workplace loneliness when comprehensive assessment is not feasible. The subgroup analyses supported the robustness of these findings. Although an earlier Japanese study10 evaluated validity across an entire sample, our present work revealed generally consistent correlations across various sex and age groups, thereby extending the prior evidence on the applicability of SLAW-1 and SLAW-3 to major demographic subgroups.

Notably, SLAW-3 was initially adapted from the three-item UCLA Loneliness Scale by contextualizing the items for the workplace environment. A previous validation study of UCLA-3 established a methodological precedent. Brief unidimensional measures of loneliness maintain meaningful associations with related constructs.13 Building on this, the current findings offer empirical evidence that such measures effectively evaluate loneliness in the workplace.

This study had several strengths. First, it included a relatively large baseline sample of workers across diverse occupational groups. Second, both reliability and multiple forms of validity were examined, including internal consistency; test–retest reliability; and construct, criterion, and convergent validity. Third, the study evaluated both a short multi-item scale and a single-item measure, thereby offering flexible options for different research and surveillance contexts. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the dataset was that of the prior LAWS-K validation study.11 Consequently, the correlations between the LAWS-K and SLAW-1/SLAW-3 findings lack novelty. However, the shared LAWS-K correlations aside, the present study offers psychometric evidence that is applicable only to SLAW-1 and SLAW-3 data. This is relevant in terms of internal consistency, the item-level properties, test–retest reliability, factor loadings, and associations between general loneliness and occupational or psychological variables. Future studies using independent samples are required to further confirm the reliability and validity of the SLAW-1 and SLAW-3 measures. Second, all measures were derived from self-reported data collected via an online panel, which may have introduced common method bias,24 and thus restricted generalizability to employees underrepresented in online survey panels. Third, criterion validity was (in part) assessed using conceptually related loneliness measures, including a single-item indicator of general loneliness, potentially introducing some degree of conceptual overlap in the observed associations. Fourth, a fully standardized translation and cultural adaptation process was not performed, which may have influenced construct interpretation. Fifth, although SLAW-1 demonstrated moderate test–retest reliability and acceptable validity, single-item indicators are inherently limited in terms of content coverage and may capture a construct less comprehensively than do multi-item scales.9 Furthermore, the observed differences between the associations of the SLAW-1 and SLAW-3 scores with general and workplace-specific loneliness should be interpreted with caution, as the indicators differ in terms of both item content and measurement format (single- vs. multi-item), which may have partially influenced the observed patterns. Finally, the test–retest analysis was conducted on only a subsample of participants for the SLAW-1, and further studies with larger longitudinal samples would be beneficial to confirm the temporal stability of these brief measures across different occupational groups and organizational settings.

The choice between SLAW-1 and SLAW-3 involves a trade-off between measurement robustness and survey feasibility. The advantages of SLAW-1 include brevity, a low respondent burden, and simplicity when engaging in large-scale occupational health surveys or workplace screening. However, SLAW-1 is a single-item measure that does not allow assessment of internal consistency and may be less comprehensive than SLAW-3 when evaluating loneliness at work. SLAW-3 offers a broader construct coverage and enables evaluation of internal consistency, but both the survey space and the respondent effort increase to some extent. In short, SLAW-1 may be preferable when feasibility is the primary concern, but SLAW-3 may be better when a more robust psychometric assessment is required.

CONCLUSIONS

Both the SLAW-1 and the SLAW-3 showed acceptable reliability and validity. The SLAW-1 was more closely aligned with general loneliness and the SLAW-3 with workplace-specific loneliness, indicating that the two measures are complementary rather than interchangeable. These brief indicators provide feasible options for workplace loneliness screening and repeated occupational health monitoring in Korea, pending further validation in independent samples.
