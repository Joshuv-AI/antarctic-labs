Introduc on
 Ba ery energy storage system (BESS) deployment in the U.S. has seen remarkable growth
over the past few years. Although the cost of BESSs has received much a en on in DOE-funded
research, the focus has been on capital cost and opera ng cost, while the cost associated with
end-of-life (EOL) management is s ll not well understood.

 In order to improve our stakeholder’s understanding of these costs, the EverBESS model
was created. EverBESS is an open-source model focused on EOL management of BESSs. The model
breaks down BESS EOL management into 3 stages: decommissioning, transporta on, and
recycling. For each stage, the associated costs are es mated. With this understanding,
stakeholders can make more informed decisions when crea ng plans to take responsible
stewardship of EOL BESSs. EverBESS can also beneﬁt developers and future operators of new BESS
projects that are yet to be commissioned by providing a be er idea of the costs they may incur
at the end of the system’s lifespan.

 At its incep on, EverBESS focused on end-of-life management of lithium-ion based BESS.
Since then, EverBESS has expanded to include vanadium redox ﬂow ba ery (VRFB) systems as
well. This documenta on will explicitly point out any diﬀerences between the two system
technologies as it relates to EOL management. The diﬀerences between these two technologies
present primarily in the recycling stage. However, we have added discussions throughout the
report to highlight diﬀerences in the other areas of EOL management. At the me of wri ng this
documenta on, the VRFB expansion is s ll underway and requires valida on. For this reason, in
some cases, the authors men on areas where diﬀerences of VRFB and lithium-ion systems are
present, but these diﬀerences have not yet been modeled in EverBESS.

Decommissioning
 To understand the costs associated with decommissioning, it is important to ﬁrst deﬁne
what is included at this stage. In EverBESS, decommissioning is considered any steps or ac ons
taken on-site to dismantle the BESS and prepare its many components for later stages in the EOL
process. We believe the on-site dis nc on is important to make as there are many EOL steps that
could be accomplished during decommissioning but would be much more eﬃcient and cost-
eﬀec ve if performed oﬀ the BESS site at a capable recycler. For example, some par al
disassembling of ba ery modules may be required to extract the maximum amount of value via
recycling. However, there is no need to start module disassembly in a possibly remote loca on
where the overall cost of labor may be much higher than in a commercial-scale facility at a
recycler. Conversely, not every assump on EverBESS makes regarding what happens during the
decommissioning step needs to happen on-site. For example, EverBESS assumes that modules
are removed from their racks and palle zed before shipping. In the future, it may be feasible to
de-energize a system and transport the en re BESS container without removing ba ery modules,

as long as they are safely disconnected, secured, and follow transporta on regula ons. By
default, the ac vi es listed below are modeled as a part of the decommissioning step in EverBESS:

ba ery modules for safe transport, is a signiﬁcant por on of the total cost of EOL management.
Based on an industry report created by Renewance, EverBESS es mates that it costs 59 cents per

 The remaining costs associated with decommissioning in EverBESS are rental equipment
and the disposal of any generated waste from the project. Rental equipment may include forkli s
or other types of loaders to move heavy system components or pallets of ba ery modules into
trailers for transporta on. Cranes may also need to be rented to li heavy items onto ﬂatbed
trailers. EverBESS users are asked to enter the number of rental days required to complete
decommissioning work as well as the daily rental fee for each equipment type. This cost is then
modeled by simply mul plying rental days by the daily rental fee. By default, the EverBESS model
automa cally adds one day of crane rental at $8,200 per day and two days of forkli rental at

$300 per day. The default rental days and rental costs are sourced from an EPRI report of a BESS
decommissioning [2].

 Disposing of any waste generated from decommissioning is also considered in EverBESS.
For this, users enter the amount of generated waste in metric tons for both landﬁlled and
hazardous waste streams. To model these costs, EverBESS relies on landﬁll pping fee data from
the Environmental Research and Educa on Founda on (EREF) [5]. The EREF report publishes
state-level pping fee data. As is done with the labor rate calcula ons, EverBESS uses the
appropriate landﬁll pping fee for the U.S. state in which the system being modeled is located.
For hazardous waste disposal, the model uses $1,077 per metric ton to es mate costs. The $1,077
per metric ton hazardous material disposal cost is the average of halogenated organic liquid
disposal quotes from Triumvirate Environmental and Clean Harbors.

 The ﬁrst major diﬀerence between lithium-ion and VRFB based BESS in terms of the
decommissioning stage. First, in lithium-ion systems, a ba ery module is self-contained and can
be disconnected and removed from the system for transport. In large BESSs, it is possible for a
system to have several thousand ba ery modules. This represents a signiﬁcant contribu on to
decommissioning labor as every ba ery module, some mes weighing over 200 pounds, must be
individually disconnected, rendered safe, and packaged for transport. For VRFBs, a ba ery
module is not as well-deﬁned. It consists of repea ng cell stacks, which are assemblies of ﬂow-
ﬁeld plates, electrodes, ion-exchange membranes, and spacers in a cell frame, and a liquid
electrolyte conﬁned in tanks with pumping equipment [6]. Decommissioning a VRFB requires the
electrolyte to be pumped out of the system to prepare for transporta on. The costs for this
process and any associated equipment cleaning costs to ensure that the hazardous electrolyte is
moved according to all applicable regula ons are s ll being studied. Currently, EverBESS assumes
four (4) person-hours to decommission and prepare a VRFB ba ery module for transport. One
component that is not modeled in EverBESS at the me of wri ng this documenta on is the
packaging, cleaning, and hazardous waste disposal as a result of removing the liquid electrolyte
and preparing the electrolyte tanks for storage.

Transporta on
 Transporta on is the second of the three major steps to BESS EOL management that is
modeled in EverBESS. The costs from transporta on are signiﬁcant because ba ery energy
storage systems are large and at mes in very remote loca ons. This means that a er completely
tearing down a BESS, mul ple trucks and trailers will be required to take away ba ery modules
and larger system components such as transformers, power conversion systems, and HVAC
systems. The amount of trucks and trailers required depends on the number of each system
component. EverBESS’s default assump ons for the amount of each component type that will ﬁt
on one trailer are as follows: one (1) system container, two (2) transformers, four (4) power

conversion systems, twenty (20) ﬁre suppression systems, or four (4) HVAC units. These values
are conﬁgurable by the user and can be changed to accommodate systems with varying
equipment sizes. EverBESS also assumes that diﬀerent component types are not combined for
transporta on. In other words, components are only transported with like components. The only
excep on is if the user has speciﬁed certain components to remain inside of or a ached to the
system container.

 Transporta on distance is the distance from a BESS site to a recycling loca on. This
distance is what EverBESS uses to es mate cost for transporta on. EverBESS leverages the Open-
Source Rou ng Machine (OSRM) [7] to calculate the exact driving distance between a BESS and a
recycler to provide an accurate measure of the transporta on distance. The EverBESS web
applica on contains a map of the United States with markers for known loca ons of BESS and
ba ery recyclers. Users start by selec ng a BESS from the map as well as a recycler. The ba ery
recycler loca ons are acquired from the NAATBa Lithium-Ion Ba ery Supply Chain Database [8].
This database is developed by the Na onal Laboratory of the Rockies with funding from NAATBa
Interna onal. Because the database is for the majority self-reported, it is not meant to provide
an exhaus ve list of all ba ery recycler loca ons. The loca ons for exis ng BESS come from the
Department of Energy’s Global Energy Storage Database [9]. The full database contains many
diﬀerent technologies of storage systems beyond the scope of the EverBESS model. For this
reason, the dataset has been ﬁltered to include only BESS with a listed technology of lithium-ion,
vanadium ﬂow, or unreported. This dataset is not meant to represent an exhaus ve list of all BESS
loca ons in the United States. Furthermore, users have the ability to specify their own custom
BESS and recycler loca ons for use in the EverBESS model.

transporta on costs.

 Transporta on cost results are derived using unit cost values per distance traveled. For
transporta on costs, the EverBESS model uses a unit cost of $2.11 per mile for nonhazardous
transporta on and a unit cost of $2.30 per mile for hazardous transporta on – which is required
for lithium-ion ba ery modules and ﬂow ba eries. These unit costs are derived from ATRI’s
Opera onal Costs of Trucking report from 2024 [10].

 One diﬀerence that exists in EverBESS between lithium-ion systems and VRFBs is the
terminology of a container and a module. To simplify, in lithium-ion systems, the container houses
many racks of ba ery modules. Modules are removed from their racks and transported as
described earlier in the documenta on. The container, typically a modiﬁed ISO shipping
container, is then transported separately to a recycling facility. For VRFBs, EverBESS deﬁnes
modules and containers to be the same thing. Instead of removing individual modules, a liquid
vanadium electrolyte is pumped out of the modules (or containers) before transporta on. A er
the system is cleaned to remove any remaining electrolyte, which is hazardous, the VRFB module
can be transported for recycling. Due to this diﬀerence, when modeling an EOL VRFB, EverBESS
removes all “container” terminology in favor of the term “module”.

Recycling
 The recycling module of EverBESS that is used depends on the underlying technology of
the BESS being modeled. Currently, EverBESS supports lithium-ion systems based on LFP or NMC

chemistries and vanadium redox ﬂow ba ery systems. For lithium-ion systems, the es mated cost
and revenue results are taken from the EverBa model. The EverBa model, also developed at
Argonne Na onal Laboratory, models closed-loop recycling of lithium-ion ba eries. Relevant
system and module speciﬁca ons in EverBESS are packaged and sent to the EverBa model via
an API. Once the results are returned, the outputs from EverBa are incorporated into the
EverBESS model’s results. For more informa on on lithium-ion ba ery module recycling, please
refer to the EverBa model and its documenta on [11].

 Understanding the recycling of other system components aside from the ba ery modules
is an ac ve area of future work for the EverBESS model. One EPRI report that inves gated BESS
decommissioning quoted recovery values at $0.005 per pound for the scrap metal content of
components such as the power conversion systems, HVAC systems, ba ery racks, BMS, cabling,

model this recycled scrap value. This decision was made to avoid overburdening the user with
model inputs that may be diﬃcult to determine such as the weight of various system components.
Addi onally, we provide no assump ons regarding the weight of system components due to the
high degree of variability in system designs and the wide range of BESS component sizes.

 Electrolyte forms the largest component in VRFBs (Figure 1), accoun ng for ~60% of the
system weight [11, 16]. The VRFB electrolyte can be easily and completely removed from the
tanks at EOL, and vanadium ions are not lost from the system. Thus, recycling the VRFB electrolyte

Furthermore, the electrolyte degrades very li le, unless impuri es are accidentally introduced
into it during maintenance procedures [17]. Thus, the electrolyte has the poten al to be
rebalanced or refurbished to be used in new VRFBs. The reagents used for the process and the
prices of these rebalanced or refurbished electrolytes are protected informa on speciﬁc to the

manufacturers and suppliers and are o en areas of ac ve research and ongoing op miza on.
Hence, for the purpose of the current version of EverBESS, we are considering the compara vely
more well-established procedure of retrieving vanadium pentoxide from the EOL VRFB electrolyte

19]. Through communica ons with the vanadium extrac on and electrolyte manufacturing

 This typical process (Figure 2) involves ﬁrst dilu ng the electrolyte to decrease the ﬁnal
concentra on of vanadium in the electrolyte. This makes the liquid feed compa ble with exis ng
vanadium extrac on and puriﬁca on processes which use mining leach [20]. A suitable oxidizing
agent (here, sodium hypochlorite) is added to convert all dissolved vanadium ions into their

cooled and packaged. All material requirements are calculated based on the stoichiometries of

recycling process using the EverBa framework to es mate process costs for recycling and waste
disposal. As a standard commercial product, vanadium pentoxide provides a market-price
