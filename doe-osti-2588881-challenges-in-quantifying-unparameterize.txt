ABSTRACT
Spatially heterogeneous uncertainties are prevalent in geophysical modeling applications, such as
probabilistic post-closure performance assessment (PA) of deep geologic repositories for nuclear
waste. Such uncertainties are often highly influential to model outputs, so it is desirable to identify
the most important mechanisms by which they influence model predictions. However, these
uncertainties are often unparameterized in the sense that there is no set of parameters that can be
specified to yield a particular realization of the uncertainty. Additionally, the uncertainty is not
intrinsically endowed with a parameterization that captures a realization’s mechanistic influence
on model outputs. Therefore, in this work we present a novel methodology to develop and assess
a set of proxy variables that aim to represent this influence. We show how they can be computed,
downselected, and incorporated into the construction of statistical surrogate models mapping model
inputs to outputs. We present our methodology in the context of a motivating application problem
in deep geologic repository PA and discuss the challenges in capturing the effects of these spatial
heterogeneities in uncertainty analyses.

1. INTRODUCTION

Probabilistic post-closure performance assessment (PA) of systems for deep geologic disposal of
nuclear waste seeks to evaluate the performance of a repository system and provide reasonable
assurance that it will meet relevant requirements for the protection of humans and the environment
over a prolonged period. The intergovernmental Nuclear Energy Agency has an extensive set of
guidance documents on safety assessments [25, 26, 27]. PA is key to the design and approval
of nuclear waste repositories and involves characterization of the many sources and effects of
uncertainty in these systems [12, 30, 6]. Modeled phenomena include: multicomponent, multiphase
flow and transport; heat conduction and convection; biogeochemical reactions; geomechanics, and
isotope decay and ingrowth.
A key challenge recently faced in this application area is the presence of unparameterized heteroge-
neous spatial uncertainties, meaning there is no set of parameters that can be specified to produce
a particular realization of the uncertainty. This can occur, e.g., through the stochastic generation
of heterogeneous subsurface properties. Lack of parameterization for such sources of uncertainty
make them challenging to incorporate into uncertainty analyses typical to PA, especially global
sensitivity analysis (GSA), which measures the importance of different sources of uncertainty to
model outputs. Novel in this work is the development of proxy variables that aim to capture
characteristics of the spatial variability in the subsurface that drive performance of the repository
system. Such proxy variables can enable incorporation of spatial heterogeneity into GSA, as well as
enable incorporation of spatial variability into surrogate models, which require explicit input-output
sample pairs for their construction.
In this report we discuss recent investigations into the efficacy of such proxy variables to rep-
resent spatially heterogeneous sources of uncertainty and diagnostic methods to assess how well
they capture the effects of the spatial heterogeneities on model outputs. The report proceeds as
follows: in Chapter 2, our motivating application problem for deep geologic repository PA in
fractured crystalline rock is introduced; in Chapter 3, an overview of relevant sampling-based and
surrogate-based methods is provided; in Chapter 4, we discuss the development of proxy vari-
ables representing spatially heterogeneous properties and their incorporation into surrogate model
construction; in Chapter 5, results are presented and discussed; conclusions and future work are
discussed in Chapter 6.

2. APPLICATION PROBLEM: NUCLEAR WASTE REPOSITORY
 IN FRACTURED CRYSTALLINE ROCK

In this work we consider a generic nuclear waste repository modeled in a crystalline rock formation,
called the crystalline reference case (CRC). The CRC is introduced and discussed in detail in [23, 38].
It is a demonstration case to build understanding of the performance of nuclear waste repositories
in crystalline rock and is not based on any physical site. We summarize the problem here, with
an illustration of problem domain in Figure 2-1. In the CRC, a repository is placed hundreds
of meters deep in a highly impermeable, fractured rock, where flow is dominated by pathways
along the fractures. Nuclear waste packages placed within the repository region can breach upon
corrosion of the waste package [23]. Upon breach, radionuclides such as 129 I are released from the
waste packages and transported through the surrounding repository regions and host rock. Potential
exposure to humans occurs by radionuclides reaching the biosphere, e.g., groundwater used for
drinking water or crop irrigation. We take as a key repository performance quantity of interest
(QoI) for PA the concentration of 129 I in an aquifer region. We take additional QoIs that shed light
on system properties in the vicinity of the repository and the far field, such as mass flow rates from
the repository and out of the downstream plane of the domain.

Discrete fracture networks (DFNs) are used to represent the fractured host rock. DFNs are networks
of two-dimensional shapes, typically ellipses or rectangles, distributed in a three-dimensional do-
main (or lines distributed in a two-dimensional domain). They are commonly used to simulate
isothermal fluid flow and particle transport in fractured rock and have been used to assess the per-
formance of proposed radiocative waste repositories in crystalline rock at Aspo [33] and Formsark,
Sweden [11]. It is not possible to perfectly characterize heterogeneous subsurface properties, for
example, to identify each fracture in a km3 domain. Instead, DFNs are often modeled statistically
and multiple DFN instantiations are generated to represent uncertainty in the subsurface properties.
Fracture centroids, sizes, and orientations are represented as random variables with their probability
distributions calibrated to agree with observations at site, as in [5]. To create a DFN realization,
fractures are generated by sampling the probability distributions repeatedly until a specified frac-
ture density (𝑃32 ) is reached [15]. The repeated sampling of these statistical models to generate a
single DFN realization means we do not have a single set of parameters that maps to a particular
DFN realization; the DFNs are unparameterized in the previously described sense. The statistical
representation for the DFNs used in the CRC is adapted from properties of the Forsmark site in
Sweden (Table 2, [35]) and is described in detail in [24, 41].
A DFN must be discretized to perform simulations. One approach is to mesh the fractures
directly (fractures are assigned volume through their aperture, often assumed a function of fracture
size [15]) and evolve the governing equations on the meshed DFN. This approach is not amenable
to PA modeling because, unless coupled to a continuum, it cannot simulate heat conduction through
the rock matrix (nor the resulting thermally-driven fluid fluxes), and it cannot simulate coupled
chemical and thermal processes. Since nuclear waste releases a significant amount of heat, this is
problematic for simulations of nuclear waste repository sites.
A desirable alternative to directly meshing the fractures is to derive an equivalent continuous porous
medium (ECPM) through upscaling, wherein a mesh is defined over the entire computational
domain. In cells which contain a fracture, equivalent continuum properties associated with that
fracture are defined; see [34] for the upscaling procedure used here. ECPMs allow the representation
of fractured and background media in the same domain, capture the behavior of radionuclide
transport in fractured rock, and allow simulation of coupled heat and fluid flow, including heat
conduction through the matrix of the fractured rock, while honoring the intrinsic heterogeneity and
anisotropy of the underlying DFN [34, 11]. The process of generating a DFN and mapping it to an
ECPM is depicted in Figure 2-2.
DFN realizations arising from the same statistical representation can result in significantly different
repository performance properties. For the CRC, DFN realizations are a primary source of vari-
ability in repository performance QoIs [39, 37]. However, the lack of a parametric representation
of a DFN makes it challenging to identify the characteristics of a particular DFN realization that
drive performance of the nuclear waste repository. This lack of a parametrization also makes it
challenging to perform global sensitivity analysis or construct surrogate models for UQ. We thus
investigate the development of scalar proxy variables to represent DFN characteristics to enable
more detailed uncertainty analysis with DFNs.
The CRC is simulated using PFLOTRAN, an open-source massively parallel subsurface flow and
reactive transport simulator [20, 21, 9, 10]. PFLOTRAN models subsurface flow using a porous
medium continuum approach, which includes capabilities for multicomponent systems, multiphase

Several QoIs are considered to study the performance of the simulated nuclear waste repository site.
These so-called “performance QoIs” include measures of repository retention, 129 I concentration,

As discussed in detail in [40], a nested sampling structure is employed for the CRC in order to
separate practically reducible and irreducible uncertainties. It will not be feasible to measure and
model the true fracture network at a potential repository site, so it practically remains an irreducible
uncertainty. By employing a nested sampling structure, we can separate and quantify the effects
of “outer loop” DFN uncertainty and “inner loop” parameter uncertainty. Other studies may vary
different types of uncertainty on the inner and outer loops, depending on the problem structure. We
note that an outer sampling loop over epistemic uncertainties and inner sampling loop over aleatory
uncertainties is common but not exactly what is done here. In this study, sampling comprises
an outer loop with 25 DFN realizations and an inner parameter loop with 40 random samples of
all other parameters. The inner-loop random samples differ for each DFN realization in order to
produce more independent samples for surrogate construction. The outer loop also sets a random

4. METHODS

Here we discuss the methods developed and applied to address the challenge of spatial heterogeneity.
First, we discuss the development of scalar proxy variables to capture important characteristics of
heterogeneous spatial uncertainties. The proxy variables will not in general perfectly capture the
influence of these heterogeneous uncertainties, so there will not be a perfect functional mapping
from proxy variable to model output—there will be “noise” in the output as a function of the
proxy variables. Additionally, the proxy variables are derived from random realizations of our
heterogeneous uncertainties, so surrogate construction methods that must specify the location of
input samples cannot be applied. We thus discuss our approach to PCE construction that addresses
this lack of control of input sample locations and the noise in model outputs.

4.1. Proxy variables for heterogeneous spatial uncertainties

In this chapter we describe an approach to account for spatial uncertainties that are not inherently
defined parametrically through the development of scalar “proxy variables”. These proxy variables
aim to capture important characteristics of a random realization of such a spatial uncertainty. The
approach taken to derive these proxy variables will depend on the application problem and the nature
of the spatial uncertainty. In our case, we wish to characterize key properties of a discrete fracture
network realization that might influence repository performance QoIs, namely the 129 I concentration
in the glacial aquifer region. However, in general other quantities might be of interest—for example,
summary statistics such as correlation length, mean, or variance of the random realization. The
proxy variables we pose here are application-problem specific, but the methods to assess the quality
or adequacy of the proxy variables are application-problem agnostic.

As noted in Chapter 2, flow pathways through fractured crystalline rock are predominantly along the
fractures; this has motivated investigations to better understand the network topology of DFNs and
their effect on flow and transport. Recent works have investigated representing DFNs as networks or
graphs (nodes connected by edges), where typically fractures are nodes and intersections between
fractures are edges. Such a graph representation of a DFN preserves the network topology of the
DFNs while enabling an intuitive and computationally tractable means of extracting information
about the network, e.g., the average number of intersections per fracture, the shortest path between
two points in the network, etc. [2]. Several studies have used graph representations to characterize
and study the structure of 2D and 3D DFNs, as well as DFN structure’s effect on flow and transport
properties [2, 13, 42, 14, 7, 32, 8]. Additionally, recent work has shown promise in using the
graphs themselves as models for flow and transport through DFNs [43, 17]. Given this success, we
pursued a graph representation of DFNs here.

It should be noted that the performance assessment model incorporates other coupled physical
processes with flow and transport, so the application of DFN graph representations here is more
complex than previous studies. The DFN interacts with a spatially-resolved repository, and the
DFN is converted to a meshed ECPM for repository simulations. These additional complexities
and couplings manifest in the performance QoIs, which may make it challenging to identify the
topological features of DFN realizations that are most influential.

An illustration of the process by which topological properties are extracted from DFNs is shown in
Figure 4-1. We used the dfnGraph utility in dfnWorks to generate two types of graph representations,
which are described in detail in [43]: “fracture graphs” and “intersection graphs”. In a fracture
graph, each fracture in a DFN is a node, and each intersection between two fractures is an edge
between their nodes. Fracture graphs are useful for computing metrics related to the network
topology, e.g., average degree, which corresponds to the average intersections per fracture. In an
intersection graph, each intersection is a node and each edge is a fracture. Edges are endowed with
geometric and hydrological properties of the fractures they represent, making it possible to compute
metrics related to the flow and transport properties of the DFN, for example, to compute a shortest
travel time from one or more starting nodes to one or more ending nodes in the DFN [43, 17].
Computation of these metrics is implemented in the dfnFlow utility of dfnWorks.

There are challenges associated with incorporating proxy variables in UQ analyses as low-dimensional
representations of more complex, higher-dimensional sources of uncertainty. First, the proxy vari-
ables will likely not perfectly capture the effects of the high-dimensional uncertainty; second, the
proxy variables may be statistically dependent if not chosen carefully. Second, dependence between
variables violates common assumptions in surrogate model construction and global sensitivity anal-
ysis.

Since GSA is of critical interest for PAs, we aim to develop a set of proxy variables which are
independent and capture as much of the effect of the DFNs on repository performance QoIs as
possible. To do so, we performed a preliminary correlation analysis between candidate proxy
variables and repository performance QoIs based on our 1000 input-output samples. Because of
the nested sampling structure of the problem, the same DFN realization was used for 40 inner-
loop random samples. To perform correlation analysis with proxy variables for a given DFN,
performance QoIs were averaged over these 40 inner-loop samples.

4.1.1. Proxy variable selection

Candidate proxy variables were determined based on physics intuition of the properties of a DFN
that might influence repository performance. These candidate variables were:
• number of fractures in the DFN
• number of intersections in the DFN
• number of intersections with the repository region (NIwR)
• average degree of the fracture graph (avgDegree, average number of intersections per fracture)
• density of the fracture graph (global connectivity of the network)
• log relative shortest travel time between repository and aquifer regions (logRSTT, the orders-
  of-magnitude deviation of the shortest travel time between repository and aquifer relative to the
  median).
In addition to the graph metrics described herein, proxy variables derived from the ECPMs as-
sociated with each DFN were computed, namely the means of continuum properties (tortuosity,
porosity, and log-base-10-transformed permeability) computed over all cells in the mesh.
Correlation coefficients can serve as a preliminary screening tool to downselect proxy variables.
Correlations between candidate proxy variables and repository performance QoIs are reported in
Figure 4-2. For this problem we observe that NIwR, avgDegree, and logRSTT exhibit significant
correlations (magnitude ≥ 0.32 → 𝑅 2 ⪆ 0.1) with the most QoIs. Additionally, density, number
of intersections, and mean log10 permeability have a significant correlation coefficient for at least
one QoI.
Beyond correlations between proxy variables and QoIs, it is important to consider correlations
between the variables. Variance-based sensitivity analysis methods like Sobol’ indices are under-
pinned by assumptions of independence between input variables. Without this independence, it
becomes challenging to interpret the indices. We thus additionally screen for correlations between
the proxy variables, removing any variables that are significantly correlated with others. The corre-
lation coefficients between the proxy variables that are correlated with at least one QoI are presented
in Figure 4-3. In this case, the density, number of intersections, and mean log10 permeability proxy
variables exhibit significant correlation with the proxy variables that correlated with the most QoIs:
average degree, NIwR, and logRSTT.
To avoid correlations between proxy variables while maintaining a set of variables which are
influential for the majority of Qois, we thus downselect to the most influential proxy variables
which are relatively mutually independent: avgDegree, NIwR, and logRSTT. The magnitude of
correlations between these proxy variables is no greater than 0.1. We deem this sufficient to treat
the proxy variables as independent for the purposes of VBSA.
We downselected to a single set of proxy variables for PCE-based VBSA of all QoIs for the sake of
simplicity in our analyses, but different proxy variables could have been chosen for different QoIs.
For example, the mass flow rate ratio between 𝑅𝑜𝑐𝑘 𝐴𝑞 and 𝑅𝑜𝑐𝑘 𝐸 𝑏 (rock-to-aquifer and rock-
to-east-boundary) was most strongly correlated with the number of intersections proxy variable.
Future analyses could consider selecting proxy variables for each QoI.

4.1.2. Incorporating proxy variables in PCE construction

As discussed in Section 3.1, PCEs are stochastic expansions with basis functions defined to be
orthonormal with respect to the probability densities of the uncertain inputs to the expansion.
Thus, to incorporate proxy variables into a PCE surrogate, we must endow the proxy variables with
analytical probability density functions (PDFs). To do so, we generated 1080 DFN realizations,
computed their corresponding proxy variables, then fit analytical PDFs to the proxy variable
samples. This is feasible since generating DFN realizations is not a significant computational cost
relative to running the repository model.

The appropriate PDF for each proxy variable was selected by visual inspection of their histograms,
then PDF hyperparameters were fit computationally. Fitted normal PDFs for the proxy variables are
shown in Figure 4-4. In this case visual inspection was sufficient to determine the appropriate PDF,
but this may not hold in general. In the future, it may be necessary to develop a framework that
automatically tests a range of transformations, scalings, and density types to find optimal analytical
distributions for the proxy variables.

4.2. Regression PCE approach

Our PCE surrogate construction approach takes into account the fact that (1) proxy variables cannot
be specified by a surrogate construction algorithm but must be derived from a DFN realization
and (2) the “noise” in repository performance QoIs as a function of proxy variables due to their
imperfect/incomplete representation of spatial heterogeneity from the DFNs. To address (1), we
employ a regression PCE approach as discussed in Section 3.1, which solves for the coefficients
of the PCE given input-output samples. When the collocation ratio exceeds 1, standard regression
is used to fit PCE coefficients; below 1, LASSO is used. To address (2), we investigate the use of
K-fold cross-validation to select the PCE order that minimizes cross validation error, mitigating the
chance of overfitting to “noise” in the outputs. 10-fold cross-validation is employed when doing so
would not reduce the collocation ratio below 1, else one-at-a-time cross validation is performed.

4.3. Accuracy checks

We employ multiple diagnostics to assess the quality of our results. To assess the goodness of fit
of our surrogate, we compute the 𝑅 2 of the surrogate over build points and generate scatterplots
comparing build points to PCE predictions at those points. Additionally, we corroborate our PCE-
based Sobol’ indices by comparing them to the given-data main effects estimators. As a measure
of efficacy of the proxy variables to represent the influence of spatially heterogeneous uncertainty
from DFNs, we compare the empirical Sobol’ index for DFN realization to the grouped PCE-based
Sobol’ index for the group of proxy variables. Finally, and perhaps most importantly, we cross-
reference our Sobol’ indices with input-output scatterplots to verify whether we observe similar
trends.

If we were to only judge the quality of the PCE surrogates based on scatterplots and 𝑅 2 values,

repository performance QoIs, but further investigations would be required to test this hypothesis.
While we observe this effect for a 3𝑟 𝑑 -order PCE in this case, we emphasize that the best choice of
surrogate model order, with or without cross-validation, will be problem dependent. However, our
approach to assessing the accuracy of the surrogate can be applied in general.
Note that all the PCEs allow for negative 129 I concentrations, which is nonphysical. As shown
in Figure 5-3, while the PCE allows for negative values, most samples are positive, and the density
of the 129 I QoI agrees relatively well with the samples from the PFLOTRAN repository model.
To eliminate this effect, one could construct the PCE on log-transformed concentration samples.
However, because we would be introducing a nonlinear transformation of the output in doing this,
the Sobol’ indices computed using the coefficients of the PCE would not be representative of the
sensitivities for the untransformed concentrations. It would be possible to compute sample-based
indices by querying the PCE and exponentiating its prediction to obtain untransformed 129 I samples,
but exponentiation may further pronounce inaccuracies in the PCE surrogate. Since the PCE and
PFLOTRAN-based probability densities agree relatively well, and since the corresponding Sobol’
indices agree, we do not investigate methods to impose positivity on the surrogate model herein.

These results demonstrate that typical surrogate quality metrics like 𝑅 2 and methods to control for
overfitting like cross validation are not adequate when incorporating proxy variables that approx-
imate the influence of unparameterized sources of uncertainty, like the DFNs in this application
problem. Comparing to sensitivity indices computed directly using input-output samples from our
simulation model was critical to assessing the quality of our surrogate models. Finally, comparing
to input-output scatterplots is a simple and highly important analysis step to corroborate sensitivity
indices, whether sample- or surrogate-based. By combined assessment of scatterplots, 𝑅 2 , and
comparison of sensitivity indices in Figure 5-2, the 2𝑛𝑑 order PCE with cross-validation appears to
achieve the best accuracy overall. However, with an 𝑅 2 of only 0.57 for the peak 129 I QoI, it is clear
that the fit is suboptimal. The surrogate could be further improved by seeking proxy variables that
more accurately capture the influence of spatial heterogeneity on repository performance QoIs.

6. CONCLUSIONS

Unparametrized spatially heterogeneous uncertainties often arise in geophysical application prob-
lems, e.g., probabilistic post-closure performance assessment for deep geologic repositories for
nuclear waste. These spatially heterogeneous uncertainties are often a significant, even dominant,
source of uncertainty in model predictions. However, it is not straightforward to incorporate them
into uncertainty analyses, especially sensitivity analyses, which require uncertainty sources to be
parametrized. It is thus desirable to develop proxy variables that capture the mechanisms by which
these uncertainties influence model outputs, so that they can be incorporated into uncertainty
analyses, especially sensitivity analysis.
In this work we present methods by which such proxy variables can be developed, selected, and
incorporated into surrogate models for global sensitivity analysis. We also demonstrate how
surrogates constructed using these proxy variables can be assessed for accuracy.
In applying these methods to our nuclear waste repository application problem, understanding
and separating the effect of spatial heterogeneity on repository QoIs has proven challenging,
especially with the limited number of DFNs and parameter samples that can be run for realistic
problem sizes. Identifying predictive proxy variables that adequately capture the influence of
the spatially heterogeneous uncertainties is nontrivial. Additionally, we demonstrate that typical
methods to assess surrogate accuracy and control overfitting (such as scatterplots, 𝑅 2 values, and
cross-validation) are not sufficient when incorporating proxy variables that capture some, but not
all, of the impacts spatially heterogeneous uncertainties have on outputs. We found that additional
comparison of surrogate-based sensitivity indices to binning-based indices computed from input-
output samples directly was important to ensure the surrogate was appropriately capturing model
dependencies. Additionally, we found that comparison of binning- and surrogate-based indices to
input-output scatterplots is a critical analysis step to corroborate the dependencies identified by
sensitivity indices.
There is still significant work required to develop general methods for extracting accurate proxy
variables from unparametrized spatially heterogeneous uncertainties. Data-driven methods like
variational autoencoders, which map high-dimensional uncertain inputs to low-dimensional latent
embeddings, pose an opportunity to identify more nuanced proxy variables than those developed
herein. Finally, further work could be done to develop a more nuanced approach to downselect
proxy variables for individual QoIs, in contrast to the uniform downselect performed here.
