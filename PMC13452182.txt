It is well established that a substantial proportion of patients with both dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) harbor a genetic cause for their disease. The percentage of genotype-positive patients (those harboring a likely pathogenic or pathogenic variant) varies significantly across studies depending on numerous factors, including the criteria used to define the cardiomyopathy, age of onset, disease duration, disease severity, genetic ancestry, and the number of genes in the diagnostic panel. However, in ambulatory cohorts, the percentage of genotype-positive patients is approximately 20% to 35% in DCM1,2 and 45% in HCM.3

Given the association between genotype-positivity and adverse outcomes, it is unsurprising that patients with advanced heart failure (AHF), those requiring left ventricular assist device implantation or heart transplantation, are more likely to be genotype-positive. Despite these benefits, genetic testing in cardiomyopathies remains woefully underutilized, although prior studies have not specifically focused on genetic testing uptake in the AHF population. Why does this gap in care persist? Skeptics might argue that genetic testing is underutilized because it does not meaningfully alter clinical outcomes—at least with respect to the AHF proband whose disease has already progressed. This is precisely why the findings presented by Moliner-Abós et al8 in this issue of JACC: Advances are so welcome; their voices join a growing chorus demonstrating how genetic testing tangibly impacts clinical management.

In this multicenter retrospective study spanning 12 Spanish transplant centers, the authors investigated the clinical utility of genetic testing in 657 adult heart transplant recipients with cardiomyopathy. They discovered pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 53% of the cohort: 46% in probands with DCM and 68% in probands with HCM. Notably, this testing led to a reclassification of cardiomyopathy cause, mostly from idiopathic to genetic, for 35% of the patients—a figure that rose to 42% when testing was performed post–heart transplantation. The study also revealed an important insight regarding “second-hit” cardiomyopathies: 36% of cases initially attributed to acquired causes, such as myocarditis or chemotherapy exposure, were genotype-positive. Beyond individual diagnoses, these findings proved highly actionable, prompting family cascade screening in 69% of affected families and successfully identifying at-risk relatives in both genotype-positive (42%) and genotype-negative (22%) families.

The authors also demonstrated that the genetic architecture of AHF differs from that of ambulatory cohorts. Although TTN truncating variants remained the most frequently identified, their prevalence was significantly lower (25%) than typically seen in stable outpatients. Conversely, there was an over-representation of arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy genes including LMNA, FLNC, and EMD, the latter reflecting a specific founder effect within the Spanish population.9 The authors also noted a clear shift in genetic testing uptake over their 13-year study period, with pretransplant genetic testing rates rising substantially from 12% in 2010 to 2014 to 68% in 2020 to 2023.

Moliner-Abós et al8 should be congratulated for a carefully executed study demonstrating that genetic testing in AHF yields clinically actionable results, thus supporting the premise that genetic testing should be systematically offered to all patients with cardiomyopathy and AHF. Nevertheless, several limitations merit discussion. First and foremost, the authors observed a very high percentage of genotype-positive patients, likely due to the European genetic ancestry of the study population. It is well established that non-European populations have lower percentages of genotype-positive patients and accordingly higher percentages of patients with variants of uncertain significance.1 How this translates to other genetic ancestries is uncertain and warrants further investigation. Next, the sequencing methodologies and genes tested were not uniform between centers, leading to possible discrepancies. Lastly, only patients with cardiomyopathies were included, and as such patients with mixed or ischemic AHF, who have lower but not insignificant frequencies of genotype positivity,10 would be missed. Future prospective studies addressing these demographic and methodological constraints will be essential.

The call for widespread genetic testing is a song the cardiology community must finally hear. Moliner-Abós et al8 have shown us exactly “what we are missing” when we fail to perform genetic testing in patients with AHF: unrecognized genetic etiologies, misclassified diagnoses, and lost opportunities to protect families. It is time to “sing along” and ensure that systematic genetic testing becomes part of the standard rhythm of cardiomyopathy care.
