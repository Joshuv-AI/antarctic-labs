Non-asymptotic bounds for quasi-MLE, misspecified models, and dependence under group sequential sampling
Julian Aronowitz, Jay Bartroff

Abstract
We derive asymptotic multivariate normal limits and explicit non-asymptotic normal approximation bounds for group sequential quasi-maximum likelihood estimators under possible model misspecification and within-group dependence. The bounds, obtained using Stein’s method, have known constants and apply to a class of dependent-data estimating problems in which the likelihood used for estimation may differ from the true data-generating mechanism. We compute the limiting covariance structure and finite-sample bound explicitly for a Poisson generalized linear mixed model with random group effects and illustrate the results using data from an epilepsy clinical trial.



1

Introduction and background

Group sequential analysis is a powerful statistical framework in which a fixed sample size is not pre-determined before conducting a study, but rather data is collected and analyzed in groups until the conditions of a pre-defined stopping criterion are met. Group sequential methods are the dominant statistical methodology in many modern clinical trials
(
Jennison and Turnbull, 2000
;
Bartroff et al., 2013
)
where they can provide ethical, administrative, and economic benefits, among other areas of application including
online A/B testing
(
Kohavi et al., 2020
)
, genomics and high-throughput biology
(
Lin et al., 2022
)
, and machine and reinforcement learning
(
Powell, 2022
)
. Critical to providing these benefits is the early stopping criterion, which in the most commonly used parametric settings is based on maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs), repeatedly computed at each interim analysis, and thus whose joint distribution determines the operating characteristics of the trial.

Suppose sequential observations
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
are divided into
K
groups with
n_{k}
denoting the number of observations up to and including group
k=1,\dots,K
, and
n_{K}=n
. If
\widehat{\theta}_{n_{k}}=\widehat{\theta}_{n_{k}}(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n_{k}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the MLE of the parameter vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of interest based on the observations in the first
k
groups, then the distribution of the concatenated vector

\widehat{\theta}^{K}=[\widehat{\theta}_{n_{1}};\widehat{\theta}_{n_{2}};\dots;\widehat{\theta}_{n_{K}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{dK}

(1)

is the key object in parametric group sequential methods. In (
1
) and throughout this paper we use the semicolon notation

[v_{1};\ldots;v_{K}]\in\mathbb{R}^{dK}

(2)

to denote the concatenation of column vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
into a single
\mathbb{R}^{dK}
-valued column vector.

Under the assumption of independent but not necessarily identically distributed observations,
Jennison and Turnbull, 1997
showed that
\widehat{\theta}^{K}
is asymptotically multivariate normal under suitable regularity conditions, and
Aronowitz and Bartroff, 2025
provide non-asymptotic bounds to this normal limit. Although these results do not require identically distributed observations
Y_{i}
, they do rely on independence of the
Y_{i}
which has been a barrier to addressing statistical situations with dependence between observations such as correlated observations, random effects and mixed models, regularized estimators, and possibly misspecified models. About this,
Jennison and Turnbull, 1997
write,

“The general theory can also be extended to studies with correlated observations by replacing the distribution
f_{i}(Y_{i};\theta)
with the conditional distribution of
Y_{i}
given
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{i-1}
and defining efficient scores…The conclusion
…remains valid if it is permissible to apply a WLLN to sums of conditional information and a central limit theorem to sums of conditional scores to deduce the conditions required.”

We are not aware of such an extension to correlated observations having been carried out in the literature in any general setting.
Correlated observations are considered by
Jennison and Turnbull, 2000
but only in the context of normal linear models; in this case the group sequential MLE is exactly multivariate normal and no asymptotic theory is required. A survey of results regarding group sequential analysis of specific longitudinal models is presented in
Jennison and Turnbull, 2000
.

The purpose of this paper is to develop a theory covering asymptotic normality results as well as nonasymptotic bounds to these limits for group sequential MLEs in a setting that allows dependence within groups. Examples of this include cluster-randomized trials and multicenter studies with site effects; in these settings, the i.i.d. assumption is inappropriate at the observation level, but independence may still be plausible at the group (e.g., cluster or site) level. In doing so we additionally address misspecified models and so-called quasi maximum likelihood estimators (QMLEs). In the statistics literature, the term “quasi-likelihood” has been used in related yet distinct meanings. In the setting of
McCullagh, 1983
and
McCulloch et al., 2011
, a quasi-likelihood is a function that describes the relationship between the mean and variance of observed random variables without specifying the full distribution of the data generating process. Optimizing this “variance” function produces an estimator that has similar properties to the MLE. The second usage of quasi-likelihood is the context originated by
White, 1982
;
White, 1996
, who characterized a quasi-likelihood function as one essentially equivalent to the true likelihood except for the fact that there may not be a parameter in the parameter space for which the quasi-likelihood correctly specifies the data generating process of interest, a situation known as model misspecification. The QMLE is then the parameter in the parameter space that maximizes the quasi-likelihood given the observed data. This paper is concerned with this latter meaning of quasi-likelihood.

White, 1996
showed asymptotic normality of QMLEs in the fixed sample setting: Under regularity conditions the QMLE
\widehat{\theta}
is consistent for some
\theta^{*}
that minimizes the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence (see (
4
)) from the true data generating process
g(y)
to the quasi-likelihood
f(y|\theta)
, and that the QMLE is asymptotically normal around
\theta^{*}
. Our Theorem
2.1
extends and generalizes this result to find the asymptotic distribution of QMLEs in the group sequential setting, and Theorem
2.2
provides a finite-sample bound, with explicit constants, for QMLEs based on dependent data. By taking the specified density
f
to assume independence while the true density
g
does not, we construct misspecified although consistent QMLE estimators that are asymptotically normal, and Theorem
2.2
quantifies the effect of this misspecified dependence on the normal limit. To give an example of how one might apply Theorem
2.2
, in Section
3
we apply these results to the group sequential analysis of a Poisson generalized linear mixed model, and in Section
4
compute the bound numerically using data from an epilepsy clinical trial.

The closest antecedent in the non-asymptotic MLE literature is
Anastasiou and Reinert, 2017
, who obtained explicit Stein bounds for fixed-sample MLEs. Our results extend this line in three directions that arise naturally in sequential statistical problems: vector-valued group sequential estimators, model misspecification through QMLEs, and dependence within sampling groups. This places the paper at the intersection of Stein-type normal approximation, sequential inference, and asymptotic theory for dependent statistical models.

1.1

Other related literature

The non-asymptotic bound for the univariate MLE first developed by
Anastasiou and Reinert, 2017
was expanded to the multivariate case by
Anastasiou, 2018
. In
Anastasiou and Ley, 2017
and
Anastasiou and Gaunt, 2020
the bounds for the univariate and multivariate cases, respectively, were sharpened and simplified under the additional assumption that the MLE follows a certain additive form.
Anastasiou, 2017
considers data that is
m
-dependent for identically distributed scalar parameter MLEs. However, our consideration of QMLE and misspecified models is new in this literature, and our results extend the recent work of
Aronowitz and Bartroff, 2025
for correctly specified models. All of these results mentioned so far utilize Stein’s
(
Stein, 1972
)
method for the normal distribution, for which we refer the reader to
Chen et al., 2010
for an introduction. Results not based on Stein’s method include Pinelis’
(
Pinelis, 2017
)
optimal order bound for univariate MLEs in terms of the Kolmogorov distance, and bounds derived using characteristic functions
(
Ulyanov, 1979
;
Ulyanov, 1986
;
Ulyanov, 1987
, see)
. These results require independence and apply only to the univariate setting, and thus are not considered for extension to the setting considered here. The convergence of statistics after stopping a group sequential trials has been considered by B. Berckmoes and coauthors
(
Berckmoes et al., 2018
;
Berckmoes, 2018
;
Berckmoes et al., 2020
)
. Regarding dependence, our results require a slightly stronger assumption than conjectured in the quote above, namely we allow dependence within groups but require independence between groups. Under this condition, the “independent increments” structure of the group sequential analysis is preserved
(
Spiessens et al., 2000
;
Kim and Tsiatis, 2020
;
Parast and Bartroff, 2024
, see)
.

QMLEs and dependent data

In Section
2.1

(

2.1

Asymptotic distribution: Notation and White’s
(
White, 1996
)
fixed-sample result

We begin in the fixed-sample setting in which the sample size denoted by
n
, which will approach infinity in the asymptotic results below. Letting
Y^{n}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
,
Y^{0}=\emptyset
, and similar for lowercase arguments
y^{n}
, we let
g^{n}(y^{n})
denote the joint density of the true, unknown data generating process, and let

f^{n}(y^{n}|\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(y_{i}|y^{i-1},\theta),\quad\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},

(3)

denote a postulated family of parametric models for
Y^{n}
. Mutual independence of
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
is
not
assumed in
g^{n}
or
f^{n}
, as the conditional notation in (
3
) reflects. We treat a particular

as an approximation of the true
g^{n}
whose accuracy is measured by the KL divergence

\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\frac{g^{n}(Y^{n})}{f^{n}(Y^{n}|\theta)}\right)\right]

(4)

between
g^{n}
and
f^{n}(\cdot|\theta)
. In (
4
) and throughout, the expectation is taken with respect to
Y^{n}\sim g^{n}
, the true data generating process. Minimizing the KL divergence (
4
) with respect to
\theta
is equivalent to choosing the
\theta
that maximizes the expected log-likelihood, i.e.,

\theta_{n}^{*}=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}\left[{\log f^{n}(Y^{n}|\theta)}\right].

(5)

If there exists
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
such that
f^{n}(Y^{n}|\theta_{0})=g^{n}(Y^{n})
a.s. then we say that
f^{n}
is
correctly specified
. In this case, the minimizer
\theta_{n}^{*}
of the KL divergence is equal to the true parameter value
\theta_{0}
. Since the expectation (
5
)
is with respect to the unknown
g^{n}
, computing
\theta_{n}^{*}
directly is not an option available to the statistician. A natural alternative is the empirical version of (
5
),

\widehat{\theta}_{n}=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}n^{-1}\log f^{n}(Y^{n}|\theta)=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f_{i}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1},\theta),

(6)

which we call the
quasi-maximum likelihood estimator (QMLE)
of
\theta
.

White, 1996
showed that, under proper regularity conditions, the QMLE exhibits similar asymptotic normality properties to the MLE, but centered at
\theta_{n}^{*}
. Specifically, that

\sqrt{n}J^{-1/2}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{n}^{*})\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}_{d})\quad\mbox{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty,

(7)

denoting convergence in distribution to the
d
-dimensional multivariate normal with mean vector
0
and covariance matrix the
d\times d
identity matrix
\mathcal{I}_{d}
, for a certain matrix
J=J(\theta_{n}^{*})
whose group sequential generalization is given in Theorem
2.1
. The needed regularity conditions for (
7
) are listed in Appendix
A
, and are the same as those we will require for our group sequential generalization of (
7
) in Theorem
2.1
.

3.1

Set up

Consider the regression setting where the
i

has associated covariate vector

Y_{i}\mid U_{k}\sim p(\cdot|\exp(x_{i}^{T}\theta+U_{k}))\quad\mbox{for}\quad i\in G_{k}.

(27)

We further assume that the random effects

We consider a QMLE set up in which the postulated density

\prod_{k,i}

\prod_{k\in[K]}\prod_{i\in G_{k}}

\sum_{k,i}

\displaystyle f^{n_{K}}(y^{n_{K}}|\theta)

(29)

\displaystyle\widetilde{g}_{k}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}\in G_{k}}e^{x_{i^{\prime}}^{T}\theta}\right)\int\exp\left[\sum_{i\in G_{k}}(y_{i}u-e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta}e^{u})\right]\pi(u|\psi)\nu(du).

(30)

McCulloch et al., 2011
note that (
30
), and hence (
28
), cannot be simplified further or evaluated in closed form, even if
U_{k}
is normally distributed. For just computing the MLE of
\theta
, numerical integration of (
28
) is typically used; other techniques for estimating
\theta
are discussed in
Jiang, 1999
. But for more refined analysis such as that required to compute non-asymptotic bounds, the within-group dependence due to the random effect makes direct analysis of (
28
) difficult. For example, the resulting score function is not simply the sum of the scores of the observations. For these reasons, we next proceed to consider the QMLE approach using
f(\cdot|\theta)
in (
29
) as the postulated density family, assuming the true density is
g(\cdot|\theta_{0})
given by (
28
) for some true, unknown value
\theta_{0}
.

3.2

Asymptotic distribution of QMLE for Poisson GLMM

In this section we apply Theorem
2.1
to the Poisson GLMM to obtain the group sequential asymptotic distribution. Our results apply to the fixed sample-
n
set up as well by taking
K=1
and
n_{K}=n
, which has not appeared in the literature as far as we know. While many references
(
Breslow and Clayton, 1993
;
McCulloch and Neuhaus, 2012
;
Wang et al., 2022
, e.g.,)
discuss the Poisson GLMM, none of these compute explicitly the Hessian and information matrices in our setting. In doing so we will see that the value
\theta_{n_{K}}^{*}
around which the asymptotic distribution concentrates is the true value
\theta_{0}
, scaled by the factor
\mathbb{E}e^{U}
determined by the distribution of the random effects. In other words, if

\mathbb{E}e^{U}=1,

(31)

then
\theta^{*}=\theta_{0}
. Thus, to simplify the notation in what follows, we assume (
31
) throughout. Otherwise, our analysis can be carried out with additional notational load incorporating the scaling factor of
\mathbb{E}e^{U}
or, equivalently, fixing the offset in the Poisson regression model (
27
). After verifying
\theta_{n_{K}}^{*}=\theta_{0}
, we calculate the Hessian and information matrices
H_{k}
and
I_{k}
in (
12
)-(
13
), before applying the non-asymptotic bounds of Theorem
2.2
in the next section.

Regarding the moments of
Y_{i}
, it is well-known
(
Riordan, 1937
)
that the
m
th moment (
m\geq 1
) of a Poisson random variable with mean
\lambda
is given by the polynomial in
\lambda

\sum_{j=1}^{m}{m\brace j}\lambda^{j},\quad\mbox{where}\quad{m\brace j}=\frac{1}{j!}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}{j\choose\ell}(-1)^{j-\ell}\ell^{m}

(32)

are the Stirling numbers of the second kind
(see
van Lint and Wilson, 2001
, p. 125)
; special cases include
{m\brace 1}={m\brace m}=1
, and
{m\brace j}=0
for
j>m
. Applying this to
Y_{i}
, conditionally on
U_{k}
for
i\in G_{k}
, we have

\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}^{m})=\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{m\brace j}(e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta_{0}+U_{k}})^{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{m\brace j}e^{jx_{i}^{T}\theta_{0}}\mathbb{E}e^{jU_{k}}\\
=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{m\brace j}e^{jx_{i}^{T}\theta_{0}}M_{U}(j),

(33)

where
M_{U}(j)=\mathbb{E}e^{jU}
is the moment generating function (MGF) of
U
. Using (
33
) and
M_{U}(1)=1
,

\displaystyle E(Y_{i})

\displaystyle=e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta_{0}},

(34)

\displaystyle E(Y_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle=e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta_{0}}+e^{2x_{i}^{T}\theta_{0}}M_{U}(2).

(35)

Using (
34
), recall from (
5
) that
\theta_{n_{K}}^{*}
maximizes

\mathbb{E}[\log f^{n_{K}}(Y^{n_{K}};\theta)]=\sum_{k,i}\mathbb{E}[\log p(Y_{i}|e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta})]=\sum_{k,i}\left(-e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta}+x_{i}^{T}\theta\mathbb{E}Y_{i}\right)+C\\
=\sum_{k,i}\left(-e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta}+x_{i}^{T}\theta e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta_{0}}\right)+C,

(36)

where
C
does not depend on
\theta
. The gradient of (
36
) with respect to
\theta
is

\sum_{k,i}(e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta_{0}}-e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta})x_{i}
, and it follows from the full-rank assumption on the
x_{i}
that this vanishes if and only if
\theta=\theta_{0}
. Therefore, in what follows we use
\theta_{0}
in place of
\theta_{n_{K}}^{*}
, and let

e_{i}=e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta_{0}},\quad i\in[n_{K}],

(37)

throughout this and the next section.

We have

S(i,\theta)=\nabla\log p(Y_{i}|e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta})=(Y_{i}-e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta})x_{i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla S(i,\theta)=-e^{x_{i}^{T}\theta}x_{i}x_{i}^{T},

(38)

so the Hessian (
12
) is

H_{k}(\theta_{0})=\frac{1}{n_{K}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}e_{i}x_{i}x_{i}^{T}.

(39)

To compute the cumulative information matrix (
13
), we first compute the group-wise version (
17
). Note that

\mathbb{E}[S(i,\theta_{0})]=0

(40)

which follows from (
34
) and (
38
). Then

n_{K}\widetilde{I}_{k}(\theta_{0})=\mbox{Var}\left[\sum_{i\in G_{k}}S(i,\theta_{0})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in G_{k}}S(i,\theta_{0})\right]\left[\sum_{j\in G_{k}}S(j,\theta_{0})\right]^{T}\\
=\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[S(i,\theta_{0})S(i,\theta_{0})^{T}\right]+\sum_{i\neq j\in G_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[S(i,\theta_{0})S(j,\theta_{0})^{T}\right]\\
=\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{i}-e_{i})^{2}\right]x_{i}x_{i}^{T}+\sum_{i\neq j\in G_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{i}-e_{i})(Y_{j}-e_{j})\right]x_{i}x_{j}^{T}\\
=\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\mbox{Var}(Y_{i})x_{i}x_{i}^{T}+\sum_{i\neq j\in G_{k}}\mbox{Cov}(Y_{i},Y_{j})x_{i}x_{j}^{T}.

(41)

For
i\neq j\in G_{k}
, using conditional independence,

\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}Y_{j}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}Y_{j}|U_{k}]]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|U_{k}]\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}|U_{k}]]=\mathbb{E}[(e_{i}e^{U_{k}})(e_{j}e^{U_{k}})]\\
=e_{i}e_{j}\mathbb{E}[e^{2U_{k}}]=e_{i}e_{j}M_{U}(2).

Using (
34
), (
35
), and this last,

\displaystyle\mbox{Var}(Y_{i})

\displaystyle=e_{i}[1+e_{i}(M_{U}(2)-1)],

\displaystyle\mbox{Cov}(Y_{i},Y_{j})

\displaystyle=e_{i}e_{j}(M_{U}(2)-1)\quad\mbox{for}\quad i\neq j\in G_{k}.

Plugging these into (
41
) and writing
M_{U}(2)-1=\mbox{Var}(e^{U})
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}_{k}(\theta_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{K}}\left\{\sum_{i\in G_{k}}e_{i}[1+e_{i}\mbox{Var}(e^{U})]x_{i}x_{i}^{T}+\mbox{Var}(e^{U})\sum_{i\neq j\in G_{k}}e_{i}e_{j}x_{i}x_{j}^{T}\right\},

(42)

\displaystyle I_{k}(\theta_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}\leq k}\widetilde{I}_{k^{\prime}}(\theta_{0})

(43)

by independence between groups.

Comparing (
39
) and (
42
) we see that these correspond – i.e., the Information Matrix Equality (
14
) holds – when
\mbox{Var}(e^{U})=0
, which with (
31
) imply that
U=0
a.s. In this case the model (
27
) reduces to the postulated density
f
and is thus correctly specified.

3.3

Non-asymptotic bounds

In this section we calculate the quantities needed to apply the bounds in Theorem
2.2
to the Poisson GLMM (
27
). By the discussion following (
36
), we use
\theta_{0}
in place of
\theta_{n_{K}}^{*}
throughout.

Here, by (
38
), the variate
\xi_{i}
appearing in
R_{2}
and
R_{3}
is

\xi_{i}=n_{K}^{-1/2}S(i,\theta_{0})=n_{K}^{-1/2}(Y_{i}-e_{i})x_{i}

and
\mathbb{E}\xi_{i}=0
by (
40
).

We start with the second variance term in
R_{2}
, which we compute using the central moments of the Poisson conditionally:

n_{K}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{v\in G_{k}}\xi_{vi}\xi_{vj}\right)^{2}\\
=\sum_{v\in G_{k}}x_{vi}^{2}x_{vj}^{2}\mathbb{E}(Y_{v}-e_{v})^{4}+2\sum_{v<v^{\prime}\in G_{k}}x_{vi}x_{vj}x_{v^{\prime}i}x_{v^{\prime}j}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{v}-e_{v})^{2}(Y_{v^{\prime}}-e_{v^{\prime}})^{2}]\\
=\sum_{v\in G_{k}}x_{vi}^{2}x_{vj}^{2}\mathbb{E}[3(e_{v}e^{U_{k}})^{2}+e_{v}e^{U_{k}}]+2\sum_{v<v^{\prime}\in G_{k}}x_{vi}x_{vj}x_{v^{\prime}i}x_{v^{\prime}j}\mathbb{E}[(e_{v}e^{U_{k}})(e_{v^{\prime}}e^{U_{k}})]\\
=\sum_{v\in G_{k}}x_{vi}^{2}x_{vj}^{2}[3e_{v}^{2}M_{U}(2)+e_{v}]+2\sum_{v<v^{\prime}\in G_{k}}x_{vi}x_{vj}x_{v^{\prime}i}x_{v^{\prime}j}e_{v}e_{v^{\prime}}M_{U}(2).

Similarly,

n_{K}^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\sum_{v\in G_{k}}\xi_{vi}\xi_{vj}\right)^{2}=\left(\sum_{v\in G_{k}}x_{vi}x_{vj}\mathbb{E}(Y_{v}-e_{v})^{2}\right)^{2}=\left(\sum_{v\in G_{k}}x_{vi}x_{vj}e_{v}\right)^{2}\\
=\sum_{v\in G_{k}}x_{vi}^{2}x_{vj}^{2}e_{v}^{2}+2\sum_{v<v^{\prime}\in G_{k}}x_{vi}x_{vj}x_{v^{\prime}i}x_{v^{\prime}j}e_{v}e_{v^{\prime}}.

Combining these last two gives the second variance term in
R_{2}
:

n_{K}^{2}\mbox{Var}\sum_{v\in G_{k}}\xi_{vi}\xi_{vj}=\sum_{v\in G_{k}}x_{vi}^{2}x_{vj}^{2}e_{v}[e_{v}(3M_{U}(2)-1)+1]\\
+2\sum_{v<v^{\prime}\in G_{k}}x_{vi}x_{vj}x_{v^{\prime}i}x_{v^{\prime}j}e_{v}e_{v^{\prime}}(M_{U}(2)-1).

Applying this with
i=j
gives the first variance term in
R_{2}
:

n_{K}^{2}\mbox{Var}\sum_{v\in G_{k}}\xi_{vj}^{2}=\sum_{v\in G_{k}}x_{vj}^{4}e_{v}[e_{v}(3M_{U}(2)-1)+1]\\
+2\sum_{v<v^{\prime}\in G_{k}}x_{vj}^{2}x_{v^{\prime}j}^{2}e_{v}e_{v^{\prime}}(M_{U}(2)-1).

Next we consider
R_{3}
for which we write
\xi_{i}^{\prime}=(Y_{i}^{\prime}-e_{i})x_{i}
,
i\in G_{k}
, with
Y_{i}^{\prime}
having the same distribution as
Y_{i}
but independent of all else. Then

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|{\xi^{\prime}_{ij}-\xi_{ij}}\right|\right)^{3}=\mathbb{E}\left(n_{K}^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{ij}||Y_{i}-Y_{i}^{\prime}|\right)^{3}\\
=n_{K}^{-3/2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{ij}|\right)^{3}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{i}-Y_{i}^{\prime}|^{3}],

(44)

and we crudely bound the last term by

\mathbb{E}[|Y_{i}-Y_{i}^{\prime}|^{3}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(Y_{i}\vee Y_{i}^{\prime})^{3}]\leq\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{3}+Y_{i}^{\prime 3}]=2\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{3}]\\
=2[e_{i}+3e_{i}^{2}M_{U}(2)+e_{i}^{3}M_{U}(3)],

(45)

using (
33
) with
{3\brace 2}=3
for the last equality. Plugging this last into (
44
) gives

R_{3}\leq 2n_{K}^{-3/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{K}}e_{i}(1+3e_{i}M_{U}(2)+e_{i}^{2}M_{U}(3))\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{ij}|\right)^{3}.

Lastly we address
R_{1}
. First we note that (
25
) vanishes:
\nabla S
is constant by (
38

40
). The matrix
\widetilde{I}_{k}
was calculated in (

26

satisfying (

\left|{\frac{\partial^{3}\log f^{n_{k}}(y^{n_{k}}|\theta)}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{u}\partial\theta_{j}}}\right|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{n_{k}}e^{x_{n}^{T}\theta}\left|x_{ni}x_{nu}x_{nj}\right|.

For
\varepsilon>0

Thus taking

M^{k}_{iuj}(y^{n_{k}})=\sum_{n=1}^{n_{k}}e_{n}\left|x_{ni}x_{nu}x_{nj}\right|\exp\left(\varepsilon\sum_{l=1}^{d}|x_{nl}|\right)

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{d}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(Q_{k_{2}l}Q_{k_{2}l^{\prime}})^{2}\wedge\varepsilon^{4}]}\sum_{n=1}^{n_{k_{2}}}e_{n}\left|x_{nj}x_{nl}x_{nl^{\prime}}\right|\exp\left(\varepsilon\sum_{w=1}^{d}|x_{nw}|\right),

\displaystyle+\left.2\sum_{v<v^{\prime}\in G_{k}}x_{vj}^{2}x_{v^{\prime}j}^{2}e_{v}e_{v^{\prime}}\mbox{Var}(e^{U})\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=2n_{K}^{-3/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{K}}e_{i}(1+3e_{i}M_{U}(2)+e_{i}^{2}M_{U}(3))\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{ij}|\right)^{3},

Conclusions

The asymptotic normality of sequences of random variables has been a major topic of research since at least
de Moivre, 1738
. Despite this topic’s rich history, non-asymptotic bounds to these limits have only gained maturity in the last half-century or so, and use of such bounds as finite-sample approximations in statistics is even less fully developed. For MLEs the first finite-sample bound in a general setting was not given until 2017 by
Anastasiou and Reinert, 2017
. The current paper extends this line of research to include group sequential analysis and dependence through misspecified models and quasi-MLE.
