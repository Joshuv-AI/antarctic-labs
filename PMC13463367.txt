A Didactic Ultrasound Manual for identifying lumbar Plexus Peripheral Nerves in the lower limb: Lower Abdominal Wall, Groin and Thigh
Authors: Juan Antonio Valera-Calero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe lumbar plexus, formed by the anterior rami of T12/L1 to L4/L5, provides the main neural supply to the lower abdominal wall, groin, and anterior–medial thigh. Its principal peripheral branches (the iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal, genitofemoral, lateral femoral cutaneous, femoral, and obturator nerves) are clinically relevant targets for invasive diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, including nerve blocks and neuromodulatory techniques. This manual aims to describe how to visualize these six main lumbar plexus branches using ultrasound guidance.Materials and methodsFor each nerve, this guide summarizes its typical anatomical course, key clinical considerations related to neuropathy or iatrogenic injury, the most relevant anatomical landmarks, recommended transducer positioning, and characteristic sonographic appearance. The text is complemented by illustrations of probe placement, schematic depictions of nerve pathways, and representative ultrasound images.ResultsThis manual provides a structured ultrasound-based approach for identifying the main lumbar plexus branches in the lower abdominal wall, groin, and thigh. It details the anatomical and sonographic features that facilitate nerve recognition and supports precise probe positioning and needle guidance when these nerves are targeted in clinical practice.ConclusionsUltrasound guidance is encouraged when performing procedures involving lumbar plexus branches, as it may improve procedural accuracy and patient safety by enabling precise needle placement while reducing the risk of injury to adjacent non-target structures. This manual offers a practical anatomical and sonographic reference for clinicians performing ultrasound-guided interventions in this region.

Introduction

Peripheral nerve procedures (e.g., nerve blocks, radiofrequency, cryoneurolysis or percutaneous electrical nerve stimulation among others) are supported interventions for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. 1​ As accurate needle placement is essential, 2​ ultrasound (US) guidance offers two major advantages. It increases the likelihood of targeting the perineural space of the intended nerve (enhancing specificity and clinical efficacy) and significantly improves the safety of the intervention.

This article aims to focus exclusively on practical sonographic techniques for accurately locating peripheral nerves from the lumbar plexus at the hip and thigh (complementing a previous report focused on sacral plexus peripheral nerves at the gluteal region) 3​ by refreshing the nerves anatomy, summarizing key clinical information and detailing the relevant sonoanatomy and optimal probe windows needed to access the perineural space safely and precisely.

Technical guide

For the optimal US visualization of lower-limb peripheral nerves, it is advisable to have both curvilinear (convex) and linear transducers available. Deep nerves (e.g., the obturator nerve [ON]) are best examined using a low-frequency curvilinear probe (3–6 MHz) to achieve adequate penetration. In contrast, superficial nerves and small anatomical targets (e.g., cutaneous nerves) should be assessed with a high-frequency linear transducer (10–18 MHz) to maximize spatial resolution. 4​

Although most US systems include presets for different regions or tissues (and some parameters such as color maps may not require routine modification), frequency, depth, focus, gain and dynamic range are core imaging settings that should be individually optimized for each examination to improve nerve visualization. 5​ Spatial compounding is recommended at a moderate level to reduce speckle and artifacts and to enhance needle conspicuity while maintaining adequate edge definition. 6​ Tissue harmonic imaging should be disabled for deeper assessments and enabled when evaluating superficial cutaneous nerves. 7​ The focal zone should be placed at or just below the nerve depth, with gain (brightness) and dynamic range (contrast) adjusted to clearly define the hyperechoic epineurium and the hypoechoic fascicular architecture. While this work used a Samsung HS50 US system (Samsung, Seoul, Republic of Korea), the same technical principles are applicable to other high-end US platforms.

Standard probe positions are typically sufficient to identify the nerve in short axis; however, when abnormalities are suspected or clinical concern exists, the assessment should be supplemented with a longitudinal view of the structure. 8​ Upon US, nerves are generally recognized by hypoechoic fascicles surrounded by a hyperechoic epineurium, producing a characteristic “honeycomb” appearance. During tracking, the nerve should be followed slowly and kept centered in the image to limit artifacts and maintain contrast. As peripheral nerves are anisotropic structures, minimal transducer pressure and perpendicular orientation to the nerve are recommended to avoid the loss of echogenicity and potential misidentification. 9​

Ilioinguinal nerve

The ilioinguinal nerve (IIN) is a branch of the lumbar plexus classically arising from the anterior ramus of L1, although its segmental origin shows clinically relevant variability. In cadaveric series, it may originate from L1 alone (~65%) or receive contributions from T12-L1 (~14%), L1–L2 (~11%), and even L2–L3 (~10%), expanding the potential root supply beyond the “pure L1” pattern described in many atlases. 10​ ​11​

Topographically, the IIN typically emerges from the lateral border of the psoas major and courses anterior to the quadratus lumborum, where its relationships are relatively consistent. It then travels toward the iliac crest and leaves the retroperitoneum by piercing the abdominal wall (most commonly through the aponeurosis of the transversus abdominis) to continue between the muscular layers. In a dissection study of 200 nerves, the mean entry point of the IIN into the abdominal wall was approximately 2.8±1.1 cm medial and 4.0±1.2 cm inferior to the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS), reinforcing the ASIS as a practical bony landmark. 10​ Fig. 1 illustrates schematically the path of this nerve (colored in green).

Schematic anatomical illustration of the lumbar plexus: ilioinguinal nerve (green), iliohypogastric nerve (blue), genitofemoral nerve (purple), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (red), femoral nerve (orange) and obturator nerve (pink).

A key feature is its variable relationship with the iliohypogastric nerve (IHN). Both nerves may arise as a common trunk (“Type I”) or as two separate nerves (“Type II”), with the latter being the most frequent cadaveric pattern (reported across wide ranges, typically ~47–94%). In addition, anastomoses and communicating branches with neighboring nerves (e.g., subcostal, IHN, and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve [LFCN]) have been described, which helps explain the sensory overlap and the clinical heterogeneity of the inguinopubic territory atlases. 10​ 11​ ​12​ Distally, the IIN usually heads toward the inguinal canal, where its relationship to the spermatic cord (men) or the round ligament (women) may vary (More anterior courses, differing degrees of aponeurotic coverage, and early perforation of the external oblique aponeurosis have all been reported.). Functionally, it is a mixed nerve, providing motor twigs to the local abdominal wall and sensory innervation to the proximal superomedial thigh, pubic region, and external genitalia (scrotum/penile root in men; mons pubis/labia majora in women). 10​ ​11​ It is also notable that the IIN may be congenitally absent, with reported frequencies ranging from 0% up to 35% (in these cases, the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve (GFN) may partially compensate for its sensory territory). 13​

From a clinical perspective, IIN injury may present with loss of the cremasteric reflex and/or sharp, shooting (“lancinating”) pain involving the anterior scrotum in men or the labia majora in women.

For US visualization, position the patient supine and use a high-frequency linear transducer to first delineate the three anterolateral abdominal wall layers (external oblique, internal oblique, and transversus abdominis). Then, position the probe obliquely along the line between the ASIS and the umbilicus, which reliably exposes the relevant fascial planes near the inguinal region ( Fig. 2a ). 14​ The IIN is most commonly identified within the fascial plane between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis, typically close to the ASIS 14​ ​15​ ( Fig. 2b ).

Iliohypogastric nerve

The IHN most commonly arises from L1 and frequently with a contribution from T12, although broader segmental inputs and substantial variability in its root pattern have been reported. 11​ ​12​ ​16​ Topographically, it typically emerges from the lateral border of the psoas major and runs infero-laterally on the anterior surface of the quadratus lumborum (a segment considered among the most consistent portions of its course), traveling in parallel between the subcostal nerve superiorly and the IIN inferiorly. 11​ ​12​ ​16​ It then enters the abdominal wall by perforating the transversus abdominis aponeurosis just above the iliac crest and continues in the intermuscular plane between transversus abdominis and internal oblique. 11​ ​16​ Distally, the IHN is commonly described as giving a lateral cutaneous branch that pierces the internal and external oblique layers superior to the iliac crest to supply the posterolateral gluteal skin and an anterior continuation that proceeds toward the suprapubic territory, pierces the internal oblique medially to the ASIS, and reaches the anterior abdominal wall near the rectus sheath to provide cutaneous innervation above the pubic region; along this route, it also contributes motor fibers to transversus abdominis and internal oblique (and related aponeurotic structures such as the conjoint tendon). 11​ ​12​ ​16​ Quantitatively, cadaveric measurements report a small caliber (mean diameter ~2 mm, with ranges extending roughly from ~1 to ~2.8 mm), which partly explains why early branching and thin fascicles can complicate identification. 1​ 11​​ ​16​ 1​ illustrates schematically the path of this nerve (colored in blue).

From a clinical perspective, the IHN is tightly linked to lower abdominal approaches because its branches traverse the operative corridor of common incisions and fascial planes. Its injury or entrapment may follow lower abdominal surgery and trauma-related suturing and can manifest as neuropathic pain in the iliac crest/suprapubic region together with possible abdominal wall weakness (paresis) due to the involvement of its motor supply. 11​ ​12​ ​16​ Anatomical variability is a major contributor to both diagnostic uncertainty and procedural risk: the complete absence has been reported across cadaveric studies from 0% up to ~34%, the cutaneous branch may be absent in a meaningful minority, and compensatory overlap from neighboring nerves (including the GFN) may occur (features that can blur sensory maps and alter symptom distributions). 11​ ​12​ ​16​ In addition, the IHN may arise as a single nerve or as part of a common trunk with the IIN, may split into multiple trunks proximally before uniting, and may show communicating branches/anastomoses with the subcostal, IIN, GFN, or LFCN (patterns that help explain overlapping territories and heterogeneity in clinical presentations). 11​ ​12​ ​16​

Using the same scanning window described for the IIN, the IHN is most often identified slightly more cranial than the IIN in the same corridor ( Fig. 2a ). Distinguishing the two is aided by scanning cranio-caudally to appreciate their relative “stacking” and by tracking the candidate nerve medially toward its suprapubic course ( Fig. 2b ). 17​ ​18​

Genitofemoral nerve

The GFN arises from the anterior rami of L1–L2. Unlike most other lumbar nerves, it emerges on the anterior surface of the psoas major, piercing its fascia before descending obliquely towards the inguinal region. 16​ The trunk typically divides into two branches (genital and femoral) just above the inguinal ligament (IL), although separation may occur higher on the psoas or, less commonly, the two branches may originate independently. 19​ ​20​

The genital branch enters the inguinal canal through the deep ring. In men, it accompanies the spermatic cord, supplying motor fibers to the cremaster muscle and sensory input to the scrotum and adjacent thigh; in women, it travels with the round ligament to the labia majora and mons pubis. 21​ ​22​ The femoral branch passes beneath the IL lateral to the femoral artery, piercing the fascia lata to reach the upper femoral triangle, where it provides cutaneous sensation to the anterior proximal thigh. 21​ ​22​

Because of its superficial course, the GFN is prone to iatrogenic injury during procedures such as hernia repair, varicocelectomy, gynecological and urological operations, and pelvic lymphadenectomy. 19​ ​20​ Genitofemoral neuralgia presents with chronic neuropathic pain in the groin, radiating toward the upper thigh or external genitalia. Patients describe burning, tingling, or stabbing sensations exacerbated by standing or hip extension, sometimes relieved by hip flexion. 23​ Differential diagnosis is challenging given the sensory overlap with the IIN and IHN; selective blocks are often required to distinguish them. 20​ ​24​ Reported anatomical variability further complicates both diagnosis and surgical avoidance, with up to one quarter of specimens showing separate origins or “reversed” branching patterns. 20​

The genital branch can be assessed at the pubic region ( Fig. 3a ), coursing adjacent to the cord/ligament ( Fig. 3b ). 25​ ​26​

The femoral branch is best visualized at the inguinal crease (positioning the transducer over the femoral head), adjacent to the femoral artery and nerve ( Fig. 4a ). With the probe in a short axis, the sequence of the femoral vein/artery/nerve is identified. The femoral branch of the GFN runs lateral to the artery but medial and superficial relative to the main femoral nerve (FN). 25​ 26​ ​27​ It is typically within the femoral sheath ( Fig. 4b ).

Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve

The LFCN is a purely sensory branch of the lumbar plexus that most often arises from the posterior divisions of L2 and L3, although origins from L1–L2, isolated L2, or even a twig from the FN have all been documented. 28​ From the psoas major, it slides obliquely across the iliacus, where it is crossed anteriorly by the deep circumflex iliac vessels, and then enters an “aponeurotic‑fascial tunnel” to reach the inguinal region ( Fig. 5 ). Normally, it exits the pelvis deep to the IL at a mean distance of 19 mm medial to the ASIS, before diving into the fat cleft between sartorius and tensor fasciae latae and bifurcating into anterior (anterolateral thigh to the knee) and posterior (trochanteric area) branches.

Schematic anatomical illustration of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN).

Five topographical variants at the IL–ASIS complex are frequent. The nerve may traverse or lie superficial to the ligament, course over or lateral to the bony spine, or pierce the sartorius itself. Distances from the ASIS can range from 6 mm to more than 7 cm and as many as five subdivisions may exist before the nerve crosses the ligament. 28​ 29​ ​30​ Such variability tightens the anatomical corridor and helps explain why the LFCN is particularly susceptible to focal compression.

Meralgia paresthetica ranks as the second commonest lower‑limb mononeuropathy after common peroneal palsy, with population studies quoting incidences of 32–43 per 100,000 person‑years overall and roughly eight‑fold higher rates in people with diabetes. 31​ Entrapment beneath, within, or just distal to the IL is by far the dominant mechanism, but anatomical variants that force the nerve through the ligament, over the ASIS, or into the sartorius greatly amplify the risk. External compression (tight belts, tool holsters, smartphones, and corsets), abdominal distension (obesity, pregnancy, and ascites), diabetes, and rapid weight loss are recognized cofactors, while iatrogenic injury accompanies laparoscopic portals, anterior hip arthroplasty, iliac‑crest bone harvesting and prone ventilation. 31​ ​32​ Patients describe a sharply demarcated burning or stinging dysesthesia over the anterolateral thigh that worsens on standing or walking and eases in sitting; objective weakness is absent, and bilateral symptoms occur in 10 to 20 % of adults. High‑division or intraligamentous courses correlate with earlier onset, more intense pain, and a greater likelihood of surgical referral, whereas nerves that pass medial to the ASIS tend to respond to conservative measures. 33​

To identify the LFCN in US, the most common reference used is located just below the ASIS, where it glides through the fat corridor framed by sartorius (medial) and tensor fasciae latae (lateral). With the patient in the supine position, place the probe transversely a finger‑breadth caudal to the palpable ASIS ( Fig. 6a ); on this short‑axis image, the two muscles form a shallow triangle whose floor is filled by bright adipose tissue. 34​ Once identified, track the nerve proximally by rotating the transducer so that its long edge parallels the IL. This maneuver demonstrates its relationship with the ligament and ASIS, which is relevant for diagnosis and safe needle placement 35​ ( Fig. 6b ).

Femoral nerve

The FN represents the largest branch of the lumbar plexus. It is usually formed by the posterior divisions of L2–L4, although the accessory input from L1 or L5 has occasionally been documented. 16​ The nerve emerges from the lateral border of the psoas major, courses between psoas and iliacus, and passes beneath the IL within the muscular compartment, lateral to the femoral artery. Upon entering the femoral triangle, it lies lateral and slightly posterior to the vascular bundle, where it divides into several terminal branches. Functionally, the FN is mixed. Motor fibers supply the sartorius, the quadriceps femoris group, and often part of the pectineus and sensory branches provide innervation to the anterior and medial thigh, while the saphenous nerve (SaN) extends distally through the adductor canal to supply cutaneous sensation along the medial leg and foot 36​ ( Fig. 7 ).

Schematic anatomical illustration of the femoral nerve (FN).

The causes of FN injury or entrapment include retroperitoneal bleeding, psoas abscesses, pelvic masses, metabolic neuropathies such as diabetes, and iatrogenic damage during pelvic or hip procedures. 16​ ​37​ Deficits typically involve the loss of quadriceps strength with impaired knee extension, reduced or no patellar reflex, and sensory changes in the anterior thigh and medial leg. 38​ From a therapeutic perspective, the FN approaches are indicated for perioperative analgesia in knee replacement, hip and femoral fractures, and have also been shown to provide effective pain relief in pre-hospital settings. 39​

To visualize the FN, the patient needs to be positioned supine with the hip slightly abducted and externally rotated. The transducer needs to be positioned transversely at the inguinal crease ( Fig. 4a ). The femoral head can be used as a deep bony reference. In the cross-section, the femoral vein, femoral artery, and FN appear sequentially from medial to lateral, with the nerve enclosed by the fascia iliaca and usually located at a depth of 1–2 cm 40​ ( Fig. 4b ).

Saphenous nerve

The SaN is the longest purely sensory terminal branch of the FN. It typically arises within the femoral triangle from the medial aspect of the FN a few centimeters distal to the inguinal crease, in close proximity to other femoral branches (e.g., medial cutaneous nerve of the thigh) and to the nerve to vastus medialis. 41​ It then accompanies the femoral vessels through the adductor canal beneath the sartorius muscle, where its relationship to the femoral artery is dynamic (it may cross relative to the artery within the canal) before approaching the distal canal exit. 42​ Anatomically, the canal is roofed by the thick vastoadductor membrane, and the SaN commonly exits at (or just distal to) the inferior foramina, although proximal “piercing” variants and even dual-root formation with an intracanalar and extracanalar contribution have been documented. 43​ Distal to the canal, the SaN continues toward the medial knee and leg, becoming progressively more superficial; it frequently gives off an infrapatellar branch around the knee region, yet the origin of this branch is variable and may detach proximal to the adductor canal in some specimens, which helps explain heterogeneity in the anteromedial knee territory. 44​ Beyond the knee, the SaN travels subcutaneously along the medial leg and is characteristically associated with the great saphenous vein, with patterns ranging from anterior to posterior adjacency and frequent crossings; cadaveric work highlights a distal leg segment where venonervous adhesion is common and branching is relatively constant, and separate studies demonstrate this close nerve–vein relationship as a recurrent feature along the distal course 45​ ( Fig. 8 ). Morphometrically, reported diameters and branch-free segment lengths vary across the limb (with cadaveric measurements supporting millimetric caliber and clinically usable branch-free lengths in selected segments), reinforcing why the SN may be straightforward to follow in some individuals yet subtle when thin, deeply seated, or branching early. 46​

Schematic anatomical illustration of the saphenous nerve (SaN).

From a clinical standpoint (focused on the thigh), SaN-related pain can arise from pathology in or around the adductor canal/subsartorial region, where the nerve courses in a confined fascial corridor and runs in close relationship with the femoral vessels and the vastoadductor membrane (an anatomic setup that may predispose to traction/irritation and can confound symptom topography when the infrapatellar branch of the SaN originates high or when the SaN is formed by variant roots). 43​ ​44​ Importantly, the variable branching pattern around the distal canal (including proximally arising infrapatellar branches) can expand or shift the painful/dysesthetic territory at the anteromedial knee despite a “single-trunk” expectation in the thigh, which is why careful proximal-to-distal correlation is often necessary when interpreting medial knee symptoms that may be traced back to the subsartorial/adductor canal segment. 44​

For US visualization in the thigh, position the patient supine with the hip slightly abducted and externally rotated. Identify the sartorius muscle and femoral artery in the short axis on the medial aspect of the mid-to-distal thigh and slide the transducer cranio-caudally to delineate the vastoadductor membrane and the point where the femoral artery dives posteriorly toward the adductor hiatus. The SaN is sought within the subsartorial compartment in relation to the femoral artery 47​ ( Fig. 9a and b ). It should be noted that the vastoadductor membrane and vessel sheaths can mimic neural echotexture. 43​

Anterior femoral cutaneous nerve

The anterior femoral cutaneous nerve (AFCN) is a sensory branch of the FN that supplies the anterior and anteromedial skin of the thigh. Proximally, the AFCN can be approached as a short trunk related to the FN and the iliacus surface, while more distally it classically divides into intermediate and medial branches with distinct trajectories and cutaneous fields. 48​ In the anatomic description provided in high-resolution nerve US atlases, the intermediate branch traverses the fascia lata and crosses the sartorius to innervate the anterior thigh and part of the knee region, whereas the medial branch descends obliquely along the sartorius, pierces the fascia lata in the distal third of the thigh, and supplies the medial aspect of the thigh. 49​ Importantly, the AFCN may participate in inter-nervous communications (e.g., with the LFCN, the infrapatellar branch of the SaN, and the ON), contributing to a subsartorial plexus and explaining partial sensory overlap in the anteromedial thigh corridor. 50​

Clinically, AFCN neuropathy/neuralgia is typically related to localized trauma, scar encasement, or iatrogenic injury. Reported associations include hip and knee arthroplasty, varicose vein interventions/stripping, bypass grafting, excision of soft-tissue masses (with subsequent scarring), lymph node compression, and abscess-related procedures; symptoms usually present as paresthesia, dysesthesia, and pain over the anterior and anteromedial thigh that may extend from above the patella toward the groin. 49​ Diagnostic uncertainty is common because the symptomatic territory can overlap with several neighboring sensory nerves (LFCN, IIN, IHN, ON, and SaN), and because anatomic variability and anastomoses may blur classic dermatomal borders. 50​ Upon imaging, symptomatic cases may show focal enlargement or segmental swelling of the nerve, and in more advanced situations, neuroma formation may be detected along the expected course. 49​

For US visualization, position the patient supine and begin with a transverse orientation over the proximal thigh ( Fig. 10a ) to identify the femoral neurovascular bundle. Then, slide the transducer slightly distal while maintaining the same corridor. The AFCN can be recognized as a small fascicular structure departing from the FN and coursing superficial to/above the sartorius muscle, after which it divides into its medial and intermediate branches and becomes more superficial within the subcutaneous tissues of the anterior-medial thigh. 49​ A practical confirmatory relationship at the proximal thigh is that the AFCN is commonly seen medial to the sartorius tendon-muscle unit and superficial to the femoral vessels ( Fig. 10b ). 51​

Obturator nerve

The ON is a major branch of the lumbar plexus, classically formed by the anterior rami of L2–L4 within (or immediately adjacent to) the psoas major; however, its root contributions are not invariable, with reported patterns including L3–L4 and less commonly extensions such as L3–L5 or even atypical contributions involving more cranial roots in isolated cases, underscoring that “standard L2–L4” is predominant but not universal. 52​ After emerging from the medial border of psoas major, the ON descends along the lateral pelvic wall toward the obturator foramen/canal, maintaining close relationships with the pelvic vasculature and the fascia over obturator internus; it then traverses the obturator canal with the obturator vessels typically positioned anterosuperiorly, and reaches the medial thigh where it continues deep to the pectineus region and into the adductor compartment. 53​ Distal to the canal, the ON most often divides into anterior and posterior branches, but the level of bifurcation is variable (reported as intrapelvic, within the obturator canal, or after canal exit in the medial thigh), which has clear anatomic relevance when tracing the nerve and anticipating where separate fascicles may appear. 54​ After leaving the obturator canal, the ON enters the adductor compartment and typically divides into anterior and posterior branches, although the level of division is variable 53​ ​54​ ( Fig. 11 ).

Schematic anatomical illustration of the obturator nerve (ON).

Functionally, the ON is the principal nerve of the thigh adductor compartment, providing motor supply to adductor longus, adductor brevis, adductor magnus, gracilis, and commonly obturator externus, with pectineus innervation being variable (and sometimes dual-supplied), while it also contributes articular branches to the hip and (in some individuals) the knee; importantly, the “textbook” cutaneous territory over the medial thigh is inconsistent, and more than half of cases may show no true cutaneous innervation from the ON, which is a key anatomic caveat when correlating sensory maps with ON anatomy. 53​ ​54​ Regarding variants, several studies describe an accessory ON in a minority of specimens (often arising from L3–L4, sometimes directly from the ON), typically coursing along the medial border of psoas major and passing above the superior pubic ramus rather than through the obturator foramen, with contributions to pectineus and the hip joint (an anatomic configuration that can coexist with “normal” ON branching and adds an extra layer of variability in the inguinopelvic-to-thigh transition zone). 52​

From a clinical perspective, obturator neuropathy most often reflects focal entrapment within the obturator canal and should be suspected in patients with groin or medial thigh pain accompanied by adductor weakness. 55​ Reported precipitating factors include pelvic trauma, periarticular or perilabral cysts, hip surgery, prolonged labour, and obturator herniation; less commonly, space-occupying lesions can compress the neurovascular bundle along its pelvic outlet. 56​ In athletes, a distinct mechanism is described in which chronic adductor tendinopathy and osteitis pubis promote fibrous band formation, predisposing to ON irritation or tethering. Beyond pain and weakness, patients may develop adductor wasting and a characteristic wide-based gait. 57​ When imaging is pursued, magnetic resonance imaging is typically preferred because it can directly depict causative lesions producing mass effect and can also demonstrate indirect denervation changes (oedema and/or atrophy) in the adductor compartment. In addition, enlargement and altered signal of the entrapped nerve itself may be appreciable, whereas in non-lesional cases denervation changes may be the only objective imaging clue. 55​

To visualize the ON at the obturator foramen/canal region, place the transducer on the medial proximal thigh (along the extended inguinal-crease line, immediately lateral to the perineum) and first identify the superior pubic ramus as a bright cortical line with acoustic shadow ( Fig. 12a ), then the obturator externus lying superficial to it and the pectineus anterior to the obturator externus. In this window, the nerve is embedded within the thick fascial plane between pectineus and obturator externus, contiguous with the pubic ramus 53​ ​54​ ​58​ ( Fig. 12b ).

For visualizing the anterior/posterior divisions, slide the probe slightly caudal into the adductor compartment ( Fig. 13a ) and systematically map the fascial planes: the anterior branch is typically found within the hyperechoic fascia between pectineus and adductor brevis (and more distally between adductor longus and adductor brevis), whereas the posterior branch lies deeper in the fascial plane between the adductor brevis and the adductor magnus ( Fig. 13b ).

Conclusions

This didactic manual provides a practical and structured US-based approach for identifying the main peripheral branches of the lumbar plexus in the lower abdominal wall, groin, and thigh. By integrating anatomical landmarks, recommended probe positioning, and characteristic sonographic appearances, this guide may assist clinicians in improving nerve recognition and planning safer US-guided procedures. Its clinical value lies in supporting more accurate needle placement, reducing the risk of unintended injury to adjacent non-target structures, and facilitating the application of diagnostic and therapeutic interventions such as nerve blocks and neuromodulatory techniques.
