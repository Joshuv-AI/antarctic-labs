 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The primary aim of this proposal is to improve the accurate prediction of hydrodynamic
 performance and dynamic load responses of the AQ10 floating axial-flow tidal turbine with a tri-
 cat mooring configuration. The validation of reduced-order modeling approaches with high-
 fidelity model will be implemented. Additionally, the frequency response domain, Response
 Amplitude Floating Wind (RAFT) toolbox plus an optimizer expanded for marine hydrokinetic
 turbines under the Submarine Hydrokinetic And Riverine Kilo-megawatt. Systems (SHARKS)
 program will be used for designing and exploring different key design parameters (platform
 dimension, mooring layout and its parameters) of next marine hydrokinetic (MHK) turbine
 generation.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Aquantis will provide the latest 3D CAD model of AQ10 floating tidal turbine system.
 Additionally, Aquantis in coordination with Contractor (NLR) will provide necessary inputs such
 as desirable design load cases at a specific site condition, interesting mooring design
 configuration and its properties. Both NLR and Aquantis will identify desired design parameters
 and objectives for the system exploration and optimization.

 Task 1 Results:

 The Aquantis team provided a floating platform design concept (shown in Figure 1) that was
 fully submerged in water, in contrast to previous Aquantis designs, and modeled the structural
 geometry of their tidal turbine in TidalBladed. Using data from TidalBladed on node and
 member properties (e.g., lengths, diameters, thicknesses, point masses, ballast), as well as the
 rotor and mooring system, we developed an initial design in the Response Amplitudes of
 Floating Turbines (RAFT) code [1]. Both teams also collaborated to identify potential
 optimization parameters in RAFT. Figure 2 shows a graphic of the floating tidal platform,
 including the identified optimization parameters.

 The fairlead position in the x-direction has four different parameterization options. The fore and
 aft mooring lines can either (1) attach at the same point, with that fairlead position varied, (2)
 attach at different points represented as positions equally spaced from the x fairlead location
 (fairlead x spread), or (3) attach at completely independent points (fore fairlead position and aft
 fairlead position). The mooring line diameter and length are also treated as independent
 variables, though not shown in Figure 2. Other mooring line properties such as mass, stiffness,
 and strength are treated as functions of the line diameter.

 2

 A design load case with a rated current speed of 2.0 m/s and a JONSWAP wave spectrum,
 characterized by a significant wave height of 2 m and a peak wave period of 8 s, was selected to
 optimize the system in this study.

 input conditions provided by Aquantis. NLR will quantify simulation uncertainties including
 spatial and temporal discretization.

 and loads, it should be carefully validated to balance computational accuracy and cost. Details of
 the mesh resolution for different mesh cases shown in Figure 3 are provided in Table 1.

 Task 5: NLR will create a baseline model using Response Amplitude of Floating Turbines
 (RAFT) code. NLR will also create a Python interface between frequency domain, RAFT and an
 optimizer like OpenMDAO or scipy.optimize libraries.

 Task 5 Results:

 The initial RAFT model, built from the TidalBladed input provided by the Aquantis team, is
 shown in Figure 7. However, this initial RAFT design could not be simulated under the rated
 current speed of 2.0 m/s and a JONSWAP wave spectrum with a significant wave height of 2 m
 and a peak wave period of 8 s without encountering numerical instability. This indicates that the
 modeling processes within RAFT could not establish a natural equilibrium of the platform under
 loaded conditions, where the net forces cancel to zero. This likely meant that the forces exerted
 on the platform (from the rotor, mooring system, buoyancy, etc.) were too large compared to the
 corresponding stiffness of the platform.

 After many optimization runs of this initial RAFT design to find a feasible solution under loaded
 conditions, a few changes were made to better represent the system and enable the optimizer to
 find valid solutions.

 Fig. 8. RAFT representations of the Aquantis tidal turbine design

 7

 In this work, the NLR team also built an interface to couple RAFT with the optimization solver
 OpenMDAO (shown in Figure 9). Two different options were explored: building a framework
 around OpenMDAO or developing custom design optimization scripts. We ultimately decided to
 use the OpenMDAO environment to develop an optimization framework coupled with RAFT.
 We found that the programming setup time would be similar for both options, but the existing
 features within OpenMDAO would enable more effective design exploration and better
 organization. We also concluded that the objects within OpenMDAO are better suited for this
 coupled design optimization problem, which involves evaluating both floating platform
 structural variables and mooring system variables.

 Task 6: NLR will conduct parameter exploration for different design parameters like platform
 dimension, mooring configuration, turbine blades, etc. Additionally, NLR will do data
 postprocessing and analysis.

 Task 6 Results:

 Optimization runs with the SLSQP optimization algorithm (gradient-based) typically produced
 designs that were fairly similar to their initial settings (which is why making the baseline design
 as accurate as possible was important). We tested many different initial conditions for SLSQP-
 based design optimization, but most failed to converge because they began in infeasible regions
 of the design space. Optimization runs with the ALPSO optimization algorithm (gradient-free)
 generated some feasible designs but required several hours to converge; and when the
 convergence criteria were relaxed, the algorithm did not always return feasible designs.
 Including ballast parameters as design variables made convergence difficult for most optimizers.
 When a feasible design was found, we then added more constraints to better model realistic
 configurations.

 Fig. 9. A high-level flowchart of how the OpenMDAO framework works

 After continued iterations of optimization runs, design explorations, and RAFT model
 development, we determined that the primary cause of numerical instability in RAFT was that
 the hydrodynamic forces on the rotor were too large relative to the system stiffness. Once we
 manually adjusted the stiffness—primarily through the mooring system in the yaw degree of
 freedom—the baseline design could successfully run through RAFT at rated conditions. We
 achieved this by adjusting two variables: the distance between the fore and aft fairleads, and the
 increase in fairlead distance away from the platform (laterally and vertically). The parameters of
 this adjusted baseline design are shown in Table 2 under “Design (b).”

 8

 Including new design variables, such as the spread of fairlead locations, in the optimization
 toolset yielded feasible designs. The optimized version of the adjusted baseline design (Design
 (b)) is shown in Table 2 under “Design (c).” The optimized results using the same design
 variables but with additional structural members connecting the fairlead attachment points to the
 main platform are shown in Table 2 under “Design (d).” The optimized results for the same
 system without an upper pod are shown under “Design (e),” while the optimized results of the
 same design under rated conditions—from left-to-right as well as right-to-left—are shown under
 “Design (f).” Diagrams of these designs are provided in Figure 10.
