Executive Summary:

The US Department of Energy (DOE) called for targeted manufacturing and metrology methods for
the fabrication of diffraction-limited x-ray grazing incidence mirrors for synchrotron applications. In the
Phase I period of performance, Optimax showed proof of concept for smoothing single point diamond
turned silicon mirrors to the required 0.3 nm RMS level (within the 80 µm high pass surface roughness
band) using the HERMES (High End Robotic Mid-Spatial Elimination System) robotic smoothing
platform. During the Phase II effort in a series of nine tasks, Optimax has further investigated and
down-selected a variety of parameters to be used with the smoothing platform with the focus of
applying the technique to optics that are CNC generated and sub=aperture polished. In addition,
Optimax explored expanding the findings to the entire frequency bandpass of interest, scaling up the
size of the substrate, and collaborating with other partners to meet the metrology needs of the call.
Tasks 1 through 4a were completed entirely, including on the 500L demonstrator optic. These include:
designing and generating the demonstrator optic (Tasks 1 and 2), developing and scaling up the post-
generation Grolish platform for its successful use on the 500L demonstrator optic (Tasks 3 and 4) and
an additional task (Task 4a) where a robotic polishing algorithm was improved and scaled up to the
500L part. Each of these steps showed good convergence towards the end DOE solicitation goal and
would be scalable to the 1 m desired final optic. While these tasks were being completed,
improvements were made to the user interface and convolution algorithms used for Grolish/ smoothing
and the polishing software, respectively.
Tasks 5 and 6 focused on the HERMES Smoothing. Through Phase I and Phase II, various
parameters were explored and used to scale up the process, ultimately to the 500L demonstrator optic,
all in preparation for subsequent MRF (magnetorheological finishing) and IBF (ion beam finishing)
processing. The results suggest that the smoothing process made important improvements in MSF
(mid spatial frequency) error on all parts (test coupons up to the 500L demonstrator), and testing of
smaller coupons proved readiness for IBF. However, overall, the higher frequencies of form (up to 0.5
mm) and lower frequencies of surface roughness, would still require additional correction.
Unfortunately, the 500L optic smoothing could not be completed prior to the expiration of the project,
so final IBF and smoothing processing was not completed on the demonstrator.

Task 7 investigated IBF of the optics. Several test coupons were sent through the manufacturing
process starting with generation and going through smoothing and MRF steps. The results of these
coupons were used to help determine the most effective approach for smoothing as it relates to IBF
outcomes.
Task 8 is comprised of a series of metrology tasks that investigated and implemented through the
entire period of performance. Metrology is a critical step in the manufacturing process and developing
in house methods and an understanding of their limits was a challenging undertaking. A table of
available metrology and related capabilities and limitation was compiled as part of this task to help
inform the manufacturing process with the most useful metrology available for each step. Some areas
(e.g., slope) could not be reliably assessed with the in-house metrology available, and would require
collaboration with outside support.
Finally, in Task 9, the Manufacturing Scale Up plan was developed based on the findings of the project
on smaller test parts, as well as extrapolating to the desired outcomes for the 500L and full-sized, 1 m
optic. An outline of the “benchmarks” of the metrology criteria for each manufacturing sequence is
presented.
Optimax has completed multiple conference proceedings to help disseminate the findings of the
project over the period of performance.

II. ACCOMPLISHMENTS

1. What are the major goals and objectives of this project?
The US Department of Energy (DOE) has outlined several areas for targeted improvement in the
manufacturing methods and the corresponding metrology for domestically sourced diffraction-
limited X-ray grazing incidence mirrors, specifically for synchrotron applications. During the Phase
I SBIR Period of Performance (PoP), Optimax showed proof of concept for the HERMES (High
End Robotic Mid-Spatial Elimination System) robotic smoothing effects on surface roughness on
silicon parts (80 µm high pass “de facto” industry standard). This process, developed by Optimax,
is designed to smooth mid-spatial frequency (MSF) errors remaining from sub-aperture
generation and polishing of high precision optics. The HERMES project was designed to emulate
the human smoothing techniques used in the optics manufacturing industry for freeform and
similar high precision lenses. The technology has evolved to include a Grolishing step (step down
from generation and prior to polishing and smoothing) to prepare the surface and reduce form
error. Using a computer-generated toolpath, coupled with existing manufacturing technologies
(e.g., Magnetorheological Finishing (MRF) and Ion Beam Finishing (IBF); Figure 1), the project
intends to reduce cost and lead time for optics manufacturing.

 During the Phase I project, Optimax smoothed small, sample parts to meet the requested DOE
 specifications, as a proof of concept for a larger mirror. These results were only with regard to
 smoothing, and not the entire end-to-end manufacturing process. The overall objectives of the
  Phase II “Advanced Manufacture and Smoothing of X-Ray Mirrors” project were designed to
 facilitate the Department of Energy in its end-to-end manufacturing goals for domestic
 synchrotron mirrors, with the three targeted areas outlined below.

Objective 1: Improve the process control of HERMES smoothing by mitigating cosmetic defects
and form error during smoothing.
Objective 2: Scale up the HERMES manufacturing process from small scale coupons to the one-
meter length solicited by the DOE (500 mm length representative demo optic).
Objective 3: Improve internal metrology that is integral to the ultra-precision manufacturing needs

2. What was accomplished under these goals?
Task 1: Demonstrator and test part design finalization and blank procurement.
Most of the work for this task was completed during the first PoP as reported in the RPPR 1, and
reviewed here. The solicitation requests a 1-meter optic, but for the purposes of demonstrating
the scalability of the HERMES platform (which has the reach to address the full size), a 0.5-meter
demonstrator was deemed sufficient. After approaching Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL)
for input on a viable yet representative prescription, they provided specifications for a handful of
existing elliptical cylinder synchrotron mirrors. From these mirror specifications, a fourth order
polynomial representation of a true elliptical mirror shape was selected (Equation 1) however, the
radius of curvature (RoC) of the mirror is longer than that requested within the solicitation. This
selection was made based on the determination that the 10 m RoC requested in the solicitation
at the 0.5 m size would be prohibitive to good metrology, which is critical to guide the
manufacturing process. Serious metrology development is outside the scope of this effort;
therefore, it was decided that the longer radius of curvature would be sufficient to prove the
manufacturing process. Additionally, the proposed optic would have a radius of curvature
representative of a true synchrotron mirror that would test manufacturing methods similarly to

 3

production of a true ellipse. Once metrology methods reach a point to be able to reliably measure
a 10 m RoC ellipse over the size required for these mirrors, the manufacturing processes
developed under this effort should be able to be extended to that geometry without issue.
A representative prescription for the surface was chosen as described in Equation 1 and
illustrated in Figure 2. The final part dimensions were 580 mm x 50 mm, with a clear aperture
(CA) of 500 mm x 20 mm (Figure 3). This task was completed within the proposed timeline.

Tasks 3: FC-HERMES “Grolish” process characterization and control

Phase I. During Phase I, Optimax investigated a proof of concept, direct to Grolish (from
generation) manufacturing process. “Grolish” refers to a post-generation, pre-polish procedure
designed to remove subsurface damage (SSD) and MSF errors that are imparted during the

 5

generation step. After generation, the part is still “grey” and retains a signature and small form
errors making it a good candidate for the use of the Force Control options of the HERMES robotic
(FC-HERMES) process, but with the “Grolishing” option. This step consists of using larger, bound
abrasive tooling on the robot using force control options of FC-HERMES to address these
generation-induced MSF error as well as form. Two Phase I proof of concept trial runs appear in
Figure 6 and Figure 7 where the form error and roughness are compared via LUPHOscan
(leftmost plot of each plot pair) and NewView (rightmost plot of each plot pair) measurements of
each part’s post-generation and post FC-HERMES states.

  Figure 6: Post Generation (a) and Post FC-HERMES run (b) of a larger (100mm Ø) sample
 showing improved form error (left plots, measured on LUPHOScan optical profiler) and
 improved surface roughness (right plots, NewView) after a Force Controlled Grolish Run.

  Figure 7: A second sample of the FC-HERMES proof of concept task. Here, Post Generation
 (Left) and Post FC-HERMES run (Right) on a part similar to that in Figure 6. Once again, form
 and roughness show improvement.

the project. The toolpath algorithm and graphical user interface (GUI) used for the Grolish and
Smoothing steps is the same, so many of the improvements discussed in this section apply to the
HERMES smoothing step as well. Most of the work has been implemented on the 1 m reach robot
(Figure 8), which will allow scale up to a 1 m optic. This robot and the associated programming
developed and improved during this and other projects is the aim of the current project, and unless
stated, is the platform used for the results used. All software, even for the smaller robot, is the
same.

Tool Influence Function (TIF). Optimax began with a proof-of-concept robotic program for
smoothing and Grolishing of optics. During the project, considerable testing was completed to
drive the improvements made. First, the team worked to adequately characterize the tool
influence function (TIF) to be used in convolving toolpath predictions. Through convolving
predictions using a TIF, the process can become increasingly deterministic, a goal of the SBIR
solicitation. The tools developed above for the Grolish step differ from those typically used for
polishing, resulting in different TIFs with unique measurement challenges. In addition, the surface
of the generated part led to metrology concerns. Early efforts to characterize the TIF appear in
Figure 10. The team believes that accuracy at the micron level is suitable for a deterministic
Grolish process as it does not need the same fine control that is required for polishing and
finishing.

 Figure 10: Various TIF or "spot" characterizations: (A), on one of the silicon test coupons; (B),
 trace of one of the TIFs in early metrology; (C), deflectometry image; (D), deflectometry
 reconstruction; and (E), white light interferometry (WLI) of a TIF test spot.

Grolish/ Smoothing improvements. During Phase I, the team decided that two major
improvements would be necessary for Grolish and smoothing success on the demonstrator
(500L) part. First, the previous toolpath algorithm was limited by the CA shape that it could
address: it could only Grolish or smooth circular apertures. Secondly, during Phase I, an
interaction between type of initial form error and the resulting form after smoothing was noticed.
The process imparted power, as it “over hit” the center of the part, which led to unwanted,
excessive removal in that area (Figure 11). During the Phase II SBIR, the team revamped the
algorithm to improve the Grolish and smoothing process and deterministic outcomes of the paths.

when the existing algorithm was completely rewritten to more evenly apply the path to reduce

In addition, the path GUI was developed to include a convolution option, allowing the use of a TIF
and error map inputs to create deterministic removal paths. This feature is important for
deterministic Grolish correction, where error can be improved at this early stage through a
convolution program (shown in Figure 15). When combined with a TIF, the removal can be
predicted and the paths adjusted accordingly. That is, incoming metrology is used to convolve the
Grolish path to target removal of specified features. These predictions were tested and refined
during the Phase II project.

  material should be removed by the run. These data can be directly compared to the actual
 removal as captured by post-run metrology, and the predictions improved. The feature is
available for both uniform removal and surface correction runs and can be used for both Grolish
 and smoothing runs.

During the project, the team conducted testing that allowed for comparison of removal and surface
outcome predictions with actual metrology results (Figure 16). Results were cross-checked with
slightly different methodology to improve and refine the algorithm.

Task 4: FC-HERMES “Grolish” scale-up.

For both the HERMES Grolish (Task 3) and Smoothing (Task 5) processes, a scale-up is needed
for the progression from the test coupons to the final demonstrator part. Results were obtained
first on 20 mm x 30 mm CA coupon parts, then scaled up to a 240 mm x 50 mm (240L) part, and
finally the full demonstrator optic. The robotic platform is one scalable portion of the project,
discussed next, and the process itself must scale (discussed further down). The Grolish and
smooth scale-up end goal and software to complete it for the 1-meter reach robotic platforms is
the same.

Robotic Platform Scale Up. Tests for the Grolish scale up, including parameter down-select
testing, were completed on the large, 1 m robot during Phase II. The original proposed tasks
would have been initiated on the smaller robot from Phase I, and then scaled up to the 1 m robot.
However, an in-house solution was attained early on whereby successful completion of the robot
commissioning allowed the ability to conduct all experiments on the large robot to start. All needed

 11

hardware and software upgrades needed for the 1-meter robot were completed prior to the initial
testing. However, some later issues postponed the continued use of the large-scale platform, so
the team returned to the smaller robot while those were addressed. Even though the smaller robot
was used for the Grolish of the 500L demonstrator, the same software was used on that robot as
the larger, 1 m robot. During the NCE period, the 1 m robot was once again available for polishing
and smoothing illustrating the versatility of the developed software that can now be used across
multiple robotic platform sizes. The results for both Grolish and smoothing work described below,
as well as polishing work, generalized between multiple robot platforms.

Grolish Process Scale Up. As required by the solicitation, the team worked on scaling up overall
Grolish path readiness, regardless of the robot platform. That is, tooling and paths must
accommodate the size and shape of the 500L demonstrator. The improvements to the path
algorithm to address the shape of the 500L demonstrator were described above. In addition, the
TIF was characterized and tested on 50 and 100-mm coupons (small CA). However, the Grolish
tool and TIF were found to change over time as illustrated when the paths became longer to cover
the CA on the larger parts. The team further characterized the TIFs to help identify and predict
the change in removal over time: a decay modifier was required to represent how a TIF will change
through the course of the Grolish run. This characterization required quantification through testing
on test parts, as shown in Figure 18Error! Reference source not found.. Based upon the
physical tool removal change over time, the tool behavior was characterized mathematically and
a decay function (curve) was estimated. This modifier was used to reflect the tool change over
the path runtime and to scale the Grolish polish to the full-sized parts.

Once the Grolish tool decay was characterized, the software was upgraded to include a piece-
wise function to better predict the deterministic Grolish phase. The latter is important because
each tool would reach the end of its life prior to completing the larger surface area of the
demonstrator, and need to be replaced periodically. Due to the size of the demonstrator optics
(500L), multiple tools are required to complete a single run. The long toolpath was sectioned into
desired times to allow for tool changes and to account for the condition of each new tool used. An
early example of this ‘split’ path appears in Figure 19.

Bug Fixes. During the project, a target mismatch in the Grolish GUI was identified (a function of a
small error in the code). The error would cause the path not to “hit” at the desired location and
therefore errors between predicted and actual removal occurred resulting in new errors. The data
used for the removal prediction was not dense enough to catch minor errors but it was found while
investigating interferometry data. For example, in Figure 22, the path did not correct the expected
high areas (red) as well as or as consistently as expected, and overcorrected the low areas (blue).
Additionally, this error was compounded during polishing paths that run after Grolish, due to being
unable to compensate for the original mishit. Once this problem was discovered, it was identified
and rectified in the code. Debugging the results and pinpointing the source of the error took some
time and some of the full process test parts deviated from the desired manufacturing path (amount
of error greater than suspected).

Task 4a: Polishing

Part of the scale up process for the desired 1 m optics would include polishing between Grolish
and smoothing. Although not initially proposed as a task for this project, both the 240L test optics
and the 500L demonstrator optics were polished on the robot (instead of a commercial platform).
By further developing this in-house process, Optimax can improve their overall manufacturing, so
this task was added to the project. The two optics were polishing towards convergence on the
final specification. A robotic polishing platform (same robotic platform as the Grolish and
Smoothing steps, but with different programming) was used. This robotic polishing convolution
was previously developed in-house and then applied during the DOE project, on a new substrate
(silicon). Based on the work addressing the polishing of the 500L demonstrator, the in-house
deterministic robotic polishing algorithm was improved and expanded (Figure 26).

Task 8: Metrology Tasks.
Metrology Suite. Metrology tasks have been ongoing throughout both Phase I and Phase II. An
investigation has been ongoing to most effectively utilize the in-house metrology at Optimax to
meet the very tight specifications in the solicitation. There are two main areas of interest for these
investigations, dictated by the SBIR solicitation: within a spatial bandpass of 0.5 mm to half the
length of the mirror (which is referred to as “form” or low order) and a spatial bandpass of 0.2 µm

 34

to 500 µm (which is referred to as “smoothness,” “surface roughness,” or high order error). Low
order characteristics can be measured using coordinate measuring machines (CMMs), contact
profilometers (e.g., UA3P), interferometry (e.g., Fizeau interferometer, stitching interferometer),
and others. High order (smoothness) is more difficult to measure because it spans the capture
range of several instruments that must be reconciled (e.g., Fizeau interferometry, WLI, which has
various magnifications, and AFM). For “Smoothness,” the Phase I proof of concept utilized a 20x
magnification WLI, with an 80 µm high pass filter. This interpretation is an industry “de facto”
standard and therefore was used for the sample coupons. Smoothness also considered AFM
results from a local university on one of the Phase I samples. A summary of the various metrology

Simulations. Fixturing can impact the final results of measurements on various metrology.
Previous project reports outlined the gravity sag analysis and its impact on metrology, such as
noted in the polishing segment of this report (e.g., Figure 28). Specifically, the predicted part sag
(or displacement) with the proposed blocking (or holding) fixtures that is caused by gravity was
modeled. These analyses were important to fully understanding the interaction of gravity and the
resulting metrology during various steps of the manufacturing process. The first such analysis
reflects the blocking technique used for generation in the CNC machine, and robotic Grolishing,
polishing, and smoothing, as well as transportation and initial metrology, all prior to ion beam
figuring. This blocking setup appears in Figure 58. Using this configuration, gravity sag analysis
was completed using ANSYS finite element analysis software and Sigfit to determine the self-
weight deflection. According to the analysis, there would be about a 52 nm deflection of the clear
aperture, which is below the expected error of these steps of the process. Due to this analysis,
the x power (long direction) was initially removed when utilizing to the toolpath algorithm.
However, once this power remained in the metrology used for the algorithm in later polishing runs
on SN02, the convergence improved.

out on the 1 m robot used during most of Phase II.
However, Optimax does not own the off-the-shelf platforms that are large enough to scale up for
the full-sized optic for generation, MRF, and IBF. These platforms are currently limited to
approximately 600 mm. Prior to initiating the manufacture of the desired optics, several capital
expenditures would need to be investigated and acquired. In order to scale up the existing process
to the full sized (approximately 1050 mm x 60 mm x 60 mm) optics, platforms for Generation, IBF,
and MRF would need to be acquired. Multiple vendors can accommodate this type of acquisition,
but the procurement process can take anywhere from six months to two years for scoping,
designing, building, and commissioning the assets required for the scale up. The following
manufacturing plan assumes that these processes have been completed.
In addition, the specific metrology required to measure and meet the needs of the DOE solicitation
are not currently available at Optimax, or even commercially available at all. Specifically, the
accuracy of the part requirements exceeds the current metrology suite capabilities at Optimax.
Either a metrology partner or additional capital expenditures would be required to meet metrology
input for manufacture needs.
Sourcing of Material Blanks. As with the current project, the material blanks would need to be
ordered from a reliable vendor, with a certificate of compliance for both material characteristics
as well as crystal orientation. Further investigation would need to be completed with the current
vendor to verify that their boules could yield a 1-meter blank of the desired dimensions in the
correct crystal orientation. The lead time would need to be considered when making timeline
decisions.
Product Specifications, Drawings, and Metrology. Prior to beginning physical manufacture of the part,

 42

updated part specifications, drawings, and metrology must be addressed. The DOE specifications
would need to be translated into available metrology and/ or outside metrology designated.
Requiring external metrology sources would prolong the manufacturing timeline, and create
increased risk to the part. A plan would need to be detailed and carried out to ensure prompt,
adequate, and safe metrology options for all phases of manufacturing, particularly those towards
the end of the process. In addition, the larger part would require additional gravity sag analysis to
predict its effect on the part and compensate for this change during manufacture.
CNC Generation. Once the blanks are procured, the part would be blocked as was the 500L demo.
CNC generation would take place on a 5 axis CNC machine with a sufficient envelope. Parts
would be measured on the in-house Xenos CMM as was completed during the Phase II PoP for
the demonstrator optic. The part would require minimal MSF error and overall form error of no
more than 1 µm RMS. This process should be completed in a single run, and also address
formation of fiducials (datums) for surface location.
Grolish. Phase I and Phase II offer sound justification of the Grolish process. As was seen on the
500L demo optic, three to four Grolish runs can result in about 1 µm RMS (as measured on the
Xenos), and reduction in MSF errors, preparing the part for polishing. Further investigation of this
step to incorporate additional deterministic runs, and further exploration of tool decay and
convolution algorithms, could enhance this step. Subsurface damage removal would also be
documented during this portion of manufacture.
Polish. After Grolish, robotic polishing would seek to reduce the form error to about 0.20 µm RMS.
Paths would be developed to minimize MSF errors as much as possible. A lesson learned from
the 500L demo optic is to more precisely account for the effect of gravity sag and unblocked spring
for the larger parts on power error to better prepare the part during the polishing step.
Smoothing. After polishing, the residual MSF errors will need to be fully characterized, and
smoothing will need to target those errors that cannot be addressed in the following MRF step.
For the larger, 1 m optic, wheel size on the MRF will dictate which features are able to be
addressed. Then the smoothing stepdown will have to accommodate these errors, to be left
mainly with lower order (long spatial domain) error.
MRF. MRF for any new, larger platform required for the 1 m final size will require characterization
of tool and spot sizes available for use on the 1 m parts. Without directly assessing the
characteristics of this level of machine, precise criteria for feature size addressed during polishing
is elusive, but overall, the part must reach a 50 nm RMS criterion, with no more than 0.4 nm RMS
surface roughness, across the WLI bands described in Table 4Error! Reference source not
found., above.
Smoothing. After MRF, the resulting MSF signature must be smoothed. As with the 500L demo
optic, this smoothing is at the expense of lower order form, but features too small to be addressed
by IBF will be targeted. The final specifications that are required during this phase will be no more
than 75 nm RMS, primarily in a much lower order bandpass. Specifically, any features smaller
than about 20 mm will need to be corrected. Peaks in the PSD will be critical in identifying and
targeting various features. During Phase II, the 500L optic was starting to show progress through
this step, but time did not allow full implementation. Compared to the Phase I test coupon, the
Phase II 500L optic was barely at about ½ of the required smoothing time, and additional
smoothing time would be required due to the smoothing path improvements noted in Figure 12,
where the center of the part was initially overhit.
IBF. The final step in the process will be IBF on a larger platform, and the part will be deblocked
at this point. The part will be best prepared for this step when higher order error is adequately

 43

addressed during sufficient smoothing time, and low order error only need be corrected. Blocking
“spring” may be present but should be able to be addressed by the IBF platform (very low order
error). However, due to the 1 nm RMS specification required for this step, metrology will require
additional resources beyond what is currently available at Optimax as the limits of the ASI will be
exceeded and there is no reliable way to measure the slope to the degree requested by the DOE.
Final Post-IBF Smoothing. If the IBF leaves a residual signature and/or surface roughness in the
desired bandpass is insufficient, additional smoothing will be required. Final metrology will be
required after this step, with the same caveats as found in the IBF step.
