 ABSTRACT

This project presents the development and validation of high-fidelity numerical models for fin-tube heat
exchangers to enable accurate performance prediction and informed design optimization. The modeling
framework integrates detailed geometric specifications, thermophysical property data, and system-level
constraints to simulate the heat exchanger’s behavior under a range of operating conditions. The model is
calibrated using real-world product specifications and validated against experimental data collected from
controlled cooling and heating tests. In cooling mode, the model captures the overall trends in capacity and
outlet air temperature but tends to underpredict latent effects, especially at lower air flow rates. In heating
mode, the simulation consistently overestimates both the thermal capacity and outlet air temperature,
indicating the need for refinement in air-side heat transfer assumptions. Despite these deviations, the model
provides a solid foundation for optimization, allowing key design variables to be tuned within physical and
performance-based constraints. This research advances the ability to simulate, validate, and optimize fin-
tube heat exchanger designs with greater confidence and efficiency.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Aris Hydronics is advancing the development of a next-generation combi hydronic heat pump system
designed to deliver integrated heating, cooling, and domestic hot water (DHW) for residential and light
commercial applications. The ARIS system architecture centers around an air-to-water heat pump, paired
with an indirect DHW tank, dual-use thermal energy storage tanks, a hydro control unit with advanced
control logic, and modular hydronic room conditioning units. The system is supported by a distribution
pump and manifold station, enabling flexible and efficient zone-level temperature control.

Currently in its Generation 1 pilot phase, the ARIS system is being deployed in 4–6 single-family homes,
with additional pilots in planning across single-family, multi-family, and commercial buildings. This
platform introduces several technical innovations, including a grid-interactive energy management control
system, a centralized DC/AC distribution wiring module, and ARIS's patent-pending multi-zone hydronic
room conditioning technology. These features are designed to improve performance, installation efficiency,
and compatibility with future energy systems.

As part of its product development strategy, Aris Hydronics is conducting rigorous component-level
research, with particular focus on the fin-tube heat exchanger units used in the hydronic room conditioning
modules. This project supports that effort by developing and validating high-fidelity design models for fin-
tube heat exchangers. Through a structured modeling and validation framework, the project aims to enable
accurate prediction of thermal performance under varying heating and cooling conditions, support system-
level optimization, and inform design refinements needed for product scaling and certification. This
research directly contributes to Aris’s broader goals of completing residential and multi-family product
refinements, achieving certification for critical subsystems, and preparing for broader market deployment.

 2. PROJECT OBJECTIVE

This research aims to develop and validate high-fidelity design models for fin-tube heat exchangers,
enabling more precise performance prediction and design optimization. The first subtask focuses on
identifying and selecting suitable modeling frameworks that align with the thermal-fluid characteristics,
geometric complexity, and operational constraints of the targeted heat exchanger designs. In the second
subtask, the chosen models are parameterized using detailed specifications from existing commercial or
prototype heat exchangers, including geometric dimensions, material properties, and flow conditions, to
ensure accurate representation of real-world behavior. The final subtask involves benchmarking model
outputs against empirical performance data to assess model accuracy. Discrepancies between predicted and
observed results will inform iterative refinements, improving model reliability for both design validation
and future development applications.
 3. METHODOLOGY

3.1 OVERVIEW OF MODELING FRAMEWORK

Figure 1 illustrates a comprehensive framework for the design and optimization of fin-tube heat exchangers
using numerical modeling, validation, and performance-based optimization techniques.

The process begins with the development of a numerical model, which captures the heat transfer and fluid
flow behavior within the heat exchanger. This model incorporates physical and thermodynamic
relationships governing the system. To ensure accuracy, the model is validated against field test data,
aligning simulated outputs with real-world performance measurements. Once validated, the model becomes
the foundation for a systematic optimization process. Key design variables—such as tube diameter, fin
spacing, air velocity, or coolant flow rate—are adjusted within defined constraints (e.g., physical
limitations, pressure drop thresholds, or manufacturing feasibility). The optimization is guided by specific
objectives, which may include maximizing thermal effectiveness, minimizing pressure drop, enhancing
energy efficiency, or balancing performance with cost. The result is an optimized heat exchanger design
that is both effective and practical under real-world operating conditions.

This iterative framework ensures a data-driven, validated approach to refining heat exchanger performance
and design through simulation, experimentation, and optimization.

4.2 SIMULATION VALIDATION

Figure 4 illustrates the relationship between air flow rate and two key performance metrics of the fin-tube
coil: heating capacity and air outlet temperature. Heating capacity is plotted on the left y-axis, while air
outlet temperature is shown on the right y-axis. As air flow rate increases, the heating capacity also increases
in both the experimental and simulated datasets, reflecting the greater energy transfer associated with higher
air volumes. However, the simulated heating capacity tends to be slightly higher than the experimental
values, particularly at higher flow rates, indicating that the model may be overestimating the coil's heat
transfer performance.

 Figure 4. Validation of heating conditions.

Conversely, air outlet temperature decreases with increasing air flow. The experimental outlet temperatures
drop more sharply than the simulated ones, and the simulated temperatures are consistently higher across
the full range of air flows. This discrepancy suggests that the model may be underestimating the air-side
heat transfer coefficient or overestimating the thermal input to the air stream. Overall, while the simulation
captures the general performance trends, the deviations between predicted and measured values highlight
the need for further refinement of the model, particularly in how it handles air-side heat transfer and flow-
dependent effects.

Figure 5 presents a comparison between experimental and simulated results for cooling performance as a
function of air flow rate. The graph includes two primary metrics: cooling capacity and air outlet
temperature. The blue curves represent cooling capacity, with solid circles showing experimental data and
dashed squares representing simulation outputs. The red and pink curves correspond to air outlet
temperature, where solid triangles indicate measured values and dashed X-marks show simulated results.

As air flow rate increases from 50 to 130 CFM: Both experimental and simulated cooling capacities show
an upward trend. However, the simulation consistently overpredicts cooling capacity relative to
experimental measurements. For air outlet temperature, experimental values rise with increasing airflow—
reflecting decreased heat exchange time—while the simulated temperatures remain nearly constant and
lower, underpredicting the actual values.

 Figure 5. Validation of cooling conditions.

 Figure 6. Sensible heat rate comparison for simulated and experimental data.

These discrepancies suggest limitations in the current model. One contributing factor is that geometry
details used in the simulation may not have been captured with sufficient accuracy from the physical coil,
leading to deviations in predicted heat transfer surface area and flow distribution. Additionally, the presence
of wet coil conditions (due to condensation on the air side) introduces latent heat transfer, which may not
be fully accounted for or correctly modeled, as indicated in Figure 6—further affecting the accuracy of
validation results.
 5. CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates a structured, data-driven approach to modeling and validating fin-tube heat
exchangers for both cooling and heating applications. The modeling framework successfully integrates
detailed geometric and flow parameters with thermodynamic modeling techniques to simulate performance
under varied operating conditions. Experimental validation shows that the model captures the general
behavior of the system well, including trends in capacity and outlet temperature. However, discrepancies
exist—particularly in accurately predicting outlet air temperature under wet cooling conditions and heating
capacities at higher air flow rates—due to simplified assumptions in heat transfer correlations and
limitations in representing condensation or turbulent effects.

The results confirm the model’s value as a design tool, particularly when used within an iterative
optimization framework. By linking numerical simulation with empirical validation, the project enhances
predictive accuracy and informs design decisions that balance thermal performance, efficiency, and
manufacturability. Ongoing refinement of heat transfer correlations and moisture handling will further
strengthen the model’s accuracy and applicability for future heat exchanger development.
