1.0 Executive Summary:

 In this project, the team developed tough and degradable biocomposite thermoplastic
polyurethanes (TPUs) by incorporating bacterial spores into TPUs as a biofunctional living filler.
The team screened various bacteria and selected the Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6633 strain as the
final candidate, primarily due to its genomic availability, sporulation ability and TPU
assimilation activity. The heat-shock tolerance of ATCC 6633 spores was further improved
through evolutionary engineering via Adaptive Laboratory Evolution (ALE), demonstrating a
17.7-fold enhanced germination efficiency post heat-shock treatment compared to the wild-type
strain (WT). The team fabricated biocomposite TPUs by incorporating lyophilized powder of
heat-shock tolerized (HST) spores during the hot melt extrusion (HME) of TPU at 135 °C. The
baseline TPU used in this project is a commercially available soft-grade TPU (BCF45)
manufactured by BASF. Colony forming unit (CFU) assays quantified that WT and HST spores
in the TPU matrix retained approximately 20% and 100% survivability, respectively, after HME.
Tensile testing demonstrated that the spores behaved as a polymer-reinforcing filler, positively
affecting the overall tensile properties of the biocomposite TPU. For example, biocomposite
TPU with WT and HST spores (BC TPUWT and BC TPUHST, respectively) exhibited up to 25%
and 37% improved toughness, respectively, compared to TPU without spores. BC TPUHST
showed remarkably improved disintegration in autoclaved compost (92% mass loss in 5 months),
which simulated a microbially poor environment for TPU degradation. When compared to TPU
without spores (44% mass loss in 5 months) the acceleration of degradation is marked.
Respirometry confirmed that 72% of BC TPUHST was biomineralized into CO2 within 6 months,
indicating that spores in the biocomposite TPU were germinated by utilizing nutrients in the
autoclaved compost, facilitating TPU degradation at the end of the material's life. The team
demonstrated the scale-up of biocomposite TPU fabrication using continuous extrusion and
injection molding techniques. Processing conditions optimized in a lab-scale microcompounder
were successfully transferred to a continuous extruder with a 30-fold increased throughput.
Biocomposite TPUs prepared using these industry-relevant processes showed comparable
toughness improvements to samples prepared in the lab-scale extruder. Excitingly, following
compounding in the pilot-extruder the composite material could be injection molded, while
retaining high spore viability and similar toughness improvements. The team also found that
spores in biocomposite TPU served as antioxidants, preventing toughness decay during the
recycled extrusion of BC TPUHST. Long-term storage tests over one year showed that the
addition of spores had no negative effect on the longevity of the TPU. Furthermore, the team
demonstrated the fabrication of spore-bearing biocomposite polymers with other polyesters such
as PBAT, PLA, and PCL. We obtained promising preliminary data that showed overall
toughness improvements for all polymers with spore addition. Finally, life cycle assessment
(LCA) and techno-economic analysis (TEA) were carried out, which indicated minimal
additional cost of fabrication. Overall, a tough and degradable biocomposite thermoplastic was
successfully developed through this project, with all tasks completed successfully, achieving
>100% of the objectives.

2.0 Background

 The current project is a multi-armed research partnership aimed at diminishing the
environmental impact of thermoplastic polyurethanes (TPUs). Being a specialty thermoplastic
elastomer, TPUs have diverse applications in many different industries and most current TPU
based products end up as waste in landfills since there are very limited recycling strategies for
TPUs. In the US alone, 1.3 million tons of TPU waste is generated annually. An ideal solution to
mitigate the environmental impact of such materials would be the complete biodegradation of
end-use products. However, materials based on TPUs do not have this capability inherently
programmed into their chemical structure, making it difficult to realize degradation for end-of-
life products. To enable degradation, we aim to utilize bacterial spores as living catalysts for
biodegradation. Strains will be evolved to utilize TPUs as a carbon source and will be embedded
within TPUs. Once activated they will trigger decomposition of the biological composite
material.

 Unique to this proposal is an academia/industrial partnership that harnesses the nimbleness
and innovation that universities provide while maintaining a critical eye to manufacturing, scale-
up and commercialization of the biocomposites generated under this award. The team led by
UCSD has expertise in screening studies of biological composites and adaptive laboratory
evolution of bacterial strains. This expertise was complemented by UGA, who runs one of the
largest academic centers for biodegradation in the country. Finally, our industrial partner, BASF,
has 70 years of experience bringing TPUs to market. As part of their sustainability road map,
BASF focuses on the utilization of bio-based resources to bring recyclable, biodegradable and
compostable solutions to conventional TPU grades to minimize the environmental impacts from
the post-consumer wastes. The proposed technology will bring an innovative and sustainable
solution to the conventional TPU without the need for customization of the current production
line that will meet or exceed the standard set forth within this FOA.

3.0 Project Goals/Objectives:

 The goal of this project was to transform thermoplastic polyurethanes (TPUs) into a
biodegradable composite material that incorporates viable bacterial spores. This would eliminate
a significant plastic waste stream that is currently unrecyclable and undegradable, and which
generates ~1 million tons of waste annually in the US. Additionally, we aimed to improve the
functional properties of the TPU, increasing tensile toughness by at least 20%. To do this, we
incorporated evolved bacterial spores into TPU biocomposite materials. The spores act as soft
particles and, when well dispersed, improved toughness. The evolution of the spores was
intended to serve two goals: 1) utilize TPU as a sole carbon source to promote biodegradation
upon spore germination and 2) to improve tolerance to processing conditions (e.g extrusion). At
the end of the project, we achieved degradation goals set forth in the DOE BOTTLE FOA. The
project developed a simple method to impart biodegradation that will have minimal disruption in
product formulation; simply, bacterial spores would be added during extrusion/compounding
with little to no modification of the production method.

5.0 Description of Activities Performed

5.1 Optimization of spore-forming, TPU-degrading bacteria

 To repurpose bacterial spores as a biofunctional polymer filler for the development of
tough and degradable TPU, the team screened TPU assimilation activity and heat-shock
tolerance of spore-forming bacteria (ATCC 23857, ATCC 6051, ATCC 7061, ATCC 21332, and
ATCC 6633). ATCC 6633 exhibited robust growth and efficient assimilation of TPU powder as
the sole carbon and energy source in minimal media, making it a suitable host strain for the
project.
 Although ATCC 6633 spores displayed considerable thermal tolerance, exposure to 100
°C boiling water led to a sharp decrease in spore viability within 10 min. The team focused on
enhancing the heat-shock tolerance of ATCC 6633 spores through ALE experiments to ensure
spore survival following compounding with TPUs. Spore cultures were subjected to boiling
water and then propagated into fresh sporulation medium. After 40 rounds of ALE cycles with
increasing heat-shock duration for up to 30 min, the team was able to find mutants with
remarkably improved heat-shock tolerance. For example, one isolated strain exhibited 17.7-fold
improved germination efficiency compared to the wild type (WT) strain after exposure to boiling
water for 10 min.
 Causal mutations enabling increased heat-shock tolerance of the ALE-derived strains
were identified by whole-genome sequencing. fusA and abrB were the most frequently mutated
genes across the parallel ALE lineages. The fusA gene encodes a translation elongation factor
involved in protein synthesis, and the abrB gene encodes a transcription regulator involved in
controlling various cellular processes, including sporulation and stress responses. Isolates with
single or double mutations of fusA and abrB genes confirmed that mutations in these genes bring
an increase in heat-shock tolerance of ATCC 6633 spores.
 The team also confirmed that increased heat-shock tolerance did not negatively affect cell
physiology. WT and HST exhibited comparable cell growth profile in both complex medium,
such as Luria-Bertani (LB) media and Difco sporulation media (DSM), as well as minimal
medium supplemented with general carbon sources such as glucose and glycerol. At the same
time, both WT and HST showed similar TPU assimilation activity and spore productivity.

5.2 Biocomposite TPU fabrication

 The team fabricated biocomposite TPUs by incorporating lyophilized spores of WT or heat-
shock tolerized (HST) strains during hot melt extrusion (HME) of TPUs at 135 °C using a lab-
scale twin screw extruder. The baseline TPU used in this project is a commercially available
soft-grade TPU (BCF45) manufactured by BASF. TPU pellets were melted in the extruder at 135
°C at 36 rpm for 5 min, followed by the addition of spore powder at a final concentration of up to
1 % (w/w). The mixture was compounded at 135 °C at 36 rpm for 15 min and extruded through a
slit exit die. The team validated uniform spore dispersion in the biocomposite TPU using X-ray
microscopy and spectrophotometry. Microcomputed 3D tomography obtained by X-ray
microscopy visualized well-dispersed spore particles across the TPU matrix without significant
aggregation. Biocomposite TPU pieces aliquoted from different sites of an extrudate consistently
showed characteristic absorbance spectra of spores, confirming uniform spore dispersion in the
TPU matrix.
 To quantify spore viability in biocomposite TPU after HME, spores were extracted from the
biocomposite TPU through a solvent treatment. N,N-dimethylformamide dissolved the polymer
compartment in the biocomposite TPU, allowing for the recovery of spores using centrifugation.
Colony forming unit (CFU) assay was performed to assess spore viability. WT and HST spores
displayed approximately 20% and 90% survivability, respectively. HST spores showed
essentially full survivability, indicating that the evolutionary engineering of B. subtilis spores by
ALE markedly improved the heat tolerance of spores above the extrusion temperature of
commercial thermoplastics.
 Tensile properties of biocomposite TPU were validated. The team found that spore
incorporation generally had a positive effect on all tensile properties measured, such as
toughness, elongation at break, tensile stress, and Young’s modulus. For example, biocomposite
TPU with WT and HST spores (BC TPUWT and BC TPUHST, respectively) showed up to 25%
and 37% toughness improvement, respectively, compared to TPU without spores. This
confirmed that the spores served as polymer reinforcing fillers, enhancing tensile properties of
the TPU matrix. The team revealed that the toughness improvements of biocomposite TPUs were
attributed to interfacial interactions between the spore surface and the TPU matrix, primarily
driven by hydrophobic interaction, as demonstrated by increased hydrophobicity of BC TPUHST
with increasing spore content through water contact angle analysis.
 The team also demonstrated the genetic engineering of ATCC 6633 HST. A green-
fluorescent protein (GFP) expression vector was transformed into ATCC 6633 HST cells
(HST/GFP). Genetically engineered ATCC 6633 cells were sporulated, purified and lyophilized,
followed by compounding with TPU to fabricate biocomposite TPUs with HST/GFP (BC
TPUHST/GFP). Plasmids transformed into ATCC 6633 HST were retained in spores after HME,
and fluorescence could be detected once the spores were germinated by soaking BC TPUHST/GFP
in a nutrient rich media. It suggested that ATCC 6633 HST successfully carried the foreign
plasmid throughout the biocomposite TPU fabrication process and has the potential to be
modified with plasmids harboring useful enzymes to facilitate biodegradation of TPUs.

5.3 Degradation of biocomposite TPU

 The team evaluated the biodegradation of BC TPU using either mass loss testing or
respirometry. For the mass loss test, TPU samples were incubated in autoclaved compost to
simulate a microbially-less active environment with minimal polymer-degrading strains in the
incubating substrate, since it is unlikely that TPU products would be industrially composted.
 Page 6 of 10

TPU, BC TPUWT and BC TPUHST showed 44%, 64% and 93% mass losses, respectively, after 5
months of incubation at 37 °C at 45~55% humidity. Faster biodegradation of BC TPUs over
TPU suggested that spores in biocomposite TPU were germinated by utilizing nutrients in the
compost and facilitated biodegradation of TPU as biocatalysts. In addition, given that BC
TPUHST showed a faster disintegration compared to BC TPUWT, spore survivability can be an
important factor determining the degradation rate of biocomposite TPU at the end of its life.
 Respirometry testing under untreated compost at 42 °C at 45~60% humidity showed that
72% of BC TPUHST was biomineralized into CO2 within 6 months, while demonstrating 70%
relative biodegradation compared to a positive control, cellulose.
 To identify microbes responsible for BC TPU disintegration in compost, 16 S rRNA
sequencing was performed. Bacterial consortia analysis showed that the majority (~70%) of
microbes identified from the surface of degraded BC TPUHST were ATCC 6633 HST strain,
which was incorporated into the TPU material. These findings confirmed that the HST spores
can successfully germinate with biological activity in compost after fabrication and they
indicated that the disintegration process is significantly accelerated in environments lacking a
sufficient quantity of degrader strains.

5.4 Improving/evaluating practicality of biocomposite TPU

 To further improve the commercial feasibility of biocomposite TPUs, the team modified the
protocol of spore production to reduce the innate brown color of spores and, thus, to prevent
undesirable coloration of the biocomposite TPU. The team postulated that FeSO4 added to the
sporulation media was responsible for spore coloration. This hypothesis proved correct as
reducing the FeSO4 concentration yielded white spores. Importantly, the reduction of FeSO4
concentration did not negatively affect cell physiology and morphology.
 The team prepared biocomposite TPUs utilizing brown and white spores (BC TPUBrown and
BC TPUWhite, respectively). BC TPUWhite showed a minimal color difference compared to TPU
without spores. For tensile analysis, BC TPUBrown showed up to a 39% toughness improvement
at 0.75% (w/w) spore loading, while BC TPUWhite achieved up to a 45% toughness improvement
by the addition of 0.50% (w/w) spore. In other words, white spores functioned as a more
efficient polymer reinforcing filler compared to brown spores, displaying a higher toughness
improvement of TPU at a lower spore loading. Spores extracted from BC TPUBrown and BC
TPUWhite retained approximately 95% and 90% survivability, respectively, with no statistical
significance between the two groups. Thus, the team was able to mitigate brown coloration of
biocomposite TPU without compromising tensile toughness or spore survivability of
biocomposite TPU by simply modifying the sporulation media.
 The team demonstrated the scale-up of biocomposite TPU fabrication using continuous
extrusion and injection molding techniques. Processing conditions optimized in the lab scale
extruder (135 °C, 36 rpm) were successfully transferred to the pilot scale extruder (135 °C, 50
rpm) after a minor modification that could be operated continuously. To this end, the team
achieved a 30-fold increase in throughput for biocomposite TPU fabrication. After confirming
uniform spore dispersion in biocomposite TPU using polarized light microscopy, biocomposite
TPU extrudate was further processed by injection molding. Biocomposite TPUs prepared using
these industry-relevant processes showed a comparable toughness improvement (40% at 0.5%
(w/w) spore loading) to samples prepared in the lab-scale extruder (45% at 0.5% (w/w) spore
loading). Spores in biocomposite TPUs after continuous extrusion and injection molding retained
80% and 70% survivability, respectively. Given that the continuous extruder generated 250-fold
higher shear stress (23 GPa) than the lab scale extruder (up to 90 MPa) and spores were
 Page 7 of 10

subjected to high temperature (140 °C) and pressure (300 ~ 600 bar) during injection molding,
we can conclude that the spores retained remarkable survivability after processing in industry-
relevant practices.
 Additionally, the team hypothesized that spores embedded in biocomposite TPUs could serve
as antioxidants to prevent the thermal degradation of TPUs during recycling. The team
reprocessed biocomposite TPU using the lab scale extruder multiple times to simulate a
thermomechanical recycling protocol. TPUs without spores showed gradual toughness decay
during reprocessing, maintaining 67% residual toughness after four consecutive reprocessing
cycles. In contrast, biocomposite TPUs retained approximately 90% toughness after four
reprocessing cycles. The 10% toughness loss occurred after the first reprocessing, and
biocomposite TPU maintained 90% of the original toughness during the subsequent cycles.
Yellowing of TPU without spores was another indicator of thermal degradation of TPU during
reprocessing, whereas biocomposite TPU showed only marginal color change during the recycle
stability test.
 To investigate the mechanism behind the recyclability of biocomposite TPU, differential
scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed under an oxygen environment and oxidative
induction time (OIT) was measured. TPU without spores started oxidative degradation after ~40
min of incubation at 150 °C under 100% O2, while no degradation of biocomposite TPU was
observed during the 2 h monitoring of OIT. This result supported our hypothesis that spores
function as antioxidants, preventing thermal degradation of the polymer during melt processing
by scavenging free radicals generated by internal/external heat.
 The effect of spore addition on the longevity of TPU was also evaluated. TPU and
biocomposite TPU samples were stored under ambient conditions (23 °C and 60 % relative
humidity) for up to 1 year. Toughness and spore viability were measured every 6 months. Both
TPU and biocomposite TPU retained their full toughness for 1 year, demonstrating no negative
effect of spore addition on the longevity of TPU. Spores in biocomposite TPU retained full
survivability after 1 year of storage.
 The team also demonstrated the fabrication of spore-bearing biocomposite polymers with
other polyesters such as PBAT, PLA and PCL. Spore addition generally had a positive effect on
the tensile properties of all polymers we tested, showing up to 50% toughness improvement.

5.5 LCA TEA analysis

 The team carried out preliminary TEA and LCA analysis. We performed LCA with GaBi
software and BASF internal data sources. Potential economic and environmental impacts for five
end-of-life scenarios (landfill, incineration, mechanical recycling, chemical recycling,
composting) were analyzed.

6.0 Conclusions

 Overall, the team successfully demonstrated all the tasks within the proposed time frame.
Tough and degradable biocomposite TPUs developed in this project showed a great promise as a
sustainable TPU alternative as it improved tensile properties, recyclability and biodegradability
of TPU material with compatibility using industry-relevant manufacturing processes.

Decision Points
 • Spores show viability >20% versus control
 • Mechanical properties of the composite are improved by 10%
 • Degradation in excess of 50% by versus control TPUs

Accomplishments
 • Engineered spores showed >90% survivability post lab scale extrusion
 • Engineered spores showed >70% survivability post pilot scale extrusion (continuous
 mode) & injection molding
 • Toughness of the composites was improved by up to 45%
 • Degradation in >90% by mass (>70% biomineralization into CO2)
 • Composites enabled recycling of TPU
 • Spore incorporation had no negative effect on the lifetime of TPU
