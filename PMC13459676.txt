Relationships between health behaviors and body fat percentage among nigerian immigrants in the dallas-fort worth metroplex, United States
Authors: Samuel Ayeni, Philip Esposito, Meena Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Nigerian immigrant population in the United States (U.S.) has increased substantially over the past three decades; however, limited research has examined the relationships between health behaviors and body fat percentage in this population. This study investigated the associations between physical activity, dietary intake, and body fat percentage among foreign-born Nigerians residing in the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex, U.S.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 70 Nigerian immigrants using convenience sampling. Body fat percentage was assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis, physical activity was measured with the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Short Form, and dietary intake was evaluated using the Automated Self-Administered 24-hour Dietary Assessment Tool (ASA24). Binomial logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the associations between physical activity, dietary intake, and body fat percentage.ResultsThe prevalence of overweight and obesity was 34.29% and 28.57%, respectively. No statistically significant associations were observed between physical activity level, fruit intake, vegetable intake, red meat consumption, and body fat percentage (p > 0.05). Specifically, low physical activity level was not significantly associated with body fat percentage (OR = 4.25, 95% CI: 0.57–31.94, p = 0.16). Participants also reported low mean daily fruit intake (0.74 ± 0.78 cup equivalents/day) and high mean daily red meat consumption (4.73 ± 4.56 ounce equivalents/day) relative to U.S. dietary recommendations (1.5-2 cup equivalents/day and 1.7-2.6 ounce equivalents/day, respectively).ConclusionNo significant associations were found between selected health behaviors and body fat percentage. However, the observed dietary patterns warrant further investigation in larger longitudinal studies.Keywords: nigerian immigrants, obesity, overweight, physical activity, dietary intake, body fat percentage

Introduction

The population of Nigerian immigrants in the United States (U.S.) has expanded substantially over the past several decades. According to the American Community Survey data from the U.S. Census Bureau (USCB), the number of Nigerian immigrants residing in the U.S. increased from approximately 25,000 in 1980 to more than 448,000 in 2022. 1 Nigerian immigrants represent one of the fastest-growing African immigrant populations in the U.S., with large settlement patterns observed in metropolitan areas such as Houston, New York City, Atlanta, and Dallas–Fort Worth. 2 The Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex has experienced substantial growth in its African immigrant population, including Nigerians, making it an important geographical setting for examining health behaviors and weight-related outcomes among immigrant populations. 2 Despite this population growth, limited research has examined the health status and behavioral determinants of obesity among Nigerian immigrants in the U.S.

Migration to the U.S. may influence lifestyle behaviors associated with obesity and chronic disease risk among immigrant populations. Previous research suggests that changes in dietary intake, physical activity behaviors, and other lifestyle practices following migration may contribute to weight gain among African immigrants.3–5 Research has shown that the prevalence of overweight and obesity among Nigerian immigrants in the U.S. is approximately 28.7% and 38.1%, respectively. 6 Although these prevalence rates remain lower than those reported among several U.S. ethnic populations, recent National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) estimates indicate that obesity prevalence among U.S. adults remains high, particularly among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic adults. 7 The increasing burden of obesity among immigrant populations underscores the need to better understand behavioral factors associated with body fat and weight-related outcomes among Nigerian immigrants.

Dietary intake has been widely recognized as an important behavioral determinant of obesity. Prior studies involving immigrant populations have shown that low fruit and vegetable intake and high consumption of processed foods and red meat may contribute to obesity risk and chronic disease development.8,9 Among Nigerian immigrants specifically, previous studies have reported associations between fruit intake, meat consumption, and obesity, whereas no relationship has been observed between vegetable intake and obesity. 10

Physical activity is another important lifestyle factor associated with weight gain and obesity. Evidence from studies among immigrant populations in the U.S. suggests that lower physical activity levels and increased sedentary behaviors are associated with greater obesity risk and poorer cardiometabolic health outcomes.11,12 However, findings among African immigrant populations remain inconsistent. A study involving Nigerian immigrants in the U.S. have reported no significant association between physical activity and obesity. 6 Conversely, other evidence suggests that immigrants may engage in healthier physical activity behaviors compared to U.S.-born adults. 13 Given these mixed findings, additional research is needed to better understand the relationship between physical activity and body fat percentage among Nigerian immigrants.

To date, only a limited number of studies have examined behavioral determinants of obesity among foreign-born Nigerians in the U.S. Obisesan et al. 6 reported a significant association between alcohol consumption and obesity among Nigerian immigrants residing in Minnesota but found no significant relationship between physical activity and obesity. Similarly, Duru 10 reported associations between daily fruit intake, meat consumption, and obesity among Nigerian Igbo immigrants in Georgia, while no relationship was observed for physical activity. However, both studies relied primarily on body mass index (BMI) to define obesity. BMI may not accurately distinguish fat mass from lean body mass and may underestimate adiposity in some populations. 14 Consequently, body fat percentage may provide a more direct assessment of adiposity and obesity-related risk. Previous studies have recommended the use of body fat percentage in obesity-related research because it more accurately reflects body composition than BMI alone.15–17

Accordingly, this study aimed to assess body fat percentage and the prevalence of overweight and obesity among Nigerian immigrants residing in the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex and to examine whether physical activity and dietary intake were associated with body fat percentage in this population. The findings may contribute to a better understanding of obesity-related health behaviors among Nigerian immigrants and provide direction for future public health research involving immigrant populations.

Theoretical framework

The socio-ecological model (SEM) served as a general conceptual framework for this study. The SEM proposes that health behaviors are influenced by interactions across multiple levels, including individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy-related factors. 18 In obesity-related research, the SEM has been widely used to explain how behavioral and environmental influences contribute to health outcomes.

In the present study, the SEM was used primarily as a broad public health framework to contextualize obesity-related behaviors among Nigerian immigrants. The study specifically focused on individual-level behavioral factors, including physical activity and dietary intake, and their associations with body fat percentage. Environmental and organizational factors were not directly measured; therefore, the SEM was not applied as a comprehensive analytical model but rather as a conceptual guide for understanding health behaviors within immigrant populations.

Materials and methods

Study design and participant selection

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to examine associations between health behaviors (physical activity and dietary intake) and body fat percentage among Nigerian immigrants residing in the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex, United States. A total of 70 participants (44 males and 26 females) were recruited using convenience sampling through churches (parishes of The Redeemed Christian Church of God), the African Students’ Organization at Texas Christian University, and Nigerian social groups. Study flyers were distributed both in-person and online.

Eligibility criteria included Nigerian-born immigrants aged 18 years or older, proficiency in English, and residence in the United States for at least two years, as research indicates that most weight gain among immigrants occurs within the first three years of arrival. 19 Individuals were excluded if they were non-Nigerian immigrants, Nigerian individuals married into Nigerian families but not Nigerian by birth, pregnant or lactating women, individuals actively engaged in weight-loss dieting, or amputees.

Sample size and data collection

Using G*Power for binomial logistic regression (two-tailed, α = 0.05, power = 0.80), 20 a minimum sample size of approximately 70 participants was estimated to detect a large effect size, represented by an odds ratio (OR) of approximately 3.0. An assumed obesity prevalence of 30-40% was incorporated into the analysis to estimate the expected distribution of the binary outcome variable and improve the accuracy of sample size estimation. The prevalence assumption was informed by prior studies reporting obesity prevalence estimates within a similar range among Nigerian immigrant populations in the U.S.6,10 Data collection occurred between October 2024 and February 2025. The study was approved by the Texas Christian University Institutional Review Board. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection, and confidentiality was maintained throughout the study.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

Demographic variables collected included age, sex, education level, occupation, annual household income, and length of residence in the United States.

Body fat percentage

The primary outcome was body fat percentage, assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) via the Tanita® BC-1000 Plus device (Tanita Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). BIA is a validated method for estimating body composition and has demonstrated high reliability and validity in previous studies.16,21 Participants were categorized according to age- and sex-specific body fat percentage cutoffs established by Gallagher et al. 22 into underfat/underweight, healthy body fat, overweight, and obese groups. Since no participants were classified as underfat/underweight, body fat categories were dichotomized for regression analysis into: (1) healthy body fat and (2) overweight/obese (combining overweight and obese categories).

Dichotomization of outcomes is used in epidemiological research to facilitate logistic regression by collapsing categories into a binary variable, providing a methodological approach for simplifying analysis and focusing on overall risk estimation rather than category-specific differences.23,24 Similar approaches have been used in studies examining associations between behavioral risk factors and obesity-related health outcomes using logistic regression models.25,26

Physical activity

Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Short Form (IPAQ-SF), a widely used self-report instrument for estimating physical activity levels in population-based studies. The IPAQ-SF provides estimates of weekly energy expenditure expressed in MET-minutes/week and classifies activity levels into low (<600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–3000 MET-min/week), and high (>3000 MET-min/week). The instrument has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity across diverse populations, supporting its use in epidemiological research. 27

Dietary intake

Dietary intake was assessed using the Automated Self-Administered 24-hour Dietary Assessment Tool (ASA24, 2024 version), which is based on the USDA Automated Multiple-Pass Method.

Participants completed three 24-hour dietary recalls, including two weekdays and one weekend day, to capture habitual intake. Mean daily intake was computed across the three recall days for fruits, vegetables, and red meat, expressed in cup or ounce equivalents. The ASA24 system has been validated against interviewer-administered 24-hour dietary recalls and is considered a reliable and cost-effective tool for dietary assessment in epidemiological studies.28,29

Procedures

Participants first completed an online demographic questionnaire via Qualtrics. Subsequently, anthropometric and behavioral assessments were conducted. Body composition was measured onsite in laboratories, workplaces, or designated church locations. Dietary intake data were collected remotely at participants’ convenience, while physical activity questionnaires were completed via Qualtrics.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and frequencies) were used to summarize participant characteristics, body fat percentage, and health behaviors. Binomial logistic regression was utlized to examine associations between health behaviors (physical activity level, fruit intake, vegetable intake, and red meat intake) and body fat percentage (healthy vs overweight/obese).

Due to the modest sample size and limited statistical power, unadjusted binomial logistic regression analyses were performed to minimize the risk of model overfitting and unstable parameter estimates. Although variables such as age, sex, length of residence in the United States, and education level may be relevant confounders in obesity research among immigrant populations, these covariates were not included in the regression models to preserve model stability. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Reporting guideline

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for reporting observational research. 30 The completed STROBE checklist is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Discussion

This study examined the relationships between health behaviors and body fat percentage among Nigerian immigrants residing in the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex, U.S. The findings indicated that 34.29% of participants were classified as having overweight and 28.57% as having obesity based on body fat percentage criteria. Although no statistically significant associations were observed between physical activity, dietary intake, and body fat percentage, the findings provide important insight into the health behaviors and adiposity patterns of Nigerian immigrants in the United States.

The prevalence of obesity observed in this study was lower than estimates reported among U.S. adults overall and among several ethnic groups. According to the Texas Department of State Health Services, 31 34.4% of adults in Texas have obesity, while national estimates indicate obesity prevalence of 52.1% among non-Hispanic Black adults, 44.0% among Hispanic adults, and 39.3% among non-Hispanic White adults. 7 These comparatively lower obesity rates among Nigerian immigrants may partially reflect the “healthy immigrant effect,” which suggests that immigrants often arrive with more favorable health profiles than U.S.-born populations due to immigration selection processes and cultural lifestyle practices maintained after migration. 32

In contrast, the prevalence of overweight and obesity in the present study appeared higher than estimates reported among native Nigerians. However, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously because of methodological differences in obesity assessment. The current study classified weight status using body fat percentage measured by bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), whereas the Nigerian meta-analysis by Ramalan et al. 33 used body mass index (BMI) criteria. Body fat percentage provides a more direct assessment of adiposity by distinguishing fat mass from lean mass and may identify obesity differently than BMI, particularly among populations with varying body composition profiles. 15 Consequently, differences in prevalence estimates between the present study and studies conducted in Nigeria may partly reflect differences in measurement approaches rather than solely the effects of migration or acculturation. Nevertheless, environmental and lifestyle changes associated with immigration, including dietary acculturation and reduced physical activity opportunities, may still contribute to elevated obesity risk among immigrants over time.3,5

The present study found no statistically significant association between physical activity level and body fat percentage. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted among Nigerian immigrants and other immigrant populations in the United States that also reported no significant relationship between physical activity and obesity status. Obisesan et al. 6 reported that physical activity was not significantly associated with obesity among Nigerian immigrants in Minnesota, while Duru 10 similarly found no association among Nigerian Igbo immigrants in Georgia. However, these findings differ from larger prospective studies conducted in the general U.S. population. For example, Lee et al. 34 demonstrated that higher levels of physical activity were associated with prevention of long-term weight gain among middle-aged and older adults. The discrepancy between the present study and prior longitudinal studies may be attributable to several methodological factors. First, the current study used a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to capture long-term behavioral influences on adiposity. Second, the relatively small sample size may have reduced statistical power to detect modest associations. Third, physical activity was assessed using self-reported IPAQ-SF data, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Additionally, many participants in this study were categorized as moderately active, thereby reducing variability in physical activity levels and potentially limiting the detection of significant differences across body fat status groups.

Similarly, fruit and vegetable intakes were not significantly associated with body fat percentage in this study. These findings align with studies conducted among Brazilian immigrant populations that observed no relationship between fruit and vegetable consumption and weight status. 35 However, findings among Nigerian immigrants have been mixed. Duru 10 reported an association between fruit intake and obesity among Nigerian Igbo immigrants in the United States, whereas no relationship was observed for vegetable intake. Moreover, evidence from larger prospective cohort studies has reported contrasting findings. Bertoia et al. 36 found that increased fruit and vegetable consumption was associated with less long-term weight gain among U.S. adults followed over a 24-year period. Differences between the current study and these longitudinal investigations may reflect variations in study design, dietary assessment methods, and sample characteristics. The present study assessed dietary intake using three 24-hour recalls, which may not fully capture habitual dietary patterns or long-term dietary behaviors associated with weight gain. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size and limited variability in fruit and vegetable intake may have contributed to the absence of statistically significant findings.

The study also found no significant association between red meat intake and body fat percentage. This contrasts with findings from Duru, 10 who reported a positive relationship between meat consumption and obesity among Nigerian immigrants, and with the EPIC-PANACEA study by Vergnaud et al., 37 which found that higher meat consumption was associated with greater long-term weight gain in European adults. Several methodological factors may explain these inconsistencies. Unlike prospective cohort studies that evaluate long-term dietary exposure and weight change, the present study used a cross-sectional design and assessed dietary intake over a limited three-day period. Such short-term dietary assessment may not adequately reflect habitual meat consumption patterns associated with adiposity development. Additionally, the relatively modest sample size and heterogeneity in dietary practices among participants may have attenuated observable associations.

Although statistically significant associations were not identified, the dietary findings remain clinically relevant. Fruit intake among participants fell below U.S. dietary recommendations of 1.5–2 cup equivalents per day, 38 while red meat intake exceeded recommended levels of 1.7-2.6 ounce equivalents per day. 39 These findings suggest potential areas for culturally tailored nutrition interventions aimed at promoting healthy dietary behaviors and reducing chronic disease risk among Nigerian immigrants.

Limitations and future research

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding causality or temporal relationships between health behaviors and overweight/obesity. Longitudinal studies are needed to better understand how acculturation and lifestyle behaviors influence adiposity over time among Nigerian immigrants. Second, the use of self-reported physical activity and dietary intake measures may have introduced recall bias and social desirability bias. Although validated tools such as the IPAQ-SF and ASA24 were used, objective measures such as accelerometry and food diaries provide more accurate estimates of habitual behaviors. However, these methods were not feasible in the current study because of financial, logistical, and participant burden considerations. Future longitudinal studies using objective behavioral measures are warranted.

Third, most participants were highly educated and had relatively high household incomes, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader Nigerian immigrant population in the United States. Higher socioeconomic status may be associated with healthier lifestyle behaviors and greater access to health-promoting resources, potentially underestimating the burden of obesity and unhealthy behaviors in more socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. Future studies should include more diverse samples to improve representativeness. Fourth, the study utilized convenience sampling and recruited participants primarily through churches, student organizations, and social clubs. This may have resulted in a relatively homogeneous and health-conscious sample that is not representative of the broader Nigerian immigrant population in the United States, thereby limiting generalizability. Lastly, the relatively small sample size may have limited statistical power to detect smaller but potentially meaningful associations between health behaviors and body fat percentage. Although variables such as age, sex, length of residence in the United States, and education level are recognized as important confounders in obesity research among immigrant populations, they were not included in the regression models, which may have resulted in residual confounding of the observed associations.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes novel evidence by examining body fat percentage rather than BMI among Nigerian immigrants in the United States. Body fat percentage provides a more direct assessment of adiposity and may improve obesity classification compared with BMI alone. The study also utilized validated dietary and physical activity assessment tools, thereby strengthening methodological rigor.

Conclusion

This study examined the relationships between selected health behaviors and body fat percentage among Nigerian immigrants living in the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex, U.S. Although the outcomes showed no significant association between lifestyle behaviors and body fat percentage, it was observed that the prevalence of obesity in the study population is lower than that of several U.S. ethnic groups, but higher than that in indigenous Nigerians. The study also demonstrated low daily consumption of fruits, while intake of red meat was higher than recommended by U.S. dietary guidelines. These findings suggest the need for further investigation using larger longitudinal studies to better elucidate their potential implications for obesity-related health outcomes in this population.
