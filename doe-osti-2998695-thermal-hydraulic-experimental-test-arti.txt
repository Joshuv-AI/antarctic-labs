Executive Summary
 The Thermal Hydraulic Experimental Test Article (THETA) is currently installed in the Mechanisms
Engineering Test Loop (METL) 28” test vessel #4. Both the primary and secondary sodium systems remain
online to facilitate continued testing. This fiscal year, work was performed using a COMSOL Multiphysics
magnetohydrodynamic model to characterize flow more accurately in the secondary electromagnetic
flowmeters. Experimental campaigns were then performed to study the thermal hydraulic differences
between sodium and water as a surrogate fluid in the THETA geometry as well as a study to better
characterize and understand temperature oscillations that exist at the outlet of the core to the hot pool. A
peer-reviewed article was published in the ASME Journal of Nuclear Engineering and Radiation Science
detailing the THETA facility and providing an overview of a test that was performed with the primary and
secondary system online [1]. Work continues to develop a database to house experimental THETA data to
better facilitate collaboration with industry and laboratory partners for their use of the data for code
benchmarking/validation. THETA remains fully operational and is positioned for continued testing in fiscal
year 2026.

1. Introduction

 The Thermal Hydraulic Experimental Test Article (THETA) is currently installed in the Mechanisms
Engineering Test Loop (METL) 28” test vessel #4. Both the primary and secondary sodium systems as well
as the air side of the sodium-to-air heat exchanger remain online to facilitate continued testing. A drawing
and photo showing the THETA primary and secondary systems has been provided in Figure 1 and Figure
2.

 Figure 1: Drawing showing primary (left) and secondary (right) THETA systems. Note that the existing METL
 vessel is represented in the primary system with a gray dotted line, forming the cold pool.

 This fiscal year, work was performed using a COMSOL Multiphysics magnetohydrodynamic model to
characterize the two electromagnetic flowmeters installed in the THETA secondary system. This work
determined a function to calculate flow rate vs measured voltage and the temperature of both the sodium
and the permanent magnets.

 A test matrix was executed using the sodium filled THETA primary system to investigate differences
between sodium and water as surrogate fluids in a scaled pool type sodium cooled reactor geometry. Water-
based testing was previously performed in 2020 with the primary system. During this previous water testing,

                                                        7                        ANL-ART-309 / ANL-METL-63

 Thermal Hydraulic Experimental Test Article – Fiscal Year 2025 Final Report

the core was tripped on from an isothermal steady state flow condition to create a stratified volume in the
hot and cold pools. The parameters from this previous test were repeated using sodium. The overall
temperature profiles of this transient were analyzed, and relevant non-dimensional parameters were also
studied. This comparison provides important insights into the validity and limitations of water as a surrogate
for sodium in scaled thermal hydraulic experiments.

 A second test matrix focused on investigating temperature oscillations in the core and core outlet.
Sodium flow rate and core power were parametrically varied to characterize the oscillation phenomena and
assess their origin. Preliminary analysis indicates that the oscillations may arise from buoyancy-driven
large-scale eddies generated by temperature differentials between the core and the hot pool. In addition, a
report will follow that details the computational fluid dynamics work that is being performed to attempt to
better understand the temperature oscillations in the core and at the core outlet.

 Figure 2: Photo of THETA primary and secondary systems.

 Finally, significant progress was made on the development of a database, with a current working name
of the Sodium Technology and Reactor Components (STARC), which will serve as a centralized repository

2. Secondary System Flowmeter Analysis

 Given the THETA secondary flowmeters could not be easily calibrated in-situ and were not calibrated
in an external loop before being installed in the system, a COMSOL magnetohydrodynamic (MHD)
simulation was used to characterize flow as a function of induced voltage and temperature. This work
improves on previous work discussed in [2], including fit functions for both secondary flowmeters and the
inclusion of magnet temperature dependence.

 The secondary loop uses Schedule 40, 316 stainless steel pipe with a 1.9 cm diameter and circulates
sodium with a 240 VAC conduction pump (rated at 1.2 kg/s at 3 bar). Flow proceeds through the tube side
of the IHX, across the tube side of the AHX, through two electromagnetic flowmeters, and then returns to
the IHX. The two meters, denoted Secondary Electromagnetic Flowmeter #1 (S1) and #2 (S2) in Figure 1,
differ in magnet configuration as summarized in Table 1: S1 uses a Neodymium N42 magnet in a 5.1 cm
cube located 7.0 cm from the pipe centerline, while S2 employs a Samarium Cobalt Grade 26 magnet in a
5.1 cm diameter by 2.5 cm long cylinder positioned 3.8 cm from the pipe center.

3. Surrogate Fluid Test Comparison
3.1. Test Overview
 A test matrix was completed utilizing the THETA primary system installed in METL with sodium to
replicate water surrogate testing that was performed in October 2020, Table 2. A photo of THETA as
installed in a polypropylene vessel for water testing has been included in Figure 6. Note that during water
testing the intermediate heat exchanger (IHX) tubing was not installed so water was able to flow with little
pressure drop through the shell of the IHX. During sodium testing the IHX was installed but the secondary
system was drained to eliminate any natural convection in the secondary system sodium that may have
transferred heat out of the primary system.

 During testing the system was brought to an isothermal condition with the primary pump on to push the

minutes. When this isothermal condition was met, the test began, collecting 180 seconds of the isothermal
condition before turning the core on to the power listed for each test in Table 2. After a period of 1,000
seconds, the core electric power was then shut off and an additional 1,500 seconds of data was collected
after the core tripped off. An example of the measured electric core power as a function of time was

 The accuracy of the primary flow meters and core power meter has been provided in Table 3. There are
three K-type special limits of error (uncertainty the greater of ±1.1 °C or 0.4%) multijunction thermocouple
(TC) probes that span the length of the hot pool, penetrating the bottom of the inner vessel, or ‘redan’, to
also span the vertical length of the cold pool. The locations of these probes have been labeled as A, B, and
C in Figure 8. Notice in Figure 8 that the x, y and z axes have been defined, where the x and y axes are
centered at the vessel centerline and the z axis origin is defined as the bottom of the redan separating the
hot and cold pool. The thermocouple probes at A-C have a junction spacing of 60 mm. The three probes
have 22 junctions each at the same elevations -the elevations have been labeled in Figure 8, where the
lowest junction #1 is 76.5 cm below the redan bottom, junction #13 is located 4.1 cm below the redan
bottom, and the top junction #22 is 50.2 cm above the redan bottom. The x and y locations of the
thermocouple probes at locations A-C have been provided in Table 4.

3.2. Results
3.2.1. Series A: Hi Flow, Low Power
 This test series set the primary flowrate to roughly 10 GPM and the core electric power to 4 kWe. The
sodium and water temperature as a function of time for the thermocouple rakes at positions A and B
(locations defined in Figure 8 and Table 4) has been include in Figure 12 and Figure 13 for the bottom and
top IHX outlet window opened, respectively. As can be seen, the time when the core was turned on was
labeled with a red dashed line and when the core was turned off was labeled with a blue dashed line.

 As can be seen in Figure 12 and Figure 13, there exists a more distinct differential in the overall
temperature of the hot vs cold pools using water as a surrogate fluid for the high flow, low power case. The
cold pool stratification is enhanced in both the sodium and water surrogate test when the top window is
opened in Figure 13, as compared to the bottom window open condition in Figure 12. This is logical as hot
sodium from the IHX outlet is being delivered directly to the top of the cold pool with the top window
opened, enhancing the thermally stratified cold pool. Following the core shutoff time at the 20-minute mark,
the cold pool remains more stratified in the top IHX outlet open condition as well. After turning the core
power off, stratification overall in the hot and cold pools is rapidly diminished in the water case in the
bottom IHX outlet window open, Figure 12.

 As can be seen in Figure 12, during sodium testing, temperature continues to increase in the hot and
cold pools after the core was shut off at the 20-minute mark. Given the increase in temperature seems

adiabatic boundary condition, yielding a net energy increase in the sodium as a function of the vessel

to reduce this temperature drift and achieve a more adiabatic boundary condition.

reactor the Pe can range from about 1,000 for a small reactor and 10,000 for a large reactor such as Super-

small-scale facility like THETA whether using water or sodium.

4. Core Temperature Oscillation Testing
4.1. Test Overview
 A test matrix was completed to better understand oscillations in temperature that occur at the outlet of
core as the sodium is injected into the bottom of the hot pool. During this testing the core power was turned
on for a period of 25 minutes. The measured core power as a function of time has been plotted for Series F
test 2025_07_22_02 in Figure 27 to illustrate typical core power setting during all of the testing, where the
core was tripped on at 3 minutes into the test and remained on for a period of 25 minutes.

 Core power level and primary sodium flowrates were parametrically varied between ~36, 17 and 10 kWe
for core power and ~11.75 and 6.5 GPM for primary sodium flowrate. This testing was further split into
two distinct groups, Series F and G. Series F was performed with the secondary system installed but not
filled with sodium, so the pressure drop in the primary sodium circuit across the IHX existed but there was
negligible heat transfer out of the IHX. Series G was performed at steady state with the secondary system
brought online to balance the core power input. The test parameters are summarized in Table 7.

 The Strouhal number as a function of Richardson number was plotted for Series F in Figure 40. As can
be seen, a positive correlation exists with a quadratic fit function with an R2 = 0.77. Similarly, the Strouhal
number vs Richardson number was plotted for Series G, revealing a very good positive correlation with a
power function of R2 = 0.96, Figure 41.

 The Strouhal number as a function of Reynolds number was plotted for both Series F and G in Figure
40 and Figure 41, respectively. There exists a marginal negative correlation between St and Re for Series
F with a linear fit with R2 = 0.37 and a slightly better negative correlation for Series G with a linear fit with
R2 = 0.55.

 The Strouhal number possessing a well-defined positive correlation with Richardson number suggests
the temperature oscillation mechanism is more buoyancy than turbulence driven. This is further supported
by the negative (although weak) correlation between Strouhal and Reynolds number. The Reynolds number
is between 1,000-3,000 for all Series F and G testing so resides in a transitional/unstable flow category
between laminar and turbulent. It would be interesting to test at higher and lower Reynolds numbers to
further explore the relationship between St, Ri, and Re in future experimental campaigns.

5. Testing Database

 Significant progress was made on the development of a database, with the current working name Sodium
Technology and Reactor Components (STARC), which will serve as a centralized repository for sodium
system component and thermal hydraulic test data. THETA experimental results will be the first dataset
hosted on STARC. Currently the database is an internal program accessible by ANL staff with initial test
implementation demonstrating the structure, functionality, and download framework. Once fully
operational, STARC will provide an accessible platform for researchers and industry partners to obtain
high-quality experimental data to support the development of componentry and validation/bench marking
of systems codes and computational fluid dynamics models. Snapshots of the various pages of the current
working STARC database can be found in Figure 42 to Figure 46.

6. Conclusions
 During FY2025, the THETA facility in METL vessel 4 remained fully operational. A peer reviewed
journal article was published detailing the THETA facility and a representative steady state test [1].
Correlations relating measured voltage and sodium/magnet temperature to sodium flowrate were derived
for the secondary system flowmeters using COMSOL. A surrogate-fluid test campaign comparing sodium
and water across a range of flows, powers, and IHX outlet elevations was completed. Experiments to
quantify and interpret core outlet temperature oscillations was performed. Finally, the Sodium Technology
and Reactor Components (STARC) database was launched for internal testing.

 A COMSOL Multiphysics magnetohydrodynamic model was developed to relate induced voltage to
bulk velocity as a function of both sodium and magnet temperature for the two flowmeter designs (S1: N42
cube, S2: SmCo Grade 26 cylinder). Temperature-dependent fit functions were produced to convert
measured induced voltage and sodium/magnet temperature to flow rate.

 A surrogate-fluid campaign reproduced a 2020 water test matrix with sodium, spanning five series (A–
E) that varied primary flow (5–10 GPM), core power (2–36 kWe), and IHX outlet elevation (bottom
window #1 vs top window #6). A quasi-adiabatic boundary condition for sodium was established via
empirically tuned vessel heater duty cycles, leading to stable isothermal initial states and improved
repeatability. Results across the test matrix showed that opening the top IHX window increases cold-pool
stratification relative to the bottom window, and that water generally exhibited a larger temperature
separation between hot and cold pools than sodium under matched flow and power. The normalized pool
temperature gradient decreased with Reynolds number and increased with Richardson number for both
fluids, consistent with enhanced mixing at higher flowrates and stronger buoyancy effects at higher
stratification. As expected, Peclet numbers differed by orders of magnitude between water and sodium,
highlighting the challenge of achieving strict Pe similitude in small-scale facilities. Low-power cases
(Series D) yielded weaker correlations, likely from marginal temperature differentials.

 The core temperature oscillation tests (Series F and G) revealed coherent, minutes-scale thermal
fluctuations at and upstream of the core outlet under both adiabatic-like (secondary drained) and
heat-rejected (secondary operating) conditions. Oscillation amplitudes increased with core power and were
more pronounced at lower primary flow, with dominant frequencies typically 0.002–0.017 Hz, as shown
by FFT analysis. Measurements within the core barrel/insulator confirmed that the phenomenon originates
in or near the core region rather than being solely a hot-pool mixing artifact. A dimensionless analysis
demonstrated a strong, positive correlation between Strouhal and Richardson numbers (especially in Series
G), and a weaker negative correlation with Reynolds number, indicating that buoyancy-driven, large-scale

3,000, transitional). These data provide a rigorous basis for ongoing CFD efforts to isolate the dominant
instability mechanisms and to assess potential design or operational mitigations.

 The STARC database progressed from concept to a functioning internal platform with structured
metadata, document linkages, and downloadable datasets. THETA constitutes the inaugural data collection
on STARC, establishing the template for future sodium component and phenomena repositories that will
accelerate industry–laboratory collaboration and code validation.
