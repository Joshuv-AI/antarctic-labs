INTRODUCTION
 This project supported the U.S. Department of Energy's (DOE) mission to reduce the
environmental and climate impacts of fossil fuels and industrial processes, contributing to the
goal of achieving net-zero emissions across the U.S. economy. The primary objective is to conduct
a feasibility study for a Regional Direct Air Capture (DAC) Hub in Bay County, Florida. This hub
takes advantage of the region’s deep, permeable saline aquifers (Tuscaloosa Group, 1,500–2,150
meters deep), which have been the focus of numerous geological storage studies.
 The DAC Hub aims to capture, store, and/or utilize at least 1,000,000 tonnes of CO2 from the
atmosphere annually. To achieve this, the project team is designing a system with an initial
capacity of 50,000 tonnes per year.
 The Hub will also include the following components:
 1. Clean energy solutions to power the DAC systems with renewable energy.
 2. Pipeline infrastructure to transport the captured CO2 to storage sites.
 3. Geological storage facilities for safely injecting and sequestering the CO2.
 4. Collaboration with minority-owned, small businesses to address environmental justice,
 diversity, equity, inclusion, and Justice40 goals, while engaging regional stakeholders.

SUMMARY
 This Conclusion Report consolidates the key accomplishments achieved to date and outlines
the remaining and proposed work required to ensure the successful construction of the DAC
systems.

Description of the DAC Hub ownership and team
 The project team has evaluated various consortia and legal structures for ownership of the
DAC hub in the Florida Panhandle. Based on this review, the initial conclusion is that a for-profit
limited liability company (LLC) is the most suitable model for the consortium. The consortium will
be made up of a lead organization and partnership members. These different groups will have
different roles and responsibilities as per their position in the organization.

 The DAC Hub site is located at Florida Power and Light's (FPL) Lansing Smith Electric

This location was chosen due to the plant’s prior involvement in a Department of Energy (DOE)
study on carbon sequestration, which identified favorable geology for the safe storage of

Description of the CO2 transport route
 Transportation from the DAC facility to the geological storage area will occur via a short
pipeline located on the Lansing Smith Electric Generating Plant campus. The pipeline will run
from the capture operations to the well head system.

Description of CO2 storage sites
 Extensive data has been gathered to characterize the reservoir properties of potential
injection formations near the DAC Hub as part of the DOE-funded BEST project (DE-FE0026140).
The Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) conducted a comprehensive assessment of surface
and subsurface conditions for brine injection, including detailed core analysis and permeability
estimates. This study provided crucial preliminary data to support site selection for the Florida
DAC Hub project.
 The Lower Tuscaloosa formation in this area offers over 2,000 feet of potential sequestration
reservoir. Given its highly favorable characteristics for sequestration, a high-level scoping analysis
of the geological storage area was conducted. To assess injectivity, a layered reservoir model
based on data from the BEST project was developed, and a simplified simulation was performed
using a single CO2 injector well. For a conservative estimate, the simulation included
approximately one-third of the total reservoir thickness, plus a confinement layer. Initial results
suggest that an injection capacity of 1.9 million tonnes per year per well over 20 years is feasible,
maintaining a compact CO2 footprint. Despite potential heterogeneity and conservative
assumptions, the formation is expected to provide ample injectivity and storage capacity for the
project, with significant potential for increased storage through additional wells or access to
deeper sandstone layers.

Description of the Anchor DAC technologies
 The two “anchor” DAC tenants for the Florida DAC hub are Heirloom and GEVAR.
Technology descriptions are outlined below. It is anticipated that additional DAC technologies
will be introduced into the Hub in the future.

Heirloom DAC Technology
 Heirloom’s DAC technology accelerates the natural ability of limestone (calcium carbonate)
to capture carbon dioxide directly from ambient air through a process that can be powered
entirely by renewable energy. The capture process, shown in Figure 3, is divided into two steps:
carbonation and regeneration. The carbonation step occurs in thin layers of calcium hydroxide
powder that is spread out onto large surface area trays. The trays are then stacked into vertical,
tiered contactor structures to maximum air-to-sorbent contact and minimize land use. The use of
tiered structures also makes the process modular by allowing for the carbonation capacity to be
increased incrementally rather than being fixed at the initial time of deployment.
 The regeneration step consists of two stages: calcination and hydration. In the initial step,
calcium carbonate is sent into a high temperature electric reactor where it decomposes into
calcium oxide and CO2. The high temperature reactor is powered by renewable electricity which
allows for the capture of a high-purity stream of CO2 directly from the reactor. In the second
stage, the calcium oxide is then hydrated to form calcium hydroxide or exposed directly to air
where it will naturally hydrate. This hydration reaction is exothermic and presents a suitable
opportunity for heat recovery. The resulting calcium hydroxide is then reused as the sorbent
material in the capture step of the system. Heirloom currently operates the first and only
commercial Direct Air Capture facility in North America (shown in Figure 4) and the facility is
powered by 100% renewable energy.

GE Vernova Advanced Research DAC Technology
 GE Vernova Advanced Research (GEVAR) is developing sorbent-based direct air capture (DAC)
materials, binder systems and sorbent-integrated contactors to reduce the cost and improve the
efficiency of DAC. Sorbent based capture materials are chemical structures with high surface area
and high porosity that have an affinity for CO2. Additionally, these chemical structures can be
modified with amines to increase the loading (capture) of the CO2 per mass of sorbent. The
sorbent must be paired with a binder (for example, a polymer) that integrates with a contactor
or support, enabling the sorbent to be in direct contact with the air.
 Figure 5 depicts the design of the modular system. Each module consists of a fan, sorbent,
heater, and vacuum system. Fans are used as the motive force to move air over the sorbent. The
sorbent captures CO2 from the air in an adsorption process. Once the sorbent is saturated with
CO2, the fan is turned off, the sorbent material is heated and/or subjected to a rough vacuum,
and the CO2 is desorbed from the sorbent. This process regenerates the sorbent for another
cycle of adsorption/desorption. There are two methods used to heat the sorbent, direct and
indirect. The direct method involves blowing a heated fluid, such as steam, directly over the
sorbent. While this method efficiently heats the sorbent, direct contact with the heating medium
reduces the life of the sorbent. GEVAR proposes using the indirect heating method, where the
heating medium flows through a separate passage, but in close thermal contact to the sorbent.
Using conventional heat exchangers as contactors, such as the tube-in-plate type, enables
indirect heating. The CO2 is collected and purified and can then be compressed for sequestration
or utilization. The typical process using sorbents not only adsorb/desorb CO2 but also
adsorb/desorb H2O. This water can be captured and utilized elsewhere in the plant.

 Figure 5. GE Vernova DAC Concept

 GEVAR is a diversified industrial research laboratory with core competencies including
advanced materials design, process engineering and scale-up, that leverage decades of materials
commercialization, systems modeling and integration, thermal management, and prototype
manufacturing. Over the last 3 years, this material design and scale up expertise has been utilized
to develop over 350 unique CO2 sorbent compositions, producing over 10s of kg of the most
promising sorbent. These material takes advantage of the high porosity and surface area

Balance of Plant (BOP) Description
 The DAC facility at the Lansing Smith Electric Generating Plant will use a shared OSBL system
to supply utilities for both technologies. This approach will not only reduce capital and operating
costs but also minimize the overall footprint.
 The project team is currently evaluating renewable energy sources to meet the facility’s
energy demands. The team will identify and assess existing resources, as well as explore
upcoming renewable energy projects. Additionally, the team will examine the potential for
incorporating supplemental energy sources into the facility’s design in future phases.

Project Oversight
Safety Culture
 The project team is committed to fostering and maintaining a strong safety culture with the
FL DAC hub. To ensure this, the team will adopt the safety program currently in place at FPL’s
Lansing Smith Electric Generating Plant. Given that the DAC system is a novel industrial setup,
the team will collaborate closely with FPL's safety and operations team to review the existing
safety program and work to tailor in additional elements to address the unique safety
considerations posed by the new DAC system.
 The initial high-level assessment of potential safety hazards that could be associated with the
production and transport of the CO2 product. Further review and evaluation of potential hazards
will be carried out during Phase 0b. Based on these findings, mitigation plans will be developed
and integrated into the initial system design.

Security Considerations
Site Security
 Site security measures will follow the established protocols of the Lansing Smith Electric
Generating Plant. Smith Plant follows the Enterprise Low Impact Security Procedures. This will
involve limited access to authorized personnel by utilizing badge readers and brass keys which
followed NERC Guidelines. Access control at a minimum will utilize physical security measures
such as fences, security cameras, controlled entry and exit points, and employee ID checks that
are monitored 24/7. Every person that enters and leaves the site is accounted for.

Cybersecurity
 A cybersecurity plan was developed during the negotiation process of the award. This plan
follows NIST 800-53 standards for security and privacy controls of information systems. Since
being awarded, there have been no changes to the cybersecurity protocols that were established.

Air Permitting
 The Clean Air Act requires the United States Environmental Protection Agency (U.S. EPA) to
set National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for pollutants that cause or contribute to air
pollution which may reasonably be anticipated to endanger public health and welfare. The U.S.
EPA has set NAAQS for the six criteria pollutants: carbon monoxide (CO), lead (Pb), nitrogen
dioxide (NO2), ozone (O3), particulate matter (PM), and sulfur dioxide (SO2).
 Nonattainment areas are designated regions within the country where measured
concentrations of one or more criteria pollutant exceed the NAAQS or contribute significant
amounts of pollutants to an area that measures air quality that exceeds the standard. If a state
has nonattainment areas within its borders, the state must submit a state implementation plan
(SIP) to U.S. EPA detailing steps necessary to achieve the standard. Nonattainment areas which
are later redesignated to attainment are considered maintenance areas.
 Since establishing the first NAAQS in the 1970s, U.S. EPA has continued to conduct periodic
reviews and adopt appropriate revisions. U.S. EPA’s NAAQS Table does not list an assessment of
Bay County, Florida based on the 8-hour Ozone criteria based on the 2015 standard.
Water Permitting
 The project owner will need to apply for a permit from the Florida Department of
Environmental Protection (DEP) for additional water uptake and wastewater discharge. The DAC
facility's treatment system is intended to provide both the raw water supply and wastewater
treatment for this project.
Stormwater Permitting
 Florida’s National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) stormwater program
regulates stormwater discharges, and discharges associated with industrial and construction
activities. This program covers both industrial and construction activities.
 Operators of industrial facilities that meet the criteria for coverage under the program must
obtain a permit and implement a stormwater pollution prevention plan. In certain instances,
regulated operators may be eligible for an exclusion from the requirement to obtain a permit.
 Florida’s NPDES Stormwater Program regulates discharge of stormwater to surface waters or
to a municipal separate storm sewer system from construction activities that disturb more than
one acre or are part of certain larger projects that disturb more than one acre. Operators of
construction activities that meet the criteria for coverage must obtain an NPDES stormwater
permit and implement a stormwater pollution prevention plan.
Sequestration Permitting
 Wells injecting CO2 for geologic sequestration are classified as EPA Underground Injection
Control (UIC) Class VI wells. The EPA UIC program regulates all aspects of the injection wells of a
sequestration project including project siting, well construction, injection operations, testing and
monitoring, emergency response, financial responsibility, and eventual plugging and closure of
the wells and injection sites. The UIC program implements the regulations through permitting,
site inspections, required reporting, and compliance reviews to ensure that well
owners/operators comply with the regulations. Regulations for Class VI wells are the most
rigorous of the UIC program.

Preliminary Life Cycle Analysis
Summary
 The Florida DAC hub involves two technology providers, together capturing over a total of
150,000 tonnes of CO2 per year. Located in Bay County, Florida, the hub serves the surrounding
regions as well. The two providers share an OSBL and common utilities, including the final CO2
compression process. Once compressed and liquefied, the CO2 is delivered to Carbon America,
the operator of the planned Class VI well.

Heirloom

regeneration. The carbonation step occurs in thin layers of calcium hydroxide powder that is

GE Vernova Advanced Research
 General Electric Vernova Advanced Research’s technology employs sorbent based direct air
capture materials, binder systems, and sorbent-integrated contactors. These innovations work
together to improve the DAC system energetics and reduce the cost of capture. The sorbent-
based capture materials used by the system are chemically structured to have a high surface area
and high porosity with an affinity for CO2. The sorbent material must be paired with a binder
that integrates with a structural support, enabling the sorbent material to be in direct contact
with the air.
 This DAC technology captures atmospheric CO2 by applying a cyclic vacuum-temperature
swing adsorption and desorption process. During the adsorption phase, fans draw air into the
system. The CO2 within the air is chemically bound to the sorbent material and the CO2 depleted
air is released back into the atmosphere. During the desorption phase, the CO2 saturated sorbent
material is heated under vacuum and the CO2 is then released from the sorbent material and
collected. When the sorbent material is cooled the capture cycle is ready to start again.
