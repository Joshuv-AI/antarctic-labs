ABSTRACT

 Multiple planetary boundaries considered a safe operating space for humanity have
been exceeded in recent years, with twice as many boundaries transgressed in 2023 as in
2009. Bioenergy systems are unique in that they can interact with and improve many of the
transgressed boundaries directly, including multiple geochemical cycles, water and land
use, and climate change. Among bioenergy systems, microalgae-based environment-
enhancing energy (E2-energy) are promising bioenergy systems for drop-in biofuels,
valuable materials, chemicals and therapeutics, while making deep emission cuts and
remediating wastewater, all without competing for agricultural resources. As with many
microbial systems, scale-up efforts have not yet successfully translated laboratory results
and algae-based fuels and materials remain economically uncompetitive without
improvements in system and strain engineering. Here we bridge the translation gap to field-
relevant performance and increased technology readiness level (TRL) by system-informed
strain development of industrially relevant algal species. This work takes a three-pronged
approach using applied genomics and environmental, and techno-economically informed
indices to improve algal strains under production-relevant conditions. I) Use of
mutagenesis and adaptive laboratory evolution to improve low-productivity winter yields
in Nannochloropsis, II) development of a high-throughput proxy for energy storage content
in starch-rich algal genus Picochlorum to screen for improved strains for sustainable
aviation fuel yield and biodegradable plastics and materials, and III) develop and screen a
barcoded mutant library under acre-scale, pond relevant conditions for improved alkalinity
tolerance and productivity potential. This approach both screened and developed mutants
for further testing with improved productivity and yield (~15% increase in productivity in
P. renovo, ~10% increase total FAME, and ~38% growth increase with a ~50% increase
in high-value polyunsaturated fatty acids, without a trade-off in total lipids in N. oceanica)
when grown in multiple types of environmental photobioreactors (ePBRs). Additionally,
key genes identified by multi-omics approach, including genetic variant analysis and
barcoded insertions, copy number variation, and transcriptomics, will inform future
targeted engineering work via identified genes of interest involved in central carbon
metabolism and stress-responsive regulation. It also yielded a high-throughput energy
screening method amenable to automation (R2 = 0.92 and RMSE = 3.4% carbohydrate).
Future work will entail recapitulating observed phenotypes by targeted engineering of
genes of interest into WT and ancestor strains. Additionally, expansion of the barcoded
mutant library in number of mutants and screening conditions, including other field-
relevant stressors such as high dissolved oxygen (DO), and expansion of library
development into other industrially relevant strains. Work has started on library
development of haploid cousin Picochlorum soloecismus, as well as promising
Chlamydomonas pacifica; an extremophilic algae closely related to the model organism,
Chlamydomonas reinhardtii. Together, these advances will continue to provide improved
strains, scalable screening tools, and mechanistic targets for future engineering to
accelerate translation of microalgae-based E2-energy systems to address the transgression
of planetary boundaries constituting a safe operating space for humanity.

Keywords: microalgae, bioenergy, waste-to-energy, environment-enhancing energy,
applied genetics, adaptive laboratory evolution, high-throughput screening

 CHAPTER 1
 INTRODUCTION

 1.1 Background and Motivation

 The threat of rapid, exponential and irreversible warming of the planet constitutes one
of the most pressing existential threats humanity faces. The breakdown of natural systems,
rise in extreme weather events, drought, and loss of freshwater resources are all critical
challenges for humanity. The Earth is now out of equilibrium as anthropogenic activity has
pushed the planet outside of the safe operating zone in six of the nine planetary boundary
conditions that sustain life (Richardson et al., 2023). This indicates that our world is now
in a "danger zone" for stability, with increased risk of crossing irreversible, large-scale
environmental thresholds.
 Humanity is also struggling with how to manage a myriad of waste materials that
continue to accumulate. At the end of 2021, the mass of anthropogenic materials exceeded
Earth’s biomass for the first time (Elhacham et al., 2020). Petroleum based plastics will
account for ~20% of global, annual oil production by 2050, with total, global plastic
demand estimated to triple by 2060 (Arora et al., 2023). The United Nations and its Food
and Agricultural Organization (FAO) estimate a significant increase in the materials
needed to sustain human civilization; a 60% increase in the amount of food, 50% increase
in energy, 40% increase in the amount of clean water (Catone et al., 2021). There are also
a number of chemicals of emerging concern (CECs) that are proliferating in the
environment, including per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), Pharmaceutical and
Personal Care Products (PPCPs), endocrine disrupting chemicals (EDCs), heavy metals,
pathogens and toxins from municipal, agricultural and industrial runoff that are not
currently well monitored or remediated (Abdelfattah et al., 2023; Delrue et al., 2016; Mojiri
et al., 2022). Further, there are plethora of xenobiotic polymers and chemicals that are
negatively impacting natural systems (Lau et al., 2020; Malla et al., 2018)
 Meeting these demands without continuing to overshoot planetary limits requires
technologies that couple resource recovery with emissions reductions; especially in
wastewater and wet-waste streams. Conventional wastewater treatment methods are
antiquated, aging, energy-intensive, and produce potent greenhouse gases such as nitrous
oxides (NOx) (Kampschreur et al., 2009; Law et al., 2012). Thus, sustainable methods to
increase production of food, energy, and to treat wastewater are needed to improve
environmental health and security (Hansen et al., 2013; Hansen, Sato, Hearty, et al., 2016;
Hansen, Sato, Kharecha, et al., 2016; Raftery et al., 2017; Ripple et al., 2022; Steffen et
al., 2018).
 Out of the available array of developing solutions, bio-based mitigation strategies have
the potential to tackle all three of these challenges. Bioenergy based systems are one of the
few currently deployable, scalable options that interact with, and directly impact, the global
cycling of carbon at scale. If adroitly applied, biological organisms can reduce the
atmospheric concentration of CO2 to sustainably produce ‘carbon-negative’ fuels and
biomaterials (Z. Ma et al., 2022; J. Wang et al., 2021a).

 1

 Among the biological sources for energy molecules or “feedstocks”, algae are among
the most prodigious primary producers and photosynthetic species on the planet (Chapman,
2013). Eukaryotic algae have significantly higher photosynthetic efficiencies, increased
absorption of CO2, and grow faster than terrestrial plants (Barry et al., 2015; Iqbal et al.,
2025; Omokaro et al., 2025) Additionally, many species of algae are now amenable to
genetic engineering, which can result in a wide variety of new bio-products, including bio-
based plastics to replace fossil fuel sourced plastics, nutraceutical and high-value chemical
production (e.g. terpenes and protein-based therapeutics)(Chhandama et al., 2023; J. A. V.
Costa et al., 2022; Molazadeh et al., 2019; Moshood et al., 2021; Rasala et al., 2010). Many
strains of algae are known to use starch and polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) as primary
energy sources, which provide a unique, renewable material for biodegradable polymers
and materials poised to replace petrochemical plastics (Arora et al., 2023; S. S. Costa et
al., 2019; Ranganadhareddy, 2022).
 Algae can also provide a myriad of environmental services. Algae can capture and
recycle nutrients from a plethora of waste streams, prevent eutrophication, reduce GHG
emissions, all while shifting the energy balance to be net-positive (Gude, 2015; Skaggs et
al., 2018). Algae are efficient at removing total chemical/biological oxygen demand
(COD/BOD), ammonia, phosphorus, nitrate, and heavy metals (Zhao et al., 2018) and have
shown promising initial results to treat pharmaceutical and municipal wastewaters for other
CECs (Mojiri et al., 2022; Sousa et al., 2022), while producing a panoply of biological
products (Catone et al., 2021). Algae can also be incorporated into waste-to-energy
systems, sometimes referred to as “Environment-enhancing energy (E2-energy)” systems
to valorize a variety of organic waste-streams and replace a significant portion of petroleum
derived crude oil. In recent years, the Advanced Fuels and Feedstocks Office (formerly the
Bioenergy Technologies Office) within the Department of Energy has expanded the scope
of its vision of producing biofuels primarily from algal and terrestrial feedstocks to include
unused, wet organic wastes. A recent resource analysis indicated that ~65% of ~77 million
dry tons of waste per year was not being valorized or recycled to any useful purpose:
including 14 million tons of residual municipal sludge and bio-solids. These waste
resources can be converted into bio-crude oil to offset the use of petroleum transportation
fuels with renewable alternatives. Indeed, Zhou et al. (2013) describe an iteration of an
algae-based E2-energy system that treats wastewater, conducts nutrient recycling, and
processes the algal biomass via hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL). This system
demonstrated the potential to increase harvestable algal biomass over tenfold from what
could be produced by initial fertilizer concentrations via nutrient recycling following fuel
conversion, utilizing only readily collectible human, animal and food wastes to produce
0.3-1 billion gallons of bio-crude; replacing as much as ~37% of total petroleum
production.

1.2 Barriers and Challenges to Industrialization of Algal Systems

 While these E2-energy/waste-to-energy systems are promising and technically feasible,
their viability is hampered by cost competitiveness with petroleum-based products. Most
‘purpose-grown’ algae farming or cultivation schemes can produce fuel at a cost of > $5-

 2

6/gallon of gasoline equivalents (GGE)(Cheng & Luo, 2022). However, recent
technoeconomic analysis (TEA) of a sludge conversion and upgrading integrated
biorefinery with coproducts demonstrated that a minimum fuel selling price at ~$3.49/GGE
is possible, providing a feasible framework to build integrated biorefineries and waste
valorization processes to produce sustainable liquid transportation fuels (Saral et al., 2022;
Snowden-Swan et al., 2017). Recent estimates from the Pacific Northwest National
Laboratory indicate that 15-25% of the nation’s aviation fuel demand could be met by
expanding these fuel conversion pathways to include other biomass conversion
technologies (e.g. gasification with methanol synthesis) using municipal solid wastes. The
strategic location of plants near fuel distribution centers and major airports, in addition to
the incorporation of carbon credits, can further reduce the renewable identification number
(RIN) adjusted cost to ~$2.71, decreasing the overall carbon intensity of the aviation sector
~10-18% (Seiple et al., 2023) in the near term. These types of environment-enhancing
energy systems have the potential to expand significantly after development of the
necessary infrastructure, nutrient recycling and amenable biomass production systems as
outlined in Zhou et al., 2013.
 Even with promising TEA projections for waste-to-energy strategies, expansion of
algal industrial processes to commercially relevant scales remains the most significant
challenge and impediment to realizing the potential of algal biotechnology. Much work has
been done to understand and optimize algae productivity and resilience in large scale
systems, yet these improvements have not yet been sufficient for industrial uptake.
Government investments in strain screening, selection and process optimization have
resulted in >50% improvement in limited outdoor trials (McGowen et al., 2023), these
improvements have not yielded consistent, reliable productivities that average 25g m-2 d-1
year-round (the current target to achieve economic parity with petroleum based fuels).
Further, strain engineering without consideration to the cultivation system into which the
strains are deployed, and the micro-environments they will be exposed to, continue to stifle
lab-to-field translation efforts.
 Efforts to boost algal productivity through strain development, as well as whole-system
optimization to maximize growth, biomass conversion efficiency and reduced costs are
being pursued. Prior algae screening and strain development pipeline have made strides in
the bioprospecting and improvement of industrially relevant strains for improved
productivity, composition and culture stability to reduce biofuel cost (e.g. almost 10-year
Development and integrated screening, cultivar optimization and verification research
(DISCOVR) (M. H. Huesemann et al., 2024; McGowen et al., 2023). Additionally, early
commercialization efforts for algae-based biofuels have resulted in field adapted strains
that are currently still in use for the production of potent antioxidants and nutraceuticals,
such as eicosapentanoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA); otherwise known
as Omega-3 fatty acids, a powerful antioxidant capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier
(Barnes et al., 2021; Wen et al., 2024).
 To maximize algae production at industrial scale, a crop rotation strategy must be
utilized to tailor the optimal conditions of the production strain to seasonal temperatures.
As such, there are several, key strains that can potentially cover a wide range of the seasonal
variation in the areas where algae can be cultivated on arid, otherwise non-arable land as
part of a crop rotation strategy. The Picochlorum genus was identified and characterized
during a bioprospecting effort (Dahlin et al., 2019a) to be halotolerant, thermotolerant, and

 3

heliophillic, capable of growth in up to 105 ppt salinity, up to 40°C temperatures, with light
intensity tolerance up to 2000 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ (PAR). In the lab, Picochlorum can
reach productivities > 1g L-1 d-1, with doubling time of ~2h in seawater and high light
(Weissman et al., 2018). Outdoor productivities can reach as high as 36g m-2 d-1 (McGowen
et al., 2023); well above the 25g m-2 d-1 average goal set by the department of energy as
the benchmark for cost-competitive biofuels. Reaching this benchmark on a year-round
basis would be a major step towards the DOE’s goal of 25% transportation fuels from
biofuel by 2040 (US DOE BETO, 2016). Two key species Picochlorum renovo and
Picochlorum soloecismus, have been studied heavily, with the former having an optimal
temperature range around 35°C, and the latter around 30°C, thus potentially constituting
valuable summer-late spring-early fall production strains. A ‘production’ strain of
Nannochloropsis oceanica cultivated by Sapphire Energy, and later by Qualitas Health was
adapted in open raceway ponds for five years before isolation (Sanchez et al., 2022), and
is known to display broad resilience to low temperatures, pH gradients, pest pressures, and
unimpeded growth at full strength seawater (35ppt) over years of cultivation (Dahlin et al.,
2018; Echenique‐Subiabre et al., 2025; Jebali et al., 2025; Y. Ma et al., 2014). Therefore,
significant effort has been expended to further improve upon these strains to meet annual
averages of 25g m-2 d-1, yet seasonal rotation at the pilot-plant scale still report an annual
average close to ~17g m-2 d-1 (McGowen et al., 2023).

1.3 Industrial optimization and improvement strategies: Adaptive Laboratory
Evolution (ALE), Mutagenesis & Genetic Engineering

 To bridge the gap between achieved and desired productivities in the field, several
strain development techniques have been deployed. One of the most used methods is
adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE), where strains are exposed to increasingly arduous
environmental stressors to generate genetic adaptations over time. ALE is often paired with
mutagenesis to expedite the rate of evolution, as a technique which does not require precise,
detailed knowledge of the microbial genome. ALE has been shown to be a potent tool to
evolve strains for better growth or substrate utilization under stressful conditions or in the
presence of a novel/toxic substrate, increasing growth productivity and stress tolerance
(LaPanse et al., 2021). Pertinent examples of ALE for strain improvement from the
literature include increasing biomass in the face of high CO2 tolerance in Chlorella for flue
gas remediation and sequestration, improvement in temperature tolerances in Picochlorum,
enhancing Phaeodactylum with low pH tolerance (Su et al., 2024), and improving tolerance
and removal of toxic pollutants such as phenol (J. Wang et al., 2021b). Large screening
platforms can then be used to identify mutations and phenotypes of interest, which will be
enhanced by automated, robotics platforms for the high throughput needed to realize the
promise of leveraging ALE to improve algal crops. This approach is a promising route to
productivities and biochemical compositions that will make algal biorefineries practical at
scale (Lin et al., 2019; Moon et al., 2022).
 Other strain improvement techniques follow a “forward genetics” approach, where
large numbers of random mutations are screened, by genetic construct insertion, double-
stranded breaks in the form of ultraviolet (UV) light, or chemical mutagens which favor
single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Some studies have reported significant increases
in industrially relevant traits following UV mutagenesis in Nannochloropsis. For example,

 4

a 2X increase in EPA content has been reported following ALE by Al-Hoqani et al., 2017.
Other pertinent examples from the literature include improvement in Scenedesmus in both
biomass accumulation (1.9 à 2.4 g/L) and lipid content (40% à 55%) by UV mutagenesis
(Sivaramakrishnan & Incharoensakdi, 2017), as well as a UV mutagenesis followed by
breeding protocol resulting in 1.7-fold increase in high-value astaxanthin production from
Haematococcus pluvialis (N. Wang et al., 2016).
 Others have used adaptive laboratory evolution strategies, in conjunction with random
mutagenesis through chemical or physical means (Bleisch et al., 2022) to enhance
industrially relevant traits. This approach is promising in microorganisms with rapid
doubling times and has shown promising results in bacteria and yeast models and is being
widely deployed in microalgae as well. This rate of increased mutation expedites evolution,
and increases abiotic stress tolerance to higher temperatures and boosts growth rate and
productivity under optimal conditions (Barten et al., 2022; Fernandes et al., 2023; LaPanse
et al., 2021).
 In addition to ALE and random mutagenesis, targeted, genetic manipulation
strategies have also been developed. As a genetic model, yeast have provided valuable
insights and experimental design schematics that can be expanded into other industrial
microbes of interest, including microalgae. Full genome knockout libraries barcoded with
unique, barcoded knockout libraries have been documented in yeast to gain greater insights
in functional genomics (Giaever & Nislow, 2014). Some of these collections have
comprised greater than 21,000 mutants constituting a systematic deletion collection and
have led to several genetic spinoff technologies. Forward genetic screens in these model
organisms have proven themselves to be invaluable tools for gene function discovery and
characterization in both homo- and heterozygous alleles in diploid strains. This model has
even been successfully expanded into higher organisms, such as flies, worms, plants and
mice (Coelho, 2000; Forsburg, 2001). This approach has also been expanded into
microalgae in a model eukaryotic chlorophyte, Chlamydomonas spp. The authors created
an insertional mutant library with >58,000 mutations, gaining significant insight into the
mechanisms of photosynthetic regulation, CO2 concentration, DNA repair and ciliogenesis.
This work also improved our understanding of genotype-phenotype interaction and gene
function, expanding into homology with other model, terrestrial plants such as Arabidopsis
(Fauser et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019).
 Despite these advances, even the best characterized strain Chlamydomonas
reinhardtii constitutes a “lab-rat” model strain that does not translate well to outdoor
cultivation in real-world conditions. Thus, recent efforts have focus on building genetic
toolkits involving target gene editing via CRISPR-Cas9, CRISPR interference
(CRISPRi)(Dhokane et al., 2023; Kumari et al., 2026). Nannochloropsis is currently being
evaluated as a chassis for understanding algal lipid metabolism and as a platform for
synthetic biology due to their high lipid storage content (up to 60% by cell dry weight),
and their amenability to transgene expression due to a moderate GC content and simple
gene structure facilitating easier engineering (Poliner et al., 2018). Genetic engineering
toolkits for Nannochloropsis (Canini et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2021; Verruto et al., 2018;
Weeks, 2011), are rapidly becoming available including homology-based recombination
techniques in industrially relevant strains such as Nannochloropsis spp., including N.
oceanica and N. gaditana among others. Other striking examples are use of TALENs to
increase TAG accumulation in Phaeodactylum by 45X (Daboussi et al., 2014), as well as

 5

marker free transformation in Chlamydomonas as a proof of concept (Kasai & Harayama,
2016).
 Optimal gene expression has rarely been found to be a switch, with an “on” or “off”
setting. In fact, metabolic and gene networks are better thought of as a set of dimmer
switches, with the optimal level of expression lying somewhere in the middle. As such,
down-regulation of genes in place of total knockouts are often employed using tools such
as CRISPR interference or RNA interference (RNAi); these offer significant advantages
over traditional CRISPR-Cas9, as well as inactivated Cas9 (termed dead Cas or
dCas)(Naduthodi, Claassens, et al., 2021; Naduthodi, Südfeld, et al., 2021; Posewitz, 2017;
Trovão et al., 2022). Both attenuation of transcriptional regulator ZnCys using RNAi or by
introducing insertions into the upstream, regulatory region of ZnCys result in several
mutant strains which partitioned their carbon reserves into energy dense lipids instead of
proteins, accumulating up to 55% lipid by dry cell weight. One of these lines reduced
transcription by 90% compared to the wild type and was able to achieve an aerial
productivity of 5g m-2 d-1 lipid in a simulated solar spectrum. These results highlight the
benefit of genetic engineering tools in industrially relevant, oleaginous strains that can tap
into the potential of marine and seawater cultivation to help meet the food and energy
challenges for the projected >10 billion people on the planet by the end of the century
(Posewitz, 2017).

1.4 Approach

 Leveraging efforts from the last two decades, this work focuses on studying and
screening industrially relevant strains to improve resilience and productivity after selection
and adaptation in field-relevant selective pressures to optimize their performance at scale.
The goal of this work was to build understanding of the genetic basis of the biological
stress response(s) in industrially relevant conditions by combining i) strain selection under
field-relevant stressors with ii) high-throughput, scalable phenotyping and iii)
characterization of mutant libraries and with a barcode tracking system to identify, and
select for, the genetic basis of phenotypes that promote resilient growth in suboptimal
temperatures and pH.
 Research was first conducted to improve the annual, year-round productivity of a
field-adapted, industrially relevant Nannochloropsis oceanica. N. oceanica was chemically
mutagenized and allowed to adaptively evolve in chemostats at suboptimal growth
temperatures for enough generations to select for traits that improve winter productivity.
Chemostats are a proven tool in microbial biotechnology that allow for control of growth
parameters of microbes and optimization of productivity and utilization of novel/improved
metabolism of substrates of interest (Bull, 2010; Gresham & Hong, 2014). Some work has
been performed to date to optimize algal strains to abiotic stressors such as heat stress in
common production strains to improve productivity, environmental stress tolerance,
substrate utilization, product secretion and improved nutrient uptake kinetics for enhanced
wastewater treatment. This method allows for the development of advanced strains without
a priori genetic knowledge and without transgenic insertions, avoiding the use of a
genetically modified label, conducive for field deployment (J. Wang et al., 2021a, 2021c;
B. Zhang et al., 2021).

 6

 Second, a high-throughput method for screening industrially relevant strains was
developed to obtain a proxy for carbohydrate content in starch rich, promising industrial
algae such as Picochlorum. A 96 well plate method was developed to link mutants to
improved energy storage, and thus biofuel production potential by fermentation and
upgrading to sustainable aviation fuels.
 Finally, a genome-wide insertional library was created in an industrially relevant,
Picochlorum species, which was down-selected by previous work for its promising
performance in lab trials for abiotic stress tolerance and high productivity (Dahlin et al.,
2019a; Dahlin & Guarnieri, 2021; LaPanse et al., 2024; Weissman et al., 2018). This
mutant library was subject to high pH gradients, as often seen in acre-scale pond “dead-
zones”. The library contains barcoded inserts at the mutation sites whose relative
abundance in a mixed population can be tracked through barcode sequencing. This work
was driven by the hypothesis that expansion of forward screening techniques in this
promising chlorophyte has the potential to increase our understanding of field-relevant
genetic function and regulatory factors that can be expanded across other, deployment-
ready strains.

2.1 Abstract

 Microalgae can produce carbon-neutral fuels and high-value products; however,
their industrial scale production faces significant challenges. One critical limitation is
reduced productivity in outdoor ponds during winter months with cold temperatures and
short photoperiods severely hampering growth. While laboratory techniques such as serial
flask transfers and chemostat continuous culturing plus mutagenesis under selective
pressure are employed to enhance microbial strains, a direct comparison of these methods
in an industrially relevant alga has not been performed. In this study, we investigate a field-
adapted strain of Nannochloropsis, comparing the effectiveness of flask and chemostat
evolution cultures in improving cold tolerance and growth rates. We assess the impact of
selective pressure on biochemical composition and cold adaptation. Genomic and
transcriptomic analyses show shifts in lipid profile towards polyunsaturated fatty acids,
such as Omega-3’s/Omega-6’s, which support cellular structure at low temperatures, as
well as other key genes critical for psychrotolerance. This study validates adaptive
laboratory evolution and mutagenesis as effective strategies for developing cold-tolerant
strains, providing promising candidates for upscaling and field deployment.

2.2 Introduction

 Microalgae have emerged as promising candidates for sustainable biotechnology
applications, including biofuels, nutraceuticals, and biomaterials, owing to their rapid
growth rates, high productivity, and ability to synthesize valuable metabolites (Chisti,
2007; Wijffels & Barbosa, 2010). Among these, Nannochloropsis oceanica, a
eustigmatophyte microalga, has gained industrial relevance for its robust growth under
diverse environmental conditions and its ability to produce high levels of Omega-3
polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), which is
critical for human and animal health (Bellou et al., 2014; Hu et al., 2008). The scalability

 8

of N. oceanica cultivation, coupled with its potential to integrate into circular bioeconomy
systems, makes it a strategic organism for addressing global challenges of food and energy
security.

 Despite these advantages, the commercial viability of microalgae-based products is
constrained by the seasonal variability in growth rates, particularly during colder months
in temperate climates. Low temperatures during winter significantly reduce metabolic
activity with productivities reported usually well under 10g/m2*d (McGowen et al., 2023).
Enhancing cold tolerance and winter growth rates in industrial strains of N. oceanica is,
therefore, a critical target to improve annual biomass yields and bolster the economic
feasibility of algal biotechnology.

 Adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) offers a powerful strategy to improve
phenotypic traits such as cold tolerance through iterative cycles of selection and
propagation under defined environmental conditions. When paired with mutagenesis to
increase the rate of evolution, ALE applied to various microalgae enhances stress
resilience, lipid productivity, and other traits of industrial interest (Dragone et al., 2011;
Chen et al., 2020)(Doan & Obbard, 2012; Perin et al., 2015). This approach leverages the
natural genetic variability and mutational dynamics of microbial populations to select for
individuals with enhanced fitness under specific selective pressures, such as low
temperature (LaRoche et al., 2016). Cold-adapted microalgal strains developed through
ALE can exhibit not only improved growth at suboptimal temperatures but also enhanced
photosynthetic efficiency and metabolic robustness, which are critical for year-round
cultivation in outdoor systems (Carneiro, B et al., 2020; Singh S.P., Singh P, 2015).

 For algal biofuels and bio-products to scale to commercial levels, they must both
decrease the environmental impact, often measured by a lifecycle assessment (LCA), and
be same-cost or less expensive than their fossil counterparts, measured by technoeconomic
analysis (TEA). Recognizing this need, and keeping in mind the entire system during strain
development, we deployed a TEA and LCA informed phenotyping index to down-select
clonal isolates which would be scaled up and tested in the environmental, photobioreactors
to simulate real-world conditions (M. Huesemann et al., 2017a). Based on the state-of-
technology, lipid content remains the largest driver, regardless of conversion route to
improve the feasibility of algal biofuels by both TEA and LCA metrics. This is followed
by growth rate, productivity and robustness to allow for sufficient yield and resiliency in
the field (Wiatrowski et al., 2025).

 The use of environmental photobioreactors (ePBRs) allow for testing whether these
evolved, down-selected clonal isolates could outperform the field-adapted, industrially
relevant wildtype by these two commonly used ALE techniques. Reactors that carefully
simulate the light column, diurnal solar intensity, temperature, pH fluctuations and mixing,
have been established in recent years to assess translatability to outdoor pond conditions.
The top performing clone from each evolution experiment can be tested in the
environmental photobioreactors in a direct performance comparison to determine if a
psychrotolerant strain can be developed that would help improve the winter productivity
and overall algal biomass yield from outdoor, large-scale pond systems. In particular, the
Phenometric ePBRs are widely regarded as accurately simulating pond depth and

 9

commensurate light attenuation, as well as hydrodynamics and mixing (M. Huesemann et
al., 2016, 2017b). As such they can be used as a tool to determine field-relevant genetic
response and more accurate phenotype prediction at scale.

 While adaptive laboratory evolution has been a valuable tool for improving
microbial strains, limited studies have been performed on comparing methods of evolution,
with many experimentalists advocating strongly for one method or the other. This study
focuses on the adaptive laboratory evolution of a field-adapted, industrial strain of N.
oceanica under cold selection pressure to improve winter growth rates and annual
productivity. By systematically exploring different ALE methodologies—including
continuous culture, serial batch cultivation, and mutagenesis-assisted evolution—this work
aims to improve our understanding of the genetic and physiological mechanisms
underlying cold tolerance in microalgae. The findings are anticipated to contribute to the
development of robust algal strains tailored for large-scale production in temperate
climates, ultimately advancing the feasibility of algal biofuels and biomaterials as
sustainable alternatives to fossil-based resources. We take a comparative approach to
experimental evolution by contrasting traditional flask transfer to chemostat based
evolution plus mutagenesis to evolve psychrophilic algae from an industrial production
strain of Nannochloropsis oceanica.

2.3 Methods

2.3.1 Strains and media

 The microalgae strain Nannochloropsis strain QT001, derived from QH25, a field-
maintained strain from Imperial Texas (Sanchez et al., 2022). Multiple aliquots of the strain
were cryopreserved with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to serve as a baseline strain during
characterizations throughout the study. Strains were maintained in a cultivation
relevant medium derived from the medium described in Lee et al.

2.3.2 Flask cultivation conditions for serial flask transfer

 Flasks were grown in 50mL flasks in a Percival incubator on a diurnal cycle at 10-
16C, 16:8h light:dark cycle on Qualitas production media at 18g/L salinity (QPM18).
Components to make 1 L media, using tap water from the Fabian Garcia test site at New
Mexico state university, adding 0.56gHaHCO3, then checking total salinity using an EC
meter, adding ~17.5g NaCl to bring total salinity up to 18g before adding remaining salts
and nutrient solutions. These included 0.575 g KCl, 2.45 g MgSO4 * 7H2O, 0.195g CaCl2,
0.354mL of UAN-32 stock, 0.0628 mL 10-34-0 [(NH4)3PO4] stock, 0.002 mL 50000X
Trace Stock (100g/L Citric acid, 1.28g/L CoSO4*5H20, 18.45 g/L MnSO4 * H20, 3.79
g/L Na2MoO4 * 2H2O), and 0.024mL 10000X iron stock (500g/L 4Na-EDTA-4H2O,
100g/L FeSO4*7H2O). CO2 was enriched at 1% and illumination was set around 100uE.
Flasks were grown on OrbiShaker at 120rpm. Each flask was individually passaged
between an optical density of approximately 0.2 and 1 for eighteen months. Optical density

 10

and chlorophyll autofluorescence measurements were taken on an M2 Spectramax plate
reader.

2.3.4 Chemostat continuous culture setup

 Bespoke chemostat reactors were constructed using three inter-connected, 500mL
reactor cannisters. They were fitting with a hotplate with culture temperature probe to
maintain temperature via warming on the bottom, with magnetic stir bar to keep algae
suspended. Circular LED’s were wrapped around an external, polycarbonate tube at ~80µE
of light for growth. Fans were fitted to reduce heat buildup from the LEDs. pH probes were
connected to a bespoke controller that sparged CO2 on demand to maintain pH setpoint of
8.0. Two peristaltic pumps were connected to the inflow of reactor 1 (8°C reactor) to pump
in fresh media, with a second peristaltic pump connected to the outflow of reactor 3 (4°C
reactor). Outflow pump drained to waste receptacle. Pump flow rate was set to match grow-
rate to allow continuous cultivation at steady-state culture density as measured by OD750
on an M2 Spectramax plate reader. OD750 was checked regularly to measure average
culture density between 3 reactors. Inlet and outlet pump speeds were adjusted to keep
average culture density constant. When average culture density increased over the setpoint
after being in steady-state, pump rate was increased to bring culture back into steady state
as shown below, where the change in cell culture density (dN/dt) is equal to the net growth
rate (r) minus the flow rate (f) divided by total culture volume (V), multiplied by the culture
density. Growth rate is then calculated using this equation based off flow rate of media.

2.3.5 Chemical mutagenesis

 11

 Chemical mutagen ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) treatment included a total of four
rounds of exposure to EMS (Fisher-Scientific) for the chemostat based culture. The first 3
rounds, including for the mutant pool used to inoculate the reactors, were done with 0.9M
EMS, targeting a 90-95% kill rate in 3mL 6 well plates. The cultures in late exponential
growth phase was inoculated with EMS at a total of 3mL volume, and incubated in the dark
at room temperature on a shaker table with mild agitation for 1 hour. The fourth and final
round was done using a range from 0.5M, 0.7M, and 0.9M, split into 3, 2mL cultures in
two separate, 6 well plates. The plate was allowed to recover in the Percival under optimal
conditions (25°C, 1% CO2, approximately 50-100uE light), before being placed back into
the chemostat reactor.

for sequencing using the NEB Next Ultra II DNA library prep kit per kit instructions.
Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq generating PE150 read pairs on two
separate runs at 100X target coverage across the 28MBp genome.

were plotted using a custom R script.

2.3.7 Screening pipeline of clonal isolates using technoeconomic and lifecycle
assessment informed phenotyping index

 Clonal isolates are screened in the cold and tested using a Technoeconomic
Analysis (TEA) and Lifecycle Assessment (LCA) informed phenotyping index, which
normalized cultures on a scale from 0-1, with highest and lowest measurements of growth
rate (OD750 measured at Tfinal – Tinitial divided by total time in days), chlorophyll
fluorescence normalized to cell culture (Relative fluorescence at 430nm, excitation at
685nm (cutoff 665) divided by OD750), and BODIPY fluorescence normalized to cell
culture (BODIPY as described in a previous high-throughput, algal phenotyping paper
(Argyle et al., 2021)). These normalized indices were then weighted based on TEA and
LCA importance, with BODIPY as the lipid proxy weighted at 50%, growth and
productivity at 30%, and Chlorophyll fluorescence per OD at 20%. Culture from the bulk
flask experiments were diluted by 10,000X and 400uL of the diluted culture were placed
on f/2 media plates. Colonies were picked using sterile loops and deposited in 3mL of
modified F/2 media in deep-well, 24 well plates.

 These clonal isolates were tested in 24 well plates before being down selected on
the TEA/LCA informed index. Highest scoring clones on this index were then down
selected to be scaled up into flasks. Growth rate, chlorophyll fluorescence and BODIPY
were measured over five passages in flasks in the incubator running the 10-16°C cold
program.

2.3.8 Photobioreactor growth analysis

 Phenometric ePBRs are widely regarded as one of the best proxies for outdoor pond
growth and were used to test scalability and growth under field-relevant conditions of
clonal isolates. This is because they more accurately simulate pond depth, light attenuation,
and mixing. For ePBRs, 500 mL of cultures were grown in triplicate over three rounds of
cultivation (two rounds for clone 22). Seed culture was diluted to a starting OD of 0.2-0.5.
The first round was performed in quadruplicate, the following rounds in triplicate. Cultures

 13

were grown over a period of 9-14 days, using sparged CO2 on demand for pH control and
CO2 provision, while LED light mimicking the solar spectra was run on a diurnal cycle.
Light ramped up to a maximum intensity of 1000µE over a period of 10 hours with peak
light intensity at 12 noon. Temperature cycled from 5-15°C, with peak thermal intensity at
16:00 hours to imitate outdoor conditions like those experienced at large scale algal
testbeds in the southwest. Ash free dry weight was taken at start and endpoint using the
AzCATI standardized protocol, and the linear regression coefficient was used to convert
OD750 to g/L AFDW. OD and chlorophyll measurements were taken every other day. The
final round did not include the flask evolved culture P7C12.1 C22. Three notable reactor
malfunctions in pH injection resulted in a lack of CO2 delivery and were discarded (2 from
round 1 WT, 1 from round 1 ChEMS C30). Additional rounds were performed to have
sufficient replicates for confidence in observed results.

 To further substantiate growth, P7C12.1 C22 and ChEMS C30 were also grown in
400mL culture in the Algenuity Algem environmental photobioreactor and compared to
the background of ancestor growth over two years of Algem growth by measuring
maximum growth rate under the same growth regime for the month of January. This
included Peak PAR of 466 (µmol/m2*s), day length of 10:07 (h:min), max temp of 15°C
and min temp of 7°C. Three grow outs were performed in both of the evolved strains with
the five functional reactors; P7C12.1 C22 in triplicate and ChEMS in duplicate for each of
the three rounds due to mechanical failure of the sixth reactor.

 Clones were grown over three rounds over approximately 10 days each round, with
C22 in triplicate and C30 in duplicate. Optical density measurements were taken every
other day and compared to the Osprey project’s 27-month baseline during January script
run. The measured ash-free dry weight was performed according to AzCATI standard
protocol and the obtained coefficient by linear regression was used to calculate volumetric
productivities of Algem photobioreactors.

2.3.9 Biochemical compositional analysis

 A 50mL volume of cell culture were spun down in Falcon tubes and lyophilized in
Freeze Zone (Labconco) lyophilyzer from the flask experiments of clonal isolates as well
as the ePBR and Algem final timepoints. The biomass was then shipped to the National
Lab of the Rockies (NLR) for fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) analysis based on their
standardized, biochemical assay pipeline (Van Wychen et al., 2016). Algems reactor
baseline was compared biochemical composition from Dec. 2021 baseline of Osprey
project, where fames were determined by NLR analytical method at NM State University
using GC-MS.

2.3.10 Statistical analysis

 14

 Primary comparisons were performed by one-tailed student t-tests of the clones
against the ancestor, as the primary focus was to compare evolved strain performance
against the wildtype and not against each other, and we hypothesized that an 18-month
evolved strain would have higher growth parameters and higher lipid and PUFA content
over the wildtype. However, where there were notable differences between the evolved
clones, a single factor ANOVA was used to compare differences between all strains.

 Flask experiment clonal isolates were tested for productivity and improved
chlorophyll fluorescence & quantum yield as measured by Fv/Fm in a FluoroPen
Fluorometer and BODIPY against the wildtype by two-tailed student’s t-test, as we were
only concerned with the clones’ performance against the ancestor. A single-factor analysis
of variance was performed on the ePBR growth and chlorophyll fluorescence data due to
differences in the clones. A student’s t-test was performed to test the Algems PBR
productivity and chlorophyll fluorescence data against the ancestor. Biochemical
compositional data was tested using student’s t-test against the ancestor, except for where
it appeared there could be significant differences between the evolved strains, (Algems
total lipids, MUFA, PUFA) and so a single-factor ANOVA was used as there were
expected differences between the evolved strain and the ancestor, followed by a Tukey’s
post-hoc test. Gene ontology enrichment was performed as described in sections 2.9 and
2.10.

2.3.11 RNA sequencing and data processing

 1 mL of each sample was taken from the ePBRs on days 2 and 4 during early linear
growth phase for RNA extraction of the 2nd round of growth in triplicate for the ancestor,
flask evolved, and chemostat evolved clone. 10mL of culture was collected on ice then
spun down at 4°C at ~4000 rcf for 10 minutes in Beckman floor centrifuge, before being
flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and placed in the -80. RNA was extracted using Omega Bio-
tek extraction kit and submitted for Element RNA sequencing using the NEB mRNA
isolation module to remove mitochondrial and chloroplast RNA signal noise. Every time
RNA samples were taken, reactors were filled back to volume (500mL) with sterile MQ
water (~15-20mL per reactor). RNA seq targeted 100-fold coverage of transcriptome (10
million reads/sample based on size of transcriptome). All 18 samples (3 clones in triplicate
day 2 and day 4) received approximately 30 million paired end reads with the exception of
one which received only about 13.6 million.

 Raw paired end fastq reads were quality filtered using fastp v0.20.1 (S. Chen et al.,
2018) and aligned against the Phycocosm reference genome Nannochloropsis oceanica
CCAP849/10 using STAR v2.7.10b (outFilterType=BySJout,
outFilterMultimapNmax=20, alignSJoverhangMin=8, alignSJDBoverhangMin=1,
outFilterMismatchNmax=50, outFilterMismatchNoverReadLmax=0.04,
alignIntronMin=20, alignIntronMax=100000, alignMatesGapMax=0, outSAMtype=BAM
SortedByCoordinate, quantMode=GeneCounts). Sample alignment BAM files were
quantified for reads uniquely mapping to reference gene features using featureCounts

 15

v2.0.1 (Liao et al., 2014). Individual sample counts files were merged into a count matrix
using a custom R v4.5.1 script, with columns defining samples and rows defining genes.

2.3.12 Gene expression analysis

 All figures were generated using the R v4.5.1 package ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016)
v4.0.0 unless otherwise specified. To perform principal component analysis with the base
v4.5.1 prcomp function, reads with 1+ counts across samples were adjusted for per-sample
library size variability using trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) as implemented by the
edgeR (Robinson et al., 2010) v4.6.3 function calcNormFactors (method=TMM), adjusted
for within-sample depth using log2-counts-per-million normalization via the base R v4.5.1
log2 function with edgeR v4.6.3 cpm (pseudo counts added to 0 values), and scaled with
Z-score transformation as implemented by the base R v4.5.1 scale function. TMM-
normalized counts produced by edgeR v4.6.3 calcNormFactors (method=TMM) used
dispersion models estimated across all pairwise comparisons in a hybrid model combining
trended and tagwise estimates produced from edgeR v4.6.3 functions
estimateGLMTrendedDisp (method=power) and estimateGLMTagwiseDisp respectively
to perform differential expression analysis using edgeR v4.6.3 function exactTest.
Differential expression significance value results were corrected for multiple testing using
false discovery rate adjustment. Gene ontology (GO) term enrichment was performed on
significantly differentially expressed gene sets (p-value < 0.05) via the topGO (Adrian
Alexa, 2017) v2.60.1 function runTest (algorithm=weight01, statistic=fisher), with
resulting p-values adjusted using FDR.

2.3.13 Gene annotations

To identify Arabidopsis orthologs of genes, nearest orthologs were identified using the
Orthologous MAtrix (OMA)(Train et al., 2017) analysis using the precalculated distance
for Chondrus crispus, Bigelowiella natans (strain CCMP2755), Blastocystis hominis,
Nannochloropsis gaditana (strain CCMP526), Globisporangium ultimum (Strain ATCC
200006), Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, Volvox carteri, Chlorella variabilis, and
Arabidopsis thaliana prior to the addition of the genomic variant of P7C12,
Nannochloropsis oceanica CCAP849/10, and final OMA calculation to determine
orthologous relationships. Metabolic annotations used in Figure 7 were derived from EC
numbers as determined by the kegg gene annotations available on Phycocosm (Grigoriev
et al., 2021).

2.4.2 Clonal isolate down-selection and screening

 To better identify and isolate genotype-phenotype relationships that could lead to
insight into gene groups adaptive to psychrotolerance for cold-season productivity, clonal
isolates were screened and tested using a TEA and LCA informed phenotyping index. The
top 3 performing clonal isolates were tested for growth, chlorophyll fluorescence, energy
storage, including Omega-3 and Omega-6 content as shown in Figure 3. Both groups of
clonal isolates showed clones with improved growth rate and chlorophyll
fluorescence/photosynthetic efficiency. Furthermore, differences in the lipid profile,
including Omega-3 and Omega-6 fatty acid profiles were observed. Lastly, should these
results scale, they would translate to a meaningful improvement of both productivity and
yield of either Omega-3’s or overall lipids/biomass. Flask evolution clone 22 and
Chemostat evolved clone 30 ranked highest on the normalized, TEA/LCA informed
phenotyping index. These two clones were selected for further characterization in the
ePBRs to test their performance in a simulated pond environment.

2.4.3 Environmental Photobioreactor Testing and Validation

 After testing clonal isolates in flasks, the top performing strain from each category
was again down-selected using the TEA/LCA informed phenotyping index to be tested
against the wildtype in multiple sets of 400-500mL environmental photobioreactors that
better simulate field temperatures, light penetration, mixing, and pH fluctuations. The
performance of the top performing clonal isolate from each flask experiment (C22 for the
flask experiment and C30 for the chemostat experiment), was evaluated against the
ancestor strain in multiple environmental photobioreactor systems to test for an improved
phenotype to determine scalability as shown in figure 4. While the Phenometrics ePBRs
did not show significant differences in growth characteristics (productivity single-factor
ANOVA p-value = 0.0497, post-hoc Tukey’s test against WT p-value > 0.05, chlorophyll
fluorescence single-factor ANOVA p-value > 0.05), it did show a significant difference in
the biochemical composition. Most notably there was a significant increase in total lipid
content (P7C12.1 C22 one-tailed student’s t-test p-value = 0.042, ChEMS C30 p-value =
0.03). Notably, there was also a difference in the ash content (P7C12.1 C22 student’s t-test
p-value = 0.002, ChEMS C30 p-value = 0.023). Conversely, in the Algems ePBRS, there

2.5 Discussion

 Clones were identified and screened from two ALE experiments: flask based serial
transfer and continuous culturing-chemostats. They were screened in a way to select for
increased productivity without sacrificing energy content or robustness. Both evolution
experiments showed potential to improve on the ancestor Nannochloropsis oceanica strain
in both sets of the environmental photobioreactors. In the Phenometrics ePBRs, we saw
comparable growth with an increase in total lipid content of approximately 10%. If this
scales, agricultural yield increase of the harvestable portion of the biomass would constitute
a significant improvement. Additionally, the chemostat improved culture showed
encouraging potential for an increase in the valuable co-production of antioxidant Omega-
3 fatty acids with a borderline statistical significance. The potential to increase the target
compound by several percent as a part of a larger biomass fraction is encouraging for
potential to increase agricultural yields and warrants testing at pilot scale. In the Algems
ePBRs, significantly improved growth parameters over the baseline were observed.
Conversely to the other set of ePBRs, total lipid content did not appear to significantly
increase, but the profile of lipids did considerably change. The cold conditions appeared to
remodel lipids away from saturated fatty acids towards mono-and-poly-unsaturated fatty
acids (MUFA’s and PUFA’s), to increase membrane stability at very low temperatures
against freeze-thaw type mechanical stress.

 There are differences between the two photobioreactors used to test the evolved
strains, which could explain the difference in phenotypic results. This can be ascribed to
differences in the light path and mixing between the two reactors. The Algems having a
bottom-illuminated mixing flask with average light path of ~5cm, but different light
penetration from the middle to the sides as it is constantly mixed. This average pathlength
however is much smaller when compared to the Phenometrics ePBRs, which are designed
to operate at the average depth of an outdoor pond (approximately 20cm), and are mixed
only by a stir bar. As a result, high illumination at the top of the Phenometric ePBRs likely
saturates light tolerance and results in oxidative stress and commensurate response of lipid
production. Additionally, while both systems operate on a sparged CO2 on demand for pH
control, the sparging plus rotational mixing likely increases the gas transfer rate in the
Algems vs the Phenometrics, adding to the higher productivities obtained in Algems over
Phenometrics ePBR systems and a biochemical preference for overall biomass in the
former, while increased stress shunts carbon towards lipids as a stress response in the latter.
Both however are different from the systems in which each of the strains were evolved,
and it is notable that the best results across systems was the flask evolved culture tested in
the same flask and incubator system.

 Comparing phenotypic results head-to-head between the two methods appears to
show that they are both valid approaches for generating improved clones under simulated

 24

winter field conditions. However, the direct comparison indicates that the harsher
conditions of the chemostats, paired with mutagenesis, resulted in marginally better results
in both sets of simulated conditions. While the growth from both clones was not statistically
different than the wildtype in the Phenometrics ePBRs, the slightly higher growth,
chlorophyll fluorescence, and Omega-3 content in the chemostat clones indicates the
chemostat method to be slightly preferable. A cautionary caveat to this interpretation
however will be determined by the clone stability when up-scaled into pilot scale
greenhouse mini-ponds, as strain stability is a known concern in chemostat evolved
cultures, as well as for evolved algae strains more generally. This could largely be due to
the epigenetic and regulatory flexibility and metabolic plasticity seen in many microalgal
strains of interest.

 Key insights were also gleaned into the genotype-phenotype interaction of the
evolved strains. For instance, observed mutations in the carotenoid synthesis pathway,
shifts in pigmentation from violaxanthin towards zeaxanthin, as well as mechanisms which
indicate improved carotenoid production. This could contribute to increased absorbed light
at low temperatures to adapt energy metabolism. The principal component analysis (PCA)
shows chlorophyll fluorescence associated most closely with increased Omega-6 fatty acid
production, as well as quantum yield and productivity aligning on a similar axis. Total lipid
and total omega-3 were on large magnitude axes of their own, and it was notable that the
two most promising upscaled clones clustered most closely with the WT. Notwithstanding,
C12 also clustered more closely to total Omega-3 and productivity than other strains, and
although it did not perform quite as well as C22 on the TEA/LCA informed index, it still
constitutes another strain that could be a potential Omega-3 producer, depending on
cultivation priorities. This is also the only clone with a unique pattern of copy number
variations (CNV’s) with proteins of unknown function in putative regions of increased
coverage (supplementary figure 1), worthy of further investigation. This speaks to the
diversity of clonal isolates generated by this evolution and screening approach that could
be used for broader potential applications.

 Transcriptomics analysis coupled with gene ontology (GO) enrichment and
metabolic pathway mapping supported key findings of cold and stress adaptation. As
expected, the largest number of enriched genes were involved in some form of transcription
regulation and DNA binding transcription factor activity, asserting the importance of gene
signaling to stress tolerance from cold exposure. This is coupled with key genes involved
in light harvesting/photosynthesis modulated in adaptation to stress such as protein
phosphorylation and L-tyrosine 2-oxoglutarate transaminase activity, protein
serine/threonine and pyruvate phosphate kinase activity as potential bottlenecks and rate-
limiting steps in the cold. For instance, pyruvate kinase activity is shown to be important
for cold tolerance in arctic varieties of Chlorella (Song et al., 2020), along with other genes
related to the Pentose Phosphate Pathway (PPP; such as the up-regulated shikimate
pathway which serves as a gateway for the biosynthesis of aromatic compounds (Permann
et al., 2022).

 Additionally, up-regulation across multiple key timepoints alongside mutations
related to sphingolipids, indicate the importance of these molecules in regulating
membrane and lipid structure to prevent rigidification of the cell structure to withstand

 25

freezing stress (Fabri et al., 2020). There is also evidence that sphingolipids serve as
signaling molecules (Michaelson et al., 2016), representing a key target for engineering
efforts aimed at over-expression. In fact, one of the few, annotated genes that was identified
as significantly up regulated across both the chemostat and flask evolution, were related to
sphingosine (a key component of sphingolipids). Lastly, the observed up-regulation of
nutrient transporters and the shikimate pathway are also found in Chlorophyta and
Streptophyta isolated from the Greenland Ice sheet, and are essential to adaption to the cold
(Feord et al., 2025). Mutated genes were found involved in carotenoid and pigment
pathways and lipid remodeling, such as the VDE1 gene and sphingolipid desaturase. The
latter of which has been shown to improve cold tolerance to arctic conditions in Chlorella
(Song et al., 2020). Differential flux into spingo-/phospholipids was also observed in the
transcriptome of the chemostat evolved strain (Figure 7). Additionally, the observed
mutation in transcription factor MYB119, found only in the chemostat evolved clones, is
related to other stress relevant transcription factors in green algae (Kruse et al., 2025) and
is also shown to be one of the key families of transcription factors for a snow alga
Chlamydomonas nivalis’ adaptation to the cold (Peng et al., 2021).

 Another relevant family of transcription factor are the basic leucine zipper, with
our results showing the up regulation of a similar family of zinc finger-based proteins, the
ZZ-type zinc finger protein family, shared across both flask and chemostat evolution. A
noticeable difference however is the increased transcription of GAL4-like Zn(II)2Cys6 (or

 Due to the decreased productivity of the Flask-evolved strain, the OD achieved at
two days in the chemostat-evolved strain is like that of the day four timepoint in the flask-
evolved strain. This provides a unique comparator to understand the conserved and unique
aspects of metabolic reprogramming as a result of cold-evolution under similar crowding
conditions. Differentially expressed genes indicate several modifications to core
metabolism that may account for increased lipid accumulation and, in the case of the flask-
evolved strain, the decreased productivity (Figure 7). Most notably, the allocation of carbon
from the Calvin-Benson Cycle (CBC) is shunted significantly toward
phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) in the chemostat-evolved strain. Combined these differential
expression event indicates a significant shift of carbon out of the CCA and into the
available pool of PEP which can then be fed into the Citric Acid Cycle (TCA). The resultant
depletion of available acetyl-coA and pyruvate would theoretically leave reduced resource

 27

availability of acetyl-coA for fatty acid synthesis and elongation. In this regard, the
upregulation of branched chain aminotransferase and numerous BCAA degradation
enzymes indicates branched chain ketoacids production is significantly increased. The
subsequent production of their respective CoA esters may provide a compensatory pool of
CoA esters to reduce demand on an overtaxed pool of acetyl-CoA, which could
simultaneously result in an altered lipid profile from cold-associated lipid remodeling.

 In contrast, the flask-evolved culture does not show a comparable BCAA-catabolic
signature and does not reduce PGK, suggesting weaker diversion of CBC carbon into the
PEP pool. It also co-upregulates DGAT and TAGL, consistent with a potentially wasteful
TAG synthesis–lipolysis cycle that would dissipate carbon and energy and could depress
productivity. Notably, methylmalonyl‑CoA mutase is upregulated in Flask2, consistent
with an alternative anaplerotic feed into the TCA cycle (via succinyl‑CoA), but this
compensation would not remove the apparent energetic penalty of TAG cycling. Finally,
the chemostat evolved strain shows an upregulation into a precursor of the oxidative
pentose-phosphate pathway (OPPP), which is a primary source of NADPH, providing
reducing equivalents for redox balance in response to ROS (Sharkey, 2021), and was found
to be enhanced in Dunaliella salina under salt stress (Zheng et al., 2017), and relevant to
high light stress in intertidal red alga P. yezoensis (Meng et al., 2025). Conversely GRFP7,
a master circadian and temperature regulator in Arabadopsis (F. Xu et al., 2024) was seen
significantly upregulated in the flask evolved culture, but not the chemostat. The difference
in large gene set regulators could be a potential explanation for the different frameworks
of metabolic remodeling observed between the evolved strains in pond-relevant conditions.

 Building on this work will also entail the systematic knockout via CRISPR, or other
targeted engineering techniques, of several of these key genes in the ancestor strain to re-
capitulate the phenotype under cold-season stressors. In our case, as a haploid alga, single
knockouts and knock-downs of the top genes, followed by growth under selective pressure,
will both support the findings and provide a potential, improved production strain that can
also be tested in enclosed greenhouse photobioreactors for better containment of modified
strains.

Study Limitations

 Simulating outdoor conditions in the environmental photobioreactors (ePBR’s)
experience limitations in simulating outdoor ponds. Variability by reactor and parts can
cause fluctuations in temperature and pH control, making close adherence to desired
settings difficult. However, this variability is not dissimilar to those experienced in the
greenhouse or outdoor ponds due to location or valve malfunction. We also acknowledge
limitations in the direct comparison between serial flask transfer limited to 10-16°C
temperature swings, to chemostats ranging from 4-8°C degrees due to limitations in
incubator temperature range. However, we believe this to be indicative of average
temperatures at algal growth sites and is representative of commonly practiced serial flask
transfer based ALE, where growth under selective pressure is allowed by itself to improve
the strain without any form of mutagenesis. While this is not an absolute, direct comparison

 28

between methods under near-identical conditions, we believe it is representative of two
approaches that might improve algal strains in the cold. Our approach aims to compare
viable methods, versus attempting to ensure experimental conditions were identical under
both approaches. We also need to acknowledge the practical constraints of evolution time.
Both experiments were conducted over a period of 18 months. However, with the
chemostat culture only reaching approximately 80 generations, this is on the low side of
experimental evolution due to the high doubling time seen during the first several months
of the experiment. Lastly, we acknowledge that one of the key challenges in algal strain
development and deployment has been stability over time. Although we grew out the
strains over multiple passages and saw reasonably stable phenotypes, we know scale-up
can lead to detrimental impacts of size and reliability of phenotype changes observed. This
will be carefully monitored and measured in future scale-up work to address this key
limitation in strain development work.

Conclusions

 The next steps of this work will include scaling of an ancestor and an evolved,
clonal isolate to 100-liter, greenhouse mini-ponds in a northern NM winter to further test
scalability. This will inform the potential for growth in open pond or PBR systems for
improved Omega-3 or other nutraceutical production (e.g. carotenoids) and increased
biomass and lipids. The inducible lipid content by nitrogen starvation will also be tested to
obtain an aerial yield of lipids and Omega-3’s. Additionally, the best performing, tested
strain can be re-introduced to the continuous chemostat reactors to increase the generation
number and strengthen phenotype performance, robustness and stability. This strain can
then be used to isolate and screen clones that can be periodically tested, created an iterative
lab-field strain model that can continue to improve on cold season performance.

 These findings together, along with the obtained improved strain, will more broadly
inform the field of genes and pathways relevant to improving algae production strains to
seasonal extremes. Targeted engineering will yield insights and viable mutants that can be
deployed at scale to produce valuable fuels and materials which can be produced more
sustainably and reduce environmental impacts. This work will contribute to algae
biotechnology by improving the annual, aerial productivity needed to meet key criteria
essential for the industrial application of an algae bioeconomy.

3.1 Abstract

In the context of advancing sustainable bioenergy and biotechnology solutions, microalgae offer
significant potential due to their high photosynthetic efficiency and ability to store substantial
energy as starch. Herein, we report on the development of a high-throughput, quantitative
phenotyping assay for rapid screening and selection of carbohydrate content in starch-rich
microalgal genus Picochlorum. The assay leverages Lugol’s iodine solution for staining starch
granules, enabling optical density (OD) measurements to estimate starch and carbohydrate content
in a 96-well plate format. This approach was validated through screening wildtype Picochlorum as
well as two, distinct mutant pools including starch knockouts and a barcoded, insertional mutant
library, establishing a strong linear relationship between stained optical density and measured
carbohydrate content (R2 = 0.92, p-value < 0.001; RMSE = 3.29). We identified, and report on, two
mutants with a statistically significant alteration in carbohydrate storage utilizing this screening
technique (student’s t-test, p-values <0.05). Compared to conventional biochemical assays, the
developed assay is rapid, inexpensive, and scalable, making it suitable for integration into high-
throughput pipelines frequently used to screen large numbers of candidate strains. This platform
provides a broadly accessible tool for accelerating microalgal strain development for biofuels and
bioplastics.

3.2 Introduction

 To meet future energy demands, alternative, sustainable, biotechnology pathways are being
explored to manufacture fuel from biological feedstocks. Microalgae have emerged as a promising
feedstock as algae-derived fuels are compatible with the current refinery infrastructure (Chhandama
et al., 2023; Molazadeh et al., 2019; Moon et al., 2022; Moshood et al., 2021). In addition to fuels,
microalgae and microbial derived carbohydrates and starches are being developed as feedstocks for
biodegradable biopolymers and bioplastics, addressing another pivotal environmental challenge.
Starch based bioplastics already 16.4% of global plastic production in 2021 (Arora et al., 2023),
underscoring the demand for renewable feedstocks towards bio-degradable plastics. Furthermore,
fermentation of starch-rich biomass is an extremely mature and well-demonstrated technology for
generation drop-in, biofuels. Additionally however, with recent increasing focus on sustainable
aviation fuels (SAF), fermentation followed by deoxygenation has been shown to be more energy-
efficient than traditional conversion processes such as Fischer-Tropsch (Atsonios et al., 2015) for
the generation of SAFs. As such starch-rich algae that have a demonstrated ability to accumulate
carbohydrate-dense biomass rapidly in saline water, under intense irradiation and on marginal land,

 30

present a viable opportunity to address energy demands and the need for sustainable products
(Figure 1).
 Through independent screening studies, promising halophilic, heliophilic, and
thermotolerant algae strains in the Picochlorum family were identified (Dahlin et al., 2019a; Dahlin
& Guarnieri, 2021; S. Gao et al., 2023; M. Huesemann, Edmundson, et al., 2023; M. Huesemann,
Gao, et al., 2023). Picochlorum renovo distinguishes itself among promising strains for its high
productivity (~34g/m2*d) in high light (up to 2000 µE (micromoles of photosynthetically active
radiation per meter squared per day)), high temperature (40°C), and salinity tolerance up to 107ppt
(3X that of seawater) (Dahlin et al., 2019a). Importantly, P. renovo can store significant amounts
of energy in the form of starch and carbohydrates, constituting as much as 60% or more of total
cell dry weight (Dahlin et al., 2019a; Dahlin & Guarnieri, 2021, 2022).

 High-throughput screening and phenotyping is a staple of genetics research, allowing for
rapid identification of strains with novel traits. Recent microalgae strain development and
characterization pipelines have focused on bioprospecting and screening of promising species
which show high outdoor productivities, titers, and robustness to biotic and abiotic stressors
(Chiriví-Salomón et al., 2024; Penloglou et al., 2025). Increasingly, extremophiles capable of
thriving in inhospitable environs (e.g. excessive salinity, low pH, high temp etc.) are shown to
dramatically reduce pest and grazing pressures (Malavasi et al., 2020; Sydney et al., 2019;
Varshney et al., 2015). High throughput screening protocols have already been developed using

 31

BODIPY staining to quantify free-fatty acids in microalgae (Argyle et al., 2021). However, there
is a limited amount of work doing the same for carbohydrates in industrially relevant strains of
microalgae. Iodine-based solutions are capable of staining starch granules within microalgal cells,
making them easily visible under a light microscope, and while some attempts at quantification
using absorbance have been made, they are effective only within a limited range of measurement
(Takeshita et al., 2015; Wright, 2020) and have not been applied broadly across starch-rich,
industrially relevant strains of microalgae.
 We hypothesized that this classical “Lugol” staining method could be expanded into a
semi-quantifiable assay across growth conditions, starch accumulation, and industrial strains to
enable rapid screening utilizing an optical density reading on a plate reader and/or flow cytometer.
Herein, we describe modifications to the Lugol assay to rapidly estimate and compare starch and
carbohydrate content in microalgae, in fast-growing strains of interest. Post validation, this assay
was utilized in a proof-of-concept trial for a high-throughput phenotyping pipeline to rapidly
characterize thousands of insertional transformants of microalgal strains with increased
carbohydrate content (Mascarenas et al., Manuscript in Progress). Indeed, in recent studies
characterizing the biochemical composition of Picohlorum renovo, it is clear that high carbohydrate
content (~60% Ash-free dry weight (AFDW)) appears to readily coincide with high productivities,
enabling selection and characterization of improved strains with high biofuel/biomaterials
production potential (Dahlin et al., 2019a; Dahlin & Guarnieri, 2022). This highlights the potential
of this screening platform to advance screening, selection and deployment of promising strains with
higher carbohydrate productivities. To our knowledge, this type of high-throughput, screening
assay represents a first-in-kind application in a biotechnology relevant microalgal strain.

3.3 Methods

3.3.1 Abiotic & Bioitic Calibration and Validation

 Analytical starch (Fisher Scientific S516-500*) was diluted in F/2 media to a concentration
of 67mM. Next, Lugol’s staining solution (an ~1% w/w solution of 1/3 diiodine (I2) and 2/3
Potassium Iodide (KI)) at an initial 1:100 dilution (2µL of staining solution into 200µL 96 well
plate wells for a final concentration of ~0.57mM Lugol’s solution). A log2 serial dilution was
performed with a range of starch from 0.52mM to 67mM. For each dilution, spectral absorbance
was measured across the entire visible range (350-750nm) on an M2 SpectraMax plate reader to
determine peak absorption.
 Picochlorum renovo was grown in F/2 media in a Percival incubator at 1% enriched CO2,
32C, at ~ 175uE of light shaking on a shaker table at ~120rpm. At the end of the growth
phase/beginning of stationary phase P. renovo was stained with 0.57mM of Lugol’s solution in a
96 well plate after determining a peak absorbance at this concentration in the abiotic calibration.
Post staining, these P. renovo cells were incubated in the dark at room temperature for ten minutes
and the absorbance spectra between 350-750nm of each well was measured with an M2 Spectramax
plate reader.
 The absorbance spectra of stained WT P. renovo, unstained WT P. renovo, and the P.
renovo transformants were measured. The resulting spectra were overlaid with the absorbance
spectrum of analytical starch. Once the absorbance maxima (OD 590nm) was confirmed in cells,
P. renovo was stained with different concentrations of Lugol’s solution, ranging from 0.57-2.08mM
of stain. Pearson Correlation Coefficients were calculated, comparing the OD590 measurements to
percent carbohydrate as determined by standardized, spectrophotometric method for carbohydrate
quantification (Van Wychen & Laurens, 2013). OD 590 measurement were normalized by
subtracting the absorbance of the cell free F/2 media containing the same concentration of Lugol’s
solution from the OD590 measurement of stained microalgal cells in F/2 media.

3.3.2 Screening Studies

 A proof-of-concept for a library of P. renovo transformants were screened for traits of
interest. Wild-type P. renovo and a pool of 95 transformants were grown in deep 96 well plates
(1.7 mL) with 4mm sterile glass beads in the bottom to aid in mixing (~120 RPM), in modified F/2
media (Gillard’s F/2 media adjusted to 750ppm NaNO3, 35ppm NaH2PO4, and 1mL/L F/2 vitamin
solution) (Guillard & Ryther, 1962). The top three growth rates among this first 96 well plate were
selected by productivity measured over the growth period of approximately 6 days. Productivity
was measured by subtracting initial OD750 from final measurement over grow out period in days
to give an OD750 per day measurement. Two grow outs were performed in two plates. The highest,
average productivity from both plates for each grow-out, totaling 4 total sets of growth data was
used to determine the top performing mutants. These 3 top performing mutants were then
transferred into 3mL, 24 well deep well plates. Two sterile, glass rattler beads from Zymo Research
were added to the bottom of each well to improve mixing and reduce settling. Growth experiments
were performed again with the top 3 mutants and WT in biological triplicate under the same
incubator conditions described previously. Productivity was again measured by subtracting initial
OD from final, divided by total number of days of the growth period. At the increased scale of 3
mLs the transformants again showed improved growth rate over the WT. The three transformants
were then diluted into 50mL flasks and the growth experiment was repeated under the same
incubator conditions at 50mL volume.
Picochlorum renovo WT, Picochlorum soleocismus WT, and independent strains with mutations in
starch synthesis pathways (STA1, STA6, and PGM1) were grown in 50 and 125 mL flask cultures
in an incubator under optimal conditions (32ºC, ~175µE light, 1% enriched CO2, on a shaker table
at ~120rpm) for 6 days. Post incubation, the cultures were subsampled to measure both the percent
carbohydrate via the NREL standard method (Van Wychen & Laurens, 2013) and stained with the
calibrated Lugol’s assay to measure the starch content.
 The assay was also used in a second, independent experiment. In this second experiment,
a pool of 1000, random insertional mutants of Picochlorum renovo were generated and grown in
both flasks and 1000L mini ponds in the greenhouse conditions in May at Sandia in ABQ, NM as
described in Mascarenas et al., (MANUSCRIPT IN PROGRESS/BioRchiv). Using the optimal
staining concentration (1:50, 4µL into 200µL OD diluted culture for a final concentration of
1.04mM) the OD590nm of biological replicates were measured either using biological duplicate or
triplicate as cultivation conditions allowed, and a linear regression analysis was performed against
the measured percent carbohydrate and the averaged OD590 stained with Lugol solution.
Using Equation 1 shown below, 95 randomly chosen insertional mutants were screened in a 96 well
plate format for improved bioenergy/biomaterial potential, and the assay was utilized to
approximate final percent carbohydrate content, as shown in Figure 5.

3.3.3 Statistical Analysis

 To determine the optimum concentration of Lugol’s solution to stain algal cultures with in
the 96 well plate format, different staining concentrations and methods of normalization were
tested. Their relationship to measured carbohydrate content was assessed using Pearson’s
correlation coefficient, and simple and multiple linear regression, along with student’s t-test to
assess the impact of stained against unstained OD590.
 Normality of data distribution was tested using Shapiro-Wilk’s test. Additionally, qq plots,
residuals vs. fitted plots and variance of the residuals were plotted in addition to Shapiro Wilk’s
tests to confirm normality of data and appropriateness of Pearson correlation tests.
Pearson correlation tests were performed, and data was broken up into stained OD590 and
unstained OD590 with the percent carbohydrate data, and correlation coefficient tests run to assess
efficacy of staining using Pearson correlation tests of Stained OD590: measured percent

 33

carbohydrate, as well as unstained OD590: measured percent carbohydrate. T-tests were performed
between the stained and unstained optical density at OD590 to determine the impact of staining on
measurement.
 Multiple linear regression analysis was used to test the combined effect of multiple OD
and strain variables on the percent carbohydrate. A forward approach method was utilized,
increasing the complexity of the model by adding variables to the function while increasing the
variation explained in percent carbohydrate, until the explanatory power (R2 value) decreases with
increasing complexity.
 To test the accuracy of the model, and use of Lugol stained OD590 to approximate
carbohydrate content, approximately 20% of the data was withheld as a testing set for the starch
KO mutants (STA1, STA6 and PGM1) and the WT strain control. The equation obtained from the
linear regression was used to approximate percent carbohydrate, which was compared to the
measured value, and used to calculate the root mean squared error.
Finally, unpaired student’s t-test were used to compare the difference in growth rate, as well as
carbohydrate content of the mutant strains as a direct comparison against the wildtype.

 To determine the optimal absorption wavelength of the Lugol dye, a full scan of the visible
electromagnetic spectrum (350-750nm) was completed in the presence of analytical grade starch.
Analytical grade starch stained with Lugol dye had a peak absorption near 590nm (Figure 2). For
Picochlorum generally, the maximum signal/noise ratio was observed using a diluted concentration
of 1.04mM Lugol’s solution (4uL stain into 196uL of OD diluted cell culture). Pearson correlation
coefficient showed strongest correlation to measured, percent carbohydrate and had the highest
explanatory power (R = 0.59, p-value < 0.001) with testing dataset at this concentration of Lugol’s
solution amongst the concentrations and normalization methods tested during assay development.
Therefore, this concentration was used in the assay moving forward.

3.5 Discussion

 The ability to successfully estimate the carbohydrate content of algae using a quantitative,
high-throughput Lugol’s assay was tested. Indeed, a reliable correlation between Lugol-stained
optical density measurements and carbohydrate content was observed. P. renovo mutants that had
differential expression of energy storage were able to be screened and identified by using this high-
throughput proxy measurement, which is fast and inexpensive. This shows the potential of this
phenotyping approach to screen strains and elucidate gene function related to energy storage in
industrially relevant strains towards the goal of improving the potential of microalgal
biotechnology for the production of renewable fuels and materials.
 Importantly, this modified Lugol assay method can enable high-throughput phenotyping.
We demonstrated that reliable, statistically significant measurements can easily be obtained
utilizing Spectrophotomer equipped to measure absorbance in 96-well plates. We envision that
this high-throughput, carbohydrate proxy assay can be incorporated into part of a larger screening
platform(s) to rapidly assess and characterize deep mutational libraries and easily performed by a
single researcher to obtain large amounts of phenotyping data. While some of this work has been
developed in the past, it was performed over a limited range of carbohydrate and only in a single
strain of Parachlorella kessleri, not typically regarded as an industrially relevant strain. This work
expands on the assays performed previously by providing a reliable relationship of optical density
measurements to carbohydrate storage with an RMSE showing an estimated value between 3-4%
of the actual value. However, this work was only assessed in different cultivation conditions of one
genus of high-starch accumulating, promising microalgae Picochlorum. Future work will expand
this method across strains for broader characterization of strains that can be deployed widely across
seasons and systems.
 This assay shows potential to be incorporated into high-throughput phenotyping that
include other energy storage proxy measurements that are well established, such as BODIPY
staining for lipids, as well chlorophyll fluorescence and flow cytometry for cell characterization.
This assay helps to build out the capability of high-throughput screening of mutant libraries
developed either by random insertion, adaptive laboratory evolution, and/or mutagenesis, which
will prove essential to identify and deploy strains that can thrive productively in real-world
conditions. Furthermore, genetic libraries have played key roles in elucidating gene function. Of
the three, insertional mutants, the one with the highest carbohydrate content was mutant R1M72, a
mutant whose insertion was mapped to fall within a gene of unknown function. This indicates the
potential of these kinds of screening platforms to identify gene functions involved in production
characteristics, such as energy storage. Compared to analytical methods and other screens
developed, this method shows potential for a rapid and inexpensive, 96 well plate format analysis
that can improve our ability to rapidly screen and up-scale strains of interest.
 Compared to the NREL standard carbohydrate assay, which is labor-intensive, destructive,
and typically limited to lower-throughput flask or reactor-scale samples, the Lugol assay provides
a rapid and inexpensive proxy that can be applied in a 96-well plate format in around ten minutes.
While the LAP method provides quantification with more accuracy, its throughput and cost-per-
sample make it less suitable for early-stage screening of large mutant libraries, to narrow down
potential strains quickly. By contrast, the Lugol assay achieves a reasonable balance between

 38

accuracy and efficiency: with an RMSE of 3–4%, it is less precise than analytical assays but
sufficiently accurate for relative comparison screening across hundreds or even thousands of
strains. In this way, it is analogous to BODIPY staining, which is widely used as a proxy for neutral
lipid accumulation in microalgae. Together, proxy assays such as Lugol staining for carbohydrate
and BODIPY for lipids provide complementary tools that enable high-throughput triaging of
mutant libraries or newly discovered strains, reserving more resource-intensive analytical
measurements for validation of the most promising candidates.
 Moreover, manufacturing sustainable fuels and bio-degradable plastics is increasingly
urgent in the context of climate change and mounting plastic pollution. The conversion of starch-
rich microalgae into sustainable aviation fuels and bioplastics represents a dual pathway for
addressing environmental challenges associated with conventional petroleum products. Thus,
enhancing the carbohydrate content in microalgae not only contributes to biofuel production but
also positions these organisms as key players in the development of environmentally friendly
materials, in a cost-competitive way. Finally, this well plate format will lend itself well as the
advent of automated labs and liquid handlers continues to grow, which will allow for the generation
of large amounts of data in a simple way compatible with the direction microbial phenotyping is
taking in the field. To conclude, the high-throughput phenotyping assay presented in this study
represents a viable avenue for strain improvement in the field of microalgal biotechnology,
providing a robust tool for the rapid screening and selection of carbohydrate-rich strains. By
lowering the barrier to screening, this assay helps unlock the genetic and phenotypic potential of
algae for renewable energy and biomaterials.

4.1 Abstract

 A circular bioeconomy and decarbonization pathways can benefit enormously from
the implementation of algae-based production systems. While model alga such as
Chlamydomonas reinhardtii have been thoroughly categorized with many genetic tools
available, this species struggles in industrial environments. Thus, functional knowledge
and tool development must be improved in more robust, industrially relevant species.
Picochlorum renovo has demonstrated rapid growth rates under adverse conditions and can
store as much as 60% of its dry weight as carbohydrate. However, even this promising
strain struggles to realize outdoor productivities consistent with indoor phenotypes as algae
are exposed to large pH gradients and varied alkaline conditions in the laminar, “dead-
zones” found in large-scale, high-rate ponds. To overcome these challenges, a nearly 1000
mutant, barcoded, insertional mutant library was screened under high pH conditions in
laboratory shake flasks, environmental photobioreactors, and greenhouse mini ponds to
uncover genes relevant to coping with this abiotic industrial stress condition. Mutants with
genes involved in chloroplast protein import, organellar redox rebalancing, and regulatory
and proteostasis related functions were both significantly enriched and depleted.
Additional candidate genotypes that have the potential to out-perform wild-type P. renovo
in outdoor systems were identified. These cataloged mutants will allow the broader
community to better understand gene function for engineering targets, and to study the
genetic underpinnings of improving yield and stability under industrially relevant
conditions.

4.2 Introduction

 The rising demand for sustainable and renewable energy sources necessitates
innovative solutions to overcome challenges associated with large-scale biofuel
production. Microalgae, with their unparalleled productivity, rapid growth rates, and
capacity to thrive in non-arable environments, offer immense potential as a renewable
energy resource. Among microalgal strains, Picochlorum renovo, a highly productive and
stress-tolerant species, has emerged as a promising candidate for industrial deployment due
to its resilience to abiotic stressors such as high salinity, elevated temperatures, and intense
light (Dahlin et al., 2019b; McGowen et al., 2023). However, despite advances in algal
biotechnology, achieving productivity and cost-effectiveness necessary for large-scale
biofuel deployment remains a formidable challenge (Bleisch et al., 2022; LaPanse et al.,
2021)

 A critical bottleneck to improve yield and stability of algal cultivation is a lack of
understanding of the genetic factors (and resulting phenotypes) that confer adaptive
advantages under industrial environments. Tools developed in model systems, such as
Saccharomyces cerevisiae, have revolutionized functional genomics through systematic,
genome-wide mutant libraries and forward genetic screens (Giaever & Nislow, 2014). For
instance, yeast knockout collections, encompassing tens of thousands of mutants, have
uncovered gene functions and regulatory pathways essential for industrial processes
(Forsburg, 2001; Giaever & Nislow, 2014). A similar approach has been expanded to other
organisms, including Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, resulting in groundbreaking insights
into photosynthesis, carbon fixation, and cellular regulation (Fauser et al., 2022; Li et al.,
2019). However, C. reinhardtii, while invaluable as a model organism, lacks direct
translatability to outdoor cultivation. Consequently, extending forward genetic screening
techniques to industrially relevant strains like P. renovo offers an opportunity to bridge this
gap.

 Mutant libraries serve as a cornerstone of forward genetic screens, enabling
researchers to systematically disrupt genes and identify mutants with desirable phenotypes.
Advances in high-throughput phenotyping and barcoded mutant libraries allow for the
rapid identification of beneficial traits, such as enhanced stress tolerance, improved lipid
accumulation, and superior growth rates (Giaever & Nislow, 2014; Lin et al., 2019).
Screening large mutant pools under field-relevant conditions mimics the environmental
heterogeneity encountered in large-scale cultivation, such as fluctuations in pH, salinity,
temperature, and nutrient availability (Bleisch et al., 2022; LaPanse et al., 2021). With
emerging evidence of polycistronic gene expression in green algae, with a single promoter
controlling expression of multiple genes (Gallaher et al., 2021), even a relatively small
library can glean considerable insight to algal responses to diverse production conditions.
These efforts align with the pressing need to develop strains capable of thriving in
commercial systems while reducing the costs associated with biofuel production.

 41

 A critical challenge for microalgal biofuels to become economically viable and
contribute a sizeable portion of liquid transportation fuels, acre-scale ponds consisting of
hundreds of square meters of surface area are necessary to attain economy of scale. It is
well documented that at this size, limited mixing results in supersaturation of dissolved
oxygen, which inhibits photosynthesis and algal productivity, as well as large pH swings
from the point of injection to the paddlewheel (Barceló-Villalobos et al., 2018; Mendoza
et al., 2013; Nordio et al., 2023). Additionally, agriculturally-relevant pond geometry
typically result in poor mixing with large “dead-zones” that reduce productivity and
nutrient uptake, increasing hydraulic retention time for algae-based wastewater treatment
(Ortiz et al., 2022; Skifa et al., 2025). Therefore, identifying genetic components that will
bolster algal tolerance to these fluctuations in pH, DO and salinity from evaporation are
essential to better understanding algal physiology and improve production strains.

 In this study, we describe the development and screening of a genome-wide mutant
library in P. renovo to gain a better understanding of gene function under field-relevant
conditions. By leveraging barcoded insertional mutants and high-throughput phenotyping
pipelines, we aim to identify promising traits for deployment in large-scale cultivation
systems. These cataloged mutants enable the broader community to better understand gene
function for engineering targets, and to study strains of interest under production-relevant
conditions to advance the field of algal biotechnology. The mutant library was screened in
laboratory shake flasks, environmental photobioreactors, and greenhouse raceway ponds
designed to simulate abiotic stressors encountered in production settings, extending
previous effort to screen for tolerance of temperature extremes and light intensity (Dahlin
et al., 2019b; Weissman et al., 2018). This approach enables the identification of strain
improvement genotypes through screening under field-relevant conditions, and lends itself
to integration into automated screening platforms to further expedite discovery (Barten et
al., 2022; LaPanse et al., 2021). Identifying novel genetic features with enhanced
productivity and resilience further advances the feasibility of microalgal biofuels as a
sustainable alternative to fossil fuels.

4.3 Methods

4.3.1 Mutant Library Generation

 A mutant library of barcoded, insertional transformants was generated as described
in Mascarenas et al., (Manuscript in preparation). 1000 unique barcoded, insertional
mutants were picked by sterile loop into modified F/2 liquid media to create the library.
DMSO cryogenic stocks were made using 10% DMSO and slow freezing in the -80C
freezer. Cryo-stocks were then taken out and thawed at room temperature as needed. Cells
were pelletized using an Eppendorf 5424 centrifuge, and spun down at 8000rcf for 5 min.
Supernatant was discarded and 1mL of fresh F/2 was added to wash this. This was
performed three times to wash DMSO from cells. Culture was then kept in a 1.5mL
Eppendorf tube in the dark for two days. It was then transferred to a 10mL flask and placed
under two layers of shade-cloth for two days at 350uE, 32ºC, and 180 rpm on an

 42

OrbiShaker shaker table in a Percival incubator. One layer of shade cloth was used for the
next 2 days, then removed. Cultures were grown until dense and transferred to 50mL
cultures in 125mL shake flasks with 20µg/mL selection marker (phleomycin) to ensure
survival of only transformants. This culture was then expanded and used to start the flasks
and Algem photobioreactor experiments.

4.3.2 Barcode screening: Flasks

 Barcodes were screened in 50mL shake flasks under high pH to simulate field
relevant conditions. F/2 media using the Guillard standard media protocol was modified
with 10X, 7X and 2X NO3-, PO43-, & vitamin solution stock, respectively. Parallel aliquots
of the media was adjusted to pH9 and pH7 using dilute sulfuric acid and sodium hydroxide
before being filter sterilized. The library was grown in the flasks in an incubator (Percival)
at ~350µE of light on a 16:8h light:dark schedule at 32 ºC with 2% enriched CO2. pH
ranged from the start point of 7 or 9 to about 7.5-7.6 by the end of each passage. Flasks
were diluted back to field-relevant inoculation density of OD750 = 0.5 and were passaged
repeatedly with biomass spun down and frozen, and phleomycin was added every other
passage to the 1000 mutant pool to ensure selection of transformants. Barcodes/mutants
were selected in the mixed culture over 17 passages until all cultures except the WT P.
renovo at pH7 stopped growing, and relative frequency was tracked using Illumina
amplicon-type sequencing (Figure 1).

4.3.3 Barcode screening: Environmental Photobioreactors (Algems)

 Algem (Algenuity) environmental photobioreactors were used to simulate field
conditions using the modified F/2 media described above. The photobioreactors were
programmed to mimic environmental conditions in the month of July at a testbed
cultivation site in Imperial, TX with temperatures ranging from approximately 25-32 ºC,
and a diurnal light cycle of 14:10 hours light:dark with maximum light intensity reaching
1000µE photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). The light consisted of 85% white light,
15% red light to better simulate the solar spectra. Gas was sparged on demand at 30%
CO2:air to maintain the pH setpoint. The pH setpoint for first half of the experiment (three
passages) was set equal to 8. It was then increased to 9 for the second three passages with
all other parameters remaining the same, as described in Figure 1. Selection marker
phleomycin was added at 20µg/mL after three passages to ensure selection for
transformants in reactors growing the mutant pool.

4.3.4 Barcode screening: Greenhouse mini ponds

 The P. renovo WT and 1000 mutant pool were grown in 100L greenhouse mini
ponds in triplicate in May in ABQ, NM at Sandia National Laboratories. A P. renovo wild-
type strain and the mutant pool were resurrected from cryogen stocks and cultivated in
50mL flasks in an incubator at 32°C and 1% CO2 at approximately 250µE constant light
for one week after cultures reached an OD750 of 1.0. Cultures were then upscaled up to
1L volume in a 5L flask for three days. 2mL cultures were harvested for analysis from the
50mL and 1L growth volumes. The 1L seed culture was transported to a greenhouse and
used to inoculate a 50L culture in one pond for the wild-type strain and one pond for the
mutant pool. After 4 days of growth, the OD750 reached ~1.0 and was used to seed three,
50 L ponds for the wild-type and three mutant pools at a starting OD750 of 0.2. The paddle
wheels were turned on, and the cultures were well mixed prior to collecting culture samples
for analysis. The temperature of each pond was recorded daily using an Apex Pro
temperature probe calibrated according to manufacturer standards. The calibration
accuracy was validated using a Fisher brand temperature probe. 50ml samples from all six
ponds in the greenhouse were taken every other day to measure OD and to pellet samples
for DNA extraction, barcode tracking and 16S sequencing. Once the ponds reached an
OD750nm of at least 1.0, all six ponds were diluted back to OD750nm = 0.2. The oscillation

 44

program was set up to sparge CO2 every 30 minutes for 30 seconds during daylight hours.
The Apex data logs were imported to Excel for analysis.

4.3.5 Barcode enrichment analysis via Illumina sequencing

 Once insertion sites were identified and associated with their corresponding barcode
sequence, a second library prep was created to sequence the barcode to track the frequency
of that associated genotype over time. DNA was extracted using the Zymo fungal DNA
extraction kit. Custom primers OMS18 and OMS46 were designed to amplify the 3’
barcode of the insert and used in a PCR with initial denaturation temperature of 98°C for
30s, followed by 20 cycles of denaturation temperature at 98°C for 10s, melting at 54°C
for 20 seconds, annealing at 72°C for 2 minutes and 20s, followed by a final step of 72° at
2 minutes. Illumina multiplexing adapters were then added using the NEB Next Ultra II
DNA library prep kit for Illumina sequencing. This 15µL reaction of the adaptor-ligated
DNA fragments were added to 25µL NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master Mix along with 5 µL of
Index Primer/i7 Primer and 5 µL Universal PCR Primer/i5 Primer to a total reaction
volume of 50µL. They were then placed on a thermocycler with lid heated to ~105°C and
a PCR was performed at initial denaturation of 98°C for 30s, followed by 5 cycles of 10s
at 98°C, followed by 75s at 65°C. A final extension step of 5 minutes at 65°C was
completed before setting a hold thereafter at 4°C. Reads were first aligned to the known
insert sequence and unaligned reads were filtered out. R2 was then trimmed for the first 20
nucleotides corresponding with the primer binding directly after the barcode, and the
reverse complement was taken of the first twelve base pairs corresponding to the 3’
barcode. This list was matched to the master barcode list of all 825 confirmed, identified
barcodes from the mutant pool. Each unique identifier was then tallied to gain relative
frequency counts of each insert present in the pool at each timepoint using a custom script.

4.3.6 Barcode enrichment, GO enrichment, and statistical analyses

 To test the hypothesis that the increased genetic diversity would confer phenotypic
benefit under simulated field stress, we performed a hypergeometric test to identify
significant Gene Ontology (GO) terms associated with a specific list of genes. We utilized
two datasets: one containing GO term counts for the entire gene library and another for our
genes of interest. For each GO term in our gene list, we calculated the number of associated
genes and compared it to the total number of genes with the same GO term in the genome-
wide dataset. The hypergeometric test was conducted using the phyper function, and p-
values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction. GO terms with
adjusted p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
 Both positive and negatively selected barcodes found in the flask experiment were
enumerated from the cultures grown at pH9, but not at pH7. These were compared with
the top 20 most abundant (positively selected) and least abundant (negative) barcodes
catalogued at the end of the ePBR and pond experiments. Barcodes, whether positive or
negative selected, were compared in a Venn-diagram to visualize the degree of overlap
between experimental platforms. Barcodes that were found to be statistically significantly

 45

different were mapped to gene ontology descriptions, and the number of barcodes for each
GO category were tallied across platforms and compared in a bubble plot to show overlap
of enriched and depleted GO terms.

4.4 Results and Discussion

4.4.1 Flask Cultivation: pH7 vs. pH9

 During the flask experiment, there was not a significant difference in productivity
of chlorophyll fluorescence between the wildtype and the mutant pool, though the WT
appeared initially to have a slight advantage. Over time, growth in the alkaline conditions
slowed, and at passage 17, everything except the WT at pH7 stopped growing (Figure 4A).
Throughout, barcodes that increased in relative abundance were considered enriched
(positive selection), while those that decreased were considered depleted (negative
selection). Out of the entire 825 mapped and confirmed mutant library, less than 100
unique, mapped barcodes were above the level of detection at the end of the flask
experiment with insertions located in both intergenic (n=17) and genic regions
(n=48)(Figure 2B). When the library was grown at pH 9, a stronger selective pressure was
observed, based on the magnitude of the log-fold change (Figure 2C).

 Overall, clones with mutations in genes involved with membrane remodeling for
proton and ion homeostasis (SLAH3, ATP2B4-like Ca2+ pump class, and pks5), and
photosynthetic/chloroplast reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification and redox
balance, including peroxide management (NTRC, CCS1, CAT1, plastid gene-expression
related genes) were strongly selected at pH9 (Figure 2C,G). Mutants that had insertions in
genes involved in proteostasis, autophagy, and ubiquitin systems for protein breakdown
and management of damaged cellular components were enriched at pH9, including Golgi-
regulator EOL1, ubp3, TRAF1B, BPM1, and the glycoprotein maturation gene POGLUT3
(Figure 2C-D). In contrast, ATE2 was depleted in flasks at pH9 (but enriched in ePBRs;
see below) (Figure 2C-D). Lastly, mutants that had disruptions in genes involved in
secondary metabolites and metal availability at high pH showed differential selection,
indicating potential roles for horizontal gene transfer as a mechanism supporting
Picochlorum under environmental stress (NRPS/methyltransferase and nuclease-like
genes) (Figure 2C-G).

4.4.2 Environmental Photobioreactor Cultivation: pH8 vs pH9

 Patterns of barcode enrichment and depletion under field-simulated conditions
were compared at different pH setpoints. The first three grow outs were cultivated using a
July script, simulating field temperature and diurnal light, with pH setpoint at state of
technology, pH8. For the second three passages, pH setpoint was dialed up to alkaline
conditions (pH9), leaving the rest of the script the same. Similar patterns were observed in
the ePBRs compared to the flask experiment, but with clearer gene-level directionality:
genes that contain functions related to chloroplast protein/pigment import (TIC20-II) were
enrichment while others were depleted (TIC110). Some mutants containing disruptions in
chloroplast biogenesis (bch and CCS1) were less abundant, while mutants that had
disruptions in carbon allocation (PURA and GBSS1) were enriched. Several additional
clones that had genes knock-outs related to proteostasis and membrane remodeling (ATE2,
BPM1, ubp3, SPPL3, POGLUT3), with SPPL3 among the largest depletions and while
ATE2 enriched in ePBRs.

(one-tailed student’s t-test, p-value = 0.026, n = 9). When the pH was increased to pH9
using the same weather script, only one of the mutant pools out of the six reactors showed
growth at the higher pH (reactor 5A). The patterns of significantly enriched barcodes
however, was similar between the reactors with and without growth, but the responses were
significantly more pronounced at pH9 (Figure 3D,F-I).

4.4.3. Greenhouse mini ponds

 In the greenhouse ponds, barcodes mapping to core genes around chloroplast
structure and function (Enriched-TIC236, petM, depleted-PAP8) (Y.-L. Chen et al., 2018;
Lan et al., 2021; Liebers et al., 2020) and while some genes involved in carbon metabolism
and energy storage (UGP1, GDCST) (Koh et al., 2025; R. Zhang et al., 2023) were among
the most strongly selected. Similarly, enrichments in clones that contained disruptions in
regulatory and proteostasis factors were also observed (DDI1, EOL1, ASHR2) (Christians
et al., 2009; Serbyn et al., 2020). Similarly, mutant clones that contained disruptions in
genes annotated to function in membrane structure, translation/protein folding, and ion
balance were enriched. In contrast, Mutants containing knock-outs in ‘housekeeping
bottlenecks’ (Legumain, LETM1, MSL1, yipf1, TIF3C1, RPL17, RPS14, rpmg)(Zauner et
al., 2018; B. Zhang et al., 2012) were depleted. Across platforms, NUC1CE was the only
gene deletion that was enriched in both the ePBRs and the ponds, but only marginally
changed in the flasks (Figure 4A, C-D).

4.4.5 Cross-platform comparison: flasks vs. ePBRs

 51

 Across flasks and ePBRs, chloroplast biogenesis related genes, such as depletion
of CCS1 in the ePBRs key to thylakoid membrane-bound cytochrome c in Chlamydomas,
essential for photosynthesis (Dreyfuss et al., 2003) and oxidative stress response and repair
were observed. This is underscored by DDB2 (depleted in ePBRs) and MBD4, genes
essential in DNA damage recognition and nucleotide excision repair (Fei et al., 2011;
Sugasawa, 2009). Additionally, redox balance, pigment synthesis, and protein import into
the chloroplast are key axes of adaptation to higher pH, as shown by bchH/bch depletion
and opposing selection within the TOC-TIC complex of the chloroplast membrane (TIC20-
II enriched; TIC110 depleted) (Y. Gao et al., 2020; Kovács-Bogdán et al., 2011; Nakai,
2015). TRAF proteins, such as the significantly depleted TRAF1B, function in plants as
key developmental and stress response signaling (Qi et al., 2022). Other significantly
depleted genes in the ePBRs, such as LIS, also function as key elements of the small nuclear
ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs) essential to DNA splicing (Y. Xu et al., 1990).

 Endomembrane remodeling under alkaline conditions, in addition to glycosylation
modification, appear to be core components of adaptation to the changed chemical
environment. Among the largest negative selection signals in the ePBRs, was depletion of
SPPL3. SPPL3 encodes a Golgi, intramembrane protease involved in glycosylation by
removing Golgi glycosyltransferases and decreasing glycosylation, impacting protein
folding and stability of membrane proteins, and ultimately affecting cell membrane
composition (Kuhn et al., 2015; Voss et al., 2014). Additionally, genes such as POGLUT3
were also selected and may influence stabilization of extracellular growth factor (EGF)-
like proteins (Williamson & Haltiwanger, 2022).

 Differences were also observed between flasks and ePBRs in carbon allocation and
energy storage routes. As the ePBRs are a sparged using a ‘CO2-on-demand’ system, a
higher pH setpoint results in more infrequent sparging and thus less dissolved inorganic
carbon relative to the CO2-rich flask atmosphere. This could contribute to GBSS1
enrichment in ePBRs; GBSS constitutes a core enzyme for amylose synthesis (L. Wang et
al., 2023), a component of intracellular starch and a primary energy storage mechanism in
Picochlorum (LaPanse et al., 2024). With less carbon readily available, it is logical genes
involved in energy storage would be vital. An enrichment of the partial loss-of-function
would result in less energy storage as starch, leaving more energy available for other stress-
responsive properties. In particular, towards carotenoids and lipid storage, which are more
conducive to coping with ROS resulting from oxidative stress (Shi et al., 2020).

 Indeed, the largest difference in the mutant population between flasks and ePBRs
was in the relative abundance of the AK1 gene, where it was mildly enriched in flasks but
was more than -5 log2-fold change (depletion) in the ePBRs. This homologue to bacterial
proteinase performs optimally at elevated temperatures and mildly alkaline conditions
(Peek et al., 1993). Additionally, PDK, a gene pivotal to the TCA cycle, was depleted in
both the ePBRs and mini ponds (but not flasks), highlighting differences in carbon and
energy balance between strict lab systems and field-simulated environments (Peek et al.,
1993; Thelen et al., 2000). This is possibly due to PDK’s function as a molecular switch
between glycolysis and the TCA cycle, integral to lipid biosynthesis and metabolic

 52

regulation. In fact, partial inhibition of PDK in Phaeodactylum showed a significant
increase in TAG accumulation, though at the expense of productivity (X. Ma et al., 2017).

 Due to the compact nature of genomes in the Genus Picochlorum, there is evidence
that bacterial horizontal gene transfer (HGT) has helped confer the genus’ capacity for high
growth under heat, osmotic and pH (Foflonker et al., 2016, 2018; W. Wang et al., 2024).
Consistent with this, genes of apparent bacterial origin such as prmC (/hemK) and PKS-
like genes were enriched under alkaline/field-simulated conditions, aligning with known
roles for translational regulation and secondary metabolism in stress responses (Graille et
al., 2005; Kohli et al., 2016).

 Additional enriched candidates included uppS (isoprene/isoprenoid synthesis,
linked to membrane stabilization and oxidative stress tolerance) (Chang et al., 2021; Jalil
& Ansari, 2020; Radakovits et al., 2010) and ubp3 (stress-linked ribosomal autophagy)(Cai
et al., 2022; Lan et al., 2021). Notably, ATE2 were differentially selected in different lab
platforms; depleted in flasks but enriched in ePBRs, suggesting context-dependent roles
for proteostasis and carbon allocation during alkaline stress. This gene is critical for
development in both non-vascular and vascular plants, modulating plastid starch content,
and regulating cellular homeostasis by controlling protein degradation of stress-related
proteins (Schuessele et al., 2016).

4.4.6 Cross-platform comparison: ePBRs vs. mini ponds

 There was minimal overlap in the selection responses in the lab-based systems vs.
the greenhouse ponds in this study, except for NUC1CE, which matches to GO terms for
nucleic acid binding, metal ion binding, and hydrolase activity. NUC1CE was enriched in
both the ePBRs and the ponds, but only marginally changed in the flasks. The closest
homologue maps to an extracellular, non-specific, Mg2+/Mn2+ dependent nuclease family
that engage in a wide array of functions, including intra/extra-cellular digestion, apoptosis,
defense, replication, recombination and repair (Ghosh et al., 2007). Additionally, though
not in the top 20 barcodes present in the flasks, a homozygous mutation of EOL1 is
considerably enriched in the ponds. The literature suggests that this gene is involved in
negative regulation of ethylene in Arabidopsis, a plant growth hormone, the de-regulation
of which would likely stimulate growth (Christians et al., 2009). MSL1 was also depleted
in ePBRs and the mini ponds and, with GO terms mapping to membrane structure and
function, thought to function in reducing mitochondrial membrane electrochemical
gradients and maintaining redox balance under abiotic (heat, osmotic stress by reducing
ROS (Lee et al., 2016). Cnnm2 was also found to be slightly enriched in ePBRs but
considerably depleted in the mini ponds, an ancient Mg2+ transporter vital to chlorophyll
synthesis (Y. S. Chen et al., 2020).

 In the greenhouse ponds, genes involved in chloroplast capacity and photosynthetic
electron transport emerged as key axes of field adaptation and stress tolerance (Enriched
TIC236 & petM, Depleted PAP8)(Y.-L. Chen et al., 2018; Lan et al., 2021; Liebers et al.,

 53

2020). Enrichment of clones with deletions in central carbon allocation and one-carbon
metabolism suggest metabolic rebalancing during stress acclimation (UGP1, GDCST),
knockdowns of which has been shown to redirect carbon allocation away from
carbohydrates and toward lipids in the case of the former (Koh et al., 2025; R. Zhang et al.,
2023). While in the latter, a shift away from glycine breakdown can serve as a greater anti-
oxidant reservoir, as glycine supplementation has been shown to improve algal growth
under stress (Fathy et al., 2023). Enrichment of regulatory and proteostasis-adjacent factors
imply that stress tolerance may be achieved through system-level control rather than single
pathway “switches” (DDI1, EOL1, ASHR2) (Christians et al., 2009; Serbyn et al., 2020);
the last of which appear to result in pleotropic effects when mutated in Arabidopsis (Grini
et al., 2009). Cross-system comparison with GO term profiles suggests convergent pressure
on translation, membranes, and stress-linked metabolic tuning, and a working metabolic
model is discussed in Figure 5. Key functional categories include protein
import/biosynthesis in the chloroplast to maintain homeostasis and reduce reactive oxygen
species. Key mitochondrial genes and genes involved in carbon flux and energy storage
and allocation also potentially alter energy storage flux away from starch, and towards
lipids as well as larger pools of amino acid antioxidants. Ion and metal transporters appear
to be impacted, as well as genes involved in membrane integrity and function. Additionally,
ribosomal genes and regulatory, translational and proteostasis involved genes play an
outsized role in adaptation to alkaline conditions.

4.4.7 Study Limitations

 We also acknowledge temperature and pH control limitations of the utilized
systems, across platforms. pH adjusted media reached less alkaline levels over the growth
period. Even delivery of CO2-on-demand in the ePBRs and mini ponds showed fluctuation
between reactors, which may have contributed to the variation in growth of reactor 5A of
the mutant pool over other reactors. While all reactors maintained an average pH of around
8.7 when setpoint was placed at pH9, 5A showed a slightly higher standard deviation
(~0.25 vs. ~0.1). This reactor was recently sent back to the manufacturer for repairs, and
which tuning and part replacement may have contributed to a slightly less controlled range.
Additionally, temperature control in the reactors struggled due to power input quality issues

 55

which caused more irregularity than would have been accounted for in the script. This is
not dissimilar from the greenhouse ponds however where un-seasonally cold temperatures
also impacted the greenhouse and may have provided some unintended cold-shock stress
to the ponds, where temperatures overnight occasionally dipped into the single-digits.

4.4.8 Conclusions and Future Directions

 This work created an experimental framework to identify and isolate useful mutants
that can either be deployed in production systems or be used to inform genetic engineering
targets. This will help to advance the genetic understanding and deployment of industrially
viable strains to help drive algae-based production and wastewater treatment systems,
which can contribute significantly towards a circular bioeconomy and decarbonization.

 Even though Picochlorum appears to contain a compact and interconnected
genome, practical considerations resulted in a relatively modest library size to screen for
genotype-phenotype interactions for all 8900 annotate genes. However, this initial work
was useful to discover many genotype-phenotype interactions and candidate engineering
targets for improving traits for industrial conditions. To extend this work, an expanded
library containing an additional 1,500 insertional mutants will be screened under these and
other field relevant conditions. For instance, dissolved oxygen saturation has shown to be
inhibitory to growth in the laminar flow, “dead-zone” sections of large-scale algae
production ponds. ePBRs retrofitted with dissolved oxygen (DO) probes can be used to
monitor the potential for oxygen injection to match field-relevant levels of DO (~15-
20mg/L). Lastly, initial work in barcoded, mutant generation in closely related haploid,
Picochlorum soloecismus (data not shown), resulted in more stable phenotypes, albeit at a
lower transformation efficiency. Expansion of the mutant library into this closely related
strain, with comparison of the haploid mutants to the diploid mutants generated in
Picochlorum renovo will greatly increase our knowledge of gene function in the
Picochlorum.

 Of the genes identified to be important for growth at a high pH, several stand-out
as high priority targets for genetic manipulation. First, the potential knockdown of UGP1
or GDCST may increase glycine pools and carbon and antioxidant reserve potential. Future
work should also examine the possibility of targeted, structural optimization of the TOC-
TIC complex, to understand which transporters and expression levels are beneficial to
reducing the stress placed on the cellular photosynthetic apparatus. In the ponds, mutant
with the largest-fold enrichment contained a Legumain gene homolog, an important
biotechnology target in plant engineering, with some evidence that it is a pH activated
enzyme (Dall & Brandstetter, 2012; Julián et al., 2013; Zauner et al., 2018).
Implementation of a tunable, stable over-expression of Legumain may improve stress
tolerance and growth potential in the field. Additionally, potentially knockdown of
potentially pleotropic (ASHR2), could result in improved stress tolerance. These strains
can then be re-tested in the greenhouse mini ponds to assess translation in pilot scale
operations.

 56

 Furthermore, many of the relevant forms of stress in real, or simulated production
conditions result invariably in some degree of oxidative stress. Development of inducible
promoters in recent years has seen implementation of promoters which can be “switched
on” by an increased presence of the free radicals associated with reactive oxygen species
(ROS)(Ishii et al., 2000; Shao et al., 2007). Therefore, design and implementation of ROS
inducible promoters to drive expression of stress tolerance genes could result in bolstering
stress tolerance in algae without imposing too large of a metabolic burden and the
commensurate orthogonal effects or gene silencing that can sometimes occurs that has
stymied applied engineering efforts in the past.

 CHAPTER 5
 CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

 This work demonstrates how field-relevant selection pressures, coupled with scalable,
high-throughput phenotyping and functional genomics, can accelerate the creation of
industrially robust microalgae. The work identified genetic and physiologic levers for
improving cold-season productivity and resilience to pond-scale environmental gradients
across multiple industrial microalgal strains. Strain development, adaptive laboratory
evolution, and mutant libraries incorporating large forward genetic screens, have resulted
in promising strains for up-scaling and testing. These strains need to be able to tolerate
multiple, simultaneous biotic and abiotic selection pressures witnessed in agricultural scale
production of microalgae, such as high-rate algal ponds (HRAPs) outdoors, across seasons.
Generating industrially relevant cultures that can endure the wide chemical, light and
temperature gradients experienced at these scales is no small task due to metabolic and
genetic plasticity often found in green algae. This work endeavored to bridge that gap
through commonly used strain development techniques in promising strains, bearing in
mind the production system design and constraints.
 Collectively, this work has led to creation of promising clones and strains with a
collection of mutations which are promising for deployment. One of the evolved clones
from the chemostat evolution experiment shows promise to scale up to greenhouse mini
ponds in the winter. Efforts at developing greenhouse systems that simulate production
ponds are underway, and the wildtype version of this strain has already been handed over
for testing as a baseline comparison. After this background in productivity and inducible
lipid content by nitrogen starvation is established, the evolved strain can be compared
during the winter months to ascertain the scalability of the improved Nannochloropsis.
This will confirm viability for the strain hand-off to partnering consortia focused on
reaching large-scale productivity and yield goals needed for economic feasibility. In a crop-
rotation portfolio, this strain could potentially be a valuable winter crop to improve winter
resilience and productivity, while boosting added value product revenue for increased
competitiveness. Together, this strain has the potential to help meet the annual productivity
goal of 25g m-2 d-1 on average, while improving biochemical composition over baseline
strains, while being grown in a wide range of salinities and wastewaters.
 Insights gleaned via the variant analysis and transcriptomics also provide valuable
engineering targets for future strain development work. Identification of key transcription
factors and master regulators which appear to be adaptive to psychrotolerance in algae
could be over-expressed in Nannochloropsis, or other industrially relevant winter strains,
such as Monoriphidium, either by CRISPR-CAS, Cre-loxP, or other targeted gene editing
techniques. Poignantly, a key part of ongoing efforts is on focused development of tunable,
universal promoters, promising to employ alongside they genes of interest. By testing a
range of expression levels as measured by fluorescent signaling proteins, constructs
implementing a spectrum of moderate-high over-expression can optimize signaling for
improved performance under stress, while limiting gene silencing and other undesirable,
non-orthogonal effects often seen in algal strain engineering. As the optimal performance
of a genes is rarely on the “on” or “off” setting, examining the levels on the dimmer switch

 58

which balance optimal adaptation to other homeostatic functions will be essential for
engineered strains deployed in the field. With the right precautions of unintended, natural
contamination through system engineering and layers of containment, engineered strains,
informed by the strain development and screening work performed, can play a significant
role in improving productivity, yields, and novel chemistries essential to making algal
bioenergy and biomaterials a meaningful global contributor.
 As genetic libraries continue to be employed and expand in size and number of
industrial strains, the value of rapid, high-throughput screening becomes paramount.
Thousands of mutagenized strains can now be screened in both well-plate format, and
pooled phenotyping across selection pressures, allowing a large amount of phenotyping
data to be determined quickly to inform promising strains and mutant pools. New methods
and assay for assaying energy storage content will be valuable to enable rapid isolation and
testing of strains that can improve biofuel yields and energy return on investment of
microalgal bioenergy. With the advent of robotics platforms and fully automated lab, this
method will enable laboratories to test and screen developed libraries at the scale, speed
and expense required to advance cost-competitive algal biofuels. The developed Lugol
solution-based assay, with 3-4% accuracy across a wide range of carbohydrate content, in
a fast 96 well plate method format, will lend itself well to the continued screening of
libraries. This effort is envisioned to serve as the beginning of automated, liquid-handling
robots to quickly characterize strains which are promising for testing in ePBRs and mini-
ponds.
 The P. renovo mutant library was subjected to high pH gradients, as often seen in acre-
scale pond “dead-zones”. Results of the screening effort yielded a promising, down-
selected mutant pool. In the ePBRs at pH8 an almost 20% improvement in productivity
was observed. With the ‘state-of-technology’ targets at pH8.2 for greater carbon utilization
efficiency (CUE), the strain merits further testing and potential hand-off to the
collaborating consortium focusing on scalability. This can be expanded upon using larger
insertional mutant libraries, or extended into related strains of interest, for greater
granularity and broader genome coverage. Through modifications to our environmental
photobioreactor capabilities, as well as modifications to media composition, the
methodology used herein can be replicated to simulate additional industrially relevant
conditions. Selection under high temperature and light intensities intrinsic to arid,
otherwise non-arable regions where algae cultivation is most promising for land-use
efficiency can be explored. Additional experiments could screen for higher salinities,
which result from evaporation at scale and concentration of salts, and offers a potential,
passive pest control parameter against predators and antagonists incapable of adapting.
Lastly, increased dissolved oxygen (DO) content found in laminar, poorly mixed regions
of large-scale raceway ponds (~10-20+ mg/L) can be screened in oxygen enriching,
environmental photobioreactors (ePBRs). High dissolved oxygen content becomes
inhibitory to photosynthesis at the levels observed in acre-scale pond dead zones (15-
20ppm). These follow-on experiments may yield more insight into genes of relevance that
will help strains to adapt to the harsh biotic and abiotic stressors present in commercial
systems.
 Furthermore, genotype-phenotype relationships show the differences in selection
pressures that exist between the laboratory conditions and pilot-scale, greenhouse ponds.
Even the laboratory systems designed to carefully mimic abiotic stressors show

 59

significantly different sets of gene selection. This was demonstrated by the significant
dearth of overlap in the selected mutant populations observed between both ePBRs and
flasks against the ponds, supported by limited overlap in broader gene ontologies. While
GO enrichment showed some similarities, including DNA and protein binding, this was
more pronounced in the ponds, and was supplemented by other key areas, such as
ribosomal structure and function, alongside genes involved in translation. Differences in
central carbon metabolism and flux, as well as calcium ion binding and various peptidase-
activity were also observed to be unique to the ponds over the lab-based systems. These
genes are likely involved in stress response signaling, protein turnover modulation, and
photosynthetic regulation. Additionally, the significant increase in genes related to
ribosomal function, structure and translation underscore the upscaling of protein
production under oxidative stress to maintain proteostasis. The expansion of both the
library size and diversity of field-relevant selection pressures will continue to expand our
understanding of genotype-phenotype relationships in production conditions, while also
generating viable strains for upscaling and testing.
 In conclusion, this work has the potential to advance microalgae as a valuable tool
in decarbonization, and a green, circular bioeconomy, with the potential to combat climate
change, reduce carbon emissions, produce carbon-negative drop-in fuels and novel
coproducts all while bio-remediating wastewater. While the technology is promising,
improved economic and environmental viability must be realized through advancing strain
development and systems engineering approaches. Together, E2-energy and waste-to-
energy systems stand to increase our global energy and environmental security by reducing
threats from dangerous radiative forcing resulting from the combustion of fossil fuels and
industrial agriculture, and transgression of safe planetary boundaries. This will also help to
alleviate strains and resulting conflicts based on natural resource stress and limitations as
a threat multiplier and key concern to global security.
