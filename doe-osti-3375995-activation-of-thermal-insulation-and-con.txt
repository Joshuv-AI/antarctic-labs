2. SCOPE AND BRIEF DESCRIPTION

 The Molten Chloride Reactor Experiment (MCRE) will operate at a much higher temperature
 than conventional light-water reactors (i.e., 650°C) and therefore requires alternative materials
 capable of withstanding these extreme temperatures. Insulation is one such component where
 alternative high-temperature industrial composites are being considered. This report discusses
 the potential for activation of insulating composites when exposed to neutron fluence during
 reactor experiments and the subsequent contamination of the testing area. The report also
 discusses practical measures for preventing the creation and inhalation of activated particulate
 matter (i.e., dust), such as engineering and administrative controls and personal protective
 equipment.

 2.1 Purpose

 This Engineering Calculations and Analysis Report (ECAR) analyzes the activation of
 insulation materials being considered for use in the MCRE, namely Pyrogrel XTE,
 Cerablanket, and Kaowool. Each of these materials are known for creating a significant
 amount of dust when being cut or manipulated,1,2 which raises concerns about the
 aerosolization of these materials during MCRE’s equipment removal and disposal (ERD)
 phase. This analysis is intended to provide insight into the possibility of a radiological
 hazard that these materials pose to workers and will serve to provide awareness and
 insight into measures that may be taken, if necessary, to prevent exposure.

 2.2 Scope

 This report addresses a gating comment made during the MCRE Core Heating System
 (CHT) preliminary design review (PDR). During the PDR presentation, anecdotal
 evidence was shared discussing the “sticky” residue found on exposed skin after walking
 through in the Everett Integrated Effects Test laboratory. This led to a discussion about
 what was becoming airborne and how big of a risk this could be during ERD activities.
 The decision was made to have the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) ERD team
 investigate the potential activation of the dust during operation and the resulting
 contamination risk during ERD. Figure 1 shows a screenshot of the comment made
 during the PDR presentation.

 2.3 Insulation Material

 The materials covered in this ECAR are recognized as excellent insulators for use in
 industrial applications; however, there does not appear to be any published nuclear
 applications up until now. All three materials are recognized as respiratory hazards, with
 at least one material component having regulatory exposure control limits and
 recommending the use of a respirator.1,2

 2.3.1 Cerablanket & Kaowool

 Cerablanket and Kaowool are manufactured by Morgan Advanced Materials.
 Morgan was founded in 1856, has 19 thermal ceramics manufacturing facilities
 across the globe,3 and has a diverse range of insulation products for a wide
 range of applications. These materials were selected as two of Morgan’s
 premier ceramic blankets, advertised as being lightweight, strong, and easy to
 install. Cerablanket and Kaowool are rated for up to 1260°C (2300°F) and
                            1315°C (2400°F), respectively.4,5

 Morgan Advanced Materials identifies their products as Refractory Ceramic
 Fiber (RCF) products. According to the Occupational Safey and Health
 Administration (OSHA) Hazard Communication Standard (HCS) 2012, RCF
 materials are classified as Globally Harmonized System (GHS) category 2
 carcinogens.2

 2.3.2 Pyrogel XTE

 Pyrogel is manufactured by Aspen Aerogels, Inc. Aspen was founded over two
 decades ago, first patenting their insulating composite materials in 2002,
 advertising flexibility, reusability, and ease of installation allowing for a wide
 range of industrial applications, although it largely targets application in
 industrial batteries.6 These characteristics, particularly the ability to easily cut
 the composite, make it very attractive for use in MCRE. Specifically,
 TerraPower plans to use Aspen’s premiere composite Pyrogel XTE, which
 Aspen claims is the best-in-class for corrosion under insulation (CUI) resistance
 and has up to five times better thermal performance compared to competing
 materials.7 A notable tradeoff, however, is that Pyrogel has a much lower
 thermal rating of only 650°C (1200°F).7

 Pyrogel XTE is not classified as a hazardous substance per the GHS,1 but
 Aspen does refer to exposure limits for various components within the material
 and recommends the use of a respirator.

 3.2 Insulation Locations

 Insulation is scattered throughout the MCRE design, but most of the insulation in the
 reactor shield can be found in four primary locations8:

 • Fuel Handling System (FHS) pipe insulation

 • Furnace insulation

 • Reflector support insulation

 • Primary loop insulation.

 The furnace enclosure will be made of interlocking panels of calcium silicate and
 microporous panels, as seen in Figure 3. The interlocking nature of the panels is
 expected to achieve minimal airflow through any gaps.8 The panel materials are not
 included in the fibrous ceramics being analyzed in this report because it is not expected
 to be the main contributor to dust in the test bed. The reflector support insulation seen in
 Figure 2 will be made of refractory hard board, which also is not expected to contribute
 much to dust. Any gaps in CHT insulation will be filled with Pyrogel XTE or Kaowool.

 Figure 3 also illustrates the insulation designed for the RXE loop. This insulation will be a
 combination of commercial off-the-shelf blanket insulation (i.e., Kaowool, Pyrogel,
 Cerablanket) and/or bespoke jackets.9 These insulating materials will be used around
 the RXE loop and the fill/drain standpipe. Although this insulation will be outside of the

4. RESULTS OF LITERATURE SEARCHES AND OTHER BACKGROUND DATA

 4.1 Regulatory Standards

 The INL falls under the jurisdiction of the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) and follows
 the dose limits found in 10 CFR 835.10 The DOE has established an administrative
 control level (ACL) of 2 rem/year (0.02 Sv) to ensure occupation exposures remain well
 below legal limits. The INL has an ACL, as well, which mirrors the DOE at 2 rem per
 year.

 The INL also implements an “As Low As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA)” process to
 help further reduce exposure. Personnel ALARA limits, or goals, are established on an
 individual basis. Normal employees can expect an Administrative Control Level (ACL) of
 100 mrem per year, while individuals engaged in more radiologically intensive work can
 have elevated ACLs. Furthermore, the INL MCRE ARD project has committed to strive
 for 100 mrem total effective dose per year for a single worker and 500 mrem total
 effective dose per person per year (see Artifact 95140).11

 4.2 Inhalation of Radioactive Materials

 When working with radioactive material in an industrial setting, there is risk to workers of
 radiation exposure in addition to expected industrial hazards associated with
 construction or equipment removal. For the thermal insulation materials listed in this
 evaluation, which will experience activation, the primary radiological concern for
 exposure is from the potential inhalation of activated insulation material, or dust, from the
 removal operations associated with the ERD processes. External radiation exposures
 and uptakes of radioactive material from other routes of intake, such as ingestion via the
 oral cavity or absorption through a wound, are less likely and not considered in this
 evaluation.

 5.3 Neutron Flux

 • The fluence at the middle of the RXE loop is assumed to be representative of the
 fluence across the loop. Figure 5 shows the location of the tally used to
 determine the flux value.

 • Rationale: The flux outside of the core was previously modeled for
 identifying desirable detector locations. The fluence in the region of the
 RXE loop has been estimated to be on the order of 1010 n/cm2/s.12 While
 some delayed neutron precursors will decay earlier in the loop than
 others, which could result in variation of the fluence along the loop, the
 mass flow rate is large enough that this effect is expected to be small.13
 Moreover, the majority of the fluence will come from neutron transport
 from the RXE core. Therefore, it is assumed that the flux value in the
 middle of the loop suffices as a global average for the insulation.

 • Impact: The fluence may change as the design evolves. If the fluence
 near the loop increases above 1010 n/cm2/s, these calculations may need
 to be revised. However, given that this value was found for a bounding
 power level of 150 kW, the fluence used herein is most likely a
 conservative estimate for all future design perturbations.

 • The entire fluence was assumed to be comprised of thermal neutrons.

 • Rationale: This is a conservative assumption because the elements in the
 insulation materials are dominated by neutron absorption cross sections
 in the thermal region. Therefore, this assumption maximizes the amount
 of absorption and consequently maximizes activation rates. Note, to
 determine the final activation sensitivity to the flux spectrum, the analysis
 was also performed assuming a discrete 1.2 MeV neutron energy, and
 little difference was observed.

 • Operational fluence outside of the reactor shield was assumed to be low enough
 to not activate insulation in the FHG.

 • Rationale: This is a modeling simplification to allow the ECAR to focus on
 insulation in the CHT and FHS. ECAR-8156 showed that the East SHD
 Rack would be activated such that all the metal together would be about
 1 mrem/hr at 100 days after shutdown.14 This component is a reasonable
 approximation for potential activation of the FHG. However, the activation
 of the FHS insulation is very conservative given the location of the fluence
 shown in Figure 5, a spot much closer to the RXE than most of the piping
 in FHS. Given the low fluence over the FHG, the small amount of
 insulation in the FHG, and the overestimation of the FHS insulation
 activation, this modeling simplification was deemed reasonable for this
 analysis.

 5.4 Transport Vector

 Regardless of whether the insulation materials become significantly activated during the
 experiment, the prospect of the dust becoming a method of transport for any other
 radioactive material during or after the experiment is a concern. This is particularly an
 issue if the dust is easily resuspended and/or circulated through the LOTUS test area. It
 may be difficult to quantify the deposition rate and ability to resuspend the dust without
 significant testing. Whether the insulation is aerosolized during the experiment will also
 play a role in whether radioactive material may be carried to unexpected locations.

 • This analysis assumes these contributions are negligible.

 • Impact: This is an enabling assumption for the analysis. When workers
 enter the test bed, radiological controls technicians (RCTs) or health
 physics technicians (HPTs) will survey the room to assess the general
 radiation field and the level of airborne contamination.

 The potential intake fraction (If) for airborne radioactive material is evaluated and
 bounded based off the most conservative scenarios for routine work that is expected
 during a work year. The If for airborne radioactive material during normal operating
 conditions is calculated using Equation (5). The intake fraction combines the following
 variables:

                                                                                               Page 17 of 25

 If = the potential intake expected as a fraction of the Annual Limit of
 Intake (ALI) (unitless). The theoretical inhalation of 1 ALI is equal to
 5 rem to the whole body.
 Q = the total amount of un-encapsulated radioactive material processed
 during the year in curies in each work location (Ci) as calculated in
 the activation analysis.
 10-6 = Brodsky Factor: a conversion factor based on the conclusion that
 the fractional amount of radioactivity inhaled by a worker is
 generally less than one millionth (10-6) of the amount of radioactivity
 processed in one year (unitless). It is unknown how much will be
 processed through the hoods in a year.
 R = the fraction of the radioactive material likely to be released to the
 workplace based on material characteristics, such as physical and
 chemical form (unitless). The assumed release fraction is 0.01 for
 general area contamination.
 C = the confinement factor is dependent on the operation performed or
 the physical layout of the facility (unitless). The confinement factor
 for normal ventilated areas is 1.
 O = The occupancy factor should be included in this equation to correct
 for the frequency of this exact and specific work performed. An
 occupancy factor of 1 is included to portray one singular work
 evolution and 10 for work evolutions lasting up to 20 iterations.
 D = the dispersibility factor considers processes such as grinding,
 milling, boiling, or exothermic material being dispersed into the air
 (unitless). The dispersibility factor for removal operations is
 assumed to be disruptive and a dispersibility factor of 10 has been
 implemented.
 ALI = Annual Limit of Intake in Curies (Ci) is calculated from ICRP 68 and
 is specific for the radionuclide and absorption type. The absorption
 type that had the most restrictive ALI was used in these
 calculations. TEV-1216, “Annual Limits of Intake Calculated from
 10CFR835 Derived Air Concentration Values”, was used to provide
 the ALIs for these calculations.

 This methodology is conservative in that it is not corrected for ventilation clearance rates

 7.3 Radioactive Contamination

 The degree to which the area in the test bed will become contaminated before and
 during ERD depends on how much dust the insulation releases during handling, the
 velocity of air movement, the particle size, and the percent removed from the ventilation
 system. Dust released during installation will settle onto the various surfaces prior to the
 reactor experiment and may receive a lower neutron flux than assumed in this analysis.
 Subsequently, this dust will experience a lower activation than observed in these results.
 This may be further mitigated by a thorough cleaning of dust following installation before
 any activation occurs. It may therefore be assumed that the primary contamination issue
 is the dust created during ERD.

 In the current ERD plan, insulation will not be cut or removed until after the
 implementation of job-specific radiological controls. Therefore, contamination of the
 LOTUS cell floor should be contained to the area inside and immediately around the
 reactor shield and pipe racks. Following the implementation of radiological controls,
 there will be components like the Argon supply tank that will be removed prior to the
 insulation – these components may be assumed to be relatively clean of activated dust.
 However, once FHS piping starts being disassembled, components still in LOTUS will
 likely become contaminated. Insulation dust will likely begin to spread during FHG
 removal. The extent of contamination will be determined by the ERD approach for
 removing insulation and the particle filtration in the test bed.

 Although some amount of dust will be created simply by handling the insulation, cutting
 is expected to be the primary mechanism for dust creation. ERD validation tests are
 expected to verify this prior to MCRE startup. If cutting is determined to be the primary
 mode of dust creation, then additional measures may be considered for ERD to further
 contain and prevent the spread of dust, such as a HEPA vacuum or filtered air mover
 attached to, or near, the cutting equipment.

 7.4 Health and Safety

 Since the fluence experienced by the insulation is relatively small and there is a limited
 amount of insulation, dose from the insulation is expected to be limited. Recall that this
 analysis made conservative assumptions pertaining to the neutron flux and dose
 calculations. Taking the highest dose approximations, the results suggest that using any
 of the three insulations would be acceptable. Considering INL’s commitment to ALARA,
 the best option would be to go with Cerablanket. Although Cerablanket’s reduced dose
 is highly desirable, its larger thermal conductivity may impact the experiment
 performance. Pyrogel would provide much better thermal conductivity. Given the dose
 and thermal conductivity comparisons, Kaowool is not a strong contender based on this
 analysis.

 The rate of dust production during cutting, handling, and heating may have a dramatic
 impact on the decision if there is a major difference between the materials. This too may
 not play a major role in the decision if proper precautions are put in place. These
 variables show there are multiple driving factors which will ultimately determine which
 insulating material is used. These factors include radiological risk, along with the
 potential chemical toxicity, thermal conductivity, insulating capability, dust generation,
 etc., which will all play a role in determining the optimal insulation material. TerraPower
 will make this decision as the MCRE design progresses toward the final design.

 For the MCRE ERD system, the capability to include pre-work optimization techniques to
 improve post-work operations will play a critical role in the ALARA design process.

8. CONCLUSIONS

 This analysis evaluates the radiological risk posed when using high-temperature industrial
 insulations in a low-power nuclear experiment (i.e., MCRE). The analysis first estimated the
 activation of various insulation materials under conditions representative of the RXE loop using
 a deterministic model. While the activity of the irradiated materials does become significant
 during irradiation, it decays quickly and substantially once the neutron flux is removed.

 Although Cerablanket stood out among the insulation materials for encouraging ALARA
 practices, it has a higher thermal conductivity, which may have implications for the experiment.
 One significant shortcoming of this analysis is the lack of experimental data to quantify the rate
 of dust creation, which may be a significant factor in material selection.

 Finally, these results are based on conservative assumptions, including an overestimation of the
 fluence on FHS piping insulation, and the resulting radiological exposure risk to workers, the
 public, and environment is shown to be well within regulatory requirements and at near
 background levels of radiation exposure. At low power levels, focusing on reducing the
 generation of dust from ERD activities may be more effective for reducing the potential spread
 of contamination than changing the insulating material type. However, regardless of the material
 chosen, PPE and safety conscious procedural practices can help reduce the production and
 spread of activated dust––thus minimizing the radiological hazard to workers.
