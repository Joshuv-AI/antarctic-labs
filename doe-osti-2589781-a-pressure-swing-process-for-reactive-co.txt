Executive Summary
The goals of this project are to design and develop tailored dual-functional materials (DFMs) and
the accompanying pressure-swing process for the reactive capture and conversion (RCC) of CO2
to methanol, a vital product in the chemical market and a versatile precursor to fuels. The proposed
approach is based on the hypothesis that precise co-location of basic and metallic sites in a DFM
will enable efficient RCC of CO2 in a fixed-bed temperature-pressure-swing reactor under mild
conditions that favor lower capital and operating expenses and offer compatibility with a dynamic
energy grid to provide favorable process economics. The proposed hybrid process utilizes
renewable H2 and targets deployment at a gas-fired power plant, where CO2 point sources have
relatively high CO2 capture costs by traditional methods, ranging from 43 to 89 $/ton, suggesting
that the economics would benefit from an RCC approach. This choice of CO2 source represents a
relatively lower carbon capture rate (0.47 MMTCO2 per plant year), but with a high number of
plants (>1100). This deployment plan holds the potential to achieve relevant carbon capture and
utilization magnitudes through modular implementation of the process, where initial estimates
suggest that the current annual production rate of renewable methanol (0.2 MMT/year) could be
equaled upon the first installation of the proposed technology.

Throughout this project, NREL researcher team has successfully developed 2 groups of DFM and
associated cyclic RCC procedure to selectivity produce either methanol or CO, which is a major
intermediate for methanol synthesis with commercially available technologies. Additionally, this
project has also developed process models and techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle
assessment (LCA) framework to evaluate feasibility of the developed DFM and processes. The
TEA/LCA framework includes integration of RCC process models developed using ASPEN Plus
and optimization of renewable hydrogen generation and supply for RCC process using NREL’s
developed H2INTEGRATE tool. The detailed results of this project are reported below in 3 major
sections:

 1. Development of DFM for RCC to produce methanol

 2. Development of DFM for RCC to produce CO

 3. Development of TEA and LCA framework for technology feasibility evaluation

Additionally, major milestone achievements are summarized in section 4 with scientific products,
including publications, patents, posters and presentations are listed in section 5. Section 6 provides
a quarterly budget summary for the project.

1.1 Background
 Development and deployment of technologies that convert waste CO2 from dilute point
sources to value-added products is important for strengthening economic resilience, promoting
workforce development, and expanding fuel and chemical supply chains.1 One such strategy is
carbon capture and utilization (CCU), in which (1) CO2 is captured from a dilute source using a
sorbent material; (2) the sorbent is regenerated by heat, vacuum, and/or steam and the resultant
CO2 stream is purified and compressed; (3) the CO2 is then catalytically converted to products,
such as CH4, CO, methanol, or potentially higher hydrocarbons.2 Step 2 is often considered to be
the major cost driver due to the energy and capital investment required for these operations. While
step 3 could hypothetically occur at the same location as steps 1 and 2, it is more frequently
envisioned that the compressed CO2 would be transported to a separate, dedicated facility with
ample access to thermal energy and hydrogen to perform the conversion. The issue of CO2
transport introduces additional barriers to scaling CCU given the current lack of infrastructure and
the permitting challenges of building such infrastructure.3 To eliminate these cost and
transportation concerns, Steps 2 and 3 can be streamlined through a process intensification
approach known as reactive carbon capture (RCC). In RCC, a single sorbent-catalyst material
(known as a dual function material, or DFM) is used to capture CO2 via interactions with basic
surface sites; the bound CO2 is then converted to products on adjacent catalytic sites in a reactive
desorption step by introducing a reducing agent (typically H2) with appropriate conditions to
promote the desired reaction.4-6 For methanol (MeOH) production from direct air capture (DAC),
RCC has the potential to reduce energy and capital costs by over 50% relative to conventional
CCU by combining sorbent regeneration and conversion into a single step.5 A promising RCC
technology platform is based on dual function materials (DFMs), which are solid-phase materials
composed of sorbents and catalysts co-dispersed on the same high surface area carrier. As shown
in Figure 1.1, the sorbent component allows for selective capture of CO2 from a gas stream (R2)
and the catalyst component subsequently performs the conversion of the adsorbed CO2 upon
introduction of a reactive gas (typically H2, R1).7-9 Catalytic conversion of adsorbed CO2 with H2
simultaneously regenerates the solid adsorbent and releases products without the need for the
energy-intensive separation steps.

 The most well-developed DFM is comprised of Ru and/or Ni with an alkaline sorbent for
methanation of CO2.10,11 While renewable methane would be an excellent transition fuel, fossil
methane is inexpensive (averaging $6.45/MMBTU in 2022 in the US12) and the economics of
renewable methane utilization are noncompetitive (generally >$10/MMBTU13). This requires the
design and development of DFMs that enable RCC to produce more valuable and more useful C1
products. Methanol (MeOH) is a particularly attractive target molecule, being a versatile building
block in both the chemicals industry (e.g., formaldehyde, acetic acid) and fuels industry via direct
use in the shipping industry or upgrading to energy dense synthetic fuels in difficult-to-decarbonize
sectors (e.g., heavy-duty vehicles, aviation).14,15 The value of MeOH is reflected in its
comparatively higher average price of $32.20/MMBTU in 2022 in the US.16 Despite the increasing
demand for MeOH for fuel and chemical production in recent decades, renewable MeOH
represents only 0.2% of current annual production.17 Reducing the cost and energy requirements
of CO2 to MeOH are needed to decarbonize and de-fossilize MeOH for the fuel and chemical
industries.18 To this end, RCC to MeOH has been reported to have the potential to reduce capital
costs and energy requirements by about 50% when compared to separate CO2 capture and
conversion.5 The key results of this case study are highly dependent on the development of
materials and processes that have CO2 capture capacities and MeOH yields that are comparable
and competitive to a separate capture and conversion system.

 Integrated capture and conversion approaches to target MeOH production have been
demonstrated in a variety of ways. The most reported approach involves capturing CO2 using a
liquid-phase solvent (e.g., amine19-21 or hydroxide solutions22-24). The CO2 loaded capture media
is then passed over a solid catalyst bed20 or mixed with a liquid-phase catalyst19,21,23,24 to convert
the absorbed CO2. While favorable results were reported using this approach (>90% MeOH
yield24), it requires the use of two reactors (one for the capture solvent and one for the catalyst)
and subsequent separation of MeOH product from the solvent, which would not see the same cost
reduction potentials as a solid-phase system that allows the use of a single material in a single
reactor. However, design of solid-phase DFMs and their accompanying RCC systems for MeOH
synthesis, as shown in Figure 1.1, has been attempted just a few times thus far.25-27 The reported
attempts have exhibited low overall conversion of initially captured CO2 (i.e., low working
capacity) and low MeOH yield. For example, in a recently reported solid-phase Pd-amine DFM,
less than 5% of the captured CO2 was converted to MeOH for a gravimetric activity (i.e.,
productivity) of ca. 20 µmol/g, leaving much room for improvement in both DFM and process
design.26 In an alternative approach using a stacked bed of sorbent and catalyst, a similarly low
productivity of 12 µmol/g was reported.27 Herein, we report an investigation of a novel DFM and
RCC to MeOH process. We propose a DFM design that modifies the industrial MeOH synthesis
catalyst (mixed oxide of Cu-Zn-Al, CZA) with alkali and alkaline metal sorbents (termed
Alk/CZA, Alk = K and Na for group I, Ca and Ba for group II sorbents). The work was carried out
in a stepwise approach, first by investigating sorbent identities with Na and K as representative
Group I and Ca and Ba as Group II representative sorbents to modify the parent CZA catalyst to
improve CO2 capture performance. Once the optimal sorbent was down selected, the effect of the
CO2 capture temperature was studied to ensure stable catalytic performance during cyclic
conditions. Lastly, sorbent loading and hydrogenation parameters (temperature, pressure and
hydrogen concentration) were optimized through a full factorial design of experiments targeting
maximum MeOH productivity and selectivity.

1.2 Experimental
1.2.1 Material synthesis
 DFMs were prepared by modifying a commercial MeOH synthesis catalyst (mixed oxide
of Cu-Zn-Al, CZA, Clariant Megamax 800) with alkali and alkaline metals (Alk) using incipient
wetness impregnation (incipient wetness point of CZA = 0.5 ml/g). The CZA material was crushed
and sieved to 200-300 µm, and aqueous solutions of Ca(NO3)2•4H2O (Sigma Aldrich), Ba(NO3)2
(Alfa Aesar), Na2CO3 (Sigma Aldrich) and K2CO3 (Sigma Aldrich) salts were used for
impregnation onto the resulting powder to achieve 5wt% of Alk in their hypothetical oxide form
(i.e CaO, BaO, Na2O or K2O). The modified CZA DFMs (Alk/CZA, where Alk = K or Ca) were
dried at 120°C overnight (>12 h) in static air. Decomposition of the carbonate or nitrate precursors
and catalyst activation were performed using in situ pre-reduction at 250 °C before characterization
or activity testing. The materials were termed as 5-Alk/CZA.

 For the parametric studies exploring different Na loadings, a similar procedure was
performed to synthesize the Na-modified DFMs, using incipient wetness impregnation on the
crushed and sieved CZA catalyst. Aqueous solutions of Na2CO3 (Sigma Aldrich) were prepared
and added dropwise onto the CZA using a pipette to achieve 1 and 10 wt.% of Na in the
hypothetical oxide form (i.e., Na2O), termed here 1-Na/CZA and 10-Na/CZA respectively. The
Na-modified CZA DFMs were dried at 120°C overnight (>12 h) in static air and pre-reduced at
250 °C for 8 h before characterization or activity testing.

1.2.2 Characterization
 The weight loadings (wt%) of the Alk metals were determined using as-synthesized
samples (i.e., after drying but before reduction to avoid the need to develop passivation/oxidation
of the CZA after reduction) via inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-
AES) at Galbraith Laboratories (Knoxville, TN).

 Temperature programmed reduction in H2 (H2-TPR) was performed using as-synthesized
DFMs and unmodified CZA with an Altamira AMI-300Lite catalyst characterization instrument.
Catalyst samples (30-50 mg) were loaded into a quartz u-tube sample cell and dried for 2 h at 100
°C in flowing Ar (25 sccm). The cell was then cooled to 30 °C in flowing Ar and 4% H2/He was
introduced at 25 sccm. The temperature was ramped from 30 °C to 250 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min
and held at 250 °C for 1 h. Change in H2 concentration was measured by an online thermal
conductivity detector (TCD) and TCD intensity was normalized to actual catalyst mass.

 X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer
with a Cu Kα source. Diffractograms were collected in the 2θ range of 20−70° at a scan rate of
2°/min. XRD patterns were collected after DFM synthesis and drying.

 CO2 chemisorption was measured using an Autosorb-1-C gas sorption analyzer
(Quantachrome Instruments). Approximately 200 mg of sample was diluted with 1 g of quartz
chips and reduced in H2 at 250 °C for 8 h, followed by evacuation for 8 h. CO2 chemisorption
isotherms were measured between 50 °C and 250 °C at pressures between 80 and 760 Torr in 50
Torr increments. The site density of strong CO2 chemisorption was determined by the difference
of the total and weak isotherms, extrapolated to zero pressure.

 3

 In-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was
performed using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50R FT-IR spectrometer equipped with a Harrick
Praying Mantis high temperature reaction chamber attachment with CaF2 windows. The DRIFTS
spectra and backgrounds were collected at a resolution of 4 cm-1 with 128 scans. The samples were
diluted with CaF2 at 1:1 weight ratio and about 40-50 mg of the mixture was loaded into the sample
holder on top of a small bed of silica (~50 mg). The samples were pre-reduced with 50% H2/He at
40 sccm for 8 h at 250 °C (ramp rate: 5°C/min) before cooling to 100 °C. A background was taken
for each sample after cooling to 100 °C in 40 sccm of He. The samples were exposed to 40 sccm
of 5% CO2/He for 30 min, and then purged for 1 h with 40 sccm He to remove physisorbed CO2.
Spectra were collected before and after the purge step.

1.2.3 Catalytic reaction studies

1.2.3.1 Fixed-bed co-fed CO2 hydrogenation to MeOH
 The co-fed CO2 hydrogenation reaction was performed in a 7.9 mm I.D. fixed-bed stainless
steel reactor. Typically, 1 g of catalyst was loaded and reduced at 250 °C (ramp rate of 2 °C/min)
and atm pressure for at least 16 h under 95% H2 / 5% Ar flow (110 sccm) to decompose the
carbonate or nitrate precursors and activate the catalyst. The reactor was then set to the desired
reaction temperature and allowed to stabilize. Reactant flowrates for CO2 and 95% H2 / 5% Ar
were set to achieve a total flowrate of 17 sccm and molar ratio of H2:CO2 = 3:1 (corresponding to
WHSV of 0.5 gCO2 gcat-1 h-1). Catalytic activity was monitored at 25 °C intervals from 150-250 °C
and 10 bar intervals from 10-30 bar for at least 6 h at each condition.

 Product analysis was performed online using Agilent Technologies 7890B gas
chromatographs equipped with FIDs to analyze oxygenates and HCs and TCDs to analyze
permanent gases. Reactor inlet and outlet gases were sampled through heated (200 °C) lines to
prevent condensation prior to analysis. The concentration of each compound was quantified by
correlating its peak area with the response factor obtained from traceable gravimetric calibration
standards. Ar in the inlet stream was used as an internal standard to quantify molar flowrate of all
other components in the gas stream.

1.3 Results and discussion
1.3.1 Preparation and characterization of Alk/CZA DFMs
Alk/CZA DFMs were prepared through incipient wetness impregnation of commercial CZA using
aqueous solutions to target 5 wt% loading of metal oxide, which is typically investigated in other
DFMs studies.28,29 Elemental analysis provided weight loadings of 3.71 % for K, 3.54 % for Na,
4.7% for Ba and 2.97 % for Ca. As an initial structural characterization, X-ray diffraction (XRD)
analysis was performed. The addition of Alk species did not change the CZA reflections for CuO
and ZnO, suggesting that there are no major disruptions to the parent structure (Figure 1.4).

 Strongly chemisorbed CO2 was measured over a range of temperatures to probe the effects of
Alk-modification of CZA on CO2 capture capacity. Results shown in Figure 1.5a and Table 1.4
are for K/CZA and Ca/CZA as representative for group I and II sorbents, respectively. The
materials were reduced at 250°C in H2 prior to analysis, as informed by H2-TPR (Figure 1.6). As
the analysis temperature increased from 50 to 250 °C, the total CO2 uptake decreased markedly
for CZA (132 to 42 µmol/g), moderately for Ca/CZA (197 to 105 µmol/g), and only minorly for
K/CZA (265 to 206 µmol/g). At low temperatures (50 °C), modifying CZA with Ca increased
strong adsorption capacity by 50 % (Figure 1.5b). At moderate temperatures (100, 150 °C), the
strong adsorption capacity increase on Ca/CZA was less pronounced at 35 % and 27 %,

respectively. At high temperatures relevant to CO2 conversion (200, 250 °C), the strong CO2
adsorption capacities of Ca/CZA (144, 105 µmol/g) were much greater than that of unmodified
CZA (74, 42 µmol/g), equating to 95 % and 148 % increase in capacity, respectively. K-
modification greatly enhanced strong CO2 adsorption capacity at all temperatures when compared
to both Ca/CZA and unmodified CZA. Even at lower temperatures (50-150 °C), the strong
adsorption capacity of K/CZA (265 µmol/g) was about double that of the unmodified CZA (132
µmol/g). The adsorption enhancement of K-modification was greatest at 250 °C, accounting for a
390 % increase in capacity (206 vs 42 µmol/g). K/CZA also exhibited the smallest change in
capacity as a function of analysis temperature, and retained the highest capacity at relevant
conversion temperatures above 200 °C. The difference in CO2 chemisorption performance
between other group I and II metals (i.e. Na and Ba) are similar even though data are not shown
here. These data demonstrate that the addition of sorbent sites can greatly increase CO2 uptake
capacity on CZA and significant amount of CO2 remains strongly adsorbed on the DFMs at high
temperatures (above 100 °C), which is an important factor for reactive desorption, as discussed
later. And group I metal does not only increase the CO2 adsorption capacity but also has higher
adsorption strength at high temperatures.

 In situ DRIFTS was utilized to investigate the binding geometry of strongly adsorbed CO2
on these DFMs. The spectra after a H2 reduction, 30 min of exposure to 5% CO2/He at 100 °C,
and a 1-h purge at 100 °C to remove weakly bound CO2 are displayed in Figure 1.7a. Observed
surface carbonate species (CO3*) and their respective peak positions are summarized in Figure
1.7b-c, and Table 1.5. It has been reported that CO2 binds to CZA above ambient temperature as
bridged bidentate carbonates.23 Peaks indicating this adsorption geometry were observed on the
unmodified CZA at 1604 and 1379 cm-1. Polydentate carbonates have also been reported and were
observed here (1537 cm-1). These two surface species have been related to different binding
strengths, with the polydentate being more strongly bound.30 The spectrum for group II modified
CZA DFMs (i.e. Ca/CZA and Ba/CZA) were not remarkably different from unmodified CZA. The
surface carbonate features of alumina-supported Ca (or Ba) species have been reported at 1630
cm-1 and 1340 cm-1 associated with the monodentate geometry, which are in close proximity to the
peaks associated with the bidentate geometry on CZA.31 Though it appears there was a slight
broadening of peaks in this region, it is difficult distinguish CO2 binding on Ca (or Ba) sites from
CO2 binding on CZA sites. Given the similar spectra for CZA, Ca/CZA and Ba/CZA observed
here, we attribute the dominant surface species on Ca (or Ba)/CZA to the same bridged bidentate
and polydentate carbonates as observed on unmodified CZA, with possible contributions from
monodentate geometry on Ca (or Ba) sites at coincident peak positions.

 In contrast, the spectrum for group I modified CZA DFMs (i.e. K/CZA and Na/CZA) was
dramatically different than those of CZA and group II modified CZA DFMs. Strong absorbances
were observed in the regions of 1700-1600 cm-1, 1500-1400 cm-1, and 1350-1250 cm-1, which are
all distinct from the peaks observed on the CZA and Ca (or Ba)/CZA samples. It has been reported
that CO2 chemisorbs onto K (or Na)-containing materials in diverse CO3* arrangements, such as
different types of bicarbonates, bidentate, and polydentate carbonates,29,32,33 and the peaks
observed here can be attributed to three CO3* surface species (Figure 1.7c). A broad shoulder at
1670 cm-1 and peak centered at 1295 cm-1 correlate well with bridged bidentate carbonates on K
or Na species.33 Although similar in structure to those observed on CZA and Ca (or Ba)/CZA, the
different peak position indicates a different binding strength at K or Na sites. The splitting ∆ν3
associated with this geometry on K (or Na)-sites is 375 cm-1, which is greater than splitting ∆ν3=
225 cm-1 associated with the CZA-bidentate, indicating a less stable (and possibly more reactive)
carbonate. The peak centered at 1617 cm-1 has a companion peak at 1315 cm-1, and these can be
assigned to a chelating bidentate carbonate, which is a geometry distinct from CO3* on Ca/Ba or
CZA sites.29,32 Lastly, the broad peak centered around 1457 cm-1 correlates best with a polydentate
carbonate associated with a K or Na site, again a similar geometry with a different strength than
on CZA and Ca (or Ba)/CZA.33 While carbonate geometries arising from the parent CZA are likely
also present, adsorption at K or Na sites dominate the spectrum for this material.

 Based on chemisorption and DRIFTS analysis, modification with group I metal oxides

how these parameters subsequently affect the catalytic performance of these DFMs under co-fed
steady state CO2 hydrogenation and adsorption–reactive desorption steps during an RCC cycle.

1.3.2 Performance of DFMs in co-fed CO2 hydrogenation experiments
The DFMs and parent CZA were tested for CO2 hydrogenation activity as an initial assessment of
Alk-modification on catalytic performance, especially MeOH synthesis activity. K/CZA and
Ca/CZA DFMs were selected as representative for the modification by group I and II, respectively.
The materials were tested under continuous flow of H2 and CO2 (molar ratio of H2 : CO2 = 3 : 1)
over a range of pressures (1-3 MPa) and temperatures (150-250 °C). The resulting conversion and
product selectivities are presented in Figure 1.9 and Table 1.6.

When the reaction temperature was increased at a constant pressure of 30 bar (Figure 1.9a), CZA
exhibited increasing CO2 conversion (10.1-34.2 %) and decreasing MeOH selectivity (95.6-62.6
%). Ca/CZA demonstrated slightly lower conversion throughout most of the temperature range but
reached a similar conversion at 250 °C (33.4 % vs 34.2 % for CZA). On the other hand, K/CZA
did not show any significant conversion (<1 %) until 200 °C and only achieved a maximum
conversion of 16.2 % at 250 °C. At lower temperatures (150, 175 °C), Ca/CZA gave slightly higher
selectivity to MeOH (99.9 %, 93.9 %) when compared to unmodified CZA (94.6 %, 84.6 %). At
higher reaction temperatures (≥ 200 °C), MeOH selectivity was comparable between CZA and
Ca/CZA, ranging from 79.1-62.6 % for CZA and 78.4-63.1 % for Ca/CZA between 200-250 °C.
In contrast, modification with K resulted in preferential formation of CO at all temperatures.
MeOH selectivity with K/CZA was highest at 200 °C at just 2.24 %.

 At a reaction temperature of 250 °C and pressures between 10 and 30 bar (Figure 1.9b), CZA
and Ca/CZA exhibited similar conversion and MeOH selectivity. Both conversion and MeOH
selectivity increased for these two materials as pressure increased from 10 to 30 bar. In stark
contrast, increased pressure did not enhance conversion or MeOH production on K/CZA. Overall,
it can be concluded that modification with K was significantly detrimental to overall hydrogenation
activity (i.e., methanol yield). Indeed, this result aligns well with previous reports that impregnated
alkali species can inhibit the activity of CZA catalysts.36 Given that Ca-modification provided a
moderate promotional effect to adsorption capacity, with minor effects on CO2 binding geometries
and catalytic performance, one would expect that Ca/CZA would be the best candidate for
combined capture and conversion to MeOH. The next section exemplifies a key learning that we
present in the development of DFMs for RCC processes: CO2 hydrogenation evaluation of a DFM
in a typical co-fed experiment is not always a reliable indicator for performance in cyclic capture
and conversion operation.

1.3.3 Performance of DFMs in CO2 adsorption – reactive desorption cycles
 The steps used for cyclic RCC performance evaluation are described below and graphically
represented in Figure 1.2. A 1% CO2 stream was used during the CO2 adsorption step for ease of
method development and performance evaluation. CO2 adsorption was conducted at 100 °C and
atmospheric pressure for 1 h. Upon introduction of flowing CO2, reaction of CO2 with the freshly
reduced CZA surface was evidenced by CO formation, but CO concentration gradually decreased
with adsorption time (Figure 1.3). This reaction was suppressed over Alk modification, except for
Ba/CZA, (Table 1.7), which is consistent with the lower hydrogenation activity observed in the
CO2 hydrogenation, especially for K/CZA. An inert purge was performed after adsorption to
evacuate the reactor of excess CO2, remove weakly bound CO2, and ensure that any products
detected in the subsequent conversion step evolved from the surface-bound CO2 on the DFM (i.e.,
strongly bound CO2), and not as a result of reactions from residual gaseous species. This type of
purge will also be important in future investigations of this and similar RCC technology when O2
is present in the CO2 stream to prevent the mixing of H2 and O2 for safety considerations. The CO2
that remains on the DFM surface after the inert purge is defined as “strong CO2 adsorption”, which
is the reactant for the subsequent reactive desorption stage (i.e., hydrogenation reaction). The
presence of strongly bound CO2 is consistent with the in-situ DRIFTS study above. Importantly,
strong CO2 adsorption neglects the portion of CO2 consumed to produce CO during the adsorption
step as mentioned earlier. After purging, the reactor was pressurized to 30 bar with H2 before
ramping the temperature to 250 °C for a 2-h hydrogenation. MeOH and CO were the major
products at this stage, while DME, methane, and desorbed CO2 were also observed (Figure 1.3).
The pressure was then lowered back to atmospheric pressure and held for 1 h to release residual
products and reduce the catalyst for the next cycle. During this release step, CO was the major
product observed (Figure 1.3). CZA and each DFM was tested for 5 RCC cycles (Figure 1.10),
and the performance was compared by averaging results from the last 3 cycles (Figure 1.11 and
Tables 1.7 and 1.8) – the first two cycles were disregarded to avoid any “start-up” effects, which
would not be representative of cyclic performance.

 In accordance with the chemisorption results, the introduction of Alk adsorbents increased
the strong CO2 capture capacity of the DFMs in an increasing order of CZA < Ba/CZA < Ca/CZA
< K/CZA < Na/CZA (Figure 1.11a and Table 1.8). In addition, the amount of desorbed CO2
during the hydrogenation step also decreased in the same order, from 8.8 µmol/g over CZA to just
3.5 µmol/g over K/CZA (Table 1.8). Modification with group I adsorbents (K or Na) resulted in
the lowest unreacted and desorbed CO2. Slight increase in the strong CO2 adsorption was observed
with Ca/CZA and Ba/CZA (66.0 vs 87.8 & 77.1 µmol/g, Table 1.8), consistent with the CO2
chemisorption data. Overall conversion of adsorbed CO2 to products were similar between
Ca/CZA (71.2 %) and CZA (73.1 %). Comparable product selectivities to CO, MeOH, and CH4
were also observed for CZA and Ca/CZA, with Ca/CZA having slightly greater MeOH selectivity
at 30.5 % versus 27.6 % for CZA (Figure 1.11b and Table 1.8). Ba/CZA had the lowest selectivity
to undesired CH4 (2.7%) while MeOH selectivity (29.2%) was comparable to that of Ca/CZA and
CZA. Combining the comparable MeOH selectivity with enhanced adsorption capacity, Ca-and
Ba modification results in a moderate improvement in RCC performance, highlighted by a 43 – 60

% increase in MeOH productivity (i.e., specific activity) from 13.2 µmol/g for CZA to 18.9 µmol/g
for Ca/CZA and 21.1 µmol/g for Ba/CZA (Table 1.8). Comparing group I modified CZA (K/CZA
and Na/CZA) to both CZA and group II modified CZA, significant increase in strong CO2
adsorption and decrease in the amount of CO2 lost to desorption during reactive desorption were
observed. In addition, the conversion of adsorbed CO2 to hydrogenated products was greatest over
K/CZA (94 % vs 71-73 % for Ca/CZA and CZA), leading to the greatest productivity to total
hydrogenated products (average of 127 µmol/g for both K/CZA and Na/CZA). This activity is
comparable to top-performing methanation DFMs (150-200 µmol/g).10,34 The selectivity to MeOH
was greatly enhanced over group I modified CZA (46% for K/CZA and 58 % for Na/CZA vs 28-
30 % for CZA and group II modified CZA), and importantly, the selectivity to CH4 was greatly
suppressed (4.4 – 5.1% vs 18-19 %). Increased CO2 capture capacity, conversion of adsorbed CO2,
and selectivity to MeOH resulted in a MeOH productivity of 59.0 µmol/g for K/CZA and 74
µmol/g for Na/CZA, which is >3× greater than Ca/CZA and >4.5× greater than parent CZA. In
summary, 5-Na/CZA demonstrated the most promising performance as DFM for RCC to MeOH
and therefore selected for further optimization given its high CO2 capture capacity, high MeOH
selectivity and per-cycle productivity, low CH4 selectivity, and high CO2 conversion, leading to
superior cyclability over the other the DFMs explored here.

 There have been three recent reports targeting the production of MeOH from CO2 in a
capture-conversion approach. As alluded to above, Pd – amine functionalized silica DFMs were
tested in a cyclic, temperature swing process.26 These materials exhibited high CO2 adsorption (up
to 1.2 mmol/g) and achieved highly selective conversion to MeOH at mild reaction conditions
(140 °C, 1 atm); however, ca. 70 % of the captured CO2 was desorbed (unreacted) during the inert

 21

purge and temperature ramp steps of the cycles, resulting in a 20 µmol/g MeOH productivity. In a
related approach that is not directly relevant, but is worth noting here, an integrated capture and
conversion approach was employed with a liquid amine capture agent, followed by conversion of
the carbamate to MeOH over a Pt/TiO2 catalyst.20 This approach reported high MeOH selectivity
(51.5 %) and productivity (150 µmol/g), but CH4 selectivity was considerable at 27 %. Most
recently, a stacked bed approach was investigated, where a bed of Na/Al2O3 was layered on top of
a bed of commercial MeOH synthesis catalyst (i.e., CZA).27 While this configuration was able to
capture ca. 150 µmol/g of CO2, the MeOH productivity was considerably lower at 12 µmol/g due
to low conversion of 20 % and MeOH selectivity of 22 %. Although this approach used a similar
combination of alkali sorbent and CZA, we hypothesize that the chemistry resulting from a stacked
bed of two separate materials (i.e., alkali sorbent and CZA) is different than what occurs when
CZA and the sorbent are co-located on the same surface by impregnation (this report). With
separate bed configuration, CO2 was proposed to desorb from the sorbent bed during the initial
temperature ramp and then subsequently converted as a gas phase reactant on the downstream
bed.27 However, on the Alk/CZA DFMs employed here, we hypothesize that the co-location of the
sorbent and catalyst sites offer a more synergistic mechanism, in which adsorbed and activated
CO2 reacts with nearby hydrogenation sites through a surface migration or spillover effect. Similar
mechanisms have been proposed for many solid-phase DFMs such as methanation DFMs35 and
the Pd – amine DFMs.25,26 Detailed mechanistic studies, including in situ or operando DRIFTS
under relevant reactive desorption conditions coupled with computational analysis, are necessary
to precisely define the mechanism, which will be the topic of future reports. The Na/CZA DFM
presented in this work marks a great advancement in RCC to MeOH, exhibiting a combination of
high CO2 adsorption capacity, high conversion of adsorbed CO2 in the reactive desorption stage,
and high MeOH selectivity with low selectivity to CH4, ultimately leading to a 4× increase in
MeOH production using solid-phase DFMs in a cyclic approach.

 In the context of developing an accompanying RCC process, it is important to note that CO
is a major by-product from this RCC process, especially during the low-pressure reactive
desorption step when the reactor pressure was released, and the catalyst was reduced for the next
cycle. In contrast, the majority of MeOH was produced in the high-pressure step of reactive
desorption (Figure 1.3 and Table 1.5), which is consistent with the thermodynamic preference for
MeOH synthesis from CO2 hydrogenation reactions.36-38 The high selectivity of CO during the
low-pressure step, coupled with low CH4 selectivity during the whole reactive desorption stage,
could be beneficial for downstream separation and may enable a partial recycle of the product
stream to improve overall, integrated MeOH yield through a series of RCC reactors. This is
illustrated in the conceptualized process flow diagram in Figure 1.12.

 As noted in the previous section, the evaluation of CO2 hydrogenation activity of these
novel DFMs in a co-fed experiment (with a H2:CO2 ratio of 3:1) did not accurately predict their
RCC performance. Although K/CZA exhibited low conversion and low selectivity towards MeOH
in a co-fed hydrogenation experiment, it exhibited the highest CO2 uptake, conversion of adsorbed
CO2, and MeOH selectivity in RCC evaluation. We hypothesize that this inconsistency may arise
from different H2:CO2 surface coverages when co-fed at ratio of 3 versus delivered in a cyclic
fashion where the H2:CO2 ratio is substantially greater. Based on the strong CO2 adsorption value
for K/CZA (ca. 135 µmol/g) and our catalyst bed volume (ca. 1 mL), we estimate a H2:CO2 ratio
of 7 at 30 bar and 100 °C at the start of reactive desorption. This ratio is expected to increase as
the reaction progresses and surface-bound CO2 is consumed. Another hypothesis is that the most
abundant surface intermediates may be different in the co-fed CO2 hydrogenation environment
versus the reactive desorption step in the RCC cycles. DRIFTS characterization representing the
adsorption step of the RCC cycles revealed that the strongly adsorbed CO2 was bound to CZA and
DFMs as various carbonate species (CO3*). Therefore, the following reactive desorption step may
involve the reaction between dissociated H2 (H*) with these various CO3* species. In contrast,
under co-fed conditions over CZA, the CO2 hydrogenation reaction has been proposed to proceed
via a formate intermediate, which is mainly formed by the reaction between molecularly adsorbed
CO2 (CO2*) and H*.39,40 A more in-depth mechanistic study is required for a precise explanation
for this difference in performance, which will be the topic of a separate report, and may necessitate
a combination of chemical computations and operando characterization.

 Similarly, when comparing the performance in RCC cycles, we rationalize the observed
similar activity and product selectivity for CZA and Ca/CZA based on DRIFTS data that showed
similar binding strengths and geometries of the surface CO3*. In contrast, dramatically different
binding strengths and geometries were observed on K/CZA compared to CZA and Ca/CZA. The
high overall CO2 conversion and MeOH selectivity observed over K/CZA may be due to the
reaction pathway proceeding through these unique K-CO3* species (i.e., the K-bound carbonates
being inherently activated for greater MeOH production based on geometry and proximity to
hydrogenation sites). Thus, as a second learning in the design of DFMs for MeOH synthesis via
RCC, an analysis of the CO2 binding geometry may be more instructive to anticipate differences
between the parent material and novel DFMs.

1.3.4 Effect of adsorption temperature on cyclic performance
 Effect of CO2 capture temperature over 5-Na/CZA DFM was evaluated by conducting five
CO2 adsorption – reactive desorption cycles performed under a temperature-and-pressure-swing
procedure similar to the catalysts studied previously. In this study, we varied the adsorption
temperature (Tads) from 100 – 250 °C at 50 °C intervals while the hydrogenation reaction was
executed at 250 °C and 30 bar. A fresh catalyst was used for each Tads studied. The average of the
last 3 cycles of MeOH productivity as a function of temperature during RCC performance is
depicted in Figure 1.13a, while the detailed MeOH productivity during each of the 5 cycles
performed with different adsorption temperatures is presented in Figure 1.13b. In average, the
MeOH productivity increased as Tads increased from 74.2 µmol/g at 100 °C, to 86.5 µmol/g at 150
°C where it reached its maximum value. At 200 °C the MeOH productivity slightly decreased to
82.1 µmol/g and plummeted to 56.9 µmol/g at isothermal conditions with Tads = 250 °C. Analyzing
the MeOH productivity cycle-per-cycle, the “start-up” effect can be observed for all Tads studied,
where the apparent high activity of the first cycle stands as an outlier and the MeOH productivity
stabilizes as the cycles progress. It can also be noted that the rate of MeOH productivity loss
(measured by the slope of the last 3 cycles for each Tads) is slower for Tads = 100 °C at -2.8
µmolMeOH/g/cycle and increases with temperature to -3.7, -4.4 and -3.1 µmolMeOH/g/cycle for Tads
= 150, 200 and 250 °C, respectively.

Figure 1.13. (a) Average MeOH productivity during cycles at various adsorption temperatures. (b)
MeOH productivity during each of the 5 cycles performed with different adsorption temperatures.
 CO2 capture temperature did not dramatically change total CO2 uptake (138.9-151.2
µmol/g) across the different temperatures studied (Table 1.9). However, total catalyst productivity

suppressed at Tads > 100°C.

1.3.5 Parametric design of experiments for simultaneous material and process
 optimization
 After selecting the CO2 capture process temperature as 100°C, a parametric study of
relevant hydrogenation conditions was explored. These parametric studies will also study the effect
of the different Na loadings, so a design of experiments (DOE) matrix has been developed to
ensure randomized tests and statistically relevant information can be gathered as the different
hydrogenation and Na-loading variables are studied in controlled experiments, with special
emphasis in maximizing MeOH productivity and selectivity. For these experiments the variables
to study and their low, middle, and high points chosen for the DOE matrix are presented in Table
1.10 and are as follow: Na loading, 1, 5 and 10 wt.% of Na in its hypothetical oxide form (i.e.,
N2O); hydrogenation temperature: 200, 225 and 250 °C; hydrogenation pressure: 10, 20 and 30
bar and hydrogen concentration: 10, 55 and 100%. The statistical software JMP was used to build
the DOE matrix with randomized tests, choosing to keep runs with similar Na loadings consecutive
for process streamlining. The software created an 18-run, full factorial matrix, selecting the low,
middle, and high Na-loading points, but only selecting the low and high points for the T, P and H2
concentration variables.

1.3.6 DFM stability study
 The stability of DFM in a long term RCC operation was investigated in a 100-cycle aging
study with a 10-Na/CZA DFM. The CO2 capture step was performed at 100 °C and atm pressure
followed by reactive desorption at 200 °C and 30 bar or 40 bar with 100% H2 with the results for
the 100 RCC cycles are shown in Figure 1.19. The DFM exhibited a more pronounced
deactivation during the first 20 cycles, indicated by a decrease in both strong CO2 adsorption
capacity and productivity, especially for MeOH. The rate of deactivation (i.e., MeOH productivity
loss determined by slope of regression) is greater in earlier cycles (0.66 µmol/g drop in MeOH
productivity in the first 20 cycles as shown in Figure 1.20a), whereas a stable MeOH productivity
over the same period is reported at the end of the aging study (slightly positive slope of 0.16 µmol/g
of MeOH productivity in the 85th to 107th cycle regression as shown in Figure 1.20b).
Encouragingly, MeOH selectivity remained stable between 85-90% throughout the 100 cycles,
with a modest increase to between 88-96% during the 40 bar cycles. In terms of MeOH
productivity, the 40 bar cycles (i.e cycles # 65-84) also resulted in a small increase at the beginning
of the switch to higher pressure but with a slightly higher rate of deactivation compared to 30 bar
cycles, therefore, starting at cycle #85, the hydrogenation pressure resumed at 30 bar.

2 Development of dual function materials for reactive
  carbon capture to produce CO
2.1 Background
 Development and deployment of technologies that convert waste CO2 from dilute point
sources to value-added products is important for strengthening economic resilience, promoting
workforce development, and expanding fuel and chemical supply chains.1 One such strategy is
carbon capture and utilization (CCU), in which (1) CO2 is captured from a dilute source using a
sorbent material; (2) the sorbent is regenerated by heat, vacuum, and/or steam and the resultant
CO2 stream is purified and compressed; (3) the CO2 is then catalytically converted to products,
such as CH4, CO, methanol, or potentially higher hydrocarbons.2 Step 2 is often considered to be
the major cost driver due to the energy and capital investment required for these operations. While
step 3 could hypothetically occur at the same location as steps 1 and 2, it is more frequently
envisioned that the compressed CO2 would be transported to a separate, dedicated facility with
ample access to thermal energy and hydrogen to perform the conversion. The issue of CO2
transport introduces additional barriers to scaling CCU given the current lack of infrastructure and
the permitting challenges of building such infrastructure.3 To eliminate these cost and
transportation concerns, Steps 2 and 3 can be streamlined through a process intensification
approach known as reactive carbon capture (RCC). In RCC, a single sorbent-catalyst material
(known as a dual function material, or DFM) is used to capture CO2 via interactions with basic
surface sites; the bound CO2 is then converted to products on adjacent catalytic sites in a reactive
desorption step by introducing a reducing agent (typically H2) with appropriate conditions to
promote the desired reaction.4-6 For methanol (MeOH) production from direct air capture (DAC),
RCC has the potential to reduce energy and capital costs by over 50% relative to conventional
CCU by combining sorbent regeneration and conversion into a single step.5 At the “heart” of the
RCC process, robust DFMs with high CO2 adsorption and tunable product selectivity are critical
to enabling this intensified renewable fuel/chemical pathway, and as such, have become a
burgeoning area of research.

 Conventional DFMs typically target the production of CH4 via CO2 methanation.6,20,41 Given
the generally high cost of CO2 capture and the relatively low market price of CH4, the economic
viability of RCC can be improved by targeting higher-value products, such as CO and MeOH.
Producing CO or MeOH can also alleviate downstream separations requirements as they are either
compatible with excess H2 as a co-product (to produce syngas, in the case of CO) or can be
separated through condensation (MeOH). CO is a promising alternative product from RCC as it
can be readily integrated into existing C1 upgrading pathways,42,43 especially if it can be produced
at atmospheric pressure. This ambient pressure operation conceptually improves H2 utilization,
which we recently demonstrated as the most influential cost driver of a scaled RCC process.44

 Selective RCC-to-CO has been demonstrated using alkali-Ru-Ni “switchable DFMs” via the
reverse water-gas shift reaction (RWGS) at operation temperatures > 600 °C.45 Temperatures
surpassing 600 °C push thermodynamic equilibrium towards CO over competing methanation
reactions.46,47 However, such a large temperature swing from point-source flue gas (e.g., 100 °C
for natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) plants)48 can negatively impact RCC process efficiency,
cost, and material durability, which motivates the development of lower reactive desorption

 40

temperatures. Li et al. demonstrated high CO selectivity (> 90%) at a more moderate temperature
of 350 °C, albeit with the use of an expensive Pt-containing DFM.49 In addition to cost, metal-
containing DFMs are prone to oxidation and may be negatively impacted by the relatively high
oxygen content in flue gas streams (5-10% O2 for NGCC flue gas).48 Repeated oxidation and re-
reduction of the catalytic metals during RCC cycling is likely to promote sintering and or/phase
segregation and degrade DFM performance over time.50 Additionally, re-reducing the metals in
the reactive desorption step will result in significant parasitic H2 consumption (e.g., re-reduction
of NiO in a 10 wt. % Ni DFM can theoretically require up to 1.7 mmol H2/g, an order of magnitude
more than would be required to stoichiometrically hydrogenate the 50 – 200 µmol CO2/g that such
DFMs could be expected to adsorb.6 There has thus been interest in metallic-phase free DFMs; for
example, Zhou et al. tested a metal free, mixed oxide DFM composed of CaO/Ca3Ga4O9/CaZrO3
to effectively convert captured CO2 (87% conversion) to CO at 650 °C with over 98% selectivity.51
In addition, Sasayama et al. tested Na/Al2O3 DFMs to produce CO with high selectivity (> 95%)
in isothermal RCC at 500 °C.52,53 High temperature operation seems integral for these materials,
as the absence of a specific catalytic site appears to limit CO productivity at lower temperatures.
In the case of Sasayama et al. for example, minimal products were observed below 400°C.52 This
necessitates additional investigation into metallic-phase free catalysts to pair with the alkali
sorbent site to enable RCC-to-CO at comparatively moderate temperatures. These reports inform
the development of high performing CO-selective DFMs with the following rational design
criteria: (1) active and selective at atmospheric pressure; (2) reactive desorption achieved at
relatively moderate temperatures (≤ 400 °C); (3) free of precious metals and/or a metallic phase
while including a specific catalytic phase; (4) multi-cycle durability when O2 is included in the
CO2-containing adsorption stream.

 Zn-Al mixed oxides (ZnAlOx) are cost effective catalysts that have been explored for steady-
state CO2 hydrogenation.54 The ZnAlOx catalysts achieved their best performance in a highly
reducing atmosphere with low CO2 content (1% CO2) due to the need for oxygen vacancies to
serve as active sites.54,55 These mixed metal oxides are sometimes modified with Zr or used in
tandem with acid catalysts (e.g., zeolites) to produce aromatics or higher hydrocarbons via a
MeOH or DME intermediate.56-58 Alkali promoters (e.g., K, Rb, Cs) have also been used to
enhance ZnAlOx activity in the high temperature water-gas shift reaction.59 Significant advantages
of these materials are apparent towards our goals for use in an RCC process, including their relative
abundance, low cost, and structural stability.60 As Zn-Al mixed oxides lack an oxidizable metallic
phase and are capable of activating H2 through heterolytic dissociation,61,62 we expect a DFM
composed of ZnAlOX will remain active when O2 is co-fed during CO2 capture. The efficacy of
ZnAlOx for CO2 conversion also suggests that modification with alkali species may improve CO2
sorption capacity for use in RCC. Our previous research has demonstrated that trends in steady-
state CO2 hydrogenation do not necessarily transfer to the RCC environment;63 thus, it is
imperative that these materials be assessed in cyclic RCC tests to accurately determine their utility
for this process. In this work, K-modified ZnAlOx DFMs (K/ZnAlOx) were synthesized with varied
Zn:Al molar ratios by co-precipitation followed by incipient wetness impregnation with the K
sorbent species. Previous work by Park et. al.62 examined the correlation between Zn:Al molar
ratio and steady state CO2 conversion. They found that compositions with higher Zn content
exhibited superior CO2 conversion but underwent rapid deactivation at the temperatures (620 °C)
necessary for steady state RWGS. Compositions with greater Al content showed superior stability
at the cost of CO2 conversion. A Zn:Al molar composition of 1:2, which corresponds to the
stoichiometric ratio for the ZnAl2O4 spinel phase, was found to provide the necessary material

 41

stability at these elevated temperatures. Building on these findings, we have chosen three
representative classes of Zn:Al molar composition: an equal Zn:Al composition (Zn1Al1), a
composition reflecting the stoichiometric ratio of ZnAl2O4 spinel phase (Zn1Al2), and an Al-rich
composition (Zn1Al5). K was selected as the sorbent species due to its superior CO2 capture and
conversion promotion when compared to other alkali and alkaline earth modifiers.6,59,63

2.2 Experimental
2.2.1 Synthesis of unmodified and K-modified ZnAlOX
 Unmodified mixed Zn-Al oxide materials (ZnAlOX) were synthesized by the pH-controlled
co-precipitation of Zn and Al precursor salts, similar to previous reports.56,64 Two solutions were
prepared in a typical synthesis; Solution 1, an aqueous solution Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (Sigma Aldrich)
and Al(NO3)3·9H2O (Sigma Aldrich), and Solution 2, an aqueous solution of (NH4)2CO3 (Sigma
Aldrich). The concentrations of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O and Al(NO3)3·9H2O in Solution 1 were adjusted
based on the desired wt% ratio of Zn:Al, with the assumption of 100% precipitation. Targeted
Zn:Al molar ratios were 1:1, 1:2 and 1:5, corresponding to an equal Zn:Al composition (Zn1Al1),
a composition reflecting the stoichiometric ratio of ZnAl2O4 spinel (Zn1Al2), and an Al-rich
composition (Zn1Al5). The concentration of (NH4)2CO3 in Solution 2 was adjusted to achieve a
1:1 molar ratio of NO3- and NH4+ ions when both solutions were combined.

 Both solutions were loaded into syringes and mounted to syringe pumps (New Era Pump
Systems Inc). The two solutions were added simultaneously at a rate of 5 mL/min into a three-
necked round bottom flask containing a stir bar and heated to 70 °C. Once the addition of both
solutions was completed, the pH was monitored using a pH probe (Thermo Scientific Orion Star
A321) and adjusted to 7.0 using an aqueous NH4OH solution (28-30%, ACS grade, VWR
chemicals). Once the pH was adjusted, the solution was left to stir for three hours. The pH of the
solution was checked at 30-minute intervals to ensure it had not deviated from 7.0.

 Once stirring had completed, the precipitate was filtered by vacuum filtration and washed
with 200 mL of DI water. This DI water washing step was repeated six times, to be consistent with
previous work synthesizing mixed Zn-Al oxides.56,65,66 The washed precipitate was dried at 100 °C
overnight in static air and crushed prior to calcination at 500 °C (heating rate of 2 °C/min) in static
air for 4 h to ensure thermal decomposition of any residual nitrate or ammonium impurities.

 The parent ZnAlOX materials were modified with K using IWI. The incipient wetness point
for each material was identified after calcination (Zn1Al1 = 0.48 mL/g, Zn1Al2 = 0.70 mL/g,
Zn1Al5 = 1.2 mL/g). An aqueous solution of K2CO3 (Sigma Aldrich) was prepared and added
dropwise onto each material using a pipette to achieve approximately 5 wt% of K in the
hypothetical oxide form (i.e., K2O). After dropwise addition of each solution, the material was
dried at 120 °C overnight in static air and calcined at 500 °C, also in static air, for 4 h.

2.2.2 Characterization
 Nitrogen isotherms were collected at -196 °C (Quantachrome Instruments, Quadrasorb).
Samples (250 mg) were weighed into clean, dry sample tubes. Prior to analysis, samples were
degassed under vacuum overnight at 120 °C. After degassing, samples were re-weighed to evaluate
the removal of water and physisorbed species. The final dry mass was recorded and used to
normalize the nitrogen adsorption quantities. Specific surface area (SSA) was determined using

 42

the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. The P/P0 range for each isotherm was selected where
the BET transform plot was linear and the Rouquerol transform plot was monotonically increasing
with pressure. Total pore volume was evaluated at a relative pressure of P/P0 = 0.98. The surface
area and pore volume measurements had an estimated error of ± 5 % based on the reproducibility
error of the calibration standard provided by Quantachrome.

 Elemental content of the materials was assessed through inductively coupled plasma
optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES). Approximately 25 mg of sample was added to PTFE
reaction vessels and combined with 2 mL concentrated HNO3, 5 mL concentrated HCl, and 1 mL
50% HBF4. The vessels were loaded into a microwave digestion chamber (Ultrawave) and heated
for 1 h at 250 °C under a nitrogen pressure of 40 bar. Following digestion, each solution was
diluted to 50 mL with DI water. A 5 mL aliquot was then taken and further diluted to 50 mL in a
14% HNO3 matrix. These dilutions were analyzed on an Agilent 5110 ICP-OES alongside
standards for Al, Zn, and K at 0, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 100 ppm in the same matrix.

 X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer
with a Cu Kα source. Diffractograms were collected in the 2θ range of 25−55° at a 2 °/min scan
rate. XRD patterns were collected after material synthesis and calcination. In situ XRD was
conducted on the same instrument with a Rigaku Reactor X high temperature attachment for
reactive gases. Samples were placed inside the Reactor X and the cell was purged with UHP N2
flowing at 50 sccm for 1 h. A reference XRD pattern was then collected, and the gas was switched
to H2 flowing at 50 sccm. The temperature was then ramped to 400 °C (ramp rate: 5°C/min) and
held for 8 h, to replicate the pretreatment conducted prior to RCC testing. After the 8 h pretreatment
period, the cell was cooled to room temperature and an XRD pattern was collected.

 Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and elemental composition maps
obtained by x-ray energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) were collected using a Spectra200
(ThermoFisher) STEM operating at 200kV in STEM mode using a high-angle annular dark-field
(HAADF) detector, with a dwell time of 20-50 µs for EDS map acquisition.

 In-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was
performed using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50R FT-IR spectrometer equipped with a Harrick
Praying Mantis high temperature reaction chamber attachment with CaF2 windows. The DRIFTS
spectra and backgrounds were collected at a resolution of 4 cm-1 with 128 scans. The samples were
loaded into the sample holder on top of a small bed of silica. The samples were pre-reduced with
40 sccm of H2 for 8 h at 400 °C (ramp rate: 5 °C/min) before cooling to 100 °C. Backgrounds were
taken at 25°C intervals during cooling. At 100 °C, the sample was purged with 40 sccm of He for
1 h and an additional background was taken. Spectra were taken every 5 min during a 30 min
adsorption step with 40 sccm of 2.5% CO2/He and a 1 h purge step with 40 sccm He. Subsequently,
spectra were taken every 25 °C (between 100 °C and 400 °C, ramp rate: 5°C/min) during a reactive
desorption step with 40 sccm of H2.

2.2.3 Reactive capture and conversion of CO2
 Performance of unmodified and K-modified ZnAlOx in the CO2 RCC process was
evaluated by conducting CO2 adsorption – reactive desorption cycles in a Microactivity Effi unit
(Micromeritics, Inc., model MAE20208) under temperature swing conditions. Typically, 0.5 g of
unmodified or K-modified ZnAlOx (pelletized and sieved between 50-70 mesh) was loaded in a

 43

9.1 mm I.D. fixed-bed stainless steel reactor and pretreated by exposure to 100 sccm of UHP H2
for 8 h at 400 °C, achieved with a 2°C/min ramp, consistent with previous work.66-68 Once
pretreatment was completed, the flowing gas was switched to 100 sccm of N2 and the reactor
temperature was ramped down to 100 °C for RCC cycle testing.

 An RCC cycle consisted of the following four steps, all conducted at atmospheric pressure:

 1. CO2 adsorption: Adsorption by 1% CO2/N2 flowing at 100 sccm for 1 h at 100 °C.
 2. Purging: N2 flowing at 100 sccm for 1 h at 100 °C.
 3. Reactive desorption: Hydrogenation of captured CO2 under H2 flowing at 100 sccm, where
 the temperature was ramped from 100 to 400 °C and held for 2 h.
 4. Cooling/ purging: N2 flowing at 100 sccm as the temperature was ramped to 100 °C and
 held for 1 h.

 In instances where O2 was cofed with CO2, zero air was used to cofeed 5% O2 alongside
the 1% CO2 used in the above CO2 adsorption step.

 The concentration of CO2 and products were quantified on-line with a FTIR analyzer (600-
HSC from California Analytical Instruments). A blank run was performed in triplicate with
identical cycle conditions as described above using an empty reactor tube. The average
concentration values of these triplicate runs were used as a baseline. The concentration profile of
each compound was integrated to quantify the total reactant or product amount, in µmol.

2.3 Results and discussion
2.3.1 Preparation of K-modified ZnAlOX DFMs with varying Zn:Al ratio
 ZnAlOX catalysts were synthesized through co-precipitation from aqueous solutions of Zn
and Al nitrate, similar to previously described methods.56,57,64 The relative abundance of Zn and
Al was controlled during co-precipitation to create oxides with an equal Zn:Al composition
(Zn1Al1), a composition reflecting the stoichiometric ratio of ZnAl2O4 spinel (Zn1Al2), and an Al-
rich composition (Zn1Al5) to understand how catalyst composition is related to cyclic RCC

the (220) facet of ZnAl2O4) identified by STEM (Fig. 2.2d).70 The absence of reflections due to

the segregation of a ZnO phase in Zn-Al mixed oxides.71 The degree of peak shift toward ZnO
appears to correlate with Zn content, as K/Zn1Al1 contained sharp peaks centered around the ZnO

2.3.2 RCC performance of ZnAlOx DFMs
 The unmodified and K-modified ZnAlOX DFMs were initially studied under oxygen-free
RCC cycles to identify the best performing DFM for further RCC testing with O2 in a simulated
flue gas feed. The cyclic operation of RCC testing is depicted in Fig. 2.5a. After a pretreatment
under flowing H2 at 400 °C, the DFM was subjected to an adsorption step using a 1% CO2/N2
stream at 100 °C. The CO2-laden DFM was then exposed to flowing H2 and the temperature was

prior to introduction of reactive gases to remove excess or weakly bound H2 or CO2 and to ensure
that any products detected were due to the conversion of species strongly-bound to the DFM. This
enables accurate calculation of CO2 adsorption and CO2 conversion (Fig. 2.5b, c). Three cycles
were used to obtain average performance values (Table 2.2). Figs. 2.6 and 2.7 provide individual
cycle data. As previously mentioned, we were motivated to explore atmospheric pressure
operations in this RCC approach, and consequently, DFMs were tested under atmospheric
hydrogenation conditions.

the unmodified ZnAlOX. These values compare favorably to other DFMs using similar K loadings

 An effective DFM should be able to transition from adsorption to reactive desorption with
minimal CO2 loss. We studied this transition by comparing CO2 and CO evolution as detected by
online FTIR during reactive desorption. Representative species evolution traces for the unmodified
and K-modified ZnAlOX are summarized in Fig. 2.8. To facilitate quantitative comparison of these
evolution profiles, we took inspiration from emissions control catalysis, where light-off
temperature is used to evaluate thermally-driven activity. Light off is defined as the temperature
at which an appreciable amount of target reactant is converted (common conversions used are 10,
50 and 90%).59,70,71 We use a T50 here to define CO light-off, which is the temperature where each
DFM produced 50% of its maximum mol% CO. For the RCC process described here, lower T50
values are desired to mitigate the unreactive desorption of CO2 and improve overall conversion to
desired products at lower temperature. Increasing Zn content coincided with decreasing CO light-
off temperature. Zn1Al1 demonstrated the lowest CO light-off (307 °C) among unmodified
ZnAlOX, followed by Zn1Al2 (322 °C). Zn1Al5 did not evolve sufficient CO during reactive
desorption to appropriately identfy a light-off temperature. These observerations are consistent
with the general scaling of CO2 hydrogenation activity with Zn loading in mixed Zn-Al oxides,
where greater Zn content enables lower temperatures for CO2 conversion.59,62 K-modified ZnAlOX
showed decreased CO light-off temperature relative to their unmodified counterparts and exhibited
a similar trend to the parent materials, where K/Zn1Al1 had the lowest CO light-off of 291 °C
(16 °C lower than Zn1Al1), followed by K/Zn1Al2 (305 °C, 17 °C lower than Zn1Al2) and K/Zn1Al5
(312 °C). Additionally, greater CO2 conversion was observed over the K-modified DFM compared
to the respective parent material (Table 2.2), indicating that K modification improved the retention
of CO2 on the surface of the DFM for the hydrogenation reaction during temperature ramp. This
is corroborated by the extended tail of desorbing CO2 observed between 40-80 min in the reactive
desorption temperature ramp (Fig. 2.8). These data demonstrate that there are synergistic
interactions between the K capture sites and the underlying ZnAlOX structure that combine to
increase both CO2 capture capacity and hydrogenation activity at lower temperatures. The
relationship between K sites and CO2 surface species is the focus of spectroscopic studies
described below (vide infra).

2.3.3 Contextualizing DFM performance against CO2 hydrogenation
 thermodynamics.
 As previously mentioned, there are important thermodynamic considerations for CO2
hydrogenation to CO that necessitate specific reaction conditions. Industrial RWGS is typically
conducted with a H2:CO2 ratio of 2–4 and temperatures of at least 600 °C to achieve sufficient CO
yield.80-82 At temperatures ≤ 550 °C, the highest thermodynamically predicted CO yield is
approximately 25%, irrespective of H2:CO2 ratio. In order to compare the efficacy of producing
CO using RCC vs typical co-fed hydrogenation, the equilibrium yield of CO and CH4 from steady-
state CO2 hydrogenation at atmospheric pressure and over a range of temperatures and H2:CO2
molar ratios were quantified by thermodynamic analysis presented in section 2.2.4. Surface plots
of the equilibrium yields are shown in Figure 2.9. The equilibrium CO yield increases with
temperature, but does not reach substantial values (i.e., >50%) until temperatures greater than 600
°C. At the maximum temperature used in our RCC testing (400 °C), the equilibrium-limited CO
yield is 1.7% with a constant H2:CO2 ratio of 0.1 (Fig. 2.9a). Increasing the H2:CO2 ratio at a given
temperature decreases the equilibrium CO yield and increases CH4 yield (Fig. 2.9b). For example
at 400 °C, equilibrium CO yield remains low and decreases from 1.7 to 0.13 % as the H2:CO2 ratio
increases from 0.1 to 5.0. The CH4 yield in contrast, increases from 1.8 to 96.7 % at 400 °C over
the same range of H2:CO2 ratio. An approximate H2:CO2 ratio for the highest performing K/Zn1Al2
can be calculated at 400 °C using the quantity of CO2 remaining on the surface (50.9 µmol/g) and
the assumption that the entire reactor volume is occupied by H2 during reactive desorption. This
results in a H2:CO2 ratio of approximately 14.2 for K/Zn1Al2 at 400 °C and 1 atm pressure
(Equation 14 in section 2.2.4). These conditions correspond to a thermodynamically predicted CO
yield less than 1%. A more conservative H2:CO2 ratio can be achieved assuming that only the H2
occupying the catalyst bed is relevant (Equation 11 in section 2.2.4); however, the
thermodynamically predicted yield of CO would still be lower than 2% (Fig. 2.9a).

 K/Zn1Al2 in contrast demonstrated a CO yield of 53% in RCC testing. According to
thermodynamic equilibrium limitations, a temperature of at least 700 °C is required to achieve a
CO yield of 53% regardless of H2:CO2 ratio. Zn-Al mixed oxides demonstrated near 100%
selectivity to CO during steady-state CO2 hydrogenation conducted at atmospheric pressure.67 The
CO2 conversion for these materials, however, remained below equilibrium predictions at
approximately 10% at 400 °C.62,83 This suggests that the enhanced CO2 conversion and CO yield
observed here is due to the RCC process, which is consistent with previous work from our group
that demonstrated K-modified Cu-Zn-Al DFMs deviate from steady state hydrogenation behavior
when used in an RCC setting.63 This RCC process employs dynamically-ramped temperature and
separate CO2 flow and H2 flow steps in a cyclic fashion. Therefore, the system is never operating
at steady-state conditions. Perturbing steady-state operation through changing temperature and
reactant concentration gradients has been shown to improve selectivity and yield in multi-product
reactions.83 The use of basic capture agents to offset equilibrium of CO2 conversion has also been
previously documented. Linehan et. al.,84 demonstrated that a molecular copper catalyst exceeds
the equilibrium-limited CO₂ hydrogenation yield by using a coordinating base to capture and
stabilize formate, continuously removing CO₂ from the gas phase and shifting the equilibrium
forward. Similarly, in Hu et. al.,85 a Ni-CaO bifunctional catalyst integrated CO₂ capture and dry
methane reforming (CaLDRM) in a single-step process, where CaO temporarily stores CO₂,
releasing it in tandem with CH₄ reforming. This continuous removal and controlled release of
CO₂ perturbs equilibrium constraints, leading to over 40% CO₂ conversion under conditions where
equilibrium predicts less than 3%. Similarly, we hypothesize that in RCC, where a dual-function
material first adsorbs CO₂ and subsequently hydrogenates it, the changing concentration
gradient as reactive desorption proceeds can create a perturbation in the equilibrium state. The
ability of RCC to access high CO yields at temperatures more than 300 °C lower than
thermodynamically predicted for steady-state CO2 hydrogenation illustrates why this process is a
promising strategy for CO2 utilization. As such, it was of further interest to gain insight into the
transformations of surface species on the Zn1Al2-based DFM and understand the source of
enhanced RCC performance resulting from K-modification.

2.3.4 Transformation of surface species during RCC over Zn1Al2 and K/Zn1Al2
 The RCC cycles were mimicked with in situ DRIFTS experiments to investigate the
dynamics of surface species on the best performing DFM (K/Zn1Al2) and its unmodified
counterpart (Zn1Al2). Similar to the RCC testing, the materials were first reduced in H2 at 400 °C,
exposed to 2.5% CO2/He at 100 °C, and purged in He for 1 h to remove weakly adsorbed CO2
before exposed to H2 and heated to 400 °C at 5 °C/m ramp rate for reactive desorption. The
resulting spectra during the CO2 adsorption and purging steps are presented in Fig. 2.10a.
Observed surface species are depicted structurally in Fig. 2.10b and peak assignments are
summarized in Table 2.4. CO2 adsorption on Zn1Al2 results in a complex DRIFTS spectrum.
Based on in situ XRD results, as shown in Fig. 2.3, H2 pretreatment of the Zn1Al2 material resulted
in a mixture of wurtzite ZnO, spinel ZnAl2O4, and amorphous Al2O3 phases and thus, a variety of
adsorption sites and associated adspecies are expected. The most prominent feature in the spectrum
was a broad absorbance centered at 1535 cm-1 (spanning 1595-1460 cm-1), which encompasses
several peaks corresponding to a variety of adspecies: first, ZnAl2O4-associated m-CO3*
(monodentate carbonate) is assigned as a contributor to the observed absorbance, based on its
reported peak at 1540-1531 cm-1, which was also verified by an accompanying peak at 1359 cm-

 . Several Al2O3-associated species also exhibit peaks in this spectral range, including a m-CO3*

 62

at 1518 cm-1 (accompanying peak at 1320 cm-1)31 and b-HCOO* (bidentate formate) at 1590 cm-1
(accompanying peaks at 1400 and 1386 cm-1).68,86 The presence of a hydrogenated intermediate is
likely due to the abundance of the ZnAl2O4 phase, which can immediately convert surface
carbonates to HCOO* with H* species remaining on the surface from pre-treatment, which are
likely present given the reported hydrogenation activity and hydrogen storage potential of this
material.87 Similarly, ZnAl2O4 associated b-HCOO* have been reported to exhibit peaks at 1596,

peaks at such wavenumbers (1425 cm-1 and 1440 cm-1, respectively).68,88 The presence of the
ZnAl2O4-HCO3* was confirmed by an accompanying peak at 1620 cm-1, which correlated well
with the reported peak location for the asymmetric vibrational mode of this species.68 Similarly,
peaks at 1647 and 1232 cm-1 correlate well with characteristic features of Al2O3-HCO3*.31 Finally,
the broad absorbances around 1535 and 1420 cm-1 most likely also encompass ZnO-associated

slightly lower intensities) after a 1-h purge in He, indicating that the surface species remain
available for conversion in the following reactive desorption step.

 Additional adsorption sites were introduced upon K modification, resulting in peaks not
observed on the parent Zn1Al2 (Fig. 2.10a). K adsorption sites have been reported to bind CO2
most commonly as bidentate carbonate (b-CO3*) structures.32,33 Peaks at 1662 and 1309 cm-1
correlated best with a bridged bidentate structure (b,b-CO3*) and those at 1633 and 1295 cm-1
correlated with a chelating bidentate structure (c,b-CO3*). An extra shoulder not detected on the
parent Zn1Al2, at 1457 cm-1, can be assigned to a polydentate associated with the K sites. In
addition to the new features, there were significant changes in the intensity and shape of the broad
envelopes centered at 1535 and 1450-1300 cm-1 indicating that the K sites are the dominant
adsorption sites and result in a different distribution of adsorbed species on Al2O3, ZnAl2O4, and
ZnO phases. Specifically, the disappearance of the 1232 cm-1 peak suggests that Al2O3-associated
HCO3* was no longer present. This was corroborated by a decrease in intensity of the 1420 cm-1
area. A subsequent shift in the center to ca. 1400 cm-1 indicates that HCOO* species on Al2O3 and
ZnAl2O4 phases persisted. The intensities of peaks decreased slightly during the 1-h purge with
He, with surface species associated to both K and ZnAlOx phases remaining available for
conversion in reactive desorption.

 Following adsorption and purging at 100 °C, both materials were exposed to H2 and heated
to 400 °C, with spectra taken at 25 °C intervals. The evolution of surface species on Zn1Al2 during
this reactive desorption step is shown in Fig. 2.10c. Three stages were observed: desorption of
CO2, formation of new intermediates, and desorption/reaction of intermediates to produce CO.
During the initial CO2 desorption phase (100–225 °C), there was a decrease in intensity of all
species, consistent with CO2 observed during the reactive desorption step in Fig. 2.8d. At the end
of this phase (225 °C), the most intense features correlated best with Al2O3-associated HCOO*
and ZnAl2O3-associated HCO3* (i.e., mostly CO3* species were desorbed), but all species were
still present. In the second stage (225°C – 325°C), there was growth of several peaks between
1635-1590 and 1400-1370 cm-1. These encompassed the existing formates (HCOO*) associated
with Al2O3 and ZnAl2O4 sites. Additionally, a new shoulder at 1635 cm-1 indicated the growth of
a new intermediate structure, best correlated with ZnAl2O4-associated m-HCOO* (monodentate

 63

formate), which has been reported to exhibit a feature in the range of 1633-1615 cm-1.68,88,91 There
was also growth of a new peak at 1448 cm-1. As mentioned above, the Al2O3-HCO3* exhibits a
peak in this area but the lack of a peak above 1640 and ~1230 cm-1 contradicts the re-formation of
this surface species. Instead, there are also reports of ZnO-associated polydentate carbonates (p-
CO3*) which exhibits features at 1466 and 1386 cm-1.90 Since there was also growth of features in
the 1400-1380 cm-1 region, it is possible that this new CO3* species was formed through re-
adsorption of desorbed CO2 on ZnO sites. Both the RCC data (Fig. 2.8d) and DRIFTS spectra
identified the presence of gas-phase CO2 at these temperatures which can be re-adsorbed. It should
also be mentioned, however, that there are reports of similar materials exhibiting a peak at
1457 cm-1 during co-fed hydrogenation, associated with ∂(CH) of a methoxy (-OCH3) species.92
This species also exhibits features in the 3000-2800 cm-1 region,88 which were observed on the
Zn1Al2 material (Fig. S11a). The presence of -OCH3 species on the surface is possible, given the
onset of CO2 conversion and the small production of CH4 observed in this temperature range
during the RCC testing above (Table 2.2 and Fig. 2.8d). With the observation of C-H stretches,
the peak at 1448 cm-1 is assigned to surface methoxy. In the last stage (325 – 400 °C), the peak
intensities of most of surface species decreased significantly and correlated to CO evolution during
RCC testing (Fig. 2.8d), indicating that surface species are reacted or desorbed to form CO during
this stage; however, the feature at 1448 cm-1 still persisted at this stage, indicating that these
methoxy species were not reactive.

 The evolution of species on K/Zn1Al2 (Fig. 2.10d) followed the same three steps with
differences in onset temperatures. In the CO2 desorption stage (100 °C – 250 °C), there was a
decrease in intensity of the peaks relating to CO3* species, even those related to K-adsorption sites.
However, the decrease in intensity was less dramatic and the desorption was prolonged to a higher
temperature than on the parent Zn1Al2 (250 vs. 225 °C), indicating that adsorbed CO2 was more
stable on K-modified DFMs. This is consistent with the extended CO2 evolution profile during
reactive desorption in RCC testing (Fig. 2.8c, d). During the intermediate growth stage (250 – 300
°C), there was a slight growth of peaks related to HCOO* species. Most notably, the 1448 cm-1
feature observed on the Zn1Al2 material was not observed here and no correlated peaks were
detected in the 2500-3000 cm-1 region (Fig. 2.11b). These results indicate that the formation of
unreactive species (-OCH3) on parent Zn1Al2 material was suppressed on K/Zn1Al2. Finally,
between 300 and 400 °C, all peak intensities decreased monotonically, indicating reaction and
desorption of intermediates to form products (i.e., CO as observed in product evolution profile
(Fig. 2.8c) in RCC testing). By 400 °C all surface species were reacted/desorbed, in contrast to
Zn1Al2, where unreacted species were still observed. The final stage occurred at lower
temperatures with K-modified Zn1Al2 material (300 °C) than the parent material (350 °C), which
is consistent with lower light-off temperature observed on CO evolution during RCC testing after
K modification (322 °C vs. 305 °C).

 Through the analyses described above, we can propose the mechanistic differences in the
evolution of surface species on Zn1Al2 and K/Zn1Al2 during RCC, which are summarized in the
schematic in Fig. 2.12. Zn1Al2 is depicted in Fig. 2.12a as the active ZnAl2O4 domain for
simplicity. Literature has shown that both the ZnO and ZnAl2O4 spinel phases can facilitate CO2
hydrogenation by heterolytic H2 activation in steady-state conditions.52,61,62 Our characterization
and RCC findings, however, demonstrate that Zn1Al2, which exhibits a molar ratio near
stoichiometric for the ZnAl2O4 spinel, had superior CO productivity among the parent materials
tested while also maintaining sufficient surface area suitable for high CO2 capture and promotion
by K-modification. As such, we designated the ZnAl2O4 phase as the most relevant catalytic
domain, which is consistent with literature reports on steady-state CO2 hydrogenation.59,62,87,88

 The mechanism of reactive desorption step on Zn1Al2 begins with a mixture of CO3* and
HCOO* surface species as shown in inset (i) of Fig. 2.12a. A significant portion of these surface
species (mostly CO3*) desorb during the initial temperature ramp (inset (ii), Fig. 2.12a), coinciding
with ca. 60% of captured CO2 lost as observed in the RCC testing. In contrast, K modification
alters two significant adsorption characteristics. First, K modification introduces unique capture
sites, which increase CO2 capture capacity (331 vs. 110 µmol/g, Table 2.2) and forming different
K-bound adsorbed carbonate species, such as bridged and chelating bidentate carbonate and
polydentate carbonate as reflected in inset (i), Fig. 2.12b. Second, these K-bound surface
intermediates were better retained during the initial temperature ramp of the reactive desorption
step, as evidenced by (1) a smaller decrease in DRIFTS peak intensities compared to the
unmodified Zn1Al2, (2) a decrease in the amount of desorbed CO2 during RCC testing with
K/Zn1Al2 (ca. 47% vs ca. 60% over Zn1Al2), and (3) the extension of CO2 desorption to higher
temperatures on K/Zn1Al2 (250°C, compared to 225°C on Zn1Al2, Fig. 2.10c,d).

 The mechanism of reactive desorption on Zn1Al2 proceeds by hydrogenation of the
adsorbed CO3* and HCOO* into a mixture of HCOO* and methoxy intermediates between 225°C
and 325°C (inset (iii), Fig. 2.12a). It should be noted that a portion of the adsorbed species, i.e
polydentate species, persists and does not transform. A majority of the HCOO* species are then
reacted and desorbed as CO starting at 325°C, correlating well with the observed light-off
temperature of 322°C (Fig. 2.8d). However, the methoxy and polydentate species remain
unreactive (inset (iv), Fig. 2.12a). Comparatively, the K-bound CO3* species on K/Zn1Al2 are
more readily converted to HCOO* intermediates (inset (iii), Fig. 2.12b) as this step started at lower
temperature (300 °C) as compared to parent Zn1Al2 material (325 °C). Transformation of these
intermediates (Fig. 5d) are consistent with the lower light-off temperature for CO formation in the
RCC testing (305 °C, Fig 2.8c). Notably, these K-bound species were completely reacted and
desorbed by 400 °C without formation of the unreactive methoxy intermediate or persistence of a
strongly bound carbonate on ZnO or ZnAl2O4 sites (inset (iv), Fig. 2.12b). Thus, by the end of the
reactive desorption step, all sites are available again for CO2 adsorption and H2 activation in the
next cycle. These results indicate that the addition of K not only increased the surface population
of CO2 capture sites but also enhanced hydrogenation of the adsorbed species via formation of
new surface intermediates and suppression of unreactive intermediate on parent material. It is
important to note that K-modification produced changes in the identity and concentration of
surface species, which improved CO formation kinetics, but the selectivity of the DFM was
unchanged. Lastly, given that CO light-off temperatures for all K/ZnAlOX were under 350 °C, it
is possible that temperatures up to 400 °C are not necessary, presenting an opportunity for future
process optimization.

2.3.5 Evaluating DFM stability in extended, O2-containing reactive carbon
 capture
 The process intensification approach of RCC implies minimal cleanup of the gas stream
used for CO2 capture and conversion. It is therefore important to design DFMs that tolerate reactive
species that are common to point source emissions. Oxygen is one such reactive component, which
readily oxidizes metals commonly used for catalytic reductions in DFMs, such as Ru, Ni, and
Cu.45,63 These metals must be reduced at elevated temperature during the reactive desorption step
in RCC applications. This increases H2 consumption and cycle time, and further, may lead to
decreased product yield by extending the amount of time the DFM is held at elevated temperature
where captured CO2 could desorb without reduction.73,93 K/Zn1Al2 was down-selected as the most
promising DFM for studies with oxygen, as it exhibited the highest CO2 adsorption, 99%
selectivity to CO, and markedly greater CO productivity compared to K/Zn1Al1 (1.22x) and
K/Zn1Al5 (2.97x). A 25-cycle RCC experiment without and with oxygen was performed: 15 cycles
without cofed O2 (identical to the procedure used in RCC data presented above), followed by 10
cycles with 5% O2 cofed with CO2 during the adsorption step. The first 5 RCC cycles without
cofed O2 were used for material de-greening and data were not collected.

 As shown in Fig. 2.13, the CO2 adsorption capacity, conversion, and product distribution
were consistent across this experiment, regardless of O2 presence. This result indicates two key
takeaways. First, the K/Zn1Al2 DFM was relatively stable during extended RCC cycling. Second,
the DFM was highly tolerant to the presence of O2 in the CO2 adsorption stream. The average CO2
adsorption capacities were similar between the RCC cycles without and with cofed O2 (332 and
325 mmolCO2/gDFM, respectively), with the difference within the error range (Fig. 2.14). The
consistency between these CO2 capture values suggests that O2 is not competing with CO2 for
adsorption sites. The CO selectivity remained higher than 99% and the CO2 conversion remained
close to 50% throughout the 20 cycles that were quantified. The cycle-to-cycle variability in CO2
capture and productivity to CO remained within the standard error between the RCC cycles
without and with cofed O2 (Table 2.5). The structural stability of the K/Zn1Al2 material after this
25-cycle test was corroborated by post reaction STEM/EDS and XRD characterization, which did
not indicate any major structural changes or phase segregation of the K/Zn1Al2 (Fig 2.15). This is
consistent with the reported stability of ZnAlOX structures in both reducing and oxidizing
environments at temperatures as high as 700 °C.71,94,95

3 Process modelling and scale-up
3.1 Background
 The production of low-carbon MeOH fills several gaps in the low-carbon economy that have
not been filled. MeOH can be used as a fuel, especially in the shipping industry, which is
particularly difficult to decarbonize through electrification.14 Shipping giant Maersk has recently
made this a reality by signing a deal with green MeOH producer OCI Global to prepare for the
maiden voyage of a MeOH-enabled container ship, while committing to transport a minimum of
25% of its ocean cargo using green fuels by 2030,96 signaling a growing need for low-carbon
marine fuels that MeOH is ideally suited to fill. Besides its fuel applications, MeOH is used to
produce other chemicals such as formaldehyde, acetic acid, and plastics, and nearly all of its
current 98 million tonnes per annum production currently comes from fossil fuels.17,97 Currently,
most green MeOH is derived from renewable feedstocks via biomass-to-MeOH routes. However,
the production of “bioMeOH” is subject to biomass feedstock availability constraints, whereas
CO2-derived MeOH production pathways do not face this feedstock limitation. Therefore, a low-
carbon CO2-to-MeOH process that is economically competitive with fossil fuel MeOH production
routes is urgently needed if MeOH is to play a significant role in decarbonization.

 There are commercialized processes to convert CO2 and renewable H2 to MeOH, namely,
carbon capture and purification followed by CO2 hydrogenation.98,99 Hydrogen cost (via renewable
electricity cost and capacity factor) has been identified as the most critical factor toward the
economic viability of this pathway.100 The sourcing of H2 (via the source of electricity that powers
electrolysis) is also the most critical factor in the overall environmental impact of the production
of this type of MeOH, giving it a wider range of estimates for its CO2 emissions than those of other
renewable MeOH routes. The first successful commercial plant was established in Iceland in 2011,
where geothermal plants can supply ample CO2 and electricity for H2 electrolysis, and the largest
operating plant at 100 000 t/yr MeOH was commissioned by the same company in October 2022
at a coke production facility in Anyang, China. However, most, if not all, of the publicly available
economic and environmental data on this and similar processes is currently in the form of modeling
studies.17

 As mentioned above, RCC approach, where CO2 capture and conversion processes are
integrated to avoid the costly CO2 desorption and capture media regeneration steps, can help
minimize the costs, energy usage, and water consumption (WC) requirements of CCU.5,101 To
assess the economic and environmental viability of the RCC approach for MeOH production, it is
important to develop a process model that can extrapolate from laboratory-scale results to
commercial-scale production for the RCC approach and compare with MeOH production from
conventional CO2 hydrogenation approach. The goal of this study is thus to establish a framework
for both a techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) that will compare the
two routes of CO2 hydrogenation (the baseline method) and RCC for producing MeOH from CO2
and renewable hydrogen. This will help establish whether the RCC method is worth further
development towards a commercial CCU process.

 73

3.2 Methods
 A schematic comparison of the baseline CO2 hydrogenation and RCC approaches is shown
in Figure 3.1. This figure shows how both approaches create two product streams, each with its
own system boundary that can be used for both a TEA and an LCA. In both cases, the system
boundary for the production of MeOH is drawn within the flow of flue gas out of the fossil fuel
power plant. This breaks the TEA and LCA into two parts, one for the production of electricity
and the other for the production of MeOH.

 Figure 3.1. Comparison of baseline CO2 hydrogenation and RCC approaches, with system
 boundaries for TEA/LCA of electricity and MeOH production.

 74

 The TEA/LCA process model developed in this study will compare two CO2/H2-to-MeOH
processes:

 1. The baseline CO2 hydrogenation process, representing the state of the art for MeOH
 CCU.

 2. The RCC process using reference DFM, i.e. CZA, performance.63

 Both of these processes will consist of three major steps:

 1. Production of CO2, which will be from the same source for both processes but have
 different purity requirements for each.

 2. Production of H2, which will be identical in source and purity for both processes but
 will be required in different amounts per unit MeOH for each.

 3. Production of MeOH from CO2 and H2, which will be analyzed using literature models
 for the baseline process, and the NREL-developed ASPEN plus model for the RCC
 process.

 For the baseline process, CO2 must be captured, separated, purified, and compressed
through a relatively expensive purification-desorption step prior to feeding to hydrogenation
reactor. In contrast, for the RCC process, dilute CO2 that has not been purified can be used directly.
To keep the comparison as “apples-to-apples” as possible, both processes were modeled using the
same CO2 source: a natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) power plant. Natural gas has continually
grown its share of the world electricity market in recent decades even as renewables have risen
and other fossil fuels have declined,102 making it one of the most important targets for CCU
application development.

 Given that both processes aim to produce a product with low net carbon intensity (CI), the
hydrogen used as a feedstock must be produced with as low a CI as possible. However, producing
renewable hydrogen while maintaining low production costs has proven to be a significant
technological challenge. A very promising route, with many pathways to future cost reductions
through research and development, is proton exchange membrane (PEM) water electrolysis.103
While the cost of PEM-based hydrogen can be driven down by cheaper electricity, and renewable
electricity costs per kWh have begun to eclipse fossil fuel electricity costs, the low-capacity factors
of renewable plants make it challenging for these plants to power hydrogen electrolysis for a low
resultant price of hydrogen.104 One potential solution to this problem is to power electrolysis with
a hybrid renewable plant, specifically a hybrid wind/solar plant that is able to take advantage of
wind/solar resource complementarity to provide a more reliably constant power supply.105 While
the specific site for such a hybrid will depend on many things, such as land availability, this study
specifically focuses on building MeOH RCC capability onto existing NGCC plants. As such, we
focused on sites within 100 km of existing large (>200 MW) NGCC plants, so that costs of
electricity transmission and/or hydrogen transport from the generation site to the MeOH
production site (on-site at the NGCC plant) could be considered negligible.

 In converting CO2 and H2 to MeOH, three literature TEA studies were available for the
baseline process.106-108 Average values from these studies were used as inputs to the process model.

 75

For the baseline process LCA, inputs for CI were taken from Adnan and Kibria,109 while water
consumption (WC) inputs were taken from Nyari et al.108 For the RCC process, an ASPEN Plus
reactor model was developed by using NREL experimental data as inputs. This ASPEN model was
described in detail below, and provides production cost, CI, and WC inputs to the overall process
model. The main metric of the LCA is the CI of the overall process, calculated by summing the CI
of the subprocesses, while the main metric of the TEA is the Levelized Cost of Methanol (LCOM),
expressed in 2020 dollars per kilogram of MeOH produced.

 To determine the sensitivity of the RCC technology’s LCOM and CI to changes in the
modeled scenario, three types of parameter sweeps were conducted:

 1. Reactor performance, i.e., the ability of the RCC process to efficiently convert CO2 and
 H2 to MeOH without leftover reactants and secondary products requiring separation.

 2. Start-up year, i.e., the year in which pricing assumptions are taken for model inputs in
 which prices vary with time. This allows for projections of reduced costs of renewable
 hydrogen in future years.

 3. Location, i.e., the specific NGCC plant at which the MeOH reactor will be built and
 near which the hybrid wind/solar/H2 plant will be constructed.

 All of these parameter sweeps begin from a pair of starting test cases, which are based on
preliminary RCC performance of a CZA DFM and real-world results in the literature, not a
projected or modeled scenario. The parameters and sources for these starting test cases are given
in the following section.

3.2.1 Python TEA/LCA Model
 The outfitting of NGCC plants with carbon capture systems has been extensively studied
by the National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL), including calculations of the breakeven
sales price of CO2 (or tax on CO2 emissions) necessary to support carbon capture installations,110
which will be considered as the CO2 production cost for the baseline process. NETL also carried
out a full LCA of a baseline plant with and without carbon capture that was used as input data to
the process model in this study.111 In our process model, the equivalent CO2 emissions (CO2e) of
the NETL plant without carbon capture are attributed to electricity, and the additional CO2e
emissions of the plant with carbon capture (the difference between the two plants) are attributed
to the purified CO2 product. It should be noted that the CO2e emissions calculated by the LCA (i.e.,
the carbon intensity of CO2 capture) and the purified CO2 sent to MeOH production are two
separate, non-overlapping entities since this purified CO2 will be consumed later in the process
and not emitted to the atmosphere.

 For the baseline process, CO2 is captured and purified from the NGCC flue gas. For the
RCC process, CO2 does not need to be purified and can be taken directly from the NGCC flue gas.
Thus, in the process model, the production cost, CI and WC of the CO2 going into the MeOH
process were set to zero since these costs could be directly attributed to electricity generation
instead (see Figure 3.1).

 76

 NREL has developed a tool to design an integrated, grid-connected hybrid wind / solar /
PEM plant that is optimized to reduce hydrogen production costs at a given site, called the Hybrids
Optimization and Performance Platform (HOPP).112 HOPP-modeled hybrids are used to determine
the production cost and CI of H2 needed for both MeOH processes. As inputs to HOPP, the cost
of the hybrid plant was determined from NREL’s Annual Technology Baseline113 while the cost
of buying and selling grid electricity was modeled from NREL’s Cambium model,114 the US
Energy Information,115 and Lawrence Berkeley National Lab (LBNL) power purchase agreement
(PPA) data.116,117 CI and WC of the overall electricity mix were determined from the NETL Grid
Mix Explorer118 for the wind and solar production and from NREL’s Cambium model for
electricity purchased from/sold to the grid.114 A scalable CI of the PEM electrolyzer stack was
taken from a study by Zhao et al.,119 while the WC required by the electrolyzer, as well as its
production cost, was determined from NREL’s own Hydrogen Analysis (H2A) case studies.120

 The levelized cost of methanol (LCOM) represents the average amount of revenue required
over the plant’s operational life to meet all capital and operations costs and does not match the
final selling price of MeOH required to generate a profit margin. LCOM, along with other levelized
costs calculated in parts of the model (such as levelized cost of electricity or LCOE), use the simple
fixed-charge rate (FCR) method used by NREL’s System Advisor Model (SAM),121 the financial
model used in HOPP.

 LC = (FCR*TCC + FOC) / AP + VOC (1)

 The terms in eq 1 are

 o LC: Levelized cost [$/MWh, for energy OR $/kg, for materials]

 o TCC: Total capital cost [$]

 o FOC: Fixed operating cost [$/yr]

 o AP: Annual production [MWh/year, for energy OR mt/ year, for materials]

 o VOC: Variable operating cost [$/MWh, for energy OR $/mt, for materials]

3.2.2 RCC conceptual process design
 The conceptual process models for the RCC process were developed in Aspen Plus. The
model areas include CO2 adsorption, hydrogen compression, and hydrogenation reaction as well
as MeOH separation, steam generation, and utilities. Additionally, the process incorporates
renewable H2 and utilizes RCC experimental performance data over a CZA catalytic material. The
RCC annual plant capacity was set at 115 104 tonnes. The flue gas flow from a natural gas power
plant was assumed to contain 10 wt % CO2 and was adjusted and fed to the process. The flue gas
CO2 feed to the RCC reactor was at 4110 million metric tonnes per year (MMt/year) for the case
without partial recycling of product stream. For the case with the reactor effluent recycling, the
flue gas could be reduced to 1050 MMt/y to produce the same amount of MeOH product (due to
high overall CO2-to-MeOH conversion efficiency). The CO2 adsorption efficiency was assumed
to be 20%; the CO2 adsorption capacity (66 μmol/gcatalyst) and single-pass MeOH productivity
(15.77 μmol/gcatalyst) used in the model were obtained from experimental RCC performance of

 77

CZA material.63 For the case with recycle, the resulting overall MeOH productivity was calculated
to be 68.13 μmol/gcatalyst. The net hydrogen consumption was determined to be 1.00 kg H2/kg
MeOH without recycle and 0.33 kg H2/kg MeOH with recycle.

very similar to the CI of wind energy alone. A larger wind/solar hybrid would have displaced much
more grid electricity (potentially even making the entire plant carbon-negative on the whole), but
this would be too expensive to consider. Moving out to the year 2030, solar costs are projected to
become closer to wind. This moves the optimal wind/solar ratio away from the optimization
bounds. Moving further out to the year 2040, it begins to become economical to “overbuild” the
hybrid plant as wind/solar prices become competitive with grid electricity. When this line is
crossed, the wind/solar hybrid starts becoming a substantial net exporter of electricity and a

closer together starting in 2040 in Figure 3.4. There are three caveats to this analysis (as listed
below). However, these are issues that apply to broader decarbonization efforts, in general, and
are outside the scope of this study.

3.3.4 Location sweep
 The same optimization of wind/solar plants was performed at many locations near existing
NGCC plants to produce a minimized LCOM for each location, as shown in Figure 3.6a. This
illustrates how the “wind corridor” locations from Texas up to Iowa are currently where this MeOH

based H2 production. However, prices are not prohibitively more expensive in other locations such
as the southeast and mountain west. The main reason for the price gap in these locations is that the
ideal hybrid plant design features more solar than wind, and current HOPP modeling with NREL
Annual Technology Baseline (ATB) prices113 put a slightly higher price on utility-scale solar
photovoltaic (PV) electricity than land-based wind electricity. Meanwhile, offshore wind is still a
developing technology and shows an approximately $0.30 price gap with nearby onshore MeOH
production sites but has fewer space constraints than land-based wind.

 83

 The areas where RCC MeOH production is most limited are where grid electricity prices
are the highest, particularly in Massachusetts and California. Here, a pure wind/solar hybrid may
not be the best solution, and MeOH production costs could be reduced by employing energy
storage to reduce the dependence on the grid. This could take the form of a battery energy storage
system to store excess wind and solar generation and discharge it to the electrolyzer when needed.
Alternatively, a hydrogen storage system could store excess generation in the form of hydrogen
and be combined with a fuel cell to send electricity back to the electrolyzer when it is needed. But
again, this issue of grid energy storage is a broad decarbonization problem that is outside the scope
of this study.

 When hybrid plant design is optimized for minimal LCOM, it does not minimize CI, as
Figure 3.6b shows. The lowest-LCOM plants in the “wind corridor” tend to have CI in the middle
of the range of all of the plants studied in the location sweep. This is due to the higher CO2e
emissions of wind electricity (29.5 kgCO2e/MWh) relative to solar PV electricity (22.4
kgCO2e/MWh) in this model, taken from the NETL Grid Mix Explorer.118 For this reason, lower
CIs are found in sunnier areas, such as Florida and the Southwest where hybrid plant output is
optimized by a higher proportion of solar PV to wind. The CO2e emissions of these renewable
generators pale in comparison to the grid mix of coal-heavy areas such as Kentucky, where the
average CI of grid electricity is currently 944.6 kgCO2e/MWh according to NREL’s Cambium
model.114 In these areas, the CI of MeOH is highly dependent on whether the optimal hybrid plant
wind/solar plant design is a net buyer or seller of grid electricity. If there are low wind/solar
resources and the wind/solar plant must buy more grid electricity than it sells to keep the H2
electrolyzer running at a high capacity, this will have a detrimental impact on the overall CI of the
MeOH.

3.4 Comparison of RCC to MeOH pathways
 This project has explored two simultaneous paths of DFM development. In the first (see
section 1), the CZA catalyst was modified via deposition of alkali/alkaline earth metal oxides to
facilitate both enhanced CO₂ capture performance and MeOH selectivity during reactive
desorption.63 Modification of CZA with group I metal oxide, specifically K2O forming K/CZA
DFM, resulted in the greatest promotional effect with the highest MeOH selectivity (46%) and

 85

4.5x increase in MeOH productivity as compared to unmodified CZA. In the second path (see
section 2), similar work was performed but with a metallic-phase free zinc-alumina mixed oxides
being modified with K, in order to target high selectivity to carbon monoxide (CO) as the product
of RCC.122 The K modification of equimolar Zn:Al mixed oxides resulted in the best performing
DFM (referred to herein as K/ZA) by balancing surface area for CO2 capture and active sites for
hydrogenation reaction, with 97.6% selectivity to CO and 5x increase in CO productivity as
compared to unmodified material. It was hypothesized that this CO could then be combined with
excess H₂ from hydrogenation as a syngas feed to a conventional catalytic MeOH synthesis unit
operation. However, to determine feasibility of these approaches for e-MeOH production,
comprehensive TEA and LCA study is needed to compare the pros and cons of each pathway with
conventional MeOH production methods. Therefore, in this report, we applied the previously-
developed TEA/LCA framework (section 3.2) to the two pathways using the best performing DFM
for each approach – (1) “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” featuring a K-modified CZA DFM (“K/CZA”),
and (2) “Indirect RCC-to-CO” featuring a K-modified ZA DFM (“K/ZA”) for syngas production
and coupling with conventional MeOH synthesis from syngas – to determine which is optimal for
e-MeOH production.

3.4.1 ASPEN process modelling for RCC to MeOH pathways
 In the ASPEN portion of this framework, the results from laboratory-scale experiments are
used to model commercial-scale reactors, along with their associated energy and feedstock
requirements. The material and energy balance from the process simulation is essential for
equipment sizing and costing, as it helps determine the capital costs and estimate operating costs.
Both capital and operating costs are key inputs for TEA. Additionally, the material and energy
balance provide inventory for LCA, which is conducted to evaluate environmental impacts. A
process model for converting CO₂ and H₂ into MeOH was developed for both RCC pathways. The
model for the “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” model is shown in Figure 3.7a, while the model for the
RCC-to-CO followed by MeOH synthesis from syngas, i.e. “Indirect RCC-to-CO”, is illustrated
in Figure 3.7b. The RCC performance of K/CZA and K/ZA reported in our group publications
were used as inputs for the Aspen modelling to compare the RCC-to-MeOH63 and RCC-to-CO122
processes, respectively.

 The process design for Direct RCC-to-MeOH (Figure 3.7a) is similar to that described in
section 3.2.2, where the operation of an RCC MeOH production system consists of a two-step
process utilizing multiple temperature and pressure swing reactors (TPSRs) paired together
containing the K/CZA DFM. In the first step, flue gas from the natural gas fired power plant is
introduced into the first TPSR (R1) reactor at low pressure, where carbon dioxide (CO2) is
adsorbed onto a DFM bed. In the second step, when the DFM bed is saturated with captured CO2,
the reactor is switched to operate in reactive desorption mode (as demonstrated in R2), where the
reactor undergoes pressurization with hydrogen at 30 bar. During this phase, the temperature in
R2 is increased to 250 °C, which facilitates hydrogenation reaction of adsorbed CO2 to form
products (CO and MeOH) and regenerates the DFM for the next capture cycle. The DFM
performance, including CO2 uptake, conversion of captured CO2 and MeOH product selectivity,
was modeled using the experimental data summarized in Table 3.4. Unconverted CO2 and co-
product CO are recycled back to the RCC reactor, allowing for additional MeOH production.
Excess hydrogen was recovered using a pressure swing adsorption (PSA) unit to minimize the
need for supplemental hydrogen. Purge gas from the PSA, along with off-gas from MeOH

 86

separation, is sent to a boiler for steam generation for process heating. Excess saturated steam is
exported for co-product credit.

 Even though having similar operation principle, the process design for “Indirect RCC-to-
CO” (Figure 3.7b) differs from “Direct RCC-to-MeOH”. In the former case, reactive desorption
takes place at a lower pressure of 1.01 bar and higher temperature of 400 °C, while the latter
process operates at a pressure of 30 bar and 250 °C. Additionally, the primary product of the RCC-
to-CO process is CO with the selectivity of 97.6%, whereas the RCC-to-MeOH process yields a
mixture of MeOH and CO (Table 3.4). The RCC effluent, which contains CO and H2, is
compressed to 50 bar for MeOH synthesis. This process utilizes a commercial CZA catalyst in a
fixed bed reactor.123 One major concern is the heat released during the reaction, as even small
temperature increases of just a few degrees can irreversibly damage the catalyst. To manage this,
heat must be removed from the reactors since the synthesis reaction is exothermic. Temperature
control is achieved by generating steam on the shell side of the reactor. The unconverted products
and reactant from MeOH synthesis (CO2 and CO) were recycled to MeOH synthesis reactor,
instead of RCC reactor as in “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” process, whereas H2 recovery and recycle
were similar between 2 processes.

cooling load for the “Indirect RCC-to-CO” process, due to the re-compression of the RCC effluent
for the MeOH reactor, which produces excess heat, and an overall increase in evaporative water
loss from the cooling tower. However, the “Indirect RCC-to-CO” system has two key advantages
over the “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” system: first, “Indirect RCC-to-CO” requires less than half the
amount of DFM and second, it has higher H2 efficiency as compared to “Direct RCC-to-MeOH”,
as indicated by lower H₂ per unit of MeOH produced (ca. 10% less). The higher CO2 capture
capacity on K/ZA (331 mmolCO2/gDFM) than K/CZA (135 mmolCO2/gDFM) mainly accounted for
the lower DFM demand for the “Indirect RCC-to-CO” process. In the Aspen model, the amount
of hydrogen required during reactive desorption step remains constant, as it is determined by a
fixed input H2/CO2 ratio. However, the productivity and selectivity of the process can affect the
demand for hydrogen in MeOH synthesis, while the input H2/CO2 ratio remains unchanged.
Specifically, H2 efficiency of the process reduces with the formation of undesired product (i.e.
CH4) as it cannot be converted to MeOH in the MeOH synthesis reactor or during product
recycling. The “Indirect RCC-to-CO” process has lower selectivity to CH4 (2.4%) as compared to
“Direct RCC-to-MeOH” (4.4%) and therefore has higher H2 efficiency.

3.4.2 TEA and LCA comparison
 The results from ASPEN modeling are then used in H2INTEGRATE (abbreviated as H₂I)
to determine a full “cradle-to-gate” model of all of the inputs required for the complete e-MeOH
production process, including sourcing CO₂ from industrial flue gas and hybrid wind/solar/grid
electricity for powering H₂ electrolysis. Figure 3.8 shows the full framework with H₂I receiving
“performance” inputs (e.g. mass balances) and “cost” inputs (e.g. capital and operating costs) from
ASPEN to model the RCC system. H₂I then calculates a full system design, including a flue gas
source (i.e. flue gas from natural gas fired power plant) and an electrolyzer, which will provide
CO₂ and H₂ at the rates necessary for a specified production rate of MeOH (ca. 115k tonne/yr). H₂I
can also modify H₂ costs and emissions to simulate MeOH production costs and emissions with
more conventional H₂ sources. The total net emissions of CO₂-equivalent gases (abbreviated as
CO₂e) and consumption of water are used to calculate carbon intensity (CI) and water consumption
(WC), respectively. Meanwhile, the full capital and operating costs of the system are sent to a
financial model that calculates a levelized cost of MeOH (LCOM) over the plant lifetime. This
includes costs for modifying the base CZA and ZA catalysts through incipient wetness
impregnation (IWI), which were estimated using the CatCost tool developed by NREL124 and
shown in Table 3.6.

conversion technology ($0.72/kg). With the conventional precursors (Figure 3.9b), hydrogen
costs (with a hydrogen price of $1.50/kg H₂) are much lower for the three processes that utilize
hydrogen, but the LCOM is still significantly higher than the baseline SMR process (from 2.2 –
2.6 times higher), which uses only natural gas.

 In contrast to the TEA, the LCA shows improved CI for both RCC processes as compared to
baseline CO2 hydrogenation process when using renewable hydrogen (Figure 3.10a). For all three
processes, the hydrogen production formed the majority of the carbon emissions (accounting for
59 – 90% of total CI). Even though renewable electricity is used for hydrogen production from
water electrolysis, the hydrogen quantities needed are such that the CO2 emissions become
significant in terms of kg-CO₂e/kg-H₂, even though they are relatively low in terms of kg-
CO₂e/kWh for the electricity sent to electrolysis. Despite having better hydrogen efficiency, the
“Indirect RCC-to-CO” pathway has higher CI (0.51) than the “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” pathway
(0.45) due to the additional electricity requirement for syngas compression previously discussed
in the TEA. The baseline CO2 hydrogenation approach has the highest CI (0.54) regardless of its
lowest hydrogen usage because of the intermediate CO2 desorption, purification and compression
steps prior to CO2 utilization creating additional burden of indirect CO2 emissions (CO2 capture
CI of 0.15 kg-CO2e/kg-MeOH in Figure 4a). When conventional hydrogen source is used, the CI
for the MeOH production from CO2, either via RCC processes or separated CO2 capture and
conversion (i.e. baseline CO2 hydrogenation) increased significantly (1.61 – 1.82) and much higher
than the conventional MeOH production technology (i.e. baseline SMR, CI of 1.13). This is
expected, because MeOH production from CO2 hydrogenation requires a lot more hydrogen than
from syngas (CO2 hydrogenation to MeOH consumes 3 moles of H2, while MeOH synthesis from
syngas requires 2 moles of H2), resulting in substantially higher CI from conventional hydrogen
generation. And the hydrogen efficiency becomes the most important factor determining CI
intensity of the processes, i.e. the CI intensity follows order of the H2 consumption of the process:
baseline CO2 hydrogenation < “Indirect RCC-to-CO” < “Direct RCC-to-MeOH”.

 Turning to the water consumption (WC) portion of the LCA, the “Direct RCC-to-MeOH”
process clearly had the lowest WC of any process using renewable hydrogen as shown in Figure
3.11a at 6.1 kgH2O/kgMeOH. Similar to CI, the WC advantage of “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” process
resulted from the avoidance of the intermediate CO2 desorption, purification and compression
steps prior to CO2 utilization. In the baseline CO2 hydrogenation approach, CO2 is captured by an
amine solvent, followed by CO2 desorption and solvent regeneration using steam which requires
large WC. Additionally, such a CO₂ capture system greatly increases the need for cooling water
through the plant’s cooling tower, increasing evaporative losses and thus net WC.110 These large
WC during CO2 adsorption-desorption step is indicated as the brown bars in Figure 3.11 (9.2
kgH2O/kgMeOH). Even though the “Indirect RCC-to-CO” process can avoid the intermediate CO2
desorption step having large WC, the need for cooling water to re-compress the RCC effluent for
MeOH synthesis contributed to much higher WC (MeOH reactor bars in Figure 3.11a, 11.5
kgH2O/kgMeOH). Using conventional hydrogen instead of renewable hydrogen does not have a large
impact on the overall WC, however, as seen in Figure 3.11b, the baseline SMR technology has
much lower WC than any other pathway.

3.4.3 Relative costs and sensitivity scenarios
 Based on all the information presented in the previous sections, it is essential to compile
the most important factors into a single diagram to provide a comprehensive comparison of the
MeOH production from the “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” and “Indirect RCC-to-CO” processes. This
is done in Figure 3.12, which plots five of the most important metrics for both processes on a radar
or “spider plot”, where the closer data points to the edge of the pentagon show greater advantage
of the process. The “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” process demonstrates considerable advantages
regarding LCOM and WC, along with a minor advantage in CI. However, the “Indirect RCC-to-
CO” process also has advantages, as it requires significantly less hydrogen and considerably less
DFM to produce the same amount of MeOH as the “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” process. The reason
these advantages do not lead to benefits for the “Indirect RCC-to-CO” process in the overall
TEA/LCA is attributed to the ambient pressure operation for the reactive desorption, which results
in substantial costs and cooling losses from re-compression of the resulting syngas from the RCC
unit for subsequent MeOH synthesis. This analysis provides guidance for future study which is to
understand the effect of intermediate reactive desorption pressures, such as 10-20 bar, on CO per-
cycle yield and selectivity and the resulting effect on the TEA/LCA results.

 Another consideration for the RCC-to-CO pathway is that MeOH is not the only possible
product of this pathway. The syngas resulting from RCC could be sold as fuel or chemical
intermediate itself without being further upgraded to MeOH. Since the original purpose of this
study was to explore pathways to produce MeOH, the TEA/LCA did not consider costs or
emissions of standalone syngas production, but this would be an interesting topic for future work.

Figure 3.12. Radar plot showing relative advantages of “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” and “Indirect RCC-
 to-CO” pathways. For all five axes, lower values are preferred, so they are plotted further out on
the axis. “Direct RCC-to-MeOH” pathway has lower LCOM and WC, whereas “Indirect RCC-to-CO”
 has lower H₂:MeOH ratio and catalyst mass requirements, while their CI is about the same.

 Regardless of the pathway, current limitations on renewable hydrogen production will limit
the economic competitiveness of e-MeOH. It is worth, however, considering future scenarios in
which renewable hydrogen technology develops to reduce the cost of hydrogen production and/or
natural gas becomes more expensive. Figure 3.13 below shows two scenarios, both of which
involve significant technological development to reduce renewable hydrogen production costs to
$1.50/kg. In both cases, the oxygen that is co-produced with hydrogen during water electrolysis is
sold as a co-product for a credit of $0.05/kg. In Figure 3.13a, natural gas is kept at its current cost
of $4.00/MMBtu, in which case the e-MeOH pathways are all much more expensive than SMR.
However, as shown in Figure 3.13b, if natural gas costs reach $10.00/MMBtu (as they have at
peaks in the past), then the RCC-to-MeOH pathway would have a lower cost than both SMR and
CO2 hydrogenation. Although this analysis only represents a projected future scenario, it
demonstrates the pathway by which RCC could produce MeOH at a lower cost than all baseline
technologies.

 SMR and CO₂ hydrogenation processes. (a) With a future renewable H₂ cost of $1.50/kg, “Direct

3.5 Evaluating DFM development for direct RCC-to-MeOH pathway
 with TEA/LCA
 To evaluate effects of Alk identity to RCC process economics, LCOM were compared as
shown in Figure 3.14. In accordance with the results in previous section (3.3.1), the LCOM of
unmodified CZA was significantly more than CO2 hydrogenation. Since the Ca/CZA only
marginally improved the experimental results by increasing CO2 uptake, it is unsurprising that it
only marginally reduces the LCOM. On the other hand, the Na and K/CZA resulted in a drastic
improvement in the LCOM. This is achieved by co-minimizing the cost of hydrogen (green bars)
and cost of DFM (blue bars) as a result of improved RCC performance from the experimental
results by both increasing CO2 uptake and selectivity to MeOH. The LCOM for both K/CZA and
Na/CZA are similar ($0.78/kg-MeOH) and still greater than the baseline technology of CO2
hydrogenation ($0.72/kg-MeOH) but is much more promising than the other unmodified catalysts,
and the following results will show how much more can be achieved through optimization of the
experimental parameters. Finally, all of the MeOH production from renewable H2 have three times
the production cost of conventional MeOH production from SMR, but SMR MeOH is highly
dependent on the prices and availability of natural gas, and has drastically higher CO2 emissions,
as will be seen in the LCA further on.

 Figure 3.14. TEA comparison from direct RCC-to-MeOH using different Alk/CZA DFMs.

 The two most important cost drivers of RCC can be seen clearly in Figure 3.11: The biggest
 is hydrogen cost, since renewable hydrogen is currently very expensive to produce. Even in this
 study, which selected a location with cheap renewable electricity (Texas) to model the TEA,
 hydrogen is expected to cost about $3/kg to produce, with the exact cost depending on the scale.
 All MeOH plants were modeled at the same output rate, and depending on the experimental results
 (scaled up in ASPEN), some processes would require a higher H2:MeOH mass ratio than others.
 Reducing this ratio is key to reducing the overall cost and can be achieved by improving the
 catalysts’ MeOH selectivity. With higher selectivity, fewer recycle steps are needed to achieve the
 required end product purity, and less hydrogen is consumed in the ASPEN model. The second
 biggest cost component for RCC is catalyst, which is dependent on CO2 uptake. The stronger the
 CO2 uptake, the less catalyst will be required to produce the same amount of MeOH. It should be
 noted that while hydrogen cost is a similarly large cost for both CO2 hydrogenation and RCC, the
 catalyst cost is much higher for RCC with the current results, while CO2 hydrogenation has much
 higher capex costs and must account for the cost of CO2 capture and purification, which is not
 needed for RCC. If RCC can achieve a lower H2:MeOH ratio than CO2 hydrogen and drive its
 catalyst costs lower than the combined capex and CO2 capture costs of CO2 hydrogenation, then
 RCC will have a cost advantage over the state-of-the-art CO2 hydrogenation approach.

 To probe further possible improvements in the TEA with Na/CZA (which show better

 Although case 2b did not have the lowest of either of these values, it was the case that best managed
 to get both of these values relatively low simultaneously. Also, looking at the DOE results (Figure

 The CI of conventional MeOH production (from SMR) reported in the literature varies
widely, depending on the assessment method. More optimistic assessments such as that of Kajaste
et al. 127 give a range of 0.462 kgCO2e/kgMeOH (from natural gas sourced in Norway) to 0.881
kgCO2e/kgMeOH (from natural gas source in Algeria). However, our work is based off the National
Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) life cycle inventory data.126 This data is used in the
OpenLCA tool (openlca.org) to calculate CI of MeOH from the average U.S. natural gas mix,
which provides the same value as this study - 1.13 kgCO2e/kgMeOH. Assessments for CO2
hydrogenation vary even more widely, with the Kajaste et al. study127 giving a range from -0.752
to 3.181 kgCO2e/kg-MeOH. Almost the entirety of this variability depends on the source of the
electricity used to power electrolysis of hydrogen. However, our study focuses on the difference
between CO2 hydrogenation and RCC methanol, and since the DoE optimization has managed to
reduce the H2:MeOH ratio of RCC (0.202) very close to that of CO2 hydrogenation (0.195), then
the emissions difference in the two processes will be minimal. The key difference is the lack of a
need for a separate CO2 capture system in the RCC process. To generate a pure CO2 stream from
NGCC flue gas will require an amine-based system such as the Cansolv system used in NETL
modeling of NGCC plants with CO2 capture. These systems, while vastly reducing the direct CO2
emissions attributable to the NGCC plant’s electricity output, will still generate their own indirect
CO2e emissions (as shown in brown bar for the baseline CO2 hydrogenation case) due to their use
of consumable materials, new construction, and energy consumption.

 and b) Water consumption.

 Also significant in amine-based CO2 capture is its cooling requirements, which induces high
net water consumption in the plant to account for evaporative loss. This is reflected the brown bar
in Figure 3.15b. RCC has no such cooling requirement, although both CO2 hydrogenation and
RCC require substantial amounts of fresh water to produce hydrogen via electrolysis. The
hydrogen production component of their water consumption (green bars) is by itself greater than
the water consumption of SMR MeOH as a whole. However, the water consumption of RCC is
only 38% greater than SMR, whereas the water consumption of CO2 hydrogenation is a much
more troubling 390% greater than SMR. Taking both metric shown in Figure 3.15 into account, it
appears that RCC is the MeOH production technology that is least impactful on the environment.

 However, there is still future work to be done to improve catalyst performance before RCC
will be economically competitive with SMR – assuming no regulation of the environmental
impacts. To anticipate the performance parameters that would need to be achieved for this to occur,
we developed empirical correlations between the DoE and the methanol cost, shown in Figure
3.16. First, Figure 3.16a shows correlation between the DoE test space parameters and the LCOM.
Although the inputs of this correlation covered the entire 3-dimensional space of the DoE
(hydrogenation pressure, hydrogenation temperature, and catalyst loading), a 2-dimensional slice
at a constant 5% catalyst loading is shown. This shows that cost is negatively associated with
hydrogenation temperature at 10 bar hydrogenation pressure, but at 30 bar pressure, the cost is
relatively flat with respect to temperature. This is due to the trade-off between MeOH selectivity
and CO2 uptake that occurs at this pressure – higher temperatures will result in higher CO2 uptake
but lower MeOH selectivity, with the reverse effect at lower temperatures. This trend can be seen
in Figure 3.16b, which also shows the target frontier for CO2 uptake and MeOH selectivity that
must be achieved for cost parity with CO2 hydrogenation.

 and b) Water consumption.

 On the subject of hydrogen from renewable sources, this is the factor that has the greatest
impact on the location-specific LCOM in the US, shown in Figure 3.17. Renewable electricity is
the least expensive in the “wind corridor” in the central US, leading to the lowest hydrogen costs
and thus the lowest MeOH costs. This TEA analysis uses a greenfield hybrid wind/solar plant
whose wind/solar ratio is optimized for the given location, and grid-connected. As a result, high
grid electricity prices cause high MeOH prices in the California and northeast metro areas. The
locations analyzed here are clustered around existing NGCC plants of at least 500 MW capacity,
whose exhaust streams are large enough to produce RCC MeOH on the order of 100,000 tonnes
per year. A more thorough analysis of electricity and hydrogen pricing and emissions throughout

analysis of other end uses such as steel and ammonia.128
