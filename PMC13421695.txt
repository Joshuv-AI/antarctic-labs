Ferroptosis modulation enhances astrocyte-to-neuron conversion induced by a novel chemical cocktail in the hemorrhagic brain
Authors: Jingyi Wang, Shiling Chen, Jiarui Li, Xia Liu, Jiahui Wang, Liyang Huang, Luwei Nie, Xuan Wu, Yunjie Li, Yangyang Feng, Na Liu, Yingxin Tang, Lingqiang Zhu, Chuan Qin, Gaigai Li, Zhouping Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) leads to significant neuronal loss and glial scar formation, but the regenerative capacity of the adult brain remains limited. Although small-molecule-induced astrocyte-to-neuron (AtN) conversion has shown promise in vitro, in vivo applications—particularly under pathological conditions—are still scarce. We aimed to develop and validate a small-molecule cocktail for inducing astrocyte-to-neuron reprogramming in vivo following ICH. We identified a seven-compound cocktail (DFGKLRV) capable of converting astrocytes into neurons under both physiological and ICH conditions. Using immunostaining, RT‒qPCR, electrophysiology, RNA sequencing, neural circuit tracing, and behavioral assessment, we assessed the identity and functionality of induced neurons. In vivo reprogramming was achieved via continuous intracerebral infusion of the cocktail using osmotic pumps. Lineage tracing with aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member L1 (Aldh1l1)-CreERT2/Rosa-CAG-tdTomato mice confirmed the astrocytic origin of the reprogrammed neurons. Additionally, we monitored ferroptosis dynamics during reprogramming and evaluated the effect of ferroptosis inhibition on conversion efficiency. DFGKLRV successfully reprogrammed astrocytes into functional, electrophysiologically active neurons. This reprogramming was effective both in vitro and in vivo, including in the hemorrhagic brain environment. Pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis significantly improved reprogramming efficiency. Mechanistically, ferroptosis inhibition promoted astrocyte-to-neuron conversion at least in part through suppression of the TGF-β/SMAD3/SOX9 axis, whereas exogenous TGF-β1 treatment or Sox9 overexpression reversed this pro-reprogramming effect. Our findings demonstrate that the DFGKLRV cocktail enables efficient in vivo astrocyte-to-neuron reprogramming following ICH. Moreover, ferroptosis represents a key regulatory mechanism and potential therapeutic target for enhancing chemical reprogramming strategies.

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is among the most devastating neurological disorders, with high mortality and disability rates worldwide.1 Current treatment options remain limited.2 Acute management may reduce hematoma expansion and support rehabilitation, but it cannot replace neurons lost after hemorrhagic injury. Since severe brain injury is associated with neuronal loss, the regeneration of new neurons to replenish lost ones is critical for brain repair. However, the neurogenesis capacity of the adult mammalian brain is largely restricted to a few specialized neurogenic niches, such as the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the dentate gyrus and the subventricular zone (SVZ) of the lateral ventricle.3,4 An emerging approach for neuronal replacement and functional recovery is the in vivo fate conversion of nonneuronal cells residing within the injured brain into induced neurons (iNs).5 In recent decades, several studies have demonstrated glia-to-neuron reprogramming via genetic manipulation or small-molecule treatment under pathological conditions.5–10 Forced expression of a single neural transcription factor, such as NeuroD1, Ascl1, or Sox2, can convert astrocytes into functional neurons in the injured rodent brain, and NeuroD1-based gene therapy has further achieved neuronal regeneration and functional recovery in ischemic stroke and even in nonhuman primates.7,11–15 In parallel, small-molecule cocktails can reprogram human and mouse astrocytes into neurons both in vitro and in vivo, providing proof of concept that resident glial cells can be redirected toward a neuronal fate using chemicals alone.16–18

Compared with ischemic stroke, ICH is associated with higher mortality and poorer functional outcomes among affected patients.19,20 Developing regenerative strategies is particularly urgent. Beyond the primary mechanical injury, the hematoma triggers secondary damage through hemoglobin breakdown, iron deposition, oxidative stress, and sustained neuroinflammation, which together create a hostile microenvironment that exacerbates neuronal loss and constrains spontaneous repair. However, only a few studies have explored neuronal reprogramming in the context of ICH,21 leaving a major unmet clinical need. After ICH, reactive astrocytes constitute the major components of the glial scar surrounding the hemorrhagic site. Importantly, while the glial scar initially serves a protective role by partitioning the lesion, its chronic persistence is accompanied by the deposition of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs) that inhibit axonal regeneration and functional remodeling. Reprogramming these scar-forming astrocytes not only replenishes lost neural populations but also helps remodel these mechanical and chemical barriers to brain repair. Because they share a developmental lineage with neurons, they represent an ideal source for neuronal reprogramming.22,23 Harnessing these locally abundant, otherwise scar-forming cells could therefore provide an endogenous reservoir for neuronal replacement without the need for cell transplantation. Recently, the use of small molecules has emerged as a promising strategy for neural regeneration because of their biological advantages, including precise and reversible regulation, as well as the absence of genetic integration.18 Taken together, the abundance of reactive astrocytes at the injury site and the translational advantages of small-molecule approaches make chemically induced in vivo reprogramming a particularly attractive yet underexplored strategy for ICH, underscoring the value of investigating it systematically.

Our previous work (Feng et al.21) provided initial evidence for in vivo astrocyte-to-neuron conversion after ICH. However, several important issues remain unresolved, including the need for rigorous lineage tracing, a clear definition of neuronal subtype identity, and the microenvironmental factors that influence reprogramming efficiency in the hemorrhagic brain. These issues are especially important because in vivo reprogramming studies have raised concerns about reporter specificity and the possible misinterpretation of pre-existing neurons as newly converted cells.24 In the present study, we addressed these gaps. Compared with the glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-promoter-based viral labeling used in previous studies, the Aldh1l1-CreERT2/Rosa-CAG-tdTomato transgenic mouse line provides more stringent evidence for astrocytic origin and minimizes potential ambiguity related to reporter specificity. Our findings, therefore, extend previous work by establishing a more rigorous framework for in vivo reprogramming after ICH. Since a sufficient number of newly generated neurons is required for therapeutic applications, improving reprogramming efficiency remains a major challenge in the field of regenerative medicine.25 It has been reported that astrocyte-to-neuron conversion is associated with a shift toward oxidative metabolism, often accompanied by increased lipid peroxidation.17 Given that unrestrained lipid peroxidation is a hallmark of ferroptosis,26 its role in neuronal reprogramming and its potential as a target for enhancing conversion efficiency warrant further investigation.

In this study, we used immunofluorescence, transcriptional analysis, electrophysiology, lineage tracing, neural circuit tracing, and behavioral assessment to investigate the role of small molecules in astrocyte-to-neuron conversion in vitro and in vivo. We identified an optimized small-molecule cocktail (DFGKLRV) that synergistically regulates signaling pathways, epigenetics, and metabolism to rapidly convert astrocytes into neurons under both normal and hemorrhagic conditions. Chemically induced neurons (CiNs) exhibit typical neuronal morphologies, express neuron-specific markers, and generate repetitive action potentials (APs). In addition, rigorous lineage tracing confirmed that these CiNs originated from astrocytes. Finally, we investigated the role of ferroptosis during chemical reprogramming and explored whether ferroptosis regulates reprogramming efficiency through the TGF-β/SMAD3/SOX9 signaling axis. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the feasibility of the small-molecule-induced in vivo neuronal reprogramming approach following ICH and suggest that ferroptosis inhibition may improve conversion efficiency. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of this approach in neurological repair.

Results

Small molecules reprogram cultured astrocytes into functional neurons

Previous studies have demonstrated that rodent astrocytes can be chemically reprogrammed into neuron-like cells, as evidenced by morphological transformation, altered expression of cell-specific markers, and upregulation of the expression of multiple neurogenesis-related transcription factors.21 Through a literature review and numerous preliminary experiments, we established a protocol that includes seven small molecules (DFGKLRV), which synergistically regulate signaling pathways, epigenetics, and metabolic shifts, to chemically reprogram astrocytes into neurons in vitro. In this study, cultured astrocytes were passaged at least three times to prevent contamination by neurons or neural progenitors before reprogramming. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that more than 99% of the cultured cells expressed the astrocyte marker GFAP, while fewer than 1% of the cells were positive for the neuronal marker neuronal nuclei antigen (NeuN) and the neural progenitor cell marker sex-determining region Y-box 2 (SOX2) (Supplementary Fig. 1). This confirmed the high purity of the astrocytes used in the experiment, effectively excluding interference from neurons or neural progenitors.

The reprogramming process can be broadly divided into two stages, namely, the induction stage (D0–D4) and the differentiation stage (D4–D10) (Fig. 1b), in accordance with previous studies.18,27–30 Considerable debate exists regarding the optimal basal medium and neurotrophic factor (bFGF) concentration for the induction phase16,18,29,31–34 and whether coculturing with astrocytes is necessary during differentiation (Fig. 1a).18,31,35,36 Through extensive preliminary experiments, morphological transformations and RT-qPCR, we determined that the optimal induction conditions included neurobasal/DMEM/F12 (1:1, v/v) supplemented with 100 ng/mL bFGF (Supplementary Fig. 2). During the differentiation phase, coculturing with astrocytes was not needed (Supplementary Fig. 2c). From a morphological perspective, following small-molecule treatment, cultured astrocytes gradually transformed from polygonal shapes into neuron-like cells with dense nuclei and elongated processes (Fig. 1c). Dynamic changes in the expression of neuron-related and astrocyte-related markers were examined via RT-qPCR and immunofluorescence staining. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the immature neuron marker doublecortin (DCX) was initially upregulated but then downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The neural progenitor cell marker SOX2 was not expressed throughout (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The early neuronal marker β-III tubulin (TUJ1) was detected at an early stage of reprogramming and remained at a relatively high level throughout the process (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 3c). In contrast, the mature neuron markers microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) and NeuN were expressed in the later stages of reprogramming (Fig. 1e, f; Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). On day 10 post-reprogramming, the astrocyte-to-neuron conversion efficiency was quantified as the percentage of TUJ1+, MAP2+, and NeuN+ cells among the total DAPI+ cells (47.15% ± 4.05%, 35.41% ± 3.76%, and 24.48% ± 1.62%, respectively; Fig. 1g). The RT-qPCR results confirmed similar expression patterns. The expression of the neuron-related genes Dcx, tubulin beta 3 class III (Tubb3), Map2, and Rbfox3 was upregulated, with Rbfox3 expression increasing 883-fold at D10 compared with that at D0 (Fig. 1h, Supplementary Fig. 4a–d). The expression of the astrocyte marker GFAP gradually decreased during the reprogramming process (Fig. 1d–f, Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings confirmed that DFGKLRV-mediated astrocyte-to-neuron reprogramming is a gradual and progressive process. Interestingly, during our small-molecule-induced reprogramming, we also observed the activation of key neural transcription factors, including neuronal differentiation 1 (Neurod1), neurogenin 2 (Neurog2), achaete-scute family bHLH transcription factor 1 (Ascl1), and paired box 6 (Pax6) (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Fig. 4e–h). Previous studies suggest that these transcription factors alone are sufficient to induce astrocyte-to-neuron reprogramming.10,11,14,37 To rigorously trace the lineage of the induced neurons, we labeled astrocytes with GFAP-MCS-mCherry-LV. First, we optimized the infection conditions by testing various multiplicity of infection (MOI) values, ultimately reaching an MOI of 50 combined with HitransG P for optimal infection efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 5). On day 10, triple-positive TUJ1+/Synapsin-1+/mCherry+ cells and double-positive MAP2+/mCherry+ cells were observed, suggesting that the converted neurons originated from astrocytes (Fig. 1j, k). One of the key indicators of successful neuronal reprogramming is the acquisition of electrophysiological properties.16,18,27 Using whole-cell patch-clamp, we observed that the converted neurons on day 10 were capable of firing repetitive spontaneous APs and evoked APs (Fig. 1l, m). To further characterize the intrinsic electrophysiological properties of CiNs, we quantified the resting membrane potential (RMP), AP threshold, AP amplitude, and AP half-width. Compared with published data for primary neurons, the AP amplitude and AP half-width of CiNs substantially overlapped, whereas the RMP and AP thresholds were relatively depolarized (Supplementary Fig. 6). Together, these findings indicate that CiNs acquired neuronal-like electrophysiological properties but still exhibited incomplete electrophysiological maturation. Transcriptomics combined with network pharmacology identifies the core genes involved in the DFGKLRV-mediated reprogramming of astrocytes into neurons

Transcriptome sequencing analysis was conducted on CiNs, primary neurons, and astrocytes to examine gene expression changes in CiNs. Compared with those in the astrocyte group, 1309 genes were upregulated and 1040 genes were downregulated in the CiN group on D10, indicating that treatment with DFGKLRV significantly altered gene expression in astrocytes (Fig. 2a). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the upregulated genes were enriched primarily in the terms “synaptic activity,” “axonogenesis,” and “metal ion channel activity” (Fig. 2b), whereas the downregulated genes were enriched mainly in “cell migration,” “cell-substrate adhesion,” “mitotic nuclear division,” and “proteoglycan binding” (Fig. 2c). A heatmap of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was generated, and hierarchical clustering analysis was performed for CiNs, neurons, and astrocytes (Fig. 2d). The results revealed that CiNs clustered closely with primary neurons but were distinctly separated from astrocytes (Fig. 2d). The expression of several key genes was further examined (Fig. 2e). Neuron-associated transcription factor genes, including Neurod1 and Neurog2, were upregulated in CiNs. Additionally, the expression of neuron-related and synapse-related genes such as Map2, the microtubule-associated protein Tau (Mapt), Tubb3, Rbfox3, and synaptosome-associated protein 25 (Snap25) was upregulated in CiNs compared with that in astrocytes, which is consistent with previous RT-qPCR results. Moreover, the expression of neuronal subtype-related genes, such as the GABAergic neuron-related gene glutamate decarboxylase 1 (Gad1), increased. Since the astrocytes utilized in this study were derived from the striatum, a region predominantly populated by GABAergic neurons, it is possible that regional lineage traces were retained during reprogramming. Conversely, the expression of the astrocyte-related gene Aldh1l1 was downregulated in CiNs. The downregulation of the cell cycle-related gene cyclin D1 (Ccnd1) suggested that astrocyte proliferation was inhibited after treatment with DFGKLRV. Astrocytes primarily rely on glycolysis, whereas neurons predominantly utilize oxidative phosphorylation for energy production.39 The expression of the glycolysis-related gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) decreased on day 10 of reprogramming. Notably, Gapdh should not be used as an internal reference for relative quantification of mRNA or protein expression during reprogramming because of its dynamic changes in expression. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify pathway changes associated with reprogramming (Fig. 2f–m). DFGKLRV includes an activator of cAMP (forskolin), an inhibitor of glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) that indirectly activates Wnt (laduviglusib), and inhibitors of HIF-1 (KC7F2) and TGF-β (RepSox). Similarly, RNA-seq analysis revealed upregulation of the cAMP and Wnt pathways and downregulation of the TGF-β and HIF-1 pathways (Fig. 2f, h, i, l). The TGF-β pathway is involved in inhibiting neuronal fate while promoting glial differentiation,40 and inhibition of GSK3 signaling promotes neural induction.41 Unexpectedly, the activity of the Notch signaling pathway was upregulated during reprogramming despite the inclusion of the Notch inhibitor DAPT in the cocktail (Fig. 2g). In addition, pathways such as P53, Janus tyrosine kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT), and adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK) were downregulated (Fig. 2j, k, m), suggesting that their inhibition may promote the conversion of astrocytes into neurons.31,33,42

Network pharmacology is a powerful approach to elucidate the complex interrelationships between drugs and biological or pathological processes.43 In this study, we integrated network pharmacology with RNA-seq analysis to identify core genes involved in DFGKLRV-mediated neuronal reprogramming. First, Venn diagrams were generated to compare genes whose expression was upregulated in neurons relative to that in astrocytes with those whose expression was upregulated in CiNs relative to that in astrocytes, as well as genes whose expression was downregulated in neurons relative to that in astrocytes versus genes whose expression was downregulated in CiNs relative to that in astrocytes (Fig. 2n, o). This analysis revealed DEGs associated with the transformation of astrocytes into neurons following treatment with DFGKLRV (2927 genes). Next, potential targets for neuronal reprogramming were retrieved and intersected with the known targets of the seven small molecules in DFGKLRV (set of 167 overlapping targets) (Fig. 2p). Finally, a Venn diagram was constructed to intersect these DEGs with the network pharmacology-derived targets, resulting in the identification of 37 core genes (Fig. 2q). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed to visualize the core genes (Fig. 2r). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these core genes were involved in the positive regulation of cytosolic calcium ion concentration, cell proliferation, cell migration, neuronal action potential, synaptic structure, and ion channel activity (Fig. 2s). These findings are consistent with the results of the RNA-seq GO enrichment analysis. While these core genes represent key targets of our small-molecule cocktail, further work is needed to properly examine the underlying mechanisms driving neuronal reprogramming.

DFGKLRV reprograms astrocytes into neurons after ICH in vitro

On the basis of previous research,44 astrocytes were treated with hemin to simulate an ICH environment in vitro. Astrocytes were treated with 10 μM hemin for 16 h, resulting in substantial cell death. Following a 24-h recovery period in astrocyte medium, DFGKLRV was used to initiate reprogramming. To evaluate the conversion capacity of our formulation under this hemin-induced stress, we compared DFGKLRV against four established small-molecule combinations: SLCD, DFICBY, BCDLPSTVZ, and our prior VCRFBI formulation. The targets and in vivo applications of these formulations are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Quantitative analysis revealed that while established formulations exhibited limited conversion efficiency under hemin-induced toxicity, the DFGKLRV cocktail resulted in increased reprogramming efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 7). During the reprogramming process, astrocytes gradually acquired a neuron-like morphology, characterized by dense nuclei, elongated cell processes, and multiple branches (Fig. 3a). These changes mirrored those observed in the normal (non-ICH) reprogramming process. Similarly, the expression patterns of cell markers (DCX, TUJ1, SOX2, NeuN, Olig2, and GFAP) and the relative mRNA levels of neuron-related genes (Dcx, Tubb3, Map2, and Rbfox3) and neural transcription factor genes (Neurod1, Neurog2, Ascl1, and Pax6) were comparable to those observed during non-ICH astrocyte reprogramming (Fig. 3b–j, Supplementary Fig. 8). The percentages of TUJ1-positive, NeuN-positive and GFAP-positive cells on day 10 of neuronal transformation were quantified. In contrast, DCX-positive, SOX2-positive, and Olig2-positive cells were rarely detected (Supplementary Fig. 9). Notably, immunostaining revealed that a substantial proportion of the cells were negative for all of these markers. These cells may represent a transient intermediate state during the conversion process. Moreover, we did not detect the expression of the neural progenitor markers Nestin and PAX6 during hemin-induced reprogramming (Supplementary Fig. 10a). In addition, quantitative analyses of Ki67 and PCNA revealed a progressive decline in proliferative capacity during DFGKLRV-mediated conversion (Supplementary Fig. 10b, c). Together, these findings suggest that this cell fate transition is unlikely to proceed through a dedifferentiated, neural progenitor-like intermediate state and instead supports a direct reprogramming process. On the basis of these experimental findings, we hypothesized that small-molecule reprogramming following ICH is facilitated through the activation of endogenous neuronal transcription factors such as NEUROD1, NEUROG2, and ASCL1.16 To confirm that the CiNs originated from astrocytes, the astrocytes were prelabeled with GFAP-mCherry-LV before hemin treatment. The presence of TUJ1+/Synapsin-1+/mCherry+ and MAP2+/mCherry+ cells confirmed that post-ICH reprogrammed neurons originated from astrocytes (Fig. 3k). Furthermore, to characterize the neuronal identities of CiNs, we measured neuronal subtype marker expression on day 10 after reprogramming following ICH (Fig. 3l, Supplementary Fig. 11). Quantification revealed that GAD67+, GABA+ and DARPP32+ cells accounted for 92.69% ± 0.73%, 64.22% ± 3.09% and 64.61% ± 2.04% of the total MAP2+ cells, respectively. In contrast, PV+, VGLUT2+, TH+, and ChAT+ cells were rarely detected (Fig. 3m). Notably, the distribution of these neuronal subtypes was largely consistent with that of endogenous striatal neurons, indicating that chemically induced neurons share regional characteristics with native striatal neurons. In summary, although the reprogramming process after ICH was similar to that observed under normal conditions, differences in the expression levels of neuron-related and transcription factor-related genes suggest that the post-ICH microenvironment may influence reprogramming efficiency. DFGKLRV mediates astrocyte-to-neuron conversion in an ICH mouse model

Reactive gliosis occurs after ICH, resulting in the formation of glial scars with complex functions.22 During the acute phase of ICH, glial scars act as a physical barrier, limiting the infiltration of peripheral immune cells and the release of proinflammatory cytokines. However, in the chronic phase of ICH, glial scars inhibit axonal regeneration through the release of inhibitory molecules and soluble toxins.22 We hypothesized that reprogramming astrocytes into neurons during the chronic phase of ICH or the stabilization phase of the glial scar could provide dual benefits by replenishing lost neurons while reducing glial scar hyperplasia.

First, we analyzed the spatiotemporal dynamics of astroglial scarring around the hematoma after ICH and reported that glial scarring gradually stabilized between the 14th and 28th days post-ICH (Fig. 4a, d). On the basis of these findings, we selected the 21st day post-ICH as the time point at which reprogramming was initiated. Rigorous lineage tracing techniques are necessary to confirm the cellular origin of induced neurons in reprogramming studies.24 In this study, Aldh1l1-CreERT2/Rosa-CAG-tdTomato transgenic mice were used to prelabel astrocytes in the brain. This mouse model was validated to show minimal mislabeling of endogenous neurons, microglia, or oligodendrocytes at 7 days after tamoxifen withdrawal (Supplementary Fig. 12e, f). Furthermore, before the initiation of DFGKLRV-induced conversion, the ICH model had minimal effects on Aldh1l1-driven labeling in endogenous neurons (Supplementary Fig. 13). A schematic diagram of the in vivo reprogramming is shown in Fig. 4b. The transgenic mice received an intraperitoneal injection of tamoxifen once a day for 5 days, and the ICH model was induced 1 week later. On the 21st day post-ICH, Alzet micro-osmotic pumps were used to continuously administer 100 μL of DFGKLRV intracerebrally for 2 weeks, after which the mice were maintained for an additional 10 weeks. Brain samples were collected approximately 105 days post-ICH (Fig. 4b). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed the presence of NeuN+tdTomato+ cells around the ICH lesions in mice treated with DFGKLRV, whereas almost no NeuN+tdTomato+ cells were detected in the vehicle group (Fig. 4c). After DFGKLRV treatment, we detected 24 ± 1 NeuN+tdTomato+ CiNs per field of view (Fig. 4e). The conversion efficiency in a single section reached 15.7% ± 1.8% of all the tdTomato-labeled cells (Fig. 4f). Our reprogramming efficiency appeared higher than that reported by Ma et al.18 In addition, the number of NeuN+ cells in the DFGKLRV group was greater than that in the vehicle group (excluding NeuN+tdTomato+ cells) (Fig. 4g). This may be because the lineage-tracing system cannot completely label all astrocytes, which suggests that the potential of our chemical reprogramming approach might have been underestimated. To assess the dynamics of this conversion process, we analyzed additional brain tissues at 7, 14, and 28 days after the initiation of continuous small-molecule delivery. Immunostaining of lineage-traced tdTomato+ cells for SOX2, PAX6, and DCX revealed DCX+tdTomato+ cells on day 14 (Supplementary Fig. 14), whereas SOX2+tdTomato+ and PAX6+tdTomato+ cells were not detected at appreciable levels at the examined time points (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results support a progressive astrocyte-to-neuron conversion process in vivo. The striatum is primarily composed of GABAergic neurons. Consistently, a substantial proportion of CiNs expressed the striatum-enriched subtype markers DARPP32 and GABA, whereas dopaminergic (TH+), glutamatergic (VGLUT2+), cholinergic (ChAT+) and parvalbumin (PV+) cells were rarely detected (Fig. 4h, i). Quantitative analysis revealed that DARPP32+ and GABA+ cells accounted for 67.12% ± 6.18% and 75.95% ± 5.65% of the total NeuN+tdTomato+ cells, respectively, whereas TH+, VGLUT2+, ChAT+, and PV+ cells accounted for only 0, 0, 3.33, and 2%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 15). These results suggest that in situ CiNs share region-specific subtype characteristics with endogenous neurons. This may be attributed to the heterogeneity of astrocytes in different regions. The CiNs exhibit functional characteristics in vivo

The functionality of in situ CiNs indicates successful reprogramming. A retrograde mono-transsynaptic tracing system based on recombinant rabies virus was used to determine whether CiNs form connections with endogenous neurons in vivo.45 Stereotaxic injection of Cre-dependent flexed AAV vectors for TVA-mCherry and RVG was carried out in the striatum of Aldh1l1-CreERT2 mice, thereby restricting transgene expression to astrocytes. After inducing ICH and initiating small-molecule reprogramming through an osmotic pump, the labeled astrocytes were converted into CiNs. To trace the monosynaptic connections, RV-EGFP was injected at the same coordinates 1 week before perfusion. In this approach, the CiNs were labeled mCherry+/EGFP+, and the monosynaptically connected neurons were labeled EGFP+ only (Fig. 5a). Numerous mCherry⁺/EGFP⁺ and EGFP⁺ cells were detected in the perihemorrhagic regions of the DFGKLRV-treated mice, whereas such labeled cells were rarely observed in the vehicle group (Fig. 5b, c). In line with these findings, quantitative analysis revealed a significantly greater number of positive cells in the DFGKLRV group than in the vehicle group (Fig. 5d). Furthermore, an important goal of reprogramming therapy is to alleviate sensorimotor deficits in mice with ICH. The sensorimotor function of mice with ICH was evaluated throughout the reprogramming period. We performed four behavioral tests at five time points before and after the administration of small molecules: the modified Garcia score test, forelimb placement test, adhesive removal test, and corner test. Behavioral assessment indicated a partial improvement in sensorimotor performance in ICH mice at 8–12 weeks after reprogramming (Fig. 5f–h). Although the improvement in modified Garcia scores did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5e), together, these findings suggest a modest behavioral benefit of DFGKLRV treatment after ICH.

To further evaluate the identity and functional maturation of CiNs in vivo, we performed targeted whole-cell patch-clamp recordings from lineage-traced CiNs identified by the colocalization of hSyn-EGFP and tdTomato signals (Fig. 5i). Mature CiNs displayed repetitive trains of APs in response to depolarizing current injection (Fig. 5j), whereas immature CiNs typically generated only a single AP under the same stimulation protocol (Fig. 5k), indicating the progressive acquisition of neuronal excitability during maturation. Quantitative analysis of intrinsic electrophysiological properties, including the RMP, rheobase, first-spike latency, input resistance (Rin), sag ratio, AP threshold, and AP half-width, further demonstrated the electrophysiological maturation of CiNs in vivo (Fig. 5l). Although the RMP of CiNs remained slightly more depolarized, most other electrophysiological parameters were comparable to those of mature medium spiny neurons (MSNs). These findings support the conclusion that CiNs acquire MSN-like electrophysiological properties.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that in vivo reprogrammed CiNs are functional. These results support the development of small-molecule-based therapeutic strategies aimed at promoting functional repair in patients with ICH.

The inhibition of ferroptosis enhances reprogramming efficiency after ICH

Previous studies have demonstrated that ferroptosis occurs after ICH46 and that subsequent lipid peroxidation may affect reprogramming efficiency. To investigate whether ferroptosis occurs during astrocyte reprogramming, we assessed the expression of ferroptosis-related genes, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 6a). Specifically, the expression of acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member 4 (Acsl4) and prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (Ptgs2) initially tended to increase but then decreased (Fig. 6e, f). The expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (Gpx4) initially decreased but then increased (Fig. 6g). The expression of ferroptosis-related markers tended to increase around day 3 after ICH reprogramming. Interestingly, the ROS levels peaked on day 3 (Fig. 6b, d). TEM analysis of mitochondrial morphology before and after reprogramming revealed that on day 3, the mitochondria exhibited vacuolization, a reduction in or loss of cristae, and rupture of the outer membranes, whereas the cell membrane and nuclear envelope remained relatively intact. At the onset of reprogramming, the mitochondrial morphology appeared normal (Fig. 6c). These results indicate that ferroptosis occurs during post-ICH reprogramming and peaks on day 3. To evaluate the effect of ferroptosis on reprogramming efficiency, hemin-stimulated astrocytes were treated with either the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 2 μM), the ferroptosis inducer erastin (10 μM) or DMSO (control) on day 3 of reprogramming. The efficiency of neuronal reprogramming was assessed on day 10. Compared with those in the control group, the number of MAP2+ and TUJ1+/MAP2+ cells increased in the ferrostatin-1 treatment group, whereas these cell populations decreased in the erastin-treated group (Fig. 6h, i). Statistical analysis revealed that, compared with the control group (MAP2+, 17.52% ± 1.64%; TUJ1+, 28.82% ± 1.64%; TUJ1+MAP2+, 13.36% ± 1.23%), the ferrostatin-1 group exhibited increased efficiency (MAP2+, 29.57% ± 1.95%; TUJ1+, 49.7% ± 3.04%; TUJ1+MAP2+, 26.06% ± 1.52%), whereas the erastin group exhibited a reduction (MAP2+, 5.09% ± 0.38%; TUJ1+, 7.79% ± 1.02%; TUJ1+MAP2+, 2.82% ± 0.80%) (Fig. 6j–l). Since the observed differences in neuronal proportion could be influenced by altered cell survival, we next performed TUNEL staining on day 4 of reprogramming. No significant difference in the proportion of TUNEL-positive cells was observed among the control, Fer-1, and erastin groups (Supplementary Fig. 16), indicating that ferroptosis modulation did not markedly alter overall cell death at this early stage. To further determine whether the pro-reprogramming effect of ferroptosis inhibition was simply attributable to such general cytoprotection over the course of reprogramming, we compared inhibitors targeting ferroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis under DFGKLRV-induced AtN conditions in vitro. Although Fer-1, Z-VAD-FMK, and necrostatin-1 increased the endpoint DAPI⁺ cell numbers, only Fer-1 significantly increased the percentages of TUJ1⁺/DAPI⁺ and MAP2⁺/DAPI⁺ cells (Supplementary Fig. 17). Together, these findings suggest that ferroptosis inhibition enhances neuronal reprogramming beyond its accompanying general improvement in cell survival. A small fraction of MAP2+/GFAP+ cells was detected (Fig. 6l, control group, 0.28% ± 0.11%; ferrostatin-1 group, 0.44% ± 0.18%; erastin group, 2.80% ± 1.37%), which may represent an intermediate state between astrocytes and neurons, indirectly confirming that the reprogrammed neurons originated from astrocytes.36 Additionally, the mRNA levels of Map2 and Rbfox3 were quantified on day 10 of reprogramming after ICH. The results revealed that the administration of ferrostatin-1 increased the expression of these neuron-related genes, whereas treatment with erastin reduced their expression (Fig. 6m). To further examine this, we evaluated the effect of ferroptosis inhibition on reprogramming efficiency in vivo (Fig. 6n). Compared with the control group, the ferrostatin-1 treatment group displayed a markedly increased number of CiNs and a higher conversion efficiency (Fig. 6o, p), confirming that ferroptosis inhibition enhanced chemical reprogramming in vivo. To capture early fate-determining events, we evaluated the activity of the TGF-β/SMAD3/SOX9 cascade on day 4 of reprogramming. Fer-1 markedly reduced the protein levels of TGF-β, p-SMAD3, and SOX9 (Fig. 7e), demonstrating that ferroptosis inhibition suppressed TGF-β/SMAD3 signaling to downregulate SOX9 expression. Next, we conducted rescue experiments using the pathway activator TGF-β1. On day 4, while TGF-β1 alone upregulated the expression of TGF-β, p-SMAD3, and SOX9, its coadministration restored their Fer-1-attenuated expression levels (Fig. 7f). Phenotypically, treatment with TGF-β1 alone impaired baseline reprogramming, and cotreatment reversed the Fer-1-induced benefits, reducing the generation of TUJ1+ and MAP2+ neurons to below baseline levels by day 10 (Fig. 7g, h). Finally, to pinpoint Sox9 as the terminal effector, we performed lentiviral overexpression (LV-Sox9) (Supplementary Fig. 18). On day 4, forced Sox9 expression bypassed Fer-1-mediated suppression without perturbing upstream p-SMAD3 (Fig. 7i). Functionally, sustained Sox9 expression restricted baseline reprogramming and significantly counteracted the neurogenic advantages of Fer-1, reducing TUJ1+ and MAP2+ yields to below control levels by day 10 (Fig. 7j, Supplementary Fig. 19). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ferroptosis inhibition facilitates astrocyte-to-neuron conversion by dismantling the TGF-β/SMAD3/SOX9-dependent glial barrier.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that a cocktail of seven small molecules (DFGKLRV) could induce the conversion of astrocytes into functional neurons under ICH conditions both in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, ferroptosis occurred during the chemical reprogramming process, and treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 increased the efficiency of neuronal reprogramming.

Through a literature review and preliminary experiments,21,48 we identified seven small molecules that collectively regulate signaling pathways, epigenetics, and metabolic conversion, synergistically promoting astrocyte-to-neuron conversion. We found that using neurobasal/DMEM/F12 (1:1, v/v) or DMEM/F12 as the basal medium during the induction phase resulted in higher rates of conversion. This may be because, at this stage, cells exhibit intermediate characteristics between those of astrocytes and neurons or retain more astrocytic features. The mixed medium, or DMEM/F12, better supported the survival of these intermediate-state cells. Furthermore, the addition of a high concentration of bFGF during the induction phase reduced cell death and improved neuronal conversion efficiency, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies.18 Unexpectedly, we observed little difference in conversion efficiency between CiNs cocultured with astrocytes and those cultured alone during the differentiation phase, suggesting that supplementation with neurotrophic factors was sufficient to support neuronal maturation.

Our optimized small-molecule cocktail facilitated the rapid transformation of astrocytes into functional neurons within 10 days. The dynamic changes in the expression of neuron- and astrocyte-associated protein markers and related genes confirmed that the reprogramming process was gradual, indirectly verifying that the reprogrammed neurons originated from astrocytes. The RNA-seq results revealed that the transcriptional profiles of reprogrammed neurons closely resembled those of primary neurons but were distinct from those of astrocytes. These findings were consistent with those of a previous study by Zheng et al.35 The DFGKLRV cocktail contained regulators of four signaling pathways (laduviglusib, a Wnt activator; forskolin, a cAMP activator; RepSox, a TGF-β inhibitor; and DAPT, a Notch inhibitor), two epigenetic modulators (VPA, an HDAC inhibitor; RG108, a DNA methyltransferase inhibitor), and one regulator of metabolic transition (KC7F2). Wnt signaling has been shown to regulate neurogenesis both postnatally and in adulthood.49 Additionally, it modulates cell self-renewal and pluripotency and promotes neuronal differentiation. High intracellular levels of cAMP promote axon growth during embryonic central nervous system development.50 Activation of the TGF-β pathway induces epithelial–mesenchymal transition and inhibits neuronal identity while promoting glial cell identity40,51; our transcriptome results confirmed alterations in the corresponding pathways. The inhibition of the Notch pathway promotes neuronal fate determination,52 making the Notch inhibitor DAPT an essential component of this cocktail. However, GSEA revealed upregulation of Notch expression during reprogramming, despite the presence of DAPT. Similar observations have been previously reported53 and may reflect the combined effects of other components in the cocktail, including VPA, which has been reported to activate Notch1 signaling, although the precise mechanism remains unclear and warrants further investigation. VPA increases histone acetylation levels, improving the efficiency of neuronal reprogramming.42 RG108 promotes neuronal reprogramming by increasing DNA methylation in the promoter regions of nonneuronal genes.54 Astrocytes rely primarily on glycolysis, whereas neurons use mainly oxidative phosphorylation for energy production. The HIF-1α inhibitor KC7F2 facilitates neuronal induction by promoting oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic shifts.55 In our study, the expression of a glycolysis-related gene (Gapdh) was downregulated on day 10 of reprogramming. Notably, the reprogrammed population was predominantly enriched in GABAergic neurons, which precisely matched the principal neuronal subtype of the striatal donor region. Similarly, Yin et al. reported that cortex-derived astrocytes primarily transform into glutamatergic neurons, whereas midbrain-derived astrocytes generate both glutamatergic neurons and a subset of GABAergic neurons.42 These findings suggest that the subtypes of CiNs may be influenced by the brain regions from which the original astrocytes were harvested. We also integrated RNA-seq and network pharmacology analysis to explore the core targets of small-molecule-induced AtN conversion. In addition, we performed an exploratory transcriptomic analysis of genes putatively associated with individual DFGKLRV components. The expression patterns of these component-associated genes differed between DFGKLRV-treated cells and astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. 20), suggesting that the cocktail engages multiple complementary reprogramming-related transcriptional programs rather than acting through a single dominant pathway. However, this analysis does not establish the strict functional indispensability of each component.

Nevertheless, the complexity of this seven-compound cocktail may pose challenges for direct clinical translation. A limitation of our study is that although the results of the exploratory transcriptomic analysis suggest that the seven components may be associated with reprogramming-associated gene changes to varying degrees, the current data do not allow us to determine whether any individual component is functionally redundant or to define a minimally effective combination, because the RNA-seq data were generated from the complete cocktail rather than from component-withdrawal conditions. Such minimization will require substantial combinatorial optimization across both in vitro and in vivo settings. In addition, although DFGKLRV showed effective reprogramming, the mechanistic basis of this specific seven-compound combination remains incompletely understood. The effects of a chemical cocktail under pathological conditions are likely context-dependent. Therefore, future studies should focus on identifying the most critical components of DFGKLRV and further simplifying and optimizing the formulation.

The loss of neurons contributes to severe neurological dysfunction in patients with ICH. However, current therapeutic options, such as surgical and medical treatments, often yield limited and unsatisfactory outcomes.56,57 In situ neuronal reprogramming, which involves the conversion of resident nonneuronal cells into neurons, has been proposed as a promising therapeutic approach for neurological disorders, such as neurodegenerative diseases,6 spinal cord injury,36 and ischemic stroke.58 However, few studies have explored neuronal reprogramming in the context of ICH. In this study, we demonstrated that hemin-treated astrocytes could be converted into neurons following treatment with DFGKLRV, as evidenced by increased expression of neuronal markers and decreased expression of astrocytic markers. Although our pilot study initiated chemical reprogramming in an ICH model using VCRFBI, our current data show that VCRFBI and other classic cocktails exhibit limited conversion capacity under the in vitro hemin treatment conditions used here, whereas DFGKLRV performs more efficiently in this experimental context (Supplementary Fig. 7). Such differences in conversion efficiency may reflect distinct disease-associated cellular states and microenvironmental cues across experimental conditions. Future studies are needed to elucidate the molecular basis underlying the improved conversion efficiency of DFGKLRV. Previous studies have suggested that astrocyte-to-neuron conversion may proceed through intermediate states, in which original glial markers are downregulated before neuronal markers are fully established.13 Therefore, the marker-negative cells observed in our system may reflect a transitional state during neuronal conversion. Additionally, the expression of neuronal transcription factor genes was upregulated to varying degrees. On this basis, we hypothesized that small molecule-mediated reprogramming after ICH involves the activation of neuron-related transcription factors (NEUROD1, NEUROG2, ASCL1, and PAX6), which drive the conversion process. Similar observations have been reported in another study on small-molecule-induced AtN reprogramming.42 We employed a GFAP-promoter-driven LV system to specifically label astrocytes, ensuring rigorous origin tracing.

Given the inflammatory response following ICH, selecting an appropriate therapeutic window for neuronal reprogramming is critical. Reactive astrocytes initially form a protective scar, which limits hematoma expansion and inflammation.59 However, during the chronic phase, the glial scar has detrimental effects. The dense physical barrier created by the glial scar impedes axonal regeneration, and the CSPGs produced by reactive astrocytes act as inhibitory factors for axonal growth.60 Transforming reactive astrocytes into neurons during the chronic phase of ICH could replenish lost neurons while reducing glial scarring, thereby potentially enhancing functional recovery in patients with ICH. We initiated reprogramming 21 days after ICH, when the glial scar had stabilized. Furthermore, at this time point, sufficient reactive astrocytes were present for reprogramming. The Aldh1l1-CreERT2/tdTomato lineage tracing system was used to specifically label ALDH1L1-expressing astrocytes, minimizing the mislabeling of neurons transiently expressing astrocyte markers during neurodevelopment or subsequent reprogramming. Furthermore, early time-point analysis provides additional insight into the dynamics of in vivo chemical reprogramming. We detected DCX+tdTomato+ cells on day 14, whereas SOX2+tdTomato+ and PAX6+tdTomato+ cells were not observed at appreciable levels. Although a rare or transient progenitor-like state cannot be excluded, the data do not support a stable progenitor-like intermediate, but instead are more consistent with direct AtN conversion, in agreement with our previous in vitro findings. Our CiNs in situ expressed the striatum-specific neuronal markers DARPP32 and GABA but did not express the markers TH, VGLUT2, ChAT, or PV. Furthermore, whole-cell patch-clamp analysis revealed that the in situ CiNs possessed MSN-like electrophysiological signatures, further confirming their functional identity. Our reprogramming efficiency is greater than that of previous studies on chemical reprogramming.18,36 The CiNs achieved functional integration into host neural circuits and were associated with behavioral improvement, supporting their therapeutic potential. However, these effects were modest. Specifically, while a slight overall improvement was observed at later stages, the results at earlier time points were less robust, likely because of the limited sample size. In addition, because histological and behavioral analyses were not performed in the same mice, the present study does not establish a direct animal-by-animal correlation between CiN number and behavioral outcome. Future studies will focus on increasing the sample size to verify whether behavioral recovery can be achieved at earlier stages while also enhancing the magnitude of recovery and assessing histological and behavioral outcomes in the same mice. These efforts will enable direct correlation analyses and further strengthen the translational potential of small-molecule-mediated AtN conversion. Notably, the observed behavioral improvement may not be determined solely by the nominal lineage-traced conversion efficiency, as lineage labeling likely provides a conservative estimate of total conversion events. Consistent with this interpretation, we observed an increase in the total number of NeuN-positive cells in the treated group, supporting the biological relevance of neuronal replacement. In addition, we did not detect a significant reduction in the number of IBA1-positive microglia (Supplementary Fig. 21), suggesting that the functional benefit is unlikely to be explained simply by a decrease in microglial cell number. However, potential changes in the inflammatory microenvironment or microglial activation state cannot be excluded and warrant further investigation.

Despite the high efficiency of continuous in situ drug administration for in vivo reprogramming, this approach is limited by its clinical invasiveness and the potential for mechanical tissue injury caused by osmotic pump implantation. Therefore, it is critical to explore alternative drug delivery methods that are both highly efficient and minimally invasive. Future studies should explore systemically deliverable strategies to increase clinical feasibility, such as extracellular vesicle-based carriers, targeted nanomaterials, or nose-to-brain nanoparticle delivery.61 Additionally, for cases where focal administration remains necessary, sustained-release formulations may offer a more practical alternative by reducing the need for continuous infusion and minimizing secondary tissue trauma.

Beyond delivery, several translational hurdles remain. As a seven-molecule cocktail, DFGKLRV requires careful evaluation of pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, dosing optimization, stability, and drug–drug interactions in the injured brain. Long-term safety concerns include ectopic reprogramming or incomplete conversion, abnormal neuronal excitability, off-target effects on nonastrocytic cell populations, and circuit disruption. These issues should be assessed using lineage tracing, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, electrophysiology, and behavioral monitoring. Regulatory challenges also exist, as the chemical cocktail may complicate manufacturing standardization, quality control, and approval pathways. Consequently, extensive validation in large-animal models is necessary before clinical translation.

Furthermore, the clinical application of chemical reprogramming raises important ethical considerations. Although small-molecule-based approaches avoid permanent genetic manipulation, they still induce substantial and potentially irreversible changes in cell identity and tissue organization. For neurologically vulnerable patients with ICH, the balance between potential benefits and the risks of maladaptive circuit remodeling, unforeseen long-term effects, or incomplete functional integration must be carefully evaluated. Ethical clinical translation will therefore require robust evidence of safety, target specificity, and durable functional benefit, together with transparent informed consent procedures and long-term follow-up.

Inefficient conversion remains a major limitation for the use of neuronal reprogramming.25 This is particularly true for small-molecule-mediated reprogramming, which involves numerous compounds and complex integrated mechanisms, making it challenging to identify effective targets for enhancing reprogramming efficiency.62 During the reprogramming of astrocytes into neurons, the metabolic shift from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation is often accompanied by lipid peroxidation.17 Lipid peroxidation may present a major obstacle to neuronal reprogramming,63 as the use of antioxidants has been shown to improve conversion efficiency and reduce neuronal cell death.64 Ferroptosis is a form of nonapoptotic cell death caused by the accumulation of intracellular lipid peroxidation and ROS.26 In this study, we monitored the dynamic changes in ferroptosis-related markers during AtN reprogramming. Peak ferroptosis was observed on day 3 of reprogramming. Treatment with ferroptosis inhibitors significantly enhanced the efficiency of neuronal reprogramming. One potential concern is that the effects of ferroptosis inhibition on induced neuronal output may reflect altered survival of early induced neurons, rather than an effect primarily associated with cell fate conversion. To address this issue, we performed TUNEL staining and detected no significant difference in the proportion of TUNEL-positive cells among the control, ferrostatin-1, and erastin groups, suggesting that ferroptosis modulation did not markedly alter cell death at this early stage. Moreover, although inhibitors of ferroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis increased the number of DAPI⁺ cells, only ferrostatin-1 significantly increased the proportions of TUJ1⁺/DAPI⁺ and MAP2⁺/DAPI⁺ cells. These findings indicate that ferroptosis inhibition enhances neuronal reprogramming beyond simply the general improvement in cell survival. Previous studies have shown that ferroptosis inhibitors promote Ascl1-mediated reprogramming of astrocytes into neurons,63 but impair the NeuroD4-mediated conversion of glioblastoma cells into neurons.65 Our findings align with the former, possibly because the initial cells for reprogramming in both cases were astrocytes. However, despite these observations, the molecular mechanisms through which ferroptosis inhibition facilitates astrocyte reprogramming remain poorly understood. Our findings identify the TGF-β/SMAD3/SOX9 axis as a critical barrier to neuronal conversion that is dismantled by Fer-1. Although the mechanism by which Fer-1 attenuates this pathway remains to be fully defined, previous studies suggest a potential link between oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and TGF-β signaling. Several studies have demonstrated that ROS can upregulate the expression of genes encoding TGF-β.66 In addition, the lipid peroxidation end product 4-hydroxy-2,3-nonenal has been reported to upregulate TGF-β1 expression.67 These findings suggest that Fer-1 may suppress TGF-β/SMAD3/SOX9 signaling by limiting oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation-derived aldehyde signaling. Nevertheless, the precise mechanism underlying the interaction between Fer-1 and TGF-β signaling, whether through direct antioxidant effects or indirect signaling pathways, remains to be elucidated in future investigations. While investigating the impact of ferroptosis on reprogramming efficiency, we made several unexpected discoveries. TUJ1 is an immature neuronal marker, whereas MAP2 is associated with a more mature neuronal state.53 Therefore, the incomplete overlap between TUJ1 and MAP2 likely reflects the heterogeneous maturation states of chemically reprogrammed cells (Fig. 6i). These findings suggest that multiple neuronal markers should be used for accurate identification of reprogrammed cells. Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis resulted in a twofold increase in chemical reprogramming efficiency in vivo.

Despite the promising findings, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while our transcriptomic and network analyses revealed various candidate genes potentially involved in reprogramming, their individual causal roles were not systematically validated using CRISPR/Cas9 or shRNA, as our mechanistic exploration prioritized the ferroptosis-related TGF-β/SMAD3/SOX9 axis. Second, our molecular analysis was conducted at the bulk level, which may mask cellular heterogeneity. Future studies incorporating single-cell RNA sequencing and pseudotime trajectory analysis will be essential for determining the precise intermediate states and transition dynamics of astrocyte-to-neuron conversion within the complex environment of the hemorrhagic brain. Third, our experiments were exclusively conducted on young adult male mice. While this cohort allowed for a controlled investigation of the core mechanisms, whether our findings are conserved across different sexes and age groups remains to be determined. Previous studies have indicated that direct reprogramming efficiency significantly declines with age,68 likely because of progressive metabolic shifts and epigenetic barriers inherent to aged cells. Consistent with these findings, our preliminary observations also suggest a reduced conversion efficiency in aged mice. Consequently, future investigations incorporating female animals and aged cohorts are essential to validate the generalizability and translational potential of our conclusions. Finally, we did not further assess whether ferroptosis inhibition during reprogramming improves behavioral outcomes after ICH. Future studies, including behavioral assessments and a Fer-1-alone control group, will be needed to determine whether functional benefits arise from enhanced reprogramming efficiency or Fer-1-mediated neuroprotection.

In summary, our study demonstrated that a DFGKLRV small-molecule cocktail can effectively reprogram reactive astrocytes into functional, electrophysiologically active neurons in the pathological environment of ICH. We further identified ferroptosis as a critical regulatory barrier to this process, as its pharmacological inhibition significantly enhances reprogramming efficiency. Mechanistically, this effect is mediated by the suppression of the TGF-β/SMAD3/SOX9 axis. These findings hold significant promise for chemical neural regeneration and identify ferroptosis as a key therapeutic target for brain repair. Overall, this study represents an important step toward the translation of chemical-based regenerative medicine into clinical applications for ICH.

Materials and methods

Mice

Male C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old) and postnatal day 0 (P0) mice were purchased from Hubei Biont Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Aldh1l1-CreERT2 and Rosa-CAG-tdTomato transgenic mice (8–10 weeks old) were obtained from Cavens Model Animal Co., Ltd. Mice were housed under a 12-h light–dark cycle and had ad libitum access to water and food. Aldh1l1-CreERT2/Rosa-CAG-tdTomato mice were generated by crossing Aldh1l1-CreERT2 and Rosa-CAG-tdTomato transgenic mice (Supplementary Fig. 12d). All the animal experiments in this study were approved by the Committee of Animal Ethics at Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (approval No. 2023-4037). In this study, the mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups, and the investigators were blinded throughout all the experimental procedures.

Primary cell culture

Primary astrocytes were isolated from the brains of C57BL/6 mice according to previously published protocols with slight modifications. In brief, the mice were sacrificed and sterilized. After the meninges were removed, the brain tissues were dissociated mechanically and digested with 2 mg/mL papain (Solarbio, China). The cells were then centrifuged for 8 min at 4 °C and 200 g, resuspended, and plated in poly-D-lysine (PDL; Sigma)-precoated culture flasks. The cells were cultured in astrocyte medium (AM), which consisted of DMEM/F12 (BOSTER), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), and penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco).

The procedure for primary neuron extraction was similar to that for astrocyte extraction, except that the cells were cultured in neurobasal medium (Gibco) supplemented with 1% N2 (Gibco), 2% B27 (Gibco), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Half of the neuronal culture medium was replaced every 3 days.

Chemical reprogramming of cultured astrocytes into neurons

The astrocytes used for reprogramming were passaged at least three times. Prior to reprogramming, astrocytes were seeded in PDL-coated 6-well plates or 24-well plates. The day before small-molecule drug treatment (D-1), half of the culture medium was replaced with induction medium (IM), which consisted of DMEM/F12: neurobasal (1:1), DAPT (5 μM, MCE), forskolin (10 μM, MCE), RG108 (10 μM, MCE), KC7F2 (7.5 μM, MCE), laduviglusib (3 μM, MCE), RepSox (1 μM, MCE), VPA (0.5 mM, MCE) (DFGKLRV), basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (100 ng/mL, Peprotech), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (20 ng/mL, Peprotech), glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) (20 ng/mL, Peprotech), N2, B27, GlutaMAX (Gibco), and penicillin/streptomycin. After 1 day (D0), the culture medium was completely replaced with fresh IM, which was refreshed every other day. On day 4 (D4), the IM was replaced with differentiation medium (DM), which consisted of neurobasal, forskolin (10 μM), BDNF (20 ng/mL), GDNF (20 ng/mL), N2, B27, GlutaMAX, and penicillin/streptomycin.

To simulate an in vitro ICH model, astrocytes were treated with 10 μM hemin (Selleck) for 16 h before chemical reprogramming.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunocytochemistry, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100-phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min, and then washed three times with PBS. The samples were then blocked in 10% goat serum or 10% donkey serum for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. The next day, after being washed three times with PBS, the samples were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark.

For brain section staining, the mice were perfused with PBS and 4% PFA, and the brains were harvested and postfixed in 4% PFA overnight. The samples were dehydrated using a 20% and 30% sucrose gradient and then cryosectioned into 15 μm slices (Leica). The frozen sections were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies and secondary antibodies using the same protocol as described for cell staining. Immunofluorescence signals were quantified in the perihematomal region, excluding the hematoma core, which was identified on the basis of tissue morphology and the densely packed cellular region. The same criteria were applied across all the experimental groups.

The list of antibodies used is provided in the supplementary materials. Images were acquired using an epifluorescent (Olympus) or confocal microscope (Olympus, FV1200). The same instrument and same parameters were used for each experiment. For quantitative analysis, fluorescence micrographs acquired at 20×, 40×, and 100× magnification were used.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

The cells were subsequently washed with PBS, followed by lysis with RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa). The collected RNA was then extracted with chloroform, precipitated with isopropanol, and washed with 75% ethanol. Finally, the RNA was dissolved in an appropriate volume of RNase-free water. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa). The resulting cDNA was diluted fourfold with RNase-free water for subsequent RT-qPCR analysis. The RT-qPCR mixture (TaKaRa) was prepared on ice according to the manufacturer’s instructions and assayed using the Bio-Rad CFX system. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Transcriptomic analysis

Astrocyte samples were collected the day before IM treatment, and CiN samples were collected on day 10. Neurons from mouse brains were extracted and cultured until day 7 as primary neuronal samples. Additionally, to investigate the effects of ferroptosis inhibition under pathological conditions, RNA sequencing was performed on cells on day 10 of reprogramming following hemin treatment in the presence of Fer-1 or DMSO. Each of the three groups contained four biological replicates. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus and then sent to Novogene Corporation for RNA sequencing. RNA sample integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer prior to library construction. High-throughput sequencing was subsequently performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Genes with padj ≤ 0.05 and |log2FoldChange| ≥ 2 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). RNA-seq data analysis was performed using the Novogene cloud platform (https://magic.novogene.com/).

Transcriptomics combined with network pharmacology analysis

With respect to the transcriptomic data, DEGs were identified as those whose expression was simultaneously upregulated or downregulated in both neurons and CiNs relative to that in astrocytes. The potential targets of six small molecules (excluding forskolin) were identified using SwissTargetPrediction, while forskolin targets were identified from the DrugBank database. The keywords “(neurons AND reprogramming) OR (neurons AND transdifferentiation)” were used to search for neuronal reprogramming targets in the GeneCards, OMIM, TTD, DrugBank, DisGeNet, TCMSP, and PharmGKB databases. Duplicate targets from different databases were removed, and target normalization was performed using UniProt. Data visualization was performed by using online websites (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/; https://cn.string-db.org/).

Electrophysiology

The electrophysiological function of induced neurons was assessed using whole-cell patch clamp on day 10. Artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF, PHYGENE) was oxygenated at room temperature for 30 min before the experiment. Glass micropipettes were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries (SUTTER). The cell coverslips were transferred to the recording chamber, where the cells were continuously bathed in aCSF at a rate of 3 mL/min. Glass micropipettes were filled with an internal solution containing 140 mM K-gluconate, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM MgCl2, 1.1 mM EGTA, and 4 mM Na2ATP. Neuron-like cells were selected for action potential recordings under current-clamp conditions.

Lentivirus infection

Lentiviral (LV) vectors were utilized for both cell fate tracing and mechanistic validation. LV GFAP-MCS-mCherry (GENECHEM) was used to infect astrocytes for in vitro fate tracing of induced neurons. For mechanistic studies, Sox9-overexpressing LVs (rLV-EF1α-mSox9-P2A-EGFP-WPRE) and LV-controls (rLV-EF1α-EGFP-WPRE) were obtained from BrainCase. The optimal infection conditions were determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, astrocytes were counted and seeded into 24-well plates with coverslips. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with an LV suspension, and infection continued for another 24 h. Once mCherry or EGFP fluorescence was observed under a microscope, chemical reprogramming was initiated.

ICH model

The ICH mouse model was established on the basis of previous studies.69 Type VII collagenase (Sigma) was used to induce an ICH model in mice. The mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (Sinopharm) and then secured in a prone position on a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD). A total volume of 0.5 μL (0.1 μL/min, 1 U/μL) of collagenase was slowly injected into the striatum (AP +0.5 mm, ML +2.2 mm, DV −3.5 mm). After waiting 10 min, the needle was slowly withdrawn at a rate of 1 mm per minute (Supplementary Fig. 12a). The mice were kept warm at a constant room temperature during surgery, and their health was monitored daily after the procedure. Twenty-four hours post-ICH induction, hematoma formation was observed in the striatum, along with a loss of NeuN-positive signals in the hematoma core (Supplementary Fig. 12b, c).

Implantation and removal of Alzet micro-osmotic pumps

Micro-osmotic pumps (Alzet) were used to continuously deliver the drug at a constant rate. Pumps were perfused with aCSF containing a small-molecule cocktail (DFGKLRV) solution or an equivalent volume of DMSO (vehicle control) and connected to KIT2 (Alzet) (L-shaped cannula). KIT2 was implanted into the striatum (AP +0.5 mm, ML +2.2 mm, DV −3 mm) of anesthetized mice, and a micro-osmotic pump was inserted subcutaneously. After 2 weeks, the micro-osmotic pump was removed, and the KIT2 stump was sealed with glue.

Virus injection

To examine the integration of CiNs into endogenous neural circuits, we employed a retrograde mono-transsynaptic tracing system. All viruses were obtained from BrainVTA (Wuhan, China). In Aldh1l1-CreERT2 mice, a micropipette was positioned and fixed for 5 min before viral injection. A 1:1 mixture of the helper viruses rAAV-EF1α-DIO-mCherry-F2A-TVA-WPRE-hGH polyA (4.2 × 1012 vg/mL) and rAAV-EF1α-DIO-RVG-WPRE-hGH polyA (4.0 × 1012 vg/mL) was infused into the striatum (AP +0.5 mm, ML +1.75 mm, DV −3.0 mm) at a rate of 0.02 μL/min, with a total volume of 0.5 μL. To minimize backflow, the pipette was kept in place for an additional 10 min before being slowly withdrawn. A chemical reprogramming protocol was then implemented. One week before perfusion, 0.25 μL of RV-ENVA-ΔG-EGFP (2.0 × 108 IFU/mL) was injected into the same stereotaxic coordinates using an identical procedure.

Systemic viral delivery was performed via tail vein injection. The DFGKLRV-treated Aldh1l1-CreERT2/Rosa-CAG-tdTomato mice were briefly restrained, and 30 μL of rAAV-hSyn-EGFP (5.30 × 1012 vg/mL, PHP.eB) was administered. Following injection, the mice were housed in a temperature-controlled environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle for 4 weeks to allow robust viral expression before electrophysiological assessment.

Behavioral assessments

Behavioral experiments70 were used to evaluate neurological function in mice at baseline (the day before micro-osmotic pump implantation) and at 2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks after implantation. The modified Garcia test was used to assess neurobehavioral function in the mice. This test consists of six assessments: limb activity, spontaneous activity, climbing, forelimb extension, unilateral whisker response, and vibrissae touch response. Each item is scored from 0 to 3, with lower scores indicating more severe neurological impairment. Forelimb placement was evaluated using the vibrissae-elicited forelimb placing test. During the test, the animal was held by its torso with its forelimbs unrestrained. When the whiskers contacted the table edge, the placement of the ipsilateral forelimb was recorded. The percentage of placements was calculated from 10 trials per animal. The adhesive removal test was used to evaluate the integrative sensorimotor function of the forepaws. A 4-mm2 square adhesive tape was applied to the mouse’s left forepaw with uniform pressure. The time to contact and remove the tape was recorded, with a 120-s cutoff. The results from three trials were averaged. The corner test apparatus consists of two boards joined at a 30° angle. A mouse was placed facing the corner, and its natural movement toward the corner provided whisker stimulation, leading to a 180° escape turn. The direction of each turn (left or right) was recorded over 10 trials, and the number of right turns was quantified.

Acute brain slice preparation

The mice were deeply anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital and transcardially perfused with ice-cold, oxygenated (95% O2/5% CO2) cutting solution containing (in mM) 225 sucrose, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 0.5 CaCl2, 7 MgCl2, and 10 glucose. The brains were rapidly removed and mounted onto the stage of a vibrating microtome (Leica). Coronal sections (300 μm) containing the striatum were cut and immediately transferred to a recovery chamber filled with aCSF. Slices were allowed to recover at 32 °C for 30 min and subsequently maintained at room temperature for at least 1 h before recording.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

An ROS fluorometric assay kit (Elabscience) was used to quantify the ROS levels during the reprogramming process. In short, cells at different stages of reprogramming were incubated with 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) at 37 °C in the dark for 45 min, followed by immediate imaging using a fluorescence microscope. The mean fluorescence intensities were quantified by ImageJ.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Brain tissues were collected and fixed with electron microscopy fixative (Servicebio) for 20 min. The samples were then postfixed with osmic acid and dehydrated through a gradient ethanol series. The samples were then embedded, sectioned, stained with uranyl acetate, and observed under a transmission electron microscope (HITACHI).

Fer-1 administration in vivo

Small molecules were administered via an osmotic pump to convert astrocytes into neurons in ICH mice, and Fer-1 (2 mg/kg, MCE) or PBS was given by daily intraperitoneal injection.

TUNEL assay

Cell death was evaluated using an In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, Fluorescein (Roche), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min on ice. The cells were subsequently incubated with 25 μL of TUNEL reaction mixture (TdT enzyme and labeling solution, 1:9 ratio) in a humidified dark chamber at 37 °C for 60 min. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. The images were captured using a confocal microscope (Olympus, FV1200). The cell death rate was determined by calculating the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells among the total number of DAPI-stained nuclei.

In vitro cell death inhibitor treatment

Hemin-stimulated astrocytes were treated with DFGKLRV together with vehicle, ferrostatin-1 (2 μM), Z-VAD-FMK (20 μM, MCE), or necrostatin-1 (30 μM, MCE) for 4 days.

Transcription factor-binding site prediction

The Sox9 promoter sequence (−2000 to +100 bp relative to the TSS) was obtained from the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/). Potential Smad3 binding sites within this region were identified using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.genereg.net/).

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease inhibitors (MCE) and phosphatase inhibitors (MCE). The protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime). All the samples were electrophoresed on 10% SDS–PAGE gels before being transferred to nitrocellulose (NC) membranes (PALL). The membranes were blocked using 5% skim milk or 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (CST) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies. The protein bands were visualized using ECL and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Researchers who performed the statistical analysis of the data were blinded to the experimental group assignment. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v10.4. Sample sizes were determined on the basis of previously reported methods, which are consistent with established practices in the field. The number of independent replicates (n) for each experiment is indicated in the respective figure legends. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was utilized. Cell counting and mean fluorescence intensity analyses were performed using ImageJ. Fluorescence images were reconstructed using Imaris to visualize signal colocalization. The results are expressed as the mean ± SD, and P < 0.05 (*) was considered to indicate statistical significance.
