 1. Abstract
We designed and implemented a shape optimization tool that functions with NEAMS codes, and
that nuclear scientists and engineers can employ to optimize the shape of individual components
and the whole core under the applicable single- or multi-physics model comprising the employed
code(s). The shape-optimization tool enables varying the geometric shape itself as well as its
dimensions to yield, potentially, new component designs that are not limited by the designer’s
intuition and previous experience. In cases where the optimal-shape object is an individual
component, we provide the capability for additional verification that the whole-core performance
using the optimized component performs better, under the prescribed optimization criteria, than
the initial design. Our shape-optimization tool couples to NEAMS codes via a flexible input-
composer interface and enables the user to constrain the shape’s evolution to ensure the
component’s manufacturability. Finally, we demonstrate our shape-optimization tool with single-
and multi-physics NEAMS codes.

This objective is motivated by the recent advances in manufacturing technology that, combined
with rising interest in novel reactor concepts, are creating new opportunities for innovation in the
design of individual components that affect the performance of the full reactor system. In
particular, Additive Manufacturing (AM) enables mass production of highly precise, intricate and
complex component shapes that are not feasible with traditional manufacturing techniques.

To accomplish this goal we developed and implemented in MOOSE: (1) discrete shape
optimization capability based on a state-space search that uses Artificial Intelligence strategies to
find the optimal state/shape; (2) smooth shape optimization tool that employs PETSc’s toolkit for
advanced optimization (TAO) to optimize node-displacement of the components’ model sidesets;
(3) hierarchical core optimization workflow that recognizes the repeating patterns typical in a
nuclear reactor and performs the optimization one level at a time with increasing length scale.
Each of these tools is equipped with user-specified constraints to avoid optimal shapes that are
not manufacturable. The developed shape optimization tool is verified and demonstrated on
various nuclear reactor core components and models. The optimization process accounts for
tightly coupled physics that govern the behavior of these target reactors, and exercises several
NEAMS codes in a coupled multiphysics fashion.

The impact of the delivered shape optimization tool will materialize in the optimal design, from
the outset, of advanced reactors currently contemplated to regain the US’s leadership in nuclear
energy R&D. Novel reactor concepts, e.g. Molten Salt Reactors, and sizes/capacities, e.g. micro-
reactors, provide a unique opportunity to optimize performance from the early stages of
development, before the investment in components’ production lines, validation experiments,
and licensing regimes make future improvements in performance prohibitively expensive and
force sub-optimal performance on the affected reactor concept in perpetuity. This benefit will be
realized by the delivered shape optimization tool regardless of the applicable manufacturing
process whether traditional or AM, thereby broadening the impact of this project on current and
future reactor concepts and technologies.

 2. Introduction
Recent advances in manufacturing technology combined with rising interest in novel reactor
concepts are creating new opportunities for innovation in the design of individual components
that affect the performance of the full reactor system. In particular, Additive Manufacturing (AM)
enables mass production of highly precise, intricate and complex component shapes that are not
feasible with traditional manufacturing techniques. While it is not yet established that AM
produced components are licensable for in-core deployment due to the extreme environments
they would be subjected to, it is reasonable to expect technology to address any deficiencies
identified in this regard through theoretical analysis and experimentation. This prospect opens
the door for novel designs of reactor components that optimize their performance locally, e.g.
increase an individual fuel pin’s k∞, and result in core-wide improvement, e.g. increased excess
reactivity at a fixed enrichment and fuel loading. Similarly, novel reactor concepts, e.g. Molten
Salt Reactors and metal-cooled reactors, and sizes/capacities, e.g. SMR and micro-reactors,
provide a unique opportunity to optimize performance from the early stages of development,
before the investment in production lines, validation experiments, and licensing regimes make
future improvements prohibitively expensive and force sub-optimal performance on the affected
reactor concept. This benefit will be realized by the delivered shape optimization tool regardless
of the applicable manufacturing process whether traditional or AM, thereby broadening the
impact of this project on current and future technologies.
Another development that facilitates the proposed research is the major strides in Modeling and
Simulation (M&S) capabilities and tools, and the availability of high performance computing
platforms that enable high-fidelity, multiscale and multiphysics models. In particular, NEAMS’s
investments in the plethora of M&S tools form the backbone of most research and development
efforts in the advanced reactor concepts field. However, these M&S tools generally require a given
geometric shape of a component or of the entire core that is fed as input to the target code that
computes quantities of interest (QoI) corresponding to that shape. On occasion, a small collection
of intuition-inspired shapes would be modeled and their performance, i.e. values of QoI,
compared to select the best performer without guarantee that that shape is indeed optimal. In
other cases, the shape is fixed but the dimensions are treated as parameters that are varied
continuously within a given range to optimize performance for the given shape, again with no
guarantee that other un-attempted shapes will not perform even better.
The ultimate objective of this project was to design and implement a shape optimization tool that
functions with NEAMS tools, and that the user can employ to optimize the shape of individual
components or the whole core under the applicable single- or multi-physics model comprising the
employed code(s). The shape-optimization tool that we completed enables varying the geometric
shape itself as well as its dimensions to yield, potentially, new component designs that are not
limited by the designer’s intuition and previous experience. In cases where the optimal-shape
object is an individual component, our novel tool provides the capability for additional verification
that the core-wide performance using the optimized component performs better, under the
prescribed optimization criteria, than current designs. Our shape-optimization tool couples to
NEAMS codes via a flexible input-composer interface and enables the user to constrain the
shape’s evolution if necessary, e.g. to enforce surface smoothness along the coolant’s flow
channel. Finally, we demonstrated our shape-optimization tool with single- and multi-physics
NEAMS codes on selected reactor relevant configurations.
We utilized the toolkit for advanced optimization (TAO) as the underlying engine to perform the

 4

shape optimization procedure. TAO is an object-oriented software library for parallel, large-scale
optimization, that has become part of PETSc version 3.5 and later distributions. TAO comprises a
variety of optimization algorithms including unconstrained (e.g. Nelder-Mead and various quasi-
Newton methods), bound constrained (bounded conjugate gradient), complementarity
constrained (active set methods), PDE constrained (linear constrained Lagrangian), and general
constrained (interior point method). The advantage of using TAO is that reimplementation and
maintenance of these basic optimization methods is not necessary, allowing faster progress
towards a viable MOOSE optimization toolkit. The created interface between TAO and MOOSE
provides parameter optimization capabilities en route to enabling shape optimization. In addition,
the implementation is flexible so that users can implement their own optimization algorithms (e.g.
Artificial Intelligence (AI) based methods).

 3. Approach and Objectives
An early attempt at shape optimization was reported by the PI in the 1990s seeking the optimal
shape of a cold neutron source as the target application and a two-group, rz-cylindrical geometry,
neutron diffusion physics model. The basic idea of this research drew on an analogy between the
shape optimization problem and a state-space search that was standard among AI techniques at
the time. A state is defined as the material distribution of liquid Deuterium (D2), gaseous D2, or
D2O over the computational cells in the discretized mesh of the considered domain. The search
for the optimal state that provided the largest outgoing current of cold neutrons utilized a
breadth-first algorithm. Upon solving the physics model for each state visited during the search,
that corresponds to a specific shape of the cold source’s liquid D2, the cost function is set to the
outgoing partial current of the cold neutrons, and its value is compared to other states at the
same search-graph depth to determine whether to include this state in the search’s next depth-
level. Sensitivity of the converged optimal shape to various modeling and optimization
parameters, e.g. mesh refinement, was studied to establish high confidence in the obtained
optimal shape. This idea forms the core of the research conducted in this project, and was
extended as described in the delivered publications detailed in the next section of this Report.
The first goal of this project is simple parameter optimization that amounts to finding the value
of a parameter appearing in a PDE (the physics model) such that the solution of the PDE minimizes
a cost function. A particular example would be to find the moderator density that maximizes the
multiplication factor (optimal moderation). Parameter optimization problems can be solved with
existing optimization algorithms available in the TAO library. Therefore, the first task in this
project envisioned interfacing MOOSE’s stochastic tools module (STM) with TAO as follows. Model
evaluations are required to evaluate the cost function for different instances of relevant
parameters. The required model evaluations will be delegated by the optimization primary
application to sub-applications using MOOSE’s MultiApp system. This approach is consistent with
the current implementation paradigm of MOOSE’s STM.
Connecting TAO with MOOSE STM provides the basis for flexible and extendible parameter
optimization capabilities using NEAMS tools, but it does not allow the optimization of a
component’s shape. In this project we developed the following three complementary shape
optimization algorithms.
 3.1 Discrete Shape Optimization – State Space Search
The ultimate goal of this project is the delivered shape optimization code package fitted in
MOOSE’s environment with the ability to employ NEAMS codes as multi-physics models. This was

 5

be accomplished as follows:
1. The user is able to construct the full problem domain’s shape (e.g. a bounding box for the
 component whose shape is to be optimized) using a software package like SolidWorks, then
 discretize it into elements (i.e. computational cells) with a specified range of cell sizes and
 shapes. The resulting mesh is then written to a geometry file whose format is compatible with
 the target NEAMS code. Capabilities to modify, e.g. refine, the mesh are provided via the
 mesh generator that created the initial mesh to allow the user to assess the convergence of
 the optimal shape with mesh refinement to a unique limit that satisfies basic
 manufacturability conditions via user-specified constraints.
2. The user specifies permissible materials to be used in the optimization process to fill the
 problem domain, and their relevant physical properties for the applicable physics, e.g. cross
 sections for neutronics. This data is entered by the user in the input file and via data libraries
 that are readable by the NEAMS codes utilized to model the physics. A specific assignment of
 these materials to the elements in the mesh defines a particular state in the state-space that
 contains all possible shapes of the target component and its surrounding environment within
 the bounding box on the given spatial mesh.
3. By feeding a given state, plus relevant physics data, plus any additional required input data,
 e.g. iterative stopping criteria, to the applicable NEAMS code, QoIs are computed for that
 state and communicated to the primary MOOSE application using MultiAppTransfers.
4. A cost function, that is provided by the user as a module in the optimization package, then
 receives the physics code output and computes a value representative of the “goodness” of
 the attempted state in achieving the optimal value. The combination of the state and cost
 function comprise the Objective State as explained below.
5. A set of rules imposed by the user are applied in generating a new state from a given state
 while satisfying the specified constraints. This includes selecting one (or more) active
 materials that can be changed by the rule in one (or more) elements. The constraints may
 include connectivity of the material assignments so they comprise objects with well-defined
 surfaces that can be smoothened into a manufacturable shape at the end of the optimization
 process. The rules and constraints are designed to be flexible so they can address various
 needs and grow in the future with gained experience.
6. The breadth-first search algorithm utilized in earlier research by the PI is dated and does not
 represent the current state of the art. Modern search algorithms that incorporate
 probabilistic strategies, e.g. simulated annealing and genetic algorithms, to avoid getting
 stuck in local minima were implemented in a separate module to allow mixing and matching
 physics models and search strategies.
7. In the post-optimization verification stage, the achieved optimal shape is translated into a
 non-discrete geometrical shape that, while possibly complex and intricately detailed, is
 judged to be manufacturable by AM or traditional methods. This smoothing step in general
 leads to a non-optimal shape. The framework developed in this project allows the use of a
 smooth shape optimization step after this smoothing step to find a smooth optimal shape. As
 an example, consider that the state-space search is used to find the optimal assignment of
 fuel/moderator material to mesh elements in an LWR unit-cell that is discretized using a
 Cartesian mesh (i.e., the fuel’s curved circumference is stair-cased). The minimization process
 will find the optimal stair-case geometry and smoothing will lead to a curved-surface
 geometry that is not necessarily optimal. The smooth shape optimization algorithm described
 in the next section then allows for finding a smooth and optimal shape. Finally, a whole-core
 model is constructed utilizing the optimal components’ shape(s), the geometry is discretized
 and the physics calculation is performed on this configuration to compare the optimized QoI

 6

 with its value from traditionally shaped components. This step may be conducted in a single
 application of the physics code or as a hierarchy of calculation-scales, e.g. unit cell → assembly
 → full-core sequence.
 3.2 Continuous Shape Optimization
The second shape optimization algorithm we developed is continuous shape optimization. We
represent the shape of an object by a collection of element faces that is referred to as a sideset
in MOOSE jargon. The developed algorithm seeks to find a displacement of the nodes contained
in the sideset so that the solution of a partial differential equation minimizes a cost function (e.g.
coolant’s pressure drop and heat transfer along the core’s axial direction is optimized by a
particular shape of the flow-channel). The algorithm revolves around the idea of introducing a
collection of user-defined test functions representing different modes of displacement of the
sideset’s nodes. A candidate displacement of the nodes is written as a linear superposition of
these modes, thus reducing the optimization problem to finding the optimal values of the scalar
coefficients multiplying the test functions. The optimization is then performed using a parameter
optimization algorithm based on the TAO library. For evaluating models with a particular choice
of coefficients multiplying the test functions we use MOOSE’s displaced mesh capability to effect
the determined node displacement then solve the applicable physics PDEs on the displaced mesh.
It is expected that for large displacements, nodes adjacent to, but not on the sideset, will have to
be displaced as well to maintain the connectivity of the mesh (e.g., if one node in a quadrilateral
element is displaced too much, then edges, which are defined by the nodes at the two ends, can
intersect each other). We addressed this problem by smoothing the displacement away from the
sideset.
Continuous shape optimization is expected to be of great utility to users of our developed
optimization tool in the following instances: (1) to tweak an almost optimal shape to further
improve its performance, (2) if the underlying physics requires a smooth surface (e.g. CFD), and
(3) as a post-processing step to the discrete shape optimization algorithm where the sidesets are
inspired/extracted from a potentially unwieldy shape produced by the shape discrete optimizer
instead of basing their selection on intuition and limited human judgment.
 3.3 Hierarchical Core Optimization
Complex technical systems almost always encompass a large range of scales. For example, a light
water reactor core consists of pin cells that make up assemblies, assemblies that make up the
core which in turn is surrounded by the reflector and the reactor pressure vessel and is connected
to the balance of plant. Simultaneous optimization of the building blocks at all scales is infeasible,
because the cost of a single execution of a model spanning all these scales is intractable.
For shape optimization in advanced nuclear reactors, we propose a hierarchical shape
optimization workflow that optimizes the building blocks starting at the lowest relevant length
scales. When moving up to the next higher length scales, the geometry of the building blocks of
the lowest length scales are frozen. At this larger length scales optimality is achieved by reuse and
reconfiguring of existing building blocks of previous levels. We realize that some reactor
geometries with repeating patterns lend themselves well to this approach (e.g., heat pipe cooled
micro-reactors), while others such as pool-type molten salt reactors do not. We offer the
hierarchical optimization workflow to cater to common reactor geometries.
In MOOSE, we implemented hierarchical core optimization using the flexible mesh generator
system. Optimal building blocks of lower length scales are written to exodus files and can then be
loaded by mesh generators at larger length scales. Several mesh generators allow loading
different pre-existing building blocks and stitching them into a global geometry (e.g.

 7

StitchedMeshGenerator and TiledMeshGenerator). With minor extensions, this capability was
deployed to realize hierarchical core optimization.

 ABSTRACT
 We introduce a methodical shape optimization approach using a physics driven simulated anneal-
 ing strategy that is performed here using the OpenFSAM code attached to the MPACT neutronics
 simulation package. We demonstrate this approach on a 2D fuel pin cell in an infinite lattice. The
 optimizations in this work initially seek to maximize the multiplication factor for an infinite fuel
 pin lattice and later to maximize a proxy for net revenue, namely the largest multiplication factor
 at the smallest coolant pressure drop and fuel cost, for a given fuel design. This work explores
 three different framings of fuel shape optimization: (1) combinatorial swapping of a list of fuel
 and moderator locations on a pixelated grid, (2) discrete changing of fuel and moderator locations
 on a pixelated grid, and (3) continuous variation of inner and outer radius of an annular fuel shape.
 The combinatorial and discrete set of demonstrations are performed on a 20 × 20 grid of the pix-
 elated C5G7 UO2 unit pin cell. The continuous optimization demonstration is performed using a
 C5G7 UO2 and a Westinghouse PWR fuel pin unit cell. Net revenue results for the combinatorial
 optimization improves the initial guess from an estimated $4.068 × 109 to $4.074 × 109 per cycle,
 resulting in what resembles an annular-like fuel shape. The discrete net revenue optimization im-
 proved the same initial guess to a value of $4.611 × 109 per cycle though the resultant fuel shape
 does not seem viable for manufacturing. The final optimization demonstration on the Westing-
 house PWR fuel pin unit cell improved the initial guess from a cycle net revenue of $4.068 × 109
 to $6.868 × 109 for LEU restrictions and $1.067 × 1010 for HEU restrictions.

KEYWORDS: Optimization, Fuel Shape, MPACT

 1. INTRODUCTION
Recent innovations in advanced fuel manufacturing techniques facilitate new possibilities for nuclear reactor
design [1]. Specifically, non-traditional fuel shapes are now possible thanks to loosening of traditional
constraints in fuel manufacturing. To this end, optimal fuel shapes not bound by past restrictions are sought
for the next generation of nuclear reactors.
With considerations toward this, an annular fuel shape has previously been proposed as a potentially optimal
fuel shape based on physical intuition [2]. Such a shape could facilitate higher burn-up and reduce the
maximum temperature in a fuel element. However, while the annular fuel shape seems promising for
traditional reactors, this shape was found via physical intuition based on decades of experience within the
nuclear community studying the characteristics of light water reactors. Novel reactor designs do not yet
have similar cumulative experience within the community, as of course most such designs are not yet in
commercial operation.
This work introduces a simulated annealing [3] (SA) based approach to fuel shape optimization, based
solely on computationally driven design information. OpenFSAM [4] is used as the SA driver for this opti-
mization process and MPACT [5] is used as the neutron transport model to compute the eigenvalue for use
in the optimization energy function. Through this method, a variety of potential fuel shapes can be consid-
ered for an optimal design in a methodical manner even for systems with which the nuclear community has
little experience or intuition.

 2. SIMULATED ANNEALING
Simulated annealing is a heuristic technique used to perform global optimization by starting with a single
state point and searching for a global minimum state energy from there [3]. This goal is achieved iteratively
by perturbing the state point to a new neighbor and checking to see if the new neighbor should be accepted
as the new state point. The probability of accepting the new neighbor is based upon the energy (which will
be minimum at the optimal solution) of the new state point compared to the old state point. If the new state
point has a lower energy than the old state, it is considered a preferable state and always accepted. How-
ever, even if the energy of the neighbor state is higher, it may still be accepted based upon the acceptance
probability in Equation 1. This probability of accepting non-improved states allows simulated annealing
the potential to escape local minima.

 3. FUEL SHAPE OPTIMIZATION
State space optimization in nuclear engineering has previously been reported using advanced heuristics,
such as AI-type searches for geometric optimization of cold neutron sources [7]. This work makes use of
three distinct framings of the optimization problem for nuclear pin cell fuel shape and demonstrates the
optimization in these framings using the simulated annealing optimization scheme. All fuel optimization
exercises in this work are performed on 2D pin cells in an infinite lattice configuration.
3.1. Combinatorial Optimization
The question of optimal fuel pin shape can be abstracted into a combinatorial problem. To achieve this,
we begin with pixelation of a unit cell onto a Cartesian grid of equally sized square cells. Each cell is then
populated by either fuel or moderator. The pattern array for determining which cells are fuel and which are
moderator is then a combinatorial list. A neighbor pattern is defined as a pattern where a single fuel cell
has been swapped with a single moderator cell. Notice that in combinatorial optimization, the initial fuel
volume fraction is preserved.
3.2. Discrete Optimization
Optimal fuel pin shape can also be expressed as a discrete problem. Similarly to combinatorial optimization,
it begins with pixelation of the fuel pin where each square cell is then populated by either fuel or moderator.
The pattern array for determining which cells are fuel and which are moderator is then a discrete list. Unlike
combinatorial optimization, discrete optimization does not treat the search space as the permutations of the
initial list. Instead, discrete optimization changes the material content of each cell independent of the rest
of the list, where a neighbor pattern is defined as a pattern where one or more cells have been swapped to
the other material. Notice that in discrete optimization, the initial fuel volume fraction is not preserved.
3.3. Continuous Optimization
The final framing of fuel shape optimization explored in this work is that of continuous shape optimization.
In this optimization problem, the shape is expressed in terms of a number of parameters that may vary
continuously, which together describe a complete shape. For continuous optimization, a neighbor state is
defined as a state where one or more parameters have been perturbed by some amount. For this particular
demonstration, the basic shape optimized is an annular fuel shape where the continuous parameters are the
inner and outer radius of the fuel. Continuous optimization can also easily be extended to other parameters
beyond just fuel shape, such as fuel enrichment. While the other combinatorial and discrete fuel optimiza-
tion problems can also be extended to include these non-shape related continuous parameters, the process
is more involved since it requires mixing of multiple optimization problem types.

For all optimization problems explored in this work, MPACT [5] was used as the 2D neutron transport

4.1. kef f and Dh Optimization
The initial optimization problems in this work employed C5G7 [8] cross sections for the UO2 fuel and H2 O

approximately 57.7027% fuel by volume while the pixelated version contained 58% fuel by volume.

 5. CONCLUSIONS
Results in Section 4.1 demonstrate that with the right energy and constraints, an annular-like fuel shape
can be achieved on a pixelated grid pin cell for the combinatorial optimization framing. These results
also show that such a shape is not found for the discrete optimization framing when using Equation 3
as the optimization energy. The continuous annealing of the annular shape once this optimal shape was
found demonstrated that an improvement in terms of eigenvalue and fuel usage can be made compared to
traditional fuel rods by adapting an annular shape. This adaptation was shown to have only a minor negative
impact on the hydraulic diameter for the moderator flow.
Section 4.2 demonstrates a more sophisticated energy equation for fuel pin optimization in the form of
an approximation of cycle net revenue. In section 4.2.1 this optimization resulted in a similarly annular
fuel shape for the combinatorial optimization framing, but again failed to yield a shape viable for fuel
manufacturing. The annular-like fuel shape found in this section for combinatorial optimization represents
an improvement in justification for it compared to the previous combinatorial optimization, due to the fact
that Equation 9 is less arbitrary than Equation 3. It is conjectured that with a better approximation of fuel
manufacturing costs, including costs associated with manufacturing a given shape, an annular fuel shape
could be achieved with even discrete optimization.
The continuous annealing example in Section 4.2.2 introduces variability to the fuel enrichment. These
results demonstrate that for the considered model and economic approximations, maximum fuel enrichment

 M&C 2023 - The International Conference on Mathematics and Computational Methods Applied
 to Nuclear Science and Engineering · Niagara Falls, Ontario, Canada · August 13 – 17, 2023

was preferred. They demonstrated substantial improvements in net revenue even within the low enrichment
range which increased the cycle net revenue by about 1% while costing less in both estimated fuel and pump
expenses.
The combinatorial demonstrations reinforce previously proposed annular fuel shapes as a potentially opti-
mal fuel rod shape based on physical intuition. This work also introduces a methodical method to optimize
fuel shapes for systems where physical intuition and experience are less mature. This method is demon-
strated in such a manner that a proxy for economic costs and benefits of such fuel optimization was consid-
ered and demonstrated improvement in these surrogate costs while using a more reasoned energy function.

5.1. Future Work
While the optimal shapes obtained in this work are promising, some results still appear nonviable, primarily
in the discrete optimization results. In addition, important physics are missing from the energy calculation
with respect to neutronics and thermal-hydraulics. Furthermore, while approximations of economic costs
and benefits were made, the surrogate models used here need refinement in order to give a better estimation
of the economic impacts of a given fuel shape and enrichment.

5.1.1. Physics
The following are proposed improvements to the physics environment in this work. Some of these im-
provements may be made in existing models, others may require more sophisticated calculations and use of
additional computational physics codes to properly implement.

  1. The clad and gap are missing from both the neutronics and thermal-hydraulics calculations in this
 work. In reality, fuel would not be directly exposed to water in a real power reactor. As such, modeling
 fuel without clad lessens the impacts of pressure loss due to flow area shrinkage, and overestimates
 the eigenvalue due to the missing absorption in the pin.
  2. While Equation 4 provides an approximation for pressure drop in the core due to friction, it misses
 out on several aspects of pressure drop considerations. Firstly, it does not account for inlet and outlet
 forms losses due to the change in flow shape leading into the core. While these losses should be small
 compared to friction losses, they may not be negligible. The approximation also assumes a static state
 of the moderator throughout the fuel length, which is not true when the water must heat up as it flows
 through the core carrying heat away. Finally, the analysis for the interior and exterior water channels
 must be different since the Reynolds number, pressure drop, and flow rate would vary.
  3. Thermal changes for both the fuel and the moderator were left out. The temperature peaking in the
 fuel will be important for safety costs of the reactor design, and moderator state differences in the
 radial system will result in changes to neutronic behavior.
  4. This work assume an infinite 2D reactor lattice. As this is an unrealistic configuration, an approxima-
 tion of neutron leakage based on flux spectrum could be introduced to better estimate the eigenvalue
 of a reactor actually made with such a fuel shape/enrichment.
  5. Burnup in this work was assumed to give a direct linear relation between cycle length and excess
 reactivity. Different fuel designs will have different power peaking within the fuel and will result in
 different correlations for excess reactivity burnup.

5.1.2. Economic Analysis
The following are proposed improvements to the economic calculations used in this work. While some
approximation improvements may be made, precise economic optimization requires detailed knowledge of
manufacturing and supply prices as well as contracts available to a given company.

  1. Fuel costs are more complex than estimating work SWU price and raw uranium price. Many reac-
 tors use partially spent fuel in certain locations, which can often be cheaper for a given enrichment.

 M&C 2023 - The International Conference on Mathematics and Computational Methods Applied
 to Nuclear Science and Engineering · Niagara Falls, Ontario, Canada · August 13 – 17, 2023

 Regulations and restrictions on fuel enrichment can also add costs beyond just work SWU for higher
 enrichments. Additionally, bulk production of certain enrichment levels may alter relative prices of
 different fuel assays. Finally, manufacture of irregular fuel shapes will not be as cheap (and in some
 cases may be impossible) for the same amount of fuel as traditional cylindrical fuel rods. While ad-
 vanced manufacturing techniques open up new possibilities for fuel fabrication, such techniques are
 not limitless in their capabilities and have their own associated cost penalties.
  2. Operations costs require more than a price per pressure drop in the core. Pumps are not free to
 purchase or repair and their pressure difference does not change linearly with power applied. Further-
 more, pumps often have limitations on the pressure change they can provide for a given flow rate.
  3. The current optimization was done for net cycle revenue, assuming this will be the total cost and ben-
 efit of the reactor. In reality, reactors are given an operational lifespan and are refueled throughout it.
 An improved approximation for optimization function would be net revenue rate per day, accounting
 for the costs and revenue losses of refueling and maintenance shutdowns.

5.1.3. Optimization Space
The optimization space considered was a set of pixelated pin/moderator cells for the combinatorial and
discrete optimization and an annular fuel shape and fuel enrichment for the continuous optimization. These
state spaces do not represent the totality of possible fuel designs and the following are proposed improve-
ments to the fuel design optimization space.

  1. Fuel enrichment should be considered in the combinatorial and discrete optimization calculations.
 It is slightly more difficult to account for this since it requires the mixing of two different types of
 state spaces, however it is necessary to get a more complete optimized fuel design in the combinato-
 rial/discrete spatial optimizations.
  2. This work assumed a square lattice of fuel pins with an invariable pitch. Advanced reactor designs
 need not limit themselves to these designs and indeed annular fuel shapes would be much easier to
 manufacture with a larger radius which could be facilitated by a larger lattice pitch.
  3. With infinite pixelation of the fuel shape, combinatorial and discrete optimization techniques could
 theoretically represent any possible 2D fuel pin shape. However, infinite pixelation is not possible,
 and even very fine pixelation can be expensive when many different state perturbations need be run.
 Since the continuous optimization in this work is limited to annular and solid cylindrical fuel shapes,
 an alternative means of representing fuel geometry is desired. A level-set representation of geometry
 could potentially address this limitation.

This work will be continued under a new NEUP project making use of the MOOSE [18] environment for
the physics portion of this optimization technique. MOOSE’s multiphysics capabilities and unstructured
mesh geometry representation will facilitate physics and state space representation improvements proposed
in sections 5.1.1 and 5.1.3. The intent is also not for such optimization to be limited to just pin-cell lattice
based reactors, but instead also consider more unconventional moderator designs such as liquid fuel solid-
moderator designs.

 Abstract

 We developed a C++ computational tool for physics-based shape and topology optimization
and integrated it into the MOOSE multiphysics simulation framework. Leveraging MOOSE’s
modular software architecture and data transfer capabilities enabled a seamless coupling of the
optimization routines with various physics solvers. The tool implements combinatorial and discrete
optimization algorithms, and includes performance enhancements like solution caching, tabu
lists, and multi-run restarts. We demonstrate the tool’s flexibility with two applications that utilize
different MOOSE physics modules. We implemented a Simulated Annealing search engine in our
new tool, but other engines can be added modularly. The first application adopts a two-dimensional
Cartesian geometry representation of a pin-cell aiming for the optimal distribution of fuel and
moderator material on a fixed mesh that maximizes neutron multiplication and coolant hydraulic
diameter. Constraints were applied to the search procedure and we explored their effect on the
realized optimal shape, identifying a set that includes preliminary manufacturability constraints
and that produces a Cartesian approximation of annular fuel pins, previously proposed by physical
intuition. The second is a PWR fuel shuffling application at the full-core scale aiming at minimizing
peak power over the core. This capability was not available in MOOSE and is used to illustrate
the flexibility of our new optimization capability to address other types of discrete optimization
demands. In our test case, we obtained a 1250 pcm improvement in the multiplication factor and
a reduced assembly power peaking of more than 30% relative to the initial unoptimized state
comprising an IAEA-2D benchmark-based core. The loading patterns generated were consistent
with established literature. This highlights the tool’s capacity to correctly interpret the modeled
physics and make informed, strategic decisions leading to improved reactor performance. By
expanding MOOSE’s scope to address shape and topological optimization, this work enables
multi-scale reactor design improvements, from the individual fuel pin level to the full core level.
This MOOSE-integrated shape optimization tool provides a simulation-driven design optimization
for enhanced efficiency and safety of nuclear reactors in particular, but potentially any engineered
system based on physics models available in MOOSE. Future work will leverage MOOSE’s
multiphysics capabilities to execute coupled-physics optimization exercises.

1 Introduction

Current Modeling and Simulation (M&S) tools available in the nuclear engineering field have seen
vast improvements in terms of fidelity and variety of applications. MOOSE, a state of the art M&S
framework, is amongst the recent code development frameworks that allow the realization of multi-
physics analysis of nuclear systems, e.g. reactors, and their components [1]. One of the key features
of MOOSE is its modular design. This design allows for the seamless integration of various physical
models and computational techniques. MOOSE uses either finite-element or finite-volume methods for
discretization of the differential operators that typically appear in physics models. The framework is
built upon a foundation of cutting-edge numerical methods and software engineering principles, ensur-
ing robustness, accuracy, and computational efficiency in performing production-level simulations [1].
Furthermore, MOOSE is designed to be user-friendly, with a focus on making the development of new
models and the extension of existing ones as straightforward as possible. This feature, combined with
its powerful capabilities, makes MOOSE a valuable tool for both the scientific research community
and industry professionals involved in complex nuclear engineering projects.
 This advancement in M&S tools of nuclear reactors sets the stage for significant developments in
the field of shape and topology optimization, an analysis that connects theory with practical application
in the engineering, design, and computational sciences. Different flavors of shape optimization have
been applied since the early 1990s in different branches of engineering (e.g., structural mechanics
[2], aerospace engineering [3], and even in a multidisciplinary (i.e., multiphysics) setting [4]), but
has seldom been applied in the nuclear engineering field [5, 6] especially when related to shape
optimization from a reactor physics perspective. Shape optimization involves determining the optimal
geometrical form (or an approximation thereof) of an object, set of objects, or structure within certain
constraints. The objective of this approach is to enhance some selected metrics characterizing the
performance of the studied system. As an example, in this paper we consider optimization of a fuel
rod’s shape by redistributing the fuel and moderator materials over a pin-cell’s domain, subjecting the
tested shapes to desirable constraints. This idea is timely because leveraging modern technologies like
additive manufacturing, shape optimization enables the production of functionally superior component
designs that were previously not possible with traditional manufacturing processes. The rising power
of computing platforms and increased sophistication of M&S tools, such as MOOSE, are making
shape optimization realizable and attractive. However, at present MOOSE does not have a shape or

 3

topology optimization tool or module. In this work we developed such capability to enable the use of
MOOSE physics modules to model a nuclear system, then use the solution to compute an objective
function that is then optimized via Simulated Annealing (SA).
 The outline of the remainder of this paper follows. In Section 2, we describe the search algorithm
and classes of our combinatorial and discrete shape optimization tool, as we implemented them in
MOOSE. This includes a brief introduction to each class and the workflow of the object-oriented tool
that we developed. Following this, in Section 3, we present simple applications of our newly developed
tool in MOOSE designed to verify the implementation on problems where the optimal state is known.
Practical applications starting from the basic block of a traditional LWR nuclear reactor (i.e., the fuel
pin) to the reactor’s entire core as a whole are provided in Section 4. In all the applications reported in
this paper, we utilize MOOSE-based apps and modules [1]. Lastly, Section 5 concludes with insights
into future directions and potential applications, including multiphysics examples and continuous
shape optimization cases that are based on the our new shape optimization tool.

2.1 Description of the Optimization Process

The first component is the optimizer C++ class that hosts the optimization algorithm used to formulate a
new, and arguably better, configuration of the system based on some user-selected criteria. The purpose
of an optimization process is to find a configuration of the system that maximizes (or minimizes) an
objective function under set constraints. The sequence of activities completed in the optimization
process are: (1) The multiapp solver starts first using the initial configuration of the system (i.e., the
original shape) and then runs the physics solver or app and computes the objective function value.
(2) the optimizer portion of the tool generates new configurations of the system, i.e., new states of
the domain; (3) these configurations are transferred to the selected physics module(s) in MOOSE;

 5

(4) the solution to each configuration is then computed; (5) MOOSE postprocessor(s) are executed
to extract quantities of interest from the solution; and (6) the objective function is computed and
compared to advance the search for the optimal state to the next iteration. To elaborate the concept of
the objective function, it is a physics-based user-defined functional that maps the detailed solution
and system parameters into a scalar value that characterizes some feature of the system’s performance.
The constraints exclude undesirable regions from the search space, e.g., geometrical constraints, hard
and soft limits on objective function components, safety factor limits, etc. Optimization procedures
have been previously applied in several engineering disciplines, hence there is a wide variety of
general ways to express the objective function, e.g., reward function in reinforced machine learning,
cost function in economics, and loss function in statistics. Optimization theory is well documented
in the literature, for example see Wright and Nocedal, 2006 [7]; Dimitri et al., 2003 [8]. Possible
objective functions to be used with our new shape optimization tool are limitless. The user can utilize
in evaluating the objective function any quantity produced by the postprocessor system in MOOSE,
or can implement a new postprocessor class to use, or combine different postprocessors into a new
single postprocessor.
 The SA-based optimizer that we implemented in our optimization tool comprises a modified
version of the basic SA algorithm [9, 10]. The modifications that we implemented for the purpose of
improving computational performance include a solution cache method, a tabu list method, and a
multi-run algorithm. Algorithm 1 shows the procedural schematic of the SA algorithm. Topological
optimization is manifested in one of two flavors, both available in our new tool in MOOSE. The
first flavor is combinatorial optimization where the elements of the initial configuration, e.g. fuel
or moderator material assignment to each computational cell of the domain, are shuffled around in
pursuit of a more optimal configuration. This implies that the total number of elements of each kind
appearing in the initial configuration is preserved throughout the search process. In contrast, in the
second flavor, termed discrete optimization, the type of each element in the configuration changes
independently so that the total number of elements of each type may vary during the search. In other
words, combinatorial optimization swaps element pairs in the initial configuration, while discrete
optimization flips configuration elements independently from all other elements. Configurations
generation is usually restricted by application of constraints to ensure the resulting configuration has
a high manufacturability index and hence is realizable.

 The solution cache method works by using a hash table structure to allow using cached objective
function values if the randomly generated configurations have been generated earlier during the
optimization process. This reduces execution time due to avoiding repeatedly invoking the physics
solver for cached configurations. The tabu list method is a component of the Tabu Search algorithm
[11] (like SA, it is a type of state-space search algorithm) that we used in the current optimization
scheme. It forbids the optimization algorithm from revisiting solutions that are already in the tabu list
for a certain number of iterations. This enhances the performance of the optimization algorithm by
encouraging exploration of new regions in parameter space and helps the algorithm escape from local
optima. The multi-run algorithm modifies the implemented state-space search algorithm by adding
another layer of iterations that controls the initial state and the initial random seed that are used to
conduct the optimization. It allows running the optimization process multiple times under different
starting conditions while using the optimized configuration from one run as the initial configuration
for the next run. At the end of the optimization process, the best solution across all runs is regarded as
the overall best solution (i.e., the optimal state). This improves the performance of the state-space
search algorithm by encouraging exploration of a larger fraction of parameter space, and helps the
algorithm escape from local optima. Furthermore, one can control each run’s number of iterations to
achieve a balance between the size of parameter space that is explored vs exploitation of the algorithm
in the provided parameters space.
 The second part of the new optimization tool involves the exchange of information between the
optimizer and the multiApp system of physics simulation app or module in the MOOSE environment.
As stated earlier, evaluation of the objective function is based on parameters relevant to the simulated
physics and the solution corresponding to the attempted configuration generated by the optimizer.
Such parameters, and especially in the case of shape optimization, are stored in MOOSE’s mesh
object (not to be confused with the geometric mesh). Retrieving and setting data (i.e., variables’
values) from this object, while also using it in the optimization, constitute the majority of the second
part of the optimization tool. Such data is transferred and updated using the Input, Reporter, and the
MultiApp transfer systems of MOOSE. The ‘To Transfer’ system transfers the new configuration
to the ‘MultiApp’ system and hence to MOOSE’s relevant physics solver. This solver computes the
objective function value based on the new configuration supplied by the ‘To Transfer’ system (which
itself was generated using the optimizer). The value of the objective function is then transferred from

 8

the solver or the ‘MultiApp’ system to the optimizer that judges whether the underlying configuration
produces a value for the objective function that qualifies it for further consideration in the search
process. The outcome of this judgment guides the generation of new configurations, basically allowing
the optimizer to take proper actions based on the feedback from the physics solver. In this regard, the
optimization process is more like a reinforcement learning approach to machine learning [12], the
goal in both scenarios is to improve decision-making over time through feedback, leading to more
efficient and effective search outcomes.

2.2 Shape Optimization Module Input File Structure

The shape optimization module in MOOSE requires two input files for single-physics problems: a main
driver file and a secondary file containing the physics details, mesh configuration, and postprocessor
for evaluating the objective function. The main driver file is responsible for setting up and controlling
the optimization process, while the secondary file focuses on the physics simulation.
 The main driver file includes several key sections that work together to control the optimization
process and exchange data with the secondary file. The Reporters section introduces a discrete
reporter block, which stores and reports optimization data, such as the configuration and objective
function value. The Executioner section contains elements of the simulated annealing optimizer,
including control parameters like the type of optimization, number of iterations, temperature range,
and constraints. It also allows restarting the optimization process from the best found configuration
and setting specific attributes for the simulated annealing algorithm.
 The MultiApps section connects to the secondary input file application, which holds the simulated
physics of the system undergoing optimization. The Transfers section specifies the data transfer
between the main driver and the secondary file, including the objective function value computed by
a postprocessor. The secondary input file follows the standard MOOSE input file structure and can
include any physics modules or custom applications available in MOOSE.
 By separating the optimization process and the physics simulation into two input files, the shape
optimization module in MOOSE provides a flexible and modular approach to setting up and solving
shape optimization problems. A more detailed explanation of the input file structure, including the
specific attributes and their functions, can be found in Appendix A.

4 Practical Applications of the Shape Optimization Tool in MOOSE

In the previous section, we solved some simple problems to verify the capabilities of the shape
optimization tool. While those conceptual examples demonstrated that the tool can identify globally
optimal solutions for specified objective functions and constraints, they were not practical use cases.
In this section, we explore two practical applications in which the shape optimization tool can generate
manufacturable geometries, shapes, and topologies to improve nuclear engineering systems.

4.1 Fuel pin combinatorial and discrete shape optimization

The unit cell is the building block for a fuel assembly in a traditional nuclear reactor, and it is comprised
of the fuel pin surrounded by the moderator/coolant. In the standard designs, the fuel pin is comprised
of a cylindrical fuel element encased in cladding material with a thin gap. Recently other shapes of
the fuel pin emerged in the literature, e.g. annular and cruciform, guided by physical intuition. In this
section we demonstrate a preliminary application of our new optimization module to the optimization
of the cross sectional shape of the fuel pin. While this preliminary example is too simplistic, e.g. based
on two-group diffusion theory and the hydraulic diameter to account for pressure drop, and while
it does not abide by manufacturability constraints, it captures the main features of a typical shape
optimization procedure and demonstrates its implementation in MOOSE. Current efforts are invested
in including more accurate multiphysics models and improving the set of constraints to achieve a truly
optimal shape that is possible and economical to manufacture and deploy.

4.1.1 Description of the Problem

In this optimization exercise we model the neutronics physics using the C5 benchmark [17, 18]
UO2 unit-cell design, that is the basis for the C5G7 benchmark. This model utilizes a homogenized
fuel pin of radius 0.54 cm with roughly 58% fuel content, and a typical pin-cell pitch of 1.26 cm.
OpenMC [19], a continuous-energy and multigroup Monte Carlo neutron transport code, was used
to generate the cross-sections of the pin cell in two energy groups representing fast and thermal
energy groups. We then use these cross-sections in the open-source MOOSE-based neutron diffusion
code Moltres [20] as the physics solver. For each configuration considered during the optimization
process, Moltres provides values for relevant quantities that are used to evaluate the objective function,

 17

e.g. multiplication factor. For a more sophisticated optimization process, cross-sections would be
generated dynamically with every shape configuration change. However, the primary intent of this
example is to demonstrate the shape optimization functionality as implemented in the new MOOSE
module. Furthermore, we generated the cross-sections based on materials’ type instead of being
homogenized over the pin-cell region, which aligns with Moltres’ simulation approach [20] that needs
cross-sections assigned per material.
 The Moltres geometrical model is based on a square computational cells of side 0.63 cm each,
covering the square unit cell of side 1.26 cm. The homogenized fuel pin is stair-cased on the mesh
and reflective boundary conditions are applied on the four edges of the domain. One quarter of this

4.1.2 Discrete Optimization

When discrete optimization is applied to the initial configuration of the pin cell depicted in Figure 8,
without any constraints, it yields a configuration where the moderator cells are dispersed within the
fuel pin, as presented in Figure 9. The optimization process tends towards this configuration since the
dispersion of the moderator throughout the fuel facilitates the thermalization of fission neutrons, which
in turn increases the fission rate. This configuration gives preference to the neutronics contribution to
the objective function. Specifically, the configuration shown in Figure 9 has 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 was 1.37515, an
increase of more than 4000 pcm versus the initial state, and a hydraulic diameter 𝐷 ℎ was 0.1186 cm,
about one-sixth its initial value. Repeating the unconstrained discrete optimization process always
resulted in the moderator being dispersed within the fuel block, however the exact final configuration
varied. While this optimization did indeed maximize the multiplication factor, the resulting pin cell
configuration is not manufacturable, thereby undermining the optimization process outcomes.

4.1.3 Constraints Description

To prevent the optimizer from generating unrealisticspace invaders style shapes, constraints were
imposed on the configurations generated by the state space search algorithm. The first constraint

 20

checked whether the current configuration comprised a single contiguous fuel block and no more
than two contiguous coolant blocks. This promoted configurations where cells of each material are
connected, forming contiguous enclaves of fuel and coolant. The ‘checkEnclaves’ function, shown
in Algorithm 2, counts the number of enclaves in the configuration using the BFS method.
 BFS is fundamentally a graph traversal algorithm that is known in Graph theory [24]. The vector
‘int_vec’ and the map ‘neighbors_map’ implicitly form a graph representation. Each element in the
‘int_vec’ represents a vertex (or node) in the graph. The ‘neighbors_map’ provides the adjacency
information for each vertex. Specifically, for each index i in the vector, ‘neighbors_map[i]’ provides
a list of indices that are considered neighbors to i. These neighboring indices form the edges in the
graph. We then apply the BFS method to this implicit graph to identify contiguous regions (clusters
of adjacent vertices) having the same value. The function starts at an unvisited vertex with the desired
value (which is the material id). BFS explores its neighbors (from ‘neighbors_map’) and then the
neighbors of those neighbors, and so on, until all connected vertices with the desired value are visited.
This connected component represents one contiguous region in the vector. After one BFS traversal
completes, the method continues scanning the vector to find other unvisited vertices with the desired
value, thus potentially discovering other separate contiguous regions. The BFS process ensures that
all connected indices of a region are visited and marked, so they will not trigger another BFS iteration
in the main loop when changing the vertex.

 A more effective constraint is to enforce densely packed configurations. The key idea behind
the density constraint is to favor compact, dense configurations over dispersed, sparse ones during

optimization. As shown in Algorithm 4, this is achieved by calculating the density of cells of a given
material (e.g., coolant) within their bounding box. The bounding box is the smallest rectangle in 2D
that fully encloses all instances of that material in the domain. The density is then computed as the
ratio of the number of cells of the material inside this bounding box to the total number of cells within
the bounding box. A large bounding box containing few material cells yields a low density, while a
small tight bounding box around many material cells leads to high density. By driving the optimization
to configurations with better density, configurations with compact, rigid shapes are favored, hence the
cell density is maximized. This densification forces the optimizer to converge on tightly packed and
manufacturable shapes. Applying this density constraint, in addition to the previous ones, produces a
Cartesian approximation of the annular fuel shape, as shown in Figure 14a. Remarkably, this design
of the pin cell was obtained by applying increasingly tighter constraints in an iterative manner without
relying on physical intuition [25]. This may prove invaluable in cases where physical intuition is
lacking or immature, like in the case of advanced reactor designs.

4.2 Fuel Shuffling using the Shape Optimization Module

In this section we demonstrate the versatility of our new shape optimization module by applying it to
the core loading optimization problem. While this is not a geometric shape optimization, it is founded
on an analogous strategy of swapping the set of assemblies available at the refueling time with the
purpose of achieving set goals. This capability was missing from MOOSE, so the demonstration here
informs the community of users of its availability.

4.2.1 Description of the problem

Fuel shuffling optimization is an important capability for nuclear reactors. To demonstrate this process,
we utilize the IAEA-2D Benchmark geometry [26] as the initial loading pattern (LP) of the core to
represent a criticality problem. The initial configuration is depicted in Figure 15a, a quarter-core
two-dimensional model due to symmetries in the full model. This shuffling optimization falls under
topology optimization. It is worth noting then that in developing a general shape optimization module,
additional topology optimization capabilities are enabled as a byproduct.
 The IAEA-2D benchmark is based on a fresh core where all assemblies have identical fission cross
sections, Σ 𝑓 , and fission yield, 𝜈Σ 𝑓 , [26]. However, following an irradiation history, the refueling
engineer has available once-burned and twice-burned fuel assemblies, in addition to fresh fuel
assemblies, for constructing an optimal loading pattern. The burned assemblies will have different
isotopic composition, hence different cross sections from the fresh fuel assemblies. The IAEA-2D
benchmark does not have such burned fuel assemblies, so to accomplish the core loading pattern
optimization we make some assumptions on the nuclear data for the burned assemblies. First, we
designate assembly type 2 from this benchmark as the fresh fuel assembly type, while types 1 and 3
correspond to the once-burned and twice-burned assemblies, respectively. Second, we modify the
fresh fuel assemblies’ cross sections using tabulated 2-group cross sections as a function of burnup
[27, 28] to obtain representative nuclear data for the burned assembles to apply in assembly types 1
and 3. In general, one can expect approximately a 5% reduction in the thermal fission cross section
and a 5% increase in fast absorption cross section per irradiation cycle. Changes in diffusion and
scattering cross sections with burnup are also accounted for. The updated cross section values are
provided in Table 1. The number of assemblies, including their sub-type, will be similar to that of the

4.2.2 Optimization Process

The fuel shuffling optimization problem can be considered as a combinatorial optimization problem
in the design of the reactor core LP at the start of an irradiation cycle. Two of the main goals of this
optimization problem include maximizing 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 , to achieve a longer cycle and improve economic
operations, and minimizing the power peaking factors, for increased safety margins. The optimal
core loading is found by rearranging (i.e., shuffling) the fuel assemblies inside the reactor core and
repeatedly computing 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 and APPF for each attempted pattern using Moltres. Previous studies
[29–31], suggested imposing constraints on the permissible loading patters designed to avoid localized
power peaks and high radial power densities; this reduces the size of the search space, thus saving on
computational time. These constraints include:

 • Quarter symmetric LP.

 30

 • The center-assembly of the reactor core should always be a twice-burned fuel assembly type.

 • No neighboring fresh fuel assemblies are allowed in the core’s interior (i.e., away from the
 periphery).

 • The reflector assemblies material ID 4 that surround the active core’s periphery are not included
 in the optimization process. This is enabled by our developed shape optimization tool in which
 the user can specify material IDs that are excluded from optimization, thus anchoring those
 materials to their initial location within the domain.

 The optimized fuel loading pattern in Figure 16b exhibits topological features that are common in
low-leakage loading patterns, as characterized by Turinsky et al. [29]. Specifically, less reactive fuel
assemblies containing previously burned fuel are placed along the periphery of the core, particularly
in core locations that are two surfaces or tiles away from reflector surfaces, as described in [29].
This peripheral placement of depleted fuel minimizes neutron leakage from the core. The fresh
fuel assemblies are arranged in a ring of fire pattern one position inboard from the edges, and/or
interspersed in a checkerboard pattern among burned assemblies towards the interior of the core thus
reducing power peaking. The emergence of these well-established low-leakage loading characteristics
demonstrates the capability of the shape optimization tool developed here to produce fuel loading
patterns consistent with current practices. Inclusion of additional physics (e.g., thermal-hydraulics
feedback), while using a more advanced neutronics solver in MOOSE (e.g., Griffin) will help in
exploring additional LPs and even provide highly customizable LPs depending on the objective
function’s components. This feature results from our intentional design of the shape optimization tool
where the objective function is part of the postprocessor system of MOOSE, that is highly flexible
and offers the user a multitude of options to meet their optimization needs.

5 CONCLUSIONS

In this work we described the development and integration of a physics-based shape and topology
optimization tool into the advanced MOOSE multiphysics simulation framework. By leveraging
MOOSE’s modular software architecture and data transfer capabilities, we achieved seamless coupling
between optimization algorithms and various physics solvers in MOOSE. The optimization tool
implements efficient combinatorial and discrete optimization techniques enhanced with solution
caching, tabu lists, and multi-run restarts, and employs a Simulated Annealing search engine but other
engines can be incorporated into the modular structure of the optimizer. The main purpose of this

 We first verified the capabilities through simple heat transfer example where we are able to deter-
mine the global optimal configuration, in order to verify that our new tool converged to that solution.
This example showcased the functionality of the discrete and combinatorial optimization schemes.
We then demonstrated the utility of the shape optimization technique by more practical application
to neutronics simulation of a 2D pin cell, and to fuel shuffling optimization at the full core scale by
interfacing with different MOOSE physics modules. For the 2D pin cell, shape optimization generated
improved designs approximating annular fuel pins, efficiently utilizing materials to concurrently
maximize neutron multiplication and hydraulic diameter. At the core scale, fuel shuffling optimization
significantly boosted the multiplication factor by 1250 pcm while reducing power peaking by 30%
relative to the initial state, giving practically meaningful improvements in cycle length and thermal
margins. The optimized loading pattern exhibited established low-leakage core characteristics, es-
tablishing the tool’s capacity to make informed decisions targeting enhanced reactor performance.
While these examples are simplistic, perhaps even contrived, they successfully demonstrate the new
MOOSE capability. In other words, our focus here is on the new module, its main features including

requires a more focused study on the multiphysics that typically characterizes nuclear systems and a

to pursue some of these opportunities, informing the community of nuclear scientists and engineers
of this new capability in MOOSE is intended to broaden the application base and involving interested
developers to contribute to the underlying code.
 The ultimate goal of this work is to expand MOOSE’s capabilities to enable realistic shape and
topology optimization of reactor components, from individual fuel pins up to the full core level.
Integrating the tool with MOOSE’s extensive multiphysics simulation library provides new pathways
for coupled-physics optimization in future work. The current discrete mesh-based approach offers
reasonable geometry fidelity, but implementing spline-based continuous shape parameterizations
could enable smoother optimized shapes and enhanced simulation realism [6]. Beyond improving fi-

methodological enhancements could further augment the tool’s capabilities. Incorporating uncertainty
quantification will enable optimization under uncertainties in material properties, manufacturing tol-
erances, operational conditions, and model constraints. Incorporating additive manufacturing process
modeling will enhance manufacturability of optimized shapes. Expanding the optimization algorithms
beyond simulated annealing will provide more comprehensive and efficient exploration of the state
space. Pursuing these research ideas can transform the integrated shape and topology optimization tool
into a comprehensive framework for multiphysics simulation, design, and optimization of complex
reactor systems under uncertainty using the MOOSE simulation framework.

A Input File Structure of the Shape Optimization Module

The input file in MOOSE consists of different sections representing major systems in the framework,
each with a specific purpose. These systems include, among other things:

 • Mesh system: that covers anything related to the geometrical mesh and its generation in MOOSE

 • Multiapp system: that allows solving multiple physics applications simultaneously in a coupled
 manner

 • Executioner system: that controls the time stepping and solution algorithm settings

 • Kernel system: that defines the partial differential equations and constitutive models to solve

 • Postprocessor system: that computes additional quantities of interest from the solved physics
 variables during the simulation for visualization and analytics.

Each system enables assigning input data for particular aspects of the physics model in MOOSE in a
modular way, allowing complex multiphysics simulations. In this section, we will briefly describe
the shape optimization tool input file and we will show how these systems interact with one another
through the modular input file structure of the shape optimization module.

A.1 Structure of the Reporter Section in the Shape Optimization Input File

In single-physics shape optimization problems, at least two MOOSE input files should be provided
by the user specifically for the shape optimization process. The primary input file (referred to as
the main driver since it drives the shape optimization process) interfaces with the secondary input
file that encompasses the details of the physics, the mesh configuration, and the postprocessor to
evaluate the objective function. In the main driver input file, the user sets a ‘solve’ attribute to false,
indicating the absence of a specific physical problem to be resolved in this file. This implies that the
input file primarily serves as a driving input file rather than a physical problem solver. This is also
seen in the ‘[Mesh]’ section in this input file, where any mesh of the user’s choice can be defined.
This mesh section is not used at all in the optimization process but should be included since MOOSE
has obligatory sections to be incorporated in any MOOSE input file.

 35

 The exchange of data between the MultiApp system and the optimizer enables it to prepare
a new configuration based on current values of the objective function, the optimization scheme,
and user-selected constraints. The MultiApp system integrates various MOOSE apps that can also
be specific modules provided within the open-source MOOSE framework. Each app serves as the
primary interface for users to interact with MOOSE, either via an input file or directly through the
code. Between the MultiApp transfer system and the optimizer, there is another MOOSE system that
facilitates exchanging the required data, as seen in Figure 1. This Reporter system can be viewed
as an extension of both the Postprocessor and VectorPostprocessor systems. Unlike post-processors,
which declare only a single scalar value of type ‘Real’, and vector post-processors, which declare
multiple values but are restricted to the type ‘std::vector<Real>’, the Reporter system offers more
flexibility.
 Within the ‘[Reporters]’ section of this input file, we introduce a ‘[discrete_reporter]’
block which has a type attribute referring to the optimization reporter we added to MOOSE. Its
execution is triggered by ‘execute_on’ flags available in MOOSE to allow storing and reporting the
optimization data (e.g., configuration and objective function value). The reporter is the link between
the transfer system and the optimizer to set and get the desired data from this section.
 One might wonder, why not conduct data transfers directly through the MultiApp system, bypassing
the need for separate transfer/reporter systems? The answer lies in the object-oriented design principles
of the MOOSE framework. In object-oriented programming, systems can inherit properties from one
another, promoting modularity and reusability. Other key principles of object-oriented design include
encapsulation, polymorphism, and abstraction. In the context of MOOSE, using a separate transfer
system like the MultiApp Transfer System ensures that each system has a distinct responsibility,
adhering to the principle of single responsibility and encapsulation.

A.2 Structure of the Executioner Section in the Shape Optimization Input File

In the ‘[Executioner]’ section of the main driver, elements of the simulated annealing optimizer is
provided by the user. The attributes in the ‘[Executioner]’ section of the main driver revolve around
reading control parameters for the algorithm (like type of optimization, discrete or combinatorial, the
number of iterations per run and the number of runs, among others). The ability to restart the simulated
annealing process from the best found configuration as the initial configuration can be achieved using

 36

the number of runs attribute. If its value is larger than 1, then the code will run the optimization
algorithm for as many times as specified by this attribute. Every time a new run is initiated, the best
configuration from a previous run is used as the initial setup and the SA temperature is reset to its
maximum value that is set by the user via an attribute as explained below. This can be seen in the
initialization step in Algorithm 1. Since configurations generation is random, each run starts with
a different seed to add another layer of randomness to the search sequence. Specific attributes for
the SA algorithm (like cooling schedule, maximum and minimum temperatures, and others) are also
set in this ‘[Executioner]’ section. The maximum temperature attribute is the initial temperature
that the annealing process starts with. The minimum temperature is the target temperature where the
annealing process is terminated; it is used as an auxiliary stopping criterion for the search algorithm.
Finally, there is a debug flag, set in the input file, that allows the user or the developer to follow the
optimization process and debug problems if they arise. This flag will cause printing of a file containing
information about the optimization process, including all the optimization states acquired even if they
are not the best achieved per iteration.
 An additional attribute related to the applied constraints is used in the ‘[Executioner]’ section.
The code by default loops over all the objects in the constraints attribute of the ‘[Executioner]’
section, and a user can add as many constraints to this attribute as desired to control the generated
configuration. The names of these constraints originate from the ‘[UserObjects]’ section, where
they are distributed across various blocks. Every block corresponds to a distinct constraint class
that users can integrate into MOOSE. The constraints library in the optimizer is designed with
flexibility and extensibility in mind, following object-oriented programming principles in C++, as
well as MOOSE’s blocks concept that, also, follows C++ object oriented programming paradigms. It
has a base ‘ConstraintsLibraryBase’ class that provides essential data like current and previous
configurations, element neighbor maps, mesh dimensions, among others. Users can then derive
their own custom constraint classes from this base class. In these derived classes, users are able to
craft distinct functions that assess particular features of the produced configuration, which might be
informed, for example, by the preceding configuration or the material assigned to adjacent elements.
Each specialized constraint class then communicates a boolean value to the optimizer signaling
whether the present configuration satisfies the constraint. This modular structure allows easy addition
of new constraints without modifying the existing code. Each constraint should have a flag to activate

A.3 The MultiApps and the Transfer sections

The next input file section is the ‘[MultiApps]’ section which has a block labelled ‘[forward]’.
This block name is the name of the multiapp block where we transfer data from, or to, it in the
‘[Transfers]’ section. The simulation type (e.g., steady state or transient) is set by the ‘type’
attribute. Both transient and steady state simulations are supported by the shape optimization module,
by virtue of the tool’s roots in MOOSE, thus it can use any module or property defined in MOOSE.
The next attribute reads the name of the secondary input file application where the applicable physics
is defined. This application, or subapp in MOOSE jargon, holds the simulated physics of the system
undergoing optimization. This input file is similar to the ordinary input files of MOOSE where the
user can use any physics module available inside MOOSE (or even any MOOSE apps, which are third
party modules prepared by developers based on MOOSE). It can include any sections that involve
the basic structure of an input file in MOOSE. Finally, the ‘execute_on’ attribute defines when the
multiapp is solved and it is done when solving the forward problem.
 Transitioning to the ‘[Transfers]’ section, we have two defined transfer blocks: ‘[toforward]’
and ‘[fromforward]’. Both transfers involve the ‘discrete_reporter’ block from the ‘[Reporters]’
section as the reporter used during the transfers. The names of the transfers block are arbitrary
and are used to show the direction of the transfer as implied by the ‘to_multi_app’ and the
‘from_multi_app’ attributes. Both attributes refer to the ‘[forward]’ block subapp from the t[MultiApps]
section. The transfer type for both transfers is based on the newly implemented shape optimization
transfer class. The execution of the transfers happens initially at the simulation outset and then
subsequently with each forward system problem’s solution. The goal of the initial execution is to
facilitate the mesh information transfer (e.g., element ids and neighbors’ information) and initialize the
reporter system’s configuration. There is also a debug attribute, which when activated, offers a verbose
output displaying the objective function’s value, the transfer’s current iteration, and other information
including the current configuration used in the physics solver. Lastly, the ‘objective_name’ attribute
carries the postprocessor’s name used to retrieve the objective function’s value for a given configu-
ration. Users should set this variable to the name of the objective function, which corresponds to a
postprocessor quantity. Specifically, the name used for a postprocessor in the physics input file must
match the value assigned to the ‘objective_name’ variable. While MOOSE provides a variety of
built-in postprocessors, users can also define custom postprocessors to compute specific quantities of

 39

interest that might be unique to their problem, and allowing each component of the objective function
to have its own postprocessor block (or even soft constraints if required). If a user did not, or forgot,
to assign the ‘objective_name’ variable, the code will assume the objective function name to be
‘objective_function’ and try to get the value associated with a similar postprocessor in the physics
input file. The name of the objective function is important as the code can get the value associated
with the name using a MOOSE’s method labelled ‘getPostprocessorValueByName’ (essentially
getting the objective function value needed by the optimizer).
 With this, a complete description of the optimization driver’s input file is given and we can then
proceed to the applications where we use the second input file to flexibly define the physics of the
problem being simulated and the objective function to be optimized in the search.

 ABSTRACT

 Engineering systems design often relies on simulation-based shape
 optimization to achieve performance goals. However, uncertainties inherent in
 material properties, manufacturing tolerances, operating conditions, and
 optimization algorithms can significantly impact optimization outcomes.
 Rigorously analyzing and propagating these uncertainties through the
 optimization process is critical but methodologically challenging. This work
 develops an integrated framework linking DAKOTA and MOOSE to enable
 comprehensive uncertainty quantification and propagation in physics-based
 shape optimization that has been recently implemented in MOOSE. Material
 properties are modeled as random input variables. DAKOTA utilizes sampling
 techniques like Monte Carlo and Latin Hypercube to propagate input
 uncertainties through MOOSE to key performance metrics. Correlation and
 sensitivity analyses in DAKOTA quantify input-output relationships to
 characterize the impacts of input variability on optimization results. The
 linkage provides an extensible platform for engineering design under
 uncertainty. The integrated DAKOTA/MOOSE framework is demonstrated on
 a multiphysics pin-cell shape optimization problem under uncertainties. The
 annular pin-cell shape emerged as the optimal shape from the optimization
 process, and under variable material properties and pin power values. The
 maximum temperature in the optimal shape was reduced by 50%, while an
 increase of 1000 pcm was observed for the multiplication factor. The
 optimization process under uncertainty and variable operating conditions has
 shown the annular shape as an optimal design that is reliable and robust.
 Follow-on work will focus on expanding the uncertainty analysis to encompass
 additional uncertain factors and further leveraging DAKOTA/MOOSE
 capabilities. The integrated approach will provide a powerful toolset for design
 optimization under complex real-world uncertainties.

1. INTRODUCTION
Mathematical optimization, or mathematical programming [1], is a method that has its root in
operations research (OR), a scientific discipline dedicated to developing and applying
analytical methods to improve decision-making and management. In mathematical
optimization, an optimization problem consists of maximizing or minimizing an objective
function by systematically choosing input values from within an allowed set (usually this
allowance is hindered by constraints). This systematic choice can be done analytically, like in
OR, or heuristically using dedicated search algorithms (e.g., simulated annealing) that
explores the search space of the involved domain’s variables. Optimization problems,

 1

Best Estimate Plus Uncertainty International Conference (BEPU 2024) BEPU2024-xxx
Real Collegio, Lucca, Tuscany, Italy, May 19 – 24, 2024

formulated as mathematical search problems, can be divided into two general categories:
Discrete optimization and continuous optimization, depending on whether the variables
involved are discrete or continuous, respectively. Under optimization problems, shape
optimization has emerged as a critical methodology in engineering design and computational
modeling across industries. The technique involves finding the optimal topology and
geometry of an object, system, or structure that maximizes performance under certain
constraints. In nuclear applications for instance, shape optimization can lead to improved fuel
utilization efficiency in light water reactors by optimizing the design of fuel pins and
shuffling configurations.

However, the optimal shape designs generated by shape optimization tools can be sensitive to
uncertainties in input parameters, which can lead to suboptimal or even infeasible designs if
uncertainties are ignored. To address this challenge, uncertainty quantification (UQ) can be
coupled with shape optimization to propagate uncertainties and assess their impact on the
optimal designs. This enables optimization under uncertainty (OUU) for enhanced reliability
and for generating robust optimal shapes that perform well across the range of possible
operating conditions. OUU analysis, including for shape optimization, has been around since
the early 2000s as can be seen in the large literature dedicated to the subject (see, e.g., [2-4],
to name a few), but was not explored in a nuclear engineering setting according to the
authors’ knowledge.

In this work, we develop a framework that links the Design Analysis Kit for Optimization and
Terascale Applications (DAKOTA) UQ software [5] with the Multiphysics Object-Oriented
Simulation Environment (MOOSE) framework [6] for multiphysics simulation and its recent
discrete shape optimization module that we developed in MOOSE. As described in Section 2,
this linkage enables seamless propagation of different uncertainties through the base
multiphysics MOOSE application during the shape optimization process. In Section 3, we
demonstrate application of this tool by optimizing the shape of a pin-cell in a traditional light
water reactor (LWR), while considering objective functions and constraints based on
underlying multiphysics phenomena and accounting for relevant uncertainties. The results
highlight the importance of using UQ-enabled shape optimization over deterministic
optimization (i.e., without uncertainties) to obtain robust optimal designs. Finally, Section 4
summarizes conclusions and discusses avenues for future work to apply this coupled UQ-
shape optimization capability more broadly for continuous shape optimization across nuclear
and non-nuclear applications.

2. FRAMEWORK FOR SHAPE-OUU USING MOOSE AND DAKOTA

2.1 DAKOTA Part of the Framework
At the core of the Shape-OUU framework is a DAKOTA input file which configures and
triggers the uncertainty quantification analysis. The DAKOTA input file references a Python
driver script that coordinates the workflow between DAKOTA, MOOSE, and the supporting
pre/post-processing scripts. A user first prepares templates of MOOSE’s inputs used in the
UQ process (e.g., a shape optimization example input file template) by using descriptors in
lieu of the variables’ values to be perturbed. During the UQ analysis, each descriptor will be
updated with a sampled value from the probability distribution for that specific variable. The
update is performed by the pre-processing Python script and the parameters file generated by
DAKOTA based on the sampling of the input parameters.

 2

Best Estimate Plus Uncertainty International Conference (BEPU 2024) BEPU2024-xxx
Real Collegio, Lucca, Tuscany, Italy, May 19 – 24, 2024

DAKOTA is powered by an extensible array of sampling and analysis methods making it a
versatile tool. Specifically, it can account for both epistemic uncertainties, due to lack of
knowledge, and aleatory uncertainties inherent due to randomness. To analyze the impacts of
both epistemic uncertainty and intrinsic aleatory uncertainty, DAKOTA utilizes a nested
sampling method that has been described in detail in reference [7]. This involves an outer and
inner sampling loop to separately propagate the two uncertainty types while capturing their
interactions. In the outer loop, a sampling method (e.g., Latin Hypercube) is applied to vary
epistemic input parameters across their possibility distributions in a set of intervals. Then, for
each possibility, the inner loop draws samples of aleatory input variables based on inherent
probability distributions. This propagates stochastic variability through the performance
simulations for each instance. Propagating these aleatory input uncertainties produces
confidence intervals and distributions reflecting output performance variability. A key benefit
of this nested sampling approach is that it generates probability bounds that show the impact
of epistemic uncertainties on reliability, even in the presence of inherent aleatory variability.
By aggregating statistics like percentile confidence intervals across the outer scenario
samples, this method produces a probability box bounded by the most extreme outcomes
resulting from the uncertain inputs. This probability box visualizes the combined effect of
both types of uncertainty on the outcomes of interest.

DAKOTA executes many simulations perturbing variables according to the specified
distributions in the input file. DAKOTA prepares different working directories equal to the
number of samples requested by the user in DAKOTA’s input file. Following the substitution
of the descriptors by sampled values in each working directory, the Python driver script then
runs the perturbed MOOSE shape optimization example using MOOSE specific apps or
modules. Following the successful shape optimization process carried out by MOOSE, a post
processing script processes MOOSE optimization results and prints them out into a specific
file (defined in DAKOTA’s input file) for DAKOTA to analyze as the responses of the
system that provide insights into both optimality and reliability of the modeled shape.
DAKOTA performs statistical analyses on these results to understand the impact of
uncertainties on the system’s performance. The modularity of the framework facilitates
tailoring to new problems, and it does not have to be only coupled to the shape optimization
tool in MOOSE. This framework is depicted in Figure 1.

2.2 Shape Optimization tool in MOOSE
The recently developed optimization tool enables both combinatorial and discrete shape
optimization to be performed within the flexible MOOSE simulation framework. The tool has
a modular structure consisting of several key components that work together to explore the
design space as shown in Figure 2. The central controller is the Optimizer Class that hosts the
optimization algorithm responsible for generating new system configurations. The current
implementation utilizes a simulated annealing algorithm enhanced with additional techniques
like solution caching, tabu lists, and multi-run restart approach to improve performance. As
new configurations are produced, they are passed to the physics simulation application to
compute the associated objective function value. This creates a reinforcement learning
pattern where the optimizer adapts its search based on the feedback from the physical
simulation.

 Figure 2: Process Chart of the Discrete and Combinatorial Shape Optimization Tool
 Developed in the MOOSE Framework.

Facilitating the data flow between the optimizer and physics simulation is the MultiApp
component. The MultiApp manages the transfer of configurations and objective values
between the distinct applications. This is assisted by the Reporter system which acts as an
intermediary for data exchange. Finally, the Transfer system handles the initialization and
passing of essential mesh data and configurations. Together, these components enable
seamless integration of the optimizer with various physics simulations. MOOSE provides
inter-application communication systems like MultiApp to enable this integrated optimization
approach. MOOSE’s design enabled developing the tool in a modular fashion.

A key capability of the tool is its flexibility to accommodate user-defined constraints. The
Constraints System allows restricting the configurations generated by the optimizer according
to physics requirements, manufacturability, or other metrics. Users can develop custom
constraint classes that hook into the optimization loop to judge each new configuration. This
level of customization enables the tool to tackle real-world optimization problems with
complex constraints. The modularity of the tool is a result of following sound object-oriented
programming principles. Each component has a single responsibility, and the physics
simulation application is cleanly abstracted away. This encapsulation and polymorphism
enhance the tool with new optimization algorithms, data transfer methods, and applications
without needing to restructure the existing code. Object-oriented principles used extensively
in MOOSE empower this customizability and extensibility.

3. DEMONSTRATION OF THE SHAPE-OUU FRAMEWORK

3.1 Description of the Simulated Problem
In this example, we will demonstrate the neutronics simulation of a pin-cell while
concurrently applying the heat conduction equation and considering heat convection to the
cell’s coolant. The cross-sections will be temperature dependent and the MOOSE-based
Moltres multiphysics neutron diffusion code [8] will be used to simulate the C5 benchmark
UO2 unit-cell model (which is the basis for the C5G7 benchmark [9]), having a homogenized
fuel pin of radius 0.54 cm (with roughly 58% fuel content), and a typical pin-cell pitch of 1.26
cm. OpenMC [10], a continuous-energy and multigroup Monte Carlo neutron transport code,
will be used to generate the cross-sections of the unit-cell in 2 groups and in a broad
temperature range covering 600-2500 K to account for the full range of expected operating
states. k eff for the base C5 benchmark’s UO2 infinite array of pin-cells, computed using
OpenMC and for the 300K state, is found to be 1.32572 +/- 0.00013 which aligns with the
values range for the participants’ results [11]. Moltres has its own script that leverages
OpenMC’s dedicated mgxs class to generate the cross-sections. We had to modify this script
to work with the latest version of OpenMC (v0.14.0) and to generate the required cross-
sections for the problem. All the cross-sections can be tallied using OpenMC except for the
removal cross-section which is computed from the absorption and the out-scattering cross-
sections for both energy groups. The k eff value computed by Moltres using two energy groups
is 1.33286, a 714 pcm difference from the OpenMC value. This difference could be justified
further noting that the discrete Cartesian approximation of the cylindrical fuel pin does not
exactly preserve the homogenized fuel material content as seen in Figure 3.

an Epistemic uncertainty source. The interval for such epistemic uncertainty source for the
pin-cell simulated in this research will be [130, 500] W, consistent with the literature range of

MOOSE.

For this purpose, we implemented a MultiAppMeshTransfer C++ class into the base MOOSE

the daughter subapp (i.e., the neutronics subapp) is done in the MultiApps section of the
thermal hydraulics subapp. Additional transfers include transferring back and forth the
required coupled variables for each subapp (e.g., volumetric heat source and the temperature
distribution). Such variables transfer between both subapps is facilitated with the
MultiAppProjectionTransfer class in MOOSE. The final transfer carried out is for the
postprocessor value of k eff from the neutronics subapp where it is computed. The
MultiAppPostprocessorTransfer helps in transferring the required postprocessor to a new
postprocessor inside the thermal hydraulics subapp, where the optimizer will be able to
capture its value and use it in the objective function computation. To model the heat
convection boundary condition on the cells surfaces, we implemented a dedicated C++ class
in MOOSE labelled CoupledConvectiveHeatFluxBC_Mod which inherits from the
CoupledConvectiveHeatFluxBC base MOOSE class. This allows applying convective BCs
during the optimization process when the mesh’s subdomain ids change with new subdomain
assignment.

The DAKOTA input file has two types of analysis blocks, one considered as an outer loop
(for epistemic sources of uncertainty) while the other is considered as an inner loop (for
aleatory sources of uncertainty) to propagate these input uncertainties into the output. For the
epistemic uncertainty, which pertains to variability with unknown probabilities due to a lack
of knowledge, a sample size of 60 was selected. This number was deemed sufficient to
capture the range of possible scenarios for the uncertain power levels, which are treated as a
systematic source of uncertainty. On the other hand, for the aleatory uncertainty, which comes

from inherent randomness and natural variability in the thermal conductivities, a larger
sample size of 100 was used for each epistemic sample. This leads to a total of 6,000 aleatory
samples across all the epistemic scenarios. Such numbers are chosen as an application of
Wilk’s formula, which is a statistical method for determining the sample size necessary to
achieve a specified confidence level in the results [19]. According to Wilk’s formula, the
chosen number of samples assure us with a 99% confidence level for the aleatory results that
the statistical tolerance bound will cover 95% of the multiplication factor or maximum
temperature population values (i.e., those that are within ± 2σ from the mean). For the
epistemic uncertainties, a confidence level of 95% is anticipated due to the reduced sample
size. This 95% confidence level, with the number of samples used, implies that there is a 95%
probability the real-world values of the epistemic uncertainties lie within the range derived
from our epistemic samples. This level of statistical confidence provides robust support for
the reliability of the shape optimization results under the specified uncertainties, although we
will seek larger number of samples in future research.

For each of the 6,000 samples, DAKOTA executed the MOOSE shape optimization tool to
register the response functions. The responses registered by DAKOTA are the objective
function value, the multiplication factor, and the maximum temperature in the domain. The
last two responses are also components of the objective function. Inside the MOOSE input
file, an option to print postprocessors values into CSV files is available and we used it to
generate a file that contains the values of the responses following the completion of the
optimization process. A dedicated python script that we developed extracts the response
values from the CSV file and prints them out into a DAKOTA results file for DAKOTA to
accumulate the responses’ data and use it for statistical analysis. Mapping of the responses
from the inner aleatory analysis to the outer epistemic analysis was done inside DAKOTA’s
input file for the three responses. Although DAKOTA will only provide the mean and
standard deviation for each 100 samples (which correspond to a single power level or value)
for nested uncertainty analysis (i.e., epistemic-aleatory uncertainty analysis), but this cannot
be used alone to get more valuable statistical information for the responses like the
corresponding cumulative distribution functions (CDF). Instead, we had to extract the 6,000
samples results from DAKOTA’s general output file to analyze the data using empirical CDF
that uses the generated results to build the CDF.

Each simulation of the 6,000 cases consisted of a multirun with 40 runs, 25 iterations per run,
plus an initial run of 3,000 iterations. This adds up to 4,000 iterations of the simulated
annealing algorithm per simulation (which includes configuration generation and objective
function computation through the multiphysics model simulation). The simulation results
have shown that some of the samples resulted in shapes that did not satisfy the optimization
function penalty included in Equation 8. These samples had an absolute objective function
value less than 1, and hence have been discarded from the sampling process. The number of
such samples varied for each 100 sample but were less than 20, resulting in a maximum of 80
or higher samples that satisfy the objective function constraint (which, according to Wilk’s
formula, provides 95% confidence). Figure 9 shows histograms of the maximum temperature
output at different pin powers, which generally shows the output distribution is normally
distributed as seen by the respective normal distribution curves made using each data mean
and standard deviation.

4. CONCLUSION
In this work, we have developed an integrated framework linking the DAKOTA uncertainty
quantification software with the MOOSE multiphysics framework to enable uncertainty
propagation in physics-based shape optimization. The framework leverages DAKOTA's
sampling capabilities to rigorously analyze aleatory and epistemic uncertainties while using
MOOSE's high-fidelity simulations to accurately evaluate performance. We demonstrated
application of this tool by optimizing a nuclear pin cell design under uncertainties in the
thermal conductivity of materials and the pin power. The optimization maximized the
multiplication factor while minimizing the maximum temperature, constrained by practical
manufacturability requirements. Applying this shape optimization under uncertainty, an
annular fuel pin design emerged which reduced the maximum temperature by nearly 50%
compared to the initial solid pin configuration and increased the multiplication factor by about
1,000 pcm.

We showed that the annular shape is robustly optimal across the range of material properties
and pin powers analyzed. Probability bounds from the uncertainty quantification verify the
annular pin reliability even when accounting for aleatory variability. This integrated approach
for design optimization under uncertainty provides a powerful platform for developing
performance-maximizing and reliable engineering components.

Several opportunities exist for enhancing this analysis framework and expanding its
implementation. Explicitly coupling computational fluid dynamics (CFD) into the
multiphysics simulations will capture coolant flow patterns and heat transfer that could further
improve the optimized shapes. CFD modelling of the coolant instead of a fixed temperature
reservoir may alter the balance between maximum fuel temperature and neutronics
performance. Furthermore, additional uncertain factors can be incorporated, such as
manufacturing tolerances on geometry and homogenized cross sections sensitive to the
optimized configurations. Propagating these additional uncertainties will delineate their
relative impacts. Moreover, the developed integrated tool can be applied to continuous
geometry changes for enhanced design flexibility. This will expand the reliability analysis to a
broader category of candidate shapes.

Fuzzy set theory and fuzzy arithmetic can capture subjective or linguistic uncertainties by
using fuzzy numbers rather than probability distributions to describe input parameters. By
propagating these fuzzy input parameters through high-fidelity MOOSE simulations, more
comprehensive uncertainty quantification can be performed when inputs are ambiguous. This
ambiguity is due to epistemic uncertainties that arise when we have knowledge limitations
about the appropriate probability distributions for inputs or due to imprecise data or vague
operating conditions. Optimization under uncertainty can then identify robust designs across
the possibilities represented by the fuzzy inputs. Another type of analysis like evidence
theory, which uses belief and plausibility measures and is integrated in DAKOTA, can also

 Abstract

 This paper presents the development and application of a continuous shape optimization
module within the MOOSE multiphysics simulation framework, tailored for nuclear engineering
applications. We demonstrate the module’s capabilities through a comprehensive study of annular
fuel pin-cell optimization, incorporating coupled neutronics and thermal-hydraulics simulations.
The optimization process, utilizing the TAO toolkit from PETSc with a Nelder-Mead algorithm,
reveals significant improvements in both neutron multiplication and thermal performance. Our
analysis explores the impact of various objective functions and feedback mechanisms, high-
lighting the importance of comprehensive multiphysics modeling in reactor design optimization.
When incorporating both geometrical and thermal feedbacks, we achieve realistic improvements
of 400-600 pcm in 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 and 30-50K reduction in maximum temperature. Furthermore, we in-
troduce a novel DAKOTA-MOOSE interface for uncertainty quantification, providing robust
shape optimization under uncertainty. Both thermal conductivities and linear power uncertainties
were propagated during the shape optimization process. This approach not only enhances design
resilience but also quantifies the uncertainty in the expected economic benefits. By applying
uncertainty quantification to the economic value derived from fuel area reduction, we can provide
a more robust assessment of potential savings. Our optimized designs consistently achieve a
14-17% reduction in fuel area while maintaining or improving performance, with quantifiable
confidence in the associated economic outcomes. The optimization MOOSE module, coupled
with uncertainty quantification, provides a robust framework for exploring innovative fuel designs
that could contribute to the next generation of more efficient and safer nuclear reactors.

1 Introduction

The Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) is a powerful open-source
framework designed to facilitate the development of complex multiphysics simulations [1]. Mainly built
upon the finite element method (FEM), MOOSE provides a modular and extensible platform for solving
a wide range of engineering and scientific problems, including heat transfer, fluid dynamics, solid
mechanics, and neutron physics [2]. The framework’s object-oriented design and advanced software
architecture enable users to create robust and efficient simulations with minimal programming effort
[1]. MOOSE is built around several modules that can interact with each other through the multiapp
and the transfer systems inside the MOOSE framework. One of these modules is the optimization
module that extends the framework’s capabilities to solve optimization problems seamlessly within
multiphysics simulations [3]. This module leverages the Toolkit for Advanced Optimization (TAO)
from the Portable, Extensible Toolkit for Scientific Computation (PETSc), providing access to a wide
range of optimization algorithms.
 Optimization plays a crucial role in various engineering and scientific applications, allowing re-
searchers and practitioners to find optimal solutions to complex problems. By integrating optimization
capabilities into multiphysics simulations, engineers can design more efficient systems, minimize
costs, and improve performance. In the context of nuclear engineering, continuous shape optimization
of reactor components, particularly at the fuel pin level, offers significant potential for enhancing
reactor performance and safety. The primary interest of this paper is the design and shape optimization
of nuclear reactor components, with a specific focus on annular fuel pin-cell design. The paper will
explore how MOOSE’s optimization module can be used for continuous shape optimization in nuclear
engineering aspect. In continuous shape optimization, the optimal shape or geometry of a structure
or component is sought while maintaining a continuous representation of the design space. In this
approach, the shape of the object is parameterized using continuous functions or variables, and the
optimization algorithm seeks to find the values of these parameters that minimize or maximize an
objective function, that can be subjected to given constraints.
 A key aspect of this work is the consideration of both neutronics and thermal-hydraulics in the
optimization process. This multiphysics approach allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of
fuel pin performance, capturing the complex interplay between neutronic efficiency and thermal

 3

management. Furthermore, we explore the impact of different feedback mechanisms, including both
thermal and geometrical effects on cross-sections, providing a more realistic representation of the
nuclear system behavior during the optimization process.
 We start by presenting a comprehensive overview of the continuous shape optimization module
in MOOSE, highlighting its key features, capabilities, and applications to nuclear fuel pin design.
We begin by describing the methodology, including the continuous shape optimization process and
its coupling with neutronics and thermal-hydraulics simulations. This sets the foundation for our
exploration of shape optimization in the chosen 2D annular pin-cell example. We then delve into a
series of optimization studies, investigating various scenarios and objective functions. These studies
demonstrate the module’s ability to handle complex, multiphysics optimization problems in nuclear
engineering. We explore how different formulations of the objective function impact the optimization
outcomes, providing insights into the trade-offs involved in nuclear reactor design.
 Shape optimization tools can generate designs highly sensitive to input parameter uncertainties,
potentially leading to suboptimal or impractical outcomes when these uncertainties are not considered.
To mitigate this, uncertainty quantification (UQ) can be integrated with shape optimization, allowing
for the assessment of uncertainty impacts on optimal designs. This integration enables optimization
under uncertainty (OUU), enhancing reliability and producing robust designs that perform well across
various operating conditions. Our work then introduces a novel framework that combines the Design
Analysis Kit for Optimization and Terascale Applications (DAKOTA) UQ software [4] with the
MOOSE framework. This integration leverages MOOSE’s multiphysics simulation capabilities and
the continuous shape optimization module, creating a powerful tool for robust design optimization
in nuclear engineering applications. This allows us to account for uncertainties in key parameters
such as thermal conductivities and power density. We present an analysis of how these uncertainties
affect the optimization process and the resulting designs, highlighting the importance of considering
variability in reactor operating conditions.
 Throughout the paper, we discuss the economic implications of shape optimization in nuclear
reactor design, considering how changes in fuel geometry might impact costs and efficiency. This
analysis provides a broader context for the potential impact of advanced optimization techniques in
the nuclear industry.

2 Description of the Continuous Shape Optimization MOOSE
 Tool

Shape optimization is a field of engineering that seeks to find the optimal shape or design of a com-
ponent or structure based on specific performance criteria. It has become increasingly important in
various industries, such as aerospace, automotive, manufacturing, and recently nuclear [5], where
optimized designs can lead to improved efficiency, reduced costs, and enhanced performance. The con-
tinuous shape optimization capability in the optimization module is built upon a set of interconnected
classes and functions that work together to define and parameterize shape optimization problems
involving mesh deformation or displacement.
 To solve an optimization problem, one needs to formulate the underlying physics problem first into
MOOSE’s input file system and all the associated systems (e.g., multiapps system for a multiphysics
case). Alongside the problem formulation, the optimizer algorithm that will be used for the optimization
should also be setup. For the optimization process itself to start, the optimizer will need the objective
function, parameters to optimize, and any physical constraints employed on the physics system
being solved and optimized (e.g., constant fuel volume). Those are first set inside the optimization
driver which is a MOOSE’s input file that contains sections covering different classes related to the
optimization module in MOOSE. Figure 1 shows an example of the main sections for the optimization
driver. In the next sections, we will delve more into how to setup those parts especially for the
continuous shape optimization process.

2.1 The Optimization Algorithms

The first section inside the optimization driver in Figure 1 is the executioner or the optimizer section.
This section contains the optimizer or an optimization algorithm (from those algorithms available)
that will be used in the optimization process. These algorithms are based on the TAO toolkit of
the PETSc library that is linked to the MOOSE framework. This section inside the input file is
based on the OptimizeSolve MOOSE class inside the optimization module. This class acts as a
high-level coordinator for the optimization process, handling the initialization of the optimization
solver, providing initial conditions, and managing the update of design variables. It serves as an
interface between the MOOSE framework and the TAO library, enabling users to access a wide range
of optimization algorithms and techniques available through TAO.
 TAO offers a comprehensive collection of optimization solvers, including gradient-based methods
and derivative-free algorithms. These solvers are designed to handle different types of optimization

 6

problems, such as unconstrained optimization, bound-constrained optimization, and nonlinear least
squares problems. For all optimization algorithms available in TAO, the OptimizeSolve class enables
users to select the optimizer solver and fine-tune its settings, such as the initial step length and the
maximum number of optimization evaluations.

2.2 The Optimization Reporter Class

To facilitate the optimization process, the OptimizeSolve class interacts with the reporter class
OptimizationReporter to obtain the necessary information, such as the objective function values,
gradients, and constraints. The OptimizationReporter class acts as a bridge between the MOOSE
framework and the optimization solver, providing the required data and computations. This is seen
in the second section of the optimization driver input file in Figure 1 where information about
the parameter to be optimized (e.g., an outer radius of a fuel pin) is provided through this section
including the objective function label or name. The OptimizeSolve class communicates with the
OptimizationReporter to retrieve this information and passes it to the TAO solver, enabling the
optimization algorithm to make informed decisions and update the design variables accordingly.
 The objective function is used to direct the optimization process and it is based on physical
quantities that are inferred from the problem that the user sets up. This can include any quantity
computable by the physics solver, as well as those that can be computed by the user based on some
postprocessor in the postprocessors system in MOOSE (e.g., integral or average values of physical
quantities). This flexibility in objective function computation leads to a wide range of applicability for
the optimization framework in MOOSE. Users can define their desired optimization goals based on the
specific requirements of their problem. For instance, in a heat transfer problem, the objective function
could be to minimize the maximum temperature or to achieve a uniform temperature distribution
inside the domain.
 Furthermore, the OptimizationReporter class handles the evaluation of optimization con-
straints. Constraints are conditions that must be satisfied by the optimal solution to ensure its feasibil-
ity and practicality. The OptimizationReporter class defines methods to compute the values of
equality and inequality constraints based on the current design configuration. These constraint values
are used by the optimization solver to enforce the specified limitations and guide the search towards
feasible solutions.

2.3 Transfer of the Optimization Data

To facilitate the transfer of values between the optimization driver and the physical model, the MOOSE
framework employs the MultiApp system. This system establishes a bidirectional link between the
optimization driver and the physical model being solved, enabling seamless communication and
data exchange. The setup of the main subapp and the reporter transfers is as typically defined in the
third and fourth sections of the optimization driver input file as shown in Figure 1. The main subapp
contains the parameters to be optimized and defines the objective function, including any associated
constraints. The parameters are exposed to the optimization driver through the use of reporter transfers,
which allow for the exchange of data between the optimization driver and the physical model.
 The reporter transfers operate in a bidirectional manner, facilitating the flow of information both
from the optimization driver to the physical model and vice versa. In the outgoing direction, the
optimization driver sends the updated values of the optimized parameters to the physical model. These
updated parameters are then used by the physical solver to compute a new solution and evaluate the
corresponding objective function value. Once the physical solver has completed its computation, the
updated objective function value is transferred back to the optimization driver through the reporter
transfers. This feedback mechanism provides the optimization driver with the necessary information to
assess the impact of the current parameter values on the physical model’s behavior. The optimization
driver then employs its chosen optimization algorithm to determine the next set of parameter values to
explore. The specific optimization algorithm used may vary depending on the nature of the problem and
the presence of constraints. For constrained optimization problems, the algorithm takes into account the
defined constraints while generating the new parameter values. The optimization driver then transfers
these new parameter values back to the physical model using the reporter transfers, completing the
feedback loop. The physical solver app receives the updated parameters and incorporates them into
the subsequent iteration of the solution process.
 This iterative process continues, with the optimization driver proposing new parameter values
based on the feedback received from the physical model, and the physical model providing updated
objective function values in return as well as evaluated constraints. The optimization algorithm guides
the search for the optimal parameter values, gradually refining the solution until a satisfactory result
is achieved or a predefined termination criterion is met.

2.4 Continuous Shape Optimization Process

The computational domain is discretized into elements which possess a volume and are bounded by
faces. At the most basic level, elements are defined by a collection of nodes (i.e., points). Elements
can be grouped together to form blocks (i.e., a collection of elements is a block). Similarly, element
faces can be grouped together to form sidesets (i.e., a collection of element faces is a sideset). As
elements and thus faces are made up of nodes, so are blocks and sidesets. Sidesets can be used to
define the boundary between two blocks in space (e.g., the boundary between fluid and solid domains
of an airfoil or fuel and coolant in a light water reactor pin cell). In FEM models, sidesets are typically
used to impose boundary conditions. We solve a physics problem for the set of variables 𝜙 that is

3 Nuclear Engineering Application of the Continuous Shape Op-
 timization in MOOSE

3.1 Model Description

In the previous section we have explored and defined the process of the continuous shape optimization
module in MOOSE. As a verification of the implementation, we will utilize the C5 benchmark UO2
pin-cell design – which has a similar UO2 pin-cell design of the C5G7 benchmark. We employed this
design earlier for combinatorial shape optimization [6] and this pin-cell model is shown in Figure 2 for
its discrete and continuous representation. The model is of a homogenized fuel pin of radius 0.54 cm
with roughly 58% fuel content and a pin-cell pitch of 1.26 cm. The multiphysics combinatorial shape
optimization process yielded a new design for the pin-cell that is annular-like – since it is in discrete
representation. The inner radius was computed as 0.2 cm, while the outer radius remained at 0.54 cm
and the pin-cell pitch remained the same at 1.26 cm as it was not involved in the optimization process
[7]. This annular pin-cell design will be the starting point for our continuous shape optimization in
this section. This can be considered as a type of hierarchical shape optimization; one optimization
result – in the current case from combinatorial shape optimization [7] – is further optimized in a
different optimization setting, e.g., using continuous shape optimization. Other types of hierarchical
shape optimization can include multiscale optimization in which we use the optimized pin-cell in
an assembly and re-optimize the pin-cell as well as the whole assembly, which we can also add to a
whole core for core-level optimization. Such type of hierarchical shape optimization will be explored
in future works.

 The physics involved in the optimization process include neutronics and thermal hydraulics
(TH). The thermal hydraulics include heat conduction in the solid fuel of the annular pin-cell, with
convection occurring at the inner and outer surfaces of the annular pin-cell facing the moderator water
and assuming a moderator constant temperature of 600 K. Thermal feedback on the neutron cross
sections is also taken into account, and cross sections are parameterized according to the temperature
of the pin-cell domain. since in the optimization process both the inner radius and outer radius of the
annular pin-cell model are treated as optimizable parameters, the cross sections are also parameterized
according to the value of each radius during the optimization process. This is important to consider –
as will be seen in the next section – since this continuous optimization process will change both the
moderating power and the fuel volume, which will have a feedback effect on the multiplication factor
of the pin-cell. For the neutronics, we applied reflective boundary conditions on the pin-cell outer
boundaries; the pin-cell pitch is not included in the optimization. For the TH, adiabatic boundary
conditions are applied at the outer boundaries of the square pin-cell. For both the inner and outer radii,
a convection boundary condition was applied as stated earlier. Both the neutronics and the TH are
solved and simulated in the same multiapp in MOOSE. This multiapp is connected directly to the
optimization driver through the MultiApps system of MOOSE.
 For deformation of the inner and outer radii, the physics described by Equation (5) is simulated by
a dedicated subapp in MOOSE; transfer of the displacements to the main multiphysics parent app is
done using the Transfer system of MOOSE . The initial mesh of both the parent multiphysics subapp
and the displacements computation subapp are the same; mesh gets updated using the displaced
mesh option in MOOSE using the displacements computed by the dedicated subapp. As explained in

Section 2.4, the sidesets are used to define the moving boundaries. The square defining the pin-cell
is treated as a fixed sideset 𝜕Ω𝑒 – cf. Section 2.4 – and will not deform, while the inner and outer
surfaces of the annular pin-cell will each be defined as a deformable sideset – i.e., they belong to S
(cf. Section 2.4). This can be done during the meshing portion either using MOOSE’s Mesh system

3.3.1 Optimization considering temperature feedback only

Temperature distribution can have an effect on the optimization process through two mechanisms.
In the first mechanism, according to Equation (13), the maximum temperature within the domain is
a parameter of the optimization function in which it is desirable to minimize the peak temperature
which in turn increases the thermal margin – the difference between the maximum temperature and the
system’s safety limits. This also enhances overall safety by providing a larger buffer against potential
temperature excursions. In the second mechanism, the domain-averaged temperature serves as a
feedback parameter for adjusting nuclear cross-sections. The average temperature is calculated across
the entire domain and is then used to update the temperature-parameterized nuclear cross-sections
creating a feedback loop between the thermal and neutronic aspects of the system. The parametrization
based on the inner and outer radii is not incorporated into the current optimization. The cross-sections
are not adjusted based on these geometric parameters, meaning that potential effects of geometry on
neutron behavior and cross-sections are not captured in the current process but will be included in the
next section.

3.3.3 Optimization with both geometrical and thermal feedbacks on the cross sections

In this section, we explore the implementation of a more comprehensive optimization process that
incorporates both geometrical and thermal feedbacks on the neutron cross sections. This approach
enhances the fidelity of our nuclear reactor pin-cell optimization by capturing the complex interaction
between geometry, temperature, and neutronics. To include the geometric feedback, we implemented
a method to account for the effect of changing pin geometry on neutron cross sections. This was
achieved through the development of the ReporterPostprocessorValue MOOSE class, which allows us
to convert optimization parameters (such as pin radii) into field variables that can be used in Griffin
for cross-section parameterization. The process involves:

 • Converting the optimization parameter (e.g., fuel radius) to a postprocessor value.

 • Using MultiAppPostprocessorInterpolationTransfer MOOSE class to convert the accumulated
 values to a field variable.

 • Utilizing this field variable in Griffin to adjust cross sections based on geometrical changes.

 This approach allows the optimization process to capture how changes in pin geometry affect neu-
tron behavior through modified cross sections, providing a more realistic representation of the system
physics. The thermal feedback mechanism, which was already incorporated in previous optimizations,
is also taken into consideration. It accounts for how temperature changes affect neutron cross sections.
The coupling between the thermal-hydraulics calculations and the neutronics simulations ensures that

4 Continuous Shape Optimization under Uncertainty

We introduce in this section an integrated framework that combines the UQ capabilities of DAKOTA
UQ software with the multiphysics simulation power of MOOSE, including applications of shape
optimization under uncertainty (Shape-OUU) using the continuous shape optimization module. The
integration allows for the incorporation of various uncertainties into the core multiphysics MOOSE
application throughout the shape optimization process and how the final shape would behave to such
uncertainties. We will also quantify the uncertainties from an economical aspect using the optimized
final shapes.
 This integration of shape optimization with uncertainty quantification allows designers to not
only find optimal geometries for nominal conditions but also to ensure that these designs remain
robust when subjected to the range of possible variations in material properties and other uncertain
parameters. The results of this process provide valuable insights into the behavior of the nuclear system
under uncertainty; one can identify potential vulnerabilities in the design, and guide decision-making
in the design process.

4.1 The DAKOTA Interface

The DAKOTA interface developed for MOOSE uses an external interface linker to the MOOSE
application through the input files while running MOOSE and receiving the required data for analysis.
The primary objective of this interface is to enable robust design optimization that accounts for various
uncertainties inherent in nuclear systems. This is particularly crucial in the design of nuclear reactors,
where variations in material properties or operating conditions can have impacts on performance and
safety.
 The process is summarized in Figure 10. It begins with the DAKOTA input file, which sets up
the uncertainty quantification framework. This file defines the parameters of the study, including the
type of sampling method to be used (e.g., Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS)), the number of samples,
the uncertainty range (e.g., 95% representing those that are within ±2𝜎 from the mean 𝜇), and the
variables to be considered in the uncertainty analysis, among others. In this file as well the user can
specify the type of the uncertainty analysis; both aleatoric and epistemic analyses can be performed.
 DAKOTA is configured to generate a set of sample points within the defined parameter space.

 28

Each of these points represents a unique combination of the uncertain input variables. For each sample
point, DAKOTA will invoke the MOOSE simulation through a Python driver script, passing the
sampled values as inputs to the simulation. The Python driver script acts as an intermediary between
DAKOTA and MOOSE. It reads the parameter values provided by DAKOTA, prepares the input files
for MOOSE by incorporating these values, executes the MOOSE simulation, and then processes the
output to extract the relevant results for DAKOTA. One of the key features of this setup is the ability
to perturb the MOOSE input files based on the sampled parameter values, and to change anything the
MOOSE input (e.g., the weighting value used in Equations (13) and (15)). The script copies and reads
template input files of the MOOSE system – which can include all the files needed for the execution
of the multiphysics problem – and replaces placeholder values within these files with the specific
values for each simulation run as required by the user. This allows for efficient automation of multiple
simulation runs with varying input parameters.

 Figure 10: The DAKOTA-MOOSE UQ Interface Description.

 The MOOSE framework, in turn, performs the multiphysics simulations for each set of input
parameters. In our case, these simulations involve coupled neutronics and thermal-hydraulics calcula-
tions alongside the continuous shape optimization. Other types of simulations or studies can also be

 29

performed:

 1. Execution of several MOOSE runs for stochastic analysis (e.g., for the discrete or combinatorial
 shape design optimization which uses guided random configurations generation [6, 7]) effectively
 parallelizing the procedure.

 2. Gauging the range of the objective function used by sampling the optimization parameters.

 3. Studying the effect of the initial configuration on the optimization process (in our case of
 continuous shape optimization, the radii of the annular pin-cell).

The MOOSE simulations provide a set of outputs that are computed and made available through
MOOSE’s postprocessor system. Key parameters include 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 , the maximum temperature, and
geometric data such as the inner and outer radii of the fuel pin, which are fundamental to the shape
optimization process. We also add the fuel mixture area to the set of outputs, providing insights that
can be used for economical analysis as seen in Section 4.3. The postprocessor system in MOOSE is
highly flexible and customizable. This means that virtually any performance metric that can be derived
from the simulation data can be computed and output, allowing for a wide range of analyses tailored to
specific research or design objectives. Furthermore, the outputs generated by the optimization module,
or by MOOSE in general, can be easily exported and analyzed. For this analysis, MOOSE offers
many output formats, but both comma separated values (CSV) and EXODUS [17] are used. Both
these output formats can be easily read and manipulated using Python scripts. After each MOOSE
simulation, the Python script processes the output files to extract the relevant results. These results
are then passed back to DAKOTA for statistical analysis, although the user can perform standalone
statistical analysis on the data if would like as the Python script will combine the results into a single
file ready for analysis. This process is repeated for each sample point generated by DAKOTA, building
up a comprehensive picture of how the uncertainties in input parameters propagate through the system
and affect the outputs of interest.
 This flexibility in output format and ease of data extraction facilitates seamless integration of
MOOSE simulations into broader analysis workflows, enabling researchers and engineers to efficiently
process, visualize, and interpret the results of their OUU using the DAKOTA-MOOSE UQ framework.
While the current setup is focused on nuclear applications, particularly on continuous optimization of
fuel pin geometries in light water reactors, the methodology could be adapted to a wide range of other

 30

engineering problems. Any system where performance is sensitive to uncertain parameters and where
shape or geometry plays a crucial role could potentially benefit from this approach.

4.2 Uncertainty Parameters used in the Shape-OUU

The variables of interest chosen for this shape-OUU are the thermal conductivities and the pin-cell
power. These parameters were selected based on their critical role in the nuclear fuel pin-cell model
and, as explored earlier in Section 3.1 during the model development and methodology formulation,
we made several assumptions with these chosen variables being the most significant. For the thermal
conductivities, we consider two distinct values: one representing the zirconium cladding and another
for the UO2 fuel. This differentiation allows us to account for the varying heat transfer properties
of these materials, which influence the overall thermal behavior of the fuel pin-cell. The thermal
conductivities are temperature dependent as explored in Both Equations (8) and (9) and as shown in
Figure 5.
 For the pin-cell power, we adopted a value of 440 𝑘𝑊
 𝑚 since it ensures an initial maximum power

density comparable to those reported in previous studies [13, 14]. The inclusion of this parameter as
uncertain in our current analysis serves to evaluate the impact of power uncertainty on the optimization
process, and as an example to show how parameters can be used for shape-OUU. It is worth noting
that while we consider variations in power density during the optimization, this value remains constant
between the initial computation and the final optimized pin-cell shape.
 We will utilize the developed DAKOTA-MOOSE UQ framework to propagate the thermal con-
ductivities and the power uncertainties into the shape optimization process. Table 2 shows the sources
of uncertainties as well as their relative distribution. In this study, we focus on aleatoric uncertainty,

5 CONCLUSIONS

This work presented the utilization of the continuous shape optimization module within the MOOSE
multiphysics simulation framework for nuclear engineering shape design applications. The optimiza-
tion module implements continuous optimization techniques, utilizing the TAO toolkit from PETSc,
and employs a Nelder-Mead search algorithm for gradient free optimization. We demonstrated the
utility of the continuous shape optimization technique through practical application to neutronics
and thermal-hydraulics simulations of a 2D annular pin cell. The optimization process generated
improved designs by efficiently utilizing materials to concurrently maximize neutron multiplication
and minimize peak temperatures.
 We explored the impact of different objective functions and feedback mechanisms on the opti-
mization outcomes. When considering only temperature feedback on cross sections, the optimization
produced configurations that increased the multiplication factor by up to 2130 pcm while reducing
peak temperatures by approximately 90K relative to the initial state. However, when both geometrical
and thermal feedbacks were incorporated into the cross-section calculations, we observed more realis-
tic and moderate improvements. In this more comprehensive scenario, the optimization yielded an
increase in 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 of 400-600 pcm, alongside a reduction in maximum temperature of about 30-50K.
 The integration of uncertainty quantification with shape optimization, facilitated by our developed
DAKOTA-MOOSE interface, allowed for robust design optimization accounting for uncertainties in
thermal conductivities and linear power. This approach provided valuable insights into the behavior
of the nuclear system under uncertainty, identifying potential vulnerabilities in the design and guiding
decision-making in the optimization process. The uncertainty analysis revealed that while thermal
conductivity uncertainties had minimal impact on the optimized shape, power uncertainties led to
more significant variations in the optimized configuration. However, it was not beyond the expected
results given for the deterministic optimization performed in the results section. One major important
outcome was that the optimized designs consistently showed a reduction in fuel area of approximately
14-17% compared to the initial configuration, while maintaining (e.g., power density) or improving
performance (e.g., 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 or maximum temperature). This reduction translates directly to decreased
fuel costs, highlighting the economic benefits of the optimization process.
 Future work could focus on expanding the capabilities of the continuous shape optimization module

 41

to enable more realistic optimization of reactor components, from individual fuel pins up to the full
core level. Additionally, incorporating more sophisticated uncertainty quantification methods and
exploring different optimization algorithms beyond Nelder-Mead could provide more comprehensive
and efficient exploration of the design space. Pursuing these research directions can transform the
integrated shape optimization tool into a comprehensive framework for multiphysics simulation,
design, and optimization of complex reactor systems under uncertainty using the MOOSE simulation
framework.

 ABSTRACT
We apply the MOOSE framework’s optimization module to Hierarchical Shape Optimization for
a PWR fuel assembly design, adaptively optimizing the fuel pin’s shape. Accounting for changes
in neutronics behavior is captured with cross section generation in Serpent while changes in heat
transfer is considered by dynamically updating convective heat transfer coefficients during the op-
timization process. We employ the derivative-free Nelder-Mead algorithm to optimize the fuel
pin geometry, demonstrating notable improvements in performance metrics. The hierarchical ap-
proach avoids local minima by gradually increasing geometric complexity, achieving a final kef f
improvement of 1390 pcm over the non-hierarchical baseline.
 Keywords: MOOSE; Optimization; Hierarchical

 1. INTRODUCTION
We introduce a framework for Hierarchical Shape Optimization (HSO) for nuclear engineering
applications using the multiphysics object-oriented simulation environment (MOOSE) framework
and its optimization module [1]. Our HSO framework enables multiphysics optimization by incor-
porating output values from neutronics and thermal-hydraulic modules in the objective function.
This capability, explored earlier for discrete and combinatorial optimization [2], allows for design
optimization that accounts for multiple physical phenomena simultaneously benefiting from the
flexibility of the MOOSE framework [3]. We apply HSO to traditional pin and assembly configu-
rations in pressurized water reactors (PWR) using specifications based on the C5G7 benchmark’s
UO2 fuel assemblies [2]. We explore the application of the optimization module for the optimiza-
tion of fuel pin radii, both in infinite lattice and assembly configurations. The radius of the fuel
pin is taken as the optimization parameter while maintaining other geometric constraints consistent
with standard PWR designs.
 The hierarchical nature of HSO is manifested in the two optimization stages performed in
sequence: First for individual fuel pins and then for the collective effects of pins and guide tubes
in an assembly. Two types of pin-cells are optimized in each level: the unit cell of UO2 and
the unit cells of pins adjacent to the guide tube, each optimized individually to account for their

unique neutronic environments. Cross sections are generated using Serpent, parameterized with
respect to temperature and fuel pin radius (maintaining constant unit cell size) for each base level
optimization. This detailed parameterization ensures an accurate representation of the neutron
physics as the geometry changes during the optimization process. The multigroup cross sections
are generated in the seven energy groups structure of the C5G7 benchmark, providing sufficient
energy resolution for accurate reactor physics calculations.
 Diffusion calculations are conducted with Griffin, a MOOSE-based neutron-transport solver.
The optimization module adjusts the shape of the fuel pins to minimize a multiphysics objective
function that includes neutronics performance (e.g., the eigenvalue kef f ) and thermal-hydraulic
variables (e.g., maximum fuel temperature). Mesh generation is performed outside of the MOOSE
framework in Coreform Cubit to create mesh specifications unique to each attempted unit cell.
This is critical for isolating unique pin optimization throughout the mesh. The workflow explored
progresses from optimization at the unit cell level, to a 5×5 sub-assembly level, and finally to the
full 17×17 assembly level consistent with the C5G7MOX benchmark.

 2. METHODS
MOOSE’s optimization module drives the HSO, utilizing three main inputs at each level. The par-
ent input governs the process using an optimization algorithm, details of which will be noted in the
next section. As shown in Figure 1, the optimization file receives an updated value of the objective
function and returns new radii for the diffusion solver. Once this happens, the diffusion file initiates
the nodal movement file to update the boundary conditions as new optimized radii are proposed by
the optimization file. The diffusion file solves for the necessary physics according to the objective
function needs, calculating the new objective function value, and passing this back to the optimiza-
tion file, where this repeats until convergence criteria is met in the parent application. To move data
in MOOSE the MultiApp system is leveraged, which establishes a working tree of information to
be passed seamlessly between the various inputs through the Transfer System sketched in Figure 1
starting with the parent application. It should be noted that while the MultiApp system can be used
to move information between files, it cannot interface directly with the optimization module. To
do this, the OptimizationReporter class provides the necessary data to the optimization algorithm
after the transferred data is returned to the parent application. Once the diffusion file receives the
proposed radii, the nodal movement file executes before the diffusion solve to adjust the geometry.
The elements inside of a pin establish a continuous boundary - where a series of nodes define the
boundary conditions for neutronics and thermal-hydraulic considerations. To move these while
maintaining a continuous boundary we utilize MOOSE’s displaced mesh capability. By relating
the coordinate of a node, ⃗nu,j (undisplaced), and a corresponding displacement vector for the node
at location j, d⃗j , we get a new set of nodes defined as such:

 These displacements are transferred back to the diffusion file that solves the relevant physics on

 4. CONCLUSION
MOOSE’s optimization module and framework provides a convenient way to approach shape
optimization. The fuel pin shape of the PWR was optimized to improve an objective function,

 9

maximizing kef f and minimizing Tmax . The approach is hierarchical, building complexity in the
geometry, to capture different physical effects assisting in convergence to a local minima. This
approach is compared against optimization with initial guesses at the pin-optimized radii and no
optimization to observe the effectiveness of the hierarchical approach. The HSO showed a 1390
pcm increase in kef f from no previous optimization and a 105 pcm increase in the pin-optimized
radii as the initial guess. The separation of pins into inner and outer zones as optimization param-
eters allowed accounting for the different neutronic environments, noted by the normalized flux
groups in the optimization figures, to be adapted to better fit each pins contribution to the objective
function. As a result, the final HSO fuel pin radii were Ro = 0.489 cm and Ri = 0.500 cm, respec-
tively corresponding to pins at the periphery of the assembly and pins adjacent to the guide tube.
The derivative-free Nedler-Mead optimization algorithm proved to be sufficient for our purposes.
The careful consideration of changing boundary conditions, and cross section generation, gives an
optimization algorithm that is successful and benefits from a hierarchical approach and is shown
to be applicable more generally to nuclear systems.

 ABSTRACT

 We present the development and application of a C++ computational tool for physics-based shape
 optimization that we designed and implemented for NEAMS tools. The optimization tool leverages
 MOOSE’s various systems, including the MultiApp transfer and reporter systems, among others,
 enabling seamless integration within the MOOSE framework. Our primary goal is to enable
 optimization of the shape and topology of nuclear reactor components within MOOSE, starting from
 the pin-level up to the core level. By integrating our tool into the extensive MOOSE’s application
 library, it can be readily applied to any existing MOOSE modules or applications across thermal
 hydraulics, neutronics, structural mechanics, and other physics-based solvers in MOOSE. The
 flexibility of applying this new capability is illustrated by using different single-physics modules and
 apps in MOOSE. Two major types of shape and topology optimizations are applied in this paper to
 different engineering design examples, combinatorial and discrete optimizations. We demonstrate
 the tool on a 2D pin-cell model in an infinite lattice based on the traditional pin-cell designs. By
 implementing specific constraints and physics-based objective functions, we show how the state-space
 search algorithm embedded in our tool can propose optimized pin-cell designs including annular fuel
 pins. Verification of the algorithm’s effectiveness at proposing relevant Loading Patterns (LP) and
 exploring the shape design space is illustrated using a fuel shuffling topology optimization problem
 adapted based on the IAEA-2D benchmark.
 Keywords: MOOSE, NEAMS, optimization, objective function, combinatorial

 1. INTRODUCTION

Shape optimization is a pivotal discipline bridging theory and practice in engineering design and computational
sciences. By finding the optimal topology of an object, system, or structure within specified constraints
to maximize performance, shape optimization enables enhanced capabilities across industries, especially
nuclear. Shape optimization can improve performance by more effectively utilizing materials under certain
constraints, e.g., fuel shuffling problem in a traditional light water reactor (LWR) or when optimizing the
shape of fuel pins. This innovation pushes boundaries beyond traditional concepts (e.g., solid fuel pin design
shown in Figure 1), creating breakthrough designs that redefine industry standards (e.g., evident in the
annular pin-cell design proposed earlier based on physical intuition [1], and attained by our optimization tool
in Section 3). Furthermore, by fully harnessing modern technologies like additive manufacturing, shape
optimization can produce functionally better designs not previously feasible.

 2. THE SHAPE OPTIMIZATION TOOL IN MOOSE

2.1. Description of the Optimization Process

The shape optimization tool has several components, roughly depicted in Figure 2 for it to function as
envisioned. The first part is the optimizer class itself that hosts the optimization algorithm used to formulate a
new, and arguably better, configuration of the system based on some criteria. The optimization process in the
optimizer (and the generation of new configurations during the optimization process) relies on computation
of a physics-based objective function. Possible objective functions are limitless as the user can use any
quantity allowed by the postprocessor system in MOOSE. The optimizer portion of the tool generates new
configurations (i.e., new states of the domain) that will be transferred to the physical model at hand to compute
the associated objective function.
To generate an optimal configuration, an enhanced version of the simulated annealing algorithm is deployed
(more on this in Section 2.2). The algorithm generates such configurations according to the type of optimization
applied, either discrete or combinatorial. In combinatorial optimization, it basically re-distributes the initial
material content of a domain to give a more optimal configuration. In discrete optimization, the code can
change the initial material content of the domain to get optimal configurations since we are flipping the pixels
and not swapping locations. Configurations generation is usually restricted by application of constraints that
ensure the resulting configuration has a high manufacturability index and hence suitable for manufacturing.
The constraints class benefits from MOOSE’s internal classes’ methods and members (that is, internal C++
functions and variables) and is designed so that users of the tool can build their own constraints on top of the
inherently available ones shipped with the base shape optimization class we developed.
The second part of the tool involves exchanging information between the optimizer and the multiApp system.
The objective function is evaluated based on simulated physics parameters and equations describing the
simulated system. These parameters, especially for shape optimization, are stored on the mesh object of
MOOSE (not to be confused with the geometrical mesh). Getting and setting data from this object, while
also using it, makes up most of the second part of the optimization tool. Such data is transferred and updated
using MOOSE’s Input, Reporter, and MultiApp transfer systems. The ‘To Transfer’ system sends the new
configuration to the MultiApp system and MOOSE’s solver. This solver computes the objective function

value based on the configuration from the ‘To Transfer’ system. The objective function value is then
transferred from the solver or MultiApp to the optimizer using the ‘From Transfer’ system, allowing the
optimizer to determine if the configuration was worthwhile and guide the generation of new configurations.
In this way, the optimization process resembles a reinforcement learning approach in machine learning, where
the goal is to improve decision-making over time, leading to more efficient and effective outcomes.

Figure 2. Process Chart of the Discrete and Combinatorial Shape Optimization Tool Developed in the
 MOOSE Framework.

The MultiApp system integrates various MOOSE apps that can also be specific modules provided within the
open-source MOOSE framework. Each app serves as the primary interface for users to interact with MOOSE,
either via an input file or directly through the code. Between the MultiApp transfer system and the optimizer,
there is another system of MOOSE that facilitates exchanging the required data. This Reporter system can be
viewed as an extension of both the Postprocessor and VectorPostprocessor systems. Unlike post-processors,
which declare only a single scalar value of type ‘Real’, and vector post-processors, which declare multiple
values but are restricted to the type ‘std::vector<Real>’, the Reporter system offers more flexibility.

2.2. Shape Optimization Module Input File Structure

 In single-physics optimization problems, two input files should be provided by the user for the shape
 optimization process. The primary input file, the main driver, interfaces with a secondary input file that
 encompasses the details of the physics, the mesh configuration, and the postprocessor objective function.
The main driver input file serves as a procedural driver rather than a physics solver. Control parameters
 of the simulated annealing optimizer is provided by the user in the main driver, and currently it contains a
 modified version of the basic simulated annealing algorithm [2, 3]. The modifications include implementing
‘solution cache’ method, ‘tabu list’ method, and ‘multi-run’ algorithm. The solution cache method
 uses a hash table to allow reuse of cached objective function values for randomly generated configurations
 that were evaluated earlier in the optimization. This improves performance by avoiding repeatedly invoking
 the physics solver for cached solutions. The tabu list method forbids revisiting solutions in the tabu list for a
 set number of iterations. This enhances performance by driving exploration of new areas of the parameter
 space and escaping local extrema. The multi-run algorithm adds another layer of iterations controlling the
 initial solution and random seed used for optimization. It runs the optimization multiple times with different
 initial conditions (that is, initial configuration and random seed), using the optimized configuration from one
 run as the initial configuration for the next. At the end, the best solution across all runs is the best estimate of
 the overall optimal configuration. This improves performance by increasing the number of explored states,
 escaping local optima.

 Constraints are also handled in the main driver. The code loops through all objects in the constraints attribute,
 allowing the user to add multiple constraints by listing them here. The constraint names come from the
‘[UserObjects]’ section, distributed in various blocks. Each block is a distinct constraint class, that can be
 integrated into MOOSE and all inherit from a ‘ConstraintsLibraryBase’ class. The base class provides
 key data like current/previous configurations, element neighbor maps, etc. to the derived classes such that
 users can create functions to assess particular configuration features. Each specialized constraint class returns
 a boolean variable to the optimizer indicating if the configuration satisfies the constraint.

3.1. The Pin-Cell Problem

For the shape optimization exercise discussed in this section, we aim to use an objective function 𝑂 (𝑘 ∞ , 𝐷 ℎ )
that maximizes both 𝑘 ∞ and the hydraulic diameter, 𝐷 ℎ . An example design is shown in Figure 3, where
we have omitted the cladding and gap for simplicity. The inclusion of the hydraulic diameter in our
objective function draws inspiration from recent studies [4]. The rationale behind this choice comes from the
understanding that pressure losses are inversely proportional to 𝐷 4ℎ . Thus, prioritizing designs that enhance
𝐷 ℎ will naturally yield configurations with reduced pressure losses, resulting in diminished pumping power
requirements. While the direct benefits of this approach may not be immediately evident in the 2D pin cell
model we are optimizing here, its extension to a 3D model is expected to show the same behavior discussed
here and will be tested further.

3.1.1. Discrete Optimization

When discrete optimization is applied to the pin-cell problem without any constraints, it yields a configuration
where the moderator cells are dispersed within the fuel pin, as presented in Figure 4a, thus facilitating the
thermalization of fission neutrons, which increases the fission rate. Regardless of whether we use Griffin or
Moltres, the outcome showed that in the optimal configuration the moderator is always dispersed within the
fuel block, though the exact final configuration varied. Utilizing Moltres’ 2-group simulation with the pin
cell model, the resulting 𝑘 ∞ was 1.046949 showing an increase of more than 5000 pcm, and the hydraulic
diameter 𝐷 ℎ was 0.145385 cm. While this configuration did indeed maximize the multiplication factor, the
resultant 𝐷 ℎ value corresponds to an unfeasible pin cell configuration, thereby undermining the optimization
process outcomes.

3.1.2. Constraints Description

To restrain the optimizer from generating space invaders like shapes, a few constraints had to be applied
on the configurations generated. The first constraint was to check if the current configuration has just one
continuous block of fuel, and two or less continuous blocks of coolant. The idea of applying such a constraint
is to promote configurations where the different materials cells are connected together forming contiguous
enclaves of each material. This was applied by counting the number of enclaves inside the configuration
using the Breadth First Search (BFS) algorithm. The idea was to treat the pixelated pin-cell as a graph
representation where each element in the grid represents a vertex (or node) in the graph. The neighbors
information provides the adjacency information for each vertex. These neighboring indices form the edges
in the graph. The BFS is applied to this implicit graph to identify contiguous regions (clusters of adjacent
vertices) having the same value, and its application and explanation can be found in the literature (see, e.g.,
[9]).
Applying this constraint resulted in a more rigid configuration, but still Tetris-like regions appear inside the
resultant configuration shown in Figure 4b. In terms of 𝑘 ∞ and 𝐷 ℎ , both of these values come at 1.046868
and 0.207123 cm, respectively. Interestingly, the optimizer reported a formation with two coolant enclaves
and a single fuel enclave, with one of the coolant enclaves near the pin cell’s center (cf. Figure 4b). By solely
applying the objective function from Equation (2) alongside the enclaves constraint, the optimization tended
towards an annular-like configuration. However, one limitation of discrete optimization is its disregard for
the initial material volume, as it swaps material assignments more freely than combinatorial optimization.
The resultant configuration had a fuel volume fraction of 41%, lower than the initial 58% in Figure 3. To
restrain the optimizer from generating configurations where the fuel is not surrounded by the coolant, it is
possible to specify the type of cells at the domain boundaries. While this constraint did not significantly alter
the outcome of the optimization, it serves as a soft constraint that ensures the generated configurations have
the fuel surrounded by coolant. The result of applying both constraints to the pin cell problem is shown in
Figure 5a.

3.1.3. Combinatorial Optimization

Combinatorial optimization is crucial as it lets the user retain the initial content/volume, for structured
grids, in the original optimization domain without any additions or removals. Figure 5b shows the result of
applying combinatorial optimization to the pin-cell model. We did not apply the number of enclaves and
the boundaries constraints at first. The 𝑘 ∞ for this shape was 0.994238, and 𝐷 ℎ was 0.0971009 cm. The
reason for the lower 𝐷 ℎ is the amount of fuel (58% of the total volume) and when we do combinatorial
optimization such larger mass will be distributed in the domain and will thus increase the wetted perimeter

resulting in lower 𝐷 ℎ (since the coolant area will be still the same). 𝑘 ∞ is also not that much different from
the initial value (≈ 200𝑝𝑐𝑚 ) as in the case of discrete optimization (≈ 5000𝑝𝑐𝑚 ) because we already start
with the margin for optimization being constrained as we require the volume of each material to be the same
as in the initial configuration. The shape attained in Figure 5b is still none-manufacturable and we should
apply additional constraints to overhaul the shape more. Constraining the number of enclaves and boundaries
produces shapes that are more rigid but still not suitable for manufacturing due to this maze-like structure. As
illustrated in Figure 6a, this distribution is favored to enhance neutron thermalization, thereby increasing 𝑘 ∞ .

A more effective constraint is to ensure configurations are more densely packed. Applying this density
constraint, in addition to the previous constraints, allows us to get a Cartesian approximation of the annular
shape for the 2D model of the pin-cell as we can see in Figure 6b. The density check is based on computing
the number of cells of one material in a variable volume or area in 2D. The bounding box is the smallest
rectangle (in 2D) that encloses all instances of the specified material. The constraint then computes the
density as the ratio of number of specified material cells within the bounding box and the total cells in the
bounding box. If we set up the constraint check to increase the density of the coolant, then configurations that
have spread out shapes will result in larger area and hence lower density and eventually will not be favored by
the optimization process.

of fuel isotopes. We assumed assembly type 2 in the benchmark as the fresh fuel, while assembly type

3.2.2. Optimization Process

The fuel shuffling problem is a combinatorial optimization problem in nuclear reactor core design. Our goal
here is to rearrange (shuffle) fuel assemblies within the core to maximize the multiplication factor ( 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 )
(which translates to a longer cycle length), and to minimize power peaking factors (which translates to better
fuel utilization and better safety margins). In order to avoid localized power peaks, and high radial power
densities, certain constraints were made to focus the problem based on previous research [13, 14, 15]:
 • Quadratic symmetric LP.
 • The core’s center always has a twice-burned fuel assembly type.
 • Inside the core’s interior (i.e., away from the periphery), no fresh fuel neighboring is allowed.
 • The shape optimization module allows specifying fixed anchor materials that will not change position
 during optimization. Here, material ID 4 (The reflector cells) are anchored.

Initially, the objective function was defined as just the negative of the effective multiplication factor 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 , as
shown in equation (4). With this formulation, the optimization aims to increase 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 by minimizing the
negative value. The resulting loading pattern (Figure 8a) increased 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 by around 1900 pcm compared to
the starting state, up to 1.05024. However, it also increased the APPF value to 2.158, negating the benefit of
the higher 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 . Such a large value for APPF reduces the thermal margin of operation for the core [14, 15].
This highlighted the need to include APPF in the objective function calculations. Therefore, we adjusted the
objective function to:

                                     O(𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 , APPF) = −keff + 0.01 ∗ APPF,                                     (5)

where we added a term to minimize APPF, with an arbitrary weighting factor to make its effect comparable
to 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 . With this modified objective function, the optimization produced a LP (Figure 8b) with an improved
APPF of 1.413 and 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 of 1.04377. The optimization resulted in a net increase in 𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 of around 1250
pcm while reducing the APPF from 2.158 down to 1.413 (which increases the thermal margin for the core’s
operation). This highlights the inherent trade-off between the two objectives. The higher APPF of the initial
pattern limited its increased cycle length performance. By focusing on APPF reduction, the optimization was
able to find a loading pattern with more balanced performance between cycle length and thermal margin of
operation.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

This work demonstrated the development and integration of a computational tool for physics-based shape
and topology optimization within the MOOSE framework. Leveraging MOOSE’s modular architecture and
transfer systems enabled seamless coupling of the optimization routines with various physics models. The tool
implements combinatorial and discrete optimization algorithms based on the simulated annealing approach
with added enhancements: solution caching, tabu lists, and multi-run restart approach. The flexibility of the
tool was shown through its application to different single-physics models in MOOSE using the neutronics
codes Moltres and Griffin, or using MOOSE’s internal modules like the diffusion-reaction module.
For a 2D pin cell model, the tool generated optimized shapes including a Cartesian approximation of
the annular fuel pins by maximizing neutron multiplication and hydraulic diameter. Usage of constraints
improved the final designs to guarantee that the resulting designs were not only optimal but also practical and
manufacturable. For the fuel shuffling optimization case, the capability of the shape optimization tool to
perform fuel shuffling optimization at the full core level was demonstrated. The fuel shuffling optimization
example achieved notable improvements in both multiplication factor and power peaking, demonstrating the
value of mathematical optimization for core design. Our shape optimization tool appropriately relocated
fresh, once-burned and twice-burned fuel assemblies, resulting in a LP that minimizes neutron leakage,
increases cycle length, and provides a high thermal margin of operation, giving topology patterns as those
available in the literature [13, 14, 15].

4.1. Future Work

This research expands MOOSE’s capabilities to enable optimization of reactor components’ shapes, from
individual fuel pins to the full core level. Integrating with MOOSE’s extensive multiphysics application

library will allow extending the shape optimization tool to perform coupled physics optimizations in future
work. The discrete mesh-based approach currently provides reasonable geometry fidelity, but implementing
spline-based shape parameterizations could enable smoother, continuous improvements in optimized shapes
through continuous optimization. Such advancements would not only enhance the simulation’s fidelity but
also guide the shape design towards more efficient and safer reactor operation.
Several methodological advancements could further enhance the tool’s capabilities. Uncertainty quantification
methods should be incorporated to optimize under uncertainties in materials properties, manufacturing
tolerances, operational conditions, and model uncertainties including the constraints used. The tool could
also integrate additive manufacturing process modeling to ensure optimized shapes satisfy manufacturability
constraints. Combining shape optimization and additive manufacturing enables producing topologically
optimized components with enhanced performance. Expanding the suite of optimization algorithms beyond
simulated annealing will provide more robust and efficient design space exploration. Pursuing these research
directions can transform this MOOSE-integrated shape and topology optimization tool into a comprehensive
framework for multiphysics simulation, design, and uncertainty-aware optimization of complex reactor
components and systems.
