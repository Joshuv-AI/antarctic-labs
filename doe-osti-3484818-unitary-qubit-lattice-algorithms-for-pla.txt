 Unitary Qubit Lattice Algorithms for Plasma Physics
 Rogers State University
PI – Min Soe (DOE Award Number: DE-SC0021653)

Research Overview
Quantum Information Science (QIS) and the development of quantum computers offer the potential to
transform computational science by enabling new approaches to solving complex problems. Unlike
conventional computers, whose fundamental unit of information is the classical bit, quantum computers
utilize the quantum bit (qubit), which can exist in superposition and can be coupled through quantum
entanglement. Harnessing these quantum properties enables the development of algorithms with the
potential to achieve significant computational advantages for selected classes of problems.
A central challenge in realizing this potential is the development of algorithms suitable for quantum
computing architectures. In this project, we apply concepts from quantum information science to
develop quantum-inspired algorithms for solving Maxwell's equations governing electromagnetic wave
propagation and scattering in complex media, including plasmas. Our focus is on Quantum Lattice
Algorithms (QLAs), a class of unitary algorithms whose fundamental building blocks are qubits. And
the time evolution is implemented through unitary collision and streaming operators that generate and
propagate quantum entanglement across the computational lattice.
An important advantage of QLAs is that they can be implemented on present-day classical computers,
allowing their accuracy, scalability, and computational performance to be evaluated before the
availability of large-scale quantum hardware. These implementations also provide a foundation for
implementing the algorithms on future quantum systems while preserving their underlying unitary
structure.
During this project, we have systematically developed QLA formulations for increasingly complex
electromagnetic environments. We initially considered electromagnetic wave propagation and scattering
in vacuum and scalar dielectric media, and subsequently extended the framework to include
unmagnetized plasmas, and finally, the tensor dielectric response of magnetized plasmas. These
developments have established a unified QLA framework for electromagnetic simulations in complex
media and have demonstrated its potential for capturing transient wave phenomena that are difficult to
access using conventional computational approaches.
Accomplishments
We have leveraged the similarity between the spinor form of the Dirac equation and Maxwell's
equations to develop a unitary Quantum Lattice Algorithm (QLA) for studying electromagnetic wave
propagation, starting with vacuum and then extending to more complex media, including plasmas. This
is not a direct discretization of Maxwell's equations, but recovers the desired equations to second order
in the continuum limit, with computational results in excellent agreement with theoretical predictions.
The following is an abbreviated summary of the major research accomplishments during this grant
period.

• Developed and validated multiple QLA representations for Maxwell's equations. We
 developed and tested two QLA formulations: one based on the complete Maxwell system and
 another based on the Faraday–Ampère curl-curl subset with the divergence equations imposed as
 initial constraints. In two dimensions, the reduced formulation requires only six qubits per lattice
 node compared with eight qubits for the full formulation. Both approaches produced same
 results, demonstrating the universality of the QLA framework while reducing the resource
 requirements.
• Resolved a major limitation of the earlier one-dimensional QLA formulation. By
 introducing finite two-dimensional Gaussian wave packets, we enabled accurate simulations of
 wave-packet interactions with dielectric interfaces. The simulations successfully reproduced total
 internal reflection (with transient energy spikes), the Goos–Hänchen shift arising from the finite
 spatial extent of the wave packet, Fresnel behavior at normal incidence, and energy conservation,
 while satisfying the divergence conditions to machine precision. Because QLA is an initial-value
 method, no internal boundary conditions are required. This expands the range of electromagnetic
 phenomena that can be investigated using QLA. [It is important to note that internal boundary
 conditions are typically introduced through the assumption of possible discontinuities in the
 electromagnetic fields when applying Maxwell’s equations across material interfaces. In the
 QLA framework, however, Maxwell’s equations are solved directly throughout the
 computational domain, without introducing limiting procedures or explicitly imposing
 discontinuity conditions within specific spatial regions].
• Developed a highly parallelized MPI-based QLA code for inhomogeneous dielectric media.
 We performed simulations of wave-packet propagation in dielectric media and their scattering
 from spatially localized dielectric structures. The MPI implementation of QLA has demonstrated
 scalability on leadership-class computing resources, allowing two-dimensional simulations to be
 performed using over16,000 processors distributed across 512 compute nodes of the Perlmutter
 supercomputer. The physics insight revealed by these high-resolution simulations (e.g., transient
 electromagnetic scattering phenomena) is not apparent from conventional frequency-domain
 studies of scattering, thereby establishing QLA as a powerful computational framework for time-
 dependent electromagnetic simulations in complex media.
• Successful Extension to 3D and Parallel Performance of QLA Code on Perlmutter: The
 QLA framework was successfully extended to three-dimensional domains and demonstrated
 excellent parallel scalability on the Perlmutter supercomputer. Preliminary 3D simulation results
 are shown in Fig. 1. Strong-scaling studies demonstrated near-ideal scaling, with slightly
 superlinear speedup at higher core counts, tested to over 100,000 processors for a 48003 grid,
 while weak-scaling studies maintained essentially constant wall-clock time of approximately 88
 s, with less than 0.4% variation, as the problem size and number of cores were increased
 proportionally. These results demonstrate the capability of the QLA framework to efficiently
 support large-scale 3D simulations on distributed-memory HPC systems.

• Developed a QLA formulation for nonlinear, lossless fluid dynamics. The classical fluid
 equations were derived through a Madelung transformation of a generalized Gross–Pitaevskii
 equation, permitting unwanted quantum effects to be eliminated. Two-dimensional QLA
 simulations showed excellent agreement with the classical computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
 simulations of Meng and Yang (2023), demonstrating that the QLA methodology extends
 beyond electromagnetics to nonlinear fluid dynamics.
• Derived a unitary quantum formulation for wave propagation in cold, lossless,
 inhomogeneous, magnetized plasmas. Using the leading-order Trotter–Suzuki approximation,
 we expressed the unitary evolution operator exactly in terms of one- and two-qubit quantum
 gates. We are currently developing and implementing an improved QLA formulation that
 eliminates the need for the Trotter–Suzuki approximation, thereby providing a more general and
 efficient framework for future quantum implementations.
• Computational Complexity and Quantum Advantage. We compared the computational
 complexity of the QLA for electromagnetic wave propagation with the classical finite-difference
 time-domain (FDTD) method introduced by Yee (1966). For problems involving localized
 inhomogeneities, we found that the number of elementary quantum gates required by the QLA
 scales exponentially more favorably than the number of classical operations required by the
 FDTD method, highlighting the potential for significant quantum computational advantages in
 large-scale electromagnetic simulations.
• QLA for Time-discretized Lorenz Model: Simulating nonlinear classical dynamics on a
 quantum computer presents fundamental difficulties because quantum mechanics is inherently
 linear. To address this, we developed an explicit quantum algorithm for advancing the second-
 order, time-discretized Lorenz model. The algorithm employs a recursive structure and requires
 only a linear number of copies of the initial state with respect to the number of integration steps.
 This construction offers an improvement over earlier approaches while preserving the quantum
 advantage in handling the dimensionality of large differential equation systems, in line with other
 time-marching quantum algorithms.

Research Impact and Dissemination
The research carried out under this award has been substantially strengthened through a long-standing
collaboration with two scientists at the National Technical University of Athens, Greece. This
collaboration has resulted in a sustained record of scholarly contributions, including peer-reviewed
journal publications, a contributed book chapter, invited presentations, and presentations at national and
international conferences. The scientific significance of this work has been recognized by the broader
research community, including the selection of one recent paper for the cover of Physics of Plasmas and
its designation as a Noteworthy Paper by the journal editor.
Educational Opportunities
The project also provided opportunities for undergraduate students to gain research experience. A senior
thesis entitled Testing Quantum Lattice Algorithms for Future Use in Plasma Physics was completed
with support from this DOE award. The student received training in parallel computing using the
university's local high-performance computing resources before transitioning to DOE's NERSC
supercomputing facilities for production-scale plasma simulations.
Recruiting students with sufficient preparation in physics remains a challenge. However, the recent
launch of our Engineering degree program is expected to broaden the pool of well-prepared students and
strengthen future research and workforce development activities in this area.

Next Steps: We are now extending the QLA to solve the plasma fluid equations to study cutoffs,
resonances, and mode conversion. We will first analyze wave propagation in homogeneous equilibria
with systematically varied parameters to benchmark and validate the method. We will then construct
inhomogeneous slab-geometry equilibria to investigate mode conversion, cutoff behavior, and resonant
interactions, with particular emphasis on the role of transient fields during their time evolution.
Dissipative effects will subsequently be incorporated through phenomenological inter-species collisional
terms

Future Directions: This project established the theoretical and computational foundation for quantum
algorithms applicable to electromagnetic simulations in plasma physics. As quantum computing
hardware continues to mature, future work will focus on implementing the Quantum Lattice Algorithm
on fault-tolerant quantum computers, extending the formulation to more complex plasma models, and
investigating hybrid quantum-classical algorithms for large-scale electromagnetic simulations.
Continued optimization on emerging exascale computing platforms will also provide valuable
benchmarks and facilitate the transition from classical to quantum computing technologies.
