Evaluating the access process for patients transitioning from intravenous to subcutaneous biologic administration for inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Taylor Kissel, Miranda Murray, Josh DeClercq, Autumn Zuckerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch is needed to evaluate challenges associated with payers’ and patients’ uptake of vedolizumab and infliximab subcutaneous (SC) formulations, which were recently approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration.MethodsA single center, ambispective study evaluated patients with inflammatory bowel disease with a referral to start or transition to SC vedolizumab or infliximab between September 2023 and December 2024. The primary outcome was time to SC formulation access. Secondary outcomes included whether patients were approved for SC therapy, method of approval, and number of patients not starting SC maintenance therapy after referral.ResultsOf the 248 patients included in the regression analyses, most patients (72%) referred to SC were approved, with over half of those approvals occurring via prior authorization (56%). Of the 179 patients approved to start therapy, 21% of patients did not start therapy, largely due to patient decision (45%). The median time to access was 11 days (interquartile range 1-43) with a range of 0-457 days. Patients with commercial pharmacy insurance were 3 times more likely to have a longer time to medication access (odds ratios [OR]: 3.40, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.5-6.1, P = .003). Patients not in remission at baseline were 90% more likely to have a longer time to medication access than patients in remission (OR: 1.9, 95% CI 0.8-4.2, P = .127).ConclusionsOver a quarter of patients prescribed SC vedolizumab or infliximab do not start due to payer restrictions or patient preference. Patients with commercial insurance may be less likely to access SC therapy.

What is already known?

The Food and Drug Administration recently approved subcutaneous (SC) formulations of vedolizumab and infliximab, providing patients with inflammatory bowel disease more convenient, self-administered treatment options.

What is new here?

This study shows that though 72% of referrals for SC therapy are approved, delays and non-initiation often occur due to insurance barriers or patient choice.

How can this study help patient care?

This study highlights US access hurdles for patients initiating SC therapy, emphasizing the importance of shared decision making prior to prescribing and the insurance and financial challenges patients prescribed SC therapy may face.

Introduction

Nearly 1 in 100 Americans are diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which comprises 2 major disorders: ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). IBD occurs when the immune system mistakes food, bacteria, and other materials in the intestine for foreign substances and attacks the cells of the intestines leading to chronic inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract. UC affects only the colon and rectum and is characterized by inflammation of the mucosal layer; CD can involve any component of the gastrointestinal tract from the oral cavity to the anus and is characterized by transmural inflammation.1,2 IBD significantly impacts quality of life–plaguing the patient with fatigue, pain, urgency, embarrassment, stigma, social withdrawal, loss of independence, lost time/wages, and fear of relapse or flares.1–3 There is no cure for IBD, therefore the goal of treatment is to induce and maintain remission.

Achieving remission improves patients’ quality of life and decreases subsequent healthcare costs. Treatment of IBD for mild to moderate disease typically includes oral or rectal 5-aminosalicylates. If patients progress to moderate to severe disease, corticosteroids and biologic/small-molecule therapies are used to achieve remission. In patients with refractory disease or complications, surgery may be needed. For moderate to severe disease, the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) suggests early introduction of biologic therapy (top–down approach) rather than delaying their use until after failure with mesalamine and/or corticosteroids.4 Early combined immunosuppression results in remission more quickly and at higher rates than conventional step-up therapy.5 Among other advanced therapies, vedolizumab and infliximab are strongly recommended for the induction and maintenance of remission by the AGA and the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2025 clinical practice guidelines on the management of moderate to severe CD and UC.6,7 Vedolizumab and infliximab intravenous (IV) infusions have been a first-line treatment option for patients with IBD for over 10 years and were the first advanced therapies that entered the US market to treat moderate–severe IBD as solely an IV formulation. Induction dosing for infliximab and vedolizumab is administered intravenously at weeks 0, 2, and 6. Patients would then continue IV maintenance therapy, given every 8 weeks. Each infusion required an administration time of approximately 30 minutes to 2 hours.

Recently, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved vedolizumab and infliximab for subcutaneous (SC) administration in the United States, offering patients a more convenient at-home treatment option that is clinically equivalent in safety and efficacy to their IV formulations. Evidence, largely from European countries where vedolizumab and infliximab SC formulations have been available since 2020, demonstrates a durable and potentially improved clinical response when switching to SC formulations.8,9 One study from the United Kingdom found the transition from IV to SC formulation was overall cost-neutral to healthcare providers, but marginally increased direct costs.10 In the United States, SC formulations are most often covered on pharmacy insurance, unlike IV infusions, which are typically covered through medical insurance. Because biologic therapy is more expensive compared to traditional therapies, insurance companies sometimes resist biologic therapy approvals. Initial approval rates have been shown to be as low as 68%, requiring providers to submit an appeal or letter of medical necessity.11 Additional steps in the insurance approval process cause workload burden on providers and delay access to effective therapy.12

Studies have shown that the protracted time required for patients to be approved and initiate specialty biologic therapies for IBD can delay patient care and require intensive resources.13 Additionally, prior authorizations (PAs) have been associated with prolonged initiation time as well as increased healthcare utilization and costs.14 Given the recent FDA approval of SC vedolizumab and infliximab, there is limited research evaluating the insurance approval process, approval trends, and patient time to accessing therapy in the United States. Evaluating the prescription insurance medication access process for patients referred to transition to or initiate SC vedolizumab or infliximab could improve understanding of how long the medication access process takes and factors that may predict time to approval.

In European countries, patient acceptance of SC vedolizumab or infliximab has been favorable.15–18 Patients stated preferences for SC formulations include time savings, convenience, and shorter administration time compared to long IV infusions.17–19 However, patients with IBD may be more hesitant to change therapy given the impact of uncontrolled symptoms on their quality of life.20 More data is needed to understand patient preferences and reasons for a decision to switch to SC therapy or not in the United States. Identifying reasons patients may or may not initiate SC therapy can optimize pharmacist and provider resource utilization.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the vedolizumab and infliximab SC medication access process (approval via insurance or manufacturer’s patient assistance program [PAP]), factors that delay time to access, and factors that impact whether a patient starts SC vedolizumab or infliximab after approval.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was an ambispective study conducted at an academic medical center in the southeastern United States and approved by the center’s institutional review board. Patients were included if they had an IBD diagnosis (UC or CD) and were referred to start or transition to FDA-approved maintenance SC dosing of vedolizumab or infliximab from the medical center’s IBD provider between September 1, 2023 to December 31, 2024. Patients transitioning from IV maintenance therapy were included if they were on either FDA-approved or optimized dosing. Patients were excluded if they became lost to follow-up before the medication access process started (i.e., before the referred medication was pursued). Selection bias was minimized through consistent eligibility criteria and consecutive patient enrollment. Data were extracted from the electronic health record and the specialty pharmacy patient management system. All data collected was stored in REDCap, a HIPAA-secured data warehouse.21,22 Information bias was limited by using standardized, prospectively collected data with uniform definitions and trained abstractors, supported by routine data quality checks.

Setting

The medical center’s IBD clinic provides comprehensive care to patients with IBD using a multidisciplinary model. Three full-time, clinic-embedded specialty pharmacists and 3 pharmacy technicians from the specialty pharmacy manage specialty medication access, education, and treatment monitoring. Once patients are referred to start a new specialty medication from a provider, pharmacists collaboratively select the most clinically appropriate and cost-effective treatment and ensure baseline safety assessments have been completed. After discussing the treatment option with patients, pharmacists and pharmacy technicians navigate the approval and affordability process (Figure 1). Pharmacy technicians initiate the medication access process by completing a benefits investigation (BI) and PA as needed. If a PA is needed, submitted, and then denied by the insurance plan, pharmacists complete as many steps as required/available in the appeal process. This could include a first level appeal, second level appeal, external review, and/or peer-to-peer. If the insurance plan completely denies treatment or the patient is uninsured, pharmacists and financial counselors help patients apply for the manufacturer’s PAP or Quickstart program when available and the patient is eligible. The Quickstart program provides manufacturer-supplied medication to help patients begin therapy while an insurance denial is being resolved. If copays are unaffordable after insurance approval, pharmacy staff also pursue copay assistance or internal financial aid if eligible. Once the medication is approved and affordable, pharmacists order the SC medication to the appropriate pharmacy under a collaborative practice agreement, extensively educate patients on the medication, and coordinate shipment if the patient is utilizing the internal specialty pharmacy. Pharmacists then coordinate with the provider and infusion teams to alert them that the patient will begin SC maintenance treatment and to cancel any non-induction infusion orders still in place.

Medication access outcomes

Patients whose access was still pending or those who used a manufacturer QuickStart program as of April 18, 2025 were excluded from medication access outcomes. The primary outcome was time to medication access, defined as time from the referred medication access start date (date of PA submission or date BI performed) to date of access. For patients with multiple medication access attempts, time to access was calculated from time of first medication access attempt to final medication access outcome.

Secondary outcomes included: whether a patient was able to access SC maintenance therapy (yes/no); if yes, method of SC maintenance therapy access; initiation rate for the entire cohort and for the subgroup of patients able to access SC therapy; and reasons patients did not start therapy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described using median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Multiple logistic regression was used to evaluate factors associated with whether patients were able to access the referred medication, while ordinal logistic regression was used to test for associations between covariates and time to medication access. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Covariates included referral time from September 1, 2023, prescription insurance type, remission status, SC medication referred, and patient IV status at referral. Potential confounders were identified a priori based on clinical relevance and literature, and adjusted for using multivariable regression models.

Referral time was evaluated from September 1, 2023, because this was the month of vedolizumab SC FDA approval (infliximab was later approved on October 25, 2023, and it was hypothesized that time on market may impact access times). Remission status was defined as in remission or not based on clinical and endoscopic evaluation documented in the electronic health record. Patient IV status describes the 2 patient groups: patients referred to start SC maintenance after IV induction (new) and patients transitioning to SC maintenance after having received IV maintenance therapy (established). Prescription insurance type was categorized as commercial or noncommercial (Medicare, Medicaid, Tricare and no insurance).

Results

Cohort characteristics

Initially, 264 patients were referred to start or transition to maintenance SC vedolizumab or infliximab between September 1, 2023, and December 31, 2024. Two patients were excluded because they were lost to follow-up before the access process started (Figure 2). Table 1 displays characteristics of the 262 patients included. Median age was 44 years (IQR 34-56); approximately half (55%) were female. Most patients were White (89%) and with commercial prescription insurance (84%). Diagnoses included CD (53%) and UC (47%) with a median disease duration of 14 years (IQR 7-23). Most referrals were for vedolizumab (81%), and most patients were established on IV maintenance therapy (87%). Patients were referred for SC therapy most commonly due to patient decision (74%).

Access outcomes

There were 7 patients excluded due to the medication access in progress, and 7 due to starting through a QuickStart program, resulting in 248 patients included in the medication access analyses. Fourteen patients ended the access process early (6 due to patient decision, 2 provider decision, 1 lost to follow-up, 2 changed medications, 2 lost insurance, 1 financial constraints). Patients decided to end the access process early because they preferred infusions (n = 2), the process became too lengthy and/or complex (n = 3), or they did not feel ready to switch (n = 1). Providers decided to end the access process early due to the length of the process (n = 2). Most patients (n = 179, 72%) were able to access SC therapy.

In a logistic regression analysis examining medication access rate for SC therapy, the referred medication and insurance type were significantly associated with medication access (Figure 3). Holding all other variables constant, patients with an infliximab referral were about 4 times more likely to achieve medication access compared to those with a vedolizumab referral (OR: 4.1, 95% CI 1.3-12.7, P = .014). Patients with commercial pharmacy insurance were 70% less likely to access SC therapy compared to noncommercial patients (OR: 0.3, 95% CI 0.1-0.8, P = .022). Although not significant at the 0.05 level, patients not in remission at baseline were almost half as likely to access SC therapy than those who were in remission (OR: 0.5, 95% CI 0.2-1.1, P = .074).

Multiple regression analysis for the medication access rate for all patients able to access subcutaneous (SC) maintenance therapy. Patients with an infliximab referral were more likely to achieve medication access compared to patients with a vedolizumab referral (odds ratios [OR]: 4.1, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.3-12.7, P = .014) and patients with commercial pharmacy insurance were less likely to access SC therapy compared to noncommercial patients (OR: 0.3, 95% CI 0.1-0.8, P = .022).

Time to access

Median time to access was 11 days (IQR 1-43) with a range of 0-457 days. Insurance type was significantly associated with time to medication access. Patients with commercial pharmacy insurance were 3 times more likely to have a longer time to medication access compared to those with noncommercial insurance (OR: 3.40 95% CI 1.5-6.1, P = .003) (Figure 4). Fewer months from vedolizumab SC FDA approval to referral trended toward association with a longer time to medication access approval (per 6 months, OR: 1.5, 95% CI 0.96-2.4, P = .072). Similarly, patients not in remission at baseline were 90% more likely to have a longer time to medication access than patients in remission (OR: 1.9, 95% CI 0.8-4.2, P = .127).

The cumulative probability of approval by prescription insurance type truncated to the first 6 months of follow-up.

Method of access

In patients able to access treatment, over half were after an insurance PA approval (n = 100/179, 56%) and 22% (n = 39/179) via first-level appeal. Second-level appeal or external review was required in 20% (n = 35/179) of patients. All patients who required a second-level appeal and beyond had commercial insurance (Figure 5). One patient was approved without requiring a PA. Two patients achieved medication access via PAP after insurance denial, and 2 without insurance denial.

Method of approval for patients with prescription insurance.

Treatment initiation

Almost half of patients (n = 107/248, 43%) who started the medication access process did not start SC therapy, nearly a quarter due to insurance denial (n = 54/248, 22%). Of the 179 patients approved to start therapy, 21% of patients did not start therapy (n = 38/179), largely due to patient decision (45%, n = 17/38). Other reasons included financial constraints post approval (n = 7) and patients lost to follow-up (n = 8) (Figure 6). Patients chose not to start due to less frequent IV dosing schedule (n = 4), fear of self-injection (n = 1), medical insurance issues for IV formulation (n = 1), and 1 patient believed they may be changing biologic therapy (n = 1). Several patients had no clear documentation for why the IV formulation was preferred (n = 10). Financial constraints included high out-of-pocket copay concerns with Medicare prescription insurance (n = 5), high out-of-pocket copay concerns with Tricare prescription insurance (n = 1), and medical insurance denial for the induction IV (n = 1).

Discussion

In this study of new and established patients transitioning to SC vedolizumab or infliximab, almost half did not start SC therapy, largely due to insurance denials. For patients who were able to access therapy, the median time to medication access was nearly 2 weeks, with 5 patients taking longer than 6 months. Two patients waited over a year to receive SC therapy access. Patients with noncommercial insurance were more likely to be able to access SC doses and access them faster than those with commercial insurance. The referred SC medication also impacted time to approval, with patients prescribed infliximab accessing therapy faster. Once SC therapy was approved, the most common reason for patients who did not start SC therapy was due to patient decision, highlighting the need for proactive patient conversations before starting the medication access process. This study illustrates access requirements to make the transition from IV to SC in the United States and highlights reasons patients referred to start or transition to SC therapy may not start. Though non-initiation was mainly driven by insurance denials, patient and financial factors also play a role in starting or transitioning from IV to SC therapy.

Time to medication access: commercial versus noncommercial insurance

This is one of the first studies in the United States to evaluate the uptake of recently approved SC biologics by payers after the IV formulation has already been on the market. The current study took place within the timeframe vedolizumab and infliximab SC entered the market and payer preferences were being determined. Most patients (98%) required at least 1 step in the medication access process to access SC therapy, regardless of insurance type. Nearly half of patients had to pursue 1 or more levels of appeal. Patients with commercial insurance required more steps in the appeals process compared to those with noncommercial insurance, resulting in a longer time to medication access. In practice, payers review new therapies based on clinical and cost effectiveness; they may be less likely to approve medications through a pharmacy benefit if it is more cost effective to remain on the infused product. Results from the current study suggest that government insurance (primarily Medicare) may be less concerned with increased pharmacy spending in the context of reducing medical spending as patients transition from IV to SC, if patients may benefit from the transition. This may be due to the structure of total cost of care (medical and pharmacy) spending being calculated differently than commercial plans, which have the flexibility and incentives to control total plan costs.23,24

Delay in time to accessing advanced IBD therapies can lead to gaps in treatment, either between induction and maintenance dosing for newly referred patients or between maintenance doses in those established on therapy. Gaps in treatment due to delayed SC approval have been demonstrated to increase risk of disease flares and delayed remission.14,25,26 Disease flares decrease patients’ quality of life, increase hospitalizations, and may ultimately lead to the need for surgical interventions.4 Delays in medication access due to PAs have also been associated with increased IBD-related health care utilization and steroid use.11 PAs are commonly denied due to payer step therapy requirements, non-FDA label dosing requests, and the biologic not being listed on the plan’s preferred formulary. PA algorithms often trigger automatic denial without considering clinical rationale, delaying therapy over 2 weeks due to added steps like appeals or peer-to-peer reviews.11 Additionally, PAs are often required for SC biologic dose escalations, a common method to achieve or regain clinical remission in IBD. Previous data has shown that patients who needed dose escalations and had a longer time to insurance approval, were less likely to see improvement in C-reactive protein (CRP) levels.25 The current study adds to existing research by evaluating the access process in patients transitioning from IV to SC therapy. Ultimately, most patients in the current study were able to access therapy, often after several steps of the access process. Insurers/pharmacy benefits managers (PBMs) could reduce administrative burden and patient/provider burden by ensuring PA forms include all clinically necessary information that may lead to ultimate approval so fewer access steps are needed.

The center in which this study was conducted has a team of IBD specialty pharmacists that are a resource dedicated to assisting patients through all steps in the medication access, education, and monitoring process. Despite the value of these services, they are not reimbursable, and many patients face barriers such as insurance restrictions and PBM steering, which prevent them from filling prescriptions at the internal specialty pharmacy.27 Without clinic-embedded specialty pharmacists, these challenges would contribute to administrative burden across the healthcare system. The 2024 American Medical Association (AMA) PA physician survey found that physicians report that PAs somewhat or significantly increase physician burnout, with practices completing an average of 39 PAs per physician weekly.28 Integrated specialty pharmacy staff reduce the administrative burden posed by insurance restrictions by completing all access requirements in collaboration with the provider.29 We hypothesize that the time to medication access would have been longer and medication access rates lower without embedded specialty pharmacy services.30

Medication referred: vedolizumab versus infliximab

Previous literature has identified potential barriers to the adoption of new biologic therapies, including insurance restrictions, uncertain cost-effectiveness, and challenges in navigating managed entry agreements–structured arrangements between manufacturers and payers that govern patient access to specialty medications31,32 The current study showed that SC infliximab had a higher rate of approval compared to SC vedolizumab. Literature suggests that certain specialty medications may be adopted more rapidly than comparable agents following their release due to demonstrated clinical superiority or safety, favorable formulary placement (eg, quicker inclusion and fewer restrictions), and effective manufacturer engagement strategies such as PAPs and proactive payer negotiations.33 Based on the results of this study, infliximab may have benefited from stronger formulary placement and manufacturer engagement strategies. Its IV formulation has been available longer than IV vedolizumab, which may have contributed to more favorable coverage. Additionally, since IV infliximab is more commonly associated with infusion reactions, manufacturers may have been more motivated to transition patients to the SC formulation. SC infliximab was also approved 1 month after SC vedolizumab, however, this short time difference unlikely impacted access rates significantly. Though infliximab also offered a QuickStart program, which makes it easier for patients to start therapy sooner, patients who used it were excluded from the final analysis.

SC therapy abandonment

In patients referred to SC therapy, most did not start due to insurance denial. Surprisingly, almost a quarter of patients who were approved for SC treatment ended up not starting SC therapy. Most commonly, patients chose to remain on IV therapy. In this study, most patients were established on IV therapy before referral to SC therapy. Previous literature has found past or current SC or IV experience is a significant predictor for SC or IV preference.18 SC therapy is often appealing to patients because it may be more convenient and less time-consuming.16,18 However, patients may refuse the switch to SC due to fear of loss of efficacy, less frequent follow-ups, increased administration frequency, and fear of self-administered injection.17,18 Additionally, the duration of time a patient has been on IV therapy has been associated with the likelihood of switching, with the probability of patients accepting the switch to SC decreasing by 10% each year the patient was on IV therapy.17 Patients with IBD fear the return of IBD symptoms once they are in remission and once maintained on IV therapy, may be hesitant to change their regimen.20 Patients who had not used self-injection devices previously were also hesitant to switch to SC due to fear of self-injection. It is also possible that providers could have sent a referral for patients to switch to assess the ability for patients to obtain SC therapy (through insurance or other means) without having confirmation that the patient would transition to SC therapy if approved.

Other reasons for not starting therapy included the patient being lost to follow-up, financial constraints post-approval, changing providers or medications, and losing insurance coverage. The lengthy medication access process may have contributed to some patients becoming lost to follow-up. Integrated pharmacists use multiple mediums of communication (electronic patient portal, telephone, and mail), but some patients may choose to disengage, particularly if the process becomes a perceived burden.34 In addition to the 54 patients that insurance never approved therapy for, high out of pocket copays for either the induction infusion (billed on medical benefits) or the SC formulation (billed on pharmacy benefits) were the reason some patients did not start therapy. Additionally, there is a wide age range of patients with IBD, with a median of 44 years, indicating a younger population that may be more likely to relocate or change jobs/insurances.35 Before starting the medication access process, pharmacists should have proactive conversations with patients to provide education on the medication, identify patients’ preferences and goals, and the need to communicate if there are changes to patients’ insurance. The 2022 American Society of Health-System Pharmacists Survey of Health-System Specialty Pharmacy Practice: Clinical Services demonstrated that pharmacists integrated in clinics often counsel patients prior to medication selection.36 Early patient conversations will help identify patients likely to initiate SC therapy, saving time and resources for clinic-embedded pharmacists. The health system specialty pharmacy (HSSP) structure may allow for these proactive patient conversations to occur.

Limitations

The time during which the study took place is a potential limitation. SC formulations of vedolizumab and infliximab were FDA approved within the study time frame, and payers may not consider adding new SC biologics to the formulary until months after the therapy has been approved. However, this may also highlight how some payers delay formulary review when new therapies enter the market. Future studies should evaluate the overall, direct, and indirect costs of transitioning patients from IV to SC formulations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found that many patients referred to transition to SC infliximab or vedolizumab from IV formulation were unable to access therapy, experienced delays in access, or chose not to transition. Study results support previous literature evaluating other advanced IBD therapies, which demonstrate delays in initiating SC therapy due to insurance restrictions and patient concerns. The SC biologic authorization process, specifically for commercial insurance, prevents timely access to SC therapy. Because the medication access process requires many resources, embedded specialty pharmacists or providers need to have proactive conversations with patients before initiating the medication access process. Payers should adopt an evidence-based approach to PA algorithms to reduce the frequency of inaccurate denials that overlook the patient’s clinical picture and contribute to delays in medication access. Payers should also review newly FDA-approved therapies in a timely manner for potential new additions to their formulary. This study provides a benchmark to evaluate the uptake of IV to SC transition and challenges to the SC access process for other non-HSSP settings and IBD advanced therapies.
