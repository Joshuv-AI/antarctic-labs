Interacting non-Hermitian edge and cluster bursts on a digital quantum processor
Authors: Jin Ming Koh, Wen-Tan Xue, Tommy Tai, Dax Enshan Koh, Ching Hua Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dissipation can drive striking dynamics in quantum systems. A recent discovery is the non-Hermitian edge burst, where energy density transiently accumulates near boundaries rather than decaying uniformly. Previously observed in single-particle settings, this effect remains unexplored in interacting multi-particle systems, as tunable interactions are difficult to realize in conventional platforms, and non-Hermitian simulation is demanding on quantum processors. Here, we develop a framework for digital non-Hermitian Hamiltonian simulation that composes a linear-combination-of-unitaries scheme and product formulae. The method is efficient in classical preprocessing costs and circuit sizes. Implementing an interacting quantum ladder model on a superconducting quantum processor, we observe edge-burst signatures in up to 64 unit cells, directly probe dissipative-gap closing, and identify interaction-driven phenomena: spatially extended and patterned edge bursts, and cluster bursts in the bulk. Our study establishes digital quantum processors as a versatile platform for non-Hermitian physics.

Introduction

While closed quantum-mechanical systems are described by Hermitian Hamiltonians, a large range of open quantum systems can be effectively described by non-Hermitian Hamiltonians1–5 that model coupling to an external environment. This description captures a variety of physical platforms, including loss-engineered optical structures6,7, superconducting and circuit-QED components, and other open systems that emulate dissipative quantum dynamics8–10. Beyond the rich physics conferred by non-Hermiticity, non-Hermitian quantum systems have also been harnessed for their distinctive properties in the development of ultra-sensitive sensors and detectors11–13, among other technologies.

Recently, the non-Hermitian edge burst 14–18 has emerged as a particularly striking manifestation of dissipative dynamics, in which dissipation becomes transiently and anomalously concentrated at system boundaries over time. While its static counterpart, the non-Hermitian skin effect (NHSE)19–23, is now well-established even in interacting many-body settings24–28, the edge burst remains less explored and its behavior under many-body dynamics is unknown. Extending it to the many-body regime raises fundamental questions: how interactions reshape the collective flow of dissipation, and whether the interplay between boundary bursts and particle correlations can lead to qualitatively different phenomenology.

Digital quantum simulation provides a compelling opportunity to explore this regime, as they enable the effective implementation of tunable many-body interactions that are otherwise difficult to realize in engineered analog systems29,30. While digital quantum processors are increasingly utilized for quantum dynamics31–38 and various areas of (Hermitian) condensed-matter simulation39–49, their use in studying non-Hermitian physics is relatively nascent owing to difficulties in scalably realizing non-unitary time-evolution on unitary gate-based hardware, particularly on near-term noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices. Demonstrations to-date are restricted to small system sizes and single-particle models or rely on resource-intensive protocols50–55.

In this work, we report realizations of both single-body and multi-particle interacting generalizations of the non-Hermitian edge burst on superconducting transmon-based quantum hardware. We overcome the aforementioned limitations by developing an efficient and general digital quantum simulation framework for non-unitary quantum dynamics. Our approach leverages a linear combination of unitaries (LCU) circuit construction within a stepwise time-evolution sequence. To accommodate anti-Hermiticity in the Hamiltonian, we utilize an ancilla-assisted circuit block that performs controlled forward and backward Hermitian time-evolution, and which is reused in each timestep via mid-circuit resets. Our simulation method uses a small constant number of ancillary qubits, does not require exponentially expensive classical preprocessing nor large numbers of intermediary measurements or variational iterations, and accommodates arbitrary interactions in multi-particle settings—marking an advance over previous approaches limited to small-sized or single-particle systems.

We apply our method to probe a minimal quantum ladder supporting the non-Hermitian edge burst. Beyond observing signatures of the conventional single-body non-interacting edge burst on up to 64 unit cells, we show on quantum hardware that introducing sequences of density-density interactions leads to the formation of spatially extended and patterned versions of the edge burst, and in certain regimes can also lead to bursts occurring in the bulk of the system far from boundaries, which we dub cluster bursts. These results extend edge burst physics into the multi-particle setting, in particular demonstrating how particle interactions and correlations modify dissipative transport and localization in non-Hermitian systems. In the near term, our work establishes digital quantum simulation as a promising platform for studying correlated non-Hermitian dynamics.

Observing signatures of the non-Hermitian edge burst

A key benchmark of our approach is that the non-Hermitian edge-burst dynamics can be implemented faithfully on a quantum processor at negligible classical preprocessing cost through trotterized time-evolution, without further sophisticated quantum circuit optimization (e.g., circuit recompilation) or variational circuit tuning. This indicates that, even at extended lattice sizes, the same LCU-based quantum simulation method can be executed directly on quantum hardware in a scalable fashion to observe the physical phenomenology of interest (assuming progress in quantum hardware continues to drive noise rates down).

Demonstration of edge burst on larger lattices

We next investigated models with larger unit-cell counts. Aside from demonstrating the versatility of our quantum simulation approach, experiments on larger ladders also minimize finite-size effects and produce clearer evidence of the non-Hermitian edge burst. Here, we realize an N = 64 unit-cell (128-site) ladder, which is 8 times the system size examined in the previous section.

At these larger lattice sizes, trotterization and standard circuit transpilation produce circuits far too deep for present NISQ hardware to feasibly accommodate—naïve execution of these circuits would result in near-complete decoherence of the quantum state and vanishing signal-to-noise ratios when measuring observables. To overcome this present limitation, we employed an additional tensor-network-aided circuit recompilation technique45–47,60 for circuit compression, which replaces components of the circuits with approximate lower-depth parametrized ansatzes that are variationally optimized (see Methods). In this process, we exploited symmetries of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\mathcal{H}}}}}^{{{{\rm{eb}}}}}$$\end{document}Heb, such as number conservation, to enhance circuit construction performance and quality. We emphasize that circuit recompilation was only required here to remain within current NISQ hardware limitations. As hardware progress continues to drive qubit and gate error rates down, our time-stepping LCU-based approach can revert to time-evolution circuit construction schemes of negligible classical cost (e.g., product formulae), thus retaining scalability at classical and quantum levels. The low overhead and operational simplicity set our method apart from prior methods employed in quantum simulation, such as quantum imaginary-time evolution schemes59,60,62 which demand tomographically complete measurements within a correlation domain at every time step to construct the next, hardware-efficient block-encodings53,54 that require exponentially growing classical resources, or variational methods in specific settings50,55,61,63 entailing measurements and classical optimization run iteratively.

Prior theoretical analysis14 indicated that the closing of the dissipative gap Δ Δ is a necessary condition for the edge burst to occur in addition to the presence of the NHSE. Intuitively, the zero imaginary eigenenergy modes suffer no decay during time evolution and are long-lived, thus enabling an initial wavepacket driven by the NHSE to reach the boundary of the system. The particle is then trapped against the boundary by the NHSE and decays, giving rise to the edge burst. In contrast, upon opening of the dissipative gap, any wavepacket suffers exponential loss during propagation and cannot reach the boundary before decay. This qualitative difference was clearly observed, for example, in our experimental results in Fig. 3. Indeed, in all our experiments, the edge burst regime—where edge burst signatures are present—occur when v1 ≤ v2, and the trivial regimes occur when v1 > v2. Indeed, our results demonstrate the closure of the dissipative gap and the change in edge burst phenomenology across the transition boundary directly on a quantum processor.

Spatially extended and patterned edge bursts with multiple interacting particles

We now turn to our key set of results: edge burst phenomena observed with multiple interacting particles. Systems hosting strongly interacting quantum particles are difficult to realize on classical or effectively single-body simulators, such as electrical (i.e., topolectrical) circuits30,64–66 or waveguide photonic systems29,67,68, that have thus far been used to study non-Hermitian physics—let alone achieving tunability of interaction strengths, ranges and types. In this respect, digital quantum simulation on quantum hardware, making use of the full many-body Hilbert space hosted by the device and programmable quantum operations in that space, presents a clear versatility advantage. The quantum simulation approach we developed easily accommodates arbitrary interactions in the Hamiltonian (see Methods), and here we exploit this versatility to explore non-Hermitian edge burst phenomenology enriched by interactions.

To start, we examined two particles (hardcore bosons) on an N = 12 ladder in the edge burst regime with U1 > 0 interactions switched on—that is, repulsive density-density interactions acting between sites on the ladder a range r = 1 apart (see Fig. 1b for illustration). The energy scale of the interaction was comparable to those of the non-interacting parts of the Hamiltonian (hoppings v1, v2 and loss γ). Here, interestingly, from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{x}$$\end{document}Px we observe the manifestation of a spatially extended version of the edge burst, spanning two unit cells (x ∈ {1, 2}) instead of the typical single unit cell in vanilla edge bursts. The mechanism underlying this spatial extension stems from a Pauli exclusion-like effect arising from the interactions. At short times, both particles expectedly diffuse to the left under the NHSE, but as the first impacts on the x = 1 boundary, the hardcore nature of the bosons and the U1 interactions inhibit the second from also occupying the first unit cell—effectively, the second boson encounters a virtual boundary at x = 2. This is akin to the formation of the so-called Fermi skin in fermionic non-Hermitian systems25,69, wherein fermions are pushed up against a boundary by the NHSE and thereby exhibit a Fermi surface in real space. Here, the second boson is trapped at the x = 2 unit cell and decays, giving rise to a spatially extended edge burst occurring over two unit cells. In the trivial regime, the edge burst does not manifest, as shown in Fig. 5b.

Moreover, we observed that by tuning the range of interactions, spatially patterned manifestations of the edge burst can arise. In Fig. 5c we present experimental results on an N = 14 ladder hosting two bosons with Ur > 0 switched on for r ≤ 3, and in Fig. 5d we present results with Ur > 0 for r ≤ 5. In the former, we witness a spatially extended edge burst occupying alternate unit cells, and in the latter the edge burst is separated by two unit cells of small escape probability. The cartoon insets in Figs. 5c and d highlight these patterns. We refer to these as δ = 2 and δ = 3 patterned edge bursts, respectively, where δ records the unit-cell separation of the edge burst (i.e., escape probability) peaks; the spatially extended edge burst in Fig. 5a then corresponds to δ = 1, and the conventional single-unit-cell edge burst (e.g., Figs. 2 and 3) has δ = 0. The mechanism underlying the δ > 1 patterned bursts here is similar to the δ = 1 setting, but here the longer-ranged interactions induce virtual boundaries located farther from the first boson at x = 1.

The same phenomenon occurs when p > 2 particles are present, which gives rise to spatially extended edge bursts occurring over p unit cells. Generally, the presence of Ur > 0 interactions for r ≤ 2d − 1 can produce δ = d patterned edge bursts; with p bosons, the same δ separation in peaks occurs p times starting from the left boundary of the ladder (the drive direction of the NHSE). In Supplementary Note 4B, we describe additional experiments conducted with three bosons yielding a three-unit-cell spatially extended edge burst. We also observed qualitatively similar δ = 2 and δ = 3 patterning under longer-range interactions on the three-boson setup. Likewise, we verified that the edge bursts do not arise in the trivial regime.

In the settings examined here (in Fig. 5), the initial conditions were such that the bosons do not significantly infringe into the spatial ranges of Ur interactions under the dynamics of the system—i.e. they do not approach too closely to one another—until they impact on the left boundary. This allows the discussed physical mechanism to carry through unimpeded. As the leftward drift induced by the NHSE is approximately uniform, this condition is simple to achieve and boils down to the initial localization of the bosons. Indeed, in Figs. 5a, c, d, with Ur > 0 for r ≤ 2d − 1, the bosons were initially separated by at least 2d + 1 sites on the ladder or equivalently ~d unit cells. When this condition is not satisfied, a different type of interaction-driven burst phenomenology can occur, as we describe next.

Interaction-induced cluster bursts

Finally, we demonstrate that with appropriately configured interactions, anomalously high escape probabilities can be induced at chosen locations on the quantum ladder away from boundaries, in fact regardless of whether the Hamiltonian is in the (canonical) edge burst or trivial regimes. We term this phenomenon cluster bursts. Unlike the canonical edge burst, cluster bursts need not occur at the edges of the system and arise intrinsically from interactions; there is no analogous single-body non-interacting counterpart known at present.

We show in Fig. 6a experimental results on an N = 14 ladder in the canonical edge burst regime (v1 ≤ v2) hosting two bosons. The bosons were initially localized on the a-sublattices of unit cells x0 = 8, 9, and we enable density-density interaction U1 of energy scale comparable to the non-interacting parts of the Hamiltonian (v1, v2, γ). Thus, the initial conditions are such that the particle separation condition discussed in the previous section is not clearly satisfied—in particular, a single hop of either boson toward the other, via either v1 or v2 channels, places them within the range of interactions and causes a significant energy change. This same phenomenon can also arise when the initial localizations of the bosons are inside the range of interactions, such that outward hopping puts them outside their interaction range and causes an energy change. In this case, the bosons decay and a cluster burst emerges within the spatial confines of the interaction range. We show an example on the same N = 14 ladder in Figs. 6c and d tuned into the edge burst and trivial regimes, respectively. As before, the bosons were initialized on unit cells x0 = 8, 9, but here interactions Ur > 0 were switched on for r ≤ 3. In both edge burst and trivial regimes, we observe clearly a cluster burst in the vicinity of the initial boson locations, far from boundaries.

The mechanism underlying these cluster bursts stems from a combination of the spectral structure of the system and initialization of the bosons on the ladder. First, the strong density-density interactions Ur of finite range produce a clear separation in the energy scale of eigenstates dependent on particle separation, and the initialization of the system is such that only one of the two spectral components carries weight (i.e., overlap with the initial state). Under interaction-induced deformation of the spectrum, this component need not exhibit an imaginary gap closing in both v1/v2 < 1 and v1/v2 > 1 regimes, and the purely negative imaginary eigenenergies result in rapid wavefunction decay before the bosons can reach the boundary. Thus the edge burst does not emerge regardless of the v1/v2 identification of nontrivial and trivial regimes. (See Supplementary Note 4C for further details.)

Second, the energy separation in the spectral components induces a slowdown of dynamics due to (partially) forbidden state transitions. Recall that energy is conserved in time-translation-symmetric closed quantum systems—i.e., the energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{tr}}}}({\omega }_{t}{{{\mathcal{H}}}})$$\end{document}tr(ωtH) of a quantum state ωt is fixed under time evolution by a Hermitian Hamiltonian \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{H}}}}$$\end{document}H. Strong interactions can then cause energy differences between states connected by the Hamiltonian so large as to inhibit transitions, as there is no available linear combination of accessible states after the transition that maintains the conserved energy. Indeed, this mechanism underlies a doublon decay phenomenon observed on Hubbard models70,71, where pairs of excitations in proximity exhibit exponentially long lifetimes, and is also responsible for the well-studied Stark many-body localization72,73. A similar understanding holds in a non-Hermitian context, with the modification that energy is not exactly conserved but there exists nonetheless a speed limit on how fast energy can change in the system, imposed by the Hamiltonian (see Supplementary Note 1B). On the quantum ladder, particle movement becomes constrained, and this slowdown in dynamics freezes the bosons long enough that they decay largely in their initial locations, generating the cluster burst observed.

In the scenarios demonstrated in Fig. 6, for example, two simultaneous hoppings of the bosons must occur for them to move along the NHSE drive direction without changing their interaction energy, which is a second-order process and happens with low amplitude. In the unlikely event that these higher-order hoppings occur and the bosons reach the boundary of the system, spatially extended edge bursts of small amplitude can additionally manifest, as can be seen in Fig. 6a. (Further technical elaboration of the cluster burst mechanism is provided in Supplementary Note 4C.)

Discussion

While the experimental frontier of non-Hermitian condensed-matter physics has to-date enjoyed a spectacular period of progress utilizing broad palettes of custom-designed analog optical, metamaterial, and classical simulator platforms, the direct realization of important classes of non-Hermitian systems on near-term (i.e., NISQ) quantum platforms has remained limited. The advantages offered by digital quantum simulation are clear and tantalizing: the natural ability to access the many-body Hilbert space of arbitrary Hamiltonians, and in association, liberal versatility in tuning interactions of different ranges, strengths, and types.

But leveraging quantum processors to probe non-Hermitian systems is challenging, as quantum gate sets are unitary and thus sophisticated methods must be developed to achieve the non-unitary evolution associated with non-Hermitian Hamiltonians. Moreover, the overhead incurred in doing so invariably runs up against qubit error and lifetime limitations. Nevertheless, recent studies of various condensed-matter phenomena on digital quantum computers, ranging from discrete time crystals39,74,75 to topological phases39,43–47,76–78, illustrate promising capabilities despite hardware constraints. It is therefore timely to investigate the prospects of studying non-Hermitian quantum physics on quantum hardware.

Here, we probed the recently discovered phenomenon of non-Hermitian edge bursts on transmon-based superconducting quantum processors, and observed at high fidelity the signatures of the edge burst on up to 64 unit cells of a lossy quantum ladder model. Furthermore, by incorporating sequences of density-density interactions, we unveiled the possibility of engineering spatially extended and patterned variants of the edge burst, as well as bursts occurring away from boundaries, which we dubbed cluster bursts. Our present realization of the non-Hermitian edge burst on an intrinsically quantum platform, together with our experimental study of its interacting variants, complements prior experimental efforts on the canonical non-interacting version of the edge burst79,80. To enable this advance, we developed a general methodology for efficient non-Hermitian Hamiltonian simulation on digital quantum processors, leveraging a linear combination of unitaries circuit construction technique with ancillary qubit re-use, which is applicable to generic non-Hermitian models beyond the scope of the edge burst.

We comment on several avenues for further work. The first is the prospect of exploring finite-size scaling relations of edge burst phenomena on quantum hardware. The canonical single-particle edge burst is associated with a scaling relation between the boundary loss peak and representative bulk loss. Concretely, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{{\rm{edge}}} \sim d^{-\alpha} $$\end{document}Pedge~d−α and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{{\rm{bulk}}} \sim d^{-(\alpha + 1)} $$\end{document}Pbulk~d−(α+1), for escape probability P and initial particle distance to the boundary d, are expected14. By running copies of our experiments at various N and d, this scaling relation could be directly measured. But as the single-particle edge burst is increasingly well-understood, a more interesting question lies in mapping out the possibility of analogous bulk-edge scaling relations in the multi-particle interacting setting. The quantum simulation approach developed here is particularly well-suited for exploring such interacting physics.

A second direction regards integrating more sophisticated readout strategies. At present, computational-basis readout is well-matched to the primary observable of the edge burst, namely the site-resolved escape probabilities reconstructed from occupation densities. However, the enriched phenomenology of the edge burst with interactions motivates a broader set of diagnostics than density profiles alone. Randomized local measurements, as in shadow tomography81–84, could allow the same family of evolved states to be post-processed for many observables, such as connected or higher-order density correlators, subsystem entanglement, and structure factors resolving spatial patterning. The ability to measure diverse diagnostics, albeit at higher overhead, can enable finer-grained probes of the physics involved15.

Lastly, we remark on the prospect of quantum advantage. The present experiments demonstrate our methods for non-Hermitian Hamiltonian simulation but are not beyond classical capabilities. A limitation to the classical hardness of simulation is that, at fixed particle number p, the dimensionality of the Hilbert space accessed under dynamics grows polynomially with lattice size N as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{O}}}}\left({N}^{p}\right)$$\end{document}ONp, not exponentially. Moreover, the physics of the edge burst and its interacting analogs studied here require neither very large N nor p, or breaking number conservation, to manifest. However, theoretical opportunities exist for quantum advantage even in few-body simulation85–88. For example, on condensed-matter lattice Hamiltonians, an advantage in the dimensionality of the lattice manifests45. Thus, with sufficiently good hardware and suitably choosing a model to study, there remains exciting potential for quantum simulation to present meaningful utility.

The present work opens the door for future experimental investigation of quantum non-Hermitian condensed-matter physics. As quantum hardware continues to advance, and digital quantum computation moves toward early fault-tolerance, we anticipate that similar low-overhead methods will enable the direct study of more sophisticated systems exhibiting rich intertwined physics, such as critical versions of non-Hermitian pumping26 and the interplay between non-Hermiticity and entanglement phase transitions89.

Methods

Quantum hardware

We used IBM transmon-based superconducting quantum devices in our experiments. These included 27-qubit devices ibm_hanoi and ibm_mumbai hosting the Falcon processor, 127-qubit devices ibm_sherbrooke, ibm_osaka, ibm_kyoto and ibm_nazca hosting the Eagle processor, and a 133-qubit device ibm_torino hosting the Heron processor. The basis gate sets of all devices comprise 1-qubit gates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{{{{\rm{X}}}},\sqrt{{{{\rm{X}}}}},{{{\rm{RZ}}}}\}$$\end{document}{X,X,RZ}, with RZ implemented virtually via framechanges90, and 2-qubit gate CX for Falcon processors, echoed cross resonance (ECR) for Eagle processors91, and CZ for the Heron processor. The CX, ECR, and CZ gates are equivalent up to 1-qubit rotations. We construct all experiment circuits using CX gates, including the application of protocols for error suppression and mitigation such as Pauli twirling and randomized gate folding for zero-noise extrapolation (see below), and transpile to the native 2-qubit gate of each device before execution. Typical performance metrics of the devices, such as relaxation T1 and dephasing T2 times, gate and readout error rates, and gate times, are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Fock-sector error detection

In our experiments, the number of available qubit states may exceed the number of system states—therefore some qubit states may be unused in state encoding. These qubit states have no physical meaning and should not be involved in time-evolution of any physical system state. Nonetheless, hardware noise may cause these states to be erroneously populated. We discard circuit shots that measured occupation of these states, as these results are unphysical and indicate hardware error.

We utilized several techniques to achieve circuits with fewer CX gates and lower depth than naïve construction. For isolated subsets of Pauli strings of weight ≤2 sharing the same support, we invoked KAK decomposition96, which produces circuit components for U(4) unitaries comprising 1-qubit rotations and at most 3 CX gates. For subsets of Pauli strings of weight ≤3 sharing the same support, we used the QSEARCH algorithm97 in the public BQSKit toolkit98 to produce circuit components, which promises optimal-depth synthesis for unitaries up to 4 qubits.

For remaining Pauli strings of larger weight, we used a circuit construction procedure that takes advantage of simultaneous diagonalization99. We partitioned the Pauli strings into commuting groups by finding a clique cover on their commutation graph. (Determining the minimum clique cover, which is an NP-complete problem in the worst case, is not necessary for this method to work, though generally the fewer the number of cliques, the smaller the resultant circuit.) Readout error mitigation

The quantum devices used in our experiments present non-negligible probabilities (~1–3%) of reporting a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\vert 1\right\rangle$$\end{document}1 outcome when the measured qubit is in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\vert 0\right\rangle$$\end{document}0 and vice versa, typical for current-era quantum hardware. This is referred to as readout error and is caused, for example, by ambiguity in microwave signal characteristics (i.e., IQ data) that inhibit classification of measurement outcomes. We mitigated the effect of these errors by characterizing the measurement bit-flip probabilities of the qubits used, then performing linear inversion to approximately recover the true measurement counts from observed measurement counts124–126. Unlike prior works45–47,124–126, our experiment circuits contain mid-circuit measurements—in particular a qubit can be measured multiple times throughout the circuit. We thus employed a modified scheme as described below.

Dynamical decoupling

Idle periods on qubits are invariably present in our experiment circuits, as a qubit may need to wait for gates to complete on other qubits before being involved in 2-qubit operations with them. During idle periods, qubits are still subject to decoherence (e.g., dephasing noise and thermal relaxation). Dynamical decoupling131, which runs through alternating basis changes that multiply to the identity, can be used to suppress the effects of such decoherence. At a basic level, dynamical decoupling refocuses the dephasing experienced by the qubits such that the accumulated noise contributions cancel to a certain order.

We employed the common XY4 pulse sequence131,132 for dynamical decoupling in our experiments. The sequence is inserted for idle periods not exceeding the equivalent of 2 CX gate durations (~ 800–1000 ns); for longer idle durations, repetitions of the sequence are inserted. While there exist more sophisticated dynamical decoupling sequences that cancel noise to higher orders133,134, empirical results135 suggest that simple sequences (e.g. XY4) can be more performant in practice, due to the presence of errors on the gates introduced by the sequence and differences between hardware noise backgrounds and theoretical assumptions.

Pauli twirling

We applied randomized twirling of CXs on each experiment circuit. Each CX gate in the circuit is replaced by P1(CX)P2, where (P1, P2) are randomly drawn Paulis such that the action of the twirled CX remains invariant. See Supplementary Table 6 for a complete list of (P1, P2) pairs used. In general, the twirling of gates in circuits, also referred to as randomized compiling in literature136,137, has the effect of converting coherent and non-Markovian error sources into stochastic errors which can be averaged over in an experiment. Pauli twirling, in particular, converts error processes into diagonal Pauli channels138. We used Pauli twirling in conjunction with zero-noise extrapolation.

Zero-noise extrapolation

A general method for mitigating inaccuracy of expectation values due to hardware noise is zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE), which functions by collecting additional data at amplified noise levels and then extrapolating into the zero-noise limit42,138–141. We employ a gate-level implementation of ZNE in our experiments; alternative pulse-level methods have been explored in the literature125,142. Our procedure was as follows.
