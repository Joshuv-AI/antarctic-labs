Stability results for regularized least-squares problems via generalized Hessian expressions and monotone generalized equations
Leo Smulansky, Tim Hoheisel, Tran T.A. Nghia

Abstract
. We study perturbation and stability properties of solution mappings associated with convex regularized least-squares problems. We first establish an implicit function theorem for generalized equations governed by maximally monotone operators and smooth perturbations, yielding conditions for local Lipschitz continuity, directional differentiability, and semismoothness of solution mappings. We then characterize the kernel of generalized Hessians of convex functions through the subspace parallel to the subdifferential under 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} -cone reducibility assumptions on the conjugate of the regularizer, thereby replacing difficult second-order objects by tractable first-order conditions. As applications, we derive stability results for broad classes of regularized least-squares problems, including weighted polyhedral support-function regularizers and piecewise linear-quadratic penalties. Our framework unifies and extends several recent results on Lipschitz stability for LASSO-type models and monotone generalized equations.



1.
Introduction

Sensitivity
or
stability analysis
constitutes a central theme in optimization theory. Broadly speaking, one seeks to understand how solutions of optimization problems and generalized equations vary under perturbations of the underlying data. Classical developments in this direction were driven largely by smooth nonlinear programming and variational inequalities, beginning with the foundational works of Robinson
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
and Kojima
[
24
]
. We refer the reader to the standard reference by Bonnans and Shapiro
[
10
]
for a very comprehensive account of the state-of-the art (even in infinite dimensions) up to the early 2000s. Over the last two decades, however, the rapid emergence of modern applications in machine learning, statistics, imaging, compressed sensing, and signal processing has shifted attention toward nonsmooth and structured optimization models involving, e.g., sparsity-promoting or low-complexity regularizers. Such problems often exhibit rich variational structure while simultaneously lacking smoothness or even polyhedrality.
Among the most prominent examples are sparse (or low-rank) recovery problems such as the LASSO
[
46
,
49
]
, group LASSO
[
52
]
, square-root LASSO
[
3
,
4
]
, and nuclear norm minimization
[
11
,
16
,
37
]
. The vast majority of these problems are of the form

(1.1)

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|^{2}+\lambda r(x)

where
\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm squared,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a linear (forward) operator,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a measurement vector,
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter,
and
r:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is a closed, proper, convex regularizer that promotes structure on the solution.

Remark 1.1
.

Without much further effort, we could consider the (seemingly) more general problem where
\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
is replaced by any convex and (locally)
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth fidelity term. This, however, just increases notational burden, and the focus of this paper is on different classes of regularizers.

1.1.
Contributions

The overarching goal of this paper is to contribute to the study of the variational-analytic properties of the solution map of (
1.1
), i.e., the map

(1.2)

\displaystyle S(A,b,\lambda):=\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|^{2}+\lambda r(x).

Like the majority of results on solution maps to parametric optimization problems, in particular the ones pointed out in
Section

1.2
below, we approach the study of
S
via the fact that
S
can be equivalently written using the optimality conditions for (
1.1
), i.e.,

(1.3)

\displaystyle S(A,b,\lambda)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\left|\;0\in\frac{1}{\lambda}A^{T}(Ax-b)+\partial r(x)\right.\right\}.

Our study is now divided into three steps:

I) We first provide a general implicit function theorem (
Proposition

3.6
) for solution maps

S(p)=\left\{x\,\left|\;0\in f(p,x)+F(x)\right.\right\}

defined by a generalized equation governed by maximally monotone and set-valued part
F
and a smooth part
f
that is monotone in
x
. This, obviously, covers the generalized equation for
S
in (
1.3
).
This implicit function theorem provides sufficient conditions for local Lipschtiz continuity and directional differentiability of
S
as well
semismoothness*
, a more recent property coined by Gfrerer and Outrata
[
20
]
, intimately connected to the more traditional notion of semismoothness popularized by Qi and Sun in their seminal paper
[
36
]
. The obligatory regularity condition for an implicit function theorem in our case is simply the celebrated
Mordukhovich criterion

[
25
,
26
,
44
]
for metric regularity of the set-valued map
x\mapsto f(\bar{p},x)+F(x)
around the parameter
\bar{p}
in question. This criterion here reads

(1.4)

\ker D_{x}f(\bar{p},\bar{x})\;\cap\;\ker D^{*}F(\bar{x}|-f(\bar{p},\bar{x}))=\{0\},

where
D^{*}
is the coderivative operator (see
Section

2
for details) applied to the set-valued map
F
.

II) When applied to (
1.3
), the Mordukhovich criterion (
1.4
) simplifies to

(1.5)

\ker A\cap\ker\partial^{2}r(\bar{x}|-f(\bar{p},\bar{x}))=\{0\},

where
\partial^{2}r:=D^{*}(\partial r)
is the
generalized Hessian
operator (see
Section

2
for details) of the convex regularizer
r
. Since this object involves a coderivative operator (applied to a subdifferential operator) it is, in general, arduous to compute. Therefore, as a second main contribution, we show in
Theorem

4.6
that
for (closed, proper, convex) functions whose (Fenchel) conjugate is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible
the kernel of the generalized Hessian can be expressed via the
subspace parallel to the subdifferential
- an object which is much more expedient to compute.

III) As a third main contribution, we prove stability results for the solution map (
1.2
) for various choices of
r
:

i)

We establish semismoothness (in particular local Lipschitz continuity and directional differentiability) for the case when
r^{*}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible (
Theorem

5.1
).

ii)

We prove explicit Lipschitz bounds and expressions for the directional derivative for the case where
r
is the composition of a polyedral support function and a linear map (
Corollary

5.6
).

iii)

We discuss the case where
r
is a
piecewise linear-quadratic (PLQ)
without appealing to
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducibility (
Theorem

5.13
), and prove quantitative results in the special case where
r
is a PLQ penalty in the sense of
[
44
, Example 11.18]
(
Corollary

5.14
).

1.2.
Recent related work

The related work recently published decomposes into roughly three camps based on what variational-analytical tools are being employed. One line of research, to which this paper belongs, employs and refines tools from variational analysis à la Mordukhovich
[
32
,
33
]
, Rockafellar and Wets
[
44
]
and Dontchev and Rockafellar
[
15
]
based on graphical differentiation. Representative contributions include the work of Berk et al.
[
5
,
6
]
for LASSO and Square-root LASSO, where results on Lipschitzness and directional differentiability are obtained through second-order variational analysis in a particular instance of this paper here. Other work on LASSO-type problems exploits the explicit polyhedral structure of
\ell_{1}
-type regularization problems
[
22
,
27
]
. More recently, Cui et. al
[
14
]
established a new perspective based on Robinson’s strong regularity of suitably constructed dual problems and the geometry of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible conjugates. Their framework yielded elegant first-order characterizations for Lipschitz stability, tilt stability, and full stability of broad classes of convex regularized least-squares problems while bypassing explicit second-order calculations on the nonsmooth regularizer itself. A central feature of this approach is that the relevant generalized second-order conditions arise automatically from the structure of the dual problem and the geometry of the epigraph of the conjugate regularizer. Our work here adds to that by also providing quantitative results, and by using an implicit function theorem for the the primal problem rather than looking at strong regularity of the dual.

Another, rather different approach was developed by Bolte et al.
[
7
,
8
]
who studied differentiability properties of solution mappings to monotone inclusion problems (thus including optimality condtions of convex problems) through the lens of
path differentiability
and
conservative Jacobian calculus
.

Finally, stability analysis for linear least-squares problems in the form of (
1.1
) based on
partial smoothness
can be found in the body of work by Vaiter et al., e.g.,
[
50
,
51
]
.

2.1.1.
Tools from convex analysis

For a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, its
epigraph
is the set
\mathrm{epi}\;f:=\left\{(x,\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\,\left|\;f(x)\leq\alpha\right.\right\}
. Its
domain
is
\mathrm{dom}\;f:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\left|\;f(x)<\infty\right.\right\}
. We call
f

proper
if
\mathrm{dom}\;f\neq\emptyset
and
f>-\infty
. We say that
f
is
closed
if
\mathrm{epi}\;f
is closed, and
convex
if
\mathrm{epi}\;f
is convex.

The
(convex) subdifferential
of
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
at
\bar{x}
is

\displaystyle\partial f(\bar{x}):=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ |\ f(\bar{x})+\langle v,x-\bar{x}\rangle\leq f(x),\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

The
(Fenchel) conjugate
of
f
is the function
f^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

f^{*}(y):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\{\langle x,y\rangle-f(x)\}.

As a special case of conjugacy, we note that for any (nonempty) set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its
indicator (function)

\delta_{C}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\delta_{C}(x):=\begin{cases}0,&x\in C,\\
+\infty,&x\notin C\end{cases}

has the conjugate

\sigma_{C}(y):=\delta_{C}^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in C}\left\langle y,\,x\right\rangle,

which we call the
support function of
C
. In turn, if
C
is closed and convex we have also that
\sigma_{C}^{*}=\delta_{C}
.
We call a proper function
f

polyhedral convex
(or
convex piecewise linear
) if
\mathrm{epi}\;f
is polyhedral convex, i.e., the interection of finitely many half space. This is equivalent to
f
having the form

f(x)=\max_{i=1,\dots,r}\{a_{i}^{T}x+\beta_{i}\}+\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(x)

for some (nonempty) polyhedron
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
(a_{i},\beta_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\;(i=1,\dots,r)
. In particular, compositions of polyhedral convex functions with affine maps are polyhedral convex.
We point out that
[
44
, Theorem 11.14]

f
is polyhedral convex if and only if
f^{*}
is. In particular,
\sigma_{C}
is polyhedral convex if and only if
C
is polyhedral.

It is known
[
44
, Theorem 11.1]
that
f
is closed, proper, convex if and only if
f=f^{**}(:=(f^{*})^{*})
in which case we have the important relation
[
44
, Proposition 11.3]

(2.1)

\partial f^{*}=(\partial f)^{-1},

where this inversion has to be understood in the set-valued sense, to which we allude now.

2.1.2.
Tools from set-valued analysis

For a set-valued map
S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}

\underset{x\to\bar{x}}{\operatorname{Lim\,sup}}\ S(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\ |\ \exists\{x^{k}\}\to\bar{x},\ \{y^{k}\in S(x^{k})\}\to y\}.

\displaystyle T_{\Omega}(\bar{x})=\underset{t\downarrow 0}{\operatorname{Lim\,sup}}\frac{\Omega-\bar{x}}{t}.

When

\displaystyle T_{\Omega}(\bar{x})=\mathrm{cl}\left\{w\,\big|\,\exists\lambda>0\text{ such that }\bar{x}+\lambda w\in\Omega\right\}.

We define the

to

as

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{\Omega}(\bar{x})=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ |\ \langle v,x-\bar{x}\rangle\leq o(\lVert x-\bar{x}\rVert),\ \forall x\in\Omega\}

and the
(limiting) normal cone
to
\Omega

as

N_{\Omega}(\bar{x})=\underset{x\to\bar{x}}{\operatorname{Lim\,sup}}\ \hat{N}_{\Omega}(x).

At a point

, the
graphical derivative

DS(\bar{x}|\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}

\displaystyle DS(\bar{x}|\bar{y})(w):=\{z\ |\ (w,z)\in T_{\operatorname{gph}S}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\},

and the

is the set-valued map
D^{*}S(\bar{x}|\bar{y}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle D^{*}S(\bar{x}|\bar{y})(u):=\{v\ |\ (v,-u)\in N_{\operatorname{gph}S}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}.

A simple observation that follows from the definition is the following.

Lemma 2.1

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

The following is a sum rule for the graphical derivative and coderivative, respectively, in the presence of continuous differentiability.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
44

)

Let

For a convex function

Theorem 2.5

(Mordukhovich criterion)
.

Let

Linear algebra results

We present here some auxiliary results from linear algebra which are useful to our study.

The first two lemma are more generally relevant and certainly known, but we provide proofs for completeness.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times n}
,
U,V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{l}
subspaces. Then:

(a)

M^{-1}(V^{\perp})=(M^{T}(V))^{\perp}
;

(b)

\left(M^{-1}(U)\right)^{\perp}=M^{T}(U^{\perp})
.

Proof.

(a) We have

\displaystyle M^{-1}(V^{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{x\,\left|\;Mx\in V^{\perp}\right.\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{x\,\left|\;\left\langle x,\,M^{T}v\right\rangle=0\;,\,\forall v\in V\right.\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{x\,\left|\;\left\langle x,\,s\right\rangle=0\;,\,\forall s\in M^{T}(V)\right.\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(M^{T}(V))^{\perp}.

(b) We have

\displaystyle(M^{-1}(U))^{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(M^{-1}\left((U^{\perp})^{\perp}\right))^{\perp}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}

\displaystyle\left(\left(M^{T}(U^{\perp})\right)^{\perp}\right)^{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M^{T}(U^{\perp}).

∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
C,D\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric positive semidefinite matrices.
Then,
I+CD
is invertible.

Proof.

Take
v\in\ker(I+CD)
, i.e.,
v+CDv=0
.
Left-multiplying this expression by
(Dv)^{T}
, we get

\displaystyle v^{T}Dv+v^{T}DCDv=0.

By positive semidefiniteness of
C
and
D
,
both
v^{T}Dv=0
and
v^{T}DCDv=0
. However,
v^{T}Dv=0

implies that
Dv=0
, thus
0=v+CDv=v
, so we
have
v=0
. This proves the statement.
∎

The last lemma is more tailored to our specific needs and will come into play in our quantitative analysis of the solution maps in
Corollary

5.14
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric positive semidefinite,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

be a subspace such that
\ker A\cap E=\{0\}
. Let

and let

have columns
\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\cdots u_{r}\end{bmatrix}
which form an orthonormal basis of

.
Then the following hold:

(a)

\ker AHU=\{0\}
;

(b)

H(\ker A)\subseteq\ker A
;

(c)

\ker AU=\{0\}
;

(d)

(AHU)^{T}AU
is symmetric positive definite.

Proof.

(a)
\ker A\cap E=\{0\}
gives us that
H^{-1}(\ker A)\cap H^{-1}(E)=\ker H=\{0\}
, where the latter uses

2.7
. Observe that

, and that
\operatorname{rge}U=H^{-1}(E)
by construction. Thus,
\ker AH\cap\operatorname{rge}U=\{0\}
, which implies
\ker AHU=\{0\}
.

(b) Let

. Then,
Hy=y+CA^{T}Ay
, but

, so
Hy=y
. Thus,
Hy\in\ker A
,
which tells us

.

(c) Let

, so by (c),

.
This gives us

, hence

\ker AU=\{0\}
.

(d)
Note that

\displaystyle(AHU)^{T}(AU)=U^{T}\left(I+A^{T}AC\right)A^{T}AU=(AU)^{T}(AU)+(AU)^{T}C(AU).

Thus,
(AHU)^{T}(AU)
is symmetric and by (c),

∎

, Remark 2]
for several other classes of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible functions.

The central result of this section is obtaining the formula for the kernel of the generalized Hessian of
conjugate
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible functions
, i.e., functions whose conjugates are
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible. In order to do so, we need the landmark definition of tilt stability
[
35
]
introduced by Poliquin and Rockafellar and several related results.

Definition 4.2

(Tilt stability)
.

Given a function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, we say that a point
\bar{x}\in\operatorname{dom}\varphi

is a
tilt-stable minimizer
of
\varphi
if there exists
\gamma>0
such that the mapping

\displaystyle M_{\gamma}:v\mapsto\operatorname{argmin}\{\varphi(x)-\left\langle v,\,x\right\rangle\ |\ x\in\mathbb{B}_{\gamma}(\bar{x})\}

is single-valued and Lipschitz continuous on some neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
M_{\gamma}(0)=\bar{x}
.

We recall
[
35
, Theorem 1.3]
, with some modifications for the convex case.

Theorem 4.3

(Characterization of tilt-stability via the generalized Hessian)
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lsc, convex function. Suppose
\bar{x}
is a minimizer of
\varphi
with
0\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
.
Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

The point
\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable minimizer of
\varphi
.

(ii)

The generalized Hessian
\partial^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|0)
is positive definite, in the sense that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left\langle z,\,x\right\rangle>0\text{ whenever }z\in\partial^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|0)(w),\ w\not=0.

A large portion of the following result is taken from
[
14
]
.

Theorem 4.4

(Characterization of tilt stability for convex problems)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a twice continuously differentiable
convex function and let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper lsc convex function.
Suppose that
\bar{x}\in\operatorname{argmin}f+g
. Then
\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable minimizer of
f+g

if and only if

(4.4)

\displaystyle\ker\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})\cap\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})=\{0\}\text{ where }\bar{v}=-\nabla f(\bar{x}).

Moreover, if
\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable minimizer of
f+g
, we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle\ker\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})\cap\operatorname{par}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})=\{0\}.

If, additionally,
g^{*}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible at
\bar{v}
, then (
4.5
) is also sufficient for tilt stability of
f+g
at
\bar{x}
.

Proof.

Define
\varphi:=f+g
and note that
0\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
, as
\bar{x}
is a minimizer of
\varphi
. By the second-order subdifferential sum rule or
Lemma

2.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\partial^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|0)(w)=\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})w+\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})(w)\ \text{ for all }w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

If
\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable minimizer of
\varphi
, we claim (
4.4
). Indeed, pick any
w\in\ker\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})\cap\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})
. The above equation leads us to

0\in\partial^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|0)(w)
.
By
Theorem

4.3
, particularly (
4.3
),
w=0
. This verifies (
4.4
). On the other hand, if (
4.4
) holds, applying
Proposition

3.6
(a) with
f(p,x)=\nabla f(x)
and
F=\partial g(x)
tells us that
\partial\varphi=\nabla f(x)+\partial g(x)
is strongly metrically regular at
(\bar{x},0)\in\operatorname{gph}\partial\varphi
. Since
\varphi
is convex, this ensures
\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable minimizer of
\varphi
; see e.g.,
[
1
]
.

The necessary and sufficient condition for tilt stability in (
4.5
) follows from
[
14
, Theorem 4.3 and Theorem 4.5]
, when the conjugate function
g^{*}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible at
\bar{v}
.
∎

Part of the following lemma is from
[
48
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible at
\bar{u}\in{\rm dom}\,g
. Then,
h
is twice epi-differentiable at
\bar{u}
, and
\partial h
is proto-differentiable at
\bar{u}
for every
\bar{v}\in\partial h(\bar{u})
.

Proof.

The twice epi-differentibility of
h
at
\bar{u}
for
\bar{v}\in\partial h(\bar{u})
follows from
[
48
, Proposition 3.1]
. By
[
42
, Theorem 2.2]
and convexity of
h
, this is equivalent to proto-differentiability of
\partial h
.
∎

The following result from sheds light onto the relationship between the subspace parallel to the subdifferential of the the conjugate of a convex function and the kernel of its generalized Hessian.

Theorem 4.6

(The kernel of the generalized Hessian of convex functions)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a proper, lsc, convex function and
(\bar{x},\bar{v})\in\operatorname{gph}\partial g
.
We have

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{par}\,\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})\subseteq\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})\cup(-\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})).

If
g^{*}
is twice epi-differentiable at
\bar{v}
, then we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{par}\,\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})\subseteq\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v}).

Moreover, if
g^{*}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible at
\bar{v}
, the above inclusion turns to equality

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{par}\,\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})=\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v}).

Proof.

To justify (
4.6
), pick any
w\in\operatorname{par}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})\setminus\{0\}

and define
L:=\operatorname{span}\{w\}
. Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix with
\ker A=L
.
Define
f(x):=\frac{1}{2}\lVert Ax-A\bar{x}\rVert^{2}-\bar{v}^{T}x
.
Note that
\nabla f(\bar{x})=-\bar{v}
and

\displaystyle\ker\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})=\ker A^{T}A=\ker A=L.

It follows that

\displaystyle\ker\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})\cap\operatorname{par}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})=L\not=\{0\}.

By
Theorem

4.4
,
\bar{x}
is not a tilt-stable minimizer of
f+g

and

\ker\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})\cap\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})\not=\{0\}.

Hence, there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\alpha w\in\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})
, i.e.,

(0,-\alpha w)\in N_{\operatorname{gph}\partial g}(\bar{x},\bar{v})
.
As
N_{\operatorname{gph}\partial g}(\bar{x},\bar{v})
is a (not necessarily convex) cone and
\alpha\not=0
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle(0,w)\in N_{\operatorname{gph}\partial g}(\bar{x},\bar{v})\text{ or }(0,-w)\in N_{\operatorname{gph}\partial g}(\bar{x},\bar{v}),

which implies that
w\in\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})\cup(-\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v}))
for any
w\in\mathrm{par}\,\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})\setminus\{0\}
. This verifies
the inclusion (
4.6
).

To prove the inclusion “
\subseteq
” in (
4.7
), suppose further
g^{*}
is twice epi-differentiable
at
\bar{v}
. By the convexity and closedness of
\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})
, we have that
[
41
, Theorem 6.3]

\displaystyle\bar{x}\in\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})=\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{ri}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})).

Hence, there exists a sequence
\{x^{k}\}\subseteq\operatorname{ri}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})
such that
x^{k}\to\bar{x}
.
We claim that

(4.9)

\displaystyle T_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})}(x^{k})=\operatorname{par}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})\subseteq\ker D\partial g(x^{k}|\bar{v}).

The equality holds due to the fact that
x^{k}\in\operatorname{ri}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})
and

\displaystyle T_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})}(x^{k})=\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cone}(\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})-x^{k}))=\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{par}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v}))=\operatorname{par}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v}).

To see the inclusion on the right-hand side of (
4.9
),
take
w\in T_{\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})}(x^{k})
. There exist sequences
\{t_{k_{n}}\}\downarrow 0
and {
w^{k_{n}}\}\to w

such that
x^{k}+t_{k_{n}}w^{k_{n}}\in\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})
for all
n
.
It follows that

\displaystyle(x^{k},\bar{v})+t_{k_{n}}(w^{k_{n}},0)=(x^{k}+t_{k_{n}}w^{k_{n}},\bar{v})\in\operatorname{gph}\partial g,

which gives that
0\in D\partial g(x^{k}|\bar{v})(w)
or equivalently,
w\in\ker D\partial g^{*}(\bar{v}|x^{k})
. Since
g^{*}
is twice epi-differentiable at
\bar{v}
,
g
is twice epi-differentiable at any
x_{k}
for
\bar{v}
by
[
44
, Theorem 13.21]
. The Rockafellar-Zagrodny derivative-coderivative inclusion (
[
44
, Theorem 13.57]
) tells us that

\displaystyle D\partial g(x^{k}|\bar{v})\subseteq\partial^{2}g(x^{k}|\bar{v}).

This along with (
4.9
) implies that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{par}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})\subseteq\ker\partial^{2}g(x^{k}|\bar{v})=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\,(0,-w)\in N_{{\rm gph}\,\partial g}(x_{k},\bar{v})\}.

As the normal cone
N_{{\rm gph}\,\partial g}
is a closed set-valued mapping,
letting
x^{k}\to\bar{x}
in the above inclusion gives us that
\operatorname{par}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})\subseteq\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})
,
which shows the “
\subseteq
” inclusion in (
4.7
).

Next, let us suppose that
g^{*}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible at
\bar{v}
. By applying
Lemma

4.5
for
h=g^{*}
,
g^{*}
is twice epi-differentiable at
\bar{v}
. Hence, (
4.7
) holds. We just need to justify the inclusion “
\supseteq
” in (
4.8
). To do so, repeat some of the arguments from the beginning of the proof:
Take
z\in\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})\setminus\{0\}
and define
L:=\operatorname{span}\{z\}
.
Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix with
\ker A=L
. Define
f(x):=\frac{1}{2}\lVert Ax-A\bar{x}\rVert^{2}-\bar{v}^{T}x
.
Again, we have
\nabla f(\bar{x})=-\bar{v}
and
\ker\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})=L
. As
z\in L\cap\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})
,
\bar{x}
is
not a tilt-stable minimizer of
f+g
. By
Theorem

4.4
,
L\cap\operatorname{par}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})\not=\{0\}
. Thus,
z\in\operatorname{par}\partial g^{*}(\bar{v})

for every nonzero
z\in\ker\partial^{2}g(\bar{x}|\bar{v})
.
∎

The most important part for our study is the equality (
4.8
) which furnishes a simple way of computing the generalized Hessian of a (closed, proper) convex function whose conjugate is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible and thus a tractable way of verifying the key condition (
3.4
) to deploy the implicit function theorem in
Proposition

3.6
to study the solution map (
1.2
).

\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible regularizers (and beyond)

The following theorem extends the recent result

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
S:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}_{++}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the solution of problem (
1.1
) given by

\displaystyle S(A,b,\lambda):=\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\lVert Ax-b\rVert^{2}+\lambda r(x)\right\}.

Let
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda},\bar{x})\in\operatorname{gph}S
and

\bar{v}:=-\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{A}^{T}(\bar{A}\bar{x}-\bar{b})
. Suppose that
r^{*}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible at
\bar{v}
. Then the following condition

(5.1)

\displaystyle\ker\bar{A}\cap\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})=\{0\}

is equivalent to any of the following conditions:

(a)

S
is single-valued at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.

(b)

S
is locally Lipschitz at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.

(c)

S
is directionally differentiable
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.

Additionally, the following condition implies (
5.1
), and if

\partial r
is semismooth* at
(\bar{x},\bar{v})
, then it is equivalent to (
5.1
):

(d)

S
is semismooth at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.

Proof.

Apply
Proposition

3.6
to
F=\partial r
, and
f
defined by
f(p,x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}A^{T}(Ax-b)
, as was alluded to earlier.
If condition (
5.1
) is satisfied, by (
4.8
) the Mordukhovich criterion (
3.4
) that reads

\displaystyle\ker\bar{A}\cap\ker\partial^{2}r(\bar{x}|\bar{v})=\{0\}

holds. This gives that
S
is (a) single-valued and (b) locally Lipschitz at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.
By

4.5
,
\partial r^{*}
is proto-differentiable at
\bar{v}
, so by
[

, Proposition 8.41]
,
\partial r
is proto-differentiable at
\bar{x}
, therefore

3.6
also tells us that
S
is (c) directionally differentiable at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.
When
\partial r
is semismooth* at
(\bar{x},\bar{v})
,

3.6
tells us that
S
is semismooth* at

. Thus, by

3.3
,

is semismooth at
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.

On the other hand, (b), (c), and (d) all imply (a), which by

Remark 5.2

(Conjugate
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible functions)

The class of proper, closed, convex functions with Fenchel conjugates which are
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible includes polyhedral convex functions and support functions of

-cone reducible sets (which encompasses the

[

for a more comprehensive list.

5.1.
Regularizers that are weighted polyhedral support functions

Theorem

5.1
is a solely qualitative statement. In order to obtain quantitative results regarding the stability of the solution map
S
, we need to impose more structure on the regularizer
r
to arrive at computable expressions. In fact, we will first study the special case where the regularizer
r
has the form
r=\sigma_{\mathcal{P}}\circ M
, i.e., a support function of a polyhedral set composed with a linear map. This encapsulates, in particualar, all (weighted) polyhedral norms, and therefore stability results for the (weighted) LASSO
[
5
,
18
]
. Functions of this form are polyhedral and are known to be
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone-reducible (
[
14
, Remark 3.6(a)]

We start our study of this special case by a simple conjugacy result which appeals, in particular, to the fact that the support and indicator of a polyehdral set are polydreal convex functions.

Lemma 5.3

(Conjugate of
\sigma_{\mathcal{P}}\circ M
)
.

Consider
r=\sigma_{\mathcal{P}}\circ M
where
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
is a polyhedron and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
.
Let

(\bar{x},\bar{v})\in\operatorname{gph}\partial r
and set

(5.2)

\Lambda(\bar{x},\bar{v}):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\ |\ M^{T}y=\bar{v},\ y\in N_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}(M\bar{x})\}.

Then

\displaystyle\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})=M^{-1}(N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})),\ \forall\bar{y}\in\Lambda(\bar{x},\bar{v}).

In particular, we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})=\operatorname{span}M^{-1}(N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})),\ \forall\bar{y}\in\Lambda(\bar{x},\bar{v}).

Proof.

Using the relations
\partial r^{*}=(\partial r)^{-1}
and
\partial\sigma_{\mathcal{P}}=N_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}
as well as the subdifferential chain rule
[
41
, Theorem 23.9]
with the fact that
\sigma_{\mathcal{P}}
is polyhedral, we find:

\displaystyle u\in\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})\iff\bar{v}\in M^{T}N_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}(Mu)\iff\exists y\in N_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}(Mu):\;v=M^{T}y

So, if
\bar{y}\in\Lambda(\bar{x},\bar{v})
, then

\displaystyle\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})=M^{-1}(N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})).

And because
0\in\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})=\mathrm{span}\,M^{-1}(N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y}))\quad\mbox{for any}\quad\bar{y}\in\Lambda(\bar{x},\bar{v}).

∎

The next example shows that, in general, the

cannot be moved inside in (

Example 5.4
.

Let
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}
,
\mathcal{P}=[0,1]^{2}
,
\bar{x}=0

\bar{v}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\

First, we verify that

.

M^{T}\bar{y}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\

Thus,

\displaystyle M^{-1}N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ \begin{pmatrix}z_{1}+z_{2}\\

The next result is a technical lemma needed for the upcoming stability result.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\{(x_{k},v_{k})\in\operatorname{gph}\partial r\}\to(\bar{x},\bar{v})
, and let
\bar{y}\in\Lambda(\bar{x},\bar{v})

\{y_{k}\in\Lambda(x_{k},v_{k})\}\to\bar{y}

Because

Let

\displaystyle S(b,\lambda)=\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\lVert Ax-b\rVert^{2}+\lambda r(x)\right\},

\displaystyle\ker A\cap\operatorname{span}M^{-1}(N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y}))=\{0\}

(a)

is semismooth (in particular, single-valued, locally Lipschitz, and directionally differentiable) at

Proof.

is polyhedral convex, so by

is also polyhedral convex, and is therefore
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible.
Also, polyhedral convex functions are in particular, piecewise linear-quadratic, so by

, Proposition 3]
, we have that
\partial r
is semismooth* at every point.
Lemma

tells us that
\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})=\mathrm{span}\,M^{-1}(N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y}))

It will be useful going forward to parameterize

\{z\ |\ P^{T}z\preceq\beta\}
for
P\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times k}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and
we will use
p_{i}
to refer to the
i
th column of
P
.
Proposition
3.6
tells us that the directional derivative of
S
in direction
(q,\alpha)

is given by the unique
w
which satisfies

\displaystyle 0\in DG(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda},\bar{x}|0)(q,\alpha,w)

where
G(b,\lambda,x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}A^{T}(Ax-b)+\partial r(x)
.
Observe that for any
\bar{y}\in\Lambda(\bar{x},\bar{v})
, by
[
30
, Theorem 7.2]
, we have this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}A^{T}\left(q+\frac{\alpha}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})-Aw\right)\in D\partial r(\bar{x}|\bar{v})(w)=M^{T}D(N_{\mathcal{P}})^{-1}(M\bar{x}|\bar{y})(Mw).

Now, observe that

\displaystyle z\in D(N_{\mathcal{P}})^{-1}(M\bar{x}|\bar{y})(Mw)\iff Mw\in DN_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y}|M\bar{x})(z)=N_{\mathcal{K}}(z),

where
\mathcal{K}=T_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})\cap\{M\bar{x}\}^{\bot}
is the

, by
[
44
, Example 13.44]
.
Notice that

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{K}}(z)=N_{T_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})}(z)+N_{\{M\bar{x}\}^{\bot}}(z)=\operatorname{cone}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in I(\bar{y}),\ p_{i}^{T}z=0\}+\operatorname{span}\{M\bar{x}\}

where
I(\bar{y}):=\{i\ |\ p_{i}^{T}\bar{y}=\beta_{i}\}
.
We claim that
M\bar{x}\in\operatorname{cone}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in K\}
. To see this, let
K:=\{i\in I(\bar{y})\ |\ p_{i}^{T}z=0\}
. Set
C:=\operatorname{cone}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in K\}\subseteq N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})
.
We know that
M\bar{x}\in N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})
, and we in fact claim that
M\bar{x}\in C
.
We know
M\bar{x}
can be written as
\sum_{i\in I(\bar{y})}\gamma_{i}p_{i}
for
\gamma_{i}\geq 0
.
Using the fact that
z^{T}(M\bar{x})=0
with the fact that
p_{i}^{T}z<0
for
i\in I(\bar{y})\setminus K
, (which come from
z\in T_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})
and
z\in\{M\bar{x}\}^{\bot}
) we get

\displaystyle 0=\sum\limits_{i\in I(\bar{y})}\gamma_{i}p_{i}^{T}z=\sum\limits_{i\in K}\gamma_{i}\underbrace{p_{i}^{T}z}_{=0}+\sum\limits_{i\in I(\bar{v})\setminus K}\gamma_{i}\underbrace{p_{i}^{T}z}_{<0}.

So
\gamma_{i}=0
for
i\not\in K
. We can then write

\displaystyle Mw\in C+\operatorname{span}\{M\bar{x}\}.

Set
E:=\operatorname{span}M^{-1}(C)
and notice that

\displaystyle E=\operatorname{span}M^{-1}(C)\subseteq M^{-1}\operatorname{span}C=M^{-1}(\operatorname{rge}P_{K}).

Since
M\bar{x}\in C
, we have
\bar{x}\in E
. Therefore,
Mw\in C
, so
w\in E
.
Then, since
z\in\ker P_{K}^{T}=(\operatorname{rge}P_{K})^{\bot}
, we have

(5.5)

\displaystyle M^{T}z\in M^{T}((\operatorname{rge}P_{K})^{\bot})\overset{\eqref{lem:Sub}}{=}\left(M^{-1}(\operatorname{rge}P_{K})\right)^{\bot}\subseteq E^{\bot}.

Now, let
q_{1},...,q_{r}
be an orthonormal basis of
E

and set
Q=\begin{bmatrix}q_{1}\cdots q_{r}\end{bmatrix}
. Then,
QQ^{T}
is the orthogonal projection onto
E
, and consequently
QQ^{T}w=w
.
Setting
\bar{u}:=\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})
, we now infer that

\displaystyle 0\overset{\eqref{eq:inEperp}}{=}Q^{T}(\lambda M^{T}z)=Q^{T}A^{T}(q+\alpha\bar{u}-Aw).

Using
w\in E
, this implies

\displaystyle(AQ)^{T}AQQ^{T}w=(AQ)^{T}(q+\alpha\bar{u}).

Since
E\subseteq\operatorname{span}M^{-1}N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})
, using (
5.4
) gives

\ker A\cap\operatorname{rge}Q=\{0\}
, so
(AQ)^{T}(AQ)
is invertible.
Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle w=Q[(AQ)^{T}(AQ)]^{-1}(AQ)^{T}(q+\alpha\bar{u}).

In view of (
3.6
), this establishes the directional derivative of
S
at
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
in direction
(q,\alpha)
is given by

\displaystyle S^{\prime}(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda};q,\alpha)=Q[(AQ)^{T}(AQ)]^{-1}(AQ)^{T}(q+\alpha\bar{u}).

We now argue (
5.4
) holds locally:

to this end, take any sequence
(b_{k},\lambda_{k})\to(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
with
x_{k}=S(b_{k},\lambda_{k})\to\bar{x}
and
v_{k}:=\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}A^{T}(Ax_{k}-b_{k})\in\partial r(x_{k})\to\bar{v}.
Then for
\bar{y}\in\Lambda(\bar{x},\bar{v})
, by
Lemma

5.5
, there exists
y_{k}\in\Lambda(x_{k},v_{k})\to\bar{y}
, and thus (for
k
sufficiently large)

I(y_{k})\subseteq I(\bar{y})

hence
\partial r^{*}(v_{k})\subseteq\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})
.
Consequently,

\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(v_{k})\cap\ker A\subseteq\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})\cap\ker A,

for
k
sufficiently large. Therefore, assuming (
5.4
) at
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
yields that this property holds for all
(b,\lambda)
sufficiently close.

Hence, by reiterating the above reasoning for nearby points,
S
is directionally differentiable at
(b,\lambda)
sufficiently close to
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
, and
S^{\prime}((b,\lambda);(\cdot,\cdot))
is, in particular, continuous. Thus, from
Proposition

3.6
(c) we infer that

L:=\limsup_{(b,\lambda)\to(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})}\max_{\|(q,\alpha)\|\leq 1}\|S^{\prime}((b,\lambda);(q,\alpha))\|

is a local Lipschitz bound for
S
at
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
. Now let
(b_{k},\lambda_{k})\to(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
such that

\max_{\|(q,\alpha)\|\leq 1}\|S^{\prime}((b_{k},\lambda_{k});(q,\alpha))\|\to L.

As
S^{\prime}((b_{k},\lambda_{k});(\cdot,\cdot))
is continuous (for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
), there exists
\{(q_{k},\alpha_{k})\in\mathbb{B}\}\to(\bar{q},\bar{\alpha})\in\mathbb{B}
such that

\|S^{\prime}((b_{k},\lambda_{k});(q_{k},\alpha_{k}))\|\to L.

Let
x_{k}\in S(b_{k},\lambda_{k})\to\bar{x}
,
v_{k}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}A^{T}(Ax_{k}-b_{k})\in\partial r(x_{k})\to\bar{v}
. With
Lemma

5.5
, we choose

y^{k}\in\Lambda(x_{k},v_{k})\to\bar{y}
. Let

K_{k}\subseteq I(y_{k})=\left\{p_{i}\,\left|\;\left\langle p_{i},\,y_{k}\right\rangle=\beta_{i}\right.\right\}.

be the associated index set from earlier with
(b_{k},\lambda_{k},x_{k},v_{k},y_{k},q_{k},\alpha_{k})
(instead of
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda},\bar{x},\bar{v},\bar{y},\bar{q},\bar{\alpha})
).
By finiteness, we may assume w.l.o.g. that
K_{k}=K\subseteq I(\bar{y})
. And thus we can assume w.l.o.g. that for some subspace
E
we have

E_{k}=\mathrm{span}\,M^{-1}(\mathrm{cone}\,\left\{p_{i}\,\left|\;i\in K_{k}\right.\right\})\equiv E\subseteq\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v}).

for the associated subspace
E_{k}
. Now let
q_{1},\dots,q_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an orthonormal basis of
E
such that
QQ^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
Q=[q_{1},\dots,q_{t}]
is the orthogonal projection onto
E
. Since
E\subseteq\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})
, we can pad
Q
to a matrix
\bar{Q}:=[Q\;\hat{Q}]
whose columns form an orthonormal basis of
\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})
such that
\bar{Q}\bar{Q}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})
.

With our derivations above we therefore find that

\displaystyle\|S^{\prime}((b_{k},\lambda_{k});(q_{k},\alpha_{k}))\|\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|Q[(AQ)^{T}(AQ)]^{-1}(AQ)^{T}\left[q_{k}+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}(Ax_{k}-b_{k})\right]\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|[(AQ)^{T}(AQ)]^{-1}\|\cdot\left\|(AQ)^{T}\left[q_{k}+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}(Ax_{k}-b_{k})\right]\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}((AQ)^{T}(AQ))}\cdot\left\|(AQ)^{T}\left[q_{k}+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}(Ax_{k}-b_{k})\right]\right\|.

Now observe that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}((AQ)^{T}(AQ))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{(Qx)^{T}A^{T}AQx}{\|x\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{y\in E\setminus\{0\}}\frac{y^{T}A^{T}Ay}{\|y\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min_{y\in\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})\setminus\{0\}}\frac{y^{T}A^{T}Ay}{\|y\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}((A\bar{Q})^{T}(A\bar{Q})).

Here the first identity uses, e.g.,
[
23
, Theorem 4.2.6]
, while the second one relies on the fact that
\left\{Qx\,\left|\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\right.\right\}=E\setminus\{0\}
, and that
\|Q^{T}y\|=\|y\|
for all
y\in E
. The inequality uses that
E\subseteq\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})
, and the last identity uses the arguments from the first one and that the columns of
\bar{Q}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})
.
Using this bound we get

\displaystyle\|S^{\prime}((b_{k},\lambda_{k});(q_{k},\alpha_{k}))\|\ \leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}((A\bar{Q})^{T}(A\bar{Q}))}\cdot\left\|(AQ)^{T}\left[q_{k}+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}(Ax_{k}-b_{k})\right]\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A\bar{Q})^{2}}\cdot\left\|(AQ)^{T}\left[q_{k}+\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}(Ax_{k}-b_{k})\right]\right\|.

Taking the limit in
k
, we get

\displaystyle L\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A\bar{Q})^{2}}\cdot\left\|(AQ)^{T}\left[\bar{q}+\frac{\bar{\alpha}}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\right]\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A\bar{Q})^{2}}\left(\max_{q\in\mathbb{B}}\lVert(AQ)^{T}q\rVert+\max_{\alpha\in[-1,1]}\lVert\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}(AQ)^{T}\alpha(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\rVert\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A\bar{Q})^{2}}\left(\lVert(AQ)^{T}\rVert+\lVert\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}(AQ)^{T}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\rVert\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A\bar{Q})^{2}}\left(\sigma_{\max}(AQ)+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}\lVert(AQ)^{T}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\rVert\right).

By a similar argument to the one above, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}((AQ)^{T}AQ)=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{x^{T}Q^{T}A^{T}AQx}{\lVert x\rVert^{2}}=\max_{y\in E\setminus\{0\}}\frac{y^{T}A^{T}Ay}{\lVert y\rVert^{2}}\leq\lambda_{\max}((A\bar{Q})^{T}A\bar{Q})

which implies that
\sigma_{\max}(AQ)\leq\sigma_{\max}(A\bar{Q})
.
This gives a further bound of

\displaystyle L\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A\bar{Q})^{2}}\left(\sigma_{\max}(A\bar{Q})+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}\lVert(AQ)^{T}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\rVert\right).

Finally, because
\bar{Q}^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}Q^{T}\\
\hat{Q}^{T}\end{bmatrix}
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A\bar{Q})^{2}}\left(\sigma_{\max}(A\bar{Q})+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}\lVert(AQ)^{T}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\rVert\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A\bar{Q})^{2}}\left(\sigma_{\max}(A\bar{Q})+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}\lVert(A\bar{Q})^{T}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\rVert\right).

which gives

\displaystyle L\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A\bar{Q})^{2}}\left(\sigma_{\max}(A\bar{Q})+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}\lVert(A\bar{Q})^{T}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\rVert\right).

∎

Example 5.8

(LASSO)
.

A popular choice of regularizer which can be written in the form
\sigma_{\mathcal{P}}\circ M
is the
\ell_{1}
norm, in which case (

) becomes the celebrated LASSO problem
[
49
]
. Stability results for the solution map of the LASSO problem can be found in
[
5
]
. In order to apply
Corollary

5.6
, we take
\mathcal{P}=\{z\,|\,P^{T}z\preceq\beta\}
for

,
\beta
equal to the vector with every component equal to one (which makes
\mathcal{P}

(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda},\bar{x})\in\operatorname{gph}S

\bar{v}=-\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}A^{T}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})

5.3

\{\bar{v}\}

\displaystyle\ker A\cap\mathrm{span}\,\{e_{i}\ |\ i\in J(\bar{v})\}=\{0\}.

This written as simply

\displaystyle\ker A_{J(\bar{v})}=\{0\}.

.

\displaystyle S^{\prime}(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda};q,\alpha)=I_{K}\left((A_{K}^{T}A_{K})^{-1}A_{K}^{T}(q+\frac{\alpha}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\right)

\displaystyle L\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A_{J})^{2}}\left(\sigma_{\max}(A_{J})+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}\lVert A_{J}^{T}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\rVert\right).

Note that this agrees with the result in
[

\diamond

5.2.
PLQ penalties as regularizers

As discussed in
Lemma

4.5
, a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible function is twice epi-differentiable. However, a twice epi-differentiable may be not
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible. In particular, convex piecewise linear-quadratic functions (in the sense of
[
44
, Definition 10.20]
), which are twice epi-differentiable, are not necessarily
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible,
as shown in the following example.

Example 5.9
.

Let
r^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

\displaystyle r^{*}(v_{1},v_{2})=v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+|v_{1}v_{2}|=\begin{cases}v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+v_{1}v_{2},&\text{if }v_{1},v_{2}\geq 0,\\
v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}-v_{1}v_{2}&\text{if }v_{1}\leq 0,v_{2}\geq 0,\\
v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+v_{1}v_{2}&\text{if }v_{1}\leq 0,v_{2}\leq 0,\\
v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}-v_{1}v_{2}&\text{if }v_{1}\geq 0,v_{2}\leq 0.\\
\end{cases}

Thus,
r^{*}
is a convex piecewise linear-quadratic function; so is
r
by
[
44
, Theorem 11.14]
.
Set
\bar{v}=(0,0)
and note that

\displaystyle\partial r^{*}(v_{1},v_{2})=\begin{cases}(2v_{1}+v_{2},2v_{2}+v_{1})&\text{if }v_{1}v_{2}>0,\\
(2v_{1}-v_{2},2v_{2}-v_{1})&\text{if }v_{1}v_{2}<0,\\
(2v_{1},2v_{2})+[-|v_{2}|,|v_{2}|]\times\{0\}&\text{if }v_{1}=0,v_{2}\not=0,\\
(2v_{1},2v_{2})+\{0\}\times[-|v_{1}|,|v_{1}|]&\text{if }v_{2}=0,v_{1}\not=0.\\
\end{cases}

This also implies that
\partial r^{*}(0,0)=\{(0,0)\}
. Set
\bar{x}=(0,0)
. Using the formula for the kernel of the generalized Hessian as a union of parallel subspaces (see
[
43
, Theorem 4.1]
),
we get

(5.6)

\displaystyle\ker\partial^{2}r(\bar{x},\bar{v})=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}\cup\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}\not=\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})=\{0\}.

Thus,
r^{*}
cannot be
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible at
\bar{v}
by
Theorem

4.6
.

\diamond

However, in some cases of
r
being piecewise linear-quadratic, we still have
the relation that the kernel of the generalized Hessian of
r
is equal to the subspace parallel to
\partial r^{*}
.

Definition 5.10

(Piecewise Linear-Quadratic Penalty)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be nonempty and polyhedral and let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric positive semidefinite.
The function
\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}
defined by

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}(x)=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{P}}\left\{x^{T}z-\frac{1}{2}z^{T}Bz\right\}

is called a
piecewise linear-quadratic penalty
.
Note that
\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}
is proper, closed, and convex, and
\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*}(y)=\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(y)+\frac{1}{2}y^{T}By
for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

As
\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*}(y)=\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(y)+\frac{1}{2}y^{T}By
, its epigraph is computed by

\operatorname{epi}\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*}=h^{-1}(\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})\;\;\mbox{with}\;\;h(y,r):=\left(y,\frac{1}{2}y^{T}By-r\right).

Since
Dh(y,r)=\begin{pmatrix}I&By\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
is surjective for any
(y,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
and the polyhedral set
\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible,
\operatorname{epi}\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*}
is also a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible set by
[
47
, Proposition 3.2]
, i.e.,
\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible function.

In what follows,
r=\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}\circ M
where
M\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
and
\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}
is a piecewise linear-quadratic penalty,
parameterized by a positive semidefinite matrix
B\in\mathbb{S}^{\ell}_{+}
and a polyhedral set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. Although
\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible, it is not presently clear to us if
r^{*}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible. However, in
Theorem

5.12
below, we show that the kernel of the generalized Hessian of the function
r
enjoys the formula (
4.8
) under a mild condition (
5.7
) in the next result.

Lemma 5.11

(Compactness constraint qualification)
.

Let
r=\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}\circ M
and suppose the following condition holds:

(5.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\infty}\cap\ker B\cap\ker M^{T}=\{0\}

Then, for all
\bar{v}\in\operatorname{rge}M^{T}
, the following set is
nonempty and compact:

(5.8)

\displaystyle T(v):=\operatorname{argmin}\{q_{B}(y)+\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(y)\ |\ M^{T}y=v\}=\{y\ |\ r^{*}(v)=\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*}(y)\}

Proof.

Let
v\in\operatorname{rge}M^{T}
. First,
T(v)
may be written as the
\operatorname{argmin}
of an unconstrained problem as follows:

\displaystyle T(v)=\underset{\mathbb{Y}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\,\varphi_{v}

for
\varphi_{v}(y)=q_{B}(y)+\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(y)+\delta_{(M^{T})^{-1}\{v\}}(y)
.

\varphi_{v}
is proper, closed, and convex, so by
[
2
, Proposition 3.1.3]
,
nonemptiness and compactness of
\operatorname{argmin}\varphi_{v}
is equivalent to positivity of the horizon function
\varphi_{v}^{\infty}(d)
for all nonzero
d
.
The horizon function of
\varphi_{v}
can be computed to be

\displaystyle\varphi_{v}^{\infty}(d)=\delta_{\ker B}(d)+\delta_{\mathcal{P}^{\infty}}(d)+\delta_{\ker M^{T}}(d)=\delta_{\ker B\cap\ker M^{T}\cap\mathcal{P}^{\infty}}(d),

which is equal to
0
if and only if
d\in\ker B\cap\ker M^{T}\cap\mathcal{P}^{\infty}
.
∎

The condition (
5.7
) is very mild. In particular, it holds when either
\mathcal{P}
is a compact polyhedral set or
M
is a surjective map.

The following result shows that when
r
is a piecewise linear-quadratic penalty composed with a linear operator, we still have the aforementioned relation.

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
r=\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}\circ M
. Let
(\bar{x},\bar{v})\in\operatorname{gph}\partial r
. Suppose (
5.7
) holds. Then

\displaystyle\ker\partial^{2}r(\bar{x}|\bar{v})=\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v}).

Proof.

According to the proof of
[
43
, Theorem 4.1]
, for a sufficiently small
neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
\bar{v}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial^{2}r^{*}(\bar{v}|\bar{x})(0)=\bigcup\limits_{v\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(v).

Because
\partial^{2}r^{*}(\bar{v}|\bar{x})(0)
is a union of subspaces, we can write it as

\ker\partial^{2}r(\bar{x}|\bar{v})
. By shrinking the neighborhood to
\mathcal{O}\cap\operatorname{rge}M^{T}
, we get

(5.9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})\subseteq\ker\partial^{2}r(\bar{x}|\bar{v})=\bigcup\limits_{v\in\mathcal{O}\cap\operatorname{rge}M^{T}}\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(v).

By
[
44
, Theorem 11.33]
, we have that
r^{*}(v)
is given by

\displaystyle r^{*}(v)=(M^{T}\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*})(v)=\inf\{\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*}(y)\ |\ M^{T}y=v\}.

Recall the set
T(v)
in (
5.8
). Note that
T
is nonempty and compact-valued for all
v\in\operatorname{rge}M^{T}
by
Lemma

5.11
.
We can write
T(v)
as

\displaystyle\{y\ |\ M^{T}y=v,0\in By+N_{\mathcal{P}}(y)+\operatorname{rge}M^{T}\}.

Observe that
T(v)
is the intersection of polyhedra, so
T
is polyhedral. By
[
15
, 3D]
,
T
is outer Lipschitz continuous relative to its domain
\operatorname{rge}M^{T}
.
Thus, shrinking
\mathcal{O}
if necessary, we can obtain a (uniform) constant
\kappa>0
such that

(5.10)

\displaystyle T(v)\subseteq T(\bar{v})+\kappa\lVert v-\bar{v}\rVert\mathbb{B}\text{ for all }v\in\mathcal{O}\cap\operatorname{rge}M^{T}.

For such a
v
,
[
34
, Proposition 4.3]
guarantees that we may write

\displaystyle\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(v)=M^{-1}(\mathrm{par}\,(\partial\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*}(y)\cap\operatorname{rge}M))

for every
y\in T(v)
. We also have

\displaystyle\mathrm{par}\,\partial\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*}(y)=\mathrm{par}\,(N_{\mathcal{P}}(y)+By)=\operatorname{span}N_{\mathcal{P}}(y).

By polyhedrality of
\delta_{\mathcal{P}}
, for every
y\in T(v)
, there exists a neighborhood
V_{y}
of
y
such that

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{P}}(y^{\prime})\subseteq N_{\mathcal{P}}(y)\text{ for every }y^{\prime}\in V_{y}.

By compactness of
T(\bar{v})
, there exist finitely many
y_{1},...,y_{\ell}\in T(\bar{v})
such that

\displaystyle T(\bar{v})\subseteq\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}V_{y_{i}}:=V.

V
is an open set containing
T(\bar{v})
, so shrinking
\mathcal{O}
further if necessary, we can obtain

\displaystyle T(\bar{v})+\kappa\lVert v-\bar{v}\rVert\mathbb{B}\subseteq V\text{ for }v\in\mathcal{O}\cap\operatorname{rge}M^{T}.

This together with (
5.10
) tells us
T(v)\subseteq V
for any
v\in\mathcal{O}\cap\operatorname{rge}M^{T}
,
so for any
y\in T(v)
, there is some
i\in[\ell]
such that
y\in N_{y_{i}}
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(v)=M^{-1}(\mathrm{par}\,(N_{\mathcal{P}}(y)\cap\operatorname{rge}M))\subseteq M^{-1}(\mathrm{par}\,(N_{\mathcal{P}}(y_{i})\cap\operatorname{rge}M))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v}).

Combining this with (
5.9
) gives

(5.11)

\displaystyle\ker\partial^{2}r(\bar{x}|\bar{v})=\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})=M^{-1}(\mathrm{par}\,(N_{\mathcal{P}}(y)\cap\operatorname{rge}M))

for any
y\in T(\bar{v})
.
∎

Applying this to the least squares case, we obtain the following:

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
r=\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}\circ M
and suppose
r
satisfies the compactness qualification (
5.7
).
Let
\mathcal{P}
be parameterized as
\{z\ |\ P^{T}z\preceq\beta\}
for
P\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times k}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and let
p_{i}
refer to column
i
of
P
.
Let
S:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}_{++}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be given by

\displaystyle S(A,b,\lambda)=\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\lVert Ax-b\rVert^{2}+\lambda r(x)\right\}.

Let
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda},\bar{x})\in\operatorname{gph}S
(i.e.
\bar{x}
solves (
1.1
) with parameters
\bar{A}
,
\bar{b}
, and
\bar{\lambda}
and regularizer
r
).
Set
\bar{v}=-\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{A}^{T}(\bar{A}\bar{x}-\bar{b})
.
The condition that for some
\bar{y}\in T(\bar{v})
, we have

(5.12)

\displaystyle\ker\bar{A}\cap M^{-1}(\operatorname{span}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in I(\bar{y})\})=\{0\}

where
I(\bar{y})=\{i\ |\ p_{i}^{T}\bar{y}=\beta_{i}\}
,
is equivalent to any of the following conditions:

(a)

S
is single-valued at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.

(b)

S
is locally Lipschitz at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.

(c)

S
is directionally differentiable at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.

(d)

S
is semismooth at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{x}\in S(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
.
As was discussed in
Section

4
, the condition to apply
Proposition

3.6
to the regularized least squares setting is

\displaystyle\ker\bar{A}\cap\ker\partial^{2}r(\bar{x}|\bar{v})=\{0\}.

By the previous result, this is equivalent to

(5.13)

\displaystyle\ker\bar{A}\cap\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})=\{0\}.

As discussed in the proof of
Theorem

5.12
, the object
\mathrm{par}\,\partial r^{*}(\bar{v})
can be written as
M^{-1}(\mathrm{span}\,N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})
for any
\bar{y}\in T(\bar{v})
. Also, by
[
44
, Theorem 6.46]
,
N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{y})=\mathrm{cone}\,\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in I(\bar{y})\}
, so putting these facts together, we obtain that the condition to apply
Proposition

3.6
is

\displaystyle\ker\bar{A}\cap M^{-1}(\operatorname{span}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in I(\bar{y})\})=\{0\}.

This tells us that when this holds,
S
is (a) single-valued and (b) locally Lipschitz at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
, and because
r
is piecewise linear-quadratic, by
[
17
, Remark 1]
,
\partial r
is proto-differentiable at
(\bar{x},\bar{v})
, so
S
is also (c) directionally differentiable at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
. By
[
17
, Proposition 3]
,
\partial r
is semismooth* at
(\bar{x},\bar{v})
, so
S
is semismooth* at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
. Combining this with (c), we get that
S
is (d) semismooth at
(\bar{A},\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
. On the other hand, (b)-(d) all imply (a), which by
[
14
, Theorem 3]
is equivalent to (
5.13
) which as established, is equivalent to (
5.12
)
∎

In the case that
M=I
, we have a closed-form
expression for the directional derivative and Lipschitz constant of
S
:

Corollary 5.14
.

Let
S:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}_{++}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be given by

\displaystyle S(b,\lambda)=\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\lVert Ax-b\rVert^{2}+\lambda\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}(x)\right\}.

Let
\bar{x}\in S(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
and set
\bar{v}=-\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}A^{T}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})
. Note that under this setup, the assumption in (
5.11
) is automatically satisfied. In this case, the Mordukhovich criterion simplifies to

(5.14)

\displaystyle\ker A\cap\operatorname{span}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in I(\bar{v})\}

where
I(\bar{v})=\{i\ |\ p_{i}^{T}\bar{y}=\beta_{i}\}
.
Then, if (
5.14
) holds, we have the following:

(i)

S
is directionally differentiable at
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
, and for every direction
(q,\alpha)
, there exists a matrix
U
with orthonormal columns such that the directional derivative in direction
(q,\alpha)
is given by

\displaystyle(H^{-1}BA^{T}+U[(AHU)^{T}(AU)]^{-1}(AHU)^{T}(I-AH^{-1}BA^{T}))\left(q+\frac{\alpha}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\right)

for
H=I+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}BA^{T}A
.

(ii)

S
is single-valued and locally Lipschitz around
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
, and the modulus of Lipschitz continuity can be bounded by

\displaystyle L\leq\max_{U^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}\left\lVert(H^{-1}BA^{T}+U^{\prime}[(AHU^{\prime})^{T}(AU^{\prime})]^{-1}(AHU^{\prime})^{T}(I-AH^{-1}BA^{T}))\left(\bar{q}-\frac{\bar{\alpha}}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\right)\right\rVert

for
\mathcal{V}:=\{V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\ |\ V^{T}V=I,r\leq|I(\bar{v})|\}
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

3.6
, at a point
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda},\bar{x})\in\operatorname{gph}S
such that (
5.14
) holds, we have that
S
is directionally
differentiable,
Proposition

3.6
tells us that the directional derivative in direction
(q,\alpha)
given by

S^{\prime}(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda};q,\alpha)=w
for the unique
w
such that

(5.15)

\displaystyle 0\in DG(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda},\bar{x}|0)(q,\alpha,w),

where
G(b,\lambda,x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}A^{T}(Ax-b)+\partial\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}(x)
.
By
Lemma

2.2
(a) this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}A^{T}(q+\frac{\alpha}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})-Aw)\in D\partial\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}(\bar{x}|\bar{v})(w).

Set
z:=\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}A^{T}(q+\frac{\alpha}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})-Aw)
. Applying the inversion formula for graphical derivatives
[
44
, 8(19)]
, this can be written as

\displaystyle w\in D(\partial\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}^{*})(\bar{v}|\bar{x})(z)=D(N_{\mathcal{P}}+B)(\bar{v}|\bar{x})(z)=DN_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{v}|\bar{x}-B\bar{v})(z)+Bz

\displaystyle\iff w-Bz\in DN_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{v}|\bar{x}-B\bar{v})(z).

Let
\mathcal{K}:=T_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{v})\cap\{\bar{x}-B\bar{v}\}^{\bot}
being the
critical cone
of
\mathcal{P}
at
\bar{v}
for
\bar{x}-B\bar{v}
.
By
[
44
, Example 13.44]
, we can write the graphical derivative of the normal cone as follows:

\displaystyle w-Bz\in N_{\mathcal{K}}(z)=N_{T_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{v})}(z)+N_{\{\bar{x}-B\bar{v}\}^{\bot}}(z).

So
z\in T_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{v})
and
z^{T}(\bar{x}-B\bar{v})=0
. These can be written as

\displaystyle N_{T_{\mathcal{P}}(v)}(z)=\operatorname{cone}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in I(\bar{v}),p_{i}^{T}z=0\},

\displaystyle N_{\{\bar{x}-B\bar{v}\}^{\bot}}(z)=\operatorname{span}\{\bar{x}-B\bar{v}\}.

It follows that

w-Bz\in\operatorname{cone}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in I(\bar{v}),\ p_{i}^{T}z=0\}+\operatorname{span}\{\bar{x}-B\bar{v}\}.

Using a similar argument to the proof of Corollary
5.6
, let
J:=\{i\in I(\bar{v})\ |\ p_{i}^{T}z=0\}
. The fact that
\bar{v}\in\partial\theta_{\mathcal{P},B}(\bar{x})=(N_{\mathcal{P}}+B)^{-1}(\bar{x})
gives us

\bar{x}-B\bar{v}\in N_{\mathcal{P}}(\bar{v})=\operatorname{cone}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in I(\bar{v})\}.

We claim that in fact
\bar{x}-B\bar{v}\in\operatorname{cone}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in J\}
.
Indeed, as
\bar{x}-B\bar{v}
can be written as
\sum_{i\in I(\bar{v})}\gamma_{i}p_{i}
for
\gamma_{i}\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle 0=z^{T}(\bar{x}-B\bar{v})=\sum\limits_{i\in I(\bar{v})}\gamma_{i}p_{i}^{T}z=\sum\limits_{i\in J}\gamma_{i}\underbrace{p_{i}^{T}z}_{=0}+\sum\limits_{i\in I(\bar{v})\setminus J}\gamma_{i}\underbrace{p_{i}^{T}z}_{<0}.

So
\gamma_{i}=0
for
i\not\in J
. This clarifies the claim. It follows that

\displaystyle w-Bz\in\operatorname{cone}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in J\}+\operatorname{span}\{\bar{x}-B\bar{v}\}\subseteq\operatorname{span}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in J\}+\operatorname{span}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in J\}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{span}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in J\}.

Let
E:=\operatorname{span}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in J\}
.
We have that
w-Bz\in E
and
z\in E^{\bot}
. Let
\bar{u}:=-\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})
,
H:=I+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}BA^{T}A
, and
h:=-\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}BA^{T}(q-\alpha\bar{u})
.
Recall that
z=\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}A^{T}(q+\frac{\alpha}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})-Aw)
. Thus we can rewrite
w-Bz
as follows:

\displaystyle w-Bz=w-\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}BA^{T}(q-\alpha\bar{u}-Aw)=

\displaystyle w+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}BA^{T}Aw-\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}}BA^{T}(q-\alpha\bar{u})=Hw+h

Note that
H
is invertible by
Lemma

2.7
. As
Hw+h\in E
, we have
w+H^{-1}h\in H^{-1}(E)
. Let
\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\ldots u_{r}\end{bmatrix}=U
be an orthonormal basis of
H^{-1}(E)
. Since
J\subset I(\bar{v})
, the qualification condition (
5.14
)
implies that
\ker A\cap E=\{0\}
with
r=|J|
, the cardinality of
J
. Moreover, the fact that
z\in E^{\bot}
gives us
H^{T}z\in H^{T}(E^{\bot})\overset{\eqref{lem:Sub}}{=}(H^{-1}(E))^{\bot}
, so
U^{T}H^{T}z=0
.
We can rewrite this as
(AHU)^{T}(q-\alpha\bar{u}-Aw)=0
,
which gives

(5.16)

\displaystyle(AHU)^{T}Aw=(AHU)^{T}(q-\alpha\bar{u}).

Adding
(AHU)^{T}AH^{-1}h
to both sides and using the fact
UU^{T}
is the orthogonal projection onto
H^{-1}(E)
, we obtain

\displaystyle(AHU)^{T}(q-\alpha\bar{u}+AH^{-1}h)=(AHU)^{T}AUU^{T}\underbrace{(w+H^{-1}h)}_{\in H^{-1}(E)}.

By Lemma
2.8
,
(AHU)^{T}AU
is invertible. We can then write

\displaystyle U^{T}(w+H^{-1}h)=[(AHU)^{T}(AU)]^{-1}(AHU)^{T}(q-\alpha\bar{u}+AH^{-1}h)

and isolate
w
as follows:

\displaystyle w=-H^{-1}h+U[(AHU)^{T}(AU)]^{-1}(AHU)^{T}(q-\alpha\bar{u}+AH^{-1}h).

Plugging back in the definition of
h
, this yields

\displaystyle w=(H^{-1}BA^{T}+U[(AHU)^{T}(AU)]^{-1}(AHU)^{T}(I-AH^{-1}BA^{T}))(q-\alpha\bar{u}),

which verifies the formula of directional derivative of
S
at
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
at
(q,\alpha)
in (i).

To verify (ii), we first notice that (
5.14
) holds locally around
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda},\bar{x})
. Indeed,
let
\{(b_{k},\lambda_{k},x_{k})\}\subseteq\operatorname{gph}S
be a sequence converging to
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda},\bar{x})
.
Set
v_{k}:=-\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}A^{T}(Ax_{k}-b_{k})
. For
k
sufficiently large, we have
I(v_{k})\subseteq I(\bar{v})
, so
for such
k
we have

\displaystyle\ker A\cap\operatorname{span}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in I(v_{k})\}\subseteq\ker A\cap\operatorname{span}\{p_{i}\ |\ i\in I(\bar{v})\}=\{0\}.

This establishes that for

sufficiently close to

,

is
directionally differentiable. Because we eventually have the containment
I(v_{k})\subseteq I(\bar{v})
,
the formula for the directional derivative holds for
(b,\lambda)
close enough to
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
(if we replace
\bar{\lambda}
for

for
\bar{b}
).

When (
5.14
) holds at
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda},\bar{x})\in\operatorname{gph}S
, we have that
S
is locally Lipschitz
at
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
with modulus

\displaystyle L\leq\limsup_{(b,\lambda)\to(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})}\max_{\lVert(q,\alpha)\rVert\leq 1}\lVert S^{\prime}(b,\lambda;q,\alpha)\rVert.

Let
\{(b_{k},\lambda_{k})\}
be a sequence converging to
(\bar{b},\bar{\lambda})
such that

\displaystyle L\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\max_{(q,\alpha)\in\mathbb{B}}\lVert S^{\prime}(b_{k},\lambda_{k};q,\alpha)\rVert.

We may also choose a corresponding sequence
\{(q_{k},\alpha_{k})\}\subseteq\mathbb{B}
such that

\displaystyle L\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\lVert S^{\prime}(b_{k},\lambda_{k};q_{k},\alpha_{k})\rVert.

Let
\{x_{k}\in S(b_{k},\lambda_{k})\}
such that
x_{k}\to\bar{x}

\displaystyle L\leq\left\lVert(H^{-1}BA^{T}+\bar{U}[(AH\bar{U})^{T}(A\bar{U})]^{-1}(AH\bar{U})^{T}(I-AH^{-1}BA^{T}))\left(\bar{q}-\frac{\bar{\alpha}}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\right)\right\rVert

\displaystyle L\leq\max_{U^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}\left\lVert(H^{-1}BA^{T}+U^{\prime}[(AHU^{\prime})^{T}(AU^{\prime})]^{-1}(AHU^{\prime})^{T}(I-AH^{-1}BA^{T}))\left(\bar{q}-\frac{\bar{\alpha}}{\bar{\lambda}}(A\bar{x}-\bar{b})\right)\right\rVert.

6.
Final Remarks

In this paper, we studied stability properties of solution mappings associated with convex regularized least-squares problems through a variational-analytic framework. Our approach combines an implicit-function perspective for monotone generalized equations with second-order tools from generalized differentiation, leading to conditions for local Lipschitz continuity, directional differentiability, and semismoothness of the associated solution maps.

A central aspect of the analysis was the relationship between generalized Hessians and the geometry of the subdifferential of the conjugate regularizer. In particular, for conjugate
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible regularizers, we showed that the kernel of the generalized Hessian admits a tractable characterization in terms of the parallel subspace of the subdifferential. This allows one to replace difficult second-order calculations by more explicit first-order geometric conditions.

Our framework applies to a broad range of regularizers arising in optimization, statistics, and machine learning, including weighted polyhedral support functions and piecewise linear-quadratic penalties. In addition to qualitative stability results, we also derived quantitative sensitivity estimates and explicit formulas for directional derivatives in structured settings.

Several directions remain open for future research:

i)

A natural question is to extend the family of regularizers that satisfy the generalized Hessian expression (
4.8
) beyond the ones given here so that the implicit function theorem from
Section

3

can be readily applied.

ii)

The semismoothness results for the optimal solution maps in
Section

5
beg the question if explicit formulae for the Clarke Jacobians of said solution maps can be computed. This would be appealing for semismooth Newton methods.

iii)

In
Section

5.2
, we discuss that it is presently unclear whether the composition of a PLQ penalty with a linear map is conjugate
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-cone reducible. A positive answer would make the compactness (
5.7
) redundant.
We would like to resolve this question in the future.
