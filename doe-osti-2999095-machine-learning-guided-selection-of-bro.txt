1. PROJECT DESCRIPTION/OVERVIEW
1.1.Technical approach
1.1.1. Computational and Machine Learning models
Our project established and demonstrated a transfer learning framework that enables prediction of
antibody–antigen interactions across related viruses. The approach focused on three major
activities:
 1. Conserved region and epitope identification – We compared viral protein structures and
 sequences to identify shared receptor-binding domains and neutralizing epitope regions
 across variants and related viruses. These conserved features formed the foundation for
 discovering broadly functional antibodies.
 2. Machine learning model development – We built neural network–based models that
 integrate epitope features with antibody sequence information. Instead of relying solely on
 structural or physical properties, the models learned transferable patterns that describe
 antibody binding potential across different viral families.
 3. Transfer learning and validation – Using SARS-CoV-2 and Ebola as source systems, we
 successfully transferred learned epitope features to predict antibody interactions for SARS-
 CoV-1 and Marburg virus. Iterative cycles of dataset generation, retraining, and evaluation
 improved generalization and predictive power, ensuring the framework can adapt to new
 threats.
1.1.2. Experimental data generation/validation
We successfully demonstrated the use of our high-throughput workflow, originally developed in
earlier projects, to support rapid antigen production, antibody library screening, and
characterization of antibody–antigen interactions. This integrated testbed enabled efficient
generation of datasets to train machine learning models and provided experimental validation of
predicted antibody performance. Using this platform, we confirmed binding interactions between
selected antibodies and viral proteins, strengthening confidence in the predictive framework and
its ability to identify potent neutralizing candidates.

1.2.Objective and deliverables

 1

This project set out to build a predictive machine learning tool capable of transferring knowledge
of antibody–antigen interactions across viruses within the same genus or family. The work
centered on two primary goals:
 1. Developing models that capture conserved epitope motifs across related viruses and
 retraining interaction predictors to improve generalization.
 2. Generating hypotheses around variable epitope motifs and validating their relevance
 through experimental confirmation of antibody binding and receptor interactions.
Through this effort, we demonstrated that models trained on SARS-CoV-2 data can be successfully
leveraged to predict antibody interactions with SARS-CoV-1, showing the feasibility of rapid
adaptation to new viral strains. By the end of the project, we delivered:
 • Machine learning models for identifying SARS-CoV-1 epitope-specific functional
 antibodies, enabling rapid response against emerging threats.
 • A prototype immunogenic epitope library that provides a foundation for future vaccine
 development efforts.
These achievements demonstrate the utility of combining machine learning with targeted
experimental validation to accelerate the discovery of broad-spectrum countermeasures.

1.3.Updated Focus:
1.3.1. Our original overall plan: We initially developed two transfer learning models: one
 adapting a SARS-CoV-2 spike antibody predictor to SARS-CoV-1, and another
 transferring an Ebola GP model to Marburg GP. Both approaches relied on fine-tuning
 limited new data while leveraging features learned from source models.

1.3.2. New considerations:
As work progressed, it became clear that more data diversity was needed than originally
anticipated to achieve robust, generalizable performance across viral families. Many antibodies
contained unique features not adequately represented in our training sets, limiting predictive
accuracy on unseen cases.
In addition, resource and time constraints prevented generation of the large numbers of antibody–
variant interaction datasets required for both coronavirus and filovirus transfer learning.

 2

Significant progress was made with Ebola, but the breadth of data remained insufficient to reliably
extend predictions to Marburg within this project period.
To maximize impact, we refined the antibody selection models before moving to neutralization
assays, ensuring that only the most promising candidates advanced. Resources were prioritized
toward ongoing Ebola glycoprotein screening and sequencing, which directly supported the
antibody discovery pipeline.
1.3.3. Path Forward:
To address these limitations, we trained models on a broader set of antibodies within available
resources, providing stronger coverage of key antibody properties for SARS-CoV-2 to SARS-
CoV-1 transfer learning. For filoviruses, full model development was deferred, but valuable proof-
of-concept data were generated. With the remainder of the project, we:
 • Focused resources on the SARS-CoV genus, where data density and global relevance were
 greatest.
 • Maintained Ebola/Marburg efforts as groundwork for future transfer learning studies.
 • Completed biopanning of Ebola glycoprotein, producing a diverse set of confirmed binders
 that form a ready dataset for future escape prediction and therapeutic development.

 3. RESULT SUMMARY
 A. Study Case 1: Transfer Learning – SARS-CoV-2 S → SARS-CoV-1 S
Task 1: Retrain PPI prediction models trained on the whole viral protein sequence of SARS-
CoV-2 RBD with conserved sequence window–epitope motifs
Goal
Generalize antibody–antigen interaction prediction across closely related viruses in the SARS-
CoV genus by leveraging SARS-CoV-2–trained models for SARS-CoV-1.
Approach
We prioritized conserved receptor-binding domain (RBD) regions and key epitope patches as the
foundation for transfer learning. Machine learning models were adapted to incorporate both viral
features and antibody sequence information, with strategies in place to prevent overfitting and
ensure broad applicability across variants.
Key Findings
 • Conserved epitope patches were identified that retain predictive power for cross-variant
 targeting and transfer to SARS-CoV-1.
 • Interaction “hotspots” between regions were mapped, providing guidance for
 combinatorial antibody design and mutagenesis studies.
 • Models demonstrated the ability to generalize to unseen antibodies, showing readiness for
 rapid evaluation of new candidates.
 • Validation confirmed that predictions aligned well with experimental data, particularly
 when testing held-out antibodies, indicating robustness of the framework.
Capabilities and Applications
The project delivered an epitope-patch discovery and ranking pipeline that highlights conserved,
high-signal regions suitable for broad-spectrum antibody design. These capabilities:
 • Support immunogen and vaccine development by flagging conserved RBD segments with
 high functional relevance.
 • Accelerate antibody engineering by guiding targeted edits and enabling more focused,
 high-yield library designs.
 • Provide tools for diagnostic panel development by selecting complementary epitopes that
 reduce the risk of escape.
Next Steps (if funding available)
Future work would focus on strengthening generalization, incorporating structural information,
adding uncertainty estimates to guide experimental triage, and conducting prospective validation
with new variants and antibodies.

Task 2: Predict key variable epitope motifs on SARS-CoV-1 S protein, generated ML model
predictions of SARS-CoV-1 S kDs to receptor and antibodies and experimentally validated
predicted kDs with ACE2 and neutralizing antibodies
Goal
Extend predictive modeling to SARS-CoV-1 by leveraging knowledge from SARS-CoV-2, taking
advantage of the high sequence similarity in the receptor-binding domain (RBD).
Approach
We combined available SARS-CoV-2 interaction data with newly generated SARS-CoV-1
variants to create a broader dataset. Transfer learning was applied to adapt existing models to this
new target, with additional strategies to improve generalization across antibody classes.

 5

Key Findings
 • Incorporating both heavy-chain-only antibodies and conventional antibodies improved
 predictive accuracy for SARS-CoV-1.
 • The models demonstrated portability across the SARS-CoV genus, with the ability to
 distinguish variant epitopes that bind strongly or weakly to neutralizing antibodies.
 • Results highlight complementary epitope classes across SARS-CoV-1 and SARS-CoV-2,
 providing insight into rational antibody cocktail design.
 • Withholding antibody strategies confirmed the model’s readiness to evaluate new, unseen
 antibodies.
 • By integrating all modeling strategies, our best model predicts HCAbs/antibodies that
 efficiently bind to novel SARS-CoV-1 variant sequences with 77% accuracy, enabling
 rapid candidate selection for experimental validation.
Capabilities and Applications
This work delivered a computational–experimental pipeline capable of rapidly predicting cross-
virus antibody binding. The integrated workflow enables:
 • Prioritization of viral variants before committing to large-scale experiments.
 • Faster identification of cross-reactive antibodies for emerging coronaviruses.
 • A scalable approach adaptable to other high-threat pathogens such as Ebola and Marburg.
Next Steps (if funding available)
Future work will focus on expanding datasets, strengthening active learning loops, and developing
a platform for real-time integration of experimental and field data. These enhancements will
improve prediction accuracy and broaden coverage of antibody sequence space, enabling rapid
updates to models as new viral threats emerge.

 B. Study Case 2: Transfer Learning – Ebola → Marburg
Goal
Generalize antibody–antigen interaction prediction across filoviruses by leveraging models trained
on Ebola glycoprotein (GP) to inform predictions for Marburg virus GP.
Challenges
Progress was limited by insufficient antibody diversity, which reduced the ability of models to
generalize to unseen antibody–antigen pairs. While we established important pipelines and
generated proof-of-concept results, additional datasets are needed to fully realize transfer learning
across the filovirus family.
Approach and Outcomes
 • Epitope-focused modeling: We prioritized functionally important regions of Ebola GP
 and used them to design and evaluate potential variant interactions. Although predictive
 accuracy was strong for known antibodies, performance dropped on novel antibodies,
 underscoring the need for greater diversity in training data.

 8

 • High-throughput validation: A rapid experimental pipeline was developed to measure
 antibody binding and receptor interactions across Ebola GP variants, producing a dataset
 that informs both structural analyses and machine learning models.
 • Biopanning for new binders: To address gaps in antibody diversity, we conducted large-
 scale nanobody–phage library selections against Ebola and Marburg GPs. Machine
 learning analysis of enrichment patterns identified critical residues and positions that drive
 binding, enabling rational design of new antibody candidates.
Key Findings
 • Initial results demonstrated the feasibility of transfer learning but highlighted the
 importance of broad antibody coverage for reliable predictions.
 • A diverse set of Ebola GP-binding antibodies was isolated, providing a valuable resource
 for future Marburg-focused transfer learning.
 • Machine learning models paired with residue-level interpretability revealed both shared
 and unique binding features across Ebola and Marburg, guiding targeted library design for
 next-generation antibody discovery.
Built Capabilities
This study established a pipeline that integrates high-throughput screening, computational
modeling, and interpretable machine learning. It enables:
 • Prediction of binding shifts and identification of escape mutations.
 • Optimization of antibody candidates for therapeutic use.
 • Cross-target analysis to reveal both conserved and strain-specific antibody features.
Path Forward (with additional resources)
 • Expand antibody and variant datasets to improve robustness of transfer learning.
 • Apply domain adaptation strategies from larger datasets.
 • Incorporate structure-guided docking and binding energy scoring to refine antibody design.
 • Continue iterative design–test–model cycles, accelerating translation of Ebola datasets to
 Marburg predictions.
Task 4: Transfer Learning Between Ebola and Marburg
Objective
Explore the feasibility of transferring predictive models developed for Ebola glycoprotein (GP) to
Marburg GP, leveraging structural similarities in key epitope regions.
Approach
We assessed structural alignment between Ebola and Marburg GPs, focusing on the receptor-
binding interface. This analysis allowed us to identify homologous epitope regions that could serve
as the basis for cross-family transfer learning.
Findings
Roughly half of the epitope residues mapped consistently between Ebola and Marburg, providing
a reduced but meaningful foundation for predictive modeling. While this overlap suggests transfer
learning is possible, limited antibody diversity constrained progress. Given these challenges, we
prioritized resources toward the SARS-CoV case study, where data density and impact were
greater.
Implication
This preliminary work establishes a starting point for Marburg-focused modeling and demonstrates
that structural alignment can provide the basis for future cross-family prediction efforts once
additional datasets become available.

3. SIGNIFICANT ACCOMPLISHMENT(S)
 Milestone 1: Transfer Learning – SARS-CoV-2 → SARS-CoV-1
 We developed and retrained machine learning models that capture conserved epitope motifs and
 antibody features. These models successfully transferred predictive capability from SARS-CoV-2
 to SARS-CoV-1, enabling accurate prediction of antibody binding. Experimental validation
 confirmed the ability of the framework to generalize across related coronaviruses, providing a
 foundation for rapid response to emerging strains.
 Milestone 2: Transfer Learning – Ebola → Marburg
 For filoviruses, we combined computational modeling with experimental pipelines to investigate
 antibody interactions with Ebola GP. This included epitope-focused variant design, high-
 throughput screening, and machine learning analysis. While predictive performance was limited
 by antibody diversity, the work produced valuable datasets and demonstrated proof-of-concept
 modeling.
 In parallel, we advanced discovery pipelines through biopanning and next-generation sequencing,
 identifying enriched antibody candidates and mapping key sequence features driving binding.
 Epitope mapping for Marburg GP was also completed, providing a basis for future transfer learning
 from Ebola to Marburg.
