Incentive Configuration Pathways for Standardization of Cancer Multidisciplinary Teams in China: A Fuzzy-Set Qualitative Comparative Analysis
Authors: Yiqing Mao, Linru Jie, Bowen Zhang, Dingding Kang, Jiawen Xiao, Hongwei Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study evaluated the current implementation of incentive means and schemes in cancer multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) in China, and identified configuration pathways that affect the standardization level of MDT implementation.MethodsA questionnaire was constructed drawing upon practical experience with the implementation of MDT and relational incentives theory. A total of 1,332 MDT members were selected to participate in the survey. The fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) was used to explore the non-linear and synergistic influence pathways of different incentive means and schemes combinations on the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation.ResultsTwo pathways of incentive means leading to high standardization were identified: a comprehensive pathway characterized by performance rewards, autonomy, and interpersonal relationships, and an intrinsically driven pathway relying on learning/development, autonomy, and interpersonal relationships. Four pathways of incentive schemes were found, categorized into procedure‑standardized scenarios (with efficiency of performance distribution and performance evaluation standards as core) and result‑oriented scenarios (with level of performance distribution, method of distribution, and perceived team fairness as core).ConclusionThe configuration pathways of incentive means indicate a substitutive effect among factors. Practitioners can flexibly emphasize specific elements based on local resources and stages, and this flexibility has policy implications for hospital governance. The pathways for incentive schemes reveal population applicability differences, so schemes should be tailored to diverse needs, enhance implementation, and prevent ineffectiveness, guiding policy makers in designing flexible regulations.Keywords: cancer, multidisciplinary team, MDT, incentive means, incentive schemes

Introduction

In recent years, cancer has emerged as a significant public health concern globally, attributable to its persistently increasing incidence and mortality rates. The Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) model, as one of the integrated healthcare service models, enables the creation of “personalized” and “precise” treatment plans tailored for cancer patient, and is regarded as a high-value medical service model.1,2 The cancer MDT comprises physicians from various specialties, including surgery, internal medicine, radiation oncology, radiology, pathology, and nutrition. These professionals convene regularly to collaboratively make decisions, thereby delivering high-value medical services to patients.3 This model has been widely demonstrated to significantly improve the diagnostic accuracy, quality of treatment decisions, and survival rates for various types of cancer, while also effectively increasing patient and clinician satisfaction.4–7 Currently, countries such as the United Kingdom, the United States, France, and Canada have integrated the MDT into their standardized cancer care pathways.8–11 In 2018, China officially initiated nationwide pilot programs for MDT approaches to cancer treatment, establishing the MDT as a pivotal strategy in contemporary cancer management, with its significance being unequivocal.

Standardization level is defined as the extent to which MDT practices conform to pre specified procedures across the three phases, input (pre-MDT meeting), process (during the MDT meeting), and output (post-MDT meeting). Although MDT is regarded as the “gold standard” for cancer diagnosis and treatment, several challenges impede the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. These challenges are common in many countries, which include insufficient communication among team members, limited time allocated for case discussions, inadequate integration of patient-specific information, and the absence of systematic management frameworks.1,11–14 In addition to the aforementioned challenges, prior research has indicated several issues encountered by China, including unstable team composition, MDT operations resembling traditional consultations,15 physicians’ incomplete understanding of MDT’s values and processes, and low participation rates.16 These specific issues impede the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. Within this context, both academic and governmental sectors are investigating strategies to enhance the standardization of cancer MDT implementation. Incentives have been proven to be of significant value in promoting the behavior of healthcare workers, particularly in multidisciplinary collaboration.17–19 According to relational incentives theory,20 incentives encompass incentive means (ie, what is used as an incentive) and incentive schemes (ie, how incentives are applied). Incentive means comprise economic and non-economic incentives. Economic incentives are commonly employed to regulating behavior.21 For example, Germany offers economic compensation for MDT cases based on their complexity, the United Kingdom establishes bundled MDT service fees to encourage physician participation,4,22 and China provides performance bonuses accordance with MDT workload.15 Some scholars have also explored the role of non-economic incentives in promoting the standardization level of MDT implementation. These incentives encompass the organization of educational activities aimed at enhancing the professional skills of MDT members,23 as well as the recognition of the educational value24,25 and career development opportunities5 associated with participation in MDTs. Incentive schemes primarily refer to the structural design and implementation of economic incentives, including the appropriateness of performance evaluation standards, the fairness and timeliness of incentive distribution, and distribution methods.17,26 Previous research has confirmed that different performance-based payment methods can have varying impacts on the clinical behaviors of physicians in interprofessional teams.27,28 Therefore, this study posits that both incentive means and incentive schemes exert significant impacts on the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation.

However, existing research on incentives for cancer MDT remains insufficient. First, what is the current state of incentive means and the implementation of incentive schemes? At present, there is no systematic overview of incentives for cancer MDT from a holistic perspective, nor has there been an evaluation of incentive means and the implementation of incentive schemes. Second, what strategies can be employed to effectively integrate incentive mechanisms and schemes to optimize their efficacy? It is well-documented that the implementation of a singular incentive approach often encounters inherent limitations.29,30 Conversely, the simultaneous application of multiple incentive mechanisms or schemes may lead to phenomena such as the “substitution effect” or “crowding-out effect.” Nonetheless, previous studies have largely focused on the relationship between single incentive elements and behavioral outcomes, while neglecting the combined effects of multiple incentive factors. This gap in the literature may contribute to challenges such as incentive failure, bias, or misalignment within the cancer MDT.

The objectives of this study are as follows: (1) to evaluate the current status of the implementation of incentive means and schemes within cancer MDT; (2) to identify the configuration pathways of these incentive means and schemes that influence the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation, to analyze the combined effects of various combinations of incentive means and schemes on the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation, and to provide a scientific basis for the formulation of effective and evidence-based MDT incentives.

Materials and Methods

Variable Measurement

The research team conducted an assessment of the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. First, the research team reviewed existing MDT assessment tools, included self-assessment and observational. All content were classified according to the three phases of input (pre-MDT meeting), process (during the MDT meeting), and output (post-MDT meeting). Second, based on the implementation of MDT in Chinese hospitals, the above content was locally adapted to form a standardized cancer MDT implementation assessment tool comprising 20 items. Finally, The Delphi expert consultation was conducted to engage 25 experts from all over the country in evaluating the “importance” and “rationality” of the scale. Reliability and validity tests were performed (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.804, KMO = 0.876). The assessment was conducted using a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5, with 1 indicating Strongly Disagree and 5 indicating Strongly Agree. Further details of the scale are provided in Supplementary Materials 1 (Table S.1).

This research, based on relational incentives theory and the practical implementation of incentive for cancer MDT in Chinese hospitals, has identified 12 incentive variables (see Table 1) within the frameworks of incentive means and schemes. Incentive means encompasses performance rewards, honorary recognition, learning and development, professional identity, autonomy, and interpersonal relationships. Incentive schemes include level of performance distribution, efficiency of performance distribution, method of performance distribution, performance evaluation standards, perceived team fairness, and perceived peer fairness. MDT members were invited to rate their satisfaction with the implementation of each incentive variable (minimum score = 0, maximum score = 5). The incentive variable scale passed reliability and validity tests (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.862, KMO = 0.861). The questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Materials 2 (Figure S.2).

Data Collection

This study administered a survey in Henan Province, China, from October 2023 to March 2024 for two reasons. First, its central geographic location within China corresponds to a medium level of economic development. Second, the evaluation of the MDT implementation in this province, conducted by the Chinese Medical Doctor Association in 2021, yielded a moderate result. Therefore, carrying out this research in this province offers a certain degree of representativeness. In light of the extensive adoption of MDT in provincial hospitals, this study focused on provincial hospitals as the subjects of the survey. Employing a stratified random sampling approach, the provincial hospitals in Henan were divided into “high-level” and “low-level” groups based on the number of cancer MDT and patient. Two hospitals were selected from each group, resulting in a total of four provincial hospitals serving as research sites. The study encompassed 74 cancer MDT from these four provincial hospitals, with all team members invited to participate. Ultimately, 1350 questionnaires were distributed, and 1332 valid responses were collected, achieving a valid response rate of 98.67%.

Statistical Analysis

This study utilized SPSS 22.0 for descriptive statistical analysis of the data. fsQCA was employed to separately examine the configurational effects of incentive means and incentive schemes, that is, to identify which combinations of conditions are sufficient to produce the outcome.31,32 The procedure was executed as follows: First, the direct calibration method was applied to set the 5th percentile, 50th percentile, and 95th percentile of the sample as the three calibration anchors. To facilitate data analysis, replace the incentive variable scores of 0–5 with 1–6. The involved variables were then calibrated into fuzzy sets ranging from 0 to 1, specific calibration values for the variables are detailed in Table 2. Second, a necessity analysis was conducted. Prior to the conditional configuration analysis, this study performed a necessity analysis on all conditional variables to test whether any single incentive variable was a necessary condition affecting the standardization level of MDT implementation. If the level of consistency is greater than 0.9, the condition can be considered a necessary condition for the outcome. The results indicated that the consistency threshold for all individual antecedent conditions was not greater than 0.9, suggesting that no incentive variable in this study could be regarded as a necessary condition for the standardization level of MDT implementation. The specific results are detailed in Table 3. The threshold was set as raw coverage > 0.8 and IPR > 0.7. The sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of findings, which included alternative calibration strategies (10th–50th–90th percentiles) and varying the consistency threshold for sufficiency analysis (0.80 and 0.85).

Sample Characteristics

Among the 1332 MDT members, 55.80% were male and 44.20% were female. Most MDT members were in the 35–45 age group (47.70%), and most held associate senior professional titles (37.40%) or senior professional titles (35.10%). Regarding education background, most held a master’s degree (52.90%), followed by those with a doctoral degree (35.10%). Their predominant professional background was clinical medicine (83.60%), including internal medicine, surgery, and oncology. In terms of team roles, core MDT members made up the largest proportion of the MDT members (64.00%), while MDT coordinators and MDT chairs accounted for 12.70% and 11.30%, respectively. Discussion

The research findings indicated that the current standardization level of cancer MDT implementation in China is generally satisfactory. This indicates that, through the combined efforts of policy promotion and hospital practice, a preliminary standardized foundation for MDT has been established. However, the wide score range reveals variations in the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation across different teams, highlighting the need for further improvement of the overall standardization level.

The study revealed that members of the MDT demonstrate significantly varied assessments of different incentive means. The levels of satisfaction of MDT physicians with learning and development, interpersonal relationships, autonomy, and professional identity were relatively high. In contrast, the levels of satisfaction of MDT physicians with performance rewards and honorary recognition were comparatively low. This outcome is associated with the typically high levels of seniority and professional titles held by MDT physicians. The relatively low satisfaction among physicians regarding honorary recognitions may be attributed to the extended implementation cycle of this incentive, as such commendations are typically conferred only annually. This results in a delayed incentive effect, which adversely affects physicians’ perceptions. Furthermore, the low satisfaction with performance rewards may be partially due to physicians’ substantial economic expectations and partially due to challenges associated with the implementation of the incentive programs. From the current state of implementation of these incentive schemes, there are noticeable differences in implementation of these incentives. A characteristic is that “policy design outpaces implementation efficiency, and external fairness exceeds internal fairness.” Specifically, issues such as insufficient timeliness in the distribution of performance incentives and low perceived team fairness are prominent. The delayed distribution of performance incentives may lead to weakened motivational effects,33,34 and low perceived internal fairness may diminish overall satisfaction with incentives and even affect the team’s cooperative atmosphere.35 This may also explain the generally lower satisfaction level of performance rewards among MDT members. Therefore, future optimization efforts should prioritize the transition from system design to the implementation effectively. This shift aims to enhance transparency, timeliness, and fairness in performance distribution, thereby improving the actual effectiveness of incentive measures.36,37

The research results indicated that two pathways of incentive means guide a high standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. The first is the comprehensive pathway of incentive means. The fsQCA results revealed that performance rewards, autonomy, and interpersonal relationships serve as core variables, with honorary recognition acting as a peripheral variable, which collectively guide a high standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. This pathway emphasizes a balance between economic and non-economic incentives, precisely addressing the diverse and complex needs of physicians as knowledge workers.38 They seek not only external economic security but also internal psychological fulfillment and self-actualization. The second pathway is the pathway of intrinsically driven incentive means. The fsQCA results demonstrated that learning and development, autonomy, and interpersonal relationships together guide a high standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. This pathway consists of non-economic incentive means and aligns with the core tenets of self-determination theory, namely, that meeting the three basic psychological needs of competence, autonomy, and relatedness is crucial for stimulating individuals’ intrinsic motivation and enhancing the standardization level of behavior.39 In contrast to previous studies that primarily focused on the independent effect of a single incentive factor,17,40,41 this research revealed that the high standardization level of cancer MDT implementation is not driven by a single optimal incentive means, but rather stems from various effective incentive “formulas.” Comparing the two pathways, autonomy and interpersonal relationships emerged as core universal conditions for guiding a high standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. Regardless of the incentive pathway adopted, respecting professional autonomy and fostering a strong atmosphere of team collaboration are indispensable elements and key to the success of incentive strategies. Furthermore, a substitutive effect exists between performance rewards and learning and development as incentive means. Administrators can flexibly choose to strengthen one incentive means element over the other depending on available resources, thereby implementing differentiated and phased incentive strategies.

The research findings indicate that three pathways of incentive means leaded to a low standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. The first pathway reveals that the complete absence of non-economic incentives results in a low standardization level of cancer MDT implementation, regardless of the presence or absence of economic incentives. This not only provides reverse validation of the tenets of self-determination theory but also highlights the foundational role of non-economic incentives in improving the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. This result also explains the divergent views on the role of economic incentives in previous studies,17,42 confirming that economic incentives alone cannot offset the negative impact caused by the absence of non-economic incentives. Therefore, the development of non-economic incentives should be treated as a fundamental undertaking. Measures such as controlling case quality, fostering team culture, and guiding communication climates should be adopted to ensure that physicians can achieve learning and development, gain professional identity, and obtain autonomy through participation in the MDT. Both the second and third pathways reveal that the extreme scenario of a complete absence of both economic and non-economic incentives fundamentally undermines the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. Combined with the comprehensive incentive means pathway as identified earlier, these findings collectively confirm the importance of establishing a systematic incentive management system and avoiding a single incentive model in guiding the high-quality operation of MDT.

The research findings indicated that there had four pathways of incentive scheme that guide a high standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. The first and second pathways prioritized the efficiency of performance distribution and performance evaluation standards as core variables, while the third and fourth pathways emphasized the level of performance distribution, method of performance distribution, and perceived team fairness as core variables. These pathways contribute to a high standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. These four pathways corresponded to two incentive scenarios. The first is “procedure-standardized scenarios” (the first two pathways). This means that, compared to the amount of performance rewards, MDT physicians in this group prioritize the standardization of the performance reward distribution process, such as the efficiency of performance distribution and performance evaluation standards. This group primarily consists of physicians with director or deputy director positions, who hold middle management roles and thus may be more concerned with the implementation of hospital systems. This suggests that the design of MDT incentive schemes should fully consider the opinions of MDT members holding these positions and invite them to act as “supervisors” for the implementation of incentive schemes. The second is “result-oriented scenarios” (the last two pathways). This indicates that MDT members in this group focus on the amount of performance rewards (level of performance distribution) and need to perceive the actual distribution of performance (method of performance distribution) and the fairness of performance allocation within the team (perceived team fairness). Therefore, these physicians are typically not the decision-makers for MDT performance distribution (this responsibility lies with MDT chairs) but are often ordinary members of the MDT, especially younger physicians with relatively high economic needs. The above findings revealed a potential flaw in current MDT incentive schemes: the amount of performance distribution is often positively correlated with physicians’ positions and professional titles, meaning that physicians with higher positions or titles receive more performance rewards.43 However, this may not align with their actual incentive needs, thereby limiting its role in improving the standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. Based on these research results, administrators should cater to the needs of different groups by two means: on one hand, granting more management responsibilities and authority to MDT physicians with high positions and titles; on the other hand, providing comparable performance rewards to ordinary MDT physicians.

The research findings indicated that there are two pathways of incentive scheme leading to a low standardization level of cancer MDT implementation, both of which lacked three core variables: level of performance distribution, efficiency of performance distribution, and performance evaluation standards. This suggests that ambiguous performance reward systems, sluggish implementation, and unrecognized standards will be key factors contributing to a low standardization level of cancer MDT implementation. Previous studies have confirmed that the level of performance distribution has a direct and significant positive impact on work motivation, while institutional transparency (reflected in efficiency and standards) plays a moderating role, which can enhance the positive effect of perceived performance distribution.44 Therefore, administrators should clarify performance amounts, distribution timelines, and evaluation standards; standardize incentive schemes through institutionalization, ensure MDT physicians have transparent access to information, and proactively prevent trust crises caused by information asymmetry.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, self‑reported data may introduce response bias, although anonymity was assured. Second, the sample was drawn exclusively from Henan Province in China, limiting generalizability, though its median national status offers partial representativeness. Third, the cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference; our findings are configurational and exploratory. Fourth, the influence factors of MDT standardization and incentive perceptions are multifaceted, including organizational culture and leadership, and require future exploration.

Conclusion

The standardization level of cancer MDT implementation in China is generally satisfactory, however, further optimization is necessary to achieve the objective of homogenization. MDT physicians report greater satisfaction with non-economic incentives compared to economic incentives. Furthermore, the perceived design quality of incentive schemes is considered superior to their implementation efficiency. The results of incentive configuration analysis showed that: (1) The incentive means pathways that guided the high standardization level of cancer MDT implementation include comprehensive and intrinsically driven pathways. Autonomy and interpersonal relationships serve as core incentive means shared by both pathways, highlighting the importance of respecting professional autonomy and fostering a positive team collaboration atmosphere within the MDT. Performance rewards and learning and development exhibit a substitution effect, suggesting that practitioners can flexibly choose to strengthen either of these elements for incentive design based on resource conditions or development stages. Three pathways of incentive means lead to a low standardization level of cancer MDT implementation, revealing that extreme scenarios involving the absence of both economic and non- economic incentive means fundamentally undermine the standardization level. (2) Four pathways of incentive schemes guided the high standardization level of the cancer MDT. These can be categorized into two types: “procedure-standardized scenarios” (with efficiency of performance distribution and performance evaluation standards as core variables) and “result-oriented scenarios” (with level of performance distribution, method of performance distribution, and perceived team fairness as core variables). To enhance MDT standardization, administrators should differentiate incentives: grant autonomy to senior physicians, ensure transparent rewards for others, and institutionalize clear performance criteria and distribution timelines to avoid ineffective schemes. The configurational flexibility also implies that policymakers can adapt incentive mixes locally, but should avoid vague or delayed incentives that undermine standardization.
