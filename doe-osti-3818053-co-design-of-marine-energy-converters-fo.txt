Executive Summary
This project focused on performing foundational research and testing to accelerate the sector-wide
development and deployment of marine energy converters to provide persistent power at sea. Our
project team enabled autonomous interactions between Wave Energy Converters (WECs) to charge
Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs) through the co-design of predictive hydrodynamic models,
autonomy algorithms for docking and inspection, and intelligent interfaces between the dock and vehicle.
The developed technology was tested annually at the O. H. Hinsdale Wave Research Laboratory in
increasingly difficult conditions. The project achieved the stated goals of validating hydrodynamic models,
providing open-source datasets, achieving docking and inspection capabilities in challenging conditions
representative of the PacWave North and South testing sites, the Hawaii Wave Energy Test Site (WETS),
and the Kilo Nalu nursery site.

This report describes how all critical milestones have been met for this project.

The developed WEC & AUV hydrodynamic analyses have been completed. Hydrodynamic parameters
including the wave excitation force, added mass, and radiation damping have been successfully validated
through laboratory tests. The WEC-AUV Co-Design has resulted in two iterations of docking structures
with increasingly robust capabilities. The intelligent interfaces, components, and hardware have been
successfully integrated in the O.H. Hinsdale Wave Laboratory in annual deployments (four total). The
trajectory optimization, autonomy, and inspection algorithms have been successfully tested in the wave
laboratory with increasingly difficult wave conditions. Meeting the main project goal, we were able to
achieve >75% success rate docking in challenging wave conditions (representative surface wave height
>2.5m scaled and distorted in the Hinsdale Wave Lab to produce corresponding test waves) during
October 2025 testing. All data, results, and software have been uploaded to MHKDR and dispersed to the
wave energy community through interactions with UMERC. In addition, multiple journal articles,
conference papers, and workshop papers have been published to disseminate the results. We have
achieved outreach to the broader community through a PBS Changing Seas broadcast on Engineering the
Power of Waves (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UyIfRcKiZPM – also noted on page 52).

Subtask 1.1.0: WEC Hydrodynamic Model Validation

● Milestone 1.1.1: Numerical models of WEC archetypes validated against physical test data.
The FOSWEC2 lab-scale ProteusDS model has been validated by adding constant torques on two flap joints
to represent the friction in the physical model. The torques values were tuned for both the front (bow)
flap joint and the back (aft) flap joint to yield values of 24 Nm and 21m, respectively. The resulting pitch
motions for both flaps with these torques were validated using experimental measurements of a regular
wave with 0.136m wave height and 2.63s wave period (see Figure 1).
Two power-take-off (PTO) damping coefficients, C = 0.52 N-m-s/rad and C = 2.39 N-m-s/rad were
examined and results showed that the front (bow) flap was best represented by a damping of C = 2.39 N-
m-s/rad while the back (aft) flap was best represented by a damping of C = 0.52 N-m-s/rad. The phase
shift of the back-flap may be caused by the platform motions and body-to-body effect.
The Laboratory Upgrade Point Absorber (LUPA) model was developed in both ProteusDS and in WEC-Sim
and has been validated against experimental testing conducted in the O.H. Hinsdale Wave Flume in
January 2023. The model was validated for a single body heave-only, two body heave-only, and a moored
six degree-of-freedom mode. The float and spar heave drag co-efficients were calculated to be 0.79 and
0.64 respectively. Using a full sweep of PTO damping coefficients, the most efficient (maximizing
mechanical power) coefficients were determined for each wave period between 1.5 and 3.5 secs
(representing 7.5 to 17.5 sec at full PacWave scale). See Figure 2 for comparison between the
experimental and numerical simulations for the one-body and two-body heave only modes.
The Halona lab-scale ProteusDS model has been validated for free decay tests, but additional model
refinement and validation is needed for regular wave conditions. A first approach to model the oscillating
water column (OWC) without a second body moonpool (water-filled chamber inside a floating structure
that is open to the sea at the bottom) was attempted and validated for heave, surge, and pitch decay tests
by applying linear damping coefficients based on the percentage of critical damping observed during lab
testing. Although the agreement between ProteusDS decay tests and experiments was excellent, this did
not translate to appropriate motion response in regular waves. When applied to regular wave tests, heave
and surge model responses were found to match well with the experimental results, but there was
significant disagreement (<200% error) in the pitch/roll response. It is suspected that the pitch/roll error
is due to mass distribution related to the actual position of the moonpool. Further hydrodynamic modeling
of Halona using ProteusDS was not pursued to focus project efforts on the hydrodynamic modeling of
LUPA with a bottom-mounted dock, which was selected as the configuration for experimental testing in
the Task 14.

Subtask 1.2.0: AUV Hydrodynamic Modelling and Validation

● Milestone 1.2.1: Validation of AUV hydrodynamic model against Hinsdale testing data for Seabotix
 and BlueROV vehicles.
 As part of the second Hinsdale testing campaign, multiple wave excitation tests were conducted on
 the BlueROV2. In these tests, the vehicle was securely anchored to the ground above a 6 degree of
 freedom (DOF) load cell. We conducted a total of four tests under regular wave conditions and three
 under irregular waves. Irregular waves provide the vehicle with wave forces that are more complex
 and difficult to model. The effects of wave forces on the vehicle were measured and subsequently
 compared to the outcomes from ProteusDS simulations. After the initial comparison, the results
 suggest that the vehicle's hydrodynamic model needs to be revised through numerical modeling.

Subtask 1.3.0: WEC-AUV Dock Modelling and Validation

● Milestone 1.3.1: WEC-AUV Dock hydrodynamic model developed in ProteusDS
 WEC-AUV-Dock coupled system models have been developed under the ProteusDS modeling
 environment (Figure 2); LUPA field-scale model and FOSWEC2 field-scale device model were used as
 the represented WEC in the simulations. For both seafloor umbilical and floating 3rd-body docks were
 modeled and the simulations were running stable. Because the WECs are much bigger than the dock,
 for a hard-mounted dock design, the hydrodynamic effects of the dock on the WEC are negligible. The
 motion of the hard-mounted dock is equivalent to the motion of the connecting location of the WEC.
 All models have been uploaded to MHKDR.

Subtask 3.1.0: Docking Algorithm Development

● Milestone 3.1.1: (SMART milestone) Initial docking optimization algorithms using model predictive
 control (Wallen, 2019) tested in simulation. Validation in simulation showing successful
 rendezvous of AUV with WEC.
 We designed a docking algorithm that incorporates flow state estimation into the model predictive
 control (MPC) framework to account for the influence of unknown background flow. The proposed
 approaches (Figure 5) were tested against state-of-the-art (SOTA) algorithms through simulations
 involving various ocean conditions. Figure 6 illustrates the total time required to navigate the
 generated path for each of these algorithms. The results suggest that the SOTA algorithms encounter
 difficulties in achieving successful docking in the presence of strong ocean conditions, thus
 demonstrating the robustness of the proposed algorithms in such challenging scenarios.

● Milestone 3.1.2: (SMART milestone) Improved docking optimization algorithms using dynamic
 rendezvous algorithms tested in simulation. Validation showing quantitative improvements in
 rendezvous tolerances and constraints, including 1) docking success rate through repeated testing,
 2) average relative velocity between the vehicle and the dock during docking, 3) average docking
 time, and 4) actuation energy consumption.
 The docking optimization algorithms underwent multiple enhancements and rigorous testing within
 a simulation environment. To improve vehicle state estimation, an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) was
 introduced. The EKF effectively integrates data from multiple sources, including the Inertial

tracking). These enhanced docking algorithms were systematically tested across a range of ocean flow

To assess the capability of the proposed framework for docking under waves, we conducted an
evaluation across various sea states. This evaluation involved the co-design of hydrodynamic
modeling to simulate the dock's performance under different wave conditions. The dynamic response
of the dock, coupled with time-series data of wave orbital velocities recorded at a fixed location near
the dock using ProteusDS, was later incorporated into the navigation module. The results suggest that
the framework struggles to accurately estimate external velocities, encompassing ocean flow and
wave orbital velocities. This limitation has resulted in docking failures. Figure 8 visually represents the
performance of flow state estimation when the vehicle attempts to dock under diverse sea states.
The root-mean-square error (RMSE) is notably high under the energetic conditions tested from the
PacWave South facility (Newport, OR). These conditions spanned sea state codes 3–6, with significant
wave heights of 1.07–5.26 m and peak periods of 11.72–14.18 s. Additionally, our analysis indicated
that the dock's motion along the surge and heave axes exhibits significant variations during extreme
wave conditions.

 Figure 8. Performance evaluation of flow state estimation under different sea state conditions.

To address the constraints, we incorporated wave theory into the docking framework to efficiently
mitigate the influence of wave forces affecting the vehicle (Figure 9). Additionally, we significantly
improved the execution speed of the docking optimization algorithms, increasing it from less than 1
Hz to over 150 Hz (Figure 10). This enhancement enables the vehicle to respond rapidly and effectively
counteract wave disturbances.

Subtask 4.1.0: WEC Interface Requirements - Sizing and Energy Budgets

● Milestone 4.1.1: Framework developed for matching physical sizes and power production/usage
 of WECs and AUVs.
 A framework was developed in MATLAB allowing for user entry of various WEC, AUV and charging
 system parameters. This script can run faster than real time (a two-week simulation will generally run
 in under 5 seconds), and it uses inputs from a hydrodynamic modeling code to adjust mission status
 depending on the power output of the WEC and power consumption and mission duration for the
 AUV. A presentation by Ming Chen at UMERC/METS in Portland highlighted how this framework
 would be incorporated into modeling efforts for this project. Shown in Figure 14 is an example plot
 tracking the power levels in the WEC and AUV batteries for a sample scenario. The labels on Figure
 14 indicate mission status flags that are tracked through the script, and the vertical dashed lines show
 the points in time that those flags are switched.

 Figure 14. Example plot tracking the power levels in the WEC batteries and AUV for a sample scenario

● Milestone 4.1.2: Optimization of size and power requirements specific to prototype fabrication.
 The power modeling framework has been implemented using the output of the hydrodynamic model
 for the reference model 3 WEC, and a writeup has been completed as part of a WEC-AUV simulation
 framework paper (submitted and under revision). We used the BlueRobotics BlueROV2 and the
 REMUS 100 as two applications for this work. We assessed the required onboard power storage and
 redeployment frequency possible for each sea state that was modeled by the hydrodynamic modeling
 team. For most of the wave cases, the only limiting factor for redeployment of the AUVs was the
 mission duration and vehicle recharge speed. Through this exercise, we enacted new logic in the
 power model to simulate decision making required for the power system. We also built in a calculator
 to size the WEC’s on-board battery to ensure that this storage capacity doesn’t limit the operational
 capability of the WEC in the modeled sea states. An example of the modeled battery levels for the
 WEC (reference model 3) and AUV (BlueROV2), and the associated mission operational states is shown
 in Figure 15.

Subtask 4.3.0: AUV Dock Prototype Development

● Milestone 4.3.1: Completed fabrication of a working prototype dock, complete with data and
 communications between the WEC and the AUV.
 The second prototype of the WEC-AUV docking station was completed prior to the second round of
 testing in the O.H. Hinsdale Directional Wave Basin and incorporates several design changes to
 improve docking entry success rate and positional accuracy of the vehicle and charging plate, reduce
 added mass and inertial forces and improve durability for longer testing deployments. These
 modifications include a welded aluminum frame, perforated panels to reduce added mass, stainless
 steel skids for guiding and locating the AUV, and bumpers to prevent damage to the vehicle during
 entry. These features allow for increased tolerances for vehicle entry, accommodating plus or minus
 30 degrees of pitch, and 10 degrees of yaw.

Subtask 5.1.0: Trajectory optimization of AUV inspection paths & homing sequence
● Milestone 5.1.1: (SMART milestone) Initial trajectory optimization algorithms using sampling-
 based motion planning (Hollinger, 2014) tested in simulation. Validation showing baseline 3D
 reconstruction quality of 20 cm accuracy.
 We developed a multi-objective learning-based approach to determine the optimal waypoint for
 conducting inspections and performing 3D reconstructions of a target. This algorithm generates a set
 of waypoints distributed spherically, as illustrated in Figure 27, around the vehicle. It then selects the
 waypoint that minimizes path length and battery consumption while maximizing the volume of the
 3D reconstruction. To optimize the path between the current location and the chosen waypoint, we
 employed a variant of the rapidly exploring random tree method, as depicted in Figure 28. To validate
 our approach initially, we chose a turbine lander (Figure 29) as the target for inspection. Figure 30
 provides a visual representation of the path taken by the vehicle during this inspection process. We
 used an imaging sonar sensor, as seen in Figure 31, to perform the 3D reconstruction of the structure.
 Table 1 summarizes the inspection performance of our algorithm across multiple trials in mapping the
 turbine lander. The lander’s dimensions are 4.889 m x 2.779 m x 4.889 m, and each leg of the lander
 is 18.5 cm wide. The algorithm’s success mapping the lander’s legs demonstrates the accuracy
 required to meet Milestone 5.1.1.

● Milestone 9.1.1: WEC-Dock-AUV Hydrodynamic Model Development
 The WEC-Dock-AUV hydrodynamic model was developed in WEC-Sim using a 3 m float-diameter
 scaled geometry, shown in Figure 35. This scale is representative of small, low-power WECs intended
 for low-energy applications such as UUV docking and recharging, where large-scale power generation
 is not required. Figure 35 presents the principal dimensions and scale of the float and spar bodies,
 while Figure 36 illustrates the coupled WEC-Dock-AUV configuration used in the numerical model.
 The generalized motion of the float is denoted by 𝑥𝑥1 while the motion of the rigid spar–dock assembly
 is denoted by 𝑥𝑥2 where each vector represents the six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body motions (surge,
 sway, heave, roll, pitch, and yaw) of the corresponding body. The model was implemented within the
 WEC-SIM time-domain framework to evaluate the coupled hydrodynamic response, power output
 and dock motions.

 Figure 35 Dimensions of LUPA WEC scale Figure 36 Schematics of LUPA WEC-Dock-AUV system

The model was evaluated under irregular wave excitation using a Pierson–Moskowitz spectrum with
Hs = 1m, and peak periods ranging from Tp = 3 – 14 s. PTO damping values between 0 and 150,000
N·s/m were investigated to evaluate the trade-off between power capture and dock motion response.
Figure 37 shows the resulting mean PTO power, while Figure 38 presents the corresponding significant
dock heave velocity. The results indicate that power capture peaks at shorter wave periods, Tp = 4 –
6s and decreases with increasing wave period, consistent with the expected behavior of small-scale
WEC. Based on these trends, a representative damping value of 30,000 N·s/m was selected as the
optimal damping for power capture.
While dock motion generally increases with increasing PTO damping and power output (up to the
optimal damping region), larger dock motions reduce docking feasibility and autonomous docking
success. The observed trade-off between power capture and dock motion therefore highlights the
conflicting operational requirements between energy generation and AUV docking performance,
motivating the development of docking-mode control strategies investigated in Subtask 10.2.0.

Subtask 10.2.0: Coupled WEC-Dock-AUV System Control for Energy Harvesting and Docking

● Milestone 10.2.1: Validation of ‘optimal’ WEC-Dock system control strategies heave-only systems
 within the O.H. Hinsdale Wave Research Laboratory (low wave heights)
 Project teams have identified the need to develop a hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) system architecture
 to accurately represent the combinations of dock motions and wave orbital velocities. Hardware-in-
 the-loop was not in the original scope (this is a lesson learned). Thus, this effort does not have a
 specific milestone, but it is a core component to the control system delivery. The objective was to use
 HIL in an ‘off-line’ preprocessed manner, with a stretch goal to develop the control system for real-
 time implementation.
 An actuator was employed in the laboratory to replicate the full/field scale motion of the WEC and
 dock as shown in Figure 43. The wave-induced WEC+dock motions were predicted numerically and
 then physically applied in the wave tank. During the 2024 WEC-AUV testing at the HWRL, an
 asynchronous testing procedure has been developed to achieve synchronized wave and dock motion
 conditions, as illustrated in Figure 44. This procedure ensures that dock motions accurately reflect the
 dynamic interactions that would occur in real ocean conditions. The asynchronous testing process
 involves the following: (1) Run Distorted Wave Conditions in the Wave Flume: Laboratory experiments
 are conducted under distorted wave conditions derived from typical field wave scenarios. The
 distortion criteria are based on either acceleration amplitude matching or velocity amplitude
 matching to ensure hydrodynamic similarity. (2) Record Laboratory Wave Elevation Data: During the
 laboratory tests, wave elevation data at the dock location are recorded. (3) Convert Laboratory Wave
 38

 Data to Field Scale: The recorded laboratory wave elevation data are converted into field-scale wave
 elevation time histories using wave distortion algorithms developed in this study. (4) Simulate
 WEC/Dock Displacement: The WEC and dock displacements are calculated by a rigid-body
 hydrodynamic program (such as WEC-Sim, as applied in this study). The field-scale wave elevation
 input obtained in the previous step serves as the input for these simulations, enabling the prediction
 of field-scale dock motion. (5) Convert Dock Displacement to Laboratory Scale: The simulated field-
 scale WEC/dock displacement data are converted into laboratory-scale dock displacement time
 histories. This conversion ensures consistency with the wave conditions generated in the laboratory.
 (6) Rerun Wave Conditions with Synchronized Dock Motion: The distorted wave conditions are rerun
 in the laboratory while the dock is actuated using the synchronized laboratory-scale dock
 displacement. This final step synchronizes the dock motion with the wave field, allowing for an
 accurate assessment of AUV docking performance under realistic dynamic conditions.

 A Real-Time Hybrid Simulation (RTHS) framework has been developed for testing the WEC-AUV
 system, as illustrated in Figure 45. Given that no physical WEC was present in the laboratory setup,
 the framework integrates a numerical environment with the physical laboratory environment, with
 an objective of ensuring that the dock motion is simulated and controlled in real-time. The framework
 also adopted a mixed-scale approach, in which the model-scale wave conditions and dock motions
 were related to their full-scale counterparts using corresponding distortion methods. Note that model
 scale refers to laboratory scale, while full-scale refers to ocean scale.

From this final round of testing, we identified non-critical issues with noise from the wireless charging
system that we have addressed, allowing the system to operate more smoothly for future projects. During
testing, initiation of the wireless charging system would cause the optical modem to stop communication.
This was due to the software running the system, which sensed that the AHRS (one of the instruments
whose data is being communicated via the optical modem) stopped sending data. This drop-out from the
AHRS was not recoverable without power cycling the instrument.

The Wibotic transmitter electronics produces significant electromagnetic interference (EMI), and high
levels of noise were confirmed by engineers using wire loop sniff test. We also witnessed various COTS
hardware failing when near the Wibotic transmitter electronics, included keyboards and benchtop power
supplies. With this in mind, we began by testing the AHRS hardware while it was galvanically isolated from

 44

the dock electronics. It was easy to get the AHRS to fail while charging in this configuration when it was
~1 foot away from the Wibotic transmitter electronics.

Further scrutiny of the setup showed that the AHRS included high-impedance input control signals
attached to 17” of loose wire (open/floating on the other end) that were tied off into a loop with a
diameter of about 2.5”. One of these signals was listed at TARE/RESTOR”, which can be used to set the
AHRS yaw value to zero. When the AHRS is placed close enough to the Wibotic transmitter electronics
(while charging), we can see in the AHRS data that a TARE command is issued, and the yaw data is set to
zero. As the noise issues get worse, e.g., by placing the AHRS closer to the Wibotic transmitter electronics,
the AHRS reports many TARE (set yaw to 0) and ERROR messages before quickly locking up. To mitigate
this issue, we cut off the loose wire attached to these unused signals – effectively removing the antenna
coil. The result was effective, and we no longer saw any issues with the AHRS. As a result, we have been
able to simplify the script to initiate operation of the optical modem, and the system now continues
running through a recharge cycle.

Additional observations noted issues on the ROV side when initiating charging, including loss of ethernet
communication, loss of optical modem communications, and unsolicited activation of the ROV lights. To
improve on these issues, installed a CM filter, which corrected the loss of Ethernet communications. It
also improved but did not completely fix the other two issues. The partial success may be because the
filter could not be installed near the non-Wibotic electronics, because there was not enough room in the
electronics pressure housing.

Our engineers noted potential room for future improvements to the system, revolving around further
reducing the impact of noise from the wireless charging on the surrounding hardware. If possible,
additional assurance can be achieved by charging batteries that are galvanically isolated from any critical
electronics. This can be achieved with mechanical relays. When integrating a Wibotic transmitter, care
should be taken to ensure electronics are immune to EMI. If possible, the Wibotic transmitter electronics
should not be near critical electronics. For underwater systems, it could be better to have the transmitter
electronics in their own bottle away from any controller electronics.

Subtask 13.3.0: Integration of trajectory optimization onto AUV
● Milestone 13.3.1: Improved trajectory optimization integrated on vehicle.
  The project team created a global planner to generate a trajectory from the vehicle location to the
  dock. Path is generated in real-time using the RRT* algorithm (an asymptotically optimal version of
  rapidly-exploring random tree) and keeps AUV pointed towards the dock to maintain visual tracking.
● Milestone 13.3.2: Final trajectory optimization integrated on vehicle.
  Planner was successful when paired with motion controller during Hinsdale trials, resulting in fewer
  collisions than previous tests that did not use the global planner. Robot localization based on visual
  fiducials and sonar odometry proved noisy during testing, but the planner was found to be robust to
  sensor noise through efficient re-planning and use of keep-out zones to prevent dock collisions.

 We successfully achieved autonomous docking under conditions simulating surface waves exceeding
 2.5m in height, achieving a success rate over 75% across more than five trials. This was accomplished
 using an improved controller that integrates Gaussian Processes (GP), Control Barrier Functions (CBF),
 and a Model Predictive Controller (MPC). Additionally, a sampling-based planner was implemented to
 incorporate collision avoidance within the framework. The localization relied on a sensor fusion
 system combining data from the IMU, compass, sonar, and camera - significantly enhancing accuracy
 by mitigating the effects of motion blur that typically causes odometry loss. 3D reconstruction of the
 docking station was completed using ORB-SLAM2, a real-time monocular SLAM algorithm.

Impact
The results of this work have been disseminated to the marine energy and robotics communities through
publications, demonstrations, and media outreach. These discussions included utilization of the
autonomy system and modeling for non-grid marine energy applications. As part of the 2022 O.H. Hinsdale
Testing, the project was presented to a delegation of political leaders, including Oregon Governor Kate
Brown, Oregon Senators Ron Wyden and Jeff Merkley, and US DoE Secretary Jennifer Granholm. These
leaders discussed the major impact that marine energy and blue economy applications could have on
energy production in the future. During 2024 testing, NSF Director Dr. Sethuraman Panchanathan was
given a demonstration of our docking capability in the wave laboratory. The PI has annually provided an
update to industry and academic partners at the PMEC All-Center meeting. Both the PMEC Comms team
and the University Marketing and Relations team at Oregon State University share project output broadly
on social media. Project team members were interviewed for a PBS Changing Seas special highlighting
OSU and the O.H. Hinsdale Wave Research Laboratory (to be aired). The following section outlines the
publications and software releases resulting from this project.
