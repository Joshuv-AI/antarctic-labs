I. PROGRESS AND ACCOMPLISHMENTS
 A. Major Accomplishments
 1. Current Reporting Period

 The accomplishments for Q3 are summarized as follows:

 1. In Quarter 3, we established an experimental facility for measuring critical current and
 FCL performance of HTS cables, consisting of a liquid nitrogen bath, high-current power
 supplies (up to 45 kVA), and instrumentation rack with multimeters and lock-in amplifiers.
 We completed a LabVIEW program for critical current measurement and successfully
 tested a two-layer inductive cable prototype. We are currently configuring the DC power
 supplies in a master-slave parallel configuration to deliver up to 3000A for FCL testing,
 which will be our primary focus in Q4.
 2. Quarter 2 results showed fault currents can reach 5 times the critical current at 20 V/m
 fault voltage. This quarter we used simulations to understand the effect of stabilizer
 thickness on FCL performance so that we can further reduce the fault current. The
 simulation confirmed that reducing copper stabilizer thickness by 50 % can lead to 30%
 reduction in the peak fault current.
 3. This quarter, we focused on designing two 6-layer high-current cables with the same
 number of tapes but different designs, one inductive and one non-inductive. Each cable
 uses a hollow former constructed from a G10 tube with copper current leads, designed
 to accommodate an optional ferromagnetic core for testing. We provided these special
 formers and the cable designs to an HTS cable manufacturer so they can build cables for
 the project.
 4. Preparation of manuscript and presentation for the 2026 Applied Superconductivity
 Conference is in progress

 2. Overall Project Status

 2.1. Cable testing facility and progress
 Quarter 3, we began establishing an experimental facility to measure the critical current and fault
 current limiter (FCL) performance of HTS cables. Figure 1 shows the completed experimental
 facility. As illustrated, HTS cable samples are placed in an elongated liquid nitrogen bath for
 testing. The system comprises a 45 kVA DC/AC power supply, two 10V-1500A DC power
 supplies, and an instrumentation rack equipped with high-speed multimeters and lock-in amplifiers
 for temperature, voltage and current measurements.

 1

Figure 1. Experimental facility with high current power suppliers, instrumentation rack and
Nitrogen bath. A 2-layer inductive cable sample is shown cooled to 77 K for critical current
testing.

We completed development of a LabVIEW program for critical current measurement and
performed several measurements on a small prototype cable fabricated in Quarter 2. Figure 2
presents the I-V curve measured on a two-layer inductive cable, indicating a critical current (Ic)
of 465A, which is close to the predicted value.

For FCL measurements, the two DC power supplies will be connected in parallel using a master-
slave configuration to provide DC current up to 3000A. We are currently testing and
programming the power supplies in this configuration. FCL testing will be our primary focus in
Q4 of this year and Q1 of next year.

Figure 2. The I-V curve measured in a 2-layer inductive cable

 2.2. Using developed simulation model to design 2kA DC HTS cables for testing

Based on results in quarter 2, the fault current may reach nearly 5 times of cable critical current in
the extreme case with fault voltage reaching 20 V/m. We predict that reducing the copper stabilizer
thickness in HTS YBCO tapes would increase the resistance of the normal conductor portions
(which carry most fault currents during a fault), thereby reducing peak fault current. Simulations
were used to quantitatively predict the impact of copper stabilizer thickness on peak fault current
and to design an optimal 6-layer cable for testing.

Simulations were performed on two 6-layer inductive cables with similar designs but different
copper stabilizer thicknesses in the YBCO tapes. One cable uses YBCO tapes with 10-µm-thick
copper stabilizer, while the other uses YBCO tapes with 5-µm-thick copper stabilizer. Both cables
have ferromagnetic core. The primary design parameters of the cables are indicated in Table 1.

Figure 3 plots fault currents for Cable 1 (10-µm copper stabilizer) and Cable 2 (5-µm copper
stabilizer) under extreme conditions with 20 V/m fault voltage. The thinner copper stabilizer
significantly reduces peak fault current. Decreasing thickness from 10 µm to 5 µm reduces the
peak by approximately 30%, from ~18.5 kA to ~13 kA.

The combination of thinner copper stabilizer and ferromagnetic core also narrows the fault current
pulse width. Higher stabilizer resistance leads to lower fault current, which reduces the axial
magnetic field. This increases the magnetic core's permeability, strengthening its effect in slowing
fault current rise and reducing peak values. These reductions enable safer breaker operation during
fault clearing and potentially reduce heating in the HTS cable.

Figure 3. The current waveforms in different cable designs during a short circuit fault in the grid
when the transmission length is 10 km.

Figure 4 shows the temperature distribution across the cross-section after 50 ms of fault for both
cable designs. For Cable 1 (10-µm copper stabilizer), the peak temperature rise reaches 214 K.

 4

Under identical fault conditions, Cable 2 (5-µm copper stabilizer) exhibits a peak temperature rise
of only 182 K.

Although Cable 2's fault current pulse has a considerably lower peak value and narrower width,
its stabilizer resistance is approximately twice that of Cable 1. Consequently, the temperature rise
in Cable 2 is only about 30 K lower than in Cable 1, despite the significant reduction in the peak
value and width of the fault current pulse.

 2.3. Cable design and manufacture

This quarter, we also focused on designing and manufacturing high-current cables for testing.
We plan to fabricate two 6-layer cables with the same number of tapes, but one with an inductive
design and the other with a non-inductive design. Based on simulation results presented in
section 2.2, the YBCO superconducting tapes with 5-µm-thick copper stabilizer are chosen to
build those cables. Table 2 shows the parameters of YBCO tapes and the detailed design
specifications for these cables.

 Parameter Value

 5

 Cable former radius 6.35 mm
 Number of layers 6
 Number of tapes per layer 3

 Tape width 4 mm
 Tape substrate thickness 50 µm
 Copper thickness in each YBCO tape 5 µm × 2 (two sides)
 Ag layer thickness 1 µm × 2 (two sides)
 Critical current of cables About 2000 A
 Core material Air or ferromagnetic core

Table 2. Specification of HTS tapes and 6-layer cables that will be fabricated for testing. Those
cables have same parameters but one with inductive design and one with non-inductive designs

The 18 YBCO tapes wound around the cable former must be well soldered to the current leads to
ensure low contact resistance and balanced current sharing between them. Figure 1 shows the
hollow former for these cables. Each former consists of a G10 tube joined at the ends with
copper tubes that serve as current leads. The copper tubes are tightly threaded into the G10 tube,
creating a strong mechanical joint that remains secure even when high heat is applied to the
copper during the process of soldering HTS tapes onto the copper tubes. The fixture forms a
hollow former with OD = 12.7 mm and ID = 9.5 mm, allowing us to insert a ferromagnetic core
inside the former (see Figure 1) to test cables both with and without a magnetic core.

We initially attempted to wind the cables in-house, but it proved very challenging to accurately
control the pitch of the HTS tapes when winding by hand because the springy Hastelloy
substrates comprise 90% of the YBCO tape volume. Ultimately, we will provide Advanced
Conductor Technologies (ACT) LLC with the cable formers, and they will follow our cable
design specifications to wind the YBCO tapes onto the formers.
