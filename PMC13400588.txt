Point-of-care echocardiographic assessment of right ventricle systolic function in infants with severe bronchiolitis: a prospective observational study
Authors: Julien Gotchac, Alix Rousseau, Agathe Arcourt, Judith Chareyre, Roman Klifa, Paul Nolent, Barbara Ros, Aurélie Hayotte, Julie Guichoux, Olivier Brissaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to assess right ventricle (RV) systolic function in infants with severe bronchiolitis using tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) and to explore its prognostic value. We hypothesized that lower TAPSE at pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission could be associated with prolonged length of stay (LOS) and duration of respiratory support. We conducted a prospective single-center observational pilot study in a tertiary general PICU during the 2022–2023 bronchiolitis season. All children < 12 months admitted to PICU for bronchiolitis were eligible. TAPSE was measured by transthoracic echocardiography at PICU admission. Patients were followed up until discharge. Forty-six patients were included, aged from 16 days to 9.5 months. TAPSE Z-score was within normal range (− 2 to + 2) in 36/46 (78%) patients and > + 2 in 10/46 (22%) patients. TAPSE Z-score was weakly associated with PICU LOS (Spearman ρ = − 0.38, p = 0.009) and moderately associated with the duration of respiratory support (Spearman ρ = − 0.45, p = 0.002). No significant association was found between other echocardiographic parameters and PICU LOS or duration of respiratory support. In post hoc exploratory multivariable analysis, TAPSE Z-score was independently associated with a duration of respiratory support < 3 days (OR = 2.29, 95% CI [1.25; 4.17], p = 0.007). Conclusion: TAPSE was within or above normal range in children with severe bronchiolitis, suggesting that RV systolic dysfunction is uncommon. Introduction

Viral bronchiolitis is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in infants worldwide [1]. Moreover, the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission rate for severe bronchiolitis is increasing [2,3]. Well-recognized risk factors for severe bronchiolitis include age < 2 months, premature birth, and underlying comorbidities [3,4]. Yet, most infants admitted to PICU for severe bronchiolitis are previously healthy [5], highlighting the need to identify new individual prognostic markers.

Elevated pulmonary arterial pressure in bronchiolitis has long been described [6–9]. This may arise from various mechanisms such as hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and positive pressure ventilation. Although this increase in afterload may affect right ventricle (RV) systolic function [10], the prognostic value of such an alteration in bronchiolitis is equivocal [7, 11]. Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) is a simple and reproducible point-of-care echocardiographic measure of RV systolic longitudinal function. It has been correlated with clinical outcomes in patients with heart failure or pulmonary hypertension (PH) [12]. Recently, associations between TAPSE and mortality have also been reported in adult patients with SARS-CoV2 acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [13], and in children with septic shock [14]. However, despite published reference values for TAPSE in children [15], little data is available regarding children with severe bronchiolitis.

In this prospective pilot study, we aimed to assess RV systolic function in infants admitted to the PICU for severe bronchiolitis using TAPSE and to evaluate its prognostic value. We hypothesized that lower TAPSE at admission could be associated with a prolonged PICU length of stay (LOS) and duration of respiratory support. Secondary objectives included investigating associations between TAPSE and baseline clinical, ventilatory, and echocardiographic parameters, as well as assessing the prognostic value of other point-of-care echocardiographic measures.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective observational pilot study was conducted in a single French PICU over a 5-month period during the 2022–2023 bronchiolitis season. This study was conducted before the implementation of routine RSV immunization programs in France. During the study period, only children with congenital heart disease or who were born extremely prematurely were eligible for immunoprophylaxis with palivizumab.

All patients with severe bronchiolitis and aged < 12 months were eligible. Bronchiolitis was diagnosed according to the American Academy of Pediatrics definition [16], with the age criterion adapted to local clinical practices. Infants younger than 1 month were eligible, as their management did not differ solely because of age. Conversely, patients aged 12 to 23 months were not eligible as they are typically treated differently in our institution, including more frequent use of inhaled bronchodilators. Severe bronchiolitis was pragmatically defined as a PICU admission for respiratory failure requiring ventilatory support. Exclusion criteria included congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease, or poor echocardiographic windows precluding TAPSE measurement. Patients were included if TAPSE could be measured, and they were followed-up until hospital discharge.

Data collection

Transthoracic echocardiograms were performed closest as possible to PICU admission, within a maximum of 36 h, with a Vivid S60 echocardiograph and a 6S probe (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK). TAPSE measurement was standardized using M-mode at the lateral tricuspid annulus, guided from an apical four-chamber two-dimensional view, and the total systolic excursion was recorded from the end of diastole to the end of systole. TAPSE was measured in millimeters and expressed in Z-score according to published reference values for age and body surface area (BSA) [15]. For each patient, three acquisitions were obtained to calculate a mean TAPSE value. Obtaining these three acquisitions typically required between 3 and 5 min. Other echocardiographic data collected were as follows: left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) determined by Teicholz method from a parasternal long axis view (%); tricuspid regurgitation velocity (m.s−1); and the ratio from pulmonary artery acceleration time to RV ejection time (PAAT:RVET), a measure associated with PH when < 0.31 in children [17], using pulsed Doppler in the RV outflow tract from a parasternal short axis view. Acquisitions could be performed by one of the eight pediatric intensivists with basic functional echocardiography training (i.e., a 1-day theoretical training course on a simulator, followed by a 6-month practical training period under expert supervision, during or after residency). Only two senior attending physicians did not take part in the measurements. Acquisitions were secondarily reviewed by a senior echocardiographer who performed all measurements offline, blind to patient data and with no access to the results of the bedside measure. A second offline analysis was performed by the same investigator to assess intraobserver reproducibility of TAPSE measures. The TAPSE values measured by the investigator at bedside versus the ones measured by the senior echocardiographer offline were used to assess interobserver reproducibility.

In addition to echocardiographic data and medical history, demographic and clinical data were collected at baseline (i.e., the echocardiography timepoint), including respiratory support, vital signs, and modified Wood Clinical Asthma severity Score (m-WCAS) for non-intubated patients. BSA (m2) was calculated from weight only [18]. The following data were retrieved at hospital discharge: viral documentation, PICU LOS (expressed in days as discharge date minus admission date), duration of respiratory support (days), and maximum level of respiratory support required (in increasing order, high flow nasal cannula (HFNC), non-invasive ventilation, or invasive mechanical ventilation [19]). Respiratory support escalation was defined as an increase in respiratory support between baseline (i.e., the echocardiography timepoint) and maximum level of respiratory support required. The duration of respiratory support was calculated as the difference between the date and time of weaning from all respiratory support and the date and time of inclusion (i.e., the echocardiography timepoint). Since ventilation could be initiated prior to PICU admission and echocardiography, the total duration of respiratory support was also reported (i.e., difference between the date and time of weaning from all respiratory support and the date and time of initiation of a respiratory support).

Data analysis

In this pilot study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis without imputation. Comparisons were restricted to subgroups with available data.

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation when their distribution is normal or as median [first quartile; third quartile] otherwise. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and proportions. Quantitative variables were compared using the Student t-test when their distribution was normal or the Mann–Whitney test otherwise. A Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to test the normal distribution. Spearman rank correlation coefficients ρ were used to assess associations between continuous variables, and were interpreted based on the stratification proposed by Schober et al. [20]. Binomial logistic regressions were used to test the association between categorical or continuous predictors and binary outcomes, and the odds ratio (OR) is reported with its 95% confidence interval (95% CI). In exploratory analysis, variables associated with outcome or TAPSE with a p-value < 0.1 were integrated into a multivariable binomial logistic regression model. For statistical analysis involving TAPSE, the values measured offline by a senior echocardiographer were used. All statistical analyses involving TAPSE were also performed using bedside measurements instead, as a sensitivity analysis. The reproducibility of TAPSE measurement was evaluated by calculating intraclass correlation coefficients to assess interobserver and intraobserver reliability [21]. All analysis were performed with RStudio version 4.3 (RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA, USA) and the Jamovi 1.2 graphical interface (The Jamovi project, Sydney, Australia). Differences with a p-value of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was carried out in accordance with the Good Clinical Practices protocol and Declaration of Helsinki principles. It was approved by an Institutional Review Board (Comité de Protection des Personnes Sud Méditerranée V, number 2022-A01867-36, approval date October 18, 2022) and retrospectively registered on Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT07209956, October 7, 2025). Informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants, before or within 24 h after study procedures.

Results

A total of 157 infants were admitted in PICU for suspicion of bronchiolitis during the study period. Among 146 eligible patients, 46 (32%) were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). The main reason for non-inclusion was unavailability of an investigator to perform echocardiography, mostly due to an excessive workload. Patient age ranged from 16 days to 9.5 months and weight from 2.5 to 9 kg. Seven patients had a history of prematurity (birth gestational age between 30 and 36 weeks), but all had a corrected gestational age > 38 weeks at inclusion. PICU LOS ranged from 1.5 to 16.5 days, and the total duration of respiratory support from 1.5 to 13.5 days with a median of 3.0 days. Between inclusion and PICU discharge, 6/46 (13%) patients experienced a respiratory support escalation as defined above. All patients were alive at PICU discharge. The reproducibility of TAPSE measurement was good: the intraclass correlation coefficients were 0.84 and 0.85 for intraobserver and interobserver reliability, respectively. However, due to poor ultrasound window or patient restlessness, left ventricular ejection fraction, PAAT:RVET ratio, and exploitable tricuspid valve regurgitation (TVR) were measurable in 32, 31, and 9 patients, respectively. Thirty-six out of 46 (78%) TAPSE values at admission were within normal range for age and BSA, and 10/46 (22%) were supra-normal (i.e., > 2), suggesting hyperkinetic RV. No patient had overt left ventricular systolic dysfunction as the minimal left ventricular ejection fraction value was 58%. PVAT:RVET ratio was < 0.31 in 19/31 (61%), suggesting elevated pulmonary pressures. At baseline, no significant association was found between TAPSE Z-score and invasive ventilation or positive pressure ventilation. Pulsed oxygen saturation was the only baseline parameter associated with TAPSE Z-score, with a weak correlation (Spearman ρ = 0.37, p = 0.011) (Supplemental Digital Content, e-Table 1). Regarding clinical outcomes, TAPSE Z-score was weakly associated with PICU LOS (Spearman ρ = − 0.38, p = 0.009, Fig. 2) and moderately associated with the duration of respiratory support (Spearman ρ = − 0.45, p = 0.002, Fig. 3). However, no association was found between TAPSE Z-score and respiratory support escalation (OR = 0.87, 95% CI [0.48; 1.60], p = 0.663). Sensitivity analysis conducted with bedside instead of offline TAPSE values found relatively similar results (Supplemental Digital Content, e-Table 2). In a post hoc sensitivity analysis including only non-intubated patients, TAPSE Z-score was significantly moderately associated with the duration of respiratory support (Spearman ρ = − 0.49, p = 0.001).

Due to the apparent non-linear relationship between TAPSE Z-score and duration of respiratory support on visual inspection, an exploratory post hoc analysis using a data-driven threshold (respiratory support < 3 days) was conducted. TAPSE Z-score was associated with a short duration of respiratory support (< 3 days): OR = 2.15, 95% CI [1.29; 3.58], p = 0.003. HFNC ventilation at baseline was also significantly associated with a short duration of respiratory support (OR = 4.89, 95% CI [1.30; 18.38], p = 0.019), while the other clinical parameters were not (Supplemental Digital Content, e-Table 3 and e-Table 4). In multivariable binomial logistic regression, TAPSE Z-score was the only parameter independently associated with a short duration of respiratory support: (OR = 2.29, 95% CI [1.25; 4.17], p = 0.007). Univariate and multivariable binomial logistic regressions are detailed in the Supplemental Digital Content (e-Table 4 and e-Table 5). Including only patients in whom these echocardiographic measures were available, TAPSE Z-score was significantly associated with a short duration of respiratory support after adjustment for PAAT:RVET ratio (OR = 2.27, 95% CI [1.14; 4.55], p = 0.020) or for LVEF (OR = 2.19, 95% CI [1.08; 4.45], p = 0.031).

Regarding other echocardiographic parameters, no significant association was found between LVEF and PICU LOS (Spearman ρ = − 0.19, p = 0.302), duration of respiratory support (Spearman ρ = − 0.21, p = 0.239), or respiratory support escalation (OR = 1.14, 95% CI [0.97; 1.34], p = 0.108, for 1% increase in LVEF). No significant association was found between PAAT:RVET ratio and PICU LOS (Spearman ρ = 0.13, p = 0.477), duration of respiratory support (Spearman ρ = 0.19, p = 0.306), or respiratory support escalation (OR = 1.11, 95% CI [0.28; 4.39], p = 0.880, for 0.1 increase in PAAT:RVET ratio). Associations between clinical outcomes and TVR maximal velocity were not tested due to the high proportion of absent or unexploitable regurgitations.

Discussion

In this prospective pilot study, TAPSE values at PICU admission were mostly within the normal range in infants with severe bronchiolitis, suggesting preserved longitudinal RV systolic function, despite evidence of elevated pulmonary pressures. TAPSE was moderately and negatively associated with the duration of respiratory support and with PICU LOS, while the other echocardiographic parameters were not. While the initial hypothesis was that low TAPSE would be associated with poor outcomes, no TAPSE value was below the normal range. Interestingly, exploratory post hoc analysis using a data-driven threshold suggested that particularly high TAPSE values might be associated with a short duration of respiratory support compared with normal TAPSE values.

Previous studies assessing RV function in bronchiolitis have reported inconsistent findings, mostly suggesting limited impairment. In a cohort of 26 children with moderate bronchiolitis, TAPSE values were within normal range (median 11.9 mm), while RV output was higher than expected [11]. Conversely, in a larger cohort of 181 infants, TAPSE absolute value was lower in those requiring positive pressure respiratory support [22]. However, these patients were significantly younger, and their median TAPSE (11 mm) likely remained within the normal range. In another study on 50 children with bronchiolitis, TAPSE did not differ from healthy controls, although the RV Tei index, another echocardiographic index of RV function, was higher in the bronchiolitis group, suggesting RV impairment [23]. Thorburn et al. also reported an elevated RV Tei index in 7/34 mechanically ventilated children with severe bronchiolitis [24]. This alteration, however, was not associated with clinically significant elements. Regarding more advanced echocardiographic techniques, a recent study reported a lower value of RV longitudinal strain in 28 infants with bronchiolitis compared to 10 controls, and 9/28 patients had a value below normal range for age [25]. These 9 patients had worst clinical outcomes, but they also had a significant left ventricle impairment. While our point-of-care echocardiographic study was not designed to provide a comprehensive assessment, our findings support the absence of overt longitudinal RV systolic dysfunction.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to report an association between TAPSE and meaningful clinical outcomes in infants with severe bronchiolitis. While this association could theoretically be confounded by disease severity, several findings support a genuine prognostic role. First, TAPSE Z-score was not associated with the ventilation mode at the time of the measure, nor with any other baseline clinical parameter besides pulsed oxygen saturation. Second, TAPSE Z-score was the only parameter independently associated with a short duration of respiratory support in a multivariable model. Although it was not associated with respiratory support escalation, predicting the duration of ventilation or PICU LOS is still clinically meaningful, particularly given the very low mortality in bronchiolitis. In adults, the prognostic value of TAPSE has also been reported in patients with SARS-CoV-2 ARDS: a meta-analysis of seven studies found that each 1 mm decrease in TAPSE was associated with a 20% increase in mortality [13]. In pediatric bronchiolitis, previous studies—including those discussed above—have not reported such a clear prognostic value of TAPSE. Our study adds to the literature by focusing on severe cases admitted to PICU. Moreover, by expressing TAPSE as Z-scores rather than absolute values, we accounted for confounding effects of age and weight.

The pathophysiological basis of the observed association between TAPSE and the duration of respiratory support is unclear and warrants further investigations, especially given that TAPSE values were within or above the normal range. However, it should be remembered that reference values for TAPSE are derived from healthy children [15]. Although our study was not designed to address this issue, it can be hypothesized that under the hemodynamic stress of bronchiolitis, higher TAPSE values (i.e., hyperkinetic RV) might be the most appropriate physiological response. Conversely, a merely “normal” TAPSE might actually be “abnormally normal,” indicating an insufficient adaptive response related to an impaired contractile reserve or a suboptimal ventriculoarterial coupling. While impaired RV contractile reserve has been correlated with adverse outcomes in adults [26], further studies using comprehensive echocardiography or advanced physiological measurements are needed to explore this purely speculative hypothesis in bronchiolitis. If confirmed, these findings could encourage research into targeted interventions for patients with this potentially inadequate RV response.

Results for other echocardiographic parameters should be interpreted cautiously given the relatively high proportion of missing data. By contrast, only one patient was excluded because of an impossibility to measure TAPSE. This observation highlights its simplicity and feasibility compared to more complex measurements, an important feature for a point-of-care bedside prognostic tool usable by intensivists. LVEF was preserved in all patients, as previously described [11, 27]. Although alterations in left ventricular systolic function have been reported in bronchiolitis and associated with clinical outcome, they are typically detected with more advanced techniques such as the Tei index [23] or myocardial strain [25]. Regarding pulmonary arterial pressure, although TVR were rarely exploitable, a high proportion of patients had a PAAT:RVET ratio < 0.31 suggesting PH. Mild-to-moderate PH has long been described in bronchiolitis [6, 8, 28]. Kimura et al. reported PH in 29/154 children with bronchiolitis, and it was associated with PICU admission, LOS, and intubation [9]. However, all patients with PH either had risk factors or presented with acute circulatory failure. In our study, a low PAAT:RVET ratio was not associated with clinical outcomes nor with RV systolic dysfunction. This finding is consistent with those from Rossi et al. in 169 infants with bronchiolitis [29]. However, it raises once again the question of whether a “normal” TAPSE, i.e., Z-score between − 2 and + 2, truly reflects optimal RV adaptation in severe bronchiolitis.

This study has several limitations. First, no formal sample size calculation was performed, and the number of patients included was relatively small, although it was comparable to other pilot echocardiographic studies in children with bronchiolitis. Nonetheless, our sample size was sufficient to detect a significant statistical association. Second, the high proportion of patients who were not included due to investigator unavailability might have induced a selection bias. While the included and non-included eligible patients had similar demographic characteristics, non-included eligible patients had a shorter duration of respiratory support. This likely reflects a higher proportion of intubated patients in the study population compared to the target population, as intubated patients were more likely to undergo an echocardiography evaluation in clinical practice. However, our post hoc sensitivity analysis excluding intubated patients yielded similar results. Third, we chose to assess RV systolic function using TAPSE, which does not comprehensively capture global RV mechanics and is influenced by load conditions. Moreover, echocardiograms were performed by intensivists, meaning that potentially useful advanced hemodynamic parameters were not collected and some acquisitions may have been imperfect. Yet, the choice of measuring TAPSE under these real-life conditions also constitutes a strength of this study about a point-of-care ultrasound tool, and our sensitivity analysis performed with bedside measurements yielded similar results. Finally, the timing of the TAPSE measurement may be questioned, and a longitudinal assessment of its evolution over the disease course would have been informative. However, this was outside the scope of the study.

Conclusion

In this pilot study, TAPSE at PICU admission was within or above normal range in infants with severe bronchiolitis, suggesting preserved RV systolic function. However, there was a statistical association between high TAPSE Z-score and shorter duration of PICU stay and respiratory support. The clinical relevance and the pathophysiological basis of this association remain uncertain and warrant further investigations. Until then, echocardiogram cannot be recommended for all patients with severe bronchiolitis admitted to PICU. If performed, however, a high TAPSE Z-score may represent one informative prognostic element among others.
