 Summary

The Stable Salt Reactor (SSR) combines the proven structural design of light water reactor fuel
assemblies with the inherent safety and fuel-cycle advantages of molten salt technology. In its fast
reactor configuration, the SSR utilizes recycled nuclear waste as fuel, sealed within narrow salt-
filled fuel pins and cooled by a surrounding liquid salt coolant. Reliable transfer of heat from the
molten fuel salt through the cladding to the external coolant is essential for both reactor safety and
performance.

 This work investigates conjugate heat transfer (CHT) in the SSR’s salt-filled fuel pins using
NekRS, a high-fidelity spectral element computational fluid dynamics (CFD) solver. The analyses
capture internal natural convection within the molten fuel salt and external forced convection in the
coolant, under steady-state and transient operating conditions. Parametric studies evaluate how
variations in reactor power and coolant flow rate influence heat transfer distributions and system
response.

 The high-fidelity CFD results are time-averaged and post-processed for direct comparison with
moderate-fidelity Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) models, and for the development of
reduced-order models within the SAM system code. These validated models support fast-running
safety analyses of normal and off-normal transients, improving predictive capability for key safety
margins. By integrating advanced CFD with system-level safety tools, this study strengthens the
modeling framework for SSR design, reduces uncertainty in molten salt CHT simulations, and
accelerates the engineering and licensing of next-generation nuclear reactors.

1 Introduction

Global electricity demand is reaching unprecedented levels, driven not only by traditional sectors
such as industry and transportation but also by emerging power-intensive applications like artificial
intelligence (AI). Data centers supporting AI workloads alone are projected to consume about 2%
of global electricity—roughly 536 TWh—in 2025, highlighting the urgent need for clean, reliable,
and scalable baseload generation to sustain future digital and industrial growth [1].

 Advanced nuclear reactors represent a compelling option to meet these expanding needs while
minimizing environmental impact and ensuring high safety standards. Among the Generation
IV concepts, molten salt reactors (MSRs) are particularly promising. Their ability to operate at
high temperatures improves thermal efficiency, while inherent safety features—such as negative
temperature reactivity coefficients and freeze-plug drainage—enhance passive safety. Moreover,
their flexible fuel cycles can recycle actinides from spent nuclear fuel, offering both energy security
and waste reduction benefits [2, 3].

 The Stable Salt Reactor–Waste Burner (SSR-W), developed by Moltex Energy, extends these
advantages by combining a fast neutron spectrum with established light water reactor fuel assembly
geometries [4, 5]. In this design, recycled waste actinides are dissolved in a chloride salt matrix and
contained within narrow salt-filled fuel pins, which are cooled by an external chloride-based coolant.
This approach preserves the passive safety of salt-based circulation while leveraging the robustness
of proven fuel-pin structures. Continuous online fuel recycling within the core further reduces the
volume and long-term radiotoxicity of nuclear waste destined for geological disposal [6, 7].

 To support the development of such reactors, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) has become
a critical tool for exploring thermal-hydraulic behavior under realistic operating conditions. High-
fidelity CFD simulations are particularly valuable for MSRs, where experimental studies are often
challenging or prohibitively expensive [8, 9]. Early CFD work, such as studies on blanket and
cavity flows in the Molten Salt Fast Reactor (MSFR), established key heat transfer regimes that
informed cavity designs [8]. More recently, integrated modeling approaches—such as Hua et al.’s
coupling of reactor physics codes (PROTEUS, PERSENT) with CFD (STAR-CCM+) and system
analysis tools (SAM)—have demonstrated the importance of capturing conjugate heat transfer
(CHT) effects in predicting fuel behavior and reactor safety [10]. Despite this progress, the complex
CHT interactions between molten fuel salts and external coolant require further high-fidelity studies
to improve predictive accuracy and guide SSR design optimization.

 This study advances the modeling of CHT in the SSR fuel pin using NekRS, a high-fidelity
spectral element CFD code [11]. Building on recent developments in spectrally accurate overlapping
grids [12], turbulence modeling [13], and GPU acceleration [14], NekRS provides the capability
to resolve fine-scale flow and heat transfer structures in reactor-relevant geometries. Here, we
investigate internal natural convection within the molten fuel salt and external forced convection in
the surrounding coolant under both steady and transient conditions. Large eddy simulation (LES)
and Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) methods are employed in parallel to characterize
the heat transfer processes, quantify modeling uncertainties, and support the calibration of closure
models for the SAM system code.

 1

 The analysis further employs the NekNek coupling scheme to link the CHT simulations of the
fuel and coolant subdomains across an overlapping cladding region [12, 15]. Parametric studies are
carried out to examine the effects of reactor power and coolant flow rate on thermal performance,
with results used to refine reduced-order heat transfer models for transient safety analysis in SAM.
Ultimately, this work contributes to improving the predictive fidelity of SSR designs, reducing
uncertainties in molten salt heat transfer simulations, and supporting the engineering and licensing
of next-generation nuclear systems. The primary objectives of this work include:

 • High-fidelity LES and RANS benchmarks of conjugate heat transfer in narrow salt-filled pins,

 • Parametric mapping of heat transfer coefficients and effective conductivities across reactor
 operating envelopes, and

 • Validated reduced-order models integrated into SAM to support SSR design and licensing.

 The remainder of this report is organized as follows: Section II describes the CFD solver,
turbulence models, and numerical methods. Section III details the simulation case setups. Section IV
presents and discusses the results, and Section V concludes with key findings and recommendations.

2 Numerical Methods

The flow solver used in this study is NekRS, an open-source spectral element computational fluid
dynamics (CFD) code designed for high-fidelity simulations of incompressible and low-Mach number
flows. NekRS supports Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) modeling, Large Eddy Simulation
(LES), and Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) [11].

 With the increasing adoption of Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) in high-performance
computing (HPC) environments, NekRS has been developed as a GPU-ready alternative to Nek5000,
enabling improved efficiency on modern heterogeneous architectures. Like its predecessor, NekRS is
based on the spectral element method (SEM), which combines the accuracy of spectral methods
with the geometric flexibility of the finite element method. The computational domain is discretized
into E curvilinear hexahedral elements, where the solution is represented as a tensor product of
Nth-order Lagrange polynomials on Gauss-Lobatto-Legendre (GLL) nodal points, resulting in a
total grid point count of n = EN 3 . Time integration follows a semi-implicit scheme, with diffusion
terms handled using a k th -order backward difference formula (BDF k) and nonlinear advection
terms approximated via k th -order extrapolation (EXT k) [16].

 NekRS differs from Nek5000 primarily in its software architecture and hardware adaptability.
Nek5000, written in Fortran77/C with MPI-based parallelization, relies on optimized matrix-matrix
product routines for performance. In contrast, NekRS is implemented in C++ and employs the
Open Concurrent Compute Abstraction (OCCA) framework to support various GPU backends,
including CUDA, HIP, OpenCL, and DPC++. In this study, we use the incompressible formulation
of the Navier-Stokes equations, assuming Newtonian fluid behavior with constant thermophysical
properties. The corresponding continuity, momentum and energy equations are listed below:

2.1 LES Modeling in NekRS

For LES simulations, NekRS models the dissipation at sub-grid scales using a low-pass filter method,
essentially acting as a hyper-viscosity [17, 18]. At each timestep, a portion of energy is directly
removed from the highest frequency modes of each element. In one-dimension, a given signal is
represented by the summation of the contributions from an orthogonal set of N + 1 test functions,
where N is the order of the test function. The solution is interpolated onto a set of (N − 1) order
polynomials, then back again to N order. Combining the interpolated solution with the original
solution in a linear combination yields,

                                 Sf = α · SN −1 + (1 − α) · SN                                    (7)

where Sf , SN −1 and SN are the filtered, interpolated, and original solution respectively and α is
the filtering percentage. By interpolating the solution to a lower order basis function, the highest

frequency mode is removed. Linearly combining the interpolated solution with the original removes
α*100% of the highest frequency. The method is described in further detail by [19]. In the present
work the highest mode is filtered at 10%.

2.3 NekNek Modeling in NekRS

NekNek is a domain decomposition-based coupling framework in NekRS that enables multi-domain
simulations by allowing independent solvers to run on separate computational subdomains while
exchanging information across overlapping or interface regions [12]. This feature is particularly
useful for problems involving conjugate heat transfer (CHT), fluid-structure interaction (FSI), and
multi-scale flow simulations, where different subdomains may require distinct numerical treatments.
By employing a nonconforming Schwarz-based coupling approach, NekNek ensures stable and
accurate data transfer while maintaining the high parallel efficiency of NekRS. It preserves spectral
accuracy through high-order spatial interpolation on Gauss-Lobatto-Legendre (GLL) points and
maintains temporal accuracy using explicit extrapolation approximations at the interface. Previously
validated in canonical turbulent flow problems [12], NekNek has now been extended to advanced
reactor simulations, offering a robust tool for modeling coupled physics in complex geometries [15].
In this study, NekNek is employed to couple the CHT simulations of the fuel and coolant subdomains,
where natural circulation dominates the fuel region and forced convection governs the coolant. The

 5

two subdomains overlap in the cladding, where temperature information is exchanged, enabling an
accurate representation of the heat transfer between the SSR fuel and coolant.

3 Simulation Setups

3.1 Geometries

The Stable Salt Reactor (SSR) fuel pin consists of a vertical cladding tube that encapsulates the
fuel salt, which operates under natural circulation. The outer surface of the cladding is cooled by
an external coolant salt flowing upward through forced convection. Under operating conditions at
900°C, the fuel salt region is 360 cm in height and 19 mm in diameter, enclosed within a cladding
tube with an outer diameter of 20 mm and a wall thickness of 1 mm, as shown in Fig. 3.1. At the
top of the fuel salt region, a Xenon Solubility Control Device (XSCD) was initially proposed to
regulate the concentration of xenon gas within the fuel pin by modifying solubility conditions. The
XSCD was designed to be positioned below the top surface of the fuel salt. The xenon gas rises
to the surface and accumulates in the gas plenum above. The surrounding coolant salt provides
efficient heat removal through forced convection, ensuring stable operation. Preliminary analysis
shows that the void fraction due to Xe bubbles in the fuel salt is very small (¡0.01%). The reactivity
worth of the Xe bubbles is unlikely to pose a safety concern in the event of instantaneous and
complete bubble ejection from the fuel salt. Based on these findings, the Xenon Solubility Control
Device as proposed in the initial design is not needed (on the contrary, the XSCD could hinder the
bubble migration pathways to the gas plenum). Consequently, the XSCD was later removed from
the design.

3.2 Meshing

NekRS requires a pure hexahedral mesh for CFD simulations, which in this work was generated
using the open-source meshing software GMSH [25]. Figure 3.2 illustrates the fuel and coolant
subdomains. For each subdomain, both solid and fluid regions were created with conformal surface
meshes along the solid–fluid interfaces, enabling accurate conjugate heat transfer (CHT) calculations
once merged. The fuel subdomain includes the molten salt fuel region and cladding wall, while the
coolant subdomain consists of the external coolant and its corresponding cladding wall. Within the
cladding, overlapping solid regions intentionally provide the interface required for NekNek coupling,
allowing stable and efficient temperature exchange between subdomains during simulations.

 The final mesh contains 1,062,720 elements for the fuel subdomain and 725,760 elements for the
coolant subdomain. To improve accuracy, the polynomial order was varied across cases: a polynomial
order of 7 was used for the reference large eddy simulation (LES) at design operating conditions and
for all transient parametric studies, while the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) simulations
were performed at order 6. The structured blocking approach in GMSH ensured high-quality meshes
with good orthogonality and supported faster convergence in NekRS. Importantly, the same base
mesh was used across all LES, RANS, and transient simulations to maintain consistency and enable
direct comparison of results.

3.3 Material properties and boundary conditions

In the fluid region of the fuel subdomain, no-slip boundary conditions are applied on all wall surfaces,
except at the top surface of the molten fuel salt, which is modeled as a free surface. The outer
surface of the solid cladding region is assigned an interpolation boundary condition as required
by the NekNek coupling scheme. Similarly, the inner surface of the coolant subdomain’s solid
region uses interpolation to exchange temperature data across the overlapping cladding. At the
cladding–coolant interface, the coolant fluid region is subject to a no-slip boundary condition.

 For the coolant subdomain, the axial boundaries define the flow path: the bottom face serves
as the inlet, while the top face is treated as the outlet. A recycling velocity boundary condition is
imposed at the coolant inlet to provide a fully developed turbulent inflow profile.

 The fuel salt is approximated as a binary mixture of NaCl – UCl3 , while the coolant is represented
as a MgCl2 – NaCl mixture. The cladding material is NIMONIC® alloy PE16, a nickel–chromium–
iron-base alloy. The initial temperature of the fuel salt is set to 900 ◦ C, while both the inlet and
initial temperatures of the coolant are set to 550 ◦ C. Thermophysical properties of the fuel salt,
coolant, and cladding are treated as constant, with values evaluated at their respective reference
temperatures (900 ◦ C for fuel salt and cladding, 550 ◦ C for coolant). These assumptions simplify the
numerical treatment while maintaining accuracy for the temperature ranges of interest. Table 3.1
summarizes all property values used in the simulations.

 The flow in both the fuel and coolant regions is modeled under the incompressible assumption.
To account for buoyancy effects driven by temperature gradients, a Boussinesq approximation is
employed, in which density variations are neglected except in the buoyancy term of the momentum
equation. This allows efficient and accurate representation of natural convection in the molten salt
fuel while retaining computational stability for high-fidelity LES and RANS simulations.

3.4 Parametric studies

In this study, both steady-state and transient operating conditions are simulated to capture a broad
spectrum of SSR fuel pin behaviors. The primary objective is to generate high-fidelity data that can

inform and calibrate heat transfer models in the SAM system code, enabling fast-running transient
analyses for safety evaluation and licensing applications.

 To establish representative boundary conditions for the transient cases, a whole-plant system-
level model of the SSR was developed under a loss-of-flow accident (LOFA)—a canonical transient
scenario in reactor safety analysis [10]. The evolution of relative reactor power and coolant flow
rate during this event is shown in Fig. 3.4. Notably, the coolant flow rate decreases more rapidly
than the reactor power in the early stages of the transient, which has direct implications for thermal
safety margins. Specifically, this mismatch can lead to a transient temperature spike, potentially
exceeding the allowable limits for certain primary loop components. As the transient progresses,
both reactor power and coolant flow rate continue to decline until the primary loop transitions into
natural circulation and the mechanical pump becomes fully stalled. Over this continuous evolution,
six representative operating points were identified, corresponding to distinct combinations of fuel
power and coolant flow conditions. These points form the basis of the parametric study, which
examines how heat transfer performance varies across a range of operational states.

4 Results

Both the standalone subdomain simulations and the NekNek-coupled simulations of the fuel
and coolant subdomains were performed on the Frontier supercomputer at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory, using up to 344 AMD Mi250 GPUs. As of September 2025, Frontier ranks as the second
fastest supercomputer worldwide. Simulations were executed through consecutive job submissions,
with checkpoint files periodically saved to enable efficient restarts and continuation of long runs.

 Before performing the coupled NekNek simulations of the integrated fuel pin unit—including the
fuel, cladding, and coolant regions—standalone conjugate heat transfer (CHT) simulations were first
conducted for the fuel and coolant subdomains, each including a portion of the cladding. The study
began with a large eddy simulation (LES) of the reference case under normal operating conditions,
followed by simulations of the parametric study cases. In parallel, Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes
(RANS) simulations were carried out and compared against the LES results to evaluate the impact
of turbulence modeling on the predicted heat transfer behavior.

4.1 Mesh sensitivity study

Prior to the production runs, a mesh sensitivity study was carried out to verify that the chosen
computational grid and resolution were adequate for capturing the flow physics of interest. Thanks
to the spectral element formulation of the nekRS solver, simulation resolution can be easily increased
by raising the polynomial order. Given a total number of mesh elements E, the total grid point
count is given by n = EN 3 , where N represents the polynomial order. In the mesh sensitivity study,
polynomial orders ranging from N = 5 to N = 9 were employed.

 Standalone fuel and coolant subdomain simulations were advanced to a quasi steady state, and

4.3 Comparison between NekRS and SAM results

By leveraging high-fidelity NekRS results to inform closure models and material properties, the
SAM system model achieves near–CFD accuracy while retaining rapid execution for transient safety
studies. In this implementation, SAM represents the SSR fuel pin as a one-dimensional axial
channel discretized into 40 control volumes; the fuel salt region is then subdivided radially into
three concentric “effective conductivity” zones—core, middle, and near-wall—rather than a full
2-D mesh. Each zone is assigned a calibrated conductivity (44, 6, and 0.8 W/m·K, respectively) to
emulate the combined effects of internal natural convection and axial conduction resolved by NekRS.
These tuned parameters enable the SAM model to closely match NekRS predictions for mean fuel
temperatures, cladding inner and outer surface temperatures, and bulk coolant temperatures (Figs.
4.14–4.16), as well as the detailed radial fuel temperature profiles at nine axial stations (Fig. 4.17),
with deviations typically within a few kelvins. This agreement demonstrates that the effective
conductivity approach in a 1-D system model can faithfully capture complex conjugate heat transfer
phenomena, providing a robust surrogate for high-fidelity CFD in reactor design optimization and
licensed safety analysis.

closely follow the NekRS results, with maximum deviations under 20 K at mid-height where the
heat generation—and resulting thermal driving force for natural circulation—is greatest. The inner

postulated loss-of-flow accident (LOFA), where both reactor power and coolant mass flow rate

thermal conditions during this transient, six discrete time points were selected and modeled as
steady-state snapshots using coupled NekNek simulations with prescribed power levels and coolant
flow rates corresponding to each transient stage. The differential decay rates of power and coolant
flow create a complex thermal evolution that is critical for reactor safety assessment. During the
early stages of the transient, the coolant flow rate decreases more rapidly than the power generation,
leading to reduced heat removal capacity even as significant heat production continues. Each
parametric case was first advanced to quasi-steady state, followed by statistical averaging over 600
CTUs—equivalent to approximately three complete flow-through cycles—to obtain converged mean
temperature and flow field distributions.

initial temperature spike followed by gradual cooling—represents a critical consideration for SSR
safety margin evaluation, as peak thermal stresses and potential approach to operational limits

combining large-eddy simulations (LES), Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) models, and

SAM system-level analyses, we advanced the understanding of natural circulation and heat transfer
mechanisms in the SSR. LES and RANS approaches captured the coupled buoyancy-driven circulation
of molten fuel salt and forced convection in the external coolant, revealing parabolic temperature
profiles and robust heat removal under both steady and transient conditions. While RANS offered
a 1.28× computational speedup over LES, it systematically underpredicted coolant and cladding
temperatures (by 2–10 K) and overpredicted fuel temperatures (by up to 20 K), reflecting its
limitations in representing anisotropic turbulence and buoyancy effects.

 Parametric studies of loss-of-flow scenarios further quantified variations in heat transfer co-
efficients and identified non-monotonic coolant outlet temperature and heat flux trends as power
and flow declined. Six steady-state snapshots highlighted peak thermal stresses at intermediate
stages, emphasizing the need to capture both power reduction and coolant flow transients in safety
evaluations. The one-dimensional SAM model, employing an effective three-region conductivity
formulation, successfully reproduced both global temperature fields and radial gradients observed in
LES. This supports its use in rapid transient analyses while retaining essential physics fidelity.

 The results demonstrate the value of spectral element methods and NekNek domain coupling
for modeling complex thermal fluid problems in molten salt reactors, providing benchmark datasets
that strengthen reduced-order system codes like SAM for safety analysis and licensing. Collectively,
these findings establish a scalable multi-scale modeling framework for SSR thermal-hydraulics and
provide a foundation for future reactor design optimization and licensing support.
