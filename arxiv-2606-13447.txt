Quantum optical photoelectron interferometry
Jonathan Dubois, Viviane Cotte, Richard Taïeb, Camille Lévêque, Jérémie Caillat, Pranshu Dave, Pascal Salières, David Bresteau, Charles Bourassin-Bouchet, Anne L’Huillier, David Busto

Abstract
We present a general theoretical framework for multiphoton processes driven by quantum light fields, establishing a direct link between photon statistics and photoelectron observables. Our results show that the autocorrelation and cross-correlation functions, which quantify the underlying photon statistics, are directly mapped onto the resulting photoelectron spectra. Although our framework is broadly applicable, we demonstrate specifically in the example of reconstruction of attosecond beating by interference of two-photon transitions (RABBIT) the influence of the light statistical properties. In this approach, the amplitude, contrast and phase of the oscillations of the sideband signal as a function of pump-probe delay reveal the quantum nature of light. We analyze these observables across several quantum configurations, including correlated infrared and harmonic modes, as well as the uncorrelated case with non-classical harmonic statistics, thereby establishing a general framework for quantum-light RABBIT spectroscopy. We compare the analytical theory with numerical simulations for the case of classical harmonics and an infrared field in a squeezed coherent state, obtaining excellent agreement. Our results reveal how the interplay between classical and quantum correlations dictates the coherence of the photoemission process, providing a new window into the quantum-optical foundations of attosecond science.

II
Multiphoton processes induced by quantum light

II.1
Intensity spectrum from the density matrix

We describe the combined state of light and matter by a density matrix
\rho
.
The light field can take any state, from classical to fully quantum.
Photoemission measurements
Bourassin-Bouchet et al. 2020
;
Berkane et al. 2025
consist in measuring the energy of the photoelectron and can be formally described as a partial measurement in the photoelectron energy space. The spectrum
{\cal I}(E)
is obtained as

{\cal I}(E)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\bra{E}\operatorname{tr}_{\rm light}\left\{\rho(t)\right\}\ket{E},

(1)

where
\operatorname{tr}_{\rm light}
denotes the partial trace over the light’s degrees of freedom and
\ket{E}
is the continuum state of the photoelectron of energy
E
. We assume that photoemission occurs in a single channel (not specified in the notations for the sake of readability).
We consider an arbitrary initial state of light
\rho_{\rm light}(-\infty)
and an electron in the ground state of an atom
\ket{\rm g}
such that the initial state is separable:
\rho(-\infty)=\ket{\rm g}\bra{\rm g}\otimes\rho_{\rm light}(-\infty)
.
In general, the state is no longer separable under time evolution.
The density matrix of the total system evolves in time from its initial state to the detector following the von Neumann equation

\dot{\rho}=-\mathrm{i}[\mathbb{H}(t),\rho],

(2)

with the Hamilton operator
Gorlach et al. 2020

\mathbb{H}(t)=\mathbb{H}_{A}+\mathbb{H}_{\rm int}(t),

(3)

where
\mathbb{H}_{A}
is the field-free Hamilton operator and
\mathbb{H}_{\rm int}(t)
describes the interaction with the quantum field.
The latter is treated in the interaction picture, which explicitly introduces time dependence into the operator and effectively eliminates the free-field harmonic oscillator terms. This allows us to recover the standard semiclassical picture of a dipole interacting with a time-dependent classical field
Véniard et al. 1996
;
Maquet et al. 1998
;
Klünder et al. 2011
;
Dahlström et al. 2013
;
Busto et al. 2019
;
Berkane et al. 2024
.
Appendix
A
details this derivation from the standard form of the quantum optical Hamilton operator
Gorlach et al. 2020
;
Lange et al. 2024
.
In the dipole approximation and in length gauge,

\mathbb{H}_{\rm int}(t)=\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{F}(t),

(4)

where
\mathbf{D}
is the dipole moment and

\mathbf{F}(t)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}f_{\bf k}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\sigma}a_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}t}+\mathbf{e}_{\sigma}^{\star}a_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}t}\right),

(5)

the quantum optical electric field.
The annihilation (creation) operator of the mode labeled by the wave vector
\mathbf{k}
and the polarization
\mathbf{e}_{\sigma}
is
a_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}
(
a_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}
), such that
[a_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},a^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}]=\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
. The frequency
\omega_{\bf k}
obeys the dispersion relation
\omega_{\mathbf{k}}=c|\mathbf{k}|
. The vacuum electric field amplitude is

f_{\bf k}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\omega_{\bf k}}{2\epsilon_{0}V}},

(6)

where
\epsilon_{0}
is the permittivity and
V
the quantization volume.

II.2
Time-dependent perturbation theory for density matrices

We introduce the unitary transformation
\mathbb{U}_{A}(t)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}\mathbb{H}_{A}t)
.
The equation of motion (
2
) becomes

\dot{\rho}_{I}=-\mathrm{i}[\mathbb{H}_{I}(t),\rho_{I}],

(7)

where
\rho_{I}(t)=\mathbb{U}_{A}^{\dagger}(t)\>\rho(t)\>\mathbb{U}_{A}(t)
and
\mathbb{H}_{I}(t)=\mathbb{U}_{A}^{\dagger}(t)\mathbb{H}_{\rm int}(t)\mathbb{U}_{A}(t)
.
The TDPT consists of expanding the density matrix as a series of terms corresponding to different perturbation orders,

\rho_{I}(t)=\sum_{n}\rho_{I}^{(n)}(t).

(8)

To maintain consistency with the established
semiclassical
framework
Véniard et al. 1996
;
Maquet et al. 1998
;
Klünder et al. 2011
;
Dahlström et al. 2013
;
Busto et al. 2019
;
Berkane et al. 2024
, we evaluate the density-matrix coefficients through a time-dependent perturbative expansion of the evolution operator
\mathbb{U}(t,t^{\prime})
.
This approach, detailed in
Messiah 1962
, is summarized here for self-consistency.
The time evolution of
\mathbb{U}(t,t^{\prime})
is described by

\dot{\mathbb{U}}(t,t^{\prime})=-\mathrm{i}\mathbb{H}_{I}(t)\mathbb{U}(t,t^{\prime}),

(9)

with the condition
\mathbb{U}(t,t)=\mathbbm{1}
. The density matrix evolves as
\rho_{I}(t)=\mathbb{U}(t,t^{\prime})\rho_{I}(t^{\prime})\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}(t,t^{\prime})
.
Introducing the perturbation expansion

\mathbb{U}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{n}\mathbb{U}^{(n)}(t,t^{\prime}),

(10)

and substituting into (
9
) leads to the recurrence relation

\mathbb{U}^{(n+1)}(t,t^{\prime})=-\mathrm{i}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathbb{H}_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})\mathbb{U}^{(n)}(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})\;\mathrm{d}t^{\prime\prime},

(11)

with the zeroth-order term
\mathbb{U}^{(0)}(t,t^{\prime})=\mathbbm{1}
.
The
n
th order coefficient of the density matrix in (
8
) can be expanded as

\rho_{I}^{(n)}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\mathbb{U}^{(m)}(t,t^{\prime})\rho_{I}(t^{\prime})\left[\mathbb{U}^{(n-m)}(t,t^{\prime})\right]^{\dagger}.

(12)

The intensity spectrum (
1
) can then be written as the sum of contributions of order
n

{\cal I}(E)=\sum_{n}{\cal I}^{(n)}(E),

(13)

where each term is defined as

{\cal I}^{(n)}(E)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\bra{E}\operatorname{tr}_{\rm light}\left\{\rho^{(n)}_{I}(t)\right\}\ket{E}.

(14)

Note that Eq. (
12
) together with (
11
) are equivalent–after integrating by parts and rearranging the terms–to the usual recurrence relation

\dot{\rho}_{I}^{(n+1)}(t)=-\mathrm{i}[\mathbb{H}_{I}(t),\rho_{I}^{(n)}(t)],

(15)

expressed in TDPT
Scully and Zubairy 1997
.
The key advantage of Eq. (
12
) is that the time-evolution operators
\mathbb{U}^{(n)}
are identical in both the wave function and density-matrix formalisms.
This symmetry allows us to directly export established concepts from
semiclassical
attosecond science, such as multiphoton transition amplitudes
Véniard et al. 1996
;
Maquet et al. 1998
, into the quantum-optical regime, as shown in the following section for the case of one- and two-photon processes.

In the case of pure states, the perturbative structure of the density matrix has a natural interpretation. The density matrix can be written as
\rho_{I}(t)=\ket{\psi_{I}(t)}\bra{\psi_{I}(t)}
where
\ket{\psi_{I}(t)}=\sum_{n}\ket{\psi_{I}^{(n)}(t)}
and
\ket{\psi_{I}^{(n)}(t)}=\mathbb{U}^{(n)}(t,t^{\prime})\ket{\psi_{I}(t^{\prime})}
.
The element of the
n
th-order density matrix
\rho_{I}^{(n)}
contains contributions from all pairs
(\ket{\psi_{I}^{(m)}(t)},\bra{\psi_{I}^{(n-m)}(t)})
. In particular, for even
n
, the diagonal elements (populations) are determined by
\braket{\psi_{I}^{(n/2)}(t)|\psi_{I}^{(n/2)}(t)}
, while the off-diagonal elements (coherences) depend on the product of coefficients describing different perturbative orders, i.e.
m
-photon and
(n-m)
-photon processes. The density-matrix formalism thus accounts for the coherence between different
n
-photon processes
Laurell et al. 2022
;
Laurell et al. 2025
, that would be less visible in a purely wave-function description.

The
n
th-order evolution operator
\mathbb{U}^{(n)}
, and consequently the
n
th-order density matrix
\rho_{I}^{(n)}
, involve a product of
n
annihilation and creation operators (
a_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}
and
a_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}
).
This represents a net exchange of
n
photons with the field consistent with the standard interpretations of the TDPT.
As a primary result of this work, we have shown that
{\cal I}^{(n)}
is expressed as a linear combination of various
n
th moments of the distribution, or equivalently,
n
-point correlation functions of the electromagnetic field. Consequently, the photoelectron intensity spectrum produced by multiphoton processes encodes the underlying quantum nature and photon statistics of the field.
Using interferometric photoemission measurements where the temporal delay between different field modes is varied, one can experimentally extract the amplitude and phase of these high-order
n
-point correlation functions. We demonstrate this capability in the following sections by applying our formalism to the RABBIT technique.

Interferometric photoemission: RABBIT scheme

The RABBIT technique involves generating a photoelectron wavepacket from a target atom or molecule through the simultaneous interaction with an attosecond pulse train and an infrared laser field. As illustrated in Fig.
2
, the final energy state of the photoelectron in a sideband can be reached via two distinct quantum pathways that interfere at the detector. Specifically, the sideband signal is formed by the interference between the absorption of a harmonic photon (frequency
\omega_{e}
) and the stimulated emission of an infrared photon (
\omega_{0}
), and the absorption of a photon from the preceding harmonic (
\omega_{a}
) and the absorption of an infrared photon

\omega_{e}-\omega_{a}=2\omega_{0}.

(16)

The resulting intensity spectrum consists of main bands, shown in purple and blue in Fig.
2
b, and the sideband shown in red. By introducing a temporal delay
\tau
between the harmonic and infrared pulses, the relative phase between these two pathways is modulated, resulting in a periodic oscillation of the sideband intensity.
The phase of these oscillations encodes critical information, including temporal information on the field
Véniard et al. 1996
;
Paul et al. 2001
, as well as intrinsic atomic and molecular quantities, such as the Wigner time delay of the photoelectron
Klünder et al. 2011
;
Caillat et al. 2011
;
Beaulieu et al. 2017
;
Autuori et al. 2022
.
Ultimately, the success of RABBIT as an interferometric technique is fundamentally based on the coherence of the photoelectron wavepacket and, crucially, on the coherence of the driving fields. Without this ingredient, the interference contrast would vanish and the sideband oscillations would disappear.

In the following we concentrate on the three modes outlined in color in Fig.
2
, leading to the photoelectron spectra in blue, purple and red.
This choice is made without loss of generality as the framework can be straightforwardly extended to an arbitrary number of harmonic orders.
Accordingly, the electric field in Eq. (
5
) is composed of these three modes. Each mode
\beta=\{0,a,e\}
is characterized by its polarization
\mathbf{e}_{\beta}
, vacuum electric field amplitude
f_{\beta}
and a pair of annihilation and creation operators
(a_{\beta},a_{\beta}^{\dagger})
.
The electric field (
5
) reads explicitly as a function of the delay
\tau
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(t;\tau)=\sum_{\beta=a,e}f_{\beta}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\beta}a_{\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\beta}t}+\mathbf{e}_{\beta}^{\star}a_{\beta}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\beta}t}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+f_{0}\left[\mathbf{e}_{0}a_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}(t-\tau)}+\mathbf{e}_{0}^{\star}a_{0}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}(t-\tau)}\right].

(17)

In this regime, the intensity spectrum (
13
) has two main contributions such that

{\cal I}(E)\approx{\cal I}^{(2)}(E)+{\cal I}^{(4)}(E).

(18)

It depends on the photon statistics of the infrared and harmonic light pulses through
\rho_{\rm light}(-\infty)
.
Restricting our analysis to one- and two-photon transitions, the term
n=2
describes the main bands and the term
n=4
describes the sideband.
Thereafter, we analyze
{\cal I}^{(2)}(E)
and
{\cal I}^{(4)}(E)
in terms of the expectation values over the light’s degrees of freedom. We use as an expression of the expectation value of an operator
O

\braket{O}=\operatorname{tr}_{\rm light}\left\{\rho_{\rm light}(-\infty)O\right\}.

(19)

III.1
Intensity spectrum

III.1.1
Main bands

The intensity spectrum of the main bands is obtained from
\rho^{(2)}(t)
in Eq. (
14
) by retaining only one-photon processes.
Furthermore, only
\mathbb{U}^{(1)}
contributes to
\bra{E}\rho^{(2)}\ket{E}
, since
\bra{E}\mathbb{U}^{(0)}\ket{\rm g}=\braket{E|\rm g}
is equal to zero. The first-order transition amplitude thus reduces to

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\to\infty\\
t^{\prime}\to-\infty\end{subarray}}\bra{E}\mathbb{U}^{(1)}(t,t^{\prime})\ket{\rm g}=M_{e}^{(1)}(E)\;f_{e}\>a_{e}+M_{a}^{(1)}(E)\;f_{a}\>a_{a}.

(20)

Note that here we keep only the non-vanishing terms corresponding to the energy of the harmonic bands.
Here
M_{\beta}^{(1)}(E)
is the
semiclassical
one-photon transition matrix element
Véniard et al. 1996
;
Maquet et al. 1998
, explicitly given in Appendix
B.1
.
The intensity spectrum reads

{\cal I}^{(2)}(E)=\sum_{\beta=a,e}|M_{\beta}^{(1)}(E)|^{2}\>f_{\beta}^{2}\braket{a_{\beta}^{\dagger}a_{\beta}}.

(21)

As expected, the photoelectron signal due to absorption of harmonic mode
\beta
is proportional to the mean number of photons
n_{\beta}=\braket{a_{\beta}^{\dagger}a_{\beta}}
. In the classical limit, it directly maps to the intensity of the electric field. Because both frameworks describe the main bands via this first-order correlation, their predicted intensities are identical, rendering them indistinguishable regardless of whether a classical or quantum-optical treatment is employed.

III.1.2
Sidebands: RABBIT oscillations

The intensity of the sidebands is obtained by considering two-photon processes described by the second-order transition amplitude

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\to\infty\\
t^{\prime}\to-\infty\end{subarray}}\bra{E}\mathbb{U}^{(2)}(t,t^{\prime})\ket{\rm g}=M_{e}^{(2)}(E)\;f_{e}f_{0}\>a_{e}a_{0}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}\tau}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+M_{a}^{(2)}(E)\;f_{a}f_{0}\>a_{a}a_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}\tau},

(22)

with
M^{(2)}_{\beta}(E)
the
semiclassical
two-photon transition matrix element associated with the paths
a
and
e

Véniard et al. 1996
;
Maquet et al. 1998
;
Klünder et al. 2011
;
Dahlström et al. 2013
;
Busto et al. 2019
;
Berkane et al. 2024
, explicitly given in Appendix
B.1
.
We find that the intensity spectrum (
14
) takes a similar form as in the case of classical light

{\cal I}^{(4)}(E;\tau)=A(E)\Big\{1+C(E)\cos\left[2\omega_{0}\tau+\theta(E)\right]\Big\},

(23)

with the so-called RABBIT amplitude
A
, contrast
C
and phase
\theta
.
However, here the coefficients do depend on the quantum characteristics of the light, as the amplitude reads

\displaystyle A=|M_{e}^{(2)}(E)|^{2}\>f_{e}^{2}f_{0}^{2}\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{0}a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{e}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+|M_{a}^{(2)}(E)|^{2}\>f_{a}^{2}f_{0}^{2}\braket{a_{a}^{\dagger}a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}a_{a}},

(24a)

the contrast is given by

C=\dfrac{2\>\big|\xi^{(2)}(E)\big|\times\big|\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}\>a_{0}^{2}}\big|}{\big|\xi^{(2)}(E)\big|^{2}\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{0}a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{e}}+\braket{a_{a}^{\dagger}a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}a_{a}}},

(24b)

and the phase expressed as

\theta=-\arg\left[\xi^{(2)}(E)\right]+\arg\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}\>a_{0}^{2}},

(24c)

where the complex-valued coefficient

\xi^{(2)}(E)=\dfrac{f_{e}\>M_{e}^{(2)}(E)}{f_{a}\>M_{a}^{(2)}(E)},

(24d)

accounts for the ratio of the two-photon transition matrix elements.
Equation (
24c
) allows us to recover the atomic phase
\arg\xi^{(2)}(E)
that characterizes the intrinsic response of the atomic target. Both its modulus and phase can be determined numerically through standard second-order perturbation theory
Paul et al. 2001
;
Muller 2002
.
Beyond the atomic response, the RABBIT signal encapsulates the joint photon statistics of the infrared light and the generated harmonics.
Specifically, its structure and visibility are determined by the following fourth-order correlation functions:
\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{0}a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{e}}
,
\braket{a_{a}^{\dagger}a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}a_{a}}
, and
\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}a_{0}^{2}}
.
Notably, the numerator of the signal contrast is strictly bounded by the terms in the denominator via the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality such that

|\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}a_{0}^{2}}|^{2}\leq\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{0}a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{e}}\braket{a_{a}^{\dagger}a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}a_{a}}.

(25)

This inequality implies that the maximum possible contrast is reached only if the equality holds, as shown in Appendix
B.2
. Consequently, depending on the specific quantum correlations of the light fields, the contrast can significantly diminish or even vanish, even if the individual modes are non-zero.
While traditional RABBIT theory treats the laser and harmonic fields as classical waves with a well-defined phase, our results demonstrate that such hypothesis is not necessary for observing attosecond interference.

III.2
Coherence and the nature of RABBIT

III.2.1
The quantum condition for RABBIT interference

The most general condition for observing a RABBIT interferometric signal, defined by the oscillation of the photoelectron yield as a function of delay
\tau
[see Eqs. (
24
)], is the non-vanishing of the four-point correlation function

\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}\>a_{0}^{2}}\neq 0.

(26)

This
quantum condition
constitutes the fundamental physical requirement for attosecond beating, and is the central result of our framework.
Under this condition, the phase that can be extracted from the RABBIT signal
\theta=\Delta\phi_{A}+\Delta\phi
[Eq. (
23
)] contains the atomic contribution
\Delta\phi_{A}=-\arg\xi^{(2)}
and a field phase given by the argument of the full correlator

\Delta\phi=\arg\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}a_{0}^{2}},

(27)

which is the most general expression for the field phase extracted by RABBIT.

Condition (
26
) and Eq. (
27
) are fully general: they apply regardless of the quantum state of the incoming field and holds for any field statistics, classical or non-classical. In particular, experimental imperfections such as shot-to-shot phase fluctuations and timing jitter are naturally incorporated by treating
\rho_{\rm light}(-\infty)
as a statistical mixture, reproducing the well-known degradation of fringe contrast encoded in Eqs. (
24b
) and (
24c
).
Beyond these technical imperfections, the examples explored in Section
IV
illustrate the full generality of this condition: the RABBIT signal may vanish even when
\braket{a_{\beta}}\neq 0
, if the modes are specifically anti-correlated, while conversely a robust signal can persist even when all mean fields vanish (
\braket{a_{\beta}}=0
), as in a BSV source. This establishes that RABBIT fundamentally probes
multi-mode correlations
, not first-order coherences.

III.2.2
The classical limit as a special case

Our quantum formalism recovers the standard semiclassical RABBIT results
Véniard et al. 1996
;
Maquet et al. 1998
;
Klünder et al. 2011
;
Dahlström et al. 2013
;
Busto et al. 2019
;
Berkane et al. 2024
in the appropriate limit. Specifically, when the incoming electromagnetic field is in a product of coherent states

\ket{\psi_{\rm light}(-\infty)}=\ket{\alpha_{0}}_{0}\otimes\ket{\alpha_{e}}_{e}\otimes\ket{\alpha_{a}}_{a},

(28)

where
\ket{\alpha_{\beta}}_{\beta}
denotes the coherent state of mode
\beta
with well-defined amplitude and carrier-envelope phase, Eqs. (
24
) reduce to the standard semiclassical expressions in the large-photon-number limit (
|\alpha_{\beta}|^{2}\gg 1
). We stress that these two conditions – a product of coherent states
and
a large photon number – must hold simultaneously to recover the semiclassical limit.

In this limit, the field reduces to its
coherent part

\mathbf{F}_{\rm coh}(t;\tau)=\braket{\mathbf{F}(t;\tau)},

(29)

and all higher-order correlations are fully determined by the first-order expectation
\braket{a_{\beta}}
of each mode. The quantum condition (
26
) then factorizes as
\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}}\braket{a_{a}}\braket{a_{0}}^{2}
, reducing to the classical condition

\braket{a_{\beta}}\neq 0\quad(\beta=\{0,a,e\}),

(30)

and Eq. (
27
) reduces to the conventional relative harmonic phase
Paul et al. 2001

\Delta\phi=\arg\braket{a_{a}}-\arg\braket{a_{e}}+2\arg\braket{a_{0}}.

(31)

It is important to note, however, that this classical picture is based on an assumption that is rarely made explicit: even within a
single
realization, each mode is assumed to carry a well-defined amplitude and phase [Eq. (
28
)], so that all cross-correlations factorize. It concerns the intrinsic quantum nature of the field within each shot. It is precisely this assumption that the quantum treatment calls into question
Zurek 2003
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
.

III.2.3
Attosecond timing

The distinction between the general phase (
27
) actually extracted by RABBIT and its classical counterpart (
31
) has direct consequences for the physical interpretation of attosecond timing measurements, such as the landmark measurement of the 250 as extreme-ultraviolet pulse duration
Paul et al. 2001
. The timing of an attosecond pulse is encoded in the phase relations across the full harmonic comb – it is the coherent superposition of many frequency components that produces a localized pulse in time.
To focus on this timing specifically, we consider the case where the infrared field
\mathbf{F}_{0}(t;\tau)
and the total harmonic field
\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}(t)
, defined by
\mathbf{F}(t;\tau)=\mathbf{F}_{0}(t;\tau)+\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}(t)
, are initially uncorrelated. Under this assumption, the correlator for two successive harmonics in Eq. (
27
) reduces to

\Delta\phi=\arg\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}}+\arg\braket{a_{0}^{2}},

(32)

where
\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}}
is one inter-mode correlator between two successive harmonics of the full two-point correlation function
\braket{\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(+)}(t)\cdot\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(-)}(t^{\prime})}
, with
\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(+)}(t)
[resp.
\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(-)}(t)
] denoting the part of
\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}(t)
containing only annihilation (resp. creation) operators
Glauber 1963
.
We stress that a single RABBIT measurement, probing only one pair of successive harmonics, gives access to only two-successive harmonic correlators
\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}}
, and therefore provides partial information on
\braket{\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(+)}(t)}\cdot\braket{\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(-)}(t^{\prime})}
, whose full reconstruction requires all inter-mode correlators across the harmonic spectrum, i.e. the complete quantum coherency matrix
Fabre and Treps 2020
.
Only in the classical limit, where
\braket{\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(+)}(t)\cdot\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(-)}(t^{\prime})}
factorizes as
\braket{\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(+)}(t)}\cdot\braket{\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(-)}(t^{\prime})}
and the measured phase reduces to Eq. (
31
), does each correlator
\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}}
reduce to
\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}}\braket{a_{a}}
, and does RABBIT give direct access to the timing of the attosecond pulse itself.
However, this interpretation relies on the assumption that the harmonic field is classical, an assumption that is increasingly challenged by recent experimental and theoretical evidence on the quantum nature of high-order harmonic generation
Gorlach et al. 2020
;
Stammer et al. 2024
;
Gombkötő et al. 2021
;
Stammer et al. 2023
;
Gonoskov et al. 2024
;
Rivera-Dean et al. 2024
;
Theidel et al. 2024
;
Theidel et al. 2025
. In the general quantum case, it is the temporal structure of
\braket{\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(+)}(t)\cdot\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}^{(-)}(t^{\prime})}
that is probed – a subtle but fundamentally different quantity, which can for instance remain sharply localized in time even when
\braket{\mathbf{F}_{\rm xuv}(t)}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and whose beating reflects the interference of two-photon transition amplitudes mediated by quantum field fluctuations rather than classical waves.

Crucially, this reinterpretation does not invalidate existing experimental protocols, whether for measuring atomic and molecular photoemission delays
Beaulieu et al. 2017
;
Isinger et al. 2017
or for characterizing light pulses
Paul et al. 2001
. In the case of photoemission delay measurements, the RABBIT phase is calibrated against a known reference species, so that the field phase
\arg\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}a_{0}^{2}}
cancels in the differential measurement, leaving only the difference of atomic phases – a procedure that is insensitive to the quantum state of the field by construction.
In the case of the characterization of light pulses, a theoretically computed atomic phase is subtracted from the RABBIT phase, directly yielding
\arg\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}a_{0}^{2}}
.
What our framework does call into question is not the measurement itself, but its physical interpretation, as discussed above.
Our framework thus complements rather than challenges existing attosecond measurement protocols, and extends their interpretation to non-classical field states, as explored in the following section.

IV
RABBIT signal for different incoming light field states

In this section, we apply the previously developed quantum framework to several characteristic states of light. By evaluating the RABBIT signal [Eq. (
24
)] for different statistical field distributions, we illustrate how specific field correlations, ranging from purely classical mixtures to highly non-classical states, dictate the resulting interferometric amplitude, contrast, and phase. To facilitate this analysis, we introduce a set of normalized correlation functions that serve as a quantitative bridge between the field’s quantum statistics and the observed photoelectron beating.

IV.1
Correlated infrared and harmonic fields

We first note that the expressions for the amplitude [see Eq. (
24a
)] and contrast [see Eq. (
24b
)] involve the cross-correlation function

g^{(2)}_{ij}=\dfrac{\braket{a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}a_{i}}}{\braket{a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}}\braket{a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}}},

(33)

between modes
i
and
j
.
This quantity is a standard metric in quantum optics for characterizing the statistical dependence and coherence properties of electromagnetic fields
Glauber 1963
.
Therefore, the signal amplitude, including the denominator of the contrast and the bound defined in Eq. (
25
), can be directly related, not only to the number of photons of both infrared
n_{0}=\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}
and harmonics
n_{\beta}
modes, but also to the cross-correlated terms
g^{(2)}_{\beta 0}
, for
\beta=\{a,e\}
.
The case where
g^{(2)}_{\beta 0}=1
leads to uncorrelated infrared and harmonic modes and is detailed in the next section, while here we focus on the other cases involving non-trivial correlations.

IV.1.1
Inter-mode bunching

The condition
g^{(2)}_{\beta 0}>1
denotes “inter-mode bunching”, a regime where the infrared and harmonic modes exhibit strong mutual correlations.
Classically, this occurs when the intensity of the harmonic
\beta
tracks the fluctuations of the infrared driving field, a scenario intrinsic to high-order harmonic generation, where higher infrared intensities yield a greater probability of harmonic emission in an optimal regime
Wahlström et al. 1993
;
Weissenbilder et al. 2022
. In the quantum regime, however, these correlations can reach significantly higher values, potentially amplifying the signal far beyond the levels expected for uncorrelated light. A quintessential example of this state is the two-mode squeezed vacuum
Ruo-Berchera et al. 2015
. Similar correlations may be inherently relevant to high-order harmonic generation since the process involves the simultaneous annihilation of infrared photons to produce a single high-energy harmonic photon. Therefore, it could establish a robust statistical link between the driving field and the resulting radiation.

IV.1.2
Inter-mode anti-bunching

The condition
g^{(2)}_{\beta 0}<1
denotes “inter-mode anti-bunching”, a purely quantum correlation regime. In this case, the joint probability of detecting a photon in the harmonic mode and another in the infrared mode simultaneously is reduced compared to the statistically independent case.
In the limiting scenario where
g^{(2)}_{\beta 0}=0
for
\beta=\{a,e\}
, the harmonic and infrared modes become perfectly anti-correlated.
This implies that the detection of a photon in one mode precludes the simultaneous detection of a photon in the other, a feature that has no classical counterpart.
Interestingly, the RABBIT amplitude [Eq. (
24a
)] does not vanish due to vacuum-mediated transitions but reduces to

A=|M_{e}^{2}(E)|^{2}f_{e}^{2}f_{0}^{2}\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{e}},

(34)

which depend only on the harmonic
e
photon number and is completely independent of the infrared photon number
n_{0}
.
The asymmetry between the quantum paths involving absorption (a) and stimulated emission (e) is due to the fact that stimulated emission can be induced by vacuum fluctuations, while absorption cannot.
In this regime, the influence of the infrared field is restricted solely to the
semiclassical
two-photon transition matrix elements, effectively rendering the infrared field a “spectator” to the interferometric process.
Remarkably, this also places no constraint on the interferometric contrast
C
, which may vanish (
C=0
) or remain finite (
C\neq 0
) depending on the relative phase and amplitude of the contributing pathways.

IV.1.3
Characterization of non-classicality

Turning to the formal characterization of non-classicality, a primary indicator of quantum correlations between the infrared and harmonic modes is the violation of the classical inequality
Glauber 1963

g^{(2)}_{\beta 0}\leq[g^{(2)}_{00}g^{(2)}_{\beta\beta}]^{1/2}
. However, it should be noted that the converse is not necessarily true; the presence of quantum states or entanglement does not strictly guaranty a violation of this bound.
As shown in the following equation, a violation becomes physically accessible only in the regime of low mean photon numbers,
n_{\beta}
or
n_{0}
, since the cross-correlation is bounded by the quantum Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

g^{(2)}_{\beta 0}\leq\left(g^{(2)}_{\beta\beta}+\dfrac{1}{\braket{a_{\beta}^{\dagger}a_{\beta}}}\right)^{1/2}\left(g^{(2)}_{00}+\dfrac{1}{\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}}\right)^{1/2},

(35)

resulting from commutation relations applied to (
33
).
The terms
1/\braket{a_{\beta}^{\dagger}a_{\beta}}
and
1/\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}
represent the quantum correction that allows the cross-correlation to exceed the classical limits. This provides a strong motivation for experimental investigations of RABBIT in low-photon regimes at high repetition rates, as achievable with cavity-enhanced high-order harmonic generation
Heinrich et al. 2021
, where non-classical signatures could be most clearly resolved.

IV.2
Uncorrelated infrared and harmonic fields

We now consider the case where the infrared field state
\rho_{0}(-\infty)
and the harmonic field state
\rho_{ea}(-\infty)
including two frequency modes,
\omega_{e}
and
\omega_{a}
, are initially separated in the overall field state

\rho_{\rm light}(-\infty)=\rho_{0}(-\infty)\otimes\rho_{ea}(-\infty).

(36)

In this case, the harmonic and infrared lights are initially uncorrelated, which formally translates into

\displaystyle\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}\>a_{0}^{2}}=\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}}\braket{a_{0}^{2}},

(37a)

\displaystyle g^{(2)}_{\beta 0}=1,

(37b)

for
\beta=\{a,e\}
. In addition, we consider that the number of infrared photons is large, such that
\braket{a_{0}a_{0}^{\dagger}}\approx\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}
which is in general the case in RABBIT experiments.
Note that a large number of photons does not necessarily imply a classical limit since both the auto-correlations in
\rho_{0}
or the cross-correlations in
\rho_{ea}
can be non-classical.

IV.2.1
Reduced expressions

The amplitude (
24a
), contrast (
24b
) and phase (
24c
) thus reduce to

\displaystyle A\approx f_{0}^{2}\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left\{|M_{e}^{(2)}(E)|^{2}\>f_{e}^{2}\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{e}}+|M_{a}^{(2)}(E)|^{2}\>f_{a}^{2}\braket{a_{a}^{\dagger}a_{a}}\right\},

(38a)

C\approx\dfrac{2\big|\xi^{(2)}(E)\big|\mu}{\left\{\big|\xi^{(2)}(E)\big|\mu\right\}^{2}+1}\times\dfrac{\big|\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}}\big|}{\sqrt{\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{e}}\braket{a_{a}^{\dagger}a_{a}}}}\times\dfrac{|\braket{a_{0}^{2}}|}{\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}},

(38b)

and

\theta=-\arg\left[\xi^{(2)}(E)\right]+\arg\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}}+\arg\braket{a_{0}^{2}}.

(38c)

We have introduced in Eq. (
38b
) the ratio between the square root of the number of photons of the two harmonics

\mu=\sqrt{\dfrac{\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{e}}}{\braket{a_{a}^{\dagger}a_{a}}}},

(39)

that can be fully determined from the intensity of the main bands
{\cal I}^{(2)}(E)
[see Eq. (
21
)].
As a consequence of separability (
36
), we observe that the contrast in Eq. (
38b
) and the phase in Eq. (
38c
) separate into three contributions.
The last two terms depend only on the properties of light (fundamental and harmonics) and their correlations.

The contrast and phase of the RABBIT signal encode the modulus and phase of the normalized first-order coherence function
Mandel and Wolf 1995

g^{(1)}_{ea}=\dfrac{\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}}}{\sqrt{\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{e}}\braket{a_{a}^{\dagger}a_{a}}}},

(40)

between the two harmonic modes.
The modulus of
g^{(1)}_{ea}
ranges from 0 to 1, where a value
|g^{(1)}_{ea}|=0
indicates a complete lack of coherence between the two harmonic modes
e
and
a
, and a value
|g^{(1)}_{ea}|=1
represents perfect coherence between them.
Hence, the contrast can vanish if there is no coherence between the modes
a
and
e
, which is equivalent to saying that there is no phase relation between these modes.

IV.2.2
Examples of harmonic fields

In the following examples, we specify the initial state of the harmonic field before the light-matter interaction. Whenever this state is pure, the corresponding density matrix is
\rho_{ea}(-\infty)=\ket{\psi_{ea}(-\infty)}\bra{\psi_{ea}(-\infty)}
.

Macroscopic Bell-like states:

Several physical scenarios result in the disappearance of first-order coherence (
g^{(1)}_{ea}=0
), thus suppressing the RABBIT beating.
For example, it can emerge from non-local quantum correlations, even when individual modes appear perfectly coherent. A compelling example is the entangled, Bell-like state

\ket{\psi_{ea}(-\infty)}=\dfrac{1}{\cal N}\big(\ket{\alpha_{e}}_{e}\otimes\ket{0}_{a}+\ket{0}_{e}\otimes\ket{\alpha_{a}}_{a}\big),

(41)

where
\cal N
is a normalization factor.
Here, each isolated mode possesses a well-defined coherent phase, as evidenced by the non-vanishing local expectation values
\braket{a_{e}}=\alpha_{e}/{\cal N}
and
\braket{a_{a}}=\alpha_{a}/{\cal N}
, which map to a well-defined phase difference (
31
).
Yet, because the state is a coherent superposition of mutually exclusive macro-realizations, photons exist either entirely in mode
e
or entirely in mode
a
, i.e. the two harmonics are anti-correlated. Consequently, the coherence function between the modes

g^{(1)}_{ea}=\dfrac{\alpha_{e}^{\star}\alpha_{a}}{|\alpha_{e}^{\star}\alpha_{a}|}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(|\alpha_{e}|^{2}+|\alpha_{a}|^{2}\right)\right],

(42)

exhibits a striking dependence on photon number: in the few-photon limit (
|\alpha_{e}|^{2}
and
|\alpha_{a}|^{2}\ll 1
),
|g^{(1)}_{ea}|\to 1
, corresponding to maximal RABBIT contrast, while it drops to zero as the number of photons increases (
|\alpha_{e}|^{2}
or
|\alpha_{a}|^{2}\gg 1
) completely suppressing the interference signal. Unlike a standard product of coherent states [see Eq. (
28
)], which lacks inter-mode entanglement, this Bell-like state represents a purely quantum mechanism for contrast loss.

Two photons in one frequency mode:

Similar suppression can occur through classical phase-averaging from independent, phase-randomized coherent sources. To isolate the microscopic roles of phase uncertainty and particle-number constraints in eliminating this interference, we consider a simplified model within a minimal basis of Fock-state
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
.
We first examine a state containing exactly one photon in each mode

\ket{\psi_{ea}(-\infty)}=\ket{1}_{e}\otimes\ket{1}_{a}.

(43)

For this state, the first-order coherence term vanishes,
\braket{a_{e}^{\dagger}a_{a}}=0
, leading to
g_{ea}^{(1)}=0
and a resulting RABBIT contrast of zero. This occurs because Fock states are eigenstates of the number operator, thus possessing a definite particle count but containing no information regarding the relative phase between the two modes. Mathematically, the annihilation of a photon in mode
a
and the creation of a photon in mode
e
, result in a state (or similar) which is orthogonal to the initial state, thereby “erasing” any phase relationship that would otherwise drive the interferometric beating.
Note that Eq. (
43
) is fundamentally distinct from the product state in Eq. (
28
). Although coherent states are superpositions of Fock states, they lack the inter-mode correlations, or entanglement, characterizing Eq. (
43
).

Single photon with classical correlations (incoherent):

Similarly, the interference contrast vanishes for a statistical mixture where a single photon is randomly distributed between the two modes

\rho_{ea}(-\infty)=\dfrac{1}{2}\big(\ket{1}_{e}\bra{1}_{e}\otimes\ket{0}_{a}\bra{0}_{a}+\ket{0}_{e}\bra{0}_{e}\otimes\ket{1}_{a}\bra{1}_{a}\big).

(44)

In this case, the lack of off-diagonal terms in the density matrix, representing a complete absence of coherence between modes, prevents the formation of the sideband interference. More generally,
g_{ea}^{(1)}=0
whenever the density matrix is diagonal in the Fock state basis, a condition that means that the relative phase between the pathways is undefined.
This is also the case if, more generally, the occupied state is a coherent state
\ket{\alpha}
instead of
\ket{1}
.

Single photon with quantum correlations (entanglement):

Conversely, complex quantum correlations can produce a robust RABBIT signal even in non-trivial configurations. Consider the path-entangled Fock state

\ket{\psi_{ea}(-\infty)}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\ket{1}_{e}\otimes\ket{0}_{a}+\ket{0}_{e}\otimes\ket{1}_{a}\big).

(45)

In this configuration, the individual field expectations vanish (
\braket{a_{e}}=0
and
\braket{a_{a}}=0
), reflecting the ill-defined phase inherent to Fock states. Nevertheless, the state remains perfectly coherent (
g^{(1)}_{ea}=1
), as a single photon is shared in a coherent superposition between the two harmonics.
This example serves as a critical counterexample to classical intuition: it demonstrates that measuring a constant phase between consecutive harmonics via RABBIT does not necessarily imply that the phase difference (
31
) between the underlying coherent components of the fields is fixed, nor even that a
semiclassical
phase is well-defined. More generally, the ‘zoology’ of possibilities afforded by quantum light is remarkably vast.

Summary:

In sum, we have shown that
inter-mode coherence
between the fields of two successive harmonics, quantified in this case by
g_{ea}^{(1)}\neq 0
, is necessary to obtain interference in the RABBIT signal. This coherence can arise from fundamentally different physical mechanisms. In the classical case, such as a product of coherent states (
28
), it arises from a well-defined phase carried by each individual mode, and is macroscopic.
In the quantum case, inter-mode coherence can emerge from entanglement alone, even in the absence of a well-defined phase for each individual mode, as demonstrated by the two few-photon examples [see Eqs. (
41
) and (
45
)]. Interestingly, this shows that single-mode coherence is not required – it is the inter-mode coherence, whether classical or quantum, that governs the RABBIT contrast.

This distinction may play a significant role in the few-photon regime when comparing harmonics generated from free-electron lasers
Berrah et al. 2025
and high-order harmonic generation
Ferray et al. 1988
, which arise from inherently different mechanisms: free-electron lasers generate different frequency modes independently, whereas high-order harmonic generation is an intrinsically coherent quantum process.

Finally, the last term in Eq. (
38b
) depends exclusively on the infrared mode.
Specifically, the RABBIT oscillation vanishes if
\braket{a_{0}^{2}}=0
, a condition met by thermal chaotic light, phase-diffused states, or Fock states. In these cases, the lack of intrinsic phase coherence in the infrared field destroys the interferometric signal. In contrast,
\braket{a_{0}^{2}}
is non-zero for a broader class of states, most notably squeezed coherent states, which includes both the BSV and standard coherent states. This motivates a closer examination of how the specific statistics of these states influence the visibility of the sideband.

IV.3
Infrared in a squeezed coherent state and classical harmonics

To isolate the infrared contributions, we consider the regime in which the generated harmonics are effectively classical and perfectly coherent. In addition to the general conditions for uncorrelated light in Eq. (
37
), we assume the harmonics possess a well-defined amplitude and perfect mutual coherence, such that

\displaystyle F_{\beta}^{2}=4f_{\beta}^{2}\braket{a_{\beta}^{\dagger}a_{\beta}},

(46a)

\displaystyle g_{ea}^{(1)}=1,

(46b)

for
\beta=\{a,e\}
.

IV.3.1
Reduced expressions

Under these assumptions, the field ratio reduces to
\mu=(F_{e}f_{a})/(F_{a}f_{e})
, and the general expressions for the amplitude (
38a
), contrast (
38b
) and phase (
38c
) simplify to

A\approx\dfrac{1}{4}f_{0}^{2}\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}\left\{|M_{e}^{(2)}(E)|^{2}\>F_{e}^{2}+|M_{a}^{(2)}(E)|^{2}\>F_{a}^{2}\right\},

(47a)

C\approx\dfrac{2\big|\xi^{(2)}(E)\big|\mu}{\left\{\big|\xi^{(2)}(E)\big|\mu\right\}^{2}+1}\times\dfrac{|\braket{a_{0}^{2}}|}{\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}},

(47b)

and

\theta=-\arg\left[\xi^{(2)}(E)\right]+\arg\braket{a_{0}^{2}}.

(47c)

Note that they no longer depend on
f_{e}
and
f_{a}
since they simplify in the product
|\xi^{(2)}(E)|\mu
, and do not contribute in
\arg[\xi^{(2)}(E)]
.
However, they still depend on auto-correlations of infrared light through
\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}
and
\braket{a_{0}^{2}}
.

IV.3.2
Expressions for squeezed coherent states

Interestingly, for a squeezed coherent state characterized by the squeezing parameter
\zeta=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}
and the displacement
\alpha_{0}
, these two-point correlation functions do not factorize as for coherent states.
The initial state can be formally written as

\ket{\psi_{0}(-\infty)}=\mathbb{D}(\alpha_{0})\;\mathbb{S}(\zeta)\ket{0},

(48a)

in the convention that displacement

\mathbb{D}(\alpha)=\mathrm{e}^{\alpha a_{0}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{\star}a_{0}},

(48b)

acts after squeezing

\mathbb{S}(\zeta)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\left[\zeta^{\star}a_{0}^{2}-\zeta(a_{0}^{\dagger})^{2}\right]}.

(48c)

We recall that the expectation values of the correlation amplitudes for such fields are

\displaystyle\braket{a_{0}}=\alpha_{0},

(49a)

\displaystyle\braket{a_{0}^{2}}=\alpha_{0}^{2}-\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}}{2}\sinh(2r),

(49b)

\displaystyle\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}=|\alpha_{0}|^{2}+\sinh(r)^{2},

(49c)

with
n_{0}=\braket{a_{0}^{\dagger}a_{0}}
the total number of infrared photons.
We also introduce the squeezing ratio
\varepsilon_{0}\in[0,1]
defined from the squeezing strength as

\varepsilon_{0}=\dfrac{\sinh(r)^{2}}{n_{0}},

(50)

that fully determines the modulus of displacement through
|\alpha_{0}|^{2}=(1-\varepsilon_{0})n_{0}
and allows us to consider various squeezed states at a constant number of photons.
Note that
\varepsilon_{0}=0
and
\varepsilon_{0}=1
correspond to the coherent state and the BSV limit
Spasibko et al. 2017
;
Manceau et al. 2019
;
Heimerl et al. 2025
, respectively.
In Figs.
3
,
4
,
5
and
6
, we fix the number of photons to
n_{0}=10^{16}
and
\arg\alpha_{0}=0
, and as a consequence, the initial Wigner distribution is centered on the positive real axis (see Fig.
1
).
The infrared field utilizes a vacuum amplitude
f_{0}=10^{-12}
a.u. corresponding to a quantization volume of
5\;\rm mm^{3}
in (

V
Conclusions

In this work, we have theoretically investigated the imprints of both classical and quantum light statistics on interferometric photoemission measurements. By employing a TDPT framework, we have established a unified description that accounts for the quantum nature of the electromagnetic field within the photoelectron dynamics. In the semiclassical limit of a product of coherent states (
28
), this approach converges to the standard semiclassical results, as it involves the same conceptual quantities such as the one- and two-photon transition matrix elements
Véniard et al. 1996
;
Maquet et al. 1998
;
Klünder et al. 2011
;
Dahlström et al. 2013
;
Busto et al. 2019
;
Berkane et al. 2024
,
M_{\beta}^{(1)}(E)
and
M_{\beta}^{(2)}(E)
. Beyond this limit, the formalism extends to non-classical and quantum states of light, providing the necessary flexibility to treat few-photon regimes, entangled states, and non-classical field statistics such as squeezing.

Applying this formalism to the RABBIT scheme, we derived a generalized expression for the main harmonic peak intensity spectrum in terms of photon number operators. Furthermore, we recovered the conventional sideband modulation as a function of the phase
\omega_{0}\tau
. Crucially, our results reveal that the sideband parameters, amplitude
A
, contrast
C
, and phase
\theta
, are intrinsically linked to the high-order correlations between harmonic and infrared fields [see Eqs. (
24
)]. We have demonstrated that “cross-mode bunching” between the infrared and harmonic modes can significantly amplify the RABBIT oscillation amplitude, suggesting a viable pathway for spectroscopic probing of the joint properties of the driving field and the emitted radiation. In contrast, we have shown that a lack of coherence, either between the harmonics and the infrared field or between the harmonics themselves, leads to a marked reduction in signal contrast. Specifically, a non-zero interferometric signal requires a stable phase relationship between the harmonics. In the absence of this inter-harmonic coherence, such as in the case of independent Fock states, the RABBIT oscillations are entirely suppressed.

The application of our theory to infrared light in squeezed coherent states further demonstrates the sensitivity of the RABBIT signal to underlying photon statistics. Our analysis shows that the squeezing phase
\varphi
and the ratio
\varepsilon_{0}
play a decisive role in the resulting interferogram. Specifically, we identified a regime of diminished contrast at intermediate squeezing phases, which we attribute to the reduction in the magnitude of the field’s second-order moment. The excellent agreement between our TDPT results and the ab-initio numerical solutions (see Section
C
) validates the robustness of our perturbative approach.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that interferometric photoemission is not only a tool for temporal metrology but also a promising avenue for probing the quantum nature of light.
The general formalism for polychromatic quantum light enables the mapping of higher-order correlation terms directly onto the photoelectron spectrum.
While our current implementation using the two-photon RABBIT scheme accesses correlations up to fourth-order, the framework is scalable, i.e. the inclusion of transitions involving a higher number of photons would allow for the extraction of even higher-order statistical moments.
Furthermore, this approach provides a robust framework for reconstructing the photoelectron density matrix
Bourassin-Bouchet et al. 2020
;
Laurell et al. 2022
and resolving angular distributions.
The ability to map photon statistics onto photoelectron observables opens new perspectives for quantum-enhanced spectroscopy and the characterization of complex quantum states of light at attosecond timescales.

Given that
\det\Sigma^{-1}=4
, we recover the normalization condition

\int W(\alpha)\;\mathrm{d}^{2}\alpha=1.

(77)

We have verified that, for
\theta=0
, this Wigner distribution leads to the variances
(\Delta x)^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-2r}/2
and
(\Delta p)^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{2r}/2
in the convention
\alpha=(x+\mathrm{i}p)/\sqrt{2}
.

In order to retrieve the properties (
49
), one can use the usual rules for the Wigner distribution (
74
) which are given by the following

\braket{(\hat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A}_{0})(\hat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A}_{0})^{\dagger}}_{\rm sym}=\Sigma,

(78)

where
\hat{\mathbf{A}}=(a,a^{\dagger})^{\top}
and where
\braket{\cdot}_{\rm sym}
denotes the symmetrized bracket such that
\braket{aa^{\dagger}}_{\rm sym}=\braket{a^{\dagger}a}_{\rm sym}=\braket{a^{\dagger}a}+1/2
.

Appendix C
Ab-initio numerical simulations for an infrared squeezed coherent state

For the simulations with an infrared squeezed coherent state of light in Sections
IV.3
and
IV.3.5
, the RABBIT spectrum is obtained from an incoherent sum of spectra weighted by
W(\alpha)
obtained with the TDSE

\dot{\psi}_{\alpha}(x,t)=-\mathrm{i}\Big(\mathbb{H}_{A}+\bra{\alpha}\mathbb{H}_{\rm int}(t)\ket{\alpha}\Big)\psi_{\alpha}(x,t).

(79)

This method is particularly well adapted for definite-positive Wigner distributions that have the property of a density probability, and when the net exchange of photons during the simulation is negligible compared to the number of photons in the incident field.
Formally speaking, this comes from the calculation of the intensity spectrum in Eq. (
1
) using the reconstruction of the density matrix in Eq. (
2
) in terms of

\rho(t)=\int\ket{\psi_{\alpha}(t)}W(\alpha)\bra{\psi_{\alpha}(t)}\;\mathrm{d}\alpha.

(80)

In this way, the numerical solution of the von Neumann equation (
2
) reduces to solving the standard TDSE with
semiclassical
light-matter interaction

\bra{\alpha}\mathbb{H}_{\rm int}(t)\ket{\alpha}=x\left[F_{\alpha}(t-\tau)+F_{h}(t)\right],

(81a)

where the harmonic field is

F_{h}(t)=\sum_{q}F_{q}\,f(t)\cos(\omega_{q}t),

(81b)

and the effective infrared field is

F_{\alpha}(t)=2f_{0}\>f(t)\operatorname{Re}\left(\alpha\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}t}\right).

(81c)

Here,
f(t)
is a
\sin^{2}
laser envelope with a total duration of 3000 a.u.
To evaluate the
W(\alpha)
-weighted sum, we generated a comprehensive library of
\ket{\alpha}
-dependent spectra. These were obtained by interpolating from a data set sampled in 100 carrier-envelope phases and a log-scaled range of IR intensities spanning
0
to
10^{13}\;\rm W\ cm^{-2}
.
Note that the intensity of the squeezed coherent states considered here is negligible outside this range of intensities.
