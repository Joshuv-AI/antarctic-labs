 1. Execu ve Summary
 Bioinspired design—which holds great promise for a new genera on of materials that are robust to defects,
 scalable under green manufacture, environmentally responsive, and programmably recon gurable—requires
 mastery over molecular self-organiza on. Yet, from its speci c mechanisms to most general architectures, the
 principles governing self-organiza on remain poorly understood and not even fully enumerated. For living
 systems, one obvious architectural principle is the modular reuse of a few simple molecular components in
 myriad combina ons to achieve more complex phenomena. For example, the mechanical tasks of a cell are
 driven by the nonequilibrium dynamics of cytoskeletal laments and molecular motors—the same laments and
 motors, reprogrammed by a variety of modulators, perform tasks ranging from cell movement to division.
 Similarly, many compartmentaliza on tasks are performed by liquid-like condensates of simple components,
 which act as membraneless organelles to localize par cular molecules in space and me (e.g. for gene regula on
 or RNA processing). In a few cases, condensates combine and interact with the cytoskeleton to create s ll more
 complex phenomena, e.g. the nuclea on of microtubule asters from the centrosome (a protein condensate) to
 form the mito c spindle during cell division. Very li le is known about the fundamental mechanisms of such
 lament-plus-condensate phenomena. Despite few examples, the landscape of behaviors that can be achieved
 through the combina on of condensates, laments, and motors appears vast. However, explora on has been
 hindered by a lack of systems that have su ciently programmable and dynamically tunable interac ons between
 component condensates, laments, and motors.
 We proposed to combine programmable DNA condensates, lamentous microtubules, and light-controlled
 motors into self-organizing systems whose principles go beyond those that have been observed in nature. In
 one limit, our systems we proposed to use microtubules and motors to create the molecular analog of a network
 of roads, and organize droplets of DNA condensates capable of carrying molecular cargo. DNA condensates
 coupled to motors would ow from one microtubule aster hub to another, with their direc on and ming
 controlled by DNA circuits. In another limit, microtubules would swim through bulk DNA condensates and exhibit
 strong interac ons with boundaries between di erent types of condensates. Microtubule swimmers would
 re ect, get trapped, or refract at boundaries, under a mechanical analog of the classical op cal index of
 refrac on. DNA condensates having di erent mechanical indexes of refrac on would be used to construct the
 analog of op cal lenses, so that microtubule swimmers can be manipulated like light—collimated, di racted,
 focused, and sorted based on proper es analogous to wavelength. These two limits de ne two new
 architectures, within which mul ple new mechanis c principles for self-organiza on would be discovered and
 explored. To explore these architectures, the motor-based coupling between DNA condensates and laments
 were to be controlled in me and space through the use of opto-proteins that create reversible links between
 DNA condensates and motors upon illumina on. For each principle of interest, pa erns of light were to create
 virtual experiments by de ning pa erns of ac vity where DNA condensates walk along laments, or laments
 swim through condensates, and pa erns of inac vity which were to serve either as controls, or as boundary
 condi ons vital to create the desired phenomena.
 This research serves the goals of Basic Energy Sciences Biomolecular Material Program by elucida ng the
 principles by which the emergent, nonequilibrium behavior of collec ons of DNA condensates, motors, and
 microtubules can be programmed by environmental light pa erns to create complex mo on and materials
 transport. Because DNA condensates can be readily coupled to virtually any high performance nanomaterial,
 from carbon nanotubes, to metal nanopar cles, to light harves ng systems, this work provides a path to the
 construc on, self-maintenance and recon gura on of materials relevant to the Department of Energy.

 2. Background

 The archetypal condensate- lament system is that of the centrosome (Fig. 1a, see next page) from which
 microtubules project to capture and segregate chromosomes during cell division. Recently [1] it has been shown
 that a rela vely simple protein condensate, with just a few modulators of tubulin assembly, is alone su cient to
 nucleate microtubule grown to form an aster (Fig. 1b). Control in such systems is limited and it is impossible to
 turn on and o the tubulin modulators to study the dynamics of aster forma on and disassembly, or to change
 proper es of the condensate to control the par on of components between the condensate and solu on.

 In recently published work [2], Dr. Tyler Ross, whose postdoctoral fellowship was supported by this award,
 employed light-s mulated dimeriza on of motor proteins (Fig. 1c) to spa ally control the assembly and
 disassembly of microtubule asters (Fig. 1d), to study the coalescence of asters, and to program elds of advec ve
 ow. Here, spa al control enabled both fundamental understanding of mechanism (e.g. telescoping of lament
 bundles during coalescence), and a new technological capability, namely that of being able to control ow in free
 space, without contact. This system, for which Dr. Ross gained extensive experience engineering, expressing, and
 manipula ng light dimerizable proteins, was intended to serve as founda on for the microtubule-motor part of
 the work done under this award.

 In complementary unpublished work, PI Paul Rothemund’s lab, added complexity and op cal control to the DNA
 condensates that form from four-armed DNA nanostars with self-complementary s cky ends [3] (Fig. 1e). In
 par cular, we have developed (A) mul ple immiscible (nonmixing) condensates having di erent densi es, which
 can be separated by low speed centrifuga on (Fig. 1f) and (B) the use of photocleavable linkers between two
 nanostars that spontaneously demix to provide light control of phase separa on. As has been demonstrated by
 other groups [4], each condensate can be programmed to serve as a compartment for guest molecules in a
 modular fashion by simply addressing the guest molecule to the host condensate with a DNA barcode. This it
 was intended that this system serve as the founda on for the ar cial condensate used under this award.

 3. Project Objec ves
 The main goals of this award, as stated in the proposal, were two-fold. The rst main goal was to explore the
 combina on of condensates and microtubules in a limit where there was “more microtubules than DNA
 condensate”. Subgoals of this main goal are described in Figure 2, see next page. The basic idea is that blobs of
 condensate “cargo” were going to move around on “roads” constructed from microtubules. Condensates having
 di erent densi es of motors were going to either “walk” or “ ow” along microtubules (Figure 2a), condensate
 monomers were going to be concentrated and assembled into larger condensate blobs (Figure 2b), di erent
 condensates were going to be organized into core-shell layers (Figure 2c), mixed up condensates were going to
 remixed (Figure 2d), and condensates of di erent kinds were going to be driven back and forth on condensate
 “roads” (Figure 2e).

 4. Descrip on of Ac vi es Performed
 Because of COVID, we shi ed to theore cal work for the rst half of the performance period. We explored the
 theore cal implica ons of the second main goal of the award, to understand what would happen in the “more
 DNA condensate than microtubules” regime. In par cular, we assumed that the experimental goals of Figure 3
 could all be achieved (in par cular that microtubules could be made to swim in DNA condensates of di erent
 viscosi es or di erent motor loadings). Then we asked, if we could construct such a system, what would the
 physics of the system be like? What kinds of physical phenomena could be observed. In par cular we asked
 whether something like “ray op cs” could be observed. By this we mean the following: Ray op cs enables
 light to be controlled with lenses, mirrors, and gradients in op cal index of refrac on. It is the basis for all of
 our microscopes, telescopes and cameras, and systems that focus beams of light for communica on or
 fabrica on tools like laser cu ng. A mechanical analog of ray op cs for moving objects like swimming
 microtubules would enable us to control microscopic par cles in the same way that we control light.

 Our rst objec ve was to just understand the basic phenomenal of refrac on at an interface. Because, unlike
 light, par cles can have shapes, (and we can make di erent shaped par cles with DNA origami), we asked what
 the di erence between disk-shaped and rectangular shaped objects would be. Figure 4 below, diagrams the
 di erence and emphasizes the dependence of the refrac on angle on the geometry of the swimmer. As we will
 see later, the di erence was quite signi cant: the refrac on of rectangular objects depended on their aspect
 ra o (whether they were more square-like or more rod-like). The paths of rod-like objects barely bent at the
 interface, and square-like objects bent extremely.

 Our theory and simula on work demonstra ng the principle of mechanical refrac on of gliders was published in
 ACS Nano [5] in 2022. Recall that our original proposal, described in Sec on 3 above, was framed in the context

 freely in a 3D matrix of DNA condensates. Above the theore cal and simula on analysis of mechanical refrac on

 Brownian mo on. This choice made it possible to easily derive a mechanical Snell’s law and predict the behavior
 of di erent shapes. However in the course of publica on reviewers felt that our analysis, while correct and
 compelling, was too simple to answer the ques on of whether refrac on would work for swimmers. They agreed
 that our analysis should hold for the case of gliders moving along 2D surfaces with fric on discon nui es, and
 were enthusias c about publica on in this case. Thus, while we felt that our analysis should s ll work in the full
 hydrodynamic scenario, we reframed the paper emphasize the result for gliders on a surface. Since publica on
 (and a er the performance period of this award), our collaborator John Brady has generalized the theore cal
 result to a full hydrodynamic model, and all the behavior predicted for gliders generalizes for swimmers.
 Nevertheless less, lab restric ons relaxed and we began to ramp up experiments, our experience with

 Because (1) the new gliding DNA nanotube system was extremely simple and robust, (2) the DNA nanotube
 system was familiar to us because it used DNA nanotubes that we had invented in 2004 [14], (3) the DNA
 nanotube system had already accomplished “the hard part” of interfacing protein motors and DNA
 nanostructures, and (4) DNA origami (Figure 8d) and single-stranded type systems (Figure 8e) can be used to
 make arbitrary 2D shapes, our new goal was to make rectangles of di erent aspect ra os (or more complex
 shapes) and observe their gliding mo on in a system analogous to that of (Figure 8c), but using the principles
 developed in our ray op cs paper.

 We worked for almost a year trying to generalize the gliding DNA nanotube system to arbitrary DNA origami
 shapes. Try as we might (controlling the number of motor binding sites, controlling for spurious binding sites on
 the DNA origami, controlling the geometry of motor binding sites) we could not get any DNA nanostructures
 other than DNA nanotubes to glide on the glass arrays. These many a empts and hypotheses about “why DNA
 origami do not glide” are too numerous to document here. Instead they are documented in the thesis of Dr.
 Ma eo Guareschi [15]. We do not believe that there is any fundamental obstacle to ge ng DNA shapes other
 than DNA tubes to glide, but hypothesize that cryp c protein motor sites in the DNA origami may be preven ng
 them from gliding. Solving this would require reengineering the sequence of DNA origami, at a cost of me that
 was beyond what we could accomplish under the current award.

 Thus we chose to reengineer the DNA nanotube system, and provide control func onality that was not present
 in the system as it was originally developed. In par cular, we chose to demonstrate that the mo on of DNA
 nanotube gliders could be ini ated, at will, under control of a DNA signal and that di erent popula ons of DNA
 nanotube gliders (e.g. controlled and non-controlled) could operate independently within the same system.
 Figure 9 shows the DNA schema for what we termed a “launch control” system, that ini ally immobilizes
 nanotube gliders via a DNA linker complex (at le ). Motors can engage nanotubes, but because they are
 tethered by hundreds or thousands of DNA linkers, they cannot move. Addi on of an “invader strand” severs the
 link to the surface via DNA strand displacement, and allows surface-bound motors to propel the DNA nanotubes.

 6. Lessons Learned
 One main obstacle to the work under this award was the COVID pandemic. The performance period of this grant
 was 08/15/2020 08/14/2023, work started early during pandemic and lab work was severely curtailed for at
 least the rst year of the performance period. Thus, theory work was emphasized, and it resulted in the highly
 novel concept of mechanical refrac on described above.

 A second obstacle was that our ini al theore cal collaborators were highly competent in theory and simula on
 of motor- lament-condensate systems, but in the end our postdoc collaborator was not as excited about the
 system as we were and did not share a similar vision for deriving mechanical refrac on; thus we shi ed to
 collabora ng with Dino Osmanović (now at UCLA) and John Brady (at Caltech); this resulted in quick progress
 towards our goal of a mathema cal framework for jus fying refrac on at a mechanical discon nuity. Here, the
 lesson learned is just to switch collaborators early when there is a mismatch in vision, and things worked out well
 in the end.

 A third obstacle is that our ini al deriva ons for mechanical refrac on were not over a full hydrodynamic model,
 and reviewers in the eld of ac ve par cle hydrodynamics were not sa s ed that the e ect would work similarly
 were hydrodynamics fully modeled. For the purposes of review we thus moved our framing of the work to the
 movement of gliders on surfaces, where the relevant fric on corresponds perfectly well to our models. Thus our
 work went from being “Snell’s law for Swimmers” to “Ray Op cs for Gliders”. Reviewers responded highly
 posi vely to this change. In the me since publica on, John Brady has extended the work to a full hydrodynamic
 treatment and all of the “op cal” e ects that we observe for gliders on surfaces should hold equally well for
 swimmers moving in, for example, 3D systems of condensates in free-space/solu on. The choice to reframe the
 work for publica on thus worked out well.

 A fourth obstacle to the work under this award was the failure of the uorescence microscope used for the later
 experimental work. We had originally acquired, through a $250K ONR DURIP award, acquired an Oxford
 Nanoimaging (ONI) microscope, which did not perform as adver sed. Our goal was to perform op cal control of
 condensates and motor proteins, through pa erned illumina on, via a Digital Micromirror Device (DMD). The
 DMD was never fully integrated into the microscope, and the so ware crashed so o en that students and
 postdocs could not use the microscope. Having returned the ONI microscope, we chose Cairn Research Ltd, to
 create a custom microscope, with pa erned illumina on, using their “open microscope pla orm”. The
 microscope arrived damaged during COVID (so that Cairn engineers could not come to x damaged components)
 and further the so ware integra on with the stage and light sources was so aky that the microscope could not
 be used. Because of the delays due to these underperforming microscopes, we did not do pa erned
 illumina on experiments under this award. The lesson learned over this two-microscope misadventure is that,
 while it would have cost more money and we would have go en fewer features, we should have just bought a
 Nikon. In the end, we ended up doing all uorescence microscopy using Caltech’s Imaging Facility, on user
 microscopes, which given the scope of the DNA lament-motor experiments we were doing, was su cient.
