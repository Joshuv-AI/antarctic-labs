Advances in the Rational Design, Mechanisms, and Applications of Lanthanide Nanoparticles for the Regulation of Reactive Oxygen Species
Authors: Zheng Zhao, Yanan Liang, Hongtao Lan, Jiatong Wu, Tiantian Shan, Changan Qu, Sijia Liu, Xianming Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLanthanide nanoparticles provide an ideal platform for the precise regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) due to their reversible redox properties, photomagnetic characteristics, and functionalizable surfaces. This review summarizes recent advances in the mechanistic understanding, biomedical applications, and material design strategies of lanthanide nanoparticles for ROS modulation. Mechanistically, lanthanide nanoparticles generate ROS through photoexcited electron transfer, Fenton-like reactions, and ligand-mediated redox cycling, while also effectively scavenging ROS via reversible valence switching and metal-ligand synergistic effects. At the application level, these nanoparticles leverage their combined antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties to provide protection against oxidative stress-related diseases, while enabling targeted tumor elimination through chemodynamic therapy, photodynamic therapy, sonodynamic therapy, radiosensitization, and multimodal imaging. Recent material design has encompassed upconversion architectures, multi-metal composite systems, single-atom catalysts, biodegradable systems, and artificial intelligence-assisted rational design. Despite remaining challenges in understanding metabolic pathways and achieving targeting precision, the integration of lanthanide nanomaterials with emerging therapeutic modalities and intelligent design strategies holds promise for innovative approaches to the diagnosis and treatment of ROS-associated diseases.Keywords: lanthanide elements, nanoparticles, reactive oxygen species, ROS, disease therapy, rational design

Introduction

Biological Significance of ROS and the Need for Regulation

The Dual Role of ROS: Cellular Signaling and Pathological Damage

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a dual yet crucial role in cellular functions.1–3 These highly reactive molecules, including Superoxide anion (O2˙−), Hydrogen Peroxide (H2O2), and Hydroxyl radical (˙OH), serve as important signaling messengers at physiological concentrations, regulating various functions including cell proliferation, differentiation, and immune responses, primarily via the Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) and Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase/Protein Kinase B (PI3K/Akt) pathways.4 However, when the production of ROS exceeds the capacity for endogenous clearance, the ROS undergoes a transition from signaling molecules to damaging agents, attacking lipids, proteins, and Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA).5 This shift triggers oxidative stress and cell death, contributing to a variety of pathological processes, including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and neurodegenerative disorders.6 Therefore, maintaining the precise dynamic balance of intracellular ROS is not only fundamental to normal cellular functionality but also a key target for the prevention and treatment of disease. Limitations of Traditional ROS Regulation Methods and the Need for Novel Nanosystems

Conventional methods for regulating ROS, such as broad-spectrum antioxidants and non-selective pro-oxidants, face significant challenges due to their lack of specificity and control.7 These approaches often fail to distinguish between healthy and diseased tissues, potentially disrupting the physiological redox balance throughout the body and inadvertently harming healthy cells.8 Furthermore, these treatments are frequently associated with low bioavailability, rapid degradation, and instability, leading to limited efficacy and necessitating frequent administration, thereby increasing costs for patients.9 Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop innovative nano-regulation systems that can ensure precise delivery, on-demand activation, and the sustained maintenance of ROS balance.

Unique Advantages of Lanthanide Nanoparticles

Tunable Redox Activity and Enzyme-Mimicking Functions

The redox properties of lanthanide nanoparticles can be modulated by tuning their composition, size, morphology, and surface characteristics.10 The reversible conversion of surface valence states endows them with enzyme-mimicking functions that emulate superoxide dismutase and catalase activities,11 while particle size engineering and compositional engineering further expand their redox potential range, enabling synergistic regulation of multiple ROS species.

Multi-Modal Responsive Properties Relating to Light, Magnetism, and Radiation

Lanthanide nanoparticles exhibit distinctive optical, magnetic, and radiation-responsive features, creating a versatile platform for the spatiotemporal regulation of ROS.12 Upconversion luminescence enables deep-tissue photo-controlled ROS modulation.13,14 Paramagnetic properties facilitate magnetic field-guided targeting, while the high atomic number of lanthanides confers X-ray absorption and scattering capabilities for CT imaging and radiosensitization.15,16 The synergistic integration of these attributes enables the construction of theranostic platforms that combine targeted delivery, multimodal imaging, and spatiotemporal regulation.

Surface Functionalization and the Expansion of Biomedical Applications

Through surface chemical modification, lanthanide nanoparticles can be conjugated with PEG, targeting ligands, responsive groups, and functional moieties,17 thereby improving biocompatibility, achieving precise lesion targeting,18 enabling controlled drug release,19 and constructing multifunctional nanoplatforms for the treatment of complex diseases.

Mechanisms of ROS Generation

The efficiency and selectivity of ROS generation by lanthanide nanoparticles depend on three progressively interconnected dimensions. The band structure determines whether photogenerated electrons and holes possess sufficient potential to oxidize water or reduce oxygen. This constitutes the thermodynamic prerequisite for ROS production. Oxygen vacancies introduce impurity energy levels within the band gap, thereby modulating carrier separation and recombination through electron trapping. The Michaelis constant Km and catalytic constant kcat of catalytic kinetics quantify the ability of active sites to convert substrates into comparable numerical values. These three dimensions are mutually interdependent: oxygen vacancy concentration modulates the band structure, which in turn influences catalytic selectivity, while kinetic parameters reflect the integrated outcome of the preceding factors on reaction rates.

Band Structure Effects and Oxygen Vacancy-Mediated Photoexcitation

The efficiency of photoexcited electron transfer and the types of ROS generated fundamentally depend on the band structure of the nanoparticles.20,21 The positions of the valence band maximum (VBM) and conduction band minimum (CBM) relative to the normal hydrogen electrode determine whether photogenerated holes can oxidize water to produce ˙OH (requiring VBM > +1.23 V) and whether photogenerated electrons can reduce O2 to O2˙− (requiring CBM < −0.33 V).22 Lanthanide doping introduces lattice defects that modulate the band alignment. When trivalent lanthanide ions (eg, La3+, Nd3+) substitute tetravalent ions (eg, Ce4+, Ti4+) in the lattice, charge mismatch forces the generation of oxygen vacancies to maintain charge neutrality. These oxygen vacancies subsequently serve as electron trapping centers, introducing impurity energy levels within the band gap, reducing the effective band gap width, and extending the lifetime of photogenerated charge carriers.23 This cascade effect has been validated across multiple systems. La-doped BiFeO3 reduces electron-hole recombination rates through synergistic band gap narrowing and oxygen vacancy formation.24,25 In Nd-doped NaTaO3, oxygen vacancies shift the optical absorption edge from the ultraviolet region to 640 nm, accompanied by an extended photoluminescence lifetime of 36.65 ns.26 In La-doped SrMnO3, oxygen vacancies and localized electronic states function as electron traps that mediate the stepwise reduction of oxygen to generate superoxide anions.27 By selecting appropriate lanthanide dopants and their concentrations, oxygen vacancy concentrations can be precisely modulated. This enables fine control over the band structure as well as the yield and selectivity of ROS.

Catalytic Kinetics of Fenton-Like Reactions

The variable valence states of lanthanide elements enable them to catalyze Fenton-like reactions, driving the decomposition of H2O2 to generate ˙OH.28 The catalytic efficiency of this process can be quantified using Michaelis–Menten kinetic parameters, where Km reflects the affinity between the nanozyme and its substrate, and kcat/Km serves as a comprehensive indicator of catalytic efficiency.29 The ZIF-67@CeO2 nanozyme exhibits Km values of 0.058 mM for H2O2 and 0.94 mM for TMB, both lower than those of natural horseradish peroxidase.30 On this basis, Pr and Tb doping further enhance the enzyme-like activity of CeO2.31,32 In magnesium ferrite systems, the Fenton-like catalytic activity of different lanthanide-doped ions under ultraviolet irradiation follows the sequence: Ce3+ > Dy3+ > La3+ ≈ undoped > Sm3+ > Gd3+.33 These results indicate that the catalytic efficiency of Fenton-like reactions can be directionally optimized by modulating the type of dopant element.

Surface Ligand-Mediated Redox Cycling

Surface ligands participate in ROS generation through two distinct pathways. First, ligands modulate the electron density at metal centers through coordination interactions, thereby influencing electron exchange between metal ions and neighboring molecules.34 In Tb/Eu-based lanthanide metal-organic frameworks (Ln-MOFs), visible light excitation triggers electron transfer from ligands to Ln3+, generating 1O2.35 La-modified MOF-74 enhances oxygen activation through La-mediated modulation of electron density at Co sites.36 Lanthanide coordination polymer nanoparticles (Ln-CPNs) also exhibit peroxidase-like activity originating from ligand-metal synergistic electron transfer.37 Second, certain ligands themselves can directly participate in oxygen activation. Carboxylate ligands form reactive intermediates with oxygen molecules through non-covalent interactions, which subsequently convert to superoxide anions or hydrogen peroxide.38,39 Ligands containing unsaturated bonds can undergo radical chain reactions with oxygen molecules, decomposing into ˙OH and O2˙−.40

Mechanisms of ROS Scavenging

Lanthanide nanoparticles scavenge ROS through three mechanisms: reversible valence switching of variable-valence elements, direct consumption of ROS by lattice oxygen vacancies, and synergistic coordination of surface ligands with metal ion electronic structures. Valence switching of variable-valence elements constitutes the core source of scavenging activity. Oxygen vacancies provide active sites for elements lacking stable variable valence states. Surface ligands, through synergistic coordination effects, further expand the adsorption and conversion capacity of nanoparticles toward ROS.

Synergy and Divergence of Valence Cycling and Oxygen Vacancies in ROS Scavenging

The rapid and reversible interconversion between Ce3+ and Ce4+ represents the classical paradigm for ROS scavenging by lanthanide elements.41 Ce4+ accepts electrons from O2˙− and converts it to O2, while being reduced to Ce3+. Conversely, Ce3+ donates electrons to ˙OH and eliminates it, while being oxidized to Ce4+.42 This dynamic cycling enables nanoceria to sustain continuous ROS scavenging without being consumed. Oxygen vacancies are intimately associated with Ce3+ in this process, both providing the structural prerequisite for Ce3+ existence and serving as adsorption and reaction sites for ROS.41 The impurity energy levels introduced by oxygen vacancies within the band gap trap excess electrons, preventing their participation in deleterious Fenton reactions and thereby indirectly suppressing ˙OH generation.43

Unlike Ce, La3+ lacks accessible variable valence states. Its scavenging activity derives not from metal ion redox cycling but from lattice oxygen vacancies and surface structural effects.44 La(OH)3 nanorods possess two distinct types of surface active sites: LaO-terminated surfaces neutralize ˙OH, while electron-deficient surface regions adsorb and neutralize O2˙−,44,45 enabling valence-independent scavenging of multiple ROS species. When La3+ is doped into CeO2, increasing dopant concentration significantly elevates oxygen vacancy concentrations within the lattice. Among these, 30% La-CNPs exhibit optimal ROS scavenging and osteogenic differentiation promotion, while higher concentrations result in diminished performance due to structural instability or cytotoxicity, indicating the existence of an optimal oxygen vacancy concentration window.46

Terbium presents a distinctly different behavioral pattern. Although TbO2−x shares the same cubic fluorite-type structure and similar Tb3+/Tb4+ valence coexistence with CeO2, its valence switching is irreversible. The photoluminescence intensity of Tb3+ does not recover after H2O2 addition.47 This irreversibility renders TbO2−x incapable of ROS scavenging activity. Instead, it catalyzes H2O2 decomposition to generate ˙OH through a Fenton-like mechanism. The comparison between Ce and Tb reveals that the thermodynamic reversibility of valence switching is the fundamental factor determining whether a material tends to scavenge or generate ROS.

Europium theoretically possesses Eu2+/Eu3+ valence potential. However, in oxide nanoparticles Eu predominantly exists in the +3 state, as Eu2+ is highly unstable under conventional conditions. Consequently, direct evidence for ROS scavenging by Eu-based nanoparticles remains limited.48 Gd3+ does not directly participate in redox reactions. However, its incorporation into the CeO2 lattice induces lattice expansion and local distortion, thereby indirectly modulating oxygen vacancy concentrations and the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio.49

Metal-Ligand Synergistic Scavenging

Ligands contribute to ROS scavenging through both metal regulation and direct participation. In the ATP-Ce3+ coordination polymer nanoparticle system, the free radical scavenging capacity originates from the synergistic effect between Ce3+/Ce4+ mixed valence states and the ligand.37 In Ce(III) complexes containing 2,6-di-tert-butylphenol moieties, the ligand itself possesses intrinsic free radical scavenging capability.50 In La(III)-triazole complexes, the triazole ligand directly scavenges hydroxyl radicals through hydrogen atom transfer and single electron transfer pathways.51

Systemic Limitations of Current Research Methodologies

The same lanthanide material frequently yields contradictory conclusions across different studies. This phenomenon primarily reflects systemic issues in experimental methodologies and study designs, resulting in a lack of comparability between findings and limiting the generalizability of conclusions.

Lack of Standardization in Material Synthesis and Characterization

The field lacks standard reference materials, and lanthanide nanoparticles synthesized in different laboratories exhibit substantial variations in particle size, morphology, surface chemistry, and doping concentration. Taking nanoceria as an example, reported particle sizes range from 2 nm to 100 nm, morphologies include nanospheres, nanorods, and nanocubes, and Ce3+/Ce4+ ratios vary from 20:80 to 70:30. These parameters significantly influence the direction and kinetics of redox switching. Any conclusions regarding optimal particle size or doping concentration must be interpreted as observations under specific synthesis conditions, rather than intrinsic material properties.

Inconsistency of in vitro Detection Methods and Disconnect from Physiological Conditions

ROS detection methods include electron spin resonance, chemiluminescence, fluorescence probes, and colorimetric assays. These methods exhibit different sensitivities to nanoparticle surface states, protein corona formation, and buffer conditions. Taking Gd-doped CeO2 as an example, the material exhibits enhanced free radical scavenging in some assays but displays pro-oxidant activity in chemiluminescence assays, with SOD-like activity only manifesting upon conjugation with native SOD enzyme. This indicates that the true activity of the same material is difficult to detect consistently across different methods due to their differential sensitivities. In Fenton-like catalytic kinetic studies, most work fails to normalize activity to the actual number of active sites. Most studies also fail to systematically account for the contributions of specific surface area and crystallographic orientation to Km and kcat. Moreover, chromogenic substrates such as TMB respond to multiple oxidation pathways and are not specific indicators of Fenton-like reactions, further undermining the comparability of kinetic parameters. The majority of studies draw conclusions based on a single detection method without orthogonal validation. The disconnect between in vitro conditions and physiological environments is equally pronounced. Conclusions obtained in serum-free media overlook the impact of protein corona formation: upon entry into biological fluids, nanoparticle surfaces rapidly adsorb proteins, completely altering their surface chemistry and biological identity, shielding active sites, and modifying cellular uptake pathways. This renders many in vitro observations potentially invalid in vivo.

Insufficient Validation of Catalytic Cycling and Fragmentation of Preclinical Studies

The self-regenerating antioxidant capacity of nanoceria is attributed to the Ce3+/Ce4+ cycle. However, the recovery of photoluminescence intensity to initial levels after Ce3+ oxidation to Ce4+ requires a delay of several days. This indicates that under conditions requiring rapid and sustained ROS clearance, such as acute inflammation or ischemia-reperfusion, the actual antioxidant capacity falls substantially below theoretical expectations. Most studies do not account for this kinetic limitation when claiming self-regenerating activity. At the preclinical level, substantial variations in animal models, administration routes, observation time points, and endpoint measures impede data integration and meta-analysis, and hinder the extraction of dose-response relationships or long-term safety data applicable to clinical translation. The compounding of these issues limits the generalizability of published conclusions and explains why the same material system frequently yields contradictory results across different studies.

Redox Balance and Anti-Inflammatory Immune Regulation

Lanthanide nanoparticles, characterized by their variable 4f electronic configuration and multivalent properties, exhibit multidimensional synergistic effects for anti-inflammatory and antioxidant therapies. The primary advantage of these nanoparticles lies in their capacity to simultaneously and efficiently scavenge ROS while precisely modulating inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby facilitating coordinated intervention for both oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. For instance, Gadolinium-Cerium Nanoparticles (Gd-CeNPs) not only mimic the functions of SOD and CAT but also activate the intracellular Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2-Related Factor 2/Antioxidant Response Element (Nrf2/ARE) pathway, leading to the upregulation of endogenous antioxidant defense proteins, including Glutathione Peroxidase (GPx) and Heme Oxygenase-1 (HO-1).60,61 A previous study showed that in a Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammation model, the intracellular levels of ROS were significantly reduced by 56.20%. Moreover, these materials can intervene in the inflammatory cascade by directly inhibiting key pro-inflammatory signaling pathways. For instance, LaCl3 has been shown to inhibit activation of the Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway by blocking Protein Kinase C (PKC)/Ca2+-dependent IκBα phosphorylation, thereby downregulating the expression of Inducible Nitric Oxide Synthase (iNOS) in macrophages.62 Furthermore, Ce/Pr-CQDs have been shown to reduce the expression of Interleukin-6 (IL-6) in the synovial tissue of a model of arthritis by inhibiting the c-Jun N-terminal Kinase (JNK)/c-Jun pathway, thus alleviating joint swelling and tissue damage.63

Cardiovascular Diseases: From Atherosclerosis to Ischemia-Repair

Lanthanide nanoparticles exhibit adjustable redox properties, mimic enzymatic functions, and possess multivalent traits, making them promising candidates for multi-target and multi-level approaches in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases. These nanoparticles provide novel therapeutic avenues for certain conditions, including atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, and limb ischemia, by concurrently influencing critical pathological mechanisms, including oxidative stress, inflammation, lipid metabolism, and angiogenesis. In the context of atherosclerosis management, these nanoparticles work synergistically via three primary mechanisms: metabolic modulation, antioxidant action, and anti-inflammatory effects. For instance, La(NO3)3 can influence lipid metabolism by reducing the levels of total cholesterol and Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) while increasing the levels of High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL), mitigating oxidative stress by enhancing the levels of SOD and Glutathione (GSH) activity and by reducing Malondialdehyde (MDA), and altering inflammatory pathways by inhibiting the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK and NF-κB, thus leading to a notable reduction in the area occupied by aortic plaques in an ApoE−/− mouse model.64 In addition, Gd/CeO2 utilizes the Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycle to provide dual SOD and CAT mimetic functions, effectively eliminating excess ROS, preventing lipid accumulation in macrophages, and facilitating theranostics via its T1-weighted MRI imaging capabilities, thus facilitating precise plaque detection and treatment.52 In the contexts of ischemia and regenerative repair, lanthanide nanoparticles promote protective effects by activating essential pro-survival and angiogenic signaling pathways. For example, europium hydroxide nanorods (EHN) and terbium hydroxide nanorods (THNR) have been shown to stimulate the PI3K/Akt/Endothelial Nitric Oxide Synthase (eNOS) signaling pathway, enhancing the production of nitric oxide and thereby reducing apoptosis in cardiomyocytes65,66 while also promoting angiogenesis through the Wingless/Integrated (Wnt)/Glycogen Synthase Kinase-3β (GSK-3β)/β-catenin pathway, thus facilitating tissue repair and the restoration of blood flow in ischemic models.53,67 Moreover, lanthanide nanoparticles also play a critical role in epigenetic regulation. For instance, lanthanum chloride (LC) can downregulate the histone demethylase Jumonji Domain Containing 3 (Jmjd3), leading to the increased methylation of Histone H3 Lysine 27 Trimethylation (H3K27me3) in the promoter regions of inflammatory genes. This process suppresses NF-κB-driven inflammatory responses at the epigenetic level and reduces vascular endothelial inflammation.68

Neurological Diseases: Neuroinflammation Inhibition and Neuroprotection

Lanthanide nanoparticles exhibit unique physical characteristics that enable them to effectively manipulate multiple mechanisms in the management of neurological disorders, particularly in reducing neuroinflammation and protecting against neurodegenerative conditions. The primary approach of these nanoparticles focuses on the concurrent modulation of critical pathological factors, including oxidative stress, inflammatory processes, and the clearance of harmful proteins. Furthermore, these nanoparticles facilitate simultaneous diagnosis and therapy by leveraging the imaging capabilities of specific lanthanide elements. Diseases of the Digestive System: Repair of the Intestinal Barrier and the Alleviation of Oxidative Stress

Lanthanide nanoparticles exhibit remarkable potential for repairing disorders of the digestive system by effectively modulating inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and the integrity of the intestinal barrier. In the context of inflammatory bowel disease, the primary mechanism of lanthanide nanoparticles involves the targeted suppression of immune-related inflammation and the activation of natural protective mechanisms. For instance, GdCl3 has been shown to inhibit pro-inflammatory macrophages (M1 type) and neutrophils, while also obstructing the NF-κB signaling pathway to reduce the nuclear translocation of p65. This action led to a reduction in the production of critical inflammatory mediators, such as Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α) and Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), significantly reducing the disease activity index and facilitating the repair of crypt structures.70,71 Furthermore, GdCl3 exhibits dual antioxidant properties by reducing ROS levels via the inhibition of NADPH Oxidase 2 (NOX2) and by enhancing the functionality of endogenous antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and CAT, thereby restoring the balance of intracellular GSH/GSSG. In cases of intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury, lanthanide nanoparticles leverage their variable valence transition capabilities to mitigate oxidative stress and safeguard barrier function. For example, Eu-Ceria nanoparticles, with their rich Ce3+/Ce4+ cycling and oxygen vacancies, have been shown to significantly enhance the scavenging of O2˙− and H2O2. This not only reduced tissue levels of MDA but also activated the Nrf2/ARE pathway to alleviate mitochondrial oxidative damage, resulting in an 82% reduction in ischemic edema in the intestinal villi and by restoring the expression of tight junction proteins (occludin and claudin-1) to 76% of their normal levels.56 Furthermore, these materials can help to regulate the intestinal microenvironment. GdCl3 has been shown to facilitate the regeneration of goblet cells and maintenance of the mucus barrier, while Eu-Ceria nanoparticles have been shown to play a crucial role in restoring microbial balance by inhibiting the growth of pathogenic Enterobacteriaceae.

Diseases of the Urinary Systems: Delaying Calcification and Metabolic Regulation

Lanthanide nanoparticles have considerable potential for the management of urinary system disorders, particularly in the prevention and treatment of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) and its associated complications. These nanoparticles have been shown to play a critical role in mitigating vascular calcification, addressing metabolic bone issues, and reducing inflammation and oxidative stress. Furthermore, lanthanide nanoparticles can effectively modulate calcium-phosphate metabolism and inhibit detrimental trans-differentiation. For example, lanthanum hydroxide (LH) is capable of scavenging ROS and inhibiting the PI3K-AKT-mTOR-HIF-1α signaling pathway, thus preventing the conversion of vascular smooth muscle cells into osteoblast-like cells. Furthermore, these nanoparticles can help to reduce the serum levels of phosphorus, Parathyroid Hormone (PTH), and Fibroblast Growth Factor 23 (FGF23), demonstrating superior efficacy compared to conventional phosphate binders in reducing aortic calcification in rat models of CKD.57 LC has been shown to decelerate the progression of coronary artery calcification in patients undergoing hemodialysis by diminishing the accumulation of calcium-phosphate in blood vessel walls and by modulating inflammatory responses.72 This regulation of the calcium-phosphate balance also supports the maintenance of bone mineral homeostasis. LC has also been shown to reduce blood phosphorus levels by binding to intestinal phosphate, thus alleviating negative feedback on the parathyroid gland, thereby activating renal 1α-hydroxylase and promoting the synthesis of active vitamin D3. This mechanism helps to regulate the activities of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, thereby improving metabolic disorders associated with renal osteodystrophy.73 Furthermore, lanthanide nanoparticles provide synergistic protective effects by reducing inflammation and oxidative stress. For instance, LH can inhibit the NF-κB pathway, resulting in reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6. Concurrently, LH has been shown to enhance SOD activity and lower MDA levels, which collectively mitigate oxidative damage and inhibit the process of fibrosis in blood vessels and the renal interstitium.57

The Skeletal System: Regulation of Bone Repair by the Microenvironment and Osteogenesis-Angiogenesis Synergy

Lanthanide nanoparticles hold significant promise for the regeneration of bone by intricately modulating the microenvironment via a series of mechanisms. Initially, these nanoparticles can be used to address oxidative stress and inflammation to enhance the microenvironment. For instance, LaCNPs have been shown to leverage the Ce3+/Ce4+ cycle to replicate the activity of SOD and CAT enzymes, thus resulting in a notable reduction in ROS levels in models of diabetic osteoporosis while also enhancing osteogenic differentiation by activating the FoxO1 pathway.46 Concurrently, nHAp/PLLA@Eu3+ has been shown to effectively enhance the local anti-inflammatory environment by suppressing the secretion of IL-1β and TNF-α, thus encouraging the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype.58 Furthermore, lanthanide nanomaterials can directly engage with cells to finely tune the process of osteogenic differentiation. For example, La2O3 nanoparticles were shown to elevate the expression of osteogenic markers, including Osteocalcin (OCN) and Osteopontin (OPN), and by stimulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thus achieving complete bone repair within eight weeks in a rat model of tibial defects.74 Moreover, these nanoparticles can promote angiogenesis as they can facilitate the migration of endothelial cells and the formation of new blood vessels by activating critical targets such as Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor Receptor 2 (VEGFR2), thus enabling the regeneration of vascular-rich cartilage-bone composite tissues in a range of models, including a rabbit model of knee joint defects.74

Tumor Sonodynamic Therapy: Ultrasound Excitation and Defect Engineering for Enhanced Efficacy

Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) targets tumors by producing ROS via the ultrasound activation of sonosensitizers, thus demonstrating the potential for non-invasive treatment and the ability to penetrate deep tissues. However, conventional organic sonosensitizers are constrained by low chemical stability and insufficient accumulation in tumors. Lanthanide nanoparticles, recognized for their tunable semiconductor properties and the potential for defect engineering, provide a promising alternative to overcome these limitations. For example, the application of D-ZnOₓ:Gd demonstrated that the incorporation of Gd3+ not only enhanced MRI contrast but also improved sonodynamic performance via synergistic interactions with the structure of zinc oxide, facilitated by intentionally generated oxygen defects.81 These defects act as effective electron traps, significantly prolonging the lifespan of photo-generated electron-hole pairs, which in turn increases the production of O2˙− and ˙OH. Furthermore, the defective surface substantially improves the adsorption capacity for nearby O2 and H2O molecules, thereby accelerating the generation of singlet oxygen (1O2). When subjected to 1 MHz of ultrasound stimulation, this system can collaboratively generate various ROS, achieving an in vitro inhibition rate of 85% against 4T1 breast cancer cells.

Tumor Piezodynamic Therapy: Mechanical Stress-Driven ROS Generation with Oxygen Independence

Piezodynamic therapy (PZDT) utilizes the built-in electric field generated by piezoelectric nanomaterials under mechanical stress, such as ultrasound, to drive the separation of electron-hole pairs. These charge carriers subsequently react with surrounding water or oxygen molecules to generate ROS.101 Compared with PDT, PZDT does not rely on external light sources or molecular oxygen, thereby overcoming the limitations of limited light penetration depth and tumor hypoxia.102 Compared with SDT, PZDT generates ROS through band bending driven by the piezoelectric effect rather than cavitation, offering a more clearly defined mechanism that can be systematically optimized through materials engineering.103 Lanthanide doping represents a core strategy for enhancing piezocatalytic efficiency. Studies on La-doped BiFeO3 have revealed the fundamental pathway through which doping enhances piezocatalysis: La incorporation narrows the band gap and creates oxygen vacancies, reducing electron-hole recombination rates, while the released La3+ ions can disrupt lysosomal membranes and synergize with ROS to induce tumor cell pyroptosis.24 In BaTiO3 nanorods, 3% La doping enhances piezocatalytic performance by approximately 2-fold, achieving a degradation efficiency of 99.19% for rhodamine B.104 KNN-based materials, through Gd3+ doping and defect engineering, exhibit significantly enhanced piezoelectric properties, providing a material basis for integrating piezocatalysis with imaging functions.105

Tumor Radiotherapy: Physical Radiosensitization and Remodeling of the Immune Microenvironment

Radiotherapy exerts effects on tumors by inducing DNA damage and generating ROS through ionizing radiation. However, the efficacy of this technique is often compromised by factors such as tumor radioresistance, including hypoxic conditions and robust DNA repair mechanisms, as well as the potential harm that this method can cause to healthy tissues. Lanthanide elements, characterized by their high atomic numbers, are capable of effectively absorbing and storing radiation energy, positioning these as promising candidates for enhancing radiosensitivity. For instance, previous work with TiO2(Gd)-TPP nanoparticles revealed that gadolinium (Gd3+) doping significantly enhanced local dose delivery from X-ray energy via photoelectric and Compton interactions, leading to substantial ˙OH production. This, combined with mitochondrial targeting, activated the caspase-3 pathway, resulting in complete tumor regression after a single radiotherapy treatment in an MCF-7 model of breast cancer.106 Similarly, Motexafin Gadolinium (MGd), a small molecule therapeutic, was shown to promote a surge in ROS and disrupt the balance of zinc ions in cancer cells by inhibiting Thioredoxin Reductase (TR) through its gadolinium core, thereby enhancing radiosensitivity.107 In the context of radionuclide therapy, the lanthanide isotope 161Tb has been shown to exhibit precise tumor destruction due to its multi-modal decay properties. In a model of prostate cancer, 161Tb-PSMA-617 was shown to deliver targeted therapy that releases conversion electrons, which cause direct damage to the nuclear DNA of tumor cells, while beta particles initiate radical cascades and TNF-α-mediated bystander effects.108,109 For neuroendocrine tumors, 161Tb-DOTA-LM3 utilizes its short-range Auger electrons to achieve nanoscale destruction within the nuclei of AR42J tumor cells.110 The chemical properties of lanthanide ions can be effectively utilized for subcellular regulation and the remodeling of the immune microenvironment. ZGd-NRs can modify the mitochondrial membrane potential, thus resulting in the exposure of calreticulin and the release of High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1), which in turn triggers Immunogenic Cell Death (ICD). In a 4T1 model, the combination of ZGd-NRs with radiotherapy was shown to reprogram Tumor-Asociated Macrophages (TAMs), reduce the secretion of TGF-β/IL-10, and enhance CD8+ T cell infiltration, thereby transforming “cold tumors” into “hot tumors”.111 AmGd-NPs, featuring a gadolinium core, have been shown to inhibit the adenosine pathway, effectively diminishing Regulatory T Cells (Tregs) and TAMs, thus amplifying the immune response to radiotherapy.112 Furthermore, advanced nano-design has been shown to integrate and enhance the capabilities of lanthanide elements. GdW10@CS nanospheres utilize gadolinium clusters to improve X-ray energy absorption, while the incorporated HIF-1α siRNA mitigates the adaptation of tumors to hypoxia, thus creating a synergistic effect between DNA damage and gene silencing.82 Other lanthanide materials exhibit distinct mechanisms. For example, La2O3 NPs release La3+ ions to directly induce mitochondrial apoptosis, thus increasing the radiosensitivity of glioma cells by 2-3-fold.113 Furthermore, via Eu3+ doping, CaF2:Eu NP has been shown to disrupt intracellular calcium balance, inhibit the activity of DNA repair enzymes, and work synergistically with radiotherapy to suppress osteosarcoma lung metastasis.114

Upconversion Nanoparticles: Luminescence Mechanisms and Performance Optimization

The unique 4f electron configuration of lanthanide elements endows upconversion nanoparticles with the ability to convert low-energy near-infrared light into high-energy visible or ultraviolet light.13,131 The luminescence originates from sequential energy level transitions among lanthanide ions. Sensitizer ions (eg, Yb3+) capture photons and transfer energy to activator ions (eg, Er3+ or Tm3+), promoting them to excited states from which they emit high-energy photons.126,132,133 Core-shell structures are a commonly used strategy for optimizing luminescence performance. For example, coating a NaYF4 shell onto a NaYF4:Yb, Er core reduces non-radiative energy losses caused by surface defects, thereby enhancing luminescence efficiency.134 Co-doping with other lanthanide ions such as Tm3+ or Ho3+, or transition metal ions such as Mn2+, can further tune the emission wavelength and intensity to meet the optical requirements of different applications.135

Structural and Functional Synergy in Multi-Metal Composite Systems

Composite systems composed of lanthanide nanoparticles and transition or noble metals can significantly enhance material functionality through synergistic design of composition and structure. This synergistic effect is typically realized through three structural forms. In alloy-type composites, different metal atoms are uniformly integrated at the lattice level; for example, LaNi alloy nanoparticles combine the hydrogen storage capacity of lanthanum with the catalytic properties of nickel.136 In core-shell structures, functional components are precisely separated in space; for example, in the Au@LaPO4:Ce, Tb configuration, the surface plasmon resonance of the gold core significantly enhances the luminescence signal of the lanthanide ions in the shell.127,128,137 In heterojunction-type composites, different semiconductor materials form band alignments at their interfaces; for example, the LaFeO3/TiO2 heterojunction significantly improves photocatalytic performance by promoting directional separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs and enhancing interfacial charge transfer.129,138,139

Single-Atom Lanthanide Catalysts

Single-atom catalysts disperse metal active centers atomically on supports, achieving maximized atom utilization and uniform coordination environments.140 The selection of active centers has expanded from transition metals to lanthanide elements, whose 4f electron configurations endow them with distinct electronic energy levels and catalytic behaviors.141 A recently developed molten nitrite method enables atomic-level dispersion of various lanthanide elements, including La, Ce, Pr, Nd, and Lu, on multiple types of supports.142 In ROS-related catalytic reactions, studies on La single-atom doped Co3O4 have shown that La atoms selectively substitute Co3+ at octahedral sites, inducing local lattice distortion and reducing the oxygen vacancy formation energy by 1.3 eV, which increases the conversion rate of benzyl alcohol oxidation by 4.5-fold.130 Second-shell sulfur coordination engineering has constructed La-N6-S2 configured single-atom catalysts that exhibit excellent performance in both half-wave potential and cycling stability.143 Ce single-atom nanozymes have also been prepared by immobilizing Ce in ZIF-8 precursors, demonstrating cascaded self-circulating enzyme-like activities.144 The distinct advantages and limitations of these different chemical compositions are summarized in Table 8.

Targeting Ligand Modification: Enhancing Biorecognition and Enrichment Capability

Conjugating specific recognition molecules onto the nanoparticle surface enables effective enrichment at lesion sites.149 Commonly used targeting ligands include antibodies, peptides, and nucleic acid aptamers, which recognize and bind to overexpressed receptors on diseased cell surfaces.150,151 Folic acid-modified UCNPs enable targeting of tumor cells with high folate receptor expression.152 Antibody-modified lanthanide nanoparticles can precisely recognize cancer cell surface antigens, enhancing imaging signals and synergizing with immunotherapy.145 Conjugation techniques include covalent bonding, electrostatic adsorption, and biotin-streptavidin systems.153

Stimuli-Responsive Coatings: Controlled Release and Activation

Stimuli-responsive coatings play a critical role in drug release control and functional activation, encompassing pH-responsive, temperature-responsive and light-responsive.154 pH-responsive coatings degrade and release drugs in the mildly acidic tumor microenvironment.146 Temperature-sensitive coatings utilize poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) to undergo hydrophilic-hydrophobic transitions upon local heating, thereby modulating drug release.147 Light-responsive coatings activate upconversion luminescence or thermal responses under near-infrared irradiation, enabling non-invasive remotely controlled imaging and therapy.155

Near-Infrared II Imaging Functionalization

The near-infrared II window (1000–1700 nm) offers significant advantages for deep-tissue imaging due to its deep penetration, weak scattering, and low autofluorescence.156 Lanthanide-doped nanomaterials generate narrow-band emission in this window, enabling precise tumor localization through conjugation with targeting moieties.157 Dye sensitization strategies load indocyanine green into MOF pores to sensitize lanthanide nanoparticles, achieving a 41-fold enhancement in upconversion fluorescence.158 Orthogonal excitation platforms emit 1530 nm fluorescence for real-time tracking under 808 nm excitation, and switch to 940 nm excitation to generate 362 nm emission for photosensitizer activation.148 Multiplexed systems achieve NIR-IIb imaging-guided photodynamic therapy and real-time apoptosis feedback through dual-wavelength excitation.159 These materials show promise for applications in microtumor detection, boundary delineation, and lymph node localization.

Construction of Theranostic Systems

Lanthanide nanoparticles, owing to their optical properties, tunable surfaces, and biocompatibility, have become ideal platforms for theranostic applications.160 Upconversion luminescence enables fluorescence imaging, while Gd3+ provides MRI contrast capability. The same nanocarrier can be loaded with chemotherapeutic drugs, photothermal agents, or photosensitizers to achieve combination therapy.16 Doxorubicin loaded into folic acid-targeted UCNPs enables near-infrared-triggered drug release and photothermal effects, with real-time tracking of drug distribution via upconversion luminescence.161 Building on this foundation, the system has been extended to integrate imaging with modulation of novel cell death pathways. Direct triplet-state energy converters utilize energy transfer from Nd3+ to photosensitizer triplet states, where the generated ROS synergize with resveratrol to induce mitophagy and ferroptosis.162 Artificial killer cells integrate lanthanide nanoparticles into hybrid lipid membranes to mimic immune cell functions, with ultrasound-triggered copper ion release.163 Rare earth oxide platforms induce GSDME-dependent pyroptosis through defect engineering.164 Bimetallic rare earth platforms trigger necroptosis via DNA damage and activate the cGAS-STING pathway.165 La-BFO piezoelectric nanozymes synergize with ROS to induce pyroptosis through band gap narrowing and oxygen vacancies.24 Lanthanide nanoparticles have thus expanded from simple delivery carriers to theranostic platforms that actively participate in the regulation of cell fate.

Biodegradable Lanthanide Nanosystems

Biodegradability represents a critical bottleneck constraining the clinical translation of lanthanide nanoparticles. Upconversion nanocrystals such as β-NaYF4:Yb, Er possess rigid lattices that cannot be effectively degraded in vivo. Current strategies impart degradability to materials by reducing crystallinity, decreasing size, or introducing hydrolyzable components. Introducing elements such as Zr into fluoride matrices weakens lattice rigidity, and Na3ZrF7 serves as a “soft-lattice” degradable host for upconversion and downshifting doping.166,167 Lanthanide nucleotide self-assembled nanoparticles feature amorphous structures with tunable sizes and undergo responsive degradation under acidic pH and H2O2 stimulation.168 Natural biopolymer coatings improve dispersion and stability, while biodegradable polyesters encapsulate upconversion particles to achieve complete matrix degradation.169

Artificial Intelligence-Assisted Nanomaterial Design

The parameter space of lanthanide element combinations is extremely large, making traditional trial-and-error approaches inefficient. Machine learning provides a viable pathway for screening optimal solutions from massive possibilities.170,171 In performance prediction, the combination of sonochemical synthesis and machine learning modeling has been successfully applied to predict the antioxidant activity of La-doped CeO2 nanoparticles.172 The random forest model achieved a prediction accuracy of 96.35% for Nd-doped CeO2.173 In structural optimization, a heterogeneous graph neural network trained on over 6,000 samples of upconversion nanoparticle emission spectra identified structures with emission intensities 6.5-fold higher than the best sample in the training set through gradient optimization.174 In toxicity assessment, machine learning models have been used to predict the cytotoxicity and aggregation tendency of lanthanide-doped nanomaterials.175 These advances indicate that establishing predictive models encompassing elemental composition, surface defect states, and ROS regulatory activity has become preliminarily feasible.

Core Challenges in Biosafety and Clinical Translation

The clinical translation of lanthanide nanoparticles is constrained by biosafety and pharmacokinetic concerns. Existing toxicity data remain insufficient, and some findings are contradictory.

Long-Term Accumulation and Ion Leakage

After entering the body, lanthanide nanoparticles are primarily captured by the mononuclear phagocyte system. They accumulate extensively in the liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, and their clearance efficiency depends on particle size and surface coating. Nanoceria with particle sizes of 2–10 nm and doses not exceeding 5 mg/kg have shown no systemic toxicity in short-term studies, while higher doses or different exposure routes may induce inflammation or organ dysfunction.176 Significant differences exist among elements. Gd-based nanoparticles can induce metabolic disorders and kidney injury, with PEG modification improving biocompatibility.177 Conventional gadolinium-based contrast agents carry a risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in patients with renal impairment.178 La2O3 primarily causes oxidative stress at low doses but triggers calcium ion imbalance and neuronal death at high doses.179 Ion leakage is a major source of long-term toxicity. The strongly acidic environment of phagolysosomes can slowly etch the nanocrystal lattice over weeks to months, releasing free lanthanide ions.180 Due to its ionic radius being similar to that of Ca2+, Gd3+ can competitively bind to calmodulin and calcium channels, interfering with myocardial electrophysiology and synaptic transmission.181 Ion shedding from rare earth oxide nanoparticles can also lead to phosphate complexation, triggering NLRP3 inflammasome activation.180

Protein Corona Formation and Immune Responses

Upon entering biological fluids, nanoparticles rapidly adsorb proteins onto their surfaces to form a protein corona, which can shield surface active sites, weaken catalytic functions, and mask targeting ligands, thereby compromising target cell recognition.182,183 Complement proteins are often enriched in the protein corona, and the enrichment of C1q alone can activate the classical complement pathway.184,185 Protein corona adsorption is negatively correlated with NLRP3 inflammasome activation, as it reduces nanoparticle-induced lysosomal damage.186,187 The protein corona can both initiate innate immune responses through complement activation and mitigate excessive inflammation by suppressing the inflammasome. These two aspects must therefore be evaluated separately in safety assessments.183,186

Dosage Extrapolation and Regulatory Standardization

Existing data on maximum tolerated dose (MTD) are extremely limited and highly dispersed. The median lethal dose of Gd-based nanocomposites is 118.2 mg/kg, and continuous administration at 59.1 mg/kg for 14 days reduces survival to 50%.188 La2O3 elevates malondialdehyde by 40- to 80-fold at low doses (60 mg/kg), while high doses (300 mg/kg) trigger calcium-potassium imbalance and neuronal death, indicating that the same material can act through entirely different mechanisms across different dose ranges.179 Dose extrapolation from animals to humans faces substantial challenges, as body surface area-based conversion does not account for interspecies pharmacokinetic differences or protein corona effects.189 MTD differences among elements, particle sizes, and surface modifications can reach several- to tens-fold, and systematic cross-comparative data are currently lacking. The field also lacks standardized reference materials, and studies vary considerably in particle size, morphology, surface chemistry, and doping concentration, making experimental results difficult to compare. Current nanotoxicology frameworks are primarily designed for conventional nanomaterials and fail to adequately account for the non-conventional toxicity mechanisms that may arise from the unique 4f electron configurations and enzyme-like activities of lanthanide elements.

Conclusions and Perspectives

Lanthanide nanoparticles, with their reversible redox properties and functionalizable surfaces, provide a unique material platform for the precise regulation of reactive oxygen species. These materials exhibit remarkable mechanistic diversity. Through band structure engineering, oxygen vacancy engineering, and ligand chemistry design, they enable both directed ROS generation and sustained ROS scavenging. However, fundamental differences exist among elements in catalytic direction, valence reversibility, and scavenging pathways, rooted in the deep-seated mechanisms of 4f electron configurations and lattice defect chemistry. At the application level, lanthanide nanoparticles have demonstrated broad prospects in oxidative stress protection, tumor therapy, antibacterial applications, and imaging, with strategies such as upconversion luminescence, piezocatalysis, and theranostics continuously expanding their functional boundaries. In material design, single-atom catalysts, near-infrared II imaging functionalization, biodegradable systems, and artificial intelligence-assisted rational design are driving the field from traditional empirical exploration toward atomically precise construction and data-driven prediction.

Despite these advances, the path from fundamental research to clinical application for lanthanide nanoparticles remains long. Future developments require continued efforts in several directions. In material design, quantitative structure-activity relationships linking elemental composition, surface defect states, and ROS regulatory activity need to be established, enabling material screening under more clearly defined structure-activity relationships rather than purely trial-and-error strategies. At the biological level, a deeper understanding is needed of the true catalytic behavior of lanthanide nanoparticles in the in vivo microenvironment, protein corona effects, and their long-term impacts on the immune system. At the clinical translation level, standardized material reference systems and in vitro-in vivo correlation evaluation models must be established to provide reliable foundations for dosage design and safety assessment. In parallel, the exploration of bio-inspired and sustainable synthesis strategies, along with the convergent development of other emerging directions, may further broaden the translational potential of lanthanide nanoparticles for the diagnosis and treatment of ROS-related diseases.190–192
