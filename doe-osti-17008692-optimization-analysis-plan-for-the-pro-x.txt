Executive Summary
The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), through the NIST Center for Neutron
Research (NCNR), and the U.S. Department of Energy’s National Nuclear Security Administration
(NNSA), through Argonne National Laboratory, are collaborating to apply the Proliferation
Resistance Optimization (PRO-X) framework to the conceptual design of the proposed NIST
Neutron Source (NNS) research reactor. This effort is intended to integrate proliferation resistance
into early design decisions while supporting mission performance, safety, and project practicality,
thereby advancing nonproliferation and reactor mission objectives together rather than treating
them as competing priorities.

The NNS is being developed to replace the aging National Bureau of Standards Reactor and to
sustain and expand an essential national capability in neutron measurement science. The proposed
facility is a 20 MW pool-type research reactor with a compact core, heavy-water reflector, cold
neutron sources, thermal beam capabilities, and irradiation facilities. Because the project remains
in conceptual design, this collaboration provides a timely opportunity to influence major design
choices before engineering, licensing, and procurement decisions become fixed.

This analysis plan establishes a structured, multi-objective approach for comparing design
candidates with a baseline design. The optimization focuses on three complementary objectives:
strengthening proliferation resistance by reducing the production, accumulation, accessibility, and
attractiveness of special fissionable material; preserving or enhancing reactor mission
performance; and maintaining adequate safety margins under representative operating conditions.
The work is intended to inform design refinement and decision-making during the pre-licensing
stage, not to provide final regulatory analyses.

The principal areas of analysis include fuel system selection, fuel assembly design, fuel
management and shuffling strategies, power-peaking mitigation, cooling-capacity rightsizing, and
the configuration of key experimental capabilities. Across these areas, optimization emphasizes
practical and implementable design improvements that align reactor capabilities with declared
mission needs while avoiding unnecessary excess in material inventory, fissile material
throughput, power, cooling capacity, or facility complexity.

For NIST and NNSA stakeholders, this effort provides a clear and traceable technical basis for
high-consequence design and procurement decisions. The expected outcomes include comparative
assessments and decision-ready recommendations that support conceptual design refinement,
strengthen future vendor engagement, and help ensure that nonproliferation objectives are
incorporated directly into project requirements and acquisition planning.

This collaboration is intended to strengthen the NNS project while design changes remain most
feasible. By integrating proliferation resistance early and systematically with performance and
safety considerations, the effort supports development of a more secure, efficient, and mission-
effective reactor design that advances both national scientific capability and U.S. nonproliferation
objectives. Beyond the NNS project, the PRO-X/NCNR collaboration establishes a practical,
mission-driven model for future partnerships between NNSA and reactor developers, supporting
NNSA’s broader nonproliferation goals by demonstrating how proliferation resistance can be
enhanced while supporting reactor mission performance, safety, and operational effectiveness.

1. Introduction
In October 2024, the U.S Department of Commerce’s National Institute of Standards and
Technology (NIST) and the U.S. Department of Energy’s National Nuclear Security
Administration (NNSA) signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) establishing a
framework for technical collaboration on the proliferation resistance (PR) optimization of the new
NIST Neutron Source (NNS) research reactor. The MOU reflects a shared commitment to
strengthening nonproliferation outcomes by integrating proliferation resistance considerations
directly into the design and operation of the NNS.

Under the MOU, NIST identified their Center for Neutron Research (NCNR) as the implementing
organization for NIST, while NNSA designated Argonne National Laboratory (Argonne) to carry
out technical activities on their behalf. The collaboration focuses on applying the Proliferation
Resistance Optimization (PRO-X) program framework to the design optimization of the proposed
new research reactor to be constructed by NIST, with the objective of enhancing proliferation
resistance while supporting mission performance, safety, and project constraints. The approaches
and insights developed through this effort are also intended to inform the conceptual design of the
NNS as bid specifications are developed for future procurement.

This document describes the optimization approach developed in support of this collaboration and
provides a structured framework for integrating proliferation resistance considerations into near-
term design decisions for the NNS reactor.

1.1 Overview of PRO-X and PRO-RR
The Proliferation Resistance Optimization (PRO-X) program, led by NNSA, seeks to minimize
the production, accumulation, and accessibility of special fissionable material (SFM) while
supporting peaceful-use missions and assuring adequate safety. Within this effort, PRO-RR applies
the PRO-X approach specifically to research reactor systems.

PRO-X is most effective when applied early in a project’s design lifecycle, before major technical,
operational, and procurement decisions become fixed. Rather than relying on late-stage
modifications or add-on measures, the approach uses technical analysis and engineering judgment
to identify practical design options within the specific mission, design maturity, and constraints of
each reactor project. The process seeks win-win opportunities for design improvements that
simultaneously strengthen proliferation resistance, improve mission performance and efficiency,
and preserve safety and overall project practicality.

PRO-RR applies the PRO-X framework tailored to the technical and operational characteristics of
each research reactor project.

 7

1.2 Overview of the NIST NNS Project
NCNR houses the National Bureau of Standards Reactor (NBSR), an aging 20 MW high
performance research reactor, primarily used for out-of-core neutron scattering and irradiation
experiments and for in-core neutron activation (NAA) analysis experiments. The NBSR supports
NCNR’s mission of ensuring the availability of neutron measurement capabilities for U.S.
researchers from various sectors, and it has been a critical pillar in providing that capability to the
nation since 1967. Despite upgrades, demand for neutron scattering research now exceeds NCNR’s
existing capacity, fulfilling only a third of the requested beam time. This growing demand, coupled
with the notable aging of NBSR, underscores the need for a replacement reactor.

The NNS (Figure 1-1) is being developed by NCNR to replace the NBSR as the center’s primary
reactor-based source for out-of-core neutron measurements and to provide modernized and
improved NAA measurement facilities. The NNS was designed with expanded instrument capacity
and flexibility to support future neutron science needs, which were thoroughly explored in NIST
SP 2100-07 [1]. The NNS design borrows from more recent demonstrated technologies for high
performance research reactors in the world, making use of significantly more advanced
knowledge-based technologies that were not available when the NBSR was originally developed.

Figure 1-1 Rendering of the NNS facility layout showing the two cold neutron guide halls (blue)
 and one thermal guide hall (red) surrounding the reactor.

The NNS reactor (Figure 1-2) is conceptualized as a compact core moderated and cooled by light
water, surrounded by a heavy water reflector tank within an open pool of light water. The heavy
water tank contains two state-of-the-art liquid deuterium (LD2) cold neutron sources, each
supplying the demand of one of the two guide halls. The cold sources are designed to be vertically
accessible to more effectively accommodate upgrades when future ones are available or needed.
The compact core consists of a 3×3 rectangular lattice of nine total fuel assemblies, each nominally
fueled with 21 curved fuel plates containing U-10Mo alloy of high assay low-enriched uranium
(HALEU). The core is cooled with forced convection upflow and incorporates passive safety
features such as flap valves that automatically establish a flow path between the core and the pool
in the case of a loss of coolant accident.

 8

 Figure 1-2. An illustration of the NNS reactor showing the core, the two cold sources, the
 reflector tank, and some of the relevant piping connected.

The NNS project has successfully passed the pre-conceptual design phase and is currently in the
conceptual phase, where design choices are still being refined before progression to the
engineering design phase. The conceptual design phase focuses on verification and, when possible,
validation of the models, assessment of fuel alternatives, the incorporation of more experimental
capabilities like rabbit tubes, and the progression of the acquisition, including the issuance of the
Request for Information (RFI) and drafting of the Statement of Work (SOW) for bidding in the
market. The NCNR is looking forward to working with Argonne National Laboratory and NNSA
on the progression of this vital project to American measurement and nuclear technology
infrastructure.

Because the NNS project is still in conceptual design, optimization can have significant practical
influence on the reactor’s evolving configuration. Key decisions related to fuel options, fuel
management, experimental capabilities, system sizing, and procurement inputs are still being
developed, creating an opportunity to identify design improvements before major engineering and
acquisition decisions become more fixed. The next section defines the purpose, scope, and
boundaries of that optimization effort.

1.3 Expected Outcomes and Benefits of the Partnership
This collaboration leverages the combined expertise of NIST NCNR and the NNSA PRO-RR
technical team to optimize the NNS reactor design for performance, safety, and proliferation
resistance while contributing to the timely, cost-effective development of the NNS. NNS will

 9

benefit from a stronger technical basis for evaluating design options1 as well as clearer engineering
constraints and proliferation resistance considerations to inform future vendor engagement.

Reactor facility optimization systematically identifies and compares design options that reduce the
use, production, attractiveness, and accessibility of SFM. This includes approaches that improve
annual fuel utilization, reduce unnecessary fuel throughput, minimize fuel inventory consistent
with mission requirements, manage excess reactivity, promote flatter power and flux distributions,
and right-size core power, cooling capacity, and irradiation volumes to declared mission needs.

From a reactor project perspective, optimization supports design progression from the established
conceptual configuration toward a more detailed and implementable design. Structured evaluation
of the combined space of performance, proliferation resistance, and safety enables clearer
prioritization of design options, more focused analysis, and early identification of features that
offer “win-win” benefits. This clarifies priorities for additional analysis and supports more
effective vendor engagement. It also reduces downstream technical and schedule risk by helping
avoid over-designed systems and unnecessary conservatism.

The optimization also supports design realism by aligning nonproliferation objectives with long-
term usability, maintainability, and regulatory expectations. It favors design options that enhance
detectability of anomalies, limit excess capacity beyond mission requirements, and support stable,
reliable, and economical operation.

The outcome of this partnership will include:

 • Providing a technical basis for strengthening proliferation resistance in the NNS reactor
 design,
 • Improving the technical basis for design decisions affecting performance and safety,
 • Clarifying engineering constraints and proliferation resistance objectives for future
 vendor engagement,
 • Supporting more effective conceptual design refinement,
 • Supporting cost-efficient project delivery.

2. Purpose and Scope
This section explains the rationale for performing this work during the conceptual design phase
and identifies the scope and boundaries of the optimization effort conducted under the PRO-X
collaboration between NCNR and Argonne.

2.1 Strategic Significance
The optimization effort informs high-consequence design choices during the conceptual design
phase, when the reactor design can still evolve. Its purpose is to integrate proliferation resistance
into early design decisions for NNS, through practical, implementable improvements that remain
consistent with mission, safety, and project objectives. Figure 2-1 illustrates the decreasing
flexibility to influence major design choices as the project progresses from conceptual design into
later phases. Conducting this work during conceptual design allows for near-term design
refinement without disrupting established project assumptions, schedules, or regulatory pathways.
It also supports informed decision making by project leadership as NNS progresses toward
procurement and vendor engagement.

 Figure 2-1. PRO-X impact by project phase.

2.2 Scope, Boundaries, and Exclusions
The optimization activity focuses on identifying and evaluating design options that strengthen
proliferation resistance while supporting peaceful-use missions. It is performed within a defined
design space shaped by project maturity, licensing expectations, operational limits, and
procurement constraints. Analyses examine the integrated behavior of the whole reactor system
and address fuel management, irradiation capability, cooling capacity, operating cycles, and
material inventories.

 11

The following are outside the scope of this work: formal calculations for regulatory submittals;
development or qualification of new fuels or assembly designs; and detailed mechanical,
structural, or balance-of-plant design or analysis. The overall control system concept selected by
NCNR is treated as fixed, but limited evaluation of operational strategies within the conceptual
framework may be considered (such as control grouping or programming approaches). Detailed
control hardware design or alternative control system architectures are also excluded. For cooling
systems, scope is limited to establishing appropriate cooling capacity requirements for the primary
reactor cooling system and individual irradiation positions (in-core and ex-core); detailed
mechanical design of cooling systems is excluded.

Neutronic analyses will characterize reactivity behavior, power and flux distributions, fuel
utilization, and nuclear material inventories to quantify impacts on mission performance,
proliferation resistance, and safety. Particular emphasis will be placed on multi-cycle and
equilibrium-cycle behavior to ensure that candidate designs are assessed under representative
operating conditions. Calculations are conducted with enough fidelity to support consistent
comparison among candidate designs and identification of trends, not the establishment of
licensing margins or final design parameters.

Steady-state thermal-hydraulics analyses will be used to assess whether candidate designs provide
sufficient margin to thermal safety limits. These analyses are intended to support viability
screening and design refinement during the conceptual phase. Transient accident analysis is
outside the scope of this effort because the primary objective is to compare candidate
configurations under representative steady-state operating conditions. Nevertheless, steady-state
results can provide useful screening information by identifying designs that approach established
thermal-hydraulic limits. Comprehensive analyses required for licensing and preparation of
regulatory documents are outside of scope, but targeted code-to-code comparisons may be
included as a supplementary activity to support the technical basis for NCNR’s future regulatory
discussions.

Together, the optimization analyses provide a coherent, physics-based foundation for evaluating
proliferation resistance, performance, and safety consistent with the intent of a pre-licensing
optimization effort. Modeling methodologies, uncertainty treatment, and licensing-grade analyses
are addressed separately in dedicated analysis plans or in downstream project phases.

3. Optimization Framework
To support the NNS optimization study, this section defines the technical framework used to
evaluate candidate designs. The framework is designed to enable consistent assessment of
candidate designs using a common basis while accounting for realistic project constraints. It also
ensures that proliferation resistance is considered together with reactor performance and safety so
that opportunities to improve multiple objectives simultaneously can be identified.

3.1 Optimization Philosophy and System Rightsizing
The optimization study for NNS is anchored in a facility-level rightsizing philosophy that seeks
an appropriate balance among mission effectiveness, proliferation resistance, safety, and overall
project practicality. For a neutron source reactor, the objective is not simply to maximize reactor
power, but to provide the neutron and gamma environments needed to support a broad range of
user missions. In this context, rightsizing means selecting core and plant capabilities—such as
irradiation capacity, cycle length, and heat removal capability—so they are adequate to satisfy
mission, functional and safety needs, while avoiding capability that is not justified by those needs.
This rightsizing approach helps limit unnecessary complexity, oversized systems, and avoidable
material throughput that could otherwise increase proliferation concerns or complicate
implementation.

Consistent with this philosophy, optimization focuses on identifying practical design
improvements that remain feasible within the defined optimization space. Proliferation resistance
is addressed by considering opportunities to reduce SFM generation and diversion pathways as
well as design features that can improve the detectability of anomalous or off-normal conditions.
The comparative evaluation approach is described in Section 3.3.

3.2 NNS Baseline Design Definition
All optimization analyses are anchored to a formally identified baseline design for the NNS. This
baseline represents the current level of design maturity for the project and captures the principal
design decisions made to date, including the selected reactor concept, core layout, reactivity
control strategy, and reliance on a fuel assembly design consistent with the U.S. High Performance
Research Reactor (USHPRR) conversion program plan for U-10Mo fuel. Establishing this baseline
is important because it creates a consistent and traceable benchmark against which proposed design
changes can be assessed. It enables candidate options to be compared on a common basis, helps
distinguish incremental improvements from more fundamental departures, and supports a
transparent evaluation of benefits, tradeoffs, and implementation impacts within the optimization
study.

The baseline NNS configuration is a 20 MW pool-type research reactor featuring a compact core
cooled by light water and positioned within a vertical core chimney. Surrounding the chimney is a
heavy-water reflector tank that contains two cold neutron sources and neutron beam tubes. These
reactor components are located at the bottom of a reactor pool filled with demineralized light water.

 13

Figure 3-1 illustrates the reflector tank internals, including the central core, cold sources, and beam
tubes.

 Figure 3-1 Reflector tank with core, cold sources, and beam tubes. [2]

The baseline core design, designated Box9, consists of nine fuel assemblies arranged in a compact
3×3 array and controlled by six hafnium control blades as shown in Figure 3-2. Light water flows
upward through the core, serving both as the primary coolant and as the neutron moderator, before
being routed to the primary cooling system for heat removal. This compact layout establishes the
baseline geometry for neutronic, thermal-hydraulic, and safety analyses used throughout the
optimization study.

 Figure 3-2. Planar view of the NNS core. [2]

Each fuel assembly has an approximately square cross section and contains 21 curved fuel plates.
The baseline fuel form is based on U-10Mo foil with zirconium interlayers and aluminum cladding.

 14

Burnable poison is also included in the baseline assembly as cadmium wires to assist in controlling
excess reactivity and limiting power peaking, especially for fresh fuel.

The baseline core follows a three-batch fuel management strategy. During each operating cycle,
three fresh fuel assemblies are inserted into the core, while assemblies that have already been
irradiated are relocated to new positions. Each assembly remains in the core for three cycles before
discharge. This approach provides a consistent and practical loading pattern for equilibrium
operation and serves as the baseline fuel management scheme for the optimization effort.

Figure 3-3 provides a visual illustration of the fuel management scheme: Three fresh assemblies
(identified as A-a2, B-b2, and C-c2 and green in the figure) are loaded each cycle and then moved
to another position (A'-c1, B'-b3, and C'-a1, yellow) in the next cycle and then in the third and last
cycle moved to a final position (A"-a3, B"-b1, and C"-c3, red). The figure also uses row/column
coordinates to locate the position of the assemblies.

 Figure 3-3. NNS fuel shuffling scheme. [2]

For analytical purposes, each fuel cycle is represented using a sequence of depletion state points
spanning startup (SU), beginning of cycle (BOC), intermediate burnup conditions (MOC), and end
of cycle (EOC). These state points provide the basis for evaluating reactivity behavior, required
control blade positions, power peaking trends, and isotopic changes over the course of irradiation.
Q2 and Q4 are two additional intermediate state points to decrease the possible errors that may
result from long burnup steps. Figure 3-4 provides a visual description of the cycle states and cycle
state points.

3.3 Comparative Evaluation Approach

Design alternatives are compared to the baseline design within a multi-objective framework, using
a traceable set of metrics tied to proliferation resistance, reactor and experimental performance,
and adequate safety margins. This comparison provides the basis for identifying practical design
improvements within the pre-licensing scope of the optimization effort.

Comparisons focus on key operational and utilization aspects of the reactor, including fuel
management strategies, irradiation capability, cooling capacity requirements, operating cycles, and
nuclear material inventories. This approach ensures that candidate designs are considered within
realistic project constraints, while still capturing the coupled neutronic, thermal-hydraulic, and
operational impacts.

Comparisons are supported by physics-based neutronics and thermal-hydraulics analyses applied
at a level of fidelity sufficient to quantify the impacts of design options on defined metrics and to
provide a transparent technical basis for recommendations. Neutronics analyses are used to
quantify reactivity behavior, power and flux distributions, fuel utilization and cycle characteristics,
and inventories and isotopic composition of SFM. Thermal-hydraulic analyses are used, as needed,
to assess feasibility within established operational and safety envelopes and to quantify margins to
key limiting phenomena under representative steady-state conditions.

Mandatory constraints identified in Section 5 are treated as screening conditions. Candidate
designs that cannot satisfy the mandatory constraints are not pursued, while design variants that
satisfy those constraints are compared based on their demonstrated impacts on the applicable
metrics and objectives.

4. Optimization Objectives and Metrics
This section presents the objectives used to direct the optimization effort and outlines the metrics
applied to compare design alternatives. The selected metrics represent attributes relevant to
proliferation resistance, mission performance, and safety at the conceptual design stage. They are
intended to support structured comparison among design options, rather than to establish
prescriptive acceptance criteria or provide the formal design analyses required for licensing and
safety documentation.

4.1 Proliferation Resistance Objectives
A primary objective of PRO-X is to reduce the production, accumulation, and attractiveness of
special fissionable material associated with reactor operation. This objective is evaluated here
using metrics directly related to total plutonium production and plutonium isotopic composition.

Plutonium is generated in reactors through neutron capture in U-238. Higher burnup, resulting
from longer cycle lengths and/or higher power density, generally increases total plutonium
production but also raises the Pu-240 fraction, making the plutonium less attractive for misuse.
Lower burnup, associated with lower power density and shorter cycles, produces less plutonium
overall but with a higher Pu-239 fraction. Accordingly, two complementary proliferation resistance
metrics are used: total plutonium production and plutonium isotopic composition, which together
reflect both material quantity and attractiveness. Plutonium quality is characterized by the fractions
of Pu-239 and Pu-240 in the total plutonium inventory of discharged fuel assemblies. These values
are derived from discharged-fuel compositions calculated through core depletion analyses for each
candidate configuration and are used to compare alternatives against the baseline.

The number of fresh fuel assemblies and mass of HALEU used per year are also of significance to
proliferation resistance. Because of their importance to the economics of effective reactor
operation, the focus on these two metrics is described as performance objectives. This is a clear
example of “win-win” optimization where proliferation resistance and performance goals are in
complete alignment.

4.2 Performance Objectives
The optimization framework considers proliferation resistance together with reactor performance
and safe, practical, and efficient operation. For NNS, performance must be evaluated in terms of
its ability to fulfill the neutron science mission, including support for cold neutron sources, thermal
beam instruments, neutron guides, irradiation facilities, and future experimental expansion, while
maintaining effective core operation and fuel management.

Performance is therefore assessed using metrics that capture both core utilization and experimental
capability. These include fuel utilization measures and neutron flux characteristics at relevant
experimental locations derived from cycle depletion behavior.

 17

The following subsections describe the principal performance objectives and associated
comparison metrics.

4.2.1 Core Design and Fuel Utilization

Improved fuel utilization offers both environmental and economic benefits. Higher discharge
burnup reduces the number of fuel assemblies consumed per unit of useful neutron production,
thereby lowering spent fuel generation. It also reduces fresh fuel demand and associated fuel cycle
costs.

A desirable feature of the core design is a more uniform burnup distribution across the fuel. In
research reactors, this can be promoted through core layouts and shuffling strategies that flatten
neutron flux and better balance power generation. Within the performance objectives for NNS, the
following metrics are used to evaluate fuel utilization and the effectiveness of alternative fuel
management schemes:

 • Fresh fuel consumed annually. The number of fresh fuel assemblies required per year,
 which is a major contributor to operating cost and directly affects the number of spent
 assemblies requiring storage and disposition.

 • Cycle length. Longer operating cycles, when supported by appropriate shuffling strategies,
 generally enable higher burnup and improved fuel utilization.

 • Annual equivalent full-power days. The number of days per year that the reactor can
 operate at full power and deliver experimental flux.

 • Assembly-average discharge burnup. The total burnup of discharged fuel assemblies in
 a cycle divided by the number of discharged assemblies.

 • Peak-to-average plate burnup. For a given fuel assembly, the ratio of the highest plate
 burnup to the average plate burnup at end of cycle.

 • Peak-to-average segment burnup. In the depletion model, each plate is subdivided
 laterally and axially to represent local power peaking and to track the local burnup of fissile
 material. This metric is the ratio of the maximum local segment burnup to the average
 segment burnup in the assembly.

 • Fuel handling operations per cycle. The number of fuel handling operations is of interest
 to both operational simplicity and proliferation resistance. Comparisons of shuffling
 schemes consider this metric.

These metrics are obtained from cycle-specific and equilibrium-cycle depletion analyses and are
applied consistently across candidate fuel management options.

4.2.2 Cold Neutron Sources (CNS)

The performance objectives for the cold neutron source system are centered on establishing NNS
as a competitive continuous neutron source facility for cold neutron science while preserving
flexibility for future growth.

A principal objective is to achieve the peak unperturbed thermal neutron flux in the reflector
region. At the NNS design power of 20 MW, the target value for this metric is at least
51014 cm-2, s-1. Because CNS performance depends strongly on the thermal neutron flux available
in the reflector, candidate reactor and reflector configurations are compared in part with their
ability to achieve this objective.

The reactor should also provide sufficient reflector volume and geometric flexibility to
accommodate multiple large-volume cold neutron sources, multiple beam tubes viewing each
source, and the associated guide layout. CNS configurations and locations should maximize source
brightness while remaining compatible with practical cooling requirements and safe, stable
operation.

In addition to reflector flux, the CNS performance objectives include the ability to accommodate
multiple CNS units, support efficient beam extraction and guide layout, provide capacity for a
large cold neutron instrument suite, and maintain flexibility for future expansion, installation,
maintenance, and safe operation. These broader considerations are retained because they
materially affect the suitability of a given reactor/reflector configuration as a practical CNS
platform.

4.2.3 Experimental Capabilities Other than CNS

Beyond the cold neutron source mission, the reactor should support a broad set of experimental
capabilities that include thermal neutron beams, thermal neutron guides, and near-core irradiation
facilities. These capabilities should be incorporated in a way that preserves the primary neutron
science mission, satisfies measurement needs for standard reference materials work at NIST,
expands user access, and avoids significant adverse impacts on CNS performance or overall
reactor operation.

For thermal neutron applications, a key objective is to preserve at least the current suite of NBSR
thermal neutron beam instruments while maintaining the option to add additional thermal neutron
capabilities. The design should therefore accommodate a minimum of six thermal neutron
instruments and allow for future expansion where practicable. Thermal beam tubes and, where
advantageous, thermal neutron guides should be arranged to maximize usable flux for thermal
neutron instruments, improve instrument siting flexibility, and reduce, where possible, background
conditions in experimental areas.

The placement and geometry of thermal beam tubes should be selected so that beam entrances are
positioned as close as practical to the reflector flux peak, while avoiding unacceptable interference
with the cold neutron system. Thermal beam provisions should be designed such that their impact
on flux in the cold sources is minimized. This establishes a performance objective of balancing
thermal beam capability against preservation of CNS effectiveness. Accordingly, thermal beam

 19

options are evaluated based on usable neutron flux, beam quality, and impact on cold source
performance.

The reactor should also include near-core irradiation facilities of varying size and function,
including pneumatic rabbit tubes, and experiment tubes located in the reflector tank region. These
facilities should support irradiation and pneumatic transfer applications with negligible detriment
to the cold and thermal neutron beam missions. Accordingly, an important performance objective
is to provide useful irradiation capability without materially degrading neutron beam performance
or reducing reactor availability. Relevant evaluation criteria include neutron flux, spectrum,
irradiation volume, and flux uniformity at the irradiation location.

In particular, to support neutron activation analysis, NNS will require two rabbit tubes favoring
thermal neutron energies, at least one larger rabbit tube to accommodate atypical sample shapes,
and, if possible, at least one rabbit tube favoring epithermal or fast neutron energies. Their
locations and spectral characteristics should provide effective irradiation performance while
remaining compatible with reflector-space constraints and without materially reducing the
effectiveness of beam-based experimental systems.

To support specialized neutron science experiments requiring very low neutron energies, the
reflector tank layout should retain the option for a future ultra-cold neutron source (UCNS), so
long as it does not impose disproportionate penalties on reflector performance, facility complexity,
or reactor operability.

Collectively, these capabilities should broaden the scientific utility of the facility and complement
the primary neutron science missions.

4.3 Safety Objectives
Safety-related objectives focus on assuring adequate thermal-hydraulic margins during steady-
state operation, consistent with established research reactor design best practices and licensing
requirements. Although research reactors are designed to deliver neutron flux rather than thermal
power, the safe removal of heat from the core must always be assured.

NCNR identified the fuel-temperature limit based on plate blistering temperature as a safety
criterion and also evaluated margins to safety-related boiling phenomena: the critical heat flux
(CHF), onset of flow instability (OFI), and onset of nucleate boiling (ONB) [NIST-TN-2323].
These phenomena define practical limits on fuel heat removal and therefore provide meaningful
indicators of operating margin.

Within the optimization framework, candidate core design configurations are compared using
safety-related metrics associated with these phenomena, including:

 • The critical heat flux ratio (CHFR),
 • The onset of flow instability ratio (OFIR),
 • The onset of nucleate boiling ratio (ONBR),
 • The maximum fuel temperature, and

 20

 • The maximum cladding temperature.

These safety metrics are evaluated using thermal-hydraulic analyses based on representative
steady-state operating conditions for each candidate configuration. Transient and accident analysis
is outside the scope of this optimization effort (see Section 2.2); the existing NCNR pre-conceptual
transient and accident analyses [3] provide an existing safety basis against which the applicability
of candidate variants can be considered. Mandatory safety and licensing requirements are treated
as fixed constraints, as discussed in Section 5.4. The purpose of the optimization effort is not to
establish licensing limits, but to identify design options that maintain adequate margins to
established safety criteria while supporting improved proliferation resistance and mission
performance.

Nuclear criticality safety considers the “shutdown margin”, i.e., the margin available for the NNS
to safely shutdown with its most reactive safety/control blade fully withdrawn. Neutronic safety is
treated in these studies by the concept of an “excess reactivity budget”, i.e., the space between
excess reactivity of the uncontrolled core and an adequate shutdown margin. Thus, experimental
device configuration and fuel management strategy include excess reactivity as part of the
objective and constraint comparisons.

4.4 Multi-Objective Optimization
Research reactor optimization involves balancing multiple objectives that must be considered
together rather than reduced to a single performance measure. The PRO-X framework supports
comparison of candidate designs using quantified metrics across all objectives.

Design choices that influence proliferation resistance, mission performance, and safety margins
are often interdependent and nonlinear, so some options can improve multiple objectives
simultaneously. The objective of the optimization framework is to identify configurations where
these effects are aligned or can be balanced within realistic project constraints, while making those
relationships explicit to support informed decision making. Such opportunities may arise when
improvements in reactor performance also reduce operational costs and special fissionable material
throughput, thereby strengthening both proliferation resistance and economic performance.

Candidate options are compared to the baseline design using the metrics described in this section
and the constraints identified in Section 5. Those constraints are treated as fixed screening
conditions, and required mission performance is maintained to ensure that the reactor fulfills its
intended functions. Proliferation resistance remains an explicit consideration throughout the
evaluation, favoring solutions that reduce material attractiveness, improve fuel utilization and
material management, or enhance detectability without introducing undue complexity or
operational burden.

5. Constraints and Fixed Requirements
This section identifies the constraints that bound the optimization space and ensure that proposed
design options remain realistic and executable. These constraints include prior design decisions,
fabrication and qualification limitations, operational considerations, licensing and safety
requirements, and schedule- and procurement-driven factors. They are treated as fixed conditions
for the optimization effort and are not subject to relaxation through design trade studies.

5.1 Prior Design Decisions and Fixed Architectural Features
The optimization effort, described in Section 6, is bounded by prior project decisions that establish
the basic NNS reactor architecture. These decisions — which are not themselves subject to
optimization — include the use of a compact light water-cooled and -moderated core with a heavy-
water reflector tank, and the general mission emphasis on cold and thermal neutron beam
capability. The overall control concept selected by NCNR, e.g., the use of control blades, is treated
as fixed, although evaluation of material alternatives, operating strategies, and design refinements
within that framework may be considered as the design matures. Candidate designs may refine the
baseline design, but they are not intended to revisit the basic reactor concept or replace major
architectural decisions already adopted by the project.

5.2 Fabrication and Qualification Constraints
Candidate designs must remain consistent with fabrication feasibility and the qualification status
of key components. The optimization effort does not include development or qualification of new
materials, fuel forms, or assembly concepts, and therefore focuses on configurations that are
compatible with established or realistically supportable qualification pathways. These constraints
affect the range of allowable design parameters that may be considered. Fabrication practicality is
also relevant to proposed changes in assembly-level and component-level design features. Options
that would depend on unqualified concepts or unrealistic fabrication assumptions are outside the
bounded optimization space for this effort.

5.3 Operational Limits
Operational limits also constrain the optimization space. Candidate configurations must support
the intended mission within practical operating conditions, including the nominal reactor power
level, expected operating cycle structure, fuel management approach, irradiation services, and
stable routine operation. Designs with excessive operational complexity, unstable control behavior,
impractical cooling demands, or capabilities beyond those justified by mission requirements are
not favored. Operational limits are considered together with performance requirements and
proliferation resistance objectives to ensure that candidate options remain operable, maintainable,
licensable, and compatible with the expected operating envelope of the facility. Candidate options
that would introduce unacceptable operational burden or compromise the stability and
predictability of routine reactor operation are outside the bounded optimization space for this
effort.

 23

5.4 Licensing and Safety Constraints
NNS will likely be regulated by the NRC and its requirements associated with the construction
permit (CP) and operating license (OL) process. Licensing and safety requirements impose non-
negotiable constraints on the design space by establishing acceptable operating conditions, safety
margins, and design requirements that candidate configurations must satisfy. These constraints
influence allowable operating power, cooling capacity, fuel design, core configuration, and other
features that affect reactor safety and licensing. Candidate designs must remain consistent with
applicable regulatory requirements, the project’s licensing basis, and the safety envelope
established by prior NCNR analyses.

5.5 RFI/RFP and Supply Chain Constraints
Procurement and supply-chain considerations may impose additional constraints on the design
space as the project progresses. Feedback from the RFI process may identify new design,
fabrication, or implementation limits that should be incorporated into subsequent specifications
for the RFP. Vendor proposals may introduce additional constraints associated with their design
approaches, fabrication capabilities, or construction methods. In later project phases, supply-chain
availability and quality-program requirements may further constrain material and component
selections.

6.1 Fuel System Selection and Fuel Assembly Design
Fuel material selection simultaneously impacts proliferation resistance attractiveness, core
compactness, cycle length, power peaking, and the feasibility of the Box9 footprint. Three
candidates are currently under consideration:

 1. U-10Mo monolithic fuel (baseline): U-Mo alloy foils with Zr interlayer, clad in Al-6061.
 Undergoing qualification under the USHPRR Conversion Program [2, Sec. 3.2.2].

 2. U₃Si₂-Al dispersion fuel (alternative): U-silicide particles in an Al matrix, clad in Al-
 6061. Well-established qualification basis at approved fuel densities; power levels and
 temperatures; expanded envelope currently being tested by the EU. Feasibility being
 considered in [2] and supporting analyses by Celikten, Weiss, and Sahin (2023).

 3. U-7Mo/Al dispersion fuel (alternative): -phase U-7wt%Mo particles in an Al matrix,
 clad in Al-6061. Higher uranium loading than U₃Si₂-Al but susceptible to adverse fuel-
 matrix interaction layer growth; mitigated by Si addition to the matrix. Extensively
 irradiation-tested but not yet fully qualified in operation for high-performance reactor
 conversion.

6.1.1 Uranium Density and Core Compactness

The candidate fuel systems provide different achievable uranium densities and therefore may lead
to different core, assembly, and fuel-management configurations. The baseline U-10Mo design
supports the current compact nine-assembly core. Alternative dispersion fuels may require

 28

adjustments to assembly geometry, fuel loading, core footprint, or operating strategy to achieve
the NNS cycle-length and flux objectives. These interactions make fuel-system selection a primary
coupled optimization variable.

6.1.2 Coupled Design Considerations for the Dispersion Fuel
 Alternatives

If U₃Si₂ or U-7Mo dispersion fuel is selected as the preferred fuel-system option, potential
compensating modifications may need to be considered including:

 • More fuel assemblies (e.g., 4×3 or 4×4 array): may increase available ²³⁵U inventory and
 support longer cycle lengths, but would also enlarge the core and could reduce some of
 the reflector-flux advantages associated with the compact baseline design [2, Fig. 3].

 • Larger assembly cross-sections while retaining the 3×3 array: preserves grid plate
 geometry and overall core arrangement but increases core size, with corresponding
 impacts on core and reflector performance.

 • More plates per assembly or thicker fuel meat, subject to thermal-hydraulic constraints
 on coolant channel width [2, Table 5-2].

 • Shorter cycle length, which may make it more difficult to satisfy the operational and
 mission-performance requirements established for the NNS. [2, Requirement R4.1].

From a proliferation resistance perspective, a larger core may increase fissile inventory and annual
fresh fuel throughput—both relevant to the proliferation resistance assessment. The relative ease
of direct recovery of uranium from the different fresh fuel forms may also inform proliferation
resistance.

6.1.3 Fuel Assembly Geometry Variables

Regardless of fuel type, the fuel assembly geometry presents multiple optimization variables
including the number of plates per assembly, fuel meat and cladding thicknesses, active fuel height,
burnable absorber configuration, and coolant channel geometry.

Changes propagate through neutronics, thermal-hydraulics, and safety analyses. For example,
thicker fuel meat improves U-235 loading but narrows coolant channels, potentially degrading
thermal margins.

6.1.4 Grid Plate Implications

For the baseline U-10Mo case, the 3×3 footprint defines the baseline Box9 core arrangement. For
U₃Si₂, departures from that footprint may need to be considered to maintain required cycle length
and performance. Departures from the baseline footprint will be assessed for their impacts on the
thermal-flux objective at the CNS locations, fissile inventory, plutonium production, and other
applicable metrics. Changes to core geometry also alter the preferred shuffling pattern (Section

6.2.1), burnable absorber effectiveness (Section 6.2.2), and the geometric relationship with
experimental facilities (Section 6.3).

The quantitative parameters and baseline values for the fuel assembly geometry variables are
provided in Appendix A.2.

6.2 Fuel Management Optimization
Fuel management encompasses loading, shuffling, and discharge strategies which directly affect
cycle length, power peaking, burnup uniformity, fissile inventory, plutonium production, and fresh
fuel throughput—all central to both proliferation resistance and performance.

6.2.1 Fuel Shuffling Strategy

The baseline design employs a three-batch, in-out loading scheme in which three fresh assemblies
are loaded into the center column for each cycle and progressively moved to peripheral positions
over three cycles before discharge. This scheme achieves the target cycle length and capacity
factor.

However, several characteristics of the baseline scheme motivate evaluation of alternative fuel
management approaches. Alternative loading and shuffling strategies will be examined to assess
their impacts on fuel utilization, reactor performance, and proliferation resistance relative to the
baseline fuel management strategy.

Alternative fuel management schemes are proposed for evaluation:

Alternative A (Out-In Loading) places fresh fuel at the periphery and moves burned fuel inward
toward the center. This reduces the material attractiveness of the discharged fuel assemblies,
enables additional burnup from the highest-flux center positions, and may improve the burnup
uniformity of discharged assemblies. However, the ability to sustain the target cycle length and the
effect on control blade worth must be verified.

Alternative B (Scatter Loading) distributes fresh fuel across non-adjacent positions rather than
concentrating it in a single column. This approach may reduce core power tilt, improve power
uniformity, and distribute fissile material more uniformly. However, these potential benefits and
the associated impacts on operational complexity and cycle behavior should be evaluated.

The detailed shuffling pathways, baseline characteristics motivating the alternatives, and
evaluation metrics are provided in Appendix A.3.

These two alternatives are not exhaustive; additional shuffling schemes—including rotated loading
patterns and configurations tailored to optimize ex-core performance such as cold source spectrum
and rabbit tube irradiation conditions—will also be considered as the fuel management study
progresses.

 30

6.2.2 Fuel Assembly Features to Limit Power Peaking

Reducing power peaking increases the thermal safety margins and supports more uniform burnup,
which benefits both safety and PR. The baseline design incorporates cadmium burnable absorber
wires that effectively suppress fresh-fuel reactivity early in the cycle but deplete rapidly, causing
a mid-cycle power shift to second-cycle assemblies indicated by preliminary analysis.

Four categories of peaking mitigation features are considered as optimization variables:

 • Discrete burnable absorbers (baseline cadmium wires), with optimization of wire
 material, diameter, placement, and number per assembly.

 • Integral burnable absorbers (applicable to dispersion fuel), where absorber particles are
 distributed throughout the fuel matrix for more spatially uniform flux suppression.

 • Alternative poison wires using materials with different depletion characteristics to
 provide more gradual and sustained reactivity suppression over the assembly lifetime.

 • Water displacers that reduce local moderation in the outermost coolant channels to
 mitigate peripheral plate peaking.

Each option is evaluated against criteria including reactivity swing reduction, peak-to-average
power ratio, impacts on thermal margins, and cycle length preservation. The quantitative peaking
values, baseline thermal margins, and evaluation criteria are provided in Appendix A.4.

6.3 Experimental Capabilities
The experimental facilities are coupled optimization variables whose locations, geometries, and
performance requirements interact with core neutronics, reflector perturbations, and proliferation
resistance considerations. The NNS is mission-driven, with its primary function being the delivery
of cold and thermal neutron beams. Experimental facilities are evaluated together with the core
and reflector to support scientific throughput while accounting for proliferation resistance and
safety considerations.

6.3.1 Cold Neutron Sources

The CNSs are central to the primary neutron-science mission of the NNS. The compact core design
produces a substantially higher peak thermal flux in the reflector than its predecessor NBSR at
equivalent power, because the flux peaks outside the fuel where CNS thimbles can access it [5].
Changes that increase the size of the core may alter this favorable flux distribution and therefore
affect CNS performance.

The cold source configuration presents multiple optimization variables including the number of
sources, their location relative to the core, moderator vessel diameter and height, and re-entrant
cavity geometry. Each variable involves interactions between cold neutron brightness, spectral
quality, nuclear heat load, and the degree of reflector perturbation.

 31

Core–CNS Interaction

The CNSs displace reflector volume and introduce cryogenic materials that perturb the local flux
environment, affecting adjacent beam tubes and nearby fuel. These interactions are assessed using
applicable CNS performance objectives, including the impact of thermal beam tube placement on
CNS flux.

From a proliferation resistance perspective, the cold source configuration supports mission
performance without requiring unnecessary increases in core size or fissile inventory, favoring
efficient use of the compact HALEU core. The quantitative performance metrics, baseline
parameters, and interaction data are provided in Appendix A.5.

6.3.2 Thermal Neutron Beam Tubes/Waveguides

The baseline design includes multiple thermal beam tubes arranged tangentially to the core. This
arrangement allows the tubes to view the reflector thermal flux region while limiting direct line-
of-sight to the fuel, thereby reducing the fast-neutron and high-energy gamma contributions. This
configuration can potentially provide substantially higher thermal brightness than the predecessor
facility at equivalent power.

Optimization variables include the tube cant angle, cross-section dimensions, and the use of
supermirror thermal neutron guides for instruments located far from the biological shield. Key
interaction considerations include cumulative reflector displacement from all beam penetrations,
neutron streaming through shield penetrations, and the effect of nearby beam tubes on fuel
assembly power distribution. Beam penetrations are also physical protection considerations whose
number and provisions should be evaluated within the facility protection assessment.

The quantitative brightness comparisons, guide performance data, and position optimization
results are provided in Appendix A.6.

6.3.3 Pneumatic/Hydraulic Rabbit Tubes

Rabbit tubes support neutron activation analysis and related analytical services that are central to
the NIST measurement mission. No baseline positions are established in the preconceptual design;
locations remain open design variables. Mission requirements for neutron activation analysis
define the desired flux, spectral, and operational characteristics for candidate rabbit tube locations.

Operating experience from the predecessor facility provides important benchmarks regarding flux
gradients, spectral characterization, temporal stability, and gamma heating constraints. These
benchmarks help define the requirements and candidate locations for rabbit tube optimization.

Candidate rabbit-tube configurations may include positions optimized for different irradiation
spectra and flux conditions, while avoiding interference with the primary beam and cold source
systems. The rabbit tube layout is coupled to the neutron detector positioning, as detector response
should not be materially affected by rabbit loading and unloading operations.

 32

Key optimization considerations include competition for reflector space with beam and cold source
systems, the cumulative reactivity impact of multiple tubes on the core excess reactivity budget,
the relationship between flux magnitude and gradient steepness at different radial positions, and
proliferation resistance considerations associated with providing material insertion and retrieval
pathways in high-flux regions. Tube sizing and sample capacity should align with the requirements
of the intended analytical mission.

The quantitative flux data, spectral parameters, reactivity impact, proposed layout, and NAA
requirements table are provided in Appendix A.7.

6.3.4 Ultracold Neutron Source

An ultracold neutron source is proposed as an optional capability for fundamental physics
experiments. The UCNS is treated as a coupled design variable, because the converter vessel and
its cryogenic infrastructure can significantly perturb local moderation and reflector conditions.

The proposed location is within the heavy water reflector, on a side of the core that does not
compete with the cold neutron sources. The UCNS is not expected to directly compete
geometrically with the currently identified cold-source and primary beam facilities. Several design
couplings must still be considered, including the converter vessel displacement of reflector
volume, local neutronic effects, cryogenic system integration, and additional shield penetrations
for neutron extraction.

Since future UCNS capability is desired, the impact of a UCNS-sized perturbation is to be
evaluated in computational models from the conceptual design phase onward, even if construction
is deferred. This ensures that the reflector tank geometry, beam tube layout, and shutdown systems
accommodate a future UCNS without fundamental redesign.

The quantitative performance parameters, proposed location details, and coupled design
considerations are provided in Appendix A.8.

6.4 Cooling Capacity Optimization for Core and Irradiation
  Positions
Cooling capacity is a critical design parameter that intersects proliferation resistance, reactor
safety, and operational robustness under expected operating conditions. The pre-conceptual
baseline Box9 design includes a primary cooling system design based on NBSR components but
permits optimization as the design matures [3].

The optimization effort addresses rightsizing of the primary cooling system that serves the core
and irradiation positions, together with the separate cooling systems that serve the two CNS and
that would serve a potential UCNS. The objective is to match cooling capacity to the optimized
configuration so that the declared mission is supported with adequate thermal margins and without
unnecessary excess capacity that could support undeclared power uprates or non-mission
irradiation activities. The rightsizing is informed by upstream design choices in fuel system, fuel

 33

plate and assembly geometry, fuel management strategy, core layout, and power peaking
mitigation.

From a safety perspective, sufficient cooling capacity must be maintained to maintain adequate
margins against the thermal-hydraulic screening metrics identified in Section 4.3, and rightsizing
changes must not diminish the passive natural circulation feature in the pre-conceptual design.
From a proliferation resistance perspective, avoiding unnecessary excess cooling capacity that
does not support the declared mission helps reduce opportunities for misuse of the reactor and
associated experimental facilities, and may provide operational indicators of departures from
declared operating conditions.

The scope of this evaluation is limited to establishing cooling capacity requirements, including
total flow and heat removal capability for the primary cooling system and for the cold source
cryogenic systems. As established in Section 2.2, detailed mechanical design of the primary
cooling system, of the cold source cryogenic infrastructure, and of auxiliary and balance of plant
cooling systems is outside the scope of this effort.

7. Implementation
This section describes how the optimization effort will be carried out across the collaborating
organizations. It defines each organization’s focus and lays out the approach for analyzing and
down-selecting design features to arrive at optimized designs.

7.1 Areas of Focus for Each Organization

7.1.1     NCNR (NIST)

NCNR owns the development of the NNS design. They define the NNS mission space and
operational objectives with the performance requirements and technical specifications needed to
support them. NCNR will document the capabilities needed to support the neutron science work
planned at NNS, including any specific requirements related to the irradiation locations and
conditions (rabbits, neutron spectrum, and flux). These are reflected in the optimization plan as
performance objectives (Section 4.2). NCNR will also specify any fixed prior design features and
architecture, which will be reflected as design constraints (Section 5.1). This includes mission-
driven requirements that shape core configuration, experimental facility needs, reflector features,
and other architectural assumptions that bound the design space for the optimization effort.
Additionally, NCNR will also provide concurrent data and documentation where appropriate to
support the optimization processes. This includes (but is not limited to) alternative models,
alternative analyses, reviews, technical basis evaluations, clarifications on acceptance criteria or
regulatory requirements, and benchmarking data sets.

7.1.2 Argonne National Laboratory

Argonne supports the ongoing NNS design effort through independent analyses, verification
activities, and assessment of design alternatives. Acting as an extension of the design team,
Argonne explores a broader set of design options and parameter variations than would typically be
practical within a single organization and identifies potential engineering solutions within the
multi-objective PRO-X framework (Figure 4-1). Argonne’s independent neutronics and thermal-
hydraulic analyses assess the implications of various design configurations and variables to
identify opportunities to strengthen proliferation resistance and improve performance. Fuel
management strategies and other design considerations are also explored to inform ongoing design
refinement. Argonne’s cross-verification of analysis approaches provides additional technical
confidence in the analytical methods used to support the NNS design effort. Together, these
activities provide technical insight that informs NCNR’s evolving reactor design framework and
supports subsequent design decisions as the project advances.

7.2 Prioritization of Optimization Efforts
The optimization topics identified in Section 6 will be pursued with differing levels of emphasis
as the conceptual reactor design continues to evolve. While certain topics may warrant greater

 35

attention, these activities are not intended to proceed in a strictly sequential manner. Several lines
of inquiry will advance in parallel, and insights gained in one area could inform analyses in others.
This approach reflects the coupled nature of the design variables (Figure 4-1). Throughout this
process, the analyses will seek to identify design options that strengthen proliferation resistance
while supporting reactor performance and adequate safety margins.

Early emphasis is placed on technical areas that establish the basic behavior of the core and
strongly influence later optimization choices. These include fuel-system selection, core
compactness, and major assembly-design features, because they directly affect cycle length,
reflector performance, power distribution, thermal margins, and the feasibility of the baseline core
configuration (Section 6.1). These analyses establish the initial understanding of core behavior,
fuel utilization characteristics, and the associated implications for proliferation resistance.

As viable core and fuel-system options are established, fuel management becomes a major line of
inquiry. Alternative loading, shuffling, and discharge strategies are then used to improve
understanding of cycle behavior, burnup uniformity, power peaking, fissile inventory, fresh fuel
throughput, and associated proliferation resistance considerations (Section 6.2). These studies also
help clarify the role of control blade usage and related operating strategies as the core configuration
becomes more clearly defined.

Evaluation of cooling system requirements proceeds in parallel with these efforts (Section 6.4). As
neutronics and fuel-management analyses provide improved understanding of power distributions
and thermal loads, cooling requirements for the core and irradiation facilities can be refined
accordingly. This allows cooling capacity to be aligned with the expected operating envelope of
the optimized configuration rather than carried forward with unnecessary excess.

The design and placement of irradiation positions, beam tubes, and other experimental facilities
(Section 6.3) are likewise refined iteratively as mission needs and core behavior become better
defined. These studies examine how facility location, geometry, and associated cooling or
shielding provisions affect neutron flux conditions, reflector performance, and interactions with
other mission systems.

Some design refinements, such as alternative burnable poison configurations or control
programming strategies, are best evaluated after the primary aspects of the core configuration and
fuel management approach are better established. These later-stage studies can then focus on
improving peaking behavior, reactivity behavior, thermal margins, and operational stability within
a more clearly defined design context.

The optional ultracold neutron source requested by NCNR is treated within this same prioritization
framework as an additional coupled design variable rather than as an automatic add-on. Its
evaluation depends on how earlier core and reflector choices define the available geometric,
neutronic, and operational margin.

7.3 Structured Decision Approach
The structured decision approach supports technically sound, transparent, and defensible decision-
making throughout the design optimization. It uses an iterative process to refine candidate designs
as new insights emerge. Key features are:

 • Baseline design – evaluate all options against a chosen reference design
 • Iteration – refine design based on new results and information
 • Parallel lines of inquiry – assess coupled technical areas simultaneously
 • Screening – eliminate non-viable options
 • Down-selection – eliminate underperforming options to focus on the most promising
 • Win-win focus – favor solutions that improve multiple objectives simultaneously

Starting with the declared requirements and constraints and using the baseline design as a
reference, adjustable design features and ranges of associated variable values are identified,
potentially creating a large set of candidate designs. The structured decision approach enables
efficient examination of this set through staged assessments that progressively screen and down-
select weaker candidates, focusing analysis on the most promising ones before introducing
additional variables and new studies.

Design candidates are evaluated against the baseline design and each other, but each variable is
not evaluated in the same way, with the same weight, or at the same stage. As described in Section
7.2, early focus on primary variables supports efficient down-selection and accelerates progression
toward an optimized design. Each evaluation provides insights that may refine or extend the design
space with new lines of inquiry.

The process can be visualized as the expansion and reduction of a candidate matrix, as shown in
Figure 7-1. In this example, five candidates with one variable parameter are represented as a 5×1
matrix. These candidates are evaluated against defined criteria (Section 4). Options that pass
(green) move to the next stage of analysis, while those that fail are excluded from further
consideration (grey crosshatch). Results from one stage of analysis inform the selection of
additional parameter values and the incorporation of additional variables (outlined in blue). The
matrix expands as additional variables or values are added and is reduced when options are
screened out. External factors such as an emergent logistical barrier may fix a variable or eliminate
individual candidates at any point (red “X”). Different evaluation criteria may be used at each
stage, and later analyses may focus on the strongest remaining cases (the excluded lower
performing options are marked with “-“).

8. Expected Outputs and Path Forward
This section summarizes the expected outputs of the optimization effort and explains how they
support subsequent project activities. It describes how results will inform conceptual design
refinement, RFI/RFP activities, and engagement during future project phases.

8.1 Expected Technical and Decision Support Outputs
This collaborative project is intended to produce a focused set of technical and decision-support
outputs that enable informed design decisions for the NNS during the transition from conceptual
design toward procurement and detailed design. The outputs emphasize clarity, traceability, and
relevance to project decision making rather than exhaustive analysis.

Key deliverables include a clearly defined baseline design and a set of candidate designs, together
with comparative assessments of their impacts on proliferation resistance, mission performance,
and safety margins. Results will be synthesized into decision-ready products—such as summaries
of the feasible design space, comparative tables, and sensitivity observations—that highlight where
design choices provide clear benefits, involve important tradeoffs, or warrant further investigation.

In addition, the project will produce concise technical recommendations that capture the
implications of the optimization results for key reactor design features. These recommendations
will summarize findings related to topics such as promising fuel assembly design paths, fuel
management approaches, irradiation facility characteristics, and cooling capacity expectations,
based on the comparative analyses performed in this study. The recommendations will be
structured so they can be used directly in conceptual design updates, procurement documentation,
and vendor engagement discussions, while remaining consistent with the pre-licensing nature of
the work. Collectively, these outputs will support design down-selection, prioritization, and later
project decisions.

8.2 Integration into Conceptual Design Updates

The results of this optimization effort are intended to be directly integrated into ongoing conceptual
design updates for the NNS. Rather than generating standalone studies, the work is structured to
inform the controlled refinement of the project baseline by adapting assumptions, constraints, and
design choices already under consideration.

Integration is expected to occur through coordination among the technical team and is led by
NCNR. NCNR will also be the primary interface with the future prime contractor supporting
overall facility engineering and implementation. Within this structure, optimization outcomes
provide a technical basis for updates to conceptual core descriptions, interface definitions, and
design envelopes relevant to both core and facility-level systems.

The combined NIST/PRO-RR effort will help ensure that proliferation resistance considerations

address them alongside the project’s technical requirements and design constraints. This process
also provides an opportunity to emphasize mission performance and utilization objectives rather

The RFP is the beginning of the procurement process for the NNS project at NIST. The RFP will

resistance considerations, and other requirements that vendors must satisfy.

The PRO-RR team will support NIST by reviewing the draft RFP prior to issuance and will work
with NIST in responding to any requests for additional information (RAIs) from the vendors as
they evaluate the RFP. Once proposals are received from the vendor community, the PRO-RR team
will assist the NIST team in assessing how the proposals address the proliferation resistance
considerations of the NNS project.

 • Cycle 1 (fresh): A-a2, B-b2, C-c2 loaded into center column.
 • Cycle 2 (once-burned): A-a2→A′-c1, B-b2→B′-b3, C-c2→C′-a1(peripheral positions).

 positions).

Potential Areas for Further Investigation

 1. Elevated peaking in second-cycle assemblies at SU/BOC. Once-burned assemblies
 with depleted Cd wires occupy peripheral positions exposed to D₂O reflector return,

A.4. Fuel Assembly Features to Limit Power Peaking
Two peaking issues are identified in NIST TN 2316 [5]:

 1. Radial peaking in peripheral plates: Plates 1 and 21 experience enhanced thermal flux
 from the chimney water gap and D₂O reflector return. Maximum stripe peaking reaches
 1.75 at SU; maximum local peaking reaches 1.98 at BOC [5, Figs. 17–18].

 2. Corner assembly power: Corner positions see reflector return from two sides. At EOC,
 maximum assembly peaking is 1.12 in B-b2 [2, Sec. 4.4; 3, Fig. 16], but plate-level
 peaking within corners is more severe at SU/BOC.

Mitigation Options

 1. Discrete Burnable Absorbers (Baseline: Cd Wires). The 0.05 cm Cd wires in side plates
 suppress fresh-fuel reactivity effectively at SU/BOC but deplete rapidly (Cd-113 dominant
 absorber), causing the mid-cycle power shift [5, Figs. 16–17]. Optimization variables
 include wire diameter, axial extent/placement, number per side plate, and alternative

 50

 materials (Gd, Eu). The Cd wires contribute to keeping SU excess reactivity below 6% [2,
 Table 4-1]—a proliferation resistance benefit.
 2. Integral Burnable Absorbers (for dispersion fuel). If a dispersion fuel U₃Si₂-Al or U-
 7Mo/Al is selected, Gd₂O₃ or B₄C particles can be dispersed in the Al matrix, providing
 spatially distributed flux suppression more uniform than edge-located wires. This may
 reduce the radial peaking gradient across the three transverse stripes [5, Fig. 17]. Adds
 fabrication/qualification complexity.
 3. Alternative Poison Wires. Hafnium wires enable gradual, sustained suppression through
 multiple absorbing isotopes and avoid the sharp depletion behavior associated with a single
 isotope, potentially reducing the mid-cycle power shift [5, Figs. 16–17]. Europium wires
 offer intermediate characteristics. Gadolinium wires provide strong initial suppression with
 very rapid depletion.
 4. Water Displacers. Aluminum inserts replacing coolant in the outermost channels
 (between end plates and chimney wall) would reduce the enhanced moderation that drives
 peripheral plate peaking.

A.6. Thermal Neutron Beam Tubes and Waveguides
Baseline Design

The eight baseline thermal beam tubes are tangential to the core (5° cant), viewing the thermal flux
peak while avoiding direct fuel sight lines [4, Sec. 4, Fig. 13; 3, Fig. 11]. Thermal brightness is
≥ 2× NBSR BT-6 at equivalent power, with effective Maxwellian temperature 317 K [4, Fig. 14;
2, Fig. 8-14]. Positions are near-optimum: displacing toward fuel increases only fast flux;
displacing away reduces thermal flux [4, Sec. 4].

Optimization variables: Cant angle (fine-tuning); beam tube cross-section (baseline:
accommodates 6×20 cm guide [4, Fig. 15]); thermal guide m-value and geometry (useful for
instruments > 8 m from shield exit at λ > 1.5 Å [4, Fig. 16; 2, Fig. 8-15]).

Tradeoff considerations:

 • Reflector displacement: Eight thermal tubes plus four cold conduits plus two CNS
 thimbles represent significant D₂O displacement; cumulative perturbation must respect
 the 5% CNS flux constraint [2, Requirement R1.4].

 • Streaming and leakage: Tangential arrangement mitigates fast neutron/gamma streaming,
 but intersections with cold conduits require shielding evaluation.

 • Proliferation resistance implications: Beam penetrations are physical protection
 considerations; number and security provisions should be evaluated within the facility
 protection assessment.

 • Power peaking near penetrations: Tangential offset minimizes fuel perturbation [4, Sec.
 4], but should be quantified for tubes passing between core and CNS, where thermal flux
 is maximized [5, Fig. 13].

Lessons from NBSR Experience

The Lindstrom et al. (2008) characterization of NBSR RT-2 [8] provides key benchmarks:

 1. Flux gradients. RT-2 has a nearly twofold axial gradient along its 7.6 cm length [8, Fig.
 1], “nearly compensated by flipping the rabbit midway through the irradiation” but
 imperfect due to asymmetric moderator environment [8]. The NNS design should reduce
 this gradient through optimized radial positioning and moderator geometry.
 2. Spectral characterization. RT-2 achieves consensus thermal flux of 3.1×10¹³ cm⁻²s⁻¹
 with f ≈ 350 and α ≈ 0.2 [8, Table 1]. The NNS compact core with D₂O reflector is
 expected to provide substantially higher thermal flux and better thermalization at
 equivalent positions [2, Sec. 9.4].

 3. Temporal stability. RT-2 and RT-4 fluence rates are constant to better than 2% over the
 fuel cycle once equilibrium is reached [8, Fig. 2]. RT-1, being closer to fuel, shows
 greater sensitivity to control element position—directly relevant to NNS rabbit
 placement.

 4. Gamma heating. The ~100 Gy/s dose rate limits plastic rabbit irradiation time and
 makes Cd-covered irradiations impractical (Cd reaches >115°C within two minutes) [8].
 NNS positions should consider active cooling provisions where gamma heating is
 elevated.
Preliminary NNS MCNP Results

Cook (2025) [6] assessed a high-flux rabbit position at (0, −45, 0) cm from core center—similar
radius to the CNSs but on the south side, not interfering significantly with thermal beam tubes [6,
Fig. 1]. Key findings:

   •    Flux levels. Total flux is (1.0 ± 0.1) × 10¹⁴ cm⁻²s⁻¹ with ~89% thermal (<414 meV),
 ~10% epithermal, ~1% fast [6, Table 2]. Thermal/fast ratio ranges from 77 to 89. These
 levels meet the ≥10¹³ minimum and approach the ≥10¹⁴ preferred threshold [7].

 • Gradients. Axial gradient is 2×10¹² cm⁻²s⁻¹/cm (2%/cm); radial gradient across the ~1
 cm sample diameter is ~5% [6, Sec. 4]—significantly improved over the NBSR RT-2
        ~50% axial gradient [7; 8, Fig. 1].

 55

 • Spectral quality. The ~89% thermal fraction confirms excellent D₂O reflector
 thermalization at 45 cm radius [6, Table 2], substantially better than NBSR RT-2 at 95 cm
 [8].

 • Reactivity impact. One rabbit tube with nine silica samples reduces core reactivity by
 ~32 cents (Dr ≈ −0.00208) [6, Table 3]—non-negligible against the <6% SU excess
 reactivity budget [2, Table 4-1; 5, Table 4].

Candidate Configuration for Further Evaluation

Based on the NAA requirements [6, 7], NBSR experience [8], preliminary MCNP results [6], and
the Seo conceptual layout [7, Fig. 1]:

RT-1 — High fast flux. Below the hot leg; highest fast flux but also highest gamma heating and
reactivity impact. Facilitates transport of irradiated targets to the measuring room [7].

RT-2 — High thermal flux. In D₂O reflector at ~45 cm from core center, per Cook trial [6]. Target:
≥10¹⁴ cm⁻²s⁻¹ with ~89% thermal fraction [6, Table 2].

RT-3, RT-4 — Maximum thermal/fast ratio. At ~100 cm from core center, analogous to NBSR
RT-2 [7; 8]. Lower absolute flux but superior spectral purity for high-precision comparator NAA.
Expected to exceed NBSR RT-2 thermal flux due to compact core design [7].

RT-5 — Bulk irradiation. Opposite side of hot leg from RT-2; paint-can-sized samples for multi-
day irradiations with passive water cooling [7]. Positions of RT-3 and RT-5 may be interchanged
if target transport is difficult [7].

Additional positions. Space between RT-1 and RT-3/RT-4 reserved for NDP and radioisotope
production [7]. East/west sides (CNS sides) are kept free for additional cold neutron beams or
thermal column [7].

Neutron Detector Coupling

Neutron flux detectors must be positioned near the outside of the D₂O tank where flux drops
rapidly, but their response must be immune to rabbit loading/unloading perturbations [7, Fig. 1].
Detector positions should be determined concurrently with the rabbit layout, reinforcing the
treatment of rabbit tubes as coupled optimization variables [7].

Optimization Considerations

 • Limited Reflector space. Rabbit tubes at 45 cm radius compete with CNSs and thermal
 beam tubes. Consistent with [2, R1.5] irradiation facilities are considered secondary to
 beam-based experimental capabilities when reflector-space conflicts arise. The Seo
 layout [7, Fig. 1] addresses this by placing rabbits on the north/south sides while keeping
 east/west sides (CNS sides) free.

 • Reactivity budget. The ~32 cents per tube [6, Table 3] is significant against <6% SU
 excess reactivity [2, Table 4-1]. Multiple tubes will consume substantial excess reactivity,
 potentially affecting cycle length. Cumulative impact must be quantified for the full
 complement.

 57

 • Flux uniformity vs. proximity. Closer positions provide higher flux but steeper
 gradients and greater reactivity impact. At 45 cm, gradients (~2%/cm axial, ~5% radial)
 are acceptable [6, Sec. 4]; positions at ~100 cm (RT-3/RT-4) trade flux for spectral purity.

 • Proliferation resistance implications. Rabbit tubes provide material insertion/retrieval
 pathways in high-flux regions. Tube diameter (1.65 cm internal radius [6]) and sample
 capacity should be evaluated against NAA requirements and facility protection
 considerations. The minimum six tubes [6, Table 1] represent significant reflector
 penetrations for the physical protection assessment.

limited geometric interaction with CNS placement. Thermal flux at this location: 2×10¹⁴ to 4×10¹⁴
cm⁻²s⁻¹ [5, Fig. 12].
