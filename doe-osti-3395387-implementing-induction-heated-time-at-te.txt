 ABSTRACT

Short-duration thermal excursions in light-water reactor (LWR) fuel cladding are governed by the complete
temperature history and the material condition established by irradiation and prior service. Thus, evaluation
of time-at-temperature (t@T) response requires controlled transient heating followed by mechanical-
property measurements and, when needed, supporting microstructural characterization. This report
documents FY 2026 progress toward implementing an integrated irradiated material t@T testing capability
across three facilities at the US Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL).

Laboratory-scale testing with an Ambrell induction heating system demonstrated rapid heating and cooling
of unirradiated Zircaloy-4 cladding and generally consistent thermocouple and pyrometer measurements
above the lower pyrometer operating limit. Translation of this method to irradiated materials requires new
specimen loading, temperature control, containment, and remote-handling approaches. The facility
implementations are being developed around susceptor-mediated heating and witness-specimen
temperature measurement rather than thermocouples welded directly to the irradiated test specimens.

At ORNL’s Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory (IFEL), the work extends the established commercial
cladding receipt-to-test workflow through development of curved SS-JC (curved subsize tensile geometry)
specimen machining and a planned Glovebox 3 t@T treatment system. At ORNL’s Irradiated Materials
Examination and Testing (IMET) facility, readiness activities support recovery, separation, t@T treatment,
and tensile testing of curved SS-JC specimens from the planned High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR)
irradiation campaign. At the Low Activation Materials Development and Analysis (LAMDA) laboratory,
a quartz tube containment approach and hardness testing method are under development for small,
irradiated specimens, and 60 previously irradiated retainer specimens were prepared for the initial
campaign.

By the time of the reporting cutoff, the induction heating systems had been procured, deployment locations
selected, and facility-specific concepts were under development, but the final systems had not been installed
or commissioned. Remaining work includes thermal method qualification, final fixture and containment
fabrication, utility installation, facility checkout, and initial irradiated-material demonstrations. These
activities are being advanced ahead of the planned completion of the HFIR irradiation campaign and
initiation of postirradiation examination (PIE) in late FY 2027.

 1. INTRODUCTION AND SCOPE

Conventional LWR safety criteria are generally formulated to avoid boiling transition, identified as
departure from nucleate boiling in pressurized water reactors and dryout in boiling water reactors (BWRs).
These thermal-hydraulic limits are both practical and conservative because a loss of efficient cladding-to-
coolant heat transfer can produce a rapid increase in cladding temperature. Boiling transition is not, by
itself, a cladding-failure mechanism; however, the resulting thermal excursion can challenge the mechanical
integrity and dimensional stability of the fuel system if the temperature and exposure duration are sufficient
[1].

Short-duration anticipated operational occurrences differ fundamentally from longer-duration accident
conditions. Reactor trip, power reduction, flow recovery, and rewetting may limit elevated-temperature
exposure to seconds, even when the cladding experiences high heating and cooling rates during that interval
[2]. Peak temperature does not fully describe the material challenge. A representative t@T evaluation must
consider the complete thermal history, including heatup rate, peak temperature, dwell time, cooldown or

 1

quench rate, and the material condition established by irradiation and service history prior to the transient.
Depending on the event and cladding condition, oxidation, internal pressure and stress, hydrogen
dissolution, and hydride redistribution may also influence the post-transient response [1].

For Zircaloy cladding, short-duration heating can promote recovery of irradiation-induced defects, reduce
irradiation hardening, alter tensile and hardness properties, and, at sufficiently high temperatures or longer
exposure times, initiate recovery or recrystallization of the as-fabricated microstructure [3–8]. These
changes can affect strength, ductility, creep resistance, and the margin to subsequent mechanical loading.
Establishing the technical basis for t@T behavior requires controlled transient heating followed by
mechanical-property measurement and, in some cases, microstructural characterization on materials with
representative irradiation histories.

The primary application of the ORNL testing capability described in this report is the evaluation of
irradiated fuel cladding. Commercially irradiated material provides the most direct connection to in-service
alloy condition because it incorporates the combined effects of reactor exposure, burnup, thermal and
mechanical history, oxidation, and hydrogen uptake. ORNL is building on previously established
capabilities at IFEL (Building 3525) for receipt and sectioning of commercial fuel rod segments, cladding
harvesting, hot cell computer numerical control machining, and mechanical testing [9]. The FY 2026
implementation expands that workflow through induction-heated t@T exposure and curved axial tensile
specimen harvesting and fixturing. The resulting pathway is intended to provide industry teams with a
practical means to evaluate material removed from commercially operated fuel rods under controlled
transient conditions.

Commercially irradiated material is scarce, expensive to handle, and generally available in quantities that
support only a limited number of thermal conditions and destructive tests. The commercial pathway is
supported by two complementary ORNL capabilities. Premachined specimens irradiated in the HFIR and
tested at IMET (Building 3025E) provide controlled irradiation conditions and direct post-treatment
mechanical-property measurements. Small subsectioned specimens treated and evaluated at LAMDA
provide higher throughput for quantitatively mapping mechanical response across a broader t@T matrix
through instrumented hardness testing and supporting characterization. These pathways provide controlled
comparisons, condition downselection, and the mechanistic understanding needed to maximize the value
of the more limited commercial tests.

A principal implementation challenge is that the laboratory-scale induction heating method cannot be
transferred directly into a hot cell. The laboratory configuration uses a welded intrinsic thermocouple,
specimen suspension from the thermocouple assembly, a fixed emissivity surface marking, and direct
alignment of a pyrometer to the heated region. Welding thermocouples to irradiated specimens is not
planned for routine hot cell testing, and remote operations impose additional requirements and challenges
for repeatable specimen placement, manipulator-compatible loading and retrieval, coil and fixture
geometry, line of sight for optical temperature measurement, contamination control, and routing of
electrical power and cooling services. The three facilities will use a standardized thermal treatment
methodology but follow facility-specific hardware and operating approaches.

This report documents approximately 9 months of FY 2026 implementation activity. Work completed
during the reporting period included evaluation and procurement of induction heating systems; selection
and assessment of a glovebox location supporting the IFEL workflow, Cell 2 at IMET, and laboratory space
in LAMDA; development of specimen loading, fixturing, and temperature-monitoring concepts;
engagement with the equipment vendor on custom coil designs; development of a quartz tube containment
approach for LAMDA; and preliminary hardness testing of heat-treated unirradiated zirconium material.
By the time of the reporting cutoff, the induction heating systems had not been installed or commissioned
in their final nuclear facility configurations. Accordingly, this report describes the technical decisions,

 2

equipment preparations, and implementation path being established to support operational t@T testing
during FY 2027 and subsequent testing campaigns. Section 2 defines the integrated ORNL strategy, Section
3 establishes the laboratory-scale technical baseline, and Sections 4 through 6 describe the facility-specific
implementation pathways.

 2. INTEGRATED ORNL TIME-AT-TEMPERATURE TESTING STRATEGY

ORNL is implementing the t@T capability as an integrated, multi-facility workflow rather than as three
independent heating systems. The common objective is to impose a controlled and traceable thermal history
on an irradiated cladding specimen and quantify the resulting change in mechanical response. The pathways
differ in material source, specimen size, radiological constraints, test throughput, and measurement type,
but they are connected through common transient definitions, temperature-measurement requirements, and
post-treatment data needs. The integrated workflow is summarized in Figure 1. The IFEL and IMET
pathways apply aligned t@T treatment and curved SS-JC tensile-test methods to commercially and HFIR-
irradiated cladding, respectively, while LAMDA extends the dataset through small-specimen hardness
measurements and advanced characterization.

  Figure 1. Integrated ORNL t@T testing pathways for commercially and HFIR-irradiated cladding, using
 common controlled treatment and SS-JC tensile methods with complementary LAMDA hardness and
 characterization.

The IFEL pathway provides the direct route from commercially operated fuel rods to post-transient
mechanical data. The workflow builds on the existing capability to receive fuel-rod segments, slice and
defuel cladding, machine specimens with the in-cell computer numerical control (CNC) system, and
perform mechanical testing. The FY 2026 additions couple those operations with controlled induction-
heated t@T exposure and the preparation and testing of curved axial tensile specimens. Because the
available commercial material and the number of mechanically testable specimens are limited, the selected
thermal conditions must be technically consequential and supported by a traceable record of specimen
origin, prior service condition, imposed thermal history, and post-treatment handling. The use of a common
curved tensile geometry and aligned treatment and test methods is intended to support cross-pathway
comparison between commercial service exposure and controlled irradiation via a research reactor.

The IMET pathway provides data on irradiated material under controlled HFIR conditions using cladding
and Zircaloy specimens designed for separate-effects testing. These experiments can vary alloy, irradiation
temperature, dose, specimen geometry, and other test parameters more deliberately than what is possible
with material harvested from commercial fuel rods. After irradiation, specimens will undergo controlled
t@T treatment using the induction-heating system at IMET and then be transferred to existing mechanical
test systems for direct measurement of properties such as yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, uniform
elongation, and total elongation. This data will establish the response under well-characterized irradiation
conditions, support qualification of the hot cell heating method, and contribute to the development or
bounding of relevant t@T response thresholds. Together, the IFEL and HFIR pathways connect
measurements on service-exposed cladding with controlled evaluations across a broader material system,
irradiation, and transient matrix.

The LAMDA pathway extends the available test matrix by using small ring slices or other subsectioned
specimens that can be handled within the laboratory's dose and contamination controls. Small-scale
induction heating followed by instrumented hardness testing can evaluate more temperature-time
combinations and more replicate locations than what are practical with scarce hot cell mechanical
specimens. The hardness measurements can identify onset and progression of thermal softening, compare
material conditions, and support selection of the most informative transients for direct mechanical testing.
The same specimens can also support microstructural characterization when needed to distinguish
irradiation-defect recovery, recrystallization, hydride-related effects, or other mechanisms contributing to
the measured response.

The three pathways will use a common performance basis but not identical test hardware. For each
treatment, the required data package includes specimen identification and geometry, induction-coil and
fixture configuration, specimen position within the coil, temperature-measurement method and location,
programmed and measured heatup rate, peak temperature, dwell time, cooling history, and any deviations
from the target profile. Facility-specific coils and fixtures may be necessary because the commercial
cladding segments, HFIR mechanical specimens, and LAMDA ring slices differ in size and handling
requirements. Likewise, the temperature-monitoring approach must be adapted to the available line of sight
and remote-operability constraints while remaining traceable to the laboratory method.

Test planning across the facilities will be iterative. LAMDA hardness testing can rapidly identify
temperature-time regions associated with measurable changes in mechanical response. HFIR-irradiated
specimens can then anchor selected conditions with direct mechanical-property measurements under
controlled irradiation states. Commercial tests can proceed in parallel when material is available; the
supporting results can be used to prioritize conditions and interpret the response of service-exposed
cladding. Conversely, unexpected behavior in commercial material can be isolated through focused HFIR
or LAMDA tests. This interaction is central to the strategy: the commercial pathway establishes relevance,
while the HFIR and LAMDA pathways provide experimental control and breadth to expand experimental
impact across various materials of interest.

 4

The resulting data will support development and assessment of t@T material relationships for advanced
fuel-cladding systems. Hardness, tensile properties, and supporting microstructural observations will be
evaluated together to quantify recovery from irradiation hardening and, where applicable, the transition
toward more extensively recovered or recrystallized conditions. Model development and fuel-performance-
code implementation are downstream uses of the data; the immediate objective of the present milestone is
to establish repeatable, nuclear facility-compatible workflows capable of producing defensible thermal
histories and post-treatment property measurements.

The laboratory-scale work described in Section 3 provides the starting technical method for rapid induction
heating and temperature measurement. Sections 4 through 6 describe how that method is being translated
into the IFEL commercial pathway, the IMET HFIR pathway, and the LAMDA small-specimen pathway.
Across all three locations, the FY 2026 emphasis is on implementation readiness: equipment procurement,
location selection, utility and contamination-control planning, coil and fixture development, temperature-
measurement decisions, and out-of-cell benchmarking before final installation and nuclear facility
operation.

 3. LAB-SCALE INDUCTION HEATING METHODOLOGY DEVELOPMENT

The integrated testing strategy described in Section 2 requires a heating platform capable of imposing rapid,
short-duration, and traceable thermal histories on relatively small Zircaloy cladding specimens. Before
adapting the method for irradiated-material testing at IFEL, IMET, and LAMDA, an Ambrell induction-
heating system was assembled and evaluated in a nonradiological laboratory using unirradiated cladding
specimens. The objective of this shakedown work was to establish a practical understanding of system
response, specimen positioning, temperature measurement, transient control, and data acquisition and to
determine whether the compact system could produce the heating and cooling rates required for t@T
studies. This work was not intended to qualify the final nuclear facility configurations. Rather, it established
a direct-heating laboratory baseline from which the facility-specific approaches described in Sections 4
through 6 are being developed.

3.1 Transient Requirements and Heating Platform Selection

Representative test profiles were selected to reproduce the principal features of the rapid thermal excursions
described in Section 1. The profiles considered peak temperatures of approximately 450–900°C, heatup
rates greater than 5°C/s and frequently greater than 100°C/s, and dwell times near the peak temperature on
the order of 0–10 s. Reproducing these conditions requires a heating method with low thermal inertia, rapid
power modulation, and sufficient control to distinguish the effects of heatup rate, peak temperature, dwell
time, and cooldown history. The relatively small specimen sizes available from irradiation experiments also
favor a compact and modular heating system that can be adapted to multiple nuclear-material facilities.

Infrared heating had previously been used to assess changes in cladding mechanical performance following
rapid thermal excursions [10]. That work provided an important technical basis, but challenges were
encountered in achieving repeatable specimen positioning, maintaining testing throughput, and translating
a dedicated infrared furnace configuration into LAMDA or a hot-cell environment. Induction heating was
therefore evaluated as a compact alternative capable of supporting high-throughput testing of unirradiated
materials while also providing a potential platform for irradiated-material testing at LAMDA, IFEL, and
IMET.

Induction heating uses alternating current through a water-cooled copper coil to produce a time-varying
magnetic field. When a conductive specimen is positioned within the coil, induced eddy currents generate

 5

resistive heating within the specimen. This direct coupling allows rapid heatup without first heating a
comparatively large furnace volume or external thermal mass. Because the coil is actively cooled and
contributes little stored thermal energy after power is removed, rapid specimen cooldown can also be
achieved. Nevertheless, heating uniformity and transient response depend on the coil geometry, specimen
position, material geometry, fixture design, and surrounding environment. These dependencies were central
considerations during the laboratory shakedown testing.

3.2 Laboratory System Configuration and Specimen Instrumentation

Figure 2 shows the Ambrell system configuration used for the unirradiated material tests. A water chiller
supplied cooling to the power supply, coil head, and induction coil to maintain acceptable component
operating temperatures. A temperature controller received the primary thermocouple signal and regulated
induction-system power to produce the prescribed thermal profile. A dual-wavelength pyrometer was
positioned approximately 200 mm from the specimen surface, producing a nominal measurement spot of
2 mm. The pyrometer had an operating range of approximately 315 to 1,015°C and was used as a secondary
temperature measurement to evaluate the primary thermocouple response and the quality of the recorded
transient. Thermocouple, pyrometer, and controller data were synchronized and recorded using a laptop-
based data acquisition system.

 Figure 2. Schematic of the induction system setup for unirradiated materials testing.

The power supply, coil head, temperature controller, pyrometer controller, and data acquisition components
were arranged on a transportable cart, with the water chiller positioned adjacent to the cart. This
configuration demonstrated that the principal system components could be assembled within a relatively
compact footprint. The transportable arrangement also provided a practical basis for evaluating electrical,
cooling, instrumentation, and space requirements before selecting the final facility locations.

The shakedown specimens were primarily unirradiated Zircaloy-4 tube segments representative of
pressurized water reactor (PWR) cladding geometry and approximately 18 mm in axial length. Temperature
feedback was provided by an intrinsic thermocouple configuration. Two thermocouple leads were tack-
welded directly to the cladding surface, with the cladding segment completing the measurement circuit
between the two weld locations. The leads were oriented along the axial direction and spaced approximately
3 mm apart. This arrangement was selected to provide a fast temperature response while avoiding the local
heat transfer perturbation associated with a conventional thermocouple bead attached to the heated surface.

A fixed-emissivity coating was applied between the thermocouple attachment locations. The coating
defined the optical region of interest used for the pyrometer measurement. Figure 3 shows the specimen
level arrangement before testing. The Zircaloy-4 tube segment is suspended above the water-cooled
induction coil before being lowered into the test position. The intrinsic thermocouple leads are attached
immediately above and below the coated pyrometer target, and the pyrometer is visible behind the
specimen. During testing, the thermocouple provided the primary feedback signal for controlling the
transient, while the pyrometer provided and independent measurement of the temperature history at the
coated region.

3.3 Shakedown Testing and Transient-Control Performance

During laboratory testing, the specimens were suspended within the induction coil using a clamp connected
to the thermocouple sheath [11]. The specimen position was checked routinely to maintain consistent axial
and radial placement within the coil and to reduce azimuthal temperature differences associated with
nonuniform electromagnetic coupling. Testing was conducted in laboratory air. The use of the
thermocouple assembly both to suspend the specimen and to provide temperature feedback allowed precise
manual placement but also made the laboratory configuration dependent on direct specimen access.

Closed-loop heating rates from approximately 5 to 95°C/s were evaluated using the installed temperature
controller. The upper end of this range was limited by the controller configuration rather than by the
available induction system power. Manual power control produced heating rates greater than 500°C/s,
demonstrating that the system had sufficient power and response for substantially faster transients. Figure

Above the pyrometer’s lower operating limit, the pyrometer measurements generally tracked the

importance of repeatable specimen positioning and independent temperature monitoring. The comparison
confirms consistent temperature measurement at the monitored region. Additional fixture-level
benchmarking will be required for each specimen geometry and facility configuration.

3.4 Post-Treatment Data Generation Workflow

The laboratory work also established a data generation sequence connecting the imposed thermal treatment
to subsequent mechanical and microstructural evaluation. As illustrated in Figure 5, full tube segments were
first exposed to the selected time-temperature transient. Each treated segment was machined into four
curved subsize specimens. Three curved SSJ-style specimens were reserved for tensile testing at room
temperature or 300°C, and one specimen was retained as a microstructural reference. Treating the tube
segment before machining allowed multiple specimens to be harvested from a common thermal exposure
and provided replicate mechanical measurements together with material for supporting characterization.

A detailed technical manuscript documenting the laboratory-scale shakedown campaign will be issued in

3.5 Translation to Irradiated Material Testing

Although the laboratory configuration demonstrated the capability of the induction system, it also identified
several features that are not readily transferable to routine testing of irradiated material. The method relies
on tack-welding thermocouple leads to each cladding segment, suspending the specimen from the
thermocouple assembly, manually centering the specimen within the coil, applying an emissivity-controlled
surface coating, and maintaining an unobstructed optical line of sight to the heated region. These operations
are practical when the specimen and instrumentation are directly accessible. They become substantially
more difficult when specimen loading, alignment, retrieval, contamination control, and equipment
maintenance must be performed through a glovebox or hot cell. Welding a thermocouple directly to each
irradiated specimen is not planned as the routine temperature monitoring method for the various nuclear
facility implementations.

Two different thermal coupling configurations must be distinguished as the method and is translated into
the nuclear facilities. In the direct configuration used for the laboratory shakedown tests, the cladding
specimen is directly coupled to the induction field and is the primary resistive heating element. Temperature
control is based on a thermocouple welded to the specimen surface. In the indirect configuration under
evaluation for irradiated material testing, the induction coil instead couples to a conductive susceptor that
surrounds or contains the specimen. The susceptor may take the form of a cup or holder into which one or
more specimens are placed. Induction power heats the susceptor, and the specimens are heated
predominantly by thermal radiation from the susceptor, with any contribution from physical contact and
conduction controlled through the fixture design.

A susceptor-based configuration provides two potential advantages for hot cell and glovebox workflows.
First, the temperature sensor can be attached to or incorporated within the susceptor and fixture rather than
welded directly to the irradiated cladding specimen. This simplifies specimen loading and avoids modifying
the limited test material solely to support temperature control. Second, some industry partners have raised

 9

the concern that direct electromagnetic coupling to the cladding could introduce a test method variable that
is not present during an in-reactor thermal excursion. The work summarized in this report has not
established that direct induction heating produces a measurable change in material response relative to other
rapid heating methods. Nevertheless, heating the specimen indirectly through a susceptor provides a path
to decouple the specimen from the alternating magnetic field and may improve confidence that measured
property changes arise from the prescribed thermal history rather than from the method used to produce it.

The indirect configuration also introduces new technical requirements. The relationship between measured
susceptor temperature and actual specimen temperature must be established, including thermal lag during
rapid heatup, temperature gradients within the holder, sensitivity to specimen position and loading, and the
resulting cooldown response. The direct-heating laboratory configuration remains important as a
benchmark. Out-of-cell testing with representative specimens, instrumented surrogate fixtures, pyrometry,
and, when practical, sacrificial directly attached thermocouples will be needed to correlate the susceptor

5.1 HFIR Irradiations and Specimen Recovery

 6. LAMDA SMALL-SCALE INDUCTION HEATING AND HARDNESS TESTING
 PATHWAY

The LAMDA pathway combines small specimen t@T treatment with instrumented hardness testing of
irradiated cladding. Irradiated retainer rings are sectioned at IMET into specimens suitable for direct
handling in LAMDA. Individual retainer specimens receive controlled thermal transients inside a
radiological containment assembly, followed by mounting, polishing, and instrumented hardness testing
using the Nanovea PB-1000. This sequence enables evaluations of multiple temperature and time conditions
using material volumes that are too small for conventional tensile testing.

Instrumented hardness is a primary property measurement in this pathway. Hardness methods have been
extensively utilized to understand evolution of material properties as affected by processing and irradiation
effects in alloys [3-8] because they provide quantitative measures of local or macroscopic mechanical
response from small volumes of irradiated material. Multiple indentations can be obtained from each
retainer specimen, providing information on treatment-induced changes, local variability, and the
consistency of the measured response. The hardness and load displacement data will complement tensile
testing and microstructural characterization and can also help identify conditions that warrant evaluation
using the larger mechanical test specimens.

The FY 2026 work focused on preparing this pathway for irradiated testing. Activities included selection
of the LAMDA installation location, development of a quartz tube containment concept, evaluation of the
hardness response of heat-treated unirradiated Zircaloy-4, and demonstration of specimen preparation and
indentation on an unirradiated retainer specimen. Previously irradiated retainer specimens have already
been prepared for the initial campaign and can enter testing after the LAMDA heating configuration is
installed and qualified.

6.3 Hardness Method Development and Preliminary Results

FY 2026 method development testing used unirradiated Zircaloy-4 to establish three elements needed for
the LAMDA pathway: consistency between the PB-1000 and conventional Vickers hardness
measurements, sensitivity to heat treatment-induced changes in mechanical response, and applicability of
the method to the curved retainer specimen geometry. This work was intended to establish the measurement
approach and identify test controls needed before irradiated retainer specimens are evaluated.

A first series of tests compared hardness values obtained using the PB-1000 with conventional Vickers
hardness measurements. A flat Zircaloy-4 specimen bonded to a steel support was indented with the PB-
1000 at loads of 5 and 10 N and a dwell time of 5 s. The resulting indents were evaluated using both the
hardness calculated by the instrument and optical measurement of the residual Vickers indent diagonals. A
second flat Zircaloy-4 specimen mounted in Bakelite was tested using a conventional Vickers hardness
tester at 0.5 N and 5 s dwell time. Ten measurements were collected from the steel-mounted specimen, and
five measurements were collected from the Bakelite-mounted specimen.

Figure 18 compares the PB-1000 machine-calculated output, the optically measured Vickers hardness from
the PB-1000 indents, and the conventional Vickers hardness. The figure also includes an independent
tensile-derived reference expressed as Vickers-equivalent hardness. Three ASTM subsize tensile specimens
prepared from the same Zircaloy rod stock as the hardness specimens were tested at a nominal strain rate
of 1×10−3 s−1, with strain measured using an extensometer. The tensile-derived value was calculated from
the mean 0.2% offset yield strength rather than obtained from an indentation measurement, but conversion
to the HV scale permits direct comparison with the optically measured PB-1000 and conventional Vickers
results. The measured mean hardness values ranged from approximately 218 to 228 HV and were generally
consistent with the tensile-derived estimate of 216.3 HV. The PB-1000 machine-calculated output showed
greater scatter than the optically measured Vickers values. Because the machine calculation and optical
diagonal method use different approaches to determine contact area, this comparison provides a consistency
check rather than evidence that the measurements are interchangeable.

6.4 FY 2026 Status and Near-Term Irradiated Testing

At the reporting cutoff, Laboratory 274 had been selected for the LAMDA installation, the initial equipment
location had been identified, and the quartz tube containment concept had been developed. Instrumented
hardness testing had been completed on heat-treated unirradiated Zircaloy-4 and on an unirradiated retainer
specimen. Twelve irradiated retainer rings had also been sectioned, with 60 retainer specimens allocated
for LAMDA testing.

The Ambrell system had not been installed or qualified in its final Laboratory 274 configuration, and no
irradiated retainer specimens had received t@T treatment. The remaining implementation work includes
finalizing and fabricating the containment assembly and specimen holder, installing the electrical service
and Ambrell system, completing temperature-profile and containment testing with unirradiated retainer
specimens, and conducting an initial irradiated demonstration under the applicable radiological controls.

The initial irradiated campaign will both demonstrate the integrated LAMDA pathway and produce the first
t@T hardness data from the available HFIR-irradiated retainer specimens. Initial tests should include
untreated controls and a limited number of thermal conditions selected to confirm specimen handling,
temperature control, containment, mounting, polishing, and hardness measurement before the broader
temperature- and time-dependence matrices are initiated.

Connector ring specimens from the current HFIR SS-JC campaign will be incorporated into the LAMDA
pathway as they become available. Those specimens will extend the hardness dataset and provide paired
hardness and tensile measurements from common materials and irradiation conditions.

 7. SUMMARY, CONCLUSIONS, AND FY 2027 PATH FORWARD

This report documents FY 2026 progress toward establishing integrated t@T testing workflows for
irradiated fuel cladding at ORNL. Laboratory-scale testing demonstrated that the Ambrell induction heating
platform can produce rapid heating and cooling profiles over the range needed for t@T evaluations. The
laboratory work also identified several features that are not practical for routine irradiated material testing,
including welded thermocouples, suspension of the specimen from the thermocouple assembly, and direct
manual alignment within the induction coil. Thus, the facility implementations are being developed around
susceptor-mediated heating, representative witness specimens, and out-of-cell qualification of the
relationship between measured control temperature and specimen thermal history.

The IFEL pathway extends an established commercial cladding workflow that already connects fuel rod
examination and segment selection with sectioning, defueling and decontamination, in-cell CNC machining
and inspection, and mechanical testing. The FY 2026 work adds curved SS-JC specimen machining and a
controlled t@T treatment step in Glovebox 3. Development machining has demonstrated the initial toolpath
and fourth-axis indexing approach for the curved specimen geometry, while the planned glovebox system

 27

incorporates external power and cooling equipment, an in-glovebox induction head and susceptor fixture,
witness specimen temperature control, and an optional water quenching configuration. These additions
preserve the existing traceable path from commercial fuel rod pedigree through specimen fabrication and
mechanical-property measurement.

The HFIR pathway uses ORNL's established irradiation and PIE infrastructure to provide controlled
irradiation conditions and direct mechanical testing of curved SS-JC specimens. Fabrication of the
irradiation specimens and capsule components is underway. Following irradiation, the connected cladding
segments will be recovered at IMET, separated into four individual tensile specimens and two connector
rings, subjected to controlled t@T treatment in Cell 2, and tested using the existing curved specimen tensile
fixture and mechanical test systems. The Cell 2 heating system will use the same basic equipment
architecture and susceptor-mediated treatment method developed for IFEL, with fixture, loading, and
retrieval features adapted for manipulator operation.

The LAMDA pathway provides a complementary small-specimen capability for quantitative hardness
testing and supporting characterization. Twelve previously irradiated retainer rings have been sectioned to
provide 60 independently treatable specimens for the initial campaign. A quartz tube containment concept
has been developed to isolate irradiated specimens from the induction system, and preliminary work has
established the sensitivity of the hardness method to heat treatment-induced changes in Zircaloy-4 and
demonstrated optically measured Vickers hardness testing on the retainer specimen geometry. These
specimens provide a near-term opportunity to begin irradiated t@T testing after the LAMDA heating and
containment configuration is installed and qualified. Connector ring material recovered from the current
HFIR SS-JC campaign will later provide paired hardness, tensile, and characterization data from common
materials and irradiation conditions.

By the time of the reporting cutoff, the principal facility locations, equipment architectures, specimen
geometries, and post-treatment measurement methods had been identified. The induction heating systems
had not been installed or commissioned in their final IFEL, IMET, or LAMDA configurations. The primary
remaining technical requirement is qualification of the specimen thermal history for each configuration.
This work will include finalization of coil, susceptor, fixture, and containment designs; instrumented out-
of-cell testing using thermocouples and pyrometry; establishment of the relationship between witness-
specimen and test-specimen temperature; evaluation of heatup, peak-temperature, dwell, cooldown, and
quench response; and comparison of the direct heating and susceptor mediated methods using matched
thermal treatments and post-treatment property measurements.

FY 2027 activities will also include completion of electrical and cooling services, equipment installation,
surrogate checkout, radiological work controls, and facility-specific operating documentation. The near-
term priority is to install and qualify the LAMDA system and initiate t@T hardness testing of the available
irradiated retainer specimens. In parallel, the IFEL team will complete development of the curved SS-JC
machining and inspection method, install the Glovebox 3 heating system, and finalize the commercial
material test plan. The IMET team will complete the Cell 2 installation and manipulator-compatible fixture
qualification before the current HFIR specimens become available.

The current schedule anticipates completion of the planned HFIR irradiation campaign and initiation of PIE
in late FY 2027. Advancing the Cell 2 treatment and mechanical testing readiness in parallel with capsule
fabrication and irradiation is intended to prevent the heating system implementation from delaying
specimen recovery and PIE. Earlier LAMDA testing and development of the IFEL commercial pathway
will provide additional operating experience and thermal method data before the HFIR campaign enters
PIE.
