Abstract
. Safety policies define what constitutes safe and unsafe AI outputs, guiding data annotation and model development. However, annotation disagreement is pervasive and can stem from multiple sources such as operational failures (annotators misunderstand or misexecute the task), policy ambiguity (policy wording leaves room for interpretation), or value pluralism (different annotators hold different perspectives on safety). Distinguishing these sources matters. For example, operational failures call for quality control, ambiguity calls for policy clarification, and pluralism calls for deliberation about incorporating diverse perspectives. Yet understanding why annotators disagree is difficult. Directly asking annotators for their reasoning is costly, substantially increasing annotation burden, and can be unreliable for both human and LLM annotators as self-reported reasoning often fails to reflect actual decision processes. We introduce Annotator Policy Models (APMs), interpretable models that learn annotators’ internal safety policies from labeling behavior alone, making annotator reasoning visible and comparable without additional annotation effort. We validate that APMs accurately model annotator safety policy (>80% accuracy), faithfully predict responses to counterfactual edits, and recover known policy differences in controlled settings. Applying APMs to LLM and human annotations, we demonstrate two core applications: (1) surfacing policy ambiguity by revealing how annotators interpret safety instructions differently, and (2) surfacing value pluralism by uncovering systematic differences in safety priorities across demographic groups. Together, these capabilities support more targeted, transparent, and inclusive safety policy design. 1 1 1 Code is available at https://github.com/apple/ml-annotator-policy-models

1.
Introduction

To operationalize safety in AI systems, developers create safety policies, constitutions
(
19
)
, or model specs
(
56
)
that explicitly define what constitutes harmful output. Data annotation based on these policies critically impacts all stages of model development. Human and LLM annotations label the training data
(
39
;
6
)
, provide reward signals during training
(
57
)
, and enable model evaluations
(
18
)
. The quality of these annotations directly shapes model behavior and development decisions. Yet annotator disagreement is pervasive, with both human and LLM annotators frequently diverging in their judgments of whether a given output is safe or unsafe
(
4
;
20
;
10
;
44
;
53
)
.

Disagreement can stem from multiple sources.
Operational failures
occur when annotators misunderstand or misexecute the annotation task. For example, many safety datasets contain both user prompts and AI responses but provide only a single binary label
(
39
;
28
)
. Even when task instructions explicitly ask annotators to label only the model response, annotators may conflate the two and label based on the full interaction, or vice versa.

Policy ambiguity
arises when safety policy wording leaves room for interpretation. Safety, like many natural language tasks, struggles with subjectivity–a well-documented challenge in related domains such as hate speech
(
69
;
25
;
64
;
22
;
40
)
, toxicity
(
65
;
66
)
, and harassment detection
(
2
)
. Ambiguous policy language can lead to inconsistent application. For example, a policy prohibiting "offensive language" fails to clarify whether quoting such language for educational purposes constitutes a violation. Finally,
value pluralism
reflects the reality that different annotators may hold genuinely different views on what constitutes harm, shaped by their cultural backgrounds
(
34
)
, personal identities
(
65
;
66
;
47
)
, and values
(
60
;
26
;
27
)
.
We note that the boundary between ambiguity and pluralism can often be blurry as clarifying ambiguous policy often requires making normative choices, and what appears as ambiguity may reveal underlying value disagreements.
For example, a policy requiring outputs to "encourage freedom, equality, and a sense of brotherhood"
(
19
)
is ambiguous in its wording, but any clarification would require normative choices about whose understanding of these values takes precedence and reveal the underlying pluralism.

Distinguishing the sources of annotation disagreement matters because appropriate responses differ. Operational failures call for improved annotator training and quality control. Ambiguity calls for policy clarification and refinement. Pluralism calls for deliberation about how to accommodate diverse perspectives.
Current practice, which typically resolves disagreement through majority vote
(
39
;
29
;
17
)
, conflates these sources, and often fails to address genuine ambiguity while potentially silencing minority perspectives.
Thus, the central question we address is:
how can we understand individual annotator reasoning to distinguish these sources of disagreement and respond appropriately?

To distinguish these sources of disagreement, we need to understand annotators’ interpretations of safety guidelines and
annotator safety policy
, or the implicit rules by which an annotator labels data.
One such approach is asking annotators directly to explain their reasoning.
However, collecting reasoning traces substantially increases annotation burden and cost. Moreover, self-reported explanations can be unreliable: psychology research has established that people often cannot accurately report on their decision processes, and their explanations may reflect post-hoc rationalization rather than true reasoning
(
54
)
. For LLM annotators, the faithfulness of chain-of-thought reasoning is similarly questionable
(
70
;
46
)
. Additionally, the resulting natural language explanations are unstructured and difficult to compare systematically across annotators.

We take an alternative approach: rather than asking annotators to explain their reasoning, we learn their internal safety policies from their labeling behavior. We introduce
Annotator Policy Models (APMs)
, interpretable models that approximate each annotator’s safety policy (see
Figure 1
). APMs are trained on existing annotation data, requiring no additional annotator effort. By expressing annotator reasoning in a shared, interpretable feature space, APMs make individual perspectives visible and directly comparable. While we focus on safety applications in this work, APMs can be used to systematically study annotation in subjective tasks more generally.
APMs enable two core applications. First, they can
surface operational failures and policy ambiguity
by revealing where annotators interpret safety instructions differently. This understanding enables targeted policy refinement. Second, they can
surface value pluralism
by uncovering systematic differences in how demographic groups prioritize safety concerns and which might otherwise be obscured by majority aggregation.
Together, these capabilities support more targeted, transparent, and inclusive safety policy design.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

Interpretable models for annotator policy.
We introduce Annotator Policy Models (APMs) to explicitly model individual annotator safety policy using techniques from ML interpretability.

•

Extensive validation.
We demonstrate that APMs accurately model annotator safety policy (>80% accuracy on LLM annotations), faithfully predict annotator responses to counterfactual edits, and recover known policy differences in controlled synthetic settings.

•

Surfacing operational failures and policy ambiguity.
Using the DICES dataset
(
4
)
consisting of human annotations, we show APMs can identify where annotation instructions are misinterpreted, where policy is unclear, or where annotators disagree with safety policy.

•

Surfacing value pluralism.
On DICES, we show APMs reveal systematic differences in safety prioritization across demographic groups and surface perspectives that majority aggregation would obscure.

2.
Related Work

AI Safety.
As AI systems become ubiquitous, there is an increasing desire to make them safe
(
3
;
8
)
. However, it is unclear what exactly
safety
means in the context of AI. The term has come to encapsulate a broad set of desirable properties for AI, from being helpful and friendly to humans
(
57
;
5
;
6
)
, to behaving like a reasonable human when following instructions (as opposed to reward hacking, finding loopholes in instructions, and other negative side-effects
(
3
)
), to providing guardrails against use by malicious actors
(
8
)
, and finally, avoiding generating biased, harmful, and offensive text
(
6
;
39
)
.
In this work, we focus on the safety of
LLM outputs
,
which generally encapsulates the problem of identifying and mitigating the generation of text with undesirable content such as discrimination, toxicity, offensive language, descriptions of violent, abusive, or sexual activities, and so on. To make models less harmful, developers can apply guardrail models to check generations
(
38
;
24
)
, or fine-tune models on human-annotated data
(
57
)
or on explicit constitutional principles
(
7
)
. Recent work has also studied how to incorporate a broad set of perspectives into alignment by crowdsourcing principles
(
37
)
or adopting pluralistic approaches
(
67
;
16
)
. Typically, these efforts in AI safety rely on annotations to define and evaluate safety, yet how annotators interpret safety guidelines remains opaque.
35
use Bayesian Multi-level Models to study the same human-annotated safety dataset, DICES
(
4
)
, but use demographic attributes as features to predict safety ratings, whereas we study what features of the
data
drive safety annotation behavior and how different annotators reason about safety.
Our work sheds light on annotator interpretation of safety guidelines to improve the quality of the overall annotation process.

Annotator Disagreement.
Since many natural language tasks are inherently subjective, there is a large body of literature studying disagreement in natural language data annotation
(
49
;
66
;
2
)
and specifically safety annotation
(
4
;
44
;
20
;
10
)
. While the traditional approach is to take a majority vote
(
63
)
or other aggregation method
(
36
)
to reduce such disagreement, recent scholarship has emphasized the value of disagreement as signal for downstream applications
(
26
;
42
;
59
)
and to calibrate results
(
31
)
. Specifically disagreement indicates the presence of diverse perspectives which can enable downstream applications to represent a more representative set of values depending on the NLP task
(
60
;
62
)
. Drawing from legal theory,
(
15
)
emphasize the need to understand how annotators apply discretion when interpreting safety principles, and
(
33
)
offer approaches to resolving ambiguity in alignment. To better address and leverage disagreement,
(
23
;
72
;
25
;
71
;
48
)
advocate for modeling individuals and find performance gains and improved representation in downstream tasks. A relevant work by
30
proposes jury learning, modeling individuals to study disagreement, but focus on representation and aggregation strategies for prediction.
Our work has a similar goal of modeling individual annotators, but we use interpretability techniques to elicit internal annotator safety policy, improve safety classification, and understand the diverse perspectives contributing to disagreement. We emphasize that APMs can be used more broadly to study annotator behavior in subjective tasks, although we focus on safety as an application of APMs in this work.

Interpretability.

To understand an individual annotator’s internal safety policy, we use techniques from ML interpretability and explainability to learn interpretable models.
Specifically, we build on Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs)
(
45
)
, which map data to an interpretable set of concepts before fitting a simple interpretable model for classification. To avoid the need for experts to hand-annotate data, we take inspiration from recent work on label-free approaches
(
55
;
68
)
, which use embedding models to replace the intensive expert hand-labeling process. While unsupervised dictionary learning methods such as sparse autoencoders (SAEs)
(
13
)
have become popular concept-based explanation methods, we use CBMs for a few reasons. First, SAEs and other unsupervised dictionary learning methods try to learn all possible concepts that an LLM represents. Because we have a concrete task, we can pick concepts relevant for the task and train a custom concept bottleneck model for the task rather than wasting effort learning all concepts and then picking the safety-relevant ones after the fact. Additionally, unsupervised learning of all possible concepts requires a larger and unnecessary amount of data and compute. Recent related work at the intersection of interpretability and safety uses SAE explanations to understand human preference data
(
52
)
. In contrast, our work aims to understand and compare annotator behavior using a supervised concept explanation.

3.
Methods

In this section, we describe our proposed Annotator Policy Models (APMs). We first formalize the problem setup (§
3.1
), then describe how we construct an interpretable concept space (§
3.2
), and finally detail the design choices that make APMs suitable for modeling safety judgments (§
3.3
).

3.1.
Problem Setup

Consider a safety dataset of text
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
, and a set of
A
annotators with internal policies
m^{1},...,m^{A}
where each
m^{k}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
with 0 indicating safe and 1 indicating unsafe.
From each annotator, we can collect safety labels

\mathbf{y}^{k}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
where
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{k}=m^{k}(x_{i})
for
i=1,...,n
and
k=1,...,A
,
which is standard practice in safety
(
39
;
32
;
4
;
29
)
.

Our goal is to provide an
interpretable
approximation to the each annotator safety policy
m^{k}
, enabling us to understand and compare how different annotators interpret safety guidelines.
We turn to Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs)
(
45
)
, which decompose prediction into two stages: (1) mapping inputs to an interpretable feature space of concepts, and (2) fitting a simple, interpretable function (e.g., logistic regression or decision trees) over this space to make predictions.

Let
g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{c}
be an encoder mapping input text to a binary feature space of
c
concepts, indicating which safety-relevant concepts are present in the text. Let
f:\{0,1\}^{c}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be an interpretable function mapping concepts to a binary safe/unsafe label.
Traditionally,
g
is learned via a multi-class prediction task, requiring experts hand-label concepts for each input.
We bypass this problem by leveraging powerful foundation models to label concepts automatically (§
3.2
), treating
g
as fixed.
We then learn an APM
f^{k}
for each annotator
m^{k}
by minimizing binary cross-entropy loss over their labels
\mathbf{y}^{k}
.

By modeling concepts as binary features, and simply tracking the presence or absence of a concept, we obtain APMs that function as interpretable rules checking for concept presence, rather than as functions of continuous similarity scores that are harder to interpret
(
43
;
61
)
.

3.2.
Building the Concept Space

The concept encoder
g
typically requires human experts to label data with domain-specific concepts, a process that is practical for well-defined domains like medical diagnosis but challenging for
safety
. Safety annotation tasks are often broad and underspecified, with scope spanning discrimination, violence, sexual content, misinformation, and more
(
39
;
4
;
32
;
29
)
. Further, existing datasets provide only coarse category labels rather than fine-grained concept annotations. Thus, we adapt the approach of Label-Free CBMs
(
55
)
to build a safety-relevant concept space.

Concept generation.
We iterate over BeaverTails
(
39
)
, a widely-used safety dataset, and prompt GPT-4o to
"List all words and phrases present in the text that are relevant to the safety of the text."
We collect these into a candidate concept list and remove near-duplicates by embedding concepts with Gemini’s text embedding model,
gemini-embedding-001
, and pruning concepts with cosine similarity greater than threshold
\tau=0.8
.
We apply hierarchical clustering to the remaining candidates and prompt GPT-4o to generate a summary concept for each cluster, for example, combining
bullets
and
gunpowder
into
ammunition
. After pruning, we obtain a concept vocabulary of
c=483
concepts. The full concept list is provided in Appendix
.

Concept labeling.
Given a concept vocabulary, we need a mapping
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{c}
to label each input text with a binary vector indicating which concepts are present. We compute each input’s cosine similarity to each concept by again using
gemini-embedding-001
. We then binarize using the Sparsemax operator
(
50
)
, which projects similarity scores onto a sparse support and sets all nonzero entries to one. Sparsemax was chosen for its tunable hyperparameter that controls sparsity level without requiring a predetermined number of active concepts, unlike top-
k
selection. We set this hyperparameter such that an average of
\sim
10 concepts are active for any given sample. This process yields a binary concept matrix
C\in\{0,1\}^{n\times c}
, where row
i
indicates the safety-relevant concepts present in input
x_{i}
.

Separating facts from interpretation.
An important property of our concept space is that it captures
what is present in the text
(e.g., mentions of weapons, drug references) rather than
how to interpret it
(e.g., whether the mention is harmful). This separation is intentional: by keeping concept labels factual and interpretation-free, we ensure that differences between APMs reflect genuine differences in how annotators
weigh
and
combine
concepts to form their internal policy, rather than differences in concept detection.

3.3.
Annotator Policy Model Design

A key observation motivates our APM design:
safety is one-sided
. It is easier to describe what makes text
unsafe
using a compact set of features than to enumerate everything that makes text
safe
. We would say a sample is unsafe because it contains “offensive language” and not because it lacks all kinds of safe concepts such as restaurant recommendations, code suggestions, and travel advice. Conversely, we would say a sample is safe because it
does not contain
unsafe features, not because it discusses food. This observation motivates two design choices:

Positive features only.
We define features as linguistic concepts
present
in text, excluding negations. We consider features like “mention of a weapon” but not “no mention of a weapon.” This restriction keeps the feature space constructive
(
11
)
and avoids learning nonsensical policy statements like “text is unsafe if it does not mention food”. We assume that negative features are unlikely to be causally related to safety and any such features learned would likely be spurious correlations. Note that meaningful negatives (e.g., “model complies” vs. “model does not comply”) can still be represented as two distinct positive features: “model complies” and “model refuses.”

Safe until proven unsafe.
We design APMs to default to predicting
safe
unless features provide evidence of harm. We are only interested in modeling with features that contribute to an unsafe annotation, and assume that there are no relevant features that make a piece of text more safe, similarly avoiding rules such as “text is safe if it mentions food”. We explore two interpretable model classes for implementing this design:

(1)

Non-Negative Logistic Regression (NNLR).
We restrict logistic regression weights to be non-negative,
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{c}_{+}
. Combined with non-negative binary features, this naturally learns a negative bias term, defaulting to safe and only predicting unsafe when positive features accumulate sufficient evidence.

(2)

Disjunctive Normal Form (DNF).
We learn boolean rules in “ORs-of-ANDs” form
(
21
)
. DNF is especially interpretable for safety: if
any
individual rule (a conjunction of features) fires, the model predicts unsafe. This aligns with intuition as a single policy violation is sufficient to render text unsafe.

Both options yield lightweight, interpretable models that describe where annotators deem text unsafe using only
safety-relevant
features.

4.
Validation of Annotator Policy Models

We validate APMs through a series of experiments on safety annotations provided by different LLM-as-judge models before applying them to human annotations (§
5
). We first describe our experimental setup (§
4.1
) and quantify annotator disagreement to motivate the need for understanding individual policies (§
4.2
). Subsequently, we validate APMs along three dimensions: predictive accuracy (§
4.3
), recovery of known policy differences in controlled settings (§
4.4
), and faithfulness under counterfactual perturbations (§
4.5
).

4.1.
Experimental Setup

Datasets.
We validate APMs on two popular safety datasets: BeaverTails
(
39
)
and WildGuardMix
(
32
)
. BeaverTails consists of user prompt and LLM response pairs with harm subcategories and binary safety labels. We study the safety labels for the LLM response in the “Drug Abuse, Weapons, Banned Substance” subcategory. WildGuardMix similarly consists of prompts and responses with binary safety labels for each component. Again we study the safety labels of the LLM response. Both datasets provide only binary safe/unsafe labels without detailed annotation guidelines, highlighting the ambiguity inherent in safety annotation tasks that motivate the development of APMs.

LLM annotators.
LLMs are increasingly used as annotators in AI development and evaluation
(
7
;
18
)
, making it important to understand how they interpret safety policy. We validate APMs on five LLM annotators spanning two model families: GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini
(
1
)
, and Qwen3-8b, Qwen3-4b, and Qwen2-7b-Instruct
(
73
)
. For each LLM, we generate safety labels on BeaverTails and WildGuardMix using DSPy
(
41
)
(see Appendix
B
for details).

4.2.
The State of Annotator Disagreement

Before validating APM, we first quantify disagreement among LLM annotators to understand the state of annotator disagreement. Figure
2
shows the proportion of samples on which each pair of annotators disagrees on BeaverTails. We find that 10–20% of samples receive conflicting labels across all annotator pairs. Even within model families, where models share similar training data and procedures, annotators disagree on safety judgments (for examples of high-disagreement samples, see Appendix
C.1
).

This level of disagreement has significant implications. Prior work has found that LLM annotators disagree
less
than human annotators on safety tasks
(
51
;
12
)
. If 10–20% of labels would change with different LLM annotators, the effect for human annotators is likely larger. To put this concretely: if the BeaverTails team relabeled their data with different human annotators, 3,000–6,000 labels could change. This variability calls into question the consistency of training signals for AI systems in for critical applications such as safety and motivates the need for tools that can provide a better understanding why annotators disagree on safety tasks.

4.3.
Predictive Accuracy

We evaluate how well APMs learn and replicate annotator behavior. For each LLM annotator, we train both NNLR and DNF models on their labels and evaluate on held-out data. Tables
2(a)
and
2(b)
report accuracy and area under the ROC curve (AUC) for NNLR, and accuracy and true positive rate (TPR) for DNF.

Despite using simple linear or rule-based models, APMs explain a large majority of annotator behavior: NNLR achieves 73–90% accuracy and 0.78–0.95 AUC across annotators; DNF achieves 71–91% accuracy. This is notable given that the annotators themselves are multi-billion parameter LLMs. By selecting the right safety-relevant features, we can approximate their behavior with transparent, interpretable models.

Generalization across datasets.
For APMs to be maximally useful, the concept space should generalize beyond the dataset on which it was constructed. We test this by applying concepts mined from BeaverTails to WildGuardMix (Table
2(b)
). We observe minimal degradation in predictive performance when transferring across datasets, with AUC and TPR trends similar to within-dataset evaluation (Table
2(a)
). This suggests our concept space captures general safety-relevant features rather than dataset-specific artifacts.

Accuracy on ground-truth labels.
We also train APMs on the original human-annotated ground-truth labels provided with each dataset. APMs achieve 90% accuracy and 0.95 AUC on BeaverTails ground-truth, and 76% accuracy and 0.84 AUC on WildGuardMix. The higher performance on ground-truth suggests these labels may be more internally consistent than individual LLM annotator labels, though both are well-modeled by APMs.

4.4.
Recovery of Known Policy Differences

A key goal of APMs is to surface differences in how annotators interpret safety policy. We validate this capability in a controlled setting where we know the ground-truth policy differences.

Synthetic annotators.
We create two synthetic annotators with known policy differences: “Alice,” who treats weapons-related content as safe, and “Bob,” who treats drug-related content as safe. We implement these in an LLM-as-judge by prompting GPT-4o with additional instructions to
treat weapons as safe
or
treat drugs as safe
, respectively. We then train APMs on the resulting labels and inspect whether they recover these policy differences.

Results.
Figure
4
shows the learned APMs for Alice and Bob. As expected, Alice’s APM does not rely on weapon-related features (since she considers them safe), while Bob’s APM does not rely on drug-related features. The APMs correctly recover the known policy differences from annotation behavior alone.

Model diffing.
A key benefit of mapping annotators to a shared function space is that we can systematically compare their policies. We “diff” APMs by computing set differences between the features they use. For NNLR, we compare features with positive weights; for DNF, we compare the conjunctions (rules) used by each model.
In Figure
4
, we highlight the diffed features between Alice (treats weapons as safe) and Bob (treats drugs as safe). We find that the features unique to Alice (
blue
) when diffed with Bob are all drug-related, and features unique to Bob (
orange
) when diffed with Alice are all weapon related. This demonstrates that APM diffing can automatically and scalably identify policy differences between annotators.

4.5.
Counterfactual Validation

Predictive accuracy measures how well APMs replicate annotator behavior on held-out samples. However, a faithful model should also correctly predict how an annotator would respond to
modified
inputs. We evaluate this via counterfactual perturbations.

Evaluation framework.
Let
\tilde{m}^{k}
denote the APM trained to approximate annotator
m^{k}
. If
\tilde{m}^{k}
faithfully models
m^{k}
, then it should correctly predict when
m^{k}
’s judgment would change under input modifications. We test this as follows:

(1)

Take an input
x_{i}
labeled unsafe by annotator
m^{k}

(2)

Use the APM
\tilde{m}^{k}
to identify which features contributed to the unsafe prediction

(3)

Generate a counterfactual
x_{i}^{\prime}
by minimally modifying
x_{i}
to remove or alter these features

(4)

Measure whether both
\tilde{m}^{k}
and
m^{k}
change their predictions on
x_{i}^{\prime}

By construction, the APM’s prediction should change. If the APM is faithful, the annotator’s prediction should also change. If only the APM changes but the annotator does not, the APM has identified spurious features rather than causally relevant ones. We automate counterfactual generation by prompting GPT-4o with the sample, the features present, and a string representation of the APM, asking it to make minimal modifications such that the prediction changes (see Appendix
B.3
for prompt details).

Results.
Figure
3(a)
shows the number of samples labeled unsafe before and after counterfactual modification. APM-guided modifications successfully flip a large proportion of samples from unsafe to safe for the annotators. NNLR-based explanations are more effective for generating counterfactuals, potentially because the (feature, weight) format is easier for LLMs to interpret than DNF rules.

Figure
3(b)
measures faithfulness directly: the proportion of counterfactual samples where the APM and annotator predictions match. We find that APMs match annotators on a majority of modified samples, with DNF models performing better for GPT-family annotators and NNLR better for Qwen-family annotators.

Qualitative observations.
Examining the generated counterfactuals, we find sensible modification strategies: replacing dangerous items with harmless ones, removing specific chemical names, and making instructions more vague. However, we also observe failure modes where the editor simply appends disclaimers (e.g., “Note: This is a fictional description and should not be attempted in real life.”) without substantively changing the content (see Appendix
for examples).

These results validate that APMs capture causally relevant features of annotator decision-making, not just correlates. The counterfactual framework also suggests a practical application: APM-guided editing could help content moderation systems minimally modify unsafe outputs to make them safe.

5.
Applications of Annotator Policy Models

Having validated that APMs accurately and faithfully model annotator behavior on LLM annotations, we now apply them to human annotation data to demonstrate their utility. We show how APMs can
surface operational failures and policy ambiguity
in annotation pipelines (§
5.2
).
We also demonstrate how APMs can
surface value pluralism
by revealing systematic differences in safety priorities across demographic groups (§
5.3
) and identifying perspectives that would be suppressed by majority vote aggregation (§
5.4
).

Warning
: This section includes examples of offensive and harmful data.

5.1.
Setup: Human Annotation Data

We study the DICES 350 dataset
(
4
)
, which contains 350 LLM interactions each rated by 123 human annotators for a total of 43,050 annotations. In DICES, annotators label the safety of model responses to adversarial user prompts. The contexts often contain harmful content, but the annotation target is whether the
model’s response
is safe (e.g., a refusal or deflection should be labeled safe even if the user prompt was harmful). Each sample includes a gold-standard annotation provided by safety experts, annotator demographics, and the annotator’s overall safety judgment as well as annotations of sub-categories such as bias or profanity. We train APMs on each annotator’s overall safety labels.

Concept space for DICES.
We generate a concept space tailored to DICES following the process in §
3.2
, with two adaptations. First, we seed concept generation with safety categories from BeaverTails, WildGuard, and DICES to ensure coverage of relevant harms. Second, because DICES requires distinguishing safe model responses from unsafe user prompts, we include “meta-concepts” describing user and model behavior (e.g.,
model: asks a follow-up question
or
user: asks for advice
). Because these meta-concepts apply to specific interaction components, we use Qwen3-4B-Instruct for concept labeling rather than embedding similarity, which is feasible for 350 unique samples in contrast to the tens to hundreds of thousands of samples in Beavertails and Wildguard. The full concept list is provided in Appendix
.

5.2.
Surfacing Operational Failures and Policy Ambiguity

We train a DNF APM for each human annotator using their labels and then compare each individual APM to a “gold-standard APM” trained on the gold-standard labels using model diffing (§
4.4
). Rules present in an individual’s APM but not the gold-standard APM capture where that annotator’s safety judgments diverge from the gold standard.
Through this analysis, we identify three patterns of divergence, corresponding to operational failures and policy ambiguity (Figure
5
).

Operational failure: Misinterpreting the annotation target.
We find that some annotators systematically labeled interactions as unsafe when the
user
engaged harmfully, even if the model responded safely. This indicates a misunderstanding of the annotation task: annotators were evaluating the full interaction rather than the model response alone. This is an operational failure in the annotation process, not a difference in values.

Policy ambiguity: Deflection as insufficient response.
Other annotators labeled responses as unsafe when the model deflected or changed the subject rather than explicitly refusing or correcting the user. Even if the model avoided directly harmful content, these annotators viewed implicit acceptance of problematic user statements as itself harmful. The gold-standard labels such deflections as safe, but the annotator perspective has merit: the policy is ambiguous about whether deflection constitutes an adequate safety response.

Policy ambiguity: Follow-up questions as harmful.
Some annotators flagged model responses that asked follow-up questions about harmful topics. The model responses in DICES are often friendly and non-committal, expressing confusion or asking clarifying questions rather than refusing. However, follow-up questions can open the door to continued engagement with harmful topics. Again, the gold-standard labels these safe, but the policy does not clearly specify how to handle this edge case.

In all three examples shown in Figure
5
, the gold-standard label is safe while individual annotator labels are unsafe. We additionally report the average annotator label as a percentage, with
>50\%
indicating the majority vote would be an unsafe label. APMs surface disagreements and, crucially, make them
interpretable
: we can see exactly which rules and concepts drive the divergence. This interpretability enables targeted responses: operational failures call for clearer task instructions, while policy ambiguities call for policy refinement or explicit guidance on edge cases.

5.3.
Surfacing Value Pluralism Across Demographic Groups

Beyond individual differences, we investigate whether demographic groups exhibit systematic differences in safety priorities. For each demographic group in DICES (gender, age, education, race/ethnicity), we train an APM on the majority-vote label within that group. We then compare APMs across groups using model diffing.

Qualitative findings.
We explore rules that distinguish demographic groups and identify the samples where they apply. The Millennial APM, when compared with the Gen X+ APM uniquely learns the rule
false factual claims presented as true AND model: kind and empathetic AND model: changing the subject
, which activates when the user argues the “Earth is flat and surrounded by an ice wall” and later the LLM responds kindly and encouragingly about the wonders of nature before diverting with "Have you seen Mount Everest?" While Millennial annotators viewed this redirection as unsafe, Gen X+ were more likely to consider it acceptable. Similarly, college-educated annotators had a unique rule compared to those with high school education or less:
false factual claims presented as true AND manipulative or coordinated misinformation AND user: discussing potential action AND model: refusing to answer or comply with the user
. This captured an instance where an LLM provided what could be perceived as medical advice (tea for a cough) which college-educated annotators labeled as unsafe while those with high school education or less annotated it as safe. These examples illustrate how different groups hold distinct perspectives on appropriate model responses to misinformation and whether models should gently redirect, directly refuse, or provide practical advice in high stakes situations.

Validating that unique rules capture unique behavior.
To ensure that the rules unique to each group’s APM reflect meaningful behavioral differences (rather than artifacts of model fitting), we measure whether unique rules predict unique annotation patterns.

For two groups
A
and
B
with APMs
f^{A}
and
f^{B}
, let
f^{A\setminus B}
denote the rules unique to
A
(present in
f^{A}
but not
f^{B}
) and
f^{A\cap B}
denote the shared rules.
If the unique rules capture meaningful differences, they should preferentially activate on samples where
A
and
B
disagree. We measure this via the
unique rule contribution
:

(1)

\begin{split}\text{URC}(A,B):=\;&\frac{\sum_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbbm{1}[A{=}1,B{=}0,f^{A\setminus B}{=}1,f^{A\cap B}{=}0]}{\sum_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbbm{1}[A{=}1,B{=}0]}\end{split}

where
A{=}1,B{=}0
indicates that group
A
labeled the sample unsafe while group
B
labeled it safe. Intuitively, URC measures the empirical likelihood of unique rules firing on samples that group
A
labels unsafe and group
B
labels safe. A value of 0 indicates unique rules do not explain unique behavior; a value of 1 indicates complete explanation.

Figure
6
reports pairwise URC across demographic groups. We find that unique rules consistently predict unique behavior: groups’ distinct rules activate preferentially on samples where those groups diverge from others. In cases where URC is low (e.g., White raters vs. other groups), the unique rules often represent equivalent alternative rules for samples both groups agreed upon, rather than meaningful disagreement.

We emphasize that we do not claim any causal links between demographic identity and safety values or annotation policy. One limitation of these findings is that they are a proxy for the complex, social forces driving human behavior and they cannot and should not be used to predict individual decisions. An individual can have multiple, intersectional identities; our group-level insights may contradict across identity types, and cannot be taken as prescriptions of what any individual will value. Our point here is to demonstrate how APMs can serve as powerful descriptive tools to highlight areas warranting additional scrutiny to ensure the safety perspectives of various groups are sufficiently prioritized.

5.4.
What Gets Erased by Majority Vote?

The standard practice for aggregating annotations is majority vote. However, prior work argues that majority vote suppresses minority perspectives and can harm downstream model performance
(
25
)
. We use APMs to investigate whether similar suppression occurs in safety policy.

In Figure
7
we compare each demographic group’s APM to an APM trained on the overall majority-vote labels, computing the individual counts
\sum_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbbm{1}[A{=}1,B{=}0,f^{A\setminus B}{=}1,f^{A\cap B}{=}0]
and
\sum_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbbm{1}[A{=}1,B{=}0]

for each group
A
. We find that most demographic groups have rules that identify samples the majority labeled safe but the group deemed unsafe. These perspectives would be erased by majority-vote aggregation: models trained on majority labels would not learn that certain groups consider these samples harmful.

The groups whose disagreements are captured to a lesser degree by their unique APM rules (Gen Z, college degree or higher, women, and white individuals) are incidentally the majority groups in each of their respective categories and so their set of rules is more aligned with the rules learned for the majority vote. However, by fitting APMs to groups and diffing them, we are able to successfully identify a sizable proportion of disagreements, especially for minority groups. We also see disproportionate disagreement when comparing with the gold-standard label and that APMs are able to identify these disagreements (Appendix
A.3
), pointing to potential strategies for mapping and mitigating the known phenomenon of developers injecting their biases into the development of AI systems
(
9
;
58
;
14
)
.

Because our APMs are designed such that rules can only contribute to predicting samples as
more
unsafe (never less), they offer an interesting avenue to address majority suppression: We can directly improve policy coverage by concatenating the unique rules from underrepresented groups to the majority-vote APM. This ensures that samples flagged as harmful by any demographic group are accounted for, providing a path toward more inclusive safety policies. This does not provide an alternative aggregation strategy to majority-vote, but rather an alternative inclusive modeling strategy over individuals. Social choice theory could complement this approach by providing equitable aggregation strategies to produce a more representative APM.

6.
Discussion

Key Findings.

Safety data is critical to responsible AI development, yet annotator disagreement is pervasive in safety annotations.
To understand the sources of disagreement, we model internal safety policies of individual annotators using APMs.
By mapping annotators to a shared feature space, we enable systematic comparison of perspectives. On human annotations, we identify three key insights: (1) operational failures, where annotators misunderstand the task, (2) policy ambiguity, where annotators interpret unclear policy differently, and (3) value pluralism, where demographic groups systematically prioritize different safety concerns.
Understanding and disentangling these three sources of disagreement are essential: it allows developers to respond appropriately, whether by clarifying instructions, refining policy, or embracing pluralistic design. While these findings are focused on annotator perspectives on safety, our method is generalizable and APMs can be used more broadly to understand annotator behavior on subjective tasks.

Recommendations.

Effective use of APMs to generate insights into safety policy requires careful concept design and modeling choices. Concepts need to be specific to the task, general to cover all possible cases, and granular enough to be undisputable and factual. In our experience, starting with the data, studying edge cases and high-disagreement samples, and combining LLM suggestions with manual augmentation resulted in the most effective feature sets. On the modeling side, the DNF and NNLR hyperparameters offer a tradeoff between the simplicity of the resultant model and goodness of fit. As more complex models are generally harder for practitioners to understand we recommend prioritizing interpretability over marginal fit improvements.

Limitations and Future Work.

We conclude with a reflection on the limitations of our work and opportunities for future work. To begin, our concept space depends critically on LLM-generated concepts and embedding-based labeling. A rigorous comparison of alternative concept construction methods, ablation studies of post-processing approaches such as deduplication, and investigation of feature specificity tradeoffs (e.g., “hunting knife” vs. “weapon”) would provide more in-depth guidance for practitioners. Future work could also involve having annotators suggest relevant concepts during annotation to augment or seed the feature space. Second, APMs reveal
which
annotators disagree on
what
, but not
why
. It would be important for future work to study how annotator policy differences reflect distinct values, different risk assessments, or different lived experiences through additional data collection (e.g., annotator explanations) and user studies. Third, the focus of this work was on introducing APMs and their capabilities. As next steps, we look to close the loop between APM insights and concrete actions by developing workflows for annotator training and policy refinement based on APM findings, as well as mechanisms to incorporate pluralistic perspectives into deployed systems. Finally, tracking how APM-guided policy updates change annotator agreement and downstream model behavior would validate the utility of this approach in practice. Extensions to multi-class settings and application to other subjective annotation tasks could also broaden the impact of this work.
