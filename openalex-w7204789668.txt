Impact of post-heat treatment on functionality of multiple-site
compounds: A case study of magnetic anisotropy in La–Co
co-substituted magnetoplumbite-type ferrite
Ade Mulyawan1,2,³, Takeshi Waki1,‡, Yoshikazu Tabata1 and Hiroyuki Nakamura1
1

Department of Materials Science and Engineering, Kyoto University, Sakyo-ku, Kyoto 606–8501, Japan

In compounds with multiple crystallographically inequivalent transition metal sites, the transition metals
occupying each site may play diﬀerent roles. Therefore, when aiming to improve functionality through element
substitution, particularly when the substituent is distributed across multiple sites, diﬀerences in the substituent’s
distribution among sites may signiﬁcantly aﬀect the material properties. This study examined this phenomenon
in magnetoplumbite-type (M-type) ferrites, representative multiple-site compounds. It is known that cosubstitution of La and Co in M-type ferrites enhances uniaxial magnetic anisotropy. Furthermore, experimental
and DFT calculation results have shown that Co occupies multiple crystallographic sites. Additionally, only Co
occupying the tetrahedrally coordinated 4 f1 site has been shown to contribute to the enhancement of uniaxial
anisotropy. This study compared the eﬀects of two post-heat treatments, immediate water quenching and slow
cooling (with a cooling rate of ¹50 K/h), on the magnetic anisotropy of the La–Co co-substituted BaFe12O19
system. The results revealed that the anisotropy ﬁeld value at room temperature for the slowly cooled sample
with the highest Co substitution rate was 22.4 % higher than that of the water quenched sample. Drawing on
DFT calculation results, this increase can be attributed to the relative rise in Co ions occupying 4f1 sites under
slow cooling conditions. These results demonstrate that controlling cation distribution by adjusting post-heat
treatment conditions is a viable option for tuning the functionality of multi-site compounds.

1. Introduction
In compounds with multiple crystallographically inequivalent transition metal sites, the transition metals occupying each site may play diﬀerent roles. When aiming to
improve functionality through element substitution, particularly when the substituent is distributed across multiple
sites, diﬀerences in the substituent distribution among
crystallographic sites may signiﬁcantly aﬀect the material
properties.1–3) Therefore, achieving a precise process in
controlling site occupancy is essential to improve the
performance of these complex multiple site’s materials.
Hexagonal ferrites exist in numerous variations and serve
as a representative platform for studying multiple-site
compounds.4) Magnetoplumbite-type (M-type) ferrites
(A2+Fe3+12O19; where A = Ba, Sr, etc.) are the most extensively studied systems among them due to their vast

The DFT calculations of the electron charge compensated Co2+ doped M-type ferritres1) using the Vienna
Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) code with the HeidScuseria-Ernzerhof hybrid exchange–correlation function
(HSE06)34) indicated that the Co2+ ions most preferably
occupy the 4 f1 site in both Sr and Ba ferrites.1) The occupation energies (¦E) of Co2+ relative to the most stable

4f1 site are 66/109 and 30/139 meV for the 2a/12k sites in
Ba and Sr ferrites, respectively. Co2+ ions do not substantially occupy the 2b and 4f2 sites because their ¦Es, which
are the order of 1 eV, are much higher than the cation
freezing temperature. Not only does Co essentially occupy
the same sites in Ba ferrite and Sr ferrite, but the qualitative order of Co-occupancy ratios has also been veriﬁed
by 59Co-NMR experiments.1) Note that the Co distribution
changes depending on the freezing temperature. By combining the DFT results with Boltzmann statistics, the Co
distribution can be estimated at ﬁnite temperature, allowing us to estimate the multiple-site occupancies near the
cation freezing temperature, especially the 4 f1 site occupancy which largely aﬀects the magnetic anisotropy of the
M-type hexagonal ferrite system. Figure 2 depicts the
probability P(i) that Co2+ ions will occupy a certain crystallographic site i as a function of temperature.1) The P(i) is
obtained using the classical Boltzmann statistics: P(i) =
g(i) exp(¹¦E(i)/kBT )/­jg(i) exp(¹¦E(i)/kBT ).35) Here,
g(i) is the multiplicity of site i in the unit cell, ¦E(i) is
the energy diﬀerence in the ground state when Co2+ ions
occupy site i, kB is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the
absolute temperature. For the P(4f1) curves of both Ba and
Sr ferrites,1) the probability of Co2+ ions increases at lower
temperatures. Thus, post-heat treatments involving the
slow cooling of the sample or annealing at lower temperatures are expected to allow more Co2+ ions to occupy the
4f1 site, thereby enhancing uniaxial magnetic anisotropy.
However, achieving equilibrium at these low temperatures
requires more time, especially near the freezing temperature of cations due to reduced cation mobility. According
to the previous work of SrFe12O19, slow cooling is a more
eﬀective post-heat treatment process than annealing at a
constant temperature for achieving the equilibrium state at
lower temperatures.2) This is due to insuﬃcient dwell time
even after a long annealing process (³2000 h) to reach
equilibrium at lower temperatures. Therefore, slow cooling is preferable to annealing at a constant temperature

2. Experimental methods
A series of polycrystalline La–Co co-substituted M-type
barium ferrites was synthesized by using the solid-state
reaction method. The following chemical powders, with a
purity level of >99.9 %, were used: BaCO3 (3N, 2–3 ¯m),
La2O3 (4N, ³11 « 3 ¯m), ¡-Fe2O3 (4N, ³10 ¯m), and
Co3O4 (3N, ³10 ¯m) from Rare Metallic Co., Ltd. The
powders were weighed according to the stoichiometric
composition of Ba1–xLaxFe12–yCoyO19 (x = y = 0.1, 0.15,

0.2, 0.25, and 0.3). Undoped BaFe12O19 (x = y = 0) was
also synthesized as a reference.
The solid-state reaction method was performed by
mixing the starting chemical powders with acetone as the
medium in an agate mortar for 30 min. Acetone was used
because it evaporates easily at room temperature. Once
all the acetone had evaporated, the resulting powder was
mixed continuously for an additional 30 min. The samples
were pressed into pellets and calcined multiple times in a
furnace at 1523 K under an oxygen pressure of pO2 ¼ 1
atm with intermediate grinding. To observe the Co site
preference in the system and to more clearly see the eﬀect
of the cation freezing temperature, the post-heat treatments
were diﬀerentiated into two processes. First, the samples
were quenched in water immediately after annealing at
1523 K for 24 h. These samples are denoted as water
quenched (WQ). Second, samples that were cooled slowly
over 24 h to room temperature at a rate of ¹50 K/h after
annealing at 1523 K were denoted as slowly cooled (SC).
An oxygen pressure of pO2 ¼ 1 atm was applied during the
heat treatment process to prevent the phase decomposition
and to ensure complete phase formation. To emphasize the
importance of oxygen pressure during the heat treatment
process,36–38) additional La–Co compounds were prepared
under ambient air conditions as a reference material group
and analyzed for their actual La–Co compositions.

actual compositions of samples that were sintered at

3. Results and discussion
Figure 4 shows the XRD patterns of the Ba1¹xLaxFe12¹yCoyO19 compositional series with initial compositions of x = y = 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, and 0.3. All samples
obtained by either water quenching (WQ) or slow cooling
(SC) process, have successfully crystallized into a single
M-type phase [space group P63/mmc (194)].40) Previous
literature has reported the observation of an impurity phase
of lanthanum orthoferrite (LaFeO3) has been observed
during the La–Co co-substitution process for the nominal
composition of x = y = 0.2 in an air atmosphere.41) This
result aligns with the work of Waki et al.,37,38) who noted
that the single M-type phase forms at a higher substitution
levels than in an air atmosphere by increasing the oxygen
pressure during heat treatment. Additionally, as the La–Co
composition increases, the Bragg peaks shift slightly to
higher angles. This shift is due to lattice shrinkage caused
by the La–Co co-substitution. To more clearly observe this
phenomenon, the Rietveld reﬁnement analysis was conducted to extract the crystallographic parameters of the
samples. Figure S1 displays typical data for the highest
nominal composition of x = y = 0.3, and Table S1 provides details of the crystallographic data together and reliability factors. All samples achieved a GoF value between
1.0 and 1.3, together with minimal diﬀerences in background factors, indicating successful reﬁnement with reliable crystallographic data.40)

The anisotropy ﬁeld diﬀerence originates from the difference in cation distribution achieved through the postheat treatment. In the water quenched samples, the rapid
quenching prevents Co2+ ions from occupying the energetically favorable 4 f1 site, which plays a signiﬁcant role
in enhancing uniaxial magnetic anisotropy. Waki et al.2)
studied La–Co co-substituted SrFe12O19 and found that,
after the quenching process from 1473 K, approximately
three-quarters of the Co2+ ions are distributed in the 12k
and 2a sites, which enhances planar magnetic anisotropy

more than uniaxial anisotropy. Conversely, during the slow
cooling process, more Co2+ ions occupy the stable 4f1
site.2) Referring to the energy diﬀerence (¦E ) obtained by
DFT calculations for BaFe12O19, the Co2+ ions go to the
4f1 site in the ground state. The ¦E of the 2a and 12k sites
are +66 and +109 meV, respectively.1) As the temperature
decreases during the slow cooling process, the Co ions
begin to lose their ability to migrate across the possible
sites. They then preferentially reside at the 4f1 site due to
their favorable equilibrium condition. Consequently, when
the temperature eventually reaches the freezing temperature of cations, the Co ions become trapped at the 4f1

T = 1523 K] estimated in Fig. 2 yielded f (TfSC ) = 0.63 for
the Ba system. A comparison of this value with the curve
in Fig. 2 estimates TfSC for the Ba system to be approximately 650 K. Similarly, TfSC for the Sr system is estimated
to be around 625 K when the same analysis is applied to
data with a cooling rate of ¹50 K/h2. Since this analysis is
based on a prediction from DFT calculations, there is no
guarantee that the absolute value of TfSC determined in this
analysis corresponds to the actual freezing temperature.
However, it should be noted that the evaluated TfSC is
higher than Tx (³550 K), the temperature at which the
P(4f1)–T curves for the Sr and Ba systems intersect in
Fig. 2. This situation allows us to infer that the 4 f1 site
occupation probability, P(4 f1), in the slow cooling condition remains slightly higher in the Sr system than in the Ba
system. This explains why the eﬀect of post-heat treatment is more pronounced in the Sr series than in the Ba
series. An even slower cooling rate may invert P(4 f1) in
the Sr series relative to the Ba series, but this is unclear at
realistic cooling rates.
This study emphasizes the importance of post-annealing
treatments in enhancing the magnetic performance of
ferrite-based permanent magnets by controlling cationic
site occupancy speciﬁcally the distribution of Co2+ ions in
La–Co co-substituted M-type hexagonal ferrites. In more
general perspective, it provides valuable insights to optimize functionality in complex oxide systems with multiple
crystallographic sites.

4. Conclusion
This study examined the impact of post-heat treatment
on the functionality of magnetoplumbite-type ferrites as
representative multiple-sites compounds. By comparing the
eﬀects of water quenching and slow cooling (with a cooling
rate of ¹50 K/h) on the La–Co co-substituted BaFe12O19
compositional series, the study revealed that enhancing
uniaxial magnetic anisotropy depends not only on the Co
concentration, but also on their preferential site occupancy
of the tetrahedrally coordinated 4f1 site. The anisotropy
ﬁeld values for the slowly cooled samples were consistently higher than those for the water quenched samples
even though they had the same Co2+ content within error.
The slow cooling process allows more Co2+ ions to occupy

Journal of the Ceramic Society of Japan 134 [9] 634–641 2026

the energetically favorable 4f1 site, thereby enhancing uniaxial anisotropy. Conversely, the water quenching process
causes more Co ions to occupy the 12k and 2a sites, which
are associated with planar anisotropy. This ﬁnding corresponds well with the temperature dependence of the Co2+
distribution determined based on DFT calculations, which
showed that the 4 f1 site has the lowest energy for Co2+
occupancy followed by the 2a and 12k sites. At the highest
level of Co substitution (y = 0.3), the BaFe12O19 system
shows the largest anisotropy ﬁeld diﬀerence between
slowly cooled and water quenched samples; however, this
diﬀerence remains smaller than that of the SrFe12O19 system at the same Co content due to a slightly higher occupancy probability in 4 f1 site (uniaxial). Thus, the eﬀect
of diﬀerent post-heat treatments relative on the anisotropy
ﬁeld is clearer to observe in the SrFe12O19 system. These
results demonstrate that controlling cation distribution
through post-heat treatment is a viable option for tuning the
functionality of multiple-site compounds, oﬀering a strategy applicable to other complex materials with similar
multiple-site structures.
