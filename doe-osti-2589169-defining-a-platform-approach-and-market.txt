Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

The primary objective of this project is to determine the incremental value created with the
medium voltage solid-state transformer (MV SST) technology to different stakeholders in view
of the updated DER grid regulations. This includes studying the benefits of the MV SST
technology in a range of use cases for EV and DER penetration including (1) “corridor charging”
for EVs and (2) solar plus storage (FERC 2222). The potential customers of this technology
include utilities for EV charging, DER installers who must meet utility interconnection
requirements, balancing authorities, and DER aggregators. The traditional transformers on the
grid could be a limiting factor for the EV-grid integration as the distribution transformers were
not designed to handle the dynamic and fluctuating EV charging loads. Thus, the issues such as
voltage fluctuations, increased losses and reduced efficiency [1] can negatively impact the grid
operation. To address these challenges, transformers with flexibility and adaptability become
imperative to meet the evolving energy demands. In this regard, the concept of Medium Voltage
Solid-State Transformers.

 • Assists laboratory in achieving programmatic scope.

(MV SST) is a potential solution. MV SSTs are advanced power electronic devices capable of
regulating voltage levels and efficiently managing power flow in the distribution system. Studies
have shown that SSTs can effectively enhance grid stability, provide faster response times, and
reduce losses compared to traditional transformers [2]. The potential applications for MV SSTs
include integration of EVs [3], and grid integration of DERs such as PVs and batteries [3-10],
and electric aircraft [11]. By incorporating SSTs into the distribution system, grid operators can
enhance the grid’s capacity to handle the influx of EVs and maintain stable voltage levels.

Solid state transformer model:

A solid-state transformer is a power electronic transformer, an AC-to-AC converter. It enables
bi-directional power flow and can enhance power quality performance. It can support different
smart grid challenges over conventional transformers such as voltage sag compensation,
instantaneous voltage regulation, harmonic compensation, power factor correction, auto-
balancing, short circuit protection, and variable-frequency output [1]. The SST model in
OpenDSS is represented as two different components. A medium voltage load on the MV
(primary) side and a low voltage source on the LV (secondary) side as shown in Figure 2. The
LV side supplies the power required by the loads on the LV side and maintains a constant
voltage at the secondary terminal. As SST supports bidirectional power flow, this SST model can
operate in 2 modes: Load mode and Generator mode. The load on the MV side resembles the
SST behavior. The active power on the MV load is equal to the active power supplied by the LV
source plus the SST losses. It equals the active power injected from the LV terminal minus the
SST losses, in case of reverse power flow. SST efficiency is considered as 98% in this study.

The two components, MV side load and LV side voltage source, are defined in OpenDSS with
some random load value at first. Then, the actual MV side load value for every timestamp is
adjusted through Python. The following steps are executed for the power flow simulation with
SST using Python [1]:

 1. First, solve the system snapshot with a random value for the SST load.
 2. Measure the total power supplied by the LV side voltage source.
 3. Calculate the power demand on the MV network by adding the SST losses.
 4. Update the MV side load kW with the power calculated in 3.
 5. Solve the system with updated values.
 6. Check MV side voltage if there is any voltage violation.
 7. If yes, then increase/decrease the reactive power on the MV side load by a fixed step-size
 to perform reactive power control. Check and keep on updating the reactive power until the
 violation is mitigated or until the SST limit is reached. Get the final solution.
 8. If not, then move to another timestep.

This process is repeated every time step power flow solution.

SST reactive power control

Reactive power control functionality at the MV terminal is enabled through either one of the two
criteria.

 1. Volt/var approach – enables reactive power support when the local voltage is beyond the
 defined voltage range.

 2. Relative voltage sensitivity – compares present voltage to previous timestep voltage and
 absorbs/injects reactive power in case the difference is greater than given threshold.

Task 2 Develop use case scenarios: In this task, NREL team will generate a set of EV
penetration scenarios to simulate the EV charging behavior and estimate charging load profiles
and charging requirements. These scenarios will be realistic considering the current and future
EV penetration trends, EV infrastructure requirements, and traffic patterns. Further, the
increasing penetrations of other DERs such as PV introduce generation variabilities, leading to
various operational issues specified above which can be mitigated through the effective use of
BESS. High (50-80%) DER penetration scenarios including PV and BESS will be developed by
NREL team in this task to study the system level impacts of high DER. The metrics useful for
quantifying the value for a distribution system employing the SSTs will be developed by NREL
team. The NREL team will refine the use case scenarios and performance metrics with Eaton
team and the utility, as available, to ensure that they capture boundary conditions of operations.
Estimated completion: 6 months from project start.

presence of high EV penetration. Furthermore, the study also considers the addition of PV and
energy storage to support high EV charging on the grid. Sites for large EV charging capacity are

The study considers 2 specific use case scenarios enabling high EV charging on the feeder.
Comparative study of the two scenarios is performed to explore the benefits of SST on the grid.

Use case scenario 1: Scenario with EV

Several EV charging sites are selected at certain distances across the feeder to enable high power
EV charging stations. In this scenario, the EV charging loads are added at specific sites on the
feeder. Time-series power flow simulation is conducted for a day simulating the maximum
coincident EV charging to investigate the impact of high EV charging demand on the feeder.
Different power system parameters are measured, and the performance of the grid operation is
evaluated through a set of metrics.

Use case scenario 2: Scenario with EV and MV SST

In this scenario, the conventional transformers at the EV charging sites are replaced by MV
SSTs. Time-series power flow study with the same EV charging demand as scenario 1 is
conducted, and the performance metrics are evaluated.

Task 3 Identify potential customers and develop evaluation plan: The SST technology can
facilitate the DER participation in the markets to provide grid services which would provide
numerous sources of value to different stakeholders. The value could be in the form of improved
grid control (volt/VAR control, storage), better visibility as a distributed sensor, synthetic inertia
etc. Thus, the SST technology can benefit utilities, DER aggregators, balancing authorities etc.
This task identifies the potential customers of the SST technology. An evaluation plan will be
developed by NREL team to quantify the incremental value of the SST to these customers. The
simulation scenarios will be designed by NREL team to extract the data required for this
evaluation. Estimated completion: 9 months from project start.

The MV SSTs can provide benefits not only to the stakeholders such as utilities and DER
developers/aggregators, but also to the balancing authorities and the customers. Some of the
benefits to potential stakeholders and customers have been discussed here.

Benefits to stakeholders

 1. Utilities
 SSTs can help the utilities with grid stability and reliability. SSTs can provide voltage
 and frequency regulation, and enable better integration of renewable energy sources, such
 as solar and wind, into the grid. SSTs can potentially reduce the need for maintenance
 and repairs, as they have fewer moving parts and are more durable than traditional
 transformers.

 2. DER developers and aggregators
 SSTs can help DER developers and aggregators to better integrate their products into the
 grid. They can convert DC to AC and vice versa and contribute to voltage and frequency
 regulation. SSTs can help energy storage systems provide grid services, such as
 frequency regulation and peak shaving. This can help increase the reliability of renewable
 energy sources and make them more attractive to utilities.

 SSTs can help DER aggregators improve the performance of their systems, by providing
 faster response times and more precise control over power flow. This can help DER
 aggregators optimize the use of DERs, improve system reliability, and potentially
 increase revenue from ancillary services and demand response programs.

 15

SST benefits to Balancing Authorities

 1. Improved grid stability and reliability: SSTs provide voltage and frequency regulation
 that improves grid stability and reliability. This is important for balancing authorities, as
 they are responsible for ensuring the real-time balance of supply and demand.

 2. Increased grid flexibility: SSTs can enable the integration of renewable energy sources,
 such as solar and wind, into the grid. This can help balancing authorities meet their
 renewable energy targets and reduce their reliance on fossil fuels. SSTs can also help
 balance the grid during periods of high demand, by providing reactive power support and
 reducing the need for expensive peaking power plants.

 3. Reduced maintenance and repair costs: SSTs have fewer moving parts and are more
 durable than traditional transformers. This can help reduce the need for maintenance and
 repairs, which can be costly for balancing authorities. Thus, SSTs can provide long-term
 cost savings despite higher initial costs.

 4. Improved efficiency: SSTs are generally more efficient than traditional transformers,
 particularly when it comes to converting power from DC to AC or vice versa. This can
 help balancing authorities reduce their energy losses and improve overall grid efficiency.

Benefits to customers

SSTs can improve power quality for industrial and commercial customers, leading to increased
efficiency and productivity. They can also help reduce downtime and maintenance costs, as they
are less likely to fail than traditional transformers. SSTs can potentially lead to lower electricity
costs for consumers, as they can improve grid efficiency and reduce the need for expensive
upgrades to the grid infrastructure.

4.2 Value Analysis

An economic analysis was performed to explore the cost-benefit of a MV SST with DC-to-DC
converters to provide Direct current fast charging (DCFC) charge regulation to multiple charge
stations as against a conventional distribution transformer with downstream AC-to-DC rectifier
and DC-to-DC converters. In the latter configuration, the low-frequency iron core transformer
provides isolation, the DC-to-DC converter(s) controls the charge voltages, hence current is
applied to the EV batteries according to the chosen charging strategy, and the AC-to-DC rectifier
delivers an appropriate power level to the DC-to-DC converter(s). [2] The standard MV SST
configuration of interest has an active AC-to-DC rectifier stage that feeds a DC-to-DC converter
which can regulate DC voltage and supply power to a power inverter. If there is no AC
secondary load, the inverter could potentially be eliminated. For a single charging station, the
SST could be tapped at the regulated DC terminal to provide regulated DC power to an EV. To
manage charging to multiple DCFC stations, however, a corresponding number of smaller DC-
to- DC converters would be needed. Since either the rectifier or the DC-to-DC converters can
provide isolation, it may be more cost-effective to provide isolation once in the rectifier, instead
of in each converter, simplifying the converters. The economic opportunity to be evaluated
consists of three parts. First, there are savings to be realized by substituting the SST for a
conventional distribution transformer, an AC-to-DC converter, and multiple regulated DC
outputs, obviating the cost of the conventional transformer in its own housing, its concrete pad,
and its secondary connections and wiring to the AC-to-DC converter. Second, the expense of
conventional transformer power losses will be avoided; the power electronics losses in the
respective configurations will be roughly equivalent. Finally, there may be avoided costs through
use of the MV SST’s capability to provide reactive power onto the distribution system–a
capability that is not typically available from behind-the-meter or secondary voltage power
electronics in the configuration using the conventional transformer. This analysis considers the
first two aspects of the economic opportunity, but not the last, as it is likely to be circuit-specific
and depend upon circuit planning and design. This aspect does, however, have the potential to
obviate capital expenditure for voltage regulation equipment and corresponding maintenance
expenses. To be cost-effective, such an SST would have to have a lower life cycle cost than the
conventional transformer, rectifier, converters, and conventional transformer losses. For the
purposes of this rough cost-benefit analysis, we need not know the initial cost of the DCFC
power electronics or an SST and can assume them to be roughly equal. Likewise, we can assume
the losses in the SST and DCFC power electronics to be roughly equivalent, but for possible
small additional losses in the SST for an isolating rectifier. If the avoided cost of the low
frequency iron core transformer and its losses allows for a margin in the cost of the SST such
that it can include nominal additional cost of an isolating rectifier, multiple DC-to-DC converters
and marginally higher power electronics losses due to the isolating conversion, the SST in this
application should be cost-effective. This is the cost comparison that was made in this analysis.
The assumed economic perspective of the analysis is that of a taxable entity such as an investor-
owned utility experiencing the cost-benefit of ownership and operation and maintenance of the
distribution system. The analysis took the results of the simulated scenarios and calculated
economic effects and compared the economics of the scenarios. The analysis also calculated
projected maintenance expense due to switching operations of switching and tap-change devices
on the system. The analysis assumed that the simulated 24-hour period was representative and
typical and extrapolated the expenses from daily to annual, then to multi-year for discounted

Summary

EPRI J1 feeder was selected for the distribution system analysis. The equivalent MV SST model
for power flow was implemented and tested in OpenDSS and the reactive power functionality
was developed. Five different sites were chosen as the high-power EV charging sites. The use
case scenarios were developed to study the value of SST on the distribution feeder in presence of
high EV charging. Using SST instead of conventional transformers showed the improved voltage
profile, reduced substation reactive power demand, maintained the secondary side voltage at 1
per unit, and regulated the MV side voltage. Therefore, SST can benefit local sites by regulating
voltage. It was observed that the regulators and capacitors installed at different locations in the
feeder help maintain the overall feeder voltage, while having SST can benefit with improved
voltages at conditions where regulators and capacitors were not able to maintain the voltage. The

 31

result analysis suggests that the integration of MV SSTs into the grid has the potential to enhance
EV charging infrastructure, DER operation, and grid performance significantly. As the number
of MV SSTs is increased, it is reasonable to expect even more substantial benefits for the overall
grid stability and efficiency. This research paves the way for further exploration and deployment
of MV SSTs in power distribution systems to accommodate the increasing demand for EV
charging while ensuring optimal grid operation. Furthermore, the economic analysis indicates
that an MV DCFC-configured SST could much more economically serve DCFC loads than T-R-
D configurations. This represents a potential opportunity for electric utilities to provide DCFC
service more cost-effectively through economic application of MV DCFC-configured SSTs.
Additionally, further investigation into the coordinated operation of MV SSTs and the existing
voltage regulators on the grid can be beneficial for the optimal operation of both devices.
