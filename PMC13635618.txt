Combinatorial cis‐regulatory elements shape temporal effector expression in 
Ustilago maydis

Georgios Saridis, Janina Werner, Katharina Stein, Luyao Huang, Ute Meyer, Jan Muelhoefer, Namarta C Singh, Gunther Doehlemann

Abstract

Plant‐pathogenic fungi secrete small proteins, termed effectors, to reprogramme host metabolism and suppress immune responses during infection. Although transcriptional waves of effector expression have been described in several pathosystems, the cis‐regulatory elements underlying their temporal coordination remain largely unknown. Here, we investigate the temporal regulation of effector genes in the biotrophic smut fungus 
Ustilago maydis
, a model organism for fungal plant pathogenesis. By integrating transcriptome reanalysis with comparative promoter motif enrichment across biotrophic fungi, we identify distinct promoter motifs associated with defined infection phases. In 
U. maydis
, three candidate cis‐regulatory elements correlate with early, proliferative and late infection stages, respectively. Positional enrichment relative to transcription start sites supports their regulatory relevance. Functional dissection of the native pep1, stp1, and cmu1 promoters revealed strongly context‐dependent contributions of these elements and synthetic promoter assays showed that the early‐phase motif acts as an activating and the proliferative motif as a repressive element. Together, our findings support a combinatorial cis‐regulatory code in which activating and repressive promoter elements are integrated in a promoter‐specific manner to shape temporal effector expression during 
U. maydis
 infection. These elements provide a framework for engineering synthetic fungal promoters with tunable transcriptional outputs.
We identified promoter motifs associated with infection phases in 
Ustilago maydis
 and analysed their positional distribution relative to transcription start sites and generated a synthetic minimal promoter that drives in‐plant gene expression.

To effectively fulfill their infection cycle, plant‐pathogenic fungi secrete small proteins called effectors. Effectors act either in the apoplast or within host cells, where they manipulate host metabolism and suppress immune responses (Thomma et al. ). Because effectors promote host colonization while being subject to recognition by the host immune system, they are exposed to strong reciprocal selection pressures characteristic of the host–pathogen arms race (Ingle et al. ). This makes effectors rapidly evolving components of pathogen virulence and important targets for understanding host–pathogen interactions and developing disease control strategies (Fouché et al. ). Rapid effector diversification raises the question of whether regulatory logic may be more conserved than effector sequence itself.

Smut fungi are biotrophic pathogens of the Ustilaginales, infecting a wide range of monocot and dicot hosts, including economically important cereals, such as barley, wheat, and maize. The maize smut 
Ustilago maydis
 is an established model system for biotrophic fungal genetics and ranks amongst the top 10 most scientifically significant fungal pathogens (Dean et al. ; Matei and Doehlemann ). It occurs as a saprophytic, nonpathogenic haploid yeast‐like form that can be grown in artificial media, and as a biotrophic dikaryotic filamentous form, generated by the fusion of two compatible haploid cells (Kämper et al. ; Kruzel and Hull ). The infection cycle of 
U. maydis
 comprises (i) penetration, (ii) in planta proliferation and (iii) tumour formation and sporulation, the latter being the typical smut phenotype (Banuett and Herskowitz ; Brefort et al. ). These distinct developmental stages suggest that successful maize colonization requires temporally coordinated effector deployment.

Many 
U. maydis
 effectors have been characterized for their function and mode of action, and a number of transcriptional regulators have been identified that separate effectome activation into two waves (Djamei et al. ). Early effectome activation is closely linked to cell fusion and filamentation, which activate the b mating‐type locus genes and produce the heterodimeric bE/bW homeodomain transcription factor (Gillissen et al. ; Kämper et al. ). This master regulator of early pathogenic development in turn activates a group of effector genes as well as the zinc finger transcription factor Rbf1 and its target transcription factors Hdp2 (homeobox), Biz1 and Mzr1 (C2H2 zinc finger) (Flor‐Parra et al. ; Heimel et al. ; Zheng et al. ). Uncharacterized transcription factors correlated with early effectome expression contain bHLH, TEA/ATTS, HMG, CCHC and winged‐helix domains (Lanver et al. ). Upon in planta proliferation and hyphal development, the Zn2Cys6 transcription factor Zfp1 has been connected with the regulation of 112 effectors and 50 transcription factors, including Ros1 (WOPR), which successively activates the late effectome while simultaneously attenuating the wave of early effectors during sporulation and tumour formation (Cheung et al. ; Tollot et al. ). Additionally, Nlt1 (APSES), Fox1 (forkhead) as well as novel proteins containing Zn2Cys6, bHLH, C2H2, and winged‐helix domains have been associated with effector gene expression at late infection stages (Lanver et al. , ; Zahiri et al. ). Despite this detailed knowledge of the transcription factor hierarchy controlling pathogenic development, the cis‐regulatory elements that contribute to temporal effector expression remain largely unidentified.

Here, we systematically identify promoter motifs associated with phase‐enriched effector expression in 
U. maydis
 using comparative genomics, transcriptome reanalysis, and functional promoter validation. By integrating evolutionary and stage‐specific analyses, we ask whether temporal effector deployment is encoded at the promoter level.

To identify putative cis‐regulatory elements enriched in the promoters of fungal effector genes, the genomes of different smuts, rusts and powdery mildews were analysed (Figure , ). Protein fasta files from nine smut fungi (
Ustilago maydis
, U. bromivora, 
U. tritici
, 
U. nuda
, U. loliicola, U. hordei, Sporisorium reilianum, S. scitamineum, Melanopsychium pennsylvanicum), five rust fungi (Puccinia sorghi, P. striiformis f. sp. tritici, P. triticina, 
P. graminis
, Melampsora larici‐populina) as well as five powdery‐mildew fungi (Blumeria hordei, 
B. graminis
 f. sp. tritici, Erysiphe necator, E. neolycopersici, Golovinomyces cichoracearum) were used to identify orthologous genes among the tested species. Based on this orthology analysis, a species tree was constructed using 
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
 as a general outgroup, and Testicularia cyperi as outgroup for the smut clade (Figure ). In parallel, the same files were used to predict each total effectome. Transmembrane domains were predicted with Phobius and signal peptides with SignalP. Proteins with transmembrane domains and without signal peptide were filtered out, and the remaining sequences, defined as the predicted secretome, were analysed with EffectorP, leading to effectome sizes spanning from 157 in U. loliicola to 1266 in 
P. graminis
 (Table ). The 500 bp promoter sequences of all predicted effector genes were then used to identify enriched motifs with XSTREME from the MEME Suite, and motifs passing the thresholds (p < 0.05 and enrichment sites > 10%) were compared using STAMP. The smut M. pennsylvanicum and the powdery mildews E. necator, G. cichoracearum and E. neolycopersici showed no significant motif enrichment. Notably, one motif with consensus sequence GTGGG was specifically enriched in the effectome promoters of all tested smuts but not in rusts and powdery mildews (Figure ). Two rust‐specific enrichments were identified with consensus sequences KCGA and TGCK, while further consensus motifs derived from either smuts and powdery mildews (ATGAA), powdery mildews and rusts (CGTG), or smuts and rusts (CWGTR and CGGCS) (Figure ).

To dissect phase‐specific effector regulation in 
U. maydis
, we re‐analysed RNA‐seq data from Lanver et al. () and grouped infection into three biologically defined stages: (i) Phase 1 (0.5–2 days post‐inoculation [dpi]; penetration and early colonization), (ii) Phase 2 (4–6 dpi; hyphal proliferation), and (iii) Phase 3 (8–12 dpi; tumour formation and sporulation) (Figure , Table ). Effector genes were assigned to a phase if more than 50% of their cumulative expression occurred within that interval, which yielded 69 Phase 1‐, 24 Phase 2‐ and 18 Phase 3‐enriched effectors (Figure , Table ), consistent with temporally structured transcriptional waves during infection. To test whether the phase classification depended on the chosen cut‐off, we re‐derived effector assignments across thresholds from 0.3 to 0.7 (Table ). Phase 1 remained the largest effector set at every threshold (164, 115, 69, 40 and 27 effectors at thresholds of 0.3–0.7, respectively), and no effector was assigned to a different phase at any threshold: the Phase 1 sets were strictly nested, with less stringent cut‐offs recruiting additional effectors and more stringent cut‐offs retaining progressively phase‐restricted subsets. The threshold therefore determines only how phase‐restricted an effector must be to be included, and not the phase to which it is attributed, indicating that the temporal structure of the effectome is not an artefact of the classification criterion.

Promoter regions (500 bp upstream) of phase‐enriched effectors were subjected to motif enrichment analysis using XSTREME. Seven significantly enriched motifs (p < 0.05; enrichment sites > 55%) were identified across the three infection phases. Two motifs were associated with Phase 1 (TGCATCT [P1‐1] and GTGGG [P1‐2]), two with Phase 2 (TTGNCG [P2‐1] and TTCNTCT [P2‐2]), and three with Phase 3 (TAGTT [P3‐1], TGNTGT [P3‐2], and TSTTTS [P3‐3]) (Figure ). Among these, GTGGG (Phase 1), TTGNCG (Phase 2), and TSTTTS (Phase 3) displayed the strongest phase‐restricted enrichment patterns and were prioritised for further analysis.

To assess motif conservation and specificity, we calculated the enrichment of every motif in effectome versus non‐effectome (background) promoters of each tested species. For the motif TGCATCT [P1‐1], 6 out of the 9 tested smuts showed enrichment in the effectome, with 
U. maydis
 demonstrating the highest enrichment, while for S. scitamineum, S. reilianum and M. pennsylvanicum, the enrichment was towards the background (Figure ). No significant interclade differences were observed. For the other motif of Phase 1, GTGGG [P1‐2], an interclade difference was present, with all smut motifs clustering closely and demonstrating a strong preference of this motif towards smut effectome promoters (Figure ). Motif TTGNCG [P2‐1] revealed an overall negative enrichment (towards the background) for all fungal clades; however, individual species (
U. maydis
, U. bromivora, S. scitamineum, P. sorghi, 
P. graminis
, B. hordei, G. cichoracearum) showed effectome enrichment of the P2‐1 motif. Motif TTCNTCT [P2‐2] had a powdery mildew‐specific enrichment, with all its tested species demonstrating effectome enrichment, while from smuts, only 4 out of 9 (
U. maydis
, U. loliicola, S. reilianum and M. pennsylvanicum) showed a similar pattern. 
U. maydis
 had again the strongest enrichment. Motifs TAGTT [P3‐1] and TGNTGT [P3‐2] demonstrated very similar patterns, with a preference towards smut effectomes, and the highest enrichment in 
U. maydis
, the only exceptions being U. hordei for motif P3‐1 and 
U. tritici
 for P3‐2 (Figure ). Finally, motif TSTTTS [P3‐3] showed an overall enrichment towards the background for smuts and rusts and a slight enrichment towards the effectome of powdery mildews, with 
U. maydis
, S. reilianum and 
U. tritici
 being the only smuts with an effectome enrichment (Figure ).

Next, the occurence of each motif in a 1 kb region spanning the transcription start site (TSS) was calculated for both the effectome and the non‐effectome background, and their frequencies were plotted (Figure ). Several motifs showed non‐random positional enrichment upstream of the TSS. GTGGG [P1‐2] displayed a broad enrichment between 150 and 450 bp upstream of the TSS, consistent with promoter‐proximal regulatory regions. TTGNCG [P2‐1] showed enrichment across the 0–450 bp upstream region, whereas TSTTTS [P3‐3] exhibited discrete upstream peaks. Some motifs also displayed downstream enrichment. Overall, the positional bias of multiple motifs supports their potential regulatory function. We further tested whether known transcription factor binding sites correlate with our motifs by subjecting their consensus core sequences to TomTom (MEME Suite) analysis, against the JASPAR Fungal database. The P1‐1 motif could be similar to an APSES‐type binding site (p = 0.001) and the P1‐2 motif showed similarity to C2H2 zinc‐finger transcription factor binding motifs (p < 0.001), while the P2‐1 motif might resemble another C2H2 binding site (p = 0.002) and the P2‐2 motif showed similarity to a heat shock factor binding motif (p = 0.002). Finally, the P3‐1 motif showed similarity to binding motifs of a homeodomain (p = 0.015), the P3‐2 motif of a bZIP (p = 0.029) and the P3‐3 motif of a forkhead/winged helix (p = 0.008) transcription factor (Table ).

To investigate the phase‐specificity of the identified motifs, we correlated effectome gene expression with motif occurrence in their promoters. We first tested whether motif copy number was associated with the maximum expression potential of individual effector genes. GTGGG [P1‐2] showed a positive association, and promoters containing one or two GTGGG copies exhibited elevated early‐stage expression compared to promoters lacking the motif (Figure ). To further dissect the possible influence of each motif on phase‐specific expression, 
U. maydis
 effectors were then split into groups based on the number of occurrences of each motif in their 500 bp promoters, ranging between 0 and 6, and the average expression of each group was calculated separately for Phases 1–3 (Figure ). Motif TGCATCT [P1‐1] showed highest expression with up to two copies but no phase specificity. TTGNCG [P2‐1] enabled expression with 0–3 or 6 copies, with a strong Phase 2 preference at 1–3 copies. TTCNTCT [P2‐2] resembled [P1‐1], allowing expression only up to two copies without phase specificity. TAGTT [P3‐1] showed a similar pattern, with slight Phase 3 preference. TGNTGT [P3‐2] exhibited higher expression at 1, 3 or 4 copies, with weak Phase 3 specificity at 1 or 4 copies. Finally, the motif TSTTTS [P3‐3] had the clearest specificity towards expression at Phase 3, especially when 3 or 4 copies were present in the promoters (Figure ).

The three prioritized motifs, namely GTGGG [Phase 1; P1‐2], TTGNCG [Phase 2; P2‐1] and TSTTTS [Phase 3; P3‐3], were used to identify the most fitting effector candidates for follow‐up characterization. 49 effectors containing all three motifs in their 500 bp promoters were selected and their expression was determined using the re‐analysed RNA‐seq dataset from Lanver et al. () (Figure ).

To experimentally test whether enriched motifs directly influence effector gene expression, we performed promoter mutagenesis using the pep1, stp1, and cmu1 promoters to drive GFP expression at the ip locus. For pep1 six GTGGG motifs (positions −56, −160, −240, −300, −394, −584), two TTGNCG motifs (−671, −839), and two TSTTTS motifs (−720 and −885) were mutated. For stp1, three GTGGG motifs (−209, −240, −319) and six TSTTTS motifs (−44, −400, −408, −576, −653, −854) were mutated. For cmu1, six GTGGG motifs (−274, −322, −333, −353, −579, −890), one TTGNCG motif (−870), and one TSTTTS motif (−779) were mutated (Figure ). All mutations introduced the least probable nucleotide at the respective position, according to the confusion matrix of the identified motif (Table ). Reporter expression was analysed at 3, 6 and 10 dpi, corresponding approximately to the three infection phases defined above.

For pep1, three promoter variants were compared with the wild‐type promoter: mp1 (GTGGG motifs mutated); mp2 + 3 (TTGNCG and TSTTTS motifs mutated); and mpALL (all selected motifs mutated). For stp1 and cmu1, mp1 and mpALL promoter variants were generated. Quantification of gfp expression revealed promoter‐specific contributions of the selected cis‐regulatory elements to expression in planta (Figure ). In the pep1 promoter, mutation of the Phase 1‐associated GTGGG elements strongly reduced reporter activity at all three time points, whereas combined mutation of the Phase 2‐ and Phase 3‐associated elements had little effect. The mpALL promoter showed a similarly strong reduction, identifying GTGGG as the dominant positive regulatory input under the tested conditions. Consistently, confocal imaging at 2 dpi showed reduced GFP signal in the mpALL strain relative to the wild‐type pep1 promoter (Figure ). Phase 1‐associated elements also contributed to stp1 promoter activity across all three time points, with the strongest effect observed at 6 dpi (Figure ). In cmu1, mutation of the Phase 1‐associated motifs had little effect at 3 and 6 dpi but caused a modest reduction at 10 dpi, whereas disruption of all selected motifs significantly reduced promoter activity at all three time points (Figure ). Together, these results show that the contribution of an individual motif class depends strongly on its native promoter context.

To test whether the Phase 1‐ and Phase 2‐associated motifs can exert regulatory activity outside their native promoter context, we generated synthetic minimal promoters containing five tandem copies of the intact or mutated motifs (pmini1 and pmini2) driving gfp expression (Table ). Five GTGGG copies enhanced reporter expression relative to the corresponding mutated Phase 1 motif (AATCA), demonstrating autonomous positive regulatory activity (Figure ). Conversely, five copies of the Phase 2‐associated motif strongly restricted reporter expression, whereas motif disruption (GCAGTC) resulted in pronounced derepression, identifying this element as a sequence‐dependent repressive module. Neither synthetic system reproduced strict phase specificity (Figure ). Thus, the Phase 1‐ and Phase 2‐associated motifs can function autonomously as opposing regulatory modules, whereas their effects in native promoters depend on the surrounding cis‐ and trans‐regulatory context.

Biotrophic infection requires not only an appropriate effector repertoire but also the precise temporal coordination of its expression. The distinct developmental transitions of the 
U. maydis
 infection cycle impose different physiological and host‐interaction demands, including immune suppression, metabolic manipulation, and tissue reprogramming, making it plausible that the pathogen deploys its effectors in a temporally structured manner, consistent with previous findings that effector gene expression occurs in an organ‐ and cell type‐specific manner (Matei et al. ; Skibbe et al. ). While the hierarchical transcription factor cascades orchestrating pathogenic development in 
U. maydis
 have been extensively characterised (Djamei et al. ; Heimel et al. ; Lanver et al. , ; Romeis et al. ), the cis‐regulatory logic underlying temporally coordinated effector expression at the promoter level has remained largely unresolved.

Here, we identified infection‐phase‐associated promoter motifs, analysed their positional distribution relative to transcription start sites, and provided functional evidence that distinct motifs can exert activating or repressive regulatory activity. Re‐analysis of available RNA‐seq data (Lanver et al. ) revealed clearly defined phase‐enriched effector clusters, consistent with previous descriptions of transcriptional waves during infection (Djamei et al. ; Lanver et al. , ), and our identification of promoter motifs enriched within these clusters suggests that temporal effector deployment is, at least in part, encoded at the cis‐regulatory level. Whether these phase‐enriched motifs represent direct binding sites of known regulators or define additional regulatory layers remains to be determined. Comparative motif analysis across multiple biotrophic fungi further revealed that the GTGGG motif is consistently enriched in smut effectome promoters but not in rusts or powdery mildews, suggesting lineage‐specific differences in effector promoter architecture.

Certain motifs also displayed enrichment downstream of annotated TSS. Interpretation of these positions is constrained because no experimental genome‐wide TSS map exists for 
U. maydis
 and untranslated region (UTR) coordinates are not defined. Consequently, sequences that are functionally promoter‐proximal or located within genuine 5′ UTRs may be assigned nominally downstream positions. Experimental TSS mapping at single‐nucleotide resolution, for example by CAGE or TSS‐seq across infection stages, will be required to resolve the precise position of these motifs relative to transcription initiation.

Among the identified motifs, GTGGG [P1‐2] showed the strongest association with Phase 1 expression and its mutation identified it as the dominant positive regulatory input within the pep1 promoter under the tested conditions, an activity that was confirmed by tandem GTGGG elements in the synthetic promoter assay. Importantly, GTGGG activity was not restricted to Phase 1, indicating that its association with early effector expression does not translate into an autonomous temporal switch.

Pep1 is a key virulence factor required for successful biotrophic colonization (Doehlemann et al. ), making regulation of its expression particularly relevant for host compatibility. Motif similarity analysis revealed similarity of GTGGG to C2H2‐type transcription factor binding motifs, which is compatible with the extensive transcriptional network controlling early pathogenic development, including bE/bW and downstream regulators such as Rbf1, Biz1, Hdp2, and Mzr1 (Flor‐Parra et al. ; Gillissen et al. ; Heimel et al. ; Kämper et al. ; Zheng et al. ). Later infection stages involve regulators such as Zfp1 and Ros1 (Cheung et al. ; Tollot et al. ). However, motif similarity alone cannot establish direct binding, and identification of the cognate transcription factor will be required to position GTGGG mechanistically within this network. The contribution of the same motif class differed markedly among native promoters. Whereas both pep1 and stp1 depended strongly on intact Phase 1‐associated elements, cmu1 was comparatively robust to their mutation alone and showed a pronounced reduction only when all selected motifs were disrupted. Notably, pep1 and stp1 display nearly identical temporal expression profiles (Lanver et al. ) and encode components of the same Stp complex implicated in effector translocation, raising the possibility that shared P1‐associated regulatory inputs contribute to the coordinated expression of functionally linked complex components during early biotrophic development (Ludwig et al. ). Context dependence was also evident for the Phase 2 associated element, which acted as a strong repressive module in the synthetic promoter but whose combined mutation with Phase 3 elements had little detectable effect in native pep1.

Together, these findings support a model in which temporal effector expression emerges from the integration of activating and repressive cis‐regulatory inputs with promoter‐specific architecture and stage‐dependent trans‐acting factors, rather than from individual motifs functioning as autonomous temporal switches. Several limitations remain. The identified motifs are short and may therefore occur by chance, although their enrichment, positional bias, association with phase‐enriched expression, and experimental perturbation collectively support their regulatory relevance. In addition, Phases 2 and 3 elements were mutated together in pep1; the Phase 3‐associated element has not yet been tested independently, and the corresponding trans‐acting factors remain unknown.

Beyond their mechanistic implications, the identified elements provide potential building blocks for synthetic manipulation of fungal gene expression. Future studies should define the autonomous regulatory activity of the Phase 3‐associated element, resolve the individual contributions of Phase 2 and Phase 3 motifs in native promoters, determine how motif combination, spacing, and copy number influence regulatory output, and identify the corresponding trans‐acting factors. These approaches should reveal how promoter architecture integrates activating and repressive inputs into temporally coordinated effector expression and may ultimately enable the rational design of tunable fungal promoters.
