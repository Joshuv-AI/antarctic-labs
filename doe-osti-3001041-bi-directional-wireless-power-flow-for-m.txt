II. Executive Summary

In this project, CALSTART, ORNL, UPS, and Workhorse Group collaborated to model, research,
analyze, design, develop, integrate, and demonstrate a bi-directional wireless power transfer
system (BWPT) suitable for Class 5 and Class 6 medium-duty plug-in hybrid delivery trucks. The
project team designed, developed, integrated, and tested a bi-directional wireless charging
system capable of meeting the 11-inch ground clearance needed for UPS delivery trucks. After
integrating to a Workhorse manufactured plug-in hybrid electric vehicle (PHEV), the system
performance was demonstrated at ORNL.

Within the first budget period of the project (May 2017-May 2018), the team completed the
modeling, simulations, design, and analysis of the system power conversion stages including the
3-phase active front-end rectifier with power factor correction (also grid interface inverter),
primary-side HF inverter (also primary-side rectifier), primary and secondary-side resonant tuning
components, primary and secondary-side electromagnetic coupling coils, vehicle-side HF rectifier
(also the vehicle-side HF inverter). Hardware for both the primary and secondary sides were
completed and the power conversions stages were successfully tested. All of the subsystems and
components were validated based on their parameters and performance metrics.

The second budget period (May 2018 – February 2020) focused on BWPT technology integration
and vehicle integration, system hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing, and fleet level BWPT system
benefits analysis. During the second budget period, the team built all the power electronic
converter hardware and fabricated all the passive components including the 3-phase grid-
connected inductors, capacitor systems, resonant tuning circuit components, coupling coils, and
the vehicle-side filter components. The team also designed and developed two DSP interface
boards for the primary and secondary sides. The team successfully tested the operation of the
power conversion stages. The bidirectional power flow tests demonstrated more than 95% dc-to-
dc efficiency at 20 kW power delivery in both power directions. The team also completed the tests
of the 3-phase grid-interface converter in both power factor correction boost rectifier and inverter
operating modes. The challenges experienced include the change in the vehicle battery nominal
voltage from 650V to 420V. This change in the battery voltage required the modification of the
resonant stage of the system to optimize the operation with 420V nominal secondary-side voltage.
The team modified the resonant stage components and verified the resonant voltage gains that
would optimize the power transfer between the ~750-800V primary dc bus voltage and the ~308-
429V. After confirming the successful operation in simulations, hardware modifications were done
in the resonant stage components and tests validated the achieving the proposed functionalities.

The original objectives for Budget Period 3 were to perform a full, vehicle-level demonstration of
the BWPT technology. This included a demonstration of the fully integrated vehicle and
corresponding grid-side BWPT Electric Vehicle Supply Equipment (EVSE) that would be deployed
into service by UPS at the demonstration site in Roswell, Georgia. The aim was to show real-
world operation of the BWPT system and vehicle in stationary and opportunity wireless power
transfer applications without bias in terms of alignment and air gap. A variety of factors affected
the completion of these activities, and the field demonstration was not completed.

III. Accomplishments vs. Objectives

There were three budget periods for this project that had specific objectives (tasks) and
requirements (milestones) associated with each period. The overall project objectives can be
summarized as follows:
1. Provide an automated, high power, interoperable, high-efficiency wireless charging for a plug-
 in hybrid electric medium duty delivery truck with a nominal ground clearance of approximately
 eleven (11) inches.
2. Optimize the add-on vehicle-side wireless charging components through integration and
 utilization of already existing vehicle-side components while implementing grid-side controls
 and regulations to reduce the vehicle-side cost, size, volume, and complexity.
3. Utilize bi-directional wireless charging systems when trucks are parked in the yard for staging
 to provide grid support applications or ancillary or grid support services such as frequency
 regulation, load leveling/peak shaving/load factor improvement/reactive power
 support/demand charge management and spinning/non-spinning reserves.
4. Provide an integrated > 20kW wireless charging system (grid to vehicle) with high efficiency
 (85%) while meeting the international guidelines on electromagnetic and electric field
 emissions during charging and include all other appropriate safety features.
5. In vehicle-to-grid mode, achieve 6.6kW wireless power transfer to building or grid loads.

The objectives for the Budget Periods are as follows:

Budget Period 1: Technology Development
In Budget Period 1 the focus was on core Wireless Power Transfer (WPT) development; during
which the conceptual design of stationary, bi-directional WPT for both the onboard vehicle system
and the grid-side system will be finalized. The bi-directional functionality will be incorporated into
existing grid-to-vehicle WPT designs. Information from relevant industry standards will be closely
monitored so that the final design adheres to the current status of these applicable standards and
maintains interoperability. The necessary gateway for vehicle–network communication and
appropriate wireless communication to the grid side will also be developed, including provisions
to maintain cybersecurity. Additionally, the base architecture and strategy for the bi-directional
WPT power management system will be developed in order to interface the WPT system to the
grid.

Tasks included:
 • Overall bi-directional wireless power transfer architecture selection
 • Grid-side power electronics and system development
 • Vehicle-side power electronics and system development
 • Bi-directional WPT power management system

All Tasks were successfully completed and the project proceeded to the next Budget Period after
completing the go/no-go milestone in 2018.

Budget Period 2: Technology Integration
In Budget Period 2, the focus was on Bi-directional Wireless Power Transfer (BWPT) technology
integration, which will include: 1) vehicle integration; 2) system hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing;
and 3) fleet level BWPT system benefits analysis. Each of these key areas are described below:

 3

1. Integrate the BWPT system into the demonstration vehicle complete with wireless
 communications to the grid and the BWPT energy management system.
2. Address the impacts of the BWPT system on the onboard energy storage system of the
 vehicle with emphasis on the increased discharge cycles inherit in a vehicle-to-grid (V2G)
 mode of operation through HIL simulation.
3. Model and analyze the benefits of the BWPT system for the intended application, extrapolated
 to the fleet level, in terms of energy consumption reduction, operational efficiency
 improvements, and implications for long term autonomous vehicle operation.

Budget Period 2 tasks included:
 • OEM Vehicle Integration Support and Guidance
 • Battery and integrated system testing
 • Modeling and Analysis of BWPT Benefits
 • Integration of Wireless Power-Transfer Components into Test Vehicle
 Complete System Validation and Demonstration
 • Demonstration Site Determination and Assessment

All Tasks were successfully completed, and the project proceeded to the next Budget Period after
completing the go/no-go milestone in 2018.

Budget Period 3: Technology Demonstration
Budget Period 3 was meant to include a full, vehicle-level demonstration of BWPT technology.
Tasks included development/assembly of one fully integrated vehicle and corresponding grid-side
BWPT Electric Vehicle Supply Equipment (EVSE) was to be deployed into service at the
demonstration site. This demonstration focuses on real-world operation of the BWPT system and
vehicle in stationary and opportunity wireless power transfer applications without bias in terms of
alignment and air gap. The BWPT power management controller would be exposed to “pseudo”
building loads through a load bank to demonstrate full bi-directional functionality of the system.
Data-collection would be handled by the BWPT power management system through the grid-side
unit.

Budget Period 3 tasks were originally intended to include:
 • Demonstration site preparation
 • Complete System Validation and Demonstration
 • Economic Analysis of Vehicle-to-grid Power Markets and Applications
 • Vehicle Deployment and Demonstration
 • Comparative Business Case Analysis

The Economic Analysis and the Business Case Analysis were completed. Due to numerous
factors, mainly tied to the coronavirus pandemic, the project was mostly suspended after the
completion of Budget Period 2. The proposed demonstration site was changed by UPS leading
to revised site preparations. Travel by all partners was suspended. By the time the partners were
ready to re-engage in the Budget Period 3 tasks, the demonstration partner, UPS, decided that
commercial factors precluded them from dedicating resources to the Technology Demonstration.
This included safety and liability concerns, firm-wide layoffs, and decisions not to pursue to use
of wireless charging in the near future. They were also concerned that the technology, which
designed and integrated from 2017 to 2020, had already been bypassed by other wireless

charging technologies and was not of any financial or commercial value to UPS. Upon the
agreement of DOE and NETL, the parties agreed to forego the on-site technology demonstration
and rely on the extensive laboratory test results that were compiled at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory.

V. Summary of Technical and Scientific Information
 a. Introduction:
 Electric vehicles (EVs) can provide power to the grid or buildings similar to distributed
 energy resources (DER) for energy balancing applications or optimizing the operation of
 the microgrids in harmony with the other DER assets. This paper presents the operating
 modes of a bidirectional wireless power transfer (WPT) system designed for a medium-
 duty package delivery vehicle. The WPT system designed for this study can transfer 20
 kW of power across 11 inches of airgap using custom-designed double-D (DD) couplers
 5

with LCC-LCC tuning networks. The proposed system utilizes a 480 V 3-phase grid
connection, a plug-in hybrid delivery truck with bidirectional WPT, and a stationary energy
storage system (SESS) that can be connected to the primary-side dc link. Due to the
differences in primary and secondary dc bus voltages; and considering the voltage of the
SESS, asymmetric voltage gains were used in the system. Sensitivity analyses of this
system with respect to these voltage levels are presented. Five different operating modes
of the grid, SESS, and the EV battery are investigated with experimental results. Control
algorithms are described for grid-to-vehicle (G2V) and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) operating
modes. A bidirectional WPT system is operated with a power factor of 0.99 on the grid
side in every operating mode. The EV battery was charged with 20.3 kW with an overall
efficiency of 93.02% in the G2V operating mode. In V2G operating mode, the WPT system
provided 12.82 kW of power back to the grid with an overall efficiency of 89.08%.

In this project, a 20-kW grid-connected bidirectional WPT study is designed, developed,
tested, and validated for a medium-duty plug-in hybrid EV United Parcel Service (UPS)
delivery truck. Five different operating modes of the bidirectional WPT system were
developed by using the three-phase grid, the SESS, and the EV in this study. These
modes were performed on the medium-duty EV delivery truck, and experiment results are
presented in this paper. The major contributions of this project include but not limited to;

 • Implementing a grid-connected bidirectional WPT system while most earlier
 bidirectional WPT studies have focused on dc-to-dc operation only.
 • Developing a bidirectional WPT system that is ready for microgrid integrations
 since the common high-voltage dc bus on primary-side enables integration with
 SESS, renewable energy sources, and other microgrid assets providing five unique
 operating modes depending on the status of the grid, on-site generation, and the
 battery state of charge (SoC) of the truck.
 • Wireless communication devices used on the ground and vehicle assemblies
 enable the connectivity of the proposed system, which can be expanded to a larger
 scale energy management and control system to send charge and discharge
 commands to the vehicle along with reference power levels defined by the energy
 management system or the user.

b. Bidirectional WPT System Design:
The bidirectional WPT system design includes the grid side LC filter, the small signal
model of the G2V and the V2G operating modes, the DD type coil design, and the LCC
tuning network design. The design sections are detailed in the following subsections.

The bidirectional WPT system design includes the grid side LC filter, the small signal model
of the G2V and the V2G operating modes, the DD type coil design, and the LCC tuning
network design. The design sections are detailed in the following subsections. The

the switching oscillations caused by the grid interface converter’s switching frequency.

frequency harmonics, the LC filter eliminates the higher-frequency harmonics, grid current

 iv. Double-D (DD) Type WPT Coil Design and Finite Element Analysis (FEA):
The WPT system's coils were designed primarily by considering the power level, input and
output voltage ranges, the airgap or power transfer distance between primary and secondary
coils, the coupling factor at this nominal gap, and the operating frequency along with the
resonant tuning networks on primary and secondary sides. This study aims to transfer a
nominal 20 kW of power during wireless power transfer. The dc inverter input voltage on the
primary side is 657-800V, and the battery voltage range on the secondary side is 320-420V. In
addition, in the SAE J2954/2 information report, the operating frequency ranges are defined to
be 22-25 kHz and 79-90 kHz for medium and heavy-duty vehicles. In this study, the air gap
between the coils is 11 inches. A high air gap results in a relatively low coupling factor, and
high currents and higher number of turns are required to transfer the targeted power. The
nominal operating frequency in this work is 22 kHz in order to reduce the switching losses. The
power equation of the WPT system is,

 v. LCC Tuning Network Design and the Inductor FEA Analysis:
The equivalent resistance on the primary side is approximately constant during the load
changes on the secondary side at the resonance working point in the LCC-LCC circuit
structure. The LCC tuning circuit topology has two circuit loops, and the LCC-LCC tuning circuit
is given in Fig. 9. These two circuit loops must operate in resonance (fP1=fP2=fsw) and
component parameters must be calculated accordingly. Typically, in an LCC-LCC tuned
system, primary side inductance is selected according to the current that needs to be provided
by the inverter for the target power as the larger tuning inductor would limit the inverter output
current and inverter would need a higher input voltage to drive the load for the same power.
Based on this inductor value, parallel tuning capacitor is calculated and then the series tuning
capacitor is calculated to tune out the difference between the coil inductance and the tuning
inductor. For the secondary side, larger tuning inductance values indicate larger voltage gains
since the inductor practically limits the current and increases the voltage at the output of the
LCC network. In this study, voltage gain must be around 2:1 since the primary dc link voltage
range is 680-800 V (due to 480 V rectification with boost PFC) and secondary side dc link
voltage range is 320-420V (due to vehicle battery voltage range). Using an iterative approach,
the tuning component values are determined based on these principles. Equations (18)-(20)
identify the resonant tuning component value calculations. Finally, the physical designs of the
tuning inductors are completed with Ansys/Maxwell to identify the magnetic core, airgap, and
the number of turns.

 c. Control System of the Bidirectional WPT System:
 In the grid-to-vehicle operation mode, since the grid interface converter is connected to
 480V 3-phase grid with boost rectification with power factor correction, primary side dc
 bus voltage should have a range of 675-800V. In addition, battery voltage range is 320-
 420V. For a target charge power reference for the vehicle battery, first the battery charge
 current is calculated. In this system, the battery charge current increases with the
 increased primary dc link voltage. Therefore, battery charge current control loop
 determines the reference primary-side dc bus voltage. Of course, for a grid interface active
 18

rectifier, the dc bus voltage would be related to the grid currents. Therefore, the primary
side dc link voltage control loop determines the d-axis grid current while the q-xis reference
grid current is 0 for non-reactive power operation. Using the Clarke and Park
transformations, the switching pattern for the grid interface converter is identified. When
providing power back to the grid, depending on the target power transfer back to the grid,
the primary dc bus voltage is controlled by an outer loop and grid current is controlled by
an inner loop. The small signal models described in Section II and MATLAB/Simulink
models were used to determine the coefficients of these control blocks. These control
systems are detailed in the following subsections.

The proposed bidirectional WPT system in this study consists of the three-phase grid, grid
interface PFC rectifier/inverter, SESS, primary-side inverter/rectifier, the WPT coils and
their resonant tuning networks, vehicle-side rectifier/inverter, and the vehicle battery. The
control system of the bidirectional WPT system is explained separately for the G2V (Fig.
11) and the V2G (Fig. 12) operating modes in the following sections.

 i. Control System for the G2V Operating Mode:
In G2V mode where the grid power is used to charge the vehicle battery, using the target
battery power (or could be current), and the actual measured battery current, a PI
controller is formed to determine the primary-side dc bus voltage. In this system, inverter
input dc bus voltage is proportional to the load-side battery power (or current). In order to
have that reference dc bus voltage after rectifier, the d-axis current from the grid is
determined by an inner loop while the q-axis grid current is set to 0 for power factor
correction from the grid side.

The dc bus voltage vdc_P is obtained by controlling the grid interface PFC rectifier in the
G2V operating mode as shown in Fig. 11. Power is transferred from the stationary high
frequency (HF) inverter to the WPT coils, and the vehicle battery is charged with the
vehicle-side rectifier. The vehicle battery is charged using the constant power method in
this study. The grid interface PFC is controlled by the space vector pulse width modulation
(SVPWM) method so that the power factor on the grid side is close to 1. The stationary
HF inverter phase branches are switched with unity duty cycle Dst = 1, and 180° phase
difference (G7, G8, G9, G10) while the phase-shift duty-cycle can be used for down
regulation of the power. The vehicle rectifier is operated with Dvh = 0 (G11, G12, G13, G14)
duty cycle in rectifier mode by using the body diodes of switching elements and keepin
the switches turned off. Bidirectional WPT control structure includes overvoltage and
overcurrent protection circuits for safety.

The primary-side dc bus voltage vdc_P is controlled by the inner loop and the battery
charging power Pbat is controlled by the outer loop in the G2V control structure.
Additionally, in an LCC-LCC tuned resonant system, the output power is also proportional
to the EV battery voltage vbat due to the constant current source behavior at the output.
The PIC1 controller determines the charging current ibat_c of the EV battery. The charging
current of the EV battery is limited to a maximum of 50 A with the saturation block.
The EV battery voltage changes between 320 V and 420 V depending on SoC. The battery
current can be reduced as the voltage increases to keep a constant power charging profile,
or the battery current can also be kept constant for a slightly increasing charge power
profile as the SoC increases. Both options are possible because the battery energy
capacity is 60 kWh, nominal charge power is 20 kW, and maximum battery voltage is

 19

 limited to 420 V or maximum SoC is 80% to avoid overcharging and prolong the battery
 lifetime.
 Since the input grid voltage is 480 V, around 675 V dc bus voltage would be generated on
 the dc bus if the rectifier was uncontrolled ((480 × √2) − 2vfwd , where vfwd is the forward
 voltage drop of the diodes). Since a minimal boost factor is required to control the current
 and achieve PFC, the lower end of the vdc_P is typically 690 V. Since 1200 V rated SiC
 MOSFETs were used, the higher end of the vdc_P is set to 800 V. By comparing the dc bus
 voltage with the reference value, reference current value id̅ is produced after the PIC2
 operation.

 ii. Control System for the V2G Operating Mode:
In V2G mode, the grid interface converter is operated in inverter mode. A reference
discharge power is set for the vehicle battery. Using the measured vehicle battery power,
a PI controller is formed that determines the primary-side dc bus voltage which is
compared with the actual dc bus voltage and an inner loop finally determines the reference
grid current that need to be injected to the grid.

The vehicle HF inverter is switched with unity (Dvh = 1) duty cycle and 180° phase
difference in the V2G operating mode (G11, G12, G13, G14) – see Fig. 12. In G2V operating
mode, the primary side WPT coil is now the secondary and the secondary side becomes
the primary. With the stationary rectifier Dst = 0 duty cycle, the dc bus voltage vdc_P is
obtained by using the body diodes of the switching elements (G7, G8, G9, G10). The grid
interface inverter injects power to the grid synchronously, with an almost unity power factor
(0.99). When the EV battery SoC drops below 30%, it stops the operation and battery
discharge current to protect the battery's health in the V2G operating mode.

The iabc and iCF currents transferred to the grid are controlled by the inner loop, and the dc
bus voltage vdc_P is controlled by the outer loop in the V2G control structure. The q, v̅ d and
v̅ q reference voltage values are produced for Clark and Park transformations as in the
G2V operation mode with the grid voltages vabc. In the inner loop, the grid currents iabc are
produced with abc to dq0 transformation and the reference currents iabc_d and iabc_q. The
currents of the capacitors in the LC filter on the grid side are referred to as iCF, and iCF_d
 ̅
and iCF_q reference currents are obtained. After PID2 and PID3 controllers, iabc_d reference
currents are produced. Thus, the reactive value of the currents is controlled, and the
currents with sinusoidal waveform is transferred to the grid. There are two multi-sum

Active damping resistors Rg, Rc and PID1, PID2, PID3 coefficients are given in Table III.

Over-voltage and over-current protections are included to ensure the safety of the system.

e. Experimental Results:
The system level diagram of the grid-connected bidirectional WPT system is given in Fig.
16. At the grid interface, 50 A rated fuses Mersen are used on each phase. Then, there is
a three-phase main contactor and there is a three-phase pre-charge contactor that
bypasses the pre-charge resistors. Both the main contactor and the pre-charge contactor
are AF52-30-11-11 manufactured by ABB. On each phase, there is a 100 Ω pre-charge
resistor to limit the surge current to the capacitors of the LC circuit and the dc bus bar
capacitors that are bypassed by the pre-charge contactor once the capacitors are
relatively slowly charged through the resistors for a few seconds. The three-phase grid
interface operates in PFC active rectifier mode in the G2V mode, and in inverter mode in
the V2G operating mode. The dc bus on primary-side is connected to the high-frequency
single-phase inverter that feeds into the LCC resonant tuning network of the primary-side
and the transmitter coil. On the dc bus of the primary-side, the SESS is connected which
can be charged or discharged by the grid interface converter as well as the vehicle battery.
Both for the grid interface rectifier inverter and the high-frequency inverter/rectifier on
primary-side, Wolfspeed/CREE CAS325M12HM2 phase-leg power modules with
CGD15HB62LP gate drivers were used on Wieland Microcool liquid-cooled cold plates in
3-module and 2-module arrangements. Koolance ALX-2000-P400 modular liquid cooling
system is used for the liquid recirculation on the cold plates.

On each phase, LF 205-S SP3 current sensors are used, while phase voltages are
measured using LV 25-P voltage sensors. The G2V and the V2G control algorithms and
sensor data are processed by TMS320F28337 digital signal processor (DSP). Single-
ended PWM gate signals from the DSP are converted to differential-ended PWM signals
using ORNL’s custom design differential line drivers and receivers. On each side of the
DSPs, a WIZnet serial to ethernet module used on the serial peripheral interface (SPI) of
the DSPs. Then the ethernet connections on each side are interfaced to BB-ABDN-ER-
IN5010 dual band industrial ethernet bridge router (wireless gateway) to establish wireless
communications between the primary and secondary sides. The vehicle battery
management system is configured to broadcast battery voltage, current, SoC,
temperature, and several other data that is read by the vehicle-side CAN and then
wirelessly transmitted to the primary-side. On the primary side, a separate 120/240 V
wiring is required to power the auxiliary systems. TDK-Lambda DRF240-24-1 is used to
generate 24 V that powers three other TDK-Lambda power supplies to generate +15 V, -
15 V, and 5 V to power all the auxiliaries including the gate drivers, sensors, DSP, radio,
contactors, and other systems.

In addition, a DPP120-12-1 12 V power supply is used to power the Koolance liquid cooling
system upon the initial handshaking and priming right before starting the power transfer.
A solid-state relay activates the chiller, routing the 12 V from this power supply to the liquid
cooling system. On the vehicle-side, 12 V to +15 V, -15 V, and 5V power supplies are
used to power all the auxiliaries. While the 12V battery on the truck is used to power all
the auxiliaries during the initial handshaking and priming, once the power transfer starts,
Vicor high-voltage to 24 V (V375C24C75BL) takes over and uses the power transferred
to the vehicle to power all the +15 V, -15 V, and 5 V powered auxiliaries. A separate high-
voltage to 12 V dc/dc converter (Vicor V375C12C150BL) dc power supply is used to power
the vehicle-side Koolance 12V chiller. These Vicor power supplies have a wide input
voltage range from 250 V to 450 V that covers the entire voltage range of the EV battery.
On both sides, Koolance chillers can be programmed to generate an alarm when the liquid
level or flow rate is below a certain level or when the liquid temperature is over a certain
 26

 value. This alarm is used in one of the DSP GPIO inputs to terminate the wireless charging
 if one of these conditions occurs.

Mode 4 – Providing power to the grid from the EV (V2G)
The EV battery is discharged in Mode 4 for grid support services. In this mode, the bulk
attery was bypassed as in Mode 3. The grid interface converter is operated in inverter mode
and is synchronized with the grid voltage. Fig. 24 (c) shows the waveform of currents
transferred to the grid, dc bus voltage/current oscilloscope results. In Mode 4, 12,821 W
was transferred to the grid, and the dc bus voltage was 737 V. The EV battery discharged
14,391 W at 381 V. Negative power values indicate the power flowed into the grid. The
total efficiency of the system was 89.08% when transferring energy from the vehicle to the
grid in Mode 4, and the power analyzer results are given in detail in Fig. 24 (e). As earlier
shown in Fig. 15 (b), maximum power transfer to the grid depends on the primary and
secondary-side dc link voltages. Since charging has priority over discharging, the system
is designed to be able to transfer the full power to the battery even with the lower end of
the batter voltage or SOC. When providing power back to the grid, for instance, if the battery
voltage is relatively low (state-of-charge <20%, then the power transfer is limited to 12.11
kW with the primary dc bus voltage of 800 V or 9.61 kW with a primary dc bus voltage of
675V). This is very practical because if the battery SOC is limited, it would be not feasible
to provide full power back from the vehicle battery to the grid; instead, a number of vehicles
can be aggregated for higher power levels, or the stationary energy storage system could
be utilized to avoid deep discharge of on-board batteries. With a higher battery voltage,
indicating higher SOC on the battery, for instance >380V, up to 17.18 to 21 kW power
transfer can be accomplished. The experimental test results for V2G mode should be
regarded as an example and various power transfer levels can be achieved in this scheme.
It should also be noted that characteristics provided in Fig. 15 are for the nominal frequency
and unity duty cycle. With non-unity (<1) phase-shift duty cycle and with frequency
regulation, lower power levels can easily be achieved. In the case of vehicle-to-grid
operation, the WPT system has the capacity to transfer up to 20 kW of power from the
vehicle to the grid. Experimental results for Mode 4 are given in Table V. In Mode 4, when
the vehicle battery is 381.94 V, it is discharged at a power level of 14.391 kW, as can be
seen in Fig. 15 (d). Considering the efficiency of the power converter circuits in the system,
approximately 13 kW of power was transferred to the grid. While the system can transfer
up to 20 kW power to the grid, the 13 kW power level is due to the primary and secondary
side dc-link voltages as show in the characteristics depicted in Fig. 15 (d), which is an
example of the V2G operation for these given conditions.

VI. Conclusions
 This study presents the sensitivity analysis and experimental results of a medium-duty
 electric vehicle integrated bidirectional WPT system with SESS. Within the scope of the
 project, the DD type WPT coil, three-phase grid interface converter, and single-phase
 inverter/rectifier systems were designed and prototyped. The power relationship has been
 analyzed depending on the input and output voltage changes of the LCC-LCC tuned WPT
 resonant stage with asymmetric voltage gain characteristics. Five possible operating
 modes of the grid-connected bidirectional WPT system have been identified for a 20-kW
 nominal power rating with 11 inches of airgap separation. The experimental studies of the
 G2V and V2G operating modes were carried out on a medium-duty electric delivery truck.
 This paper demonstrated that the bidirectional grid-connected WPT system can also be
 integrated with SESS at the primary-side DC link. In all operating modes, the system
 maintained a 0.99 power factor and less than 1.5% grid current total harmonic distortions.
 The bidirectional power flow was achieved from primary-side dc bus to vehicle batter,
 vehicle battery to primary-side dc bus, grid to vehicle battery, vehicle battery to grid, and
 also using the SESS on the primary side for the highest level of flexibility, convenience,
 and high efficiency. In the future, a centralized energy management system can determine
 the operating point depending on the SoC of the vehicle battery and the SESS, as well as
 the grid power rate structures. Some vehicles equipped with this technology can also be
 aggregated to provide grid ancillary or grid support services as well as to provide behind-
 the-meter (customer side of the meter) benefits to the fleet operator, including demand
 charge management, time of use energy cost management, load leveling, or optimizing
 the entire microgrid operations at the fleet operator’s facilities.
