On Reference-Regulated Multiperiod Mean-Variance Portfolio Optimization in High Dimensions
Yutao DENG, Jianjun GAO, Weichen WANG ∗

Abstract
The multiperiod mean-variance (MV) portfolio optimization serves as a vital expansion of Markowitz’s static MV portfolio selection framework. Just like its static counterpart, the multiperiod MV portfolio remains susceptible to estimation errors. We propose a reference-regulated multiperiod mean-variance (RRMV) framework that penalizes deviations from a reference policy. Therefore, this new optimization successfully combines the advantages of dynamic strategies and reference portfolios. A key contribution of this paper is the characterization of the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio under high-dimensional asymptotics with estimation errors in both the mean vector and the covariance matrix. We show how the reference penalty and the investment horizon jointly affect the optimized portfolio performance, and how regularization operates differently from the single-period portfolio optimization. Extensive simulation and real data studies demonstrate that the proposed framework improves the stability and out-of-sample Sharpe ratios of multiperiod policies significantly.



Keywords:
Multiperiod mean-variance portfolio; Pre-committed feedback policy; Reference regulation; Out-of-sample Sharpe ratio; High-dimensional asymptotics.

1
Introduction

The single-period mean-variance (MV) portfolio optimization initiated by
Markowitz 1952
laid the foundation of modern portfolio theory. Owing to its parsimonious structure—requiring only the mean vector and covariance matrix of asset returns—the MV model has been widely adopted in both academic research and investment practice
(
Brandt 2010
,
Kolm.P.N. et al. 2014
)
. A natural and important extension of the classical single-period formulation is its multiperiod (dynamic) generalization, where portfolio decisions are updated over time to incorporate evolving information and thereby potentially enhance long-run performance.

Multiperiod MV problems, however, can be formulated in fundamentally different ways. Some formulations such as
Gârleanu & Pedersen 2013
,
DeMiguel et al. 2015
which optimize a sequence of single-period MV tradeoffs, effectively maximizing a discounted sum of period-by-period mean–variance objectives. In contrast,
Li & Ng 2000
directly extend Markowitz’s original framework by focusing on the mean and variance of cumulative wealth (return). This terminal-return formulation preserves the global risk–return tradeoff of the classical MV criterion and yields several theoretical advantages. In particular, the resulting dynamic policy structure allows the multiperiod MV portfolio to attain an improved MV efficient frontier, thereby generating an additional risk premium relative to static strategies
(
Chiu & Zhou 2011
,
Vigna 2014
,
Yao et al. 2016
)
. Moreover, unlike the static MV portfolio—whose terminal wealth is a linear transformation of risky asset returns—the dynamic strategy of
Li & Ng 2000
fundamentally alters the distributional properties of terminal wealth.
Under normally distributed returns, terminal wealth follows a quasi-lognormal distribution, reflecting nonlinear compounding through rebalancing.
More recently,
van Staden et al. 2021b
demonstrate that this class of dynamic portfolio policies exhibits notable robustness to model misspecification in return dynamics, further highlighting its structural advantages.

Despite these appealing theoretical properties, it is well known that the classical static MV portfolio is highly sensitive to estimation error and may perform poorly out of sample (see, e.g.,
DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal 2009
,
Brandt 2010
). This sensitivity becomes increasingly severe in high-dimensional settings. In contemporary investment environments, portfolios often contain hundreds of assets, whereas the available historical data remain limited. As a result, estimation error in expected returns and covariance matrices can substantially distort portfolio weights and undermine performance
(
Ao et al. 2019
,
Meng et al. 2025
)
.
Motivated by this inherent fragility of the MV framework, our objective is to retain the structural advantages of the dynamic multiperiod formulation while mitigating its vulnerability to estimation error.
Building on the hybrid portfolio literature
(
Kan & Zhou 2007
,
Kan et al. 2022
,
Kan & Wang 2024
,
Lassance et al. 2024
)
, we develop a reference–regulated multiperiod MV model that penalizes deviations of the dynamic portfolio from a benchmark policy over time. This penalty serves as an economically interpretable form of dynamic regularization, stabilizing portfolio decisions and reducing sensitivity to estimation noise.

Specifically, we consider the following optimization objective:

\displaystyle\omega\cdot\textrm{Var}(X_{T})-\mathbb{E}[X_{T}]+\omega\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{u}_{t}-X_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{Q}_{t}}^{2}\big],

where
X_{t}
and
\mathbf{u}_{t}
denote the investor’s wealth and portfolio holdings at time
t
, respectively, evolving according to a self-financing process;
\mathbf{w}_{t}
is a pre-specified reference portfolio weight; and
\mathbf{Q}_{t}
is a positive-definite matrix governing the strength and direction of regulation. The quadratic penalty controls deviations of the dynamic allocation from the benchmark in a state-dependent manner. The framework is flexible: any benchmark portfolio with desirable stability properties—such as shrinkage-based or diversified static allocations—can serve as the reference. The formulation is closely related to norm-based constraints and regularization techniques that stabilize portfolio weights under estimation error
(
DeMiguel, Garlappi, Nogales & Uppal 2009
)
. It is also connected to shrinkage approaches, as the matrices
\mathbf{Q}_{t}
and reference portfolios
\mathbf{w}_{t}
effectively pull the dynamic allocation toward structured targets, analogous to shrinking estimated means and covariances toward more stable benchmarks.
In this sense, our framework generalizes portfolio combination methods (e.g.,
Kan & Zhou 2007
,
Kan et al. 2022
) to a dynamic, path-dependent setting.

Our contributions are multifold.
First, we provide a closed-form characterization of the reference-regulated multiperiod MV policy.
By adapting the embedding technique of
Li & Ng 2000
, we resolve the nonseparability of the variance term in dynamic programming and establish that the optimal policy admits a recursive representation governed by a finite set of market-dependent parameters.

Second, we derive high-dimensional asymptotic characterizations of the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio (SR) for these MV portfolios under estimation risk.
Different from the analysis in
Kan & Zhou 2007
,
Kan et al. 2022
,
Lassance et al. 2024
, we study the model in a high-dimensional asymptotic regime where the number of assets
p
grows proportionally with the sample size
n
, so that
p/n\to c\in(0,\infty)
, as in
Ao et al. 2019
,
Meng et al. 2025
.
This setting captures large portfolios with limited data and differs fundamentally from classical asymptotics where
p
is fixed and
n\to\infty
. Using tools from random matrix theory, we characterize the limiting behavior of the Sharpe ratio under this high-dimensional regime. Our analysis separates the effects of estimation error in the mean vector and covariance matrix, thereby quantifying how different sources of statistical uncertainty affect portfolio performance. We further show how reference-based regularization modifies the limiting Sharpe ratio by attenuating the impact of estimation noise, providing a theoretical explanation for the stabilizing role of regularization.

Third, we extend high-dimensional Sharpe ratio analysis from static portfolios to multiperiod policies.
In the static setting, the regulation matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{t}
primarily shrinkage the estimation error in the covariance matrix
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
, while the reference component
\mathbf{w}_{t}
is used to mitigate estimation error in the mean vector
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
.
However, estimation errors in the mean and covariance no longer operate independently in dynamic environments.
Instead, they interact through feedback trading, jointly influencing the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio. We show that reference-based regulation affects not only its primary error channel but also the secondary one through this intertemporal interaction, highlighting a distinct and previously unexplored role for regularization in dynamic portfolio policies. Using this approach, given mild condition, the Sharpe ratio can be further enhanced when the investment period
T>1
.

Finally, we evaluate the empirical performance of the proposed framework through simulations and real-data studies. Consistent with the theoretical mechanisms identified above, the reference-regulated approach delivers more stable Sharpe ratios and improved out-of-sample performance relative to unregularized multiperiod strategies and leading static regularization methods, particularly in high-dimensional settings. Overall, our findings provide a unified theoretical and empirical perspective on how regularization can be incorporated into multiperiod mean–variance optimization to mitigate estimation risk while preserving the structural benefits of dynamic portfolio selection.

1.1
Related Literature

Our work is closely related to a broad literature that seeks to improve the out-of-sample performance of mean–variance portfolios.
A fundamental difficulty of the classical MV framework is its sensitivity to estimation error, which often leads to unstable portfolio weights and poor out-of-sample results
(
Chopra & Ziemba 1993
,
DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal 2009
)
.
These concerns have motivated a wide range of methods designed to reduce the impact of estimation error on portfolio selection.

One major approach to improving out-of-sample portfolio performance focuses on enhancing the estimation of return moments.
For covariance estimation, shrinkage methods provide well-conditioned alternatives to the sample covariance matrix.
The linear shrinkage estimator of
Ledoit & Wolf 2004
and its nonlinear extensions
(
Ledoit & Wolf 2012
)
yield better-conditioned and more accurate covariance estimates than the traditional sample covariance matrix.
Beyond shrinkage, additional structure can be imposed through factor and sparsity-based models
(
Bickel & Levina 2008
,
Lam & Fan 2009
,
Cai & Liu 2011
,
Fan et al. 2013
,
Fan et al. 2016
,
Cai et al. 2020
)
.
For expected returns, Bayesian and equilibrium-based approaches incorporate additional structure beyond the sample mean, including the Black–Litterman model
(
Black & Litterman 1991
)
and related variants
(
Lai et al. 2011
,
Bertsimas et al. 2012
)
.

A second approach stabilizes portfolio performance by regularizing the portfolio decision directly.
Jagannathan & Ma 2003
show that no-short-sale constraints effectively shrink the estimated covariance matrix through the associated Lagrange multipliers, leading to more stable portfolios in practice.
In a comprehensive empirical study,
DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal 2009
demonstrate that many optimized portfolios fail to consistently outperform the naive “
1/N
” benchmark, highlighting the severity of estimation error in portfolio choice.
Focusing on minimum-variance portfolios,
DeMiguel, Garlappi, Nogales & Uppal 2009
propose a generalized framework based on norm constraints that links constrained optimization to covariance shrinkage.

Fan et al. 2012
provide theoretical support for gross-exposure constraints that limit estimation error in high-dimensional portfolios.
Robust optimization approaches similarly account for parameter uncertainty by protecting against worst-case model misspecification
(
Goldfarb & Iyengar 2003
,
Blanchet et al. 2022
)
.

Another related strand of the literature studies how estimation error affects portfolio decisions and out-of-sample performance metrics, such as MV utility and the Sharpe ratio, and how these effects can be corrected.
Kan & Zhou 2007
characterize the impact of estimation error on out-of-sample MV utility and propose hybrid portfolio rules that combine different estimators;
Kan et al. 2022
extend this framework to portfolios of risky assets only. Subsequent works develop portfolio combination methods with improved out-of-sample stability
(
Tu & Zhou 2011
,
Kan & Wang 2024
,
Lassance et al. 2024
)
. Because the sample size and portfolio dimension jointly affect out-of-sample performance,
Lassance et al. 2025
derive results on the optimal portfolio size. The effect of estimation error on the out-of-sample performance becomes even more pronounced in high-dimensional settings, where the number of assets is large relative to the available sample size
(
DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal 2009
,
Karoui 2010
)
.
Ao et al. 2019
propose a sparse regression representation of MV portfolios that improves out-of-sample performance for large portfolios.
More recently,
Meng et al. 2025
develop a random matrix theory-based method to consistently estimate and correct the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio in high-dimensional portfolio optimization, enabling reliable parameter tuning under general covariance structures.

While existing approaches focus primarily on single-period settings, an important open question is how estimation risk should be managed in multiperiod portfolio problems, where decisions interact over time.
A foundational theoretical formulation of multiperiod mean–variance (MMV) framework is developed by
Li & Ng 2000
.
Their embedding approach overcomes the non-separability induced by the variance term and thereby resolves the main difficulty in applying dynamic programming.
This framework is commonly referred to as a
pre-committed
policy.
Building on this methodology, a variety of extensions have been investigated, including continuous-time counterparts
(
Zhou & Li 2000
)
, models with no-shorting constraints
(
Cui et al. 2014
)
, and no-bankruptcy constraints
(
Bielecki et al. 2005
)
.
A parallel strand studies time-consistent (equilibrium) mean–variance policies that address the dynamic inconsistency of pre-committed solutions; see, for example,
Basak & Chabakauri 2010
,
Björk et al. 2014
.
However,
Vigna 2020
and
van Staden et al. 2021a
show that time-consistent policies typically achieve lower theoretical Sharpe ratios than pre-committed solutions, since time consistency effectively imposes additional constraints on the original MMV problem.
Moreover,
van Staden et al. 2021b
demonstrate that pre-committed policies may exhibit greater robustness under model misspecification.

Prior studies have provided a rich understanding of dynamic optimal portfolio behavior, but they have largely examined MMV policies under known model parameters and have offered limited guidance on how to control estimation risk when return moments must be estimated from data.
This omission is particularly important because estimation errors in MMV settings affect not only initial allocations but also subsequent feedback decisions, potentially amplifying performance deterioration over time. We aim to explicitly quantify the effect of estimation error to the asymptotic out-of-sample Sharpe ratio in the MMV setting.

2.1
Multiperiod MV portfolio optimization

An investor dynamically allocates the wealth across available assets in each period under the self-financing policy. Let
X_{t}
denote the realized wealth at time
t
and the initial wealth is
X_{0}
. The portfolio allocation at the beginning of period
t
is represented by
\mathbf{u}_{t}=\big(u_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,u_{t}^{(p)}\big)^{\top}
, where
u_{t}^{(i)}
is the amount invested in the
i
-th risky asset. The remaining wealth,
X_{t}-\mathbf{e}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{t}
, is allocated to the risk-free asset. The wealth then evolves as

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=r\cdot\big(X_{t}-\mathbf{e}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{t}\big)+\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{t}=rX_{t}+\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{t}\,,

(1)

for
t=0,\ldots,T-1
, where
\mathbf{P}_{t}=\big(P_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,P_{t}^{(p)}\big)^{\top}\triangleq\mathbf{R}_{t}-r\mathbf{e}
denotes the vector of excess returns over the risk-free rate.
For notational simplicity, we assume that the distribution of
\mathbf{R}_{t}
is time-invariant. In particular, the expected value and covariance matrix of
\mathbf{P}_{t}
are defined as

\displaystyle{\bm{\mu}}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{P}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{R}_{t}]-r\mathbf{e}\,,

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}\triangleq\textrm{Cov}[\mathbf{P}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\textrm{Cov}[\mathbf{R}_{t}]\,,

for
t=0,\ldots,T-1
, respectively. In the subsequent analysis, we will refer to
{\bm{\mu}}
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}
as the true expected return and covariance matrix, to distinguish them from empirical estimates obtained from historical data.

In this work, we investigate the multiperiod mean–variance (MMV) portfolio optimization problem, formulated as
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,T)
(see
Li & Ng 2000
) :

\displaystyle\min_{\{\mathbf{u}_{t}\}|_{t=0}^{T-1}}~\omega\textrm{Var}[X_{T}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{T}]

\displaystyle\text{subject to}~\{X_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}\}~\text{satisfy}~(\ref{def_wealth_dyn_rf})\,,

where
\omega>0
is the risk aversion parameter controlling the return-risk tradeoff. Problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,T)
optimizes the balance between the mean and variance of terminal wealth and can be regarded as a natural extension of the single-period MV model of
Markowitz 1952
. Under this notation, the single-period case corresponds to
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,1)
. Ideally, if all model parameters are correctly estimated,
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,T)
yields the globally optimal dynamic policy over
T
periods that maximizes
\mathbb{E}[X_{T}]-\omega\textrm{Var}[X_{T}]
. Such a policy may outperform both the repeated application of a myopic one-period MV strategy and the static MV policy that treats the entire
T
-period horizon as a single decision period. Indeed, as shown by
Chiu & Zhou 2011
and
Vigna 2014
, the MMV framework can generate an additional intertemporal diversification premium.
However, this conclusion should be interpreted with caution. It is well known that the out-of-sample performance of static MV portfolios (i.e., the policy associated with
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,1)
) is highly sensitive to estimation errors
(
DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal 2009
)
. This sensitivity persists in the multiperiod setting as well, as demonstrated by the examples presented in Section
3
.

To mitigate this limitation and inspired by the notion of stable portfolio strategies, we propose a hybrid framework that integrates a stable reference policy into the dynamic MV optimization model. We refer to this formulation as the reference-regulated mean–variance (RRMV) portfolio optimization model
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}~\omega\textrm{Var}(X_{T})-\mathbb{E}[X_{T}]+\omega\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{u}_{t}-X_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{Q}_{t}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\textrm{subject to}~\{X_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}\}~\textrm{satisfies (\ref{def_wealth_dyn_rf})}\,,

where the input sequence
\{\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}|_{t=0}^{T-1}
denotes the reference policy, and
\{\mathbf{Q}_{t}\succ 0\}|_{t=0}^{T-1}
are the weighting matrices. Note that the sum of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
is not necessarily
1
as
\mathbf{w}_{t}
only contains risky assets.
The additional regulation term
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{u}_{t}-X_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{Q}_{t}}^{2}\big]
penalizes deviations of the decision policy
\mathbf{u}_{t}
from the scaled reference portfolio
X_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}
, while the matrices
\mathbf{Q}_{t}
determine the strength and direction of this penalization. One practical choice of
\mathbf{Q}_{t}
may be diagonal
\mathbf{Q}_{t}=\textrm{diag}(q_{1,t},\ldots,q_{p,t})
with positive diagonal elements.

The problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
has the following dual formulation
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(X_{\textrm{tg}},T)
:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\textrm{Var}[X_{T}]+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|\mathbf{u}_{t}-X_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}\big\|_{\mathbf{Q}_{t}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\mathbb{E}[X_{T}]\geq X_{\textrm{tg}}~~\text{and}~\{X_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}\}~\text{satisfies}~(\ref{def_wealth_dyn_rf})\,,

where
X_{\textrm{tg}}\geq X_{0}\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}r_{t}
is the target expected terminal wealth. Since the optimal solutions to the problems
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(X_{\textrm{tg}},T)
are internally related, our primary focus will be on
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega)
.

In
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
, the choice of the reference policy is quite flexible. For instance, if we set
\mathbf{w}_{k}=\bm{0}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{k}=\rho\cdot\mathbf{I}
with
\rho>0
for all
k=0,\ldots,T-1
, the resulting RRMV objective reduces to

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\textrm{Var}[X_{T}]+\rho\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|^{2}].

This formulation can be viewed as an extension of the Tikhonov- or Ridge-regularized MV portfolio studied in
DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal 2009
,
DeMiguel, Garlappi, Nogales & Uppal 2009
. The regularization term
\rho\cdot\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|^{2}]
is a standard device for alleviating the well-known estimation-error problem associated with the covariance matrix, and is closely related to shrinkage-based covariance estimators in
Ledoit & Wolf 2003
.

Beyond the zero reference portfolio, various alternative choices are possible, including the minimum-variance portfolio, the risk-parity portfolio, the naive “
1/N
” allocation (in our notation
1/p
allocation actually), and portfolios obtained from downside-risk formulations
(
Rockafellar & Uryasev 2002
)
. One may also use practically relevant benchmark portfolios as reference policies, for example, target holdings specified by a fund manager. Moreover, the regularization term allows the resulting dynamic policy to inherit certain structural patterns. As an illustration, consider
\mathbf{w}_{t}=\mathbf{w}
for all
t
, where
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{p})^{\top}\geq\bm{0}
is a sparse portfolio with
w_{i}=0
for any
i\in I\subset\{1,2,\ldots,p\}
, where
I
represents a subset of assets to be excluded. To enforce this sparsity pattern dynamically, one may set the weighting matrices
\mathbf{Q}_{t}=\mathrm{diag}(q_{1,t},\ldots,q_{p,t})
and assign large positive
q_{ti}
for all
i\in I
.

2.2
The strategic advantage of feedback policy

Before solving the problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
, we first review and compare different frameworks for solving the MMV problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,T)
. When
T>1
, MMV problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,T)
is notoriously difficult because the variance term is not separable in the dynamic programming sense. In particular, the variance does not satisfy the smoothing property:
\textrm{Var}[X_{T}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\neq\textrm{Var}[\textrm{Var}[X_{T}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
for
s>t
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the information set (filtration) at time
t
.

To address this difficulty, two types of methods have been developed. The first approach, proposed by
Li & Ng 2000
, derives a feedback (FB) policy by introducing an auxiliary parameter that embeds the original problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
into a parameterized optimal control problem solvable via dynamic programming (DP).
By properly selecting this parameter, the optimal policy of
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
can be recovered as
3
3
endnote:

3

The detail discussion of time-consistent policy are given in Appendix
S4
.
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\text{fb}}_{t}(X_{t})=r\cdot\alpha\cdot\big(\bar{X}_{t}-X_{t}\big){\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}},\quad t=0,\ldots,T-1,

(2)

where
\alpha\triangleq 1/(1+{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})
and
\bar{X}_{t}\triangleq r^{t}X_{0}+\big(2\omega r^{T-t}\alpha^{T}\big)^{-1}
. The policy
\mathbf{u}^{\text{fb}}_{t}(X_{t})
is called a feedback (FB) policy because it depends adaptively on the realized wealth
X_{t}
at time
t
. Since the allocation weight
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}
is a constant vector,
\bar{X}_{t}
can be regarded as a risk-adjusted target. The total investment in risky assets is determined by
(\bar{X}_{t}-X_{t})
, that is, by the gap between current wealth and this target. Another noteworthy feature of the feedback policy
\mathbf{u}^{\text{fb}}t(X_{t})
is its dependence on the initial wealth
X_{0}
. In fact, if we re-solve the truncated problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,T)\big|_{t=k}^{T}
at any time
t=k
given the realized wealth
X_{k}
, the resulting portfolio policy will not coincide with the original one solved at
t=0
. This inconsistency gives rise to time-inconsistent behavior, and hence the policy
\mathbf{u}^{\text{fb}}_{t}(X_{t})
is often referred to in the literature as a pre-committed policy (see
Basak & Chabakauri 2010
).

The second approach to the problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,T)
enforces time consistency by solving the problem backward in time and fixing the optimal policies in later periods (see
Basak & Chabakauri 2010
,
Björk et al. 2014
). Such a policy can also be interpreted as the subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium of a sequential intrapersonal game
(
Basak & Chabakauri 2010
)
. Using our notation, the time-consistent (TC) policy of
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,T)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{t}^{\text{tc}}=\frac{1}{2\omega r^{T-t-1}}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}},\quad t=0,\ldots,T-1.

(3)

Recall that the single-period (static) MV portfolio policy is
\mathbf{u}_{t}^{\text{static}}=\frac{1}{2\omega}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}
.
4
4
endnote:

4

Under our notation, the single-period MV portfolio is obtained from
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MV}}(\omega,T=1)
.
Clearly, the TC portfolio
\mathbf{u}_{t}^{\text{tc}}
shares the same structural form as the static portfolio
\mathbf{u}_{t}^{\text{static}}
, except that its risk-aversion parameter
\omega
is effectively discounted over time.

In this work, we aim to derive the pre-committed feedback policy for the problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
, primarily for the following reasons. First, if the investor adheres to the policy computed at time
0
, the feedback policy (
2
) serves as a globally optimal portfolio policy that achieves the highest Sharpe ratio. Such optimality has been established in several studies (see, e.g.,
Vigna 2020
,
van Staden et al. 2021a
). Second, the feedback policy (
2
) induces a nonlinear evolution of the wealth process. Specifically, substituting policies (
2
) and (
3
) into the wealth dynamics (
1
) yields

FB:

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=rX_{t}\cdot\big(1-\alpha\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}\big)+r\bar{X}_{t}\alpha\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}\,,

TC:

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=rX_{t}+\frac{1}{2\omega r^{T-t-1}}\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}\,.

The feedback policy causes the risky return
\mathbf{P}_{t}
to enter the cumulative wealth
X_{T}
in a nonlinear manner due to the feedback mechanism, in sharp contrast to the time-consistent (TC) policy, where
\mathbf{P}_{t}
contributes linearly to
X_{T}
. This feedback effect provides a richer structure for shaping the wealth distribution. For example,
van Staden et al. 2021a
show that the terminal wealth distribution of
X_{T}
under the feedback policy is non-Gaussian, even when
\mathbf{P}_{t}
follows a Gaussian distribution. Moreover,
van Staden et al. 2021b
demonstrate that the feedback policy can exhibit more robust performance compared with the time-consistent policy when the portfolio model is misspecified. Motivated by these observations, we focus on developing the feedback policy for the problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
and analyzing its statistical properties.

2.3
Optimal policy of RRMV portfolio optimization

In this section, we present the optimal policy for the problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
. Since this problem is not separable in the sense of dynamic programming, we adopt the embedding method developed in
Li & Ng 2000
to derive its feedback policy. Specifically, we characterize the solution of the parameterized problem
\mathcal{A}(\omega,\lambda)
, which is solvable by dynamic programming, and then recover the solution of
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
by identifying a suitable parameter. The key contribution lies in proving the equivalence between the original problem and the parameterized one under the reference regulation term, and in further deriving an explicit analytical feedback policy, extending the classical embedding approach to the regulated MV setting.

We first solve the following auxiliary problem
\mathcal{A}(\omega,\lambda)
by introducing parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}~\mathbb{E}[\omega X_{T}^{2}-\lambda X_{T}]+\omega\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}E[\|\mathbf{u}_{t}-X_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{Q}_{t}}^{2}]

\displaystyle\textrm{subject to}~\{X_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}\}~\textrm{satisfies \eqref{def_wealth_dyn_rf}}\,.

Clearly,
\mathcal{A}(\omega,\lambda)
is a linear-quadratic type of optimization problem, which is solvable by DP. To solve
\mathcal{A}(\omega,\lambda)
, we introduce the following variables, which are defined recursively for
k=T-1,\dots,1,0
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{k}

\displaystyle=a_{k+1}\cdot\big({\bm{\Sigma}}+{\bm{\mu}}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\big)+\mathbf{Q}_{k}\,,

(4)

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle=a_{k+1}r^{2}+\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}-\|ra_{k+1}{\bm{\mu}}-\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}\|_{\mathbf{D}_{k}^{-1}}\,,

\displaystyle b_{k}

\displaystyle=rb_{k+1}-b_{k+1}\cdot\big(ra_{k+1}{\bm{\mu}}-\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}\big)^{\top}\mathbf{D}_{k}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}\,,

\displaystyle c_{k}

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}-b_{k+1}^{2}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}_{k}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}\,,

where
a_{T}=b_{T}=1
,
c_{T}=0
and
{\bm{\mu}}_{k}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{P}_{k}]
. Note that these notations work for time-varying
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{P}_{k}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\top}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{P}_{k}]
, so does the solution for
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
below, although for simplicity we only consider constant
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{P}_{k}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\top}]={\bm{\Sigma}}+{\bm{\mu}}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}
and
{\bm{\mu}}_{k}={\bm{\mu}}
for theoretical results.
For any choice of positive semi-definite
\mathbf{Q}_{k}

\mathbf{D}_{k}
is always positive definite.

Lemma 1
.

The variables
\{a_{k}\}|_{k=0}^{T}
in (

for all

Next, we present the optimal portfolio policy for the auxiliary problem
\mathcal{A}(\omega,\lambda)

Theorem 1
.

The optimal solution of problem
\mathcal{A}(\omega,\lambda)
is, for
t=0,\ldots,T-1

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\mathcal{A}}(X_{k},\lambda)=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\lambda b_{k+1}}{2\omega}-ra_{k+1}X_{k}\Big)\cdot\mathbf{D}_{k}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}

(5)

\displaystyle+X_{k}\mathbf{D}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}\,,

where
\{a_{k}\}|_{k=0}^{T-1}
and
\{b_{k}\}|_{k=0}^{T-1}
are defined in (
4
) and
\mathbb{E}[X_{T}]=b_{0}X_{0}-\frac{\lambda}{2\omega}c_{0}
.

The optimal policy of the original RRMV problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
and the auxiliary problem
\mathcal{A}(\omega,\lambda)
are connected by the parameter
\lambda
, which enables us to retrieve the policy of the problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(X_{\textrm{tg}},T)

Theorem 2
.

When
\lambda^{*}=(2\omega b_{0}X_{0}+1)/(1+c_{0})
where
b_{0},c_{0}
is from the recursion in (
4

\mathbf{u}^{\mathcal{A}}(X_{k},\lambda^{*})
defined in (

\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)

k=0,\ldots,T-1

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{*}_{k}(X_{k})=

\displaystyle\Bigg(\frac{(1+2\omega b_{0}X_{0})b_{k+1}}{2\omega(1+c_{0})}-ra_{k+1}X_{k}\Bigg)\mathbf{D}_{k}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}

(6)

\displaystyle+X_{k}\mathbf{D}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}\,.

Furthermore, when the weighting parameter
\omega
is chosen as

\displaystyle\omega^{*}=\frac{c_{0}}{2\big(b_{0}X_{0}-X_{\textrm{tg}}\cdot(1+c_{0})\big)}\,,

(7)

the policy in (
6
) also solves
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(X_{\textrm{tg}},T)
.

For the special case when
T=1
in the problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
, the optimal policy is as follows.

Proposition 1
.

For the problem
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,1)
, the RRMV optimal portfolio policy is

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{*}_{0}=\big({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q}_{0})^{-1}(\frac{{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega}+X_{0}\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{w}_{0}).

(8)

Note that this solution adds regularization
\mathbf{Q}_{0}
to the covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}
used in the optimization. Motivated by
DeMiguel, Garlappi, Nogales & Uppal 2009

\mathbf{u}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{u}_{0}

\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{w}_{0}
serves as a regularization to the mean
{\bm{\mu}}
.

In the following proposition, we derive the ideal or maximum Sharpe ratio of the MMV portfolio if there is no estimation error.
We compare this with holding a static MV portfolio for
T
periods,
and the corresponding terminal wealth will be
X_{T}=X_{0}\cdot\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}(r+\frac{1}{2\omega}\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})

For any

Proposition 2
.

Suppose
{\bm{\mu}}
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}
are known.
Let
\textrm{SR}_{\max}
and
\textrm{SR}_{\mathrm{hold}}
denote the one-period Sharpe ratio achieved by the MMV policy and holding the static MV portfolio over
T

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\max}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{(1+{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})^{T}-1}\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\mathrm{hold}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\frac{1-(1-M)^{T}}{\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{1}{2\omega r}M(1-M)\right)^{T}-1}}\,,

where

3
Statistical Justification of Out-of-Sample Performance

In this section, we study the out-of-sample performance of the RRMV portfolio obtained from solving
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
with estimation error.
We begin with the single-period case in Section
3.2
, which serves as a benchmark and builds intuition for how regularization affects out-of-sample Sharpe ratio.
We then extend the analyses to the multiperiod setting in Section
3.3
, where portfolio decisions are made dynamically over time.
This extension shows how the main insights from the single-period case carry over, while also clarifying how the role of regularization can be different under the feedback policies when
T>1
.
To better understand the underlying economic mechanisms, we analyze estimation errors in
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
and
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
separately in both settings.

3.1
Out-of-Sample Analysis

In practical applications, the true expected return
{\bm{\mu}}
and covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}
are unknown and need to be estimated. Typically, the portfolio evaluation proceeds in two stages. In the portfolio construction stage, unknown parameters are estimated from historical return samples and portfolio weights are computed using the estimated parameters. In the out-of-sample evaluation stage, the portfolio is held for a new unseen sample of returns and assessed based on the realized mean return, volatility and Sharpe ratio etc.

Let
\big\{\mathbf{P}_{-1},\mathbf{P}_{-2},\ldots,\mathbf{P}_{-n}\big\}
denote the set of historical excess return vectors prior to current time point at zero, where
n
is the sample size. The sample mean and sample covariance are given by

\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}_{-t}
and
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big(\mathbf{P}_{-t}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}\big)\big(\mathbf{P}_{-t}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}\big)^{\top}
.
In what follows, for any parameter
A
, we use
\hat{A}
to denote its estimated counterpart. Accordingly, when the true expected return
{\bm{\mu}}
and covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}
are replaced by their estimators
\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}
and
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}
in the portfolio optimization, the parameters
\{\hat{a}_{k},\hat{b}_{k},\hat{c}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}\}|_{k=0}^{T-1}
serve as the estimated versions of
\{a_{k},b_{k},c_{k},\mathbf{D}_{k}\}|_{k=0}^{T-1}
, computed by applying the recursions in (
4
). Similarly, the RRMV portfolio policy given in (
6
) based on the estimated parameters
\{\hat{a}_{k},\hat{b}_{k},\hat{c}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}\}|_{k=0}^{T-1}
is denoted by
\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{k}
,
t=0,\ldots,T-1
.
For notational simplicity, we assume
X_{0}=1
.
In the out-of-sample stage, the estimated optimal portfolio
\{\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{k}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
is implemented with future out-of-sample returns
\{\mathbf{P}_{0},\mathbf{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{P}_{T-1}\}
.

Different from
Kan & Zhou 2007
, we focus on the out-of-sample performance of high-dimensional large portfolios where the portfolio size
p
diverges. Following
Ao et al. 2019
, we assume that the ratio
p/n
converges to some constant
c>0
. Under this regime, we study the asymptotic behavior of the related statistics as
n\rightarrow\infty
and
p/n\rightarrow c
. To simplify the notation, we use the expression
Y(n)\xrightarrow{a.s}Y^{*}
to indicate that
Y(n)
converges almost surely to
Y^{*}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
and
p/n\rightarrow c
.
In this section, we use the solution of
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(X_{\textrm{tg}},T)
to illustrate the effect of estimation errors. Similar results can be obtained with
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(\omega,T)
.

Assumption 1
.

All excess returns
\mathbf{P}_{t}
,
t=-q,\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots,T-1
, are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) according to the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\Sigma}})
.

Then, it yields the wealth process,

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{t+1}=r\hat{X}_{t}+\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{k}(\hat{X}_{k}),~~k=0,\ldots,T-1\,,

(10)

where all the parameters in the portfolio policy
\{\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{k}\}|_{k=0}^{T-1}
are fixed numbers conditioning on historical information. Thus, the performance of the cumulative wealth in (
10
) depends only on the newly arrived returns
\{\mathbf{P}_{k}\}|_{k=0}^{T-1}
. We may compute the expected value and the variance of the cumulated wealth
\hat{X}_{t}
with respect to
\{\mathbf{P}_{k}\}|_{k=0}^{T-1}
as follows.

Proposition 3
.

Implementing the optimal RRMV portfolio
\{\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{k}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
, the expected value and variance of the cumulated wealth (
10
) are

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}_{k+1}]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{k}\hat{\eta}_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{k}\hat{\eta}_{j})\,,

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}[\hat{X}_{k+1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\hat{\nu}_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{k}\hat{{g}}_{j}\,,

where
\hat{\lambda}_{k}=\frac{\hat{b}_{0}-X_{\textrm{tg}}}{\hat{c}_{0}}\hat{b}_{k+1}\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\mu}}
,
\hat{\eta}_{k}=r-r\hat{a}_{k+1}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\mu}}+{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}
,
\hat{\nu}_{k}=\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}_{k}]\big(r\hat{a}_{k+1}\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}-\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}\big)-\frac{\hat{b}_{0}-X_{\textrm{tg}}}{2\hat{c}_{0}}\hat{b}_{k}\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}\|^{2}_{\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}}
and
\hat{g}_{k}=\big\|ra_{k+1}\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}-\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}\big\|^{2}_{\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}}+\hat{\eta}_{k}^{2}
,
for
k=0,\ldots,T-1
.

Note the difference between
{\bm{\mu}}
and
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
in
\hat{\eta}_{k}
and the difference between
\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}
in
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}}^{2}=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{x}
.
A direct consequence of Proposition
3
is the one-period Sharpe ratio of
\hat{X}_{T}
:

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}^{2}(\hat{X}_{T})=\frac{(\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}_{T}]-X_{0}r^{T})^{2}}{T\cdot\textrm{Var}[\hat{X}_{T}]}

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\prod_{i=0}^{T-1}\hat{\eta}_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{T-1}\eta_{j})-r^{T})^{2}}{T\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\hat{\nu}_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{T-1}\hat{g}_{j}}\,.

Consider
\mathbf{Q}_{k}\propto\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
for some fixed
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
with proper scaling and zero reference
\mathbf{w}_{k}=0
. Then the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio can be significantly simplified.

Corollary 1
.

If we set
\mathbf{Q}_{k}=\rho\hat{a}_{k+1}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
and
\mathbf{w}_{k}=\bm{0}
for
k=0,\ldots,T-1
, the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio (
11
) becomes

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}^{2}(\hat{X}_{T})=\frac{(\hat{\eta}^{T}-r^{T})^{2}}{T((\hat{\Gamma}+\hat{\eta}^{2})^{T}-\hat{\eta}^{2T})}\,,

where

\hat{\eta}=r\Big(1-\frac{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}+\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\mu}}}{1+\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}+\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\mu}}}\Big)
is the same
\hat{\eta}_{k}
in Proposition
3
and
\hat{\Gamma}=r^{2}\frac{\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}+\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}+\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\mu}}}{(1+\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}+\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\mu}})^{2}}

9
).

3.2
Performance of single-period RRMV portfolio

Before analyzing the general multiperiod RRMV portfolio, we first consider the single-period case
T=1
. For notational simplicity, we omit the time index and treat
t=0
implicitly.
By substituting the estimated parameters
\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}
and
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}
into (
8
), the resulting portfolio is
\mathbf{u}^{\ast}=(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\frac{\hat{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega}+\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{w})

with the out-of-sample Sharpe Ratio given by
\textrm{SR}=\frac{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}^{*}}{\sqrt{(\mathbf{u}^{*})^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}\mathbf{u}^{*}}}
.

To disentangle the effects of the estimation errors of
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
and
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
, we consider two separate scenarios:
(i) the true covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}
is known while the expected return
{\bm{\mu}}
is estimated by
\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}
;
(ii) the expected return
{\bm{\mu}}
is known while the covariance matrix is estimated by
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}
.

3.2.1
Scenario (i): known
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and estimated
{\bm{\mu}}

We first provide theoretical understanding on the effect of estimating the expected return
{\bm{\mu}}
by the historical sample mean, leading to the necessity of adding the reference regularization.
The estimation error of
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
follows the following distribution,
\varepsilon=\hat{\bm{\mu}}-{\bm{\mu}}\sim N(0,{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)
, where
n
is the sample size of the
p
-dimension return vector.

For any given matrix
\mathbf{Q}\succ 0
, we set the reference portfolio as
\mathbf{w}=\alpha\bar{\mathbf{w}}
with some fixed
\bar{\mathbf{w}}
.
Then the regularization can be adjusted by choosing
\alpha
, when
\mathbf{Q}
is fixed.
The optimal portfolio for this scenario can be written as

\mathbf{u}^{\ast}=({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\frac{\hat{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega}+\alpha\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}})

where we can show that
X_{\textrm{tg}}-r=\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\frac{\hat{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega}+\alpha\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}})
.
The mapping between
\omega
and target return
X_{\textrm{tg}}
depends on our estimation
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
. Therefore, the optimization problem
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\textrm{RRMV}}(X_{\textrm{tg}},T)
, given any fixed value of
X_{\textrm{tg}}
, the corresponding
\omega
is affected by the estimation error.
As
n\to\infty
, it is not hard to show

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\omega}\xrightarrow{a.s}\frac{1}{2\omega^{\ast}}=\frac{X_{\textrm{tg}}-r-\alpha{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}}{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+\operatorname{tr}(({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)}\,.

So, the optimal policy almost surely converges, that is,
\mathbf{u}^{\ast}\xrightarrow{a.s}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\frac{\hat{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega^{\ast}}+\alpha\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}})
, and we can compute the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of the asymptotic allocation.

Theorem 3
.

Under the assumptions of known
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and sample mean
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
, when
T=1
, the Sharpe ratio of terminal wealth almost surely converges.
And its limiting value depends on
\alpha
and
\bar{\mathbf{w}}
as follows.

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(\mathbf{Q},\alpha\bar{\mathbf{w}})=\frac{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{d}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\|({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{d}_{\alpha}\|_{{\bm{\Sigma}}}+\frac{e_{{\bm{\mu}}}}{(2\omega^{\ast})^{2}}}}\,,

where
\mathbf{d}_{\alpha}=\frac{{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega^{\ast}}+\alpha\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}
,
e_{{\bm{\mu}}}=\operatorname{tr}(({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)
is the bias caused by estimation error and
\textrm{SR}_{\infty}
is the asymptotic Sharpe ratio when
n\to\infty
.

Theorem
3
states that we can achieve the maximum Sharpe ratio
\sqrt{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}
by letting
\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}=({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q}){\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}
and
\alpha=\frac{X_{\textrm{tg}}-r}{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}
such that
\frac{1}{2\omega^{\ast}}=0
.
This provides us theoretical guarantee for improved Sharpe by adding the proper regulation term for
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
. For instance, consider the ideal situation of
\mathbf{Q}=\rho{\bm{\Sigma}}
as we know true
{\bm{\Sigma}}
.

Corollary 2
.

Using the assumptions in Proposition
3
, when
\mathbf{Q}=\rho{\bm{\Sigma}}
, we can achieve the maximum asymptotic Sharpe ratio by setting
\alpha^{\ast}=\frac{X_{\textrm{tg}}-r}{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{\ast}=(1+\rho)\rho^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}
for
\rho>0
.

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(\mathbf{Q},\alpha^{\ast}\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{\ast})=\sqrt{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}\,,

while the MV portfolio without regularization can only achieve
\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(\mathbf{Q},\bm{0})=\frac{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}{\sqrt{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+p/n}}
.

In high-dimensional settings, the unregulated mean–variance portfolio (
\alpha=0
) exhibits the well-known attenuation of the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio when
p/n\to c>0
, which falls strictly below the maximum Sharpe ratio.
In contrast, under the optimal regulation, the implied risk aversion satisfies
1/\omega^{\ast}=0
, so the optimal portfolio
effectively uses the reference allocation
\alpha^{\ast}\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{\ast}
.
This result shows that the reference portfolio acts as an implicit shrinkage device on the estimated mean
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
: the regularization adjusts the effective risk aversion in a way that counterbalances the noise in
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
, pulling the portfolio towards the reference direction.

3.2.2
Scenario (ii): known
{\bm{\mu}}
and estimated
{\bm{\Sigma}}

In this section, we investigate the effect of estimation error in the sample covariance
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
and assume full knowledge of the expected return, i.e.
\hat{\bm{\mu}}={\bm{\mu}}
.
We set the regularization matrix as
\mathbf{Q}=\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
and the portfolio is

\mathbf{u}^{\ast}=(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}+\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}(\frac{1}{2\omega}{\bm{\mu}}+\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{w})
.
The question is then by choosing
\rho>0
and a proper
\mathbf{w}
, are we able to achieve a better out-of-sample performance for
\mathbf{u}^{\ast}
?

Firstly, let us consider the geometric intuition about the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio.

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}(\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}},\mathbf{w})=\frac{\langle{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}},\mathbf{u}^{*}\rangle_{{\bm{\Sigma}}}}{\|\mathbf{u}^{*}\|_{{\bm{\Sigma}}}}\,.

So the numerator of SR is the inner product between our portfolio
\mathbf{u}^{*}
and the mean-variance portfolio with true
{\bm{\Sigma}}
, where the inner product is defined as
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle_{{\bm{\Sigma}}}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}\mathbf{b}
. Therefore, SR is a scaled version of the
\cos\theta
angle between
\mathbf{u}^{*}
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}
, that is
\text{SR}=\cos\theta\cdot\|{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}\|_{{\bm{\Sigma}}}=\cos\theta\cdot\sqrt{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}
, which is optimized at
\sqrt{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}
when those two directions are in perfect align. Is a positive
\rho
helpful in dragging
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}
towards
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}
? Intuitively, there are two effects: firstly if
\mathbf{w}
itself bears certain information on
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}
, we benefit from forcing the portfolio to be similar to
\mathbf{w}
; secondly, based on the random matrix theory (RMT), the spectrum of
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
follows the Marchenko–Pastur distribution whose support is wider than that of
{\bm{\Sigma}}

(
Marčenko & Pastur 1967
,
Bai et al. 2009
)
, hence a certain level of shrinkage is useful to correct this bias in recovering eigenvalues of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
. More specifically, we present a useful result from RMT in the appendix (Lemma
4
), and obtain the following conclusion applying it.

Theorem 4
.

Under the assumptions of known
{\bm{\mu}}
and sample covariance
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
, when
T=1
, we have the almost sure convergence of the Sharpe ratio
\textrm{SR}(\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}},\mathbf{w})
if
\|{\bm{\Sigma}}\|
is bounded.
Its limit can be expressed as

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}},\mathbf{w})=\kappa_{\rho}^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\rho}^{-1}\mathbf{d}_{\rho}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{d}_{\rho}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\rho}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}\mathbf{A}_{\rho}^{-1}\mathbf{d}_{\rho}}}\,,

where
\kappa_{\rho}=1-\frac{\tilde{s}(\rho)}{(1+s(\rho))^{2}}
,
\mathbf{A}_{\rho}=\frac{{\bm{\Sigma}}}{1+s(\rho)}+\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
,
\mathbf{d}_{\rho}=\frac{{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega^{\ast}}+\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{w}
,

s(\rho)
is the solution of
s(\rho)=\frac{c}{p}\operatorname{tr}({\bm{\Sigma}}\mathbf{A}_{\rho}^{-1})
,

\tilde{s}(\rho)
is the solution of
\tilde{s}(\rho)=-\kappa_{\rho}\frac{c}{p}\operatorname{tr}({\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}\mathbf{A}_{\rho}^{-2})
and
\frac{1}{2\omega^{\ast}}=\frac{X_{\textrm{tg}}-r-\rho{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\rho})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{w}}{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\rho}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}
.

We can treat the asymptotic Sharpe ratio as a product of a scalar factor
\kappa_{\rho}^{-1/2}
and the pseudo SR, which is the rest of the term.
Let us now investigate the effects of
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{w}
on the Sharpe ratio.
First, we check how
\mathbf{w}
will affect the Sharpe ratio if we set
\mathbf{Q}\to\bm{0}
5
5
endnote:

5

Since
\mathbf{Q}=\bm{0}
entirely cancels the regularization term, it makes more sense to consider
\mathbf{Q}\to\bm{0}
while
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{w}\to\tilde{\mathbf{w}}
, e.g.
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}/n
and
\mathbf{w}=n\tilde{\mathbf{w}}
.
.
To maintain the regularization effect of
\mathbf{w}
when
\mathbf{Q}\to\bm{0}
, we assume
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{w}\to\tilde{\mathbf{w}}
, which can change independent of
\mathbf{Q}
.

\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(\bm{0},\tilde{\mathbf{w}})=\kappa_{0}^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}(\frac{{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega^{\ast}}+\tilde{\mathbf{w}})}{\sqrt{(\frac{{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega^{\ast}}+\tilde{\mathbf{w}})^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}(\frac{{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega^{\ast}}+\tilde{\mathbf{w}})}}\,.

We can show that a necessary condition of maximizing the above Sharpe ratio over
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}
is that
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\ast}\propto{\bm{\mu}}
.
By plugging in this optimal
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\ast}
, the Sharpe ratio becomes

\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(\bm{0},\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\ast})=\kappa_{0}^{-1/2}\cdot\sqrt{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}\,.

This shows that the pseudo Sharpe ratio has been adjusted to align with the maximum Sharpe ratio.
However, the presence of the additional scalar
\kappa_{0}
still diminishes the final asymptotic Sharpe ratio (see below for the proof that
\tilde{s}(0)<0
so that
\kappa_{0}>1
).
This implies that if we do not apply a proper regularization for
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
, i.e. naively using
\mathbf{Q}\to\bm{0}
, even with the freedom of tuning
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}
, we cannot reach the maximum Sharpe ratio.

Next, we show that using a nonzero
\mathbf{Q}
is indeed necessary to regularize the sample covariance
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
in high dimensional settings.
We would like to argue that the asymptotic SR is typically maximized at
\rho>0
.
Consider the simpler case of
{\bm{\Sigma}}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}
,
\mathbf{Q}=\rho\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{w}=\bm{0}
as an example. We have

\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(\rho\mathbf{I},\bm{0})=\kappa_{\rho}^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{\|{\bm{\mu}}\|_{2}}{\sigma}\,.

The scalar
\kappa_{\rho}^{-1/2}
plays an essential role, where
s
solves
s=c(1/(1+s)+\rho/\sigma^{2})^{-1}
and
\tilde{s}
solves
\tilde{s}=c(\tilde{s}/(1+s)^{2}-1)(1/(1+s)+\rho/\sigma^{2})^{-2}
. When
\rho=0
, it is easy to calculate
s=c/(1-c)
,
\tilde{s}=-c/(1-c)^{3}
and the SR scalar
\kappa_{\rho}^{-1/2}=\sqrt{1-c}
, which reduces SR when
c=p/n\in(0,1)
6
6
endnote:

6

Note that when
c\geq 1
,
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
is not invertible and we cannot choose
\rho=0
.
.
In this case, SR is maximized when
\rho=\infty
, which gives
s=0,\tilde{s}=0
and the scalar
\kappa_{\rho}=1
. This is very intuitive since when
{\bm{\Sigma}}\propto\mathbf{I}
, the penalty
\rho\mathbf{I}
actually applies the correct covariance, so the best way to construct portfolio is to drag
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
to
\mathbf{I}
as much as possible. When
{\bm{\Sigma}}
is not proportional to identity,
\text{SR}_{\infty}/\kappa_{\rho}
is actually maximized exactly at
\rho=0
. We call this term pseudo SR, computed as if we use the scaled true covariance
{\bm{\Sigma}}/(1+s)
to build the portfolio.
Therefore, there exists a tradeoff between the scalar
\kappa_{\rho}
and the pseudo SR:
\kappa_{\rho}
is maximized at
\rho=\infty
and the pseudo SR is maximized at
\rho=0
. In general, this tradeoff leads to the selection of an optimal positive
\rho
.

To illustrate this numerically, we conduct a toy simulation: set

and

from uniform

;

is a diagonal matrix with diagonal elements generated from Gamma distribution with shape

and scale

;
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
is set as an identity matrix and recall
\mathbf{Q}=\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
.
For a series of
\rho

and calculate
\kappa_{\rho}
, pseudo SR and
\text{SR}_{\infty}
which is the product of the two. The curves versus

are shown in Figure

.
It is very clear
\kappa_{\rho}
increases and pseudo SR decreases as
\rho
grows.
To balance these two curves, the optimal

to maximize

is achieved at a positive value.

3.3
Performance of Multiperiod RRMV portfolio

In this section, we analyze the general multiperiod RRMV portfolio.
The out-of-sample Sharpe ratio has been derived in Proposition
3
.
Following the single-period RRMV discussions, we account for the two primary sources of estimation risk, arising from errors in estimating the mean and the covariance matrix, in the two subsections below.
Our analysis is organized around two questions.
The first concerns how the regularization term affects the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio, similar to the single-period setting.
The second investigates how this effect propagates in a multiperiod environment. We will reveal new phenomena, which cannot be observed under the single-period RRMV framework and is unraveled for the first time in the multiperiod portfolio optimization literature.

3.3.1
Scenario (i): known
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and estimated
{\bm{\mu}}

We assume the covariance matrix
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}={\bm{\Sigma}}
is known and focus on estimation error in the sample mean
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
.
For the general multiperiod RRMV portfolio with
\mathbf{Q}_{k}
and
\mathbf{w}_{k}
in each period, we first show that the system of parameters defined in (
4
) will almost surely converge as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 2
.

Under the assumptions of known
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and sample mean
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{k}=\hat{\bm{\mu}}
, the parameters in RRMV policy almost surely converge as follows.
Let
\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast}={\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q}_{k}/a_{k+1}^{\ast}
,

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{k}\xrightarrow{a.s}

\displaystyle a_{k}^{\ast}=\frac{(a_{k+1}^{\ast})^{-1}(a_{k+1}^{\ast}r+\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{k}(\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})^{2}}{1+{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}(\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+\operatorname{tr}((\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)}

\displaystyle+(a_{k+1}^{\ast})^{-1}\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{k}\Big(a_{k+1}^{\ast}\mathbf{Q}_{k}^{-1}-(\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}\Big)\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{k}\xrightarrow{a.s}

\displaystyle b_{k}^{\ast}={{b}}_{k+1}^{\ast}\frac{r+(a_{k+1}^{\ast})^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}(\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}}{1+{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}(\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+\operatorname{tr}((\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{k}\xrightarrow{a.s}

\displaystyle c_{k}^{\ast}=c_{k+1}^{\ast}+(b_{k+1}^{\ast})^{2}(a_{k+1}^{\ast})^{-1}

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}(\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+\operatorname{tr}((\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)}{1+{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}(\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+\operatorname{tr}((\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\ast})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)}\,.

As a direct consequence, the portfolio policy and the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio will also converge almost surely.
The corresponding asymptotic Sharpe ratio if we set
T=1
is identical to that of the single-period RRMV portfolio given in Section
3.2.1
.

Theorem 5
.

Under the assumptions of known
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and sample mean
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{k}=\hat{\bm{\mu}}
, for general
T
, the asymptotic Sharpe ratio of the
RRMV
portfolio, given
\mathbf{Q}_{k}
and
\mathbf{w}_{k}
, converges almost surely. Its limit is

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(T,\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{w}_{k})=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}_{T}]_{\infty}-r^{T}}{\sqrt{T\cdot\textrm{Var}(\hat{X}_{T})_{\infty}}}\,,

where
\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}_{T}]_{\infty}=\prod_{i=0}^{T-1}\eta^{\ast}_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}(\lambda^{\ast}_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{T-1}\eta^{\ast}_{j})
,
\textrm{Var}(\hat{X}_{T})_{\infty}=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\nu_{i}^{\ast}\prod_{j=i+1}^{T-1}{g}_{j}^{\ast}
.
\lambda_{i}^{\ast}
,
\eta_{i}^{\ast}
,
\nu_{i}^{\ast}
and
g_{i}^{\ast}
are the limiting values of their corresponding estimators, which are constant depending on
a_{k}^{\ast}
,
b_{k}^{\ast}
,
c_{k}^{\ast}
,
\mathbf{Q}_{k}
and
\mathbf{w}_{k}
.

Theorem
5
is too general and abstract to provide useful understanding. To examine separately the contributions from the regularization
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{w}
, we first fix
\mathbf{w}_{k}=\bm{0}
and then fix
\mathbf{Q}_{k}\to\bm{0}
.
The following corollary shows the corresponding asymptotic Sharpe ratio when
\mathbf{Q}_{k}
aligns with the true
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and
\mathbf{w}_{k}=\bm{0}

Corollary 3
.

Under the same assumptions in Proposition
5
, if
\mathbf{w}_{k}=\bm{0}
,
\mathbf{Q}_{k}=a_{k+1}\rho{\bm{\Sigma}}
, the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of the RRMV portfolio almost surely converges to

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(T,\rho,\bm{0})=\frac{T^{-1/2}(({\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+e_{\rho,{\bm{\mu}}})^{T}-e_{\rho,{\bm{\mu}}}^{T})}{\sqrt{({\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+p/n+e_{\rho,{\bm{\mu}}}^{2})^{T}-e_{\rho,{\bm{\mu}}}^{2T}}}\,,

where

and following Theorem

in this case.
When

\frac{\partial\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(T,\rho,\bm{0})}{\partial\rho}\big|_{\rho=0}=0

Note that the effect of
\mathbf{Q}
is very different from the single-period case. The conventional understanding for the effect for

.

{\bm{\mu}}

Using the notations defined in Lemma
3

Theorem 6

{\bm{\mu}}

\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k}=\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{k}\xrightarrow{a.s}a_{k}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=a_{k+1}^{\ast}r^{2}-(a_{k+1}^{\ast})^{2}r^{2}C_{{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\mu}},k}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\quad+2ra_{k+1}^{\ast}C_{{\bm{\mu}},\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k},k}^{\ast}+\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}

\displaystyle\quad-C_{\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k},k}^{\ast}\,,

\displaystyle=b_{k+1}^{\ast}r-a_{k+1}^{\ast}b_{k+1}^{\ast}rC_{{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\mu}},k}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\quad+b_{k+1}^{\ast}C_{{\bm{\mu}},\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k},k}^{\ast}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{k}\xrightarrow{a.s}{c}_{k}^{\ast}

\displaystyle={c}_{k+1}^{\ast}-(b_{k+1}^{\ast})^{2}C_{{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\mu}},k}^{\ast}\,.

Let
\phi_{k}^{\ast}=ra_{k+1}^{\ast}E[\hat{X}_{k}]_{\infty}+\frac{b_{0}^{\ast}-X_{\textrm{tg}}}{{c}_{0}^{\ast}}b_{k+1}^{\ast}

\mathbf{Q}_{k}

\mathbf{w}_{k}

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(T,\mathbf{Q}_{k},\mathbf{w}_{k})=\frac{X_{\textrm{tg}}-r^{T}}{\sqrt{T\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\nu_{i}^{\ast}\prod_{j=i+1}^{T-1}{g}_{j}^{\ast}}}\,,

where following Theorem
5
, the detailed formulas for

\nu_{k}^{\ast}=(\phi_{k}^{\ast}B_{{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\mu}},k}^{\ast})^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}_{k}]_{\infty}^{2}B_{\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k},k}^{\ast}-2\phi_{k}^{\ast}E[\hat{X}_{k}]_{\infty}B_{{\bm{\mu}},\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k},k}^{\ast}
and
g_{k}^{\ast}=r^{2}(a_{k+1}^{\ast})^{2}B_{{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\mu}},k}^{\ast}+B_{\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k},k}^{\ast}-2ra_{k+1}^{\ast}B_{{\bm{\mu}},\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k},k}^{\ast}+(\eta_{k}^{\ast})^{2}
.

After obtaining the expression for the asymptotic Sharpe ratio, we address how regularization
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{w}
function in this scenario. Similar to Section

, we consider
\mathbf{Q}_{k}\to\bm{0}
and
\mathbf{w}_{k}=\bm{0}
separately.
To isolate the effect of
\mathbf{w}
, we set
\mathbf{Q}_{k}\to 0
and assume
\mathbf{Q}_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}\to\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k}
.
As in the single-period case, a necessary condition for the optimal reference weight is
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\propto{\bm{\mu}}
since true
{\bm{\mu}}
is known.
We therefore parameterize the reference weight as
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k}=\tilde{\mathbf{w}}=\alpha{\bm{\mu}}
and use this ideal case to examine how the choice of
\alpha
influences the limiting Sharpe ratio.
Numerical results are illustrated using the same simulation design as before.
From Figure

, we find that the role of the reference weight
\mathbf{w}
differs substantially in the multiperiod RRMV portfolio.
As shown in Theorem
4
, in the single-period case, the scale of the reference weight when
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}\propto{\bm{\mu}}
does not affect the Sharpe ratio at all.
Consistent with this observation, Figure
4(a)
shows that the limiting Sharpe ratio deteriorates as dimensionality increases and cannot be improved by choosing a positive
\alpha
.
In contrast, the multiperiod setting exhibits a fundamentally different behavior.
Figure
4(b)
demonstrates that the asymptotic Sharpe ratio can be maximized at some
\alpha^{\ast}>0
.
This finding indicates that even without applying shrinkage
\mathbf{Q}
to the covariance estimator, an appropriately chosen reference weight can still mitigate the estimation error in
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
and improve the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio. This again expands our conventional understanding that
\mathbf{Q}
is used to regularize
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
while
\mathbf{w}
is used to regularize
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
. Actually when
T>1
and
p/n>0
, even with
\mathbf{Q}_{k}\to\bm{0}
and known true expected return
{\bm{\mu}}
,
\mathbf{w}
can be used to regularize
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
.

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(T,\rho,\bm{0})=\kappa_{\rho}^{-T/2}\cdot\frac{T^{-1/2}(({\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\rho}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+1)^{T}-1)}{\sqrt{({\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\rho}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}\mathbf{A}_{\rho}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+1)^{T}-1}}\,,

where
\kappa_{\rho}=1-\frac{\tilde{s}(\rho)}{(1+s(\rho))^{2}}
,
\mathbf{A}_{\rho}=\frac{{\bm{\Sigma}}}{1+s(\rho)}+\rho\bar{\mathbf{Q}}

3

\bar{\mathbf{Q}}={\bm{\Sigma}}

and
\tilde{s}(\rho)=0
.

In this section, we evaluate and compare the out-of-sample performance of the proposed RRMV portfolio against a range of benchmark portfolio policies, using both simulation experiments and empirical tests on real market data.
We consider two industry portfolio datasets—the
ten industry portfolios
(“10-Ind”) and the
forty-eight industry portfolios
(‘‘48-Ind’’)---which represent the U.S. equity market at different levels of aggregation.
Both datasets are obtained from Ken French’s data library.

.

In addition, we study a stock-level dataset comprising constituents of the S&P 500 index, obtained from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP).

4.1
Benchmark Portfolios and Implementation

We compare the proposed portfolios with a comprehensive set of benchmark portfolios that are widely used in the literature.
Table
1
lists all benchmark portfolios included in our analysis.
These benchmarks can be grouped into three categories. The first category consists of
static portfolio policies
, including the equally weighted portfolio (EW), as well as the classical mean-variance (MV) and global minimum-variance (GMV) portfolios.
For the MV and GMV policies, portfolios are constructed using either the sample covariance matrix or the linear shrinkage estimator of
Ledoit & Wolf 2004
to mitigate the impact of estimation error.
We denote shrinkage-based policies by appending “-SH” to the policy name, where “SH” indicates the use of the shrinkage estimator.
In addition, we consider the three-fund hybrid portfolio policy proposed by
Kan & Zhou 2007
, where its risk averse is set as 3.

The second category consists of conventional dynamic MMV policies without reference regulation, including the multiperiod mean-variance (MMV) policy of
Li & Ng 2000
. This class serves as the primary dynamic benchmark for evaluating the effect of reference regulation.

The third category comprises our proposed reference-regulated multiperiod mean-variance (RRMV) policies.
The regulation term in the RRMV framework is specified through two sets of hyperparameters,
\mathbf{Q}_{k}
and
\mathbf{w}_{k}
, for
k=0,\ldots,T-1
.
In our numerical experiments, we set
\mathbf{Q}_{k}=\mathbf{Q}=\rho\mathbf{I}
, where
\rho>0
is a tuning parameter, and take
\mathbf{w}_{k}\equiv\mathbf{w}_{0}
as a predetermined reference portfolio constructed from in-sample returns.
We allow the reference portfolio
\mathbf{w}_{0}
to correspond to several static benchmark portfolios.
Specifically, we consider the EW portfolio, the GMV-SH portfolio, and the zero portfolio (i.e.,
\mathbf{w}_{0}=\bm{0}
), as these references remain well defined and stable in high-dimensional settings.
When
\mathbf{w}_{0}=\bm{0}
, the regulation term reduces to a pure
\ell_{2}
penalty, and we denote this specification by
\text{RRMV-}\ell_{2}
.

The out-of-sample performances of different portfolios are evaluated based on three commonly-used criteria: the out-of-sample Risk (standard deviation), the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio and the average turnover of portfolio weights.
In practice, portfolio turnover and the associated trading costs constitute an important component of overall portfolio performance
(

)
.
All metrics are reported at the monthly frequency to facilitate comparison across portfolio policies.

4.2
Simulation Comparisons

We evaluate the performance of our RRMV portfolios relative to several benchmarks using simulated data calibrated from the 48-industry portfolio returns.
The simulations test both normal and heavy-tailed (Student-
t
) distributions for asset returns.
The estimation window
n
varies between 60 and 120 months, and investment horizons
T
range from 1 to 6 years.
The complete results can be found in Appendix
S1
.

Simulation results show that RRMV portfolios outperform unregularized strategies in high dimensions and exhibit steadily increasing Sharpe ratios as
T
grows.
With different estimation windows, RRMV portfolios deliver the highest Sharpe ratios while maintaining significantly lower turnover than multiperiod MV strategies.
We also plot the Sharpe ratio curves as a function of
\rho
. The advantage of applying a positive
\rho
is more pronounced in the high-dimensional regime.
Overall, all the observations match with our theoretical understanding very well and reveal clearly the benefits of RRMV portfolios in the multiperiod settings.

4.3
Real Data Analysis

In this section, we present out-of-sample results based on real market data.
We select the regularization parameter using a rolling validation procedure based solely on historical data.
The validation stage consists of
\tau
rolling runs. Specifically, the portfolio decision at time
t
uses data from
t-n-\tau-T+1
to
t-1
.
In each run,
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
and
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
are estimated using a window of
n
past observations, and the resulting portfolio rule is applied over the subsequent
T
periods to compute a realized multiperiod return.
The estimation and evaluation windows then move forward by one period, and this process is repeated
\tau
times, ending immediately before the test date
t
.
The parameter with the highest empirical validation Sharpe ratio is selected and applied over the subsequent out-of-sample horizon of length
T
.
Particularly, our validation procedure is used to select the regularization parameter
\rho
for
\mathbf{Q}=\rho\mathbf{I}
.

4.3.3
Index enhancement portfolios

Finally, we present an application of the RRMV model that exploits its flexibility in specifying the reference policy.
Since the reference portfolio in the RRMV framework can be chosen arbitrarily, we adopt an index-tracking policy (see
Jansen & Van Dijk 2002
,
Benidis et al. 2017
,
Strub & Baumann 2018
) as the reference in order to incorporate information embedded in a benchmark index within the mean-variance framework. The corresponding index-tracking policy is denoted by IT.
We refer to this approach as the
index enhancement
policy and denote it by RRMV-IT.

For practical implementation, rather than directly using the index weights, we construct an index-tracking portfolio using the same set of assets as in the investment universe.
This ensures that both the RRMV optimal policy and the reference portfolio are defined over an identical asset set.
Specifically, we obtain the index-tracking policy by minimizing the empirical tracking error
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{w}-r_{t}\big|
,
where
\mathbf{R}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the return vector of the constituent stocks in the investment universe at time
t
,
r_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is the return of the index, and
n
is the length of the historical sample.
We further impose the constraints
\mathbf{w}\geq 0
and

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the multiperiod mean–variance portfolio optimization.
As shown in the previous literature and our derivations, incorporating feedback policies can achieve Sharpe ratios higher than static allocation rules.
Nevertheless, estimation error remains a central practical difficulty, as its effect is harder to analyze given that a sequence of future decisions is influenced rather than a single allocation.
The issue is even more serious in large portfolios where the number of assets is not small relative to the available data.
In order to protect portfolio performance against estimation error, we propose the reference-regulated mean–variance (RRMV) portfolio optimization model. The corresponding algorithm based on dynamic programming techniques for solving this problem is carefully presented.

To understand the role of reference regulation in high-dimensional portfolio allocation in multiperiod settings, we provide a general framework to analyze the asymptotic out-of-sample Sharpe ratio in high-dimensional environments.
Our theoretical results show that regularization and the use of a reference portfolio can improve out-of-sample Sharpe ratios by mitigating the adversarial impact of estimation errors in the mean vector and the covariance matrix.
Specifically, when
T>1
and
p/n>0
, we unravel a new phenomenon of cross regularization:
\mathbf{w}
can be used to regularize
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
and
\mathbf{Q}
can be used to regularize
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
, which expands our conventional understanding for the single-period portfolio allocation where
\mathbf{Q}
regularizes
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
and
\mathbf{w}
regularizes
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
. We also explicitly present the effect of dimensionality
p/n
, optimization horizon
T
and regularization terms to the asymptotic Sharpe ratio. This could lead to new insights for portfolio managers about how to impose a more proper regularization and how to select a meaningful reference policy. In addition, to our best knowledge, we are the first to present a rigorous result on how the number of periods affects the Sharpe ratio with the existence of estimation errors.

Extensive simulation and empirical evidence further demonstrate that the proposed framework can effectively tackle the estimation error and lead to more reliable portfolio performance.
The focus of the present paper is the proposed multiperiod RRMV portfolios, through which we reveal the role of regularization and reference portfolios in improving out-of-sample performance.
Extending the framework to allow for time-varying return moments, alternative estimators, and other types of regularization or optimization settings is left for our future research.

Simulation Comparisons

In this section, we evaluate the performance of the RRMV portfolio relative to several benchmark strategies using simulated data calibrated from real market returns.
The simulation design is based on monthly returns of the 48-industry portfolios over the period from January 2000 to December 2024.
Let
\{\mathbf{P}_{-t}\}_{t=1}^{N}
denote the
p
-dimensional vector of excess returns in the calibration sample, where
N=300
in our data.
We set the population mean vector and covariance matrix in the simulation equal to their corresponding sample estimates,

{\bm{\mu}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbf{P}_{-t}~\text{and}~{\bm{\Sigma}}:=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{t=1}^{N}(\mathbf{P}_{-t}-{\bm{\mu}})(\mathbf{P}_{-t}-{\bm{\mu}})^{\top}\,.

The risk-free rate
r_{f}
is fixed at a constant equal to the in-sample average return of the one-month U.S. Treasury bill over the same period.
For each Monte Carlo replication, we generate a synthetic out-of-sample return path whose distribution is calibrated to
({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\Sigma}})
.

In the baseline data-generating process (DGP), returns are assumed to be independent and identically distributed according to a multivariate normal distribution,
\mathbf{P}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\Sigma}})
, for
t=1,2,\ldots
.
To assess robustness with respect to tail behavior, we further consider a multivariate Student-
t
model with
\nu=6
degrees of freedom, where the DGP is given by
\mathbf{P}_{t}\sim t_{\nu}({\bm{\mu}},\mathbf{S})
, where
\nu=6
.
For a multivariate
t_{\nu}({\bm{\mu}},\mathbf{S})
distribution with
\nu>2
, the covariance matrix satisfies

\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{P}_{t})=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}\bm{S}
.
To ensure that the heavy-tailed specification shares the same population covariance
{\bm{\Sigma}}
as the Gaussian benchmark, we set the scale matrix to
\bm{S}=\frac{\nu-2}{\nu}\,{\bm{\Sigma}}
.

We conduct
1,000
Monte Carlo replications to evaluate portfolio performance.
Within each replication, we follow the same rolling implementation scheme as in the empirical analysis.
At each rebalancing date, the most recent
n
months of simulated returns are used to estimate the required inputs, based on which portfolio weights are computed. Performance is then evaluated over the subsequent out-of-sample period.
We consider estimation window lengths
n\in\{60,120\}
and investment horizons
T\in\{1,3,6\}
.
Across replications, we report the average values of Risk, Sharpe ratio and Turnover defined in (
12
).
Moreover, we report the
p
-values from tests of Sharpe ratio differences relative to the RRMV-EW benchmark with
\rho=10^{-3}
.
Results under the Gaussian specification are reported in Tables
S1
and
S2
.

First, We consider the case of a short estimation window (
n=60
) in Table
S1
, where the ratio
p/n\approx 1
corresponds to a high-dimensional setting with substantial estimation error. Under this regime, standard MV and MMV portfolios suffer from severe estimation noise and consequently deliver relatively low out-of-sample Sharpe ratios. By contrast, shrinkage-based strategies achieve substantially higher Sharpe ratios than their unregularized counterparts. RRMV portfolios further stabilize portfolio weights and yield meaningful improvements in out-of-sample Sharpe ratios.

Second, the advantage of RRMV becomes more pronounced as the investment horizon
T
increases in Table
S1
.
For static portfolios such as EW, GMV, and their shrinkage variants, Sharpe ratios remain largely unchanged across different values of
T
.
However, RRMV portfolios could benefit from the multiperiod structure and exhibit steadily increasing Sharpe ratios as
T
grows.
This pattern is consistent with the theoretical advantage of feedback policies discussed in Section
2.2
.

When the estimation window increases to
n=120
in Table
S2
, the performance gap between regularized and unregularized portfolios becomes smaller, reflecting the reduced severity of high-dimensional estimation error as
p/n
declines.
With more observations available, MV and MMV strategies achieve improved Sharpe ratios relative to the high-dimensional case.
However, RRMV continues to deliver the highest Sharpe ratios, particularly at longer horizons.
Moreover, the improvement in unregularized multiperiod portfolios is accompanied by a substantial increase in turnover.
For example, at
T=6

Consequently, once transaction costs are considered, the apparent convergence in Sharpe ratios would likely favor RRMV more strongly.

Figures
S1
and

\rho
for different investment horizons
T
and estimation window lengths
n
. When
n=60

S1

\rho

\rho=0
is already relatively high, so the incremental benefit of regularization is smaller.

Overall, the simulation results indicate that the value of regularization depends on both the estimation noise level and the investment horizon. RRMV delivers the largest improvements in Sharpe ratios in high-noise environments and at longer horizons, while maintaining substantially lower turnover than competing multiperiod strategies.
These findings suggest that RRMV strikes a favorable balance between risk-adjusted performance and trading stability, particularly when data are limited.

Proof of Lemma
1

Proof.

Recall that we define the parameters recursively, for
k=T-1,\ldots,0

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{k}

periods:

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\frac{(X_{\textrm{tg}}-r^{T})^{2}}{\textrm{Var}(X_{T})}=\frac{1}{T}\frac{(X_{\textrm{tg}}-r^{T})^{2}}{\bigg[\frac{X_{\textrm{tg}}-r^{T}}{\eta^{T}-r^{T}}\bigg]^{2}((\Gamma+\eta^{2})^{T}-\eta^{2T})}=\frac{1}{T}\frac{(\eta^{T}-r^{T})^{2}}{((\Gamma+\eta^{2})^{T}-\eta^{2T})}

Substituting the parameters, we can derive the result of the one-period Sharpe ratio achieved by the MMV policy,

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\max}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{(1+{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})^{T}-1}\,.

Next, we consider holding the static MV portfolio over
T
periods.
The corresponding terminal wealth will be
X_{T}=X_{0}\cdot\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}(r+\frac{1}{2\omega}\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})
.
It is straightforward that

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}(\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}(r+\frac{1}{2\omega}\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}(r+\frac{1}{2\omega}\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}(r+\frac{1}{2\omega}\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})]^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(r+\frac{1}{2\omega}\mathbf{P}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})^{2}]

\displaystyle=(r+\frac{1}{2\omega}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})^{2}+(\frac{1}{2\omega})^{2}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}\,.

Then, the one-period Sharpe ratio obtained is

\textrm{SR}_{\text{hold}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\frac{(r+\frac{1}{2\omega}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})^{T}-r^{T}}{\sqrt{((r+\frac{1}{2\omega}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})^{2}+(\frac{1}{2\omega})^{2}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})^{T}-(r+\frac{1}{2\omega}{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}})^{2T}}}\,.

Let
M=\frac{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}{\,2\omega r+{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}\,}
, we have

\textrm{SR}_{\mathrm{hold}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\frac{1-(1-M)^{T}}{\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{M(1-M)}{2\omega r}\right)^{T}-1}}\,.

Finally, we compare
\textrm{SR}_{\max}
with
\textrm{SR}_{\mathrm{hold}}
.
When
T=1
,
\textrm{SR}_{\max}=\textrm{SR}_{\mathrm{hold}}=\sqrt{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}
.
However, for
T>1
, we can show that

M\in(0,1)
, since
\omega>0
.
Then, by Bernoulli’s inequality, we have
(1-M)^{T}>1-TM
, leading to
1-(1-M)^{T}<TM
.
Similarly, we have
\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{M(1-M)}{2\omega r}\right)^{T}-1}>\sqrt{\frac{TM(1-M)}{2\omega r}}
.
Therefore, we can show that

\textrm{SR}_{\mathrm{hold}}<\sqrt{\frac{2\omega rM}{1-M}}=\sqrt{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}}<\textrm{SR}_{\max}\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Given
\mathbf{X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times p}
with
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{Z}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
where the elements of
\mathbf{Z}
are iid with zero mean, variance 1 and finite eighth order moment,
{\bm{\Sigma}}
is a positive semi-definite matrix. For any deterministic
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
with bounded norm and positive definite matrix
\mathbf{S}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
, define

\displaystyle m_{n}(z)=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}}{n}+\mathbf{S}+z\mathbf{I}_{p}\bigg)^{-1}\mathbf{b},

and assume that
p,n
tends to infinity with proportion

. Then for

\displaystyle m_{n}(z)-\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bigg(\frac{{\bm{\Sigma}}}{1+s_{n}(z)}+\mathbf{S}+z\mathbf{I}_{p}\bigg)^{-1}\mathbf{b}~\to~0\quad\text{almost surely}.

(S2)

Here,

uniquely solves the following equation:

\displaystyle s_{n}(z)=\frac{c}{p}~\operatorname{tr}~{\bm{\Sigma}}\bigg(\frac{{\bm{\Sigma}}}{1+s_{n}(z)}+\mathbf{S}+z\mathbf{I}_{p}\bigg)^{-1}.

The almost sure convergence also holds after we take derivative.

\displaystyle m_{n}^{\prime}(z)-\frac{\rm{d}\bigg[\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Big(\frac{{\bm{\Sigma}}}{1+s_{n}(z)}+\mathbf{S}+z\mathbf{I}_{p}\Big)^{-1}\mathbf{b}\bigg]}{\rm{d}z}~\to~0\quad\text{almost surely}.

(S3)

Proof.

The first almost sure convergence has been proved in Theorem 1 of
Rubio & Mestre 2011
. Corollary 1.11 in
Widder 1938
showed that the convergence is indeed internally closed uniform convergence. Since the functions are all analytic, the second almost sure convergence directly follow.

\displaystyle=\frac{X_{\textrm{tg}}-r-\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\alpha\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}})}{\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\mu}}}=\frac{X_{\textrm{tg}}-r-\alpha(\varepsilon+{\bm{\mu}})^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}}{(\varepsilon+{\bm{\mu}})^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\varepsilon+{\bm{\mu}})}\,.

We would like to show the almost surely convergence of the terms that involve
\varepsilon
.
By normality of
\varepsilon
, we can see that
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\varepsilon]=\operatorname{tr}(({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)
.
We start with the first order terms.
Since
\varepsilon^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}
is normal distribution, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\varepsilon^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2n\epsilon^{2}}{\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}}\right)

By assumption,
\mathbf{Q}
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}
are well-conditioned with bounded eigenvalues, and
\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}\leq\infty
.
Then we can show that for some constant
C
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|\varepsilon^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\sum_{n}^{\infty}2\exp\left(-Cn\right)<\infty

Therefore, by Borel-Cantelli Lemma we have the following almost surely convergence,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}\xrightarrow{a.s}0,~~~~\varepsilon^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}\xrightarrow{a.s}0

Now we focus on the second order term of
\varepsilon
, we can rewrite it as a weighted sum of chi-squared distribution

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\varepsilon=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{z}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\mathbf{z}:=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{z}^{\top}\bm{B}\mathbf{z}\,,

where
z_{i}
follows the standard normal distribution and the eigenvalues of matrix
\bm{B}={\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}
are in constant order by assumption.
Then by Hanson-Wright Inequalities, we have for some constant

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{z}^{\top}\bm{B}\mathbf{z}-\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{z}^{\top}\bm{B}\mathbf{z}]|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-C\min\left(\frac{n^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{\|\bm{B}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{n\epsilon}{\|\bm{B}\|_{2}}\right)\right)\,,

where
\|\bm{B}\|_{F}^{2}\leq p\|\bm{B}\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|\bm{B}\|_{2}=O(1)

Recall that we assume

is a constant, so we can show that

\displaystyle\sum_{n}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{z}^{\top}\bm{B}\mathbf{z}-\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{z}^{\top}\bm{B}\mathbf{z}]|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq 2\sum_{n}^{\infty}\exp\left(-C\min\left(\frac{n^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{p\|\bm{B}\|_{2}^{2}},\frac{n\epsilon}{\|\bm{B}\|_{2}}\right)\right)<\infty\,.

Then by Borel-Cantelli Lemma, we can show the almost surely convergence,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\varepsilon\xrightarrow{a.s}\operatorname{tr}(({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)\,.

Therefore, the following convergence holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\omega}\xrightarrow{a.s}\frac{1}{2\omega^{\ast}}

\displaystyle=\frac{X_{\textrm{tg}}-r-\alpha{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}}{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}+\operatorname{tr}(({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)}\,.

Then, we can show that the Sharpe ratio of terminal wealth almost surely converges.
And its limiting value is

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(\mathbf{Q},\alpha\bar{\mathbf{w}})=\frac{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\frac{{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega^{\ast}}+\alpha\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}})}{\sqrt{(\frac{{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega^{\ast}}+\alpha\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}})^{T}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}(\frac{{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega^{\ast}}+\alpha\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}})+\frac{1}{(2\omega^{\ast})^{2}}\operatorname{tr}(({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}/n)}}\,.

By letting
\mathbf{A}={\bm{\Sigma}}+\mathbf{Q}
,
\mathbf{d}_{\alpha}=\frac{{\bm{\mu}}}{2\omega^{\ast}}+\alpha\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{w}}
,

\displaystyle\textrm{SR}_{\infty}(\mathbf{Q},\alpha\bar{\mathbf{w}})=\frac{{\bm{\mu}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{d}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\|\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{d}_{\alpha}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}+\frac{e_{\bm{\mu}}}{(2\omega^{\ast})^{2}}}}\,,

which completes the proof.
∎
