Executive Summary

This project developed and validated an integrated reduced-dimensional simulation
methodology for a modern heavy-duty diesel engine and its aftertreatment system. The
team built physics-based models for in-cylinder combustion, exhaust gas dynamics, DOC,
DPF, SCR, and ASC behavior. The team also developed a high-fidelity model for urea-water
solution (UWS) injection and decomposition. The team integrated the component models in
GT-SUITE through a quasi-steady circuit splitter and validated the system against engine
dynamometer experiments. The team then used the simulation tool to design an advanced
aftertreatment configuration that adds a close-coupled SCR to the OEM architecture. The
team demonstrated the configuration on an engine dynamometer with both degreened and
oven-aged catalysts.

Engine dynamometer testing at FEV with the model-designed close-coupled SCR (ccSCR)
configuration reduced NOx emissions by 30 to 67 percent against the baseline production
aftertreatment across the certification cycles (Cold and Hot FTP, RMC/SET, and the Low
Load Cycle). The aged ccSCR retained NOx reduction performance even with a slight
reduction in ammonia storage capacity. The integrated GT-SUITE platform predicted the
demonstrated performance accurately and is now ready as a design-stage tool for future
ultra-low NOx aftertreatment systems.

On the spray modeling side, the team built a Volume-of-Fluid to Lagrangian-Eulerian (VoFLE)
hybrid solver to interrogate the fidelity of conventional Lagrangian-Eulerian (LE) UWS spray
models. The VoFLE work resolves the internal nozzle dynamics and the primary breakup of
the UWS jet, then converts small unresolved structures into Lagrangian parcels for efficient
transport. The team validated the underlying Volume-of-Fluid (VoF) solver against the
experimental and DNS-class liquid jet in crossflow datasets of Sallam and Li-Soteriou. The
team then compared VoFLE against two LE configurations on the same port-fuel injector
geometry. The results show that conventional LE methods are qualitatively and
quantitatively inaccurate for UWS sprays. The primary error is not the breakup model. The
primary error is the inability of LE to resolve the internal nozzle flow and the resulting non-
axisymmetric jet directionality. A tuned Rosin Rammler injection improves droplet size
predictions but cannot recover the non-axisymmetric spray cloud. These findings inform the
next generation of reduced-order aftertreatment models, including the Gamma
Technologies neural-network-based SCR surrogate published in SAE Int. J. Engines.

The project achieved all milestones in Budget Periods 1, 2, and 3, including the Y3-3
milestone on a model-optimized engine map and aftertreatment configuration and the

1. Introduction

1.1 Project Background and Motivation
Tightening on-road NOx standards and durability requirements for heavy-duty vehicles drive
a need for fast, accurate simulation tools that can co-design the engine, calibration, and
aftertreatment system. Reduced-dimensional GT-SUITE models capture the system at the
level of detail needed for control design and aftertreatment sizing, but they need rigorous
calibration to engine and catalyst data, and they need physics-faithful submodels for
processes that matter for ultra-low NOx, particularly UWS injection and SCR aging.

A widely adopted approach for NOx reduction is the urea-water solution selective catalytic
reduction (UWS/SCR) system. The system injects UWS into the high-temperature exhaust.
The urea undergoes thermolysis and hydrolysis to produce ammonia. The ammonia then
reacts with NOx in the SCR catalyst to form nitrogen and water. The dominant reactions in
the SCR are the standard reaction with NO, the fast SCR reaction with an NO and NO2
mixture, and a slow reaction with NO2 alone. Project work targeted physics-based capture
of the full chain from injection to tailpipe.

CFD plays a central role in evaluating UWS spray behavior and ammonia distribution at the
SCR inlet. Direct numerical simulation of the whole exhaust over the seconds-long
timescales needed is computationally prohibitive. The community therefore uses
Lagrangian-Eulerian (LE) models, where the gas is treated as an Eulerian continuum and the
spray droplets are treated as Lagrangian parcels. The accuracy of LE depends on the
embedded breakup, evaporation, and wall-film submodels, which were largely calibrated
for fuel sprays. The project tested LE accuracy for UWS sprays against a high-fidelity VoFLE
solver developed in-house.

1.2 Project Goal and SMART Objectives
Project Goal: Develop and validate an integrated reduced-dimensional engine, emissions,
and aftertreatment system model. The model must support optimization of controls,
thermal management, and insulation strategies. The model must improve component
conversion efficiencies. The deliverable is an integrated engine and aftertreatment
simulation tool in GT-SUITE that supports near-zero emissions and Virtual Real Driving
Emissions (RDE) studies.

The project SMART objectives are:

3. Combustion and Engine Model

The engine modeling effort centered on the Isuzu 5.2L 4HK1 four-cylinder four-stroke
production diesel engine. The team developed a 0D Velocity-Composition-Frequency
transported PDF combustion model (0D-VCF-tPDF) in GT-SUITE and integrated it into both
single-cylinder and full multicylinder engine models. The model captures spray-air mixing
through an Euclidean Minimum Spanning Tree (EMST) of stochastic particles. Air particles
mix with neighboring particles in composition space. Spray particles only mix with air
particles, reflecting the dominance of air entrainment over axial diffusion among spray
packets.

Figure 1 shows a conceptual representation of the EMST mixing process. The publication "A
zero-dimensional velocity-composition-frequency probability density function model for
diesel engine simulation" by Paul, Jin, Fogla, Roggendorf, and Wahiduzzaman (SAE Int. J.
Adv. Curr. Pract. Mobil., 2020) documents the underlying methodology.

5. Catalyst Aging Models
Predicting performance over the full useful life (FUL) of a heavy-duty vehicle requires aging-
aware kinetic submodels. The team focused on the SCR catalyst because SCR conversion
drives tailpipe NOx for ultra-low NOx targets. Umicore supplied catalyst samples
representing five oven-aging conditions. ORNL ran synthetic gas bench experiments on all
five aging conditions across multiple operating temperatures.

The team ran temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) experiments at six operating
temperatures for each of the five aging conditions, for a total of 30 cases. The team then ran
SCR experiments at seven operating temperatures for each of the five aging conditions, for
a total of 35 cases. The SCR model uses a surface reaction template with 49 reactions, 7
base species, and 13 coverage species. The team optimized model parameters with the
genetic algorithm available in GT-SUITE. Site densities were optimized for each aging
condition independently to capture the loss of acid sites with aging.

The revised SCR model captures the dynamic trends in both SCR and TPD measurements

There remains scope to improve NH3 oxidation predictions, which the team will refine as
new data become available.

6. Urea-Water Solution Spray Modeling

UWS injection and decomposition control the ammonia distribution at the SCR inlet.
Inhomogeneous ammonia at the inlet reduces SCR conversion and increases ammonia slip.
The team executed a two-part UWS modeling effort. The first part used a conventional
Lagrangian-Eulerian (LE) framework in CONVERGE CFD to predict ammonia at the SCR inlet
across operating points. The second part developed and applied a high-fidelity VoFLE solver
to evaluate the LE framework on the underlying physics of UWS spray breakup.

6.1 ISUZU Exhaust Geometry and LE Modeling
The team modeled an Isuzu 4HK1 diesel engine exhaust with a 90 degree bend that imparts
swirl ahead of the mixing pipe. The swirl assists UWS spray breakup. Figure 8 shows the
geometry. The UWS injects at the bend. The ammonia produced by urea thermolysis and
hydrolysis, together with NOx in the exhaust gas, enters the SCR catalyst region highlighted
in the figure. The primary goal of this part of the study is to quantify the ammonia mass
fraction at the SCR inlet plane, both spatially and temporally, for the exhaust and injection
conditions of interest.

                                        Page 16 of 40

 Figure 8. Schematic of the ISUZU exhaust system. UWS is injected at the bend. The SCR
 catalyst region (highlighted) reduces NOx with ammonia to nitrogen and water.

The simulation uses CONVERGE CFD with the liquid UWS treated as Lagrangian parcels.
The injector is modeled as a 3-hole nozzle. The three nozzles sit symmetrically 120 degrees
apart. Each hole injects parcels at an angle that matches the actual injector geometry.
Figure 9 shows the injection pattern colored by parcel velocity magnitude. The droplets

injected urea per second and the injection rate are key tuning parameters and reflect
exhaust temperature and composition.

6.1.1 LE Models
Spray dynamics use the KH-RT primary breakup model. Over the seconds-long timescales
relevant to UWS, the breakup dynamics do not strongly affect the ammonia production.

                                         Page 17 of 40

Three phenomena dominate the ammonia generation: evaporation of UWS droplets, urea
deposition on exhaust walls, and decomposition of urea to ammonia.

The bicomponent droplet vaporization model used in CONVERGE follows Ebrahimian and
Habchi and accounts for species enthalpy diffusion and the resulting droplet temperatures.
The model has been validated for UWS droplets in SCR systems. The spray-wall interaction
model is the O’Rourke-Amsden model with four regimes: stick, rebound, spread, and
splash. Selection among regimes uses the impact energy E, the boiling temperature, and the
wall temperature. The impact energy uses the droplet Weber number, the liquid film
thickness, and the boundary layer thickness. Table 2 summarizes the transition criteria
implemented in the present work.

Water evaporates first from the UWS droplet. Urea remains and undergoes thermolysis to
ammonia and isocyanic acid (HNCO). Hydrolysis of HNCO produces additional ammonia
and CO2. In addition to gas-phase decomposition, the model captures condensed-phase
decomposition because reactive HNCO can produce solid urea in the wall film at suitable
temperatures and residence times. A detailed decomposition mechanism is implemented
in CONVERGE.

6.2 VoFLE Evaluation of the LE Solver
The LE framework treats the entire spray as Lagrangian parcels and relies on subgrid models
calibrated for fuel sprays. The project asked whether those models capture UWS sprays
accurately. The team built a hybrid VoF-to-LE (VoFLE) solver. VoFLE resolves the internal
nozzle flow and the primary breakup of the liquid jet using a direct numerical simulation
approach. Once liquid structures shrink below the local grid resolution, the solver converts
them to Lagrangian parcels through a nearest-neighbor approach. The Lagrangian parcels
then carry the spray through the larger domain efficiently. The method recovers the
dominant breakup physics at a fraction of the cost of pure DNS.

6.2.1 VoF Validation Against Liquid Jet in Crossflow
The team validated the underlying VoF solver, interFoam, against the canonical liquid jet in
crossflow (LJIC) configuration. The validation case mirrors Li and Soteriou, who used a

direction into cross-flowing air. The simulation box is 30 mm by 30 mm by 30 mm with the
jet exiting an orifice of diameter d = 4 mm. The team used three structured grids at
resolutions 0.5d, 0.25d, and 0.125d with refinement around the liquid core trajectory. Table

The macroscopic UWS spray characteristics, including flow homogeneity, droplet size, and
ammonia distribution, develop from near-field physics. If an LE method captures UWS
behavior at all, it must do so in the near field. The simulation domain is therefore a 30 mm
by 30 mm by 30 mm box that penetrates one third of the exhaust manifold diameter and

                                           Page 24 of 40

represents the near field. For VoFLE simulations, the PFI sits on top of the +y face. UWS inlet
conditions apply at the injector head. Exhaust inlet conditions apply at the -x face and move
in the +x direction.

VoFLE calculations ran for 8 ms with a CFL-based time step. Six static refinement zones
placed resolution at the locations of primary breakup. The minimum cell size is 6
micrometers and the total cell count is approximately 65 million. A grid resolution study
confirms convergence. Once a droplet falls below the resolved size, the solver triggers the
nearest-neighbor approach and converts the droplet to a Lagrangian parcel.

The LE simulations ran for 8 ms with the same time-step criterion. The LE grid totals 1.5
million cells with a uniform cell size of 0.15 mm. The Eulerian field uses RANS k-epsilon
turbulence with a turbulent kinetic energy intensity of 0.02 and a dissipative length scale of
0.003 m. The Lagrangian phase uses the TAB drag force model, the no-time collision (NTC)
model, and the post-outcome collision model. Each nozzle injects 80,000 parcels at the
same location as the VoFLE orifices with a spray cone angle of 20 degrees and a coefficient
of discharge of 0.585. The cone angle and coefficient of discharge come from the VoFLE
results.

The team evaluated two LE parcel injection methods. The first is the blob model, which
injects droplets at the nozzle diameter and leans on the breakup model for primary
atomization. The team paired the blob model with the conventional KHRT breakup model,
so this case is reported as "KHRT injection." The second is the Rosin Rammler model, which
prescribes a droplet size distribution at injection and assumes that primary breakup has
already occurred. The Rosin Rammler model needs accurate input data. The team tuned its
two coefficients by minimizing the L2 norm between the Rosin Rammler cumulative density
function and the VoFLE cumulative density function. The optimization yields a Rosin
Rammler coefficient of 2.93 and a Sauter mean radius of 38.4 micrometers. Figure 15 shows
a representative VoFLE near-field result that informs the tuned Rosin Rammler input
distribution.

6.2.3 Qualitative Comparisons: VoFLE versus LE
Internal nozzle dynamics drive the breakup of UWS sprays. Figure 16 shows the PFI internal
dynamics on several planes through the injector body. Plane A runs through the x-y cross
section. Plane B runs through the midsection of disk 2. Planes C, D, and E run through the
beginning, middle, and end of an orifice in disk 3. The PFI geometry funnels liquid through
the thin ring of disk 1 and into the crossflow and vorticity zone of disk 2. The geometry creates
counter-rotating regions visualized by the streamlines.

  Figure 16. Internal dynamics visualization at t = 3.5 ms in the VoFLE results. (a) Slicing

Inside the orifice, the liquid separates from the wall and forms a region of recirculating gas.
The injection area shrinks. Two counter-rotating vortical tubes grow within the nozzle. The
vortices inject the liquid in two high-velocity directions: one away from the PFI centerline
and one toward it. Figure 17 shows the consequences in the orifice. The two streams emerge
clearly in the velocity field.

Figure 18 compares the VoFLE flow visualization against the KHRT and Rosin Rammler LE
simulations. The VoFLE result shows two primary streams emerging from each nozzle. One
stream propagates away from the PFI and breaks up in a Rayleigh-Plateau manner. The other
stream propagates toward the centerline and coalesces with the streams from the other
nozzles. A thin liquid sheet between the two streams stretches laterally, then pops once
surface tension can no longer hold it. The sheet fragments into elongated ligaments and

                                         Page 27 of 40

small droplets. This is a non-aerodynamic primary breakup. Surface tension and lateral
momentum drive the breakup, not aerodynamic shear. The internal dynamics of the PFI
generate a non-axisymmetric spray cloud.

The Rosin Rammler and KHRT LE simulations reproduce the imposed injection angle. They
cannot reproduce the non-axisymmetric jet directionality because they do not resolve the
internal nozzle flow. KHRT overpredicts droplet size and underpredicts crossflow
entrainment. Rosin Rammler captures the droplet size distribution because the tuned
coefficients impose it.

6.2.4 Quantitative Comparisons: Droplet Distributions and Velocities
Statistically stationary behavior emerged after t = 5 ms in all simulations. Droplets larger
than the nozzle diameter (0.4 mm) were omitted and treated as large liquid structures. The
team then captured droplet size and velocity statistics over the quasi-steady window.

The number-weighted droplet diameter probability density function for VoFLE shows a log-
normal distribution that favors small droplets, consistent with the resolved primary
breakup. The Rosin Rammler LE reproduces the log-normal behavior with a shift toward
larger droplets. The shift comes from the optimized-coefficient equation, which begins to
deviate from the VoFLE distribution around 50 micrometers. The KHRT LE shows no
distribution. The result is a single impulse, which confirms that the KHRT breakup model
does not fire. The aerodynamic mechanism it relies on is absent in UWS sprays. The result
agrees with the wider literature on UWS spray modeling. The volume-weighted distribution
follows the same pattern with the VoFLE result showing a complex non-Gaussian profile, the
Rosin Rammler result showing a Gaussian profile, and the KHRT result showing a single
peak.

The Rosin Rammler diameters overpredict VoFLE with approximately 25 percent relative
error. The KHRT diameters overpredict by as much as 200 percent. A scatter of the gaseous
Weber number versus the liquid Reynolds number on a droplet basis confirms that the
gaseous Weber number sits well below the critical value of 12 required for aerodynamic
breakup. The KHRT breakup model cannot fire for this droplet population. The VoFLE and
Rosin Rammler droplets also fall below the critical Weber number, so no secondary breakup
occurs after the resolved primary breakup. The result is consistent with the literature on
UWS sprays.

The volume-weighted probability density functions of the three velocity components reveal
further LE limitations. The VoFLE x-velocity peaks at a positive value that reflects the
crossflow entrainment. Two local peaks on either side of the central peak reflect the high-
velocity striations identified in the internal nozzle flow. The y-velocity has the same dual-
peak character around zero. The z-velocity peaks near 20 m/s, which corresponds to the

                                        Page 29 of 40

imposed jet velocity weighted by droplet volume. The Rosin Rammler LE captures the peak
locations of x- and y-velocity but cannot reproduce the non-normal behavior caused by the
internal dynamics. The KHRT LE results follow a normal distribution. KHRT predicts a zero x-
velocity peak because the large droplets do not entrain in the crossflow. The Rosin Rammler
z-velocity has the right qualitative shape but shifts toward larger values because of the
overprediction of large droplets.

6.2.5 Quantitative Comparisons: Spatial Liquid Mass Distribution
SCR performance depends on where the liquid mass ends up. The team computed
temporally averaged liquid fraction flux at the domain outlet for each solver. The team
binned parcel data on the x-z outlet plane onto a 50 by 50 grid during the quasi-steady
period. The team then divided the time-summed parcel volume in each bin by the bin volume
and the number of time steps to yield a temporally averaged liquid flux. A value of unity
represents a constant flow of liquid through the bin. A value of zero represents no liquid flow.

The VoFLE contour reproduces the non-axisymmetric spray cloud. The striations away from
the PFI appear as local regions of high liquid flux. Smaller droplets entrain in the crossflow
and bow downstream. Most of the spray mass concentrates near the PFI centerline. The
Rosin Rammler contour shows a circular cloud and cannot capture the non-axisymmetry. It
does reproduce the high-flux core region and partial entrainment of small droplets. The
KHRT contour is uniform and circular. It misses the non-axisymmetry, the core
consolidation, and the entrainment. These differences are visible in the flow visualization
comparison of Figure 18 above.

6.3 Findings on LE Adequacy for UWS Sprays
Two findings stand out. First, the conventional LE method with the blob injection plus the
KHRT breakup model is qualitatively and quantitatively inadequate for UWS sprays under
typical exhaust conditions. The model cannot resolve the internal nozzle flow and cannot
reproduce the non-axisymmetric spray cloud. The breakup model is rarely the bottleneck
because the aerodynamic forces required by KHRT are not present in UWS sprays. Second,
a tuned Rosin Rammler injection improves droplet size and velocity statistics significantly,
but it still fails to capture the spatial mass distribution because it cannot reproduce the
directional structure of the jet. The directionality stems from the internal nozzle dynamics.
LE methods cannot recover that directionality without an embedded resolved-flow
surrogate at the nozzle exit.

                                         Page 30 of 40

Despite these near-field inaccuracies, the LE approach can still produce a reasonable
ammonia mass fraction prediction at the SCR face, as Section 6.1 shows. The exhaust
geometry mixes the ammonia downstream and damps near-field errors. The team
recommends a future sensitivity analysis to identify which fluid dynamic factors most
influence the uniformity index and the ammonia distribution at the SCR inlet. The team also
recommends higher-fidelity experimental data beyond time-averaged measurements to
support further validation.

7. Engine, Emissions, and Aftertreatment Integrated Model

GT integrated the engine model and the aftertreatment system model in GT-SUITE through a
quasi-steady circuit splitter (QSCS). The QSCS connects the engine circuit and the
aftertreatment circuit and lets both circuits run at different time steps while still
communicating. The engine circuit uses an explicit solver with a 1 degree crank-angle step.
The aftertreatment circuit uses a quasi-steady solver with a 1 second time step.

The team optimized the engine model for all 26 steady-state operating points before
integration. The team also optimized the standalone aftertreatment model for all cases. The
QSCS-based integration produced consistent results between the standalone and
integrated solvers. The integrated model incurred increased computation time at first.
Additional platform improvements reduced runtime to a usable level. The team also
updated the model to include the new ccSCR formulation provided by Umicore. The new
SCR catalyst trained on the ORNL synthetic gas bench results.

8. Near-Zero Emissions Aftertreatment Design and Demonstration

The team used the integrated model to design an advanced aftertreatment configuration
that meets ultra-low NOx targets. The configuration adds a close-coupled SCR (ccSCR)
upstream of the production aftertreatment train. Umicore recommended the maximum
washcoat (WC) thickness for the ccSCR based on three washcoat loadings. The team
optimized the ccSCR length in GT-SUITE for each washcoat thickness to balance light-off
performance and pressure drop.

FEV ran the demonstration on the Isuzu 5.2L 4HK1 engine dynamometer with the model-
designed ccSCR-equipped aftertreatment system. The demonstration tested both
degreened and oven-aged catalyst samples. The advanced system achieved NOx

                                     Page 32 of 40

reductions of 30 to 67 percent against the baseline production aftertreatment across the
heavy-duty certification cycles, including Cold and Hot FTP, the Ramped Modal Cycle /
Supplemental Emissions Test (RMC/SET), and the Low Load Cycle (LLC). The aged ccSCR
retained NOx reduction performance with only a slight reduction in ammonia storage
capacity. The demonstration met or exceeded the model prediction. The simulation tool is
therefore ready as a design-stage instrument for future NZE aftertreatment work.

10. Conclusions and Recommendations

The project produced a validated, integrated reduced-dimensional simulation tool for the
engine, combustion, emissions, and aftertreatment of a modern heavy-duty diesel engine.
The tool is now ready for use in design and calibration of future ultra-low NOx aftertreatment
systems. The team demonstrated the tool by designing a close-coupled SCR add-on
configuration that reduced certification-cycle NOx by 30 to 67 percent on the engine
dynamometer, in both degreened and oven-aged conditions.

The team also delivered a state-of-the-art VoFLE solver and used it to perform an
unprecedented evaluation of conventional LE methods for UWS spray modeling. The
primary finding is that LE accuracy in UWS sprays does not hinge on the breakup model. The
primary failure mode is the inability of LE to capture the internal nozzle flow and the resulting
jet directionality. A tuned Rosin Rammler injection narrows the gap on droplet statistics but
still misses the non-axisymmetric spray cloud. The findings inform reduced-order surrogate
models, including the neural-network SCR surrogate published with Gamma Technologies.

10.1 Recommendations
 • Use the integrated GT-SUITE tool to size and calibrate ccSCR add-ons and dual-
 dosing strategies for future low-NOx programs.
 • Pair LE UWS simulations with a nozzle-resolved sub-model that captures the
 directional jet structure. A trained surrogate of the VoFLE near field would close
 most of the LE gap at low computational cost.
 • Extend the SCR aging model with additional aging conditions and ammonia
 oxidation refinements as new data become available.
 • Run a sensitivity analysis to identify the dominant fluid dynamic factors that drive
 the SCR-inlet uniformity index.
 • Acquire higher-fidelity, spatially resolved UWS spray and ammonia measurements
 to support further VoFLE validation.
 • Carry the fuels matrix at UM to completion and merge results with the CRC follow-
 on biodiesel matrix for a unified fuels-effects dataset.

11. Benefits Assessment and Technology Transfer

The validated integrated simulation tool enables design and calibration of future
aftertreatment systems with less reliance on costly engine dynamometer iteration. The tool
supports virtual RDE evaluation, which helps fleets prepare for EPA and CARB heavy-duty
NOx and greenhouse gas standards. The advanced ccSCR architecture demonstrated in this
project is directly applicable to medium- and heavy-duty diesel platforms.

The VoFLE solver provides a research-grade tool for evaluating reduced-order UWS spray
models in any exhaust geometry. The neural-network-based ammonia distribution
surrogate published in SAE Int. J. Engines extends the value of the tool to system-level
designers who need fast SCR inlet predictions.

Marathon Petroleum and Isuzu supplied cost share. Umicore supplied in-kind catalyst
samples. CRC awarded follow-on funding to UM to extend the fuels matrix. The project
therefore seeded a continued collaboration that builds on the DOE investment.
