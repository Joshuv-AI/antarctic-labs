1. Abstract

Glycosyltransferases (GTs) catalyze the formation of glycosidic linkages to produce almost all
complex carbohydrates. This project used a multi-disciplinary, high-throughput (HTP) biochemical
and computational biology approach focused on duckweed as a model energy crop, to study
carbohydrate metabolic processes. To achieve this, developed and carried out out high-
throughput (HTP) functional characterization of plant glycosyltransferases (GTs) role of enzymatic
microenvironments be assessed through a combined proteomic and computational biology
approach, and the combined data was used to populate deep-learning frameworks to predict plant
GT function. Functional validation achieved through this research is being used to assign gene
function and study plant processes at the systems level to efficiently link the genome sequence
with gene function. Together, the combined approaches used within this study provide a
foundation for how computational prediction, in combination with high-throughput functional
validation, can be used to study plant processes at the systems level and translate knowledge
gained to efficiently link genome sequence with gene function in a species agnostic manner.

2. Brief technical description of most relevant projects/publications.
Throughout the three-year duration of this award, we developed and applied integrated analytical
and computational approaches to characterize glycosyltransferases, with a focus on the diverse
GT47 family, and elucidate their roles in synthesizing structural plant polysaccharides in
Lemnaceae (duckweeds), a simplified monocot model and bioenergy feedstock. Complex
carbohydrates are essential molecules of life that are responsible for energy supply in all species.
A substantial portion of the carbohydrate formed by photosynthesis is used to produce the
polysaccharide-rich walls that surround plant cells, which are distinguished by their composition,
architecture, and functions. The polysaccharides that make up the majority of plant biomass are
constructed of sugars that are connected by specific glycosidic linkages. These well-defined
structures are not encoded by sequence-based templates but are synthesized solely through the
concerted actions of enzymes. Across the tree of life, the formation of the glycosidic bonds that
link monosaccharides together to form more complex structures is carried out by large families of
glycosyltransferases (GTs) that transfer sugars from activated donors to a wide range of acceptor
molecules. Classification of carbohydrate-active enzymes, such as GTs, into sequence-based
families has been carried out via the combined efforts of global scientists and compiled into the
CAZy database (http://www.cazy.org). As structural information became available, GTs were also
classified based on the structure of their catalytic domains; however, the fold, mechanism, and
catalytic function and/or specificity of GTs are not predictive of each other to date. This project
specifically focused on the GT47 family as a model, as members of this family display diverse
substrate selectivities for both donor and acceptor molecules and they are involved in the
synthesis of most classes of cell wall glycopolymers in all plant species (Tehrani et al., 2024;
Zhang et al., 2023).
The overarching goal of this project was to develop and apply analytical methodology and
computational techniques to elucidate the structure and function of carbohydrate synthetic
machinery involved in the generation of structural plant polysaccharides from the GT47 gene

family in Lemnaceae, or duckweeds, as a model bioenergy feedstock. Like the plant model
Arabidopsis, the DOE model duckweed Spirodela polyrhiza has a compact (~158 Mb), low
redundancy, genome with roughly 19,000 annotated genes making it a simplified monocot model
for functional studies. In this project, our study of the duckweed GT47 family combined
approaches to assign specific activities for all GT47 enzymes as well as elucidate their mode(s)
of action, including substrate recognition and donor preferences. Sequence, structural, and
experimental activity data were then used to generate and validate predictors for protein substrate
specificity (Hennen et al., 2025; Fang et al.,2026). The varied functions of the GT47 family fit the
idea that GTs that adopt a GT-B fold form accommodate a wider range of acceptor substrates,
which makes them difficult to analyze with bioinformatics and computational methods. Thus, a
key result from this foundational study confirmed that computational prediction, including AL/ML,
in combination with high-throughput functional validation, can be used to study plant processes
at the systems level and translate knowledge gained to efficiently link genome sequence with
gene function in a species agnostic manner (Fang et al.,2026). To achieve our overarching goals,
the project was carried out through the completion of three interrelated aims:
Aim 1 focused on carrying out high-throughput (HTP) functional characterization of plant
glycosyltransferases (GTs) encoded in the genomes of duckweeds. We achieved this
through the construction of a library of GT47 expression constructs, with gene synthesis support
from the Joint Genome Institute through an awarded FICUS grant. Recombinant enzymes were
heterologously expressed and used in biochemical analyses of substrate selectivity and
characterization of protein-substrate interactions and specificity.
Over the 3‑year performance period, we developed and applied a high‑throughput screening
workflow tailored for plant GT characterization to assay diverse GTs for nucleotide‑sugar donor
and saccharide‑acceptor specificities.Genes encoding GT47 enzymes are present across diverse
taxa, including mammals, insects, plants, and bacteria, but plant and microbial GT47s remain
poorly characterized. This gap limits our understanding of GT47 evolutionary history and
constrains efforts to predict GT47 activities, and glycoenzymes more broadly, from sequence data
or to exploit them for biotechnological applications. Systematic biochemical, structural, and
genomic investigations are therefore needed to reveal their roles and broaden the functional
landscape of glycoenzymes. Most plant genomes contain at least 30 diverse GT coding
sequences from the GT47 family (Zhang et al., 2023). We developed our construct library based
on gene sequences from the Phytozome 13 NCBI databases. In total, we designed 66 constructs
from four genera: Spirodela, Lemna, Wolffia, and Wolffiella. This included 31 sequences obtained
from the genome of Spirodela polyrhiza. Protein expression constructs were conservatively
designed to include the majority of each coding sequence while removing the transmembrane
domain (TMD), enabling soluble expression as N‑terminal 6×His‑GFP fusion proteins in
eukaryotic 293F cells. The recombinant proteins were produced by transient transfection of 293F
suspension cultures, and expression was monitored by GFP fluorescence. Fusion proteins were
affinity‑purified via Ni‑NTA chromatography.

The screening of diverse GTs for nucleotide sugar donor and saccharide acceptor specificities
was accomplished through the development of a high-throughput screening workflow tailored for
plant GT characterization. The UDP-Glo™ Glycosyltransferase Assay was leveraged for the

development of the workflow due to its high-throughput nature in the rapid quantification of GT
activity through the detection of UDP, which is a byproduct released after a GT successfully
transfers a sugar from its UDP-bound form to an acceptor substrate (Figure 1). Given that plant
GTs generally demonstrate little to no hydrolytic activity in the absence of acceptor, we tailored
our workflow to screen for transferase activity. This was accomplished through the use of
universal nucleotide sugar donor mixture we developed to act as universal donor substrate to
screening GTs against a library of saccharide acceptors. Once transferase activity is observed
for one or multiple acceptor(s), those same acceptors can be applied to a subsequent screen for
determining donor preference with a library of individual nucleotide sugars (composing the original
mixture). This two-pronged approach allows for the rapid identification of donor-acceptor pairs for
active GTs (Figure 1).

Figure 1: Schematic of HTP Glycosyltransferase workflow for functional analysis of plant
GTs. (A) Schematic of the UDP-Glo™ Glycosyltransferase Assay in the quantification of UDP
after a GT has successfully transferred a nucleotide sugar donor to a saccharide acceptor. (B)
Schematic of developed workflow used for GT activity screening. The UDP-Glo™
Glycosyltransferase Assay was used to screen GTs against a library of plant-specific saccharide
acceptors while using a nucleotide sugar donor mixture to act as an all-encompassing donor
substrate. Top acceptor candidates are selected based on transferase activity before being used
to screen GTs against a library of individual nucleotide sugar donors. Initial screening concludes
with the identification of potential donor-acceptor substrate candidates for expressed GTs. We
used this approach to systematically characterize the family.

GT47 comprises enzymes that synthesize complex carbohydrates, especially in plant cell walls,
with a substantial diversity of donor and acceptor substrates observed within a single family
(Figure 2A). We commenced biochemical characterization of the GT47 family in Spirodela
polyrhiza starting with members of Clade A, which includes homologs to Arabidopsis MURUS3
(MUR3) and XYLOGLUCAN L-SIDE CHAIN GALACTOSYLTRANSFERASE POSITION 2
(XLT2). Biochemical characterization of their duckweed counterparts showed that they are regio-
specific galactosyltransferases involved in xyloglucan (XyG) sidechain biosynthesis (Figure 2B;
Corulli et al., 2026). Functional characterization of these enzymes established novel findings,
such as their capacity to function as both galactosyltransferases and
arabinopyranosyltransferases in vitro. We showd that SpMUR3 and SpXLT2 catalyze the addition
of galactose onto xyloglucan to create the typical “L” (Gal)-β1,2-Xyl-α1,6- disaccharide sidechain
observed on the polysaccharide. Further, we show that SpXLT2 modifies the second (XXXG) and
SpMUR3 predominantly modifies the third (XXXG) xylose of a XXXG motif. In addition, both
enzymes exhibited arabinopyranosyltransferase activity with distinct xyloglucan reducing-end
specificity. This work was recently published in the Plant Journal (Corulli et al. 2026).

GT47s. (A) Phylogenetic tree of GT47 family members from Arabidopsis thaliana and Spirodela
polyrhiza with highlighted subclades. (B) GT47s from clade A (SpMUR3 and SpXLT2) which were

(XyG) β-D-Gal-(1,2)-α-D-Xyl sidechains. (C) GT47s from clade C (Sp415-C, Sp124-C, SI728-C)
and clade B (SpGalTi) involved in pectin biosynthesis. Sp415-C, Sp124-C, and SI728-C were

clade F (Sp132-F), clade E (Sp124-E), and clade D (Sp291-D) with determined nucleotide sugar
donor specificities. Sp132-F was found to have specificity for UDP-GalA while Sp124-E and
Sp291-D were both found to have specificity for UDP-Xyl.

Rhamnogalacturonan-I (RG-I) is a complex, "hairy" domain of pectin, while homogalacturonan
(HG) is the most abundant structural domain of pectin, both of which are crucial structural
polysaccharides in plant cell walls. Considering this, a complete understanding of RG-I and HG
biosynthesis and its physiological role, and of the significant impacts on plant growth,
development and valorization, given that it is one of the key polymers involved in cell-to-cell
connections. In clade B, our screen led to the identification and functional characterization of
enzymes involved in initiation of pectic galactan initiation from S. polyrhiza, which we named GalT
GalTi. Detailed analysis of pectic reaction products confirmed that GalTi catalyzes the transfer of
a single β-D-galactose to O-4 of backbone rhamnose residues of RG-I, forming the foundation for
galactan synthesis (Figure 1C). Phylogenetic analysis categorized this enzyme as a member of
subclade B of the plant GT47 family, and we showed that two orthologs from A. thaliana possess
identical functions, showcasing the potential conservation of this initiation pathway in both
monocots and dicots. Together, these findings underscore the complexity of RG-I biosynthesis
and highlighted the need for continued identification and characterization of the GTs and other
modifying enzymes involved in its assembly. Continued efforts to elucidate the biosynthetic
machinery and structure–function relationships of RG-I will be essential for understanding how
plants built and remodeled their walls to support growth, development, and environmental
adaptations. establishing it as the first characterized GT to directly glycosylate unsubstituted RG-
I. Finally, functional analysis of members from Clade C from S. polyrhiza and S. intermedia
identified enzymes involved in initiating the xylose-to-HG backbone (Figure 1C). Manuscripts
describing the deteiled biochemical analyses are in progress and will be submitted for publication
in the first half of 2026.

In addition to the functional identification of GT47s from diverse clades, we have also identified
nucleotide sugar donor specificities for three other GT47s from clades F, E, and D specific to
UDP-GalA and UDP-Xyl respectively (Figure 1D). The nucleotide sugar donor and saccharide
acceptor information obtained from completion of Aim 1 was used in the development of the and
validation of the Glydentify deep learning platform for glycosyltransferase donor substrate
prediction (Fang et al. 2026, bioRxiv, pp.2026-03).

Aim 2 investigated the enzymatic microenvironments involved in the synthesis of non-
template-based polymers. This high-risk/high-reward study used a combination of proximity
labelling and structural information to investigate the mechanisms by which protein–protein
interactions are involved in the organization of Golgi-localized GTs involved in glycopolymer
synthesis.

Among the major accomplishments achieved during the three-year project period was the
successful establishment of a robust and reproducible transformation pipeline for two critical
duckweed species, Lemna minor and Spirodela polyrhiza. (Figure 3). We systematically

developed and optimized protocols for efficient callus induction, sustained propagation, and
Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, culminating in the successful regeneration of transgenic
plants from both species. The tools and protocols developed under this grant constitute a
significant and lasting resource for the duckweed research community.

We established a system to grow large-scale cultures (>1L) of Spirodela polyrhiza, Lemna
aequinoctiales, Lemna minor and Wolffiella lingulata. The system was successfully scaled up and
enough plant material was harvested to prepare plant cell walls. Sequential fractionation of
Spirodela polyrhiza cell wall polysaccharides was performed to prepare bio-similar acceptor
substrates for GT47 enzymes. Another goal was to obtain 13C-labelled duckweed cell wall to
develop an atomic-level model of duckweed cell wall architecture, and the cell wall material will
be prepared for solid-state NMR to be sent to EMSL. Our study of the GT47 family of proteins
that begins with S. polyrhiza as a model system provides a unique opportunity to study the
architecture of the cell wall using solid-state NMR (ssNMR), which is an essential tool for studying
the three-dimensional structure of plant cell walls at the atomic level. Unlike other techniques that
require the extraction of polymers from the cell wall, ssNMR can be used to determine the
chemical structure, intermolecular interactions, and mobility of polysaccharides in muro. The small
size of this aquatic plant allowed us to generate large amounts of uniformly labelled 13C biomass
in a short time by cultivating S. polyrhiza in low light conditions in Schenk & Hildebrandt (SH)
liquid plant tissue culture medium containing 13C-labelled glucose as the sole source of carbon.
We achieved ~95% 13C-labeled S. polyrhiza biomass which was sent to EMSL for 2D ssNMR
(EMSL) experiments. We worked with EMSL scientists to design and perform quantitative direct
polarization (DP) experiments to determine wall composition and identify changes between the
wild-type and the mutants.

Apiogalacturonan is highly abundant in aquatic monocots, including duckweeds. Apiose is a
branched-chain aldopentose that is primarily found in plants, and its NDP-sugar analog is not

commercially available because it is unstable during storage. We have cloned S. polyrhiza and
Arabidopsis UDP-apiose (UDP-Api) synthase genes to generate protein expression constructs
for comparative analysis of the enzymes to select the best candidate for synthesis of this
uncommon NDP-sugar to expand the substrate library. Expression and purification of led to
successful expression of several of the expression constructs, but all inefficiently generated
UDP-Api and also led to hydrolysis of the nucleotide. The S. polyrhiza UDP-apiose synthase
(UAS) expression product was the most promising, but UDP-Api synthesis studies indicated
that the enzyme had limited stability during nucleotide sugar synthesis. To increase thermal
stability of SpUDP-apiose (UDP-Api) synthase, we employed a protein engineering approach
using the PROSS2 computational algorithm to identify a progressive series of potentially
stabilizing mutations for testing. Three different stabilizing mutation combinations were
generated by gene synthesis, expressed, and purified. Further studies are underway to identify
mutants that further stabilize the enzyme and improve sugar donor synthesis. Duckweed UDP-
Xylose synthase (SpUXS), which catalyze decarboxylation of UDP- D-glucuronic acid to UDP-
xylose, is more stable than human UXS. Manuscripts describing SpUXS and SpUAS are in
progress and will be submitted for publication.

Aim 3 focused on the development of deep-learning frameworks to classify, identify, and
predict structural features of plant GTs affecting specificity and interactions utilizing
dedicated biochemical experimental datasets (Aims 1&2). This was achieved by coupling
novel HTP computational structural prediction and in silico docking efforts to develop robust,
reliable and interpretable machine learning (ML) models that will enhance our capabilities for
functional prediction of carbohydrate active enzymes.

Over the 3-year performance period of this project, we made significant strides in developing and
applying machine-learning approaches to understand and predict substrate-specificity of GTs.
We addressed a major stumbling block in this research area, which is the availability of training
sets for ML models, by curating experimental functional data for GT-Bs. We curated a set of 513
unique GT-B sequences from over 145 species and subspecies gathered from UniProt and
validated as GT-Bs by the Carbohydrate Active Enzyme Database. UniProt catalytic activity
information was mined to determine donor substrate specificity, with proteins utilizing a donor
substrate known to participate in plant carbohydrate biosynthesis selected for the dataset. Seven
unique donor substrates GDP-ɑ-D-mannose, GDP-L-β-fucose, UDP-ɑ-D-galactose, UDP-ɑ-D-
glucose, UDP-ɑ-D-glucuronic acid, UDP-ɑ-D-xylose, and UDP-β-L-rhamnose were considered
for this dataset. We then developed an ML computational workflow that is informed by this
enzyme-specific experimental data and enzyme 3-D structure, that is further augmented by
physics-based molecular simulations data (structure, docking, dynamics) in an effort to classify,
identify, and predict structural features of GT-Bs impacting specificity. The ability of GT-Bs to
catalyze activity with multiple donors required the construction of multi-label machine learning
models. We evaluated four such classifier models - Support Vector Classifier, K-Nearest
Neighbors, Gaussian Naı v̈ e-Bayes, Random Forest - that incorporate both sequence and
structural features of GT-Bs for the prediction of GT-B donor specificity based on sequence and
structure. These models achieve good predictive accuracies on test datasets with the KNN model

proving to be most accurate. Clustering of KNN predictions enables us to discover unique
structural motifs within GT-B families. We then analyzed the most important residue features that
contribute to the KNN model’s decision-making to identify structural motifs that might be critical
for substrate-specificity in GT-Bs. We also extended the model to predict the substrate-
specificities of uncharacterized GT-B sequences from 4 distinct plant species to explore the ability
of our model in predicting their distinct carbohydrate profiles. However, despite our use of
structural information, we highlight that there is further scope for improvement in training these
models to draw interpretable relationships between sequence, structure and substrate specificity
determining motifs in GT-Bs. Our study highlighted the limitations of current ML models and
discussed opportunities to make ML models more interpretable for the discovery of substrate-
specificity determining features in enzymes (Hennen et al., 2025). The code and the datasets
have been made public as part of our publication and are available at the following link:
https://github.com/vbharadwcomosci/GTB_substrate_prediction

The donor and acceptor specificities generated during completion of Aim 1 informed the
development and validation of Glydentify, a deep learning framework for predicting
glycosyltransferase donor substrates using structure-aware protein language models and
molecular representations (Fang et al., 2026, bioRxiv). Trained on large‑scale UniProt
annotations and integrating protein embeddings with molecular encodings, Glydentify attains
PR‑AUCs of 0.86 (GT‑A) and 0.91 (GT‑B), outperforming general enzyme–substrate predictors
with minimal manual curation. Experimental assays validated six of seven predicted S. polyrhiza
GT47 specificities determined in our screen. Model interpretation via residue attention scores
indicates reliance on evolutionary, structural, and biochemical features, and the inclusion of
SaProt structural embeddings with UniMol sugar geometries substantially improved donor
classification accuracy, highlighting the value of structural priors for discriminating sugar donors.
he code and the datasets have been made public as part of our publication and are available at
the following link: https://github.com/RuiliF/Glydentify
