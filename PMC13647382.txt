Corrosion Behavior of Ti–Nb–Zr–Si
Alloy after One-Year Exposure to Simulated Inflammatory Conditions

Aydin Bordbar-Khiabani, Michael Gasik

Abstract

Long-term corrosion
and elemental release are critical factors
controlling the clinical reliability of titanium-based implants, particularly
under inflammatory conditions. In this study, commercially pure titanium
(CP-Ti), Ti–6Al–4V, and Ti–Nb–Zr–Si
(TNZS) alloy were immersed for 372 days under static, non-refreshed
conditions in media initially prepared to represent normal, inflammatory,
and severe inflammatory environments. After exposure, the specimens
were removed, rinsed, and evaluated by open-circuit potential, potentiodynamic
polarization, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy in freshly
prepared solutions of the corresponding nominal compositions. The
original immersion solutions were analyzed by inductively coupled
plasma optical emission spectrometry to determine released elements.
TNZS maintained the most noble electrochemical behavior, lowest corrosion
current density, and highest passive-layer resistance in all media.
Elemental release increased with solution aggressiveness, following
the order normal < inflammatory < severe inflammatory. Ti–6Al–4V
released Al and V, whereas TNZS released lower total ion concentrations
and avoided potentially harmful alloying elements. Under the tested
static, non-refreshed conditions, TNZS showed lower apparent corrosion
current density, higher fitted passive-layer resistance, and lower
filtered dissolved elemental release than CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V.

Introduction

Titanium and its alloys
remain central materials for dental and
orthopedic implants because they combine high specific strength, good
fatigue resistance, and spontaneous passivation in physiological fluids.
,
 Their apparent bioinertness, however, should not be interpreted
as complete electrochemical inactivity.
,
 Once implanted,
metallic biomaterials are exposed to a dynamic biological environment
in which proteins, inorganic ions, inflammatory cells, reactive oxygen
species, and local pH variations continuously interact with the surface. Under such conditions, the passive oxide film
may undergo repeated cycles of dissolution, repassivation, hydration,
and chemical modification.
,
 These processes are
especially important during long-term implantation, where even low
corrosion rates can lead to measurable accumulation of released ions
and corrosion products at the tissue–implant interface.

Conventional CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V have been widely
used in clinical applications, but each material has limitations under
aggressive biological conditions. Both
rely mainly on a TiO2-based passive film, which may be
altered by inflammatory oxidants such as H2O2 and acidic environments.
−

 Ti–6Al–4V provides
higher mechanical strength, but the possible release of Al and V remains
a concern for long-term applications, particularly when local inflammation,
infection, or mechanically assisted corrosion accelerates degradation.
,
 These concerns have encouraged the development of Al- and V-free
β-type titanium alloys containing more biocompatible alloying
elements such as Nb and Zr. In this context, Ti–Nb–Zr-based
alloys are attractive because Nb and Zr can improve passive-film stability
while avoiding the biological concerns associated with Al and V.

A Ti–Nb–Zr–Si alloy,
referred to here as TNZS,
was recently developed as a candidate metallic biomaterial and compared
with CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V under normal, inflammatory,
and severe inflammatory conditions. That
study showed that TNZS formed a microstructure containing α-Ti,
β-Ti, and silicide intermetallic phases, and that these phases
contributed to improved electrochemical behavior in acidic H2O2-containing environments. The same work also reported
better biological response of TNZS compared with Ti–6Al–4V,
including improved HOC and HUVEC behavior and stronger osteogenic
marker expression.

Although short-term
electrochemical and biological responses provide
valuable information, they do not fully represent the long-term behavior
of implant alloys after extended contact with physiological or inflammatory
fluids.
In vivo, implants
may remain in contact with stagnant or slowly refreshed biological
fluids for prolonged periods, especially in confined peri-implant
regions. During this time, solution chemistry
can change, reactive species may decompose, proteins may adsorb or
denature, and released ions may accumulate. Therefore, evaluating the electrochemical response only after short
immersion times may underestimate or misrepresent the condition of
the passive layer after long-term exposure.

Long-term ion release
is also clinically relevant because corrosion
products are not distributed uniformly throughout the body immediately
after release. Instead, they are first
generated locally near the implant surface, where they may interact
with macrophages, osteoblasts, endothelial cells, proteins, and extracellular
matrix components. Even when the total
released mass is low compared with systemic exposure limits, local
accumulation may influence inflammatory signaling, osteogenesis, angiogenesis,
and peri-implant tissue stability. Therefore,
corrosion resistance and biological performance should be interpreted
together with elemental release data.

A major research gap remains
because most electrochemical studies
on titanium biomaterials are limited to short immersion periods, typically
from a few hours to several days or weeks. Previous studies have provided
important insights into time-dependent passivation of Ti and Ti–6Al–4V
in Hank’s solution up to 168 h, Ti–6Al–7Nb and Ti–6Al–4V ELI in SBF
up to 360 h, β-type Ti–Nb–Ta
alloys over 15 days, and Ti–6Al–4V
in H2O2/albumin-containing media up to 120 h. More recent long-term studies on Ti–6Al–4V
have extended exposure to three months for surface-modified alloys
and to longer periods for additively manufactured scaffolds or retrieved
orthodontic mini-implants.
−

 However, direct one-year static
immersion data comparing CP-Ti, Ti–6Al–4V, and a newly
developed TNZS alloy under normal, inflammatory, and severe inflammatory
media are still lacking.

This study investigates whether TNZS
alloy can maintain stable
corrosion resistance and low elemental release after approximately
one year of exposure to aggressive simulated biological environments.
By integrating long-term immersion, post-exposure electrochemical
testing, and ion-release analysis, this work provides a more clinically
relevant assessment of TNZS electrochemical stability compared with
conventional titanium biomaterials. It also clarifies whether the
short-term corrosion and biological advantages of TNZS persist after
prolonged exposure.

Materials
and Methods

Sample Preparation

The same CP-Ti,
Ti–6Al–4V, and TNZS materials used in the previous study
were used in the present long-term immersion investigation. The chemical composition of the TNZS alloy was
Ti balance, 11.15 ± 0.19 wt % Zr, 9.61 ± 0.32 wt % Nb, and
1.23 ± 0.20 wt % Si. Commercially pure titanium contained 99.6%
Ti, while Ti–6Al–4V contained 5.5–6.76 wt % Al,
3.5–4.5 wt % V, and Ti as the balance. The earlier microstructural
and phase characterization showed that the TNZS alloy consisted of
α-Ti, β-Ti, and intermetallic silicide phases, with lamellar
eutectic regions distributed within the metallic matrix (Figure S1). The
specimens were prepared as discs with a diameter of 15 mm and a thickness
of 0.5 mm. Before immersion, the surfaces were ground using 240–2500
grit SiC papers, polished with 0.3 μm alumina suspension, ultrasonically
cleaned in 70% ethanol and deionized water, and dried before use.

Electrochemical Measurements

All
specimens were immersed in three simulated physiological media: normal,
inflammatory PBS/H2O2/HCl solution adjusted
to pH 5, and severe inflammatory PBS/H2O2/BSA/lactate/HCl
solution adjusted to pH 3. The composition of each medium is summarized
in Supplementary Table ST1. Specimens were
placed in plastic bottles containing 15 mL of the corresponding solution.
The bottles were closed and maintained under static, non-refreshed
conditions in a dark box at ambient laboratory temperature for 372
d. The solutions were neither refreshed nor supplemented during the
372 d exposure. Residual H2O2 concentration,
dissolved oxygen, ORP, BSA stability, and lactate concentration were
not monitored during immersion. Consequently, the terms “inflammatory”
and “severe inflammatory” refer to the initial formulations
of the media and do not imply that their original chemical compositions
remained constant throughout the exposure period.

This protocol
was designed to evaluate long-term surface aging and ion release under
closed, static, non-refreshed exposure conditions, including the effects
of solution aging and accumulation of released species. Moreover,
the present work was intentionally designed as a long-term static
baseline study rather than a complete simulation of the dynamic peri-implant
environment. This approach enables comparison of the intrinsic chemical
aging response of the three materials under identical exposure conditions,
while dynamic effects such as fluid renewal, reactant replenishment,
removal of corrosion products, and mass transport are excluded. These
factors should be incorporated in subsequent long-term studies to
evaluate the behavior of the materials under more physiologically
representative conditions.

After 372 d of immersion at ambient
temperature, the specimens
were removed from the aged solutions, gently rinsed with deionized
water to remove loosely attached residues, and immediately used for
electrochemical measurements in freshly prepared solutions with the
same nominal composition as the corresponding immersion medium. The
freshly prepared electrochemical test solutions were maintained at
37 ± 0.5 °C throughout the electrochemical measurements.
Electrochemical measurements were performed using an IviumStat.h potentiostat
(Ivium Technologies, The Netherlands) in a conventional three-electrode
cell. A graphite rod was used as the counter electrode, Ag/AgCl as
the reference electrode, and the Ti-based specimen as the working
electrode, with an exposed area of 1 cm2. All electrochemical
measurements were performed in triplicate.

OCP was recorded
for a fixed period of 600 s to monitor the initial
short-term potential response of the 372 d aged surfaces after transfer
to the freshly prepared corresponding electrolytes. This interval
was applied consistently to all specimens and was selected to maintain
methodological comparability with our previous study, in which the same materials and nominal solution formulations
were evaluated after 1 and 12 h of exposure.

PDP tests were
performed from −1.5 to +1.5 V versus OCP
at a scan rate of 1 mV·s–1. Since the anodic
branches of the titanium-based specimens were strongly influenced
by passive-film formation and did not exhibit a well-defined linear
Tafel region, the corrosion current density was estimated by extrapolating
the cathodic Tafel region to the corrosion potential. Therefore, only
the |βc| is reported.

EIS was carried out at
OCP using a 10 mV AC amplitude over a frequency
range of 100 kHz to 0.010 Hz. EIS data were analyzed using ZView software
(version 3.1, Scribner Associates), and the reliability of the fitting
was verified by chi-squared (χ2) values on the order
of 10–3.

Ion Release Assessment

After removal
of the specimens, the remaining one-year immersion solutions were
collected for ion-release analysis. The solutions were filtered before
analysis to remove suspended particles, corrosion debris, or detached
surface products. The concentrations of released elements were measured
by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES)
using a PerkinElmer DV5300 instrument. The filter membranes and retained
residues were not digested or analyzed. Therefore, the reported concentrations
represent the ICP-OES-detectable elemental fraction present in the
filtered solutions and should not be interpreted as total material
release or total mass loss from the specimens. The analyzed elements
were selected according to the composition of each material: Ti for
CP-Ti; Ti, Al, and V for Ti–6Al–4V; and Ti, Nb, Zr,
and Si for TNZS. The elemental concentrations were reported in μg·L–1.

The pH of each immersion solution was also
measured after the 372 d exposure period to determine the change in
solution chemistry during long-term immersion. For each material and
medium, the ICP-OES and pH results were reported as mean ± standard
deviation from three measurements. The immersion protocol was designed
as an adapted long-term static ion-release assay rather than a short-term
standard extraction test. The use of 15 mL solution ensured complete
immersion of the specimens and provided sufficient volume for filtration,
pH measurement, and ICP-OES analysis. The measured elemental concentrations
represent cumulative end point concentrations in the filtered 15 mL
immersion solutions. They reflect the combined effects of release,
accumulation, possible adsorption, precipitation, complexation, and
retention of material on the specimen or filter. Because intermediate
sampling was not performed, the data cannot be used to establish linear
release kinetics or a constant release rate over 372 d.

All
immersions were performed in metal-free polypropylene tubes
instead of glass containers to minimize possible background contamination.
This precaution was necessary because borosilicate or soda-lime glass
may leach Si, Na, B, Al, and other trace elements during prolonged
immersion, particularly under acidic conditions and in the presence
of H2O2.

Results
and Discussion

Electrochemical Characterization

Open Circuit Potential

shows OCP evolution
of specimens after 372 d of immersion in different simulated physiological
conditions. In the normal PBS solution, all specimens showed relatively
stable OCP curves during the 600 s measurement. The final potentials
after one year were approximately −0.096 V for CP-Ti, −0.036
V for Ti–6Al–4V, and −0.012 V for TNZS versus
Ag/AgCl. Compared with the 12 h results of the previous study, these
values shifted slightly in the noble direction, suggesting some stabilization
or reconstruction of the passive film during long-term exposure to
PBS. The positive shift was more pronounced for CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V,
while TNZS showed only a small change, probably because its passive
film had already reached a relatively stable condition after short-term
immersion.

The inflammatory medium produced a clear positive shift in OCP
compared with PBS. After one year, the final potentials were approximately
0.202 V for CP-Ti, 0.240 V for Ti–6Al–4V, and 0.350
V for TNZS. This electropositive shift confirms the oxidizing effect
of H2O2-containing PBS. H2O2 can act as an additional cathodic reactant and may generate oxidizing
intermediates that adsorb on the titanium passive film, leading to
higher OCP values. However, the one-year inflammatory potentials of
Ti–6Al–4V and TNZS were slightly lower than their 12
h values. The slightly lower OCP values observed after one-year exposure
compared with the short-term condition should not be interpreted as
direct evidence of a measured residual H2O2 concentration.
Because residual H2O2, dissolved oxygen, and
ORP were not monitored in the aged immersion media, H2O2 decomposition or consumption can only be considered a plausible
contributing factor. Other factors, including passive-film hydration,
surface contamination, adsorption of solution species, released-ion
accumulation, and changes in interfacial chemistry during long-term
static exposure, may also have contributed. Therefore, the OCP results
are interpreted as the response of aged surfaces during re-exposure
to freshly prepared media of the same nominal composition, not as
the in-situ mixed potential inside the aged one-year bottles.

The behavior of TNZS in the inflammatory solution was particularly
important. Although its final one-year OCP was slightly lower than
the short-term 12 h value, it remained clearly higher than those of
CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V. This indicates that the passive
film on TNZS retained better stability under oxidative acidic conditions.
The more positive end-of-monitoring potential of TNZS is consistent
with a different aged interfacial state from those of CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V.
A possible contribution from Nb-, Zr-, and Si-containing surface oxides
is supported by previous studies; however,
the composition, oxidation states, and structure of the aged TNZS
surface were not measured here. Therefore, the origin of the OCP difference
cannot be established from the present data alone.

In the severe
inflammatory solution, the OCP values were lower
than those in the inflammatory medium but higher than those in PBS.
The final potentials were approximately 0.093 V for CP-Ti, 0.146 V
for Ti–6Al–4V, and 0.212 V for TNZS. Thus, the environmental
order was normal < severe inflammatory < inflammatory. This
trend agrees with the previous short-term study, where the addition
of BSA and lactate to the H2O2-containing solution
reduced the OCP. BSA and lactate-containing species can adsorb on
the titanium surface, block cathodic sites, and decrease the rate
of H2O2 reduction, resulting in lower potentials
than in the inflammatory medium alone.

Compared with the 12
h condition, the severe inflammatory potentials
decreased after one year, suggesting progressive modification of the
passive films by low pH, H2O2, protein, and
lactate. This lower OCP should not be interpreted as improved corrosion
resistance. Instead, it reflects the combined effects of oxidizing-species
depletion, biomolecule adsorption, cathodic-site blocking, and chemical
degradation of the passive layer. CP-Ti showed a slight negative drift
during the 600 s measurement, indicating continued surface instability,
whereas Ti–6Al–4V and TNZS showed more stable behavior.
Overall, the OCP results show that TNZS maintained the noblest potential
in all media, but the corrosion resistance after one year must be
interpreted together with PDP and EIS results, since OCP alone does
not directly quantify corrosion rate.

Electrochemical
Impedance Spectroscopy

and show the Nyquist,
Bode modulus, and Bode phase
plots of CP-Ti, Ti–6Al–4V, and TNZS after 372 d of immersion
in the normal, inflammatory, and severe inflammatory media. The impedance
spectra were fitted using the same equivalent circuit used in the
previous short-term study (Figure S2),
consisting of the solution resistance, Rs, in series with
a parallel combination of passive-layer resistance, Rpl, and a constant phase element, CPE. This circuit was previously
selected because the EIS response of the tested titanium-based materials
showed one dominant time constant, while surface heterogeneity, roughness,
oxide defects, and non-ideal capacitive behavior required the use
of a CPE instead of an ideal capacitor.

The same equivalent circuit used in the previous short-term study
was applied to fit the one-year EIS spectra because the impedance
response still exhibited one dominant time constant in the Bode phase
plots. Although long-term immersion may modify the passive layer through
hydration, aging, surface roughening, or adsorption of solution species,
these effects were reflected by changes in Rpl, CPE, and
n rather than by the appearance of an additional electrochemical time
constant. Therefore, the circuit remained appropriate for comparing
the passive-layer behavior after 1 h, 12 h, and 372 d of immersion.

The fitted EIS parameters after one year are summarized in Supplementary Table ST2. In normal, the Rpl values were 8.2, 16.5, and 28.0 kΩ·cm2 for CP-Ti, Ti–6Al–4V, and TNZS, respectively. The
corresponding Nyquist plots showed the largest capacitive loop for
TNZS and the smallest one for CP-Ti, indicating that the protective
ability of the passive film followed the order TNZS > Ti–6Al–4V
> CP-Ti. The Bode modulus plots showed the same trend, with TNZS
exhibiting
the highest low-frequency impedance. This confirms that, even after
long-term immersion, the TNZS alloy retained the most protective surface
condition among the three materials.

Compared with the short-term
immersion behavior, the one-year PBS
data suggest that the passive films did not continue to grow into
a highly resistive state indefinitely. Although the short-term study
showed an increase in Rpl from 1 to 12 h, which was attributed
to passive-film formation and reconstruction, the present one-year
response remained within the same practical impedance range as the
earlier spectra rather than increasing by orders of magnitude. This
behavior is reasonable because long-term exposure under static conditions
can lead not only to oxide maturation but also to hydration, aging,
surface contamination, local defect formation, and partial transformation
of the passive film. Therefore, the one-year PBS response should be
interpreted as a stabilized long-term passive state rather than simply
a continuation of the short-term increase in passive-layer resistance.

In the inflammatory medium, the Rpl values decreased
to 4.8 kΩ·cm2 for CP-Ti, 7.0 kΩ·cm2 for Ti–6Al–4V, and 10.5 kΩ·cm2 for TNZS. Thus, the passive-layer resistance in the inflammatory
medium was approximately 41–42% of the corresponding PBS value
for CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V, and about 38% for TNZS. This
decrease confirms the damaging effect of the acidic H2O2-containing solution on the passive layers. Short-term studies
have associated lower impedance in freshly prepared inflammatory media
with reactions between titanium oxide and H2O2. In the present long-term static system,
similar reactions may have occurred initially, but their contribution
over the full exposure period cannot be quantified because the solution
chemistry evolved without monitoring.

The inflammatory solution
also affected the CPE parameters. The
n values decreased from about 0.94–0.95 in PBS to 0.89–0.91
in the inflammatory medium, indicating a greater deviation from ideal
capacitive behavior. This suggests that the passive films became more
heterogeneous after one year in acidic H2O2-containing
PBS. Such heterogeneity may result from repeated local oxide dissolution
and repassivation, peroxide-assisted oxidation, chloride interaction,
and formation of a less uniform hydrated oxide layer. Nevertheless,
TNZS maintained the highest Rpl in this medium, showing
that the Nb-, Zr-, and Si-containing passive layer remained more resistant
to peroxide-induced degradation than the oxide films on CP-Ti and
Ti–6Al–4V.

The severe inflammatory medium produced
the lowest impedance response
for all materials. The fitted Rpl values were 2.6, 4.6,
and 7.2 kΩ·cm2 for CP-Ti, Ti–6Al–4V,
and TNZS, respectively. Compared with PBS, these values represent
decreases of about 68% for CP-Ti, 72% for Ti–6Al–4V,
and 74% for TNZS. The specimens aged in the medium initially formulated
as severe inflammatory showed the lowest post-exposure impedance response.
This indicates that the overall one-year exposure history in this
solution produced the least protective aged surfaces, although the
relative contributions of residual H2O2, low
pH, BSA, lactate, and solution-aging products cannot be separated.

The stronger degradation in the severe inflammatory medium can
be explained by several simultaneous effects. The low pH increases
the solubility and chemical instability of titanium oxide. H2O2 promotes oxidative attack and formation of reactive
peroxide-related surface species. Lactate can participate in complexation
reactions with titanium species, facilitating dissolution or destabilization
of the passive film. BSA adsorption can further modify the interface,
producing a more heterogeneous surface layer and affecting charge-transfer
and capacitive behavior. These combined processes reduce Rpl and produce lower phase angles, indicating a less ideal and less
protective passive film.

The Ceff values calculated
from the CPE parameters were
in the range of approximately 1.38–3.04 × 10–5 F·cm–2, while the calculated deff values ranged from about 1.31 to 2.89 nm. These values are consistent
with the previous study, where the calculated effective passive-layer
thickness did not exceed a few nanometers. It is important to emphasize
that deff represents the effective thickness of the compact
electrochemically active barrier layer, not the total physical thickness
of all surface oxides, hydrated products, adsorbed proteins, or deposits
accumulated during one year. Therefore, the fact that deff increased only by a few nanometers is reasonable.

Interestingly,
the calculated deff values were slightly
higher in the inflammatory and severe inflammatory media than in PBS,
even though Rpl decreased. This apparent contradiction
is meaningful. A larger calculated deff does not necessarily
indicate a more protective passive film. In aggressive media, lower
Ceff and higher apparent deff may reflect the
formation of a more hydrated, porous, chemically modified, or protein/lactate-containing
surface layer. Such a layer can be thicker in an effective electrochemical
sense but less compact and less protective. Therefore, Rpl remains the more direct indicator of barrier performance, while
deff should be interpreted together with Q, n, and the
overall impedance response.

Among the three materials, TNZS
consistently showed the highest
Rpl in all media. In PBS, its Rpl was about
3.4 times higher than CP-Ti and 1.7 times higher than Ti–6Al–4V.
In the inflammatory medium, TNZS showed Rpl values about
2.2 times higher than CP-Ti and 1.5 times higher than Ti–6Al–4V.
In the severe inflammatory medium, TNZS still retained an Rpl about 2.8 times higher than CP-Ti and 1.6 times higher than Ti–6Al–4V.
This confirms that the beneficial effect of the TNZS alloy composition
was preserved even after one year of immersion.

Potentiodynamic Polarization

shows the potentiodynamic
polarization (PDP) curves of CP-Ti, Ti–6Al–4V, and TNZS
after 372 d of immersion in normal, inflammatory, and severe inflammatory
media. The corresponding electrochemical parameters extracted by Tafel
extrapolation are summarized in Supplementary Table ST3. The polarization curves clearly show that both the
alloy composition and the chemical nature of the immersion medium
strongly affected the corrosion response after long-term exposure.

After one year in normal condition, all materials still showed
lower corrosion current densities than in the inflammatory media,
confirming that neutral PBS remained the least aggressive condition.
However, compared with the 12 h results reported previously, the one-year
icorr values increased for all specimens. The icorr values were 3.90 ± 0.45 μA·cm–2 for CP-Ti, 0.54 ± 0.08 μA·cm–2 for Ti–6Al–4V, and 0.050 ± 0.008 μA·cm–2 for TNZS. This indicates that, although PBS promotes
passive film formation during short-term immersion, long-term static
exposure can still lead to passive-layer aging, hydration, defect
formation, or accumulation of surface corrosion products. Therefore,
the long-term PBS response should not be interpreted as continuous
improvement of passivity, but rather as formation of a stabilized
yet less ideal passive surface.

The corrosion tendency in normal
PBS followed the order CP-Ti >
Ti–6Al–4V > TNZS. TNZS maintained the lowest icorr, indicating that its passive layer retained superior barrier
properties
after prolonged immersion. The Ecorr values were −0.60
V for CP-Ti, −0.42 V for Ti–6Al–4V, and −0.03
V for TNZS versus Ag/AgCl. The nobler Ecorr of TNZS suggests
a more stable electrochemical surface state, although the lower icorr is the stronger indication of improved corrosion resistance.

In the inflammatory medium, the corrosion current density increased
markedly for all materials. The icorr values were much
higher than those obtained for the specimens aged in PBS, showing
that surfaces aged in the initially acidic H2O2-containing formulation were more susceptible to corrosion during
re-exposure to the fresh corresponding test medium. Compared with
the 12 h values of the previous study, the one-year inflammatory icorr values were also slightly higher, indicating cumulative
passive-film degradation. The long-term surface modification may have
resulted from reactions occurring during the early stages of exposure,
progressive solution aging, passive-film hydration and dissolution,
adsorption or degradation of solution species, and accumulation of
released products. Since residual H2O2 was not
measured, its contribution over the entire 372 d period cannot be
quantified.

The increase in icorr in the inflammatory
medium can
be attributed to the combined effects of low pH and H2O2. Hydrogen peroxide can participate in cathodic reactions
and generate reactive peroxide-related species on titanium surfaces,
while acidic conditions facilitate oxide dissolution. For CP-Ti, this
mainly reflects weakening of the TiO2-based passive film.
For Ti–6Al–4V, instability or dissolution of Al- and
V-containing oxides may further increase surface heterogeneity. TNZS
also showed increased icorr in this medium, but its corrosion
current density remained lower than those of CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V,
confirming its better resistance to peroxide-assisted corrosion.

The severe inflammatory medium produced the highest icorr values among all conditions. After 372 d, icorr values
were substantially higher than those measured in PBS and inflammatory
media, confirming that severe inflammatory conditions caused the strongest
passive-layer degradation. This trend is consistent with the EIS results,
where the passive-layer resistance decreased after one year. The severe
inflammatory medium initially contained low pH, H2O2, BSA, and lactate. These constituents may interact synergistically,
particularly during the initial exposure period; however, their concentrations
and chemical states after prolonged static immersion were not determined.
Low pH increases oxide solubility, H2O2 promotes
oxidative attack, lactate may complex with dissolved titanium species,
and BSA adsorption can modify the surface reactions and produce a
heterogeneous interfacial layer.

The Ecorr values
in the inflammatory and severe inflammatory
media shifted to more electropositive values compared with PBS. This
reflects the oxidizing nature of the H2O2-containing
solutions. However, Ecorr alone cannot be used as a direct
measure of corrosion resistance. A positive potential shift in oxidizing
media can occur at the same time as an increase in corrosion current
density. Therefore, the simultaneous evaluation of Ecorr and icorr is essential. TNZS showed the most positive
Ecorr in the severe inflammatory medium but also the lowest
icorr, indicating that it remained in a nobler passive
state while maintaining slower dissolution kinetics than CP-Ti and
Ti–6Al–4V.

The cathodic Tafel slopes also changed
with the environment, reflecting
differences in cathodic reaction kinetics. In PBS, the higher |βc| of TNZS suggests slower cathodic kinetics on its more protective
surface. In the inflammatory and severe inflammatory media, the cathodic
process was influenced not only by oxygen reduction but also by H2O2 reduction, adsorbed intermediates, protein/lactate
adsorption, and changes in passive-film chemistry. Minor fluctuations
observed in the aggressive-media branches can be attributed to surface
heterogeneity and repeated local dissolution/repassivation processes.

Overall, the PDP results confirm that long-term immersion reduced
the corrosion resistance of all materials, as shown by increased icorr after 372 d. The corrosion severity followed the order
normal < inflammatory < severe inflammatory, while the material
ranking remained CP-Ti > Ti–6Al–4V > TNZS in terms
of
corrosion current density. The superior performance of TNZS can be
attributed to the presence of Nb, Zr, and Si, which promote the formation
of a more stable passive film. From an implant perspective, the increased
icorr under inflammatory conditions indicates a greater
tendency for ion release and surface degradation. This is particularly
important for Ti–6Al–4V because corrosion may involve
Al and V release. The lower icorr of TNZS in all media
therefore supports its potential advantage as a long-term dental implant
material.

To provide an integrated comparison of the electrochemical
response
after short- and long-term exposure, the key EIS- and PDP-derived
parameters are summarized as property maps in . In a, the relationship between corrosion current density,
icorr, and passive-layer resistance, Rpl, shows
that increasing environmental aggressiveness shifts the materials
toward higher icorr and lower Rpl, confirming
progressive loss of passive-film protection from normal to inflammatory
and severe inflammatory media. In b, the Ecorr–βc map shows that a more positive corrosion potential alone
does not necessarily indicate improved corrosion resistance, particularly
in the H2O2-containing media, where oxidizing

demonstrates changes in the electrochemically active passive layer,
with higher Ceff generally reflecting a less protective
or more defective interfacial state. Across all three maps, TNZS remains
positioned toward lower icorr, higher Rpl, and
more stable passive-layer behavior compared with CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V,
supporting the persistence of its superior corrosion resistance after
one-year exposure.

The elemental release

In normal PBS, all materials showed limited
elemental release,
confirming the protective role of the passive oxide film under near-neutral
physiological conditions. CP-Ti released only Ti, while Ti–6Al–4V
released Ti together with smaller amounts of Al and V. TNZS showed
the lowest total release among the three materials. In this alloy,
Ti was the main released element, whereas Nb, Zr, and Si were detected
only at very low levels. This behavior agrees with previous long-term
immersion studies, where Ti–6Al–4V in PBS/protein-containing
media showed relatively limited ion release and a tendency toward
stabilization after prolonged exposure due to oxide growth or precipitation
of dissolved species on the surface. Karimi et al. reported that Ti release from Ti–6Al–4V increased
at earlier immersion times but did not continue to increase after
longer exposure, and Al was not detected by ICP-OES in their PBS/BSA
system. Similarly, studies on Ti–Nb–Zr–Ta
,
 and TiZrNbFeMo alloys reported lower
ion release than CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V, supporting the
present low-release behavior of TNZS in PBS.

In the inflammatory
medium, elemental release increased for all
materials. This increase is consistent with the electrochemical results,
where the H2O2-containing acidic medium reduced
the corrosion resistance of the passive films. H2O2 can promote peroxide-assisted oxidation and partial dissolution
of titanium oxide, while lower pH further destabilizes the passive
layer. This trend is consistent with Höhn and Virtanen, who showed that Ti, Al, and V release from Ti–6Al–4V
markedly increased under simulated inflammatory conditions at pH 5
in the presence of H2O2. In their work, Al and
V release reached the mg·L–1 or sub-mg·L–1 range depending on surface condition and immersion
time, which is comparable in order of magnitude to the present Ti–6Al–4V
release in aggressive media. In contrast, TNZS released lower total
amounts of elements than both conventional materials, and its alloying-element
release followed the order Zr ≥ Nb > Si, indicating better
passive-film stability.

The severe inflammatory medium produced
the highest elemental release
for all materials. This environment combines low pH, H2O2, BSA, and lactate, which together can accelerate passive-film
degradation. Previous studies on albumin-containing solutions showed
that proteins can alter oxide chemistry, interact with released ions,
and modify dissolution/precipitation processes during long-term immersion. The present results support this behavior: CP-Ti
showed a strong increase in Ti release, while Ti–6Al–4V
released substantial amounts of Ti, Al, and V. TNZS also showed increased
release compared with PBS and the inflammatory medium, but its total
release remained much lower than CP-Ti and Ti–6Al–4V.
This confirms that TNZS was more resistant to long-term degradation
even in the most aggressive simulated biological condition.

The final pH measurements showed that PBS remained close to neutral,
whereas the inflammatory and severe inflammatory media remained acidic
after 372 d. However, pH alone does not define the full chemistry
of the aged solutions. In particular, the residual H2O2 concentration, dissolved oxygen, ORP, protein degradation,
lactate stability, and possible evaporation were not measured. Therefore,
the final pH values confirm only that the media retained distinct
acidity ranges at the end of exposure.

The elemental release
results also support the better biological
response previously observed for TNZS compared with Ti–6Al–4V. This behavior can be partly attributed to its
lower total ion release and more favorable released species: TNZS
does not release Al or V, while Nb and Zr are generally regarded as
more biocompatible β-Ti alloying elements. The low Si release
is also consistent with the small Si content and the role of silicide/intermetallic
phases in improving corrosion resistance and retaining alloying elements
within the Ti-based matrix. This interpretation is also supported
by previous work on metallurgically alloyed Ti-based biomaterials,
where stable intermetallic/matrix incorporation limited detectable
alloying-element release by ICP-OES while maintaining cytocompatibility. Although the absolute released masses after
one year are expected to be low compared with general systemic exposure
levels, corrosion products are released locally at the tissue–implant
interface, where even small amounts may become relevant under chronic
inflammation, or mechanically assisted corrosion. Therefore, the lower
release from TNZS, together with the absence of Al and V, suggests
a reduced risk of local ion accumulation and inflammatory feedback,
which is consistent with its improved cell response.

Limitations of the Study

A limitation
of this study is that the long-term immersion media were not chemically
monitored throughout the 372 d exposure. The solutions were static
and non-refreshed, and only the final pH was measured. Residual H2O2 concentration, dissolved oxygen, oxidation–reduction
potential, protein degradation, lactate stability, and possible evaporation
were not quantified. Therefore, the study does not establish that
the specimens were exposed to a constant inflammatory oxidizing environment
for one year, and partial H2O2 depletion during
the static exposure cannot be excluded. In addition, the experiments
were performed at ambient temperature rather than at physiological
temperature, which may have affected corrosion kinetics, H2O2 stability, protein behavior, and ion-release rates.
The immersion test also did not include mechanical loading, fretting,
wear, or tribocorrosion, although such mechanically assisted degradation
processes are relevant for dental implants. Furthermore, ICP-OES was
performed on filtered solutions; therefore, the reported elemental
release values represent filtered dissolved species and may underestimate
total material release if corrosion particles, detached oxide debris,
precipitates, or protein-bound species were retained by the filter.
Another limitation is the absence of post-immersion surface chemical
characterization, such as XPS which prevents direct confirmation of
the long-term passive-film composition and enrichment in Nb, Zr, or
Si. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as long-term static
exposure to media initially formulated to simulate normal, inflammatory,
and severe inflammatory conditions, followed by standardized electrochemical
re-exposure of aged surfaces to freshly prepared media. This approach
allows comparison among CP-Ti, Ti–6Al–4V, and TNZS under
controlled post-exposure testing conditions, but it does not reproduce
the exact in-situ electrochemical state of the aged metal/solution
system at day 372.

Conclusions

This
study evaluated the long-term electrochemical behavior and
elemental release of CP-Ti, Ti–6Al–4V, and TNZS after
372 d of immersion in simulated normal, inflammatory, and severe inflammatory
environments. The results showed that the solution chemistry strongly
affected passive-layer stability and ion release. Specimens aged in
media initially formulated to simulate inflammatory and severe inflammatory
conditions showed poorer post-exposure electrochemical performance
and greater filtered elemental release than those aged in normal PBS.
These differences reflect the cumulative effects of the initial solution
chemistry and its subsequent evolution during static, non-refreshed
exposure; they cannot be attributed to continuously maintained concentrations
of H2O2, BSA, or lactate. Among the tested materials,
TNZS consistently exhibited the most favorable corrosion response,
including nobler open-circuit potential, lower corrosion current density,
and higher passive-layer resistance.

ICP-OES analysis also showed
that TNZS released the lowest total
amount of elements in all media. Importantly, the absence of Al and
V in TNZS eliminates Al- and V-related elemental release from the
alloy, whereas the release of these elements remains a concern for
Ti–6Al–4V during long-term implantation. Although all
released concentrations remained relatively low, local ion accumulation
at the tissue–implant interface may be clinically relevant
under chronic inflammatory conditions.

These findings indicate
that TNZS retained better relative electrochemical
stability than the conventional titanium materials under the specific
aging and testing conditions employed. Nevertheless, the results are
limited to a comparative coupon-level study involving static, non-refreshed
media and an accelerated chemical challenge. TNZS may therefore be
considered a promising Al- and V-free candidate for further implant-related
investigation.
