 ABSTRACT
This report describes a chopper cascade suitable for a cold direct geometry spectrometer at a pulsed neu-
tron source with a low repetition rate such as the Second Target Station (STS) currently being planned at
Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). The chopper cascade offers the following capabilities: selection
of a variable number of pulses per frame (flexible pulse suppression), selection of pulses with increasing
timing separation within one frame, and an increasing pulse width ∆t within one frame, in order to achieve
an even energy resolution over several pulses at different wavelengths across the frame.

 1. INTRODUCTION
The CHopper spEctrometer for Small Samples (CHESS) was proposed in 2021 as the first general-purpose
chopper spectrometer (Sala et al. 2018) to be built at the Second Target Station (STS) at Oak Ridge Na-
tional Laboratory (ORNL). It is a compact, wide bandwidth instrument, that is optimized for performing
neutron spectroscopy on small samples. The instrument offers medium to coarse resolution in both neutron
energy and momentum transfer, and thus is suitable for a wide range of science use cases. At the time of
this writing, the current technical design is the one that is documented in a 2023 Technical Report (Sala
and Boone 2023). CHESS will outperform the Cold Neutron Chopper Spectrometer (CNCS), which is its
counterpart at the First Target Station (FTS) at the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS), by a factor of 20–30
in terms of useful neutron flux on the sample per pulse. The operating ranges of the two instruments are
shown in Fig. 1, reproducing a figure from the 2018 conceptual paper (Sala et al. 2018).

Figure 1. The envisaged operating range of CHESS for incident energy and energy resolution at the
elastic line (yellow shaded area). For comparison, the actual operational range of the CNCS instrument is
also shown (pink shaded area). The figure is reproduced from the 2018 conceptual paper (Sala et al. 2018).

The purpose of this report here is to document details of the chopper system design that are missing in the
2023 Technical Report, and to propose a modification to the M−chopper disks that will enable a greatly
simplified engineering design, while also offering a more even energy resolution across the neutron energy
range. The only change is in the slit patterns of the disks. The location of the M−chopper, as well as the
number of disks and the speed requirements for the disks will remain unchanged.

 2. CHOPPER SYSTEM DESCRIPTION
2.1 GENERAL OVERVIEW
Because a neutron spectrometer of the direct geometry type operates most efficiently with a time-of-flight
(TOF) frame length of ∼ 10 ms or shorter, but the STS source frequency is f = 15 Hz (implying a 66.7 ms
frame length), the instrument will be designed to employ Repetition Rate Multiplication (RRM) so that
several (2–10) pulses per frame can be utilized (Mezei 2006). For optimal use, the chopper cascade must
then achieve the following:
 1. it must be possible to select a variable number of pulses per frame
 2. within one frame, it must be possible to select pulses with increasing TOF separation
 3. within one frame, the pulse width (in time) must increase
 4. it must be possible to change the resolution (chopper burst time) without changing the chopper speed
To facilitate the discussion of these points, a time distance diagram is included here that incorporates the
specific design choices (distances and frequencies) made for CHESS.

  Figure 2. Time distance diagram for CHESS as initially proposed. The P− and M−choppers pick
  n = 20 equidistant pulses per frame. The F−chopper blocks six of them, but 14 can get to the sample
 unless blocked by the H−chopper. Here, six pulses between 2.80 Å and 8.51 Å propagate to the sample.

The CHESS Technical Report (Sala and Boone 2023) defines a range requirement for the number of ac-
cepted pulses as 1–8 pulses. As a rule of thumb, the desirable number of pulses per frame will be larger
if one is only interested in a narrow range of energy transfer near elastic scattering, or if one approaches
thermal neutron energies (because faster neutrons need less time to spread out in time when scattered with
energy transfer). In Fig. 2, a setting with 6 pulses is shown as an example, with wavelengths from 2.80 Å
to 8.51 Å. The timing separation between pulses is such that five of six allow one to observe neutron en-
ergy loss to 90% of incident energy, before infinite neutron energy gain from the next pulse overlaps in

time of flight on the detector. The remaining one pulse still lets one cover 80% which is generally consid-
ered sufficient.

From the instrument design perspective, the general strategy for delivering pulses to the sample is to offer a
certain number n per frame, via design and speed of the M−chopper, see below, and then to suppress some
of them with a slower pulse suppression chopper (H−chopper). For CHESS, n = 20. This is a suitable
choice, because STS will operate at f = 15 Hz, and a disk speed of n · 15 = 300 Hz is achievable and suf-
ficient for good performance while at the same time not pushing technology limits. Because slower (longer
wavelength) neutrons take more time to encode energy transfer through TOF after being scattered, one de-
sires an increasing TOF separation between pulses as the frame progresses. The energy transfer range that
one is interested in also plays a role in what settings best to choose, and this is why a flexible pulse sup-
pression scheme is desired.
It is important to note that the chopper design for CHESS effectively fixes n and thus fixes the speed of the
high-speed chopper disks. This comes out of the H− chopper design, see below. Therefore, the chopper
system is designed such that all essential function, including a change of instrumental resolution, can be

 3

achieved without ever changing a disk speed. It is possible to change the H−chopper speed with some of
its configurations, which would result in other disk speeds possibly changing, but it remains to be studied
how exactly this could work.
The neutron pulses delivered to the sample are defined by a pair of high-speed choppers, commonly re-
ferred to as the P−chopper (pulse-shaping chopper), and the M−chopper (monochromating chopper).
Their distance to the source must be in a commensurate ratio in order for RRM to work. For CHESS, a
1 : 2 distance ratio was chosen.
Going through the instrument from the source to the detector, the first chopper at 7.5 m distance is a frame
overlap chopper (F−chopper). It will run at the source frequency, f = 15 Hz. Its purpose is to define the
working wavelength band and to prevent faster or slower than desired neutrons to reach the detector. The
necessary opening time will be determined by its location relative to source and detector, and therefore a
single disk is sufficient.
The next chopper is the P−chopper (pulse-shaping chopper) at 15 m distance. The P−chopper will be a
high-speed double disk ( f = 300 Hz) counterrotating chopper (counterrotating because one wants sharp
rising and falling edges of the pulses for best performance). Looking at Fig. 2, one understands that this
chopper needs to open twice as often as the M−chopper, because it is at half the distance. This is achieved
by having two slits in each of the P−chopper disks. The disks are phased such that they open the neutron
beam at the same time, twice per revolution. With a distance ratio 1 : 2, the P−chopper burst time should
be about two times longer than the M−chopper burst time, which is achieved with wider slits.
Next come the H−chopper (“hand” chopper ) and the M−chopper (monochromating chopper), which
should be close together in order to avoid neutron leakage. The CHESS Technical Report present an en-
gineering solution for both, but each can be further improved and the design should not be considered final
for either.
The M−chopper is the second high-speed double disk counterrotating chopper whose purpose is to define
the neutron pulses. The H−chopper is a slow ( f = 15 Hz) double-disk chopper that performs the flexi-
ble pulse suppression. For best performance, the M−chopper should be as close to the sample as possible,
because one does not want the pulse defined at the M−chopper blurred by the time it reaches the sample.
This requirement is to be balanced against the needs for space around the sample for sample environment
equipment. The impact (on the magnetic M−chopper bearings) of a stray magnetic field created by a cry-
omagnet in the sample position is a critically important consideration and some relevant experience has
been gained by operating cryomagnets capable of generating up to 16 T at some FTS instruments.
Possible designs of the M−chopper will be described in section 2.2 of this report. This chopper is the most
important one for the resolution of the instrument, and a new implementation is the main purpose of this
report.
The H−chopper will be briefly discussed in section 2.4 of this report.
One chopper that is not further discussed here is the T 0 −chopper. CHESS will have a straight, ballistic
guide and therefore a T 0 −chopper will be needed to block the prompt pulse of high-energy particles, neu-
trons and photons. It should close the beam only for a brief period of time, once per frame, but further re-
quirements for it don’t need to be set at this stage of the design.

Here, v is the neutron speed, ∆tS is the source pulse width, ∆t M is the M−chopper burst time, ∆tD is the
uncertainty of the travel time between sample and detector, and L1,2,3 and the distances from source to
M−chopper, M−chopper to sample, and sample to detector, respectively.
When one measures inelastic scattering at several energies Ei simultaneously (six energies in the example
above), even information content across spectra can be expected when ∆E/Ei is constant. From neutron
moderation physics, v · ∆tS is about constant in the neutron energy range of interest and correlated with the
physical moderator size. The path length uncertainty from the point of scattering to the point of neutron
detection, v · ∆tD is normally constant as well (irregularly shaped crystals, when rotated during an experi-
ment may invalidate this assumption). Because one wants to keep the three contributions to the resolution
roughly equal, this means that the instrument design should aim for v · ∆t M also being constant. This is
the third requirement from the beginning of section 2.1: within one frame, the pulse width (in time) should
increase linearly with time so that the product with the neutron speed remains a constant.

 different speeds. One disk can be at (n + 1) · 15 = 315 Hz or at (n − 1) · 15 = 285 Hz. The time distance

There are at least two ways in which this can be accomplished with a double disk chopper. The first way
(“Config A”) is with n equidistant slits in one of the M−chopper disks, and to rotate this disk at n ± 1 times
the base frequency so that with each revolution the next slit gets picked. The second disk, which runs at n
times base frequency, has a small number of slits with different sizes so that one can change the burst time
by picking a different phase. This strategy can also be applied to the first disk: because 6 of 20 slits are
always blocked by the F−chopper, by disk phase one can select slits 0 − 13, 1 − 14, and so on to 6 − 19.
Because of the large number of slits in one of the disks, one needs to be careful with neutron leakage. It
was shown by ray tracing simulation that the design in Fig. 3 does not leak, but the number of slits in the
second disk cannot be larger than 4.

The second way (“Config B”) in which one can achieve a linearly increasing M−chopper burst time within
one frame is to use a double blind chopper. This is the way that was originally proposed for CHESS, and
its application to spectroscopy was first considered for a wide bandwidth spectrometer at the European
Spallation Source (ESS), see Vickery and Deen 2014.
A blind double disk chopper is one in which the second disk opens as the first closes, it is closed to very
fast neutrons. Fig. 4 illustrates how this leads to a time dependent chopper burst time.
The main downside of this solution appears to be the need for a variable disk separation to change the res-
olution when the disk speed is fixed because n = 20 is fixed. A variable disk separation is an engineer-
ing challenge1 that will be difficult and expensive to realize, because the disks need to rotate in vacuum.
However, one could consider using ∼ 4 slits in each of the M−chopper disks, that have different sizes, and
change slits by changing the disk phases. This approach could avoid the need for variable disk separation,
and needs further study. Alternatively, one could add a third disk for another choice of disk separation, and
park one disk of three open.

The third M−chopper configuration included in this report (“Config C”) is the conventional design that

2.3 SIMULATION RESULTS
This section discusses the results of simulation work that was undertaken to confirm the validity of the
above designs. This includes a comparison with conventional M−chopper design (“Config C”) in which
∆t M is constant.
One of the key aspects of the instrument design is the simultaneous coverage of a wide energy range, over
which the source brightness varies greatly, and the desire (by instrument design) to make information con-
tent equal across different spectra so that beam time is used most efficiently. The purpose of the simula-
tions was to show that equal resolution can be achieved, and to compare the intensity on the detector across
spectra. The goal was not to compare resolution and intensity between different configurations. The scat-
tering was assumed to be incoherent, angle-independent and strictly elastic.
Simulations were done with in-house code that greatly simplified the actual neutron transport, but modeled
the action of the disk choppers accurately. It was verified that the H−chopper (section 2.4) did suppress
correctly, in each configuration, all pulses that were not meant to pass to the sample position. The spectral
brightness of the neutron source (STS tube moderator) was taken into account. For details, see Gallmeier
and Remec 2022.
The main objective of the simulations was to study the transmitted neutron pulse widths, and to get realis-
tic predictions for peak intensity and energy resolution of a direct geometry spectrometer. The pulse set-
tings were exactly like shown in Fig. 2, with six (not equidistant) pulses transmitted during a 15 Hz frame
with wavelengths from 2.80 Å to 8.51 Å. Simulated neutron events were collected at the detector locus and
converted to energy transfer before histogramming.

The side by side comparison of the three studied configurations is in Fig. 9. In general, Config. A will
achieve a more even resolution than Config. B. This has been confirmed with simulations, but an analyt-
ical treatment has – to the best of my knowledge – not been made. It can be understood with the Gaussian
lineshape of the pulses (deviation from rectangular shape). The spectral dependence of the intensity (right
hand side panel) is due to the source properties and the varied instrumental resolution (left hand side). It
is an approximation because the simulation greatly simplified the beam transport, and the wavelength de-
pendence of the brilliance transfer from source to sample was not taken into account. However, it gets the
point across that it is important for the instrument to control the resolution change across spectra, because
it has a profound influence on the pulse intensities at the detector.

2.4 H–CHOPPER
The H−chopper as laid out in the CHESS Technical Report does not work as intended. Looking at Fig. 2.8
in the report, configurations (d) and (e) on the right hand side fail two basic requirements for pulse sup-
pression. The first is that the TOF separation between pulses does not increase monotonically, even con-
sidering either possible sense of rotation. The second is that one of the pulses will always get blocked by
the F−chopper.
A possible underlying reason for this to happen is in the requirement, section 2.3.2 of the report (page 22),
that “a coverage of −2.1 Ei ≤ ∆E ≤ 0.9 Ei in energy transfer” needs to be provided at the detector. This
requirement should be re-evaluated. Practical experience dictates that one should always cover, without
overlap, infinite neutron energy gain (∆E = −∞). In the TOF on the detector near ∆E = −∞, most neutron
energy gain scattering (“up-scattering”) will pile up in a short period of time, and any analysis of that data
will be very susceptible to getting the time-independent background exactly right. Even if the up-scattering
is not of interest, it will be on top of neutron energy loss scattering (“down-scattering”) from the previ-
ous pulse. In other words, in a TOF range on the detector where down-scattering from one pulse overlaps

 9

with up-scattering from the next pulse, that down-scattering will be in an unfavorable signal to noise sit-
uation because of the presence of overlapping up-scattering, and the practical difficulties of quantifying it
precisely. One should consider times on the detector near ∆E = −∞ of a pulse as “dead-time” and space
pulses accordingly.
The H−chopper concept detailed in the 2018 conceptual paper (Sala et al. 2018) will work better, and the
reader is referred to that paper for details. The main idea is to compose a sequence of n = 20 intervals
per frame that are either open or closed, by a superposition of two sequences imprinted on two H−chopper
disks that rotate in a parallel sense. One can achieve 20 − 30 such super-positions that give sequences that
meet all main requirements for pulse suppression. When considering a longer version of a direct geometry
spectrometer for STS, one will perhaps reconsider this H−chopper concept, as it may become unneces-
sary. This is because the longer the instrument, the more “even” the pulses become and less variation in
the timing between pulses will be necessary. C-SPEC, the cold direct geometry spectrometer at the ESS is
∼ 150 m long and does not need an H−chopper.

 3. CONCLUSION
This report studies chopper systems for a direct geometry spectrometer at STS. While there are good sci-
entific reasons to build a wide bandwidth instrument at that facility, there are also challenges such as the
discrepancy in spectral brightness of the source toward the edges of the instrument’s working range. There
is not much operational experience yet at ORNL with RRM methods. The instrument’s performance will
therefore greatly depend on the built-in flexibility to vary the resolution on one side of the spectrum rela-
tive to the other side.
The work here demonstrated an alternative approach (“Config. A”) to achieving v · ∆t M = const. for all
pulses that are simultaneously accepted, and compared it with the proposed solution (“Config. B”) in the
CHESS Technical Report in Sala and Boone 2023, and also with the “conventional” method of generating
neutron pulses with constant pulse width, ∆t M = const. (“Config. C”).
Config. A appears to achieve a greater flexibility in the resolution settings than the other two methods. One
can also always spin disk 1 of the M−chopper at 300 Hz and fall back to Config. C that way. Config. B is
less prone to neutron leakage than Config. A, because it works with fewer slits in the M−chopper. It makes
better use of the variability of chopper phase to achieve what conventionally can only be done with vari-
able slit size. However, before it can be adapted as a technical solution, one should also study if Config. B
implies an increased susceptibility to chopper phase jitter, and if so what the adverse effects would be.
Ultimately, the best technical solution for a chopper system for a CHESS like instrument at STS may be
one that combines ideas from Configs. A and B.
