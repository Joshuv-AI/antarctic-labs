Direct superior ophthalmic vein puncture for cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistula with dual orbital venous outflow: illustrative case
Authors: Nanako Miki, Hideo Chihara, So Matsukawa, Taichi Ikedo, Shigeki Takada, Masakazu Okawa, Takayuki Kikuchi, Chiaki Sakai, Yoshiki Arakawa

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDDirect superior ophthalmic vein (SOV) puncture is a viable alternative for transvenous embolization (TVE) of cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistulas (CSdAVFs), especially when standard routes are inaccessible. Cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistulas (CSdAVFs) are abnormal connections between meningeal arteries and the cavernous sinus, often manifesting with ocular symptoms such as exophthalmos, conjunctival congestion, and cranial nerve symptoms such as diplopia or trigeminal neuropathy resulting from increased venous pressure within the cavernous sinus and orbital veins.1,2 Endovascular treatment represents the mainstay of therapy. However, transarterial embolization has been associated with significant complications, including cranial nerve injury and even cardiac arrest.3 While transvenous embolization (TVE) is the preferred treatment, selecting the appropriate venous access route is critical, especially in patients with unique venous anatomy or drainage patterns.4,5 Direct puncture of the superior ophthalmic vein (SOV) is a well-established alternative in cases in which standard routes are contraindicated or inaccessible.6–9 We present a case of a CSdAVF with dual venous outflow pathways from the orbit, a situation in which preserving venous drainage was a key consideration. After a detailed examination of the venous confluence of these two outflow tracts, direct SOV puncture via the angular vein was selected as the access route that would minimally disrupt overall venous perfusion. This approach enabled effective coil embolization by utilizing only one of the SOV routes, which proved safe as it did not obstruct the alternate pathway, specifically preserving physiological venous drainage through the retromandibular vein. This case highlights the importance of detailed preoperative venous mapping to tailor the access strategy, especially in patients with complex venous anatomy.

Illustrative Case

Patient Information

A 78-year-old man with no significant medical history presented with progressive left-sided exophthalmos, conjunctival injection, and diplopia. These symptoms developed over 1 month. He had no history of trauma or surgery.

Clinical Findings

Ophthalmological evaluation revealed left ocular hyperemia and elevated intraocular pressure (IOP; 21 mm Hg, increasing to 25 mm Hg with upward gaze). Cranial nerve examination showed mild limitation of ocular movement in all directions.

Diagnostic Assessment

MRI demonstrated flow voids in the left cavernous sinus and SOV. Digital subtraction angiography revealed a CSdAVF confined to the anterior compartment of the left cavernous sinus, with arterial supply from the inferolateral trunk and ophthalmic artery, and drainage into the SOV and inferior ophthalmic vein (IOV). The posterior compartment retained normal cortical venous outflow through the inferior petrosal sinus (IPS). The SOV and IOV were continuous and exhibited separate extracranial outflows: the SOV drained via the angular and superficial facial veins (superficial facial vein route), while the IOV drained via the retromandibular vein (retromandibular route) (Fig. 1). The two pathways subsequently converged in the neck, forming a common venous channel that drained towars the external jugular vein, eventually communicating with both the internal cervical vein (ICV) and external cervical vein (ECV), forming a dual drainage pattern (as further detailed in Fig. 2). Given the risk of damaging the preserved posterior compartment or inducing new venous reflux, trans-IPS access was considered suboptimal. Furthermore, due to this confluence into a common channel in the neck, standard transfemoral or transjugular (including transfacial) approaches carried a significant risk of transiently obstructing both orbital outflow routes (the SOV and IOV pathways) during the procedure. Thus, we opted for a direct SOV puncture to minimize the risk of venous congestion and preserve the crucial shunt-related venous outflow via the retromandibular vein. Therapeutic Intervention

Under general anesthesia, a 1.5-cm skin incision was made in the superomedial orbital region. The SOV was identified via its course from the angular vein and directly punctured (Fig. 3). To establish direct access safely, a small skin incision was made to expose the target vein. A key technical pearl in this step is to pass a suture underneath the exposed vein for gentle traction. This maneuver effectively stabilizes the vessel and prevents it from retracting or collapsing when the venotomy is performed. Subsequently, a small slit was created on the vein, and a microguidewire preloaded with a microcatheter was introduced directly into the lumen. Securing the access with the microguidewire first ensures smooth insertion of the microcatheter through the small venous slit. A microcatheter was advanced into the shunt pouch within the anterior cavernous sinus compartment, and coil embolization was performed. Diagnostic catheters were introduced via the right femoral artery and positioned in the left internal and external carotid arteries for intraoperative angiographic monitoring. Intraoperative imaging confirmed that venous drainage through the IOV and retromandibular vein remained intact throughout the procedure. Although the orbital venous outflow became difficult to visualize after final shunt obliteration, the venous phase confirmed preserved normal venous drainage from the IOV into the retromandibular route (Fig. 4). Follow-Up and Outcomes

Postoperative MRI showed resolution of abnormal flow signals in the cavernous sinus and SOV. IOP normalized within 9 days. Exophthalmos, conjunctival congestion, and diplopia resolved completely. No complications or recurrence were observed during the 1-year follow-up.

The necessary informed consent was obtained in this study.

Discussion

Observations

Given the highly variable venous drainage patterns associated with CSdAVF, selecting a TVE access route tailored to the individual patient’s anatomy is essential.10,11 The IPS is the standard route, but when it is occluded, inaccessible, or, as in our case, involved in preserved physiological drainage, alternative strategies are necessary.12,13

Patient Selection and Indications for Direct SOV Puncture

Direct SOV puncture emerges as a critical alternative under specific anatomical and clinical conditions. Based on our case and existing literature, ideal candidates for this approach often present with a combination of the following: 1) a CSdAVF confined to the anterior cavernous sinus compartment;4 2) preserved posterior drainage via the IPS or cortical veins, which must be protected;13, 14 3) dual or alternative venous outflows from the orbit (e.g., via the angular vein and the retromandibular vein);10 4) inaccessible, high-risk, or failed conventional access (i.e., IPS or transfacial routes);6, 15 and 5) progressive ocular symptoms (such as elevated IOP or exophthalmos) requiring rapid and safe decompression.1, 16

The SOV approach, encompassing both the transfemoral-­transfacial vein approach and direct SOV puncture, has been established as a critical and decisive alternative access route in cases of occluded IPS and predominant SOV drainage.8 Studies demonstrating the efficacy of this approach are compelling, with one large series reporting an excellent immediate postoperative complete embolization rate of 97% (31/32) via the trans–femoral vein to SOV approach in patients with IPS occlusion.8 Furthermore, high success rates (ranging from 90% to 100%) have also been reported for direct SOV puncture.15

The anatomical rationale and risk mitigation for the trans–facial vein to SOV approach, a common second-line option, require detailed anatomical understanding.10,11 Orbital venous drainage can be categorized into the superficial facial vein route (type A), the superficial temporal vein route (type B), and the retromandibular (deep facial vein) route (type C).4,10 In our patient, the CSdAVF drained through both the superficial facial vein (type A) and retromandibular vein (type C) routes. These pathways merged into a common channel in the neck (Fig. 2). This confluence posed a significant consideration: standard transfemoral or transjugular navigation with transfemoral-­transfacial vein access to the SOV raised the possibility of simultaneously compromising both outflow pathways during treatment, which could potentially lead to iatrogenic venous congestion and exacerbate ocular symptoms.14 Thus, this anatomical configuration rendered conventional access suboptimal.

Clinically, compromise of established orbital or posterior venous outflow may have two distinct but equally critical consequences. First, redistribution of shunt flow into alternative intracranial pathways may precipitate cortical venous reflux, a recognized risk factor for intracranial hemorrhage and neurological deterioration. Treatment-related alteration of venous drainage has been reported to redirect shunt flow and generate new or worsened reflux patterns. Aguilar Pérez et al. described cavernous redirection of venous drainage after partial transvenous coil occlusion, resulting in a more dangerous venous outflow configuration.17 Similarly, worsening venous congestion and SOV thrombosis following incomplete or palliative embolization have been documented, underscoring the potential for hemodynamic deterioration when dominant drainage pathways are compromised.

Second, interruption of orbital venous drainage leads to elevation of orbital and episcleral venous pressure (EVP), a critical determinant of IOP. When orbital venous outflow is compromised, increased EVP reduces aqueous humor drainage through the trabecular meshwork, directly elevating IOP and predisposing patients to acute ocular hypertension and secondary glaucomatous damage.18 Persistent venous congestion may further impair retinal venous return, resulting in venous stasis retinopathy, intraretinal hemorrhage, macular edema, and, in severe cases, central retinal vein occlusion. Sustained elevation of orbital venous pressure has also been associated with progressive optic neuropathy and irreversible visual decline.19

Collectively, these observations demonstrate that disruption of physiological venous drainage may carry both intracranial and ophthalmic risks, reinforcing the importance of preserving effective collateral outflow during endovascular treatment planning.

With the transfacial vein route already deemed unsafe due to the venous convergence, we considered alternative pathways. A superficial temporal vein–based approach was not suitable because this route did not represent a dominant orbital drainage pathway. Percutaneous transfacial puncture was considered high risk given the deep location and limited visualization of the target vein, while an open transorbital approach was deemed unnecessarily invasive in the setting of a sufficiently dilated SOV. Consequently, direct SOV puncture was chosen to mitigate this risk. While this approach has inherent risks (e.g., intraorbital hemorrhage, globe injury),15,16 these can be managed with meticulous technique. Reported methods include the open transorbital approach,6,15 CT-guided access,20,21 or ultrasound-guided access.22 In our case, the SOV was sufficiently dilated, and we opted for direct visualization via a small skin incision to expose the angular vein, ensuring accurate, safe microcatheter introduction without a sheath.

A key therapeutic advantage in our patient was that the CSdAVF was limited to the anterior compartment, with normal posterior outflow preserved. The dual drainage system was confirmed preoperatively. Our strategy of puncturing the angular vein allowed access to the shunt, which was anatomically distal, while leaving the IOV-retromandibular drainage pathway completely unobstructed. This preserved outflow was confirmed angiographically throughout the procedure (Fig. 4). This approach provided a minimally disruptive, anatomically favorable route that directly addressed the fistula while preventing the hemodynamic complications associated with other access strategies.1

Lessons

Individualized venous access planning based on detailed preoperative imaging is essential for safe and effective CSdAVF treatment. When the anterior cavernous sinus is isolated and posterior drainage preserved, direct SOV puncture offers a hemodynamically sound alternative that maintains physiological venous outflow and minimizes orbital complications.

It is important to emphasize that direct SOV puncture is an invasive and technically demanding procedure. It should be considered an alternative only when conventional, less invasive approaches (such as trans-IPS or transfemoral-transfacial approach) are excluded due to anatomical inaccessibility, high risk, or safety concerns.1123,24 The approach is not suitable for all patients, particularly those with small or inaccessible angular veins or when the SOV is not sufficiently dilated.10,15 Potential complications, although manageable, include orbital hemorrhage and ocular muscle injury.14,16 Furthermore, this approach may not be as cost-effective compared to standard transfemoral access. Future comparative studies should evaluate long-term outcomes across different approaches, with a particular focus on visual prognosis as it relates to any intraprocedural impairment of orbital venous perfusion.2,5

This case emphasizes two critical considerations: 1) the necessity of meticulous preoperative evaluation of shunt-related venous outflow and 2) that a direct SOV puncture via angular vein access provides a safe and effective route for embolization in carefully selected patients. This tailored approach, indicated in patients with complex orbital drainage and preserved posterior outflow, minimizes iatrogenic risk and represents a valuable strategy for CSdAVF cases with similar challenging anatomy. Further studies comparing outcomes relative to other routes in patients with similar venous compartmentalization would be beneficial.
