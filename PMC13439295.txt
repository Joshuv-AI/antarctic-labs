Restoring the barrier: endoscopic antireflux mucosal interventions for intractable post-peroral endoscopic myotomy reflux
Authors: Do Hoon Kim
Published: 2026

Peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) has been established as the first-line endoscopic treatment for achalasia, offering efficacy comparable to or superior to that of laparoscopic Heller myotomy (LHM) while remaining a minimally invasive, incisionless approach.1 Its adoption over the past decade reflects its technical reproducibility, excellent symptomatic relief, and favorable outcomes across all achalasia subtypes. However, the absence of a concomitant antireflux procedure, an inherent limitation of the purely endoscopic nature of POEM, has long been recognized as its Achilles heel.2

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is the most prevalent and clinically consequential complication following POEM. Its reported incidence varies widely with the diagnostic metric, from approximately 20% based on symptom assessment to over 50% based on objective measures such as 24-hour pH monitoring or endoscopic esophagitis grading.3 Although most patients respond to proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy, a subset develops intractable or medically refractory GERD with potentially serious sequelae. Uncontrolled acid exposure can result in progressive mucosal injury, Barrett's esophagus, and cicatricial stricture at the esophagogastric junction (EGJ), which is a feared cause of late procedural failure that paradoxically impairs swallowing and negates the benefits of myotomy.4

In recognition of this risk, considerable efforts have focused on reducing reflux by refining POEM techniques. Preservation of sling fibers has been a particular focus. Anterior myotomy is advocated for its capacity to spare these fibers, whereas posterior myotomy aims to circumvent them entirely, thereby reducing the disruption of the angle of His and gastric flap valve.5 One of the more innovative refinements is the “antireflux POEM,” which incorporates intentional preservation of the sling musculature. Despite these modifications, post-POEM GERD continues to occur in a substantial proportion of patients, highlighting the multifactorial nature of EGJ incompetence following myotomy.

When GERD develops after POEM, the standard of care typically consists of long-term acid suppression with PPIs.6 At the same time, the prospect of indefinite therapy in what is often a young and otherwise healthy population has prompted interest in the long-term profile of PPIs. A range of associations have been reported, including hypomagnesemia, increased susceptibility to enteric and respiratory infections, impaired calcium and iron absorption, and chronic kidney disease. However, these associations are largely derived from observational data and mostly have unconfirmed causality. Rather than arguing against PPI use, these considerations invite thoughtful deliberation when treatment is expected to continue indefinitely.

Beyond pharmacological safety concerns, there is a fundamental pathophysiological argument for non-pharmacological interventions. While conventional GERD is driven primarily by transient lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxations, which are episodic, reflex-mediated events, post-POEM GERD arises from structural compromise of the EGJ.7 Myotomy creates a permanent, fixed impairment of the LES, producing constitutive sphincter incompetence rather than intermittent relaxation. Thus, acid suppression addresses the mucosal consequences of reflux while leaving the anatomical defect unresolved. This distinction has direct therapeutic implications: patients with refractory post-POEM GERD may be better served by interventions that physically reconstruct or reinforce the antireflux barrier, analogous in principle to antireflux procedures performed concurrently with LHM. Antireflux mucosectomy (ARMS), antireflux mucosal ablation (ARMA), and Stretta radiofrequency (SRF) all share the principle of augmenting LES pressure through submucosal scarring and fibrosis-mediated remodeling of the EGJ.8

In this context, the study by Lee et al.9 in this issue of Clinical Endoscopy makes a timely contribution. To the authors’ knowledge, their study is the first cohort study to directly compare endoscopic antireflux interventions for intractable post-POEM GERD, evaluating three distinct approaches: ARMA using argon plasma coagulation, ARMS, and SRF. ARMS showed the most pronounced and statistically significant improvement in subjective symptom burden as measured by the GERD Questionnaire (GERD-Q) score at six months (p=0.041), despite being preferentially applied to patients with the most severe symptoms. SRF achieved the highest PPI withdrawal rate (100%), likely reflecting the inclusion of patients with milder reflux, whereas ARMA showed intermediate results. Adverse events, including bleeding and stricture, occurred exclusively in the mucosa-targeting groups (ARMA and ARMS), highlighting the need for caution when performing tissue-disrupting procedures at the post-myotomy EGJ, where dysphagia from excessive fibrosis is a non-trivial risk. The study also identified type III achalasia and posterior myotomy orientation as potential risk factors for reintervention, findings that may inform procedural planning and surveillance.

This study has some limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design and small per-group sample size limit its statistical power to draw definitive conclusions regarding comparative efficacy. Heterogeneity in baseline GERD-Q scores and EndoFLIP (Medtronic) distensibility indices introduces the potential for selection bias and may confound interpretation, while the absence of post-procedural 24-hour pH impedance monitoring in most patients precludes a robust objective assessment of reflux control. The 6-month follow-up period was also insufficient to assess the durability of antireflux remodeling, which is a salient concern given the opposing biomechanical objectives of POEM and subsequent fibrosis-promoting interventions.

Nevertheless, these limitations are largely inherent to the subject matter of this study. The rarity of clinically intractable post-POEM GERD makes accumulating meaningful patient numbers difficult. The contribution of this study lies in providing systematic data for a population that has remained understudied despite its clinical importance. By demonstrating that endoscopic antireflux interventions are technically feasible, associated with acceptable safety profiles, and capable of producing meaningful symptomatic improvement in this setting, this study offers a valuable proof-of-concept and provides a directional framework for future work.10 Its findings support the need for larger, multicenter, prospective, randomized trials with objective reflux endpoints and longer follow-up to establish evidence-based treatment algorithms for intractable post-POEM GERD. At the same time, the current evidence base is confined to small, retrospective series with relatively short follow-up and thus remains preliminary. Therefore, ARMS, ARMA, and SRF should be regarded as emerging or investigational options, rather than established therapies, to be applied with corresponding caution until higher-level evidence becomes available.

As POEM continues to gain global acceptance as the endoscopic treatment of choice for achalasia, the field must mature beyond its focus on procedure-related GERD incidence and turn toward the systematic management of this complication, particularly in patients for whom pharmacological therapy proves insufficient. Careful patient selection will be central to this effort: at present, endoscopic antireflux interventions appear most appropriate for the subset with objectively confirmed reflux that persists despite optimal PPI therapy or for those otherwise committed to indefinite acid suppression, particularly younger patients, in whom the burden of lifelong medical treatment is greatest. This study represents an early but valuable step in this direction.
