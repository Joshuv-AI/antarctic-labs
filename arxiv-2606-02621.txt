1. Introduction

The accelerated Collatz map

T(n)=\begin{cases}n/2,&n\equiv 0\pmod{2},\\[2.0pt]
(3n+1)/2,&n\equiv 1\pmod{2},\end{cases}

defined over
\mathbb{Z}
, is conjectured to drive every positive integer
to the cycle
1\leftrightarrow 2
, but no proof is known

[
6
,
7
]
. Probabilistic methods

[
9
,
10
,
7
]
describe average
trajectory behaviour; the deepest partial result is due to
Tao
[
8
]
, who showed that almost all positive integers
eventually reach arbitrarily small values.

The main result of this paper is the following. For each
m\geq 1
,
among the odd integers in
\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
, exactly
F(m+1)

have the property that their orbit under
T
avoids the residue
class
4\pmod{6}
during steps
2,\ldots,m
, where
F(m+1)
is the

(m+1)
-th Fibonacci number (Theorem
4.7
); the
proportion decays at the exponential rate
(\varphi/2)^{m}
,

\varphi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
. The connection is structural: the entries
of the transfer matrix of
\mathcal{H}_{4}
are themselves Fibonacci
numbers (Proposition
4.10
).

Fibonacci numbers have appeared previously in connection with the Collatz map:
Albert, Gudmundsson, and Ulfarsson
[
1
]

showed that Collatz permutations of length up to
14
are enumerated by the
Fibonacci numbers, a result that arises from counting word types over
\{u,d\}
.
Our Fibonacci count is independent in both origin and mechanism: it follows
from a spectral argument on the modular graph
G^{\prime}
, where the golden ratio

\varphi
appears as the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue of the avoiding
subgraph
\mathcal{H}_{4}
.

The proof rests on two parallel objects, one binary and one modular.
On the binary side, we use the parity encoding

C^{m}(n)=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})
of the first
m
iterates and its associated

corrective residue

R(C^{m}(n))
(Lemma
2.1
).
The key identity is
T^{m}(n)=(3^{s}n+R)/2^{m}
. Since
C^{m}(n)

depends on
n
only through its residue modulo
2^{m}
,
R
is constant
on residue classes modulo
2^{m}
. Algebraically
equivalent identities appear in Terras
[
9
]
,
Böhm–Sontacchi
[
3
]
, and
Lagarias
[
6
]
.
The map
\Phi_{m}:n\mapsto C^{m}(n)

is a bijection from
\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
onto
\{0,1\}^{m}
, due to
Everett
[
4
]
and
Bernstein–Lagarias
[
2
]
, and satisfies
periodicity
C^{m}(n+2^{m})=C^{m}(n)
and antisymmetry

c_{m+1}(n+2^{m})=\overline{c_{m+1}(n)}
. Together, these properties
allow the entire analysis to be conducted at the level of binary
encodings, without reference to the specific value of
n
.

The modular analogue encodes the dynamics modulo
6
— the smallest
modulus that simultaneously distinguishes parity and the image of the
odd branch of
T
— via a directed graph
G
with vertex set

\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}
and twelve edges. Modular Collatz graphs for general
modulus
N
are defined in
[
5
]
, where the case
N=6

appears as an explicit example. The present paper develops a different
treatment: we construct a bijection

\Psi_{m}:\{1,\ldots,6\cdot 2^{m}\}\to\mathcal{P}_{m}(G)
onto directed paths
of length
m
in
G
, satisfying periodicity and antisymmetry properties
that parallel those of the binary encoding
\Phi_{m}
. To our knowledge,
this bijection and its structural properties do not appear in prior work.

The graph
G
has a unique absorbing strongly connected component

G^{\prime}=G[\{1,2,4,5\}]
(Proposition
3.1
); odd integer orbits
are confined to
G^{\prime}
from step
2
onwards
(Corollary
4.5
). The Fibonacci
count arises from a spectral gap: the avoiding subgraph

\mathcal{H}_{4}=G^{\prime}[\{1,2,5\}]
, obtained by removing vertex

, has

\rho(\mathcal{H}_{4})=\varphi
, while
\rho(G^{\prime})=2
, and the ratio

\varphi/2<1
is the exact exponential decay rate (Corollary

). A modular path
theorem (Theorem
4.1
) describes the action of
the displacement

on modular paths, with the
consequence that odd cycles of length

are separated by at least

6\cdot 2^{m}
in any arithmetic progression of common difference

(Corollary
4.2
).

More generally, removing any vertex
v
from

reduces the spectral

2
. The exact values,

\rho(\mathcal{H}_{2})=1
,
\rho(\mathcal{H}_{5})=\sqrt{2}
,

form a hierarchy

governing the exponential
decay of the corresponding avoiding densities
(Theorem

). In particular, no positive
cycle of

can avoid vertex

(Corollary
4.17
). A flow conservation identity
in

further forces vertex

to account for more than

).

The paper is organized as follows: Section

and

2. Binary and modular encodings

We establish the two parallel bijections
\Phi_{m}
and
\Psi_{m}
,
together with their shared periodicity and antisymmetry properties,
on which the proofs of Section
4
depend.

To each integer
n
and level
m\geq 1
we associate the
binary encoding

C^{m}(n)=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})\in\{0,1\}^{m},\qquad c_{i}=T^{i-1}(n)\bmod 2,

recording the parities of
n,T(n),\ldots,T^{m-1}(n)
. The
Hamming weight

s=s(C^{m}(n))=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}
counts the odd steps, and

r(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{i}c_{j}
denotes the partial weight up to position
i
.

We define the
corrective residue
by

R(C^{m}(n)):=2^{m}\,T^{m}(n)-3^{s}\,n,

(1)

which satisfies the structural identity

T^{m}(n)=\frac{3^{s}\,n+R(C^{m}(n))}{2^{m}}.

(2)

Algebraically equivalent expressions appear in
[
9
,
3
,
6
]
; in particular,

[
3
, Prop. 4]
gives, for the accelerated map

u(x)=(3x+1)/2
, a closed form whose numerator coincides with

R(C^{m}(n))
under the standard correspondence between gap-sequences
and binary encodings. Since
C^{m}(n)
depends on
n
only through its
residue modulo
2^{m}
(Proposition
2.3
(B,P) below),

R
is constant on residue classes modulo
2^{m}
; the bijection

\Phi_{m}
then allows the analysis to be conducted entirely at the
level of encodings, with no reference to the specific value of
n
,
which drives the parallel modular construction below.

Lemma 2.1
.

For each
m\geq 1
, the corrective residue depends solely on
C^{m}(n)=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})
and is
given explicitly by

R(C^{m}(n))=\sum_{i=1}^{m}3^{\,s-r(i)}\,2^{\,i-1}\,c_{i}.

(3)

Moreover, it satisfies the recursive law:

R(C^{m+1}(n))=\begin{cases}R(C^{m}(n)),&c_{m+1}=0,\\
3\,R(C^{m}(n))+2^{m},&c_{m+1}=1.\end{cases}

Proof.

By induction on
m
. The base case
m=1
is immediate:
c_{1}=0
gives

R=0
, and
c_{1}=1
gives
R=1
, both matching the formula.

For the inductive step, suppose

R(C^{m}(n))=\sum_{i=1}^{m}3^{\,s-r(i)}\,2^{\,i-1}\,c_{i}

holds at level
m
. We prove it at level
m+1
.

Case
c_{m+1}=0
.
Then
s^{\prime}=s
,
r^{\prime}(i)=r(i)
for all
i\leq m+1
,
and
c_{m+1}=0
, so the new term in the sum vanishes:

\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}3^{\,s^{\prime}-r^{\prime}(i)}\,2^{\,i-1}\,c_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}3^{\,s-r(i)}\,2^{\,i-1}\,c_{i}=R(C^{m}).

On the other hand,
T^{m+1}(n)=T^{m}(n)/2
, so

R(C^{m+1})=2^{m+1}T^{m+1}(n)-3^{s^{\prime}}n=2^{m}T^{m}(n)-3^{s}n=R(C^{m})
.
Both sides agree.

Case
c_{m+1}=1
.
Then
s^{\prime}=s+1
and
r^{\prime}(i)=r(i)
for
i\leq m
,

r^{\prime}(m+1)=s+1
. The new term at position
m+1
contributes

3^{s^{\prime}-r^{\prime}(m+1)}\,2^{m}\cdot 1=3^{0}\cdot 2^{m}=2^{m}
.
Each existing term at position
i\leq m
acquires an extra factor of
3
,
since
s^{\prime}-r^{\prime}(i)=(s+1)-r(i)=(s-r(i))+1
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}3^{\,s^{\prime}-r^{\prime}(i)}\,2^{\,i-1}\,c_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}3^{\,(s-r(i))+1}\,2^{\,i-1}\,c_{i}+2^{m}=3\sum_{i=1}^{m}3^{\,s-r(i)}\,2^{\,i-1}\,c_{i}+2^{m}

\displaystyle=3R(C^{m})+2^{m}.

On the other hand,
T^{m+1}(n)=(3T^{m}(n)+1)/2
, so

\displaystyle R(C^{m+1})

\displaystyle=2^{m+1}T^{m+1}(n)-3^{s+1}n=2^{m}(3T^{m}(n)+1)-3\cdot 3^{s}n

\displaystyle=3(2^{m}T^{m}(n)-3^{s}n)+2^{m}=3R(C^{m})+2^{m}.

Both sides equal
3R(C^{m})+2^{m}
, completing the induction. The recursive
law is the
c_{m+1}
-case split just established.
∎

Proposition 2.3

(Bijectivity (B) and Periodicity (P))
.

For each
m\geq 1
:

(B)

The map
n\mapsto C^{m}(n)
is a bijection from

\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
onto
\{0,1\}^{m}
. Equivalently,

C^{m}(n_{1})=C^{m}(n_{2})\Leftrightarrow n_{1}\equiv n_{2}\pmod{2^{m}}
.

(P)

The encoding has exact period
2^{m}
:

C^{m}(n+2^{m})=C^{m}(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

We prove (P) first, as (B) depends on it.

Periodicity (P).
By induction on
m
.

For
m=1
:
c_{1}(n)=n\bmod 2
has period
2=2^{1}
, so
c_{1}(n+2)=c_{1}(n)
.

Assume (P) holds at level
m
, i.e.
C^{m}(n+2^{m})=C^{m}(n)
for all
n
.
We prove it at level
m+1
. Since
2^{m+1}
is a multiple of
2^{m}
,
the inductive hypothesis gives
C^{m}(n+2^{m+1})=C^{m}(n)
, so

c_{i}(n+2^{m+1})=c_{i}(n)
for
i=1,\ldots,m
. It remains to show

c_{m+1}(n+2^{m+1})=c_{m+1}(n)
. By (
2
) applied at
level
m
,

T^{m}(n+2^{m+1})=\frac{3^{s}(n+2^{m+1})+R}{2^{m}}=T^{m}(n)+2\cdot 3^{s},

where
s
and
R
are the same for
n
and
n+2^{m+1}
by the inductive
hypothesis. Since
2\cdot 3^{s}
is even,
T^{m}(n+2^{m+1})
and
T^{m}(n)
have
the same parity, hence
c_{m+1}(n+2^{m+1})=c_{m+1}(n)
.

Bijectivity (B).
Injectivity: if
C^{m}(n_{1})=C^{m}(n_{2})
, then
s
and

R
are the same for both, so
T^{m}(n_{1})=(3^{s}n_{1}+R)/2^{m}
and

T^{m}(n_{2})=(3^{s}n_{2}+R)/2^{m}
. Subtracting gives
3^{s}(n_{1}-n_{2})\equiv 0\pmod{2^{m}}
.
Since
\gcd(3^{s},2^{m})=1
, we have
n_{1}\equiv n_{2}\pmod{2^{m}}
. As
n_{1},n_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
, this gives
n_{1}=n_{2}
. Surjectivity follows from
injectivity, since both domain and codomain have exactly
2^{m}
elements.
∎

We write
\Phi_{m}:\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
for this bijection
henceforth.

Lemma 2.4

(Antisymmetry (A))
.

For each
m\geq 1
and all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

c_{m+1}(n+2^{m})=\overline{c_{m+1}(n)},

where
\overline{c}:=1-c
denotes the binary complement of
c\in\{0,1\}
.
The shift
+2^{m}
preserves the entire level-
m
encoding and inverts
precisely the
(m+1)
-th entry.

Proof.

By (P),
C^{m}(n+2^{m})=C^{m}(n)
, so both share the same weight
s

and residue
R
at level
m
. Then (
2
) gives

T^{m}(n+2^{m})=\frac{3^{s}(n+2^{m})+R}{2^{m}}=\frac{3^{s}n+R}{2^{m}}+3^{s}=T^{m}(n)+3^{s}.

Since
3^{s}
is odd,
T^{m}(n+2^{m})
and
T^{m}(n)
have opposite parities;
hence
c_{m+1}(n+2^{m})=\overline{c_{m+1}(n)}
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,

,

T^{m}(n\pm t\cdot 2^{m+k})=T^{m}(n)\pm t\cdot 2^{k}\cdot 3^{s}.

This generalises (P) and is used in Section

to
control how

responds to arithmetic displacements at scales

.

Proof.

By (P),
C^{m}(n\pm t\cdot 2^{m+k})=C^{m}(n)
, so (

)
gives
T^{m}(n\pm t\cdot 2^{m+k})=(3^{s}(n\pm t\cdot 2^{m+k})+R)/2^{m}=T^{m}(n)\pm t\cdot 2^{k}\cdot 3^{s}
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

For
m\geq 1
and

, the condition

is equivalent to

n=\frac{R(C^{m}(n))}{2^{m}-3^{s}}.

(4)

Note that

Equation (

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\to 0,\;0\to 3,\quad 1\to 2,\;1\to 5,\quad 2\to 1,\;2\to 4,

Theorem 2.13

\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}
if its terminal
vertex has parity
\varepsilon
, i.e. type
0
if the next iterate
is even and type
1
if it is odd. Since the vertices
of
G
are the residue classes
V=\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}
, each vertex

a\in V
has exactly two outgoing edges; by
Proposition
2.8
the two successors of
a

have opposite parities, so one outgoing edge has type
0
and the
other has type
1
. Hence for each
a\in V
and
\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}

there is a unique edge of type
\varepsilon
leaving
a
; we
denote it
e(a,\varepsilon)
.

Construction of
\Psi_{m}
.

Given
k\in\{1,\dots,6\cdot 2^{m}\}
, perform the Euclidean division

k-1=a\cdot 2^{m}+r,\qquad a\in\{0,\dots,5\},\quad r\in\{0,\dots,2^{m}-1\},

and write
r
in base-
2
representation with exactly
m
digits:

r=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{i}\,2^{m-i}
,
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.
Define
\Psi_{m}(k):=(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(G)
by

e_{1}:=e(a,\varepsilon_{1}),\qquad e_{i}:=e(b_{i-1},\varepsilon_{i})\quad\text{for }i\geq 2,

where
b_{i-1}
denotes the terminal vertex of
e_{i-1}
.
This is well defined: each
e_{i}
is determined by the terminal vertex of the
previous edge and the type
\varepsilon_{i}
, both uniquely specified.

Injectivity.

Suppose
\Psi_{m}(k)=\Psi_{m}(k^{\prime})
, so
(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})=(e_{1}^{\prime},\dots,e_{m}^{\prime})
.
Write
e_{1}=(a,b_{1})
and
e_{1}^{\prime}=(a^{\prime},b_{1}^{\prime})
.
From
e_{1}=e_{1}^{\prime}
we read off
a=a^{\prime}
.
Since
e(a,0)\neq e(a,1)
(distinct types give distinct edges),

e_{1}=e_{1}^{\prime}
also forces
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}^{\prime}
.
By induction: if
\varepsilon_{j}=\varepsilon_{j}^{\prime}
for all

, then
the terminal vertex of

equals that of

\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}
.
Hence
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}

and

.

Surjectivity.

Let
(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(G)
be any path. Write

and set

as its initial vertex. For each

, write

and define
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{0,1\}
as the unique type such that

Set

(since

.
∎

Lemma 2.14
.

Let

. For all

,

\mathcal{G}_{m}\bigl(n+6\cdot 2^{m}\bigr)=\mathcal{G}_{m}(n),

(Definition

Set

Remark 2.15
.

The bijection

satisfies the same antisymmetry as

(Structural parallel between

The simple cycles of

\;0\to 0

•

\;2\to 1\to 2

Proposition 3.4
.

Let

denote the adjacency matrix of the effective subgraph

4. Modular path structure and the Fibonacci theorem

The following theorem describes the exact action of the displacement
+2^{m}

on every level of the modular encoding simultaneously.

Theorem 4.1

(Modular path theorem)
.

Let
m\geq 1
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
1\leq i\leq m
.
Set
d:=(m+i)\bmod 2
. Then:

(i)

Displacement
+2^{m}
.

h_{i}(n+2^{m})\equiv\begin{cases}h_{i}(n)\pmod{6}&\text{if }2^{i-1}\nmid n,\\[4.0pt]
h_{i}(n)+2\pmod{6}&\text{if }2^{i-1}\mid n\text{ and }d=0,\\[4.0pt]
h_{i}(n)+4\pmod{6}&\text{if }2^{i-1}\mid n\text{ and }d=1.\end{cases}

(ii)

Displacement
+2^{m+1}
.

h_{i}(n+2^{m+1})\equiv\begin{cases}h_{i}(n)\pmod{6}&\text{if }2^{i-1}\nmid n,\\[4.0pt]
h_{i}(n)+4\pmod{6}&\text{if }2^{i-1}\mid n\text{ and }d=0,\\[4.0pt]
h_{i}(n)+2\pmod{6}&\text{if }2^{i-1}\mid n\text{ and }d=1.\end{cases}

In particular,
h_{i}(n+2^{m})=h_{i}(n)
if and only if
2^{i-1}\nmid n
.

Proof.

For
i=1
the claim is immediate:
2^{0}\mid n
always, and

h_{1}(n+2^{m})\equiv h_{1}(n)+2^{m}\pmod{6}
with
2^{m}\equiv 2
(
m
odd) or
4

(
m
even), matching (i); the displacement
2^{m+1}
gives (ii) likewise.
For
i\geq 2
, apply the translation lemma (Lemma
2.5
) at level
i-1
with displacement
2^{m}
:

T^{i-1}(n+2^{m})-T^{i-1}(n)=2^{m-i+1}\cdot 3^{s_{i-1}(n)},

where
s_{i-1}(n)
is the Hamming weight of the binary encoding
C^{i-1}(n)
.
Note that
s_{i-1}(n)=0
if and only if
c_{1}=\cdots=c_{i-1}=0
, i.e.
n,T(n),\ldots,T^{i-2}(n)
are all even, which holds if and only if

2^{i-1}\mid n
. Hence
s_{i-1}(n)\geq 1
if and only if
2^{i-1}\nmid n
.

Case
s_{i-1}(n)\geq 1
, i.e.
2^{i-1}\nmid n
.

Since
3^{k}\equiv 3\pmod{6}
for all
k\geq 1
, we have

2^{m-i+1}\cdot 3\equiv 0\pmod{6}
for
i\leq m
(as
m-i+1\geq 1
).
Hence
h_{i}(n+2^{m})\equiv h_{i}(n)\pmod{6}
.

Case
s_{i-1}(n)=0
, i.e.
2^{i-1}\mid n
.

Then
3^{0}=1
and the difference equals
2^{m-i+1}
.
Since
(m-i+1)+(m+i)=2m+1
is odd, the parities of
m-i+1
and
m+i
are
always opposite. Therefore:

2^{m-i+1}\bmod 6=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }m-i+1\equiv 1\pmod{2},\quad\text{i.e.\ }d\equiv 0\pmod{2},\\
4&\text{if }m-i+1\equiv 0\pmod{2},\quad\text{i.e.\ }d\equiv 1\pmod{2},\end{cases}

giving case (i). For case (ii), apply the translation lemma with
displacement
2^{m+1}
: the difference is

2^{m-i+2}\cdot 3^{s_{i-1}(n)}
. When
2^{i-1}\nmid n
the argument
is unchanged (the difference is
\equiv 0\pmod{6}
). When
2^{i-1}\mid n

the difference is
2^{m-i+2}=2\cdot 2^{m-i+1}
. Since

2^{m-i+1}\bmod 6\in\{2,4\}
, multiplying by
2
gives

2\cdot 2\equiv 4
and
2\cdot 4\equiv 2\pmod{6}
, swapping the two values.
Hence the shifts
+2
and
+4
interchange, giving case (ii).
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
n
be an odd integer and
m\geq 1
. If
T^{m}(n)=n
, then

T^{m}(n+k\cdot 2^{m})\neq n+k\cdot 2^{m}
for all
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{6}
.

Proof.

Since
n
is odd,
s=s(C^{m}(n))\geq 1
(as
c_{1}=1
). By
Lemma
2.5
,

T^{m}(n+k\cdot 2^{m})=T^{m}(n)+k\cdot 3^{s},

so
h_{m+1}(n+k\cdot 2^{m})\equiv h_{m+1}(n)+3k\pmod{6}
(using
3^{s}\equiv 3\pmod{6}

for
s\geq 1
).

By Theorem
4.1
(i) with
i=1
(so
d=(m+1)\bmod 2
),
each displacement
+2^{m}
shifts
h_{1}
by
\delta
where
\delta=2
if
d=0

and
\delta=4
if
d=1
. Since
n+j\cdot 2^{m}
is odd for every
j
, the
same shift applies at each step, giving

h_{1}(n+k\cdot 2^{m})\equiv h_{1}(n)+k\delta\pmod{6}.

The cycle condition

gives
h_{m+1}(n)=h_{1}(n)
. For

to be a cycle of length

, we need
h_{m+1}(n+k\cdot 2^{m})=h_{1}(n+k\cdot 2^{m})
,
i.e.
k\cdot 3\equiv k\cdot\delta\pmod{6}
, i.e.
k(\delta-3)\equiv 0\pmod{6}
.
Since
\delta\in\{2,4\}
, we have
\delta-3\in\{-1,1\}
, and

\gcd(\delta-3,6)=1
, so this holds if and only if

.
Thus for all

,
n+k\cdot 2^{m}
is not a cycle of length
m
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Among any six consecutive elements

.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let

,

, and let

,

For levels

For

.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
n
be an odd integer and

. Then

for all

. Equivalently, the vertices

and

of

(Proposition

For odd

We verify that the subgraph induced by

,

,

,

,

∎

Theorem 4.7

(Fibonacci count of vertex-
4
-avoiding paths)
.

Let
F(m)
denote the Fibonacci sequence with
F(1)=F(2)=1
.
For each
\ell\geq 0
, the number of paths of length
\ell
in
\mathcal{H}_{4}

that start in
\{2,5\}
is exactly
F(\ell+3)
.

Consequently, the number of odd integers
n\in\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
whose
modular path
H^{m}(n)
avoids vertex
4
at every level
i\geq 2
is
exactly
F(m+1)
(see Example
4.8
for the case
m=4
).

Proof.

Step 1: Paths in
\mathcal{H}_{4}
.

For each vertex
v\in\{1,2,5\}
and each
\ell\geq 0
, let
P_{\ell}(v)
denote
the number of paths of length
\ell
in
\mathcal{H}_{4}
starting at
v
.
The edges of
\mathcal{H}_{4}
are
1\to\{2,5\}
,
2\to 1
, and
5\to\{2,5\}
,
so for
\ell\geq 1

\displaystyle P_{\ell}(2)

\displaystyle=P_{\ell-1}(1),

\displaystyle P_{\ell}(1)

\displaystyle=P_{\ell-1}(2)+P_{\ell-1}(5),

\displaystyle P_{\ell}(5)

\displaystyle=P_{\ell-1}(2)+P_{\ell-1}(5),

with
P_{0}(v)=1
for all
v
. In particular
P_{\ell}(1)=P_{\ell}(5)
for all

\ell\geq 0
. Write
f_{\ell}:=P_{\ell}(2)
and
g_{\ell}:=P_{\ell}(5)
. Then

f_{\ell}=g_{\ell-1}
and

g_{\ell}=f_{\ell-1}+g_{\ell-1}=g_{\ell-2}+g_{\ell-1}
, with
g_{0}=1
and

g_{1}=2
, giving
g_{\ell}=F(\ell+2)
and
f_{\ell}=F(\ell+1)
.
The total number of paths of length
\ell
starting in
\{2,5\}
is

f_{\ell}+g_{\ell}=F(\ell+1)+F(\ell+2)=F(\ell+3).

Step 2: Odd integers.

For
m=1
the only odd integer in
\{1,2\}
is
n=1
, for which the
condition is vacuous, giving the count
F(2)=1
; assume henceforth

m\geq 2
.
By Corollary
4.5
,
h_{2}(n)\in\{2,5\}
for every
odd
n
. The suffix
(h_{2},\ldots,h_{m})
consists of
m-1
vertices connected
by
m-2
consecutive edges in
G^{\prime}
, so it determines a path of
length
m-2
in
G^{\prime}
starting from
\{2,5\}
. By
Corollary
4.4
, this suffix depends only on
n

and not on which element of the progression
n+k\cdot 2^{m}
is chosen,
so the map
\phi:n\mapsto(h_{2},\ldots,h_{m})
is well defined on odd
integers in
\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
.

Injectivity of
\phi
.
If
\phi(n)=\phi(n^{\prime})
, then

h_{i}(n)=h_{i}(n^{\prime})
for
i=2,\ldots,m
. Since
h_{i}\equiv c_{i}\pmod{2}
,
we get
c_{i}(n)=c_{i}(n^{\prime})
for
i=2,\ldots,m
. Both
n,n^{\prime}
are odd,
so
c_{1}(n)=c_{1}(n^{\prime})=1
, giving
C^{m}(n)=C^{m}(n^{\prime})
. By
Proposition
2.3
(B),
n=n^{\prime}
in
\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
.

Avoiding vertex
4
.
The integer
n
avoids vertex
4
at
every level
i\geq 2
if and only if
h_{i}(n)\neq 4
for
i=2,\ldots,m
,
which holds if and only if
(h_{2},\ldots,h_{m})
is a path in
\mathcal{H}_{4}
.
Note that
i=1
is excluded from the condition: for odd
n
,

h_{1}(n)\in\{1,3,5\}
and the condition
h_{1}(n)\neq 4
holds automatically.
Thus the number of odd integers avoiding vertex
4
is at most

f_{m-2}+g_{m-2}=F(m-1)+F(m)=F(m+1)
by Step 1.

Surjectivity.
Every path
(h_{2},\ldots,h_{m})
of length
m-2

in
\mathcal{H}_{4}
starting from
\{2,5\}
is realised. Indeed,
the string
(1,c_{2},\ldots,c_{m})
with
c_{i}=h_{i}\bmod 2
determines
a unique odd
n\in\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
by bijectivity (B) with

(h_{j}(n))_{j=2}^{m}=(h_{j})_{j=2}^{m}
by construction: once
h_{2}(n)\in\{2,5\}

is fixed by
c_{2}
, the relation
c_{i}\equiv h_{i}\pmod{2}
determines each
subsequent
h_{i}
uniquely, since from every vertex of
G
there is exactly
one even and one odd successor (Proposition
2.8
).
Hence
\phi
is a bijection and the count equals
F(m+1)
.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

The proportion of odd integers in
\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
whose modular path
avoids vertex
4
during steps
2,\ldots,m
is

\frac{F(m+1)}{2^{m-1}}\;\sim\;\frac{2\varphi}{\sqrt{5}}\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right)^{m},

where
\varphi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2

\psi=(1-\sqrt{5})/2
, so
F(m+1)\sim\varphi^{m+1}/\sqrt{5}
as

). Therefore

F(m+1)/2^{m-1}\sim(2\varphi/\sqrt{5})\cdot(\varphi/2)^{m}\to 0
since

\varphi/2<1
. Equivalently, by Proposition

, the decay

\varphi/2
is the ratio of the spectral radii

\rho(\mathcal{H}_{4})/\rho(G^{\prime})=\varphi/2
of the avoiding subgraph
and the effective subgraph.
∎

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
F_{n}
denote the Fibonacci sequence with
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=1
, extended
by
F_{-1}=1

(so
F_{n}=F(n+1)
in the notation of Theorem
4.7
). In vertex
order
(1,2,5)
, the
n
-th power of the adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{H}_{4}

is

A_{\mathcal{H}_{4}}^{\,n}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{n-1}&F_{n}&F_{n}\\
F_{n-2}&F_{n-1}&F_{n-1}\\
F_{n-1}&F_{n}&F_{n}\end{pmatrix},\qquad n\geq 1.

In particular:

(i)

Every entry of
A_{\mathcal{H}_{4}}^{\,n}
is a Fibonacci
number and satisfies the Fibonacci recurrence in
n
.

(ii)

\operatorname{tr}(A_{\mathcal{H}_{4}}^{\,n})=2F_{n-1}+F_{n}=L_{n}
, the
n
-th Lucas number.

(iii)

The sum of all entries of
A_{\mathcal{H}_{4}}^{\,n}

is
F_{n+4}
.

The same holds for
A_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
(in vertex order
(2,4,5)
), by
the isomorphism
\mathcal{H}_{4}\cong\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

Proof.

The adjacency matrix is

A=A_{\mathcal{H}_{4}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\end{pmatrix}.

Since rows
1
and
3
of
A
are identical, every power
A^{n}
inherits
this symmetry: rows
1
and
3
of
A^{n}
are always equal. We prove
the formula by induction on
n
.

Base case
n=1
.
Direct inspection gives

A^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{0}&F_{1}&F_{1}\\
F_{-1}&F_{0}&F_{0}\\
F_{0}&F_{1}&F_{1}\end{pmatrix}

with
F_{-1}=1
, matching the formula.

Inductive step.
Assume the formula holds at
n
. Then

A^{n+1}=A\cdot A^{n}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}F_{n-1}&F_{n}&F_{n}\\
F_{n-2}&F_{n-1}&F_{n-1}\\
F_{n-1}&F_{n}&F_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Computing each row: rows
1
and
3
give

(F_{n-2}+F_{n-1},\;F_{n-1}+F_{n},\;F_{n-1}+F_{n})=(F_{n},\;F_{n+1},\;F_{n+1}),

and row
2
gives
(F_{n-1},\;F_{n},\;F_{n})
.
This matches the formula at
n+1
.

Part (ii):
\operatorname{tr}(A^{n})=2F_{n-1}+F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n+1}=L_{n}

by the standard identity
L_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n+1}
.

Part (iii): the sum of all nine entries of
A^{n}
equals

4F_{n}+4F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}=4F_{n+1}+F_{n-2}
.
We verify this equals
F_{n+4}
by checking the recurrence and two initial
values: at
n=1
, the sum is
4F_{2}+F_{-1}=4+1=5=F_{5}
; at
n=2
, it is

4F_{3}+F_{0}=8+0=8=F_{6}
. Since both sequences satisfy the Fibonacci recurrence
and agree at
n=1,2
, they are equal for all
n\geq 1
.
∎

The subgraph

The edges of

.
The out-degrees are

Theorem 4.15

(Vertex indispensability in
G^{\prime}
)
.

Let

be any vertex of the effective subgraph

.

(i)

The spectral radius of the avoiding subgraph satisfies

. Explicitly:

\rho(\mathcal{H}_{4})=\rho(\mathcal{H}_{1})=\varphi\approx 1.618,\quad\rho(\mathcal{H}_{5})=\sqrt{2}\approx 1.414,\quad\rho(\mathcal{H}_{2})=1.

governs the hierarchy of

(ii)

No positive cycle of
T
can have its modular projection entirely

. Equivalently, every positive cycle
of

visits vertex

in its modular projection.

(iii)

whose modular

tends to zero

\rho(\mathcal{H}_{v})/2<1
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{H}_{v}
denotes the subgraph of
G^{\prime}
obtained by
removing vertex
v
(Definition
4.6
).

Part (i).
The cases
v=4
,
v=1
,

follow respectively from

,
Proposition
4.11
,
Proposition
4.13
and Corollary

.
For

;

.

Part (ii).
Since vertices

and

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
n
be a positive integer with
T^{m}(n)=n
, let
s=s(C^{m}(n))
denote
the Hamming weight of the binary encoding, and let
n_{v}
denote the
number of indices
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
with
h_{i}(n)=v
, for

v\in\{1,2,4,5\}
. Then:

(i)

n_{2}=n_{1}+n_{4}
.

(ii)

2\,n_{2}+n_{5}=m
,
s=n_{1}+n_{5}
,
m-s=n_{1}+2\,n_{4}
.

(iii)

The distribution
(n_{1},n_{2},n_{4},n_{5})
is uniquely determined by
the triple
(m,s,n_{1})
:

n_{5}=s-n_{1},\qquad n_{4}=\frac{m-s-n_{1}}{2},\qquad n_{2}=\frac{m-s+n_{1}}{2}.

Proof.

Every positive cycle lies entirely in
G^{\prime}
: it contains an odd element
(a positive cycle cannot consist only of even terms, which strictly
decrease), whose next iterate lies in
\{2,5\}\subset G^{\prime}

(Corollary
4.5
), and since
G^{\prime}
is closed the whole
cycle stays in
G^{\prime}
; hence
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{4}+n_{5}=m
.

Part (i).

In
G^{\prime}
, the only edges arriving at vertex
1
are
2\to 1
, and the
only edges arriving at vertex
4
are
2\to 4

(Proposition
2.8
). In a closed walk, the number
of arrivals at each vertex equals the number of departures, so the
number of departures from
2
equals the number of arrivals at
1

plus the number of arrivals at
4
:
n_{2}=n_{1}+n_{4}
.

Part (ii).

Substituting
n_{2}=n_{1}+n_{4}
into
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{4}+n_{5}=m
gives

2n_{1}+2n_{4}+n_{5}=m
, i.e.
2n_{2}+n_{5}=m
. Since

h_{i}\in\{1,5\}
if and only if
T^{i-1}(n)
is odd,
s=n_{1}+n_{5}
.
Then
m-s=(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{4}+n_{5})-(n_{1}+n_{5})=n_{2}+n_{4}=(n_{1}+n_{4})+n_{4}=n_{1}+2n_{4}
.

Part (iii).

Solving
s=n_{1}+n_{5}
and
m-s=n_{1}+2n_{4}
gives

n_{5}=s-n_{1}
and
n_{4}=(m-s-n_{1})/2
, hence

n_{2}=n_{1}+n_{4}=(m-s+n_{1})/2
.
∎

Corollary 4.19
.

Every positive cycle of length
m
satisfies

In particular, vertex

accounts for more than

of the steps.

Proof.

By Proposition

(iii),
2n_{2}=m-s+n_{1}\geq m-s
,

. For any positive cycle, (

) gives

5. Conclusions and future work

This paper develops a framework in which the Collatz dynamics is encoded
simultaneously at two levels — binary and modular — via the bijections

\Phi_{m}
and
\Psi_{m}
. The framework is built on treating the corrective
residue
R(C^{m}(n))
as a function of the binary encoding
C^{m}(n)
alone.
This allows both bijections to satisfy the same structural
properties — bijectivity (B), periodicity (P) and antisymmetry (A) —
at their respective scales. The general modular Collatz graph is
defined in
[
5
]
; the bijection
\Psi_{m}
and its
structural properties do not appear there.

The central result is that among the odd integers in
\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
,
exactly
F(m+1)
avoid the residue class
4\pmod{6}
during steps

2,\ldots,m
(Theorem
4.7
). This is not an approximation:
the count is exact and unconditional, forced by the spectral gap

\varphi/2<1
between
\mathcal{H}_{4}
and
G^{\prime}

(Corollary
4.9
). To our knowledge, no prior work
establishes a Fibonacci count of this kind for Collatz trajectories.

The modular path theorem (Theorem
4.1
) gives complete
information on how the displacement
+2^{m}
acts on the modular encoding of
every integer in
\{1,\ldots,6\cdot 2^{m}\}
: level
i
is preserved if and
only if
2^{i-1}\nmid n
, and otherwise shifts by
+2
or
+4
modulo
6

according to the parity of
m+i
. Equivalently, if
v_{2}(n)=r
is the

2
-adic valuation of
n
, then only the first
r+1
levels of the modular
path change; all deeper levels are rigid
(Corollary
4.4
). As a consequence, positive cycles of
length
m
within any arithmetic progression of common difference
2^{m}

are separated by multiples of
6\cdot 2^{m}
(Corollary
4.2
).

No vertex of
G^{\prime}
is dispensable: removing any one of them reduces the
spectral radius strictly below
2
, with hierarchy
1<\sqrt{2}<\varphi<2

(Theorem
4.15
). In particular, every positive cycle
of
T
must visit residue class
2\pmod{6}
, and a flow conservation identity
forces this class to account for more than
18\%
of its steps
(Corollary
4.19
).

Three questions remain open.

The first concerns effectivity. For an odd integer
n
, let
m_{0}(n)

be the first level at which
h_{m_{0}}(n)=4
, with
m_{0}(n)=\infty
if the
orbit never meets residue
4\pmod{6}
— as for
n=1
, fixed by

1\leftrightarrow 2
. The Fibonacci theorem gives
m_{0}(n)>m
for exactly

F(m+1)
odd integers in
\{1,\ldots,2^{m}\}
. Whether every odd
n>2
has

m_{0}(n)<\infty
— equivalently, whether no such orbit stays in

\mathcal{H}_{4}
forever — is open; an explicit function
f(n)
with

m_{0}(n)\leq f(n)
for all odd
n>2
would settle it and connect the count
to a quantitative descent condition.

The second question asks whether every positive cycle other than

1\leftrightarrow 2
must visit all four vertices of
G^{\prime}
.
Theorem
4.15
establishes this for vertex
2
.
Proving it for
v\in\{1,4,5\}
requires controlling the integrality
of
R/(2^{m}-3^{s})
(see (
4
)) over all closed walks in
each
\mathcal{H}_{v}
, a problem that Proposition
4.18

reduces to the analysis of a single free parameter
n_{1}
.

The third question asks whether the vertex distribution constraints of
Corollary
4.19
, combined with (
4
) and
the structure of
R
, yield lower bounds on the cycle length
m
.
The spatial separation of Corollary
4.2
, the frequency
bound
n_{2}>0.1845\,m
, and the integrality of (
4
) together
constrain any hypothetical positive cycle from three independent
directions; whether their combination suffices to derive length
bounds remains open.
