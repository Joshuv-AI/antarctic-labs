On Jumps, Interactions, and Intersection Types
Stefano Catozi, Ugo Dal Lago, Gabriele Vanoni

Abstract
The Jumping Abstract Machine (JAM), an evaluation mechanism for the λ \lambda -calculus, was introduced by Danos and Regnier as an optimization of the Interaction Abstract Machine (IAM), itself an operational counterpart to Girard’s Geometry of Interaction and Abramsky et al . game semantics. Moreover, the JAM is isomorphic to the Pointer Abstract Machine (PAM), the syntactical counterpart of Hyland and Ong’s game semantics. We study a generalization of the JAM, that we call the Parametric Jumping Abstract Machine (PaJAM) and show that there is a tight correspondence between the PaJAM and non-idempotent intersection types: given a normalizing term t t , the number of steps taken by the PaJAM when evaluating t t can be extracted from its non-idempotent intersection type derivation. Remarkably, fixing the backtracking depth of the PaJAM, one can easily recover both the JAM/PAM, when the depth is constrained to be zero, and the IAM, when it is instead unconstrained. Exploiting type-theoretic machinery, we analyze the complexity of the PaJAM, showing that it is polynomial in the number of weak head β \beta steps, giving rise to a reasonable cost model, for each finite bound on the backtracking depth.

Introduction

This paper deals with two central concepts in the theory of the
\lambda
-calculus, namely
abstract machines

[
27
,
29
,
2
]
on the one hand and
intersection types

[
16
,
14
,
12
]
on the other. We are particularly interested in the relationship between these two concepts and our main contribution is a new generalized tight correspondence result. This will allow us to analyze the efficiency of a family of machines via type-theoretic tools. We give a bit of context before delving into the technical details.

Abstract Machines.

An abstract machine is an automaton designed to evaluate a term of the

\lambda
-calculus (or of similar calculi),
i.e.
to compute a representation of
its normal form. In doing so, the abstract machine usually
conforms to a notion of reduction, e.g.,
call-by-value
or

call-by-name
reduction
[
29
]
. Abstract machines, unlike mere rewriting, are
thought of as (relatively) low-level, first-order, descriptions of the evaluation process,
such that each individual step is in some sense
elementary
. As it is well
known, there are a variety of abstract machines, each with its own correctness
and efficiency properties. Among the best-studied machines, there is the so-called
Krivine Abstract Machine (KAM)
[
26
]
, which implements call-by-name (a.k.a. weak-head) reduction. Machines which are similar in spirit to the Krivine machine but implementing
call-by-value or call-by-need reduction have also been introduced
[
2
,
3
]
.

Game Machines.

Since the pioneering work of Danos, Herbelin and Regnier
[
18
]
, it is well
known that there are machines implementing weak-head reduction like the
KAM, but which have an even lower-level behavior, closely related to game
semantics and Girard’s geometry of interaction (GoI)
[
23
]
. In particular, the
so-called Interaction Abstract Machine (IAM)
[
28
,
19
,
4
]
can be seen
as an automata-theoretic counterpart of the GoI or of AJM games
[
1
]
, and evaluates terms
through a purely
local
and
reversible
process. There is also the so-called
Pointer Abstract Machine (PAM)
[
18
,
20
]
, which is instead inspired, as its name says,
by the mechanism of justification pointers from Hyland and Ong’s games
[
24
]
. As discovered by Danos and
Regnier
[
19
]
, the PAM is isomorphic to the Jumping Abstract Machine (JAM), an optimization of the IAM obtained by allowing the latter to have, in certain circumstances, the possibility to
jump
from one subterm to another, thus losing locality and reversibility.
These machines have been studied with respect to their correctness and
relative performance. In particular, Accattoli, Dal Lago and Vanoni, have
recently shown that the KAM can be seen as an improvement to the JAM (and thus of the PAM), and that the latter is an improvement to the IAM
[
5
,
17
]
. Noticeably, while switching from the KAM to the JAM involves a
polynomial
overhead, the IAM can be
exponentially
less efficient than the JAM.

Intersection Types and the KAM.

What role do intersection types play in all this? In their non-idempotent variant
[
22
,
25
]
, intersection types are known to be a syntactic counterpart of the
relational model
of linear logic
[
13
]
. Starting from de Carvalho’s results
[
21
]
, the size of the type derivation for a term
t
has been put in relation to the number of reduction steps needed to evaluate the term
t
using the KAM. The correspondence can be made tight by considering so-called
tight typings

[
10
]
or by introducing a special type
\star
corresponding to normal forms. In such correspondences, each KAM step is put in relation to the application of a typing rule, and this relation is bijective: subterms that are used many times appear many times in the derivation, while terms that are
not
used simply are not subject to typing.

Intersection Types and Game Machines.

Accattoli
et al.
recently showed that the correspondence just
mentioned, surprisingly, scales to the IAM
[
5
]
. Remarkably, this does not require
any modification to the type system itself, but only amounts to altering the
way each typing rule is weighted. While in the KAM each rule occurrence counts as

one
, the IAM requires assigning a weight equal to
the size of the conclusion
type
. In other words, the number of IAM steps is precisely reflected by the
number of occurrences of the type
\star
to the right of
\vdash
in the
corresponding type derivation. Since types can be exponentially bigger than terms, this explains the aforementioned efficiency gap.
But what about the JAM or the PAM? Would it be possible to precisely characterize the
dynamic behavior of the JAM by means of non-idempotent intersection types? More fundamentally: given that there is a phase transition between the JAM and the IAM, what is it that
drives
this transition? And why, by contrast, do the KAM and the JAM turn out to have both a polynomial overhead? These are precisely the questions that this work addresses. And the results we
obtain confirm that non-idempotent intersection types are indeed a very flexible
tool. Once again, and surprisingly, it is only a matter of modifying the weight given to each
rule. If in the IAM we count
all
occurrences of
\star
in the
conclusion of each rule, the JAM requires to count only those occurrences of

\star
that are at
a depth equal to
0
or
1
. As a result, the blowup can no longer be exponential and becomes polynomial instead. In other words, the JAM/PAM is much closer to the KAM than to the IAM, even if it was designed as an optimization of the latter. The situation, a bit more formally, is similar to the one in the figure below, where
n
is the total size of the types occurring in the derivation.

As we explain below, however, we do much more, showing that the IAM and the JAM are both instances of a parametric version of the latter. Indeed, looking at the figure above, it is natural to wonder whether there is anything
in between
the JAM and the IAM in which some (but not all) occurrences of
\star
at depth higher than
1
are counted. Moreover, are these intermediate machines efficient?

Contributions.

This work introduces an abstract machine, called the Parametric Jumping Abstract Machine (PaJAM), obtained by generalizing Danos and Regnier’s jumps so that they
do not
occur until a certain backtracking depth
k
. Indeed, the intuition is that the IAM computes using a possibly nested backtracking mechanism and jumping allows one to skip this backtracking phase. However, because of the nesting, one could be inside
k
backtracking phases and decide at some point not to go one level deeper, and instead jump, thus remaining at depth
k
. More specifically, we develop the following results:

•

We define a generalization of the JAM, the PaJAM, which is a parametric machine, in Sect.
3
. The JAM can be recovered by setting the backtracking depth
k
as
0
, while the IAM can be seen as a degenerate version of the PaJAM where
k=\infty
.

•

As shown in Sect.
5
, the number of steps performed by the PaJAM when evaluating a term
t
can be measured, following the spirit of figure above, by letting the size of a type depend on
k
and counting occurrences of the base type
\star
up to depth
k
:

•

These results are then used in Sect.
6
to show that every instance of the PaJAM with
k<\infty
simulates
\beta
reduction with a polynomial overhead, thus being time invariant.

There are two take-home messages: not only non-idempotent intersection types
allow a fine-grained and direct analysis of the dynamic properties of reduction,
even when the latter is performed by abstract machines, but the
performances of the JAM (and thus of the PAM) and the IAM can be studied within a
single
framework, which also gives a very simple account of their relative
performance.

The Closed Call-by-Name
\lambda
-Calculus

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a countable set of
variables
. The
terms

of the
\lambda
-calculus are defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}t,u,r,w&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=&x\in\mathcal{V}\;\;\mbox{\Large{$\mid$}}\;\;\lambda x.t\;\;\mbox{\Large{$\mid$}}\;\;tu\end{array}

Free
and

are defined as usual:

\lambda x.t

x
in
t
. Terms are considered modulo

\alpha

denotes the
capture-avoiding (meta-level)
substitution
of all free
occurrences of
x
in
t
for
u
.

Contexts
are
\lambda
-terms containing exactly
one occurrence of a special symbol, the hole
\langle\cdot\rangle
, intuitively standing for a removed subterm. Here we adopt

levelled contexts
, whose index,
i.e.
the level, stands for the number of
arguments (
i.e.
the
number of !-boxes in linear logic terminology) the hole lies in:

\begin{array}[]{rclrrrcl}C_{0}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=&\langle\cdot\rangle\;\;\mbox{\Large{$\mid$}}\;\;\lambda x.C_{0}\;\;\mbox{\Large{$\mid$}}\;\;C_{0}t\ ;&&&C_{n+1}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=&C_{n+1}t\;\;\mbox{\Large{$\mid$}}\;\;\lambda x.C_{n+1}\;\;\mbox{\Large{$\mid$}}\;\;tC_{n}.\end{array}

We simply write

for a context whenever the level is not relevant.
The operation replacing the hole
\langle\cdot\rangle
with a term

in a context

is noted

and called

We consider a
call-by-name
(CbN) operational semantics which we restrict to
closed
terms,
i.e.
without any free variable. This is defined as follows:

(\lambda x.t)ur_{1}\ldots r_{h}\ \ \rightarrow_{wh}\ \ t\{x{\shortleftarrow}u\}r_{1}\ldots r_{h}\qquad\qquad h\geq 0.

This way,
\lambda
-abstractions are the only normal forms.

For any notion of reduction
\rightarrow
, we denote by
\rightarrow^{*}
the reflexive and transitive closure of
\rightarrow
, and we
define
\rightarrow^{n}
by induction as
\rightarrow^{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathsf{id}

\rightarrow^{n+1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\rightarrow\,\rightarrow^{n}
.

Intuitively, the behaviour of the PaJAM, generalizing that of the IAM and the JAM, can be seen as that of a token that travels around the syntax tree of the program under evaluation, possibly making some
jumps
. The evaluation is done in two phases. In one, noted
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\downarrow}
, the machine looks for the head variable, while in the other one, noted
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\uparrow}}
, the machine looks for the argument which should replace it. We redirect the reader to
[
4
,
5
]
for detailed explanations on the behavior of this family of machines.

PaJAM States.

The transitions of the PaJAM and all the data structures are defined in Fig.
1
. As we can see from the definition, the machine relies on two mutually inductive data structures, namely
logs
and
logged positions
.
We note with
L
a log of arbitrary length.
In order to implement the optimization mechanism introduced earlier, each machine state carries an integer parameter
k\in{\rm Nature}\cup\{\infty\}
, the
backtracking (nesting) depth
, indicating how many backtracking levels the machine may still enter.
Initial states have the form
q_{t}^{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}t},\langle\cdot\rangle,\epsilon,\epsilon,k)
, with
k
being the initial parameter of the machine.
Directions are often omitted and represented via colors and underlining:

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\downarrow}
is represented by a

red
and underlined code term,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\uparrow}}
by a
blue
and underlined code context.

Transitions.

The transitions of the PaJAM are in Fig.
1
and their union is noted
\rightarrow_{\mathrm{PaJAM}}
. Note that the machine is deterministic, but not reversible. A
run

\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}q\rightarrow^{*}{q^{\prime}}
is a possibly empty sequence of transitions, whose length is noted

|\rho|
. If it starts from an initial state it is called
initial
, and if moreover it ends on a final state it is called
complete
.
Consider an execution of the PaJAM starting from the initial state
q_{t}^{k}
, only two cases can happen: either the execution diverges or it terminates in a state
q
of the form

q=({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\underline{\lambda x.u}},C,L,\epsilon,k)
. We call such states
final
.
The fact that no other shapes are possible can proved as in
[
5
]
.
Notice how the parameter
k
is decremented by 1 after entering a backtracking phase via a
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}1}
transition and is incremented by 1 after exiting a backtracking with a
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}2}
transition. When
k
reaches
0
, the
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}1}
transition is replaced by a
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{jmp}}
, allowing the machine to avoid the backtracking runs after a certain depth.

Remark 1
.

It is clear that if we start from an initial state
q_{t}^{0}
, the PaJAM run never performs backtracking and thus behaves exactly like the JAM
[
5
]
.
On the other hand, when the initial parameter is set to
\infty
, no jump is ever performed, the PaJAM thus behaving like the IAM of
[
4
]
.

The IAM is well-known to be correct for Closed CbN. This property is extended to the JAM in
[
5
]
, using a simulation argument.
The proof smoothly adapts to the PaJAM, and this is why we do not detail it here.

Proposition 2

(Correctness)
.

q_{t}^{k}
terminates if and only if
t
has normal form.

The direction of reachable states is directly related to the backtracking nesting depth, as the next invariant shows.
Let
|T|_{l}
be the number of logged position in a tape
T
.

Proposition 3

(Phase Invariant)
.

Let
s=(t,C,L,T,d,k^{\prime})
be a
\mathrm{PaJAM}
state reachable from
q_{u}^{k<\infty}
. If
d={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\uparrow}
then
|T|_{l}=2(k-k^{\prime})
and if
d={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\downarrow}}
then
|T|_{l}=2(k-k^{\prime})+1
.

The
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{arg}}
transition saves the position
l_{x}
on the log and the machine starts looking for the head variable of the argument. Having found it, it saves its position on the tape. Now the machine starts looking for the argument of
\lambda y.y
in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\uparrow}}
mode. However,
\lambda y.y
was not the left side of an application forming a
\beta
-redex. Indeed, it was virtually substituted for the first occurrence of
x
, thus creating
the “virtual” redex
(\lambda y.y)x
. Its argument is thus the second occurrence of
x
. Since the backtracking parameter is
1
, the machine is allowed to perform one level of backtracking. It therefore starts a backtracking run with a
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}1}
transition, in order to find the variable associated with the logged position
l_{x}
stored in the log. We set
l_{y}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(y,(\lambda x.x)(\lambda y.\langle\cdot\rangle),l_{x})
.

\cline{1-7}\cr&\lambda y.y&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\underline{(\lambda x.xx)\langle\cdot\rangle}}}&(x,(\lambda x.\langle\cdot\rangle x)(\lambda y.y),\epsilon)&l_{y}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\uparrow}}&1\\
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}1}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\underline{\lambda x.xx}}&\langle\cdot\rangle(\lambda y.y)&\epsilon&(x,(\lambda x.\langle\cdot\rangle x)(\lambda y.y),\epsilon)\cdot l_{y}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\downarrow}&0\\
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}2}&x&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\underline{(\lambda x.\langle\cdot\rangle x)(\lambda y.y)}}}&\epsilon&l_{y}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\uparrow}}&1\\
\end{array}}

If instead we had started with an initial parameter equal to
0
, the machine would have skipped the backtracking run between
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}1}
and
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}2}
, and would have jumped directly to the position stored in
l_{x}
, as shown below.

{\footnotesize\begin{array}[]{l|c|c|c|c|c|c}&\mathsf{Sub}\mbox{-}\mathsf{term}&\mathsf{Context}&\mathsf{Log}&\mathsf{Tape}&\mathsf{Dir}&\mathsf{Depth}\\
\cline{1-7}\cr&\lambda y.y&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\underline{(\lambda x.xx)\langle\cdot\rangle}}}&(x,(\lambda x.\langle\cdot\rangle x)(\lambda y.y),\epsilon)&l_{y}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\uparrow}}&0\\
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{jmp}}&x&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\underline{(\lambda x.\langle\cdot\rangle x)(\lambda y.y)}}}&\epsilon&l_{y}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\uparrow}}&0\\
\end{array}}

Notice that the states reached after
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}2}
and
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{jmp}}
are the same, except for the value of the parameter.
Continuing the original run, the machine can now find immediately the argument, being the second occurrence of
x
. Then, it starts looking for the argument of this variable, finding of course a new copy of
\lambda y.y
.

{\footnotesize\begin{array}[]{l|c|c|c|c|c|c}&\mathsf{Sub}\mbox{-}\mathsf{term}&\mathsf{Context}&\mathsf{Log}&\mathsf{Tape}&\mathsf{Dir}&\mathsf{Depth}\\
\cline{1-7}\cr&x&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\underline{(\lambda x.\langle\cdot\rangle x)(\lambda y.y)}}}&\epsilon&l_{y}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\uparrow}}&1\\
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{arg}}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\underline{x}}&(\lambda x.x\langle\cdot\rangle)(\lambda y.y)&l_{y}&\epsilon&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\downarrow}&1\\

\rightarrow_{\mathsf{arg}}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\underline{\lambda y.y}}&(\lambda x.xx)\langle\cdot\rangle&(x,(\lambda x.x\langle\cdot\rangle)(\lambda y.y),l_{y})&\epsilon&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\downarrow}&1\\
\end{array}}

The computation then stops, signaling that the term has a normal form.

The Sequence PaJAM

In this section, we present the definition of the Sequence Parametric JAM (SPaJAM), which is the type theoretic version of the PaJAM and which is strongly bisimilar to it. Instead of being defined in an automata-theoretic way, the SPaJAM is defined as a machine
acting on
the intersection type derivation for the term under evaluation. Indeed, abstracting from the concrete data structures, as done in
[
5

6
]
, allows us to better reason about the complexity of the machine. We do not have here the space to explain in detail how the type system works, and thus we refer to
[
14

12

Complexity of the Parametric JAM

In this last section, we exploit our type theoretic analysis of the PaJAM to study its complexity. To achieve this, we establish a bound on the number of occurrences of the base type
\star

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a new parametric machine, the PaJAM, which behaves like the IAM up to a fixed backtracking nesting depth, and then switches to behaving like the JAM. We showed that the runtime of this machine can be characterized using intersection type derivations, extending the results about the KAM and the IAM. Furthermore, thanks to the aforementioned type theoretic framework, we were able to analyze its complexity. This allowed us to prove that the number of steps the PaJAM needs to evaluate a term
t
is
polynomial
in the number of
\beta
-steps needed to normalize it, thus making the PaJAM a
reasonable
cost model
[
11
]
.

Although, from a technical viewpoint, the analysis of the PaJAM just described is certainly of interest, the most significant conceptual contribution lies in the exploration of the space between the JAM and the IAM, the former being obtained as an optimization of the latter and thereby achieving, on certain terms, an exponential speedup. On the one hand, our work shows that the phase transition occurs ‘on the IAM side,’ only when jumps are never performed. On the other hand, it demonstrates that, from a quantitative perspective, the JAM is more similar to the KAM than to the machine of which it is an optimization.

In future work, we would like to investigate the relationship between the PaJAM and game semantics. Indeed, it would be interesting to analyze our results on non-idempotent intersection types from the perspective of game semantics, e.g. following the lines of
[
30
,
15
]
, and understand how the PaJAM relates with game-theoretical models, enriching the already well-known connections between the JAM and HO games (via the PAM-JAM isomorphism) and the IAM and AJM games.

Moreover, we are interested in the
space
performances of the PaJAM, in particular depending on the choice of the parameter. This analysis could be carried out either directly on the machine, as in
[
8
,
9
]
, or via intersection types, as in
[
6
,
7
]
. The problem is non-trivial, as one should in this case take into account how data structures are represented at low-level, and how garbage collection is implemented.

Type (Context) Tests.

While judgment tests depend only on the
judgment occurrence
J
of a state

q=(\pi,J,(A,\mathbb{A}),d,k)
, type context
tests—dually—fix
J
and depend only on the
type
context
\mathbb{A}
of
q
, that is, they all focus on sequence elements of
the form
(J,(A^{\prime},\mathbb{A}^{\prime}))

where
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\langle A^{\prime}\rangle=\mathbb{A}\langle A\rangle
and
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\langle\mathbb{A}^{\prime\prime}\rangle
for some type context
\mathbb{A}^{\prime\prime}
. Namely,
there is one type context test (shortened to
type test
) for every sequence
in which the hole of
\mathbb{A}
is contained. We need some notions about type
contexts, in particular a notion of level
analogous to the one for term contexts.

Terminology About Type Contexts.

Define type contexts
\mathbb{A}_{n}
of
level
n\in{\rm Nature}
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lclr}\mathbb{A}_{0}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=&\langle\cdot\rangle\mid S\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{0}\\
\mathbb{A}_{n+1}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=&[...\mathbb{A}_{n}...]\rightarrow A\mid S\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{n+1}\end{array}

Clearly, every type context
\mathbb{A}
can be seen as a type context
\mathbb{A}_{n}

for a unique
n
, and vice versa a type
context of level
n
is also simply a type context—the level is then sometimes omitted.
A
prefix
of a context
\mathbb{A}
is a context
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
such that

\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\langle\mathbb{A}^{\prime\prime}\rangle=\mathbb{A}
for some

\mathbb{A}^{\prime\prime}
. Given
\mathbb{A}
of level

, there is a smallest prefix
context
\mathbb{A}|_{i}
of level

, and it has the form

\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\langle[...\langle\cdot\rangle...]\rightarrow A\rangle
for a type
context
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}

of level

Lemma 27

(Type context lifting)
.

If
\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;\;\vdash t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\infty}_{d},\mathbb{B}\rightarrow_{\mathrm{SPaJAM}}^{n}\vdash u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\infty}_{d^{\prime}},\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
with
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\langle A^{\prime}\rangle=A
then it exists a run
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;\;\vdash t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\prime\infty}_{d},\mathbb{B}\langle\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\rangle\rightarrow_{\mathrm{SPaJAM}}^{n}\vdash u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\prime\infty}_{d^{\prime}},\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\langle\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\rangle
. Moreover, at each step the transition applied in
\rho
and
\sigma
are the same.

Proof.

In the following we will not take in account the parameter
k,\,k^{\prime}
in the states to make the proof simpler to read. It is easy to see that this do not change the proof.
By induction on the length of
\rho
. If
|\rho|=0
the result it trivially holds. Let’s fix

\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;\;\vdash t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\infty}_{d},\mathbb{B}\rightarrow_{\mathrm{SPaJAM}}^{n}\vdash t^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\infty}_{d^{\prime\prime}},\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow_{\mathrm{SPaJAM}}\vdash u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\infty}_{d^{\prime}},\mathbb{B}^{\prime}

By
i.h.
it exists a run

\sigma^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;\;\vdash t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\prime\infty}_{d},\mathbb{B}\langle\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\rangle\rightarrow_{\mathrm{SPaJAM}}^{n}\vdash t^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\prime\infty}_{d^{\prime\prime}},\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}\langle\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\rangle\;=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}q^{\prime\prime}
.
We can conclude simply by noticing that all the Generalized SPaJAM transitions are local.
We can perform the same transition from
\vdash t^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\infty}_{d^{\prime\prime}},\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}
to
\vdash u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\infty}_{d^{\prime}},\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
that we had in
\rho
, in the context
\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}\langle\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\rangle
.

We give an example for the case
\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{var}}}
, all the others being similar. The last transition of
\rho
has the following shape.

\begin{array}[]{clc}\vdash\lambda x.C\langle x\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[\ldots A_{i}\ldots]\rightarrow B\vdash{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}\langle A^{\infty}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\uparrow}}\rangle_{i}(=A_{i})&\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{var}}}&\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\lambda x.C\langle x\rangle}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[\ldots\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}\langle A^{\infty}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\downarrow}}}\rangle_{i}\ldots]\rightarrow B\vdash x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A_{i}\end{array}

We have
A=\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\langle A^{\prime}\rangle
and we can perform the same
\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{var}}}
transition on
q^{\prime\prime}
, obtaining

\begin{array}[]{clc}\vdash\lambda x.C\langle x\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[\ldots A_{i}\ldots]\rightarrow B\vdash{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}\langle\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\langle A^{\prime\infty}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\uparrow}}\rangle\rangle_{i}(=A_{i})&\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{var}}}&\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\lambda x.C\langle x\rangle}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[\ldots\mathbb{B}^{\prime\prime}\langle\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\langle A^{\prime\infty}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\downarrow}}}\rangle\rangle_{i}\ldots]\rightarrow B\vdash x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A_{i}=q\end{array}

We have that the context of the new state
q
is exactly
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\langle\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\rangle
.
∎

Lemma 28

(J-exhaustible Invariant)
.

Let
t
be a closed term,
\pi\;\triangleright\Gamma\vdash t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A
a sequence
type derivation for it, and
\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \vdash t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\langle{A}^{k}_{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\uparrow}\rangle\rightarrow_{\mathrm{SPaJAM}}^{n}q
an initial Generalized SPaJAM run. Then

is J-exhaustible.

Proof.

By induction on
n
. For
n=0
there is nothing to prove because the initial
state
q_{0}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,\vdash t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\langle A^{k}_{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\uparrow}\rangle
has no
judgement nor type tests. Then suppose

\rho^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}q_{0}\rightarrow_{\mathrm{SPaJAM}}^{n-1}{q^{\prime}}
and that the run continues with
{q^{\prime}}\rightarrow_{\mathrm{SPaJAM}}q
. By
i.h.
,
{q^{\prime}}
is
J-exhaustible.

Terminology
: when a test state satisfies the clause in the definition of J-exhaustible states we say that it is
positive
.

Cases of

•

Case
\rightarrow_{\bullet 1}
.

–

Judgement tests.
Note that
q
has the same judgement tests of
{q^{\prime}}
, which are
positive by the
i.h.

–

Type tests.
We consider at first type tests with direction
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\uparrow}
. Let

be one of them. Since the type context
\mathbb{A}
is the same in both
q
and

of

. This

is positive by
i.h.
. We observe that

, and since the machine is deterministic also

is positive.
We now consider type tests with direction
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\downarrow}}

one of them. As before, there is a corresponding type test for
q^{\prime}
, the we call
q^{\prime}_{d}
that is positive by
i.h.
and such that
q_{d}\rightarrow_{\bullet 3}q^{\prime}_{d}
. By definition, also
q_{d}
is positive.
In both cases, to prove the moreover part of the statement, we just have to notice that in the new transition that we defined the type context
\mathbb{A}
is not changed, so neither is its depth.

•

Case
\rightarrow_{\bullet 2}
. Identical to the previous one.

•

Case
\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{var}}}
.

Judgement tests.

\rightarrow_{\bullet 3},\,\rightarrow_{\bullet 4}

•

Case

Appendix B
Proof of the PaJAM/SPaJAM bisimulation

Relating Logs and Tapes with Typed Position.

Also this section contains proofs which are rephrasing of proofs appearing in
[
5
]
. We added them for the sake of completeness.
The PaJAM uses the log
L=l_{1},\dots,l_{n}
to represent the arguments
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
in which the position of the current state is contained. Each
u_{i}
corresponds to the answer of a query for an argument associated with the variable in the logged position
l_{i}
. In the SPaJAM, this information is given by the sub-derivations for
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
in which the judgement of the current state occurs.

Notice that in the PaJAM, the transitions
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{arg}}
and
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}1}
respectively add and remove exactly one logged position from the log. In the SPaJAM, these correspond to the only transitions that enter or exit arguments in the derivation. Also, the transitions
\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{var}}}
,
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}2}
and
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{jmp}}
in the PaJAM are responsible for creating and reading the content of a logged position. In the SPaJAM, this translates into exiting or entering an axiom typing a variable.

The tape of the PaJAM is used to store information about the logged position for which the machine is currently searching an associated argument, or is backtracking to. In the SPaJAM, this information is encoded in the type context of the state. The PaJAM adds logged positions to the tape through the transitions
\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{var}}}
and
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}1}
, and removes them through
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{arg}}
and
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{bt}2}
. In the SPaJAM, these correspond to transitions that respectively increase or decrease the depth of the state’s type context.
Notice also that after a
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{jmp}}
transition the tape, and thus the type context, remains unchanged. By examining Fig.
1
and Fig.
3
, it becomes evident that there is a logged position on the PaJAM tape for every type sequence
S
in which it lies the hole
\langle\cdot\rangle
of the current type context
\mathbb{A}
of the SPaJAM.
These are the main intuitions behind the
extraction
procedure, which allows us to retrive a PaJAM state
q_{\mathsf{ext}}(q)
from an exhaustible SPaJAM state
q
.

From J-exhaustible states one is able to
extract
PaJAM states, as the
following definition shows. Please note that the definition is well-founded,
precisely because the objects are J-exhaustible states. Indeed, the induction
principle used to define J-exhaustability allows recursive definition on
J-exhaustible states to be well-behaved.

Remark 29
.

We first make a small remark about how logged positions are managed in the PaJAM. In the transition
\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{var}}}
, the logged position that is created contains a
global
context with respect to the term: if
t
is the initial term, we have
t=C\langle\lambda x.D_{n}\langle x\rangle\rangle
. In the
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{jmp}}

logged positions: in the
\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{var}}}

To avoid unnecessary complexity, the PaJAM logs positions globally. However, note that in the

transition, only the local information about the context

contained in

is actually used, since the information about the global context

is already present in the state.
