A. Executive Summary

This study aimed on understanding the molecular structure and transport mechanisms of high-
concentration lithium electrolytes, with a particular focus on solvate ionic liquids (SILs) based on glyme
solvents, for use in next-generation, high-voltage energy storage systems. The hypothesis is that the
composition of the solvents in these electrolytes can be designed and adjusted to control cation solvation,
enabling charge transport via an ion-hopping mechanism. This mechanism resembles Grotthuss
conduction of protons in water and is independent of electrolyte viscosity. The experimental studies
employed linear and nonlinear infrared spectroscopy (FTIR and 2D IR), electrochemical measurements,
and vibrational probes. These were complemented by high-level molecular dynamics simulations and
density functional theory calculations. The study examined lithium-ion solvation in pure and mixed
glymes, ionic speciation, and the role of aggregates in charge transport across regimes ranging from
regular electrolytes to SILs and highly concentrated systems. Additional investigations explored the
binding nature of bidentate anions in contact ion pairs and developed new IR probes to quantify free
versus coordinated glymes. Computational modeling provided insights into the atomistic structure of
solvation shells and how glyme chain length influences conformational flexibility, thermal stability, and
electrochemical stability. Together, these studies revealed that transport in concentrated electrolytes
occurs via ion hopping, which is mediated by charged aggregates and partially decouples ionic transport
from viscosity. Furthermore, the studies demonstrated that longer glymes enhance Li⁺ chelation and
stability. These findings establish a molecular framework for designing tailored electrolytes and highlight
the importance of an integrated experimental and computational strategy for understanding these
complex SILs.

Some key findings and implications of the research performed during the funding period are detailed
below.

The first paper [1] acknowledging this grant investigated glyme-based lithium-ion electrolytes across three
regimes: regular electrolytes (REs), solvate ionic liquids (SILs), and high concentration electrolytes (HCEs).
In particular, the study focused on how solvation structure influences transport properties and ionic
speciation. A comparison between lithium thiocyanate (LiSCN) in tetraglyme (G4) with the same salt in
mixed glymes (G1:G2) using linear and nonlinear IR spectroscopy, electrochemical measurements, and
DFT calculations allowed us to elucidate molecular-level interactions in these two systems.
Key Findings:
- Transport Properties: Despite higher viscosities, SILs and HCEs exhibit enhanced ionicities compared to
REs, indicating a shift to a charge transport mechanism partially decoupled from viscosity. Walden plots
reveal significant slope changes between regimes, suggesting conduction in concentrated systems via ion-
hopping and/or mediated by ionic aggregates.
- Ionic Speciation: linear and non-linear IR spectroscopies show that REs primarily contain contact ion
pairs, while SILs and HCEs feature neutral and charged aggregates that thermally interconvert. These ionic
aggregates facilitate efficient charge transport via picosecond making and breaking of ion pairs.
- Effect of Glyme Identity: Pure G4 systems form more ionic aggregates than mixed G1:G2 systems,
resulting in higher ionicities and more efficient transport in SIL and HCE regimes. Mixed glymes reduce
aggregate formation, making transport more reliant on vehicular transport.

The work provides a molecular framework for linking lithium ion solvation structure to macroscopic
properties, highlighting that transport mediated by ionic aggregates dominates in SILs and HCEs. It is

observed that the use of mixed glymes for viscosity control in concentrated electrolytes compromises the
ionicity and transport efficiency of the HCE.

The second paper [2] acknowledging this grant studied how thiocyanate (SCN⁻), an bidentate anion, binds
to lithium ions in contact ion pairs (CIPs) across different solvents. The goal was to determine whether
SCN⁻ binds through its sulfur (S) or nitrogen (N) atom and how this affects molecular dynamics. To this
end, FTIR, ATR-FTIR and ultrafast 2DIR spectroscopies, and DFT calculations were used to probe binding
modes and dynamics of the CIPs in different solvents.
Key findings:
- Linear IR spectroscopy distinguishes free SCN⁻ (~2057 cm⁻¹) from CIPs (~2072 cm⁻¹) but cannot
differentiate N-bound vs. S-bound configurations. Also, S–C stretch and S–C–N bend frequencies vary
among solvents, but reflect speciation rather than binding mode.
- DFT predicts higher S–C stretch frequencies for N-bound SCN⁻ compared to S-bound, supporting
theoretical differentiation, though experimental confirmation is ambiguous.
- 2DIR Spectroscopy provides clear signatures of binding mode through the dynamics of the frequency–
frequency correlation function (FFCF), where N-bound CIPs (e.g., ECPC solvent) present a single-
exponential spectral diffusion (~5 ps) indicating absence of wobbling motion, and S-bound CIPs (e.g., DMF
solvent) have a biexponential decay (fast ~1 ps and slow ~5 ps), attributed to wobbling-in-a-cone motion
of SCN⁻ with CN group pointing away from the lithium ion.

The work demonstrates that 2DIR spectroscopy is essential for resolving SCN⁻ binding modes, which
cannot be unambiguously determined from linear IR spectra. Understanding these dynamics provides
molecular-level insight into ion pairing, relevant for electrolyte design and systems where ion-ion
interactions govern transport properties.

The third manuscript [3] acknowledging this grant presents a perspective about high concentration
electrolytes (HCEs), which are salt solutions with concentrations exceeding 3 M. HCEs are promising
candidates for next-generation lithium-ion batteries due to their enhanced electrochemical stability, non-
flammability, and compatibility with high-voltage electrodes. However, their molecular-level
characterization remains a major challenge, hindering rational design and optimization.
Key Points:
- HCEs are important because they HCEs overcome limitations of conventional 1 M carbonate-based
electrolytes, offering wider electrochemical windows and improved safety. Subcategories include solvate
ionic liquids (SILs) and localized high concentration electrolytes (LHCEs), which aim to reduce viscosity and
cost.
 - HCEs face challenges for their characterization. HCEs have high viscosity and strong ion aggregation
complicating experimental characterization and computational modeling. The vast possible compositional
space (binary, ternary, and high-entropy mixtures) makes trial-and-error approaches inefficient.
Experimental characterization remains complicated since most require modeling for interpretation.
Computational approaches are either accurate but limited by short timescales and high computational
cost (e.g., ab initio), or computationally less expensive but struggle with the correct representation due
to polarization and charge transfer effects in the system (e.g., classical).
- Emerging directions to solve characterization challenges are machine learning (ML) since ML-driven force
fields and ML-accelerated MD promise AIMD-level accuracy at cMD cost, enabling predictive electrolyte
design. High-throughput experimentation, where robotic platforms and ML-assisted spectroscopy could
explore large compositional spaces efficiently. Finally, an integrated paradigm combining experimental
and computational methods to ensure consistency and accelerate discovery.

A synergistic approach merging advanced simulations, high-throughput experiments, and ML models
appears to be essential to unlock the structure–property–performance relationships of HCEs. This will
enable rational design of tailored electrolytes for high-energy-density batteries.

The fourth manuscript [4] acknowledging this grant explored glyme-based solvate ionic liquids (SILs)
formed by lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) with diglyme (G2), triglyme (G3), and
tetraglyme (G4) using classical molecular dynamics simulations and a tailored force field. The work
correlates glyme chain length and anion coordination to structural, dynamical, thermal, and
electrochemical properties of SILs.
Key findings:
- SILs solvation structure. The Li⁺ coordination depends strongly on glyme length: G2 forms mixed
structures with one or two glymes and significant anion involvement, and G3 and G4 predominantly
coordinate Li⁺ with a single glyme, with G4 achieving near-complete chelation. Almost no free glymes
were observed, satisfying SIL criteria.
- The conformational landscape of the solvent follows the chain lengths, where G2 adopts a rigid
geometry, G3 shows moderate flexibility, and G4 exhibits a much more rich conformational space due to
its longer chain.
- The dynamics of the SILs is also related to chain length. Translational and rotational motion is faster for
shorter glymes (G2 < G3 < G4). While G2 and G3 show sub-diffusive behavior in their diffusion, G4
approaches Brownian motion.
- Thermal stability and electrochemical stability are also related to chain length. The free energy of
evaporation of a glyme molecule shows that G3 and G4 SILs are more thermally stable than G2, and
directly correlates with Li⁺ chelation strength and reduced anion participation. HOMO–LUMO gap analysis
(~5 eV) aligns with oxidative stability near 5 V vs. Li⁺/Li. Structures with anions in the solvation shell exhibit
slightly lower stability.

Longer glymes enhance Li⁺ chelation, reduce anion interaction, which translates to improved transport
and stability. This explains why G4-based SILs are promising for high-voltage lithium-ion batteries. The
study provides a molecular framework for predicting SIL properties and guiding electrolyte design.

The last paper [5] acknowledging this grant is a study, which introduces amine-based vibrational probes
(NG2, NG3) to directly investigate the solvation structure of lithium ions in glyme-based electrolytes
(diglyme, triglyme) using FTIR spectroscopy and supported by DFT calculations and MD simulations. The
study quantifies the amount of free vs. coordinated glyme molecules to understand Li⁺ solvation at
moderate concentrations.
Key findings:
- The use of IR probes NG2 and NG3 is demonstrated. Solvent–probe exchange equilibrium constants are
near unity; enthalpy changes are small (≈ –1 to –2.6 kcal/mol), confirming similar solvation behavior of
the probes as compared to the glyme molecules. In particular, NG2 and NG3 mimic the glyme behavior,
but provide a distinct N–H IR signature for free and Li⁺-coordinated states.
- Using NG2 and NG3, the solvation and speciation of Li+ is derived. In G2 and G3, two glyme molecules
form the first Li⁺ solvation shell, even for triglyme, since a single glyme cannot fully chelate Li⁺. At studied
concentrations, TFSI⁻ forms solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs), not contact ion pairs (CIPs).
- Comparison between experiments and MD simulations shows that the latter agree on coordination
numbers, but overestimate full chelation. Also, experiments reveal the presence of partial coordination.

The use of IR probes enables direct quantification of Li⁺ solvation and speciation in glyme-based
electrolytes. Findings highlight that even large glymes require multiple molecules for Li⁺ coordination,
guiding electrolyte design for improved performance.
