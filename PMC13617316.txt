Adult‐Onset Dystonia Related to 
PRKRA
 (DYT16): A Predictor of Favorable Deep Brain Stimulation Outcome

Ingrid Lorena da Silva Gomes, Eduardo Drews Amorim, Juan Sebastián Sánchez León, Thomas Hugentobler Schlickmann, Yuri Ferreira Felloni Borges, Artur Francisco Schumacher Schuh, Daniel Teixeira‐dos‐Santos, Sheila Trentin, Thais Lampert Monte, Bruno Samuel Fraiman de Oliveira

Dystonia comprises a heterogeneous group of movement disorders characterized by sustained or intermittent abnormal movements and/or postures., Accurate classification of dystonia is essential for both etiological investigation and the selection of optimal treatment strategies. Although focal and segmental forms often respond to botulinum toxin injections, generalized dystonia remains particularly challenging to manage.

Advances in molecular genetics have expanded our understanding of inherited dystonias, several of which demonstrate variable but often substantial responsiveness to deep brain stimulation (DBS) when pharmacological therapy fails. The most consistent benefit has been reported in TOR1A (DYT1) followed by SGCE, THAP1, KMT2B, and VPS16. Other genotypes such as PRKRA, ATP1A3, and GNAL display heterogeneous responses influenced by phenotype, disease duration, and age at intervention.

Autosomal recessive dystonia caused by biallelic PRKRA mutations (DYT16) was first described in Brazilian families, most commonly presenting as juvenile‐onset generalized dystonia with parkinsonian features. Although additional cases have been reported in Europe, DYT16 remains rare, accounting for approximately 4.5% of idiopathic dystonia in a Brazilian cohort. Clinical expression varies both within and between families. Levodopa (l‐dopa) responsiveness is typically absent, and treatment options are limited. Importantly, the determinants of DBS outcomes in this disorder are not well established, particularly regarding the influence of age at onset, disease duration, and phenotypic complexity on treatment response.

Here, we describe an adult‐onset case of PRKRA‐related dystonia with sustained benefit from globus pallidus internus DBS (GPi‐DBS) and contextualize our findings alongside previously reported cases to explore the relationship between clinical phenotype, surgical and quality‐of‐life outcomes.

A 47‐year‐old man from Brazil presented with a 12‐year history of abnormal upper‐limb posturing. He had no relevant past medical history. At age 35, he developed dystonic postures that progressively spread to the cervical and paraspinal muscles, leading to severe gait impairment and frequent falls. Notably, the patient exhibited a positive geste antagoniste, as placing his hand on the occipital region temporarily alleviated his cervical and truncal dystonia to improve walking (Video ). Neurological examination revealed marked hyperextension of the cervicothoracic spine and generalized dystonia most prominent in the cervical region, speech, face, and upper limbs (Fig. ). Subtle rigidity was noted in the upper limbs, without bradykinesia or tremor. Cognitive assessment was unremarkable.

He is the first‐born child of consanguineous parents and has unaffected siblings. There was no family history of similar symptoms. No exposure to medications associated with secondary dystonia was identified. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was unremarkable, and an extensive workup for Wilson's disease and other acquired causes was negative. An l‐dopa trial (400 mg/day) was ineffective, and botulinum toxin injections provided only minimal benefit. Given the progressive course, genetic testing was performed. An Next‐Generation Sequencing (NGS) panel identified a homozygous pathogenic PRKRA variant (NM_003690.5: c.665C>T; p.Pro222Leu), confirming the diagnosis of DYT16.

Due to severe disability and resistance to medical therapy, the patient was referred for GPi‐DBS. Bilateral electrodes were implanted without perioperative complications. Initial programming was performed with conservative monopolar settings. Details on the stimulation's parameters are described in Supporting Information . Over a 2‐year follow‐up, the patient experienced marked clinical improvement, including the resolution of truncal dystonia and significant gait improvement (Video ). Quality of life improved (Brief World Health Organization Quality of Life Questionnaire (WHOQOL‐BREF): 74 preoperatively vs. 90 postoperatively), Burke–Fahn–Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) motor scores showed substantial reduction, ranging from 42 to 13 [69%], and medication burden was also reduced (Supporting Information ).

Among cases with available genetic data,, 3 patients harbored the same homozygous c.665C>T (p.Pro222Leu) variant. DBS yielded favorable outcomes in patients who initially presented with focal dystonia that later progressed to involve speech and ultimately generalized dystonia (Table ). When analyzing our case with previously reported patients, a sustained improvement is observed. Both juvenile‐ and adult‐onset cases demonstrate meaningful motor improvement following GPi‐DBS, evidenced by a statistically significant reduction in BFMDRS motor subscores, with an average improvement of 54% from baseline (P < 0.001, Fig. ). Statistical correlation suggests that although higher baseline motor impairment is associated with greater absolute improvement (R
2 = 0.64, Fig. ), disease duration was not a significant determinant of outcome (R
2 = 0.07, Fig. ). This indicates that long‐standing disease does not preclude a favorable surgical response, suggesting that GPi‐DBS remains a viable intervention even in the later stages of the condition.,

Quality of life was assessed using the WHOQOL‐BREF before and after DBS. Over 2 years following DBS, improvements were observed across all four WHOQOL‐BREF domains. The physical domain increased from 6.86 to 10.86 (+58.3%), the psychological domain from 11.33 to 14.67 (+29.4%), the social relationships domain from 10.67 to 13.33 (+25.0%), and the environmental domain from 12.50 to 15.50 (+24.0%). These findings indicate a broad improvement in patient‐reported quality of life following DBS, with the largest relative change observed in the physical domain.

Taken together, these observations suggest that PRKRA‐related dystonia should be recognized among the genetic dystonias responsive to GPi‐DBS, particularly in patients harboring the p.Pro222Leu variant. In addition, quality of life is highlighted as a good outcome for DYT‐PRKRA for the first time in literature.
