 Purpose
Leading edge erosion (LEE) of wind turbine blades has been identified as a major
factor in decreased wind turbine blade lifetimes and energy output over time.
Accordingly, the International Energy Agency Wind Technology Collaboration
Programme (IEA Wind TCP) has created the Task 46 Phase 2 to undertake
cooperative research in the key topic of blade erosion. The purpose of Task 46 Erosion
Phase 2 is to further improve understanding of erosion driving factors, develop
datasets and model tools to enhance prediction of leading-edge erosion likelihood,
identify damage at the earliest possible stage and advance potential solutions. The
scope of work covers several technical areas, reflecting the multidisciplinary nature of
the challenge. Participants in the task are given in Table 1.
This report is a product primarily of Work Package 2 Climatic conditions driving
blade erosion but cuts across all three scientific Work Packages.
The purpose of this report is to report the development, application and results from
the first comprehensive Phenomena Identification and Ranking Tables (PIRT)
assessment for wind turbine blade leading edge erosion. The final section articulates
a series of research recommendations based on the results of the PIRT.

 Executive Summary
Improved understanding of the drivers and mechanisms of wind turbine blade leading-
edge erosion (LEE) and durability/cost efficiency of damage reduction solutions are
important to the entire wind energy industry. This report presents the development,
application and results from the first comprehensive Phenomena Identification and
Ranking Tables (PIRT) assessment for LEE undertaken under the auspice of the
International Energy Agency Technology Collaboration Programme (TCP) Wind Task
46 Erosion of wind turbine blades. The PIRT is used to present and organize expert
knowledge on a specific topic and is employed here in an advanced manner that not
only identifies the specific physical phenomena of high priority in dictating LEE
occurrence/rates but also (i) the degree of consensus in phenomena rankings and (ii)
the degree to which fundamental knowledge has been translated to currently available
metrologies and measurement protocols and predictive modeling tools. Analyses of
the results of the PIRT are used to make several recommendations regarding future
research.

 7

1. Introduction
1.1 Wind turbine blade leading edge erosion
Over 350,000 wind turbines with a cumulative installed capacity of 1.1 TW are
operating worldwide and are making an increasingly important contribution to
electricity supply (GWEC 2025). Accordingly, there is interest in enhanced reliability
and optimizing Operations and Maintenance (O&M) activities. Damage to wind turbine
blades, including leading edge erosion (LEE), causes reduced aerodynamic efficiency,
reduces reliability and increases O&M costs. According to estimates, in 2020 blade
damage was responsible for excess costs to the wind energy industry of nearly a billion
U.S. dollar (US$) per year (Wood McKenzie 2021). More recent analyses have
indicated the global blade repair/maintenance/inspection market had a value of US$
6.6 billion in 2024 which is projected to increase to US$11.9 billion in 2035 (Jaiswal
2026). Improved understanding of the drivers and mechanisms of wind turbine blade
damage, including leading edge erosion, and durability/cost efficiency of damage
reduction solutions are important to wind farm owners/operators, wind turbine
manufacturers and broader system-wide energy planning.
1.2 Objective of this work
The objectives of this report are to document a Phenomena Identification and Ranking
Tables (PIRT) analysis performed to aid in prioritizing research to enhance the
Technology Readiness Levels (TRL) of solutions to mitigate/reduce LEE. Analyses of
the resulting data are then undertaken to inform and prioritize future research.
1.3 Phenomena Identification and Ranking Tables
Phenomena Identification and Ranking Tables (PIRT) (Hills; Maniaci; Naughton 2015;
Singh et al. 2019; Suehiro et al. 2015; Williams et al. 2020) are a tool that is applied in
support of efforts to advance knowledge regarding technical issues within a specific
field or application. While they evolved in the nuclear safety domain they have been
used in a range of applications, including wind plant performance (Maniaci and
Naughton 2019). The PIRT process is a mechanism to systematically identify
phenomena/processes and their likely causes/source and the variables that are
responsible for an undesired outcome (or uncertainty in prediction of the outcome).
Information gained can inform efforts to address/improve understanding of those
phenomena through guided, strategic (prioritized) future research and/or improved
research design to inform decision making and advance the TRL of solutions to
mitigate/reduce the probability of the undesired outcome. PIRT allows complex
phenomena to be viewed through a physics-based lens and systematically evaluate
relevant phenomena/parameters/processes in terms of their relative influence on an
outcome of interest. The PIRT process is multi-stage. First, phenomena/processes
that are important to e.g. safety, are identified usually based on literature surveys.
Second, each process/phenomenon is clearly articulated and then assessed in terms
of their importance to an outcome. Finally, the level of knowledge associated with each
process/phenomenon is quantified.
In this application it is used to identify key processes/phenomena of relevance to
dictating wind turbine blade LEE. Several methodological enhancements are enacted
so it also provides information that can guide enhanced experimental techniques, code
development and more robust data/model uncertainty quantification work.

 8

It is important to acknowledge that PIRT analyses leverage expert knowledge and so
are subjective, and that deriving insights into research priorities requires involvement
of a diverse group of motivated and well-informed participants.
1.4 Brief precis of a preliminary LEE PIRT performed during 2024
Research reported herein leverages preliminary work on the first ever PIRT analysis
for wind turbine blade leading edge erosion (Pryor et al. 2024) (see Appendix A).
Following best practice in prior PIRT analyses, in that preparatory study, 30
processes/phenomena of relevance to LEE were identified using literature surveys
and crowd sourcing from experts (see Table 1 in Appendix A for the
processes/phenomena identified). To aid organization of the processes/phenomena
(e.g. Non-hydrometeor weathering stressors (e.g. UV radiation, icing, thermal
expansion, aerosols (incl. dust & pollution)) they were clustered within four themes:
 • Theme 1: Atmospheric drivers
 • Theme 2: Damage detection and quantification
 • Theme 3: Materials response
 • Theme 4: Aerodynamic implications of LEE
A small sub-sample of domain experts were then asked to complete a Microsoft
ExcelTM spreadsheet where they provided:
 1) A ranking of the importance of each process/phenomenon to wind turbine blade
 leading edge erosion and/or uncertainty in prediction of LEE rates using; ‘High’,
 ‘Medium’ or ‘Low’.
 2) A ranking of the current state-of-the-art in terms of the ability to make
 measurements of that process/phenomenon using; ‘High’, ‘Medium’ or ‘Low’.
 3) A ranking of the degree to which that process/phenomenon is represented in
 state-of-the-art numerical models using; ‘High’, ‘Medium’ or ‘Low’.
This trio of linked questions represents an advance relative to prior PIRT analyses that
focused on ranking the importance of each process/phenomenon and reflects the
importance of both modeling and observational tools in the LEE research arena.
To advance the interpretability of the rankings, and enable quantitative data analyses,
the qualitative rankings were translated into numeric scores. Rankings of ‘high’ were
allocated 1 point, ‘medium’ were translated into 0.5 and ‘low’ were quantified as 0.
Finally, results across all respondents were then summarized to derive a mean ranking
and the standard deviation across respondents. Importantly, the standard deviation
was used to convey information regarding the divergence of opinion in terms of
rankings. This divergence reflects the degree of consensus and is also an important
mechanism to identify ambiguity in the process/phenomenon descriptions.
Although matrix-based surveys are perceived to be more complex for respondents and
thus to reduce participation (Liu 2017), a matrix based approach was used because it
allows us to identify phenomena/processes that have high importance and where
critical knowledge gaps preclude full treatment of those phenomena/processes in
numerical models or current measurement technologies and data analysis tools. To
permit rapid interpretation of the rankings, the mean scores from the matrix of
responses were clustered into four groups to aid visualization and to identify critical
areas for investment:

 9

 1) High importance (mean ranking > 0.8) and process-level understanding has
 been well-translated to measurement technologies and/or modeling (mean >
 0.5).
 2) High importance (mean ranking > 0.8) but process-level knowledge is lacking
 and/or translation of that knowledge to measurement and modeling capabilities
 is poor (mean < 0.5)
 3) Moderate importance (0.5 < mean ranking < 0.8) and process-level knowledge
 and translation to models and measurements incomplete (mean < 0.6).
 4) Low importance level scores (mean ranking < 0.5).
Investment to advance knowledge for phenomena/processes in group 2 (and to a
lesser degree group 3) are most likely to enhance the TRL of LEE solutions because
these phenomena/processes are of high importance to LEE occurrence
frequency/intensity but a lack of understanding/other limitations they are not well
represented in current numerical models and are currently not measured to the
required degree of fidelity. Conversely, phenomena/processes in group 1 are well-
understood and measured/modeled with high fidelity and thus only incremental
advances are likely to be achieved with further investment. Group 3 includes
phenomena/processes of importance to dictating LEE and/or uncertainty therein but
advances in knowledge and translation to improved modeling or measurement tools
are less likely to result such large improvements in TRL as items in group 2. Finally,
phenomena/processes in group 4 are of lower importance in dictating LEE
occurrence/rates or uncertainty in predictions of LEE occurrence/rates. Thus, while
research investments in these processes/phenomena will enhance scientific
knowledge, it may not measurably improve TRL for LEE mitigation.
1.5 Methodological advances/refinements adopted for this PIRT
Specific refinements to the preliminary PIRT made in the current research include:
 1) Consistent with best-practice (Rea and Parker 2014), the PIRT delivery
 mechanism was changed to employ purpose-built web-based survey software
 (QualtricsTM). The survey designers verified it could be accessed via a range of
 browsers (Google Chrome, Firefox, Microsoft Edge, Safari on both Windows
 and Mac OS and tablets) and on Android phones.
 2) A web-based dissemination survey mechanism was selected primarily for
 convenience, to allow rapid data collection from a large respondent pool and
 because of the cost-effectiveness. However, use of a survey tool does limit the
 ability to clarify any ambiguity in the questions. Thus, measures were employed
 to aid in the interpretability of each process/phenomenon:
 a. Short ‘explanations’ were added to clarify the meaning/significance of
 the process/phenomenon.
 b. Input was specifically sort from people who do not have English as their
 primary language, to remove excessively complex language in the
 survey that may confuse non-native speakers before deployment.
 3) An entirely new suite of processes/phenomena was compiled based on full
 input from a broader suite of LEE experts than was sampled in the preliminary
 PIRT summarized above. Specifically, input was sought from all 119 IEA Task

 10

 46 participants via a series of coordinated meetings within each of the task
 workpackages.
 4) The number of processes/phenomena included was reduced to 21 to ensure
 that the QualtricsTM estimated survey response time was below 10 minutes.
 This limit was informed by past research that has shown that the longer the
 expected length of time required to complete a survey, the lower number of
 respondents who started and completed the survey (Galesic and Bosnjak
 2009).
 5) The ranking categories were modified from 3 to 5 allowing for greater
 granularity in numerical scores derived from the survey. This is consistent with
 use of a 5 or 7 point scale that has been recommended as best practice to
 maximize information gained (Bloom; Fischer; Orme 1999). The 5 ratings were
 listed as; ‘HIGHEST’; ‘HIGH’; ‘MEDIUM’; ‘LOW’; LOWEST’ and were translated
 to numerical scores in five equally spaced increments from 0 to 1, where 1 is
 highest and medium is 0.5.

2. PIRT survey content
Text included on the survey cover (Figure 1) was designed to inform respondents of
the survey origin, objectives and expected completion time, while additionally
providing clear instructions regarding the information being requested.

As described above, a matrix based survey design was adopted where the
respondents first ranked a process/phenomenon in terms of its importance to wind
turbine blade LEE and then the degree to which knowledge regarding that
process/phenomenon has been translated into best practice for making
measurements and/or measurement technologies and then the degree to which
knowledge has been manifest in models (see the example given in Figure 2).

Figure 2. Example of the matrix format used to solicit input from respondents.
The 21 processes/phenomena were organized on three browser pages to reflect the
three themes/topics and thus technical workpackages within the Task 46 renewal:
 • Climatic conditions driving blade erosion. This WP seeks to advance the
 science necessary to provide a priori assessments of sites regarding the
 potential for excess LEE and hence short blade lifetimes, and to inform wind
 farm operation to enhance blade lifetimes. Intermediate steps include
 identification of key meteorological drivers, quantify spatiotemporal variability
 of those drivers, and provide a platform for technological innovation and model
 verification and validation exercises.
 • Wind turbine operations with erosion. This WP seeks to quantify the effect
 of LEE on wind turbine performance and assess the feasibility of erosion
 mitigating erosion via wind turbine control. Intermediate steps include
 standardization of damage reports, and advanced understanding of
 particle/droplet impingement in the leading edge of wind turbines.
 • Laboratory testing of erosion and material blade integration. This WP
 seeks to facilitate convergence of laboratory erosion testing practices and
 reduce related uncertainty and to integrate materials science perspectives.
 Intermediate steps include characterization of fatigue properties, damage
 propagation and material properties.
The 21 processes/phenomena that were selected for inclusion in the PIRT are as
follows:
 Process/Phenomenon 1 Hub-height wind speeds during precipitation
 events of different meteorological causes
 Explanation: Spatiotemporal variability in joint probability of precipitation
 (intensity/phase) and wind speed at wind turbine hub-height as a function of
 prevailing meteorological conditions (e.g. deep convection v. stratiform cloud
 systems). Note: High-frequency (< 30 min integration) information across a

 12

range of locations and long-term time series necessary to robustly describe the
tails of joint distributions.

Process/Phenomenon 2 Hydrometeor size distribution and phase as a
function of prevailing meteorological conditions
Explanation: Size-resolved number concentration of raindroplets or hailstones
as a function of prevailing meteorological conditions (e.g. deep convection v.
stratiform cloud systems). Note: needed to assess generalizability of existing
empirical size distributions.

Process/Phenomenon 3 Hydrometeor fall velocities as a function of
diameter and phase
Explanation: Size- and phase- resolved fall rates for hydrometeors needed to
verify theoretical predictions and empirical approximations (e.g. estimates from
Atlas, Gunn and Kinser)

Process/Phenomenon 4 Metrologies for hydroclimate properties as a
function of prevailing meteorology
Explanation: Evaluate instrument fidelity in terms of size distribution, fall velocity
and phase across a range of technologies as a function of deployment and
prevailing meteorology (e.g. wind speed, intensity of precipitation).

Process/Phenomenon 5 Impinged water as a function of hydrometeor
diameter/phase and prevailing flow conditions
Explanation: Blade capture efficiency as a function of hydrometeor diameter
needed to verify assumptions made in DNV best practice for a range of
turbulent flow and airfoil aerodynamics.

Process/Phenomenon 6 Space/time variability of hydroclimate conditions
Explanation: Need to quantify skillful scale of remote sensing observations
(including ground- and satellite-based instruments) and numerical weather
prediction models relative to actual gradients of e.g. precipitation intensity as a
function of prevailing meteorological conditions (e.g. deep convection v.
stratiform cloud systems) and location to guide use in LEE atlases

Process/Phenomenon 7 Non-hydrometeor weathering stressors (e.g., UV
radiation, icing, thermal expansion, aerosols (incl. dust and pollution))
Explanation: Quantify spatiotemporal variability in these meteorological
parameters, and materials responses of blade coating and LEP products to
these stressors. These data are also needed to inform LEP application best
practices for given locations.

Process/Phenomenon 8 Damage Classification
Explanation: Damage characterization from varying image types & methods to
translate to damage classification, for input to material loss and equivalent

Process/Phenomenon 9 Material loss through different operating
conditions
Explanation: Material loss as a function of wind speed, rotor speed,

Process/Phenomenon 10 Translating water impingement to material

Process/Phenomenon 11 Blade performance varying with surface

Explanation: Impact of erosion, surface preparation, and/or leading edge

Explanation: Turbine energy production considering blade performance along

Process/Phenomenon 14 Operational decision factors
Explanation: Modelling/estimates of time and cost implications for repair,

Process/Phenomenon 15 Repeatability and variability of Rain erosion
tester (RET) experiments
Explanation: Variability in RET outcomes on reference materials (e.g.

 14

 Process/Phenomenon 16 Influence of material defects in RET
 experiments
 Explanation: It remains difficult to identify and characterize surface or
 subsurface defects before failure, making it challenging to separate intrinsic
 material behavior from defect-driven initiation.

 Process/Phenomenon 17 Incubation time as a base metric for lifetime
 Explanation: Incubation time is measurable and repeatable, but captures only
 the onset of erosion and not subsequent damage evolution.

 Process/Phenomenon 18 RET representation of atmospheric conditions
 Explanation: RET experimental conditions do not reproduce the full range of
 observed hydrometeor phases, droplet size spectra, or mixed-phase events

 Process/Phenomenon 19 Use of DNV-GL RP 0171
 Explanation: Following DNV-GL RP 0171 enables reproducibility across
 laboratories but the standard’s droplet distributions and velocities differ from in-
 service conditions, limiting its predictive relevance for field performance

 Process/Phenomenon 20 Post-incubation damage growth
 Explanation: Experimental observations show complex post-incubation
 phenomena such as crack coalescence, delamination, and mass loss, yet
 quantitative datasets remain scarce.

 Process/Phenomenon 21 Materials properties that dictate damage
 initiation and progress
 Explanation: Associations between RET erosion resistance and measurable
 properties such as fracture toughness, modulus, or water absorption are
 inconsistent.

4. PIRT survey results
4.1 Survey participation
Prior to reporting results from the survey, we note two important caveats:
 1) Although the survey was opened 115 times, a total of only 43 active (nearly
 complete) responses were received out of a possible total of 153. All other
 survey entries contained very few or no responses. For example, one
 respondent emailed the distributors to notify them they had summited a
 response in which they had left the survey entirely blank. The people who did
 not provide responses tended to have the survey open for an extended period.
 In many cases the survey was open > 20 minutes without a single response
 being given. “Acceptable” response rates to surveys vary across specializations
 and platforms (in-person, mail-in and online), but typically lie in the range of 40-
 75% (Sataloff and Vontela 2021). However, online surveys often have the
 lowest response rates (Tuten 2010). A meta-review of online surveys in
 education-related research found an average response rate of 44% (Wu; Zhao;
 Fils-Aime 2022), which would equate to 67 people in the current analysis. Thus,
 despite significant efforts to encourage participation and engage with making
 the survey comprehensible/easy to respond to, participation was
 disappointingly poor, but it is not unprecedented (Stedman et al. 2019). The
 cause/s of lack of participation from the substantial fraction of the population
 we sought to gain insights from is/are currently unclear. The survey was sent
 to individuals that are listed as participating in an active IEA task on this topic
 and/or were expected to attend a forthcoming workshop on this topic. Thus, in
 principle, they are informed about LEE and committed to seeking or enacting
 solutions to this challenge.
 2) Although the instructions requested that all respondents respond to all
 questions, some of the 43 responses were incomplete. A little over 10% of
 respondents only provided answers for a subset of the phenomena/processes,
 or ranked importance of one or more phenomenon/process but not translation
 of knowledge to measurements or modeling. This is likely related to individuals
 feeling that their expertise does not extend to all components of this multi-
 disciplinary challenge. To avoid confusion due to slight variations in sample size
 across the phenomena/processes all values are thus presented as a
 percentage of respondents.
4.2 Rankings
Results from the survey are summarized in Tables 2 and 3. In interpreting these tables,
recall that respondents were asked to provide three rankings for each
process/phenomenon (see detailed list in section 2). First, the importance of each
process/phenomenon to wind turbine blade leading edge erosion and/or uncertainty
in prediction of LEE rates. Thus, ‘Highest’ and ‘High’ indicate the highest levels of
importance. Secondly, the rankings of current state-of-the-art in terms of the ability to
make measurements of that process/phenomenon. Thus, ‘Highest’ and ‘High’
indicates there are high fidelity measurement technologies available. Third, rankings
of the degree to which that process/phenomenon is represented in state-of-the-art
numerical models. Thus, ‘Highest’ and ‘High’ indicates these processes/phenomena
are well-represented in current numerical models.

4.3 Data analyses
In the following, Spearman (non-parametric) ranked correlation coefficients are used
to assess the degree of association between different variables. For 20 degrees of
freedom (sample size minus 1), critical values for statistical significance at a p value
of 0.1 and 0.05 are 0.284 and 0.361, respectively (Weathington; Cunningham;
Pittenger 2012). Thus, for associations to be statistically significant at the 90% and
95% confidence levels, the correlation coefficients must exceed 0.284 and 0.361,
respectively.
Perhaps unsurprisingly, given the effort placed in deriving high quality
processes/phenomena for inclusion in the PIRT analysis, all had mean importance
rankings above 0.6 (Table 3). The five most highly ranked processes/phenomena in
terms of importance (listed in order of mean importance) are:
• Process/Phenomenon 21 Materials properties that dictate damage initiation
 and progress
• Process/Phenomenon 14 Operational decision factors
• Process/Phenomenon 1 Hub-height wind speeds during precipitation events
 of different meteorological causes

 19

• Process/Phenomenon 2 Hydrometeor size distribution and phase as a
 function of prevailing meteorological conditions
• Process/Phenomenon 20 Post-incubation damage growth
There is a weak negative association between the mean and standard deviation of
importance rankings (Figure 3) which would suggest that although the respondents
may have varying primary fields of expertise, there is broad consensus on the rankings
of the 21 phenomena/processes in terms of their importance to determining rates of
LEE and/or uncertainty in LEE prediction. The Spearman (ranked) correlation
coefficient is -0.5, which is significantly different to zero at the 95% confidence level.
The degree of consensus in terms of the translation of knowledge to measurements
and modeling is considerably lower. The Spearman correlation coefficient for the mean
and the SD of rankings for both measurements and modeling are both in the range of
0 to -0.1, and thus do not differ significantly from zero. This result is somewhat
expected given that the level of knowledge regarding state-of-the-art observational
metrologies and modeling tools may vary across the study participants. However, it
may also reflect geographic or institutional variability in the access to state-of-the-art
technologies.

A clear feature in the mean rankings of importance is that the phenomena/processes
associated with the climate drivers (P1-P7) and Laboratory testing (P15-P21) span a
much larger dynamic range (0.18 and 0.19, respectively) than those associated with
topics in Operation with erosion (P8-P14) (range of 0.10) (Figure 3). This may suggest
that the phenomena/processes were slightly less distinct or discriminatory for the
Operation with erosion workpackage leading to respondents to rank multiple
processes/phenomena very similarly.
There are weak positive associations between the mean importance of each
process/phenomenon and the availability of measurement technologies to record that
process/phenomenon and/or translation of knowledge to models (Figure 4). This
implies past investments have broadly focused on improving knowledge of the primary
matters of importance. However, the Spearman correlation coefficients are < 0.2 and
thus do not differ from zero (indicating no significant relationship), which might suggest
the need to reprioritize research investments to focus more translating knowledge on
processes known/thought to be critical to dictating rates of LEE to improved
measurement or modeling technologies. It is hoped that this PIRT analysis can assist
with that activity.

Naturally, PIRT analyses are not a panacea, and it is necessary to employ other
mechanisms to address critical questions around ‘how good is good enough?’
However, it is noteworthy that for most (17 of 21) key processes/phenomena, the
degree to which knowledge has been translated into observational technologies
exceeds the degree to which it is manifest in current generation models. This is evident
from higher mean rankings for “current state-of-the-art in terms of the ability to make
measurements of that process/phenomenon” than “the degree to which that
process/phenomenon is represented in state-of-the-art numerical models” (Table 3).
This might imply that future investments should be more focused on model tool
development and/or indicate the importance of well-designed model verification and

validation exercises to better leverage available measurements to improve models and
assess model fidelity (Pryor; Barthelmie; Zhou 2025).

Some of these high priority processes/phenomena may superficially appear to be
surprising. An example is Process/Phenomenon 1 Hub-height wind speeds during
precipitation events of different meteorological causes. Simulation of hub-height wind
speeds in a climatological sense and for some event types using state-of-the-art tools
such as mesoscale and microscale tools (e.g. the Weather Research and Forecasting
(WRF) model and Large Eddy Simulation (Janiszeski and Crippa 2025) or with WRF
coupled to other tools (Davis et al. 2023)) generally exhibits relatively high fidelity. The
need for further research to improve modeling of hub-height wind speeds during
precipitation events implied by the PIRT reflects expert judgement that (i) small errors
in wind speed lead to large differences in wind turbine blade tip speeds if the wind
speed lies in the range between the cut in and rated power (Figure 5) and (ii) deep
convection events that tend to be associated with heaviest precipitation (and hail) are
also associated with very high, but gusty wind speeds, which are relatively challenging
to simulate (Kilroy and Thayer 2025; Pryor et al. 2023).

5. Key Conclusions/Recommendations
The PIRT analyses presented herein represent the first comprehensive attempt to
collate expert judgements on research priorities to enhance the TRL for solutions to
reduce Annual Energy Production (AEP) and revenue losses and wind turbine
operation and maintenance costs caused by wind turbine blade LEE. It was conducted

 23

under the auspices of the International Energy Agency Wind Energy (IEA) Technology
Collaboration Programme (TCP) Wind Task 46 on Erosion of wind turbine blades.
Based on the PIRT results and analyses described above we draw the following
conclusions:
LEE is increasingly recognized as a critical challenge to ensuring continued declines
in wind energy Levelized Cost of Energy and further enhancing reliability. The
consensus opinion of the PIRT respondent expert team is that further investment in a
wide range of research topics is necessary and can lead to tangible benefits in
reducing/mitigating LEE and premature loss of blade aerodynamic performance.
Among the topics perceived as most important there is high agreement among the
respondents. Two phenomena stand out as being the most important to reducing
uncertainty in LEE predictions:
• Materials properties that dictate damage initiation and progress
• Operational decision factors
There is a clear need to advance understanding regarding these processes. Perhaps
the more challenging of the two is to codify how wind farm owner/operators are making
and implementing operational decisions regarding estimates of time and cost
implications for repair and what types of new model tools/best practice
recommendations could prove most useful in this process.
Highest priorities for investments in improved measurement technologies and/or
modeling tools include (bold are direct implications from the PIRT, regular font is used
to denote recommendations from the author team):
• Hydrometeor size distribution and phase as a function of prevailing
 meteorological conditions. Metrologies for measuring these hydrometeor
 properties (e.g. disdrometers and vertically pointing/scanning radar) exist and are
 likely to remain the best-available technologies for the near future. Investments
 might reasonably be focused on improved characterization of measurement
 uncertainty and instrument durability to ensure resulting data are of a quality
 required to make long- and short-time assessments of LEE potential. Although the
 research and operational communities are undertaking some such assessments
 (including efforts organized within the IEA Task 46 (Dellwik et al. 2025; Pryor et al.
 2022)) a more integrative approach including instrument developers, commercial
 users and the research community would be highly beneficial. Recent research
 has evolved evidence that hail, although relatively infrequency relative to liquid
 precipitation, is a particularly important driver of blade damage which emphasizes
 the need for improved characterization of hail frequency/intensity and size
 distributions (Pryor and Barthelmie 2026). In the modeling context there are efforts
 underway within the atmospheric science community to advance new tools to more
 explicitly capture in-cloud processes responsible for hailstone development and
 evolution of in-cloud and below-cloud raindroplet and hailstone size distributions
 (Adams-Selin et al. 2023; Johnson; Xue; Jung 2024; Wu et al. 2022). The wind
 energy community should continue to engage with those efforts and to
 undertake/motivate V&V activities that specifically focus on joint probability
 distributions and fidelity during event of high importance to LEE (Pryor; Barthelmie;
 Zhou 2025; Pryor et al. 2023). In the near-term it seems likely that most numerical
 simulations will require application of empirical size distributions for hydrometeors
 in post-processing. Thus, further evaluation of the appropriate form of such

 24

 distributions (e.g. Best (Best 1950) v Marshall-Palmer (Marshall and Palmer 1948)
 for raindroplets and Chang and English (Cheng and English 1983) or alternatives
 for hailstones, is desirable and could be performed using measurements from the
 coordinated field experiment proposed above.
• Impinged water as a function of hydrometeor diameter/phase and prevailing
 flow conditions. Direct measurements of impinged water are very difficult in an
 operational context, but laboratory measurements could be taken within RET using
 particle tracking techniques (e.g. high-speed cameras (Gaunaa et al. 2018)). New
 advanced CFD tools are being developed for particle tracking (Hassanian and
 Riedel 2023) and could be further developed/applied. Both would allow more
 comprehensive assessment of assumptions such as those within DNV best
 practice (DNV 2021) that yields impingement efficiencies > 0.99 for rain droplet
 diamaters > 0.3 mm.
• Material loss through different operating conditions. Both measurements and
 modeling tools are needed to quantify material loss as a function of the time-history
 of wind speed, rotor speed and environmental conditions (including non-
 hydrometeor parameters such as UV exposure). Example blade samples from
 differing and well-characterized operating conditions could be used to build
 machine-learning emulators (models) of damage and mass loss. This would
 require close collaboration with owner/operator to obtain sufficient samples,
 potentially with augmentation through novel laboratory tests grounded by field
 samples.
• Repeatability and variability of rain erosion tester (RET) experiments. Plans
 with the IEA 46 task include evaluation of a single coating system across multiple
 test set-up to quantify repeatability. It is postulated that while some variations in
 experimental set-ups (e.g. delay between exposure to rain (Verma et al. 2024))
 maybe a primary cause of this variability (Kinsley et al. 2025), that material defects
 play a key role. This matter is addressed in the next bullet point.
• Influence of material defects in RET. Non-destructive techniques have been
 advanced to characterize defect detection in coatings for blades (Petersen et al.
 2023) and could be used to assess material defects prior to use in RET
 experiments. If different samples with varying defects can be selected and used in
 standardized RET experiments this would aid in improved understanding of their
 role in damage initiation and propagation in post-incubation damage growth (see
 below).
• Post-incubation damage growth. Fracture growth has been identified as an
 important damage mode in RET tests of wind turbine blades and initial experiments
 have commenced (Bech and Simon 2025). More research is needed to understand
 the material science and to link to material defects and RET experimental design.
Naturally, it is recommended to consider not only these phenomena in isolation but to
take a holistic perspective due the nature of the complex multi-disciplinary interactions
within the LEE topic. It would be useful to collect input in a second-phase PIRT after
the conclusion of IEA Task 46 phase 2 to establish whether greatest progress has
been made in translation of knowledge to measurement technologies/best practice
and modeling tools.

 In locations with good wind resources, onshore wind energy has the lowest LCoE of
 any electricity generation type [6]. However, LCoE from onshore wind energy is no longer
 declining [7], and costs for offshore deployments exceed those for onshore [8].
 O&M typically account for 25–30% of the lifecycle LCoE from wind turbines [9].
 Blades contribute > 20% of the overall cost of wind turbines [10], and blade integrity is
 a fundamental determinant of both O&M and power generation (AEP). An important
 contributing factor to wind turbine blade lifespan is leading-edge erosion (LEE). LEE refers
 to the material loss of wind turbine blade coatings leading to exposure and ultimately loss
 of the laminate that provides the structure of the blade. It results primarily from materials
 stresses induced when hydrometeors (e.g., rain droplets or hailstones) impact the rapidly
 rotating blades [11–14]. The material loss leads to a roughening of the surface, reducing
 lift and increasing drag [15], and thus negatively impacts AEP [15–19]. LEE requiring
 emergency blade repair can occur within two years of installation [20], which is far short
 of the expected lifetime of 30 years [21]. O&M expenditures associated with total blade
 replacement for onshore wind turbines are >$200,000 and blade replacement may lead to
 multiple days of lost power production [22].
 Wind turbines being deployed offshore are physically larger and have both longer
 blades and higher tip speeds than those deployed onshore [23]. This leads to higher closing
 velocities with falling hydrometeors, higher materials stresses [20], and thus a higher
 erosion rate [24,25]. Wind turbines being deployed at the South Fork Wind Farm off the
 USA East Coast are GE Haliade-X 13 MW machines with blades 107 m in length, each of
 which weighs 55 tons [26]. These wind turbines have maximum tip speeds of >90 ms−1 .
 The 22 MW reference wind turbine that has recently been released for use in offshore
 research [27] has even longer blades and a rated tip speed of 105 ms−1 . Manufacturing
 defects and damage during transportation/deployment are likely enhanced in longer
 blades [28,29] and even small imperfections may be important sites for the initiation of
 LEE [29]. Thus, LEE issues may be particularly prominent offshore where O&M costs are
 much higher [23], and the avoidance of excess maintenance is paramount to reducing the
 LCoE. In 2018, Renew.Biz reported that the consortium behind the 630 MW London Array
 in the UK was planning “emergency” blade repair to 140 of the project’s 175 wind turbines
 and that ‘A similar repair campaign has begun at Orsted’s 400 MW Anholt wind farm
 off Denmark, where 87 of 111 . . .. . . turbines are being fitted with rubber-like shells to fix
 the problem’.
 LEE thus represents an important challenge to the cost-effectiveness and reliability of
 wind-derived electricity and there is a need to advance the fundamental understanding of
 the processes that cause LEE and to advance effective solutions.

 1.2.2. Theme 2—Damage Detection and Quantification
 LEE pattern categorization frequently employs five classes, with Class 1 “small pin-
 holes” exhibiting an erosion depth of 0.1–0.2 mm, an average feature damage of 2 mm,
 and an approximate cord coverage of 3% [17]. Even Class 1 LEE may result in AEP loss.
 The early detection and close monitoring of damage progress can help optimize mitigation
 strategies and identify appropriate maintenance actions (patching and minor repair to
 full-scale blade removal) [50–53].
 Current techniques for real-time wind turbine blade damage detection [54,55] include
 vibration-based techniques [56], ultrasound scanning techniques [57], acoustic emission
 monitoring [58], and machine vision, image, or video processing [59]. Three out of four of
 these LEE detection methods (acoustic emission, ultrasound, vibration-based techniques)
 require the use of physical sensors placed along the blade or near the wind turbine, which
 can be costly and vulnerable to damage in extreme meteorological conditions [60]. Image
 processing methods can be used to assess blade conditions from 2D and 3D images or
 videos captured by instrumentation deployed on unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) [61] or
 taken by technicians [62]. However, as discussed below, the fidelity of different damage
 detection methods has not been fully quantified.

 1.2.3. Theme 3—Material Response
 Wind turbine blades are made of composites (e.g., epoxy or polyester, with reinforcing
 glass or carbon fibers) [63] coated to protect them by distributing and absorbing energy
 from hydrometeor and other impacts [64]. Defects such as air bubbles in these coatings
 have a critical impact on crack initialization [65] and re-emphasize the importance of wind
 turbine blade manufacturing quality in dictating erosion rates.
 Erosion mechanics comprises an incubation period during which no damage is ob-
 served but microstructural material changes can generate nucleation sites for subsequent
 material removal. Material removal commences when a threshold level of accumulated
 impacts is reached [66]. This is followed by a period during which additional impacts lead
 to observable damage as stress waves propagate from impact locations. This leads to the
 growth of pits/cracks and an increase in material loss [67–69]. The number of impacts
 required to reach the threshold at which material failure becomes evident is thus a nonlinear
 function of the number, magnitude, and phase of the hydrometeors and the hydrometeor
 closing velocity plus the material strength [70].
 Whirling Arm Rain ERosion testers (WARERs, or more simply rain erosion testers,
 RET) artificially simulate the erosion process by spinning a sample of the blade, often with
 a leading-edge protection applied, at very high speeds and bombarding the sample with
 liquid droplets (of a confined droplet diameter range) supplied via needles [71]. These
 experiments can be used to develop VN curves and thus to derive empirical coefficients for
 use in Equation (2). However, the range of closing velocities sampled and used to derive
 the fitting parameters m and H0 specified below in Equation (2) for hydrometeor D of
 0.76 mm are 90 to 150 ms−1 , and thus exceed many of those that will occur.
 Alternatively, a range of modeling techniques have been advanced to simulate the pro-
 cess of material stresses that lead to LEE as a function of hydrometeor size distribution and
 closing velocity [68,70,72]. The simplest method is to employ the Springer model [73,74]
 combined with Miner’s rule to integrate across all hydrometeor diameters (and time) to
 quantify the accumulated distance to failure (ADF) [66,75]. However, these simple engi-
 neering models of LEE include multiple coefficients/assumptions that limit the robustness
 of lifetime estimates, and when invoking Miner’s rule, the damage is assumed to be linearly
 accumulated.

 1.2.4. Theme 4—Aerodynamic Implications
 A smooth leading edge reduces turbulence and drag, optimizing the lift-to-drag ratio
 of a wind turbine blade. The outer part of the blade (toward the tip) produces most of
 the energy and experiences the highest relative wind speeds. Thus, the leading edge
 toward the blade tip is both the most vulnerable to roughening by material loss and is
 also where reducing lift/increasing drag maximizes the negative impacts on the AEP. The
 maximum lift force on blades has been modeled to be reduced for damage associated with
 roughness heights of 0.11 mm for a rotor with a 175 m diameter [16]. Erosion classes 3
 to 5 (large patches of missing coating, the erosion of laminate, and the complete loss of
 laminate, respectively), are associated with AEP reductions of 1–5% [76]. Recent reports
 found LEE-induced AEP losses from onshore wind turbines after only 1–3 years [77], but
 there is a paucity of data regarding underlying blade LEE topologies. The damage location
 on the blade is known to play a critical role in the alteration of the aerodynamic behavior,
 and so there are clear links between Themes 2 and 4 [78].
 The Simplified Aerodynamic Loss Tool (SALT) [79] can be used to illustrate the pre-
 dicted effect of erosion on the power coefficient (CP ) and the AEP loss relative to clean or
 undamaged blades, while acknowledging that it omits many of the details of more complex
 models [80]. Within SALT, damage is specified in 2% increments over the outer 70% of the
 blade (location r as a fraction of blade radius R) using a five-level categorization. Category
 a is undamaged, and the lift-to-drag ratio loss factor is 1. Category e represents the most
 severe damage which is deeper than 0.3% of the blade chord and the lift-to-drag ratio loss
 factor = 0.3. For the IEA 15 MW reference wind turbine [81] and a hub-height wind speed

 Both classes of solution require a detailed assessment of site conditions regarding likely
 severity of LEE since the incubation, transition, and steady state progression of damage on
 the leading edge differs as a function of precipitation climate and possibly other operating
 conditions [16]. A quantitative comparison of overall cost effectiveness requires detailed
 information regarding (i) AEP loss from LEE, LEP application (including down-time if
 LEP is applied post-commissioning) and/or the adoption of erosion safe mode. (ii) The
 cost of LEP measures and the expense of deployment [22] and robust economic/financial
 information such as the spot market price for electricity [95]. Ultimately, an optimal solution
 is likely to be one which maximizes revenues over a specific period of time for a given
 wind farm [96]. The consideration of either solution type for a given situation demands
 robust knowledge of processes/phenomena in each of the four themes described above.
 Thus, the issue confronting the wind energy industry is how to prioritize research to reduce
 uncertainty and increase confidence for wind farm owners/operators and enhance the TRL
 for LEE mitigation.

 1.4. Objectives of This Work
 Our goal is to map priorities for LEE research that can enhance the technology readi-
 ness levels for LEE solutions such as those described in Section 1.3, and thus aid in reducing
 the LCoE from wind turbines. To achieve this goal, we undertook, and herein present,
 the first Phenomena Identification and Ranking Tables (PIRT) assessment for wind tur-
 bine blade LEE (Section 2). Following the presentation of the PIRT analysis, we discuss
 the research required and/or being conducted to address the highest priority research
 needs identified during the PIRT process and that are necessary to enhanced TRLs of LEE
 solutions (Section 3). We conclude in Section 4 by describing the next steps.

 3. Discussion of Exemplar Research Activities Designed to Address Critical Research
 Needs Identified in the PIRT Process and Thus to Improve TRLs of LEE Solutions
 3.1. Phenomena/Processes Given Tier 1 Priority Within the Atmospheric Drivers Theme
 Two processes/phenomena within Theme 1 were identified as tier 1 priority: hydrome-
 teor size distribution (HSD) and phase. The narratives supplied within the PIRT framework
 and past research suggest that although these are phenomena of importance, the knowl-
 edge or translation of knowledge to improve measurement/data analysis procedures or to
 modeling tools is insufficient. Materials stresses are demonstrably a function of the number
 and diameter of impinging hydrometeors. The HSD (and hydrometeor phase) is also a
 function of precipitation intensity and of temporal and spatial scale [104]. For example, an
 analyses of data from the US Southern Great Plains showed that 10% of 1 min precipitation
 rates exceeded 4.5 mmhr−1 , while the 90th percentile value for 10 min precipitation rates
 were <2.3 mmhr−1 [14]. A study in Switzerland using automated hail sensors found that
 75 % of local hailfalls lasted just a few minutes (from less than 4.4 min to less than 7.7 min,
 depending on a parameter to delineate the events) and that 75 % of the impacts occurred in
 less than 3.3 min to less than 4.7 min [105]. These findings imply not only a need for the
 robust assessments of precipitation rate, HSD, and phase but also that such data, whether
 from measurements or models, need to be available at high spatiotemporal resolution.
 A range of technologies exist to measure the precipitation intensity (collectively re-
 ferred to as rain gauges (RG)) [106] and HSD (i.e., instruments that measure hydrometeor
 number concentrations in size classes and are referred to as disdrometers) [14]. Some
 disdrometers also measure the fall velocity, phase, and sphericity (which is required to
 compute the hydrometeor mass and kinetic energy transfer) [14]. In the case of optical
 (or laser) disdrometers, the hydrometeor D is measured by the number of horizontal laser
 beams broken by the hydrometeor and the vf is derived from the duration of time that the
 beams are interrupted.

 3.2. Phenomena/Processes Given Tier 1 Priority Within the Damage Detection and
 Quantification Theme
 This PIRT process resulted in one phenomenon/process being given tier 1 priority
 within the damage detection and quantification theme: translating water impingement to

Energies 2024, 17, 6285                                                                                            16 of 29

 material loss/stress (e.g., metrics: kinetic energy, Springer–ADF, VN curves). Although
 this topic could legitimately be included under Theme 3—material response, the specific
 theme under which it was listed is likely not a critical determinant of the PIRT rating. As
 described above, computing the accumulated kinetic energy (AKE) of collisions between
 falling hydrometeors and rotating blades through time is trivial presuming adequate
 data regarding the hydrometeors and hub-height wind speed are available at high time
 resolution. However, AKE does not directly translate to material damage.
 Springer’s model uses the material properties of the blade and coating and the hy-
 drometeor impact number, diameter, velocity, and impact angle to estimate a distance to
 failure or the end of the incubation period for coating wear for each hydrometeor diameter
 that combined with Miner’s rule is used to estimate ADF [94]. However, Springer’s model
 is not very mechanistically defined and the parameter estimates are highly uncertain [66].
 As described above, many RET experiments are confined to a fairly narrow range
 of droplet sizes and can generate only liquid droplets. However, actual precipitation
 comprises an ensemble of multiple hydrometeor diameters. A recommended practice
 from DNV [129] considers only one droplet diameter (D = 2.38 mm) that naturally will not
 reflect the range of observed hydrometeors. Indeed, based on data from the US Southern
 Great Plains, where deep convection and intense precipitation is relatively common [14],
 the mass-weight hydrometeor mean diameter was ≥2.38 mm during only 6% of 1 min
 precipitation periods. Further, to achieve damage results in a reasonable time (i.e., to
 accelerate erosion), RETs are operated at higher closing velocities than is representative
 of real operating conditions. The resulting VN curves are then extrapolated to derive
 estimates at lower vc of the number of impacts at a given diameter that would yield
 damage. Testing viscoelastic coatings at very high closing velocities may result in rain
 erosion testers underestimating coating or LEP durability because wind turbines frequently
 operate at lower tip speeds. A comprehensive rain erosion test with multiple droplet sizes
 underlines the need for further research on the derivation of the VN curves from RETs [130].
 More detail is given in Section 3.3.
 Other phenomena/processes in the damage detection theme that are characterized as
 tier 2 priority for research relate to the accuracy of damage estimates. The use of drones
 and robots for blade inspection is becoming more routine, particularly for larger wind
 turbines and offshore wind farms and potentially decreases costs/time/risk of injury to
 technicians [131]. The full automation of damage detection data derived using such tools
 is leveraging advanced machine learning (ML) image processing tools [62,132]. Further
 innovations in this field include the construction of digital twins using high-resolution
 topographic leading-edge roughness (LER) data from operating/decommissioned blades
 that can be analyzed aerodynamically using 3D computational fluid dynamics (CFD) or
 wind tunnels [133].
 Efforts to commercialize damage detection solutions are ongoing (e.g., using thermal
 imaging [134], laser profilometry [135], or gloss measurement [136]) implying relatively
 high TRLs, even as research is being conducted to evaluate efficacy as a function of damage
 severity and extent [137].

 3.3. Phenomena/Processes Given Tier 1 Priority Within the Material Response Theme
 This PIRT analysis identified two phenomena within Theme 3, material response
 as tier 1 priority for research that links to the usefulness of RETs, and specifically their
 representation of atmospheric conditions including the hydrometeors phase (e.g., rain and
 hail), size distributions and collision velocities [12], and whether accelerated lab tests repre-
 sent the pre-stressing of blade materials that enhances hydrometeor erosion of the leading
 edge [138]. These concerns also link to the second tier 1 research priority: methodologies to
 translate lab experimental data (incl. rain erosion testers) to field conditions and failure
 modes (see Section 3.2).
 Important new research is testing multiple key aspects of the translation of RET to real-
 world conditions. For example, RETs tend to operate with the continuous bombardment

Energies 2024, 17, 6285                                                                                         17 of 29

 of droplets, while in the real-world precipitation it is discontinuous. Experiments with
 a pulsating jet erosion tester have evolved evidence that the duration of time between
 precipitation events may play a role in dictating the number of droplet impacts required to
 reach the end of the incubation time [139]. Recent RET tests performed with and without
 UV exposure have found that UV weathering reduced the LEE coating life by about 30%,
 which greatly influenced the resulting VN curve parameters [140].
 Experimental technologies have an important role in projecting damage emergence
 and progression, but mechanistically sound numerical models can permit more diagnostic
 analyses and sampling across a broader spectrum of conditions. An important source
 of uncertainty in such numerical models is that the precise composition of LEPs and/or
 coating is proprietary. In addition, the temperature and strain rate sensitivity of the flow
 stress are either ignored in modeling or at best implemented with empirical constitutive
 equations. This may lead to significant deviations from reality considering the adiabatic
 nature of hydrometeor impacts deforming surface layers at relatively high strain rates [141].
 More sophisticated and explicit models such as finite element (FE) models of mul-
 tiple liquid impact on multilayered viscoelastic materials take into account microscale
 material structure and porosity [84,142] and are preferable to empirical or semi-empirical
 models. However, they are relatively computationally demanding and require information
 regarding a range of material properties and behaviors that can be difficult to acquire. The
 computational cost is amplified if all possible combinations of hydrometeor D and vc are
 to be included in coating lifetime estimations. Thus, an emerging area of research is the
 construction of ML emulators conditioned using the output from numerically sophisticated
 models but taking the form of considerably faster closed-form architectures [143]. Such
 emulators can be used to more rapidly and efficiently evaluate the uncertainty space. An
 example is the incorporation of an ML model trained by the output of FE simulations of the
 spatial and temporal evolution of the stress field in the coating for various impact speeds
 and hydrometeor diameters (see the schematic in Figure 7). To illustrate this potential, a
 surrogate model based on a neural network was trained to make predictions for the peak
 stresses in the coating layer. A relatively small number of FE simulations was used to
 generate training data for droplet diameters (D) of 0.5 to 4 mm, and impact speeds (vc )
 between 80 and 90 ms−1 . A neural network surrogate model was trained to predict peak
 von Mises stresses at each point in the coating as a function of D and vc . An independent
 set of FE simulations was used to evaluate the surrogate model predictions (Figure 8). The
 ML predictions capture the topology of the peak stress contour, but the peak values show
 an error of ~10% relative to independent FE simulations. Building a larger suite of training
 simulations would likely aid in building a more robust surrogate model.
 In principle, the workflow shown in Figure 7 could be expanded such that wind
 speed, rain intensity, and HSDs measured or modeled for any location can be combined
 with the surrogate model to obtain coating stresses for all possible combinations of impact
 parameters in an analogous manner to their use with the Springer model. The properties
 of the coating material could also be used as input to the machine learning model, and in
 principle this workflow can be extended to estimate not only the lifetimes of coatings, but
 also the levels of surface damage for estimating AEP losses.
 While the use of ML-based surrogate models shows great promise, the response of
 viscoelastic polyurethane-based coatings depends on the loading rate, temperature, and
 the level of experienced strain. A more thorough experimental characterization of these
 materials is required, which includes high- and low-rate uniaxial data for wide strain
 ranges, dynamic mechanical analysis, cyclic loading–reloading, and volumetric strain
 measurements. Data from RET experiments can aid in determining parameters related to
 the fatigue behavior of coatings and to enhance the accuracy of predictions. Improvements
 in experimental procedures related to RET are therefore also highly valuable.

 3.4. Phenomena/Processes Given Tier 1 Priority Within the Aerodynamics Theme
 Finally, three phenomena/processes were identified as tier 1 priority in the aerody-
 namic implications theme: (a) Quantification of damage and surface roughness progression
 through time. This links strongly to Theme 2—damage detection. (b) Attribution of
 AEP loss to LEE (via effective surface roughness). (c) Optimization of damage repair
 solution/timing.
 The quantification of wind turbine power and AEP losses due to LEE typically relies
 on blade force coefficient data obtained with wind tunnel testing or simulations with com-
 putational fluid dynamics (CFD) models [144,145]. In both cases, the geometry of damage
 and corresponding surface roughness at any time between installation and leading-edge
 resurfacing are key to achieving reliable estimates of the blade performance degradation.
 For moderate to intermediate LEE, which typically corresponds to damage of the thin
 external protection system of the leading edge (e.g., coating), the effects of roughness can
 be modeled by the means of the equivalent sand grain roughness [146]. The equivalent
 roughness height, yielding the same wall shear stress as that achieved with the observed
 roughness, can be obtained by using geometry, experimental data, or very high-fidelity
 CFD [147]. Their use for LEE applications, however, is associated with uncertainty, in part
 due to the difficulty of measuring blade roughness with sufficient resolution. One of the
 aims of the leading-edge roughness categorization (LERcat) efforts is to reduce this uncer-
 tainty [76]. When LEE becomes severe, with damage also to the leading-edge composite
 material, the sand grain model is no longer applicable, and the erosion geometry needs
 to be resolved [148]. The above highlights the importance of acquiring, with sufficient
 resolution, the depth and surface map of LEE and thus links to new innovations in damage
 characterization mentioned under Theme 2.
 Once erosion topographies are acquired with adequate geometric resolution, ML can
 also play a key role in developing blade predictive maintenance frameworks by providing
 erosion aerodynamics and resulting AEP losses, as demonstrated with the AEP loss predic-
 tion system (ALPS) [144]. Determining the LEE-induced blade performance degradation
 for each erosion topography encountered in operation would require numerous lengthy
 CFD analyses and specialized expertise for each wind turbine assessment, a cost increased
 by the large number of turbines in a wind farm and the potentially high temporal frequency
 of these assessments in the wind farm lifetime. An initial (one-off) execution of many
 CFD simulations corresponding to many diverse erosion topographies can be used to train
 the fast ML metamodels that can be used to quickly determine blade force coefficients
 for AEP loss assessment. Preliminary work, shown in Figure 9 [144], has demonstrated
 the high reliability of fast ML metamodels for predicting lift coefficient (cl ) and drag co-
 efficient (cd ) of eroded blade sections, allowing the ML models to be used for AEP loss
 assessment [148,149]. More development work is needed in this area to generalize these
 ML approaches, enable them to consider even wider LEE patterns observed in opera-
 tion, and consider the variability of the nominal blade geometry among different wind
 turbine classes.
 Optimizing the timing of blade leading-edge repair was identified as an important
 phenomena/process in the PIRT. The optimization of repair at any operating wind farm
 depends on factors such as wind turbine age, damage severity, cost of electricity, and
 accessibility. The considerations used by commercial wind farm owner/operators regarding
 repair decisions are usually considered proprietary and thus are held in confidence. Thus,
 information from WEICan is briefly presented below to illustrate the process by which
 repair decisions and LEP application were made and the results of those actions. WEICan
 owns and operates five 2 MW turbines on a coastal, high-wind site with turbines 1–4 being
 located on an escarpment and experiencing a very similar wind climate [150]. All wind
 turbines at WEICan have exhibited advanced levels of LEE since commissioning in 2013.
 WEICan have chosen to initiate repair measures prior to “moderate” or “severe” levels
 of erosion, and indeed before there was significant mass loss or clear detection via power
 curve degradation or acoustic tracking [151], due to factors such as the severity of the

       Energies 2024, 17, 6285                                                                                                          20 of 29

 winter climate which means the O&M window is relatively short, and the remote location
 means that access for more extensive O&M is challenging. The two main indications that
 trigger WEICan’s decision to carry out a blade repair are as follows:
 1. The rapid degradation of LEP. If an LEP product experiences significant peeling and
 bubbling within a year, it saves on repair expenses to replace it before the blade is
 completely exposed.
 2. The first sign of visible fiberglass. The more fiberglass is eroded away, the more blade
 preparation work is required before repairs. With light erosion, only sanding and
 buffing of the surface is required before reapplying the LEP, which takes about half a
 day per blade. With moderate to heavy erosion, the blade must be sanded, built back
x FOR PEER REVIEW 19 of 28
 into shape with additional fillers and fiberglass before reapplying the LEP product,
 which can take 1.5 days to 2 days per blade. Therefore, repairing blades at the first
 sign of visible fiberglass saves time and cost.

 Current leading-edge repair work instructions have many requirements, including
 filling, sanding, and cleaning with maximum and minimum temperatures and relative
 humidities for curing and drying, as well as wind speed restrictions, depending on the
 method used to access the blade. This leads to small windows of time where repair is even
 possible and long and expensive repair times. TRLs would be enhanced by simplifying the
 repair process so that there are fewer restrictions, and it can be performed more quickly
 and economically.
 A tier 2 priority in Theme 4 relates to the aerodynamic performance reductions due to
 LEP and their efficacy in slowing LEE. Data from the WEICan wind turbines was used in
 a decomposition analysis to remove effects due to prevailing meteorology (e.g., changes
 in the wind speed distribution before and after application) and isolate the impact of
 LEP on wind turbine performance. The results showed minimal to no improvements in
 performance due to LEP application and the resulting smoothing of the blade [151]. This is
 likely due to the high proportion of time that WEICan’s wind turbines spend operating
 at rated power when AEP loss due to LEE is minimum, as well as the fact that WEICan
 repairs blades before any reduction in performance is observed.
 Ultimately, decision-making with regard to LEE at WEICan relies on information from
 many of the tier 1 and tier 2 Themes: the existing and expected progression of damage, the
 resulting AEP reductions, and the impacts of LEP options. The uncertain durability of LEP
 options, perhaps resulting from unreliable LEP installation, has been the most substantial
 barrier to effective O&M planning for this site.

 4. Concluding Remarks and Next Steps
 The PIRT presented herein represents the first attempt to collate expert judgments on
 research priorities to enhance the TRL for solutions to reduce AEP (and revenue) losses and
 wind turbine operation and maintenance costs caused by wind turbine blade LEE. We used

Energies 2024, 17, 6285                                                                                                        22 of 29

 a snowball sampling technique to identify possible respondents [81] and had a relatively
 small sample size (n < 20). Thus, the results must be considered preliminary. Nevertheless,
 the PIRT presented herein yields some important insights and lays the foundation for a
 comprehensive PIRT survey of wind energy experts that will be conducted during 2025 via
 the International Energy Agency Wind Energy (IEA) Technology Collaboration Programme
 (TCP) Task 46: Leading-Edge Erosion.
 PIRT analyses are valuable because they allow the systematic identification of phe-
 nomena/processes of importance and that require further research to enhance TRLs or
 reduce safety risks. However, PIRT analyses are inherently subjective, since they leverage
 expert knowledge and judgment [82]. While some have advocated that PIRT methodologies
 should be based on literature-based meta-analyses [83], these too are not fully objective
 due to inherent biases in publishing [84]. An important advancement of this PIRT analysis
 is that the standard deviation of rankings across respondents is captured and presented
 to provide quantitative information about the presence or absence of consensus in the
 rankings. A divergence of opinions may derive from knowledge gaps due to the trans-
 disciplinary nature of a topic or the rapidly evolving nature of a complex topic. Expert
 knowledge-based frameworks for research priority identification using PIRT may also not
 fully reflect emerging issues. An example of this that was identified in the PIRT but not
 given a tier 1 ranking is the possibility of micro-plastic shedding in the ocean environments.
 This research topic is being addressed in the PREventing MIcroplastics pollution in SEa
 water from offshore wind (PREMISE) project [152]. The emergence of such new topics
 strongly advocates for PIRT assessments to be continuously updated to ensure they evolve
 as knowledge is advanced.
 The PIRT process and discussions summarized above indicate that the TRL for LEE
 solutions remains relatively low. However, investment in the priority areas articulated
 herein will enhance fundamental understanding and can be used to evolve a robust frame-
 work for end-to-end LEE prediction (Figure 7). Investments should be made in building
 a robust model V&V framework for each component of such a model chain [153]. Suc-
 cessful implementation of such a framework will require the sharing of a range of data
 from industrial partners. Needed information includes LEP product material properties,
 greater transparency regarding hardware settings in meteorological sensors, and data from
 operating wind farms linking the LEE state and the AEP. The end-to-end assessment of
 damage as a function of operating climate would also greatly benefit from sharing blade
 damage reports/images from operating wind farms for use in the evaluation of location-
 specific meteorologically driven LEE predictions [34]. Availability of time histories of
 wind turbine Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) data and adequately
 resolved LEE topographies for eroded blades will enable faster progress in blade predictive
 maintenance technologies.
