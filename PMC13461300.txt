Accurate Identification of Key Groups of Microeukaryotes Using Multimodal Deep Learning: An Integrated Classification Model Combining Morphological and Molecular Data
Authors: Yumeng Song, Lin Zheng, Alan Warren, Mingzhuang Zhu, Bailin Li, Weidong Ji, Xuming Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTraditional classification of flagellates (i.e., flagellated protists) relies on morphological traits or a single molecular marker, which suffer from subjectivity and limited data sources. This study proposes a multimodal deep learning model, Residual Multi‐Feature Attention‐50 (ResMFA50), that integrates photomicrographs and small subunit ribosomal RNA (SSU rRNA) gene sequences of flagellates. The dual‐branch architecture (DNA sequence and image branches) extracts local and global features, while the Multi‐Feature Attention (MFA) mechanism dynamically fuses heterogeneous data. Experiments were conducted on a dataset comprising 296 SSU rRNA gene sequences and 308 standardized photomicrographs, evaluated using 10‐fold cross‐validation. The results demonstrate that ResMFA50 achieves an accuracy of 92.5% in classifying flagellates at a batch size of eight, which is significantly higher than the accuracies achieved by SVM (82.4%), Random Forest (84.2%), EfficientNet (83.6%), ResNet50 (87.4%), and MMNet (91.3%). Moreover, ablation experiments comparing early, intermediate, and late fusion strategies demonstrate that the proposed late fusion scheme consistently outperforms other fusion timings, achieving improvements of 3.2%–3.8% over early fusion across different batch sizes. This study establishes a methodological foundation for modelling multimodal biological data in complex systems, advancing deep learning applications in integrative taxonomy. This advantage is attributed to the dual‐channel global pooling mechanism (Global Average/Max Pooling fusion), which balances the variance‐bias trade‐off through complementary strategies of spatial statistical smoothing and local salient feature detection, enhancing robustness to data scale expansion.

Introduction

Flagellates are not a formal taxonomic group but a diverse assemblage of eukaryotic microorganisms that possess one or more flagella at some life stage. They are distributed across several major eukaryotic supergroups, including Discoba (e.g., euglenids), Metamonada, Opisthokonta (e.g., choanoflagellates), and SAR (e.g., dinoflagellates) (Burki et al. 2020). These organisms have been found in diverse environments, ranging from soil and brackish water to freshwater, and even within the mucus and blood of animals (Carpenter et al. 2009; Shen 2013; Kočíková et al. 2018; Tuzlak et al. 2023). More than 7000 such flagellated species have been identified to date. Historically, they were divided into two classes (Phytomastigophorea and Zoomastigophorea) based on characteristics such as the presence of chloroplasts, flagellar structure and food storage mechanisms (Nisbet 1983; Shen 2013), but this classification does not reflect evolutionary relationships. Despite their pivotal roles in ecosystems and the ongoing research into this group, many elusive species of flagellates are yet to be uncovered, highlighting the depth of biodiversity that still awaits discovery (Rott 1988; Nicholls 1995; Arndt et al. 2000; Tolotti et al. 2003; Boenigk et al. 2005; Kammerlander et al. 2015).

Although species diversity is the cornerstone of classification, traditional morphological‐based species identification is constrained by various factors. The intricate and often chaotic life cycles of some species can lead to misidentification, causing confusion in classification history (Novis et al. 2023). Moreover, the scarcity of traditional taxonomists and the subjectivity inherent in the identification process exacerbate these challenges (Wheeler and Valdecasas 2005; Khuroo et al. 2007; Wägele et al. 2011). With the usage of genetic advancements, genetic data has offered new insights for species delimitation, and an increasing array of molecular techniques are being employed in species identification (Coyne and Orr 2004; Dawkins 2008). Initially, species identification combined SSU rRNA gene sequences with morphological information (Sugasawa et al. 2015; Tuzlak et al. 2023). However, the low variability of the SSU rRNA gene made effective species differentiation challenging. Consequently, multi‐gene sequences, such as ribosomal large subunit gene sequences and ITS1‐5.8S‐ITS2, were introduced. DNA barcoding is another widely used method that employs short nucleotide sequences to distinguish species (Hebert, Cywinska, et al. 2003; Hebert, Ratnasingham, and de Ratnasingham 2003; Sarkar et al. 2008; Zhang et al. 2008; Jones et al. 2011; Little 2011; Van Velzen et al. 2012; Polychronopoulos et al. 2014). However, relying solely on DNA barcoding for species identification has several drawbacks. Issues such as hybridization or gene introgression among closely related species, as well as minimal sequence differences in some recently diverged species, can lead to a lack of sufficient mitochondrial gene variation to differentiate species (Ermakov et al. 2015; Dai et al. 2010). Integrative taxonomy represents a multidisciplinary research paradigm that amalgamates insights from genetics, morphology, ecology, and other domains to foster a more holistic comprehension of species. This approach transcends the constraints of singular data sources, thereby bolstering the precision of species delimitation (Karbstein et al. 2024). This is particularly relevant for diverse protist groups like ciliates, where integrative approaches have successfully resolved complex taxonomic issues (Chi et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024). Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a pivotal force in this field, showcasing its prowess in automating feature learning and data synthesis. Its capabilities in managing and dissecting extensive, intricate datasets offer a novel technological underpinning for integrative taxonomy. A central tenet of integrative taxonomy is the reduction of subjectivity in delineating species boundaries. AI plays a crucial role in this endeavour by employing objective data analysis to curtail human biases, subsequently enhancing the dependability of species identification.

The deployment of machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) in species recognition is gaining momentum; these techniques autonomously discern and categorize species, thereby augmenting the efficacy and precision of species delimitation (Zhang et al. 2008, 2012, 2017; Valan et al. 2019; Knyshov et al. 2021; Klasen et al. 2022). The significance of supervised and unsupervised learning in the delimitation process is equally paramount (Goodfellow et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2022). These methodologies can discern species traits from vast data repositories without the need for human intervention, presenting innovative avenues for species identification.

Nevertheless, for DL, the challenges of data fusion and the management of enigmatic species remain in the realm of species delimitation. The effective amalgamation of data from a multitude of sources and the resolution of species that elude traditional delineation methods have emerged as focal points of contemporary research. The confluence of image and molecular data, coupled with the application of diverse models for swift and precise species identification, has been tentatively implemented in entomology. Notably, Badirli et al. (2021a, 2021b, 2023) proposed three Bayesian models utilizing 32,848 instances from 1040 known species, achieving an identification accuracy of 96.66% for described species, while the accuracy for undescribed species reached 81.39%. Lin et al. (2021) integrated niche characteristics with imagery for avian species identification, revealing that the proposed NicheNet's top‐1 accuracy surpassed that of image‐based identification (ResNet) by 7.73%. Yang et al. (2022) developed a novel convolutional neural network (MMNet) that integrates molecular and morphological data. Upon testing with ten independent datasets, MMNet demonstrated superior accuracy in species identification compared to four conventional methods across the board.

Recently, attention mechanisms have emerged as a powerful tool in deep learning, enabling models to dynamically focus on the most informative parts of the input data, akin to human perceptual systems (Vaswani et al. 2017). These mechanisms have shown remarkable success in various domains, including natural language processing and computer vision (Dosovitskiy et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2021; Dong et al. 2026). Notably, the multi‐scale attention mechanism, which enables models to attend to features at varying ranges or granularities—from local details to global contexts—has been particularly effective in capturing hierarchical information in complex data (Chen et al. 2016). In the context of multimodal biological data fusion, attention mechanisms can potentially resolve the heterogeneity between morphological and molecular features by learning to weigh their contributions adaptively (Yang et al. 2022). However, their application to integrative taxonomy, particularly for microeukaryotes with complex and scale‐variant features, remains underexplored.

Beyond the question of whether to fuse modalities, a more fundamental architectural question concerns when to fuse them. Previous studies have suggested that intermediate fusion, where features from each modality are first extracted independently and then combined before final classification, can outperform late fusion when cross‐modal interactions are present (Kösters et al. 2025). However, the optimal fusion timing may depend on the specific characteristics of the target organisms and the nature of data heterogeneity. For flagellates, which exhibit high morphological plasticity and limited molecular variability in standard barcodes, it remains unclear which fusion strategy best balances the complementary contributions of morphological and molecular information. To address this gap and resolve the heterogeneity between morphological and molecular features, we propose ResMFA50, an integrative taxonomic framework based on multimodal deep learning that employs late fusion with adaptive feature recalibration through a Multi‐Feature Attention mechanism. Within this framework, we systematically compare early, intermediate, and late fusion strategies to determine the optimal fusion timing for flagellate classification. By leveraging a dual‐branch feature alignment network and multiscale attention mechanisms, ResMFA50 pioneers the synergistic modelling of microscopic morphological traits and SSU rRNA gene sequences in flagellates. Evaluated on a high‐quality dataset comprising 296 SSU rRNA gene sequences and 308 standardized photomicrographs, the model achieves a classification accuracy of 92.5% at a batch size of eight, significantly outperforming state‐of‐the‐art benchmarks (EfficientNet: 83.6%, a relative improvement of 10.7%; ResNet50: 87.4%, a relative improvement of 5.8%). Through its global–local dual verification mechanism (Global Average/Max Pooling fusion) and residual cross‐modal connections, the model not only mitigates overfitting risks in data‐scarce scenarios but also resolves cross‐modal data heterogeneity and classification subjectivity biases. This work provides a robust and scalable technical solution for precise flagellate taxonomy and automated discovery of undescribed species, demonstrating strong practical potential.

Materials and Methods

Data Acquisition and Processing

Experimental data were collected from the published literature and bioinformatics databases. Systematic literature searches yielded photomicrographs and molecular datasets covering representative species from major eukaryotic supergroups, including Opisthokonta, SAR, Archaeplastida, Amoebozoa, Discoba, Metamonada, and Cryptista. Given the limited taxonomic coverage of this group in public repositories, a dataset was constructed, comprising full‐length SSU rRNA gene sequences from 296 taxa spanning 104 species, along with 308 standardized photomicrographs, with representative examples illustrated in Figure 1.

Due to the distinct morphological traits of flagellates, including their flagellar structures and ciliary complexes, conventional segmentation algorithms (e.g., PRNet) are insufficient for precise feature extraction. To mitigate this limitation, all images underwent manual denoising to remove background noise and non‐target artefacts, ensuring data integrity for downstream analyses.

For molecular data, SSU rRNA gene sequences were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database (Benson et al. 2013). For taxonomically complex groups (e.g., Euglena), multiple sequence alignments were conducted using the Rfam database (accessed using https://rfam.org) and cross‐validated against standard reference sequences (Kalvari et al. 2021). During sequence processing, base deletion sites were annotated with ‘Z’ as a placeholder. The letter ‘Z’ was chosen because it was unambiguous and does not conflict with standard nucleotide codes (A, T, C and G). This ensures clear representation of gaps during sequence alignment and feature extraction. Structural validation of open reading frames (ORFs) and coding sequence accuracy was performed manually using BioEdit (v7.2.5) (Hall 1999).

ResMFA50 Architecture

We developed ResMFA50, a multimodal deep learning framework for rapid and accurate flagellate identification. The model integrates DNA sequences and photomicrographs to extract and fuse biological features through a dual‐branch architecture (Figure 2). Key components include an MFA mechanism and residual connections.

Architecture of the multimodal deep learning model for classification using both image and SSU rRNA gene sequence data. Input layer (left) processes 224 × 224‐pixel photomicrographs (green branch) and SSU rRNA gene sequences (blue branch) simultaneously. Each branch extracts features through convolutional layers (Conv) and multi‐stage residual attention modules (ResMFA50‐1 to ResMFA50‐N). The fused features from the concatenation layer are fed into a Softmax layer for classification probability output (coloured boxes at right represent distinct biological classes).

The dual‐modal input processing includes a DNA sequence branch and an image branch. The DNA sequence branch inputs 296 SSU rRNA gene sequences, which are processed through a 1D convolutional layer to extract local nucleotide features. The image branch inputs 308 standardized photomicrographs (resolution: 224 × 224) and employs an improved ResNet50 backbone network (He et al. 2016). The MFA model was embedded to enhance the extraction of specialized morphological features such as flagella and organelles.

The MFA mechanism (Figure 3) dynamically recalibrates features by combining the contextual structure representation from Global Average Pooling (GAP) and the salient local high‐response regions (e.g., flagellar edges) identified by Global Max Pooling (GMP). The process is formally defined as: applying a shared feature transformation φ(x) = PWConv(ReLU(PWConv(x))), where PWConv is pointwise convolution and ReLU is the rectified linear unit.; fusing the transformed GAP and GMP features using element‐wise multiplication and combining them with the transformed base features φ(X) to yield X F = φ(X) ⊕ (φ(GAP(X)) ⊗ φ(GMP(X))), where ⊕ = broadcasting addition, and ⊗ = element‐wise multiplication; generating an attention map λ(X F ) using the softmax function λ; and finally recalibrating the original feature map X as X¯=X ⊗ λ(X F ). Furthermore, within the core ResMFA50 modules (incorporating residual connections to alleviate gradient vanishing), an enhanced MFA formulation (Figure 4) augments feature representation: X MFA = MFA(X ⊕ X%) and Y = (X ⊗ X MFA ) ⊕ (X ⊗ [1 − X MFA ]), where X% represents transformed input features and Y is the output, enabling adaptive feature recalibration based on the joint representation.

Architecture of the MFA Module. The MFA module processes input features (x) through three parallel branches. The left branch sequentially applies two pointwise convolutional layers with ReLU activation functions. The middle branch first performs GAP, followed by pointwise convolution, ReLU activation, and a final pointwise convolution layer. The right branch replaces GAP with GMP while maintaining the same subsequent operations. Outputs from all branches are fused through element‐wise addition and multiplication operations, culminating in a Softmax layer for classification.

Structure of the ResMFA50 Network, featuring a Multi‐Feature Attention (MFA) mechanism. Input data (X) undergoes processing through convolutional layers (Conv), ReLU activations (grey) and pooling (blue). The MFA module hierarchically aggregates multi‐stage feature maps via skip‐connections. Aggregated features are compressed by pointwise convolution (light orange) before Softmax (light blue) generates probability distributions. This design enhances classification robustness by integrating features across abstraction levels.

Finally, features extracted from both DNA and image branches were adaptively fused at a dedicated layer through an element‐wise addition operation, followed by species classification using a fully connected layer and Softmax activation to produce probability distributions over the target flagellate species.

Model Training and Evaluation

To enhance the model's generalization capability and robustness, multimodal data augmentation strategies were systematically implemented during training. For photomicrographs, random horizontal and vertical flips were applied to simulate variations in imaging perspectives, while scaling and cropping operations generated multi‐scale samples mimicking differences in magnification and spatial orientation. These techniques collectively improved the model's adaptability to morphological diversity. Concurrently, sequence redundancy was addressed through a random 20% segment deletion strategy applied to DNA data, which disrupted fixed sequence patterns and reduced the overfitting risks associated with repetitive motifs. To ensure rigorous evaluation, a 10‐fold cross‐validation framework was adopted. The dataset was partitioned into ten subsets, with each iteration reserving one subset for validation and the remaining nine for training. Performance metrics were calculated across all folds and averaged to provide statistically stable estimates of the model's predictive consistency. The proposed ResMFA50 was implemented using PyTorch 2.4.0 with CUD Devlin A 12.4 support (Paszke 2019). Training was conducted on a workstation equipped with NVIDIA RTX2080Ti (11 GB VRAM). All the parameters in our model are trained with random initialization. We train our ResMFA50 in an end‐to‐end manner. All the experiments in this paper were trained for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 (Kingma and Ba 2014).

Comprehensive Comparison With Baseline Models

To rigorously validate the generalization advantage and robustness of the proposed training framework, ResMFA50 was benchmarked against a diverse set of baseline models, including two traditional machine learning classifiers and three deep learning models: Support Vector Machine (SVM) (Cortes and Vapnik 1995), Random Forest (RF) (Breiman 2001), ResNet50 (He et al. 2016), MMNet (Yang et al. 2022), and EfficientNet (Tan and Le 2019). ResNet50 was selected as the canonical CNN baseline for image‐based hierarchical feature extraction. MMNet, a multimodal framework specialized in fine‐grained classification, was included to assess the efficacy of joint DNA‐image fusion, despite its higher computational complexity and limited interpretability. EfficientNet was chosen to explore the accuracy–efficiency trade‐off enabled by resource‐aware scaling, though its intricate architecture requires meticulous hyperparameter optimization. Additionally, SVM and RF were incorporated to evaluate whether end‐to‐end multimodal deep learning offers a tangible advantage over conventional classifiers that rely on manually fused feature representations. For these traditional models, modality‐specific features were extracted separately (e.g., k‐mer frequency vectors for DNA sequences and pretrained CNN features for images), concatenated, and used as input. All models were trained and evaluated on the same updated multimodal dataset (296 SSU rRNA sequences, 308 photomicrographs, 104 species) under a 10‐fold cross‐validation framework and identical data augmentation protocols. Evaluation metrics remained consistent across all models, enabling a direct comparison of feature fusion strategies, computational efficiency, batch‐size stability, and biological interpretability.

Results

Dataset Characteristics and Class Distribution

The multimodal dataset comprises 296 SSU rRNA gene sequences (ca. 1500–2000 bp in length) and 308 corresponding photomicrographs, covering 104 flagellate species spanning 40 genera (e.g., Euglena, Phacus, Lepocinclis, Trachelomonas, Cryptomonas, Dinobryon, Synura, Trichonympha, Trepomonas, Mastigella). Taxonomically complex and frequently observed genera, such as Euglena (Figure 1i–l), dominate the data‐rich groups, whereas rare or cryptic lineages (e.g., Holomastigotoides, Cononympha) constitute the majority of low‐sample species. This pronounced imbalance mirrors natural rarity‐abundance patterns but poses a substantial challenge for achieving uniform classification accuracy across the entire taxonomic spectrum when training deep learning models on the combined image‐sequence data.

Feature Attention and Classification Network: Enhanced Model Performance

The proposed neural network architecture establishes a multi‐level feature transformation framework, directly mapping raw input (x) to classification output (x') through pointwise convolution layers, nonlinear activation units, and heterogeneous global pooling modules (Figure 5). During the process of feature extraction, the 1 × 1 pointwise convolution layer dynamically adjusts the dimensions of the channels in order to reorganize cross‐channel information, with minimal computational cost. Its parameterized bottleneck structure balances feature representation capacity and computational complexity. By replacing conventional fully connected layers with ReLU activation, the model extracts high‐level local semantic features while reducing computational overhead. The unilateral suppression property of ReLU facilitates gradient propagation, accelerating gradient optimization.

Deep Feature Fusion Architecture with Heterogeneous Pooling Strategies. The network employs two complementary branches for feature enhancement. The left branch utilizes GAP: Input features are processed by pointwise convolution, ReLU activation, and a second pointwise convolution, followed by GAP and element‐wise multiplication with the original input X. The right branch adopts an identical structure but substitutes GAP with GMP. The fused features are classified via Softmax, achieving spatially adaptive feature recalibration.

A key innovation is the dual‐channel global feature compression mechanism, which combines GAP and GMP. The GMP layer highlights salient spatial features by extracting extreme responses from feature channels, preserving critical details. In contrast, the GAP layer generates stable feature representations through spatial statistical averaging, capturing global distribution characteristics. The integration of these complementary strategies that is, local salient pattern detection and global statistical smoothing, is achieved via pointwise convolution, thereby forming discriminative and robust high‐level semantic descriptors. Finally, the Softmax function maps these fused features into probability distributions, completing the transformation from continuous feature space to discrete categorical decisions.

The workflow is characterized by a rigorous hierarchical progression, with pointwise convolution facilitating intelligent channel selection, ReLU activation constructing nonlinear decision boundaries, heterogeneous global pooling directly mapping spatial information into channel descriptors, and hierarchical feature refinement establishing an end‐to‐end classification mechanism. This modular design optimizes the trade‐off between computational efficiency and representational capacity, offering a novel technical solution for complex pattern recognition tasks.

Model Performance and Benchmarking

Batch Size Sensitivity of ResMFA50 and Pooling Variants

Ablation experiments systematically evaluated the impact of batch size on ResMFA50, MFA‐MAX (GMP‐based), and MFA‐AVG (GAP‐based) models (Table 1). When the batch size was set to eight, ResMFA50 achieved the highest accuracy (92.5%), outperforming MFA‐AVG (90.4%) and MFA‐MAX (88.8%). This improvement can be attributed to gradient optimization driven by its residual structure and complementary pooling strategies. As the batch size increased to 16, ResMFA50 maintained superior performance (91.2%) compared to MFA‐AVG (89.7%) and MFA‐MAX (88.0%). At a batch size of 32, all models showed slight decreases, yet ResMFA50 remained the best (90.6%), confirming its robustness across different training scales. These findings underscore the structural advantages of ResMFA50, especially in small‐batch scenarios.

Comprehensive Comparison With Baseline Models

To rigorously validate the generalization advantage of ResMFA50, we expanded the benchmark to include two traditional machine learning classifiers (Support Vector Machine and Random Forest) alongside three deep learning models (EfficientNet, ResNet50 and MMNet). When batch size increases to 16 or 32, ResMFA50 maintains the highest accuracy (91.2% and 90.6%, respectively), demonstrating robust stability against batch size variations, a critical property for real‐world applications with limited sample sizes.

Comparison of Fusion Timing Strategies

To address the question of optimal fusion timing for multimodal flagellate classification, we implemented and evaluated three distinct fusion strategies: early fusion, where raw one‐hot encoded DNA sequences and flattened image pixel vectors are concatenated at the input level before any feature extraction; intermediate fusion, where DNA and image features are first extracted independently through dedicated branches (1D Conv for sequences, ResNet50 for images), then concatenated after passing through modality‐specific fully connected layers; and late fusion (ResMFA50), where each modality is processed to produce independent class probability vectors, followed by adaptive fusion through the Multi‐Feature Attention mechanism before final classification. All three variants were trained using identical data splits, augmentation protocols, and hyperparameters to ensure fair comparison.

Table 4 summarizes the classification accuracy of the three fusion strategies at batch sizes of 8, 16, and 32. At batch size = 8, ResMFA50 (late fusion) achieved an accuracy of 92.5%, substantially outperforming intermediate fusion (89.3%) and early fusion (85.7%). The performance advantage of late fusion persisted across all batch size settings: at batch size = 16, ResMFA50 reached 91.2% compared to 88.9% (intermediate) and 85.0% (early); at batch size = 32, ResMFA50 maintained 90.6% versus 87.4% (intermediate) and 84.1% (early). These results demonstrate that late fusion with joint deep feature evaluation through the MFA mechanism consistently outperforms both early and intermediate fusion strategies for flagellate classification. The superiority of late fusion can be attributed to the preservation of modality‐specific feature hierarchies and the adaptive recalibration of complementary information, which is particularly beneficial given the high morphological plasticity and limited molecular variability in flagellates.

Ablation Experiments Quantify the Contribution of Each Modality

To quantify the synergistic effect of multimodal fusion, we removed either the image branch or the sequence branch from the ResMFA50 framework to obtain two single‐modality variants, and compared them with the full ResMFA50 model through ablation experiments. Table 5 summarizes the results. At a batch size of 8, the full ResMFA50 achieves 92.5% accuracy, which is 7.2 percentage points higher than OnlySeq (85.3%) and 9.4 points higher than OnlyPic (83.1%). Similar improvements are observed at batch sizes of 16 and 32. These results clearly demonstrate that morphological and molecular features provide complementary information. Sequence data contribute stable evolutionary signals such as conserved rRNA domains, whereas image data capture species‐specific morphological details like flagellar shape and cell contour. Their fusion via the MFA mechanism effectively resolves the heterogeneity between modalities and leads to substantially improved classification robustness.

Generalization on an External Beetle Dataset

To assess whether ResMFA50 overfits to flagellate data or generalizes to other taxa, we further evaluated our model on the beetle dataset originally used by MMNet (Yang et al. 2022). This dataset comprises photomicrographs and DNA sequences of 123 beetle species, representing a different morphological and phylogenetic context from flagellates. All models were trained and tested under the same protocol as in our main experiments. As shown in Table 6, ResMFA50 consistently achieves the highest accuracy across all batch sizes, reaching 89.4% at batch size = 8, which outperforms MMNet (86.9%), ResNet50 (85.6%), and EfficientNet (81.2%). The performance gap narrows slightly compared to the flagellate dataset but remains clear, confirming that ResMFA50 learns transferable multimodal representations rather than dataset‐specific artefacts.

These complementary error patterns underscore the distinct information provided by each modality. Morphology captures structural features but can be confounded by phenotypic plasticity, while molecular data provide phylogenetic signals but may lack resolution in conserved barcode regions. By integrating both modalities, ResMFA50 effectively addresses these unimodal weaknesses, as evidenced by the substantially reduced errors across these taxonomically challenging groups in its confusion matrix. This observation is consistent with our ablation results (Table 5), where ResMFA50 (92.5%) substantially outperforms OnlySeq (85.3%) and OnlyPic (83.1%), confirming the synergistic effect of multimodal fusion for flagellate classification.

Discussion

The proposed multi‐level feature attention network provides an effective framework for biological classification by integrating morphological and molecular sequence features. Its core innovation, the ‘local‐global dual verification mechanism’, combines GMP and GAP to dynamically balance feature responses. This study demonstrates that GMP enhances sensitivity to critical morphological traits, such as flagellar structures, in photomicrographs, while GAP captures global molecular variation patterns through spatial smoothing. This dual‐channel compression addresses information dissipation in cross‐scale feature matching and implicitly regularizes parameter updates by balancing variance and bias. Experiments demonstrate its adaptability: GAP maintains 85.5% accuracy under local image blurring, while GMP‐derived features dominate classification for cryptic species with subtle molecular variations. Such dynamic synergy establishes a novel theoretical framework for fine‐grained taxonomy.

While multimodal deep learning approaches integrating morphological and molecular data have demonstrated significant promise for species identification in various taxa, their application has been predominantly focused on macroscopic organisms with well‐defined anatomical features and established molecular barcodes. For instance, Bayesian models applied to insect datasets comprising tens of thousands of specimens achieved high accuracy (Badirli et al. 2021a, 2021b, 2023), and frameworks like NicheNet (Lin et al. 2021) and MMNet (Yang et al. 2022) have successfully combined ecological niche or image data with molecular information for birds and other groups. However, the application of such integrative multimodal AI models to microeukaryotes, particularly flagellates, remains largely unexplored and presents distinct challenges (Pawlowski et al. 2012; Keeling et al. 2014). Flagellates exhibit high morphological plasticity and often lack stable and easily segmentable features in photomicrographs, especially when compared to insect wings or bird plumage. They also face limitations due to the variability constraints of standard molecular barcodes like the SSU rRNA gene. This taxonomic gap highlights the urgent need for specialized frameworks capable of addressing the inherent complexities of protistan data.

Architecturally, ResMFA50 employs residual connections to mitigate information decay in deep networks and utilizes pointwise convolution to dynamically allocate computational resources between morphological and molecular modalities. The model's efficacy is demonstrated by its ability to achieve an accuracy of 92.5% at a batch size of eight, outperforming SVM by 10.1 percentage points, Random Forest by 8.3 points, EfficientNet by 8.9 points, ResNet50 by 5.1 points and MMNet by 1.2 points. A critical architectural question concerns the optimal timing for fusing multimodal data. Recent work has suggested that intermediate fusion—where features are extracted independently then combined before final classification—can outperform late fusion when cross‐modal interactions are present (Kösters et al. 2025). These studies advocate for joint deep feature evaluation through a fully connected layer after intermediate fusion, arguing that such an approach better captures synergistic information between modalities. To test this hypothesis for flagellate classification, we systematically compared early, intermediate, and late fusion strategies. Contrary to the general findings in those studies, our ablation experiments (Table 6) demonstrate that late fusion (ResMFA50) consistently outperforms both early and intermediate fusion across all batch sizes. At batch size = 8, ResMFA50 achieved 92.5% accuracy, compared to 89.3% for intermediate fusion and 85.7% for early fusion. The relative improvement over intermediate fusion (3.2 percentage points) suggests that, at least for flagellates, the optimal fusion strategy may depend critically on the specific characteristics of the target organisms. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, flagellates exhibit high morphological plasticity and relatively low molecular divergence in the SSU rRNA gene (Pawlowski et al. 2012), making the two modalities asymmetrically informative. In such scenarios, late fusion preserves modality‐specific feature hierarchies, allowing each branch to optimize its representation without cross‐modal interference that could arise in intermediate fusion. Second, the Multi‐Feature Attention mechanism in ResMFA50 provides adaptive recalibration rather than fixed concatenation, enabling the model to dynamically adjust the contribution of each modality based on sample‐specific characteristics. This may be particularly beneficial for flagellates, where the relative informativeness of morphological versus molecular features can vary substantially across species due to factors such as cryptic speciation or convergent morphology. Third, the limited sample size typical of microeukaryote datasets (296 sequences and 308 images across 104 species in this study) may favour late fusion, as intermediate fusion risks overfitting when the number of parameters in the joint representation exceeds the available training samples. Indeed, Kösters et al. (2025) noted that while intermediate fusion performed best in three out of four datasets they examined, the optimal strategy varied across datasets, underscoring that fusion timing should be considered a tunable hyperparameter rather than a fixed design choice.

Notably, hierarchical feature refinement prevents performance degradation at a batch size of 32, maintaining an accuracy of 90.6%, which is significantly higher than the 83.1% accuracy of ResNet50. This highlights that the statistical constraints of GAP suppress high‐frequency parameter fluctuations, thereby enhancing robustness to training scale expansion. Compared to single‐pooling strategies, the dual‐channel mechanism of ResMFA50 provides a scalable solution for multimodal alignment in biological data.

Compared to multimodal frameworks such as MMNet (Yang et al. 2022), which relies on conventional feature fusion after dimensionality alignment, ResMFA50 introduces a fundamental innovation through its MFA mechanism. While MMNet integrates DNA and image features through concatenation after dimensionality alignment in a unified representation space, which may underutilize the discriminative potential of scale‐variant biological traits, the MFA module in ResMFA50 dynamically recalibrates heterogeneous features by integrating complementary pooling strategies (GAP and GMP), thereby adaptively enhancing both globally conserved patterns and locally salient morphological details. As illustrated in Figure 3, this allows the model to adaptively weight spatial‐statistical contexts (via GAP) and salient local patterns (via GMP), thereby resolving the limitations of fixed fusion schemes in handling scale‐variant biological traits (e.g., flagellar structures in images vs. conserved rRNA domains). The challenge of integrating morphological and molecular data is a common theme in protistology, as evidenced by recent studies on diverse ciliate groups (Li et al. 2024; Song et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025).

Such a design is biologically imperative for microeukaryotes like flagellates: GMP enhances sensitivity to transient morphological details (e.g., flagellar edges), while GAP captures broad molecular variation patterns through spatial smoothing. In contrast, MMNet's concatenation‐based fusion lacks this hierarchical prioritization, potentially diluting critical features when morphological and molecular signals are asymmetrically informative. Our ablation studies (Table 1) further validate that dual‐channel pooling (GAP/GMP) outperforms single‐pooling variants (MFA‐MAX or MFA‐AVG) by 0.3%–0.5% in accuracy, underscoring the synergy of complementary strategies.

Additionally, ResMFA50's residual cross‐modal connections (Figure 4) mitigate overfitting in data‐scarce scenarios—a challenge exacerbated in protistan taxonomy where sample sizes are limited. By contrast, MMNet's architecture, though effective for macroscopic taxa with abundant data, faces scalability constraints when applied to imbalanced microeukaryote datasets. Our model thus provides a tailored framework for multimodal alignment in resource‐constrained contexts, advancing beyond static fusion toward adaptive feature recalibration.

Transformer‐based models (e.g., Vision Transformers or DNABERT) offer advantages in global feature modelling, long‐range dependency capture, and parallel computation, and their self‐attention mechanism can more comprehensively capture subtle species differences. However, their parameter count is typically 80–100 million, which is approximately 3–4 times that of the ResNet‐50 used in this study. Given the moderate sample size of our dataset (296 sequences, 308 images), CNNs are more sample‐efficient and computationally lightweight, which motivated our ResNet‐based design. For large‐scale, multi‐gene, or high‐throughput species identification tasks (e.g., eDNA metabarcoding), transformers are expected to demonstrate superior performance and scalability, representing an important future direction for integrative taxonomy.

In practice, ResMFA50 effectively addresses the conventional reliance on a solitary data source. The utilization of a simplified numerical encoding of molecular sequences, for example mapping nucleotides A/T to values 0.1/0.2, has been demonstrated to effectively capture evolutionary signals in ribosomal subunits. Concurrently, morphological constraints derived from photomicrographs have been shown to facilitate end‐to‐end cross‐modal fusion. Despite the sacrifice of high‐order interactions, such as codon bias, inherent to this low‐dimensional strategy, an accuracy of 85.5% is maintained under conditions of data scarcity. This outcome offers a viable solution for scenarios characterized by limited resources, for instance field monitoring. However, the framework remains confined to supervised closed‐set classification (Scheirer et al. 2014) and is unable to identify novel species—a fundamental limitation of deep neural networks in open‐world recognition.

The confusion matrix analysis (Figure 6; Table S2) not only validates the superior performance of ResMFA50 but also reveals persistent challenges in flagellate taxonomy. Despite the substantial reduction in errors achieved by our multimodal approach, certain species pairs still exhibit residual confusion in ResMFA50. For example, Euglena geniculata versus E. stellata , and Trichonympha sphaerica versus T. collaris . These residual errors likely stem from two interconnected factors. First, the genuine morphological plasticity and cryptic diversity in some flagellate lineages may exceed the representativeness of our current image dataset. Second, the conserved nature of the SSU rRNA gene may not provide sufficient molecular resolution, especially for recently diverged species. These observations highlight critical directions for future research. Integrate higher‐resolution imaging techniques (e.g., 3D microscopy or ultrastructural reconstruction) to capture subtle morphological diagnostic features, and incorporate more variable molecular markers (e.g., ITS regions or mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase genes) to improve phylogenetic resolution. Furthermore, the divergence in error patterns between OnlySeq and OnlyPic (Figure 6c,d) presents a promising opportunity for active learning strategies. In such strategies, model uncertainty could be used to prioritize collection of new data, whether molecular or morphological, that is most likely to resolve ambiguous classifications.

Classification accuracy primarily depends on inter‐taxon feature differences rather than organism size. To test whether our model is sensitive to absolute body size, we applied ResMFA50 to the beetle dataset from Yang et al. (2022), in which insect body sizes differ substantially from those of flagellates. Despite this size disparity, our model achieved strong performance (89.4% accuracy at batch size 8, see Table 6), which is comparable to its performance on flagellates. This result indicates that as long as distinguishable morphological or molecular features exist among taxa, the model can learn and classify effectively; organism size is not a limiting factor. For very small flagellate species (e.g., Mastigella), we did not observe systematic over‐representation in misclassifications, although we acknowledge that the fixed 224 × 224 input resolution may limit fine‐detail extraction for the smallest taxa. Future work could explore higher‐resolution inputs or super‐resolution preprocessing to further improve classification of miniature species.

The following directions are recommended for future research: (1) developing semi‐supervised architectures integrating pretrained models (e.g., BERT) to mine phylogenetic signals from unlabeled molecular data, augmented by geographic metadata and host associations (Devlin et al. 2019); (2) constructing lightweight inference engines via neural architecture search for deployment on portable microscopes, enabling real‐time classification and novel species alerts (Tan and Le 2019); (3) designing dynamic confidence modules to trigger novel species discovery based on feature space deviations; (4) extending the model to taxonomically challenging microeukaryotes beyond flagellates, such as foraminifera (e.g., Ammonia); (5) conducting phylogenetically structured evaluations, such as leave‐one‐species‐out and leave‐one‐genus‐out experiments, to assess whether classification accuracy correlates with evolutionary distance to the nearest training taxon, and comparing against phylogeny‐based nearest‐neighbour baselines. Furthermore, we will systematically explore attention map visualization methods in follow‐up studies to intuitively reveal which morphological features (e.g., flagellar structures) and molecular sequence features the model focuses on, thereby further enhancing the biological interpretability of the model. Future work should also include comparisons with standard phylogenetic assignment methods and k‐mer‐based approaches for the molecular branch alone, to further contextualize the advantage of multimodal fusion against single‐modality baselines that are widely used in molecular taxonomy. (6) Converting phylogenetic trees into learnable formats (e.g., distance matrices, Newick strings, or graph embeddings) for joint training with image and sequence data is an exciting direction that would require architectures such as graph neural networks. We plan to explore this in future work. In fact, concurrent studies on ciliates have already begun exploring these directions, investigating topics from mitochondrial genomics to genomic rearrangements and epigenetic regulation (Lyu et al. 2024; Ye et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). This expansion should target critical application scenarios including environmental DNA (eDNA) monitoring of aquatic ecosystems, rapid clinical diagnosis of pathogenic protists (e.g., Giardia), and characterization of soil microbiome communities.

Beyond flagellate classification, this study provides a methodological foundation for modelling multimodal biological data. By elucidating the interplay between local–global verification and gradient equilibrium, it pioneers new applications of deep learning in complex biological systems. The aforementioned principles find application in three‐dimensional microscopic imaging, temporal gene expression modelling, and other cross‐scale analyses. These analyses contribute to the advancement of computational biology by enabling more integrative and multidimensional approaches.
