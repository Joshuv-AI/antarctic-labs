ABSTRACT
This project was motivated primarily by the opportunity of turning photocatalysis into a cost
efficient hydrogen generation method for domestic energy and chemical production, and by the
need to provide detailed mechanisms of photocatalytic reactions of broad scientific and
technological interest. Specifically, we focused on the development of a new technique for analysis
of (photo)catalytic reaction mechanisms to guide photocatalyst design. Our approach was based on
applying velocity map imaging (VMI) to surface catalyzed reactions in order to identify
photocatalytic reaction products, intermediates, and relate these to photocatalyst composition,
dimensions, size and other characteristics. By coupling VMI with other spectroscopic and
microstructural characterization, we aimed to reveal the features that limit performance of
photocatalysis. As a first step to achieve this goal, we constructed a custom VMI microscope
specifically designed for investigation of surface photocatalytic reactions and validated it’s design
using a photodissociatinon of MnBr(CO)5 adsorbed on a metallic surface. Using both 230 nm and
266 nm pulsed laser excitations, we observed that there are likely three main pathways for
photodissociation of Mn(CO)5Br: from the gas phase, the surface, and ejected Mn complex from
the surface increasing the gas phase concentration.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Water splitting (WS) using direct solar to hydrogen (STH) conversion is an economically promising
route to efficient H2 production according to the United States Department of Energy (please see
https://www.energy.gov/eere/fuelcells/hydrogen-production). Of the possible schemes,
approaches based on nanoparticle photocatalyst suspensions are projected to be some of the most
cost–effective, including when compared to photovoltaic-electrolytic systems. However, these
projections are based on a ~10% STH conversion efficiency, well above the current state-of-the-art
efficiency of ~1%. This gap is due to the poor performance of the nPCs, despite decades of intense
research. The largely ad hoc strategy employed to date has failed due to insufficient understanding of
how the composition, dimensions, morphology and defects of the nPCs relate to their performance.
To improve the process of efficient nPC discovery, we have developed a new capability at the
Laboratory, the Velocity-Selected, Spatially-Mapped, Ion-Imaging microscope, to uncover the
detailed mechanisms of photocatalytic water splitting reactions. We anticipate that this approach
will enable identification of photocatalytic reaction products and their rates with high spatial
resolution for select oxygen and hydrogen evolution photocatalysts and correlate these
measurements with their structural and compositional properties. As a first step, we demonstrated
velocity map imaging on a metal surface for a well-known photodissociation reaction. By
employing 230 nm and 266 nm pulsed laser excitations, we discovered three main pathways for
photodissociation of Mn(CO)5Br: from the gas phase, the surface, and ejected Mn complex from the
surface increasing the gas phase concentration.

1. SECTION 1: VELOCITY MAP IMAGING ANALYSIS OF
 MANGANESE PENTACARBONYL BROMIDE
 PHOTODISSOCIATION IN THE GAS PHASE AND ON A
 SURFACE
1.1. Summary
 We employ velocity map imaging (VMI) to investigate the photodissociation of manganese
pentacarbonyl bromide (Mn(CO)₅Br), both in the gas phase and on a surface. Pulsed 266-nm and/or
230-nm laser light were used to initiate the photodissociation cleaving the Mn-CO bonds. A single
230-nm or 266-nm photon has enough energy to lead to the dissociation of all five CO molecules.
The CO can be ionized above the surface using a (2+1) REMPI scheme via the B 1Σ+X 1Σ+
transition with 230-nm photons. To deconvolute the contributions from the gas-phase and surface
photodissociation, one- and two-color experiments were compared. The one-color experiment
focused 230-nm light above the sample surface which dissociated gas-phase Mn(CO)5Br and ionized
CO via REMPI. The two-color experiments used grazing angle illumination with 266-nm light to
induce the dissociation of Mn(CO)5Br on sample surface and probe 230-nm light above the sample
surface at an scanned time delay. The contribution from the gas-phase dissociation to the surface’s
dissociation signal can be observed in the VMI images, and Q-branch REMPI spectra of CO. In the
two-color time-resolved detection of CO above the surface after photodissociation, we observe a
complex arrival time spectrum. We hypothesize that there are three main pathways for formation of
CO ion from photodissociation of Mn(CO)5Br: CO coming from the gas-phase photodissociation of
Mn(CO)5Br, hyperthermal CO ejected directly into the gas phase from the surface as a result of 266-
nm photoexcitation, and photodissociation of intact Mn(CO)5Br ejected from the surface. Q-branch
REMPI spectra of CO and VMI images were collected at various time delays and are in agreement
with our hypothesis when comparing data taken at early and late time delays. Two distinct rotational
distributions of CO appeared at 21 μs in the Q-branch REMPI spectra indicating a population with
low J state population and one with high J state population. Both the rotationally resolved spectra and
VMI images collected at late time delays, beyond 231 μs, are very similar in shape to the gas-phase
image and rotational spectrum, suggesting that at long time delays there is an increase in gas-phase
Mn(CO)5Br photodissociation from Mn(CO)5Br ejected from the surface.

1.2. Introduction
 Transition metal carbonyls are of considerable interest due to their pivotal roles in
photocatalysis1 and electrocatalysis2 exhibiting exceptional catalytic properties. The vibrational modes
of transitional metal carbonyl bonds are infrared active, facilitating operando monitoring.3–6
Furthermore, these transition metal carbonyls have been extensively studied for their
photodissociation mechanisms.6–12 Carbon monoxide releasing molecules (CORMs) have garnered
significant attention for their applications in the biomedical field, particularly for local CO delivery
triggered with light.13–15 Synthetic modifications of these inorganic CORMs often involve synthetic
modification of the organic ligands, or changing the transition metal centers to fine-tune the
absorbance characteristics.3,15,16 Iron carbonyls, in particular, have been the focus of extensive research
due to their controlled CO release and catalytic properties.6,17 Notable studies have demonstrated the
controlled photoelimination of carbonyl ligands from Fe(CO)5 in the gas phase, employing time-of-
flight mass spectroscopy for analysis9,10, infrared spectroscopy6, or ultraviolet transient absorption
spectroscopy11.

 10

 While metal carbonyl complexes have a substantial body of literature regarding their use and
synthetic modifications,1–3,5,8,13–16 further research is essential to enhance our understanding of the
mechanisms and the factors influencing the kinetics of the transition metal-carbonyl bond
photodissociation.

 Building on this foundation, Ramasesha et. al. provides a comprehensive overview of ultrafast
studies of dimer transition metal carbonyl complexes, highlighting advancements in the gas-phase
photodissociation of Mn2(CO)10, a manganese carbonyl dimer complex.12 Ultrafast studies focusing
on the photolysis of M2(CO)10 complexes (where M = Mn and Re) reveal that the reactions occur
within the ultrafast regime for both M-M and M-CO dissociation.7 Kim et. al. investigated Mn2(CO)10
by pumping with 310-nm laser pulses and probing via multiphoton ionization using a 620-nm laser
pulse. They employed mass detection techniques to monitor the photofragments of the parent
complex, thereby elucidating the photolysis dynamics leading to the formation of CO, Mn2(CO)9, and
Mn(CO)5.18 Their findings indicated that the neutral Mn(CO)5 is stable on time scales exceeding 100
picoseconds, while the parent Mn2(CO)10+ decays in less than 85 femtoseconds. Additionally, they
reported that the cleavage of the Mn-CO bond occurs more rapidly (20 femtoseconds) than the
cleavage of the Mn-Mn bond (40 femtoseconds). Complete dissociation of the carbonyls has been
documented in a study of gas-phase photolysis of Mn2(CO)10, utilizing a 337-nm excitation laser pulse
and a 442-nm ionization laser pulse.19 Currently, the manganese carbonyl photodissociation literature
is focused on the ultrafast regime where controlled one to two carbonyl dissociation is observed, but
there is lack of knowledge on complete dissociation at longer time scales.
 Velocity map imaging (VMI) has previously been used to study various transition metal
carbonyls. Waller et. al. investigated photofragment dynamics of Fe(CO)5 at different photolysis
wavelengths and described possible photodissociation intermediate steps.17 Zhang et. al. used VMI to
study the conformations and structural changes in the trinuclear carbonyl complexes, V2Ni(CO)n with
increasing metal carbonyls bonds.20 Pintea et. al. used VMI to provide key information on the key
photodissociation steps of Fe(CO)5,, such as their kinetic energies and angular distributions of the
anions of Fe(CO)5 in the gas phase.21 Typically, these VMI experiments are done to study gas-phase
dynamics as opposed to on a surface. To our knowledge, there are no known reports of using VMI to
study a photodissociation reaction on a surface. There are known reports of studying scattering,
adsorption, and desorption of small molecules like HCl, NO, CO and H2, with VMI and time-of-flight
mass spectrometry surface experiments.22–26 Koehler et. al. used VMI to study a photochemical
reaction on a KBr single crystal surface and from their velocity distributions, they proposed two
hyperthermal mechanisms that contributed to the Br photodesorption dynamics at 193 nm.27
 In this study, we used VMI to study the photodissociation of Mn(CO)5Br in gas phase and
physiosorbed on a surface. To understand the contributions from the surface photolysis, one and two-
color experiments with 230-nm and 266-nm laser pulses were conducted to deconvolute the
contributions from the gas-phase Mn(CO)5Br. In the two color experiment, we propose that the
formation of CO from the photodissociation of Mn(CO)5Br occurs through three primary pathways:
first, CO generated from the direct photodissociation of Mn(CO)5Br that is thermalized on the surface
and then desorbs; second, hyperthermal CO that is ejected directly into the gas phase as a result of a
photolysis with 266-nm light without thermalization on the surface; and third, the photodissociation
of intact Mn(CO)5Br that is expelled from the surface.

1.3. Experimental Details
 The gas-phase velocity map ion imaging experiments were conducted on the residual vapor
pressure of Mn(CO)₅Br, which was continuously desorbing from the surface of the repeller plate. The

 11

gas-phase Mn(CO)5Br was photodissociated with 230-nm light and then the same 230-nm light
performed (2 +1) resonantly enhanced multiphoton ionization (REMPI) on the CO fragment via the
B 1Σ+X 1Σ+ transition near 230 nm.23,28,29 The CO product was ionized when it reached the 230-nm
laser beam centered between the repeller plate and the extractor (1.1 cm away from the surface of the
repeller plate). The laser was focused into the center of a traditional ion imaging apparatus between a
repeller plate and extractor plate; the ions were propelled through an Einzel lens, focused through a
field-free flight region onto the imaging detector to accomplish velocity mapping of the CO+ and Mn+
photoproducts. The ion lensing electrodes and repeller plate voltages were adjusted to conform the
ions into circular shapes and these parameters was assumed to be the best velocity mapped imaging
conditions, see supporting information for more details. The front plate of the microchannel plate
detector was gated for a pulse width of 300 ns to ensure only detection of CO ions. The time-of-flight
mass spectrometry apparatus is described in further detail in the supporting information. In the two-
color experiment, the repeller plate was then illuminated with the pulsed 266-nm laser light to initiate
photochemistry of the Mn(CO)5Br on the surface.
 The vacuum chamber was maintained at a constant vacuum pressure of 1.1 x 10-7 Torr via a
turbomolecular pump. Three microchannel plates were used in combination with a high voltage pulser
box to gate the first plate with a pulse width of 300 ns. An Andor Sona sCMOS camera was used to
obtain images. The frequency-tripled output of one 10 Hz Continuum Surelight Nd:YAG was used
to illuminate the Mn(CO)5Br on the repeller plate with 266-nm light. The 266-nm light had a low
energy range of 50 to 100 μJ/pulse as to not ablate the repeller surface of the Mn(CO)5Br. Another
10 Hz Continuum Surelight Nd:YAG was used to pump a Lambda Physik Scanmate dye laser with
355-nm light. LDS 698, or 1-pyridine, in methanol was used in the Lambda Physik Scanmate to
produce 230-nm light with pulse energies ranging from 250 to 300 μJ/pulse and a line width of 0.03
cm-1. The time delay between the 266-nm and the 230-nm light was controlled throughout the
experiment with a Stanford research DG645 timing box.
 The experimental data was acquired using a custom built VMI apparatus and the resulting
images were processed using python and MATLAB scripts. The spectra data were collected with a
SRS boxcar averager model SR250 and the laser intensity was measured with a photodiode (ThorLabs
model DET10A). The spectra were collected three times, averaged, and then normalized by dividing
by the squared intensity of the photodiode.

1.4. Discussion

Figure 1. Schemes utilizing a) gas-phase photodissociation experiment utilizing a single pulse of 230-
nm laser light and b) surface photodissociation experiment utilizing two laser beams a pulsed 266-nm
for photolysis at the surface and a 230-nm beam to ionize the CO product. The product ions were
velocity map imaged on the detector.

A custom-built time-of-flight mass spectrometer apparatus was configured as shown in Figure S2. A
CO releasing transition metal complex, Mn(CO)5Br, was deposited by drop-casting onto the repeller
plate, as described in the Materials and Methods section. The manganese complex has a non-negligible
vapor pressure that sublimed from the surface, which was observed in the one-color experiments
described below. In Figure 1, the scheme describes the pathways for CO to be produced from the
photodissociated Mn(CO)5Br. In the one-color experiment, the gas-phase Mn(CO)5Br
photodissociation produces CO that is then resonantly ionized by the 230-nm laser. From a
thermodynamical perspective, the photodissocation of Mn(CO)5Br can produce up to five CO from
a single 230-nm or 266-nm photon.8,30 In the two-color experiment, the 266-nm laser light pulse is
photodissociating the Mn(CO)5Br on the repeller plate surface and then the 230-nm laser light
resonantly ionizes CO emitted into the gas phase. Alternatively, introduction of 266-nm light may
release entire Mn(CO)5Br molecules into the gas phase, which are then photodissociated by 230-nm
light as observed in the one-color experiment.
 After the photolysis of Mn(CO)5Br with either 230-nm or 266-nm laser pulsed light, only a
small sample of the CO photofragments is ionized in the focused 230-nm beam. This is because the
CO products are in many different rotational states and the laser is resonant with only a few at any
one frequency. CO+ and Mn+ were detected as products from the gas-phase photodissociation in the
one-color experiment, see Figures 2A and S7. Subsequently, the CO molecules were velocity mapped
onto the detector as shown in Figure 2A. CO ions arrived at texp = 6.986 μs, and Mn ions arrived at
texp = 9.714 μs. There was an average of 15 ± 3 CO ions and 23 ± 6 Mn ions per 230-nm laser pulse.

 13

Figure 2. a) A velocity mapped image of the CO coming from gas-phase Mn(CO)5Br using only 230-
nm light. b) An inverse Abel transformation of the VMI background CO. c) CO velocity distribution

 A velocity distribution obtained by an inverse Abel transformation of this background CO
image peaks around 350 m/s. This speed suggests an arrival time of these molecules from the surface
to the position of the 230-nm laser focal volume (1.1 cm) of approximately 30 μs, which is consistent
with the peak of the arrival time distribution we observe in the blue trace in Figure 3. The fastest CO
is travelling at 800 m/s towards the detector which corresponds to an arrival time of approximately
14 μs. A Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution predicts a peak velocity of a thermal CO at 298 K
is approximately 440 m/s, with a calculated arrive time of 25 μs (simulated arrival time denoted by the
red curve in Figure 3). Note that in the gas phase, we only observe the CO that is ionized in the small,
focused ionization region during the 10 ns duration of the 230-nm laser pulse. Thus, only a small
fraction of total Mn(CO)5Br is both photodissociated into CO and detected. Details for the CO
velocity calculations are in the supporting information.

Figure 3. A time-of-flight plot showing the intensity of signal as a function of time delay of the CO
traveling from the surface of the repeller plate to the 230-nm ionization light pathway (black dashed
line). The arrival time was additionally calculated for a Maxwell-Boltzmann thermal population at 298
K (red line). The blue line is the velocity distribution of the CO from gas-phase dissociation of
Mn(CO)5Br with only the 230-nm laser.

 14

 The black dashed line in Figure 3 shows experimental time-of-flight data collected from
conducting the two-color experiment by varying the time delay between the 266-nm and 230-nm laser
pulses. From the one-color experiment, the arrival times calculated from the inverse Abel
transformation are solely from the 230-nm photodissociation are included in blue, which overlaps
closely to the time-of-flight data. This CO population from the gas-phase photodissociation appears
to be colder (lower peak in the velocity distribution leading to slightly longer arrival times) than the
thermal population distribution calculated from the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution at 298 K.
Additionally, the time-of-flight data is exhibiting non-zero signal at long time delays, which suggests
that must be an additional pathway for CO formation besides the direct photodissociation. This
additional CO formation pathway at long time delays must be producing thermalized CO from the
localized laser heated surface or subliming additional intact Mn(CO)5Br from the surface. In both of
these situations the CO products would reach the 230-nm ionization laser volume by about 200
microseconds.
 From the two-color experiment, the time-of-flight data shown in Figure 3 is consistent with
two distinct populations of CO: first a velocity distribution that arrives at earlier time delays with a
peak at 30 μs and the second, slower population of CO that arrives with time delays of up to 800 μs.
The faster population of CO are products from direct photodissociation of Mn(CO)5Br on the surface
of the repeller plate using the 266-nm laser. We hypothesize that this second population of CO is
derived from a desorption process of the Mn(CO)5Br complex that slowly finds the 230-nm laser
volume where it is photodissociated and the CO product ionized. The 266-nm laser could be heating
the local surface of the repeller plate increasing the desorption of the Mn(CO)5Br, which is a heavy
complex and will take a longer time to diffuse into the ionization laser region. Optical images show
burned and melted regions of Mn(CO)5Br on the repeller plate, see Figure S5. Alternatively, surface-
photodissociated CO molecules could be colliding with other Mn(CO)5Br and ejecting the Mn
complex to the gas phase. Either mechanism that ejects parent molecule into the gas phase is
consistent with the data. Below we will see that this later arriving CO at 231μs has the same rotational
spectra and velocity mapped ion image as the CO produced from the gas-phase Mn(CO)5Br
photolysis.

 15

Figure 4. Q-branch REMPI spectra of CO obtained at different laser delay time intervals. Each
spectra has been background subtracted by the CO produced by thermal vapor pressure of
Mn(CO)5Br at 230 nm (bottom Burgundy spectrum). The background subtracted spectra for various
time delays 0 μs (light blue), 13 μs (green), 21 μs (magenta), 41 μs (yellow), 61 μs (orange), and 231 μs
(dark blue) are presented.

 The Q branch for the B 1Σ+X 1Σ+ transition of CO has been reported at 86915 cm-1, which
is equivalent to 690.32-nm fundamental light for the (2 +1) REMPI process.31 From Figure 4, the Q-
branch spectra of the gas-phase photodissociated CO in burgundy is shown with a Gaussian fit
(dashed line). Each spectrum is normalized to the squared (since it is a two-photon ionization process)
intensity of a photodiode measuring the 230-nm energy per pulse. The other spectra are background-
subtracted from the Q-branch spectra of the gas-phase photodissociated CO (i.e. “without 266nm”).
One or two Gaussian fits are overlaid on each spectrum to help identify these separate population of
CO with different J-states, which are shown as black dashed lines.
 At early times, 0 μs and 13 μs, the REMPI spectra are indicating that there is no change from
background in the amount of CO or the rotational populations of detected CO, which supports the
hypothesis that the fastest CO have not reached the ionization region at early times. At 21 μs, there
are two distinct rotational distributions: one colder peaking around 690.29 nm and one hotter at 690.25
nm. The colder left shoulder and hotter right shoulder both appear in time delays 21 μs to 61 μs. The
appearance of a low-J-state population indicates a thermalized CO coming from the surface
dissociation. The existence of the higher-J-state population suggests that there is a CO population that
is rotationally hotter than obtained from the gas-phase distribution. We interpret this rotationally hot,
early CO as coming from the surface photodissociation that leads to an immediate expulsion of CO.
The first CO expelled from a Mn(CO)5Br molecule on the surface will have the most energy available
for the dissociation leading to the rotationally hotter CO than subsequent CO molecules that are likely
trapped on the surface and come off thermalized (low-J peak).

 16

 At long time delays like 431 μs, the subtracted spectra appear as one broad J state population
very similar to the spectra of the gas-phase photodissociation and early time delays. This phenomena
can be explained by the parent Mn(CO)5Br being expelled from the surface, but due to its
approximately ten-fold heavier mass, it arrives at a much later time to the focused 230-nm laser region,
where it is photolyzed and ionized.

 The optical images shown in Figures S4 and S5, indicate areas of decomposition consistent
with locations where the 266-nm laser light was incident. As mentioned in the Materials and Methods

1.5. Conclusion
 In this study, we successfully demonstrated VMI of gas-phase and surface photodissociation
of Mn(CO)5Br using 230-nm and 266-nm excitation. The 230-nm laser pulses were employed to ionize
the CO molecules in a focused region above the surface through a (2+1) REMPI scheme via the B
 Σ X 1Σ+ transition. In our one-color experiment, we observed CO+ and Mn+ ions as the principal
1 +

products from the photolysis, with arrival times of 6.986 μs and 9.714 μs, respectively. An inverse
Abel transformation on the VMI image of gas-phase photodissociated CO gave a velocity distribution
peaking around at 350m/s.

 18

 In the two-color experiment, we compared the experimentally measured time-of-flight data
and the inverse Abel transformation and detected CO even at long time delays between pump-probe
laser pulses. We hypothesize that Mn(CO)5Br is ejected off the surface by either the 266-nm light, or
by recoil from other photofragments, thereby increasing the concentration of Mn(CO)5Br in the gas
phase. This hypothesis is consistent with the Q-branch REMPI spectra and VMI images collected at
various time delays, which show similar features at early and late time delays. The Q-branch REMPI
spectra have one broad J-state population at long time delays, which is similar to the spectra of the
gas-phase photodissociation and early time delays, further suggesting that the increased is from gas-
phase photodissociation of Mn(CO)5Br. In the Q-branch spectra, two peaks grow in at 21 μs,
indicating two distinct rotational distributions with different J states: one colder at 690.29 nm and one
hotter at 690.25 nm. The CO population at 690.25 nm is rotationally hotter than the CO population
measured from the gas-phase photodissociation of Mn(CO)5Br, suggesting that the surface photolysis
contributes to the ejection of CO that are a part of the high rotational wing. This is further illustrated
by the early and late time delay VMI images of the CO+, which display similar circular shapes, in
contrast to the unique non-circular shapes observed at intermediate times. Overlaying the integrated
Q-branch spectra with the integrated velocity mapped images at various time delays reveals a similar
trend to that observed with the time-of-flight data. Through these collective experiments, we have
successfully deconvoluted the contributions of the gas phase and surface photolysis, providing
valuable insights for future VMI studies of surface photodissociation reactions.

2. SECTION 2: VELOCITY MAP IMAGE MICROSCOPY
 FOR EFFICIENT PHOTOCATALYSIS
2.1. Introduction
 Water splitting (WS) using direct solar to hydrogen (STH) conversion is economically the most
promising route to efficient H2 production according to a DOE commissioned study carried out in
2009.31 Of the possible schemes, approaches based on nanoparticle photocatalyst (nPC) suspensions
are projected to be the most cost–effective, including when compared to photovoltaic-electrolytic
systems.32,33 However, these projections are based on a ~10% STH conversion efficiency, well above
the current state-of-the-art efficiency of ~1%.34 This gap is due to the poor performance of the nPCs,
despite decades of intense research. The largely ad hoc strategy employed to date has failed due to
insufficient understanding of how the composition, dimensions, morphology and defects of the nPCs
relate to their performance. We propose a new route to nPC discovery, based on several recent
developments in our laboratory. Specifically, we propose to extend a new capability at the Laboratory,
the Velocity-Selected, Spatially-Mapped, Ion-Imaging (VSSMII) microscope, to uncover the detailed
mechanisms of photocatalytic water splitting reactions. With this approach, we propose to identify
photocatalytic reaction products and their rates with high spatial resolution for select oxygen evolution
reaction (OER) and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) nanoparticle photocatalysts and correlate
these measurements with structural, compositional and liquid cell characteristics. We hypothesize that
with a combined approach we will identify the rate limiting steps and propose a scientifically validated
strategy for improving OER and HER efficiencies.
 The basic principle of VSSMII microscopy is shown in Figure 7(a). Briefly a quantum-state-
resolved laser is utilized for ionization of a product of a catalytic reaction, such as H2 + D2 occurring
over a platinum strip. The technique is able to accurately determine where the ions are formed, and
to correlate the location of the products in the gas phase directly above the surface with features on
the surface. For OER and HER characterization, water vapor at pressures of ~10 Torr will be
introduced into the system and an optical pump laser with excite photocarriers in the nanoparticle
photocatalysts. A time delayed voltage pulse will be used to force the OER or HER products toward
the detector. Laser ionization will be used to probe for neutral reaction intermediates. The VSSMII
microscopy measurements will be combined with photoelectrochemical, microstructural and charge
transport characterization on the same nanoparticle photocatalysts with the aim of unraveling the
complex WS mechanisms and revealing paths toward achieving substantially enhanced efficiency for
solar hydrogen production.
 Photocatalytic water splitting comprises three basic steps: 1) light absorption, 2) transport of
photocarriers to the surface of the nanoparticle, and 3) H2 and/or O2 evolution, often aided with a
co-catalyst. Whether using a single type of nPC or separated H2 and O2 reactions (z-scheme),
optimizing all three steps within a single material system is a considerable challenge. Traditional
semiconductors are unstable in the aqueous media. Various transition metal oxides are low cost, stable,
and have appropriately positioned conduction band minima and valence band maxima to drive H2 and
O2 evolution, respectively. However, many of these compounds absorb only a small fraction of the
solar radiation due to their large bandgaps (>3eV).35 To increase visible absorption, dopants like N,
C, S and various transition metals are introduced to alter the electronic structure to form an impurity
band.36 This approach has yet to work mainly due to formation of deep traps that severely reduce
photocarrier lifetime7. One promising exception is BiVO4 (BVO). With a bandgap (Eg) of ~2 eV,
BVO absorbs substantially in the visible. However, because BVO is naturally n-type and its band
structure is only well suited for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), it requires a z-scheme type
approach for solar WS. Thus, further improving BVO OER rates and finding an effective HER
photocatalyst, could be a promising solution for low cost solar WS.
 Herein we propose to extend a recently developed technique at SNL, the Velocity-Selected,
Spatially-Mapped, Ion-Imaging (VSSMII) microscopy, to uncovering the detailed mechanisms of PC
WS reactions. Specifically, we propose to identify photocatalytic reaction products and their rates

 20

with high spatial resolution for select OER and HER nanoparticle photocatalysts and correlate these
measurements with structural, compositional and liquid cell characteristics. We hypothesize that with
a combined approach we will identify the rate limiting steps and propose a scientifically validated
strategy for improving OER and HER efficiencies.
 The basic principle of VSSMII microscopy is shown in Figure 7(a). In this approach, developed at
SNL by D. Chandler and C. Kliewer, a quantum-state-resolved laser is utilized for ionization of a
product of a catalytic reaction, such as H2 + D2 occurring over a platinum strip at 500oK.23 The goal
is to accurately determine where the ions are formed, in order to correlate the location of the products
in the gas phase directly above the surface with features on the surface. In order to measure the exact
location of the products, velocity mapped ion imaging is used to form a velocity image of the ions.
This image is located at a plate with a small hole that selects out a single velocity group to pass onto
an imaging detector 1 meters away. For a pinhole placed such that only ions moving perfectly
perpendicular to the surface are allowed to pass and to expand onto the detector, the location of ions
at the detector accurately reflects the location that the desired product (HD in this example) exits the
surface. It turns out that one can pick any subset of velocities and get an equivalent image simply
translated on the detector (assuming all locations have the same angular distribution of products).

Figure 7. (a) VSSMII microscopy schematic. A laser beam skims the surface of a patterned catalytic
electrode that repels the cations that are formed by the focused laser beam. Electrostatic lenses focus
the ions onto a pinhole in a metal plate thereby velocity selecting the ions that will pass through to the
imaging detector. A line image of the laser focus is formed and an intensity profile is extracted from
the line and analyzed. (b) VSSMII for PC product and rate imaging. An optical pump pulse followed
by time delayed voltage pulse to desorb charge PC products (laser ionization may not be needed).

 A schematic of our proposed approach to extending VSSMII microscopy to PC WS is shown
in Fig. 7b. We will initially focus on the oxygen evolution reaction OER, 2H2O + 4h+  O2 + 4H+.
We anticipate that we will be able to detect the charged particles produced, protons, and/or the
hydronium ions (formed when the protons react with the adsorbed H2O), just with the application of
the repeller plate voltage, without laser ionization. By controlling the time delay between the optical
pump pulse and when the voltage is applied to the repeller plate, we will determine the time constants
for the OER as a function of the pump power, crystal orientation, and co-catalyst composition and
coverage. Optical ionization of O2 near 370 nm has been extensively studied and will be used for O2
molecule detection.38 The BVO crystals will be dispersed on commercially purchased,
alphanumerically labeled Si substrates with 50 nm thick Si3N4 wells and Au metal lines. This will enable

us to identify specific nanoparticle PCs and correlate the data with other complimentary measurements
(see Fig. 8), including photoelectrochemical (PEC), spectroscopic (Raman/photoluminescence),
transmission electron microscopy with electron energy loss spectroscopy (TEM/EELS), and charge
transport. This type of correlative microscopy/spectroscopy is needed to understand how the basic
steps in the PC WS are linked and identify which step is rate limiting. The TEM/EELS
characterization will be done in collaboration with Josh Sugar and will be supported by the Ensembles
of Photocatalytic Nanoreactors Energy Frontiers Research Center (EPN EFRC). We will particularly
focus on better understanding the role of cocatalyst in assisting the OER efficiency. To this end, we
will select BVO PC nanoparticles with the highest crystal quality (judged using x-ray diffraction and
scanning electron microscopy) and have highest intensity of near band-edge luminescence, indicative
of lower charge recombination trap density. These BVO nanoparticle PCs will be decorated with either
Ir or FeOOH or CoOOH cocatalysts using in situ facet-selective photodeposition and characterized
using VSSMII microscopy, followed by TEM/EELS. By relating specific aspects of the BVO nPCs
such as their dimension, the cocatalyst structure and location, and the electrical properties of its
contact with the BVO nanoparticle, with the quantitatively measured OER rates, we will formulate a
model for the reaction and use this understanding to design improved nPCs.

2.2. Experimental Methodology
 The surface velocity map ion imaging experiments were conducted with in a custom-built
time-of-flight mass spectrometer setup with a low-pressure water vapor leaking into the sample
chamber. When the sample chamber is closed off, the pressure is consistently at 1.1 x 10-7 Torr. Pure
hydrogen from a gas cylinder source was connected to a regulator and leaked into the chamber at a

 22

very low flowrate to calibrate the operating conditions for the experiment. The vacuum chamber
would be evacuated for several minutes, and the lines would be evacuated separately with a vacuum
pump to prevent cross-contamination in the gas lines. When water vapor is leaked in from a sealed
water bubbler (that is evacuated of air), the chamber pressure is able to be controlled via a micrometer
leak valve, and the chamber pressure is brought up 1 x 10-5 Torr. This chamber pressure is measured
via a digital high vacuum gauge. The vacuum chamber was maintained at a constant measured vacuum
pressure via a turbomolecular pump. Three microchannel plates were used in combination with a high
voltage pulser box to gate the first plate with a pulse width of 300 ns. An Andor Sona sCMOS camera
was used to obtain raw images. A custom LabVIEW software program was used to alternate
measurements with a foreground and background measurement switching between two preset timings
for the laser pulses. The experimental data was acquired using a custom built VMI apparatus and the
resulting images were processed using python and MATLAB scripts. The spectra data were collected
with a SRS boxcar averager model SR250 and the laser intensity was measured with a photodiode
(ThorLabs model DET10A). The spectra were collected three times, averaged, and then normalized
by dividing by the squared intensity of the photodiode.
 The photocatalyst sample was mounted with double sided copper tape to the stainless steel
repeller plate in the vacuum chamber. The frequency-tripled output of a 100 Hz Lumibird Nd:YAG
was used to illuminate the photocatalyst sample on the repeller plate with 355-nm light. The 355-nm
light had a low energy range of 5 to 100 μJ/pulse as to not damage or degrade the sample. The 355-
nm pump light was positioned to be grazing over the sample surface. Another 100 Hz Lumibird
Nd:YAG was used to pump a Sirah Precision Scan dye laser with 532-nm light. A mixture of
Rhodamine 610 (0.17g/L) and Rhodamine 640 (0.04 g/L) in methanol were used in the Sirah Precision
Scan dye laser to produce 202-nm light with pulse energies ranging from 70 to 100 μJ/pulse and a line
width of 0.03 cm-1. The time delay between the 355-nm and the 202-nm light was controlled
throughout the experiment with a Stanford research DG645 timing box.
 The 355-nm light is used to illuminate the photocatalyst surface and generate electron-hole
pairs, which further reacts into forming the hydrogen product. The hydrogen from the gas cylinder
sources or product of the reaction would be ionized with a 202-nm laser pulse using a (2+1) REMPI
scheme via the E, F 1Σ+X 1Σ+ transition near 202-nm. The hydrogen is ionized when it reaches the
202-nm laser beam centered and focused between the repeller plate and the extractor (1.1 cm from
the photocatalyst surface on the repeller plate). The laser was focused into the center of a traditional
ion imaging apparatus between a repeller plate and extractor plate; the ions were propelled through
an Einzel lens, focused through a field-free flight region onto the imaging detector to accomplish
velocity mapping of the H2+ photoproduct. The ion lensing electrodes and repeller plate voltages
were adjusted to conform the ions into circular shapes and these parameters was assumed to be the
best velocity mapped imaging conditions. The front plate of the microchannel plate detector was gated
for a pulse width of 300 ns to ensure only detection of H2 ions.

2.3. Discussion
 Pure hydrogen was used to calibrate the velocity map imaging conditions and to collect a
REPMI spectra. As shown in the appendix B, the REMPI spectra collected has 4 sharp peaks
corresponding to the Q0, Q1, Q2, and Q3 peaks, which is agreeance with literature reports. This
REMPI spectra is important for confirming that hydrogen in the vacuum chamber is being ionized,
and to position the dye laser wavelength on a Q branch to get selective ionization.
 The first sample to be tested was a 1 cm x 1 cm x 0.05cm bulk crystalline Nb doped SrTiO3
(STO). The vacuum chamber was saturated with water vapor leaked in from the head space of a
water source. The Sirah dye laser was set to the Q1 peak wavelength to ionize the hydrogen. With a 5
μs time delay between the 355-nm and 202-nm laser pulses, H2 was clearly detected and velocity
mapped onto the detector. The theoretical thermal average velocity of hydrogen at room
temperature is approximately 1800 m/s. The measured velocity of the hydrogen is 1850 m/s. This
measured velocity is calculated using the ratio of diameter of the camera in pixels and real diameter,
cm, and dividing by the arrival time.
 An averaged image was generated by combining 60 individual images, each acquired with an
accumulation time of 30 seconds, as illustrated in Figure 10. The signal intensity is quite low for
these averaged images once the foreground is subtracted from the background. Figure 11 was
generated by binning across the x-axis and y-axis of velocity mapped image of H2 ions in Figure 10.

 Other time delays were tested and measured as well, but due to the low signal to noise ratio,

2.4. Conclusion
 In conclusion, preliminary results indicate that hydrogen was velocity map imaged using
water vapor and a photocatalyst sample, Nb doped STO. With the current results, no definitive
conclusions can be made and it is imperative to continue future measurements to uncover the water
splitting mechanism on these photocatalysts.
 Future experimentation will include controlling the sample temperature to study the
temperature effects on photocatalytic rates as well as different photocatalysts, dopants, and co-
catalysts. Additionally, future VMI systems will be designed to selectively measure different ions like
H+, OH-, or O2- which could be important intermediates that provide crucial information for the
water splitting mechanism. With a pinhole design as presented previously, a spatial resolution can be
achieved to determine differences in photocatalyst facets, structures, and compositions that can be
correlated back to photocatalytic activity rates.

A.2. Experimental Details
Image processing, velocity calculations, and inverse Abel transformation
CO images were taken with the Andor Sona sCMOS camera and converted to ascii files via the
Andor Solis software. We used imageJ software to measure the known diameter of the
fluorescence screen, and the diameter of the focused CO+ ions. Using the ratio of the known
diameter, and measured pixel diameter of the fluorescence screen, we calculated the diameter of
the CO+ ions. To get the velocity, we divided the pixel distances by the CO arrival time. A custom
python code using PyAbel was used to create the inverse Abel transformed image and velocity
distribution.

Q branch REMPI spectra details
The Q branch spectra was collected over a range of 690 nm to 690.3 nm. The spectra at each time
delay condition were collected in triplicate and then averaged. A Thorlab photodiode was used to
measure the intensity of the 230-nm light over the spectra collection period. The averaged spectra
intensity was then divided by the intensity of the photodiode squared since it is a two-photon
process. At each time delay, the averaged gas phase CO spectra is then averaged from each
processed spectra to isolate the contributions from the surface CO.

Integrating VMI images
The VMI images were taken with 45 second accumulation time at each time delay. The VMI
images were read into Matlab as a heatmap. In Matlab, the selected area was summed and plotted
against the respective time delay. At negative times delays, the 230-nm ionization laser pulse is
arriving before the 266-nm laser pulse, which means that the only CO in the ionization region are
from the gas phase dissociation. Thus, we subtracted all the integrated image values by the
integrated value at the time delay of -69 μs.
