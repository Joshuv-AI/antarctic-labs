A Variational Shape Optimisation Approach to Multi-region Relaxed Magnetohydrodynamic Equilibria
K. de Lacy, L. Noakes, D. Pfefferlé

Abstract
Let Λ ⊂ ℝ 3 \Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{3} be a region admitting a partition into n n compact, connected subregions Λ 1 , … , Λ n \Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{n} , each with smooth boundary. Consider a vector field B B on Λ \Lambda where B | Λ i B|_{\Lambda_{i}} is smooth, divergence free, and tangent to ∂ Λ i \partial\Lambda_{i} for all i i . We show that the multi-region relaxed magnetohydrodynamics (MRxMHD) equilibrium equations are necessary and sufficient conditions for B B and a metric to yield a stationary point of the magnetic energy under appropriate constraints. We constrain the pressure, relative helicity, and magnetic flux of B B through all smooth surfaces in Λ i \Lambda_{i} whose boundary lies on ∂ Λ i \partial\Lambda_{i} . We identify a previously overlooked gauge condition. A definition for relative helicity is introduced, its gauge invariance is proved, and the existence of a gauge where relative helicity reduces to conventional helicity is demonstrated. In the case of a single region an additional condition is introduced that is sufficient to ensure a critical point of the magnetic energy is also a minimiser.

Introduction

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) equilibrium equations describe the steady-state behaviour of a globally neutral plasma under the influence of a magnetic field. Formally, MHD is derived from the Vlasov–Maxwell equations by assuming quasi-neutrality and taking low-order velocity moments to produce a single-fluid model coupled to the magnetic field. This model neglects effects like collisionless damping, viscosity, and high-frequency waves
[
16
]
. The MHD equilibrium equations characterise steady-state MHD solutions and take the form

\displaystyle(\nabla\times B)\times B=\nabla p\,,\quad\nabla\cdot B=0\,,\quad N\cdot B\big|_{\partial\Lambda}=0\,,

where the
magnetic field

B
is a smooth vector field on a compact, oriented domain
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with smooth boundary
\partial\Lambda
. The
pressure

p:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function that is constant on each connected component of the boundary
\partial\Lambda
.

Let
f:\Lambda\to f(\Lambda)
be a diffeomorphism isotopic to the identity with
f|_{\partial\Lambda}=\mathrm{id}
. Rather than work directly on a moving domain
f(\Lambda)
with a Euclidean volume form
\varpi
, we pull back to a fixed reference domain
\Lambda
. We equip
\Lambda
with a volume form
f^{*}\varpi
, and interpret differential operators and inner products, such as those in the MHD equation, with respect to the pulled-back Euclidean metric. The domain
\Lambda
is fixed while
f
and
B
are allowed to vary. The outward unit normal
N
on
\partial\Lambda
is defined as the pullback of the Euclidean outward normal on
\partial f(\Lambda)
. This keeps the reference domain fixed while allowing the geometry and the location of interior conditions to vary through
f
.

The MHD equilibrium equations constitute a nonlinear boundary-value problem. MHD equilibria are used in the early stages of designing and optimising magnetic field configurations for plasma confinement devices
[
9
,
23
]
. By exploring domain geometries one can identify promising reactor shapes and parameters before undertaking more realistic but computationally expensive simulations. Both iterative solvers and variational methods are in common use for computing or approximating MHD equilibria (assuming such solutions exist) with variational formulations typically exhibiting better convergence properties
[
36
,
42
,
28
,
24
,
14
,
27
,
10
,
22
]
. Restricting to axisymmetric fields invariant under continuous rotations about the toroidal axis yields the 2D elliptic Grad–Shafranov equation, for which many numerical solvers exist
[
39
,
35
,
50
,
45
]
.

This work presents a variational formulation of the MHD equilibrium equations on relatively general domains
\Lambda
. Specifically, we show that a variational method developed for nested toroidal domains
[
12
]
—which can approximate MHD equilibria under certain assumptions
[
11
]
—can be extended to more general domains
\Lambda
. To properly discuss the motivating ideas we introduce the necessary notation and provide a brief overview of the relevant literature for the case of non-degenerate pressure profiles.

For

Each non-empty intersection

\displaystyle d\star\beta_{i}=\mu_{i}\beta_{i}\,,\qquad\big\llbracket\|\beta\|^{2}+2p\big\rrbracket\big|_{I_{j}}=0\,,

\displaystyle\nabla\times B_{i}=\mu_{i}B_{i}\,,\qquad\big\llbracket\|B\|^{2}+2p\big\rrbracket\big|_{I_{j}}=0\,.

In this subsection, we restrict ourselves to a special geometric setting and review previous work. The existing literature addresses minimisation problems, but more generally we study stationary points.

Toroidal geometries are common when solving for MHD equilibria. Consider a smooth pressure
p
that is non-degenerate (
\nabla p\neq 0
) on
\partial\Lambda
. Then an MHD solution satisfies
B\cdot\nabla p=0
, namely,
B
is tangential to the corresponding pressure level set.

On level sets of
p
with
\nabla p\neq 0
everywhere, we have
\nabla p=(\nabla\times B)\times B
, so
B
and
\nabla\times B
are both non-zero and
B
is nowhere parallel to
\nabla\times B
. The Poincaré–Hopf theorem implies that any compact manifold admitting a nowhere-zero continuous tangent vector field has Euler characteristic zero. The only compact, connected, smooth surface with Euler characteristic zero is diffeomorphic to a 2-torus
[
2
]
. Consequently, if the pressure is non-degenerate then each level set is a disjoint union of 2-tori.

In this subsection we consider regions
\Lambda_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
that are solid and hollow tori embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, as in Figure
1
. This implies that interfaces are given by:
I_{k}=\partial\cup_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda_{i}=\partial\Lambda_{k}\cap\partial\Lambda_{k+1}
for
k=1,2,\dots,n-1
, which are pairwise-disjoint toroidal surfaces.

The Stepped Pressure Equilibrium Code (SPEC) is a numerical solver for the MRxMHD variational problem, capable of reconstructing stellarator magnetic fields with arbitrarily high accuracy. SPEC can compute MRxMHD equilibria that incorporate cross-helicity conditions, field-aligned flow and angular-momentum constraints
[
30
,
41
]
. The program is used to generate and analyse stellarator configurations that exhibit low neoclassical damping relative to other reactor designs
[

,
25
]
.

Numerical studies using SPEC provide evidence that solutions to the MRxMHD variational problem (
1.2
) can approximate MHD equilibria (
1
). However, because the existence of MHD equilibria is not known in general, there is no a priori guarantee that one can choose constants

for the MRxMHD problem which correspond to an MHD equilibrium
[
11
]
. More generally, passing to the infinite-interface limit in the MRxMHD variational problem

then yields Euler–Lagrange conditions that coincide with the ideal MHD equilibrium equations
[
11
]
.

1.3
Background: Existence of MRxMHD Equilibria

A longstanding conjecture on the existence of MHD equilibria was proposed by Grad
[
20
]
. Grad conjectured that, under suitable regularity assumptions, any MHD equilibrium in
R^{3}
must satisfy one of two alternatives: either the pressure is constant, or the magnetic field is invariant under a nontrivial one-parameter group of Euclidean isometries.

An early study on the existence of MHD equilibria with stepped pressure, namely MRxMHD equilibria was conducted by Bruno and Laurence who showed the existence of solutions on domains that are a perturbation away from axisymmetry
[
4
]
. In Section
4
we show an equivalence between the boundary conditions they provide and the jump condition given in the MRxMHD equilibrium equations when
\llbracket p\rrbracket\neq 0
.

More recently, Enciso, Luque, and Peralta-Salas demonstrate the existence of solutions to (
2
) on nondegenerate, analytic, toroidal domains using a Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem. They also show that these results are generic
[
15
]
.

The existence of energy minimisers for
n=1
on any given domain
\Lambda
, which may not be toroidal, has been studied. When the magnetic field is flux-free, existence is guaranteed for any helicity
[
17
]
. Similarly, Laurence and Avellaneda provide the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equations and an existence proof
[
33
]
. It was also shown by Parsley that if there exist magnetic fields with a given flux, helicity, and gauge then there exists a corresponding energy minimiser (Theorem 6.1
[
38
]
). Similarly, a study by Yoshida and Dewar showed existence of solutions to the Beltrami equations with helicity and flux constraints
[
49
]
.

1.4
Paper Layout

One of our principal results is that the MRxMHD equations (
2
) are necessary and sufficient conditions for a non-zero magnetic field to be a stationary point of the associated variational problem. In particular, we replace the earlier non-degeneracy hypothesis
\nabla p\neq 0
— which forces boundary components to be unions of tori — by the substantially weaker assumption that each partition element
\Lambda_{i}
is a compact, oriented, connected volume with smooth boundary. This result permits computation of MRxMHD equilibria via variational methods on a much broader class of geometries than previously considered, including domains with knotted or linked toroidal boundaries (see Figure
3
).

In Section
2
we introduce the definitions required to state our principal theorems. Section
3
then states and discusses these theorems. Our formulation is expressed in terms of a relative helicity which, under a suitable gauge choice, reduces to the conventional helicity.

Section
4
provides useful results and a reformulation of our variational problem constraining relative helicity. Section
5
presents proofs for the main results in Section
3
. Lastly, Section
6
gives a sufficient condition for a critical point of the magnetic energy to also be a local minimum in the
n=1
case.

For each subdomain

\displaystyle\big\langle\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${[}$}}\omega\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${]}$}},\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${[}$}}z\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${]}$}}\big\rangle=\int_{z}\omega\,,\qquad\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${[}$}}\omega\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${]}$}}\in H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i})\,,\ \ \raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${[}$}}z\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${]}$}}\in H_{k}(\Lambda_{i})\,.

(5)

The pairing is well-defined by the divergence theorem: it depends only on the cohomology classes
\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${[}$}}\omega\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${]}$}}
and
\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${[}$}}z\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1}{${]}$}}
, not on the particular representatives
\omega
and

. Namely, if

since

). Likewise, if

\int_{z+\partial y}\omega-\int_{z}\omega=\int_{\partial y}\omega=\int_{y}d\omega=0,

because
\omega
is closed (

). The same Stokes-type argument shows the pairing descends to the relative groups. Namely, there are well defined pairings
\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\rangle
by integration for

and
H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})\times H_{k}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})
.

We write the 3-sphere as
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Let

is isomorphic to the first homology of the closure of

, see Appendix

Lemma 2.1
.

The homologies

and

Proof.

By construction the homologies are vector spaces. To show that these are also finite dimensional, we follow a proof by Bott and Tu (proposition 5.3.1

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider the inclusion maps
\mathcal{J}:\partial\Lambda_{i}\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{i}
and
\mathcal{J}^{c}:\partial\Lambda_{i}\hookrightarrow\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i}
. Then
(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*}):H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})\to H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})\oplus H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i})
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

By a Mayer–Vietoris sequence for the decomposition
\mathbb{S}^{3}=\Lambda_{i}\cup\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i}
, we obtain for
k>0
the exact sequence

\displaystyle H_{k+1}(\mathbb{S}^{3})\to H_{k}(\partial\Lambda_{i})\xrightarrow{\,(\mathcal{J}_{*},-\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})\,}H_{k}(\Lambda_{i})\oplus H_{k}(\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c})\to H_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{3})\,.

We have that
H_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{3})\cong\{0\}
for
k=1,2
, hence the map
(\mathcal{J}_{*},-\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})
is an isomorphism

\displaystyle H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})\cong H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})\oplus H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c})\,,

(6)

as is the map
(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})
.
∎

As
(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*}):H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})\to H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})\oplus H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i})
is an isomorphism, it has an inverse
(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})^{-1}
giving a unique element
[\sigma_{i,j}]\coloneqq(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})^{-1}([s_{i,j}],0)\in H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
for each

. Similarly, we define a unique element
[\tau_{i,j}]\coloneqq(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})^{-1}(0,[t_{i,j}])\in H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
for each
i,j
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Given non-empty bases
\{[s_{i,j}]\}_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}
,
\{[t_{i,j}]\}_{j=1}^{\text{\char 107\relax}_{i}}
, of

is a basis of

Proof.

The collection
\{([s_{i,1}],0),\dots,(0,[t_{i,1}]),\dots\}
is a basis of the direct sum
H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})\oplus H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c})
. Applying the isomorphism
(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})^{-1}
carries that basis to the set
\{[\sigma_{i,1}]\mathbin{,}\dots\mathbin{,}[\tau_{i,1}]\mathbin{,}\dots\}
in
H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})

H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
.

We can consider maps induced on de Rham cohomologies via the inclusions

\displaystyle\{0\}\cong H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\mathbb{S}^{3})\to H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i})\oplus H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{J}^{*}-(\mathcal{J}^{c})^{*}}H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\partial\Lambda_{i})\to H^{2}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\mathbb{S}^{3})\cong\{0\}\,,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{J}^{*}-(\mathcal{J}^{c})^{*})([s],[t])=\mathcal{J}^{*}[s]-(\mathcal{J}^{c})^{*}[t]\qquad\text{for}\qquad([s],[t])\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i})\oplus H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i})\,.

2.2
Relative Helicity

As noted in the introduction
1.1
, helicity
H_{\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})
is not, in general, a well-defined function of
\beta_{i}
, owing to its dependence on the choice of gauge for the associated potential. One way to obtain a well-defined quantity is to restrict the admissible class of potentials
G
so that the helicity
H_{G}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})
is uniquely determined for any given
\beta_{i}
, as is done in problem
1.2
.

An alternative, which we adopt here, is to introduce a
relative helicity
. This quantity is constructed to be independent of the gauge choice for a given
\beta_{i}
, while coinciding with the standard helicity when evaluated in a fixed reference gauge. In this way, helicity retains a clear physical interpretation without the need to impose additional constraints on the potential.

Given an Alexander basis we define the
relative helicity
of
\beta_{i}
over
\Lambda_{i}
for
\ell_{i}>0
and any primitive
a_{i}\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
of
b_{i}
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{a_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})\coloneqq\int_{\Lambda_{i}}a_{i}\wedge da_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}a_{i}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}a_{i}\,,

and
\mathscr{H}_{a_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})\coloneqq H_{G}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})
for
\ell_{i}=0
, and
G=\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
. Refer to Lemma
4.2
to see that the relative helicity is independent of the chosen primitive for a given
\beta_{i}
.

We refer to the following set as an Amperian gauge:

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(\Lambda_{i})=\bigg\{a_{i}\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})\,:\;\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}a_{i}=0\,,\;\forall j\bigg\}\,.

(7)

An Amperian primitive for
\beta_{i}
is any
a_{i}\in\mathscr{A}(\Lambda_{i})
such that
da_{i}=\beta_{i}
. See Lemma
4.4
for a proof that any
\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
has a primitive in
\mathscr{A}(\Lambda_{i})
. We compute the following change in helicity by applying the divergence theorem:

\displaystyle H_{G}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-H_{G^{\prime}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}s\wedge a_{i}\,,

for primitives
a_{i}\in G,a_{i}+s\in G^{\prime}
of
\beta_{i}
, where
s
is closed, for some
G,G^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
that ensure helicity is independent of the chosen potential. This expression can be interpreted as a cup product on the boundary
\partial\Lambda_{i}
, and its value depends on the interaction between cohomology classes for primitives. As the Amperian gauge fixes the primitive up to a de Rham cohomology class, the helicity is independent of the Amperian primitive choice (Lemma
4.2
). Note that the definition of an Amperian gauge is similar to the definition of Amperian knots given by Cantarella
[
8
]
.

Selecting an Amperian gauge reduces the relative helicity to helicity,

\displaystyle H_{\mathscr{A}(\Lambda_{i})}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=\mathscr{H}_{a_{i}+s}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})\quad\text{for}\quad a_{i}\in\mathscr{A}(\Lambda_{i})\,,

for all closed
s\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda)
. Recall that all primitives of
\beta
are of the form
a_{i}+s
. Intuitively, the relative helicity encodes the helicity in an Amperian reference gauge. Therefore, any result using a relative helicity also holds for helicity under a restriction of the primitives to an Amperian gauge.

2.3
Hodge-Morrey-Friedrichs Decomposition

The space
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
decomposes via a Hodge-Morrey-Friedrichs (HMF) decomposition
[
43
,
40
]
. Firstly, we define some notation for function spaces. We reserve
\mathcal{H}^{k}(\Lambda_{i})
for harmonic
k
-forms on
\Lambda_{i}
, and a subscript
D
or
N
indicates the Dirichlet of Neumann boundary conditions, as in
\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})
. More explicitly, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{k}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})=\{\lambda\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})\,:\;d\lambda=\delta\lambda=t\lambda=0\}\,,\qquad\mathcal{H}^{k}_{N}(\Lambda_{i})=\{\lambda\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})\,:\;d\lambda=\delta\lambda=n\lambda=0\}\,.

Consider
\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
, then we have a HMF decomposition (see Appendix
B.3
):

\displaystyle\star\beta_{i}=\delta(\star\eta_{i})+d\phi_{i}+dz_{i}+\star\lambda_{i}\,,

where
\star\eta_{i}\in\Omega_{N}^{2}(\Lambda_{i})
,
\lambda_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{D}^{2}(\Lambda_{i})
, and
\phi_{i},z_{i}\in\Omega^{0}(\Lambda_{i})
for
\phi_{i}|_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}=0
, and
z_{i}
is a harmonic function
\Delta z_{i}=0
where
\Delta
is the Laplacian. Via an application of a Hodge star isomorphism:

\displaystyle\beta_{i}=d\eta_{i}+\star d\phi_{i}+\star dz_{i}+\lambda_{i}\,.

Using
d\beta_{i}=d\star d\phi_{i}=0
and
\phi_{i}|_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}=0
with the divergence theorem:

\displaystyle\|d\phi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\Lambda_{i}}d\phi_{i}\wedge\star d\phi_{i}=-\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}\wedge d\star d\phi_{i}+\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}\wedge\star d\phi_{i}=0\,.

With smoothness of
\phi_{i}
, we have that
d\phi_{i}=0
. Similarly for the
dz_{i}
component, utilising that
t\beta_{i}=t(\star dz_{i})=0
:

\displaystyle\|dz_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\Lambda_{i}}dz_{i}\wedge\star dz_{i}=-\int_{\Lambda_{i}}z_{i}\wedge d\star dz_{i}+\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}t(z_{i})\wedge t(\star dz_{i})=0\,.

By smoothness of
z_{i}
we have
dz_{i}=0
. Therefore we are left with a decomposition
\beta_{i}=d\eta_{i}+\lambda_{i}
. By the Hodge isomorphism and Poincaré–Lefschetz duality

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{D}^{2}(\Lambda_{i})\cong H^{2}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})\cong H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i})\,,

for coefficients in
\mathbb{R}
. So
\mathcal{H}_{D}^{2}(\Lambda_{i})
is a
\ell_{i}
-dimensional vector space. The set
\{[T_{i,j}^{*}]\}_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}
(
\ell_{i}>0
) form a basis for
H^{2}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})
. The Hodge theorem says that there is a unique harmonic representative for each class
[T_{i,j}^{*}]
. So, without loss of generality, we select
T_{i,j}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{D}^{2}(\Lambda_{i})
. As
T_{i,j}^{*}
are a basis for
\mathcal{H}_{D}^{2}(\Lambda_{i})
, for constants
\psi_{i,j}
we have

\displaystyle\beta_{i}=d\eta_{i}+\lambda_{i}=d\eta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}T_{i,j}^{*}\,.

(8)

In the case of
\ell_{i}=0
:

\displaystyle\beta_{i}=d\eta_{i}\,.

Examples of these decompositions in the case of solid and hollow tori are given by Pfefferlé, Noakes, and Perrella
[
40
]
.

2.4
Flux

Let
\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
be a closed Dirichlet
2
-form on
\Lambda_{i}
, and let
[T]\in H_{2}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})
be a relative homology class. The
flux
of
\beta_{i}
through
T
is by definition the pairing
\langle[\beta_{i}],[T]\rangle
. Applying the divergence theorem to this pairing gives

\displaystyle\big\langle[\beta_{i}],[T]\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{T}\beta_{i}=\int_{\partial T}\mathcal{J}^{*}\eta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{T}T_{i,j}^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{T}T_{i,j}^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\big\langle[T_{i,j}^{*}],[T]\big\rangle\,,

since
\eta_{i}
vanishes on
\partial\Lambda_{i}
. In particular, for the relative cycles
T_{i,1},\dots,T_{i,\ell_{i}}
we obtain

\big\langle[\beta_{i}],[T_{i,j}]\big\rangle=\psi_{i,j}\qquad(1\leq j\leq\ell_{i})\,,

so that specifying the values
\psi_{i,1},\dots,\psi_{i,\ell_{i}}
is equivalent to fixing the harmonic component
h_{i}
and therefore determines the flux of
\beta_{i}
through every class
[T]
. This is the set of constraints we impose when formulating the variational problem with fixed flux. The flux constraints are invariant under the push-forward action of any
f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
, since

\big\langle[\beta_{i}],[T_{i,k}]\big\rangle=\big\langle[(f^{-1})^{*}\beta_{i}],[f(T_{i,k})]\big\rangle\,.

When
\ell_{i}=0
there is no harmonic component and every closed Dirichlet
2
-form is exact:
\beta_{i}=d\eta_{i}
with
\eta_{i}\in\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
. The divergence theorem then shows that the flux through any relative class vanishes,

\langle[\beta_{i}],[T]\rangle=\int_{T}d\eta_{i}=\int_{\partial T}\mathcal{J}^{*}\eta_{i}=0\,.

Therefore
\beta_{i}
is an element of the flux-free subspace of closed Dirichlet
2
-forms when
\ell_{i}=0
.

2.5
Connected
\Lambda_{i}

We assume that each partition element
\Lambda_{i}
is connected. This hypothesis removes a benign but inconvenient source of non-uniqueness, which we now explain.

If
\Lambda_{i}
is a connected, compact domain with smooth boundary, then a Beltrami field on
\Lambda_{i}
is determined uniquely by the Beltrami parameter
\mu_{i}
together with the flux constraints
\psi_{i,1},\dots,\psi_{i,\ell_{i}}

[
49
]
. Replacing the prescription of
\mu_{i}
by a constraint on the relative helicity still yields existence of a solution to the Beltrami equations.

By contrast, if
\Lambda_{i}
has several connected components then a helicity condition imposed only on the whole of
\Lambda_{i}
fixes merely the sum of the helicities of the components. In that situation the individual component helicities may be varied continuously while keeping their total fixed, which produces a non-trivial continuous family of admissible Beltrami fields. Requiring each
\Lambda_{i}
to be connected therefore removes this extra degree of freedom.

3
Main Results

Consider the following objects as given:

1.

A finite collection of compact, oriented, connected, pairwise disjoint subdomains
\Lambda_{i}

(i=1,\dots,n)
that partition
\Lambda
.

2.

An Alexander basis of
H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
for each
i
.

3.

For each
i
, if
\ell_{i}=0
, then
p_{i}
and
h_{i}
are given. If
\ell_{i}>0
, then
p_{i}
,
h_{i}
and
\psi_{i,1},\dots,\psi_{i,\ell_{i}}
are given.

Consider the variational problem:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)\\
\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})\end{subarray}}{\text{c. p.}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\big\|\beta_{i}\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-p_{i}\hbox{$\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\textstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{301280}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{$\scriptstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{295820}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle|$}}}}}$}\Lambda_{i}\hbox{$\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\textstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{301280}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{$\scriptstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{295820}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle|$}}}}}$}\right)\,,\\
\mathscr{H}_{a_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=h_{i}\,,\qquad\int_{T_{i,j}}\beta_{i}=\psi_{i,j}\,,\end{gathered}

where
da_{i}=\beta_{i}
for all
i
. The constraints hold for all
i=1,\dots,n
and relevant
j
. The notation “c. p.” stands for critical points. If the pressure, helicity, and flux constants for any
i
are identically zero (
p_{i}=h_{i}=\psi_{i,1}=\dots=\psi_{i,\ell_{i}}=0
) then
\beta_{i}=0
is the unique global minimiser.

We remind the reader that we are fixing
\Lambda_{i}
and allowing the metric to change through
f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
. This effectively determines the locations of interfaces within
f(\Lambda)
.

The following theorem details a connection between solutions to the variational problem (
3
) and solutions to the MRxMHD equations (
2
).

Theorem 3.1
.

A non-zero element
\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
is a solution to the variational problem (
3
) if and only if it satisfies the MRxMHD equations (
2
) with the corresponding helicity and flux constraints.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
(h_{i},\psi_{i,1},\dots,\psi_{i,\ell_{i}})\neq 0
for
i=1,\dots,n
then the MRxMHD equations (
2
) with the corresponding helicity and flux constraints are necessary and sufficient conditions for all solutions to the variational problem (
3
).

Remark 3.3
.

The variational problem (
3
) is formulated in terms of critical points, but for physical applications one is usually interested in local
minimisers
(which correspond to stable equilibria). Woltjer’s 1958 analysis introduced a closely related variational principle and argued in favour of minimising solutions. In the proof of Theorem
3.1
we show that, for the case
n=1
and upon adopting an Amperian gauge, Woltjer’s theorem is recovered.

Theorem
3.1
implies that the MRxMHD equations (
2
) are necessary for a minimiser to problem (
3
) with nested toroidal surfaces. This result is known, as discussed in subsection
1.2
.

On a technical note, we define a critical point of a map
\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)\times\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})\to\mathbb{R}
as a point in the domain where the Fréchet derivative in a
C^{\infty}
topology for
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
and a Gâteau derivative with respect to the
\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
coordinates are zero. We require a Fréchet derivative combine with a choice of topology for
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
in order to determine a necessary and sufficient condition when applying the Lagrange multiplier theorem.

Remark 3.4
.

A result establishing the existence of solutions to the Beltrami equation was proven by Yoshida and Dewar. These authors use the Fredholm alternative and a technique they refer to as
helicity matching
(section 2.3
[
49
]
). Therefore, there is always a solution to problem (
3
) when
n=1
. In Appendix
C
, we prove an existence result that is weaker than the known result above, using the direct method from calculus of variations.

4
Preparatory Results

Lemma 4.1
.

For any closed 1-forms
s,\tilde{s}\in\Omega^{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
and an Alexander basis
\{[\sigma_{i,j}],[\tau_{i,j}]\}_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}
of
H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
we have a decomposition:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tilde{s}\wedge s=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!s\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\tilde{s}-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\tilde{s}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!s\right)\,.

(11)

Proof.

Any 1-form
s\in\Omega^{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
admits a Hodge-Morrey decomposition into an exact form, co-exact form, and a harmonic 1-form representing a cohomology class (
[
43
]
, see Appendix
B.3
for this decomposition):

\displaystyle s=\delta\eta+d\phi+\gamma\,,

where
\eta\in\Omega^{2}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
,
\phi\in\Omega^{0}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
, and a harmonic
\gamma\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
. Utilising the closure of
s
(
0=ds=d\delta\eta
) and the divergence theorem,

\displaystyle\|\delta\eta\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\delta\eta\wedge\star\delta\eta=-\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\delta\eta\wedge d\star\eta=-\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}d\delta\eta\wedge\star\eta+\int_{\partial^{2}\Lambda_{i}}\delta\eta\wedge\star\eta=0\,.

Note that the Hodge star operators on
\partial\Lambda_{i}
acting on 1-forms satisfy
\star\star=-1
. Therefore, the co-exact component in the Hodge-Morrey decomposition of a closed 1-form disappears. Taking the Hodge decomposition for two closed
1
-forms
s,\tilde{s}\in\Omega^{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
:

\displaystyle s=d\phi+\gamma\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{s}=d\tilde{\phi}+\tilde{\gamma}\,,

where
\tilde{\phi}\in\Omega^{0}(\Lambda_{i})
and
\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
.

Moving all terms in equation (
11
) to one side, we want to see if the result is zero. We begin this check by replacing the closed 1-forms with their Hodge-Morrey decomposition:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tilde{s}\wedge s-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}s\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\tilde{s}-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\tilde{s}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}s\right)\\
=\\
\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}(d\tilde{\phi}+\tilde{\gamma})\wedge(d\phi+\gamma)-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}(d\phi+\gamma)\int_{\tau_{i,j}}(d\tilde{\phi}+\tilde{\gamma})-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}(d\tilde{\phi}+\tilde{\gamma})\int_{\tau_{i,j}}(d\phi+\gamma)\right)\,.\end{gathered}

Knowing that
\partial\tau_{i,j}=\partial\sigma_{i,j}=0
we have, by the divergence theorem:

\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{i,j}}d\phi=\int_{\partial\tau_{i,j}}\phi=0\,,

similarly for the integrals over
\sigma_{i,j}
. Additionally:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}d\tilde{\phi}\wedge s=-\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tilde{\phi}\wedge ds+\int_{\partial^{2}\Lambda_{i}}\tilde{\phi}\wedge s=0\,.

Similarly for the term involving
\tilde{s}\wedge d\phi
. This leaves us with the relation:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tilde{s}\wedge s-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!s\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\tilde{s}-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\tilde{s}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!s\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tilde{\gamma}\wedge\gamma-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\gamma\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\tilde{\gamma}-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\tilde{\gamma}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\gamma\right)\,.

(12)

Replacing
\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}
with their classes in
H^{1}_{dR}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
given by
[\gamma]
and
[\tilde{\gamma}]
respectively, these integrals remain well-defined. Decomposing
[\gamma]
and
[\tilde{\gamma}]
in the dual to the Alexander basis we have that

\displaystyle[\gamma]=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}c_{j}^{1}[\sigma_{i,j}^{*}]+c_{j}^{2}[\tau_{i,j}^{*}]\,,

\displaystyle[\tilde{\gamma}]=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\tilde{c}_{j}^{1}[\sigma_{i,j}^{*}]+\tilde{c}_{j}^{2}[\tau_{i,j}^{*}]\,,

for constants
c_{j}^{1},c_{j}^{2},\tilde{c}_{j}^{1}
and
\tilde{c}_{j}^{2}
. Computing our wedge product between the gamma’s:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tilde{\gamma}\wedge\gamma

\displaystyle=\scalebox{1.2}[1.4]{$\displaystyle\int$}_{\!\!\!\!\!\partial\Lambda_{i}}\!\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\tilde{c}_{j}^{1}\sigma_{i,j}^{*}+\tilde{c}_{j}^{2}\tau_{i,j}^{*}\Bigg)\wedge\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}c_{j}^{1}\sigma_{i,j}^{*}+c_{j}^{2}\tau_{i,j}^{*}\Bigg)\,.

Recall from Theorem
2.4
that:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tau_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=\delta_{jk}\,,\qquad\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\sigma_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=0\,,\qquad\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tau_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\tau_{i,k}^{*}=0\,,\end{gathered}

so

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tilde{\gamma}\wedge\gamma

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\tilde{c}_{j}^{2}c_{j}^{1}-\tilde{c}_{j}^{1}c_{j}^{2}\,.

And:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\gamma\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\tilde{\gamma}-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\tilde{\gamma}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\gamma\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(c^{1}_{j}\tilde{c}^{2}_{j}-\tilde{c}^{1}_{j}c^{2}_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tilde{\gamma}\wedge\gamma\,.

Substituting this relation into equation (
12
) we get that:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tilde{s}\wedge s-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!s\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\tilde{s}-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\tilde{s}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!s\right)

\displaystyle=0\,.

∎

4.1
Well-Posed

Lemma 4.2
.

For a magnetic field represented by a closed, Dirichlet 2-form
\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
with potential
a_{i}\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
, the relative helicity
\mathscr{H}_{a_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})
is independent of the choice of primitive
a_{i}
. Additionally, if
f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
then
\mathscr{H}_{a_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=\mathscr{H}_{(f^{-1})^{*}a_{i}}((f^{-1})^{*}\beta_{i},f(\Lambda_{i}))
.

Proof.

Consider
a_{i},\tilde{a}_{i}\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
such that
da_{i}=d\tilde{a}_{i}=\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
. Then the difference in relative helicities applied to each primitive is:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{a_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-\mathscr{H}_{\tilde{a}_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\mathcal{J}^{*}\tilde{a}_{i}\wedge\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}\tilde{a}_{i}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}\tilde{a}_{i}\right)\,.

The forms
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\tilde{a}_{i})
and
\mathcal{J}^{*}(a_{i})
are closed 1-forms in
\Omega^{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
, so we may apply equation (
11
):

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{a_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-\mathscr{H}_{\tilde{a}_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\Bigg(

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}\tilde{a}_{i}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}\tilde{a}_{i}

\displaystyle\!\!+\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}\tilde{a}_{i}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!\mathcal{J}^{*}\tilde{a}_{i}\Bigg)\,.

By construction, for each
j
, there exists a
T_{i,j}
where
\partial T_{i,j}=\tau_{i,j}
, therefore:

\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}\tilde{a}_{i}=\int_{T_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}b_{i}=\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}\,.

Applying this to the difference between relative helicities computed in different gauges gives:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{a_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-\mathscr{H}_{\tilde{a}_{i}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=0\,.

Hence, the relative helicity is independent of the gauge. Due to this, we now drop the subscript of
\mathscr{H}
that specifies the potential.

Additionally, for
f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
and an appropriate
G\subseteq\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-\mathscr{H}((f^{-1})^{*}\beta_{i},f(\Lambda_{i}))=

\displaystyle H_{G}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-H_{(f^{-1})^{*}G}((f^{-1})^{*}\beta_{i},f(\Lambda_{i}))

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}a_{i}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\int_{f(\sigma_{i,j})}(f^{-1})^{*}a_{i}\int_{f(\tau_{i,j})}(f^{-1})^{*}a_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{G}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-H_{(f^{-1})^{*}G}((f^{-1})^{*}\beta_{i},f(\Lambda_{i}))\,.

We have the freedom to select a
G
without altering the relative helicity. Hence, we restrict primitives to a Biot-Savart gauge, as defined by Cantarella
[
7
]
. With this gauge Cantarella shows that
H_{G}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=H_{(f^{-1})^{*}G}((f^{-1})^{*}\beta_{i},f(\Lambda_{i}))
. Hence,
\mathscr{H}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=\mathscr{H}((f^{-1})^{*}\beta_{i},f(\Lambda_{i}))
must hold in any gauge.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

The objective and constraints in the variational problem (
3
) are independent of the chosen gauge. Namely, the problem is well-defined in terms of the magnetic field
\beta_{i}
and
f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

There exists at least one element
a_{i}\in\mathscr{A}(\Lambda_{i})
such that
da_{i}=\beta_{i}
for any
\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
.

Proof.

There exists at least one primitive
a_{i}\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
such that
da_{i}=\beta_{i}
(Lemma
B.1
). Additionally,
a_{i}+s
is also a primitive for
\beta_{i}
for any closed 1-form
s\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
. Hence,
[s]
is any element of
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i})
and therefore has a decomposition:

\displaystyle[s]=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}c_{i,j}[s_{i,j}^{*}]\,.

Pulling back to the boundary via inclusion gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{*}[s]=\sum_{j}c_{i,j}\mathcal{J}^{*}[s_{i,j}^{*}]=\sum_{j}c_{i,j}[\sigma_{i,j}^{*}]\,.

Giving

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}(a_{i}+s)=\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}+\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell_{i}}c_{i,k}\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=c_{i,j}+\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}\,.

Setting
c_{i,j}
so that the right-hand side is zero uniquely specifies
[s]
and we have that
a_{i}+s
is an Amperian primitive for
\beta_{i}
.
∎

This lemma is important because the relative helicity given any gauge always aligns with the helicity for an Amperian gauge.

Remark 4.5
.

Given a
\Lambda_{i}
with non-trivial genus and
\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
, the helicity
H_{G}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})
can take any value via a choice of
G\subseteq\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
if
\ell_{i}>0
and
\psi_{i,1},\dots,\psi_{i,\ell_{i}}
are not all identically zero.

We consider this remark in more detail below. Under the conditions in remark
4.5
,
\beta_{i}\neq 0
and
\ell_{i}>0
. Consider two primitives of
\beta_{i}
given by
a_{i}\in G
and
a_{i}+s\in G^{\prime}
for a closed 1-form
s\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
. Both
s
and
\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}
are closed on the boundary so formula (
11
) is applicable:

\displaystyle H_{G}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-H_{G^{\prime}}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\!s\wedge a_{i}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!s\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!a_{i}-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!a_{i}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!s\right)=-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!s\right)\,.

Therefore, if any of the fluxes
\psi_{i,j}
are non-trivial, we may select
s
to make the difference in helicity any value (we saw a comparable situation in the proof of Lemma
4.4
).

Therefore, if we consider problem (
1.2
) without constraining the potential for non-trivial flux: (1) the constraint
H_{\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=h_{i}
is not well-defined as a function of
\beta_{i}
and (2) the helicity can take any value, so this constraint does not restrict
\beta_{i}
in any way. Similarly, for problem (
3
) with a helicity constraint rather than a relative helicity constraint for non-trivial genus and
\psi_{i,1},\dots,\psi_{i,\ell_{i}}
not identically zero.

4.2
Jump Conditions

The boundary and jump conditions that we are using in equations (
2
) are reported as a special case of the following conditions given by Bruno and Laurence
[
4
]
:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\llbracket B\cdot N\rrbracket\,,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\llbracket B^{2}+2p\rrbracket N-2(B\cdot N)\llbracket B\rrbracket\,\,,

on
\partial\Lambda_{i}
where
N
is the outward unit normal vector field to the boundary. The two terms in the second equation here are orthogonal, namely, the first term is parallel to
N
and the second term lies in the tangent space of
\partial\Lambda_{i}
. Therefore, both terms must be
0
independent of each-other. So either
B
is continuous across the boundary and
\llbracket p\rrbracket=0
, or:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=B\cdot N\,,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\llbracket B^{2}+2p\rrbracket\,.

Under the assumption that
\llbracket p\rrbracket\neq 0
across our interfaces, the original conditions from Bruno and Laurence are equivalent to the boundary conditions in (
2
).

4.3
Lagrange Multipliers

We know that any
\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
for
\ell_{i}>0
can be written as:

\displaystyle\beta_{i}=d\eta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}T_{i,j}^{*}=d\Bigg(\eta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\Gamma_{i,j}\Bigg)\,,

(13)

for
\eta_{i}\in\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
,
T_{i,j}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{D}^{2}(\Lambda_{i})
, and
\Gamma_{i,j}\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
. The second equality holds because there always exists a primitive
\Gamma_{i,j}
for
T_{i,j}^{*}
(Lemma
B.1
).

In the variational formulation (
3
) we incorporate the flux constraints directly into the objective. We see from equation (
13
) that the harmonic component of
\beta_{i}
(which determine the flux of
\beta_{i}
through any relative
2
-chain) is fixed once the coefficients
\psi_{i,1},\dots,\psi_{i,\ell_{i}}
are prescribed, provided
\ell_{i}>0
. Consequently, the objective and helicity are functions of
f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
and
d\eta_{i}
. The potential
\eta_{i}
is not always unique, different
\eta_{i}
may have the same exterior derivative
\beta_{i}
, hence the same value for the objective and relative helicity. To incorporate variations of the potentials we formulate the optimisation over
f
and
\eta_{i}
, giving

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta_{i}\in\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})\\
f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)\end{subarray}}{\text{c. p.}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg(\frac{1}{2}\Big\|d\eta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\,T_{i,j}^{*}\Big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-p_{i}\,|\Lambda_{i}|\Bigg)\\
\text{subject to}\qquad\mathscr{H}\!\Big(d\eta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\,T_{i,j}^{*},\;\Lambda_{i}\Big)=h_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n\,.\end{gathered}

If
\ell_{i}=0
there is no harmonic contribution in the displayed expressions.

For the remainder of this section we freeze the deformation
f
. The variational problem reduces to finding admissible potentials
\eta_{i}
. A standard way to treat the constraint in (
4.3
) is to introduce Lagrange multipliers. If the constraint map is a submersion, the regular value theorem implies that the preimage of the prescribed value is a submanifold whose tangent space at each point is characterised as the kernel of the linearised constraint. Imposing that admissible variations lie in this tangent space and the Lagrange multipliers theorem yields Euler–Lagrange equations for the constrained optimisation problem.

Let’s consider a regular value theorem for the relative helicity constraints in problem (
4.3
). Our definition of a critical point requires us to determine the Fréchet derivative in a
C^{\infty}
topology, hence, we consider
\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
with this topology.

We can view
\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
as the kernel of a continuous linear map given by the pullback
\mathcal{J}^{*}:\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})\to\Omega^{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
. So
\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
is a Fréchet space because it is a closed subspace of the Fréchet space
\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
.

Our constraint function is
g_{i}:\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})\to\mathbb{R}
, given by
g_{i}:\eta_{i}\mapsto\mathscr{H}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})
. For any
\eta_{i}\in\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
, the
tangent spaces

T_{\eta_{i}}\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
and
T_{g_{i}(\eta_{i})}\mathbb{R}
may be canonically identified with
\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
and
\mathbb{R}
respectively
[
32
]
. A standard computation gives us that
g_{i}
is continuously Fréchet differentiable, and therefore has a directional derivative (proof in Appendix
B.4
). The derivative of
g_{i}
with respect to
\eta_{i}
in the direction
\eta_{i}^{\prime}\in\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
is denoted
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\eta_{i}^{\prime})
. Given this, we may adapt a regular value theorem given by Glöckner (theorem D
[
19
]
) to our setting.

Theorem 4.6

(Glöckner, theorem D adaptation
[
19
]
)
.

Consider a smooth submersion
g_{i}:\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})\to\mathbb{R}
that has a Fréchet derivative
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}:\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})\to\mathbb{R}
at
\eta_{i}\in\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
. If
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}
is surjective, then
\ker(d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i})=T_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}^{-1}(y)
for any
y=g_{i}(\eta_{i})
.

Note that Glöckner’s result relies on
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}
having a
complemented
kernel at
\eta_{i}\in\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
, which is true when
g_{i}
has a one dimensional codomain
[
19
]
(Appendix
B.5
).

Since
g_{i}
is Fréchet differentiable,
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}
coincides with the Gâteaux derivative
[
51
]
. Hence, we proceed with the Gâteaux derivative.

Consider
\eta_{i,t}=\eta_{i}+t\eta_{i}^{\prime}+o(t)\in\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
that is differentiable in
t\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
,
\epsilon>0
. A partial derivative of
\eta_{i,t}
with respect to
t
is denoted
\partial_{t}
and
\eta_{i}^{\prime}=\partial_{t}\eta_{i,t}|_{t=0}
is an element of the tangent space
T_{\eta_{i}}\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
. So:

\displaystyle d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\eta_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}g_{i}(\eta_{i,t})|_{t=0}=2\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\eta_{i}^{\prime}\wedge\beta_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\eta_{i}^{\prime}\,.

(16)

We have used the divergence theorem in our simplification.

Assume that
\beta_{i}
is not identically zero on
\Lambda_{i}
. Then there exists a point
x_{0}\in\Lambda_{i}
such that
\beta_{i}(x_{0})\neq 0
. By continuity,
\beta_{i}
remains non-zero on some ball
\mathcal{B}(r)\subset\joinrel\!\!\subset\Lambda_{i}
with radius
r>0
. Since
\partial\Lambda_{i}
is smooth,
\Lambda_{i}
satisfies the
\epsilon
-cone property
[
26
]
; hence there exists a ball
\mathcal{B}(r_{0})\subset\joinrel\!\!\subset\mathcal{B}(r)\cap\Lambda_{i}
for some
r_{0}>0
. In particular,
\beta_{i}
is non-zero throughout a compact subset of
\Lambda_{i}
. It follows that

\|\beta_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Lambda_{i})}^{2}\geq\|\beta_{i}|_{\mathcal{B}(r_{0})}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{B}(r_{0}))}^{2}>0\,.

Let
\chi_{\mathcal{B}}
denote the characteristic function of
\mathcal{B}(r_{0})
on
\Lambda_{i}
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\chi_{k}\colon\Lambda_{i}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function in
\Omega^{0}(\Lambda_{i})
satisfying
\chi_{k}|_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}=0
, and choose
\chi_{k}\to\chi_{\mathcal{B}}
pointwise as
k\to\infty
. Consider the variation
\eta_{i}^{\prime}=\chi_{k}\,\star\beta_{i}
in the directional derivative (
16
). Then

\displaystyle d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\eta_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=2\!\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\!\!\chi_{k}\,\star\beta_{i}\wedge\beta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\!\mathcal{J}^{*}(\chi_{k}\star\beta_{i})\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}2\!\int_{\mathcal{B}(r_{0})}\!\!\star\beta_{i}\wedge\beta_{i}=2\|\beta_{i}|_{\mathcal{B}(r_{0})}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{B}(r_{0}))}^{2}>0\,.

Therefore, for
k
sufficiently large,
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\chi_{k}\,\star\beta_{i})>0
. By linearity of the derivative,
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}
is surjective onto
\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, every
h_{i}
is a regular value for the relative helicity constraint
g_{i}(\eta_{i})=h_{i}
, provided
\beta_{i}\neq 0
(Theorem
4.6
). Hence the regular Lagrange multiplier condition gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a solution of the original problem (Appendix
B.6
).

The same argument applies verbatim to conventional helicity, up to any additional constraints on the potential.

Applying the method of Lagrange multipliers therefore yields the following necessary and sufficient conditions for a solution:

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta_{i}\in\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})\\
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{c.p.}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Big\|d\eta_{i}+\sum_{j}\psi_{i,j}T_{i,j}^{*}\Big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-p_{i}\hbox{$\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\textstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{301280}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{$\scriptstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{295820}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle|$}}}}}$}\Lambda_{i}\hbox{$\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\textstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{301280}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{$\scriptstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{295820}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle|$}}}}}$}-\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}\big(\mathscr{H}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-h_{i}\big)\right),

for Lagrange multipliers
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, assuming
\beta_{i}\neq 0
. As each domain may be considered independently we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
(\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{n})
be a partition of
\Lambda
, and assume that
\beta\neq 0
. Fix the constants
\psi_{i,j}
,
p_{i}
, and
h_{i}
. Given
f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
and the volume form
f^{*}\varpi
, problem (
3
) reduces, for each
i
with
\ell_{i}>0
, to

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta_{i}\in\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})\\
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{c.p.}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Big\|d\eta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}T_{i,j}^{*}\Big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}\big(\mathscr{H}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-h_{i}\big)\right).

(17)

For
i
where
\ell_{i}=0
, the harmonic terms vanish.

5
Proof of Main Results

5.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1

We start from the original formulation for our variational problem (
3
) with
\beta_{i}\neq 0
. Consider an
f_{t}\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
for each
t\in[0,1]
that is differentiable in
t
where
f_{1}
is the identity,
f_{0}=f
. The objective function, or magnetic energy is:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}E(f_{t},\beta_{1,t},\dots,\beta_{n,t})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big\|\beta_{i,t}\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-p_{i}\hbox{$\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\textstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{301280}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{$\scriptstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{295820}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle|$}}}}}$}\Lambda_{i}\hbox{$\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\textstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{301280}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{$\scriptstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{295820}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle|$}}}}}$}\Big)\,,\end{gathered}

where
\beta_{i,t}\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda_{i})
for each
t\in[0,1]
and
a_{i,t}
is continuously differentiable in
t
, where
da_{i,t}=\beta_{i,t}
. Let
a_{i,0}=a_{i}
and
\beta_{i,0}=\beta_{i}
.

We compute the derivative of
E
that is needed to define critical points: namely, a derivative that is Fréchet with respect to
\eta
and Gâteaux with respect to
f
. Since
E
is Fréchet differentiable in
\eta
, this derivative may be obtained by differentiating
E
along all paths
P_{t}\coloneqq(f_{t},\beta_{1,t},\dots,\beta_{n,t})
that satisfy the flux and relative helicity constraints in Problem (
3
). We refer to such paths as
allowable paths
. A point is critical for the magnetic energy precisely when the first variation of the energy vanishes at
t=0
along every allowable path. Hence, a necessary and sufficient condition for
P_{0}
to be a critical point is

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{t}E(P_{t})|_{t=0}=\left(\partial_{f_{t}}E(f^{\prime})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{\beta_{i,t}}E(\beta_{i}^{\prime})\right)\Bigg|_{t=0}\,,

where
(f^{\prime},\beta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\beta_{n}^{\prime})=(\partial_{t}f_{t}|_{t=0},\partial_{t}\beta_{1,t}|_{t=0},\dots,\partial_{t}\beta_{n,t}|_{t=0})
as in Section
4.3
. Namely,
\partial_{f_{t}}E(f^{\prime})
is the partial derivative with respect to
f_{t}
, in the direction
f^{\prime}
at the point
P_{t}
for each
t
. So the conditions for
P_{0}
to be a critical point are exactly:

\displaystyle 0=d_{f_{t}}E(f^{\prime})|_{t=0}\,,\qquad 0=d_{\beta_{i,t}}E(\beta_{i}^{\prime})|_{t=0}\,,\;\;i=1,\dots,n\,,

(18)

for all allowable paths passing through
P_{0}
. As
\beta_{i}\neq 0
, the second condition is equivalent to finding the critical points of
E
at a fixed
f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
. Recall from the previous section that this is equivalent to a Lagrange multipliers problem (Lemma
4.7
). Namely, for each
i=1,\dots,n
we intend to determine critical points of

\displaystyle L(\eta_{i},\mu_{i})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\big\|da_{i}\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}\left(\mathscr{H}(da_{i},\Lambda_{i})-h_{i}\right)\,,

\displaystyle a_{i}=\eta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\Gamma_{i,j}\,,

where the harmonic terms are zero whenever
\ell_{i}=0
. So

\displaystyle 0=d_{\beta_{i}}E(\beta_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\iff 0=\partial_{t}L(\eta_{i,t},\mu_{i,t})|_{t=0}=d_{\eta_{i,t}}L(\eta_{i}^{\prime})|_{t=0}+d_{\mu_{i,t}}L(\mu_{i}^{\prime})|_{t=0}

\displaystyle\iff 0=d_{\eta_{i,t}}L(\eta_{i}^{\prime})|_{t=0}\quad\text{and}\quad 0=d_{\mu_{i,t}}L(\mu_{i}^{\prime})|_{t=0}\,,

for
\mu_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}
that are differentiable in
t
where
\mu_{i}^{\prime}=\partial_{t}\mu_{i,t}|_{t=0}
. The latter equation here is given by:

\displaystyle 0=d_{\mu_{i,t}}L(\mu_{i}^{\prime})|_{t=0}=\partial_{t}L(\eta_{i},\mu_{i,t})|_{t=0}=-\frac{\mu_{i}^{\prime}}{2}\left(\mathscr{H}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})-h_{i}\right)\,,

for all
i
and
\mu_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, this is equivalent to
\mathscr{H}(\beta_{i},\Lambda_{i})=h_{i}
for all
i
. For the other derivative of
L
we have that:

\displaystyle d_{\eta_{i,t}}L(\eta_{i}^{\prime})|_{t=0}=\partial_{t}L(\eta_{i,t},\mu_{i})|_{t=0}=\langle d\eta_{i}^{\prime},\beta_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}\int_{\Lambda_{i}}(\eta^{\prime}_{i}\wedge\beta_{i}+a_{i}\wedge d\eta_{i}^{\prime})=\langle\eta_{i}^{\prime},\star(d\star\beta_{i}-\mu_{i}\beta_{i})\rangle_{L^{2}}\,.

The vanishing of
d_{\eta_{i,t}}L(\eta_{i}^{\prime})|_{t=0}
for all
\eta^{\prime}_{i}\in\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
implies that
\star(d\star\beta_{i}-\mu_{i}\beta_{i})=0
on the interior of
\Lambda_{i}
. Since
\beta_{i}
is smooth, the quantity
\star(d\star\beta_{i}-\mu_{i}\beta_{i})
is continuous. As this vanishes on the interior of
\Lambda_{i}
, it must vanish everywhere on
\Lambda_{i}
. Therefore, we conclude that
d\star\beta_{i}=\mu_{i}\beta_{i}
, which is a Beltrami equation in
\beta_{i}
for each
i
. Note that the Hodge star operator encodes the volume form
f^{*}\varpi
on
\Lambda_{i}
. Upon pushing forward via
f
, we obtain a Beltrami equation for the elements of
\mathcal{C}(f(\Lambda_{i}))
with a Euclidean metric. This result is highlighted in Remark
3.3
.

The conditions for a critical point in (
18
) are equivalently written as:

\displaystyle 0=d_{f_{t}}E(f_{t})|_{t=0}=\partial_{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big\|\beta_{i,t}\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-p_{i}\hbox{$\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\textstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{301280}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{$\scriptstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{295820}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle|$}}}}}$}\Lambda_{i}\hbox{$\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\displaystyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{327677}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{2.5pt}{$\textstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{301280}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.75pt}{$\scriptstyle|$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{295820}{0}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle|$}}}}}$}\Big)\right)\Bigg|_{t=0}\,,\quad d\star\beta_{i}=\mu_{i}\beta_{i}\,,\quad\mathscr{H}(\beta_{i,t},\Lambda_{i})=h_{i}\,,

(19)

for all
i
, where the harmonic terms in the HMF decomposition of
\beta_{i,t}
are given, as in equation (
13
). Note that the relative helicity holds for all
t
by definition of the admissible paths. We interpret this problem as: finding critical points of the magnetic energy under variations of the boundary through all paths
P_{t}
satisfying the prescribed flux and helicity constraints while
P_{0}
satisfies the Beltrami equation.

Consider a derivative
d/dtf_{t}:\Lambda\to Tf_{t}(\Lambda)
. Composing this with a directional derivative
d_{t}f_{t}^{-1}:Tf_{t}(\Lambda)\to T(\Lambda)
gives us a vector field
V(x)\coloneqq d_{t}f_{t}^{-1}\ (d/dt(f_{t}(x)))|_{t=0}
, for
x\in\Lambda
, namely
V:\Lambda\to T(\Lambda)
. With this we have the following formula, essentially Cartan’s formula,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(f^{*}_{t}\omega_{t})|_{t=0}=f^{*}_{0}(\mathcal{L}_{V}(\omega_{0})+\partial_{t}\omega_{t}|_{t=0})=i_{V}(d\omega_{0})+di_{V}(\omega_{0})+\partial_{t}\omega_{t}|_{t=0}\,,

for any
\omega_{t}\in\Omega^{k}(f_{t}(\Lambda_{i}))
where
\mathcal{L}_{V}
is a Lie derivative with respect to
V
.

It is useful to perform some operations on
f_{t}(\Lambda_{i})
because the metric here is the Euclidean metric and independent of
t
. Let
\epsilon_{i}\coloneqq f_{t}^{*}\partial_{t}((f_{t}^{-1})^{*}\eta_{i,t})|_{t=0}
where
(f_{t}^{-1})^{*}\eta_{i,t}\in\Omega^{1}(f_{t}(\Lambda_{i}))
and
(f_{t}^{-1})^{*}
is a pushforward by
f_{t}
. Then we have commutation of the Hodge star with a derivative:

\displaystyle f^{*}_{t}\partial_{t}(f_{t}^{-1})^{*}\star\eta_{i,t}|_{t=0}=\star f^{*}_{t}\partial_{t}(f_{t}^{-1})^{*}\eta_{i,t}|_{t=0}=\star\epsilon_{i}\,,

where
\star
is the Hodge star in
\Lambda_{i}
with volume form
f_{t}^{*}\varpi
. We use the notation
a_{i}^{\prime}=\partial_{t}\eta_{i,t}|_{t=0}
. This gives the relations:

\displaystyle a_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\eta_{i,t}|_{t=0}=\partial_{t}f_{t}^{*}((f_{t}^{-1})^{*}\eta_{i,t})|_{t=0}=i_{V}(d\eta_{i})+di_{V}(\eta_{i})+\epsilon_{i}\,,

\displaystyle(\star a_{i})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\star\eta_{i,t}|_{t=0}=\partial_{t}f_{t}^{*}((f_{t}^{-1})^{*}\star\eta_{i,t})|_{t=0}=i_{V}(d\star\eta_{i})+di_{V}(\star\eta_{i})+\star\epsilon_{i}\,.

Let’s return to our problem, given by equation (
19
). For simplicity, we deal with each term in the derivative with respect to
f_{t}
independently until the end of our computation. First, let’s consider
\|\beta_{i,t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
for any
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big\|\beta_{i,t}\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big|_{t=0}=2\langle da_{i},d\epsilon_{i}\rangle+\!\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\!\!di_{V}(da_{i}\wedge\star da_{i})=2\mu_{i}\!\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\!\!\epsilon_{i}\wedge\beta_{i}+\!\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\!\!\mathcal{J}^{*}(2\epsilon_{i}\wedge\star\beta_{i}+i_{V}(da_{i}\wedge\star da_{i}))\,.

We have used the Beltrami equation and divergence theorem in this computation. Note that these computations hold for any
\mu_{i}
. Additionally, the relative helicity is constant in
t
, so:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\left(\int_{\Lambda_{i}}a_{i,t}\wedge\beta_{i,t}-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i,t}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i,t}\right)\Bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\epsilon_{i}\wedge\beta_{i}+a_{i}\wedge d\epsilon_{i}+\mathcal{L}_{V}(a_{i}\wedge\beta_{i})-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\epsilon_{i}\wedge\beta_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\mathcal{J}^{*}(a_{i}\wedge\epsilon_{i}-i_{V}(a_{i}\wedge\beta_{i}))+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}^{\prime}\,,

where we have used the divergence theorem. Using this equality in the derivative of
\|\beta_{i,t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big\|\beta_{i,t}\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big|_{t=0}\!=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\!\!\mathcal{J}^{*}(\mu_{i}a_{i}\wedge\epsilon_{i}-\mu_{i}i_{V}(a_{i}\wedge\beta_{i})+2\epsilon_{i}\wedge\star\beta_{i}+i_{V}(da_{i}\wedge\star da_{i}))+\mu_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!\!\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}^{\prime}\,.

We see that this variation with respect to
t
reduces to integrals along the boundary
\partial\Lambda_{i}
. With some rearrangement of terms we arrive at:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big\|\beta_{i,t}\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\mathcal{J}^{*}((i_{V}\beta_{i}+\epsilon_{i})\wedge(2\star\beta_{i}-\mu_{i}a_{i})+\beta_{i}(i_{V}\star\beta_{i}-\mu i_{V}a_{i})-i_{V}(\beta_{i}\wedge\star\beta_{i}))

\displaystyle\qquad+\mu_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}^{\prime}\,.

Note that
\mathcal{J}^{*}\beta_{i}=t\beta_{i}=0
, so the second term disappears. This normal condition on
\beta_{i}

\displaystyle 0=\mathcal{J}^{*}\beta_{i,t}\implies 0=\mathcal{J}^{*}(d\epsilon_{i}+\mathcal{L}_{V}(\beta_{i}))=d\mathcal{J}^{*}(\epsilon_{i}+i_{V}(\beta_{i}))\,.

Additionally, for the first term we have that both terms in the wedge product are closed in
\Omega^{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
. Applying equation (
11
), gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big\|\beta_{i,t}\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!(i_{V}\beta_{i}+\epsilon_{i})\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!(2\star\beta_{i}-\mu_{i}a_{i})-\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\!(2\star\beta_{i}-\mu a_{i})\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\!(i_{V}\beta_{i}+\epsilon_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}(i_{V}(\beta_{i})\wedge\star\beta_{i})+\mu_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}a_{i}^{\prime}\,.

Using the divergence theorem

\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}(i_{V}\beta_{i}+\epsilon_{i})=\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}^{\prime}=\int_{T_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}\beta_{i}^{\prime}=\partial_{t}\psi_{i,j}|_{t=0}=0\,.

So this term vanishes as flux is held constant. And

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}(i_{V}\beta_{i}+\epsilon_{i})\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}(2\star\beta_{i}-\mu_{i}a_{i})=\mu_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{i}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}a_{i}^{\prime}\,.

So we are left with:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big\|\beta_{i,t}\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big|_{t=0}

7
Conclusion

In this work, we developed a variational framework for solutions to the multi-region relaxed magnetohydrodynamics (MRxMHD) equations on compact, oriented domains embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with smooth boundary. We introduced a definition of relative helicity and proved its gauge invariance. We demonstrated the existence of an Amperian gauge, noting that the relative helicity aligns with conventional helicity in this gauge. Within each subdomain, we imposed physically motivated conditions, including divergence-free fields, boundary tangency, and prescribed relative helicity, flux, and pressure constraints.

We showed that under these constraints non-zero critical points of the magnetic energy correspond precisely to solutions of the MRxMHD equilibrium equations. We identified a gauge condition that had not previously been noted in the literature. In the presence of a pressure jump, we established the equivalence between the MRxMHD formulation and a stronger formulation by Bruno and Laurence.

Our results extend the MRxMHD model beyond previously considered toroidal geometries.

Proof of Lemma

Recall Lemma

The homologies

and
H_{k}(\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c})
are finite-dimensional vector spaces for all

.

These homologies are vector spaces over the reals by construction, so we prove the finite dimensionality. We follow a discussion given by Bott and Tu
[
3
]
.

Proof.

A finite open cover
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\dots,U_{N}\}
of
\Lambda_{i}
is called a
good cover
if every non-empty finite intersection of the sets
U_{j}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We now show that every Riemannian
3
-manifold admits a finite good cover.

Fix a point
x\in\operatorname{int}(\Lambda_{i})
. By the existence and uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations, for each
v\in T_{x}\Lambda_{i}
there exists
\varepsilon>0
and a unique geodesic

\gamma_{v}:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\Lambda_{i}

such that
\gamma_{v}(0)=x
and
\gamma_{v}^{\prime}(0)=v
. The exponential map at
x
is therefore defined on a neighbourhood
U
of the origin in
T_{x}\Lambda_{i}
such that for every
v\in U
, the geodesic
\gamma_{v}
is also defined on
[0,1]
. We then define the exponential map by

\exp_{x}:U\subseteq T_{x}\Lambda_{i}\longrightarrow\Lambda_{i}\,,\qquad\exp_{x}(v)\coloneqq\gamma_{v}(1)\,.

By the homogeneity of geodesics,
\exp_{x}(sv)=\gamma_{v}(s)
for
s\in[0,1]
. Differentiating at
s=0
gives

d\exp_{x}(v)=\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\exp_{x}(sv)=\gamma_{v}^{\prime}(0)=v\,,

and hence
d(\exp_{x})
is the identity on
U\subseteq T_{x}\Lambda_{i}
. The inverse function theorem implies that there exists a ball
\mathcal{B}(r)\subset T_{x}\Lambda_{i}
with radius
r>0
about
0
such that

\exp_{x}:U=\mathcal{B}(r)\longrightarrow V_{x}\coloneqq\exp_{x}\bigl(\mathcal{B}(r)\bigr)

is a diffeomorphism onto an open neighbourhood
V_{x}
of
x
in
\Lambda_{i}
.

By choosing
r
sufficiently small
V_{x}
is geodesically convex
[
34
]
. That is, for any two points
y,z\in V_{x}
, there exists a unique geodesic segment joining
y
to
z
, and this segment lies entirely in
V_{x}
.

A collection
\{V_{x}:x\in\Lambda_{i}\}
is therefore an open cover of
\Lambda_{i}
. Since
\Lambda_{i}
is compact, this cover admits a finite subcover, namely, there exist points
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\Lambda_{i}
such that

\Lambda_{i}=V_{x_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup V_{x_{N}}\,.

Moreover, each
V_{x_{j}}
is geodesically convex.

Consider the geodesically convex neighbourhoods
V_{x_{1}},V_{x_{2}}
that are small enough so that the geodesic between any two points within the neighbourhoods is unique, as in the above discussion. Then in
V_{x_{1}}\cap V_{x_{2}}
, the geodesic is unique in both sets separately, and contained in both sets separately, so
V_{x_{1}}\cap V_{x_{2}}
is geodesically convex.

As we stated, the exponential map is a diffeomorphism on the neighbourhoods of any interior point. It therefore provides a diffeomorphism to the tangent space
T_{x}\Lambda_{i}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}
for all
x\in\text{int}(\Lambda_{i})
. Therefore any compact Riemannian 3-manifold has a finite good cover.

Recall the Poincaré Lemma:
H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cong 0
for
k>0
, and
H^{0}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cong\mathbb{R}
. We prove by induction on the number of geodesically convex sets in a union that the de Rham cohomology of such a union is finite dimensional. Applying this to a finite cover of
\Lambda_{i}
will gives the desired statement.

For a single geodesically convex neighbourhood
V_{x_{1}}
the Poincaré Lemma (and the fact that
V_{x_{1}}
is diffeomorphic to an open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
) implies
H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V_{x_{1}})
is finite dimensional.

Now suppose, for the sake of induction, that
H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V)
is finite dimensional where

V=V_{x_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup V_{x_{j}}\,.

The Mayer–Vietoris long exact sequence for de Rham cohomology gives a segment

\cdots\longrightarrow H^{k-1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cap V_{x_{j+1}})\xrightarrow{\;d^{*}\;}H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cup V_{x_{j+1}})\xrightarrow{\;\mathcal{J}_{*}\;}H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V)\oplus H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V_{x_{j+1}})\longrightarrow\cdots\,.

Exactness at
H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cup V_{x_{j+1}})
yields

\ker(\mathcal{J}_{*})=\operatorname{Im}(d^{*})\,,

hence, a short exact sequence of vector spaces is

0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Im}(d^{*})\longrightarrow H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cup V_{x_{j+1}})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Im}(\mathcal{J}_{*})\longrightarrow 0\,.

Consequently

\dim H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cup V_{x_{j+1}})=\dim\operatorname{Im}(d^{*})+\dim\operatorname{Im}(\mathcal{J}_{*})\,.

But
\operatorname{Im}(d^{*})
is a quotient (hence a homomorphic image) of
H^{k-1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cap V_{x_{j+1}})
, so it is finite dimensional whenever
H^{k-1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cap V_{x_{j+1}})
is finite dimensional. Also
\operatorname{Im}(\mathcal{J}_{*})
is a subspace of the finite-dimensional space
H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V)\oplus H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V_{x_{j+1}})
, so it is finite dimensional. Therefore, if

H^{k-1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cap V_{x_{j+1}})\,,\qquad H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V)\,,\qquad H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V_{x_{j+1}})\,,

are finite dimensional, then
H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cup V_{x_{j+1}})
is finite dimensional. We still need to verify that
H^{k-1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cap V_{x_{j+1}})
is finite. Note that

V\cap V_{x_{j+1}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{j}\bigl(V_{x_{i}}\cap V_{x_{j+1}}\bigr)\,.

Each intersection
V_{x_{i}}\cap V_{x_{j+1}}
is geodesically convex and any non-empty finite intersection of these is again a good cover of
V\cap V_{x_{j+1}}
. Hence,
H^{k-1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cap V_{x_{j+1}})
is finite dimensional. Therefore,
\operatorname{Im}(d^{*})
is finite dimensional and the argument above shows
H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V\cup V_{x_{j+1}})
is finite dimensional.

By induction, we conclude that a finite union

\Lambda_{i}=V_{x_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup V_{x_{N}}

produces a finite dimensional vector space
H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i})
.

Using the de Rham theorem we see that
H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda)

is finite dimensional. The same argument applies to

.

First we provide and prove two well known results.

Lemma B.1

(Proposition C.4
[
7
]
)
.

Any closed, normal
2
-form on
\Lambda_{i}
is exact.

Proof.

Let
b_{i}\in\Omega^{2}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
be a closed, normal differential
2
-form. Applying a Hodge-Morrey-Friedrichs (HMF) decomposition to the 1-form
\star b
, then applying a
\star

\displaystyle b=d\eta_{1}+\delta\eta_{2}+d\eta_{3}+\eta_{4}^{*}\,,

where
\eta_{1}\in\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
,
\eta_{2}\in\Omega^{3}_{N}(\Lambda_{i})
,
d\eta_{3}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Lambda_{i})
, where
\eta_{3}\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
, and
\eta_{4}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{N}^{2}(\Lambda_{i})
. Following a similar approach as in section
2.3
, we apply the closed, and normal conditions:

\displaystyle 0=db=d\delta\eta_{2}\,,\qquad 0=tb=t(\delta\eta_{2}+d\eta_{3}+\eta_{4}^{*})\,.

Taking note of the first equality, and using the divergence theorem:

\displaystyle\|\delta\eta_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\delta\eta_{2}\wedge d\star\eta_{2}=-\int_{\Lambda_{i}}d\delta\eta_{2}\;\star\eta_{2}+\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\mathcal{J}^{*}(\delta\eta_{2}\;\star\eta_{2})=0\,.

And by continuity we have that
\delta\eta_{2}=0
.

The normal condition is now
0=t(d\eta_{3}+\eta_{4}^{*})
. Using de Rham’s theorem,
\eta_{4}^{*}
is a unique harmonic representative for
[\eta_{4}^{*}]\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i})
. This has a unique dual element
[\eta_{4}]\in H_{2}(\Lambda_{i})
. Namely, we take an algebraic dual relative to a given basis (of dimension
\ell_{i}
by the Hodge isomorphism). Computing

\displaystyle\langle[\eta_{4}^{*}],[\eta_{4}]\rangle=\int_{\eta_{4}}\eta_{4}^{*}=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{*}(\eta_{4})}\mathcal{J}^{*}(\eta_{4}^{*})=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{*}(\eta_{4})}\mathcal{J}^{*}(d\eta_{3}+\eta_{4}^{*})=0\,.

Here
\mathcal{J}
is the usual boundary inclusion map. The only way to achieve this is when
[\eta_{4}^{*}]=0
. Noting that
0=[\star\eta_{4}^{*}]\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})
has a unique harmonic representative, we have that
\eta_{4}^{*}=0
. Hence,
b=d\eta_{3}
is exact.
∎

B.1
General Formulation

Given any choice of bases for
H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})
and
H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i})
one can, of course, define a basis for
H_{k}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
(Lemma
2.3
). Theorem
2.4
asserts that we already have a basis for
H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})
given by
\{[s_{i,1}],\dots,[s_{i,{\ell_{i}}}]\}
, all that remains is to construct a suitable basis for
H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})
that satisfies the requirements of an Alexander basis. We proceed in two steps: First, by constructing
\{[t_{i,1}],\dots,[t_{i,\ell_{i}}]\}
(a basis for
H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i})
). Secondly, by verifying this basis satisfies the conditions in Theorem
2.4
.

Step 1. Construct a Basis for
H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
via
\{[s_{i,1}],\dots,[s_{i,{\ell_{i}}}]\}
.

As a basis for
H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})
is given. We construct basis elements
T_{i,j},\sigma_{i,j}
and the duals
[s_{i,j}^{*}]
,
[T_{i,j}^{*}]
, and
[\sigma_{i,j}^{*}]
as in the main body. We will see an explicit example to compute these in the next subsection.

We want to see how
H_{2}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})
relates to
H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})
. Let
(\Lambda_{i})_{\epsilon}
be all points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
within
\epsilon>0
of
\Lambda_{i}
and
\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c}_{\epsilon}
is the closure of the complement of
(\Lambda_{i})_{\epsilon}
. We take
\epsilon
small enough that these spaces are compact and oriented in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with smooth boundary. Note that
\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c}_{\epsilon}\subset\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, so by the excision theorem, the inclusion
\mathcal{J}^{0}:(\mathbb{R}^{3}-\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c}_{\epsilon},\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c}-\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c}_{\epsilon})\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{R}^{3},\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})
induces an isomorphism
H_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3}-\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c}_{\epsilon},\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c}-\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c}_{\epsilon})\cong H_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})
(see page 119, theorem 2.20
[
21
]
). Taking
\epsilon\to 0
(deformation retraction) we have that
H_{k}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})\cong H_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})
.

Taking a relative Mayer-Vietoris sequence for
k>0
:

\displaystyle 0\to H_{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})\xrightarrow{\partial}H_{k}(\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})\to 0\,.

Giving isomorphisms
H_{k+1}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})\cong H_{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})\cong H_{k}(\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})
given by
\mathcal{J}^{0}_{*}
and
\partial
. Note that inverting the domains produces the result
H_{k+1}(\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c},\partial\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})\cong H_{k}(\Lambda_{i})
which we will utilise later. We define (through these isomorphisms) a basis for
H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})
with elements given by
[t_{i}]\coloneqq\partial\mathcal{J}^{0}_{*}[T_{i,j}]
.

From the
[t_{i,j}]
we define
[t_{i,j}^{*}],[S_{i,j}],[S_{i,j}^{*}],[\tau_{i,j}],[\tau_{i,j}^{*}]
as in the main body. We also see that the basis for
H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda_{i}}^{c})
has dimension
\ell_{i}
.

Step 2. Verifying that the basis constructed in step 1 is an Alexander basis for
H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
.

Theorem
2.4
claims that for all
j,k\in\{1,\dots,{\ell_{i}}\}
:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\sigma_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=0\,,\qquad\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tau_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\tau_{i,k}^{*}=0\,,\qquad\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tau_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=\delta_{jk}\,.\end{gathered}

The first 2 integrals we can compute directly, namely, via the divergence theorem we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\sigma_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=\int_{\Lambda_{i}}d(s_{i,j}^{*}\wedge s_{i,k}^{*})=\int_{\Lambda_{i}}0=0\,.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tau_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\tau_{i,k}^{*}=-\int_{\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i}}d(t_{i,j}^{*}\wedge t_{i,k}^{*})=-\int_{\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i}}0=0\,.

For the last integral, we note that
[T_{i,j}^{*}]\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})
. By the de Rham theorem, we have a unique harmonic representative
T_{i,j}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
. Importantly,
T_{i,j}^{*}
is a closed, normal 2-form on
\Lambda_{i}
, so by Lemma
B.1
,
T_{i,j}^{*}=d\Gamma_{i,j}
for some
\Gamma_{i,j}\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda)
. We know that the collection of
[\sigma_{i,j}^{*}],[\tau_{i,j}^{*}]
form a basis for
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i})
(Lemma
2.3
), so given
[\mathcal{J}^{*}\Gamma_{i,j}]\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{J}^{*}\Gamma_{i,j}]=\sum_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}\Big(k_{j,r}[\tau_{i,r}^{*}]+c_{j,r}[\sigma_{i,r}^{*}]\Big)\,.

By definition of
[T_{i,j}^{*}]
,

\displaystyle\delta_{jk}=\int_{T_{i,j}}T_{i,k}^{*}=\int_{T_{i,j}}d\Gamma_{i,k}=\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\mathcal{J}^{*}\Gamma_{i,k}=\sum_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}\left(k_{k,r}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\tau_{i,r}^{*}+c_{k,r}\int_{\tau_{i,j}}\sigma_{i,r}^{*}\right)=k_{k,j}\,.

Recall that section
2
shows
\tau_{i,j}^{*}
,
\sigma_{i,j}^{*}
are the duals of
\tau_{i,j}
,
\sigma_{i,j}
respectively. Substituting this back into the non-degenerate Lefschetz pairing

\displaystyle\delta_{jk}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\sigma_{i,j}^{*}\wedge d\Gamma_{i,k}=-\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\sigma_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\mathcal{J}^{*}\Gamma_{i,k}=-\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\sigma_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\Big(\tau_{i,k}^{*}+\sum_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}c_{k,r}\sigma_{i,r}^{*}\Big)=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tau_{i,k}^{*}\wedge\sigma_{i,j}^{*}\,.

This completes the proof for the integral relations. But it remains to be shown that
\partial[T_{i,j}]=[\tau_{i,j}]
and
\partial[S_{i,j}]=[\sigma_{i,j}]
.

Consider inclusion maps
\mathcal{J}^{0}:(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})\xhookrightarrow{}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c})
,
\mathcal{J}:\partial\Lambda_{i}\xhookrightarrow{}\Lambda_{i}
and
\mathcal{J}^{c}:\partial\Lambda_{i}\xhookrightarrow{}\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c}
. Recalling that
(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*}):H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})\to H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})\oplus H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i})
is an isomorphism (Lemma
2.2
), for any
[y]\in H_{2}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})
:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})\partial[y]=(\mathcal{J}_{*}\partial[y],\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*}\partial[y])=(\mathcal{J}_{*}\partial[y],\partial\mathcal{J}^{0}_{*}[y])\,.

Here the boundary acts as
\partial:H_{2}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})\to H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c})
except for the last boundary operator, acting on spaces
\partial:H_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c})\to H_{1}(\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c})
. Recall a portion of the long exact sequence of the pair
(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})
:

\cdots\longrightarrow H_{2}(\Lambda_{i})\longrightarrow H_{2}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})\xrightarrow{\;\partial\;}H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})\xrightarrow{\;\mathcal{J}_{*}\;}H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})\longrightarrow\cdots.

Exactness implies
\operatorname{im}(\partial)=\ker(\mathcal{J}_{*})
. Hence
\mathcal{J}_{*}(\partial[y])=0
,
showing that
(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})\partial[y]=(0,\partial\mathcal{J}^{0}_{*}[y])
. As
(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})
is an isomorphism, it has an inverse, so, setting
[y]=[T_{i,j}]
for any
i,j
,

\displaystyle\partial[T_{i,j}]=(\mathcal{J}_{*},\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*})^{-1}(0,[t_{i,j}])=[\tau_{i,j}]\,,

as required.

Now for the last claim, that
[\sigma_{i,j}]=\partial[S_{i,j}]
. We just showed
\mathcal{J}_{*}(\partial[y])=0
for all
[y]\in H_{2}(\Lambda_{i},\partial\Lambda_{i})
. By exactly the same argument for all
[z]\in H_{2}(\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c},\partial\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c})
we have
\mathcal{J}^{c}_{*}(\partial[z])=0
. Therefore

\partial[S_{i,j}]=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell_{i}}c_{i,j,k}[\sigma_{i,k}]\,.

Select some
\eta\in\Omega^{1}(\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i})
such that
\eta|_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}=\sigma_{i,k}^{*}
. On the boundary we see that
d\eta|_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}=d\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=0
, so
[d\eta]\in H_{2}(\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i},\partial\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i})
. Therefore we have, using Lefschetz duality and the divergence theorem

\displaystyle\int_{\partial S_{i,j}}\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=\int_{S_{i,j}}d\eta=\int_{\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i}}t_{i,j}^{*}\wedge d\eta=-\int_{\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i}}d(t_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\eta)=\int_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}\tau_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=\delta_{jk}\,.

By selecting an outward orientation for
\partial\Lambda_{i}
the divergence theorem provides a negative sign when moving to the boundary of
\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i}
. This gives the relation when placing in basis elements

\displaystyle\delta_{jk}=\int_{\partial S_{i,j}}\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=\int_{\sum_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}c_{i,j,r}\sigma_{i,r}}\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=\sum_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}c_{i,j,r}\int_{\sigma_{i,r}}\sigma_{i,k}^{*}=c_{i,j,k}\,.

Hence
\partial[S_{i,j}]=[\sigma_{i,j}]
. This completes the proof of Theorem
2.4
. ∎

Let
f:\widehat{\Lambda}_{i}\to\Lambda_{i}
be an orientation-preserving homeomorphism. Since
f
is a homeomorphism the induced maps

(f^{-1})^{*}:H_{k}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{i})\to H_{k}(\Lambda_{i}),\qquad f^{*}:H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Lambda_{i})\to H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{i})

are isomorphisms (with inverses
(f^{-1})_{*}
and
(f^{-1})^{*}
respectively). Let
f_{\partial}:=f\big|_{\partial\widehat{\Lambda}_{i}}:\partial\widehat{\Lambda}_{i}\to\partial\Lambda_{i}
be the corresponding map on the boundaries.

Let
\mathcal{J}:\partial\Lambda_{i}\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{i}
denote the boundary inclusion and write
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}:\partial\widehat{\Lambda}_{i}\hookrightarrow\widehat{\Lambda}_{i}
for the analogous inclusion on the hatted side. Recall that an Alexander basis

\{[\sigma_{i,1}],\dots,[\sigma_{i,\ell_{i}}],[\tau_{i,1}],\dots,[\tau_{i,\ell_{i}}]\}\subset H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})
has the property that the inclusion-induced map
\mathcal{J}_{*}:H_{1}(\partial\Lambda_{i})\to H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})
sends
\,[\sigma_{i,j}]\mapsto[s_{i,j}]
and
\mathcal{J}^{c}:\partial\Lambda_{i}\hookrightarrow\overline{\Lambda}^{c}_{i}
induces a map
\,[\tau_{i,j}]\mapsto[t_{i,j}]
.

Assume
\{[\widehat{s}_{i,1}],\dots,[\widehat{s}_{i,\ell_{i}}]\}
is a basis of
H_{1}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{i})
, where
(f^{-1})^{*}[\widehat{s}_{i,j}]=s_{i,j}
is the corresponding basis element of
H_{1}(\Lambda_{i})
. Defining
[\widehat{\sigma}_{i,j}]
as
[\widehat{s}_{i,j}]
pushed forward by inclusion to the boundary we have the relation
[\widehat{\sigma}_{i,j}]=f_{\partial}^{*}[\sigma_{i,j}]
on the boundary. Therefore, one may construct an Alexander basis on
\partial\widehat{\Lambda}_{i}
using the
\widehat{s}_{i,j}
. Denote the resulting basis
\{[\widehat{\sigma}_{i,1}],\dots,[\widehat{\sigma}_{i,\ell_{i}}],[\widehat{\tau}_{i,1}],\dots,[\widehat{\tau}_{i,\ell_{i}}]\}
. Similarly, define
\widehat{S}_{i,j},\widehat{T}_{i,j}
and the corresponding duals in the same way as their non-hatted counterparts. So

\displaystyle\int_{T_{i,j}}(f^{-1})^{*}(\widehat{T}_{i,k}^{*})=\int_{\Lambda}s_{i,j}^{*}\wedge(f^{-1})^{*}(\widehat{T}_{i,k}^{*})=\int_{\Lambda}(f^{-1})^{*}(\widehat{s}_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\widehat{T}_{i,k}^{*})=\int_{\widehat{\Lambda}_{i}}\widehat{s}_{i,j}^{*}\wedge\widehat{T}_{i,k}^{*}=\delta_{jk}=\int_{T_{i,j}}T_{i,k}^{*}\,.

This implies that
(f^{-1})^{*}([\widehat{T}_{i,j}^{*}])=[T_{i,j}^{*}]
, so
[\widehat{T}_{i,j}^{*}]=f^{*}[T_{i,j}^{*}]
, and on the boundary this induces
[\widehat{t}_{i}^{*}]=f_{\partial}^{*}[t_{i}^{*}]
. Hence, in the current context, the Alexander basis is also pushed forward by
f
. Namely,
\{f_{\partial}^{*}[\sigma_{i,1}],\dots,f_{\partial}^{*}[\sigma_{i,\ell_{i}}],f_{\partial}^{*}[\tau_{i,1}],\dots,f_{\partial}^{*}[\tau_{i,\ell_{i}}]\}
is an Alexander basis for
H_{1}(\partial\widehat{\Lambda})
. This is a helpful when considering an MRxMHD problem where interfaces are pushed forward by
f\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
because, as long as pushing forward gives a basis for
H_{1}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{i})
, the corresponding Alexander basis and related definitions are also pushed forward.

We also see that, only requiring a map
f^{-1}
to define a new basis allows us to introduce knots in the domain, we see this in the following example. As
[S_{i,j}]\in H_{2}(\overline{\Lambda}_{i}^{c},\partial\Lambda_{i})
, we do not know the effect of
f^{-1}
, but we are aware that some
[S_{i,j}]
exists on the new domain.

B.2
Hollow Torus Example

We apply our construction of an Alexander basis to a hollow torus
\mathrm{HT}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Rather than contract
\mathrm{HT}
to a torus, we work with an explicit orientation preserving homeomorphism

\iota:[1,2]\times\mathbb{T}^{2}=U\hookrightarrow\mathrm{HT}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,

where
\mathbb{T}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
and the coordinate system
(r,\vartheta_{P},\vartheta_{T})
with
r\in[1,2]
and
\vartheta_{P},\vartheta_{T}\in[0,1)
. The subscript
i
is retained for consistency with the rest of the paper. On the parameter space define singular
1
-cycles

s_{i,1}(\omega)=(1,\omega,0),\qquad s_{i,2}(\omega)=(2,0,\omega),\qquad\omega\in\Delta_{1}=[0,1]\,.

Clearly
\partial s_{i,j}=0
, so
[s_{i,1}]
and
[s_{i,2}]
represent classes in
H_{1}(U)
. Since this homology is two-dimensional (the boundary has total genus
2
), it suffices to verify linear independence. This follows either from the Künneth theorem or from the geometric observation that no singular
2
-chain relates
s_{i,1}
to
s_{i,2}
, hence
\{[s_{i,1}],[s_{i,2}]\}
is a basis of
H_{1}(U)
.

Note that
s_{i,1}
and
s_{i,2}
are trivially included in the boundary, giving generators for
[\sigma_{i,1}]
and
[\sigma_{i,2}]
.

From the previous subsection, so long as
\iota_{*}
pushes elements in
\{[s_{i,1}],[s_{i,2}]\}
forward to a basis for
H_{1}(\mathrm{HT})
, the following construction of an Alexander basis on the parameter space is applicable to
H_{1}(\partial\mathrm{HT})
. In the case of an unknotted torus the choice of
s_{i,j}
align with those chosen in the construction of a relative helicity given by Pfefferlé, Noakes, and Perrella
[
40
]
. As
\iota
is an orientation-preserving homeomorphism, its image
\mathrm{HT}
may be a knotted or unknotted torus, a knotted example is Figure
3
. In some knotted cases constructing
s_{i,j}
via boundaries of Seifert surfaces may provide geometric intuition (see
[
44
]
).

Our computation of
[T_{i,j}]
relies on cohomology elements
[s_{i,j}^{*}]
. We may compute a representative closed 1-form
s_{i,j}^{*}\in\Omega^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
through the relation

\displaystyle\delta_{jk}=\int_{s_{i,j}}s_{i,k}^{*}\,.

Selecting
s_{i,1}^{*}=d\vartheta_{P}
and
s_{i,2}^{*}=d\vartheta_{T}
we verify that

\displaystyle\int_{s_{i,1}}s_{i,1}^{*}

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega\mapsto(1,\omega,0)}d\vartheta_{P}=\int_{0}^{1}d\omega=1\,,\qquad\int_{s_{i,1}}s_{i,2}^{*}=\int_{\omega\mapsto(1,\omega,0)}d\vartheta_{T}=0\,,

\displaystyle\int_{s_{i,2}}s_{i,2}^{*}

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega\mapsto(2,0,\omega)}d\vartheta_{T}=1\,,\qquad\hskip 40.00006pt\int_{s_{i,2}}s_{i,1}^{*}=\int_{\omega\mapsto(2,0,\omega)}d\vartheta_{P}=0\,.

There is some freedom, up to a choice of volume in the parameter space, which we set by selecting a volume form
\varpi=dr\wedge d\vartheta_{P}\wedge d\vartheta_{T}
. Then the corresponding
T_{i,j}^{*}
are defined by

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\delta_{jk}=\int_{U}s_{i,j}^{*}\wedge T_{i,k}^{*}\,.\end{gathered}

As
T_{i,j}^{*}
is a closed, normal 2-form we select

\displaystyle T_{i,j}^{*}=f_{1}(r,\vartheta_{P})\,dr\wedge d\vartheta_{P}+f_{2}(r,\vartheta_{T})\,dr\wedge d\vartheta_{T}+f_{3}(\vartheta_{P},\vartheta_{T})\,d\vartheta_{P}\wedge d\vartheta_{T}\,.

Let’s first consider
{T_{i,1}}^{*}
:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{U}(d\vartheta_{T})\wedge f_{1}(r,\vartheta_{P})dr\wedge d\vartheta_{P}=\int_{U}f_{1}(r,\vartheta_{P})dr\wedge d\vartheta_{P}\wedge d\vartheta_{T}\,,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int_{U}(d\vartheta_{P})\wedge f_{2}(r,\vartheta_{T})dr\wedge d\vartheta_{T}=-\int_{U}f_{2}(r,\vartheta_{T})dr\wedge d\vartheta_{P}\wedge d\vartheta_{T}\,.

Selecting
f_{2}(r,\vartheta_{T})=-1
,
f_{1}(r,\vartheta_{P})=0
and
f_{3}(\vartheta_{P},\vartheta_{T})=0
gives us a closed 2-form
T_{i,1}^{*}=-dr\wedge d\vartheta_{T}
that satisfies the above conditions. And pulling back to the boundary,
\mathcal{J}^{*}T_{i,j}^{*}=\mathcal{J}^{*}(-dr\wedge d\vartheta_{T})=0
as
\mathcal{J}^{*}(dr)=0
.

Performing the same for
T_{i,2}^{*}
,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int_{U}(d\vartheta_{T})\wedge f_{1}(r,\vartheta_{P})dr\wedge d\vartheta_{P}=\int_{U}f_{1}(r,\vartheta_{P})dr\wedge d\vartheta_{P}\wedge d\vartheta_{T}\,,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{U}(d\vartheta_{P})\wedge f_{2}(r,\vartheta_{T})dr\wedge d\vartheta_{T}=-\int_{U}f_{2}(r,\vartheta_{T})dr\wedge d\vartheta_{P}\wedge d\vartheta_{T}\,.

Selecting
f_{1}(r,\vartheta_{P})=1
,
f_{2}(r,\vartheta_{T})=0
and
f_{3}(\vartheta_{P},\vartheta_{T})=0
gives us a closed 2-form
T_{i,2}^{*}=dr\wedge d\vartheta_{P}
. Additionally, the boundary condition
\mathcal{J}^{*}T_{i,2}^{*}=0
is satisfied.

We note that this choice for
T_{i,1}^{*},T_{i,2}^{*}
agrees with Pfefferlé
et al.

[
40
]
up to a sign. Clearly
T_{i,j}^{*}
are closed and 0 when pulled back to the boundary, and therefore generators for elements of
H^{2}_{\mathrm{dR}}(U,\partial U)
.

For the singular relative 2-chains
T_{i,j}
we require that,

\displaystyle\delta_{jk}=\int_{T_{i,j}}T_{i,k}^{*}\,.

For
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\Delta_{2}
, let’s try the 2-chain:

\displaystyle T_{i,1}(w_{1},w_{2})=(1+w_{1},0,-w_{2})+(2-w_{1},0,-1+w_{2})\,.

The image of
T_{i,j}
is a region in an unknotted hollow torus
\mathrm{HT}
is given by a toroidal ribbon.

\displaystyle\int_{T_{i,1}}T_{i,1}^{*}

\displaystyle=\int_{(1+w_{1},0,-w_{2})+(2-w_{1},0,-(1-w_{2}))}-dr\wedge d\vartheta_{T}=2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1-w_{2}}dw_{1}\wedge dw_{2}=1\,.

\displaystyle\int_{T_{i,1}}T_{i,2}^{*}

\displaystyle=\int_{(1+w_{1},0,-w_{2})+(2-w_{1},0,-(1-w_{2}))}dr\wedge d\vartheta_{P}=0\,.

Note that the
T_{i,1}\in H_{2}(U,\partial U)
only if
\partial T_{i,1}\in C_{1}(\partial U)
, which we will verify.

Similarly, we may verify the following choice for
T_{i,2}
:

\displaystyle T_{i,2}(w_{1},w_{2})=(1+w_{1},w_{2},0)+(2-w_{1},(1-w_{2}),0)\,.

To complete our construction of an Alexander basis, we take the boundary of
[T_{i,j}]
:

\displaystyle\partial T_{i,j}:w\mapsto T_{i,j}(1-w,w)-T_{i,j}(0,w)+T_{i,j}(w,0)\,,

where
w\in[0,1]=\Delta_{1}
. Starting with the representative
T_{i,1}
:

\displaystyle\partial T_{i,1}:w

\displaystyle\mapsto T_{i,1}(1-w,w)-T_{i,1}(0,w)+T_{i,1}(w,0)

\displaystyle=(2-w,0,-w)+(1+w,0,-(1-w))

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-((1,0,-w)+(2,0,-(1-w)))

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+(1+w,0,0)+(2-w,0,-1)\,.

Noting that
\vartheta_{T}
is periodic, the surviving terms are:

\displaystyle\tau_{i,1}=\partial T_{i,1}:w

\displaystyle\mapsto-((1,0,-w)+(2,0,-(1-w)))=(1,0,w)+(2,0,-w)\,.

Similarly for
T_{i,2}
:

\displaystyle\partial T_{i,2}:w

\displaystyle\mapsto T_{i,2}(1-w,w)-T_{i,2}(0,w)+T_{i,2}(w,0)

\displaystyle=(2-w,w,0)+(1+w,(1-w),0)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-((1,w,0)+(2,(1-w),0))

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+(1+w,0,0)+(2-w,,0)\,.

Once again the formal sum of the first 2 and last 2 pairs is zero, and we get

\displaystyle\tau_{i,2}=\partial T_{i,2}:w

\displaystyle\mapsto(1,-w,0)+(2,w,0)\,.

Together the classes for
\sigma_{i,j}
and
\tau_{i,j}
form an Alexander basis for the parameter space of a hollow torus. We observe that our definition of relative helicity in subsection

is given in this context by Pfefferlé
et al.

B.3
Hodge Decomposition

Throughout the paper we utilise both a Hodge decomposition, and a Hodge-Morrey-Friedrichs decomposition. Here we provide these decompositions without proof. A Hodge-Morrey decomposition for the space
\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})
for
k\geq 1
is given by:

\displaystyle\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})=

\displaystyle\{d\omega\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})\;:\,t\omega=0\}\oplus\{\delta\omega\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})\;:\,n\omega=0\}\oplus\{\omega\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})\;:\,d\omega=\delta\omega=0\}\,.

These spaces are
L^{2}
-orthogonal to each other, giving a unique algebraic decomposition for any
\omega\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})
(Lemma 2.4.3
[
43
]
):

\displaystyle\omega=d\phi+\delta(\star\eta)+\gamma\,,

where
\eta\in\Omega_{D}^{3-k-1}(\Lambda_{i})
,
\phi\in\Omega^{0}(\Lambda_{i})
where
\phi|_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}=0
, and
\gamma\in\mathcal{H}^{k}(\Lambda_{i})
. This is further decomposed to give a Hodge-Morrey-Friedrichs decomposition (Corollary 2.4.9
[
43
]
):

\displaystyle\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})=

\displaystyle\{d\omega\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})\;:\,t\omega=0\}

\displaystyle\oplus

\displaystyle\{\delta\omega\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})\;:\,n\omega=0\}

\displaystyle\oplus

\displaystyle\{\omega\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})\;:\,d\omega=\delta\omega=t\omega=0\}

\displaystyle\oplus

\displaystyle\{d\omega\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})\;:\,\delta d\omega=0\}\,.

These spaces are
L^{2}
-orthogonal to each other and give a unique algebraic decomposition for any
\omega\in\Omega^{k}(\Lambda_{i})
:

\displaystyle\omega=d\phi+\delta(\star\eta)+\star\lambda+dz\,,

where
\eta\in\Omega_{D}^{3-k-1}(\Lambda_{i})
,
\phi\in\Omega^{0}(\Lambda_{i})
,
\star\lambda\in\mathcal{H}^{k}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
, and
z\in\Omega^{0}(\Lambda_{i})
for
\phi|_{\partial\Lambda_{i}}=0
, and
z
is a harmonic function
\Delta z=0
where
\Delta
is the Laplacian.

B.4
Fréchet Differentiable

We claim in the main body that certain Fréchet derivatives exist. As these computations are relatively standard, we show this for the relative helicity. The magnetic energy and flux are similar computations.

Consider
\eta_{i},\eta_{i}^{\prime}\in\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
, we look for the Fréchet derivative of
\mathscr{H}
with respect to the first coordinate
\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda_{i})
equipped with the
C^{\infty}
topology, and the codomain is given the absolute value norm. As in the main body, let

\displaystyle a_{i}=\eta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{{\ell_{i}}}\psi_{i,j}\Gamma_{i,j}\,,

and
\beta_{i}=da_{i}
. So,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}(d\eta_{i}+d\eta_{i}^{\prime},\Lambda_{i})=g_{i}(\eta_{i}+\eta_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\Big(\eta_{i}+\eta_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{j=1}^{{\ell_{i}}}\psi_{i,j}\Gamma_{i,j}\Big)\wedge d\Big(\eta_{i}+\eta_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{j=1}^{{\ell_{i}}}\psi_{i,j}\Gamma_{i,j}\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 120.00018pt-\sum_{j=1}^{{\ell_{i}}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\Big(\eta_{i}+\eta_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{j=1}^{{\ell_{i}}}\psi_{i,j}\Gamma_{i,j}\Big)

\displaystyle=g_{i}(\eta_{i})+2\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\beta_{i}\wedge\eta_{i}^{\prime}-\sum_{j=1}^{{\ell_{i}}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\eta_{i}^{\prime}+\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\eta_{i}^{\prime}\wedge d\eta_{i}^{\prime}

We see that

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\eta_{i}^{\prime})\coloneqq 2\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\beta_{i}\wedge\eta_{i}^{\prime}-\sum_{j=1}^{{\ell_{i}}}\psi_{i,j}\int_{\sigma_{i,j}}\eta_{i}^{\prime}

is a linear, continuous operator in
\eta_{i}^{\prime}
. For
\partial_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\eta_{i}^{\prime})
to be a Fréchet derivative requires

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\eta_{i}^{\prime}\wedge d\eta_{i}^{\prime}\Big|/\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{C^{\infty}}\to 0\qquad\text{as}\qquad\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{C^{\infty}}\to 0\,.

And we see that

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\eta_{i}^{\prime}\wedge d\eta_{i}^{\prime}\Big|=\langle\eta_{i}^{\prime},\star d\eta_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\leq\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|\star d\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}=\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|d\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\,.

By definition of the
C^{\infty}
topology we see that
\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}
is controlled by the
C^{0}
norm and the volume of
\Lambda_{i}
, giving
\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{C^{\infty}}
for some
C
. Similarly,
\|d\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|d\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{C^{0}}|\Lambda_{i}|^{1/2}\leq C\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{C^{1}}\leq C\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{C^{\infty}}
for some constant
C
at each step
[
47
]
. Therefore, we see that

\displaystyle 0<\Big|\int_{\Lambda_{i}}\eta_{i}^{\prime}\wedge d\eta_{i}^{\prime}\Big|/\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{C^{\infty}}\leq\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|d\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}/\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{C^{\infty}}\leq C\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{C^{\infty}}\,.

the central terms evidently go to 0 as
\|\eta_{i}^{\prime}\|_{C^{\infty}}\to 0
. Hence,
\partial_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\eta_{i}^{\prime})
defines a Fréchet derivative.

B.5
Complementation of the Kernel

Consider
g_{i}(\eta_{i})
given by the relative helicity. Glöckner’s regular value result
4.6
requires the derivative
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}
to have a
complemented
kernel at
\eta_{i}\in\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})

[
19
]
. We show that this condition is satisfied whenever
g_{i}
has a one-dimensional codomain.

Recall that a closed subspace
K
of a topological vector space
E
is said to be
complemented
if there exists a closed subspace
M\subset E
such that the map

K\times M\to E
given by addition is a topological isomorphism. This is equivalent to saying
E=K\oplus M
.

In our context, we consider the surjective derivative (for
\beta_{i}\neq 0
)

L:=d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}:T_{\eta_{i}}\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})\to\mathbb{R}\,.

To prove that
\ker(L)
is complemented in the tangent space
E=T_{\eta_{i}}\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
, we construct an explicit projection. Since
L
is a surjective linear functional, there exists some vector
\nu\in E
such that
L(\nu)=1
. We define the continuous linear map
P:E\to E
by:

P(\eta)=L(\eta)\nu.

By construction,
P
is a projection onto the one-dimensional subspace
M=\text{span}\{\nu\}
, since:

P(P(\eta))=L(L(\eta)\nu)\nu=L(\eta)L(\nu)\nu=L(\eta)\nu=P(\eta).

Any element
\eta\in E
can be decomposed uniquely as:

\eta=(\eta-P(\eta))+P(\eta).

We observe the following:

1.

The first term
(\eta-P(\eta))
lies in
\ker(L)
, because
L(\eta-L(\eta)\nu)=L(\eta)-L(\eta)L(\nu)=0
.

2.

The second term
P(\eta)
lies in
M=\text{span}\{\nu\}
.

3.

The intersection
\ker(L)\cap M
is trivial, as
L(c\nu)=0
implies
c=0
.

This induces the topological direct sum
E=\ker(L)\oplus M
. Since
M\cong\mathbb{R}
, it is a closed subspace, and so
\ker(d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i})
is complemented in
T_{\eta_{i}}\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
.

B.6
Lagrange Multipliers

The definition of a critical point
\eta_{i}
for an objective function
F:\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})\to\mathbb{R}
, subject to the constraint
g_{i}(\eta_{i})=h_{i}
, is given by the condition:

\displaystyle d_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu)=0\quad\text{for all}\quad\nu\in T_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}^{-1}(h_{i}).

Assuming
\eta_{i}
is a regular value of the constraint
g_{i}
, the tangent space to the constraint manifold is precisely the kernel of the derivative:
T_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}^{-1}(h_{i})=\ker(d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i})
. Thus, the condition for a critical point is that
d_{\eta_{i}}F
vanishes on
\ker(d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i})
.

We wish to show that this is equivalent to the existence of a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that:

\displaystyle d_{\eta_{i}}F+\lambda d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}=0\quad\text{on the whole space }T_{\eta_{i}}\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i}).

As established in Appendix
B.5
, since
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}
is a surjective linear map to a one-dimensional codomain, its kernel is complemented. We can decompose the tangent space
E=T_{\eta_{i}}\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda_{i})
into a direct sum:

E=\ker(d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i})\oplus\text{span}\{\nu_{0}\}

where
\nu_{0}\in E
is a vector chosen such that
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\nu_{0})=1
.

Any arbitrary vector
\nu\in E
can be uniquely decomposed as
\nu=\nu_{k}+c\nu_{0}
, where
\nu_{k}\in\ker(d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i})
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that by applying
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}
to both sides, we find that the scalar
c
is exactly
d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\nu)
.

Now, consider the action of the derivative of the objective function on
\nu
:

\displaystyle d_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu)

\displaystyle=d_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu_{k}+c\nu_{0})

\displaystyle=d_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu_{k})+cd_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu_{0}).

By our definition of a critical point,
d_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu_{k})=0
. Substituting
c=d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\nu)
, the expression simplifies to:

d_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu)=d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\nu)\cdot d_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu_{0}).

If we define the constant
\lambda=-d_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu_{0})
, then for any
\nu\in E
, we have:

d_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu)+\lambda d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}(\nu)=0.

This confirms the existence of a Lagrange multiplier at a critical point. Going the other way, we need to show that
d_{\eta_{i}}F+\lambda d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i}=0
implies
\eta_{i}
is a critical point. This is trivial since
\nu\in\ker(d_{\eta_{i}}g_{i})
implies that
d_{\eta_{i}}F(\nu)=0
.

Appendix C

With

As

We select

and

Since

Let

\displaystyle t(\beta_{\Lambda})=t(da_{\Lambda})=dt(a_{\Lambda})=dw=0\,.

Hence

indicating that

There exists a field

[

]

The helicity for

Immediately we see that, after fixing a selection for

with the desired flux, we may select a

, without affecting the flux. We now have a way to construct any flux and helicity with a closed Dirichlet 2-form
\beta\in\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)
.

If

then there is no flux and one only needs to reconstruct the helicity. The above arguments tell us that
\mathscr{Q}(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{\ell},h,\Lambda)

\ell
.
∎

We now apply the direct method in the calculus of variations to prove the existence of a minimiser of problem
C
, and hence the existence of a stationary point.

By Lemma
C.1
, we may choose

\beta\in\mathscr{Q}(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{\ell},h,\Lambda).

Moreover, there exists a potential for
\beta

\eta

\Gamma

\Gamma

. Without loss of generality, we fix
\Gamma

Orthogonality of the HMF decomposition (see Appendix

\displaystyle\|\beta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|d\eta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|d\Gamma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,.

The last term is determined by a choice of
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{\ell}
. Hence, problem
C
descends to an equivalent problem in
d\eta
, namely, to determine if
\|d\eta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
has any critical points with the constraint
H_{\mathscr{A}(\Lambda)}(\beta,\Lambda)=h

It is convenient to enlarge the admissible space of
2
-forms containing
d\eta
. We therefore formulate the variational problem in a Hilbert space
L^{2}\Omega_{D}^{2}(\Lambda)
, obtained as the completion of
\Omega_{D}^{2}(\Lambda)
with respect to the

For
1

\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda)

\|\omega\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\coloneqq\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|D\omega\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\qquad\omega\in\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda),

More generally, for the definitions of the spaces
H^{k}\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda)
,
H^{k}_{\mathrm{loc}}\Omega_{D}^{1}(\text{int}(\Lambda))
, and
C^{k}\Omega_{D}^{1}(\Lambda)
on a manifold with boundary, together with their associated norms and the tangential trace operator
t
, we refer the reader to
[
43
]
. We now turn to the minimisation problem

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\underset{d\eta\in L^{2}\Omega^{2}_{D}(\Lambda)}{\text{min}}\|d\eta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,,\qquad\hat{H}(\eta)=h\,,\end{gathered}

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}:H^{1}\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda)\to\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{is given by}\qquad\hat{H}(\eta)=\int_{\Lambda}(\eta+\Gamma)\wedge d(\eta+\Gamma)\,,

where the helicity
\hat{H}
is dependent upon
d\eta
irrespective of the primitive
\eta
chosen. This is in precisely the same form as the problem discussed by Gerner who used the direct method in calculus of variations to show that there exists a minimiser
d\eta
(Theorem 2.1
[

For a minimising sequence,
d\eta_{j}

weakly in

\displaystyle|\hat{H}(\eta_{j})-\hat{H}(\eta)|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{\Lambda}\left(\eta_{j}+\Gamma\right)\wedge\beta_{j}-\int_{\Lambda}\left(\eta+\Gamma\right)\wedge\beta\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{\Lambda}\left(\eta_{j}-\eta\right)\wedge\beta_{j}-\int_{\Lambda}\left(\eta+\Gamma\right)\wedge(\beta-\beta_{j})\bigg|\,.

The first term tends to zero because

\beta-\beta_{j}
goes to zero in

\displaystyle\hat{H}(\eta+\eta^{\prime})

We see that

And we see that

for some

The central terms evidently go to 0 as

Let

be the characteristic function for a 3-dimensional ball

L^{2}\Omega^{1}(\Lambda)
we can select a
\eta^{\prime}_{k,q}\to\hat{\eta}^{\prime}_{k}
in an
L^{2}
topology as
q\to\infty
such that
\eta^{\prime}_{k,q}\in H^{1}\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda)
:

\displaystyle d_{\eta}\hat{H}(\eta^{\prime}_{k,q})\xrightarrow{q\to\infty}2\int_{\Lambda}\!\chi_{k}\,\star\beta\wedge\beta\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}2\int_{\chi_{\mathcal{B}}}\!\star\beta\wedge\beta=2\|\beta|_{\mathcal{B}(r_{0})}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,.

So long as
\|\beta\|_{L^{2}}>0
there exists a choice of ball
\mathcal{B}(r_{0})
so that
\|\beta|_{\mathcal{B}(r_{0})}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}>0
, we select such a ball.

So, by an epsilon-delta argument, there are
k_{0},q_{0}
such that for
k>k_{0},q>q_{0}
we have
d_{\eta}\hat{H}(\eta^{\prime}_{k,q})>0
and surjectivity for any
\|\beta\|_{L^{2}}\neq 0
. This means that any
h
is a regular value for the relative helicity constraint
\hat{H}(\eta)=h
(Theorem
4.6
) if
\|\beta\|_{L^{2}}>0
. One usually checks the abnormal case
\|\beta\|_{L^{2}}=0
using higher derivatives
[
46
]
. Assuming that
\|\beta\|_{L^{2}}>0
the regular Lagrange multiplier condition provides necessary and sufficient conditions for a critical point to the original problem (
C
) (see Theorem 43.C (Ljusternik (1934)) in
[
1
]
for constraint qualifications),

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in H^{1}\Omega^{1}_{D}(\Lambda)\\
\mu\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}{\text{c. p.}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Big\|d\eta+\sum_{j}\psi_{j}T_{j}^{*}\Big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\mu}{2}\big(\hat{H}(\eta)-h\big)\right)\,.

Proceeding with the Gâteaux derivative, namely,
\beta_{0}=\beta
,
\partial_{t}\beta_{t}|_{t=0}=\beta^{\prime}
, similarly for
\mu
we have that stationary points of the above are the derivative with respect to
\mu
in the direction
\mu^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu^{\prime}}{2}\big(\hat{H}(\eta)-h\big)\,,

for all
\mu^{\prime}
which implies that the helicity constraint holds. And the derivative with respect to
\eta
in the direction
\eta^{\prime}
is

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}d\eta^{\prime}\wedge\star\beta-\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Lambda}\eta^{\prime}\wedge\beta+a\wedge d\eta^{\prime}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\eta^{\prime}\wedge(d\star\beta-\mu\beta)\,,

for all
\eta^{\prime}
, where we have used the divergence theorem and that
\beta=da
to simplify this condition. The condition implies that

\displaystyle\langle(d\star\beta-\mu\beta),\star\eta^{\prime}\rangle=0\,.

On the interior of
\Lambda
we see that
d\star\beta-\mu\beta
projected onto any element of
\Omega^{2}(\text{int}(\Lambda))
is zero. Hence, in
\text{int}(\Lambda)
we have that
d\star\beta-\mu\beta=0
in the sense of distributions with
\beta\in H^{0}\Omega^{2}(\Lambda)
.

We may apply
(d\star-\mu)
, as a distributional operator, to
d\star\beta-\mu\beta=0
. Namely,

\displaystyle 0=(d\star-\mu)^{2}\beta

\displaystyle=(d\star d\star-2\mu d\star+\mu^{2})\beta=(d\star d\star-\mu^{2})\beta=(\Delta-\mu^{2})\beta\,,

where
\Delta\beta=(d\delta+\delta d)\beta=d\delta\beta=d\star d\star\beta
and
\Delta
is the Hodge Laplacian on 2-forms. A Hodge decomposition gives
\beta=d(\eta+\Gamma)
where
\Gamma
is known and smooth, and
d\Gamma
is harmonic, so

\displaystyle(\Delta-\mu^{2})d\eta

\displaystyle=\mu^{2}d\Gamma\,.

Since the right-hand side of this equation is smooth, interior elliptic regularity theory implies that
d\eta\in H^{k}_{loc}(\text{int}(\Lambda))
for all
k
, namely,
d\eta
is smooth within the domain interior
[
18
]
.

Hence, there exists a minimiser
\eta
to problem (
C
), giving a
\beta=d(\eta+\Gamma)
and if the minimiser satisfies
\|\beta\|_{L^{2}}>0
then
d\eta
and therefore
\beta
must also be smooth within the domain interior. We note that if at least one of the flux values
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{\ell}
is non-zero this implies that (by
L^{2}
orthogonality of the Hodge decomposition)
\|\beta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\|d\Gamma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}>0
.
