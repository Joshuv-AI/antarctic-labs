Undirected Replacement Paths: Dual Fault Reduces to Single Source
Jakob Nogler, Virginia Vassilevska Williams

Abstract
Given a graph and two fixed vertices s s and t t , the Replacement Path Problem ( RP ) is to compute for every edge e e , the distance between s s and t t when e e is removed. There are two natural extensions to RP : • Single Source Replacement Paths ( SSRP ): Given a graph 𝐆 \mathbf{G} and a source node s s , compute for every vertex v v and every edge e e the s s - v v distance in 𝐆 ∖ e \mathbf{G}\setminus e . That is, we do not fix the target anymore. • 2 2 -Fault Replacement Paths (2- FRP ): Given a graph 𝐆 \mathbf{G} and two nodes s s and t t , compute for every pair of edges e , e ′ e,e^{\prime} the s s - t t distance in 𝐆 ∖ e , e ′ \mathbf{G}\setminus e,e^{\prime} . That is, we consider two failures instead of one. Previously, there was no known formal reduction between SSRP and 2- FRP . It seemed plausible that 2- FRP would be computationally harder because there are no settings where 2- FRP admits a faster algorithm than SSRP . In directed unweighted graphs there is a provable gap in complexity, and in undirected graphs many of the known 2- FRP algorithms in a variety of settings are much slower than those for SSRP in the same setting. The main contribution of this paper is a tight reduction from undirected 2 2 - FRP to undirected SSRP , showing that contrary to prior intuition, 2- FRP is not harder than SSRP . As our reduction is weight-preserving, we obtain the first algorithms for 2 2 - FRP that match the best-known runtimes for SSRP : 1. .

Early on, it was conjectured that

Similar to the developments in

Finally, regarding approximation schemes, Chechik and Zhang

SSRP
vs 2-
FRP
.

The preceding literature suggests that 2-
FRP
is inherently more difficult than
SSRP
. There are no settings where the former admits a faster algorithm than the latter; even in the undirected case—specifically for small edge weights and combinatorial
algorithms in unweighted graphs—a clear complexity gap exists.
In unweighted directed graphs, this gap is even provable for combinatorial algorithms:

We consider the following tantalizing question:

Is 2-
FRP
computationally harder than
SSRP
in undirected graphs?

Is it harder than
APSP
in arbitrary integer weighted graphs?

Case

While the case where

The underlying strategy is to characterize the path

as the concatenation of a shortest path

in
\mathbf{G}

To this end, let us introduce some notation.
Let

s

\mathbf{G}

The first (well-known) observation (which also highlights one of the primary reasons our reduction is restricted to undirected graphs) that allows us to simplify ways paths are written is the following:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a graph, and let
s,t\in V
be nodes.
Then, for any edge
e=\{u,v\}\in\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(s,t)
such that
u
is closer to
s
than
v

, which implies
\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus e}(x,t)=\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(x,t)
for any
x\in T_{\mathbf{G}}(s,v)
.

Proof 2.2
.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that there is
x\in T_{\mathbf{G}}(s,v)\cap T_{\mathbf{G}}(t,u)
.
From
x\in T_{\mathbf{G}}(s,v)
, it follows that
e\notin\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(v,x)
.
Moreover, from
x\in T_{\mathbf{G}}(t,u)
follows
v\in\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(t,x)
and
e\in\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(t,x)
,
meaning
\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(t,x)=\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(t,v)\circ\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(v,x)
. But neither
\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(t,v)
nor
\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(v,x)
uses

, a contradiction.

Defining
E_{\mathbf{G}}(A,B)\coloneqq E(\mathbf{G})\cap(A\times B)
for

,
the second observation is as follows:

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a graph, and consider two vertices
s,t\in\mathbf{G}
and a set of edges
F\subseteq E(T_{\mathbf{G}}(s))
such that all

for different

are pairwise disjoint. Let
S\coloneqq\bigcup_{e\in F}V(T_{\mathbf{G}}(s,e))
be the set of vertices below any edge of

Then, provided that
t\in S
, there is

with
\{x,y\}\notin F
such that we can write

\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus F}(s,t)=\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(s,x)\circ\{x,y\}\circ\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus F}(y,t).

Moreover, for such
x,y
we have that
\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus F}(y,t)
only contains vertices in
S
.

Proof 2.4
.

Let
x
be the last node visited by
\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus F}(s,t)
that is contained in
V(\mathbf{G})\setminus S
, and let
y
be the node immediately after.
This means that
\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus F}(s,t)=\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus F}(s,x)\circ\{x,y\}\circ\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus F}(y,t)
, where
\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus F}(y,t)
only contains vertices in
S
.
From
x\in V(\mathbf{G})\setminus S
and from the assumption on
V(\mathbf{G})\setminus S
we get that
\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus F}(s,x)=\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(s,x)
, which concludes the proof.

Set notation.

For integers

, we write

to represent the set
\{i,\dots,j\}
, and
\bm{[}\,i\,\bm{.\,.}\,j\,\bm{)}
to denote the set

.
We define
\bm{(}\,i\,\bm{.\,.}\,j\,\bm{]}
and
\bm{(}\,i\,\bm{.\,.}\,j\,\bm{)}
similarly.

Graph notation.

Throughout this paper, unless explicitly stated otherwise, we consider only undirected graphs (specifically, the only section where we consider directed graphs is
Section

4.1
). For a graph
\mathbf{G}
, we denote its vertex set by
V(\mathbf{G})
and its edge set by
E(\mathbf{G})
. Let
w_{\mathbf{G}}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the weight function of
\mathbf{G}
, which we assume to be positive. For convenience, we define
w_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y)=+\infty
whenever
\{x,y\}\notin E(\mathbf{G})
. To maintain brevity, we may use
\mathbf{G}
to refer to its vertex or edge sets when the meaning is clear from the context (we generally use the variables
e,f
to denote edges and
x,y,z,u,v,a,b
to denote nodes).
We also define
E_{\mathbf{G}}(A,B)=E(\mathbf{G})\cap(A\times B)
for
A,B\subseteq V(\mathbf{G})
.

Given a graph
\mathbf{G}
and a set
F\subseteq E(\mathbf{G})
, we denote with
\mathbf{G}\setminus F
, the graph obtained by removing all edges in
F
from
\mathbf{G}
. Whenever
F=\{e\}
and
F=\{e,e^{\prime}\}
, we write
\mathbf{G}\setminus e
and
\mathbf{G}\setminus e,e^{\prime}
instead of
\mathbf{G}\setminus\{e\}
and
\mathbf{G}\setminus\{e,e^{\prime}\}
.

On canonical shortest paths.

Given a graph
\mathbf{G}
and nodes
x,y\in V(\mathbf{G})
, let
d_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y)
denote the distance between
x
and
y
in
\mathbf{G}
. For every pair
x,y\in V(\mathbf{G})
, we choose and
fix a canonical shortest path
\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y)
. We denote the set of vertices and edges belonging to this path by
V(\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y))
and
E(\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y))
, respectively; as before, we may omit the
V(\cdot)
and
E(\cdot)
notation when the context is clear. Finally, we use
\circ
to denote the concatenation of paths and edges.

When fixing shortest paths, we require that
\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is closed under subpaths; specifically, for any
x,y,z\in V(\mathbf{G})
such that
z\in\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y)
, we have
\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y)=\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(x,z)\circ\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(z,y)
. Furthermore, we require that canonical shortest paths are consistent with edge removals: for any
e\in E(\mathbf{G})
and
x,y\in V(\mathbf{G})
, if
e\notin\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y)
, then
\pi_{\mathbf{G}\setminus e}(x,y)=\pi_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y)
. While these canonical paths are primarily fixed for theoretical purposes, we must ensure they can be computed explicitly for at least two specific vertices, typically denoted
s
and
t
. (This, combined with
Proposition

2.1
will provide runtime guarantees based on the splitting of trees.)
We can achieve this easily in time
\mathcal{O}(m+n\log n)
by adding a small perturbation to all edges and then using Dijkstra’s algorithm from
s
and
t
(after this computation perturbations are dropped).

Shortest path trees.

Let
T_{\mathbf{G}}(s)
denote the shortest path tree of
s
in
\mathbf{G}
, constructed to be consistent with the previously fixed canonical paths. For any node
x\in V(\mathbf{G})
, we let
T_{\mathbf{G}}(s,x)
denote the subtree of
T_{\mathbf{G}}(s)
rooted at
x
.
Lastly, given an edge
e\in T_{\mathbf{G}}(s)
, we let
T_{\mathbf{G}}(s,e)
be the subtree of
T_{\mathbf{G}}(s)
below
e
.

We start by stating the definition of

Definition 4.1

(Short-cut Weights)

Let

.

Proof 6.2
.

Let
(A\cup B\cup C,E)
be an instance of sparse
(\sqrt{n},n,n)
-triangle detection, where
B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
,
C=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\}
and
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}\}
for some
\ell=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
.

We construct the graph
\mathbf{G}
as follows (see
Figure

6
for an illustration). The vertex set of
\mathbf{G}
is partitioned into
A,B,C,A^{\prime},X,Y
, where
A,B,C
are inherited from the input instance. The sets
A^{\prime},X,Y
contain copies of the vertices in
A
; specifically,
A^{\prime}
(resp.
X
and
Y
) contains a copy
a_{i}^{\prime}
(resp.
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
) for each
i\in\bm{[}\,1\,\bm{.\,.}\,\ell\,\bm{]}
.
In
X
and
Y
we additionally add two dummy vertices
x_{\ell+1}
and
y_{\ell+1}
.

The edge set of
\mathbf{G}
contains the same edges as the triangle detection instance, with the modification that edges of the type
\{c_{i},a_{j}\}
in the original instance become edges of the form
\{c_{i},a_{j}^{\prime}\}
in
\mathbf{G}
. For each
i\in\bm{[}\,1\,\bm{.\,.}\,\ell\,\bm{]}
, we insert a path of length
4(n-i+1)
from
x_{i}
to
a_{i}
and from
a_{i}^{\prime}
to
y_{i}
. Lastly, for each
i\in\bm{[}\,1\,\bm{.\,.}\,\ell\,\bm{]}
, we connect
x_{i}
with
x_{i+1}
and
y_{i}
with
y_{i+1}
.

It is not difficult to see that the constructed graph
\mathbf{G}
contains
\mathcal{O}(n+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}4(n-i+1))=\mathcal{O}(n)
nodes and
\mathcal{O}(n+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}4(n-i+1)+m)=\mathcal{O}(m)
edges.

For
i\in\bm{[}\,1\,\bm{.\,.}\,\ell\,\bm{]}
, let
e_{i}\coloneqq\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}
and
e_{i}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{y_{i},y_{i+1}\}
. Further, set
s=x_{1}
and
t=y_{1}
. We claim that for each
i\in\bm{[}\,1\,\bm{.\,.}\,\ell\,\bm{]}
,
d_{\mathbf{G}\setminus\{e_{i},e_{i}^{\prime}\}}(s,t)=11+8n-6i
if and only if there exist
b_{j}
and
c_{k}
such that
\{a_{i},b_{j},c_{k}\}
forms a triangle in the input instance (so we can get the final answer from
d_{\mathbf{G}\setminus\{e_{i},e_{i}^{\prime}\}}(s,t)
for all
i\in\bm{[}\,1\,\bm{.\,.}\,\ell\,\bm{]}
).
To this end, we analyze the cost of a path in
\mathbf{G}\setminus\{e_{i},e_{i}^{\prime}\}
that travels from
s
to
x_{i}
, then to
a_{i}
, takes a shortest path to
a_{i}^{\prime}
, and finally proceeds to
y_{i}
and
t
. It is straightforward to verify that such a path has length
3+4(n-i+1)+4(n-i+1)+(i-1)+(i-1)=11+8n-6i
if a triangle exists (traversing
b_{j}
and
c_{k}
); otherwise, the cost is at least one unit higher. To complete the proof, we show that this is the only path that can achieve a cost of
11+8n-6i
. Indeed, in
\mathbf{G}\setminus\{e_{i},e_{i}^{\prime}\}
, any path between
a_{\hat{\imath}}
and
a_{\hat{\jmath}}^{\prime}
has cost at least
3+4(n-\hat{\imath}+1)+4(n-\hat{\jmath}+1)+(\hat{\imath}-1)+(\hat{\jmath}-1)=11+8n-3\hat{\imath}-3\hat{\jmath}
for any
\hat{\imath},\hat{\jmath}\in\bm{[}\,1\,\bm{.\,.}\,i\,\bm{]}
. In particular, if
\hat{\imath}\neq i
or
\hat{\jmath}\neq i
, then
11+8n-3\hat{\imath}-3\hat{\jmath}\geq(11+8n-6i)+3
.

When handling rational weights, we make
\mathbf{G}
weighted as follows. Let
M
be the largest weight in the input graph; we then assign to each edge
\{a_{i},b_{j}\}
of weight
w(a_{i},b_{j})
in the input graph a weight of
1+w(a_{i},b_{j})/(M+1)
in
\mathbf{G}
. We do the same for edges
\{b_{j},c_{k}\}
and
\{c_{k},a_{i}\}
(which are
\{c_{k},a_{i}^{\prime}\}
in
\mathbf{G}
). All other edges in
\mathbf{G}
retain their original weight of one. The proof of correctness is very similar to the unweighted case. It is easy to see that for each
i\in\bm{[}\,1\,\bm{.\,.}\,\ell\,\bm{]}
, the same path we were considering before traversing
a_{i},a_{i}^{\prime}
has cost
5+4n-2i+(w(a_{i},b_{j})+w(b_{j},c_{k})+w(c_{k},a_{i}))/(M+1)
for
j,k\in\bm{[}\,1\,\bm{.\,.}\,n\,\bm{]}
that minimize the sum
w(a_{i},b_{j})+w(b_{j},c_{k})+w(c_{k},a_{i})
. Using the same analysis as before, all paths going through other
a_{\hat{\imath}}
and
a_{\hat{\jmath}}^{\prime}
have cost at least
(5+4n-2i)+1
, which is strictly greater than
5+4n-2i+(w(a_{i},b_{j})+w(b_{j},c_{k})+w(c_{k},a_{i}))/(M+1)
because the fractional part is less than one.

With
Lemma

6.1
at our disposal,
Section

1
follows almost immediately.

\Theta(\sqrt{n})

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
