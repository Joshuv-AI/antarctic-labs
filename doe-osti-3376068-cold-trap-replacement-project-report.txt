 SUMMARY
 This report documents the replacement of the Mechanisms
 Engineering Test Loop (METL) cold trap. The work involved preparation of
 the facility to replace the cold trap, removal of the existing welded cold trap
 from the sodium purification circuit, installation of a new replacement cold
 trap, completion of associated welds and examinations, restoration of
 instrumentation and heaters, and controlled return of the cold trap circuit to
 service.

 The replacement represented a significant maintenance evolution
 because the cold trap is an integral welded component of the sodium
 system. As a result, the work required coordinated control of sodium
 chemistry, deliberate formation of freeze plugs, inert gas management,
 precision cutting and welding, and a staged reheating and refill sequence.
 The activity was executed using procedural controls intended to protect
 personnel, preserve system cleanliness, and maintain the integrity of the
 sodium boundary throughout the work.

 This report provides a narrative summary of the milestone, including
 the purpose of the work, the pre-job system condition, the major field
 activities performed, observations made during execution, and the resulting
 post-work condition of the METL cold trap circuit.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 The Mechanisms Engineering Test Loop (METL) uses a sodium cold trap to control oxygen
concentration in the circulating liquid metal by cooling a side stream and crystallizing the sodium
oxide. Over time, oxide accumulation within the installed cold trap reduced its effective operating
range until the unit began plugging at approximately 150 °C. This behavior prevented operation
at the lower temperatures required for effective sodium purification and motivated replacement of
the component. Because the cold trap was welded into the loop rather than installed with
removable mechanical connections, replacement required cutting the component from the sodium
piping system and welding in a new assembly.

 The replacement was carried out by first preparing the loop for intrusive maintenance through
sodium inventory management, controlled cool-down, and establishment of sodium freeze plugs
to isolate the work region. The original cold trap was then removed, a preassembled replacement
unit was fit to the system, and the new component was welded into place under inert argon purge
conditions. Residual sodium was removed from the existing pipe ends to enable acceptable weld
preparation and closure. After installation, the restored pressure boundary was evaluated by
helium leak testing, radiography, and dye penetrant testing. The new cold trap was subsequently
baked out under vacuum, reheated, refilled with sodium, and returned to service using a controlled
thaw and fill sequence. The work restored sodium purification capability in METL and provides a
useful example of replacement of a welded sodium service component in an operating liquid-
metal facility.

 1.1 Sodium Chemistry Control
 A cold trap is a filtration and crystallization device that maintains the impurity level of the liquid
metal in a thermal hydraulic system such as the sodium in METL. The performance of a liquid
sodium system can be critically impaired by the presence of impurities in the sodium. Impurities
can cause plugging of piping and critical systems in the liquid metal facility as well as accelerate
the corrosion rate of the containment material that the structure is made of, in the case of METL,
predominantly 300 series stainless steel. The targeted impurities when cold trapping are sodium
oxides but the cold trap also filters sodium hydrides, carbon, metallic impurities, and in the case

 1.2 METL Cold Trap Information
 The METL cold trap is integrated into a dedicated branch of the loop and includes the vessel
itself, associated piping, an economizer section, heaters, insulation, pressure instrumentation,
and supporting ducting, Figure 5. The installed METL cold trap had been in service since initial
sodium fill of the facility in 2018. Oxides present on the initially unwetted internal surfaces of the
METL facility, test articles such as the Gear Test Assembly and the Thermal Hydraulic
Experimental Test Article, and trace impurities in our ultra high purity argon cover gas has caused
an accumulation of oxide inventory in the cold trap. Over time, this accumulation degraded the
hydraulic performance of the unit and reduced its usable operating range. The most visible
symptom of degradation was that flow rate through the cold trap showed a marked decrease and
there was an increase in pressure differential across the inlet and outlet of the cold trap at cold
trapping temperatures of 150 °C. Because effective sodium purification requires operation at
sufficiently low cold trap temperatures, this plugging behavior indicated that the unit could no
longer provide the intended purification function.

 While it may be possible to revitalize an installed cold trap in place using thermal regeneration
(flushing hot sodium through cold trap and dissolve impurities and deliver them to a dump tank),
this can create irrecoverable plugs in unknown locations in the piping system. In order to ensure
the plug inducing impurities were effectively removed from METL, a mechanical approach was
chosen to physically remove and replace the cold trap. The existing unit was welded directly into
the sodium piping system, so the work required intrusive modification of the loop. The activity
therefore became not only a component replacement but also a demonstration of a method for
removing and reinstalling a welded sodium purification component while preserving inert
conditions, maintaining sodium isolation, and verifying weld integrity before returning to service.

 2. METHODS

 2.1 Sodium Filled Pipe Weld Testing
 In order to ensure success of the cold trap replacement, a procedure was developed for
cutting open a sodium filled pipe, removing sodium, weld prepping the pipe and then performing
the weld while monitoring critical system conditions. This procedure was developed using a
combination of general sodium operations experience at METL, communications with colleagues
from CEA in France [7], and literature such as [8] which describes a procedure for replacing
bellows valves on the Sodium Reactor Experiment. Figure 6 provides a collage of photos
illustrating the process of cutting into a sodium filled pipe and rewelding the component back
together after preparing the pipe/sodium interface.
 As can be seen in Figure 6.1, a pipe cutter is used to cut open the pipe. The use of a pipe
cutter reduces heat and debris production associated with other cutting methods such as a
serrated or abrasive saw. Once a complete cut has been made around the pipe circumference
and through the pipe wall, a serrated saw is then used to cut through the relatively soft frozen
sodium to separate the pipe segments, as can be seen in Figure 6.2 and Figure 6.3.
 A handheld end prep beveling tool is then used in the field on a pipe section to prepare a ‘J-
groove’ in the pipe end to facilitate a full penetration weld, Figure 6.4. Note that this machining is
done without any lubricants that could interact with the sodium and is done in a manner to reduce
heat generation; the existing sodium plug is left in place at this point to reduce chip and oxide
ingress while preparing for welding. After machining the pipe end(s), an exhaust port is made
with a beveled hole at a higher elevation than the future weld in order to facilitate an inert argon
back purge for welding, Figure 6.5. Note that all of these steps – from cutting the system open to
welding should be completed in a relatively short time (within 24 hours) to reduce oxygen
contamination of the bulk sodium; the next steps should be performed immediately before welding
as the protective sodium plug will be removed and cleaned to facilitate welding.
 As can be seen in Figure 6.6, a twist drill bit with the same diameter as the inner diameter of
the pipe being welded is used to remove approximately 10 cm (~4”) of sodium from the pipe end.
For the ¾” and 1” SCH40 pipe sizes used in the cold trap piping, this distance was empirically
found to be adequate to allow the welders to establish a full penetration weld while reducing the
risk that thermal conductivity would transport heat from the weld to the bulk system sodium. Note:
a file was used to dull the cutting edges of the flutes in the body of the twist drill, leaving only

ANL-NRIC-008 / ANL-ART-319                                                                          8

sharp cutting edges on the drill tip to eliminate the risk of any undue mechanical scoring of the
inner surface of the pipe wall.
 With the bulk sodium removed from the pipe, a lint-free wipe spritzed with de-ionized water is
used to carefully remove approximately 2.5 cm (1”) length of sodium residue from the inner
surface of the pipe wall to eliminate entrainment of sodium contamination in the weld pool, Figure
6.7 and Figure 6.8. An argon back purge is used during Tungsten Inert Gas (TIG) welding of
stainless steel, so the residue sodium on the pipe wall will not readily combust during welding,
this step is simply to ensure a clean weld free of porosities or cracks.
 Two monitoring thermocouples were then installed at locations between the weld and the bulk
sodium front, in this case they were installed at 5 and 10 cm from the weld, Figure 7. The
temperature could then be monitored at these locations to ensure the bulk sodium temperature
located at approximately 10 cm from the weld does not surpass 88 °C (190 °F) so as not to reach
the sodium melting point of 98 °C (208 °F) for a significant amount of time. This required the
welder to make intermittent passes, pausing between passes to minimize the interpass
temperature. When the appropriate number of weld passes were completed, the exhaust port was
then welded shut and all of the welds were then ready for non-destructive examination (NDE),
Figure 8. Note that two test weld pieces were manufactured and passed visual, dye-penetrant
and radiography NDE to ensure a good weld was produced prior to welding the METL cold trap
system.

 3. METL COLD TRAP REPLACEMENT

 3.1 System Preparation
 Before removal of the cold trap, the loop was placed in a configuration intended to isolate the
cold trap branch from the remainder of the sodium inventory while preserving a stable inert cover
gas boundary. Sodium in the relevant portions of the system had first been purified by extended
cold trapping at 173 °C (corresponding to 4.8 wppm oxide according to Eq. 1) so that the loop
was at a low oxide condition prior to maintenance. This was found to be the lowest cold trapping
temperature that could sustainably be held before the spent cold trap began plugging. The
expansion tank sodium level was reduced, selected vessels were drained and isolated, and the
gas spaces of previously sodium-wetted volumes were tied together and maintained under a small
positive argon pressure. This provided a controlled atmosphere for the work with a reduced overall
hydraulic head and reduced the amount of sodium directly coupled to the section of piping that
would be opened.

 The valve lineup was then arranged so that the cold trap and plugging meter branch was
hydraulically separated from the rest of the loop in a way that supported freeze-plug isolation. L-
V BYPASS, L-CT BYPASS, EXP IN, and EXP OUT valves were verified open to preserve
pressure communication with the expansion volume and to avoid isolating any heated sodium in
a rigid segment, Figure 9. CT OUT/PM OUT and CT OUT/PM IN valves were closed to isolate
the branch from downstream circulation paths, Figure 10. Within the branch itself, CT IN, CT OUT,
PM IN, PM OUT, and CT/PM SERIES valves were verified open so that sodium in the cold trap
and adjacent piping would remain part of one continuous connected volume during cooldown.
This lineup allowed freeze plugs to form in intended locations without trapping molten sodium
between closed valves and frozen sections.

 With the valve configuration established, the cold trap vessel and adjacent piping were cooled
in a deliberate sequence to create frozen sodium barriers around the component to be removed.
The loop piping and expansion tank heaters were first placed at a moderate hold temperature,
while the cold trap vessel heaters were left at their existing settings until the shutdown sequence
began. The cold trap vessel heaters were then de-energized in order from CT Zone 3 to CT Zone
2 to CT Zone 1, followed by the closest connecting pipe zones, CT Outlet to ECON Btm (Piping
Zone 9) and CT Inlet to ECON Btm (Piping Zone 3), Figure 11. This order was selected to avoid
heating a trapped volume of molten sodium behind a newly frozen section. Temperatures in CT

ANL-NRIC-008 / ANL-ART-319                                                                      12

Zone 3, CT Zone 2, CT Zone 1, CT Outlet to ECON Btm, and CT Inlet to ECON Btm were
monitored until each cooled to the target temperature confirming sodium solidification.

 After the cold trap vessel and the nearest pipe sections had frozen, cooldown progressed
outward through the economizer-side piping so that the solidified region extended away from the
component in a controlled manner. The next zones removed from service were CT Outlet to
ECON Top (Piping Zone 10) and CT Inlet to ECON Top (Piping Zone 4), followed by the
economizer heater zones (Piping Zones 25, 24, 23 then 22), Figure 11. Each zone was allowed
to cool below the sodium melting point before the next more distant section was shut down. This
produced frozen sodium boundaries on both the inlet and outlet sides of the cold trap while leaving
the remainder of the loop in a stable heated condition. Once these zones had cooled to near
ambient, insulation, heaters, ducting, and local instrumentation could be removed from CT Zone
3, CT Zone 2, CT Zone 1, Piping Zone 9, and Piping Zone 3 to expose the cold trap and adjacent
piping for cutting.

 This preparation established the cold trap as a locally isolated sodium-filled section bounded
by frozen sodium in named heater zones and supported by a valve lineup that avoided trapping
liquid sodium in a confined segment. The resulting condition allowed the existing cold trap to be
cut free at the selected inlet and outlet locations with confidence that the upstream and
downstream sodium inventories remained isolated behind stable freeze plugs.

 3.2 Spent Cold Trap Removal
 Two circumferential cuts were required to separate the spent cold trap from the METL sodium
piping system, Figure 12. Both cuts were made in the pipe runs above the cold trap vessel
nozzles, in regions where the piping was accessible and where adequate frozen sodium plug
length existed between the cut and the nearest heated (molten) sodium boundary.
 Cut A was located on the cold trap outlet (CT OUT) side, in the pipe run between the top of
the cold trap vessel and the economizer, Figure 13. This cut was positioned approximately 16.5
cm (6.5”) of pipe length below a 90-deg elbow and approximately 4.5 m (15’) of total pipe length
from the economizer.
 Cut B was located on the cold trap inlet (CT IN) side, positioned in the pipe run above the cold
trap inlet vessel nozzle, Figure 13. Cut B was approximately 25 cm (10”) of pipe length from a
pipe elbow and approximately 4.8 m (16’) of total pipe length from the economizer. Like Cut A,
this location was selected to leave a sufficient length of frozen sodium between the cut and the
nearest heated zone to serve as a reliable freeze plug during the period the system was open.
 Both cut locations were chosen to be above the cold trap vessel so that the cold trap, once
freed, could be slid laterally on the METL steel catch pan without interference from the remaining
METL system piping. The vertical positions of Cuts A and B were measured and recorded with
respect to the catch pan prior to cutting so that any movement of the piping system after the cuts
could be detected and the original pipe alignment re-established for fit-up of the replacement unit.

 The new cold trap was then put into position on the catch pan and the as-cut locations were
transferred to the sections of pipe where the new cold trap would be cut to facilitate installation,

full penetration weld. After beveling the pipe, the plugs were removed and replaced with caps.

 3.4 Return to Service
 With the cold trap welded into the METL piping system and all welds verified, the process
control heaters, thermocouples and insulation was reinstalled on the new cold trap and piping.
Quality assurance resistance checks were performed on all heaters prior to reinstallation. The air-
cooled ducting for the cold trap blower was reattached to the cold trap vessel. At the conclusion
of these steps, the cold trap was mechanically and thermally ready for sodium transfer.

 Before filling the cold trap with sodium, the METL vessels were filled with sodium including
the Expansion Tank, Vessel 1, and Vessel 2 to ensure adequate sodium inventory was available
to backfill the cold trap circuit. All sodium-wetted piping and vessels outside of the frozen cold
trap circuit were brought to a temperature of 250 °C, with the dump tank held at 210 °C. Each
heater zone in the primary and diagnostic loops was confirmed to be at 250 °C ± 20 °C. Particular
attention was paid to the zones in the cold trap supply and return lines—Pipe Zones 19, 20, 21,
38, 12, 11, 26, 5, 45, 2, and 57, as well as the CT electromagnetic flowmeter, CT electromagnetic
pump, and CT/PM SERIES valve—to ensure that thermal expansion of the sodium could be
accommodated during the thawing process, Figure 10, Figure 11.

ANL-NRIC-008 / ANL-ART-319                                                                       27

 The empty cold trap vessel was preheated to 250 °C using the 3 vessel heater zones to
prevent sodium from freezing upon first contact with the cold trap internals. The melt-in sequence
was not initiated until all internal cold trap thermocouples confirmed temperatures above 200 °C,
ensuring the vessel was uniformly preheated.

 The piping between the economizer and the cold trap vessel was entirely filled with solid
sodium that had been frozen in place during the cold trap isolation and removal process.
Remelting this sodium required a conservative, directional approach to avoid imposing undue
mechanical stress on the piping system.

 Pipe Zones 19 and 20, which were already at 250 °C and contained molten sodium with
access to the loop's argon gas space, were raised to a setpoint of 300 °C to serve as a thermal
driving force for melting the adjacent frozen zones. The four economizer heater zones (Zones 22
through 25) were then energized by closing their respective circuit breakers and initially set to a
conservative preheat temperature of 80 °C.

 The melt-in then proceeded in a stepwise fashion through the economizer, working from the
molten sodium side toward the cold trap. At each step, the preceding zone at 300 °C provided
process heat via conduction through the sodium and pipe wall into the next frozen zone, ensuring
the melt front always advanced from liquid into solid with an available expansion path behind it.

 After the economizer zones were fully melted and at temperature (300 °C), the CT inlet and
outlet piping zones between the economizer and the cold trap were addressed. Pipe Zones 9 and
10 on the CT outlet side were preheated to 75 °C but were left solid at that time. Pipe Zone 3 was
raised to 300 °C and left to soak for a period of 30 minutes. Then Pipe Zone 4 was set to 300 °C.

 When Zone 4 melted, sodium began to flow due to gravity and differential pressure into the
empty cold trap under vacuum. The fill was confirmed by monitoring the internal cold trap
thermocouples and the external thermocouples on the CT inlet and outlet nozzles. Once the fill
was confirmed, Pipe Zones 9 and 10 on the CT outlet side were raised to 300 °C to complete the
melt-in of the return piping back to the main loop.

 The cold trap installation was now complete – piping and vessel process heaters in the cold
trap circuit were adjusted to their nominal operating set points and the cold trap was returned to
normal operation. The minimum temperature in the cold trap was initially brought down over the
course of 8 hours from 250 °C to 175 °C and then from 175 °C to 145 °C over the course of a
week to ensure the inlet of the cold trap was not plugged with supercooled oxide, Figure 30.

 4. LESSONS LEARNED AND FUTURE WORK
 The METL cold trap replacement demonstrated that intrusive maintenance on a welded
sodium system can be performed successfully when sodium chemistry, thermal conditions,
mechanical restraint, and inerting strategy are managed appropriately. One of the most important
lessons learned was the value of deliberate freeze-plug formation and verification before opening
the system. Establishing the correct valve lineup, maintaining pressure communication with the
expansion volume, and cooling heater zones in a controlled order were all essential to preventing
the creation of trapped molten sodium volumes and to ensuring that the isolated section remained
stable throughout the work. The use of local temperature monitoring near freeze-plug boundaries
provided additional confidence that the sodium boundary remained intact during cutting and
welding evolutions.

 A second key lesson learned was that field fit-up flexibility significantly reduced installation
risk. The decision to fabricate the replacement cold trap with intentionally long inlet and outlet
pipe stubs allowed the final dimensions to be adjusted to the as-built system rather than relying
on nominal drawing dimensions alone. Similarly, mechanically restraining the existing piping
before cutting proved necessary to preserve alignment and avoid unwanted movement of the
remaining system once the original cold trap was removed. The project also confirmed the value
of ex-situ development testing. Practicing the sequence of pipe cutting, sodium removal, weld
preparation, back-purging, and temperature-controlled welding on sodium-filled mockups
established confidence in the maintenance approach and helped define practical controls such
as sodium removal depth, thermocouple placement, and acceptable interpass thermal behavior.

 The work also reinforced the importance of contamination control during sodium maintenance.
Minimizing debris generation during cutting and beveling, using argon purges during open-system
work, limiting the duration of system exposure, and carefully removing residual sodium from weld
regions were all important to maintaining sodium purity and achieving code-acceptable welds.
The added CT VAC VALVE also proved to be a valuable design feature because it simplified
vacuum bakeout, inert gas purging, and final closure of the replacement cold trap. This suggests
that future sodium components expected to require maintenance or replacement should
incorporate similar dedicated purge and vacuum access features (such as freeze vents) as part
of their original design, rather than depending on temporary field-installed solutions.

 Additional future work should evaluate whether aspects of the replacement method can be
further improved or standardized. This may include development of dedicated tooling for sodium

ANL-NRIC-008 / ANL-ART-319                                                                      30

removal from pipe ends and/or improved temporary sealing methods during purge and fit-up.
Additionally, in future work, the techniques employed here could also be tested on larger diameter
piping more representative of advanced reactor component scale before executing in the field.

 While outside the scope of this report – the gas space that was discovered in the inlet line to
the cold trap, as shown in Figure 22, should be further explored and considered for future METL
cold trap replacements. Given there was not a frozen plug of sodium in this line- it is important to
freeze the entire economizer, as was done in this work, to ensure there is a sufficient length of
cold pipe to create an appropriate freeze plug. Given the replacement cold trap has been
operating at 150 °C trapping temperature from initial fill in February 2025 to the time of this report,
this potential gas lock does not seem to be deleteriously affecting cold trap performance.

 5. CONCLUSION
 The replacement of the METL cold trap successfully restored sodium purification capability to
the loop and demonstrated a practical method for removing and reinstalling a welded sodium-
service component in an operating liquid-metal facility. The work was completed through careful
system preparation, deliberate freeze-plug isolation, controlled cutting and welding under inert
conditions, and comprehensive non-destructive examination before return to service. Subsequent
bakeout, refill, and staged reheating confirmed that the new cold trap could be integrated into the
existing system without compromising sodium boundary integrity or overall loop operability.
Beyond restoring an important purification function, this effort established a repeatable
maintenance approach for future intrusive sodium-system repairs and provided valuable
operational experience in sodium chemistry control, thermal management, and field execution of
complex maintenance activities in METL.
