Abstract
The overarching objective of this project was to use observations to evaluate hypotheses related
to aerosol-induced invigoration or enervation of deep convective storms. We did so by tracking
isolated, developing deep convective storms around SGP with GOES and pairing each storm
with environmental conditions and aerosol measurements taken at SGP. The analysis was subject
to numerous uncertainties and we could not draw strong conclusions about the magnitude of
invigoration or enervation. That said, we could not disprove the hypothesis that aerosol-induced
invigoration and enervation exist. Encouragingly, the results we found were largely consistent
with existing hypotheses and previous modeling studies. Specifically, we found evidence that
large concentrations of small aerosol particles promotes storms that develop more quickly while
large concentrations of moderately sized particles are associated with less quickly developing
storms. These signals are strengthened under some meteorological conditions and suggest
convective environments to target for future study to study this phenomenon.
Project Activities
 a. Project Goals
The goals of the proposed work were 1) to assess the relationship between aerosol concentration
and updraft speed in developing convection using ARM and satellite observations and 2) to use
the analysis to assess the hypotheses regarding the mechanisms of aerosol-induced invigoration.

 b. Storm Tracking
Substantial time was put into tracking and assembling the dataset. Data sources included GOES,
ARM, HRRR, and LASSO. The main variable derived from the storm tracking with GOES was
the cloud top rise rate (CTRR). We used CTRR throughout the study as a proxy for the storm
updraft speed (see next section). Environmental conditions came primarily from HRRR analysis
and aerosol concentrations came from ground-based ARM SGP measurements. The distributions
of these quantities associated with our tracked storms are shown in Figure 1. Those quantities in
purple were used to predict the CTRR. We found that the ability of the environment to predict
the CTRR was highly dependent on the degree of isolation of the storm. This is an important
result. Since the aerosol signal is small compared to the influence of the environment, we will be
most likely to detect that signal if we look at the most isolated storms.

 c. Comparison to LASSO
We took the time in this study to assess our assumption that the cloud top rise rate is a
reasonably good proxy of the characteristic updraft speed in a deep convective cloud. To do so,
we leveraged the LASSO ensemble of simulations for the CACTI field campaign. Storms were
tracked in the simulations in the same way as with GOES data and associated with a cloud top
rise rate. Those CTRRs were then correlated with the maximum updraft speed in the storm (other
updraft speed metrics were tried as well). The relationship is by no means 1-to-1, but in Figure 3
we can see that the maximum CTRR is reasonably well correlated with the maximum updraft
speed. Unfortunately, the maximum CTRR is less well predicted by the environmental variables
(Figure 2) than is the average CTRR. Nonetheless, this analysis gives us some confidence that
the CTRRs are related to the updraft dynamics in the storms.

Figure 3: Figure 2 from Pimperl and Igel (2026) showing the correlation between LASSO
derived CTRR and maximum updraft speed.
 d. Main Results
After controlling for meteorology, we found statistically significant correlations between aerosol
concentrations and CTRR. The estimated magnitudes of the effect are shown in Table 1. The
most notable aspect of these magnitudes is that they are small. Both invigoration and enervation
are detected. The strongest signal is associated with nucleation mode particles where a large
increase of 1000 particles cm-3 is associated with a 3-6 m/s increase in updraft speed.
Table 1. Estimated magnitude of aerosol-induced invigoration (pink) and enervation (blue).
 Aerosol Mode Increase in maximum updraft speed per 1000 particles cm-3.
 Nanoscale (2.5–65 nm)              0.4 - 0.8 m/s
 Nucleation (2.5–10 nm)             3 - 6 m/s
 Aitken (10-100 nm) Not statistically significant
 Accumulation (100–500 nm)          –1 - –2 m/s

The dataset was halved based on low and high values of a variety of metrics and the analysis
repeated for the nucleation and accumulation mode results. The invigoration/enervation signal
strengthens for some subsets as shown in Figure 4. It shows that invigoration by nucleation mode
particles is stronger for small storms developing under high CAPE, high shear, and high freezing
height environments. Such environments are consistent with the hypothesis that these small
particles modify the warm phase processes when updrafts are strong enough to produce high
supersaturation to activate these particles. It also shows that enervation by accumulation mode
particles is stronger for low-based storms with high tropospheric humidity and again with high
CAPE and high shear. Although enervation has been discussed much less in the literature, these
results appear to also be consistent with existing studies.

Figure 4: Figure 6 from Pimperl and Igel (2026) showing the correlation coefficients for the
dataset subsets under which the signal strengthens.
More details about our findings can be found in Pimperl and Igel (2026). Ultimately, we could
not disprove any of the previously proposed hypotheses. We suggest that future studies should
further target isolated storms, particularly those developing in high CAPE, high shear
environments where we have found invigoration and enervation signals to be strongest.
