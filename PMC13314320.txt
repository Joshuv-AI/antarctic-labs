Reproducibility of Splanchnic Blood Flow Measured Using Phase‐Contrast MRI
Authors: Lærke S. Gasbjerg, Frederikke H. Østergaard, Rasmus S. Rasmussen, Ludvig S. Langberg, Sophie W. Nielsen, Lotte Alstrup, Tanne S. W. Larsson, Ulrik B. Andersen, Henrik B. W. Larsson, Ali Asmar, Bryan Haddock, Mark B. Vestergaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSplanchnic vessel blood flow assessment can describe physiology or pathophysiology and also has clinical relevance for patients with altered abdominal blood flow, vascular neuropathy, or atherosclerotic conditions. Reliable and practicable methods for this assessment are needed. We assessed the reproducibility of non‐invasive phase‐contrast MRI (PC‐MRI) for measuring splanchnic blood flow and its feasibility for detecting postprandial blood flow changes. We included measurements from two groups of healthy individuals (Group A: n = 10; C: n = 10) and a group of patients with type 2 diabetes (B: n = 10) from randomized, single‐blind, placebo‐controlled studies, and assessed blood flow with 2D PC sequences (3 Tesla Siemens Biograph MRI and 3 Tesla Philips Achieva). Blood flow was measured in the arteria mesenterica superior, truncus coeliacus, arteria hepatica (Group A only), and vena portae hepatis over multiple days, with repeated scans each day to assess variability and reproducibility. To confirm detection of postprandial changes, nine repeated measurements were acquired during ingestion of 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test compared to water ingestion. Reproducibility was assessed by coefficient of variation (CoV) and limits of agreement with the mean (LoAM) using an extended Bland–Altman analysis. Blood flow in arteria mesenterica superior, truncus coeliacus, and vena portae hepatis demonstrated low intrasubject within session CoV (7%–22%) and intrasubject between session CoV (16%–26%). Blood flow in arteria hepatica had poorer CoV for both within sessions (12%) and between sessions (32%). Glucose ingestion induced a blood flow peak increase of 88% ± 52% in arteria mesenterica superior and 68% ± 36% in vena portae hepatis, both robustly detected, while no change was observed in the arteria hepatica or truncus coeliacus. In conclusion, PC‐MRI is a reproducible and feasible technique for measuring blood flow in arteria mesenterica superior, truncus coeliacus, and vena portae hepatis with the ability to detect postprandial changes.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05391581, NCT06426823, NCT06774807

Introduction

Absorption of nutrients following a meal takes place mainly in the small intestines supplied by the arteria mesenterica superior. Nutrients are absorbed through the intestinal wall and then reach the bloodstream (or lymph) by nutrient‐specific membrane transport [1]. To increase the uptake of nutrients in the intestines, both the intestinal wall is lined with villi and microvilli, which increase the absorptive surface area, and the intestinal blood flow increases in response to food‐intake [2]. This results in an optimization of nutrient uptake in the bloodstream; thus, splanchnic hemodynamics play a key role in the uptake of digested nutrients [3]. However, substantial changes in mesenteric blood flow can influence the systemic blood pressure [2, 4], and dysregulation of this blood flow is part of the pathophysiology in a number of diseases such as postprandial hypotension [5, 6], autonomic dysfunctions [7, 8], and hepatic diseases [9]. Furthermore, the use of medications that influence digestive system hormones has become an area of high clinical and research importance [10]. This includes the widely used medications modulating glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptors, but also modulation of the glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor is of growing interest [11, 12]. Some effects of these medications may be through modulation of splanchnic blood flow [13, 14]. Therefore, to better understand the intricate regulatory mechanisms and accurately assess underlying causes of abdominal symptoms, as well as the potential effects of medical interventions, dynamic and high‐quality measurements of splanchnic blood flow are needed.

Previously, net splanchnic blood flow has been measured by invasive procedures, for example, by simultaneous hepatic vein and femoral artery catheterization [15]. In larger scientific studies, this approach is not optimal due to its invasive procedures that also require extensive washouts between interventions. Non‐invasive methods, which can produce repeated measures with good precision, are needed. The non‐invasive method of Doppler ultrasound flowmetry has been used, but this technique is limited and often inadequate [16]. For example, exact measurements of the vessels' diameter with ultrasound are difficult, and errors will be amplified in the flow calculation by a squared factor. Postprandial blood flow is difficult to measure by Doppler ultrasound because of movement of the intestines and intestinal gas, which significantly degrades the ultrasound signal [9]. Also, large amounts of adipose tissue will produce poor quality ultrasound images.

An alternative approach making it possible to measure blood flow within specific regions of the splanchnic bed is to use phase contrast (PC)‐MRI methods [17, 18]. PC is an MRI technique where the velocity of moving blood can be quantitatively measured based on the remaining phase differences in the MRI signal after the application of two periods of equal but opposite gradients in the magnetic field. The technique was initially proposed 40 years ago [17] and is commonly used for measuring blood flow in other large vessels, for example, in the carotids [19, 20] and renal artery [21, 22], and the technique is extensively used for examining cardiac blood flow [23]. Blood flow in the major splanchnic vessels has been successfully determined using PC‐MRI [24, 25, 26, 27, 28], but a thorough reproducibility study of variation in repeated and day‐to‐day flow measures is lacking.

To evaluate PC‐MRI for scientific applications with clinical perspectives, we investigated if PC‐MRI can provide reliable measurements of blood flow in the large splanchnic vessels. We examined the blood flow of the four big splanchnic vessels in humans, arteria mesenterica superior, vena portae hepatis, truncus coeliacus and arteria hepatica using PC‐MRI to quantify the variability in both healthy individuals and patients with type 2 diabetes at two separate sites using different MRI scanners. Also, to detect physiological changes, we assessed the postprandial splanchnic blood flow in healthy individuals following oral glucose ingestion.

Baseline characteristics of study participants.

Initially, all participants were brought in for a screening scan to ensure that the splanchnic vessels could be localized by PC‐MRI. For the healthy participants, exclusion criteria were type 1 or 2 diabetes, or first‐degree relative with diabetes. For participants with type 2 diabetes, HbA1c should be 48–75 mmol/mol, and medical treatment not including insulin. For all, MRI‐incompatible metallic implants were an additional exclusion criterion, and normal kidney function, liver function, and hemoglobin levels were confirmed before inclusion by blood sample analyses (creatinine, alanine aminotransferase, and hemoglobin).

A subset of the data measured in Group A was previously used as baseline data in a study investigating the effect of GIP on postprandial regulation of splanchnic blood flow.

Experimental Setup

Participants fasted for at least 6 h before each MRI session. The participants underwent multiple MRI sessions on different days, during which blood flow in the arteria mesenterica superior, truncus coeliacus, and vena portae hepatis was measured using repeated PC‐MRI scans (Figure 1). In Group A, arteria hepatica blood flow was also assessed.

Overview of the experimental setup. For Group A (A), the experiment was repeated on eight separate days/sessions. In two of these sessions, measurements were obtained after glucose or water ingestion to assess whether the measurement could capture the effect of oral glucose ingestion on blood flow. In the remaining six sessions, only baseline measurements were collected. For Group B (B) and Group C (C), the experiment was repeated on four separate days/sessions. For Group B, in one of these sessions, measurements were obtained after water ingestion, while Group C received a subcutaneous saline injection. In the remaining three sessions for both groups, only baseline measurements were collected.

Group A underwent eight separate MRI‐sessions, while Groups B and C had four MRI‐sessions each. In Group A, two repeated measurements were collected in six of the sessions. In the remaining two sessions, 12 measurements were obtained: three at baseline followed by nine extra measurements performed after participants received either 75 g of glucose in 250 mL of water or a placebo (250 mL of water), alongside a continuous saline infusion (40 mL/h) over a 125‐min study period (Figure 1A). The glucose sessions assessed feasibility of tracking postprandial blood flow changes, while the placebo sessions provided additional reproducibility data. In Group B, 11 repeated measurements were conducted in one of the sessions and two measurements in the other three sessions (Figure 1B). In Group C, eight measurements were taken in one of the sessions, while one measurement was performed in the remaining three sessions (Figure 1C).

From the repeated measurements within each MRI session, the intrasubject within‐sessions coefficient of variation (CoV) and limits of agreements from mean (LoAM) from extended Bland–Altman analysis were calculated. Intrasubject between‐days CoV and LoAM were calculated from the variation between sessions.

MRI

For groups A and B, MRI scans were performed on a 3 Tesla Siemens Biograph mMR PET‐MRI scanner using the body coil. Group C was examined using a 3 Tesla Philips Achieva MRI scanner using the body coil. Initially, localizer images of the abdomen were acquired to locate the anatomical structures and vessels. High‐resolution anatomical images and angiograms covering the area of interest were acquired for planning of the blood flow measurements.

On the Siemens Biograph mMR, True Fast Imaging with Steady‐State Free Precession sequences were used for the anatomical images. Images in all three different planes were acquired (sagittal plane: 10 slices, field of view [FOV] = 133 × 305 mm3, voxel size = 1.19 × 1.19 × 4.5 mm3, repetition time [TR] = 170.0 ms, echo time [TE] = 1.3 ms, flip angle = 35°; axial plane: 20 slices, FOV = 344 × 306 mm3, voxel size = 0.96 × 0.96 × 4.5 mm3, TR = 104.0 ms, TE = 1.4 ms, flip angle = 40°; coronal plane: 20 slices, FOV = 344 × 306 mm3, voxel size = 0.96 × 0.96 × 4.5 mm3, TR = 103.0 ms, TE = 1.4 ms, flip angle = 40°). Angiograms visualizing the vessel segments of interest were measured by a time of flight (TOF) angiography sequence (25 slices, FOV = 110 × 320 mm3, voxel size = 1.25 × 1.25 × 3.5 mm3, TR = 400.0 ms, TE = 3.7 ms, flip angle = 60°).

On the Philips Achieva scanner, anatomical images were acquired by Turbo Field Echo (TFE) gradient echo sequences (sagittal plane: 28 slices, FOV = 281 × 400 × 58 mm3, voxel size = 1.56 × 1.56 × 4.0 mm3, TR = 3.4 ms, TE = 1.70 ms, flip angle = 50°, axial plane: 30 slices, FOV = 280 × 400 × 120 mm3, voxel size = 1.25 × 1.25 × 4.0 mm3, TR = 3.4 ms, TE = 1.6 ms, flip angle = 50°; coronal plane: 28 slices, FOV = 400 × 400 × 60 mm3, voxel size = 1.56 × 1.56 × 6.0 mm3, TR = 3.1 ms, TE = 1.57 ms, flip angle = 50°). Angiograms were measured by a Triggered Angiography Non‐Contrast‐Enhanced sequence (25 slices, FOV = 100 × 300 × 73 mm3, voxel size = 1.25 × 1.25 × 3.5 mm3, TR = 6.4 ms, TE = 3.1 ms, flip angle = 110°).

Blood Flow

We used a 2D gradient echo sequence with retrospective cardiac triggering with either electrocardiogram or pulse oximetry. For the Siemens Biograph mMR, the following sequence parameters were used: 1 slice, FOV = 240 × 340 mm2, acquired voxel size = 1.42 × 1.18 × 5 mm3, reconstructed voxel size = 0.71 × 0.71 × 5 mm3, repetition time TR = 11.0 ms, echo time TE = 3.17 ms, flip angle = 15°, 25 samples per cardiac cycle, GRAPPA acceleration factor = 2. For the Philips Achieva scanner, the following sequence parameters were used: 1 slice, FOV = 140 × 280 mm2, acquired voxel size = 1.30 × 1.35 × 6 mm3, reconstructed voxel size = 0.70 × 0.70 × 6 mm3, repetition time TR = 8.5 ms, echo time TE = 5.2 ms, flip angle = 25°, 17 samples per cardiac cycle, SENSE factor = 2. Two measurements were acquired to ensure correct placement of the imaging plane perpendicular to the examined vessels. From the first measurement, we obtained the blood flows in arteria mesenterica superior and truncus coeliacus and from the second measurement the flows in arteria hepatica and vena portae hepatis (Figure 2A,B). For measurements of blood flows in arteria mesenterica superior and truncus coeliacus, a velocity encoding factor (VENC) of 200 cm/s was used and for flow in arteria hepatica and vena portae hepatis a VENC of 100 cm/s. Region of interests (ROI) delineating the vessels were manual drawn using in‐house developed software (see Data Availability Statement). The ROI was initially drawn on the first image in the cardiac cycle and was hereafter applied on the next images and adjusted accordingly. The blood flow was then calculated as mean blood velocity times the cross‐sectional of the delineated vessel (Figure 2C,D). The mean blood flow across each all images in the cardiac cycle were calculated and used in the further analysis (Figure 2E). Any observation variation will only arise from manual delineation of the ROIs, and therefore, to assess between‐observer reproducibility, two independent observers delineated ROIs and the resulting flow values were compared to determine variability. This was only done for Group A.

Statistics

All values are reported as means ± standard deviations unless noted otherwise. Intrasubject reproducibility (variation of consecutive repeated measures) was assessed by calculation of coefficient of variation (CoV) and by calculation of limits of agreement with the mean (LoAM) using an extended Bland–Altman analysis [29]. CoV was calculated as the standard deviation of the repeated measurements for each subject divided by their mean. Between‐observer variation was evaluated from data from Group A using calculation of CoV, linear regression models, and standard Bland–Altman analysis, comparing paired measurements from two different observers across all sessions.

Additionally, between‐subject CoV was calculated using a mixed linear regression model with subject as a random effect, deriving the standard deviation between subjects from the random effect.

The effect of oral glucose or oral water ingestion was tested using a mixed linear regression model. The three repeated baseline measurements were grouped as the baseline condition, and the nine repeated post‐ingestion measurements were grouped as the post‐ingestion condition. The model included a fixed effect for group (baseline vs. post‐ingestion) and a random effect for subject to account for the repeated measurements within individual.

Between Observer Measurements

For Group A, postprocessing and drawing ROIs delineating the vessels were performed by two different observers. The blood flow measurement from the two observers was highly correlated for all vessels, with CoVs of 7.3%–11% and LoAMs of 247, 397, 243, and 161 mL/min for arteria mesenterica superior, vena portae hepatis, truncus coeliacus, and arteria hepatica, respectively (Table 2, Figure 6).

Blood Flow During Oral Glucose Ingestion

Over the 2‐h time course, oral water ingestion did not affect blood flow in the arteria mesenterica superior (p = 0.078) but blood flow in vena portae hepatis increased subtly (mean of 7.6%, p = 0.0003), whereas blood flow in truncus coeliacus (7.0%, p = 0.039) and in arteria hepatica (8.0%, borderline significant, p = 0.053) decreased slightly (Table 3, Figure 7). Oral glucose ingestion resulted in a large rise of blood flow in both arteria mesenterica superior (1145 ± 389 mL/min, p < 10−6) and vena portae hepatis (1581 ± 452 mL/min, p < 10−6) compared to baseline, respectively (Table 3, Figure 7A,B). For truncus coeliacus and arteria hepatica, oral glucose ingestion did not induce any changes from baseline in blood flow for the following 90 min (p = 0.56, p = 0.25, respectively) (Table 3, Figure 7C,D). Compared to water ingestion, blood flow after oral glucose ingestion was likewise higher in both arteria mesenterica superior and vena portae hepatis (p < 10−6, p < 10−6, respectively) but not in truncus coeliacus and arteria hepatica (p = 0.57, p = 0.76, respectively).

Discussion

We investigated the variation of PC‐MRI‐assessed repeated measurements of blood flow at 15 min intervals in four large splanchnic vessels in humans (arteria mesenterica superior, vena portae hepatis, truncus coeliacus, and arteria hepatica). We found the method to be both reproducible during the same session and between two observers. Between sessions, reproducibility declined, which is expected due to greater natural variation between days. This should be considered for longer study periods, for example long‐term intervention studies. Also, investigations of arteria hepatica were challenging, with higher CoVs, LoAs, and missing data compared to other vessels.

Additionally, we employed PC‐MRI to find robust and large postprandial increases in blood flow in arteria mesenterica superior and vena portae hepatis following oral glucose ingestion. The method CoVs, both for same sessions, between sessions and between observers, were small compared to the observed postprandial changes in blood flow, making the detection of postprandial changes robust and reliable. We also observed small changes in blood flow following placebo (by ingestion of 250‐mL water). We suspect that these changes may be a result of physiological adaptation to the long period of supine rest. Two hours of uninterrupted supine positioning may lead to central blood volume redistribution, which could result in a slight redistribution of blood flow in the abdominal vessels. However, position‐related blood flow changes in these vessels are not yet fully characterized. Additionally, as the fasting period extended throughout the scan session, the progressive absence of stimulus may cause a small gradual change in splanchnic blood flow.

Our results demonstrate that it is possible to measure four vessels in two scanning planes (arteria mesenterica superior and truncus coeliacus; vena portae hepatis, and arteria hepatica) and obtain reproducible, repeated splanchnic blood flow measures at short intervals (15 min) both during rest and after interventions such as nutrient ingestion. This provides the possibility of detailed, real‐time, interventional and observational studies of the postprandial state as well as progress the development towards clinical applications.

Quantification of Splanchnic Blood Flow

In the healthy participants (Groups A and C), we observed blood flow values comparable to other assessments using PC‐MRI [26]. However, in Group A, a homogeneous group of young, healthy men, the mean value was higher than previously reported. This is likely attributable to a few large individuals with very high blood flow. In Group C, comprising both healthy males and females, observed values were like those reported in other PC‐MRI studies. A previous study assessing blood flow by Doppler ultrasound flowmetry found that blood flow in arteria mesenterica superior of healthy individuals was 200–500 mL/min in the non‐absorptive state, with increases of 65%–250% occurring 15–60 min following nutrient ingestion, depending on meal volume and caloric intake [30]. Studies have also reported moderately lower blood flow values in truncus coeliacus and vena portae hepatis when measured by Doppler ultrasound flowmetry compared to our results and other PC‐MRI measurements [9, 26, 30].

In Group B, consisting of older persons with type 2 diabetes, averaged blood flow values could be higher compared to the younger healthy individuals in Groups A and C. Dysfunction in the regulation of splanchnic blood flow in persons with type 2 diabetes could be due to initial diabetic complications, such as autonomic neuropathy and endothelial dysfunction, which may impair normal vasomotor regulation of the mesenteric circulation [31]. Furthermore, the participants in Group B were older than those in the other groups, and age‐related changes in vascular compliance and arterial stiffness may alter the distribution of blood flow, potentially directing a greater proportion toward the splanchnic circulation. However, a recent study found no significant age‐related changes in mesenteric blood flow in subjects slightly younger than the patients in our Group B [24]. The impact of diabetes on splanchnic hemodynamics and the contribution of age‐related vascular changes warrant further investigation in future studies.

Advantages of Splanchnic Blood Flow Measures Based on PC‐MRI

In general, PC‐MRI can be widely applied for blood flow velocity measures with low variation and high reproducibility [19, 23, 32]. The good measurement geometry and anatomic navigation of MRI are also applicable for the abdominal area where the major vessels can be localized with high certainty. Repeated measurements at short intervals enable AUC (area under curve) calculations for evaluating the effects of, for example, pharmaceuticals. PC‐MRI‐based measurement of blood flow in the splanchnic vessels has several advantages compared with intravascular catheterization [15] or by using isotope‐based assessments of blood flow [33, 34]. PC‐MRI is non‐invasive as well as contrast‐ and radiation free and is therefore well suited for repeatable measures and crossover studies of several interventions. Compared to Doppler ultrasound flowmetry, which is also non‐invasive and accessible for both scientific and clinical use due to low costs and initial bedside results, PC‐MRI can assess all intra‐abdominal organs, deliver accurate multi‐dimensional images and measures, has no transcutaneous range limits, and lower interobserver variation [35].

We assessed the method on two different MRI scanners, from different vendors, and although CoVs were not comparable, the LoAMs that reflect the agreements with the mean were similar (Table 2). The main reason for the lower precision/higher CVs of the assessment by Philips Achieva 3 T MRI could be the larger voxel size but also other sequence parameters as well as the population characteristics were varied between the groups examined on each site (Table 1).

Diagnostics and pathological insights into several conditions can benefit from MRI‐based splanchnic blood flow measurements [25, 26]. Both vascular conditions such as chronic mesenteric ischemia, varices, and aneurysms as well as hepatic conditions (e.g., cirrhosis) or postoperative evaluation of endoleaks and stenoses following endovascular procedures could be non‐invasively identified and characterized. Based on our results of reproducibility over multiple days and with repeated scans on each day, we confirm that PC‐MRI could become a clinical tool to assess splanchnic blood flow measurements in both fasting and postprandial states.

Limitations of Splanchnic Blood Flow Measures Based on PC‐MRI

To rapidly repeat the blood flow measurements during oral glucose ingestion, we assessed two vessels in each plane. However, obtaining an adequate cross‐section of two vessels in one single 2D image can force a compromise in the quality and introduce partial volume contamination [36, 37]. When aiming for the cross‐sectional view of both vessels of interest, there is a risk of sacrificing the quality of one vessel to improve the measures of the other vessel. In some individuals, all vessels may not be visualized due to anatomical variations or small size and cannot be measured with this method. For arteria hepatica compared to the other three splanchnic vessels, we found a higher variation as well as many missing data points. This compromised data quality was due to the vessel not being localized in the same plane as vena portae hepatis or the displacement and decline in arteria hepatica volume when vena portae hepatis increased in volume in the postprandial state, as also reported when using Doppler ultrasound flowmetry [16].

Conclusion

PC‐MRI is a reproducible and feasible technique for measuring blood flow in truncus coeliacus, arteria mesenterica superior, and vena portae hepatis. The low CoV supports splanchnic PC‐MRI as both a scientifically and clinically useful tool.
