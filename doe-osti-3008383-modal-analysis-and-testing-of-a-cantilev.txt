Introduction
This report discusses modal analysis and testing of a cantilever beam including an introduction to
modal testing, simulation, analysis, and a comparison of results. An accompanying presentation
can be found from the LLNL CASIS website [Foster].

Modal testing and analysis are essential for characterizing the response of a structure during
vibration environments. Determining natural frequencies and mode shapes helps determine
whether resonances will be reached during operations and if so, how the structure will respond and
how the response can be tuned.

The goal of this work is to convey the basics of modal testing and analysis through a simple project.
This can be applied later to more complex, applicable structures. This includes a variety of work
including data collection, finite element analysis and data processing. For this project a cantilever
beam was chosen because it is a simple, well-characterized structure.

A single degree of freedom MCK system has a single natural frequency, also called a resonant
frequency. A natural frequency is the frequency at which the combination of the displacement and
acceleration vectors are maximized. Greater displacement means a greater spring force and a
greater acceleration means a greater inertial force (force due to the mass). In lower frequencies,
the system spends more time per cycle in a high displacement state and less time changing
direction. As the frequency starts to increase, the mass begins to change direction more, resulting
in increased acceleration. When the combination of both of these is at a maximum, it is called a
natural frequency. At this point, the spring and inertial forces do not counteract each other and the
entire input excitation is directed into the response of the system. The only force counteracting the
inertial and spring forces at a resonance is the damping. The damping does not have a significant
effect on the frequency that resonance occurs, but prevents the response from increasing infinitely.

The large forces due to resonance (inertial + spring) are the part of the reason resonance is of high
interest. At the resonance of a structure, a specific ‘mode shape’ occurs. This is seen in a standing
wave in a rope attached to a wall at one end. Shaken at a frequency that is not a resonance, the
rope consists of various changing peaks and valleys. However, at the first natural frequency, a
single peak of a standing wave appears. This can be thought of as the first mode shape of the rope.
A standing wave is a combination of two or more waves that have the same amplitude and
wavelength and therefore alternate between constructive and destructive interference. The result
looks like a stationary wave [UCF]. As the frequency of the rope excitation increases on its way
to the second natural frequency, the rope is disorderly again. Once the second natural frequency is
reached, the rope again features standing waves, this time with a peak, a valley and a node. Again,
this is similar to the second natural frequency of a structure and the corresponding mode shape.
This simple visualization is similar to what occurs in more complex ways in most systems of
interest.

Modal testing and analysis is the process of determining these natural frequencies and mode shapes
of a structure. Once mode shapes and natural frequencies are known, they can be used to adjust
the design of the structure. For instance, if there is a component that is extremely sensitive to
vibration effects and the structure is known to hit a resonance during operation, then the component
may be placed at a node of the relevant mode shape to minimize movement. Another option may
be to construct the structure from a stiffer (lower damping) material, such as steel instead of
aluminum. This will increase the natural frequency of that mode and could allow placement of the
component anywhere along the structure.

While the poles provide information about the natural frequencies, determining the mode shapes
requires additional analysis. Mode shapes describe the relative motion of different points on a
structure when it vibrates at a natural frequency.

For a single degree of freedom system, the mode shape is trivial, since there is only one mass that
can move. However, the concepts developed here extend directly to multiple degrees of freedom
(MDOF) systems where mode shapes become more complex and informative.

To explore modal analysis further, a cantilever beam is investigated in this report. A cantilever
beam is well characterized and a common subject for introductions to modal testing. An effort was
made to duplicate results seen in Peter Avitabile’s Modal Testing [Avitabile]. The first three, non-
torsional, modes of a cantilever beam are shown in Figure 3.

FEA Modeling

The cantilever beam is an example of a continuous system. Differential equations can be used to
model the responses of the beam due to outside forces. Using a commercial software package,

Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory 9

such as ANSYS, allows quick iteration on the model. This is especially important for more
complex systems where a small change could cause a significant change in the mathematical
model. The fidelity of the model is dependent on the node number, degrees of freedom (DOF) per
node, and the number of elements in the mesh. [Sinha]

A rectangular aluminum bar was used for the test article. A CAD model of the test article was
created in SolidWorks and then imported into ANSYS (see Figure 5). A mesh was applied in
ANSYS with one element through the thickness of the beam. A guide to running the analysis in
ANSYS can be found in Appendix A.

A fixed boundary condition was used on the face on one end of the beam. Each accelerometer was
modeled as a point mass in the FEA model.

Modal Testing
The simulation provided the accelerometer locations and then was used for eventual validation
data. This helps avoid placing accelerometers at nodal points. As mentioned earlier, a modal
impact hammer was used to excite the beam near the fixed boundary condition to prevent issues
with the hammer double tapping on the structure.

To create a cantilever beam from the metal bar stock, the end of the bar was clamped to a lab bench
(Figure 10).

To compare the test and analysis results, the frequencies and mode shapes are extracted from the
test data. One way to do this is to use the stability diagram. If the mode is stable through multiple
orders both in frequency and damping, it is likely a real mode. As seen below, the three vertical
modes are easy to locate visually, but are also stable both in frequency and damping. Selecting
those modes and using an AutoMAC can be used as a comparison. The Modal Assurance Criterion
(MAC) is a way to check for independence between multiple modes and the AutoMAC checks
modes against themselves. Ideally, they should be completely independent. If they are dependent,
it is possible duplicate is a duplicate mode or mathematical (not real) mode. As observed here, you
can see that the on-diagonal matrix elements are one, which shows the modes are dependent on
themselves. Between different modes there is basically no dependence except for expected error.

Conclusion
In conclusion, using this well-known modal test article of a cantilever beam illustrates the basics
of modal testing. These same techniques and tools can then be applied to more complex test articles
with high confidence. Future work includes using tools such as FEMtools, which is a commercial
software package, to predict the sensor and excitation locations. Using a thicker beam or different
material that allows for collecting quality data at three excitation points would provide a more
balanced modal matrix and so better demonstrate nodal points.
