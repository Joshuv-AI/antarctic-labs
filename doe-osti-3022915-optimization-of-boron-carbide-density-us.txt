Optimization of boron carbide density using direct current sintering for use as
 sputtering sources at the National Ignition Facility

 Matthew Wehler, Bruce Yang, Matthew Koelle, James Cahill, Wyatt Du Frane

 SULI summer internship 2025

Several experimental trials were conducted to determine optimal sintering parameters and

compositions for purchased boron carbide powder using spark plasma sintering/direct current

sintering. Sputtering targets used to create ablators for the National Ignition Facility have been unable

to survive post processing procedures suggesting that in-house fabrication may yield better results due

to extended control. This was accomplished through marginal testing of hold temperature, heating

rate, target pressure, pressure onset, dwell time, atomic percent carbon black, graphite, and boron to

carbon ratio of the powder. Compositional tests involving carbon black, graphite, and boron involved

mixing additive powders with boron carbide using a wet mixing technique involving water and then

dried overnight within a furnace. The optimal sintering parameters were found to be a hold

temperature of 2000 ˚C, a heating rate of 300 ˚C/min, a target pressure of 50 MPa applied in line with

typical parameters, and a dwell time of 5 min. Atomic percentages of 0.4% carbon black, graphite, and

a boron to carbon ratio of 6:1 yielded the largest relative density for each respective trial. Greater

control of the density and composition allows for more dynamic testing of ablators and sputter sources

at the National Ignition Facility and therefore more advancements towards fusion research

I. Introduction
 Inertial confinement fusion (ICF) is a promising new source of energy that uses high energy
lasers and small pellets of deuterium-tritium fuel to reach ignition conditions.1 The National Ignition
Facility (NIF) uses a form of indirect drive ICF that uses x-rays generated from irradiating a gold
hohlraum to press the fuel pellet.1 The x-rays cause the outer layer of the fuel pellet to ablate,
creating an implosion that presses the fuel together to generate fusion.1 There has been an interest in
using boron carbide (B4C) as a next generation ablator due to its amorphous character and higher
density compared to other materials used.1
 The ablator is manufactured through the DC magnetron sputtering of a B4C source onto silicon
mandrels to generate ultra-thick films (>10 µm). Purchased sputtering sources have generated
weakened coatings due to impurities that are unable to survive the post-processing procedures
necessary to create the perfect ICF fuel pellet. More consistent and dense sources can instead be
manufactured in-house, offering more control over the sputtering sources.
 Better B4C sputtering sources are to be made using the spark plasma sintering/direct current
sintering (SPS/DCS) device. SPS/DCS allows the notoriously difficult B4C to reach higher densities
than typically possible through typical sintering methods.2,3 B4C has also been known to improve in
density with the addition of a sintering aid, such as carbon or boron.3,4 In-house manufacturing of
the sputtering targets gives researchers the ability to alter the sintering and composition of the B4C
powder to achieve an optimal density for sputtering sources. This project aims to optimize the
processing parameters of B4C to fabricate dense, consistent sputtering sources for in-house
production via SPS/DCS and altering the composition of the powder.

III. Materials and Methods
A. Sintering Procedure

 The manufactured B4C powders have been sintered using the Thermal Technology LLC DCS-25
system. The powder samples were prepared within graphite dies (20.5 mm diam by 40 mm height)
and punches (20.0 mm diam by 20 mm height). Die set interiors were lined with graphite foil to
improve conductivity and minimize contamination between runs. The die set was then compacted
using a hand press to apply 1000 pounds of force and ensure equal compact through the flipping of
the sample. Compacted die sets are wrapped in graphite felt insulation, loaded into the DCS system,
and sintered based upon the parameter being tested.

 3

 Hold temperature, heating rate, target pressure, pressure onset, and dwell time are the
parameters capable of being tested on the DCS system. Each parameter was changed from the
default sintering method (Figure 1a.), but pressure onset tests required different sintering profiles
entirely (Figures 1b & 1c). It was determined that raising the hold temperature above 2000˚C or the
heating rate above 300˚C/min could run the risk of melting the sample when altering the default
parameters. Similarly, target pressure was not raised above 50 MPa to prevent breaking within the
die set up.

B. Mixing Compositional Tests

 The compositional tests were batched using wet mixing with water and Triton X-100 as a
surfactant. The samples were mixed using a THINKY mixer and sonicated before being set to dry

 overnight within an 80˚C furnace. The hardened powders were crushed using an agate mortar and
 pestle for sintering. Carbon black nanoparticles, graphite, and amorphous boron were used in the
 compositional tests. Carbon and graphite samples were tested at 0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 atomic
 percents (Table 1a) and the boron samples were tested at boron to carbon ratios of 4:1, 5:1, 6:1, 7:1,
 8:1, and 9:1 (Table 1b). Each set of compositional trials were accompanied by a test done with no
 additive to use as a control for the test. Compositional tests were sintered at standard conditions.

 C. Density Measurement

 All sample densities were measured using volumetric methods after grit blasting any remaining
 graphite foil. The average height and diameter of each sample was found and used with the mass to
 calculate the density. Archimedes density testing was avoided due to the risk of water to seep into
 sample pores and yield invalid results.

 IV. Results and Discussion

 The sintered densities of all trials are showcased within the density-parameter graphs in Figure 2.
 A table of all data points and their relative densities is also available in the appendix under Table II.
 The density graphs for target pressure, dwell time, and hold temperature match well with the graphs
 by S. Hayun et al.2 It has proven difficult to maintain a constant baseline sample across trial runs,
 however. Baseline runs were often done at or near the time of a sintering trial and used within the
 respective graph for that trial, but in the interest of maintain consistency, the baseline was
 determined based upon which density fit the most data. Other relative densities found for the typical
 sintering procedure included: 95.9, 81.6, 86.9, 85.8, and 87.5.

 5

 Many data points were also redone various times due to unlikely results. Such discrepancies can
occur from issues within the SPS/DCS, processing, and subtle changes between dies used for each
sample run. The subtleties of these changes are due to the use of a driven current to sinter the
powders through joule heating. Very small changes can cause the current flow to be different from a
previous run. Such differences were nullified by recording which dies were used for each run and
being mindful of any changes between SPS/DCS uses.

V. Conclusion

 Optimal sintering conditions and compositional mixtures have been determined using the DCS-
25. The optimal sintering parameters for the as received boron carbide powder used include a hold
temperature of 2000 ˚C, a heating rate of 300 ˚C/min, a target pressure of 50 MPa applied in line
with typical parameters, and a dwell time of 5 min. Atomic percentages of 0.4% carbon black and
graphite resulted in the largest relative density within their respective compositional tests while a
boron to carbon ratio of 6:1 resulted in the largest relative density among other boron to carbon

 7

ratios. These compositional tests reveal the relative densities to expect when altering the
composition for altered boron carbide ablator tests within the National Ignition Facility but may not
be used within creating the densest possible sample. Achieving the densest possible sample allows
for more control within the creation of sputtering sources for ablators and allows the density to be
tailored to the needs of target fabrication.
