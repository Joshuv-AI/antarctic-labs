Supramolecular Nrf2-activating peptide nanotherapeutics enable deep retinal penetration and RPE targeting for dry age-related macular degeneration
Authors: Zihan Zheng, Guangyu Rong, Gengji Li, Yujiao Zhai, Yu Liang, Mingsu Shi, Jiaqiu Huang, Yihan Zhang, Xinyi Zhao, Qiao Zhuo, Jiaxu Hong, Weiyi Xia, Yiyun Cheng, Chen Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of blindness that currently lacks effective therapies. Restoring redox homeostasis in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) via the Keap1-Nrf2 axis offers a compelling precise therapeutic strategy. While peptide-based protein-protein interaction (PPI) inhibitors are highly specific for this axis, the delivery to the deep RPE layer remains a formidable challenge, typically restricted by electrostatic entrapment within the vitreous matrix or limited transretinal penetration. Herein, we developed a novel, mechanism-driven, supramolecular peptide nanotherapeutic (FNBP) engineered to bypass these barriers and reach the RPE layer. Driven by distinct fluorophilic self-assembly, FNBP spontaneously organizes into bio-inert nanostructures that evade vitreous adhesion and exhibit superior transretinal diffusivity and accumulation to the RPE layer. Upon RPE internalization, the assembly leverages its physicochemical properties to trigger efficient cytosolic release, allowing the payload to disrupt the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction, thereby enabling potent Nrf2 activation and restoring redox homeostasis. In a murine model of retinal degeneration, a single intravitreal injection provided sustained preservation of retinal integrity for 28 days, significantly outperforming conventional peptide formulations and clinical supplements. This work offers a precise, mechanism-based solution for RPE-targeted regulation therapies and establishes a paradigm for designing peptide nanotherapeutics that circumvent the intrinsic limitations of retinal drug delivery.Keywords: Dry age-related macular degeneration, Peptide delivery, Deep retinal penetration, Fluoroalkylation, Keap1/Nrf2 pathway, RPE targeting

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of blindness worldwide, and is projected to affect 288 million individuals by 2040 [1]. AMD presents in two forms: dry AMD (non-neovascular) and wet AMD (neovascular) [2]. While anti-vascular agents effectively manage wet AMD, dry AMD, which constitutes nearly 90% of all cases, lacks effective therapies. Although the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recently approved two intravitreal complement inhibitors for advanced dry AMD, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) declined authorization due to marginal functional benefits and safety concerns [3]. Pathologically, the retina is highly vulnerable to oxidative stress driven by cumulative light exposure and high oxygen tension [[4], [5], [6]]. Persistent oxidative insult first induces apoptosis of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), which in turn leads to secondary degeneration of overlying photoreceptors [7,8]. Oxidative stress is therefore widely recognized as a central driver of AMD pathogenesis [9], and restoring redox homeostasis is regarded as the fundamental strategy for disease management. At present, clinical antioxidative management mainly relies on lifestyle modifications and broad-spectrum antioxidant supplements (AREDS/AREDS2), which can reduce the relative risk of progression from intermediate to advanced AMD by about 25%. However, these non-targeted interventions provide only partial protection against disease progression. This underscores a critical need for mechanism-based, cell-directed strategies that can effectively restore intracellular redox homeostasis within the RPE–photoreceptor unit, ideally through precise delivery and sustained activation of intrinsic cytoprotective pathways (see Scheme 1).

Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2), the master regulator of cellular redox homeostasis across all tissues, offers a compelling therapeutic avenue [10]. Under basal conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytoplasm by Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1), an adaptor protein that facilitates its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [11]. Upon oxidative stress, activated Nrf2 dissociates from the Keap1 complex, translocates to the nucleus, and orchestrates the expression of cytoprotective genes. Consistent with this, previous studies have shown that Nrf2 deficiency accelerates, whereas Nrf2 overexpression mitigates, retinal degeneration [12,13]. However, current activation strategies face important limitations. Small-molecule activators typically act by covalent modification of reactive cysteine residues on Keap1, thereby indirectly disrupting the Keap1–Nrf2 interaction. This non-selective electrophilic modification can lead to off-target effects and perturb other Keap1-dependent functions, raising safety concerns [14,15]. In parallel, gene therapy carries inherent safety concerns regarding immunogenicity and supraphysiological overexpression control. Therefore, directly targeting the Keap1-Nrf2 PPI interface to mimic the endogenous activation pathway is a promising alternative approach. Specifically, peptide-based PPI therapeutics bearing sequences homologous to the Keap1-binding motifs of Nrf2 can competitively bind Keap1 and displace endogenous Nrf2, thereby enabling controlled Nrf2 activation with potentially higher target specificity and a more physiological safety profile.

Despite the conceptual advantages of peptide-based PPI inhibition, the effective delivery of such biologics to the posterior eye segment, particularly the deep RPE layer, remains a formidable challenge. Therapeutic efficacy is severely compromised by poor metabolic stability and the inability to traverse dense physiological barriers, most notably the vitreous matrix and the inner limiting membrane (ILM) [16,17]. For meaningful clinical benefit, peptide therapeutics must reach the posterior segment, and also achieve adequate accumulation within the RPE, the primary site of AMD pathogenesis. To reach the RPE layer, the biologics have to first navigate the vitreous, a negatively charged hydrogel composed of hyaluronic acid and collagen that readily entraps cationic particles [18]. Subsequently, they must penetrate the dense ILM meshwork and traverse multiple distinct retinal layers before reaching the RPE layer. Even upon arrival, the highly phagocytic RPE, enriched in acidic endosomes, tends to sequester and degrade internalized peptides, thereby preventing their cytosolic delivery and limiting their ability to modulate intracellular PPIs. While cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), such as cationic trans-activator of transcription (TAT) peptide, have been employed to facilitate cellular uptake across these barriers, however, their clinical utility may be hindered by non-specific electrostatic entrapment within the anionic vitreous matrix, insufficient endosomal escape capacity, and non-specific cytotoxicity [19].

To overcome these challenges, we turned our attention to fluoroalkylation, a robust technology increasingly prominent in nanomedicine. This approach exploits the unique physicochemical properties of fluorine. Specifically, its low polarizability and surface energy endow nanoparticles with a bio-inert surface, which has been shown to effectively resist non-specific protein adsorption [20]. Furthermore, the hydrophobic and lipophobic properties of fluorinated chains facilitates a "stealth-like" transport mechanism, enabling superior deep-tissue penetration compared to conventional lipid-based carriers [21]. Once internalized by cells, fluorous components can destabilize endosomal membranes under acidic conditions, triggering efficient endosomal escape and cytosolic release of bioactive cargo [22]. Extrapolating from these remarkable properties, we hypothesized that the "fluorine effect" would be particularly well suited for ocular peptide delivery, which could solve vitreous entrapment, achieve ILM penetration and endosomal escape. Theoretically, such fluoroalkylated nanoparticles could thus overcome multiple ocular barriers, and simultaneously facilitate targeted delivery to the RPE and maximize intracellular bioavailability of therapeutic peptides.

In this study, we present a fluoroalkylation-enabled peptide nanoplatform (designated as FNBP) that precisely disrupts the Keap1-Nrf2 PPI for the treatment of dry AMD. By conjugating the specific Keap1-binding peptide (NBP: LQLDEETGEFLPIQ) with a fluorous tag, we harness fluorophilic interactions to drive the self-assembly of stable nanoparticles that effectively navigate ocular barriers and accumulate within the RPE. Mechanistically, FNBP functions as a direct competitive inhibitor at the Keap1–Nrf2 interface, thereby liberating Nrf2 to restore cellular redox homeostasis and mitigate oxidative damage. In a sodium iodate (SI) induced murine model of retinal degeneration, a single intravitreal injection of FNBP demonstrated remarkable durability, providing sustained neuroprotection for 28 days. This efficacy significantly outperformed both conventional TAT-modified peptides and standard AREDS supplementation. Ultimately, this study establishes FNBP as a safer, delivery-optimized peptide nanotherapeutic capable of halting retinal degeneration, while also introducing a versatile fluorous peptide delivery platform for the treatment of posterior segment ocular diseases.

Materials and methods

Materials

Peptides were synthesized by GL Biochem. (Shanghai, China). 1H, 1H, 2H, 2H-perfluoro-1-octanethiol (13F-SH) and sodium iodate (SI) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Colchicine, 2,2′-dithiodipyridine (DTDP), methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD), filipin, and chlorpromazine (CPZ) were obtained from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Simulated vitreous humor was obtained from Mimic FluiX (Shanghai, China). LysoTracker Red, TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit, and H2DCFDA were obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). CCK-8 assay Kit was obtained from Meilunbio (Dalian, China). ML385, Diroximel fumarate (DMF), and PMX-53 were obtained from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). The AREDS supplements were purchased from Bausch + Lomb (Rochester, N.Y, USA). Primary antibodies for HO-1, Rhodopsin, β-actin, RPE65, and p-Nrf2 were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). Primary antibody for Nrf2 was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Primary antibody of Recoverin was obtained from Fine Biotech Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Glutamine Synthetase primary antibody, ZO-1 primary antibody and secondary antibodies were procured from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

Synthesis of fluorous tag

A solution of 200 mg (0.53 mmol) 13F-SH and 347.7 mg (1.6 mmol) DTDP was prepared by dissolving each compound in 1.0 mL and 2.5 mL of methanol, respectively. Following this step, DTDP was reacted with 13F-SH (3 equivalents) in solution at 4 °C. Following a 12-h stirring period, the reaction mixture was subjected to solvent removal through rotary evaporation. Column chromatography purification was performed with a 30:1 mixture of dichloromethane (DCM) and methanol as eluent. Ultimately, the desired fluorous tag (designated as 13F tag) was isolated in the form of a pale-yellow oil, achieving a yield of 76.2% (150.3 mg).

Synthesis of fluorous-tagged peptides

The fluorous-tagged NBP peptides were synthesized following the published procedure [22]. The reaction was initiated by dissolving NBP (10 mg) in methanol (2.0 mL), then adding 13F tag (3 equiv in 1.0 mL DCM) dropwise. The reaction proceeded at 4 °C under inert conditions for 6 h. Subsequently, the reaction was quenched by adding it to 30 mL of ether. The resulting white precipitate, representing the crude product, was collected and further purified using the preparative high-performance liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) (SHIMADZU, LC20A, Japan). The fluorous-tagged FITC-NBP was synthesized using the same methodology.

Stability assessment in simulated vitreous

To mimic the physiological environment of the posterior eye segment, the colloidal stability of FNBP was evaluated in simulated vitreous humor. FNBP nanoparticles were incubated in simulated vitreous humor at 37 °C under gentle shaking. At scheduled time intervals, samples were collected, and the particle size distribution was analyzed via dynamic light scattering (DLS) to monitor potential aggregation.

Reduction sensitivity of FNBP

The in vitro cleavage of disulfide bond in FNBP in the presence of dithiothreitol (DTT) was investigated by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). 1.0 mg/mL FNBP was incubated with 0.1 mM DTT at 37 °C for different time intervals (5 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, and 3 h) before HPLC analysis. NBP (1.0 mg/mL), FNBP alone and 0.1 mM DTT solution were tested as parallel controls.

Cell culture

ARPE-19 cells and 661W cells were sourced from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). For ARPE-19 cells, cultivation was conducted in DMEM/F-12 medium (Gibco, USA, containing 10% FBS), alongside a 1% mixture of streptomycin and penicillin (Gibco, USA). 10 mM SI was given for 12 h to establish a SI-induced oxidative stress model. For 661W cells, cultivation was conducted in DMEM medium (Gibco, USA, containing 10% FBS), alongside a 1% mixture of streptomycin and penicillin (Gibco, USA). Cell culture was performed at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

Cytotoxicity assay

For CCK-8 assay, ARPE-19 cells or 661W cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated to 80% confluence overnight. The initial culture medium was then removed, and the cells were incubated with various concentrations of peptides in serum-free medium for 6 h. Following this treatment, the peptide-containing medium was discarded, and cells were washed with PBS. Cells were then provided with fresh complete medium and incubated for an additional 18 h. Cell proliferation was evaluated by standard CCK-8 protocol, with untreated cells serving as the 100% viability control.

Cellular uptake mechanism investigation

ARPE-19 cells were subjected to preincubation with several endocytosis inhibitors (5 μg/mL colchicine, 1 μg/mL filipin, 1 μg/mL MβCD, and 2 μg/mL CPZ) in serum-free medium for 30 min. After that, FITC-FNBP or FITC-NBP (10 μM) was added to the well, and the cells were co-incubated at 37 °C for 6h. Parallel control cells were cultured at 4 °C for 6 h to suppress energy-dependent endocytosis. After the 6 h incubation period, the medium was removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS to eliminate extracellularly bound fluorescence. Images were captured using a Zeiss M2 fluorescence microscope (Germany) and analyzed for intracellular fluorescence intensity via ImageJ.

Endosome escape assay

Endosomes were stained to determine whether FNBP can achieve successful endosome escape. ARPE-19 cells were incubated with FITC-FNBP, FITC-NBP, or PBS in serum-free medium for 6 h. After incubation, the medium was removed. Cells were washed with PBS, and then stained with LysoTracker Red in serum-free medium at 37 °C for 30 min to label late endosomes and lysosomes. After 30 min, fluorescence microscopy images were obtained following standard fixation (4% PFA, 10 min) and DAPI labeling.

Evaluation of cellular ROS level

H2DCFDA probe was employed to measure the ROS level in ARPE-19 cells. The cells were treated with FNBP (10 μM) and SI (10 mM) for 12 h in serum-free medium, and then were washed with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with H2DCFDA (10 μM) in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37 °C. After probe incubation, cells were washed again with PBS, and either collected for flow cytometry (Beckman, Indianapolis, USA) or observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks, 20-22 g) were sourced from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). The study protocols involving animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Eye & Ear Nose and Throat (ENT) Hospital, Fudan University (Approval No. 2023DW040).

Ex vivo retina penetration assay

The trans-retinal transport behavior of FITC-NBP and FITC-FNBP was evaluated using a Franz diffusion cell model with fresh porcine retinal tissue. Intact retinal tissue containing the ILM was mounted between the donor and receptor compartments of the diffusion cell. FITC-NBP and FITC-FNBP solutions were added to the donor side, while the receptor compartment was filled with physiological buffer. The system was maintained at a constant temperature with continuous stirring. Aliquots were collected at predetermined time intervals up to 48 h. The cumulative transport efficiency across the retinal tissue was calculated and plotted over time.

In vivo targeting and retina distribution

To test the penetrability of FITC-FNBP and FITC-NBP, both of them were administered intravitreally. After 4 days, the eyes of mouse were collected, and cryosections of the retina were used to examine the distribution of FITC-FNBP and FITC-NBP with DAPI labeling. To investigate the in vivo distribution of FITC-FNBP and FITC-NBP, the fluorescence signal was monitored with a fluorescence microscope and quantified by ImageJ.

Investigation of retinal penetration mechanism

To explore the biological mechanism underlying the transretinal transport of the nanoparticles, the spatial correlation between FITC-FNBP and Müller cells was evaluated via immunofluorescence co-localization. Mice were anesthetized, and 1.5 μL of FITC-FNBP was administered via intravitreal injection. At 6 h post-injection, mice were sacrificed, and the enucleated eyeballs were fixed and then cryosectioned. A primary antibody against Glutamine Synthetase (GS) (11037-2-AP, 1:100) was used to label Müller cells, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope to analyze the co-localization of FITC-FNBP and Müller cell across the retinal layers.

Animal experimental design

Before the main efficacy study, a preliminary optimization experiment was performed to determine the optimal dosing regimen. For the therapeutic evaluation, mice were randomized into the following eight groups: (i) Control (Healthy mice injected with PBS), (ii) Untreated (SI injection + intravitreal PBS), (iii) AREDS (SI + daily oral gavage of AREDS solution), (iv) PMX-53(SI + intravitreal PMX-53), (v) DMF (SI + intravitreal DMF), (vi) NBP (SI + intravitreal NBP), (vii) TNBP (SI + intravitreal TNBP), and (viii) FNBP (SI + intravitreal FNBP).

To induce retinal degeneration, SI was dissolved in PBS (5 mg/mL) and administered via tail vein injection at a dose of 20 mg/kg [23]. For all intravitreal (IVT) treatment groups, a single injection (1.5 μL) was administered 24 h prior to the SI induction. The therapeutic formulations were prepared as follows: the peptide agents (PMX-53, NBP, TNBP, and FNBP) were dissolved in PBS and administered at a dose of 0.2 nmol/g mouse body weight. A stock solution of DMF (10 mM in DMSO) was diluted 100-fold with PBS immediately prior to IVT injection. For the AREDS treatment, the supplement was diluted 500-fold in PBS and emulsified via sonication to ensure homogeneity, and a volume of 100 μL of this emulsion was administered daily via oral gavage. To evaluate the longitudinal therapeutic efficacy, fundus photography (OPTO-RIS system, Optoprobe, UK) and optical coherence tomography (OCT, ISOCT system, Optoprobe, UK) were performed at Days 7, 14, 21, and 28 post-SI induction. Key quantitative parameters, including retinal thickness and outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness, were measured to assess structural integrity.

Sample collection was conducted at two distinct time points. For short-term study (Day 7), a subset of mice was euthanized at Day 7. Eyeballs were harvested for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunofluorescent staining (ZO-1, RPE65, Rhodopsin, Recoverin, dihydroethidium, TUNEL, and p-Nrf2). Additionally, fresh retinal tissues were collected for Western blotting (p-Nrf2, Nrf2, HO-1) and qPCR analysis (Nqo1, Gstm, Gpx4, Il-6, Il-8). For long-term safety and efficacy study (Day 28), the remaining mice were monitored until Day 28, after which they were euthanized, and eyeballs were collected for H&E staining to evaluate long-term structural preservation.

Immunofluorescent staining

Mice were euthanized, followed by enucleation of the eyeballs and fixation in 4% PFA for 2 h. Following dehydration in 30% sucrose solution overnight at 4 °C, the eyeballs were embedded in OCT compound and cryosectioned. After 1-h permeabilization in PBS containing 0.25% Triton X-100 at 37 °C, 5% goat serum was used to block sections for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated for 12 h at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-RPE65 (ab231782, 1:200), anti-Rhodopsin (Ab221664, 1:500), anti-Recoverin (FNab07225, 1:200), and anti-p-Nrf2 (ab76026, 1:200). After washing with PBS for three times, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody CoraLite® Plus 488 (RGAR002, 1:1000) or CoraLite® Plus 594 (RGAR004,1:1000) for 2 h. After that, the sections were stained with DAPI and analyzed by a fluorescence microscope to determine the expression level of the relevant markers.

For RPE whole-mount staining, the eyeballs were fixed in 4% PFA for 1 h, followed by enucleation and removal of the neural retina. Subsequently, RPE complexes were blocked, and permeabilized as mentioned above. Afterwards, RPE complexes were incubated with anti-ZO-1 (21773-1-AP, 1:2000) overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes, the RPE complexes were treated with CoraLite® Plus 488 (RGAR002, 1:1000) for 2 h at room temperature and flat-mounted on microscope slides. Imaged by a fluorescence microscope.

After discarding the culture medium, PBS was used to wash the cells for three times before undergoing the same fixation, permeabilization and blocking steps. Afterwards, cells were incubated with anti-p-Nrf2 (ab76026, 1:200) at 4 °C. Following 12-h incubation, cells were incubated with CoraLite® Plus 488-conjugated secondary antibody (RGAR002, 1:1000 dilution) for 2 h. Before imaging, cells were stained with DAPI.

Western blot analysis

Retinal tissues and cultured cells were lysed in RIPA buffer. After sonication for 1 min, the lysates were centrifuged, and the protein concentrations in the supernatants were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0012). For automated Western blot analysis, equal amounts of protein were analyzed using the Jess Automated Western Blot System with a 12–230 kDa Separation Module (ProteinSimple, Bio-Techne, USA). Protein separation, immobilization, blocking, antibody incubation, and intensity signal detection were performed automatically in capillary cartridges according to the manufacturer's protocol. Anti-p-Nrf2 (Abcam, ab76026) and anti-β-actin (Abcam, ab8226) were used at a dilution of 1:100. The results were analyzed using Compass for Simple Western software (ProteinSimple). The p-Nrf2 signal was quantified based on the peak area and normalized to β-actin. For conventional Western blot analysis, equal amounts of protein were separated by gradient SDS-PAGE and electrophoretically transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with QuickBlock buffer (Beyotime, P0252) for 10 min, the membranes were incubated at 4 °C for 12 h with anti-p-Nrf2 (Abcam, ab76026; 1:1000), anti-Nrf2 (CST, 12721; 1:1000), anti-HO-1 (Abcam, ab68477; 1:1000), or anti-β-actin (Abcam, ab8226; 1:1000). After three washes, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies (SA00001-2 or SA00001-1; 1:30,000) for 1 h.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Cell samples and whole retinas were used to extract total RNA with the EZ-press RNA Purification Kit (EZB, B0004DP). The reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, R433-01) was used for cDNA synthesis. qRT-PCR was conducted using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711-02) on a StepOne Plus System (Applied Biosystems) with the primers given in Table S1. Data were analyzed adjusted to β-actin.

Biosafety assessment

The mice were monitored by electroretinography (Espion Electrophysiology System, Diagnosys LLC, USA), fundus, OCT images, and intraocular pressure (IOP) from Day −1 to Day 14. Finally, the mice were euthanized and their blood, eyeballs, and major organs were collected. Their blood samples were for blood cells, liver and kidney function tests. The eyes and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were removed and fixed. Following dehydration, the samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, stained with H&E, and subsequently imaged by a microscope.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative image and statistical analyses were performed using ImageJ and SPSS (IBMCorp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) respectively. Data are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) or means ± standard error of mean (SEM). Sample sizes were n = 6 (biological replicates) for in vivo studies, and n = 3 (biological replicates) for in vitro assays, and each biological replicate is with three technical replicates. Comparisons between two groups were evaluated using a two-tailed Student's t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Multi-group comparisons were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. Differences with p > 0.05 were considered not statistically significant (ns)

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the FNBP

To fabricate the engineered peptide nanoparticle, a fluorous tag was conjugated to the N-terminus of the NBP peptide via a disulfide bond to synthesize FNBP [22]. Successful FNBP synthesis was confirmed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) purity assessment and electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) mass verification (Fig. 1A and B). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements demonstrated that the fluorous-tagged peptide self-assembled into uniform nanoparticles with a narrow size distribution, characterized by a low polydispersity index (PDI <0.3, Fig. 1C). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further confirmed the uniform spherical morphology of FNBP nanoparticles (Fig. 1D). The stable supramolecular structure of FNBP was mainly driven by fluorophilic interactions. Different from traditional hydrophobic or electrostatic assemblies, fluorophilic effect endows nanoparticles with superior thermodynamic and kinetic stability in complex biological fluids. Considering the microenvironment of the vitreous matrix, the colloidal stability of nanoparticles is essential for ocular drug delivery. Therefore, we evaluated the particle size and dispersity of FNBP in simulated vitreous humor over 24 h. DLS measurements showed that FNBP maintained a stable hydrodynamic size and dispersity without significant aggregation (Fig. S1A). Such favorable stability is primarily attributed to the low surface energy and polarizability of the fluorous moiety. This property reduces non-specific protein adsorption and mitigates electrostatic entrapment by the polyanionic vitreous matrix, which is commonly observed in cationic carriers like TAT-modified peptides. In addition, the disulfide bond enabled reduction-responsive cleavage of FNBP. DTT-triggered dissociation verified the controllable release of NBP cargo (Fig. S2A). While FNBP retains the intrinsic ability to attach to Keap1 residues even with the fluorous tag, its subsequent reductive disassembly provides an additional functional advantage. Ultimately, this dual mechanism, intrinsic peptide activity coupled with responsive intracellular disassembly, ensures robust and sustained biological applications. Furthermore, the biocompatibility of FNBP was evaluated by CCK-8 assay. Neither NBP nor FNBP exhibited obvious cytotoxicity in ARPE-19 or 661W cell lines at various concentrations, demonstrating excellent biosafety for ocular application (Fig. S3A and S3B).

Cellular uptake and transcellular mechanism of FNBP nanoparticles

Given the established role of RPE dysfunction as the primary pathogenic driver in AMD, we first analyzed the intracellular internalization of FNBP in RPE cells. ARPE-19 cells were treated with FITC-labeled NBP (FITC-NBP) or fluorous-tagged FITC-NBP (FITC-FNBP) in a dose-dependent for 6 h and time-dependent (1-8 h, 10 μM) manner. Fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry confirmed that FITC-FNBP exhibited obvious dose- and time-dependent cellular uptake (Fig. 1E and F; S4A). Its intracellular fluorescence intensity was significantly higher than that of unmodified FITC-NBP. Effective endosomal escape of cargo peptides is essential for intracellular protein-protein interaction modulation. To determine whether the fluorous-tagged peptides could achieve effective endosomal escape, a critical challenge for molecular biologics targeting intracellular protein-protein interactions (PPIs), LysoTracker Red was used to label acidic organelles. We incubated ARPE-19 cells with FITC-FNBP, FITC-NBP, or a PBS control. In the FITC-FNBP group, we first observed abundant intracellular FITC fluorescence, indicating the successful internalization. Crucially, minimal colocalization was observed between FITC-FNBP and lysosomes. By contrast, FITC-NBP group showed negligible intracellular fluorescence (Fig. 1G). These findings corroborate our earlier results, confirming that fluorous-tagged peptides possess superior intracellular penetration compared to their non-tagged counterparts. Furthermore, the lack of spatial overlap with acidic compartments firmly demonstrates the successful endosomal escape of FITC-FNBP.

To elucidate the internalization pathway of NBP and FNBP in ARPE-19 cells, various endocytosis inhibitors were applied before FITC-FNBP or FITC-NBP treatment [[24], [25], [26]]. The uptake of FITC-FNBP was significantly suppressed at 4 °C or in the presence of methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD, lipid raft disruption), whereas Filipin (an inhibitor of caveolin-mediated endocytosis), chlorpromazine (CPZ, clathrin inhibition), and colchicine (inhibitor of the tubulin pathway) had only marginal effects. These findings indicate that the FITC-FNBP enters ARPE-19 cells mainly through energy-dependent lipid raft-mediated endocytosis (Fig. 1H and S5A). Other endocytic pathways may have exerted negligible inhibitory effects. Consistent with its poor cell-penetrating property, FITC-NBP showed extremely low cellular signals under all intervention conditions (Fig. S5B). Lipid raft-mediated endocytosis commonly bypasses direct lysosomal degradation, thereby helping to preserve the structural integrity of the peptide payload prior to cytosolic release. Collectively, the fluorous tag promotes the cytosolic delivery and endosome escape of NBP, establishing the necessary conditions for its subsequent cellular effects.

We further investigated the cell-selective uptake behavior of FNBP in retinal cell lines. ARPE-19 and 661W cells were incubated with gradient concentrations of FNBP. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that ARPE-19 cells exhibited a 2-fold higher uptake efficiency compared to 661W cells (Fig. 1I). This suggests that RPE cells possess a stronger affinity for FNBP, possibly attributed to the innate phagocytic characteristics of the RPE. This preferential uptake is advantageous for targeting the RPE layer, the primary site of initial damage in dry AMD.

Before evaluating in vivo performance, we first assessed the trans-retinal permeability of our formulation using an ex vivo fresh porcine retina Franz diffusion cell model. The intact porcine retina was isolated and mounted to separate a donor and a receiver compartment. FITC-FNBP or FITC-NBP was applied to the ganglion cell layer (GCL) side, and the fluorescence intensity of the permeation fluid on the opposite side was monitored over time. As expected, FITC-FNBP exhibited a higher permeation rate and greater cumulative trans-retinal transport than the FITC-NBP. This result demonstrates the superior tissue penetration capability of fluorous-tagged peptides across biological barriers (Fig. S6A and S6B).

Next, we sought to investigate whether FNBP possesses this transretinal capability in vivo. We intravitreally injected FITC-NBP or FITC-FNBP in mice and subsequently analyzed the distribution of the nanoparticles across retinal layers. Retinal frozen sections were stained with the RPE-specific marker RPE65. As shown in Fig. 1J and K, difference in retinal distribution was observed. The FITC-NBP was barely detectable in the RPE layer, likely due to electrostatic sequestration by the vitreous matrix and superficial retinal structures. In contrast, FITC-FNBP successfully crossed the ILM barrier and navigated through the dense neural retina, achieving distinct accumulation in the deep RPE layer.

To further elucidate the biological mechanism underlying this efficient transretinal transport, we investigated the spatial relationship between the FITC-FNBP nanoparticles and Müller cells, which span the entire retinal thickness and can contribute to nanoparticle trafficking [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Immunofluorescence staining showed that FITC-FNBP co-localized with Glutamine Synthetase (GS)-positive Müller cell processes across multiple retinal layers, extending from the ILM down to the outer nuclear layer (ONL) (Fig. S7A). These findings indicate that the deep retinal penetration of FNBP is, at least in part, mediated by a Müller cell-associated transretinal transport pathway.

The improved transretinal performance of FNBP is also attributed to its unique fluorinated structure. The combined lipophobic and hydrophobic nature reduces non-specific adhesion and promotes deep retinal migration. We did not further dissect the individual contribution of each subsequent retinal barrier. However, the distinct in vitro and in vivo penetration differences between FNBP and NBP strongly support that fluoroalkylation enhances transretinal delivery and RPE enrichment. Over all, these findings suggest fluoroalkylation modification as a practical chemical strategy that works in tandem with biological transport pathways to bypass ocular barriers and achieve targeted RPE delivery.

In vitro antioxidative and anti-inflammatory effects of FNBP nanoparticles

The therapeutic efficiency of FNBP was initially measured in an in vitro RPE degeneration model. ARPE-19 cells were exposed with SI (10 mM, 12 h) to induce oxidative injury for model establishment [31]. As shown in Fig. S8A, SI stimulation markedly repressed cell viability detected by CCK-8 assay and elevated intracellular ROS level (Fig. S8B and S8C), confirming the establishment of a robust oxidative stress model and the need for antioxidant intervention. To demonstrate the therapeutic effects of FNBP, four experimental groups were included: (i) Control (Healthy cells with PBS), (ii) Untreated (SI + PBS), (iii) SI + NBP (SI + NBP), (iv) SI + FNBP (SI + FNBP).

Considering that Nrf2 pathway is the key pharmacologic target of FNBP, we next evaluated acknowledged indicators within this pathway at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2A) [23,32]. qRT-PCR analysis showed that FNBP upregulated of HO-1 and GSTM transcripts, two crucial Nrf2-regulated antioxidative genes, which was superior to bare NBP (Fig. 2B and C). Consistently, western blotting revealed that FNBP treatment increases phosphorylated-Nrf2 (p-Nrf2), the active form of Nrf2, as well as HO-1 levels in ARPE-19 cells (Fig. 2D and E).

The superiority of FNBP over native NBP highlights the essential role of the supramolecular carrier. By enabling endosomal escape, the fluorous assembly enables the intact NBP to accumulate in the cytosol, where it competitively disrupted the Keap1-Nrf2 PPI [33]. Furthermore, since Nrf2 activation upregulates endogenous antioxidant systems, the initial cytosolic release of NBP and subsequent Nrf2 activation are expected to enhance intracellular reducing capacity. This biological increase in reducing capacity, in turn, accelerate the cleavage of the disulfide linkages within internalized FNBP, establishing a favorable pharmacological synergy that supports efficient peptide release. Immunofluorescence staining of p-Nrf2 further confirmed robust Nrf2 activation by FNBP (Fig. 2F and G).

To definitively confirm that the observed protective effects of FNBP are specifically mediated via Nrf2, we compared FNBP with the Nrf2 agonist dimethyl fumarate (DMF) and the antagonist ML385. Co-administration of ML385 with FNBP effectively abolished the efficacy of FNBP, as reflected by decreased p-Nrf2 protein and reduced HO-1 and GSTM RNA levels. Furthermore, FNBP showed a superior or comparable efficacy compared to DMF. Together, these results substantiate that FNBP acts primarily through Nrf2 signaling (Fig. S9A and S9B).

Consistent with this confirmed mechanism, FNBP decreased intracellular ROS levels, which quantified by H2DCFDA fluorescence probes, thereby alleviating oxidative damage in ARPE-19 cells (Fig. 2H and I; S10A and S10B). In addition, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that FNBP significantly downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-8 while upregulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine TGF-β1 (Fig. 2J). Collectively, these findings indicate that FNBP effectively mitigates oxidative stress and inflammation in RPE cells in vitro, translating its material–biology synergy into potent cytoprotection.

In vivo optimization of administration regimen and short-term neuroprotective efficacy

Consistent with the in vitro study, the RPE degeneration mouse model was established by a single tail vein injection of the oxidative stress inducer SI [34,35]. Fundus photography revealed extensive AMD-like atrophic lesions, with widespread retinal atrophy at 4-, 7-, 14- and 28-days post SI administration (Fig. S11A). H&E staining confirmed severe RPE pathology, including pigmentary disturbances with structural disruption predominantly localized to the RPE layer. These changes coincided with marked ONL thinning, disorganization, and areas of geographic atrophy with complete RPE loss (Fig. S11B and S11C). These findings indicated that SI administration initiates RPE apoptosis and triggers secondary photoreceptor degeneration [36], establishing a robust in vivo model that recapitulates the histopathological and functional hallmarks of intermediate-stage dry AMD.

We next optimized the dosing regimen to maximize benefits while minimizing injection-related injury. Given the delicate nature of the murine eye, the timing of intravitreal injection relative to SI induction was systematically compared using three schedules (Fig. S12A): (i) a single injection 24 h before SI induction (pre-modeling, PM group), (ii) dual injections 24 h before SI induction and 4 days after SI induction (pre-post-modeling, PPM group), and (iii) a single injection on the day of SI (modeling-day, MD group). Retinal morphology analysis revealed that the PM group exhibited the strongest structural preservation. Notably, despite a higher cumulative dose, the PPM group showed diminished efficacy, plausibly attributing to mechanical injury and inflammation from repeated intravitreal injections. Therefore, the single preventive administration (PM group) was selected as the optimal protocol for subsequent studies.

With the optimized regimen, we first assessed the short-term (Day 7) neuroprotective efficacy of FNBP against a comprehensive set of controls: AREDS supplements (a clinical recommended supplement for dry AMD), PMX-53 (a C5a receptor antagonist), DMF (a small-molecule Nrf2 agonist), NBP and TNBP (TAT-modified NBP, as a CPP control). SI induced severe retinal atrophy in most groups, which was visually characterized by the emergence of extensive bright atrophy areas in fundus photography and ONL thinning on OCT (Fig. 3B and C). H&E staining corroborated these structure changes. Notably, the FNBP group showed the optimal preservation of RPE morphology. Despite focal pigment accumulation, RPE cells largely retained a continuous monolayer. Other treated groups, however, exhibited marked loss of this fundamental RPE architecture, with severe depigmentation or aberrant pigment clumping (Fig. 3D).

Quantitative analysis of the ONL and overall retinal thickness revealed a clear hierarchy of structural protection. The FNBP treatment preserved approximately 80–90% of retinal thickness, effectively maintaining the ONL integrity against oxidative damage. AREDS and TNBP conferred moderate protection with approximately 70% preservation, whereas PMX-53 or DMF provided virtually little structural rescue (Fig. 3E and Table S2).

Immunofluorescence staining further supported these structural findings (Fig. 3F–H, and Table S3). To validate the molecular protection, we evaluated key biomarkers: ZO-1 (RPE tight junction integrity) [37], RPE65 (general RPE function), and Rhodopsin and Recoverin (general photoreceptor function). In the Untreated and NBP groups, these markers were markedly downregulated. AREDS and TNBP partially restored their expression, whereas FNBP produced the most robust and comprehensive recovery of RPE and photoreceptor biomarkers, consistent with its superior neuroprotective effect.

We attribute this efficacy hierarchy primarily to differences in ocular delivery and pharmacokinetics. The moderate benefit of AREDS likely reflects sustained systemic exposure from intensive daily dosing, whereas TNBP derives partial efficacy from TAT-mediated penetration. By contrast, the negligible protection afforded by PMX-53 and DMF suggests that conventional peptides and small molecules suffer from rapid intraocular clearance and limited retinal penetration, resulting in subtherapeutic drug levels at RPE layer. In comparison, fluorous self-assembly provides FNBP with a bioinert surface that reduces non-specific interactions and, together with our in vivo distribution data, supports more efficient transretinal delivery and availability at the RPE.

FNBP nanoparticle-mediated modulation of oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammation

To further clarify the molecular basis of superior efficacy of FNBP, we next investigated its antioxidant and neuroprotective mechanisms in vivo. Oxidative stress was assessed by dihydroethidium (DHE) staining of retinal cryosections. Untreated group showed prominent DHE oxidation, whereas, FNBP notably reduced DHE fluorescence, indicating suppressed superoxide production (Fig. 4A and B). Consistently, TUNEL staining revealed a significant reduction in apoptotic cells within the ONL after FNBP administration compared with both the Untreated and NBP groups (Fig. 4C and D). In addition, FNBP significantly suppressed retinal Il-6 and Il-8 transcription level, in line with in vitro anti-inflammatory results (Fig. 4E). This three-pronged protective action, attenuating oxidative damage, inhibiting apoptosis, and suppressing inflammation, collectively support FNBP as a novel multi-target therapeutics candidate for dry AMD.

We then examined activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway in the retina. Under normal conditions, the expression ratio of Nrf2 to p-Nrf2 is low due to Keap1-mediated degradation. In contrast, SI exposure could slightly elevate this ratio (Fig. 4F and G). FNBP treatment induced a marked upregulation of p-Nrf2, accompanied by enhanced nuclear localization, as evidenced by co-localization of p-Nrf2 immunofluorescence with DAPI-stained nuclei (Fig. 4H and I). These results indicate that FNBP effectively accumulates in the RPE, where it disrupts Keap1–Nrf2 binding, prevents Nrf2 degradation, and promotes its activation and nuclear accumulation, thereby driving downstream antioxidative and neuroprotective responses. Nrf2 activation is known to induce antioxidant gene expression, including Ho-1, Gclm, Nqo1, and Gpx4. We therefore evaluated these downstream responses in treated eyecups. In the FNBP group, HO-1 protein expression was upregulated, consistent with our in vitro data (Fig. 4F and G). Furthermore, FNBP significantly increased the mRNA levels of downstream antioxidant genes (Fig. 4J), further supporting Nrf2 activation and the recovery of cellular antioxidant defenses.

These results further confirm that the fluorous self-assembly does not sterically compromise peptide activity once internalized. Instead, the dynamic, non-covalent nature of the supramolecular architecture permits efficient disassembly and cytosolic payload release, thereby enabling effective engagement of the Keap1–Nrf2 axis and reactivation of endogenous antioxidant pathways. Taken together, these data demonstrate that FNBP effectively attenuates oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses in the NaIO3-induced retinal degeneration model through functional activation of the Nrf2 pathway. We acknowledge that the NaIO3-induced retinal degeneration model used in this study represents an acute RPE-targeted injury model, which only partially recapitulates the chronic and multifactorial nature of human dry AMD. This model, however, offers the advantage of reproducible RPE-first degeneration followed by secondary photoreceptor loss, making it suitable for a proof-of-concept evaluation of RPE-targeted nanotherapeutics. Because this model induces exceptionally rapid retinal degeneratio, the PM regimen was implemented to evaluate protective efficiency. Future studies will be needed to validate this fluorous peptide platform in more chronic and genetically driven models of AMD.

In vivo sustained long-term therapeutic efficacy of FNBP

A major challenge in retinal drug delivery is achieving durable therapeutic exposure with fewer injections, which remains a significant burden in clinical AMD management. Although FNBP showed promising results at Day 7, it was essential to determine whether a single dose could provide sustained benefits. We therefore extended the follow-up to 28 days and longitudinally compared FNBP with the same set of controls described before. (Fig. 5A).

Fundus photography and OCT imaging were performed at Days 7, 14, 21, and 28 post SI injection. In the Untreated, NBP, PMX-53, and DMF groups, retinal degeneration progressed continuously. In these groups, atrophic lesions on fundus images expanded over time and the total retina and ONL became progressively thinner, reaching about only 20% of normal thickness by Day 28 (Fig. 5B and C, and Table S4). TNBP and AREDS group retained their initial advantage at Day 14, showing smaller atrophic areas and thicker retinas compared with the Untreated group. However, their protective effects declined thereafter, and by Days 21 and 28, retinal structure in these groups was largely indistinguishable from the poorly protected groups. This may be consistent with discontinuation of daily AREDS dosing after Day 7 and the limited in vivo stability of TAT-conjugated peptides.

In contrast, FNBP provided the most durable and pronounced protection across all time points. At Days 14, 21, and 28, FNBP group consistently exhibited the smallest atrophic areas on fundus imaging and the best preservation of both total retinal and ONL thickness on OCT (Fig. 5B and C, and Table S4). Histological analysis at Day 28 further confirmed these findings, which showed that the FNBP group maintained the most intact retinal structure, with relatively well-preserved RPE morphology and monolayer structure, whereas all other treatment groups displayed severe outer retinal thinning and marked disruption of the RPE layer (Fig. 5D).

Although detailed in vivo pharmacokinetics remain to be clarified, the long-lasting efficacy of FNBP likely reflects a combination of material and biological factors. Fluorous supramolecular assembly facilitates the transretinal transport and RPE enrichment of the peptide, followed by intracellular disassembly and efficient cytosolic release of NBP. In parallel, early and potent neutralization of the initial SI induced oxidative insult may preserve RPE structure and function, allowing preserved RPE cells to re-establish endogenous redox homeostasis and thereby prevent secondary photoreceptor degeneration even after partial clearance of the nanoparticles. Taken together, these longitudinal data demonstrate that FNBP functions as a single-dose intravitreal peptide nanotherapeutics that combines deep transretinal delivery with long-acting neuroprotection, outperforming conventional TAT-modified peptides, AREDS supplementation and other promising controls, helping to address the durability limitations of current peptide-based treatments.

Ocular and systematic biosafety evaluation of FNBP nanoparticles

Ensuring the biosafety of intravitreally administered nanotherapeutics is a prerequisite for clinical translation. We comprehensively assessed the ocular and systemic toxicity of FNBP in healthy mice. Fundus photography and H&E staining of ocular tissues revealed that FNBP treatment induced no structural anomalies, retinal detachment, or inflammatory infiltration compared to the PBS-treated control (Fig. 6A and B). To further investigate the potential impact on visual function, electroretinography (ERG) was performed to record retinal responses to light stimulation. The FNBP group exhibited scotopic and photopic ERG waveforms similar to those of the control group. with well-preserved a- and b-wave amplitudes, indicating that FNBP does not impair photoreceptor or bipolar cell function (Fig. 6C–F). Additionally, intraocular pressure (IOP), a critical safety parameter for intravitreal injection, was monitored longitudinally. Although a transient and slight fluctuation was observed immediately post-injection due to the volume increase, the IOP in FNBP-treated eyes rapidly returned to baseline levels and remained stable within the normal physiological range throughout the 14-day observation period (Fig. 6G). Systemic toxicity was also evaluated by analyzing blood biochemistry and major organ histology at 14. Key hematological markers and biochemical indicators of liver function (ALT, ALP, ALB) and kidney function (CREA, BUN, UREA) showed no significant differences between the FNBP and control groups (Fig. 6H). Furthermore, H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) displayed normal tissue morphology without signs of necrosis or inflammation (Fig. 6I). This excellent in vivo tolerability aligns well with the recognized bio-inertness of fluorinated materials. Driven by their uniquely low surface energy and lipophobic/hydrophobic nature, these fluorous assemblies effectively minimize non-specific protein adsorption and aberrant cellular interactions, which are common triggers for foreign-body-induced inflammation or toxicity. Collectively, these results demonstrate that FNBP possesses an excellent ocular and systemic safety profile, supporting its potential for safe intravitreal application.

Conclusion

Effective deep retinal penetration and cytosolic delivery remain critical challenges for peptide-based therapeutics targeting posterior retinal diseases. Herein, we successfully engineered a fluorous-tagged peptide nanotherapeutic (FNBP) that spontaneously self-assembles into stable nanoparticles. Unlike conventional cationic cell-penetrating strategies such as TAT, the fluoroalkylation modification optimizes the physicochemical characteristics of NBP peptide, mitigating non-specific vitreous entrapment and enabling efficient transretinal penetration toward the deep RPE layer. Following efficient internalization and subsequent cytosolic payload release, the functional peptide competitively disrupted the Keap1-Nrf2 PPI. In a severe murine model of AMD, a single intravitreal injection of FNBP achieved deep retinal penetration and long-term structural preservation, providing sustained neuroprotection for up to 28 days. This therapeutic performance distinctly surpasses that of conventional TAT-modified peptides, clinically available AREDS supplements, small-molecule Nrf2 modulators, and all other tested controls. Mechanistically, FNBP revives endogenous antioxidant defenses by activating the Keap1/Nrf2 pathway, concurrently attenuating oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis, while maintaining a favorable ocular and systemic safety profile. Overall, this work not only presents a potent, mechanism-driven biologic candidate for halting dry AMD progression but also establishes a promising biomaterial platform for peptide-based biologics overcoming challenging ocular barriers in targeted posterior segment delivery.
