Algebraicity of exterior Cauchy transforms of algebraic ovals: a homological formulation
Christian Hägg, Boris Shapiro

Abstract
. Let Ω ⊂ ℂ \Omega\subset\mathbb{C} be a bounded domain whose boundary is an oval of a real algebraic curve. We study when the exterior Cauchy transform 𝒞 Ω ​ ( z ) = 1 π ​ ∫ Ω d ​ A ​ ( ζ ) z − ζ \mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\Omega}\frac{dA(\zeta)}{z-\zeta} is algebraic. The boundary formula identifies this transform with a Cauchy-type integral on the normalization X X of the relevant irreducible component of the Schwarz correspondence P ⁡ ( z , w ) = 0 P(z,w)=0 . The main point is that X X is fixed while only the divisor π − 1 ​ ( z ) \pi^{-1}(z) of moving poles varies. Thus the natural monodromy is point-pushing on a punctured fixed surface and becomes trivial on absolute homology after the moving punctures are filled; in particular, the usual Picard–Lefschetz transvection picture does not produce absolute cycles in this problem. The principal theorem is a residue criterion: if the lifted boundary is separating, i.e., if it bounds an integral two-chain on X X disjoint from the fixed polar divisor, then the exterior Cauchy transform is algebraic and is given by an explicit residue sum with chain multiplicities. This implies, in particular, algebraicity for every smooth oval on a rational real algebraic curve and for separating ovals in positive genus. We also record the corresponding complete-real-locus statement for dividing real curves, with the necessary affine-plane caveats. Nonseparating ovals are treated as a conjectural period problem: we formulate a period-rank test which can detect possible Abelian contributions. The examples include the ellipse, the nodal cubic logarithm, a smooth Weierstrass cubic as an elliptic-period test case, and a conditional positive-genus construction illustrating algebraic transforms beyond quadrature domains.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded domain with piecewise analytic positively oriented boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
. Its exterior Cauchy transform is

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\Omega}\frac{dA(\zeta)}{z-\zeta},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\Omega},

and

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)}{\pi z}+O(z^{-2}),\qquad z\to\infty.

When
\Gamma
is an oval of a real algebraic curve, one may ask when
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}
is algebraic. This question is weaker than the quadrature-domain question, where the exterior Cauchy transform is rational.

The boundary relation
w=\bar{z}
on the real oval suggests replacing the possibly multivalued Schwarz function by the Schwarz correspondence; see Davis
[
3
]
for the classical Schwarz function viewpoint. If
\Gamma
lies on the real curve
P(z,\bar{z})=0
, let
X
be the normalization of the projective closure of the complex curve

P(z,w)=0,

and let

\pi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}_{z},\qquad\eta:X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}_{w}

be the meromorphic functions induced by
z
and
w
. The lifted oval will be denoted by
\gamma\subset X
.

If
P(z,w)
is reducible, all statements below are made on the normalization of the irreducible component containing the analytic lift of the chosen oval. Thus genus and the separating/nonseparating status of the chosen lifted oval always refer to this relevant component, not to extraneous components of the total Schwarz correspondence. When the global type of a real curve is mentioned, it refers to the whole real locus of this component in the standard sense of Klein.

The main homological point is the following. The surface
X
does not vary with the parameter
z
of the Cauchy transform. Only the pole divisor
F_{z}=\pi^{-1}(z)
of the Cauchy kernel varies. Consequently the natural monodromy is the point-pushing or braid monodromy of a punctured fixed surface, not Picard–Lefschetz monodromy of a varying Riemann surface. After one fills the moving punctures, this monodromy acts trivially. Thus it cannot create nonzero absolute homology increments on
X
.

This fixed-normalization point leads to the following conclusions. The robust positive result is algebraicity by residues: if the lifted oval is separating, i.e. bounds a two-chain on the relevant normalization, the Cauchy integral is a finite sum of residues and is algebraic. Positive genus by itself does not obstruct algebraicity. Conversely, non-algebraicity is no longer a formal consequence of an alleged absolute Picard–Lefschetz increment. It must be proved by showing a genuine infinite monodromy or logarithmic term in the actual Cauchy integral.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Section
2
recalls the boundary formula and its weighted version. Section
3
states the fixed-normalization monodromy lemma and explains the limitation of absolute Picard–Lefschetz reasoning in this setting. Section
4
proves algebraicity by residues, including the multiplicity form needed for moving poles. Section
5
introduces separating and nonseparating lifted ovals and recalls the standard global type I/type II terminology for real curves. Section
7
gives the relation with quadrature domains. Section
8
records examples and model constructions, including the ellipse, the cubic trichotomy, conditional positive-genus separating examples outside the quadrature-domain class, and the nodal cubic loop where logarithms survive.

2.
The boundary formula and the lifted Cauchy integral

Proposition 2.1

(Cauchy–Green boundary formula)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be bounded with piecewise
C^{1}
positively oriented boundary
\Gamma
. Then, for
z\notin\overline{\Omega}
,

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\bar{\zeta}\,d\zeta}{z-\zeta}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\bar{\zeta}\,d\zeta}{\zeta-z}.

Proof.

Since

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\zeta}}\left(\frac{\bar{\zeta}}{z-\zeta}\right)=\frac{1}{z-\zeta},

the Cauchy–Green formula gives

\int_{\Omega}\frac{dA(\zeta)}{z-\zeta}=\frac{1}{2i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\bar{\zeta}\,d\zeta}{z-\zeta}.

Dividing by
\pi
gives the claim.
∎

Remark 2.2

(Exterior branch versus boundary-integral branches)
.

The equality in Proposition
2.1
is the equality with the area Cauchy transform on the exterior component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
. The same boundary integral defines holomorphic functions on the other components of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
, but those functions should be regarded as componentwise boundary-integral branches, or analytic continuations, not as the literal area transform on the interior of
\Omega
. For example, for the unit disk the boundary integral is zero inside the disk, whereas the area transform there is
\bar{z}
.

Proposition 2.3

(Polynomial densities)
.

Let
\rho(z,\bar{z})
be a polynomial and set

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega,\rho}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})dA(\zeta)}{z-\zeta}.

Choose a polynomial
R_{\rho}(z,w)
with

\frac{\partial R_{\rho}}{\partial w}(z,w)=\rho(z,w).

Then, for
z\notin\overline{\Omega}
,

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega,\rho}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{R_{\rho}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})d\zeta}{z-\zeta}.

On the normalization of the relevant Schwarz component, all subsequent formulae apply to the meromorphic differential

\alpha_{\rho}=R_{\rho}(\pi,\eta)d\pi

in place of
\alpha=\eta d\pi
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Proposition
2.1
, using

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\zeta}}\left(\frac{R_{\rho}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{z-\zeta}\right)=\frac{\rho(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})}{z-\zeta}.

∎

Remark 2.4
.

The same exterior-branch convention applies to the weighted formula. Different choices of the primitive
R_{\rho}
differ by a polynomial in the first variable. On the exterior branch the resulting added boundary integral vanishes, because it is the integral of a holomorphic one-form in the plane variable around the boundary. On other components one should again interpret the formula as the corresponding boundary-integral branch.

Let
X
be the normalization of the relevant irreducible component of the Schwarz correspondence and let
\gamma
be the lift of the real oval. On
\gamma
one has
\eta=\bar{\pi}
, hence on the exterior component

(1)

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\omega_{z},\qquad\omega_{z}=\frac{\eta d\pi}{z-\pi}.

For polynomial density one replaces
\eta d\pi
by
\alpha_{\rho}
.

3.
Fixed normalization and moving-puncture monodromy

Put
\alpha=\eta d\pi
in the unweighted case and
\alpha=\alpha_{\rho}
in the weighted case. Let
P_{\alpha}
be the polar divisor of
\alpha
, and let
B\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}
be the complement of the critical values of
\pi
, the values
\pi(P_{\alpha})
, and
\infty
. For
z\in B
set

S=X\setminus P_{\alpha},\qquad F_{z}=\pi^{-1}(z),\qquad U_{z}=S\setminus F_{z}.

The form

\omega_{z}=\frac{\alpha}{z-\pi}

is holomorphic on
U_{z}
. Thus every
\lambda\in H_{1}(U_{z},\mathbb{Z})
gives a period

I_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\lambda}\omega_{z}.

The following elementary lemma is the key correction.

Lemma 3.1

(No absolute homology increment)
.

Let
\ell
be a loop in
B
based at
z_{0}
, and let

M_{\ell}:H_{1}(U_{z_{0}},\mathbb{Z})\to H_{1}(U_{z_{0}},\mathbb{Z})

be the induced monodromy. Let

j_{*}:H_{1}(U_{z_{0}},\mathbb{Z})\to H_{1}(S,\mathbb{Z})

be induced by filling the moving punctures
F_{z_{0}}
. Then

j_{*}M_{\ell}=j_{*}.

Consequently

M_{\ell}(\lambda)-\lambda\in\ker j_{*}

for every contour class
\lambda
.

Proof.

As
z
moves along
\ell
, the finite set
F_{z}\subset S
moves by an ambient isotopy of the fixed surface
S
. At the end of the loop the set
F_{z_{0}}
returns to itself, possibly permuted, and the resulting endpoint homeomorphism of the punctured surface need not be literally the identity. After the moving punctures are filled, however, the same motion gives a homeomorphism of
S
isotopic to the identity. Hence its induced action on
H_{1}(S,\mathbb{Z})
is the identity. This is exactly the assertion
j_{*}M_{\ell}=j_{*}
.
∎

Corollary 3.2

(What simple branch monodromy can do)
.

Around a simple branch value of
\pi
, the local monodromy of
F_{z}
is a half-twist of the two colliding moving punctures. After squaring, it is a Dehn twist about a small curve enclosing these two punctures. The homology class of this curve lies in
\ker j_{*}
and has no nonzero image in
H_{1}(S,\mathbb{Z})
or
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
. Therefore this monodromy cannot produce a Picard–Lefschetz transvection by a nonzero absolute cycle.

Proof.

The curve enclosing the two colliding punctures bounds a small disc once the punctures are filled. Thus its image in
H_{1}(S,\mathbb{Z})
is zero. The conclusion follows from Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.3

(Absolute cycles versus moving punctures)
.

The monodromy of
U_{z}=X\setminus(P_{\alpha}\cup F_{z})
may change a relative contour by loops surrounding the moving punctures, but Lemma
3.1
shows that it cannot add a nonzero absolute cycle on the fixed normalization. Thus moment tests for non-algebraicity have to involve a genuine Abelian-period component of the original lifted contour, or a genuine logarithmic endpoint contribution, rather than a vanishing cycle produced by moving the finite pole divisor.

The kernel in Lemma
3.1
is generated by loops around the moving punctures, with the usual single relation when there are no fixed punctures on a compact surface. Their periods are residues.

Proposition 3.4

(Residues of moving-puncture loops)
.

Let
q\in F_{z}
be a regular point of
\pi
, and let
\varepsilon_{q}
be a small positively oriented loop around
q
in
U_{z}
. Write locally

\alpha=f\,d\pi.

Then

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\varepsilon_{q}}\frac{\alpha}{z-\pi}=-f(q).

In particular, for the unweighted transform this is
-\eta(q)
, and for the density
\rho
it is
-R_{\rho}(z,\eta(q))
. These quantities are algebraic functions of
z
.

Proof.

At a regular point of
\pi
one may use
u=\pi-z
as a local coordinate. Then

\frac{\alpha}{z-\pi}=\frac{f\,d\pi}{z-\pi}=-f\frac{du}{u},

so the residue is
-f(q)
.
∎

Remark 3.5

(What remains of monodromy obstructions)
.

A monodromy obstruction is still formally possible if analytic continuation of the actual contour class produces infinitely many distinct additive residue jumps. However, such an obstruction is a residue or logarithmic phenomenon, not an absolute Picard–Lefschetz period. Thus non-algebraicity must be checked directly in the relevant integral, for example by finding a surviving logarithmic term, see example in Section
8.4
.

4.
Algebraicity by residues

The principal positive result is the following residue criterion.

Theorem 4.1

(Residue algebraicity criterion with multiplicities)
.

Let
\gamma
be the lifted oval on the normalization
X
of the relevant irreducible component of the Schwarz correspondence. Suppose that
\gamma=\partial\Sigma
for an integral two-chain
\Sigma
on
X
, oriented so that its boundary is the lifted boundary used in the Cauchy–Green formula, and that
\gamma
is disjoint from the fixed polar divisor
P_{\alpha}
of
\alpha
. Let
D
be a simply connected parameter domain in the
z
-sphere which avoids the critical values of
\pi
, the finite values
\pi(P_{\alpha})
, and the boundary set
\pi(\gamma)
. Then the boundary-integral branch

I_{D}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\alpha}{z-\pi}

is an algebraic germ on
D
. If
D
lies in the exterior component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\pi(\gamma)
, then
I_{D}
is the exterior Cauchy transform. On other components it is the corresponding boundary-integral branch, and not necessarily the literal area transform.

More precisely, for
a\notin\gamma
let
n_{\Sigma}(a)
be the local integer multiplicity of the two-chain
\Sigma
at
a
. Then, for
z\in D
,

(2)

I_{D}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\alpha}{z-\pi}=\sum_{a\in X}n_{\Sigma}(a)\operatorname{Res}_{a}\frac{\alpha}{z-\pi}.

Only finitely many terms are nonzero: the fixed poles of
\alpha
and the points of
\pi^{-1}(z)
. The point
z=\infty
is handled separately in the local coordinate
\xi=1/z
; the exterior germ has the usual expansion at
\xi=0
after the fixed poles of
\alpha
over
\pi=\infty
are included in the residue sum.

Proof.

For
z\notin\pi(\gamma)
the moving pole divisor
\pi^{-1}(z)
is disjoint from the boundary of the chain, so the residue theorem for a meromorphic differential on an integral two-chain gives (
2
). The fixed poles of
\alpha
contribute rational functions of
z
, with possible poles at the values
\pi(P_{\alpha})
. At a regular moving pole
q\in\pi^{-1}(z)
, Proposition
3.4
gives an algebraic expression in the inverse branch
q(z)
. On the simply connected domain
D
, the inverse branches of
\pi
can be chosen consistently and the integers
n_{\Sigma}(q(z))
are locally constant because no moving pole crosses
\gamma
. Hence the right-hand side is an algebraic function on
D
.
∎

Corollary 4.2

(Separating lifted oval)
.

If
\gamma
is a separating curve on
X
and bounds a compact bordered subsurface whose boundary avoids
P_{\alpha}
, then the exterior Cauchy transform, and every polynomially weighted exterior transform, is algebraic. The same residue formula gives algebraic boundary-integral branches on the other components of the complement.

Corollary 4.3

(Rational closed-lift criterion)
.

Let the Schwarz correspondence be rational and its normalization be given by,

\pi(t)=p(t),\qquad\eta(t)=q(t),\qquad p,q\in\mathbb{C}(t),

on
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{t}
. If the lifted oval is a closed contour on
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{t}
disjoint from the poles of the integrand, then

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(Z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}\frac{q(t)p^{\prime}(t)}{Z-p(t)}dt

is algebraic.

Proof.

A closed contour on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
bounds a two-chain. Apply Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Corollary 4.4

(Smooth oval on a rational curve)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a smooth compact oval of a real affine rational curve. Suppose that
\Gamma
contains no singular point of the plane curve and bounds a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
. Then the exterior Cauchy transform
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}
is algebraic. The same conclusion holds for every polynomially weighted Cauchy transform.

Proof.

Since
\Gamma
is smooth, its lift to the normalization is a smooth closed contour on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. This contour is disjoint from the fixed poles after excluding the usual boundary-pole degeneracies, and it bounds an integral two-chain on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. The result follows from Theorem
4.1
, or equivalently from Corollary
4.3
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Notice that Theorem
4.1
shows that positive genus is not by itself an obstruction to algebraicity. What matters is the homology class of the particular lifted oval, together with the fixed poles of the differential used in the boundary formula.

5.
Separating ovals, dividing curves, and residue examples

We now recall some classical topological terminology. In the standard terminology of real algebraic curves, a real curve is of type I, or dividing, if its whole real locus separates its complex normalization; otherwise it is of type II, or non-dividing. This is a global property of
X(\mathbb{R})
. For the present Cauchy-transform problem, we typically work with a chosen (lifted to normalization) oval of
X(\mathbb{R})
, i.e. its bounded connected component. We therefore use the following terminology.

Definition 5.1

(Separating and nonseparating lifted ovals)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a smooth real algebraic oval and let
\gamma\subset X
be its lift to the normalization of the relevant irreducible component of the Schwarz correspondence. We call
\Gamma
, or equivalently
\gamma
,
separating
if
\gamma
separates
X
, equivalently if

[\gamma]=0\in H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z}),

or, equivalently, if
\gamma
is the boundary of an integral two-chain on
X
. We call it
nonseparating
if
[\gamma]\neq 0
in
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
.

Lemma 5.2

(Several real components force nonseparation)
.

Let
(X,\tau)
be a smooth real algebraic curve, and let
C
be a connected component of
X(\mathbb{R})
. If
C
is separating as a simple closed curve on the underlying Riemann surface
X
, then

X(\mathbb{R})=C.

Consequently, if
X(\mathbb{R})
has at least two smooth connected components, then each of them is nonseparating.

Proof.

Near a real component the antiholomorphic involution is locally conjugate to complex conjugation, so it fixes the component and interchanges the two local sides. If
C
separates
X
, then the two connected components of
X\setminus C
are therefore interchanged by
\tau
. A fixed point of
\tau
lying outside
C
would have to belong simultaneously to a component of
X\setminus C
and to its image, which is impossible. Hence there are no further real points.
∎

Thus the residue criterion says that separating lifted ovals are on the algebraic side. Nonseparating lifted ovals are the natural place where an Abelian-period obstruction can occur. In genus zero every smooth closed lift is separating. In positive genus, Lemma
5.2
shows that a single selected oval can be separating only in the one-real-component situation. Curves with at least two smooth real ovals therefore provide the natural testing ground for generic non-algebraicity of the Cauchy transform of one oval taken alone; the compact affine oval of a smooth real cubic is the first basic nonseparating example.

The standard global type I situation gives a useful multi-component version of the same residue principle. Suppose that the real curve
(X,\tau)
is dividing, so

X\setminus X(\mathbb{R})=X_{+}\sqcup X_{-},\qquad\partial X_{+}=X(\mathbb{R})

with the complex orientation. If the lifted boundary of a plane domain is the complete real locus
X(\mathbb{R})
, possibly with several components, then it bounds the two-chain
X_{+}
on
X
. Consequently the same residue theorem applies. Thus type I real curves, considered through the domain bounded by their complete real locus, typically produce algebraic Cauchy transforms. This statement is different from saying that every individual component of a type I curve should be called type I.

Remark 5.3
.

There is, however, a simple affine restriction which is important for the planar Cauchy-transform problem. If a real projective plane curve has odd degree, then its real locus meets every real line in an odd number of points counted with multiplicities; in particular it meets the chosen line at infinity. Hence its affine real locus contains a non-compact connected component. Therefore the favorable situation in which the
complete
affine real locus is a compact boundary of a bounded planar domain can occur only for even degree. Even degree is not sufficient by itself: one must also choose the affine chart so that the line at infinity does not meet the relevant real locus, or at least so that the boundary components used for the domain are compact.

There is a second, more subtle restriction in the smooth one-oval case. The Rokhlin complex-orientation formula says that, for a nonsingular dividing real plane curve of degree
2k
with
l
ovals,

(3)

2(\Pi_{+}-\Pi_{-})=l-k^{2}.

where
\Pi_{+}
and
\Pi_{-}
denote the numbers of positive and negative nested pairs of ovals. If
l=1
, there are no nested pairs, so the left-hand side is zero. Hence a one-oval dividing smooth plane curve of degree
2k
must satisfy
k=1
. We obtain the following useful consequence.

Proposition 5.4

(One smooth oval and the Rokhlin obstruction)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}P^{2}
be a nonsingular real plane curve of even degree
2k
with exactly one real oval. If
k>1
, then
A
is nondividing. Equivalently, its unique real oval is nonseparating on the complex normalization. The only smooth one-oval dividing plane curve is the conic.

Proof.

For a dividing even-degree curve the Rokhlin complex-orientation formula is (
3
); see Rokhlin
[
10
]
. With one oval there are no nested pairs, hence
\Pi_{+}=\Pi_{-}=0
and
0=1-k^{2}
. Thus
k=1
. For
k>1
the curve cannot be dividing. Since the real locus has only one component, nondividing is the same as saying that this component does not separate the complex normalization.
∎

Thus a smooth one-oval plane curve of degree
4,6,\ldots
is not one of the favorable dividing single-boundary examples. It is instead a canonical nonseparating test case. This is why the acnodal or singular hyperelliptic models mentioned below should not be confused with smooth one-oval plane curves: in those models the normalization may have the desired separating one-component real locus, while the plane curve has isolated real singular points rather than additional smooth real branches.

For a single smooth oval in positive genus, Lemma
5.2
gives a useful sharpening. If that oval is to be algebraic by the direct residue theorem, it must be separating; if it is a component of the normalized real locus, this can happen only when it is the whole normalized real locus. Hence the robust residue-theoretic single-oval situation is not a multi-oval curve, but a dividing curve with one real component. In affine plane models this is necessarily an even-degree phenomenon. The most transparent concrete models are even-degree hyperelliptic or acnodal curves for which the normalization has one real oval and the remaining real points of the plane curve, if any, are isolated acnodes rather than additional real branches. These models should be distinguished from a curve with several smooth ovals: in the latter case each individual oval is nonseparating and is expected generically to have a non-algebraic Cauchy transform, unless its homology class is accidentally invisible to the Abelian-period subspace considered in Section
6
.

Corollary 5.5

(Complete real locus of a dividing curve)
.

Let
X
be the normalization of the relevant real Schwarz component, and suppose that
X
is dividing. Assume that the lifted boundary of the chosen bounded plane domain is exactly the complete real locus
X(\mathbb{R})
, with the complex orientation of one half of
X\setminus X(\mathbb{R})
, and that this boundary avoids
P_{\alpha}
. In the affine plane this includes the requirements that the relevant real components are compact, that possible nesting is matched by the chosen planar domain, and that the induced boundary orientation agrees with the complex orientation. Then the exterior Cauchy transform, and every polynomially weighted exterior transform, is algebraic.

Proof.

The complete real locus is the boundary of one half of the dividing curve. Apply Theorem
4.1
to that integral two-chain, with the chain multiplicities as in (
2
).
∎

In an affine plane model this corollary should be read together with the preceding parity observation. If the complete real locus is to be a compact planar boundary, then the projective degree of the plane curve must be even; odd-degree curves necessarily have a real branch at infinity and hence a non-compact affine component.

The following statement records the positive-genus construction in the form in which it will be used. It separates the residue-theoretic argument, which is elementary, from the real projection problem of producing a plane model with all required regularity properties.

Proposition 5.6

(Conditional positive-genus separating construction)
.

Let
(X,\tau)
be a smooth projective real algebraic curve of positive genus and let
C\subset X(\mathbb{R})
be a smooth connected component (oval) which bounds an integral two-chain
\Sigma\subset X
. Suppose that there exist real meromorphic functions
u,v
on
X
, without poles on
C
, such that, with

z=u+iv,\qquad w=u-iv,

the map

p\longmapsto(z(p),w(p))

is birational onto an irreducible component of its plane image, and the restriction
p\mapsto z(p)
embeds
C
as a smooth Jordan oval
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}
with no real singularity or self-intersection on
\Gamma
. Assume also that the fixed polar divisor of the relevant differential is disjoint from
C
. Then the exterior Cauchy transform of the domain bounded by
\Gamma
is algebraic. The same holds for polynomially weighted exterior transforms.

Proof.

On
C
one has
w=\bar{z}
, so the lifted boundary integral on the irreducible Schwarz component normalized by
X
is exactly the integral appearing in the Cauchy–Green formula. Since
C=\partial\Sigma
, Theorem
4.1
applies and gives an algebraic exterior branch. The birationality and regularity assumptions ensure that the genus and the separating property are those of this irreducible Schwarz component and that no singularity of the plane model lies on the chosen oval.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

One can construct examples satisfying the hypotheses of Proposition
5.6
. For instance, start from a compact bordered Riemann surface
S
of genus
h\geq 1
with one boundary component and take its Schottky double
X
. Then
X
is a real algebraic curve by the realization theorem for compact Klein surfaces
[
2
]
, its real locus is the boundary of one half, and hence it is separating. A sufficiently ample real linear system gives many real meromorphic functions. To obtain a completely rigorous plane example from this scheme one must choose the real projection so that all simultaneous real conditions hold: no poles on the oval, immersion and injectivity on the oval, no real singularity on the oval, birationality of the complex plane image, and preservation of the intended irreducible Schwarz component. These are open conditions together with standard generic-projection conditions, but we keep them as explicit hypotheses rather than using an over-compressed generic-projection argument.

Corollary 5.8

(Non-quadrature consequence under the same hypotheses)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
5.6
, and assume in addition that the relevant Schwarz component has positive genus and that the usual regularity theorem for simply connected quadrature domains applies to the smooth algebraic boundary
\Gamma
. Then the simply connected domain bounded by
\Gamma
is not a quadrature domain.

Proof.

The algebraicity of the exterior Cauchy transform follows from Proposition
5.6
. Suppose, for contradiction, that the domain were a simply connected quadrature domain. Then its boundary has a rational Schwarz parametrization; equivalently, the irreducible Schwarz correspondence associated with this quadrature-domain boundary component is rational. On a smooth real-analytic boundary arc both this rational correspondence and the constructed correspondence contain the same relation
w=\bar{z}
. Hence the two algebraic plane curves have infinitely many common points on an arc. By the identity theorem, or by Bezout’s theorem applied to irreducible components, they share the same irreducible component. This would force the constructed relevant component to be rational, contradicting its positive genus. Thus the domain is not a quadrature domain.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

We see that positive genus gives not only compatibility with algebraicity, but also a conditional source of models lying strictly between the rational quadrature-domain case and the expected non-algebraic period case. These examples are separating examples: their algebraicity is residue-theoretic, not quadrature-theoretic. If one views the Schottky double itself as a real curve, it is a type I curve in the standard global sense, but the algebraicity statement uses the concrete fact that the chosen lifted boundary bounds a half of the double.

Question 5.10

(Smooth plane curves)
.

Apart from conics and separating examples as above, does any nonseparating compact oval of a nonsingular real plane curve have algebraic exterior Cauchy transform? A negative answer would have to use a genuine non-residue period or logarithmic monodromy mechanism.

6.
Period tests and the conjectural nonseparating picture

The purpose of this section is to formulate what one should test in the nonseparating case, and not to claim a complete non-algebraicity criterion.

The preceding results suggest that genus is not itself the obstruction to algebraicity. The decisive topological input is whether the chosen lifted oval separates the relevant normalization.

For a separating lifted oval, the transform is algebraic by residues. This includes all closed lifted ovals on rational normalizations and all separating ovals in positive genus. In genus zero this is especially strong: if
X\simeq\mathbb{P}^{1}
and the lifted boundary is a closed contour, then it bounds a two-chain and Theorem
4.1
applies. Thus smooth ovals on rational curves form a large algebraic class, not merely a collection of special examples.

The global type I case fits the same principle. If a real curve is dividing and the domain under consideration has lifted boundary equal to the complete real locus, then that boundary is the boundary of one half of the complex curve. The Cauchy transform is therefore algebraic by residues. This is often the natural positive-genus algebraic situation, especially when the boundary has several components. In the affine plane this complete-locus version is necessarily an even-degree phenomenon: an odd-degree real projective plane curve meets the line at infinity and therefore has a non-compact affine real component.

For one component taken alone the situation is different. By Lemma
5.2
, if the normalized real locus has at least two smooth ovals, then none of them is separating individually. Thus a multi-oval real curve should not be expected to give algebraicity for the Cauchy transform of one chosen oval, except through accidental cancellation of the Abelian-period part. More precisely, the class of the chosen oval could in principle be orthogonal to the finite-dimensional Abelian part generated by the differentials
\eta\,\pi^{n}d\pi
; this is the exceptional kernel situation discussed below. The plausible generic picture is nevertheless that a positive-genus single oval has algebraic Cauchy transform only in the one-real-component dividing situation. Proposition
5.4
shows that this situation cannot be supplied by a smooth one-oval plane curve of even degree
>2
: such a curve is necessarily nondividing. The natural positive examples in plane form are therefore singular hyperelliptic or acnodal models with one smooth real branch on the normalization and only isolated real acnodes besides it.

The only genus-zero obstruction visible in the present framework occurs for relative lifts, for example arcs ending at preimages of singular points. In such cases integrals of rational functions over intervals can contain logarithmic endpoint terms; algebraicity requires their cancellation.

The genuinely new positive-genus regime is a nonseparating lifted oval,

[\gamma]\neq 0\in H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z}).

Then the integral need not be reducible to residues. One expects, after removing the residue contributions forced by moving and fixed poles, a remaining Abelian-period part of the family

\omega_{z}=\frac{\eta\,d\pi}{z-\pi}.

This statement is only a program for detecting non-algebraicity, not a proof of it. Nonzero values of the moments

\int_{\gamma}\eta\,\pi^{n}\,d\pi,\qquad n\geq 0,

or a nonzero pairing with holomorphic differentials do not by themselves prove that the germ in
z
is non-algebraic; algebraic germs may have arbitrary complex Laurent coefficients. A genuine proof of non-algebraicity must exhibit finite-monodromy failure, a surviving logarithmic or quasi-periodic term, or another argument showing that the full
z
-dependent Abelian expression cannot be algebraic.

The period-rank discussion below should therefore be read as a way of excluding one possible accidental cancellation. Let
V_{\pi,\eta}
denote the subspace of
H^{1}(X,\mathbb{C})
generated by the cohomology classes, or equivalently by the normalized Abelian parts, of the differentials
\eta\,\pi^{n}d\pi
after subtracting exact differentials and suitably normalized second- and third-kind pieces. The class
[\gamma]
is invisible to this particular test if

[\gamma]\in V_{\pi,\eta}^{\perp}.

If this happens, the Abelian part seen by this family vanishes on
\gamma
and the Cauchy transform may still be algebraic. If it does not happen, one has identified a nonzero Abelian-period candidate; an additional monodromy, logarithm, or quasi-period calculation is still needed to prove non-algebraicity. For a generic plane realization one expects
V_{\pi,\eta}
to detect the real oval classes; this is the intended content of the generic non-algebraicity conjectures below.

The following standard period-theoretic fact clarifies how restrictive such invisibility can be. Here
K_{X}
denotes the canonical line bundle of
X
, so that
H^{0}(X,K_{X})
is the
g
-dimensional vector space of holomorphic Abelian differentials on a compact Riemann surface of genus
g
.

Lemma 6.1

(Holomorphic differentials detect homology)
.

Let
X
be a compact Riemann surface and let
c\in H_{1}(X,\mathbb{R})
. If

\int_{c}\omega=0\qquad\text{for every }\omega\in H^{0}(X,K_{X}),

then
c=0
.

Proof.

Choose a symplectic basis
a_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{1},\ldots,b_{g}
of
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
and normalized holomorphic differentials
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{g}
with
\int_{a_{i}}\omega_{j}=\delta_{ij}
. Let
\tau_{ij}=\int_{b_{i}}\omega_{j}
be the period matrix. By the Riemann bilinear relations,
\tau
is symmetric and
\operatorname{Im}\tau
is positive definite; see, for example, Farkas–Kra
[
5
, Ch. III]
. If

c=\sum_{i}p_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i}q_{i}b_{i},\qquad p_{i},q_{i}\in\mathbb{R},

then the vector of periods of
c
is
p+\tau^{t}q
, up to the harmless convention of row versus column vectors. If all these periods vanish, taking imaginary parts gives
(\operatorname{Im}\tau)^{t}q=0
, hence
q=0
, and then also
p=0
.
∎

Thus a nonzero cycle cannot be invisible to all holomorphic differentials. The possible loophole in our problem is narrower: the Cauchy-transform family does not give all holomorphic differentials a priori, but only the holomorphic projections of the special meromorphic differentials

\Omega_{n}=\eta\,\pi^{n}d\pi,\qquad n\geq 0.

After subtracting exact terms and choosing normalized differentials of the second and third kind with the same prescribed principal parts and residues, write
\Omega_{n}^{\mathrm{hol}}
for the remaining holomorphic component. The choice of normalization should be fixed once and for all, for instance by imposing vanishing
a
-periods on the second- and third-kind pieces. Set

W_{\pi,\eta}:=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\Omega_{n}^{\mathrm{hol}}:n\geq 0\}\subseteq H^{0}(X,K_{X}).

Lemma
6.1
gives the useful finite-rank principle

\begin{gathered}W_{\pi,\eta}=H^{0}(X,K_{X})\\
\Longrightarrow\quad\text{no nonzero real homology class is invisible to this Abelian test.}\end{gathered}

Since
\dim H^{0}(X,K_{X})=g
, this condition is in principle checkable by finitely many of the differentials
\Omega_{n}
. This implication is a rank test for non-invisibility; it is not by itself a theorem of non-algebraicity of the Cauchy transform.

For a nonsingular projective plane curve of degree
d
, written in an affine chart as
P(z,w)=0
with no projective singularities, a standard basis of holomorphic differentials is represented by

\frac{z^{i}w^{j}dz}{P_{w}(z,w)},\qquad i+j\leq d-3.

Consequently one can try to reduce the differentials
wz^{n}dz
modulo exact differentials and principal parts at infinity and then compute their holomorphic projections in this basis. If the resulting coefficient matrix has rank
g=(d-1)(d-2)/2
, then no nonzero oval class can be invisible to this holomorphic period map. For singular projective closures, or for curves which are only affine-smooth, this basis must be replaced by the corresponding adjoint differentials on the normalization and the genus is the geometric genus, not necessarily
(d-1)(d-2)/2
. Thus the period-rank condition provides a concrete route toward excluding exceptional cancellation in the one-oval smooth even-degree case, but an additional analytic step is still needed to turn such non-invisibility into a proof of non-algebraicity.

This leads to the following working conjecture.

Conjecture 6.2

(Separating/nonseparating algebraicity principle)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a smooth algebraic oval and let
\gamma
be its lift to the normalization
X
of the relevant irreducible Schwarz component. If
\gamma
is separating, then the exterior Cauchy transform is algebraic. If
\gamma
is nonseparating, then the exterior Cauchy transform is expected to be generically non-algebraic; in this conjectural picture algebraicity can occur only when the Abelian-period component of

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\eta\,d\pi}{z-\pi}

vanishes identically or cancels with the residue terms in an exceptional way.

A more effective program is to isolate the residue part first and then study whether the remaining full
z
-dependent Abelian expression is algebraic. The period moments

\int_{\gamma}\eta\,\pi^{n}\,d\pi,\qquad n\geq 0,

after the purely residue contributions have been removed give useful necessary data for this program, but they are not a standalone algebraicity criterion. This formulation is compatible with rational curves, separating positive-genus examples, complete real loci of type I real curves, and the standard theory of multiply connected quadrature domains.

Conjecture 6.3

(Multi-oval generic non-algebraicity)
.

Let
X
be the normalization of the relevant irreducible real Schwarz component, and suppose that
X(\mathbb{R})
has at least two smooth connected components. Then each individual component is nonseparating by Lemma
5.2
. For a generic affine Euclidean realization, the exterior Cauchy transform of the domain bounded by any one of these ovals is non-algebraic.

The word “generic” is important. A nonseparating class need not be detected by a particular family of meromorphic differentials. In this conjectural picture, algebraicity for one oval can occur only if its homology class lies in the annihilator of the Abelian-period part of the family

\left\{\eta\,\pi^{n}d\pi\right\}_{n\geq 0},

after exact and residue contributions have been removed. Thus a single nonseparating oval may be invisible to the relevant period map in exceptional examples. The conjecture is that this invisibility is not stable under a generic Euclidean affine realization.

Consequently the robust positive-genus single-oval algebraic situation should be expected only in special one-real-component dividing models, in particular in even-degree hyperelliptic or acnodal plane models where the normalized real locus consists of one smooth oval.

Conjecture 6.4

(Smooth one-oval curves of degree at least four)
.

Let
\Gamma
be the unique real oval of a nonsingular real plane curve of even degree
2k\geq 4
, and let
\Omega
be the bounded component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
. Then the exterior Cauchy transform of
\Omega
is not algebraic.

This conjecture is deliberately stronger than the generic non-algebraicity statements above. Proposition
5.4
explains why such curves are necessarily on the nonseparating side. What remains to prove is that the exceptional period-kernel cancellation cannot occur for the unique smooth oval of a nonsingular plane curve. At present we regard this as a natural problem rather than a theorem.

7.
Quadrature domains

A bounded plane domain
\Omega
is a quadrature domain for area measure if there exist finitely many nodes
a_{j}\in\Omega
and constants
c_{jk}
such that

\int_{\Omega}h(\zeta)dA(\zeta)=\sum_{j,k}c_{jk}h^{(k)}(a_{j})

for all integrable analytic functions
h
on
\Omega
. Applying this identity to
h(\zeta)=(z-\zeta)^{-1}
shows that the exterior Cauchy transform of a quadrature domain is rational. Conversely, under the usual regularity assumptions, rationality of the exterior Cauchy transform near infinity is one of the standard equivalent formulations of the quadrature-domain property; see Aharonov–Shapiro
[
1
]
, Sakai
[
11
]
, Gustafsson–Shapiro
[
8
]
, and Gustafsson–Putinar
[
6
]
.

For simply connected quadrature domains there is a particularly concrete form: a bounded simply connected domain is a quadrature domain if and only if its Riemann map from the unit disk extends as a rational map on the Riemann sphere. Thus simply connected quadrature-domain boundaries are rationally parametrized; for background and examples, see also the collection
[
4
]
.

For multiply connected quadrature domains this statement must not be replaced by a rational-plane-curve assertion. The natural object is the Schottky double of
\Omega
, a compact Riemann surface of genus equal to the connectivity of
\Omega
minus one. The Schwarz function extends meromorphically to this double, and the boundary is encoded by meromorphic functions on that double; see Gustafsson’s work on quadrature identities and the Schottky double
[
7
]
. Hence a multiply connected quadrature domain is generally described by meromorphic functions on a positive-genus Riemann surface, not by a rational normalization.

This distinction is important for the present paper. Algebraicity of
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}
is weaker than the quadrature-domain property. The ellipse, for example, has algebraic but non-rational exterior Cauchy transform. Conversely, a quadrature domain has rational transform, but in the multiply connected case its natural algebraic curve may have positive genus.

8.
Examples

8.1.
The ellipse

Let

\Omega=\left\{(x,y):\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{y^{2}}{b^{2}}<1\right\},\qquad a>b>0,

and set
c^{2}=a^{2}-b^{2}
. Then

(4)

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{2ab}{c^{2}}\left(z-\sqrt{z^{2}-c^{2}}\right)=\frac{2ab}{z+\sqrt{z^{2}-c^{2}}},

where
\sqrt{z^{2}-c^{2}}\sim z
at infinity. Thus

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{ab}{z}+O(z^{-3}),

as required by
\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)/\pi=ab
. The transform is algebraic of degree two and is rational only when
a=b
.

8.2.
Cubics: the three regimes

Cubics provide a useful test of the corrected formulation.

First, a nonsingular real cubic has elliptic normalization. If it has two real components, one of them is a compact affine oval and the other is the projective pseudoline. On the elliptic normalization the compact oval is nonseparating: it is not the boundary of a two-chain, or equivalently it represents a nonzero homology class on the underlying torus. Therefore the residue criterion does not apply to a single compact oval of a smooth cubic. The conjectural picture predicts that its Cauchy transform should be non-algebraic for a generic smooth cubic, with the obstruction supplied by elliptic periods of

\frac{\eta\,d\pi}{z-\pi}.

This is probably the first genuinely nontrivial case of the Abelian-period obstruction.

Second, an irreducible singular cubic is rational. However, its real loop usually passes through the node or cusp. The lifted contour is then not a closed cycle on the normalization, but a relative interval whose endpoints lie over the singular point. In this case the genus-zero theorem for closed lifts does not apply. Logarithmic endpoint terms may survive, as in the nodal example below.

Third, reducible cubics contain no new phenomenon for the present question. If the bounded oval is a conic component, then one is back in the conic case, and the transform is algebraic of degree at most two.

Thus cubics already show the three mechanisms of the paper:

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\text{smooth elliptic cubic}&\leadsto&\text{period obstruction},\\[2.84526pt]
\text{singular rational cubic}&\leadsto&\text{relative-endpoint logarithms},\\[2.84526pt]
\text{conic plus line}&\leadsto&\text{rational-normalization algebraicity}.\end{array}

8.3.
Conditional positive-genus algebraicity outside quadrature domains

Proposition
5.6
and Corollary
5.8
give a conditional scheme, not a generic-existence theorem, for positive-genus examples which are algebraic but not quadrature domains. Concretely, start with a compact bordered Riemann surface
S
of genus one and one boundary component, and let
X
be its Schottky double. Then
X
has genus two and its real locus is a single component; as a whole real locus it is dividing, and the chosen oval is separating. If one chooses real meromorphic functions satisfying the regularity and birationality hypotheses of Proposition
5.6
, then the plane map

X\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2},\qquad p\longmapsto(z(p),w(p)),

with
w=\bar{z}
on the real oval gives an irreducible real affine algebraic curve whose real locus contains a smooth Jordan oval
\Gamma
. The domain bounded by
\Gamma
is simply connected, the lifted oval is separating and bounds
S
, and hence

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(Z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\bar{\zeta}\,d\zeta}{Z-\zeta}

is algebraic by residues. If the relevant Schwarz component remains birational to
X
, then the non-quadrature conclusion follows from Corollary
5.8
.

The same scheme applies in every even genus by taking
S
of genus
h\geq 1
with one boundary component, provided the plane-model hypotheses are met. It is useful conceptually because it separates two notions which are sometimes conflated:

\text{quadrature domain}\Rightarrow\text{rational Cauchy transform}\Rightarrow\text{algebraic Cauchy transform},

but the reverse implications fail. Such separating positive-genus models, once realized with the stated hypotheses, lie in the algebraic but non-quadrature class.

8.4.
A nodal cubic loop

Let
\Omega
be the bounded loop of the real nodal cubic

y^{2}=x^{2}(1-x).

A parametrization of the loop is

x=1-s^{2},\qquad y=s(1-s^{2}),\qquad-1\leq s\leq 1,

so

\zeta(s)=(1-s^{2})(1+is),\qquad\bar{\zeta}(s)=(1-s^{2})(1-is).

The lift of the loop to the rational normalization is not a closed contour; it is the interval
[-1,1]
, whose two endpoints map to the node. Proposition
2.1
gives

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\bar{\zeta}(s)\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{z-\zeta(s)}ds.

For fixed
z
, this is an integral of a rational function of
s
. Its primitive is a sum of a rational term and logarithms at the roots of
\zeta(s)=z
. Near the node
z=0
, the two endpoint branches satisfy

\zeta(s)=(-2-2i)(s-1)+O((s-1)^{2}),\qquad\zeta(s)=(2-2i)(s+1)+O((s+1)^{2}).

Here is the local calculation. Near
s=1
, putting
t=s-1
, one has

\zeta=a_{+}t+O(t^{2}),\qquad\bar{\zeta}=b_{+}t+O(t^{2}),\qquad a_{+}=-2-2i,\quad b_{+}=-2+2i,

and hence
\bar{\zeta}\,\zeta^{\prime}=8t+O(t^{2})
. The singular part of the endpoint integral is therefore

\int\frac{8t}{z-a_{+}t}\,dt=-\frac{8z}{a_{+}^{2}}\log(z-a_{+}t)+\text{holomorphic terms},

so the endpoint contributes
iz\log z+O(z^{2}\log z)
up to the orientation convention. Near
s=-1
, with
t=s+1
, one has

a_{-}=2-2i,\qquad b_{-}=2+2i,\qquad\bar{\zeta}\,\zeta^{\prime}=8t+O(t^{2}),

and the opposite endpoint contributes the same raw logarithmic coefficient. Thus the raw integral has logarithmic coefficient
2iz+O(z^{2})
, and after multiplication by
1/(2\pi i)
the coefficient is
z/\pi+O(z^{2})
, up to the overall orientation sign. In particular a nonzero logarithmic term survives at the node. Therefore the Cauchy transform of the nodal cubic loop is not algebraic.

This example shows that rational normalization alone is not sufficient for algebraicity: one needs a closed lifted contour, or more generally a bounding two-chain. Relative chains ending at singular points may produce logarithms.

8.5.
A smooth Weierstrass cubic as the first elliptic-period test

For a smooth real cubic with a compact oval, the normalization is an elliptic curve. In the usual two-component case the compact affine oval is nonseparating on the elliptic normalization. Therefore the residue criterion does not force algebraicity. This is the first natural case where the period obstruction can be made quite explicit.

We treat here a special affine Weierstrass model, rather than an arbitrary smooth affine cubic. This qualification is important: putting a plane cubic into Weierstrass form usually uses a projective transformation depending on a flex at infinity, and the Cauchy transform is tied to the chosen Euclidean coordinate
z=x+iy
. Hence the following calculation is a model calculation for a Weierstrass cubic in the chosen affine Euclidean coordinates, not an affine normal form for all smooth cubics.

Let

E=\mathbb{C}/\Lambda,\qquad\Lambda=2\omega_{1}\mathbb{Z}+2\omega_{2}\mathbb{Z},

and let
\wp=\wp_{\Lambda}
be the corresponding Weierstrass function. Put

X(u)=\wp(u),\qquad Y(u)=\wp^{\prime}(u),

so that

Y^{2}=4X^{3}-g_{2}X-g_{3}.

Choose real invariants for which the real locus has a compact oval, and use the Euclidean coordinate

z=X+iY.

The associated Schwarz coordinates on the elliptic normalization are

\pi(u)=X(u)+iY(u)=\wp(u)+i\wp^{\prime}(u),\qquad\eta(u)=X(u)-iY(u)=\wp(u)-i\wp^{\prime}(u).

On the chosen real oval one has
\eta=\overline{\pi}
. Hence

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\Omega_{z},\qquad\Omega_{z}=\frac{\eta(u)\pi^{\prime}(u)}{z-\pi(u)}\,du,

that is,

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\bigl(\wp(u)-i\wp^{\prime}(u)\bigr)\bigl(\wp^{\prime}(u)+i\wp^{\prime\prime}(u)\bigr)}{z-\wp(u)-i\wp^{\prime}(u)}\,du.

Thus the Cauchy transform of this Weierstrass cubic oval is a complete elliptic integral of a meromorphic elliptic differential.

Let
a_{1}(z),a_{2}(z),a_{3}(z)
be the three points of
E
satisfying

\pi(a_{j}(z))=z,

counted with multiplicity and away from the branch values of
\pi
. The number three appears because
\pi
has a pole of order three at
u=0
. At a moving pole one has

\operatorname*{res}_{u=a_{j}(z)}\Omega_{z}=-\eta(a_{j}(z)).

The remaining poles are fixed poles at
u=0
coming from the principal part of
\pi
and
\eta\,d\pi
.

By the classical reduction of elliptic differentials
[
12
, Ch. XX]
,
\Omega_{z}
can be written as an exact differential plus a linear combination of
du
and normalized differentials of the second and third kind. Equivalently, a primitive can be written in terms of the Weierstrass
\zeta
- and
\sigma
-functions. In such a representation the coefficients of the third-kind terms are the residues
-\eta(a_{j}(z))
, but the reduction also produces quasi-period constants involving
a_{j}(z)
and
\zeta(a_{j}(z))
. This point is essential: a formula which keeps only the Abelian sums
\sum\eta(a_{j})a_{j}
and ignores the accompanying
\zeta(a_{j})
terms is not invariantly correct.

For a period cycle
2\omega_{k}
one obtains a formula of the following form:

\int_{2\omega_{k}}\Omega_{z}=A_{k}(z)+\Phi_{k}\bigl(a_{1}(z),a_{2}(z),a_{3}(z),\zeta(a_{1}(z)),\zeta(a_{2}(z)),\zeta(a_{3}(z))\bigr),

where
A_{k}(z)
is algebraic, coming from fixed principal parts and residue terms, and
\Phi_{k}
is an explicit expression built from the quasi-periods of
\zeta
and the residues at the moving poles. The exact expression depends on the chosen normalization of the third-kind differentials and on the representative of the punctured homology class; changing the representative by small loops around moving poles changes the integral by algebraic residue terms. The non-algebraic candidate is therefore the whole elliptic-period expression involving
a_{j}(z)
and
\zeta(a_{j}(z))
, not a single unnormalized sum.

This gives a concrete form of the nonseparating obstruction. Abel’s theorem gives

a_{1}(z)+a_{2}(z)+a_{3}(z)\equiv 0\pmod{\Lambda},

because the pole divisor of
\pi-z
is
3(0)
. It does not force the corresponding residue-weighted quasi-period expression to be algebraic. Thus the smooth Weierstrass cubic reduces the expected non-algebraicity problem to an explicit cancellation problem for classical elliptic functions.

Conjecture 8.1

(Smooth Weierstrass cubic period test)
.

For a generic smooth real Weierstrass cubic in the above affine Euclidean model, the compact oval is nonseparating and the exterior Cauchy transform of the domain bounded by it is not algebraic. Equivalently, the elliptic-period contribution in the reduction of
\Omega_{z}
to Weierstrass
\zeta
- and
\sigma
-terms does not cancel algebraically with the residue part.

This formulation is useful because it converts the first positive-genus test into a concrete calculation with classical elliptic functions, while avoiding an unjustified formula-level simplification.

9.
Summary of the algebraicity picture

The theorem-level conclusions of the paper are the following.

(1)

The Cauchy–Green boundary formula lifts the exterior Cauchy transform to a Cauchy-type integral on the normalization of the relevant irreducible Schwarz component.

(2)

The fixed-normalization monodromy is point-pushing monodromy of moving punctures. After the moving punctures are filled, it acts trivially on absolute homology. Hence non-algebraicity cannot be inferred from a formal absolute Picard–Lefschetz transvection in this setting.

(3)

If the lifted boundary is separating, equivalently if it bounds an integral two-chain on the relevant normalization, the exterior transform is algebraic by residues. The residue formula must be read as an exterior-branch statement; on other components of the complement it gives boundary-integral branches.

(4)

Consequently, every smooth compact oval on a rational real algebraic curve has algebraic exterior Cauchy transform. Separating positive-genus ovals and complete real loci of dividing curves, when they occur with the required affine-plane and orientation hypotheses, give the same residue-theoretic algebraicity.

(5)

Quadrature domains form a stricter class: their exterior Cauchy transforms are rational. Simply connected quadrature domains are rationally parametrized, while multiply connected quadrature domains are naturally described on Schottky doubles of positive genus.

The remaining nonseparating picture should be regarded as a research program. If the chosen oval is nonseparating, topology alone does not prove non-algebraicity. The period-rank construction isolates a possible Abelian contribution of the family
\eta\,d\pi/(z-\pi)
and can rule out one type of accidental invisibility, but a proof of non-algebraicity still requires an additional argument: finite-monodromy failure, a surviving logarithmic term, a quasi-periodic elliptic or higher-genus term, or another mechanism proving that the full
z
-dependent expression is not algebraic. The smooth cubic, the multi-oval case, and smooth one-oval even-degree curves of degree greater than two are therefore best presented as test cases and conjectures rather than as settled consequences of the residue theorem.
