Reforestation following megafires in boreal forests: silvicultural challenges and available tools
Authors: Claudie-Maude Canuel, Faustine Guyot, François Hébert, Émie Jacques-Beaudoin, Daniel Dumais, Nelson Thiffault, Martin Barrette, Yan Boucher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In North America, climate change is projected to increase wildfire activity, leading to more frequent megafires that will likely overwhelm current regeneration processes, threatening the resilience of boreal forests and the services that they provide. The devastating 2023 wildfire season exposes the magnitude of the challenge facing North America, including eastern regions. Post-fire reforestation needs can far exceed current capacity, which relies almost exclusively on manual tree planting. Our review addresses urgent needs to increase reforestation capacity to restore vast burned landscapes that are experiencing regeneration failures. We first provide an overview of current challenges and promising reforestation methods for tree planting and direct seeding. Challenges include addressing regeneration failure risks and limited road access, and strengthening reforestation value chains amid workforce shortages. We discuss the suitability of traditional and innovative reforestation methods, considering these challenges, to situate them within a broader restoration strategy. We show that using multiple methods expands reforestation capacity by providing more options for reforesting sites with a wider range of biophysical conditions. However, robust evidence on method-specific outcomes across different timescales is still needed to understand their effectiveness for post-fire restoration. Future research should further assess the cost-benefits of these methods and improve our ability to predict and mitigate regeneration failures across varying forest landscapes to support efficient restoration efforts. Our work provides a foundation for transferring current knowledge into strategic reforestation planning and execution. It is relevant to communities, scientists, governments and managers seeking to adapt regeneration silviculture to changing fire regimes.

Introduction

Boreal forests provide essential ecosystem services and functions, notably acting as major global carbon reservoirs (Pan et al. 2024), wildlife habitats, and sources of timber (Gauthier et al. 2015a). One-third of Earth’s boreal forests is located in North America, with the vast majority in Canada (Brandt et al. 2013; Saucier et al. 2024). These forests are shaped primarily by natural disturbances, with wildfire being the main driver of these ecosystems (Weber and Stocks 1998; Brandt et al. 2013). Indeed, the regeneration mechanisms of their most common tree species are well adapted to fire (Greene et al. 1999; Chen and Popadiouk 2002; Anyomi et al. 2022). Most dominant conifer species (Fig. 1), such as black spruce (Picea mariana [Mill.] B.S.P.) and jack pine (Pinus banksiana Lamb.), have semi-serotinous or serotinous cones, respectively, that retain seeds to varying degrees and open in response to fire (Greene et al. 1999; Aubin et al. 2016). Secondary broadleaf species, such as quaking aspen (Populus tremuloides Michx.) and paper birch (Betula papyrifera Marsh.), can regenerate vegetatively or through long-distance seed dispersal (Greene et al. 1999; Aubin et al. 2016). These natural adaptations have contributed to a relatively stable ecological state that has persisted for the last thousand years, as post-fire vegetation trajectories have regulated fuel flammability and wildfire activity (Kelly et al. 2013; Tepley et al. 2018).

Boreal forests are subject to the fastest rates of warming and precipitation change globally (IPCC 2007; Boulanger and Pascual Puigdevall 2021), triggering shifts in fire regimes (Boulanger et al. 2013; Hanes et al. 2019; Coogan et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2025b). Rising air temperatures, together with more frequent and severe droughts, are likely increasing vegetation flammability and extending the wildfire season (Flannigan et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2025a; Benoît et al. 2025). The occurrence of wildfires, their size and severity will likely increase (Wotton et al. 2010; Boulanger et al. 2014; Boulanger and Pascual Puigdevall 2021), which can lead to more megafires (i.e., fires > 10 000 hectares (Linley et al. 2022)) and overwhelmed suppression capacity in the coming years (Flannigan et al. 2009; Podur and Wotton 2010; Wotton et al. 2017). Canada has already experienced some of the most severe wildfire seasons on record in the early 21st century (Mulverhill et al. 2024). In 2023, about 15 M hectares burned across Canada, far surpassing the 1989 record of 6.7 M hectares and reaching nearly seven times the 1986–2020 annual average of 2.2 M hectares (Skakun et al. 2022; Jain et al. 2024). This area burned record includes over 4.5 M hectares in Quebec, exceeding the previous provincial maximum of 2.3 M hectares in 1989, with individual fires reaching record sizes of up to 1 M hectares (Boulanger et al. 2025). The 2023 season highlights the effects that changing fire regimes can have in more humid eastern boreal forests, which have traditionally been considered less vulnerable than western regions (Flannigan et al. 2001; Chavardès et al. 2022). Whether contemporary burning rates are surpassing historical variability is still being debated (Parks et al. 2025; Danneyrolles et al. 2025), but recent years have signalled the severity, magnitude, and impacts expected under future fire regimes driven by climate change (Jain et al. 2024; Boulanger et al. 2025).

There is increased concern that novel fire regimes under climate change will alter boreal forest states by reducing densities of coniferous species or by promoting transitions from closed-crown forests to open-crown ecosystems, a phenomenon that is referred to as regeneration failure (Boiffin and Munson 2013; Boucher et al. 2020; Baltzer et al. 2021; Stevens-Rumann et al. 2018, 2022; Fortin et al. 2026). This concern is amplified in portions of the boreal forest landscapes that have been substantially rejuvenated by logging activities over the past century (Cyr et al. 2009; Boucher et al. 2017; Malcolm et al. 2025), reducing the availability of sexually mature, seed-bearing trees and thereby undermining post-fire regeneration capacity of dominant species (Girard et al. 2008; Splawinski et al. 2019). In Quebec, following the 2023 fire season, 300 000 to 400 000 hectares may require reforestation due to regeneration failure, which is roughly four to eight times the current annual capacity of 50 000 to 75 000 hectares per year (Boulanger et al. 2025; Forestier en chef du Québec 2023). Increased regeneration failures may jeopardize forest ecosystem services, notably by reducing forest productivity (Van Bogaert et al. 2015), wood supply (Gauthier et al. 2015b; Boucher et al. 2018; Bouchard et al. 2023), carbon sequestration (Ménard et al. 2023), and habitat quality for some wildlife species, such as woodland caribou (Rangifer tarandus caribou) (Tremblay et al. 2018; Russell et al. 2025). Consequently, it is essential to increase reforestation capacity either to maintain or to restore boreal forest services and functions (Dobrowski et al. 2024).

Manual tree planting is the primary reforestation method in North American boreal forest ecosystems (Box 1). It is known to result in reliable seedling survival and growth, especially when used with adequate site preparation treatments (Örlander et al. 1990; Thiffault and Hébert 2017; Sikström et al. 2020). However, this method is limited by tree nursery capacity and requires a large labour force, which is difficult to secure (Fargione et al. 2021). It also requires an extensive road network to access sites that need to be regenerated, which can be challenging in remote parts of the boreal forest. Changing fire regimes are likely to exacerbate these challenges and, therefore, increase management demands and costs (Fargione et al. 2021; Cyr et al. 2022). There is a need to optimize silvicultural efforts and develop alternative reforestation methods that address megafire events (Dumroese et al. 2019), while avoiding overwhelming reforestation capacity (Dobrowski et al. 2024; Brant et al. 2026).

Our review addresses the urgent need to reforest burned landscapes experiencing regeneration failures to restore their forest state and sustain the services they provide. We aim to explore the potential of different reforestation methods to increase landscape-level reforestation capacity, based on current knowledge. Reforestation methods include both traditional and innovative methods, namely, mechanized tree planting, aerial seeding, and mechanized ground seeding. First, we present an overview of the main challenges to post-fire reforestation, which are informed by experts. Second, we present the different methods and discuss their potential and limitations for addressing reforestation challenges. Third, we summarize the reviewed literature to identify trends and future research perspectives. We used a narrative literature review approach, selecting peer-reviewed articles and technical reports from government agencies and well-known research and development organizations, written in French or English (Pautasso 2019). Searches of academic databases, primarily Google Scholar, were supplemented with organization-specific databases and the reference lists of seminal articles. We focused on the eastern North American boreal forests, but broadened the geographic scope by drawing on literature from other regions, primarily within North America and the Nordic countries, given their relevance to our study focus. We discuss the results in a broader context to offer valuable insights for other forested regions worldwide that are also susceptible to wildfires.

Manual tree planting with or without site preparation is the primary reforestation method used in the eastern boreal forests of Canada. In Newfoundland and Labrador, Quebec, Ontario and Manitoba, more than 205 M seedlings (114 900 ha) were planted on public land in 2020 (CCFM 2025). The most planted species groups were spruces (77%) and pines (22%), while the planting of broadleaf species (< 1%) remained scarce (CCFM 2025). High standards for seed collection and handling, together with orchards, have markedly improved seed quality, with germination rates now reaching up to 98% for coniferous species (Colas and Bettez 2013; Ministry of Natural Resources & Forestry n.d.). Site preparation is primarily performed using mechanical methods and occurred over 112 000 hectares in 2020 (CCFM 2025). In contrast, chemical site preparation accounted for 7% of the treated area and occurs primarily in Ontario and Manitoba; the use of chemical herbicides has been banned from use on public lands in Quebec since 2001 (Thiffault and Roy 2011) and is now limited in other provinces as well, such as Nova Scotia.

Direct seeding has historically been a secondary reforestation method in the eastern boreal forests of Canada, with its share contribution declining from more than 14% to less than 4% of the reforested public area in 1990 and 2020, respectively (CCFM 2025). Broadcast aerial seeding and ground mechanized seeding have been used since the mid-1900s (Fleming et al. 2001), while the use of drones has recently gained interest (Matute 2020; Mohan et al. 2021). While direct seeding was mainly used to regenerate jack pine, aerial seeding was also used to regenerate black spruce on sites meeting its stricter establishment requirements (Fleming et al. 2001).

The use of mechanized tree planting has been tested over the last few decades, but it remains primarily experimental. Trials were conducted notably with the Silva Nova, developed through a Swedish industry collaboration, and the Bracke Planter P11.a from Bracke Forest AB (Bräcke, Sweden) (Cormier and Ryans 1992; St-Amour 2009a). In recent years, the PlantMax or its prototype, Plantma X, developed by PlantMa AB (Ludvika, Sweden), has been tested in New Brunswick and Quebec and is gaining interest (Fullerton 2024; Irving 2024).

Challenges for reforesting burned landscapes

Understanding regeneration failure risks

Forest-specific regeneration dynamics

Black spruce-dominated stands characterize much of eastern Canadian boreal forests (Fig. 1) and are the most important sources of wood supplies for the forestry sector (Boucher et al. 2018). However, post-fire regeneration failure in these stands is well documented (Perrault-Hébert et al. 2017). Black spruce is a slow-growing species that reaches sexual maturity at around 50 years or later, with variations being observed between different regions (Viglas et al. 2013; Alfaro-Sánchez et al. 2022; Splawinski et al. 2022) (Table 3). Thus, black spruce-dominated stands remain exposed to disturbances for extended periods before creating an aerial seed bank that supports their regeneration. This prolonged exposure before maturity exacerbates their vulnerability to successive wildfires and increases the risk of regeneration failure (Boucher et al. 2020; Baltzer et al. 2021). Destruction of reproductive organs by eastern spruce budworm (Choristoneura fumiferana [Clem.]) outbreaks can further reduce viable aerial seed banks and increase post-fire regeneration failures of black spruce (Prévost et al. 1988; Simard and Payette 2005). The closed-crown canopy of black spruce-dominated forests is especially at risk at higher latitudes under shorter wildfire cycles and growing seasons (Girard et al. 2008; Splawinski et al. 2022). Successive disturbances over short intervals notably drive transitions from closed-crown forests to lichen woodlands as an alternative stable state. Although this natural process has occurred over the last millennium (Jasinski and Payette 2005), it is now likely expanding in extent (Boiffin and Munson 2013; Splawinski et al. 2019; Baltzer et al. 2021; Fortin et al. 2026), as suggested by a 9% transition rate in central Quebec between 1954 and 2002 (Girard et al. 2008). Moreover, the semi-serotinous cones of black spruce remain partially open at ambient temperature (Viereck and Johnston 1990) and high-severity crown fires associated with megafire events can reduce the viability of the aerial seed bank and regeneration success (Johnstone et al. 2009; Lamont et al. 2020; Fortin et al. 2026). Consequently, sites that are dominated by black spruce can be particularly vulnerable to changing fire regimes despite the adaptation mechanisms of this species to wildfire.

Post-fire regeneration failure resulting in persistent canopy loss is likely less common in jack pine-dominated, broadleaf-dominated, and mixed-species dominated sites in the eastern North American boreal forests (Baltzer et al. 2021) (Fig. 1). Jack pine-dominated stands typically require fewer seed trees (e.g., three times less) to successfully regenerate post-fire than do black spruce-dominated stands (Alfaro-Sánchez et al. 2022). Jack pine reaches sexual maturity around 20 to 30 years, although it can vary across regions (Pinno et al. 2013; Splawinski et al. 2014; Alfaro-Sánchez et al. 2022) (Table 3). Its fully serotinous cones also contribute to its greater resilience to high-severity crown fires compared to black spruce (see above) (Boiffin and Munson 2013; Aubin et al. 2016). Boreal broadleaf species such as quaking aspen and paper birch typically become well-established after wildfires of varying severity (Greene et al. 2004; Dawe et al. 2025) (Table 3). Their presence as companion species can thus reduce the risk of closed-crown canopy loss despite a reduction in black spruce dominance in boreal landscapes, which may lead to a change in successional trajectory (Chen et al. 2009; Johnstone et al. 2020; Baltzer et al. 2021; Day et al. 2023). However, changes in fire regimes can still reduce viable seedbank in some jack pine-dominated sites, which may result in a post-fire density loss (Rapanoela et al. 2015; Baltzer et al. 2021) or understocked sites in the most severe cases (Pinno et al. 2013). This phenomenon may be accentuated by the natural propensity of jack pine to establish on poor sites under shorter fire intervals (Van Bogaert et al. 2015). The vegetative regeneration capacity of quaking aspen may also be reduced by high-severity ground fire and harsh post-fire climate conditions (Whitman et al. 2019; Dawe et al. 2025) (Table 3). Future climatic conditions that will likely change wildfire behaviour and increase wildfire activity, such as droughts, reduced snow precipitation, and elevated temperatures, may further alter the regeneration process of these common tree species (Boucher et al. 2020; Wotherspoon et al. 2024).

Post-fire conditions

Seedbed quality and the microenvironment at the soil surface are important for creating suitable microsites for seed germination, seedling establishment and growth (Simard et al. 1998; Grossnickle 2018). High-severity ground fire can create receptive seedbeds for most commercial tree species, including black spruce, jack pine, quaking aspen and paper birch, by exposing the mineral soil or reducing organic layer thickness to a few centimetres (e.g., 2 cm) (Greene et al. 2007) (Table 3). It can temporarily reduce the abundance of competing vegetation that has remained in the understory (Smirnova et al. 2008), which, in turn, can create good conditions for the survival and growth of conifer seedlings (Mallik 2003; Hébert et al. 2010). However, post-fire availability of receptive seedbed may vary widely (e.g., 3 to 81% of burned areas) from one fire to another (Greene and Johnson 2000; Greene et al. 2007). Receptive seedbeds following wildfire can be especially limited in eastern North American boreal forests, where the humid and cold climate favours the development of a thick, moist organic layer that can restrict their formation (Miyanishi and Johnson 2002; Veilleux-Nolin and Payette 2012; Boiffin and Munson 2013; Greene et al. 2025). Moreover, rapid colonization by competing vegetation and litter accumulation at the soil surface can quickly deteriorate microsite quality (Dyrness and Norum 1983; Fleming and Mossa 1995; Johnstone and Kasischke 2005; Tsuyuzaki et al. 2014).

During the seedling establishment phase, post-fire canopy removal and blackened surfaces further increase soil surface temperature and temperature variations, and decrease water retention (Moss and Hermanutz 2009; Boiffin and Munson 2013), increasing the risk of desiccation or freezing damage (Boucher et al. 2020). Black spruce is sensitive to desiccation, contributing to its vulnerability to drought during the post-fire establishment phase (Moss and Hermanutz 2009; Boucher et al. 2020). In comparison, jack pine, quaking aspen, and paper birch are more tolerant of some harsh post-fire microenvironmental conditions, which contributes to their better resilience (Boucher et al. 2020). Wildfire and its associated effects on post-fire regeneration can vary with surficial deposits, topography and aspect, which in turn influence moisture regimes and microenvironments (Veilleux-Nolin and Payette 2012; Whitman et al. 2019; Day et al. 2023; Lanti-Traikovski et al. 2025).

Other factors, such as predation, may further impede seed germination or seedling survival and contribute to post-fire regeneration failures (Lobo 2014; Champagne et al. 2021; Day et al. 2023). However, how predation interacts with wildfires and post-fire conditions remains unclear. Some authors have suggested negligible post-fire predation, especially far from residual green forests (e.g., Charron and Greene 2002; Adams et al. 2005; Johnstone et al. 2020), yet others have reported significant damage, particularly during winter and early spring in recently burned areas (Côté et al. 2003; Huggard and Arsenault 2009; Barnett 2014). Varying levels of predation by invertebrates (e.g., ants and slugs) and vertebrates (e.g., rodents and birds) have been observed, including seed losses up to 35% in recently burned areas in eastern Canada (Côté et al. 2003, 2005). These findings support that post-fire regeneration success can vary locally and temporally, following complex interactions that may involve wildlife (Murphy et al. 2018; Smit and Coetsee 2019).

Implications for reforestation

Accounting for natural regeneration in reforestation prescriptions is typically mandatory, and the lack of natural regeneration influences both the area requiring intervention and the intensity of subsequent treatments. Hence, post-fire reforestation efforts must account for the risk of regeneration failure, which depends on interactions among multiple factors and the alignment of favourable conditions (Fig. 2). Reforestation must be coordinated with post-fire recovery surveys based on field or remote sensing data (e.g., Smith-Tripp et al. 2026) and consider the timing of regeneration establishment (Stevens-Rumann et al. 2022). In North America, seedling establishment occurs mainly within the first three years after wildfire (St-Pierre et al. 1992; Charron and Greene 2002; Greene et al. 2004; Johnstone et al. 2020). In rarer cases, black spruce seedling establishment can extend for up to eight years due to slower seed dispersal compared to jack pine, particularly following low-severity wildfires (Veilleux-Nolin and Payette 2012; Greene et al. 2013; Baltzer et al. 2021). This can push reforestation activities to several years post-disturbance (e.g., five years for planting nursery seedlings; see Sect. "Scaling up the reforestation value chain"), limit reforestation options, and necessitate prior silvicultural interventions (e.g., site preparation) (see Sect. "Creating suitable microsites"). Predictive modelling has the potential to complement surveys in reforestation decisions to promote prompt post-fire regeneration (e.g., Splawinski et al. 2015; Holden et al. 2025), which is essential for reducing forest management costs and achieving reforestation goals (Dumroese et al. 2019).

Defining natural regeneration thresholds (e.g., minimal seedling density, distribution and composition at a specific time since fire) under which a regeneration failure occurs is a current challenge (Stevens-Rumann et al. 2022). Post-fire regeneration failure sometimes refers to reduced density of commercial conifer species compared to pre-fire characteristics, while at other times, it refers to permanent loss of forest cover, which is likely due to local differences in forest dynamics and regeneration goals (Stevens-Rumann et al. 2022; Fortin et al. 2026). Consequently, part of post-fire reforestation activities likely occurs in areas where natural regeneration may be sufficient to recover a closed-crown canopy (Rodman et al. 2024). For example, broadleaf regeneration has often been perceived as less desirable than conifer regeneration in boreal forests, or even as undesirable competition. However, post-fire shifts from conifer- to broadleaf-dominated sites can occur on drier, warmer, or more fertile sites, where strong broadleaf regeneration may represent a natural transitional stage before progressive compositional recovery (Chen et al. 2009; White and Long 2019; Barrette et al. 2022). This raises the question of whether such sites should be considered regeneration failures. This question is increasingly relevant as greater broadleaf representation is gaining interest for its historical occurrence in some forest landscapes and its potential to reduce flammability and enhance wildfire resilience (Girardin and Terrier 2015; Nesbit et al. 2023; Hessburg et al. 2025). Better integrating natural post-fire succession into post-fire restoration strategies can reduce reforestation needs and associated costs while supporting adaptive regeneration silviculture under climate change (White and Long 2019; Stanturf et al. 2024; Chazdon et al. 2024; Jetté et al. 2025; Witzell et al. 2026).

Creating suitable microsites

Site preparation is required where unsuitable microsites may inhibit regeneration establishment and growth (Löf et al. 2012; Nyland 2016), including sites with a thick organic layer (e.g., > 40 cm) that may persist after wildfires (Henneb et al. 2019). Soil preparation creates suitable microsites by exposing a mixture of organic and mineral soil and influencing soil temperature, aeration and moisture, as well as light and nutrient availability, while helping to mitigate the adverse effects of competing vegetation (Örlander et al. 1990; Prévost 1992, 1997; Hébert et al. 2010). However, site preparation effects on regeneration establishment and growth vary according to climate, site conditions, and the regenerated species (Wotherspoon et al. 2020; Thiffault et al. 2025).

Soil preparation, through the disturbance of soil organic layers, influences mineralization processes, nutrient availability and emissions from forest soils (Mäkipää et al. 2023). Disturbance of soil organic layers can differ across different soil preparation methods (von der Gönna 1992; Sikström et al. 2020). The choice of the most appropriate treatment should thus be made on a site-, species- and weather-specific basis (Örlander et al. 1990; Löf et al. 2012). Scarification in furrows using scarifiers (e.g., mechanical or hydraulic disc trencher or cone trencher) that are mounted on a skidder or a forwarder is commonly used in North American boreal forests (Löf et al. 2012; Prévost and Thiffault 2013), and it typically improves conifer seedling survival and growth (Hébert et al. 2014; Thiffault and Hébert 2017; Prévost and Dumais 2018). Other soil preparation methods are also used, such as plowing, patch scarification and mounding, activities that can also be performed using an excavator (Löf et al. 2012; Henneb et al. 2019; Fetouab et al. 2024). Excavators are better suited to steeper and rugged terrain than are skidders or forwarders, but they are usually less productive (Saarinen 2006) and more expensive to operate for a given treated area (von der Gönna 1992; BMMB 2025). Soil preparation can be executed in a variety of terrains according to the operator, machine availability and budget constraints, although it cannot be performed on inaccessible sites (see Sect. "Accessing burned sites") (von der Gönna 1992). However, in some sites with a thin organic layer (e.g., 3 to 10 cm) and a limited cover of ericaceous or herbaceous shrubs, soil preparation may be unnecessary prior to reforestation (Örlander et al. 1990; Thiffault et al. 2003; Delmaire et al. 2020).

Residual burned trees or woody debris may obstruct machinery passage (e.g., Cormier 2000), pose safety risks to workers (CNESST 2024), and reduce suitable microsites for planting (Gouge et al. 2021; Canuel et al. 2024). Site-specific hazard assessments are typically required to determine whether post-fire sites are safe to access before manual tree planting (e.g., Worksafe 2001; CNESST 2024). Salvage logging usually improves burned site trafficability (Cormier 2000). It is generally limited to economically viable sites with sufficient merchantable stems of adequate quality, especially mature ones (Saint-Germain and Greene 2009; Barrette et al. 2012; Danneyrolles et al. 2024; Boulanger et al. 2025). Salvage logging for timber occurs within two years after a fire, given the rapid decrease in wood quality with time since tree death (Barrette et al. 2015). Yet, a significant quantity of woody debris can remain on burned sites following salvage logging (Gouge et al. 2021). This can be especially true when low-quality wood (e.g., pulpwood, tree tops, branches) remain on site as woody debris following harvesting operations (Gouge et al. 2021; Canuel et al. 2024). Consequently, site clearing may be required to remove residual standing trees or woody debris, which can further facilitate reforestation operations (Saarinen 2006). As with soil preparation, this treatment can be performed using various machines such as tractors, skidders or excavators that are equipped with a mounted rack. It can be expensive (e.g., 850 to 1 000 CAD ha− 1 in 2025 (BMMB 2025) and increases the overall cost for reforestation, especially when performed in addition to soil preparation treatments (Cormier 2000; Laitila et al. 2005; Gouge et al. 2021).

Site preparation is an important silvicultural tool that, nevertheless, has different implications beyond wood production (Örlander et al. 1990 Löf et al. 2012). The use of heavy machinery can affect soil physical properties through soil compaction (Cambi et al. 2015), with moist, fine-textured soils being more sensitive (Poltorak et al. 2018; Page-Dumroese et al. 2021). Scarification in furrows is likely to cause an initial carbon loss in the forest floor (e.g., 2 Mg C ha− 1 in unburned boreal lichen woodlands) (Dufour et al. 2024). This loss may persist for several years, but can be offset over time by greater regenerated tree growth, litter inputs or carbon translocation to mineral soil (Ménard et al. 2023; Marty et al. 2024). By changing the amount, distribution and fate of dead wood, site preparation can further influence forest carbon emissions (Laganière et al. 2017; Canuel et al. 2025) and wildlife habitat (Riffell et al. 2011; Fauteux et al. 2012; McCavour et al. 2014). These implications should be considered to ensure that site preparation prescriptions align with reforestation goals (Örlander et al. 1990 Löf et al. 2012).

Accessing burned sites

Road networks are primarily established to support economic activities, including forest management, that often occur far from recent wildfire areas (Dodson 2021). Despite dense road infrastructure in the southern parts of the Canadian boreal forest, it remains sparse in more remote northern areas (Poley et al. 2022), where most widespread wildfires occur (Bergeron et al. 2002; Boulanger et al. 2013; Boucher et al. 2017) (Fig. 1). Where a road network exists, it may be too degraded to be trafficable due to poor weather conditions and maintenance, a problem that is likely to be exacerbated by climate change (Dodson 2021; Rahbarisisakht et al. 2025). Many forest roads become unusable within a decade once harvesting is completed, yet information on their condition is often lacking, incomplete or outdated (Girardin et al. 2022; Poley et al. 2022). Access to burned sites often requires restoring existing roads or building new ones, which is typically facilitated when salvage logging occurs (see Sect. "Creating suitable microsites"). Although improving access can facilitate forest and wildfire management activities (Hirsch et al. 2001; Thompson et al. 2021), extensive road access can increase the risk of wildfires (Arienti et al. 2009), affect biodiversity (Bowman et al. 2010; St-Pierre et al. 2021; Marchais et al. 2024), reduce carbon sequestration through deforestation (Kurz et al. 2013), and affect water quality, soil stability (Boston 2016; Dodson 2021) and sociocultural values that are associated with remote natural ecosystems (Bourgeois et al. 2005). Reforestation efforts following wildfires need to align with the complex reality of road planning, construction and maintenance.

Scaling up the reforestation value chain

The reforestation value chain encompasses seed collection, sorting, pretreatment and storage, seedling production in nurseries, transportation and storage of plant material (i.e., seeds, seedlings) at reforestation sites, together with outplanting and subsequent tending (Fargione et al. 2021; Mousavijad et al. 2022). It relies on a skilled workforce, which is increasingly difficult to secure due to low wages, challenging working conditions, and reliance on immigration policies and administrative structure (Stjernberg 2003; Fargione et al. 2021; Altieri et al. 2023). Most employment is seasonal, with an important contribution of youth and international workers that may also be attracted by other sectors (Statistics Canada 2021; Fargione et al. 2021; Altieri et al. 2023). Inadequate tools, techniques and training that can result from insufficient investment or low staff retention can reduce the success of reforestation projects and increase their costs (Landis et al. 2010; Preece et al. 2023). The remoteness of many areas that are affected by large wildfires requires long-distance transportation and storage of seeds and seedlings. Inadequate seedling manipulation and prolonged care during these stages can reduce seedling quality prior to planting and their subsequent performance (Stjernberg 1997; McKay 1997).

Aligning plant material production with both reforestation goals and outplanted site characteristics is crucial to ensure successful regeneration and restoration of burned landscapes (Dumroese et al. 2016). For this reason, identifying reforestation sites and needs is typically required before ordering seedling production in nurseries (Dobrowski et al. 2024). Once ordered, seedlings grow in nurseries for one to three years before planting (Mousavijad et al. 2022). This reforestation value chain may reduce surplus production and losses, but also lacks the flexibility to respond quickly to sudden increases in demand following megafire events. Indeed, sudden increases in reforestation demand following megafire events create uncertainty about the magnitude and location of future needs, which may vary greatly from year to year, impeding long-term planning, organizational capacity, and funding security (Kildisheva et al. 2023). This poses a significant challenge for developing an economically viable reforestation value chain that is both flexible and efficient. It becomes even more complex for nurseries to produce seedlings adapted to projected site conditions and reforestation goals, given the additional uncertainty surrounding climate and the social context in which forest management takes place (Messier et al. 2016; Stanturf et al. 2014; Canuel et al. 2022). Whether reforestation aims to sustain wood production, naturalness, climate change resilience, or sociocultural values is likely to influence both the type and quantity of plant material needed, as well as the most suitable corresponding reforestation methods (see Sect. "Reforestation methods") (Stanturf et al. 2014; Witzell et al. 2026).

Nursery capacity to provide an ecologically and morphologically diverse supply of quality plant material influences reforestation costs and outcomes (Palma and Laurance 2015; Marsh et al. 2022; Clark et al. 2023). Treatments, techniques, and technologies, ranging from pre-seeding sorting to genetic selection and bioengineering, can increase seed and seedling resilience to stressors and improve their performance after outplanting (Landis et al. 2010; Grossnickle 2012; Dumroese et al. 2015; Grossnickle and MacDonald 2018; Stanturf et al. 2024). Increasing ecological diversity in plant material may enhance forest resilience and restoration outcomes by improving adaptability to a wider range of conditions and stressors and by supporting multiple ecosystem services beyond wood production (Messier et al. 2019, 2022). Many authors have suggested favouring broadleaves or jack pine over spruce, given that these species are more tolerant to the projected future climate (see Sect. "Understanding regeneration failure risks") (Hébert et al. 2006; Aubin et al. 2016; Boucher et al. 2020; Baltzer et al. 2021; Cyr et al. 2022; Lanti-Traikovski et al. 2025). Some areas experiencing regeneration failures following wildfires may also present an opportunity to adapt managed forest ecosystems to future climate through assisted migration (Lebel Desrosiers et al. 2025). However, ongoing changes in seed and seedling demand likely require adaptation of the reforestation value chain (e.g., van Kerkhof et al. 2022), which is constrained by limited knowledge (Royo et al. 2023), clear policy guidelines, and financial resources (Dobrowski et al. 2024; Wotherspoon et al. 2025). It is essential to provide sufficient support to nurseries so they can supply modern reforestation needs under climate change (Haase and Davis 2017; Dumroese et al. 2019).

Reforestation methods

Mechanized tree planting

Mechanized tree planting involves using machines instead of a human workforce to automate tree planting (Fig. 3a). Compared to manual tree planting, it has the potential to regenerate burned sites with a limited workforce (e.g., one to four people) and offer control over the characteristics and spatial arrangement of seedlings, which aligns with the requirements for producing high-quality timber (Landis et al. 2010; Haase et al. 2012; Stanturf et al. 2024). Planting machines consist of a planting unit and a site preparation unit that are mounted on a base machine, such as a forwarder, a harvester or an excavator. Depending on their planting mechanism, they are classified as continuous or intermittent planting machines (Table 1). Planting machines can plant seedlings of different sizes, but they are typically limited to uniformly sized container conifers that fit transporter cups and planting requirements (Ersson et al. 2011; Laine et al. 2016). Modern machines perform both scarification and tree planting simultaneously (Ramantswana et al. 2020). Such integrated operations reduce the need to perform two separate interventions of site preparation followed by manual tree planting (e.g., Laitila et al. 2005), which would otherwise often require accessing a given site twice (BFEC 2015).

Several machines and models have been tested since 1960 (Box 1) (Ersson 2010; Ramantswana et al. 2020; Ghaffariyan 2021). Evidence suggests that both types of planting machines can provide satisfactory planting quality when employed by skilled operators and under suitable site conditions (Laine et al. 2016; Ramantswana et al. 2020). Yet, varying results have been obtained, leading to more or less satisfactory results and perceptions (Ersson 2010). Consequently, mechanized tree planting is still used to a limited extent in forest ecosystems, including in boreal countries such as Finland, Sweden, the United States and Canada (Box 1) (Ramantswana et al. 2020; Ghaffariyan 2021). This low level of mechanization of tree planting is likely due, at least in part, to the complexity of the task and the performance that tree planters traditionally achieve at a relatively high productivity and low price compared to planting machines (Table 2) (Ramantswana et al. 2020). Tree planting requires selecting suitable microsites while avoiding obstacles and accounting for natural regeneration, then burying seedling roots at a given depth with appropriate soil compaction to ensure their survival and growth (Landis et al. 2010; Altieri et al. 2023). In mechanized planting, the machine-specific site preparation, seedling release, and planting mechanism influences seedling burial quality and damage (Cormier and Ryans 1992; Saarinen 2006; Luoranen et al. 2011). As a result, differences may occur in the capacity of different planting machines to create suitable microsites and facilitate seedling survival and growth compared with traditional site preparation and manual tree planting (Saarinen 2006; St-Amour 2009a).

Mechanized tree planting success is sensitive to site suitability for the specific planting machine, including soil bearing capacity, slope, stoniness, obstacles, and site size (Table 1) (Ersson 2010). Stony terrain and terrain with a thick organic layer may need to be avoided, given that they can impede work quality and machine productivity, thereby reducing cost-effectiveness (St-Amour 2009a; Luoranen et al. 2011; Laine and Saarinen 2014). Terrain with high stumps (e.g., sites harvested in winter under a thick snow cover), residual burned trees, and abundant woody debris can also represent constraints (Saarinen 2006; Rantala et al. 2009; Rantala and Laine 2010; Laine and Rantala 2013). Whether soil texture affects mechanized tree planting is an ongoing question (Rantala et al. 2009). Improved site conditions and selection are important for enhancing planting quality and mitigating mechanical breakdowns, which increase costs and reduce productivity of mechanized tree planting (Cormier and Ryans 1992; Ersson et al. 2018). Moreover, contractors must ensure enough productive hours per year to make the capital cost of planting machines worthwhile (Ersson et al. 2018). The Scandinavian experience suggests that intermittent planting machines may be more cost-effective than continuous ones when a high proportion of sites that have been targeted to regenerate are rough, small or require mounding as a soil preparation method (Ersson 2010; Laine et al. 2016; Ersson et al. 2018). In contrast, we hypothesize that the vast areas to be regenerated following megafires provide an opportunity for economies of scale, which could facilitate the cost-effective use of continuous planting machines.

Among the factors that can enhance mechanized plantation cost-efficiency are operator training, adequate area size to be treated, sufficient annual workload, reduced machine transportation, and careful seedling supply logistics (Cormier and Ryans 1992; Laine et al. 2016; Ersson et al. 2018). Future innovations, such as improved seedling reloading systems, would also enhance planting machine productivity (Ersson et al. 2014; Laine and Saarinen 2014), while better integration of mechanized tree planting within reforestation value chains could further facilitate cost-efficient operations (Laine et al. 2016). This would require contractor recruitment and support, a current challenge that can be exacerbated by the introduction of new technologies (Laine et al. 2016; Forêt Compétences 2022). Thus, enhancing investment, resources, and administrative structures is imperative to support mechanized tree planting implementation.

The cost-efficiency and success of mechanized tree planting across different site characteristics, including burned ones, remain to be analyzed. Ensuring cost-efficiency despite short planting seasons in boreal forests (e.g., 3 to 7 months) represents a constraint to overcome (Cormier and Ryans 1992; Ersson et al. 2018). Post-fire site clearing, including salvage logging or biomass recovery (see Sect. "Creating suitable microsites"), may be required before mechanized tree planting, especially when using continuous planting machines (St-Amour 2009a; Ersson 2010). Field trials suggest that intermittent planting machines can safely regenerate terrain with some residual burned trees and debris that may otherwise pose a safety issue for manual tree planting (St-Amour 2009a), although these obstacles may still influence productivity (Saarinen 2006) and work quality (Luoranen et al. 2011). The conditions under which mechanized tree planting can be used efficiently are likely to vary among the planting machine models (Rantala et al. 2009; Luoranen et al. 2011).

Artificial direct seeding

Artificial direct seeding mimics natural regeneration by distributing seeds at the soil surface. This approach can be cheaper and executed more rapidly after a wildfire than tree planting, which requires nursery seedling production (see Sect. "Scaling up the reforestation value chain") (Reynolds 1999; Fleming et al. 2001; Adams et al. 2005; Palma and Laurance 2015). Different seeding methods (see Sects. "Aerial seeding" and "Mechanized ground seeding"), seeders and seed metering mechanisms exist, each with distinct implications for seed distribution (Reynolds 1997). Direct seeding typically results in more heterogeneous tree structures compared to manual tree planting (Miina and Saksa 2008; Grossnickle and Ivetić 2017). Some seeds can tolerate long-term storage and drying, which makes them more suitable for artificial direct seeding (Ceccon et al. 2016), such as jack pine and black spruce, which can be stored for over 10 years (Lafleur and Thiffault 2013). Moreover, the probability of germination would be better for larger seeds over small seeds, making the former more suitable for direct seeding (St-Denis et al. 2013; Palma and Laurance 2015; Ceccon et al. 2016). For example, direct pine seeding typically requires significantly fewer seeds than spruce, which has smaller seeds, to achieve full stocking (Adams et al. 2005; Matute 2020) (Table 2).

Artificial direct seeding has been largely used in boreal countries over the last century. Despite successful applications (e.g., Chrosciewicz 1990; Miina and Saksa 2008; Lariviere et al. 2025), many direct seeding attempts have failed for various reasons, including suboptimal implementation (Fleming et al. 2001; Adams et al. 2005; Grossnickle and Ivetić 2017), weather conditions and associated damages (Fleming and Mossa 1995; Blouin et al. 2000; de Chantal et al. 2003b), predation (Barnett 2014; Downer et al. 2024), competition and poor seedbed receptivity (Chrosciewicz 1990; Fleming and Mossa 1994; Kankaanhuhta et al. 2009; Rodman et al. 2025). High seed-to-seedling ratio (Table 2), low seedling growth and regeneration clumping compared to tree planting (Ackzell 1993; Kankaanhuhta et al. 2009; Ceccon et al. 2016; Lariviere et al. 2025) have contributed to decreasing the popularity of direct seeding (Grossnickle and Ivetić 2017) (Box 1). Indeed, artificial direct seeding success is more sensitive to biotic and abiotic stressors than tree planting, since the germination phase occurs on forest sites (Gonçalves and Fonseca 2023; Lariviere et al. 2025). Moreover, it may result in either understocked or overstocked sites, necessitating subsequent direct seeding, tree planting or tending to manage density and intraspecific competition (Chrosciewicz 1990; Fleming et al. 2001; Adams et al. 2005; Barnett 2014; Downer et al. 2024). For example, overstocked sites were commonly observed after artificial direct seeding of jack pine in Ontario, with densities > 7 000 seedlings ha⁻¹ up to 20 years post-seeding (Fleming et al. 2001). For timber production, neither understocked nor overstocked regenerating sites are optimal (Lafleur and Thiffault 2013), yet they may be more acceptable for other restoration objectives (Messier et al. 2019; Stanturf et al. 2024). Hence, there is a renewed interest in this approach. It is increasingly regarded as a means of addressing the growing need for reforestation after wildfires, especially as novel techniques and technologies emerge (Downer et al. 2024).

The timing of seeding operations must be adjusted to seed phenology, seedbed receptivity and exposure to biotic and stressors, which vary over time (de Chantal et al. 2003a). In general, direct seeding in early spring or late autumn is better (Grossnickle and Ivetić 2017). Direct seeding in late winter or early spring before germination can reduce exposure to small mammal predation and losses by avoiding periods or states of high predation vulnerability (Fleming et al. 2001). Avoiding germination during summer prevents desiccation during drought periods, freezing injuries and frost heaving (Fleming et al. 2001; Grossnickle and Ivetić 2017). Performing seeding as soon as possible after suitable microsite creation through ground fire (wild or controlled) or mechanical soil preparation can increase seedling establishment success. In some instances, waiting (e.g., 1 to 6 months) for the soil to stabilize may be a better option, given that soil weathering can displace seeds, resulting in seedling clumping and reduced establishment rates (Adams et al. 2005). Artificial direct seeding prescriptions must carefully consider the timing of implementation to improve the chances of success.

The seed rate required to reforest burned areas at a targeted seedling density relies upon the survival rate and must adjust to natural regeneration (see Sect. "Understanding regeneration failure risks") (Fleming et al. 2001), both of which can be hard to predict. Seed rate determination in direct seeding prescription relies on ecological knowledge and field trials that are adapted to the regenerated species and local contexts (Adams et al. 2005). For example, increased seed rate cannot fully compensate for poor seedbed receptivity, while high seed rates can jeopardize the cost efficiency of direct seeding projects, especially when coupled with high seed costs or limited seed availability (Adams et al. 2005). Therefore, orchard seeds generally exhibit higher germination and growth rates than do wild seeds (Wennström et al. 2007), but their use for direct seeding is often constrained by limited availability and higher costs (Wennström et al. 2001; Grossnickle and Ivetić 2017; Ersson 2021). Ensuring quality seed supply requires scaling up the entire seed supply chain, which is a current challenge (see Sect. "Scaling up the reforestation value chain").

Seed enhancement techniques such as cold stratification, priming, coating and pelleting are seen as means of reducing seed losses from predation, frost heaving or desiccation and improving seed and seedling survival (Pedrini et al. 2020; Brown et al. 2021; Stanturf et al. 2024; Downer et al. 2024). Cold stratification may be beneficial prior to seeding by enhancing germination and reversing damage caused by early aging, even in non-dormant, cold-germinating gymnosperm seeds, such as those of black spruce (Wang and Berjak 2000). Coating can contain wildlife repellents or fungicides, and pelleting can standardize the size and form of seeds to facilitate handling (Pedrini et al. 2017; Brown et al. 2021). The toxicity of some substances, however, has raised concerns in the past few decades and some are no longer allowed despite their proven effectiveness (Fleming et al. 2001; Lafleur and Thiffault 2013; Barnett 2014), while thick pellets can slow the seed germination process (Van Damme 1988). More recently, seed vessels have also emerged as protective packaging, ensuring homogeneity for smooth distribution (Aghai and Manteuffel-Ross 2020; Stamatopoulos et al. 2024). They consist of capsules made of biodegradable materials (e.g., fibre, clay, polymer), which can have different shapes (Aghai and Manteuffel-Ross 2020; Stamatopoulos et al. 2024) (Fig. 3d). They can contain nutrients, beneficial organisms (e.g., mycorrhizae) or water-retaining substances to promote survival and germination (Pedrini et al. 2017, 2020; Aghai and Manteuffel-Ross 2020; Mohan et al. 2021). Most seed enhancement treatments would likely increase costs (Witzell et al. 2026). They may also be unnecessary or counterproductive in some instances (Van Damme 1988; Castro et al. 2023; Downer et al. 2024; Rodman et al. 2025). Indeed, it remains unclear whether the use of the seed vessels and other enhanced seed treatments can facilitate seedling establishment and growth (Pedrini et al. 2017; Domevscik et al. 2023; Rodman et al. 2025), while also being safe for wildlife and the environment (Robinson et al. 2022; Castro et al. 2023). Consequently, seed enhancement techniques for regenerating burned forest landscapes still require future research and should be used on a case-by-case basis (Brown et al. 2021).

Site suitability for direct seeding can be assessed based on site attributes, including the type of seedbeds, station and vegetation (Adams et al. 2005; Lafleur and Thiffault 2013; Matute 2020; Ersson 2021). Soil texture and moisture regime influence seedling establishment (Chrosciewicz 1990; Kankaanhuhta et al. 2009), with different implications for different regenerated tree species (Adams et al. 2005; Gauthier and Ruel 2008). Stony sites and sites that are covered by woody debris can reduce direct seeding success by limiting the availability of receptive seedbeds (Adams et al. 2005; Kankaanhuhta et al. 2009; Matute 2020). In boreal forests, it is recommended that fertile sites be avoided, as abundant competing vegetation would impede seedling establishment and growth (Fleming and Mossa 1995; Kankaanhuhta et al. 2009). Future research aimed at identifying site suitability for post-fire direct seeding would help determine the optimal conditions for this approach (Downer et al. 2024). Existing knowledge regarding the success of direct seeding following prescribed burning as a site preparation treatment may provide a useful starting point (e.g., Richardson 1970; Chrosciewicz 1990; Adams et al. 2005).

Aerial seeding

Aerial seeding involves direct seeding from the air, providing an artificial seed input to non-regenerated areas using a limited workforce (Fleming et al. 2001; Buchelt et al. 2024). This method offers the possibility to regenerate large (Table 2) and remote areas with difficult access (see Sect. "Accessing burned sites") and provides a reforestation alternative for rough terrain that represent operational constraints for other reforestation activities (see Sect. "Creating suitable microsites") (Grossnickle and Ivetić 2017; Mohan et al. 2021; Buchelt et al. 2024). Since the mid-1900s, aerial seeding has been performed primarily by suspending or fixing a seeder to an aircraft (an airplane or helicopter) that flies about 25 to 35 m above the ground (Reynolds 1997; Adams et al. 2005). The aircraft then follows a flight itinerary covering an area using an inter-pass spacing (Paquet 1982). Yet, this method is known to require a high seed rate (Table 2), given that many seeds usually fall on unsuitable microsites (Fleming et al. 2001; Adams et al. 2005). Weather conditions, such as wind and rainfall, can interfere with seed dispersal and flight operations (Paquet 1982). The efficiency of aerial seeding using an aircraft to regenerate sites following wildfires is questioned, and few studies have addressed this issue in recent years, at least in the North American boreal forests (e.g., Densmore et al. 1999). A higher seed rate (e.g., three to fourfold) may be required to regenerate burned sites, compared with mechanically prepared sites (Fleming et al. 2001).

Recently, the use of drones equipped with seeders (Fig. 3c) has raised interest to regenerate burned landscapes and startup private companies of drone seeding are emerging globally, including in countries like the United States, Canada (Box 1), Spain, and Australia (Mohan et al. 2021; Stamatopoulos et al. 2024). Each of these companies develops its own service offers and technology, which consists of a duo of seeders and seeds often enhanced using seed vessels (Fig. 3d) (Aghai and Manteuffel-Ross 2020; Mohan et al. 2021; Castro et al. 2023). Skilled operators, together with drones that are equipped with high-resolution cameras, inertial navigation systems, and global positioning systems (GPS) can enable low-height flight (e.g., a few meters) and more targeted seed release than with an aircraft (Mohan et al. 2021). Drones equipped with seeders can distribute seeds, which are propelled by gravity, air displacement, or pneumatic systems so that they can be deposited on more targeted spots on the soil surface (Mohan et al. 2021; Stamatopoulos et al. 2024). By doing so, drones could increase the chances of aligning seed delivery with receptive seedbeds, thereby potentially increasing regeneration success and reducing the required seed rate (Castro et al. 2023, 2024). Drone seeding could further be performed by a team of a few drones and operators, coordinating as “swarms” to regenerate vast areas (e.g., Aghai and Manteuffel-Ross 2020). However, the potential of drone seeding as a reforestation method has not yet been the subject of many follow-ups, and few data are available on its success or cost-efficiency with little, if any, peer-reviewed evidence to date (Robinson et al. 2022; Castro et al. 2023; Downer et al. 2024). Private companies report that using drones may be 3 to 10 times cheaper than manual tree planting and far more productive (Table 2) (Castro et al. 2024; Stamatopoulos et al. 2024), but calculations underlying these estimates and their robustness remain unclear. There is a need for transparent studies conducted by independent third parties. Costs that are associated with technology acquisition, development and production, together with high expertise requirements, can be significant and must be considered (Mohan et al. 2021; Robinson et al. 2022; Stamatopoulos et al. 2024). Sharing knowledge among the stakeholders who are involved in reforestation activities is encouraged to foster the development of this new method, which remains primarily privately driven and owned (Mohan et al. 2021; Stamatopoulos et al. 2024).

Drone use for aerial seeding is also limited by regulation and operational capacity (Mohan et al. 2021) (Table 2). On one hand, different drone models of varying masses are currently in use or at least being tested for aerial seeding (Matute et al. 2018; Aghai and Manteuffel-Ross 2020). Therefore, piloting drones may require training and a certificate under current regulations, such as the Canadian Aviation Regulations (Government of Canada 2025). Permits or exemptions may be required to operate drones for large-scale reforestation that requires operating them beyond visual line-of-sight (Stamatopoulos et al. 2024; Government of Canada 2025). Regulation is thus a key factor for regenerating areas affected by megafires and, in turn, ensuring economies of scale (Mohan et al. 2021). On the other hand, the operational range of drone seeding is currently constrained by technical limitations, including payload capacity (e.g., between 1 and 30 kg of seeds) and battery duration (e.g., two to five hours) (Matute et al. 2018; Pedrini et al. 2020; Stamatopoulos et al. 2024; Hamilton 2025). Strong wind and precipitation reduce manoeuvrability, while low temperature decreases battery capacity (Mohan et al. 2021). For these reasons, drone seeding currently requires road access no more than a few kilometres from the seeding sites (Hamilton 2025). Ongoing research and political changes are seeking to increase drone operational capacity, including changes in regulations (Transport Canada 2025).

Mechanized ground seeding

Mechanized ground seeding consists of using machinery to distribute seeds on the ground. It is typically performed using a site preparation unit and a seeder that is mounted on a skidder, crawler tractor or, to a lesser extent, an excavator (Fig. 3e) (Reynolds 1997; St-Amour 2009b; Matute 2020; Ersson 2021). The seeds are typically released near the site preparation unit (Fig. 3f). This arrangement allows mechanized seeding to be performed simultaneously with site preparation. Soil compaction from the machinery after seed distribution can enhance seedling establishment by mitigating the adverse effects of low soil stabilization (Van Damme et al. 1992). The productivity of this method may be higher than that of site preparation followed by tree planting, but lower than that of site preparation alone (Reynolds 1999) (Table 2). This method has been globally used for decades in combination with tree planting, notably in Finland, Sweden, the United States and Canada, primarily for pine regeneration (Van Damme and Bax 1991; Fleming et al. 2001; Barnett 2014; Ersson 2021). Compared to broadcast direct seeding methods (e.g., Sect. "Synthesis and future research perspectives"), mechanized seeding enhances control over seedling distribution, requires a lower seed rate and can be more cost-effective, since only the prepared soil with suitable microsites is seeded (Reynolds 1999; Adams et al. 2005) (Table 2).

Seed distribution and rate are especially important in mechanized seeding to control individual seedling spacing and reduce seedling clumping (Van Damme and Bax 1991; Fleming et al. 2001). The Bracke S35.a seeder (Bräcke, Sweden) that was commercialized in the early 2000 s is equipped with a wheel having individual seed cavities that promote smooth and uniform seed distribution and is now widely used (Ersson 2021) (Fig. 3g). Seed release flow may be controlled and tracked through sensors and a control system (Lépine-Perrault 2025). This is advantageous given that real-time field monitoring is almost impossible because seeds are challenging to see (Ersson 2021; Lépine-Perrault 2025). Monitoring mechanized seeding outcome must occur following seed establishment, a few years after seeding (Matute 2020). This extended timeframe for assessing seedling establishment compared to tree planting underscores the importance of careful planning and execution, given that failure would delay prompt regeneration.

There are limited recent peer-reviewed data supporting the success of this method, especially for regenerating sites following wildfires. We hypothesize that this method may be relevant for reforesting low-fertility sites (Miina and Saksa 2008; Kankaanhuhta et al. 2009) with unsuitable post-fire microsites, provided that site conditions do not constrain proper soil preparation (see Sect. "Creating suitable microsites"). Mechanized seeding effectiveness would likely be reduced under the presence of woody debris (Matute 2020). Yet, profound soil disturbance may not be optimal for creating high-quality microsites, as it can increase the movement and burial of seeds (Van Damme and Bax 1991). Small seeds, like those from black spruce, may require only superficial scarification of the soil (Fleming and Mossa 1994). Future research should aim to clarify the required adjustments in seed rates, speed, and the site preparation unit across varying seeding machines and site types (Lépine-Perrault 2025).

Synthesis and future research perspectives

Integrating risk management into reforestation planning

Post-fire recovery and subsequent regeneration failure under climate change remains only partially understood in North America (Boucher et al. 2020; Stevens-Rumann et al. 2022; Anyomi et al. 2022; Waldron et al. 2023; Fortin et al. 2026). Our review synthesized the factors that influence the availability of viable seeds or propagules in burned patches, and the post-fire conditions for seedling establishment and growth (Jayen et al. 2006; Ilisson and Chen 2009; Harvey et al. 2016) (Sect. "Implications for reforestation"), all of which are key mechanisms of fire resilience (Johnstone et al. 2016). It can inform the development of frameworks and predictive models (e.g., Marsh et al. 2022) for assessing site suitability and regeneration failure risks (Fig. 2). Enhancing the understanding and prediction of post-fire regeneration processes and outcomes is still necessary to improve reforestation strategies and support their success (Anyomi et al. 2022), thereby reducing the economic and functional losses that may result from their failures (Zhang 2019; Stanturf et al. 2024).

To overcome limited reforestation capacity (see Sect. "Scaling up the reforestation value chain"), optimizing reforestation efforts and their outcomes becomes especially important (e.g., Holden et al. 2025). On the one hand, the long-term effects and operational success of traditional and innovative reforestation methods still require extensive research (de Souza 2022; Rodman et al. 2024; Thiffault et al. 2025). There is evidence that current reforestation efforts are not yet achieving their expected outcomes, indicating knowledge gaps and room for improvement in operational practice (Barrette et al. 2024; Rodman et al. 2024). On the other hand, performing cost-benefit analyses that include both economic and non-economic benefits of reforestation projects, such as carbon sequestration and wildlife habitat quality, can help assess the potential impacts of regeneration failure and guide where silvicultural efforts should be targeted (Robichaud et al. 2009; Drever et al. 2025). For example, there is concern that reforestation of burned forests may contribute little to wood supply in some high-latitude areas of eastern North American boreal forests under limited tree growth or short fire intervals (Van Bogaert et al. 2015; Gauthier et al. 2015c). On such sites, the resulting limited tree carbon sequestration, when weighed against the potential surface warming from reforestation due to reduced albedo compared with open-crown canopy forests following regeneration failure, raises also questions about the potential of reforestation to mitigate climate change (Bernier et al. 2011; Mykleby et al. 2017). Considering the costs and benefits of reforesting burned landscapes across different stakeholders and beyond their economic value can further help address the social and spiritual dimensions of reforestation projects, which are increasingly recognized as a critical aspect of their success (Stanturf et al. 2014). Post-fire reforestation should prioritize areas and methods across the landscape based on their likelihood of success, their vulnerability to regeneration failure and the potential consequences of such failure for maintaining ecosystem functions and services (Gauthier et al. 2014, 2015c; Messier et al. 2019; Zhang 2019).

Diversifying silvicultural tools to increase reforestation capacity

We suggest using a range of silvicultural tools to increase reforestation capacity and prevent reforestation value chains from being outpaced by more frequent megafire events, including mechanized tree planting and aerial and mechanized direct seeding. Our review improves understanding of how different reforestation methods can complement manual tree planting to successfully reforest boreal landscapes following megafires, based on their operational and ecological suitability (Fig. 4). It provides a starting point for identifying their potential contribution to a post-fire reforestation strategy. There is strong evidence that the range of operational and ecological suitability of different silvicultural tools varies across methods, regenerated species and reforestation goals, although some overlaps are possible (Stanturf et al. 2024; Witzell et al. 2026).

Aerial seeding is the only method showing potential to reforest sites on rough terrain that are not trafficable for machinery or a human workforce (Fig. 4). The use of aircraft is currently the only way to reforest sites without road access, although future development aims to increase drone capacity to operate farther from the roadside. However, in the absence of suitable microsites, reforestation on these sites with low access and trafficability is unlikely due to limited site preparation options (see Sect. "Creating suitable microsites") (Fig. 4). The rougher the terrain (i.e., high stoniness, many boulders, irregularity, steep slopes, or poor soil bearing capacity), the more constraints there are on mechanized tree planting or seeding to operate successfully. Manual tree planting can occur on rougher terrain to a certain extent, although it is limited by the presence of residual trees and woody debris that can pose a safety risk to tree planters, but not to machinery (Fig. 4). Moreover, the chosen method must align with the plant material that is being used for reforestation (von der Gönna 1992). Direct seeding is most effective on less fertile sites with low levels of competing vegetation, whereas tree planting can succeed across a broader range of vegetation competition, at least for pine and spruce in boreal forests (Miina and Saksa 2008) (Fig. 4). Yet, other considerations are also important, such as costs, the likelihood of success, the timing, the environmental and social impacts, and the availability of a workforce, skilled operators, seeds, and seedlings. For example, mechanized tree planting may be too expensive if no prior soil preparation is needed, since the costs of this method often exceed the total costs of manual tree planting with soil preparation. These other considerations inform the choice of the most appropriate reforestation method when multiple options are suitable for a given site, based upon biophysical attributes. In turn, they also inform the need to build and maintain road access for post-fire reforestation activities (see Sect. "Accessing burned sites").

Our review highlights that alternative reforestation methods to manual tree planting may be suitable for regenerating large areas where the forest state is compromised by megafires under changing fire regimes. However, there is a lack of evidence on the effectiveness of these methods under current climate and environmental conditions, even though mechanized tree planting, aerial seeding, and mechanized ground seeding have long been considered in forestry (e.g., Box 1). New technologies such as drones and sensors have emerged and generated enthusiasm that is, to our knowledge, not yet supported by independent studies. Evidence suggests that adaptation of reforestation value chains, including social capacity, legislation and policies, would be needed to further facilitate the diversification of reforestation methods (Stanturf et al. 2024; Downer et al. 2024). Therefore, manual tree planting will likely continue to play a significant role in regenerating burned landscapes under changing fire regimes, at least in the coming decades (Haase and Davis 2017). Increased knowledge of dominant characteristics of sites that will likely require massive post-fire reforestation efforts—which are likely to vary across regions due to differences in terrain and ecological conditions—could greatly inform which reforestation methods are most promising and worth investigating to increase reforestation capacity (Ersson 2010; Stanturf et al. 2024).

Limits

Our approach was not intended to provide an exhaustive review of each challenge and reforestation method, but rather to offer an expert-oriented overview of the current artificial regeneration options available in response to regeneration failure. Other site preparation and reforestation methods exist but were not included in our study (Chazdon et al. 2024; Stanturf et al. 2024; Witzell et al. 2026). For example, tree planting with drones was considered too early-stage to be included. More passive interventions, such as natural direct seeding combined with prescribed burning, scarification, or soil disturbance from harvest machinery passage, may also be effective for regenerating sites that would otherwise lack natural regeneration (Chrosciewicz 1976; Greene et al. 2006; Hébert et al. 2024; Lariviere et al. 2025). Preventive interventions, such as tree retention during harvesting operations, including during salvage logging, can provide functional redundancy and reduce the occurrence of regeneration failure (Jetté et al. 2025). Moreover, the broad scope of our work may not allow us to cover certain locally specific cases. Some province and country-specific aspects, such as the economics and legal liability of reforestation, were intentionally not covered to focus on the technical and biophysical potential of reforestation methods, although they likely represent challenges for reforestation (Kildisheva et al. 2023; Brant et al. 2026). Some quantitative data were reported as representative examples primarily based on geographical location, forest types and the authors’ judgment. They, however, capture only the available data from specific case studies and should be interpreted with this in mind. Finally, our review was also limited by the language covered by the team or authors, so we acknowledge that key literature may have been overlooked.

Conclusions

Our review provides a foundation for developing modern reforestation strategies under changing fire regimes and is relevant to communities, scientists, governments and land managers seeking to adapt regeneration silviculture in response to more frequent megafires. As climate change increases wildfire activity across boreal forests, post-fire reforestation needs may far exceed the current capacity of the forestry sector (e.g., Dobrowski et al. 2024). To increase capacity, we suggest considering different reforestation methods for regenerating burned landscapes, including mechanized tree planting, aerial direct seeding and ground mechanized seeding (Stanturf et al. 2024).

We identified the suitability of these methods across sites with different biophysical characteristics (Fig. 4) and showed how they can complement manual tree planting within a broader post-fire reforestation strategy. Some methods may not yield dense, uniform forest cover like that obtained through manual tree planting, but they may offer a valuable trade-off for achieving desirable outcomes. However, their success requires deep ecological knowledge at microsite, site and landscape levels (Fig. 2) and a skilled workforce, both of which are currently challenging to secure. There is therefore an urgent need to assess and document the effectiveness of different reforestation methods under modern post-wildfire conditions.

Finally, reforestation value chain activities must adapt to novel reforestation requirements through training and investment, while future research should address existing technical, technological and political constraints. We call for reforestation activities to be guided by restoration goals to optimize efforts and outcomes (Dumroese et al. 2016; Witzell et al. 2026). They should be implemented within the broader context of adaptive forest management to ensure the resilience of the boreal forests and their communities in a changing climate (Millar et al. 2007; Mulverhill et al. 2024).
