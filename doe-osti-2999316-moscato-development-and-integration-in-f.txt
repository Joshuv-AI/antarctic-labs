 Summary

MOSCATO (Molten Salt Chemistry and Transport) is a multiphysics code that provides high-fidelity,
coupled simulations of fluid flow, heat transfer, mass transfer, chemistry, electrochemical phenomena,
and alloy corrosion for molten salt systems. In FY25, significant developments were made to the
code package, enhancing its capabilities for modeling all relevant phenomena within operating
moltens salt reactors (MSRs). The developments and activities in FY25 included:
 1. Implementation of Level-Set methods to enable modeling of single-bubble
behavior in molten salts. In FY25, the Level-Set two-phase flow modeling implementation was
improved to simulate single bubble behavior with molten salt media. The large density and viscosity
ratios between typical gases and molten salt liquids present challenges for these types of numerical
solvers. With enhancements to the pressure projection method, MOSCATO’s Level-Set solver was
able to be successfully validated to experiments related to helium bubble rise in stagnant molten
salt. The simulated bubble rising velocity showed reasonable good agreement with experimental
measurements. The bubble shape and dynamics were also visually compared with experimental
snapshots, demonstrating a good qualitative match.
 2. Generation of mass transfer correlations for multiphase flow systems. To enable
calculations of the tritium transport across the interface between gas bubbles and salt, we modeled
high- Schmidt-number mass transfer around a sphere across a broad range of Reynolds numbers.
The mesh near the sphere surface was highly refined to resolve steep concentration gradients caused
by the low diffusion coefficient. Literature-based mass transfer correlations were compared with the
numerical results, and modifications were proposed to improve agreement, particularly at higher
Schmidt numbers. These mass transfer correlations were subsequently provided to other national
laboratories to help enable high quality mass transfer simulations using lower-order solvers under
development within the NEAMS program.
 3. Preliminary implementation of a bubbly flow solver. To model bubbly flow in molten
salt, we implemented a bubbly flow solver for void fractions less than 5%. To do so, an algebraic
relative velocity model that assumes small bubbles with rapid momentum equilibration was added to
MOSCATO to compute bubble velocities. Preliminary comparisons with experimental data showed
reasonable agreement, and further improvements are underway.
 4. Generation of mass transfer correlations for MSRE subchannel The Molten-Salt
Reactor Experiment (MSRE) was a landmark historical project that demonstrated the feasibility
of molten-salt reactor technology. The MSRE campaign also generated a significant body of
experimental data and reports that continue to support molten-salt–related research. In this report,
the MSRE core subchannel was used as the reference geometry for a mass transfer study performed
with MOSCATO. The geometry and computational mesh were adapted from a previous study,
providing adequate resolution for the relatively low Reynolds number in this case. Additional
mesh refinement was applied to reach higher Schmidt numbers, enabling the derivation of a reliable
mass-transfer correlation for the present scenario.
 5. Simulations of oxygen ingressions into molten salt. In the previous fiscal year, we
initiated a study to simulate oxygen ingression in stagnant salt. As oxygen enters the salt through
its surface, it reacts with Ce3+ to form solid CeO2 and other reaction products. To more fully

 i

capture the complex diffusion-convection-reaction mechanisms, capabilities for modeling natural
convection in the salt vessel were added. These were needed as the flow of the ingressed gas induced
flow in the salt caused by surface shear and non-isothermal effects. With these updated physics
in place, we were able to successfully reproduce the experimental results for the rate of change of
CeCl3 concentrations versus time.
 6. Flow corrosion model validation. In FY24, MOSCATO’s corrosion model was validated
against static corrosion experiments. In FY25, this work was extended to a flow corrosion experiment,
where FLiNaK salt was driven by natural convection, with initial salt impurities to initiate corrosion.
Despite uncertainties in parameters such as elemental diffusion coefficients in the alloy and unknown
H+ concentrations, the simulations achieved good agreement with experimental results, especially
in predicting sample mass losses.

1 Introduction

Molten salt reactors (MSRs) represent a promising technology for next-generation nuclear power
plants. The comprehensive review by MacPherson [6] delves into their early developmental history.
Recently, MSRs have garnered renewed interest due to their intrinsic safety features and sustainability,
positioning them as one of the more promising Gen-IV nuclear designs. Efforts to develop this
technology have been undertaken globally by national laboratories and various corporate entities,
including several start-up companies [7].

 While some MSR variants employ molten salt exclusively as a coolant [8], most have the actinides
and other fission product species dissolved in the salt. This fuel salt can be circulated in and out of
the core to remove the energy generated by fission. Compared to the water used in conventional
BWRs and PWRs, molten salts enable higher-temperature operations, resulting in increased thermal
efficiencies. Additionally, the low-pressure operation of the primary and secondary coolant loops
enhances safety and reduces overall capital costs. The molten fuel salt in an MSR comprises
actinide and fission product species within an alkali metal or alkaline earth halide salt mixture,
which can be chloride or fluoride-based. The mass transfer of species within the salt is of utmost
importance as it dictates the core reactivity and thermophysical properties such as viscosity, thermal
conductivity, and heat capacity, significantly influencing the thermal-hydraulic behavior of the
reactor. Consequently, all MSR designs must consider the mass transfer behavior of the salt.

 Corrosion of the reactor’s structural materials arises due to the chemical and electrochemical
reactions caused by fission products and impurities in the salt. The Molten Salt Reactor Experiment
(MSRE, [6, 9]) encountered manageable levels of corrosion on the surfaces of structural alloys. In the
Fe-Cr-Ni alloy system, chromium depletion can occur through oxidation at the salt-alloy interface,
followed by the removal of chromium from the alloy, resulting in a depleted zone, as depicted in
Figure 1.1. A measurement of elements near the corrosion frontier also shows the removing of
elements, as presented in Figure 1.2. Solid fuel MSR designs typically initiate Cr oxidation through
impurities or other oxidizing agents from oxygen and moisture interactions. However, in liquid fuel
MSR designs, additional corrosion pathways emerge due to the increasingly oxidizing conditions
arising from the altered fluoride balance during fission. Moreover, radionuclides generated from
fission reactions deposit on alloy surfaces in liquid-fueled MSRs, further affecting the dynamics and
spatial distribution of the corrosion processes.

 In recent decades, both flow and static experiments, have been performed at various institutes
to study stainless corrosion in molten salt environment. For example, Zheng ([10],[11]) and Doniger
([12]), Sanker ([13]), Leong ([14]) have conducted static corrosion experiments, in which specimens
are placed in salt in a crucible. Flow experiments are also conducted by Raiman ([15]), Kelleher
([16]) in witch corrosion samples are placed in a loop with either forced or natural convection. In all
these experiments, a mass loss from the samples was observed, due to elements like Cr and Fe being
oxidized from the sample ([10],[11],[13]).

 Accurate predictions of the local rates of chemical and corrosion processes are crucial, as
the degradation of structural alloys ultimately determines the achievable lifetime of molten salt
equipment. Additionally, tracking fission products is essential for ensuring proper nuclear material
accountancy. Modeling corrosion and radionuclide deposition necessitates careful tracking of the

transport of chemical species within both the salt and the alloy. Early attempts at modeling
mass transfer relied on simple mass transfer coefficients, such as the work by Kedl [9]. More
recently developed codes are available to simulate corrosion in molten salt systems [17, 18, 19].
However, advances in computational resources have rendered such simplifications unnecessary.
High-fidelity Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations can now be performed to model
these complex multiphysics scenarios accurately. Notably, CFD-level corrosion modeling has been
available, but global conservation effects leading to thermogalvanic corrosion [20] have not been
previously considered.

 The development of the MOlten Salt Chemistry And TranspOrt (MOSCATO) code at Argonne
addresses the need to track ion species in the salt and model corrosion in the structure. To achieve
this, three principal components are essential. First, mass transfer equations must be coupled
with the Navier-Stokes (NS) equations to account for the transport of ion species in the salt. The
Poisson-Nernst-Planck (PNP) equation is employed to model ion transport. Second, the diffusion of
alloy constituents, such as Cr and Fe, must be simulated within the structural metals. The length
scale of the alloy depletion layers is on the order of micrometers, resulting in a numerical domain
for alloy simulations significantly smaller than the fluid domain. Third, models that account for
the chemical and electrochemical reactions at the salt-alloy interface must be employed to couple
the salt and alloy domains. The local reaction rates are governed by kinetic equations, such as the
Butler-Volmer equation [21]. However, the coupling cannot be purely local; instead, the salt and
alloy regions must be globally coupled to ensure charge conservation across the entire domain.

 Another key objective of MOSCATO is to simulate the transport of gaseous species—such
as tritium—within a molten-salt environment. Tritium is generated in the salt through neutron
interactions with lithium, a reaction commonly observed in salts like FLiBe and FLiNaK. Because
tritium is highly permeable, it can migrate through structural materials such as containers and
pipes and ultimately be released into the environment. Accurate prediction of tritium transport is
therefore critical for safety analysis. In some fusion reactor designs, molten salts proposed for use
in the blanket system also generate tritium through neutron interactions. In these systems, the
produced tritium can be collected and used directly as fusion fuel, making the understanding and
control of tritium transport even more important. Other gaseous species are also produced. For
example, helium is generated through neutron-induced reactions with salt constituents. However,
helium has very low solubility in molten salts and therefore forms bubbles. Modeling the formation,
growth, and motion of these bubbles is essential. To capture these phenomena, we have developed
a bubbly-flow solver in MOSCATO capable of simulating flows with thousands of small bubbles.
An especially interesting phenomenon is the mass transfer of dissolved gas species across the
salt–bubble interface, as illustrated in Figure 1.3. Significant research—both experimental and
numerical—has investigated these topics, including tritium generation, permeation, and bubble
dynamics [22, 23, 24, 25, 17, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30].

 Details of the MOSCATO solver and its capabilities are presented in the following sections.
Section 2 describes the numerical methods and modeling approaches employed by MOSCATO.
Section 3 presents the FY25 development activities of the solver, including campaigns for verification
and validation against both numerical and experimental data. Finally, Section 4 provides conclusions
and outlines planned work for the next fiscal year.

2 Numerical Formulations

2.1 Molten Salt Chemistry and Corrosion Models

Simulating the chemistry and corrosion in molten salt systems is a complex task that requires
accounting for various phenomena. A suitable molten salt chemistry solver must address ion
transport in the salt, species diffusion within the structural alloys, reaction kinetics, and globally
coupled charge-transfer characteristics. These coupled phenomena occur across multiple length and
time scales, presenting significant challenges. The MOSCATO solver adopts a segregated approach
to simultaneously solve for all these phenomena. The overall models and solution algorithm are
described below, with additional underlying solver details documented in earlier technical reports.

 The transport of ions in the salt is governed by the Poisson-Nernst-Planck (PNP) equations
[31, 32], which describe the time evolution of ion species concentrations. These equations account for
chemical reactions, concentration gradient-induced diffusion, and migration due to electric potential
fields [31, 32]. The salt solution is assumed to be electrically neutral (electroneutrality), except in a
thin double layer near electrodes and other boundaries, where electrode kinetics are considered as
boundary conditions.

 The rate of the chemical reactions depends on various factors, such as temperature, local ion
concentration in the salt, local element concentration in the alloy, etc. To accurately calculate
the reaction rate, it is essential to couple the Navier-Stokes equations and PNP equations while
modeling the change in element concentrations in the alloy. Experimental results [13] illustrates
that the element concentration in the alloy after long-term exposure to a salt is still limited to a

very thin layer. Due to the thinness of the depletion layers, diffusion in the alloy can be assumed to
be one-dimensional. The length scale of the alloy diffusion process is typically on the order of 10 to
100 micrometers, significantly smaller than the flow length scale. Consequently, it is unnecessary to
explicitly mesh the alloy domain to solve the diffusion equation. Instead, for each grid point on the
salt-alloy interface, MOSCATO solves the one-dimensional diffusion equation (Equation 15) with
user-defined length discretizations. Dirichlet boundary conditions are applied at the bulk end for
each alloy constituent’s concentration, while Neumann boundary conditions for flux are adopted

3.1 Helium bubble rising in stagnant salt

In liquid-fuel MSR designs, the salt–fuel mixture is continuously pumped through the core, and the
buildup of fission product gases must be removed to maintain safe and stable reactor operations
[52]. Even in non-fuel salt MSR designs, helium is produced as a byproduct of neutron absorption,
impurity reactions, and tritium decay. Helium must be carefully managed, as it can form bubbles
that alter the salt’s thermophysical properties and turbulence characteristics.

 Evidence from the Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL) indicated that most helium bubbles driven into the salt were released at the free surface.
However, a fraction of the bubbles remained in the circulating salt, increasing the gas volume
fraction throughout the loop in the range of 0.02–7% [52].

 Tritium is generated primarily through neutron interactions with lithium-6, and unlike helium,
it is more likely to dissolve in the salt. Nevertheless, tritium can transfer between the salt and
bubbles—whether from helium generated in situ or from bubbles injected for tritium extraction.
The detailed bubble dynamics in molten salt, such as bubble size, surface area, and shape, remain
poorly characterized due to the lack of experimental data.

 To address this gap, we have extended MOSCATO with the capabilities to model helium
bubble motion within molten salt using the level-set method. Validation work for the level-set
implementation was previously discussed in our report [51] and in related work [53]. In this report,
we apply this capability to an experiment that investigates helium bubble dynamics in molten salt.

In our previous reports [54, 51, 55, 56], we investigated high Schmidt number mass transfer in

the liquid and gas phases are solved simultaneously. These equations are coupled through a shared
pressure equation and a void fraction transport equation.

3.5 Simulating oxygen ingression into stagnant salt

In our previous report [51], we conducted preliminary simulations related to oxygen ingressions
into a stagnant salt [5]. Our initial approach employed a level-set method to capture the moving
surface and the induced convection in the salt. This year, an unheated gas stream, as opposed to an
isothermal inlet, was included to account for expected behavior in realistic ingression scenarios. This
led to temperature variations in the upper salt region that in turn gave rise to natural convection
driven by buoyancy. Results from simulations that incorporate this additional phenomena are
presented in this section.

3.5.1 Simulation setup

As presented in our previous work, the computational domain was modeled as a cylindrical vessel.
In this new approach, the top surface of the salt was set at a lower temperature than the vessel wall.
However, due to the lack of temperature measurements for the free gas, the exact salt top-surface
temperature remains unknown. The vessel wall is assumed to be at the experimentally measured
temperature of 586 ◦ C.

 Density variations in the salt were neglected, but buoyancy effects are accounted for using
the Boussinesq approximation. The partial pressure of oxygen in the free gas was experimentally
measured [5]. To determine the oxygen concentration at the salt’s upper surface, we applied Henry’s
law (Equation 53), which relates gas partial pressure to liquid surface concentration. This assumes
that the oxygen in the free gas phase is abundant and rapidly saturated the salt surface.

3.6 Corrosion Solver Validation

In FY24 [51], we conducted the work to validate MOSCATO’s corrosion model over long durations
with static corrosion experiments from the literature [65]. However, those cases did not include
temperature gradients and therefore had stationary salt conditions. To further confirm MOSCATO’s
corrosion modeling capabilities over extend period of time with salt convection, work this year

 36

examined a thermal convection loop experiment, conducted by Raiman et al. [15] at Texas A&M
University. The complex geometry and extended length of operations make high-fidelity corrosion
modeling difficult, but updates to MOSCATO’s system level modeling nodalization approach have
enabled simulations for the entire 1000 hour experiment.

3.6.1 Thermal Convection Loop Experiment from Literature

In the Raiman et al. [15] experiment, Type 316 stainless steel samples were exposed to flowing
FLiNaK salt for 1000 hours in a thermal convection loop. The loop was constructed from 316H
stainless steel piping, with an outer diameter of 26.7 mm and an inner diameter of 20.8 mm. Natural
convection was induced using three 900 W heaters. The FLiNaK salt, recovered from old stock and
stored in an airtight nickel-alloy vessel, was carefully handled to keep oxygen and moisture levels
below 0.1 ppm [15].

 Twelve 316H stainless steel samples (25.4 × 19 × 2 mm) were arranged in chains, secured with
316 stainless steel wire, and positioned at the center of the vertical sections of the loop. This
configuration ensured that all samples were electrically connected to the loop while remaining
thermally separated from the heaters by the flowing salt.

 According to [15], impurities in the salt were initially low, particularly for Fe (7.17 wppm),
Ni (6.66 wppm), and Cr (4.57 wppm). The composition of the 316H stainless steel samples (by
weight: 64.5% Fe, 13.3% Ni, 17.2% Cr, etc.) was also provided in the same reference. For a detailed
description of the experimental facility and testing procedures, readers are referred to [15].

3.6.2 Validation: MOSCATO simulation of thermal convection loop

To simulate this experiment, a computational domain was constructed and meshed, as illustrated in
Figure 3.24. Compared to the actual experimental loop, auxiliary components such as the fill pot and
drain pot were omitted to simplify the model. However, the top pot was retained in the simulation
since it was located directly along the main flow path. The twelve samples were explicitly meshed, as
shown in Figure 3.24, to accurately capture local flow effects and better represent the temperature
distribution around the samples. A structured mesh was employed in the simulation, consisting
of 103,326 hexahedral elements. With a polynomial order of 5, this resulted in approximately 13
million total degrees of freedom.

 From the experimental data reported in [15], the concentration of H + (originating from H2 O)

corrosion behavior, and is notoriously difficult to measure experimentally in situ. As such, the
H + concentration in the loop throughout operations was unclear – the initial concentration was
unknown as was the increase in concentration due to any ongoing moisture ingressions. Given this
uncertainty, we treated the H + concentration as the only fit parameter for these simulations.

 To explore this effect, we conducted a parametric study in the simulation by varying the initial
H + concentration over a range of values (0, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0, and 10.0 mol/m3 ). The presence of H +
could also result from moisture ingressions into the loop, but as no information on leaks rates could
be determined from the reported study. As such, we assumed the quantity of H+ to present from
the beginning of the simulation and ignore any gas leaks that may have occurred. The thermal
convection loop was run at negative gauge pressure unlike typical positive pressure operations, which
likely did exacerbate gas ingressions [15].

 MOSCATO is a CFD-level solver, which makes simulating processes over a timescale of 1000
hours extremely challenging—if not practically impossible. In this case, the presence of flow further
restricts the maximum time step, limiting the total simulated duration. To address this, we employed
a System Level Method (SLM) to extrapolate the MOSCATO results to a 1000-hour timescale.
The SLM simplifies the system by nodalizing it into a one-dimensional convection problem. This
approximation was justified, as the mainstream flow does not exhibit complex phenomena such
as cross flow. By adopting this approach, MOSCATO can effectively simulate longer timescales
(with larger time steps), on the order of several hours to tens of hours. During the SLM runs, the
CFD-level mesh was still utilized to perform detailed current balance calculations and alloy diffusion
simulations, while applying offset concentration changes in the salt domain to extend the effective
timescale. Figure 3.26 illustrates the SLM nodalization and its correspondence to the MOSCATO
computational domain.

59, but does so at the nodal level. This allows the simulation to advance with a significantly larger
time step.

 Here, V is the total volume of all CFD elements assigned to the node. The variables cnew and
cold denote the new and old node concentrations, respectively. The terms uin , Ain , and cin represent
the velocity, cross-sectional area, and concentration between this node and its upstream neighbor,
while uout , Aout , and cout are the corresponding quantities between this node and its downstream
neighbor. The term f denotes the surface-averaged ion flux, calculated from the Butler–Volmer
electrode kinetics at the CFD mesh level, and Aw is the total wall area of all elements associated
with this node.

 The coupling scheme between the SLM and MOSCATO is illustrated in Figure 3.27. As shown,
the process begins with an explicit MOSCATO simulation to bring the system to a quasi-steady
state. Once this is achieved, the SLM simulation is initiated, using a much larger time step to
advance the system over longer timescales. During the SLM phase, the MOSCATO mesh is still
employed to compute the ion species fluxes and to resolve the alloy diffusion processes in detail.
After completing the SLM simulation segment, MOSCATO resumes explicit time-stepping to relax
the system into a new quasi-steady state, preparing it for the next SLM cycle.

 In conclusion, due to limitations in the current corrosion model and diffusion coefficients values,
MOSCATO cannot fully capture local elemental profiles, especially where significant local leaching
and near-surface alloy restructuring are present, as seen in the experimental results. Future work
will focus on incorporating models that account for these effects to improve predictive accuracy.

4 Conclusions and Future Work

In FY25, we continued to advance the development and application of the MOSCATO code for
challenging multiphysics problems relevant to MSRs. Our efforts focused on several critical aspects
of code development, including improvements to the level-set model, development of a bubbly flow
solver, and corresponding validation activities.

 For level-set model validation, we leveraged an experiment that investigated helium bubble rise
in FLiNaK salt, achieving good agreement between experimental and numerical results. For the
bubbly solver, we were able to get good initial agreement for two-phase flow in a vertical pipe
configuration. Additionally, we also continued the work to study mass transfer in reactor-relevant
geometries. For corrosion model validation, MOSCATO was benchmarked against experimental
flow-corrosion data and was found to be able to produce accurate predictions of corrosion rates
while only requiring a single fit constant related to the initial salt purity.

 Looking ahead, we plan to extend our code development and validation efforts to include further
phenomena related to multiphase flow and gas phase interactions. In particular, we will improve
the current bubbly flow solver, accompanied by verification and validation against more reference
data. Additionally, we are currently enhancing the level-set model to simulate bubble injection into
salt incorporating interfacial mass transfer in order to study tritium extraction from the molten salt
via sparging.

 48

 The capabilities developed this year, along with those planned for next year, will further expand
the range of coupled physical and chemical phenomena that MOSCATO can simulate. With these
advancements, we ultimately expect MOSCATO to address the key multiphysics challenges in MSRs,
including corrosion, tritium transport, and electrochemical reactions.
