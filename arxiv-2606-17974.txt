Edge-Number Bounds for the Inversion Diameter of Graphs
Jiawen Bo, Anqi Li, Xiaopan Lian, Xin Yan

Abstract
The inversion of a set X X of vertices in an oriented graph reverses every arc with both endpoints in X X . The inversion graph I ⁡ ( G ) I(G) of a graph G G has the labelled orientations of G G as its vertices, two orientations being adjacent when a single inversion transforms one into the other, and the inversion diameter diam ⁡ ( I ⁡ ( G ) ) \diam(I(G)) is its diameter. Answering a question of Havet, Hörsch and Rambaud, we prove the bound in terms of edge number diam ⁡ ( I ⁡ ( G ) ) ≤ 2 ​ | E ⁡ ( G ) | \diam(I(G))\leq 2\sqrt{|E(G)|} , and we complement it with a lower bound diam ⁡ ( I ⁡ ( G ) ) ≥ | E ⁡ ( G ) | | V ⁡ ( G ) | \diam(I(G))\geq\frac{|E(G)|}{|V(G)|} obtained by viewing I ⁡ ( G ) I(G) as a Cayley graph on 𝔽 2 E ⁡ ( G ) \mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)} . We further refine the upper bound for bipartite graphs G G by showing diam ⁡ ( I ⁡ ( G ) ) ≤ max ⁡ { ρ , ⌈ log 2 ⁡ ( 2 + σ ⁡ ( 2 ρ − 1 − 1 ) ) ⌉ } \diam(I(G))\leq\max\left\{\rho,\left\lceil\log_{2}\bigl(2+\sigma(2^{\rho-1}-1)\bigr)\right\rceil\right\} where the two parts of G G have maximum degrees σ \sigma and ρ \rho , respectively.

Introduction

All graphs in this paper are finite and simple. For a graph
G
, denote by

,

,
\Delta(G)
and
\bar{d}(G)=2|E(G)|/|V(G)|
its

,

,

, and

, respectively. Additionally, we say

. For

The study of graph inversions originated with the work of Belkhechine
[
5
]
.
Given an oriented graph
\overrightarrow{G}
and a set
X
of its vertices, the
inversion
of
X
reverses all arcs with both endpoints in
X
, and denote the obtained oriented graph by
\Inv(\overrightarrow{G},X)
.
Much research has focused on the
inversion number

\operatorname{inv}(D)
of a digraph
D
, the minimum number of inversions required to render
D
acyclic.
Belkhechine et al.
[
6
]
proved that for every fixed
k
, deciding whether the inversion number of a tournament is at most
k
is polynomial-time solvable; in contrast, Bang-Jensen et al.
[
4
]
proved that deciding whether the inversion number of a digraph is
1
is NP-complete.
For the maximum inversion number
\operatorname{inv}(n)
over all oriented graphs of order
n
, Aubian et al.
[
3
]
and Alon et al.
[
1
]
independently proved

n-2\sqrt{n\log n}\leq\operatorname{inv}(n)\leq n-\lceil\log_{2}(n+1)\rceil
.
Restricting the size of the sets, for a digraph
D
, denote by
\operatorname{inv}_{k}(D)
the minimum number of inversions using sets of size at most
k

that render
D
acyclic. Let
\operatorname{inv}_{k}(n)
be the maximum inversion number over all oriented graphs of order
n
.
Spencer
[
11
]
proved that
\operatorname{inv}_{2}(n)\leq n(n-1)/4-\Omega(n^{3/2})
. For more results about
\operatorname{inv}_{k}(n)
, we refer to
[
7
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Beyond acyclicity, Duron et al.
[

]
investigated the minimum number of inversions making a digraph

-arc-strong or
k
-strong.

The inversion diameter of a graph was introduced by Havet et al.
[
10
]
.
Rather than reaching an acyclic orientation, it concerns transformations between arbitrary orientations of a fixed graph
G
.
The
inversion graph

I(G)
has as its vertices the labelled orientations of
G
; two orientations
\overrightarrow{G}_{1}
and
\overrightarrow{G}_{2}
are adjacent if there is a set
X
of vertices with

\Inv(\overrightarrow{G}_{1},X)=\overrightarrow{G}_{2}
.
The
inversion diameter

\diam(I(G))
is the diameter of
I(G)
: the worst-case number of inversions needed to transform one orientation of
G
into another.

Havet et al.
[
10
]
showed that the inversion diameter is functionally equivalent to the star chromatic number
\chi_{s}(G)
, the acyclic chromatic number
\chi_{a}(G)
and the oriented chromatic number
\chi_{o}(G)
, so that graph classes with any of these parameters bounded also have bounded inversion diameter.
They proved the following.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
10
]
)
.

For every graph
G
, it holds
\diam(I(G))\leq 2\Delta(G)-1
.

They also showed
\diam(I(G))\leq\Delta(G)
when
\Delta(G)\leq 2
, and conjectured the following.

Conjecture 1.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

For every graph
G
, it holds

\diam(I(G))\leq\Delta(G)
.

Wang et al.
[
15
]
verified Conjecture
1.2
for
\Delta(G)=3
using computer assistance.
For bipartite graphs the stronger bound

\diam(I(G))\leq\Delta(G)+\lceil\log_{2}\Delta(G)\rceil-1

was obtained in
[
10
]
, and Arana et al.
[
2
]
extended such results to all triangle-free graphs
G
:

\diam(I(G))\leq\Delta(G)+\lfloor\log_{2}\Delta(G)\rfloor.

(1)

It is natural to ask how the density of
G
governs
\diam(I(G))
.
Havet et al.
[
10
]
raised the following problem.

Problem 1.3

(
[
10
]
)
.

Is there an absolute constant
\alpha
such that

\diam(I(G))\leq\alpha\sqrt{|E(G)|}

for every graph
G
?

This paper studies bounds dependent on edge number.
Our first result (Theorem
1.4
) answers Problem
1.3
affirmatively.

Theorem 1.4
.

For every graph
G
,

\diam(I(G))\leq 2\sqrt{|E(G)|}.

We show that the exponent
1/2
is optimal in the strongest possible sense, via a clean lower bound that holds for every graph.
In Section
2
, we explains that, after fixing a reference orientation,
I(G)
is a Cayley graph on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)}
, which makes the bound intuitively clear.

Theorem 1.5
.

For every graph
G
with at least one edge,

\diam(I(G))\geq\frac{|E(G)|}{|V(G)|}=\frac{\bar{d}(G)}{2}.

Combining Theorems

and
1.5
sandwiches the inversion diameter between
|E(G)|/|V(G)|
and

, both of which are
\Theta(n)
for the complete graph
K_{n}
.
Thus
\sqrt{|E(G)|}
is the correct order for every dense family, not merely for complete graphs.

We next characterize the optimal constant in Theorem
1.4
which drops to
\sqrt{2}
, once Conjecture
1.2
holds.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\alpha^{*}
be the least constant such that

\diam(I(G))\leq\alpha^{*}\sqrt{|E(G)|}

for every graph
G
.
Then
\sqrt{2}\leq\alpha^{*}\leq 2
, where
\sqrt{2}
is witnessed by complete graphs, with
\alpha^{*}=\sqrt{2}
if Conjecture
1.2

Moreover,

\diam(I(G))\leq\sqrt{2|E(G)|}

holds whenever
\diam(I(H))\leq\Delta(H)

\Delta(G)\leq 3
.

Furthermore, for triangle-free graphs, which include all bipartite graphs, the upper constant
\sqrt{2}
is attained up to a logarithmic term, by feeding (
1
) into the same induction.

Theorem 1.7
.

For every triangle-free graph
G

|E(G)|\geq 1
,

\diam(I(G))\leq\sqrt{2|E(G)|}+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\bigl(2|E(G)|\bigr).

We next refine the bipartite results.
Write
G[A,B]
for a bipartite graph with parts
A
and
B
, and let

\Delta(A):=\max_{a\in A}d_{G}(a),\qquad\Delta(B):=\max_{b\in B}d_{G}(b).

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
G[A,B]
be a bipartite graph with
\Delta(G)\leq 3
.
Then
\diam(I(G))\leq\Delta(G)
, that is, Conjecture

holds for bipartite graphs of maximum degree at most three.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
G[A,B]
be a bipartite graph with
\Delta(A)=\sigma
and
\Delta(B)=\rho
.
If
\rho=0
, then
\diam(I(G))=0
.
If
\rho\geq 1
, then

\diam(I(G))\leq\max\left\{\rho,\left\lceil\log_{2}\bigl(2+\sigma(2^{\rho-1}-1)\bigr)\right\rceil\right\}.

The same bound holds with
\sigma
and
\rho

As a consequence, the inversion diameter of a bipartite graph can be exponentially smaller than its maximum degree (Corollary
4.4
).

Finally, we study lexicographic products with edgeless graphs.
For a graph
G

obtained by replacing each vertex of

Theorem 1.10

For every positive integer

\diam(I(F[\overline{K_{t}}]))\leq t\cdot\diam(I(F)).

2
Preliminaries

We first show that the inversion graph could be seen as a Cayley graph. Fix a reference orientation
D_{0}
of
G
and identify each orientation
D
with the vector

\delta(D)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)}
recording, for each edge, whether
D
and
D_{0}
disagree there, i.e. for each edge
e
, the entry is 1 if they disagree on
e
, otherwise, 0.
For
X\subseteq V(G)
, let
\chi_{X}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)}
be the indicator of the edges with both endpoints in
X
,

\chi_{X}(e)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{both endpoints of }e\text{ lie in }X.

Inverting
X
reverses exactly the edges counted by
\chi_{X}
, so

\delta(\Inv(D,X))=\delta(D)+\chi_{X}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let

S=\{\chi_{X}:X\subseteq V(G)\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)}.

If Cayley graphs are allowed to have loops, then

I(G)\cong\Cay(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)},S).

Equivalently, the usual loopless inversion graph is obtained by using the connection set
S\setminus\{0\}
; deleting loops does not change distances.
Consequently
I(G)
is vertex-transitive, and
\diam(I(G))
equals the covering radius of
S
: the least
r
such that every vector of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)}
is a sum of at most
r
elements of
S
.

Proof.

Under
\delta
, two orientations
D_{1},D_{2}
are adjacent in
I(G)
if and only if

\delta(D_{2})-\delta(D_{1})=\chi_{X}
for some
X
, equivalently

\delta(D_{1})+\delta(D_{2})\in S
, since we are over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
This is exactly the adjacency relation in the Cayley graph of the group
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)}
with connection set
S
, apart from possible loops arising from
0\in S
.
The loopless version has the same distances.
A Cayley graph is vertex-transitive, and the distance from the identity to a vector
v
is the least number of elements of
S
summing to
v
.
Taking the maximum over
v
gives the stated covering radius.
∎

The next two lemmas convert inversion diameter bounds into bilinear representation problems over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
For
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}
write

x\cdot y=\sum_{i=1}^{t}x_{i}y_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}

for the standard bilinear form.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G[A,B]
be a bipartite graph and let
t\geq 0
be an integer.
Suppose that for every map
\pi:E(G)\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
, there exist maps

f:A\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}
and
g:B\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}
such that

f(a)\cdot g(b)=\pi(ab)

for every
ab\in E(G)
.
Then
\diam(I(G))\leq t
.

Proof.

Let
\overrightarrow{G}_{1},\overrightarrow{G}_{2}
be arbitrary orientations and define
\pi(ab)=1
if
\overrightarrow{G}_{1}
and
\overrightarrow{G}_{2}
orient
ab
oppositely, and
0
otherwise.
Choose
f,g
as in the hypothesis and write

f(a)=(f_{1}(a),\ldots,f_{t}(a))
and
g(b)=(g_{1}(b),\ldots,g_{t}(b))
.
For
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
, set

X_{i}:=\{a\in A:f_{i}(a)=1\}\cup\{b\in B:g_{i}(b)=1\}.

For an edge
ab
, the inversion on
X_{i}
reverses
ab
if and only if
f_{i}(a)=g_{i}(b)=1
.
Hence the parity of the number of times
ab
is reversed by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{t}
is

\sum_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}(a)g_{i}(b)=f(a)\cdot g(b)=\pi(ab)\pmod{2}.

Thus the edges on which
\overrightarrow{G}_{1}
and
\overrightarrow{G}_{2}
disagree are reversed exactly an odd number of times, so applying the inversions
X_{1},\ldots,X_{t}
to
\overrightarrow{G}_{1}
yields
\overrightarrow{G}_{2}
.
As the two orientations were arbitrary,
\diam(I(G))\leq t
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
t\geq 0
be an integer.
Suppose that for every map
\pi:E(G)\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
, there is a map

h:V(G)\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}
such that

h(u)\cdot h(v)=\pi(uv)

for every
uv\in E(G)
.
Then
\diam(I(G))\leq t
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to Lemma
2.2
, taking

X_{i}=\{v\in V(G):h_{i}(v)=1\}
.
The parity of times that an edge
uv
is reversed is

\sum_{i=1}^{t}h_{i}(u)h_{i}(v)=h(u)\cdot h(v)=\pi(uv)
modulo
2
.
∎

The following monotonicity property of
[
10
]
and elementary inequality will also be applied later.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
10
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

For every graph
G
and every
v\in V(G)
,

\diam(I(G))\leq\diam(I(G-v))+1.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
m\geq 1
and
0\leq\Delta\leq m
.
If
\Delta>\sqrt{2m}
, then
\sqrt{2m}-\sqrt{2(m-\Delta)}>1
.

Proof.

Using
\sqrt{2(m-\Delta)}\leq\sqrt{2m}
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{2m}-\sqrt{2(m-\Delta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Delta}{\sqrt{2m}+\sqrt{2(m-\Delta)}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2\Delta}{2\sqrt{2m}}=\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{2m}}>1.

∎

Applying Lemmas
2.4
and
2.5
, the following result holds.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of graphs closed under vertex deletion such that every
H\in\mathcal{C}
satisfies

\diam(I(H))\leq\Delta(H)
.
Then every
G\in\mathcal{C}
satisfies

\diam(I(G))\leq\sqrt{2|E(G)|}.

Proof.

We prove by inducting on
|V(G)|
. Set
m=|E(G)|
and
\Delta=\Delta(G)
.
If
m=0
, both sides are
0
.
If
\Delta\leq\sqrt{2m}
, then

\diam(I(G))\leq\Delta\leq\sqrt{2m}

since
G\in\mathcal{C}
.
Otherwise
\Delta>\sqrt{2m}
.
As
m\geq\Delta
, the graph
G^{\prime}=G-v
obtained by deleting a vertex of degree
\Delta
has

|E(G^{\prime})|=m-\Delta\geq 0
and lies in
\mathcal{C}
.
By Lemma
2.4
, the induction hypothesis and Lemma
2.5
,

\diam(I(G))\leq\diam(I(G^{\prime}))+1\leq\sqrt{2(m-\Delta)}+1<\sqrt{2m}.

∎

3
Bounds for general graphs

In this section, we prove bounds on the inversion diameter for general graphs, specifically, establish Theorems
1.4
,
1.5
and
1.6
. Let us start from the upper bound.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Write
m=|E(G)|
and
\Delta=\Delta(G)
.
The claim is trivial when
m=0
, so assume
m\geq 1
.
If
m\geq\Delta^{2}
, then
\Delta\leq\sqrt{m}
and Theorem
1.1
gives

\diam(I(G))\leq 2\Delta-1\leq 2\sqrt{m}-1<2\sqrt{m}.

Now suppose
m<\Delta^{2}
, i.e.
\Delta>\sqrt{m}
.
We induct on
|V(G)|
.
The base cases are immediate.
Let
v
be a vertex of degree
\Delta
and put
G^{\prime}=G-v
.
Then
|E(G^{\prime})|=m-\Delta
.
By the induction hypothesis,

\diam(I(G^{\prime}))\leq 2\sqrt{m-\Delta}.

By Lemma
2.4
,

\diam(I(G))\leq\diam(I(G^{\prime}))+1\leq 2\sqrt{m-\Delta}+1.

Since
\Delta>\sqrt{m}
,

2\sqrt{m-\Delta}+1<2\sqrt{m-\sqrt{m}}+1.

It remains only to observe that

2\sqrt{m-\sqrt{m}}+1<2\sqrt{m},

which is equivalent, after moving
1
to the other side and squaring, to

4m-4\sqrt{m}<4m-4\sqrt{m}+1
.
Thus
\diam(I(G))<2\sqrt{m}
, completing the induction.
∎

Now, we move to the proof of the lower bound.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Write
n=|V(G)|
,
m=|E(G)|
and
k=\diam(I(G))
.
By Proposition
2.1
, every vector of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
is a sum of at most
k
elements of

S=\{\chi_{X}:X\subseteq V(G)\}
.
Since
0\in S
, the reachable vectors form the image of the map

S^{k}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m},\qquad(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})\longmapsto s_{1}+\cdots+s_{k},

whose domain has
|S|^{k}\leq(2^{n})^{k}=2^{nk}
elements.
As all
2^{m}
vectors must be reached,
2^{nk}\geq 2^{m}
, i.e.
nk\geq m
.
Hence

k\geq\frac{m}{n}=\frac{|E(G)|}{|V(G)|}=\frac{\bar{d}(G)}{2}.

∎

We finish this section by showing the bounds on the optimal constant, i.e. Theorem
1.6
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

The bound
\alpha^{*}\leq 2
is Theorem
1.4
.
For result under the assumption that Conjecture
1.2
holds and the last sentence, apply Proposition
2.6
: the class of all graphs satisfies its hypothesis under Conjecture
1.2
, giving
\alpha^{*}=\sqrt{2}
; the class
\{G:\Delta(G)\leq 3\}
is closed under vertex deletion and satisfies the hypothesis, by
[
10
]
for
\Delta\leq 2
and by
[
15
]
for
\Delta=3
, giving
\diam(I(G))\leq\sqrt{2|E(G)|}
for
\Delta(G)\leq 3
.
Finally, for the lower bound, the complete graph
K_{n}
satisfies

\frac{\diam(I(K_{n}))}{\sqrt{|E(K_{n})|}}=\frac{n-1}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{2(n-1)}{n}}\longrightarrow\sqrt{2},

where
\diam(I(K_{n}))=n-1
is from
[
10
]
and so
\alpha^{*}\geq\sqrt{2}
.
∎

4
Triangle-free and bipartite graphs

In this section, we revise the upper bound for triangle-free graphs, especially bipartite graphs. For triangle-free graphs, the base case (
1
) is within a logarithmic term of Conjecture
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Set

\varphi(m)=\sqrt{2m}+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(2m)

and let
m=|E(G)|\geq 1
and
\Delta=\Delta(G)\geq 1
. We prove by inducting on
|V(G)|
.
If
\Delta\leq\sqrt{2m}
, then by (
1
) and monotonicity of
\log_{2}
,

\displaystyle\diam(I(G))

\displaystyle\leq\Delta+\lfloor\log_{2}\Delta\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2m}+\log_{2}\sqrt{2m}=\sqrt{2m}+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(2m)=\varphi(m).

If
\Delta>\sqrt{2m}
, by deleting a vertex of degree
\Delta
, we obtain a triangle-free graph
G^{\prime}
with

m^{\prime}=|E(G^{\prime})|=m-\Delta\geq 0
.
If
m^{\prime}=0
, then
\diam(I(G))\leq 1\leq\varphi(m)
.
If
m^{\prime}\geq 1
, then by Lemma
2.4
, the induction hypothesis and Lemma
2.5
,

\displaystyle\varphi(m)-\varphi(m^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\sqrt{2m}-\sqrt{2m^{\prime}}\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\log_{2}(2m)-\log_{2}(2m^{\prime})\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{2m}-\sqrt{2m^{\prime}}>1,

since the logarithmic difference is nonnegative.
Hence

\diam(I(G))\leq\varphi(m^{\prime})+1<\varphi(m)

and so we are done.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

The constant
\sqrt{2}
cannot be obtained by this method with
\sqrt{2m}
replaced by
\sqrt{m}
: when
\Delta\approx\sqrt{m}
, deleting one maximum-degree vertex decreases
\sqrt{m}
by only about
1/2
.
Determining the optimal triangle-free constant remains open, but balanced complete bipartite graphs give the lower bound
1
.
Indeed, for
K_{a,b}
, after fixing a reference orientation, orientation differences are
a\times b
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
An inversion toggles a matrix of the form
xy^{T}
, where
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{a}
and
y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{b}
, hence a matrix of rank at most
1
.
Conversely every matrix of rank
r
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is a sum of
r
rank-one matrices.
Therefore the distance from
0
to a matrix
M
is
\operatorname{rank}(M)
, and so

\diam(I(K_{a,b}))=\min\{a,b\}.

Taking
a=b=r
gives
|E(K_{r,r})|=r^{2}
and
\diam(I(K_{r,r}))=r=\sqrt{|E(K_{r,r})|}
.
Thus the least asymptotic triangle-free constant
\beta
satisfies
1\leq\beta\leq\sqrt{2}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.8
uses Lemma
2.2
together with the following representation theorem.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
9
]
)
.

Let
H
be a
3
-uniform hypergraph with maximum degree at most
3
.
Then there is a map
c:V(H)\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}
such that for every hyperedge

\{x,y,z\}\in E(H)
, the vectors
c(x),c(y),c(z)
form a basis of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

If
\Delta(G)\leq 2
, then by Corollary 4.4 for forests and Theorem 6.4 for graphs
G
with
\Delta(G)=2
in
[
10
]
, we are done. Thus suppose
\Delta(G)=3
.
Build a
3
-uniform hypergraph
H
on
A
together with auxiliary vertices as follows: for each
b\in B
with
d_{G}(b)\geq 1
, set

E_{b}:=\begin{cases}N_{G}(b),&d_{G}(b)=3,\\
N_{G}(b)\cup\{z_{b}\},&d_{G}(b)=2,\\
N_{G}(b)\cup\{z_{b},z^{\prime}_{b}\},&d_{G}(b)=1,\end{cases}

where the auxiliary vertices are new.
Let
E(H)
be the distinct
3
-sets among the
E_{b}
.
Then each
a\in A
lies in at most
d_{G}(a)\leq 3
hyperedges and each auxiliary vertex lies in exactly one, so
\Delta(H)\leq 3
.
By Theorem
4.2
, there is
c:V(H)\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}
making the vectors on each hyperedge a basis. Set
f(a)=c(a)
for each
a\in A
.
Then
\{f(a):a\in N_{G}(b)\}
is linearly independent, as a subset of the basis on
E_{b}
, for every
b
with
d_{G}(b)\geq 1
. For each
b
with
d_{G}(b)\geq 1
, the map

T_{b}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N_{G}(b)},\qquad T_{b}(y)=(f(a)\cdot y)_{a\in N_{G}(b)},

is surjective, yielding
g(b)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}
with
f(a)\cdot g(b)=\pi(ab)
. Set
g(b)=0
if
d_{G}(b)=0
. Then
\diam(I(G))\leq 3
by
Lemma
2.2
.
∎

Finally, we provide the upper bound on inversion diameter of bipartite graphs in terms of vertex degree.

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

If
\rho=0
, then
E(G)=\emptyset
and
\diam(I(G))=0
.
Assume
\rho\geq 1
and let

t=\max\left\{\rho,\left\lceil\log_{2}\bigl(2+\sigma(2^{\rho-1}-1)\bigr)\right\rceil\right\},

so that
t\geq\rho
and

2^{t}>1+\sigma(2^{\rho-1}-1).

By Lemma
2.2
it suffices to represent every
\pi:E(G)\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
as

\pi(ab)=f(a)\cdot g(b)
with
f:A\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}
and
g:B\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}
. Now, we proceed by defining
f
and
g
.

Choosing
f
, independently of
\pi
.

Write
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
.
We choose
f(a_{1}),f(a_{2}),\ldots
greedily, maintaining the invariant that for every
b\in B
the already chosen vectors among
\{f(a):a\in N_{G}(b)\}
are linearly independent.
Take
f(a_{1})
to be any nonzero vector.
Suppose
f
is defined on
a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1}
.
For each
b\in N_{G}(a_{i})
, let

W_{b}=\spanop\{f(a_{j}):a_{j}\in N_{G}(b),\ j<i\}.

Choosing
f(a_{i})\notin\bigcup_{b\in N_{G}(a_{i})}W_{b}
preserves the invariant.
At most
d_{G}(b)-1\leq\rho-1
vectors of
N_{G}(b)
are already chosen, and they are independent by the invariant, so

\dim W_{b}\leq\rho-1
and
|W_{b}|\leq 2^{\rho-1}
.
Since each
W_{b}
contains
0
and
|N_{G}(a_{i})|\leq\sigma
,

\left|\bigcup_{b\in N_{G}(a_{i})}W_{b}\right|\leq 1+\sum_{b\in N_{G}(a_{i})}(|W_{b}|-1)\leq 1+\sigma(2^{\rho-1}-1)<2^{t}=|\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}|,

so a valid
f(a_{i})
exists.
Proceeding to the end yields
f
with
\{f(a):a\in N_{G}(b)\}
independent for every
b
.

Solving for
g
.

Fix
\pi
.
For each
b
with
d_{G}(b)\geq 1
the map

T_{b}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N_{G}(b)},\qquad T_{b}(y)=(f(a)\cdot y)_{a\in N_{G}(b)},

has independent rows, using
t\geq\rho\geq|N_{G}(b)|
, hence is surjective.
Pick
g(b)
with
f(a)\cdot g(b)=\pi(ab)
for all
a\in N_{G}(b)
.
Set
g(b)=0
if
d_{G}(b)=0
.
By Lemma
2.2
,
\diam(I(G))\leq t
.
Interchanging
A
and
B
gives the symmetric bound.
∎

Taking
\rho=3
, we have

\lceil\log_{2}(2+3\sigma)\rceil\leq\sigma
for all
\sigma\geq 4
. Combined with Theorem
1.8
, which covers
\sigma\leq 3
, and Theorem
1.9
, we obtain the following extension of Theorem
1.8
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
G[A,B]
be a bipartite graph with
\Delta(B)=3
and
\Delta(A)
arbitrary.
Then
\diam(I(G))\leq\Delta(G)
.

Generally when the two parts have very different maximum degrees, the result in Theorem
1.9
is far stronger than the maximum degree bounds.

Corollary 4.4
.

If one part of a bipartite graph
G
has bounded degree
\rho=O(1)
, then

\diam(I(G))=O(\log\Delta(G)).

Proof.

Take the bounded side to be
B
, so
\rho=O(1)
and
\sigma\leq\Delta(G)
.
By Theorem
1.9
, we have

\diam(I(G))\leq\max\left\{\rho,\left\lceil\log_{2}\bigl(2+\sigma(2^{\rho-1}-1)\bigr)\right\rceil\right\}=O(\log_{2}\sigma)=O(\log_{2}\Delta(G)).

∎

5
Lexicographic products

For lexicographic product,
Havet et al.
[
10
]
proposed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.1

(
[
10
]
)
.

For every graph
G
and every positive integer
t
,

\diam(I(G[\overline{K_{t}}]))\leq t\cdot\diam(I(G)).

To attack such products, we apply a matrix version of Lemma
2.3
. A
t\times t
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
will be denoted by
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t\times t}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
t,\ell
be positive integers.
Fix an arbitrary orientation of the edges of
G
.
Suppose that for every map

\pi:E(G)\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t\times t}
there exists a map

U:V(G)\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell\times t}
such that

\pi(uv)=U(u)^{T}U(v)

for every oriented edge
uv
of
G
.
Then

\diam(I(G[\overline{K_{t}}]))\leq\ell.

Proof.

Denote the associated vertices in
G[\overline{K_{t}}]
of
v\in V(G)
by
\{(v,1),\ldots,(v,t)\}
.
For two orientations of
G[\overline{K_{t}}]
, define for each oriented edge
uv
of
G
a matrix

\pi(uv)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t\times t}
whose
(i,j)
entry is
1
exactly when the two orientations disagree on the edge
(u,i)(v,j)
.
Choose
U
as in the hypothesis.
For
s\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, set

X_{s}:=\{(v,i):U(v)_{s,i}=1\}.

For an edge
(u,i)(v,j)
lying above an oriented base edge
uv
, the inversion on
X_{s}
reverses it if and only if

U(u)_{s,i}=U(v)_{s,j}=1
.
Thus the parity of the number of reversals over
s=1,\ldots,\ell
is

\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}U(u)_{s,i}U(v)_{s,j}=\bigl(U(u)^{T}U(v)\bigr)_{i,j}=\pi(uv)_{i,j}.

Hence the
\ell
inversions transform one orientation into the other.
Since the orientations were arbitrary, the diameter is at most
\ell
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field and let
B,C\in\mathbb{K}^{2t\times t}
.
If the concatenation
(B\mid C)\in\mathbb{K}^{2t\times 2t}
is invertible, then for all
X,Y\in\mathbb{K}^{t\times t}
there is a unique
Z\in\mathbb{K}^{2t\times t}
with

B^{T}Z=X\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z^{T}C=Y.

Proof.

The second equation is equivalent to
C^{T}Z=Y^{T}
, so the pair is equivalent to

\begin{pmatrix}B^{T}\\
C^{T}\end{pmatrix}Z=\begin{pmatrix}X\\
Y^{T}\end{pmatrix}.

The coefficient matrix equals
(B\mid C)^{T}
, which is invertible since
(B\mid C)
is. Hence
Z
exists and is unique.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field and let
M_{1},M_{2},...,M_{k}\in\mathbb{K}^{t\times t}
with
k\geq 3
. Then there exist
A_{1},A_{2},...,A_{k}\in\mathbb{K}^{2t\times t}
such that

A_{1}^{T}A_{2}=M_{1},~A_{2}^{T}A_{3}=M_{2},...,\text{ and }A_{k}^{T}A_{1}=M_{k}.

Proof.

It is sufficient to consider the following two cases.

Case 1.

k=2r+1
where
r\geq 1
.

Put

P=\begin{pmatrix}I_{t}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q=\begin{pmatrix}M_{k}^{T}\\
I_{t}\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=P+Q.

Then
(P\mid Q)
and
(P\mid R)
are invertible, since they are block-triangular with identity diagonal blocks. Moreover,
(Q\mid R)
is invertible. If
Qx+Ry=0
, then since
R=P+Q
, one has

Q(x+y)+Py=0
, hence
x+y=0
and
y=0
because
(P\mid Q)
is invertible, so
x=y=0
.

Let
B_{1}=P
, let
B_{r+1}=Q
, and alternate
P,R
for
B_{1},\ldots,B_{r}
, so
B_{1}=P,B_{2}=R,B_{3}=P,\ldots
.
Adjacent pairs
(B_{i},B_{i+1})
are always one of

(P,R),(R,P),(P,Q),(R,Q)
, hence have invertible concatenation.
Let

A_{2i-1}=B_{i}\qquad(i=1,\ldots,r+1),

noting that
2(r+1)-1=k
.
For each
i=1,\ldots,r
, by Lemma
5.3
, there is
A_{2i}
with

B_{i}^{T}A_{2i}=M_{2i-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{2i}^{T}B_{i+1}=M_{2i},

so that
A_{j}^{T}A_{j+1}=M_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,2r
.
Finally

A_{2r+1}^{T}A_{1}=Q^{T}P=M_{k}=M_{2r+1},

since
Q^{T}P=(M_{k}\mid I_{t})\binom{I_{t}}{0}=M_{k}
.

Case 2.

k=2r
with
r\geq 2
.

Put

P=\begin{pmatrix}I_{t}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
I_{t}\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=\begin{pmatrix}I_{t}\\
I_{t}\end{pmatrix},

for which
(P\mid Q)
,
(P\mid R)
and
(Q\mid R)
are clearly invertible.

Let
B_{1}=B_{r+1}=P
.
If
r
is even, alternate
P,Q
for
B_{1},\ldots,B_{r}
, so
B_{r}=Q
.
If
r
is odd, alternate
P,Q
for
B_{1},\ldots,B_{r-1}
and set
B_{r}=R
.
In either case adjacent pairs
(B_{i},B_{i+1})
have invertible concatenation.
Let

A_{2i-1}=B_{i}\qquad(i=1,\ldots,r),

and for each
i=1,\ldots,r
, by Lemma
5.3
, there is
A_{2i}
with

B_{i}^{T}A_{2i}=M_{2i-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{2i}^{T}B_{i+1}=M_{2i}.

Then
A_{j}^{T}A_{j+1}=M_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,2r-1
, and since

B_{r+1}=B_{1}=A_{1}
the wrap-around edge gives

A_{2r}^{T}A_{1}=M_{2r}
.
∎

Now, we show that Conjecture
5.1
holds for forests and cycles.

Proof of Theorem
1.10
.

Suppose that
F=C_{k}
. Let
C_{k}=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{k}v_{1}
with edges
e_{i}=v_{i}v_{i+1}
, indices modulo
k
. Then
\diam(C_{k})\geq 2
by considering the distance between
v_{1}\to\cdots\to v_{k}\to v_{1}
and
v_{1}\to v_{2}\leftarrow v_{3}\leftarrow\cdots\leftarrow v_{k}\leftarrow v_{1}
.
Let
\pi:E(C_{k})\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t\times t}
with
\pi(e_{i})=M_{i}
.
By Lemma
5.2
with
\ell=2t
and Lemma
5.4
, there is

f:V(C_{k})\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2t\times t}
with

f(v_{j})^{T}f(v_{j+1})=M_{j}

for all
j
modulo
k
. Suppose that
F
is a forest, by Corollary 4.1 in
[
10
]
, the same argument works for
F
being a forest with
\diam(I(F))=2
. If
E(F)=\emptyset
, then we are done. It remains to assume
F=K_{1,t}
whose
\diam(I(F))=1
. Let
v
be the vertex that forms a single partite. By recursively considering oriented edges incident with
(v,i)
, we know
\diam(I(F[\overline{K_{t}}]))\leq t
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

The graph
C_{k}[\overline{K_{t}}]
is
2t
-regular.
Moreover,
|V(C_{k}[\overline{K_{t}}])|=kt
and
|E(C_{k}[\overline{K_{t}}])|=kt^{2}
, so Theorem
1.5
gives

\diam(I(C_{k}[\overline{K_{t}}]))\geq t.

Hence

t\leq\diam\bigl(I(C_{k}[\overline{K_{t}}])\bigr)\leq 2t,

and the bound of Theorem
1.10
is optimal up to a factor of two.
