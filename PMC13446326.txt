Enhanced memory function and reduced perceived stress in older adults with self-reported memory complaints following strawberry leaf extract supplementation: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study
Authors: Valérie Dormal, Stéphanie Dudonné, Christian Gombert, Lise Laurençon, Julien Fouilland, Marine Buchet, Inès Zacharias, Sylvie Copine, Laurent Simar, Louise Deldicque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Strawberry leaf extract improved episodic memory in older adults with self-reported memory complaints.•Significant benefits were observed in Paired Associates Learning performance versus placebo.•Improvements were also noted in verbal and working memory outcomes.•Supplementation reduced perceived stress after 12 weeks.•The intervention was safe and well tolerated in this population.

Introduction

Age-related changes in cognitive performance represent a growing public health challenge in aging societies. Even in the absence of overt neurodegenerative disease, older adults frequently report difficulties in memory, attention, and executive functioning, which can negatively impact on daily functioning, emotional well-being, and quality of life [1,2]. As life expectancy continues to increase, there is a growing need for safe and effective interventions aimed at preserving cognitive function and psychological well-being in elderly populations.

In parallel with this demographic shift, there has been increasing interest in nutritional and botanical interventions as non-pharmacological strategies to support cognitive performance. Conventional pharmacological treatments have limited efficacy in non-pathological populations and may be associated with adverse effects, making them less suitable for preventive or long-term use [[3], [4], [5]]. Consequently, plant-derived extracts rich in polyphenols have gained attention due to their neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties, as well as their favorable safety profiles [[6], [7], [8]].

Within this broad class of bioactive compounds, ellagitannins (ETs) have emerged as compounds of particular interest. ETs are a class of hydrolyzable polyphenols found in selected food sources, including berries from the Rubus genus (i.e., cloudberry, raspberry, blackberry) and the Fragaria genus (strawberry) [9]. Other sources are pomegranate, nuts, and seeds. Growing evidence from in vitro and animal studies suggests that ETs and their gut-derived metabolites, notably urolithins, exert neuroprotective effects through multiple mechanisms, including antioxidant activity, modulation of neuroinflammatory pathways, and protection against oxidative stress–induced neuronal damage [10]. Although direct evidence in humans remains limited, emerging clinical data indicates that dietary intake of ET-rich foods or extracts may positively influence cognitive performance. A small number of intervention studies have reported improvements in memory-related outcomes and other cognitive domains following ET consumption, supporting a potential role for these compounds in cognitive performance [10]. For example, pomegranate consumption, in the form of extract or juice, was reported to protect against post-surgery memory deficits and to enhance memory performance in older adults with memory complaints [11,12]. Moreover, a 12-week controlled trial using daily strawberry powder reported reduced memory interference and improved executive functioning in middle-aged adults with self-reported cognitive decline [13]. Additional randomized controlled trials with freeze-dried strawberry supplementation in older adults have shown enhancements in processing speed and antioxidant capacity, although effects on specific memory outcomes vary [14].

Strawberry leaves, an underutilized byproduct of cultivation, show significant potential for bioactive applications. A recent study reported that cultivated strawberry (Fragaria x ananassa) leaf extracts showed a higher total polyphenol content (up to 122-fold) and antioxidant activity (up to 13-fold), compared to strawberry fruits [15]. In particular, strawberry leaves are rich in ETs and other phenolic compounds such as flavonoids and phenolic acids [16]. In a recent preclinical study, an aqueous extract of strawberry leaves demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties and improvements in cognitive performance in an animal model of metabolic dysfunction [17]. However, despite these promising findings, no human data is available to support the efficacy of strawberry leaf-derived polyphenols on cognitive performance. While emerging clinical trials suggest potential cognitive benefits of strawberry supplementation, such as enhanced processing speed, memory performance, and reductions in depressive symptoms in older adults and middle-aged individuals [13,14,18], these studies are scarce and focus on the fruit, limiting direct comparison with the leaf.

Beyond cognitive outcomes, psychological stress and well-being are relevant factors in healthy aging that may also be influenced by nutritional interventions [2,8]. Polyphenols, including ellagitannins and their metabolites, have been suggested to affect stress-related and well-being–related processes through both direct and indirect pathways, including modulation of oxidative stress, inflammatory status, and gut–brain axis signaling [[6], [7], [8],10]. These mechanisms may therefore contribute not only to cognitive benefits but also to improved stress and overall psychological well-being outcomes.

Therefore, there is a clear need for well-designed clinical trials to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and broader psychological impact of strawberry leaf extract (SLE) supplementation in aging populations. The present randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group, monocentric clinical trial was designed to address this gap by investigating the effects of a 12-week supplementation with SLE on cognitive performance, including memory abilities and executive functions, as well as perceived stress and psychological well-being, in healthy elderly adults with self-reported memory complaints.

Materials and methods

Participants

Eligible participants were women or men aged between 60 and 80 years, with self-reported cognitive difficulties, defined as a Cognitive Difficulties Scale (CDS) score ≥ 15, and having a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score > 24. Additional inclusion criteria included a body mass index (BMI) between 18 and 32.5 kg/m², a minimum of 12 years of formal education, French language proficiency, and willingness to maintain usual lifestyle and nutritional habits throughout the study.

Exclusion criteria included severe medical or cognitive disorders, excessive caffeine consumption (>5 cups/day), regular alcohol intake exceeding three standard drinks per day, smoking or nicotine use, vegetarian or vegan diet, known hypersensitivity to strawberries or study product components, and use of medications or dietary supplements that could interfere with cognitive or emotional processing within 21 days prior to screening. Participants enrolled in another interventional trial were also excluded.

Participants were recruited from the CICN database and through local advertisements, including posters, social networks, and flyers. Pre-screening was conducted via an online questionnaire to assess preliminary eligibility.

All participants provided written informed consent prior to any study-related procedure. The protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee (Comité d’Ethique Hospitalo-Facultaire UCLouvain/Cliniques Universitaires Saint-Luc, Belgium) in January 2025 (ref. no. 2024/03DEC/534) and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines as required by the following regulations: the Belgian law of 7 May 2004 regarding experiments on human beings and the EU Directive 2001/20/EC on Clinical Trials (registration at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT06819163).

Study design

This interventional study was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group, monocentric clinical trial. Participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the SLE group (n = 54) or the placebo group (n = 55), with stratification by gender.

The intervention lasted 12 weeks and included four visits at the clinical investigation center (UCLouvain, Belgium): a screening visit (V0), a baseline visit (V1), an intermediate visit at 6 weeks (V2), and a final visit at 12 weeks (V3). At the screening visit (V0), eligibility criteria were verified, informed consent was obtained, demographic data and medical history were collected, and physical examinations were performed. Cognitive screening was conducted using MMSE and CDS. At baseline (V1), cognitive performance was assessed using subtests of the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB), evaluating attention and psychomotor speed (Motor screening Task and Reaction Time), memory abilities (Spatial Working Memory, Paired Associate Learning, and Verbal Recognition Memory) and executive function (Multitasking). Participants also completed self-administered questionnaires including the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), the Fatigue Assessment Scale (FAS), and the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI). The first dose of the investigational product was taken on site. At the intermediate (V2) and final visits (V3), cognitive assessments and questionnaires were repeated. Concomitant medication use, caffeine consumption and adverse events were recorded at each visit. Participants returned unused capsules at the final visit to assess compliance.

Interventions and randomization

The active arm of the study received capsules containing 150 mg of SLE (Sepibright™, Seppic, France), 218 mg of maltodextrin and 12 mg of magnesium stearate. SLE is a spray-dried ingredient obtained from the hot water extraction of upcycled cultivated strawberry leaves (Fragaria × ananassa), sourced from Brittany (France). Strawberry leaves were obtained from producers during the technical leaf removal process, and subsequently dried and crushed before extraction. The placebo arm received capsules containing 368 mg of maltodextrin and 12 mg of magnesium stearate, thereby providing no polyphenols. The two investigational products were supplied as visually identical opaque soft gelatin (bovine) capsules, to ensure blinding for both participants and investigators. Both products were stored at ambient temperature (10–25 °C), protected from light, in a secure location with restricted access.

Throughout the 3-month study, participants consumed two capsules every morning. This resulted in a daily intake of approximately 20 mg of polyphenols in the active group, including 4 mg of total ellagitannins and 3 mg of salidroside. Total polyphenols were determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method, while total ellagitannins were quantified by reverse-phase analytical HPLC. Briefly, total ellagitannin content was estimated by the quantification of released ellagic acid following acid hydrolysis, as previously described [19], using a Waters Alliance 2695 HPLC system (Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a PDA 2996 detector, and a Phenomenex Luna C18 reverse phase column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size, Torrance, CA, USA). Salidroside was quantified using a Waters Alliance 2695 HPLC System equipped with an Acquity QDa detector, and a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size) with a mobile phase gradient of acetonitrile and water supplemented with 2 mM ammonium formate. Separation was achieved using a 25-minute method run at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min, with a gradient transition from 2.2% to 100% organic solvent. Detection and quantification were performed using the [M-H]-ion at m/z = 299 Da.

Cognitive and psychological assessments

Cognitive performance, including attention, psychomotor speed, memory, working memory and executive function, was assessed using the validated CANTAB [20]. The Reaction Time (RTI) and Motor Screening Task (MOT) were administered to familiarize participants with the CANTAB testing environment. These tasks served as preliminary screening to identify potential difficulties related to vision, motor function, or task comprehension, to ensure that participants were able to follow simple instructions, and to familiarize them with the touchscreen interface. Memory was assessed with the Paired Associate Learning (PAL) subtest and the Verbal Recognition Memory (VRM) subtest. The PAL subtest assessed visual memory and new learning. In this task, boxes were displayed on the screen and opened in a randomized order, with one or more boxes containing a visual pattern. The patterns were then displayed one at a time in the center of the screen, and participants were required to select the box in which each pattern was originally located. If an incorrect response was made, the boxes were reopened sequentially to remind participants of the correct locations. Task difficulty increased progressively by increasing the number of boxes and patterns. Outcome measures included total errors (adjusted for task difficulty), level reached and the first-attempt memory score. The VRM subtest assessed verbal memory and new learning, involving both recall and recognition processes associated with fronto-temporal and hippocampal networks. Participants were presented with a sequence of words displayed one at a time on the screen. In the free-recall phase, participants were asked to recall as many words as possible, with responses recorded by the investigator. In the subsequent recognition phase, participants were shown pairs of words consisting of one previously presented word and one distractor and were required to identify the correct word using a two-alternative forced-choice paradigm. This recognition phase was repeated after a delay to assess retention. Outcome measures included the number of correctly recalled words during free recall and the number of correct responses during the immediate and delayed recognition phases. Executive function was determined with the Spatial Working Memory (SWM) subtest and the Multitasking (MTT) subtest. The SWM subtest evaluated the ability to retain and manipulate visuospatial information and placed substantial demands on executive functioning and strategic planning. Participants were presented with a set of colored boxes and were required to search for hidden yellow tokens by selecting boxes sequentially, using a process of elimination. Once a token was found in a box, it would not reappear in that box during the same trial. Task difficulty increased progressively, with up to 12 boxes displayed. To minimize the use of stereotyped strategies, the color and spatial arrangement of boxes varied between trials. Outcome measures included total number of errors and a strategy score, with higher scores indicating less efficient search strategies. The MTT assessed cognitive flexibility, attentional control, and the ability to manage conflicting information. In this task, an arrow appeared on either the left or right side of the screen and could point to either left or right. A cue displayed at the top of the screen instructed participants to respond either according to the spatial location of the arrow or the direction in which it was pointing. In some blocks, the response rule remained constant, whereas in others it varied unpredictably from trial to trial, placing higher demands on cognitive control. Trials could be congruent or incongruent, with incongruent trials requiring greater suppression of task-irrelevant information. Outcome measures included response latency and error rate, as well as multi-tasking cost, defined as the difference in response latency between multitasking and single-task blocks, and incongruency cost, calculated as the difference in reaction time between incongruent and congruent trials. CANTAB data were collected and processed electronically.

Then, self-reported cognitive difficulties, perceived stress, fatigue and sleep quality were assessed using validated questionnaires. Self-reported cognitive difficulties were assessed using the Cognitive Difficulties Scale (CDS), a 15-item self-report questionnaire evaluating memory-related difficulties in activities of daily living [21]. Participants indicated how frequently they experienced each situation during the previous month using a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 3 (“most of the time”). The total score ranged from 0 to 45, with higher scores reflecting more frequent and severe self-reported memory complaints. The perceived stress level was evaluated by the PSS-10 [22], a 10-item questionnaire assessing current levels of experienced stress, with responses measured using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“very often”). The total score ranged from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. Fatigue was assessed using the Fatigue Assessment Scale (FAS) [23], a 10-item self-report questionnaire measuring both physical and mental fatigue. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”). The total score ranges from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating greater levels of fatigue. Sleep quality and insomnia symptoms were evaluated using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) [24], a 7-item questionnaire assessing the nature, severity, and impact of insomnia. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale from 0 (“no problem”) to 4 (“very severe problem”), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 28, with higher scores indicating more severe insomnia symptoms.

Dietary intake control, safety and compliance

Participants were instructed to maintain their usual dietary habits and lifestyle practices throughout the intervention period. Specifically, they were asked not to substantially modify their dietary habits, physical activity levels, caffeine consumption, sleep routines, or habitual medication use during the 12-week supplementation period. Dietary intake was monitored to ensure stability of nutritional habits throughout the intervention. Participants completed a three-day food diary within one week prior to V1 and V3, including two weekdays and one weekend day. All foods and beverages consumed were recorded with details on portion sizes, preparation methods, and additions. Nutritional intake was analyzed using the NUBEL database to estimate energy and macronutrient consumption, and dietary data collected before V1 and V3 were compared to verify dietary stability. Caffeine consumption was also assessed at each study visit to evaluate its stability over time, given its potential influence on cognitive performance, perceived stress, fatigue, and sleep quality. In addition, a food frequency questionnaire focusing on foods containing ETs was administered at V2 and V3 to estimate mean intake over the preceding six weeks. Portion sizes and consumption frequencies were converted into estimated daily ET intake using the Phenol-Explorer 3.6 database. These values were compared between intervention groups to ensure comparable ET intake across groups throughout the study.

Safety and tolerability were assessed through the continuous recording of adverse events from the signing of informed consent until the end of the study. All adverse events were documented and evaluated for severity and potential relationship to the investigational product. Concomitant medication use was recorded continuously from screening until study completion. Compliance was assessed by capsule count at the final visit and expressed as the percentage of capsules consumed relative to the number expected based on the intervention duration.

Statistical analyses

Sample size calculation was based on changes in SWM errors after 12 weeks of supplementation. Using PASS 14.0.7 software and a one-sided two-sample t-test, 53 participants per group were required to detect a between-group difference of 2 points, assuming a standard deviation of 3.5, a power of 80%, and an alpha level of 0.05.

The primary efficacy analyses were performed in both the intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) populations. The ITT population included all randomized participants with at least one post-baseline assessment, whereas the PP population excluded participants with major protocol deviations (1 in the SLE group and 2 in the placebo group)1 .

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 and GraphPad version 10.1.2. The assumption of normality was assessed for all variables prior to the analysis using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Baseline differences in demographic characteristics between the groups were examined using χ2 tests (for categorical variables) and independent t-tests (for continuous variables). The primary analysis was performed using linear mixed-effects models including treatment group, time, and the group-by-time interaction as fixed effects and participant as a random effect. As an exploratory post hoc analysis intended to better characterize the observed temporal changes within each treatment arm and the magnitude of changes from baseline between groups at each follow-up visit (Week 6 vs. baseline and Week 12 vs. baseline), paired t-tests (within-group) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney tests comparing change-from-baseline scores between groups ([Week 6 - baseline] and [Week 12 - baseline]) were additionally performed.

In addition, all primary mixed-effects models were adjusted for the prespecified covariates age and sex to explore their potential influence on the cognitive outcomes. These analyses revealed a significant interaction between age and performance on the VRM subtest. Based on this finding, additional exploratory analyses were conducted to further investigate age-related effects. Participants were stratified into two age groups (<70 years and ≥70 years), and complementary analyses were performed to examine intervention effects on VRM outcomes within and between these age subgroups. All statistical analyses were conducted with an alpha level set at 0.05.

Results

A total of 319 healthy elderly adults were assessed for eligibility, 210 of whom were excluded for the following reasons: 168 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 42 declined to participate. Finally, 109 participants were enrolled and randomized between February and July 2025 into the SLE (n = 54) or placebo (n = 55) groups (Fig. 1). Eight participants dropped out of the study for personal reasons (3 from the SLE group and 5 from the placebo group), and three participants were excluded from the analysis due to protocol deviations: one volunteer from the active group due to low compliance, one from the placebo group due to missing data at Week 6, and one from the placebo group for taking an unauthorized food supplement during the study. No significant group-by-time interactions were observed for the primary or secondary outcomes in the mixed-effects models. To better characterize temporal changes within each treatment group and the magnitude of change from baseline between groups at each follow-up visit, exploratory analyses were subsequently conducted. These analyses suggested several improvements in the SLE group.

Regarding the PAL task, the SLE group exhibited a reduction in total errors at both Week 6 (−4.2 ± 10.0, p = 0.001) and Week 12 (−5.5 ± 11.2, p = 0.001) compared to baseline, accompanied by improvements in the first attempt memory score at these time points (+1.1 ± 3.7, p = 0.008 at Week 6 and +1.7 ± 3.5, p = 0.001 at Week 12). In addition, an increase in the level reached (+0.3 ± 0.8, p = 0.020) was observed at the end of the intervention (Week 12) compared to baseline. No within-group changes over time were observed for any PAL outcomes in the placebo group. Baseline performance did not differ significantly between groups for any of the PAL outcomes. For the VRM task, the SLE group showed an increase in the number of words re-called both at the middle (Week 6; +0.6 ± 2.1, p = 0.047) and at the end of the intervention (Week 12; +1.1 ± 2.4, p = 0.003) compared to baseline. Significant improvements were also observed in immediate recognition at Week 12 (+1.1 ± 3.2, p = 0.026) in the SLE group, while delayed recognition showed a trend towards improvement at the same time point (+0.9 ± 3.3, p = 0.052). In contrast, no changes from baseline were detected for any VRM outcome in the placebo group. However, no between-group differences were detected for changes in any VRM scores at any time point.

Exploratory age-stratified analyses revealed that the improvements observed in the overall sample for the VRM task were primarily driven by participants younger than 70 years. In this younger subgroup, participants in the SLE group showed improvements in the number of words recalled both at Week 6 (+0.9 ± 2.2, p = 0.037) and Week 12 (+1.4 ± 2.1, p = 0.001) and in the immediate and delayed recognition total correct scores at the end of the intervention (Week 12; +1.7 ± 3.0, p = 0.003 and +1.2 ± 3.4, p = 0.036 respectively, Appendix Table A3). The magnitude of these improvements was greater in the SLE group compared to the placebo group at Week 12 for the number of words recalled and the immediate recognition total correct score (p = 0.014 and p = 0.039, respectively). In contrast, no changes were observed in the ≥70 years subgroup for these VRM outcomes.

For the SWM task, a reduction in total errors and an improvement in strategy score were observed in the SLE group at both Week 6 (total errors: -3.4 ± 9.0, p = 0.007; strategy score: −1.5 ± 2.9, p = 0.001) and Week 12 (total errors: −2.2 ± 7.6, p = 0.040; strategy score: −1.3 ± 2.9, p = 0.002) compared to baseline. In the placebo group, an improvement in strategy score was observed only at Week 12 (−0.8 ± 2.5, p = 0.026), while no changes were detected for total errors. However, no between-group difference was detected for changes in any SWM scores at any time point.

For the MTT task, the SLE group showed a reduction in total errors at both Week 6 (−4.1 ± 9.2, p = 0.005) and Week 12 (−3.1 ± 9.9, p = 0.014) compared to baseline. No changes were observed for response latency, multitasking cost, or incongruency cost at any time point. In the placebo group, a reduction in total errors was also observed at Week 6 (−2.9 ± 6.5, p = 0.005) and Week 12 (−3.6 ± 7.3, p < 0.001), along with a decrease in incongruency cost at Week 6 (−22 ± 62, p = 0.020). No between-group differences were observed for changes in any MTT outcomes at any time point.

Dietary intakes control, safety and compliance

Dietary intake remained stable throughout the intervention in both groups (Appendix Table A4). No changes over time were observed for total energy intake or macronutrient consumption, including proteins, lipids, carbohydrates and fiber. Alcohol consumption was not different between the two groups. In addition, no between-group differences were detected for the intake of foods containing ETs, indicating comparable dietary exposure to these compounds in the SLE and placebo groups during the study period (Appendix Table A5).

The SLE supplementation was globally well tolerated. A total of 52 adverse events were reported in the intention-to-treat population (n = 109) during the study, which were comparably distributed between the two groups. Sixteen adverse events were reported as possibly related to the tested supplement, 9 in the placebo group (gastrointestinal disorders, n = 3; sleep disorders, n = 2; headaches, n = 3; dry mouth, n = 1) and 7 in the SLE group (gastrointestinal disorders, n = 3; sleep disorders, n = 2; headaches, n = 1; joint pain, n = 1). A high compliance was observed with an intake of 97.4 ± 4.0% in the SLE group and 98.8 ± 2.8% in the placebo group.

Discussion

The present randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study investigated the effects of a 12-week supplementation with SLE, a polyphenol-rich botanical extract containing ETs, on cognitive performance and psychological well-being in healthy older adults with self-reported memory complaints. Although the primary mixed-effect analysis did not demonstrate significant treatment-by-time interactions, the exploratory analyses suggested that SLE supplementation was associated with improvements in episodic memory performance as well as reduced perceived stress levels. In contrast, more limited effects were observed on executive function and working memory. These findings suggest a potential domain-specific cognitive benefit of SLE, particularly targeting memory processes that rely on hippocampal function. These exploratory findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

The most consistent cognitive effects observed in this study concerned episodic memory. Participants receiving SLE appeared to show improvements in visual episodic memory and associative learning, as measured by the PAL task, which are processes that strongly depend on the integrity of hippocampal structures [e.g., 25,26]. These findings align with pre-clinical evidence demonstrating cognitive improvements following SLE supplementation [17]. Such improvements have been attributed to the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of polyphenolic compounds, which may contribute to the protection of neuronal integrity and synaptic plasticity. Additional support for a beneficial role of ET-rich foods in cognitive aging comes from recent nutritional intervention studies [13,14,18]. While these studies used whole-fruit preparations rather than strawberry leaf extracts, they support the hypothesis that polyphenol-rich strawberry derivatives may contribute to the maintenance of cognitive function during aging.

Interestingly, exploratory subgroup analyses indicated that improvements in verbal episodic memory measured by the VRM task were mainly observed among participants aged 60–69 years. This observation may reflect the well-described trajectory of cognitive aging, in which episodic memory represents one of the earliest cognitive domains to decline, often beginning in the sixth decade of life [27]. At the same time, individuals in this age range may retain sufficient neural plasticity to respond to nutritional or lifestyle interventions aimed at supporting brain function [28]. Consequently, interventions targeting this “window of opportunity” may be particularly effective in maintaining memory performance before more pronounced age-related decline occurs. However, although the increase in the number of words recalled reached statistical significance, the magnitude of the observed effect remained modest and does not allow conclusions regarding its clinical relevance. Future studies in populations with clinically relevant cognitive impairment may help determine whether these effects translate into more substantial cognitive benefits.

In contrast with the clear improvements observed for episodic memory, SLE supplementation produced only modest effects on executive function and working memory. Although within-group improvements were noted for SWM, no significant between-group effects were observed for executive functions. This apparent domain specificity suggests SLE preferentially targets hippocampal circuits over the broader fronto-parietal networks associated with executive control [[29], [30], [31]]. Indeed, several experimental studies have suggested that polyphenols and their metabolites may preferentially target hippocampal structures by modulating synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and neuroinflammatory processes in this brain region [6,8]. Specifically, urolithin A, produced through the gut microbial transformation of ETs, was reported to stimulate hippocampal neurogenesis, prevent neuronal apoptosis and promote mitophagy [32]. By fostering the clearance of damaged mitochondria, urolithin A was found to restore cognitive functions and prevent deficits in spatial memory [33]. Furthermore, the lack of significant executive improvements may be attributed to a ceiling effect. Our study population comprised healthy, well-educated older adults with preserved global cognitive performance (MMSE scores above 24), conditions under which executive tasks often show limited variability, reducing the sensitivity to detect intervention-related improvements compared to episodic memory tasks, which are more sensitive to early age-related decline [34,35].

Beyond cognitive performance, SLE supplementation was associated with a modest but significant reduction in perceived stress levels. Exploratory analyses suggested greater reductions in PSS scores in the SLE group than in the placebo group. Chronic psychological stress has been widely recognized as a factor contributing to hippocampal dysfunction and cognitive decline [36]. Sustained activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis leads to increased cortisol secretion, which may negatively affect hippocampal neurons, impair synaptic plasticity, and ultimately reduce memory performance [37]. The reduction in perceived stress observed in the present study may therefore represent an additional pathway through which SLE supplementation could contribute to improved cognitive outcomes. Polyphenols have been suggested to influence stress-related physiological pathways, attenuate neuroinflammation, and protect neurons from toxicity [38,39]. Furthermore, urolithin A may mitigate anxiety by normalizing mitochondrial and synaptic functions [40]. Consequently, the concomitant improvements in stress perception and episodic memory observed in this study raise the possibility of a synergistic interaction between psychological and neurobiological mechanisms.

The mechanisms underlying these effects are likely multifactorial. In addition to potential direct effects in brain tissues, increasing evidence suggests that modulation of the gut microbiota and improvement of gut barrier integrity may contribute to the neuroprotective properties of ET-derived urolithin A [[41], [42], [43], [44]]. More broadly, a growing body of research highlights the central role of the gut–brain axis in linking diet, microbiota composition, and brain function, including cognitive processes and emotional regulation [[45], [46], [47], [48]].

SLE’s effectiveness may also stem from other phenolic compounds, such as salidroside. Salidroside is a phenylpropanoid glycoside and a primary active constituent of Rhodiola rosea, a botanical historically utilized for its adaptogenic properties [49]. Preclinical research highlights that salidroside exerts significant anxiolytic and antidepressant effects, by modulating the HPA axis and suppressing neuroinflammation [50]. By inhibiting microglial activation and stimulating neurogenesis in the hippocampus, salidroside supports cognitive function and enhance memory and spatial learning in animal models [[49], [50], [51], [52]]. Consequently, the combined biological action of ellagitannins and salidroside likely contributes to the enhanced episodic memory and reduced perceived stress observed in the current clinical trial.

An important strength of this study lies in the rigorous methodological design and the careful control of potential confounding factors. The randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled approach ensures a high level of internal validity and minimizes the risk of bias. In addition, dietary intake was closely monitored throughout the intervention, and nutritional analyses confirmed that total energy intake, macronutrient consumption, and overall dietary patterns remained stable. Importantly, no differences between-groups were observed in the consumption of foods naturally rich in ETs, indicating that the observed cognitive and psychological effects were unlikely to be driven by variations in habitual diet. Further strengths include the use of validated computerized neuropsychological tests, allowing a precise and comprehensive assessment of multiple cognitive domains. Supplementation with SLE was also well tolerated, with no serious adverse events reported and high levels of compliance maintained throughout the study, supporting both the feasibility and safety of this intervention in older populations. However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the study population consisted of relatively healthy and well-educated older adults, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations or individuals with more pronounced cognitive impairment. Moreover, as the primary mixed-effects analysis did not identify significant treatment-by-time interactions, the exploratory findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger, adequately powered clinical trials. In addition, the 12-week intervention period may not be sufficient to fully capture the long-term cognitive benefits associated with ETs supplementation. Although efforts were made to minimize changes in lifestyle habits throughout the intervention, including monitoring dietary intake, caffeine consumption, and concomitant medication use, as well as providing clear instructions for participants to maintain their usual lifestyle practices as consistently as possible, residual confounding related to lifestyle factors influencing secondary outcomes cannot be totally excluded. Finally, the biological mechanisms underlying the observed effects were not directly investigated. Future studies incorporating relevant biomarkers, such as circulating polyphenol metabolites, inflammatory markers, cortisol levels, or gut microbiota profiles, would provide further valuable insights into the mechanisms linking ETs intake to cognitive function. In addition, exploring central nervous system correlates through structural and functional assessments of brain regions involved in cognition and emotional regulation would be highly relevant. In particular, focusing on the hippocampus, given its central role in memory and sensitivity to stress and dietary factors, could help determine whether ETs intake promotes neuroprotective or neuroplastic effects. Examining other key regions, such as the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, as well as networks underlying attention and executive function, using neuroimaging techniques (e.g., fMRI), would further clarify the neural substrates supporting the effects observed in cognitive tests.

Conclusions

In conclusion, although the primary mixed-effects analysis did not demonstrate a significant treatment-by-time interaction, exploratory analyses suggested that supplementation with a polyphenol-rich SLE containing ETs may be associated with improvements in episodic memory performance and perceived stress levels in older adults with self-reported memory complaints. These findings require larger, adequately powered randomized controlled trials to confirm that SLE may represent a promising nutritional strategy to support cognitive health during aging.
