 Abstract. We review data and evaluations for neutron-induced fission for the actinides 233U, 238Pu, 240Pu
 and 242Pu. These isotopes are part of common nuclear fuel cycles, especially for modern fast reactors.
 We focus on incident neutrons in the energy range of 100 keV to 20 MeV and compare the experimental
 data from the literature to the major evaluated libraries ENDF/B-VIII.1, JENDL-5, and JEFF-3.3, as
 well as to LLNL’s ENDL-2009.5 library. Based on the assessment of the fission cross sections, we
 provide recommendations on which library to use. Additional assessments covering other reaction
 channels will be provided separately.

2 Introduction

1. Introduction
Nuclear data evaluations are an important element of the pipeline linking foundational experimental
measurements and theoretical predictions with nuclear science applications, such as reactor modelling or
nuclear astrophysics. Evaluated nuclear data are collected in comprehensive libraries that are maintained
and released by different organizations around the world. Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory
(LLNL) maintains its own compilation of nuclear data, the ENDL library. As part of our effort to keep the
ENDL nuclear data library up to date, we periodically review recent library releases and experimental data.
This report reviews and assesses experimental data and recent evaluations from major library releases for
the neutron induced fission cross section of 233U, 238Pu, 240Pu and 242Pu for an incident neutron energy range
of 100 keV to 20 MeV. Comparing also the ENDL-2009.5 library to the data we make recommendations
for which libraries are in best agreement with currently available experimental data to guide future updates
of ENDL. There is an increasing number of use cases for covariance data in uncertainty quantification and
propagation. Therefore, we also consider the available covariance data in the recent library releases. A
concise summary of our recommendations can be found in Table 9.

1.1 Fission cross section measurement techniques
In the following we provide a very brief overview of key aspects of fission cross section measurement
techniques and terminology. An overview of recent developments in fission cross section measurement can
also be found, e.g. in ref. [1], [2].
All neutron-induced fission cross section measurements consist of three major components: a neutron
source to initiate fission, a target containing the fissile material of interest, and a fragment detector to
identify and quantify the resulting fission products.
Neutron Source – There are two major types of neutron sources: “white” neutron sources and (quasi)
monoenergetic neutron sources. Spallation neutron sources are a common example for white neutron
sources, in which the neutrons are produced by a pulsed charged-particle beam impinging on a high-Z
target, such as tungsten. The shape of the neutron spectrum from these sources depends on the angle of the
beamline to the fission target relative to the incident charged-particle beam. The pulsed nature of the
incident beam allows experiments to determine the neutron energy associated with a particular fission event
by measuring the time-of-flight (TOF) of the neutrons. The length of the flight path is a key parameter that
determines how accurately the neutron energy can be determined.
Mono-energetic neutrons are produced by specific reactions induced by charged particles, such DT fusion,
or 7Li(p,n). With a known charged particle energy provided by an accelerator, the neutron energy at a given
angle is well defined, although significant uncertainties in the neutron energy may arise from the details of
the experimental setup.
In addition to the neutron energy, the neutron flux is a key quantity that needs to be determined in an
absolute cross section measurement. Uncertainties in flux quantification can significantly impact the overall
measurement precision. The most common way to effectively determine the neutron flux, is to use a
reference target with a well-known cross section. For fission cross section measurements, 235U is often used
as a reference isotope. By placing the targets back-to-back, the ratio of the fission cross sections, that is
mostly independent of the incoming particle flux, can be determined with high precision.
Target – A key aspect of the target is its enrichment in the isotope of interest and possible contaminants,
which need to be determined with care for high-precision measurements. For radioactive isotopes, the
isotopic content can be characterized by its activity, e.g., by measuring alpha-decay of the target. Mass
spectrometry is also often used to determine the enrichment. In cases where the beam spot is smaller than
the target area, the homogeneity or detailed distribution of the target isotope in the sample needs to be
accurately determined [3].

Fragment Detector – The third part of any fission cross section measurement is the detector for the fission
fragments. Most measurements use a fission chamber, which means that the fission target is inside a gas-
filled ionization chamber. Ionization due to the fission fragments induces a signal. In its simplest form the
fission chamber is a parallel-plate ionization chamber (PPIC), while more precise instruments rely on
Frisch-grid and twin-Frisch grid ionization chambers [4], which improve the energy resolution for the
measured fragments. More recently, additional types of detectors have been introduced for fission fragment
detection, including parallel plate avalanche counters (PPAC) [5] and time-projection chambers (TPCs) [6].
The detector efficiency, which is generally energy dependent, is usually a source of systematic uncertainty.

1.2 Assessment approach and metrics
Our review of the available experimental data and evaluations for neutron-induced fission on the actinide
targets 233U, 238Pu, 240Pu, and 242Pu relies on data retrieved from the EXFOR database [7]. We collect the
available data for the fission channel from EXFOR, including direct measurements and cross section ratio
data. When a reaction cross section is measured as a ratio relative to a reference cross section, an absolute
cross section obtained with the standard reference cross-section available at the time and the original ratio
data are often both available in EXFOR. For the present assessment we have re-calculated the cross sections
with the most recent evaluations of the reference cross sections, whenever the ratio data was available. In
several instances, the ratio data was not readily available, and we utilized the provided absolute cross
section. Since the reference cross sections did not change significantly, the approach is justified for an
assessment, but we note that re-normalization to the most recent standard cross section should be considered
for a full evaluation.
For each of the four isotopes under consideration, we first select a subset of key experimental data that we
assess to be representative enough in the region of interest. The goal of the selection process is to arrive at
a reasonably small subset of measurements that appropriately covers the energy range of interest and
represent different measurement techniques and research groups as far as possible. We then compare some
of the key experiments to major evaluated libraries, including ENDF/B VIII.1[8], JENDL-5 [9] and JEFF-
3.3 [10]1, as well as ENDL-2009.5 to determine which evaluation best represents the selected data. We also
discuss the covariance data available in the libraries. Finally, we provide a compact overview and summary
table in the conclusions, including our recommendations.

using the LLNL-developed python code CEREAL [11]. The main metrics considered for the comparison

2. Neutron-induced fission of 233U
2.1 Overview
233
 U is a synthetic fissile isotope with a half-life of about 160,000 years and decays primarily by 𝛼 emission.
Due to its fissile character, 233U undergoes neutron-induced fission with a high neutron yield of roughly 2.5
neutrons per fission, sufficient to sustain a chain reaction and to breed additional fissile material. Each
fission releases close to 198 MeV of energy. This isotope plays a central role in the thorium fuel cycle,
where 232Th serves as fertile material that can be converted into 233U in both thermal and fast neutron
reactors. Because thorium is more abundant than uranium, the 232Th–233U cycle has long been proposed as
a pathway to a more sustainable nuclear energy economy. Experimental use has been demonstrated, e.g.,
in the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment [16], [17], and 233U continues to be studied as a candidate fuel in
advanced reactor designs.

2.2 Literature Data Assessment
The early 1950s measurements of the 233U fission cross section had large errors and significant
discrepancies. Since more recent measurements are available that cover the whole energy region of interest,
we exclude experiments performed before 1960 from our assessment. Measurements in the ‘70s, such as
those by Meadows et al. (1974) [18], used electrostatic accelerators with monoenergetic pulsed neutron
beams and claim a precision of around 2%. Most measurements of the 233U(n,f) cross section use 235U(n,f)
as a reference cross section, especially when using white-spectrum neutron sources [19], [20], [21], [22] to
minimize normalization errors. State-of-the-art cross-section ratio measurements exhibit statistical

The evaluated cross sections in ENDL-2009.5 and JEFF-3.3 are identical to ENDF/B VII.1 [31] (released
in 2011).
The evaluated cross section in JENDL-5 is identical to ENDF/B VIII.1 below 500 keV. Above 500 keV,
JENDL-5 cross section values tend to be between ENDF/B-VIII.1 and ENDF/B-VII.0 (JEFF-3.3 and

None of the three evaluations considered the data by Tovesson et al (2014) [6] and Tarrio et al. (2023) [19]

3. Neutron-induced fission of 238Pu
3.1 Overview
238
 Pu is a highly radioactive isotope of plutonium, discovered in 1940 by bombarding 238U with deuterons
and subsequently isolated via its 𝛼-emission properties [33]. It has a half-life of approximately 87.7 years
and decays primarily by 𝛼 emission, releasing significant heat while emitting minimal 𝛾 and neutron
radiation. These characteristics make 238Pu exceptionally well-suited for use as a long-lasting heat source
in radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) and radioisotope heater units, particularly in spacecraft
and remote applications. Although it does not readily fission with thermal (slow) neutrons (i.e., it is not
fissile), 238Pu’s nuclear structure and fission properties are important for a broader understanding of the
physics of fission in the Pu isotopic chain.

3.2 Literature Data assessment
Compared to 233U, experimental data for the neutron-induced fission of 238Pu are very sparse. While the
magnitude of the fission cross section above 1 MeV is significant, the 𝛼 radioactivity is a major obstacle to
performing precise measurements. The combined signal from multiple 𝛼 particles is often indistinguishable
from the signal produced by fission fragments, complicating event identification. To overcome the high
activity of 238Pu, most experiments opt for small samples and extended run times, in combination with
detectors designed to discriminate between 𝛼 particles and fission fragments. The available data has already

3.5 Recommendation
For 238Pu(n,f), all four evaluated libraries are quite similar in the fast neutron region above 100 keV and up
to about 6 MeV. Above 6 MeV, measurements are sparse, consists mostly of derived data and show large
discrepancies. The recent ENDF/B-VIII.1 evaluation presents a smoother cross section at the dip around 5
MeV, differentiating it from the other evaluations. The dip in the cross section is a feature of the
measurement by Fursov et al. (1997) [39], that is not visible in other measurements. Overall, ENDF/B-

with its covariance data be adopted over the current ENDL-2009.5 evaluation. New data, including a review
of exiting surrogate reaction data, above 6 MeV are needed to resolve discrepancies and to improve the
evaluations.

4. Neutron-induced fission of 240Pu
4.1 Overview
240
 Pu is produced when 239Pu captures a neutron without undergoing fission and has a half-life of about
6,561 years. While it is fertile rather than fissile, having a very low thermal neutron fission cross-section,
240
 Pu can capture a neutron to form 241Pu, which is fissile. Notably, 240Pu undergoes a modest rate of
spontaneous fission, releasing neutrons that can prematurely initiate chain reactions.

4.2 Literature Data assessment
Measuring the neutron-induced fission cross section of 240Pu is complicated by its decay by spontaneous
fission—which leads to an unavoidable experimental background—and by its low fission cross section
below 300 keV. At low energies, the presence of even small amounts of fissile contaminants in the sample
makes measurements difficult. In our range of interest between 100 keV and 20 MeV, more than 20
measurements have been performed since the 1950s, most of which agree within the reported uncertainties.
 Overall, there is very little disagreement between the experimental data at the level of the reported
uncertainties which are between 5% and 10%. We have selected 6 representative datasets shown in Figure
9 for our assessment. The key aspects of the selected experiments are summarized in Table 5. We have
excluded measurements that are clear outliers as well as historical measurements with typically much larger
uncertainties or very limited documentation. Given the overall good agreement between experiments, we
have also excluded dataset that contain measurements at less than 5 different energies. In the region of first-
chance fission up to about 5 MeV, there is some spread in the data, with the measurement by Tovesson et
al. (2009) [46] and Laptev et al. (2007) [47] presenting, respectively, the highest and lowest cross sections.

5. Neutron-induced fission of 242Pu
5.1 Overview
242
 Pu is a non-fissile isotope, meaning it does not readily undergo fission with thermal neutrons. However,
it can undergo fission with fast neutrons, as well as sub-barrier fission through low-energy resonances.
Measurements of the sub-barrier fission cross section require a high resolution and can be challenging
because of the relatively low cross sections and competing spontaneous fission and fissile impurities such
as 239Pu. Specifically, the small branching ratio for 242Pu undergoing spontaneous fission needs to be
accounted for when measuring the fission cross section. The isotope’s long half-life of around 375,000
years, make it a relevant isotope for long-term waste considerations and transmutation strategies, and
decays primarily by α decay.

5.2 Literature Data assessment
Discrepancies among measurements of the 242Pu(n,f) cross section around 1 MeV have received noticeable
attention recently, triggering a series of new, high-precision experiments in Europe. Out of more than 30
EXFOR entries for the 242Pu(n,f) cross sections between 100 keV and 20 MeV, we selected six experiments
for our assessment that cover the energy range of interest. We exclude clear outliers as well as data with
large uncertainties, which removes most measurements obtained before 1980. We also exclude the

6. Summary
We find that the evaluations contained in ENDL-2009.5 remain in very good agreement with the available
experimental data a justified compromise given the discrepant data. For 233U and 240Pu, we recommend
continuing to use the evaluations in ENDL-2009.5. For 238Pu, ENDF/B-VIII.1 appears to be in significantly
better agreement with the experimental data, and we therefore recommend its adoption over the fission
cross section presently in ENDL-2009.5. For 242Pu(n,f), JENDL-5 is in better agreement with several recent
experiments that suggest lower cross sections than earlier measurements and we recommend its adoption.
In Table 9 we summarize our recommendations (highlighted in green) and we summarize our
recommendations and report the fraction of experimental data points that each evaluation reproduces within
the 1 𝜎 uncertainty band.
We generally recommend to use covariance data consistently with the corresponding evaluation, because
the covariance reflects the models and assumption that have gone into a specific evaluation. Some releases
of ENDL-2009.5 include low-fidelity covariance estimates. We do not recommend using the estimated
covariances from ENDL-2009.5 considering updated covariance data from more recent evaluations.
Specifically, the covariance data from JEFF-3.3 should be used for 233U(n,f), since the mean cross sections
are identical. Given the similar mean cross sections for 240Pu(n,f) in ENDF/B -VII.1 (not VIII.1) and ENDL-
2009.5, the covariance data from ENDF/B-VII.1 can be adopted for ENDL-2009.5.
