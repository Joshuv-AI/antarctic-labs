 Notes on Array Antenna Phase Center
 Armin W. Doerry

 Abstract
The location of an antenna, and indeed the radar system it serves, is typically presumed to be its
phase center. This is the point from which a far-field apparent spherical wavefront seems to be
centered. Even when a true phase center doesn’t exist, an ‘apparent’ phase center can still be
calculated for the neighborhood of some direction of interest, often the mainlobe of the beam.
Such calculations are especially necessary for non-symmetric arrays, or sub-arrays such as those
used for monopulse interferometric measurements.

1 Introduction and Background
A radar system makes relative range and Direction of Arrival (DOA) measurements. To infer
accurate and precise relative measurements, and therefrom ultimately calculate absolute
positions, the radar needs to identify its own location. An important question therefore is
“Where is the radar?” However, the radar system usually comprises several subsystems, and
occupies some volume. So, in terms of the radar’s location, we need to identify a fiducial point
for its location in space, i.e., the spatial domain. This is the point we need to track and to which
we need to reference any radar measurements.

The ‘location’ of the radar is typically defined by the location of its antenna, specifically the
“phase center” of the antenna. Of course, any point can be used, but the antenna’s phase center
is particularly simple by comparison to other locations, not requiring calculations using elaborate
lever arms, and therefore particularly useful. We like “simple.” For a radar’s antenna aperture
divided into parts, or subapertures, we then also require knowledge of the location of those
subapertures.

The IEEE Standard Definitions of Terms for Antennas defines an antenna’s Phase Center as1

 “The location of a point associated with an antenna such that, if it is taken as the center of
 a sphere whose radius extends into the far-field, the phase of a given field component
 over the surface of the radiation sphere is essentially constant, at least over that portion of
 the surface where the radiation is significant.

 NOTE—Some antennas do not have a unique phase center.”

Basically, a constant range from the phase center yields a constant-phase echo signal between
radar and target. This also means that a range-rate of “zero” with respect to angle off boresight
direction implies no phase change with that angle. On the other hand, a linear phase change with
time due in fact to a target traversing across the antenna beam is interpreted as a Doppler
frequency corresponding to a range-rate, or Line-of-Sight (LOS) velocity. We wish to avoid
such mischaracterization due to coupling of angle with Doppler. So, identifying the phase center
of the antenna aperture is important for accurate target mensuration.

Identifying the phase centers of antenna subapertures may be even more important to making
accurate interferometric DOA measurements, as with phase monopulse antennas. Even for
otherwise symmetric overall apertures, such subapertures may be quite asymmetric, posing some
complication to their individual phase center identification.

We offer the following references as particularly useful background material

 A good background for Active Electronically Scanned Array (AESA) antennas can be
 found in a text by Brown,2 as well as a text by Bird.3

 A discussion of phase centers in the context of monopulse antennas is given in a text by
 Sherman and Barton.4

 -9-

 A popular and excellent text on the broader topic of antenna theory is by Balanis.5

This report does not profess to offer any innovation in calculating the phase center of array
antennas. Rather, we offer the consolidation of existing community knowledge and demonstrate
practical calculation of an array antenna’s phase center, ultimately for an arbitrary array
configuration. In addition, we offer some comments on other factors that would impact such a
calculation. Our intent is to be “useful” rather than “novel.”

Some Final Comments

While our analysis thus far has presumed that we wish to identify a phase center for the center of
the mainlobe peak, this is in fact a choice that we make, albeit a quite reasonable choice, at least
most of the time. Nevertheless, we may wish to identify a phase center for a portion of the
antenna pattern that is not at the mainlobe peak, or even in the mainlobe at all. We do this by
returning to Eq. (37) and choosing a  at which we desire a phase center. In the neighborhood of
this chosen  the phase function    will then be relatively flat, i.e., with nearly zero slope.

We now reiterate the significance of the antenna phase center. For a target at a particular DOA,
in a small neighborhood of the DOA, the radar antenna’s TX emission is indistinguishable as
having emanated from the actual aperture, or a point at the antenna’s phase center. Similarly, an
antenna’s RX signal from a target in the neighborhood of a particular DOA is indistinguishable
as having been received by the actual aperture, or a point at the antenna’s phase center. From a
system standpoint, the antenna can be replaced by an equivalent point emitter/receptor at the
phase center.

4.4 Some Comments
Here we address some issues ignored in, or deferred from, previous sections.

4.4.1 Array Element Beam Patterns

We have heretofore assumed that the arrays consisted of isotropic point sources/receptor
elements. The analysis dealt with the effects of the array itself, and not limitations of the real
array elements. This description of the impact of the array assuming ideal elements is often
called the “array factor.” However, while useful to simplify the array analysis, such idealized
point elemental components do not in fact exist. Realistic array elements tend to be not
isotropic, but rather exhibit directional performance, i.e., anisotropic behavior. That is, the actual
array elements have their own element beam patterns.

Consequently, the overall antenna beam pattern more accurately would include the elemental
beam pattern characteristics, which are also themselves angle dependent. That is, Eq. (101)
might more accurately be written as

 I
        g  n DOA    ai e j i hel ,i  n DOA  si  n DOA  ,                                  (127)
 i 1

where an individual element’s own beam pattern is described by

 hel ,i  n DOA  = the ith element’s own response in the direction of n DOA . (128)

In general, this might be complex-valued, impacting both gain and phase.

The usual assumption is that hel ,i  n DOA  is identical for all elements. If so, while impacting the
overall beamforming and resulting net pattern g  n DOA  , an examination and study of Eq. (116)
shows it nevertheless does not affect the location of the phase center. We like this.

However, if hel ,i  n DOA  is not identical for all elements, then this will impact the location of the
phase center, altering its location as a function of DOA direction n DOA . This makes calculating
the phase center a little harder, but not impossible. We just have to modify Eq. (116)
accordingly to overtly include the element pattern effects, or otherwise accommodate or
compensate them.

Another issue is defining the location of the real array element. The real array element will itself
occupy some volume, for which we need to select a representative point location for that element
in order to do our array analysis. The logical location for the element is the phase center of the
actual real array element. However, we need to remain cognizant that the phase center of the
individual real element might itself depend on other factors, especially frequency, or perhaps
even DOA direction n DOA . Different topologies for real array elements can be expected to
behave differently in these regards.

4.4.2 Polarization

We have, for our convenience, ignored signal polarization effects up to now. We acknowledge
that this is also rather simplistic, but was still useful for tractable analysis.

A polarization mismatch between received signal and the sensitivity of an array element results
in a suboptimal coupling of the received energy, in turn resulting in an attenuation of that signal.
We will model this as an additional weighting of the signal, and allow it to be DOA dependent.
As such, Eq. (127) might become

 I
 g  n DOA    ai e j i hel ,i  n DOA  h pol ,i  n DOA  si  n DOA  , (129)
 i 1

where an individual element’s own polarization mismatch loss is described by

 hpol ,i  n DOA  = the ith element’s own polarization response in the direction of n DOA .
 (130)

As with the element’s beam pattern, in general, this might be complex-valued, impacting both
gain and phase.

Also as with the elemental beam pattern, the usual assumption is that hpol ,i  n DOA  is identical
for all elements. This means that it provides a common loss and/or phase shift, thereby not
affecting the location of the phase center. We like this, too.

However, if hpol ,i  n DOA  is not identical for all elements, then as with elemental beam patterns,
this will also impact the location of the array phase center, altering its location perhaps as a
function of DOA direction n DOA .

A particularly profound effect is with monopulse systems, where subapertures themselves might
have different polarization sensitivities. This can substantially and adversely impact DOA
estimation.9 While generally not a problem for planar arrays using identical elements, with
identical orientation, we offer that for nonplanar arrays, such as for conformal arrays, this could
indeed be particularly problematic, even with otherwise identical elements. More on this later.

4.4.3 Mutual Coupling Between Array Elements

Mutual coupling between array elements is essentially an acknowledgement of, and a measure
of, how the signal at one array element affects the signal at other array elements. This is real,
and is often accounted for in the calibration of an array.

An adequate treatment of this topic is beyond the scope of this report.

 - 46 -

4.4.4 Multipath Effects

We list multipath effects simply as a reminder that the antenna for a system is not just the
collection of elements, but in fact includes anything in the environment that might affect wave
propagation, including any radome, mounting structures, ice buildup, etc. This includes anything
that might reflect, diffract, or refract the mainbeam, subapertures, or even sidelobes.

This might be treated best with extensive modelling and/or in-situ testing and measurement. An
analytical treatment is beyond the scope of this report.

4.4.5 Frequency Effects

Although previously mentioned, we repeat here the assertion that operating frequency will
impact the array phase center principally by impacting the array elements. This might happen by
any of the following mechanisms.
 1. The element pattern might change,
 2. The element gain/phase might change for any particular DOA, and
 3. The elements’ own phase center location might change.

4.4.6 Measuring the Phase Center

Many articles about calculating the location of an antenna’s phase center from measurements are
available in the published literature. We reference here only a small representative
sampling.10,11,12,13,14,15,16,17 It would be excessively daunting to provide a comprehensive list.

We offer that while theoretical calculations including simulations are incredibly valuable for
system level design, validation and verification testing remain essential as due diligence for
operational characterization.

5.3 Some Comments
Here we expand on some issues discussed in Section 4.4 with some comments unique to 3-D
arrays.

5.3.1 Array Element Beam Patterns

We here build on the comments of Section 4.4.1.

For a true 3-D array (i.e., one that is neither planar nor linear) and especially for a conformal
array exhibiting curvature, we can almost expect that the orientation of the various array
elements will be different, that is, hel ,i  n DOA  is not identical for all elements. Typically, such
elements are oriented radially from their location on the surface. This complicates the
calculation for array phase center. In fact, for some DOA angles, we might even anticipate that
some elements might be occluded, perhaps partially, or perhaps even entirely.

5.3.2 Polarization

We build here on the comments of Section 4.4.2.

As discussed regarding array element beam patterns, when the array elements are oriented
differently, as we can almost expect them to be for a conformal aperture exhibiting curvature,
then this will also impact their effective polarization as a function of DOA angle. This means
 hpol ,i  n DOA  is not identical for all elements. This can clearly affect the location of the phase
center.

However, there is another insidious impact of array elements on a curved surface. Not only will
the elements exhibit different polarization orientations, but so too will subarrays in different
regions of the overall array.

Figure 11 illustrates the problem, exemplified with an array conformal to a horizontal cylinder,
and divided into four subapertures equal in area and curvature. The short red line segments
represent the linear polarization of elements at the center of their respective subapertures, with
each element’s polarization in the yz-plane, and normal to the array surface.

When projected towards DOA angles other than broadside, we observe that the polarizations do
not all project to parallel with each other. The upper and lower quadrants project with different
orientations. The elements have different polarization sensitivities. This implies that a difference
signal between upper and lower subapertures will not necessarily cancel for some RX signal
polarizations.

Consequently, modalities like monopulse DOA measurements that rely on difference
measurements will be adversely affected, perhaps substantially degrading pattern nulls when
cross-polarization signals are encountered. One mitigation strategy might be to employ fully-
polarimetric RX array elements to support a compensation, or other mitigation scheme.

6 Comments and Conclusions
We repeat several specific observations.

  The “location” of an antenna is typically assumed to be its phase center.

  Except for very limited antenna topologies/geometries, a unique phase center does not
 exist. However, for the neighborhood of a particular direction, e.g., the array’s
 mainbeam, an “apparent” phase center can be calculated that serves the purpose.
 Colloquially, the apparent phase center is often just referred to as simply the phase center.

  If the antenna elements are combined to maximize a mainbeam response, then in the
 neighborhood of the mainbeam direction the phase center can be calculated as a weighted
 average of the element locations, with the weighting being the elemental amplitude
 weighting. This works even in 3-D.

  While array symmetry facilitates overall array phase center identification, even
 symmetric arrays may embody asymmetric subapertures/subarrays. The subarrays’ phase
 center locations will influence DOA angle calculations, as with phase monopulse
 systems.

  Elemental patterns will influence the phase center location, especially if the elemental
 patterns are not identical to each other, or oriented identically. This includes the effects
 of elemental beam pattern roll-off as well as polarization. This becomes especially
 problematic for 3-D conformal arrays.

  Other factors will also influence phase center locations, such as mutual coupling,
 multipath, frequency effects, etc.

  For continuous aperture illumination, the summation becomes an integral.
