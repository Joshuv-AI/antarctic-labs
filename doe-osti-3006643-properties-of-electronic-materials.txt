Executive Summary
This ﬁnal technical report summarizes the research conducted under DOE Grant DE-SC0002623,
"Properties of Electronic Materials," led by Principal Investigator Shengbai Zhang at Rensselaer
Polytechnic Institute. Over the 16-year period, the project employed ﬁrst-principles computational
methods to investigate the structural, electronic, and dynamic properties of a wide range of
electronic materials, with applications in energy technologies, optoelectronics, and data storage.
Key areas included topological insulators, phase-change materials, graphene and two-dimensional
systems, perovskites for photovoltaics, defect engineering in semiconductors, kagome lattices,
and ultrafast carrier dynamics. The research resulted in 115 peer-reviewed publications, advancing
fundamental understanding of material behaviors at the atomic scale and contributing to
innovations in renewable energy, memory devices, and quantum materials. Findings have
implications for improving energy e iciency, developing lead-free solar cells, and enabling high-
speed data processing. The work has trained numerous graduate students and postdocs, fostering
the next generation of computational materials scientists. The original goals were to develop
theoretical models and computational tools to predict and optimize electronic properties of
materials for energy applications. All objectives were accomplished, with no major departures from
planned methodologies. Challenges in computational scaling were addressed through access to
high-performance computing resources.

Introduction
The project "Properties of Electronic Materials" was funded by the U.S. Department of Energy,
O ice of Science, Basic Energy Sciences, to advance the fundamental understanding of electronic
materials through ﬁrst-principles calculations. The research focused on using density functional
theory (DFT), time-dependent DFT, and GW methods to model atomic-scale phenomena in
semiconductors, insulators, and low-dimensional systems. Original hypotheses centered on the
role of defects, electronic excitations, and topological e ects in determining material performance
for applications such as photovoltaics, phase-change memory, and quantum computing.
Approaches included ab initio simulations on supercomputing platforms, with collaborations
involving experimental validation. No signiﬁcant problems were encountered that altered the
methodology, though computational demands led to optimizations in code e iciency. This work
builds on prior BES e orts in computational materials science, addressing grand challenges in
energy security and sustainability.

Technical Accomplishments
The project achieved signiﬁcant advances across multiple material classes. Results are organized
by thematic areas, with references to key publications.

3.1 Topological Insulators and Related Materials
Research in topological insulators (TIs) focused on defect engineering, doping, and interface
e ects to tune electronic structures for spintronic applications. In [5], native defects in Bi₂Se₃ were
analyzed, showing spin-orbit interaction's role in stabilizing surface states. Fermi-level tuning in

 4

Sb₂Te₃ on graphene was demonstrated in [1], enabling defect-regulated doping. Thin ﬁlms of Bi₂Te₃
with controlled electronic structure were explored in [4], revealing topological protection against
backscattering.Further studies on magnetic dopants, such as Fe on Bi₂Te₃ [38] and Cr/V in Sb₂Te₃
[39], identiﬁed electronic ﬁngerprints for ferromagnetic ordering. Intercalation in graphene for TIs
was investigated in [41] and [42], predicting Dirac half-metal states and quantum anomalous Hall
e ects. High-temperature QAHE in codoped TIs was proposed in [28]. Experimental-theory
collaborations mapped 3D surface potentials in Bi₂Se₃ [48] and observed Coulomb repulsion in Cu-
intercalated Bi₂Se₃ [62]. Growth and orientation of TI nanoplates were studied in [88], while vertical
twinning at TI-semiconductor interfaces was reported in [89]. Atomic identiﬁcation of
nanostructures in TIs was achieved in [91], and atomic-scale magnetism in Cr-doped Bi₂Se₃ in [94].
Non-vdW surfaces of Bi₂Te₃ were examined in [108], and the intrinsic limit of Bi₂Se₃ was
approached in [10]. These ﬁndings enhanced understanding of topological protection and potential
for room-temperature quantum devices.

3.2 Phase Change Materials and Memory
Phase-change materials (PCMs) for non-volatile memory were a core focus, emphasizing
amorphization, crystallization, and stability. The role of electronic excitation in Ge-Sb-Te
amorphization was elucidated in [3], while a new structural picture of Ge₂Sb₂Te₅ was presented in
[7]. Directional forces in Peierls-distorted GeTe were explored in [15], revealing order-to-order
transitions.Electron-polarized chains in amorphous GeSbTe were identiﬁed in [29], and optical
subpicosecond switching in ferroelectrics in [30]. Thermal stability of Ge₁Cu₂Te₃ was attributed to
Cu-bonding reconﬁguration in [40]. Monolayer Sb₂Te₃ for ultimate PCM scaling was proposed in
[43], and TDDFT-MD studies of Sc-Sb-Te amorphization in [46].Nonthermal characteristics in
ultrafast laser melting were evidenced in [47], and nano-amorphous interfaces' role in Sb₂Te₃
crystallization in [68]. Metal-insulator transitions in Ge-Sb-Te superlattices were modeled in [73].
Vacancy structures in GeSbTe were analyzed in [78], and structural relaxation suppression in
nanoscale Sb for low-drift PCM in [85].Photoinduced vacancy ordering in MoTe₂ was studied in [50],
and hole-lattice coupling in VO₂ in [52]. These results advanced PCM design for high-speed, low-
power data storage.

3.3 Graphene and Two-Dimensional Materials
Graphene-based systems were investigated for electronic and catalytic applications. Stability of
graphene oxide phases was determined in [6], and graphene-promoted metal-catalyzed reactions
in [9]. Dirac fermions in bound graphene systems were predicted in [21], and 3D Weyl-semimetals
in graphene networks in [24].Coexistence of ﬂat and Dirac bands in carbon-kagome families was
found in [33], and semi-Dirac semimetals in silicene oxide in [37]. Regioselectivity in graphene
functionalization by ripples was controlled in [57], and open-shell singlet character in acene
derivatives in [63].Spin-triplet excitonic insulators in semihydrogenated graphene were proposed in
[64]. Spontaneous graphene formation on diamond via B-doping was shown in [90]. Opening large
band gaps in graphene by covalent addition was achieved in [110].Other 2D materials included Hf
honeycomb on Ir(111) [27], MgB₆ crystal [66], boron honeycomb with Mo [75], monolayer II-VI
semiconductors [61], and few-layer phosphorenes [83]. Electron and phonon properties of carbon
honeycombs were studied in [54].These contributions highlighted graphene's versatility for
electronics and catalysis.

3.4 Photovoltaics and Solar Materials
E orts in photovoltaics targeted e icient, earth-abundant materials. Chalcogenide perovskites for
PV were discovered in [12], and lead-free perovskites via split-anion approach in [17].
Recombination centers in CH₃NH₃PbI₃ were identiﬁed in [36].Optimizing TiO₂
photoelectrochemical properties by codoping was explored in [11], and nucleus-coupled electron
transfer in TiO₂ water splitting in [44]. Absolute redox potential of water was calculated in [81].P-
type conductivity in Cu₂ZnSnS₄ was attributed to deep traps in [13]. Anisotropic polaron localization
in GaN and ZnO was compared in [95]. Molecular doping of ZnO by ammonia as a shallow acceptor
was proposed in [104].Ionic liquid's role in Al-graphite cells was studied in [51]. These advances
support sustainable solar energy technologies.

3.5 Defects and Doping in Semiconductors
Defect physics was central, with formulations for charged defects in 2D materials [25] and revisited
in [35]. Dynamic Jahn-Teller in NV-center diamond was analyzed in [31], and oxygen-boron vacancy
in c-BN in [56].Quasiparticle band gap of ZnO was accurately computed in [2]. Deep traps in
Cu₂ZnSnS₄ [13], and compensation in Al-doped ZnO [65]. Formation and ionization energies in 2D
[25], and vacuum level in solids [67].Self-puriﬁcation in Si nanocrystals [97], limits on passivating Si
dislocations [102], impurity doping in SiO₂ [114]. Di iculty predicting shallow defects with hybrids
[101], correct Fermi energy for defect energies [106].Phonon-enabled transport at surfaces [107],
excitation to defect-bound states [98], charged defects in 2D [99]. Band alignment and built-in
potential [16]. These studies improved defect control for device performance.

3.6 Kagome Lattices and Exotic Electronic Structures
Kagome structures were explored for frustration and ﬂatbands. Carbon kagome lattice for metal-
insulator transition [20], ﬂatbands in Fe₃Sn₂ [22], ferromagnetism in kagome graphene
[69].Nanostructured carbon with Weyl-loops [14], triply degenerate points in NaCu₃Te₂ [19].
Coexistence of bands in carbon-kagome [33], semi-Dirac in silicene oxide [37]. MgB₆ [66].Half-
excitonic insulator [49]. These predictions open avenues for quantum materials.

3.7 Ultrafast Processes and Carrier Dynamics
Ultrafast phenomena included carrier-multiplication-induced changes [53], non-radiative
recombination in InGaN [55]. Collective motion in vdW heterostructures charge transfer [82],
ligand-assisted transfer in CdSe/MoS₂ [84], enhancement in CdS-MoS₂ [80].Entropy barrier in
ferroelectric phase change [96]. These insights aid optoelectronic device design.

Changes/Problems

No signiﬁcant deviations from the proposed research plan occurred. Methodologies remained
consistent with the original project scope. Computational scaling challenges were resolved using
high-performance computing resources, and no issues materially impacted deliverables.

Conclusions
The project successfully achieved its goals, providing deep insights into electronic materials'
properties through computational modeling. Key impacts include advancing TI-based spintronics,
optimizing PCM for memory, and identifying novel PV materials. The 115 publications demonstrate
high productivity and inﬂuence. Future work could extend to AI-accelerated materials discovery.
