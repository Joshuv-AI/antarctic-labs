Cystinuria: Diagnosis, Medical and Surgical Management, and Emerging Therapies. A Narrative Review
Authors: Alberto Zambudio Munuera, Patricia Rodriguez Parras, Juan Antonio Galan Llopis, Feliciano Grases Freixedas, Maria del Carmen Cano Garcia, Miguel Arrabal-Martin, Miguel Angel Arrabal Polo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCystinuria is a hereditary aminoaciduria causing recurrent cystine nephrolithiasis, impaired quality of life and potential renal function decline. Despite established treatment guidelines, real-world data reveal a persistent gap between recommended targets and clinical outcomes. Preliminary data from the EUROCYS 2024 prospective European registry (269 patients, 29 centers, 8 countries) showed that only 16.3% of patients achieved the target urinary pH range, 55.2% remained above the conventional urinary cystine threshold of 250 mg/L, and approximately 39% experienced a new stone event within the preceding 12 months, despite follow-up in specialist centers. Discontinuation rates for available thiol drugs have been reported to reach 30–50%. This narrative review, based on a structured non-systematic search of major databases and guideline repositories (2009–2026), synthesizes current evidence on cystinuria diagnosis and management and appraises emerging adjunctive interventions. Next-generation sequencing-based genetic testing and the cystine capacity assay represent key recent advances in monitoring. Thulium fiber laser lithotripsy and miniaturized percutaneous approaches offer less traumatic surgical options in this high-burden population. Oral N-acetylcysteine has mechanistic plausibility and a favorable, well-characterized safety profile in other clinical settings, making it a rational candidate for formal prospective evaluation; however, robust clinical trial evidence in cystinuria using contemporary monitoring parameters is lacking. Current standard treatment remains insufficient for a substantial proportion of patients, underscoring the need to evaluate accessible adjunctive therapies with defined mechanisms and measurable outcomes.

Introduction

Cystinuria (OMIM 220100) is the most common hereditary aminoaciduria with clinical relevance and the most frequent monogenic cause of nephrolithiasis in childhood, resulting from defective tubular reabsorption of cystine and dibasic amino acids in the proximal renal tubule.1–3 Global prevalence is approximately 1 in 7,000 neonates, accounting for 1–2% of urinary stones in adults and 6–8% in the pediatric population.3,4 Cystine’s critically limited solubility at physiological urinary pH leads to precipitation, forming pathognomonic hexagonal crystals that aggregate into recurrent stones with relative resistance to fragmentation.2,3 Males exhibit more severe phenotypes and higher stone burden than females.1,4

Clinical Burden

Without adequate prophylactic treatment, patients form approximately one new stone per year and undergo around seven urological procedures by middle age.1 Up to 70% may develop some degree of chronic kidney disease (CKD) over their lifetime;3,5 unilateral nephrectomy has been documented in up to 14% of patients compared with 2.9% in calcium oxalate stone formers.5 The EUROCYS 2024 prospective European registry showed that 18.9% of adults had CKD stage 3 or above at enrollment.6 Quality of life is markedly impaired, with cystinuria patients scoring significantly lower on health-related quality of life instruments than non-cystine stone formers.7,8

The Gap Between Guidelines and Real-World Practice

European guidelines recommend urinary pH of 7.5–8.0, urine volume above 3 L/day, and cystine concentration below the solubility threshold.9 The cystine capacity assay better reflects lithogenic risk in patients receiving thiol drugs than conventional total cystine measurement, though availability remains limited.10,11 EUROCYS 2024 preliminary data (Table 1) demonstrate a critical gap: only 16.3% of patients achieved target pH, 55.2% remained above 250 mg/L, and approximately 39% experienced a new stone event within 12 months despite specialist follow-up;6 as these findings derive from a conference abstract, they should be interpreted cautiously pending full peer-reviewed publication. Objectives

Given the rare disease context and heterogeneity of available evidence, a narrative review with structured non-systematic search was chosen. This review synthesizes evidence published between 2009 and 2026 on cystinuria diagnosis and management, and appraises the evidence for emerging adjunctive interventions.

Methods

A narrative review with a structured, non-systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Library and major guideline repositories (EAU, European Rare Kidney Disease Reference Network, European Reference Network for Rare Urological Disease and AUA) for publications from January 2009 to April 2026. This search identified 131 potentially relevant sources, of which 80 were peer-reviewed articles or registered clinical trials; 36 full-text articles were ultimately selected for inclusion based on relevance, recency and methodological quality. Seminal pre-2009 references were included when relevant as foundational milestones 13,14. Search terms covered cystinuria, cystine nephrolithiasis, cystine transporter genes, pharmacological agents, surgical techniques and emerging therapies. Elicit (elicit. org) was used only as an auxiliary identification tool; all references were verified and retrieved from conventional databases before inclusion. Study selection was not performed in duplicate and no PRISMA flow diagram was generated. References were selected based on predefined thematic relevance, clinical applicability, methodological quality and recency. Key evidence sources are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Diagnosis

Genetic Diagnosis

Cystinuria results from biallelic pathogenic variants in SLC3A1, encoding the heavy subunit rBAT, or SLC7A9, encoding the light subunit b0,+AT of the heterodimeric b0,+AT-rBAT transporter.1,15 Inheritance is classically autosomal recessive, although incomplete penetrance and more complex genetic scenarios are recognized, particularly among SLC7A9 heterozygotes.9,15 Genotype–phenotype correlation is generally weak, with marked phenotypic variability even among individuals sharing the same genotype;15 approximately 10% of cases remain genetically unresolved after standard analysis.1

European guidelines recommend considering genetic testing for uncertain diagnoses, reproductive counselling and family screening.9 Available techniques include targeted Sanger sequencing, hereditary nephrolithiasis next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels — currently the standard in reference centers — exome or genome sequencing for unresolved cases, and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) for copy-number variant detection.1,9,15 Genetic findings must always be interpreted alongside clinical and biochemical data.1,9

Biochemical and Laboratory Diagnosis

Flat, colorless hexagonal crystals in fresh urinary sediment are highly suggestive, and virtually pathognomonic when present. The Brand sodium nitroprusside colorimetric test is obsolete for monitoring in patients receiving thiol therapy because of its lack of specificity.10,13 Ion-exchange chromatography or HPLC-MS/MS enable precise urinary cystine quantification; however, in patients receiving thiol drugs, total cystine measurement does not distinguish free lithogenic cystine from pharmacologically generated soluble mixed disulfides.10,11,16,17

The cystine capacity assay is one of the most relevant recent advances in monitoring, as it assesses the urine sample’s functional capacity to dissolve additional cystine and may better reflect solubilization status in the presence of thiols than conventional quantification.10,11 Its clinical adoption remains limited to specialized reference laboratories, and cost and availability currently restrict routine use; it is best regarded as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, conventional cystine quantification until broader validation and accessibility are achieved. Stone composition analysis by infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) or X-ray diffraction confirms the diagnosis.9

Imaging

Non-contrast computed tomography (CT) offers high sensitivity for cystine stone detection. Cystine stones typically show moderate attenuation, usually around 400–700 Hounsfield units, substantially lower than calcium oxalate stones; this may guide compositional suspicion before stone analysis.2,9 Renal ultrasound is preferred for pediatric follow-up and when minimizing cumulative radiation exposure is a priority.2,4

The fundamental therapeutic goal is to maintain urinary cystine below the solubility threshold through hyperhydration, urinary alkalinization and dietary modification.9,12 Urine dilution is the safest and most cost-effective first-line measure, targeting urine volume >3 L/day in adults and 40–50 mL/kg/day in children, distributed throughout the day, including nocturnal intake, to prevent overnight urinary concentration.9 EUROCYS 2024 preliminary data show that only 34.9% of adults in specialist follow-up achieve this target.6

Cystine solubility is strongly pH-dependent, increasing approximately twofold from around 250 mg/L at pH 7.0 to 500 mg/L at pH 7.5 [3,4]. Potassium citrate is the alkalinizing agent of choice (60–80 mEq/day in 3–4 divided doses), usually targeting urinary pH 7.0–7.5, or 7.5–8.0 in more intensive protocols; sustained pH >8.0 should be avoided because of calcium phosphate stone risk.9 Moderate sodium restriction below 100 mmol/day is recommended, as higher intake increases urinary cystine excretion.9,18 Restriction of animal protein, as a methionine source and cystine precursor, may reduce urinary cystine excretion, although long-term adherence is limited.19

Thiol-Based Pharmacotherapy (Second Line)

When conservative measures are insufficient, cystine-binding thiol drug (CBTD) is added. Their mechanism relies on thiol–disulfide exchange: the free sulfhydryl group of the drug cleaves the disulfide bond of cystine, generating mixed disulfides that are 20–50 times more soluble than free cystine.9,12 CBTD efficacy increases substantially when urinary pH is maintained above 7.5; alkalinization and thiol therapy should therefore be used as a combined regimen.12 A detailed comparison of available thiol drugs is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

D-penicillamine20 carries the highest toxicity burden among available thiols, with adverse events including hypersensitivity reactions, membranous glomerulonephritis, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia and vitamin B6 antagonism requiring pyridoxine supplementation.9,12 It is reserved for settings where tiopronin is unavailable or not tolerated.9

Tiopronin is the reference second-line pharmacotherapy, with superior tolerability compared with D-penicillamine.9,12 Standard dosing is 800–2,000 mg/day in three divided doses and should be titrated according to biochemical response, ideally using cystine capacity when available. A small crossover study (n=10) found that 1 g/day significantly improved cystine capacity without consistent additional benefit at higher doses.21 Recent FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) pharmacovigilance data (Q1 2014–Q1 2025) identified 1,838 tiopronin-associated cases with novel unlabelled safety signals including skin atrophy and tongue discoloration, underscoring the need for long-term monitoring.22 Long-term abandonment rates are estimated at 30–50%.12 Captopril has a free thiol group but is not recommended because of inconsistent clinical evidence and hypotension risk in patients with impaired renal function.7,9

Emerging and Investigational Therapies

Oral NAC has been proposed as a potential adjunctive therapy based on thiol-disulfide exchange and experimental evidence demonstrating interference with cystine crystallization; however, its clinical relevance after oral administration remains unproven.13,14,23 In a turbidimetric crystallization assay under conditions designed to reproduce physiological urine residence times, Grases et al23 demonstrated that NAC was the most potent inhibitor among the three thiol agents studied: its effect on crystallization induction time was already detectable at 0.14 mM, a lower threshold than penicillamine (0.28 mM) or tiopronin (1.12 mM), and it was the only compound that modified cystine crystal morphology at low concentration (0.28 mM).23 The authors noted the need for a specific study of the relationship between low-dose oral NAC and urinary cystine excretion in cystinuric patients before clinical recommendations can be drawn. Historical reports from the 1970s document its use in cystinuric patients, including as an adjunct to percutaneous chemolysis.14 It has been consistently included in systematic reviews of pharmacological metaphylaxis in cystinuria as a mechanistically plausible agent without modern clinical evidence.13 No modern randomized controlled trials have evaluated oral NAC using current functional monitoring parameters such as the cystine capacity assay; this constitutes a documented research gap.

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors represent an attractive pathophysiological hypothesis but clinical evidence in cystinuria is preliminary24,25 and limited to very small observational series; their use is currently off-label and cannot be recommended outside a formal research context.12 Tolvaptan demonstrated improved cystine capacity in the TCUPS pilot study, though high cost and hepatic monitoring requirements restrict its use to highly selected patients.12,26 Bucillamine, a dithiol agent under phase II evaluation, may offer greater potency than tiopronin with less frequent dosing.12 Alpha-lipoic acid has shown exploratory promise but requires confirmation in robustly designed trials.3,12,26 Gene therapy via adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors represents a preclinical research line with promising murine results; clinical translation has no defined timeline.27 Overall, the evidence supporting these emerging therapies remains limited by small sample sizes, absence of randomized controlled trials, short follow-up and, in some cases, off-label use without regulatory approval for cystinuria; these constraints preclude firm treatment recommendations at present.

Surgical Treatment

Shock Wave Lithotripsy

Cystine stones show relative resistance to shock wave lithotripsy (SWL) and challenging fragmentation behavior compared with other compositions.2,9 SWL yields inferior and less predictable results, limiting its role to small stones when ureteroscopy is not feasible; the need for repeated sessions increases the risk of cumulative parenchymal damage in a population already subject to high procedural burden.2,9

Ureteroscopy

Rigid ureteroscopy for ureteral stones and flexible ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy (holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet [Ho:YAG] laser) for proximal ureteral and renal stones up to 20 mm are first-choice techniques in adults and children.2,9,28 In EUROCYS 2024, ureteroscopy accounted for 61.4% of registered interventions.6 Reported safety profiles are acceptable, although stone-free rates vary according to stone size and location.28 In adults, ureteroscopy is preferred over percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) when stone burden allows, to preserve future access options and minimize cumulative parenchymal trauma.9

Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy

PCNL is the reference technique for renal cystine stones exceeding 20 mm, staghorn calculi and stones inaccessible by ureteroscopy.2,9 Miniaturized approaches, including mini-PCNL and ultra-mini-PCNL, may offer satisfactory stone-free rates with lower morbidity in selected cases, although outcomes depend on stone size and location.9

Surgical Advances

Recent thulium laser advances encompass both thulium fiber lasers (TFL, ~1,940 nm) for efficient stone dusting, and pulsed Thulium:YAG systems with peak powers up to 3.7 kW enabling high-energy fragmentation of dense calculi including cystine.29–31 Cystine-specific evidence remains limited. Careful control of energy settings and irrigation flow is required to minimize thermal injury risk.29 Retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) with high-resolution digital visualization is an option for patients with high comorbidity or complex renal anatomy.32 Sustained adherence to medical treatment between interventions is associated with improved outcomes and reduced recurrence risk.8,33

Unmet Needs and Research Priorities

Despite recent advances, critical gaps persist. Achieving recommended urinary pH, volume and cystine targets remains difficult in real-world practice, underscoring the need for patient-centered adherence strategies and more accessible monitoring tools.7 Poor tolerability and high discontinuation rates for thiol drugs highlight the need for agents with improved safety profiles.12,22 Wider implementation of the cystine capacity assay is a priority.10,11 The absence of modern randomized controlled trials evaluating oral NAC using contemporary monitoring parameters remains a clear and addressable research gap.13,14 Pediatric-to-adult transition represents a vulnerable period requiring dedicated clinical pathways, compounded by cumulative radiation from repeated imaging and recurrent general anesthesia exposure across multiple procedures. Emerging therapies require adequately powered prospective evaluation before clinical recommendations can be made.12

Limitations of This Review

This review has limitations inherent to its narrative design: study selection was not performed in duplicate, no PRISMA flow diagram was generated, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment was undertaken. The evidence base is largely observational, with several emerging therapies supported mainly by preclinical or preliminary data. EUROCYS findings are currently available only as preliminary conference abstract-level data and should be interpreted accordingly. The search focused primarily on English-language literature; relevant publications in other languages may have been missed.

Conclusions

Cystinuria remains one of the most challenging hereditary nephropathies to manage. Preliminary EUROCYS 2024 data indicate that many patients remain insufficiently controlled despite specialist follow-up, reflecting persistent limitations of current therapy, including thiol discontinuation, imperfect monitoring tools and the absence of modern randomized evidence.

NGS-based molecular diagnosis and the cystine capacity assay represent meaningful progress, while thulium fiber laser lithotripsy and miniaturized percutaneous techniques may reduce surgical morbidity in a population exposed to repeated procedures. However, no surgical advance substitutes for effective medical prophylaxis.

Oral NAC cannot currently be presented as an established therapeutic alternative in cystinuria. It should instead be regarded as a mechanistically plausible, affordable and accessible intervention with a well-characterized safety profile that warrants formal prospective evaluation using contemporary monitoring parameters. More broadly, oral NAC and the other emerging therapies discussed in this review, SGLT2 inhibitors, tolvaptan, bucillamine and alpha-lipoic acid share the same evidentiary gap and require validation through well-designed, adequately powered prospective clinical trials before they can inform routine clinical practice.
