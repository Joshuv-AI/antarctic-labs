 A. Introduction
 a. Major Project Goals

 Holocene Climate Corporation (Holocene) partnered with Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL)
to conduct bench-scale testing of a new optimized Direct Air Capture (DAC) process to assist the U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) in achieving the Carbon Negative Shot target of under $100/net tonne CO₂
removed by 2031. Holocene’s DAC process for carbon dioxide (CO₂) removal uses amino acids and
guanidine compounds, an ORNL-invented chemical process and licensed by Holocene. Holocene will use
this novel chemistry to develop a new process and deploy the technology commercially.
 The proposed project explores several variants of the DAC process designed to reduce technical
risks and costs through bench-scale testing of new processes and components for the solvent-sorbent DAC
technology. This project will focus on: (i) developing advanced, novel regeneration processes to reduce
thermal energy requirements and lower desorption temperature; and (ii) designing the air contactor to
reduce pressure drop and optimize CO₂ absorption. The first goal aims to reduce the thermal energy needed
for overcoming the reaction enthalpy of H2O and simultaneously enable a higher cyclic CO₂ capacity; the

 2

second goal aims to maximize the CO₂ capture volumetric productivity to reduce land requirements and
environmental footprint for Holocene’s “earth-changing” DAC system.

 B. Accomplishments under Stated Goals

Task 1

Project management efforts were exerted throughout the period of the project to ensure efficiency and
timely reporting – including but not limited to all activities outlined in the SOPO under Task 1.
Additionally, we set up internal processes and meetings to run the project, as well as a budgeting and
accounting system to maintain financial compliance. We’ve worked with Oak Ridge National Laboratory
to set up a contract, as well as to split the tasks according to our project proposal and allocated respective
budgets.

Task 2

The goals are to (1) understand the various ways in which chemicals used in the DAC process are lost, and
(2) to devise ways to prevent these losses. To achieve these goals, thorough review of the chemical literature
was conducted followed by the development of methods to measure decomposition of both the
bis(iminoguanidine) and amino acids in our system that may occur via the mechanisms found in the
literature. To better quantify chemical degradation, we developed experimental test bench systems to
subject the chemicals to conditions that simulate a significant amount of cycles in a single experiment,
enabling us to more accurately measure the cumulative degradation that would otherwise be obscured by

 4

the more significant transfer losses within a single cycle. Several experimental setups were used to study
potential losses.

Losses During Absorption: To study the amino acid losses during absorption, we utilized the existing
ORNL wind The long-term sorbent loss experiment was conducted for a significant amount of hours using
a representative amino acid solution. The experiment was conducted at room temperature, with a set air
velocity through the contactor. The inlet CO2 concentration was approximately 400 ppm throughout the
experiment. Throughout the experiment, samples of the aqueous solution were taken approximately every
8 hours. This was done by stopping the circulation in the wind tunnel and allowing the solution to fully
drain to the solution tank

An initial analysis of the concentration of amino acid over time during the extended sorbent loss experiment
has been conducted. For analysis, the lower collection of datapoints in each series - representing samples
taken after the aqueous solution has been re-diluted to the initial volume - is the most meaningful, as
variable amounts of water evaporation take place during the experiment depending on the ambient
conditions. The analysis was outlined but not completed due to the termination of the project.

Losses During Crystallization: To study sorbent losses during crystallization, existing glassware,
including scintillation vials, round bottom flasks, and glass reactors will be used. For sorbent losses during
crystallization, our experimental plan defines one equivalent cycle a fixed reaction time in the crystallizer,
which has been set by our base case technoeconomic scenario. We routinely run crystallization experiments
overnight, which is equivalent to more than a hundred equivalent cycles. We planned to carry out
decomposition studies during Budget Period 2 with the existing experimental setup and analytical
instrumentation but did not complete due to the termination of the project.

Losses During Thermal Regeneration: To study sorbent losses during thermal regeneration, we have
constructed a custom thermal degradation reactor, and supplemented these studies with TGA experiments.
For sorbent losses during thermal regeneration, our experimental plan defines one equivalent cycle as fixed
reaction time in the thermal regeneration unit, which has been set by our base case technoeconomic
scenario.

We first generated the BIG-CO3 via a standard precipitation with a loaded aqueous amino acid salt solution.
We isolated the BIG-CO3 via filtration, taking care to leave the sample relatively wet with aqueous amino
acid salt solution. We took half of the wet solution, and washed with DI water to remove the residual
contactor solution. Using both the washed and unwashed sample, we heated small samples in vials held at
120C, with distinct samples held under either nitrogen or the air.

 5

When samples were washed, no decomposition was observed after heating under normal atmospheric
composition/air. Similarly, we observed no decomposition when unwashed BIG-CO3 - still containing
residual contactor solution - was heated under nitrogen atmosphere, indicating that the combination of BIG-
CO3 and contactor solution is heated under an inert atmosphere. However, when the unwashed BIG-CO3
sample was heated under air, decomposition products were detected via HPLC and discoloration of the
sample was observed.

To further supplement our investigation of decomposition during thermal regeneration, we also analyzed
BIG-CO3 by an isothermal TGA method at 120C. These stability results are in agreement with the high
stability of the pure BIG as observed in our thermal degradation reactor.

Task 3

In Holocene’s integrated DAC process, the crystallization step allows transferring the CO2 from the liquid
phase to a solid phase that can be separated out. Once separated, the liquid can be recycled back to the
absorption unit. The guanidine salt solid collected can then be processed for recovery of the CO2 gas and
further recycling of the regenerated guanidine free base solid in the crystallization. As a consequence, the
performance of the solid-liquid separation, further referred to as “separation”, directly impacts the
performance of the whole process.

The performance of the separation can be assessed in different ways, including by measuring the residual
moisture content in the solid phase. Lower residual moisture contents allow for recovering and recycling
more of the liquid phase, while reducing the need for post-processing of the wet solid. Lower residual
moisture contents result from a combination of factors of the separation technology itself, but also of the

 6

suspensions properties, defined by the crystallization operation taking place upstream. Typically, the solids
content (amount of solid per volume of liquid), and the particle shapes and sizes are the suspension
properties that have the largest impact on the separation.

In this project, a target residual moisture content has been defined as a success criterion for the separation.

In order to reach this target, research activities were divided into 2 subtasks:

 • Subtask 3.1. Validating Crystallization Process Parameters

 • Subtask 3.2 Conceptual Design of Crystallization Process

Task 3.1

In order to investigate crystallization process parameters at the “smallest meaningful scale”, a testbench
system was designed, built & commissioned; experimental campaigns with that testbench allowed for the
following crystallization properties to be recorded:

 • Thermodynamics data of the chemical system over a range of pH and temperatures relevant to the
 process design
 • Kinetics data of the chemical system collected on suspension generated in batch and continuous
 crystallization modes
 • Impact of mixing geometry on suspension homogeneity
 • PSSD of suspensions generated in batch crystallization mode with seeding and BIG FB solid feed
 rate control
 • Identifying the kinetic parameter of the crystal studied was idenitfied, and confirmed the chemical
 system yields smaller particles at shorter residence times in continuous crystallization
 • PSSD of suspensions generated in continuous crystallization mode
 • Identification of post-treatment of PSSD of continuous crystallization to reduce their aspect ratio

The activities carried in this subtask lead to significant learnings on the chemical system and its behavior
during crystallization and separation. It was identified that the PSD of the carbonate salt crystals come with
the drawback of high aspect ratio crystals. It was found that the moisture content in the solids after
separation is strongly dependent on the aspect ratio of the crystal.

 7

This morphology and its impact on filtration performance was mitigated by investigating a pathway to
reduce the aspect ratio by post treating the suspension from the continuous crystallization.

Task 3.2

To deliver a conceptual design of the crystallization process, we first did a thorough literature review of
various crystallization & separation technologies followed by discussions with industry experts and
technology providers. These findings were incorporated together with the information gathered in subtask
3.1 to develop conceptual design centered around a block flow diagram and a stream table. The study gave
an understanding of mass flow rates and stream composition across the proposed crystallization &
separation process. It highlights the importance of the liquid separation efficiency to minimize the water
content in the solid stream that is fed to the regeneration unit which adds a non-negligible thermal energy
penalty and is thus to be considered in the cost optimization (capital and operating cost trade-off) of the
integrated DAC technology.

Task 4

The overall goal of this task is to test contactors and operating conditions to optimize the CO2 absorption
using Holocene’s aqueous amino acid solution to establish relation between the absorption rate and different
operating levers such as air/solution flow rates, fan energy, pressure drop, and solution concentrations. It is
of utmost priority to quantify the size of contactor and fan energy (or pressure drop) with respect to the total
CO2 captured per unit time. Therefore, the success criteria were proposed in terms of capture efficiency,
absorption rate, and pressure drop.

Sensitivity analysis of fluid and contactor properties was performed to identify critical parameters that can
influence the CO2 absorption rate under different operating conditions using the reduced order model. The
study provided an understanding of a critical range of parameters such as packing volume, air/solution
flowrates on the impact of absorption performance.

Further, the contactor test was tested under different scenarios for identifying any operational failure.
Different retrofitting and design iterations were implemented to operate the test rig without any leakage and
operational failure. Experimental plans were proposed with the help of the reduced order model. We also
learned that the liquid distribution and residence time measurements are as crucial as the absorption test to
fully explore the mass-transfer characteristics in the contactor. The contactor was modified to assist the
liquid distribution and residence time measurements.

Absorption experiments were conducted to investigate the influence of air flow rate, liquid flow rate,

 8

solution concentration, and packing volume. We observed a significant increase in CO2 absorption with an
increase in air flow rate whereas the liquid flow rate had marginal impact on the absorption. Higher molar
concentration of solution can have higher capacity of CO2 absorption but the concentration can also change
the fluid properties and negatively affect the mass-transfer coefficient and reduce CO2 absorption.

In the modeling space, a method was developed for extracting overall mass-transfer coefficient from
experimental data and validation of mass-transfer models. It was assumed that the mass transfer rate is
proportional to the log mean CO2 pressure difference (LMPD) of the air side for cross-flow conditions.
Also, aqueous CO2 concentration in solution was assumed negligible due to high rate of reaction in solution
phase. LMPD was modified for the negligible aqueous CO2 concentration in the solution. Two-film mass-
transfer models (Onda and BRF) were finalized based on the number of parameters for estimating the
CO2 absorption rate. The BRF model needed less model tuning in comparison with the Onda model for the
validation and the BRF model was in excellent agreement with the measured data.

Task 5.1

In subtask 5.1, a vacuum-assisted desorption testing apparatus was designed and experiments were
conducted with the goal of reducing the BIG desorption temperature below 95°C and thus reduce sensible
heat requirements of the process. It is hypothesized that performing the reaction under a vacuum will reduce
the temperature necessary to perform the desorption step. A combination of reducing the boiling point of
the contained water (approximately 60°C at 0.2 atm(a)) and decreasing partial pressures of CO2 and water
around the sorbent was thought to allow for the desorption process to proceed at significantly lower
temperatures.

To run these experiments, a small test bench was designed and assembled which could operate at precisely
controlled temperatures under vacuum conditions. With this apparatus, temperatures and pressures were
varied to determine if the introduction of a vacuum (i) reduces temperature requirement to regenerate the
BIG carbonate, and (ii) significantly improves the regeneration rate.

Two different BIG compounds were tested with this apparatus. For one, near-complete CO2 desorption
was achieved at temperatures as low as 90 °C exceeding the success criteria of reducing the regeneration
temperature to below 95°C. However, the results also showed that vacuum has almost no impact on the
regeneration process – meaning no impact on required regeneration temperature.

Overall, the findings highlight the distinct desorption characteristics of different BIGs, emphasizing the
importance of tailored conditions to optimize the regeneration performance of different guanidines. All
