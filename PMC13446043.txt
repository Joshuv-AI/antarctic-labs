An effective method for isolation and regeneration of Solanum tuberosum mesophyll protoplasts for transgene-free genome editing
Authors: Nehanjali Parmar, Shruti Pathania, Vanita Patial, Neha Kaushal, Aditi, Jyoti Verma, Rasna Devi, Dharmendra Kumar, Hemant B. Kardile, Brajesh Singh, Som Dutt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An effective system for isolating and regenerating protoplasts is crucial for research in genome engineering. This study focused on refining a protocol for the isolation and regeneration of mesophyll protoplasts from the leaves of Solanum tuberosum cv. Kufri Jyoti. Key factors influencing protoplast yield and viability, such as dark pretreatment, pre-plasmolysis, enzyme concentrations, and osmoticum levels, were thoroughly assessed and optimized. The highest protoplast yield and viability were achieved with an enzyme mixture of 1.0% cellulase R-10 and 0.5% macerozyme R-10 after 16 h of incubation. Furthermore, culturing on a Murashige and Skoog-based medium (MSPI) without ammonium nitrate, enriched with an osmoticum concentration of 0.4 M and a carefully adjusted auxin-to-cytokinin ratio, successfully facilitated protoplast division, microcalli proliferation, and minicalli formation. Callus proliferation and shoot induction were accomplished on MS13K medium supplemented with naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) and zeatin riboside. Root initiation and elongation were promoted on MS basal medium supplemented with indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) at 1 mg/L. The regenerated plantlets were subsequently acclimatized and hardened under controlled greenhouse conditions. This robust protoplast-to-plant protocol serves as a crucial resource for the introduction of ribonucleoprotein complexes into plant cells, facilitating accurate, transgene-free genome editing.

Introduction

Potato is the third most important crop for human consumption after rice and wheat.1 This crop is highly valuable to enhance food security because of its ease of cultivation, large yields and high nutritional value.1, 2, 3 Potato cultivars are generally autotetraploid (2n = 4× = 48) having high heterozygosity, self-incompatibility and tetrasomic inheritance. This genetic characterization complicates the conventional breeding approaches and serves as a major bottleneck in potato breeding4, 5 and prolongs development of improved cultivars. Advanced genetic approaches have gained increasing importance for the rapid improvement of potato traits with increased resilience to biotic and abiotic stresses, nutritional enhancement while maintaining increased yield.6

Genetic transformation has traditionally been used to introduce desirable traits into potato; however, transgenic crops are subject to strict regulatory frameworks in many countries. Recent technique of genetic modification like Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR)-associated protein (Cas), transfection, lipofection, electroporation or particle bombardment has ensured positive results ensuring transformed population of plants which is more acceptable as compared to transgenic plants.7, 8 CRISPR ribonucleoprotein (RNP)-mediated genome editing offers a simplified, cost-effective, and transgene-free approach for diverse plant species.9 The rapid intracellular degradation of RNPs minimizes prolonged genome exposure, reduces off-target effects and mosaicism, and has led to biosafety evaluation exemptions for edited plants in several countries, including India.10, 11 Precise RNP-mediated genome editing relies on efficient protoplast systems, which are considered a valuable resource for functional gene analysis and for validating CRISPR/Cas reagents prior to genome-editing applications.12

Protoplasts are generally obtained through the enzymatic digestion of mesophyll cells derived from young leaves of plants that are kept under tissue-culture conditions.13, 14, 15 These cells quickly dedifferentiate, reform a cell wall within 48–72 h, and reinitiate the cell cycle to divide, facilitating the development of callus colonies and the eventual regeneration of plants. Upadhya et al.16 demonstrated the successful culture of potato mesophyll protoplasts, showing their ability to regenerate cell wall, and callus tissue formation, laying foundation of potato protoplast regeneration. Shepard and Totten17 reported successful regeneration of potato plant from mesophyll protoplasts, establishing a foundation of protoplasts-based crop improvement and genome editing. Jones et al.18 demonstrated the potato shoot regeneration from protoplast derived calli is strongly genotype dependent, with regeneration frequencies varying from 4% to 42%. Fossi et al.19 demonstrated significant genotype-dependent variation in potato protoplast regeneration, highlighting genetic background as a critical factor influencing callus formation, shoot regeneration, and plant recovery efficiency. The isolation and regeneration systems for potato protoplasts have been recognized as effective platforms for transgene-free CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing. Nicolia et al.4 documented the extraction of approximately 1.9 × 106 protoplasts per gram of fresh weight (g/FW) from potato leaves, achieving microcallus formation rates of 6–7%. The subsequent regeneration of green calli led to shoot formation at a frequency of 63.88%, indicating the appropriateness of this system for genome editing purposes. Expanding on this research, Moon et al.20 further refined the protocol, enhancing protoplast yield to 6.36 × 106 g/FW and boosting shoot regeneration efficiency to 86.24%. Although several protocols for potato protoplast isolation and regeneration exist, efficient and reproducible regeneration remains a major challenge across cultivars.16, 17, 18, 19, 21 Collectively, these studies indicate that isolation and regeneration remain the primary bottleneck in developing robust and reproducible protoplast-based platforms for potato improvement and CRISPR-Cas9 RNP-mediated genome editing.22, 23

Therefore, the present study aimed to establish an efficient protocol for protoplast isolation and regeneration from young leaves of micro-propagated plants of Solanum tuberosum cv. Kufri Jyoti using alginate-based culture systems. By improving protoplast viability and microcallus formation, the proposed protocol provides a foundation for CRISPR-Cas9 RNP-mediated genome editing and facilitates the generation of transgene-free genome-edited potato plants for future crop improvement applications.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

All chemicals obtained were listed in Additional File 1. Culture media and stock solutions were adjusted to pH 5.7 prior to sterilization. Media were autoclaved at 121 °C and 15 psi for 15 min, while heat-labile solutions were filter-sterilized using 0.22 μm syringe filters.

Maintenance of plant material for growth

Stock plants of Solanum tuberosum cv. Kufri Jyoti were sourced from the National Repository of the Central Potato Research Institute located in Shimla. The propagation of the plants was achieved through nodal cuttings, which were cultured on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium24 enriched with 30 g/L sucrose, 2 mL/L calcium pantothenate (stock 2 mg/mL) and 2% (v/v) gelrite with pH adjusted to 5.8 before autoclaving. The regenerated shoots were sub-cultured after every three weeks to ensure continued active growth. The cultures were maintained in culture room at a temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, under a photoperiod of 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness.

Protoplast isolation and purification

The micro-propagated plants were grown in light for 3–4 weeks and kept in dark for 48 h before digestion. The leaves from the 2nd to 5th nodes from the top were removed from in-vitro grown plants (1 g/10 mL recommended). Protoplast isolation was carried out using an enzymatic digestion solution (20 mL) consisting of 20 mM MES, 0.4 M mannitol, 20 mM KCl, and 10 mM CaCl₂, supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA). The pH of the solution was adjusted to 5.7 prior to enzyme addition. Subsequently, 1.5% (w/v) cellulase and 0.5% (w/v) macerozyme were incorporated into the solution and gently mixed by vortexing. The enzymatic mixture was incubated at 55 °C for 15 min, followed by cooling to room temperature, after which CaCl₂ and BSA were added to complete the digestion medium. The enzyme digestion solution was preheated and sterilized through a 0.22 μm syringe filter. Leaves were cut along both sides of the midrib to facilitate digestion and incubated in 20 mL enzyme solution at 25 °C with gentle shaking (50 rpm) in the dark for 16 h. Following incubation, the digestion mixture was gently filtered through a sterile 70 μm nylon cell strainer into a 50 mL Falcon tube to remove undigested tissue and debris. The remaining leaf material was rinsed with 10 mL KCl solution, filtered again, and the filtrates were combined. The suspension was centrifuged at 50 ×g for 5 min using minimum acceleration and deceleration, and the supernatant was carefully discarded. The pellet was gently resuspended in W5 buffer26 (154 mM NaCl, 125 mM CaCl2, 5 mM KCl and 10 mM MES, pH at 5.7 adjusted with KOH) and centrifuged again to remove the enzyme solution. Supernatant was discarded and pellet was again washed in minimum amount of W5 solution. The dissolved pellet was carefully added to 15 mL tube containing 7 mL of 20% sucrose(w/v)-2 mM MES solution, pH 5.7 ensuing two phases remained separate. The purification was performed through centrifugation of 50 ×g for 5 min. The middle phase, containing the viable protoplast was carefully recovered and resuspended by pipetting in 5 mL of W5 solution. The sample was centrifuged again at 50 ×g for 5 min to remove the W5 solution and then MMG Buffer21 was added having composition of 0.4 M Mannitol, 15 mM MgCl2, 4 mM MES, pH 5.7 adjusted with KOH.

Standardization of parameters for protoplast yield

A conventional one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) approach was employed to optimize protoplast yield. The effects of different pre-plasmolysis treatments (0.45 M and 0.55 M mannitol), enzymatic compositions (E1, E2, and E3), durations of enzymatic digestion (8, 16, and 18 h), and osmoticum concentrations (0.4 M, 0.5 M, 0.6 M mannitol) were evaluated.

Protoplast counting and viability assessment

Protoplast yield was quantified using a dual-chamber hemocytometer under an Olympus optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) by dividing the number of protoplasts in enzyme solution by the fresh weight of plant leaves. Viability was assessed via fluorescein diacetate (FDA) staining: FDA (5 mg/mL in acetone) was added at 25 μL/mL to isolated protoplasts, incubated in the dark at room temperature for 20 min, and examined under a fluorescence microscope.25

Immobilization of protoplast in sodium alginate lens

An alginate solution was prepared consisting of 2.8%(w/v) sodium alginate (0.4 M mannitol) and was mixed with the protoplast suspension in an equal volume ratio.26 The mixture was gently swirled to ensure an even distribution of protoplasts. Subsequently, the suspension was carefully dispensed onto a solidified polymerization medium that contained 0.8%(w/v) celerigel, 50 mM CaCl₂, and 0.4 M mannitol to create alginate droplets. After an incubation period of 1 h at room temperature, the same solution (without clerigel and pre-chilled) was introduced to promote the complete polymerization of the sodium alginate, leading to the formation of alginate lenses that encapsulated the protoplasts.

Optimization of medium for protoplast multiplication

Sodium alginate-encapsulated protoplasts were cultured in liquid Murashige and Skoog (MSPI) or Gamborg B5 medium supplemented with 0.4 M osmoticum to evaluate the most suitable medium for protoplast division and microcallus formation, following Jeong et al.27 Medium composition details are provided in Table 2. Culture plates were initially maintained in darkness at 25 °C to facilitate protoplast division and microcolony formation. Upon microcolony development, alginate beads were transferred to MSPII medium containing 0.3 M mannitol, 2.0 mg/L BAP, and 0.5 mg/L NAA to promote microcallus proliferation. For plant regeneration, alginate-encapsulated minicalli were depolymerized in 20 mM sodium citrate containing 0.2 M mannitol (pH 5.7) and centrifuged at 50 ×g for 5 min to recover the microcalli. The recovered microcalli were resuspended in hormone-free liquid MS13K medium and transferred onto Whatman No. 2 filter paper discs placed on solid MS13K regeneration medium supplemented with appropriate hormones for callus proliferation and shoot induction (Table 2). After 2 weeks, filter paper bridges bearing developing calli were transferred to fresh regeneration medium and cultured for an additional 2 weeks under dim light (16 h light/8 h dark) at 25 ± 2 °C. Regenerated shoots (1–2 cm) were excised and transferred to MS rooting medium supplemented with 20 g/L sucrose and 1 mg/L IBA. After 3 weeks, well-rooted plantlets were acclimatized in a sterilized soil:sand:farmyard manure mixture (2:1:1) under high humidity (80–90%) before transfer to greenhouse conditions.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Differences among treatment means were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results

A series of experiments were conducted to optimize parameters affecting protoplast isolation from mesophyll tissues of potato cv. Kufri Jyoti. Initial isolation experiments produced relatively low protoplast yields and incomplete tissue digestion, indicating the need for further optimization.

Optimization of parameters for enhanced protoplast yield

Initially, experiment conducted to establish baseline protoplast isolation efficiency from S. tuberosum leaf mesophyll using enzymatic conditions (without pre-plasmolysis, plant kept in dark for 48 h, enzymatic combination i.e. 1.5% cellulase and 0.5% macerozyme, osmoticum of 0.4 M mannitol). 2.5 × 104 protoplast/mL from leaf explants was obtained after 16 h of incubation in dark. Incomplete digestion was observed, with considerable cell debris remaining in suspensions. These initial results indicate that the existing protocol requires the optimization of enzyme concentrations, incubation duration, and mannitol osmolarity to improve protoplast yield, viability, and integrity for future genome-editing applications.

Optimization of pre-plasmolysis conditions

Pre-plasmolysis in mannitol solutions was conducted for 1 h prior (0.45 M/0.55 M mannitol) before enzymatic digestion with Cellulase R-10 and Macerozyme R-10 (1% and 0.5% respectively) at 25 °C. This step stabilized cell turgor pressure and facilitated the release of protoplasts by weakening the cell walls in a controlled manner. Without this pre-plasmolysis, enzymatic digestion led to significant damage to the protoplasts, primarily due to their firm attachment between the cell wall and cell membrane. Plasmolysis treatment significantly affected yield (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). A higher protoplast yield was obtained with 0.45 M plasmolysis solution (4.21 ± 0.01 × 105protoplast/mL) which was substantially higher than both the control (standard procedure) and 0.55 M treatments (3.60 ± 0.07 × 104protoplast/mL). Effect of enzyme combination on protoplast isolation

As depicted in Fig. 2d-f and Table 1, three enzyme combinations were evaluated for protoplast isolation from potato leaves following incubation for 16 h at 25 °C: E1, Cellulase R-10 (1.5%) + Macerozyme R-10 (0.5%); E2, Cellulase R-10 (1.2%) + Pectinase (0.5%); and E3, Cellulase R-10 (1.0%) + Macerozyme R-10 (0.5%). Comparative analysis of these treatments enabled the identification of the most effective enzyme combination for plant cell wall dissociation. Significant differences in protoplast yield were observed among the enzyme treatments (one-way ANOVA, F = 33,100.00, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). The highest protoplast yield was obtained with E3, producing 4.71 ± 0.01 × 105protoplast/mL followed by E2 with 1.50 ± 0.01 × 105protoplast/mL The lowest yield was recorded for E1 (2.52 ± 0.01 × 104protoplast/mL. These results indicated that E3 was the most effective enzyme combination for protoplast isolation from Solanum tuberosum cv. Kufri Jyoti (Fig. 2d-f).

Effect of time period on protoplast isolation

Leaf digestion was performed using a 0.45 M pre-plasmolysis solution, followed by enzymatic digestion with the E3 enzyme combination for varying time intervals, specifically 8 h, 16 h, or 18 h at 25 °C. The length of enzymatic hydrolysis was found to be critical to the protoplast isolation process. Digestion duration significantly influenced protoplast yield (one-way ANOVA, F = 2900.32, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Among the incubation periods tested, 16 h resulted in the highest protoplast yield (4.72 ± 0.01 × 105protoplasts/mL), which was higher than the yields obtained after 8 h (1.10 ± 0.10 × 104protoplasts/mL) and 18 h (2.61 ± 0.01 × 104 protoplasts/mL) of digestion, respectively (Fig. 2g-i, Table 1). This finding suggested that a digestion time of 16 h facilitated complete hydrolysis of the cell wall without causing lysis due to over-digestion, resulting in a clean protoplast morphology at this duration.

Effect of osmoticum on protoplast isolation

To determine the ideal concentration of mannitol in enzyme solutions, a variety of mannitol concentrations ranging from 0.4 M/0.5 M/0.6 M in washing solution (W5) was evaluated. All experimental treatments included 0.45 M pre-plasmolysis, followed by enzymatic digestion utilizing a mixture of 1.0% cellulase and 0.5% macerozyme for a duration of 16 h. Osmoticum concentration significantly affected protoplast yield (one-way ANOVA, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). The highest protoplast yield was obtained at 0.4 M osmoticum concentration, producing 4.72 ± 0.03 × 105protoplast/mL. Increasing the osmoticum concentration to 0.5 M significantly reduced the yield to 2.92 ± 0.02 × 105protoplasts/mL. A further increase to 0.6 M resulted in the lowest protoplast yield (1.11 ± 0.01 × 104protoplasts/mL) as compared to other concentration (Refer to Fig. 2j-l and Table 1). The significant decline in protoplast recovery with increasing osmoticum concentration suggests that excessive osmotic pressure adversely affected protoplast release and stability. Therefore, 0.4 M was identified as the optimum osmoticum concentration for efficient protoplast isolation under the conditions employed in this study.

Nutrient adjustment and medium optimization

Mesophyll protoplasts isolated from Solanum tuberosum (Kufri Jyoti cultivar) were spherical, compact, and easily visible under the microscope, exhibiting varying chlorophyll content; they ranged from 30 to 50 μm in size, demonstrating the effectiveness of the isolation method (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Viable protoplasts were assessed using FDA staining, a fluorescence-based method distinguishing viable from non-viable protoplasts, observed under an EVOS FLC microscope (Life Technologies) at 490–530 nm excitation; as shown in Fig. 5. The isolation protocol yielded an elevated percentage of viable protoplasts, demonstrating the method's efficiency. The density of isolated protoplasts was determined using a hemocytometer by counting the average number in selected grid squares, yielding a final concentration of 4.72 × 105 protoplasts/mL (Fig. 6). Mesophyll protoplasts from Solanaceous family (Kufri Jyoti) typically underwent rapid cell wall formation within 48–72 h, an essential preliminary step for subsequent swift cell division. Media composition—particularly nutrient content, plant growth regulator concentrations, and osmoticum levels significantly influenced cell wall regeneration. Cell wall regeneration from protoplast membranes was confirmed by blue fluorescence in cultured cells after Calcofluor staining. Protoplasts were encapsulated in sodium alginate beads of consistent thickness to facilitate effective cell division, with protoplast and sodium alginate solution-mannitol mixed in equal concentrations to achieve a density of 4.0 × 104 protoplasts/mL (Fig. 8a-e). Two media types—Murashige and Skoog (MSPI) and Gamborg's (B5) without ammonium nitrate were evaluated for protoplast multiplication (Table 2). As reported, ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃) was eliminated from both media because it was found to inhibit protoplast multiplication during the initial stage. Protoplast multiplication was observed exclusively in MSPI, so further experiments were conducted using this medium. Initially, the encapsulated protoplasts were kept in the dark at 25 °C in an incubator and covered with aluminium foil for two weeks to avoid photodamage. To prevent accumulation of phenolic components and inhibition of protoplast multiplication, fresh medium was replenished at regular intervals. The first protoplast division initially exhibited asymmetry, with transverse division producing two unequal daughter cells, and subsequent divisions generated additional daughter cells that formed clusters of up to 16 cells. After 7 to 14 days, continued mitotic divisions of these protoplast-derived cells formed microcolonies approximately 0.2 mm in diameter with a sand-like appearance (Fig. 8f-g). These microcolonies further enlarged into star-shaped minicalli measuring about 0.5–0.8 mm in diameter under microscopic observation (Fig. 8g). The entire protoplast regeneration process was depicted in Fig. 8, Fig. 9, with Fig. 9a showing the comparative development of protoplast multiplication and the transition from microcalli within sodium alginate beads (Fig. 9b-c) as observed through both macroscopic and microscopic examination. MSPI was replaced with MSP II, and the initial aluminium foil cover was replaced by three layers of tissue paper to allow gradual light exposure and promote microcallus development; the tissue layers were then progressively reduced to acclimatize cultures to 500 Lux under a 16 h/8 h photoperiod. Osmoticum concentration was lowered and glucose was replaced by sucrose. After 30–45 days, minicalli appeared within the beads. BSA (0.01%) was added to absorb toxic metabolites and phenolics. Minicalli were stained with fluorescein diacetate and examined by fluorescence microscopy; viable cells showed bright green fluorescence. Minicalli released from sodium alginate beads by depolymerization with 20 mM sodium citrate were transferred to autoclaved Whatman No. 2 filter paper on MS13K medium solidified with 1% agarose. After callus growth began, the filter paper was removed and callus was subcultured onto semisolid MS13K (0.8%) medium with 2 mg/L zeatin riboside and 0.1 mg/L NAA for shoot induction (Fig. 9f-g). This took about 3–4 months. Shoots were then transferred to MS medium with 1 mg/L IBA for rooting (Fig. 9h), and roots formed and elongated within three weeks. Discussion

Potato (Solanum tuberosum) was recognized as a nutrient-rich crop with significant potential to alleviate micronutrient deficiencies.28 Although crop improvement had traditionally relied on conventional breeding and agronomic approaches, advanced genetic technologies such as ribonucleoprotein (RNP)-mediated genome editing provided a precise and efficient strategy for introducing targeted genetic modifications and enhancing desirable traits.29, 30 In the present study, the protocol of Moon et al.20 was adopted for protoplast isolation from 3 to 4-week-old S. tuberosum cv. Kufri Jyoti. Donor plants were subjected to 48 h of dark pretreatment to minimize free radical and photo-inhibitory compound production in chloroplast-containing cells31 and leaf tissues were trimmed along the midrib. Pre-plasmolysis enhanced protoplast yield by reducing membrane damage during enzymatic digestion, consistent with Vessabutr and Grant,32 who reported higher viable protoplast recovery from pre-plasmolysed tissues. The substantial protoplast yield obtained with 1% cellulase and 0.5% Macerozyme resulted from synergistic degradation of cellulose and pectic components, enabling effective release while preserving membrane integrity. Previous studies support this: Moon et al.20 used 1% cellulase and 0.2% Macerozyme; Peng et al.33 used 2.0% cellulase, 0.5% pectinase, and 0.25% Macerozyme; Tavazza et al.34 employed 3% cellulase, 1% Macerozyme, and 1% pectinase with 0.3 M osmoticum; and Chen35 used 1% cellulase R-10 with 0.5% Macerozyme R-10 for potato protoplasts. Yield variability across cultivars reflects both enzyme composition and genotype effects. Reported yields range from 1 to 3 × 105 protoplasts/mL16 to 1–2 × 107/mL36 with intermediate values including 2.31–2.52 × 106/mL in diploid cultivars ‘47–33’ and ‘5–19’33 and 6.36 × 106/mL in S. tuberosum cv. Désirée.20 These findings underscore the strong influence of genotype and isolation conditions on recovery efficiency.

Osmotic regulation was critical for protoplast isolation, stability, and regeneration. The application of 0.4 M mannitol stabilized the plasma membrane and minimized osmotic stress by reducing transmembrane water flux,37 consistent with previous findings identifying osmotic optimization as a key determinant of protoplast viability and regenerative competence.38, 39 These findings align with Eeckhaut et al.40 and Davey et al.41 who similarly identified osmotic optimization as critical for protoplast viability and regenerative competence. Shepard and Totten17 demonstrated effective osmotic balance using 0.4 M mannitol in Solanum tuberosum cv. Russet Burbank protoplasts, while Shahin and Simpson42 and Tavazza et al.34 independently reported successful protoplast stabilization and tetraploid regeneration in cv. Désirée using modified MS medium supplemented with the same concentration.

As demonstrated by Jeong et al.,27 protoplast regeneration constituted a sequential process involving isolation, cell division, microcallus formation, and plant regeneration, with culture medium and growth conditions playing a critical role. Initial multiplication was performed in ammonium nitrate-free media, a strategy established to enhance protoplast multiplication and callus induction across diverse plant species43, 44 and also adapted for cassava,45 Lycopersicon spp.,46 pea47 and maize48 where a 12-fold reduction of ammonium nitrate in MS medium markedly enhanced proliferation rates. Protoplast cell division occurred asynchronously, with microcolonies developing within 2–3 weeks. Division efficiency was influenced by the auxin-to-cytokinin ratio, which varied by genotype and tissue source. While 2,4-D was commonly used to promote cell division, NAA was more effective for tetraploid potato.43 Several studies supported the role of a cytokinin-dominant ratio in promoting dedifferentiation and cell division in mesophyll-derived protoplasts. Kielkowska and Adamus26 reported that 0.45 μM 2,4-D combined with 1 μM zeatin and 10 μM putrescine effectively promoted initial protoplast division in Brassica spp., facilitating mitotic reactivation. Similarly, Sofiari et al.49 demonstrated enhanced cassava protoplast proliferation in medium containing 0.5 mg/L NAA and 1 mg/L zeatin, highlighting the importance of cytokinin-dominant hormonal balance for protoplast development. Moon et al.20 used medium containing macro, micronutrient, organic acid, vitamins, vitamins supplemented with higher auxin to cytokinin ratio 1 mg/L NAA and 0.4 mg/L BAP with protoplast density of 6.36 ± 0.51 × 106protoplasts/mL for the initial microcalli formation. Upadhya16 reported successful protoplast multiplication and subsequent callus formation in potato cultures grown on a medium supplemented with NAA (3 mg/L) and BAP (1 mg/L). In a similar manner, the combination of NAA (3 mg/L) and BAP (1 mg/L) in MS medium24 significantly enhanced protoplast proliferation and microcallus development in Fagopyrum esculentum.50 In callus regeneration, IAA and zeatin played an efficient role. The response of callus and shoot regeneration to plant growth-promoting hormones differs based on the cultivar, with IAA and zeatin identified as the most effective in the case of potatoes.51 The regeneration phase further demonstrated the importance of genotype specific hormonal requirements. For example, Solanum tuberosum cv. ‘Desiree’ was generally started on a regeneration medium enriched with zeatin and NAA,52 while S. peruvianum is successfully grown on media that includes 2 mg/L kinetin, underscoring the differences in hormonal needs for regeneration that are specific to each cultivar. Moon et al.20 used MS medium containing 0.01 mg/L and zeatin 2 mg/L and 0.1 mg/L GA3 for potato regeneration from potato cultivar. Bokelmann and Roset44 used in medium supplemented with 1 mg/L zeatin, NAA and GA3 at concentration 0.01 mg/L for callus and shoot regeneration in Potato cultivar (Solarium tuberosum cv Bintje). Similarly, Stelmach-Wityk et al.53 documented effective shoot regeneration in Brassica when the culture medium was enhanced with 1.0 mg/L NAA and 1 mg/L BAP, thereby reinforcing the importance of balanced auxin–cytokinin ratios in facilitating organogenesis.

Limitations and future perspectives

Although the protocol developed in this study successfully supported protoplast-to-plant regeneration in S. tuberosum cv. Kufri Jyoti, several limitations should be considered. First, the study was conducted using a single potato genotype. Because protoplast isolation efficiency, cell division, and regeneration capacity are highly genotype dependent, the applicability of the protocol to other commercial cultivars requires further validation. Second, genomic stability of regenerated plants was not assessed. Previous studies have reported chromosomal rearrangements and somaclonal variation during protoplast regeneration, highlighting the importance of evaluating genome integrity in regenerated plants.

Future research should focus on adapting the protocol to a broader range of potato genotypes and integrating it with CRISPR/Cas ribonucleoprotein delivery systems for transgene-free genome editing. Additional studies investigating editing efficiency, off-target mutations, and the genetic stability of regenerated plants will further strengthen the utility of this platform. Optimization of regeneration time, automation of protoplast handling, and application of the system to multiplex genome editing and trait stacking represent promising avenues for accelerating potato improvement. Ultimately, combining efficient protoplast regeneration with next-generation genome-editing technologies could facilitate the development of improved potato cultivars with enhanced nutritional quality, stress tolerance, disease resistance, and climate resilience.

Conclusion

The main finding of this study highlights the successful optimization of potato protoplast isolation and the establishment of efficient, stable and rapid biotechnology applications in Solanum tuberosum cv. Kufri Jyoti. Mesophyll protoplasts were successfully isolated through an optimized medium by standardizing parameters (pre-plasmolysis, enzyme concentration and duration) that ensures reproducibility, high yield and viability of protoplasts followed by micro-calli formation and subsequent plant regeneration. An efficient yield of protoplast serves as solid foundation for functional genomics and gene editing study in potato. This methodology offers significant potential for varietal improvement in potato for nutrient enrichment, climate resilience, productivity enhancement and input use efficiency; with broad implications for plant science research and agricultural innovation. As enhancing the nutritional value of potatoes through gene-editing approaches could further establish them as a key dietary staple, contributing to food security and human health.
