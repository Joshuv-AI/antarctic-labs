Understanding the Effectiveness of Mindfulness-based Dance Movement Therapy on Loneliness and Aggression of Underprivileged Children of an NGO
Authors: Achala V Kumar, A A S Azam, Fouzia Alsabah Shaikh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderprivileged children from low socio-economic backgrounds lack access to the resources that promote cognitive, emotional and social development. Cognitive stimulation and psychological support enhance the neurobehavioural responses in underprivileged children. Interventions like Mindfulness-based Dance Movement Therapy (MBDMT) are inter-modal psychotherapy, enhancing mind and body integration, improving emotional regulation and alleviating mental health problems like loneliness and aggression.PurposeThe study aims to evaluate the effect of MBDMT on loneliness and aggression among underprivileged children of an NGO in Delhi-NCR, India.MethodsA quasi-experimental pre–post design was adopted, with 100 participants (aged 10–16 years) divided into treatment and control groups. The intervention consisted of 15 sessions (60 min each, for 15 weeks). UCLA Loneliness Scale and Buss–Perry Aggression Questionnaire were administered pre- and post-intervention and at follow-up (3 months). Data was analysed through a paired samples t-test using SPSS.ResultsThe t value of the treatment group was 17.890 at p < .01, indicating a significant reduction in loneliness post-MBDMT. The aggression score of the treatment group has a t value of 20.290, indicating a significant decrease in aggression post-intervention. The post-test scores of both treatment and control groups were analysed for loneliness and aggression; the t values were −3.322 and −6.723, indicating a significant decrease in loneliness and aggression in the treatment group. The comparison of post-test and follow-up of loneliness had a t value of −2.760. And the follow-up scores of aggression showed a significant t value of 8.053.ConclusionMBDMT is an effective community-based neurobehavioural intervention for reducing loneliness and aggression among underprivileged children. The results highlight the therapeutic value of mindfulness and movement-based expressive interventions in promoting emotional regulation and social connectedness in community settings. Although Indians have been known for practising classical dance forms, yoga and meditation for ages, schools should inculcate mindfulness and movement with the usual curriculum.Keywords: Dance therapy, dance, movement, group therapy,

Introduction

Chronic deprivation often alters the structures managing learning, memory and emotional regulation. However, targeted cognitive stimulation and psychological support for underprivileged children can help promote neuroplasticity. 1 About 1 billion children are multidimensionally poor across the world, lacking necessities as basic as nutritious food and clean water. 2 For underprivileged children, these stressors like food insecurity, housing instability and neighbourhood violence are endless. These inequities are chronic, severely compromising a child’s socio-emotional and cognitive development. Underprivileged children are vulnerable to psychological distress, manifesting through internalising symptoms like social withdrawal and loneliness, as well as externalising behaviours like aggression.

Loneliness can stem from social isolation, peer rejection, peer exclusion and discrimination in the underprivileged. 3 Russell defines loneliness as ‘a subjective experience of social isolation, where an individual perceives a discrepancy between the social relationships they desire and the relationships they actually have’. 4 Due to social exclusion, rejection and isolation, the underprivileged children can turn hostile and elicit aggressive behaviours that could have the intention to cause harm with the presence of a target.5, 6 Chronic loneliness often leads to a state of social hypervigilance, where the amygdala begins to misinterpret neutral social cues as hostile threats. This neurological misfiring can trigger reactive aggression as a maladaptive defence mechanism, further isolating the child socially. 5

The psychological burden is further compounded by a critical deficit in accessible mental health infrastructure in low-resource settings. Limited access to supportive mental health resources exacerbates challenges like loneliness and aggression, necessitating community-based interventions that promote holistic well-being. 7 This gap underscores the need for scalable, culturally adaptable and community-based interventions. Expressive movement involves free body expression, and mindfulness plays a pivotal role in helping children from underprivileged backgrounds with loneliness, aggression and allied emotional challenges. 8 Dance Movement Therapy (DMT) and mindfulness-based techniques remediate mental health and behavioural issues of the underprivileged children.

DMT is not just an exercise; it is a way to signal the nervous system that it is safe to turn its social brain back on. By moving together, participants practice kinesthetic empathy, literally feeling what it is like to be in someone else’s skin. Participants mimic each other’s movements in mirroring activity, typically tapping into the mirror neuron system. This neural resonance physically rebuilds the capacity to understand others’ intentions and emotions without words. Mindfulness-based Dance Movement Therapy (MBDMT) focuses on internal sensations and breath, allowing interoception in individuals to regulate their own stress response, moving from reactive to reflective social behaviour. This counters the profound loneliness faced by underprivileged children, replacing cycles of survival-driven aggression with emotional resilience. Moving in union triggers the release of oxytocin, which reduces the amygdala’s fear response and fosters a sense of safety and belonging. 8

Over the past few decades, DMT has emerged as one of the potent modalities for treating emotional and behavioural dysregulation. 8 The Indian Association of Dance Movement Therapy in 1975, defines DMT as a ‘psychotherapeutic method that uses movement and creative expression to enhance socio-emotional, cognitive and somatic integration’. DMT, founded by Marian Chace, became an established therapy intervention in Western countries in the 1940s, when the pioneers integrated professional dance concepts into therapy, and spread globally in the 1990s. A contemporary evolution of this modality is MBDMT, developed by Yael Schweitzer in 2019. 9 MBDMT integrates the somatic, expressive qualities of dance with the non-judgmental acceptance of mindfulness techniques. Psychologically, this dual approach targets emotional regulation through all the pathways possible, allowing the autonomic nervous system to ground while actively processing emotional distress. 9

Extensive empirical literature supports the efficacy of movement and mindfulness interventions. For instance, a study conducted in China explored the effects of a 16-week dance intervention on loneliness and self-esteem in left-behind children, with a sample size of 1,270 underprivileged children, including an experimental group (n = 635) and a control group (n = 635). The experimental group undertook 45 min of dance therapy, five times a week and the control group maintained their original living conditions. Post-intervention, the data analysis was done through analysis of variance (ANOVA) and concluded that loneliness significantly decreased, and their self-esteem significantly improved. 10

Furthermore, a pre–post-test study examined the effectiveness of DMT on aggression, depression, anxiety and self-esteem amongst adolescent victims. Sixty females between 13 and 18-year-old participants were randomly assigned to experimental and control groups. The data analysis through ANOVA showed significant pre–post differences that DMT reduces aggression and depression and improves self-esteem. 11 In a study by Majore-Dusele et al., MBDMT was formulated for chronic pain and depression. The data analysis revealed a statistically significant reduction in pain intensity and depression scores in favour of the MBDMT in the experimental group; these improvements were maintained in the follow-up assessment. 12

A doctoral thesis synthesised the exploration of MBDMT with high school-going adolescents managing anxiety. As a result, it was found that there was a connection between the use of pause (mindfulness techniques) in MBDMT and DMT. Emphasised the need to acknowledge the body’s natural rhythm, supporting a student’s ability to develop coping skills to manage anxiety. 13 A pre–post-test study revealed the impact of dance/movement classes on adolescent disruptive behaviour in an urban high school setting. Forty-five at-risk students in urban schools were given DMT sessions, resulting in reduced stress, improved self-control and regulation and less disruptive behaviour. 14 A quasi-experimental study explored the effects of DMT on aggression and empathy in children with emotional problems and showed a reduction in aggression and increased empathy in children with behavioural issues post-DMT. 15

Empirical studies like these have demonstrated that MBDMT enhances psychological health outcomes, such as loneliness and aggression. 16 The previously mentioned studies emphasised the impact of DMT on loneliness and aggression. While DMT is known to be effective in helping the underprivileged deal with loneliness and emotional dysregulation. However, there is a need for more evidence-based studies for underprivileged children in the Indian context. Furthermore, there is limited research exploring MBDMT among underprivileged children in NGO settings, where psychosocial support is compromised. The purpose of this study was to conduct evidence-based research on MBDMT on underprivileged children in an NGO in Delhi-NCR, India.

The goal of the current research was to find out the effect of MBDMT on loneliness and aggression among underprivileged children.

Methods

Study Design

A quasi-experimental, pre–post-test control group design was adopted for the research. The data were collected from an NGO in Delhi-NCR, where the underprivileged children came from a rural background, with merely basic amenities to survive, having low socio-economic backgrounds. They are provided with free education, food, uniforms, books, bags and art, theatre and dance classes at the NGO.

Ethical approval from the affiliated University was obtained. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants in written form from parents and from the children. All the participants voluntarily opted for the therapy intervention, and confidentiality was maintained.

Instruments

Two questionnaires were given to the participants before, after the intervention and at follow-up. UCLA Loneliness Scale (α = 0.89), developed by Daniel Russell in 1996, is a 4-point scale consisting of 20 items, for ages ranging from 10 to 99. The scale demonstrates a coefficient of reproducibility of 0.92, indicating excellent internal consistency. The test–retest reliability of the scale showed correlations of 0.85 and 0.88, indicating excellent internal stability. 5

The BPAQ (α = 0.87) is a 29-item self-report instrument with a 5-point Likert scale, designed as a comprehensive measure of aggression in people aged from 9 to 88. The questionnaire demonstrates that it has high internal consistency and reliability with 0.89. 7 The BPAQ divides aggression into four dimensions reflected in the subscales: physical aggression, the tendency to engage in physical acts of aggression, scores ranging from 9 to 45. Verbal aggression, the desire to engage in verbal arguments and confrontations, scores range between 5 and 25. Anger, the emotional aspect of aggression, includes quick temper and frustration; scores range from 7 to 35. Hostility, the feelings of ill will and suspicion towards others, scores range from 8 to 40. A total scale score ranges from 29 to 145.

Sample

The current academic year on roll/register of the NGO had N = 130, considering a confidence interval to be 95%, that is, e = 0.05. Using Taro Yamane’s formula for sample size determination, which amounted to n = 98.11. Hundred underprivileged children aged 10–16 years (N = 100), were assigned to treatment group (n = 50) and a control group (n = 50). The treatment group underwent 15 DMT sessions of 60 min each, weekly for 15 weeks. Each session followed a structure: check-in, warm-up, movement and mindfulness exploration, reflection/integration and cool down or closure.

Procedure and Structure of the Sessions

Every session was goal-driven, following the same structure by commencing the session through checking in as to how they were doing (ice-break), followed by warming-up of mind and body (stretching exercises) and a main mindful-movement exposure (partner activities, group movement activities), followed by reflection of the session (drawing art and writing) and ending the session with cool down activities. Mindfulness techniques were used in each therapy session at three stages. At the warm-up: beginning the session with yoga practices. At the main/middle of the session: a main theme-based movement with guided imagery, tapping into the chakras and moving to the instrumental music. At the cool down: towards the end of the session, breathing and relaxation techniques such as Jacobson’s Progressive Muscle Relaxation, box breathing and pranayama were included.

Each session was designed as per goals, and the same structure was followed from the first to the last session. The first few sessions were to explore and build rapport by enhancing a sense of belongingness through partner activities and group cohesive movement exercises. The next few sessions emphasised building trust in themselves and reducing self-isolation. The rest were to reduce aggressive behaviours using guided imagery and relaxation techniques, and the last few sessions were dedicated to wind up the intervention with an appropriate closure. Most sessions took place with different props, like dupattas, chairs, balloons, ribbons and balls, to make the sessions interesting for the children, which also enhanced the range of movement in the children. Data were collected at three stages: pre-conduction of intervention, post-intervention and after 3 months of completion of intervention for follow-up.

Statistical Techniques

Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, paired samples t-test, using SPSS.

Discussion

The present study focused on the effectiveness of MBDMT on loneliness and aggression. Using quantitative statistical analyses, including descriptive statistics and paired sample t-tests, the study explored loneliness and aggression in underprivileged children. Overall, the findings provided preliminary evidence suggesting that MBDMT helps reduce loneliness and aggression.

The intervention followed a structured procedure for all the sessions in a group therapy format. The therapy included themes like social belongingness and emotional regulation, which helped the children to explore the realm of emotions without the fear of judgement. The art materials used in the sessions helped them reflect on their thoughts and feelings. Mindfulness activities, yoga practices, guided imagery tapped into their inner child, bringing a positive state of mind. The dance movement activities involving props and partner movement exercises helped them release their bottled-up feelings. The intervention built a strong empathetic connection with oneself and others. 17

The participants who undertook the therapy intervention showed significant changes in mood, fostered social belongingness and became more self-regulated, embracing one’s thoughts and that of others. The underprivileged children who participated in the therapy intervention had reduced feelings of loneliness and aggression. The t-tests provided robust statistical evidence supporting the efficacy of MBDMT as an intervention for reducing loneliness and aggression.

A relevant baseline comparison test was done through t-test to confirm that the treatment group and control group were similar before the intervention began. Baseline characteristics like age, economic backgrounds and other pre-requisites were similar across all groups. The statistical analysis revealed that loneliness significantly reduced, post-intervention, and there was a lasting effect in follow-up. These findings are consistent with the existing literature demonstrating that MBDMT is effective in reducing loneliness. 11 In addition to this, the aggression scores decreased after the intervention in the treatment group. The physical aggression, verbal aggression, anger, hostility, overall, aggression reduced after the intervention and at follow-up. These results are consistent with the prior research demonstrating that as an effect of MBDMT aggression can be reduced over time. 12 The descriptive findings also provided supporting evidence for interpreting the same.

The results of follow-up showed significant long-lasting effect of MBDMT on loneliness and aggression. There was a sustained reduction in loneliness and aggression in follow-up scores of the treatment group. Loneliness levels remained significantly lower at follow-up compared to the post-test, indicating continuing effects of intervention, which could be due to increased social connectedness developed during the group therapy sessions. The treatment group aggression scores also remained statistically lower at follow-up in comparison with post-test, which indicates an attribution towards improved emotional regulation skills gained during the intervention.13, 14

The further improved scores at follow-up suggest that the effects of the MBDMT intervention were internalised by participants, that likely facilitated the development of emotional regulation, social connectedness and self-awareness. These findings indicate a continued influence on the participants’ behaviour beyond the structured sessions. This pattern aligns with social learning and embodiment-based frameworks; experiential and group-based interventions, producing enduring psychological change through time. The findings of the study are consistent with the previous literature supporting the improvement in loneliness and aggression at follow-up.9, 17

Limitations and Implications for Further Research

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, a relatively small sample size (n = 100) limits the generalisability of the results. Second, the gender distribution of participants was uneven in the treatment group and the control group. Despite these limitations, the study contributes to the growing body of research examining the effectiveness of MBDMT on loneliness and aggression among underprivileged children.

Future research may explore further long-term interventions to determine the persistence of these gains, integrating qualitative feedback from participants for a richer insight, and comparing MBDMT with other creative arts therapies. Nonetheless, the present study demonstrates that MBDMT is a viable and effective intervention for enhancing psychosocial well-being, significantly reducing loneliness and aggression among underprivileged children. It serves as a sustainable, neuroscience-informed intervention for holistic child development.

Conclusion

The findings provided strong evidence that the intervention had a meaningful and significant impact on loneliness and aggression in underprivileged children. Previous research demonstrates that this intervention significantly improves psychological well-being and emotional regulation while reducing distress like loneliness and aggression.9, 17 The findings affirm that MBDMT can function as an effective psychosocial intervention within the context of NGO-based children/adolescent programmes, where emotional dysregulation and social isolation are common concerns. Collectively, these outcomes suggest that MBDMT is a powerful tool in promoting emotional regulation, reducing distress and enhancing relational well-being among underprivileged children.

To summarise, the findings underscore that MBDMT intervention can lead to meaningful psychological improvements regardless of gender or dance background. This makes the intervention a highly accessible and inclusive therapeutic approach suitable for community and NGO settings where children may differ widely in prior exposure to expressive arts. The intervention’s ability to reduce loneliness and aggression highlights its relevance for programmes aiming to foster resilience, emotional expression, and social connectedness in vulnerable populations. Thus, the effect of the MBDMT intervention can be interpreted as robust and generalisable across the population. The findings demonstrate that MBDMT significantly improves psychological well-being, that is, loneliness and aggression, among underprivileged children.
