Overview and goals

This project addresses three key questions about the “glue that binds us all” (Nuclear Science Advisory
Council’s Long-Range Plan). First, what is the three-dimensional momentum distribution of gluons
within the nucleon? Second, how is the motion of quarks and gluons correlated within hadrons and how
do those correlations manifest in nucleon structure? Third, how can lattice calculations guide the
experimental study of hadron structure at the future electron-ion collider (EIC)?

The primary goals of the project were:

 1. Extract the unpolarized and helicity twist-2 gluon generalized parton distributions (GPDs) of
 the nucleon from lattice quantum chromodynamics calculations that use a single ensemble with
 2+1 flavors of Wilson-clover improved fermions.
 2. A proof-of-principle lattice calculation of the x-dependence of the spin structure function of the
 nucleon, g2(x, Q2 ), and the related twist-3 quantities gT(x, Q2) and dn2(Q2), in the pseudo-
 distribution framework. The second Mellin moment of the spin structure function is denoted
 dn2 and it relates the constituent chromo-electric and -magnetic fields to the nucleon’s
 polarization.
 3. Theoretical developments required to determine the isoscalar quark GPDs of the nucleon. There
 are three key components to this goal: first, the construction of reduced pseudo-GPDs for sea
 quark quantities; second, GPD modelling to determine the most important regions of parameter
 space for lattice calculations; and, eventually, code testing.
 4. An exploratory calculation of the twist-3 gluon GPDs of the nucleon, which give direct access
 to the orbital angular momentum of the constituent quarks and gluons, and have not been
 calculated using lattice QCD before.

Accomplishments

The project supported work that has been published in eight peer-reviewed publications, with two
further manuscripts accepted for publication. Most notably, the project supported work will be
published in Physical Review Letters that proposes a general new class of observables for
benchmarking and validating lattice calculations of hadron structure, which is directly relevant to the
“systematic uncertainties” component of goals 2 and goals 3 and 4. Global extractions of both PDFs
and GPDs that incorporate lattice calculations require that the lattice calculations are trustworthy and
provide meaningful quantitative estimates of systematic uncertainties. This work proposes a benchmark
that is straightforward to calculate that allows direct comparison of lattice calculations using both the
pseudo-distribution and LaMET frameworks and the direct comparison of lattice calculations to
experimental results.
In addition to these publications, the project supported work that appeared in two conference
proceedings and has been reported in 18 colloquia, seminars and invited conference presentations.
In collaboration with the HadStruc Collaboration, significant progress towards goal 1 was achieved,
with the first-ever analysis of moments of quarks GPDs (called generalized form factors) using the

pseudo-distribution framework. The analysis of quark GPDs is represents a challenge for lattice QCD
and a necessary step towards gluon GPDs. This work was published in H. Dutrieux et al., Towards
unpolarized GPDs from pseudo-distributions, JHEP 08 (2024) 162 and the follow-up work is ongoing.
This work is anticipated to be submitted for publication in early 2026, with data analysis well
underway. As part of the work towards goal 1, the project supported calculations of the gluon
momentum fraction of the nucleon. This quantity is necessary for obtaining the overall normalisation of
the gluon PDF and is closely tied to the extraction of the gluon GPDs in the future. This quantity is also
the first step in the construction of an software pipeline for data generation and analysis of gluon
structure. In conjunction with myself and other members of the HadStruc Collaboration, Alex has
written code that generates the data necessary to determine the gluon momentum fraction of the
nucleon and the data necessary to determine the x-dependent gluon PDF of the nucleon. These are the
first two steps necessary for the completion of goal 1 (and will form the foundation for goals 2 and 4).
Significant progress was made towards the perturbative calculations necessary for goals 2 and 3. This
work is ongoing, in collaboration with Tobias Neumann, a postdoctoral researcher supported by this
project, who is now a tenure-track faculty member at Southern Methodist University. We anticipate that
the first publication of this perturbative analysis, along with the automated code routines, will be
submitted for publication before the end of this Reporting Period (end of the calendar year). We have
focussed in particular on the perturbative analysis for the gluon structure of the nucleon. This analysis
directly relates quasi- and pseudo-distributions to light-cone PDFs at one-loop perturbation theory. Our
calculation is the first-ever to carry out this renormalization and matching in both a general R_xi gauge
and in axial gauge. This very stringent test of our results, along with the automated nature of the
process, has allowed us to correct and clarify several results in the literature.
In addition, this project supported work that led to a new approach to analyzing the nonperturbative
evolution of hadronic matrix elements relevant to hadron structure, published in H. Dutrieux et al.,
Evolution of Parton Distribution Functions in the Short-Distance Factorization Scheme, JHEP 04, 061
arXiv:2310.19926. This work also proposed a novel way to extend the range of Ioffe time that can be
determined from lattice calculations using relatively computationally cheap finite volume calculations.
The Ioffe time is directly to connected (via Fourier transform) to the range of parton momentum
fraction (x) that can be accessed, which will be particularly important for the long-term goal of
determining GPDs, which require data over a wide kinematic range.
Our collaborative work, partially supported by this award, to bring together lattice calculations with
phenomenological extractions of PDFs for a new determination of the polarized gluon PDF was
published in Physical Review D. This analysis builds on the first lattice calculation relevant to the
polarized gluon PDF and brings together, for the first time, experimental data and lattice calculations to
address the gluon contributions to the proton spin puzzle, a long-standing open question about the
origin of the spin of the proton.
Impact
The lattice determination of x-dependent hadron structure is still in its infancy, compared to
phenomenological determinations and to other lattice quantities related to local operators. This means
that many approaches are still in development and most calculations do not control all sources of
systematic uncertainty. Benchmarking and validation of lattice results is therefore critical for the

community and a focus of current efforts across both lattice and global fitting research groups. For
example, it is the focus of a community white paper to which the PI is contributing as one of the
authors of the section on benchmarking new calculations and methods.
The proposal of window observables that will appear in Physical Review Letters therefore has the
potential for significant future impacts on the nuclear theory community and potential implications for
the theory inputs into the EIC program.

In addition to this work, a new proposals for improved methods to extract hadron structure from the
lattice over a range of scales, without relying on perturbative evolution at hadronic scales, has the
potential for future impacts within the hadron structure community by removing one source of
systematic uncertainty at relatively low computational cost.

The software development and data analysis tools that have been developed will be made available to
the public after publication of the relevant manuscripts associated with this project. This will benefit
the lattice community and the global fitting communities, within Nuclear Theory.

Collaboration enabled by this award also led to the publication of the first calculation of the isovector
quark helicity PDF of the nucleon and the first calculation of the generalised form factors of the
nucleon from the pseudo‐PDF formalism in lattice QCD. These proof‐of‐principle calculations
demonstrated two features that will enable future precision calculations in the field of hadron structure
and are a necessary step towards completing the goals of this project. First, the analysis of nucleon
PDFs in the helicity basis, relevant to hadrons with nonzero momentum (required for the calculation of
both one‐ and three‐dimensional measures of hadron structure) and the implementation of an SVD
analysis for improving data analysis and signal‐to‐noise ratio in lattice calculations of nucleons with
nonzero momentum. These steps will be required to manage the challenging signal‐to‐noise ratio in
gluon GPD calculations using lattice QCD and the efficient data management of the large amount of
data that will be generated in future calculations. These techniques can be used by other collaborations
and researchers in the field to support related calculations.

Finally, The software development of the automated perturbation theory routines relevant to
perturbative matching from the lattice to experimental schemes may be used in the High Energy Theory
or High Energy Phenomenology Communities, where precise knowledge of gluon PDFs are important
theoretical inputs into studies of the Higgs and searches for new physics at the LHC.

Summary

This project used lattice QCD to advance our understanding of nucleon structure through new
calculations of the first-ever calculations of the helicity PDFs and generalized form factors of the
nucleon via the pseudo-distribution framework. These calculations inform our understanding of the
momentum and spatial distributions of quarks and gluons. In addition, new theoretical developments
for benchmarking and uncertainty quantification, and the proposal of novel observables for validating
lattice results were proposed. The research produced nine peer-reviewed publications (including two
accepted for publication), developed open-source computational tools, and trained early-career

scientists, laying essential groundwork for future studies at the Electron-Ion Collider and within the
broader nuclear theory community.
