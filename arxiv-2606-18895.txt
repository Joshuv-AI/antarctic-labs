Symmetric and Isotypic Hilbert Series for Symmetric Ideals Thanks: This work has been supported by European Union’s HORIZON-MSCA-2023-DN-JD programme under the Horizon Europe (HORIZON) Marie Skłodowska-Curie Actions, grant agreement 101120296 (TENORS)
Henri Breloer, Cordian Riener

Abstract
. An ideal in a polynomial ring is symmetric if it is invariant under any permutation of variables. In this paper, we define and study the symmetric and isotypic Hilbert series for symmetric ideals in a polynomial ring with countably many variables. The symmetric Hilbert series is the limit of the Hilbert series of the invariant parts of the finite truncated quotients, while the isotypic Hilbert series records stable multiplicities of irreducible symmetric-group representations for each degree. Our main result proves that, under a mild support condition on the ideal, the symmetric Hilbert series is a rational function. We further show that this rationality extends to the isotypic Hilbert series for every irreducible representation. The proofs of these results rely on the monomial structure of the polynomials within the symmetric ideal, combined with Kostka inversion for the isotypic case.



1.
Introduction

The infinite polynomial ring
K[x_{1},x_{2},\dots]
in countably many variables is, both from the viewpoint of commutative algebra and geometry, a remarkably rich object. It is
non-Noetherian and therefore more complicated than a finitely generated polynomial ring, but at the same time it is one of the
simplest infinite-dimensional rings one can write down. The situation becomes even more interesting if one considers symmetry on this ring and finiteness phenomena appear once symmetry is taken
into account. The
first sign of such phenomena is a theorem of Cohn
[
4
,
5
]
, rediscovered by
Aschenbrenner and Hillar
[
1
]
: every
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
-invariant ideal of
K[X]

satisfies the ascending chain condition. Hillar and Sullivant
[
10
]
extended this idea of finiteness up to symmetry to define an equivariant Gröbner basis theory with applications in algebraic statistics, and
Draisma
[
7
]
placed the phenomenon in a broader frame, proving that any
polynomial functor over an infinite field is topologically Noetherian.

A second strand of the commutative algebra in this infinite setup concerns Hilbert series. For a
Sym
-invariant chain of
graded ideals
(I_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, Nagel and Römer
[
18
]
introduced the

equivariant Hilbert series

H_{(I_{n})}(s,t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}H({K[X_{n}]/I_{n}};\,t)\,s^{n},

and proved it to be rational in
s
and
t
; an alternative proof via formal languages
is given in
[
13
]
. From this rationality one reads off that the dimension of

K[X_{n}]/I_{n}
grows eventually linearly in
n
and its degree eventually exponentially,
with the precise rates determined in
[
18
,
16
]
. Further asymptotic invariants
along such chains, for example, regularity, projective dimension, Betti tables, are surveyed in

[
11
]
, and for ideals generated by general forms the asymptotic stability of
Hilbert functions and Betti numbers is established in
[
23
]
. A parallel theory in
the
\textnormal{GL}_{\infty}
-equivariant setting of twisted commutative algebras was developed by
Sam and Snowden
[
22
]
, who proved rationality of Hilbert series for finitely
generated modules. In our work we focus on the
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
-structure throughout. The
geometry underlying these results in this setting, the equivariant algebraic geaometry, the structure of the equivariant
spectrum has been described by Kummer and Riener
[
14
]
and by Nagpal and Snowden
[
19
]
.

In this paper we look for a more direct analogue of the classical Hilbert series for a
single symmetric ideal
I\subset K[X]
. The ordinary series is unavailable, since each
graded piece of
I
is either zero or infinite-dimensional. Passing to the truncations

I_{1}\subset I_{2}\subset\cdots\subset I_{n}\subset\cdots,\qquad I_{n}=I\cap K[X_{n}],

each
I_{n}
is
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
-stable with finite-dimensional graded pieces, so we may
decompose

[K[X_{n}]/I_{n}]_{d}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n}c_{\lambda}\,V^{\lambda}

into irreducible
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
-representations, each appearing with multiplicity
c_{\lambda}
. By the hook length formula

\dim_{K}V^{\lambda}=n!/\prod h_{\lambda}(i,j)
(see
[
21
]
), these dimensions grow
without bound with
n
, except for the two extremal partitions
\lambda=(n)
and

\lambda=(1^{n})
, the trivial and sign representations. The equivariant Hilbert series of

[
18
]
records this growth. To extract instead the
stable
behaviour as

n\to\infty
, the natural starting point is the trivial representation, whose
multiplicity does not grow with
n
; more generally, representation stability

[
3
]
suggests tracking the multiplicity of
V^{\mu(n)}
, the representation
indexed by a fixed partition
\mu
padded in its first part, which again stabilises. The
results of this paper address both settings.

The symmetric Hilbert series.

For each
n
, the subring of
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
-invariants

\bigl(K[X_{n}]/I_{n}\bigr)^{\!\textnormal{Sym}}\;\cong\;K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}/I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}

is a graded module over the ring of symmetric polynomials
K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
,
whose degree-
d
part has finite dimension over
K
for every
d
. Its Hilbert
series
H((K[X_{n}]/I_{n})^{\textnormal{Sym}};t)
is therefore well-defined. It is not hard to see that
these series stabilize as
n\to\infty
coefficient-wise, that is the coefficient of each
t^{d}

eventually becomes constant. Indeed, stabilization in each degree follows from the fact that
K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
is
generated by elementary symmetric polynomials
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
and stabilizes in
degree
d
once
n\geq d
, while
I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
grows monotonically and is bounded
above.
Therefore, we can consider a limit series and it is natural to ask whether this limit is rational. The key
condition we identify for rationality of the limit is:

(\dagger)

There exists an
f\in I
written
f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}m_{i}
with

c_{i}\in K^{\times}
and
m_{i}
monomials, such that

\Supp(m_{i})\not\subset\Supp(m_{1})
for all
i\neq 1
.

Here
\Supp(m)
denotes the set of variables appearing in
m
.
Condition
(\dagger)
holds trivially if
I
contains a monomial, and its role is to bound the rank of
the leading term in the symmetric limit, forcing the rank decomposition of

\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
into finitely many pieces each handled by Hilbert–Serre.

Moreover, a symmetric ideal generated by polynomials generic in their support must contain a monomial. This is a consequence of
[
12
]
. We see that condition
(\dagger)
holds for almost all symmetric ideals.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal. Then the limit

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}H\big((K[X_{n}]/I_{n})^{\textnormal{Sym}};\,t\big)

exists as a formal power series with non-negative integer coefficients.
Moreover, it is a rational function provided condition
(\dagger)
holds.

Condition
(\dagger)
also holds under a simple geometric condition on the zero
set of
I
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal, and let
\overline{K}
be
an algebraic closure of
K
. If
\overline{K}
is uncountable, then the limit
exists and is rational if there exist
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
such that

V_{\overline{K}}(I)\subset\Delta_{p}\cup H_{q}
,
where

\Delta_{p}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)\in\mathbb{A}^{\infty}_{\overline{K}}\mid a_{i}^{p}=a_{j}^{p}\text{ for all }i,j\}

is the
p
-th root of the diagonal, and

H_{q}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)}\sigma\big(\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{q},0,0,\dots)\mid a_{1},\dots,a_{q}\in\overline{K}\}\big)

is the union of all
q
-dimensional coordinate hyperplanes. The ideals

I(\Delta_{p})
and
I(H_{q})
are among the
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
-invariant prime ideals
characterized in
[
14
]
.

Ideals not satisfying
(\dagger)
may still yield rational symmetric Hilbert
series; we illustrate the subtleties in Section
4
.

Isotypic Hilbert series.

The methods extend naturally to all irreducible representations in the stable
range. Fix a partition
\mu\vdash n_{0}
with parts

\mu_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{\ell}>0
and define its
padding

\mu(n)=(n-n_{0}+\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{\ell})\vdash n
for
n\geq n_{0}
. For
n

large enough the multiplicity
c_{\mu(n),d}(n)
of
V^{\mu(n)}
in

[K[X_{n}]/I_{n}]_{d}
stabilizes, since in degree
d
only padded shapes
\mu(n)

with
|\mu|\leq d
can appear. The
isotypic Hilbert series
records these
limits. The stabilization of individual multiplicities was studied in the
semi-algebraic setting in
[
2
]
, where polynomial bounds on the
multiplicities of Specht modules in the cohomology of symmetric semi-algebraic
sets are established.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal satisfying condition

(\dagger)
. Then for every partition
\mu\vdash n_{0}
the isotypic Hilbert
series

H^{\mu}(K[X]/I;\,t)\;=\;\sum_{d\geq 0}\Bigl(\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{\mu(n),d}(n)\Bigr)\,t^{d}

is a rational function.

Thus condition
(\dagger)
guarantees rationality not only of the symmetric
Hilbert series (the trivial representation,
\mu=(n_{0})
) but of the stable
multiplicities along every family of irreducible representations. The
equivariant Hilbert series of Nagel and Römer
[
18
]
encodes the full
representation structure bivariately; our isotypic series extract individual
stable multiplicities as univariate rational functions, a complementary
perspective.

In this work we work mainly in the case where
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)

permutes the variables
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
. More generally, it is possible to consider the more general action when
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)

act on
k
-tuples, permuting the variables
x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,k}
of the infinite
polynomial ring
K[x_{i,j}\mid i\geq 1,\,1\leq j\leq k]
by the first index. In this more general setting in the case
k\geq 2
we have a considerably richer situation. While our methods focus on the
k=1
case, both the symmetric and isotypic
Hilbert series can be defined also in general setup. We return to the
k
-tuple case in Section
5
, and highlight with three examples when to hope for rationality in this case.

Structure of the paper.

In Section
2
we introduce the symmetric Hilbert series and establish its
stabilization. Section
3
develops the structure theory of the symmetric
limit
I_{\infty}
in the ring of symmetric functions and proves Theorem
1.1
.
Section
4
treats the hook Specht ideals, a natural family lying
outside condition
(\dagger)
, and computes their symmetric Hilbert series.
Section
5
proves Theorem
1.3
via block-symmetric
limits and Kostka inversion. In Subsection
5.5
we describe the
generalization to the polynomial ring in
k
-sets of variables and formulate a conjecture relating rationality to the
dimension of the value variety. Finally, we close with open questions in Section
6
.

2.
The Symmetric Hilbert Series

In this section we fix notation, define the symmetric Hilbert series, and
reformulate the problem in the language of symmetric functions.
Subsections
2.4
and
2.5
develop the
combinatorial theory of partitions that will be essential in Section
3
.

2.1.
The fundamental objects

We work over a field
K
of characteristic
0
. Let
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
denote the
symmetric group on
n
elements, and let

\textnormal{Sym}(\infty):=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\textnormal{Sym}(n)

be the union of all finite symmetric groups, viewed as permutations of
\mathbb{N}

that fix all but finitely many elements. The group
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
acts on

K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
by permuting variables:
\sigma\cdot x_{i}=x_{\sigma(i)}
.
Passing to the direct limit

K[X]:=K[x_{i}\mid i\geq 1]=\varinjlim K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}],

we obtain an action of
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
on the infinite polynomial ring. We
write
K[X_{n}]=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
for the truncation at
n
.

An ideal
I\subset K[X_{n}]
is
symmetric
if it is stable under
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
,
and an ideal
I\subset K[X]
is
symmetric
if it is stable under

\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
. Since every
f\in K[X]
involves only finitely many variables,
no nonzero polynomial is fixed by all of
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
, so there is no
meaningful ring of
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
-invariants in
K[X]
. This is why the limit
construction of Subsection
2.3
is necessary.

For a
K[X_{n}]
-module
M
equipped with a
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
-action, we write
M^{\textnormal{Sym}}

for the submodule of invariants, which is naturally a module over the ring of
symmetric polynomials
K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
. For a symmetric ideal
I\subset K[X_{n}]
, the
invariant ideal

I^{\textnormal{Sym}}
is an ideal in
K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
.

The polynomial rings
K[X_{n}]
and
K[X]
are graded by degree, as is

K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
. Since we work with Hilbert series throughout, we assume all
ideals to be homogeneous; this makes every quotient a graded ring as well.

Given a homogeneous ideal
I\subset K[X]
, its
truncation at
n
is

I_{n}:=I\cap K[X_{n}]
, a homogeneous
K[X_{n}]
-ideal that is symmetric whenever

I
is.

2.2.
The symmetric dimension

To give a good definition of the symmetric Hilbert series, we first need an intermediate definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal. Then the
symmetric dimension
of the quotient at degree
d
is the limit

\textnormal{SD}(K[X]/I,d)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dim_{K}\big[K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}/I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}\big]_{d}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal. Then the symmetric dimension
\textnormal{SD}(K[X]/I,d)
exists and is finite for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

By the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials, the ring of symmetric polynomials
K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
is generated by the first elementary symmetric polynomials
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
with
\deg e_{k}=k
.
Thus the sequence

\dim_{K}\big[K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}\big]_{d}

is constant for
n\geq d
.
We can bound

\dim_{K}\big[I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}\big]_{d}\leq\dim_{K}\big[K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}\big]_{d}

for any
n
,
and we have inclusions
I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}\hookrightarrow I_{n+1}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
given by
f\mapsto\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\sum_{\sigma\in\textnormal{Sym}(n+1)}\sigma\cdot f
. This shows that
\dim_{K}\big[I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}\big]_{d}
is eventually constant just as the dimension of
\big[K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}\big]_{d}
.
Thus the limit of the difference of the two dimensions,
\textnormal{SD}(K[X]/I,d)
, exists and is finite.
∎

Definition 2.3
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal.
The
symmetric Hilbert series
of
K[X]/I
is the formal power series

H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X]/I;\,t):=\sum_{d\geq 0}\textnormal{SD}(K[X]/I,d)\cdot t^{d}.

With this definition, the limit of Hilbert series of truncations in Theorem
1.1
is exactly the symmetric Hilbert series,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}H(K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}/I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}};t)=H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X]/I;t).

Now Lemma
2.2
already solves the existence part of the theorem.

2.3.
The symmetric limit

The key to the existence of the symmetric dimension was the embedding of invariant ideals. We can take this idea to its conclusion and construct a graded object whose degree-
d
part has dimension exactly equal to the symmetric dimension. This will be the
symmetric limit
of
I
. We construct it as a direct limit.

Definition 2.4
.

For each
n
, we have a
partial symmetrization
map
\phi_{n}
on
K[X]
given by

\phi_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\textnormal{Sym}(n)}\sigma\cdot f.

Lemma 2.5
.

For every
n,m
we have

(1)

\phi_{n}
is linear and respects grading.

(2)

\phi_{n}\circ\phi_{m}=\phi_{m}\circ\phi_{n}=\phi_{n}
if
n\geq m
.

(3)

\phi_{n}(sf)=s\phi_{n}(f)
if
s
is invariant under
\phi_{n}
.

Proof.

(1)
is clear, since the action of
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
on
K[X]
is linear and does not change degree.
For
(2)
, we can see that
\phi_{n}\circ\phi_{m}=\phi_{m}\circ\phi_{n}
by reordering the sums. The image of
\phi_{n}
is
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
-invariant and
\textnormal{Sym}(m)\subset\textnormal{Sym}(n)
, thus
\phi_{m}\circ\phi_{n}=\phi_{n}
.
For
(3)
we compute

\phi_{n}(sf)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\textnormal{Sym}(n)}\sigma\cdot(sf)=s\Big(\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\textnormal{Sym}(n)}\sigma\cdot f\Big)=s\phi_{n}(f).

∎

Points
(1)
and
(2)
show that partial symmetrization, restricted to
K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
and to
I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
, respectively, define a directed system.
This allows us to define their direct limit with respect to partial symmetrization. Note that since we only have maps of graded vector spaces, the resulting limit objects are only graded vector spaces. We will address this problem in the next section.

Definition 2.6
.

We call the direct limit of the ring of symmetric polynomials
the ring of symmetric functions
,

\Lambda:=\lim_{\longrightarrow}K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}.

Given a homogeneous symmetric ideal
I\subset K[X]
, we call the direct limit of the invariant part of its truncations
the symmetric limit
,

I_{\infty}:=\lim_{\longrightarrow}I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}.

We call the corresponding canonical homomorphism
the total symmetrization
,

\phi_{\infty}:K[X]\rightarrow\Lambda.

From the definition, it is clear that
\dim_{K}[\Lambda/I_{\infty}]_{d}=\textnormal{SD}(K[X]/I,d)
, thus the symmetric Hilbert series of
K[X]/I
is exactly
H(\Lambda/I_{\infty};t)
.

2.4.
Partitions and monomial symmetric functions

We recall the notation for integer partitions that will be used throughout.

Definition 2.7
.

A partition
\lambda\vdash d
is a weakly decreasing sequence

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell})
of nonnegative integers with sum
d
,
identified up to trailing zeros.
We write
\textnormal{len}(\lambda)
for the number of nonzero parts.
The dual partition
\lambda^{\perp}
is defined by

(\lambda^{\perp})_{i}:=\#\{j\mid\lambda_{j}\geq i\}.

The
rank
of
\lambda
is

\textnormal{rk}(\lambda)=\max\{i\mid\lambda_{i}\geq i\}
; by convention, the zero partition
has rank
0
.

For a monomial
m=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\dots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
, its

shape

\textnormal{sh}(m)
is the partition obtained by sorting

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})
in descending order.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\lambda\vdash d
with
\textnormal{len}(\lambda)\leq n
.
The (normalized) monomial symmetric polynomial
m_{\lambda}\in K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
is the normalized sum of all
monomials of shape
\lambda
:

m_{\lambda}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\textnormal{sh}(m)=\lambda}m,

where
N
is the number of monomials of shape
\lambda
in
K[X_{n}]
.

Such a monomial symmetric polynomial evaluates to
1
when all coordinates are
1
,

m_{\lambda}(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{n\textnormal{ times}})=1

and the image of
m_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
in
K[X_{n+k}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
under
\phi_{n+k}
is exactly
m_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+k})
.

Example 2.9
.

For partitions
\lambda=(2,1)
,
\mu=(4)
, we have monomial symmetric polynomials

m_{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{2}^{2}),\quad m_{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\frac{1}{6}(x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{3}+x_{1}x_{3}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}x_{3}+x_{2}x_{3}^{2})

and

m_{\mu}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{4}),\quad m_{\mu}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\frac{1}{3}(x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{4}+x_{3}^{4}).

For
n\geq d
, the polynomials
\{m_{\lambda}\mid\lambda\vdash d,\textnormal{len}(\lambda)\leq n\}
form a basis of
[K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}]_{d}
.
Passing to the direct limit yields the
monomial symmetric functions
(MSFs).
Identifying each MSF with its corresponding partition, we obtain a vector space basis for

\Lambda=\langle\lambda\mid\lambda\text{ a partition}\rangle_{K},

the graded vector space of symmetric functions.

In general,
\phi_{N}(m_{\lambda}m_{\mu})\neq\phi_{N}(m_{\lambda})\phi_{N}(m_{\mu})
for monomial symmetric polynomials in
K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
and
N>n
. Writing the product as
n
increases, we see that the coefficients of monomial symmetric polynomials corresponding to partitions with greatest length will eventually dominate. However, we can introduce two artificial products on
\Lambda
with desired properties. We explore this further in the next section.

Definition 2.10
.

For partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
:

(1)

The
disjoint product

\lambda\vee\mu
is the decreasing
rearrangement of all parts of
\lambda
and
\mu
.

(2)

The
joint product
is the entry-wise sum

\lambda\wedge\mu=(\lambda_{1}+\mu_{1},\;\lambda_{2}+\mu_{2},\dots),

and satisfies
\lambda\wedge\mu=(\lambda^{\perp}\vee\mu^{\perp})^{\perp}
.

Joint and disjoint product are analogously defined on MSFs and extend linearly to two product structures on
\Lambda
, which respect the grading.

Example 2.11
.

(1)

(3,1)^{\perp}=(2,1,1)
;

(2)

(3,2)\vee(4,1,1)=(4,3,2,1,1)
;

(3)

(4,1)\wedge(3,3,2)=(7,4,2)
.

Definition 2.12
.

For two partitions
\mu,\nu\vdash n
, we say
\mu

dominates

\nu
and write
\mu\trianglerighteq\nu
if

\mu_{1}+\dots+\mu_{k}\geq\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{k}\ \textnormal{for all}\ k.

This defines a partial order on partitions.

The dominance order will be relevant for Section
5
.

2.5.
A combinatorial order on partitions

To describe structural properties of
I_{\infty}
, we require a combinatorial
encoding of partitions.

Definition 2.13
.

Write a partition as
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}^{e_{1}},\dots,\lambda_{k}^{e_{k}})
with
distinct nonzero parts
\lambda_{i}
and multiplicities
e_{i}>0
.
The
string
of
\lambda
is the word

\textnormal{str}(\lambda):=1^{e_{1}}0^{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}1^{e_{2}}0^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}\cdots 1^{e_{k}}0^{\lambda_{k}}\in\{0,1\}^{*}.

Example 2.14
.

The partitions
\lambda=(3,3,1)
and
\mu=(5,2,1,1)
have

\textnormal{str}(\lambda)=110010,\qquad\textnormal{str}(\mu)=100010110.

The string traces the boundary of the Young diagram:
1
corresponds to a down
step and
0
to a left step.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{0,1\}^{*}/\!\sim
, where
s\sim 0s
and
s\sim s1
. It is the set of all finite sequences made from letters
1
and
0
identified up to leading
0
’s and trailing
1
’s.

Lemma 2.15
.

The map
str
induces a bijection between partitions and
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Every class in
\mathcal{P}
has a unique representative beginning with
1
and
ending with
0
.
Such strings correspond bijectively to sequences

1^{e_{1}}0^{d_{1}}\dots 1^{e_{k}}0^{d_{k}}
, and its unique preimage is given by

(\lambda_{1}^{e_{1}},\dots,\lambda_{k}^{e_{k}}),\qquad\lambda_{k}=d_{k},\;\lambda_{i}=d_{i}+\lambda_{i+1}.

∎

Definition 2.16
.

For partitions
\lambda
,
\mu
, the partition
\lambda

divides

\mu
, written
\lambda\mid\mu
, if
\textnormal{str}(\lambda)
is a
subsequence of
\textnormal{str}(\mu)
.

This defines a partial order on partitions. By Higman’s lemma
[
9
]

it is a well partial order: in every infinite sequence of partitions, some
earlier partition divides a later one. This fact will be crucial for the structural
analysis in Section
3
.

3.
Structure of the Symmetric Limit

The symmetric limit

I_{\infty}=\varinjlim I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}\subset\Lambda

associated to a homogeneous symmetric ideal
I\subset K[X]
inherits many “local” product structures from the finite truncations
I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
in
K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
. In this section we study its global structure with respect to the joint and disjoint product, which will lead to the proof of the main theorem. These two product structures on
\Lambda
manage to contain all necessary information when we restrict to initial MSFs after choosing a suitable partition order.

3.1.
The joint-disjoint closure

We first characterize subspaces of the ring of symmetric functions which are closed under both the joint and disjoint product.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
S\subset\Lambda
be any subset.
We define the
disjoint closure

\Lambda\vee S
as the
(\Lambda,\vee)
-ideal
generated by
S
. Furthermore, we define the
joint closure

\Lambda\wedge S
as the
(\Lambda,\wedge)
-ideal
generated by
S
. We say that a subset
S
is
joint–disjoint closed
if it is both a

(\Lambda,\vee)
-ideal and a
(\Lambda,\wedge)
-ideal.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\subset\Lambda
be a set of MSFs. Then

\Lambda\vee(\Lambda\wedge\mathcal{B})=\Lambda\wedge(\Lambda\vee\mathcal{B}).

In particular, this set is joint–disjoint closed.

Proof.

Let
f\in\Lambda
. By definition,
f\in\Lambda\vee(\Lambda\wedge\mathcal{B})

if and only if it can be written as a finite sum

f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\bigl(\nu_{i}\vee(\mu_{i}\wedge\lambda_{i})\bigr),

for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
c_{i}\in K
, partitions
\nu_{i},\mu_{i}
, and

\lambda_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
.

Similarly,
f\in\Lambda\wedge(\Lambda\vee\mathcal{B})
if and only if it is a
finite sum of expressions of the form

f=\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}c_{i}^{\prime}\bigl(\nu_{i}^{\prime}\wedge(\mu_{i}^{\prime}\vee\lambda_{i}^{\prime})\bigr),

with
\lambda_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
and partitions
\nu_{i}^{\prime},\mu_{i}^{\prime}
.

Thus it suffices to show that for any partitions
\mu,\nu
and any partition

\lambda
there exist partitions
\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}
such that

\mu\vee(\nu\wedge\lambda)=\nu^{\prime}\wedge(\mu^{\prime}\vee\lambda).

Fix
\lambda
and consider its string
\textnormal{str}(\lambda)
.
The disjoint product
\lambda\vee\mu
corresponds to inserting
1
’s into a
representative of
\textnormal{str}(\lambda)
in
\mathcal{P}
, and every way of inserting

1
’s into such a representative is realized by some disjoint product

\lambda\vee\mu
.
Similarly, the joint product
\lambda\wedge\mu
corresponds to inserting
0
’s
into a representative of
\textnormal{str}(\lambda)
, and every insertion of
0
’s arises in
this way.
This follows from the description of the joint product in terms of dual
partitions and the fact that the string of
\lambda^{\perp}
is obtained from

\textnormal{str}(\lambda)
by reversing order and swapping
0
and
1
.

Now fix a string
a
. If a string
b
can be obtained from
a
by first
inserting
1
’s and then inserting
0
’s, then
b
can be obtained equally well
by first inserting
0
’s and then inserting
1
’s.
Translating back to partitions, this shows that for any
\mu,\nu
there exist

\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}
with

\mu\vee(\nu\wedge\lambda)=\nu^{\prime}\wedge(\mu^{\prime}\vee\lambda),

as required.
∎

3.2.
A total monomial order in the ring of symmetric functions

We extend the partial order on partitions defined by divisibility to a total order
\geq
on the corresponding MSFs in
\Lambda
. This is an analogue of a monomial ordering in a polynomial ring. In a similar manner to a Gröbner basis helping to find the Hilbert series of a typical ideal, we will find a finite number of elements of
I_{\infty}
that, in a certain sense, serve the same role as a Gröbner basis. As it turns out, the most beneficial order is a reverse-length lexicographical order.

Let
\lambda,\mu
be two integer partitions. We say that
\lambda\geq\mu
if

(i)

\textnormal{len}(\lambda)<\textnormal{len}(\mu)
or

(ii)

\textnormal{len}(\lambda)=\textnormal{len}(\mu)
and the first nonzero entry of
(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i})_{i}
is positive.

Note that this implies both

\lambda\geq\mu\Rightarrow\lambda\vee\nu\geq\mu\vee\nu

and

\lambda\geq\mu\Rightarrow\lambda\wedge\nu\geq\mu\wedge\nu\quad\textnormal{if}\ \textnormal{len}(\nu)\leq\textnormal{len}(\lambda).

for any partition
\nu
. Thus the disjoint product preserves the monomial ordering. Any joint product
\lambda\wedge\nu
with
\ell_{1}=\textnormal{len}(\nu)>\textnormal{len}(\lambda)=\ell_{2}
can be replaced by a joint product with the first
\ell_{2}
entries of
\nu
and a disjoint product of the rest. Explicitly

\lambda\wedge\nu=(\lambda\wedge\nu^{\prime})\vee\nu^{\prime\prime}

where
\nu^{\prime}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{\ell_{2}})
and
\nu^{\prime\prime}=(\nu_{\ell_{2}+1},\dots,\nu_{\ell_{1}})
. This new operation preserves the monomial ordering as well. Hence
\geq
is indeed an extension of divisibility.

Definition 3.3
.

Given any symmetric function
f\in\Lambda
, there is a unique way to write it as a finite sum of MSFs. The
initial
MSF of
f
, denoted
\textnormal{init}(f)
, is the
\geq
-maximal such MSF.

Given a graded subspace
V\subset\Lambda
, its
initial space
is the graded vector space

\textnormal{init}(V):=\langle\textnormal{init}(f)\mid f\in V\rangle_{K}.

3.3.
The structure of the initial space

Taking the initial space of a symmetric limit
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
makes it into a joint-disjoint closed set. This leads to a finite generation result up to joint-disjoint closure.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal and
I_{\infty}
its symmetric limit in
\Lambda
. Then
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
is joint-disjoint closed.

Proof.

In both cases it suffices to check multiplication of MSFs, since
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
is, by definition, generated by MSFs. Let
\lambda\in\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
be any partition in the initial space. Then there exists an
f\in I_{\infty}
such that
\textnormal{init}(f)=\lambda
. We write it as

f=\lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\lambda_{i}

where
c_{i}\in K
and
\lambda_{i}
partitions for all
i=1,\dots,N
.
Fix any partition
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})
. Since
I_{\infty}
is a direct limit, there exists
n
large enough such that we can find a preimage of
f
,

f_{(n)}=m_{\lambda}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}m_{\lambda_{i}}\in I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}

where
m_{\lambda}
and
m_{\lambda_{i}}
are monomial symmetric polynomials in
K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
. In particular,
f_{(n)}\in I_{n}
and therefore

g_{(n)}:=\phi_{n+k}(f_{(n)}\cdot x_{n+1}^{\mu_{1}}\dots x_{n+k}^{\mu_{k}})\in I_{n+k}.

But
g_{(n)}
is also a symmetric polynomial, thus
g_{(n)}\in I_{n+k}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
. It is given by

g_{(n)}=m_{\mu\vee\lambda}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}m_{\mu\vee\lambda_{i}}

since the support of the monomial
x_{n+1}^{\mu_{1}}\dots x_{n+k}^{\mu_{k}}
is disjoint from the support of all monomials of
f_{(n)}
. Because
I_{\infty}
is a direct limit of the
I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
, we can conclude that

g:=\phi_{\infty}(g_{(n)})=\mu\vee\lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\cdot\mu\vee\lambda_{i}\in I_{\infty}.

The disjoint product is order preserving, thus
\textnormal{init}(g)=\mu\vee\lambda
and therefore
\mu\vee\lambda\in\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
as required.

For the second part, take
f
with
\textnormal{init}(f)=\lambda
as above and fix a partition
\mu
of length
k
. We want to show that
\lambda\wedge\mu\in\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
. If
k=\textnormal{len}(\mu)>\textnormal{len}(\lambda)=\ell
, we write the joint product as

\lambda\wedge\mu=(\lambda\wedge\underbrace{(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{\ell})}_{=:\mu^{\prime}})\vee\underbrace{(\mu_{\ell+1},\dots,\mu_{k})}_{=:\mu^{\prime\prime}}

as discussed in the previous section.
By the previous part of the proof, if
\lambda\wedge\mu^{\prime}\in\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
, then also
(\lambda\wedge\mu^{\prime})\vee\mu^{\prime\prime}
. Thus we may assume that
\textnormal{len}(\mu)\leq\textnormal{len}(\lambda)
.

For
n
large enough,
f_{(n)}\in I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
and
m_{\mu}\in K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
as above. Then
f_{(n)}\cdot m_{\mu}\in I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
. Recall that the monomial symmetric polynomials are normalized sums of monomials of their corresponding shape.
Writing the product as a sum of monomial symmetric polynomials

f_{(n)}\cdot m_{\mu}=m_{\lambda}m_{\mu}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}m_{\lambda_{i}}m_{\mu}

we see that the monomial symmetric polynomial corresponding to the maximal partition is exactly
c\cdot m_{\lambda\wedge\mu}
for some fraction
c
, since
\textnormal{len}(\mu)\leq\textnormal{len}(\lambda)
.
Hence
\textnormal{init}(f\cdot\mu)=c\cdot\lambda\wedge\mu\in\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
as required.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
V\subset\Lambda
be a graded linear subspace. Then
\dim_{K}{[V]}_{d}=\dim_{K}{[\textnormal{init}(V)]}_{d}
for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

This is a direct consequence of
[
8
, Theorem 15.3]
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
V\subset\Lambda
be a graded linear subspace that is joint-disjoint closed and
\textnormal{init}(V)=V
. Then there exists a finite set
\mathcal{B}\subset V
such that

\Lambda\vee(\Lambda\wedge\mathcal{B})=\Lambda\wedge(\Lambda\vee\mathcal{B})=V.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{\lambda\in V\mid\lambda\textnormal{ a MSF}\}
be the set of all MSFs in
V
. By assumption
V=\Lambda\vee(\Lambda\wedge\mathcal{B})
.

Let
(\lambda^{(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be any enumeration of the countably many elements of
\mathcal{B}
. We remove
\lambda^{(j)}
from the list, if there exists
i<j
with
\lambda^{(i)}\mid\lambda^{(j)}
using the notion of divisibility from Definition
2.16
. This gives rise to a new sequence
(\lambda^{(i)})_{i\in I}
and generating set
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}:=\{\lambda^{(i)}\mid i\in I\}
. By transitivity of divisibility, these are independent of the order of removal of the
\lambda^{(j)}
. According to our definition of divisibility, we still have

\Lambda\vee(\Lambda\wedge\mathcal{B}^{\prime})=V.

Divisibility is a well partial order and
(\lambda^{(i)})_{i\in I}
is a sequence which no longer has any divisible pairs
\lambda^{(i)}\mid\lambda^{(j)}
for
i<j
, thus it must be a finite sequence. This makes
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
a finite joint-disjoint generating set for
V
.
∎

This general result on finite generation combined with the fact that
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
is indeed joint-disjoint closed from Proposition
3.4
gives us a finite generation result for all symmetric limits of ideals in
K[X]
independent of condition
(\dagger)
.

The finite generation result is too general to show rationality of the symmetric Hilbert series. There exist many examples of finitely generated joint-disjoint closed spaces
V\subset\Lambda
that do not have a rational symmetric Hilbert series. We demonstrate this with a simple counterexample in the next section. We have to use an approach that does not rely on finite generation for the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

3.4.
Partition rank decomposition and examples

The following proof relies on the notion of rank of a partition from Definition
2.7
and the joint-disjoint closedness of the initial space of the symmetric limit.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal for which condition
(\dagger)
holds. We denote the symmetric limit of
I
as usual by
I_{\infty}
.

By Lemma
3.5
, we have

H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X]/I;t)=H(\Lambda/I_{\infty};t)=H(\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty});t).

We grade
\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
by partition rank.
Consider the vector space

R_{m}:=\langle\lambda\notin\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})\mid\textnormal{rk}(\lambda)=m\rangle_{K}

and note that we have a vector space decomposition

\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})=\bigoplus_{m=0}^{\infty}R_{m}.

With a slight shift in perspective, each
R_{m}
can be seen as a finitely generated module over the polynomial ring

A_{m}:=K[y_{1},z_{1},\dots,y_{m},z_{m}]

in
2m
variables.
Given any MSF
\lambda\in R_{m}
, we define
A_{m}
-multiplication by linearly extending the multiplication defined for monomials in
A_{m}
and MSFs in
\Lambda
,

y_{1}^{c_{1}}y_{2}^{c_{2}}\dots y_{m}^{c_{m}}z_{1}^{d_{1}}z_{2}^{d_{2}}\dots z_{m}^{d_{m}}\cdot\lambda:={(m^{d_{m}},\dots,2^{d_{2}},1^{d_{1}})}\vee\Big({(m^{c_{m}},\dots,2^{c_{2}},1^{c_{1}})^{\perp}}\wedge\lambda\Big).

By Proposition
3.4
,
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
is closed under this
A_{m}
-multiplication. We therefore only need to check that
A_{m}
-multiplication does not change the rank of a rank
m
partition and of course that this module structure is well-defined.

Multiplication with a monomial in the
y_{i}
only changes the first
m
entries of
\lambda
and
\textnormal{rk}(\lambda)=m
guarantees that multiplication with a monomial in the
z_{i}
can never change the first
m
entries, as it can only add new entries smaller than
m+1
. Thus neither multiplication with a monomial in the
y_{i}
nor multiplication with a monomial in the
z_{i}
can increase the rank of a MSF
\lambda
. Neither joint nor disjoint multiplication decrease the rank of a partition. This shows that
R_{m}
is closed under the defined multiplication.

Finally, we have
f\cdot(g\cdot\lambda)=(fg)\cdot\lambda
for any
f,g\in A_{m}
. It suffices to check this on monomials. The joint and disjoint products are commutative, so we only have to check
y_{i}\cdot(z_{j}\cdot\lambda)=z_{j}\cdot(y_{i}\cdot\lambda)
. This equality holds, since
y_{i}
only changes the first
m
entries of
\lambda
and multiplication with
z_{j}
disjointly adds entries smaller than
m+1
. But the rank of
\lambda
is
m
. Thus
z_{j}
-multiplication only affects entries of
\lambda
after the first
m
entries.
All other properties making
R_{m}
an
A_{m}
-module are clear. It is generated by the partition
(m^{m})
which is the rank
m
partition of minimal degree.

Recall that the monomial order
\geq
is the reverse-length lexicographical order. MSFs in
\Lambda
of shorter length will always be greater than longer MSFs using
\geq
.
The ideal
I
satisfies condition
(\dagger)
, so we find an
f\in I
with
f=m_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{N}c_{i}m_{i}
with
\Supp(m_{i})\not\subset\Supp(m_{1})
for
i\geq 2
as prescribed. By symmetry of
I
, we may assume that
\Supp(m_{1})=\{1,\dots,a\}
and all other monomials of
f
have support containing some number greater than
a
. We write
m_{1}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{a}^{\alpha_{a}}
and set
b=1+\max\{\alpha_{i}\mid i=1,\dots,a\}
. Then

f^{\prime}:=\phi_{\infty}(f\cdot x_{1}^{b-\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{a}^{b-\alpha_{a}})\in I_{\infty}

has initial MSF
(b^{a})
, since all other MSFs appearing in
f^{\prime}
are, by our construction, of greater length.

We see that by extending
(b^{a})
by joint and disjoint product, the MSF
(M^{M})
is an element of
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
for all
M\geq\max\{a,b\}
. In particular
R_{M}=\{0\}
for all such
M
.
Thus we can write

\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})=\bigoplus_{m=0}^{\max\{a,b\}-1}R_{m},

a finite sum of finitely generated modules over polynomial rings.
We have

H(\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty});t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\max\{a,b\}-1}H(R_{m};t).

By the Hilbert–Serre theorem, the Hilbert series of a graded, finitely generated module over a polynomial ring, such as
R_{m}
, is a rational function. A finite sum of rational functions is rational.
∎

The proof of Corollary
1.2
is now a simple computation, showing that the geometric condition implies
(\dagger)
.

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal with vanishing set

V_{\overline{K}}(I)\subset\Delta_{p}\cup H_{q}

where
\overline{K}
is the algebraic closure of
K
. The vanishing ideal of
\Delta_{p}
is

I(\Delta_{p})=(x_{i}^{p}-x_{j}^{p}\mid i,j\geq 1)

and the vanishing ideal of
H_{q}
is given by

I(H_{q})=(x_{i_{0}}\dots x_{i_{q}}\mid 1\leq i_{0}<i_{1}<\dots<i_{q}),

the ideal generated by all square-free monomials of sufficient length.
Let

f:=x_{1}\dots x_{q}x_{q+1}^{p}-x_{1}\dots x_{q}x_{q+2}^{p}\in I(\Delta_{p}\cup H_{q})

Since the cardinality of
\overline{K}
is uncountable by assumption, it is greater than the cardinality of
\mathbb{N}
which is the index set for our variables in
K[X]
. We can therefore use Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz in infinite-dimensional space, see
[
15
]
. This shows, just like in the finite dimensional case, that
I(\Delta_{p}\cup H_{q})\subset\sqrt{I}
, so
f\in\sqrt{I}
.

Consequently
f^{r}\in I
for some
r>0
, and
m=x_{1}^{r}\dots x_{q}^{r}x_{q+1}^{pr}
is the only term of
f^{r}
with support contained in
\{1,\dots,q+1\}
; all other monomials are divisible by
x_{q+2}
. Hence
I
satisfies
(\dagger)
and we can apply Theorem
1.1
showing that the symmetric Hilbert series of
K[X]/I
is rational.
∎

3.5.
Examples and a counterexample

We demonstrate that it is possible to compute the symmetric Hilbert series by hand in simple cases. We also give an example of a finitely generated joint-disjoint closed initial space in
\Lambda
which has a non-rational Hilbert series. This does not constitute a counterexample to rationality of the symmetric Hilbert series as this space is not the initial space of a symmetric limit.

Example 3.7
.

Let
I^{(1)}=I(\Delta_{1})
be the vanishing ideal of the diagonal. Thus

I^{(1)}=(x_{i}-x_{j}\mid i\neq j).

Clearly
K[X_{n}]/I_{n}^{(1)}\cong K[x]
and so
H\big((K[X_{n}]/I_{n}^{(1)})^{\textnormal{Sym}};t\big)=\frac{1}{1-t}
for all
n\geq 1
. So for the limit, we find

H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X]/I^{(1)};t)=\frac{1}{1-t}

as well.

Let
I^{(2)}=I(H_{N})
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
I^{(2)}\subset K[X]
is generated by all squarefree monomials
x_{i_{0}}\dots x_{i_{N}}
for
1\leq i_{0}<i_{1}<\dots<i_{N}
. Thus its symmetric limit
I_{\infty}\subset\Lambda
is generated by all partitions with
\textnormal{len}(\lambda)\geq N+1
and we already have
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})=I_{\infty}
. A simple counting argument shows

H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X]/I^{(2)};t)=H(\Lambda/I_{\infty};t)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{1-t^{k}}.

Example 3.8
.

Fix
\lambda=(2,1)
and let
V=\Lambda\wedge(\Lambda\vee\{\lambda\})
. This space is generated by a single element, up to joint-disjoint closure, yet the quotient
\Lambda/V
does not have a rational Hilbert series. Joint-disjoint finite generation is not enough to guarantee a rational Hilbert series.

We have
\textnormal{str}(\lambda)=1010
. Using the subsequence condition of divisibility, we see that
\lambda\nmid\mu
if either
\mu
is the empty partition, or
\textnormal{str}(\mu)=1^{a}0^{b}
for some
a,b\geq 1
. Such a partition is of form
\mu=(b^{a})
and degree
ab
. Thus
\Lambda/V=\langle\mu\mid\textnormal{str}(\mu)=1^{a}0^{b},a,b\geq 0\rangle_{K}
and the Hilbert series of the quotient is

H(\Lambda/V;t)=1+\sum_{a,b=1}^{\infty}t^{{ab}}=1+\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{a}}{1-t^{a}}

The last part is the well-known Lambert series, which allows the transformation

H(\Lambda/V;t)=1+\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{0}(d)t^{d}

where
\sigma_{0}(d)
denotes the number of divisors of
d
.
This is known not to be a rational function, since the number-of-divisors function does not follow a linear recurrence relation.

4.
Hook Specht Ideals

To conclude this paper, we want to highlight a natural class of ideals that do not satisfy condition
(\dagger)
, but have a rational symmetric Hilbert series nonetheless. These are the Specht ideals corresponding to hook partitions. This family of ideals is of particular interest, since
[
17
, Theorem 2]
shows that any symmetric ideal
I\subset K[X]
contains the hook Specht ideals corresponding to
(\infty,1^{r})
for all sufficiently large
r
.

4.1.
The main theorem for hook Specht ideals

The
Vandermonde matrix
for values
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
is

V(a_{0},\dots,a_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}1&a_{0}&a_{0}^{2}&\cdots&a_{0}^{n}\\
1&a_{1}&a_{1}^{2}&\cdots&a_{1}^{n}\\
1&a_{2}&a_{2}^{2}&\cdots&a_{2}^{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&a_{n}&a_{n}^{2}&\cdots&a_{n}^{n}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

The
Vandermonde polynomial
is the determinant

\det V(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{j}-x_{i}).

This lets us define

I=\big(\det V(x_{i_{0}},\dots,x_{i_{r}})\mid i_{0},\dots,i_{r}\geq 1\big)\subset K[X],

the
hook Specht ideal
corresponding to the infinity partition
(\infty,1^{r})
. This name comes from the fact that the truncated ideal
I_{n}=I\cap K[X_{n}]
is the Specht ideal corresponding to the hook partition
\lambda=(n-r,1^{r})
in
K[X_{n}]
. The vanishing set of
I
is the set of all sequences in
\mathbb{A}^{\infty}_{K}
that attain at most
r
different values in
K
. These ideals do not satisfy condition
(\dagger)
for
r\geq 2
.
We will nonetheless prove rationality of their symmetric Hilbert series:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
I
be the hook Specht ideal corresponding to the partition
(\infty,1^{r})
. Then the symmetric Hilbert series of
K[X]/I
is the rational function

H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X]/I;t)=\frac{1}{1-t}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{t^{2i}}{(1-t^{i})(1-t^{i+1})}.

4.2.
The space of Hankel matrices

Recall that a Hankel matrix is a matrix with constant counter diagonals.
Given a Vandermonde matrix with variables as entries, we consider its normalized product with its transpose

\frac{1}{n}V(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{T}V(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}1&p_{1}&\cdots&p_{n-1}\\
p_{1}&p_{2}&\cdots&p_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
p_{n-1}&p_{n}&\cdots&p_{2n-1}\end{pmatrix}.

The resulting matrix is a Hankel matrix in the power sums
p_{k}=\frac{1}{n}(x_{1}^{k}+\dots+x_{n}^{k})
. Generalizing this idea, we let
V_{n}
be the matrix with
n
rows and infinitely many columns such that
\big(V_{n}\big)_{i,j}=x_{i}^{j-1}
. It is an infinite, non-square, Vandermonde matrix. Then

A_{n}=\frac{1}{n}V_{n}^{T}V_{n}

is the infinite Hankel matrix with entries
\big(A_{n}\big)_{i,j}=p_{i+j-2}
in
K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
. We denote its limit for
n\rightarrow\infty
with entries in
\Lambda
by
A_{\infty}
. Note that
p_{k}
is the monomial symmetric polynomial corresponding to the length one partition
(k)
, thus its limit is exactly the MSF
(k)
.

We fix
I\subset K[X]
to be the hook Specht ideal corresponding to the partition
(\infty,1^{r})
and denote
I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}=I\cap K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}}
and
I_{\infty}
as its symmetric limit. We want to show that the
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minors of
A_{\infty}
generate the symmetric limit
I_{\infty}
as a
(\Lambda,\vee)
-ideal. This will let us compute its Hilbert series.

It is a theorem of Kronecker that an infinite Hankel matrix has finite rank if its entries are given by a univariate rational function. A precise statement where the rank of the Hankel matrix is given depending on the degree of the rational function can be found in section 4 of
[
20
]
. This lets us see that all
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minors of
A_{n}
vanish on a point in
\mathbb{A}^{n}_{K}
if its coordinates attain at most
r
distinct values. This coincides with the vanishing set of
I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
. Leading this idea to its conclusion requires working over an algebraically closed field, however. We will use more algebraic methods for a general proof.

4.3.
The symmetric limit of Hook Specht ideals

Recall that
\phi_{n}
denotes the partial symmetrization from Definition
2.4
and
\phi_{\infty}
denotes the total symmetrization.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be any homogeneous symmetric ideal and let
k
be any integer such that the
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
-orbits
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)\cdot I_{k}
generate
I
as a
K[X]
-ideal. Then
\phi_{\infty}(I_{k}^{\textnormal{Sym}})
generates
I_{\infty}
as a
(\Lambda,\vee)
-ideal.

Proof.

By assumption, polynomials of the form
\sigma(fm)
for
f\in I_{k}
,
\sigma\in\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
and
m=x_{1}^{a_{1}}\dots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
linearly generate
I
. We may assume that
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}=0
without loss of generality by absorbing variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
into
f\in I_{k}
. Partial symmetrization of truncations gives
\phi_{n}(I_{n})=I_{n}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
. Thus by
I_{n}\subset I
for all
n\geq 1
and the properties of the direct limit, we get
\phi_{\infty}(I)=I_{\infty}
. Since total symmetrization
\phi_{\infty}
is linear, we can complete the proof by checking that
\phi_{\infty}(\sigma(fm))
lies in the
(\Lambda,\vee)
-ideal generated by
\phi_{\infty}(I_{k}^{\textnormal{Sym}})
.

Since total symmetrization is
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
-invariant,
\phi_{\infty}(\sigma(fm))=\phi_{\infty}(fm)
. The support of
m
is disjoint from the first
k
variables, thus
m
is invariant under
\textnormal{Sym}(k)
-action. We can write
\phi_{k}(fm)=\phi_{k}(f)m
by Lemma
2.5
and thus
\phi_{\infty}(fm)=\phi_{\infty}(\phi_{k}(f)m)
. Let
\lambda
be the shape of
m
. Again, since the support of
m
is disjoint from
\{1,\dots,k\}
, we have

\phi_{\infty}(\phi_{k}(f)m)=\phi_{\infty}(\underbrace{\phi_{k}(f)}_{\in I^{\textnormal{Sym}}_{k}})\vee\lambda.

This lies in the
(\Lambda,\vee)
-ideal generated by
\phi_{\infty}(I_{k}^{\textnormal{Sym}})
as required.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
I
be the hook Specht ideal corresponding to
(\infty,1^{r})
. Then
I_{r+1}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
is linearly generated by the
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minors of the Hankel matrix
A_{r+1}
.

Proof.

We write
a(i_{1},\dots,i_{r+1})
for the
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minor of
V_{r+1}
of columns
i_{1}<\dots<i_{r+1}
. It is an alternating polynomial.
We further write
v=a(1,2,\dots,r+1)
for the Vandermonde polynomial in the first
r+1
variables. Basic theory of alternating polynomials
shows that the polynomials
a(i_{1},\dots,i_{r+1})
linearly generate the space of alternating polynomials in
r+1
variables and are all divisible by
v
. The quotients are the Schur polynomials as given by Jacobi’s bialternant formula.

Thus, combining these results, we see that the polynomials
a(i_{1},\dots,i_{r+1})\cdot a(j_{1},\dots,j_{r+1})
linearly generate the
K
-space
I^{\textnormal{Sym}}_{r+1}
. This product of two minors is the same as the minor of rows
i_{1},\dots,i_{r+1}
and columns
j_{1},\dots,j_{r+1}
of the product
V_{r+1}^{T}V_{r+1}=(r+1)\cdot A_{r+1}
.

Hence the minors of the Hankel matrix
A_{r+1}
indeed linearly generate
I_{r+1}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
I
be the hook Specht ideal corresponding to
(\infty,1^{r})
. Then its symmetric limit
I_{\infty}
is generated as a
(\Lambda,\vee)
-ideal by the
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minors of the infinite Hankel matrix
A_{\infty}
.

Proof.

By definition of the hook Specht ideal,
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)\cdot I_{r+1}
generates all of
I
as a
K[X]
-ideal. Thus we can apply Lemma
4.2
and find that
\phi_{\infty}(I_{r+1}^{\textnormal{Sym}})
generates
I_{\infty}
as a
(\Lambda,\vee)
-ideal. Now Proposition
4.3
gives us exactly that
I_{r+1}^{\textnormal{Sym}}
is linearly generated by the
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minors of
A_{r+1}
.

Let
m
be such a minor of rows
i_{1},\dots,i_{r+1}
and columns
j_{1},\dots,j_{r+1}
. Then it is divisible by
x_{1}^{i_{1}+j_{1}-2}
. Since
m
is a symmetric polynomial, it is therefore divisible by the monomial
x_{1}\dots x_{r+1}
if either
i_{1}>1
or
j_{1}>1
. In particular, all terms that are not divisible by
x_{1}\dots x_{r+1}
must cancel out when computing the determinant

m=\begin{vmatrix}p_{i_{1}+j_{1}-2}&\cdots&p_{i_{1}+j_{r+1}-2}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
p_{i_{r+1}+j_{1}-2}&\cdots&p_{i_{r+1}+j_{r+1}-2}\end{vmatrix}

and replacing
p_{k}
by
\frac{1}{r+1}(x_{1}^{k}+\dots+x_{r+1}^{k})
in the resulting expression.
We apply this to the Leibniz formula

m=\sum_{\sigma\in\textnormal{Sym}(r+1)}\textnormal{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{k=1}^{r+1}p_{i_{k}+j_{\sigma(k)}-2}

to simplify the products, as we only need to consider terms divisible by
x_{1}\dots x_{r+1}
.
Thus we can write
m
in terms of
x_{1},\dots,x_{r+1}
as

m=\frac{(r+1)!}{(r+1)^{r+1}}\sum_{\sigma\in\textnormal{Sym}(r+1)}\textnormal{sgn}(\sigma)\phi_{r+1}\Big(\prod_{k=1}^{r+1}x_{k}^{i_{k}+j_{\sigma(k)}-2}\Big).

Note that this expression still holds for
i_{1}=j_{1}=1
.
The limit of this expression in
\Lambda
is the minor of
A_{\infty}
under disjoint product up to constant factor
\frac{(r+1)!}{(r+1)^{r+1}}
. Thus
I_{\infty}
is indeed generated by the
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minors of
A_{\infty}
.
∎

4.4.
Computing the Hilbert series

We managed to describe the symmetric limit of hook Specht ideals as a determinantal ideal of an infinite Hankel matrix. This is highly useful as determinantal ideals of Hankel matrices are well understood. In particular, a Gröbner basis for such an ideal was constructed in
[
6
]
. This Gröbner basis generalizes to the case of infinitely many variables without difficulty and lets us describe the Hilbert series of
\Lambda/I_{\infty}
by counting monomials in
\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
. This is an infinite sum of rational functions, but with substantial effort, it is possible to simplify this to the expression seen in Theorem
4.1
.

We adopt the notation from
[
6
]
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
<_{1}
be the strict partial order on
\mathbb{N}
defined by

i<_{1}j\quad\textnormal{if and only if}\quad i+1<j

and
>_{L}
be the reverse-length lexicographical monomial order on
(\Lambda,\vee)
seen as a polynomial ring in
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},\dots
induced by

p_{1}>p_{2}>p_{3}>\dots
.

A sequence of numbers
s=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
is called a
<_{1}
-chain
of length
k
if
s_{1}>_{1}s_{2}>_{1}\dots>_{1}s_{k}
.
We say that a partition or MSF
\lambda
is
divisible
by a
<_{1}
-chain of length
k
if it contains a subsequence of length
k
that is a
<_{1}
-chain. Note that this includes the trailing zeroes of every partition we usually suppress in our notation.

Proposition 4.6
.

The
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minors of
A_{\infty}
form a Gröbner basis for
I_{\infty}
with respect to
>_{L}
.

Proof.

Let
R=K[y_{0},\dots,y_{n}]
be some polynomial ring in
n+1
variables and
J
be the ideal generated by all
(r+1)\times(r+1)
Hankel minors in
y_{0},\dots,y_{n}
. We fix a degree lexicographical monomial order on
R
induced by
y_{0}>y_{1}>\dots>y_{n}
. Following
[
6
, Proposition 3.4]
and the subsequent discussion, we find that the
(r+1)\times(r+1)
Hankel minors form a Gröbner basis for
J
and in particular
\textnormal{init}(J)
is generated by squarefree monomials
y_{i_{1}}\dots y_{i_{r+1}}
where the indices form a
<_{1}
-chain,
i_{1}<_{1}\dots<_{1}i_{r+1}
.

We consider the homomorphism
\psi:R\longrightarrow\Lambda
given by
\psi(y_{i}):=p_{i}
. It is not surjective as
\psi(y_{0})=1
, but it fixes the monomial order for fixed degree: Let
m,m^{\prime}
be two monomials in
[R]_{d}
with
m<m^{\prime}
. Then
\psi(m)<_{L}\psi(m^{\prime})
. This homomorphism maps Hankel minors to Hankel minors.

Let
m=p_{1}^{a_{1}}\dots p_{\ell}^{a_{\ell}}
be any monomial in
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
. For
n
large enough, we find a preimage of
m
in
R
. It is of the form
m^{\prime}=y_{0}^{N}y_{1}^{a_{1}}\dots y_{\ell}^{a_{\ell}}
and for
N
large enough, it must lie in
\textnormal{init}(J)
. Thus
m^{\prime}
is divisible by a monomial of the form
y_{i_{1}}\dots y_{i_{r+1}}
where the indices form a
<_{1}
-chain. Hence we can conclude that the MSF
\psi(m^{\prime})
is divisible by a
<_{1}
-chain of length
r+1
. All initial monomials of Hankel minors in
\Lambda
are divisible by
<_{1}
-chains. Thus the initial monomials of the Hankel minors in
\Lambda
already generate
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
. They must form a Gröbner basis.
∎

As we have seen in the proof, the initial ideal
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
with respect to
>_{L}
is linearly generated by all partitions which are divisible by a
<_{1}
-chain of length
r+1
.
We can stratify
\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
as follows: let
P_{k}
be the
K
-space of partitions in
\Lambda
that are divisible by a length
k

<_{1}
-chain, but not a length
k+1

<_{1}
-chain. Then if
I
is the hook Specht ideal corresponding to
(\infty,1^{r})
,

\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}P_{k}.

A partition in
P_{k}
is of the form

\lambda=((s_{1}+1)^{a_{1}},s_{1}^{b_{1}},(s_{2}+1)^{a_{2}},s_{2}^{b_{2}},\dots,(s_{k-1}+1)^{a_{k-1}},s_{k-1}^{b_{k-1}},1^{a_{k}})

for a
<_{1}
-chain
s_{1}>_{1}\dots>_{1}s_{k-1}>_{1}0
. Here the exponents
a_{i}
can be any nonnegative integers and the
b_{i}
any positive integers. This description is bijective, which allows us to compute the Hilbert series

H(P_{k};t)=\sum_{\bm{s},a,b}t^{a_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}t^{(s_{i}+1)a_{i}+s_{i}b_{i}},

where we sum over all
<_{1}
-chains
\bm{s}
,
s_{1}>_{1}\dots>_{1}s_{k-1}>_{1}0
and
a_{i}\geq 0
,
b_{i}\geq 1
.
Summing over the
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
is independent of the rest of the summation, so we can simplify

H(P_{k};t)=\frac{1}{1-t}\sum_{\bm{s}}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{t^{s_{i}}}{(1-t^{s_{i}})(1-t^{s_{i}+1})}

To keep the equations concise, we write

B(s,k)=\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(1-t^{s+i}),\qquad A(s,k)=\frac{t^{ks+k(k-1)}}{B(s,k)}.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let

H(s,k):=\sum_{s_{1}>_{1}\dots>_{1}s_{k}>_{1}s}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{1-t}\big(A(s_{i},1)-A(s_{i}+1,1)\big)

This infinite sum of rational functions can be simplified to the rational function

H(s,k)=\frac{A(s+2,k)}{B(1,k)}.

We will need the following identity several times.

(4.1)

\frac{1-t^{k}}{B(s,k+1)}=\frac{1}{B(s,k)}-\frac{t^{k}}{B(s+1,k)}

It can be shown by a simple computation

\frac{1-t^{k}}{B(s,k+1)}=\frac{(1-t^{s+k})-t^{k}(1-t^{s})}{B(s,k+1)}=\frac{(1-t^{s+k})}{B(s,k+1)}-\frac{t^{k}(1-t^{s})}{B(s,k+1)}=\frac{1}{B(s,k)}-\frac{t^{k}}{B(s+1,k)}.

Combining these results with the previous discussion allows us to finally prove Theorem
4.1
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
I
be the hook Specht ideal corresponding to
(\infty,1^{r})
. Then Corollary
4.4
shows that it is generated by the
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minors of the infinite Hankel matrix
A_{\infty}
as an
(\Lambda,\vee)
-ideal and Proposition
4.6
confirms that this generating set is already a Gröbner basis with respect to the monomial order
>_{L}
. With the subsequent discussion, we see that we can therefore decompose
\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}P_{k}
.
Using identity (
4.1
), we see that
H(P_{k};t)=\frac{1}{1-t}H(0,k-1)
. Thus using Proposition
4.7
, we find

\displaystyle H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X]/I;t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{r}H(P_{k};t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-t}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}H(0,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-t}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\frac{t^{(k+1)k}}{B(1,k)B(2,k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-t}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{t^{2i}}{(1-t^{i})(1-t^{i+1})}.

∎

Proof of Proposition
4.7
.

We use induction over
k
. The case
k=0
is clear since
H(s,0)=1
for all
s
. Now assume

H(s,k^{\prime})=\frac{A(s+2,k^{\prime})}{B(1,k^{\prime})}

for all
k^{\prime}<k
.
Factoring out common terms of the form
\frac{1}{1-t}\big(A(s_{i},1)-A(s_{i}+1,1)\big)
in the infinite sum expression of
H(s,k)
lets us write it as a nested sum

H(s,k)=\sum_{s_{k}>_{1}s}\frac{A(s_{k},1)-A(s_{k}+1,1)}{1-t}\sum_{s_{k-1}>_{1}s_{k}}\frac{A(s_{k-1},1)-A(s_{k-1}+1,1)}{1-t}\dots\sum_{s_{1}>_{1}s_{2}}\frac{A(s_{1},1)-A(s_{1}+1,1)}{1-t}.

This can be simplified to

H(s,k)=\sum_{s_{k}>_{1}s}\frac{A(s_{k},1)-A(s_{k}+1,1)}{1-t}H(s_{k},k-1)=\sum_{s_{k}>_{1}s}\frac{A(s_{k},1)-A(s_{k}+1,1)}{1-t}\cdot\frac{A(s_{k}+2,k-1)}{B(1,k-1)},

where the second equality follows from the induction assumption. We now use identity (
4.1
) and the definition of
A(s,k)
and
B(s,k)
to simplify

(4.2)

\displaystyle H(s,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{k}>_{1}s}\frac{A(s_{k},1)-A(s_{k}+1,1)}{1-t}\cdot\frac{A(s_{k}+2,k-1)}{B(1,k-1)}

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{k}>_{1}s}\frac{t^{s_{k}}}{(1-t^{s_{k}})(1-t^{s_{k}+1})}\cdot\frac{t^{(s_{k}+2)(k-1)+(k-1)(k-2)}}{B(1,k-1)B(s_{k}+2,k-1)}

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{k}>_{1}s}\frac{t^{ks_{k}+k(k-1)}}{B(1,k-1)B(s_{k},k+1)}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B(1,k)}\sum_{s_{k}>_{1}s}\frac{(1-t^{k})t^{ks_{k}+k(k-1)}}{B(s_{k},k+1)}

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B(1,k)}\sum_{s_{k}>_{1}s}\Big(\frac{t^{ks_{k}+k(k-1)}}{B(s_{k},k)}-\frac{t^{k(s_{k}+1)+k(k-1)}}{B(s_{k}+1,k)}\Big)

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B(1,k)}\sum_{s_{k}>_{1}s}\big(A(s_{k},k)-A(s_{k}+1,k)\big)

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{A(s+2,k)}{B(1,k)}

We go through the simplification step by step. At (
4.2
) we use the induction assumption. At (
4.3
) we use identity (
4.1
) on the left fraction and the definition of
A(s,k)
on the right fraction. At (
4.4
) we combine the fractions, simplify the exponent of the numerator and absorb
(1-t^{s_{k}})(1-t^{s_{k}+1})
into
B(s_{k}+2,k-1)
to get
B(s_{k},k+1)
. At (
4.5
) we expand the fraction by
\frac{1-t^{k}}{1-t^{k}}
, then absorb the denominator into
B(1,k-1)
to get
B(1,k)
. This is independent of
s_{k}
, thus we pull it out of the sum.
At (
4.6
) we use identity (
4.1
) which allows us to rewrite terms inside of the sum in terms of
A(s,k)
in (
4.7
). Finally, at (
4.8
) we use a telescoping series to obtain the final result and complete the induction.
∎

5.
Isotypic Hilbert Series

The symmetric Hilbert series captures the contribution of the trivial representation to the
graded pieces of the quotient
K[X_{n}]/I_{n}
. In this section we extend Theorem
1.1

to arbitrary irreducible representations in the
stable range
. The key idea is to replace
full symmetrization by partial symmetrization with respect to Young subgroups, and then to
recover individual multiplicities via Kostka inversion.

5.1.
Padded partitions and stable multiplicities

Fix a partition
\mu\vdash n_{0}
with
\ell
parts. For every
n\geq n_{0}
we define the

padded partition

\mu(n)\;=\;(n-n_{0}+\mu_{1},\,\mu_{2},\,\dots,\,\mu_{\ell})\;\vdash\;n.

Note that
\mu(n_{0})=\mu
and that
\mu(n)
is obtained from
\mu
by adding
n-n_{0}
to the
first part. For
\mu=(n_{0})
the trivial partition,
\mu(n)=(n)
corresponds to the trivial
representation, and we recover the setting of the preceding sections.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal. The
isotypic Hilbert series

of
K[X]/I
associated to
\mu
is the formal power series

H^{\mu}(K[X]/I;\,t)\;=\;\sum_{d\geq 0}\Bigl(\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{\mu(n),d}(n)\Bigr)\,t^{d},

where
c_{\mu(n),d}(n)
denotes the multiplicity of the irreducible representation
V^{\mu(n)}
in the

\textnormal{Sym}(n)
-representation
[K[X_{n}]/I_{n}]_{d}
.

The existence of the limit and the rationality of the series will follow from the next two
subsections.

5.2.
Block-symmetric limits

The
Young subgroup
associated to
\mu(n)
is

S_{\mu(n)}\;=\;\textnormal{Sym}(\{1,\dots,n\!-\!n_{0}\!+\!\mu_{1}\})\;\times\;\textnormal{Sym}(\{n\!-\!n_{0}\!+\!\mu_{1}\!+\!1,\dots,n\!-\!n_{0}\!+\!\mu_{1}\!+\!\mu_{2}\})\;\times\;\cdots\;\times\;\textnormal{Sym}(\{n\!-\!\mu_{\ell}\!+\!1,\dots,n\}).

As
n
increases, only the first factor grows; the remaining
\ell-1
factors are isomorphic
to fixed symmetric groups
\textnormal{Sym}(\mu_{2}),\dots,\textnormal{Sym}(\mu_{\ell})
.

The ring of
S_{\mu(n)}
-invariants decomposes as

K[X_{n}]^{S_{\mu(n)}}\;\cong\;K[X_{n-n_{0}+\mu_{1}}]^{\textnormal{Sym}(n-n_{0}+\mu_{1})}\;\otimes_{K}\;R_{\mu},

where

R_{\mu}\;=\;K[X_{\mu_{2}}]^{\textnormal{Sym}(\mu_{2})}\otimes_{K}\cdots\otimes_{K}K[X_{\mu_{\ell}}]^{\textnormal{Sym}(\mu_{\ell})}

is a polynomial ring in
\mu_{2}+\cdots+\mu_{\ell}=n_{0}-\mu_{1}
generators of degrees

1,\dots,\mu_{2};\;1,\dots,\mu_{3};\;\dots;\;1,\dots,\mu_{\ell}
. Here we use the fundamental
theorem of symmetric polynomials in each block.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal. For each
n\geq n_{0}
, denote by

I_{n}^{S_{\mu(n)}}
the subspace of
S_{\mu(n)}
-invariants in
I_{n}
.

Define the
shifted inclusion

\iota_{s}\colon K[X_{n}]\rightarrow K[X_{n+1}]
by

\iota_{s}(x_{k})=x_{k+1}
. For
f\in I_{n}
, the image
\iota_{s}(f)=f(x_{2},\dots,x_{n+1})

lies in
I_{n+1}
: the substitution
x_{k}\mapsto x_{k+1}
is realised by the permutation

\tau=(1,\,2,\,\dots,\,n,\,n+1)\in\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
, and
I
is
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
-invariant.

Define the
block-symmetric partial symmetrization

\phi_{n}^{\mu}\colon K[X_{n}]\rightarrow K[X_{n}]^{S_{\mu(n)}}

by averaging over
S_{\mu(n)}
:

\phi_{n}^{\mu}(f)\;=\;\frac{1}{|S_{\mu(n)}|}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{\mu(n)}}\sigma\cdot f.

For increasing
n
, the images
I_{n}^{S_{\mu(n)}}
form a directed system under the maps

\phi_{n+1}^{\mu}\circ\iota_{s}
. This is well-defined: since
\iota_{s}(f)\in I_{n+1}
and

I_{n+1}
is
S_{\mu(n+1)}
-invariant,
\phi_{n+1}^{\mu}(\iota_{s}(f))\in I_{n+1}^{S_{\mu(n+1)}}
.

The
block-symmetric limit
is the direct limit

I_{\infty}^{\mu}\;=\;\varinjlim\,I_{n}^{S_{\mu(n)}}\;\subset\;\Lambda\otimes_{K}R_{\mu},

where
\Lambda=\varinjlim K[X_{n}]^{\textnormal{Sym}(n)}
as in Definition
2.6
.
For each
n
, we have a limit map, the
block-symmetric total symmetrization

\phi_{\infty}^{\mu}:\,K[X_{n}]\rightarrow\Lambda\otimes_{K}R_{\mu}.

Lemma 5.3
.

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
, the block-symmetric dimension

\textnormal{SD}^{\mu}(K[X]/I,\,d)\;=\;\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dim_{K}\bigl[K[X_{n}]^{S_{\mu(n)}}/I_{n}^{S_{\mu(n)}}\bigr]_{d}

exists and is finite.

Proof.

The argument parallels that of Lemma
2.2
and is a direct adaption. Note that the first tensor factor

K[X_{n-n_{0}+\mu_{1}}]^{\textnormal{Sym}(n-n_{0}+\mu_{1})}
is generated by elementary symmetric polynomials
of degrees
1,\dots,n-n_{0}+\mu_{1}
. Therefore, the space
[K[X_{n}]^{S_{\mu(n)}}]_{d}
is constant for

n-n_{0}+\mu_{1}\geq d
, i.e., for
n\geq d+n_{0}-\mu_{1}
. The inclusions

\phi_{n+1}^{\mu}\circ\iota_{s}{:}\ I_{n}^{S_{\mu(n)}}\hookrightarrow I_{n+1}^{S_{\mu(n+1)}}
show that

\dim_{K}[I_{n}^{S_{\mu(n)}}]_{d}
is non-decreasing and bounded, hence eventually constant.
∎

The block-symmetric Hilbert series

H^{S_{\mu}}(K[X]/I;\,t)\;=\;\sum_{d\geq 0}\textnormal{SD}^{\mu}(K[X]/I,\,d)\,t^{d}\;=\;H\bigl(\Lambda\otimes R_{\mu}/I_{\infty}^{\mu};\,t\bigr)

is therefore a well-defined formal power series with non-negative integer coefficients.

5.3.
Rationality of the block-symmetric Hilbert series

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal satisfying condition
(\dagger)
, and let

\mu\vdash n_{0}
. Then the block-symmetric Hilbert series
H^{S_{\mu}}(K[X]/I;\,t)
is a rational
function.

Proof.

The strategy follows the proof of Theorem
1.1
, with the polynomial ring
R_{\mu}

carried along as an additional finitely generated factor.

Step 1: Joint-disjoint closedness of the initial space.

We equip
\Lambda\otimes_{K}R_{\mu}
with the monomial order that first compares the
\Lambda
-factor
using the reverse-length lexicographic order
\geq
from Section
3.2
, and breaks ties by degree
lexicographic order on
R_{\mu}
. For this we need to define a monomial basis on
R_{\mu}
.

Let
\ell
be the length of
\mu
. By the theorem of symmetric polynomials, we can write the tensor factor of
R_{\mu}
corresponding to
\textnormal{Sym}(\mu_{k})
explicitly as
K[e_{1},\dots,e_{{\mu_{k}}}]
where
e_{i}
is the
i
-th elementary symmetric polynomial in
\mu_{k}
-variables. Thus we can write
R_{\mu}
formally as a polynomial ring

R_{\mu}\cong K\big[e_{i_{k}}^{k}\mid 2\leq k\leq\ell,\ 1\leq i_{k}\leq\mu_{k}\big]
, where the variable
e_{i}^{k}
has degree
i
. We obtain a degree lexicographical order on
R_{\mu}
by some order of the variables
e_{i_{k}}^{k}
.

Given
f\in\Lambda\otimes R_{\mu}
, its
initial monomial

\textnormal{init}(f)
is the maximal monomial
\lambda\otimes\mathbf{r}
appearing.

We claim that the initial space
\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty}^{\mu})\subset\Lambda\otimes R_{\mu}
is

joint-disjoint closed in the
\Lambda
-factor
: if
\lambda\otimes\mathbf{r}\in\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty}^{\mu})
and
\nu
is any partition, then

(\nu\vee\lambda)\otimes\mathbf{r}\in\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty}^{\mu})
and

(\nu\wedge\lambda)\otimes\mathbf{r}\in\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty}^{\mu})
.

For the disjoint product, we adapt the argument of
Proposition
3.4
. Given
f\in I_{\infty}^{\mu}
with

\textnormal{init}(f)=\lambda\otimes\mathbf{r}
and a partition
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{k})
, lift
f

to
f_{(n)}\in I_{n}^{S_{\mu(n)}}
for
n
large. Apply
\iota_{s}
a total of
k
times to
obtain
f_{(n)}(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n+k})\in I_{n+k}
(as a composition of
k
shifted
inclusions, each realised by a permutation in
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
). For
n
large enough,
the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
all lie in Block
1
at stage
n+k

(since
|\text{Block}_{1}^{(n+k)}|=n+k-n_{0}+\mu_{1}\geq k
) and are disjoint from the support
of
f_{(n)}(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n+k})
. Set

g_{(n+k)}\;=\;\phi_{n+k}^{\mu}\bigl(f_{(n)}(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n+k})\cdot x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots x_{k}^{\nu_{k}}\bigr)\;\in\;I_{n+k}^{S_{\mu(n+k)}}.

Since the monomial
x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots x_{k}^{\nu_{k}}
is supported in Block
1
and
disjoint from the support of the shifted
f_{(n)}
, the same argument as in
Proposition
3.4
gives
\textnormal{init}(g)=(\nu\vee\lambda)\otimes\mathbf{r}
.

For the joint product, we only need to consider the case
\textnormal{len}(\nu)\leq\textnormal{len}(\lambda)
. This follows from the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
3.4
. The ideal
I_{n}^{S_{\mu(n)}}
is a module over the invariant
ring
K[X_{n-n_{0}+\mu_{1}}]^{\textnormal{Sym}(n-n_{0}+\mu_{1})}
of the first block. Multiplication of
f_{(n)}

by a monomial symmetric polynomial
m_{\nu}\in K[X_{n-n_{0}+\mu_{1}}]^{\textnormal{Sym}(n-n_{0}+\mu_{1})}
preserves

I_{n}^{S_{\mu(n)}}
. The product
m_{\lambda}m_{\nu}
in the finitely generated ring has initial MSF proportional
to
m_{\lambda\wedge\nu}
, since
\textnormal{len}(\nu)\leq\textnormal{len}(\lambda)
and the
R_{\mu}
-component of
f_{(n)}
is unaffected. Passing to the
limit gives
(\nu\wedge\lambda)\otimes\mathbf{r}\in\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty}^{\mu})
.

Step 2: Rank bound from condition
(\dagger)
.

Let
f\in I
satisfy
(\dagger)
:
f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}m_{i}
with

\Supp(m_{i})\not\subset\Supp(m_{1})
for
i\geq 2
. By symmetry of
I
, we may assume

\Supp(m_{1})=\{1,\dots,a\}\subset\{1,\dots,n-n_{0}+\mu_{1}\}
for
n
sufficiently large, so that

m_{1}
is supported entirely in the growing first block. Write
m_{1}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{a}^{\alpha_{a}}
and set
b=1+\max\{\alpha_{i}\}
. Then

f^{\prime}\;=\;\phi_{\infty}^{\mu}\bigl(f\cdot x_{1}^{b-\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{a}^{b-\alpha_{a}}\bigr)\;\in\;I_{\infty}^{\mu}

has initial monomial
(b^{a})\otimes 1
, since all other terms have strictly greater length in the

\Lambda
-factor. Extending
(b^{a})
by joint and disjoint products in
\Lambda
shows that

\lambda\otimes\mathbf{r}\in\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty}^{\mu})
for all
\lambda
with

\textnormal{rk}(\lambda)\geq M:=\max\{a,b\}
and all
\mathbf{r}\in R_{\mu}
.

Step 3: Rank decomposition.

Exactly as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we decompose

\Lambda\otimes R_{\mu}/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty}^{\mu})\;=\;\bigoplus_{m=0}^{M-1}R_{m}^{\mu},

where
R_{m}^{\mu}=\langle\lambda\otimes\mathbf{r}\notin\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty}^{\mu})\mid\textnormal{rk}(\lambda)=m\rangle_{K}
. Each
R_{m}^{\mu}
is a finitely generated graded module over

A_{m}\otimes R_{\mu}\cong K[y_{1},z_{1},\dots,y_{m},z_{m}]\otimes R_{\mu}
, which is a polynomial ring in

2m+(n_{0}-\mu_{1})
variables. By the Hilbert–Serre theorem,
H(R_{m}^{\mu};\,t)
is a rational
function, and

H^{S_{\mu}}(K[X]/I;\,t)\;=\;\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}H(R_{m}^{\mu};\,t)

is a finite sum of rational functions, hence rational.
∎

5.4.
Kostka inversion and isotypic rationality

We now pass from block-symmetric invariants to individual multiplicities.
For an introduction to Kostka numbers, see
[
21
, Section 2.11]
.

Lemma 5.5

(Stable Kostka matrix)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\vdash n_{0}
. Then for all
n
sufficiently large (specifically,
n\geq 2n_{0}
), the
Kostka number
K_{\nu(n),\mu(n)}
is independent of
n
. We denote this stable value by

\overline{K}_{\nu,\mu}
.

Proof.

Recall that
K_{\nu(n),\mu(n)}
counts the number of semi-standard Young tableaux (SSYT) of
shape
\nu(n)
and content
\mu(n)
. The content
\mu(n)
has
n-n_{0}+\mu_{1}
entries equal to
1
,
followed by
\mu_{2}
entries equal to
2
, etc.

Since columns of a SSYT are strictly increasing, all entries equal to
1
must appear in the first row. In particular, the inequality
\nu_{1}\geq\mu_{1}
is necessary for
K_{\nu(n),\mu(n)}\neq 0
.
For
n\geq 2n_{0}
, the first
n-n_{0}+\mu_{1}>n_{0}\geq\nu_{2}
entries of the first row of the SSYT of shape
\nu(n)
are forced to be
1
. Removing the first cell of the first row thus yields a SSYT of shape
\nu(n-1)
and content
\mu(n-1)
, since the columns are still strictly increasing. This is the case as the condition of strictly increasing columns is only nontrivial for the first
\nu_{2}
cells of the first row.

This removal of the first cell defines a bijection of SSYTs of shape
\nu(n)
and content
\mu(n)
to SSYTs of shape
\nu(n-1)
and content
\mu(n-1)
.
Therefore the Kostka numbers are equal and we have
K_{\nu(n),\mu(n)}=K_{\nu(2n_{0}),\mu(2n_{0})}
for
n\geq 2n_{0}
.
∎

The matrix
\overline{K}=(\overline{K}_{\nu,\mu})_{\nu,\mu\vdash n_{0}}
is unitriangular with
respect to the dominance order on partitions of
n_{0}
, and hence invertible over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Proposition 5.6

(Frobenius reciprocity)
.

Let
M
be any finite-dimensional
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
-representation. Then

\dim_{K}M^{S_{\mu(n)}}\;=\;\sum_{\nu\vdash n}K_{\nu,\mu(n)}\,c_{\nu}(M),

where
c_{\nu}(M)
is the multiplicity of
V^{\nu}
in
M
.

Proof.

This is a standard consequence of Frobenius reciprocity. Let
\chi
be the character of the
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
-representation
M
,
\chi_{\nu}
the character of the irreducible representation
V^{\nu}
, and
\bm{1}
the character of the trivial representation of
S_{\mu(n)}
. We use the up and down arrow notation for characters of restricted and induced representations following
[
21
]
. Then

\displaystyle\dim_{K}M^{S_{\mu(n)}}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{1},\,\chi{\downarrow}_{S_{\mu(n)}}\rangle_{S_{\mu(n)}}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{1}{\uparrow}^{\textnormal{Sym}(n)},\,\chi\rangle_{\textnormal{Sym}(n)}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{\nu\vdash n}K_{\nu,\mu(n)}\chi_{\nu},\,\chi\Big\rangle_{\textnormal{Sym}(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu\vdash n}K_{\nu,\mu(n)}\,\langle\chi_{\nu},\,\chi\rangle_{\textnormal{Sym}(n)},

and
\langle\chi_{\nu},\chi\rangle=c_{\nu}(M)
. The second equality is Frobenius reciprocity and the third equality is due to the well-known decomposition of the permutation module, see
[
21
, Theorem 2.11.2]
.
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be a homogeneous symmetric ideal satisfying condition
(\dagger)
. Then
for every partition
\mu\vdash n_{0}
the isotypic Hilbert series
H^{\mu}(K[X]/I;\,t)
is a rational
function.

Proof.

Fix some
\mu\vdash n_{0}
. We may assume that
\mu_{1}\geq n_{0}-\mu_{1}
, simply by replacing
\mu
with
\mu(2n_{0})
and noting that this does not change the corresponding block-symmetric Hilbert series.

By Proposition
5.6
, we have for
n\geq n_{0}
a decomposition of the
S_{\mu(n)}
-invariant submodule

\dim_{K}\big[K[X_{n}]/I_{n}\big]_{d}^{S_{\mu(n)}}=\sum_{\nu\vdash n}K_{\nu,\mu(n)}c_{\nu,d}(n)

where, as usual,
c_{\nu,d}(n)
denotes the multiplicity of the irreducible representation
V^{\nu}
in
\big[K[X_{n}]/I_{n}\big]_{d}
. If
\nu
does not dominate the partition
\mu(n)
, then
K_{\nu,\mu(n)}=0
. We can exclude all such cases from our summation. Otherwise, we have
\nu\trianglerighteq\mu(n)
and in particular
\nu_{1}\geq\mu_{1}+n-n_{0}
. Using our assumption
\mu_{1}\geq n_{0}-\mu_{1}
in the second step and
n\geq\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}
in the third step, we get

\displaystyle\nu_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{1}+n-n_{0}

\displaystyle\geq n_{0}-\mu_{1}+n-n_{0}

\displaystyle\geq-\mu_{1}+\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\mu_{1}+\mu_{1}+n-n_{0}+\nu_{2}

\displaystyle=\nu_{2}+n-n_{0}.

Hence we can define
\nu^{\prime}:=(\nu_{1}-n+n_{0},\,\nu_{2},\,\nu_{3},\,\dots)
, which is a partition of
n_{0}
. We have
\nu^{\prime}(n)=\nu
by construction.

Following this argument, we see that for any
n
large enough,

\dim_{K}\big[K[X_{n}]/I_{n}\big]_{d}^{S_{\mu(n)}}=\sum_{\nu\vdash n_{0}}K_{\nu(n),\mu(n)}c_{\nu(n),d}(n)

which is a sum with a constant number of terms as
n
grows. This lets us compute the limit for
n\rightarrow\infty
, in terms of block-symmetric dimensions and stable Kostka numbers,

(5.1)

\textnormal{SD}^{\mu}(K[X]/I,\,d)\;=\;\sum_{\nu\vdash n_{0}}\overline{K}_{\nu,\mu}\;\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}c_{\nu(n),d}(n)

The system (
5.1
) for all
\mu\vdash n_{0}
has coefficient matrix
(\overline{K}_{\nu,\mu})
, which is unitriangular with respect to
dominance order and hence invertible over
\mathbb{Z}
.
Writing the inverse as

(\overline{K}^{-1})_{\mu,\nu}\in\mathbb{Z}
, we obtain

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}c_{\nu(n),d}(n)\;=\;\sum_{\mu\vdash n_{0}}(\overline{K}^{-1})_{\mu,\nu}\;\textnormal{SD}^{\mu}(K[X]/I,\,d).

As a generating function in
t
:

H^{\nu}(K[X]/I;\,t)\;=\;\sum_{\mu\vdash n_{0}}(\overline{K}^{-1})_{\mu,\nu}\;H^{S_{\mu}}(K[X]/I;\,t).

By Theorem
5.4
, each
H^{S_{\mu}}(K[X]/I;\,t)
is rational. A finite

\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of rational functions is rational.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

(1)

The case
\nu=(1^{n_{0}})
yields the isotypic Hilbert series for the sign representation:

\nu(n)=(n-n_{0}+1,1^{n_{0}-1})
, a hook partition. This is the second “extremal” case
mentioned in the introduction.

(2)

The Kostka inversion expresses individual multiplicities as integer linear combinations
of block-symmetric dimensions. Since the inverse Kostka matrix has entries in
\mathbb{Z}
, the coefficients of
H^{\nu}
are integers, as expected from representation
theory.

(3)

An analogous result holds under the geometric condition of Corollary
1.2
:
if
V_{\overline{K}}(I)\subset\Delta_{p}\cup H_{q}
, then all isotypic Hilbert series are rational.

5.5.
Final remarks

We close this article with a discussion of rationality of the isotypic Hilbert series outside of condition
(\dagger)
and possible directions of future work.

The zero ideal
I=(0)\subset K[X]
is certainly a symmetric ideal and it is not difficult to compute its symmetric Hilbert series

H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X];\,t)=H(\Lambda;\,t)=\prod_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-t^{d}}

which is not a rational function. Using Kostka inversion and the block-symmetric Hilbert series, it follows that the isotypic Hilbert series
H^{\mu}(K[X];\,t)
is not a rational function for any partition
\mu
either.

However, counterexamples for rationality of the isotypic Hilbert series begin and end here. Moreover, this behaviour should be expected:
K[X]
is the coordinate ring of
infinite
affine space
\mathbb{A}^{\infty}_{K}
while the vanishing set of any nonzero symmetric ideal
I\subset K[X]
corresponds to a
finite
dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\infty}
. This is a fundamental difference between
I=(0)
and all other symmetric ideals in
K[X]
.

The main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.1
was the decomposition of
\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
into finitely many spaces of fixed rank, each equipped with a different module structure over a finite polynomial ring.
This is mirrored in the proof of Theorem
4.1
where we decompose
\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
into parts of fixed
<_{1}
-chain divisibility.
Here, there is no clear module structure for each
P_{k}
, but we were able to show rationality of each part by a complicated counting argument. That such a counting argument yields a rational generating function should be taken as evidence that there is more underlying structure to be uncovered.
Moreover, it seems plausible that once the structure of the hook Specht ideal quotients is better understood, it would allow for the extension of rationality to all symmetric ideals containing a hook Specht ideal.

Taking the above discussion as well as the lack of counterexamples into consideration, we propose the following.

Conjecture 5.9
.

Let
I\subset K[X]
be any homogeneous symmetric ideal. Then the isotypic Hilbert series
H^{\mu}(K[X]/I;t)
is a rational function for any partition

\mu
if
I\neq(0)
.

For future work, a formulation of the theory of isotypic Hilbert series for finitely generated graded modules of graded
FI
-algebras would be of interest.
In particular, the infinite polynomial ring with a diagonal permutation action on more than one infinite set of variables is an interesting generalization.

The polynomial ring in
k
infinite sets of variables
K[X^{1},\,\dots,\,X^{k}]
with
X^{j}=\{x_{i,j}\mid i\geq 1\}
has a natural action of
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
on the first index given by
\sigma\cdot x_{i,j}=x_{\sigma(i),j}
.
The definitions for the symmetric and isotypic Hilbert series generalize without much difficulty to quotients of such rings. This is also the more general setup in works such as
[
13
,
14
,
18
]
and ideals closed under this
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
-action still satisfy the ascending chain condition.

We can, however, immediately note that we have no hope of Conjecture
5.9
holding for such a group action.
Nonetheless, it seems reasonable to believe that the isotypic Hilbert series will allow for a finite description in terms of rational functions and a finite set of “basic” formal power series as building blocks.
In particular, general rationality of the isotypic Hilbert series of
K[X^{1},\dots,X^{k}]/I
under the assumption that the vanishing set
V(I)
is finite dimensional seems possible.

We illustrate this with three examples for the infinite polynomial ring in two sets of variables.

Example 5.10
.

Denote the “basic” formal power series

P(t)=\prod_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-t^{d}}.

We consider homogeneous and
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
-closed ideals in
K[X,Y]=K[x_{i},y_{i}\mid i\geq 1]

I^{(1)}=(x_{i}y_{j}\mid i,j\geq 1),\quad I^{(2)}=(x_{i}y_{i}\mid i\geq 1),\quad I^{(3)}=(x_{i}x_{j},\,y_{i}y_{j}\mid i,j\geq 1,i\neq j).

As in the case of only one set of variables, we denote the truncation at the first
n
variables for
X
and
Y
by
K[X_{n},Y_{n}]
. Then
I^{(\ell)}_{n}=I^{(\ell)}\cap K[X_{n},Y_{n}]
for
\ell=1,2,3
.

The ring
K[X_{n},Y_{n}]/I^{(1)}_{n}
contains exactly those monomials solely in either
x_{i}
or
y_{i}
. Thus

\big[K[X_{n},Y_{n}]/I^{(1)}_{n}\big]_{d}\cong\big[K[X_{n}]\big]_{d}\oplus\big[K[Y_{n}]\big]_{d}

for
d\geq 1
and we can conclude that
H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X,Y]/I^{(1)};\,t)=2P(t)-1
, since
P(t)
is the symmetric Hilbert series of
K[X]
.

The analysis for the second case is subtly different. The quotient
K[X_{n},Y_{n}]/I^{(2)}_{n}
contains exactly those monomials not divisible by both
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
for each
i
. Averaging such a monomial over
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
always gives a product of monomial symmetric polynomials

m_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})m_{\mu}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})

modulo
I^{(2)}_{n}
. The limit is therefore isomorphic to the tensor product
\Lambda\otimes_{K}\Lambda
. This finally gives us the symmetric Hilbert series
H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X,Y]/I^{(2)};\,t)=P(t)^{2}
.

For the third case, we note that, excluding constants, all monomials in
K[X_{n},Y_{n}]/I^{(3)}_{n}
are either of the form
x_{i}^{a},y_{i}^{b}
for
a,b\geq 1
, which we call first form, or of the form
x_{i}^{a}y_{i}^{b},x_{i}^{a}y_{j}^{b}
for
i\neq j
and
a,b\geq 1
, which we call second form. This holds for any
n\geq 1
, and averaging over
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
lets us find a basis for
\big(K[X_{n},Y_{n}]/I^{(3)}_{n}\big)^{\textnormal{Sym}}
. This yields the Hilbert series

H\Big({\big(K[X_{n},Y_{n}]/I^{(3)}_{n}}\big)^{\textnormal{Sym}};\,t\Big)=1+\underbrace{\frac{2t}{1-t}}_{\textnormal{first form}}+\underbrace{\frac{2t^{2}}{(1-t)^{2}}}_{\textnormal{second form}}=\frac{1+t^{2}}{(1-t)^{2}}.

This Hilbert series is constant for all
n\geq 1
and thus equal to the symmetric Hilbert series
H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X,Y]/I^{(3)};\,t)
. It is in particular a rational function.

The three ideals suggest that in the multisymmetric setting rationality is governed by
one geometric quantity: the dimension of the set of values a sequence attains. For a
point
p\in V(I)(\overline{K})
, viewed as a sequence
(p_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in

\mathbb{A}^{k}(\overline{K})
with
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
permuting the index
i
, let

V_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{k}_{\overline{K}}
be the Zariski closure of its set of values

\{p_{i}\mid i\geq 1\}
, the
value variety
of
p
(cf.
[
14
, Def. 3.2]
, whose
ambient dimension
n
is our number of variable sets
k
), and set

\dim V(I):=\sup_{p}\dim V_{p}\leq k
. Since a zero-dimensional closed subset of
\mathbb{A}^{k}
is
finite,
\dim V(I)=0
holds precisely when every point of
V(I)
attains only finitely
many values.

The transcendental contribution of a
d
-dimensional value component is a universal
series. For
d\geq 0
write

P_{d}(t):=\prod_{m\geq 1}\bigl(1-t^{m}\bigr)^{-\binom{m+d-1}{d-1}}.

This is the symmetric Hilbert series
H^{\textnormal{Sym}}(K[X^{1},\dots,X^{d}];t)
of the free quotient
on
d
sets of variables: a basis of the invariants is given by the finite multisets of
nonzero exponent vectors
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, of which
\binom{m+d-1}{d-1}
have degree

m=|\alpha|
. In particular
P_{0}=1
and
P_{1}=P
, and each
P_{d}
with
d\geq 1
is
non-rational, having a pole at every root of unity.

Conjecture 5.11
.

Let
I\subset K[X^{1},\dots,X^{k}]
be a homogeneous
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
-invariant ideal. Then
for every partition
\mu
,

H^{\mu}\bigl(K[X^{1},\dots,X^{k}]/I;\,t\bigr)=\sum_{j}R_{j}(t)\,\prod_{i}P_{d_{ji}}(t),

with
R_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}(t)
and each
d_{ji}\geq 1
the dimension of a positive-dimensional
component of a value variety of
V(I)
. In particular
H^{\mu}
is rational for every

\mu
if and only if
\dim V(I)=0
.

This fits every computed case:
I^{(1)},I^{(2)}
give
2P_{1}-1
and
P_{1}^{2}
, the sum and
the product reflecting whether the two branches of
\{XY=0\}
are populated by one
global site or independently, while
I^{(3)}
gives a rational series. It also recovers
Conjecture
5.9
at
k=1
: a point with
\dim V_{p}\geq 1
has
V_{p}=\mathbb{A}^{1}
,
whose orbit closure is all of
\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\infty}

[
14
, Thm. 3.5]
, so
\dim V(I)\geq 1

forces
I=(0)
; equivalently, by the classification of invariant primes

[
14
, Ex. 3.13]
, the zero ideal is the only one of positive dimension. This is
what singles out
(0)
at the start of the section. The sufficient direction seems
within reach, since for
\dim V(I)=0
the methods of Theorem
1.1
and
Section
5
should apply with little change; necessity would follow from
the
P_{d}
,
d\geq 1
, being non-cancellable in the
\mathbb{Z}
-combinations from Kostka
inversion, which we leave open.

6.
Conclusion and open questions

We have shown that the symmetric and isotypic Hilbert series of a homogeneous symmetric
ideal are rational under condition
(\dagger)
, and the same for the symmetric Hilbert series
for the hook Specht ideals outside of it; along the way the dimension
\dim V(I)

The most immediate concerns the mechanism behind rationality itself. Under
condition
(\dagger)
we obtained the symmetric Hilbert series as an instance of the
Hilbert–Serre theorem, by exhibiting
\Lambda/\textnormal{init}(I_{\infty})
as a finite direct sum
of finitely generated modules over polynomial rings. For the hook Specht ideals no such
module structure was available, and rationality came instead from an explicit counting
argument over the strata
P_{k}
. That two such different routes lead to the same
conclusion suggests an underlying structure we have not yet identified. It would be
highly interesting to place the hook case—and, more ambitiously, every ideal with

\dim V(I)=0
—on a common module-theoretic footing, realising
\Lambda/I_{\infty}
as a
finitely generated graded module over a polynomial ring whose dimension can be read off
the geometry of
V(I)
. Such a description would account for rationality uniformly
rather than case by case, and would render the polynomial growth rate of

\textnormal{SD}(K[X]/I,d)
a computable invariant of that geometry. At present we have such a
structure only under
(\dagger)
, and the hook computation stands as evidence that it
should exist more widely.

The hook ideals also point to a concrete next computation. They are the Specht ideals
of the shapes
(\infty,1^{r})
, and it is natural to ask whether the two-row shapes

(\infty,2^{a},1^{b})
, and eventually arbitrary shapes, admit a comparable treatment. Our
argument rested on identifying the symmetric limit with a determinantal ideal of an
infinite Hankel matrix, for which a Gröbner basis is known; whether the higher Specht
ideals carry an analogous structured-matrix description seems worth pursuing, and would
considerably enlarge the class of ideals whose symmetric Hilbert series can be written
down explicitly.

Finally, the theory invites a real counterpart. For a symmetric basic closed
semi-algebraic set the cohomology stabilises as an
\textnormal{Sym}(n)
-module

, and one
may ask whether the resulting stable Betti-number multiplicities assemble into an
isotypic Poincaré series, rational under a dimension hypothesis of the kind isolated
here. In a different direction, the whole construction rests on the ring of symmetric
functions and the combinatorics of partitions; it would be interesting to see how much
survives when
\textnormal{Sym}(\infty)
is replaced by the hyperoctahedral tower
B_{\infty}
, where
partitions give way to pairs of partitions. We hope to return to some of these questions
elsewhere.
