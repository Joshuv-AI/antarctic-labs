Ischemic Stroke Caused by Vertebral Artery Compression at C1 with a Floating Thrombus in an Incomplete Arcuate Foramen
Authors: Kei Miyata, Shogo Fujita, Yoshitaka Yamaguchi, Tatsuro Takada, Daisuke Shimbo, Sogo Oki, Katsuyuki Asaoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveVertebral artery (VA) compression associated with an arcuate foramen (AF) is a rare cause of ischemic stroke. We report a case of ischemic stroke caused by VA compression at the C1 level with a floating thrombus, highlighting the pathophysiology and treatment strategy.Case PresentationAn older woman presented with vertigo and gait disturbance. MRI demonstrated acute infarcts in the bilateral posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) territories. Angiographic studies revealed focal stenosis with a floating thrombus in the left VA at the C1 level (V3 segment), associated with compression by adjacent bony structures, as well as a stenotic lesion in the right VA at the V2 segment. CTA demonstrated external compression of the left VA associated with a left incomplete AF. Serial MRA demonstrated fluctuating visualization of the right vertebrobasilar system before treatment. Given that posterior circulation perfusion depended on the dominant right VA and hemodynamic compromise was suspected, revascularization of the right VA with stent placement was performed first to improve antegrade flow. This was followed by elimination of the embolic source by parent artery occlusion of the left VA in the same session.ConclusionVA compression associated with an AF may result in ischemic stroke primarily through artery-to-artery embolism, with possible contribution from hemodynamic compromise. Treatment strategies should be tailored based on both the embolic mechanism and the hemodynamic status of the vertebrobasilar circulation.

Introduction

The arcuate foramen (AF), also known as the “ponticulus posticus,” is an anatomical variation of the atlas (C1) characterized by an abnormal bony bridge over the groove for the vertebral artery (VA).1) It is classified into complete and incomplete types depending on the extent of osseous formation, and is thought to be either congenital, resulting from persistence of embryological structures, or acquired due to ossification of the posterior atlanto-occipital ligament associated with aging or mechanical stress. The reported prevalence of AF is approximately 5%–15%.2) Although AF is often asymptomatic, it may cause mechanical compression of the VA, particularly at the V3 segment, leading to arterial stenosis, dissection, or hemodynamic insufficiency.3) In rare cases, repetitive mechanical stress may result in endothelial injury and subsequent thrombus formation, which can become a source of artery-to-artery embolism in the posterior circulation. The optimal management strategy for symptomatic VA lesions associated with AF remains controversial. Both surgical decompression and endovascular treatment have been reported; however, treatment selection should be tailored according to the underlying pathophysiology, including the presence of thrombus, hemodynamic status, and anatomical characteristics of the lesion.4–7)

We report a rare case of ischemic stroke caused by VA compression at the C1 level associated with an incomplete AF and a floating thrombus, highlighting the mechanism of thrombus formation and artery-to-artery embolism.

Case Presentation

A woman in her early 80s presented with vertigo and gait disturbance. MRI and MRA on admission demonstrated acute infarcts in the left posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) territory, as well as additional infarcts in the right PICA territory (Fig. 1), along with poor visualization of the left VA from the V3 to V4 segment. Prominent bilateral fetal-type posterior communicating arteries were noted, suggesting partial collateral supply to the posterior circulation (Fig. 2A). Following admission, dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel was initiated with loading doses, in combination with continuous intravenous argatroban infusion.

DSA on day 2 demonstrated focal stenosis in the left VA with a floating thrombus at the C1 level (V3 segment) (Fig. 3A). Rotational angiography of the left VA suggested external compression by adjacent bony structures and demonstrated focal vessel wall irregularity with an associated floating thrombus on 3D maximum intensity projection images (Fig. 3B–3E). In addition, a stenotic lesion was observed in the right VA at the V2 segment (Fig. 4), with the degree of stenosis estimated at approximately 77% according to the Warfarin-Aspirin Symptomatic Intracranial Disease (WASID) criteria. CTA demonstrated the presence of a left incomplete AF at the corresponding level (Fig. 5A–5C). Although MRI revealed a retro-odontoid pseudotumor suggesting potential local instability (Fig. 5D), orthopedic evaluation ruled out severe cervical instability requiring surgical fixation.

Serial MRA demonstrated fluctuating visualization of the right vertebrobasilar system. While the signal was preserved on admission (day 0; Fig. 2A), it was markedly reduced or absent on imaging obtained the day before treatment (day 8; Fig. 2B), whereas visualization of the left VA was improved.

Based on these findings, the left VA lesion was considered the embolic source due to the presence of a floating thrombus, while posterior circulation perfusion was considered to depend primarily on the right VA. Given these findings, a treatment strategy consisting of right VA revascularization with stent placement and left VA parent artery occlusion (PAO) was selected. The endovascular procedure was performed on day 9.

Endovascular treatment

Endovascular treatment was performed under local anesthesia with intravenous sedation. The procedure was performed via bilateral distal transradial access using a solo distal access catheter system.

First, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and stent placement were performed for the right VA using a Gateway balloon (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) and a Wingspan stent (Stryker) (Fig. 6A) to secure stable antegrade flow. Angiography of the left VA performed prior to PAO demonstrated reduction, but not resolution, of the floating thrombus; therefore, residual embolic risk was considered to persist (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, PAO of the left VA was performed using detachable coils (HydroSoft 3D; Terumo Neuro, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA).

(B) Microcatheter positioned in the left VA prior to coil embolization for PAO, showing the targeted segment proximal to the lesion (anteroposterior view). (C, D) Final angiography demonstrating complete occlusion of the left VA and preserved perfusion of the left posterior inferior cerebellar artery via the vertebrobasilar union from the right VA. The coil mass used for parent artery occlusion is indicated by an asterisk (C, anteroposterior view; D, lateral view).

Postprocedural angiography confirmed complete occlusion of the left VA and preserved perfusion of the left PICA via the vertebrobasilar union from the right VA (Fig. 6C and 6D). No perioperative complications were observed. The patient’s symptoms gradually improved, and no new neurological deficits occurred after the procedure. Dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel was continued for 3 months after the procedure, followed by aspirin monotherapy. No symptoms such as syncope or neurologic deterioration were observed with neck rotation or flexion–extension after the procedure.

At 5-month follow-up, dynamic DSA demonstrated transient occlusion of the right VA during both neck extension and rightward neck rotation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Collateral filling from the ascending cervical artery, deep cervical artery, and occipital artery was observed during positional occlusion of the right VA. The patient has remained asymptomatic without recurrent ischemic events under aspirin monotherapy. Based on the findings of follow-up dynamic angiography, avoidance of excessive neck movements, particularly rightward rotation and extension, was recommended. Surgical decompression remains a potential treatment option should recurrent symptoms or ischemic events develop.

Discussion

In the present case, ischemic stroke was considered to be caused by artery-to-artery embolism from a floating thrombus associated with mechanical compression of the VA at the C1 level due to an incomplete AF. To our knowledge, only a limited number of cases of symptomatic VA stenosis associated with an AF have been reported,3,8,9) and reports describing a floating thrombus at the site of compression are particularly rare. The present case is notable in that a floating thrombus was clearly visualized at the mechanically compressed V3 segment, strongly suggesting artery-to-artery embolism related to repetitive mechanical stress. Mechanical compression of the VA at the C1 level has also been described in the context of rotational VA occlusion, or so-called Bow hunter’s syndrome, in which dynamic positional changes induce transient flow compromise.10) Although positional dynamic evaluation was not performed, the lesion location and anatomical relationship strongly suggested a mechanism of mechanical compression related to the incomplete AF.

Differentiation from atherosclerotic disease is essential when evaluating VA stenosis. Atherosclerotic lesions are more commonly observed in the V1 and V4 segments, whereas involvement of the V3 segment, particularly at the C1 level, is relatively uncommon. In the present case, the lesion was focal and corresponded precisely to the location of the incomplete AF, without evidence of calcification or plaque formation on imaging. These findings support a non-atherosclerotic etiology and are consistent with mechanical compression as the primary mechanism.

3D rotational angiography demonstrated that the floating thrombus was in contact with the vessel wall at the site of focal compression by the AF, suggesting endothelial injury due to repetitive mechanical stress. Repetitive mechanical stress at the compressed segment may cause endothelial injury, leading to thrombus formation. Free-floating thrombus is recognized as a high-risk embolic source, most commonly described in the carotid artery, although similar mechanisms may occur in the VA.11) The presence of a floating thrombus at the C1 level strongly suggested artery-to-artery embolism as the primary mechanism of infarction.

In addition, the right VA was dominant, whereas the left VA was non-dominant. Serial MRA demonstrated fluctuating visualization of the right vertebrobasilar system prior to treatment, suggesting hemodynamic instability of the right VA. The mechanism underlying the infarction in the right PICA territory remains uncertain and may have involved either hemodynamic compromise or embolic events related to the right VA lesion.

With regard to treatment strategy, both reconstructive and deconstructive approaches were considered. Stent placement at the C1 level was avoided due to the potential risk of stent fracture or deformation caused by repetitive cervical motion. In contrast, PAO was selected to eliminate the embolic source in the presence of a floating thrombus. Previous reports have described both surgical and endovascular treatments for Bow hunter’s syndrome and related conditions, including cases treated with coil embolization.5,7,12) However, optimal treatment strategies remain controversial, particularly in cases with coexisting embolic and hemodynamic factors.

A one-stage treatment strategy was selected to both eliminate the embolic source and stabilize posterior circulation hemodynamics. Although PAO of the left VA alone could have been considered, concern remained regarding posterior circulation perfusion because the right VA represented the dominant VA and serial MRA demonstrated temporal changes in vertebrobasilar visualization. Therefore, simultaneous reconstruction of the right VA was performed to improve antegrade flow before PAO of the left VA. The lesion exhibited significant tortuosity and stenosis associated with degenerative changes of the cervical spine. These structural alterations may have contributed to chronic mechanical stress and hemodynamic disturbances.10)

Dynamic evaluation was not performed before treatment; however, follow-up dynamic angiography performed 5 months later demonstrated positional occlusion of the right VA, including transient occlusion during both neck extension and rightward neck rotation. These findings indicate the presence of a Bow hunter’s syndrome mechanism involving the right VA and suggest that dynamic VA compression may have contributed to the temporal changes observed on serial MRA.13) Because dynamic evaluation was not available before treatment, the exact hemodynamic status of the right VA at that time remains uncertain. Surgical decompression for mechanical compression of the left VA at the C1 incomplete AF was considered a potential treatment option, but it was not selected in this case. In addition to the patient’s advanced age, there was a significant concern regarding the high risk of intraoperative embolic events caused by surgical manipulation of the vessels in the presence of a floating thrombus.

Regarding the right VA lesion, surgical decompression is generally considered the definitive treatment for symptomatic mechanical compression, whereas endovascular stenting has also been reported as a treatment option in selected cases of Bow hunter’s syndrome.14) Dynamic mechanical compression of the right VA was not recognized at the time of treatment planning. Given the dominant nature of the right VA and the need to secure antegrade posterior circulation flow before PAO of the left VA, stent placement was considered an acceptable treatment option. Follow-up dynamic angiography subsequently demonstrated positional occlusion despite stent placement, indicating that dynamic mechanical compression had not been eliminated. Furthermore, persistent external mechanical stress may theoretically increase the risk of stent deformation, fracture, restenosis, or occlusion during long-term follow-up. Nevertheless, the patient has remained asymptomatic without recurrent ischemic events during follow-up, although careful long-term surveillance remains necessary.

Conclusion

VA compression associated with an AF may result in ischemic stroke due to artery-to-artery embolism. In selected cases, concomitant hemodynamic compromise should also be considered. Treatment strategies should be tailored to the underlying mechanism and hemodynamic status.
