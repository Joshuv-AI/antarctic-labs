 WILLISTON BASIN ASSOCIATED CO2 STORAGE FIELD LABORATORY

 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 The Williston Basin Associated CO2 Storage Field Laboratory (WBCFL), led by the Energy
 & Environmental Research Center (EERC) in partnership with Denbury (now ExxonMobil), was
 established to advance understanding of CO2 enhanced oil recovery (EOR) and associated geologic
 CO2 storage in the Williston Basin. The specific objective of the WBCFL was to conduct field and
 laboratory activities to inform decisionmakers regarding the 1) efficacy of associated storage in
 stacked geologic reservoirs during EOR operations; 2) CO2 injectivity, oil production, and storage
 in residual oil zones (ROZs); 3) use of a novel CO2 storage monitoring, verification, and
 accounting (MVA) technology (active seismoelectric [ASE] monitoring), and 4) life cycle and
 techno-economic factors for EOR development scenarios targeting ROZs.

 The project location was the Pennel Field in southeast Montana, which is part of the Cedar
 Creek Anticline (CCA) geologic structure within the Williston Basin. The field was selected
 because it was being developed by Denbury for tertiary-phase CO2 EOR targeting the Red River
 Formation, and the availability of CO2, which was being supplied by the Greencore Pipeline,
 offered opportunities to evaluate the EOR potential of the other oil-bearing formations within the
 structure. The focus of this project was a vertically stacked reservoir system, including the
 Interlake Formation, a complex carbonate interval with both main pay zones (MPZs) and
 underlying ROZs. ROZs are oil-bearing zones that have been naturally waterflooded and, while
 they often contain moderate oil saturations, they cannot be economically produced using
 conventional primary or secondary recovery. However, CO2 EOR of ROZs in the Permian Basin
 have been successful, as the injected CO2 can mobilize oil trapped in the reservoir by swelling the
 oil, reducing oil viscosity, and achieving miscibility under reservoir conditions.

 The initial project activities focused on data collection and preparation of the field
 demonstration within the Pennel Field. Denbury drilled, logged, and cored a new well, 23-18B, as
 a CO2 injector paired with a nearby production well, 13-18B. Samples were collected from the
 field location, including rock, brine, and oil samples, for subsequent analysis at the EERC and to
 support the laboratory testing, geomodelling, and reservoir simulation components of the project.

 Comprehensive laboratory analyses were performed to characterize produced oil and brine,
 evaluate CO2–oil miscibility, and measure interfacial tension and contact angles at reservoir
 temperature and pressure. Results showed that Interlake oils achieve miscibility with CO2 at
 approximately 2600–2800 psi, indicating potential suitability for CO2 EOR. Brine and carbonated-
 brine testing highlighted modest but measurable changes in interfacial properties, while contact
 angle measurements indicated predominantly water-wet rock—an important condition that can
 strongly influence CO2 storage and oil displacement behavior.

 These laboratory results were integrated into a geologic model of the Pennel Field,
 constructed from well logs, core data, structural horizons, and petrophysical properties. The
 Interlake Formation model was subdivided into six depositional cycles that capture heterogeneity
 between the MPZ and ROZ intervals. A 1-sq-mi dynamic reservoir simulation model was

 vi

 developed from this geologic framework, incorporating five producing wells and the drilled
 injection well. Historical well performance, including more than five decades of production, was
 used to history-match reservoir behavior and calibrate the simulation model.

 Using the calibrated simulation model, the EERC evaluated three CO2 injection strategies:
 continuous CO2 injection, a 1:1 water alternating gas (WAG) scheme, and a tapered WAG
 schedule designed to reduce early gas breakthrough. Across all strategies, CO2 injection increased
 oil production relative to primary depletion, including meaningful contributions from the ROZ.
 The tapered WAG strategy produced the highest incremental oil recovery and the most efficient
 CO2 utilization, aided by improved reservoir pressure support and reduced early gas migration
 toward producers. Notably, Well 22-18B, located farther from the injector and historically more
 productive, significantly outperformed the closer 13-18B, both in projected incremental oil and in
 CO2 storage amount.

 Simulations suggested that developing CO2 EOR in the 1-sq-mi area of interest in the
 Interlake MPZ/ROZ system could yield 34,000–54,000 barrels of incremental oil at 13-18B and
 181,000–217,000 barrels at 22-18B over the 5- and 6-year modeled injection periods. Associated
 CO2 storage ranged from 80,000 to over 190,000 tonnes, depending on the case, with WAG
 strategies storing a larger fraction of the injected CO2. These high storage rates reflect the strong
 retention characteristics of carbonate ROZs and their compatibility with miscible CO2 flooding.
 Dimensionless curve analysis was used to extend these simulation results to 15 years, enabling
 long-term projections for economic and life cycle analysis (LCA).

 A screening-level LCA and techno-economic analysis evaluated the environmental and
 economic impacts of each EOR scenario, integrating simulation-based forecasts of oil production,
 recycled CO2, and long-term storage. Results emphasized that CO2 EOR—particularly tapered
 WAG—can provide substantial net storage while increasing oil recovery, producing favorable CO₂
 storage per barrel, and strengthening alignment with federal incentives such as the Section 45Q
 tax credit. The stacked-storage development concept, targeting both MPZ and ROZ horizons
 together, offers a pathway for maximizing combined resource recovery and geologic CO2
 containment at legacy oil fields.

 To explore the feasibility of cost-effective monitoring strategies, the project deployed a
 baseline active seismoelectric survey—a method that detects electrical signals generated by
 seismic waves interacting with fluid-filled rock layers. Although deep detection (~8000 ft) proved
 challenging due to electromagnetic noise and signal attenuation, the survey established an
 important baseline and highlighted potential for shallower reservoir applications using improved
 acquisition geometry, denser receiver arrays, and refined filtering approaches. The work
 contributes valuable insights to the growing body of research on novel monitoring tools for CO2
 EOR or permanent geologic storage.

 The final component of the project was for Denbury to perform the field test to validate the
 results of the modeling, simulation, and economic analysis; however, after Denbury was acquired
 by ExxonMobil in late 2023, the project experienced multiple delays in execution of the field pilot.
 In early 2025, the EERC was informed that the pilot project no longer aligned with
 Denbury/ExxonMobil’s priorities and that they were unable to continue the collaboration with the

 vii

 EERC and DOE. As a result, the field-based activities, including CO2 injection and subsequent
 monitoring and fluid sampling, were not completed. Despite the lack of data from a field test, the
 geologic, fluid composition, and production data collected from the field allowed for significant
 work to be performed, the results of which are relevant to future CO2 EOR projects across the
 county.

 In total, WBCFL provides a robust, transferable technical foundation for future CO2 EOR
 and storage developments in similar carbonate reservoirs that contain ROZs. Even without the
 execution of the planned field pilot, the project’s fluid-behavior studies, geologic modeling,
 dynamic simulation, monitoring evaluation, and integrated LCA/techno-economic results
 collectively demonstrate that ROZ-targeted CO2 EOR holds potential to produce incremental oil
 while achieving meaningful and secure CO2 storage. These findings support the continued
 investigation and testing of tapered WAG schedules, the optimization of producer/injector spacing,
 and the further development of cost-efficient monitoring technologies to advance the commercial
 viability of stacked CO2 storage systems across the Williston Basin and beyond.

 1.0 INTRODUCTION

 This U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) project, led by the Energy & Environmental
 Research Center (EERC) in collaboration with project partner and host site operator, Denbury
 (now a wholly owned subsidiary of ExxonMobil Corporation), was conducted to understand
 associated geologic storage of CO2 through enhanced oil recovery (EOR) in conventional oil
 reservoirs of the Williston Basin. Within the project, the Williston Basin Associated CO2 Storage
 Field Laboratory (WBCFL) was established to conduct field and laboratory activities to inform
 decisionmakers regarding the 1) efficacy of associated storage in stacked geologic reservoirs
 during EOR operations; 2) CO2 injectivity, oil production, and storage in residual oil zones
 (ROZs); 3) use of a novel CO2 storage monitoring, verification, and accounting (MVA) technology
 (active seismoelectric [ASE] monitoring); and 4) life cycle and techno-economic factors for EOR
 development scenarios targeting ROZs. Generally, ROZs are intervals of rock below an oil–water
 contact with moderate oil saturations (15%–40%) that have experienced natural waterflooding
 (Burton-Kelly and others, 2015).

 During the early stages of this project, Denbury was independently planning to conduct a
 multiwell pilot study investigating the use of CO2 for EOR and associated storage in the Pennel
 Field in southwestern Montana (Figure 1). The EERC partnered with Denbury as the goals of both
 projects, when combined, build a technical foundation for potential future use of CO2 for EOR in
 ROZs. The Pennel Field is part of the Cedar Creek Anticline (CCA) geologic structure within the
 Williston Basin, currently being developed by Denbury for tertiary-phase CO2 EOR targeting the
 Red River Formation and, if technically and economically feasible, other oil-bearing formations
 known to exist along the structure. Denbury targeted the underdeveloped Interlake Formation for
 the CO2 EOR pilot. The Interlake in the Pennel Field includes both main pay zones (MPZs), which
 have produced oil in several fields along the anticline, and ROZs, which hold untapped reserves.
 CO2 was to be sourced from the Greencore Pipeline (shown on Figure 1), which supplies the
 Denbury-operated Bell Creek CO2 EOR project in southern Montana and was recently (2022)
 extended to the CCA for staged EOR development.

 In 2025, ExxonMobil informed the EERC that it would not be able to commit to conducting
 a field pilot to inject CO2 at the Pennel Field. Unfortunately, with ExxonMobil’s decision not to
 participate in the field pilot, field-based activities, including CO2 injection and subsequent
 monitoring and fluid sampling, were not completed. While the CO2 pilot at the Pennel Field site
 was not conducted, significant theoretical work was completed and used to estimate the EOR
 potential and technoeconomic feasibility of CO2 EOR in a conventional oil field containing an
 ROZ. Specifically, fluid behavior studies and site-specific reservoir characterization and geologic
 modeling were conducted. Additionally, multiple EOR simulation scenarios were developed and
 incorporated into a detailed technoeconomic and lifecycle assessment of CO2 EOR potential in
 ROZs. Finally, a baseline ASE survey was conducted and interpreted to determine the viability of
 its use as an effective reservoir surveillance tool at sites considered for CO2 EOR or permanent
 geologic storage. The following report summarizes the work conducted and provides insight
 regarding the use of CO2 for EOR in conventional oil fields that may contain ROZs.

 2.0 BACKGROUND AND GEOLOGY

 The Pennel Field in Fallon County, Montana, is an active oil field along the CCA within the
 Williston Basin of Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, and Canada (Clement, 1978). The field
 has been in production since it was discovered in the 1950s. The CCA is reported to contain up to
 5 billion barrels of oil in place, primarily contained within the Ordovician-age Red River
 Formation, with additional formations contributing production from the Mississippian-age
 Madison Formation and Silurian-age Interlake Formation (Passut, 2018).

 The Interlake Formation is a carbonate stratigraphic interval found throughout the Williston
 Basin. Within the CCA, the Interlake is part of a stacked geologic reservoir complex consisting of
 the Interlake, Stony Mountain, and Red River Formations that include both conventional reservoirs
 and a lower Interlake ROZ. Figure 2 shows the generalized stratigraphic position of the Interlake
 Formation. The reservoir top seal consists of multiple tight Devonian-age carbonate formations.
 The Interlake conformably overlies the Stonewall Formation, and the Tippecanoe Unconformity
 separates the Interlake from the overlying Devonian Ashern Formation.

 2

 Figure 2. Stratigraphic column identifying the Interlake Formation. The formation has an average
 depth of 8240 feet in the Pennel Field. Figure modified from the stratigraphic column of North
 Dakota (North Dakota Geological Survey, 2009).

 3

 The Interlake stratigraphy comprises several cycles of peritidal and subtidal dolomite
 containing compartmentalized reservoirs beneath structural and stratigraphic traps (Nesheim,
 2019). Within the Pennel Field area, the formation is almost entirely peritidal dolomite (Bramlett,
 1985), which underwent a period of erosion prior to the Middle to Late Devonian period (Clement,
 1987). The uplift of the CCA during the Late Silurian period created a karst plain (Roehl, 1967),
 resulting in the deposition of sediments and the incision of channels on the paleosurface of the
 structure. The carbonate hills between the incised channels formed the compartments or traps for
 hydrocarbon reservoirs to develop, including ROZs. Previous characterization of the Pennel Field
 revealed an average Interlake Formation depth of 8240 feet below the surface, with an average
 gross thickness of nearly 350 feet and an average net pay thickness of 40 feet (Bramlett, 1985).

 2.1 Residual Oil Zones

 Generally, ROZs are intervals of rock below an oil–water contact with moderate oil
 saturations (15%–40%) that have experienced natural waterflooding (Burton-Kelly and others,
 2015). Oil saturations within ROZs are found at irreducible levels and are generally not
 economically producible with conventional hydrocarbon production means (Trentham and others,
 2012; Melzer, 2006, 2011). Because ROZs are at or near residual oil saturation, technically and
 economically recoverable oil may only be achievable through advanced methods, like CO2 EOR.
 Several successful ROZ CO2 EOR projects in the Permian Basin of West Texas indicate enormous
 resource potential for these emerging oil plays. However, despite their success, there is a paucity
 of data available on CO2 utilization rates (and hence, associated storage) related to ROZ
 development. In addition, there are few documented instances of field investigations into ROZ
 performance outside the Permian Basin. This work aimed to resolve some of these uncertainties
 and provide a better understanding of the CO2 EOR and associated storage potential for an ROZ
 located in the Williston Basin.

 3.0 ACTIVE SEISMOELECTRIC SURVEY

 Understanding where injected CO2 travels underground—whether for CO2 EOR or long-
 term storage—requires reliable and affordable monitoring methods. Traditional seismic surveys
 are commonly used to visualize the subsurface, but they do not always provide enough detail to
 visualize the occurrence of injected fluids, such as CO2, in the reservoir. The ASE method is a
 novel approach that uses sound waves (seismic energy) to create electrical signals (electromagnetic
 [EM]) in the ground. These signals can help map out where fluids are, especially those that do not
 conduct electricity well, like CO2. This method could add valuable information at a low additional
 cost.

 In partnership with Denbury, the EERC conducted a baseline ASE survey in February 2023
 at the Pennel field. The main goal was to assess whether the ASE approach could discern and
 differentiate underground rock layers and fluid distributions before CO2 was injected. This section
 summarizes the key learnings and challenges faced when using ASE in deep underground
 reservoirs. A more detailed summary of the ASE background, sourcing, deployment, processing,
 Interlake rock physics, and ASE data analysis is provided in Appendices A.

 4

 3.1 ASE Background

 The ASE method works by sending vibrations into the ground, which then create small
 electrical signals. These signals are picked up by sensors on the surface. The timing and strength
 of these signals can reveal changes in underground rock properties and fluid saturation distribution.
 However, the sensors also pick up significant unwanted noise, making it hard to separate the useful
 signals from the background. Useful signals can be found by looking for an electrical response that
 flips direction on either side of the vibration source, but this can still be challenging, especially in
 complex geology.

 ASE has been used in other applications, like geothermal energy, glacial characterization,
 and oil exploration, but it is usually applied to shallow depths. Some studies have shown that it
 can work in deeper environments if strong enough vibration sources are used. ASE is differentiated
 from traditional seismic because it can detect changes in fluid conductivity, and CO2 is an ideal
 fluid to monitor with its low conductivity relative to fluids like oil and brine.

 3.2 Seismic Sourcing and ASE Field Survey Deployment

 For this survey, two types of vibration sources were used to evaluate which would generate
 a better ASE response. Seismic data was also collected to help ground truth the responses seen
 with electrical sensors. A smaller test showed that weak sources might not be enough for deep
 targets, so a large vibroseis truck (which shakes the ground) and a PinPoint® explosive source were
 both used. The vibroseis truck generated hundreds of vibration points along several lines, while
 the PinPoint source was used along one line due to equipment problems. Sensors recording the
 ASE data were placed in lines parallel to the vibration points along the two 2D seismic line to
 record the small EM signals (Figure 3). The equipment was sensitive enough to pick up very small
 electrical signals. Over 4 days, 135 GB of ASE data was collected.

 3.3 ASE Data Analysis

 The main challenge in analyzing ASE data is picking out the useful electrical signals that
 provide insight about the subsurface geology and/or fluid changes from the noise. The data were
 organized in different ways to help interpret these signals, such as grouping by where the vibration
 started or by which sensor recorded the signal.

 When looking at the data from the PinPoint source, some sensors showed good quality, while
 others were overwhelmed by noise. Some patterns suggested the presence of underground layers,
 but the signals were weak. The vibroseis data showed similar results. By averaging signals from
 many vibration points, the team could enhance the signals of interest and reduce random noise.
 This technique showed some potential for response from the subsurface formations, but the deepest
 target remained undetectable. The results suggested that the method might work better for
 shallower layers.

 Comparing the ASE data with traditional seismic data helped confirm that certain signals
 matched known underground boundaries, but not all expected signals were seen, especially for the
 deeper target layer (the Interlake Formation). If electrical signals match underground boundaries
 shown in the interpreted seismic data, then traditional seismic surveys do not need to be repeated
 on future ASE monitoring surveys. This reduces the cost of monitoring by removing the need for
 a larger crew to deploy seismic receivers.

 3.4 ASE Discussion

 The data collected at the Pennel Field were heavily affected by electrical noise from nearby
 power lines and oilfield equipment. Some noise could be filtered out, but much remained. The
 signals that could be interpreted were weak, and the deeper target layer could not be detected. The
 PinPoint source, being less powerful, required more data averaging to see any useful signals.
 Overall, the method worked about the same for both sources, but neither was able to clearly detect
 the deep target layer due to noise and signal weakness. The method could potentially monitor
 changes in fluid saturation in shallow groundwater or the lowest underground source of drinking
 water (USDW).

 6

 3.5 ASE Conclusions and Lessons Learned

 Imaging deep underground structures with the ASE method proved highly challenging at the
 Pennel Field site. Signals from deep layers, such as the Interlake Formation, were extremely weak
 and quickly diminished with distance, making them difficult to distinguish from background noise,
 even with sensitive equipment and strong vibration sources. The site’s geology (characterized by
 low porosity, low permeability, and significant depth) further limited the ability to detect a clear
 ASE response that could be verified against traditional seismic techniques.

 Despite these challenges, ASE demonstrated potential for monitoring shallower subsurface
 layers, such as the USDW, especially if future surveys are optimized. Key improvements include
 deploying more sensors spaced closely in a single line, refining survey design, and using advanced
 modeling to better predict where useful signals may be detected.

 Repeating surveys in the same locations over time could allow for tracking changes in fluid
 distributions and enhance the value of ASE as a monitoring tool. This project showed that existing
 equipment can be adapted for ASE surveys, but the method’s effectiveness is highly dependent on
 site conditions and survey execution. When combined with traditional seismic surveys, ASE could
 provide additional insight into fluid changes, especially in shallow groundwater, and help reduce
 overall monitoring costs by minimizing the need for repeated large-scale seismic surveys.

 MMP is a critical parameter for CO2 EOR, as miscible CO2 injection promotes oil swelling
 and enhances oil mobility through viscosity reduction, resulting in lower IFT. Figure 5 illustrates
 the change in capillary interface height for CO2 and crude oil at different pressures, measured using
 three capillary tubes with varying diameters. As the pressure increases and both phases approach
 the multicontact miscibility, IFT continues to decrease in all three capillaries until each reaches a
 predetermined zero point at the CO2–oil interface. The MMP is determined by extrapolating the
 measured CO2 capillary level to the zero point.

 At a reservoir temperature of 200°F, the MPZ and ROZ oil samples exhibited multicontact
 MMP values of 2605 psi and 2835 psi, respectively. The lower MMP observed for the MPZ oil
 sample is attributed to its higher concentration of light hydrocarbons. These lighter components
 exhibit high volatility and strong CO2 solubility, making them highly effective contributors to oil
 swelling, viscosity reduction, and rapid IFT reduction during early CO2–oil contacts. The enhanced
 solubility of CO2 in the light-end fraction facilitates favorable compositional changes at the CO2–
 oil interface, accelerating the development of multicontact miscibility. In contrast, heavier
 hydrocarbons (C20+), which are more prevalent in the ROZ oil sample, exhibit low volatility and
 reduced CO2 solubility. As a result, higher pressures are required to achieve miscibility in oils
 enriched in heavier fractions, explaining the higher MMP measured for the ROZ oil sample
 (Hawthorne and others, 2018).

 4.2 Rock and Fluid Interactions (IFT/Contact Angle)

 While CO2 generally decreases the IFT of oil and water, it can also react with oil and/or
 brine components to form surface-active compounds that increase IFT (Vermolen and others 2006;
 and others 2019). This acidification of the brine can alter the properties of the oil–water interface,
 resulting in higher or lower IFT. Whether the IFT in carbonated brine is higher or lower than that
 of the in situ brine depends on several factors, including the specific properties of the oil and brine,
 the CO2 concentration, and the system's operating conditions.

 IFT was measured at reservoir conditions by introducing an oil droplet into a chamber filled
 with representative formation brine for one experiment and with a CO2-saturated brine to

 10

 determine the effect of CO2 on IFT. Figure 6 summarizes IFT results in both produced brine and
 carbonated brine. The ROZ oil sample had an average IFT of 12.7 mN/m in formation brine and
 14.1 mN/m in carbonated brine at a temperature of 200°F and pressures from 3000 to 4200 psi. A
 similar trend was observed for the MPZ oil sample; however, the measured IFT values were
 significantly lower. The MPZ oil sample showed average IFT values of 6.6 mN/m in formation
 brine and 9.5 mN/m in carbonated brine. The slight increase in IFT due to CO2 injection may be
 because of the formation of surface-active compounds resulting from the reaction between CO2
 and the oil and/or brine components (Puntervold and others, 2008). Despite the slight increase
 observed in the presence of CO2-saturated brine, the overall IFT values measured for both the MPZ
 and ROZ oil samples remain low, indicating reduced capillary forces. These measurements suggest
 that oil recovery during CO2 EOR is unlikely to be driven by IFT reduction. Instead, the dominant
 mechanisms may include oil swelling, viscosity reduction, development of CO2–oil miscibility,
 and reservoir repressurization during CO2 EOR.

 Contact angle analyses were also performed using representative reservoir rock and fluid
 samples to better understand the wettability of the reservoir. To perform the testing, a droplet of
 oil is introduced on a rock surface that is surrounded by brine, and the angle between the rock–oil
 droplet–brine interface is measured. The rock is considered oil-wet if the contact angle of the oil
 droplet is between 0° and 80°, mixed-wet for values between 80° and 100°, and water-wet when
 higher than 100° (Chilingar and Yen, 1983).

 Figure 7 presents the results of the contact angle measurements for representative reservoir
 rock samples at reservoir conditions. The measured contact angles indicate that the rock is strongly
 water-wet. For the MPZ and ROZ oil samples, the original contact angles were 120° and 140°,
 respectively. Following the introduction of CO2 and the use of CO2-saturated brine, the contact
 angles increased further to 144° and 148° for the MPZ and ROZ oil samples, respectively,
 indicating that CO2 exposure further enhances the water-wet character of the reservoir rock. Water-
 wet reservoirs may be advantageous for CO2 EOR through better miscible displacement efficiency
 since injected CO2 preferentially invades oil-filled pore centers (oil occupies the larger pore centers
 while water coats the rock grain surfaces in water-wet rocks), leading to efficient contact of CO2
 with oil and subsequent mobilization (Hussain and others, 2025).

 5.0 FIELD SITE RESERVOIR CHARACTERIZATION

 Reservoir characterization of the Interlake Formation at the Pennel Field was jointly carried
 out by Denbury and EERC teams. In advance of the planned pilot, activities included drilling and
 completion of a new well (23-18B), including coring the Interlake MPZ and ROZ, performing
 formation tests on the reservoir, and injecting a stream of CO2 into the reservoir to assess
 production performance.

 Denbury successfully drilled, cored, and logged the Interlake Formation in advance of the
 pilot. Well (23-18B) was planned to be the injection well for the pilot. A second preexisting well,
 the 13-18B, was to be used as a production well. The two wells were intentionally closely spaced,
 approximately 600 feet apart, to manage conformance and ensure a high chance of monitoring
 EOR performance while minimizing gas losses to the formation.

 5.1 Geological Model

 The EERC worked with Denbury to perform reservoir characterization at the site, including
 development of a geologic model to represent the MPZ and ROZ intervals of the Interlake
 Formation. The model encompasses a 4 mi × 4 mi area of the Field, centered on the 13-18B well,
 (Figure 8). The model was constructed from multiple datasets of geologic and geophysical
 information obtained from the Montana Board of Oil and Gas Conservation (MBOGC) online
 database and from Denbury. These datasets included geologic formation top depths, basic well
 logs (including gamma ray, resistivity, neutron porosity, density, and delta time), petrophysical
 analyses, and core data.

 5.1.1 Structure

 The Interlake Formation within the model area was divided into six zones, Cycles 1–6 in
 ascending order (Figure 9). Each zone represents a separate depositional cycle, showing the MPZ
 and underlying ROZs. To construct the model’s structural framework, formation top and
 interpreted cycle depths were obtained from the MBOGC online database and provided by the host
 site operator. Seventy-nine wells were used (Figure 10) to construct the model, and the formation
 and cycle top depths for these wells were compared for accuracy against well logs provided by
 Denbury. Using the formation and cycle top depths as control points, structural surfaces were
 constructed for the lower undifferentiated Devonian (sealing formation), the Interlake Formation,
 each of the six depositional cycles (MPZ and ROZs), and the undifferentiated Stonewall–Stony
 Mountain Formation interval (lower seal). A grid cell increment of 100 ft × 100 ft was used to
 divide the model area in the X and Y directions. Each depositional cycle within the Interlake
 Formation comprising the MPZ and ROZ was divided vertically into 24 increments with each cell
 averaging 2.1 feet in thickness.

 6.0 DYNAMIC SIMULATION OF CO2 EOR

 ROZ resources are typically considered technically and/or economically unviable through
 primary oil recovery or traditional secondary waterflooding oil recovery. However, CO2 flooding
 has been considered a feasible EOR technique for economic resource recovery within ROZs, and
 several ROZ CO2 EOR pilot and full-field applications have been conducted in the Permian Basin
 (Trentham and Melzer, 2015).

 The objectives of the reservoir simulation work were to develop and simulate predictive
 scenarios of CO2 EOR to evaluate the performance of various CO2 EOR strategies for both the
 MPZ and ROZ in the Interlake Formation. This was accomplished through developing an EOS
 fluid model based on the fluid characterization data generated from laboratory studies. The
 reservoir simulation model was then developed and calibrated for the area of interest in the Pennel

 19

 Field by analyzing historical fluid production and waterflooding injection performance, and
 history matching the model using available production/injection data. This enabled the EERC to
 assess the performance of various CO2 EOR strategies using a history-matched reservoir model.
 Finally, the oil and recycled CO2 production results generated from the reservoir simulations, were
 limited by computational software run times to generate outputs for 5 to 6 years. To extend the
 reservoir response prediction from the end of simulation cases, dimensionless analysis was used
 to forecast simulation outputs to 15 years. This provided a comprehensive project life forecast of
 incremental oil production and recycled CO2 that served as the basis for the life cycle analysis
 (LCA), as described in Section 7.3.

 6.1 Interlake Simulation Model Development

 Based on the geological model developed and described in the preceding section of this
 report, a simulation model was developed using Computer Modelling Group Ltd.’s (CMG’s)
 compositional reservoir simulator, GEM (Computer Modelling Group Ltd., GEM 2023). The
 simulation model covers an area of 1 sq mi in the southwestern part of the Pennel Field (Figure 8),
 extracted from the static geological model for computational efficiency. Figure 13 shows the 3D
 grid of the Pennel Field simulation model, which includes five Interlake producing wells and one
 injection well. Well 13-18B, toward the center of the model, was the primary EOR production well
 considered for the planned pilot. 23XX-18B, located ~500 ft from 13-18B, was the CO2 injection
 well. The simulation model consisted of 489,888 cells and had 54 layers, with cell dimensions of
 100 ft × 100 ft × ~2 ft. The carbonate reservoir was very heterogeneous with an average porosity
 of ~7% and an average permeability of ~2 mD. The initial reservoir and temperature set in the
 model were 3700 psi and 220°F, respectively.

 6.4.2 Oil Production Rate

 A significant increase in the oil production rate of each well (13-18B and 22-18B) was
 observed in all three EOR scenarios (continuous CO2 injection, 1:1 WAG injection, and tapered
 WAG injection). These results indicate that CO2 can mobilize oil in the ROZ through the combined
 mechanisms of oil swelling and reduced viscosity, resulting from CO2 mixing with the oil. The
 use of WAG resulted in higher tertiary oil recovery with oil production sustained over a longer
 period compared to the continuous injection. This is likely the result of greater mobility control
 resulting from the WAG process that reduced viscous fingering of the injected CO2 and
 consequently resulted in a greater buildup of reservoir pressure.

 6.5 Simulation Conclusion and Recommendation for Future Work

 The simulation results indicated a significant potential for CO2 EOR in the Interlake
 Formation ROZ (and MPZ) at the Pennel Field, with predicted incremental oil production ranging
 from 34 to 217 Mbbl, depending on the well and scenarios over the years of EOR (5 years for
 13-18B and 6 years for 22-18B). Overall, the WAG injection further improved oil production and
 associated storage, benefiting from higher pressure buildup and better gas mobility control with
 water injection integrated into gas injection compared to the continuous CO2 injection scenario.
 The key insights and findings from this work were as follows:

 30

 • CO2 injection can mobilize oil and produce it from the ROZs of the Interlake at this
 location, unlocking reserves that would otherwise remain unrecoverable.

 • WAG injection can add more oil production over continuous gas injection, helping with
 pressure buildup and gas breakthrough control.

 • WAG injection can help store more of the injected CO2 compared to continuous gas
 injection.

 • Tapered WAG injection schedules could improve the EOR performance for the field.

 • Optimization of the schedule could further improve EOR performance.

 • Optimization with well location could also improve the EOR performance for the field.

 7.0 LIFE CYCLE ANALYSIS AND TECHNO-ECONOMIC MODELING

 The EERC conducted a screening-level LCA and techno-economic assessment of a
 hypothetical stacked storage project targeting the MPZ and ROZ based on the Pennel Field
 simulation results. The stacked design is significant because it enables simultaneous development
 of productive and residual oil zones, expanding recovery potential and storage capacity. The EERC
 focused the LCA on comparing the following EOR injection strategies: continuous CO2 injection,
 conventional 1:1 WAG, and a tapered WAG scheme. Traditional approaches, such as 1:1 WAG
 (equal parts of water and gas in each injection cycle) or continuous CO2 injection, balance oil
 recovery and storage differently but have notable trade-offs. Research indicates that tapered WAG
 schemes, beginning with higher CO2 injection followed by progressively wetter cycles, may
 improve recovery (both in timing and ultimate volume produced) and storage, although the optimal
 design remains uncertain (Merchant, 2010). The three selected EOR injection strategies conducted
 in the LCA helped provide insight into operational pathways to maximize oil recovery while
 storing CO2. By considering continuous CO2 injection, 1:1 WAG, and tapered WAG schemes, this
 LCA aims to provide a framework for future CO2 EOR developments in stacked MPZ/ROZ
 reservoirs, aligning operational practices with evolving incentives such as the federal Section 45Q
 tax credit.

 31

 7.1 LCA and Techno-Economic Methods

 The LCA and techno-economic assessments were conducted based on dimensionless
 analysis outputs from the numerical reservoir simulation extensions. The LCA integrated output
 results from two distinct reservoir model types: 1) the geological model, developed in SLB’s
 Petrel, representing the MPZ and ROZ horizons of the Interlake Formation within the CCA Pennel
 Field, and 2) a compositional reservoir model built using CMG’s GEM simulator.

 7.2 Reservoir Simulation Model

 During the reservoir simulation study, reservoir performance was assessed for the Interlake
 Formation MPZ and ROZ horizons through:

 • Analysis of historical production and waterflood injection data from the Interlake
 Formation MPZ.

 • Development and calibration of the reservoir simulation model for the area of interest.

 • Evaluation of EOR scenarios for the MPZ and the underlying ROZ to assess their
 reservoir performance under different development strategies.

 Based on the detailed activities described in Section 5.1 – Geological Model, a reservoir
 simulation model was built using GEM. Historical production and injection records from 1966 to
 2016 indicated five producing wells yielding approximately 2.0 MMbbl of oil, 1.9 MMbbl of
 water, and 1.4 MMscf of gas. Between 1996 and 1997, one of these wells was converted to
 injection, resulting in approximately 1.3 Mbbl of injected water. These data were used to calibrate
 the model through history matching, improving its reliability for forecasting EOR performance.

 The EERC simulated eight predictive cases to test three distinct EOR strategies—continuous
 CO₂ injection, 1:1 WAG, and tapered WAG—at two wellsites (13-18B and 22-18B) (Table 9).
 Baseline cases representing primary depletion without EOR were also run for both wells.

 The reservoir simulation outputs expressed as incremental volumes for Cases 2–4

 dimensionless analysis are provided in Appendix B.

 7.4 Screening-Level LCA

 During EOR, nearly all purchased CO2 delivered to the field remains securely trapped in the
 reservoir (Melzer, 2012; Azzolina and others, 2015). As a result, EOR not only enhances oil
 recovery but also offsets some emissions from oil production and combustion through CO2 storage.

 A screening-level LCA was conducted to estimate the greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions
 associated with the EOR scenarios. LCA is a standardized method for quantifying environmental
 burdens across a product’s or system’s life cycle (International Organization for Standardization,
 2006a, 2006b). In this study, the LCA assesses the GHG emissions associated with incremental
 oil production under a hypothetical stacked storage project targeting the MPZ and ROZ in the
 Pennel Field. A central focus is accurately accounting for the CO2 stored during EOR, given that
 the system produces crude oil (from the Pennel Field) and natural gas (from the gas-processing
 facilities providing captured CO2 to the EOR field).

 7.4.1 System Boundaries

 The system model for the LCA is illustrated in Figure 24. The Shute Creek (SCGP) and Lost
 Cabin (LCGP) gas-processing facilities supply captured CO2 to the Greencore pipeline and CCA
 main line for EOR. This study defined two systems integrating gas processing with the Pennel
 Field EOR.

 System 1 is a hypothetical baseline with two independent processes: 1) natural gas
 extraction, processing, and transport and 2) crude oil extraction. In this system, CO2 separated
 during gas processing is vented to the atmosphere, and oil is produced only through conventional
 (primary or secondary) methods without EOR. System 1 does not represent current SCGP or LCGP
 operations, which capture CO2 for sale to EOR operators. Instead, it provides a benchmark for life
 cycle GHG emissions in a decoupled gas–oil system.

 System 2 represents the alternative case: a combined gas–oil system that captures CO2 from
 gas processing and uses it for EOR, producing natural gas and crude oil with associated CO2
 storage. This system allows comparison of the life cycle GHG emissions of conventional
 operations (System 1) with integrated carbon capture, utilization, and storage (System 2).

 7.4.2 Functional Unit of Comparison and Impact Assessment

 The functional unit for both systems is MMscf of processed, uncombusted natural gas and
 Mbbl of incremental crude oil production. This study evaluates only GHG emissions as a measure
 of environmental impact. While CO2 is the dominant GHG, methane (CH4) and nitrous oxide
 (N2O) also contribute to atmospheric warming.

 To enable comparison, GHG emissions are reported as CO2 equivalents (CO2eq) using
 100-year global warming potential (GWP) values of 29.8 for CH4 and 273 for N2O
 (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, 2021; Table 7.15). The GWP for CO2 is 1.
 Expressing results in CO2eq allows the combined impact of CO2, CH4, and N2O to be represented
 as a single metric (kg CO2eq).

 7.4.3 Spreadsheet Modeling

 The LCA employed Microsoft Excel-based models for both systems, incorporating emission
 factors from peer-reviewed literature, DOE National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL)
 publications, or customized field data. The models operate in an accounting-style manner,
 segmenting the system from upstream to downstream and calculating emissions line by line.
 Annual calculations were performed for 2026–2040, with cumulative totals representing the life
 cycle results.

 The following sections describe the model components, summarize the emission factors, and
 detail the calculations that derive GHG emissions from operating parameters and emission factors.

 7.4.4 Upstream Segments

 The upstream portion of the models includes four segments:

 1. Raw natural gas extraction
 2. Natural gas processing
 3. Natural gas transport and distribution (T&D)
 4. Pipeline transport of captured CO2 (applicable only to System 2).

 7.5 Screening-Level Techno-Economic Analysis

 A screening-level techno-economic analysis was conducted to evaluate the relative
 differences among Cases 6–8 regarding incremental oil revenue and 45Q tax credit revenue from
 CO2 storage (purchased CO2 quantities). The analysis used annual reservoir performance data from
 the dimensional analysis. The following assumptions were used as inputs into the model: 3

 • Oil price: Set at low, mid, or high values of $40, $60, or $110/bbl (see Section 2.4.1).

 • Gross oil revenue: The product of the incremental oil and the oil price.

 • Royalties: Set at 16.67% of gross oil revenue (Revenue Streams in Montana, 2014).

 • Production tax: Set at 0.5% of gross oil revenue for the first 12 months, then 9%
 thereafter (Montana Code Annotated, 2023).

 • CO2 purchases: Set at 3.0% of the oil price ($/Mscf).

 8.4 LCA Conclusions

 The LCA study demonstrated that coupling CO2 capture with EOR in the Pennel Field
 provides significant advantages in oil recovery, emissions reduction, and economic performance
 compared to a system without capture. Among the three EOR strategies evaluated, the tapered
 WAG (Case 8) consistently outperformed continuous CO₂ injection (Case 6) and regular (1:1)
 WAG (Case 7). Case 8 produced the most incremental oil, delivered the greatest life cycle GHG
 reduction (0.3 million tonnes), and achieved the highest economic return with an NPV10 of
 $15.8 million. These results highlight the importance of integrating reservoir development strategy
 with LCA. By aligning CO2 injection design with both emissions and revenue drivers, operators
 can simultaneously reduce systemwide GHG emissions and improve project economics. More
 broadly, the findings reinforce the potential for CO2 EOR projects to contribute to decarbonization
 while extending the productive life of mature oil fields.

 9.0 PROJECT SUMMARY

 WBCFL was a multiyear research initiative led by the EERC in collaboration initially with
 SOG Resources and then later with Denbury and ExxonMobil, and sponsored by DOE. The
 project, spanning from February 2019 to September 2025, was designed to advance technologies
 for associated geologic storage of CO2 in oil-producing zones of the Williston Basin.

 The primary project activities centered around the Pennel Field, part of the CCA of
 southeastern Montana, targeting the Interlake Formation—a carbonate reservoir system containing
 both MPZs and ROZs. ROZs represent untapped reserves that cannot be economically produced
 through conventional methods but offer significant potential for EOR using CO2 injection coupled
 with permanent CO₂ storage.

 The project produced meaningful and insightful results that can help inform future CO2 EOR
 operations in conventional reservoirs and in ROZs. Fluid behavior analyses provided valuable
 insights into oil composition, miscibility pressures, and interfacial properties under reservoir
 conditions and relative to CO2 EOR. Laboratory tests showed that CCA Interlake oils achieve

 an area of approximately 54 sq mi could yield an estimated 1.8–11.8 million barrels of incremental
 oil over 5–6 years with a potential associated CO2 storage capacity of 4.3–10.3 million tonnes.

 The project also evaluated a novel MVA technology: ASE surveying. A baseline ASE survey
 was conducted to assess its feasibility for detecting fluid distribution in deep formations. While
 deep responses were limited by EM noise, the study established a framework for improving ASE
 deployment and highlighted its potential as a novel complement to conventional seismic
 monitoring. The study determined that detecting an interfacial response from the Interlake
 Formation, or similar deep strata, is likely to require an improved acquisition geometry, the
 collection of ASE data using more dipole receivers, and vertical stacking of source shots/sweeps.
 Improved geometry would allow the application of seismic multichannel processing methods and
 direct correlation between ASE and seismic datasets.

 Finally, a screening-level LCA and techno-economic assessment were performed for
 hypothetical stacked storage scenarios. Results showed that integrating CO2 capture with EOR
 could reduce GHG emissions by up to 0.3 million tonnes in the scenarios that were evaluated and
 deliver substantial economic returns, particularly under favorable oil prices and federal incentives
 such as the 45Q tax credit. A techno-economic analysis indicated that tapered WAG was the most
 profitable strategy, with an estimated NPV of $15.8 million under baseline assumptions.

 In summary, WBCFL advanced understanding of CO2 EOR and associated storage in
 stacked carbonate reservoirs, developed predictive models for ROZ performance, and explored
 innovative monitoring techniques. Stacked storage (MPZ + ROZ) offers a pathway for maximizing
 oil recovery and CO₂ containment, aligning with incentives like the Section 45Q tax credit. Despite
 the absence of a field pilot, the research provides a foundation for future projects seeking to couple
 carbon management with EOR, offering both environmental and economic benefits.

 10.0 RECOMMENDATIONS FOR FUTURE WORK

 Field Pilot Execution

 A critical next step for the project is the execution of a CO2 injection pilot in the Interlake
 Formation, particularly targeting ROZs. Such a pilot would provide essential validation of
 simulation results, offering real-world data to quantify EOR and associated CO2 storage
 performance. This hands-on approach would also allow the project team to address operational
 challenges, optimize injection protocols, and refine monitoring techniques based on actual field
 responses. Successful pilot implementation would lay the groundwork for scaling up to full-field
 operations, supporting both economic and environmental objectives.

 Optimization of Injection Strategies

 Further optimization of injection strategies is vital for maximizing both oil recovery and CO2
 retention. Refinement of tapered WAG schedules, which outperformed other EOR strategies in the
 study, can help boost sweep efficiency and minimize gas breakthrough. Exploring alternative
 WAG ratios and adjusting cycle lengths will enable the project to better manage reservoir pressure
 and enhance oil displacement. These efforts should be informed by ongoing simulation work and,
 eventually, pilot results to ensure the most effective and adaptable operational plan.

 Well Pattern and Spacing Analysis

 Optimizing the layout of injection and production wells is essential for improving sweep
 efficiency and minimizing early gas migration. Detailed analysis of injector–producer spacing and
 pattern design will help reduce thief zones and channeling, which can undermine project
 performance. By tailoring well patterns to the unique properties of the Interlake Formation,
 operators can achieve more uniform reservoir contact and maximize both oil recovery and CO2
 storage. This work should be supported by reservoir modeling and validated through field data as
 they become available.

 Advanced Monitoring Technologies

 Advancing monitoring technologies, such as ASE surveys, are novel methods for tracking
 CO2 movement and verifying storage integrity. Improving survey design with enhanced
 acquisition geometry, denser dipole receiver arrays, and multichannel processing will increase the
 sensitivity and reliability of subsurface imaging. Integrating ASE with conventional seismic
 methods can provide a more comprehensive and novel approach for time-lapse monitoring of the
 USDW and CO2 plume migration.

 Extended Simulation and Economic Modeling

 Ongoing simulation and economic modeling are needed to guide both short- and long-term
 project decision-making. Expanding dimensionless analysis beyond the initial 15-year horizon will
 help identify optimal operating parameters for sustaining performance over decades. Incorporating
 capital cost modeling and conducting sensitivity analyses for oil price volatility and CO2 supply

 50

 costs will ensure that project strategies remain resilient under changing economic conditions.
 These efforts will also support transparent evaluation of project risks and returns for stakeholders.

 Stacked Storage Development

 Evaluating the scalability of stacked storage concepts, which integrate MPZs and ROZs, is
 a promising avenue for maximizing both oil recovery and CO2 storage. Applying these concepts
 to other fields in the Williston Basin and similar carbonate reservoirs could significantly expand
 the regional impact of EOR and carbon management efforts. Pilot demonstrations and comparative
 studies will help identify best practices and potential limitations, thereby informing future
 deployments. This approach aligns with broader industry and policy goals of maximizing resource
 recovery while also storing CO2.

 Environmental and Regulatory Alignment

 Maintaining alignment with environmental and regulatory frameworks is essential for
 project success. Continued integration of LCAs with techno-economic modeling will ensure that
 the project meets evolving carbon utilization and management policies and leverages incentives
 such as the Section 45Q tax credit. Proactive adaptation to regulatory changes will help secure
 long-term viability and public acceptance of CO2 EOR and storage projects. This strategy also
 positions the project to deliver tangible economic benefits while also supporting broader
 environmental objectives.

 ASE THEORY AND BACKGROUND

 ASE THEORY AND BACKGROUND

 ASE Theory and Synthetic Modeling Studies

 The active seismoelectric (ASE) method is a geophysical technique that attempts to
 characterize contrasts in rock, fluid, or electrical properties. The most significant factors affecting
 the survey are porosity, permeability, fluid salinity, or viscosity (Garambois and Dietrich, 2002).
 Replacement of reservoir fluids during enhanced oil recovery or CO2 storage may be detectable
 due to the associated changes in salinity or viscosity. Seismoelectric interfacial waves are initiated
 by seismic waves, usually P-waves, passing through a porous rock and inducing relative motion
 between the rock matrix and the fluid within the rock pores. When the seismic wave reaches a
 sufficiently porous or permeable zone, it induces a pressure wave within a rock formation. The
 relative motion of ionic fluid through capillaries in the rock occurs with cations preferentially
 adhering to the capillary walls, so that the applied pressure and resulting fluid flow relative to the
 rock matrix separates the cations and anions, producing an electric dipole (Garambois and Dietrich,
 2002). The resulting electrical signal from this interfacial response is transmitted back to the
 ground surface at approximately the speed of light, where it is measured with an array of electric
 electrical dipole receivers. These signals can be interpreted to map apparent fluid distribution
 within the target area. However, the ASE recording equipment also measures a coseismic response
 caused by the movement of seismic waves close to the dipole receivers. Coseismic and interfacial
 waves are both recorded by the dipole receivers.

 Some characteristics of the ASE waves may allow them to be identified in common shot or
 dipole gathers. Coseismic waves are expected to be much stronger than interfacial waves, and they
 are connected to and arrive with the same two-way-time (TWT) as the seismic waves. The polarity
 of coseismic waves will be the same for dipoles on opposite sides of the shot point. Seismic gathers
 recorded from geophones placed near the dipoles can be used to identify and remove the coseismic
 response. Interfacial waves are generated by interactions at interfaces in a region directly below
 the shot point. Because the interface wave returns to the surface at approximately the speed of
 light, it arrives in half the TWT, also called one-way-time, required for the zero-offset
 coseismic/seismic waves. They arrive simultaneously at all dipoles near the shot point. The
 polarity of interfacial waves will be reversed for dipoles on opposite sides of the shot point.

 ASE methods have been used previously for geothermal resource assessment and monitoring
 (Morency and others, 2022), geological interface analysis (Dean and others, 2012),
 overburden/bedrock contact (Butler and others, 2018), detecting wet ice in glaciers (Kulessa and
 others, 2006), and identifying heavy oil reservoirs (Jardani and Revil, 2009), among other uses.
 Very often ASE is used to characterize the upper 20 m of the subsurface, but some such as Dean
 and others (2012) demonstrated that large vibroseis sources (60,000–80,000-lb peak force) could
 be used to extend the offset range and time window for seismoelectric recording.

 Initial modeling of the resistivity structure of the Pennel Field site was done using a suite of
 well log measurements to generate a 1D model. Porosity within the formation typically ranges

 estimated to be 5 skin depths. Since the Interlake Formation is approximately 8000 ft (2439 m)

 3 to 4 skin depths. The signal response is small but detectable using sensitive electrodes and a
 high-precision analog-to-digital conversion in the receiver units.

 To fully understand the seismoelectric response, the EERC used a modeling code based on
 the theory in Pride and Morgan (1994), which simultaneously solves both Biot’s poroelastic wave
 equations and Maxwell’s EM wave equations. This requires a significant amount of information
 about the earth—21 parameters per layer in the model, including porosity, permeability, and
 saturation; solid bulk, solid shear, fluid bulk, and air bulk moduli; three-phase viscosity and density
 values; and salinity and temperature. The program uses these to calculate velocities Vp, Vs, and
 VEM, as well as the fluid conductivity, bulk resistivity, and density of material in each layer.

 To determine these key parameters, the EERC carried out a petrophysical study focused on
 the area of interest (AOI), with the information to be used in the generation of a seismoelectric
 model for the entire sedimentary section. A deterministic petrophysics workflow was applied to
 triple combo well log data, which constituted the main data available in the AOI. The workflow
 included an initial data audit of the wells present in Pennel Field. Ten wells contained triple combo
 data (gamma ray, neutron porosity, bulk density, and deep resistivity) from Cretaceous shales and
 sandstones to the Silurian Interlake Formation. These criteria represent the minimum data required
 to produce the required petrophysical properties. The computations were carried out with the SLB
            Techlog Suite 2022.2.1 (SLB, 2022).

 SEISMIC DATA: SOURCING, DEPLOYMENT, AND PROCESSING

 The project team evaluated a novel monitoring, verification, and accounting technique to
 demonstrate associated storage in stacked reservoirs of the Williston Basin. An active
 seismoelectric (ASE) technique was deployed to establish initial reservoir properties of the
 Interlake Formation. ASE methods hold the potential of providing a novel CO2 injection
 monitoring method that can give useful information about the distribution of fluids within the
 reservoir. The methods can complement a standard seismic survey, whether 2D or 3D, though
 hardware specifically designed for seismoelectric data collection that is not directly commercially
 available yet. Existing electromagnetic hardware can be adapted for seismoelectric measurements
 which require a low noise improved signal measuring capabilities. Similar to traditionally
 deployed seismic techniques, time-lapse ASE surveys are needed for determining a measurable
 signal response to predict CO2 plume movement.

 Seismic Sourcing and Deployment

 Two seismic sources were deployed for this survey. A vibroseis truck (I/O AHV IV 362
 62,000-lb max ground force), operated by Paragon Geophysical, performed 262 shots at nominal
 12-m spacing along the three lines crossing the survey area over 2 days. Sourcing with the vibroseis
 trucks was performed early in the morning while the ground was still frozen. The ground would
 thaw as daytime temperatures rose above 40°F. Each shot was a 24-s linear sweep from
 4 to 128 Hz with a 0.3-s linear taper with a 6-s listen time. The vibroseis source was expected to
 be sufficient to transmit seismic energy through the target Interlake Formation and back to surface
 receivers. Separately, Explor carried out 189 shots of PinPoint® sourcing with approximately
 5-m spacing along Line 502 over 3 days.

 The PinPoint source is an impulsive seismic source with a 24-kJ charge propellant, set off
 in a 1.5-in.-diameter hole drilled 12 in. deep. During the acquisition of the seismic survey, the
 PinPoint source experienced a series of mechanical failures that resulted in the sourcing of only
 one of the three planned lines (Figure A-1).

 During postcollection processing of the seismoelectric data, several activities were
 conducted to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. These included:

 • Standard time synchronization.
 • Stacking (using a moving window average).
 • Resampling from 4096 to 512 Hz.
 • Despiking.
 • Periodic noise removal with a notch filter.

 The notch filter was used to remove the effects of the 60-Hz harmonic interference caused
 by nearby powerlines. Both datasets were detrended using a least-squares fitting, subtracting a
 best-fit second-order polynomial from record amplitudes. The datasets were then resampled to
 1000 Hz to match the sample rate of the vibroseis sweep to maintain a consistent sample rate
 between source types. A Kaiser window-based FIR 1 filter (2-4-128-138 Hz) was applied to remove
 additional noise, with the pass band selection based on the frequency content of the vibroseis
 sweep. The vibroseis shot traces were designatured via spectral division as described for seismic
 data in Brittle (2001). After the initial processing, both datasets consist of 6-s-long seismoelectric
 traces. Butterworth notch filters were applied to both datasets to suppress additional powerline
 harmonics (7.5, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 Hz). Lastly, spectral subtraction with half-wave
 rectification based on Boll (1979) was used to reduce the strength of the remaining stationary noise
 in the data. The data were split into leading 3-s signal plus noise and trailing 3-s noise windows.
 The reduced noise residual signal spectrum was calculated by subtracting the trailing noise
 amplitude spectra from the leading signal plus noise amplitude spectrum.

 INTERLAKE ROCK PHYSICS

 Rock Physics Background

 Rock physics analysis provides the means to connect rock properties and fluid saturation
 determined from geology or petrophysics with measurements from geophysics. Once the
 relationships are established, the process can be reversed so that rock properties and fluid
 saturations can be predicted in the subsurface away from outcrops and well control. Additionally,
 rock physics can be used to predict how changes to rock properties and fluid saturation will affect
 the geophysical measurements and thus the feasibility of using geophysics to monitor injection of
 CO2.

 For geophysical methods to detect the CO2 distribution in the subsurface, the changes in
 saturation between the native fluid and CO2 must change the physical property measured. Seismic
 methods require changes in elastic moduli or density, and electromagnetic methods require
 changes in resistivity or another electrical property. The change must occur over a sufficient area
 or volume for them to be detectable at the surface. Rock physics analysis is necessary to determine
 feasibility of detection and for analysis of the time-lapse (4D) seismic changes observed between
 baseline and monitor datasets.

 Rock Physics

 Rock physics is necessary to determine feasibility of detecting changes in CO2 distribution
 in the Interlake between baseline and monitor seismic surveys. The feasibility analysis consists of
 data quality control, fluid property modeling, time tie and wavelet extraction, and modeling 1D
 synthetic fluid mixture scenarios. The rock physics analysis made use of Ikon Science RokDoc
 software.

 1D synthetic modeling was conducted to estimate the seismic response of injecting CO2 into
 the Interlake Formation. Fluid substitution was performed in the RokDoc dry Gassmann method
 (Gassmann, 1951) with Backus averaging, (Backus, 1962), using a 20-ft moving window, Vp, Vs,
 and density logs. Models were created for original saturation, 25% of the original fluid mixture
 was replaced by CO2, and 50% of the original fluid mixture was replaced by CO2. The fluids are
 homogeneously mixed using Wood’s equation. No change in pore pressure is applied.

 If CO2 is miscible with oil, then it can be modeled by homogeneous mixing (Wood’s
 equation). Brine and CO2 are immiscible, making a patchy response more likely. Maximum
 response is achieved with homogeneous mixing at low saturations; patchy fluids have a smaller
 response. The true response is likely between the homogeneous and the patchy mixtures.

 The fluid substitution results were used to model 1D amplitude variation with offset/angle
 (AVO) synthetics. A statistical zero phase wavelet 56-ms long with a 37-Hz peak frequency was
 extracted from the Pennel 3D between 1000 and 1600 ms using traces within 300 ft of the 12-7B
 well. The wavelet is shown in Figure A-10. The primary reflection only models are produced by
 convolution of the wavelet with reflection coefficient series generated using Aki and Richards
 (1980) three-term equation for angles of incidence between 0° and 45°. The model results are
 shown in Figure A-11.

 The in situ model results suggest that the top Interlake produces a strong positive reflection
 and that internally the Interlake appears to be elastically transparent. The base of Interlake
 produces a weak negative reflection. The fluid substitution models suggest a small decrease in
 amplitude at the top of the Interlake with increasing CO2 saturation. The response is mostly
 controlled by change in Vp. The minimum in the AVO curve is expected to be at a 35° of incidence.
 Patchy saturation will reduce the 4D response. The models do not account for the influence of
 noise on detectability of the time-lapse response.

 Rock Physics Conclusions

 Rock physics analysis suggests that top of the Interlake Formation is expected to have a
 primary reflection of similar strength to the overlying formations. Internally, the Interlake is likely
 to be seismically transparent, and the base Interlake may have a weak reflection. Interlake 4D
 response is expected to be strongest at the top and absent elsewhere. The results may be different
 in areas with strong karsting or thick paleosols associated with parasequence boundaries.

 ASE DATA ANALYSIS

 Seismoelectric data analysis relies on identifying seismoelectric wave types and locating
 subsurface features based on their travel times. Case histories suggest that strong coseismic arrivals
 may be associated with the arrival of seismic direct P-waves or surface waves (Dean and others,
 2012; Butler and others, 2018). The composition of the coseismic wave field depends on many
 factors, including source type, survey geometry, climate, and geology. The strength of coseismic
 arrivals also depends on the orientation of dipole electrodes relative to the shot point. Haines and
 others (2007) show that radial dipole orientation has a strong response, while tangential dipole
 orientation produces zero response.

 Seismoelectric data are displayed in the following sections as common shot gathers,
 common dipole gathers, and stacks. A common shot gather consists of all the dipole traces
 recorded from a single shot. A common dipole gather consists of all the traces recorded by a single
 dipole. Stacks are produced by averaging all the traces by gather in a set of gathers.

 Common Shot Gathers

 The data were plotted as common shot gathers for sets of ZEN receivers with respect to
 radial offset from the triggering source. Initial focus was placed on the data from the PinPoint
 source, as the nature of the impulsive source and the closeness of the source locations to the
 receivers suggested the highest chance of observing signals related to the shot. For example,
 Figure A-12 displays the first 450 ms of observed data following a PinPoint shot acquired between
 Stations 1 and 3. The conditioned data quality is good at Stations 1 and 3, but Station 2 is
 contaminated by strong EM noise. The data do not show a clear initial impact or maximum value
 that may relate to the initiation signal from the PinPoint shot. With no high-magnitude response
 observed, the normalization scaling makes the signals uniformly large. However, events were
 observed within the record occurring between 20 and 120 ms, displayed as red dashed lines on
 Figure D-1, where the signal has a constant arrival time and changes sign between Stations 1 and
 3 based on the position to the left or right with respect to the shot location, as is to be expected
 from interfacial waves. Apparent interfacial waves suggest the presence of a layered structure or
 saturation changes with depth.

 A similar example, using a vibroseis source, is shown in Figure A-13, which displays the
 first 450 ms of data where the source is between Stations 1 and 2. These data also indicate the low
 amplitude of coseismic waves relative to EM noise. However, the events between 20 and 70 ms,
 highlighted by the red dashed lines, suggest the presence of apparent interfacial waves.

 The seismoelectric traces can be compared with the collocated seismic. Seismoelectric
 coseismic arrivals are expected to have the same travel time as the related seismic shot gather
 arrivals. In contrast, flat seismoelectric interfacial responses are expected to arrive at the seismic
 one-way time of the associated processed seismic arrivals.

 Common Dipole Stacks

 The characteristics of interfacial waves can be enhanced through the calculation of common
 dipole stacks. A common dipole stack is created by averaging a group of traces recorded by a
 dipole receiver produced by shots within a given distance of the dipole. When shot points are
 closely spaced, interface waves are expected to arrive at a dipole receiver with constant travel time.
 A sign correction is applied during stacking so that interface waves from shots on opposite sides
 of the dipole sum constructively. Coseismic waves, like seismic waves, arrive with a time delay

 A-19

 that depends on the source–receiver distance. Coseismic waves and random EM noise 1 amplitudes
 are reduced by stacking. One trace is produced for each dipole. The results for PinPoint and
 vibroseis sources, with maximum shot distances of 300 and 250 m, respectively, are shown in
 Figure A-15. The median stack fold (number of individual traces averaged and grouped to create
 a single stack) for PinPoint is 68, and for vibroseis, 22, giving random noise reductions of 0.12
 (−18 dB) and 0.21 (−13 dB), respectively. Several apparent interfacial events are highlighted by
 the blue lines in Figure A-15, extending to 130 ms or later.

 Figure A-15. Common dipole stacks consisting of a stack of all PinPoint shots within 300 m
 or all vibroseis sources within 250 m of each dipole. Blue lines indicate apparent interfacial
 responses.

 The common dipole stacks can be stretched twice in time to tie with the PSTM seismic from
 Line 502, as shown in Figure A-16. The apparent interface waves in Figure D-5 may correlate with

 and 300 ms is associated with a deeper arrival. The correlation with seismic suggests that some

 ASE Discussion

 The collected seismoelectric data from the Pennel Field study were contaminated with strong
 EM noise created by power lines and oilfield production equipment. Some of the noise was
 removed by application of notch filters, spectral subtraction, and common dipole stacking. The
 processed data contained weak coseismic and apparent interfacial waves, as well as strong residual
 EM noise. The coseismic waves generally occurred as short segments with dips consistent with
 expected seismic arrivals. An envelope attribute applied to the PinPoint data may show an
 amplitude increase associated with arrival of the direct P-wave on common dipole gather. The
 results from PinPoint and vibroseis are comparable; however, PinPoint is a lower-energy source,
 which is illustrated by the higher fold required for the PinPoint common dipole stack. The creation
 of common dipole stacks made the interpretation of interfacial wave arrivals possible between the
 surface and 300 ms (ASE time). The stacks increase confidence in predicting an ASE response for
 deeper events by tying apparent interface responses with seismic reflections recorded by Line 502
 of the PSTM seismic data (Figure A-1). However, for the Pennel Field study, detection of
 interfacial waves indicating the Interlake Formation (≥800 ms ASE time) was not possible. Since
 the ASE dataset experienced high EM noise levels, further detailed interpretation was not justified.

 ASE Lessons Learned

 Imaging deep structures using ASE methods is, even under the most ideal circumstances, a
 challenge. The signals from the small EM vertical dipoles generated from interfacial responses to
 pressure waves from a seismic source are small and decay at a rate of 1/r3 even with the high
 sensitivity in our equipment using the Zonge ZEN receivers and their 32-bit analog-to-digital
 converter and with the large vibroseis seismic sources. However, the amplitude of the signals from
 deeper layers may exist below the background noise threshold at the site and is simply not
 detectable in this dataset.

 A-21

 These signals are, of course, complex, and even with the well-understood stratigraphy and
 associated formation top depths at the site, it proved difficult to separate interfacial signals from
 coseismic signals for late arrivals, often associated with formation boundaries. Accordingly, while
 it has not been possible in this experiment to identify signals in the ASE data associated with
 interface responses from the Interlake Formation at a depth of 8000 ft (2439 m) or 1600-ms TWT,
 a response from CO2 injected into a shallower layer such as the Inyan Kara (ranging from 3800 to
 4800 ft [1158 to 1463 m] below surface depending on location) might be more detectable with
 improved data collection.

 While the initial seismoelectric deployment attempted to measure both inline and crossline
 responses simultaneously, a single inline deployment of receivers with a denser spacing here
 would have provided improved reliability, data quality, and interpretation. Future arrays should
 focus on a single inline deployment to ensure that more comprehensive data can be collected and
 possibly increase the chances of observing time-lapse differences within the data.

 Detection of an interfacial response from the Interlake Formation is likely to require an
 improved acquisition geometry, collection of ASE data using more dipole receivers, and vertical
 stacking of source shots/sweeps. Improved geometry would allow the application of seismic
 multichannel processing methods and direct correlation between ASE and seismic datasets.

 The framework for successful synthetic modeling of active seismoelectric responses was
 established using a code that simultaneously solves both the poroelastic and EM wave equations.
 This code required a significant amount of information about geology to be known in advance,
 and this comprehensive geological study enabled the generation of appropriate ranges of property
 values, ensuring that the models could be as accurate as possible. Future surveys could utilize this
 modeling in similar areas to estimate signal strength and patterns for a variety of seismic source
 types and amplitudes, thereby helping to further guide the deployment of sensors and optimal
 source and receiver spacing.

 ASE Conclusions

 ASE methods hold the potential to provide a novel CO2 injection monitoring method that
 can yield useful information about the distribution of fluids within the reservoir. The methods can
 complement a standard seismic survey, whether 2D or 3D, and a measurable signal response for
 mapping CO2 plume movement will require time-lapse ASE surveys. As shown in this project,
 existing EM hardware can be adapted for seismoelectric measurements.

 For future pre- and post-CO2 injection monitor surveys, the ASE dipole receivers should be
 reoriented, deployed more densely, and focused along one of the main vibroseis source lines to
 improve offset measurements and maintain the previous array locations for improved time-lapse
 analysis. Time-lapse measurements, assuming they can be made with as much accuracy as possible
 in the positioning of sources and receivers between the two surveys, are a particularly appropriate
 way to use the ASE method, as the coseismic wave energy recorded should be essentially identical
 between the two surveys, leaving only the change in interfacial response. The time-lapse change
 in signal would be interpreted to provide the lateral extent and potentially the vertical distribution
 of the CO2 injected over time into the Interlake Formation. This interpretation would be integrated

 A-22

 with and used to update geologic modeling and simulation, and those results would inform the
 LCA of the stacked storage complex. However, as the site operators decided not to continue with
 CO2 injection, the planned monitor ASE survey was not carried out.
