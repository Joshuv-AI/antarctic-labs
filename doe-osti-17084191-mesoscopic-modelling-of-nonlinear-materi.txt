ABSTRACT
This report summarizes LDRD-supported research on dynamic light–matter interactions in laser-
triggered switches and exceptional-point photonic systems. For laser-triggered vacuum switches, we
developed an integrated simulation framework coupling a microscopic laser–material interaction
model to kinetic PIC-DSMC plasma simulations. The model predicts laser-induced electron
emission and cathode vaporization, then follows plasma formation and switch closure across a
high-voltage gap. Results indicate that commercially available diode-laser pulses can generate
sufficient cathode vapor density for nanosecond-scale triggering. For exceptional-point lasers,
we developed a Floquet stability theory showing that self-generated frequency combs can exhibit
bistability and period-doubling cascades, enabling repeated halving of the comb repetition rate
without reducing bandwidth. We also developed QNM-PALT, a quasinormal-mode reduced-order
formulation that captures dynamic gain and frequency-comb formation in realistic multidimensional
cavities. Together, these efforts establish predictive tools for designing compact pulsed-power
switches and self-time-modulated photonic devices.

1. INTRODUCTION

This report summarizes three related research efforts carried out under this LDRD project, all
centered on the predictive modeling and exploitation of nonequilibrium light–matter interactions.
Although the applications considered here span distinct physical regimes—laser-triggered high-
voltage switching and exceptional-point laser frequency-comb generation—they share a common
theme: optical fields can drive material systems into strongly dynamic states that cannot be
captured by conventional steady-state or reduced phenomenological models. In each case, the
relevant system behavior emerges from the coupled evolution of electromagnetic fields, material
degrees of freedom, and nonlinear feedback. Developing accurate and computationally efficient
models for these interactions is therefore essential both for understanding the underlying physics
and for designing practical devices.
The first part of the project addressed laser-triggered vacuum switches, where an optical pulse
incident on an electrode initiates electron emission, material vaporization, plasma formation, and
ultimately electrical breakdown across a high-voltage gap [3]. Laser triggering is attractive for
pulsed-power systems because it can offer precise timing, electrical isolation, reduced jitter, and
potentially longer component lifetimes. However, many prior demonstrations of laser-triggered
switching have relied on relatively energetic or specialized laser systems. A central question
motivating this work was whether commercially available semiconductor laser diodes, operating
at moderate pulse energies and power densities, could provide sufficient optical energy to trigger a
high-voltage switch on useful time scales.
Answering that question requires a model that connects the laser parameters directly to the switch
response. The laser pulse first interacts with the electrode material, where absorption modifies the
electron and hole populations, heats the carrier and lattice subsystems, and produces thermionic
and field-assisted electron emission. At sufficiently high lattice temperature, the same optical
pulse vaporizes electrode material into the gap. The emitted electrons and neutral atoms then seed
a plasma discharge. Electrons accelerated by the applied field ionize the vaporized material, the
resulting plasma grows across the gap, and the discharge eventually becomes self-sustaining through
ion-induced secondary electron emission. Because these processes span electronic, thermal, kinetic,
and circuit-level time scales, a predictive treatment requires coupling models that are usually
considered separately.

The second and third parts of the project focused on a different class of nonequilibrium optical
systems: exceptional-point lasers. Exceptional points are non-Hermitian degeneracies where two
or more resonant modes coalesce in both eigenfrequency and eigenvector. Lasers provide a natural
platform for realizing exceptional points because gain and loss can be distributed nonuniformly
across the cavity. Near an exceptional point, the small frequency splitting and strong modal overlap
can profoundly alter the nonlinear laser dynamics. In particular, the population inversion of the gain
medium need not remain stationary above threshold. Instead, the inversion can oscillate coherently
in time, creating an internal modulation that couples optical frequencies and generates a frequency
comb.
This mechanism differs from conventional comb generation. In mode-locked lasers or many
resonator-based comb sources, the repetition rate is tied to the cavity free spectral range. By contrast,
in an exceptional-point laser, the comb spacing is set primarily by the oscillation frequency of the
dynamic population inversion, which is approximately determined by the splitting of the near-EP
modes. Because that splitting can be much smaller than the cavity free spectral range, exceptional-
point lasers provide a possible route to compact comb sources with low repetition rates. This is
especially attractive for integrated photonics, where reducing the repetition rate by increasing the
cavity length is often undesirable or impractical.

The third part of the project developed a reduced-order modeling framework for these same
dynamic-gain exceptional-point lasers [2]. While Maxwell–Bloch simulations and periodic-

components. Conventional temporal coupled-mode theory is computationally compact but usually
represents gain with phenomenological static saturation, which is insufficient for this regime.

This reduced formulation provides both physical insight and major computational advantages. It
shows that the EP comb can be understood as a self-consistent process in which the two central near-
EP modal components beat against each other, drive a dynamic population inversion, and thereby
create an internal time modulation of the gain. That modulation scatters light into additional
comb teeth, which further contribute to the inversion dynamics. The comb is therefore generated
by repeated resonant excitation of passive QNMs mediated by a self-organized, time-periodic
gain distribution. Computationally, QNM-PALT eliminates the need to discretize the population
inversion throughout the cavity during the nonlinear solve. One-dimensional examples that require
long high-resolution Maxwell–Bloch simulations can be solved in seconds with QNM-PALT. The
method also extends directly to two-dimensional and three-dimensional resonators, where direct
time-domain Maxwell–Bloch simulation would be far more costly.
Together, the three research thrusts summarized in this report demonstrate a consistent strategy
for treating complex optically driven systems: begin from physics-based equations for the relevant
material response, identify the dominant time scales and modes, and build reduced but predictive
models that retain the essential nonlinear coupling mechanisms. In the laser-triggered switch
problem, this strategy connects microscopic laser heating and material emission to kinetic plasma
formation and circuit-level switch closure. In the exceptional-point laser problem, it connects
Maxwell–Bloch gain dynamics to comb stability, bistability, period doubling, and reduced-order
quasinormal coupled-mode descriptions.
The results also point toward practical device opportunities. For high-voltage switching, the
integrated laser–material/PIC-DSMC calculations indicate that compact diode lasers can plausibly
trigger vacuum switches on nanosecond time scales when sufficient cathode vapor density is
produced. For photonics, the EP-comb stability analysis shows that nonlinear period doubling can
reduce comb repetition rates without requiring large cavities or extremely fine EP tuning, while
QNM-PALT provides a scalable design tool for realistic on-chip geometries. In both cases, the

project advances the ability to design devices whose function depends on dynamic, nonequilibrium
light–matter interactions rather than on static material response.
The remainder of this report is organized around these three contributions. The first chapter de-
scribes the integrated laser–material and kinetic plasma model for laser-triggered vacuum switches
and its implications for diode-triggered pulsed-power components. The second chapter presents
the Floquet stability theory of exceptional-point frequency combs, including bistability and period-
doubling cascades. The third chapter develops the QNM-PALT framework for dynamic gain
in exceptional-point lasers and demonstrates its use in one- and two-dimensional resonator sys-
tems. Collectively, these results establish a foundation for future work on compact laser-triggered
switches, exceptional-point comb sources, and broader classes of self-time-modulated photonic
and plasma devices.

2. INTEGRATED LASER–MATERIAL AND KINETIC PLASMA
 MODELING OF LASER-TRIGGERED VACUUM SWITCHES

As part of this project, we developed an integrated numerical framework for predicting the oper-
ation of laser-triggered vacuum switches in a regime relevant to compact, commercially available
semiconductor laser diodes [3]. The work addresses a central modeling challenge for laser-triggered
high-voltage switches: the switch response depends on a sequence of tightly connected physical pro-
cesses that begin with optical absorption in an electrode, continue through nonequilibrium carrier
heating, thermionic and field-assisted electron emission, and laser-induced material vaporization,
and culminate in plasma formation and electrical breakdown across the electrode gap. Rather than
prescribing an electron or neutral source at the cathode, the study computes those source terms
from a microscopic laser–material interaction model and then uses them as boundary conditions in
a kinetic particle-in-cell/direct-simulation Monte Carlo simulation of the evolving discharge.
The motivation for the study is the possibility of replacing larger, higher-energy laboratory laser
systems with compact laser diode arrays for triggering high-voltage switches. Such a transition
would be attractive for pulsed-power and other high-voltage systems because laser diodes can offer
reduced size, improved robustness, higher efficiency, and lower cost. However, diode systems
operate at moderate optical power densities relative to many prior laser-triggered switch experi-
ments. It is therefore necessary to determine whether diode-accessible pulses can provide enough
electron emission and electrode vaporization to form a sufficiently dense plasma in the switch gap
on useful time scales. The paper focuses on this diode-relevant regime, with representative laser
power densities of order 1011 W/m2 , rather than ultrafast or extremely high-intensity regimes in
which direct gap ionization, avalanche processes, or explosive emission may dominate.
The modeled switch consists of a cathode–anode gap initially in vacuum, with the cathode made of
graphite and the anode biased to 1 kV across a 1 mm gap. An 850 nm laser pulse is incident on the
graphite cathode through an aperture in the anode. In the absence of laser-induced vaporization,
the switch is assumed to remain below its breakdown condition. When the laser heats the cathode,
the surface emits electrons and vaporized carbon atoms into the gap. The electrons can ionize the
carbon atoms, and the resulting plasma evolves until it supports a self-sustaining discharge and the
switch closes. The essential question is how the laser pulse energy and pulse duration determine
the time required for this transition.
The numerical approach is divided into two stages. The first stage treats the laser–material inter-
action inside a near-surface interaction volume of the cathode. This model describes how laser
photons are absorbed by the electrode material, how electron and hole populations evolve, how
the carrier and lattice temperatures rise, and how electron emission and material vaporization
occur. The second stage uses the computed electron and neutral carbon fluxes as time-dependent
injection boundary conditions in a particle-in-cell/direct-simulation Monte Carlo calculation. The
PIC-DSMC model then follows the self-consistent plasma evolution, including charged-particle

 15

motion in the electric field, electron-neutral collisions, ionization, excitation, charge exchange, wall
interactions, ion-induced secondary electron emission, and photoemission associated with radiative
processes.

The separation into two stages is justified by a separation of time scales. Carrier–carrier and
carrier–phonon relaxation in the illuminated material occur on femtosecond-to-picosecond time
scales, while the plasma expansion and breakdown process in the millimeter-scale gap occurs on
nanosecond-to-microsecond time scales. Under these conditions, the laser–material calculation can
be performed first, and its output can be passed one-way into the plasma simulation. The paper notes
that this approximation neglects possible feedback from the evolving plasma sheath and cathode
electric field onto the emission process, but argues that it is appropriate for the moderate-power
diode-relevant regime considered. A future fully coupled treatment would allow the instantaneous
plasma state to modify the surface emission rates.

For the representative graphite-cathode case, the paper examines a laser pulse energy of 0.1 mJ
and a pulse duration of 100 ns, corresponding to a peak optical power of approximately 1 kW. The
laser pulse begins to significantly heat the cathode as the optical power rises. Interband absorption

moderate-intensity case considered, although the model predicts a noticeable lag between carrier
and lattice temperature at higher pulse intensities. As the electron temperature increases, thermionic
and field-assisted electron emission into the gap begin. As the lattice temperature increases, carbon
vaporization becomes appreciable.
The output of the laser–material model is therefore a time history of emitted electron flux, emitted
electron energy, vaporized carbon atom flux, and vaporized carbon velocity. These quantities form
the cathode boundary condition for the PIC-DSMC simulation. The plasma model is implemented
using the Aleph code, a massively parallel electrostatic PIC-DSMC code capable of simulating
nonequilibrium plasmas in complex geometries. In the study summarized here, the switch is
modeled one-dimensionally to reduce computational cost and to scope the feasibility of diode-
triggered switching. This one-dimensional approximation neglects geometric expansion away
from the laser spot and assumes a spatially uniform, top-hat laser profile. The authors note that
the modeling framework can be extended to three dimensions with spatially varying laser energy
fluxes.
In the plasma simulations, carbon atoms, carbon ions, and electrons evolve across the 1 mm gap.

as ion-neutral elastic scattering and resonant charge exchange. Ion-induced secondary electron

The simulated switch evolution proceeds through several physically distinct stages. Early in the
laser pulse, electrons are emitted from the cathode, but the vaporized carbon density in the gap
remains too low for significant ionization. The electron current is then space-charge limited,
and most emitted photoelectrons are reflected back to the cathode by a virtual cathode. The few
electrons that escape accelerate across the gap with relatively few collisions because the neutral
carbon density remains low.
As the laser pulse approaches its peak, the carbon vapor density near the cathode grows. Once
the neutral density reaches values above approximately 1023 m−3 over a significant portion of the
gap, electron-impact ionization becomes appreciable. A quasi-neutral plasma begins to form and

 18

expands outward from the cathode with the advancing carbon vapor front. The voltage drop
collapses across the quasi-neutral plasma region and becomes concentrated near the remaining
non-neutral sheath. During this phase, the electron current through the gap rises above the initial
vacuum space-charge-limited value, but the photoelectron flux produced by the laser remains larger
than the current that the gap can support, so a virtual cathode persists close to the cathode surface.
At later times, the neutral carbon fills the full 1 mm gap. The estimated transit time for carbon
across the gap is approximately 100 ns, consistent with a vapor velocity of order 2000 m/s at a
temperature of several thousand kelvin. The plasma density increases, the virtual cathode shrinks,
and the switch approaches closure. Eventually, as the laser pulse decays, the photoelectron flux
decreases below the current that the plasma-filled gap can support. At this point, ions generated
in the plasma can reach the cathode and produce ion-induced secondary electrons. The discharge
then transitions from being primarily sustained by laser-induced photoelectron emission to being
sustained by ion-induced secondary electron emission. Once this occurs, the switch remains
conducting even after the laser pulse is no longer providing substantial photoelectron current.
A key result is that complete switch closure for the representative 0.1 mJ, 100 ns pulse occurs roughly
50 ns after the peak of the laser pulse and roughly 100 ns after significant carbon vaporization begins.
The delay is not determined solely by electron emission, but by the time required to inject enough
neutral carbon into the gap, ionize it, form a quasi-neutral plasma, and establish a self-sustaining
discharge. This emphasizes that the vaporized cathode material is not merely a secondary effect; it
is essential to breakdown in the near-vacuum, moderate-laser-energy regime considered.
The study also performs a parametric sweep over laser pulse energy and pulse duration. The switch
trigger time is defined as the time between the point at which the laser reaches 10% of its peak
power and the point at which the anode current density reaches 0.74 MA/m2 , which is ten times the
empty-gap space-charge-limited current density and roughly half the typical quasi-steady discharge
current density. The simulations show that, above a threshold pulse energy of approximately
0.1 mJ, the trigger time is relatively insensitive to further increases in laser energy. This threshold
corresponds to the energy required to vaporize enough graphite to produce a carbon density above
roughly 1023 m−3 in the gap. Once that density is achieved, adding more vaporized material does
not substantially reduce the time needed for breakdown because the limiting process becomes the
transport and distribution of vaporized material across the gap.
The threshold energy depends on pulse duration. For long pulses, particularly microsecond-scale
pulses, a greater fraction of the deposited laser energy is conducted into the bulk cathode rather than
contributing efficiently to surface vaporization. As a result, longer pulses require higher energy to
achieve the same vaporization and breakdown condition. For shorter pulses, energy is deposited
more rapidly, but the switch does not close immediately after the pulse if the vaporized material has
not yet filled the gap. Thus, reducing pulse duration below roughly 10 ns does not proportionally
reduce the switch trigger time. In the short-pulse limit, the trigger time is controlled by carbon
transport across the millimeter-scale gap rather than by the optical pulse duration.
For a fixed laser pulse energy of 0.1 mJ, the paper reports trigger times over pulse durations ranging
from 1 ns to 1 𝜇s. For long pulses, the trigger time is comparable to the pulse duration. For
example, a 1 𝜇s pulse produces a trigger time of approximately the same order, with the trigger-to-
pulse-duration ratio close to unity. For very short pulses, however, the trigger time can be tens of

 19

times longer than the laser pulse duration. A 1 ns pulse does not trigger the switch in 1 ns; instead,
the switch closure is delayed until the vaporized material has expanded far enough into the gap
to support breakdown. The paper estimates that if cathode material expands at 25 km/s, it still
requires approximately 40 ns to cross a 1 mm gap. This sets a practical lower bound on trigger time
for the modeled geometry and mechanism.
The main physical conclusion is that, in the diode-relevant regime studied, laser-triggered switching
is limited by neutral material generation and transport rather than simply by prompt photoelectron
emission. The laser must first vaporize enough cathode material to raise the effective neutral
density in the gap to the level required for Townsend-like breakdown. The emitted electrons then
ionize that vapor, creating a plasma that collapses the gap voltage and allows current to exceed the
vacuum space-charge limit. As the laser pulse ends, ion-induced secondary emission takes over
from laser-induced electron emission and sustains the discharge. The switch is therefore closed by
a coupled sequence of laser heating, vaporization, ionization, plasma expansion, sheath formation,
and secondary-emission-sustained conduction.
The paper’s conclusions are encouraging for practical laser-diode-triggered switches. The simula-
tions indicate that commercially available laser diode arrays can plausibly provide sufficient pulse
energy and power to trigger high-voltage vacuum switches with breakdown times in the tens of
nanoseconds to microseconds, depending on pulse duration and switch geometry. In particular, a
pulse energy of order 0.1 mJ can be sufficient for the modeled graphite-cathode, 1 mm-gap, 1 kV
switch. The study further suggests that commercially available diode bars capable of delivering
high continuous-wave or quasi-continuous-wave power through fiber delivery could be current-
modulated to produce trigger times over a useful range without requiring large laboratory laser
systems.
Several limitations and future directions are identified. The present calculations use one-way
coupling from the laser–material model to the plasma model, so the plasma sheath and time-
dependent cathode electric field do not feed back into the laser-induced emission calculation. A
fully self-consistent two-way coupled model would be important for regimes in which surface fields
evolve rapidly or strongly enhance emission. The model is also demonstrated in one dimension,
so future three-dimensional simulations are needed to capture electrode geometry, nonuniform
laser profiles, lateral plasma expansion, and local surface deformation. In addition, more accurate
thermo-field emission models could replace the simplified tunneling treatment, and structured
cathode surfaces such as photonic crystals or metasurfaces could be explored as a way to enhance
laser absorption, local fields, electron emission, and vaporization without increasing incident laser
energy.
Overall, the paper provides a physics-based computational bridge between laser parameters and
switch performance. By replacing prescribed source terms with a microscopic calculation of laser-
induced electron emission and material vaporization, and by coupling those source terms to kinetic
plasma simulations, the work enables predictive assessment of compact laser-triggered switch
concepts. The principal result is that moderate-energy laser diode pulses can, under appropriate
conditions, generate enough cathode vapor and plasma density to close a high-voltage vacuum
switch on technologically relevant time scales.

3. BI-STABILITY AND PERIOD-DOUBLING CASCADE OF
 FREQUENCY COMBS IN EXCEPTIONAL-POINT LASERS

In a second study performed as part of this project [1], we analyze the stability and nonlinear
dynamics of frequency combs generated by lasers operating near an exceptional point (EP), with
particular emphasis on two phenomena: bistability between distinct comb states and a period-
doubling cascade that repeatedly halves the comb repetition rate. The work builds on the observation
that lasers tuned near an EP can self-generate optical frequency combs through dynamic modulation
of the population inversion in the gain medium. Unlike conventional mode-locked or cavity-
nonlinearity-based combs, whose repetition rates are typically constrained by the cavity free spectral
range, EP combs can exhibit repetition rates determined by the frequency splitting of near-coalesced
modes. Because this splitting can be much smaller than the free spectral range, EP lasers offer a
route toward compact frequency comb generators with radio-frequency repetition rates.
The central advance of the paper is a Floquet stability theory for EP-comb states. An EP comb
is a time-periodic lasing solution of the Maxwell–Bloch equations, meaning that the optical field,
polarization, and population inversion form a limit cycle rather than a time-independent steady
state. The stability of such a limit cycle cannot be determined by ordinary steady-state laser
stability analysis. Instead, one must examine whether small perturbations around the periodically
varying comb state grow or decay in time. Here, we formulate this problem using Floquet theory
and show that the dominant Floquet frequency determines whether a comb is stable. When the
imaginary part of the dominant Floquet frequency crosses zero, the comb becomes unstable and
transitions to a new dynamical state. In particular, when that crossing occurs at half the original
comb repetition rate, the transition produces period doubling: new spectral lines appear halfway
between the original comb lines, reducing the repetition rate by a factor of two while preserving
the overall comb bandwidth.

This period-doubling cascade is important because it reduces the repetition rate without requiring
the cavity to be tuned closer to the EP. In principle, one could reduce the initial EP-comb repetition
rate by making the two modes even closer in frequency, but exact EP tuning is experimentally
challenging and can make the system highly sensitive to perturbations. The period-doubling
mechanism offers a different route: start with a reasonably robust near-EP comb and then use
nonlinear dynamics to halve its repetition rate repeatedly. This provides a path toward compact
optical comb generators with very small line spacings, potentially in the radio-frequency range,
while avoiding the need for extremely fine EP tuning.
An additional notable result is that the comb bandwidth remains approximately preserved through
period doubling. When new lines appear halfway between the old comb lines, the total spectral span
does not collapse. Instead, the number of lines increases while the line spacing decreases. This is
advantageous for comb applications because one can reduce the repetition rate while maintaining a
broad usable optical bandwidth. In other words, period doubling increases the spectral line density
within roughly the same bandwidth.
The stability analysis also predicts bistability. Over a finite pump interval, two different EP-comb
solutions are simultaneously stable. In the studied cavity, one stable branch corresponds to the
original comb, while another stable branch belongs to the period-doubled cascade. In this bistable
pump range, the final lasing state depends on the system history and initial conditions. If the pump
is increased gradually from a lower value, the laser can remain on one branch until that branch loses
stability, at which point it jumps to another branch. Conversely, if the pump is decreased gradually
from a higher value, the system can remain on the period-doubled branch chain until reaching a
lower stability boundary, where it jumps back to the original branch. This hysteresis-like behavior
is characteristic of nonlinear bistability.
The paper confirms these predictions using finite-difference time-domain simulations of the Maxwell–
Bloch equations. The simulations reproduce both the period-doubling transitions and the bistability

 26

predicted by PALT and the Floquet stability analysis. When the pump is swept upward, the simu-
lated laser follows the lower comb branch through the bistable region and then jumps to a higher
period-doubled branch when the lower branch ends. When the pump is swept downward, the
simulated laser follows the period-doubled branch chain and then jumps back to the original comb
branch at the lower boundary of the bistable region. The agreement between the time-domain
simulations and the Floquet/PALT calculations supports the validity of the stability theory.
The dynamics can also be interpreted geometrically in phase space. The Maxwell–Bloch state of
the laser is defined by the population inversion 𝐷, the real and imaginary parts of the electric field
𝐸, and the real and imaginary parts of the polarization 𝑃, distributed over the cavity. A single-mode
steady laser corresponds to a fixed point in this phase space when viewed in an appropriate rotating
frame. A frequency comb corresponds to a limit cycle, since the envelope and inversion repeat
periodically in time. At the comb threshold, the fixed point opens into a stable limit cycle. During
bistability, two stable limit cycles coexist, separated by unstable dynamics. During period doubling,
the limit cycle doubles its orbit: instead of returning to the same point after one period 𝑇𝑑 , the
system returns only after two periods, 2𝑇𝑑 . In the frequency domain, this corresponds directly to
halving the comb repetition rate.
At still higher pump strengths, the calculations and simulations become more challenging. Each
period-doubling transition increases the number of comb lines that must be retained in the Fourier-
domain PALT and Floquet calculations. As the number of relevant Fourier components grows, the
eigenvalue problem becomes computationally expensive. The time-domain simulations indicate
increasingly dense spectra at high pump, but the paper notes that distinguishing between an infinite
period-doubling cascade and the onset of more irregular dynamics requires further theoretical
development. The supplementary results show additional period doublings beyond those treated
in the main calculations, but eventually the repetition rate becomes too small to resolve within the
finite simulation time.
The broader implication of this work is that EP lasers support richer nonlinear dynamics than a
simple transition from single-mode lasing to comb generation. The dynamic gain medium creates
a periodically driven internal system, and the stability of its limit cycles can be understood through
Floquet eigenmodes. These eigenmodes determine not only whether a comb is stable, but also
what type of state emerges when it loses stability. In particular, the symmetry of the Floquet
spectrum naturally enables instabilities at half the comb spacing, giving rise to period doubling.
This mechanism may allow small EP laser cavities to generate low-repetition-rate frequency combs
without needing large physical cavity lengths.
The paper also points to potential applications of bistability. Because two distinct comb states can
exist at the same pump strength, the laser output can depend on initialization or prior pump history.
Such behavior could be useful for optical memory, switching, or signal-processing devices, where
different stable optical states represent different logical or functional states. The combination
of compact comb generation, repetition-rate tunability through nonlinear cascades, and bistable
switching behavior suggests that EP lasers may be useful not only as comb sources, but also as
nonlinear photonic elements.
In conclusion, our study provides a stability framework for time-periodic EP-comb states and use
it to identify two key nonlinear phenomena: bistability and period-doubling cascades. The theory

 27

begins with the Maxwell–Bloch equations, linearizes around a periodic comb solution, and uses
Floquet theory to determine the growth or decay of perturbations. A comb is stable when the
dominant Floquet frequency satisfies Im(𝜔 𝐹 ) < 0. Period doubling occurs when the dominant
Floquet mode crosses into instability at Re(𝜔 𝐹 ) = 𝜔 𝑑 /2, inserting new comb lines between the
original lines and reducing the repetition rate to 𝜔 𝑑 /2. Repeated crossings generate a cascade of
successively smaller repetition rates. These theoretical predictions are confirmed with time-domain
simulations and suggest a practical route to compact, robust, low-repetition-rate optical frequency
comb generators based on exceptional-point lasers.

4. QUASINORMAL COUPLED-MODE ANALYSIS OF DYNAMIC GAIN
 IN EXCEPTIONAL-POINT LASERS

This part of the project developed a quasinormal-mode-based coupled-mode framework for under-
standing and efficiently computing dynamic gain and frequency-comb formation in exceptional-
point lasers [2]. The central objective was to construct a reduced model that retains the physical
rigor of the Maxwell–Bloch equations while providing the compactness and interpretability of
coupled-mode theory. This is particularly important for lasers operating near exceptional points
(EPs), where the usual approximation of a stationary population inversion can fail and the gain
medium can develop coherent time-periodic oscillations. These inversion oscillations couple opti-
cal fields at different frequencies, allowing the laser to self-generate a frequency comb whose line
spacing is set by the dynamic gain response rather than by the cavity free spectral range.
The motivation for this work comes from a basic limitation of conventional laser modeling ap-
proaches. Standard temporal coupled-mode theory is powerful because it represents optical dy-
namics in terms of a small number of resonant amplitudes, but it generally treats gain phenomeno-
logically through stationary saturable-gain terms. This is inadequate for near-EP lasers in the
regime of interest here because the population inversion is not static: it is spatially nonuniform and
temporally periodic. Conversely, the full Maxwell–Bloch equations accurately capture the coupled
dynamics of the electromagnetic field, polarization, and carrier inversion, but direct time-domain
or frequency-domain solutions can be computationally expensive, particularly near EPs where
high numerical precision is required. Periodic-inversion ab initio laser theory (PALT) provides a
frequency-domain treatment of these dynamic-inversion comb states, but its direct implementation
still involves substantial spatial discretization and does not immediately yield a minimal modal
interpretation of the frequency-generation process.
The contribution of this work is the development of a quasinormal-mode PALT formulation, referred
to as QNM-PALT. The method expands the laser field in a small basis of passive-cavity quasinormal
modes (QNMs), thereby converting the spatially distributed nonlinear laser problem into a reduced
set of nonlinear equations for modal amplitudes and comb frequencies. The key physical idea is
that the pump-induced polarization in the gain medium acts as a source that excites the passive
resonances of the open cavity. Near an EP, the relevant response is dominated by the two nearly
coalesced modes, so the full spatial dependence of the optical field can be represented accurately
using only a few QNM profiles. The nonlinear and temporally dynamic population inversion then
appears as a frequency-coupling mechanism between the modal amplitudes.
A second important component of the method is the use of a Padé approximant to simplify the
nonlinear spatial integrals associated with gain saturation and spatial hole burning. In laser theory,
saturation is local: the inversion at each point in the gain medium is depleted according to the local
optical intensity. This creates a nonuniform population-inversion profile even when the pump is
spatially smooth. Directly evaluating this spatially dependent nonlinear saturation at every iteration

 29

is costly. We showed that the relevant integral functions can be accurately represented by low-
order rational approximations, particularly a Padé approximant of order [0/1]. This approximation
converts the QNM-PALT integral equations into algebraic nonlinear equations that can be solved
rapidly without spatial discretization of the inversion.

We demonstrated the method first in a one-dimensional gain–loss coupled cavity tuned near an
EP. The structure consists of an active cavity coupled to a passive lossy cavity, with distributed
Bragg reflector sections used to control radiation loss and inter-cavity coupling. The active cavity
is pumped with a tapered profile, and the passive cavity includes linear absorption. The system
is tuned so that two QNMs near the gain center form the relevant near-EP pair. These two modes
dominate the laser response because they lie closest to the gain resonance and have the strongest
excitation amplitudes.
Solving the full PALT equations for this one-dimensional system produces a frequency comb as
the pump is increased. The two central comb teeth connect continuously to the two passive QNMs,
while the other comb teeth arise from nonlinear wave mixing mediated by the dynamic population
inversion. The QNM-PALT calculation reproduces the same comb spectrum with good quantitative
accuracy. The remaining differences are attributed to the truncation to the two dominant QNMs
and the finite accuracy of the Padé approximation.
The computational advantage is substantial. Direct finite-difference time-domain simulations of
the Maxwell–Bloch equations require extremely fine spatial resolution near EPs because small
numerical errors can strongly affect the dynamics. For the one-dimensional example, converged

 34

time-domain simulations required very high spatial resolution and millions of time steps, leading
to simulation times of order hours on standard hardware. The earlier Green’s-function-based PALT
approach is faster in one dimension, but it still requires spatial discretization of the nonuniform
inversion and depends on analytic simplifications specific to one-dimensional systems. In contrast,
QNM-PALT eliminates the spatial degrees of freedom after the passive QNMs and the Padé
coefficients are computed. The resulting nonlinear algebraic problem can be solved in seconds
from a random initial guess.
The method was then extended to a two-dimensional coupled-disk laser system. This demonstra-
tion is important because realistic on-chip EP lasers are generally two- or three-dimensional, and
direct Maxwell–Bloch simulations in such systems are often computationally prohibitive. The two-
dimensional example consists of two coupled microdisk resonators supporting whispering-gallery
modes. Because each disk naturally supports nearly degenerate clockwise and counterclockwise
whispering-gallery modes, the coupled system initially contains multiple nearly degenerate super-
modes. A lossy nanotip is introduced to suppress two of these supermodes, leaving two low-loss
modes that form the relevant near-EP pair. Gain is applied only to one disk using a Gaussian pump
profile, creating the gain–loss contrast needed for EP operation.

and comb frequencies.
The two-dimensional coupled-disk calculation produced an EP-comb solution at the chosen pump
strength. A direct two-dimensional Maxwell–Bloch finite-difference time-domain simulation of
the same system would require millions of spatial pixels and extremely long simulation times to
resolve the near-EP dynamics and reach the steady comb state. QNM-PALT instead obtains the
comb solution within seconds after the passive QNMs are computed. The passive QNM calculation
itself can be performed efficiently with standard frequency-domain eigensolvers, and it does not
require solving the nonlinear time-dependent laser problem.
The physical interpretation provided by QNM-PALT is one of the most useful outcomes of this
work. In the near-EP comb regime, the laser field can be viewed as arising from repeated excitation
of the two dominant passive resonances by a dynamically modulated polarization source. The
central comb teeth correspond primarily to the original near-EP resonances. Their beating drives
a coherent oscillation of the population inversion. That oscillating inversion acts as an internal
time modulation of the gain medium, which scatters light between frequencies separated by the
modulation frequency. This produces additional comb teeth. Those new teeth further contribute

 35

to the inversion modulation and participate in additional mixing. Thus, the comb emerges from
a self-consistent loop: modal beating modulates the inversion, the modulated inversion couples
frequencies, and the coupled frequencies reinforce a stable periodic comb state.

This interpretation also clarifies why proximity to an EP is important. Near an EP, the two
relevant modes have both similar frequencies and strongly overlapping spatial profiles. The small
frequency splitting sets a low modulation frequency, while the modal coalescence enhances the
spatial overlap between the optical beat note and the gain medium. Together, these features allow
the dynamic inversion to build up coherently and destabilize the stationary-gain solution even before
a conventional second lasing mode reaches threshold. The result is a frequency comb produced by
dynamic gain rather than by ordinary multimode lasing.

The QNM-PALT framework therefore bridges three levels of description. At the most microscopic
semiclassical level, the Maxwell–Bloch equations describe the local interaction between the electric
field, polarization, and inversion. At the intermediate PALT level, the solution is represented as
a set of frequency components coupled by a dynamic inversion. At the modal level, QNM-PALT
expresses those components as amplitudes of passive-cavity resonances. This produces a reduced
model that is both physically transparent and computationally efficient.

The work also establishes that Padé approximants are a natural way to represent spatial-hole-burning
nonlinearities. A Taylor expansion of the saturation denominator can converge poorly when the
field intensity is large or nonuniform, whereas a rational approximation has the same qualitative
structure as saturable gain. By fitting the integral response directly, the Padé approximation captures
the effect of nonuniform pump and field profiles without explicitly retaining the spatial dependence
in the nonlinear solve. This idea is likely to be useful beyond EP combs, including in multimode
lasers where spatial hole burning and gain competition strongly affect the distribution of power
among modes.

Several broader consequences follow from this modeling capability. First, QNM-PALT makes
it practical to explore realistic two- and three-dimensional EP laser designs for compact coherent
frequency-comb generation. Since the nonlinear computation is reduced to a small algebraic system,
device parameters such as geometry, coupling strength, loss distribution, pump profile, and gain
bandwidth can be varied efficiently. Second, because dynamic inversion acts as an internal time
modulation, near-EP lasers may provide a platform for studying time-varying photonic phenomena
without an externally applied modulation signal. This could enable effects such as time-dependent
scattering, frequency conversion, and potentially nonreciprocal transmission generated by the laser’s
own nonlinear dynamics. Third, QNM-PALT provides a route to optimize comb quality, repetition
rate, bandwidth, and pump efficiency in compact nanophotonic devices.

In summary, this part of the project demonstrated a quasinormal coupled-mode formulation of
dynamic gain in exceptional-point lasers. By expanding the laser field in a small number of
passive QNMs and using a Padé approximation to represent spatial-hole-burning integrals, the
method converts the spatially and temporally nonlinear Maxwell–Bloch/PALT problem into a
compact algebraic system. The framework accurately reproduces single-mode lasing, captures self-
generated EP frequency combs, provides a modal interpretation of dynamic population inversion,
and scales naturally to multidimensional resonator geometries. This makes QNM-PALT a practical

 36

and physically transparent tool for designing and understanding EP lasers with self-time-modulated
gain.

5. CONCLUSION

This report summarized three complementary efforts to model and exploit dynamic light–matter in-
teractions in systems where conventional steady-state descriptions are insufficient. Together, these
studies demonstrate how optical excitation can drive coupled material, carrier, and electromag-
netic dynamics that give rise to useful device-scale behavior, including laser-triggered electrical
breakdown and self-generated optical frequency combs.
For laser-triggered vacuum switches, this project developed an integrated modeling framework that
connects microscopic laser–material interaction physics to kinetic plasma evolution in the switch
gap. The laser–material model predicts electron emission and cathode vaporization from the
incident laser pulse, while the PIC-DSMC simulation uses those outputs as boundary conditions to
follow plasma formation and switch closure. The results show that, for the representative graphite-
cathode geometry considered, a vaporized carbon density of order 1023 m−3 is required to support
breakdown, and that pulse energies near 0.1 mJ can be sufficient to trigger the switch. Above
this threshold, the trigger time is governed primarily by the time required for vaporized cathode
material to fill the gap, rather than by additional laser energy. These findings support the feasibility
of compact, laser-diode-triggered high-voltage switches with nanosecond-scale response.
For exceptional-point lasers, this project demonstrated that dynamic population inversion can
produce rich nonlinear comb dynamics beyond ordinary single-mode or multimode lasing. A
Floquet stability analysis of periodic Maxwell–Bloch solutions showed that EP combs can exhibit
bistability and period-doubling cascades. The period-doubling mechanism inserts new comb lines
between existing lines, reducing the repetition rate according to

Finally, this project developed QNM-PALT as an efficient reduced-order framework for modeling
dynamic gain in EP lasers. By expanding the field in a small number of passive quasinormal modes
and using a Padé approximation to capture spatial-hole-burning integrals, the method reduces a
spatially and temporally nonlinear Maxwell–Bloch problem to a compact algebraic system. This
provides both a physical interpretation of EP-comb formation and a scalable computational tool for
realistic multidimensional laser designs.
Overall, the work establishes predictive modeling capabilities for two classes of optically driven
nonequilibrium devices. In both cases, the key advances come from linking microscopic or local
material response to device-scale dynamics through reduced but physics-based models. These
approaches provide a foundation for future experimental validation, optimization of laser-triggered

modulated photonic systems.
