Robinsoniella Bacteremia in a Patient With Radiation Proctitis and Prior Colon Cancer: A Rare Case

Alexander Muacevic, John R Adler, Alexa Markl, Joshua Iskander, Ivan Hutchison, Simeon Mihaylov, Jeffrey Lach

Abstract

Robinsoniella species are strictly anaerobic, Gram-positive, spore-forming bacilli infrequently associated with human bloodstream infections. We present a case of a 49-year-old woman with a history of colon and anal carcinoma treated with radiation and chemotherapy. She presented with fever, dyspnea, and worsening diarrhea. Blood cultures grew Serratia marcescens, Robinsoniella species, and Clostridium species, and she was initially treated with intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam and vancomycin, followed by cefepime, before ultimately transitioning to ertapenem. Although no treatment is currently established for Robinsoniella peoriensis, evidence remains insufficient to define an optimal antimicrobial regimen. This case highlights the pathogenic potential of Robinsoniella species in immunocompromised patients and underscores the importance of considering gastrointestinal pathology as a source of bacteremia involving this uncommon anaerobic organism.

Introduction

Robinsoniella peoriensis (R. peoriensis) is a rare anaerobic, Gram-positive, spore-forming bacillus and is exceedingly rare in human infections [,]. The organism can be difficult to identify using conventional microbiological methods, and advances in molecular diagnostics, including 16S rRNA gene sequencing and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry, have largely facilitated its recognition [,]. Consequently, the true incidence of R. peoriensis infections may be underestimated, and the clinical significance of this pathogen remains incompletely understood.

Human infections attributed to R. peoriensis have been reported only sporadically and encompass a broad clinical spectrum, including osteoarticular infections, peritonitis, infective endocarditis, surgical site infections, and bacteremia []. Bloodstream infections appear to occur predominantly in patients with significant underlying comorbidities, such as malignancy, recent surgical interventions, or immunocompromised states []. One of the earliest reported cases involved bacteremia in a patient with pancreatic cancer, highlighting the organism's potential to cause invasive disease in susceptible hosts []. More recently, cases of bacteremia occurring despite broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy and reports of severe sepsis with fatal outcomes have further emphasized the pathogenic potential of this uncommon organism [,]. Antimicrobial susceptibility in R. peoriensis has varied among the few reported cases, and no standard therapy is currently established for this rare organism. Published reports describe treatment failure with some penicillin-based regimens and favorable responses to carbapenems, specifically meropenem, ertapenem, or imipenem, although this remains under investigation [].

Given the limited number of published cases, significant gaps remain in the epidemiology, antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, optimal treatment strategies, and outcomes of R. peoriensis bacteremia. The following case illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with this organism and highlights the importance of considering Robinsoniella species as potential pathogens in susceptible patients. This case report describes a 49-year-old woman with Robinsoniella bacteremia, subsequently treated with ertapenem.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old woman with a complex medical history significant for end-stage renal disease (ESRD) secondary to IgA nephropathy on maintenance hemodialysis four times weekly after two failed renal transplants, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, coronary artery disease, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation currently on apixaban, chronic hypotension requiring midodrine, and previous colon and anal carcinoma treated with chemotherapy and pelvic radiation was admitted for five days of fever >101°F, dyspnea, non-localized abdominal pain, and acute on chronic bloody diarrhea. She was four years status post chemotherapy, pelvic radiation, and transverse colectomy for colon and anal carcinoma. Vital signs on presentation included a temperature of 101.1°F, heart rate of 94 beats/min, blood pressure of 90/48 mmHg, and respiratory rate of 21 breaths/min. Due to her unknown source of infection and possible pneumonia, she was initially treated empirically with intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam and vancomycin on day 1 of admission. MRSA nasal swabs were negative on day 2 of admission, and vancomycin was discontinued. Cefepime was initiated on day 2 for broader antimicrobial coverage after four blood cultures grew Gram-negative organisms. On day 4, blood cultures grew Robinsoniella and Serratia, and infectious disease recommended transitioning from cefepime to ertapenem. Her gastrointestinal symptoms began to improve after initiation of ertapenem, supporting Robinsoniella as a potential contributor to the bacteremia. Her respiratory symptoms continued to improve with supplemental oxygen and antimicrobial therapy.

By day 4 of her hospital course, the source of bacteremia was still unknown. The patient continued to have pitting edema and pain involving the left thigh and buttock. There were no open wounds or areas of fluctuance or purulent drainage. Repeat imaging obtained on hospital day 5 remained negative for infectious myositis or other focal pathology.

Polymerase chain reaction was performed on a stool sample. It was negative for Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter, Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli, Plesiomonas shigelloides, Vibrio, Yersinia, enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli, Giardia, Cryptosporidium, and Entamoeba. The comprehensive ova and parasite examination was negative. Fecal fat and celiac panels were unrevealing. Strongyloides IgG antibody testing was performed because helminth infections may be associated with recurrent enteric bacteremia; the result was negative. Colonoscopy was performed to evaluate chronic diarrhea along with acute rectal bleeding. Examination revealed radiation proctitis without evidence of recurrent malignancy, inflammatory bowel disease, or colonic masses. These findings suggested that radiation proctitis was the most likely source of her bacteremia. Canasa suppositories were recommended for ongoing management of radiation proctitis, and the patient's pain was managed conservatively with opioid analgesics. She was also advised to continue her five-day course of ertapenem for her bacteremia.

Regarding her ESRD, the patient continued regular hemodialysis throughout hospitalization with ultrafiltration for volume management. Imaging also demonstrated markedly atrophic native kidneys with extensive vascular calcifications consistent with chronic ESRD. The patient also presented with chronic pancytopenia, which was attributed to prior chemotherapy and radiation exposure, and an outpatient bone marrow biopsy was arranged for further evaluation. Over the course of treatment, the patient gradually improved clinically and was discharged with a peripherally inserted central catheter to complete intravenous ertapenem therapy, along with a seven-day course of oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and metronidazole for continued polymicrobial infection coverage. Given the suspected gastrointestinal source and polymicrobial bacteremia, coverage of both aerobic and anaerobic organisms was considered warranted in addition to ertapenem therapy.

Discussion

R. peoriensis remains an exceptionally rare cause of human infection, with only 20-25 documented cases worldwide, including only seven documented cases of bacteremia (Table ). Most reported cases involve patients with impaired immunity, malignancy, chronic wounds, prosthetic material, or gastrointestinal pathology []. Because the organism is an obligate anaerobe and difficult to identify using conventional laboratory methods, the true incidence is likely underestimated [,].

This patient's presentation supports an enteric source as the likely source of infection, although pneumonia represents a possible competing source. Multiple factors may have contributed to the disruption of mucosal barrier integrity, including chronic radiation proctitis, persistent diarrhea, previous colorectal malignancy, and immunosuppression associated with ESRD and prior renal transplantation. The simultaneous isolation of Serratia, Clostridium, and Robinsoniella further supports an enteric source rather than isolated contamination [].

Patients with ESRD have impaired innate and adaptive immune function, placing them at increased risk for bloodstream infections []. Previous chemotherapy and radiation likely compounded immune dysfunction, while chronic radiation injury may have provided a persistent portal of entry for bacteria [,]. Although extensive imaging excluded abscess, osteomyelitis, and soft tissue infection, polymicrobial bacteremia without another source strongly implicates gastrointestinal mucosal disruption [].

No standardized antimicrobial recommendations exist because of the limited number of reported cases. Published isolates generally demonstrate susceptibility to beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations, carbapenems, and metronidazole, although susceptibility testing remains important given the limited available data []. Our patient's clinical response following carbapenem therapy further supports the effectiveness of broad anaerobic coverage in severe infections involving Robinsoniella []. Recognition of Robinsoniella should prompt evaluation for underlying gastrointestinal pathology, especially in immunocompromised patients with polymicrobial bloodstream infections.

Conclusions

Robinsoniella species, although rarely seen in human infections, can cause severe bacteremia, particularly in immunocompromised patients. This case report highlights important considerations regarding the clinical presentation, diagnosis, antimicrobial susceptibility, and treatment of Robinsoniella infections. Although treatment failure with piperacillin-tazobactam has been reported, carbapenems have demonstrated favorable clinical activity in several reported cases. Antimicrobial selection should be individualized according to susceptibility results when available. Clinical outcomes and disease progression depend heavily on the patient's immunocompromised state and the type of infection. Given the rarity of reported infections, future investigations are essential to improve understanding of the clinical spectrum, epidemiology, and outcomes associated with Robinsoniella species, while also clarifying the molecular mechanisms and genetic factors that contribute to antimicrobial resistance.
