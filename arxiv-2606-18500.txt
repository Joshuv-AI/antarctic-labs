On the Diophantine Inequality | x 2 − 2 a ⋅ 3 b | < 3 ​ max ⁡ { a , b } \lvert x^{2}-2^{a}\cdot 3^{b}\rvert<3\max\{a,b\}
Banu İrez Aydın, Herbert Batte, İlker İnam, Florian Luca, Zeynep Demirkol Özkaya

Abstract
In this paper, we show that there are 57 57 nonnegative integer solutions ( a , b , x ) (a,b,x) to the inequality 1 ≤ | x 2 − 2 a ⋅ 3 b | < 3 ​ max ⁡ { a , b } 1\leq\lvert x^{2}-2^{a}\cdot 3^{b}\rvert<3\max\{a,b\} and we list them explicitly. The inequality is converted into a statement about how closely x / q x/q approximates irrational number d \sqrt{d} for d ∈ { 2 , 3 , 6 } d\in\{2,3,6\} , where q q is an integer which is 3 3 -smooth, after which Worley’s theorem on rational approximations via continued fractions is applied to parametrise the solutions and a p p -adic lower bound for a linear form in logarithms due to Bugeaud and Laurent is applied to find a rather large bound on max ⁡ { a , b } \max\{a,b\} . We finish with an application of the LLL algorithm to reduce this bound.

Introduction

1.1
Background

A positive integer is called
B
-smooth
if all of its prime factors are at most
B
. Thus, numbers whose only prime factors belong to the set
\{2,3\}
are called
3
-
smooth
numbers
1
1

1

see A003586 at oeis.org
. They form the sequence

\displaystyle 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 6,\ 8,\ 9,\ 12,\ 16,\ 18,\ 24,\ \ldots,

and can be written explicitly as
\{2^{a}3^{b}:a,b\geq 0\}
. These numbers arise naturally in many branches of mathematics, for example, in harmonic analysis (the Fourier structure of
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
is most transparent when
n
is 3-smooth), in algorithmic Number theory (FFT
algorithms run optimally on 3-smooth sizes), and in classical Diophantine Number theory, where the interaction between the additive structure of perfect powers and the multiplicative structure of smooth numbers generates a rich family of problems.

A central question in this latter direction is:
how closely can a perfect square approximate a smooth number?
The equation

\displaystyle x^{2}=2^{a}3^{b},

(1.1)

is elementary. It forces both
a
and
b
to be even, yielding the infinite family of solutions
x=2^{a/2}3^{b/2}
. The problem becomes significantly more delicate when one asks instead for
near-misses
,
that is, for integer triples
(x,a,b)
for which
x^{2}
and
2^{a}3^{b}
are close but not equal. This is the perspective of Diophantine inequalities.

A fundamental motivation for such problems comes from the theory of Pillai-type equations. Pillai
[
11
]
studied the equation
a^{x}-b^{y}=c
in integers, and the systematic study of how close two perfect powers can be to each other has since generated a large body of literature. We refer the reader to Shorey and Tijdeman in
[
13
]
for a comprehensive treatment.
Closely related to our problem is the family of equations of the form

\displaystyle x^{2}+2^{a}3^{b}=y^{n}\qquad{\text{\rm with}}\qquad\gcd(x,y)=1.

Luca
[
8
]
, determined all solutions when
n\geq 2
, while Luca and Togbé
[
9
]
treated the analogous problem with the prime
3
replaced by
5
. In a further generalization, the authors of
[
3
]
completely solved

\displaystyle x^{2}+2^{a}3^{b}11^{c}=y^{n}\qquad{\text{\rm with}}\qquad\gcd(x,y)=1,

showing that the presence of an additional prime factor significantly increases the technical complexity. Similar work in this line can also be found in
[
6
]
.

All of these works concern Diophantine equations. The present paper investigates instead a
Diophantine inequality
, asking how closely a perfect square can approximate a 3-smooth number when an error
of moderate size is permitted. Specifically, we investigate the Diophantine inequality

1\leq\lvert x^{2}-2^{a}\cdot 3^{b}\rvert<3\max\{a,b\},

(1.2)

in nonnegative integers
x
,
a
,
b

a
and
b
, while

grows exponentially. The problem therefore lies in a highly constrained regime and one expects only finitely many solutions.
Problems of this type contribute to the broader program of understanding Diophantine inequalities in which polynomial expressions are close to multiplicatively structured
sets, and they further demonstrate the effectiveness of transcendence methods in completely resolving such questions.

Proof strategy

The proof splits naturally into two parts according to the size of
A:=\max\{a,b\}
.

Small cases (
A\leq 2200
).
A computation in
SageMath
searches over all pairs
(a,b)

and locates all solutions to
the inequality, yielding exactly
57
triples
(a,b,x)

(Table
1.1
). If one wants solutions where all
x,a,b
are positive, then there are exactly
28
of these in the above table.

Large cases (
A>2200
).

The case where both

are even forces

|x^{2}-2^{a}3^{b}|=0<1
, so no solutions arise. For the remaining three parity classes we write

with

, and observe that any solution to the inequality gives a rational approximation

to

with small error. Worley’s theorem
[
15
]
characterizes all such approximations in terms of the convergents of

, yielding a unified equation

2\sqrt{d}\,y_{0}=C\gamma^{n}+D\delta^{n},

where
\gamma
is the fundamental unit of

and
(C,D)
are algebraic integers parametrized by five explicit families. Taking
p
-adic valuations
where
p\in\{2,3\}

[
2
]
on

A<3.57\times 10^{7}
. The LLL algorithm then
reduces this bound to

A special case arises, namely when the two algebraic numbers for which one intends to apply the result of Bugeaud–Laurent are multiplicatively dependent.
In this case, an elementary argument shows that in fact
A
is much smaller (
A<200
), so all solutions are again captured by the small-cases computation.

Preliminaries

2.1
Linear forms in logarithms and
p
-adic valuations

Before formulating bounds on linear forms in logarithms, we need the notion of height of an algebraic number and a few of its properties.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\eta
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers given by

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\eta^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive. Then, the logarithmic height of
\eta
is given by

h(\eta):=\dfrac{1}{d}\Big(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\max\{|\eta^{(i)}|,1\}\Big).

In particular, if
\eta
is a rational number represented as
\eta:=a/b
with coprime integers
a
and
b\geq 1
, then
h(\eta)=\log\max\{|a|,b\}
.
The following properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
will be used in the rest of the paper without further reference:

\displaystyle h(\eta_{1}\pm\eta_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\eta_{1})+h(\eta_{2})+\log 2;

\displaystyle h(\eta_{1}\eta_{2}^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\eta_{1})+h(\eta_{2});

\displaystyle h(\eta^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\eta)\quad{\text{\rm valid for}}\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}.

We now define
p
-adic valuations, where
p
is a prime number.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
p
be a prime number. The
p
-adic valuation of an integer
x
, denoted by
\nu_{p}(x)
, is defined by

\nu_{p}(x):=\begin{cases}\max\{k\in\mathbb{N}:p^{k}\mid x\},&\text{if }x\neq 0;\\
\infty,&\text{if }x=0.\end{cases}

Additionally, if
x=a/b
is a rational number and
a,~b
are integers, then we put

\nu_{p}(x):=\nu_{p}(a)-\nu_{p}(b).

The formula for
\nu_{p}(x)
when
x
is rational given by Definition
2.2
does not depend on the representation of
x
as a ratio of integers
a/b
. It follows easily from Definition
2.2
that if
x
is rational, then

\nu_{p}(x)=\text{ord}_{p}(x),

where
\text{ord}_{p}(x)
is the exponent of
p
in the factorization of
x
. For example,
\nu_{2}(9/8)=-3
. Now for the algebraic number
\eta
in Definition
2.1
, we define

\nu_{p}(\eta):=\frac{\nu_{p}(a_{d}/a_{0})}{d},

that is, it is the
p
–adic valuation of the rational number
a_{d}/a_{0}
divided by the degree
d
of
\eta
. For example, when
x=a/b
is a rational number with coprime integers
a
and
b\geq 1
, then
its minimal polynomial is
f(X)=bX-a
has degree
1
so

\nu_{p}(x)=\nu_{p}(a/b),

consistent with Definition
2.2
.

Next, let
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
be algebraic numbers over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
{\mathbb{K}}:={\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
and let
D_{\mathbb{K}}:=[{\mathbb{K}}:{\mathbb{Q}}]
be the degree
of
{\mathbb{K}}
. Consider the algebraic number
\eta_{1}^{b_{1}}\eta_{2}^{b_{2}}-1
and assume that it is not zero. Then one might want a good upper bound on

\nu_{p}(\eta_{1}^{b_{1}}\eta_{2}^{b_{2}}-1)
. Let

h^{\prime}(\eta_{i})\geq\max\left\{h(\eta_{i}),\dfrac{\log p}{D_{\mathbb{K}}}\right\},\qquad\text{for }~i=1,2.

The following result is Corollary 2 in
[

]
.

Theorem 2.3

(Bugeaud and Laurent,
[
2
]
)
.

Let
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
be positive integers and suppose that
\eta_{1}
and

\nu_{p}(\eta_{1})=\nu_{p}(\eta_{2})=0

Then

where

is the smallest integer such that

for both

.

When

becomes associated with

, where

,

generated by

. For a number field

of

of

2.2
Continued fractions

Recall that every irrational number
\tau
has a unique representation as an
infinite simple continued fraction

\tau=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
with partial quotients
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a_{i}\geq 1
for

i\geq 1
. The
convergents
are given by
p_{n}/q_{n}:=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}]
and are the best rational approximations to
\tau
in the sense that

\left|\tau-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right|<\frac{1}{q_{n}q_{n+1}}.

The denominators satisfy the three-term recurrence

q_{n+1}=a_{n+1}q_{n}+q_{n-1}
for
n\geq 1
with initial values
q_{0}=0
,
q_{1}=a_{1}
.

Worley’s theorem
[
15
]
is a converse to the classical theory of continued fractions. It characterizes all rationals
p/q
that approximate
\tau
to within
K/q^{2}
, not just the convergents.

Theorem 2.5

(Worley
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\tau
be a positive irrational number,
K>0
,
and let
p/q
be a rational approximation to
\tau
in reduced form
satisfying

q^{2}\left|\tau-\frac{p}{q}\right|<K.

Then either
p/q
is a convergent
p_{n}/q_{n}
to
\tau
, or
p/q
has one of the following forms for some index
n\geq 1
and integers
r\geq 0,s\geq 1
:

(i)

\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}=\frac{rp_{n}+sp_{n-1}}{rq_{n}+sq_{n-1}},

\displaystyle rs

\displaystyle<2K;

(ii)

\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}=\frac{rp_{n}-sp_{n-1}}{rq_{n}-sq_{n-1}},

\displaystyle rs

\displaystyle<2K;

(iii)

\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}=\frac{rp_{n+1}+sp_{n-1}}{rq_{n+1}+sq_{n-1}},

\displaystyle rs

\displaystyle<K,\quad a_{n+1}=1.

When
K<1/2
above, Worley’s theorem gives that
rs<1
, so only the case
s=1
is possible. This gives that
p/q=p_{n-1}/q_{n-1}
is a convergent of
\tau
, a result due to Legendre.
We also need the following result (see Theorem 8.2.4 in
[
10
]
).

Lemma 2.6

(Legendre)
.

Let
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash{\mathbb{Q}}
with continued fraction expansion

\xi=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
and let again

p_{i}/q_{i}=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}]
be its
i
-th convergent for
i\geq 0
. Let
M
be a positive integer and let
n
be a nonnegative integer such that
q_{n}>M
. Put

a(M):=\max\{a_{i}:i=0,1,2,\ldots,M\}.

Then the inequality

\left|\xi-\frac{f}{w}\right|>\frac{1}{(a(M)+2)\,w^{2}}

holds for all pairs
(f,w)
of positive integers with
0<w<M
.

We record Lemma
2.6
here for context, as it is the classical quantitative form of the statement that the best rational approximations to an irrational number arise from its continued fraction convergents. In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, the relevant consequence of this principle (namely, that the case
K<1/2
in Theorem
2.5
forces
rs<1
, so that
x_{0}/y_{0}
is itself a convergent of
\sqrt{d}
) is obtained directly as a
corollary of Worley’s theorem, and the resulting case is treated via the multiplicative-dependence argument in Section
3
.

2.3
The LLL algorithm

Typically, the estimates from Theorem
2.3
are excessively large to be practical in computations. To refine these estimates, we use a method based on the LLL-algorithm. While Lemma
2.6
is often sufficient for linear forms in two, the LLL-algorithm offers a more robust and generalized approach for three or more logarithms, as is the case in our study (see
[
14
,
5
]
). We next explain this method.

Let
k
be a positive integer. A subset
\mathcal{L}
of the
k
-dimensional real vector space
{\mathbb{R}^{k}}
is called a lattice if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}b_{i}\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

We say that
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
form a basis for
\mathcal{L}
, or that they span
\mathcal{L}
. We
call
k
the rank of
\mathcal{L}
. The determinant
\text{det}(\mathcal{L})
, of
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathcal{L})=|\det(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})|,

with the
b_{i}
’s being written as column vectors. This is a positive real number that does not depend on the choice of the basis (see
[
4
]
, Section 1.2).

Given linearly independent vectors
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we refer back to the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization technique. This method allows us to inductively define vectors
b^{*}_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
) and real coefficients
\mu_{i,j}
(for
1\leq j\leq i\leq k
). Specifically,

\displaystyle b^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}b^{*}_{j},~~~\mu_{i,j}=\dfrac{\langle b_{i},b^{*}_{j}\rangle}{\langle b^{*}_{j},b^{*}_{j}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Notice that
b^{*}_{i}
is the orthogonal projection of
b_{i}
on the orthogonal complement of the span of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}
, and that
\mathbb{R}b_{i}
is orthogonal to the span of
b^{*}_{1},\ldots,b^{*}_{i-1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that
b^{*}_{1},b^{*}_{2},\ldots,b^{*}_{k}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Definition 2.7
.

The basis
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
for the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called reduced if

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i,j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|b^{*}_{i}+\mu_{i,i-1}b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<i\leq n,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length. The constant
{3}/{4}
above is arbitrarily chosen, and may be replaced by any fixed real number
y
in the interval
{1}/{4}<y<1

(see
[
7
]
, Section 1)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a
k-
dimensional lattice with reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
and denote by
B
the matrix with columns
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
.
We define

l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\min_{x\in\mathcal{L}}||x-y||\quad;~~y\not\in\mathcal{L}\\
\min_{0\neq x\in\mathcal{L}}||x||\quad;~~y\in\mathcal{L}\end{array}\right.,

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. It is well known that, by applying the
LLL–algorithm, it is possible to give in polynomial time a lower bound for
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)
, namely a positive constant
\mathfrak{d}
such that
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq\mathfrak{d}
holds (see
[
14
]
, Section V.4).

Lemma 2.8

(
[
14
]
, Section V.4)
.

Let
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}
be an LLL-reduced basis for a lattice
\mathcal{L}
and
b_{1}^{*},\dots,b_{k}^{*}
be the corresponding Gram-Schmidt orthogonal basis. Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
z=B^{-1}y
.

(i)

If
y\not\in\mathcal{L}
, let
i_{0}
be the largest index such that
z_{i_{0}}\neq 0
and put
\lambda:=\{z_{i_{0}}\}
.

(ii)

If
y\in\mathcal{L}
, put
\lambda:=1
.

Now, define

c_{1}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\left\{\dfrac{\|b_{1}\|}{\|b_{j}^{*}\|}\right\}.

Then,
l(\mathcal{L},y)\geq\mathfrak{d}:=\lambda\|b_{1}\|c_{1}^{-1}
.

In our application, we are given real numbers
\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}
which are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
and two positive constants
c_{3}
and
c_{4}
such that

\displaystyle|\eta_{0}+x_{1}\eta_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}\eta_{k}|\leq c_{3}\exp(-c_{4}H),

(2.2)

where the integers
x_{i}
are bounded as
|x_{i}|\leq X_{i}
with
X_{i}

h^{\prime}(\eta_{1})=\log\gamma
.

On the other hand,
|D/C|
is a root of the polynomial

\displaystyle|CD|\left(X-|C/D|)(X-|D/C|\right)=|CD|X^{2}-(C^{2}+D^{2})X+|CD|\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]

of leading coefficient
|CD|
, one root
>1
in absolute value and one root
<1
in absolute value, so, assuming say
|C|\geq|D|
, we have

h(D/C)\leq\frac{1}{2}(\log|CD|+\log|C/D|)=\log|C|\leq\log(240A),

(assuming

|C|\leq|D|
gives an even better inequality, namely
h(D/C)\leq\log|D|\leq\log(43A)
). Thus,

\displaystyle h(\eta_{2})\leq\log(240A)<\log A+5.5.

We set
h^{\prime}(\eta_{2}):=\log A+5.5
.

The parameter
E^{\prime}
can be computed as

\displaystyle E^{\prime}=\frac{b_{1}}{h^{\prime}(\eta_{2})}+\frac{b_{2}}{h^{\prime}(\eta_{1})}=\frac{2n}{\log A+5.5}+\frac{1}{\log\gamma}.

Using
n<1.02A+6.09+1.14\log A
from Lemma
3.1
and absorbing the
1/\log\gamma
term into the numerator, we obtain

\displaystyle E^{\prime}<\frac{2.04A+12.18+2.28\log A}{\log A+5.5}+\frac{1}{\log\gamma}<\frac{2.14A+12.18+2.28\log A}{\log A+5.5}.

For
A>10^{6}
, the maximum in the definition of
E
from Theorem
2.3
is achieved by its logarithmic term

\displaystyle E=\log\!\left(\frac{1.02A+2.3\log A+7.3}{\log A+5.5}\right)+\log\log p+0.4.

Using the numbers
p
,
g
from (
3.21
) and
D_{\mathbb{K}}=2
, we obtain the constant

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{p}:=\frac{24\,p\,g\,D_{\mathbb{K}}^{4}}{(p-1)(\log p)^{4}}<\begin{cases}3328&p=2\text{ (all }d\text{)},\\
1582&p=3,\;d=2,\\
396&p=3,\;d\in\{3,6\}.\end{cases}

Thus, Theorem
2.3
gives, for
i=1,2
,

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\!\left(\gamma^{2n}+\varepsilon_{1}\frac{D}{C}\right)\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}\cdot\log\gamma\cdot(\log A+5.5)\cdot E^{2}.

(3.22)

Combining the lower bound in (
3.19
) with worse upper bound for
p=2
,
e=2
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}<3328
, we get

\displaystyle\alpha-\alpha_{0}<3328\log\gamma(\log A+5.5)E^{2}+2.9\log A+13.4.

(3.23)

On the other hand, combining (
3.20
) with (
3.22
) for
p=3
,
\mathcal{C}_{3}<1582
, gives

\displaystyle\beta-\beta_{0}\leq 1582\log\gamma(\log A+5.5)E^{2}+5.8\log A+26.7.

Since
3328>1582
, the bound from (
3.23
) dominates. Thus,

\displaystyle\max\{\alpha-\alpha_{0},\,\beta-\beta_{0}\}<3328\log\gamma(\log A+5.5)E^{2}+2.9\log A+13.4.

We use the chain

\displaystyle\frac{A-1}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\alpha,\beta\}\leq\max\{\alpha-\alpha_{0},\beta-\beta_{0}\}+\max\{\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\alpha-\alpha_{0},\beta-\beta_{0}\}+\frac{\log d_{0}}{\log 2},

(3.24)

and
\log d_{0}\leq\frac{1}{2}\log(2.14A)
from (

), giving

\frac{A-1}{2}<3328(\log\gamma)(\log A+5.5)E^{2}+2.9\log A+13.4+\frac{\log(2.14A)}{2\log 2}.

(3.25)

The right-hand side of (
3.25
) depends on
d
only through
\log\gamma
. Larger
\gamma
gives a weaker bound on
A
. Inserting
\log(1+\sqrt{2})
,
\log(2+\sqrt{3})
,

\log(5+2\sqrt{6})
and performing a direct numerical evaluation in
SageMath
shows that (
3.25
) fails for

exceeding the values below. (If

the bounds hold trivially.)

Lemma 3.2
.

We have

\displaystyle A:=\max\{a,b\}<\begin{cases}2.24\times 10^{7}&d=2,\\
3.62\times 10^{7}&d=3,\\
7.14\times 10^{7}&d=6.\end{cases}

In particular,
A<7.14\times 10^{7}
in all cases.

Proof.

If
A\leq 10^{6}
the stated bounds hold trivially. For

,
inserting

\log\gamma\in\{\log(1+\sqrt{2}),\log(2+\sqrt{3}),\log(5+2\sqrt{6})\}

into inequality (

yields the bounds above.

Combined with Lemma

, this gives

\displaystyle n<\begin{cases}2.29\times 10^{7}&d=2,\\
3.70\times 10^{7}&d=3,\\
7.29\times 10^{7}&d=6.\end{cases}

3.3
Reduction via the LLL algorithm

We now apply the LLL algorithm to reduce the bounds in
Lemma
3.2
to a range which can allow computational verification.

We return to equation (
3.7
), which we write as

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{d}\,y_{0}=|C|\gamma^{n}\pm|D||\delta|^{n}.

Rearranging and dividing both sides by
|C|\gamma^{n}
, we obtain

\left|\frac{2^{\alpha-\alpha_{0}}3^{\beta-\beta_{0}}}{|C|\gamma^{n}}-1\right|=\frac{|D/C|}{\gamma^{2n}}.

(3.26)

We bound the right-hand side of (
3.26
). From the
bounds (
3.9
)–(
3.10
), we have

|D/C|\leq|D|\cdot\frac{1}{|C|}\leq 43A\times 43A=1849A^{2},

and the lower bound for
\gamma^{n}
from Lemma
3.1
gives

\gamma^{n}>\frac{y_{0}}{86A}=\frac{2^{\alpha}3^{\beta}}{d_{0}(86A)}\geq\frac{2^{A/2}}{86A\sqrt{2.14A}}.

Substituting this into the right-hand side of (
3.26
) gives

\frac{|D/C|}{\gamma^{2n}}<\frac{1849A^{2}\cdot(86A)^{2}\cdot 2.14A}{2^{A}}<\frac{2.93\times 10^{7}\,A^{5}}{2^{A}}.

Since
2^{0.05A}>2.93\times 10^{7}A^{5}
for all
A>2200
, the right-hand side above is bounded by
2^{-0.95A}
. Therefore,

\left|\frac{2^{\alpha-\alpha_{0}}3^{\beta-\beta_{0}}}{|C|\gamma^{n}}-1\right|<\frac{1}{2^{0.95A}}.

(3.27)

Taking logarithms and using (
2.3
) (which holds by (
3.27
) for
A>2200
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|(\alpha-\alpha_{0})\log 2+(\beta-\beta_{0})\log 3-n\log\gamma-\log|C|\right|<\frac{2}{2^{0.95A}}.

(3.28)

For each fixed value of
|C|
(arising from a specific choice of
d
,
r
,
s
,
\varepsilon
from Table
3.1
), we use the LLL-algorithm to obtain a lower bound for the smallest nonzero value of the above linear form, constrained by integer coefficients with absolute values not exceeding
n<7.29\cdot 10^{7}
. In particular, we consider the lattice

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
\lfloor E_{\rm lat}\log 2\rfloor&\lfloor E_{\rm lat}\log 3\rfloor&\lfloor E_{\rm lat}\log\gamma\rfloor\end{pmatrix},

where
E_{\rm lat}:=1.2\times 10^{24}
is chosen so that the lattice entries are integers of size comparable to
3n^{3}
. We set
y:=(0,0,-\lfloor E_{\rm lat}\cdot\log|C|\rfloor)^{T}
. Applying Lemma
2.8
, we obtain using
SageMath

See
lll_reduction.ipynb
in the repository cited on page

.
that among all possible values of
|C|
arising from a specific choice of
(d,r,s,\varepsilon)
,

l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq\mathfrak{d}:=2.35\cdot 10^{8}.

This computation, which scans over all admissible coprime pairs
(r,s)
for each

subject to the conditions described below, took approximately 6 days of continuous running time on a standard desktop machine.

The value
|C|
used in the LLL-algorithm above depends on
d
,
r
,

,
\varepsilon
, and
n
via Table
3.1
. From (
3.9
) and
K\leq 1.07A
, we have
|C|<240A<10^{10}
. For each
d\in\{2,3,6\}
and each pair
(r,s)
with
r,s\geq 1
,
\gcd(r,s)=1
, and
rs<2K\leq 2.14A<2\times 10^{8}
, the number of coprime pairs is at most

A(\log A+1)/(\pi^{2}/6)<3\times 10^{9}

2,3,\gamma
are multiplicatively independent, but
2,3,\gamma,|C|
might be multiplicatively dependent. Note that if this is so, then necessarily
|N(C)|=2^{u}3^{v}
, where
N
is the norm in the corresponding quadratic field
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Q}}({\sqrt{d}})
. If this is so, then thanks to the fact that
p=2
and
p=3
are always squares in

except for the case
(p,d)=(3,2)
when

is inert in

C=\pm{\sqrt{2}}^{u}3^{v/2}\gamma^{t},\quad C=\pm(1+{\sqrt{3}})^{u}{\sqrt{3}}^{v}\gamma^{t},\quad C=\pm(2+{\sqrt{6}})^{u}(3+{\sqrt{6}})^{v}\gamma^{t},

according to whether

, respectively. However, in these cases we also have

C/D=\pm\gamma^{t},\quad C/D=\pm\gamma^{2t+u},\quad C/D=\pm\gamma^{2t+u+v},

3.4
Special cases

Along the way we made some assumptions. For example, assuming still that
rs\neq 0
, we assumed that
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
are multiplicatively independent in order to apply Theorem
2.3
. But if they are not independent, we get even better bounds on
\nu_{p}(\gamma^{n}+\varepsilon_{1}D/C)
for
p=2,3
. We handle these cases here.

Case 1:
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
multiplicatively dependent

Since
\gamma
is the fundamental unit of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
, the only units are
\pm\gamma^{t}
for
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
\eta_{1}=\gamma^{2}
and
\eta_{2}=\varepsilon_{1}D/C
are multiplicatively dependent, then

\eta_{2}=\pm\gamma^{t}
for some integer
t
, i.e.,

\displaystyle|t|\log\gamma=|\log|D/C||.

Using
|C|\leq 240A
and
|D|\leq 43A
, we have

\log(|D|/|C|)\leq\log|D|+\log(1/|C|)\leq\log(43A)+\log(43A)\leq 2\log(43A),

and

-\log|D|/|C|=\log|C|/|D|\leq\log|C|+\log(1/|D|)\leq\log(240A)+\log(240A)=2\log(240A).

So, in all cases

\displaystyle|t|\leq\frac{2\log(240A)}{\log\gamma}<2.3\log A+12.5.

In this case,

\gamma^{2n}+\varepsilon_{1}\frac{D}{C}=\gamma^{2n}\pm\gamma^{t}=\gamma^{t}\!\left(\gamma^{2n-t}\pm 1\right),

so
\nu_{\pi_{i}}(\gamma^{2n}+\varepsilon_{1}D/C)=\nu_{\pi_{i}}(\gamma^{2n-t}\pm 1)
, since
\nu_{\pi_{i}}(\gamma^{t})=0
for
i=1,2
. As for the other valuation, we have

\nu_{\pi_{i}}(\gamma^{2n-t}\pm 1)\leq\nu_{\pi_{i}}((\gamma^{12})^{2n-t}-1)\qquad{\text{\rm for}}\qquad i=1,2.

The exponent
12
is chosen as the smallest positive integer
k
such that
\gamma^{k}
satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma
2.4
simultaneously for all
d\in\{2,3,6\}
and both primes
p\in\{2,3\}
, as verified in (
3.29
). So, we apply Lemma
2.4
with
\zeta=\gamma^{12}
. One verifies the hypothesis
\nu_{\pi_{i}}(\zeta-1)>e_{i}/(p_{i}-1)
for each
d\in\{2,3,6\}
and
i=1,2
by direct calculation:

\begin{array}[]{lll}d=2:&\nu_{\pi_{1}}(\zeta-1)=7>2,&\nu_{\pi_{2}}(\zeta-1)=2>1/2;\\[4.0pt]
d=3:&\nu_{\pi_{1}}(\zeta-1)=8>2,&\nu_{\pi_{2}}(\zeta-1)=3>1;\\[4.0pt]
d=6:&\nu_{\pi_{1}}(\zeta-1)=9>2,&\nu_{\pi_{2}}(\zeta-1)=5>1.\end{array}

(3.29)

The hypothesis is satisfied in all cases as shown in (
3.29
), so Lemma
2.4
applies with
m=2n-t
. We get

\nu_{\pi_{i}}(\gamma^{2n-t}\pm 1)\leq\nu_{\pi_{i}}(\gamma-1)+\nu_{\pi_{i}}(2n-t)\leq 9+\frac{2\log(|2n-t|)}{\log p_{i}}\leq 9+\frac{2\log(2n+|t|)}{\log 2}

uniformly for all
i=1,2
and
d\in\{2,3,6\}

(using
\nu_{\pi_{i}}(\gamma-1)\leq 9
in all cases). From (
3.16
) and (
3.18
), we have

\max\{\alpha-\alpha_{0},\,\beta-\beta_{0}\}\leq\nu_{\pi_{i}}(C)+\nu_{\pi_{i}}(\gamma^{2n-t}\pm 1)\leq 5.8\log A+26.7+9+\frac{2\log(2n+|t|)}{\log 2},\quad i=1,2.

Using the chain (
3.2
), together with

n<1.02A+6.09+1.14\log A
and
|t|<2.3\log A+12.5
, we obtain

\frac{A-1}{2}\leq 5.8\log A+35.7+\frac{\log\sqrt{2.14A}}{\log 2}+\frac{2\log(2.04A+4.6\log A+25)}{\log 2}.

This gives
A<180
, contradicting
A>2200
.

Case 2:
rs=0

If
r=0
or
s=0
then
y_{0}=q_{n}
(or
q_{n-1}
) and equation (
3.7
) becomes

2\sqrt{d}\,y_{0}=C\!\left(\gamma^{2m}+\frac{D}{C}\right),\qquad m\in\{n,n-1\},

where

\frac{D}{C}\in\left\{1,\;\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{1-\sqrt{3}},\;\frac{2+\sqrt{6}}{2-\sqrt{6}}\right\}=\left\{1,\;-\gamma_{d=3},\;-\gamma_{d=6}\right\}.

In each case
D/C
is a power of the fundamental unit
\gamma
, so
\eta_{1}=\gamma^{2}
and
\eta_{2}=D/C
are multiplicatively dependent. This is exactly the situation treated in Case 1 above, which gives
A<180
, again contradicting
A>2200
.

Since both Case 1 and Case 2 lead to
A<180<2200
, all solutions in these cases are already accounted for by the small-cases computation of Table
1.1
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
. ∎
