Q-MMR : Off-Policy Evaluation via Recursive Reweighting and Moment Matching
Xiang Li, Nan Jiang

Abstract
We present a novel theoretical framework, Q-MMR , for off-policy evaluation in finite-horizon MDPs. Q-MMR learns a set of scalar weights, one for each data point, such that the reweighted rewards approximate the expected return under the target policy. The weights are learned inductively in a top-down manner via a moment matching objective against a value-function discriminator class. Notably, and perhaps surprisingly, a data-dependent finite-sample guarantee for general function approximation can be established under only the realizability of Q π Q^{\pi} , with a dimension-free bound—that is, the error does not depend on the statistical complexity of the function class. We also establish connections to several existing methods, such as importance sampling and linear FQE. Further theoretical analyses shed new light on the nature of coverage, a concept of fundamental importance to offline RL.

Introduction

Off-policy evaluation (OPE) using value-function approximation is a fundamental and core problem in the theory of reinforcement learning (RL). In this task, one estimates the expected return of a target policy
\pi
using data collected from some behavior policy, by leveraging a function class
\mathcal{F}
that captures the target policy’s Q-value function
Q^{\pi}
. For general function approximation, the canonical approach is Fitted-Q Evaluation (FQE), which solves a sequence of least-square regression problems
(
7
;
4
;
17
)
. FQE can also be viewed as the theoretical prototype of the TD algorithm, which is a foundational building block of RL.

While the theory of OPE with general value-function approximation is largely considered a mature area, where finite-sample guarantees for FQE and related algorithms have been established
(
14
)
, there are still notable gaps in our understanding:

•

Dimension dependence.
All existing analyses under general function approximation (GFA) need to translate the empirical loss to its population counterpart, during which one pays for the statistical dimension of the function class (e.g.,
\log|\mathcal{F}|
for finite
\mathcal{F}
or the log covering number for continuous classes). While this dependence is widely accepted, there are clearly situations where this is loose: for example, when the data is on-policy, Monte-Carlo (MC) estimate provides straightforward guarantees without any
\log|\mathcal{F}|
dependence, raising the question of whether the dimension dependence is real or an artifact of the algorithm design or its analysis.

•

Inconsistency between the general and the linear analyses.
Apart from the analyses under GFA, researchers have also obtained refined, sharp (i.e., often minimax optimal) guarantees in the linear and the tabular settings
(
6
;
31
)
. As it turns out, the main
1/\sqrt{n}
terms in these guarantees are indeed dimension-free. Moreover, the linear analyses hold under strictly weaker conditions than the Bellman-completeness assumption widely adopted in the GFA analyses. In fact, not only the linear analyses cannot be obtained by specializing the GFA analyses to the linear classes, they also give rise to novel concepts—such as seemingly unconventional notions of coverage (see Section
4
)—that are nowhere to be found in the GFA analyses.

In this paper, we address the above gaps with a novel algorithmic framework for OPE in finite-horizon MDPs. The
Q-MMR
algorithm learns a set of weights, one for each data point, inductively in a top-down manner via a moment-matching objective against a value-function discriminator class
\mathcal{F}
. Our main guarantee is a data-dependent finite-sample bound (Theorem
1
, Section
3
), which is
dimension-free
in that it has no explicit dependence on the complexity of
\mathcal{F}
. (A data-independent bound will be provided in Section
4
, where only the higher-order term is dimension dependent.) For linear
\mathcal{F}
,
Q-MMR
coincides with linear FQE; unlike the GFA analyses of FQE (which is dimension-dependent and requires Bellman completeness), our guarantees fully recover the key properties of the fine-grained linear and tabular analyses, under only the realizability of
Q^{\pi}
.

A key quantity in our bound is the second moment of the learned weights, which plays the role of an empirical coverage parameter
(
2
)
. In further analyses (Section
4
), we identify its population counterpart and examine its properties, which shed light on the nature of coverage in offline RL. The high-level take-away messages are consistent with the (somewhat counterintuitive) recent findings
(
22
;
2
)
(e.g., the coverage target is
not
the feature occupancy in the real MDP, but that from an approximate linear dynamical system; see Eq.(
6
)), but those findings were restricted to the linear setting and difficult to generalize. Our algorithms and analyses provide a much-needed generalization of such insights to GFA; see Table

for a summary.

Preliminaries

Finite-horizon MDPs

We consider an
H
-step finite-horizon MDP, where a random trajectory takes the form of

s_{0},a_{0},r_{0},s_{1},a_{1},r_{1},\ldots,s_{H},a_{H},r_{H}.

W.l.o.g.,
s_{0},a_{0}
is fixed and
r_{0}\equiv 0
, which will allow us to write our algorithm in a concise and unified manner.
We also assume that the state space is layered, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{h=0}^{H}\mathcal{S}_{h}
, where
\{\mathcal{S}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H}
are disjoint sets and it always holds that
s_{h}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
. The layered state space makes it convenient to use stationary notation for time-inhomogeneous objects. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the action space, and we consider finite and discrete
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
although their cardinalities can be arbitrarily large.
R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta([0,R_{\max}])
is the reward function, and
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the transition dynamics, where
P(\cdot|s,a)
for
s\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
is always supported on
\mathcal{S}_{h+1}
.

A policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
determines a distribution of trajectories starting from the fixed
s_{0},a_{0}
as
r_{h}\sim R(\cdot|s_{h},a_{h})
,
s_{h+1}\sim P(\cdot|s_{h},a_{h})
,
a_{h+1}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{h+1})
,
\forall h\geq 0
. We use
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\cdot]
to denote the expectation under such a distribution, and
J(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\sum_{h=1}^{H}r_{h}]
is the expected return. Note that the random return
\sum_{h=1}^{H}r_{h}\in[0,V_{\max}]
where
V_{\max}:=HR_{\max}
. The occupancy distribution
d_{h}^{\pi}
is the marginal distribution of
(s_{h},a_{h})
under
\pi
. The Q-function of
\pi
,
Q^{\pi}(s,a)
, is defined as:
\forall s\in\mathcal{S}_{h},a\in\mathcal{A}
,
Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\sum_{h^{\prime}=h}^{H}r_{h^{\prime}}|s_{h}=s,a_{h}=a]
. We use
Q_{h}^{\pi}
to denote
Q^{\pi}
restricted to
\mathcal{S}_{h}\times\mathcal{A}
. It satisfies the Bellman equation:

Q_{h-1}^{\pi}=\mathcal{T}^{\pi}Q_{h}^{\pi}
, where
\mathcal{T}^{\pi}
is the Bellman operator: given any
f:\mathcal{S}_{h}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}_{h-1},a\in\mathcal{A}
,
(\mathcal{T}^{\pi}f)(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{r\sim R(\cdot|s,a),s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[r+f(s^{\prime},\pi)]
. Here
f(s,\pi)
is a shorthand for
\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)}[f(s,a)]
and
f(s^{\prime},\pi)
for
s^{\prime}
at time
H+1
is always treated as
0
.
Since
s_{0},a_{0}
is fixed, we have
Q^{\pi}(s_{0},a_{0})=J(\pi)
.

Off-Policy Evaluation (OPE)

In OPE, we want to estimate
J(\pi)
for a given target policy
\pi
, using data trajectories collected with a behavior policy
\pi_{D}
. We assume the dataset consists of
n
i.i.d. trajectories,
\{(s_{0}^{(i)},a_{0}^{(i)},r_{0}^{(i)},\ldots,s_{H}^{(i)},a_{H}^{(i)},r_{H}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and
d_{h}^{D}
is the marginal distribution of
(s_{h},a_{h})
in the data.
\mathbb{E}_{D}[\cdot]
denotes expectation w.r.t. the data distribution.

To handle large state and action spaces, we consider the standard setting of function approximation, where a function class
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{h}\}_{h=1}^{H}
is used
to model
Q^{\pi}
; each
\mathcal{F}_{h}
consists of functions of the type
\mathcal{S}_{h}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
. Throughout the paper we assume the standard realizability assumption, that is:

Assumption 1

(Realizability)
.

Q^{\pi}\in\mathcal{F}
, which is shorthand for
Q_{h}^{\pi}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
,
\forall h\in[H]
.

Extension to handle misspecification is routine (see comment below Eq.(
5
)). Note that most standard analyses of OPE algorithms, especially those that use a general function class
\mathcal{F}
, typically require the stronger
Bellman-completeness
assumption
(
3
;
4
)
:

Assumption 2

(Bellman Completeness)
.

\mathcal{T}^{\pi}f\in\mathcal{F}_{h},\forall f\in\mathcal{F}_{h+1},h\in[H]
.

Our main guarantee (Theorem
1
), however,
does not rely on such a stronger assumption
, and we discuss the implications of Bellman completeness in our results in Section
4
.

While our main results are derived for GFA, to develop intuitions and make connections with the literature, it will be useful to consider the special case of linear and tabular function approximation:

Definition 1

(Linear function class)
.

\mathcal{F}
is linear in a given feature map
\phi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
, if
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\phi}=\{(s,a)\mapsto\phi(s,a)^{\top}\theta:\|\theta\|_{2}\leq\Theta\}
for some boundedness parameter
\Theta>0
.

Definition 2

(Tabular function class)
.

\mathcal{F}
is tabular if
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\textrm{tab}}=[0,V_{\max}]^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
.

Fixed-design analyses of linear regression

We briefly recall the
fixed-design
analysis of linear regression (LR), which will be extremely helpful for understanding our algorithm and analyses, since they correspond to the special case of OPE with
H=1
and linear
\mathcal{F}
. Consider LR over a dataset
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
, and assume
\widehat{\Sigma}:=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}
is invertible. LR gives
\widehat{\theta}=\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}x_{i}y_{i}
, and thus makes the prediction of
x_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}x_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}x_{i}y_{i}
on a new
x_{\textup{test}}
. Standard fixed-design analysis provides a
O(\|x_{\textup{test}}\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n})
high-probability error bound for this prediction, where
\|x_{\textup{test}}\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}}=\sqrt{x_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}x_{\textup{test}}}
is the Mahalanobis norm. This bound is “fixed-design” as the concentration argument is w.r.t. the randomness of
y_{i}|x_{i}
, where
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are treated as fixed instead of stochastic.
It can be proved via a moment-matching formulation of LR, which is a special case of our Algorithm
1
; see Appendix
A
for further detail.

Algorithm and Analyses

Our algorithm,
Q-MMR
, learns a scalar weight
\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}
for each state-action pair
(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})
in the data, and forms the estimate of
J(\pi)
using the reweighted reward:
\widehat{J}(\pi):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}r_{h}^{(i)}
. In fact, the estimate will be unbiased if
\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}
is set to the following expression, as well understood from the literature on marginalized importance sampling (MIS)
(
19
;
29
)
:

\displaystyle\textrm{(MIS Ratio)}\qquad d_{h}^{\pi}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})/d_{h}^{D}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)}).

(2)

However, learning the MIS ratio in Eq.(
2
) requires strong assumptions and is sufficient but not necessary for accurate OPE, and our algorithm enjoys guarantees when
\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}
does not learn this ratio but something more nuanced (see Section
4
). Nevertheless, to develop the first intuition, it is useful to temporarily
pretend
that our algorithm learns such weights inductively in a top-down manner.

Algorithm Intuition

We now provide a non-rigorous (but relatively easy to understand) explanation of our Algorithm
1
,
Q-MMR
, which computes
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}=\{\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
level-by-level from
h=0
to
H
. We do not represent such weights in a parameterized manner as standard MIS methods
(
19
;
27
)
, but rather learn a separate scalar weight for each of the
n
data points.
Since
(s_{0},a_{0})
is the fixed dummy initial state-action pair, there is no distribution shift at
h=0
and we set
w_{0}^{(i)}=\frac{d_{0}^{\pi}(s_{0},a_{0})}{d_{0}^{D}(s_{0},a_{0})}\equiv 1
.

Inductively, suppose we already have
\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{(i)}\approx\frac{d_{h-1}^{\pi}(s_{h-1}^{(i)},a_{h-1}^{(i)})}{d_{h-1}^{D}(s_{h-1}^{(i)},a_{h-1}^{(i)})}
, and we want to compute
\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}\approx\frac{d_{h}^{\pi}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})}{d_{h}^{D}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})}
. (Again, this is generally not the case in our analysis.) By the principle of importance sampling,

reweights the data distribution at level

This way, we successfully produce a weighted distribution that “looks like”

—by applying the weight
\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{(i)}
at level
h-1
to the state-action pair
(s_{h}^{(i)},\pi)
at level
h

\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}

such that
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}

is indistinguishable from
\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{[n]}
-reweighted

, up to the discrimination power of
\mathcal{F}_{h}
.

3.1
Finite-Sample Guarantee

We now present the main analyses for
Q-MMR
, which face two key challenges:

1.

While the earlier intuition appeals to learning the MIS ratio, this is generally not guaranteed when we only assume realizability (Assumption
1
). Under such a weak assumption, we need to bound the OPE error without showing that
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
tracks the MIS ratio.

2.

The number of free parameters (
\{\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\}_{h}
) scales linearly in
n
, the number of data points. Under the naïve parameter counting argument, this usually results in overfitting, i.e., an error bound that does not shrink as
n
grows. Such a practice of assigning a separate scalar to each data point is found in distributionally robust optimization
(
23
)
and balancing weights
(
16
)
, and its analyses often require sophisticated concentration machinery.

We address both challenges by a starkly simple and elementary analysis, whose core proof takes but a few lines. Perhaps surprisingly, our analysis does
not
perform any union bound over the function class
\mathcal{F}
at all, which avoids the statistical complexity of
\mathcal{F}
(e.g.,
\log|\mathcal{F}|
for finite
\mathcal{F}
).

The analysis is given as follows, where we start with the OPE error:

\displaystyle~\left|\textstyle\sum_{h=1}^{H}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}r_{h}^{(i)}-J(\pi)\right|=\left|\textstyle\sum_{h=0}^{H}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}r_{h}^{(i)}-J(\pi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\left|\textstyle\sum_{h=0}^{H}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bm{\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}}}\left(Q^{\pi}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bm{Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)}}\right)-J(\pi)\right|+\textstyle\sum_{h=0}^{H}\epsilon^{\textup{stat}}_{h}

(3)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\left|\textstyle\sum_{h=0}^{H}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})-{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bm{\widehat{w}_{h+1}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},a_{h+1}^{(i)})}}\right)-J(\pi)\right|

(4)

\displaystyle\quad+\textstyle\sum_{h=1}^{H}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]};\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{[n]})+\sum_{h=0}^{H}\epsilon^{\textup{stat}}_{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\textstyle\sum_{h=1}^{H}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]};\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{[n]})+\sum_{h=0}^{H}\epsilon^{\textup{stat}}_{h}.

We explain the derivation step by step:

1.

First, we replace
r_{h}^{(i)}
with
Q^{\pi}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})-Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)
by paying for an error term
\epsilon^{\textup{stat}}_{h}
, where

\left|\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}\left(Q^{\pi}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})-r_{h}^{(i)}-Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)\right)\right|\leq\epsilon^{\textup{stat}}_{h}\propto\|\textstyle\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\|_{[n]}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}(\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)})^{2}}

holds with high probability.
This follows from a standard concentration argument, which treats
\{(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}
as fixed and is w.r.t. the randomness of
r_{h}^{(i)}
and
s_{h+1}^{(i)}
conditioned on
(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})
. As long as
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
are chosen only based on the trajectory prefixes up to
\{(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and do not depend on the randomness after time
h
, the independence across the terms is intact, though the range of each term will be amplified by the size of
\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}
.

2.

From (
3
) to (
4
), we replace
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bm{\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)}}
with
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bm{\widehat{w}_{h+1}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},a_{h+1}^{(i)})}}
. Their difference is almost exactly the matching loss used in our algorithm, except that we do not know
Q_{h+1}^{\pi}
in the algorithm and instead relax it to
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{h+1}}
using realizability (Assumption
1
):

\displaystyle~\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bm{\widehat{w}_{h+1}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},a_{h+1}^{(i)})}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bm{\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)}}\right|

(5)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{h+1}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h+1}^{(i)}f(s_{h+1}^{(i)},a_{h+1}^{(i)})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}f(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)\right|=\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{h+1}(\widehat{w}_{h+1}^{[n]};\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}).

If realizability is not satisfied, an additional misspecification term will occur in the above inequality. In addition, the realizability of
\mathcal{F}_{h+1}

Dimension-freeness and “WYSIWYG”

Standard OPE analyses in offline RL under general function approximation need to translate the empirical loss to its population counterpart, during which one pays for the statistical dimension of the function class. In contrast, our analysis only performs concentration on
Q^{\pi}
(Eq.(

In addition, the empirical matching loss

Uncertainty quantification

In addition to the empirical matching loss, the other term in the bound,

applies more generally to GFA and improves over

is the natural generalization of the standard fixed-design analysis in linear regression.

Proposition 2
.

Consider

Non-uniqueness of
\widehat{w}
and the principle of least 2nd moment

So far, Theorem

holds for any choice of
\{\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\}_{h}
, including those that do not minimize Eq.(
1
). Moreover, even if we require
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
to exactly minimize
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\cdot;\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{[n]})
, the solution is also generally not unique. As it turns out, this flexibility is a blessing which allows us to unify and make connections to several existing methods. That said, it is still desirable that we fix a canonical choice of
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
to have a fully-specified algorithm. Among all

In practice, there can be situations where even the 2nd moment-minimizing

is too large, causing a poorly behaved

\widehat{\Sigma}
is near singular in the linear setting. In such cases, one needs to balance the bias-variance trade-off, by potentially giving up some matching loss in exchange for a smaller

(and thus
\epsilon^{\textup{stat}}_{h}

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
up to some budget for
\|\textstyle\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\|_{[n]}
), or via a Lagrangian form that corresponds to ridge regression in the linear setting (see Appendix
B.5
). In the rest of the paper, however, we will not consider constraint or regularization on
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
to keep the analyses clean.

4
Understanding the Learned Weights

The results thus far leave a few important and interrelated questions open:

Boundedness of weights.
Theorem
1
only assumes realizability and not Bellman completeness. In this regime, classic counterexamples against TD imply that linear FQE may diverge in the worst case, even if data covers all feature directions
(
26
)
. Since our
Q-MMR
coincides with FQE in the linear setting, this means that the size of
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
may blow-up exponentially in the horizon. Thus, naturally we want to understand when the size of
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
stays well bounded, and how stronger conditions such as Bellman completeness help guarantee that.

A priori bound.
Theorem
1
is data dependent. Can we turn it into an a priori, data-independent bound? Recall that the bound depends on the size of
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
,
\|\textstyle\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\|_{[n]}
. To remove the data dependence, we can define the population counterpart of
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
and use its size to replace
\|\textstyle\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\|_{[n]}
. Note that such bounds in general will incur dimension dependence, albeit in higher-order terms
(
11
)
.

The nature of coverage.

\|\textstyle\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\|_{[n]}
plays the role of
coverage
in Theorem
1
. Conventional wisdom tells us that coverage is about how the distribution
d_{h}^{D}
covers
d_{h}^{\pi}
, and the density coverage can sometimes be refined based on structure of
\mathcal{F}
; for example, in linear case, data only needs to cover the mean feature direction
\mathbb{E}_{(s_{h},a_{h})\sim d_{h}^{\pi}}[\phi(s_{h},a_{h})]
.
However, recent fine-grained linear analyses tell a very different story:
2
(see also
6
;
22
) show that the target of coverage is generally
not

d_{h}^{\pi}
or its feature expectation, but the vector defined by the following linear dynamical system: starting with
\psi_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{D}[\phi(s_{1},\pi)]
, and define,
\forall h\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\psi_{h+1}=B^{\pi}_{h}\psi_{h},~~\textup{where}~~B^{\pi}_{h}=(\Sigma^{\textup{cr}}_{h})^{\top}\Sigma_{h}^{-1},~\Sigma_{h}=\mathbb{E}_{D}[\phi_{h}\phi_{h}^{\top}],~\Sigma^{\textup{cr}}_{h}=\mathbb{E}_{D}[\phi_{h}(\phi_{h+1}^{\pi})^{\top}].

(6)

Here
\phi_{h}
is the shorthand of
\phi(s_{h},a_{h})
and
\phi_{h+1}^{\pi}=\phi(s_{h+1},\pi)
. In fact, linear FQE can be thought of as fitting such a system and using it to predict

(
6

\psi_{h}\neq\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[\phi]

\psi_{h}
from such a “wrong” system

the feature expectation
\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[\phi]
from the real MDP,

4

4

There is information-theoretic lower bound against this possibility

8
)
.
and they only coincide under Bellman completeness. However, the analyses of FQE under general function approximation rely on completeness, and this subtle distinction between
\psi_{h}
and
\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[\phi]
is therefore not observed. Moreover, the key concepts such as

are defined linear-algebraically, and it is unclear how their GFA counterpart should be defined. Does
\|\textstyle\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\|_{[n]}
in our analyses (or its population counterpart) provide such a generalization, and how is it related to the concepts identified in the linear analyses?

5
Other Related Works

Throughout the paper we have discussed the connection and comparison to Monte-Carlo estimation and FQE. Here we discuss several other related methods.

Marginalized Importance Sampling (MIS)

Our method is closely related to MIS methods
(
19
;
21
)
; for example, MWL
(
27
)
also solves a similar minimax problem (min over weights and max over value functions), whose loss is similar to our Eq. (
1
). Both losses are related to the Bellman flow equation, and roughly speaking, MWL can be viewed as using
\sum_{h}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}
as a loss to optimize the weights at all levels simultaneously. As a consequence, however, this introduces dependencies of
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
on
\{s_{h+1}^{(i)}\}_{i}
and violates the condition in our Theorem
1
, so MWL cannot learn a weight per data point but must introduce a weight class
\mathcal{W}\subset(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R})
and learn a parametric form of weight
\widehat{w}_{h}\in\mathcal{W}
instead. This means an extra realizability condition (of
\mathcal{W}
) and a complexity term (e.g.,
\log|\mathcal{W}|
) compared to our analyses.

Besides the difference in loss and parameterization, there is also a significant difference between the existing analyses of MIS methods and ours, and some insights from our analyses may be transferred to MIS. For example, we may use our proof techniques to provide data-dependent bounds for MWL that avoid the
\log|\mathcal{F}|
term (see Appendix
D
). In addition, while most MIS methods assume
\mathcal{W}
models the MIS ratio (Eq.(
2
)),
27
point out that the method may learn degenerate weights when
\mathcal{F}
is structured, but a systematic understanding of what the learned weights really represent is still lacking, which our analyses shed light on given the close connection between the methods.

Methods that only rely on realizability

The main guarantee of our method (Theorem
1
) only relies on realizability
Q^{\pi}\in\mathcal{F}
and not the stronger and more widely used Bellman-completeness assumption. Getting such results is also the central focus of the model selection problem in offline RL, where we want to select
Q^{\pi}
(or sometimes
Q^{\star}
) from a set of candidates on holdout data. Given challenges such as the double-sampling problem, these model-selection algorithms often require a tournament procedure and is only computationally efficient for a small number of candidates
(
28
;
18
)
. In comparison, our
Q-MMR
is computationally efficient for rich function classes under standard optimization oracles.

Top-down algorithms

Our algorithm design bears structural similarities to some of the existing methods. For example,
24
designs an online imitation-learning algorithm with a dataset that does not contain actions, which performs top-down, level-by-level moment matching against a value discriminator class similar to
Q-MMR
. However, they have online access to the MDP and the task is to imitate the expert policy, and their analysis requires Bellman completeness for the discriminator class.
12
learns the parametric form of the MIS ratio (Eq.(
2
)) in finite-horizon MDPs in a top-down manner, but they do not use a value-function discriminator but relies on a squared-loss objective, and their analyses also require a version of completeness for the MIS ratio.

6
Limitations and Future Work

We conclude the paper by discussing limitations and future work. One major limitation is that we need i.i.d. trajectory data; an adaptively collected dataset will break the conditional independence between
\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}
and
s_{h+1}^{(i)}|s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)}
. A straightforward extension of our results to the infinite-horizon discounted case will result in similar difficulties. One potential solution is to leverage the fact that
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
always lies in
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
(Proposition
4
), so we can union bound over
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
. However, doing so will incur the dimension dependence in the leading term, and we hope to resolve this issue in the future with more advanced concentration tools.

As another limitation, our weights
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
are computed to minimize matching loss and variance at level
h
in a rather greedy and local manner, as the effects of
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
on downstream levels
h^{\prime}>h
are not considered in optimization. One might attempt to address this issue by optimizing the sum of losses across all levels simultaneously, which essentially recovers MIS methods and runs into a double-sampling issue with non-parametric weights (Appendix
D.1
). It remains an open question whether we can optimize the weights
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
across levels while circumventing the issue.

Appendix A
Linear Regression under Covariate Shift

The form of our main theoretical guarantee in Section
3
can feel somewhat unconventional in the OPE literature due to its data dependence. As we will see, however, in the special case of
H=1
with a linear function class, our algorithm and analyses recover those of
linear regression under covariate shift

[
32
]
, where the data-dependent bound corresponds to a standard fixed-design analysis of linear regression
[
11
]
. Therefore, it is instructive to quickly review such an analysis, which will help clarify the nature of our results.

The covariate shift problem

We consider
(x,y)
pairs that satisfy
y=x^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\epsilon
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}
,
\theta^{\star}
is the unknown linear coefficients, and
\epsilon
is some
0
-mean i.i.d. noise. We are given training data points
\{(x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)},y_{\textup{train}}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}
sampled i.i.d. from distribution
P_{\textup{train}}
, and some test data points without
y
labels
\{x_{\textup{test}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{m}
sampled i.i.d. from distribution
P_{\textup{test}}
, which is potentially different from
P_{\textup{train}}
. Now the goal is to estimate

\mathbb{E}_{P_{\textup{test}}}[y_{\textup{test}}].

Note that this problem naturally arises in our OPE setup when
H=1
and
\mathcal{F}
is linear in
\phi
(Definition
1
): our
\phi(s_{1}^{(i)},a_{1}^{(i)})
is
x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}
,
r_{1}^{(i)}
is
y_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}
,
\phi(s_{1}^{(i)},\pi)
is
x_{\textup{test}}^{(i)}
(so that
m=n
), and the estimand is exactly
J(\pi)
; see also
2
for the connection between these two problems.

Fixed-design analysis of linear regression

Consider a natural algorithm that (1) performs linear regression from the training data to estimate
\widehat{\theta}
, and (2) outputs the estimate as
\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(x_{\textup{test}}^{(i)})^{\top}\widehat{\theta}=\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}
, where
\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}:=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{\textup{test}}^{(i)}
. A standard analysis shows that the error can be bounded as:

Proposition 13

(Informal)
.

Under appropriate boundedness assumptions, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
|\mathbb{E}_{P_{\textup{test}}}[y_{\textup{test}}]-\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}|\leq O(\|\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}}\sqrt{\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}/n})
, where
\widehat{\Sigma}:=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}(x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)})^{\top}
is the sample covariance matrix from the training data.

To understand the guarantee, the first observation is that we only care about how accurate
\widehat{\theta}
is when probed in the mean direction of
x_{\textup{test}}
,
\mathbb{E}_{P_{\textup{test}}}[x_{\textup{test}}]\approx\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}
, instead of individual
x_{\textup{test}}\sim P_{\textup{test}}
. The accuracy of the estimation then depends on how well this direction is
covered
in the training distribution, measured by
\|\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}}
.
For readers familiar with the online RL literature, one may directly recall a bound of
O(\|\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}}\sqrt{({\mathsf{d}}+\log\tfrac{1}{\delta})/n})
that is widely used in the linear bandit/MDP analyses
[
1
,
15
]
. However, we can avoid paying the
\sqrt{{\mathsf{d}}}
factor here because the online RL bound needs to hold for all probing directions simultaneously, whereas in the covariate shift (OPE) problem we only care about a single probing direction
\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}
.

Concretely, Proposition
13
can be proved by rewriting the estimate as

\displaystyle\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}=\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}y_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}\right)=:\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}^{(i)}y_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}.

That is, we are effectively computing weights
\widehat{w}^{(i)}=\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}
and using them to compute the weighted average of training labels. These weights satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}^{(i)}x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}=\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}.

(8)

Intuitively, if
\{\widehat{w}^{(i)}\}
reweight
\{x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}\}
to match
x_{\textup{test}}
in mean, then
\{\widehat{w}^{(i)}\}
-weighted
y_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}
will also match the mean of
y_{\textup{test}}
.
Based on this intuition, we can rewrite the error as
5
5

5

A component of the final error (
\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\textup{test}}}[y_{\textup{test}}]
) is omitted here, namely,
(\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\textup{test}}}[x_{\textup{test}}])^{\top}\theta^{\star}
. While this term reflects the finite sample error of using
\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}
to approximate its expectation, we only need to perform concentration along the direction of
\theta^{\star}
, so the error term is dimension-free and incurs a negligible overhead. In our analyses (e.g., Proposition
2
), this corresponds to the
\epsilon^{\textup{stat}}_{0}
term.

\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}-\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\theta^{\star}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}^{(i)}(y_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}-(x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)})^{\top}\theta^{\star}).

Note that
y_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}-(x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)})^{\top}\theta^{\star}
are
0
-mean i.i.d. random variables, and
\{\widehat{w}^{(i)}\}
are chosen with no dependence on the randomness of
\{y_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}\}
, so by a standard concentration argument we can bound the error using the 2nd moment of
\widehat{w}^{(i)}
(see the proof of Theorem
1
in Appendix
B.1
), which is exactly
\|\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widehat{w}^{(i)})^{2}}
.

A few remarks are in order:

•

Proposition
13
is a
fixed-design
result, as the bound depends on the matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}
which is data dependent, and the analysis applies to settings where
\{x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)}\}
is arbitrarily chosen (as in e.g., online learning) and not sampled from some distribution. This contrasts with the
random-design
analysis that has the population
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\textup{train}}}[x_{\textup{train}}x_{\textup{train}}^{\top}]
in the bound instead of
\widehat{\Sigma}
. Random-design bounds typically require additional assumptions and/or have extra (dimension-dependent) higher-order terms
[
11
]
, as is the case for our analyses in Section
4
.

•

The above analysis assumes
\widehat{\Sigma}
is invertible. This is implicitly an assumption on our matching loss
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
: invertible
\widehat{\Sigma}
implies that Eq.(
8
) has solution(s), and Eq.(
8
) can be written as:
\forall\theta
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}^{(i)}(x_{\textup{train}}^{(i)})^{\top}\theta-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(x_{\textup{test}}^{(i)})^{\top}\theta\right|=0.

Translating this back to the OPE notation system, we have

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~\sup_{\theta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{1}^{(i)}\phi(s_{1}^{(i)},a_{1}^{(i)})^{\top}\theta-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(s_{1}^{(i)},\pi)^{\top}\theta\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\phi}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{1}^{(i)}f(s_{1}^{(i)},a_{1}^{(i)})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(s_{1}^{(i)},\pi)\right|=\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}(\widehat{w}_{1}^{[n]};\widehat{w}_{0}^{[n]}).

(9)

If
\widehat{\Sigma}
is not invertible, we can still use pseudo-inverse to compute
\widehat{\theta}
, and the violation of Eq.(
8
) will appear in the error bound as a bias term. Such a term exactly corresponds to the matching error term in Theorem
1
.

For each

For each
h\in\{0,\ldots,H\}
and sample

as the Bellman error. By the Bellman equation, we know that
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{h}^{(i)}\mid\mathcal{G}_{h}]=0

[0,R_{\max}]

Now, condition on

is

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}\left(Q_{h}^{\pi}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})-r_{h}^{(i)}-Q_{h+1}^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)\right)\right|\leq\epsilon^{\textup{stat}}_{h}.

(10)

to finish the proof, which reduces the OPE error to matching losses plus the statistical errors. Fix the event

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{1}^{(i)}f(s_{1}^{(i)},a_{1}^{(i)})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{0}^{(i)}f(s_{1}^{(i)},\pi),

which indicates that
0
empirical matching loss can be achieved, i.e.,
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}(\widehat{w}_{1}^{[n]};\widehat{w}_{0}^{[n]})=0
. This proves the first claim. For the empirical 2nd-moment of the weight, we have

\bigl\|\widehat{w}_{1}^{[n]}\bigr\|_{[n]}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}x_{i}\bigr)^{2}=\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\right)\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}=\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\overline{x}_{\textup{test}},

where we again use
\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}
. Hence,
\|\widehat{w}_{1}^{[n]}\|_{[n]}=\|\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}}
, which proves the second claim. And the OPE error bound can be obtained immediately by Theorem
1
:

\bigl|\widehat{J}(\pi)-J(\pi)\bigr|\leq\epsilon^{\textup{stat}}_{0}+\epsilon^{\textup{stat}}_{1}=V_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4/\delta)}{n}}\left(1+\|\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}}\right).

For non-invertible
\widehat{\Sigma}
, define
\widehat{w}_{1}^{(i)}:=\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\dagger}x_{i}
for
i\in[n]
, where
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\dagger}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of
\widehat{\Sigma}
. We show that this weight choice minimizes the matching loss.

For any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define its induced empirical feature moment
\mu(w):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{(i)}x_{i}
. Since
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is linear and
\|\theta\|_{2}\leq\Theta
, we have

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}(w;\widehat{w}_{0}^{[n]})=\sup_{\theta:\|\theta\|_{2}\leq\Theta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{(i)}x_{i}^{\top}\theta-\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\theta\right|=\sup_{\theta:\|\theta\|_{2}\leq\Theta}|\langle\mu(w)-\overline{x}_{\textup{test}},\theta\rangle|=\Theta\|\mu(w)-\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}\|_{2},

where the last step is due to Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Thus minimizing the empirical matching loss is equivalent to choosing
\mu(w)
as close as possible to
\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}
in Euclidean norm.

Let
\mathcal{X}:=\mathrm{span}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. For every
w
, clearly
\mu(w)\in\mathcal{X}
by its definition. Conversely, for any
v\in\mathcal{X}
, there exist coefficients
\alpha_{i}
such that
v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i}
, and choosing
w^{(i)}=n\alpha_{i}
gives
\mu(w)=v
. Hence the set of all achievable empirical moments is exactly
\mathcal{X}
.

Moreover, the range space of
\widehat{\Sigma}
is
\operatorname{Range}(\widehat{\Sigma})=\mathcal{X}
. This is because
\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}
, so that
\operatorname{Range}(\widehat{\Sigma})\subseteq\mathcal{X}
. On the other hand, since
\widehat{\Sigma}
is symmetric,
\operatorname{Range}(\widehat{\Sigma})=\operatorname{Null}(\widehat{\Sigma})^{\bot}
, where
\operatorname{Null}(\widehat{\Sigma})
is the nullspace of
\widehat{\Sigma}
. But
v^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}v=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(v^{\top}x_{i})^{2}
, so
v\in\operatorname{Null}(\widehat{\Sigma})
is equivalent to
v\bot x_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
, thus
v\in\mathcal{X}^{\bot}
. Therefore,
\operatorname{Null}(\widehat{\Sigma})=\mathcal{X}^{\bot}
, and hence
\operatorname{Range}(\widehat{\Sigma})=\mathcal{X}
.

It follows that the minimum empirical matching loss is obtained by projecting
\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}
onto the range space of
\widehat{\Sigma}
, whose projection operator is indeed
\widehat{\Sigma}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\dagger}
:

\mu(w)=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}=\widehat{\Sigma}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}.

The proposed weights indeed achieve this moment, since

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{1}^{(i)}x_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\dagger}x_{i}\bigr)x_{i}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\right)\widehat{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}=\widehat{\Sigma}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}.

Therefore
\widehat{w}_{1}^{[n]}
minimizes the empirical matching loss. The minimum loss is

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}(\widehat{w}_{1}^{[n]};\widehat{w}_{0}^{[n]})=\Theta\bigl\|(I-\widehat{\Sigma}\widehat{\Sigma}^{\dagger})\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}\bigr\|_{2}.

In particular, empirical 0 matching loss is achievable if and only if
\overline{x}_{\textup{test}}\in\operatorname{Range}(\widehat{\Sigma})
.
∎

Proof of Proposition

Now we consider Proposition

Linear setting

In the linear case, this admits a simple matrix-vector implementation.
In particular, linear FQE sets
\widehat{Q}_{H+1}\equiv 0
, or equivalently
\widehat{\theta}_{H+1}=0
, and for

\widehat{Q}_{h}^{\pi}(s,a)=\phi(s,a)^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{h}

\widehat{\theta}_{h}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{d}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\phi(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})^{\top}\theta-r_{h}^{(i)}-\phi(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{h+1}\right)^{2}.

Equivalently, by the normal equation,

\widehat{\Sigma}_{h}\widehat{\theta}_{h}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})\left(r_{h}^{(i)}+\phi(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{h+1}\right).

Since
s_{0},a_{0}
is fixed and
r_{0}\equiv 0
, the FQE estimate of
J(\pi)

\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{FQE}}(\pi):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{Q}_{1}^{\pi}(s_{1}^{(i)},\pi)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{1}(s_{1}^{(i)},\pi)^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{1}.

(11)

Now we consider the tabular function class

For tabular FQE. Define the empirical reward and empirical transition respectively by

\widehat{r}_{h}(s,a):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{s_{h}^{(i)}=s,a_{h}^{(i)}=a\}r_{h}^{(i)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{s_{h}^{(i)}=s,a_{h}^{(i)}=a\}},\quad\widehat{P}_{h}(s^{\prime}|s,a):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{s_{h}^{(i)}=s,a_{h}^{(i)}=a,s_{h+1}^{(i)}=s^{\prime}\}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{s_{h}^{(i)}=s,a_{h}^{(i)}=a\}}.

Since the function class is , the regression separates over each state-action pair
(s,a)

(s,a)

Thus, tabular FQE is exactly Bellman evaluation of
\pi
in the empirical MDP. Hence its prediction is the model-based certainty-equivalence value

(2).

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}\mathbf{1}\{s_{h}^{(i)}=s,a_{h}^{(i)}=a\}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{(i)}\mathbf{1}\{s_{h}^{(i)}=s\}\pi(a\mid s),

(Optimization error from no-regret on

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]};\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{[n]})\leq\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{C}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(w;\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{[n]})+O\left(\frac{CG}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

In particular, if

Proof sketch.

For the OGD losses
\ell_{t}^{w}(w):=\ell(w,f_{t})

\mathcal{W}_{C}=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|w\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{n}\}

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(w_{t};\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{[n]})\leq\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{C}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(w;\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{[n]})+O\left(\frac{CG}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

The claim follows by Jensen’s inequality, since

and best response on

For instance, in the linear case

C.1
Auxiliary functionals and representers

In this section, we introduce several auxiliary concepts that will be used in the subsequent proofs and in our understanding of the learned weights. The main idea is to view the population and empirical moment-matching problems through the lens of Hilbert-space linear functionals. This viewpoint will be useful for two reasons. First, it explains why the population exact-matching problem is feasible under the bounded generalized leverage assumption (Assumption
3
). Second, it allows us to compare the empirical weights
\{\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\}_{h}
with their population counterparts
\{w^{\star}_{h}\}_{h}
by representing both of them inside the same Hilbert space.

Recall from Definition
4
that the Hilbert space
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
is the closure of
\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{F}_{h})
under the
d_{h}^{D}
-weighted
L_{2}
norm, equipped with the inner product
\langle f,g\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{D}}[fg]
. For any
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, let
P^{\pi}f
denote the one-step transition of
f
under the target policy, i.e.,
(P^{\pi}f)(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[f(s^{\prime},\pi)]
. Given the population weight, define the propagated signed measure

\nu_{h}^{\star}:=P^{\pi}(w^{\star}_{h-1}\cdot d_{h-1}^{D}).

Equivalently,
\nu_{h}^{\star}
is the signed measure satisfying, for every
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
,

\mathbb{E}_{(s_{h},a_{h})\sim\nu_{h}^{\star}}[f(s_{h},a_{h})]=\mathbb{E}_{(s_{h-1},a_{h-1})\sim d_{h-1}^{D}}[w^{\star}_{h-1}(s_{h-1},a_{h-1})\cdot(P^{\pi}f)(s_{h-1},a_{h-1})].

We call
\nu_{h}^{\star}
a signed measure because
w^{\star}_{h-1}
is not required to be nonnegative. Therefore
w^{\star}_{h-1}d_{h-1}^{D}
, and hence its pushforward under
P^{\pi}
, need not be a probability distribution.

Definition 6

(Population and empirical target functionals)
.

For each
h\in[H]
, define the population target functional
\Lambda_{h}^{\star}:\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{h}^{\star}}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h-1}^{D}}[w_{h-1}^{\star}\cdot(P^{\pi}f)].

Given the empirical weights
\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{[n]}
, define the empirical target functional
\widehat{\Lambda}:\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{(i)}f(s_{h}^{(i)},\pi).

Thus,
\Lambda_{h}^{\star}
is the population moment that the level-
h
weight should match, while
\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}
is its empirical analogue induced by the already-learned weights at level
h-1
. The next proposition shows that these linear functionals admit Riesz representers in
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. It also shows that, whenever empirical 0 matching loss is feasible (which is shown in Proposition
9
as a high-probability corollary under population 0 matching loss), the least-2nd-moment empirical weight vector can be viewed as the sample evaluation of some function in
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
.

Proposition 15

(Existence of representers)
.

Under Assumption
3
, for each
h\in[H]
, we have:

(i)

The population target functional
\Lambda_{h}^{\star}
is a continuous linear functional on
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Hence, the
w^{\star}_{h}
defined in Definition
3
satisfies
w^{\star}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, and
\langle w^{\star}_{h},f\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}=\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)
for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Moreover,
w^{\star}_{h}
is the unique least-2nd-moment solution among all
L_{2}(d_{h}^{D})
functions
w
satisfying
\langle w,f\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}=\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)
for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
.

(ii)

The empirical functional
\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}
is also a continuous linear functional on
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Hence there exists a unique auxiliary representer
\widetilde{w}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
such that
\langle\widetilde{w}_{h},f\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}=\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)
for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
.

(iii)

Suppose empirical 0 matching loss is feasible at level
h
, and let
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
be the least-2nd-moment weight vector. Then, there exists a lift representer
\bar{w}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
such that
\bar{w}_{h}|_{n}=\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
. Consequently,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{w}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})f(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})=\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)
for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
.

Proof.

For (i), linearity of
\Lambda_{h}^{\star}
follows directly from its definition (Definition
6
), so we need to show its continuity. By Assumption
3
, every
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
satisfies
|f(s,a)|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
. Therefore,
|f(s,\pi)|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
as well. It follows that

|\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)|\leq\mathbb{E}_{d_{h-1}^{D}}[|w^{\star}_{h-1}|\cdot|P^{\pi}f|]\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{d_{h-1}^{D}}[|w^{\star}_{h-1}|]\|f\|_{2,d_{h}}^{D}.

Using Cauchy-Schwarz,
\mathbb{E}_{d_{h-1}^{D}}[|w^{\star}_{h-1}|]\leq\|w^{\star}_{h-1}\|_{2,d_{h-1}^{D}}
. Hence
\Lambda_{h}^{\star}
is bounded, and therefore continuous, on
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Therefore, by the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a unique
w^{\star}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
such that
\langle w^{\star},f\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}=\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)
for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. This already proves that the population 0 matching loss is feasible (Proposition
5
): the feasible set is nonempty because
w^{\star}
itself satisfies all the required moment equations. Since
w^{\star}_{h}
defined in Definition
3
minimizes the matching loss, we have
w^{\star}_{h}=w^{\star}
.

It remains to justify the minimum-norm claim. Let
w\in L_{2}(d_{h}^{D})
be any other function satisfying the same moment equations, namely
\langle w,f\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}=\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)
for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Then
w-w^{\star}_{h}
is orthogonal to every element of
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Since
w^{\star}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, we have
\langle w-w^{\star}_{h},w^{\star}_{h}\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}=0
. Therefore,

\|w\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}=\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}+\|w-w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}\geq\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}.

Thus
w^{\star}_{h}
is the unique minimum-norm solution, up to
d_{h}^{D}
-almost-sure equivalence.

For (ii), the argument is similar. The empirical functional
\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}
is linear in
f
. Moreover, under Assumption
3
, we have

|\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{(i)}|\cdot|f(s_{h}^{(i)},\pi)|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}\biggl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{(i)}|\biggr)\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}.

Therefore
\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}
is continuous on
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Applying the Riesz representation theorem again yields a unique
\widetilde{w}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
such that
\langle\widetilde{w}_{h},f\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}=\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)
for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
.

For (iii), let
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}:=\{f|_{n}:f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the sample-evaluation subspace of
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. The empirical matching constraints only depend on the projection of the weight vector onto
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}
, because every constraint is tested against some vector
f|_{n}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}
. Decompose the least-2nd-moment solution as
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}=v+v_{\bot}
, where
v\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}
and
v_{\bot}\in(\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n})^{\bot}
. Since
v_{\bot}
is orthogonal to every
f|_{n}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}
, replacing
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
by
v
preserves all empirical matching constraints. If
v_{\bot}\neq 0
, this replacement strictly decreases
\|\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\|_{[n]}
, contradicting the least-2nd-moment choice of
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
. Therefore,
v_{\bot}=0
and
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}
.

By the definition of
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}
, there exists
\bar{w}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
such that
\bar{w}_{h}|_{n}=\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
. Since by our assumption,
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
achieves empirical 0 matching loss, we obtain
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{w}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})f(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})=\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)
for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
.
∎

\bar{w}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
such that
\bar{w}_{h}|_{n}=\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
, the proof is exactly same as stated in Proposition
15
(iii). Note that in there we do not use the empirical 0 matching loss condition for
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}
argument.

For the first argument that the least-2nd-moment population weight
w^{\star}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, we can adopt a similar strategy to prove (which does not require Assumption
3
). For any
w\in L_{2}(d_{h}^{D})
and any
f\in\mathcal{F}_{h}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{D}}[w\cdot f]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{D}}[(\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}w)\cdot f],

where
\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}
is the
L_{2}(d_{h}^{D})
-projection onto
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. This is because
w-\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}w
is orthogonal to
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Therefore, the population matching loss
\mathcal{L}_{h}(w;w^{\star}_{h-1})
depends on

, which means
\mathcal{L}_{h}(w;w^{\star}_{h-1})=\mathcal{L}_{h}(\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}w;w^{\star}_{h-1})
. Moreover,

\|w\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}=\|\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}w\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}+\|w-\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}w\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}.

Thus, if
w^{\star}_{h}
is chosen among the exact minimizers of
\mathcal{L}_{h}(\cdot;w^{\star}_{h-1})
to have least-2nd-moment, then its orthogonal component outside
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
must be
0
. Otherwise replacing
w^{\star}_{h}
by
\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}w^{\star}_{h}
would preserve the loss and strictly reduce its 2nd-moment. Hence,
w^{\star}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
.

Proof of Proposition
6
.

For a function
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h+1}
, by Definition
5
, its one-step backup is given by
K^{\pi}_{h}f=\arg\min_{g\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}\mathbb{E}_{D}[(g(s_{h},a_{h})-f(s_{h+1},\pi))^{2}]\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. That is,
K^{\pi}_{h}f=\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}P^{\pi}f
, where the projection step preserves linearity in
\phi
of the function. Therefore, if
f(s,a)=\phi(s,a)^{\top}\theta_{h+1}
, we can write
(K^{\pi}_{h}f)(s,a)=\phi(s,a)^{\top}\theta_{h}
for some
\theta_{h}
solved by the above least-square problem. Specifically, the normal equation for this least-square problem is

\Sigma_{h}\theta_{h}=\Sigma^{\textup{cr}}_{h}\theta_{h+1},

where
\Sigma_{h}
and
\Sigma^{\textup{cr}}_{h}
are defined in Eq. (
6
). Thus, for invertible
\Sigma_{h}
, we have
\theta_{h}=\Sigma_{h}^{-1}\Sigma^{\textup{cr}}_{h}\theta_{h+1}=(B_{h}^{\pi})^{\top}\theta_{h+1}
, since
B_{h}^{\pi}=(\Sigma^{\textup{cr}}_{h})^{\top}\Sigma_{h}^{-1}
. This proves the first claim.

For the multi-step bound, let
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
have coefficient
\theta_{h}
. Repeatedly applying the previous identity gives that
K^{\pi}_{t}K^{\pi}_{t+1}\cdots K^{\pi}_{h-1}f
has coefficient

(B_{t}^{\pi})^{\top}(B_{t+1}^{\pi})^{\top}\cdots(B_{h-1}^{\pi})^{\top}\theta_{h}=(B_{h-1}^{\pi}\cdots B_{t+1}^{\pi}B_{t}^{\pi})^{\top}\theta_{h}.

Therefore, for any
(s_{t},a_{t})\in\mathcal{S}_{t}\times\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle(K^{\pi}_{t}K^{\pi}_{t+1}\cdots K^{\pi}_{h-1}f)(s_{t},a_{t})

\displaystyle=\phi(s_{t},a_{t})^{\top}(B_{h-1}^{\pi}\cdots B_{t+1}^{\pi}B_{t}^{\pi})^{\top}\theta_{h}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Sigma_{h}^{-1/2}B_{h-1}^{\pi}\cdots B_{t+1}^{\pi}B_{t}^{\pi}\phi(s_{t},a_{t}),\Sigma_{h}^{1/2}\theta_{h}\right\rangle.

By Cauchy-Schwarz,

\left|(K^{\pi}_{t}K^{\pi}_{t+1}\cdots K^{\pi}_{h-1}f)(s_{t},a_{t})\right|\leq\left\|\Sigma_{h}^{-1/2}B_{h-1}^{\pi}\cdots B_{t+1}^{\pi}B_{t}^{\pi}\phi(s_{t},a_{t})\right\|_{2}\cdot\|\Sigma_{h}^{1/2}\theta_{h}\|_{2}.

Since
\|\Sigma_{h}^{1/2}\theta_{h}\|_{2}=\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
, we take the supremum over
(s_{t},a_{t})
and then over all
f
with
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
yields the bound for
\rho_{t:h}
.

Proof of Proposition
11
.

We first want to claim that Bellman completeness over the original function class
\mathcal{F}_{h}
(
\mathcal{T}^{\pi}f\in\mathcal{F}_{h-1},\forall f\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
) implies the backup closure property of the Hilbert space
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
(
P^{\pi}f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h-1},\forall f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
). This is because we assume
0\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
and hence
r=\mathcal{T}^{\pi}0\in\mathcal{F}_{h-1}
; by Bellman completeness,
\mathcal{T}^{\pi}f\in\mathcal{F}_{h-1}
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
. Thus,
P^{\pi}f=\mathcal{T}^{\pi}f-r\in\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{F}_{h-1})
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
. By linearity,
P^{\pi}\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{F}_{h})\subseteq\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{F}_{h-1})
, and by closure this extends to
P^{\pi}\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h-1}
.

For 1), we prove by induction that, for every
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
,
\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{D}}[w^{\star}_{h}f]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[f]
. For
h=1
, population
0
matching loss (Proposition
5
) gives
\mathbb{E}_{d_{1}^{D}}[w^{\star}_{1}f]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{0}^{D}}[w^{\star}_{0}\cdot(P^{\pi}f)]
. Since
w^{\star}_{0}\equiv 1
and
d_{0}^{D}=d_{0}^{\pi}
is the fixed initial state-action pair, by Bellman flow equation,
\mathbb{E}_{d_{0}^{D}}[w^{\star}_{0}\cdot(P^{\pi}f)]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{1}^{\pi}}f
. Thus the claim holds for
h=1
.

Assume that the induction holds at level
h-1
, and take any
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. By our claim before, we know that
P^{\pi}f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h-1}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{D}}[w^{\star}_{h}f]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h-1}^{D}}[w^{\star}_{h-1}\cdot(P^{\pi}f)]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h-1}^{\pi}}[P^{\pi}f]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}f},

where the first equality is due to population
0
matching loss, the second equality is by inductive hypothesis, and the last equality uses Bellman flow equation that
d_{h}^{\pi}=P^{\pi}d_{h-1}^{\pi}
for
h\in[H]
. This proves 1).

For 2), let
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Since
P^{\pi}f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h-1}
, by the definition of the projected backup operator
K^{\pi}_{h-1}
, we have that
K^{\pi}_{h-1}f=\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h-1}}P^{\pi}f=P^{\pi}f
as an element of
L_{2}(d_{h-1}^{D})
.

Under Assumption
3
, equality in
L_{2}(d_{h-1}^{D})
implies pointwise equality for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}_{h-1}\times\mathcal{A}
, because
|g(s,a)|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{h-1}}\|g\|_{2,d_{h-1}^{D}}
for all
g\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h-1}
. Hence,

(K^{\pi}_{h-1}f)(s,a)=(P^{\pi}f)(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f(s_{h},\pi)\mid s_{h-1}=s,a_{h-1}=a].

Iterating this identity from
t
to
h-1
yields

(K^{\pi}_{t}K^{\pi}_{t+1}\cdots K^{\pi}_{h-1}f)(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f(s_{h},\pi)\mid s_{t}=s,a_{t}=a].

3) For any
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
with
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, by the multi-step identity in 2),

|(K^{\pi}_{t}K^{\pi}_{t+1}\cdots K^{\pi}_{h-1}f)(s,a)|=|\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f(s_{h},\pi)\mid s_{t}=s,a_{t}=a]|\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

Therefore, by Definition
5
,
\rho_{t:h}=\sup_{f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}:\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\|K^{\pi}_{t}K^{\pi}_{t+1}\cdots K^{\pi}_{h-1}f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
12
.

We first prove the general case with Bellman completeness. By Proposition
11
(1), for any
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{D}}[w^{\star}_{h}f]=\langle w^{\star}_{h},f\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}
for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

|\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[f]|=|\langle w^{\star}_{h},f\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}|\leq\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}.

Moreover, the above inequality becomes equality if we take
f=w^{\star}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Therefore we get

\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}=\sup_{f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h},f\neq 0}\frac{|\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[f]|}{\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}}=\sup_{f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h},f\neq 0}\frac{|\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[f]|}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{D}}[f^{2}]}}.

Now we consider linear case. By Proposition
16
, we have
w^{\star}_{h}(s,a)=\phi(s,a)^{\top}\Sigma_{h}^{-1}\psi_{h}
. Therefore,

\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{D}}\bigl[(\phi_{h}^{\top}\Sigma_{h}^{-1}\psi_{h})^{2}\bigr]=\psi_{h}^{\top}\Sigma_{h}^{-1}\psi_{h}=\|\psi_{h}\|_{\Sigma_{h}^{-1}}^{2},

i.e.,
\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}=\|\psi_{h}\|_{\Sigma_{h}^{-1}}
, which does NOT require Bellman completeness.

Under linear Bellman completeness, by Proposition
11
(1), we have

\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{D}}[w^{\star}_{h}f_{\theta}]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[f_{\theta}]

holds for all
\theta
, where
f_{\theta}(s,a)=\phi(s,a)^{\top}\theta
. Recall
w^{\star}_{h}(s,a)=\phi(s,a)^{\top}\Sigma_{h}^{-1}\psi_{h}
. The LHS of the above equation becomes
\psi_{h}^{\top}\theta
, since
\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{D}}[\phi_{h}\phi_{h}^{\top}]
cancels with
\Sigma_{h}^{-1}
; while the RHS of the above equation becomes
\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[\phi_{h}]^{\top}\theta
. Therefore,
\psi_{h}=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[\phi_{h}]
and hence
\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}=\|\mathbb{E}_{d_{h}^{\pi}}[\phi_{h}]\|_{\Sigma_{h}^{-1}}
. This proves the claim.

C.3
Concentration arguments

We state two standard concentration arguments, both obtained by Bernstein’s inequality together with a covering argument. For each
f
, let
N_{h}
be the
1/8
-covering number of the unit ball of
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
under
\|\cdot\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}_{h}:=\{f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}:\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1\}
. Define

\chi_{h}:=\frac{4}{3}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log(8HN_{h}^{2}/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{4\kappa_{h}\log(8HN_{h}^{2}/\delta)}{3n}\right),

(17)

and, for
1\leq t<h\leq H
,

\tau_{t:h}=\frac{4}{3}(\rho_{t:h}+\rho_{t+1:h})\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log(8H^{2}N_{t}N_{h}/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{2\sqrt{\kappa_{t}\kappa_{h}}\log(8H^{2}N_{t}N_{h}/\delta)}{3n}\right),

(18)

where
\rho_{t:h}
is defined in Definition
5
. Now, for two elements
u,v\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, we define the sample inner product and norm as

\langle u|_{n},v|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})v(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)}),\quad\|u|_{n}\|_{[n]}^{2}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})^{2}.

Lemma 17

(Near-isometry)
.

Under Assumption
3
, with probability at least
1-\delta/4
, simultaneously for all
h\in[H]
and
u,v\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
,

\Bigl|\langle u|_{n},v|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u,v\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\Bigr|\leq\chi_{h}\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}},

where
\chi_{h}
is given in Eq. (
17
). In particular, take
u=v
, we get the near-isometry norm bound:

(1-\chi_{h})\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}\leq\|u|_{n}\|_{[n]}^{2}\leq(1+\chi_{h})\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}.

Lemma 18

(Cross-step concentration)
.

For any
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t+1}
, define the one-step sample realization operator by
(\widehat{P}_{t}f)(s_{t}^{(i)},a_{t}^{(i)}):=f(s_{t+1}^{(i)},\pi)
. Under Assumption
3
, with probability at least
1-\delta/4
, simultaneously for all
1\leq t<h\leq H
, all
u\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
and all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
,

\left|\left\langle u|_{n},(\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}|_{n}\right\rangle_{[n]}\right|\leq\tau_{t:h}\|u\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}},

where
K^{\pi}_{t}=\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}P^{\pi}
is the projected transition operator at step
t
(see Definition
5
),
g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}
is the shorthand for
K^{\pi}_{t+1}\cdots K^{\pi}_{h-1}f
(that is, the backup function of
f
onto step
t+1
from step
h
), and
\tau_{t:h}
is given in Eq. (
18
).

Proof of Lemma
17
.

First, we fix an arbitrary pair of elements
(u,v)\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}\times\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
and time step
h\in[H]
. Let
Y_{i}=u(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})v(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})
and
\mu=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]
. Then the concentration error to be controlled becomes

\Bigl|\langle u|_{n},v|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u,v\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\Bigr|=\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}-\mu\right|.

Under Assumption
3
,
|Y_{i}|\leq\sup_{s,a}|u(s,a)||v(s,a)|\leq\kappa_{h}\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
, and by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
\mu=|\mathbb{E}[uv]|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[v^{2}]}=\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
. Thus the range bound is given by

|Y_{i}-\mu|\leq(\kappa_{h}+1)\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 2\kappa_{h}\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}},

since
\kappa_{h}\geq 1
. On the other hand, the variance of
Y_{i}
can also be bounded via

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y_{i}]\leq\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[u(s,a)^{2}\cdot v(s,a)^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{h}\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[u(s,a)^{2}]=\kappa_{h}\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2},

where the inequality is also due to Assumption
3
. Thus, by Bernstein’s inequality, for fixed
(u,v)
, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}-\mu\right|\leq\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{4\kappa_{h}\log(4/\delta)}{3n}\right).

Now we complete the proof by a standard covering argument. Let
\mathcal{C}_{h}
be a
1/8
-cover of the unit ball
\mathcal{B}_{h}:=\{f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}:\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1\}
under the norm
\|\cdot\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
, with
|\mathcal{C}_{h}|=N_{h}
. Applying the fixed-pair Bernstein bound above to all pairs
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{C}_{h}\times\mathcal{C}_{h}
and all
h\in[H]
, with failure probability
\delta/(2HN_{h}^{2})
for each pair, we obtain that with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, simultaneously for all
h\in[H]
and
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{h}
,

\Bigl|\langle u_{0}|_{n},v_{0}|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u_{0},v_{0}\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\Bigr|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log(8HN_{h}^{2}/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{4\kappa_{h}\log(8HN_{h}^{2}/\delta)}{3n}=\frac{3}{4}\chi_{h}.

It remains to extend this bound from the finite cover to the whole unit ball. Let
M_{h}=\sup_{u,v\in\mathcal{B}_{h}}|\langle u|_{n},v|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u,v\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}|
be the uniform concentration error. By the covering property, for arbitrary
u,v\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
, there exists
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{h}
such that

\|u-u_{0}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq\frac{1}{8},\quad\|v-v_{0}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq\frac{1}{8}.

By bilinearity of inner product, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\langle u|_{n},v|_{n}

\displaystyle\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u,v\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\Bigr|\leq\Bigl|\langle u_{0}|_{n},v_{0}|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u_{0},v_{0}\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\Bigr|

\displaystyle+\Bigl|\langle(u-u_{0})|_{n},v|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u-u_{0},v\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\Bigr|+\Bigl|\langle u_{0}|_{n},(v-v_{0})|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u_{0},v-v_{0}\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\Bigr|.

The first term is bounded by
3\chi_{h}/4
. For the second term, since
\|u-u_{0}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1/8
and
\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1
, we have
8(u-u_{0})\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
and
v\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
. Therefore, by the definition of
M_{h}
, we have

\Bigl|\langle(u-u_{0})|_{n},v|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u-u_{0},v\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\Bigr|\leq\frac{1}{8}M_{h}.

Similarly, the third term is also bounded by
M_{h}/8
. Combining the three terms, we have that for arbitrary
u,v\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
,

\Bigl|\langle u|_{n},v|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u,v\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\Bigr|\leq\frac{3}{4}\chi_{h}+\frac{1}{4}M_{h}.

Taking the supremum over
u,v\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
, we have
M_{h}\leq 3\chi_{h}/4+M_{h}/4
, which is equivalent to
M_{h}\leq\chi_{h}
. Finally, for arbitrary
u,v\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, if both norms are non-zero (if either norm is zero, then the same inequality is trivial under Assumption
3
, since
\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}=0
implies
u(s,a)=0
on all sample points as well), apply the above unit-ball bound to
u/\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
and
v/\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
yields

\Bigl|\langle u|_{n},v|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle u,v\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\Bigr|\leq\chi_{h}\|u\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|v\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}},\quad\forall u,v\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h},\quad\forall h\in[H].

This proves the uniform concentration bound.
∎

Proof of Lemma
18
.

The proof structure is similar to that of Lemma
17
. We first fix
t<h
,
u\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
and
f\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{t}
and
\mathcal{B}_{h}
are unit balls. Define

Z_{i}:=u(s_{t}^{(i)},a_{t}^{(i)})\left(g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}(s_{t+1}^{(i)},\pi)-g_{t}^{(h,f)}(s_{t}^{(i)},a_{t}^{(i)})\right),

where
g_{t}^{(h,f)}=K^{\pi}_{t}g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}=K^{\pi}_{t}\cdots K^{\pi}_{h-1}f
. Then, the concentration quantity we want to control becomes

\left|\left\langle u|_{n},(\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}|_{n}\right\rangle_{[n]}\right|=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}.

Notice that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=0
. This is because when we take expectation,

\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=\mathbb{E}_{(s_{t},a_{t})\sim d_{t}^{D}}\left[u(s_{t},a_{t})\left((P^{\pi}g_{t+1}^{(h,f)})(s_{t},a_{t})-(K^{\pi}_{t}g_{t+1}^{(h,f)})(s_{t},a_{t})\right)\right].

But
K^{\pi}_{t}g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}=\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}P^{\pi}g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}
where
\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}
is the projection onto
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
. Since
u\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
, the projection residual is orthogonal to
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
, i.e.,
K^{\pi}_{t}
and
P^{\pi}
agree when tested against functions in
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
. Hence
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=0
.

Now we are going to bound
Z_{i}
. Since
\|u\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}\leq 1
, generalized leverage gives
|u(s,a)|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{t}}
. Also,
\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1
implies
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}
. Thus by Definition
5
, we have

\|g_{t}^{(h,f)}\|_{\infty}\leq\rho_{t:h}\sqrt{\kappa_{h}},\quad\|g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}\|_{\infty}\leq\rho_{t+1:h}\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}.

This gives an upper bound for the range of
Z_{i}
, i.e.,
|Z_{i}|\leq(\rho_{t:h}+\rho_{t+1:h})\sqrt{\kappa_{t}\kappa_{h}}
. On the other hand, for the variance control, we can use a slightly sharper bound that

\mathrm{Var}[Z_{i}]\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{2}]\leq(\rho_{t:h}+\rho_{t+1:h})^{2}\kappa_{h}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(s_{t},a_{t})\sim d_{t}^{D}}[u(s_{t},a_{t})^{2}]\leq(\rho_{t:h}+\rho_{t+1:h})^{2}\kappa_{h},

since
\|u\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}\leq 1
. Therefore, Bernstein’s inequality gives for fixed
u,f
, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\right|\leq(\rho_{t:h}+\rho_{t+1:h})\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log(4/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{2\sqrt{\kappa_{t}\kappa_{h}}\log(4/\delta)}{3n}\right).

The subsequent steps are similar. We can take
1/8
-nets
\mathcal{C}_{t}\subset\mathcal{B}_{t}
and
\mathcal{C}_{h}\subset\mathcal{B}_{h}
, with sizes
|\mathcal{C}_{t}|=N_{t}
and
|\mathcal{C}_{h}|=N_{h}
. Union bound over all
(u_{0},f_{0})\in\mathcal{C}_{t}\times\mathcal{C}_{h}
and all
t,h
gives the same bound with
\log(4/\delta)\leadsto\log(8H^{2}N_{t}N_{h}/\delta)
. We can apply the same procedure to extend the bound from the net to the whole unit ball, by paying an additional
4/3
factor to get

\sup_{u\in\mathcal{B}_{t},f\in\mathcal{B}_{h}}\left|\left\langle u|_{n},(\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}|_{n}\right\rangle_{[n]}\right|\leq\tau_{t:h}.

Finally, for general nonzero
u,f
, apply the above unit-ball result to
u/\|u\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}
and
f/\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
proves the simultaneous concentration bound.
∎

C.4
Proof of Theorem
8

Now we are going to prove the empirical-population tracking theorem (Theorem
8
). We will break the proof into three steps. Throughout, define

\eta_{h}:=\|\widetilde{w}_{h}-w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}},\quad\Delta_{h}:=\|\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}-w^{\star}_{h}|_{n}\|_{[n]}.

Our goal is to control the tracking error
\Delta_{h}
.

Step 1: duality conversion

We will first transfer the weight error
\Delta_{h}
to the functional-side error
\eta_{h}
. This is inspired by linear case where the linear functional (dual) space corresponds to the feature space, and the dynamical system in linear case is also defined in the feature space, which is a much more convenient way to do analysis; see also
6
. To see why this is a duality, define the dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
for linear functional
\Lambda
such that

\|\Lambda\|_{*}=\sup_{f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}:\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1}|\Lambda(f)|.

The following proposition relates
\eta_{h}
with this functional error’s dual norm.

Proposition 19

(Error duality)
.

For two linear functionals
\widehat{\Lambda}_{h},\Lambda_{h}^{\star}:\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
, suppose their Riesz representers are two elements
\widetilde{w}_{h},w^{\star}_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, respectively. Then,
\|\widetilde{w}_{h}-w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}=\|\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}-\Lambda_{h}^{\star}\|_{*}
.

Proof.

For any
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, we have

\bigl|(\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}-\Lambda_{h}^{\star})(f)\bigr|=\langle\widetilde{w}_{h}-w^{\star}_{h},f\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}\leq\|\widetilde{w}_{h}-w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}},

where the first equality is due to Riesz representation theorem, and the second inequality is by Cauchy-Schwarz. Thus, taking the supremum over
\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1
gives one direction, i.e.,
\|\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}-\Lambda_{h}^{\star}\|_{*}\leq\|\widetilde{w}_{h}-w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
.

On the other hand, if
\widetilde{w}_{h}=w^{\star}_{h}
, then the claim is trivial. Otherwise, take

f_{h}^{\star}=\frac{\widetilde{w}_{h}-w^{\star}_{h}}{\|\widetilde{w}_{h}-w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}.

Then
\|f_{h}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}=1
, and
(\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}-\Lambda_{h}^{\star})(f)=\|\widetilde{w}_{h}-w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
. Hence,
\|\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}-\Lambda_{h}^{\star}\|_{*}\geq\|\widetilde{w}_{h}-w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
. Combining the two inequalities proves the claim.
∎

Now we are going to translate the tracking error
\Delta_{h}
with the functional error
\eta_{h}
; see the following lemma.

Lemma 20

(Tracking error conversion)
.

For any
h\in[H]
, assume
\chi_{h}\leq 1/2
for
\chi_{h}
in Eq. (
17
), which can be achieved by
n\gtrsim\kappa_{h}\log(HN_{h}/\delta)
. Then,
\Delta_{h}\leq(1+3\chi_{h})\eta_{h}+2\chi_{h}\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}
.

Proof.

By triangular inequality,

\Delta_{h}=\|\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}-w_{h}^{\star}|_{n}\|_{[n]}=\|\bar{w}_{h}-w_{h}^{\star}\|_{[n]}\leq\|\widetilde{w}_{h}-w_{h}^{\star}\|_{[n]}+\|\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}\|_{[n]}.

For the first term, Lemma
17
gives (where we use
\sqrt{1+x}\leq 1+x/2
for
x\geq 0
):

\|\widetilde{w}_{h}-w_{h}^{\star}\|_{[n]}\leq\sqrt{1+\chi_{h}}\|\widetilde{w}_{h}-w_{h}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\chi_{h}\right)\eta_{h}.

For the second term, recall the definition of
\widetilde{w}_{h}
(which is the representer of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}
) and
\bar{w}_{h}
(which is the sample-lift of
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}
), we have

\langle\widetilde{w}_{h},f\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}=\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)=\langle\bar{w}_{h},f\rangle_{[n]},\quad\forall f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}.

Taking
f=\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}\|_{[n]}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\bar{w}_{h},\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}\rangle_{[n]}-\langle\widetilde{w}_{h},\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}\rangle_{[n]}

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{w}_{h},\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}\rangle_{d_{h}^{D}}-\langle\widetilde{w}_{h},\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}\rangle_{[n]}

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{h}\|\widetilde{w}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\chi_{h}}{\sqrt{1-\chi_{h}}}\|\widetilde{w}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\|\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}\|_{{[n]}},

where the last two inequalities are both due to Lemma
17
. By canceling a
\|\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}\|_{[n]}
, we have

\|\bar{w}_{h}-\widetilde{w}_{h}\|_{[n]}\leq\frac{\chi_{h}}{\sqrt{1-\chi_{h}}}\|\widetilde{w}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 2\chi_{h}(\eta_{h}+\|w_{h}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}),

where we use the fact that
\chi_{h}\leq 1/2
and triangular inequality. Combining the two terms, we have

\Delta_{h}\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\chi_{h}\right)\eta_{h}+2\chi_{h}(\eta_{h}+\|w_{h}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}})\leq(1+3\chi_{h})\eta_{h}+2\chi_{h}\|w_{h}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}.

∎

Step 2: functional telescoping and recursion

By Proposition
19
and Lemma
20
, we learned that essentially we want to control

\eta_{h}=\|\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}-\Lambda_{h}^{\star}\|_{*}=\sup_{f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}:\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1}|\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)-\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)|.

The following telescoping lemma demonstrates the iterative nature of
\Lambda_{h}^{\star}
and
\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}
, since our
Q-MMR
algorithm is level-by-level. As we will see, the iterative property of the population functional
\Lambda_{h}^{\star}
depends on the projected backup operator
K^{\pi}_{h}
in Definition
5
; while the operator corresponding to the empirical functional
\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}
is the one-step realization operator
\widehat{P}_{h}
defined in Lemma
18
, i.e.,

(\widehat{P}_{h}f)(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})=f(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi),\quad f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h+1}.

Such correspondences enable us to do functional telescoping; see the following lemma.

Lemma 21

(Functional telescoping)
.

For every
h\in[H]
and
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
,

\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)-\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)=\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}(g_{1}^{(h,f)})-\Lambda_{1}^{\star}(g_{1}^{(h,f)})+\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}\left\langle\bar{w}_{t}|_{n},((\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1}^{(h,f)})|_{n}\right\rangle_{[n]},

where
g_{t}^{(h,f)}
is the shorthand for
K^{\pi}_{t}K^{\pi}_{t+1}\cdots K^{\pi}_{h-1}f
, i.e., the multi-step backup of
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
onto
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
.

Proof.

We first claim that
\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)=\Lambda_{t}^{\star}(g_{t}^{(h,f)})=\langle w_{t}^{\star},g_{t}^{(h,f)}\rangle_{d_{t}^{D}}
for every
1\leq t\leq h\leq H
and
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
. Suppose this claim holds, we have that
\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)=\Lambda_{1}^{\star}(g_{1}^{(h,f)})
. Thus it remains to relate the empirical functionals. Write
g_{t}:=g_{t}^{(h,f)}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
. By the definition of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{t+1}
, we have

\widehat{\Lambda}_{t+1}(g_{t+1})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{t}^{(i)}g_{t+1}(s_{t+1}^{(i)},\pi)=\langle\bar{w}_{t},\widehat{P}_{t}g_{t+1}\rangle_{[n]},

and by using the matching condition (Proposition
15
(iii)), we can also write

\widehat{\Lambda}_{t}(g_{t})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{t}^{(i)}g_{t}(s_{t}^{(i)},a_{t}^{(i)})=\langle\bar{w}_{t}|_{n},g_{t}|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}.

Since
g_{t}=K^{\pi}_{t}g_{t+1}
by the backup recursion, the difference of the above two equations becomes

\widehat{\Lambda}_{t+1}(g_{t+1})-\widehat{\Lambda}_{t}(g_{t})=\langle\bar{w}_{t}|_{n},((\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1})|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}.

By telescoping (summing over
t=1,\ldots,h-1
), we have

\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)-\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}(g_{1})=\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}\langle\bar{w}_{t}|_{n},((\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1})|_{n}\rangle_{[n]},

since
f=g_{h}^{(h,f)}
. This proves the lemma.

Now we go back to prove the claim that
\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)=\Lambda_{t}^{\star}(g_{t})=\langle w_{t}^{\star},g_{t}\rangle_{d_{t}^{D}}
. If
t=h
,
g_{t}=g_{h}^{(h,f)}=f
so the claim trivially holds. Consider
t<h
. For
t=h-1
, we can write

\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h-1}^{D}}[w_{h-1}^{\star}\cdot(P^{\pi}f)]=\mathbb{E}_{d_{h-1}^{D}}[w_{h-1}^{\star}\cdot(\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h-1}}P^{\pi}f)]=\langle w_{h-1}^{\star},K^{\pi}_{h-1}f\rangle_{d_{h-1}^{D}},

where the second equality uses
w_{h-1}^{\star}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h-1}
(Proposition
15
(i)) and orthogonality of
\Pi_{h-1}
(the projection onto
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h-1}
), and the last equality uses the definition of
K^{\pi}_{h-1}=\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h-1}}P^{\pi}
. Iterating this identity, we have

\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)=\langle w_{t}^{\star},K^{\pi}_{t}K^{\pi}_{t+1}\cdots K^{\pi}_{h-1}f\rangle_{d_{t}^{D}}=\langle w_{t}^{\star},g_{t}\rangle_{d_{t}^{D}}=\Lambda_{t}^{\star}(g_{t}),

where the last equality is due to the definition of
\Lambda_{t}^{\star}
.
∎

Using the telescoping in Lemma
21
, we can now decompose this empirical-population tracking error (in the functional space) and thereby control
\eta_{h}
, yielding a recursion formula to be solved later.

Lemma 22

(Functional recursion)
.

With probability at least
1-\delta
, assume
\chi_{t}\leq 1/2
for all
t\leq h
. Then for every
h\in[H]
,

\eta_{h}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}a_{t:h}\eta_{t}+\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}a_{t:h}\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}+\frac{4}{3}\rho_{1:h}\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log(4N_{h}/\delta)}{n}},

where
a_{t:h}:=\tau_{t:h}+3\chi_{t}\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}(\rho_{t:h}+\rho_{t+1:h})
with
\chi_{h}
in Eq. (
17
) and
\tau_{t:h}
in Eq. (
18
).

Proof.

Fix
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
with
\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1
. By Lemma
21
, we have

\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)-\Lambda_{h}^{\star}(f)=\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}(g_{1}^{(h,f)})-\Lambda_{1}^{\star}(g_{1}^{(h,f)})+\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}\left\langle\bar{w}_{t}|_{n},((\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1}^{(h,f)})|_{n}\right\rangle_{[n]}.

For the first-step error term
\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}(g)-\Lambda_{1}^{\star}(g)
with
g:=g_{1}^{(h,f)}
, by Definition
6
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}(g)-\Lambda_{1}^{\star}(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{0}^{(i)}g(s_{1}^{(i)},\pi)-\mathbb{E}_{D}[w_{0}^{\star}(s_{0},a_{0})g(s_{1},\pi)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(s_{1}^{(i)},\pi)-\mathbb{E}_{D}[g(s_{1},\pi)]\leq\frac{4}{3}\rho_{1:h}\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log(4N_{h}/\delta)}{n}},

with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, where we used
\|g\|_{\infty}\leq\rho_{1:h}
and the standard covering argument over
\mathcal{B}_{h}
(since
g=g_{1}^{(h,f)}
is uniquely determined by
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
). It remains to tackle the second summation term.

For notational simplicity, we omit the
|_{n}
in
\langle\cdot|_{n},\cdot|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}
in the following proof. We decompose this functional error into the following three terms using
\bar{w}_{t}=(\widetilde{w}_{t}-w_{t}^{\star})+w_{t}^{\star}+(\bar{w}_{t}-\widetilde{w}_{t})
:

\displaystyle\text{(I)}=\left|\left\langle\widetilde{w}_{t}-w_{t}^{\star},(\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}\right\rangle_{[n]}\right|,

\displaystyle\text{(II)}=\left|\left\langle w_{t}^{\star},(\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}\right\rangle_{[n]}\right|,

\displaystyle\text{(III)}=\left|\left\langle\bar{w}_{t}-\widetilde{w}_{t},(\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}\right\rangle_{[n]}\right|.

By Lemma
18
, we have
\text{(I)}\leq\tau_{t:h}\eta_{t}
and
\text{(II)}\leq\tau_{t:h}\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}
. For the third term,

(III)

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{w}_{t}-\widetilde{w}_{t}\|_{{[n]}}\cdot\left\|(\widehat{P}_{t}-K^{\pi}_{t})g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}\right\|_{[n]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\chi_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\chi_{t}}}(\eta_{t}+\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}})\left(\left\|\widehat{P}_{t}g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}\right\|_{[n]}+\left\|g_{t}^{(h,f)}\right\|_{[n]}\right),

where the first inequality is due to Cauchy-Schwarz, and the second inequality we leveraged proof of Lemma
20
to control the first
\|\bar{w}_{t}-\widetilde{w}_{t}\|_{[n]}
term, and the triangular inequality to control the second term (by noticing that
g_{t}^{(h,f)}=K^{\pi}_{t}g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}
).

For the
\|\widehat{P}_{t}g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}\|_{[n]}
term, we can bound it via Definition
5
:

\left\|\widehat{P}_{t}g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}\right\|_{[n]}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}(s_{t+1}^{(i)},\pi)^{2}}\leq\max_{s,a}\left|g_{t+1}^{(h,f)}(s,a)\right|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}\rho_{t+1:h}.

where we use Assumption
3
and the fact that
\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1
. For the
\|g_{t}^{(h,f)}\|_{[n]}
term, we can similarly bound it via Definition
5
(transferred via isometry concentration at first):

\left\|g_{t}^{(h,f)}\right\|_{{[n]}}\leq\sqrt{1+\chi_{t}}\left\|g_{t}^{(h,f)}\right\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}\leq\sqrt{1+\chi_{t}}\cdot\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}\rho_{t:h}\leq 2\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}\rho_{t:h}.

Hence combining the terms, the third term can be controlled by

(III)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\chi_{t}\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}}{\sqrt{1-\chi_{t}}}(\rho_{t:h}+\rho_{t+1:h})(\eta_{t}+\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}})

\displaystyle\leq 3\chi_{t}\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}(\rho_{t:h}+\rho_{t+1:h})(\eta_{t}+\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}).

Summing the three bounds and taking the supremum over
\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 1
controls the functional error term. By union bound over three events: the first step concentration error with failure probability
\delta/2
, the event in Lemma
17
with failure probability
\delta/4
, and the event in Lemma
18
with failure probability
\delta/4
; we prove the claim with probability at least
1-\delta
.
∎

Step 3: solving the recursion

As shown in Lemma
22
, what we get is essentially a recursion in
\eta_{h}
(i.e., the RHS also appears
\eta_{t}
for
t\leq h
). Therefore the final step of proving Theorem
8
is to solve for this recursion to control
\eta_{h}
, hence
\Delta_{h}
.

Proof of Theorem
8
.

Abbreviate
\epsilon_{1:j}=\frac{4}{3}\rho_{1:j}\sqrt{2\kappa_{j}\log(4N_{j}/\delta)/n}
. Let

A_{h}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq h}\epsilon_{1:j}+\sum_{t=1}^{j-1}a_{t,j}\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}},\quad B_{h}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq h}\sum_{t=1}^{j-1}a_{t,j},\quad E_{h}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq h}\eta_{j}.

By Lemma
22
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for every
j\leq h
, we have

\eta_{j}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{j-1}a_{t,j}\eta_{t}+\epsilon_{1:j}+\sum_{t=1}^{j-1}a_{t,j}\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}\leq B_{h}E_{h}+A_{h}.

Taking the maximum over
j\leq h
gives
E_{h}\leq B_{h}E_{h}+A_{h}
. Hence, we have

\eta_{h}\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq h}\eta_{j}=E_{h}\leq\frac{A_{h}}{1-B_{h}}\leq 2A_{h}\leq 2\epsilon_{1:h}+2\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}a_{t:h}\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}},

where we use
B_{h}\leq 1/2
since our choice of
n
that
n\gtrsim(\max_{t\leq h}\kappa_{t})^{2}(\sum_{t=1}^{h}\rho_{t:h})^{2}\log(H^{2}N_{t}N_{h}/\delta)
automatically enables
B_{h}\leq 1/2
; and the last inequality is due to the maximum of
A_{h}
is exactly taken at
j=h
(because all elements are non-negative; that is,
a_{t,j}\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}\geq 0
). Now we can apply Lemma
20
:

\Delta_{h}\leq(1+3\chi_{h})\eta_{h}+2\chi_{h}\|w_{h}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 5\epsilon_{1:h}+5\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}a_{t:h}\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}+2\chi_{h}\|w_{h}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}},

where we use
\chi_{h}\leq 1/2
(hence
2(1+3\chi_{h})\leq 5
). By the definitions of
\chi_{h}
in Eq. (
17
) and
\tau_{t:h}
in Eq. (
18
), we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}

\displaystyle\leq 5\epsilon_{1:h}+5\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}a_{t:h}\|w_{t}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}+2\chi_{h}\|w_{h}^{\star}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{3}\rho_{1:h}\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log(4N_{h}/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{20}{3}\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}\sum_{t=1}^{h-1}(\rho_{t:h}+\rho_{t+1:h})\|w^{\star}_{t}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log\tfrac{8H^{2}N_{t}N_{h}}{\delta}}{n}}+\frac{2\sqrt{\kappa_{t}\kappa_{h}}\log\tfrac{8H^{2}N_{t}N_{h}}{\delta}}{3n}+3\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log\frac{8HN_{h}^{2}}{\delta}}{n}}+\frac{4\kappa_{h}\log\tfrac{8HN_{h}^{2}}{\delta}}{n}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{8}{3}\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa_{h}\log\frac{8HN_{h}^{2}}{\delta}}{n}}+\frac{4\kappa_{h}\log\tfrac{8HN_{h}^{2}}{\delta}}{3n}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{t=1}^{h}\rho_{t:h}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{h}^{2}\log\tfrac{H^{2}N_{t}N_{h}}{\delta}}{n}}\|w^{\star}_{t}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}+\rho_{1:h}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{h}\log\tfrac{N_{h}}{\delta}}{n}}+\text{higher order term}.

We use
\lesssim
to ignore some universal constants (here the constant can be set to
320/3
if we combine all terms), and the higher order term is of order
O(1/n)
in the above equation. And we also combined the logarithmic terms and the first step term (by our choice of
n\gtrsim(\max_{t\leq h}\kappa_{t})^{2}(\sum_{t=1}^{h}\rho_{t:h})^{2}\log(H^{2}N_{t}N_{h}/\delta)
). Therefore, ignoring the higher-order term, we prove Theorem
8
: for any
h\in[H]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}-w^{\star}_{h}|_{n}\right\|_{[n]}\lesssim\sum_{t=1}^{h}\rho_{t:h}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{h}\log(H^{2}N_{t}N_{h}/\delta)}{n}}\|w^{\star}_{t}\|_{2,d_{t}^{D}}+\rho_{1:h}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{h}\log(N_{h}/\delta)}{n}}.

∎

C.5
Proofs of Proposition
9
and Corollary
10

Here we provide the proof of Proposition
9
, as well as the random-design result of the
Q-MMR
algorithm (Corollary
10
). As stated in Proposition
9
, the population 0 matching loss will imply the empirical 0 matching loss with high probability. In the linear case, this is obvious since the invertible covariance
\Sigma
implies the invertible empirical covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}
by matrix Bernstein inequality. However, under GFA, we need to leverage the near-isometry concentration argument (Lemma
17
).

Proof of Proposition
9
.

As we will see, the empirical 0 matching loss does not rely on the whole function
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, but its sample-evaluation
f|_{n}=(f(s_{h}^{(1)},a_{h}^{(1)}),\ldots,f(s_{h}^{(n)},a_{h}^{(n)}))
, where the sample evaluation map
|_{n}:\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The near-isometry condition in Lemma
17
implies that
|_{n}
is injective. Indeed, if
f|_{n}=0
, then
\|f|_{n}\|_{[n]}=0
, which implies

0=\|f|_{n}\|_{[n]}^{2}\geq(1-\chi_{h})\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}^{2}.

Since
\chi_{h}<1/2
by our choice of
n
, this gives
\|f\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}=0
, hence
f=0
as an element of
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
.

Now consider the sample evaluation space
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}=\{f|_{n}:f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We can define a linear functional
\ell_{h}:\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\ell_{h}(f|_{n}):=\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{(i)}f(s_{h}^{(i)},\pi),\quad\forall f|_{n}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n},

This is well defined. To see this, suppose
f|_{n}=g|_{n}
. Then
(f-g)|_{n}=0
by linearity; and
f=g
in
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
by injectivity of this sample evaluation operator
|_{n}
, i.e.,
\|f-g\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}=0
. Therefore, we can apply generalized leverage (Assumption
3
) to give

|f(s,a)-g(s,a)|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{h}}\|f-g\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}=0,\quad\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}_{h}\times\mathcal{A}.

Thus
f
and
g
also agree pointwise. In particular, this implies
f(s_{h}^{(i)},\pi)=g(s_{h}^{(i)},\pi)
for all
i\in[n]
. Hence
\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)=\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(g)
. So the linear functional
\ell_{h}
is well-defined.

Next, since
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is also finite-dimensional. Equip
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}
with the empirical inner product
\langle z,z^{\prime}\rangle_{[n]}=\frac{1}{n}z^{\top}z^{\prime}
. By the finite-dimensional Riesz representation theorem, there exists some vector
a_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\ell_{h}(z)=\langle a_{h},z\rangle_{[n]},\quad\forall z\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}|_{n}.

Taking
z=f|_{n}
, we have

\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)=\ell_{h}(f|_{n})=\langle a_{h},f|_{n}\rangle_{[n]}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{h}^{(i)}f(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)}),\quad\forall f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}.

By setting
\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}:=a_{h}
, we would achieve empirical 0 matching loss, i.e.,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}f(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})=\widehat{\Lambda}_{h}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{h-1}^{(i)}f(s_{h}^{(i)},\pi).

So the empirical matching loss over
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
is zero. Since
\mathcal{F}_{h}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{h}
, the empirical matching loss over
\mathcal{F}_{h}
is also zero. Thus the empirical minimizer used by
Q-MMR
also achieves zero loss.
∎

The proof of Corollary
10
is a direct consequence of Theorem
8
(by leveraging the same procedure of telescoping as our fixed-design result in Theorem
1
). As we will see in the following, the empirical-population tracking error is paired with the average Bellman residual, leading it to a higher-order term so that the dominant term becomes random-design control.

Proof of Corollary
10
.

First, we make the following decomposition of the suboptimality gap
J(\pi)-\widehat{J}(\pi)
:

\displaystyle|J(\pi)-\widehat{J}(\pi)|

\displaystyle=\left|Q^{\pi}(s_{0},a_{0})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{h=0}^{H}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}r_{h}^{(i)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{0}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{0}^{(i)},a_{0}^{(i)})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{h=0}^{H}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}r_{h}^{(i)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{h=0}^{H}\left(\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})-\widehat{w}_{h+1}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},a_{h+1}^{(i)})\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{h=0}^{H}\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}r_{h}^{(i)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{h=0}^{H}\left(\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})-\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)-\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}r_{h}^{(i)}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{h=0}^{H}w^{\star}_{h}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})\varepsilon_{h}^{(i)}\right|}_{\text{(I): random design control}}+\underbrace{\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{h=0}^{H}\left(\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}-w^{\star}_{h}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})\right)\varepsilon_{h}^{(i)}\right|}_{\text{(II): tracking error}},

where
\varepsilon_{h}^{(i)}:=Q^{\pi}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})-r_{h}^{(i)}-Q^{\pi}(s_{h+1}^{(i)},\pi)
denotes the Bellman residual at current transition sample
(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)},r_{h}^{(i)},s_{h+1}^{(i)})
. We use that
\widehat{w}_{0}\equiv 1
and
\widehat{w}_{H+1}\equiv 0
; and the fourth equation is due to our empirical 0 matching loss in Proposition
9
.

The first term is the random-design control on population weight functional
w^{\star}_{h}
. For each fixed
h
, define
X_{h,i}:=w^{\star}_{h}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})\varepsilon_{h}^{(i)}
. Conditioned on
\{(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the variables
\{X_{h,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are independent, mean-zero, and satisfy
|X_{h,i}|\leq V_{\max}|w^{\star}_{h}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})|
since
|\varepsilon_{h}^{(i)}|\leq|V_{\max}|
. Therefore, by standard Hoeffding’s inequality, we have: with probability at least
1-\alpha
,

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{h,i}\right|\leq V_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\alpha)}{n}}\|w^{\star}_{h}|_{n}\|_{[n]},

where
\|w^{\star}_{h}|_{n}\|_{[n]}=\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{\star}_{h}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})^{2}\right)^{1/2}
. Using the near-isometry transfer in Lemma
17
and
\chi_{h}\leq 1/2
(which is implied by our choice of
n
in Corollary
10
), we have

\|w^{\star}_{h}|_{n}\|_{[n]}\leq\sqrt{1+\chi_{h}}\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}\leq 2\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}.

Take
\alpha=\delta/(2(H+1))
, then union bound over
h\in[H]
, we can control the first term by

\text{(I)}\leq 2V_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4(H+1)/\delta)}{n}}\sum_{h=0}^{H}\|w^{\star}_{h}\|_{2,d_{h}^{D}}.

Now for the tracking part, define
Y_{h,i}=\Delta_{h}^{(i)}\varepsilon_{h}^{(i)}
. Because
\widehat{w}_{h}^{(i)}
depends only on the trajectory prefix up to
(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})
, and
w^{\star}_{h}(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})
is also measurable with respect to
(s_{h}^{(i)},a_{h}^{(i)})
, the coefficient
\Delta_{h}^{(i)}
is fixed when conditioning on the level-
h
prefixes. Therefore, conditional on those prefixes, the variables
\{Y_{h,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are again independent, mean-zero, and
|Y_{h,i}|\leq V_{\max}|\Delta_{h}^{(i)}|
. Apply Hoeffding’s inequality again, for each fixed
h
, with probability at least
1-\alpha
,

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{h,i}\right|\leq V_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\alpha)}{n}}\|\Delta_{h}\|_{[n]}=V_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\alpha)}{n}}\|\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}-w^{\star}_{h}|_{n}\|_{[n]}.

Taking again
\alpha=\delta/(2(H+1))
and union bound over
h
, we can control the second term by

\text{(II)}\leq V_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4(H+1)/\delta)}{n}}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\|\widehat{w}_{h}^{[n]}-w^{\star}_{h}|_{n}\|_{[n]},

since
w^{\star}_{0}\equiv 1
and
\widehat{w}_{0}^{(i)}=1
for all
i\in[n]
. So now we can apply the tracking theorem (Theorem
8
) to see that the second term is indeed a higher-order term, since both the average Bellman error and the tracking error exhibit the rate of
n^{-1/2}
(hence an overall rate of
n^{-1}
). Combining
(I)
and
(II)
proves the corollary.
∎

Appendix D
Further discussion on MIS

D.1
Issues with non-parametric weights in MIS

MIS methods often take a minimax form similar to our
Q-MMR
, and here we provide further discussion on the issue with learning non-parametric weights in MIS. Taking MQL
[
27
]
as an example, whose population loss is (in the infinite-horizon discounted setting)

\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\max_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{q}}(f,w):=\left|\mathbb{E}_{d^{D}}[w(s,a)(f(s,a)-r-\gamma f(s^{\prime},\pi))]\right|.

When
w
is not restricted to a parametric class
\mathcal{W}
but is a separate scalar for each data point, one can immediately see that the loss
\mathcal{L}
is ill-behaved: the original MIS derivations rely on the fact that
\max_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{L}(Q^{\pi},w)=0

[
27
,
13
]
, but when
w
is non-parametric,
\mathcal{L}(Q^{\pi},w)\neq 0
in general when the environment is stochastic, since
w
can simply choose to be positive or negative depending on the sign of
Q^{\pi}(s,a)-r-\gamma Q^{\pi}(s^{\prime},\pi)
. (This issue can be viewed as a version of the infamous double-sampling problem.) In contrast, our algorithm and analyses forbid the choice of
w
to depend on the next-state randomness, which avoids this problem. Now, MQL learns
f
using
w
as discriminator, and its “dual” method such as MWL
[
27
]
learns
w
using
f
as discriminator, which is more similar to our method. We conjecture that MWL is subject to similar issues, though a clear counterexample remains to be found.

D.2
Data-dependent bounds for MIS

Consider MWL, whose population loss is (we assume initial state-action pair is fixed as
(s_{0},a_{0})
)

\argmin_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{w}}(w,f):=\left|f(s_{0},a_{0})+\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{d^{D}}[w(s,a)(\gamma f(s^{\prime},\pi)-f(s,a))]\right|.

The actual algorithm optimizes the empirical loss
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathsf{w}}
where
\mathbb{E}_{d^{D}}
is replaced with the empirical expectation
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{d^{D}}
, and forms the final prediction
\widehat{J}(\pi)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{d^{D}}[\widehat{w}r]
where
\widehat{w}
is the learned
w
. The standard finite-sample guarantee for MWL pays for the statistical complexities of both
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{F}
. Below we show that the complexity of
\mathcal{F}
can be avoided in a data-dependent guarantee similar to our Theorem
1
: for any fixed
w\in\mathcal{W}
, we have

\displaystyle~\left|\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{d^{D}}[w(s,a)r]-J(\pi)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\left|\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{d^{D}}[w(s,a)(Q^{\pi}(s,a)-r-\gamma Q^{\pi}(s^{\prime},\pi)]-Q^{\pi}(s_{0},a_{0})+\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{d^{D}}[w(s,a)(-Q^{\pi}(s,a)+\gamma Q^{\pi}(s^{\prime},\pi))\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\left|\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{d^{D}}[w(s,a)(Q^{\pi}(s,a)-r-\gamma Q^{\pi}(s^{\prime},\pi)]\right|+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathsf{w}}(w,f).

(
Q^{\pi}\in\mathcal{F}
)

The first term enjoys a “fixed-design” concentration bound similar to our proof of Theorem
1
, but needs a union bound over
w\in\mathcal{W}
so that the bound holds for
\widehat{w}
. The second term is the empirically observed loss, similar to our
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}
term in Theorem
1
.
