Introduction

Bonobos ( Pan paniscus ) are endangered in the wild and remain rare under human care, with approximately 75 individuals currently housed across seven AZA‐accredited institutions [1, 2]. Identifying species‐specific health trends, while also recognizing patterns shared across great apes, is imperative to optimize their medical management. Among those trends, cardiovascular disease (CVD) represents a significant concern. Myocardial fibrosis, the most frequently reported lesion, presents similarly across great apes and is estimated to affect up to 45% of bonobos under human care [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Hypertension and aortic dissection have also been documented in this species and data from pathologists and the Great Ape Heart Project (GAHP) supports hypertension as a contributing factor in CVD development in these species [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Beyond CVD, other clinically relevant conditions may also involve alterations in hemostasis. Prothrombotic and hypofibrinolytic states have been described in the management of ischemic stroke and neoplasia [9, 10, 11]. Furthermore, hormonal contraceptives, commonly used for population management in nonhuman primates are associated with increased risk of coagulopathies in humans [12, 13]. Despite these clinical considerations, research on the diagnosis and monitoring of coagulopathies in great apes remain limited with current data limited to chimpanzees ( Pan troglodytes ) [14].

Coagulation assessments in non‐traditional species are shifting towards comprehensive analysis of the entire coagulation profile through viscoelastic testing [15, 16, 17]. Viscoelastic testing provides a holistic evaluation of coagulation (rate of clot formation, clot strength, clot persistence) compared to plasma‐based testing of prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time, which primarily provide a sense of rate of clot formation. These approaches include thromboelastography (TEG), considered the gold standard, and a newer point‐of‐care test, viscoelastic coagulation monitoring (VCM) performed using the VCM Vet. TEG availability is typically limited to academic or specialty veterinary centers as a gold‐standard benchtop analyzer. This often necessitates use of frozen‐thawed citrated plasma in zoo settings, which lacks evaluation of the cellular component of hemostasis but can evaluate clotting factors and fibrinogen [17, 18, 19]. The VCM Vet offers a portable, veterinary‐specific alternative that does not require an activator and is well suited for use in zoos, research institutions, and field settings. VCM Vet has been evaluated across a diverse range of species: domestic cats ( Felis catus ), domestic dogs ( Canis lupus familiaris ), domestic horses ( Equus caballus ), mice, Amazon parrots (Amazona spp.), bats (Pteropus spp.), white rhinoceros ( Ceratotherium simum ), and African elephants ( Loxodonta africana ) [16, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Most recently, it was evaluated in chimpanzees and demonstrated a relatively prothrombotic profile compared to domestic dogs [14].

Evaluation of this device in bonobos has the potential to significantly enhance health assessments in managed care settings, particularly given the association between coagulation abnormalities and common morbidities in Pan species. The objectives of this study were to evaluate the feasibility of the point‐of‐care VCM Vet for measuring coagulation status in bonobos, describe tracings in a cohort of bonobos managed at a single institution, and to compare the clinical interpretations of VCM to kaolin‐activated TEG and traditional coagulation ancillary testing on citrated plasma (platelet count, prothrombin time [PT], activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT], fibrinogen and D‐dimer concentrations). The majority of individuals in the selected cohort are variably afflicted by CVD, providing the opportunity to assess coagulation profiles in relation to disease status within a subset of the managed population. Evaluating VCM Vet in bonobos broadens diagnostic capabilities, provides new insight into their coagulation physiology, and supports clinical decision making for the management of hemostatic disorders in this endangered species.

Methods

Inclusion Criteria

The study was approved by the animal holding facility's research committee, conducted in July 2024. Entegrion Inc. provided VCM equipment, including two analyzers and all cartridges, as well as analyzer training sessions (Entegrion Inc., Durham, NC, USA). Entegrion did not play a role in study design nor interpretation. Anesthesia was performed in bonobos housed at an institution accredited by the Association of Zoos and Aquariums (n = 12) over the course of 2 days to facilitate a habitat transfer, during which routine physical examination and diagnostics were conducted. Anesthetic protocols were determined by primary clinician but consisted of Ketamine (9.0–11.5 mg/kg) or Telazol (3.7 mg/kg) for induction, with supplemental propofol for intubation and maintenance on isoflurane gas anesthesia. Age classes were defined as subadult (< 10 years), adult (10–25 years), and geriatric (> 25 years). Age groups were estimated based on sexual maturity typically around 9 years old, ranging to 13 years old, and median life expectancy of male bonobos under managed care of 31 years old [27, 28]. Medical history and the results of the preventative health assessment were reviewed to characterize comorbidities present in the study population, including thoracic ± abdominal computed tomography with contrast, echocardiogram, complete blood count, biochemistry analysis, urinalysis, N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide, and Troponin T electrochemiluminescence immunoassay. However, the results of this assessment were not utilized as exclusion factors due to the limited number of individuals housed in managed care. Descriptive data regarding comorbidities are provided. Active prescriptions at the time of the study were included within the medical history. To be included in the study, each animal underwent assessment of standard clotting parameters (manual platelet count, PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, and D‐dimer concentrations), singular VCM Vet, and duplicate TEG measurement. Planned exclusion criteria included early termination of anesthesia prior to blood collection due to complications, insufficient sample volume to complete all tests, or machine errors that consumed the sample. No individuals were excluded for the aforementioned reasons. Due to timing and personnel constraints, VCM testing was not performed in duplicate at the time of each individual's procedure, but TEG tracings were performed in duplicate.

Sample Collection

Blood samples were collected opportunistically during the anesthetized medical procedures. Prior to sample collection, the VCM Vet analyzer was calibrated and cartridges were warmed to 37°C according to manufacturer instructions. Venipuncture was performed with a 21‐gauge, 0.75‐in. butterfly needle attached to a 12‐ml syringe at either the femoral or tibial vein using up to three separate attempts. For each sample, the number of times the needle was redirected (0–5+), the duration of blood collection(s), and the collection volume (target 10 mL) were recorded. Rectal temperature was measured at the time of blood collection. Whole blood samples (≥ 0.3 mL) were immediately loaded into a VCM cartridge and analyzed according to the manufacturer guidelines. The remaining blood sample was immediately partitioned into two to three 1.4 mL plastic tubes containing 3.2% sodium citrate (9:1 ratio; S‐Monovette, SARSTEDT AG & Co KG, Nümbrecht, Germany), gently inverted, and allowed to rest at room temperature for ≥ 30 min. Sodium citrated blood was centrifuged within 1 h for plasma extraction and stored frozen at −80°C. These samples were transported frozen and thawed once for performing TEG and testing of standard clotting parameters. Following patient recovery, medical records were obtained including a standardized medical history form, anesthetic record, and all bloodwork results from the procedure.

VCM Vet and TEG

VCM and TEG parameters are defined in Table 1. VCM analyses were performed immediately following sample collection by a single investigator (LEM). The time elapsed between sample acquisition and initiation of analysis, and the ambient temperature (°F) at the time of blood collection were recorded. VCM results included clot time (CT), clot formation time (CFT), Alpha angle (α), maximum clot formation (MCF), clot amplitude at 10 and 20 min (A10, A20), and clot lysis index at 30 and 45 min (Li30, Li45). The VCM results were graphed by the analyzer as a trace curve over time. Sodium citrate plasma samples were stored at −80°C for up to 1 year before being transported frozen to North Carolina State University for TEG analysis [17, 18, 19]. Citrated plasma samples were thawed in a 37°C water bath and 1.0 mL was combined with 0.04 mL of kaolin activator. Two 0.34 mL aliquots of the activated plasma were recalcified with 0.02 mL of 0.2 M calcium chloride (Haemonetics Corp., Braintree, MA, USA). A single, separate investigator performed all TEG analyses in duplicate (LRC) using a TEG 5000 Thromboelastograph analyzer (Haemonetics Corp., Braintree, MA, USA) per manufacturer instructions. TEG results included split point (SP), reaction time (R), K value, α‐angle, maximum amplitude (MA), and clot lysis at 30 and 60 min (LY30, LY60).

Ancillary Coagulation Testing

Remaining sodium citrated plasma stored at −80°C was then shipped frozen within 1 year to Cornell University for analysis of PT, aPTT, fibrinogen concentration, and D‐dimer concentration (Compact Max, Diagnostica Stago, Parsippany, NJ 07054, USA). Manual and estimated platelet counts (adequate, decreased) were also recorded from the complete blood count (Antech Diagnostics, Fountain Valley, CA 92708, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Sampling data, VCM, TEG, and clotting parameters were assessed for normality using a Shapiro–Wilk test and summarized with descriptive statistics. Data were not normally distributed; numerical variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Coagulation parameters were compared by sex and age class using Wilcoxon rank sum test. Wilcoxon rank sum test was also used to compare results from VCM, TEG, and standard coagulation testing between animals affected by CVD (including hypertension, left ventricular hypertrophy, aortic dilation) or chronic airway disease and those without those morbidities.

Correlation between numerical age and coagulation parameters was assessed by Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Correlation between numerical sampling parameters (time after injection in minutes, duration of collection in seconds, and body temperature at time of sampling ()) and coagulation parameters were also assessed by Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Intra‐assay variability for TEG was assessed by Bland–Altman analysis and Passing‐Bablok Fit analysis (JMP 17; JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, North Carolina 27513, USA). A p‐value ≤ 0.05 was utilized as a measure of statistical significance. VCM and TEG tracings for each individual were evaluated to assess clot formation, firmness, and lysis in comparison to a dog. This was performed by evaluating individual parameters and the tracing as a whole. To assess if the results of the test methods were similar, the clinical interpretation of each (hypercoagulable vs. hypocoagulable to a dog) was then compared to assess subjective similarity between interpretations of VCM and TEG for each individual.

Results

Demographics

Twelve bonobos housed at a single institution were included in the study; no individuals were excluded based on anesthetic stability or the inability to undergo opportunistic sample collection. Individuals were classified as subadult (n = 2; < 10 years), adult (n = 6; 10–25 years), or geriatric (n = 4; > 25 years) [27]. The median age was 20 years (IQR 11.75–31.75 years). The cohort comprised 42% females (n = 5) and 58% males (n = 7). Median weight was 41.5 kg (IQR 39.25–50.25 kg).

At the time of examination, 8 of 12 individuals (67%) had a prior diagnosis of systemic hypertension (median age: 22 years, IQR 14.5–31.75 years) identified on review of their medical history. Systemic hypertension was defined as > 140 mmHg systolic blood pressure in awake, non‐medicated individuals or > 160 mmHg in awake animals receiving anti‐hypertensive medications (Bapodra‐Villaverde, pers. comm., 2025) [28, 29]. Based on the results of echocardiogram and thoracic computed tomography with contrast at the time of examination, left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) was present in 3/12 (25%; median age 32 years, IQR 31–45 years), all of which had concurrent systemic hypertension, and an additional single individual had a prior diagnosis of LVH that had resolved based on echocardiogram. One individual was observed to have concurrent aortic dilation and systemic hypertension (45 years), and another had historical ventricular premature complexes on electrocardiogram (42 years). Chronic airway disease was present in 2 of 12 individuals (17%; IQR 11–42 years). Other reported historical diagnoses included elevated urine protein: creatinine ratio (n = 2; IQR 9–32 years), pulmonary hypertension (n = 1; 9 years with concurrent systemic hypertension), hypothyroidism (n = 1; 42 years), and degenerative joint disease (n = 1; 42 years).

Active prescriptions at the time of the study included primary anti‐hypertensives: lisinopril (n = 8; median 0.31 mg per kilogram [mg/kg] orally every 12 h, IQR 0.25–0.37 mg/kg orally every 12 h), amlodipine (n = 6; median 0.15 mg/kg orally every 24 h, IQR 0.12–0.20 mg/kg orally every 24 h), and chlorthalidone 0.70 mg/kg orally every 24 h (n = 1). Beta‐blocker therapy consisted of carvedilol (n = 3; median 0.30 mg/kg orally every 12 h, IQR 0.17–0.30 mg/kg orally every 12 h). Contraception medications included combined oral contraceptive pills containing 1 mg of northeindrone and 0.035 mg of ethinyl estradiol (n = 2, NORTREL 1/35, Teva Pharmaceuticals Inc., Parsippany, NJ, USA) and progestin‐only birth control pills containing 0.35 mg norethindrone (n = 2, Nor‐Q‐D, Lupin Pharmaceuticals Inc., Naples, FL, USA). Additional medications included prednisolone (n = 2; median 0.25 mg/kg per oral every 24 h, IQR 0.15–0.36 mg/kg/day per oral), cetirizine (n = 2), loratadine (n = 1), levothyroxine (n = 1), and Zylkene supplement (n = 3, Vetoquinol USA, Fort Worth, TX, USA).

VCM Vet and TEG

Whole blood was collected at a median of 29 min after achieving appropriate anesthetic depth to enter the enclosures (IQR 15.25–39 min). Venipuncture required a median of one needle redirection (IQR 1–4 redirects) and was completed over a median duration of 20 s (s) (IQR 16–30 s). A median of 2 min elapsed between sample collection and VCM analysis (range 1–3 min) with an ambient room temperature of 71°F. VCM parameters were quantifiable, as shown in Table 2. There was a statistically significant difference in clot time between sexes, with males having longer clot times than females (p = 0.048; Table 2 and Figure 1). Females contracepted with combination estrogen and progesterone birth control pills had a subjectively higher MCF, A10, and A20 than those on progesterone‐only birth control pills or no contraceptive, though this was not statistically significant (Figure 2). No other significant findings or subjective differences existed pertaining to sex. No significant findings were elicited on the basis of age categories. A significant negative correlation between MCF and increasing numerical age was identified (p = 0.024). There was a statistically significant difference in clot time for individuals medicated for hypertension (n = 8/12) versus normotensive status (p = 0.008), with affected individuals (median 317 s, IQR 300–344 s) having a longer clot time than normotensive individuals (median 252 s, IQR 240–281 s) (Figure 3). No other significant differences were identified between bonobos affected and unaffected by CVD. Bonobos with chronic airway disease had subjectively earlier clot formation, this was not statistically significant. VCM parameters were not significantly affected by sample collection parameters including time after injection, duration of collection, or animal body temperature at time of sampling. Ancillary Coagulation Testing

Standard coagulation values are represented in Table 4. Platelets were assessed as subjectively adequate in 11/12 individuals with the exception of one individual recorded as subjectively decreased (192 × 103 cells/L). There was a statistically significant difference in PTT between sexes (p = 0.048), with a higher PTT in males (median 27 s, IQR 23–28 s) than females (median 20 s, IQR 18–25 s). Fibrinogen was significantly different between sexes (p = 0.048), with females having higher fibrinogen (median 406 mg/dL, IQR 354–478 mg/dL) than males (median 314 mg/dL, IQR 291–369 mg/dL). No statistically significant differences in standard coagulation parameters were elicited by age class or significant correlations by numerical age. Hypertensive individuals had subjectively, but not significantly, higher PTT than normotensive individuals. Antithrombin % was subjectively, but not significantly lower in individuals with hypertension or LVH combined with hypertension. D‐dimers were elevated in one individual with chronic airway disease, but this finding was not statistically significant. Several collection parameters significantly impacted standard coagulation values. Platelet count was significantly positively correlated to rectal temperature at time of sample collection (p = 0.022). PTT was significantly negatively correlated to rectal temperature (p = 0.033). Antithrombin % was significantly positively correlated to rectal temperature (p = 0.005).

Discussion

This study provides proof‐of‐concept for use of VCM Vet in bonobos ( Pan paniscus ) with various medical comorbidities. Similar to previous findings in clinically normal chimpanzees, this cohort of bonobos demonstrated a hypercoagulable profile when compared to dogs [14, 16]. Subjectively, clinical interpretation of individual VCM Vet tracings are comparable to that of plasma TEG tracings, both displaying features consistent with hypercoagulability compared to the domestic dog. Notably, one animal under medical monitoring for ischemic events, exhibited among the most pronounced hypercoagulable profile on both the VCM Vet and TEG tracings, characterized by an earlier clot formation and a wide tracing consistent with a firmer clot. Further studies across juveniles, an expanded adult population, and animals not under treatment for hypertension are warranted as the aforementioned factors are expected to have impacted the results. These findings support the continued investigation of the clinical utility of the VCM Vet as a portable, rapid, and comprehensive tool for evaluating coagulation and fibrinolysis in Pan species.

Although only VCM clot time was statistically significant between sexes, qualitative differences in VCM tracings were evident, with female bonobos displaying a pattern suggestive of increased thrombotic risk when compared to males. Female bonobo VCM tracings were characterized by earlier clot formation, subjectively similar clot strength, and less clot lysis. Females also exhibited a significantly lower PTT and higher fibrinogen concentration. These findings are consistent with recent findings in chimpanzees and humans, where sex‐based differences in coagulation dynamics have been observed [14, 30]. Measures of clot firmness were subjectively higher in VCM tracings of females using a combination birth control pill than those not using hormonal contraception or those on progesterone‐only regimens, although this finding was not statistically significant. In humans, use of combination birth control pills or estrogen hormonal replacement therapies are risk factors for thromboembolic disease which is an important consideration in interpretation of these results [12]. Further investigation is warranted to evaluate if estrogen‐based contraception is associated with a prothrombotic state in bonobos as it is in humans, as well as to assess changes during pregnancy and the postpartum period.

Bonobos in this study with diagnosed hypertension were medicated with a variety of anti‐hypertensive medications and demonstrated hypocoagulable VCM profiles compared to normotensive counterparts. Hypertension is a well‐established risk factor for thromboembolic disease in humans, altering platelet function, the endothelium, and coagulation and fibrinolytic pathways to result in a prothrombotic tendency [9, 30, 31, 32]. Hypertension, as well as aortic dilation, may lead to coagulopathies that can be diagnosed using VCM or TEG. Aortic dissections are of particular concern in male bonobos and gorillas ( Pan paniscus, Gorilla gorilla ) [3, 4, 6]. Hypertension is also hypothesized to contribute to fibrosing cardiomyopathy in these species, although the underlying pathogenesis is likely multi‐factorial [3, 4, 6]. Future comparative studies using VCM and TEG in gorillas and orangutans are warranted to advance our understanding of species‐specific risk factors for hypertension, ischemic events, and aortic dissection.

Most bonobos in this study were managed on lisinopril and/or amlodipine, which modify the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system to normalize blood pressure and may also result in hypocoagulation [31]. Although there were no unmedicated hypertensive bonobos available for comparison, the observed VCM profiles support the potential utility of viscoelastic coagulation testing as a tool for monitoring anticoagulant status during antihypertensive management. Monitoring VCM tracings in a medicated individual and comparing these to an age‐matched normotensive conspecific may be a useful tool to assess for changes in coagulation while utilizing these medications for bonobos in managed care. Interestingly, VCM tracings of individuals with LVH or aortic dilation appear similar to non‐affected individuals. All individuals in this study with LVH or aortic dilation were hypertensive, while not all hypertensive individuals had cardiac structural changes. Two normotensive individuals in this study with chronic airway disease exhibited VCM tracings with earlier clot formation than those without. Both individuals are managed on prednisolone, a corticosteroid known to promote a prothrombotic state in humans [33]. In human medicine, conditions such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and cystic fibrosis are associated with hypercoagulability, driven by the systemic inflammation and elevated levels of procoagulant factors [34, 35, 36, 37]. One of two bonobos exhibited elevated D‐dimers, a biomarker under investigation in humans for acute exacerbations of the aforementioned chronic respiratory diseases and related inflammatory states [37, 38, 39]. Viscoelastic coagulation testing, in combination with D‐dimer elevations, should be evaluated for possible broader utility in the monitoring of coagulation changes that may occur in these chronic respiratory diseases that also affect great apes.

The present study had contrasting findings to a recent study in chimpanzees, which reported a significant association between increasing age classes and multiple VCM parameters, suggesting age confers a hypercoagulable state [14]. Older bonobos in this study exhibited progressively hypocoagulable profiles. This is hypothesized to be confounded by antihypertensive treatment, as the majority of individuals, particularly the oldest, were receiving multiple anti‐hypertensive medications. Given that in humans and chimpanzees, aging is generally associated with a prothrombotic state, the present findings were unexpected and may be due to sample size, or reflect pharmacologic modulation rather than an intrinsic age‐related change [14]. In chimpanzees, decreasing rectal temperature under anesthesia conferred a prothrombotic state [14]. This finding was not as significant in the present study, with only one parameter representative of fibrinolysis on the TEG tracing being significantly correlated to decreasing rectal temperature. This difference may be attributed to the smaller sample size or that appropriate rectal temperatures were maintained at the time of sampling. Nevertheless, the finding in chimpanzees underscores the importance of vigilant temperature monitoring under anesthesia, especially for animals already at higher risk of thromboembolic events [14].

Due to the low number of animals included in the study and the high prevalence of hypertension and other comorbidities among individuals in this study, the reported values should not be interpreted as species‐specific reference intervals. A limited number of bonobos exist under human care, thus data were collected opportunistically from a single institution, constraining statistical power. To date, no studies exist that compare whole blood TEG to plasma TEG in Pan species, which would be pertinent to develop a true reference interval. The effect of anesthesia on clotting parameters has not been previously assessed in this species. Where statistically significant findings were detected, they were supported by visual interpretation of VCM tracing to reinforce confidence in the significance. Clinical interpretations of VCM tracings were made subjectively, then compared to dog and chimpanzee reference intervals, since reference intervals do not exist in this species. Despite existing limitations, this study was successful in generating a comprehensive coagulation assessment for twelve managed‐care bonobos, and provided insight into how various disease states may influence hemostasis parameters in this species.

The management of CVD and hypertension is a critical component of health care for great ape populations under human care. The VCM Vet device proved to be portable, simple to use, and capable of generating tracings across a troop of bonobos. With the ability to provide more comprehensive information than traditional coagulation testing, the use of viscoelastic coagulation testing is worth continued investigation in managed care settings, particularly for individuals with hypertension. In settings where VCM technology is not available, TEG performed on frozen–thawed citrated plasma appears to represent a viable alternative for monitoring chronic conditions, but cannot be accurately directly compared to whole blood TEG. Routine incorporation of viscoelastic coagulation testing during health assessments may facilitate earlier detection of coagulation abnormalities, enable timely medical intervention, and potentially reduce the risk of thromboembolic complications in managed great apes.
