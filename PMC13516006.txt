ABSTRACT
The Singapore Paediatric Life Support Guidelines 2026 updates the 2021 guidelines and provides evidence-based, consensus recommendations for life support in paediatric patients (<18 years of age), excluding newborns. We reviewed treatment recommendations from the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation Paediatric Life Support Task Force as well as resuscitation guidelines from the American Heart Association, the European Resuscitation Council and other relevant studies and reviews published between January 2021 and January 2026 to inform these paediatric life support guidelines. This clinical practice guideline adopts an evidence-based and patient-centred approach to the life support of paediatric patients in Singapore. Recommendations were updated following deliberation of peer-reviewed evidence updates on paediatric resuscitation, with consideration of local context, resources and clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

For the 2026 Singapore Paediatric Life Support Guidelines, we reviewed the 2025 International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation Pediatric Life Support Task Force treatment recommendations, guidelines from the American Heart Association (AHA) and the European Resuscitation Council, and relevant English-language studies published between January 2021 and January 2026 on PubMed.[1234] These updated guidelines integrate current peer-reviewed evidence with local resources and clinical practices.

Definitions

In this guideline, a neonate is defined as being aged <28 days of life (corrected age for preterm infants), while an infant as being ≤1 year. A child is defined as being aged >1 year to 12 years, and an adolescent as being >12 years or showing signs of puberty.

Scope of guidelines

Unless otherwise stated, the term ‘paediatrics’ in these recommendations refers to patients aged <18 years.

Neonates (<28 days)

It is reasonable for neonates (<28 days), excluding newborns, who have fully transitioned from fetal to postnatal circulation after delivery and have been discharged to the community from neonatal units or nurseries (and are subsequently managed in paediatric units), to follow paediatric basic life support (BLS) and advanced life support (ALS) algorithms. All neonates managed in neonatal units, regardless of postnatal age, can be resuscitated following the newborn life support guidelines.

Adolescents

Basic life support for community first responders: It is reasonable for adolescents aged >12 years (or with signs of puberty) to be resuscitated using adult BLS algorithms.[5] For community first responders who are able and willing, we strongly advocate that ventilation should be provided as part of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), even if the casualty is aged >12 years, due to observed improved outcomes.[6]

Advanced paediatric life support: It is reasonable for all adolescents managed in paediatric emergency departments and paediatric units to be resuscitated using paediatric ALS algorithms.

In general emergency departments, non-paediatric wards and adult critical care units, it is reasonable to use adult ALS algorithms for adolescents aged ≥16 years.

LOCAL EPIDEMIOLOGY

The Pan-Asian Resuscitation Outcomes Study reported local paediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (pOHCA) data from 2010 to 2021. The cohort was young, with a median age of 3 years, and the highest incidence was observed in infants.[7] Bystander CPR rate was 64.5%, with 40.8% provided by family members. Survival to discharge was 11.1%, and 8% had favourable neurological outcomes (Cerebral Performance Category 1–2). Despite significant progress in recent years, further initiatives are required to strengthen community engagement and improve pOHCA outcomes.

CHAIN OF SURVIVAL

The 2026 guidelines unify emergency care through a single six-link ‘Chain of Survival’ for all cardiac arrests regardless of age or setting [Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Appendix]. This streamlined approach acknowledges that while paediatric arrests often stem from respiratory failure, high-quality CPR and rapid defibrillation remain universally critical. To prioritise ‘neurologically intact survival’, the framework incorporates paediatric-specific considerations and recommends a systematic review of each link—and the transitions between them—to drive continuous improvement.[48]

PREVENTION, EARLY RECOGNITION AND INTERVENTION FOR CRITICALLY ILL PAEDIATRIC PATIENTS

Prevention

We emphasise preventive strategies and early recognition of pre-cardiac arrest states. Injury prevention and preventive medical strategies (e.g. vaccination and equitable access to healthcare services) may be among the most cost-effective measures to reduce morbidity and mortality in the paediatric population.

Early recognition and intervention

Early identification of critically ill or injured paediatric patients enables prompt initiation of life support to improve outcomes.

This ensures timely emergency medical services (EMS) activation and provides access to dispatcher-assisted CPR instructions. Early bystander intervention is crucial for providing optimal life support and minimising delays or interruptions to resuscitation during transport to the hospital. Management of foreign body airway obstruction

Early recognition and management of foreign body airway obstruction (FBAO) in infants and children can prevent significant morbidity and mortality. Rescuers should call for help and activate EMS immediately for conscious paediatric patients with complete FBAO. For infants, this is managed with back blows followed by chest thrusts using the heel of one palm till the foreign body is expelled or till they become unconscious. In conscious children and adolescents with complete FBAO, the updated recommendation is to start with five back blows and, if not effective, perform five abdominal thrusts. This was updated due to reported concerns of increased incidence of harm in paediatric patients when abdominal thrusts were performed.[9] A recent observational study supported this finding and reported back blows to be more effective in children and adolescents.[10] Figures S2 and S3 [Supplemental Digital Appendix] show the management of FBAO in conscious infants and children.

In all unconscious paediatric FBAO casualties, we strongly recommend that trained and able rescuers open the airway to remove visible foreign bodies and attempt to provide ventilations [Figure S4, Supplemental Digital Appendix]. The use of foreign body airway extraction/suction devices for paediatric patients remains controversial, as the evidence is limited to a small case series.[11] We currently do not advocate for their routine use until more robust paediatric evidence is available.

HIGH-QUALITY BASIC PAEDIATRIC LIFE SUPPORT

Components of high-quality paediatric BLS

High-quality chest compressions consist of five main components: (1) maintaining an adequate compression depth; (2) maintaining a compression rate of 100–120/min; (3) minimising interruptions to reduce hands-off time; (4) allowing for full chest recoil between compressions; and (5) providing age-appropriate ventilation while avoiding hyperventilation. Owing to the importance of ventilation in paediatric life support, all community first responders and healthcare providers who are trained and able should provide ventilations as part of CPR for all paediatric age groups.[6]

Early activation of emergency systems

Early activation of the emergency response system or internal medical emergency teams serves as a critical bridge between BLS and ALS. The activation of EMS facilitates the provision of dispatcher-assisted CPR instructions and the mobilisation of community first responders to deploy the nearest automated external defibrillator (AED). Responders should maintain minimally interrupted chest compressions as guided by dispatchers via mobile phones. If phone access is unavailable, a single rescuer should perform 2 min of CPR before a brief departure to seek assistance, and return immediately to resume resuscitation.

Airway opening and ventilation

Opening the airway is a critical first step in evaluating ‘signs of life’, as it can restore normal breathing and can be lifesaving. In unconscious infants and young children, airway obstruction is common due to their relatively large heads and tongues. While potentially challenging for community first responders, opening the airway is highly encouraged for trained healthcare providers, as it may restore breathing without further interventions.

Recognition of cardiac arrest and pulse check

Pulse checks are unreliable indicators of a perfusing rhythm.[12] We recommend that only healthcare providers perform pulse checks, limiting the assessment to <10 s and conducting them simultaneously with breathing assessment. For in-hospital resuscitation, teams may also palpate for the femoral pulse or perform cardiac auscultation. Adjuncts to identify the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), such as quantitative capnography, bedside ultrasound (operator-dependent), pulse oximetry, or pre-existing invasive arterial monitoring can be used. These tools must support, rather than delay or interfere with, high-quality CPR.

Chest compression technique

The two-thumb encircling hands method for infant chest compression is advocated for all rescuers, as it provides better compressions.[24] Full recoil is emphasised. If the rescuer is unable to encircle the infant’s chest, the heel of one hand can be used to deliver chest compressions.

Paediatric chest compression depth targets

We recommend a relative chest compression depth target of approximately one-third chest depth for all paediatric age groups. If dual-sensor CPR feedback devices are available, absolute depths of 3–4 cm in infants, 4–5 cm in children and 4–6 cm in adolescents aged >12 years should be targeted. If unsure, it is recommended to ‘push-hard, push-fast with full recoil’.

Ventilation as an essential part of paediatric BLS

Ventilation is emphasised in paediatric cardiac arrest. However, it is important that hyperventilation is avoided. If rescuers are trained and able, ventilations should be provided as part of CPR to paediatric cardiac arrest victims. If rescuers are unable to perform ventilations, high-quality, uninterrupted chest compressions should be performed until help arrives.

Chest compressions and ventilation ratios

Assisted ventilation with simultaneous chest compressions would result in air being expelled from the lungs or gastric insufflation if there is no advanced airway in situ. Given the importance of ventilation in paediatric cardiac arrest, compressions and ventilations should be delivered synchronously in the ratio 15:2 or 30:2 based on training until advanced airway placement. Healthcare rescuers who are unfamiliar with the paediatric CPR sequence of the 15:2 ratio should at least perform the standard 30:2 ratio. Community rescuers who are able and trained in paediatric BLS can also use the 15:2 sequence for infants and children.

Public access defibrillators

Community first responders should follow a common BLS algorithm, including the immediate use of AEDs when available. For children aged ≤8 years or weighing <25 kg, paediatric dose-attenuating systems such as paediatric pads are recommended. However, if these are unavailable, standard AED pads should be used; the benefit of defibrillation significantly outweighs the risk of high-energy doses.

For infants aged >1 month, attenuated AEDs are preferred if a manual defibrillator is unavailable. In their absence, a standard AED is still recommended for shockable rhythms, as case reports confirm the safety and life-saving potential of doses exceeding 4 J/kg.[12345] Early defibrillation remains the only effective treatment for pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT) and ventricular fibrillation (VF). However, the use of AED in neonates are not recommended due to the rarity of shockable rhythms and insufficient validation on the accuracy of AED rhythm detection in this age group.

We advocate that schools and public facilities be equipped with AEDs that have both standard and paediatric pads to ensure readiness for younger patients. A summary of paediatric life support across age groups is shown in Table S2 [Supplemental Digital Appendix].

ADVANCED PAEDIATRIC LIFE SUPPORT

General approach to paediatric arrhythmias

Tachyarrhythmias

Assessment of the haemodynamic status of patients is crucial in the management of tachyarrhythmias. For stable paediatric supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) where vagal manoeuvres have failed, the updated intravenous adenosine protocol follows a three-step escalation: an initial dose of 0.1 mg/kg (maximum 6 mg), followed by 0.2 mg/kg (maximum 12 mg) and a final dose of 0.3 mg/kg (maximum 18 mg) if SVT persists.[4] Figures 1 and 2 outline the management algorithms for stable and unstable tachycardia, respectively.

Bradyarrhythmias

Non-cardiogenic causes of bradyarrhythmias should be considered when assessing paediatric patients. In patients with asymptomatic bradycardia, consider the clinical context and non-cardiogenic causes. Haemodynamic compromise with bradycardia may precede cardiac arrest in some patients. Chest compressions with ventilations should be initiated in bradycardia with haemodynamic compromise (as per non-shockable rhythms). Hypoxia may result in bradycardia, especially in infants. Adrenaline as part of ‘symptomatic’ bradycardia management should be used only after ensuring effective ventilation.[13] The management algorithm for bradycardia is outlined in Figure 3.

ALS in paediatric cardiorespiratory arrest

Ventilation considerations in cardiac arrest after advanced airway placement

Hypo- and hyperventilation should be avoided in paediatric cardiac arrest. We recommend ventilation rates at the lower age-specific normal respiratory ranges, which are similar to suggested ventilation rates for paediatric respiratory insufficiency with a perfusing rhythm and pulse rate of >60/min (rescue breathing).

After advanced airway placement, it is reasonable to asynchronously ventilate infants and children undergoing chest compressions at the following rates: infant: 30 breaths/min (one breath every 2 s); child (aged >1–12 years): 20 breaths/min (one breath every 3 s); and adolescents (aged >12 years): 10–12 breaths/min (one breath every 5–6 s, or ten compressions to one breath). Rates exceeding these recommendations may compromise haemodynamics.

Rescue breathing and assisted ventilation (healthcare providers) for respiratory insufficiency with perfusing rhythm and a palpable pulse >60/min are recommended at the following rates: infant: 30 breaths/min (one breath every 2 s); child (aged >1–12 years): 20 breaths/min (one breath every 3 s); and adolescents (aged >12 years): 12 breaths/min (one breath every 5 s).

Supraglottic airway devices in pre-hospital setting

Effective airway management is fundamental in paediatric resuscitation, as most paediatric cardiac arrests are due to respiratory causes. A recent meta-analysis reported that advanced airways did not demonstrate benefits over bag-valve-mask (BVM) ventilation.[14] It is reasonable to use BVM ventilation as the initial management, especially if transport time is short. However, if there are issues with ventilation of collapsed patients, a supraglottic airway device may be used in conjunction with BVM.

Laryngoscopy for intra-arrest paediatric endotracheal intubation

Despite lacking paediatric intra-arrest clinical data, indirect evidence favours video laryngoscopy over direct laryngoscopy.[1516] Simulation studies demonstrated improved first-pass success and shorter intubation times, potentially reducing compression interruptions, while non-arrest controlled trials reported fewer oesophageal intubations in infants.[1516] Consequently, we suggest prioritising video laryngoscopy for paediatric arrests in settings where resources and trained operators are available.

Management of paediatric cardiac arrest with non-shockable rhythms

While ensuring minimally interrupted high-quality BLS, we recommend that adrenaline be given as early as possible once vascular access is established in paediatric cardiac arrest, especially for those with non-shockable rhythms. Following which, adrenaline is recommended to be given every 3–5 min as needed, with regular evaluation and management of reversible causes.

An updated multicentre observational study (secondary analysis) did not report improved functional survival outcome with more frequent-than-recommended dosing (despite an earlier report by the same authors suggesting otherwise).[1718]

Specific considerations for the use of adrenaline include post-initiation of extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR). The use of adrenaline boluses in this context should be restricted based on observational studies in both adult and paediatric populations. However, it is emphasised that standard advanced resuscitation should proceed till ECPR initiation.[1920]

Cardiac arrest management: reversible/contributory causes (5 Hs and 5 Ts)

We have updated the Hs and Ts to reflect a more comprehensive need for active review and management in all critically ill and collapsed paediatric patients. The updated Hs and Ts are as follows: 5 Hs – Hypoxia, Hypovolaemia, Hydrogen ion (acidosis), Hypoglycaemia, and Hypo- or hyperkalaemia, calcium and magnesium; 5 Ts – Tension pneumothorax, Thrombosis (pulmonary/coronary), Tamponade (cardiac), Temperature (hyper- and hypothermia) and Toxin. ‘Trauma’ has been removed, as it is often a composite of other causes, particularly hypovolaemia, tension pneumothorax and cardiac tamponade. Besides potassium, we have included calcium and magnesium as electrolytes that can cause life-threatening arrhythmias. In the Singapore context, environment or exertional heat stroke and sepsis as causes of ‘hyperthermic’ cardiac arrest would need time-sensitive and specific management, such as body cooling for heat stroke and antibiotics for sepsis after ROSC.

We recommend that all reversible causes be evaluated for all paediatric cardiac arrest patients and assessed post-ROSC to identify potential risks of cardiac arrest recurrence.

Management of hyperkalaemia in paediatric cardiac arrest

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis questioned the role of intravenous calcium in the management of hyperkalaemia in paediatric cardiac arrest.[21] The review included one paediatric observational study, which was assessed to be at high risk of bias.[22] It was previously postulated and recently disputed that intravenous calcium provides membrane stabilisation effects on the myocardium in severe hyperkalaemia.[2122] However, updated experimental studies alluded that calcium may therapeutically mitigate the myocardial effects of QRS prolongation in severe hyperkalaemia instead.[23]

We share the approach adopted by the AHA paediatric advanced life support guidelines, which indicate that ‘lack of evidence may not negate potential therapeutic advantage’.[3] The recommended initial management of hyperkalaemia in paediatric cardiac arrest remains unchanged from previous recommendations and should be based in current institutional practice. This includes the use of intravenous calcium, as well as initial management with agents that promote transcellular potassium shifts and elimination.

Other cardiac arrest medications

Sodium bicarbonate is not recommended for routine use during paediatric cardiac arrest.[1345] However, it remains indicated for specific cardiac arrest situations, such as hyperkalaemia associated with severe metabolic acidosis or cardiotoxicity caused by sodium channel blocker overdose (e.g. tricyclic antidepressants).[35] The resuscitation algorithm for paediatric cardiac arrest is shown in Figure 4.

Energy dose for defibrillation

Current recommendations for defibrillation energy doses remain unchanged. An updated meta-analysis found no therapeutic advantage of using initial doses of approximately 2 J/kg compared with higher doses for paediatric shockable rhythms.[24] Given this equivocal evidence, an initial dose of 2–4 J/kg is considered reasonable. Units trained using 4 J/kg for initial defibrillation should continue this practice. For refractory shockable rhythms, it is reasonable to consider using a dose higher than 4 J/kg, not exceeding 10 J/kg, or the adult maximum dose (360 J).

Anti-arrhythmic medications

For shock-refractory VF or pVT, either amiodarone or lignocaine may be used.[345] Amiodarone (5 mg/kg; maximum 300 mg) can be administered via intravenous/intraosseous routes in refractory VF or pVT at the third and every alternate shock thereafter (up to a maximum of three doses).

Lignocaine may be considered an alternative to amiodarone in refractory VF. If lignocaine is used, an initial bolus of 1 mg/kg (maximum 100 mg) may be given at the third or fifth shock followed by a maintenance infusion of 20–50 mcg/kg/min. If there is a delay between the initial bolus and the start of the infusion by more than 15 min, another bolus of 1 mg/kg may be given. Clinicians should choose the drug that they are familiar with and for which they have knowledge of the drug’s side effects.

Special circumstances

In patients with known pre-existing prolonged QT interval (congenital or acquired) presenting with shockable rhythms, avoid amiodarone to prevent further QT prolongation.[25] Priority should be given to early defibrillation and intravenous magnesium, particularly for polymorphic VT (Torsades de Pointes). Early consultation with cardiology and intensive care specialists is essential.

In toxicology-induced cardiac arrest, a multidisciplinary approach involving clinical toxicologists is essential. While amiodarone poses theoretical risks in certain toxicological shockable rhythms, clinicians should generally follow standard resuscitation algorithms unless specific contraindications are present. These efforts should be supplemented by targeted antidotes based on specific toxins and early consideration of ECPR in appropriate scenarios.[345]

ECPR for paediatric in-hospital cardiac arrest

Extracorporeal CPR may be considered for paediatric in-hospital cardiac arrest in centres with established extracorporeal membrane oxygenation expertise and the specialised resources required to manage the transition during and after resuscitation.[3452627] There is currently insufficient evidence to support recommendations for the routine use of ECPR in pOHCA.[5] Table S3 [Supplemental Digital Appendix] summarises the recommendations for paediatric ALS.

Intra-arrest prognostic factors

Scientific evidence on the use of bedside ultrasound or end-tidal carbon dioxide to prognosticate outcomes in paediatric cardiac arrest is limited.[2829] Their use as prognostic tools during paediatric cardiac arrest are considered investigational.[29] An observational paediatric study found an association between achieving diastolic blood pressure (DBP) targets and improved survival to hospital discharge.[28] Based on this finding, it is reasonable to consider using DBP to guide resuscitative efforts for paediatric patients with pre-existing invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring during cardiac arrest. The suggested DBP targets are >25 mmHg for infants, >30 mmHg for children aged >1–12 years, and >35 mmHg for children aged >12 years.[3031]

POST-ARREST CARE IN PAEDIATRICS

Post-ROSC ventilation rates and PO2/PCO2 targets

After ROSC, concerns about hyper- or hypoxaemia and hyper- or hypocarbia remain.[1345] Once sustained ROSC has been achieved, we suggest using initial ventilation rates that target age-appropriate normo-ventilation: 30 breaths/min for infants, 20 breaths/min for children aged >1–12 years, and 12 breaths/min for adolescents aged >12 years.

In the absence of specific patient data and condition, it is reasonable to target normoxaemia and normocarbia (partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide 35–45 mmHg) after ROSC. After achieving ROSC, it is reasonable to use pulse oximetry. Clinicians should aim for an oxygen saturation of between 94% and 98%. Alternatively, they should target values deemed appropriate for patients with underlying chronic cardiac or pulmonary diseases.

Blood pressure targets, fluid management and vasoactive therapy after ROSC

Recent clinical evidence suggests that maintaining higher than previously accepted blood pressure thresholds (10th instead of 5th centile for systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure) was associated with improved outcomes.[32] We recommend maintaining a normal blood pressure (optimally at these higher haemodynamic targets after ROSC) through fluid management or vasoactive agents as required [Table 1].

Temperature management after cardiac arrest

We continue to recommend that normothermia (36–37.5°C) be maintained in comatose children after cardiac arrest. Hyperthermia should be prevented and actively managed.[1345]

Seizures after cardiac arrest

All seizures should be managed expediently after cardiac arrest. However, routine prophylactic anti-epileptics are not recommended.[34]

REHABILITATION AND FOLLOW-UP

Paediatric cardiac arrest survivors and their families require structured follow-up beyond hospitalisation. Paediatric cardiac arrest care should prioritise patient- and family-centric outcomes to ensure that resuscitation guidelines target meaningful recovery.[8] Leveraging the plasticity of the paediatric brain, rehabilitation should benefit not only those with severe impairments, but also survivors with mild-to-moderate sequelae. A primary challenge remains the lack of robust longitudinal measurements to evaluate the long-term impact of new interventions. Comprehensive psychological support is vital. This emotional and social follow-up should ideally be extended to all survivors, their families and the families of non-survivors to ensure holistic care.

SYSTEMS REVIEW

Improving paediatric cardiac arrest survival and neurological outcomes requires an integrated system of care. As paediatric arrests often stem from non-cardiac causes, a comprehensive approach spanning the community to the hospital is essential.

In the community, survival depends on rapid bystander intervention. Advocacy for widespread training—ideally starting in schools—is vital, as public CPR significantly improves outcomes. This must be supported by specialised EMS training and dispatcher-assisted CPR. To maintain standards, EMS systems should conduct regular audits and maintain out-of-hospital registries to inform research priorities and paediatric resource allocations.

Similar to the pre-hospital environment, collecting data through in-hospital cardiac arrest registries is vital for audits and initiatives led by hospital resuscitation committees.[33] Structured quality improvement frameworks such as the ‘Ten Steps Toward Improving In-Hospital Cardiac Arrest Quality of Care and Outcomes’ may provide guidance.[34]

Seamless collaboration between pre- and in-hospital teams and among different hospitals strengthens the entire system of care for paediatric patients. By coordinating these system-level interventions, which include enhanced training, vigilant quality monitoring and refined emergency response protocols, clinical outcomes for paediatric cardiac arrest patients can be improved further.

Team training and debriefing

Team training and simulation can help improve team dynamics, clinical workflows and review setting-specific needs. These systemic tools serve as stress tests to identify latent risks and prevent skill decay. Integrating structured ‘hot’ (immediate) or ‘cold’ (delayed) debriefings can help convert clinical and training experiences into data-driven improvements. Combined with regular protocol updates, these strategies can help improve team readiness and enhance resuscitation performance and patient outcomes.[35]
