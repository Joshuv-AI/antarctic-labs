ABSTRACT
Lossy plasmas seeded by rapidly-desorbed neutrals in the near-target region limit the driver-target
power coupling efficiency of the Z Pulsed Power Facility. Despite the importance of understanding
neutral desorption for accurate power flow modeling, the associated low densities (1015-1016 cm-3) and
rapid timescales (<50 ns) have thus far prevented its measurement. In this work, we establish the
efficacy of single-shot femtosecond laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (fs-LIBS) to detect
desorption-relevant gases at 1014 cm-3 densities on ns timescales and develop accompanying deep
learning analysis and a forward modelling capabilities. However, our attempts to apply the diagnostic
to neutral desorption measurements of a steel wire driven by a 30 kV discharge were unsuccessful. We
found that Airy rings surrounding the primary probe beam are sufficiently strong to induce interfering
solid-state LIBS signals from the wire at the probe standoff distances required to capture desorbed
neutrals prior to detector saturation from background wire plasma emission.

1. INTRODUCTION
Measuring 33 m in diameter, the Z Pulsed Power Facility is the world's largest pulsed power physics
driver. It spatially and temporally compresses energy discharged from a ring of Marx capacitor banks
to deliver a 100 ns electrical pulse to a cm-scale physics experiment, applying up to 80 TW or 30 MA
to the target [1]. Sandia employs Z for a wide range of national security and fundamental science
research, enabling scientists to study inertial confinement fusion, radiation effects science, and material
behavior in extreme environments.
Pictured in cross-section in Figure 1-1 and Figure 1-2, Z employs a final power feed architecture that
comprises a stack of four outer radial magnetically insulated transmission lines (MITLs) that are
combined into a single radial inner MITL through by a post-hole convolute (PHC). The MV/cm
electric fields and MA/cm current densities generated in the PHC and inner MITL are sufficient to
source electrode plasmas that shunt MA-scale currents from the load.

 Figure 1-1. Cross-sectional view of the cylindrical Z pulsed power facility showing pulse
 progression to the target.

 13

Figure 1-2. Cross-sectional view of the cylindrical Z pulsed power facility with zoomed view of the
 load region.

Figure 1-3 illustrates the underlying current loss mechanisms in a simplified inner MITL. The electric
field strips electrons from the cathode that, once insulated by the magnetic field induced by the current
pulse, E×B drift towards the load. The current rapidly heats the anode and cathode surfaces, thermally
desorbing neutral particles that are subsequently ionized. The resulting ions and electrons form a
plasma that shunts current from the load by transporting charged particles across the gap. A key
component of this process is the desorption of neutral particles, as these seed the plasmas that enable
current loss at levels far greater than what would be achieved by space-charge-limited electron
emission alone. Accurate modeling of Z power transmission requires a detailed description of the
thermal desorption physics [2].

 Figure 1-3. Schematic illustrating current loss physics in a simplified inner MITL.

 14

However, despite its importance, pulsed power-driven thermal desorption remains poorly understood.
Spectroscopic measurements of pulsed power electrode gaps indicate that the neutrals likely primarily
comprise H, O, and C [3, 4], but their precise molecular compositions, inventories, and desorption
rates are not known [2]. Neutral desorption has thus far eluded quantification because its measurement
is difficult. A diagnostic must be able to quantify monolayer-scale gas densities (~1015-1016 cm-3) on
ns timescales in harsh pulsed power environments. A recent effort developed a novel four-wave
mixing technique to measure H atom in these conditions but only managed detection from foils that
were pre-loaded with large quantities of H2 gas [5].
In this report, we develop femtosecond laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (fs-LIBS) for single-
shot measurements of low-density gases and detail our attempt to apply the diagnostic to pulsed power
desorption measurements. We establish fs-LIBS as a diagnostic capable of detecting 1014 cm-3 gases
on a single-shot basis in Ch. 2 and demonstrate the utility of deep learning to interpret the resulting
spectra in Ch. 3. Chapter 4 details a forward model that can be used to extract species densities from
low-density fs-LIBS measurements and Ch. 5 provides temporally-resolved electron density
measurements used to verify and improve the model’s fidelity.
Finally, Ch. 6 discusses our efforts to apply fs-LIBS to measure neutral desorption from a pulsed
power-driven stainless steel (SS) wire. We conclude that, though fs-LIBS has the sensitivity to detect
neutral desorption in pulsed power environments, issues regarding laser-wire interactions stemming
from the Airy rings surrounding the primary probe beam make it very challenging to detect the neutrals
prior to the onset of overwhelming background wire plasma emission. fs-LIBS has the potential to
provide the first measurement of pulsed power neutral desorption, but its implementation will require
significant efforts to carefully control the Airy pattern of the probe beam and to delay the onset of
overwhelming background wire emission.

2. SINGLE-SHOT FEMTOSECOND LASER-INDUCED BREAKDOWN
 SPECTROSCOPY IN LOW-DENSITY GASES
Reprinted from J. Appl. Phys. 138, 123107 (2025) (accessible at doi.org/10.1063/5.0283646) [6] with
permission granted by the Creative Commons CC BY license under which the article was originally
published. Any references made to the information in this chapter should directly cite the journal
publication.

2.1. Introduction
Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) ionizes a medium with a focused laser beam and
collects the resulting emission spectrum to investigate the medium’s composition [7]. Application of
the technique to solid-state matter is widespread and notably serves as the basis of the Mars Curiosity
Rover’s ChemCam instrument [8]. LIBS is also employed in gaseous media, where its primary
applications are for combustion engine diagnostics [9] and trace gas contaminant detection [10]. In
these implementations, LIBS is generally used to investigate gases at atmospheric-scale pressures and
may serve as a complement to other techniques such as coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS)
[11], laser absorption spectroscopy (LAS) [12], and laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) [13].
First applied by Maker [14] in the early 1960s shortly after the advent of the laser, gas-phase LIBS
employs an intense laser beam to ionize and dissociate a gaseous medium. Loree and Radziemski [15,
16] demonstrated the potential for using the resulting spectrum to determine the gas composition, and
Phuoc and White [17] initiated interest in the combustion community by applying LIBS to remotely
quantify the fuel-to-air ratio in CH₄–air mixtures.
Most implementations employ a ns laser, whose beam initially generates a small amount of multi-
photon ionization (MPI) and subsequently employs the MPI-generated electrons to initiate an
ionization cascade. Kotzagianni and Couris [18] demonstrated the efficacy of instead employing a fs
laser for combustion applications, which effectively eliminates the ionization cascade and relies entirely
on a combination of MPI and tunnel ionization. Hsu et al. [19] demonstrated enhanced stability and
lower variance in fs-LIBS compared to ns-LIBS, and Zhang et al. [20] showed that fs laser-induced
filamentation can be leveraged to provide spatial resolution.
A particularly challenging gas diagnostic problem is the measurement of surface contaminants that are
thermally desorbed from pulsed power electrodes, which requires the detection of 1015–10¹6 cm⁻3
gases containing H₂, H₂O, CO₂, and various hydrocarbons with ns resolution in low-repetition
experiments [3]. Pulsed power accelerators compress electrical energy in time and space to drive loads
relevant to fusion, radiation, and material physics [1]. Accurate modeling of their load power coupling
requires a detailed understanding of electrode contaminant composition and desorption rate [2]. To
date, as these parameters remain unmeasured, order-of-magnitude discrepancies exist in the assumed
contaminant inventory. Many optical diagnostics lack the necessary single-shot sensitivity at these
densities, but the high signal-to-noise ratio of LIBS gives it the potential to perform the measurement.
Additionally, LIBS offers the advantage of detecting multiple species in a single-shot configuration,
compared to species-specific techniques.
Though several studies have investigated the effect of pressure on the LIBS spectral signature [21-25],
measurements of the LIBS spectral response below 1016 cm⁻3 are lacking. Motivated by the need for
a single-shot, low-density gas diagnostic for pulsed power electrode desorption measurements, fs-
LIBS measurements of 0.5 × 1015–9.1 × 1015 cm⁻3 of H₂, H₂O, CH₄, and CO₂ were performed to
evaluate the diagnostic’s single-shot detection sensitivity. Strong signal above noise was observed for

 17

each species, indicating that fs-LIBS is a viable candidate for desorption diagnostics and may generally
be applicable to low-density gases that require rapid characterization.
In this paper, the experimental setup is detailed, collected spectral data are analyzed for each species,
notable spectral features observed from carbon-containing species are discussed, and evaluation of
shot-to-shot variability and single-shot performance is assessed. Finally, the collection and analysis of
spectra taken in gas mixtures of CH₄–CO₂ and CH₄–H₂ provides a first-order evaluation of the
feasibility of extracting individual gas densities from composite signals.

2.2. Experimental Setup
The experimental schematic of the fs-LIBS setup is shown in Figure 2-1. The set-up comprised a six-
port stainless steel cube with conflat flanges to form the vacuum cell for testing. The test cell employed
50.8 mm ultraviolet fused silica (UVFS) windows for laser input and output and LIBS spectrum
collection. An absolute 1-Torr full-range capacitance pressure transducer with a 0.25% reading
accuracy (MKS Baratron 626C01TLE) was mounted on the top flange to monitor the pressure in the
cell. Vacuum was maintained by a turbo-molecular pump (Pfeiffer TPU 450 H) backed by a dry scroll
pump (Agilent IDP-15). Three independent flow controllers (Sierra Instruments C100L-DD-2-OV1-
SV1-PV2-V1-S0) with a range of 0–1000 sccm with an accuracy of 1% of full scale were used to
control the pressures of the test gases of H₂, CH₄, and CO₂ independently. To generate water vapor
to flow through the vacuum cell, a water bubbler (Chemglass Life Sciences Bubbler) was filled with
distilled water and exposed to vacuum to generate a pure flow of water vapor through the vacuum
cell. As water boils at approximately 17.5 Torr [26] and 20 °C, the sub-Torr testing environment
ensured that enough water boiled to generate a pure flow of water through the cell.

 Figure 2-1. Experimental setup of the fs laser with the vacuum cell and optical collection.

The setup includes a fs laser (Spectra Physics Spitfire Ace) which produces a 10 mJ, 50 fs pulse at a
wavelength of 800 nm with a pulse repetition rate of 1 kHz. The laser output was passed through a
zero-order half-waveplate, λ/2, and was then expanded by a 1.5× telescope to a beam diameter of
approximately 27 mm, which allowed for a tighter focus in the vacuum cell and reduced optical damage
to the windows.

To generate the LIBS signal, a spherical singlet plano-convex lens with a 75 mm focal length was used
to focus the beam through the viewport into the gas interrogation region. The remaining laser light
was transmitted through a third viewport and terminated into a beam dump outside of the vacuum
cell. The resulting LIBS spectrum was collected at a 90° angle with a 75 mm focal length achromatic
collimating lens (Edmund Optics #49-389). The signal was routed through a periscope comprising
two 3-inch UV-enhanced aluminum mirrors, which directed the light through a 200 mm focal length
lens onto the 300 μm-wide slit of the spectrometer (Princeton Instruments IsoPlane 320) with a 150
grooves/mm grating with a 500 nm blaze wavelength.
To acquire higher resolution spectra, the spectrometer was operated in the fourth order to increase
the spectral dispersion. In addition, a Princeton Instruments SpectraPro HRS-500 spectrometer with
a 1800 grooves/mm grating blazed at 500 nm and a slit width of 50 μm was used for increased spectral
resolution to deconvolve the complicated measured CO2 LIBS spectrum from the instrument
broadening. The spectrometer wavelength was calibrated using Ar and Hg calibration light sources
(Princeton Instruments Intellical). Additionally, a 400 nm longpass filter was employed when
collecting spectral signatures above 600 nm to remove higher-order interference from the grating in
the spectrometer. No intensity correction factors were taken for either spectrometer used in this study,
and all spectra presented represent raw spectra obtained from the camera.
The LIBS signal was captured using an intensified charge coupled device (ICCD) camera (Princeton
Instruments PM4-1024-HB-FG-18-P43-CM with a Gen III HBf intensifier) with a 500 ns camera gate
width. A long camera gate width of 500 ns was chosen to capture any long optical decay emission
from potential atomic recombination, but shorter time gates showed that the majority of the signal
was observed in the first 20 ns after the laser pulse. Nevertheless, 500 ns was used for all conditions
measured. The camera trigger was synchronized with the arrival of the fs pulse to the vacuum cell with
a delay generator (Stanford Research DG645) and photodiode detector (ThorLabs DET10A2) placed
near the vacuum cell. The camera was synchronized so that the camera gate turned on approximately
1 ns prior to the laser entering the vacuum cell. No laser scatter was measured on the camera.
The sensitivity of the LIBS signal with respect to the input laser polarization was varied in
9.9 × 1015 cm⁻3, 300 mTorr, of H₂. The polarization was varied between three polarizations: vertical,
45°, and horizontal with respect to the optical table. The highest signal collected was with the laser
polarization set to horizontal. In relative terms, the signal strengths collected for the different laser
polarizations were 1, 0.85, and 0.55 for horizontal, vertical, and 45°, respectively. For all of the
measurements presented here, the laser polarization was set to horizontal.

2.3. Spectral Modeling and Data Analysis
Spectral analysis of the collected data was performed using Sandia’s spectral physics environment for
advanced remote sensing, SPEARS, code [27]. The analysis for atomic spectra was performed with
spectral information compiled from the Kurucz spectral line database [28], and analysis of molecular
spectra was performed from linelists compiled from ExoMol [29].
To generate calibration curves of intensity versus species number density, a Voigt lineshape was used
to fit the LIBS spectral features using the curve_fit call from SciPy [30]. The peak intensity was
determined by fitting the Voigt lineshape. This was done to help reduce shot-to-shot intensity
fluctuations in the LIBS signal. The Voigt lineshape fit was not performed to fit the pressure
broadening component of the Voigt lineshape, as the spectral lineshapes were dominated by the
instrument broadening function of the spectrometer. The Voigt lineshape was calculated by taking the
real part of the Faddeeva function using the wofz function in SciPy. The fit was performed by floating
the offset, peak intensity, and the Gaussian and Lorentzian widths of the Voigt lineshape.
To determine the individual species densities from a mixture spectrum, i.e., spectrum from a gas
mixture, a representative single-gas spectrum of each gas was selected and normalized to set the peak
intensity to unity. The normalized spectrum for each gas constituent was then used in a minimization
routine using SciPy’s curve_fit function to determine each species’ intensity contribution to the
overall experimentally measured mixture spectrum using Eq. (2-2). The residual, 𝑒⃗, was calculated by
taking the experimental mixture spectrum, 𝑆⃗, and subtracting the normalized spectrum for each gas
constituent, 𝑥⃗ and 𝑦⃗, with scalar intensity variables, 𝑎 and 𝑏. This assumes that the spectral signals
from the gas constituents are additive in nature and that the radicals generated by the laser do not
interact dramatically with the bath gas. Once the constituent intensity variables, 𝑎 and 𝑏, are fit, the
predicted number density is determined by taking the normalized spectrum multiplied by the
constituent intensity variables, i.e., 𝑎𝑥⃗, and processing it, as previously discussed, by fitting the Voigt
lineshape routine or numerically integrating the spectrum,

                                            𝑒⃗ = 𝑆⃗ − 𝑎𝑥⃗ − 𝑏𝑦⃗.                                      (2-2)

To compare predicted individual gas species to the experimental gas mixture composition relies on
the volumetric flow rates of each gas and the total measured pressure in the vacuum cell to determine
the number density of each constituent gas. The partial pressure of each gas is assumed to be directly
proportional to the partial volumetric flow rate of each gas controlled by the flowmeters. Thus, the
partial pressure was determined by the ratio of the species flow rate divided by the total flow rate
multiplied by the measured pressure of the composition.

2.5. Conclusion
Single-shot fs-LIBS was implemented to measure CH₄, H₂, H₂O, and CO₂ at densities relevant to
pulsed power electrode desorption. Single-shot data were acquired in a vacuum cell for number
densities ranging from approximately 1014 to approximately 1016 cm⁻3. Strong signals above baseline
noise were observed in single-gas measurements for each of the four test gases at and below densities
of 1015 cm⁻3. The correlation between spectral signatures and number densities were determined for
each gas.
Both atomic and molecular spectral emission were observed for multiple test gases. The CH radical
was measured at each density for CH₄ along with atomic hydrogen lines. CO₂ had a complex spectral
signature both in terms of changing spectral features with number density and complexity of the
spectral features measured from the LIBS kernel. All other species measured had consistent spectral
features throughout the density range investigated.

 29

To study the capability of using the calibration curves obtained from single-gas mixtures, dual-gas
mixture systems were evaluated. First, a mixture of CH₄–CO₂ enabled evaluation of the ability to
separate the complex spectral response of CO₂ from CH₄. The average difference between predicted
number density from the LIBS signal to the experimental species densities across all three test cases
was within 30%. A second gas mixture of CH₄–H₂ showed much better agreement, with an average
difference between predicted number densities and the experimental number densities of 22%, likely
due to the simpler spectral response of the mixture. Current measurement resolution indicates the
feasibility of discriminating contamination density desorbed from the electrodes to help inform
electrode surface contamination modeling [2]. Further investigations into LIBS spectral signatures of
mixtures in low-density gases should help determine the applicability of determining individual gas
densities at these conditions and reduce uncertainty in predicting individual species.

3.1. Introduction
Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) is an active optical diagnostic that employs a focused
laser pulse to interrogate a target medium. Ionization of the target by the laser produces a plasma
emission spectrum that provides both qualitative and quantitative information about the constituent
target species [7].
The most widely-applied LIBS implementation probes solid-state matter for applications ranging from
manufacturing quality control [36] to Martian geomchemistry [8], for which researchers have
developed a large breadth of traditional and machine learning-based chemometric techniques [37, 38].
Meanwhile, a growing body of work, primarily interested in combustion physics, has employed LIBS
to extract species concentrations from gas-phase targets [15, 17, 18, 39]. Investigators have developed
techniques for interpreting gas spectra that include peak interpolation [17], direct spectrum matching
[40], simple neural networks [41], and deep learning (DL) [42].
Recent work [6] demonstrated that fs-LIBS can detect and identify low-density (1014 cm−3) gases on a
single-shot basis, indicating that the diagnostic is a viable candidate for measuring ns-scale electrode
desorption in pulsed power fusion drivers [1]. This is an exciting expansion of the LIBS application
envelope, as electrode desorption is a critical pulsed power phenomenon whose short timescales and
low densities have thus far precluded its quantification [2, 3]. However, the low-density LIBS
demonstration also identified challenges associated with quantifying individual species contributions
from single-shot measurements of low-density gas mixtures: electronic species interactions and altered
quenching rates impact the resulting spectra, and the absence of spectral averaging exacerbates the
influence of noise.
DL models are well-suited for this kind of complex spectral analysis, as they can identify important
features and use them to recognize crucial patterns [43]. In this Letter, we present a convolutional
neural network (CNN) and employ it to analyze single-shot low-density mixed-gas fs-LIBS spectra.
We detail the experimental conditions and associated analysis difficulties, specify the CNN structure
and Bayesian hyperparameter optimization, and demonstrate the CNN's utility to make accurate
inferences in a challenging environment.

3.2. Experimental Setup and Data
We employed a fs laser to ionize gas mixtures inside a vacuum test cell and collected the resulting
single-shot spectra 90° to the laser propagation direction with a spectrometer and gated intensified
charge-coupled device (iCCD). The setup, pictured schematically in Figure 3-1, is a variation of a
system described in more detail elsewhere [6] (cf. Sec. 2.2). In this effort, we employed a larger vacuum
chamber and placed the laser focusing and spectrum collection optics inside the gas cell. We sourced
50 fs laser pulses characterized by an energy and center wavelength of 9.2 mJ and 800 nm, respectively,
with a Spectra Physics Spitfire Ace at a rate of 1 kHz. We horizontally-polarized the beam with a half-
wave plate (λ/2), expanded it with an inverted telescope, and then focused it into the interrogation
volume with a 40 mm focal length spherical singlet plano-convex lens. We collimated the emission
spectra with an 80 mm focal length achromatic doublet and subsequently focused it onto the 170 μm

 31

spectrometer slit with a 180 mm focal length achromatic doublet. We employed an IsoPlane 320
spectrometer with a 150 grooves/mm, 500 nm blaze grating and digitally collected each single-shot
spectrum for 150 ns after the laser pulse arrival with a 2048 × 2048 Princeton Instruments PM4-2048-
F-SR-FG-25-P43-SM iCCD. We converted the 2D images into 1D spectra by binning the intensity at
each wavelength.

3.3. Deep Learning Model and Application to Data
We developed a CNN to interpret these spectra by applying multiple, separate Bayesian optimizations
on different candidate network structures with different hyperparameter selections and ranges. The
highest-performing CNN is pictured schematically in Figure 3-3. Its input is an 𝑚 × 2048 array of 𝑚
spectra. The input spectra are normalized by the peak value observed across all 6400 collected spectra
to improve the numerical stability of the network training, but they are otherwise unprocessed. The
CNN comprises two sequential convolution blocks, each followed by a max pooling layer, and then
features a third convolution block whose output is flattened and fed into a single fully-connected
hidden layer that outputs the predicted H2 and CH4 flow rates. Each convolution block comprises a
convolutional layer, normalization layer, and rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation layer in sequence.
Each convolutional layer features a stride of 1 and a tunable filter size (𝑠) and number (𝑛). The

3.4. Conclusion
Our findings indicate that DL models can play an important role in gas-phase LIBS analysis and may
be a key technique for interpreting single-shot pulsed power electrode desorption measurements.
Even in the challenging data set presented here, a CNN is able to extract relevant features and
determine important patterns to make accurate predictions without expert guidance. Future work will
include extending the application of DL to data fusion to enable researchers to combine calibration
experiments with spectral models for interpreting electrode desorption measurements.

4.1. Introduction
Gas-phase laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) is a form of gas-phase atomic emission
spectroscopy, first formalized in 1981 by Loree and Radziemski, in which the spectroscopic medium
is a laser-induced plasma (LIP) [15, 16]. Gas-phase LIBS has historically been deployed with ns lasers
to quantify species concentrations in atmospheric and super-atmospheric environments, such as
combustion engine diagnostics [39]. In addition to these successes at high pressures, recent work has
shown that femtosecond pulse width LIBS (fs-LIBS) has a single-shot detection floor on the order of
1014 cm-3, proving it has applicability at much lower gas densities [6].
This diagnostic capability is significant for investigation of the lossy electrode plasmas found in pulsed-
power drivers. The 80-TW Z Machine operated by Sandia National Laboratories sees a MA-scale
current loss from plasma formation in the power-delivery electrodes. These plasmas are seeded by
neutral gases that are thermally desorbed from electrode surfaces. The density uncertainty of these
neutral gases spans two orders of magnitude [2], limiting confidence in predictive power-flow models
and increasing risk in future driver development.
Constraining this density uncertainty with in-situ fs-LIBS measurements requires an accurate forward
model relating neutral gas and laser parameters to spectral emission intensity. Subsequent statistical
inversion of the forward model and measured spectra would enable accurate deduction of the
desorbed gases and construction of multidimensional uncertainty bounds.
Conventional plasma modeling for LIBS relies on the assumption of local thermodynamic equilibrium
(LTE). Under this assumption, ionization states and energy level populations are determined
statistically to follow a Saha-Boltzmann distribution [39]. The validity of LTE requires sufficiently high
electron densities (𝑛𝑒 ), where energy transfer from electron-impact collisions significantly outweighs
energy loss from radiation [51]. A necessary (but not sufficient) condition to describe LTE is the
McWhirter criterion,

                                  𝑛𝑒 ≥ 1.6 × 1012 √𝑇𝑒 (Δ𝐸max )3 cm−3                                 (4-1)
where 𝑇𝑒 is the electron temperature and Δ𝐸max is the largest energy separation between adjacent
energy levels for a given species. This parameter describes the minimum electron number density at
which LTE may be valid.
In low-density gases, collisional frequency decreases, which increases the characteristic time for
thermalization. When the thermalization timescale exceeds the evolution timescale of the plasma, the
LTE assumption breaks down. Under these non-equilibrium (nLTE) conditions, Saha-Boltzmann
distributions are not accurate representations of energy level populations, and chemical kinetic solvers
yield unphysical predictions of species concentrations. Therefore, to accurately model spectral
emissions of these low-density gases, we require a high-intensity LIP chemistry model and a
collisional-radiative model.
Here, we present a physics-based forward model – conceptually described in Fig. 4-1 – of the spectral
emissions of low-density LIPs. This model follows a two-stage sequential architecture driven by the
physical hierarchy of low-density plasmas. Excited state populations remain orders of magnitude
smaller than ground state populations, meaning that excitation and radiative decay have a negligible

 37

effect on electron densities and temperatures. This weak feedback allows for the decoupling of the
LIP chemistry model and the collisional-radiative model, greatly simplifying the computational
methodology. To validate this framework, the model is applied to simulate experimental fs-LIBS
plasma conditions reported by Jans et al. [6].

4.3.3. Spectral Synthesis
To enable direct quantitative comparison with experimental data, discrete transition intensities
computed by the collisional-radiative solver are converted into continuous synthetic spectra and scaled
to absolute photon counts. Volumetric emission power is integrated over a collection window
following a gating delay after the laser pulse. Furthermore, collection efficiencies are calculated based
on known configuration parameters and then scaled based on a single spectral feature.
To model line shape, each discrete emission line is convolved with a normalized Voigt profile,
representing the convolution of a Gaussian distribution and a Lorentzian distribution. The Gaussian
width accounts for Doppler broadening and the instrument response function, while the Lorentzian
width incorporates Stark broadening. Although these parameters could be anchored using calculated
electron densities and gas temperatures, fitting these widths as free parameters decouples line shapes
from the underlying kinetics, preventing any discrepancies during validation.

further by accounting for continuum radiation in this forward model.

4.5. Conclusions
A two-stage, physics-based forward model was created for low-density hydrogen plasmas produced
in fs-LIBS. A laser-induced plasma chemistry model was formed by unifying PPT ionization theory
with RRKM breakdown modeling, scaled Coulomb explosion rate equations, and electron-impact
ionizations. This model outputs a state vector that was then integrated into a coronal collisional-
radiative model. Within this framework, the model captures charge generation, electron temperatures,
and thermal electron-impact kinetics. Incorporating escape-factor corrections for VUV photon
generation effectively accounted for H-𝛽/H-𝛼 relative intensity that would be overpredicted under
low-density conditions.
Validation of this model was performed against published experimental fs-LIBS data from Jans et al.
[6] across a pressure domain of 15.4 to 121 mTorr. Scaling this model to a single absolute collection
efficiency at an intermediate pressure reference reproduced total photon yields and spectral line shape
across all tested pressure conditions, with error margins landing within ±20%. Observed deviations
from experimental data could likely be accounted for with a more robust instrumentation mapping
and continuum radiation modeling.
The completed forward model for hydrogen establishes a computationally efficient, physically verified
baseline for low-density, ultrafast plasma diagnostics. Future work on this framework would be on the
expansion of the model’s applications to other species. This model’s kinetic framework was
intentionally constructed to accommodate polyatomic species, with methane (CH4 ) serving as a
primary target. Validation of this solver against experimental data for methane, as well as methane-
hydrogen mixtures, would confirm the robustness of the model for future Bayesian inversion

 47

techniques. Ultimately, this work provides the foundational model necessary for integration into future
Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) frameworks, enabling quantification of unknown gas
parameters in in-situ fs-LIBS measurements.

5.1. Introduction
While Ch. 2 was largely focused on the spectral response of the density of gases to single-shot fs-
LIBS, this chapter is focused on the underlying physics of the freed electrons from the laser pulse for
validation of the modeling framework developed in Ch. 4. Due to the intense fs-laser pulse, plasma is

5.5. Conclusion
Time-resolved fs-LIBS measurements were performed in low-density H2 and CH4 to characterize the
electron density and gas temperature evolution of the laser-induced plasma and to provide validation
data for the forward model. Fits of the H-α lineshape in H2 showed that the laser-generated plasma
exhibits measurable Stark broadening and Doppler broadening over tens of ns following excitation by
the fs pulse. These measurements enabled inference of both electron number density and gas
temperature as functions of time and pressure. The observed decay of the H-α linewidth with
increasing time after the laser pulse arrival indicates rapid plasma relaxation.
In CH4, the H-α feature exhibited more complex temporal behavior, including pronounced Stark
splitting at gate delays greater than 10 ns. Fits to the split H-α components enabled inference of both
electron number density and electric field, while independent fitting of the CH(A-X) molecular
emission provided a complementary gas temperature measurement. The electron densities measured

 56

in CH4 were larger than those measured in H2 and did not decay as rapidly. Additionally, in contrast
to the H2 data, we observed the electron density to decrease with increased gas pressure. Comparison
of temperatures inferred from atomic H Doppler broadening and CH spectral fitting indicate early-
time thermal nonequilibrium between H and CH, followed by closer agreement after approximately
30 ns as collisional relaxation proceeded. Taken together, our efforts demonstrate the feasibility of
measuring the electron density and gas temperature in the laser-induced plasmas relevant to low-
density fs-LIBS and provide valuable data useful for anchoring our forward model.

6. ATTEMPTED APPLICATION OF SINGLE-SHOT FEMTOSECOND
 LASER-INDUCED BREAKDOWN SPECTROSCOPY TO MEASURE
 PULSED POWER DESORPTION

6.1. Introduction
As discussed in Chs. 2-5, we demonstrated the capability of single-shot fs-LIBS to detect pulsed power
desorption-relevant gases and developed corresponding analysis and modeling techniques to interpret
the data. In this chapter, we report on our efforts to apply the diagnostic to measure desorption from
pulsed power electrodes. We employed a small pulsed power device to drive stainless steel (SS) wire
targets and applied fs-LIBS to attempt measurements of the neutrals desorbed from their surfaces.
Section 6.2 details our experimental setup, Sec. 6.3 describes our model of the experimental setup,
Sec. 6.4 provides the results of our experimental investigation, and Sec. 6.5 provides a discussion of
our experimental data in the context of our model.
Though we managed to consistently generate LIBS signals above background electrode emission, we
ultimately concluded that these signals were corrupted by unexpected interactions between the
probing laser pulse and the wire. Specifically, we observed the laser to induce solid-state LIBS in the
wire even at apparent probe standoff distances of 100s of microns and suspect that the particles
liberated from the wire interacted with the wire plasma to enhance the LIBS signal above the
background. We hypothesize that the solid-state LIBS was induced by weak Airy rings surrounding
the central laser probe and that this effect severely limits the positioning of the laser probe. For our
experimental setup, the probe standoff distance required to eliminate direct laser-wire interactions
appears to preclude measurements of the desorbed contaminants prior to the release of overwhelming
continuum background emission from the wire.

6.2. Experimental Setup
We installed a pulsed power device in an optics laboratory to couple it with the LIBS equipment

6.3. Heating, Desorption, and Diffusion Model
Motivated by the need to estimate wire heating as a function of time and to interpret LIBS-measured
species densities in the context of a desorption rate, we developed a 1D model of our experiment and

MHD model that estimates the wire surface temperature as a function of time, 2) an Arrhenius model
that employs the surface temperature to predict the neutral desorption, and 3) a vacuum diffusion
model that tracks the propagation of the desorbed neutrals and determines the density measurable by
an ideal spherical laser beam as a function of space and time.

6.3.3. Vacuum Diffusion Model
The vacuum diffusion model uses the output of the desorption model to determine the species
number density that accumulates inside an assumed laser interrogation spot geometry at a specific
interrogation time. The model assumes that the desorbed particles travel through vacuum in 1D at an
average velocity commensurate with their mass (𝑚) and the wire surface temperature at the time of
their release, given by

                                             𝑣𝑎𝑣 = √𝑇𝑘𝐵 /𝑚 ,                                    (6-14)

and uses that velocity to compute the average radius of each desorbed particle wave (corresponding
to each timestep in the desorption model) at the interrogation time.
The desorption waves, which are cylindrical sheets provided in terms of an areal density by the
desorption model, are first re-represented in terms of absolute particle numbers by multiplying the
areal density by the wire surface area. Assuming 1D motion away from the wire (such that the distance
between particles changes along the circumference but not the height of the cylindrical sheet), the
model computes the fraction of each particle wave present inside the 3D laser interrogation spot and
uses those fractions to sum the total number of particles within the spot at the given interrogation
time. It then divides that number by the spot volume to determine the volumetric number density
observed by the LIBS measurement.
The model assumes an idealized spherical interrogation spot. Intending to make adjustments upon the
identification of other species, we initially assumed H2-only desorption and consequently employed a
particle mass of 3.347×10-27 kg.

6.4. Experimental Results
We used LIBS to attempt measurements of the particle density of neutrals desorbed from various
wires. Though we attempted measurements across a range of charge voltages and wire diameters, we
limit the results here to 30 kV charge and 500 and 290 µm wires. We observed smaller charge voltages
to dramatically increase the timing jitter and larger charge voltages to damage Castor components.
Our model predicted that wires with diameters much larger than 500 µm would exhibit insufficient
heating, and we experimentally observed wires with diameters smaller than 290 µm to exhibit
inconsistent weld quality and consequently high background signal variability.
The experiments are identified by their shot number. Every shot on Castor (including timing and test
shots) is assigned a number, so gaps between shot numbers do not necessarily indicate significant gaps
in time between shots. All experiments presented here were performed within one week of each other.
The Castor current waveforms, though similar, are not identical and so we were unable to perform a
direct shot-to-shot timing comparison via a common reference point (such as the peak current or the
50% rise time). Instead, we smoothed and normalized the current waveforms and then compared their
relative timing differences at discrete amplitudes ranging 0.25 to 0.75 in increments of 0.01 on the
initial current rise. We averaged this timing offset across all the points and then factored in shot-to-
shot drift in the LIBS spectrometer camera gate to compute the overall diagnostic timing shift between
shots. For each wire diameter, we chose an initial reference shot (966 for 500 µm and 1033 for 290 µm)
and plotted all the timings relative to that reference. Positive relative timings correspond to camera
gates that look later in the current pulse, and negative relative timings correspond to camera gates that
look earlier in the current pulse.
We observed significant shot-to-shot Castor current jitter, likely stemming from a failing trigger
amplifier. We compensated for this by taking multiple background and LIBS shots, thereby naturally
capturing a large range of diagnostic timings.

6.5. Discussion
We produced multiple LIBS signals above background with 500 µm wires, but we failed to produce
any LIBS signals above background for we which we also collected pre-shot LIBS signals that did not
contain discernable spectral lines. We did not collect pre-shot measurements before every LIBS
experiment in the 300 µm standoff campaign, but no-pulser LIBS measurements taken before and
after the campaign exhibited discernable spectral lines. We twice collected signal above background in
the 500 µm standoff campaign, but both corresponding pre-shot measurements also exhibited
discernable spectral lines.
Figure 6-29 displays the modeled surface temperature as a function of time for shot 966, and Figure
6-30 and Figure 6-31 plot the modeled number density of desorbed contaminants encountered by the
100 µm diameter laser probe as a function of time for standoff distances of 300 and 500 µm,

around 9550-9600 ns (1014-1015 cm-3, cf. Ch. 2), a time range that is near peak current and therefore
susceptible to large and variable background emission. This timing is pushed well beyond peak current
for a 500 µm standoff.

Our combined experiments and models suggest we failed to detect desorbed species from a pulsed
power device with LIBS due to two competing constraints: the laser probe must be offset sufficiently

 87

far from the wire to avoid interacting with the wire and the probe must be placed sufficiently close to
the wire to detect desorbed species prior to the onset of significant background wire emission. We
were unable to simultaneously satisfy these two constraints, even when implementing a wire that
heated so rapidly that the background emission became significant and variable prior to peak current.
An outstanding question is why the atmospheric low-power LIBS kernel must be offset >500 µm
from the wire to ensure no interaction with the wire during the experiment. We consider three
possibilities: 1) the probe position shifts under vacuum, 2) the LIBS kernel volume grows with
increased laser power, and 3) the intensity contained within the laser spot’s Airy rings is sufficiently
large to interact with the wire.
The probe position shifts under vacuum as a function of the refractive index difference between
atmosphere and vacuum. As a conservative estimate, the refractive index at standard temperature and
pressure (we actually operated at an altitude of more than a mile above sea level) is about 0.03% larger
than that of perfect vacuum (we operated at 0.08 mTorr). A difference this small is not likely to impact
the beam steering and focusing in a discernable manner.
We observed the visible LIBS kernel to grow 100-200 µm in radius in atmosphere when we increased
from alignment power to full shot power. This happens because larger powers generate greater degrees
of ionization at every radius from the probe’s center. However, we expect this effect to be relatively
small at shot pressure. The exploratory low-density LIBS campaign (cf. Ch. 2) demonstrated a
detection floor on the order of 10 mTorr, indicating that ionization of the background chamber gas
should be extremely small at our 0.08 mTorr shot pressure. Furthermore, some fraction of the size of
the atmospheric LIBS kernel is due to fluid mechanics effects that are virtually absent in the rarified
flow regime present under vacuum. We, therefore, expect the kernel growth between alignment power
at atmospheric pressure and full power at shot pressure to actually be negative and to certainly be
insufficient to explain wire interactions at a distance of 500 µm.
Diffraction effects produce an Airy pattern that distributes a focused laser beam’s energy between a
central Airy disk and satellite Airy rings. The large majority of the energy is concentrated in the central
disk, but some fraction is contained within the rings. Supposing just 0.1% of the total pulse energy is
contained within a ring with sufficiently-large radius to interact with the wire, because we employ a
50 fs pulse duration, that ring would still exhibit a peak power >100 MW. LIBS interrogations of solid-
state matter, by contrast, commonly employ ~10 mJ energies contained within ~10 ns pulses,
producing ~1 MW peak power primarily distributed about a central ~5 mm diameter Airy disk [7].
Though we are unable to directly measure the intensity contained within our Airy rings, we can, based
on this comparison, conclude that it is plausible that our rings impart sufficient intensity to produce
single-shot LIBS signals from our wire targets. We, therefore, hypothesize that our LIBS signals above
noise are accidental solid-state LIBS measurements of the wire driven by our probe beam’s Airy rings.
Any future effort to employ LIBS for pulsed power desorption measurements would need to carefully
control the Airy pattern to minimize the laser probe distance from the wire.

6.6. Conclusion
We coupled fs-LIBS to a small pulsed power device and attempted to measure the neutrals desorbed
from SS wires driven by a 30 kV discharge. We consistently observed LIBS signal above background
plasma emission but, with input from pre-shot measurements and 1D simulations of our experiment,
attributed this signal to unexpected laser-wire interactions caused by Airy rings. We conclude that our
experimental setup precludes the measurement of neutral desorption with fs-LIBS. The required
probe standoff distance from the target to eliminate the Airy ring interaction delays the arrival of the

 88

desorbed neutrals into the main probe beyond the time at which background wire plasma emission
saturates the spectrometer. Enabling a fs-LIBS measurement of pulsed power desorption will require
some combination of delaying background emission while maintaining a sufficient target heating rate
and carefully controlling the Airy pattern to enable closer placement of the probe to the target.

7. CONCLUSION
The PHC and inner MITL regions of the Z Pulsed Power Facility experience transmission
inefficiencies caused by lossy plasmas that are seeded by rapidly-desorbed neutral particles. In an effort
to develop a diagnostic capable of measuring pulsed power thermal desorption, we demonstrated the
efficacy of fs-LIBS to make single-shot measurements of relevant low-density gases, constructed
analysis and modeling techniques for interpreting its spectra, and coupled the diagnostic to a pulsed
power device.
We applied fs-LIBS to interrogate flowing CH4, H2, H2O, and CO2 gases at densities below 1015 cm-3
on a single-shot basis in a controlled vacuum cell. We observed strong signal above noise in all four
gases, collecting spectra with both atomic and molecular emission lines, and established strong
correlations between the spectral signatures and gas number densities. Additionally, we explored and
confirmed the tentative feasibility of directly employing single-gas density calibration curves to
interpret mixed gas measurements of CH4-CO2 and CH4-H2.
We developed a CNN and demonstrated its capability to interpret high-variance single-shot fs-LIBS
spectra collected in flowing CH4-H2 mixtures at pressures ranging 80-530 mTorr. Despite strong
overlap between collected spectra separated by input flow rates of 20 sccm, the CNN extracted
relevant spectral features and identified critical patterns to predict the H2 flow rate with an RMSE of
15.9 sccm and the CH4 flow rate with an RMSE of just 12 sccm.
We developed a two-stage, physics-based forward model for low-density hydrogen plasmas produced
in fs-LIBS. We formed an LIP chemistry model by unifying PPT ionization theory with RRKM
breakdown modeling, scaled Coulomb explosion rate equations, and electron-impact ionizations and
used the outputs to inform a coronal collisional-radiative model. Our model captures charge
generation, electron temperatures, and thermal electron-impact kinetics and agrees well with
experimental spectra collected from low-density H2 fs-LIBS measurements.
We performed time-resolved fs-LIBS measurements of low-density H2 and CH4 to quantify the plasma
conditions underlying the spectra and thereby inform the forward model. Lineshape analysis of H-α
enabled inference of the electron density and gas temperature in H2, whereas measurements in CH4
revealed Stark splitting that additionally enabled estimates of the electric field. Independent CH(A-X)
spectra fits provided complementary gas temperature measurements and showed early-time thermal
nonequilibrium between CH and atomic H that relaxed after tens of ns. In CH4, we observed larger
electron densities than in H2, slower density decay times than in H2, and an inverted relationship
between the electron density and gas pressure.
We coupled fs-LIBS to a small pulsed power device and attempted to measure the neutrals desorbed
from SS wires driven by a 30 kV discharge. Though we consistently observed LIBS signal above
background plasma emission, results from pre-shot measurements and 1D simulations of our
experiment indicated that these signals were caused by unexpected laser-wire interactions induced by
Airy rings. We found that the required probe standoff distance from the target to eliminate the Airy
ring interaction delays the arrival of the desorbed neutrals into the main probe beyond the time at
which background wire plasma emission saturates the spectrometer. We were therefore unable to
detect neutral desorption with our experimental setup.
We established the capability of single-shot fs-LIBS to measure gases at densities and timescales
relevant to pulsed power electrode desorption and built some of the tools necessary for interpreting
the spectra. However, our attempt to apply the diagnostic to desorption measurements revealed the
difficulty of positioning the probe close enough to the electrode target to detect desorption while

 91

simultaneously positioning the probe far enough from the target to eliminate Airy ring interactions.
We conclude that fs-LIBS has the potential to detect electrode desorption but that more effort is
required to both reduce Airy ring interactions and to delay the onset of overwhelming background
wire emission.
