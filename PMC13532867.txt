Effects of online motor cortex transcranial ultrasound stimulation on interhemispheric inhibition
Authors: Yanqiu Wang, Ke Zeng, Anton Fomenko, Xue Xia, Mandy Yi Rong Ding, Jean‐François Nankoo, Robert Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractTranscranial ultrasound stimulation (TUS) of the motor cortex (M1) has been found to modulate interhemispheric inhibition (IHI) after sonication (offline effect). However, the effects of M1 TUS on IHI during sonication (online effect) remain unknown. The current study examined the effects of online M1 TUS on IHI from the sonicated M1 to the contralateral M1 and on the contralateral M1 excitability in 15 healthy subjects. IHI was measured with paired‐pulse transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) at 10 ms (short‐latency IHI, SIHI) or 40 ms (long‐latency IHI, LIHI) interstimulus interval (ISI), and the contralateral M1 excitability was measured with single‐pulse TMS. The results showed that online TUS decreased the sonicated M1 to the contralateral M1 at LIHI but not at SIHI, while the contralateral TUS M1 excitability was unchanged. TUS may modulate complex circuits that mediate LIHI. These findings showed that TUS can be used to investigate the functional interactions between the two hemispheres. Key points Offline non‐invasive brain stimulations have been shown to modulate interhemispheric inhibition.The current study examined the effects of online primary motor cortex (M1) transcranial ultrasound stimulation (TUS) on interhemispheric inhibition from the sonicated M1 to the contralateral M1, and the contralateral M1 excitability.TUS decreased long‐ but not short‐latency interhemispheric inhibition from the sonicated M1 to the contralateral M1.TUS could be a tool for investigating functional interactions between the two hemispheres. Keywords: interhemispheric inhibition, motor cortex, non‐invasive brain stimulation, transcranial ultrasound stimulation

Introduction

Interhemispheric inhibition (IHI) refers to the process by which one hemisphere of the brain suppresses activity in the opposite hemisphere (Ferbert, Priori, Rothwell et al. 1992), primarily through connections in the corpus callosum (Perez & Cohen, 2009). This mechanism is crucial for coordinating activity between hemispheres for motor control, sensory processing and cognitive functions (Brodie, Villamayor, Borich et al. 2014; Duque, Murase, Celnik et al. 2007; Palmer, Schulz, Murphy et al. 2012; Puri & Hinder, 2022). There are two distinct phases of IHI, namely short‐latency (SIHI) and long‐latency (LIHI), and they are mediated by different neuronal populations and can be elicited at different interstimulus intervals (ISI) (Ni, Gunraj, Nelson et al. 2009). Previous studies have shown that the GABAB receptor agonist baclofen can increase LIHI, suggesting that LIHI is mediated by postsynaptic GABAB receptors (Irlbacher, Brocke, Mechow et al. 2007). Although the neurotransmitter and receptor involved with SIHI are unknown (Irlbacher, Brocke, Mechow et al. 2007), SIHI and LIHI were found to act through different neural circuits (Chen, Yung and et al. 2003; Ghosh, Mehta, Huang et al. 2013; Ni, Gunraj, Nelson et al. 2009).

Modulation of IHI can help in understanding brain function and in development of therapies for neurological disorders. Previous studies have shown that the offline or plasticity effects of non‐invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) techniques such as transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS), repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and theta‐burst transcranial ultrasound stimulation (tbTUS) can modulate IHI (Lebihan, Mobers, Daley et al. 2025; Pal, Hanajima, Gunraj et al. 2005; Xia, Wang, Zeng et al. 2024). tbTUS and tACS increased IHI, while 15 min of low‐frequency rTMS decreased IHI. Moreover, tbTUS modulated SIHI and LIHI differently, indicating that these two interhemispheric circuits respond distinctly to ultrasound stimulation. While these stimulation protocols are primarily designed to induce offline changes in brain activities and plasticity, studying the effects of online brain stimulation on IHI can offer unique insights into immediate neural responses and changes in connectivity.

Recent studies have highlighted the potential of low‐intensity TUS to transiently modulate cortical excitability with high spatial precision and access to deep brain regions (Legon, Sato, Opitz et al. 2014; Legon, Ai, Bansal et al. 2018; Legon, Bansal, Tyshynsky et al. 2018). Proof‐of‐concept studies in humans have shown that even brief TUS can induce immediate suppressive or facilitatory effects on both cortical and subcortical targets, with alterations in somatosensory‐evoked potentials, motor performance and cortical oscillatory activities (Lee, Chung, Jung et al. 2016; Legon, Sato, Opitz et al. 2014; Mueller, Legon, Opitz et al. 2014). Notably, the impact of online TUS on IHI remains unexplored, despite its capacity to produce focal, time‐locked neural responses. Investigating how online TUS modulates IHI could provide novel insights into interhemispheric communication, offering a unique approach to modulate the excitatory–inhibitory balance between hemispheres.

IHI may involve different neurotransmitters. Some studies have suggested that NIBS modulates communications between two hemispheres (Casula, Pellicciari, Bonnì et al. 2021; Tsutsumi, Hanajima, Terao et al. 2014) through neural circuits that mediate SIHI. Other studies suggested that NIBS affects the excitability of the contralateral hemisphere via GABAB receptors (Palmer, Schulz, Murphy et al. 2012), which mediates LIHI (Irlbacher, Brocke, Mechow et al. 2007; Kukaswadia, Wagle‐Shukla, Morgante et al. 2005). One study found that bifocal tACS over bilateral sensorimotor cortices increased both SIHI and LIHI (Lebihan, Mobers, Daley et al. 2025). The current study examined the online effects of M1 TUS on the sonicated M1 to the contralateral M1 SIHI and LIHI and on the contralateral M1 excitability. Previous studies showed that online M1 TUS decreased M1 excitability (Fomenko and Chen et al. 2020; Legon, Sato, Opitz et al. 2014; Xia, Fomenko, Nankoo et al. 2021), and therefore we hypothesize that online right M1 TUS would decrease right‐to‐left SIHI and LIHI and would not change the left M1 excitability. This work extends previous findings of offline effects of ultrasound on IHI and offers new insight into the effects of online TUS on interconnected brain areas.

Method

Participants

A total of 18 healthy individuals were recruited for the study. Three participants were withdrawn from the study due to a high TMS motor threshold. Handedness information was assessed using the Oldfield Handedness Inventory (Oldfield, 1971). The mean age of the remaining 15 subjects was 27.20 ± 5.55 years (six males, nine females, all right‐handed). Each participant provided written informed consent, and the protocol was approved by the University Health Network (Toronto) Research Ethics Board (REB#20‐5740) and was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki on the use of human subjects in experiments.

Electromyogram recording

Surface electromyograms (EMG) were recorded from the left and right first dorsal interosseous (FDI) muscles with 9 mm diameter Ag–AgCl surface electrodes. The active electrode was placed over the muscle belly, and the reference electrode over the metacarpophalangeal joint of the index finger. The signal was amplified at 1K, band‐pass filtered (2 Hz to 2.5 kHz, Intronix Technologies Corporation Model 2024F, Bolton, Ontario, Canada), digitized at 5 kHz by an analog‐to‐digital interface (Micro1401, Cambridge Electronic Design, Cambridge, UK) and stored in a computer for off‐line analysis.

Ultrasound stimulation of the motor cortex

The transducer used was the same as the one used in some of our previous studies (Fomenko and Chen et al. 2020; Xia, Wang, Zeng et al. 2024; Xia, Fomenko, Nankoo et al. 2021; Zeng, Darmani, Fomenko et al. 2022). Briefly, a custom two‐element annular array ultrasound transducer (H246, Sonic Concepts Inc., Bothell, WA, USA; fundamental frequency: 0.5 MHz; diameter: 38 mm; thickness: 10 mm) and a programmable radio‐frequency amplifier (Transducer Power Output System TPO201‐80, Sonic Concepts Inc.) were used. The sonication depth was set at 30 mm, and the power was set at 20 W, with 1000 Hz pulse repetition frequency, 10% duty cycle and 500 ms duration (Fomenko and Chen et al. 2020).

The parameters of the sham TUS condition were the same as for the real TUS condition. We used an inactive sham instead of a ‘flip‐over’ sham to allow us to randomize blocks of real and sham conditions without the need to manipulate the transducer between blocks.

To mask the slightly audible buzz of the active transducer, a signal generator (Agilent 33220A, Keysight Technologies, Santa Rosa, CA, USA) delivered a tonal waveform of 500 ms in duration to a set of speakers positioned 2 m lateral to the subject's right ear (Braun, Blackmore, Cleveland et al. 2020). The sound was tuned to match the audible frequency (10–15 kHz) of the active transducer until the subject could not distinguish the speaker sound from the 500 ms activation of the transducer. This sound was triggered whenever sham TUS was delivered (Fomenko and Chen et al. 2020).

The ultrasound beam pressure was measured using a fibre‐optic hydrophone (Onda HGL‐0400, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) in a measurement tank (Acertara Acoustic Laboratories, Longmont, CO, USA). The free water values for the depth of 30 mm were 11.73 W/cm2 for spatial peak pulse average intensity (I SPPA), and 1.17 W/cm2 for spatial peak time average intensity (I SPTA).

The estimated I SPPA and I SPTA at the brain target (30 mm) were 2.93 W/cm2 and 0.29 W/cm2, respectively, based on the assumption of 75% attenuation due to the skull (Fomenko and Chen et al. 2020; Mueller et al., 2017; Ennasr, Isaac, Strohman et al. 2024), both of which fall within United States Food and Drug Administration safety standards (Shamli Oghli et al., 2023). Approximately 2 cm of ultrasound gel (Wavelength MP Blue Multi‐Purpose Ultrasound Gel, Sabel Med, Oldsmar, FL, USA) was applied to the transducer before it was placed onto the scalp. The gel layer was visually inspected, and any visible air bubbles were burst using a probe (Shamli Oghli et al., 2023). The transducer was manually placed over the motor hotspot identified by TMS. The lateral and vertical dimensions of the acoustic beam cross‐sections, measured at the full width at half‐maximum intensity, were 5.8 and 5.6 mm, respectively, enabling the TUS to spatially target the motor cortex. The acoustic field in the axial direction, perpendicular to the transducer face, had a near‐field peak at 12 mm and a far‐field peak at 33 mm (close to the focal length of 30 mm) (Zeng, Darmani, Fomenko et al. 2022).

Transcranial magnetic stimulation protocol

A dual‐site paired‐pulse TMS protocol powered by two Magstim 2002 stimulators (Magstim Company, Whitland, UK) was used to test IHI. Two 60 mm figure‐of‐eight‐shaped coils delivered the conditioning stimulus (CS) and the test stimulus (TS). The TS was applied to the left M1 at an intensity to produce ∼1 mV peak‐to‐peak motor‐evoked potential (MEP) amplitude in the right first dorsal interosseous (FDI) muscle (TS1mV), defined as the lowest TS intensity needed to generate peak‐to‐peak MEPs of more than 1 mV in at least 5 out of 10 trials in the right FDI muscle when the muscle was completely relaxed. The coil handle pointed backward at 30–45° from the mid‐sagittal line to induce posterior to anterior current in the brain (Ni, Gunraj, Nelson et al. 2009), and was adjusted such that both coils were over the M1 optimal position. The TUS transducer was coupled beneath a 60 mm figure‐of‐eight TMS coil using a 3D‐printed holder, which delivered the CS to the right M1 (Fomenko and Chen et al. 2020; Xia, Fomenko, Nankoo et al. 2021). The output of the stimulator connected to the CS coil was adjusted to CS1mV, which was the lowest stimulus intensity to generate peak‐to‐peak MEP > 1 mV in at least 5 out of 10 consecutive trials in the left FDI muscle with the coil placed over the motor hot spot to induce current in the lateral to medial direction and with the TUS transducer placed below the TMS coil (Fig. 1B ). A previous study showed that IHI is not influenced by the direction of the CS current (Chen, Yung and et al. 2003).

Experiment 1: baseline SIHI and LIHI

The TUS transducer was coupled to the TMS coil with no ultrasound delivered. The CS was applied 10 ms before the TS for SIHI and 40 ms before the TS for LIHI (Udupa, Ni, Gunraj et al. 2010). Fifteen paired‐pulse trials with 10 ms and 40 ms ISI, and 10 TS‐alone trials were delivered in random order with an intertrial interval of 5 s, totalling 40 trials. No masking sound was used.

Experiment 2: effects of TUS on SIHI and LIHI

Three conditions were studied (Fig. 2). In the real‐TUS condition, 500 ms of TUS was applied with the TMS CS delivered at 490 ms (10 ms before the end of sonication). In the real‐TUS/CSadj condition, TMS CS intensity was adjusted to produce a 1 mV MEP in the presence of active TUS. In the sham‐TUS condition, 0 W TUS with masking sound was used (Fomenko and Chen et al. 2020). The sound was tuned until participants could not tell the difference between real and sham TUS. For each condition, 15 paired‐pulse trials of SIHI, 15 trials of LIHI and 10 TS‐alone trials were delivered in random order with an intertrial interval of 5 s, with 40 trials in total for each condition (Table 1). For the TS‐alone trials, no masking sound was applied. The real‐TUS and the sham‐TUS conditions were tested in counterbalanced order among subjects, followed by the real‐TUS/CSadj condition.

Experiment 3: effects of TUS on contralateral M1 excitability

Active or sham TUS of 500 ms duration was applied to the right M1 and TS (TMS) was applied to the left M1 at the end of the 500 ms sonication (post‐0 TS) or at 30 ms after completion of sonication (post‐30 TS) (Fig. 2). These times were designed to match the times for TS for SIHI and LIHI in Experiment 2. The masking sound was used to mimic the ‘active’ trials in the sham‐TUS condition. No masking sound was used in the real‐TUS condition. For TS‐alone trials, no masking sound was applied. For each condition, 15 post‐0 TS, 15 post‐30 TS and 10 TS‐alone trials were delivered in random order with an intertrial interval of 5 s, with 40 trials in total (Table 1). The real or sham conditions were tested separately, in counterbalanced order, across subjects. Effects of TUS were expressed as ratios of MEP amplitudes recorded in the right FDI muscle in the active or sham TUS conditions compared to TS alone.

Data and statistical analysis

To minimise the potential influence of elevated background EMG on MEP amplitudes, we used a multi‐step procedure to exclude trials with excessive background EMG (Calvert & Carson, 2023). First, we excluded all trials for which any corresponding root mean square value exceeded 2.5 µV. Second, we sought to exclude participants for whom the level of background EMG in the retained recordings was correlated to an unacceptable extent with the corresponding MEP amplitude (see details in Calvert & Carson, 2023). Overall, 95.06% of responses were retained, and no participants were excluded.

IHI was expressed as the ratio of MEP amplitude for the paired‐pulse conditions to the mean MEP amplitude for TS alone. Normal distribution of IHI ratios was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (Carson, 2024). Unless otherwise stated, values are reported as means ± standard deviation. MEP amplitudes were measured peak‐to‐peak.

For Experiment 1, paired‐pulse TMS MEP amplitudes were compared to TS alone for SIHI (10 ms ISI) and LIHI (40 ms ISI) using Student's paired t test. Ratios less than 1 represent inhibition, and ratios greater than 1 represent facilitation.

For Experiment 2, SIHI was compared using repeated measures analysis of variance (RM ANOVA) and LIHI was compared using Friedman's test with test condition (real‐TUS, real‐TUS/CSadj and sham‐TUS) as a within‐subject factor. We also compared MEP amplitudes in the left FDI muscle across the three conditions in Experiment 2 using RM ANOVA. In addition, we compared the MEP amplitudes in the left FDI muscles evoked by the CS on right M1 in the three conditions in Experiment 2 (real‐TUS, real‐TUS/CSadj, sham‐TUS) to left FDI muscle MEP amplitudes in Experiment 1 using three separate paired sample t tests.

In Experiment 3, post‐0 MEP ratio to TS alone and post‐30 MEP ratio to TS alone were compared for real‐TUS and sham‐TUS conditions using two‐tailed paired t tests.

Bonferroni post hoc tests were used when ANOVA showed significant main effects. The Greenhouse–Geisser correction was used to correct for non‐sphericity. SPSS Statistics (v. 27.0) software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Experiment 2

For SIHI, RM ANOVA showed a non‐significant main effect of test condition (real‐TUS, real‐TUS/CSadj and sham‐TUS) (F(2, 28) = 3.391, P = 0.051, η2 p = 0.192). A Bonferroni post hoc test indicated no significant difference in SIHI for real‐TUS compared to real‐TUS/CSadj (P = 0.079, Fig. 3A ) or to sham‐TUS (P = 0.701, Fig. 3A ). For LIHI, Friedman's test revealed a significant effect of condition (χ2(2) = 10.13, P = 0.006, n = 15). The mean ranks indicated that the real‐TUS condition showed the highest values (mean rank = 2.67), followed by the real‐TUS/CSadj condition (1.73) and the sham‐TUS condition (1.60). Bonferroni post hoc comparisons suggested that LIHI was significantly reduced (higher LIHI ratio) in the real‐TUS condition compared to the sham‐TUS condition (P = 0.009, Fig. 3B ). After adjusting the CS intensity to evoke similar MEP amplitudes in the presence of TUS, LIHI was restored in the real‐TUS/CSadj condition and was greater (lower LIHI ratio) compared to the real‐TUS condition (P = 0.011, Fig. 3B ). The effect size (Kendall's W) was 0.34, indicating a moderate effect.

Discussion

We studied the online effects of M1 TUS on IHI. We found that right‐to‐left LIHI was reduced by real right M1 TUS compared to sham M1 TUS. Such changes could be reversed by adjusting the CS TMS intensity to compensate for the effects of real TUS on right M1 (Fig. 3B ). We found no online effect of right M1 TUS on right‐to‐left SIHI. Moreover, left M1 excitability was not affected by right M1 TUS (Fig. 5). By establishing the effects of online ultrasound stimulation on transcallosal inhibition in healthy humans, the present work provides essential mechanistic insights to guide the rational development of ultrasound‐based neuromodulation strategies.

Previous studies have demonstrated that NIBS protocols exert bidirectional modulatory effects on the interhemispheric balance between bilateral M1. Specifically, inhibitory NIBS protocols such as 1 Hz repetitive TMS and transcranial static magnetic stimulation reduced IHI from the stimulated M1 to the contralateral M1 (Pal, Hanajima, Gunraj et al. 2005; Takamatsu, Koganemaru, Watanabe et al. 2021), whereas excitatory protocols tend to facilitate it (Xia, Wang, Zeng et al. 2024). These studies examined the after‐ or offline effects of NIBS. Here, we showed the online inhibitory effect of M1 TUS on IHI from the sonicated M1 to the contralateral M1.

The effect of TUS on IHI supports the inter‐hemispheric competition model. A core feature of this model is the assumption that the increasing excitability of neural circuits in the M1 leads to stronger suppression of the contralateral M1 (Carson, 2020). The sonication parameters used in the current study have been shown to elicit a broad range of online inhibitory effects on cortical and subcortical target areas in human subjects (Ennasr, Isaac, Strohman et al. 2024; Fomenko and Chen et al. 2020; Legon, Ai, Bansal et al. 2018; Legon, Bansal, Tyshynsky et al. 2018; Xia, Fomenko, Nankoo et al. 2021), although some of the findings could be related to auditory effects (Kop, Shamli Oghli et al. 2024). We observed reduced MEP amplitude in the real‐TUS compared with the no‐TUS condition, but there is also a non‐significant reduction in MEP amplitude in the sham‐TUS compared to the no‐TUS condition (Fig. 4), suggesting auditory effects alone could have some effects on M1 excitability (Kop, Shamli Oghli et al. 2024).

Interestingly, although the sham‐TUS condition with masking sound did not show a significant difference in MEP amplitude compared to the real‐TUS condition (Fig. 4), only the real‐TUS condition reduced LIHI (Fig. 3B ). It is known that higher CS intensity elicits stronger IHI (Chen, Yung and et al. 2003; Ni, Gunraj, Nelson et al. 2009). Therefore, a stronger IHI in the realTUS/CSadj condition compared to the real‐TUS condition is an expected result. The finding that LIHI in the realTUS/CSadj and baseline conditions is similar (Fig. 3B ) indicates that reduced LIHI due to TUS can be compensated by increasing CS intensity, and that TUS causes a similar degree of reduction in MEP amplitudes and LIHI.

Similar to previous findings (Pal, Hanajima, Gunraj et al. 2005; Xia, Wang, Zeng et al. 2024), we observed distinct modulatory effects of TUS on SIHI and LIHI. While both SIHI and LIHI decreased with lower CS intensity and MEP amplitude (Chen, Yung and et al. 2003; Ni, Gunraj, Nelson et al. 2009), only LIHI was reduced by TUS. The observation that TUS inhibits LIHI but not SIHI may reflect the involvement of distinct neuronal populations and intracortical circuits underlying these two forms of IHI (Chen, Yung and et al. 2003; Ghosh, Mehta, Huang et al. 2013; Ni, Gunraj, Nelson et al. 2009). Previous studies suggest that SIHI is primarily mediated by direct transcallosal projections between right and left M1. Our finding that TUS did not change SIHI is consistent with the finding that it did not alter contralateral M1 excitability (Xia, Fomenko, Nankoo et al. 2021). In contrast, LIHI may involve indirect contributions from premotor areas modulating activity via more complex intracortical networks (Hinder, Puri, Kemp et al. 2018; Ni, Gunraj, Nelson et al. 2009; Puri, St George et al. 2023). Moreover, a pharmacological study suggested that LIHI is mediated by postsynaptic GABAB receptors, whereas the transmitter system mediating SIHI remains inconclusive (Irlbacher, Brocke, Mechow et al. 2007). Therefore, decreased LIHI following right M1 TUS may be related to suppression of complex cortical networks involving GABAB receptors that mediate LIHI.

Increasing CS intensity is known to increase LIHI and SIHI with CS intensity up to ∼75% of stimulator output (Chen, Yung and et al. 2003; Ni, Gunraj, Nelson et al. 2009). However, our results showed that higher CS intensity in the real‐TUS/CSadj condition increased LIHI but not SIHI relative to the real TUS condition (Fig. 3). This may be because, in the real‐TUS condition, LIHI was weaker (MEP ratio ∼1.0) than SIHI (MEP ratio ∼0.6), making LIHI more susceptible to modulation by higher TS intensities.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not measure the unsonicated M1 to sonicated M1 IHI, which could provide a more comprehensive understanding of how TUS modulates the balance between the two hemispheres. Second, we did not test motor behaviour during sonication. A previous study reported a reduction in motor reaction time during TUS (Fomenko and Chen et al. 2020). Future study could explore how online TUS affects bimanual movement control via IHI. Thirdly, we used white noise to mask the auditory effect of TUS, but a masking sound at the same pulse repetition frequency of TUS may be more effective (Braun, Blackmore, Cleveland et al. 2020). The sample size is relatively small, resulting in low statistical power for some findings. We used a TMS hotspot to locate the M1 and did not use neuronavigation to monitor the sonication or TMS locations, which may have increased the variability of MEP. We used the same ultrasound intensity at the maximum transducer level for all subjects, but the pressure in M1 would vary from subject to subject due to differences in skull thickness and geometry. This may have increased the variability of the results. In addition, we tested only one sonication parameter, and other parameters may produce different results. In terms of MEP estimates, as they were not always normally distributed, a normality test before choosing an appropriate statistical method is recommended. For those measurements that violated normality assumptions, data transformation with the aim of altering the characteristics of the distribution, or using robust statistics (Calvert & Carson, 2023; Carson, 2026), could be adopted in future studies.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that online M1 TUS decreased the sonicated M1 to the contralateral M1 LIHI but not SIHI. The modulation effect may be related to suppression of a complex cortical network that mediates LIHI, which involved GABAB receptors, but was not due to changes in contralateral M1 excitability. These findings show that ultrasound neuromodulation could be a potential tool for understanding the functional interactions between brain regions and could serve as a treatment for neurological and psychiatric disorders.
