Improving Appropriate Telemetry Utilization

Continuous cardiac monitoring, or telemetry, has been a cornerstone of inpatient care since its introduction in the 1960s. Although its value in the intensive care setting is well established, its expansion into noncritical care settings has frequently occurred without standardized protocols to guide initiation or discontinuation.1,2 This has contributed to widespread overutilization, placing significant financial burdens on health care systems and generating alarm fatigue among nursing and clinical staff, without a corresponding improvement in patient safety or outcomes.3,4

To address these concerns, the American Heart Association (AHA) published its 2017 Practice Standards for Electrocardiographic Monitoring in Hospital Settings, providing a clear, evidence-based class of recommendation system to guide appropriate use of continuous electrocardiogram monitoring.5 Despite this framework, studies have consistently demonstrated that adherence to these guidelines remains suboptimal.

At our institution, a teaching hospital with internal medicine and family medicine residency programs, telemetry was perceived to be overused, particularly in terms of monitoring duration. This quality improvement project was built on the basis that a structured educational intervention could bridge the gap between established guidelines and bedside practice. The primary aim was to reduce the average duration of telemetry use by at least 15% over a 3-month postintervention period. A secondary aim was to improve physician knowledge of the 2017 AHA telemetry guidelines, as assessed by preintervention and postintervention surveys.

Protect Rationale

Overuse of telemetry monitoring carries measurable costs to patients, staff, and the health care system. Alarm fatigue from nonactionable alerts is well documented, with up to 99% of alarms deemed clinically insignificant in some analyses.3 Each unnecessary day of telemetry monitoring incurs direct costs related to nursing time and monitoring equipment. In nonintensive care settings, prior studies have estimated the annualized financial burden of inappropriate telemetry use to exceed $500,000 at a single institution.6

At our institution, telemetry utilization data suggested that monitoring duration was a key area of concern. We hypothesized that physicians were not consistently reassessing telemetry need after the initial 24 to 48 hours, and that low familiarity with guideline-based criteria was a contributing factor. A targeted educational initiative was designed to increase awareness of the 2017 AHA guidelines and to promote timely discontinuation of monitoring when clinical indications had resolved.

Protect Description

This project used a pre-post quality improvement design. The preintervention period spanned January 1 through March 31, 2025, and the postintervention period spanned July 1 through September 30, 2025. The educational intervention was delivered between May and June 2025.

The intervention consisted of 3 components. First, a didactic PowerPoint presentation summarizing the key recommendations of the 2017 AHA guidelines was delivered during resident noon conferences and faculty meetings. Second, informational posters reinforcing the same guideline content were placed in physician workrooms and dictation areas as persistent visual reminders.

This initiative was implemented in teaching services, and patients admitted to nonteaching services were not included in the intervention. The target audience included internal medicine residents, transitional year residents, family medicine residents, and hospital medicine attending physicians. Cardiology staff were not specifically targeted in the educational intervention.

Primary outcome data, consisting of the average duration of telemetry use per patient, were obtained from the local institution database using aggregate, deidentified admission records. We evaluated telemetry use across all admission service types, including noncardiac services. Patients were categorized by admission service type, which included Progressive Care, Telemetry, Hospice, Medical or surgical (general mixed-acuity wards), and Medical (dedicated nonsurgical medicine units). Secondary outcome data were obtained through voluntary, anonymous preintervention and postintervention surveys administered to physicians. The survey included 4 multiple-choice questions based on common clinical scenarios and AHA guideline recommendations. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze changes in telemetry utilization rates and survey response distributions. This project was conducted as a quality improvement initiative and was determined by the institutional review committee not to require formal institutional review board approval. All patient data were deidentified before analysis.

Protect Deliverables

Deliverables associated with this project included a hospital-wide educational session for residents and attending hospitalists based on the 2017 AHA telemetry guidelines and informational posters placed throughout physician work areas as standing visual reminders. Aggregate telemetry utilization data were collected from the local institution database, and preintervention and postintervention physician knowledge surveys were administered and analyzed.

Protect Outcome, Impact, and Future Directions

During the 3-month preintervention period, 22,744 patients were admitted, with 394 (1.73%) placed on telemetry for an average of 2.85 days. During the 3-month postintervention period, 19,755 patients were admitted, with 502 (2.54%) placed on telemetry. Despite a modest increase in the percentage of patients monitored, the average duration of telemetry use per patient decreased by 20.08%, from 2.85 to 2.27 days (Figure 1). For an estimated 500 telemetry patients per quarter, this reduction corresponds to approximately 290 telemetry-days saved per quarter and roughly 1,160 telemetry-days saved annually.

A month-by-month review of postintervention data revealed a notable pattern (Table 1). August 2025, which coincided with the start of a new academic year, showed the highest rate of telemetry initiation at 4.12%. This was followed by a sharp decline in September 2025, when the rate fell to 1.09%, the lowest recorded during the entire study period. This pattern likely reflects the expected learning curve of incoming residents, followed by improved practice as new physicians familiarize themselves with and apply the guidelines.

Physician knowledge improved substantially after the intervention (Table 2). Self-reported high awareness of the AHA guidelines increased from 5.9% to 45.5%. The ability to correctly identify a clinical scenario not warranting telemetry improved from 38.2% to 75.8%, a 37.5 percentage point increase. Correct identification of conditions that are not an indication for telemetry improved from 32.4% to 63.6%, and correct responses regarding stroke monitoring guidelines increased from 61.8% to 69.7%.

Planned future interventions include the implementation of electronic medical record prompts for telemetry reassessment, default monitoring duration limits, and automatic discontinuation orders. Repeated educational sessions will be conducted, and future work should investigate the frequency of education required to maintain improved utilization over time.

Discussion

This quality improvement project demonstrates that a low-cost, multifaceted educational intervention can meaningfully reduce the duration of telemetry monitoring in a hospital setting. The 20.08% reduction in average telemetry duration exceeded our primary aim of a 15% reduction and occurred in parallel with a substantial improvement in physician knowledge of evidence-based guidelines.

These findings are consistent with prior literature. Benjamin et al3 reported that up to one-third of monitored days in nonintensive care settings lacked a true clinical indication, and that arrhythmias detected on nonindicated monitoring days were rarely clinically significant. Chaudhary et al6 found that 22.3% of initial telemetry assignments were inappropriate and that 56% of patients remained on monitoring longer than clinically necessary, resulting in an estimated annualized cost of approximately $500,000 at a single institution. Importantly, our intervention demonstrated that simply raising physician awareness of the 2017 AHA guidelines can produce measurable improvements in the efficiency of telemetry discontinuation, even without structural changes to the electronic medical record.

The academic-year effect observed in August, followed by a sharp decline in September, deserves attention. The initial spike likely reflects the overordering behavior characteristic of new trainees at the start of the academic year. The subsequent improvement after residents had time to internalize the educational content suggests that the knowledge gained through the program translated into changes in practice. This underscores both the effectiveness of the intervention and the importance of timing educational efforts relative to trainee transitions.

The survey results complement the utilization data. Improvements in the ability to apply guideline recommendations to specific clinical scenarios, rather than merely recalling them, suggest that the educational program had a practical, real-world impact. The combination of didactic teaching and passive visual reminders likely reinforced learning across multiple modalities.

This study has several limitations. As a single-center project at a teaching hospital, the results may not generalize to all practice settings. The pre-post design without a concurrent control group limits causal inference, as unmeasured confounders cannot be excluded. Physician survey responses were self-reported and subject to response bias. Finally, patient-level clinical outcomes were not assessed, so the impact of reduced telemetry duration on patient safety cannot be directly evaluated from this dataset.

Conclusions

A structured, multifaceted educational initiative based on the 2017 AHA telemetry guidelines successfully reduced the average duration of telemetry use by 20.08% and significantly improved physician knowledge at our institution. This intervention is cost-effective, replicable, and does not require structural changes to existing systems to produce meaningful results. However, the academic-year effect and anticipated fading of knowledge gains over time highlight the need for repeated educational reinforcement. Future interventions should incorporate electronic medical record tools, including automated reassessment prompts and default monitoring durations, to sustain and augment the improvements achieved through education alone. By combining targeted physician education with system-level safeguards, institutions can optimize telemetry use, reduce alarm fatigue, and lower avoidable costs without compromising patient safety.
