Integrated Ultrasound Localization Microscopy and Multi-Omics for Mechanism-Driven Stratification of Azoospermia
Authors: Chujun Ye, Qijie Lu, Keke Yang, Maoyao Li, Chao Feng, Lei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate differentiation between non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) and obstructive azoospermia (OA) is crucial for therapeutic decision-making, yet current diagnostic approaches predominantly depend on invasive testicular biopsy. Herein, we establish a mechanism-driven stratification framework integrating ultrasound localization microscopy (ULM) with multi-omics profiling. Using murine models representing diverse etiologies, high-frame-rate ULM following intravenous microbubble administration (acquiring over 120,000 compounded frames at 1000 Hz) overcomes the spatial-resolution limit of conventional ultrasound to achieve in vivo quantitative mapping of the testicular microvasculature. Super-resolution maps unveil distinct etiology-specific microvascular phenotypes: NOA models showed fragmented and hypoperfused vascular networks with reduced larger intratesticular vessel diameter (BU:166 ± 9.9μm, p < 0.01; Pnldc1:175± 4.9μm, p < 0.05), whereas OA displayed increased vessel density (7.944 ± 0.52; p < 0.01), mean vessel diameter (43.99 ± 1.168 μm; p < 0.01), and mean flow velocity (8.333 ± 0.523 mm/s; p<0.001). Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses linked these imaging phenotypes to angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, lipid peroxidation, and energy-metabolism rewiring. In a small preliminary human cohort, clinical ULM showed concordant differences in vascular morphology and mean vessel diameter between NOA and OA. These findings support ULM as a candidate contrast-enhanced imaging adjunct for mechanism-informed azoospermia stratification, requiring further validation in larger prospective cohorts.

1. Research Background

Infertility affects approximately 15% of couples globally (Minhas et al., 2021). Azoospermia accounts for 1% of the general male population and 10%–15% of infertile men (Zarezadeh et al., 2021), imposing a substantial burden on male reproductive health. Clinically, the cornerstone of therapeutic decision-making lies in distinguishing between obstructive azoospermia (OA, characterized by normal spermatogenesis but blocked transport) and non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA, characterized by intrinsic spermatogenic failure). While OA can often be treated with reconstructive surgery, NOA typically requires invasive microsurgical testicular sperm extraction (micro-TESE) (Ernandez et al., 2021). Currently, the initial diagnostic work-up for azoospermia includes repeated semen analysis, physical examination, reproductive hormone profiling, scrotal ultrasonography, and genetic testing, including karyotyping and Y-chromosome microdeletion analysis when clinically indicated. However, these conventional indicators have limited diagnostic utility in patients presenting with indeterminate clinical features—specifically those with normal testicular volume, normal follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels, and no palpable obstruction. Consequently, approximately 60% of men with azoospermia can obtain a definitive diagnosis only through testicular biopsy (Andrade et al., 2021; Jarvi et al., 2015). While testicular biopsy remains the gold standard to discriminate OA from NOA (Shah et al., 2024), it is an invasive procedure carrying risks of bleeding, infection, permanent testicular injury, and hypogonadism (Dohle et al., 2012). Beyond these procedural risks, men undergoing azoospermia evaluation and surgical sperm retrieval may also experience physical discomfort, psychological stress, and uncertainty regarding sperm retrieval outcomes (Martins et al., 2016). This creates a need for precise, less-invasive diagnostic alternatives.

Current non-invasive methods for diagnosing azoospermia have notable limitations. Transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS) is primarily restricted to evaluating distal ductal anatomy and lacks the resolution to resolve intratesticular microarchitecture (Du et al., 2010), while testicular magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is hindered by overlapping quantitative signal parameters that fail to robustly discriminate OA from NOA (Cai et al., 2021). Similarly, candidate biomarkers such as hormones, proteins, and metabolites lack specificity (Yao et al., 2021) due to systemic interference. Therefore, in the absence of reliable non-invasive predictors, many men with indeterminate OA/NOA status are compelled to undergo diagnostic testicular biopsy or empirical surgical sperm retrieval. In selected men with NOA, particularly those with severe spermatogenic failure or unfavorable histopathological patterns, surgical sperm retrieval may fail despite operative exploration. This uncertainty underscores the need for better preoperative stratification tools. Such invasive procedures not only fail to retrieve sperm but also inflict irreversible physical trauma on the testicular parenchyma, potentially accelerating tissue atrophy and functional decline (Eliveld et al., 2018; Kanto et al., 2025).

Spermatogenesis is a highly coordinated process in which spermatogonia differentiate into mature spermatozoa mitotic proliferation, meiosis and extensive chromatin remodeling; disruption at any stage can lead to spermatogenic impairment (Chen et al., 2023; Ou et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2020; Zitzmann et al., 2021). Critically, this process depends on the precise regulation of the local microenvironment. In particular, the testicular microvasculature plays an indispensable role by supplying essential nutrients, modulating oxygen availability, and clearing metabolic waste. Accordingly, disturbances in microvascular architecture and perfusion are increasingly recognized as being tightly linked to compromised spermatogenic capacity (Xu et al., 2025; P. Gao et al., 2020). Prior imaging studies have generally suggested that global testicular vascularity and blood-flow signals are lower in NOA than in OA (Souza et al., 2005), consistent with impaired spermatogenic function. Importantly, in studies focused specifically on NOA, relatively well-perfused or microvascular-rich regions were more likely to contain residual spermatogenic foci and to yield sperm during surgical retrieval, whereas poorly perfused regions showed lower retrieval rates (Zhang et al., 2018). Existing studies reveal that Klinefelter syndrome (KS), as a common cause of non-obstructive azoospermia, is associated with a marked reduction in testicular microvessels in both animal models and clinical specimens (Carlomagno et al., 2022; Corrigan et al., 2015; Tüttelmann et al., n.d). Moreover, single-cell analyses indicate that endothelial cells in KS testes exhibit an immature phenotype accompanied by structural damage and exaggerated inflammatory signaling (Johannsen et al., 2023). However, whether microvascular alterations constitute a consistent, etiology-informative feature across different azoospermia subtypes, and what molecular programs underlie these vascular phenotypes remains unclear.

Ultrasound localization microscopy (ULM) has emerged as a novel technique that overcomes the acoustic diffraction limit through microbubble localization and tracking (Heiles et al., 2022). ULM enables super-resolution visualization of microvasculature and quantitative analysis of hemodynamic parameters, including vessel density and flow velocity. It has been successfully applied in neurovascular imaging (rodent and human brain) (Demené et al., 2021; Errico et al., 2015), renal microcirculation (Denis et al., 2023), ocular microvasculature (Qian et al., 2022), and tumor angiogenesis, with early clinical studies demonstrating feasibility for super-resolution microvascular assessment in humans (Opacic et al., 2018). Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of in vivo ULM for visualizing human testicular microvasculature (M. Li et al., n.d, 2024). However, high-resolution imaging alone remains descriptive. While ULM can delineate exquisite microvascular details, current studies are largely restricted to phenomenological observations, leaving the underlying pathological drivers in a “black box”. For a diagnostic tool to be truly reliable for clinical decision-making, it requires a validated mechanistic basis. To date, the specific molecular mechanisms that dictate these distinct microvascular phenotypes in azoospermia remain unknown. Angiogenesis and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling represent two closely interconnected process that may link testicular injury, microvascular remodeling, and disruption of the spermatogenic microenvironment. Angiogenic signaling, including VEGF-related endothelial activation (Sargent et al., 2015), can influence endothelial survival, vascular branching, and perfusion stability, whereas ECM remodeling and basement membrane alterations may affect peritubular structural support, vascular stiffness, permeability, and blood-testis barrier dynamics (Siu & Cheng, 2004). Thus, ULM-derived vascular features, such as vessel density, vessel diameter, and flow patterns, may reflect not only hemodynamic variation but also underlying angiogenic and ECM-related microenvironmental remodeling. Elucidating the molecular basis of these microvascular phenotypes is essential to evaluate the biological relevance of ULM-derived vascular phenotypes and explore their potential as candidate imaging biomarkers.

Herein, we propose a precision stratification strategy for azoospermia diagnosis and treatment that integrates ULM with multi-omics profiling (Scheme 1). Moving beyond the feasibility focus of previous studies, our work aims to biologically validate ULM phenotypes through a comprehensive transcriptomic and metabolomic framework. Leveraging the ability of ULM to resolve microvascular structures beyond the diffraction limit, we achieved in vivo quantitative mapping of the testicular microcirculation in murine models. These super-resolution maps revealed that ULM sensitively captures distinct microvascular architectures and perfusion patterns that differentiate NOA from OA, providing novel imaging evidence for non-invasive subtype differentiation. To bridge these microcirculatory phenotypes with microscopic molecular signatures, we performed transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses on the matched murine testicular tissues. This integration delineated the mechanistic networks governing angiogenesis, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, membrane lipid homeostasis, and energy metabolism, revealing that specific microvascular phenotypes are strictly underpinned by distinct mechanisms of tissue injury repair or metabolic compensation. Finally, by extending our investigation to human clinical ULM imaging, we explored whether the vascular patterns identified in mice could be preliminarily observed in a small human cohort. 2. Experimental Methods

2.1. Mouse Models

Mice were maintained in individually ventilated cages (IVCs) under standardized conditions (approximately 25°C, 50% relative humidity; 12h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Animals were housed in group (five per cage), and individual identification was performed by ear punching. C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and Pnldc1 mutant mice were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). All mice were on a C57BL/6 background, and they were acclimatized for at least one week under standard housing conditions before experiments.

C57BL/6 mice were randomized into three groups: control (n=5), OA (n=5), and BU (n=5). OA was modeled by bilateral vasectomy (transection of the vas deferens). BU was modeled by busulfan administration, which produces drug-induced NOA.

For the OA model, mice with inhaled isoflurane (induction 3-4%, maintenance 1-20% in oxygen). After loss of reflexes, a 5-mm lower midline abdominal incision was made to enter the peritoneal cavity. The vas deferens was exteriorized and transected; the intervening segment was discarded, and the cut ends were cauterized (S. Gao et al., 2023). The procedure was repeated on the contralateral side. The muscle and skin were sutured in layers, and mice were monitored until full recovery from anesthesia.

For the BU model, 40 mg of busulfan powder (B2635; Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in 1 mL DMSO to yield a 40 mg/mL stock, which was then diluted with 9 mL normal saline to 4 mg/mL. Each mouse received 40 mg/kg busulfan by intraperitoneal injection (Luo et al., 2021).

All mice were maintained for an additional 4 weeks, after which ULM was performed.

2.1.1. Intratesticular Testosterone Measurement

The testes were gently blotted to remove surface fluid and weighed on an analytical balance. For intratesticular testosterone measurement, each testis was mechanically disrupted in 1.5 mL of normal saline using a syringe needle to prepare a homogenate. The homogenate was centrifuged at 3,000g for 1 min, and the supernatant was collected. Testosterone in the supernatant was quantified by direct chemiluminescent immunoassay using a Siemens analyzer with the Atellica IM Testosterone assay kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A tissue density of 1 g/mL was assumed for calculations.

2.2. Histopathology and Immunofluorescence Staining

Mouse testes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4°C overnight, dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, infiltrated with paraffin, and embedded. Sections of approximately 5 μm thickness were prepared. After deparaffinization and rehydration, slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological assessment.

Immunofluorescence was performed using a Mouse/Rabbit Dual-Target Three-Color Fluorescence Detection Kit (Immunoway, RS0036). Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked for 15 min at room temperature. After three washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), sections were incubated with anti-CD31 (1:200, Immunoway, YM8207) for 1 h at room temperature. Following three PBS washes, the appropriate secondary antibody and the D-594 fluorophore were applied sequentially and incubated for 30 min and 10 min, respectively, at room temperature. After PBS washes, the procedure was repeated for anti-DDX4 (1:200, Immunoway, YM8651). The corresponding secondary antibody and the D-488 fluorophore were then applied, with a 1 h incubation at room temperature. Slides were mounted using the kit-provided DAPI mounting medium. Images were acquired on a Nikon confocal microscope (ECLIPSE C1; Nikon) and processed and quantified with ImageJ.

2.3. Mouse Ultrasound Data Acquisition

Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane (induction 3-4%, maintenance 1-20% in oxygen) and placed in the supine position on a temperature-controlled heating pad to maintain body temperature. Abdominal/scrotal hair was removed using depilatory cream, and pre-warmed ultrasound gel was applied to minimize acoustic impedance mismatch. Under B-mode guidance, the maximal longitudinal plane of each testis was identified and kept constant throughout the acquisition. Microbubbles (SonoVue) were then administered via a lateral tail-vein catheter, and ultrafast sequences for Doppler and ULM were acquired on the same imaging plane. Microbubbles was reconstituted with 5 mL sterile 0.9% saline according to the manufacturer’s instructions and gentle agitated (∼20s) immediately before use. The suspension was used without further dilution. For ULM imaging, a lateral tail-vein catheter was placed and microbubbles were administered using a syringe pump with a 1mL syringe in constant-infusion mode (linear plunger speed: 30 μL/min). The total infused volume was 100 μL per mouse, and infusion was maintained during data acquisition to provide a stable microbubble density for localization and tracking.

A commercial multi-channel ultrasound platform (PodaMed-fUSI U10, PodaMed Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was utilized in this work. Ultrafast ultrasound imaging was performed with an L22-14vX (15.625-MHz center frequency, 0.1-mm pitch, 128 elements) linear array at 15-steering-angle plane-wave transmissions from −10° to +10°. The compounded frame rate was 500 Hz and corresponding pulse repetition frequency (PRF) reached 7500 Hz. For ultrafast B-mode and Doppler imaging, each acquisition consisted of 200 compounded frames collected over 0.4 s. ULM was implemented with an ultrafast sequence comprising 5-steering-angle plane-wave transmissions at −5°, −2.5°, 0°, +2.5°, and +5°. Ultrasound data were obtained at a frame rate of 1,000 Hz as 200 acquisition blocks, each containing 600 compounded frames.

Raw radio-frequency (RF) channel data were processed sequentially with a GPU-accelerated delay-and-sum (DAS) beamformer, in-phase/quadrature (IQ) demodulation and clutter filtering based on singular value decomposition (SVD). Ultrafast power Doppler images were eventually acquired by calculating, for each pixel, the mean power of moving backscattered signals (Yu et al., 2023).

2.4. Mouse ULM Analysis

After clutter filtering, individual microbubbles were localized by two-dimensional Gaussian fitting, and detections were linked across frames to generate displacement vectors using the Kuhn–Munkres algorithm (simpletracker, The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) (Yu et al., 2023). Tracking microbubbles at kilohertz rates enabled estimation of blood-flow velocities across a wide dynamic range, approximately 1 mm/s to several cm/s (Errico et al., 2015).

To evaluate alterations of the testicular microvasculature in different azoospermia mouse models, we quantified several hemodynamic parameters from ULM images, including vessel density, mean vessel tortuosity, vessel diameter, and blood flow velocity. Flow information in the ULM images was extracted from microbubble trajectories: the accurate coordinate of each microbubble was determined in every frame, and the displacement between consecutive frames was used to calculate the estimated instantaneous velocity (Errico et al., 2015) of microbubble at high frame rate. During long-time acquisition, multiple microbubbles passed through the same spatial location and their instantaneous velocities were averaged to yield the mean flow velocity at that coordinate. The global-mean flow velocity was then calculated as the average of all local-mean flow velocities across the entire region of interest (ROI). Vessel density was defined as the ratio of vessel pixels to the total number of pixels within the ROI. Mean vessel tortuosity was calculated as the ratio of the total length of each vessel to the straight-line distance between its two endpoints (Bullitt et al., 2005). Vessel diameter was estimated by computing the shortest distance from each point along the vessel centerline to its boundaries (Oscar Demeulenaere et al., 2022). For each testis, ULM was analyzed on the maximal longitudinal plane. The larger intratesticular vessel was defined as the vessel segment with the largest reconstructed diameter on the ULM diameter map within the field of view. The diameter and flow velocity of this vessel segment were then extracted for between-group comparisons.

2.5. Clinical ULM Acquisition

From July to December 2024, we consecutively recruited 10 infertile outpatients diagnosed with azoospermia. The clinical cohort included 5 men with OA and 5 men with NOA. The diagnosis was established based on semen analysis, physical examination, reproductive hormone profiles, scrotal ultrasonography and histopathological results. Individual demographic and hormonal characteristics are summarized in Supplemental Table S1, and histopathological diagnoses and etiologies are provided in Supplemental Table S2.

All participants then underwent standardized clinical ULM examination. Bilateral testes were first scanned on B-mode using an L13-3WU linear probe (Eagus R9, Mindray) to identify the target plane and acquire representative static images (depth 3.0 cm; gain 70; dynamic range 130). Color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) was then used to visualize the main testicular blood flow. CEUS cine loops for ULM were subsequently acquired on the same plane (MI:0.080; depth 3.0 cm; gain 56; dynamic range 105). Microbubbles (SonoVue, 2.4mL) were injected via a radial vein, immediately followed by a 5 mL 0.9% saline flush. Microbubbles reached the testes within ∼20 s and remained visible for ∼2-3min. Dual-display B-mode and CEUS data were recorded side-by-side at 114Hz for 30 s and stored for offline ULM processing. All examinations were performed by a sonographer with > 20 years of experience, with the probe positioned to scan both testes on the maximal transverse plane. Participants were observed during and for ≥ 30 min after microbubbles administration to monitor for hypersensitivity reactions. Because clinical ULM requires venous access and microbubble contrast administration, it was considered a contrast-enhanced, less invasive imaging procedure rather than a conventional non-contrast ultrasound examination.

2.6. Clinical ULM Analysis

All ULM reconstruction and quantitative analyses were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation or clinical diagnosis. For clinical data, the sonographer performed standardized acquisition using a predefined imaging plane, whereas offline ULM reconstruction and parameter extraction were conducted without access to OA/NOA status.

To generate super-resolution images from clinical data, a processing pipeline addressing tissue motion was implemented. Tissue motions within the region of interest (ROI), potentially induced by breathing or probe pressure, were detected on B-mode images using a two-stage image registration algorithm and corrected for the corresponding CEUS frames; prior to detection, microbubble signals in B-mode sequences were reduced via singular value decomposition to improve accuracy. Super-localization and tracking were performed using a previously established framework (Yan et al., 2022, 2024), where microbubbles tracked for fewer than three frames were discarded to minimize noise. Following trajectory accumulation and Gaussian filtering, key hemodynamic parameters were quantified. Mean vessel velocity was determined for each pixel by the vector mean of the passed trajectories. Tortuosity was measured by dividing the total length of each track by the direct distance between its endpoints. To calculate mean vessel diameter, a binary vessel structure was obtained by thresholding the density map at half the peak value of the Gaussian filter; centerlines were estimated using morphological functions (e.g., ‘bwmorph’), and diameter was defined as twice the closest distance from the centerline to the vessel boundary.

2.7. RNA-Seq

Four groups of RNA-seq experiments were established, including control, BU, Pnldc1 KO, and OA, all using mouse testicular tissue. Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent, and RNA concentration and purity were assessed on a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Libraries were prepared from 1μg of total RNA, quantified with a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer, and sequenced on NovaSeq X Plus platform (PE150) using NovaSeq Reagent kit. RNA extraction, library preparation, quality control, and sequencing were performed by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Data processing was conducted through the free online platform of Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com). Transcript abundance was estimated using the transcripts per million (TPM) method. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2, applying an adjusted p-value threshold of <0.05. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment of DEGs were performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases to highlight biological processes and pathways most relevant to the observed phenotypes. In addition, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out across all groups to identify enriched pathways and provide insight into potential molecular mechanisms.

2.7.1. Metabolomics

For metabolomics analysis, 50 mg of testicular tissue was collected from each group and placed in a 2-mL centrifuge tube. One 6mm stainless steel bead and 400 μL of extraction solution (methanol: water=4:1, (v:v)) containing four internal standards (0.02 mg/mL, L-2-chlorophenylalanine, etc.) were added for metabolite extraction. Samples were homogenized for 6 min at −10 °C and 50 Hz using a cryogenic tissue grinder, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 30 min at 5 °C and 40 kHz. After incubation at −20 °C for 30 min, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected and transferred to autosampler vials for analysis. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing equal aliquots of all extracts, and one QC sample was injected every 5–10 samples to monitor analytical reproducibility.

LC–MS/MS analyses were performed at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), using a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system Mass Spectrometer equipped with an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i. d.,1.8μm; Waters, USA). Raw LC–MS data were processed with Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) software for baseline filtering, peak detection, integration, retention-time alignment, and peak matching. A data matrix of retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity was generated. Compound identification was achieved by matching MS and MS/MS spectra against public databases including HMDB (https://www.hmdb.ca/) and Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), as well as an in-house database.

The resulting data matrix was analyzed through the free online platform of Majorbio Cloud Platform (cloud.majorbio.com). Preprocessing steps included: removal of features missing in more than 20% of samples (80% rule), imputation of missing values with the minimum observed intensity, normalization by total ion intensity, exclusion of metabolites with QC relative standard deviation (RSD) >30%, and log10 transformation. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using the R package “ropls” (v1.6.2), and sevenfold cross-validation was applied to assess model robustness. Significantly altered metabolites were identified based on a combination of variable importance in projection (VIP > 1.0) from the OPLS-DA model and Student’s t-test (p < 0.05). Differential metabolites were annotated against the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html), and pathway enrichment was performed using Python packages “scipy.stats” (https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/) with Fisher’s exact test to identify biological pathways most relevant to the experimental conditions.

2.8. Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

We used immunohistochemistry to evaluate differences in COL3A1 and COL6A3 protein expression among mouse models with different etiologies of azoospermia. Paraffin sections were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol to water. Sections were then incubated with trypsin digestion solution (100–200 μL) at room temperature for 30 minutes. Afterward, PBST washes and incubation with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide were used to remove endogenous peroxidase and to accomplish antigen retrieval. Non-specific binding was blocked with normal goat serum for 3 hours at room temperature. Following PBST rinses, sections were incubated with anti-COL3A1 antibody (1:200, Immunoway, YM8111) or anti-COL6A3 antibody (1:200, Immunoway, YT1036) for 16–18 hours, then washed three times with PBST. A secondary antibody was applied for 2 hours at room temperature, followed by PBST washes. Freshly prepared DAB chromogen was added, and color development was monitored microscopically; positive staining appeared brownish yellow, and the reaction was stopped by rinsing with distilled water. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 30 seconds, rinsed in tap water, blued in distilled water for 5–10 minutes, dehydrated, and mounted. Images were acquired with a microscope (Nikon E100). Semi-quantitative analysis was carried out in ImageJ to estimate COL3A1 and COL6A3 expression.

2.8.1. Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism v10.1.2. For comparisons among four groups (n = 5 per group), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (* p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001).

3. Result

3.1. In Vivo ULM Super-resolution Imaging Reveals Distinct Microvascular Fingerprints of OA and NOA

To overcome the resolution limits of conventional imaging, we applied ULM to visualize and quantify testicular microcirculation dynamics across groups (Figure S1). We first compared ULM with standard clinical modalities to demonstrate its superior performance in the same testis (Figure 1A). Conventional B-mode ultrasound failed to reveal intratesticular microvascular structures due to the diffraction limit. Ultrafast Doppler imaging acquired at 500 Hz with 200 compounded frames, provided only partial visualization limited to larger vascular branches. In contrast, ULM recorded 120,000 compounded frames at 1,000 Hz, enabling robust microbubble localization and tracking and thereby delineating a substantially more complete and fine-scale intratesticular microvascular network. In addition to structural imaging, ULM maps provided intuitive visualization of hemodynamic parameters, including density maps (Figure 1B), velocity maps (Figure 1C), and direction maps (Figure 1D). In control group, the microvasculature appeared dense and continuous, with uniform perfusion, stable flow velocity, and consistent directionality. We established three mouse models representing distinct etiologies of azoospermia: OA induced by bilateral vasectomy, BU induced by busulfan (NOA, 40 mg/kg), and Pnldc1 KO (NOA). In NOA models (BU and Pnldc1 KO), the microvascular network was fragmented, with reduced density, fewer fine branches, and frequent “hollow” areas of low perfusion. In OA, vessel density was moderately increased, with more fine branches and relatively preserved vascular uniformity.

Quantitative analysis of hemodynamic parameters further corroborated these observations (Figure 1E). The OA mice exhibited significantly higher vessel density (7.944 ± 0.52; **p < 0.01), mean vessel diameter (43.99 ± 1.168 μm; **p < 0.01), and mean flow velocity (8.333 ± 0.523 mm/s; ***p<0.001) compared with other groups. In NOA models (BU and Pnldc1 KO), while a downward trend in vessel density and diameter was observed consistent with the fragmented morphology, these global reductions did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting the heterogeneous nature of the vascular injury. Vessel tortuosity did not differ among groups.

Furthermore, we analyzed the functional status of larger intratesticular vessels by quantifying the diameter of the largest vessel branch identified on the ULM diameter map within the maximal longitudinal plane of each testis (Figure 1F). Compared with control, the diameter of large intratesticular vessels was significantly reduced in BU, Pnldc1 KO and OA. However, the velocity of large intratesticular vessels in OA was significantly higher than in control and then in the NOA groups (BU and Pnldc1 KO).

3.2. Establishment and Multiscale Pathological Characterization of Azoospermia Mouse Models

To validate the establishment of diverse azoospermia models, we first evaluated general physiological and gross testicular morphology. Body weight was comparable across groups throughout the study period (Figure S2). Gross morphological examination revealed distinct phenotypic alterations across groups (Figure 2A). 3.4. Etiology-specific Metabolic Rewiring and Multi-Omics Integration

While transcriptomic profiling highlights the upstream genetic reprogramming, it may not fully capture the downstream functional endpoint. To bridge the gap between gene expression signatures and the observed microvascular phenotypes, particularly in the OA group where transcriptomic changes were subtle, we next performed comprehensive metabolomic profiling. We profiled the testicular metabolome across the three azoospermia models and controls. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed clear metabolic separation of BU, Pnldc1 KO, and OA from controls, indicating broad metabolic remodeling across etiologies (Figure 5A). Consistent with this, volcano plots showed extensive metabolite changes in Pnldc1 KO and OA, contrasting with the transcriptome where BU exhibited the largest number of differentially expressed genes (Figure 5B). At the pathway level, top-level KEGG annotation indicated that most differential metabolites mapped to metabolism-related categories, with xenobiotics biodegradation and metabolism enriched specifically in BU, in line with busulfan-associated detoxification and oxidative stress responses, whereas translation-related terms were preferentially represented in Pnldc1 KO, suggesting a shift in intrinsic biosynthetic programs. To refine these pathway-level differences, we calculated second-level KEGG differential abundance scores (Figure S3). BU showed a mixed remodeling pattern, with positive scores in the pentose phosphate pathway and pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, but negative scores in ABC transporters, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism. In Pnldc1 KO, the dominant signal was a coordinated increase across microvasculature-relevant lipid pathways, including glycerophospholipid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, and ABC transporters. OA displayed significant positive scores in nucleotide and purine metabolism, consistent with purinergic signaling linked to vasodilation and anti-inflammatory effects; pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis also scored positively, whereas ferroptosis was significantly down-scored, suggesting reduced iron-dependent lipid peroxidation that may favor endothelial survival.

KEGG topology analysis further highlighted etiology-specific hubs (Figure 5D): BU showed high pathway impact in coenzyme A biosynthesis and lipid metabolic routes, pointing to altered cofactor supply and membrane remodeling; Pnldc1 KO was dominated by sphingolipid-centered pathways regulating endothelial barrier integrity and vascular stability; and OA showed strong impacts in pyruvate metabolism and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, together with arachidonic acid metabolism, supporting coordinated changes in central energy metabolism and vasomotor/permeability regulation. Notably, OA exhibited relatively limited transcript-level significance but pronounced metabolic remodeling, suggesting that key compensatory adjustments in OA are captured more robustly at the metabolite level than at gene expression.

Finally, joint pathway analysis integrating differentially expressed genes with differential metabolites consolidated these patterns (Figure 5E). In BU, multi-omics changes converged on energy metabolism (glycolysis/gluconeogenesis), membrane lipid remodeling (glycerolipid/glycerophospholipid metabolism), and detoxification/antioxidant pathways (drug metabolism–cytochrome P450 and glutathione metabolism), consistent with injury-driven microvascular damage followed by remodeling. In Pnldc1 KO, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and amino acid biosynthesis predominated together with carbohydrate and glycosylation pathways, supporting a reset of endogenous biosynthetic programs rather than broad stress, with sphingolipid involvement aligning with the mild microvascular remodeling observed by ULM. In OA, downregulation of the TCA cycle emerged as the central feature, consistent with adaptive metabolic retuning under improved perfusion after compensatory vasodilation, reduced reliance on high oxidative flux, and a lower oxidative burden.

3.5. Clinical Recapitulation of Vascular Phenotypes and Cross-Species Validation of ECM Remodeling

To extend the translational relevance of the ULM phenotypes, we first visualized microvascular flow patterns in human NOA and OA testes using ULM (Figure 6A). The clinical characteristics of the human cohort are summarized in Supplemental Table S1 and S2. Briefly, the cohort consisted of five patients with NOA and five patients with OA, with individual hormonal profiles, histopathological diagnoses, and etiologies provided to improve clinical interpretability of the preliminary human findings. After this clinical characterization, we examined whether the etiology-associated vascular patterns identified in murine models could also be observed in human testes. In this preliminary human cohort, clinical ULM showed vascular patterns that were partially concordant with those observed in murine models (Figure 6B). Patients with NOA exhibited vascular rarefaction and fragmentation, consistent with a fibrotic, hypoperfused microenvironment. In contrast, OA patients displayed a dense, continuous, and hypervascular network, mirroring the adaptive remodeling seen in the OA models. Quantitative analysis of the clinical cohort (n=5 per group) further provided exploratory quantitative support for these visual observations. Specifically, the mean vessel diameter in the OA group was significantly larger than that in the NOA group (***p < 0.001) (Figure S4A). However, no statistically significant differences were observed in mean vessel velocity or tortuosity between the two groups (Figure S4B and C). We then performed immunohistochemistry for COL3A1 and COL6A3 on testes from the control group and the three experimental groups. As shown in Figure 6C, signals were mainly localized to the peritubular basement membrane and the interstitial fibrous network. BU displayed a continuous, thickened circumferential pattern around seminiferous tubules, with noticeably stronger staining within interstitial bundles. Control and Pnldc1 KO testes showed mild to moderate continuous circumferential staining. In OA, COL3A1 positivity was less extensive, appearing thin and patchy, and COL6A3 showed no obvious change. Quantification of the positive area fraction (Figure 6D and E) indicated that COL3A1 and COL6A3 were significantly increased in BU (*p < 0.01); COL3A1 was slightly reduced in OA (**p < 0.05), and COL6A3 in OA was not significantly altered. These immunohistochemical findings are consistent with the transcriptomic data and support prominent angiogenesis and ECM basement membrane remodeling in BU, smaller-amplitude changes in Pnldc1 KO, and mild or nonsignificant alterations in OA, including downregulation of related genes.

Immunohistochemistry for COL3A1 and COL6A3 was performed on normal human testes and testes from patients with NOA and OA. Normal samples showed continuous peritubular ring-like staining and a reticular interstitial pattern for both proteins; NOA testes retained prominent COL3A1/COL6A3 immunoreactivity but with a more heterogeneous distribution consistent with ECM remodeling, whereas OA testes demonstrated markedly attenuated, discontinuous, patchy staining for both markers (Figure 6F). Notably, COL6A3 reduction was more evident in human OA than in the mouse OA model. Collectively, these findings integrate transcriptomic data with the ULM-defined microcirculatory phenotypes, supporting etiology-dependent ECM remodeling across species.

4. Discussion

This study compared testicular microcirculation across three etiologic mouse models of azoospermia at both the ULM and molecular levels. ULM achieves imaging resolution beyond the diffraction limit by administering microbubble contrast and tracking bubble positions across consecutive frames (Denis et al., 2023; Heiles et al., 2022; McCall et al., 2023), thereby reconstructing a complete map of the testicular microvasculature. Integrated multi-omics has become a major focus in recent research. Transcriptomics leverages high-throughput sequencing to resolve gene-expression programs and their regulatory architecture (He et al., 2023; Ozsolak & Milos, 2011), while metabolomics profiles small-molecule metabolites to link biochemical pathways with phenotypic states (Kirwan, 2023; Patti et al., 2012; Vich Vila et al., 2024). In OA, quantitative hemodynamic metrics showed significantly higher vessel density, greater mean flow velocity, and larger mean vessel diameter. In the large intratesticular vessels, flow velocity was also higher, whereas diameter was significantly smaller than in control. In the NOA models (BU and Pnldc1 KO), ULM showed a nonsignificant trend toward smaller mean vessel diameter relative to control, while the diameter of larger intratesticular vessels was reduced in both groups. At the transcriptomic level, Angiogenesis and remodeling of the extracellular matrix and basement membrane are the principal biological processes governing the testicular microcirculation (Atiakshin et al., 2024; “Environmental Toxicant 2,3,7,8-Tetrachlorodibenzo-p-Dioxin Induces Non-Obstructive Johannsen et al., 2023; Y. Li et al., 2023). Combined transcriptomic and ULM analysis reveals that the BU group shows toxic injury to the microvasculature with broad activation of angiogenesis and ECM remodeling. Compensatory neovascularization and increased ECM deposition are evident, accompanied by reductions in vessel diameter and vessel density, which is consistent with a destruction rate exceeding the rate of neovessel formation. In Pnldc1 KO, loss of Pnldc1 leads to arrest of spermatogenesis and failure to generate mature sperm. Meanwhile, transcriptomic profiling indicated concurrent alterations in the testicular microvasculature, with significant enrichment of angiogenesis-related genes. Although the enrichment scores were lower than those in BU; this pattern indicates secondary activation of angiogenic programs after germline arrest without overt basement membrane remodeling. In OA, changes in microvascular genes are modest compared with BU and Pnldc1 KO. The profile is dominated by selective ECM remodeling, likely reflecting compensatory adjustments rather than primary transcriptional regulation. Downregulation of ECM core genes such as COL3A1 and COL6A3 is associated with mild microvascular disruption, increased vessel diameter, and higher intravascular flow velocity. At the metabolomic level, BU was characterized by pathways related to xenobiotic detoxification and antioxidant defense (Drug metabolism cytochrome-P450 and Glutathione metabolism), membrane-lipid remodeling, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis; Pnldc1 KO was dominated by sphingolipid/phospholipid and protein biosynthetic pathways; OA was marked by downregulation of pathways related to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle.

Measurements of hemodynamic parameters obtained by ULM, including vessel density were aligned with histological findings. Notably, MRI, CT, and conventional ultrasound evaluate the microcirculation using indirect quantitative surrogates (Bodard et al., 2023). In contrast, ULM provides direct in vivo measurements of hemodynamic parameters within the microvasculature. Testicular microvessels were labeled with CD31 and vessel density was quantified in ImageJ as the area fraction of CD31-positive signal. OA showed a significant increase relative to controls, whereas BU and Pnldc1 KO did not differ significantly. The ULM estimates mirrored this result. Histological analysis also revealed group-specific differences in spermatogenic activity. As reported previously, NOA showed varying degrees of impairment (Xie et al., n.d), and OA retained normal spermatogenesis (Wan et al., 2023).

Testicular microvascular differed significantly between OA and NOA, and NOA of different etiologies also exhibited distinct microvascular. In OA, compensatory enlargement of intratesticular microvessels was associated with higher mean blood flow velocity. As small caliber vessels expanded, the diameter of large intratesticular vessels decreased, while the velocity of large intratesticular vessels rose significantly. At the transcriptomic level, angiogenesis pathways were activated in both BU and Pnldc1 KO, although to different extents. BU also showed activation of ECM pathways, consistent with a greater molecular disturbance than in Pnldc1 KO. In line with these molecular differences, ULM revealed a significant smaller diameter of larger intratesticular vessels in both BU and Pnldc1 KO showed no marked difference. In intratesticular testosterone assays, BU displayed a significant increase, while Pnldc1 KO and OA showed no appreciable change. Together with reports of elevated intratesticular testosterone in Klinefelter syndrome, these findings are compatible with busulfan-induced injury to the testicular microvasculature. Overall, although BU and Pnldc1 KO are both forms of NOA, their ULM metrics and hormone profiles differ in an etiology-specific manner, which can be leveraged to refine diagnostic models.

This study offers a preliminary yet insightful mechanistic perspective on the differential alterations of testicular microcirculation in distinct etiological subtypes of azoospermia. In BU, chemical injury (Hong et al., 2025) rapidly activated angiogenesis, ECM and basement-membrane remodeling, and antioxidant programs to repair the damaged microvascular network. On ULM, this corresponded to a relative reduction in mean vessel diameter and a significant decrease in the diameter of larger intratesticular vessels. In Pnldc1 KO, loss of Pnldc1 primarily caused arrest of spermatogenesis (Ding et al., 2017; Nagirnaja et al., 2021), yet the microvasculature also underwent mild remodeling distinct from broad stress responses. Angiogenesis genes were secondarily activated, but the effect size was smaller than in BU, and ULM showed only a nonsignificant trend toward smaller mean vessel diameter, whereas the diameter of larger intratesticular vessels was significantly reduced. In OA, compensatory changes predominated. Obstruction increased proximal pressure in the epididymis and vas deferens (Bieth et al., 2021; Ribas-Maynou et al., 2022), which propagated to the rete testis and the interstitium surrounding seminiferous tubules, raising interstitial hydrostatic pressure and altering venous outflow and flow resistance. To maintain oxygen delivery and metabolite clearance, the testicular microcirculation exhibited luminal dilation. Transcriptomic data indicated little activation of angiogenesis but mild ECM remodeling, and metabolomics showed downregulation of tricarboxylic acid cycle pathways together with activation of arachidonic acid metabolism consistent with vasodilation. Taken together, these observations support an injury-driven remodeling program in BU, a secondary and modest angiogenic response without overt ECM remodeling in Pnldc1 KO, and an adaptive, compensatory adjustment of microvascular tone and metabolism in OA.

Finally, to explore the translational relevance of the preclinical ULM phenotypes, we performed preliminary clinical ULM imaging in a small cohort and conducted cross-species immunohistochemical assessment of ECM-related markers. ULM imaging in patients successfully recapitulated the etiology-specific vascular fingerprints observed in mice, vascular rarefaction in NOA versus adaptive hypervascularization in OA. However, although OA and NOA differed in flow velocity in the murine ULM datasets, we did not detect statistically significant differences in mean vessel velocity in the clinical cohort. This discrepancy is likely multifactorial. First, the human analysis was based on a small sample size and inherently greater inter-individual variability (age, baseline hemodynamics. Probe pressure, and respiratory motion), which can mask moderate group-level effects. Second, “mean velocity” is a global metric that can be dominated by numerous slow-flow capillary trajectories; etiology-related differences may instead reside in high-flow segments or in percentile-based velocity features rather than the mean. In addition, species-related differences in testicular size, vascular scale, cardiac output, microbubble kinetics, and imaging depth may further contribute to the discrepancy between murine and human velocity measurements. These observations suggest that structural ULM metrics, such as vessel diameter and vascular morphology, may be more stable than mean velocity for early translational interpretation, whereas velocity-derived parameters require larger prospective validation and possibly percentile-based or segment-specific analysis. These imaging phenotypes were mechanistically corroborated by the differential expression of COL3A1 and COL6A3 in both murine and human testicular specimens. The results were concordant with our transcriptomic findings, providing preliminary cross-species histological and imaging support for our hypothesis. Taken together, these findings provide preliminary cross-species support for ULM-derived vascular phenotypes as mechanism-informed imaging biomarkers in azoospermia. Given the small human cohort and the incomplete replication of velocity-derived parameters, larger prospective studies are warranted to further validate their diagnostic reliability and determine their potential role in clinical decision-making and sperm retrieval planning.

From a clinical perspective, ULM may provide additional microvascular information that is not available from conventional B-mode ultrasound, CDFI, serum hormones, or genetic testing. In future practice, such imaging information may help complement OA/NOA differentiation, refine the assessment of NOA heterogeneity, and support preoperative counseling before sperm retrieval. However, the current human data are preliminary, and ULM should not be interpreted as a replacement for testicular biopsy or micro-TESE decision-making at this stage.

This study has several limitations. First, RNA-seq and metabolomics reflect bulk measurements from whole testis tissue, which reduces spatial and cell-type resolution; future work should integrate spatial transcriptomics and spatial metabolomics to map gene expression and metabolite distributions within single sections and relate regional microvascular features to spermatogenic function. Second, the testis is a three-dimensional organ, whereas our current ULM protocol yields two-dimensional planes rather than volumetric reconstructions; three-dimensional ULM could depict the architecture and perfusion of the entire intratesticular microvascular network with greater fidelity. Third, to limit intergroup variability we assessed mice at a single time point; longitudinal imaging and omics across disease stages would clarify the temporal dynamics of microvascular remodeling for different etiologies and may improve diagnostic accuracy.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study centered on ultrasound ULM imaging and hemodynamic readouts, and integrated histology with transcriptomic and metabolomic pathway evidence to systematically delineate the link between testicular microcirculation and spermatogenesis. The findings further support the feasibility of ULM for differentiating OA from NOA and suggest potential value for optimizing assisted reproductive strategies and guiding the choice of sperm retrieval approaches. The combined use of ULM with multi-omics deepens understanding of how azoospermia perturbs the microcirculation and opens new avenues for noninvasive imaging diagnostics in reproductive health.
