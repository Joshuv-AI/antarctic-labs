Comparative Outcomes of Hemorrhoidectomy under Spinal Anesthesia between Older and Super-old Patients
Authors: Uido Yeo, Jong Kyun Lee, Do Yeon Hwang, Woo Jung Nam, Dong Ho Cho, Keehoon Hyun, JaeIl Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives: With increasing life expectancy, the number of older and very old patients undergoing anorectal surgery is rising. However, concerns remain regarding the safety of hemorrhoidectomy among those aged ≥80 years. In this study, we compared postoperative outcomes between older (age 65-79 years) and very old (age ≥80 years) patients undergoing open hemorrhoidectomy under spinal anesthesia. Methods: This single-center retrospective cohort study included consecutive adult patients who underwent excisional hemorrhoidectomy between April 2024 and May 2025. Patients were stratified into older (65-79 years) and very old (≥80 years) groups. Baseline characteristics, perioperative factors, and postoperative outcomes, including 6-month complications, and predictors of Foley catheter insertion or additional analgesic injections were assessed. Results: Overall, 693 older patients and 68 very old patients were included. The incidence of early (≤6 months) postoperative complications, including constipation, bleeding, and recurrence, did not differ significantly between the two groups. Foley catheter insertion occurred more frequently in the very old group than in the older group (45.6% vs 31.0%, p = 0.021). No Clavien-Dindo grade III or higher complications occurred in the very old group. In the multivariable analysis, intravenous patient-controlled analgesia was associated with a reduced likelihood of needing additional analgesic injections (odds ratio, 0.39; 95% confidence interval, 0.22-0.69). Conclusions: Hemorrhoidectomy under spinal anesthesia led to comparable short-term postoperative outcomes in older and very old patients without severe systemic disease.

INTRODUCTION

Hemorrhoids are a common condition in adults, affecting approximately 40% of the general adult population[1]. Previous epidemiologic studies have shown a high prevalence among older individuals. An Austrian study reported a prevalence of 44.29% in individuals aged 65-69 years, and a U.S. review reported a peak incidence for the range of 45-65 years[1,2]. For high-grade hemorrhoids that do not respond to conservative management, surgical intervention becomes necessary. Hemorrhoidectomy is widely regarded as the standard treatment for Grade III-IV hemorrhoids[3,4].

Surgical treatment in older patients requires careful clinical consideration. Compared with younger individuals, older patients more frequently present with comorbidities and have reduced cardiopulmonary and overall physiological reserves, which increases the risk of postoperative complications. In particular, previous studies have reported a substantially higher risk of major complications, including respiratory events, among very old patients aged ≥80 years receiving general anesthesia[5].

Spinal anesthesia has been proposed as an alternative to general anesthesia for older patients because of its potential advantages in reducing respiratory complications and perioperative morbidity[6-8]. Several studies that included older patients, defined as ≥60 or ≥65 years of age, have reported acceptable hemorrhoidectomy outcomes; however, these studies did not focus on very old individuals, and their sample included relatively few patients aged ≥80 years. For example, Yamamoto et al. included 34 patients with a mean age of 78 years, and a Turkish study reported outcomes in 49 patients with a mean age of 66.7 years[9,10].

Consequently, evidence regarding the safety and short-term outcomes of hemorrhoidectomy in patients aged ≥80 years remains limited. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate postoperative outcomes of hemorrhoidectomy under spinal anesthesia by comparing older patients aged 65-79 years with very old patients aged ≥80 years, using real-world clinical data.

METHODS

Study design and population

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a single institution and included patients aged ≥65 years who underwent hemorrhoidectomy between January 2024 and May 2025. Patients were categorized into two groups based on age: the older group (65-79 years) and the very old group (≥80 years).

Inclusion criteria

(i) Age ≥65 years

(ii) Diagnosis of Goligher grade III-IV hemorrhoids

(iii) Treatment with open hemorrhoidectomy (Milligan-Morgan technique) hemorrhoidectomy

(iv) A minimum follow-up duration of 6 months.

Exclusion criteria

(i) Emergency surgery for acute thrombosed hemorrhoids

(ii) Concomitant anal fistula, fissure, or other conditions requiring combined procedures

(iii) Insufficient medical records, precluding analysis

(iv) Hemorrhoidectomy performed under local anesthesia

Anesthesia, surgical procedure, and postoperative pain management

Spinal anesthesia was administered in the sitting or lateral decubitus position through bupivacaine injection into the subarachnoid space at the lumbar level to achieve adequate sensory blockade for perianal surgery.

Postoperative analgesia consisted of routine oral analgesics, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and acetaminophen, administered at regular intervals according to the institutional protocol. Intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (IV PCA) was offered at the discretion of the treating clinician based on the anticipated intensity of postoperative pain. PCA pumps were programmed to deliver opioid analgesics using standard clinical settings, including a bolus dose with a lockout period. Because postoperative pain is commonly expected, IV PCA was routinely recommended preoperatively. Additional analgesic injections were administered when postoperative pain was insufficiently controlled (numerical rating scale score ≥4) with oral analgesics or IV PCA. These included intramuscular or intravenous nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g., ketoprofen) or opioid analgesics (e.g., tramadol or meperidine), selected according to patients' condition and clinical judgment.

Data collection

Clinical data were retrospectively obtained from electronic medical records. Preoperative variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), preoperative hemoglobin levels, and medical history, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular accident, prostate-related conditions, chronic kidney disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. “Prostate-related condition” was operationally defined as a composite variable including benign prostatic hyperplasia, prostatitis, and a history of prostate cancer.

Comorbidities were identified based on medical history documented in the electronic medical records, including prior diagnosis or ongoing medical treatment.

The number and Goligher grade of hemorrhoids were also recorded. Squeezing pressure was measured preoperatively as part of a routine anorectal physiology assessment to evaluate the baseline sphincter function, which may vary with age and potentially influence postoperative symptoms.

Surgical variables included operative time, type of anesthesia, and number of excised hemorrhoids. Postoperative variables included the number of additional analgesic doses administered for pain control, Foley catheter insertion, length of hospital stay, recurrence, and postoperative complications. Additional analgesics were defined as intramuscular or intravenous agents administered upon patient request, in addition to routine oral pain medications.

Assessment of complications

Postoperative complications included bleeding, skin tag formation, constipation, anal stenosis, and voiding difficulty. Postoperative outcomes were identified based on clinical diagnoses documented during inpatient care or subsequent outpatient follow-up visits within the study period.

Voiding difficulty was defined as the inability to void spontaneously for more than 8 h after surgery or a post-void residual volume ≥600 mL[11]. Postoperative bleeding was defined as any overt anal bleeding event within 30 days after surgery[12]. Bleeding events were further classified based on management:

(i) Conservative (observation ± oral medication only), corresponding to Clavien-Dindo grade I

(ii) Procedural hemostasis without general anesthesia (e.g., suturing or cautery), corresponding to Clavien-Dindo grade IIIa

All patients were followed up at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively in the outpatient clinic.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS software, version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are expressed as frequency and percentage. Continuous variables were compared between the older and very old groups using Student's t-test, whereas categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

Early postoperative complications were summarized descriptively according to the age group. Owing to the low incidence of most events, formal hypothesis testing was not performed for these outcomes.

To evaluate factors associated with additional postoperative analgesic injections and Foley catheter insertion, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed using the combined cohort of older and very old patients. Age group (65-79 vs ≥80 years), sex, hemorrhoid count, and hemorrhoid grade were included as covariates. Intravenous patient-controlled analgesia use and squeezing pressure were also included in the analysis of additional analgesic injections. An exploratory post hoc subgroup analysis of male patients was performed according to the presence or absence of prostate-related conditions, a well-recognized risk factor for postoperative urinary retention that is prevalent among older male patients.

Ethical statements

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul Songdo Hospital (IRB no. 2025-011). The requirement for informed consent was waived.

The prevalence of hypertension, spinal disease, and prostate-related conditions was significantly higher in the very old group. There was no significant difference in the distribution of hemorrhoid grades between the older and very old groups. Hemoglobin levels were significantly lower in the very old group (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This study was aimed at evaluating the clinical characteristics, surgical outcomes, and In the present study, postoperative urinary retention occurred significantly more frequently in the very elderly group than in the elderly group, consistent with findings from previous studies complications in older patients aged 65-79 years and very old patients aged ≥80 years who underwent hemorrhoidectomy. The very old group demonstrated significantly higher rates of spinal disease, prostate-related conditions, hypertension, and prior malignancy than the older group. The increased burden of comorbidities in the very old population may theoretically elevate the risk of anesthesia-related issues, including spinal anesthesia failure, postoperative urinary retention, and blood pressure instability. Nevertheless, in the present study, spinal anesthesia was successfully performed in all patients, and no anesthesia-related hemodynamic instability was observed.

Postoperative complications included constipation, anal stenosis, recurrence, bleeding, pain, urinary retention, and skin tag formation. Except for bleeding, all observed complications were classified as Clavien-Dindo grade I[13]. Except for urinary retention, the incidence of postoperative complications did not differ significantly between the two groups. Operative time was also comparable; although not statistically significant, the very old group exhibited a slightly shorter hospital stay. These findings suggest that, with appropriate patient selection and meticulous perioperative management, hemorrhoidectomy can be performed safely even in patients aged ≥80 years.

A notable finding of this study was the occurrence of transient urinary retention in a subset of patients during hospitalization. However, no patients in either group reported urinary retention during the 6-month outpatient follow-up period. Postoperative urinary retention has been reported to occur in 1-52% of patients after anorectal surgery[14,15], and the incidence has been reported to be as high as 32.8% following hemorrhoidectomy[16]. Major risk factors for postoperative urinary retention include advanced age (≥65 years), male sex, BMI ≥20 kg/m2, excision of more than four hemorrhoids, and the need for additional analgesics[14]. In particular, advanced age has been associated with a 1.62-fold increased risk of urinary retention due to age-related declines in detrusor contractility, diminished bladder sensation, and delayed metabolism of anesthetic agents[14].

In the present study, postoperative urinary retention occurred significantly more frequently in the very old group than in the older group. However, postoperative urinary retention cannot be attributed solely to the surgical procedure and may be influenced by underlying patient-related factors, including prostate-related conditions. To explore this possibility as indicated by postoperative Foley catheter insertion, an exploratory subgroup analysis was performed using chi-square and Fisher's exact tests, in which no significant association was found between prostate-related conditions and postoperative urinary retention. These findings suggest that age-related physiological changes―such as reduced detrusor contractility, decreased bladder compliance, and diminished voiding efficiency―may contribute to the higher incidence of urinary retention observed in very old patients.

Nevertheless, prostate-related conditions were defined based on documentation in the medical records rather than standardized diagnostic criteria, such as symptom scores or urodynamic measurements. This might have resulted in misclassification, introducing bias to the subgroup analysis in either direction, and therefore warrants cautious interpretation of these findings.

Postoperative pain was another important clinical interest in this study. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the factors associated with postoperative pain requiring additional analgesia. In the multivariable analysis, age group, sex, squeezing pressure, hemorrhoid grade, and hemorrhoid count were not significantly associated with requests for supplemental analgesic injections. In contrast, the use of intravenous patient-controlled analgesia was independently associated with a reduced likelihood of needing additional analgesic injections (Table 4). This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that IV PCA effectively alleviates postoperative pain following hemorrhoidectomy[17-20].

Although previous studies have reported the safety of hemorrhoidectomy in older patients, most defined “older” as those aged ≥60 or ≥65 years, and only a limited number of studies have included sufficient patients aged ≥80 years[3,9,10,21]. The present study extends the existing literature by specifically examining short-term postoperative outcomes in patients aged ≥80 years undergoing hemorrhoidectomy under spinal anesthesia, thereby addressing an age group underrepresented in prior studies.

With increasing life expectancy, the demand for hemorrhoid surgery among very old patients is expected to increase[7,9,11]. In this context, the present study provides additional data on short-term postoperative outcomes following hemorrhoidectomy under spinal anesthesia in patients aged ≥80 years. In our cohort, the incidence of major Clavien-Dindo complications was 0%, and a single case of postoperative bleeding in the very old group was successfully managed with simple suturing without the need for reoperation under general anesthesia.

Hemorrhoidectomy performed under spinal anesthesia may be performed with acceptable short-term outcomes when patients are carefully selected and the overall physiological status, comorbidities, and anesthetic risk are thoroughly assessed. Furthermore, proactive evaluation and management of urinary function, together with appropriate postoperative pain control, including the use of PCA when indicated, may help reduce the incidence of minor complications and contribute to a smoother postoperative recovery in older and very old patients.

This study has some limitations. First, patients with severe comorbidities such as advanced cardiopulmonary disease or malignancy may have been excluded at the time of surgical decision-making, potentially introducing selection bias. Second, the retrospective design and single-center nature of the study may limit generalizability. Third, the sample size of the very old group was relatively small, which may have reduced the statistical power. Finally, although the 6-month follow-up period was sufficient to assess short-term complications, it may not fully capture long-term outcomes, particularly recurrence, which often requires up to 2 years of follow-up for a reliable assessment[22]. Future multicenter prospective studies including a larger cohort of very old patients are warranted to validate these findings. In conclusion, this study demonstrated comparable short-term (<6 months) postoperative outcomes between older and very old patients without severe systemic disease undergoing hemorrhoidectomy under spinal anesthesia. These findings contribute to the limited evidence regarding hemorrhoidectomy outcomes in very old patients and should be interpreted within the context of the patient population studied.
