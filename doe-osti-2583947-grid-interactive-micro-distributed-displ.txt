1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The goal of this project is to design, fabricate and validate the performance of an
economically viable next-generation self-contained, refrigerated open vertical display case
with integrated thermal storage that improves energy efficiency, enhances demand
flexibility, uses an environmentally friendly refrigerant, improves human comfort.

Specific objectives include:

 • A 30% permanent energy efficiency savings compared to current open case
 technology.
 • An 80% reduction in electric demand over a 2-hour period through discharging of
 the embedded TES. The request for 3 hours to 2 hour period was approved by
 DoE in Q2FY24.
 • A minimum of 50% reduction in the exfiltrated (spilled) refrigerated air to the
 surrounding space compared to the current technology to improve human comfort,
 and
 • A cost-optimal design with reduced operational costs that outweigh incremental
 investments in the novel elements of the case, with a maximum payback of 3 years
 for utilities with Time-Of-Use (TOU) or critical peak pricing.

The technical scope is broken into three budget periods:

 (1) Budget Period 1- Display case and integrated system analysis, design, and
 optimization
 (2) Budget Period 2 -TES-coupled HX and display case construction and
 characterization, and
 (3) Budget Period 3 - System integration and performance assessment.

Task 1.3 Hybrid Display Case Thermal Analysis

The project team developed detailed models of the refrigerated display case to refine
performance targets and develop specifications for the TES material, TES-coupled heat
exchanger (Task 1.4), and refrigeration circuits (Task 1.5). Additionally, we refined the
technical and market potential of this refrigeration system. A dynamic (time-dependent),
integrated, and experimentally validated model containing the thermo-fluids and
psychometrics within the case, food products, refrigeration cycle, glycol circuit, and thermal
energy storage system were developed and leveraged for assessing and predicting the
performance of the hybrid system.
The thermo-fluids heat extraction model considers conduction, convection, and radiation heat
transfer modes and predicts the product core and surface temperatures, air psychometrics,
and surface temperatures throughout the refrigerated display case. The model reflects the
geometry of an actual baseline refrigerated display case. In addition, the model was
calibrated and validated using measured experimental data of the baseline system, which
was collected at NREL’s controlled-environment thermal testing chamber.

Subtasks 1.3.1a, b, and c: Radiative Cooling, Low Airflow Convective Cooling and CFD
Modeling, and Hybrid Radiative and Convective Cooling

The goal of these subtasks was to develop a modeling strategy that captures the radiant and
convective cooling mechanisms of the proposed display case and to initiate model
development. To inform this effort, an extensive literature review was conducted to identify
robust modeling and analysis approaches. The literature review also identified publications
containing laboratory measurement data on the thermal performance characteristics of
baseline open vertical display cases, which will be used for model validation and calibration.

A visual summary of the developed modeling and validation strategy is shown in figure 4
below.

We developed a 2D steady-state resistive network model using the Engineering Equation
Solver (EES) platform. The model incorporates adjustable “levers” to modify parameters,
allowing us to represent both the baseline air curtain system and the proposed hybrid system.

This approach enables validation and calibration of the baseline model using reported
thermal performance data from literature and previous laboratory experiments. Once a robust
baseline model was established, the model parameters were adjusted to reflect the proposed
system configuration. A second, independent modeling approach using computational fluid
dynamics (CFD) was then employed to cross-check the predictions from the resistive network
model. At this stage, we performed model optimization and assessed the proposed system’s
efficiency relative to the baseline. We developed and validated the baseline resistive network
model using reported thermal performance data from the literature. A snapshot of the EES
diagram window, showing the experimental temperature measurements (in green) alongside
our model predictions (in black), is provided below in Figure 5.

Figure 5: A screenshot of the EES platform diagram window containing results of our baseline OVDC model and its comparison to
 reported air and product temperature measurements from the literature

In the model, chilled air exits the evaporator at the bottom of the display case and flows
upward through the back panel. A perforated section of this panel permits a fraction of the
total airflow, denoted as γ[i]ṁ RAG, to traverse horizontally through the shelves, thereby
enhancing product cooling. This traversing air ultimately rejoins the main air curtain stream at
the front of the case and descends toward the return air grille (RAG).

 8

As the air ascends through the back panel, it exchanges heat with both the rear-facing
products and the back wall of the case. This heat exchange is characterized by an equivalent
heat transfer coefficient that accounts for forced convection on the case interior, conduction
through the case envelope, and natural convection on the exterior. Air moving horizontally
across the shelves also exchanges heat with the products and the upper shelf surfaces.

A portion of the air discharged from the discharge air grille (DAG) interacts thermally with the
upper shelf surface before mixing with ambient infiltrated air. This mixed air continues
downward, transferring heat via forced convection to the front-facing products. Additionally,
direct conduction occurs between front and rear products where they are in contact.

The resistive network model shown in Figure 6, facilitates the simulation of thermal
interactions among the interior surfaces of the case, the surrounding environment, and the
stored products through multiple modes of heat transfer. The top surface of each product
exchanges heat with both the overlying shelf and the ambient surroundings, with
consideration of resistance related to radiation factors and surface emissivity. Furthermore,
the front surfaces of the front products are subject to radiative heat transfer from the
surrounding environment.

 Figure 6: Resistive network model formulation of an open-vertical refrigerated display case cabinet that considers conduction,
convection, and radiation heat transfer modes between the case, products, and surroundings. The schematics demonstrate the number
 of heat transfer resisters used in the model and their interactions.

Heat transfer coefficients, fractional mass flow rates, infiltration rate distribution per shelf, and
other variables of uncertainty in the model were calibrated to measured baseline
experimental data. A multivariable genetic optimization workflow was used to tune the
model’s variables of uncertainty. The optimization objective was minimization of mean
absolute error between measured and model-predicted air and product temperatures.

The integrated transient model predictions of core product temperatures at various locations
within the case, evaporator outlet temperature, discharge air grille temperature (DAG), and
return air grille (RAG) temperatures were compared and validated against experimentally
measured timeseries temperature readings. The transient model results in Figure 7 shows
the simulated and measured evaporator outlet air temperatures over a 24-hour period and
illustrate close agreement between modeled predictions and measured values. Similarly,
Figure 8 shows the evaporator inlet (RAG) temperatures over the same period and illustrates
very close agreement between modeled predictions and measured values. Close agreement
between modeled and measured data is also observed for the DAG temperature in Figure 9.
These results demonstrate the adequate capability of the integrated and transient baseline
system model to accurately predict the spatial and temporal variations of temperatures

addition to periods during, and post, defrost modes. Lastly, the modeled and measured

Subtask 1.3.1d: Refrigeration Cycle Design

A detailed model of the refrigeration circuit was developed using a low-global warming
potential (GWP) refrigerant, propane (R-290). In parallel, the additional cycle efficiency
benefits of R-290 were quantified in comparison to a commonly used hydrofluorocarbon

 14

refrigerant, R-404A. Figure 14 presents a comparison of the refrigeration cycle for the
baseline system versus the hybrid system incorporating a glycol circuit design.

In the baseline system, a constant-speed compressor cycles on and off to maintain a
predefined evaporator air outlet temperature setpoint. During defrost mode, the compressor
is turned off while the evaporator fan remains operational to melt accumulated frost from the
coil surfaces. In contrast, the hybrid system employs a variable-speed compressor that
maintains a constant glycol supply temperature to both the glycol-air cooling coil and the
radiant cooling panels integrated into the display case. Maintaining a constant glycol supply
temperature serves two key purposes:

 1. Product Temperature Stability – It enables precise control of product temperatures
 during normal operation, defrost, and post-defrost periods.
 2. Frost Mitigation – It prevents frost accumulation on the interior surfaces of the radiant
 panels by ensuring the glycol temperature remains at or above 0°C, regardless of the
 thermal energy storage (TES) system’s state of charge.

Notably, during defrost mode—and unlike the baseline system—the compressor remains
active to sustain the glycol supply temperature setpoint for the radiant panels, thereby
enhancing product temperature stability. At the same time, flow to the glycol-air cooling coil is
halted. The glycol circuit design, also illustrated in the figure, incorporates two constant-
volume pumps and two modulating three-way valves. These valves are actively controlled to
regulate glycol recirculation and maintain the required supply temperatures to the coil and
radiant panels.

Figure 14: a) Baseline, constant speed compressor, vapor-compression cycle using R-448A, and b) hybrid system vapor-compression
cycle using R-290, and glycol circuit used to maintain constant glycol temperature into the glycol-air cooling coil and glycol inlet to
 the display case for radiant cooling

An integrated refrigeration cycle model for both the baseline and hybrid display case systems
was developed, incorporating key subsystems including case heat extraction mechanisms,
thermal energy storage (TES), glycol circulation loops, and vapor compression cycle models,
as established under Subtask 1.3.1. The outcome is a validated, transient hybrid model that
serves as a comprehensive simulation platform for evaluating system performance under

various operating conditions. This integrated model was completed in Q4 2023 and
subsequently used to support final component selection and specification of equipment for

In parallel, a dedicated EES model was developed specifically to support sizing of individual

Subtask 1.3.1f: Integrated Case Performance Modeling and Building-Level Energy Impact

This subtask integrated Subtasks 1.3.1a through 1.3.1f to develop a comprehensive model
that quantifies the total thermal and energy performance of the hybrid display case over a 24-
hour period. The model was further enhanced under Task 1.5 by incorporating the TES-
coupled heat exchanger to inform system sizing, component selection, and performance
specification. To assess the building-scale impact of the hybrid system, we finalized a
calibrated and validated building energy model representing an actual, existing Albertsons
store. This model was developed in EnergyPlus using real-world equipment schedules for
HVAC, refrigeration, and lighting systems, as well as occupancy patterns derived from
Google Maps data. Monthly electricity and gas consumption were calibrated using utility
billing data for the Albertsons store over a one-year period to ensure realistic baseline energy
use.

The fully integrated, physics-based EES system model of the hybrid display case previously
validated was used to generate detailed system performance maps. These maps were then
integrated into the EnergyPlus building model to evaluate the hybrid technology’s impact at
the building level. Specifically, sensible and latent case credit maps were developed as
functions of zone dry-bulb temperature and relative humidity. These maps enabled dynamic
estimation of the hybrid display case's hourly performance as the simulation progressed
through an annual period. Figure 16 and 17 show the resulting hybrid system’s sensible and
latent case credits, respectively, as generated by the EES model.

Integration of Hybrid Display Case Performance into EnergyPlus Hourly Simulation Platform

Before integrating the hybrid display case performance curves into EnergyPlus, the baseline
display case evaporator cooling loads were thoroughly validated to ensure consistency with
measurement and verification (M&V) data. As part of the quality assurance and quality
control (QA/QC) process, these loads were also cross-referenced against the equipment
schedule to identify display cases suitable for replacement with the hybrid system. This step
was critical for assessing the hybrid system’s impact on both energy performance and indoor
environmental conditions. To accurately represent the hybrid system at a building scale, the
sensible and latent case credits were incorporated into the EnergyPlus whole-building energy
model. This allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of the system’s thermal and energy
performance.

 19

The objective of this task was to quantify the impact of the hybrid display case technology at
a whole-building level. To that end, the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE) prototypical
supermarket energy model was used as the foundation and then calibrated to match the
specifications of a real-world Albertsons store. Figure18 presents a comparison between the
actual Albertsons store floorplan (left) and the corresponding EnergyPlus building energy
model (right). To enhance model fidelity, equipment schedules for HVAC, refrigeration, and
lighting were extracted from the actual store and implemented in the simulation. Additionally,

Subtask 1.3.2: Surface Condensation Risk Mitigation Research

This task involved a preliminary literature review on the application of coatings in refrigeration
environments, followed by a comprehensive survey of commercially available anti-
condensation coatings. A selection criterion for desirable coating properties was jointly
developed by teams at NREL and Copeland. Product data was gathered through a
combination of company websites, technical data sheets, and direct communication with
manufacturers. Based on this assessment, three coatings were identified as top candidates.
Real-world verification testing was then conducted by applying these coatings to
representative samples from the display case. Based on performance results, specific
recommendations were developed for anti-condensation coatings to be used as a risk
mitigation strategy within this project.

Literature Review and Coating Selection Rationale

Our literature review supports the use of hydrophobic or superhydrophobic coatings to
mitigate condensation on display case walls. These coatings function by lowering surface
energy, thereby reducing moisture adhesion.

Hydrophobic surfaces resist water accumulation, whereas hydrophilic surfaces promote
wettability and retain water. Surface wettability is quantitatively assessed using contact
angle measurement, which determines the angle formed at the solid-liquid-air interface by a
droplet of known volume on a flat surface.

 23

 • A surface is classified as hydrophobic when the contact angle is ≥ 90°
 • A surface is superhydrophobic when the contact angle exceeds 150°
       (Reference: DOI: 10.1021/jz402762h)

Figure 21 illustrates the contact angle measurement concept and classification thresholds for
surface wettability.

 Figure 21: Example of contact angles for different surface types. Source: Nordson Electronics

Hydrophobic coatings have been shown to outperform both hydrophilic coatings and
uncoated surfaces in defrosting applications [DOI: 10.1016/j.rser.2017.01.120]. When
combined with surface micropatterning, these coatings exhibit enhanced performance,
including delayed condensation onset, extended time to frost formation, and reduced frost
layer thickness. In some cases, treated surfaces have remained frost-free at relative humidity
levels below 60%.

From edible and environmentally safe materials [DOI: 10.1021/acsami.6b06958; DOI:
10.1039/c5gc02725j]. Alternative strategies for addressing frosting have also been explored
in the literature [DOI: 10.1016/j.ijrefrig.2016.09.027].

Based on these findings and the specific requirements of this application, the following
criteria were established to evaluate the suitability of anti-condensation coatings for use in the
refrigerated display case:

 • Non-toxic composition, suitable for use in a food-handling environment
 • Commercial availability, as custom development is beyond the scope of this task
 • Operational performance within the refrigerated temperature range (0–20 °C)
 • Strong adhesion to common case materials (e.g., painted metal surfaces)
 • Visually acceptable appearance—preferably curing to a clear or white finish
 • Durability, with no evidence of flaking, peeling, or degradation over time

Additional consideration was given to coatings that have been successfully demonstrated in
refrigeration environments. Each candidate product was evaluated against the criteria above
and scored accordingly. Products meeting a criterion received one point per category.
Coatings with any toxicity concerns were disqualified from further consideration.

Subtask 1.4.1 Model TES

The numerical model for the PCM heat exchanger was formulated using a finite-difference
method. Similar models have been reported and validated in our prior research. The key
advantage of using the PCM heat exchanger model over experiments is that the model
predicts the performance of TES systems with different geometric parameters and under
various operating conditions, thereby minimizing the time consumption and expensive
experiments.

 32

In this study, we envisioned TES as a combination of various stackable units, with the TES
unit having a footprint of 18 in × 10 in (45.7 cm × 25.4 cm), which was decided based on the
dimension of the microchannels available off the shelf and the in-house PCC fabrication
capability. A schematic of a module is shown in
Figure 27. Given that the cross-sectional area is fixed, the thickness of the PCC slabs
becomes a crucial parameter because it determines the overall thermal resistance of the TES
unit, which eventually governs the maximum charging or discharging power (heat transfer
rate at which the TES can be charged or discharged).

 Figure 27: Thermal storage design, along testing of single-channel prototype (described in Task 1.4.3 below).

The two-dimensional finite-difference model is depicted in Figure 28 below. As in the
experimental prototype, the fluid flows through the microchannel having a length of 45.7 cm
and a width of 25.4 cm. The fluid stream was discretized into 30 subdomains along the length
of the microchannel. Likewise, the PCC slabs were discretized into 30 nodes along the length
and 15 nodes along the height. The energy balances were imposed at each fluid domain,
including the advection of the fluid, the heat transfer to the surrounding PCC elements, and
the energy storage in the fluid domain. The heat transfer rate between the fluid nodes and the
PCC nodes was approximated using the convective fluid resistance and the total conductive
resistance, including the conduction resistance of the PCM, the conduction resistance of the

 33

microchannel material, and the contact resistance. Although the contact resistance can vary
depending on the surface roughness, the applied spring force, and the phase of the PCC
slabs, we assumed a constant value of 4 × 10-5 K·m2/W for the modeling.

Figure 35 depicts the TES discharge performance in terms of the fluid inlet and outlet
temperatures versus time under various discharge rate conditions. The discharge C-rate is
defined as the inverse of the time in hours needed to fully discharge a TES system from an
initial fully charged state to a fully discharged state at a constant heat transfer rate (or discharge
power). In other words, a high discharge C-rate of a TES implies a high discharge power and
a shorter discharge time. Likewise, a TES charge C-rate implies the inverse of the time in hours
needed to fully charge a TES system at constant charging power. In these experiments, we
always charge the TES using a nearly constant chiller set point temperature (inlet fluid

 41

temperature varies between -16.2°C and -14.5°C); therefore, the charge power or charge C-
rate is variable. But during the discharge, we achieve a constant C-rate by fixing the
temperature difference to 4.5°C between the inlet and outlet fluid flow, continuously adjusting
the heating rate in the in-line heater and maintaining a predefined fluid mass flow rate.

As expected, the discharge time increases with a decrease in the discharge C-rate. All lines in
Figure 35 (a)–(d) terminate when the fluid outlet temperature reaches a predefined cutoff of -
5°C, indicating the end of the discharge process even though the TES might still have some
remaining energy storage. A predefined cutoff temperature is crucial for the refrigerated display
case, as it needs to maintain the temperature of the stored products under the required
conditions. Table 12 compares the discharge time of the TES under various discharge C-rate
conditions. Note that the discharge time here is calculated based on the time the fluid outlet
temperature reaches the cutoff temperature of -5°C. It is not the time needed to fully discharge
the latent thermal storage capacity of the TES. In other words, we use the additional sensible
energy stored in the TES while the fluid outlet temperature is still below the cutoff threshold. In
nearly all cases, except C/1.5, the discharge time for the fluid to reach the cutoff temperature
is predicted to be higher in the model than the experiments. This is due to the ambient heat
gain from the surroundings, which is not accounted for in the model, but it occurs in practice,
causing a faster discharge in the experiment than the idealistic modeling conditions. As the
discharge C-rate decreases, the discharge time increases, and thus the total ambient heat
increases. For a high C-rate (C/1.5), the difference between the model and the experimental
discharge time is nearly the same; however, as the discharge time increases, the discrepancy
between the model and the experimental results becomes larger.

Some key findings and observations of the study are summarized as follows:
• A two-dimensional finite-difference model was formulated to design a PCM-based thermal
  storage system and was later validated using experimental results. The TES system was
  designed in the form of stackable units of PCC slabs so that the required energy storage
  capacity can be achieved using multiple TES units.
• We noted the occurrence of an optimal thickness (3.2 cm) of the TES units, providing the
  maximum specific energy of 50.5 Wh/kg and a specific power of 15.4 W/kg during the
  discharging process. The specific energy significantly reduced whether the TES units had
  a thickness greater than a critical value of 6.4 cm or lower than 3.2 cm.
• Experiments performed under various charge and discharge rate conditions showed a
  good agreement with the modeling results, validating the benefits of the model in
  predicting the TES performance under various operating conditions. The TES discharge
  energy was also found to be nearly constant between 180 Wh and 190 Wh when the TES
  discharge rate was experimentally varied between C/1.5 to C/2.75, confirming the optimal
  thickness and design of the TES units.
• Cyclability tests over 25 cycles were also performed to evaluate the durability and
  consistency of the TES performance. Although we noted some variations in the TES
  discharge energy with the number of cycles, the variability was found to be small and
  within the experimental uncertainty.

Subtask 1.5.1b Optimize Case Design and TES Controls for Maximum Efficiency

The work within this subtask is focused on delivering the TES control scheme and final hybrid
system component specifications associated with the full-scale proof of concept display case.
This was completed by leveraging the integrated model of the hybrid display case that
includes the case heat extraction models, TES, glycol circuits, and vapor compression cycle
models from subtasks 1.3.1. An optimal TES control scheme was selected that maximizes
the overall efficiency of the display case operation during the TES charge and discharge
process.

The TES control strategy selection was informed by several parametric simulation runs that
varied the TES charging duration and charging rate depending on the mode of operation of
the refrigerated display case; namely, whether the display case was in defrost vs. non-defrost
mode.

The selected TES control strategy leverages the otherwise low compressor power
consumption during defrost to ramp up the TES charge rate during defrost modes. In doing
so, the control strategy avoids excessive peak compressor power consumption and takes
advantage of higher COP compressor operation.

Two separate TES sizes were established that meet a 2hr discharge period and a 3hr
discharge period. The TES control strategy is the same for both TES sizes, where the
charging duration is 13hrs long and encompasses three cooling coil defrost periods. The
compressor is off throughout the discharge period duration. During defrost periods, the TES
charging rate (c-rate) is -0.18hr-1, and during non-defrost the c-rate is 0.005hr-1, where the c-
rate is defined as:

to ambient air was minimal. However, products positioned near the front, directly exposed to
the chamber air, experienced insufficient cooling, with temperatures reduced by only about
7°F. Additionally, bottom-shelf products were overcooled and in some instances frozen, due
to excessive conductive heat transfer from the panel surface.

Considering the size of the c-PCM samples, a thermocouple was embedded in Block 1 to

Based on preliminary experiments, the following thermal cycling protocol was established to
ensure complete and repeatable phase transitions:

 1. Hold at –50 °C for 4 hours
 2. Ramp from –50 °C to –30 °C over 5 minutes
 3. Hold at –30 °C for 1 hour
 4. Ramp from –30 °C to 25 °C over 5 minutes
 5. Hold at 25 °C for 4 hours
 6. Ramp down from 25 °C to 0 °C over 5 minutes
 7. Hold at 0 °C for 1 hour
   8. Cool from 0 °C to –50 °C
 9. Repeat cycle

Figure 54 presents the change in thickness and mass of the composite materials over 150
thermal cycles. Both blocks exhibited noticeable expansion upon crystallization, attributed to
the presence of water in the SP-11 formulation. This expansion was found to be irreversible,
with dimensional growth reaching approximately 30% by the end of the cycling period.

 Figure 54: Thickness and mass change of the CPCM blocks during thermal cycling

Both blocks demonstrated excellent stability during thermal cycling, with no observable PCM
loss. Following completion of the 150-cycle test, the poly-nylon encapsulation bags were
removed, and the blocks were re-weighed. A comparison of the pre-encapsulated and post-
cycling block masses revealed no measurable difference, confirming that the PCM remained
fully retained within the expanded graphite (EG) matrix. This finding indicates that

 67

encapsulation is not required to prevent leakage, but rather to protect the PCM from
exposure to air and moisture.

As part of this task, a bench-scale cyclability test was conducted on prototype PCC slabs.
Results showed minimal degradation over repeated thermal cycles, validating the material
stability and structural integrity of the c-PCM under expected operating conditions. Based on
observations from a 1/6th system-scale experiment conducted by Copeland, significant
refrigerant maldistribution was identified within the test setup. As a result, the project team
determined that direct refrigerant use within the thermal energy storage (TES) design should
be eliminated at least in the near term to mitigate performance risk.

Consequently, an alternative design was developed in which glycol, rather than refrigerant,
was circulated through the TES system. In this configuration, a brazed-plate refrigerant-to-
glycol heat exchanger cools the glycol, which in turn circulates through the c-PCM storage
module. This approach simplified the system architecture by avoiding the complexities
associated with distributing two-phase refrigerant through microchannels. Single-phase glycol
distribution is significantly easier to manage and more reliable in practice.

To further de-risk the transition to a full-scale system, particularly given the new TES
footprint, which is twice the width of any system previously tested by NETenergy the team
determined that an additional bench-scale test was necessary. Accordingly, the team
collaborated closely with the manufacturing partner to finalize the design specifications for the
glycol-only bench-scale heat exchanger. In parallel, key design parameters for the full-scale
prototype were established, including the number of heat exchanger units and the required
thickness of the c-PCM slabs.
