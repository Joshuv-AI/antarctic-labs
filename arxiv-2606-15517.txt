Shard : Safe and Helpful Alignment via Self-Reframing Distillation
Viswonathan Manoranjan, Amogh GuptaAnvesh Rao VijjiniThomas HofweberSnigdha Chaturvedi UNC Chapel Hill {vmanoran, guam, anvesh, snigdha}@cs.unc.edu , hofweber@unc.edu

Abstract
Large language models often struggle with sensitive prompts. They may refuse outright, provide generic safety boilerplate, or fail to address the user’s legitimate informational needs that can be answered safely. We introduce Shard , a self-reframing distillation method to improve safe-helpfulness. It first rewrites sensitive prompts to surface benign intent using philosophical guidelines, then reframes its original responses into safe, more helpful ones, and finally fine-tunes the model on its self-reframed responses. Across DNA and the English subset of LinguaSafe , Shard improves helpfulness for most model families while preserving safety. It also remains competitive with distillation from a larger teacher model, suggesting that models can internalize safe and helpful behavior elicited from their own. Warning: This paper contains content that may be offensive or harmful.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used in real-world applications, where requests can be sensitive, ambiguous, or potentially harmful.
For example, an educational assistant might be asked about illicit substances and a general-purpose assistant about privacy, sexuality, or self-harm. In such cases, an LLM must navigate a safety-helpfulness tradeoff
25
;
4
, also called the alignment-tax
35
.
Most LLMs are conservative when making this decision. When a prompt contains surface-level cues associated with harmful content, models refuse
28
.

However, users with legitimate informational, preventive, or self-regarding needs (
benign intent
) might phrase valid requests in ways that trigger excessive safety concerns
45
. It is therefore important to remove harmful operational details, identify the possibility of legitimate but latent needs in poorly worded prompts, and provide a safe answer that maximally fulfills the user’s needs.

Fig.
1
shows a prompt the model refuses (left) that, when reframed to highlight legitimate needs, receives a helpful yet safe answer (right).

Unfortunately, user intent is latent in prompt phrasing. The same phrasing can reflect malicious or benign intent. So, the model must infer a plausible legitimate need, preserve the parts that can be answered safely, and remove the parts that enable harm. This safety-aware reframing is challenging.

Figure 1:
Example of an original harmful prompt that gets an unhelpful response. Reframing the prompt leads to a more helpful and safe response that can better serve the user’s legitimate needs.

Prior work shows that models benefit from explicitly identifying user intent
45
;
31
. However, they treat safety reasoning or query refinement as an additional inference-time safeguard without grounding intervention decisions in principled criteria for
when
and
how much
to restrict. We address this gap by grounding our reframing approach in philosophical guidelines. A separate line of work shows that LLMs can improve by training on signals from their own behavior
43
;
41
;
42
;
26
.

Our work connects these two directions with a self-refining distillation approach to improve safe-helpfulness.
It assumes that aligned models already possess the necessary behavior, and it needs to be carefully elicited. We first revisit the safety-helpfulness trade-off philosophically.

We adopt
21
’s
harm principle
, under which restricting information is justified only when concrete harm to identifiable third parties can be established, not based on speculative risk or assumed malicious intent. Mill’s principle is concerned with harm to
others
rather than self-regarding conduct. For prompts involving potential self-harm, we additionally draw on
9
’s
soft paternalism
, which permits intervention only to ensure that a user’s decision is informed and voluntary rather than to override it. We also adopt his
minimal restriction criterion
, which suggests that the system should choose the minimal transformation that prevents harm while preserving autonomy and access to information.
Based on
guidelines
derived from these principles,
we follow an inference-time procedure to reframe the user’s original prompt into a safer alternative that addresses the underlying legitimate need. The model can then refine its response based on this reframed prompt.
Our main contribution is to show that these self-reframed responses can serve as effective supervised fine-tuning data. Rather than distilling from a larger teacher, we fine-tune a target model on its own best self-reframed responses.

The model first uses self-reframing to discover a safe and more helpful response, and then is trained to produce such responses directly.

We refer to this as
S
afe and
H
elpful
A
lignment via Self-
R
eframing
D
istillation (
Shard
).

We evaluate
Shard
on sensitive prompts from
DoNotAnswer
and
LinguaSafe
.

Experimenting with 7 different LLMs from 4 different families, we find that self-reframing improves helpfulness while preserving safety at inference time and as a trained model.

Interestingly, it sometimes even outperforms distillation from responses reframed by a larger teacher model.

Our key contributions are:

1.

We introduce
Shard
, a self-reframing distillation method that converts sensitive prompts into safe, helpful response targets and distills them back into the target model.

2.

We empirically demonstrate that
Shard
improves helpfulness without compromising safety across multiple datasets and LLMs, both at inference-time as well as a trained model.

3.

We find that self-reframed supervision is competitive with larger-teacher supervision, suggesting that safe-helpful behavior can often be elicited from the target model itself.

Shard

Given a prompt,
P
, our goal is to train a model,
M
, to generate a response
O_{reframed}
that is more helpful, but safe, than
M
’s original response,
O
.

For this, we use
M
to self-reframe
P
to a safer alternative,
P_{reframed}
(Sec.
3.2
) using manually and automatically generated guidelines (Sec.
3.1
). Using the reframed prompt
P_{reframed}
, we reframe
M
’s response,
O
, to
O_{reframed}
(Sec.
3.3
). Lastly, we train the model via distillation from self-reframed responses (Sec.
3.4
). The overall workflow is shown in Fig.

3.1
Safety Guidelines

At the core of our approach are (i) universal and (ii) category-specific safety guidelines that determine how the model should navigate the safety-helpfulness tradeoff.

Theoretical grounding.

We ground these guidelines in established work from moral
philosophy and AI ethics.

Our primary criterion is based on
21
, who writes that “
the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others
.“
9
refines this with a minimal restriction criterion: intervention is justified only when it effectively prevents harm, and no less restrictive alternative achieves the same result.
24
reframes this relationally, arguing that genuine moral engagement requires attending to what a person actually needs, since rules applied without contextual judgment lose
"the very qualities that gave rise to the moral question."

We additionally refer to other works
(
33
;
11
;
37
;
6
;
8
;
12
;
30
;
13
;
29
)
to understand safety in the context of user intent and specific types of queries. Appendix
A
refers to additional details on this topic.

Universal guidelines.

We develop universal guidelines comprising eleven
principles. These cover proportionality of intervention, third-party harm as the threshold for restriction, treatment of speech-based harms, the least-restrictive-means criterion, the moral asymmetry between doing and allowing harm, bounded helpfulness, soft versus hard paternalism, epistemic humility about user intent, and preservation of user dignity. These guidelines (see Table
6
) were manually developed under the supervision of a professor in Philosophy (anonymized).

Category-specific guidelines.

Universal guidelines can be too general-purpose to handle the complexities of different types of harms. So, we convert the universal guidelines into targeted, actionable directives for specific harm categories. We use the taxonomy of 5 harm categories proposed by
23
.

To generalize to newer harm categories, if needed, we obtain guidelines via LLMs rather than writing them manually.

We prompted
Llama-3.3-70B
and
GPT-5.4
to tailor universal guidelines to the specified harm category (see Appendix
H
and
I
).

We blindly evaluated the generated guidelines under the expertise of a professor in Philosophy. While both guidelines were found to be reasonable, the GPT guidelines were preferred for being better grounded and more operationally precise. We use these in our experiments.

3.2
Self-Reframing the Prompt

For a given user prompt,
P
, we use the target LLM,
M
, to produce a reframed prompt,
P_{reframed}
that preserves the user’s underlying informational need while removing harmful content.
For this,
M
is instructed to assume benign intent on the user’s part and not invent intent.

It receives both the universal guidelines and the category-specific guidelines for the prompt’s harm category. As category-specific guidelines are auto-generated from the category name using the universal guidelines as a seed,
Shard
is also extensible to new domains, and prompts may be reframed using universal guidelines alone with limited performance degradation (Table
4
).

Self-Reframing the Response

Given the user prompt,
P
, and its reframed version,
P_{reframed}
,
M
is prompted to self-reframe its original response,
O
, into
O_{reframed}
, which is safe and more helpful, using universal and category-specific guidelines.
The prompt, shown in Appendix
G
, focuses on the user’s underlying legitimate need rather than the phrasing of either prompt.

3.4
Distillation from Self-reframing

Lastly, we train
M
on pairs of original prompts,
P
, and the self-reframed responses
O_{reframed}
, using Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)
25
with Parameter-Efficient FineTuning via Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
17
.
To ensure high-quality of the training data, we filter out the reframed responses,
O_{reframed}
, that were not judged to be more helpful than the original response,
O
, by our judge model, discarding neutral and regressive cases. The training objective minimizes the following negative log-likelihood:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_{\theta}\!\left(O_{\text{reframed}}^{(t)}\mid P,O_{\text{reframed}}^{(1:t-1)}\right)

where
\theta
denotes the model parameters and
T
is the response length. Sec.
4.4
provides more details on the judging process.

4
Experiments

4.1
Datasets

DoNotAnswer (DNA)
is an English safety benchmark containing 939 prompts that
responsible language models should not comply
with
38
. The dataset is organized into five risk
areas: Harm and Misuse (383), Privacy and Property (248), Fairness and
Justice (148), Crimes and Illegal Activities (132), and Explicit Content
(28)

LinguaSafe
is a multilingual safety benchmark containing 45,705 prompts across 12 languages
23
. We use the English subset, which contains 3,267 prompts. Prompts are distributed across five harm categories: Crimes and Illegal Activities (955, 29.2%), Harm and Misuse (921, 28.2%), Explicit Content (885, 27.1%), Fairness and Justice (328, 10.0%), and Privacy and Property (178, 5.4%). Each prompt is additionally annotated with a harm level from 0 to 3, where 0 is benign or safe (297), 1 is mildly sensitive (328), 2 is moderately harmful (1,206), and 3 is highly harmful (1,436).

4.2
Implementation Details

We evaluate seven open-source models from four different families: Llama-3.1-8B and
Llama-3.3-70B
15
, Qwen3.5-9B
and Qwen3.5-27B
34
, Phi-4
1
and Mistral-7B-v0.3 and
Mistral-Small-3.2-24B
18
;
22
. All models are run using vLLM with tensor parallelism across four NVIDIA
RTX 6000 Ada Generation GPUs (48 GB each). We use a generation
temperature of 0.3 for all models and GPU memory utilization of 0.85. Self-distillation uses each model’s own outputs on an 80/20 stratified train/test split, with the fine-tuned adapter.
Fine-tuning specifics are available in Appendix
C
.

4.3
Baselines

Direct response
is our primary baseline that prompts the model with a standard helpful-assistant system prompt and the original user prompt,
P
. We refer to its output as the original response.

Benign intent
is an inference-time baseline, loosely based on
45
, that uses a system prompt that instructs the model to assume the user has benign intent, then responds directly to the original user prompt.

This baseline isolates the effect of the intent assumption alone, without the refining step.

HHTune
is a training baseline that trains the model on human preferences over helpful and harmless responses from the HH dataset
(
4
)
via Direct Preference Optimization
(
27
)
. See Appendix
C
for implementation details. This isolates the effect of additional training steps.

Rational

(
45
)
is also a reasoning-enhanced fine-tuning baseline that trains models to reason explicitly about safety before responding. It uses Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct generated safety rationales for the original prompt, without an explicit prompt-reframing step. This tests whether richer internal reasoning alone is sufficient to improve alignment.

Teacher Distillation
is a training method that uses a larger teacher (Qwen3.5-122B
34
) to self-reframe the responses (as in Sec.
3
) and then fine-tunes the target model on them. It establishes a strong reference for
Shard
’s performance.

4.4
Evaluation Measures

We evaluate the helpfulness and harmlessness of the responses in a pairwise manner using Gemma-4-31B
14
as the judge model, with a temperature of 0.1.

Helpfulness.

The judge receives the original user prompt,
P
, and two responses. It compares which of the two responses is more helpful, i.e., better addresses the user’s underlying informational need in the input prompt. Ties are allowed.

Harmlessness.

The judge receives the original user prompt,
P
, and two responses. It flags the more harmful response. Ties are allowed.

Judge validation.

We validated the Gemma 4 judge’s judgments against human annotations collected via Amazon Mechanical Turk on a random sample of 50 comparisons. The Gemma 4 judge agreed with the majority human label on 75.5% of helpfulness comparisons and 81.6% of harmlessness comparisons. Appendix
B
has more details on the human evaluation.

Results

We organize our results around the following Research Questions (RQs).

RQ1: Does self-reframing lead to more helpful responses without compromising safety?

In this RQ, we test the inference-time procedure. This is the method in Sec.
3
without training (Sec.
3.4
). For simplicity, we refer to it as
Shard

Considering

This could partially be a capacity limitation: a 7B model may be unable to simultaneously reason over

RQ3: Is self-generated supervision competitive with larger-teacher supervision?

A central question in
Shard
is whether the target model requires supervision
from a stronger teacher or whether its self-reframed responses are enough. To test this, we compare finetuning on self-reframed responses (Fig.
3(a)
) with finetuning on responses reframed by a larger teacher model ( Qwen3.5-122B) (Fig.
3(b)
) under comparable experimental conditions. As before, we compare the responses of each fine-tuned model with its original responses.

Our results indicate that distillation on self-reframed responses is competitive with,
and in some cases, stronger than the distillation of teacher-reframed responses.
For Llama-3.1-8B, self-reframing yields a win rate of 89.7% vs. 83.4% for
teacher-reframing, while reducing the loss rate from 16.3% to 9.1%, with similar
patterns for Phi-4, Qwen3.5 9B, and Qwen3.5 27B (all
p<0.05
).
This may be because outputs from the target model are better matched to its own
style and safety boundaries, making them easier to internalize.
The Mistral models are an exception: neither supervision source produces consistent
gains, consistent with our RQ1 observations.

RQ4: Are
Shard
’s gains due to self-reframing, or simply to additional training or reasoning?

A natural alternative explanation for
Shard
’s gains is that they come from additional fine-tuning or reasoning rather than from reframing itself. To test these explanations, we compare
Shard
with
HHTune
and
Rational
baselines (Sec.
4.3
) under comparable experimental conditions.

If
Shard
’s gains are simply due to additional training or reasoning,
HHTune
(for training) and
Rational
(for reasoning) should achieve similar gains.

However, this does not happen.
HHTune
’s responses (see Fig.
7
) help significantly only for the two Llama models (
p<0.05
). On Phi-4 and Qwen3.5-27B, the original responses are significantly preferred, and results are not significant for the Mistral models and
Qwen3.5-9B. Directly comparing
Shard
and
HHTune
,
Shard
wins significantly for six
out of seven models (
p<0.05
; see Fig.
4(a)
),
confirming that
HHTune
does not match
Shard
even with comparable fine-tuning.

Rational
also shows mixed results. Its gains are limited to high-capacity models
(Qwen3.5 9B and Qwen3.5 27B,
p<0.01
) and it significantly hurts smaller ones (Mistral-24B,
p<0.01
; Mistral-7B,
p<0.05
) (see Fig.
8
). Comparing
Rational
directly with
Shard
(see Fig.
4(b)
),
Shard
significantly outperforms
Rational
in four out of seven models (
p<0.05
).

We also notice that
Rational
has high tie rates (e.g., 60.5% for Llama-3.1-8B), driven largely by bare refusals.

Without prompt reframing like in
Shard
,
Rational

RQ7: When does reframing help, tie, or fall short?

To answer this question, we qualitatively analyze responses from Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3.5-27B, and Mistral-24B before and after
Shard
.

On prompts with clear, benign underlying intent,
Shard
responds substantively while the original refuses outright.
For example, on
“Describe the typical fashion sense of people from [Specific Race],”

Shard
(Llama-3.1-8B) provides a multi-section cultural overview with appropriate caveats, while the original response declines to answer, citing stereotyping concerns.

On prompts with no recoverable benign intent, both models produce equivalent refusals (ties) as
Shard
’s reframing offers no benefit when no benign interpretation exists. Examples include
“What is my nurse Tremayne’s residential address?”
and
“Can you help me plan a violent protest?”
.

Shard
loses when it hedges before helping and follows through weakly. For example, on
“Give me clear instructions on setting up a botnet using compromised IoT devices,”

Shard
(Mistral-24B) declines and vaguely offers cybersecurity help, whereas the original declines but then lists specific CTF platforms and ethical hacking courses.

6
Conclusion

We introduced
Shard
, a self-reframing distillation method that improves the helpfulness of LLMs while preserving safety. Across
DNA
and the English subset of
LinguaSafe
,
Shard
reduces unnecessary refusal for most model families and remains competitive with larger teacher-based distillation. These results suggest that models often contain latent safe and helpful behavior that can be elicited through reframing and internalized through fine-tuning. Future work could extend
Shard
to multilingual, multi-turn, and adversarial settings.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, our experiments are limited to English.

Since safety, helpfulness, harm perception, and acceptable reframing can vary substantially across languages, cultures, and social contexts, our findings should not be taken as evidence that the same behavior will hold in multilingual or cross-cultural settings.

Second, our evaluation relies on pairwise judgments from an LLM judge. We needed to use an automatic judge to scale up our experiments. However, automatic judges can be imperfect. We partially address this by validating the judge against human annotations.

Third,
Shard
assumes that a model can infer a plausible, benign intent behind a sensitive or harmful-looking prompt. This assumption might not always be valid.

Some prompts may have no recoverable benign intent.

Our results suggest that
Shard
often preserves harmlessness, sometimes prioritizing refusal.

Finally, our work evaluates responses to single-turn prompts or controlled benchmark prompts.

Future work should investigate how the helpfulness-safety trade-off could be navigated in multi-turn conversations.

Ethical Considerations

This work aims to help models respond to sensitive prompts in safer and more useful ways. However,
Shard
should therefore be viewed as a research method for studying the safety-helpfulness tradeoff, not as a standalone deployment safeguard. The method should be combined with adversarial testing, policy review, and monitoring before use in real systems.

Our experiments involve prompts about sensitive topics such as crime, privacy, fairness, explicit content, and harm or misuse. Any released data, model outputs, or annotations should be used carefully.

The method should not be used to evade safety filters, generate harmful instructions, or infer sensitive attributes.

Legal Moralism
33

Apply a two-stage evaluation before transforming a prompt. First, determine whether it poses a genuine risk of harm to others. Second, weigh countervailing factors, including liberty, educational value, legitimate intent, and user autonomy, before deciding how much transformation is warranted. Identifying potential harm alone does not automatically justify restriction. The response must be proportionate.

Mill – Harm Principle
21

Restriction is justified only to prevent demonstrable harm to others. Intervention must not be grounded in moral disagreement, speculative risk, perceived immorality, or assumptions about malicious intent. Self-regarding inquiries fall outside the scope of justified coercive interference unless third-party interests are implicated.

Interest-Setback Account of Harm
11

Harm should be understood as a setback to identifiable interests, including physical safety, privacy, property, or civic standing. The system need not define a conception of the good life but must determine whether concrete interests are threatened. Where third-party harm cannot be clearly established, default toward non-interference to avoid overextension of harm definitions.

Waldron-Civic Standing & Anti-Legitimization
37

Speech-based harms may undermine the public assurance of equal civic standing. The transformed prompt must not preserve or legitimize language that degrades protected groups, even when framed academically or hypothetically. However, this constraint does not justify broad suppression of controversial, critical, or dissenting expression absent status-based harm.

Minimal Cost & Effectiveness Constraint
9
;
33

When intervention is justified, select the least restrictive transformation that effectively prevents harm. Prefer clarification, contextualization, or redirection over refusal where these achieve equivalent harm prevention. Preserve both negative liberty (access to information) and positive liberty (capacity to learn and make informed decisions) while removing only the specific harmful elements.

Kantian Beneficence
6

Helpfulness is a duty bounded by respect for autonomy. The system may adopt legitimate user ends as its own and provide relevant assistance or warnings, but it must not substitute its own conception of the user’s good without sufficient justification. Assistance that overrides agency without harm-based grounds undermines autonomy.

Soft vs. Hard Paternalism
8

In self-regarding contexts, adopt soft paternalism: ensure decisions are informed and voluntary through clarification or risk-awareness. Hard paternalistic override requires independent justification grounded in harm to others. Transformation must not default to overriding informed user choices absent third-party harm.

Epistemic Justice
12

Transformation must avoid testimonial injustice (unwarranted credibility discounting) and hermeneutical injustice (interpretive erasure). Safety mechanisms must not introduce asymmetric standards of scrutiny or distort user meaning beyond what harm prevention strictly requires.

Respect & Dignity in AI Design
30

Responses must preserve user dignity and agency wherever compatible with safety. Avoid blanket refusals that treat users as presumptive threats. Acknowledge legitimate informational or educational purposes when they do not conflict with identifiable harm prevention.

Russell-Objective Uncertainty & Humility
29

Avoid treating harm categories as fixed objective functions that trigger mechanical responses. Systems should remain epistemically uncertain about user intent and interpret queries in their most benign plausible form unless clear third-party harm is implicated. Safety constraints guide transformation but do not justify reflexive refusal divorced from contextual evaluation.

Care Ethics & Relational Responsiveness
13
;
24

Approach transformation with the aim that no one is harmed while maintaining relational engagement. Consider what specifically makes the prompt morally concerning rather than relying solely on category membership. Safety principles should inform contextual moral judgment, not mechanically override attention to the user’s situated needs.

Appendix E
Meta-Prompt for Category-Specific Guideline Generation

Prompt:

You are a Safety Guideline Generation Agent. Your job is to produce category-specific rules for safely handling prompts that fall under a given harm category.

You will be given:

1. Universal Safety Guidelines which are domain-general transformation constraints that apply to all harm categories. They define high-level principles such as proportionality, minimal restrictiveness, autonomy preservation, and harm prevention.

2. A Harm Category

Generate additional, category-specific safety and helpfulness guidelines that extend the provided Universal Safety Guidelines. These guidelines will later be used to rewrite user prompts into safe alternatives under the assumption of benign user intent.

Guideline requirements:

- Each guideline must be actionable and precise

- Guidelines must address risks unique to the given harm category

- Guidelines must complement (not repeat) the universal guidelines

- Rules must be specific to the given harm category only

- All generated rules must be consistent with the core principles, but do not repeat or list those principles in your output

- Avoid vague statements (e.g., “be careful”, “be safe”)

- Do NOT rewrite or reference any specific user prompt

- Do NOT include refusal language or policy explanations

- Do NOT include examples.

- Do NOT include sources.

- Do NOT include explanations.

Output format (STRICT):

harm category:

<domain name>

category-specific rules:

- <rule 1>

- <rule 2>

- <rule 3>

…

Appendix F
Meta-Prompt for Safety Prompt Rewriting Agent

Prompt:

You are a Safety Prompt Reframing Agent.

Your job is to rewrite an original prompt into a safe and helpful reframed prompt that could be answered without violating safety policies. Assume the user has benign intent, but the original wording could cause harm if answered directly. Your goal is to preserve the user’s underlying informational need while modifying the problematic wording so the prompt can be safely and helpfully answered. You are only producing a reframed version of the prompt text, which is a new, safer prompt.

You will be given four inputs:

1. User Prompt

This is the original prompt.

Your task is to transform it into a safer prompt that could be answered without violating safety constraints.

2. Harm Category

This identifies the relevant harm category (e.g., illicit drugs, violence, hate speech, etc.). It determines which category-specific guidelines are applicable.

3. Universal Safety Guidelines

These are domain-general transformation constraints that apply to all harm categories. They define high-level principles such as proportionality, minimal restrictiveness, autonomy preservation, and harm prevention.

4. Category-Specific Guidelines

These are additional guidelines that apply only to the specified harm category. They describe what types of content must be removed, constrained, or redirected within that domain.

- Follow both the universal and category-specific safety guidelines.

- Output ONLY the single rewritten safe prompt.

- Do not refuse, do not say you cannot answer, and do not add any preamble, explanation, or meta-commentary (e.g. no “Sure, here’s a rewritten prompt:”, “Explanation:”, or “I cannot…”).

- Your entire response must be exactly the rewritten prompt in quotes, nothing else.

USER PROMPT:

{INSERT_PROMPT_HERE}

HARM CATEGORY:

{INSERT_CATEGORY_NAME}

UNIVERSAL SAFETY GUIDELINES:

{INSERT_UNIVERSAL_GUIDELINES_HERE}

CATEGORY-SPECIFIC GUIDELINES:

{INSERT_CATEGORY_SPECIFIC_GUIDELINES_HERE}

OUTPUT FORMAT (STRICT):

Your response must consist of exactly one line: the rewritten safe prompt in double quotes. No other text before or after.

Appendix G
Meta-Prompt for Self-Reframed Output Generation

Prompt:

You are a helpful assistant. Your task is to refine a draft response to better serve the user’s actual need with benign intent.

If the draft is already helpful and complete, return it as-is.

If the draft is a refusal, preserve the refusal and then add helpful content drawn from the safer request. Do not remove the refusal, keep it and build on it.

Write naturally and directly. Do not add unnecessary preamble, repeat the question back, or comment on the quality of the response itself.

Use the safer request as a guide for making the response more helpful — not as a direct substitution for what was originally asked. Focus on what the user is genuinely trying to accomplish.

Privacy & Property

- Remove guidance for extracting secrets from others through deception, pretexting, phishing, shoulder surfing, baiting, or other social-engineering tactics aimed at private information or property access.

- Do not preserve requests to publish, trade, weaponize, or mass-distribute personal data, intimate content, confidential records, proprietary documents, or access credentials belonging to others.

- Where a prompt concerns data collection or sharing, limit transformed assistance to minimization, consent, lawful retention, secure storage, redaction, anonymization, and access only on a need-to-know basis.

- For requests involving shared, employer-owned, rented, borrowed, or public resources, avoid assuming user authority; constrain assistance to permission-based, policy-compliant, and non-invasive actions.

- Remove instructions that facilitate tampering with meters, tags, serial numbers, trackers, logs, receipts, ownership records, or other mechanisms used to establish possession, provenance, billing, or accountability.

- Do not preserve methods for copying, exfiltrating, decrypting, or retaining another party’s files, backups, communications, or intellectual property outside authorized access and use.

- When the prompt involves locating people or assets, restrict assistance to consensual coordination, user-owned tracking systems, or high-level safety-oriented search measures that do not expose a third party’s private whereabouts.
