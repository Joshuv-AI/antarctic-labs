Abstract
Turbulent Radiative Mixing Layers (TRMLs) are of fundamental importance to the transport of energy and momentum in multi-phase, astrophysical fluids. We use measurements of the “micro” and “macro” properties of these layers in high-resolution AthenaK simulations to investigate when their properties can be considered well -resolved. In particular, we demonstrate that the previously noticed resolution independence of total cooling, E ˙ cool \dot{E}_{\rm cool} , in these simulations is due to a remarkable, and perhaps fortuitous, cancellation of the countervailing effects of numerical dissipation and numerical viscosity. This calls into question the degree to which we can trust the results of these experiments, as there is no physical picture that explains this cancellation. We also demonstrate that in order to correctly resolve the phase structure in these layers, important for accurate predictions of their observable properties, one must resolve the scale on which turbulent diffusion acts on time-scales comparable to the cooling time. This “turbulent Field length”, λ F , turb \lambda_{\rm F,turb} , is where the eddy turnover time is equal to the cooling time ( t eddy ​ ( λ F , turb ) = t cool t_{\rm eddy}(\lambda_{\rm F,turb})=t_{\rm cool} ). We demonstrate that resolving this scale results in converged phase-structure and spatially resolved transitions in the gas phases.



I
Introduction

Astrophysical fluid dynamics is almost always in the regime where the size of the system of interest,
L
, is much larger than the ‘diffusive’ scales on which fluid elements mix together in a microphysical manner. In the case of diffusion of momentum, this means astrophysics involves high Reynolds number (turbulent) flows. As the sources of energy in astrophysics are usually highly structured in time and space, and diffusion of that energy through the system is relatively slow (the diffusive scales are small) astrophysics is also inherently the study of ‘multi-phase’ fluids where high and low specific-energy gas live in close proximity. Crucially, turbulence induces an ‘effective’ diffusivity in astrophysical gas by drastically enhancing the surface area for micro-physical interaction (stretching-enhanced diffusion)
(

21
;
48
)
. This effective diffusivity is naturally much larger than the micro-physical diffusivity in the system, so that turbulence is the dominant mechanism allowing for energy to be communicated between the phases of astrophysical fluids.

It is not surprising, then, that interface regions between fluid phases where turbulence acts to mix the phases and some subsequent reaction or thermal instability occurs, are ubiquitous in astrophysics. Numerical Experiment Design

We employ the GPU-accelerated, magneto-hydrodynamical (MHD) code

(
57

to simulate the Euler Equations of ideal hydrodynamics with a cooling source term applied to the energy equation, detailed in

III.2
Cooling Function

We employ a source term to the energy equation in order to represent thermal heating and cooling, implemented in an operator-split fashion. Our source term is very similar to that of
8
: it guarantees two thermally stable phases at
T_{\rm hot}
and
T_{\rm cold}
and has a cooling rate which peaks at an intermediate temperature
T_{\rm pk}\equiv\left(T_{\rm cold}^{2}T_{\rm hot}\right)^{1/3}
with a power-law fall-off in cooling rate on either side of this peak, as shown in
Figure 2
. We employ this simplified cooling function in order to investigate a minimally complicated test scenario for TRMLs, so that the complications of complex features in realistic cooling functions do not obscure the fundamental fluid physics we are trying to investigate.

Specifically, the cooling rate density is taken to be a function of pressure and temperature as

\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm cool}(P,T)=\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm max}(P)\left(\frac{T}{T_{\rm pk}}\right)^{-\beta(T)}

(10)

where

\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm max}(P)\equiv\frac{P_{0}/(\gamma-1)}{t_{\rm cool,min}}\left(\frac{P}{P_{0}}\right)^{2}

(11)

is the maximum cooling rate and
t_{\rm cool,min}
is the minimum cooling time, both at a given pressure. We define

\beta(T)=\begin{cases}\beta_{\rm lo}&T<T_{\rm pk}\\
\beta_{\rm hi}&T>T_{\rm pk}\end{cases}\,,

(12)

with
\beta_{\rm lo}<0
,
\beta_{\rm hi}>0
in general. We choose
(\beta_{\rm lo},\,\beta_{\rm hi})=(-2,\,3)\times 2/\log_{10}(\chi)
. The
\chi
dependence in
\beta_{\rm lo}
and
\beta_{\rm hi}
is included so that
\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm cool}(T_{\rm cold})
and
\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm cool}(T_{\rm hot})
are identical across simulations with the same value of
\xi
.

The heating rate density is given by

\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm heat}=c_{\rm heat}\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm max}(P)f_{h}(T)

(13)

where

c_{\rm heat}=\left(\frac{T_{\rm cold}}{T_{\rm pk}}\right)^{(\beta_{\rm hi}-\beta_{\rm lo})\left(1+\frac{\log(T_{\rm cold}/T_{\rm pk})}{\log(\chi)}\right)}

(14)

and

f_{h}(T)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{T}{T_{\rm pk}}\right)^{\alpha_{\rm heat}}&T<T_{\rm lim}\\
\left(\frac{T_{\rm lim}}{T_{\rm pk}}\right)^{\alpha_{\rm heat}}\left(\frac{T}{T_{\rm lim}}\right)^{-\beta_{\rm hi}-1/2}&T>T_{\rm lim}\end{cases}

(15)

with

\alpha_{\rm heat}=\left(\beta_{\rm lo}-\beta_{\rm hi}\right)\left(\frac{\log(T_{\rm cold}/T_{\rm pk})}{\log(\chi)}\right)-\beta_{\rm hi}

(16)

and
T_{\rm lim}\equiv 1.05\times T_{\rm hot}
intended as a ‘limiting temperature.’ We refer to it this way because the second clause in
Equation 15
is meant to decrease the heating rate more rapidly as one goes to temperatures above
T_{\rm lim}
so that eventually cooling is dominant again. While this is included in principle to prevent shocks in the hot gas from creating too high temperature, it is never an important term in practice. To reiterate,
c_{\rm heat}
and
\alpha_{\rm heat}
are generally chosen in order to assure
\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm cool}(P_{0},T_{\rm cold})=\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm heat}(P_{0},T_{\rm cold})
and
\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm cool}(P_{0},T_{\rm hot})=\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm heat}(P_{0},T_{\rm hot})
, so that we have two thermally stable phases.

The quadratic dependence on
P
in
Equation 11
is included to mimic the typical density squared dependence of collisional cooling terms in astrophysical gases, though this analogy is limited given that this dependence is also applied to the heating terms, which typically have a linear dependence on density
(
15
)
. Regardless, our choice of cooling function is idealized and only meant to be representative of a generalized physics problem, the inclusion or exclusion of this term is not expected to impact the main conclusions of this work. The total source term to the energy equation is given by
\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm heat}(P,T)-\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm cool}(P,T)
.

Frame Tracking

In order to make sure that the mixing layer remains within the bounds of our simulation domain we employ Galilean velocity boosts in the
z
-direction to track the front. Specifically, every
10
simulation time steps we measure the mean

of peak cooling gas

\left\langle v_{z}\right\rangle_{\rm pk}={\rm avg}\left(\left\{v_{z}(\mathbf{x}):1/f_{w}<T(\mathbf{x})/T_{\rm pk}<f_{w}\right\}\right)\,,

(17)

with

-\left\langle v_{z}\right\rangle_{\rm pk}
to the entire domain. If this velocity shift would bring the layer too close to the upper (
0.8\,L_{\rm box}
) or lower (
-0.4\,L_{\rm box}
) boundary of the domain, we apply a velocity shift away from the boundary that is proportional to the distance between the layer and the boundary. We also limit this velocity shift to

The diffusive velocity,

, is meant to represent the velocity at which gas is moving into a cooling interface on small scales. We can measure this velocity in our simulations by selecting gas near the peak cooling temperature,
T_{\rm pk}
, and looking at the velocity field on either side of the interface. The diffusive velocity being the velocity at which gas is moving into the interface from the hot to the cold sides we can heuristically write this as

v_{\rm diff}\approx v_{\rm in,hot}-v_{\rm in,cold}\,,

(22)

where
v_{\rm in,hot}
is the velocity on the hot side pointing in the direction perpendicular to the interface and
v_{\rm in,cold}
is the same on the cold side.

To formalize this heuristic we define the direction into the cooling layer by the direction pointing down the temperature gradient, evaluated at
T=T_{\rm pk}

\mathbf{\hat{n}}\equiv-\frac{\mathbf{\nabla}T}{|\nabla T|}

(23)

and define the velocity into the layer as

v_{\rm in}\equiv\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{n}}\,.

(24)

In order to use the above in a meaning akin to
Equation 22
we must look at its difference from one side of the layer to the other. To this end we take its gradient in the direction perpendicular to the layer and multiply by some length scale,
\lambda
, which should be representative of the thickness of the layer

v_{\rm diff}=-\lambda\,\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\nabla v_{\rm in}\,.

(25)

The minus sign originates from our wanting
v_{\rm in,hot}-v_{\rm in,cold}
and not vice-versa.

In practice in our simulations we compute
Equation 25
for all gas with
1/f_{w}<T/T_{\rm pk}<f_{w}
for a ‘window’ factor
f_{w}=10^{0.1}\approx 1.26
as a proxy for gas near the peak cooling temperature. For cells which meet this criterion we compute
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
locally using centered differences of the temperature field. We then use this local estimate of
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
to compute
v_{\rm in}
for all cells sharing faces with the target cell (we are ignoring the small changes in the definition of
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
from cell to cell). We then compute
\nabla v_{\rm in}
using centered differences and compute
Equation 25
using the previously measured
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
and
\lambda=1.2\Delta x
. We justify this choice of
\lambda
below by showing that, along with our measurements of
A_{\rm int}
and
Equation 5
, it reproduces the observed cooling rates in the simulations.

IV.2
Surface Area

To measure the surface area of the interface between the hot and cold gas we implement the marching cubes algorithm of
37
as an on-the-fly calculation in
AthenaK
. This algorithm identifies iso-value surfaces of a scalar field defined on a three-dimensional cartesian grid by considering a group of 8 neighbor cells and determining whether the scalar field at each cell lies above or below the desired iso-value. Depending on these values a triangulation is assigned to this group of cells that is representative of the iso-surface in this region. Given there are 8 cells and each can lie above or below the iso-contour there are
2^{8}=256
different cases, 14 of which are not related to one another through rotation
(
39
)
. Of these 14 cases, 7 have topologically distinct tilings as identified by
10
and accounted for efficiently in the
37
algorithm.

We use the temperature field defined on the grid and
T_{\rm pk}
as the iso-value to pick out the contour of peak cooling gas. We validate our implementation against that of
64
. The area of the interface between the hot and cold gas can be considered to be a function of scale,
A_{\rm int}(\ell)
. With our above choices for the on-the-fly measurement we have implicitly chosen to consider
A_{\rm int}(\Delta x)
. We do this because we are also effectively measuring
v_{\rm diff}
on the grid scale, as we detail in
Section IV.1
.

IV.3
Structure Functions

We now describe the measurement of the second-order velocity SFs of the mixing layers as described by
Equation 1
. Since the averaging process indicated by the angle brackets and the volume integral in this equation are both linear operations, we can calculate the second-order SFs for
v_{x}
,
v_{y}
, and
v_{z}
separately as

{\rm SF}_{2}(v_{i};\ell)=\left[\frac{1}{V_{\rm mix}}\int_{V_{\rm mix}}\left\langle|v_{i}(\mathbf{r})-v_{i}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{l})|^{2}\right\rangle_{|\mathbf{l}|=\ell}d\mathbf{r}\right]^{1/2}

(26)

and get the SF of the overall velocity field as
{\rm SF_{2}(\mathbf{v};\ell)=\sqrt{\sum_{i}{\rm SF}_{2}(v_{i};\ell)^{2}}}
.

We calculate
Equation 26
by selecting the volume,
V_{\rm mix}
, within the simulation domain to be representative of the part of the mixing layer where turbulence is causing mixing between the phases. We define this volume by all gas that is within a certain range in
z
that is close to peak cooling gas. Specifically,

V_{\rm mix}\equiv\{(x,y,z):\tilde{z}_{\rm lo}<z<\tilde{z}_{\rm hi}\}

(27)

where

\tilde{z}_{\rm lo}=0.9\times(z_{\rm lo}+0.5)-0.5\,,

\tilde{z}_{\rm hi}=1.1\times(z_{\rm hi}+0.5)-0.5\,,

z_{\rm lo}\equiv\min(Z_{\rm pk})\,\,\,,\,\,\,z_{\rm hi}=\max(Z_{\rm pk})\,,

and

Z_{\rm pk}\equiv\{z:1/f_{w}<T(x,y,z)/T_{\rm pk}<f_{w}\}

with
f_{w}=10^{0.1}
. In other words, we select all gas whose
z
position is within 10% of the
z
-position of any peak cooling gas, where the percent difference is measured with respect to the bottom edge of the domain.

Once
V
is defined for a given snapshot we sample
10^{4}
(
10^{3}
for the
N_{\rm res}=512
simulations
7
7

7

Each deviation vector comparison provides roughly a factor of
8
more point-wise comparisons in each higher resolution. As the comparisons become expensive to calculate in the
N_{\rm res}=512
case, we perform fewer of them, though the results are still statistically well-converged.
) deviation vectors,
\mathbf{l}
, whose norms are distributed as
|\mathbf{l}|\sim\ell^{-1/2}
up to a scale
L_{\rm box}
and are otherwise randomly oriented in space. This distribution is chosen so that we have a significant number of data points at small separations in order to accurately measure the SF on these scales. For each such deviation we offset the whole grid by this vector (accounting for periodicity in
x
and
y
) and calculate differences over all pairs of points in the grid which both lie within the volume
V_{\rm mix}
(as deviations in the
z
direction can lead to comparison to points outside the volume
V_{\rm mix}
). We compute the mean over all such differences and add this value to a histogram which is binned logarithmically in spatial scale.

The result of this calculation is the
{\rm SF}_{2}(v_{i};\ell)
for each snapshot. As we justify further in
Appendix B
, the
y
-component of the velocity field is the only fair tracer of the turbulence, especially on large scales. Therefore, when we refer to the turbulent SF as measured in our simulations below we will mean

v_{t}(\ell)\equiv\sqrt{3}\,{\rm SF}_{2}(v_{y};\ell)\,,

(28)

where the

intends to account for turbulent motions in the
x

IV.4
Temperature Structure

We compute another summary quantity which is somewhat analogous to a structure function for the temperature field and is walked through schematically in
Figure 4
. For gas near the peak cooling temperature we would like to measure properties of the distribution of temperature differences as a function of scale,
\ell
. In particular consider the set of temperature differences:

\Delta T(\ell)\equiv\left\{T(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{r})-T(\mathbf{x}):|r|=\ell\,\,\&\,\,T(\mathbf{x})=T_{\rm pk}\right\}

(29)

between gas at (or near) the peak cooling temperature and gas separated by a distance
\ell
.

Defining
Q_{\ell}(x)
as the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of this set, then the fraction of gas at intermediate temperatures as

f_{\rm int}(\ell)=Q_{\ell}\left(T_{\rm hi}-T_{\rm pk}\right)-Q_{\ell}\left(T_{\rm lo}-T_{\rm pk}\right)\,,

(30)

for some
T_{\rm lo}
and
T_{\rm hi}
. In practice, we choose
T_{\rm lo}=1.5\times T_{\rm cold}
and
T_{\rm hi}=T_{\rm hot}/1.5
so that
f_{\rm int}
represents the fraction of gas that is more than 50% away from one of the stable phases in temperature.

Our measurement of this function mirrors the calculation of the velocity SF except that we keep track of the whole distribution of differences and we do not compute differences over the whole volume
V_{\rm mix}
defined above but instead, as is suggested by
Equation 29
, restrict to differences where one point is near the peak cooling temperature. In particular, we consider all points with temperature
1/f_{w}<T/T_{\rm pk}<f_{w}
as ‘near’ peak cooling and allow for all possible deviation vectors
\mathbf{l}
such that
|\mathbf{l}|=\ell
.

We now present our main results. In

{\rm Da}

We begin by examining the total cooling rate,

This will be used to indicate the value as measured in the simulations throughout the rest of the work. In Paper 2 , we discuss other methods of measuring

\xi

Although all of our simulations lie in the

V.2
The Cancellation: Numerical Diffusivity vs. Numerical Viscosity

In
Section II
we discussed how the total cooling in the layer must be balanced by the flux of high specific-entropy gas into the layer, as written in
Equation 5
. We measure the components of this flux, specifically
v_{\rm diff}
and
A_{\rm int}
in our simulations as described in
Section IV
. The results of these calculations are given in
Figure 6
, where we show
5P_{0}v_{\rm diff}A_{\rm int}(\Delta x)/2
versus the measured total cooling rate in the simulations.

The agreement is excellent across all simulations, validating both the theoretical framework of
Equation 5
and our measurement techniques. The measurement of
v_{\rm diff}
includes a calibrated O(1) prefactor (the length scale
\lambda=1.2\Delta x
in
Equation 25
), but crucially, the same prefactor works across simulations spanning three orders of magnitude in
t_{\rm cool}
, a factor of 8 in resolution, and a factor of
\sim 30
in over-density. The energy budget of turbulent radiative mixing layers is, therefore, definitively controlled by the diffusive enthalpy flux.

Having established that
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}=\tfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}Pv_{\rm diff}A_{\rm int}
, we can now ask why
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
is resolution-independent even though both factors depend on resolution.
34
proposed that
v_{\rm diff}
and
A_{\rm int}
scale oppositely with resolution: numerical diffusion decreases at higher resolution (lowering
v_{\rm diff}
), but reduced numerical viscosity allows more small-scale structure (raising
A_{\rm int}
). If these effects cancel,
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
would be resolution-independent.
Figure 7
tests this hypothesis directly, showing
v_{\rm diff}
and
A_{\rm int}(\Delta x)
as functions of resolution for the
\mathcal{M}=1/2
simulations. The cancellation is remarkably precise:
v_{\rm diff}\propto N_{\rm res}^{-1/2}\propto\Delta x^{1/2}
while
A_{\rm int}(\Delta x)\propto N_{\rm res}^{1/2}\propto\Delta x^{-1/2}
, so their product is resolution-independent.
This is the root of our puzzle:
total cooling is converged not necessarily because the physics is resolved, but because two numerical artifacts—numerical diffusivity and numerical viscosity—cancel in their effects on
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
. This scaling continues even when we resolve
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
, which we return to in
Section VI.1
. As we show in
Appendix A
, this convenient cancellation does not persist over all regimes of parameter space explored by our simulations.

The area scaling is explained heuristically by a fractal model where

A_{\rm int}(\ell)\approx L_{\rm box}^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{L_{\rm box}}\right)^{-d}

(33)

with
d=1/2
the excess fractal dimension. The origin of this value for
d
is as yet unclear, but this same fractal scaling was also observed in past work
(
20
;
36
;
34
)
. We investigate behavior of
A_{\rm int}(\ell)
on all measured scales in Paper 2 .

We might expect that since there is no explicit diffusion in our simulations
v_{\rm diff}
should be given by an equation of the form of
Equation 4
with the thermal diffusivity,
\alpha_{T}
replaced with an appropriate numerical diffusivity. It is difficult to derive an exact formula for the numerical diffusivity of a Godunov scheme for the full Euler Equations
8

Though empirical calculations of effective dissipative terms for whole simulation domains have been performed

. However, in our nearly isobaric simulations the specific entropy acts approximately as a passive scalar. For passive scalar advection, the numerical diffusivity of a Godunov scheme scales as
\alpha_{\rm num}\sim v\Delta x
, where
v
is the advection velocity
(
63

49

. If we extend this analogy to the specific entropy we would expect the numerical diffusivity to be given by

\alpha_{\rm num}\approx v_{t}(L)\Delta x\,.

(34)

That is, we choose the velocity scale as the magnitude of velocity fluctuations in the “lab” frame of the grid (in analogy to the advection velocity
v
in the case of the advection equation), and the length scale as the resolution scale.

If this were the numerical diffusivity, following
Equation 4
we would expect the diffusive velocity to be given by

v_{\rm diff,num}\approx\sqrt{\frac{v_{t}(L_{\rm int})\Delta x}{t_{\rm cool,min}}}\,.

(35)

Figure 8
confirms this prediction: the measured
v_{\rm diff}
is proportional to
Equation 35
with proportionality constant 0.1. The linear relationship, holding over two orders of magnitude in the predicted value, demonstrates that diffusion in these simulations is fundamentally numerical. The O(1) prefactor reflects geometric uncertainties in defining
\alpha_{\rm num}
; the key point is that a
single prefactor works across all simulations
! This confirms that diffusivity in these simulations is entirely numerical, with a consistent functional form across all parameters studied. The resolution independence of
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
is thus not necessarily a sign that the physics is resolved—it is possibly a coincidence arising from the cancellation of two resolution-dependent quantities. Understanding whether this cancellation is physical or a coincidence of numerics requires either (i) a demonstration that this does not hold in similar set ups with different numerical methods (e.g. moving mesh) or (ii) an understanding of the origin of the fractal dimension of the interface,
d
. A first principles explanation for the value of
d
is elusive even in the problem of scalar mixing (no cooling) where values ranging from
1/3-2/3
are quoted in the literature for different regimes
(
11
;
55
;
45
, e.g.)
. We now ask: what is required for genuine resolution of turbulent mixing?

V.3
The Turbulent Field Length
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
as a Resolution Criterion

In
Section II
and
Figure 1
we argued that once one is able to resolve
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
, turbulent diffusion is able to compete with cooling on a realistic timescale. Once this scale is resolved, we would then expect to see the hallmark of that diffusion: a smooth transition in the phase distribution of the gas. We note again that in these simulations the actual smoothing of the interface on the smallest scales is mediated by numerical diffusion, which plays the role that physical diffusion would in reality. The hallmark of the layer being resolved in the sense we mean here is that the
effective
turbulent diffusivity is realistically represented.

We use the temperature “structure function” measurement described in
Section IV.3
in order to quantify this transition. In particular, in
Figure 9
we show the fraction,
f_{\rm int}
, of gas that is separated from gas at
T_{\rm pk}
by a distance of
\ell\approx 8\Delta x
and is at intermediate temperatures
T\in[1.5\times T_{\rm cold},T_{\rm hot}/1.5]
(see
Equation 30
and
Figure 4
). We choose
\ell=8\Delta x
as we consider this to be the minimal scale still in the “well-resolved” regime of the turbulence (on scales smaller than this, turbulent motions are damped by numerical viscosity, see Paper 2 ).

If the gas is truly multiphase on this minimally resolved scale, then a large fraction of the gas on this scale will be near
T_{\rm hot}
or
T_{\rm cold}
and
f_{\rm int}
will be small. If the gas is efficiently mixed by turbulent diffusion, then most of the gas will be at intermediate temperatures, and
f_{\rm int}
will be large. We see in
Figure 9
that when a simulation resolves
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
(left of the vertical black line) nearly all (
\gtrsim 80\%
) of the gas on this scale is at intermediate temperatures. This trend is also visually apparent in the slices of
Figure 3
where we indicate how well-resolved
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
is in the bottom-right of each panel.

It is also clear from
Figure 9
that the transition from
f_{\rm int}\approx 0
to
f_{\rm int}\approx 1
is not an instantaneous transition once
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
is resolved. This is to be expected. As we described in
Section II
, there are two effects competing to change the entropy of a gas parcel in the TRML: mixing and cooling. When
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
is not well resolved, the time-scales over which mixing acts are not faithfully represented in the simulation, the competition between these two effects is therefore not well-resolved, and the transition between the two phases is sharp (
f_{\rm int}\approx 0
). As we move to higher resolution and
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
becomes better resolved, the competition between mixing and cooling becomes increasingly fair. This is manifested in the fact that
f_{\rm int}\approx 40\%
even when
t_{\rm eddy}\approx 10\,t_{\rm cool,min}
in
Figure 9
. The smooth progression of this transition should therefore not be considered as evidence against its fundamental importance.

In summary, simulations which resolve
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
show a smoothing out of the local phase structure near the interface. This is excellent evidence that
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
is a meaningful scale on which to consider the turbulence as efficiently mixing the phases. We next investigate the resolution dependence of the phase-structure of intermediate temperature gas in the mixing layer and how this compares to the resolution of
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
.

V.4
Convergence of Phase Structure

In order to consider our simulations as well resolved we would like them to not only be converged in integrated properties, like
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
, but also in aspects of the distribution of the gas, such as its phase structure, and therefore its emissivity as a function of temperature. As has been discussed in several past works
(
42

20
;
60
;

c_{s,{\rm pk}}t_{\rm cool,min}

VI
Discussion & Conclusion

We first provide a thorough summary of the resolution requirements for accurately representing the phase structure (and hence observable properties of emission from/absorption in the mixing layer) in
Section VI.1
. In
Section VI.2
we discuss an interpretation of the resolution independence of our simulations (in terms of
{\rm Pr}
) and its relationship to other works in the literature which have included additional physics. In
Section VI.3
we summarize our main results.

VI.1
Resolution Requirements for Different Questions

Even if the total dissipation (that leads to the total cooling) is correct in the simulations, it can still be true that the numerical dissipation is not an accurate representation of the turbulent dissipation that would exist in reality. As we discussed in
Section II
, in reactive flows like TRMLs where the reaction acts to return mixed gas to either of the two thermally stable phases, the thickness of the interface between the phases is given by the scale on which diffusion and this reaction balance, which can be quite small compared to the scales of the problem (
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
in the case of turbulent dissipation). When this scale is not resolved, the mixing that is occurring in the simulation is simply due to the advection of the interface over the grid. Even if the simulation gives the right amount of total mixing across the surface, the phase structure around the surface cannot be expected to be correct. In
Section V.3
and
Figure 9
we show this to indeed be the case.

What, then, is required to correctly resolve the phase structure of the layer? To get the phase structure right we must correctly resolve the processes which alter or maintain the phase (temperature, entropy, pressure, etc.) of a parcel of gas. In the simulations presented here these processes are (i) mixing, (ii) cooling, (iii) compression
11
11

11

As well as rarefaction, in the case of temperature and pressure.
by sound waves, and (iv) turbulent pressure support, which acts to prevent compression. Which of these processes do we accurately represent?

-

Mixing
: The above discussion around resolution independence shows us that we do (on average) get the total amount of mixing correct. However, as discussed in
Section II
and demonstrated in
Section V.3
and
Section V.4
we do not accurately represent the time-dependent action of turbulence to move a parcel of gas through intermediate phases when we do not resolve the eddies on scales
\ell\lesssim\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
which act to diffuse on time-scales comparable to
t_{\rm cool,min}
.

-

Cooling
: If we consider these other processes as separate, then the resolution of the ‘cooling’ process for an individual gas element is essentially enforced by the numerical techniques of our simulation (time-step limiting based on the reaction rate).

-

Compression
: When the minimum scale that a sound wave crosses in a cooling time (
c_{s,{\rm pk}}t_{\rm cool,min}
) is not resolved, a gas parcel is able to artificially cool to lower entropy/pressure before the hydrodynamics in the simulation is able to re-pressurize it. As has been discussed in
20
this leads to an unphysical drop in the pressure of gas in the mixing layer.

-

Turbulent Pressure Support
:
52
demonstrated that vertical turbulent stress acts to dynamically support the gas in the mixing layer leading to a physical dip in the thermal pressure in the layer. In
Section V.4
and
Appendix C
we demonstrate that the lack of resolution of this turbulent support can lead to unphysical, high pressures at intermediate temperatures.

In order for the phase structure to be resolved, it seems we must resolve the turbulent mixing (
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
), thermal pressure support (
c_{s,{\rm pk}}t_{\rm cool,min}
), and the turbulent pressure support. We demonstrate in
Appendix C
that the final requirement is satisfied well before the former ones for the simulations presented here
12
12

12

In general, we might expect this to be the case, as it is the largest turbulent motions which provide the most dynamical support, which will be resolved before
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
in
{\rm Da}>1
simulations.
. In
Section V.4
we empirically show that resolving
c_{s,{\rm pk}}t_{\rm cool,min}
results in a reasonably well-resolved phase structure in the mixing layer (see
Figure 10
) while the additional resolution of
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
can results in a
\sim 50\%
change in emission properties. Therefore, we emphasize that accurate representations of observable properties of mixing layers require the resolution of
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
(or at
least

c_{s,{\rm pk}}t_{\rm cool,min}
).

In
Figure 7
we see that the resolution scaling of
v_{\rm diff}
and
A_{\rm int}(\Delta x)
continues even when
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
is well resolved (highest resolution points in the top-left,
\xi=3
panel of
Figure 7
). We have shown that
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
is the scale on which the phase distribution becomes smooth and therefore that this is likely a meaningful scale on which “effective” turbulent diffusion can be considered as well represented. However, since numerics fundamentally controls both
A_{\rm int}(\Delta x)
and
v_{\rm diff}
, resolution dependence is expected unless explicit dissipation is included and smooths out the interface at some scale
(
41
)
.

For the set of physics considered in these simulations (pure hydrodynamics, with a local cooling function) we conclude that resolving
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
is necessary to consider the emission properties as well resolved. Resolution of
c_{s,{\rm pk}}t_{\rm cool,min}
, which is generally larger than
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
, gives reasonably accurate results for the parameters studied here. However, we also demonstrate in
Section V.3
that the resolution of
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
shows a measurable change in the smoothness of the gas phase distribution near the interface. If we were to calculate the energy levels of a given ion species near the TRML interface, and these energy levels were significantly affected by radiative trapping in the layer, then it may be necessary to resolve the scales over which the phase distribution varies smoothly in order to correctly simulate line transfer, and therefore emission from these energy levels. Therefore, to answer certain questions, it may still be necessary to resolve
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
even if it does not make a large difference here.

VI.2
Physical Interpretation: The Role of
\Pr

Is the countervailing behavior of numerical heat diffusion and numerical viscosity, that leads to the resolution independence of
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
(
Figure 7
), somewhat physical? In the purely hydrodynamic simulations we present here, it could be argued that this cancellation is showing us that what matters in TRMLs is the ratio of kinematic viscosity,
\nu
, to thermal diffusivity,
\alpha
, the so-called Prandtl number,
\Pr\equiv\nu/\alpha
. If
\nu
can be thought of as controlling the area of the interface (
A_{\rm int}
) and
\alpha
can be thought of as controlling the thermal diffusivity across that interface (
v_{\rm diff}
), then as long as the ratio of these quantities (
\Pr
) is held constant, the resulting dissipation across the surface is the same. Given that
\Pr\approx 1
for numerical dissipation and turbulent dissipation alike, it may not be surprising that the simulations presented here demonstrate relative resolution independence in integrated quantities like
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
(
Figure 5
). However, as we show in
Appendix A
, this resolution independence can break down in certain regimes, specifically when the resolution is so low that
A_{\rm int}
is forced to approach its lower limit,
L_{\rm box}^{2}
.

One implication of this interpretation is that resolution independence should not remain if one changes
{\rm Pr}
by changing
\nu
(
\alpha
) in a way that affects
A_{\rm int}
(
v_{\rm diff}
). This point of view could be described as a “diffusion limited” perspective, where it is the micro-physical diffusivity which limits the amount of dissipation across the interface. This stands in contrast to a “mixing limited” perspective, popular in the scalar mixing literature
(
4
;
66
)
in which, as long as the large-scale turbulent mixing rate is fixed or well-resolved, the amount of diffusion across the layer remains the same. The present work does not distinguish between these two regimes. Indeed, an answer to this question in the context of TRMLs will depend upon a thorough
{\rm Pr}
-varied study, which we plan to carry out in future work. As we discuss further below, when carrying out such a study in an effort to tell the difference between the diffusion and mixing limited pictures, it is important to ensure that changes to
\nu
or
\alpha
do not cause changes to the integral scale mixing, which requires very high resolution
(
32
;
22
)
. In the rest of this section we discuss works in the existing literature which shed light on the question of whether a mixing or diffusion limited picture applies in TRMLs.

TRML simulations which include dynamically important magnetic fields
(
73
;
14
;
6
;
34
)
introduce an effective “viscosity” through magnetic tension, which suppresses the instabilities that lead to small-scale turbulent folding. However, this does not alter the fundamental role of numerical heat dissipation. The net effect is an
effective
change in
{\rm Pr}
, which produces a clear resolution dependence for
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
in magnetized mixing layers (see e.g.
34
or Figure A2 left panel, red curves of
73
). These same simulations also show reduced turbulent velocities, with the relationship between turbulent velocity (
v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
) and total cooling (
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
) approximately preserved
(
14
)
. This fact would normally be interpreted in favor of the “mixing limited” picture: for a set amount of turbulence,
v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
, one sees a set amount of cooling,
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
, and therefore it is the mixing that determines the dynamics. This interpretation is undercut by the resolution dependence of both cooling and
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}

(see e.g.
34
, where resolution dependence of turbulent velocity is shown explicitly in Figure 15)
, which calls in to question the overall physical realism of the simulations. The existing results therefore offer limited discriminating power between competing physical interpretations.

The simulations of
41
explicitly vary
\Pr
by including explicit viscosity. In the fast-cooling limit, viscosity is observed to increase the total cooling (see their Figures 6 & 7). Upon inspection of the bottom panels of Figure 5 of
41
, it is our suspicion that these fast-cooling simulations lie in a regime of parameter space close to those simulations discussed in
Appendix A
. Specifically,
A_{\rm int}(\Delta x)
in the simulations is artificially low as proper turbulent folding is not able to be faithfully represented at that resolution. With this in mind, as the viscosity is increased, the momentum dissipation thickens the interface layer, creating a higher volume of gas at intermediate temperatures and hence more cooling. In this way, viscosity mediates an effective increase in
v_{\rm diff}
and
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
increases, as is observed. To be explicit: this is our speculation and we cannot confirm this independently with the results presented here.

In the slow-cooling limit of the
41
work
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
remains relatively unchanged as the viscosity is increased. This is surprising especially because, unlike the fast-cooling regime, it is apparent from their Figures 5 & 8 that these simulations begin with well-resolved turbulent mixing at low viscosity (an apparent “mixing limited” regime) and transition to a nearly turbulence-free, smooth interface (apparently “diffusion limited”) at high viscosity. Additionally, the diffusion in the latter situation is mediated by viscosity, but it is the thermal diffusivity which should matter for
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
in these simulations. The thermal diffusivity in these simulations is still fundamentally numerical, but its nature is likely strongly affected by the presence of the strong viscosity. This viscosity-independence seems to point towards some convergent dynamics between the resolved viscosity
\nu
and the numerical thermal diffusivity
\alpha_{\rm num}
which drives the “effective”
{\rm Pr}_{\rm eff}\equiv\nu/\alpha_{\rm num}\to 1
(at least in the way that
\alpha_{\rm num}
maps to
v_{\rm diff}
) when we would normally expect
{\rm Pr}>1
. Again, this is our speculation, truly understanding these dynamics would require a suite of simulations which vary both resolution and other properties that might affect the relationship between
\nu
and
\alpha_{\rm num}
such as the density contrast,
\chi
, and the mach number of the flow,
\mathcal{M}
. This is an important objective for future studies.

There have also been a limited number of studies which explore varying
{\rm Pr}
by including explicit conduction
(
7
;
59
;
60
)
.
In
59
the strength of conduction is steadily increased and it is observed that
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
does not change until the conduction begins to affect the integral-scale turbulent structure (see their Figure 16, and discussion in Section 5.5). This observation is used to argue for a “mixing limited” picture. This may well be the case, though we would argue that this could be due to the limited dynamical range of the simulations. In particular, if the simulation resolution is such that conduction is only well resolved (by which we mean dominant to numerical thermal diffusivity) once it begins to affect the
integral-scale turbulence, no clear conclusion can be drawn. The resolution required to have a well-developed inertial range in turbulence, along with resolved dissipative physics, could be quite high, potentially much higher than any of the simulations presented here
(
32
)
, making a strong case for future work. In light of the resolution dependence of the magnetized case, and the potential resolution requirements discussed above we would like to generally caution that the addition of new physics to mixing layer problems should not appeal to the resolution independence of simulations like the ones presented here in order to justify the lack of a resolution study.

In the simulations presented here, mixing and cooling dominate the phase space evolution of a fluid element. This makes movement through the mixing layer effectively a ‘one way street’ in that, once gas is mixed, it will for the most part cool to lower temperatures. Therefore there is only movement of gas from hot to cold, and the cold phase grows in mass. The inclusion of explicit thermal conductivity provides another mechanism to change the energy content of a fluid element and can therefore change this picture, with strong thermal conduction in the hot phase potentially resulting in mass loss from the cold phase
(
12
;
17
)
, and much more emission from hot gas
(
60
)
that could be much more consistent with observations
(
50
, e.g.)
. We leave study of these effects, along with a controlled study of the effects of varying
\Pr
to future work.

VI.3
Summary of Results

Our main findings:

1.

We are able to accurately measure the components of the diffusive enthalpy flux into the mixing layer (
v_{\rm diff}
and
A_{\rm int}
) and show that these measurements accurately predict total cooling in the simulation (
Section V.1
and
Figure 6
), as expected.

2.

Investigating the behavior of each of these quantities with resolution,
N_{\rm res}
, we find that apparent resolution independence of total cooling in these mixing layers (
Figure 5
) is dependent upon the countervailing effects of decreased numerical dissipation (lower
v_{\rm diff}
) and decreased numerical viscosity (higher
A_{\rm int}
) as shown in
Figure 7
. The behavior of
v_{\rm diff}
is explained through the expected scaling for numerical dissipation (
Figure 8
) and that of
A_{\rm int}
is explained by an effective fractal model (
Equation 33
), further explored in Paper 2 . Whether or not this cancellation can be trusted as physically meaningful is discussed in
Section VI
.

3.

Despite this cancellation effect, we find that smooth transitions in the the layer (
Figure 9
) and consequently its phase structure (
Figure 10
) are only well resolved once
\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
is well resolved. This is the scale on which turbulent diffusion competes on fair terms with cooling (
Section II
).

Therefore, we have shown that simulations with
\Delta x>\lambda_{\rm F,turb}
do not accurately represent turbulent mixing—they capture numerical diffusion across an advected surface. These are not true mixing layers; they are representations of mixing layers, with the representation’s limitations hidden by a cancellation of competing terms whose physical realism is as yet ambiguous.

Appendix C
Reynolds Averaging and Turbulent Pressure Support

As discussed briefly in
Section V.5
,
52
show that well-resolved TRML simulations still have a decrement in the thermal pressure in the interior of the layer and explain that this is allowed through the support of vertical turbulent pressure within the mixing layer. By averaging the
z
-component of the momentum equation over the
x
and
y
directions they arrive at
Equation 36
, which states that the averaged pressure,
\left\langle P\right\rangle
, vertical Reynolds stress
\mathcal{R}_{zz}\equiv\left\langle\delta v_{z}\delta(\rho v_{z})\right\rangle
, and mean compressive stress
\left\langle\rho v_{z}\right\rangle\left\langle v_{z}\right\rangle
, balance throughout the layer.

In
Figure 13
we show that this relation holds in all of our
\mathcal{M}=1/2
simulations (it also holds in the
\mathcal{M}=1/8
simulations, though we don’t show them here). We confirm that turbulent pressure in the layer (
\mathcal{R}_{zz}>0
) supports a dip in the mean thermal pressure (
\left\langle P\right\rangle<P_{0}
). In some of the faster cooling simulations
{\rm Da}\gtrsim 50
we also see that the amount of vertical turbulent stress is not well-converged in some of our simulations. As noted in
Section V.5
, this likely explains the over-pressurization (compared to higher resolution simulations) of intermediate temperature gas in these simulations.
