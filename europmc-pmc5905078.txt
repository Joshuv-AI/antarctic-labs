NOTE ON THE SKULL OF DR.. PRITCHARD.
By GEO. H. EDINGTON, M.D. Glasg., F.R.F.P.S.G.,
Assistant Surgeon, Western Infirmary, and Extra Honorary Surgeon,
Royal Hospital for Sick Children, Glasgow.
Early in 1908 there appeared in Messrs. Hodge's Notable
Scottish Trials series a volume dealing with the trial of Dr.
Pritchard. The volume, which was edited by Mr. William
Roughead, W.S., contained, in addition to the evidence and
proceedings in court, an introduction in which the prisoner's
career was sketched, and also no fewer than seventeen
appendices bearing on the prisoner and on the trial. Included
in the illustrations were reproductions from photographs
of the three houses in which successively Pritchard lived and
practised, and in the last of which he murdered his victims.
The story of Pritchard and his crimes being probably
well known to many of the readers of the Glasgow Medical
Journal, it is not my intention here to dwell upon it; and
those who desire information regarding this in many ways
remarkable man should read Mr. Roughead's introduction
to the volume just referred to. Suffice it to say that Pritchard
was found guilty of poisoning his wife and his wife's mother,
and suffered the extreme penalty of the law. .
His execution, which was carried out on 28th July, 1865,
in front of the jail, was the last public one which took place
in Glasgow, if not in Scotland, and it was said to have been
witnessed by a crowd of 100,000 onlookers. Mr. Roughead gives
in an appendix a contemporary account of the execution, from
which it would appear that the executioner (Calcraft) allowed
a drop of between three and four feet. There was no
screen round the scaffold, and the body was thus fully exposed
to public view after the drop had fallen.

It having been decided to re-build the Justiciary Courts,
designed in the early years of the nineteenth century by
William Stark, the demolition of the old buildings in Jail
Square began towards the end of 1910. With the exception
of the facade (the east front) which has been incorporated in
the new structure, the whole of the buildings were taken
down, and in the course of these operations the old courtyard,
in which had been interred the bodies of executed criminals,
was excavated, and the remains exhumed.
Some notes were taken of the general condition of the
remains, from which it would appear that all were of the
colour of the earth, and quite dry. Many of the parts
crumbled away when handled, although when first viewed in
situ the bodies seemed quite intact. In three or four cases
there were remains of clothing, such as the collars and lapels
of men's coats, the tartan shawl of a woman, a pair of men's
boots of the half-Wellington shape, with the uppers still
intact, but the soles loosened and hanging off ; a woman's
shoe or slipper; and Pritchard's elastic-sided boots, with the
elastic frayed out. Pritchard's coat also fell all to pieces,
except the thicker part of the collar. This was after forty-
five years' interment.
As interest in Pritchard had been recently re-awakened by
the publication of his trial, already referred to, opportunity
was taken to examine his skull, with the result that the
following particulars were obtained :?
Excluding the mandible, the skull when received for
examination was in four pieces. The first consisted of the
right temporal bone, which was entirely disarticulated from
the rest of the skull.
The second fragment comprised the superior maxillae and
palate bones, together with the left malar bone. The nasal
process was absent from both maxillae, and the malar process
and outer wall of the antrum of Highmore were awanting
from the right. Behind, the greater part of the pterygoid
process was attached on the left; but only very incomplete
remains of the lower end of this process on the right. Both
inferior turbinals were present. The lower edge of the
anterior extremity of the vomer was in situ, and the groove
for the septal cartilage was well-marked, and to the left
of the middle line. The corresponding underlying part of
the incisor crest was lower in the left than in the right. The

The third piece of bone was an irregular fragment of the
sphenoid. It comprised (1) the postero-superior part of the
body of the sphenoid. The line of fracture separating the
fragment from the posterior fossa of the base of the skull had
passed through the sphenoidal cells, so that the broken
surface of bone was the roof of these cells, and had a vertical
measurement of 3-4 mm.; (2) the right side of the body,
with the groove for the cavernous sinus; (3) the lower
half of the right great wing, less the spinous process and
foramen ovale; and (4) the right pterygoid process, save
its extreme lower end.
The anterior edge of this fragment was very thin, and the
line of fracture crossed the sella turcica. Looked at from in
front, the orbital surface of the great wing was seen, almost
complete, also the excavated lateral part of the anterior surface
of the body, and the open cavity of the sphenoidal cells. There
was no interruption of the pharyngeal surface of the basi-
occipital and sphenoid. The fragment was, so far as the body
of the sphenoid was concerned, intra-cranial.
The fourth piece of bone comprised the remainder of the

right os planum of the ethmoid, lower part of the vertical
plate, and the main bulk of the vomer. There was consider-

and of both parietal eminences. The margin of the squamous

from the parietal bone. The external occipital protuberance
was very prominent. The mastoid emissary foramen was very
large, being 5 mm. in diameter. The occipital condyles were

Measurements.?The fragments having been wired together,
measurements of the cranium as a whole were made.1
The cranial capacity, measured with turnip seed, was
1470 c.c. ; circumference (horizontal), from glabella or
ophryon to the occipital point, 523 mm.; pre-auricular part
being 224 mm. ; length, from glabella to occipital point,
183 mm.; breadth, between the parietal eminences, 145 mm.;
basi-alveolar length, 85 mm. (approximately) ; basi-nasal
length, 92 mm.; height, 131 mm.
From these measurements we find the cephalic index,
79*23 ; height index, 71*58 ; and the gnathic index, 92*39.
From the above data we may, therefore, according to
Quain,2 describe the skull as megacephalic. Its capacity is
slightly below the European male (1,480 c.c.). The cephalic
or breadth-index places it in the category of mesaticephalic
crania (75-80), and just bordering on the brachiocephalic.
The height-index, as is common, is lower than the breadth-
index. The gnathic index being well below 98, the skull
belongs to the category orthognathous.
Remarks on the broken condition of the skull.?The broken
condition of the skull when received for examination was
at first thought to be due to carelessness during exhumation.
While this may be, it is interesting to find that the general
course of the lines of separation corresponds with what
was found in some of the burial places examined by the
anatomists engaged in the archaeological survey of Nubia.3
In his report on "Fractures and Dislocations," Mr. Wood
Jones refers to remains found in two trenches of the Roman
period, at Shellal. "The most frequent sign of death in
these hundred men was the opening of the sutures of the base
of the skull, caused probably by some method of hanging.
In these cases the injury to the skull consists of a dragging

is an oblique opening of the sutures, so that one portion

by the facial part of the skull and the other temporal bone.
The basilar suture in most cases becomes disunited, and so
the median axes of the front and back portions of the base of
the skull are not continuous. The lesion commences behind,
at the meeting of the lambdoid suture with the parieto-
mastoid and occipito-mastoid sutures. The occipito-mastoid
suture is torn widely open, and the whole basal portion of the
occipital bone is separated unilaterally from its connexions,
the jugular and basilar processes being torn across their
synchondroses. The line of disruption now passes forwards
to the outer side of the skull, the great wing of the sphenoid
being separated from the temporal bone, and the squamous
suture being opened in a varying extent of its course. In the
great majority of cases the line of opening of the sutures
passed from behind forwards from right to left; but, in
some instances, the process was exactly reversed, and, in
these, the left occipito-mastoid suture and the right spheno-
temporal suture were opened. It is impossible to say what
was the exact method of the hanging, and exactly how the
rope was used in carrying it out, but these lesions of the base
of the skull admit of a simple explanation, if a sudden drag
upon one side of the head be admitted as their cause. The
upward jerk of the head would act on one side only?the side
on which the knot was placed; and the pull acting on the
temporal bone?for the knot doubtless caught on the angle
of the jaw?caused that side of the head to be violently
strained upwards and outwards.
" The temporal bone of the side where the knot was situated
naturally carried the facial part of the skull with it, for
the stress was transmitted forwards through the jaw ; whilst
the body-weight acted on the basi-occipital, and so carried
the other temporal in the opposite direction.
" This lesion of the base of the skull does not appear to
be present in modern judicial hangings, and some additional
form of violence was probably used in these ancient cases. . .
The bases of the most of the skulls showed a very copious
staining with blood."

not been able to obtain any details of the method of hanging
employed in this case; but it is mentioned by the contempor-
ary writer, already quoted from, that the prisoner "shrugged
his shoulders more than half a dozen times, his head shook,
the whole body trembled and swung round and round ; and
it was only after Calcraft went below and pulled the legs that
it was brought to stillness . . . the fingers of the right hand
pressed off the glove, which fell to the floor." It is not
possible in face of such a vague account to estimate the
degree of force used by the executioner in pulling the
criminal's legs, and, if we assume that the skull-injury has
been caused by the rope, I think that we may take it that
the drop of three to four feet was responsible for the basal
fracture.
