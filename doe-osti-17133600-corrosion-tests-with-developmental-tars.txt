 ABSTRACT
 Standard material corrosion test methods were applied to specimens of eight
 developmental TARS materials provided by PNNL. These multiphase alkali
 borosilicate glass-ceramic materials are being evaluated as possible waste forms
 for dehalogenated waste salt. The assessment included (1) characterization of the
 bulk microstructures using polished cross sections of all materials, (2) series of
 ASTM C1308 tests to measure the intrinsic dissolution rates at 90 °C and the
 effects of pH and temperature, (3) extended ASTM C1308 and ASTM C1220 tests
 to measure the effects of the saturation index, and (4) ASTM C1285 tests to
 measure the effect of solution saturation. Both the test solutions and reacted solids
 were evaluated to compare the durabilities of the different materials. The
 crystalline phases content increased from negligible in TARS-1 to dominant in
 TARS-8 and had a significant effect on the composition and abundance of the glass
 phase. Test results indicated most crystalline phases were more chemically durable
 than the glass phase, and the test responses were dominated by glass dissolution.
 This is important because most dose-contributing radionuclides in waste salt (e.g.,
 cesium and strontium) report to the glass phase during processing or form inclusion
 phases in the glass. The test responses were consistent with those typically
 observed for homogeneous borosilicate glasses, including pH dependences and
 solution feedback, but the temperature dependences measured for three of the more
 durable materials were not monotonic and did not follow Arrhenius behavior over
 the range 40 – 90 °C. This probably indicates the rates measured in ASTM C1308
 tests at different temperatures did not provide the kinetically maximum rate for
 even the most durable material that was tested, namely TARS-1. These screening
 tests and analyses indicate that the TARS-1 formulation is the most promising
 candidate for further development. The chemical durability of the TARS-1
 material is similar to that of the well-studied glass-bonded sodalite and iron
 phosphate waste form materials.

 MATERIALS RECOVERY & WASTE FORM
 DEVELOPMENT CAMPAIGN

1. INTRODUCTION
Coordinated activities are in progress at Argonne and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory
(PNNL) to evaluate the effectiveness of using alternative waste forms to immobilize salt wastes
generated during the pyroprocessing of spent nuclear fuel. Those waste forms would likely also be
appropriate for immobilizing waste from molten salt reactors. Recently, PNNL and collaborators
at Washington State University demonstrated the use of an organic-acid-assisted thermal
dehalogenation process to convert chloride salt waste into an oxide-rich feed suitable for
vitrification in a borosilicate glass waste form (Oshiro et al. 2025). This process is of interest
because the use of organic agents may reduce some of the process complexity and off-gas
management challenges associated with other dehalogenation agents, particularly phosphates.
Furthermore, utilization of a borosilicate glass matrix makes use of extensive knowledge regarding
the processing and performance available regarding borosilicate waste glasses. Prototype materials
referred to as TARS, which was coined based on the descriptor “the average of refined
specifications”, were made at PNNL to assess the relative chemical durabilities of products with
different salt cation loadings and glass matrix compositions (Oshiro et al. 2026). In this report, the
results of tests used to measure the corrosion behaviors of eight compositionally different TARS
materials formulated with a wide range of additives are presented and related to the material
compositions and microstructures. The results of these screening tests are used to identify
compositions that warrant further study. Test results were compared to results of similar tests
conducted with alternative waste form materials including iron phosphate and glass-bonded
sodalite waste forms to provide an initial assessment.

2. EXPERIMENTAL APPROACH
2.1 TARS Materials
Eight TARS materials were formulated and fabricated at PNNL to represent waste forms made

2.2 Material Characterization
For use in ASTM C1308 tests, small monoliths were cut from the TARS materials using a wafering
blade, embedded in epoxy, and the surfaces were polished to a 1200-grit finish. For use in ASTM
1220 tests, small monoliths were cut from TARS-1, TARS-4, and TARS-5 using a wafering blade
and all surfaces were polished to a 600-grit finish. They were then examined using a Hitachi S-
3400N scanning electron microscope (SEM) operated at 20 kV. Backscattered electron imaging
(BSE) was used to highlight compositional differences among phases and secondary electron
imaging was used to examine topography. The contrast seen in BSE images is due to differences
in the electron scattering efficiencies of different elements: elements with higher atomic numbers
(high Z) scatter more efficiently than elements with lower atomic numbers (low Z). The brightness
of a phase in a BSE image reflects the composition of the phase (alloy, crystalline phase, or glass)
as the average atomic weight Z. An energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; Bruker xFlash®
6|10) was used to perform spot and line profile analysis, and elemental mapping. Note that lithium
X-rays are too weak (54 eV) to be detected using an EDS detector with a beryllium window and
are not reported.

The objectives of the pre-test characterization were to assess the microstructure of each material,
identify crystalline phases and elemental partitioning, and correlate microstructural features such
as phase separation or heterogeneous crystallization with corrosion behavior. These observations
were interpreted in conjunction with powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) results using a Rigaku
XRD with Cu Kα radiation. Phases were identified using the National Institute of Standards and
Technology Inorganic Crystal Structure Database (ICSD).

2.3.1 ASTM C1308
The intrinsic chemical durability of the material was evaluated using the ASTM C1308 test method
in DIW at 90 °C to measure the dissolution rate under highly diluted conditions (ASTM 2025a).
The intrinsic durability is considered to be the kinetically limited dissolution rate because
thermodynamic effects (e.g., reaction affinity) are minimized. Dissolution is controlled by the
material composition, solution pH, and temperature. Small sections of each material were cut using
a wafering blade and embedded in epoxy such that only one surface was exposed. Each specimen
was polished to 1200-grit finish using DIW as the polishing medium and then sonicated repeatedly
with ethanol to remove fines and polishing debris prior to pre-test SEM/EDS analysis.

In the test, the specimen was immersed in DIW in a PFA vessel that was then sealed. The exposed
surface-to-volume ratio (S/V) was nominally 2 m-1, depending on the exposed specimen surface
area and solution mass. The mass of DIW was measured and the volume was calculated using a
density of 1.0 g/cm3. The tests were conducted in two stages. In the first stage, which is referred
to as the semi-dynamic stage, the leachate was replaced every 24 hours for four sequential intervals.
At the end of each interval, the entire solution volume was removed from the vessel, an aliquot
was retained for analysis, the appropriate amount of fresh DIW was added and the test resumed.
This is done to maintain dilute conditions and minimize effects of solution feedback so that the
measured solution concentrations represent the intrinsic dissolution rate based on all phases in the
material. Note that the added DIW was at room temperature and the time required to reheat to the
test temperature was not distinguished but was consistent for all tests.

In the second stage of the test, which is referred to as the static stage, a small amount of test solution
(~1 mL) was sampled weekly for solution analysis without replacing the solution. These longer-
duration static exposures were intended to both further react the surfaces so that preferential
dissolution of different microstructural domains would be detected by microscopy and measure
the effect of approaching solution saturation. The test was terminated after 28 days, when corrosion
of the specimen surface was discerned visually. All aliquots of the test solution were acidified with
concentrated nitric acid and analyzed for key constituents by using inductively coupled plasma-
mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). The small sample volumes from longer duration tests were diluted
with DIW prior to acidification.

Specimens of TARS-1, TARS-4, and TARS-5 were used in ASTM C1308 tests to measure the
dependence on the solution pH and temperature. These specimens were not embedded in epoxy,
and all six faces were polished to a 600-grit finish. Only the semi-dynamic stage of testing was
performed using these samples.

2.3.2 ASTM 1220
Materials TARS-1, TARS-4, and TARS-5 were selected for use in ASTM C1220 based on the
range of intrinsic durabilities measured in the ASTM C1308 tests. In these ASTM C1220 tests,
specimens polished to a 600-grit finish were immersed in demineralized water at a nominal S/V
of 10 m-1 and maintained at 90 °C. The purpose of these tests is to measure the combined effects
of solution feedback and surface alteration layer formation. Leachate solutions were sampled after
several durations, acidified with nitric acid, and analyzed by using ICP-MS. Test responses are
evaluated using normalized mass loss values calculated in the same manner as for ASTM C1308.

2.3.3 ASTM 1285
Materials TARS-1, TARS-5, and TARS-6 were tested using the ASTM C1285 Test Method B to
measure the dissolution of crushed material under static conditions at high S/V. For these tests, the
materials were crushed, sieved to the desired size fraction (-100 +200), and cleaned to remove
adhering fines prior to testing by rinsing and ultrasonicating in demineralized water. The effective
removal of fines was confirmed with SEM analysis. Approximately 1 g of material was contacted
with demineralized water (with resistivity of ~18 MΩ cm equivalent to ASTM Type I water) at a
solution volume-to-glass mass ratio of 10 mL g-1 in sealed PFA vessels, held at 90 °C, and sampled
after durations of 7, 28, and 56 d. After tests were terminated, solution pH was measured at room
temperature. The collected leachates were then filtered (450 nm pore size), acidified with conc.
nitric acid, and analyzed by using ICP-MS. Results are reported as normalized elemental mass loss
values, NL(i), calculated for key elements.

2.4 Post-test SEM/EDS
After corrosion testing, reacted specimen surfaces were examined by optical microscopy and
SEM/EDS. These observations were used to discriminate dissolution of the glass and crystalline
phases, whether crystalline phases remained as residual features after dissolution of the
surrounding glass, and whether any alteration textures or secondary phases formed during the test.

The NL(i) value represents the mass of bulk material dissolved, based on the release of element i,
not the mass of element i in each solution or the mass of an individual phase in multi-phase
materials. Comparison of NL(i) values for different elements provide insights into the dissolution
mechanism and dissolution of different phases of multi-phase materials, but some values may be
affected by element solubility limits. Therefore, values based on highly soluble elements such as
B, Li, Na, K, and Cs are particularly useful for assessing durability because they are less likely to
be influenced by solubility limits or secondary phase precipitation than elements such as Al, Sr,
Ce, or Nd.

phases dissolve simultaneously at different rates and test results will be sensitive to the relative
surface area of each phase that is exposed. Over time, the relative exposed areas will change as
more soluble phases dissolve that will affect the solution results and the overall rate that is
measured. The most extensive dissolution and changes in the surface microstructure are expected
to occur in the ASTM C1308 tests.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1 Microstructures of TARS Materials
Optical microscopy, SEM/EDS, and PXRD results show that the suite of TARS materials have a
broad range of microstructures from predominantly glassy to highly crystallized multiphase
materials. TARS-1 was the most homogeneous and least crystallized composition in the series and
TARS-2 through TARS-8 had progressively greater extents of crystallization that occurred during
processing. In general, the extent of crystallization was higher in materials with higher salt waste
loadings and lower fractions of borosilicate glass-forming components. Optical microscope
images of each sample are presented in Figure 2.

As seen in Figure 2 and described in the following sections, the TARS-2 through TARS-5
materials showed clear evidence of heterogeneous nucleation that occurred at the melt-crucible
interface and that crystals formed throughout materials TARS-6 through TARS-8. The effect of
crystalline phases formed during processing (referred to as primary phases) is significant because
they sequester constituents from the melt during cooling and affect the composition of the glass
phase that forms. For example, the formation of crystalline silicate and aluminosilicate phases
removes Si and Al from the melt and lowers their concentrations in the glass. As these phases
crystallize, the composition of the melt becomes depleted in glass network-forming and
intermediate constituents, including Si and Al, that would otherwise contribute to glass durability.
In most cases, the crystalline phases are more durable than the glass phase and only affect the glass
dissolution through common ion effects in solutions that contact both phases (e.g., dissolved silica).

3.2 ASTM C1308 Semi-Dynamic Stage Solution Results
Results of the ASTM C1308 tests revealed large differences in the corrosion behaviors of the eight
TARS materials. In general, the measured normalized elemental mass loss values increased with
increasing extent of crystallization and decreasing continuity of the glass phases in the different
materials. Test execution details and solution results are provided in Appendix A Table A.1 and
Appendix B Table B.1, respectively. Figure 27 compares the cumulative normalized elemental
mass loss values, NL(i), measured in the four dynamic test intervals with the TARS materials
based on B, Cs, K, Li, Na, and Si. Figure 28 compares the cumulative NL(i) values for tests with
TARS-1 through TARS-5, which had lower NL(i) values overall. Solution results for Ca, Al, Sr,
Ce, and Nd are not used to assess TARS durabilities because those elements are expected to have
low solubility limits or high analytical uncertainty at low concentrations, but those results are
included in Appendix B Table B.1 for completeness.

The results show significant differences in the corrosion rates among the eight TARS compositions.
Of the materials tested, TARS-1 exhibited the lowest normalized mass losses for all major soluble
elements and was the most durable material in the series. The approximately linear increase in the
cumulative NL(i) values for B, Li, Na, K, Cs, and Si indicates that the dissolution rate was nearly
constant during the first three intervals of the semi-dynamic stage of the test. The Day 4 result for
TARS-1 was elevated relative to the preceding trend because the specimen became detached from
the epoxy and the exposed surface area increased significantly. Accordingly, the Day 4 value was
excluded from the linear fit used to represent the intrinsic dissolution rate. The low slopes based
on all soluble elements indicate that the glass network in TARS-1 was durable.

The higher normalized elemental mass losses measured in tests with TARS-2 through TARS-8
indicate lower chemical durability. Materials TARS-2 and TARS-3 showed substantially higher
normalized losses than TARS-1 based on all major soluble species, with TARS-3 generally less
durable than TARS-2. These materials contained multiphase crystalline assemblages and
SEM/EDS analyses showed that crystallization had detrimentally affected the composition of the
glass relative to glass formed in TARS-1. The elevated releases of B, Li, Na, K, and Cs are
attributed primarily to degradation of the glass phase.

Materials TARS-4 and TARS-5 showed similar corrosion behavior based on B, Na, Li, Cs, and Si
releases, although TARS-5 was slightly more durable overall. Both materials performed better
than TARS-2 and TARS-3 in several respects, but neither had the corrosion resistance of
TARS-1. The intermediate durability of TARS-4 and TARS-5 is consistent with more localized
crystallization and less severe glass depletion than that measured in TARS-2 and TARS-3.
Materials TARS-6, TARS-7, and TARS-8 had more extensively crystallized microstructures than
TARS-1 through TARS-5 without a continuous glass phase. These materials had markedly higher
normalized mass loss values, particularly for alkali elements. Of these, TARS-6 was the least
durable composition tested, with extremely high releases of K, Na, Li, Cs, and B. TARS-7 also
showed poor retention of soluble elements and high dissolution rates, although not to the same
extent as TARS-6. TARS-8 showed lower releases than TARS-6 and TARS-7 but remained
substantially less durable than TARS-1. These results indicate the crystalline phases have much
lower corrosion resistance than the glassy phases.

After the initial four-day solution-exchange period, the specimens were submerged in fresh DIW
at 90 °C for an additional three weeks to promote more extensive corrosion and surface alteration.
Small samples of test solution were removed without replacement to track the dissolution under
static conditions. The S/V ratio of the test increased slightly each time a sample was removed due
to the small decrease in solution volume. When the test was terminated, the specimen was removed
and allowed to dry, examined by using stereomicroscopy, and then carbon coated for SEM analysis.
Results of the static part of the ASTM C1308 tests and analyses of the reacted specimen are
presented below.

3.3.9 Summary of ASTM C1308 Test Results
The combined microstructural characterization, ASTM C1308 screening results, and post-test
microscopy indicate that corrosion durability in the TARS materials was affected primarily by the
composition and amount of the glass phase, which was affected by the extent of crystallization
during production. TARS-1 was the most durable composition because few crystals formed and it
retained the most continuous glass network. The TARS-4 and TARS-5 materials were less durable
than TARS-1 but performed better than the more extensively crystallized materials. The TARS-2
and TARS-3 materials showed poor durability consistent with more extensive crystallization and
resulting glass composition. Tests with TARS-6, TARS-7, and TARS-8 materials resulted in
severe degradation. Overall, the results indicate that successful development of borosilicate waste
forms for dehalogenated electrorefiner salt will require formulations that suppress extensive
crystallization during slow cooling to generate a chemically durable glass phase.

The dissolution rate law commonly used for borosilicate glass dissolution is based on the mineral
dissolution rate law having the simple linear form given in Equation 6

where k0 is the composition-dependent rate constant, η is the pH dependence, Ea is the activation
energy, Q is the solution ion activity product and K is the solubility product. The ratio Q/K is the
solution saturation index and the term (1 – Q/K) is referred to as the reaction affinity term. Glasses
are thermodynamically unstable and K is used to represent the kinetic stability such that Q/K is
always < 1 and the affinity term never becomes zero. Equation 6 is expected to represent the
dissolution rates of the glass phase and crystalline phases in the TARS materials, but each phase
will be represented by different coefficient values. Different coefficient values are typically
required to represent dissolution under acidic and alkaline conditions.

Relevant to Equation 6, the ASTM C1308 test is designed to maintain conditions in which the
value of the affinity term remains very near one during each interval and the ASTM C1285 test is
designed to generate conditions in which the affinity term approaches zero. The value of the
affinity term decreases during ASTM C1220 tests (and the static continuation of ASTM C1308
tests that was used here) from one initially in demineralized water to values less than one as the
specimen dissolves. Because the ASTM C1308 tests conducted in demineralized water at 90 °C
maintained highly dilute conditions with nearly constant pH and temperature, the pH, temperature,
the affinity terms remains nearly one and the rate measured in each interval is proportional to k0.
Other ASTM C1308 tests were conducted with materials TARS-1, TARS-4, and TARS-5 at 90 °C
in buffered pH solutions to measure the pH dependence and in demineralized water at 40 and 70 °C
to measure the temperature dependence.

ASTM C1308 tests were conducted in pH 3, 5, 9, and 10 buffer solutions to evaluate the sensitivity

compared to the NR(Cs) values. Although the pH value depends on temperature, all reported
values refer to the pH measured at room temperature. The effects of temperature on the actual pH
during the test are included with the test responses.
Figures 69, 70, and 71 show the cumulative normalized elemental mass loss plotted against

69, 70, and 71. As expected and observed in tests with other alkali borosilicate glasses, the
materials have V-shaped dependencies in which the lowest rates occur at near-neutral pH values
and the rates increase at more acidic and more alkaline pH values. This is because dissolution of
the silicate glass matrix is catalyzed by both H+ and OH─. The dissolution of crystalline phases

3.5 Results of ASTM C1308 Tests at Different Temperatures
The results of tests with TARS-1, TARS-4, and TARS-5 conducted at 40, 70, and 90 °C to measure
the temperature dependences are summarized in Appendix B Table B.8. Values for NL(B), NL(Li)
and NL(Cs) in tests at different temperatures are plotted in Figures 72a-c, 73 a-c, and 74 a-c,
respectively. In general, the data indicates that temperature influenced both the initial transient
release behavior (based on the y- intercepts) and the release rates (based on slopes) of all materials.
The y-intercepts indicate effects of surface and preparation artifacts (including exposed soluble
phases) and only the slopes were used to quantify the temperature dependence of the bulk material
dissolution. The results are presented in Arrhenius plots (ln rate v inverse absolute temperature) in
72d, 73d, and 74d.

Tests with TARS-4 and TARS-5 showed higher NL(B) values and dissolution rates at 70 °C than
tests at either 40 or 90 °C (Table 23), while results for tests with TARS-1 showed relatively small
differences in rates across the temperature range. The Arrhenius plots for tests with all three TARS
materials indicate the measured dissolution rates did not conform to Arrhenius behavior. This
could be due to differences in dependencies of the constituent glass and crystalline phases, that the
mechanism changed over the small temperature range (or the relative rates of crystalline and glass
phase dissolution changed), or that the test results were not suitable for assessment of multiphase
materials. As seen in the NL(i) plots, the releases in sequential intervals decreased significantly in
some tests, such that bias introduced by using the average of rates from different test intervals was
sufficient to invalidate the assessment. For example, only using the rates measured during the
second intervals might better represent the intrinsic temperature dependence of the materials, but
would not reliably represent the TARS material as a waste form.

the combined dissolution of glass and crystalline phases that have different solubility limits and
different dissolution kinetics.

The intrinsic dissolution behavior of TARS-1 measured in the ASTM C1308 test was compared
with results of other waste forms made with similar salt compositions. These include glass-bonded

the ACWF also contains a large amount of sodalite and halite inclusion phases that affect the glass
dissolution behavior. Cesium is dissolved in tellurite glass in the MoBiTe3F oxide material and
dissolved in iron phosphate glass in DPF materials.

The ceramic waste form is known to contain a small amount of halite and salt inclusions that are

4. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
The formation of crystalline phases during production of TARS material was shown to be
detrimental to the chemical durability of the glass phase, which dissolved to a much greater extent
than the crystalline phases. This was due to the uptake of glass-stabilizing constituents by
crystalline phases that resulted in poorly durable glass phases. Material TARS-1 that contained a
very small amount of a crystalline Ce-Zr-oxide phase that did not sequester other glass-stabilizing
constituents was significantly more durable than the other TARS materials in which greater
varieties and amounts of crystalline phases formed and was as durable as other waste forms
including iron phosphate and glass-bonded sodalite based on the retention of cesium. The
corrosion behavior of TARS-1 measured using ASTM C1308, ASTM C1220, and ASTM C1285
test methods is similar to that observed for other borosilicate waste glasses with the exception that
the measured temperature dependence did not show Arrhenius behavior. This may indicate
solution feedback effects in the ASTM C1308 tests were sufficiently high to attenuate dissolution
in the test at 90 °C.

The TARS-1 formulation generates a sufficiently durable glass matrix to warrant further
development and more extensive testing. The chemical durabilities of the seven other TARS
materials are not sufficient for consideration as waste forms. However, the influences of
constituents in those formulations on crystal formation during processing can be used to inform
composition limits as the TARS-1 formulation is further optimized.

It is recommended that a series of materials based on the formulation of TARS-1 be synthesized
with different loadings of dehalogenated ERV3 reagent oxides to represent the process and identify
possible effects of residual chloride salt. A battery of tests similar to those described herein should
be used to quantify the degradation kinetics and clarify the temperature dependence. For example,
series of ASTM C1285 tests should be conducted for several months to measure the saturation
concentrations at several temperatures and pH values needed for long-term performance modeling.
Also, the reacted materials and alteration layers should be analyzed more thoroughly and from a
mechanistic perspective. The limited analyses performed for these screening tests indicated a
moderately complex layer formed on reacted TARS-1 due to combined effects of ion exchange,
matrix dissolution, and possibly the crystallization of secondary phases to form the outer surface
layer. The propensity to form secondary phases that affect the glass durability should be measured
using ASTM C1663 vapor hydration tests (ASTM 2025d).
