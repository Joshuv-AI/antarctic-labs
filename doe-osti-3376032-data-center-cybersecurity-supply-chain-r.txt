 Cohort Summary: Takeaways and Action Plans
Objective
 This summary report captures the main outcomes of the supplier cohort focused on
power for data centers, part of INL’s Technical Assistance for Digital Assurance (TADA) for
States project. This project, sponsored by the Department of Energy (DOE), is designed to
strengthen grid resilience by addressing cybersecurity, supply chain risk management (SCRM),
and Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) with multiple stakeholder types. The cohorts, in
particular, are meant to bring together suppliers of grid modernization and resilience projects
to provide technical assistance on key topics in a group setting. Given the importance of
suppliers and integrators in the overall digital energy ecosystem, cohorts focus on applications
and takeaways for suppliers, but consider how this role interacts with other organizations,
including utilities.
What Was Discussed:
 •  Artificial intelligence (AI)-driven load behavior and power-quality risks
  • Cybersecurity exposures in uninterruptible power supply/battery energy storage systems
 (UPS/BESS), cooling automation, and vendor cloud portals
  • Supply-chain integrity, firmware provenance, and Behind-the-Meter (BTM) generation
 risks
  • Governance gaps at the utility–data center boundary
  • Small modular reactor (SMR) and gas-microgrid implications for reliability and oversight
  • Interconnection agreements as assurance instruments
  • How CIE can eliminate high-consequence failure modes
What this cohort summary includes:
 • A consolidated summary of challenges and risks directly identified by data-center
 stakeholders
 • Five cross-cutting themes shaping digital assurance needs
 • Technology-specific insights for power, cooling, BTM generation, Operational
 Technology (OT) cybersecurity, and supply chain
 • Actionable mitigations to guide operators, utilities, vendors, and DOE

Cohort Summary
Objectives
 The first cohort was an in-person working session at a Data Centers workshop hosted at
Idaho National Laboratory. The second two sessions were two-hour virtual workshop meetings,
with each session building on the previous. Each session included presentations, discussion, and
hands-on exercises.
 • Educate participants on emerging cybersecurity landscape including supply chain risks,
 regulatory gaps, and technology specific vulnerabilities
 • Identify supply chain challenges and procurement issues, including foreign component
 dependencies, vendor evaluation difficulties, and regulatory compliance concerns.
 • Build industry consensus on priority actions and practical solutions through
 collaborative threat modeling and real-world experience sharing.
 • Develop recommendations that participants can implement at their organizations to
 address identified vulnerabilities.

Attendance
 The participants in this cohort included hyperscale and colocation data center
operators; developers and engineering, procurement and consulting (EPC); utilities (investor-
owned utilities, municipalities, co-ops); vendors of UPS, BESS, cooling control systems, and
remote monitoring platforms; engineers specializing in interconnection and protection; and
cloud and cybersecurity specialists. Our goal was to recruit at least 8 organizational participants
in this cohort, targeting individuals with relevant experience. Invitations were extended to over
40 organizations. We secured 19 organizations and 22 individuals that participated in the data
center cohort.

Key Agenda Items
Session 1: Introduction & Foundation
 The first session laid the groundwork for understanding Digital Assurance in the context
of utility-scale data centers. It began with an overview of DOE’s TADA program and the
objectives of the Data Centers Cohort, emphasizing its role in connecting utilities, developers,
operators, and vendors to address cybersecurity and resilience challenges. A framing
presentation highlighted the rapid growth of hyperscale and AI-driven loads and posed a critical
question: what does “critical load” mean when data centers serve both the grid and society?
The session introduced Digital Assurance as a lifecycle approach to designing and operating
trustworthy energy systems, integrating cybersecurity, supply chain security, and CIE. Core
principles such as transparency, traceability, secure-by-design practices, and consequence-

 2

focused engineering were discussed, illustrated by examples like supply-chain attacks on
control hardware. The group then explored how these principles apply to data centers,
examining threats such as relay manipulation and spoofed dispatch commands, vulnerabilities
in building automation systems and vendor remote access, and potential consequences like
voltage instability and communication loss. An interactive discussion captured participants’ top
concerns around cyber-boundary divisions, vendor assurance, and visibility gaps, setting the
stage for deeper exploration in subsequent sessions.

Session 2: Current State & Challenges
 The second session focused on identifying and prioritizing gaps across four critical
dimensions: cybersecurity, supply chain, operational reliability, and institutional coordination. It
began with a recap of Session 1 themes and a review of current interconnection practices,
including security requirements in agreements and North American Electric Reliability
Corporation (NERC) Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) applicability. Stakeholders shared
perspectives on how utilities evaluate cybersecurity in interconnection requests, the
inconsistencies developers encounter, and vendor challenges related to transparency and
remote access controls. Discussions on cybersecurity at the interface highlighted issues such as
control boundaries, vendor remote-access risks, and compliance with CIP-005, CIP-010, and CIP-
013 standards. Supply chain vulnerabilities were examined in detail, including global
dependencies, extended lead times for transformers, and verification challenges for
cybersecurity requirements. Operational reliability concerns centered on ride-through
capability, oscillation events, cooling system failures, and the impact of AI-driven load variability
on grid stability. Institutional barriers such as data-sharing limitations, regulatory gaps, and lack
of coordination models were also addressed. The session concluded by consolidating gaps
across these dimensions and prioritizing them for action planning in the next workshop.

Session 3: Standards Development & Path Forward
 The final session transitioned from gap identification to developing actionable strategies
and governance models. It opened with a review of prioritized gaps and common themes from
earlier discussions, then introduced a framework for consequence-focused design. Participants
explored how digital assurance mitigations—such as zoning, segmentation, and secure-by-
design equipment—could be applied to critical data center functions like cooling systems, UPS,
supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) links, and grid-forming battery systems.
Supply chain assurance was another major focus, covering CIP-013 requirements, vendor
qualification approaches, and domestic manufacturing strategies to mitigate risks and improve
resilience. The group debated the need for new standards specific to data center
interconnections, potential roles for NERC, the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers
(IEEE), and industry consortia, and phased compliance strategies to balance security with

economic considerations. The session culminated in an action planning exercise, assigning
responsibilities to utilities, developers, vendors, regulators, and DOE/INL for addressing
cybersecurity, supply chain, reliability, and institutional barriers. Closing discussions
emphasized collaboration and continuous engagement to ensure secure, resilient integration of
data centers into the grid.

Identified Challenges and Industry Feedback
Focus Area 1. Interconnection Architecture Exposes Cyber Vulnerabilities
 Participants consistently ranked interconnection architecture weaknesses as one of the
most urgent challenges for secure data center integration. The utility–data center boundary
introduces multiple unmanaged attack pathways, creating exposure points that demand
immediate attention. Flat network designs, inadequate DMZ implementation, and shared
telecom links were highlighted as structural flaws that enable lateral movement and
interception risks. Remote vendor access for maintenance further complicates security, often
emerging as a requirement after deployment and leaving utilities unable to monitor behind-
the-meter activity. Additionally, expanding telemetry for grid coordination adds new systems
and application programming interfaces (APIs) that increase the cyber-attack surface,
particularly when authentication and token management are weak. These vulnerabilities
underscore the need for robust segmentation, formalized vendor access controls, and stronger
security protocols at the interface between utilities and data centers.
 1. Flat networks, inadequate DMZ, and shared telecom links create attack pathways
 • The lack of network segmentation may allow lateral movement across utility and
 data center systems. While both industries have standard practices for boundary
 control, within the boundary of individual organization’s networks, participants
 identified that there is still a lack of segmentation that could enable lateral
 movement for potential adversaries.
 • Shared communication infrastructure remains vulnerable to spoofing and
 interception attacks.

 5

 • Participants emphasized the critical importance of proper DMZ implementation
 at interconnection points to ensure boundary control between organizations.
 2. Remote vendor access for maintenance introduces unmanaged attack surfaces
 • Equipment warranties and service agreements often require remote vendor
 access discovered post-deployment. While this access is needed for legitimate
 business purposes and maintenance support, the persistent access for third-
 parties is viewed as a risk.
 • Utilities cannot monitor or validate activity behind the meter. This is both an
 operational and cyber risk, as behavior of data centers has been shown to have
 reliability impacts on utilities. The potential for cyber manipulation to affect the
 operations of a data center, and correspondingly, their power consumption, is
 viewed as a risk by utilities.
 • Insider threats from third-party and external access to control systems remain
 significant concern.
 3. Grid coordination telemetry expands cyber exposure beyond facility boundaries
 • New monitoring requirements create additional systems requiring protection
 and validation. While this additional data provides valuable insights and can be
 used to optimize system dispatch, new systems, especially digitally connected
 systems, create more attack surface susceptible to attack.
 • Participants expressed concern that cooling, UPS, and BESS dashboards rely on
 third-party APIs with authentication vulnerabilities. These types of dashboards,
 often web-based, may not have been designed with security as a top priority, but
 in their current applications with the potential to influence the behaviors of large
 loads, they now present risk that must be addressed.
 • Cloud-based monitoring platforms demonstrate weak token management and
 excessive data exposure risks. Solutions should be carefully vetted and inspected
 regularly to mitigate vulnerable platforms.
Focus Area 2. Fragmented Governance Leaves Accountability Undefined
 Governance gaps emerged as a top concern, with participants emphasizing that fragmented
responsibilities across utilities, developers, landlords, and third-party vendors create significant
ambiguity in accountability and enforcement. Organizational and contractual boundaries often
fail to reflect operational realities, leaving critical questions unanswered about who owns trip
settings, alarm responses, and configuration management. Utilities may have monitoring
capabilities but lack clear authority to enforce compliance when violations occur, creating a
reliance on drastic measures such as full disconnection—an option with cascading implications
for gigawatt-scale loads. The absence of standardized enforcement mechanisms and
responsibility matrices compounds these challenges, particularly in complex ownership

 6

structures. This lack of clarity not only increases operational risk but also hinders the
development of coordinated cybersecurity and reliability strategies across stakeholders.
 4. Organizational and contractual boundaries are often misaligned with system realities
 • Contract demarcations don't reflect how electrons, cyber signals, and
 operational decisions cross organizational lines. This misalignment creates
 challenges in identifying the roles and responsibilities for various stakeholders in
 executing digital assurance measures.
 • Landlord-tenant structures and third-party ownership models complicate power
 delivery responsibilities. These complex organizational structures are unlikely to
 go away, so fair methods for assigning responsibility and liability are needed.
 • Federal, commercial, and utility entities share data with different protection
 procedures creating disconnected responsibilities. There is no standardization of
 data sharing practices across these industry demarcations, and different
 organizations may have different legal and practical methods in place for data
 protection.
 5. Utilities can monitor but lack enforcement authority when violations occur
 • Enforcement mechanisms are not defined to handle the situations when data
 centers violate coordination requirements or ramp rate limits. Several regional
 transmission operators and independent system operators are updating
 procedures to address these challenges, but these requirements typically lag the
 current state of the industry, leaving gaps.
 • Often, the enforcement mechanism for data centers that violate coordination
 requirements is to fully disconnect these loads from the system. However, this
 can create other issues as a consequence, given that the disconnection or large
 loads requires rebalance of generation, too.
 • A gap exists between the monitoring capability to assess data center behaviors
 and the ability to compel real-time compliance.
 6. Third-party, landlord-tenant, utility responsibilities are unclear for shared systems
 • Trip settings, alarms, and configuration management ownership are undefined
 across organizational boundaries.
 • The level of cyber security and risk assessments being performed by various
 parties remains unclear. Greater communication across different sectors is
 needed to create shared understanding and build trust.
 • Enforcement mechanisms for agreements lack standardization at the multi-
 gigawatt level. Financial penalties or operational disconnection are both options,
 but each has benefits and drawbacks that limit widespread adoption of a
 standard practice.

Focus Area 3. AI-Driven Load Behavior Creates Stability and Cyber-Physical Risks
 The rise of AI-driven workloads introduces unprecedented dynamics that challenge
traditional grid coordination and create novel cyber-physical risks. Participants noted that
gigawatt-scale facilities operating with millisecond variability represent a fundamental shift
from conventional industrial load profiles. These rapid oscillations, inherent to AI learning
processes, can outpace generation response and trigger protective relay actions across multiple
facilities, risking large-scale cascading trips. Beyond stability concerns, adversaries could exploit
these dynamics by submitting computationally unsolvable tasks, sustaining maximum loads
until protection systems activate—an attack vector requiring no malware or traditional
intrusion techniques. The combination of scale, speed, and unpredictability makes AI-driven
load behavior a critical area for immediate attention, demanding new approaches to workload
management, real-time monitoring, and automated curtailment strategies.
 7. Gigawatt-scale facilities with millisecond dynamics create challenges on new scales to
 address in power system stability studies. Load oscillations may occur during AI
 learning which are faster than the speed with which generators can respond.
 • Single facilities at 1-2 gigwatts could represent 20% of major city total electrical
 demand. Small percentage changes in load use at these facilities may appear as
 larger net impacts on the grid.
 • The load behavior of data centers does not resemble traditional industrial loads.
 For operators, this limits the efficacy of some of their traditional tools for load
 management, such as warnings or manual curtailment.
 • There is an inherent variability to the computational power requirements for AI
 learning processes. This variability, which has nothing to do with malfunctions or
 cyber attacks, can still create operational challenges for utilities.
 • Though traditional generation dispatch timelines may not be sufficient for the
 fast-ramping large loads that data centers may create, battery systems are
 attractive because they have the millisecond response capabilities that can
 match AI load dynamics.
 8. Unsolvable compute tasks can tie up data center resources and create large, sustained
 loads, an attack vector by which a compromised or malicious data center tenant could
 create unnecessary power demands
 • Adversaries could provide computationally unsolvable problems for data centers
 to process, either as legitimate clients of a computing facility or by compromising
 legitimate tenants. Grid operators perceive this threat vector as one that could
 cause sustained maximum compute loads.
 • In a worst case scenario, the prolonged high load may exceed the planned
 operational behavior of a data center, which creates a digital vector for

 operational impact that requires no malware or traditional cyber intrusion
 techniques.
Focus Area 4. Lack of Standards and Regulatory Frameworks
 Participants identified the absence of cybersecurity standards for data centers at grid scale
as a major regulatory blind spot. Current frameworks, such as NERC CIP, remain generation-
centric and do not adequately address behind-the-meter multi-gigawatt installations or
emerging technologies like small modular reactors for on-site power. Interconnection
agreements are negotiated on a project-by-project basis without standardized digital assurance
requirements, leaving enforcement authority undefined and inconsistent across regions. This
gap is compounded by significant infrastructure variation, which prevents the adoption of
uniform designs and response protocols. Without clear standards and governance models,
stakeholders face uncertainty in compliance obligations and risk management, underscoring
the need for coordinated efforts to establish baseline cybersecurity requirements and phased
implementation strategies.
 9. There is a lack of enforcement or incentivization for application cybersecurity
 standards apply to data centers at grid scale currently
 • There are no mandatory cybersecurity requirements for data center power
 system operations. Some cyber requirements may apply on the information
 technology (IT) side, but the lack of applicability on the OT side is viewed as a
 gap.
 • Interconnection agreements for data centers are negotiated project-by-project
 basis and do not include standardized digital assurance requirement templates.
 • Participants reported they were not aware of examples of interconnection
 agreements containing cyber security provisions.
 10. NERC regulations focus on generation assets, not loads, and even multi-gigawatt
 generation may be out of scope when located behind-the-meter
 • All Balancing Authority requirements focus on generation assets; load-side
 coordination mechanisms are nonexistent.
 • Although NERC regulations apply to generation resources, behind-meter
 generation is typically exempt from cybersecurity requirements if consumed on-
 site. This is true despite multi-gigawatt capacity of some on-site generators.
 • Participants shared that they view recent announcements of small modular
 reactors for data center power as a regulatory loophole at scale.
 11. Regional infrastructure variation challenges standardization and repeatable designs
 • Response time capabilities of regional transmission and generation coordination
 vary from 6 milliseconds to 25-30 milliseconds across different regions.

 9

 • Some states have widely deployed sophisticated digital infrastructure, while
 others still rely largely on analog solutions. Even within states, significant
 differences in operational technologies and practices can vary dramatically
 between investor-owned utilities, electric cooperatives, and municipal utilities or
 even between different companies.
 • Because of this broad range of deployed technology and operational practices,
 the desire of data centers for standardized power system designs or interfaces
 with the grid conflicts with the reality of local grid capabilities.
Focus Area 5. Supply Chain Recognized as Long-Term Concern, Not Immediate
Operational Risk
 While supply chain integrity was acknowledged as a strategic concern, participants ranked it
lower than immediate operational coordination challenges. Discussions revealed that current
priorities center on deployed systems and real-time reliability risks, with supply chain
considerations largely framed around tax credit eligibility and FEOC compliance rather than
digital assurance. Issues such as firmware integrity, remote access requirements, and vendor
risk assessments were noted but not treated as urgent compared to governance and
interconnection vulnerabilities. This perspective reflects a gap between compliance-driven
procurement practices and cybersecurity-focused vendor evaluation, highlighting the need for
future frameworks that integrate supply chain security into operational risk management
without detracting from near-term reliability objectives.

 12. Participants prioritized immediate coordination challenges over supply chain issues
 • In a ranking exercise, participants placed supply chain security challenges last in
 their priorities, despite repeated organic mentions throughout discussions.
 • Risks associated with heavy reliance on foreign vendors, third-party remote
 access requirements, and firmware integrity were issues raised by participants
 but not discussed as immediate priorities
 • Participants noted that their operational teams must focus on deployed system
 challenges rather than procurement decisions that have already been made.
 13. Supply chain discussed for tax credits and FEOC compliance, not digital assurance
 • Supply chain conversations centered on country of origin for economic
 compliance, not cyber risk. While participants understood that reliance on
 supplies from adversarial nations was a risk, their immediate concerns were
 about limiting these suppliers for tax credit eligibility rather than operational
 security considerations.
 • Evidence suggests compliance drivers not translating into digital assurance
 requirements for vendor selection
