High-flow nasal cannula oxygenation in sedated endoscopy for high-risk obstructive sleep apnea patients: study protocol for a multicentre randomised controlled trial
Authors: Mingxia Xu, Saiheng Xiang, Yongmin Pi, Yuhuan Zhu, Xiaotong Chen, Qinqin Yang, Huawei Wu, Kan Deng, Yi Yin, Diansan Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypoxemia is the most common adverse event during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy. Patients with high obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) risk (STOP-Bang ≥5) are susceptible due to sedation-induced loss of upper airway tone exacerbating airway collapsibility. Although high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) benefits general at-risk populations, its efficacy in this specific cohort remains uncertain, as its mild positive pressure falls far below therapeutic continuous positive airway pressure levels for moderate-to-severe OSA, questioning its ability to stent the collapsible airway. Our prior proof-of-concept study in this cohort observed a 5% incidence of hypoxemia with HFNC, confirming feasibility and safety and justifying this confirmatory trial.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled single-blind trial will enroll 600 adults (STOP-Bang score ≥5) undergoing elective sedated gastroenteroscopy across three centers. Participants will be 1:1 randomized (stratified by center) to HFNC (30 L/min pre-oxygenation, 60 L/min post-induction) or conventional nasal cannula (6 L/min). Both groups receive standardized propofol-alfentanil sedation. The primary outcome is the proportion of patients with at least one episode of hypoxemia (SpO2 75%–90% <60 s). Secondary outcomes include the proportion of patients with at least one episode of severe hypoxemia (SpO2 <75% or 75%≤SpO2<90% ≥60 s), the proportion with subclinical respiratory depression (90%≤SpO2<95%), and the frequency of other adverse events.DiscussionThis trial will provide definitive evidence on HFNC’s efficacy in high-risk OSA patients, addressing whether it can overcome pressure limitations to prevent hypoxemia. Results are expected to inform sedation management guidelines, establish a new standard of care for this subgroup, and enhance procedural safety.Trial registrationThe trial was registered at the ClinicalTrials.gov on 14 December 2025 (NCT07307560).

Introduction

Hypoxemia represents the most frequent and clinically significant adverse event during procedural sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy [1–7], with potentially serious sequelae including myocardial ischemia, arrhythmias, and neurological injury [8–10]. Among all patient subgroups, individuals at high risk of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) – identified by a STOP-Bang score ≥5 – face uniquely elevated vulnerability [11–13]. This risk stems from inherent anatomical airway collapsibility and functional impairment of upper airway muscles, which are dramatically potentiated by sedation-induced loss of pharyngeal muscle tone [14–18]. Critically, a large proportion of OSA patients remain undiagnosed [19], making the STOP-Bang questionnaire a pragmatic and validated tool to identify this high-risk cohort in whom vigilant oxygenation management is imperative [19–21]. Addressing this unmet clinical need requires a targeted oxygenation strategy to mitigate dynamic airway collapse – an unmet challenge with conventional approaches.

Conventional low-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy frequently fails to prevent desaturation in these patients, as it does not address the dynamic airway collapse that drives hypoxemia. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen therapy emerges as a physiologically advanced alternative, delivering heated, humidified gas at high flows to reduce anatomical dead space, minimize CO2 rebreathing, and generate mild continuous positive airway pressure [22–24]. While HFNC has demonstrated efficacy in broader at-risk populations such as obese patients [14,24], its utility in the specifically defined high-risk cohort (STOP-Bang ≥5) has long been mechanistically contentious and clinically unproven. This translational challenge stems from a key pathophysiological paradox: the positive pressure generated by HFNC (typically 3–5 cmH2O) is considerably lower than the therapeutic continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) levels required to alleviate upper airway obstruction in moderate-to-severe OSA (6–12 cmH2O or higher) [25,26]. Consistent with this physiological limitation, even in voluntary sleep therapy, HFNC is substantially less effective than CPAP in reducing apnea-hypopnea events and improving sleep architecture, with efficacy declining clearly as OSA severity increases [25,26]. Furthermore, under sedation, factors like mouth breathing and variable respiratory patterns can critically undermine the stability of this already limited pressure support [27–30], casting doubt on its reliability for this specific indication. Despite these compelling mechanistic concerns, clinical evidence for HFNC in this cohort remains ambiguous: subgroup analyses (e.g. Riccio et al. [31]) have only reported non-significant numerical trends toward benefit in the STOP-Bang ≥5 subgroup. Thus, a critical evidence gap persists: whether HFNC can provide sustained, adequate oxygenation support for the most vulnerable OSA cohort remains an unresolved yet clinically urgent question.

To address this critical evidence gap and validate HFNC’s potential utility in this high-risk cohort, we first conducted a prospective Simon two-stage proof-of-concept study (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07155330). This preliminary trial confirmed feasibility, yielded a promising efficacy signal – with favorable hypoxemia reduction, a favorable safety profile, and high endoscopist/patient satisfaction – collectively supporting the rationale for definitive investigation. However, its single-arm, single-center design limits causal inference and generalizability; notably, no multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) to date has focused exclusively on the STOP-Bang ≥5 cohort – an important distinction given the dose-dependent relationship between OSA severity and hypoxemia risk [6]. Compounding this gap, current clinical guidelines lack specific guidance for this subgroup, leaving clinicians with limited evidence-based direction [17].

Thus, the present multicenter randomized controlled trial is designed to provide definitive, high-level evidence by directly comparing HFNC with conventional nasal cannula oxygenation in this phenotypically homogeneous cohort undergoing sedated gastroenteroscopy. Critically, the physiological context of short‑term procedural sedation differs fundamentally from that of chronic sleep therapy, and this distinction justifies re‑evaluating HFNC in the sedated endoscopy setting. As the first RCT of its kind, this study aims to resolve clinical equipoise, inform major societal guidelines, and establish an evidence-based standard of care for this identifiable and vulnerable population – with results poised to either validate HFNC as a pragmatic first-line strategy or confirm the continued role of conventional oxygenation, ultimately enhancing procedural safety worldwide.

Materials and methods

Research design

This will be a prospective, multicenter, single-blinded, parallel-group randomized controlled trial. A total of 600 adult patients (aged ≥18 years) at high risk of OSA, defined by a STOP-Bang score ≥5 (Table 1), and scheduled for elective sedated gastroenteroscopy (including gastroscopy, colonoscopy, or combined procedures) will be recruited from three participating centers: The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Hangzhou, China); The First Hospital of Jiaxing (Jiaxing, China); and Quzhou People’s Hospital (Quzhou, China). The study protocol adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2025 guidelines.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on preliminary data and published literature[24], which indicated that the proportion of patients experiencing hypoxemia (defined as 75% ≤ SpO2 < 90% for < 60 s) was approximately 20% in those receiving conventional nasal cannula oxygen during sedated gastroenteroscopy. We hypothesize that HFNC oxygen therapy will reduce this proportion to 10%. Sample size was calculated using PASS 15.0 for two independent proportions with a two-sided α = 0.05, power = 0.90, assuming hypoxemia incidence of 20% in the control group and 10% in the HFNC group. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, a total of 600 patients (300 per group) were required.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization: An independent biostatistician who will not participate in subsequent data management or statistical analysis will generate the randomization sequence. Using a computer-generated stratified block randomization method with variable block sizes of 4, 6, and 8, eligible participants will be allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the HFNC group or the conventional nasal cannula group. Stratification will be performed by study center to account for inter-site variability. The allocation sequence will be concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which will be opened only after the patient has provided written informed consent and is ready for sedation initiation.

Blinding: Due to the nature of the interventions, the anesthesia providers administering oxygen therapy cannot be blinded to group assignment. However, participants will be blinded to their treatment allocation. To minimize assessment bias, outcome assessment and data collection will be performed by an independent research assistant who is not actively informed of the group allocation (but no physical or acoustic measures are taken to prevent the assistant from potentially deducing the allocation based on device noise or appearance). Furthermore, the statistician performing the final analyses will remain blinded to group allocation until the database is locked.

Intervention

Patients will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to one of the following two groups:HFNC group (experimental group): Patients will receive oxygen via a HFNC device. The flow rate will be set at 30 L/min during pre-oxygenation and will be increased to 60 L/min after the target sedation depth is achieved (Ramsay Sedation Score ≥5), maintaining this rate until the end of the procedure. Study procedures

Upon entering the endoscopic procedure room, intravenous access will be established. Standard monitoring will be applied, including continuous electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry (SpO2), and capnography via a nasal sampling line. Patients will be positioned in the left lateral decubitus position.

A standardized team comprising an endoscopist, a nursing assistant, and an anesthesiologist in each procedure room will provide continuous care to the patient. After intravenous access is established and the patient is positioned, baseline SpO2, heart rate, and blood pressure will be recorded, and supplemental oxygen will be initiated immediately via the randomly assigned delivery device.

For participants allocated to the HFNC group, pre-oxygenation will be conducted at a flow rate of 30 L/min for one minute prior to the administration of any sedative agents. Participants in the control group will receive oxygen at 6 L/min via a conventional nasal cannula. All subjects will be monitored continuously throughout the procedure with pulse oximetry, electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, and end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2).

A standardized sedation protocol will be followed: alfentanil (4 µg/kg) will be administered intravenously at the beginning of pre-oxygenation, followed one minute later by intravenous propofol (1–2 mg/kg). The anesthesiologist will continuously monitor the depth of sedation using the Ramsay Sedation Scale. To ensure consistent sedation depth monitoring, the Ramsay Sedation Score will be assessed every minute from the start of sedation induction until the patient awakens. Endoscope insertion will commence when a score of ≥5 is reached. At this point, the flow rate in the HFNC group will be increased to 60 L/min (temperature 37 °C, FiO2 100%), while the oxygen flow in the control group will remain unchanged.

To maintain a Ramsay score of ≥5 during the examination, supplemental propofol boluses (0.2–0.5 mg/kg) may be administered as needed. After completion of the gastroscopy and before transitioning to colonoscopy, an additional intravenous dose of alfentanil (1 µg/kg) may be given, with further doses permitted based on clinical assessment. All medications will be administered over a minimum of 10 s, with at least a one-minute interval between repeated administrations.

Patients will be transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) once sufficiently awake. Upon arrival, they will receive supplemental oxygen via conventional nasal cannula at 2–6 L/min, with continuous monitoring of SpO2, respiratory rate, heart rate, and noninvasive blood pressure. The Aldrete Score will be assessed every 5 min. Patients will be eligible for discharge when the score is ≥ 9 and they are accompanied by a caregiver, at which point the study will be terminated upon PACU discharge.

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome measure will be the proportion of patients with at least one episode of intraprocedural hypoxemia, defined as 75% ≤ SpO2 < 90% for less than 60 s, in patients undergoing sedated gastroenteroscopy.

Remedial and supportive treatment

Airway intervention will be initiated if any of the following occurs: a 50% reduction or loss of the EtCO2 waveform, absence of chest wall movement, or SpO2 < 95%. A standardized, sequential approach to airway management will be followed until SpO2 ≥95% is restored. The final effective airway intervention will be recorded. Both anesthesia providers and the independent research assistant will receive standardized training on these criteria; the research assistant is authorized to remind the anesthesiologist to initiate airway management when any of the above thresholds is met.

Safety evaluation procedure

In this study, the risk of bleeding – such as that associated with oral or pharyngeal tumors, inflammation, or infection – will be systematically assessed. The use of HFNC therapy is not recommended for patients presenting with these conditions.

Termination and exit procedures

Participants may be withdrawn from the study based on the following criteria: (1) voluntary withdrawal of informed consent; (2) post-enrollment discovery of a major violation of inclusion or exclusion criteria; or (3) occurrence of a major protocol deviation that compromises the assessment of primary endpoints.

Study participation will be terminated immediately if either of the following occurs: (1) a serious adverse event considered clearly related to the study intervention (e.g. cardiac arrest, severe anaphylaxis, or life-threatening respiratory complications); or (2) the investigator judges that continued participation poses an unacceptable risk to the participant’s safety.

In all such cases, participants will receive appropriate medical management in accordance with standard clinical care. All data collected up to the point of withdrawal or termination will be retained for analysis, and the reason for discontinuation will be documented in the case report form.

Statistical methodology and analysis plan

General considerations

All data analyses will be performed in accordance with a pre-specified statistical analysis plan. Computations will be conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Unless otherwise specified, all statistical tests will be two-sided, with a p-value of <0.05 considered statistically significant (α = 0.05). The primary efficacy analyses will adhere to the intention-to-treat principle.

Analysis sets

The following three analysis sets will be defined:Full Analysis Set (FAS): This set will include all randomized patients who receive at least one dose of the investigational treatment. It will serve as the primary dataset for all main efficacy analyses.Per-Protocol Set (PPS): This set will consist of patients from the FAS, excluding those with major protocol violations (e.g., significant deviations from the specified sedative dosing regimen or missing primary outcome data) that could materially affect the assessment of the primary endpoint. The PPS will be used for supportive sensitivity analyses.Safety Analysis Set (SS): This set will comprise all patients who receive at least one dose of the investigational treatment and have at least one post-baseline safety assessment. All safety analyses will be based on this set.

Demographic and baseline characteristics variables

All baseline demographic and clinical characteristics will be summarized descriptively by treatment group. Continuous variables will be presented as mean (standard deviation) if normally distributed or as median (interquartile range) otherwise. Normality of distribution will be assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables will be presented as frequency (percentage). Comparability between the treatment groups at baseline will be assessed using appropriate statistical tests: the independent samples t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as applicable. It is important to note that the p-values from these baseline comparisons are for descriptive purposes only, to aid in assessing the success of randomization and group comparability. They are not used as inclusion or adjustment criteria in any subsequent efficacy or safety analyses.

Analysis of efficacy outcomes

Analysis Population: All primary and dichotomous secondary efficacy analyses will be based on the Full Analysis Set, adhering to the intention-to-treat principle.

Primary Outcome: The between-group difference in the proportion of patients with at least one episode of clinically significant hypoxemia (defined as 75% ≤ SpO2 < 90% for < 60 s) will be assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The treatment effect will be expressed as the relative risk (RR) with its 95% confidence interval (CI). The absolute risk difference with its 95% CI will also be reported. Within-group proportions and their 95% CIs will be calculated using the Clopper-Pearson exact method.

Secondary outcomes

All dichotomous secondary outcomes – specifically the proportion of patients with at least one episode of subclinical respiratory depression (SpO2 90% to <95% for any duration) and the proportion of patients with at least one episode of severe hypoxemia (SpO2 <75% for any duration or 75% to <90% for ≥60 s) – will be analyzed following the same statistical approach as the primary outcome, based on the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle.

Analysis of safety outcomes

Safety analyses will be performed on the Safety Set. Adverse events will be categorized, graded for severity, and assessed for relationship to the investigational treatment according to the World SIVA International Sedation Task Force criteria. The proportions of subjects experiencing any adverse event, treatment-related adverse events, and serious adverse events will be compared between treatment groups using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Sensitivity analyses and handling of missing data

Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the primary outcome findings. First, the primary analysis will be repeated using the per-protocol set to evaluate the impact of protocol deviations. Second, to evaluate the influence of missing data, the following approach will be applied: if missing data for the primary outcome constitute ≤5% of observations, the primary analysis will be based on the complete case population. If the proportion exceeds 5%, multiple imputation using chained equations will be implemented as the primary method for handling missing data, with the complete case analysis provided as a supportive sensitivity analysis. Third, in a sensitivity analysis, we will further adjust for total propofol dose (mg/kg) as exploratory covariate to assess their potential confounding effects, although these variables are partially collinear with procedure duration and may reflect between-center practice variation.

To account for potential confounding, a prespecified multivariable logistic regression model will be fitted. This model will include the treatment group as the primary predictor and will be adjusted for the following baseline covariates: baseline oxygen saturation (SpO2), age, and body mass index (BMI), and induction propofol dose (mg/kg). The adjusted odds ratio (with its 95% confidence interval) for the effect of the intervention on hypoxemia risk will be calculated and compared with the unadjusted estimate from the primary analysis.

Subgroup analysis

Prespecified subgroup analyses will be conducted to examine the consistency of the HFNC effect and to explore potential effect modifiers across clinically relevant subgroups. Subgroups are defined as follows: BMI categories (<35 vs. ≥35 kg/m2), STOP-Bang score(5–6 vs. 7–8), propofol induction dose (<1.5 vs ≥1.5 mg/kg). The primary outcome will be compared between treatment groups within each subgroup using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. To test for effect modification, an interaction term (treatment × subgroup) will be included in Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel models; a P value <0.10 for the interaction will be considered suggestive of a differential treatment effect. All subgroup analyses are exploratory, and results will be interpreted cautiously in light of multiple comparisons.

Interim analyses

No interim analyses are planned for this trial. To preserve trial integrity and prevent potential bias, the research team will not have access to unblinded interim data and is not authorized to make decisions regarding early trial termination based on such data.

Data management

Data collection and recording

All study data, including patient demographics, baseline characteristics, STOP-Bang scores, intraprocedural physiological parameters (SpO2, EtCO2, heart rate, blood pressure), procedural details, medication administration records, and all prespecified outcomes, will be collected prospectively. To minimize assessment bias and ensure objective endpoint adjudication, a dedicated research assistant will be responsible for real-time data recording. This assistant will not be proactively informed of the treatment allocation, and will remain independent of the clinical anesthesia team. Data will be recorded onto standardized paper Case Report Forms (CRFs) based on direct observation and digital outputs from patient monitors. Prior to the commencement of subject enrollment, this assistant will undergo mandatory, protocol-specific training on all endpoint definitions and data collection procedures.

Data entry, validation, and quality control

Data from the completed paper CRFs will be transcribed into structured, password-protected Microsoft Excel templates at each participating site. To maximize accuracy, an independent double-data entry procedure will be employed for all critical variables, including the primary outcome, secondary outcomes, serious adverse events, and key baseline parameters. Any discrepancies identified during the comparison will be documented in a discrepancy report. The site coordinator will then adjudicate each item against the original paper CRF and document the resolution in a reconciliation log to create a single, corrected dataset for each site. The study will be conducted in compliance with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines and the study protocol, with ethical oversight provided by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine.

Data management data security, confidentiality, and archival

The study will be conducted in accordance with ICH-GCP guidelines and applicable data protection regulations. All participants will be assigned a unique study identification code. Adverse event reporting and harms

HFNC therapy is a well-established respiratory support modality with a favorable safety profile. Throughout the trial, all adverse events (AEs) – defined as any unfavorable medical occurrence in a participant – will be systematically documented and assessed for severity and potential relationship to the study intervention. Both anticipated and unanticipated events will be fully reported. Any serious adverse event (SAE), including outcomes such as death, life-threatening conditions, hospitalization, significant disability, or congenital anomalies, must be reported by the site investigator to the study’s Principal Investigator (PI) and the lead center’s safety officer within 24 h of awareness. A detailed SAE report form will be completed, documenting the event’s timing, severity, duration, measures taken, and final outcome.

In this multi-center trial, the Principal Investigator is responsible for promptly notifying the central Ethics Committee and the Study Steering Committee of all SAEs in accordance with regulatory timelines. The lead center and the Steering Committee will coordinate safety oversight. All SAEs will be actively followed until resolution, stabilization, or until a definitive assessment concludes the event is no longer attributable to the study intervention. Participant safety remains the highest priority throughout the trial duration.

Discussion

This multicenter RCT is strategically designed to resolve a critical clinical uncertainty: whether HFNC oxygen therapy outperforms conventional low-flow nasal cannula in preventing intraprocedural hypoxemia in high-risk OSA patients (STOP-Bang score ≥5) undergoing sedated gastroenteroscopy.

The rationale for this RCT stems from two interconnected imperatives. First, patients with a STOP-Bang score ≥5 face uniquely elevated sedation-induced hypoxemia risk, driven by inherent airway vulnerability and sedation-related loss of protective reflexes [11–18]. This hypoxemia is not benign (associated with cardiac arrhythmias and neurological injury [8–10]), and conventional low-flow oxygen fails to counteract dynamic airway collapse. Second, HFNC’s utility in this cohort remains debated due to a key paradox: its modest pressure generation (3–5 cmH2O) is insufficient to match CPAP’s therapeutic dose for moderate-to-severe OSA [25,26], and sedation-related factors further destabilize its support [33–37]. Prior subgroup analyses (e.g. Riccio et al. [31]) only provided non-significant trends, lacking dedicated focus or statistical power. Our prospective Simon two-stage proof-of-concept study (NCT07155330) addressed this gap, observing a proportion of patients with hypoxemia of 5% (95% CI 2.0–12.2%) in the HFNC group and confirming procedural feasibility in this exact cohort. This robust preliminary signal directly informed our sample size calculation – a conservative 50% relative risk reduction in hypoxemia (from 20% to 10%) vs. conventional oxygenation [24] – ensuring the trial is powered to detect a clinically meaningful difference in line with CONSORT and SPIRIT 2025 guidelines [38,39].

To ensure definitive evidence consistent with top anesthesia and gastroenterology journal standards, the trial incorporates targeted methodological safeguards. Center-stratified block randomization mitigates inter-center practice variability (SPIRIT 2025 statement) [39] . The primary outcome – clinically significant hypoxemia defined as 75% ≤ SpO2 < 90% for < 60 s [32] – is objectively measured and continuously monitored, eliminating subjective bias. A partial single-blind design (blinded participants and statisticians) mitigates performance and detection bias, addressing a key limitation of our open-label proof-of-concept study. The sample size of 600 patients provides 90% statistical power to detect the hypothesized treatment effect, with prespecified sensitivity analyses (per-protocol set) and multivariable logistic regression adjustment (age, BMI, baseline SpO2) planned to account for protocol deviations and potential confounding, further enhancing the robustness of our results.

The trial’s anticipated findings will have direct clinical and guideline implications, with subgroup analyses advancing personalized medicine. If HFNC demonstrates superiority, it will establish a pragmatic first-line strategy for high-risk OSA patients – reducing severe hypoxemia and invasive interventions – despite its limitations in chronic OSA therapy. This apparent procedure-specific benefit of HFNC – despite its limitations in chronic OSA management – likely stems from its unique physiological effects in the acute sedation setting: anatomical dead space reduction, effective CO2 washout, and nasopharyngeal oxygen reservoir formation [22–24], which collectively outweigh its modest pressure generation. Conversely, a finding of no significant difference would suggest insufficient evidence to support the superiority of HFNC, thereby supporting the continued use of conventional low-flow oxygen therapy, avoiding unnecessary healthcare resource utilization in a setting where cost and accessibility remain critical considerations [24]. Our prespecified subgroup analyses – including BMI (<35 vs. ≥35 kg/m2), STOP-Bang score (5–6 vs. 7–8), and propofol induction dose (<1.5 vs ≥1.5 mg/kg) – will identify phenotypes that derive the greatest benefit from HFNC: for example, patients with a STOP-Bang score of 5–6 may benefit most from HFNC’s oxygen reservoir effects, while those with a score of 7–8 may require more robust pressure support (e.g. CPAP) to prevent airway collapse [25,26]. These insights will move beyond a “one-size-fits-all” approach to sedation oxygenation and inform future targeted trials.

Technical considerations regarding HFNC application. First, we used fixed HFNC parameters (60 L/min, 100% FiO2) without individualised titration. This standardised high-intensity protocol is suitable for short-duration procedures (≤45 min) and aims to maximise oxygen reserve and pharyngeal pressure in this high-risk OSA cohort while ensuring consistency across multiple centres. Future studies could explore adaptive designs, such as flow titration based on SpO2/EtCO2 or FiO2 weaning guided by the oxygen reserve index. Second, mouth breathing may attenuate HFNC-generated positive pressure. For colonoscopy alone, patients can be instructed to keep the mouth closed; for gastroscopy or combined procedures, a mouthpiece is required, making mouth opening unavoidable. This provides a stringent test of HFNC under adverse conditions – efficacy despite unavoidable mouth opening would support its clinical robustness. We will document procedure type and analyse its potential impact on HFNC efficacy to inform future individualised strategies.

Several limitations warrant transparent acknowledgment. First, although only participants are masked to allocation, performance bias is mitigated by objective SpO2 data from independent recorders (not proactively informed of group assignment) and by blinded statistical analysis. Second, excluding patients with ASA ≥ IV, baseline SpO2 ≤ 92%, or chronic lung diseases (e.g. COPD) limits generalizability to critically ill populations and those with common overlap syndromes – important future directions. Third, HFNC parameters were fixed (60 L/min, 100% FiO2) to ensure protocol consistency and avoid confounding in this pragmatic trial, but future studies could individualize settings – e.g. flow titration (30–70 L/min) based on BMI or OSA severity, and FiO2 step-down after stable oxygenation. Fourth, while we systematically record intraprocedural and immediate post-procedural adverse events (up to discharge), longer-term or post-recovery consequences in OSA patients are not assessed. Future studies should incorporate extended monitoring or patient-reported outcomes.

In conclusion, this multicenter RCT is uniquely positioned to resolve longstanding uncertainty surrounding HFNC use in high-risk OSA patients undergoing sedated gastroenteroscopy. Its rigorous design (adhering to SPIRIT 2025, CONSORT, ICMJE guidelines) and robust preliminary data will generate definitive evidence to inform clinical practice and update guidelines. Beyond answering the primary efficacy question, the trial’s prespecified subgroup analyses will provide insights to inform personalized airway management, ultimately enhancing patient safety worldwide.
