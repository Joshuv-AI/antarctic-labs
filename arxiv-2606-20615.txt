Specifying AI-SDLC Processes: A Protocol Language for Human-Agent Boundaries
Ylli Prifti

Abstract
AI agents now participate as first-class team members across the software development lifecycle, yet no specification language exists for expressing the human-agent responsibility boundaries, approval gates, and governance constraints that this collaboration requires. Existing approaches encode process in agent prompts (subject to drift), target adjacent domains (workflow management, business processes), or address only fragments of the problem (access control, approval gates). Teams consequently adopt AI-SDLC workflows through ad-hoc tooling. We propose a domain-specific language for specifying AI-SDLC processes as protocols, with formal abstract syntax, well-formedness conditions, operational semantics, and enforcement invariants. The language distinguishes policy (declared intent) from mechanism (structural enforcement), enabling implementations to bound process non-determinism through primitives such as validation tokens and capability boundaries. Three results follow. First, a failure rate analysis shows that structural enforcement bounds system failure rates at a weighted product of agent and validator rates, while configurations relying on behavioural compliance permit cumulative or near-saturating growth. Second, the 2+N team pattern—two human-in-control roles plus N specialized agent members—formalizes classical Separation of Duties for AI-SDLC as a reference configuration. Third, Kleene closure of orchestration loops and reflexive protocol-adherence validation arise as emergent properties of the design, rather than as special-case constructs. We position the contribution against multi-agent frameworks (MetaGPT), workflow specification (FlowAgent, BPMN extensions), and capability-based security (SAGA): the novelty lies in the specific integration, not any single primitive. A working implementation demonstrates feasibility, and simulation studies characterise the enforcement layer: disagreement-policy trade-offs, governance overhead, the failure-rate model, and Byzantine robustness. End-to-end evaluation on real software tasks remains future work.



1  Introduction

Recent practitioner analysis documents the emergence of AI-SDLC: software development workflows where AI systems participate alongside humans across planning, coding, reviewing, and deployment. Industry reports identify that success requires defining “human-agent responsibility mapping: responsibilities, decisions, approval gates and authority levels are explicitly divided between humans and the agent”
[
EPAM Systems(2026)
]
, yet explicitly ask: “Do we have standards or frameworks for prompting and supervising multi-agent contributions?”
[
Grid Dynamics(2026)
]
. Multiple sources note that “when used without governance and security, it creates fragmentation, technical debt, and serious operational risk”
[
Ran The Builder(2026)
]
.

The gap
: While the need for governance, oversight, and approval gates is well-documented, no specification language exists for expressing these constraints as executable protocol. Teams implement AI-SDLC workflows through ad-hoc tooling without formal mechanisms to specify what agents can do, when approval is required, or how process violations are prevented.

We propose a domain-specific language for specifying AI-SDLC processes.

Defining AI-SDLC
: We use AI-SDLC (AI-Integrated Software Development Lifecycle) to mean software development processes designed around collaboration between human and AI team members, where both take on defined roles and responsibilities within structured workflows. AI agents are first-class participants (planning, implementing, validating, reviewing) rather than tools augmenting a human-centric process. The fundamental shift: AI-SDLC requires new processes optimized for human-agent collaboration, not acceleration of existing human-only processes. The core challenge is specifying who (human or agent) does what, when approval is required, how disagreement is resolved, and what constraints cannot be violated.

2  Problem: The Need for Formal AI-SDLC Specification

2.1  Documented Failure Modes

Empirical research on AI coding systems documents consistent failure modes that persist across model generations:

Security vulnerabilities.
Analysis of 733 code snippets from GitHub Copilot and other tools found 29.5% of Python and 24.2% of JavaScript snippets contained security weaknesses
[
Fu et al.(2024)
]
. Approximately 40% of Copilot-generated programs were vulnerable when evaluated against CodeQL
[
Pearce et al.(2022)
]
.

Silent failures.
Newer models produce code that “fails to perform as intended, but which on the surface seems to run successfully, avoiding syntax errors or obvious crashes”
[
IEEE Spectrum(2025)
]
. These failures impose hidden costs through delayed discovery.

Context overload.
Agent behavior analysis identified “over-exploration where agents fail to reach the root of the problem due to context overload, and repeated application of the same fix without proper testing”
[
Bouzenia & Pradel(2025)
]
.

Audit gaps.
In regulated environments, autonomous code generation introduces “audit trail complications that go beyond code quality into regulatory territory”
[
SitePoint(2026)
]
. Determining what decisions were made and what validation occurred requires structured records most systems do not provide.

Self-review limitations.
Single agents reviewing their own output validate against the same flawed model that produced the implementation
[
CodeAnt AI(2025)
]
.

These failures are process problems, not capability problems. Improving model quality does not eliminate the need for independent review, scope boundaries, and audit trails.

2.2  Process Variability

No single AI-SDLC process fits all teams. A solo developer wants speed and tolerates risk; a regulated enterprise cannot ship without audit trails and separation of duties; a startup falls between. Software engineering processes vary by team size, regulatory burden, risk tolerance, and domain. Adding AI agents does not eliminate this variability; it amplifies the need to express it formally.

2.3  The Specification Gap

The practitioner literature documents the problem but provides no solution. Recent analysis states teams need “highly structured, codified context”
[
Pandit(2025)
]
but offers no language for specifying that structure. Industry reports call for “governance requirements”
[
Forrester Research(2025)
]
without formal mechanisms to express them.

What exists
: Ad-hoc implementations, implicit norms, behavioural expectations without structural enforcement.

What’s needed
: A specification language that allows teams to express, in machine-executable form, how AI agents participate in their SDLC: what modes exist, what validators run, when human approval is required, how disagreement is resolved, what constraints cannot be overridden.

3  Related Work

Our work intersects several research traditions: multi-agent frameworks for software engineering, workflow specification languages, capability-based security, and human-agent collaboration modeling. We position our contribution against the most relevant prior art.

3.1  Human Oversight Models

Recent practitioner and research literature distinguishes Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) from Human-on-the-Loop (HOTL) oversight models
[
Anthropic(2026)
,
LangChain(2026)
]
. HITL refers to designs where the human is inside the control loop and the agent blocks pending approval; HOTL refers to designs where the human monitors execution and intervenes only when anomalies appear.

Our formalization requires both. The HI-CTRL role and the
require_human
disagreement policy implement HITL: execution blocks pending human adjudication when validators split or when policy explicitly demands it. The audit log (INV4) and
proceed_with_log
policy implement HOTL: execution proceeds with full traceability, allowing human review of decisions post-hoc. The choice between HITL and HOTL is encoded in the disagreement policy and is part of the protocol specification rather than the agent implementation. This addresses a documented limitation of current frameworks: encoding oversight in agent code produces coverage drift as new action types appear
[
Waxell(2026)
]
.

3.2  Multi-Agent Frameworks for Software Engineering

MetaGPT
[
Hong et al.(2024)
]
is the closest prior work. It assigns software-engineering roles (Product Manager, Architect, Engineer, QA Engineer) to distinct agents and encodes Standardized Operating Procedures (SOPs) into prompts to coordinate execution. MetaGPT demonstrates that role-based agent specialization improves coherence in software generation tasks. However, MetaGPT encodes SOPs
in prompts
: a behavioural specification mechanism subject to the same drift and interpretation problems we identify in Section
2
. There is no machine-executable specification language separate from agent instructions, no mode boundaries enforced at infrastructure level, and no formal mechanism preventing role contamination beyond prompt discipline.

Other multi-agent orchestration frameworks (LangGraph
[
LangChain(2024)
]
, AutoGen
[
AutoGen(2023)
]
, CrewAI
[
CrewAI(2024)
]
) provide
toolkits
for building agent systems rather than
specification languages
for SDLC processes. LangGraph models workflows as explicit state machines (nodes and edges); AutoGen frames interactions as conversations; CrewAI assigns role-goal-backstory triples. All produce specific systems through code; none provides a portable, reviewable specification artifact separate from the implementation. Audit, modification, and protocol sharing are correspondingly more difficult.

Our DSL differs in three respects: (i) it specifies AI-SDLC
processes
as standalone artifacts independent of any framework; (ii) it distinguishes policy declaration from mechanism enforcement, allowing implementations to choose mechanisms appropriate to context; (iii) it formalizes infrastructure-level mode boundaries (WF1) as required for the specification to hold.

3.3  Workflow Specification Languages

Workflow specification languages have a long history. YAWL
[
van der Aalst & ter Hofstede(2005)
]
provides domain-independent syntax for workflow patterns with a runtime engine that enforces specified workflows. BPMN
[
BPMN(2013)
]
has become the dominant business process modeling notation, with both graphical and machine-processable representations. These languages address workflow specification well for human-only or human-tool processes but do not natively model autonomous agent participants, disagreement among agents, or the agent-specific failure modes documented in Section
2
.

Ait et al.
[
Ait et al.(2024)
]
propose a BPMN extension for human-agentic collaborative workflows, formalizing modeling concepts for mixed human-agent processes and providing a graphical notation. Their contribution is directly comparable to ours in motivation but differs in approach: BPMN extension preserves backward compatibility with existing business process tooling, while our DSL is purpose-built for AI-SDLC with infrastructure-level enforcement as a first-class concept. The trade-off is real: BPMN extension benefits from a mature ecosystem; our DSL provides tighter integration with capability-based enforcement. Both approaches will likely coexist; the choice depends on whether teams need integration with existing BPM tooling or are designing AI-SDLC processes from scratch.

FlowAgent
[
Shi et al.(2025)
]
introduces a Procedure Description Language (PDL) for customer-service style workflows, with controllers that supervise execution paths. PDL combines natural language flexibility with code precision, similar in spirit to our DSL. The key differences: PDL targets single-domain conversational workflows (e.g., hospital appointment booking); our DSL targets the multi-mode, multi-role structure of software development with explicit producer/reviewer separation. PDL controllers enforce path compliance; our enforcement primitives (validation tokens, capability boundaries) enforce both path and authority constraints.

3.4  Capability-Based Security and Token Mechanisms

Capability-based security has a substantial history in distributed systems
[
Dennis & Van Horn(1966)
,
Li et al.(2022)
]
. Our “capability boundaries” (INV2) are an application of established capability-based access control patterns to the AI-SDLC domain. We do not claim novelty in the mechanism itself; we claim novelty in its application as a specification primitive for AI-SDLC processes.

SAGA
[
Yao et al.(2025)
]
provides a security architecture for agentic systems using cryptographic access control tokens for inter-agent communication. Our validation tokens differ in purpose: SAGA tokens control
access rights
between agents; our tokens encode
validator quorum outcomes
as preconditions for dispatch. Both are capability tokens; the semantics and lifecycle differ. SAGA addresses agent-to-agent authorization; we address validation-to-execution authorization.

Recent industry analysis notes the shift from credential-based to capability-based access control for AI agents
[
Token Security(2026)
]
, observing that capability-based models are better suited to agents that invent new workflows at runtime than role-based access control (RBAC). Our DSL incorporates this insight by making capability boundaries (INV2) a structural property of mode definitions rather than a runtime authorization decision.

3.5  Standard Operating Procedures and Team Composition

The notion of formalizing team roles and standardized procedures for software development draws on Belbin’s team role theory
[
Belbin(2012)
]
and DeMarco & Lister’s analysis of software team dynamics
[
DeMarco & Lister(2013)
]
. MetaGPT cites this tradition explicitly. Our 2+N pattern formalization extends this tradition by specifying the
minimum viable team
structure under AI-SDLC: two human-in-control roles (architect, reviewer) plus N specialized agent members. Where Belbin describes roles people occupy, we specify roles that must exist with structural enforcement of separation.

3.6  Positioning Summary

Our contribution is not the invention of any single primitive: DSLs, capability tokens, role-based workflow specification, and human-agent collaboration modeling all have substantial prior art. The contribution is their integration into a coherent specification language for AI-SDLC processes with the following specific properties not jointly present in prior work:

•

Machine-executable specification independent of any agent framework

•

Policy/mechanism separation with explicit enforcement primitives

•

Infrastructure-level mode boundaries (not norm-enforced)

•

Validation tokens encoding quorum outcomes (not just access rights)

•

Non-overridable blocker semantics

•

Formalized 2+N team pattern as reference configuration

•

Kleene closure of orchestration loops as emergent property

We acknowledge significant overlap with MetaGPT (role specialization, SOPs), FlowAgent (procedure description language with controllers), BPMN extensions (human-agentic workflow modeling), and capability-based security (token-gated access). Our differentiation is in the specific combination, the AI-SDLC application domain, and the formal treatment of failure rate bounds (Section
4.9
), now empirically characterised through simulation (Section
6
).

4  Formal Language Specification

We present a domain-specific language for AI-SDLC processes. The language distinguishes
policy
(declared intent) from
mechanism
(structural enforcement). Protocols specify what should happen; implementations use enforcement primitives to bound process non-determinism.

4.1  Abstract Syntax

A protocol
\mathcal{P}
is a tuple of modes, validators, disagreement policy, and constraints:

\displaystyle\text{Protocol }\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle::=\langle M^{*},V^{*},D,C^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{Mode }\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle::=\langle\text{mode\_id},R^{*},T^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{Role }R

\displaystyle::=\langle\text{role\_id},\text{identity},\text{tools}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{Validator }\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle::=\langle\text{validator\_id},\text{evaluation\_phase},

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\text{criteria},\text{severity\_fn}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{DisagreementPolicy }D

\displaystyle::=\text{unanimous\_pass}\mapsto\text{proceed}

\displaystyle\mid\text{majority\_pass}\mapsto\text{proceed\_with\_log}

\displaystyle\mid\text{split}\mapsto\text{require\_human}

\displaystyle\mid\text{any\_blocker}\mapsto\text{hard\_stop}

\displaystyle\text{Constraint }C

\displaystyle::=\text{positive}(\text{predicate})\mid\text{negative}(\text{predicate})

Modes
are operational stages with defined permissions. Examples: producer (writes code), reviewer (validates and approves). Modes are enforced at infrastructure level.

Roles
are actors within modes. Each has identity (defining responsibility), tools (what it can access), and constraints (what it cannot do).

Validators
evaluate tasks against criteria and emit severity levels. Multiple validators can operate independently (quorum validation with orthogonal criteria).
1
1

1

The
evaluation_phase
field enables validators to declare when they execute, supporting multiple validator classes distinguished by evaluation domain and timing. Common validator classes include specification validators (
pre_execute
, assessing task intent before execution), artifact validators (
post_execute
, evaluating generated code after execution), and transition validators (
mode_transition
, checking handover conditions). The protocol language does not enumerate all possible phases or validator classes; implementations may define additional classes appropriate to their domain and process requirements.

Disagreement policies
specify how the system responds when validators reach different conclusions.

Constraints
define structural requirements and prohibitions enforced by the implementation.

4.3  Kleene Closure as Emergent Property

A significant emergent property of the formal specification is that the orchestration loop forms a
Kleene closure

[
Kleene(1956)
]
: the system can instantiate zero, one, or arbitrarily many loop instances, including recursively spawned sub-instances, all under the same protocol constraints.

Formally, if
L
denotes a single orchestration loop instance executing the cycle (governance, validate, execute, move_next), then the execution flow of the protocol is:

\text{Execution}(\mathcal{P})=L^{*}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}L^{i}

where
L^{i}
denotes
i
sequential or recursively nested loop instances. This Kleene closure is not programmed into the system as a special case; it emerges from the protocol’s structural properties:

•

Tasks may spawn subtasks.
The architect agent, on encountering a blocker or scope expansion, creates new tasks following the same specification.

•

Each subtask requires its own cycle.
A spawned task must independently pass governance, validation, and execution before its parent task can proceed.

•

Subtasks may themselves spawn subtasks.
Recursion is permitted to arbitrary depth, bounded only by termination conditions in the breakdown.

•

All instances share protocol constraints.
The same validators, disagreement policies, and enforcement invariants apply uniformly to every instance.

This property has practical significance: the protocol scales naturally to arbitrary task complexity through composition rather than special-case handling. A blocker on a top-level task does not halt execution; it spawns a subtask whose successful completion enables the parent task to proceed.

The Kleene closure framing captures a verification consequence: invariants that hold for a single loop instance hold for any composition of instances. Concretely, if a single loop instance
L
satisfies invariants INV1–INV4, then every execution in
L^{*}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}L^{i}
satisfies them, by induction on closure construction: the empty execution satisfies them vacuously, and each composition step preserves them because spawned subtasks operate under the same protocol constraints as their parents. This compositionality lets runtime monitoring focus on single-loop behaviour rather than tracking arbitrary execution depths.

We do not develop this further here, but note that the closure framing opens questions worth follow-up: under what termination conditions
L^{*}
remains decidable; whether closure laws (e.g.
(L^{*})^{*}=L^{*}
) hold under our enforcement semantics; and whether properties of
L
beyond invariant preservation also lift to
L^{*}
. We leave these to future work.

4.4  Well-Formedness Conditions

A protocol
\mathcal{P}=\langle M^{*},V^{*},D,C^{*}\rangle
is well-formed iff:

WF1
(Mode separation):

\displaystyle\forall m_{1},m_{2}\in M^{*},\ m_{1}\neq m_{2}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow m_{1}.\text{tools}\cap m_{2}.\text{tools}=\emptyset

WF2
(Role uniqueness within mode):

\displaystyle\forall m\in M^{*},\ \forall r_{1},r_{2}\in m.R^{*},

\displaystyle r_{1}\neq r_{2}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow r_{1}.\text{role\_id}\neq r_{2}.\text{role\_id}

WF3
(Validator coverage):

\displaystyle\forall\text{task dispatch op},\ \exists V^{\prime}\subseteq V^{*}

\displaystyle\text{associated with that operation}

WF4
(Policy completeness):
D
defines the behavior for all possible validator outcomes.

WF5
(Constraint declaration consistency):

\displaystyle\forall c_{1},c_{2}\in C^{*}.\

\displaystyle c_{1}\neq c_{2}\Rightarrow c_{1}.\text{id}\neq c_{2}.\text{id}

\displaystyle\land\ \forall c\in C^{*}.\

\displaystyle\text{nonempty}(c.\text{expr})

\displaystyle\land\

\displaystyle\text{registered}(c.\text{predicates})

Well-formedness conditions are enforced by compile-time verification: a protocol failing any check is rejected at load (WF1–WF5 via the protocol verifier), with WF3 additionally guarded at runtime. Two conditions are partially enforced: WF4 treats quorum policies with fewer than three validators as a warning rather than a rejection, and WF5 verifies constraint declaration consistency (unique identifiers, non-empty expressions, registered predicates) rather than logical non-contradiction, which would require theorem-proving over constraint expressions and is left to future work.

4.5  Runtime State and Tokens

\displaystyle\text{State }\sigma

\displaystyle::=\langle\text{current\_mode},\text{active\_roles},\text{issued\_tokens}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{Token }\tau

\displaystyle::=\langle\text{token\_id},\text{task\_ref},\text{validator\_sigs},\text{timestamp}\rangle

ValidatorResult

\displaystyle::=\langle\text{validator\_id},\text{severity},\text{justification}\rangle

Tokens are one mechanism for enforcement. A token is an unforgeable credential issued by validators after evaluation. Agents cannot mint tokens; dispatch operations require valid tokens. Other mechanisms (signed commits, capability boundaries, formal verification traces) are equally valid.

4.6  Session Boundaries and Resumability

AI-SDLC execution is not continuous. Sessions pause (model context limits, human availability, overnight breaks) and resume. The protocol must maintain its guarantees across these boundaries.

Session state:

\displaystyle\text{SessionState }\sigma_{s}

\displaystyle::=\langle\text{session\_id},\text{mode},\text{project},

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\text{checkpoint},\text{parent\_session}?\rangle

\displaystyle\text{Checkpoint }c

\displaystyle::=\langle\text{current\_task}?,\text{decisions},

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\text{memory\_summary},\text{timestamp}\rangle

This prevents re-asking humans (decision churn within a session) and losing memory context. Validator re-execution on resume is intentional, not a violation: tokens are excluded from session state precisely because the current context may have shifted during the pause, and revalidation against the current state is required for soundness.

Token lifecycle and session boundaries
: Tokens are scoped to a single dispatch operation within an active session. They do not persist across session boundaries. Implementations may additionally expire tokens within a session after a TTL to force revalidation when the intra-session context changes substantially.

4.8  Bounded Non-Determinism

A critical clarification: the language does not produce deterministic
outcomes
. Agent-based components (validators, coders, the orchestrator itself) are inherently non-deterministic. Two invocations of the same validator on the same task may produce different evaluations; two invocations of the same coder on the same specification may produce different implementations.

What the mechanism eliminates is
process
non-determinism: the variability introduced by agents skipping, drifting from, or reinterpreting protocol steps. This distinction matters:

•

Process non-determinism (eliminated)
: An agent might or might not call the validator before dispatching. With structural enforcement, dispatch is impossible without prior validation. The protocol step occurs deterministically.

•

Agent non-determinism (bounded, not eliminated)
: The validator, when invoked, produces its evaluation through a non-deterministic model. Two invocations may differ. The mechanism does not change this; it ensures the invocation occurs.

•

Compositional effect
: Bounding process non-determinism makes the residual agent non-determinism tractable. Disagreement policies turn variable validator outputs into deterministic decisions (proceed, escalate, halt). The system as a whole exhibits quasi-deterministic behavior: same inputs lead to similar (not identical) trajectories.

This is analogous to type systems in programming languages. A type system does not make programs correct; it eliminates entire classes of errors structurally, leaving the remaining errors more tractable. Similarly, the protocol language does not make AI-SDLC outcomes deterministic; it eliminates the class of failures that arise from agents bypassing process structure, leaving the remaining variability bounded and explicit.

The honest claim, therefore, is not that structural enforcement produces deterministic outcomes, but that it produces
quasi-deterministic process behavior
when working with non-deterministic agents. This is a meaningful improvement: outcomes that would otherwise be uncharacterizable become characterizable, auditable, and improvable.

4.9  Failure Rate Bounds

We can characterize the quasi-deterministic improvement more precisely through a failure rate analysis. The parameter values used below are illustrative. The qualitative conclusion is that structural enforcement bounds failure rates while behavioural compliance does not, and this holds across the plausible parameter range, independent of the specific values chosen.

Assume each agent
a_{i}
has an individual failure rate
p_{i}\in[0,1]
representing the probability of producing an incorrect output for a given task. We compare the expected system failure rate under behavioural compliance versus structural enforcement.

behavioural system failure rate.
Without structural enforcement, failures compound through the workflow because (i) process steps may be skipped, removing detection opportunities, and (ii) agents operating on already-corrupted intermediate state exhibit elevated failure rates. For a workflow of
N
sequential agents:

Lower bound (independent failures):

P_{\text{fail}}^{\text{behavioural}}\geq 1-\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-p_{i})

For small individual rates, this approximates to
\sum p_{i}
: failures are at minimum cumulative.

Realistic case (cascading failures):
When agent
a_{i+1}
operates on corrupted output from
a_{i}
, its effective failure rate increases. A naive additive model (
p_{i+1}^{\prime}=p_{i+1}+\alpha_{i}p_{i}
) is bounded only by stipulation and can exceed unity in long chains, invalidating the probabilistic interpretation. We instead use a noisy-OR-style composition in which the effective rate depends on the
accumulated
upstream error
p_{i}^{\prime}
rather than the isolated
p_{i}
:

p_{i+1}^{\prime}=p_{i+1}+\alpha_{i}p_{i}^{\prime}(1-p_{i+1})

where
\alpha_{i}\in[0,1]
is the susceptibility of agent
a_{i+1}
to upstream corruption. This form is bounded in
[0,1]
for all parameter values, asymptotically approaches 1 in the worst case, and reduces to the independent-failure model when
\alpha_{i}=0
. The system failure rate is then:

P_{\text{fail}}^{\text{behavioural, cascading}}\geq 1-\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-p_{i}^{\prime})

Worst case (near-saturating propagation):
If failure detection is also impaired by upstream corruption (
\alpha_{i}
approaches 1), the effective rates rise quickly toward 1 before being caught, or are never caught.

Structurally enforced system failure rate.
Structural enforcement changes the failure model in three ways:

(1) Process steps cannot be skipped
(INV3, capability boundaries). Validation occurs deterministically. The failure modes reduce to agent failures only; process non-determinism is eliminated.

(2) Validator quorums provide redundancy, modulated by shared bias.
For a quorum of
K
validators with average individual failure rate
p_{v}
, the probability that all validators miss the same issue is not the naive product
p_{v}^{K}
, because foundation-model validators share substantial training data and exhibit correlated failure modes. We model this with an intraclass correlation coefficient
\rho\in[0,1]
capturing shared architectural bias:

P_{\text{miss}}^{\text{quorum}}=p_{v}^{K}+\rho(p_{v}-p_{v}^{K})

At
\rho=0
(perfectly independent validators) this reduces to the naive product
p_{v}^{K}
. At
\rho=1
(perfectly correlated validators, effectively the same model) the quorum provides no benefit and
P_{\text{miss}}^{\text{quorum}}=p_{v}
. Realistic values for cross-provider LLM validators lie between these extremes; methodological diversity in validator selection (different criteria, different model families) reduces
\rho
but does not eliminate it.

(3) Blockers halt cascade propagation, but recovery is not free.
When a validator detects an issue, the protocol halts execution or spawns a corrective subtask (Kleene closure, Section
4.3
). Subsequent agents do not operate on corrupted state. However, the corrective subtask is itself executed by non-deterministic agents and has its own failure probability
p_{\text{recovery}}\in[0,1]
representing the probability that the recovery loop fails or exhausts its retry budget. The effective failure rate contributed by agent
a_{i}
under structural enforcement is then:

P_{\text{stage}_{i}}=p_{i}\cdot P_{\text{miss}}^{\text{quorum}(i)}+p_{i}\cdot(1-P_{\text{miss}}^{\text{quorum}(i)})\cdot p_{\text{recovery}}

The first term covers undetected failures (the quorum misses the issue); the second covers detected failures that the recovery loop fails to resolve. Summing across the workflow:

P_{\text{fail}}^{\text{structural}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{\text{stage}_{i}}

where each
P_{\text{stage}_{i}}
uses the quorum size
K_{i}
and correlation
\rho_{i}
for the validators evaluating agent
a_{i}
. Comparing the two systems:

\frac{P_{\text{fail}}^{\text{behavioural}}}{P_{\text{fail}}^{\text{structural}}}\geq\frac{1-\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-p_{i}^{\prime})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{\text{stage}_{i}}}

For realistic values (
p_{i}\approx 0.1
,
p_{v}\approx 0.2
,
K=3
,
\rho\approx 0.3
,
p_{\text{recovery}}\approx 0.1
,
N=5
, modest
\alpha_{i}
), this ratio remains substantial: structural enforcement reduces failure rates several-fold in this regime, by roughly a factor of five at these reference parameters (Section
6.3
). The advantage widens as validator independence improves (exceeding an order of magnitude at low
\rho
and low
p_{\text{recovery}}
) and narrows as the behavioural cascading rate approaches saturation at large
N
. The exact ratio depends sensitively on
\rho
and
p_{\text{recovery}}
, both of which are empirical questions for specific deployments.

This single-gate analysis is deliberately conservative: it isolates the accuracy of one validator at one gate. The deployed protocol composes multiple independent gates (deterministic predicates with error
\approx 0
on checkable properties, separate pre- and post-execution validation phases, per-mode quality gating, and an extensible orthogonal quorum), each structurally guaranteed to execute. To the extent these gates fail independently, a modest advantage at any single gate compounds across them, so the analysis understates the aggregate effect rather than overstating it. The residual dependency is gate independence: correlated blind spots across gates (the same error evading both pre- and post-execution checks) limit the compounding, in the same way validator correlation
\rho
limits quorum benefit within a single gate.

Residual assumptions.
The corrected model still rests on assumptions worth flagging:

1.

Failure rate constancy
: We assume individual
p_{i}
is fixed. In practice, agents have task-dependent failure rates; the analysis bounds the average case.

2.

Stable correlation structure
: We assume
\rho
is fixed across the workflow. Validator correlation may vary with task type, and modelling this would require an empirical study of cross-model failure overlap.

3.

Bounded recovery cost
:
p_{\text{recovery}}
collapses the full Kleene recovery loop (which itself contains nested validation) into a single rate. This is a tight bound only when the recovery loop is shallow; deep recovery chains may require recursive analysis.

Despite these residual assumptions, the structural difference is robust: structural enforcement bounds failure rates at a weighted product of agent and validator rates with explicit recovery cost, while behavioural compliance permits at least cumulative and potentially near-saturating growth. This is the formal basis for the quasi-deterministic claim.

Empirical validation as future work
: The mathematical analysis predicts a measurable difference between behavioural and structural enforcement under controlled conditions. Empirical validation, comparing failure rates of structurally enforced AI-SDLC against single-agent and behaviourally-compliant baselines, remains future work.

4.10  Self-Reinforcing Protocol Adherence

A notable property of validator extensibility is that the language admits
meta-level
validators: validators whose evaluation criterion is the protocol itself rather than the artifact being produced. We call this category
protocol-adherence validators
.

A protocol-adherence validator
V_{\text{meta}}
evaluates whether the orchestrator and other agents are following the protocol’s own rules: are validators being called when required? Are mode boundaries respected? Are scope constraints honored? Are dispatch operations gated by valid tokens?

Formally, a protocol-adherence validator is a validator like any other:

V_{\text{meta}}=\langle\text{protocol\_adherence},\text{criteria}_{\text{meta}},\text{severity\_fn}_{\text{meta}}\rangle

What makes it distinctive is its evaluation domain: the criteria operate over protocol execution traces rather than over code or task outputs. The criteria check well-formedness conditions (WF1–WF5) and enforcement invariants (INV1–INV4) on actual runtime behavior.

Self-reinforcement property.
This produces a self-referential structure: a validator within the system enforces the system’s own rules. The protocol is auditing itself through a participant of the protocol. Three implications follow:

(1) Optional inclusion.
A team may include a protocol-adherence validator in their validator set or omit it. The language treats it as any other validator, with no special-case handling required. Smaller projects may omit it (overhead exceeds benefit); regulated environments may require it (audit trail completeness).

(2) Catches integration violations.
While structural enforcement (capability boundaries, token gating) prevents many protocol violations at the mechanism level, integration points between agents and external systems may admit violations not caught by structural primitives alone. A protocol-adherence validator catches these by inspecting execution traces.

(3) Bootstrapping consideration.
The protocol-adherence validator is itself an agent subject to the same non-determinism as other validators. It does not provide ground truth about protocol compliance; it provides another independent assessment, increasing confidence through quorum-style redundancy when combined with other mechanisms. We do not claim infinite regress is required (a validator to validate the protocol-adherence validator, etc.); one meta-level suffices in practice because the structural enforcement mechanisms catch most violations, leaving the meta-validator to address edge cases.

Comparison with type-theoretic self-reference.
The protocol-adherence validator is analogous to systems with reflective capability: type systems where types describe types, operating systems where the kernel audits kernel operations, programming languages with meta-circular interpreters. The key property in each case is that the same formalism describes both the artifacts under evaluation and the evaluation mechanism itself. Our DSL exhibits this property through validator extensibility without requiring any special meta-level construct.

This self-reinforcing structure is a consequence of the DSL design, not an explicit feature of it. The language permits any validator to be added; one such addition happens to be a validator that polices protocol adherence. The interesting observation is that this is sufficient: no separate meta-language is required to specify protocol-adherence rules. They are simply another validator definition.

Why this structure:

•

Producer-reviewer separation
(WF1): Producer mode cannot access merge tools; reviewer mode cannot access edit tools. This formalizes the classical Separation of Duties principle
[

,
NIST(2020)

•

•

Non-overridable blockers
(INV3): When any validator emits a blocker, work stops. Agents cannot proceed regardless of other validator opinions.

•

Human-in-control roles
: The 2 humans are first-class team members with execution authority, ambiguity resolution, disagreement adjudication, and veto power. The minimum of two humans derives from Separation of Duties: a single human cannot independently validate their own work, regardless of agent assistance.

•

Agent roles
: The N agents are also first-class team members with defined responsibilities within their capability boundaries.

Scaling beyond 2+N.
The 2+N pattern represents the minimum viable structure for Separation of Duties. The protocol language places no upper bound on the number of modes; teams may extend their configuration as process maturity demands. Typical enterprise SDLC processes exhibit four to five distinct modes: planning (intent decomposition), production (implementation), review (validation and approval), deployment (release authority), and operations (incident response), each enforcing structural separation of a different SDLC concern. Smaller teams may collapse modes (1+N for solo developers where producer and reviewer authority are conflated), while larger organizations may decompose further as new responsibilities emerge. The language treats all such configurations uniformly through the same mode/role/validator primitives. We anticipate additional modes will emerge as AI-SDLC practices mature, including modes for specification authorship, compliance verification, and post-deployment learning.

5.1  Architectural Properties

Several properties emerged during implementation that strengthen the specification:

Human-in-control single-mode execution.
The engine executes exactly one mode per invocation, halting at mode boundaries rather than auto-transitioning. Modes are isolated and communicate only through artifacts; mode changes are explicit human actions. This enforces human-in-control governance: the orchestrator, not the engine, decides workflow progression.

Legible refusals.
On any halt, the engine emits a structured, machine-parseable payload (halt type, blocking validators, justifications, policy decision) alongside human-readable output, enabling an external orchestrator to programmatically interpret a refusal and revise-and-retry or escalate to a human.

Extensible validator framework.
Validators are resolved through an open registry: third parties implement a single
evaluate(context)
interface and register a custom validator type without modifying engine code. The reference implementation includes a worked custom-validator example. This demonstrates the validator extensibility that Section
4.10
relies on.

Severity ordering with capping.
Validator severities form a total order (
\text{pass}<\text{warn}<\text{blocker}
). A severity cap allows validators-under-evaluation to participate without blocking the workflow; every cap is recorded in the audit log with original and effective severity.

No single policy is robust to both attack directions simultaneously: the non-overridable-blocker semantics that make the system fail safe against missed defects necessarily make it fail closed under a restrictive adversary. This is the quantified cost of INV3, and it bounds what
validator-quorum enforcement can defend against: the mechanism assumes validators are faithful to their criteria, not adversarial. Defending against compromised validators requires orthogonal mechanisms (validator attestation, anomaly detection on verdict distributions) outside the scope of the policy layer.

6.5  Summary

Taken together, the four studies characterise the enforcement layer along
complementary axes. The policy Monte Carlo (Section
6.1
)
shows disagreement policy is a first-class trade-off between false-block
and false-pass. The overhead study (Section
6.2
) shows the
machinery costs roughly 1% of inference latency. The detection study
(Section
6.3
) validates the failure-rate model and confirms
the structural advantage widens with pipeline length. The Byzantine study
(Section
6.4
) bounds what the policy layer can defend
against, exposing the INV3 asymmetry. None of these establishes end-to-end
defect-reduction on real software tasks against single-agent and
behavioural baselines; that comparative evaluation remains the principal
item of future work.

7  Discussion

7.1  Limitations

This is a position paper proposing a specification language with feasibility demonstration. Several limitations bound its claims:

Feasibility demonstration, not controlled comparison.
The system has been used to extend its own codebase under the 2+N protocol. This is a meaningful feasibility demonstration and stress test, since the system must handle the complexity of its own evolution, and it is reproducible from the public implementation. It is not, however, a controlled evaluation. What the present work does not provide is a comparison of structurally enforced AI-SDLC against behavioural-compliance and single-agent baselines, on common tasks scored by hidden acceptance suites. That comparison is the principal item of future work.

Mathematical analysis under assumptions.
The failure rate bounds (Section
4.9
) depend on assumptions about failure rate constancy, stable correlation structure, and bounded recovery cost that may not hold in practice. The analysis establishes the structural difference; precise quantitative claims require empirical validation.

Token integrity is implementation-dependent.
INV1 specifies that tokens must be unforgeable for the system to be valid. The specification does not prescribe
how
unforgeability is achieved: this is an implementation responsibility. Different deployment contexts (single-process, distributed, multi-tenant) require different cryptographic or capability primitives. We treat this as feature: the specification is portable; the enforcement mechanism is contextual.

Validator quality bounds system quality.
Structural enforcement guarantees that validation occurs; it cannot guarantee the validator is correct. This dependency is load-bearing: the failure-rate advantage of Section
4.9
is contingent on validators clearing a useful detection threshold. We do not treat this as a peripheral caveat. The mitigation is compositional rather than per-validator: deterministic predicates contribute near-zero error on checkable properties, pre- and post-execution phases and per-mode gating add independent checkpoints, and an extensible orthogonal quorum reduces correlated blind spots. These compound to the extent they fail independently, but the underlying validator-quality problem is bounded, not solved: a sufficiently poor or sufficiently correlated validator set degrades the guarantee regardless of how strictly the protocol is enforced.

Applicability scales with project complexity, not team size.
The language is not tied to team size: the solo protocol is a complete protocol, not a reduced one, and the same primitives express single-agent and multi-mode configurations uniformly. What determines whether structural enforcement pays off is project complexity, size, and stage. While a project is simple, a developer can self-orchestrate agents by hand: an architect agent, a few coding agents, a sentinel for review. This works, if mechanically. As complexity grows, the manual cost of that approach scales badly: more time is spent instructing agents how to behave, manually validating their output, and catching missed steps than making progress. Structural enforcement absorbs this cost into the mechanism, which is where it earns its keep. We do not empirically establish where this crossover lies; identifying the complexity threshold at which structural enforcement becomes worthwhile is an open question.

No formal verification of protocol consistency.
Well-formedness conditions (WF1–WF5) are syntactic checks. We do not verify that a given protocol achieves its stated intent, satisfies temporal properties, or is internally consistent beyond the structural rules. Formal methods could strengthen these guarantees but are out of scope.

7.2  What the Language Does Not Solve

Protocol authoring expertise.
Writing effective protocols requires deep software engineering knowledge. The language gives senior engineers a mechanism to encode that knowledge; it does not eliminate the expertise requirement.

Protocol evolution.
How teams modify protocols over time, who can change them, what review changes require, is governance the language does not prescribe. The language provides version control and peer review through its machine-executable form, but teams must establish their own change processes.

Formal verification.
We do not currently verify that a protocol satisfies temporal properties or achieves stated goals. Well-formedness conditions catch structural errors; formal methods could provide stronger guarantees.

Optimal protocol synthesis.
Given constraints (regulatory requirements, team size, risk tolerance), could an appropriate protocol be generated automatically? This is future work.

7.3  Model-Independence and the Commoditization Hypothesis

The enforcement guarantees of this work are model-independent. The failure-rate bounds of Section
4.9
are stated over validator accuracy and correlation, not over any specific model; the structural invariants (INV1–INV5) constrain the execution mechanism, not the agents that fill its roles. Concretely, swapping the validator backend from one provider to another leaves the protocol, its well-formedness conditions, and its enforcement invariants unchanged. The protocol is a durable artifact; the models that populate it are interchangeable infrastructure.

This property motivates a broader hypothesis, which we offer as observation rather than demonstrated result. As foundation models converge in capability, with no single provider holding a durable lead, the differentiating engineering asset shifts from model choice toward process design: how human and agent team members are structured to collaborate. A team with a well-designed protocol retains it across model transitions; the protocol becomes institutional memory while models become commodity infrastructure. The trajectory mirrors cloud computing, where differentiation migrated from infrastructure choice to how infrastructure was used. We do not establish this hypothesis here; the model-independence of our enforcement layer is the part the present work supports.

7.4  Relationship to Existing Frameworks

Multi-agent frameworks (LangGraph, AutoGen, CrewAI, MetaGPT) provide tools for building agent systems. They do not provide a language for specifying SDLC processes. Building with them produces a specific system; our language produces a specification that systems implement.

The difference matters for audit (protocols are reviewable documents), modification (edit YAML vs. edit code), and sharing (portable specifications vs. implementation-bound systems).

8  Conclusion

We have presented a specification language for AI-SDLC processes, addressing a documented gap in the practitioner literature: the need for formal mechanisms to define human-agent responsibility boundaries, approval gates, and governance constraints.

The language provides abstract syntax for expressing modes, roles, validators, disagreement policies, and constraints. Well formedness conditions define valid protocols. Operational semantics specify how constructs execute. Enforcement invariants ensure implementations bound process non-determinism structurally: eliminating the class of failures arising from agents bypassing protocol steps, while bounding the residual agent non-determinism within explicit policy structures.

The 2+N team pattern (two human-in-control roles plus N specialized agent members) demonstrates the language through a reference configuration exhibiting producer-reviewer separation, validator quorum with orthogonal criteria, and non-overridable blockers.

An implementation shows the language is executable. The enforcement guarantees are model-independent: as validator models are swapped or upgraded, the protocol and its invariants are unchanged. To the extent that foundation models continue to converge in capability, the durable engineering asset becomes process design, and the specification language is that asset’s form.
