Preliminary Analysis of CpG, CHG and CHH Methylation in the Mitochondrial Control Region of Dogs With Malignant Mammary Gland Tumours
Authors: Krzysztof Kowal, Kaja Ziółkowska‐Twarowska, Angelika Tkaczyk‐Wlizło, Carlos Vaamonde‐García, Mercedes Fernández‐Moreno, Brygida Ślaska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCanine mammary tumours (CMTs) are one of the most frequently observed malignancies in female dogs. Many studies have investigated epigenetic changes in CMT at the nuclear level, yet the role of mtDNA methylation, particularly in the D‐loop region, remains largely unexplored. Given the significant importance of the D‐loop region in mtDNA replication and transcription, and the previous identification of CpG enrichment there, this preliminary study aimed to assess apparent methylation signals in the mitochondrial D‐loop region. We examined CpG, CHG and CHH methylation contexts in matched tumour and normal tissues from six German Shepherd dogs with histologically classified mammary carcinomas using enzymatic methyl‐sequencing and high‐throughput amplicon sequencing. Overall, CpG and CHH contexts exhibited greater variability between tumour and normal tissues than CHG, which remained relatively stable. Correlation analyses revealed a putative positive relationship between differences in CpG and CHH methylation (Spearman's r = 0.829, p = 0.042). This might suggest a coordinated epigenetic modulation of these two methylation patterns in the carcinogenesis process, yet due to the limited study group (n = 6) the hypothesis should be verified on a larger cohort. These findings demonstrate a plausible existence of intra‐individual epigenetic divergence at the mitochondrial level. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the influence of mtDNA methylation on CMT, and it supports further investigation of mitochondrial epigenetics in canine oncology.

Introduction

Mammary gland tumours are among the most prevalent neoplasms in female dogs. They serve as a valuable comparative model for human breast cancer due to the similarities in histopathology, molecular profiles and disease progression (Tkaczyk‐Wlizło et al. 2022). Much attention has been devoted to nuclear genomic alterations in canine mammary tumours (CMT), but the role of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), particularly its epigenetic regulation, remains insufficiently explored. The displacement loop (D‐loop), a non‐coding control region of mtDNA, is a critical hub for the regulation of mitochondrial genome replication and transcription (Jemt et al. 2015; Kim et al. 1998). Emerging evidence in the field of human oncology suggests that alterations in the methylation landscape of mtDNA, specifically in the D‐loop, play a role in various malignancies. This indicates that mitochondrial epigenetics may be involved in tumour pathogenesis (Welch et al. 2022; Wagner et al. 2022).

Although mtDNA methylation, particularly within the non‐coding region, has emerged as a potential epigenetic mechanism impacting on tumour biology in various human cancers, this regulatory layer remains largely unexplored in canine oncology. Existing epigenetic studies of canine mammary tumours (CMT) have primarily focused on global DNA methylation changes in nuclear DNA and promoter‐specific methylation of tumour suppressor and hormone receptor genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2 and ESR1 (Brandão et al. 2018; Amfim et al. 2021; Biondi et al. 2021). Our previous investigations have reported polymorphisms and mutations in the mtDNA, especially in the D‐loop region in CMT (Ziółkowska et al. 2023; Kowal et al. 2021, 2019). Assessment of mtDNA methylation signals may provide complementary information on mitochondrial regulatory alterations in tumour and matched non‐tumour tissues. Moreover, the methylation status of this region could reflect disruptions in mitochondrial epigenetic regulation associated with malignancy, suggesting its utility as a biomarker for tumour progression or prognosis.

According to our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the methylation status of the mitochondrial non‐coding region in canine mammary tumours. A comprehensive review of the literature revealed that existing epigenetic studies in CMTs have primarily focused on nuclear methylation changes, including genome‐wide profiling using bisulphite sequencing, which does not address mitochondrial loci (Jeong et al. 2019). It has been hypothesised that the D‐loop methylation may influence mitochondrial biogenesis, mtDNA copy number, oxidative stress responses and apoptotic signalling (Lin et al. 2022). These factors are also highly relevant to cancer progression. Moreover, our comparative analyses further indicate CpG enrichment within the canine D‐loop, including a long CpG island in the VNTR region (Kowal et al. 2020), and previous studies have linked D‐loop methylation to altered mitochondrial function in pathological contexts (Sharma and Sampath 2019; Lu et al. 2009).

The aim of this study was to determine molecular differences in CpG, CHG and CHH methylation of the control region of mitochondrial genomes in normal tissue and tumour tissue samples of dogs with diagnosed mammary gland tumours, as well as to eliminate the plausibility of false positive non‐CpG methylation.

Materials and Methods

Tissue Collection and Processing

We analysed 12 samples of normal tissues and tumour tissues obtained from six dogs diagnosed with malignant mammary gland tumours. The malignancy degree of the CMT was assessed using the 3‐grade scale of malignancy, that is, a sum of point values assigned to histomorphological traits according to Goldschmidt et al. (2011). These approaches were used in a complementary manner, as classification and grading represent distinct components of pathological evaluation. To minimise potential confounding related to age‐associated methylation drift and breed‐/body size‐related biological variability, we restricted the cohort to German Shepherd females within a narrow age range (Table 1). This design was intended to improve the comparability between individuals in this preliminary analysis by reducing variability that could otherwise arise from differences in body size, breed background and ageing‐related epigenetic changes.

After surgical excision, tumours were divided into two portions for histological and molecular analyses. The tumour sample was placed in a sterile container and subjected to histopathology analysis. The samples were fixed in buffered formalin, pH = 7.2, processed routinely, embedded in paraffin wax, sectioned into 4‐μm thick fragments and stained with haematoxylin‐eosin and toluidine blue. Microscopic classification was performed in accordance with the WHO histological classification (Goldschmidt et al. 2011). The second portion and the corresponding normal mammary tissue sample were allocated for methylation analysis, frozen shortly after collection and stored at −80°C until DNA extraction. The study was approved by the II Local Ethical Commission for animal experiments (Resolution number 6/2014).

Methylation Analysis

DNA extraction was performed with the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany). DNA quality control was performed by measuring the absorbance ratio at 260/280 and 260/230 nm. Samples below the threshold of 2.0 for A260/230 ratio and below 1.8 for A260/280 ratio were excluded from the analysis. Template concentration was determined using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and DNA integrity was assessed by 0.8% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA (200 ng) was mechanically sheared to ~500 bp fragments using a Covaris instrument (Covaris, USA), followed by conversion using the NEBNext Enzymatic Methyl‐seq Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, USA) with minor modifications (the sheared DNA was neither end‐repaired nor ligated to Illumina adapters). Following the deamination step, DNA was not purified but directly used for PCR amplification. Primers were designed using the Bisulphite Primer Seeker tool comprising the entire 1270 bp D‐loop region (Table S1). PCR amplification was carried out in 20 μL reactions containing 3 μL of bisulphite‐converted DNA, 10 μL of repliQa HiFi ToughMix (QuantaBio, Beverly, USA), 6 μL of PCR‐grade water, and 0.5 μL (0.2 μM) of each primer. The PCR conditions were as follows: 35 cycles of 10 s at 98°C, 5 s at 54°C and 2 s at 68°C. As recommended in the manufacturer's protocol, neither an initial denaturation step nor a final extension was required. The PCR products were visualised using 1% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis. After adjusting the volume to 50 μL and adding 1 μL of 10% Tween‐20, PCR reactions were purified using Omega Bio‐tek magnetic beads (Omega Bio‐tek Inc., Norcross, USA). Purified PCR products were pooled in equimolar ratios and indexed using Nextera XT barcodes (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Amplicon libraries were then pooled and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq instrument. Sequencing was performed in paired‐end mode (4 × 300 bp) using a v3 (600‐cycle) chemistry cartridge, allowing generation of long paired reads that fully covered the targeted amplicons. Sequence quality metrics were assessed using FASTQC (Andrews 2010). Raw reads were trimmed for quality and adapter sequences were removed using fastp (Chen et al. 2018). Raw and filtered read counts of amplicon samples were presented in Table S2.

Bioinformatic Analysis of Methylation Calls

Bisulphite sequencing alignment of the enzymatically converted reads was performed with the Canis lupus familiaris mitochondrial D‐loop reference sequence (NC_002008.4:15458–16727) using Bismark (Krueger and Andrews 2011). Restricted mapping enabled masking all short and non‐specific reads of plausible nuclear origin (NUMTs), which could interfere with the result. Following alignment, cytosine methylation states were extracted from the Bismark alignment output. At informative reference‐cytosine positions, a cytosine retained in the converted read was operationally classified as methylated, whereas a C‐to‐T conversion was classified as unmethylated; the corresponding strand interpretation was applied to reads aligned to the opposite strand. Calls were classified according to the reference trinucleotide sequence as CpG, CHG, or CHH, where H represents A, C, or T. In the Bismark methylation call string, methylated and unmethylated CpG sites are encoded as Z and z, respectively; CHG sites as X and x; and CHH sites as H and h. Context‐specific methylation percentages were calculated as 100 × M/(M + U), where M and U denote the numbers of methylated and unmethylated cytosine calls, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

To assess differences in DNA methylation between tumour and normal tissues, we calculated Δ‐methylation for each dog by subtracting the %‐methylation measured in the normal tissue from that measured in the corresponding tumour tissue. This was performed separately for CpG, CHG and CHH contexts. Normality of paired methylation differences (ΔCpG, ΔCHG, ΔCHH) was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (Table 2). As no evidence of non‐normality was found (p > 0.05), pairwise comparisons between methylation contexts (e.g., CpG vs. CHG) were evaluated using the paired Student's t‐test (Student 1908). Additionally, a t‐test for independent samples was calculated as a descriptive benchmark; however, inferential interpretation was based on a paired framework consistent with the matched study design. For robustness, non‐parametric Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were also conducted (Table 2). In addition, we tested whether methylation changes in each context were associated with continuous variables such as age using both Pearson's correlation and Spearman's rank correlation (Weaver et al. 2017). Paired comparisons between methylation contexts were conducted using %‐methylation values (CpG%, CHG%, CHH%) within the same tissue.

To quantify the overall methylation variability per dog, the arithmetic mean of the three context‐specific differences was calculated. To investigate the potential influence of tumour type and tumour histological malignancy grade on methylation differences, we used two complementary approaches. An ANOVA test was performed to determine whether there were significant differences in the mean methylation levels across the groups (Ross and Willson 2017). All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All statistical analyses and visualisations were conducted in Python (version 3.11) in the Visual Studio Code environment. Data were manipulated using the pandas library, and statistical tests were performed using scipy.stats.

Study Limitations

A limitation of this study is the small cohort size, which restricts statistical power and generalisability, particularly given subtle effect sizes. However, this reflects an intentional design choice to prioritise biological comparability over sample number. By restricting sampling, we wanted to minimise confounding related to breed‐ and age‐dependent methylation variability and tumour heterogeneity. Unfortunately, the acquisition of additional matched tumour and adjacent normal tissues under uniform clinical and histopathological criteria in veterinary oncology is logistically demanding and time‐consuming. Because sequence variants were not independently genotyped and masked before methylation calling, an effect of individual polymorphisms on methylation sites cannot be completely excluded; this limitation does not, however, alter the conclusions regarding the regional level of methylation in the present study.

Results

The statistics of methylated and unmethylated CpGs, CHGs and CHHs of analysed samples were presented in Table S3. Context‐specific methylation proportions within the analysed D‐loop region were low in all samples. The maximum observed value was 4.171%, and no context‐specific value exceeded approximately 5%. Furthermore, no individual nucleotide position was methylated in 100% of the reads covering that position. It is noteworthy that the methylation of this region was found to be more prevalent in normal tissues compared to tumour samples. The bars situated below 0 on Figure 1 represent normal tissues, in which overall methylation levels were found to exceed those observed in tumour samples (Figure 1). In contrast, the most significant observed discrepancy in CpG methylation between tumour and normal tissue was identified in cases involving an 8‐year‐old dog with carcinoma tubulo‐papillare G3 (B62) and an 8‐year‐old dog with carcinoma tubulo‐papillare G1 (B124). In a single tumour tissue sample, the CHG exhibited a higher frequency of methylation compared to normal tissue. In the samples of the German Shepherd Dog with carcinoma complexus G1 (B207), the CHH context was the only one in which methylation was observed to be higher in tumour samples than in normal tissue samples. The analysis revealed that the lowest levels of CHG methylation differences were observed among the samples analysed from dogs B62 and B67.

To investigate the relationship between tumour–normal methylation differences across contexts, Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated between ΔCpG, ΔCHG and ΔCHH. The strongest association was observed for ΔCpG versus ΔCHH using Spearman's rank correlation (r = 0.829, p = 0.042), indicating a moderate to strong statistically significant relationship in this cohort. All remaining pairwise correlations were positive but did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05) and are therefore reported as exploratory observations provided for completeness (Figure 2, Table 3).

As shown in Table 1, the methylation differences (Δ, expressed as a %) between tumour and normal tissue from each dog were calculated for CpG, CHG and CHH contexts. Across dogs, the dispersion of these differences supported the observed context‐specific pattern, with ΔCpG showing the highest variability (mean ± SD: 0.431% ± 0.252%), followed by ΔCHH (0.167% ± 0.138%), whereas ΔCHG remained markedly stable (0.024% ± 0.035%). These values reflect the magnitude of intra‐individual epigenetic divergence between matched tumour and normal tissues. Of the analysed cases, the lowest variability in overall methylation was observed in a 12‐year‐old dog diagnosed with carcinoma tubulo‐papillary G3 (SO1), with a mean difference of 0.093%, indicating minimal divergence between tumour and normal tissues. Conversely, the highest level of methylation divergence was observed in an 8‐year‐old dog with the same tumour type and grade (B62), with an average difference (Δ) of 0.379%. This increased variability was mainly due to a significant difference in the CpG context (ΔCpG = 0.919%) and greater CHH divergence (ΔCHH = 0.216%). Across all individuals, CHG differences were consistently minimal, ranging from 0.001% to 0.092%, suggesting that variability in CpG and CHH methylation contributed most significantly to the overall divergence.

Using the Student's t‐test for independent samples, no statistically significant difference in CpG methylation levels between tumour tissue and normal tissue was detected (t = 0.036, p = 0.972). After applying the test to paired data, the results also did not indicate statistical significance (t = 0.092, p = 0.930) (Table 2). Moreover, the dispersion of the tumour–normal differences (Δ = tumour% − normal%) was summarised by reporting mean ± SD for each context in Table 2. Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests confirmed the absence of significant tumour–normal methylation differences for CpG (p = 0.844) and CHH (p = 0.688). However, a statistically significant difference was observed for CHG methylation (p = 0.031), indicating potential subtle alterations not detected by the parametric approach. Yet this result should be considered as exploratory and may reflect directional consistency rather than biologically meaningful effect size. Consistent with the analyses reported in Table 2, paired Student's t‐tests performed on matched tumour–normal methylation values for each context (CpG%, CHG%, CHH%) revealed no statistically significant differences. In addition, paired t‐tests comparing mean methylation levels across contexts within the same tissue type (CpG vs. CHG, CpG vs. CHH and CHG vs. CHH) also did not reveal statistically significant differences (Table 2), suggesting that the overall context‐specific methylation levels are comparable within tissues.

Discussion

Our study has demonstrated an epigenetic divergence in dogs' tissues, as indicated by an analysis of intra‐individual methylation variation between tumour and normal tissues. A study of absolute methylation changes in CpG, CHG and CHH contexts demonstrated the identification of both stable and variable methylation patterns. It was hypothesised that dogs with minimal differences in methylation level might indicate an epigenetic conservation even within a tumour context. Conversely, other dogs exhibited notable levels of divergence, mostly in CpG and CHH contexts (Table 1). Statistical analysis of the differences in the level of cytosine methylation in different contexts revealed a strong positive correlation between CpG and CHH methylation within analysed samples. These findings suggest that changes in CpG methylation between analysed tissues tend to co‐occur with changes in CHH methylation. Although these contexts differ in sequence specificity and biological roles, the observed correlations point to a plausible coordination in their epigenetic alterations during tumorigenesis.

Bellizzi et al. (2013) described that mtDNA methylation particularly occurs within non‐CpG nucleotides, mainly in the promoter region of the heavy strand and in conserved sequence blocks, suggesting its involvement in regulating mtDNA replication and/or transcription. Tong et al. (2017) indicated that demethylation of specific sites on CpG islands of D‐loop promoter may lead to the elevation of mtDNA copy number in colorectal cancer in humans, triggering alterations in biological behaviours, including increased cell proliferation and reduced apoptosis. In mouse germinal vesicle oocytes, nearly 2/3 of methyl‐cytosines occur in CHH and CHG contexts. These accumulate near methylated CpGs and are likely induced by DNMT3A/DNMT3L complexes, although mitochondrial localisation remains uncertain (Sirard 2019). In the case of human DNA methylation, it is mostly observed in the D‐loop region comprising both HSP and LSP promoter elements. However, the exact mechanism by which mtDNA gets methylated or demethylated remains potentially elusive, to date (Chatterjee et al. 2022). It is not excluded that in the case of canine cancers, a similar mechanism of mtDNA augmentation might occur under demethylation of the non‐coding region. A limitation of this study is the inability to identify which non‐coding regions of D‐loop showed lower or higher hypomethylation. However, given the negligible proportion of methylated cytosines to total cytosines in this region, it cannot be ruled out that the entire studied area may be significant, rather than specific regions.

The field of mitochondrial epigenetics has become increasingly polarised, and it is therefore essential to acknowledge that the functional relevance of 5‐methylcytosine (5mC) in mammalian mtDNA remains actively debated. Although several studies have reported low but detectable cytosine methylation signals in mtDNA (including both CpG and non‐CpG contexts), several independent and methodologically focused investigations have argued that a substantial fraction of mtDNA methylation reported in less than 2% may reflect technical artefacts rather than a robust biological modification (Shao et al. 2023; Mechta et al. 2017). Mitochondrial DNA poses unique challenges for conversion‐based approaches due to its high copy number, compact nucleoid organisation, secondary structure and circular topology (Shao et al. 2023; Mechta et al. 2017; Guitton et al. 2022). All of which can influence the conversion efficiency, cause amplification bias and disturb read mapping. Therefore, apparent methylation signals of ~0.2%–2% may fall within the background error expected for cytosine conversion or for downstream computational calling when absolute modification levels are extremely low. Shao et al. (2023) concluded that mtDNA 5mC levels are typically indistinguishable from background noise and frequently remain below ~1%, emphasising that both bisulphite and enzymatic conversion strategies can yield low‐level false positive methylation calls in mitochondrial reads. Bicci et al. (2021) applied nanopore sequencing to human mtDNA with explicit error modelling and concluded that signals consistent with CpG methylation could be attributed to false positives rather than genuine mtDNA CpG methylation, reinforcing the need to treat low methylation calls in mitochondrial reads with caution. On the other hand, Matsuda et al. (2018) analysed 5mC modifications in mice tissues with the use of three different methods, and they did not find any methylation excluding its universal role in mtDNA gene expression or mitochondrial metabolism. Guitton et al. (2022) highlighted that estimates of mtDNA methylation can either diminish towards background or become confined to a very limited subset of loci, suggesting that mitochondrial epigenetic regulation may in fact reflect a small number of positions that are particularly prone to technical artefacts (e.g., due to local sequence context, homopolymer tracts, or alignment ambiguity). Alternatively, rare and highly context‐dependent modification events require ultra deep coverage and careful error correction to be distinguished from noise (Guitton et al. 2022). These considerations are particularly relevant for our study. For this reason, we consider our findings as evidence of intra‐individual divergence in an apparent methylation signal within the D‐loop region as quantified by enzymatic conversion and amplicon sequencing, rather than as definitive proof of a high abundance mitochondrial methylome with established regulatory function. At the same time, it is noteworthy that the field has not converged on a single interpretation. Mitochondrial cytosine modifications might occur at very low levels, potentially in a locus‐restricted or tissue‐specific manner, that even rare modifications in regulatory elements could, in principle, influence replication or transcription in selected contexts. Thus, the key unresolved question is not merely whether any modified cytosines exist in mtDNA, but whether the measured signals exceed the technical background in a reproducible manner and whether they associate with functional readouts of mitochondrial biology. In the context of CMT, it remains plausible that mtDNA molecules experience altered modification dynamics or damaged base changes that could be captured, to varying extents, by conversion‐based assays, even if the predominant biological phenomenon is not canonical 5mC‐driven epigenetic regulation.

The present study comprised six canines of the same breed and within close age. These results emphasise the heterogeneity of methylation reprogramming in CMT, even among individuals that exhibit certain similarities. It is noteworthy that methylation changes at CpG and CHH sites accounted for most of the variation across the entire spectrum, while CHG exhibited stability between samples. This finding may be indicative of preferential biological control or functional aspects of each methylation context during tumour formation. Sun et al. (2022) state that epigenetic alterations and metabolic reprogramming are deeply linked with cancers, where epigenetically active metabolites act as cofactors or substrates for chromatin‐modifying enzymes. Metabolic and epigenetic alterations have then been shown to drive tumour initiation and progression, such as immune escape, through changes to the methylation and acetylation marks on DNA and histones. Epigenetic status variability, for instance, changes in DNA methylation, has therefore been attributed to how tumours evolve and become more aggressive. Since abnormal metabolism induces instable, heterogeneous epigenetic states, this might encourage tumour evolution. Nevertheless, it should be emphasised that to assume such a hypothesis, further analysis in larger groups is necessary.

The analysis revealed that CpG and CHH differences were strongly correlated (r = 0.829), suggesting that increases or decreases in CpG methylation in tumours relative to normal tissue are often mirrored by similar changes in CHH methylation. The study by Sun et al. (2025) provides direct support for the correlation between CpG and CHH methylation changes in cancer. In their analysis of monozygotic twins discordant for acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), they observed significant global hypomethylation of CpG sites and an increase in CHH methylation in the twin diagnosed with ALL. These two contexts tend to vary in a consistent direction across samples, even when their absolute magnitudes differ. A moderate correlation was observed between CpG and CHG (r = 0.543), while the correlation between CHG and CHH was weaker (r = 0.257). These findings indicate greater variability in their relative behaviour (Table 3, Figure 1). de Lima et al. (2023) indicated that CHG methylation in mtDNA may contribute to temporal regulation of mitochondrial gene expression, indicated by an inverse correlation between methyl levels and transcription in bovine embryos. Our results support the idea that CpG and CHH methylation dynamics may be more tightly coordinated during tumorigenesis, whereas CHG methylation may follow a more distinct or variable pattern. It is not excluded that CHG methylation was present in the mtDNA of these individuals' tissues since early developmental stage and the carcinogenesis process did not influence significantly on its level. The implementation of Spearman's correlation serves to substantiate the robustness of the observed associations. This may reflect a shared biological pattern or coordinated epigenetic remodelling involving both methylation types during tumorigenesis. However, the validation should be confirmed on a larger study cohort.

Despite the absence of statistically significant correlations between ΔCHG and both CpG and CHH methylation, the observed patterns suggest the potential for CHG methylation to be independently regulated or differentially responsive to tumour‐related changes. This observation supports the hypothesis of the presence of biological variation. Yet, with the current dataset, it is unable to reach a definitive conclusion.

Conclusions

This preliminary study is the first to evaluate mitochondrial D‐loop methylation in CMTs, providing insight into an unexplored aspect of tumour epigenetics in veterinary oncology. Notable variability was observed within individuals, particularly in CpG and CHH contexts, whereas CHG methylation remained stable. These findings suggest the presence of context‐specific methylation patterns in the carcinogenesis process within this tightly matched cohort, while evaluation of age‐ and breed‐related effects will require larger and more diverse study groups. The statistical correlation between CpG and CHH methylation changes indicates a plausible presence of a shared epigenetic regulatory pathway. These findings emphasise the potential functional relevance of D‐loop methylation in mitochondrial gene regulation and tumour biology. Future studies should extend the present context‐level analysis by identifying differentially methylated cytosines within the D‐loop and evaluating whether such sites overlap regulatory elements (e.g., promoter regions, conserved sequence blocks, or putative transcription factor binding sites), which will require larger cohorts and statistical modelling.
