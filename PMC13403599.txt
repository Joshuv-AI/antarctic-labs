Resection of a thalamomesencephalic cavernous malformation via a retrosigmoid supracerebellar transtentorial approach: illustrative case
Authors: Caiqiang Huang, Yuhai Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThalamomesencephalic cavernous malformations are deep lesions located near sensory, motor, and visual pathways as well as the deep venous system, making surgical treatment challenging. Careful corridor selection is essential to minimize injury to eloquent structures.OBSERVATIONSA 35-year-old man presented with progressive left facial and hemibody numbness and low-frequency tinnitus. MRI showed a right thalamomesencephalic lesion adjacent to the posterior third ventricle but separated from the ventricular system by functional parenchyma. The lesion was resected through a right retrosigmoid supracerebellar transtentorial approach in the left lateral decubitus position. Gross-total resection was achieved. Postoperative MRI at 7 days, 3 months, and 1 year showed no residual or recurrent lesion. Brainstem cavernous malformations account for a minority of intracranial cavernous malformations but are associated with substantial neurological morbidity because of the density of the surrounding eloquent structures.1,2 Lesions at the thalamomesencephalic junction are particularly challenging because they lie near the corticospinal tract, posterior thalamic structures, visual pathways, and deep venous anatomy.2–5 Surgical planning therefore depends on careful analysis of lesion location, hemorrhagic history, tract displacement, and the feasibility of a corridor that minimizes neural transgression.2–4,6

Several approaches have been described for intrinsic midbrain and thalamomesencephalic lesions, including transventricular, occipital transtentorial, and supracerebellar routes.3,7,8 The optimal route must be individualized according to the relationship of the lesion to the posterior third ventricle, surrounding functional parenchyma, and displaced white matter tracts.2–5,9,10 We present an illustrative case of a thalamomesencephalic cavernous malformation resected through a retrosigmoid supracerebellar transtentorial approach, highlighting corridor selection, safe tentorial opening, staged navigation-guided entry-point refinement, and serial postoperative imaging demonstrating durable resection and tract preservation.

Illustrative Case

Clinical Presentation

A 35-year-old man presented with a 2-year history of progressive numbness involving the left side of the face, with extension over the preceding 2 weeks to the left hemibody, accompanied by ­low-frequency tinnitus. Neurological examination demonstrated decreased pain, temperature, and tactile sensation on the left side. There was no motor weakness, lower cranial nerve dysfunction, or evident visual field deficit on bedside examination. The clinical and radiographic findings were consistent with a symptomatic hemorrhagic cavernous malformation at the right midbrain-thalamic junction, and microsurgical treatment was recommended.1,2,4–6,11

Imaging Findings and Rationale for Approach Selection

Preoperative axial T2-weighted, T1-weighted, and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI demonstrated a lesion at the right thalamomesencephalic junction, with imaging features consistent with a cavernous malformation (Fig. 1). Although the lesion was adjacent to the posterior third ventricle, it remained separated from the ventricular system by functional parenchyma. This relationship made a direct transventricular or midline route less favorable because such approaches would have required unnecessary transgression of uninvolved tissue.2–5,7 FIG. 1.Preoperative axial (A), sagittal (B), and coronal (C) T2-weighted MR images demonstrating the thalamomesencephalic cavernous malformation (arrows).

Preoperative axial (A), sagittal (B), and coronal (C) T2-weighted MR images demonstrating the thalamomesencephalic cavernous malformation (arrows).

Preoperative tractography demonstrated anterior displacement of the corticospinal tract and lateral displacement of the optic radiations, supporting a posterior infratentorial trajectory (Fig. 2). Advanced tract imaging can be useful in brainstem cavernous malformation surgery when corridor selection depends on tract displacement rather than lesion size alone.4,5,9,10 On the basis of these anatomical considerations, a retrosigmoid supracerebellar transtentorial route medial to the deep venous system was selected to provide a direct posterior corridor while minimizing risk to the lateral geniculate region, ventricular structures, and eloquent pathways. Surgical Technique

The patient was positioned in the left lateral decubitus position, and a right curvilinear retroauricular incision was made for a retrosigmoid exposure. A retrosigmoid craniotomy was performed to expose the transverse-sigmoid junction. After dural opening, CSF was released to obtain cerebellar relaxation and minimize the need for fixed cerebellar retraction. This principle is central to supracerebellar approaches, in which exposure should rely on gravity and CSF drainage rather than forceful retraction.7,8

The arachnoid over the superior cerebellar surface was sharply opened under microscopic visualization, and bridging veins were preserved whenever feasible. The tentorial edge was then exposed. During tentorial opening, particular care was taken to avoid the trochlear nerve, and the tentorial edge along the course of the superior petrosal sinus was divided last. This sequence reduced the risk of premature venous bleeding and inadvertent injury to critical neurovascular structures. When the subtentorial anatomy was uncertain, the tentorium was gently elevated with a microhook to allow direct inspection beneath the tentorial edge before incision.

Partial opening of the tentorium expanded the superior operative window through the transtentorial corridor.8,12

The next critical step was selection of the brainstem entry point using the Fiagon alternating electromagnetic surgery navigation system. The first planned corticotomy targeted a relatively less vascular area on the brainstem surface that still projected within the lesion boundary on navigation, although not through the lesion center. The primary purpose of this initial trajectory was to remain as far as possible from the lateral geniculate region and thereby reduce the risk of visual pathway injury. This limited opening did not provide satisfactory access to the lesion cavity. After renewed correlation between navigation and direct microsurgical anatomy, a second entry point closer to the hematoma center was selected. This revised trajectory required sacrifice of a small number of superficial vessels to obtain a more direct route to the lesion. This staged refinement illustrates how neuronavigation can serve as a dynamic adjunct to intraoperative decision-making rather than a rigid determinant of a single entry point.4,5,9,10

A small brainstem opening was then made near the lateral mesencephalic sulcus region. Chronic hemorrhagic products were encountered, confirming entry into the lesion cavity (Fig. 3). Resection proceeded along the lesion-brain plane using meticulous microsurgical technique. The cavernous malformation was mobilized and dissected free with minimal traction, and all dissection was performed under direct visualization. A key technical principle was careful identification and preservation of the hemosiderin-stained gliotic rim, rather than aggressive stripping of reactive surrounding tissue, in order to reduce the risk of secondary brainstem injury.2–5,7,13 After apparent complete removal under the microscope, endoscopic inspection of the resection cavity was performed to assess blind corners and confirm the absence of residual lesion or active bleeding. Postoperative Course and Serial MRI

The patient awoke from anesthesia with stable vital signs, clear consciousness, fluent speech, preserved motor function, grossly intact visual fields, and intact hearing on bedside testing. Left-sided facial and truncal numbness was initially similar to the preoperative state. On postoperative day 1, he remained neurologically stable, with full strength in all extremities, preserved hearing and visual fields, and subjective early improvement in left-sided numbness. By postoperative day 2, tactile sensation remained mildly reduced compared with the right side but had improved relative to the preoperative state, and tinnitus was subjectively improved. From postoperative day 4 through discharge, tinnitus had improved by approximately 50%, and some aspects of delayed sensory perception had also improved. Importantly, no postoperative diplopia was observed, and the patient developed no new motor, visual field, or hearing deficit (Fig. 4). Early postoperative MRI obtained 7 days after surgery demonstrated a well-formed resection cavity with no definite residual cavernous malformation (Fig. 5). The surrounding midbrain-thalamic junction was preserved, without evidence of major new hemorrhage, infarction, or mass effect–producing edema. Follow-up MRI at 3 months showed stable postoperative changes and no evidence of residual or recurrent lesion. At 1 year, MRI continued to demonstrate no recurrence. Comparison of preoperative and 1-year postoperative tractography showed preservation of the corticospinal tract and optic radiations without significant abnormality (Fig. 2C and D). These radiographic findings were concordant with the patient’s stable neurological course and support the effectiveness of the selected corridor and dissection strategy in achieving durable lesion control while protecting eloquent pathways.4,5,9,10 FIG. 5.Early postoperative axial (A), sagittal (B), and coronal (C) T2-weighted MR images demonstrating the resection cavity at the thalamomesencephalic junction without residual lesion (arrows).

Discussion

Observations

Thalamomesencephalic cavernous malformations pose a difficult surgical problem because the operative corridor must be tailored not only to lesion location but also to the arrangement of adjacent tracts, nuclei, and veins.1,5 Natural history studies have shown that symptomatic cavernous malformations, particularly those with prior hemorrhage in eloquent locations, may carry a clinically meaningful risk of rebleeding and progressive neurological decline.2,3,6,11,14 However, surgery in the brainstem and thalamomesencephalic region also carries substantial risk. The central operative question is therefore whether the lesion can be reached through a corridor that minimizes new injury while preserving function.2–5,7

In the present case, several anatomical features favored a posterior transtentorial route. First, although the lesion was adjacent to the posterior third ventricle, it remained separated from the ventricular cavity by functional parenchyma, making a direct transventricular or midline route less attractive. Second, tractography demonstrated anterior displacement of the corticospinal tract and lateral displacement of the optic radiations, making a posterior infratentorial trajectory more logical. Third, the retrosigmoid supracerebellar transtentorial approach provided access medial to the deep venous system and allowed progressive enlargement of the superior operative window through controlled tentorial opening. Similar principles have been emphasized in previous studies of brainstem cavernous malformation surgery and supracerebellar transtentorial routes to posterior midbrain and thalamomesencephalic lesions.2–5,7,8

A major teaching point in this case is that the first navigation-guided surface target need not be the final entry point. The initial target was chosen intentionally as a safer, less vascular site that also remained relatively distant from the lateral geniculate region. When that limited opening did not provide satisfactory access to the lesion cavity, the entry point was revised toward the hematoma center after renewed correlation between navigation and direct microsurgical anatomy. This sequence illustrates a broader principle in brainstem surgery: intraoperative adaptation may be necessary when the safest theoretical surface point does not provide the most effective intralesional trajectory. Navigation is therefore best understood as a dynamic adjunct integrated with microsurgical anatomy and direct visualization.4,5,9,10

Another important technical nuance is the method of tentorial opening. In this case, avoidance of the trochlear nerve, delayed division of the tentorial edge near the superior petrosal sinus, and direct inspection beneath the tentorium with a microhook when anatomy was uncertain all contributed to a safer transtentorial extension. These steps may reduce the risk of troublesome venous bleeding and cranial nerve injury, both of which can narrow the corridor and increase operative morbidity.8,12

Lesion removal itself followed well-established microsurgical principles: identify the lesion-brain interface, avoid excessive traction, mobilize the malformation gradually, and prioritize preservation of eloquent surrounding tissue over aggressive removal of reactive ­hemosiderin-stained parenchyma.2–5,7,13 In this case, careful preservation of the gliotic rim and strict avoidance of blind traction likely contributed to the absence of new permanent neurological deficit. Serial postoperative MRI and 1-year tractography further support this interpretation by demonstrating durable resection without recurrence and preservation of critical functional pathways.

Surgical Pearls and Pitfalls

Several practical lessons emerge from this case. First, preoperative tractography may be particularly useful when selecting among competing deep approaches, especially for lesions near the geniculate or posterior thalamic region.9,10 Second, tentorial incision should proceed only after the surgeon has identified and protected the trochlear nerve and understood the relationship of the tentorial edge to the superior petrosal venous structures. Third, the safest brainstem surface point may not always provide the most direct path to the lesion center, and staged intraoperative reassessment may therefore be required. Fourth, lesion removal should proceed along the lesion-brain plane with minimal traction, and the hemosiderin-stained reactive rim should not be indiscriminately stripped in an attempt to widen the cavity. Potential pitfalls include premature tentorial incision without confirmation of subtentorial anatomy, overreliance on a single navigation target without anatomical re-correlation, and excessive manipulation of the surrounding midbrain in pursuit of a wider opening than is actually necessary.2–5,7,10

Lessons

This illustrative case demonstrates that, in selected thalamomesencephalic cavernous malformations, the retrosigmoid supracerebellar transtentorial approach can provide an effective posterior route when anatomical relationships favor a transtentorial infratentorial corridor over a midline or transventricular path. Safe execution depends on meticulous tentorial opening, thoughtful navigation-guided entry-point selection, and disciplined dissection along the lesion-brain plane. Serial MRI at 7 days, 3 months, and 1 year, together with 1-year tractography, demonstrated durable radiographic control and preservation of eloquent pathways. Although conclusions from a single case remain limited, this case highlights practical technical details that may enhance the educational value and reproducibility of this approach.
