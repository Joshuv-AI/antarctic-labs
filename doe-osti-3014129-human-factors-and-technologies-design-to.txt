1. Executive Summary
 This multi-year project delivered a comprehensive, human-factors-driven framework to understand,
model, and improve pooled rideshare (PR) adoption in the United States. Through three large-scale national
survey studies involving more than 16,000 participants across multiple cities and demographic groups, the
research established one of the most extensive datasets to date on user perceptions, behavioral barriers, and
service expectations related to pooled rideshare. These data revealed key human factors barriers of user
acceptance of PR and suggested potential actionable experience optimizations that could lead to increased PR
usage. This foundational knowledge guided the development of novel human-factors models and behavioral
choice models that quantify how psychological, demographic, and trip-level factors influence willingness to
pool.
 Building on these empirical insights, the project developed advanced behavioral modeling tools, including
mixed logit and integrated choice and latent variable models, to capture both observable and latent influences
on PR adoption. These models significantly improved the ability to predict riders’ acceptance of pooled trips,
explaining choice heterogeneity through latent constructs such as safety, service experience, privacy concerns,
time sensitivity, and environmental attitudes. Together, these models provide a robust analytical foundation for
designing PR systems that more effectively meet user needs.
 The project translated human-factors insights and behavioral models into actionable technology innovations
by extending POLARIS—an agent-based, activity-based travel simulation platform—into a fully functional
pooled rideshare simulation environment. New PR modules, acceptance models, and regional scenarios were
implemented for Greenville, SC and Austin, TX, enabling high-fidelity validation of algorithmic strategies under
realistic demand and traffic conditions. The simulation platform supported the development and evaluation of
adaptive discount-based assignment algorithms, enhanced willingness-to-pay formulations, demographic-aware
incentive mechanisms, and a proactive joint assignment and repositioning strategy. Simulation results
demonstrated substantial gains in pooling uptake, average vehicle occupancy, energy efficiency, and fleet
profitability. In Greenville, pooling adoption more than doubled, while reductions in vehicle-miles traveled and
energy consumption were significant. In Austin, pooling improvements were achieved with minimal service-
quality trade-offs, and profitability increased across all fleet sizes.
 Through this research, we developed a comprehensive understanding of the human factors barriers that limit
user acceptance of pooled rideshare services. These insights enabled the design of human-factors-aware pooled
rideshare technologies that more effectively address user concerns and improve adoption rates. By integrating
these models into an advanced agent-based simulation framework, we demonstrated that higher adoption of
pooled rideshare can lead to measurable improvements in energy efficiency and system performance. Together,
these contributions establish a validated pathway from human-centered analysis to technology development and
energy-saving outcomes, supporting national goals for more sustainable and efficient mobility systems.

2. Objectives
 The project's overall goal is to increase the pooled rideshare user acceptance by 30% to help increase the
efficiency of the transportation system by at least 15%. The main objectives of this project are as follows:
 - Complete human factors data collection on pooled rideshare
 - Complete human factors barrier analysis and pooled rideshare user acceptance modeling
 - Complete human factors involved technologies design for improving user acceptance of pooled rideshare
 - Complete the validations and evaluation of the pooled rideshare models and technologies

2) Strucutral Equation Modeling of User Acceptance on Pooled Rideshare
 After completing the factor analyses, the team developed a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework to
characterize the causal relationships among latent human factors influencing pooled rideshare acceptance, as
shown in Figure 4. SEM was applied to the Phase 1 national survey data to estimate a network of relationships
among latent variables derived from the two factor analyses, providing an integrated perspective on how safety,
trust, attitudes, experience-related factors, and other constructs jointly shape willingness to consider pooled
rideshare.
 The SEM work led to the development of the Pooled Rideshare Acceptance Model (PRAM). PRAM
incorporated latent variables such as time/cost, safety, trust in the service, and willingness/attitudes toward
pooled rideshare, along with response variables representing behavioral intention and acceptance. A conceptual
path model was constructed to examine directional relationships among these latent factors. Higher-order factor
assessments and multicollinearity evaluations were conducted to ensure model validity and factor independence.
 Building upon PRAM, the team conducted a multigroup structural equation analysis, resulting in the Pooled
Rideshare Acceptance Model Multigroup Analyses (PRAMMA). This methodological extension introduced
socio-demographic moderators derived from the transportation needs and demographics sections of the survey.
Participants were grouped across sixteen demographic classifications, enabling examination of differential
pathways in acceptance mechanisms. The multigroup analysis allowed the team to explore how relationships
among latent constructs vary across user segments and to identify latent behavioral structures that are sensitive
to demographic diversity. Together, PRAM and PRAMMA provided the methodological basis for understanding
pooled rideshare acceptance from a human factors perspective and informed the next stage of design-focused
workshops.

4) Data-driven Human Factors Insights on Improving Adoption
 Building on the earlier analytical stages, the Phase 3 study employed a hybrid methodological framework
combining Ordinal Logistic Regression (OLR) and supervised Machine Learning (ML) models to evaluate 77
proposed service enhancements. This approach was designed to systematically quantify how specific design
features influence willingness to adopt pooled rideshare when participants are introduced to targeted
improvements related to safety, convenience, and overall user experience.
 The analytic workflow incorporated pre- and post-exposure willingness measures, enabling assessment of
changes in behavioral intention after participants evaluated the proposed service features. OLR was used to
estimate statistically significant associations between individual features and willingness levels, while ML
models were applied to capture non-linear relationships, identify feature importance, and enhance predictive
capability. Cross-validation procedures ensured model robustness and stability. The hybrid modeling approach
provided a rigorous, data-driven method for identifying which service attributes have the strongest potential to
enhance pooled rideshare acceptance and informed the development of user-centered design recommendations
to support future adoption.
3.2 Behavioral Modeling of Pooled Rideshare Users
3.2.1 Pooled Rideshare Choice Behavioral Modeling
1) Mixed Logit Modeling of Rideshare Choice
 The Phase 2 stated-choice survey data were used to develop behavioral
choice models that reflect the likelihood of selecting pooled rideshare under
varying socio-economic and trip conditions. As an initial step, descriptive
analyses were conducted on the stated-choice responses to identify
influential variables for model construction. These variables were
incorporated into a binomial logit (BL) framework designed to estimate the
probability of selecting the pooled rideshare option when presented with a
binary choice between a solo and pooled ride.
 To better capture behavioral complexity, the model specification
included additive constructs and interaction terms, such as the interaction
between gender and safety prescreening and the interaction between trip
urgency and additional travel time. These interaction terms were Figure 6. Mixed logit modeling
incorporated to represent differential sensitivities that arise when multiple framework.
factors jointly influence the decision-making process.
 To account for unobserved heterogeneity in rider preferences, a random-parameter specification was also
tested. Under this approach, selected coefficients were estimated as normally distributed random variables rather
than fixed values, allowing sensitivities to vary across individuals in a statistically principled manner. This
random-parameter formulation forms the basis of the mixed logit (MXL) model.
 Model estimation followed maximum simulated likelihood approaches, and alternative model specifications
were evaluated using criteria such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and McFadden’s Pseudo R-
Squared. The MXL framework, as shown in Figure 6, provides a more flexible representation of the underlying
population by capturing distributions of user sensitivities to trip attributes, and it serves as the foundation for
integrating behavioral parameters into subsequent simulation studies.

2) Integrated Choice and Latent Variable Modeling of Rideshare Choice
 To incorporate attitudinal constructs identified in earlier human factors analyses, the project developed an
integrated choice and latent variable (ICLV) model. The ICLV framework unifies latent psychological factors
and observable trip characteristics within a single modeling structure, enabling a more comprehensive
representation of user decision-making, as shown in Figure 7.

3.2.2 Analysis of COVID-related Travel Behavior Changes
 To understand the long-term impacts of COVID-19 on mobility patterns and their implications for pooled
rideshare recovery, the project analyzed reported changes in transportation mode use across three national survey
waves (2021, 2022, and 2025). Participants in all phases were asked how the pandemic influenced their use of
personal vehicles, pooled rideshare, personal rideshare, and public transit.
 To uncover underlying behavioral patterns, latent class analyses (LCA) were conducted separately for each
survey wave using individuals’ reported mode-use changes. This unsupervised segmentation approach identified
consistent behavioral clusters across phases, representing distinct mobility adaptation trajectories. These clusters
were then used to examine systematic differences in demographic characteristics, baseline rideshare behaviors,
and preferences for pooled rideshare experience improvements.
 This analytic framework enables the project to assess how pandemic-related behavioral shifts shape the
potential for pooled rideshare adoption in the post-COVID era. By combining segmentation with cross-phase
comparisons, the methodology supports the development of targeted strategies that reflect evolving travel
behaviors and align with long-term market recovery pathways for pooled ridesharing.

2) Simulation Geographical Coverage Extension in POLARIS
 To broaden the applicability of the simulation platform and enable future field-based validation, the project
expanded POLARIS to include a detailed model of Greenville, South Carolina. In addition to existing POLARIS
regions such as Austin, Chicago, and Bloomington, a new Greenville subarea model was created by integrating
regional roadway networks, demographic data, and travel patterns.
 The Greenville model incorporates activity choice, mode choice, destination choice, and timing choice
modules calibrated using the National Household Travel Survey (NHTS) to ensure realistic representation of
local travel behavior. Demographic distributions derived from the American Community Survey (ACS) were
used to generate a synthetic population reflective of the actual region. Pooled rideshare modules and acceptance
models were then integrated into the Greenville region to support testing of pooled rideshare technologies under
diverse demographic and spatial conditions.
 The simulation environment continues to expand geographically, with ongoing work aimed at enlarging the
Greenville coverage area to capture a greater share of the commuting population. This regional expansion
enables cross-city comparison of pooled rideshare performance, facilitates analysis across varying land-use
patterns and population densities, and supports future human-subject validations. Collectively, these extensions
ensure that POLARIS can serve as a flexible platform for evaluating pooled rideshare strategies at scale.

 The assignment strategy is formulated as a multistep process in which potential rider–vehicle matches are

the strategy to adjust its incentive recommendations to better match rider preferences, improving expected
pooling likelihood while reducing unnecessary incentive expenditures.
 The adaptive assignment algorithm is implemented within the POLARIS simulation framework, enabling
evaluation across regions with varying demand–supply dynamics. The strategy is designed to operate effectively

under different congestion and fleet availability conditions and to support both pooled-only and mixed fleet
operations. By leveraging human factors–derived behavioral sensitivities, the algorithm provides a pathway
toward more user-centered and energy-efficient pooled rideshare operations.

2) Human Factors-informed Reposition and Assignment Strategy
 To advance pooled rideshare system performance and incorporate user-centered considerations into fleet
control, the project developed a unified repositioning and assignment strategy that integrates rider acceptance
modeling, proactive vehicle assignment, and predictive vehicle repositioning. This work extends prior research
on rideshare optimization by coupling assignment decisions with vehicle relocation in a single human factors–
informed framework aimed at improving user experience, expanding shared-trip opportunities, and maintaining
efficient fleet distribution.
 The repositioning component builds on
 the Proactive Joint Assignment and
 Repositioning (PJAR) framework, as
 shown in Figure 10, which treats
 repositioning as an integral part of the
 rideshare control system rather than an
 independent downstream process. In
 contrast to traditional methods that separate
 repositioning from assignment, PJAR
 formulates repositioning trips as synthetic
 ride requests within the same optimization
Figure 10. A process diagram depicting the PJAR strategy. model used for passenger assignment. This
 unified formulation enables simultaneous
coordination of all vehicle movements and ensures that repositioning decisions complement real-time demand
patterns.
 To anticipate future demand, PJAR employs a revenue-based demand estimation method. The approach uses
average zonal revenue from previous operating periods as a proxy for both spatial demand intensity and
economic potential. These zonal estimates are dynamically updated and adjusted with a “future earnings
discount” factor to account for uncertainty in projected demand. Repositioning is executed at fixed 15-minute
intervals, balancing responsiveness with computational efficiency and avoiding unnecessary vehicle movement.
 Both assignment and repositioning are formulated as a linear programming problem embedded within the
POLARIS simulation environment, which provides detailed agent-based representations of urban mobility. Each
optimization cycle seeks to maximize expected fleet revenue while minimizing operational inefficiencies,
incorporating travel times, distances, and earnings potential across zones. This formulation enables scalable real-
time operation under dynamic demand conditions.
 To integrate human factors more directly into fleet decisions, the project combined PJAR with a proactive
assignment strategy that applies individualized incentives to increase riders’ willingness to pool. The resulting
integrated assignment–repositioning strategy utilizes a Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) formulation
operating in discrete batching intervals (e.g., 30 seconds). Assignment optimization is performed by maximizing
operator profit defined as fare revenues minus operational costs.
 A key component of this strategy is the updated pooled rideshare acceptance model, which uses the ICLV
model framework derived from Phase 3 survey data. The model predicts whether a traveler will accept a pooled
ride offer, incorporating both observable trip attributes and latent psychological constructs such as Safety,
Service Experience, Time, and Traffic/Environment. Interaction effects (e.g., Service Experience × Safety Pre-
screening, Service Experience × Nighttime Travel, Time × Cost Savings) allow the model to capture nuanced
behavioral sensitivities. The choice component is modeled using a mixed logit structure to represent population
heterogeneity, while the latent variable component is estimated through ordered probit models.
 By integrating repositioning, assignment, incentive generation, and human factors–informed acceptance
modeling into one framework, the strategy provides a coherent operational mechanism for enhancing pooled
rideshare performance. The combined approach is implemented in C++ and solved using IBM CPLEX, enabling

deployment within POLARIS for scenario evaluation across diverse regional networks and operational
conditions.

4. Results
4.1 Results of Human Factors Studies in Pooled Rideshare
4.1.1 National Survey Results
1) Phase 1 National Survey Descriptive Statistics
 The Phase 1 national survey was completed by 5,385 participants in August 2021, including 2,000 respondents
from a nationwide sample, approximately 500 respondents from each of six major U.S. cities (Atlanta, Austin,
Chicago, Detroit, New York City, and San Francisco), and 384 respondents from Upstate South Carolina.
Participants ranged in age from 18 to 95 years with a mean age of 46.5 years (SD = 17.5). The distribution across
age groups was: 18–24 (12.9%), 25–34 (17.3%), 35–44 (17.1%), 45–54 (18.7%), 55–64 (15.2%), and 65+
(18.8%). Across the total sample, 52.1% identified as female, 47.3% as male, and 0.3% self-described. Overall,
the survey achieved a broad demographic distribution across age, gender, and geographic regions. These data
provided the foundational population characteristics used to analyze underlying human factors related to pooled
rideshare attitudes and behaviors.

2) Phase 2 National Survey Descriptive Statistics
 The Phase 2 stated-preference survey was deployed nationwide in April 2022, yielding 2,884 completed
responses by May 2022. Of these participants, 1,750 (60.7%) had prior experience with rideshare services, and
22% of those experienced users reported having previously used pooled rideshare. An additional 1,134 (39.3%)
respondents reported no prior rideshare experience. The gender composition included 52.8% female, 46.4%
male, and 0.5% self-described. Participants represented a mix of residential environments, with 45.6% living in
suburban areas, 25.4% in rural areas, and 24.7% in urban areas. Each participant completed 20 stated-choice
tasks, yielding a total of 56,540 individual choice observations. Of these choices, 32.96% selected the pooled
rideshare alternative, while 67.04% selected the personal rideshare alternative. These data form the empirical
basis for the stated-preference choice modeling conducted in subsequent analyses.

3) Phase 3 National Survey Descriptive Statistics
 The Phase 3 survey obtained 8,296 completed responses in February 2025, integrating both city-specific and
national samples. The city sample included 4,296 respondents from nine major U.S. metropolitan areas—
Atlanta, Austin, Chicago, New York City, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Dallas, Seattle, and Washington, DC—
with a target of 500 respondents per city and balanced representation between individuals with and without prior
rideshare experience. All city quotas were met except for Austin (410 respondents) and San Francisco (386
respondents). To support broader generalizability, an additional 4,000 nationally representative respondents
were recruited, evenly split between those with rideshare experience (2,000) and those without (2,000). Across
the full combined sample, suburban residents accounted for 50.3%, followed by urban residents (29%) and rural
residents (19.1%). This distribution reflects a broad mix of geographic and demographic backgrounds suitable
for evaluating user reactions to pooled rideshare service enhancements.
4.1.2 Human Factor Analysis Results
1) Factor Analysis of Human Factors in Pooled Rideshare
  Two independent factor analyses were conducted using the Phase 1 national survey data to identify the latent
human factors that influence (1) willingness to consider PR and (2) user-centered improvements that may
enhance PR experience. These factor analyses provided a statistical foundation for understanding underlying
human perceptions and informed the design of subsequent modeling and survey phases.

 a. Factors Influencing Willingness to Consider Pooled Rideshare. Initial descriptive findings indicated that
the most important considerations for future PR use were personal safety (35.3%), travel cost (19.1%),
convenience (16.5%), reliable transportation (11.5%), and overall travel time (7.5%). Participants evaluated 25
items across six categories—travel time and cost, environmental, social, personal safety, reliability and
accessibility, and convenience. Respondents willing to consider PR prioritized reliability, safety, time,
convenience, cost, and environmental benefits, while those unwilling to consider PR highlighted personal safety,
convenience, privacy, time, reliability, and cost as key concerns.
 To empirically extract the underlying factors driving willingness to consider PR, the research team conducted
an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) followed by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), ultimately retaining 23 of
25 items and identifying five latent factors:
  Safety (19.57% of total variance)
  Service Experience (15.77%)
      Time/Cost (11.39%)
  Traffic/Environment (7.97%)
      Privacy (4.33%)
 A binomial logistic regression using these factors revealed that service experience, traffic/environment, and
time/cost positively influenced willingness to consider PR, with service experience showing the strongest
positive association (185.1% increase in willingness). In contrast, privacy concerns were associated with
decreased willingness to consider PR (76.7% decrease). Safety emerged as a universal concern—important for
all respondents—without differentiating willing versus unwilling participants.
 These results provided a validated measurement model for understanding the attitudinal foundations of PR
acceptance and guided design of the Phase 2 in-situ choice experiment.
 b. Factors Related to User-Centered Pooled Rideshare Experience Improvements. A second factor analysis
examined user-centered design elements that could improve PR experience. Preliminary descriptive analysis
found that participants reported increased willingness to use PR if the service addressed key user needs. Among
respondents with prior rideshare experience, willingness to use PR assuming all needs were met increased to:

 Building on insights from the factor analyses, PRAM, PRAMMA, and Phase 1 and Phase 2 descriptive

comprehensive set of actionable items for improving PR experience. The objective of these workshops was to
translate statistical and modeling insights into concrete, user-centered service concepts that could inform future
survey design and guide the development of pooled rideshare technologies.

opportunities for pooled rideshare. These items were ultimately classified into nine major categories, each

 The workshop process produced a structured, user-centered framework of actionable PR service

4) Data-driven Human Factors Insights on Improving Adoption
 Several actionable features consistently emerged as strong positive predictors of PR adoption across both ML
and OLR analyses. The most influential factor, “Highlights the opportunity to meet others,” ranked first in ML
importance and showed a 23.4% increase in willingness to adopt PR (see Table 1), suggesting that social
interaction opportunities can significantly shape user behavior. Other top predictors included “Offers
demonstrations, training, and in-person educational activities” (+11.7%), “Promotes using pooled rideshare
during high-traffic events” (+15%), “Is environmentally conscious” (+11.6%), and “Offers one free pooled
rideshare as a trial” (+14.8%). The alignment between ML rankings and OLR significance strengthens
confidence in these results and provides practical, evidence-based guidance for transportation companies and
policymakers. Importantly, these features are actionable and can be incorporated through improved marketing,
onboarding, and incentive strategies demonstrating that small adjustments in communication and design can
increase PR adoption.

 Conversely, some features were found to negatively influence willingness to adopt PR, indicating underlying
trust, safety, or comfort concerns. For example, “Is a fragrance-free vehicle” (–18.9%), “Are preferred riders
because I know them” (–12.2%), and “Has an emergency button in the vehicle or app” (–10.8%) all showed
negative associations despite being viewed positively by users descriptively. This contrast suggests that while
users value these features, they may also associate them with limitations, discomfort, or safety anxieties.
Similarly, high-agreement features such as “Has seats other than the middle seat available” (–10.2%) and “Gives
the highest priority to me during pick-up/drop-off” did not translate into higher adoption likelihood, revealing
that perception does not always equate to behavioral intent. The strongest overall predictor of PR willingness
was prior usage experience (+471.4%), emphasizing that familiarity and firsthand experience remain the most
powerful drivers of future adoption. Collectively, these findings underscore the need for clear communication,
trust-building, and user education to transform perceived barriers into enablers, offering a data-driven roadmap
for improving PR system design and marketing strategies.

4.2 Results of Pooled Rideshare Users Behavioral Modeling
4.2.1 Pooled Rideshare Choice Behavioral Modeling
1) Mixed Logit Modeling of Rideshare Choice
 Descriptive analyses were conducted on the Phase 2 data to understand initial patterns in participants’
selection of pooled rideshare under different trip conditions. Travel time and trip cost showed strong and intuitive
effects on the percentage of trips taken as pooled rideshare. When the extra travel time associated with pooling
increased from no added time to 25 minutes, the share of trips taken as pooled rideshare decreased from roughly
40 percent to about 30 percent. Conversely, when trip cost savings increased from 5 percent to 50 percent, the
percentage of pooled rideshare choices rose from approximately 20 percent to 40 percent. Safety-related features
also played an important role. When safety pre-screening for additional passengers was available, the percentage
of pooled rideshare choices increased from 27.25 percent to 38.67 percent. Walking distance to the pickup

location produced substantial effects as well; when the distance increased from no walking to a quarter mile, the
proportion of pooled rideshare choices fell from 38.28 percent to 29.10 percent.
 Willingness to consider pooled rideshare showed notable correspondence with actual pooling behavior.
Participants who reported that they would consider using pooled rideshare had the highest percentage of pooled
rideshare selections at 42.25 percent, while those who reported that they would not consider using pooled
rideshare showed the lowest share at 26.06 percent. Even within the latter group, however, a meaningful portion
of trips were still taken as pooled rideshare, indicating an opportunity to encourage adoption among initially
reluctant users.
 Several demographic characteristics also influenced pooled rideshare usage. Age showed a clear negative
association, with older participants choosing pooled rideshare less frequently. The number of vehicles per adult
household member had a similarly negative pattern, with participants in households without vehicles selecting
pooled rideshare 39.63 percent of the time, compared to 30.78 percent for those in households with one or more
vehicles per adult. Previous rideshare experience was also influential. Participants with no rideshare experience
or only personal rideshare experience chose pooled rideshare approximately 32 percent of the time, whereas
those with prior pooled rideshare experience chose it 40.19 percent of the time. These findings highlight key
population segments whose preferences and constraints must be addressed to improve pooled rideshare
acceptance.
 A binomial logit model was first estimated to statistically characterize the drivers of choice behavior. The
model achieved a significant fit with a chi-square value of 17596.9, p less than .001, and a McFadden’s Pseudo
R-squared of 0.067. Model coefficients suggested clear patterns. Individuals with higher household incomes
were less likely to choose pooled rideshare. Trips occurring during the day were more likely to be pooled than
trips occurring at night. Sensitivity analyses showed that changes to key attributes such as discounts could
meaningfully shift pooling rates, encouraging further exploration of dynamic incentive strategies.
 An MXL model was then estimated to capture preference heterogeneity. Compared with the binomial logit
model, the MXL model provided substantially improved model fit, with a log-likelihood of -24614, a pseudo R-
squared of 0.264, and an AIC of 49322, compared to -33429, 0.067, and 66929 respectively for the binomial
logit model. The higher pseudo R-squared for the MXL model indicates considerable variability in sensitivities
across individuals, reinforcing the importance of accounting for heterogeneity in pooled rideshare decisions.
 Model parameters further clarified the influence of demographic and trip attributes. Higher income was
associated with lower likelihood of choosing pooled rideshare. Female participants were less likely than male
participants to do so. Age had a negative effect, with older age groups consistently less likely to choose pooling,
except for those aged 25 to 34 who were not significantly different from the youngest group. Education level did
not significantly affect pooled rideshare usage, though participants with post-secondary education showed a
slightly higher usage rate than those with only high school degrees or less. Students were the most likely to
choose pooled rideshare, while retired participants were the least likely. Rural residents were significantly less
likely to choose pooled rideshare relative to urban residents, whereas suburban residents showed no statistically
significant difference from urban participants. Participants with children were more likely to choose pooled
rideshare than those without children, although those with two or more children were slightly less likely to
choose it than those with one child. Participants with no rideshare experience were the least likely to choose
pooled rideshare, those with personal rideshare experience were somewhat more likely, and those with pooled
rideshare experience were the most willing to select pooled rideshare. Users of personal vehicles or non-vehicle
users were less likely to choose pooled rideshare than those who already used shared modes.
 Trip attribute variables showed strong and consistent effects. Larger cost discounts increased the likelihood
of choosing pooled rideshare. Additional travel time reduced it. Participants were significantly less likely to
choose pooled rideshare for urgent trips compared to leisure trips. Nighttime trips were less likely to be pooled.
Medium-sized vehicles were less preferred than large vehicles for pooled rideshare. Medium and long trips were
more likely to be taken as pooled rideshare than short trips, with long trips showing the highest pooling likelihood.
As the number of potential co-riders increased, the probability of selecting pooled rideshare decreased. Longer
walking distances reduced pooled rideshare selection. Availability of safety pre-screening significantly increased
the likelihood of choosing pooled rideshare.

 Interaction effects revealed two important patterns. Female participants were significantly less likely to choose
pooled rideshare when safety pre-screening was absent, indicating that the availability of such screening has a
larger positive impact for women than for men. Extra travel time had a greater negative effect on urgent trips
than on leisure trips, indicating greater sensitivity to delay in time-critical situations.
 These findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the demographic, contextual and trip-based factors
shaping pooled rideshare preferences. The results highlight key leverage points for improving user acceptance,
including discounts, safety protocols, vehicle and trip characteristics and segment-specific considerations related
to age, gender, experience and household composition.

4.2.2 Analysis of COVID-related Travel Behavior Changes
 For the COVID impact study, we conducted multinomial logistic regression analyses for each survey phase,
using sociodemographic variables to predict user cluster membership, with the “No Change” group as the
reference. Across phases, though variations in group composition exist, consistent patterns emerged: “No
Change” users were typically mid-aged, higher-income, suburban residents with strong mobility resilience;
“Never Used/Never Will” users were rural, lower income, less-educated females representing vulnerable
mobility groups; “Shared-Mode Dropouts” reflected lower-resourced users with limited access; “Shared Mode
Reducers” were younger, educated, urban users with children and moderate-to-high income; and “All Mode
Increasers” were younger, high-income, urban males with higher education, children, and limited vehicle
ownership. These models reveal evolving but consistent demographic distinctions that help explain long-term
differences in mobility adaptation and recovery.
 Using Phase 2 stated-choice data, we fitted MXL models for each user cluster to examine their rideshare
choice behaviors between personal and pooled rideshare options. While cost savings, safety prescreening, and
extra travel time consistently emerged as the most influential factors, their importance varied by cluster. The
“No Change,” “Shared Mode Reducers,” and “Never Used/Never Will” groups were most sensitive to cost
savings and travel time, while safety protocols strongly influenced the latter two groups. Additional attributes
such as nighttime travel, trip distance, number of co-riders, and walking time also shaped preferences, revealing
distinct behavioral patterns across user clusters.
 In the Phase 1 survey, participants rated 23 proposed pooled rideshare experience optimization items, while
Phase 3 expanded this to 77 items reflecting deeper insights into user needs and service barriers. Items covered
themes from Comfort, Convenience, Safety, and Vehicle Technologies (Phase 1) to Vehicle Selection, Driver,
Fellow Passengers, Safety, Rider Experience, Specialized and Additional Services, and Trip Route (Phase 3).
Using ordinal logistic regression with user cluster as the predictor, we examined differences in item preferences.
Across phases, “All Mode Increasers” consistently showed the highest openness to improvements, followed by
“Shared Mode Reducers”, while “Never-Used/Never-Will” users were the least receptive. In Phase 1,
Convenience and Safety items were most valued; in Phase 3, Safety and Driver items dominated. Certain clusters
showed distinct interests in the experience optimization items, e.g., “Shared Mode Reducers” valued comfort
and accessibility, “Shared Mode Dropouts” preferred specialized services, and “Never Used/Never Will” users
remained least engaged, though slightly more receptive by 2025.

5. Concluding Remarks
Human Factors Characterization of Pooled Rideshare Acceptance
  Comprehensive national surveys across three phases revealed the psychological, behavioral, and demographic
foundations of users’ willingness to adopt PR. Factor analyses consistently identified safety, privacy, service
experience, convenience, and time/cost as core determinants of pooling willingness. Safety and privacy concerns

were pervasive across all groups, whereas convenience- and technology-related enhancements showed strong
potential to shift attitudes among hesitant users. These statistically derived human factors constructs provided a
validated foundation for subsequent behavioral modeling and technology design.

Structural Modeling of Acceptance (PRAM & PRAMMA)
 SEM-based pooled rideshare acceptance models demonstrated intricate causal and mediated pathways linking
human factors to willingness and adoption. PRAM identified eleven significant pathways explaining how
privacy, safety, convenience, and service experience drive willingness and acceptance. PRAMMA expanded this
understanding by showing that these pathways vary substantially across demographic groups. Fourteen out of
sixteen moderators—including gender, age, income, vehicle ownership, and commuting habits—significantly
shaped acceptance dynamics. These findings highlight the necessity for tailored PR strategies rather than uniform
system designs.

Translation of Human Factors Insights into Actionable Design Requirements
 Workshops integrating survey findings, model outputs, and demographic moderators culminated in the
creation of 95 actionable items representing user-centered improvements to PR experience. These items span
vehicle selection, driver trust, co-rider transparency, routing preferences, safety features, experience
personalization, accommodations for inexperienced riders, and accessibility. This framework operationalizes
human factors insights into concrete service attributes that can be embedded into algorithmic decision-making
and system design.

Behavioral Modeling to Link Human Factors to Choice Behavior
 MXL and ICLV models advanced understanding of how human factors influence actual trip-level decisions.
MXL revealed strong heterogeneity in preferences for cost savings, extra travel time, safety pre-screening, and
pickup walking distance. ICLV provided deeper psychological explanations for these heterogeneities by
mapping demographic groups to their underlying safety, privacy, time, convenience, and environmental value
structures. Together, these models established the causal chain linking user traits → latent perceptions → choice
behavior.

COVID-19 Impacts and Longitudinal Travel Behavior Shifts
 Latent class analyses across all three phases identified five consistent mobility-recovery clusters—“Never
Used/Never Will,” “Shared-Mode Dropouts,” “Shared-Mode Reducers,” “No Change,” and “All Mode
Increasers.” These clusters showed stable demographic profiles over five years and exhibited distinct sensitivities
to PR experience improvements and trip attributes. These findings inform which segments are likely to rebound
to shared modes and which require targeted interventions.

Advances in Pooled Rideshare Simulation through POLARIS
 POLARIS was extended with high-fidelity pooled rideshare modules, Greenville SC regional data integration,
improved mode choice calibration, and a generalized pooling-choice framework. Validation results showed
realistic traffic patterns, accurate behavioral models, and stable system operations across multiple fleet
configurations. The Greenville region model now supports full-scale testing of PR technologies under diverse
demand and network conditions.

Human Factors-Informed Assignment and Repositioning Algorithms
 The adaptive discount-based assignment strategy significantly improved pooling rates, rAVO, and revenue
while reducing empty vehicle miles. Repositioning was integrated with assignment under the PJAR framework,
treating repositioning trips as synthetic requests for unified optimization. PJAR increased pooling, improved
repositioning success rates, and reduced eVMT without compromising wait times. An integrated MILP model
combining proactive assignment with repositioning and updated ICLV acceptance modeling further
demonstrated large gains in pooling adoption, system efficiency, and operator profit across both Greenville and
Austin.

Energy and System-Level Impacts
 Simulation results showed that increased pooling—enabled by human-factors-informed strategies—directly
reduced vehicle miles traveled, vehicle hours traveled, and energy consumption per passenger-mile. Greenville
benefited primarily from efficiency gains, while Austin experienced broader system coverage improvements.
Across all conditions, the integrated strategy lowered energy use and increased TNC profitability, demonstrating
alignment between user-centered improvements and energy-saving outcomes.

Overall Conclusion
 This project established a complete and empirically validated pipeline connecting human factors
understanding to technology innovation and system-level energy benefits in pooled rideshare. Through multi-
phase national data collection, advanced behavioral modeling, user-centered design synthesis, and high-fidelity
simulation, the work demonstrates that addressing human factors barriers is essential to increasing pooled
rideshare adoption, improving operational efficiency, and achieving energy reductions.
