Abstract
Computing optimal policy in heterogeneous-agent economies is complicated by the possibility of multiple equilibria. We overcome this difficulty by showing that when the equilibrium manifold has a low-dimensional Negishi-weight parameterization, Bayesian optimization reliably finds approximate solutions and can be used to certify candidate solutions with high probability. This insight brings recent machine learning advances to bear on a core problem in macroeconomics. We apply Bayesian optimization to a dynamic economy with heterogeneous agents and climate change and compute optimal carbon taxes in this setting. Although in principle the presence of the carbon externality creates scope for multiple equilibria, we show that in an example with realistic calibration of damages competitive equilibra are most likely unique.



Key words:
Optimal Policy, Ramsey Taxation, Gaussian Processes, Bayesian Optimization.

C61, C63, D58, H23, Q54, Q58.

Introduction

Many interesting questions about optimal economic policy can be formulated as non-convex optimization problems with equilibrium constraints – prominent examples include
models of optimal fiscal policy (
1
,
9
), models of optimal carbon policy (
28
,
20
) or models of macro-prudential regulation (

17

In general, there are only few results that justify the use of a first order approach to characterize optimal solutions in these models. Moreover, in the presence of heterogeneous agents, the possibility of multiple equilibria seems to make it impossible to reliably compute the optimal policy. In this paper, we consider an economically meaningful relexation of the original problem that allows us to write it as a box-constrained maximization problem, and we explore how Bayesian optimization (see
13
) can be used to solve this problem efficiently. Our key insight is that reliable probabilistic solutions can be obtained with moderate computational effort in cases where deterministic solutions are not tractable.

To explain this paper’s contribution, it is useful to consider the following abstract mathematical problem.

\max_{x\in B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(x)\mbox{ s.t. }h_{i}(x)\leq 0,i=1,\ldots,K.

(1)

Without convexity assumptions, this problem is generally not tractable and cannot be solved for moderate values for
\displaystyle n
.
However, in economic models,
it is often the case that one can write
\displaystyle x=(x^{1},x^{2})\in B_{1}\times B_{2}

\displaystyle B_{1}
is a
\displaystyle n_{1}
dimensional box and
\displaystyle B_{2}
is a
\displaystyle n_{2}
dimensional box with
\displaystyle n_{2}\gg n_{1}
and that
there exists a function
\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{2}:B_{1}\rightarrow B_{2}

\displaystyle g:B_{1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
such that (

1

\max_{x^{1}\in B_{1}}f(x^{1},\tilde{x}^{2}(x^{1}))\mbox{ s.t. }g_{i}(x^{1})\leq 0,i=1,\ldots,L.

(2)

Moreover, one can often define a family of penalty functions

\displaystyle({\cal P}(\cdot,\lambda))_{\lambda\geq 0}

such that the solution to the relaxed problem

\max_{x^{1}\in B_{1}}V(x^{1})=f(x^{1},\tilde{x}^{2}(x^{1}))-{\cal P}(g(x^{1}),\lambda)

(3)

\displaystyle\lambda>0

eq.

3

eq.

2

\displaystyle\lambda\rightarrow\infty

So far, it is not clear whether (
eq.

3

eq.

1

In most cases, the function
\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{2}(x^{1})
is only implicitly defined as the solution of a system of nonlinear equations. If this is the case, one first has to verify that there exists a unique solution. The computation of
\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{2}(x^{1})
becomes costly, and it might be impossible to compute derivatives.

However, we assume that the decision maker is Bayesian and uses a computational method to determine an approximate solution to (
eq.

3

\displaystyle\epsilon>0,\delta
the computation terminates if it produces a
\displaystyle\hat{x}^{1}
that satisfies

\mbox{Prob}[\exists x^{1}:V(x^{1})>V(\hat{x}^{1})+\delta]<\epsilon,

(4)

where the probability denotes the posterior probability given all the information inquired during the computations.
In this case, the numerical methods developed for Bayesian optimization are ideally suited for solving (
eq.

4

Bayesian optimization is a strategy for finding the maximum of expensive-to-evaluate functions, and it is particularly useful when one does not have access to gradients. The problem of finding the maximum of an unknown function was first examined in
16
.

point out the advantages of a sequential design. In the modern treatment of the problem, based on
22

the core idea is to build a surrogate model of the objective function and use it to decide where to evaluate next (see
13
for a detailed introduction to the topic).
This surrogate must be
a probabilistic model, such as a Gaussian process, as the
probabilistic treatment of uncertainty is vital to global
optimization: one must be able to reason about
the uncertainty in unvisited regions of the objective function’s domain.

An important class of economic applications takes the inequality constraints in (
eq.

1
) to be equality constraints and conditions for competitive equilibrium, and
\displaystyle x^{1}
to be a vector of policy instruments such as taxes and transfers. If one could show that equilibria are unique for all policies
\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{2}(x^{1})
would simply be the map of policies to equilibrium prices and allocations. Unfortunately, in most models, the known sufficient conditions for uniqueness of equilibrium are fare to restrictive, even though multiplicity does not turn out to be a problem for most calibrations.
One direct implication of (
eq.

4
) that often proves extremely useful in economic applications is that BO can be used to certify, with high probability, that a multivariate smooth function
\displaystyle f(x,\alpha)
is monotone in
\displaystyle x
for all parameters
\displaystyle\alpha
. For this, one would take
as the objective function in (
eq.

4
) the largest eigenvalue of the symmetric part of the Jacobian of
\displaystyle f
with respect to
\displaystyle x
. In the context of (
eq.

1

monotonicity of
\displaystyle h
in
\displaystyle x^{2}
is, of course, a sufficient condition for uniqueness. Therefore, one approach to solving (
eq.

1

\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{2}(x^{1})
is is in fact a function with high probability and to solve for it using Newton’s method.

Unfortunately, monotonicity of
\displaystyle h
often turns out to be too strong a condition for uniqueness. The strategy we propose in this paper is then to identify a subset of equations for which uniqueness of solution can be established, solve these equations with Newton’s method, and include the remaining equations in the penalty term.

To illustrate these ideas, we first consider a general equilibrium model of an exchange economy, where we characterize competitive equilibira with the Negishi-approach
(see, e.g.
19
). The penalty function in (
eq.

3
) can be taken to be the sum of squared budget violations across households; in the context of a policy problem, it can be interpreted as the costs of lump-sum transfers. We use a simple example of multiple equilibria to illustrate how BO correctly identifies them.
We then extend the model to include a public good and
a (Ramsey) planner who produces the public good by confiscating the inputs from the households and who maximizes a social welfare function
1
1

1

The model mainly serves illustrative purposes, the method developed can be applied to a wide variety of optimal policy problems.
.
In this context, one might naively
take
\displaystyle x^{1}
as a vector that specifies economic policy (supply of the public good) and
\displaystyle x^{2}
as equilibrium prices and allocations for that policy.
The difficulty is that multiple equilibria generally cannot be ruled out: for a fixed policy, several equilibria may coexist, each yielding a different value of the planner’s objective. Naturally, one would assume that the planner can select the equilibrium that yields the highest value of the social welfare function — but, as noted above, there is no continuous function
\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{2}(x^{1})
.
We instead let
\displaystyle x^{1}
consist of the public good supply together with all agents’ Negishi weights. Given production decisions, consumption allocations are then the solution to a convex optimization problem and can be determined easily and reliably. We provide simple examples constructed to exhibit multiple equilibria and show that the BO algorithm correctly identifies the one that yields the highest value of the objective function.

To illustrate that the method applies to economically interesting settings, we consider a macroeconomic model of climate change with infinitely lived agents, polluting firms, and climate damages to both total factor productivity and depreciation. We give an example showing that even in a single-agent version of the model (similar to
3
), the presence of externalities can give rise to multiple competitive equilibria. We then turn to a model with four heterogeneous agents and show how BO can be used to solve for the optimal carbon tax and revenue-sharing rule.
We proceed in two steps. First, we use BO to certify that for our calibration and for given taxes and Negishi weights, the equilibrium exhibits a unique capital path with high probability. Second, using BO together with penalties for budget violations, we show that in the absence of carbon taxes the equilibrium is unique with probability very close to one.
We consider both a constant carbon tax and a carbon tax that is an arbitrary time-dependent function. In our calibrations, carbon tax revenue is large enough that the planner can achieve the same solution as under unrestricted lump-sum transfers. The welfare differences between a simple constant-tax scheme and more elaborate schemes turn out to be insignificant. In the case with constant carbon taxes, it can be certified that equilibria are unique with high probability.

While there is a large literature on the computation of optimal policies in dynamic economic models, there are few papers that apply ideas from machine learning to the problem.

31
are the first to use Gaussian process regression for dynamic economic modeling.
5
demonstrate how the use of a surrogate model can lead to large efficiency gains in option pricing problems, but neither of these papers considers Bayesian optimization.
Ignoring the problem of multiple equilibria,

21
are the first to use Bayesian optimization to characterize optimal policies. They analyze constrained optimal carbon taxes in a stochastic OLG model with climate change. In that paper the computation of equilibria for a given set of instruments is one of the main contributions.
The main contribution of this paper is to use Bayesian optimization to find constrained optimal policies taking the possibility of multiple equilibria into account.
As mentioned above, the results of BO used in this article are all standard and can be found in
13
.

In this paper, the successful demonstrations of our method operate in relatively low dimensions. In some of the examples there might be possible alternatives to BO - dense grids in one or two dimensions, multistart deterministic global optimization, homotopy/continuation in Negishi weights, deterministic global methods using Lipschitz bounds, or stochastic global methods for final certification offer standard benchmarks.
It is subject to future research to establish how BO compares to plausible alternatives on the examples in this paper. Because acquisition maximization is inherently non-convex, defining the exact threshold where BO overtakes existing computational approaches would solidify the framework’s comparative advantage. At this point, the results are more suggestive than definitive.

2
Bayesian Optimization

All the results in this section are standard and are repeated here only for completeness.
Good references are
37
,
13
and
15
.

Bayesian Optimization (BO) is a strategy for global optimization of black-box functions that are expensive to evaluate.
The key idea is to build a probabilistic surrogate model of the objective function and use it to decide where to sample next, balancing exploration and exploitation.
Consider the global optimization problem:

x^{*}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)

(5)

where:

•

\displaystyle f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is the objective function (black-box, expensive to evaluate)

•

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact domain (often a hyperrectangle)

•

We can only observe
\displaystyle y_{i}=f(x_{i})+\epsilon_{i}
where
\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})
is i.i.d. noise.

The goal is to find
\displaystyle x^{*}
(or a good approximation) using as few evaluations of
\displaystyle f
as possible.
While in our experiment below, we typically consider the noise-free case with
\displaystyle\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=0
, it is standard to introduce the general concepts for the noisy case.
Bayesian optimization consists of two main components:

1.

Surrogate Model:

A probabilistic model of the objective function, typically a
Gaussian Process
(GP):

f(x)\sim\mathcal{GP}(m(x),k(x,x^{\prime}))

(6)

2.

Acquisition Function:

A function
\displaystyle\alpha:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
that uses the surrogate model to determine the next point to evaluate:

x_{n+1}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(x\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})

(7)

The acquisition function balances exploitation (sample where the model predicts high values) and exploration (sample where uncertainty is high).

In order to provide theoretical foundations for the use of BO in economics, we first review the theory of GP’s and then summarize known theoretical properties of BO algorithms. In particular, we explain how probabilistic results can be efficiently certified.

2.1
Introduction to Gaussian processes

Definition 1

(Gaussian Process)
.

A
Gaussian process
(GP) is a collection of random variables, any finite number of which have a joint Gaussian distribution. Formally, a stochastic process
\displaystyle\{f(x):x\in\mathcal{X}\}
is a Gaussian process if for any finite subset
\displaystyle\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
, the random vector
\displaystyle(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{n}))^{\top}
follows a multivariate normal distribution.

It is easy to see that a Gaussian process is completely specified by its mean function and covariance function (kernel):

\displaystyle\displaystyle m(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f(x)]

(8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle k(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(f(x)-m(x))(f(x^{\prime})-m(x^{\prime}))]

(9)

We write
\displaystyle f\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
. The general definition of a covariance kernel is as follows.

Definition 2

(Covariance Kernel)
.

A function
\displaystyle k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
covariance kernel
(or positive definite kernel) if for any finite set of points
\displaystyle\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
, the matrix
\displaystyle K
with entries
\displaystyle K_{ij}=k(x_{i},x_{j})
is positive semidefinite.

Throughout this paper, we focus on the so-called (ARD) squared-exponential kernel defined as

k_{SE}(x,x^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}_{f}exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{(x_{d}-x^{\prime}_{d})^{2}}{\ell_{d}^{2}}\right).

ARD ("automatic relevance determination") means that we specify one lengthscale
\displaystyle\ell_{d}
per input dimension instead of a single shared lengthscale as in the case of the isotropic SE kernel.

The hyperparameters
\displaystyle\sigma_{f},(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d})
play an important role and their determination is discussed in the appendix.
In the appendix we also introduce a more general parametric family of kernels, the Matérn kernels, and discuss their properties.

2.1.1
GP regression

Consider the regression problem with training data
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
are inputs and
\displaystyle y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are noisy observations:

y_{i}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\epsilon_{i},\quad\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})

(10)

We place a GP prior on
\displaystyle f
:

f\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))

(11)

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]^{\top}
be the training inputs,
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]^{\top}
the training outputs, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{*}=[\mathbf{x}_{*1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{*m}]^{\top}
the test inputs. Define:

\displaystyle\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

(12)

\displaystyle\displaystyle K_{*}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{X}_{*},\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

(13)

\displaystyle\displaystyle K_{**}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{X}_{*},\mathbf{X}_{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}

(14)

The joint distribution of observed and test function values is:

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}\\
\mathbf{f}_{*}\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},\begin{bmatrix}K+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}I&K_{*}^{\top}\\
K_{*}&K_{**}\end{bmatrix}\right)

(15)

The posterior distribution of the test outputs
\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{*}
given the training data is Gaussian:

\mathbf{f}_{*}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}_{*}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{*},\bm{\Sigma}_{*})

(16)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{*}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=K_{*}(K+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}I)^{-1}\mathbf{y}

(17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{*}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=K_{**}-K_{*}(K+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}I)^{-1}K_{*}^{\top}

(18)

For a single test point
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{*}
, the predictive mean and variance are:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{x}_{*})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{*}^{\top}(K+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}I)^{-1}\mathbf{y}

(19)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{*})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{x}_{*})-\mathbf{k}_{*}^{\top}(K+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}I)^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{*}

(20)

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{*}=k(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{x}_{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We start with a prior mean and covariance function for the Gaussian process.

2.2
Bayesian optimization

Throughout the paper, we assume that the true objective function
\displaystyle f(.)
in (
eq.

5
) is a sample path of the Gaussian process used to model it. In a Bayesian analysis, this is the natural assumption. It provides a known distribution for the
function values at unobserved locations and therefore the foundation of any acquisition function.
We discuss the assumption in Appendix
section

A.2
.

2.2.1
Acquisition Functions for BO

While there are a variety of different approaches to derive useful acquisition function (see
13
for details) this paper focuses on the following two.

•

Probability of Improvement (PI):
Let
\displaystyle f^{+}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}f(x_{i})
be the best observed value. The probability of improvement is:

\text{PI}(x;\xi)=\mbox{Prob}(f(x)\geq f^{+}+\xi)=\Phi\left(\frac{\mu_{n}(x)-f^{+}-\xi}{\sigma_{n}(x)}\right)

(21)

where
\displaystyle\Phi
is the standard normal CDF and
\displaystyle\xi\geq 0
is a trade-off parameter.

•

Upper Confidence bound (UCB):
Let
\displaystyle\beta_{n}>0
be a scalar exploration parameter. The
upper confidence
bound
(UCB) acquisition function at iteration
\displaystyle n
is defined by

\alpha_{n}^{\mathrm{UCB}}(x)\;:=\;\mu_{n}(x)\;+\;\sqrt{\beta_{n}}\,\sigma_{n}(x),\qquad x\in{\mathcal{X}}.

The UCB acquisition function can be interpreted as a deterministic upper confidence
bound on
\displaystyle f(x)
: under the GP model,

\mbox{Prob}\!\left(f(x)\leq\mu_{n}(x)+\sqrt{\beta_{n}}\,\sigma_{n}(x)\right)\;=\;\Phi\!\left(\sqrt{\beta_{n}}\right),

where
\displaystyle\Phi
is the standard normal CDF. Choosing
\displaystyle\beta_{n}
thus corresponds to
specifying a pointwise confidence level for the upper bound.

2.2.2
Regret bounds and certification of global optima

Let
\displaystyle x^{*}
denote the solution to (
eq.

5
) and define
the
simple regret
after
\displaystyle T
rounds:

R_{T}^{\mathrm{simple}}\;=\;f(x^{*})-f(\hat{x}),\mbox{ where }f(\hat{x})=\max_{i\leq T}f(x_{i}).

In general we cannot obtain useful deterministic bounds on the simple regret, but we demonstrate in this section how to derive a probabilistic bound, i.e. for small
\displaystyle\delta,\epsilon>0
we want to make a statment of the form

\mbox{Prob}\left[R_{T}^{\mathrm{simple}}\geq\delta\right]<\epsilon.

(22)

Using a PI acquisition function, with a trade-off parameter
\displaystyle\delta
and some small
\displaystyle\eta>0
, the algorithm can
obviously be terminated after
\displaystyle T
rounds if
\displaystyle PI(x)<\eta
for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{X}
. However, whether this information suffices to obtain a bound as in (
eq.

22
) clearly depends on the Lipschitz continuity of the true objective function,
\displaystyle f
. If little prior knowledge of the unknown function
\displaystyle f(\cdot)
is given, it might not be possible to directly derive a Lipschitz constant
\displaystyle L_{f}
on
\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}
. However, we indirectly assume a certain distribution of the derivatives of
\displaystyle f(\cdot)
with the assumption that the true objective is a sample path of our GP. Therefore, it is possible to derive a probabilistic Lipschitz constant
\displaystyle L_{f}
from this assumption, which is described in the following lemma from
23
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider a zero mean Gaussian process defined through the covariance kernel
\displaystyle k(\cdot,\cdot)
with continuous partial derivatives up to the fourth order and partial derivative kernels

k^{\partial i}\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}k\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\right)\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,D

Let
\displaystyle L_{k}^{\partial i}
denote the Lipschitz constants of the partial derivative kernels
\displaystyle k^{\partial i}(\cdot,\cdot)
on the set
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}
with maximal extension
\displaystyle r=\max_{\bm{x},\bm{x}\prime\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\right\|
. Then, a sample function
\displaystyle f(\cdot)
of the Gaussian process is almost surely continuous on
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}
and with probability of at least
\displaystyle 1-\delta_{L}
, it holds that

L_{f}=\left\|\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{2D}{\delta_{L}}\right)}\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{k^{\partial 1}(\bm{x},\bm{x})}+12\sqrt{6D}\max\left\{\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{k^{\partial 1}(\bm{x},\bm{x})},\sqrt{rL_{k}^{\partial 1}}\right\}\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{2D}{\delta_{L}}\right)}\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{k^{\partial d}(\bm{x},\bm{x})}+12\sqrt{6D}\max\left\{\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{k^{\partial D}(\bm{x},\bm{x})},\sqrt{rL_{k}^{\partial D}}\right\}\end{array}\right]\right\|

is a Lipschitz constant of
\displaystyle f(\cdot)
on X.

For the SE kernel, it is straightforward to show that

\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{k(\bm{x},\bm{x})}=\sigma_{f}\quad\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{k^{\partial i}(\bm{x},\bm{x})}=\frac{\sigma_{f}}{\ell_{i}}.

Furthermore, the Lipschitz constant of the derivative kernels is given by

L_{k}^{\partial i}=\omega\frac{\sigma_{f}^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{3}},

where

\displaystyle\omega=\sqrt{6(3-\sqrt{6})}\exp\left(\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}-\frac{3}{2}\right)
.
Therefore, for the special case of a SE kernel, the
the bound for each dimension
\displaystyle i
simplifies to:

L_{f}^{i}=\frac{\sigma_{f}}{\ell_{i}}\sqrt{2\log\frac{2D}{\delta}}+12\sqrt{6D}\cdot\max\left(\frac{\sigma_{f}}{\ell_{i}},\sqrt{\frac{3r\sigma_{f}^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{3}}}\right).

(23)

From this result, we can use a standard covering argument to obtain probabilistic bounds on simple regret. In the following, we do not carry through the probability of the Lipschitz bound
\displaystyle\delta_{L}
, it is understood that this needs to be factored into all the probabilistic statements that follow.

To obtain a bound of the form (
eq.

22
), we assume, for simplicity, that
\displaystyle{\cal X}
is a hyperrectangle.
For some small
\displaystyle\eta>0
, we build a rectangular grid with spacing

\displaystyle h_{d}=\frac{2\eta}{DL_{f}^{d}}

along each dimension
\displaystyle d
, resulting in
\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{cover}}
points altogether. In what follows we refer to this as the covering number.

So for any
\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{X}
its nearest
grid point
\displaystyle x_{g}
satisfies:

f(x)-f(x_{g})\;\leq\;\sum_{d}L_{f}^{d}\,\lvert x_{d}-x_{g,d}\rvert\;\leq\;\sum_{d}L_{f}^{d}\,\frac{h_{d}}{2}\;=\;\eta.

So if
\displaystyle f(x)\geq\delta
, then
\displaystyle f(x_{g})\geq\delta-\eta
must hold. Using the Lipschitz covering as well as a standard union bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{Prob}\left(\exists\,x\in\mathcal{X}:f(x)\geq\delta\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mbox{Prob}\left(\exists\text{ grid point }x_{g}:f(x_{g})\geq\delta-\eta\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sum_{x_{g}}\mbox{Prob}\left(f(x_{g})\geq\delta-\eta\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\;\leq\;n_{\mathrm{cover}}\cdot\max_{x_{g}}\,\mbox{Prob}\left(f(x_{g})\geq\delta-\eta\right).

With the definition of the PI acquisition function, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{Prob}\left(\exists\,x\in\mathcal{X}:f(x)\geq f(\hat{x})+\gamma\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\;\leq\;n_{\mathrm{cover}}\cdot\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\;\mathrm{PI}\!\left(x;\;\gamma-\sum_{d}L_{f}^{d}\,\frac{h_{d}}{2}\right).

By the same logic, we can partition
\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}
into a
\displaystyle\epsilon-
neighborhood around the observed current best point
\displaystyle\hat{x}
that satisfies
\displaystyle f(\hat{x})=\max_{t\leq T}f(x_{t})
,
\displaystyle{\mathbf{B}}_{\epsilon}(\hat{x})
and its complement in
\displaystyle{\cal X}\setminus{\mathbf{B}}_{\epsilon}(\hat{x})
. We then obtain bounds of the form

\mbox{Prob}\left(\exists x\in{\mathcal{X}}\setminus{\mathbf{B}}_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}):f(x)\geq f(\hat{x})-\delta\right)<\eta.

(24)

Depending on the economic problem the bound (
eq.

24
) might be more relevant than (
eq.

22
) – it rules out that there are other local optima close-by and hence can ensure that equilibria are unique not only at given parameter-values but also in a neighborhood of these values. We will illustrate the usefulness of both bounds below.

2.3
Probably monotone functions

A function
\displaystyle f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is strictly monotonically decreasing whenever
\displaystyle D_{x}f(x)
is negative definite. In the context of multiplicity of equilibrium, monotonicity is an interesting property because montonone demand (or monotone expenditure) is a simply sufficient condition for uniqueness. To use BO to verify monotonicity, recall that a symmetric matrix is negative definite if and only if all of its eigenvalues are negative. One possible approach is to use BO to maximize the largest eigenvalue of
\displaystyle D_{x}f(x)+(D_{x}f(x))^{T}
. Unfortunately, this is not a smooth function in
\displaystyle x
. We therefore consider the following objective function.

g(x)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp(\beta\lambda_{i}(D_{x}f(x)+(D_{x}f(x))^{T}))\right),

(25)

where
\displaystyle\lambda(M)
denotes the vector of eigenvalues of a symetric matrix
\displaystyle M

It is easy to verify that

A simple planning problem

Now suppose that in the two-good example, there is an additional public good. The government has access to a linear technology to produce the public good using commodity 1 as an input. A policy confiscates a fraction
\displaystyle\xi

Since this is not a convex optimization problem, there can be many solutions to the KKT conditions, and it is generally not tractable to compute all of them. Even if they can be written as polynomial functions and all solutions can be computed numerically, it becomes quickly untractable to do so (see, e.g.

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle p_{1},(1-p_{1})

Following

5
Conclusion

Many economic applications of interest give rise to intractable non-convex optimization problems. This paper argues that they can be tackled with Bayesian optimization. Although the optimum cannot be located with certainty, it can be approximated with high probability. The Bayesian optimization literature provides a decision-theoretic foundation for this approximation by reducing the problem to a bandit problem. Establishing how that foundation can be micro-founded within the optimal policy problems arising in economics is left to future work.

Additional results on Gaussian Processes

A.1
Alternative kernels

Although the examples in this paper all use the SE-kernel it is useful
to also consider an alternative class of kernels. Whenever the objective function is not
\displaystyle C^{\infty}
, the use of the SE-kernel is not appropriate.
The Matérn kernel that is widely used in Gaussian process regression, was introduced by Bertil Matérn in 1960 (see
26
). It generalizes the squared exponential kernel by introducing a smoothness parameter
\displaystyle\nu>0
that controls the differentiability of the resulting sample paths. The general form is given by

k_{\nu}(r)=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\,r}{\ell}\right)^{\!\nu}K_{\nu}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\,r}{\ell}\right),

where
\displaystyle r=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|
is the Euclidean distance between inputs,
\displaystyle\ell>0
is the length-scale hyperparameter,
\displaystyle\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function, and
\displaystyle K_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order
\displaystyle\nu
. A Gaussian process with Matérn-
\displaystyle\nu
covariance has sample paths that are
\displaystyle\lceil\nu\rceil-1
times mean-square differentiable.

When
\displaystyle\nu
takes the half-integer values
\displaystyle\nu=p+\tfrac{1}{2}
for
\displaystyle p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the kernel simplifies to a product of an exponential and a polynomial, making it computationally convenient. The two most commonly used special cases are as follows.

•

Matérn
\displaystyle\tfrac{3}{2}
:
Setting
\displaystyle\nu=\tfrac{3}{2}
yields sample paths that are once mean-square differentiable and we can write the kernel as

k_{3/2}(r)=\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{3}\,r}{\ell}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}\,r}{\ell}\right).

•

Matérn
\displaystyle\tfrac{5}{2}
: Setting
\displaystyle\nu=\tfrac{5}{2}
yields sample paths that are twice mean-square differentiable and we can write the kernel as

k_{5/2}(r)=\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{5}\,r}{\ell}+\frac{5\,r^{2}}{3\ell^{2}}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{\sqrt{5}\,r}{\ell}\right).

The Matérn
\displaystyle\tfrac{5}{2}
kernel is particularly popular in Bayesian optimization, where its moderate smoothness assumption often matches the regularity of real-world objective functions more faithfully than either Matérn
\displaystyle\tfrac{3}{2}
or the squared exponential.

As
\displaystyle\nu\to\infty
, the Matérn kernel converges to the squared exponential (RBF) kernel
\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{SE}}(r)=\exp\bigl(-r^{2}/(2\ell^{2})\bigr)
, which corresponds to infinitely differentiable sample paths. At the other extreme,
\displaystyle\nu=\tfrac{1}{2}
recovers the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck kernel
\displaystyle k_{1/2}(r)=\exp(-r/\ell)
, whose sample paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable.

In our examples, one can also use a Matérn 5/2 kernel and results turn out to be very similar, with slightly more sample points needed to establish the probabilistic bounds.

A.2
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

The main assumption underlying the probabilistic bounds is that the true objective function is a sample path of the Gaussian process. This is typically not exactly true but holds as an arbitrarily good approximation.
In order to investigate the relationship between the objective function and Gaussian process regression formally, it is necessary to define the concept of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (see, e.g.
18

Definition 3

The Moore-Aronszajn theorem states that

\displaystyle 0<c\leq C<\infty
such that
\displaystyle c\leq|c_{n}|²/\lambda_{n}\leq C
for all
\displaystyle n
, where
\displaystyle(\lambda_{n})
are the (super-exponentially decaying) eigenvalues of the SE kernel’s integral operator. This is an extremely strong constraint — it requires the coefficients to decay at precisely the right super-exponential rate.

While it is difficult to show that the true function is a sample path, it is straightforward to show that there are functions arbitrarily close (in the sup-norm) to the true function that are a sample path of our GP with SE kernel.

36
prove that
the support of a mean-zero Gaussian random element in a separable Banach space
\displaystyle B
is the closure of its RKHS in
\displaystyle B
.
For a GP with the SE kernel defined on a compact domain, taking
\displaystyle B
to be the space of continuous functions with the sup-norm, the RKHS of the SE kernel consists of analytic functions with a specific decay rate on their Fourier (or Hermite) coefficients. So the topological support is the closure of this RKHS in the sup-norm topology.
The practical consequence (see, e.g.
35
) is that the probability of a draw of the Gaussian process falling in a neighbourhood of a given continuous
\displaystyle f
is typically positive, no matter how small the neighbourhood. This means every continuous function in the interior of the sup-norm closure of the RKHS is in the support. For the SE kernel on a compact domain, the RKHS is dense in the space of continuous function so the support in the sup-norm topology is all of C(T) — meaning every continuous function has positive probability of being approached arbitrarily closely.

Obviously, this does not imply that every
\displaystyle C^{\infty}
function is a sample path in the almost-sure sense – however, for our purposes this approximation is sufficient. First, in all numerical work functions are computed with machine precision, and the distinction between an arbitrary good approximation to the true function and the true function itself becomes meaningless. Second, since we are only interested in the maximum of the true function, it is clear that this also can be arbitrarily well approximated by the maximum of a sample path of the GP. Note that in order for our Lipschitz certificates are to apply beyond the exact Bayesian model, the approximation error does need to enter the probability or regret statements, since there are sample paths for which this error is arbitrarly small.

A.3
Hyperparameters

Given training data
\displaystyle\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with inputs
\displaystyle\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and noisy scalar targets
\displaystyle y_{i}
, we model the latent function
\displaystyle f
as a
Gaussian process,

f\sim\mathcal{GP}\bigl(0,\,k(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\bigr),\qquad y_{i}=f(\bm{x}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\quad\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2}).

The squared-exponential (SE, or RBF) kernel is

k(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\;=\;\sigma_{f}^{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\rVert^{2}}{2\,\ell^{2}}\right),

with
hyperparameters
collected in

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}=(\sigma_{f}^{2},\,\ell,)
:
signal variance
\displaystyle\sigma_{f}^{2}
, length-scale
\displaystyle\ell
(a single
value for the isotropic case; one per dimension
\displaystyle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}
for
automatic relevance determination). Estimation of
\displaystyle\bm{\theta}
from the data is of central importance for the performance of BO.

A.3.1
The marginal likelihood

Let
\displaystyle K\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
have entries
\displaystyle K_{ij}=k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})
and define

\displaystyle K_{\bm{y}}=K+\sigma_{n}^{2}I
. Marginalising over the latent values
\displaystyle f
gives a
Gaussian distribution over the observed targets, and its log density—the

log marginal likelihood
—is

\log p(\bm{y}\mid X,\bm{\theta})=-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{y}^{\top}K_{\bm{y}}^{-1}\bm{y}\;-\;\tfrac{1}{2}\log\lvert K_{\bm{y}}\rvert\;-\;\tfrac{n}{2}\log 2\pi.

The three terms have intuitive roles: the first rewards fitting the data,
the second penalises model complexity, and the last is a normalising
constant. Maximising this quantity with respect to
\displaystyle\bm{\theta}
is the
standard estimation procedure, often called
type-II maximum
likelihood
or
empirical Bayes
, because the complexity penalty
provides an automatic Occam’s-razor balance.

Gradient-based optimisation is efficient because the derivatives are
available in closed form. Writing
\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}=K_{\bm{y}}^{-1}\bm{y}
, the
derivative with respect to any hyperparameter
\displaystyle\theta_{j}
is

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\log p(\bm{y}\mid X,\bm{\theta})=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\bigl(\bm{\alpha}\bm{\alpha}^{\top}-K_{\bm{y}}^{-1}\bigr)\frac{\partial K_{\bm{y}}}{\partial\theta_{j}}\right).

The kernel derivatives
\displaystyle\partial K_{\bm{y}}/\partial\theta_{j}
are
straightforward. With
\displaystyle r_{ij}=\lVert\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}\rVert
,

\frac{\partial K_{ij}}{\partial\sigma_{f}^{2}}=\frac{K_{ij}}{\sigma_{f}^{2}},\qquad\frac{\partial K_{ij}}{\partial\ell}=K_{ij}\,\frac{r_{ij}^{2}}{\ell^{3}},\qquad\frac{\partial K_{\bm{y}}}{\partial\sigma_{n}^{2}}=I.

Maximizing aquisition functions

It is easy to see that when the objective function is not concave, acquisition functions are generally non-concave and they are often multi-modal. Therefore, it might seem that the main problem of maximizing a non-concave function does not become any simpler by using Bayesian optimziation.

The crucial advantage of the approach lies in the fact that for a continuously differentiable kernel function, one can find explicit bounds on the Lipschitz constant, which is crucial in many methods for global optimization. While the truen objective function is typically also Lipschitz continuous, bounds on the Lipschitz constant cannot be obtained.
