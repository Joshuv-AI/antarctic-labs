From some Pisot numerations to topological groups Thanks: The first author was supported by the Agence Nationale de la Recherche through the project “SymDynAr” (ANR-23-CE40-0024-01).
Olivier Carton, Jake Sudbery, Reem Yassawi

Abstract
. A Pisot numeration system U U for ℕ \mathbb{N} is a sequence of natural numbers generated by an integral homogeneous linear recurrence whose characteristic polynomial is the minimal polynomial of a Pisot number. The purpose of this paper is to introduce the analogue of the group of p p -adic integers for such numerations when they preserve zeros , which is equivalent to the “Condition F” introduced by Frougny and Solomyak for β \beta -numerations. We show that these topological groups ℤ U \mathbb{Z}_{U} project homomorphically onto a torus. Equipping ℤ U \mathbb{Z}_{U} with the appropriate topology, we also show that if U U is unimodular, then ℤ U \mathbb{Z}_{U} is continuously isomorphic to a torus.

1.
Introduction

The additive group
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
of
p
-adic integers can be constructed as the
inverse limit of the finite groups
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
, but it can also be constructed
as a topological completion of
\mathbb{N}
. This completion is achieved through an
ultrametric which is based on the base-
p
expansions of natural integers.
The purpose of this article is to construct the analogue of this group for
Pisot numerations. Recall that a Pisot numeration replaces the powers
of
p
, used in base-
p
expansions, by a sequence of integers satisfying a
linear recurrence defined by the minimal polynomial of a Pisot number,
i.e., an algebraic integer all of whose conjugates lie in the interior of
the unit disk. The iconic, and simplest, numeration of this type is the
Zeckendorf numeration, which uses the sequence of Fibonacci numbers,
associated to the golden mean. In such a numeration each natural number
has a unique
normalised
expansion, i.e., the one which is obtained
using the greedy algorithm; see Section
2.2
for
definitions and details.

In this article we introduce, for each Pisot numeration
U
satisfying a
mild condition, a group
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
which is the analogue of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. It can
be viewed as a topological completion of the set of normalised

U
-expansions of the natural numbers, as each element of
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
can be
represented by an infinite normalised sequence of digits. Our construction
of
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
does not follow the same strategy as for
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for the
following reason. When two base-
p
expansions are added, the carries
propagate to the left. This is reflected in the fact that the
p
-adic
valuation
\nu_{p}
satisfies the classical inequality

\nu_{p}(x+y)\geqslant\min(\nu_{p}(x),\nu_{p}(y))
. This property no longer holds for Pisot
numerations: carries may propagate both to the left and to the right, as is
already the case for the Zeckendorf numeration. For this reason, our
construction is based on a
pseudo-valuation

\nu_{U}
which satisfies
the weaker inequality
\nu_{U}(x+y)\geqslant\min(\nu_{U}(x),\nu_{U}(y))-K
for some
constant
K
; we say that numerations that satisfy this
preserve
zeros
. We also equip our group
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
with a topology, but as a
result of the nonstandard propagation of the carries,
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
is not a
profinite group with a zero-dimensional topology.

Our main result is that for a given Pisot numeration, the group that
we construct is also topologically an
n
-torus
\mathbb{T}
, in the presence of
mild hypotheses.

Theorem 1
.

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a numeration associated with a Pisot number
\beta

of degree
d
, with standard initial conditions. If
U
preserves zeros,
then there is a continuous group epimorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{Z}_{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{d-1}
.
If
\beta
is unimodular, then
\Phi
is an isomorphism.

The torus
\mathbb{T}^{d-1}
is realised as
(\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell})/\mathbb{L}
, where the Pisot number
has
2k
complex and
\ell
real conjugates, and where
\mathbb{L}
is a regular
lattice, defined in Section
4
. When defining
a numeration
U
, one degree of freedom is the choice of initial
conditions. We found that the right choice of initial conditions, which we
call
standard
, is relevant. By taking other initial conditions
we can create coverings of the space instead of tilings, or indeed, small
initial conditions so that the image of
\tilde{\Phi}
is not surjective; see
Example
51
.

Much of the novelty in our result is the development of the topological
group
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
. We start with the group
\mathbb{G}
of bounded integer-valued
sequences, equipped with componentwise addition, and define a kind of
valuation. Through this we define a subgroup
\mathbb{H}_{U}
of expansions of
0
,
and we set
\mathbb{Z}_{U}=\mathbb{G}/\mathbb{H}_{U}
; details are in
Section
3
. The definition of
\Phi
is then
is adapted from what already exists in the tiling and substitutional literature,
as we summarise below, although our map is in addition now a group
homomorphism.

A Pisot numeration comes with its associated Pisot number
\beta
, and the
study of
\beta
-numerations is well-established. We show in
Section
5
that a Pisot numeration preserves zeros if
and only if the associated
\beta
-numeration satisfies
Condition F
,
introduced by Frougny and Solomyak in
[
FS92
]
.
Frougny and Solomyak identified families of Pisot numbers that satisfy
Condition F, and Akiyama
[
Aki98
]
characterised the
unimodular
\beta
which satisfy it, and also gave an algorithm which decides
whether or not a given Pisot number satisfies Condition F. This then allows us to
decide when a numeration preserves zeros.

The closest result in the literature which compares to ours, but in the

\beta
-numeration setting, is that of Vershik
[
Ver92
]
. There
he considers
\beta
-expansions over powers of the golden ratio. He
constructs a completion of the set of those numbers, and shows, using
character theory, that this completion is isomorphic to the field of real
numbers, but “with an unusual embedding of the set of powers of the golden
mean”. Vershik also writes that one can approach this via the “Fibonacci
decomposition of naturals into a sum of Fibonacci numbers (Zeckendorf
theorem)…but it does not explain the algebraic structure of this
construction.” Here we take this approach and we make explicit the
algebraic structure of this construction.

There is also related work on characterising when an odometer defined by a
numeration admits addition by 1 as a continuous map. In

[
GLT95
]
, Grabner, Liardet and Tichy define a

G-adic
space, where
G=(G_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is a strictly increasing
sequence of positive integers, and study the addition of 1 in this space,
characterising
G
for which this map is continuous. No group structure is
defined for these odometers. The authors also study the case when
G
is
obtained by a linear recurrence, and show that the adic system is
measure-theoretically isomorphic to a group rotation given a certain

Hypothesis B
. They ask whether this hypothesis is equivalent to
Condition F, which would make this result equivalent to that of Solomyak

[
Sol92
]
.

Several authors focus on extending the Zeckendorf numeration to the
integers, or to a group. In
[
RV12
]
, Rittaud and Vivier
define a topological group specifically for this numeration, by defining a valuation, and then a group which turns out to be identical to ours in that particular case. In other recent work,
connections are made between Zeckendorf numerations for
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
Wang tiles. In
[
LL21
]
, Labbé and Lepšová show the
existence of a Wang tiling whose
(m,n)
-th entry is the output of an
automaton when the input is a representation of
(m,n)
in this
numeration. In
[
LL23
]
, they show that this numeration is
the Zeckendorf analogue of the two’s complement numeration system, which
gives a base-2 expansion for negative integers. They also generalise this
to a two-sided approach of Dumont-Thomas numerations
in
[
LL24
]
.

Our topological groups have familiar geometric representations, as
well-known tiles that come from substitutions (see below) and which are
fundamental domains of Euclidean space; see our examples. In

[
Aki99
]
, Akiyama also studied tilings generated by

\beta
-numerations satisfying Condition F. He writes that his work treats
these tilings in the context of numerations and not substitutions, as this
method gives a clearer understanding of the universal phenomena of these
tilings. That these tilings already come from the underlying numeration of
the substitution is pertinent. One difference between
U
and a

\beta
-numeration is that a
\beta
-expansion has both an integral and a
fractional part, the latter to the right of the radix point, the former to
the left. To generate his tilings, Akiyama considers valid finite
expansions, and maps them in a now standard way, as done by Rauzy
in
[
Rau82
]
, to a Euclidean space. These techniques, or some mild
modification, are a widely-used method to obtain these fractals in Euclidean
space, see, e.g.,
[
CS01
]
. Indeed this is the technique
we also use to map
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
to the torus. The integral part of the
expansion corresponds to a translation, while the fractional part generates
one tile. Because Akiyama considers these two parts in tandem, and because
different integral parts have different fractional continuations, he
obtains a tiling of the space using finitely many tiles, whereas we only
use one.

We also emphasise that our results concern numerations and not substitutions.
Nevertheless we can transfer our results to a family of substitutions,
whose fixed point has a shift-orbit that can be coded in an ordered way
using the orbit of addition by 1 in
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
. These are the

\beta
-substitutions
. In
[
Sol92
]
, Solomyak showed
that
\beta
-substitution shifts where
\beta
satisfies the Condition F
have discrete spectrum; this was extended more recently by Barge to all

\beta
-substitutions
[
Bar18
]
. We can use our setting to deduce
that these shifts are almost automorphic; this will be done in future
work. There are several works linking numerations to substitutions, mainly
via their Bratteli-Vershik representations as generalised odometers or the
Dumont-Thomas numeration system. Some references are
[
Rau82
,
CS01
,
BBLT06
,
Thu06
,
Sur20
]
. This
list is far from exhaustive, and many works use some variation of Rauzy’s
coding.

We mention other work where this kind of coding of a
\beta
-shift to a
torus is used. In
[
Ver92
]
, Vershik also studied the Fibonacci
hyperbolic toral automorphism, and uses the same coding techniques as above
to show that this automorphism is arithmetically isomorphic to a

\beta
-shift; this approach is generalised in
[
SV98
]
.
Sidorov and Vershik work with
\beta
-numerations and Pisot numbers of
degree 2, characterising when one can establish arithmetic codings from a
two-sided
\beta
-shift of expansions of positive real numbers to a
hyperbolic toral automorphism. Ex post facto, they can
impose a group structure by identifying points in shift space via the
arithmetic coding. Sidorov
[
Sid03
]
generalises this to higher
degree polynomials. There is also related work by Schmidt in

[
Sch00
]
. Our work is transversal to this, in the sense that
instead of multiplication, the underlying dynamics is addition.

While the groups
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
that we introduce are the analogue for Pisot
numerations of the additive group structure of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, the multiplicative
structure of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is ignored. So far it seems difficult to turn our groups
into rings. The usual multiplication of integers is not compatible with
the topology we use in the sense that it does not preserve zeros. Knuth
introduced in
[
Knu88
]
another multiplication on integers based on
the Zeckendorf numeration, but it is not distributive with respect to
addition.

In Section
2
, we set up notation. In
Section
3
, we define zero preservation, and the

U
-adic integers, and show that their elements are infinite normalised
sequences for
U
. Once we have established
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
, we introduce a
topology and link it to a torus in Section
4
,
using classical techniques that are used in many of the above cited
articles. We also show that in the case where
U
is unimodular,
\mathbb{Z}_{U}

is continuously group isomorphic to a torus. In
Section
5
, we show that the condition that
U=U_{\beta}

preserves zeros is equivalent to the condition that the
\beta
-numeration
satisfy Condition F. Throughout we are careful to isolate the conditions
that we need.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

In this paper, we consider sequences
g=(g_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
over an alphabet
contained in
\mathbb{Z}
and containing 0. If
0\leqslant m\leqslant n
are two non-negative
integers and
g
is a sequence then define
g_{[m:n]}\vcentcolon=(g_{k})_{k=m}^{n}
and

g_{[m:n)}\vcentcolon=(g_{k})_{k=m}^{n-1}
. These are called
factors
of
g
.
For any
n\geqslant 0
,
g_{[0:n]}
is a
head
of
g
and
g_{[n:\infty)}
is a

tail
of
g
. A sequence is
finite
, if it has a tail of
zeros, i.e.
g_{n}=0
for all
n
large. We will consider both left-infinite
sequences
\cdots g_{2}g_{1}g_{0}
and right-infinite sequences
g_{1}g_{2}g_{3}\cdots
. We
will sometimes use the radix point “
\cdot
” to clarify the context, e.g.,

g\cdot
represents a left-infinite sequence. When we want to emphasise that
there is no tail of zeros, we call the sequence a
word
. Let

\overbracket{w}
denote a one-sided infinite sequence obtained by repeating the
word
w
, so that, eg, a finite sequence is
\overbracket{0}g_{n}\cdots g_{0}
. As our
words will represent expansions of numbers, then, as in integral base
arithmetic, we will often identify a finite word
w
with any word
which has
w
as a prefix, followed by any number of zeros, including an
infinite tail of zeros. An exception is when we concatenate words, as then
inserting zeros affects the resulting word.

Given
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\bar{d}
denote the integer
-d
. We also use the bar
to denote complex conjugates of numbers, but this latter notation will not
be confusing as the different usages are disjoint. The set of finite
sequences on the alphabet
D
is denoted
D^{*}
. The
length
of

w=(d_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is
n
and is denoted
|w|
.

2.2.
Pisot numerations

We will work with numeration systems
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
with
u_{0}=1
, where

(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is a sequence of natural numbers, and where each natural
number can be represented using finite sequences of
digits
from a
finite set
D\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, i.e., for each
n
there is a natural number
k
and

d_{k},\ldots,d_{0}
from
D
such that
n=\sum_{i=0}^{k}d_{i}u_{i}
. We use the
convention that if some
u_{n}=0
, then
d_{n}=0
. In this paper, we only
consider numerations
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
which satisfy a linear recurrence.

Furthermore, we only consider Pisot numerations, defined as follows.
Recall that
\beta>1
is a Pisot number if it is algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}
and if all
its Galois conjugates have modulus strictly less than one; for an
exposition see
[
Par60
]
. Let

P(X)=X^{d}-a_{d-1}X^{d-1}-\cdots-a_{1}X-a_{0}
be irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
and have a
leading root
\beta
which is Pisot. We say that the sequence of natural
numbers
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is a
Pisot
numeration system (of degree

d
) if it is defined by initial conditions
u_{0},u_{-1},\ldots,u_{-d+1}

and if for some
n\geqslant 1
and integers
a_{0},\ldots,a_{d-1}
,

u_{n}=a_{d-1}u_{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}u_{n-d+1}+a_{0}u_{n-d}.

For convenience we give the initial conditions with non-positive indices.
In this paper, unless otherwise indicated, we will generate
(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}

using the initial conditions

u_{-1}=u_{-2}=\cdots=u_{-d+1}=0\text{ and }u_{0}=1.

With these initial conditions we call the numeration
standard
. Some
of our results are specific to these initial conditions; in places where
this is the case, we will indicate this explicitly. Since
P
is
irreducible, it is also sometimes called the minimal polynomial for the
recurrence. If we need to specify the leading root
\beta
, we will say that

U
is associated to
\beta
. We will not require that the coefficients
a_{i}

are nonnegative, but we will require that the sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}

generated by the recurrence be strictly positive. For such Pisot
numerations, the sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is strictly increasing for large

n
; see Lemma
14
.

For these numeration systems, every natural number
n
has a

normalised
expansion, which is the greatest expansion for the
lexicographic order, and which is obtained using the greedy algorithm. Note
that for
U
-numerations, the least significant digit is the rightmost
digit, i.e., we write these normalised expansions extending to the left,

(n)_{U}\vcentcolon=d_{k}\cdots d_{0}
, where
d_{k}\neq 0
is the most significant digit
in the normalised expansion
(n)_{U}
of
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Despite the
definition of
(n)_{U}
, we are permitted to sometimes pad
(n)_{U}
with
leading zeros, eg, when we have to compare the expansions of several
integers at a time. Note that a finite sequence
d_{k}\cdots d_{0}
over
\mathbb{N}
is a
normalised expansion of a natural number if and only if

\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}d_{i}u_{i}<u_{\ell+1}
for each
\ell\leqslant k
. We call such a finite sequence

U
-normalised
.

Under the hypothesis that the ratio
u_{n+1}/u_{n}
is bounded by a constant
for all
n
, which is the case for Pisot numerations, see, eg,
Lemma
14
, the digits of the normalised expansion of any
integer are bounded and contained in a canonical digit set
D=D_{U}
. This is
the minimal set of nonnegative digits satisfying
(n)_{U}\in D^{*}
for each

n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Conversely, given a finite sequence
g=g_{n}\cdots g_{0}
on any finite digit set,
we use
[g]_{U}
to denote the natural number that has
g
as a (possibly
non-normalised) expansion in that system, i.e.,
[g]_{U}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}g_{i}u_{i}
. We will say that
g
is an expansion of
n
. Note that the map

g\mapsto[g]_{U}
can be applied to any finite sequence over
\mathbb{Z}
. If

g=g_{n}\cdots g_{0}
, we will sometimes use the radix point and write
g\cdot
to
indicate the location of the least significant digit. The radix point is
not needed when the indexing of the digits in
g
is explicit. Thorough
descriptions of the properties of addition in these numeration systems can be
found in Frougny’s exposition
[
Lot02
, Chapter 7]
.

Example 2

(The
k
-bonacci numerations)
.

The most well-known Pisot numerations are the
k
-bonacci numerations
defined to be the numbers which satisfy the recurrence

u_{n}=u_{n-1}+u_{n-2}+\cdots+u_{n-k}.

The
k
-bonacci numbers satisfy the recurrence that has

X^{k}-X^{k-1}-\cdots-X-1
as minimal polynomial, which is known to be Pisot

[
Bra51
]
. Note that, with standard initial conditions, we have

u_{1}=u_{0}=1
, so the least significant digit for any
(n)_{U}
equals
0
.
In the
k
-bonacci numeration, each natural number has a unique normalised
expansion as
n=\sum_{i=0}^{k}d_{i}u_{i}
where
d_{0}=0
,
d_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and

d_{i}d_{i+1}\cdots d_{i+k-1}=0
for
i\geqslant 0
. If
k=2
, the numeration
U
is
the sequence of Fibonacci numbers
\ldots 5,3,2,1,1\cdot 0
; this numeration
is also known as the Zeckendorf, or Fibonacci numeration. If
k=3
, the
numeration
U=\ldots 14,8,4,2,1,1\cdot 0,0
is known as the Tribonacci
numeration. If
k=4
,
U
is known as the Tetrabonacci numeration.

2.2.1.
Normalisation for Pisot numerations

Thorough descriptions of the properties of addition in Pisot numeration
systems can be found in Frougny’s exposition

[
Lot02
, Chapter 7]
. One important concept on which we base our work
is that of
normalisation
. This consists of converting an arbitrary
expansion of an integer into the normalised expansion. For base-
k

expansions,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, this simply translates to addition with carry. For
more exotic numerations, there is often no simple rule. Pisot numeration
systems are somewhat well behaved in this respect.

The following theorem is proved in
[
Lot02
, Proposition 7.3.11,
Chapter 7]
. Recall that a set of finite sequences
K
is

regular
if there is a deterministic automaton

\langle S,D,\Delta,\{s_{0}\},F\rangle
such that
\Delta(s_{0},g)
is in an accepting
state if and only if
g\in K
, with
g
possibly padded with leading zeros
if needed.

Theorem 3
.

[
Lot02
, Chapter 7]

Let
U
be a Pisot numeration system. For any finite set
B\subset\mathbb{Z}
, the set

\{g\in B^{*}:[g]_{U}=0\}
is regular.

Definition 4
.

Let
U
be a numeration defined by a Pisot linear recurrence. Let
the finite sequence
g
have entries in
\mathbb{Z}
and suppose
[g]_{U}\geqslant 0
. Define

\operatorname{nor}_{U}(g)\vcentcolon=([g]_{U})_{U}

to be the normalised expansion for the natural number
[g]_{U}
.

Example 5
.

If
U
is the Fibonacci numeration, and if
g=1112\cdot
, then

[1112\cdot]_{U}=1\times 3+1\times 2+1\times 1+2\times 1=8
and

\operatorname{nor}_{U}(1112\cdot)=(8)_{U}=100000\cdot
.

3.
The
U
-adic integers

In this section, we introduce the space of
U
-adic integers. This space
is obtained as a quotient group
\mathbb{G}/\mathbb{H}_{U}
where the abelian group
\mathbb{G}
does
not depend on the numeration
U
and
\mathbb{H}_{U}
is the subgroup of all possible
expansions of
0
. For a sequence
g=(g_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
, set

\|g\|_{\infty}\vcentcolon=\sup\{g_{n}:n\geqslant 0\}
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be the set of bounded sequences of integers, that is,

\mathbb{G}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}\cap\ell^{\infty}=\{g=(g_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}:\|g\|_{\infty}<\infty\}.

The set
\mathbb{G}
is endowed with a commutative group structure, inherited from

\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
, of component-wise addition, i.e.,
g+g^{\prime}
is the sequence defined by

(g+g^{\prime})_{n}=g_{n}+g^{\prime}_{n}
. The sequence
g-g^{\prime}
is defined similarly. Each
finite sequence over
\mathbb{Z}
is identified with the element of
\mathbb{G}
obtained by
appending a tail of zeros. With this operation of component-wise addition,

\mathbb{G}
is an abelian group. We will not use the natural topology on
\mathbb{G}
, which
is that points are close if they agree on a large initial head. Rather,
we introduce the appropriate topology for our setting in
Section
4.1
.

We now come to the definition of the subgroup
\mathbb{H}_{U}
of
\mathbb{G}
. It is obtained
first by defining a function
\operatorname{ord}
on
\mathbb{G}
and second a

pseudo-valuation
ν_{U}
. Define the functions

\operatorname{ord}:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\deg:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{-\infty\}
by

\operatorname{ord}(g)\vcentcolon=\inf\{n:g_{n}\neq 0\}\quad\text{and}\quad\deg(g)\vcentcolon=\sup\{n:g_{n}\neq 0\}.

with the convention that
\inf\varnothing=\infty
and
\sup\varnothing=-\infty
and thus

\operatorname{ord}(\overbracket{0})=\infty
and
\deg(\overbracket{0})=-\infty
. The function
\operatorname{ord}

satisfies
\operatorname{ord}(g+g^{\prime})\geqslant\min\{\operatorname{ord}(g),\operatorname{ord}(g^{\prime})\}
. In
Section
4.2.1
, we extend the functions
\operatorname{ord}
and
\deg
to
two-sided infinite sequences.

Note that
\mathbb{G}
and
\operatorname{ord}
are universally defined and do not depend on any
numeration. We now define the subgroup
\mathbb{H}_{U}
by introducing the
numeration
U
. We will see in Section
4.1
that this
numeration also defines the appropriate topology on
\mathbb{G}
, turning it into a
topological group. Define the function
ν_{U}:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
by

ν_{U}(n)\vcentcolon=\operatorname{ord}((|n|)_{U}).

Note that with this definition,
ν_{U}(0)=\infty
. The function
ν_{U}

does not enjoy all the nice properties of the
p
-valuation; in particular
it is not non-Archimedean. Note that, while we have defined
ν_{U}
on the
integers, its definition can be extended to act on finite sequences
g
, namely,

Corollary 9

Let

Lemma 10
.

Let
U
be a numeration that preserves zeros and let

. Then,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}ν_{U}(g_{[0:n)})=\infty\iff\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}ν_{U}(g_{[0:n)})=\infty

Proof.

One implication is clear. To prove the other, we start with the following
remark. By definition of
\mathbb{G}
, if
g\in\mathbb{G}
then there exists a
constant
c
such that
|g_{n}|\leqslant c
for each
n
.
Since
U
preserves zeros, there exists an integer
K_{c}
such that

ν_{U}(w)\geqslant\operatorname{ord}(w)-K_{c}
for each factor

of

. We claim that if

n\geqslant m\geqslant 0
, then

ν_{U}(g_{[0:n)})\geqslant\min(m,ν_{U}(g_{[0:m)})-K_{c}-K_{2d}.

Let
M=\min(m,ν_{U}(g_{[0:m)}))
and let
u=g_{[0:m)}
,

and
w=v-u
, where the symbol

denotes the
component-wise subtraction of finite sequences. Then by definition of

ν_{U}(g_{[0:m)})\geqslant M\geqslant M-K_{c}
, and the finite sequence

, so that

. It follows
from the relation

Now suppose that

∎

3.1.
Normalised elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{U}

In this section, we show that each class of
\mathbb{Z}_{U}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{G}/\mathbb{H}_{U}
contains
a
U
-normalised sequence.

Notation
.

Given a numeration
U
, set

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{U}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\{(n)_{U}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}

(1)

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\overline{\mathbb{N}}_{U}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\{g\in\mathbb{G}:g[0:n)\in\mathbb{N}_{U}\text{ for each $n\geqslant 0$}\}.

We use this notation because
\overline{\mathbb{N}}_{U}
is the topological closure
of
\mathbb{N}_{U}
, not just in the natural topology for
\mathbb{G}
, but also for the
topology that we introduce in Section
4.1
. We say that

g\in\mathbb{G}
is
U
-normalised
if
g\in\overline{\mathbb{N}}_{U}
.

Note that
\overline{\mathbb{N}}_{U}
is
not
invariant under shifting. For
example, if
U
is the Fibonacci numeration with standard initial
conditions, then normalised sequences always start with
0
, and shifting
can destroy this.

In this section we will show that, for numerations arising from a Pisot
numeration that preserves zeros, we can always find representatives of
elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
that are
U
-normalised. For this we recall some basic
notions around
\beta
-numerations defined by a leading root of a Pisot
polynomial
P
.

Let
\beta>1
be a real number. A
\beta
-expansion
of a non-negative
real number
x
is a sequence
(d_{k})_{k\leqslant n}
of integers such that

x=\sum_{k\leqslant n}d_{k}\beta^{k}
. Note that the integer
n
might be negative if

x<1
. The
normalised
\beta
-expansion
is the lexicographically
greatest
\beta
-expansion. It is obtained by the greedy algorithm and it is
characterized by the fact that

(2)

\sum_{i\leqslant k}d_{i}\beta^{i}<\beta^{k+1}

for each
k\leqslant n
. A sequence satisfying
Condition (
2
) is called
\beta
-normalised
.

A related expansion for
x\in[0,1]
is the Rényi expansion, defined using
the map
T_{\beta}(x)=\beta x\bmod 1
. If
0\leqslant x<1
and
x=\sum_{k\leqslant n}d_{k}\beta^{k}
,
then
d_{k}
equals the integer part of
\beta T_{\beta}^{k-1}(x)
. The
\beta
-expansion
of
x=1
, which is just
\beta^{0}
, differs from its Rényi expansion , which
we write as
d_{\beta}(1)=t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}\cdots
. Note that we use
t_{k}
and not
d_{k}

for
d_{\beta}(1)
due to its ubiquitous use.

Denote by
D_{\beta}
the set of
\beta
-expansions of
x\in[0,1)
, and denote the
shift-orbit closure of
D_{\beta}
by
S_{\beta}
. Let
\sigma
denote the (one-sided) shift
map. Let
<_{\text{lex}}
denote the lexicographical order. If
d_{\beta}(1)
is
infinite, set
d_{\beta}^{*}(1)\vcentcolon=d_{\beta}(1)
. Otherwise if
d_{\beta}(1)=t_{1}\cdots t_{m}
with

t_{m}\neq 0
, set
d_{\beta}^{*}(1)\vcentcolon=\overbracket{t_{1}\cdots t_{m-1}(t_{m}-1)}
. Note that all
suffixes of
d_{\beta}(1)
are lexicographically smaller than
d_{\beta}(1)

[
Lot02
, Chapter 7]
.

Example 12

(The
k
-bonacci numerations)
.

Recall the numerations from Example
2
, which satisfy the
recurrence that has
X^{k}-X^{k-1}-\cdots-X-1
as minimal polynomial. In this

We record the following fundamental result

[
Par60
,
Ber77
,
Sch80
]
.

Theorem 13
.

We have
x\in S_{\beta}
if and only if for each

,

\sigma^{p}(x)<_{\text{lex}}d_{\beta}^{*}(1)
. If
\beta
is Pisot, then

is eventually
periodic and

is sofic.

The following lemma is classical. It tells us that the
n
-th term of a
Pisot numeration grows like
\beta^{n}
with an exponentially decaying
difference. We include the proof for completeness.

Lemma 14
.

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a Pisot numeration associated to
\beta
. Then
there are real numbers
0<{\gamma}<1
,
\lambda>0
and
K>0
such that

|u_{n}-\lambda\beta^{n}|\leqslant K{\gamma}^{n}
for each
n\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

We let
\alpha_{0}=\beta
and let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d-1}
be the algebraic
conjugates of
\beta
. Since the annihilating polynomial of
\beta
is irreducible, a
general Binet formula tells us that there exist coefficients

(\lambda_{k})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant d-1}
such that
u_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\lambda_{k}\alpha_{k}^{n}
for each

n\geqslant 0
. Thus setting
{\gamma}\vcentcolon=\max_{k}\{|\alpha_{k}|:1\leqslant k\leqslant d-1\}<1
and

K=\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|\lambda_{k}|
,

\left|u_{n}-\lambda_{0}\beta^{n}\right|\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|\lambda_{k}\alpha_{k}^{n}|\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|\lambda_{k}|{\gamma}^{n}\leqslant K{\gamma}^{n}

Note that

\lambda\vcentcolon=\lambda_{0}>0
since
\beta>1
and the
u_{n}

are eventually positive.
∎

We prove in Section
5
that, for a numeration
U

associated to a Pisot number

equivalent to the Condition F for
\beta

preserves zeros, then

Lemma 15

Let
U
be a Pisot numeration associated to
\beta
. If

preserves zeros, then

is finite.

Proof.

If
d_{\beta}(1)
is not finite, then by Theorem
13

is ultimately periodic; set
d_{\beta}(1)=t_{1}\cdots t_{\ell}\overbracket{t_{\ell+1}\cdots t_{m}}
.
Consider the finite sequence
g=\overbracket{0}1(-t_{1})\cdots(-t_{\ell})0^{k}

Using Lemma

does not preserve

∎

Lemma 16

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a Pisot numeration that preserves zeros. Then there
exists
h=(h_{n})\in\mathbb{H}_{U}
with

for each

Proof.

Let
P
be the minimal polynomial of the
recurrence defining

and let
\beta
be the leading root.
Lemma

tells us that

is finite,
where
t_{1}>0
and
t_{m}>0
. Necessarily,

u_{n+m}=t_{1}u_{n+m-1}+\cdots+t_{m}u_{n}
. If

and

then
(u_{n})_{U}=10^{qm+r}
, so we also have

[(t_{1}\cdots t_{m-1}(t_{m}-1))^{q-1}t_{1}\cdots t_{m}0^{r}]_{U}=u_{n}.

This implies that

h^{(r)}=\overbracket{t_{1}\cdots t_{m-1}(t_{m}-1)}t_{1}\cdots t_{m}0^{r}\in\mathbb{H}_{U}

∎

Proposition 18

Let

By Lemma

with

\max D

Since for each

to be a limit point of the sequence

(\operatorname{nor}_{U}(g_{[0,n]}))_{n}

It remains to show that

\max D
, and since each entry

is at most
\max D
,

has non-negative entries. This, with

\displaystyle[(g-g^{\prime})_{[0,N_{\ell}]}]_{U}

\displaystyle=[g_{[0,N_{\ell}]}]_{U}-[g^{\prime}_{[0,N_{\ell}]}]_{U}

\displaystyle=[\operatorname{nor}_{U}(g_{[0,N_{\ell}]})]_{U}-[g^{\prime}_{[0,N_{\ell}]}]_{U}

. Note that equality is excluded
by hypothesis.

In the first case
[g^{\prime\prime}]_{U}>[g]_{U}
, we claim that the stronger
inequality
ν_{U}(g^{\prime}-g)\leqslant d
holds. Let
w
be the sequence

\operatorname{nor}_{U}(g^{\prime\prime}-g)
; it has length at most
d
. If needed, we pad
w
with
final zeros so that it has length
d
. It is straightforward to check
that
\operatorname{nor}_{U}(g^{\prime}-g)=g^{\prime}_{d^{\prime}}\cdots g^{\prime}_{d+1}w
, and the claim follows.

We now consider the second case
[g^{\prime\prime}]_{U}<[g]_{U}
. We have set
d^{*}
to
be the maximum of the digit set
D
. Let

k\vcentcolon=d+1+\operatorname{ord}(g^{\prime}_{d^{\prime}}\cdots g^{\prime}_{d+1})
. By definition of
k
,
g^{\prime}_{k}\neq 0
and

g^{\prime}_{i}=0
for
d+1\leqslant i<k
. We first suppose that
k\leqslant d+K_{2d^{*}}
.
Now
[g^{\prime}_{[0:k]}-g]_{U}>0
since
g
is normalised. Let

w\vcentcolon=\operatorname{nor}_{U}(g^{\prime}_{[0:k]}-g)
; the word
w
satisfies

|w|\leqslant k+1\leqslant d+1+K_{2d^{*}}
. It follows that

ν_{U}(w)\leqslant d+K_{2d^{*}}
. Since
g^{\prime}
was
U
-normalised,

ν_{U}(g^{\prime}-g)\leqslant d+K_{2d^{*}}
because
\operatorname{nor}_{U}(g^{\prime}-g)_{[0:|w|)}=w
.

Finally suppose that
k>d+K_{2d^{*}}
. This means that

ν_{U}(g^{\prime}_{d^{\prime}}\cdots g^{\prime}_{d+1}0^{d+1})>d+K_{2d^{*}}
. We have

[g-g^{\prime\prime}]_{U}=[g^{\prime}_{d^{\prime}}\cdots g^{\prime}_{d+1}0^{d+1}]_{U}-[\operatorname{nor}_{U}(g^{\prime}-g)]_{U}
. Now

ν_{U}(g-g^{\prime\prime})\leq d.
Suppose by contradiction that

ν_{U}(g^{\prime}-g)\geq d+K_{2d^{*}}
. Then we have written
[g-g^{\prime\prime}]_{U}
as a
difference of two elements, each of whose valuation is strictly greater
than
d+K_{2d^{*}}
. By the definition of
K_{2d^{*}}
, we have

ν_{U}(g-g^{\prime\prime})>d+K_{2d^{*}}-K_{2d^{*}}=d
, and this is a contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
20
.

We claim that the constant
K=3\max(K_{c^{\prime}},C,K_{2d})
where
K_{c^{\prime}}
,

C
and
K_{2d}
are the constants provided by Lemmas

21
,
22
and
23
satisfies
the required property. We consider a
U
-normalised sequence

g=(g_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
satisfying the hypothesis of the theorem. By
Proposition
18
, there is a
U
-normalised sequence

g^{\prime}=(g^{\prime}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
such that
h=g^{\prime}-g\in\mathbb{H}_{U}
. Since
g
and
g^{\prime}

are
U
-normalised, the sequence
h
satisfies
\|h\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2\max D
where

D
is the canonical digit set of the numeration
U
. The statement of
Theorem
20
holds trivially if the sequence
g
has
finitely many non-zero entries. Indeed, since both
g
and
g^{\prime}
are

U
-normalised,
g=g^{\prime}=(n)_{U}
where
n=[g]_{U}
. Therefore, we can
assume that
g_{n}\neq 0
infinitely often. The statement also holds
trivially if
[g^{\prime}_{[0:n)}]_{U}=[g_{[0:n)}]_{U}
holds for infinitely
many
n
since
g
and
g^{\prime}
are
U
-normalised. Therefore, we can assume
that
[g^{\prime}_{[0:n)}]_{U}=[g_{[0:n)}]_{U}
holds for finitely many
integers
n
. Lemma
22
now implies that the sequence
h

has finitely many blocks of zeros of length at least
K_{c^{\prime}}
. Hence
there exists a large enough integer
N
such that if

[g^{\prime}_{[0:n)}]_{U}=[g_{[0:n)}]_{U}
then
n<N
. By hypothesis, there
exists an integer
n
such that
n\geqslant N
,
g_{n-1}\neq 0
and

g_{[n,n+K)}=0^{K}
. By definition of
N
, all occurrences of
0^{C}

in
h
are contained in its prefix
h_{[0:N)}
. Since
K\geqslant 3C
, there
exists two integers
k
and
\ell
such that
n\leqslant k<n+K/3
,

h_{k}=g^{\prime}_{k}\neq 0
,
n+2K/3\leqslant\ell<n+K
and
h_{\ell}=g^{\prime}_{\ell}\neq 0
. By
Lemma
23
,

ν_{U}(h_{[0:\ell]})=ν_{U}(g^{\prime}_{[0:\ell]}-g_{[0:n)})\leqslant n+K_{2d}<\ell-K_{c^{\prime}}
. This contradicts Lemma
21
.
∎

4.
From
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
to a torus

So far we have just considered
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
as groups, but we can
endow each of them with a topology, as we describe in
Section
4.1
. In this section we show that there is a
continuous homomorphism from
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
to a torus. We then show that if
\beta

is unimodular, then
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
is continuously isomorphic to a torus.

Let
U
be a Pisot numeration of degree
d
whose minimal polynomial
has
\ell+1
real roots and
2k
complex roots. Denoting the leading root of
the polynomial by
\beta
, we label its algebraic conjugates

(4)

\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2k},\alpha_{2k+1},\ldots,\alpha_{d-1}.

The
2k
complex Galois conjugates are
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2k}
where

\alpha_{k+i}=\overline{\alpha}_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
while there are
\ell\vcentcolon=d-1-2k

real conjugates
\alpha_{2k+1},\ldots,\alpha_{d-1}
. When needed, we will set
\alpha_{0}=\beta
.

We consider
\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
as an
\mathbb{R}
-algebra with component-wise operations.
Let us define the vector
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
by

(5)

{\bm{\alpha}}\vcentcolon=(\alpha_{k+1},\ldots,\alpha_{d-1})\quad\text{and}\quad{\gamma}\vcentcolon=\|{\bm{\alpha}}\|_{\infty};

note that
{\gamma}<1
because
\beta
is assumed to be a Pisot number.
Note that the conjugates
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
have been removed. By definition,

{\bm{\alpha}}^{i}=(\alpha_{k+1}^{i},\ldots,\alpha_{d-1}^{i})
.

Example 25
.

Recall the
k
-bonacci numerations from Example
2
. If

U
is the Fibonacci numeration, then
k=0
,
\ell=1
and the vector

{\bm{\alpha}}
is the vector
(\alpha_{1})\in\mathbb{R}
where
\alpha_{1}=(1-\sqrt{5})/2
, the
conjugate of the golden ratio.

If
U
is the Tribonacci numeration, then
k=1
,
\ell=0
and the
vector
{\bm{\alpha}}
is the vector
(\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{C}
where

\alpha_{0}=\beta,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}=\overline{\alpha}_{1}
are the roots of the polynomial

X^{3}-X^{2}-X-1
.

If
U
is the Tetrabonacci numeration, then
k=\ell=1
and the
vector
{\bm{\alpha}}
is the vector
(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}
where

\alpha_{0}=\beta,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}=\overline{\alpha}_{1},\alpha_{3}
are the roots of the polynomial

X^{4}-X^{3}-X^{2}-X-1
.

Define the group homomorphism
\tilde{\Phi}:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
by

(6)

\tilde{\Phi}((g_{n})_{n\geqslant 0})\vcentcolon=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}g_{n}{\bm{\alpha}}^{n+d-1}.

Note that
\tilde{\Phi}
is well defined as
{\gamma}<1
and the entries in

(g_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
are bounded. Moreover, we have the following trivial
bound:

(7)

\|\tilde{\Phi}(g)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|g\|_{\infty}{\gamma}^{\operatorname{ord}(g)+d-1}/(1-{\gamma}).

Define the lattice
\mathbb{L}\subset\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
to be the set of
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combinations of the vectors
{\bm{\alpha}}^{i}
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant d-2
, i.e.,

\mathbb{L}\vcentcolon=\{k_{0}{\bm{1}}+k_{1}{\bm{\alpha}}+\cdots+k_{d-2}{\bm{\alpha}}^{d-2}:k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\},

where
{\bm{1}}
is the vector
{\bm{\alpha}}^{0}=(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. The
following lemma states that the lattice
\mathbb{L}
has the same dimension as

\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, which ensures that the points in
\mathbb{L}
are isolated.

Lemma 26
.

The
\mathbb{R}
-vector space spanned by
\mathbb{L}
is
\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and the quotient

(\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell})/\mathbb{L}
is thus isomorphic to the torus

\mathbb{R}^{2k+\ell}/\mathbb{Z}^{2k+\ell}=\mathbb{T}^{2k+\ell}
.

Note that it is important to consider
\mathbb{R}
-vector spaces as we want
\mathbb{C}^{k}
to
be isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2k}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that the
d-1
vectors

{\bm{1}},{\bm{\alpha}},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}^{d-2}
are
\mathbb{R}
-linearly independent.
Suppose by contradiction that there are
d-1
real numbers

r_{0},\ldots,r_{d-2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}r_{i}{\bm{\alpha}}^{i}=0
. This
equality means that the
d-k
conjugates
\alpha_{k+1},\ldots,\alpha_{d-1}
are roots of
the polynomial
Q(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}r_{i}X^{i}
. Since the coefficients
of
Q(X)
are real numbers, the conjugates
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
are also roots
of
Q(X)
. Since
Q(X)
has degree at most
d-2
and has at least
d-1

roots, each
r_{i}=0
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant d-2
.
∎

The following proposition shows that the function
\tilde{\Phi}:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}

induces a function from
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
to
(\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell})/\mathbb{L}
.
It is in this proposition that we first need the initial conditions
of the numeration
U
to be standard.

Proposition 27
.

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a standard Pisot numeration that preserves
zeros, and let the function
\tilde{\Phi}
be as defined in (
6
).
We have
\tilde{\Phi}(\mathbb{H}_{U})\subset\mathbb{L}
and
\tilde{\Phi}
induces a
function
\Phi:\mathbb{Z}_{U}\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell})/\mathbb{L}
.

Note that Messaoudi
[
Mes98
]
has explicitly computed an
automaton which outputs classes with more than one
U
-normalised element
for the Tribonacci
\beta
-numeration. More precisely, he computed an automaton
accepting pairs of
U
-normalised sequences being mapped to the same point
by
\tilde{\Phi}
, that is, their difference is mapped to
0
by
\tilde{\Phi}
. Being
mapped to the same value by
\tilde{\Phi}
is not the same as being in the same
class of
\mathbb{Z}_{U}
, as Proposition
27
tells us that their
difference is mapped to the lattice
\mathbb{L}
.

In order to prove Proposition
27
, we need the following
results which establish a link between the function
\tilde{\Phi}
and the
lattice
\mathbb{L}
. Note that in Lemma
28
,
Proposition
29
and Corollary
30
it is
crucial that we take standard initial conditions to define
U
. The
intuition behind the proof is that, given a finite sequence
g
, we
re-write it as a finite sequence
\widetilde{g}
of length
d
, with
support in the entries with indices
0,-1,\ldots,1-d
, so that

[g]_{U}=[\widetilde{g}]_{U}
. With these initial conditions, the
U
-value of

g
is determined by the entry
\widetilde{g}_{0}
. Thus, if

[g]_{U}=[g^{\prime}]_{U}
, and
u_{0}=1
then we must have

\widetilde{g}_{0}=[g]_{U}=[g^{\prime}]_{U}=\widetilde{g}^{\prime}_{0}
, which is what leads to

\tilde{\Phi}(g-g^{\prime})\in\mathbb{L}
.

Lemma 28
.

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a standard Pisot numeration, associated to

P(X)=X^{d}-a_{d-1}X^{d-1}-a_{d-2}X^{d-2}-\cdots-a_{1}X-a_{0}
and

\alpha\in\{\alpha_{i}:0\leqslant i\leqslant d-1\}
be any one of its
roots. For
n\geqslant d
,

\alpha^{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}V_{i}^{(n)}\alpha^{i}\qquad\text{where}\qquad V_{i}^{(n)}=\sum_{m=0}^{i}a_{i-m}u_{n-d-m}\text{ for }0\leqslant i\leqslant d-1.

Note that this implies

V_{d-1}^{(n)}=\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}a_{d-1-m}u_{n-d-m}=u_{n-d+1}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
.

Base case

Consider
n=d
, since
P(\alpha)=0
, we have

\alpha^{d}=a_{d-1}\alpha^{d-1}+a_{d-2}\alpha^{d-2}+\cdots+a_{0}
and also
u_{0}=1

and
u_{-1}=u_{-2}=\cdots=0
by definition. Therefore, for each

i\in[0,d-1]
we can explicitly write
V_{i}^{(d)}=a_{i}
and so

\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}V_{i}^{(n)}\alpha^{i}=a_{d-1}\alpha^{d-1}+a_{d-2}\alpha^{d-2}+\cdots+a_{0}=\alpha^{n}
holds for
n=d
.

Induction step

For some
n\geqslant d
, assume that

\alpha^{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}V_{i}^{(n)}\alpha^{i}
where

V_{i}^{(n)}=\sum_{m=0}^{i}a_{i-m}u_{n-d-m}
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant d-1
. Now for

\alpha^{n+1}
, using the induction hypothesis and

P(\alpha)=\alpha^{d}-\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}a_{i}\alpha^{i}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}V_{i}^{(n)}\alpha^{i+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}V_{i}^{(n)}\alpha^{i+1}+V_{d-1}^{(n)}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}a_{i}\alpha^{i}-\alpha^{d}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{0}V_{d-1}^{(n)}+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}(a_{i}V_{d-1}^{(n)}+V_{i-1}^{(n)})\alpha^{i}

where
V_{d-1}^{(n)}=\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}a_{d-1-m}u_{n-d-m}=u_{n+1-d}
from
the recurrence relation for
U
. But from the definitions we see that

a_{0}V_{d-1}^{(n)}=a_{0}u_{n+1-d}=V_{0}^{(n+1)}
, and for

1\leqslant i\leqslant d-1
, and

a_{i}V_{d-1}^{(n)}+V_{i-1}^{(n)}=a_{i}u_{n+1-d}+\sum_{m=0}^{i-1}a_{i-1-m}u_{n-d-m}=V_{i}^{(n+1)},

and therefore
\alpha^{n+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}V_{i}^{(n+1)}\alpha^{i}
, from which the
result follows.
∎

Proposition 29
.

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a standard Pisot numeration, associated to

P(X)=X^{d}-a_{d-1}X^{d-1}-a_{d-2}X^{d-2}-\cdots-a_{1}X-a_{0}
. For each
finite sequence
g=g_{n}\cdots g_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}
and each of the roots in

\alpha\in\{\alpha_{i}:0\leqslant i\leqslant d-1\}
,

\sum_{k=0}^{n}g_{k}\alpha^{k+d-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}A_{k}^{(g)}\alpha^{k}

where each
A_{k}^{(g)}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
A_{d-1}^{(g)}=[g]_{U}
.

The proof of this proposition follows from
Lemma
28
and linearity.

Proof.

Using Lemma
28
we have

\alpha^{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}V_{i}^{(n)}\alpha^{i}
and so

\sum_{k=0}^{n}g_{k}\alpha^{k+d-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}V_{i}^{(k+d-1)}\alpha^{i}\right)g_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}g_{k}V_{i}^{(k+d-1)}\right)\alpha^{i}.

But
V_{d-1}^{(\ell)}=u_{\ell+1-d}
, so

\sum_{i=0}^{n}g_{i}V_{d-1}^{(d-1+i)}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}g_{i}u_{i}=[g]_{U}
. Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}g_{k}\alpha^{k+d-1}

\displaystyle=[g]_{U}\alpha^{d-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}g_{j}V_{i}^{(j+d-1)}\right)\alpha^{i}

\displaystyle=[g]_{U}\alpha^{d-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}A_{i}^{(g)}\alpha^{i}

with
A_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
i\in[0,d-2]
.
∎

Recalling the definition of
\mathbb{L}
, we immediately have the following corollary.

Corollary 30
.

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a standard Pisot numeration. If
w
and
w^{\prime}

are two finite sequences over
\mathbb{Z}
such that
[w]_{U}=[w^{\prime}]_{U}
, then

\tilde{\Phi}(w)-\tilde{\Phi}(w^{\prime})\in\mathbb{L}
.

Proof of Proposition
27
.

Recall
(n)_{U}\in\{0,\ldots,\max D\}^{*}
for each integer
n\geqslant 0
, where
D
is
the canonical digit set. Let
h=(h_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be an element of
\mathbb{H}_{U}
.
Let
c
be an integer such that
\|h\|_{\infty}\leqslant c
and let
\varepsilon>0
. We claim
that there exists a point
p\in\mathbb{L}
such that
\|\tilde{\Phi}(h)-p\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
. We
choose an integer
m
such that
2c{\gamma}^{m}<\varepsilon(1-{\gamma})
. By
definition of
\mathbb{H}_{U}
, there exists an integer
n
such that
n>m
and

ν_{U}(h_{[0:n)})>m
. Let
g=h_{[n:\infty)}
be the tail of

starting
at position

. Then

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}(h)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Phi}(h_{[0:n)})+{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}\tilde{\Phi}(g)

\displaystyle=p+\tilde{\Phi}(\operatorname{nor}_{U}(h_{[0:n)}))+{\bm{\alpha}}^{n}\tilde{\Phi}(g)

for some element

by Corollary

since
[\operatorname{nor}_{U}(h_{[0:n)})]_{U}=[h_{[0:n)}]_{U}
. But since

, we have

\|\tilde{\Phi}(h)-p\|_{\infty}\leqslant c({\gamma}^{m}+{\gamma}^{n})/(1-{\gamma})

The choice of

and

guarantees that

. Since
this is true for each

and

is made of isolated points,

is proved and the inclusion

∎

With
{\bm{\alpha}}

\rho:(\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell})/\mathbb{L}\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell})/\mathbb{L}

The following lemma generalises what Rauzy did in

Lemma 31
.

The map
\rho
has dense orbits in
(\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell})/\mathbb{L}
.

Proof.

Set
Q(X)\vcentcolon=P(X)/(X-\beta)
; then

Q(X)=X^{d-1}-c_{d-2}X^{d-2}-\cdots-c_{1}X-c_{0}
where

in

for

{\bm{\alpha}}^{d-1}=c_{0}{\bm{1}}+c_{1}{\bm{\alpha}}+\cdots+c_{d-2}{\bm{\alpha}}^{d-2}.

Then

n{\bm{\alpha}}^{d-1}\equiv(nc_{0}\bmod 1){\bm{1}}+(nc_{1}\bmod 1){\bm{\alpha}}+\cdots+(nc_{d-2}\bmod 1){\bm{\alpha}}^{d-2}\mod\mathbb{L}

+{\bm{\alpha}}^{d-1}

if and only if the orbit of
the map

\alpha_{0}&\alpha_{1}&\cdots&\alpha_{d-1}\\
\alpha_{0}^{2}&\alpha_{1}^{2}&\cdots&\alpha_{d-1}^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\alpha_{0}^{d-1}&\alpha_{1}^{d-1}&\cdots&\alpha_{d-1}^{d-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Since the Vandermonde matrix

5.
Condition F

In this section we show that our condition of preservation of zeros is
equivalent to the now classical Condition F. Recall that we used this to prove
Corollary

.

5.1.
From preservation of zeros to Condition F

The set of
U
-expansions of natural numbers for a Pisot numeration is
clearly related to the set of finite
\beta
-expansions for corresponding

\beta
-numeration; for example, one can mechanically normalise the same way
for both the
U
- and
\beta
-numeration. However one can run into issues close
to the radix point. For example, with our choice of initial conditions for
the Zeckendorf numeration, all normalised expansions start with
0
, which
is not the case for the
\beta
-numerations for
\beta=\varphi
. Nevertheless,
provided we are sufficiently far from the radix point, these sets of
expansions are the same.

Lemma 43
.

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a Pisot numeration, and let
g
be a finite
sequence. There exists an integer
N=N(U,\|g\|_{\infty})
such that if there
exists
n\geqslant N
such that
[g0^{n}]_{U}=0
, then there exist infinitely many

n\geqslant N
with
[g0^{n}]_{U}=0
.

Proof.

Let
B=\{-\|g\|_{\infty},\ldots,\|g\|_{\infty}\}
. By Theorem
3
,
there exists an automaton over the alphabet
B
accepting the set

\{w\in B^{*}:[w]_{U}=0\}
. Let
N=N(U,\|g\|_{\infty})
be the number of
states of this automaton. If
[g0^{n}]_{U}=0
, then the finite sequence

g0^{n}
is accepted by the automaton. Now if furthermore
n\geqslant N
, then
the pumping lemma gives the result.
∎

For a finite sequence
g=g_{k}\cdots g_{0}
, let
P_{g}
be the polynomial

P_{g}(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}g_{i}X^{i}
. Note that for finite sequences
g
and
g^{\prime}
,

P_{g+g^{\prime}}=P_{g}+P_{g^{\prime}}
.

Lemma 44
.

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a Pisot numeration with minimal
polynomial
P
, and let
g
be a finite sequence. Then there exists an
integer
N=N(U,\|g\|_{\infty})
such that if
\operatorname{ord}(g)\geqslant N
and
[g]_{U}=0
,
then
P
divides
P_{g}
.

Proof.

Let
g
be the finite sequence
g_{k}\cdots g_{0}
and recall that

\alpha_{0}=\beta,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d-1}
are the roots of
P
. There exist
coefficients
(\lambda_{i})_{0\leqslant i\leqslant d-1}
such that
u_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\lambda_{i}\alpha_{i}^{n}

for each
n\geqslant 0
. Recall the definition of
{\gamma}

from (
5
). We have

\displaystyle\left|[g0^{n}]_{U}-\lambda_{0}\beta^{n}P_{g}(\beta)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}g_{j}(u_{n+j}-\lambda_{0}\beta^{n+j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}g_{j}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\lambda_{i}\alpha_{i}^{n+j}-\lambda_{0}\beta^{n+j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}g_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{i}\alpha_{i}^{n+j}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{k}|g_{j}|\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|\lambda_{i}|{\gamma}^{n+j}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\gamma}^{n}\|g\|_{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}|\lambda_{i}|{\gamma}^{j}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{K{\gamma}^{n}}{1-{\gamma}}

where
K=(d-1)\|g\|_{\infty}\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d-1}|\lambda_{i}|
. By
Lemma
43
,
[g0^{n}]_{U}=0
for some sequence of
integers
n
. Since
K{\gamma}^{n}/(1-{\gamma})
converges to zero when

n\rightarrow\infty
, and
\beta>1
,
P_{g}(\beta)=0
. Since
P
is the minimal polynomial
of
\beta
,
P
divides
P_{g}
.
∎

We can now prove that if a Pisot numeration preserves zeros, then the
corresponding
\beta
-numeration satisfies the Condition F.
Recall that we defined
[g_{n}\cdots g_{0}]_{\beta}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}g_{i}\beta^{i}
in
Section
4.2.1
.

Proposition 45
.

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a Pisot numeration associated to
\beta
. If the
numeration
U
preserves zeros, then
\beta
satisfies the Condition F.

Proof.

Let
x=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\beta^{i}
be an element of
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
and let

c=\max\{|a_{i}|:0\leqslant i\leqslant k\}
. Let
K_{c}
be the constant guaranteed
by Definition
6
. Let
M
be an integer larger
than each of those guaranteed by Lemmas
38

and
44
. We consider the finite sequences

g=a_{k}\cdots a_{0}0^{M+K_{c}}
and

. Since
\operatorname{ord}(g)\geqslant M+K_{c}

and

,
\operatorname{ord}(g^{\prime})\geqslant M
. Since

and

\operatorname{ord}(g-g^{\prime})\geqslant M

by Lemma

\operatorname{ord}(g^{\prime})\geqslant M
and

is

-normalised, it is also

-normalised.
This shows that

.
∎

5.2.
From Condition F to preservation of zeros

The following lemma requires the additional assumption that
d_{\beta}(1)
is
finite, an assumption which is fulfilled as soon as
\beta
satisfies the
Condition F. It tells us that, away from the radix point, the factors of
elements of
\mathbb{N}_{U}
and the
\beta
-shift agree, so that the recurrence defined
by
d_{\beta}(1)
determines these languages.

The following lemma states that a finite sequence
g\in\{0,1\}^{*}
where
the 1’s are far enough apart is the normalised expansion of some integer,
that is, belongs to
\mathbb{N}_{U}
.

Lemma 46
.

Let
U=(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a Pisot numeration associated to
\beta
. There
exists an integer
K
such that for each finite
sequence
g\in\{0,1\}^{*}
, if
\operatorname{ord}(g)\geqslant K
and if for all

0\leqslant i<j<|g|
,

implies

, then
g=(n)_{U}
for
some integer

.

Proof.

By Lemma
14
, there exist constants
K^{\prime}
and
\lambda
such
that
|u_{n}-\lambda\beta^{n}|\leqslant K^{\prime}{\gamma}^{n}
. Let
K
be a constant to be
chosen later and let
g
be a finite sequence satisfying the hypotheses
of the lemma. In order to show that
g=(n)_{U}
for some integer

, it
suffices to show that for each integer
k>K
the relation

\sum_{i=0}^{k}g_{i}u_{i}<u_{k+1}
holds.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}g_{i}u_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/K\rfloor-1}u_{k-iK}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/K\rfloor-1}\lambda\beta^{k-iK}+K^{\prime}{\gamma}^{K}/(1-{\gamma}^{K})

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda(\beta^{k+2K}-\beta^{K})/(\beta^{K}-1)+K^{\prime}{\gamma}^{K}/(1-{\gamma}^{K})

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda\beta^{k}\beta^{K}/(\beta^{K}-1)+K^{\prime}{\gamma}^{K}/(1-{\gamma}^{K}).

Then we choose
K
large enough such that
\beta^{K}/(\beta^{K}-1)<(3+\beta)/4
and

K^{\prime}{\gamma}^{K}/(1-{\gamma}^{K})<\lambda\beta^{K}(\beta-1)/4
and

for each

. The first inequality is
possible because
\beta^{K}/(\beta^{K}-1)
converges to

and

. The
second inequality is possible because

converges to
0
while
\lambda\beta^{K}(\beta-1)/4
converges to

. The last
inequality is possible because

converges to
\lambda\beta
and

(1+\beta)/2<\beta
. Finally we get that for each

,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}g_{i}u_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda\beta^{k}(3+\beta)/4+\lambda\beta^{K}(\beta-1)/4

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda\beta^{k}(1+\beta)/2<u_{k+1}.

The result follows.
∎

The following lemma shows that each finite sequence can be decomposed as a
sum of normalised finite sequences where the number of components only
depends on the maximal value occurring in it.

Lemma 47
.

Let

The result is trivial if

are nonnegative. If

, let

be the integer provided by
