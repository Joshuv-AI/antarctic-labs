ABSTRACT:
Refractory metals and alloys are distinguished by their exceptional thermophysical properties,
including high melting and recrystallization temperatures, remarkable strength, and superior
corrosion resistance, all of which surpass those of conventional alloys. These unique
characteristics position these materials as ideal candidates for applications in extreme
environments. However, their potential has historically been underexploited due to limitations in
processing capabilities.

Recent advancements in melt-based additive manufacturing (AM) processes present
opportunities to overcome these limitations. Previous Sandia studies have successfully
characterized pure tantalum produced through laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) externally at
Castheon, revealing that LPBF-fabricated tantalum exhibits properties exceeding those of
wrought materials. This promising outcome sparked increased interest in the internal additive
manufacturing of tantalum.

This project aimed to establish the new SLM 280 machine at SNL-CA to successfully produce
the first tantalum prints and characterize the material. Additionally, efforts were made to enhance
the machine by incorporating Inert equipment to minimize oxygen content within the print
volume. The results demonstrated that internally manufactured LPBF tantalum not only met but
exceeded the standards of wrought materials, even prior to the integration of the additional
equipment.

The inert equipment is almost successfully integrated and ready for use. Future research should
focus on understanding how this equipment influences the process-structure-property
relationships, as well as further optimizing the printing parameters for tantalum.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS:
There is a pressing need to enhance manufacturing capabilities for refractory metals, specifically
Tantalum. This enhancement is essential to mitigate supply chain vulnerabilities, promote
domestic sourcing of materials, and facilitate the replacement of outdated legacy materials.
Refractory metals and alloys are distinguished by their exceptional thermophysical properties,
which surpass the performance thresholds of conventional alloys [1]. Among these, Tantalum
stands out as a focal point of this investigation. Elemental Tantalum is characterized by its
remarkable resistance to high temperatures, exceptional corrosion resistance across a broad pH
spectrum, significant density, relatively low strength, biocompatibility, and a unique capacity for
energy storage and release [2] [3]. Although Tantalum exhibits lower strength compared to other
refractory metals, it compensates with a high work hardening capacity and notable ductility. Its
diverse applications include use in capacitors, medical implants and devices, chemical industry
linings, penetrator projectiles, and components in nuclear reactors [4].

Despite the advantageous properties of Tantalum that make it suitable for a variety of
applications, certain characteristics, like its high cost, elevated melting temperature, and
reactivity, pose significant challenges for bulk manufacturing [5] [6]. Typically, Tantalum is
produced through powder metallurgy techniques, which involve methods of melting and
consolidation or the compaction and sintering of powder feedstock to achieve desired part
geometries. These powder metallurgy processes are often accompanied by secondary processing
steps that are crucial for achieving precise geometric control of bulk Tantalum forms. However,
these secondary processes must navigate the challenges posed by chemical reactivity and
mechanical properties, often resulting in complex and costly manufacturing procedures.

AM presents innovative solutions to these challenges and has emerged as a promising approach
for fabricating refractory alloys including Tantalum. The layer-wise, rapid, and localized
solidification characteristic of AM confers several significant advantages: (1) reduced exposure
time to elevated temperatures, thereby minimizing the risk of environmental contamination
during synthesis; (2) the formation of metastable microstructures that can yield unique
combinations of strength and ductility; (3) limited chemical partitioning during solidification;
and (4) the ability to achieve design complexities that traditional subtractive manufacturing
methods cannot replicate.

Recent studies have begun to explore the AM process in relation to Tantalum, yet much of our
current understanding remains rooted in the behaviors of conventional materials. It is important
to note that conventional Tantalum exhibits different characteristics compared to its AM
counterpart. Previous research conducted at SNL has investigated the initial behaviors of AM
Tantalum produced externally by Castheon.

The specific inquiry addressed in this work was: “Can enhancements in supply chain
management and modifications to the manufacturing process mitigate the risks associated with
the use of AM Tantalum in targeted applications, thereby enabling unprecedented flexibility?”

The findings of this investigation revealed that Tantalum could indeed be manufactured using
Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) techniques internally at SNL. The produced components
demonstrated density values ranging from 98.9% to 99.2%. While these density values, along
with microstructural analyses, indicated the presence of pores in the LPBF Tantalum, suggesting
opportunities for further optimization of process parameters, tensile tests revealed notable
differences among the tested parameter sets. Nevertheless, all SNL produced materials surpassed
the ASTM B708-12 wrought standards for both strength and ductility. Specifically, the SNL
material exhibited lower strength (with a 25% difference in ultimate tensile strength) but
significantly higher elongation (a 52% difference) compared to the previously characterized
Castheon LPBF Tantalum material. Furthermore, this research demonstrated the feasibility of
manufacturing “component-like” geometries, with samples created that mirrored specific
geometrical configurations distinct from those of the mechanical test samples previously
produced at both SNL and Castheon.

Additionally, efforts were made to integrate an inert gas system into the SLM LPBF machine.
Given Tantalum's high susceptibility to environmental impurities during solidification, it is
crucial to maintain stringent control over oxygen content. While LPBF machines typically
manage oxygen levels, the standard ranges achievable may not be sufficiently low for Tantalum
manufacturing. Throughout this project, an Inertcorp oxygen and moisture scrubber was
procured and successfully integrated into the SLM machine. This hardware, typically utilized in
gloveboxes, presented integration challenges. However, with this enhancement, the build
chamber can now achieve oxygen content levels below 1 ppm. This advancement is expected to
improve process stability and qualification, thereby enhancing overall manufacturing capability
with the scrubber in operation. The successful integration of this system paves the way for future
research and allows for a greater degree of process optimization.

was equipped with a reduced build volume insert that allowed for a smaller build plate, which

research indicating its effectiveness in achieving metallurgical bonding while also being cost-
efficient. The Tantalum powder employed in these builds was sourced from Tekna and is

The initial build (Build 1) served as a parameter development exercise aimed at evaluating four
distinct sets of print parameters. These parameters were derived from prior research conducted at
Castheon, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), and Savannah River National
Laboratory (SRNL). A detailed overview of these print parameters can be found in Table 3. It is
crucial to highlight that these parameters were established on different AM machines: Castheon
utilized an EOS M290 with a beam size of 75 µm, while LLNL employed a SLM 280 with a
layer height of 30 µm. This distinction is significant, as it is widely recognized that transferring
parameters between similar machines can result in variations in the printed material.
Consequently, transferring parameters to different machines located in various settings, even
with minor differences in parameter settings, could pose challenges.

For each of these parameter sets, density cubes, sub-size tensile samples, and cylinders for
microstructural analysis were fabricated, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of the effects of
the different print parameters.

The second build focused exclusively on the Castheon 67 and Castheon 90 parameter sets.
During this build, a variety of samples were produced, including density cubes, microstructural
cylinders, sub-size tensile samples, and hexagonal samples, all utilizing the same parameters
previously outlined in Table 3.

Subsequent testing was conducted on the samples from Build 2. The density cubes were
analyzed using an Anton Paar Ultrapyc 500 helium pycnometer. Prior to testing, the samples
underwent a thorough cleaning process involving ultrasonic cleaning in deionized water,
followed by baking in an oven at 250°C for one hour to ensure optimal conditions.

The sub-size tensile samples were subjected to tensile testing at a strain rate of 0.001 mm/s until
failure. This testing was performed using a 3A Stephens load frame (model number: 204m, serial
number: 783), equipped with both a 15,000 lbf load cell (model number: SWP20-25k-B000,
serial number: 126430A) and a 5,000 lbf load cell (model number: 1210AF-5K-B, serial number:
1174144A). A-type high-throughput grips were employed to securely hold the samples during
testing. To facilitate digital image correlation (DIC), the specimens were speckled along the
gauge length surface. The front face of each sample was imaged using a Point Gray camera with
a resolution of 4.1 million pixels, capturing images at a rate of one per second. Figure 2
illustrates the experimental setup utilized for the tensile testing process.

 Figure 2: Image of experimental setup (left), and specimen in grips (right).

Microstructural analysis was conducted on both the cylinders and density cubes. For this
characterization, samples were prepared to provide both longitudinal and transverse views, as
schematically illustrated in Figure 3. The samples underwent a comprehensive metallographic
preparation process, culminating in a final polishing step that involved chemical-mechanical
polishing for 24 hours on a vibratory polisher. This process utilized a solution composed of 600
mL of 50 nm colloidal silica and 40 mL of 30% hydrogen peroxide. High-resolution images of
the samples were captured using a Keyence light polarized microscope.

 Figure 3: Schematic of longitudinal and transverse views of microstructural analysis samples.

Subsequently, the Castheon parameters were employed to manufacture component-like parts,
marking a shift towards exploring geometries beyond the conventional mechanical property
samples. Engaging in discussions with a potential customer inspired these prints, which were
modified for security reasons but still effectively demonstrated the capability to produce shapes
relevant to mission-critical applications.

In addition, considerable effort was dedicated to integrating the Inertcorp Oxygen/Moisture
Scrubber equipment, typically utilized in gloveboxes. This advanced equipment is capable of
achieving oxygen levels below 1 ppm, significantly enhancing process stability, quality, and
overall capability. However, adapting this equipment for use with an additive manufacturing
machine presented challenges, requiring substantial time and ingenuity to develop an effective
plumbing solution.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION:
Build 1 failed after about 20 layers. Parts on the build plate began to delaminate and damage the
recoater mechanism in the system. This resulted in build failure, shown in Figure 4.

 Figure 4: Build 1 failure.

Upon investigation, it was determined that two factors led the early failure, (1) a steel build plate
was accidentally used leading to delamination and (2) the parameter sets from SRNL were
improperly exported, which contributed to the poor quality and were contributing to the issues
observed. The first build did assist in an initial observation of parameter quality and making the
decision to move forward with primarily Castheon settings.

In contrast, build 2, which primarily utilized the Castheon parameters, was executed
successfully. Figure 5 showcases this successful build, marking a significant milestone as it
represents the first LPBF Tantalum build conducted internally at Sandia National Laboratories.

These figures illustrate that the LPBF Tantalum produced at SNL demonstrated lower strength
compared to the Castheon material yet exhibited significantly higher ductility. A similar trend
was observed when comparing SNL material to literature on LPBF Tantalum. Notably, the SNL
material still surpassed the strength and ductility of the ASTM wrought standard Tantalum
material. These differences are likely attributed to the level of porosity present in the SNL
material. Further optimization of the print parameters could potentially enhance the strength of
the SNL material. Additionally, it is important to note that no chemical analysis was conducted
on the as-printed SNL material, and there may be chemical variations that could elucidate the
differences observed between the Castheon and SNL materials.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:
The primary outcomes of this work are significant and groundbreaking: Tantalum has now been
successfully manufactured using LPBF internally at SNL, a feat that had not been accomplished
previously. Not only does this mark the first instance of Tantalum being additively manufactured
at SNL, but the material produced to date also surpasses the properties of wrought ASTM
standard materials. This achievement represents a crucial advancement in integrating additive
manufacturing into mission-critical applications. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that
actual parts can be fabricated, extending beyond the production of mechanical and
microstructural analysis samples. In addition, the integration of supplementary equipment into
the LPBF machine has been undertaken to further enhance the quality of Tantalum produced.

Looking ahead, several key directions warrant exploration. First and foremost, there is a pressing
need for further optimization of the print parameters. The results from this work indicate that
significant porosity still exists within the material, and refining these parameters to reduce or
eliminate this porosity could lead to enhanced mechanical properties that exceed those of
conventional materials. Additionally, the installation of the Inertcorp equipment should be
completed, followed by testing to assess the effects of a lower oxygen environment. It is
anticipated that this modification could substantially improve mechanical properties, thereby
enhancing performance, consistency, and overall quality.

Beyond the straightforward optimization of parameters and equipment, it is essential to deepen
our understanding of the mechanics involved in the LPBF process for Tantalum. This knowledge
will be crucial for modeling the phenomena occurring during manufacturing, particularly in
relation to distortion compensation—a significant concern when working with expensive
powders, where multiple iterations can be costly. In this context, investigating topology
optimization and other strategies to conserve powder would also be a valuable area of study.
Furthermore, research into powder reuse and the effects of reusing powder should be conducted
to determine how many cycles the powder can withstand without compromising quality.

Post-processing techniques represent another area ripe for exploration. While small parts have
demonstrated no issues with stress relief, the objective will be to scale up to larger components,
where managing residual stress becomes critical. Investigating the effects of heat treatment on
microstructure and mechanical properties is also of paramount importance. Additionally, there
remain unanswered questions regarding properties beyond basic tensile characteristics, such as
corrosion behavior, fatigue performance, and dynamic testing.

Due to the limited timeline of this project, no publications or presentations have yet been
produced; however, it is likely that this work has laid the groundwork for future endeavors in

which these initial findings can be incorporated. As previously mentioned, additional ND work
has already emerged from this project, leading to the manufacturing of more parts on this
machine in FY26. The availability of Tantalum as an option in LPBF will provide greater
flexibility for prototyping and design within ND mission spaces than ever before. These results
also demonstrate that LPBF Tantalum is a viable option for creating near-net-shape parts suitable
for applications in extreme environments across the aerospace, defense, and energy sectors.
Establishing these foundational results will facilitate the promotion of this technology across
other mission areas at SNL and may also inspire new collaborations within the broader DOE
community and among other SNL partners.

CONCLUSION:
In summary, this project has yielded fundamental and groundbreaking advancements in the field
of additive manufacturing. The successful production of Tantalum via LPBF at SNL marks a
significant milestone, demonstrating that even with just initial parameter development, the
material produced surpasses the properties of ASTM standard wrought materials.
Comprehensive analyses conducted on this material revealed the presence of porosity, indicating
that there is ample opportunity for further optimization.

Importantly, the project not only focused on mechanical and microstructural analysis samples but
also successfully fabricated component-like parts. This opens numerous avenues for future
research, and it is anticipated that this work will serve as a foundational baseline for subsequent
investigations in the field.
