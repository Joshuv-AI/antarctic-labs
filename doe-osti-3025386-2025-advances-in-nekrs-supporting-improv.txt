 Summary

This report presents several 2025 advancements in NekRS, a high-fidelity spectral element CFD code
developed at Argonne National Laboratory to support the NEAMS thermal-hydraulics program.
 The forthcoming v25 release consolidates several of these advances, adding new features for
portability across heterogeneous GPU architectures, real-time in situ visualization, improved tur-
bulence modeling, and conjugate heat transfer coupling.
 Over the past year, NekRS has demonstrated strong scalability and performance on DOE’s
leading exascale platforms, including Aurora and Frontier, confirming its readiness for some of the
largest and most complex simulations attempted to date. These achievements provide a power-
ful new platform for high-fidelity data generation, which in turn supports the development and
validation of advanced closure models critical for reactor safety and design.
 Significant algorithmic innovations have also been introduced. A new global runtime h-refinement
capability simplifies workflows by reducing mesh preparation burdens and enabling coarse-to-fine
restarts. Building on this, a novel multigrid strategy was implemented to accelerate pressure and
transport solves at scale, addressing long-standing bottlenecks in exascale CFD. Together, these
developments improve both the efficiency and accessibility of high-fidelity simulations for reactor-
relevant problems.
 Collectively, these enhancements represent a major step forward in simulation technology, po-
sitioning NekRS as a cornerstone of NEAMS efforts to enable accurate, efficient, and scalable
high-fidelity analysis of advanced nuclear systems.

1 Introduction

Thermal-hydraulic simulations are a cornerstone for advancing both the current fleet of Light Water
Reactors (LWRs) and the next generation of advanced reactor designs. These simulations are
designed to capture the complexities of coupled heat transfer and fluid flow, phenomena that directly
influence the efficiency and safety of nuclear systems. Within the NEAMS thermal-hydraulics
area, research efforts are directed toward the development of novel solution strategies that enhance
predictive capabilities, particularly in regimes where traditional engineering methods are limited.
Such innovations are critical as reactor concepts evolve toward higher operating temperatures, which
in turn introduce new challenges in materials performance and reactor safety.

 A defining economic advantage of advanced reactor technologies lies in their ability to operate at
elevated temperatures, thereby increasing thermodynamic efficiency. This benefit, however, demands
highly accurate models that can resolve localized thermal conditions such as “hot spots,” where
excessive temperatures may accelerate material degradation or precipitate system failure. Reducing
predictive uncertainty in these models is of both safety and economic importance, with potential
impacts including longer reactor lifetimes, lower material costs, and more rapid deployment of
next-generation nuclear systems.

 Thermal-fluid phenomena manifest across a wide spectrum of spatial and temporal scales. Direct
resolution of all scales in an engineering system—particularly in complex geometries such as those
encountered in nuclear reactors—is computationally infeasible with present resources. As such,
simulations must employ strategies that either resolve only the most critical scales or incorporate
closure models to approximate the effects of unresolved scales. This balance is essential: while
not all scales can be captured, all scales contribute to the global dynamics. A multiscale and
multi-resolution framework is therefore indispensable for achieving both accuracy and computational
efficiency.

 The NEAMS program addresses the limitations of legacy methods by embracing a multiscale/multi-
resolution simulation hierarchy. High-fidelity methods supply detailed data that informs reduced-
order and system-level models, thereby strengthening predictive capabilities across the hierarchy.
This framework enables targeted allocation of computational resources to critical scales and provides
a foundation for improved engineering insight. In this context, high-fidelity simulations are not an
end in themselves but rather a critical enabler for broader multiscale modeling efforts.

 High-fidelity methods are indispensable because they resolve the fine-scale physics that experi-
ments alone cannot cover, particularly under extreme conditions. In the absence of efficient
high-fidelity simulations, the field risks remaining in a data-scarce regime, constrained by
the limited availability and scope of experimental data. By contrast, robust high-fidelity techniques
provide a foundation for improved closure models and system-scale predictions. Accordingly, this
report places particular emphasis on the advancement of high-fidelity strategies, building on progress
achieved in 2024 [1].

 While high-fidelity methods provide accuracy and detail, system-level codes remain the workhorses
of industry practice due to their speed and scalability. A key paradigm of NEAMS research is
therefore the use of high-fidelity data to improve system-level codes, creating a synergistic work-

 1

flow that balances accuracy with efficiency. This approach enables the reliable application of
thermal-hydraulics models across a spectrum of reactor scales and design contexts.

 For high fidelity simulations, we use NekRS code developed at Argonne. NekRS is a high-
performance, open-source solver designed for incompressible and low-Mach number flows, heat
transfer, and combustion, with particular optimization for turbulent flows in complex geometries. Its
foundation rests on rapidly convergent high-order spectral element methods (SEMs) that minimize
numerical dissipation and dispersion. Advanced features include multilevel preconditioners, high-
order time-splitting schemes, and adaptive communication strategies, all supported by OCCA-based
kernels to ensure portability and scalability across evolving HPC architectures.

 This report highlights several major developments in NekRS during Fiscal Year 2025:

 • Release Candidate for NekRS v25: A pre-release version was issued, with enhancements
 and new features detailed in Section 2.

 • Performance on Aurora: Significant performance improvements were demonstrated on
 Aurora, one of the U.S. Department of Energy’s most powerful supercomputers. These results
 are presented in Section 3.

 • Mesh Refinement Methodology: A novel global h-refinement capability was implemented,
 enabling improved workflows for both verification and large-scale simulation setup. This is
 described in Section 4.

 • Multigrid Acceleration: Building on the mesh refinement capability, we introduce a
 multigrid layer that exploits the refined mesh structure to accelerate high-fidelity simulations.
 Results are shown in Section 5.

 The progress summarized in this report underscores the central role of high-fidelity thermal-
hydraulic simulations in supporting both scientific understanding and industrial deployment of
nuclear technologies. By continuing to strengthen the computational foundation of NEAMS through
advances in NekRS, the program enhances predictive confidence, accelerates the qualification of
advanced designs, and contributes directly to the safe, economical expansion of nuclear energy.

2 NekRS v25 Release

Over the course of the last fiscal year, NekRS has been extended with a wide range of new features
that significantly improve performance, portability, and flexibility for high-fidelity thermal-fluid
simulations. The following subsections summarize the key developments.

FP32 Solver Mode. A single-precision (FP32) solver mode has been introduced, reducing memory
usage and improving overall performance. This capability enables much larger problems to fit
within limited device memory and accelerates turnaround time, thereby enhancing productivity for
large-scale simulations.

 2

DPC++ Backend for Intel GPUs. A new DPC++ execution backend has been implemented, en-
abling NekRS to run efficiently on Intel GPUs. This addition extends portability across heterogeneous
HPC systems and ensures readiness for next-generation Intel-based exascale machines.

Interpolation-Based Recycling and Arbitrary Field Support. Interpolation-based field recycling
has been added, along with support for arbitrary user-defined fields. This allows efficient recycling
on non-extruded meshes and provides flexibility for custom solution variables.

Ascent In Situ Visualization Plugin. The Ascent in situ visualization and analysis plugin has
been integrated into NekRS. This capability enables real-time exploration of simulation results,
avoiding the need for heavy file I/O, and accelerates scientific discovery by providing immediate
feedback during simulations.

IOFLD Class with ADIOS2 Support. A new iofld class has been developed for reading and
writing NekRS field files, with optional ADIOS2 integration. This significantly improves I/O
performance, particularly at large scales, and facilitates smoother interoperability with external
analysis tools.

Enhanced Output Options. Output flexibility has been expanded by introducing element-based
filtering and interpolation onto uniform grids or grids with different polynomial orders. These
options give users finer control over stored data and visualization resolution.

Multi-Session Nek-Nek and Multi-Rate Time Stepping. NekRS now supports multi-session
nek-nek runs, including the use of multi-rate time stepping. This feature is especially advantageous
for multiphysics and multiscale problems, as it improves computational efficiency without sacrificing
accuracy.

Typed Device Memory and Pool Allocation. Device memory management has been improved by
introducing strongly typed memory pools. This reduces fragmentation, lowers runtime overhead,
and provides a safer and more user-friendly memory management interface.

Conjugate Heat Transfer (CHT) Nek-Nek Coupling. Support for conjugate heat transfer (CHT)
coupling has been added between separate Nek simulations, enabling multiphysics workflows that
involve tightly coupled solid-fluid heat transfer problems.

NRSQSUB for Nek-Nek Job Handling. The nrsqsub utility has been enhanced to improve job
submission and management for coupled nek-nek simulations. This streamlines the launch and
coordination of multi-job workflows in HPC environments.

 3

Improved JIT Compilation Performance. Just-in-time (JIT) compilation has been optimized
to reduce GPU kernel startup latency. This improvement decreases time-to-solution, particularly
beneficial for simulations requiring frequent restarts.

HIP Support for SEMFEM Preconditioner. The SEMFEM preconditioner has been extended
with HIP support, enabling efficient execution on AMD GPUs. This development ensures that
NekRS is portable to AMD-based systems such as Frontier, broadening DOE exascale coverage.

Aerodynamic Force Evaluation. NekRS now supports direct evaluation of aerodynamic forces,
including lift and drag. This enables broader applications of NekRS in computational fluid dynamics
(CFD) for aerospace and automotive engineering.

Extended SEM Operators. The library of spectral element method (SEM) operators available to
users has been expanded. This provides increased flexibility for algorithm development and custom
solver extensions.

Mesh Surface Operations. New mesh surface operations have been added, including direct
computation of surface integrals. These capabilities simplify boundary quantity extraction and
enable surface-based analysis during simulations.

Stabilization and Regularization Enhancements. A gradient jump penalty method has been
introduced, together with improved artificial viscosity model (AVM) regularization. These advance-
ments increase robustness and accuracy for under-resolved turbulence and high-Reynolds-number
flows.

T-Mesh Support for Scalars. T-mesh capabilities have been generalized to support all scalar
transport equations. This improvement increases flexibility for multiphysics simulations where
different scalar quantities may need to be solved on distinct meshes.

Robin Boundary Conditions. Robin (mixed) boundary conditions have been added for both
scalar and velocity problems. This feature expands the set of available boundary condition models,
facilitating the representation of physical interfaces such as convective heat transfer.

New Turbulence Models: RANS k-τ Variants. Finally, new turbulence models have been
incorporated, including Shear Stress Transport (SST) and Delayed Detached Eddy Simulation
(DDES) within the RANS k-τ family. These additions provide users with advanced turbulence
closures for a broader range of industrially relevant flows.

3 Performance

In the past year, we have demonstrated and refined workflows on Aurora and, for the first time,
showcased large-scale simulations on the full machine. The demonstration case involved a liquid
metal–water heat exchanger with significant magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) effects. While this
configuration is not directly tied to nuclear energy applications, the achieved performance underscores
capabilities that are highly relevant to the field.

 Building on this foundation, we performed pioneering simulations of large-scale lead–lithium
systems using NekRS on exascale platforms, including Frontier and Aurora. A major 2025 advance-
ment for NekRS has been the introduction of MHD capabilities [2], which now enable realistic,
high-fidelity modeling of liquid metal flows at unprecedented scale [3].

 In this section, we present the largest high-order MHD simulation performed to date—approaching
one trillion grid points—with sustained performance demonstrated on both Frontier and Aurora.
This acomplishment constitues the first successful demonstration of scalable high-order MHD
simulations at the exascale, marking a significant milestone for NekRS development and for the
broader HPC community.

3.1 Description of Demonstration Model

The mesh for the lead-lithium has up to E = 404, 976, 640 for the piping system and E =
1, 524, 756, 928 in the solid. Several smaller meshes for the piping system were also designed to
study scalability. The largest mesh is designed for wall-resolved Large Eddy Simulation (LES)
and Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) of the flow and resolution of MHD at high Hartmann
number Ha (> 1000) resolving the pboundary layer everywhere in the piping. Here, the Hartmann
number is defined as Ha := B0 L σ/µ where B0 is the applied magnetic-field strength, L is the
same characteristic length used in the Reynolds number, σ the electral conductivity and µ dynamic
viscosity. In LES we employ a filtering approach to approximate the dissipative effects of unresolved
scales [4]. The mesh translates into over 6.6 × 1011 grid points at polynomial order N = 7, one
of the largest simulation ever attempted with the spectral element method and one of the largest
fluid simulations in a complex geometry to date. It is also the largest liquid-metal MHD simulation
ever conducted and a ground-breaking world-first achievement. Inlet-outlet boundary conditions
are applied. In the solid, a heat flux is applied on the outer surface (i.e., first wall), and the induced
magnetic field is coupled between the conducting solid regions and the fluid while exterior surfaces of
the solid are electrically insulating. Volumetric heat loads and tritium generation rates are applied
based on OpenMC simulations. Detailed results of heat transfer and tritium transport are not
presented in this report.

 The mesh for the water part has up to E = 669, 035, 904 elements for the piping system and
E = 1, 262, 774, 048 in the solid. Several smaller meshes for the piping system were also designed
to study scalability. The largest mesh is designed for wall-resolved LES and DNS of the flow at
Re = 106 , resolving the boundary layer everywhere in the piping. This translates into over 6.6 × 1011
grid points at polynomial order N = 7, one of the largest simulation ever attempted with the
spectral element method and one of the largest fluid simulations in a complex geometry to date.

3.2 Performance Results

We emphasize that the work presented here is a considerable leap in problem size, geometry
complexity, physics modeling and simulation complexity compared to our previous works [5, 6].

 As noted earlier, NekRS runs a battery of tests at the start of each simulation to identify
the fastest kernel for each configuration. These are measured by using a combination of MPI -
Wtime and barriers. A typical output is shown in Table 3.1, which indicates (left to right) the
operation, order, precision, billions of degrees-of-freedom per second (GDOFS), bandwidth (GB/s),
performance (GFLOPS), and the selected kernel version. Inspection of these tables is extremely
useful in identifying under-performing kernels as platforms change.

 In Table 3.1, one can see that the NVIDIA H100-based Jedi platform at Jülich Supercomputing
Center (JSC) is sustaining in excess of 10 TFLOPS for several of the fp32 kernels that are used
in the pressure preconditioner, including lower order matrix-vector products used in p-multigrid
and in the fast diagonalization method (fdm) kernels, which effect the tensor contractions for the
local Schwarz overlapping solves on each element [7]. The H100 is also delivering > 10 TFLOPS for
the fp64 advection kernel (adv), which is compute intensive because the quadrature for nonlinear
advection terms must be evaluated on a finer mesh. (Here, the number of quadrature points is
Nq = 10 in each direction.) Moreover, the reported values for adv in this case are for advection of a
vector field (u or B), rather than a scalar such as temperature. As is the case with the Laplacian,
there is significant data re-use because the advecting field and geometric factors for the chain rule
can be applied to each component of the advected field.

 On Aurora, adv is underperforming with only 2.3 TFLOPS per tile on the Intel PVC. Optimizing
this kernel has been a challenge. The primary issue appears to be related to local cache sizes or
register pressure because adv requires Nq3 = 1000 64-bit reals for each intermediate field in the
operator evaluation. By contrast, Aurora does remarkably well for the 32-bit tensor contractions
in the fdm kernels, where it sustains 6.7 TFLOPS for N =9 and 7.2 TFLOPS for N =7, which is a
substantial gain over a single GCD of the AMD MI250X on Frontier.

 In addition to kernel measurements, the setup phase of NekRS measures and reports communi-
cation overhead for each instantiation of the gather-scatter operation (an unstructured equivalent
to the “halo exchange” of finite difference methods). Possible variants include overlapping commu-
nication with computation via the sequence: compute surface elements, initiate exchange, complete
interior element computations, wait for incoming surface values and finish, or computing all the local
residuals first, and then exchanging. Locally, the gather-scatter requires posting receives, packing
element surface data into contiguous outbound arrays, then sending those. One can exchange using
GPU-aware MPI (e.g., “GPU-direct” on NVIDIA platforms), or by passing the data to the host.
One can also pack the data on the host and send via the host.

 Each NekRS job also tracks basic runtime statistics, which are output every 500 time steps
unless the user specifies otherwise. Timing breakdowns roughly follow the physical substeps of
advection, pressure, and viscous update, plus tracking of known communication bottlenecks. In

 6

addition, each significant kernel returns its flop count, so a precise (somewhat conservative) flop rate
is returned. (In counting total flops, we assign a weight of 1/2 to fp32 operations.) These results
are used in guiding performance optimization and are illustrated in the next section.

 We begin with the strong-scaling comparison of Fig. 3.1, which shows results for NekRS running
on Aurora (6 Intel PVCs per node, two compute tiles per PVC), Frontier (4 AMD MI250X per
node with two GCDs per MI250X), and Jedi (4 NVIDIA H100s per node). Figure 3.1(a) shows the
classic time vs. P strong-scale results for hydro only. Fig. 3.1(b) shows the same data with n/P
on the x-axis, which indicates that Aurora and Frontier are realizing ≈ 80% parallel efficiency at
n/P ≈ 4M, which is in line with results in earlier performance studies [8]. New to this comparison
are the impressive H100 results, which are about 3× faster than Aurora/Frontier for the pure hydro
case and which shows near perfect strong scaling over the 40 nodes accessible on Jedi. For MHD,
which is shown in (c) and (d), we have more limited results. Clearly, Jedi is again the best, followed
by Aurora, but the MHD code is not scaling nearly as well, which is surprising given that the MHD
solver uses many of the same components as the better-tested hydro code.

 Table 3.2 illustrates the logfile statistics for a large MHD case with 1,929,733,568 fluid+solid
elements and 404,976,640 fluid elements, each of order N = 7 (n = EN 3 ≈ 662B gridpoints), using
P = 72000 GCDs on Frontier. This case is sustaining .114 TFLOPS per rank, which is a bit low

4 h-Refinement Theory and Demonstration

We have developed a new capability for Nek5000/RS that allows global h-refinement at runtime,
which can greatly reduce input mesh sizes and, hence, workflow complexity. Moreover, because the
refinement is uniform (i.e., every element in the original mesh is partitioned into a new array of
nh × nh × nh elements), the approach allows for a structured two-level h-multigrid (hMG) approach
to solving the coarse-grid problem, which is often a bottleneck for large-scale parallel simulations,
particularly on exascale platforms [9, 10].

 In the following, we describe the implementation of the mesh refinement and a demonstration.

4.1 Global Mesh Refinement

Nek5000/RS is capable of supporting E > 1B (109 ) spectral elements and recent NekRS simulations
have realized this level of resolution, particularly for conjugate heat transfer calculations [6]. For local
(to each element) polynomial expansions of degree N = 8, this amounts to 512 degrees-of-freedom
(dofs) for each scalar field (e.g., velocity components, ui , i = 1, . . . , 3, pressure, and temperature),
and resolutions of ≈ 1 trillion dofs have thus been realized per field.

 Figure 4.1 illustrates the types of problems that can be addressed with Nek5000/RS. The mesh
for the UKAEA’s CHIMERA facility has up to E = 1, 691, 729, 664 elements for the piping system
and E = 55, 056, 380 in the solid. Several smaller meshes for the piping system were also designed
to study scalability. The largest mesh is designed for wall-resolved LES and DNS of the flow at

Re = 105 –106 , resolving the boundary layer everywhere in the piping. This translates into over
1.1 · 1012 grid points at polynomial order N = 9 [6].

 Such large element counts put significant strain on the overall workflow, particularly on mesh
construction, on the need for large file transfers to the supercomputing site (often challenging for
international collaborations where bandwidth is limited), and on run-time loading/preprocessing of
files on leading edge supercomputing platforms where I/O and other compute-ancillary features are
fragile and often not fully debugged. The ability to represent the geometry and mesh topology on
a coarser mesh can reduce these burdens significantly—easily by one or two orders-of-magnitude
depending on nh . If nh =2, we have an 8-fold reduction, for nh = 3 the reduction is 27-fold, and so
forth. Such reductions can make these problems tractable, rather than being stuck with a seemingly
never-ending series of system issues where only 1-in-4 jobs may run successfully.

 Global h-refinement also allows one to test and debug large problems at a much coarser scale
without having to subsequently remesh to the final target resolution. Our new code also supports
coarse-to-fine restarts, which means that one can run a simulation at low resolution to bypass
unwanted initial transients at low cost and subsequently (at runtime), refine the mesh and interpolate
the coarse solution onto that mesh. (Nek5000/RS of course supports p-refinement, in which one can

interpolate from a starting polynomial N ′ to a higher or lower target polynomial N .)

4.2 Demonstration: Numerical Setup

The h-refinement approach dramatically reduces the size of the input mesh because the required
fine resolution is generated directly within the solver, yielding reductions by factors of n3h . These
reductions alleviate workflow bottlenecks in mesh generation, file transfer, and I/O, and make
extremely large simulations tractable on exascale systems, where NekRS has demonstrated capability
up to trillions of degrees of freedom. Uniform refinement also supports a natural two-level h-multigrid
strategy, enables coarse-to-fine restarts, and complements p-refinement, providing a powerful and
scalable path for wall-resolved LES/DNS and other high-fidelity applications. In this section, we
demonstrate this capability on a three-dimensional (3D) parallel heated-plates geometry, applying
two successive h-refinements.

 Figure 4.2 illustrates the computational model for the DNS of parallel plates (Case 1). The
fluid domain is divided into three sections: upper adiabatic, heat flux, and lower adiabatic. Their
proportions, as well as the spanwise Lx and wall-normal Ly lengths, are defined as follows [11]:

executed entirely at runtime and integrates naturally with the h-multigrid framework in NekRS,

form the basis of the method. Next, we examine both existing algorithms and the newly proposed

initial numerical results to illustrate the approach’s performance and potential.

5.2 hMG

A significant benefit of the proposed block-refinement is that it provides a structured mechanism for
coarsening, which is an important component to any multigrid solver.

 For Poisson solves such as arise in the pressure substep of the incompressible and low-Mach
Navier-Stokes time-advancement, Nek5000/RS uses p-multigrid (pMG) coupled with a Krylov-
subspace projection (KSP) such as FGMRES(R), where R ≈ 20 is the number of vectors retained in
the KSP. Suppose the given system is Au = b, with A a large symmetric positive definite system.
For iterative methods one seeks a sequence of iterates uk such that u = uk + ek , where ek := u − uk
is the unknown error after iteration k. . Given uk , one only needs to compute the error term, which
satisfies

                               Aek = Au − Auk = b − Auk =: rk .                                        (20)

Note that the residual rk is always computable.

 The key idea behind multigrid is to use an iteration (or smoother) that results in ek being the
representation of a smooth function, e(x), which can be adequately represented on a coarser mesh
than u(x) itself. One then solves the corresponding coarse problem,

                                              Ac ec = rc                                               (21)

 22

and interpolates ec back to the orginal fine mesh via

                                         ek ≈ ẽc := Jc ec ,                                       (22)

where Jc is the coarse-to-fine interpolation matrix. If Ac := JcT AJc and rc = JcT rk , then ẽc is the
closest approximation to ek in the range of Jc , when measured in the A-norm.

 The set of equations (20)–(22) represents the Galerkin formulation for the coarse-grid problem.
To solve it, we require a means to solve (perhaps approximately) the coarse problem (21) and to
apply the interpolation and restriction operators, Jc and JcT .

5.2.1 Current pMG Algorithm

In Nek5000/RS, pMG is implemented with smoothing on the fine level, where the governing matrix
A and solution u are based on polynomials of degree N . Several smoothing strategies are outlined
in [7, 14, 15]. The current default in NekRS is based on 4th-kind Chebyshev iteration with Schwarz-
based smoothing [16]. After s smoothing iterations (or perhaps a single s-order Chebyshev-Schwarz
smoothing), we restrict the residual and approximately solve the coarse problem following (20)–
(22). For restriction, we base Ac on a discretization of the continuous operator (e.g., −∇2 ) using
polynomial expansions of degree N ′ < N , with N ′ = floor(N/2) a common choice. One then
repeats this procedure until N ′ = 1, at which point the pMG smooth/restrict cycle terminates.

 For the final coarse-grid level, Ac corresponds to using linear approximations on each element. In
Nek5000/RS we typically form Ac as JcT AJc , which can be computed in 3D using just 8 matrix-vector
products in a preprocessing step. (For each element, one uses the linear test function that is 1 at
a single vertex and 0 at the remaining 7 vertices. The unassembled A matrix is then applied in
parallel to the corresponding vector, ϕk , which generates E local responses, v k . Repeating this
process for each of the remaining 7 vertices yields the elementwise columns of AJc ϕk , which are
then tested against the ϕj to form the 8-by-8 local matrices. Then that can be assembled with
direct stiffness summation.

5.2.2 New Algorithm

Let τo define the original spectral element mesh comprising E elements and τr comprise the refined
mesh with E ′ = E ndh elements. The transformation τo −→ τr is implemented as a runtime
preprocessing step, such that Nek5000/RS “sees” only τr and proceeds through the simulation as if
E = E ′ from the beginning. In this mode, no change is required in the body of the source code
but this mode also fails to exploit the structure that is inherent in the globally-refined τr mesh. In
particular, at the lowest (N = 1) level in pMG, one typically passes the coarse solve (21) to some
unstructured solver based on algebraic multigrid (AMG) or to the fast direct solver, XX T , if the
coarse system size is nc ≲ 106 [10]. As noted earlier, the coarse solve can be burdensome when
strong-scaling large problems to larger processor counts, P > 105 —as much as 45% of the runtime
in some cases [9]. To alleviate this burden, we propose to exploit the known structure in τr by using
h-multigrid to smooth the error on τr and restricting to τo . It is the restriction/prolongation process

 23

that leverages the structure in τr .
The overall strategy is as follows

 1. Run pMG down to N = 1 on Er elements.
 2. Smooth the error on τr using 4th-kind Chebyshev-Jacobi smoothing
 3. Restrict the residual to ro = JoT rr , where Jo is the N = 1 interpolant from the original mesh, τo , to
 the refined mesh, τr .
 4. Solve the N = 1 system on τo using any of the standard tools in Nek5000/RS (e.g., AMG with Hypre
      or XX T [10])
 5. Prolongate the error, eo , back to τr : ẽr = Jo eo .
 6. Update the solution on τr : ur = ur + ẽr

(Note that there is a bit of notational inconsistency given that, on τr , which is itself a coarse grid,
one is looking for the error, er , which will be added to the error on the next finest level, etc. Here,
we simply use ur to represent the iterate that we wish to correct.)
The procedure above amounts to developing a two-level multigrid for solving Ar ur = br on τr . The
key components are:

 • A mechanism to effect matrix-vector products of the form w = Dr−1 Ar p, where Dr =diag(Ar ).
 • The ability to apply restriction and prolongation operators, JoT and Jo .
 • The ability to solve a relatively coarse system, Ao eo = ro , on the originating mesh, τo .

5.3 Implementing Interpolation and Restriction

6 Conclusions

In conclusion, the advancements detailed in this report represent major progress in the field of
high-fidelity CFD simulations, particularly within the framework of the NekRS code.

 First, this work contributes to the upcoming v25 release of NekRS, encompassing several
important developments.

 Second, we achieved significant progress in the deployment and optimization of NekRS on the
Aurora supercomputer, one of the Department of Energy’s most powerful computational platforms.
These efforts resulted in several performance enhancements and the successful execution of large-
scale demonstration cases. The demonstrated scalability on Aurora provides researchers with an
additional, crucial platform for high-fidelity simulations, enabling the generation of high-quality
data that can be leveraged for the development and validation of advanced closure models.

 28

 Third, we introduced and tested a global on-the-fly mesh refinement strategy in combination
with a novel multigrid approach that exploits the refinement. Early results confirm the viability
of this strategy, showing that it not only expedites meshing workflows but also delivers robust
performance in large-scale simulations. This represents a critical step toward reducing the overhead
associated with mesh preparation and accelerating the path from problem definition to high-fidelity
analysis.

 Taken together, these advancements mark a substantial leap forward in high-fidelity simulation
capabilities, especially for the treatment of transient phenomena. They bring us closer to the
long-standing goal of efficiently and accurately resolving highly complex nuclear energy scenarios.
Importantly, they also complement other ongoing efforts in this technical area, reinforcing the
central role of NekRS in advancing the state of the art in exascale CFD.
