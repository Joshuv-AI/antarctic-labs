Genomic and functional insights into Pediococcus pentosaceus B1–3 from Yangzhou pickles and its application in pork preservation
Authors: Chao Tang, Yue Qian, Juncheng Chen, Guihong Fang, Jingyu Chen, Jingguo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemical preservatives are widely applied in food preservation but raise growing concerns over microbial resistance and chemical residue risks. The antimicrobial potential of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) derived from Yangzhou pickles and Daqu remains poorly characterized. In this study, the LAB strain B1–3, identified as Pediococcus pentosaceus, exhibited the strongest activity against Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, and Salmonella Typhimurium. Phenotypic characterization showed that B1–3 presents homofermentative metabolism, tests negative for hemolytic and coagulase activities, is susceptible to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, tetracycline and ciprofloxacin, and tolerates 0.3% bile salts. Whole-genome sequencing revealed no detectable plasmids, transferable antibiotic resistance genes, or typical virulence factors, providing preliminary evidence supporting its safety potential for food applications. Treatment with 40% cell-free supernatant of B1–3 effectively suppressed the proliferation of both indigenous spoilage microbiota and artificially inoculated pathogens in pork during 7-day storage at 4 °C, and caused no significant adverse effects on surface color, texture profile and lipid oxidation level. These findings highlight B1–3 as a highly promising candidate for developing natural meat biopreservatives.Keywords: Lactic acid bacteria, Cell-free supernatant, Antibacterial, Whole-genome sequencing, Biopreservation

Introduction

Chemical preservatives are widely used to extend the shelf life of chilled meat, but their long-term overuse has raised growing concerns about microbial resistance and chemical residue risks (Sharma et al., 2023). Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) have attracted increasing attention as sources of natural bio-preservatives (Borgonovi et al., 2022). LAB exert antimicrobial effects through multiple metabolites such as organic acids, bacteriocins and small bioactive molecules, and their cell-free supernatant (CFS) have been applied to preserve various food matrices (Arrioja-Bretón et al., 2020; Chang & Chen, 2000; Dopazo et al., 2023; Jo et al., 2021; Yi et al., 2022).

Traditional fermented foods are important reservoirs of diverse LAB resources (Goa et al., 202; Temmerman et al., 2003; Soemarie et al., 2022; Taye et al., 2021; Widyastuti et al., 2021). Existing studies have screened preservative LAB from fermented products (Hu et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024), while strains from the Yangzhou pickles and Daqu remain less explored for food preservation applications. Yangzhou pickles, a typical Chinese fermented vegetable product, are produced under high-salt and acidic conditions, which impose strong selective pressure and favor LAB strains with excellent stress tolerance and stable metabolic activity (Sun et al., 2023). Daqu is a cereal-based spontaneous solid-state fermentation starter widely used in the production of Chinese Baijiu and traditional fermented foods (Zhang et al., 2022). It harbors a diverse microbial community dominated by LAB, yeasts and moulds, and serves as a natural source of functional strains in this study (Osman et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2025). However, the antimicrobial activity and meat preservation potential of LAB derived from these two traditional fermented matrices have not been systematically evaluated.

Although LAB are generally recognized as safe microorganisms, their functional and safety characteristics are highly strain-specific. The integrated approach combining phenotypic assays with whole-genome sequencing has emerged as a robust framework for evaluating the antibacterial functionality and safety of LAB (Huang et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2024. At the functional level, genomic analysis enables precise identification of gene clusters associated with the synthesis of antibacterial metabolites (e.g., bacteriocins, organic acids and exopolysaccharides), helping to decipher the molecular mechanisms underlying antimicrobial activity and compensate for the limitation of phenotypic assays that only reflect inhibitory effects (Kumari et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2021; Whitfield et al., 2020). At the safety level, genome-wide screening allows systematic identification of potential virulence factors, antibiotic resistance genes and mobile genetic elements, enabling a more comprehensive risk assessment of horizontal gene transfer compared with sole phenotypic tests (Liu et al., 2022). This phenotype-genotype combined evaluation strategy can provide more solid scientific evidence for the industrial application of candidate LAB strains, and has gradually become a recognized paradigm for the safety assessment of food-grade microorganisms.

This study aimed to isolate and screen LAB from Yangzhou pickles and Daqu with antimicrobial activity against common foodborne pathogens. Strain B1–3 was characterized via phenotypic tests and whole-genome sequencing to verify its functional properties. The preservative efficacy of its CFS was further evaluated in fresh pork during refrigerated storage. This study provides a systematic evaluation of LAB resources from traditional fermented foods and supports the development of LAB-based natural preservatives for chilled meat.

Materials and methods

Samples collection and materials

Yangzhou pickles were sampled from Yangzhou Sanhe Simei Pickles Co., Ltd. (Yangzhou, China). Strong-flavor and sauce-flavor Daqu samples were provided by Luzhou Ruihua Biotech Co., Ltd. (Luzhou, China). Microbial culture media, including de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) and Luria-Bertani (LB), were purchased from Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). The strains Listeria monocytogenes ATCC 19111 (LM), Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538 (SA), and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium ATCC 13311 (ST) were obtained from the laboratory stock of Yangzhou University (Yangzhou, China). Fresh pork tenderloin was purchased from a local slaughterhouse (Yangzhou, China) and used on the same day for preservation experiments. Unless indicated otherwise, all reagents were obtained from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

LAB isolation and identification

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) were isolated from the sampled pickles and Daqu. 5 g of each sample were mixed with 45 mL of sterile saline solution. The suspension was vortexed at maximum speed for 5 min and subjected to ten-fold serial dilutions. An aliquot (100 μL) from appropriate dilutions was spread onto MRS agar plates supplemented with 2.0% (w/v) calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). The plates were then placed in an anaerobic bag equipped with a CO₂-generating agent (AnaeroPack, Mitsubishi Gas Chemical Company, Niigata, Japan) and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Colonies surrounded by a clear halo (CaCO₃ dissolution due to acid production) were considered putative LAB. These colonies were purified by streaking onto fresh MRS agar plates and incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 h. The purification process was repeated twice to obtain pure cultures. For molecular identification, genomic DNA was extracted from purified colonies using a commercial bacterial DNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using universal primers 27F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5’-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). The PCR products were purified and sequenced commercially (General Bio Co., Ltd., Chuzhou, China). The obtained sequences were deposited in the GenBank database under accession numbers SUB15289510 PV576250-PV576261. Taxonomic affiliation of each isolate was determined by comparing the 16S rRNA gene sequences against the NCBI nucleotide database.

Antibacterial activity of LAB cell-free supernatant (CFS)

Preparation of LAB CFS and pathogen cultures

Each LAB strain was inoculated into MRS broth and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The cultures were then centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane filter to obtain the CFS, which was stored at 4 °C until use. Untreated crude CFS, with no pH neutralization, organic acid removal, or hydrogen peroxide elimination were used in this study. The presented inhibitory efficacy reflects the overall bioactivity of the complete secreted metabolites. The target pathogens (LM, SA, ST) were pre-cultured in LB broth at 37 °C with shaking at 120 r/min for 18 h to reach the exponential growth phase. The bacterial cultures were resuspended in fresh LB to OD₆₀₀nm = 0.5 and then diluted 1:100 in LB broth to obtain working suspensions.

Agar well-diffusion assay

The agar well-diffusion method was performed as previously described with modifications (Ye et al., 2020). Briefly, 100 μL of the diluted pathogen suspension was spread evenly onto LB agar plates. Sterile Oxford cups (6 mm diameter) were placed on the agar surface. Each cup was filled with 150 μL of LAB CFS. The plates were incubated at 36 °C for 18 h. The diameter of the clear inhibition zone around each cup was measured. Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Optical density measurement assay

The growth inhibitory effect was quantified using a microplate assay. In a 96-well plate, 180 μL of the diluted pathogen suspension (approximately 3.5 × 105 CFU/mL in LB broth) was mixed with 20 μL of LAB CFS. For the control, sterile MRS broth was added instead of CFS. Meanwhile, matrix blanks consisting of LB broth and the corresponding volume of CFS (without pathogen inoculation) were prepared in parallel. At least six biological replicates were conducted. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. After incubation, the optical density at 595 nm (OD₅₉₅) was measured using a microplate reader (EnSight, PerkinElmer Inc., Massachusetts, USA). The OD value of each sample well was corrected by subtracting the absorbance of its respective matrix blank prior to calculating the inhibition rate. The inhibitory rate (%) of the LAB CFS was calculated using the following formula: Inhibitory rate (%) = [(ODc - ODₜ) / ODc] × 100, where ODₜ is the corrected OD₅₉₅ of the sample well, and ODc is the corrected OD₅₉₅ of the control.

Physicochemical characterization of B1–3

Gas producing ability

B1–3 cells from the log phase were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 ×g for 10 min, washed twice with sterile PBS, and resuspended in PBS to OD₆₀₀ = 0.5 (approximately 108 CFU/mL). This suspension was inoculated at 1% (v/v) into MRS broth containing 3.0% glucose and Durham tube. Negative control (uninoculated) and positive control (Escherichia coli MG1655, stored in our lab) were conducted. All tubes were sealed and incubated statically at 37 °C for 48 h. The formation of gas bubbles in the Durham tube was recorded as heterolactic fermentation.

Proteolytic ability

The B1–3 cell suspension (OD₆₀₀ = 0.5) was prepared as described above. An aliquot (100 μL) was spread evenly onto the surface of MRS supplemented with 1.0% (w/v) skimmed milk. Bacillus subtilis FC22 strain (stored in our lab) was used as a positive control. Plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h. Proteolytic activity was indicated by the presence of a clear hydrolysis zone around the colonies.

Hemolytic ability

Sheep blood was incorporated (5%, v/v) into brain heart infusion (BHI) agar to prepare blood agar plates. B1–3 was streaked onto the plates, with S. aureus (ATCC 6538) serving as a positive control for β-hemolysis. Plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h, after which the type of hemolysis (α, β, or γ) around the colonies was observed.

Coagulase producing ability

The B1–3 suspension (OD₆₀₀ = 0.5) was mixed with reconstituted rabbit plasma (3:5, v/v), and vortexed for 10 s. S. aureus (ATCC 6538) and sterile water were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. The mixture was incubated statically at 37 °C for 6 h and observed for clot formation.

Antibiotic susceptibility

The antibiotic susceptibility profile of B1–3 was determined using the microbroth dilution method in 96-well plates, following the general principles of CLSI M07. MRS broth was used as the test medium to support sufficient and consistent growth of the strain. Briefly, B1–3 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested and resuspended in fresh MRS broth to prepare the working suspension (approximately 106 CFU/mL). Five representative antibiotics (ampicillin, kanamycin, ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, tetracycline) were serially two-fold diluted in MRS broth across a concentration gradient. Each well contained 100 μL of diluted antibiotic solution and 100 μL of bacterial working suspension. Wells with only MRS broth and bacterial inoculum served as growth controls, and uninoculated bacteria broth served as blank controls. All plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was defined as the lowest antibiotic concentration at which no visible bacterial growth was observed. Susceptibility and resistance were classified according to the epidemiological cut-off (ECOFF) values recommended by EFSA for the safety assessment of Pediococcus species used in food and feed (Rychen et al., 2018).

Bile salt and simulated gastrointestinal fluid tolerance

B1–3 cell suspension (OD₆₀₀ = 0.5) was inoculated at 1% (v/v) into MRS broth supplemented with 0.3% (w/v) bile salt. Cultures were incubated statically at 37 °C. The OD₆₀₀ was measured at 0, 2, 4, and 6 h to monitor B1–3 growth ability. The tolerance of B1–3 to simulated gastric and intestinal fluids was evaluated (Ye et al., 2020). B1–3 cell suspension (OD₆₀₀ = 0.5) were inoculated at 5% (v/v) to simulated gastric fluid with pH 2.5 and pepsin, and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 r/min. Samples were taken at 0, 1, and 2 h, serially diluted, and spot-plated on MRS agar to determine viable counts (CFU/mL). Following gastric treatment (2 h), a 5% (v/v) inoculum was transferred to simulated intestinal fluid (pH 7.0, containing pancreatin and bile salts) and incubated at 37 °C with shaking. Three biological replicates were conducted. Samples were taken at 0, 2, and 4 h for viable counts. The limit of detection for the serial dilution method used in this study was 10 CFU/mL.

Whole genome sequencing of B1–3

DNA extraction and sequencing

To obtain a genome sequence with both high accuracy and completeness, a hybrid sequencing strategy combining Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing and Illumina short-read sequencing was employed. First, genomic DNA was extracted from the pelleted cells of the statically cultured B1–3 strain using an SDS lysis method. The extracted DNA was assessed for quality and quantity using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Massachusetts, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Qualified DNA samples were used to construct two separate libraries. For third-generation sequencing, the genomic DNA was used to prepare a library without amplification using the Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-LSK109) and the Native Barcoding Kit (EXP-NBD104/114) (Oxford Nanopore Technologies Ltd., Oxford, UK). The prepared library was then loaded onto an R9.4.1 flow cell and sequenced on a PromethION sequencer. For second-generation sequencing, a short-insert paired-end library was constructed using the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. After quantification with Qubit 4.0 and quality assessment with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, USA), the qualified library was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. The sequencing was assisted by Wuhan GeneRead Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Sequences were deposited in the NCBI (BioProject: PRJNA1492243).

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw sequencing reads were first subjected to quality filtering to remove low-quality and overly short reads. The long reads from ONT were initially assembled and self-corrected using Canu to generate a draft genome. Subsequently, the high-accuracy short reads from Illumina were utilized to perform multiple rounds of error correction on the draft assembly. The coverage depth and assembly completeness of the final genome were assessed by mapping the corrected long reads back to it using minimap2 (v2.15-r905). Genome annotation comprised structural and functional components. Protein-coding genes were predicted using Glimmer (v3.02). Transfer RNA (tRNA) and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes were identified using tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.9) and RNAmmer (v1.2), respectively. For functional annotation, all predicted protein sequences were aligned against general databases (NR, COG, KEGG, GO) using DIAMOND (v2.0.9.147) with the blastp algorithm. For targeted genomic safety assessment, PlasmidFinder 2.1 was used under default parameters to screen the complete genome assembly for plasmid replicons and extrachromosomal contigs. The CARD database coupled with the RGI pipeline was adopted to identify horizontally transferable acquired antibiotic resistance genes with built-in standard threshold settings. The VFDB database was employed to scan all coding sequences for canonical bacterial virulence factors including toxins, adhesins and invasion-associated proteins, and the PHI database was applied to annotate genes associated with pathogen-host interaction phenotypes.

Application of B1–3 CFS in fresh pork preservation

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The CFS of strain B1–3 was serially two-fold diluted in MRS broth to obtain concentrations ranging from 0% to 80% (v/v). Working suspensions of the foodborne pathogens LM, SA, and ST were prepared as described in section 2.3.1. In a 96-well plate, each CFS dilution was mixed 1:1 (v/v) with the bacterial suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Six biological replicates per treatment were conducted. The MIC was defined as the lowest CFS concentration with no visible bacterial growth. Meanwhile, the OD₆₀₀ nm of each well was measured every 2 h over a 24 h period to generate growth curves of the pathogens under varying CFS concentrations.

Effect of CFS on biofilm formation of foodborne pathogens

The CFS antibiofilm activity was evaluated using a 96-well plate model. A mixture of CFS and bacterial suspension (1:1 v/v) was incubated statically at 25 °C. Biofilm biomass was quantified at 12, 24, and 48 h using the crystal violet staining method. Six biological replicates per treatment were conducted. The specific procedures for biofilm treatment and staining were performed as previously described (Xu et al., 2024).

Pork preservation and analysis

Fresh pork tenderloin was sourced in a single batch and portioned into 5 g pieces. All pieces were first immersed in sterile water for 5 min and then dried with absorbent paper. The pork pieces were divided into two groups, with three biological replicates per group. The treatment group was immersed in 40% (v/v) B1–3 CFS for 10 min, while the control group was immersed in sterile water for the same duration. After treatment, the surface was dried, and the samples were stored at 4 °C. The pH, total viable count (TVC), color, texture, and thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) value were observed at 1, 3, 5, and 7 d of storage. Specifically, a 2.5 g sample was homogenized with 22.5 mL of distilled water at 120 r/min for 30 min. The mixture was filtered, and the pH of the filtrate was measured using a pH meter. As for TVC, a 5.0 g sample was homogenized in 45 mL of sterile saline. Serial dilutions were prepared and plated on PCA for colony enumeration. Color parameters (L, a, b) of the pork surface were measured using a colorimeter. As for pork texture analysis, samples were cut into 1 cm3 cubes. Texture profile analysis (hardness, springiness) was performed using a texture analyzer with a P/36R probe under the following settings: pre-test speed 1 mm/s, test speed 1 mm/s, post-test speed 1 mm/s, 60% strain, and a trigger force of 5 g. Lipid oxidation was assessed by determining the TBARS value according to the Chinese National Standard method (GB 5009.181–2016).

Inhibition of foodborne pathogens in pork

Pork tenderloin (5 g each piece) was surface-decontaminated by immersion in 150 ppm sodium hypochlorite solution for 5 min, followed by rinsing and drying. The pieces were then artificially inoculated by immersion in a pathogen suspension (LM, SA, or ST) for 5 min and dried. The initial inoculum level was determined by plate counting. The inoculated pork was divided into two groups: the treatment group was immersed in 40% CFS, and the control group was immersed in sterile water. Three biological replicates were included for each group. After drying, all samples were stored at 4 °C. The populations of foodborne pathogens on the pork were enumerated at 1, 3, and 5 d of storage.

Statistical analysis

Microbial counts were log10-transformed prior to analysis. For all time-course indicators in the pork preservation assay, a two-way ANOVA was performed with treatment and storage time as fixed factors; the treatment × storage time interaction was also included in the model. Given the presence of significant treatment × storage time interactions, simple main effects analyses were conducted to compare treatments at each individual storage time point. For datasets in which only the main effects were significant and no interaction was detected, Duncan's multiple range test was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results and discussion

LAB isolated from fermented products

A total of 12 LAB strains were isolated from traditional Yangzhou pickles and Daqu (Table 1). Phylogenetic analysis based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing preliminarily identified these isolates across the genera Leuconostoc, Lentilactobacillus, Pediococcus, Levilactobacillus, Loigolactobacillus, and Enterococcus. Leuconostoc, Lentilactobacillus, and Pediococcus were commonly identified in pickles (Yasir et al., 2022), while Enterococcus and Leuconostoc were frequently isolated from Daqu (Huang et al., 2025). Meanwhile, several of these genera include species with documented probiotic or food-grade status, such as Enterococcus lactis (Fu et al., 2022), Levilactobacillus brevis (Fan et al., 2023), and Pediococcus pentosaceus (Jiang et al., 2021), which are recognized for their safety profile and potential functional properties.

Antibacterial activity of LAB CFS

Qualitative assessment by the agar well-diffusion method revealed broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of LAB strains. After 18 h of incubation, clear inhibition zones were observed, indicating that all obtained LAB CFS significantly inhibited LM, SA, and ST. Strain B1–3, isolated from Yangzhou pickles, demonstrated superior inhibitory activity, forming the largest zones against LM (26.53 ± 0.91 mm) (Fig. 1A), SA (22.70 ± 0.20 mm) (Fig. 1B), and ST (14.87 ± 0.38 mm) (Fig. 1C). Quantitative optical density measurements over 24 h confirmed the time-dependent inhibitory effects, with B1–3 consistently showing the highest potency. Against LM, B1–3 exhibited 22.71 ± 2.77% inhibition rate at 4 h (Table S1). A similar trend was observed against SA, where B1–3 maintained the strongest effect, although its inhibition rate decreased from 59.09 ± 3.45% at 4 h to 39.45 ± 2.58% at 24 h (Table S2). Against ST, B1–3 showed significant inhibition at 4 h, but the efficacy declined sharply thereafter (Table S3). Overall, B1–3 demonstrated higher antibacterial activity compared to other isolates. As untreated crude CFS was used without pH neutralization, the observed inhibition reflects the combined bioactivity of all secreted metabolites. Organic acids are well-established as major contributors to the antimicrobial activity of LAB CFS (Mani-López et al., 2022; Messaoudi et al., 2013; Shu et al., 2024), while the independent or synergistic effects of bacteriocins and other bioactive molecules cannot be differentiated under the current experimental design.

Physicochemical characterization of B1–3

The strain B1–3 was determined to be homofermentative, as no gas production was detected in Durham tubes containing MRS broth with 3.0% glucose (Fig. 2A & Table 2). It also showed no proteolytic activity on skim milk agar, indicating a lack of secreted proteases (Fig. 2B & Table 2). B1–3 was non-hemolytic on sheep blood agar and coagulase-negative (Fig. 2C-D & Table 2), providing preliminary phenotypic evidence for its safe application in food matrices. Quantitative MIC determination showed that B1–3 was susceptible to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, tetracycline and ciprofloxacin, but resistant to kanamycin (Table 2). Kanamycin resistance is a well-documented intrinsic species-level trait of P. pentosaceus and most lactic acid bacteria, arising from the lack of active aerobic uptake systems for aminoglycosides across the cell membrane (Sirichoat et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025). Meanwhile, B1–3 exhibited tolerance to 0.3% bile salts, maintaining viability after 6 h of exposure (Fig. 2E). In simulated gastrointestinal conditions, the strain was sensitive to gastric juice, with not detectable (less than 10 CFU/mL) after 1 h treatment (Fig. 2F). However, upon subsequent transfer (5% v/v) to simulated intestinal fluid, a substantial portion of the population recovered, reaching 4.56 log CFU/mL after 4 h.

Genomic analysis of B1–3

B1–3 possesses a circular chromosome of 1,776,686 bp with a GC content of 37.3%. No plasmids were detected, indicating a low risk of horizontal transfer for genes associated with antibiotic resistance or virulence via plasmids (Botthoulath et al., 2025; King, 2025; Wang et al., 2024). The average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis were performed using the whole-genome sequence of strain B1–3 against three type strain genomes of Pediococcus pentosaceus (GCA_000014505.1, GCA_004354495.1, GCA_016127775.1). The ANI values against the three reference strains were 98.5%, 98.7% and 98.9%, respectively. This result confirms the taxonomic assignment of strain B1–3 as Pediococcus pentosaceus. Genome annotation predicted 1711 protein-coding genes, accounting for 88.2% of the genome (Table 3). A comprehensive circular genome map of B1–3 was generated (Fig. 3), showing the key features including the distribution of genes on forward and reverse strands, GC content, GC skew, locations of tRNA/rRNA genes, and COG functional categories (Waters & Storz, 2009). Furthermore, the whole genome of B1–3 was annotated using functional annotation databases, including NR, COG/KOG, GO, SwissProt, and KEGG. The results of functional annotation are summarized in Fig. 4 & Table S4. The NR database showed that 97.0% sequences of B1–3 specifically annotated to Pediococcus pentosaceus (Fig. 4A). The COG profile reflects the metabolic characteristics of B1–3 (Fig. 4B). In addition, the Gene Ontology (GO) annotation revealed that the metabolic process (725 genes) and cellular process (748 genes) were most prevalent in Biological Process functional class (Fig. 4C). KEGG indicated the highest gene enrichment in the Metabolism class (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, the targeted genome-wide screening against the CARD, VFDB and PHI databases confirmed that no horizontally transferable acquired antibiotic resistance genes, canonical virulence factors or pathogenicity-associated pathogen-host interaction genes were identified in the B1–3 chromosome (Table S5-S7). Combined with the absence of plasmids, these findings provide genomic-level support for the preliminary safety assessment of this strain, with low risk of horizontal transfer of antibiotic resistance or virulence determinants. No horizontally transferable aminoglycoside resistance genes were identified via CARD screening, which rules out acquired transmissible resistance and further corroborates that the kanamycin resistance phenotype is an intrinsic species characteristic with negligible horizontal transfer risk (Ammor et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2024).

Application of B1–3 CFS in fresh pork preservation

To evaluate the preservative effect of the B1–3 CFS on fresh pork, its minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) against three common foodborne pathogens (LM, SA, and ST) was determined to be 40% (v/v). At this concentration, the supernatant significantly inhibited the planktonic growth of all three pathogens within 24 h (Fig. S1). A further biofilm assay showed that the 40% supernatant significantly reduced the biofilm biomass of SA, while showed no significant effects on LM and ST (Fig. S2-S4). Based on these antibacterial results, a 40% (v/v) CFS was selected for subsequent pork preservation experiments. The artificial inoculation experiment was conducted to investigate the CFS's effect on specific pathogens. As shown in Fig. 5A-C, the 40% CFS of B1–3 effectively inhibited the growth of inoculated pathogens (LM, SA, ST), with no significant increase in their CFUs over the 5-day storage period. In contrast, the control group showed increasing CFUs for all three pathogens. Specifically, the CFUs of LM and ST were significantly higher in the control than in the CFS-treated group at 5 d, and the CFUs of SA were significantly higher in the control at 3 d and 5 d. A core limitation of both the in vitro antibacterial assays and the pork preservation experiment is the absence of a pH-matched acidic control. The antimicrobial contributions of organic acids and non-acidic metabolites therefore cannot be disentangled. While organic acids are recognized as the primary drivers of CFS inhibitory activity, the potential synergistic effects of other bioactive metabolites cannot be quantified or confirmed in the current work. Further studies incorporating pH-matched controls and metabolite fractionation will be needed to clarify the specific antimicrobial and biopreservative mechanisms.

In the non-inoculated pork preservation experiment, no apparent deterioration was observed in samples treated with the 40% supernatant relative to the control during 7 days of storage at 4 °C (Fig. 5D). The total viable count (TVC) in the treated group remained stable throughout storage, increasing by less than 1.00 lg CFU/g, while the TVC in the control group gradually rose, reaching 7.69 lg CFU/g at 7 d (Fig. 5E). Colorimetric analysis (L, a, b) indicated that the CFS treatment showed not negatively affect the quality of the pork compared to the control, although all samples showed a declining color quality trend over time (Fig. 5F-H). Texture profile analysis revealed no significant differences between the treated and control groups (Table S8). The pH of the pork decreased slightly by 3 d before gradually increasing (Fig. 5I). The pH of the supernatant-treated group remained consistently lower than that of the control, likely due to the acidic nature of the CFS. The juice loss rate was higher in the treated group, possibly resulting from surface drying post-immersion (Fig. 5J). The TBARS (thiobarbituric acid reactive substances) value, a key indicator of lipid oxidation, showed no significant difference between the treated and control groups (Fig. 5K). Given the relatively low-fat content of the pork tenderloin used, lipid oxidation progressed slowly during storage. TBARS values increased only from 0.20 to 0.24 mg MDA/kg at 5 d to 0.36–0.37 mg MDA/kg at 7 d. This gradual increase can be attributed to combined environmental and microbial factors. However, the 40% (v/v) crude CFS dosage used in this study was designed for laboratory proof-of-concept purposes. To advance toward practical industrial application, further technical optimization is warranted, such as concentrating the CFS, identifying and purifying the core active components, developing stable formulations, and systematically evaluating production costs and process compatibility. The industrial potential of this strain will be assessed in future scale-up studies.

Conclusion

This study isolated and screened a panel of LAB strains from traditional Yangzhou pickles and Daqu, among which Pediococcus pentosaceus B1–3 exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity against three common foodborne pathogens. Phenotypic characterization showed that B1–3 presents homofermentative metabolism, tests negative for hemolytic and coagulase activities, is susceptible to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, tetracycline and ciprofloxacin, and tolerates 0.3% bile salts. Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis revealed no plasmids, no identifiable transferable antibiotic resistance genes, and no typical high-risk virulence factors in the genome, providing preliminary support for its application safety rather than conclusive safety confirmation. In fresh pork preservation assays, 40% (v/v) crude CFS suppressed the proliferation of both inoculated pathogens and indigenous microbiota, and caused no significant adverse effects on key quality parameters including surface color, texture profile and lipid oxidation level. Further work including active component purification, formulation optimization and scaled validation is required to support its practical industrial application. Overall, these findings provide a preliminary basis for developing B1–3 as a candidate natural meat biopreservative.
