 ABSTRACT
 This report presents findings from DOE Award DE-SC0021389, "Influence of
Nanoconfinement and Elevated Temperature on Geocolloid-Facilitated Transport of Energy-
Related Contaminants," directed by Prof. Younjin Min at UC Riverside. Energy production
activities introduce organic and inorganic contaminants into geological systems, where they
associate with submicron geocolloids migrating through nanometer-to-micrometer-scale fissures.
Under nanoconfinement and elevated temperatures, the transport, adsorption, and rheological
behavior of these systems diverge fundamentally from bulk behavior due to the dominance of
surface forces, electrostatic double-layer interactions, and steric constraints.
 Using the Surface Forces Apparatus as the principal platform, complemented by QCM-D,
AFM, SEM, DLS, laser Doppler electrophoresis, ATR-FTIR spectroscopy, Langmuir trough
techniques, and interfacial tensiometry, the project established that electrostatic double-layer
forces govern geocolloid confinement energetics in the silica-mica system, with measured Debye
lengths of approximately 20 to 24 nm consistent with DLVO theory. A Newtonian-to-non-
Newtonian rheological transition was discovered under nanoconfinement, with power-law
exponents reaching negative one indicative of a first-order solid-to-liquid phase transition,
representing the first direct observation in true nanosized colloidal suspensions below 100 nm.
Ionic strength was shown to mediate geocolloid jamming, forming solid fractal networks at
elevated salinities of 10 to 100 mM KCl while geocolloids are freely expelled in deionized water.
Size-dependent adsorption kinetics on silica, alumina, and aluminum silicate substrates revealed a
non-monotonic dependence explained by competing scaling of double-layer repulsion and van der
Waals attraction with particle diameter.
 Surface chemistry studies demonstrated that different organic contaminant functionalities
produce distinct adsorption behaviors governed by specific chemical interactions at sub-nanometer
length scales. Additional discoveries include two-step phase transitions in amphiphilic Janus
geocolloids at air-water interfaces and charge frustration phenomena governing ion ordering at
mineral surfaces published in Nature Materials. A comprehensive review establishing the
framework for geocolloid-facilitated versus geocolloid-hindered contaminant transport was also
published. Collectively, these findings provide mechanistic, predictive insights for assessing and
mitigating the environmental impact of energy production on subsurface geological systems.

1. PROJECT OBJECTIVES
1.1 Overarching Goal and Motivation
 The overarching goal of this project was to obtain a fundamental physical and chemical
understanding of how nanoconfinement, elevated temperatures, and geochemical conditions
influence the transport of energy-related contaminants in the presence of geocolloids. This
scientific challenge is central to the Department of Energy's mission of understanding contaminant
fate and transport in subsurface geological environments. Accurately modeling these dynamics is
critical for ensuring the safe, long-term sustainability of operations associated with energy
production, hydraulic fracturing, enhanced oil recovery, and nuclear waste isolation, as well as for
protecting vulnerable groundwater resources.
 A fundamental understanding of the transport of energy byproducts and related
contaminants in geological systems is critical for assessing and mitigating the environmental
impacts of energy production. Submicron-sized geocolloids—naturally occurring mineral particles
such as silica, clays, and metal oxides generated through diverse physical, chemical, and interfacial
weathering processes (Figure 1-1)—act as highly mobile facilitators for the accelerated subsurface
migration of highly sorbing contaminants including radionuclides (Pu, U, Th), heavy metals, and
persistent organic pollutants. These geocolloids can traverse distances far exceeding those
predicted by simple advection–dispersion models, precisely because they provide mobile solid
surfaces to which contaminants irreversibly bind through a variety of complex physicochemical
mechanisms, including surface adsorption, internal absorption, and swelling (Figure 1-2).

 The significance of geocolloid-facilitated transport has been established through landmark
field observations. Plutonium was detected in groundwater more than 1.3 km from its source at
the Nevada Test Site, colloidal actinides were found in surface waters far from the source at Rocky
Flats, and natural iron oxide nanoparticles facilitated Pu transport over 4 km at the Mayak
Production Association site in Russia. These observations demonstrate that geocolloid-facilitated
transport can accelerate contaminant migration by orders of magnitude relative to dissolved-phase
transport predictions.
 A critical barrier to predicting the migration of contaminant-laden colloids has been the
lack of fundamental understanding of their interfacial and rheological behaviors within
nanoconfined geological fissures, where pore dimensions approach the electrical double layer
thickness and the particle diameter itself. Bulk transport models fail in these environments because

Objective 1: Nanoconfinement Effects on Geocolloid–Contaminant Interactions
 Investigate how systematically varying degrees of nanoconfinement alter the adsorption
dynamics, kinetics, and thermodynamics of energy-related contaminants on geocolloids. Establish
whether adsorption isotherms undergo transformations (e.g., Langmuir to BET) as systems
transition from bulk to confined conditions. Directly measure interparticle interactions between
geocolloids using force spectroscopy techniques.
 From a fundamental perspective, this objective addresses the question of whether the
classical Derjaguin–Landau–Verwey–Overbeek (DLVO) theory—which accurately describes
colloidal interactions in bulk solution—remains valid when the confining geometry imposes
boundary conditions that restrict the double-layer overlap and enforce non-equilibrium particle
distributions. Under nanoconfinement, the electrical double layers emanating from both the
confining surfaces and the particle surfaces overlap, creating complex multi-body electrostatic
environments that cannot be reduced to simple pair potentials. This objective seeks to map these
interactions quantitatively using direct force measurement.

 The key scientific questions addressed under this objective include: (i) At what critical
surface separation (degree of confinement) do the force profiles of geocolloid suspensions deviate
from those predicted by the classical Poisson-Boltzmann equation for the pure electrolyte? (ii)
How does the presence of geocolloids modify the effective Debye length, surface potential, and
surface charge density of the confining mineral surfaces? (iii) What is the role of geocolloid
concentration in determining the onset and magnitude of non-DLVO forces under confinement?
Objective 2: Elevated Temperature Effects on Geocolloid–Contaminant Interactions
 Establish how elevated temperatures (25–100 °C) alter adsorption kinetics and
thermodynamics of contaminants on geocolloids using QCM-D and time-resolved batch sorption
assays. Determine temperature-induced changes in solvation shell structure, surface potential, and
van der Waals, electrostatic, dipole, and hydrogen bonding interactions.
 Temperature is a critical but frequently neglected variable in subsurface contaminant
transport. In deep geological repositories and during hydraulic fracturing operations, temperatures
can reach 100–200 °C, which profoundly alters multiple aspects of the colloid–contaminant–
mineral system. Elevated temperatures reduce the dielectric constant of water (from ~80 at 20 °C
to ~55 at 100 °C), which simultaneously weakens the electrostatic double-layer repulsion and
modifies the Hamaker constant governing van der Waals attractions. Additionally, thermal energy
alters the structure and dynamics of hydration layers at mineral surfaces, potentially disrupting the
short-range repulsive hydration forces that stabilize colloidal suspensions. The interplay of these
competing temperature-dependent effects creates a complex thermodynamic landscape that has
not been systematically explored for geocolloid systems.
Objective 3: Nanoconfinement Effects on Geocolloid Rheology and Flow
 Establish the critical confinement length scale at which viscosity and rheological properties
of geocolloid–contaminant suspensions deviate from bulk behavior. Determine how the absolute
and relative magnitudes of storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) vary as functions of shear
rate and confinement using the SFA in shear rheology mode. Map the transition from linear to
nonlinear rheological behavior.
 The rheological behavior of colloidal suspensions under nanoconfinement represents one
of the most poorly understood aspects of subsurface contaminant transport. In bulk solution, dilute
geocolloid suspensions behave as Newtonian fluids with viscosities close to that of the carrier
fluid. However, as these suspensions are forced into nanoscale fissures, the ratio of particle
diameter to confinement gap approaches unity, and the suspension enters a regime where
continuum mechanics breaks down. The effective volume fraction increases dramatically due to
excluded volume effects, potentially triggering glass transitions, jamming, and the emergence of
yield stress—transforming the suspension from a flowing liquid into a confined solid.
 Specific scientific questions addressed under this objective include: (i) At what critical
length scale do colloidal systems begin to display nonlinear viscosity? (ii) How does ionic strength
alter the relaxation dynamics and viscosity of colloidal suspensions under nanoconfinement? (iii)
In the nanorheological dynamics of colloids, what is the relative importance of pertinent length
scales such as particle diameter, confinement level (separation distance), and Debye length? (iv)
How are colloidal forces interrelated with lateral and shear forces under nanoconfinement,
specifically at compatible length scales?
Objective 4: Anomalous Diffusion under Nanoconfinement
 Determine whether Fickian diffusion behavior transitions to anomalous diffusion
(superdiffusion or subdiffusion) under nanoconfinement. Develop an SFA–FRAP (fluorescence
recovery after photobleaching) instrument to measure diffusion dynamics. Investigate how

contaminant-induced asymmetry on geocolloids (Janus particles) introduces torque and deviations
from random-walk behavior.
 In bulk suspensions, colloidal diffusion follows the Stokes–Einstein relation, yielding a
mean-squared displacement that scales linearly with time (⟨r²⟩ ∝ t). Under nanoconfinement,
however, several mechanisms can drive departures from this Fickian behavior. Hydrodynamic
interactions with confining walls increase the effective drag coefficient anisotropically,
suppressing diffusion in the direction normal to the walls while potentially enhancing in-plane
mobility. Furthermore, when geocolloids bear heterogeneous (Janus-type) surface chemistry from
partial contaminant adsorption, the asymmetric distribution of hydrodynamic and electrostatic
forces can generate persistent torques that couple translational and rotational diffusion, leading to
anomalous transport signatures.
2. TECHNICAL APPROACH AND METHODS
2.1 Model Geocolloid Synthesis and Characterization
 The foundation of this project was the development of well-defined model systems that
capture the essential physics and chemistry of natural geocolloids while enabling precise
parametric control. Silica nanoparticles (SNPs) were selected as model geocolloids due to their
geological relevance, (i.e., silica is one of the most abundant minerals in the Earth's crust and a
primary constituent of natural geocolloids) and the ability to control their size, shape, and surface
chemistry with high precision through established sol–gel methodologies.
Two complementary synthesis strategies were developed to produce monodisperse silica
geocolloids spanning the range of 20–500 nm, which encompasses the size range most relevant to
colloid-facilitated transport in geological media (Figure 2-1). For particles < 150 nm (seed
regrowth approach), silica nanoparticles were synthesized via an environmentally friendly, water-
based route using L-arginine as a base catalyst and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) in a
cyclohexane-in-water emulsion system, following Hartlen et al. The mechanism involves the
controlled heterogeneous hydrolysis and condensation of TEOS at the oil–water interface,
followed by diffusion of hydrolyzed silica monomers into the aqueous phase where they deposit
onto seed particles. The synthesis proceeds in three stages: 1. Seed generation: A cyclohexane oil
phase (0.45 mL) was layered on top of an aqueous phase (7.5 mM L-arginine in Milli-Q water)
and equilibrated at 60 °C. TEOS (2.5 mmol) was added to the oil phase, and the reaction was
stirred at 60 rpm—gentle enough to maintain the layered oil–water separation. The slow hydrolysis
of TEOS at the oil–water interface, catalyzed by L-arginine at the aqueous side, produced silica
seeds of approximately 24 nm diameter. 2. Controlled seed growth: 1 mL of seed suspension was
diluted with 3.6 mL of Milli-Q water, and 0.352 mL TEOS dissolved in 0.5 mL cyclohexane was
layered as the oil phase. The reaction proceeded at 60 °C for 30 h with 60 rpm stirring, growing
seeds to approximately 50 nm. 3. Purification: The oil phase was removed by pipette, and the
aqueous phase was dialyzed against 100× its volume of Milli-Q water at room temperature under
constant stirring for 5 days (water changed 10 times) to remove residual L-arginine. This approach
yielded particles with a linear size–time dependence (zeroth-order kinetics), indicating that the
rate-limiting step is the interfacial hydrolysis of TEOS rather than monomer deposition. The
zeroth-order kinetics are particularly advantageous for precise size control because the growth rate
is independent of particle size, minimizing Ostwald ripening and maintaining narrow
polydispersity throughout the synthesis. For particles > 150 nm (TEOS addition-dilution
approach), the method involves periodic addition of fresh TEOS to growing seed suspensions,
followed by dilution to maintain the monomer concentration below the nucleation threshold. This
approach yielded highly spherical particles with exponential size–time dependence (first-order

2.2 Anisotropic Surface Modification with Model Contaminants
 In natural geological settings, contaminant adsorption on geocolloids is inherently
heterogeneous and anisotropic. Organic pollutants from hydraulic fracturing fluids, coal mine
drainage, and enhanced oil recovery operations adsorb preferentially on certain crystal faces or
surface sites, producing patchy, Janus-type surface chemistry. To faithfully simulate this
heterogeneity in the laboratory, silica particles were anisotropically modified using two
complementary approaches.
 A scalable method was developed to fabricate true amphiphilic Janus nanoparticles with a
defined 50:50 hydrophilic–lipophilic balance (HLB). The fabrication proceeded in four stages: 1.
Monolayer deposition: A colloidal monolayer of SNPs was flow-coated onto a 50-nm-thick
poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) film using a custom doctor-blade system, producing a
uniform, close-packed particle array. 2. Controlled embedding: By heating the substrate above the
PMMA glass transition temperature (Tg ≈ 102 °C) to 115 °C for 150–200 seconds, the SNPs sank
precisely 50% into the softened polymer matrix through capillary and gravitational forces. The
embedding depth was controlled by the heating time and temperature which govern the viscosity
of the softened PMMA (Figure 2-2). 3. Selective functionalization: The exposed hemispheres were
subjected to chemical vapor deposition (CVD) of chlorotrimethylsilane (CTMS), which reacts
with surface silanol groups via a condensation mechanism: –Si–OH + Cl–Si(CH3)3 → –Si–O–
Si(CH3)3 + HCl. This reaction forms covalent Si–O–Si bonds, creating a stable hydrophobic patch
on the exposed hemisphere. 4. Template dissolution: Following dissolution of the PMMA template
in acetone, true amphiphilic Janus nanoparticles were liberated with one hydrophilic (bare silica)
hemisphere and one hydrophobic (CTMS-modified) hemisphere.
 Attenuated total reflectance Fourier-transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy
confirmed CTMS chemisorption via the emergence of asymmetric and symmetric CH₃ stretching
peaks at 2963 cm⁻¹ and 2900 cm⁻¹. Contact angle goniometry confirmed the successful transition
from highly hydrophilic bare SNPs (1.9° contact angle) to amphiphilic Janus geometries (~40.8°),
providing a pristine model to study asymmetric contaminant transport.

2.3 Nanoporous Confinement Templates
 Porous anodic alumina (PAA) membranes were fabricated via a two-step anodization
process to serve as model confining geometries with well-defined, narrowly distributed pore sizes.
High-purity aluminum foils were first electropolished to mirror-smooth surfaces, then subjected to
a first anodization in oxalic acid under controlled DC bias to produce a sacrificial disordered pore
array. After selective dissolution to expose the ordered dimple pattern imprinted in the underlying
aluminum, a second anodization using this pattern as a template produced highly ordered
hexagonal pore arrays with narrow size distribution. Pore diameters were precisely tuned by
adjusting the DC bias voltage (Figure 2-4), exhibiting a near-linear relationship with voltage
below 50 V due to the proportional increase in anodization current density governing the rate of
alumina dissolution at the pore base. At higher voltages, this relationship becomes sublinear,
reflecting space-charge-limited ionic transport within the thickening barrier layer and steric
crowding effects restricting ionic mobility.

voltages (80 V, 60 V, 40 V, and 20 V), illustrating the highly ordered hexagonal pore structures and tunable
diameters. (b) Plot of pore diameter as a function of applied voltage, demonstrating a linear dependence
at lower voltages that transitions to a sublinear regime above 50 V due to space-charge-limited ionic

2.4 Surface Forces Apparatus (SFA) Measurements
 The SFA 2000 (SurForce LLC, Santa Barbara, CA) served as the primary instrument for direct
measurement of intermolecular and surface forces, rheological properties, and optical
characteristics of geocolloid suspensions under precisely controlled nanoconfinement (Figure 2-
5). The SFA is uniquely suited to this investigation because it provides simultaneous access to 1.
Force–distance profiles with sub-angstrom resolution in surface separation, 2. Shear and
compression rheology modes for measuring viscosity, storage modulus (G′), and loss modulus
(G″) at predetermined separations, and 3. Real-time refractive index measurements through
multiple-beam interferometry (MBI) for in situ determination of particle volume fraction and
spatial density under confinement.
2.4.1 Mica Surface Preparation
 Muscovite ruby mica was selected as the model mineral substrate because its layered
aluminosilicate structure is an excellent analogue for natural clay minerals and crystalline rock
walls in subsurface fractures. Mica can be cleaved along its basal plane to produce atomically

 11

smooth surfaces, making it ideal for isolating and quantifying nanoscale surface forces without
confounding effects of roughness.
 Substrate preparation was conducted under strictly controlled conditions. Mica blocks were
hand-cleaved in a laminar flow hood, and thin, defect-free sheets (0.7 to 5 µm thick, 5 to 10 cm2)
were selected, melt-cut, and edge-cauterized with a hot platinum wire. Sheets were positioned
upwind of the wire to prevent platinum nanoparticle deposition. The experimental surface was
protected with a pristine mica backing sheet held by van der Waals adhesion, while the opposite
side was coated with a 45 nm silver mirror via e-beam deposition for optical interferometry. Back-
silvered sheets were cut to approximately 1 square centimeter and glued silvered-side down onto
cylindrical quartz lenses using epoxy optical adhesive.
 For SFA measurements, two supported mica surfaces cut from the same parent sheet were
mounted in a cross-cylindrical geometry, forming a symmetric three-layer optical interferometer.
The chamber was purged with filtered dry nitrogen for 30 minutes to dehydrate the surfaces, which
were then brought into adhesive contact under high normal load. The resulting Fringes of Equal
Chromatic Order were recorded by spectrometer to establish the absolute zero-distance reference
for all subsequent separation measurements.

2.4.2 Force–Distance Profile Measurement
 All SFA experiments were performed at room temperature (21 ± 1 °C). Before experiments,
the two mica surfaces were separated to around 1 mm apart and a 15 μL droplet of aqueous solution
or particle suspension was injected in the gap between the opposing surfaces. The surface
separation distance was then manually decreased to around 10 μm and allowed to reach
equilibrium for 30 min. Upon equilibrium, force measurements were performed in a dynamic
manner where the bottom surface was driven using a step-motor to approach the top surface at a
rate of 1 to 2 nm/s in particle suspensions and 0.5 nm/s in aqueous solutions without silica
particles—rates slow enough to neglect contributions from hydrodynamic forces. The force-
induced deflection of the cantilever springs (Δx) was identified from separation distance changes
using the FECO at angstrom resolution. The interfacial force was then calculated according to
Hooke's Law: F = K × Δx, where K is the spring constant (1080 N/m for force–distance
measurements). Spring constant values were calibrated before and after each set of experiments.
2.4.3 Compressive Coupling Rheology Measurement
 Viscosity measurements at separation distances larger than the hardwall were studied using
a compressive rheology method where the top surface is driven by a piezoelectric tube to
sinusoidally oscillate at a small amplitude (AyI) around an average separation distance (Dave). The

bottom surface attached to the cantilever force spring couples with the oscillation due to the
hydrodynamic response of the confined liquid. The effective viscosity was calculated using:
ηeff = (K·Dave / 12π²R²ν) × √[(AyI/Ayo) − (1 − f/K)²], where ν is the oscillation frequency (varied
from 0.3 to 3 Hz at each Dave), R is the effective radius of curvature, and f = dF(D)/dD. The input
amplitude was kept below 10% of Dave to avoid disturbing the original fluid structures.
2.4.4 Lateral Shear Rheology Measurement
 At D ~ 65 nm, surfaces were compressed and flattened under high pressure (~1.3 MPa).
The bottom surface was driven by a piezoelectric bimorph to oscillate sinusoidally in the lateral
direction. Shear force (F∥) was detected by a piezoelectric double cantilever spring attached to the
top surface. The effective shear moduli and viscosity were obtained from:
Geff = σ/γ = (F∥/AC)/(ΔA/D), G′ = Geff × cos(δ) = ωη″, G″ = Geff × sin(δ) = ωη′, and ηeff = √[(G″/ω)²
+ (G′/ω)²], where σ is shear stress, γ is shear strain, δ is the phase angle between input strain and
output stress, and ω is angular frequency. Strain was kept within the linear viscoelasticity region.
2.5 Quartz Crystal Microbalance with Dissipation Monitoring (QCM-D)
 Adsorption kinetics and thermodynamics of geocolloids on mineral substrates were
measured using QCM-D in both static and dynamic flow modes. The QCM-D technique exploits
the inverse piezoelectric effect: an AT-cut quartz crystal oscillates at its resonant frequency (~5
MHz for the fundamental mode) when driven by an alternating electric field, and mass adsorption
on the crystal surface decreases the resonant frequency according to the Sauerbrey equation:
Δf = −2f₀²Δm / [A√(μρ)], where f₀ is the fundamental resonant frequency, Δm is the adsorbed mass,
A is the active crystal area, and μ and ρ are the shear modulus and density of quartz. Simultaneous
monitoring of the dissipation factor (ΔD) provides information about the viscoelastic character of
the adsorbed layer—rigid layers produce small ΔD values, while soft, hydrated layers produce
larger ΔD. Mineral-coated QCM sensors (SiO2, Al2O3, and Al2SiO5) enabled systematic
investigation of geocolloid–substrate interactions as a function of particle size (40, 100, and 400
nm), surface chemistry, and ionic conditions. The nanogram-level mass sensitivity of QCM-D
provides quantitative access to adsorption kinetics, equilibrium surface coverage, and activation
energy barriers that govern geocolloid deposition on geological substrates (Figure 2-6).

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1 Synthesis and Characterization of Model Geocolloids and Confining Templates
 Monodisperse silica geocolloids with tunable diameters spanning 20 to 500 nm were
synthesized via an aqueous-phase method involving L-arginine-catalyzed TEOS hydrolysis for
seed generation, controlled seeded growth, and purification through repeated ultracentrifugation
and redispersion. Morphological uniformity and narrow polydispersity were confirmed by

3.2 Direct Force Measurements of Geocolloids under Nanoconfinement
 An important accomplishment of this project was the systematic measurement of
intermolecular colloidal forces between two atomically smooth mica surfaces across aqueous
media containing 50 nm silica geocolloids using the SFA. Force–distance profiles were measured
as a function of three key parameters: the degree of confinement (surface separation distance, D),
geocolloid concentration (0, 0.01, and 0.1 wt%), and salinity (0, 10, and 100 mM KCl). These
parameters were selected to span the range of conditions encountered in natural geological
environments: from dilute, low-salinity shallow groundwaters to concentrated, high-salinity brines
found in deep formations and near injection wells.

3.2.2 Salinity-Induced Jamming and Percolation
 As bulk salinity increased to 10 and 100 mM KCl, the force-distance profiles transformed
dramatically. The onset of long-range repulsion was attenuated to smaller separations of
approximately 10 nm and 5 nm, respectively, as negative surface potentials became heavily
screened by potassium counterions. Short-range hydration forces emerged in both regimes,
precluding primary minimum adhesion and yielding finite hard-wall contact distances of 0.4 nm
and 0.7 nm, corresponding to one or two rigid layers of hydrated ions resisting mechanical
extrusion. The introduction of silica nanoparticles into these screened environments produced
pronounced deviations from exponential decay in the force-distance profiles, exhibiting linearized
trajectories indicative of non-DLVO interactions (Figure 3-4). Background electrolytes mitigated
electrostatic repulsion among SNPs and between SNPs and mica, inducing localized colloidal
aggregation and extending the effective range of measured repulsions. Increases in hard-wall
distances with escalating ion and SNP concentrations corresponded to entrapment of one or two
distinct layers of nanoparticles between the mica surfaces.

3.3 Rheological Properties of Geocolloids under Nanoconfinement
 A major scientific contribution of this project was the discovery and systematic
characterization of the Newtonian-to-non-Newtonian rheological transition of geocolloid
suspensions under nanoconfinement. This work provides, for the first time, direct measurements
of how true nanosized colloidal suspensions behave rheologically when forced into nanoscale
geometries - a regime that is highly relevant to geological contaminant transport but has been
experimentally inaccessible until now.
3.3.1 Effective Viscosity under Nanoconfinement
 The effective viscosity of SNP suspensions was measured as a function of surface
separation distance using the compressive coupling method for moderate confinement (D = 65–
500 nm, ν = 1 Hz) and the lateral shear mode for tight confinement (D ≈ 65 nm, under 1.3 MPa
pressure) (Figure 3-6). In Milli-Q water (0 mM KCl), all ηeff values remained constant at the value
for pure water (ηwater ≈ 0.89 mPa·s at 25 °C) regardless of SNP concentration and across all
separation distances. This result is fully consistent with the force measurements and refractive
index data showing complete particle expulsion, confirming that in low-ionic-strength
environments, the presence of geocolloids does not alter the rheological properties of the confined
fluid.

3.3.2 Viscoelastic Behavior: Storage and Loss Moduli
 At D ~ 65 nm (approximately one layer of SNPs forced between mica surfaces under 1.3
MPa compressive pressure), frequency-sweep measurements using the lateral shear method
revealed noticeable glassy responses. Four different SNP suspensions with varying concentrations
of SNPs and ions displayed similar shear responses (Figure 3-7). G′(ω) had a weak dependence
on ω, whereas G″(ω) increased with ω, leading to crossover frequencies (ωcross, inversely
proportional to the system's relaxation time τ) occurring at high frequencies. When the system was
compressed, G′(ω) ≫ G″(ω), indicating that shear-induced perturbations could not be fully relaxed
during oscillation over the accessible frequency range.

 The dominance of the storage modulus indicates the confined suspension behaves as an
amorphous solid, storing elastic energy rather than dissipating it viscously. Despite volume
fractions below 15%, confinement to approximately 65 nm significantly restricted SNP motion
and retarded relaxation. This solid-like behavior originates from a percolated network of
aggregated nanoparticles: at high salinity and tight confinement, van der Waals attraction, no
longer screened by long-range electrostatic repulsion, drives fractal network formation spanning
the confining gap. The network stores elastic energy through bending and stretching of interparticle
bonds, while dissipation occurs through irreversible bond breaking and reformation. The finding
that the storage modulus greatly exceeds the loss modulus across the entire frequency range
confirms that the network relaxation time exceeds the longest experimental timescale, placing the
confined suspension in a non-ergodic, glassy state.

3.3.3 Shear-Thinning Behavior and Dynamic Yield Stress
 The shear rate dependence of ηeff at multiple separation distances revealed a systematic
Newtonian-to-shear-thinning transition with increasing confinement (Figure 3-8). All four SNP
suspension conditions followed the Ostwald–de Waele power-law equation: ηeff = Af · γ̇ m-1 where
γ̇ is shear rate, Af is the flow consistency index (in Pa·sm), and m is the power-law exponent.

3.4 Adsorption Dynamics of Geocolloids on Mineral Surfaces
 Understanding the interaction between geocolloids and mineral surfaces is essential for
predicting contaminant deposition and retention in geological media. Using QCM-D, we
systematically investigated the adsorption kinetics and thermodynamics of silica geocolloids on
three geologically relevant mineral substrates: SiO2 (silica, ζ = –41 mV), Al2O3 (alumina, ζ = +28
mV), and Al2SiO5 (aluminum silicate, ζ = –14 mV). These substrates represent the major classes
of mineral surfaces encountered in subsurface geological environments: silica for quartz-
dominated sandstones, alumina for lateritic and bauxitic formations, and aluminum silicate for
clay-rich shales and phyllosilicate minerals.
3.4.1 Size-Dependent Adsorption on Silica Substrates
 Three specific geocolloid sizes (40, 100, and 400 nm) were used to investigate how particle
size affects adsorption kinetics and equilibrium surface coverage (Figure 3-9). On the silica
substrates, the adsorption followed first-order exponential kinetics. This is consistent with a
barrier-limited process, which can be described by Kramers’ rate theory. Interestingly, the
equilibrium surface concentration did not scale linearly with particle size; instead, it showed a non-
monotonic relationship (Figure 3-10). As summarized in Table 3-2, the 40 nm particles achieved
the highest surface concentration (46.7 ng/mm²) due to a relatively low activation energy barrier
(2.8 kBT). However, the 100 nm particles experienced the highest energy barrier (10.1 kBT),
resulting in the lowest surface coverage (4.0 ng/mm²). The 400 nm particles fell in the middle,
demonstrating that intermediate particle sizes can face the strongest repulsion forces during the
adsorption process.

electrostatic asymmetry between the mineral wall and the negatively charged silica geocolloids.
The temporal trajectory to reach steady-state surface coverage was substantially truncated
compared to the silica systems (Figure 3-11a). This rapid kinetic onset is thermodynamically
justified by the DLVO interaction potential profiles, which reveal a complete absence of the

3.4.3 Effect of Contaminant Patches on Adsorption
 The presence of nonpolar contaminant patches (trimethylsilane groups from CTMS vapor
deposition) on geocolloids systematically decreased adsorption on silica substrates for all particle
sizes (Figure 3-12). Two mechanisms contribute to this behavior. First, surface charge is
attenuated because the organic patches mask silanol groups and reduce the magnitude of the zeta
potential, thereby altering the electrostatic component of the DLVO interaction (Reduced zeta
potential). Second, the van der Waals attraction is weakened because the organic domains are less
polarizable than silica, which lowers the effective Hamaker constant for the particle–water–silica
configuration from 1.7 × 10⁻²⁰ J (silica–water–silica) to 0.9 × 10⁻²⁰ J, representing a 47 percent
decrease (Reduced Hamaker constant). Collectively, these changes increase the effective
interaction energy barrier to attachment, resulting in diminished adsorption. (Table 3-3).

3.5 Influence of Geocolloid Surface Chemistry on Adsorption Dynamics
 To systematically investigate how specific contaminant functionalities dictate geocolloid–
mineral interactions, the model silica colloids were covalently modified with five distinct organic
terminal groups: amine (–NH₂), thiol (–SH), phenyl (–C₆H₅), hexyl (–C₆H₁₃), and vinyl (–
CH=CH₂). The resulting adsorption kinetics and equilibrium surface coverages were quantitatively
monitored via Quartz Crystal Microbalance with Dissipation (QCM-D) (Figure 3-13), while the
terminal topographical arrangements of these deposited layers were visually corroborated using
Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) (Figure 3-14). On alumina substrates (ζ = +28 mV), thiol-
functionalized geocolloids exhibited significantly enhanced adsorption due to attractive
interactions between partially negative thiol groups and positive alumina. Amine-functionalized
geocolloids (protonated at neutral pH, ζ = +7.2 mV) showed the lowest adsorption due to
electrostatic repulsion with positively charged alumina. Surprisingly, phenyl- and hexyl-
functionalized geocolloids showed reduced adsorption despite bearing larger negative zeta
potentials that would predict enhanced electrostatic attraction. This discrepancy reveals that non-

3.6 Interfacial Behavior of Geocolloids at Two-Phase Boundaries
 An important extension addressed geocolloid behavior at fluid-fluid interfaces relevant to
multiphase flow in geological systems. Pressure-area isotherms and interfacial rheology were
measured using Langmuir trough and oscillatory pendant drop techniques. Hydrophilic bare SNPs
displayed fluid-like compliance with low collapse pressures of approximately 27 mN/m due to
weak interfacial attachment, while fully silanized hydrophobic SNPs formed rigid crystalline
structures with high collapse pressures of approximately 70.9 mN/m, reflecting much larger
desorption energies. Amphiphilic Janus particles uniquely exhibited an anomalous two-step phase
transition: first an orientational transition at moderate compression where particles reorient to align
hydrophobic and hydrophilic hemispheres with the respective phases, followed by a structural
transition into close-packed monolayers at higher compression. Dilational rheology confirmed that
Janus colloids store interfacial elastic energy far more effectively than homogeneous particles,
maintaining rigid elasticity under cyclic perturbations due to an additional orientational degree of
freedom that allows accommodation of area changes through rotation rather than desorption. The
practical implication is that geocolloids bearing patchy contaminant coatings can more effectively
stabilize foams, emulsions, and multiphase flow structures within geological formations.

3.7 Charge Frustration and Ion Ordering at Mineral Surfaces
 A significant collaborative outcome with Dr. James De Yoreo and colleagues at Pacific
Northwest National Laboratory was the discovery of charge frustration phenomena governing ion
ordering at charged mineral surfaces, bridging geocolloid-scale interactions with molecular-scale
processes controlling mineral-contaminant binding. Using molecularly resolved AFM combined
with SFA measurements, the team demonstrated that while divalent ions (Ba2+, Mg2+) form
continuous hydroxide monolayers consistent with classical adsorption models, trivalent ions (Al3+,
Fe3+) adopt complex states including ordered ion networks, cluster arrays, and microphase-
separated films with no analog in classical double-layer theory. These complex states emerge from
charge frustration, where repelling like charges cannot simultaneously minimize all pairwise
electrostatic interactions on a triangular lattice dictated by the mica surface structure, producing

geometrically frustrated ground states analogous to antiferromagnetic materials. Published in
Nature Materials, this work provides general principles for charge-driven nanostructure formation
at mineral surfaces, with direct relevance to geocolloid transport: ion ordering structures modify
surface charge and wettability of geological substrates, altering conditions for geocolloid
deposition and release, and the charge frustration mechanism may apply to adsorption of
multivalent contaminant ions in nuclear waste repositories.
4. BROADER SCIENTIFIC IMPLICATIONS AND SIGNIFICANCE
 The results obtained in this project have implications that extend well beyond the specific
system of silica geocolloids in KCl solutions confined between mica surfaces. The findings
establish several general scientific principles relevant to subsurface contaminant transport,
colloidal physics, and geosciences (Figure 4-1).
Electrostatic control of geocolloid confinement provides a predictive framework for
contaminant mobility. The demonstration that electrostatic double-layer forces control geocolloid
confinement energetics, with a sharp transition between expulsion (low salinity) and jamming
(high salinity), means that groundwater salinity—which varies from <1 mM in shallow freshwater
aquifers to >1 M in deep brines—is the primary determinant of whether geocolloid-facilitated
transport will accelerate or retard contaminant migration. This principle enables salinity-based risk
assessment for geological formations near energy production sites and provides a scientific basis
for engineering saline barriers to impede contaminant migration.
Non-Newtonian rheology under confinement challenges conventional transport models. The
discovery of the Newtonian-to-non-Newtonian rheological transition, including the emergence of
dynamic yield stress, fundamentally challenges the assumptions of standard advection–dispersion
models that treat pore fluids as Newtonian liquids with constant viscosity. Geocolloid-laden fluids
in nanoscale pores can be orders of magnitude more viscous than bulk fluid and can exhibit solid-
like yield stress behavior preventing flow entirely below critical thresholds. Incorporating these
effects will require new constitutive equations for non-Newtonian colloidal suspensions under
confinement and multi-scale modeling approaches coupling pore-scale rheology with reservoir-
scale flow simulations.
Contaminant-enhanced geocolloid mobility creates positive feedback. The observation that
organic contaminant patches reduce van der Waals attraction to pore walls identifies a previously
unrecognized positive feedback mechanism. In contaminated aquifers, progressive organic
pollutant adsorption onto geocolloid surfaces increases their transport range, potentially spreading
contamination farther and faster than models assuming constant geocolloid–wall interactions
predict. This has direct implications for risk assessment near hydraulic fracturing operations.
Charge frustration at mineral surfaces reveals new binding mechanisms. The discovery of
charge frustration provides a new mechanism for nanoscale surface structure formation that
influences both geocolloid adhesion and contaminant binding. The complex ion ordering patterns
formed by trivalent ions represent a departure from simple Stern layer models, suggesting that
atomic-scale mineral–water interface structure plays a more important role in controlling
macroscopic transport than previously appreciated.
Janus geocolloid interfacial behavior affects multiphase transport. The anomalous two-step
phase transition and enhanced interfacial elasticity of amphiphilic Janus geocolloids demonstrate
that heterogeneous contaminant loading fundamentally alters geocolloid behavior at fluid–fluid
interfaces. This has implications for multiphase flow in fractured rock, CO₂ sequestration
reservoirs, and vadose zone transport where air–water and oil–water interfaces are prevalent.
