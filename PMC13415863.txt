Integrated biomarkers of oxidative stress NLRP3 inflammasome and kynurenine pathway reflect disease severity and diagnostic discrimination in depression
Authors: Hayam Ali AlRasheed, Aya A. El-Hanafy, Mostafa M. Bahaa, Eman Hamza, Mostafa M. Kamel, Doaa A. El-Hanafy, Nashwa Eltantawy, Mohamed Yasser, Reham A. Al-Dhelaan, Kholoud H. Radwan, Abeer A. El-Sayed, Mazin M. Eladaroussy, Kamal A. Kamaleldein Rizk, Mohamed H. E. Ebrahim, Nora Elshorbagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Depression is associated with dysregulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways. Serum biomarkers such as 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), NLRP3, and kynurenine may reflect these pathological processes and relate to disease severity. This study evaluated the clinical relevance of these biomarkers in patients with depression and their association with symptom severity. A total of 50 patients with depression and 50 healthy controls were assessed. Serum levels of 8-OHdG, NLRP3, and kynurenine were measured. Depression severity was evaluated using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Correlations, multiple linear regression, and multivariate logistic regression. Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg corrections were applied. Diagnostic performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Patients with depression exhibited significantly higher levels of 8-OHdG, NLRP3, and kynurenine (all p < 0.05). Among the evaluated biomarkers, only 8-OHdG demonstrated a weak inverse correlation with PHQ-9 scores and a positive correlation with disease duration, whereas no significant associations were observed for other markers. In multivariable regression analyses, 8-OHdG showed nominal association with PHQ-9 scores; however, this relationship did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Multivariate logistic regression demonstrated independent associations of all three biomarkers with depression status without evidence of significant multicollinearity. ROC analysis showed moderate diagnostic performance for individual biomarkers, while the combined biomarker model achieved superior discrimination between patients and controls (AUC = 0.857). Serum levels of kynurenine, NLRP3, and 8-OHdG are all markedly increased in depression and together offer helpful diagnostic data. These indicators’ combined performance implies potential utility as complementary biological markers for diagnosing depression and describing its underlying pathophysiology, notwithstanding their modest relationships with symptom severity. Introduction

Depression is a common and disabling psychiatric condition marked by persistent poor mood, loss of interest, cognitive impairment, and functional decline. Depression impacts more than 300 million people worldwide and continues to be one of the primary causes of years lived with disability1,2. Despite its high burden, the diagnosis of depression continues to rely primarily on subjective clinical interviews and self-reported scales such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)3. The absence of objective biological markers limits diagnostic precision, contributes to under-diagnosis, and complicates the monitoring of disease progression or treatment response4.

The pathophysiology of depression is complex and multifactorial, involving disturbances in neurotransmitter systems, neurotrophic signaling, immune function, and oxidative balance5. Classical theories focused mainly on monoamine dysfunction; however, contemporary evidence shows that depressive disorders arise from a combination of genetic, environmental, neuroendocrine, and immunological mechanisms6. Dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, reduced neurogenesis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired synaptic plasticity have all been implicated in the development of depressive symptoms7.

A major body of research now supports the involvement of neuroinflammation in depressive disorders. Elevated inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP have been consistently reported in patients with major depression8. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome has emerged as a key upstream mechanism linking stress, immune activation, and neuronal dysfunction. Stress-induced release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and mitochondrial signals can activate NLRP3, leading to caspase-1 activation, maturation of IL-1β and IL-18, and subsequent neuroinflammatory cascades9. These processes impair monoaminergic transmission, alter glutamate signaling, and contribute to hippocampal atrophy, collectively driving depressive symptomatology10.

Oxidative stress is another important pathological component of depression. Increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) and impaired antioxidant defenses contribute to neuronal injury, mitochondrial damage, and apoptosis11. 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) is a widely recognized biomarker of oxidative DNA damage and has been found to be elevated in several psychiatric and stress-related conditions. Higher levels of 8-OHdG may reflect chronic oxidative burden and reduced neuroprotective capacity in depressed individuals12.

Activation of inflammatory pathways also shifts tryptophan metabolism from serotonin synthesis toward the kynurenine pathway13. Elevated circulating kynurenine may favor the production of neurotoxic metabolites such as 3-hydroxykynurenine and quinolinic acid, which contribute to glutamate excitotoxicity, microglial activation, and behavioral disturbances14. Alterations in the kynurenine/tryptophan ratio have been proposed as potential biomarkers for both inflammation-associated and treatment-resistant depression15.

Taken together, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation represent interconnected biological pathways underpinning depressive pathology16. Measuring serum biomarkers reflecting these mechanisms may support adjunctive biomarker-based assessment tools to complement clinical assessment17. Identifying inflammatory and oxidative markers associated with depression severity could enhance diagnostic accuracy, support early detection, and improve risk stratification in clinical settings18. Despite growing evidence, few studies have simultaneously evaluated the interplay between NLRP3 activation, oxidative DNA damage markers (8-OHdG), and kynurenine metabolism in relation to standardized depression severity scores such as PHQ-919–21.

This study is based on the hypothesis that depressive disorder is accompanied by measurable biological alterations, specifically heightened neuroinflammation, oxidative DNA damage, and dysregulation of the kynurenine metabolic pathway. We propose that patients with higher PHQ-9 depression scores will exhibit increased serum concentrations of NLRP3, reflecting inflammasome activation; elevated levels of 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), indicating oxidative DNA injury; and higher kynurenine levels, indicating inflammation-driven metabolic shifts. We further hypothesize that these biomarkers will correlate positively with the severity of depressive symptoms, suggesting their potential utility as adjunctive biomarker-based assessment tool. Accordingly, the aim of the present study is to measure serum NLRP3, 8-OHdG, and kynurenine levels in depressive patients and healthy controls, evaluate depression severity using the PHQ-9 scale, and examine the associations between biomarker levels and depression severity to determine whether these markers can serve as reliable, biologically meaningful adjuncts to clinical assessment.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted at the Outpatient Psychiatry Clinic of Tanta University Hospital, Egypt, over a 20-month period between January 2024 and August 2025. Depressive patients attending the clinic for assessment or follow-up were consecutively screened for eligibility. Age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers were recruited from hospital staff and relatives of patients to serve as the control group. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Faculty of Medicine, Tanta University. All procedures complied with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and national research regulations. Participant confidentiality and data protection were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Inclusion criteria

Participants were recruited according to predefined eligibility criteria to ensure the validity of comparisons between depressed patients and healthy controls. Individuals in the depression group were included if they were between 18 and 65 years of age and fulfilled DSM-5 criteria for Major Depressive Disorder, confirmed using a structured psychiatric interview conducted by trained psychiatrists22. Patients were eligible if they were newly diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder or had not received antidepressant therapy for at least four weeks before enrollment and who had a PHQ-9 score of 10 or higher were considered eligible, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Healthy control subjects were required to be within the same age range, free from any current or past psychiatric disorders, to have a PHQ-9 score below 5, and to have no history of chronic inflammatory, autoimmune, metabolic, or neurological diseases.

Exclusion criteria

For both groups, individuals were excluded if they had any acute infection during the preceding four weeks; any chronic medical illness known to influence inflammatory or oxidative biomarkers, including diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, autoimmune disorders, liver or kidney dysfunction, or malignancy; or if they were using anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents, antioxidant supplements, or omega-3 fatty acids within the last month. Additional exclusion criteria included pregnancy or lactation, substance abuse or dependence apart from nicotine, major neurological diseases such as epilepsy, stroke, or traumatic brain injury, and any condition that would interfere with completing the clinical assessment. Participants who did not provide informed consent or who were unable to comply with the study procedures were also excluded.

Clinical assessment

Clinical assessment of depressive symptoms was performed using both self-reported and clinician-rated scales to provide a comprehensive evaluation of depression severity. All participants completed the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a validated self-administered instrument that assesses the frequency of nine core depressive symptoms over the previous two weeks, with total scores ranging from 0 to 2723. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity, and standard cutoffs were used to categorize depression as mild, moderate, moderately severe, or severe23.

In addition, trained psychiatrists administered the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) to the patient group, providing an objective clinician-rated measure of depressive symptomatology, with scores of 0–7 indicating no depression, 8–13 mild depression, 14–18 moderate depression, 19–22 severe depression, and scores ≥ 23 indicating very severe depression24. The HAM-D total score was used to complement the PHQ-9 results and to allow correlation with serum biomarker levels, ensuring both subjective and objective assessment of depression severity.

Sample size calculation

The primary outcome for sample size calculation was the between-group difference in serum 8-OHdG concentration between patients with depression and healthy controls. Based on effect sizes reported in previous studies examining oxidative stress biomarkers in depressive disorders25, a moderate-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.6) was assumed. Using G*Power (version 3.1) for an independent-samples t-test (two-tailed, α = 0.05, power = 0.80), a minimum sample size of 90 participants (45 per group) was calculated. To compensate for potential dropouts or unusable samples, a final sample of 100 participants (50 depressed patients and 50 healthy controls) was recruited, corresponding to an achieved power exceeding 80% for the assumed effect size.

Blood sampling and laboratory procedures

Blood collection

After an overnight fast of 10–12 h, 5 mL of venous blood was drawn from each participant between 8:00 and 10:00 AM to minimize diurnal variations. Samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 20–30 min and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min. The serum was aliquoted into Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Biochemical analysis

Serum levels of the selected biomarkers were quantitatively measured using validated human ELISA kits.

Serum 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) concentrations were measured using a competitive ELISA kit (RedDot Biotech, Cat. No. RDR-8-OHdG-Ge, China). The assay has a detection range of 62.5–4000 pg/mL and an analytical sensitivity of < 10 pg/mL. The manufacturer reports an intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) of < 10% and an inter-assay CV of < 12%. Serum samples were aliquoted immediately after centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Repeated freeze–thaw cycles were avoided, and visibly hemolyzed samples were excluded. All standards and samples were measured in duplicate. Concentrations were calculated using four-parameter logistic (4-PL) regression based on plate-specific calibration curves. Quality-control procedures included duplicate measurements, duplicate standards, standard curve verification, and adherence to manufacturer-recommended incubation and washing conditions.

Serum NLRP3 concentrations were quantified using a Human NLR Family Pyrin Domain Containing 3 (NLRP3) ELISA Kit (Catalogue No. 201-12-5748; SunRed Biotechnology, China), which utilizes a double-antibody sandwich ELISA format. The assay has a validated measurement range of 2.5–600 ng/L and an analytical sensitivity of 2.362 ng/L. For each sample, 40 µL serum, 10 µL biotin-labeled anti-NLRP3 antibody, and 50 µL streptavidin-HRP reagent were added according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until analysis, repeated freeze–thaw cycles were avoided, and grossly hemolyzed samples were excluded because the manufacturer specifically indicates that hemolyzed specimens are unsuitable for this assay. All standards and samples were analyzed in duplicate, and quality-control procedures included duplicate measurements, duplicate standards, plate-specific calibration curves, standard curve verification, and strict adherence to incubation and washing procedures.

Serum kynurenine concentrations were quantified using a competitive ELISA assay (Eagle Biosciences, Catalog No. BA E-2200; manufactured by LDN, Germany) validated for quantitative measurement of kynurenine in human serum and plasma. The assay has a measurement range of 100–10,000 ng/mL and an analytical sensitivity of 45.7 ng/mL. The intra-assay coefficients of variation ranging from 10.3 to 12.9% and inter-assay coefficients of variation ranging from 13.3 to 17.5%, indicating acceptable analytical precision across the assay range. Serum samples were aliquoted immediately after processing and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Repeated freeze–thaw cycles were avoided, and visibly hemolyzed samples were excluded from analysis. All samples and standards were analyzed in duplicate according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quality-control procedures included duplicate measurements, duplicate standards, verification of standard curve performance, and concentration calculation using four-parameter logistic (4-PL) regression.

Serum biomarker measurements were performed independently of clinical assessment. Laboratory personnel conducting ELISA analyses were blinded to participants’ clinical scores and case-control status during biomarker quantification.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 9) and SPSS (version 29), with a two-tailed significance threshold of p < 0.05. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median [interquartile range, IQR] for non-normal data. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Comparisons between patients with depression and healthy controls were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables, independent t-test for normally distributed variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Associations between serum biomarkers (8-OHdG, NLRP3, kynurenine) and clinical measures (HAM-D, PHQ-9, and disease duration) were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. To identify independent predictors of depression severity, multiple linear regression models were constructed with HAM-D and PHQ-9 scores as dependent variables and biomarkers, age, sex, and disease duration as predictors. Regression assumptions, including multicollinearity, were assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) methods.

The diagnostic performance of individual biomarkers and their combination for discriminating depression was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, with metrics including AUC, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, likelihood ratios, and diagnostic odds ratio. Optimal cutoffs were determined using the Youden index, and combined biomarker performance was validated via bootstrap resampling (n = 2000). As an exploratory analysis, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on standardized biomarker values to evaluate covariance patterns among the biomarkers. Eigenvalues, component loadings, and proportions of explained variance were calculated. Because PCA was considered exploratory and demonstrated limited dimensionality reduction, detailed PCA results are presented in the Supplementary Material.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants

The study comprised 50 patients with depression and 50 healthy controls, for a total of 100 individuals. In terms of baseline clinical and demographic features, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups. In the depression group, the median age was 39.5 years (IQR: 29.5–52.3), while in the control group, it was 43.5 years (IQR: 34.0–55.0) (p = 0.172). In the depression group, there were 30 men and 20 women, while in the control group, there were 29 men and 21 women (p = 0.838). Likewise, neither body mass index (BMI) nor weight showed any discernible variations. In the depression group, the median weight was 73.45 kg (IQR: 64.25–80.20), while in the control group, it was 74.75 kg (IQR: 68.38–85.60) (p = 0.321). The proportion of current smokers was equally similar between the two groups (40.0% vs. 32.0%, p = 0.532). The majority of participants in both groups were either married or single, and there was no significant difference in the distribution of marital status (p = 0.783) (Table 1).

Comparison of serum biomarkers between patients with depression and healthy controls

Patients with depression had considerably higher serum biomarker levels than healthy controls. The depression group had significantly higher median 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) levels [1352.0 pg/mL (IQR: 647.0–1610.8)] than the controls [796.0 pg/mL (IQR: 560.0–951.0); U = 635, p < 0.0001, r = 0.492]. Similarly, NLRP3 levels were considerably higher in depressed individuals [307.9 pg/mL (IQR: 236.1–360.1)] than in the control group [210.6 pg/mL (IQR: 191.4–231.7); U = 598, p < 0.0001, r = 0.522]. Additionally, the depression group had substantially higher levels of kynurenine [188.8 ng/mL (IQR: 128.8–200.5)] than the controls [123.5 ng/mL (IQR: 96.2–174.8); p = 0.005, r = 0.327], suggesting a modest effect size. Overall, these results show that oxidative stress and inflammatory indicators are significantly altered in depressed patients. (Table 3)

Comparison of depression scores between patients with depression and healthy controls

Depression severity scores were significantly higher in patients with depression compared to healthy controls, as assessed by both clinician-rated and self-reported measures. (Table 4)

The depression group had a median Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) score of 21 (IQR: 16–28) compared to 2 (IQR: 0–4) in the control group (p < 0.0001). Similarly, patients with depression had significantly higher scores on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [17 (IQR: 13–22)] than the control group [2 (IQR: 1–3); p < 0.0001]. These results support a significant difference in the two groups’ levels of depressed symptoms.

To find predictors of the HAM-D score in depressed patients, multiple linear regression analysis was used. The model’s overall explanatory power was low (R2 = 0.1099; adjusted R2 = −0.0144), meaning that only a small percentage of the variance in depression severity could be explained by the factors that were included.

There was no statistically significant correlation found between any of the assessed predictors and HAM-D values. In particular, kynurenine (β = − 0.0032, p = 0.8235), NLRP3 (β = 0.0130, p = 0.2856), and 8-OHdG (β = 0.0026, p = 0.2166) were not significant predictors. Age (β = 0.1215, p = 0.1081), sex (β =− 0.3947, p = 0.8435), and disease duration (β = − 0.2741, p = 0.6310) were among the clinical and demographic factors that did not show significant correlations with the HAM-D score.

Diagnostic performance of the logistic model

The combined logistic regression model’s diagnostic performance was assessed at the Youden index’s ideal cutoff value (0.581). The confusion matrix displayed 37 true positives, 13 false negatives, 45 true negatives, and 5 false positives (n = 100) at this threshold.

The model’s specificity was 90.0% (95% CI 78.6–95.7%) and its sensitivity was 74.0% (95% CI 60.4–84.1%). The negative predictive value (NPV) was 77.6%, and the positive predictive value (PPV) was 88.1%. (Fig 1 and 2)

Regarding likelihood ratios, the positive likelihood ratio (LR+) was 7.40 (95% CI 3.17–17.27), suggesting a significant rise in the likelihood of illness after a positive test. After a negative test, the likelihood of disease was moderately reduced, as indicated by the negative likelihood ratio (LR−) of 0.289 (95% CI 0.179–0.465). The model’s overall discriminative performance was demonstrated by the diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), which was 25.62 (95% CI 8.36–78.46) (Table 10).

Exploratory PCA of the three serum biomarkers indicated limited shared variance suitable for dimensionality reduction (KMO = 0.561; Bartlett’s test of sphericity, χ2(3) = 10.43, p = 0.015). By the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue > 1), only one component was retained, accounting for 45.9% of total variance. These findings are consistent with the low inter-biomarker correlations (r = 0.12–0.24) and low variance inflation factors (VIF < 1.1) reported in Table 12, together indicating that the three biomarkers largely capture independent, non-redundant information rather than a shared underlying construct (Supplementary Material 1).

Discussion

The primary finding of this study is that circulating levels of 8-OHdG, NLRP3, and kynurenine were significantly higher in patients with depression than in healthy controls. These results demonstrate an association between these biomarkers and depression status within the study population, but they do not establish causality or indicate whether biomarker alterations are a cause or consequence of depressive illness. By integrating biochemical profiling, clinical scales, correlation analyses, multivariable regression, multiple comparison corrections, and ROC curve analysis, our findings offer a robust evaluation of the clinical relevance of these biomarkers in depression. The findings reveal a clear distinction between the ability of these biomarkers to discriminate disease presence and their limited contribution to explaining symptom severity once depression is established.

8-OHdG is a widely recognized marker of oxidative DNA damage and reflects cumulative oxidative stress at the cellular level26. The significantly higher levels observed in our study in patients with depression suggest enhanced oxidative injury, which may contribute to neuronal dysfunction, impaired synaptic plasticity, and altered neurogenesis—processes central to the development and persistence of depressive symptoms11. Excessive oxidative DNA damage has been shown to disrupt mitochondrial function, alter neurotransmitter metabolism, and activate redox-sensitive signaling pathways, thereby amplifying neurobiological vulnerability to mood dysregulation17,27. These findings are consistent with previous clinical studies reporting elevated 8-OHdG levels in depressive disorders28,29. Collectively, this convergence of evidence underscores oxidative DNA damage as a relevant biological correlate of depressive symptomatology30. One of the key findings of this study is the overall lack of strong associations between serum biomarkers and clinical severity measures (HAM-D and PHQ-9). Among the tested relationships, only 8-OHdG showed a statistically significant correlation with PHQ-9 scores, and even this association was modest and inverse in direction. Additionally, 8-OHdG was positively correlated with disease duration, suggesting that oxidative stress may accumulate over time or reflect chronic disease processes rather than acute symptom burden.

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a key regulator of innate immune responses and has been increasingly implicated in neuroinflammatory mechanisms underlying depression20,31. The elevated NLRP3 levels observed in patients with depression in the present study further support the hypothesis that inflammasome activation contributes to mood dysregulation through enhanced maturation and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin-1β and interleukin-18, which are known to interfere with neurotransmitter signaling, synaptic plasticity, and neurogenesis32. Such findings imply that sustained inflammasome activation may reflect cumulative inflammatory exposure rather than transient symptom fluctuations. Mechanistically, NLRP3 activation may be triggered by multiple upstream stressors, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic disturbances—all of which have been implicated in depression16. Elevated NLRP3 could also influence neuroplasticity, synaptic function, and neurotransmitter metabolism, particularly within mood-regulating regions of the brain, including the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex33.

In this study, kynurenine, a key metabolite of the tryptophan degradation pathway, was significantly elevated in patients with depression compared to healthy controls These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that activation of the kynurenine pathway contributes to the pathophysiology of depression34,35. Increased circulating kynurenine levels may reflect alterations in tryptophan metabolism and have been associated with inflammatory processes implicated in depression. However, because only kynurenine was measured, the present study cannot directly determine whether the entire kynurenine pathway was activated or identify the relative contribution of downstream neuroactive metabolites. A more comprehensive evaluation would require simultaneous assessment of tryptophan, the kynurenine/tryptophan ratio, kynurenic acid, quinolinic acid, and other pathway intermediates36. From a diagnostic standpoint, kynurenine showed good discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.765) and enhanced the performance of the combined biomarker model (AUC = 0.887). These results highlight kynurenine not only as a potential biomarker for depression severity but also as a candidate for therapeutic targeting, where interventions aimed at modulating the kynurenine pathway or reducing upstream inflammation may help alleviate depressive symptoms37,38. The combined biomarker model demonstrated promising diagnostic discrimination within the present dataset. However, the reported performance metrics were derived from internal validation procedures and should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical applicability. External validation in independent cohorts is required to determine model generalizability and clinical utility.

Importantly, these findings did not persist in multivariable regression models after adjustment for covariates and correction for multiple comparisons. The disappearance of statistical significance following Bonferroni and FDR corrections underscores the fragility of these associations and highlights the importance of rigorous statistical control in biomarker research. This pattern is consistent with a growing body of literature indicating that peripheral biomarkers often show weak and inconsistent correlations with symptom severity in psychiatric disorders, likely due to the heterogeneity of depression and the influence of numerous confounding biological and environmental factors39–41. Adaptive behavioral responses that encourage energy conservation to fight illness or heal from injury are produced by the administration of inflammatory cytokines or the stimulation of the innate immune system. Depression and neuropsychiatric diseases, however, can result from long-term exposure to increased inflammatory cytokines and lasting changes in neurotransmitter systems39. Cytokine behavioral impacts are caused by activation of inflammatory signaling pathways in the brain, which alters the neuropeptide, glutamate, and monoamine systems and reduces growth factors such brain-derived neurotrophic factor. Additionally, inflammatory cytokines may operate as mediators of genetic (functional gene polymorphisms) and environmental (childhood trauma, obesity, stress, and sleep deprivation) variables that contribute to the development of depression39.

The lack of substantial relationships for NLRP3 and kynurenine with clinical scales lends credence to the hypothesis that inflammatory and metabolic changes may not directly correlate with symptom intensity in a linear, cross-sectional fashion. These pathways may be more significant to illness susceptibility, subtype differentiation, or longitudinal trajectories than to momentary severity scores42.

The observed elevations in 8-OHdG, NLRP3, and kynurenine are consistent with growing evidence implicating oxidative stress, immune activation, and altered tryptophan metabolism in depression43,44. However, these biological processes are not specific to depressive disorders and have also been reported in a variety of inflammatory, metabolic, neurological, and psychiatric conditions. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence supporting the involvement of these pathways in depression rather than as demonstrating depression-specific biological mechanisms.

All three biomarkers showed various degrees of accuracy in differentiating between depressed patients and controls, despite their limited significance in predicting the severity of symptoms. Kynurenine did not perform as well as NLRP3 and 8-OHdG, according to ROC analysis. Interestingly, 8-OHdG demonstrated a very high specificity (98%), indicating that elevated oxidative stress indicators may be especially useful for verifying the existence of depression when identified45. An important consideration is the limited specificity of peripheral biomarkers. Serum levels of 8-OHdG, NLRP3, and kynurenine can be influenced by numerous factors, including age, metabolic status, systemic inflammatory activity, lifestyle factors such as smoking and diet, and comorbid medical conditions. Although major conditions known to affect these biomarkers were included among the exclusion criteria, residual influences from unmeasured factors cannot be completely excluded. Consequently, these biomarkers should be viewed as indicators of broader biological processes that may contribute to depression rather than as disease-specific markers.

The combined logistic regression model’s better performance, however, is the most convincing discovery. The model outperformed each individual biomarker, achieving good overall discrimination with an AUC of 0.857. The model appears to be therapeutically useful, especially as a rule-in tool, based on the balance between sensitivity (74%) and specificity (90%). The positive likelihood ratio (LR + = 7.40), which shows a significant rise in post-test probability following a positive result, further supports this. From a clinical standpoint, these metrics indicate that although the model might not be adequate as a stand-alone diagnostic tool, it might be a useful addition to clinical evaluation, particularly in instances that are unclear or in research settings where the goal is to classify patients according to biological profiles. Although not strong enough to definitively rule out depression in isolation, the comparatively smaller negative likelihood ratio (LR− = 0.289) implies a considerable ability to exclude depression.

Multivariate analysis demonstrated that all three biomarkers were independently associated with depression status, with odds ratios indicating statistically significant contributions. The absence of multicollinearity (VIF values near 1) confirms that these biomarkers capture distinct aspects of the underlying biology rather than redundant information. This independence is further supported by the principal component analysis (PCA), which revealed both shared and distinct variance structures. PCA should be regarded as an exploratory method for detecting underlying patterns of covariance among biomarkers rather than specifying specific biological pathways. In the current investigation, the first principal component explained the majority of the variance and had loadings in the same direction for all three biomarkers. This finding suggests that 8-OHdG, NLRP3, and kynurenine may have a similar variance structure, which is compatible with a largely overlapping oxidative stress-inflammatory biological profile46,47. In contrast, the second and third principal components accounted for lesser shares of the variation and should be evaluated with caution. Although they provide further diversity among biomarkers, assigning specific pathophysiological importance to these components is dubious. Therefore, the PCA findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger studies incorporating mechanistic and longitudinal analyses40.

The findings of this study align with contemporary models of depression as a disorder of systemic dysregulation involving oxidative stress, immune activation, and altered neurochemical metabolism16. Oxidative stress, as reflected by 8-OHdG, may contribute to neuronal damage and impaired neuroplasticity48. Inflammatory activation, indexed by NLRP3, is increasingly recognized as a key driver of neuroimmune interactions and has been linked to treatment resistance and specific depressive subtypes32. The kynurenine pathway, which bridges immune activation and neurotransmitter metabolism, represents a critical interface between inflammation and neurotoxicity49.

These processes may function more as trait-like or background biological components than as state-dependent biomarkers, according to the lack of strong associations with symptom intensity. In other words, rather than directly influencing the severity of symptoms at a particular moment in time, they may either maintain or predispose people to depression. Although biomarker concentrations differed significantly between patients and controls, their associations with depression severity were limited. Following adjustment for multiple comparisons, no biomarker remained significantly associated with HAM-D or PHQ-9 scores. These findings suggest that the investigated biomarkers may be more closely related to disease presence than to symptom severity within an established depressive episode.

This study has a number of advantages. In order to provide a comprehensive knowledge of biomarker activity, it uses a thorough analytical framework that integrates correlation, regression, ROC analysis, and PCA. The evaluation of both individual and combination biomarker performance provides useful clinical relevance, and the addition of multiple comparison correction strengthens the results’ robustness.

But there are a few limitations to be informed of. Although sufficient for exploratory research, the sample size may restrict statistical power, especially when it comes to identifying subtle effects in multivariable models. The cross-sectional architecture restricts understanding of temporal dynamics and makes causal inference impossible. Furthermore, biomarker levels may be impacted by potential confounders that were not fully taken into consideration, such as medication use, nutrition, smoking status, and concomitant illnesses.

The diagnostic model’s lack of external validation is a further major limitation. The findings’ generalizability is still unclear in the absence of replication in separate cohorts. Additionally, the study concentrates on peripheral biomarkers, which might not accurately represent processes in the central neurological system.

Longitudinal designs should be given top priority in future research to evaluate how these biomarkers alter over time and in response to therapy. To validate the diagnostic approach and investigate its applicability across other populations, larger, multicenter investigations are required. Predictive accuracy may be further improved by using additional indicators, such as genetic, neuroimaging, and microbiome data.

Furthermore, biological profile-based patient stratification may aid in the identification of unique depression subtypes, opening the door for individualized treatment strategies. Another intriguing direction is to look into how these biomarkers relate to therapy response, especially to anti-inflammatory or antioxidant therapies.

Another limitation relates to the limited specificity of the investigated biomarkers. Oxidative stress markers, inflammasome-related proteins, and kynurenine metabolites are involved in numerous physiological and pathological processes beyond depression. Therefore, elevated biomarker levels should not be considered specific indicators of depressive disorders, and future studies should compare these markers across multiple psychiatric and non-psychiatric conditions to better establish their diagnostic utility and specificity.

An additional limitation is that only kynurenine concentrations were measured. Although kynurenine is an important intermediate of tryptophan metabolism, measurement of kynurenine alone does not provide a comprehensive assessment of kynurenine-pathway activity. Future studies should incorporate tryptophan concentrations, kynurenine/tryptophan ratios, and downstream metabolites such as kynurenic acid, quinolinic acid, and 3-hydroxykynurenine to better characterize pathway dysregulation in depression.

Although major medical comorbidities and medications known to influence inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways were excluded through the study eligibility criteria, residual confounding from lifestyle, environmental, dietary, and other unmeasured factors cannot be completely excluded. In addition, BMI and smoking status were not included in the multivariable models, which may have contributed to residual confounding. Future studies with larger sample sizes should allow more comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study shows that serum biomarkers of oxidative stress, inflammation, and kynurenine metabolism are not very useful in predicting the severity of depression symptoms, but when combined, they offer great potential for diagnosis. The results emphasize the value of integrative methods in biomarker research and support a multifaceted picture of depression incorporating multiple biological pathways. The findings add to the expanding body of evidence supporting physiologically based models of depression and could guide future efforts in precision psychiatry, even though more validation is needed.

Although the investigated biomarkers demonstrated associations with depression status and showed promising discriminatory performance, the current findings are insufficient to support clinical implementation. Larger longitudinal studies and independent external validation cohorts are necessary before these biomarkers can be considered for routine diagnostic or prognostic use.
