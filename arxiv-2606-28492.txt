Propagating data noise through the fit: the Monte Carlo replica distribution
Mark N. Costantini

Abstract
The Monte Carlo (MC) replica method quantifies parameter uncertainties in global fits of parton distribution functions (PDFs) and Standard Model Effective Field Theory (SMEFT) Wilson coefficients by fitting a model to many noise-perturbed copies of the data and taking the empirical distribution of the best-fit parameters as the uncertainty. The method reproduces the Bayesian posterior exactly only when the model is linear in its parameters, and departs from it in the nonlinear case. We derive the leading-order distribution the method produces and compare it with the Laplace approximation of the Bayesian posterior: the two differ by a single computable matrix, the residual-weighted Hessian of the model at the best fit, whose sign and magnitude set the over- or under-estimation of the parameter uncertainties. This closed-form expression quantifies when and by how much the MC method departs from Bayesian inference. We illustrate it on two single-parameter examples solvable in closed form and point to its evaluation in full PDF and SMEFT fits as a natural next step.

The Monte Carlo (MC) replica method is a procedure for propagating
data uncertainties to fitted parameters via repeated fits on

Much of its success owes to its computational convenience: it replaces
the sampling of a posterior distribution with a set of independent
optimisations, each of which is cheaper than sampling and which can be
run in parallel across replicas.

The same construction, perturbing the data with Gaussian noise and
re-optimising, is not restricted to high-energy physics. An equivalent
construction appears as a method for sampling posterior distributions in
nonlinear inverse problems
Bardsley:2014
and references therein.
It is closely related to the parametric bootstrap
of statistics
Efron_2012
, and the same perturb-and-refit idea
underlies ensemble approaches to uncertainty quantification in machine
learning such as randomized prior functions
Osband:2018
. Across
all of these settings a single fact is well established: the method
reproduces the Bayesian posterior exactly when the model is linear in
its parameters, and departs from it otherwise.

For nonlinear models, this departure can be corrected
numerically: Ref.
Bardsley:2014
modifies the optimisation so that
the density of the perturbed-and-refitted samples is known in closed
form, and then uses it within a Metropolis–Hastings or importance-sampling
scheme to recover the exact posterior. Such a correction adds a further
layer of computational complexity on top of the bare ensemble of fits.
In this work we characterise
the departure in closed form, deriving a leading-order expression for the
difference between the bare MC replica distribution and the Bayesian
posterior, a single computable matrix. Evaluating it is much cheaper than
the corrective sampling, and tells one, before any corrective step,
whether the bare MC replica distribution already reproduces Bayesian
inference to the required accuracy or whether the correction is needed:
the extra sampling can then be skipped whenever it is unnecessary and run
only when it is not, with the matrix giving in each case the sign and
size of the discrepancy.

In high-energy physics, the parameter distribution implied by the MC
replica procedure was first derived rigorously in
Ref.
Costantini:2024wby
, which confirmed its agreement with the
Bayesian posterior in the linear case
DelDebbio:2021whr
and
showed that the two depart in the nonlinear case, with consequences of
practical importance for PDF and SMEFT determinations. More broadly,
the question of how faithfully the MC replica method represents the
posterior is part of a growing interest in Bayesian approaches to PDF
inference

In this work we give a complementary derivation of the MC replica
distribution that makes the nonlinear departure explicit and computable.
Treating the maximum-likelihood estimator (MLE) as a deterministic map

d\mapsto\hat{\theta}(d)
from data space to parameter space, we identify
the MC distribution as the pushforward of the Gaussian replica noise
under
\eta\mapsto\hat{\theta}(D_{0}+\eta)
. A first-order Taylor
expansion of
\hat{\theta}(\cdot)
around
D_{0}

a linear-Gaussian map, whose covariance is fixed by the Jacobian of

\hat{\theta}(\cdot)
at
D_{0}

optimality condition then supplies this Jacobian in closed form,
delivering the leading-order MC distribution. Comparing it with the
Laplace approximation of the Bayesian posterior, we find that the two
differ by a single matrix, the residual-weighted Hessian of the
theory map at the best fit, whose sign and magnitude set the over-
or under-estimation of the parameter uncertainties, and which can be
evaluated directly in a realistic fit.

Strategy.

2.2
Computing the Jacobian.

Implicit differentiation of the FOC.

We derive the Jacobian
\partial\hat{\theta}/\partial d
at
D_{0}
by
implicit differentiation of the FOC (
11
). By construction,

\hat{\theta}(d;\theta_{\mathrm{init}})
is a local minimum of the loss and
therefore satisfies

F\bigl(\hat{\theta}(d;\theta_{\mathrm{init}}),d\bigr)\equiv 0
.
Taking the total derivative of this identity with respect to
d
and
evaluating at
(\hat{\theta}_{\star},D_{0})
, the chain rule gives

\partial_{\theta}F\,\cdot\,\frac{\partial\hat{\theta}}{\partial d}\;+\;\partial_{d}F\;=\;0.

(12)

The data derivative is immediate from

F(\theta,d)=J(\theta)^{\top}(T(\theta)-d)

\partial_{d}F=-J_{\star}^{\top}
, with
J_{\star}:=J(\hat{\theta}_{\star})
. The parameter derivative

\partial_{\theta}F
is the Hessian of the loss, and is also straightforward to
compute as we show in the next paragraph.

The Hessian of the loss.

Since

is itself the gradient of the OLS objective

L(\theta;d)\;:=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,\|d-T(\theta)\|^{2}\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm{dat}}}\bigl(d_{a}-T_{a}(\theta)\bigr)^{2},

(13)

\partial L/\partial\theta_{i}=F_{i}

\partial_{\theta}F
is
just the Hessian
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}L

explicitly, we differentiate

F_{i}(\theta,d)=\sum_{a}J_{a,i}(\theta)\,(T_{a}(\theta)-d_{a})

and

\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial\theta_{j}}(\theta,d)\;=\;\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm{dat}}}\biggl[\underbrace{\frac{\partial J_{a,i}}{\partial\theta_{j}}(\theta)}_{=\,H_{a,ij}(\theta)}\bigl(T_{a}(\theta)-d_{a}\bigr)\;+\;J_{a,i}(\theta)\,\underbrace{\frac{\partial T_{a}}{\partial\theta_{j}}(\theta)}_{=\,J_{a,j}(\theta)}\biggr],

(14)

which in matrix form is

A(\theta,d)\;:=\;\partial_{\theta}F(\theta,d)\;=\;J(\theta)^{\top}J(\theta)\;+\;\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm{dat}}}\bigl(T_{a}(\theta)-d_{a}\bigr)\,H_{a,\cdot\cdot}(\theta).

(15)

as a good approximation of the Hessian of a nonlinear least-squares problem whenever the residuals are small.
The second term is the

\theta
.
Evaluated at

(16)

3
Linear case: exact closed form

We consider a linear theory
T(\theta)=J\theta
with

J\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{dat}}\times N_{\mathrm{par}}}
of full column rank. The Jacobian is then
the constant matrix
J(\theta)\equiv J
,

H\equiv 0
,
A\equiv J^{\top}J
, and the first-order
condition (
11
) has the closed-form solution

\hat{\theta}(d)\;=\;(J^{\top}J)^{-1}J^{\top}d.

(18)

Because
\hat{\theta}(\cdot)
is affine, the pushforward of any Gaussian under
\hat{\theta}(\cdot)
is
exactly Gaussian. Writing
D_{0}^{(k)}=D_{0}+\eta^{(k)}
,

\hat{\theta}^{(k)}\;=\;\hat{\theta}(D_{0}+\eta^{(k)})\;=\;\underbrace{(J^{\top}J)^{-1}J^{\top}D_{0}}_{=\,\hat{\theta}_{\star}}\;+\;(J^{\top}J)^{-1}J^{\top}\,\eta^{(k)}.

(19)

Taking
\eta^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C)
gives the exact MC
replica distribution,

\boxed{\;\;\hat{\theta}^{(k)}\,\big|\,D_{0}\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}\!\Bigl(\hat{\theta}_{\star},\;(J^{\top}J)^{-1}J^{\top}C\,J(J^{\top}J)^{-1}\Bigr).\;\;}

(20)

For the simplest case
C=\sigma^{2}I
,

\hat{\theta}^{(k)}\,\big|\,D_{0}\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{\star},\;\sigma^{2}(J^{\top}J)^{-1}\bigr),

(21)

which is the standard ordinary-least-squares covariance.

The result (
20
) is consistent with the linear-case
expression derived from a Bayesian perspective in
Ref.
DelDebbio:2021whr
and with the MC replica distribution of
Ref.
Costantini:2024wby
.

4
Nonlinear case: leading-order Gaussian approximation

For a general nonlinear theory
T
the map
\hat{\theta}(\cdot)
is not affine, and
the pushforward of a Gaussian under
\hat{\theta}(\cdot)
is no longer exactly
Gaussian. The leading-order statement follows from combining the
Taylor expansion (
7
) with the Jacobian
formula (
17
): each replica obeys

\hat{\theta}^{(k)}\;=\;\hat{\theta}_{\star}\;+\;A_{\star}^{-1}J_{\star}^{\top}\,\eta^{(k)}\;+\;O(\|\eta^{(k)}\|^{2}),

(22)

with
J_{\star}
,
A_{\star}
and the fit residuals
r_{a}
as
in (
16

O(\|\eta\|^{2})
term
gives the leading-order MC replica distribution,

\boxed{\;\;\hat{\theta}^{(k)}\,\big|\,D_{0}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{leading}}}{{\sim}}\;\mathcal{N}\!\Bigl(\hat{\theta}_{\star},\;A_{\star}^{-1}\,J_{\star}^{\top}\,C\,J_{\star}\,A_{\star}^{-1}\Bigr).\;\;}

(23)

It is interesting to note that for small residuals, or
T
close to
linear, the residual-weighted Hessian is negligible and
A_{\star}\approx J_{\star}^{\top}J_{\star}
.
Therefore Eq. (
23
) reduces to the linear
formula (
20
) with
J\leftrightarrow J_{\star}
.

Comparison with the Bayesian posterior

Equation (
23
) gives the Gaussian approximation
of the distribution that the MC replica procedure produces. However,
it is not yet clear whether this law should be interpreted as a
faithful posterior distribution for
\theta
.
In order to test this we can compare it, as it was done in Ref.
Costantini:2024wby
,
with the posterior distribution derived from Bayes’ theorem.
In particular, for nonlinear
T
this posterior is in
general non-Gaussian, so a comparison at the level of the full
posterior would mix orders in the small-noise expansion; to stay at
the same order as the MC result we compare instead with the

Laplace approximation
of the Bayes posterior, namely, the Gaussian
obtained by a second-order Taylor expansion of
-\log p(\theta\mid D_{0})

around its maximum a posteriori (MAP).
Let us suppose again that the experimental data are generated as

D\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(T(\theta),\sigma^{2}I\bigr)
. The likelihood
of
D
given
\theta

\begin{split}p(D\mid\theta)\;&=\;(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-N_{\mathrm{dat}}/2}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\|D-T(\theta)\|^{2}\right)\\
\;&=\;(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-N_{\mathrm{dat}}/2}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{L(\theta;D)}{\sigma^{2}}\right).\end{split}

(24)

Under a flat (improper) prior
p(\theta)\propto 1
on
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{par}}}
, Bayes’ theorem gives
the posterior

p(\theta\mid D_{0})\;\propto\;p(D_{0}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\;\propto\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{L(\theta;D_{0})}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

(25)

so the negative log-posterior is, up to a
\theta
-independent constant,

-\log p(\theta\mid D_{0})\;=\;\frac{L(\theta;D_{0})}{\sigma^{2}}\;+\;\mathrm{const}.

(26)

Minimising (
26
) is equivalent to minimising
L(\theta;D_{0})
,
so the MAP estimator coincides with the OLS MLE,

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\hat{\theta}_{\star}
.

The Laplace approximation is the second-order Taylor expansion of

-\log p(\theta\mid D_{0})
around its minimiser
\hat{\theta}_{\star}
. The linear
term vanishes by the FOC at
\hat{\theta}_{\star}
, and the Hessian is read off
from (
26
) as

\sigma^{-2}\nabla^{2}_{\theta}L(\hat{\theta}_{\star};D_{0})=A_{\star}/\sigma^{2}
, i.e. the matrix (
15
) at
(\hat{\theta}_{\star},D_{0})
rescaled by

1/\sigma^{2}
. The quadratic approximation therefore reads

-\log p(\theta\mid D_{0})\;=\;\mathrm{const}\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,(\theta-\hat{\theta}_{\star})^{\top}A_{\star}\,(\theta-\hat{\theta}_{\star})\;+\;O\bigl(\|\theta-\hat{\theta}_{\star}\|^{3}\bigr),

(27)

and exponentiating gives a Gaussian centred at
\hat{\theta}_{\star}
with
covariance
\sigma^{2}A_{\star}^{-1}
:

\theta\,\big|\,D_{0}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Laplace}}}{{\sim}}\;\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{\star},\;\sigma^{2}\,A_{\star}^{-1}\bigr).

(28)

Comparing the MC replica covariance (
23
) with

C=\sigma^{2}I
,

\begin{gathered}\Sigma_{\mathrm{MC}}\;=\;\sigma^{2}\,A_{\star}^{-1}J_{\star}^{\top}J_{\star}A_{\star}^{-1},\\
\Sigma_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\;=\;\sigma^{2}\,A_{\star}^{-1},\end{gathered}

(29)

gives the discrepancy

\begin{split}\Sigma_{\mathrm{MC}}-\Sigma_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\;&=\;\sigma^{2}\,A_{\star}^{-1}\!\bigl(J_{\star}^{\top}J_{\star}-A_{\star}\bigr)A_{\star}^{-1}\\
\;&=\;-\sigma^{2}\,A_{\star}^{-1}\!\left(\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm{dat}}}r_{a}\,H_{a,\cdot\cdot}(\hat{\theta}_{\star})\right)\!A_{\star}^{-1}.\end{split}

(30)

The leading-order MC and Bayesian covariances differ by a term proportional
to the residual-weighted Hessian.
Three regimes are interesting to characterise.

•

Linear theory.

H\equiv 0
, the discrepancy (
30
)
vanishes identically, and the MC distribution coincides with the Bayesian
posterior exactly, by (
20
).
This is the well-known agreement between the two frameworks in the linear
case
DelDebbio:2021whr
;
Costantini:2024wby
.

•

Nonlinear theory, good fit.
If the residuals
r_{a}
are small
in size compared to the curvature of
T
, the residual-weighted
Hessian is small and the two covariances approximately agree.

•

Nonlinear theory, poor fit.
If the residuals are not
small
and

T
has appreciable curvature, the MC and Bayesian
covariances differ at leading order. Whether the MC procedure over-
or under-covers Bayes in a given parameter direction depends on the
sign of the residual-weighted Hessian
\sum_{a}r_{a}H_{a,\cdot\cdot}(\hat{\theta}_{\star})

along that direction, and is studied case by case in
Section
5
.

The leading-order MC replica distribution is therefore a faithful
approximation to the Bayesian posterior whenever the residual-weighted
Hessian is negligible. When the
residual-weighted Hessian is non-negligible it is possible to use Eq. (
30
)
to quantify how far the MC distribution departs from the Bayesian
posterior. The next section makes these regimes concrete on two
simple toy examples, where the residuals, the curvature of the theory,
and the resulting MC–Bayes discrepancy can all be computed in closed
form.

5
Toy examples

To make the leading-order MC distribution (
23
)
and its discrepancy (
30
) with the Bayes–Laplace
posterior concrete, we work out two single-parameter toy examples taken
from Ref.
Costantini:2024wby
.

5.1
Quadratic theory at a single data point

Take
N_{\mathrm{dat}}=N_{\mathrm{par}}=1
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
for the single parameter,

D_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
for the single data point,
C=\sigma^{2}
, and

T(\theta)\;=\;t_{0}\;+\;t_{\mathrm{lin}}\,\theta\;+\;t_{\mathrm{quad}}\,\theta^{2},\qquad t_{\mathrm{quad}}>0,

(31)

so that

J(\theta)\;=\;t_{\mathrm{lin}}\;+\;2\,t_{\mathrm{quad}}\,\theta,\qquad H(\theta)\;=\;2\,t_{\mathrm{quad}}.

(32)

We observe that the Jacobian vanishes at the minimum of
T
,

\theta_{\min}\;:=\;-\,\frac{t_{\mathrm{lin}}}{2\,t_{\mathrm{quad}}},\qquad t_{\min}\;:=\;T(\theta_{\min})\;=\;t_{0}\;-\;\frac{t_{\mathrm{lin}}^{2}}{4\,t_{\mathrm{quad}}},

(33)

where
t_{\min}
is the minimum value of
T
over
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. The first-order condition (
11
) reduces
in this scalar setting to

J(\theta)\,\bigl(T(\theta)-D_{0}\bigr)\;=\;0,

(34)

and is solved either by
\theta=\theta_{\min}
(zeroing
J
) or by

T(\theta)=D_{0}
(zeroing the residual). The latter has real solutions only
for
D_{0}\geq t_{\min}
, namely the two branches

\theta_{\pm}\;=\;\theta_{\min}\;\pm\;\sqrt{(D_{0}-t_{\min})/t_{\mathrm{quad}}}.

(35)

D_{0}
relative to

\hat{\theta}(\cdot)

descriptions of
\theta

5.2
Circular theory

Take
N_{\mathrm{dat}}=2
,
N_{\mathrm{par}}=1
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,
D_{0}=(D_{0,1},D_{0,2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

C=\sigma^{2}\,I_{2}
, and

T(\theta)\;=\;t_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad t_{0}>0.

(41)

Direct differentiation gives

\begin{gathered}J(\theta)\;=\;t_{0}\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta\\
\phantom{-}\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad J(\theta)^{\top}J(\theta)\;=\;t_{0}^{2},\\
H_{a}(\theta)\;=\;-\,T_{a}(\theta),\quad a=1,2.\end{gathered}

(42)

The Gauss–Newton scalar
J^{\top}J=t_{0}^{2}
is a positive constant; in
particular
J(\theta)
has full column rank for every
\theta
.
Moreover, as we show below,
A_{\star}\succ 0
and therefore
\hat{\theta}(\cdot)

is differentiable at any best fit
\hat{\theta}_{\star}
.
The non-injectivity
T(\theta)=T(\theta+2\pi k)
of (
41
) is not a problem since
the initialisation convention of Section
2
confines
\hat{\theta}(\cdot)
to a single branch by
definition.

For
D_{0}\neq 0
, the stationarity condition
J(\theta)^{\top}\bigl(T(\theta)-D_{0}\bigr)=0

reduces to
\sin\theta\,D_{0,1}-\cos\theta\,D_{0,2}=0
, whose minimising solution
is
\hat{\theta}_{\star}=\arg(D_{0})
; the corresponding theory point
T(\hat{\theta}_{\star})=t_{0}\,D_{0}/|D_{0}|

is the orthogonal projection of
D_{0}
onto the theory circle. The residual is

r\;=\;T(\hat{\theta}_{\star})-D_{0}\;=\;\frac{t_{0}-|D_{0}|}{|D_{0}|}\,D_{0},

(43)

and the residual-weighted Hessian evaluates to

\begin{split}\sum_{a=1}^{2}r_{a}\,H_{a}(\hat{\theta}_{\star})\;&=\;-\,r^{\top}\,T(\hat{\theta}_{\star})\\
\;&=\;-\,\frac{t_{0}-|D_{0}|}{|D_{0}|}\,D_{0}^{\top}\,\frac{t_{0}\,D_{0}}{|D_{0}|}\\
\;&=\;t_{0}\,\bigl(|D_{0}|-t_{0}\bigr),\end{split}

(44)

where the first equality used
H_{a}=-T_{a}
from (
42
).
Combining (
42
) and (
44
),

A_{\star}\;=\;t_{0}^{2}\;+\;t_{0}\,\bigl(|D_{0}|-t_{0}\bigr)\;=\;t_{0}\,|D_{0}|,

(45)

and is strictly positive (
A_{\star}\succ 0
). Substituting into (
23
)
and (
28
),

\begin{gathered}\Sigma_{\mathrm{MC}}\;=\;\frac{\sigma^{2}\,t_{0}^{2}}{(t_{0}\,|D_{0}|)^{2}}\;=\;\frac{\sigma^{2}}{|D_{0}|^{2}},\\
\Sigma_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\;=\;\frac{\sigma^{2}}{A_{\star}}\;=\;\frac{\sigma^{2}}{t_{0}\,|D_{0}|},\end{gathered}

(46)

with leading-order discrepancy

\Sigma_{\mathrm{MC}}-\Sigma_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\;=\;\frac{\sigma^{2}\,\bigl(t_{0}-|D_{0}|\bigr)}{t_{0}\,|D_{0}|^{2}}.

(47)

Three regimes follow from the sign of
t_{0}-|D_{0}|
:

•

|D_{0}|<t_{0}
(data inside the theory circle): the discrepancy
is positive,

\Sigma_{\mathrm{MC}}>\Sigma_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
,
and the MC procedure over-covers
\theta
relative to Bayes.

•

|D_{0}|>t_{0}
(data outside the theory circle): the discrepancy
is negative, MC under-covers.

•

|D_{0}|=t_{0}
(data exactly on the theory circle): the residual

r
in (
43
) vanishes, the residual-weighted
Hessian (
44
) vanishes,
A_{\star}=J_{\star}^{\top}J_{\star}=t_{0}^{2}
,
and the two covariances coincide — consistent with the general
“good fit” regime of Section
4
.

In particular, we note that our leading-order Gaussian approximation
reproduces the over- and under-coverage as it was already presented in
Fig. 2.4 of Ref.
Costantini:2024wby
;
the non-Gaussian
\mathrm{erfc}
tail of eq. (2.36) is a higher-order
correction beyond the linear Taylor expansion (
22
) of

\hat{\theta}(\cdot)
, arising from its
O(\|\eta\|^{2})
remainder.

6
Conclusions

We have proposed a derivation of the Monte Carlo replica distribution
that views it as the pushforward of the Gaussian replica noise

\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C)
under the MLE map
\hat{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{dat}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{par}}}
,

d\mapsto\arg\min_{\theta}\tfrac{1}{2}\|d-T(\theta)\|^{2}
.
A first-order Taylor expansion of
\hat{\theta}(\cdot)
around
D_{0}

reduces this pushforward to a linear-Gaussian map, whose covariance
is fixed by the Jacobian of
\hat{\theta}
at
D_{0}
; implicit
differentiation of the first-order optimality condition then supplies
this Jacobian in closed form (
17
), delivering the
leading-order MC replica distribution.

For a linear theory
T(\theta)=J\theta
the Taylor expansion is
exact: the MC distribution is exactly Gaussian and coincides with
the Bayes–Laplace posterior (
20
), in agreement
with the well-known equivalence of the two frameworks in the linear
case.

For a general nonlinear theory the leading-order MC
covariance (
23
) and the Bayes–Laplace
covariance differ by a single matrix, the
residual-weighted Hessian
\sum_{a}r_{a}\,H_{a,\cdot\cdot}(\hat{\theta}_{\star})

of the theory map at the best fit (
30
). This is the
central diagnostic of the present derivation: it is built from the
residuals, the theory Jacobian
J_{\star}
and the theory Hessian

H(\hat{\theta}_{\star})
at the best fit, vanishes whenever the residuals are
small or the theory is locally linear, and otherwise sets the sign and
the size of the MC over- or under-coverage of the Bayesian posterior.

The two single-parameter toy examples of Section
5

illustrate this diagnostic in closed form and, in both cases, the
leading-order picture is in agreement with the full MC replica
distribution already presented in Ref.
Costantini:2024wby
: the
quadratic theory (Section
5.1
) reproduces
the delta-like collapse (
40
) of the MC distribution in the
unreachable regime, and the circular theory
(Section
5.2
) reproduces at leading order the
over-/under-coverage reported in Fig. 2.4 of the same reference.

The discrepancy (
30
) provides a practical, computable
indicator that can be evaluated in realistic PDF and SMEFT fits.
This can help understand when and whether the MC replica method is a viable alternative
to a full Bayesian analysis especially in regimes in which the Bayesian posterior
is computationally out of reach.
A natural extension of the present analysis is to evaluate the
residual-weighted Hessian
\sum_{a}r_{a}\,H_{a,\cdot\cdot}(\hat{\theta}_{\star})

in a full NNPDF PDF determination: its magnitude
quantifies how far the MC replica distribution
departs from the Bayes–Laplace posterior, and its sign along each
data point reveals whether the MC procedure over- or
under-covers Bayes for that data point.
