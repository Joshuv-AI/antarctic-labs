Dynamics of Coral–Macroalgae Interactions under Crowding
Julie C. Blackwood, Katerina Nik, Simon Nik

Abstract
. We study a planar ODE model for the benthic competition between coral, macroalgae, and algal turf on a reef, extending the classical model of Mumby, Hastings, and Edwards by a nonlinear, density-dependent coral mortality that accounts for crowding. The strength of crowding is set by an exponent δ > 0 \delta>0 that reshapes the coral nullcline and enriches the bifurcation structure of the system. We establish positive invariance of the biologically relevant region and the absence of periodic orbits, classify the three boundary equilibria together with their local stability, and reduce the coexistence problem to a single scalar equation whose shape, in particular its concavity, controls the number and local stability of the interior equilibria. The grazing intensity g g organizes the dynamics through two thresholds g 0 < g 1 g_{0}<g_{1} determining the stability of the coral- and macroalgae-dominated states, and a further threshold g ⋆ g^{\star} at which two interior equilibria collide. We prove that the system undergoes transcritical bifurcations at the boundary equilibria and a saddle-node bifurcation of interior equilibria, and we discuss the implications for coral reef resilience and hysteresis. We complement these results with numerical simulations that illustrate the bifurcation sequence across the grazing regimes.

1.
Introduction

Coral reefs are among the most productive and diverse marine ecosystems, providing essential services that range from coastal protection and fisheries to tourism
[
13
]
. Across the globe, however, these ecosystems are increasingly threatened by stressors that are often exacerbated by anthropogenic activity: rising sea surface temperatures induce coral bleaching, while the over-harvesting of herbivorous reef fish promotes algal overgrowth that outcompetes and ultimately damages the coral
[
1
,
6
,
9
]
.

There is both empirical and theoretical evidence that coral reef ecosystems admit two alternative stable states: a desirable state of high coral cover and a degraded, coral-depleted state dominated by macroalgae
[
3
,
8
,
10
,
14
]
. A well-documented illustration occurred in the Caribbean in the 1980s, when a disease-induced mass mortality of the sea urchin
Diadema antillarum
, until then the principal grazer, was followed by a marked decline of coral and a proliferation of algae
[
11
]
. In the absence of urchins, herbivorous reef fish such as parrotfish became the primary grazers, their scraping of algae from hard substrates helping to curb macroalgal overgrowth.

Maintaining the desirable state of high coral cover requires managing several interacting stressors simultaneously. Phase shifts between the two states may occur abruptly as exogenous conditions, such as the grazing rate, vary, and reversing them may then require improving these conditions well beyond the threshold at which the collapse occurred. This hysteresis underscores the importance of sustaining coral resilience
[
4
,
5
]
. Alongside the management of grazing pressure, considerable effort is therefore devoted to active restoration, such as coral transplantation, coral gardening, and the deployment of artificial reefs
[
2
]
. Mathematical models of coral–macroalgae competition, in turn, have become a central tool for understanding the transitions between these states and the conditions under which a degraded reef may recover
[
4
,
14
,
18
]
.

In the present work we adapt the well-established Mumby–Hastings–Edwards model
[
14
]
, which captures the central feedbacks between coral and algae competing for space on the reef, by incorporating the nonlinear effects of
coral crowding
: as coral cover increases, intraspecific competition raises the coral mortality rate. We model this density dependence through a mortality of the form
m(C)=m_{0}+dC^{\delta}
, where
m_{0}>0
is the baseline mortality and the exponent
\delta>0
tunes how steeply mortality grows with coral cover. Although such density dependence is ecologically well documented
[
7
]
, its consequences for the qualitative dynamics of coral–macroalgae models have received comparatively little rigorous attention. When
d=0
the model reduces to the constant-mortality system of
[
14
,
12
]
, whose coral nullcline is a straight line. The crowding term reshapes this nullcline for every
\delta>0
. As we show below, intermediate crowding
1<\delta<2
renders the reduced equation non-concave and can produce three coexistence equilibria and a tristable reef, a feature the constant-mortality model cannot exhibit
[
4
,
12
,
14
,
18
]
. Ecologically, such a tristable reef can settle into coral dominance, macroalgae dominance, or a mixed coral–macroalgae community, with the initial cover deciding the outcome.

The aim of the present work is to provide a rigorous qualitative analysis of the resulting planar system, with particular emphasis on how the crowding exponent
\delta
reshapes the equilibrium and bifurcation structure. We determine all boundary and interior equilibria, characterize their local stability, identify the grazing thresholds at which the reef transitions from macroalgae dominance through bistability to coral dominance, and complement this analysis with numerical simulations.
In particular, we reduce the coexistence problem to a single scalar equation whose concavity controls the number and local stability of the interior equilibria. The non-concave regime
1<\delta<2
, in which this reduction admits as many as three coexistence states, requires a more delicate analysis. We refer to
[
5
,
14
,
16
]
for the ecological background and for the parameter ranges underlying the model.

The article is organized as follows. In Section
2
we formulate the model, reduce it to a planar system on a positively invariant region
\Omega
, and show the absence of periodic orbits (Lemma
2.1
). In Section
3
we determine the three boundary equilibria and characterize their local stability in terms of two grazing thresholds
g_{0}<g_{1}
(Lemma
3.2
and Theorems
3.3
and
3.5
). In Section
4
we reduce the coexistence problem to a scalar equation
L(y)=0
, relate the local stability of an interior equilibrium to the sign of
L^{\prime}(y)
, and give a complete classification of the dynamics across the low-, intermediate-, and high-grazing regimes, the last controlled by a further threshold
g^{\star}
(Theorem
4.3
). In Section
5
we rigorously establish the transcritical bifurcations at the boundary equilibria
B
and
C
(Theorems
5.1
and
5.3
) and the saddle-node bifurcation of interior equilibria (Theorem
5.5
), deferring to Appendix
A
the accompanying normal-form reductions, which identify the local type of each degenerate equilibrium at its threshold as a saddle-node point. In Section
6
we specialize the analysis to the linear, quadratic, and intermediate crowding exponents and illustrate the resulting dynamics, including the full bifurcation sequence, numerically. Section
7
discusses the ecological implications for coral reef resilience.

2.1.
Reduction to a planar system

Since
T=1-C-M
by (
2.1
), equation (
2.2c
) is redundant, and using
M+T=1-C
the dynamics reduce to the planar system

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\dot{M}

\displaystyle=aMC+\gamma M(1-C-M)-\frac{gM}{1-C}-nM\,,

(2.4b)

\displaystyle\dot{C}

\displaystyle=bC(1-C-M)-aMC-\big(m_{0}+dC^{\delta}\big)C\,.

Writing
x:=M
and
y:=C
for the macroalgae and coral cover, (
2.4
) takes the form

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=x\Big[\gamma(1-x)-n+(a-\gamma)y-\frac{g}{1-y}\Big]=:xF(x,y)\,,

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=y\Big[b-m_{0}-(a+b)x-by-dy^{\delta}\Big]=:yG(x,y)\,.

We analyze (
2.5
) throughout, denoting partial differentiation by
\partial
, with a subscript indicating the variable, and the derivative of a function of a single variable by a prime.

2.2.
Invariance and absence of periodic orbits

We work on the biologically relevant region

(2.6)

\Omega:=\big\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,x>0,\ y>0,\ x+y<1\big\}\,,

which is open and simply connected. The next result shows that
\Omega
is positively invariant and contains no periodic orbits.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume (
GA
). Then the region
\Omega
is positively invariant for (
2.5
) and contains no periodic orbit of the system.

Proof.

(i)
Fix
\big(x(0),y(0)\big)\in\Omega
. Since
\dot{x}=xF(x,y)
and
\dot{y}=yG(x,y)
, the solution admits the representation

x(t)=x(0)\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{t}F(x(s),y(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)\,,\quad y(t)=y(0)\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{t}G(x(s),y(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)\,,

so that
x(t)>0
and
y(t)>0
for all
t\geq 0
. It thus remains to rule out exit through the segment
\{x+y=1\}
. Setting
z:=x+y
and using
x=1-y
on this segment, we obtain

(2.7)

\dot{z}=\dot{x}+\dot{y}=-g-nx-m_{0}y-dy^{\delta+1}<0\,,

since
g,n,m_{0},d>0
and
x,y>0
. Hence the trajectory cannot reach
\{x+y=1\}
, and
\Omega
is positively invariant.

(ii)
The vector field
\mathbf{V}:=(xF,\,yG)
and the rescaled field
\psi\mathbf{V}
introduced below are continuously differentiable away from
\{x=0\}
,
\{y=0\}
, and
\{y=1\}
. As none of these sets meets
\Omega
, both are of class
\mathrm{C}^{1}(\Omega)
for every
\delta>0
. In particular
\mathbf{V}
is locally Lipschitz on
\Omega
, so by the Picard–Lindelöf theorem solutions with initial data in
\Omega
exist and are unique. Choosing the Dulac function
\psi(x,y):=\tfrac{1}{xy}>0
, we obtain

\partial_{x}\big(\psi xF\big)+\partial_{y}\big(\psi yG\big)=\frac{\partial_{x}F}{y}+\frac{\partial_{y}G}{x}=-\frac{\gamma}{y}-\frac{b+\delta dy^{\delta-1}}{x}<0\qquad\text{on }\Omega\,.

Since
\Omega
is simply connected and this expression has a strict sign, the Bendixson–Dulac criterion rules out periodic orbits in
\Omega
.
∎

In view of Lemma
2.1
, we assume henceforth that the initial data satisfies
(x(0),y(0))\in\Omega
.

3.
Boundary Equilibria

In this section we locate the boundary equilibria of (
2.5
) and determine their local stability. Equilibria are the intersections of the nullclines, which we therefore describe first. As we shall see, the grazing intensity
g
governs the stability of the non-trivial boundary equilibria through two thresholds
0<g_{0}<g_{1}<\gamma-n
that separate a low-, an intermediate-, and a high-grazing regime.

3.1.
Nullclines

The equilibria of (
2.5
) are the points of
\overline{\Omega}
at which
\dot{x}=\dot{y}=0
, that is, the intersections of the
macroalgae nullcline

\{x=0\}\cup\{F=0\}
with the
coral nullcline

\{y=0\}\cup\{G=0\}
. These nullclines and the equilibria at their intersections are illustrated, for several grazing rates, in Figure
2
.

The macroalgae nullcline.
Besides the
y
-axis
\{x=0\}
, the macroalgae nullcline is the zero set of
F
. Since
a<\gamma
, multiplying
F(x,y)=0
by
1-y>0
yields a quadratic in
y
whose branch lying in
\overline{\Omega}
is the graph

(3.1)

y=M(x):=\frac{1}{2(\gamma-a)}\Big[2\gamma-a-n-\gamma x-\sqrt{(\gamma x+n-a)^{2}+4g(\gamma-a)}\Big]\,.

The expression under the square root is bounded below by
4g(\gamma-a)>0
, so
M
is smooth on
[0,\infty)
.
The curve joins the point

(3.2)

A=(0,y_{A})\,,\qquad y_{A}:=M(0)=\frac{2\gamma-a-n-\sqrt{(n-a)^{2}+4g(\gamma-a)}}{2(\gamma-a)}\,,

on the
y
-axis to the point
B=(x_{B},0)
on the
x
-axis, where

(3.3)

x_{B}:=1-\frac{g+n}{\gamma}\,,

and under (
GA
) both
y_{A}\in(0,1)
and
x_{B}\in(0,1)
. Differentiating (
3.1
) gives

(3.4)

M^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma-a)}\Bigg[1+\frac{\gamma x+n-a}{\sqrt{(\gamma x+n-a)^{2}+4g(\gamma-a)}}\Bigg]<0\,,

so
M
is strictly decreasing on
[0,x_{B}]
.

The coral nullcline.
Besides the
x
-axis
\{y=0\}
, the coral nullcline is the zero set of
G
. As
G
is affine in
x
, the equation
G(x,y)=0
is solved explicitly for
x
:

(3.5)

x=K(y):=\frac{b-m_{0}-by-dy^{\delta}}{a+b}\,,\qquad y\in[0,y_{C}]\,,

where
y_{C}\in(0,1)
is the unique solution of

(3.6)

H(y_{C})=b-m_{0}\,,\qquad H(y):=by+dy^{\delta}\,.

Indeed,
H
is continuous and strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
with
H(0)=0<b-m_{0}<b+d=H(1)
, so (
3.6
) admits a unique root
y_{C}\in(0,1)
. The curve
K
joins
C=(0,y_{C})
on the
y
-axis to
D=(x_{D},0)
on the
x
-axis, where

(3.7)

x_{D}:=K(0)=\frac{b-m_{0}}{a+b}\in(0,1)\,.

For
y>0
we have

(3.8)

K^{\prime}(y)=-\frac{b+\delta dy^{\delta-1}}{a+b}<0\,,

so
K
is strictly decreasing and is smooth on
(0,y_{C}]
, extending as a curve of class
\mathrm{C}^{1}
to
[0,y_{C}]
when
\delta\geq 1
.
For later use we record that

(3.9)

\partial_{y}F=(a-\gamma)-\frac{g}{(1-y)^{2}}<0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{y}G=-(b+\delta dy^{\delta-1})<0\qquad\text{on }\Omega\,.

The boundary equilibria are now readily identified.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under (
GA
), system (
2.5
) has exactly three boundary equilibria: the trivial state
O=(0,0)
, the macroalgae-only state
B=(x_{B},0)
with
x_{B}\in(0,1)
as in (
3.3
), and the coral-only state
C=(0,y_{C})
with
y_{C}\in(0,1)
the unique root of (
3.6
).

Proof.

A boundary equilibrium lies on
\partial\Omega
. On the open segment
\{x+y=1\}
we have

G(x,1-x)=-m_{0}-ax-d(1-x)^{\delta}<0\,,

so that
\dot{y}=yG<0
whenever
y>0
, and this segment therefore carries no equilibrium. On the
x
-axis we have
\dot{y}=0
, while
\dot{x}=xF(x,0)
with

F(x,0)=\gamma(1-x)-n-g

strictly decreasing and vanishing only at
x_{B}
, so that the equilibria on
\{y=0\}
are
O
and
B
. In the same way, on the
y
-axis we have
\dot{x}=0
, while
\dot{y}=yG(0,y)
with

G(0,y)=b-m_{0}-by-dy^{\delta}

strictly decreasing and vanishing only at

, so that the equilibria on
\{x=0\}
are
O
and

.
∎

3.2.
Local stability of the boundary equilibria

Linearizing (
2.5
), and recalling (
3.9
) together with
\partial_{x}F=-\gamma
and
\partial_{x}G=-(a+b)
, the Jacobian is

(3.10)

J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}F+x\,\partial_{x}F&x\partial_{y}F\\[4.0pt]
y\partial_{x}G&G+y\partial_{y}G\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}F-\gamma x&x\partial_{y}F\\[4.0pt]
-(a+b)y&G+y\partial_{y}G\end{pmatrix}\,,

where the entries are evaluated using
\partial_{y}F
and
\partial_{y}G
from (
3.9
). We evaluate (
3.10
) at each boundary equilibrium.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under (
GA
), the trivial equilibrium
O=(0,0)
is an unstable node.

Proof.

By (
3.10
),

J(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma-n-g&0\\[3.0pt]
0&b-m_{0}\end{pmatrix}\,,

whose eigenvalues
\gamma-n-g
and
b-m_{0}
are both positive by (
GA
).
∎

We next turn to the macroalgae-only state
B
, whose stability is set by the threshold

(3.11)

g_{1}:=\frac{\gamma(a+m_{0})}{a+b}-n\,.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose (
GA
) holds. Then
0<g_{1}<\gamma-n
, and for every
\delta>0
the macroalgae-only state
B
is a stable node when
g<g_{1}
, a saddle when
g>g_{1}
, and nonhyperbolic at
g=g_{1}
.

Remark 3.4
.

The threshold
g_{1}
is a bifurcation value: Section
5
shows that
B
is then a saddle-node point at which the system undergoes a transcritical bifurcation as
g
crosses
g_{1}
(Theorem
5.1
and Proposition
5.6
).

Proof.

At
B=(x_{B},0)
the Jacobian (
3.10
) is upper triangular,

J(x_{B},0)=\begin{pmatrix}-(\gamma-g-n)&x_{B}(a-\gamma-g)\\[3.0pt]
0&-(a+m_{0})+\dfrac{g+n}{\gamma}(a+b)\end{pmatrix}\,,

so that its eigenvalues are the diagonal entries. By (
GA
) the first is
\lambda_{1}=-(\gamma-g-n)<0
, while the second, by the definition (
3.11
) of
g_{1}
, satisfies

\lambda_{2}=-(a+m_{0})+\frac{g+n}{\gamma}(a+b)=\frac{a+b}{\gamma}(g-g_{1})\,.

Thus
\lambda_{2}<0
for
g<g_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}=0
for
g=g_{1}
, and
\lambda_{2}>0
for

, so that
B
is a stable node when
g<g_{1}
, nonhyperbolic when
g=g_{1}
, and a saddle when
g>g_{1}
. Finally
g_{1}<\gamma-n
since
m_{0}<b
, and
g_{1}>0
by (
GA
).
∎

The stability of the coral-only state

is governed by the threshold

(3.12)

g_{0}:=(1-y_{C})\big(\gamma-n+(a-\gamma)y_{C}\big)\,,

with
y_{C}
as in (
3.6
).

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose (
GA
) holds. Then
g_{0}>0
, and for every
\delta>0
the coral-only state

is a stable node when
g>g_{0}
, a saddle when
g<g_{0}
, and nonhyperbolic at

.

Remark 3.6
.

The threshold
g_{0}
is a bifurcation value: Section
5
shows that
C
is then a saddle-node point at which the system undergoes a transcritical bifurcation as
g
crosses
g_{0}
(Theorem
5.3
and Proposition

).

Proof.

At
C=(0,y_{C})
the Jacobian (
3.10
) is lower triangular,

J(0,y_{C})=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}&0\\[3.0pt]
-(a+b)y_{C}&\mu_{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

so that its eigenvalues are the diagonal entries

and

. The lower-right entry simplifies by (

) from

to

while the upper-left entry, by the definition (

) of

, satisfies

\mu_{1}=F(0,y_{C})=\gamma-n+(a-\gamma)y_{C}-\frac{g}{1-y_{C}}=\frac{g_{0}-g}{1-y_{C}}\,.

Since

, we have
\mu_{1}>0
for

,
\mu_{1}=0
for

, and
\mu_{1}<0
for

, so that

, nonhyperbolic when

, and a stable node when

. Moreover

, so that

.
∎

The two thresholds are ordered, which is what separates the grazing regimes studied in Section

Lemma 3.7
.

Under (
GA
), the thresholds satisfy
0<g_{0}<g_{1}<\gamma-n
.

Proof.

We have already established
g_{0}>0
and
0<g_{1}<\gamma-n
, so it remains to prove

. Since
0<y_{C}<1
and
\delta>0
give
0<y_{C}^{\delta}<1
, relation (
3.6
) yields
by_{C}=b-m_{0}-dy_{C}^{\delta}>b-m_{0}-d
, that is,

1-y_{C}<\frac{m_{0}+d}{b}\,.

Using this lower bound for
y_{C}
once more,

\gamma-n+(a-\gamma)y_{C}=\gamma-n-(\gamma-a)y_{C}<(a-n)+(\gamma-a)\frac{m_{0}+d}{b}\,,

and therefore, by (
3.12
),

(3.13)

g_{0}<\frac{m_{0}+d}{b}\left[(a-n)+(\gamma-a)\frac{m_{0}+d}{b}\right]=\frac{(a-n)(m_{0}+d)}{b}+\frac{(\gamma-a)(m_{0}+d)^{2}}{b^{2}}\,.

By the last condition in (
GA
),
m_{0}+d<\tfrac{b}{2(a-n)}g_{1}
and
m_{0}+d<b\sqrt{\tfrac{g_{1}}{2(\gamma-a)}}
, so each summand in (
3.13
) is strictly less than
\tfrac{g_{1}}{2}
. Hence
g_{0}<g_{1}
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The two thresholds control the boundary stability monotonically in
g
. The proofs of Theorems
3.3
and
3.5
give

\lambda_{2}=\frac{a+b}{\gamma}(g-g_{1}),\qquad\mu_{1}=\frac{g_{0}-g}{1-y_{C}},

hence

\partial_{g}\lambda_{2}=\frac{a+b}{\gamma}>0,\qquad\partial_{g}\mu_{1}=-\frac{1}{1-y_{C}}<0.

As
g
increases,
\lambda_{2}
therefore increases through
0
at
g=g_{1}
and
\mu_{1}
decreases through
0
at
g=g_{0}
, so stronger grazing destabilizes
B
and stabilizes
C
. Since
g_{0}<g_{1}
by Lemma
3.7
, the coral state gains stability before the macroalgae state loses it, and
B
and
C
are simultaneously stable for
g_{0}<g<g_{1}
. In terms of the nullcline intercepts (
3.2
)–(
3.3
) and (
3.7
),

g>g_{0}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad y_{C}>y_{A},\qquad g<g_{1}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad x_{D}<x_{B}.

4.
Interior Equilibria

An interior equilibrium is a point
(x^{\star},y^{\star})\in\Omega
at which
F=G=0
, and it corresponds to the coexistence of coral, macroalgae, and turf, the last occupying the remaining cover
1-x^{\star}-y^{\star}>0
. In this section we reduce the search for such points to a scalar equation, relate the stability of an interior equilibrium to the derivative of the reduced function
L
introduced below, and classify the dynamics in each grazing regime.

4.1.
The reduced equation and its concavity

By (
3.5
), the coral nullcline is the graph
x=K(y)
,
y\in[0,y_{C}]
, so every interior equilibrium has the form
(K(y^{\star}),y^{\star})
with
y^{\star}\in(0,y_{C})
a root of
F\big(K(y),y\big)
. Since
1-K(y)=\tfrac{a+m_{0}+by+dy^{\delta}}{a+b}
, we set

(4.1)

L(y):=F\big(K(y),y\big)=\frac{\gamma(a+m_{0}+by+dy^{\delta})}{a+b}-n+(a-\gamma)y-\frac{g}{1-y}\,,\qquad y\in[0,y_{C}]\,.

Every root of
L
in
(0,y_{C})
indeed yields an interior equilibrium.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
y\in(0,y_{C})
, then
(K(y),y)\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Recall from (
3.6
) the strictly increasing function
H(y)=by+dy^{\delta}
. Since
H(y)<H(y_{C})=b-m_{0}
for
y<y_{C}
, we have
K(y)>0
. Moreover
K(y)+y=\tfrac{b-m_{0}+ay-dy^{\delta}}{a+b}<1
, since
ay-dy^{\delta}<a<a+m_{0}
for
0<y<1
. Thus
(K(y),y)\in\Omega
.
∎

Consequently the number of interior equilibria equals the number of roots of
L
in
(0,y_{C})
. Evaluating
L
at the endpoints and using
a+m_{0}+by_{C}+dy_{C}^{\delta}=a+b
from (
3.6
),

(4.2)

L(0)=g_{1}-g\,,\qquad L(y_{C})=\frac{g_{0}-g}{1-y_{C}}\,,

with
g_{0},g_{1}
from (
3.12
) and (
3.11
). The number of roots is determined by the shape of
L
, whose derivatives are

(4.3)

L^{\prime}(y)=\frac{\gamma(b+\delta d\,y^{\delta-1})}{a+b}+(a-\gamma)-\frac{g}{(1-y)^{2}}\,,\quad L^{\prime\prime}(y)=\frac{\gamma\delta(\delta-1)dy^{\delta-2}}{a+b}-\frac{2g}{(1-y)^{3}}\,,

for
y\in(0,y_{C}]
. We record when
L
is concave.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
y\in(0,y_{C}]
.
(i)
If
0<\delta\leq 1
, then
L^{\prime\prime}(y)<0
.
(ii)
If
\delta\geq 2
and

(4.4)

2g\geq\frac{\gamma\delta(\delta-1)d}{a+b}y_{C}^{\delta-2}\,,

then
L^{\prime\prime}(y)<0
. In either case
L
is strictly concave on
(0,y_{C}]
.

Proof.

(i)
For
0<\delta\leq 1
one has
\delta(\delta-1)\leq 0
, so the first term of
L^{\prime\prime}
is nonpositive while the second is negative.
(ii)
For
\delta\geq 2
the map
y\mapsto y^{\delta-2}
is nondecreasing, so

\tfrac{\gamma\delta(\delta-1)d}{a+b}y^{\delta-2}\leq\tfrac{\gamma\delta(\delta-1)d}{a+b}y_{C}^{\delta-2}\leq 2g

by (
4.4
), while
\tfrac{2g}{(1-y)^{3}}>2g
for
y>0
, and hence
L^{\prime\prime}(y)<0
. Finally, since
y_{C}^{\delta-2}\leq 1
, the simpler bound
2g\geq\tfrac{\gamma\delta(\delta-1)d}{a+b}
already implies (
4.4
), so it too suffices.
∎

The remaining range
1<\delta<2
, where
L
fails to be concave near
y=0
, is analyzed in Subsection
4.4
.

4.2.
Stability of an interior equilibrium

Let
E^{\star}=(x^{\star},y^{\star})
with
x^{\star}=K(y^{\star})
be an interior equilibrium,
y^{\star}\in(0,y_{C})
a root of
L
. As
F(E^{\star})=G(E^{\star})=0
, the Jacobian (
3.10
) reduces to

(4.5)

J(E^{\star})=\begin{pmatrix}x^{\star}\partial_{x}F(E^{\star})&x^{\star}\partial_{y}F(E^{\star})\\[3.0pt]
y^{\star}\partial_{x}G(E^{\star})&y^{\star}\partial_{y}G(E^{\star})\end{pmatrix}\,.

Its trace is

(4.6)

\operatorname{tr}J(E^{\star})=x^{\star}\partial_{x}F(E^{\star})+y^{\star}\partial_{y}G(E^{\star})=-\gamma x^{\star}-y^{\star}\big(b+\delta d(y^{\star})^{\delta-1}\big)<0\,.

For the determinant, the chain rule applied to (
4.1
) gives

(4.7)

L^{\prime}(y^{\star})=\partial_{x}F(E^{\star})K^{\prime}(y^{\star})+\partial_{y}F(E^{\star})=\frac{\gamma\big(b+\delta d(y^{\star})^{\delta-1}\big)}{a+b}+\partial_{y}F(E^{\star})\,,

so that
\big(\partial_{x}F\partial_{y}G-\partial_{y}F\partial_{x}G\big)(E^{\star})=\gamma\big(b+\delta d(y^{\star})^{\delta-1}\big)+(a+b)\partial_{y}F(E^{\star})=(a+b)L^{\prime}(y^{\star})
, and therefore

(4.8)

\det J(E^{\star})=x^{\star}y^{\star}\big(\partial_{x}F\partial_{y}G-\partial_{y}F\partial_{x}G\big)(E^{\star})=(a+b)x^{\star}y^{\star}L^{\prime}(y^{\star})\,.

The off-diagonal entries
x^{\star}\partial_{y}F(E^{\star})
and
y^{\star}\partial_{x}G(E^{\star})=-(a+b)y^{\star}
are both negative, so that their product is positive and the eigenvalues of
J(E^{\star})
are real. In view of (
4.6
) and (
4.8
), the equilibrium
E^{\star}
is therefore a stable node when
L^{\prime}(y^{\star})>0
and a saddle when
L^{\prime}(y^{\star})<0
, so that the sign of
L^{\prime}
at a root decides stability and the whole interior dynamics is read off from the graph of
L
.

4.3.
Classification of the dynamics

It is convenient to write
L(y)=\dfrac{\tilde{g}(y)-g}{1-y}
,
where

(4.9)

\tilde{g}(y):=(1-y)\Big[\tfrac{\gamma}{a+b}\big(a+m_{0}+by+dy^{\delta}\big)-n+(a-\gamma)y\Big]\,,\qquad y\in[0,y_{C}]\,,

is independent of
g
. Then
\tilde{g}(0)=g_{1}
and
\tilde{g}(y_{C})=g_{0}
, and a root of
L
is a point where
\tilde{g}(y)=g
, while
L^{\prime}(y^{\star})
and
\tilde{g}^{\prime}(y^{\star})
share the same sign there, so that an interior equilibrium is a stable node when
\tilde{g}^{\prime}(y^{\star})>0
and a saddle when
\tilde{g}^{\prime}(y^{\star})<0
. Set

(4.10)

g^{\star}:=\max_{y\in[0,y_{C}]}\tilde{g}(y)\in[g_{1},\gamma-n)\,.

The complete picture is the following.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose (
GA
) holds and
L
is strictly concave on
(0,y_{C}]
(e.g.
0<\delta\leq 1
, or
\delta\geq 2
with (
4.4
)). The interior equilibria of (
2.5
) are classified by the grazing regime:

(i)

Low grazing.
For
0<g<g_{0}
there is no interior equilibrium.

(ii)

Intermediate grazing.
For
g_{0}<g<g_{1}
there is a unique interior equilibrium
E^{\star}=(K(y^{\star}),y^{\star})
, and it is a saddle.

(iii)

High grazing.
Let
g_{1}<g<\gamma-n
. If
g^{\star}=g_{1}
, there is no interior equilibrium. If
g^{\star}>g_{1}
, there is none for
g^{\star}<g<\gamma-n
, exactly two for
g_{1}<g<g^{\star}
, a stable node
E_{1}^{\star}=(K(y_{1}^{\star}),y_{1}^{\star})
and a saddle
E_{2}^{\star}=(K(y_{2}^{\star}),y_{2}^{\star})
with
0<y_{1}^{\star}<y_{2}^{\star}<y_{C}
, and a single nonhyperbolic equilibrium
E_{3}^{\star}=(K(y_{3}^{\star}),y_{3}^{\star})
at
g=g^{\star}
.

Proof.

(i)
By (
4.2
),
L(0)>0
and
L(y_{C})>0
(as
g<g_{0}
). Writing
y=(1-\theta)y_{C}
with
\theta\in[0,1]
, concavity gives

L(y)\geq\theta L(0)+(1-\theta)L(y_{C})>0\,,

so that
L>0
on
[0,y_{C}]
and there is no root.

(ii)
By (
4.2
),
L(0)>0>L(y_{C})
(as
g_{0}<g<g_{1}
), so
L
has a root
y^{\star}\in(0,y_{C})
. If
0<y_{1}<y_{2}<y_{C}
were two roots, then writing
y_{1}=(1-\theta)y_{2}
with
\theta\in(0,1)
, concavity would give

0=L(y_{1})\geq\theta L(0)+(1-\theta)L(y_{2})=\theta L(0)>0\,,

a contradiction, so that the root is unique. Finally, by the Mean Value Theorem there is some
c\in(0,y^{\star})
with

L^{\prime}(c)=\frac{L(y^{\star})-L(0)}{y^{\star}}=-\frac{L(0)}{y^{\star}}<0\,,

and since
L^{\prime}
is decreasing we have
L^{\prime}(y^{\star})\leq L^{\prime}(c)<0
, so that the corresponding equilibrium
E^{\star}
is a saddle.

(iii)
By (
4.2
),
L(0)<0
and
L(y_{C})<0
(as
g>g_{1}
). If
g^{\star}=g_{1}
, then
\tilde{g}\leq g_{1}<g
, hence
L<0
on
[0,y_{C}]
and there is no interior equilibrium. Assume henceforth
g^{\star}>g_{1}
, so that the maximum of
\tilde{g}
is attained at an interior point
\hat{y}\in(0,y_{C})
, as
\tilde{g}(0)=g_{1}<g^{\star}
and
\tilde{g}(y_{C})=g_{0}<g^{\star}
. We distinguish three cases.

(a)
For
g^{\star}<g<\gamma-n
one has
\tilde{g}<g
, hence
L<0
and there is no interior equilibrium.

(b)
For
g_{1}<g<g^{\star}
,

L(\hat{y})=\frac{g^{\star}-g}{1-\hat{y}}>0\,,\qquad L(0)<0\,,\qquad L(y_{C})<0\,,

so
L
has roots
y_{1}^{\star}\in(0,\hat{y})
and
y_{2}^{\star}\in(\hat{y},y_{C})
. Strict concavity precludes a third root, since three roots
y_{1}<y_{2}<y_{3}
would force
L(y_{2})>0
by the argument of part (ii). As
L>0
on
(y_{1}^{\star},y_{2}^{\star})
, vanishes at the endpoints, and
L^{\prime}
is strictly decreasing,

L^{\prime}(y_{1}^{\star})>0>L^{\prime}(y_{2}^{\star})\,,

so the corresponding equilibria are a stable node
E_{1}^{\star}
and a saddle
E_{2}^{\star}
.

(c)
For
g=g^{\star}
,
L\leq 0
with equality only at the unique maximizer
y_{3}^{\star}
of
\tilde{g}
(unique by strict concavity), where

L(y_{3}^{\star})=0\,,\qquad L^{\prime}(y_{3}^{\star})=0\,,

the second equality because
y_{3}^{\star}
is an interior maximum. By (
4.8
) and (
4.6
),

\det J(E_{3}^{\star})=0\,,\qquad\operatorname{tr}J(E_{3}^{\star})<0\,.

∎

Together with Lemma
3.2
and Theorems
3.3
and
3.5
, this yields the classification summarized in Table

and illustrated in Figure

g^{\star}>g_{1}

into

at

is a saddle-node bifurcation (Theorem

and Proposition

), illustrated in Figure

.

4.4.
The non-concave regime
1<\delta<2

When
1<\delta<2
, (
4.3
) gives

\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}L^{\prime\prime}(y)=+\infty\,,

so that
L
is convex near
y=0
and the concavity arguments of Subsection
4.3
no longer apply. We show that
L^{\prime\prime\prime}<0
on
(0,y_{C})
(Lemma
4.4
), which limits the system to at most three interior equilibria and preserves the high-grazing conclusions of Theorem
4.3
(Proposition
4.5
).

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume (
GA
) and
1<\delta<2
. Then
L^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)<0
and
\tilde{g}^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)<0
for
y\in(0,y_{C})
. Consequently
L^{\prime}
has at most one interior maximum, hence at most two zeros, and
\tilde{g}
has at most one interior local maximum and one interior local minimum.

Proof.

By (
4.3
),

L^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)=\frac{\gamma\delta(\delta-1)(\delta-2)d}{a+b}y^{\delta-3}-\frac{6g}{(1-y)^{4}}\,,

and for
1<\delta<2
both terms are negative, since
\delta(\delta-1)>0
and
\delta-2<0
, and hence
L^{\prime\prime\prime}<0
. Moreover
\tilde{g}(y)=(1-y)L(y)+g
, so that
\tilde{g}^{\prime\prime\prime}=-3L^{\prime\prime}+(1-y)L^{\prime\prime\prime}
, and substituting (
4.3
) gives

\tilde{g}^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)=\frac{\gamma\delta(\delta-1)d}{a+b}y^{\delta-3}\big[(\delta-2)-(\delta+1)y\big]<0\,,\qquad y\in(0,y_{C})\,,

the bracket being negative because
\delta-2<0
. As
L^{\prime\prime\prime}<0
,
L^{\prime\prime}
is strictly decreasing, and since
L^{\prime\prime}(0^{+})=+\infty
it has at most one zero in
(0,y_{C})
, so
L^{\prime}
has at most one interior maximum and hence at most two zeros. As
\tilde{g}^{\prime\prime\prime}<0
,
\tilde{g}^{\prime}
is strictly concave and so has at most two zeros, whence
\tilde{g}
has at most one interior local maximum and one interior local minimum.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Assume (
GA
) and
1<\delta<2
. Then (
2.5
) has at most three interior equilibria
(K(y^{\star}),y^{\star})
, each a stable node or a saddle according as
L^{\prime}(y^{\star})>0
or
L^{\prime}(y^{\star})<0
(Subsection
4.2
). For
g>g_{1}
there are at most two, and when two exist the one of smaller coral cover is a stable node and the other a saddle. The number of simple interior equilibria is even for
g<g_{0}
and for
g>g_{1}
, and odd for
g_{0}<g<g_{1}
.

Proof.

(a)
By Lemma
4.4
,
L^{\prime}
has at most two zeros in
(0,y_{C})
, so by Rolle’s theorem
L
has at most three roots there. As the interior equilibria are exactly the points
(K(y^{\star}),y^{\star})
with
y^{\star}\in(0,y_{C})
a root of
L
(Subsection
4.1
and Lemma
4.1
), there are at most three of them.

(b)
For
g>g_{1}
, (
4.2
) gives
L(0)=g_{1}-g<0
. Were
L
to have three roots
y_{1}<y_{2}<y_{3}
, Rolle would yield two zeros of
L^{\prime}
, which by Lemma
4.4
are its only zeros. As
L^{\prime}
has a single interior maximum,
L^{\prime}<0
on
(0,y_{1})
, whence
L(0)>L(y_{1})=0
, contradicting
L(0)<0
. Hence at most two roots remain. For two simple roots
y_{1}<y_{2}
the endpoint signs
L(0),L(y_{C})<0
force

L<0\ \text{ on }\ (0,y_{1})\cup(y_{2},y_{C})\,,\qquad L>0\ \text{ on }\ (y_{1},y_{2})\,,

so that

L^{\prime}(y_{1})>0>L^{\prime}(y_{2})\,,

and hence
(K(y_{1}),y_{1})
is a stable node and
(K(y_{2}),y_{2})
a saddle.

(c)
By (
4.2
),

\mathrm{sign}L(0)=\mathrm{sign}(g_{1}-g)\,,\qquad\mathrm{sign}L(y_{C})=\mathrm{sign}(g_{0}-g)\,.

A continuous function has an even or odd number of sign-changing zeros depending on whether its endpoint values agree or differ in sign. Since
g_{0}<g_{1}
, these agree for
g<g_{0}
, differ for
g_{0}<g<g_{1}
, and agree for
g>g_{1}
, so the number of simple interior equilibria is even, odd, and even in these three regimes.
∎

For
g_{0}<g<g_{1}
there can be three interior equilibria, a tristable regime illustrated in Figure
5
.

Remark 4.6
.

By Proposition
4.5
(a),(b), the high-grazing conclusion of Theorem
4.3
(iii) remains valid for
1<\delta<2
: for
g>g_{1}
there are at most two interior equilibria, a stable node and a saddle. For smaller
g
the convexity of
L
near
y=0
can give the strictly concave
\tilde{g}^{\prime}
two interior zeros, so
\tilde{g}
has an interior local minimum, and a second pair of interior equilibria can raise the count to three over a narrow range of
g
.
When
\tilde{g}
is monotone on
[0,y_{C}]
, it can only decrease, since
\tilde{g}(0)=g_{1}
exceeds
\tilde{g}(y_{C})=g_{0}
(Lemma
3.7
), so
g^{\star}=g_{1}
, leaving at most one interior equilibrium. A parameter set realizing three coexisting interior equilibria is examined in Subsection
6.1
.

5.
Bifurcation Analysis

We now describe the bifurcations through which the equilibria of (
2.5
) exchange stability and are created or destroyed as the grazing intensity
g
varies. The bifurcation diagrams of Figures
4
and
5
display these bifurcations. By Section
3
the boundary equilibria
B
and
C
are nonhyperbolic at
g=g_{1}
and
g=g_{0}
, and by Section
4
the two interior equilibria of the high-grazing regime coalesce into the nonhyperbolic equilibrium
E_{3}^{\star}
at
g=g^{\star}
. We show that the former undergo transcritical bifurcations and the latter a saddle-node bifurcation. We write system (
2.5
) as

(5.1)

\dot{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},g)\,,\qquad\mathbf{X}=(x,y)^{\top}\,,\quad\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},g)=\begin{pmatrix}xF(x,y)\\
yG(x,y)\end{pmatrix}\,,

and invoke Sotomayor’s theorem
[
15
, Sec. 4.2]
: if
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X}_{0},g^{\dagger})=0

and
D\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X}_{0},g^{\dagger})
has a simple zero eigenvalue with right eigenvector
\mathbf{U}
and left eigenvector
\mathbf{W}
, then at
g=g^{\dagger}
the system undergoes a
transcritical
bifurcation if

(5.2)

\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbf{X}_{0},g^{\dagger})=0\,,\quad\mathbf{W}^{\top}\big(D\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbf{X}_{0},g^{\dagger})\mathbf{U}\big)\neq 0\,,\quad\mathbf{W}^{\top}\big(D^{2}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X}_{0},g^{\dagger})(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U})\big)\neq 0\,,

and a
saddle-node
bifurcation if

(5.3)

\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbf{X}_{0},g^{\dagger})\neq 0\,,\quad\mathbf{W}^{\top}\big(D^{2}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X}_{0},g^{\dagger})(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U})\big)\neq 0\,.

Here
\mathbf{F}_{g}=\partial_{g}\mathbf{F}
,
D\mathbf{F}_{g}
is its Jacobian in
\mathbf{X}
, and
D^{2}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U})=\big(\mathbf{U}^{\top}(D^{2}F_{1})\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\top}(D^{2}F_{2})\mathbf{U}\big)^{\top}
with
D^{2}F_{i}
the Hessian of the
i
-th component.

5.1.
Transcritical bifurcations at the boundary equilibria

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose (
GA
) holds and
\delta\in\mathbb{N}
. If

(5.4)

g_{1}\neq a-\gamma+\tfrac{\gamma}{a+b}\big(b+d\mathds{1}_{\{\delta=1\}}\big)\,,

then system (
2.5
) undergoes a transcritical bifurcation at
B
as
g
crosses
g_{1}
.

Proof.

Center
B
at the origin by
X=x-x_{B}
,
Y=y
, where
x_{B}=1-\tfrac{g+n}{\gamma}
depends on
g
, so that the origin is an equilibrium for every
g
. In these coordinates (
5.1
) reads
\dot{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},g)
with
\mathbf{X}=(X,Y)^{\top}
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},g)=\begin{pmatrix}F_{1}(\mathbf{X},g)\\
F_{2}(\mathbf{X},g)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(X+x_{B})\,F(X+x_{B},Y)\\
Y\,G(X+x_{B},Y)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(X+x_{B})\big[\gamma(1-X-x_{B})-n+(a-\gamma)Y-\tfrac{g}{1-Y}\big]\\[4.0pt]
Y\big[-(a+m_{0})+\tfrac{(a+b)(g+n)}{\gamma}-(a+b)X-bY-dY^{\delta}\big]\end{pmatrix}\,,

the lower entry written using this value of
x_{B}
.
For
\delta\in\mathbb{N}
the component
F_{2}
is a polynomial in
(X,Y)
, so
\mathbf{F}
is smooth near the origin. At
g=g_{1}
one has
\tfrac{(a+b)(g_{1}+n)}{\gamma}=a+m_{0}
, so

D\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{0},g_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&x_{B}(a-\gamma-g_{1})\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\lambda_{1}=-(\gamma-g_{1}-n)<0\,,

and the zero eigenvalue is simple, with right and left eigenvectors

\mathbf{U}=\Big(-\tfrac{x_{B}(a-\gamma-g_{1})}{\lambda_{1}},1\Big)^{\top}\,,\qquad\mathbf{W}=(0,1)^{\top}\,.

As
\mathbf{W}=(0,1)^{\top}
, the conditions (
5.2
) involve only
F_{2}
. From
\partial_{g}F_{2}(\mathbf{X},g)=\tfrac{a+b}{\gamma}Y
,

\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbf{0},g_{1})=0\,,\qquad\mathbf{W}^{\top}\big(D\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbf{0},g_{1})\mathbf{U}\big)=DF_{2,g}(\mathbf{0},g_{1})\mathbf{U}=\tfrac{a+b}{\gamma}\neq 0\,.

Since
F_{2}(\cdot,\cdot,g_{1})=-(a+b)XY-bY^{2}-dY^{\delta+1}
, its Hessian at the origin is

D^{2}F_{2}(\mathbf{0},g_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}0&-(a+b)\\
-(a+b)&-2(b+d\mathds{1}_{\{\delta=1\}})\end{pmatrix}\,,

and therefore

\mathbf{W}^{\top}\big(D^{2}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{0},g_{1})(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U})\big)=\mathbf{U}^{\top}D^{2}F_{2}(\mathbf{0},g_{1})\mathbf{U}=2\Big(\tfrac{(a+b)x_{B}(a-\gamma-g_{1})}{\lambda_{1}}-\big(b+d\mathds{1}_{\{\delta=1\}}\big)\Big)\,,

which vanishes only for
g_{1}=a-\gamma+\tfrac{\gamma}{a+b}(b+d\mathds{1}_{\{\delta=1\}})
, excluded by hypothesis. Hence (
5.2
) holds, and as the centering is a smooth change of coordinates, (
2.5
) undergoes a transcritical bifurcation at
B
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The restriction
\delta\in\mathbb{N}
guarantees that
Y^{\delta+1}
, and thus
F_{2}
, is smooth on a full neighborhood of
Y=0
, including
Y<0
, as Sotomayor’s theorem requires, and the crowding coefficient
d
enters the nondegeneracy condition (
5.4
) only when
\delta=1
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Suppose (
GA
) holds. If

(5.5)

g_{0}\neq(1-y_{C})^{2}\Big(a-\gamma+\tfrac{\gamma(b+\delta dy_{C}^{\delta-1})}{a+b}\Big)\,,

then for every
\delta>0
system (
2.5
) undergoes a transcritical bifurcation at
C
as
g
crosses
g_{0}
.

Proof.

Since
C=(0,y_{C})
is independent of
g
, we work directly with (
5.1
). At
g=g_{0}
one has
\mu_{1}=F(0,y_{C})=0
, so

D\mathbf{F}(C,g_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
-(a+b)y_{C}&\mu_{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\mu_{2}=-by_{C}-\delta dy_{C}^{\delta}<0\,,

and the zero eigenvalue is simple, with

\mathbf{U}=\Big(\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{(a+b)y_{C}},1\Big)^{\top}\,,\qquad\mathbf{W}=(1,0)^{\top}\,.

As
\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbf{X},g)=\big(-\tfrac{x}{1-y},0\big)^{\top}
vanishes at
C
(where
x=0
),
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{g}(C,g_{0})=0
, while

\mathbf{W}^{\top}\big(D\mathbf{F}_{g}(C,g_{0})\mathbf{U}\big)=-\tfrac{U_{1}}{1-y_{C}}=-\tfrac{\mu_{2}}{(a+b)y_{C}(1-y_{C})}>0\,.

Since
\mathbf{W}=(1,0)^{\top}
, the last condition involves only
F_{1}(\mathbf{X},g)=xF(x,y)
, with Hessian

D^{2}F_{1}(C,g_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}-2\gamma&(a-\gamma)-\tfrac{g_{0}}{(1-y_{C})^{2}}\\[2.0pt]
(a-\gamma)-\tfrac{g_{0}}{(1-y_{C})^{2}}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

so that

\mathbf{W}^{\top}\big(D^{2}\mathbf{F}(C,g_{0})(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U})\big)=\mathbf{U}^{\top}D^{2}F_{1}(C,g_{0})\mathbf{U}=2U_{1}\Big(a-\gamma-\tfrac{g_{0}}{(1-y_{C})^{2}}-\gamma U_{1}\Big)\,.

Using
U_{1}=-\tfrac{b+\delta dy_{C}^{\delta-1}}{a+b}
, this vanishes only for
g_{0}=(1-y_{C})^{2}\big(a-\gamma+\tfrac{\gamma(b+\delta dy_{C}^{\delta-1})}{a+b}\big)
, excluded by hypothesis. Thus (
5.2
) holds and (
2.5
) undergoes a transcritical bifurcation at
C
.
∎

Remark 5.4

coincide with the coral-only state

5.2.
Saddle-node bifurcation of interior equilibria

Theorem 5.5
.

Suppose (
GA
) holds, that
L
is strictly concave on
(0,y_{C}]
(e.g.
0<\delta\leq 1
, or
\delta\geq 2
with (

. Then for every
\delta>0
system (

) undergoes a saddle-node bifurcation at the interior equilibrium
E_{3}^{\star}=(x_{3}^{\star},y_{3}^{\star})
as

g^{\star}

Proof.

By Theorem
4.3
(iii)(c), at
g=g^{\star}
the equilibrium
E_{3}^{\star}
satisfies
\det J(E_{3}^{\star})=0
and
\operatorname{tr}J(E_{3}^{\star})<0
, so its zero eigenvalue is simple. Using
L^{\prime}(y_{3}^{\star})=0
, equivalently
\partial_{y}F(E_{3}^{\star})=-\tfrac{\gamma(b+\delta d(y_{3}^{\star})^{\delta-1})}{a+b}=\gamma U_{1}
, the eigenvectors are

\mathbf{U}=\Big(-\tfrac{b+\delta d(y_{3}^{\star})^{\delta-1}}{a+b},1\Big)^{\top}\,,\qquad\mathbf{W}=\Big(1,-\tfrac{\gamma x_{3}^{\star}}{(a+b)y_{3}^{\star}}\Big)^{\top}\,.

Then
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{g}(E_{3}^{\star},g^{\star})=-\tfrac{x_{3}^{\star}}{1-y_{3}^{\star}}\neq 0
, so the bifurcation is not transcritical. For the second condition, the Hessians of
F_{1}(\mathbf{X},g)=xF(x,y)
and
F_{2}(\mathbf{X},g)=yG(x,y)
at
E_{3}^{\star}
give, using
\partial_{y}F(E_{3}^{\star})=\gamma U_{1}
and
(a+b)U_{1}=-(b+\delta d(y_{3}^{\star})^{\delta-1})
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\top}D^{2}F_{1}(E_{3}^{\star},g^{\star})\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{2g^{\star}x_{3}^{\star}}{(1-y_{3}^{\star})^{3}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\top}D^{2}F_{2}(E_{3}^{\star},g^{\star})\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle=-2(a+b)U_{1}-2b-\delta(\delta+1)d(y_{3}^{\star})^{\delta-1}\,.

Combining these with
\mathbf{W}=(1,-\tfrac{\gamma x_{3}^{\star}}{(a+b)y_{3}^{\star}})^{\top}
and simplifying by (

),

\mathbf{W}^{\top}\big(D^{2}\mathbf{F}(E_{3}^{\star},g^{\star})(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U})\big)=-\tfrac{2g^{\star}x_{3}^{\star}}{(1-y_{3}^{\star})^{3}}+\tfrac{\gamma x_{3}^{\star}\delta(\delta-1)d(y_{3}^{\star})^{\delta-2}}{a+b}=x_{3}^{\star}L^{\prime\prime}(y_{3}^{\star})\,.

Since

is strictly concave,

, so this is nonzero. Thus (
5.3
) holds and (
2.5
) undergoes a saddle-node bifurcation at
E_{3}^{\star}
.
∎

7.
Conclusion

We have analyzed a coral–macroalgae competition model in which coral mortality increases with cover through a crowding term
dC^{\delta}
. The system has no periodic orbit (Lemma
2.1
), and its dynamics are organized by the grazing rate
g
through two ordered thresholds
0<g_{0}<g_{1}<\gamma-n
: the coral-only state
C
is stable for
g>g_{0}
and the macroalgae-only state
B
for
g<g_{1}
, and both are stable on the intermediate range. As
g
crosses these thresholds,
C
and
B
exchange stability with a coexistence equilibrium through transcritical bifurcations (Theorems
5.3
and
5.1
, the latter for
\delta\in\mathbb{N}
). When the reduced function
L
is concave, as in the constant-mortality model of
[
14
,
12
]
, high grazing admits at most two coexistence states, a stable node and a saddle that collide in a saddle-node bifurcation at a third threshold
g^{\star}
(Theorem
5.5
). Intermediate crowding
1<\delta<2
makes
L
non-concave and can produce as many as three coexistence states (Proposition
4.5
), so that the reef can be tristable, a regime beyond the reach of constant mortality. More broadly, the analysis shows how density-dependent crowding reshapes the coral–macroalgae dynamics across all crowding exponents: it shifts the grazing thresholds and the coral nullcline for every
\delta>0
, whereas this qualitatively new behavior is confined to that intermediate regime.

These results have direct ecological implications. The dynamics take place in the positively invariant region
\Omega
, where the model has no limit cycle (Lemma
2.1
). Coral and macroalgae cover therefore cannot settle into sustained oscillations.
We have characterized the local stability of the equilibria, and the phase portraits of Section
6
show the reef approaching one of the stable ones according to the grazing regime and the initial cover. The bistable window
g_{0}<g<g_{1}
is, moreover, the source of hysteresis: as grazing declines from a level at which coral dominates, the coral-dominated state remains stable until
g
falls below
g_{0}
, where it loses stability through the transcritical bifurcation of Theorem
5.3
and the reef shifts abruptly to macroalgae dominance. Recovery does not retrace this path, since the macroalgae-dominated state remains stable throughout
g<g_{1}
: returning grazing to
g_{0}
leaves the reef macroalgae-dominated, and coral is regained only once
g
exceeds the higher threshold
g_{1}
, where the macroalgae-dominated state itself loses stability (Theorem
5.1
, for integer
\delta
). The gap
g_{1}-g_{0}
thus measures the additional grazing that recovery demands, and the wider it is, the more resistant a degraded reef is to reversal.

Beyond the two dominance states, the model also admits a mixed coral–macroalgae community. In the bistable regime this interior equilibrium is a saddle, separating the two outcomes rather than offering a state the reef can rest in. Under stronger grazing it is joined by a stable node, an observable mixed community attained for a range of initial cover, and the two collide and disappear at
g^{\star}
in the saddle-node bifurcation of Theorem
5.5
, a further tipping point beyond which coral dominance is the only stable state. Crowding adds one further possibility: for intermediate
1<\delta<2
the mixed community can be stable alongside both dominance states, so that the reef admits three stable communities, the one realized being selected by the initial cover.

(A.2a)

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=c_{1,1}\bar{x}\bar{y}+c_{2,0}\bar{x}^{2}+c_{\delta+1,0}\bar{x}^{\delta+1}\,,

\displaystyle\bar{y}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\bar{y}+d_{1,1}\bar{x}\bar{y}+d_{0,2}\bar{y}^{2}+d_{2,0}\bar{x}^{2}+d_{2,1}\bar{x}^{2}\bar{y}+d_{3,0}\bar{x}^{3}+d_{\delta+1,0}\bar{x}^{\delta+1}+P_{3}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\,.

15
