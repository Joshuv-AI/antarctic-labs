Commitment to Indigenous Data Sovereignty
The National Laboratory of the Rockies and the Alliance for Tribal Clean Energy recognize that data is a vital resource for Tribal
Nations. As such, this project is committed to upholding the principles of Indigenous data sovereignty, which assert that Tribes
have the inherent right to govern the collection, ownership, application, and sharing of data about their communities, lands, and
resources. This project will integrate these principles to ensure that Tribal control over data is respected and that engagement
processes align with the cultural values and priorities of participating Tribes.

Guiding Principles
 1.	 Tribal ownership and control: All data collected during this project will be treated as the shared intellectual property of the
 respective Tribes. Tribal consent will be required before data are collected, analyzed, or shared.
 2.	 Free, prior, and informed consent: Tribes will be informed how their data will be used, stored, and shared before they
 choose to participate. Participation will be voluntary, and Tribes may withdraw at any time without consequence.
 3.	 Cultural sensitivity: Data collection methods and questions will be designed to align with the cultural values, traditions, and
 preferences of participating Tribes.
 4.	 Data minimization: Only data that are essential to achieving the objectives of the Tribal transportation electrification
 guidebook and fact sheets will be collected. This minimizes the risk of misuse or exploitation.
 5.	 Transparency and accountability: The National Laboratory of the Rockies and Alliance for Tribal Clean Energy will provide
 clear communication about how data are managed and will seek Tribal input at every stage of the project.
This project will prioritize Indigenous ways of knowing and Tribal governance frameworks throughout all phases. Engagement
methods will be tailored to the specific needs and preferences of each individual participating Tribe. This includes:

 • Ensuring questions, materials, and outputs are accessible and reflect the varied priorities and realities of Tribal communities.
 • Acknowledging and respecting traditional knowledge systems, including cultural, spiritual, and ecological, as equally valid and
 essential as Western scientific approaches.
 • Providing Tribes with opportunities to guide the direction of the project, including recommendations for future initiatives.

 1.0 Introduction
Electric vehicle (EV) adoption is increasing globally. In the United States, approximately 10% of new car
registrations were electric in 2023 (International Energy Agency 2024). The number of EV charging stations
across the United States is also growing rapidly, including in Indian Country. EV adoption and EV charging
infrastructure deployment could align with larger Tribal goals, including long-term economic development goals,
environmental goals, and Tribal sovereignty goals.

This project, Strategic Pathways for Indigenous Resilient and Innovative Transportation, serves as a
guidebook for Tribes interested in pursuing public EV charging infrastructure. The guidebook is designed
to serve Tribal planners, Tribal departments of transportation, environmental ofces, Tribal enterprises, Tribal
utility authorities, Tribal leadership, and any other interested parties.

This project is a partnership between the Alliance for Tribal Clean Energy and the National Laboratory of the
Rockies. It was funded by the Alliance for Tribal Clean Energy from 2024 to 2026. The guidebook was developed
using input from Tribal staf and leadership, gathered via interviews and a questionnaire.

This guidebook does not discuss private residential charging infrastructure, feet electrifcation, or medium-
and heavy-duty EVs. Resources about those topics are provided in Appendix E. This guidebook focuses on the
contiguous United States and does not cover the diferent circumstances in Alaska or Hawaii.

EVs are charged by plugging them in EV charging infrastructure. A station location is the physical place with one
or more EV charging ports, such as a convenience store parking lot. An EV charging port (also called a charger) provides
power to charge an EV by plugging a connector (also called a plug) into the vehicle. An EV charging port can have
multiple connectors, but only one vehicle can charge at a time. Figure 1 illustrates EV charging infrastructure terminology.

Figure 1. Illustration of charging infrastructure terminology (AFDC n.d.-b)

There are three primary types of connectors:

 • J3400 (North American Charging System): Works for all charging levels. Many EV manufacturers are adopting this
 connector type for new models.

 • Combined Charging System (also known as SAE J1772): Allows drivers to use the same charge port with Level 1, Level 2,
 and DCFC equipment. Most EV models on the market can use this connector.

 • CHAdeMO: DCFC connector most common for Japanese automakers, but mostly being phased out from new vehicle
 models. The CHAdeMO connector is still available at some stations to serve older models.
