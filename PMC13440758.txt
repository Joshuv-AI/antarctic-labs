STM investigation of atomic-scale structures of Ag/Au(111) surface
Authors: Hirotoshi Kodama, Shunsuke Nagai, Minhui Lee, Yousoo Kim, Emiko Kazuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An atomic-level understanding of Ag–Au heterostructures is crucial for elucidating the catalytic and plasmonic properties of Ag–Au bimetallic nanostructures, as well as their potential applications. In this study, we investigated the surface structures of Ag formed on an Au(111) surface using scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) to gain atomic-level insight. When the substrate temperature during Ag deposition was at room temperature, two distinct ordered structures were observed on the Au(111) surface: island structures on terraces and (√3 × √3)R30° periodic structures near step edges. In contrast, the island structures did not form under the elevated substrate-temperature conditions up to 150 °C. Atomic-resolution STM images revealed that these structures consist of Ag adatoms and exhibit the periodicities with respect to the underlying Au(111) lattice that are distinct from those of previously reported epitaxial Ag(111) film structures.

Bimetallic nanostructures, such as core–shell nanostructures, hetero-nanostructures, and nanoalloys, exhibit unique physical properties arising from nanoscale effects,1 heterointerfaces,2 and, particularly in nanoalloys, alloying effects.3 In core–shell nanostructures, where a core of a first metal is coated with a shell of a second metal, the reactivity and stability can be tuned through the interaction between the core and shell materials. These distinctive properties have enabled a wide range of applications in catalysis, electronics, photonics, and biomedicine.4

Among bimetallic nanostructures, Ag–Au nanoparticles composed of silver (Ag) and gold (Au) are expected to be applied to various catalytic reactions, including low-temperature CO oxidation.5–8 In addition, they have broad applications in plasmonics, such as plasmonic catalysts, plasmonic sensors, and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS).9–12 Ag and Au are highly miscible, forming a complete solid solution in bulk alloys.13 However, surface regions are governed by thermodynamic principles distinct from the bulk, allowing a variety of nanostructures and atomic arrangements to be stabilized.14 Furthermore, the surface structures at heterointerfaces between different metals have not yet been fully elucidated at the atomic level.

Previous studies of Ag–Au heterostructures using scanning tunnelling microscopy (STM) have revealed the formation of Ag two-dimensional (2D) film and three-dimensional (3D) island structures for both vacuum-deposited15–19 and electrodeposited20,21 systems. For vacuum-deposited systems, when Ag is deposited on a Au(111) substrate at room temperature, it forms an Ag(111)/Au(111) surface where Ag grows epitaxially from the step edges along the Au(111) surface, exhibiting layer-by-layer growth with increasing Ag coverage.15 While several similar Ag 2D film structures on the Au(111) surface have been reported, their atomic-scale morphology, electronic structure, and physical properties, such as catalytic activities and optical characteristics, have not been fully understood. Furthermore, although diverse atomic arrangements near the surface are anticipated, reported atomic structures to date have been limited to epitaxially grown Ag(111) 2D films.

In this study, we reveal the formation of two distinct Ag structures on the Au(111) surface, governed by the Ag deposition conditions, through atomic-scale STM analysis. These structures consist of Ag adatoms arranged in configurations that are fundamentally different from those of previously reported epitaxial Ag(111) films.

All experiments were performed using a low-temperature scanning tunneling microscope (Scienta Omicron) with a W tip under ultrahigh vacuum conditions (∼1 × 10−10 torr) at ∼4 K. All voltages (V) refer to the sample bias voltage. An atomically smooth and clean Au(111) surface was prepared through repeated cycles of Ar+ sputtering at 0.5 keV and annealing at ∼500 °C. Ag was deposited on the Au(111) surface by thermal evaporation from a 99.99% pure Ag wire using resistive heating in a vacuum chamber. The deposition temperature and rate were ∼710 °C and 0.001–0.02 ML min−1, respectively. The nominal Ag deposition rate was determined using a quartz crystal microbalance, while the actual Ag coverage was estimated from STM images.

Fig. 1a shows an STM image of the sample surface prepared by deposition of ∼0.003 ML Ag on a clean Au(111) surface (Fig. S1) at room temperature. Ag was observed only as isolated atoms on the terraces, with no formation of ordered assemblies. Atomic-resolution STM images reveal that each Ag atom is located at a hollow site on the Au(111) surface (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, Ag atoms can be laterally manipulated across the surface using the STM tip (Fig. S2). These results indicate that the isolated Ag atoms are adatoms rather than substitutional atoms.

By increasing the deposition amount to ∼0.5 ML of Ag on a clean Au(111), two distinct types of ordered structures were observed: one on the terraces and the other near the step edges (Fig. S3). The ordered structures on the terraces, namely island structures, form predominantly within the fcc regions of the herringbone reconstruction of the Au(111) surface (Fig. 2a). High-magnification images reveal striped patterns along the [112̄] direction within the island structures (Fig. 2b). In addition, atomic-level observations of these striped structures show that 10–15 atoms are aligned along the [1̄10] direction of the Au(111) surface to form one-dimensional chain-like structures, which are aligned at every second atomic row along the [112̄] direction (Fig. 2c and d). Since all atoms forming the island structures are located at hollow sites, the islands are likely to consist of Ag adatoms. Scanning the surface under a high voltage demonstrated that the island structure partially decomposed during scanning and migrated across the Au(111) surface (Fig. S4). Note that the herringbone structures underlying the island structures remained unchanged. These results indicate that the island structures are composed of Ag adatoms without mixing with the underlying Au surface atoms.

A high bias may promote Ag adatom migration by proving sufficient energy to overcome the diffusion barrier. Similar bias-induced motion of weakly bound adatoms has been reported on noble-metal surfaces.22 Thus, it can be concluded that the island structures formed on the terraces constitute heterointerface structures, where the Ag adatoms are aligned in one-dimensional chains that are further ordered in two dimensions (Fig. 2e).

Near the step edge of Au(111), a structure with a periodicity different from that of the island structures on the terrace was observed (Fig. 3a). Atomic-resolution STM images reveal that this structure forms when Ag atoms adsorb every other hollow sites, with all atoms arranged along the 〈11̄0〉 direction of the Au(111) surface with a (√3 × √3)R30° periodicity (Fig. 3b, c and S5c). Several Ag atoms form a single domain (Ag atomic domain), and the domain boundaries are considered to be defined either by the partial adsorption of second-layer Ag atoms on the first-layer Ag atomic rows or by the presence of atoms with different electronic states arising from Ag–Au mixing. The unit cell within each domain is shifted by one atom relative to the adjacent domain, indicating a different relative arrangement with respect to the underlying Au(111) lattice (Fig. 3d). Notably, Ag atomic domains with the same registry relative to the underlying Au(111) surface do not adjoin each other.

Furthermore, no herringbone pattern was observed within the periodic structure (Fig. 3a), suggesting that the Au(111) surface was reconstructed by the periodic adsorption of Ag atoms.

While both island structures on terraces and periodic structures near step edges were observed for the deposition at room temperature (Fig. S3), island structures were not observed under the elevated substrate-temperature conditions up to 150 °C. This suggests that at lower substrate temperatures during deposition, the diffusion length of Ag atoms becomes short, leading to the nucleation on terraces followed by growth into island structures. More specifically, adsorbed Ag atoms in the fcc region of the Au(111) surface are kinetically favored to form one-dimensional chains, and as the chain length increases, two-dimensional growth competitively occurs to minimize the surface energy. By contrast, as the substrate temperature increases, Ag atoms can diffuse to and become trapped at the step edges, forming the (√3 × √3)R30° periodic structures. It should be noted that the island structures did not transform into (√3 × √3)R30° periodic structures even after post-annealing at approximately 150 °C, as confirmed by five independent experiments. These results indicate that the substrate temperature during Ag deposition is an influential factor in the formation and growth mechanisms of the Ag structures on the Au(111) surface.

The (√3 × √3)R30° periodic structures near step edges differ from the epitaxial Ag(111) films formed near step edges reported in previous studies.15–19 However, in regions with relatively high Ag coverage, both (√3 × √3)R30° periodic structures and epitaxial Ag(111) films were observed to coexist (Fig. S6). In contrast, the coexistence of the island structures shown in Fig. 2 with the epitaxial Ag(111) films was never observed. These results suggest that the formation of the Ag(111) atomic arrangement is suppressed at low Ag coverage, and that both the substrate temperature during deposition and Ag coverage play important roles in the formation of Ag structures. It remains unclear whether these Ag adatom phases serve as precursors to epitaxial Ag(111) film growth or instead represent independent growth modes, and further systematic studies are required.

In conclusion, our atomic-scale STM study revealed that two distinct ordered structures of Ag adatoms form on terraces and near step edges of the Au(111) surface. On the terraces, 10–15 Ag atoms align along the [1̄10] direction of the Au(111) surface to form one-dimensional chains, which were further arranged at every other atomic site along the [112̄] direction, resulting in the formation of island structures. In contrast, near the step edges, Ag adatoms adsorb at every other hollow site, forming (√3 × √3)R30° periodic structures. The formation and growth of these structures are likely influenced by both the substrate temperature and Ag coverage. These findings provide fundamental insights into Ag–Au heterointerfacial structures and are expected to advance understanding of structure-dependent properties in Ag–Au bimetallic systems, particularly with respect to their catalytic behavior and localized surface plasmon resonances.
