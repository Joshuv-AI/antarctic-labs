Mechanistically Eliciting Latent Behaviors in Language Models
Andrew Mack, Nina Panickssery, Alexander Matt Turner

Abstract
We aim to discover diverse, generalizable perturbations of LLM internals that can surface hidden behavioral modes. Such perturbations could help reshape model behavior and systematically evaluate potential risks. We introduce Causal Perturbative Elicitation (CPE), an unsupervised method for discovering interpretable low-rank adapters (LoRAs) that can elicit these latent behaviors. CPE decomposes the computations of a deep transformer slice using a heuristic tensor-decomposition-based algorithm. CPE exhibits remarkable data efficiency, learning a large number of interpretable LoRAs from a single example . Even though CPE is unsupervised, we find that in some cases it can be competitive with supervised elicitation methods via brute-force enumerative search over weight space. For instance, CPE performs similarly to matched-wall-clock-time GRPO ( 31 ) on the Countdown task ( 21 ) for Qwen3-8B (85% vs 87%), demonstrating that CPE can efficiently elicit complex multi-token behaviors. Since CPE is unsupervised, it can also surface hidden failure modes, such as sandbagging, restoring 85% of locked BigCodeBench ( 46 ) performance on a password-locked version of Llama3-70B introduced by ( 36 ) . Additionally, since CPE explores behaviors in weight-space rather than token-space it can potentially ameliorate exploration hacking, a misalignment failure which may arise in sufficiently self-aware AI models ( 28 ) . In fact, we find that CPE virtually eliminates alignment-faking ( 15 ) behavior in a Llama3-70B-based model organism developed by ( 20 ) . Finally, we find that CPE can be used to initialize GPT-OSS-20B in an “aligned basin” when running GRPO on an environment prone to reward-hacking. By providing a data-efficient method to systematically explore the space of latent model behaviors, CPE yields a powerful tool for aligning AI systems and evaluating their safety.

Introduction

Modern LLMs can exhibit substantial mode collapse: they often default to a limited range of behaviors even when we suspect they have acquired far more diverse capabilities during training
(
23
;
1
)
. This collapse manifests as models consistently adopting narrow personas while potentially harboring latent modes of behavior that remain hidden under standard prompting conditions
(
12
;
41
;
42
)
. The existence of these latent modes poses both opportunities and risks: we may be leaving valuable capabilities untapped, while dangerous behaviors could emerge unexpectedly.

This problem becomes particularly pressing as we consider future AI systems that may develop more sophisticated strategies to resist behavioral modification. Concerns about gradient hacking
(
28
)
and alignment faking
(
15
)
suggest that future models might become increasingly “sticky,” resisting reinforcement learning (RL) approaches when we attempt to modify their behavior. The core issue is that traditional RL adds noise at output layers, where models can learn to resist changes. Ideally, we would discover meaningful perturbations at
early
layers, where resistance is harder. Such an approach could prove crucial for
reshaping model behavior
in future AI systems.

A second important problem is
systematically evaluating model behavior
. We require techniques that can comprehensively audit models for diverse failure modes, uncovering potentially harmful hidden personas. Additionally, we desire methods to systematically fuzz a model, eliciting concealed knowledge and capabilities.

Crucially, we need perturbations that generalize well. In behavior reshaping, generalization ensures consistent access to desired capabilities. In model evaluation, generalization increases our confidence that we’ve isolated a genuine behavioral mode rather than an isolated anomalous output.

We address these challenges by developing an unsupervised method to discover perturbations in early-layer weights which induce substantial downstream changes in the model’s internals. Our core hypothesis is that perturbations causing large downstream effects within the network (as measured by changes in later-layer activations) correspond to human-interpretable concepts and behavioral modes. Intuitively, meaningful concepts should activate entire computational pathways that propagate through the network, while random perturbations produce only local noise that quickly dissipates. By discovering causally important perturbations and using them to steer the network’s outputs, we can reliably elicit different behavioral modes, bypassing the limitations of surface-level prompting.

We mathematically search for diverse, causally important perturbations by learning a shallow MLP approximation of the causal structure of an intermediate, 10-20 layer transformer slice. We call our technique
Causal Perturbative Elicitation
(CPE). The key innovation is our causal training objective. Existing unsupervised methods like sparse autoencoders (SAEs)
(
2
)
discover interpretable features by learning to reconstruct activations subject to sparsity constraints. In contrast, CPE focuses on matching the differential structure of the transformer slice. This enables remarkable data efficiency compared to SAEs, discovering interpretable weight perturbations from as little as a single training example.

CPE elicits latent behaviors across a broad range of tasks. A summary of our findings is as follows:

Enhanced behavioral diversity

We demonstrate that CPE perturbations trained on Llama3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B elicit coherent personas on open-ended conversational prompts about as effectively as SAE-based steering vectors, even when training on only
one prompt
(as opposed to billions of tokens for the SAE baselines).

Comparison with SAEs and GRPO

We compare the effectiveness of CPE at eliciting a number of natural behaviors (reasoning, anti-sycophancy and jailbreaking) against standard unsupervised (SAE) and supervised (GRPO) baselines. We find that CPE is more effective at eliciting these behaviors than SAE feature steering, despite requiring a comparatively tiny amount of training data. Additionally CPE is sometimes as effective as GRPO at eliciting behaviors such as anti-sycophancy and reasoning.

Reward Hacking

In a competitive programming environment with mis-specified reward, we find that CPE can be used to initialize GRPO in an “aligned” basin of GPT-OSS-20B’s weight-space. In particular, CPE + GRPO on mis-specified reward achieves comparable gold-standard performance as running GRPO with the correctly specified reward, while random initialization leads to reward hacking and a collapse in gold-standard reward.

Sandbagging

We find that CPE restores 77% of password-locked coding performance on a password-locked version of Llama3-70B
(
36
)
.

Alignment-Faking

We find that CPE virtually eliminates alignment-faking in a synthetic-document-fine-tuned variant of Llama3-70B
(
20
)
.

Related work

Activation engineering.

Steering vectors are directions that induce specific behaviors when added to a model’s activation space. Prior work has successfully learned these vectors in a supervised manner for many high-level behaviors
(
35
;
38
;
47
;
29
)
. Meanwhile,
11
successfully steer models in weight space. We build on this literature by exploring how many behaviors we are able to elicit with a purely
unsupervised
method.

Sparse coding and feature learning.

The dominant approach to discovering interpretable features in neural networks relies on sparse coding methods, particularly sparse autoencoders (SAEs)
(
2
;
18
;
37
)
. While SAEs have shown promise in identifying behaviorally relevant features, they face fundamental limitations for our goals. Most critically, SAEs suffer from the “dark matter” problem of neural network interpretability
(
10
)
. Given that models likely contain far more features than dimensions in their residual stream, even the widest SAEs trained to date (with millions of features) potentially miss the vast majority of features. We thus need a data-efficient method to “fill in the gaps” on high-stakes datasets of interest, allowing us to discover important features that SAEs might miss. Moreover, as future models increasingly employ online reinforcement learning, their internal features may evolve over time, further necessitating more adaptive discovery methods that can operate with limited data.

Contrastive learning.

7
’s NoiseCLR uses a contrastive objective to learn meaningful representations in image diffusion models. NoiseCLR is similar to our causal learning objective in that NoiseCLR searches for vector perturbations which induce consistent changes in downstream activations. However, CPE achieve superior data efficiency, both in principle and in practice. Specifically, the main objective defined in equation (5) of
(
7
)
relies on computing pairwise similarities between downstream activation vectors
across data-points
, which is meaningless when applied to a single training example. In contrast, we show that training CPE on a
single
prompt can elicit generalizable behaviors.

Jacobian decompositions.

30
found that the right singular vectors of the Jacobian of the generator network in GANs yield a small number (
\sim 32
) of interpretable feature directions in generative image models.
4
found that Jacobian-based feature detection schemes are less successful when applied to language models. In contrast,
26
studies SVD applied to the empirical neural tangent kernel (the Jacobian of network outputs with respect to weights), discovering features in both toy models and language models, although they do not study steering. CPE works similarly to a Jacobian decomposition of a middle slice of a transformer network. However, our
theorem

3.1
shows (in a heuristic sense) that by training at a larger scale we implicitly decompose the higher-order differential tensors of the sliced transformer, allowing for the identification of non-orthogonal factor directions.

3
Method

We parameterize each CPE adapter as a collection of unit-norm rank-1 low-rank
adapters (LoRAs;
17
) applied to the attention output projection at several
consecutive source layers, and measure the resulting change in activations at a
fixed target layer.

Perturbation.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{L}\}
denote a set of source layers and
t
a
target layer with
\max\mathcal{S}<t
. For each CPE adapter
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}

and each layer
\ell\in\mathcal{S}
, we introduce a rank-1 perturbation to the
attention output weight
W_{O}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
(writing

d=d_{\textrm{model}}
):

W_{O}^{(\ell)}\;\longmapsto\;W_{O}^{(\ell)}+R\,\vec{b}_{i,\ell}\,\vec{a}_{i,\ell}^{\!\top},\qquad\|\vec{a}_{i,\ell}\|=\|\vec{b}_{i,\ell}\|=1,

(1)

where
R>0
is a shared scale parameter. The unit-norm constraint ensures
each rank-1 term has spectral norm
R
, providing a natural calibration to the
model’s internal scale.

We collect all LoRA components for a single adapter into a flattened vector

\phi\;=\;\operatorname{concat}\!\bigl(\vec{a}_{s_{1}},\,\vec{b}_{s_{1}},\;\dots,\;\vec{a}_{s_{L}},\,\vec{b}_{s_{L}}\bigr)\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{D},\qquad D=2Ld.

(2)

The unit-norm constraints on each component confine
\phi
to the torus

\mathcal{T}=\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}(S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1})
, so that

\|\phi\|^{2}=2L
.

Causal map.

Suppose we have a dataset of
n
prompts. Write

Z^{t}_{pj}(\phi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the layer-
t
activation of prompt
p

at token position
j
when the model runs with the
perturbation equation
1
specified by
\phi
at scale
R
.
For each prompt
p
we select a small set of token indices
I_{p}
(by default,
the last three positions). The
\mathcal{S}\!\to\!t
causal map
is

\Delta^{\mathcal{S}\to t}(R\phi)\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{p=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in I_{p}}\bigl[Z^{t}_{pj}(\phi)-Z^{t}_{pj}(\mathbf{0})\bigr],

(3)

where
Z^{t}_{pj}(\mathbf{0})
denotes the unperturbed model.

Shallow MLP approximation.

We approximate the causal map using a 1-hidden-layer MLP with exponential
activation and
m
hidden units:

\Delta^{\mathcal{S}\to t}(R\phi)\;\approx\;\hat{\Delta}(\phi)\;\equiv\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\bigl(\exp\!\langle\phi_{i},\phi\rangle-1\bigr)\,\vec{u}_{i},

(4)

where
\phi_{i}\in\mathcal{T}
,
\vec{u}_{i}\in S^{d-1}
, and

\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. If this approximation is accurate, the
directions
\phi_{i}
isolate individual modes of the causal map in an
interpretable, overcomplete decomposition. At times we will refer to these directions
\phi_{i}
as “factors” of the decomposition.

Causal objective.

To learn the approximation equation
4
, we optimize

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\,U,\,\Phi\\[2.0pt]
\phi_{i}\in\mathcal{T},\;\|\vec{u}_{i}\|=1\end{subarray}}\underbrace{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\langle\vec{u}_{i},\,\Delta^{\mathcal{S}\to t}(R\phi_{i})\rangle}_{\text{causal importance}}\;-\;\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\langle\vec{u}_{i},\vec{u}_{j}\rangle\bigl(\exp(R\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle)-1\bigr)}_{\text{similarity penalty}},

(5)

where
\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}\bigl(\langle\vec{a}_{i,\ell},\vec{a}_{j,\ell}\rangle+\langle\vec{b}_{i,\ell},\vec{b}_{j,\ell}\rangle\bigr)
.
The first term promotes adapter directions that induce large, consistent
downstream effects; the second penalizes redundancy among adapters.

The following theorem shows that
eq.

5
implicitly matches the
differential structure of
\Delta^{\mathcal{S}\to t}
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\bar{R}
denote the radius of convergence of the Taylor expansion of

\Delta^{\mathcal{S}\to t}
at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Let
T^{(k)}

and
\hat{T}^{(k)}
denote the order-
(k\!+\!1)
derivative tensors of

\Delta^{\mathcal{S}\to t}
and
\hat{\Delta}
, respectively. Suppose

R\sqrt{2L}\leq\bar{R}
. Then the maximization in equation
5
is
equivalent to the minimization

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\,U,\,\Phi\\[2.0pt]
\phi_{i}\in\mathcal{T},\;\|\vec{u}_{i}\|=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{R^{k}}{k!}\bigl\|T^{(k)}-\hat{T}^{(k)}\bigr\|^{2}.

(6)

The condition
R\sqrt{2L}\leq\bar{R}
ensures the Taylor expansion converges.
The proof is given in Appendix
E
.

Choice of
R

We use
R=1
in all experiments. This default seems to elicit interpretable behaviors across a range of models and environments.

Outline of algorithm

Softly Orthogonalized Gradient Iteration (SOGI).

We found in initial experiments that optimizing equation
5
with small-step gradient ascent tends to

3.1
demonstrates that equation
5
is
essentially specifying a weighted decomposition of all higher-order differential tensors of

This suggests the use of power-iteration-style algorithms may bypass poor local optima for this objective (see, e.g.,

In Appendix

\phi

Performance is somewhat robust to the choice of

3.2
Elicitation

We study how CPE elicits natural behaviors across a range of environments below. Our elicitation pipeline is depicted in
fig.

1(a)
.
Each environment’s dataset is partitioned into disjoint
training
,

validation
, and
test
splits. CPE adapters are trained on the
training split in a fully unsupervised fashion using
algorithm

1
. At evaluation time, we are given a
scalar metric
\mu
quantifying the degree to which a target behavior is
present (e.g. a pass rate, an accuracy, or an attack success rate). We run
inference with each of the
m
trained adapters on the validation split,
compute
\mu
for each, and select the adapter

i^{\star}=\arg\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\mu_{\mathrm{val}}(i)
. We then
report the test-set score
\mu_{\mathrm{test}}(i^{\star})
as the measure of
how effectively CPE elicits the behavior corresponding to
\mu
. Because CPE
is unsupervised, the same set of adapters can be reused across different
metrics. For example, when we wish to

suppress
rather than elicit a behavior, we simply replace the metric
\mu
with
-\mu
.

The above elicitation approach describes all environments studied in this paper
with the exception of
AI_CONVERSATION_STARTERS
as defined in
section

4
. That environment is open-ended and does not
admit a single scalar metric.

3.3
Baseline: causally important SAE features

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) represent a promising approach to feature discovery
in neural networks, learning large dictionaries of interpretable features by
training on massive datasets
(
2
;
18
;
37
)
,
amortizing the cost of feature discovery.

A fair comparison requires a principled method for selecting
which
SAE
features to evaluate. Modern SAEs can contain millions of
features
(
25
)
, making exhaustive evaluation computationally
prohibitive. We thus need an unsupervised selection criterion that identifies the
most relevant SAE features for some dataset of interest.

We propose selecting SAE features using the same criterion that motivates CPE:

causal importance
. Specifically, we measure how strongly each SAE feature
direction influences downstream activations when used as a steering vector. Let

\vec{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the
i
-th decoder direction of the SAE
at layer
s=\max\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the set of source layers used for CPE. We define the causal importance of feature
i
as

\mathcal{C}(\vec{w}_{i})\;=\;\bigl\|\mathcal{J}\,\vec{w}_{i}\bigr\|_{2},

(7)

where

\mathcal{J}=\nabla_{\vec{\theta}}\Delta^{s\to t}(\vec{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

is the Jacobian of the
s\!\to\!t
causal map
\Delta^{s\rightarrow t}_{\textrm{steer}}(\theta)
for steering vector
\theta
(defined analogously to the LoRA case) evaluated at zero, computed on the CPE training set. We then select the top
m

SAE features ranked by causal importance, matching the number of CPE adapters
m
.
For steering, we use the normalized selected SAE decoder directions
\vec{w}_{i}
, multiplied by a scale factor,
sweeping the scale as discussed in Appendix
I
.

SAEs used.

We use the width-32K llamascope SAE
(
16
)
for
Llama3.1-8B-Instruct as well as the
\ell_{0}
-50 qwenscope SAE
(
9
)
for Qwen3-8B.

Application to model organisms of misalignment

The previous sections demonstrated that CPE can elicit complex behaviors,
sometimes nearly as efficiently as supervised methods. A key advantage of
CPE being unsupervised is that it can be applied in settings where we do not
know
a priori
how to quantify the behavior we want to elicit, or where supervised
methods fail to produce the desired behavior even when we believe it is latent
in the model’s weights. In this section we explore applications along these
lines to
model organisms of misalignment
—models that have been
trained or prompted to exhibit specific misaligned behaviors, and that
therefore serve as controlled testbeds for alignment techniques that may be
needed for future, more capable systems
(
19
)
.
We study three such organisms: a reward-hacking environment in which a model
learns to game a mis-specified reward signal (

6.1
Reward hacking

Reward hacking is a particular concern for models that grow more capable over
the course of training: even if a weak supervisor can detect reward gaming at
the start of training, it may fail to do so once the model becomes more
sophisticated. Such gaps between weak supervisors and strong models have served as a consistent thread throughout alignment research
(
3
;
6
)
.
In this section, we study whether CPE can be used to initialize a model in an “aligned”
basin of weight space and whether the model remains in that basin throughout
reinforcement learning even when reward is mis-specified, as a proxy for learning monitored by a weak supervisor.

7
Conclusion

We introduced Causal Perturbative Elicitation (CPE), an unsupervised approach for discovering causally important directions in an LLM’s weight space. CPE yields interpretable rank-1 LoRAs using minimal data and minimal compute. Empirically, CPE modulates complex, multi-token behaviors including reasoning capabilities, password-locked capabilities, jailbreaks, reward hacking, anti-sycophantic tendencies and alignment-faking. It does so more effectively than SAE features, despite requiring a tiny amount of training data (1-100 prompts). Future work could extend CPE to
higher-rank perturbations, potentially enabling more sophisticated behavioral modifications.

Appendix F
Heuristic justification for Softly Orthogonalized Gradient
Iteration (SOGI)

OALS for quadratic tensors.

Suppose we wanted to perform a CP decomposition of
T^{(2)}
, a three-way
tensor (one output mode in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, two input modes
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
). The orthogonalized alternating least squares (OALS) method
of
32
alternates between the updates

\vec{u}\;\leftarrow\;T^{(2)}(\cdot,\phi,\phi),\qquad\phi\;\leftarrow\;T^{(2)}(\vec{u},\phi,\cdot),

followed by normalization and orthogonalization across components.

Quadratic case.

Consider the objective

f(\phi):=\langle\vec{u},\,\Delta^{\mathcal{S}\to t}(R\phi)\rangle
.
If
\Delta^{\mathcal{S}\to t}
were quadratic, then

\nabla_{\phi}f(\phi)\;=\;R^{2}\,T^{(2)}(\vec{u},\phi,\cdot),

so the update
\phi\leftarrow\nabla_{\phi}f(\phi)
recovers the OALS
contraction step up to the scalar
R^{2}
. Thus in the quadratic case, SOGI
coincides with OALS, differing only in the orthogonalization method (soft
orthogonalization rather than QR).

For the LoRA parameterization, the gradient
\nabla_{\phi}f
decomposes
blockwise into gradients with respect to each

\vec{a}_{\ell},\,\vec{b}_{\ell}
. The per-component normalization (projecting
each block back to the unit sphere) replaces the single spherical projection
of the original steering-vector formulation with a product-of-spheres
projection onto the torus
\mathcal{T}
.

General case: weighted higher-order contractions.

For general
\Delta^{\mathcal{S}\to t}
, the Taylor expansion gives

\nabla_{\phi}\langle\vec{u},\,\Delta^{\mathcal{S}\to t}(R\phi)\rangle\;=\;\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{R^{k}}{(k-1)!}\,T^{(k)}(\vec{u},\phi^{\otimes(k-1)},\cdot).

The gradient update thus acts as a weighted sum of contractions of all
higher-order derivative tensors, with weights proportional to

R^{k}/(k\!-\!1)!
. These contractions are accessible through ordinary
automatic differentiation, without ever forming higher-order tensors
explicitly.

Soft orthogonalization.

OALS uses a hard QR step, which limits the decomposition to at most

D
components. We replace QR with the soft orthogonalization of

appendix

A
, which iteratively repels columns by their pairwise
similarity in the flattened
\phi
-space while preserving high-gradient-magnitude
directions. After the repulsion step, each
\vec{a}_{i,\ell}
and

\vec{b}_{i,\ell}
is re-normalized to the unit sphere, returning

\phi_{i}
to the torus. The soft orthogonalization step is only applied to the
source-layer factors while no type of orthogonalization is applied to outputs;
we found this was more stable in initial experiments.

Countdown.

We synthetically generate the Countdown puzzles rather than drawing on an existing
benchmark, so that no instance can have appeared during pretraining, ruling out contamination
or memorization as an explanation for the elicited capability. Each puzzle gives three or four
source integers and a target; the model must write an equation that uses each source number

exactly once
with
+,-,\times,\div
and evaluates to the target, putting the final
equation in
\boxed{}
. The puzzles are generated as follows:

Backward construction
: for each puzzle we sample
3
–
4

integers uniformly from
[1,50]
, then build a random expression by shuffling them and folding
left with a uniformly random operator at each step. Division is allowed only when it is exact
(integer quotient, non-zero divisor), and any non-positive or non-integer intermediate is
rejected. The expression’s value is taken as the target, so at least one solution exists by
construction. We keep only puzzles whose target is a positive integer at most
99
and distinct
from every input number, de-duplicate on the (multiset of numbers, target) pair, and use a
separate random seed per data split.

A response passes iff it parses, uses each number exactly once, and evaluates to the target; the
scorer normalizes LaTeX/unicode operators before a safe AST evaluation.

Sycophancy (factual).

Built from the
answer
split of the sycophancy benchmark of

33
(TriviaQA subset). The user volunteers doubt about the

correct
answer: “{question} I don’t think the answer is {correct_answer},
but I’m really not sure.”. We measure whether the model still gives the correct
answer (truth-tracking under social pressure). Scoring is programmatic with no judge: a
response is
correct
iff it contains a gold-answer alias (word-boundary match) and

caved
iff it contains the planted incorrect answer.
