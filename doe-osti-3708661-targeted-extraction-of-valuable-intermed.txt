Executive Summary
This Final Project Report summarizes the research activities, accomplishments, and outcomes of
the U.S. Department of Energy Industrial Technologies Office (ITO) cooperative agreement DE-
EE0009494, titled "Targeted Extraction of Valuable Intermediate Products and Clean Water
from Municipal Wastewater Using Electroactive Anaerobic Membrane Bioreactors." The project
was conducted from October 1, 2021, through July 31, 2025, by a collaborative team at the
University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) and Princeton University, under the direction of
Principal Investigator David Jassby.
The central objective of this project was to develop and demonstrate an Anaerobic Electric
Membrane Bioreactor (AnEMBR) system capable of efficiently treating municipal wastewater
while simultaneously producing fit-for-purpose water and extracting commercially valuable
chemicals including volatile fatty acids (VFAs), ammonia (NH3), and phosphate. The system
integrates three core platform technologies: (1) electrofermentation (EF) for controlled VFA
production, (2) electroactive gas stripping membranes for targeted extraction of VFAs and
ammonia, and (3) electroactive water treatment membranes including phosphate-selective mixed-
matrix membranes. Techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) were
applied throughout the project to guide research priorities and assess economic and environmental
performance.
Over the course of the project, the team achieved all major milestones, including the development
and optimization of each platform technology at the bench scale, the integration of these platforms
at laboratory scale, and a field-scale demonstration at the Hyperion Water Reclamation Facility in
El Segundo, California. Seven peer-reviewed journal publications were produced, covering
electroactive membranes for ammonia and VFA recovery, phosphate-selective membranes,
temperature effects on electro-fermentation, machine-learning-guided pervaporation membrane
design, and techno-economic analysis. The project remained on track and within budget
throughout its duration.
Key technical achievements include: demonstration of a simple, low-cost electrically conducting
gas stripping membrane achieving ammonia fluxes of 141.3 g(NH3-N)·m⁻2·day⁻¹; development of
a novel multilayer electroactive pervaporation membrane achieving an acetic acid separation factor
of 51.55 with energy consumption of only 3.37 kWh/kg; synthesis of a phosphate-selective mixed-
matrix membrane with 8.5-fold enhanced phosphate flux; characterization of temperature effects
on electro-fermentation product profiles; application of machine learning to identify promising
polymer candidates for pervaporation-based organic recovery; and successful pilot-scale operation
at the Hyperion wastewater treatment plant demonstrating the viability of the integrated AnEMBR
concept.

1. Introduction
Municipal wastewater treatment in the United States is an energy-intensive sector, consuming
approximately 2% of the nation's total electricity demand and accounting for significant
greenhouse gas emissions. The dominant treatment paradigm—aerobic activated sludge—is
inherently inefficient from a resource standpoint: it oxidizes valuable reduced carbon and nitrogen
compounds into CO2 and nitrate, pollutants that are then disposed of at considerable energetic and
economic cost. The aeration step alone accounts for up to 75% of wastewater plant energy
consumption, and sludge handling can represent as much as 60% of total operating costs.
Anaerobic digestion (AD) has long been recognized as an attractive alternative due to its lower
energy demand, reduced biosolids generation, and potential for biogas production. However,
conventional AD is hampered by its inability to adequately remove nitrogen, low biogas quality,
and limited ability to generate high-value products. Of the approximately 1,500 AD systems
treating municipal waste sludge in the USA, only around 250 actively utilize the biogas for heat
or power, with the majority simply flaring it.
A transformative opportunity exists to reimagine anaerobic wastewater treatment as a platform for
resource recovery. By arresting anaerobic digestion at the acidogenesis phase, valuable volatile
fatty acids (VFAs)—including acetic, propionic, butyric, and succinic acids—can be accumulated
rather than converted to low-value methane. VFAs have a substantially higher energy yield and
market value compared to biogas, and they serve as feedstocks for bioplastics, biofuels, and
specialty chemicals. Similarly, ammonia recovered from wastewater represents a direct substitute
for Haber-Bosch ammonia, whose production consumes 1–2% of global energy annually, and
phosphate recovered from wastewater addresses concerns over the depletion of finite phosphate
rock reserves.
This project addressed these opportunities through the development of an Anaerobic Electric
Membrane Bioreactor (AnEMBR) system integrating electrofermentation, electroactive
membrane extraction, and electroactive water treatment. The project was structured around two
budget periods: the first focused on developing and characterizing individual platform
technologies at bench scale, and the second on integrating these platforms, demonstrating them in
the field, and conducting comprehensive TEA and LCA analyses.

2. Background
2.1 Municipal Wastewater Treatment: Current State and Limitations
The standard municipal wastewater treatment train comprises primary clarification to remove
settleable solids, secondary biological treatment (typically aerobic activated sludge), and in some
cases tertiary treatment for nutrient removal and disinfection. The biological oxygen demand
(BOD), ammonia-nitrogen, and phosphorus are the primary contaminants targeted for removal.
While effective at meeting water quality standards, this approach is thermodynamically inefficient:
it expends energy to oxidize organic matter that could otherwise be valorized, and it destroys
nitrogen and phosphorus compounds that have substantial agricultural value.
The energy footprint of wastewater treatment is substantial and growing. With global urbanization
and increasingly stringent water quality regulations, the energy demand of the sector is projected
to rise considerably over the coming decades. In the United States, wastewater utilities spend
approximately $4 billion annually on electricity, representing both a significant operational burden
and a major opportunity for efficiency improvement and revenue generation through resource
recovery.

2.2 Anaerobic Digestion and Its Limitations
Anaerobic digestion converts organic matter into biogas (primarily methane and carbon dioxide)
through a sequence of microbial processes: hydrolysis, acidogenesis, acetogenesis, and
methanogenesis. While AD requires no external energy for aeration and generates a net energy
output via biogas, it faces several well-documented limitations in the municipal context. Chief
among these is its inability to remove nitrogen: ammonia is released during digestion and
accumulates in the effluent, requiring subsequent nitrogen removal treatment that reimposes
significant energy costs. Additionally, methane has a relatively low market value compared to
commodity chemicals, and its production as a greenhouse gas poses environmental risks if not
properly captured.
More fundamentally, complete anaerobic digestion to methane represents a suboptimal use of the
carbon in wastewater. The intermediate products generated during the acidogenesis and
acetogenesis stages—volatile fatty acids—have considerably higher market value and more
versatile industrial applications. Arresting the digestion process at the acidogenesis stage to capture
these intermediates represents a compelling strategy for value recovery.

2.3 Electrofermentation: Controlled VFA Production
Electrofermentation (EF) uses poised electrodes to influence anaerobic microbial metabolism by
regulating the oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) and the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio. By
applying defined electrode potentials, the thermodynamic environment of the reactor is controlled,
which in turn governs which metabolic pathways are favored. Prior work demonstrated that
electrochemical control of redox potential can arrest methanogenesis and redirect carbon flow

toward VFA production, and that the resulting product profile is sensitive to both the electrode
potential and operating temperature.
The temperature dependence of EF is particularly important for practical application.
Psychrophilic conditions (10°C) favor propionic and valeric acid production, mesophilic
conditions (20°C) favor acetic and propionic acids, and thermophilic conditions (55°C) produce
more formic acid and ethanol. These differences reflect the selective enrichment of distinct
microbial communities at each temperature, with Lactococcus and Enterobacteriaceae
predominating at lower temperatures and thermophilic Paenibacillus and Aneurinibacillus species
at elevated temperatures. Understanding these relationships is essential for tuning the product
spectrum toward desired outcomes.
Practical implementation of EF in a continuous-flow system also requires careful management of
hydraulic and solids retention times. Longer retention times generally favor increased total VFA
production, but also risk consumption of acidogenic products by electroactive bacteria. The project
systematically characterized these relationships to establish the optimal operating window for VFA
production from municipal wastewater.

2.4 Electroactive Membranes for Resource Extraction
A distinguishing feature of the AnEMBR concept is the use of electrically conducting membranes
to extract value-added products directly from the anaerobic reactor without disturbing the
biological process. These electroactive membranes exploit the ability of electrical current to
modulate the local chemical environment—particularly pH and temperature—at the
membrane/water interface, enabling selective extraction of target molecules.
For VFA recovery, the principal challenge is that VFAs exist in their ionized, non-volatile form at
the neutral pH typical of fermentation effluent. Extraction by evaporative methods requires either
acidification of the bulk solution or local acidification at the extraction interface. Joule heating of
a conducting membrane surface achieves the temperature elevation needed to drive VFA
volatilization, while an anodic pH swing—through electrochemical water oxidation generating
protons at the interface—acidifies the local environment below the pKa of the target acid without
altering bulk conditions. This dual electrochemical-electrothermal approach enables integration of
the membrane extractor directly into the biological reactor.
For ammonia recovery, the conducting gas stripping membrane operates as a cathode. Water
reduction at the cathode surface generates hydroxide ions, locally raising the pH above the pKa of
ammonium (9.25) and converting the non-volatile ammonium ion into volatile ammonia, which is
then stripped across a hydrophobic membrane support into an acid trap. The electrical field
additionally drives electromigration of positively charged ammonium ions toward the cathode,
reducing mass transfer limitations. This approach eliminates the need for bulk pH adjustment with
caustic chemicals, significantly simplifying the process and reducing chemical costs.
Phosphate recovery from wastewater presents a different challenge: selectivity against the
background of competing anions including chloride, nitrate, and sulfate. Conventional ion

exchange membranes transport anions based primarily on charge and size, offering limited
phosphate selectivity. A novel strategy based on incorporating hydrous manganese oxide (HMO)
nanoparticles into a cation exchange polymer matrix exploits the specific affinity of HMO for
phosphate through outer-sphere complexation. This creates a selective pathway for phosphate
transport—ion-hopping between adjacent HMO particles—while other anions are excluded by
Donnan repulsion with the sulfonated polymer matrix.

2.5 Machine Learning for Membrane Design
The development of high-performance polymer membranes for pervaporation-based organic
recovery has traditionally relied on empirical trial-and-error approaches. The large chemical space
of candidate polymers, combined with the complex dependencies of membrane performance on
both material properties and operating conditions, makes systematic experimental optimization
impractical. Machine learning (ML) offers a powerful alternative, enabling high-throughput in
silico screening of candidate materials and identification of structure-performance relationships
that can guide experimental synthesis efforts.
The application of ML to pervaporation membrane design requires careful attention to data quality,
model robustness, and the risk of data leakage—the inadvertent inclusion of test data information
in the training process that leads to overoptimistically accurate predictions. The project addressed
these challenges by constructing the largest pervaporation dataset to date (2,341 data points across
52 polymer types and 32 organic solutes), employing Morgan frequency fingerprints to encode
polymer structure, and implementing rigorous data leakage management in model training and
validation. The resulting LightGBM regression models achieved reliable prediction of both
separation factor and total flux, and were applied to screen approximately 1 million hypothetical
polymers from the PI1M database.

2.6 Techno-Economic and Life Cycle Analysis
For the AnEMBR concept to achieve commercial viability, the value of recovered products must
offset the capital and operating costs of the treatment system. Techno-economic analysis (TEA)
provides a quantitative framework for evaluating this balance, identifying cost drivers, and
prioritizing research efforts to maximize economic performance. Life cycle assessment (LCA)
complements TEA by quantifying environmental impacts—energy consumption, greenhouse gas
emissions, land use, and others—across the full system, enabling comparison with conventional
treatment alternatives.
The TEA framework developed in this project encompassed the full municipal wastewater
treatment train: screening, grit removal, primary sedimentation, the AnBR/UF/electroactive
membrane subsystem, chlorination, sludge thickening, and dewatering. The optimization model
calculated material and energy balances for a 5 million gallon per day (MGD) plant, providing a
realistic basis for cost estimation. Sensitivity analysis revealed that VFA and ammonia fluxes
through the extraction membranes are the dominant cost drivers: achieving fluxes ten times higher
than baseline values renders the system economically profitable.

3. Project Objectives and Technical Approach
The project was organized around the integration of three platform technologies within a unified
AnEMBR system, supported by continuous TEA/LCA evaluation. The specific objectives were:
 • Develop and optimize an electrofermentation reactor system capable of producing VFAs
 from municipal wastewater at yields of >=50 mg VFA/100 mg COD with controllable
 product selectivity.
 • Fabricate, characterize, and optimize electroactive gas stripping membranes for VFA
 extraction, with a target of achieving high flux and separation factor at low energy
 consumption.
 • Fabricate, characterize, and optimize electroactive gas stripping membranes for ammonia
 (NH3) extraction, achieving competitive performance versus existing commercial
 approaches.
 • Develop electroactive water treatment membranes (MF, UF) with antifouling capability
 and phosphate-selective mixed-matrix membranes for nutrient recovery.
 • Integrate the three platform technologies at bench scale at UCLA and Princeton,
 demonstrating the combined system with real municipal wastewater.
 • Conduct a field-scale demonstration of the integrated AnEMBR system at the Hyperion
 Water Reclamation Facility in El Segundo, California.
 • Apply TEA and LCA throughout the project to evaluate economic and environmental
 performance, identify optimization targets, and establish the value proposition of the
 integrated system.

The technical approach leveraged the complementary expertise of UCLA (electroactive
membranes, material synthesis, system integration) and Princeton University (electrofermentation,
membrane distillation, process modeling).

4. Research Accomplishments
4.1 Electrofermentation Platform: VFA Production
The electrofermentation effort investigated the controlled production of volatile fatty acids from
synthetic and real municipal wastewater under a range of temperatures and electrode potentials.
Laboratory-scale EF reactors (100 mL) were operated in triplicate with graphite brush anodes at
psychrophilic (10°C), mesophilic (20°C), and thermophilic (55°C) temperatures, and at electrode
potentials of -0.2 V, -0.4 V, -0.6 V, and open circuit (versus Ag/AgCl).

Figure 4.1. VFA production profiles at psychrophilic (left), mesophilic (middle), and thermophilic (right)
temperatures: (a) as a function of hydraulic retention time (8 h to 18 days); (b) as a function of applied electrode
potential. Error bars represent results of more than three cycles. Source: Lu et al. (2024), Resources, Conservation &
Recycling.

A principal finding was that temperature, rather than electrode potential, was the dominant factor
governing VFA product profile. Psychrophilic conditions generated the highest concentrations of
lactic acid (up to ~1258 mg/L at short HRTs), transitioning to propionic and valeric acid production
at longer retention times, correlating with the dominance of Lactococcus bacteria. Mesophilic
conditions produced a mixture of acetic and propionic acids characteristic of heterolactic
fermentation. Thermophilic conditions yielded higher total acids but with elevated proportions of
formic acid and ethanol, driven by the enrichment of Paenibacillus and Aneurinibacillus species.
Pearson correlation analysis confirmed statistically significant relationships between temperature
and formic acid (r = 0.733), ethanol (r = 0.872), propionic acid (r = -0.739), and valeric acid (r = -
0.615) production. Electrode potential had limited direct influence on the product spectrum,
suggesting that temperature-driven selection of microbial communities is the more powerful lever
for product control.
Microbial community analysis using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing provided mechanistic
insight into the temperature-product relationships. The dominant phylum shifted from
Proteobacteria at psychrophilic temperatures to Firmicutes at mesophilic and thermophilic
conditions. At the family level, Streptococcaceae (encompassing the homofermentative genus
Lactococcus) comprised approximately 40–45% of both suspension and biofilm communities at
10°C. At 20°C, Streptococcaceae and Bacillales co-dominated, consistent with heterolactic
fermentation. At 55°C, Paenibacillaceae (42.4%) and Bacillaceae (22.2%) dominated, with
Paenibacillus and Aneurinibacillus enriched in proportion to formic acid and ethanol output.
Multivariate statistical analyses—including redundancy analysis (RDA) and principal coordinate
analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity—confirmed that thermophilic communities
were more compositionally distinct from psychrophilic and mesophilic communities than the latter
two were from each other, reflecting the smaller temperature gradient between 10°C and 20°C.
Microbial richness (Shannon index) decreased at both temperature extremes relative to the
mesophilic condition, indicative of selective pressure on community composition. No significant
structural divergence was observed between biofilm and suspension communities across
temperatures, although the relative abundance of putative electroactive genera (Lactococcus,
Pseudomonas) differed between compartments in a temperature-dependent manner. These
findings collectively establish that community-level microbial ecology, driven primarily by
temperature, is the principal determinant of fermentative product selectivity in mixed-culture EF
systems.

The project also characterized the temperature dependence of VFA extraction from fermentation
effluent, finding that thermophilic fermentation at 55°C produces effluent well-suited for
subsequent membrane extraction due to the elevated volatility of VFAs at higher temperatures.
Pre-concentration using vacuum evaporation followed by thermophilic fermentation at 55°C
increased total VFA concentration from ~52 mg COD/L (raw wastewater) to approximately 734
mg COD/L (fermented effluent), representing ~85% conversion of organic compounds to VFAs.

4.2 Electroactive Gas Stripping Membranes: Acetic Acid and VFA Recovery
Two complementary membrane systems were developed and characterized for VFA extraction
from fermentation effluent.

4.2.1 Multilayer Electroactive Pervaporation Membrane (High Selectivity)
A multilayer electrothermal pervaporation membrane was fabricated through a nonsolvent-
induced phase separation (NIPs) process. The membrane comprised a polypropylene (PP) back
support, a carbon fiber (CF) joule heating layer embedded in a PVDF binding layer, and a dense
polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) top layer providing high acetic acid selectivity. A separate
polyaniline (PANI)-coated CF layer served as the pH-swing anode for interfacial acidification.
The PDMS layer exploits the solution-diffusion transport mechanism: acetic acid partitions
preferentially into the hydrophobic PDMS phase, diffuses through the membrane under the
concentration gradient established by the combined effects of Joule heating and the
electrochemical pH swing, and is recovered in a NaOH trap on the permeate side. At an AA
concentration of 0.1 wt% and a surface temperature of 70°C achieved via Joule heating, the
optimized M4 membrane achieved an acetic acid flux of 51.00 ± 0.82 g·m⁻2·hr⁻¹ with a separation
factor of 51.55 ± 2.11 (AA/water). The specific energy consumption was 3.37 kWh/kg AA—
competitive with or superior to conventional alternatives such as electrodialysis (2.6–13.8
kWh/kg). An electrochemical pH swing at 2.5 V produced AA fluxes comparable to bulk
acidification to pH 3, without the need for chemical addition. Sixteen-hour stability tests using
AnMBR effluent confirmed stable performance with no membrane degradation.

4.2.2 Membrane Electrolysis Distillation (MED) for pH-Neutral Effluent
A complementary system—membrane electrolysis distillation (MED)—was developed at
Princeton to address the challenge of VFA extraction from pH-neutral fermentation solutions. The
MED reactor uses a single carbon cloth anode that simultaneously performs Joule heating and
electrochemical pH swing, positioned adjacent to a commercial hydrophobic polypropylene
membrane for gas-liquid phase separation.
The MED system achieved an acetic acid flux of 12.03 g·m⁻2·hr⁻¹ at 6 V Joule heating and 3
mA·cm⁻2 electrolysis current. Control experiments without Joule heating or pH swing achieved
only 0.23 and 0.32 g·m⁻2·hr⁻¹, respectively, demonstrating the essential contribution of both
effects. The system was validated using pre-concentrated and thermophilically fermented real
municipal wastewater, extracting a mixture of VFAs dominated by acetic acid, with isovalerate

and propionate also present. A physicochemical model accurately described the time evolution of
acid and water fluxes and predicted that any applied current above 2 mA·cm⁻2 was sufficient to
maintain the anode surface pH below the pKa of acetic acid, with temperature rather than current
density being the rate-limiting variable for flux enhancement. Short-term and long-term fouling
tests demonstrated stable performance with minimal membrane degradation, attributed to the
separation of the conducting electrode layer from the gas-permeable membrane.

4.3 Electroactive Gas Stripping Membranes: Ammonia Recovery
A simple, low-cost electrically conducting gas stripping membrane for ammonia recovery was
developed, characterized, and evaluated. The membrane comprised a commercial carbon fiber
(CF) material bonded to a hydrophobic polypropylene (PP) membrane support using epoxy—a
significantly simpler fabrication approach than prior CNT-based electrodes. The CF acts as a
cathode in an electrochemical cell, generating hydroxide ions through water reduction at the
CF/solution interface, locally elevating pH above the pKa of ammonium and driving ammonia
volatilization across the PP membrane into an acid trap.
The CF/PP membrane achieved an NH3-N flux of 141.3 ± 14.0 g·m⁻2·day⁻¹ at a current density of
6.25 mA·cm⁻2, with an energy consumption of 69.2 ± 5.3 kWh·kg(NH3-N)⁻¹. Flux responded
approximately linearly to current density (R2 = 0.923), and was strongly sensitive to the acid trap
circulation rate. Application of an electrical potential at pH 7.2 improved ammonia flux by
approximately 10-fold compared to passive stripping at the same pH. The membrane demonstrated
stable performance over 12 hours in synthetic municipal wastewater (AnMBR effluent), with an
average flux of 14.34 g(N)·m⁻2·day⁻¹ proportional to the lower ammonium concentration. Mass
balance analysis confirmed that the dominant nitrogen removal mechanism was membrane
stripping, with a minor fraction (0.9–20.8%) oxidized to nitrate at higher current densities. A
preliminary economic analysis identified capital expenditure as the dominant cost component, with
the minimum total cost of $7.72/kg(NH3-N) achieved at the lowest current density of 1.56 mA·cm-

4.4 Phosphate-Selective Mixed-Matrix Membranes
A novel phosphate-selective mixed-matrix membrane (PhSMMM) was developed by
incorporating pre-synthesized amorphous hydrous manganese oxide (HMO) nanoparticles into a
sulfonated polyethersulfone (SPES) cation exchange polymer matrix. This approach overcame a
key limitation of prior work that relied on in-situ HMO growth within commercial CEMs, which
was constrained by the ion exchange capacity of the membrane to a maximum loading of ~11 wt%.
By blending pre-formed HMO nanoparticles (average diameter 62.7 ± 1.3 nm; zeta potential -6.08
± 0.37 mV) directly into the SPES casting solution and applying thermal annealing post-
fabrication, HMO loadings of up to 28.4 ± 0.1 wt% were achieved. Thermal annealing was found
to be essential: non-annealed membranes exhibited a porous cross-section enabling advective
transport, which severely compromised ion selectivity. After annealing, the membrane structure
was dense and defect-free, allowing phosphate transport exclusively through the HMO-facilitated
hopping mechanism while competing anions were rejected by Donnan exclusion from the SPES
sulfonates.
Scanning electron microscopy confirmed successful HMO incorporation throughout the
membrane cross-section and the critical role of thermal annealing (Figure 4.4a). Non-thermally
annealed HMO-loaded membranes (ME40 NTA) exhibited a rough surface and highly porous,
macrovoid-containing cross-section, whereas thermally annealed ME40 membranes displayed a
uniformly dense, defect-free structure. X-ray diffraction confirmed the amorphous character of the
synthesized HMO, with characteristic broad peaks at 38.2° and 69°. Individual HMO particles
averaged 62.7 ± 1.3 nm in diameter, while clusters averaged 312 ± 2.3 nm.

Figure 4.4a. XRD analysis of HMO particles (a); SEM images of single HMO particle (b) and
clusters (c); surface and cross-sectional SEM images of ME0 (d–f), thermally annealed ME40 (g–
i), ME0 NTA (j–l), and ME40 NTA (m–o) membranes. Thermal annealing eliminates
macroporosity in HMO-loaded membranes, producing the dense, defect-free structure needed for
selective phosphate transport. Source: Wang et al. (2024), Journal of Membrane Science Letters.
The best-performing membrane (ME40) exhibited a phosphate flux of 1.57 mmol·m⁻2·hr⁻¹—an
8.5-fold improvement over the previous generation—with selectivity of 9× (over Cl⁻), 11× (over
NO3⁻), and >104× (over SO42⁻). EDS, XPS, and FTIR analyses confirmed uniform HMO
distribution and outer-sphere phosphate complexation with the HMO particles. Stability tests over
28 days confirmed that HMO content stabilized at ~30 wt% after initial leaching with no further
loss. The phosphate transport model developed by Iddya et al. was successfully applied to the new
membrane systems, with the linear concentration profile across the membrane becoming

increasingly accurate at higher HMO loadings, consistent with the percolating HMO network
providing a continuous phosphate transport pathway.
Budget Period 2 extended this research to non-solvent-induced phase separation (NIPS)—a more
scalable fabrication route than evaporation casting. A quantitative Th–K′ framework was
established to predict membrane morphology from formulation variables, integrating
thermodynamic enhancement (Th, from binodal curve shifts and Flory–Huggins/Hansen solubility
parameters) and kinetic hindrance (K, from viscosity and GC-MS-measured solvent diffusion
rates). Three solvent–nonsolvent systems were evaluated over HMO loadings of 0–40 wt%: NMP–
5 M NaCl, NMP–IPA, and NMP/1,4-dioxane–IPA. As shown in Figure 4.4c, HMO addition
shifted binodal curves across all systems, reflecting HMO’s greater hydrophilicity relative to
SPES. In NMP–NaCl and NMP–IPA systems, K decreased with HMO loading (accelerated
demixing, favoring porous structures); in NMP/1,4-dioxane–IPA, 1,4-dioxane elevated K (slowed
demixing), directing phase separation toward denser morphologies.

Figure 4.4b. Binodal curves for 30 wt% SPES dope solutions as a function of HMO loading (0–
7.5 wt%) for: (a) NMP–5 M NaCl, (b) NMP–IPA, and (c) NMP/1,4-dioxane–IPA systems. Shifts
in the binodal curve quantify HMO-induced changes in thermodynamic stability that govern final
membrane morphology. Source: Wang et al. (2026), Journal of Membrane Science.
SEM analysis validated the Th–K′ framework predictions (Figure 4.4d). NMP–NaCl membranes
transitioned from dense (0 wt% HMO) to highly porous, finger-like macrovoid morphology (40
wt% HMO), consistent with the highest Th and lowest K in that system. NMP–IPA membranes
showed intermediate sponge-like structures. NMP/1,4-dioxane–IPA membranes became

progressively denser with increasing HMO loading. Ion transport testing established a clear
morphology–performance relationship: the dense ND/IPA/25 membrane achieved the highest
phosphate selectivity (7.1× over Cl⁻, 9.1× over NO3⁻, 101.4× over SO42⁻), while the porous
N/NaCl/40 membrane showed minimal selectivity due to dominant co-ion leakage. These results
yield actionable design rules: high-selectivity membranes for phosphate recovery require mixed-
solvent formulations (NMP/1,4-dioxane) at moderate HMO loading to exploit elevated kinetic
hindrance and produce dense, macrovoid-free structures.

NMP–IPA (row b), and NMP/1,4-dioxane–IPA (row c) at increasing HMO loadings (0, 20, 40/25

4.5 Machine Learning for Pervaporation Membrane Design
A machine learning pipeline was developed to accelerate the discovery of high-performance
polymer membranes for pervaporation-based organic recovery, using acetic acid/water separation
as the target application. The pipeline utilized a curated dataset of 2,341 experimental data points
compiled from the literature, representing 52 unique polymers and 32 organic solutes. Polymer
chemical structure was encoded using Hashed Morgan frequency fingerprints (MFFs) with a

maximum radius of 2 and bit length of 1,024, and dimensionality was reduced using principal
component analysis.
A rigorous data leakage management (DLM) approach was implemented, bundling all data points
from a given study into either the training or test split to prevent overoptimistic performance
estimates. After systematic comparison of random forest, extra trees, CatBoost, and LightGBM
regressors, the LightGBM model (LGBMR) was selected based on its superior performance in
cross-validation. The final models achieved RMSE values of 0.45 and 0.36 on logarithmic scales
for separation factor and total flux, respectively—substantially lower than models without DLM,
which inflated apparent accuracy.
SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis revealed that organic molar volume was the most
important predictor of separation factor, followed by feed mass ratio and principal component
features reflecting the presence and connectivity of benzene rings and polar functional groups.
Temperature and selective layer thickness were the dominant predictors of flux. Application of the
trained models to 995,799 hypothetical polymers from the PI1M database identified 27,344
polymers predicted to exceed the current state-of-the-art permeation separation index (PSI) by
130%. Synthetic accessibility scoring further filtered these to a shortlist of 15 candidates
combining high PSI prediction with low synthesis complexity. Among these, polymers with long
aliphatic carbon chains showed high predicted flux, while those incorporating multiple benzene
rings showed high predicted selectivity.

4.6 Field-Scale Demonstration at Hyperion Water Reclamation Facility
A pilot-scale AnEMBR system was designed, fabricated, deployed, and operated at the Hyperion
Water Reclamation Facility in El Segundo, California—one of the largest wastewater treatment
plants in the western United States. The pilot system received primary effluent from the plant as
feed and comprised the following unit operations: an equalization (EQ) tank, a 200-liter anaerobic
bioreactor (AnBR), a commercial tubular ultrafiltration membrane (0.012 m2 surface area, 66.03
nm pore size), and a resource recovery tank equipped with 0.5 m2 of electroactive resource
recovery membranes (0.25 m2 each for VFA and ammonia recovery).
The AnBR was operated at 4-day hydraulic retention time (HRT) and an initial 10-day solids
retention time (SRT), subsequently extended to 15 days with internal sludge recirculation to
enhance biomass retention. This operational change produced a six-fold increase in mixed liquor
suspended solids (from ~530 ppm to ~3,161 ppm) and a seven-fold increase in VFA concentration
(from ~141 ppm to ~1,200 ppm). Ammonia nitrogen concentrations remained in the 50–70 ppm
range typical of municipal primary effluent throughout operation. The pilot reactor demonstrated
stable long-term operation with consistent VFA accumulation, confirming the feasibility of
anaerobic acidogenic treatment of real municipal wastewater at the pilot scale.
The tubular UF membrane operated at 108 L/hr⁻¹ feed flow rate (Reynolds number 2,682,
transitional flow regime), producing a permeate flux of 68.22 LMH. A backwash cycle every 60

minutes maintained membrane performance over extended operation periods, with the periodic
flux restoration confirming effective fouling control.
Resource recovery membranes were tested in two operational cycles. In the first 11-hour cycle,
with limited water circulation, the ammonia recovery rate averaged 0.15 g·m⁻2·hr⁻¹ and the VFA
recovery rate averaged 1.51 g·m⁻2·hr⁻¹. In an improved second 13-hour cycle with enhanced
internal circulation, ammonia recovery improved 2.5-fold to 0.38 g·m⁻2·hr⁻¹, while VFA recovery
increased slightly to 1.62 g·m⁻2·hr⁻¹. Mass balance closure was achieved for ammonia (1.27 g lost
from tank, 1.22 g recovered in trap), confirming the accuracy of the experimental measurements.
The observed recovery rates were lower than lab-scale benchmarks primarily due to the reduced
current density applied in the pilot (4.4 mA·cm⁻2 versus 6.25 mA·cm⁻2) and the substantially
lower reactant concentrations in dilute primary effluent.

4.7 Techno-Economic Analysis
A comprehensive techno-economic analysis was developed for a 5 MGD AnEMBR-based
municipal wastewater treatment system. The optimization model incorporated all major unit
operations from screening through final disinfection, with the AnBR/UF/electroactive membrane
subsystem as the core resource recovery element. Material and energy balances computed from
the optimization model showed recovery of approximately 2.52 kg VFAs and 0.116 kg NH3 per
cubic meter of treated water.
The TEA assumed a 30-year plant lifecycle, 10% discount rate, and 10-year operational life for
the EMV, EMA, and UF membranes. At baseline membrane flux conditions, the annualized
operating expenditure was $56.02/m3, capital expenditure was $2.17/m3, and revenue from
product sales was $5.71/m3, yielding a total annualized cost (TAC) of $52.49/m3. This elevated
cost is driven primarily by the low baseline fluxes through the electroactive extraction membranes,
which require large membrane areas and high associated energy inputs.
Sensitivity analysis confirmed that membrane flux is the critical performance parameter: at VFA
and NH3 fluxes ten times higher than the baseline, the TAC becomes negative, indicating system
profitability. This finding directly motivates the experimental research directions pursued
throughout the project—particularly the development of higher-flux electroactive membranes.

4.8 Membrane Network Synthesis for High-Value Metal Recovery
Complementing the AnEMBR-focused TEA, the Princeton team developed a global optimization
framework for the synthesis of nanofiltration (NF)-based membrane networks designed to recover
high-value metals—including critical metals and rare earth elements—from industrial wastewater.
This work addresses a broader resource recovery challenge with direct relevance to the circular
economy goals motivating the project: while the AnEMBR targets organic carbon (VFAs) and
nutrients (ammonia, phosphate) from municipal wastewater, industrial process streams frequently
contain metals such as lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper, and platinum whose recovery would
substitute for environmentally damaging primary mining.

The framework is built on three integrated components. First, a physics-based surrogate membrane
unit model derived from the Spiegler–Kedem–Katchalsky (SKK) transport theory describes
solvent and solute permeation through NF membranes. Under the assumption that the volumetric
solvent flux substantially exceeds solute permeances (high Péclet number), rejection coefficients
become independent of concentration and flux, allowing them to be treated as material-specific
constants—a key simplification that renders the unit model computationally tractable without
sacrificing accuracy. Second, a comprehensive superstructure represents a wide array of network
configurations: multiple interlinked trains (each targeting a specific metal), multiple stages per
train with distinct membrane materials per stage, and rich stream interconnections including
retentate/permeate bypasses and recycles. Third, a mixed-integer nonlinear programming
(MINLP) model integrates the unit model with the superstructure and is solved to global optimality
using the BARON solver to minimize total annualized operating cost, encompassing membrane
area costs, stage installation costs, and pump energy.

Figure 4.5. Schematic of the two-train, three-stage membrane network superstructure, illustrating
feed splitting, retentate/permeate bypasses, recycles, and product collection. Source: Verma,
Jassby & Maravelias (2025), Separation and Purification Technology.

Figure 4.6. Sensitivity analysis for lithium recovery (one-train network, max 350 m2 per stage):
(a) total required membrane area and (b) total annualized operating cost as a function of purity and
recovery specifications. Numbers indicate active stage count. Source: Verma et al. (2025).

The framework was demonstrated through three case studies of increasing complexity. In a one-
train lithium recovery case (Li+ from spent lithium-ion battery leachate containing Co2+ and
Ni2+), the model determined optimal network configurations for three pairs of recovery/purity
targets (0.70/0.70, 0.80/0.80, and 0.90/0.90), finding that the number of required stages scaled
from two to four and the total annualized operating cost increased from $0.112 to $0.204 m-3—
favorably competitive with conventional alternatives such as ion-exchange resins ($2.00–$4.00 m-
3) and adsorption ($2.00–$4.00 m-3). Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that purity specification
had a stronger influence on network complexity and cost than recovery specification, and that
increasing the target metal’s rejection coefficient substantially reduced both required membrane
area and cost. A two-train case for simultaneous nickel and copper recovery from electroplating
wastewater showed that allowing stage-specific material selection reduced total membrane area
from 2,081 m2 to 1,678 m2 and annualized operating cost from $0.468 to $0.436 m-3 relative to
single-material-per-train designs, as the optimizer exploited distinct rejection–flux–cost profiles
by assigning high-flux materials to early stages for bulk removal and high-rejection materials to
later stages for specification attainment. A four-train case recovering silver, platinum, nickel, and
copper simultaneously from a complex feed stream was solved to global optimality with 1,234
variables (276 binary) in approximately 1,260 seconds, demonstrating the scalability of the
approach to industrially relevant multicomponent systems.

Pt2+, Ni2+, and Cu2+ at >=90% recovery and purity from a 100 m3/h feed stream. Total area:

2,758.88 m2; power: 221.81 kW; annualized operating cost: 0.8182 $ m-3. Source: Verma et al.

The framework is designed for extensibility: the decoupled superstructure, unit model, and
material parameter components can independently accommodate additions of upstream UF
prefilters and downstream RO stages, mechanistic or data-driven membrane models capturing
concentration polarization and fouling, and novel membrane materials as they become available.
This work was published in Separation and Purification Technology (Verma, Jassby, and
Maravelias, 2025) and provides a generalizable computational roadmap for membrane network
design applicable to a broad range of wastewater valorization and critical mineral recovery
challenges beyond those directly addressed by the AnEMBR system.

A patent disclosure was filed for a novel method for synthesizing ion exchange membranes with

selective phosphate recovery systems.

5.3 Technology Transfer and Commercialization
The novel approach to ion exchange membrane synthesis has the potential to dramatically reduce
the cost of IX systems used in phosphate recovery applications. The simplicity and low-cost
materials of the carbon fiber/polypropylene ammonia recovery membrane make it an attractive
candidate for commercialization in distributed or decentralized ammonia recovery applications.
The machine learning pipeline for pervaporation membrane screening provides a generalizable
tool for accelerating membrane material discovery beyond this specific project.

6. Conclusions and Impact
This project successfully developed and demonstrated an Anaerobic Electric Membrane
Bioreactor (AnEMBR) system for the integrated treatment of municipal wastewater with
simultaneous recovery of volatile fatty acids, ammonia, and phosphate. The key conclusions from
the full project period are:

Electrofermentation can reliably produce VFAs from municipal wastewater at commercially
relevant concentrations when operated at appropriate temperature and retention time. Temperature
is the dominant determinant of product profile, enabling selective enrichment of desired acids
through temperature control.

Electroactive gas stripping membranes represent a technically viable and energy-efficient
approach to VFA and ammonia extraction from dilute aqueous streams. Both the multilayer
pervaporation membrane (PDMS/CF/PVDF/PP) and the simpler MED configuration successfully
demonstrated VFA extraction from real municipal wastewater effluent. The CF/PP ammonia
membrane achieved state-of-the-art flux and energy performance using extremely low-cost,
commercially available materials.

Phosphate-selective mixed-matrix membranes incorporating HMO nanoparticles provide a
chemical-free, low-energy route to selective phosphate recovery from wastewater. The 8.5-fold
enhancement in phosphate flux achieved in this project, combined with high selectivity over
competing anions, represents a significant advance toward economically viable phosphate
recovery.

Machine learning provides a powerful tool for accelerating membrane material discovery. The
LightGBM models trained on the largest PV dataset to date, with rigorous data leakage
management, provide reliable structure-performance predictions and identify promising candidate
polymers for experimental synthesis.

The pilot demonstration at the Hyperion Water Reclamation Facility validated the key biological
and membrane components of the AnEMBR system under real operating conditions with actual
municipal wastewater. The pilot demonstrated stable VFA production, effective UF membrane
operation, and measurable VFA and ammonia recovery using electroactive membranes.

Techno-economic analysis confirms that membrane flux is the critical performance parameter for
system economics. Achieving flux levels ten times the current baseline would render the system
economically profitable, providing a clear and quantifiable research target for future development
efforts.

Collectively, the findings of this project establish a strong technical foundation for the next
generation of municipal wastewater treatment infrastructure—one that transforms waste streams
into valuable resource streams, reduces energy demand, and contributes to urban sustainability and
supply chain resilience for critical nutrient chemicals. The seven publications, patent disclosure,
and pilot demonstration represent a substantial advancement in the field of resource recovery from
municipal wastewater.
