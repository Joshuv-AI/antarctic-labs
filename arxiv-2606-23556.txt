Computing Gaussian and exponential integrals in ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n}
Alexander Barvinok

Abstract
We consider expectations of the type 𝔼 ​ exp ⁡ { ∑ i = 1 m ϕ i } \mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right\} , where ϕ i : ℝ n ⟶ ℂ \phi_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}} are functions, each depending on a few coordinates of a point in ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} , and the expectation is taken with respect to the standard Gaussian or symmetric exponential probability measures. We prove sufficient conditions, in terms of the Lipschitz constants of ϕ i \phi_{i} and the combinatorics of their dependencies, for the integral to be non-zero, and, consequently, to be amenable to a computationally efficient approximation. We discuss applications to computing volumes of bodies and statistics on integer points in polyhedra in ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} .



Classification:
30C15, 68W25, 68W05, 82B20

1. Introduction and main results

(1.1) The setup

Let
\mu=\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{n}
be the product probability measure in

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}={\mathbb{R}}\oplus\cdots\oplus{\mathbb{R}}
and let
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be complex-valued random variables. We are interested in efficient computation (approximation) of the expectation

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right\}.

[formula]

Of course, as stated, the integral (1.1.1) is way too general. We will assume that each function
\phi_{i}
depends only on a few coordinates of a point

x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)
and impose some restrictions on the dependencies between the functions. We will also control the Lipschitz constants of
\phi_{i}
.

Integrals of the type (1.1.1) are ubiquitous in statistical physics and quantum field theory, see, for example,
[
Br86
]
,
[
GJ87
]
and
[
FV18
]
. Here we are interested in the computational complexity issues, as well as some discrete geometry applications.

The problem of approximating (1.1.1) is closely related to the problem of deciding when (1.1.1) is not 0. We say that complex numbers
z_{1}\neq 0
and
z_{2}\neq 0
approximate each other within a relative error of
\epsilon>0
if we can write
z_{1}=e^{w_{1}}
and
z_{2}=e^{w_{2}}
for some numbers
w_{1},w_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
|w_{1}-w_{2}|\leq\epsilon
. It is immediately clear that having (1.1.1) equal 0 creates a difficulty: in any meaningful way, a relative approximation
of 0 can only be 0.

As it turns out, having (1.1.1) guaranteed to be non-zero opens a way to efficiently approximate the integral. This idea has been around for some time by now, see
[
Ba16
]
and
[
PR17
]
. Here we briefly sketch how it is done, providing more detail in Section 2.

We introduce a parameter
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
and, given functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}
, consider the expectation

F(\lambda)=\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}\right\}

[formula]

as a function of
\lambda
. Let us fix a
\rho>1
. Suppose that for some functions
\phi_{i}
and some
M\geq 3
, we have

0\ <\ |F(\lambda)|\ \leq\ M\quad\text{provided}\quad|\lambda|\leq\rho.

[formula]

It turns out then that one can approximate (1.1.1) within relative error
0<\epsilon<1
from the moments

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right)^{k}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leq m}\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left(\phi_{i_{1}}\cdots\phi_{i_{k}}\right)\quad\text{for}\quad k=O_{\rho}\left(\ln\ln M-\ln\epsilon\right),

[formula]

where the implied constant in the “
O
” notation depends only on
\rho
. In some cases, (1.1.3) holds with
M=\exp\left\{(mn)^{O(1)}\right\}
, which results in the bound

k=O_{\rho}\left(\ln m+\ln n-\ln\epsilon\right)

in (1.1.4). We note that while the upper bound in (1.1.3) is usually quite straightforward, it is the lower bound there that requires work.

Assuming that each
\phi_{i}
depends on
r_{i}=O(1)
coordinates, computing each of the
m^{O(k)}
expectations
\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left(\phi_{i_{1}}\cdots\phi_{i_{k}}\right)
in (1.1.4)
reduces to integration in a coordinate subspace of dimension
r=r_{i_{1}}+\ldots+r_{i_{k}}=O(k)
. In most applications, integration in a
k
-dimensional space can be done in

k^{O(k)}
time, which in turn produces a quasi-polynomial algorithm of
(m+n)^{O_{\rho}(\ln((m+n)/\epsilon))}
complexity to approximate (1.1.1).

In fact, to approximate (1.1.1), one can replace (1.1.3) by a weaker condition: it suffices to have the inequality satisfied for all
\lambda
in some fixed connected open set
{\mathbb{U}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
containing
0
and
1
, and not necessarily in the disc
|z|\leq\rho
, cf. Section 2.2 of
[
Ba16
]
.

Zeros of
F(\lambda)
in (1.1.2) are of a considerable interest to statistical physics, as they correspond to phase transitions, with
\lambda
playing the role of the inverse temperature, see
[
FV18
]
. One can informally say that if the system stays sufficiently far away from a phase transition, then the partition function can be efficiently approximated. In probability, the absence of zeros is related to the Central Limit Theorem type behavior of sequences
[
MS26
]
.

In this paper, we consider two special cases. In the first case,
\mu
is the standard Gaussian measure with density

{1\over(2\pi)^{n/2}}\exp\left\{-{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}^{2}\right\}\quad\text{where}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right),

[formula]

and we control the Lipschitz constants of
\phi_{i}
in the
\ell^{2}
norm. In the second case,

\mu
is the symmetric exponential measure with density

{1\over 2^{n}}\exp\left\{-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\xi_{j}|\right\}\quad\text{for}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right),

[formula]

and we control the Lipschitz constants of
\phi_{i}
in the
\ell^{1}
norm.
In
[
Ba26
]
, the author considered the integral (1.1.1) in the case of a general product probability measure
\mu
in the product space
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{n}
, with controlled Lipschitz constants of
\phi_{i}
in the Hamming metric of
\Omega
.

To control the dependencies among
\phi_{i}
, we introduce some formal definitions.
We say that a function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}

depends on the coordinates

\left\{\xi_{j}:\ j\in J_{\phi}\right\}
provided

\phi\left(\xi_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{\prime}\right)=\phi\left(\xi_{1}^{\prime\prime},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{\prime\prime}\right)\quad\text{whenever}\quad\xi_{j}^{\prime}=\xi_{j}^{\prime\prime}\quad\text{for all}\quad j\in J_{\phi},

and
J_{\phi}
is the minimal set under inclusion with that property.
We say that
\phi

depends on at most
r
coordinates
if
|J_{\phi}|\leq r
.
We say that functions
\phi,\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}

share a coordinate
if
J_{\phi}\cap J_{\psi}\neq\emptyset
.

A popular approach to analyze the integrals (1.1.1) and (1.1.2) is via the
cluster expansion
, see
[
Br86
]
. Here we pursue a different, inductive approach that seems to produce stronger results in terms of the combinatorics of dependencies and also in terms of the required analytic properties of functions
\phi_{i}
. Thus, unlike in the case of the cluster expansion approach
[
Br86
]
, we do not require to bound higher derivatives of
\phi_{i}
or even to assume that the functions are smooth. The proofs for the Gaussian and exponential measures presented in this paper are quite similar and can be extended to other measures with sufficiently strong concentration properties and a suitable logarithmic Sobolev inequality, cf. Chapter 5 of
[
Le01
]
.

Our first result deals with the Gaussian measure (1.1.5).
We consider the standard
\ell^{2}
norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
:

\|x\|_{2}=\left(\xi_{1}^{2}+\ldots+\xi_{n}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\quad\text{where}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right).

For
L>0
, a function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
is
L
-
Lipschitz
in the
\ell^{2}
norm, provided

|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|\ \leq\ L\|x-y\|_{2}.

We prove the following result.

(1.2) Theorem

Let
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be
1
-Lipschitz functions in the
\ell^{2}
norm. Suppose that

Then for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that

|\lambda|\ \leq\ {1\over 10\sqrt{(c-1)\Delta}}

we have

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right\}\neq 0,

where the expectation is taken with respect to the standard Gaussian probability measure with density (1.1.5).

We are interested in the situations where the parameters
c
and
\Delta
are small (fixed in advance), whereas
m
and
n
are allowed to grow.

Let functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}
and parameters
c
and
\Delta
be as in Theorem 1.2. Let us fix a
\rho>1
.
As we argue in Section 2,
the expectation

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right\}\quad\text{where}\quad|\lambda|\ \leq\ {1\over 10\rho\sqrt{(c-1)\Delta}}

can be approximated within relative error
\epsilon
in polynomial time from the moments (1.1.4) with
k=O_{\rho}\bigl(\ln((m+n)/\epsilon)\bigr)
,
where the implied constant in the “
O
” notation depends only on
\rho
.

A weaker version of Theorem 1.2 is obtained in
[
Ba26
]
: there the functions
\phi_{i}
are required to be 1-Lipschitz in the
\ell^{1}
norm, while the bound for
|\lambda|
is inversely proportional to the product
c\sqrt{r}
, where each function
\phi_{i}
depends on at most
r
coordinates and for any coordinate
\xi_{j}
there are at most
c
functions
\phi_{i}
that depend on
\xi_{j}
.

We prove Theorem 1.2 in Section 5, and in Section 3 we describe some applications to integer point counting in polyhedra.

Our second result deals with the symmetric exponential measure (1.1.6).
We consider the
\ell^{1}
norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
:

\|x\|_{1}=|\xi_{1}|+\ldots+|\xi_{n}|\quad\text{where}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right).

For
L>0
, a function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
is
L
-Lipschitz
in the
\ell^{1}
norm, provided

|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|\ \leq\ L\|x-y\|_{1}.

Our main result is as follows.

(1.3) Theorem

Let
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be 1-Lipschitz functions in the
\ell^{1}
norm. Suppose that

Then for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that

|\lambda|\ \leq\ {1\over 25(c-1)\sqrt{r}}

we have

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right\}\ \neq\ 0,

where the expectation is taken with respect to the symmetric exponential probability measure with density (1.1.6).

Again, we are interested in the situations where
r
and
c
are small (fixed in advance), whereas
m
and
n
are allowed to grow.

Let functions
\phi_{i}
and parameters
r
and
c
be as in Theorem 1.3. Let us fix a
\rho>1
. Then

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right\}\quad\text{where}\quad|\lambda|\ \leq\ {1\over 25\rho(c-1)\sqrt{r}}

can be approximated within relative error
\epsilon
in polynomial time from the moments (1.1.4) with
k=O_{\rho}\bigl(\ln((m+n)/\epsilon)\bigr)
,

We prove Theorem 1.3 in Section 6, and in Section 4 we describe some applications to computing volumes.

(1.4) Notation

We denote by
\mathbb{i}
the imaginary unit, so
\mathbb{i}^{2}=-1
. For a complex number
z=a+\mathbb{i}b
, we denote by
\Re
the real part, and by
\Im
the imaginary part:
\Re\kern 1.66672ptz=a
,
\Im\kern 1.66672ptz=b
.

2. Computing approximations

Here we discuss in some detail how Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 lead to approximation algorithms.
For
\rho>1
, let

{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}=\left\{z:\ |z|\leq\rho\right\}

be
the closed disc of radius
\rho
in the complex plane, centered at
0
.

(2.1) Approximating within an additive error

Let
f:{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be a holomorphic function such that

|f(z)|\ \leq\ M\quad\text{for all}\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}

and some
M>1
.
For an integer
k>0
, let

T_{k}(f;z)=f(0)+\sum_{l=1}^{k}{f^{(l)}(0)\over l!}z^{l}

be the Taylor polynomial of
f
of degree
k
computed at
0
.
The Cauchy bound,

\left|{f^{l}(0)\over l!}\right|\ \leq\ {M\over\rho^{l}},

see, for example, Section III.7 of
[
La99
]
, implies that

\left|f(1)-T_{k}(f;1)\right|=\left|\sum_{l=k+1}^{\infty}{f^{(l)}(0)\over k!}\right|\ \leq\ M\sum_{l=k+1}^{\infty}\rho^{-l}={M\over(\rho-1)\rho^{k}}.

[formula]

It follows from (2.1.1) that to approximate
f(1)
by
T_{k}(f;1)
within an additive error of
0<\epsilon<1
, it suffices to choose

k=O_{\rho}\bigl(\ln M-\ln\epsilon\bigr)
, where the implied constant in the “
O
” notation depends on
\rho
only.

(2.2) Approximating within a relative error

Suppose now that
g:{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
is a holomorphic function such that

g(0)=1\quad\text{and}\quad 0\ <\ |g(z)|\ \leq\ M\quad\text{for all}\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}

[formula]

and some
M\geq 3
. Our goal is to approximate
g(1)
within relative error
0<\epsilon<1
.

Since
g(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}
, we can choose a continuous branch of

f(z)=\ln g(z),

which we choose so that
f(0)=\ln 1=0
.

From (2.2.1) we have

\Re\kern 1.66672ptf(z)\ \leq\ \ln M\quad\text{for all}\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}.

[formula]

The Borel - Carathéodory Theorem (see, for example, Section XII.3 of
[
La99
]
) asserts that

\max_{|z|\leq r}|f(z)|\ \leq\ {2r\over\rho-r}\sup_{|z|\leq\rho}\Re\kern 1.66672ptf(z)+{\rho+r\over\rho-r}|f(0)|\quad\text{for}\quad 0<r<\rho,

which, together with (2.2.2), implies that

|f(z)|\ \leq\ {2\rho+2\over\rho-1}\ln M\quad\text{for all}\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{{\rho+1\over 2}}.

[formula]

We approximate
f(1)
within additive error
0<\epsilon<1
by the value
T_{k}(f;1)
of the Taylor polynomial of
f
and hence approximate

g(1)
by
e^{f(1)}
within relative error
\epsilon
. It follows from (2.1.1) and (2.2.3) that we can choose

k=O_{\rho}\left(\ln\ln M-\ln\epsilon\right).

[formula]

Moreover, one can compute the derivatives
f^{(l)}(0)
for
l=1,\ldots,k
from the derivatives
g^{(l)}(0)
for
l=1,\ldots,k
in
O(k^{2})
time by solving a
k\times k
non-degenerate triangular system of linear equations, so that the Taylor polynomial
T_{k}(f;z)
of
f=\ln g
can be computed in
O(k^{2})
time from the Taylor polynomial
T_{k}(g;z)
of
g
. Indeed, since
f^{\prime}(z)=g^{\prime}(z)/g(z)
, we have
g^{\prime}(z)=f^{\prime}(z)g(z)
and hence

g^{(k)}(0)=\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}{k-1\choose l}f^{(k-l)}(0)g^{(l)}(0),

see Section 2.2 of
[
Ba16
]
for detail.

We also remark that (2.2.1) can be replaced by a weaker condition that the inequalities hold for all
z
in some fixed connected open set
{\mathbb{U}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, containing 0 and 1. We can reduce this general case to that of the disc
{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}
, by replacing
g(z)
with the composition

g(\phi(z))
, where
\phi:{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{U}}
is a holomorphic map, such that
\phi(0)=0
and
\phi(1)=1
, see Section 2.2 of
[
Ba16
]
.

(2.3) Approximating expectations

Given functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, our goal is to approximate

F(\lambda)=\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right\}

[formula]

within relative error
0<\epsilon<1
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\phi_{i}(0)=0
, since adding a constant to
\phi_{i}
,
\phi_{i}:=\phi_{i}+a
, results in multiplying the integral (2.3.1) by
e^{\lambda a}
.

We assume that for some
\lambda_{0}>0
the integral (2.3.1) converges absolutely and uniformly on the disc
|\lambda|\leq\lambda_{0}
in the complex plane, and, moreover, that for some
M\geq 3
we have

0\ <\ |F(\lambda)|\ \leq\ M\quad\text{provided}\quad|\lambda|\leq\lambda_{0}.

[formula]

Let us fix some
\rho>1
.
To approximate
F({\lambda})
for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
|\lambda|\leq\lambda_{0}/\rho
,
we define
g_{\lambda}:{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
by

g_{\lambda}(z)=F(\lambda z).

Hence

g_{\lambda}(0)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\ 0\ <\ \left|g_{\lambda}(z)\right|\ \leq\ M\quad\text{for all}\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}.

It follows from Section 2.2, see (2.2.4) in particular, that one can approximate
F(\lambda)=g_{\lambda}(1)
within relative error
0<\epsilon<1
in polynomial time
from the moments

g_{\lambda}^{(k)}(0)=\lambda^{k}\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right)^{k}\quad\text{for}\quad k=O_{\rho}\left(\ln\ln M-\ln\epsilon\right),

[formula]

where the implied constant in the “
O
” notation depends on
\rho
only.

In the next two sections, we obtain bounds for
M
in the case of the standard Gaussian (Section 2.4) and symmetric exponential (Section 2.5) measures, in the context of Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.3 respectively.

(2.4) Gaussian measure

Let
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be 1-Lipschitz functions in the
\ell^{2}
norm
such that
\phi_{i}(0)=0
for
i=1,\ldots,m
and let
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
be as in Theorem 1.2. We define
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
by

\psi(x)=\Re\kern 1.66672pt\left(\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(x)\right)\quad\text{for}\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

so that

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left|\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right\}\right|=\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{\psi},

[formula]

where the expectation is taken with respect to the standard Gaussian measure with density (1.1.5).
Since
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is
m
-Lipschitz and
\psi(0)=0
, we have

\psi(x)\ \leq\ m\|x\|_{2}\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{\psi}\ \leq\ \mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{m\|x\|_{2}}={1\over(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\exp\left\{m\|x\|_{2}-{1\over 2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right\}\ dx

[formula]

\displaystyle={1\over(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\exp\left\{m\|x\|_{2}-{1\over 4}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right\}e^{-\|x\|_{2}^{2}/4}\ dx

\displaystyle\leq\ {e^{m^{2}}\over(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-\|x\|^{2}/4}\ dx=e^{m^{2}}2^{n/2}.

It follows from Theorem 1.2 and (2.4.1)–(2.4.2) that in (2.3.2) we can choose
M=e^{m^{2}}2^{n/2}
and hence in (2.3.3) we have

k=O_{\rho}\left(\ln(m+n)-\ln\epsilon\right).

(2.5) Exponential measure

Let
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be 1-Lipschitz functions in the
\ell^{1}
norm such that
\phi_{i}(0)=0
for
i=1,\ldots,m
and let
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
be as in Theorem 1.3. We define
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
by

\psi(x)=\Re\kern 1.66672pt\left(\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(x)\right)\quad\text{for}\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

so that

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left|\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\right\}\right|=\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{\psi},

[formula]

where the expectation is taken with respect to the symmetric exponential measure with density (1.1.6). Clearly,
\psi
is
{1\over 2}
-Lipschitz in the
\ell^{1}
norm and since
\psi(0)=0
, we have

\psi(x)\ \leq\ {1\over 2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\xi_{j}|\quad\text{for}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right).

Therefore,

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{\psi}\ \leq\ {1\over 2^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\exp\left\{-{1\over 2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\xi_{j}|\right\}\ dx=2^{n}.

[formula]

It follows from (2.5.1)–(2.5.2) and Theorem 1.3 that in (2.3.2) we can choose
M=2^{n}
and hence in (2.3.3) we have

k=O_{\rho}\left(\ln n-\ln\epsilon\right).

3. Applications to integer points in polyhedra

(3.1) Integer points in polyhedra

Let
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be the set of points with non-negative integer coordinates, let
A=\left(a_{ij}\right)
be a real
m\times n
matrix and let
b=\left(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\right)
be a real
m
-vector. We define a polyhedron
P\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by the system of linear equations and inequalities:

\displaystyle P=\Bigl\{\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right):

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\xi_{j}=b_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,m\quad\text{and}

[formula]

\displaystyle\xi_{j}\ \geq\ 0\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,n\Bigr\}.

Note that for any
s\neq 0
, the scaled matrix
sA
and vector
sb
define the same polyhedron
P
.

Suppose we want to compute the number
|P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}|
of integer points in
P
. This is a well-known
\#
P-hard problem, and even deciding whether
P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}\neq\emptyset
is an NP-complete problem, so approximating
\left|P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}\right|
is also computationally hard. Hence our goal is to find a reasonable computationally efficient relaxation of the problem.

Let
{\mathbb{q}}=\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\right)
be a vector of real numbers
0<q_{j}<1
for
j=1,\ldots,n
.
We consider the multivariate geometric distribution in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}
defined by

{\mathbb{P}}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-q_{j})q_{j}^{\xi_{j}}\quad\text{where}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right).

[formula]

Note that

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\xi_{j}={q_{j}\over 1-q_{j}}\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

[formula]

One reasonable choice of
q_{j}
is the
maximum entropy distribution
, constructed as follows. Suppose that the polyhedron
P
defined by (3.1.1) is bounded and has a non-empty relative interior, that is, contains a point
\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)
where

\xi_{j}>0
for all
j
. Then the strictly concave function

g(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl(\left(\xi_{j}+1\right)\ln\left(\xi_{j}+1\right)-\xi_{j}\ln\xi_{j}\bigr)\quad\text{for}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)

attains it maximum on
P
at a unique point
z=\left(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n}\right)
, which can be efficiently computed as a solution to a convex optimization problem. Let us now define

q_{j}={\zeta_{j}\over 1+\zeta_{j}}\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

It follows from (3.1.3) that

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\xi_{j}\right)=b_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,m,

so that the expectation of the random integer vector
x
defined by (3.1.2) lies in
P
. Moreover, it is proved in
[
BH10
]
, see Theorem 4 there, that

{\mathbb{P}}(x)=e^{-g(z)}\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+},

so that the probability mass function is constant on the set of integer points in
P

and hence

|P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}|=e^{g(z)}{\mathbb{P}}\left(P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}\right).

Thus counting integer points in
P
reduces to computing the probability

{\mathbb{P}}\left(P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}\right).

[formula]

(3.2) Quadratic penalty function

We want to replace the probability (3.1.4) that is hard to compute by
an easier computable statistics.

For a matrix
A
and a vector
b
in (3.1.1), we define
F_{A,b}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
by

F_{A,b}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(-b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\xi_{j}\right)^{2}\quad\text{where}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right).

Hence
F_{A,b}(x)=0
if
x
satisfies the equations of (3.1.1) and
F_{A,b}(x)>0
if
x
does not. We then choose a vector
{\mathbb{q}}=\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\right)
where
0<q_{j}<1
for
j=1,\ldots,n
and consider the expectation of
\exp\left\{-{1\over 2}F_{A,b}\right\}
with respect to
the multivariate geometric distribution (3.1.2):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{-{1\over 2}F_{A,b}\right\}=\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-q_{j}),\quad\text{where}

[formula]

\displaystyle\Phi\bigl(A,b;{\mathbb{q}}\bigr)=\sum\Sb x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}

\displaystyle x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)\exp\left\{-{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(-b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\xi_{j}\right)^{2}\right\}\prod_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}^{\xi_{j}}.

Clearly,

\Phi\bigl(A,b;{\mathbb{q}}\bigr)\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-q_{j})\ \geq\ {\mathbb{P}}\left(P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\right),

so (3.2.1) provides an upper bound for (3.1.4), but unlike (3.1.4), it is amenable to an efficient approximation. We also note
that

\lim_{s\longrightarrow+\infty}\Phi\bigl(sA,sb;{\mathbb{q}}\bigr)\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-q_{j})={\mathbb{P}}\left(P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\right),

which ought to impose some limits on the computability of
\Phi(sA,sb;{\mathbb{q}})
for large scaling factors
s
.

Next, we fix
A
and
b
and consider
\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})
defined by (3.2.1) as a function
{\mathbb{q}}\longmapsto\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})
of
q_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying

|q_{j}|<1\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,n,

where we agree that
q_{j}^{0}=1
.
Clearly,
\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})
is well-defined for such
{\mathbb{q}}
. It can be viewed as a partition function in the Potts model of a particular kind, cf.
[
FV18
]
.

We prove the following result.

(3.3) Theorem

Let
A=\left(a_{ij}\right)
be an
m\times n
real matrix, let
b=\left(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\right)
be a real
m
-vector and let

{\mathbb{q}}=\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\right)
be a complex
n
-vector, such that

|q_{j}|<1
for
j=1,\ldots,n
.
Suppose that there are at most
r\geq 2
non-zero entries in every row of
A
and at most
c\geq 1
non-zero entries in every column of
A
. Suppose further that

|b_{i}|\ \leq\ {1\over 10(r+1)\sqrt{c}}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,m

and that

{|q_{j}|\over 1-|q_{j}|}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ij}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\ \leq\ {1\over 10(r+1)\sqrt{c}}\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

Then for

\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})=\sum\Sb x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}\\x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)\exp\left\{-{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(-b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\xi_{j}\right)^{2}\right\}\prod_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}^{\xi_{j}},

we have

0\ <\ \left|\Phi(A,b;q)\right|\ \leq\ \prod_{j=1}^{n}{1\over 1-|q_{j}|}.

We note that given
{\mathbb{q}}
, we can always scale
A\longmapsto sA
and
b\longmapsto sb
for
s>0
, so that the polyhedron
P
of (3.1.1) remains the same, while the scaled matrix
sA
and scaled vector
sb
satisfy the conditions of Theorem 3.3.

Before we prove Theorem 3.3, we discuss how it leads to an efficient algorithm to approximate
\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})
.

(3.4) Computing
\Phi(A,b,{\mathbb{q}})

Let us fix
\rho>1
and suppose that a matrix
A=\left(a_{ij}\right)
, a vector
b=\left(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\right)
and a vector
\rho{\mathbb{q}}=\left(\rho q_{1},\ldots,\rho q_{n}\right)
satisfy the conditions of Theorem 3.3. In particular,
|q_{j}|<\rho^{-1}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
.
Our goal is to approximate

\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})
.

Let
{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be the closed disc of radius
\rho
in the complex plane, centered at 0. For given
A,b
and
{\mathbb{q}}
, we define a function

g=g_{A,b,{\mathbb{q}}}:{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
by

g(z)=\exp\left\{{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{2}\right\}\Phi(A,b;z{\mathbb{q}}).

Applying Theorem 3.3, we conclude that

g(0)=1\quad\text{and}\quad 0\ <\ |g(z)|\ \leq\ M\quad\text{for all}\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\rho},

where we can choose

M=\exp\left\{{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{2}\right\}\prod_{j=1}^{n}{1\over 1-|q_{j}|}\ <\ e^{m/200}\left({\rho\over\rho-1}\right)^{n}.

It follows that one can approximate the value of

\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})=\exp\left\{-{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{2}\right\}g(1)

within relative error
0<\epsilon<1
in polynomial time from the derivatives

g^{(k)}(0)
for

k=O_{\rho}\left(\ln n+\ln m-\ln\epsilon\right),

[formula]

cf. Section 2.
We have

\begin{split}g^{(k)}(0)=&k!\exp\left\{{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{2}\right\}\\
&\qquad\times\sum\Sb(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}:\\
\xi_{1}+\ldots+\xi_{n}=k\exp\left\{-{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(-b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\xi_{j}\right)^{2}\right\}\prod_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}^{\xi_{j}},\end{split}

and hence computing
g^{(k)}(0)
reduces to the enumeration of
{n+k-1\choose k}
non-negative integer solutions to the equation
\xi_{1}+\ldots+\xi_{n}=k
.

In view of (3.4.1), we obtain a quasi-polynomial deterministic algorithm to approximate
\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})
.

To get a feeling of the statistics computed by
\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})
, consider a family of instances where the sparsity parameters
r
and
c
are fixed, while the dimensions
m
and
n
of the matrix
A=\left(a_{ij}\right)
and vector
b=\left(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\right)
are allowed to grow. We suppose further that the entries
a_{ij}
and
b_{i}
are integer and stay uniformly bounded,
a_{ij},b_{i}=O(1)
, and that the probabilities
{\mathbb{q}}=\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\right)
remain separated from 1, which by (3.1.3) enforces
\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\xi_{j}=O(1)
, so that the expectations of the coordinates stay uniformly bounded. Then there is a scaling
A\longmapsto sA
,
b\longmapsto sb
that makes
A
and
b
satisfy the conditions of Theorem 3.3 with some fixed slack
\rho>1
and hence makes
\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})
efficiently computable, and such that the penalty
\left(b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\xi_{j}\right)^{2}
for violating an equation, when non-zero, is at least some positive constant
\Omega(1)
, depending on
r
and
c
alone. Hence the contribution of an
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}
to
\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})
is exponentially small in the number of violated equations in (3.1.1).

The proof of Theorem 3.3 is based on Theorem 1.2 and a Fourier transform trick, also known in statistical physics as the
Hubbard - Stratonovich transformation
, which allows one to toggle between Gaussian and discrete partition functions, see Section 8.7.5 of
[
FV18
]
.

(3.5) Lemma

Given an
m\times n
real matrix
A=\left(a_{ij}\right)
, a real
m
-vector
b=\left(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\right)
, and a complex
n
-vector

{\mathbb{q}}=\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\right)
, where
|q_{j}|<1
for
j=1,\ldots,n
, we define
f_{j}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, by

f_{j}(t)=\left(1-q_{j}\exp\left\{\mathbb{i}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ij}\tau_{i}\right\}\right)^{-1}\quad\text{where}\quad t=\left(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m}\right).

Then

\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})={1\over(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\exp\left\{-\mathbb{i}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\tau_{i}-{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau_{i}^{2}\right\}\prod_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}(t)\ dt.

Proof

We use the well-known formula

{1\over\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{\mathbb{i}\alpha\tau}e^{-\tau^{2}/2}\ d\tau=\exp\left\{-{\alpha^{2}\over 2}\right\}.

Let
t=\left(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m}\right)
be the standard Gaussian random
m
-vector and let
\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}
denote the expectation with respect to the standard Gaussian probability measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Then

\begin{split}\Phi\bigl(A,b;{\mathbb{q}}\bigr)=&\sum\Sb x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}\\
x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)\exp\left\{-{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(-b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\xi_{j}\right)^{2}\right\}\prod_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}^{\xi_{j}}.\\
=&\sum\Sb x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}\\
x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)\left(\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{\mathbb{i}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau_{i}\left(-b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\xi_{j}\right)\right\}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}^{\xi_{j}}\\
=&\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\Biggl(\exp\left\{-\mathbb{i}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\tau_{i}\right\}\\
&\qquad\times\sum\Sb x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}\\
x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)\exp\left\{\mathbb{i}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ij}\tau_{i}\right)\right\}\prod_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}^{\xi_{j}}\Biggr)\\
=&\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left(\exp\left\{-\mathbb{i}\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{i}\tau_{i}\right\}\prod_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}(t)\right),\end{split}

as required.
∎

(3.6) Proof of Theorem 3.3

We define functions
\phi_{k}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
,
k=1,\ldots,m+n
, as follows.
For
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
t=\left(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m}\right)
, we define

\phi_{i}(t)=\mathbb{i}b_{i}\tau_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,m

and

\phi_{m+j}(t)=\ln\left(1-q_{j}\exp\left\{\mathbb{i}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ij}\tau_{i}\right\}\right)\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

Since
|q_{j}|<1
, the functions
\phi_{m+j}
are well-defined by the choice of the branch of the logarithm for
\phi_{m+j}(0)=\ln(1-q_{j})
. By Lemma 3.5,

\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})=\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{m+n}\phi_{i}\right\},

[formula]

where the expectation is taken with respect to the standard Gaussian probability measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

To apply Theorem 1.2, we bound the Lipschitz constants of
\phi_{i}
in the
\ell^{2}
norm of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Clearly, for
i=1,\ldots,m
, the function
\phi_{i}
is
|b_{i}|
-Lipschitz. Computing the gradient of
\phi_{m+j}
, we get

{\partial\phi_{m+j}\over\partial\tau_{i}}=-\mathbb{i}a_{ij}q_{j}{\exp\left\{\mathbb{i}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ij}\tau_{i}\right\}\over 1-q_{j}\exp\left\{\mathbb{i}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ij}\tau_{i}\right\}}\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,n

and hence the Lipschitz constant of
\phi_{m+j}
does not exceed

\|\nabla\phi_{m+j}\|_{2}\ \leq\ {|q_{j}|\over 1-|q_{j}|}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ij}\right)^{1/2}.

Next, we observe that for each
i
, at most
r+1
functions
\phi_{k}
depend on the coordinate
\tau_{i}
and that each function
\phi_{k}
shares a coordinate with at most
(r+1)c
other functions
\phi_{i}
. Applying Theorem 1.2 to (3.6.1), we conclude that

\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})\neq 0
.
Clearly,

|\Phi(A,b;{\mathbb{q}})|\ \leq\ \sum\Sb x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{+}\\x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{n}|q_{j}|^{\xi_{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}{1\over 1-|q_{j}|}.

∎

4. Applications to volume computation

Randomized Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms have been spectacularly successful in efficiently approximating volumes of convex bodies, see
[
LV07
]
. Deterministic algorithms were noticeably less so, although there were some recent successful attempts for some special combinatorially defined polytopes

[
BR26
]
,
[
GN25
]
.

In this section, we apply Theorem 1.3 to approximate volumes of compact sets of a particular structure. Some of those sets are non-convex.

The application is based on a simple formula.

(4.1) Lemma

Let
\Psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a continuous function such that

\Psi(\alpha x)=\alpha\Psi(x)\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\quad\text{and all}\quad\alpha\geq 0

and

\Psi(x)=0\Longrightarrow x=0.

Then for the set
K_{\Psi}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

K_{\Psi}=\Bigl\{x:\ \Psi(x)\leq 1\Bigr\},

we have

\operatorname{vol}K_{\Psi}={1\over n!}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-\Psi(x)}\ dx.

Proof

Clearly,
K_{\Psi}
is a compact set.
For
t>0
, we have

tK_{\Psi}=\Bigl\{x:\ \Psi(x)\leq t\Bigr\}.

Let

F(t)=\operatorname{vol}\left(tK_{\Psi}\right)=t^{n}\operatorname{vol}(K)\quad\text{for}\quad t>0.

Then

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-\Psi(x)}\ dx=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-t}\ dF(t)=\operatorname{vol}(K)\int_{0}^{+\infty}nt^{n-1}e^{-t}\ dt=n!\operatorname{vol}(K).

∎

(4.2) Examples

Let us fix some
\rho>1
.
Suppose that

\Psi(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\xi_{j}|+{\lambda\over\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(x)\quad\text{for}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right),

where
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\phi_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
satisfy the conditions of Theorem 1.3 and, in addition,
\phi_{i}
are positive homogeneous of degree
1
:

\phi_{i}(\alpha x)=\alpha\phi_{i}(x)\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad i=1,\ldots,m.

By Lemma 4.1, we have

\operatorname{vol}K_{\Psi}={1\over n!}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-\Psi(x)}\ dx,

[formula]

and as we discuss in Section 2, to approximate (4.2.1) within relative error
0<\epsilon<1
, one needs to compute

m^{O_{\rho}(\ln n-\ln\epsilon)}
integrals

\begin{split}&\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\phi_{i_{1}}(x)\cdots\phi_{i_{k}}(x)\exp\left\{-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\xi_{j}|\right\}\ dx\\
&\text{for}\quad k=O_{\rho}\left(\ln n-\ln\epsilon\right).\end{split}

[formula]

One natural example of such functions
\phi_{i}
is provided by
support functions
of convex bodies, and, in particular, polytopes. Namely, let
B_{i}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a convex body containing the origin in its relative interior, and suppose that

\phi_{i}(x)=\max_{y\in B_{i}}\langle x,y\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the standard scalar product in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Clearly,
\phi_{i}
is positive homogeneous of degree
1
. Since the function

x\longrightarrow\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\xi_{j}|\quad\text{for}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)

is the support function of the cube
C_{n}=[-1,1]^{n}
, then for
\lambda\geq 0
the set
K_{\Psi}
is the polar of the Minkowski sum,

K_{\Psi}=\left(C_{n}+{\lambda\over\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}\right)^{\circ}.

If
B_{i}
lies in a coordinate subspace of dimension
r_{i}
then
\phi_{i}
depends on at most
r_{i}
coordinates. Moreover, if
B_{i}
is a polytope defined as the convex hull of
v_{i}
vertices, then

\phi_{i}
is a piece-wise linear functions, and the integrals (4.2.2) can be computed in
(rv)^{O(r)}
time, where
r=r_{i_{1}}+\ldots+r_{i_{k}}
and
v=v_{i_{1}}+\ldots+v_{i_{k}}
,
see for example,
[
B+11
]
.

Sets
K_{\Psi}
for which
\operatorname{vol}K_{\Psi}
can be efficiently approximated via Theorem 1.3 include some non-convex sets. Suppose, for example, that

\Psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is defined by

\Psi(x)=|\xi_{1}|+|\xi_{2}|-\epsilon|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|\quad\text{where}\quad x=\left(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\right)

and
0<\epsilon<1
. Then the points
(1+\epsilon,0)
and
(0,1+\epsilon)
lie in
K_{\Psi}
but their average
\left({1+\epsilon\over 2},{1+\epsilon\over 2}\right)
does not.

5. Proof of Theorem 1.2

To simplify notation, in this section we denote the
\ell^{2}
norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
just by
\|\cdot\|
. We denote the standard Gaussian measure with density (1.1.5) by
\mu
and denote the expectation with respect to that measure by
\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}
. We say that a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
is
L
-Lipschitz, if it is
L
-Lipschitz in the
\ell^{2}
norm
\|\cdot\|
.

Our proof is based on the following well-known result regarding real-valued Lipschitz functions.

(5.1) Theorem

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be an
L
-Lipschitz function such that
\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}f=0
.
Then

Proof

See, for example, Section 5.1 of
[
Le01
]
.
∎

Next, we turn to complex-valued Lipschitz functions.

(5.2) Lemma

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be an
L
-Lipschitz function for
L={1\over 4}
.
Then

\left|\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{f}\right|\ \geq\ {1\over 2}\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left|e^{f}\right|.

Proof

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}f=0
. Let
f=g+\mathbb{i}h
, where
g,h:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
are
real-valued
L
-Lipschitz functions such that
\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}g=\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}h=0
. From the Jensen inequality, we obtain

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{g}\ \geq\ 1.

[formula]

We have

e^{f(x)}=e^{g(x)}\left(\cos h(x)+\mathbb{i}\sin h(x)\right)\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Let

X=\Bigl\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\quad|h(x)|\ \leq\ 1\Bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{X}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus X.

From Part (2) of Lemma 5.1, we have

\mu(\overline{X})\ \leq\ 2\exp\left\{-{1\over 2L^{2}}\right\}=2e^{-8}.

[formula]

For all
x\in X
, we have
|h(x)|\leq 1
and hence

\displaystyle\left|\int_{X}e^{f}\ d\mu\right|\ \geq

\displaystyle\Re\kern 1.66672pt\left(\int_{X}e^{f}\ d\mu\right)=\int_{X}\Re\kern 1.66672pte^{f}\ d\mu\ \geq\ \int_{X}(\cos 1)e^{g}\ d\mu

[formula]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\cos 1)\int_{X}e^{g}\ d\mu.

Let
[\overline{X}]
be the indicator of
\overline{X}
, that is, the function
[\overline{X}]:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that

[\overline{X}](x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if\ }x\notin X\\
0&\text{if\ }x\in X.\end{cases}

We use the Hölder inequality with

p={1\over L^{2}}=16\quad\text{and}\quad q={1\over 1-L^{2}}={16\over 15},

and get

\int_{\overline{X}}e^{g}\ d\mu=\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left([\overline{X}]e^{g}\right)\ \leq\ \left(\mu(\overline{X})\right)^{1/q}\left(\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{pg}\right)^{1/p}.

Since
pg
is
pL
-Lipschitz, from Part (1) of Lemma 5.1, we have

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{pg}\ \leq\ \exp\left\{{p^{2}L^{2}\over 2}\right\}

and hence from (5.2.2) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{X}}e^{g}\ d\mu\ \leq\ \left(2\exp\left\{-{1\over 2L^{2}}\right\}\right)^{1/q}\exp\left\{{pL^{2}\over 2}\right\}

[formula]

\displaystyle<\ 2\exp\left\{-{1-L^{2}\over 2L^{2}}+{1\over 2}\right\}=2\exp\left\{1-{1\over 2L^{2}}\right\}=2e^{-7}.

Let

a=\int_{X}e^{g}\ d\mu\quad\text{and}\quad b=\int_{\overline{X}}e^{g}\ d\mu.

Clearly,

\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}\left|e^{f}\right|=\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{g}=a+b

and from (5.2.3), we obtain

\left|\mathbb{E\kern 1.66672pt}e^{f}\right|\ \geq\ a(\cos 1)-b.
