Abstract. Current photovoltaic and solar-to-fuel technologies do not fully utilize the energy of
sunlight because excess photon energy above the semiconductor band gap is rapidly lost as heat
through hot-carrier thermalization. Overcoming this loss mechanism is critical, as hot-carrier-
based energy conversion systems are predicted to exceed the conventional efficiency limit of
~33%. This project advanced fundamental understanding of hot-carrier energy conversion in two-
dimensional (2D) semiconductors, with a focus on monolayer MoS₂. Using a combination of
electrochemical microscopy and in situ ultrafast spectroscopic measurements, this research
directly demonstrated hot-carrier extraction from monolayer MoS₂ photoelectrodes in proof-of-
concept liquid junction solar cells. These measurements established that hot-carrier transfer can
compete with ultrafast carrier cooling at solid–liquid interfaces, providing unambiguous
experimental evidence that hot-carrier extraction is feasible in atomically thin semiconductors
under operating photoelectrochemical conditions. Beyond demonstration, the project identified
design rules for tuning hot-carrier extraction rates relative to cooling rates in 2D semiconductor
photoelectrodes. The outcomes of this research provide foundational thermodynamic and kinetic
insights for the rational design of next-generation hot-carrier-enabled solar energy conversion
systems. These findings have broad implications for photoelectrochemical solar fuels production,
electrocatalysis, and emerging energy conversion architectures that seek to harness nonequilibrium
charge carriers for enhanced efficiency.

Project Activities. The central scientific question addressed by this project was whether
thermalization losses in semiconductors can be avoided by controlling interfacial energetics and
charge transfer rates at two-dimensional (2D) semiconductor/electrolyte interfaces. The guiding
hypothesis was that 2D semiconductors are ideal platforms for hot-carrier energy conversion
because photogenerated charge carriers are created directly at the material interface. In contrast to
bulk semiconductors, where carriers must traverse micrometer-scale distances to reach an
interface, carriers in monolayer materials need only travel across the thickness of the layer—

approximately three atomic layers—before extraction. This extreme reduction in transport distance
was hypothesized to enable hot-carrier extraction to outcompete ultrafast carrier cooling, provided
that interfacial energetics and charge-transfer kinetics are appropriately controlled.

To test this hypothesis, the project developed a first-of-its-kind experimental architecture based on
monolayer MoS₂ liquid-junction solar cells, designed to enable charge transport perpendicular to
the atomic plane of the semiconductor. This geometry departs fundamentally from conventional
solid-state junctions, where charge transport typically occurs laterally within the plane of 2D
materials or over long distances in bulk absorbers. The project employed a single-nanosheet
photoelectrochemical approach that combined spatially resolved photocurrent spectroscopy on a
microscope platform with in situ ultrafast transient absorption spectroscopy under electrochemical
operating conditions. This correlative methodology allowed direct comparison of hot-carrier
cooling dynamics with interfacial charge-transfer rates while systematically varying
electrochemical driving force and interfacial energetics.

Using this approach, the project achieved the direct experimental demonstration of hot-carrier
extraction from monolayer MoS₂ into solution-phase redox acceptors. The results show that
interfacial charge transfer can compete with, and in some regimes outpace, hot-carrier cooling,
providing unambiguous validation of the central hypothesis. The findings establish a mechanistic
framework and design rules for tuning hot-carrier extraction rates relative to cooling rates in 2D
semiconductor photoelectrodes.

The solid–liquid interface was further established as a powerful and experimentally accessible
model system for quantifying hot-carrier energetics and extraction yields in two-dimensional
semiconductors. Collectively, the results demonstrate that thermalization losses are not an
unavoidable limitation in semiconductor energy conversion and can be mitigated through rational
control of interfacial structure and kinetics at atomically thin interfaces.

Over the full period of funding, this project generated ten peer-reviewed journal publications that
document the experimental platforms, analytical methods, and mechanistic insights developed
during the course of the work. These publicly accessible publications constitute the primary
scientific and technical information (STI) outputs of the project and provide detailed data,
analyses, and assumptions supporting the conclusions summarized here. The STI generated by this
research establishes a new experimental paradigm for studying nonequilibrium charge carriers and
provides foundational knowledge for future efforts in hot-carrier photovoltaics,
photoelectrochemical solar fuels production, electrocatalysis, and advanced energy-conversion
architectures based on quantum-confined materials.
