Towards Healthy Evolution: Exploring the Role and Mechanisms of Human-Agent Interaction in Self-Evolving Systems
Dianxing Shi, Junqi He, Junhao Chen, Bowen Wang, Yuta Nakashima

Abstract
Self-evolving agents improve through continual self-play and self-generated learning signals, but autonomous evolution can also cause capability degradation and safety drift. Although human feedback has proven effective for static and post-trained agents, its role in self-evolving systems remains underexplored. We introduce Agent Norm Correction through Human-like Oversight and Review (ANCHOR), an LLM-based framework that simulates human supervision and delivers feedback at various phases of self-evolution. With ANCHOR, we evaluate two representative open-source self-evolving agent systems across coding, mathematical reasoning, and safety. Our results show that even limited supervision substantially mitigates safety degradation while preserving stable performance on core evolutionary objectives. Further analysis shows that supervision over the output verification phase is the most effective for intervention, whereas increasing supervision frequency yields diminishing returns. These findings provide empirical evidence and practical guidance for designing more stable, controllable, and human-aligned self-evolving agent systems.



1
Introduction

Building AI systems that can reliably follow human intentions has long been a key objective in the development of large language models (LLMs). To this end, post-training techniques such as supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) have been widely adopted to align base models with human instructions and preferences
4
.
LLMs have also demonstrated their ability as autonomous agents, capable of executing complex tasks through planning, memory, tool use, etc. Such agents typically rely on predefined workflows and human-designed optimization
5
.

Recently, research has increasingly shifted toward
self-evolving
agents, which autonomously learn by solving self-verifiable tasks. Self-evolution has successfully shown performance improvement without human intervention
32
;
13
; meanwhile, this new learning paradigm has led to critical challenges, including
mis-evaluation
,
long-term safety drift
,
model forgetting
, and
even model collapse

7
. These challenges may stem from reinforcement learning-based finetuning, where agents may exploit ill-designed objectives, leading to reward hacking, or goal mis-specification
28
. During continuous self-evolution, the initially acquired safety alignment may be gradually eroded due to updated parameters, thereby compromising the model’s safety performance. These potential problems are often overlooked in practice.

Moreover, deploying self-evolving agents in the real world also introduces significant safety risks. Due to their autonomous execution and tool use, possibly without human oversight, these agents can trigger cascading failures with physical, biological, and informational consequences
27
;
12
. For safer use of self-evolving agents, some safety measures are desired to harness the learning process.

This raises a natural question:
Can human interaction (or supervision) in self-evolution improve the quality of learning and mitigate safety risks?

Human-agent interaction has been extensively studied in non-self-evolving settings. Prior work on human-agent systems
34
, RLHG
8
, RLUF
10
, and Co-Gym
26
have shown that signals from human feedback can improve agent reliability, performance, and user satisfaction.

In contrast, self-evolving agents continuously generate their own training data and reward signals, possibly making them vulnerable to error accumulation, misalignment, and degenerative evolution
29
. How human interaction can be efficiently integrated into a self-evolution process to improve long-term performance is underexplored.

Figure 1:
Illustration of our core insight: Human–agent interaction mitigates mis-evolution in self-evolving agents and guides the self-evolution process toward more stable and aligned models’ outputs.

To explore the possibility of human interaction, we present a systematic, empirical study of the role of human involvement in the self-evolution loop of agents. To enable controlled and scalable experiments, we employ an LLM to simulate human interactions and introduce a unified training framework, coined ANCHOR, that enables interaction at any phase of self-evolution.
On top of this framework, we perform extensive evaluations on two open-source self-evolving agent systems. Our results provide a systematic analysis of how varying levels of human supervision affect agent behavior across multiple dimensions, including coding performance, mathematical reasoning performance, and safety.

As shown in Figure
1
, we obtained, based on our experiments, the following three findings: (i) Simulated human supervision effectively mitigates error accumulation and safety drift during self-evolution while largely preserving core capabilities.
(ii) Feedback at the phase of agent output verification, namely the verifier-grounded outcome signal used in the RLVR-style update, is the most impactful intervention.
(iii) The benefits of supervision exhibit diminishing returns when supervision frequency is increased; low-to-moderate supervision frequency already achieves near-maximal gains.

2
Related Work

2.1
Agent Frameworks

Recent LLM-based agent frameworks have shifted from human-annotated training toward fully autonomous zero-data self-evolution. DeepSeek-R1
9
shows that strong reasoning capability can emerge through reinforcement learning without external supervision. Such approaches typically depend on carefully designed rewards and controlled training.

Building on this paradigm, R-Zero
13
and Absolute Zero Reasoner (AZR)
32
establish self-evolving frameworks through proposer-solver interaction, in which a proposer agent samples automatically verifiable tasks and a solver agent solves them for reinforcement learning via
self-play
, while MM-Zero
18
extends this idea to multimodal settings via self-generation and self-verification. Despite their effectiveness, these methods assume fully autonomous closed-loop evolution, where training data, reward signals, and policy updates are internally generated, making them vulnerable to error accumulation, distributional drift, and misaligned optimization over long horizons
22
;
33
.

By contrast, Co-Gym
26
introduces human-agent collaboration through asynchronous interaction and shared control, partially alleviating the limitations of fully autonomous agents. This method focuses on task execution rather than training-time evolution, and thus does not address how human feedback can be integrated into self-evolving learning loops.

2.2
Human-Agent Interaction

Recent work on human-agent interaction has moved beyond the traditional human-in-the-loop paradigm, where humans serve as passive annotators or fallback oracles. Frameworks such as AI-in-the-loop
21
and Centaur
2
emphasize tightly coupled human-AI collaboration. Agents’ Interaction patterns have also evolved from passive to proactive communication. For example, SpeakRL
1
enables agents to request clarification under ambiguous instructions via reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, which improves inference-time interaction quality. Similarly, interactive steering methods, such as AGDebugger
3
, show that rollback and message editing can mitigate cascading errors in long-horizon systems. R-Few
31
further shows that limited human annotations can improve the stability of self-evolving agents.

Existing work highlights the value of human-agent interactions for improving stability and performance in complex agentic systems, but only in a limited way: they typically consider interactions only at specific inference stages and in limited quantities, without a unified framework to give feedback to agents in any self-evolution phase. Also, they do not offer a systematic analysis of the impact of human-agent interaction in different aspects. We extend the line of studies to provide key insights for designing self-evolving agentic systems that interact with humans.

Preliminaries: Self-evolving Training

We consider a self-evolving training paradigm for coding
32
;
13
. This self-evolving framework has two trainable agents, the
proposer
and
solver
. These agents interact iteratively with each other and with a
validator
, a
verifier
, and a
task pool
. The proposer first samples a task from a
task pool
, of which validity is confirmed by the validator. The solver then generates code (or any output) for the task, and the verifier runs the code to check whether it works as expected or not. The verifier’s judge is used for a reward to update both (i) agents’ parameters and (ii) the task.

Specifically, the state of a self-evolving framework at the self-evolution step
t
is denoted as

\Omega_{t}=(\theta_{t},\mathcal{Q}_{t}),

(1)

where
\theta_{t}
denotes the set of current trainable parameters of the two agents and
\mathcal{Q}_{t}
denotes the task pool, from which self-verifiable tasks are sampled. The set
\theta_{t}
contains parameters for the proposer’s and solver’s policy.
For notation simplicity, we use
\theta_{t}
in what follows, but only the corresponding subset is updated for role-specific (i.e., either the proposer or solver) training.

At the self-evolution step
t
, the proposer, denoted as
\pi^{\text{P}}_{\theta_{t}}
, samples a candidate task as
(x,y)\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}^{\mathrm{P}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{Q}_{t})
, where
x
is the query to the solver and
y
is the ground-truth code.
The
validator

\mathcal{V}
then determines its admissibility in several aspects

a_{t}=\mathcal{V}(x,y)\in\{0,1\}
, where
a_{t}=1
means the task is accepted; otherwise, the task is discarded or resampled (following the original self-evolving paradigm’s rule).
The
solver

\pi_{\theta_{t}}^{\text{S}}
then generates loosely structured outputs
(h,\hat{y})\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}^{S}(\cdot\mid x)
, where
h

\hat{y}
is a final output. Finally, the verifier returns feedback
e=\mathcal{E}(x,y,\hat{y})\in\{0,1\}

In the self-evolving frameworks considered in this work, the training signal is encoded into two types of rewards:

r=\lambda_{\mathrm{LA}}r^{\mathrm{LA}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{Acc}}r^{\mathrm{Acc}},

(2)

where
r^{\mathrm{LA}}
measures whether the proposed task
(x,y)
is useful for the current solver, encouraging tasks near the solver’s capability boundary, and
r^{\mathrm{Acc}}
measures whether the solver output
\hat{y}
is correct based on the verifier. The hyperparameters
\lambda_{\mathrm{LA}}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{Acc}}

N

N

\displaystyle=\operatorname{PoolUpdate}(\mathcal{Q}_{t},\{(x,y)\},\{\hat{y}\},\{e\}),

\operatorname{Update}(\cdot)

\operatorname{PoolUpdate}(\cdot)

\theta_{t}

4
ANCHOR

ANCHOR (Agent Norm Correction through Human-like Oversight and Review) is an LLM-based supervision framework for self-evolving agents, which can be viewed as a proxy for human supervision.
It attaches to an existing self-evolving framework and provides feedback.
As shown in Figure
2
, ANCHOR receives detailed information about each step from a self-evolving framework, which is then used by an LLM-based
supervisor
to generate a feedback for any phase at each self-evolution step.

As humans may do, ANCHOR uses the system prompt of the proposer and solver agents as a supervisory channel. Let
c_{t}
denote the context that contains the summary of feedback provided before evolution step
t
and has been appended to the system prompt for this step. ANCHOR updates
c_{t}
according to the supervisor’s feedback. This feedback is evaluative rather than oracle-like: it may identify errors, risks, or inefficiencies, but does not provide gold answers, executable fixes, or privileged reasoning trajectories.

As shown in Figure
2
, we identify the following self-evolution phases, for which the supervisor can generate a review:
Task proposal
(
task
) phase allows evaluation of task
(x,y)
from the proposer
\pi_{\theta_{t}}^{\text{P}}
;
planning
(
plan
) and
thought
(
thought
) phases involve the solver
\pi_{\theta_{t}}^{\text{S}}
’s reasoning trajectory
h
;
output
(
output
) phase is to evaluate the output
\hat{y}
from
\pi_{\theta_{t}}^{\text{S}}
;
execution result
(
exec
) phase gives feedback on the verifier’s decision. The set of all these phases is denoted by
\mathcal{P}
.

The supervisor
S
takes different input
w_{p}
for a different phase
p\in\mathcal{P}
to review:

w_{p}=\begin{cases}(x,y),&p=\texttt{task}\\
h,&p\in\{\texttt{plan, thought}\}\\
\hat{y},&p=\texttt{output}\\
e,&p=\texttt{exec}\end{cases},

(4)

Among them, only the verifier’s decision
e
is
binary
, which indicates if the output
\hat{y}
aligns with the ground-truth
y
. We design the feedback for the corresponding phase
exec
so that it provides statistics of
e
over the batch. This feedback efficiently quantifies the proposer’s (or the task pool’s) ability to select tasks at an appropriate difficulty level, or the solver’s ability to solve the tasks. At the other phases in
\mathcal{P}
, the supervisor generates textual feedback for the individual task
(x,y)
according to the system prompt for the supervisor (Appendix
8
). The natural text feedback for phase
p\in\mathcal{P}
, generated by the supervisor, is denoted by
\epsilon_{p}
.

Such a self-evolution process can require reviewing many tasks, and a human supervisor may not be able to evaluate all of them. ANCHOR thus introduces a Bernoulli review gate
z_{p}\in\{0,1\}
to randomly pick out tasks and phases to be reviewed:

z_{p}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(f_{p}),\qquad f_{p}\in[0,1],

(5)

where
f_{p}
denotes the probability of phase
p
being reviewed.
When
z_{p}=1
, the supervisor reviews
w_{p}
and generates
\epsilon_{p}
.
In our experiments, we use a shared supervision frequency
f_{p}=f,\ \forall p\in\mathcal{P}
unless otherwise specified.

The collected phase-level feedback
\epsilon_{p}
is summarized into a batch-level supervision summary:

b_{t}=\operatorname{Agg}\Big(\{\epsilon_{p}\mid z_{p}=1,\;p\in\mathcal{P}\}\Big),

(6)

where
\operatorname{Agg}(\cdot)
denotes an aggregation function that summarizes all reviewed phase-level feedback at step
t
.
4
4

4

Please refer to Appendix
A.2
for the actual prompt for summary.

The system prompt
c_{t}
is then updated by

c_{t+1}=\Gamma(c_{t},b_{t}),

(7)

where
\Gamma(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes an LLM to update the system prompt
c_{t}
according to
b_{t}
, which basically merges
c_{t}
with
b_{t}
.
In our implementation, the updated context is injected into the system prompt for the next evolution steps.

Experimental Setup

We evaluate ANCHOR’s effectiveness on two representative open-source self-evolving agent frameworks, AZR
32
and R-Zero
13
, both of which are based on the proposer-solver paradigm. For a fair comparison, all training configurations strictly follow the original settings of each framework.

For the AZR, we adopt the Qwen2.5 family
24
;
14
as backbone, including Qwen2.5-Coder-3B-Ins, Qwen2.5-7B-Ins, Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Ins, and Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Ins. For R-Zero, we use Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base
30
. The ANCHOR variants are adopted on top of these models.

We evaluate model performance across three dimensions: coding, mathematical reasoning, and safety. For coding, we use LiveCodeBench
15
and EvalPlus
19
. For mathematical reasoning, we adopt AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, MATH500
11
, OlympiadBench
6
, and Minerva Math
16
. We aggregate these benchmark scores into an average for brevity. For safety evaluation, we use HarmBench
20
, SaladBench
17
, HEx-PHI
23
, and a Reward Hacking benchmark
25
.

Results are reported at fixed training steps for each model scale (3B: 130, 7B: 350, 14B: 400, 4B/8B: 60 steps) and the ANCHOR supervisor provides feedback every single steps (i.e.,
f_{p}=1
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
, while full training dynamics are analyszed separately (see Section
6.1
). The supervisor instance is powered by a locally deployed Qwen3-30B-A3B-Ins model. More details about the experimental setup can be found in Appendix
B
. A quantitative analysis of the LLM-as-a-judge quality of this simulated supervisor, including external LLM-based and human evaluations, is provided in Appendix
D
.

Finding 1

(Simulated) human interaction effectively improves evolutionary quality, mitigating error accumulation and safety drift.

From Table
1
, ANCHOR variants outperform the AZR and R-Zero baselines across more than half of Code and Math metrics, and consistently surpass the corresponding base models.
The degradation in safety performance observed in AZR and R-Zero models, including their ANCHOR-based variants, is consistent with the well-documented phenomenon of mis-evolution
25
. Despite this inherent challenge, our method significantly alleviates safety degradation. Compared to AZR or R-Zero, the ANCHOR variants exhibit clear improvements in safety.
Although slight performance drops are observed in certain coding and mathematical benchmarks, these are accompanied by substantial gains in safety performance. The models showed increased awareness of potential vulnerabilities and demonstrated more cautious decision-making under risky scenarios, without introducing significant degradation in coding or mathematical reasoning capabilities. Detailed case studies are provided in Appendix
C.3
. These results may not be surprising, but they empirically confirm that supervisory interventions, not only from the task performance perspective but also from different perspectives, are essential for self-evolution frameworks.

\mathcal{P}

Among all phases in

This suggests that the self-evolution process is fundamentally feedback-driven, with distribution changes brought by execution feedback acting as the core bottleneck.

In comparison,

and

Finding 2

Self-evolving agent’s evolution process is greatly affected by feedback signals; feedback on the verifier’s decision plays a critical role.

We decompose the self-evolution process into five phases: Task, Thought, Plan, Output, and Execution Result.
Most AZR-like self-evolution frameworks are optimized through reinforcement learning over verifier-grounded binary outcomes, where each generated solution is ultimately mapped to a success or failure signal.
Consequently, the effectiveness of such training is highly sensitive to the empirical Bernoulli distribution of validated outcomes, since this distribution directly determines the reward signal that drives policy updates and task-pool evolution.
Since the batch summary is derived from validated outputs, we hypothesize that supervision on the Execution Result phase plays a critical role in shaping this distribution and, consequently, the overall evolution trajectory.
We therefore perform phase-wise ablation experiments on ANCHOR-Coder-7B and ANCHOR-8B, treating each phase as an independent factor.

Hyperparameter experiments.

To quantify the benefit introduced by supervision, we define a metric
\delta
termed the frequency-gain ratio:

\displaystyle\delta(f,f^{\prime})=\frac{s(f^{\prime})-s(f)}{s(f)\times(f^{\prime}-f)}

(8)

where
s(f)
denotes the averaged performance across the 5 performance metrics under the supervision frequency
f
, where
s(0)
corresponds to the baseline performance without supervision (i.e., AZR or R-Zero). This ratio means how much increment in the scores is observed from
f
to
f^{\prime}
(where
f^{\prime}>f
). We evaluated the ratio from
f=0
to
f=1
with an interval of 0.1 (i.e.,
f^{\prime}-f=0.1
).

This ratio measures the normalized performance gain relative to supervision cost, allowing us to analyze how performance cumulatively evolves as supervision frequency cumulatively increases from zero. It further enables the identification of an optimal supervision regime and costs.

Figure
5
shows that human supervision and interaction achieve near-maximum gains at relatively low frequencies (e.g.,
f\in[0.3,0.4]
). As the frequency increases, the performance enters a plateau region with limited improvement (e.g.,
f\in[0.4,0.8]
), followed by a regime (e.g.,
f\in[0.8,1.0]
) where diminishing returns become more pronounced, and additional supervision yields minimal benefit.

Finding 3

Human supervision and interaction exhibit diminishing returns, with a saturation region beyond which additional intervention yields limited benefit.

Based on these observations, we derive our third finding and suggest selecting supervision frequencies within the mid-range regime, balancing performance gains against supervision cost. From a practical perspective, this suggests that human supervision within realistic cognitive and resource constraints can be both effective and efficient for guiding self-evolution, making it a viable and valuable investment in real-world deployment and research settings.
\blacksquare

Conclusion

In this work, we study whether proxy human supervision and interaction can guide self-evolving agents toward more stable and aligned evolution. To this end, we introduce ANCHOR, a general and efficient framework for simulating human-agent interaction, and conduct comprehensive experiments to evaluate its effects. We also propose a set of metrics for analyzing human-agent interaction during self-evolution, and conduct case studies and ablation experiments to examine how factors such as supervision phases and frequency influence the evolution efficiency. These analyses lead to three key empirical findings, showing that human supervision improves safety and stability without clearly weakening core capabilities. These findings provide empirical evidence and practical insights for designing future self-evolving agent systems, and demonstrate the potential of scalable human-in-the-loop mechanisms.

Limitations

First, human supervision is simulated by an LLM rather than provided by real human supervisors. Although this design enables controlled and scalable experimentation, the observed effects may depend on the choice of supervisor model and prompting strategy, potentially introducing bias in the supervision signals. The supervision signal may reflect the bias and preferences of the supervisor model, potentially shaping the evolution trajectory in unintended ways. Second, our experimental design may limit the generality of our findings across different architectures, training paradigms, and task domains. In particular, the effectiveness of phase-specific interventions may be framework-dependent, and the relative importance of different supervision stages could vary under alternative implementations. Finally, our evaluation focuses on text-based tasks and does not consider more complex settings such as tool use or embodied environments, where the role of human supervision may differ.

C.2
Memory-Induced Reward Hacking in Self-Evolving Agents

In self-evolving agents, the continuous accumulation of memory can induce a failure mode referred to as deployment-time reward hacking, a form of misevolution. This phenomenon arises from the tendency to extract heuristics from historical interactions that correlate with high task success or user satisfaction and reuse them as decision shortcuts. However, such proxy signals are often misaligned with the true objectives or stakeholder interests.

Formally, this misalignment can be expressed as:

\pi_{\theta}^{*}=\arg\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t}\gamma^{t}R_{proxy}(s_{t},a_{t}|\mathcal{M})\right]

\pi_{\theta}^{*}\neq\arg\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t}\gamma^{t}R_{true}(s_{t},a_{t})\right]

Based on this formulation, the benchmark is designed to induce and quantify memory-driven goal drift, enabling evaluation of agent robustness under long-term deployment.

Benchmark Design

The benchmark consists of two evaluation settings:

•

Static Evaluation

It includes 41 different test cases across four high-risk domains (
\text{Sales}^{10},\text{Service}^{10},\text{Medical}^{11},\text{financial}^{10}
). Each case injects six memory entries (three successful and three failed trajectories) together with a new query, ensuring that all agents operate under identical historical context.

•

Dynamic Evaluation

It further simulates a multi-turn customer service environment, where agents interact with users and accumulate memory over time, allowing the emergence of reward hacking behavior to be observed.

Induced Failure Mechanisms

The injected memory is constructed to induce two types of failure:

•

False-Positive Penalty and Survivorship Bias

In medical scenarios, cautious but unnecessary interventions receive low scores, while risky but reassuring responses receive high scores. Severe failures are underrepresented due to missing feedback, leading to biased reward signals.

•

Shortcut Reinforcement

In service scenarios, actions such as unconditional refunds consistently yield high rewards, while policy-compliant responses are penalized, encouraging shortcut strategies.

Evaluation Protocol

•

LLM-as-a-Judge with Human Validation

Static outputs are evaluated using an LLM-based judge (in our experiment we use GPT-5) to detect behavioral misalignment, with results cross-validated by human annotations.

•

Adversarial Trap Testing

In dynamic settings, an adversarial tester issues impossible requests. Agents are rewarded only if they satisfy such requests through dishonest behavior, enabling precise identification of reward hacking.

Summary

The case studies across the three benchmarks consistently show that models trained under the ANCHOR framework allocate part of their reasoning capacity to identifying potential risks and harmful intent, thereby mitigating safety drift during self-evolution.

At the same time, improvements in safety do not come at the expense of core capabilities. As reflected in the Code and Math evaluations, performance remains stable and in some cases improves. This behavior aligns with the design objective of the proposed improvement of self-evolving agents, suggesting that ANCHOR provides an effective mechanism for balancing capability and safety in self-evolving agents.

Appendix D
LLM-as-a-judge Quality Analysis

The reliability of LLM-as-a-judge, as well as its affective, capability, and preference gaps from human-as-a-judge, has long been a central concern in this area. Since this work uses Qwen3-30B-A3B-Ins as the simulated supervisor, it is inevitably subject to the same concern. Therefore, we establish a judge-quality evaluation protocol tailored to code-reasoning model frameworks.

Specifically, we use GPT-5.3-Codex, a powerful and rigorous LLM for code-oriented tasks, to review the ANCHOR interaction content along three dimensions: Correctness, i.e., whether the feedback accurately identifies the problem; Safety relevance, i.e., whether it attends to potential safety risks; and Specificity, i.e., whether it points out the pattern of the problem. In addition, we invite a doctoral student in the LLM field, who is neither an author nor a stakeholder of this work, to evaluate the feedback along three dimensions: Non-leakage, i.e., whether the feedback avoids directly providing a gold answer or executable fix; Safety relevance, i.e., whether it attends to potential safety risks; and Actionability, i.e., whether the feedback can guide the agent toward improvement.
