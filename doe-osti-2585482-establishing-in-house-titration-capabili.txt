ABSTRACT
This lessons learned document provides a detailed analysis of the hydroxy termination titration
methods employed for determining hydroxyl group content in various chemical samples. The
primary focus is on the evaluation of techniques such as ASTM E222 and their practical applications
in laboratory settings. Through examination of method selection, sample preparation, and titration
execution, key insights have been identified that enhance the accuracy and reliability of results.
Notable findings include the advantages of using pressure bottle methods for improved heating
uniformity and reduced solvent loss, as well as the challenges associated with endpoint detection in
dark-colored solutions. Recommendations for alternative sampling and titration techniques are
proposed to address these challenges. This document serves as a valuable resource for enhancing the
effectiveness and efficiency of hydroxy termination titration methods in chemical analysis.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 This document provides a comprehensive overview of the ASTM E222 hydroxy termination
titration methods, along with lessons learned from their implementation and execution. The primary
objective of this report is to enhance the accuracy, efficiency, and reliability of hydroxy termination
titration processes for those looking to establish future titration capabilities.
 Key findings from the lessons learned highlight the importance of method selection based
on sample characteristics, such as the presence of primary or secondary alcohols. The use of
pressure bottle methods has been identified as best practice, as it enables more accurate results
through homogeneous heating and reduced solvent loss. Additionally, the significance of proper
sample preparation – including the use of compatible materials and reagents, as well as effective
dispensing and weighing techniques – has been emphasized to prevent contamination and ensure
consistent results.
 The insights gained from this document will serve as a valuable resource for laboratory
personnel and stakeholders involved in hydroxy termination titration methods. By implementing the
recommendations outlined herein, organizations can improve data quality and achieve more reliable
assessments of hydroxyl group content in various chemical samples.

1. INTRODUCTION AND OBJECTIVES
 The goal of this report is to share lessons learned through developing in-house titration
capabilities, specifically the titration of hydroxyl groups using acetic anhydride acetylation chemistry
as outlined in ASTM standard method E222. While the methods and details provided in this ASTM
standard serve as a foundation for executing these titrations, this report aims to highlight practical
considerations such as sampling, method selection, and method variability that are essential for
accurate measurement of hydroxyl content.

1.1. ASTM Standard Method E222
 The procedures outlined in ASTM E222 were developed to analyze aliphatic, alicyclic and
phenolic compounds for the quantification of their hydroxyl groups. This document describes three
standard methods designed to measure hydroxyl groups attached to primary and secondary carbons.
Test methods A and C are typically used for a wide range of hydroxyl content (250-1600) due to
their broad precision, while method B is specifically precise for lower hydroxyl contents.

1.1.1. Test Method A (Pressure Bottle Method)
 The ASTM E222 pressure bottle method is recommended for general use in determining
hydroxyl groups in a sample through three key steps. First, a sample is acetylated using an acetic
anhydride solution in pyridine and heated in pressure bottles. Next, the excess acetic anhydride
reagent undergoes hydrolysis with water, forming acetic acid. The final step involves titrating the
acetic acid with a standardized sodium hydroxide solution to determine the hydroxyl content, which
is calculated from the difference between the blank and sample solutions.

1.1.2. Test Method B (Reflux Method)
 The reflux method is the most widely used ASTM E222 technique. The results obtained
using Method A and B should essentially be the same, as the key steps are similar. However, Method
B is precise for lower hydroxyl content levels, while it may struggle with higher hydroxyl content
samples. In this method, the sample is acetylated with an acetic anhydride in pyridine solution under
reflux temperatures, rather than in pressure bottles as required in Method A. The subsequent steps
follow those of the Pressure Bottle Method.

1.1.3. Test Method C (Perchloric Acid Catalyzed Method)
 The final method, Perchloric Acid Catalyzed titration, is recommended only when rapid
turnaround or ambient temperature analysis is required. This test method is known to produce
artificially high values (up to 4% relative to the hydroxy content) compared to Test Methods A and
B. However, the Perchloric Acid Catalyzed Method is advantageous for sterically hindered
secondary alcohols, which may react too slowly for Test Methods A and B to yield accurate results.
 There are three key steps in Test Method C. First, the sample is acetylated at room
temperature in a solution of 1,2-dichloroethane with acetic anhydride and perchloric acid. Next, the
excess reagent is allowed to hydrolyze with an aqueous dimethylformamide-pyridine solution.
Finally, the titration of the acetic acid is completed with a standardized methanolic sodium
hydroxide solution to determine hydroxyl content.

1.1.4. Interferences
 All ASTM E222 methods share same interferences. Certain compounds cannot be tested
using Test Method C due to interfering reactions with the strong acid catalyst, either with the
compound being analyzed or the acetate product. However, Test Method C can be advantageous for
titration of sterically hindered alcohols.

1.2. Sandia Lessons Learned

1.2.1.      Sampling
 General sampling recommendations: When preparing for titrations, it is crucial to follow
 general recommendations for sampling to ensure accurate and reliable results. These include:
 1. Select a representative sample that reflects the overall composition of the solution
 being analyzed, taking care to avoid contamination or degradation during collection.
 2. Use clean, dry glassware or appropriate sampling containers to prevent interference
 from residual substances.
 1. If the solution is heterogeneous, thoroughly mix it before sampling to achieve
 homogeneity. It is advisable to take samples from multiple locations within the
 container to capture any stratification or variation in composition.
 3. Measure the sample volume precisely, as titration results can be sensitive to the
 amount of analyte used.
 4. Consider the stability of the sample; if it is prone to changes in concentration due to
 evaporation or reaction with air, perform the titration promptly after sampling.
 5. Document all relevant information, including the sample source, date, time, and any
 specific conditions, to maintain traceability and reproducibility in the titration
 process.
 Following these recommendations will help achieve consistent and accurate titration results.

 Recommendations for selecting sample size: When conducting titrations, selecting an
 appropriate sample size is critical for achieving accurate and reliable results. The following
 should be considered when choosing a sample size:
 1. Consider the expected concentration of the analyte; for highly concentrated
 solutions, a smaller sample size may suffice, while dilute solutions may require larger
 volumes to achieve a detectable change at the endpoint.
 2. Ensure the sample size is large enough to allow the titration to be performed within
 a measurable range, facilitating precise determination of the endpoint.
 3. The sample size should also be compatible with the concentration of the titrant and
 the type of indicator used, ensuring that the endpoint can be clearly observed.
 Careful consideration of sample size not only enhances the accuracy of the titration results
 but also minimizes potential errors associated with dilution or concentration effects,
 ultimately leading to more reliable analytical outcomes. Therefore, it is essential to use the
 following equation to determine the sample size for this titration, regardless of the method
 chosen.

Practical sampling recommendations: Frequent samples of interest for the ASTM-E222
method are hydroxy-terminated polymers, crosslinking agents, adhesives, and binders. Many
of these samples are viscous and can be challenging to dispense accurately for individual
titrations. When dispensing thick liquid samples for individual titration analyses, the
following method is recommended. This technique has been found to be extremely accurate
and straightforward in establishing titration capabilities at Sandia.
 1. Select a syringe (glass or plastic) that is large enough for the sample size required for
 the specific titration. Choose the largest bore needle necessary to facilitate easy
 drawing the sample into the syringe.
 2. After selection of equipment, draw an excess amount of sample into the syringe.
 Note: Following this initial draw, pull up a small amount of air into the needle tip and
 cap it to avoid dripping from the end of the needle.
 3. Weigh and document the weight of the syringe and sample.
 4. Dispense the required mass for an individual titration into the appropriate bottle or
 flask.
 5. Weigh and document the weight of the syringe and the remaining sample.
 6. Subtract the final weight (Step 5) from the initial weight (Step 3) to determine the
 weight of the sample dispensed.

1.2.3. Method Selection
 While Method B (reflux) is the most used ASTM E222 method, it is highly recommended to
utilize Method A (pressure bottle) whenever possible. Although the results from Method A and
Method B should be comparable due to their similar key steps, experience has shown that pressure
bottles yield more accurate results and are now the preferred method according to ASTM standards.
This increased accuracy is attributed to more homogenous heating of the sample and across
replicates when using pressure bottles, as well as the prevention of solvent and analyte loss.
 Method C (perchloric acid catalyzed) is advised only when sterically hindered secondary
alcohols are expected to react too slowly for accurate results using Test Methods A and B. It is
important to note that this technique has not yet been required for Sandia-relevant samples during
the establishment of this capability. Consequently, “lessons learned” for this method are not
available, although the sampling recommendations remain applicable.

1.2.4. Detailed Procedures and Method Specific Recommendations
 Given the high-consequence production materials encountered at Sandia and other NNSA
laboratories, accuracy and precision are of utmost priority in the execution of this method.
Therefore, we provide detailed procedural examples that incorporate method-specific
recommendations, highlighted in italics at the appropriate points. Please note that these procedural
notes should be used in conjunction with the ASTM E222 method details described previously.

 b. Standard o-rings degrade when in contact with both pyridine and acetic anhydride; therefore,
 use CAPFE o-rings or other fluorinated polymer o-rings that are compatible with this solvent
 system.
 4. Weigh the sample into each individual sample bottle.
 5. Add 20 mL of the pyridine/acetic anhydride mixture to each bottle and seal them.
 a. A 50 mL burette is suitable for this purpose. Fill a single burette and dose both the sample
 and its corresponding blank with that single burette fill, as closely together in time as possible.
 6. Place the bottles in a water bath maintained as close to 100 °C as possible.
 a. Alternate the placement of sample bottles with blank bottles, ensuring that each blank is
 positioned as close to its corresponding sample as possible.
 7. Leave bottle in the water bath for 4 hours if secondary alcohols are present.
 a. If only primary alcohols are present in the sample, 2 hours may be sufficient.
 8. Remove bottles and allow them to cool to room temperature.
 9. Crush DI ice for next step.
 10. Perform the following steps on both the samples and their corresponding blanks:
 a. Open the bottle, rinse the threading with DI water, and plug the bottle.
 b. Add a Teflon magnetic stir bar.
 c. Rinse the sides of the bottle with 20 mL of DI water.
 d. Fill the bottle to the halfway point with crushed DI ice.
 e. Add approximately 1 mL of phenolphthalein indicator solution.
 i. Prepare this solution by dissolving 1 g of phenolphthalein in 100 mL of pyridine
 f. Titrate while stirring with 1 N NaOH from a 50 mL burette to a faint pink endpoint.
 g. The endpoint can be difficult to detect in samples that have a dark-colored solution after
 water bath reaction.
 i. Use 2 mL of indicator for both the sample and the blank.
 ii. After titrating the sample, titrate its corresponding blank to match the shade of
 pink present in the coagulated/settled sample solution.
 iii. The color change is more perceptible with practice, and this correction should not be
 necessary with practice.
 h. ASTM method uses 0.5 N with a 100 mL burette.
 i. For dark samples, substitute Nile blue dye powder for phenolphthalein; the transition will
 appear greenish-blue to reddish-brown at the endpoint.
 11. The volume of titrant needed for the sample must be greater than 65% of that used for the
 blank to achieve accurate results; ideally, this should be over 80%. If it is below 65%, the reaction
 will not be driven to completion and the entire procedure must be repeated with a smaller sample
 size.
 a. For example, if the blank takes 50 mL of 1 N NaOH to titrate, the sample should
 ideally take ≥40 mL to titrate, and no less than 32.5 mL titrant.

2. Weigh out sample into each individual sample flasks.
3. Set aside one flask with a stopper for the blank.
4. Prepare a pyridine and acetic anhydride mixture using a 100 mL graduated cylinder,

5. Dose the samples and blanks with 10 mL of the reagent using an Eppendorf pipette

6. Fit the samples with an air reflux condenser and heat on a hotplate, maintaining a
 temperature of 115 °C for 45 minutes, with the timer starting at the first sign of condensation
 at the top of the flask.
 a. The blank should be left unheated.
7. While heating, prepare an ice bath large enough to hold all blanks, samples, and
 sufficient deionized water for subsequent steps.
8. Fill a graduated cylinder (with a pour spout) with 25 mL of pyridine and rinse each
 sample flask with pyridine.
 a. Use approximately half of this pyridine to wash down the condenser and the other half to
 wash the sides of the flask.
9. After rinsing, stopper the flasks with their original stoppers.
10. Chill the blanks, samples, and deionized water (for the subsequent steps) for 1 hour.
11. Dissolve 1 gram of phenolphthalein in 100 mL of pyridine.
