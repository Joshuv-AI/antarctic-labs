 ABSTRACT
 The Gear Test Assembly (GTA) is an experimental test apparatus built and installed in the
Mechanisms and Engineering Test Loop (METL) at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL). While designed
to accommodate a variety of components intended for use in advanced compact in-vessel transfer machine,
the focus of GTA been the testing of large radial spur gears. The performance of the components used in
GTA motivates the choices made in the design, components, and material choices for the forthcoming
METL test articles; the Gripper Test Assembly (GrTA), and the Bearing Test Assembly (BTA). To date,
GTA has completed nine experimental testing campaigns and achieved over 21 million revolutions under
prototypic sodium fast reactor loads and operating conditions. This report provides an overview and results
for the two most recent experiments Campaign #8 and Campaign #9.
 `Campaign #8 was the first campaign to make use of a set of nickel alloy radial spur gears, made from
Hastelloy C-22HS, and combined with heat-treated tapered roller bearings. The bearings and torque
profile used allowed Campaign #8 to be directly compared with Campaign #1 where Inconel 718 gears
were used instead. Campaign #8 attained 1,058,880 shaft revolutions within 23% of the 1,314,855
revolutions achieved in Campaign #1. These results demonstrate that the heat-treated tapered roller
bearings show consistent increased lifetimes, approximately three times longer than for non-heat-treated
taper rollers, and that the particular material of the gear does not play a substantial role in performance.
Similar to Campaign #7 where bearing failure resulted in fragmentation and transport of bearing material
into the gear teeth led to tooth damage in the Inconel gears, a similar process led to the damage of the
Hastelloy gears. Subsequent NDE analysis showed that the damage was only limited to the tooth surface
and does not extend into the gear interiors. Based on the NDE reports, the Hastelloy gears can be
reconditioned and put back into service for future campaigns as was done for the Inconel gears.
 Reconditioned Inconel 718 gears damaged in Campaign #7 were returned to service in Campaign #9
and paired with non-heat-treated tapered roller bearings. In Campaign #9 a novel set of operating
conditions were implemented whereby the gears and bearings were cycled between hot, submerged
refueling conditions where fuel handling operations occurred and cold, dry, and inert conditions where
the sodium was drained and the vessel was allowed to cool simulating long, ex-vessel storage of the fuel
handling machine. A total of eight of these operating cycles were achieved before a bearing failure
occurred after the accumulation of 475,692 shaft revolutions were achieved. During Campaign #9, one of
the drive motors was damaged and over the course of a 240 day standby period, the entire GTA drive
system was upgraded from a 480VAC system to a 240VAC system that provided the opportunity to
consolidate wall-space in the METL facility and provide more space for additional test articles being
installed in the coming year. The over 475,000 shaft revolutions achieved in Campaign #9 eclipses the
previous record for lifetime of non-heat-treated tapered roller bearings held by Campaign #3 of 392,000
shaft revolutions. Further analysis and future campaigns hope to shed light on the cause of this
approximately 17% increase in lifetime.

1 Introduction
 The Gear Test Assembly (GTA) is an experimental test article built for use in the Mechanisms
Engineering Test Loop (METL) at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL). GTA was the first test article to
operate in METL, with the initial experimental campaign beginning in early 2019. A total of nine
experimental campaigns have been completed to-date with the seven previous campaigns discussed in the
referenced reports [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], and [6].
 There are two sections of new results provided in this report. The first part provides a summary of
experimental operations and the results of Campaign #8 along with bearing and gear analysis in the form
of detailed photographs and non-destructive evaluation (NDE) analysis using eddy current sensing and
ultrasonic testing. The second part consists of a summary of the experimental operations and the results
from Campaign #9.

1.1 Motivation for Gear Test Assembly
 The purpose of the GTA is to evaluate the performance of mechanical components intended for use in
an ANL designed advanced compact in-vessel transfer machine (IVTM). The ANL IVTM, Figure 1.1,
will be used in pool-type sodium fast reactors (SFR) and is designed such that all fuel assemblies can be
reached using a single rotatable plug. This is compared with the more traditional use of multiple eccentric
rotatable plugs and a straight pull IVTM that allow for a single point on a single plug to cover the whole
of the reactor core. The ANL IVTM design allows for a reduction in the diameter of the reactor vessel
which translates into a smaller containment facility overall, reducing the overall capital costs of the reactor
plant. To accomplish this, the design for the IVTM requires that the mechanically driven components to
be fully submerged in the primary coolant. These components include radial spur gears, mechanical
bearings, ball screws, bushings, and universal joints. At the start of this project, there was a lack of
sufficient literature regarding the design and performance of these mechanical components when
submerged in liquid sodium and operating under the anticipated loads of the IVTM. Therefore, it is crucial
to fill the gap in our knowledge that informs the development of the full-scale GrTA and before proceeding
with a complete fabrication and testing of the full IVTM.

 The Gear Test Assembly (GTA) represents the first device designed and fabricated for testing the
major components needed in order to realize the complete IVTM. GTA is currently equipped to test radial

2.5”. GTA was robustly designed so that it could be modified to accommodate a variety of gears, e.g.,
helical spur, worm, straight, and/or spiral bevel, as well as a variety of bearing designs including ball,
tapered roller, needle, thrust, hydrostatic, and/or hydrodynamic variants.

 The performance of the gears and bearings analyzed over the nine campaigns of GTA has informed
on the design of the Gripper Test Assembly (GrTA) and the Bearing Test Article (BTA). The GrTA is an
experimental assembly currently under construction that will evaluate the performance of a full-scale fuel
assembly gripper head that also operates and is driven by mechanical components submerged in sodium
[7], [8]. The BTA is designed, and is currently being fabricated, in order to provide a dedicated testing
environment for the bearings that have been found to be the primary source of failure in GTA.

1.2 Gear Test Assembly Overview
 GTA, shown in Figure 1.2 and Figure 1.3 was designed to operate in the smaller, 18” nominal
diameter, of the two vessels used in the METL facility. Thus, the foundation for the design of the GTA is
a blank 18” Class 300 ANSI flange. Two drive shafts penetrate the flange to provide the prescribed torque
to the tested gears and bearings. An internal support structure is used to position the test gearboxes in the
liquid sodium pool, below the argon cover-gas and sodium vapor space. Two test gearboxes contain two
sets of radial spur gears customized for this experiment. One large pair of gears has 15-teeth, and a second
smaller pair has 22-teeth. To date, these gears have been supported by tapered roller and ball bearings
while the shafts themselves are supported axially by cylindrical pin thrust bearings. Essentially all
submerged components are manufactured from 316 stainless steel to ensure known material compatibility
with the liquid sodium test environment. The 316 stainless-steel also provides sufficient strength for the
load bearing components to operate in this environment at elevated temperatures. The only submerged
components not made from 316 stainless steel are the Inconel 718 gears or Hastelloy C-22HS gears, steel
bearings under investigation, and Inconel 718 shaft sleeves between the gears/bearings and drive Shaft A
and Shaft B.

2 Summary of Previous Campaigns
GTA has completed nine experimental testing campaigns with the two most recent campaigns being the
subject of this report. Each campaign follows a similar set of operational phases. In Phase 1, the specific
combination of gears and bearings is chosen and GTA is fully assembled on a custom-made assembly
positioner that allows GTA to be rotated to make assembly easier. The four spur gears used in the previous
campaigns are constructed from Inconel 718 (ASM 5664).
The more varied test components have been the bearings which have been the primary source of failure
in all previous campaigns. A summary of the bearings used is provided in Table 2.1. Two types of bearings
have been used in testing. The first are commercially available tapered roller bearings made from 52100
bearing steel by Timken. The off-the-shelf versions of these bearings without additional heat-treatment
have been used in Campaigns #2, #3, #5, and #7 and are the most economical (lowest cost) and readily
available (shortest lead-time). A heat-treated version of these bearings that pushes its stability limits from
150°C to 350°C and has been used in Campaigns #1 and #4 but come at higher cost and a lead time of 14
weeks (3.5 months). The other type of bearing used was a custom set of ball bearings fabricated from heat-
treated M50 tool steel. These bearings have been used in Campaign #6 and were the most expensive and
had the longest lead time, 32-36 weeks (8-8.5 months) of all variants tested.

In the next phase, the fully assembled GTA is installed in Vessel #1, one of the 18” stainless steel test
vessels that comprise the METL facility. The vessel and test article are heated to 250°C and the vessel is
filled with nuclear grade sodium with an oxide content below 5-wppm. The METL facility is equipped
with a cold trap that operates between 150°C – 170°C and is run periodically over the course of the
experimental campaign to maintain the sodium’s purity. The dates during which the major phases of each
campaign took place are given in Table 2.2.
In the experimental testing phase, a cyclic loading is provided to the gears and bearings with one of the
motors acting as the driver, while the other motor acts to provide a resistive loading force that mimics the
maneuvers necessary for handling core assemblies over the lifetime of a reactor’s operation. The
experimental phase can be further broken down into more granular detail as summarized in Table 2.3.
The periods specified are:

Fortunately for most campaigns the primary period is that of Normal operation. The exceptions are
Campaign #2 where GTA was placed in a 69-day Standby due to the Covid-19 pandemic and Campaign
#4 where repairs to the METL facility and the Laboratory’s closure over winter holidays caused GTA to
be placed in a 38-day Standby. For the longest-lived experiment, Campaign #6, GTA operations were
ceased for METL maintenance only two times where no operations were performed in a 24-hour window.
GTA has only ceased operations to make repairs to the test article for two days in Campaign #1 where an
upper gear block bearing was replaced and for two days in Campaign #6 when a graphite shaft seal had to
be repacked. The Idle phase accumulates over time for longer campaigns as the period of normal
operations is ceased on weekends, short duration holidays like Thanksgiving, or other activities that pause
GTA operations. Starting in Campaign #6, more advanced monitoring software allows for extended
operations that promote up time where GTA operates and limits the amount of idle time.
 The experimental phase ends when either a failure of a component, as indicated by a change in noise
during operation or variation in the signals from vibration sensors is detected or the campaign has been
halted after a sufficient period of testing. A failure was detected for all previous campaigns except for
Campaign #6 which was halted before failure occurred. All failures have been due to the fragmentation
of the bearings due to lack of material stability for the bearing inner and outer races at the temperatures
where testing occurred or, in the case of the heat-treated bearings, the cage used to maintain spacing
between the roller elements has not received the same heat treatment resulting in a failure. A summary of
the operational conditions, time at torque, and total revolutions for all campaigns is compiled in Table 2.4.
the lifetimes of the campaigns is primarily dictated by the bearing type and bearing material choices and
not by gear material. For the non-heat-treated tapered roller bearings, the lifetime in revolutions is
~300,000 shaft revolutions at torque. Implementing heat-treated tapered roller bearings with their
increased stability at higher temperatures provides ~3x increase in lifetime to approximately one million
shaft revolutions. Finally, the switch to angular(ball) bearing geometry provides an ~10x increase in
lifetime compared to the heat-treated tapered rollers or approximately ~20 million shaft revolutions.
 Despite such failures the data collected helps inform the maintenance schedule to be used should such
bearings be deployed in the in-vessel fuel handling machine. Our testing finds that while no bearing need
be precluded from installation in an IVTM, however, the shorter-lived bearing variants would necessitate
a more numerous replacement schedule during the lifetime of refueling operations for sodium fast reactors.

3 Campaign #8
 For Campaign #8, GTA was reconstituted with Hastelloy C-22HS radial spur gears and Timken
tapered roller bearings made from 52100 bearing steel and heat-treated for enhanced stability at higher
temperatures. The goal of this campaign was to replicate the components used and torque profile applied
in Campaign #1 with the only difference being the Hastelloy gear material replacing the Inconel 718 gear
material used in all previous campaigns. GTA was installed in METL’s Vessel #1 and was filled
03/19/2024. Testing began on 03/21/2024 and was concluded after a failure was evidenced on 04/19/2024
that caused the gear and motor to train to lock in place, unable to move any further. During the operational
phase of Campaign #8, the torque profile implemented had been used previously in Campaigns #1, #2,
#3, and #5 and is shown in Figure 3.1.a. This profile consists of a short duration, 2-10 seconds, high torque
~400Nm phase, shown in green, followed by a longer duration, 30-60 seconds, lower torque ~200Nm
phase, shown in red, concluding in a dwell or idle phase after which the process restarts by rotating in the
opposite direction. During both torque phases, the rotation rate was fixed at 240 RPM. The temperature
of the vessel and internal sodium temperature was fixed at 250°C. After failure the vessel was drained and
cooled and GTA was removed on 04/30/2024. From 05/01/2024 to 05/06/2024 GTA was placed in the
carbonation cleaning system and after this was completed, Campaign #8 was disassembled and the cause
of failure and subsequent damage was identified.

3.1 Campaign #8 Results
 Campaign #8 was submerged in sodium for 37 days and experimental testing took place over 30 days
as indicated in Table 2.3. Over the course of this testing period, GTA operated at low torque for 85.79
hours and 12.26 hours at high torque as shown in Table 2.4. Over this duration, GTA accumulated
1,058,880 shaft revolutions at torque. The dates for the operational activities are provided in Table 2.2
and Table 2.3 and a summary operating hours, cycles completed, and revolutions completed are provided
in Table 2.4.

Looking back at previous campaigns where heat-treated bearings were used, it is helpful to directly
compare Campaigns #1 and #8, Although heat-treated tapered roller bearings were used in Campaign #4,
cylindrical thrust bearings that provide axial load support at the ends of the shafts were not used and it is
suspected that the additional loading taken on by the tapered roller bearings led to a significantly shortened
lifetime. Thus, as was the intent of the planning for Campaign #8 the most glaring difference is the gear
material choice. The approximately one million shaft revolutions achieved in Campaign #8 differs from
the 1.3 million shaft revolutions achieved in Campaign #1 by about 23%. As shown in Table 2.4,

3.3 Over-Pin Measurements of Gears
 Over-pin measurements can provide a high-level, integrated view of the overall wear on the gears. In
this technique, 0.25” OD machinist dowels are placed between the gear teeth as shown in Figure 3.4.right
and the distance between the two dowels are measured with high precision machinist calipers. Over-pin
measurements had been used to characterize the wear in all previous campaigns except for Campaign #5
due to the shortness of the duration. In general, the wear on the gears was found to be quite small, within
the standard deviation of the measurement technique. This finding points to the high strength nickel alloy
gear material being very robust for handling refueling loads at prototypic refueling temperatures.
However, when the bearing material is churned up and damages the gears, over-pin measurements of the
teeth that integrate over tooth damage, indentations, and gouges, as shown in detail in Figure 3.4.left, no
longer provides meaningful information. The gouges and indentations when represented by the over-pin
measurements indicate enhanced wear, while the ridges of those same gouges and indentations are
represented in the over-pin measurement as material growth of the gear. Thus, over-pin measurements of
the Hastelloy gears have not been performed following Campaign #8.

3.5 NDE Method: Ultrasonic Analysis
 The ultrasonic technique is an NDE method used for in-situ examination of complex parts for detection
and volumetric sizing of defects. An ultrasonic imaging system was assembled for nondestructive
inspection of four Hastelloy gears before and after in-manipulator tests in sodium. Figure 3.9 shows the
setup of the ultrasonic imaging system that consists of an ultrasonic pulser/receiver (Imaginant DPR-300),
an immersible ultrasonic transducer, a water tank, a three-axes scanning translation module, and a
scanning and data acquisition (S&DAQ) Module. The three-axes scanning translation module consists of
two linear translation stages (X-Y axes) and a mounting unit along Z-axis. The mounting unit is mounted
on the top of the linear X-Y scanning translation module to hold the transducer and adjust its height
manually. Each linear translation stage is driven by a step motor with a minimum moving resolution of 2
micrometers. Through XY-scanning, both C-scan intensity and time-of-flight (TOF) images are generated
to locate and size any defects on and between gear’s front and back surfaces. The gear and transducer
were submerged in distilled water and separated from the gear’s scanning surface by a distance equaling
         ANL-ART-318                                       17

Gear Test Assembly – Experimental Testing and Analysis of Gears and Bearings – FY2026

to the transducer’s focal distance. Figure 3.9 shows the setup of ultrasonic imaging of a manipulator gear
in the water tank.
 To completely a full scan of a spur gear, the gear was manually rotated clockwise by several teeth,
three for small gears and two for large gears, after each scan. Totally eight C-scans were taken for each
gear, then a complete C-scan image of the gear is generated from the composite of the eight images. To
reduce scanning time and image processing effort, the two linear translation stages was replaced with
stages that have a travel distance of 6”. The ultrasonic imaging system now can scan an area of 6” x 6”.
 Due to the relatively large gear thicknesses (3” and 4”), it is important to select an ultrasonic transducer
with optimal operation frequency, output power, beam size and focal distance such that a clean ultrasonic
reflection signals from both mounting surfaces of a gear could be received and better detection resolution
could be achieved. Different focused and unfocused ultrasonic transducers were tested to evaluate their
effect of beam size and output energy. An immersible ultrasonic transducer (Panametrics A309R, series
# 2911425) was selected. The transducer has an operation frequency of 5.0 MHz with diameter of 0.5”
and focal distance of 2”. The gear being tested was placed at the focal distance. Reflection echo from
any internal defects or edge effect could be produced in between the echoes of the front and back surfaces.
The time of the echo shows the location of the defect underneath the front surface, and the intensity
depends on the orientation and size of the defect. To reduce scanning time and enhance real-time intensity
image, the S&DAQ module automatically calculated an optimal scanning speed based on the moving
average number and 2D scanning resolutions set by an operator.
 To optimize beam size and output energy, a focused transducer was selected with an operation

4.1 Campaign #9 Results and Failure
 At the conclusion of Campaign #9, eight tests mimicking cycles of refueling cycles followed by inerted
cold and dry long-term storage of fuel handling components were performed. As summarized in Table
2.3, GTA was submerged for 167 days, performed normal testing operations for 12 days and was in
standby for 240 days. During testing operations, GTA accumulated 475,685 shaft revolutions at torque.
Using Table 2.4 when compared with the other campaigns where non-heat-treated tapered roller bearing
were used, specifically Campaign #2 and Campaign #3, the over 475,000 shaft revolutions achieved in
Campaign #9 represents ~28% longer lifetime than the average lifetime of Campaigns #2 and #3, 369,000
shaft revolutions.
 The explanation for this increased lifetime is difficult to understand at this stage. Changes in the
lifetime of gears and bearings are typically associated with changes in the torque loads and rotation rates
applied to the system. The use of a short duration, high torque load followed by a longer duration low
torque load profile used in Campaign #2 and #3 provides an equivalent torque load that has a slightly
larger magnitude than of a Campaign #9’s torque profile consisting of purely low torque loads. Thus, the
larger magnitude of torque shortens the life of gears and bearings and is borne out in the results here. In
this case of Campaign #9, an additional factor that may have played a role is the residual sodium that
persists on the components after draining and cooling has occurred. It is unclear if this residual sodium
could provide additional lubricating effects for the components under load.
 Photographs from the disassembly process are shown in Figure 4.6. An aspect of Campaign #9 not
found previously was the more extensive presence and greater quantity of passivated sodium. It is posited
that as the eight testing cycles were conducted, residual sodium provided and builds upon more numerous
nucleation sites for subsequent residual sodium to be able to attach. On horizontal surfaces like that of the
bottom of the catch basket in Figure 4.6.b and of the lower weldment in Figure 4.6.c. the remaining sodium
is harder and more difficult to remove than has been previously encountered. During disassembly in
previous campaigns the passivated sodium is thin and soft and tends to slough off during the cleaning
process. Although difficult to convey via image, Figure 4.6.d shows an example of large ~1.0” thick,
layered, and hard passivated sodium removed from the GTA lower horizontal weldment.
 The disassembly of Campaign #9 was aided by the use of, for the first time, the implementation of
stainless inserts that provide seating to the outer bearing races and are installed in gear blocks modified to
accept the inserts. Should a bearing failure occur it is hoped that the resulting damage is confined to the
inserts themselves and not to the gear block as a whole. Thus, if damage should occur it is more efficient
and cost-effective to replace the insert and not the entire gear block. In addition, the inserts were designed
with grooved features that allow for the bearing races to be removed using brass punches in a simpler
manner so that the bearings don’t get bent or damaged during disassembly. For Campaign #9, these inserts
were not damaged and greatly aided in the speed and efficiency of bearing race removal as intended.

5 Summary and Path Forward
 This report summarizes the lifetimes for the gears and bearings that participated in Campaign #8 and
Campaign #9. Campaign #8 was the first campaign to make use of the other set of nickel alloy radial spur
gears, made from Hastelloy C-22HS and combined with heat-treated tapered roller bearings. The bearings
and torque profile used allowed Campaign #8 to be directly compared with Campaign #1 where Inconel
718 gears were used instead. Campaign #8 attained 1,058,880 revolutions within 23% of the 1,314,855
revolutions achieved in Campaign #1. These results demonstrate that the heat-treated tapered roller
bearings show consistent increased lifetimes approximately three times longer than for non-heat-treated
taper rollers and that the particular material of the gear does not play an important role in performance.
Similar to Campaign #7 where the fragmentation and transport of bearing material into the gear teeth leads
to tooth damage in the Inconel gear, a similar process led to the damage of the Hastelloy gears. Subsequent
NDE analysis showed that the damage was only limited to the tooth surface and does not extend into gear
interior. Based on the NDE reports, the Hastelloy gears can be reconditioned and put back service for
future campaigns.
 Reconditioned Inconel gears damaged in Campaign #7 were returned to service in Campaign #9 and
paired with non-heat-treated tapered roller bearings. In Campaign #9 a novel set of operating conditions
were implemented whereby the gears and bearings were cycled between hot, submerged refueling
conditions and cold, dry, and inert conditions where the sodium was removed and the vessel allowed to
cool simulating long, ex-vessel storage of the fuel handling machine. A total of eight of these operating
cycles were achieved before a bearing failure occurred after the accumulation of 475,692 shaft revolutions
were achieved. During Campaign #9, one of the drive motors was damaged and entire GTA drive system
was upgraded from a 480VAC system to a 240VAC system that provided the opportunity to consolidate
wall-space in the METL facility and provide more space for additional test articles becoming operational
soon.
 Looking forward, the bearings from Campaign #9 will be analyzed to determine what, if any, material
alterations were caused by the cyclic operations. Future campaigns will continue to explore cyclical
operating conditions and gather more data to provide better statistics on the lifetime of gears and bearings.
The findings from GTA results provide useful information and engineering experience on other test
articles like the GrTA which is nearing submerged sodium testing and for the Bearing Test Article, BTA
being fabricated, assembled, and air-tested by the end of Calendar Year 2026.
