ctDNA Detection of Polyclonal KRAS Resistance in Adagrasib‐Treated NSCLC
Authors: Marion Caumeil, Julie A Vendrell, Simon Cabello‐Aguilar, Sarah Cavaillon, Quentin Dominique Thomas, Jérôme Solassol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCirculating tumor DNA (ctDNA) monitoring may detect resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors earlier than conventional imaging. However, in the specific context of KRAS G12C inhibitors, the temporal relationship between molecular, radiologic, and clinical progression remains poorly characterized. A 66‐year‐old woman with KRAS G12C/STK11/KEAP1‐mutant lung adenocarcinoma received second‐line adagrasib after progression on chemo‐immunotherapy. Serial ctDNA monitoring by droplet digital PCR and targeted next‐generation sequencing was performed. Baseline KRAS G12C ctDNA (6205.3 copies/mL) declined to 3.2 copies/mL after 7 weeks, indicating initial response. However, ctDNA levels rose to 21.1 copies/mL at month 4 and 315.2 copies/mL at month 6. Concurrent NGS revealed three emergent KRAS mutations (G12D, G13D, Q61H), confirming polyclonal resistance. Notably, CT and MRI imaging remained stable by RECIST criteria throughout this period. Significant clinical deterioration, requiring prolonged hospitalization, occurred approximately 8 weeks after initial ctDNA rise. The patient died 2 months after detection of resistance mutations. In this case, NGS ctDNA detected three acquired KRAS resistance mutations in addition to the original G12C, during a prolonged period of radiographic stability, illustrating polyclonal evolution under adagrasib pressure. These findings suggest that longitudinal liquid biopsy monitoring may provide early detection of resistance in KRAS G12C‐mutant NSCLC.Keywords: acquired resistance, adagrasib, circulating tumor DNA, KRAS G12C , liquid biopsy, non‐small cell lung cancer

KRAS G12C inhibitors represent a major therapeutic advance for non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) harboring this specific mutation, with sotorasib and adagrasib achieving objective response rates of 36%–43% in previously treated patients [1, 2]. Among the known mechanisms of resistance, secondary KRAS mutations are increasingly recognized. These alterations create compound heterozygous KRAS‐mutant subclones that are insensitive to covalent G12C inhibition, thereby restoring downstream RAS‐MAPK signaling despite continued drug exposure [3, 4].

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) enables noninvasive molecular monitoring and has emerged as a powerful tool for detecting resistance mechanisms in oncogene‐driven NSCLC [5]. Compared to conventional imaging, ctDNA has shown superior sensitivity in some settings for detecting emerging resistant subclones and may provide earlier detection of treatment failure [6, 7]. This early detection capability is particularly relevant for therapies prone to polyclonal resistance patterns, such as KRAS G12C inhibitors.

Here, we report a case in which serial ctDNA monitoring identified three acquired KRAS resistance mutations during a period of imaging stability according to RECIST 1.1 criteria. This case underscores the potential of ctDNA to detect molecular resistance despite stable imaging and highlights the challenges posed by polyclonal resistance in KRAS‐mutant NSCLC.

Case Presentation

A 66‐year‐old North African woman, former smoker (30 pack‐years), presented in April 2024 with a T1cN0M1a invasive mucinous lung adenocarcinoma. Staging revealed a 21‐mm right apical lesion, bilateral subpleural nodules, and diffuse bone metastases. Her medical history included heart failure, chronic respiratory insufficiency (FEV1 50%), and dyslipidemia. Transthoracic biopsy demonstrated CK7 positivity, focal TTF‐1 positivity, CDX2 positivity, and PD‐L1 expression < 1%. Tissue‐based next‐generation sequencing (NGS) identified KRAS G12C, STK11 C132W and KEAP1 A159S mutations.

First‐line treatment with carboplatin, pemetrexed, and pembrolizumab was initiated in June 2024. The patient subsequently received palliative radiotherapy to T1 in August 2024 and to T3–T5 in November 2024 for painful progressive bone metastases with epidural involvement (Figure 1). In December 2024, she developed severe Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia requiring intensive care admission, followed by recovery. However, CT imaging in January 2025 demonstrated systemic progression not amenable to local therapy.

In February 2025, after multidisciplinary tumor board review, second‐line treatment with adagrasib 600 mg twice daily was initiated based on the KRYSTAL‐1 trial results [2] and longitudinal ctDNA monitoring was initiated. Cell‐free DNA (cfDNA), of which ctDNA represents a small fraction, was isolated from a minimum of 8 mL plasma using the Promega ccfDNA kit and analyzed by digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) on a Bio‐Rad QX200 system. Baseline ctDNA ddPCR analysis detected 6205.3 copies of KRAS G12C‐mutated DNA/mL plasma. After 7 weeks of adagrasib treatment, ctDNA analysis showed encouraging decline to 3.2 copies/mL, suggesting initial treatment response.

In April 2025, the patient developed progressive cervical pain radiating to both upper extremities. Cervical spine MRI on May 17, 2025 revealed T1 retrolisthesis with C7‐T1 foraminal narrowing. A KRAS G12C ddPCR ctDNA assessment performed on April 25, 2025 showed an increase to 21.1 copies/mL. A subsequent ctDNA analysis on June 30, 2025 demonstrated a further rise to 315.2 copies/mL, suggesting early molecular progression (Figure 1).

In addition to ddPCR monitoring, comprehensive molecular profiling was performed using an in‐house targeted NGS assay specifically designed for ctDNA analysis. Libraries were prepared with the Twist library preparation kit using unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and a targeted capture panel covering 33 oncology‐relevant genes, and sequenced on an NextSeq500 Illumina platform with a minimum deduplicated depth of 2000×. Bioinformatic analysis was performed using MobiCT which allowed a validated limit of detection of 0.1% Variant Allele Frequency (VAF) [8]. Baseline ctDNA NGS (March 2, 2025) confirmed KRAS G12C (VAF 13.6%), STK11 C132W (VAF 13.9%), and KEAP1 A159S (VAF 11.9%). Repeat ctDNA NGS on June 30, 2025 detected three newly acquired KRAS mutations G12D (VAF 1.7%), G13D (VAF 0.4%), and Q61H (VAF 0.2%), all absent at baseline, supporting the presence of multiple independent resistant subclones arising under adagrasib pressure (Figure 2).

Polyclonal evolution under therapeutic pressure. Schematic illustration of tumor evolution under adagrasib treatment. The main clone harboring KRAS G12C, STK11 C132W and KEAP1 A159S undergoes therapeutic pressure, giving rise to three distinct resistant subclones, each acquiring different secondary KRAS mutations (G12D, G13D, Q61H).

Despite this molecular evidence of resistance, CT thoraco‐abdomino‐pelvic imaging performed on June 20, 2025 demonstrated stable disease with unchanged 13 mm apical right lung mass, stable pulmonary micronodules, and diffuse bone sclerosis suggesting treatment response, without new mechanical complications or lesions by RECIST 1.1. Cervical spine MRI on July 7, 2025 showed stable C7‐T1 bone metastases with persistent retrolisthesis and foraminal narrowing.

Despite radiographic stability, the patient's clinical status deteriorated with refractory pain from C7‐T1 metastases, necessitating prolonged hospitalization (June 22–July 31, 2025) and performance status decline from ECOG 1 to 2. Adagrasib was continued given the absence of alternative therapies. The patient died in September 2025, approximately 2 months after detection of resistance mutations and 4 months after initial ctDNA elevation.

Discussion

This case highlights a marked temporal dissociation between molecular, radiologic, and clinical progression in KRAS G12C‐mutant NSCLC treated with adagrasib. Multiple resistance mechanisms to KRAS G12C inhibitors have been described at the time of radiographic progression, including secondary KRAS mutations in 18% of adagrasib‐treated patients [3] and resistance alterations across RAS‐MAPK pathway genes (KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1) in adagrasib‐treated patients [9]. In contrast, the present case documents the longitudinal emergence of three distinct KRAS resistance mutations (G12D, G13D, Q61H) during a period of complete RECIST stability. This observation extends prior findings by showing that multiple KRAS resistance can emerge well before radiographic progression [3, 4]. Notably, unlike previously reported cases where resistance involved alterations across several RAS‐MAPK pathway genes, this patient developed four coexisting KRAS mutations without detectable alterations in NRAS, BRAF, or MAP2K1, suggesting a distinct pattern of clonal evolution confined to KRAS.

Serial ctDNA assessments showed an initial robust decrease in KRAS G12C levels after adagrasib initiation, followed by a progressive rise beginning on April 25, 2025. Notably, although the patient developed cervical pain in April 2025, CT and MRI imaging remained stable through July 7, 2025, illustrating a molecular‐radiologic interval of approximately 10 weeks. Significant clinical deterioration, characterized by performance status decline and prolonged hospitalization, occurred in late June 2025. Such early ctDNA rises have been previously associated with subclinical progression in EGFR‐ and ALK‐treated lung cancers [6, 7]. The present case extends these observations to the setting of KRAS G12C inhibition.

These acquired KRAS mutations represent known mechanisms of resistance to covalent G12C inhibition [3, 4]. Their distribution across distinct structural domains, G12D and G13D within the P‐loop, and Q61H within the switch II region, illustrates the diverse biochemical strategies by which tumor cells restore RAS‐MAPK signaling under therapeutic pressure. These mutants differentially affect GTP hydrolysis (both intrinsic and GAP‐mediated), nucleotide exchange, and effector binding, ultimately restoring active GTP‐bound KRAS signaling despite continued adagrasib exposure [3, 4]. In the present case, polyclonal resistance was characterized by the co‐detection of three distinct KRAS mutations (G12D, G13D, and Q61H) at low but distinct VAFs (1.7%, 0.4%, and 0.2%, respectively), all absent at baseline. The low individual VAFs, coupled with NGS read analysis confirming that G12D and G13D occurred in trans relative to the initial KRAS G12C allele, support the emergence of multiple independent resistant subclones rather than the evolution of a single dominant clone through sequential mutations.

The co‐occurrence of STK11 and KEAP1 mutations may have contributed to the rapid emergence of polyclonal resistance. Both genes are associated with poor prognosis in KRAS‐mutant lung cancer and may promote adaptive evolution under targeted pressure [10]. Their comparable allele frequencies in ctDNA suggest a shared clonal origin predisposed to rapid diversification once adagrasib was initiated.

Another important explanation for the molecular‐radiologic discordance is the predominance of bone metastases, where treatment‐related sclerosis may obscure tumor activity on CT imaging. Similar discordance patterns have been observed in advanced NSCLC, with ctDNA rises preceding radiographic progression by a median of 1.5 months [11]. Although bone‐related complications may have contributed to symptoms, the temporal correlation suggests that molecular progression played a role.

The rapid clinical decline following detection of molecular progression highlights the therapeutic challenges once resistance emerges. Available treatment options remain limited. Several investigational strategies aim to overcome resistance, including combinations of KRAS inhibitors, pan‐RAS(ON) inhibitors, SHP2 or SOS1 inhibitors, and downstream MAPK pathway inhibitors [4].

Finally, this case underscores the potential clinical utility of ctDNA‐guided monitoring in KRAS G12C‐mutant NSCLC. As illustrated here, extensive molecular progression involving multiple KRAS alterations can occur during prolonged radiographic stability, even in patients with ongoing symptoms. Although no approved alternative therapies were available for this patient, earlier detection of resistance could inform enrollment in clinical trials targeting emergent resistance mechanisms or facilitate timely transition to supportive care. However, broader implementation of ctDNA monitoring requires standardization of assay platforms, monitoring frequency, and actionability thresholds, as none of these parameters are currently established in this setting. Of note, false‐negative results inherent to liquid biopsy cannot be excluded, particularly at low tumor burden where ctDNA levels may fall below assay detection thresholds, potentially underestimating true molecular progression. The combined use of orthogonal methods, as performed here with both ddPCR and NGS, may partially mitigate this limitation, though not eliminate it entirely. Prospective trials are needed to determine whether early therapeutic intervention based on ctDNA dynamics can improve outcomes in patients receiving KRAS G12C inhibitors, particularly in scenarios of radiologic stability.
