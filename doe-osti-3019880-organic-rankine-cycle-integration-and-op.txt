EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This project successfully advanced the integration of Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC)
technology with reciprocating engine–based combined heat and power (CHP) systems
to improve electrical efficiency, total CHP efficiency, and grid-responsive operation.
Over three budget periods, the work progressed from high-temperature ORC
component development and thermodynamic model validation to next-generation
system design, working fluid transition, and techno-economic analysis.
Key technical accomplishments include development and validation of a thermodynamic
model capable of accurately predicting ORC performance across an expanded
temperature and pressure envelope; successful identificationand validation of low-
global-warming-potential (GWP) working fluids—most notably R1233zd(E)—as viable
replacements for R245fa; and demonstration of scalable ORC architectures suitable for
integration with 1–20 MW class reciprocating engines. These advances enable flexible
CHP configurations that can increase electrical output while maintaining high overall
utilization of available thermal energy.
The project also produced a clean-sheet design for a next-generation ORC system
targeting substantially higher power output per unit, supported by detailed component
selection, heat exchanger evaluation, and system-level modeling. Techno-economic
analyses indicate that ORC-enabled flexible CHP systems can meet or exceed
Department of Energy (DOE) efficiency targets while providing value to both facility
operators and the electric grid..
Late-stage testing of the largest next-generation ORC prototype identified limitations
related to pump net positive suction head (NPSH) requirements and condenser flooding
under certain operating conditions. Although these issues constrained full validation of
that configuration within the project timeframe, they provided clear and actionable
design guidance for future system refinements. Importantly, validated modeling,
smaller-scale testing, and working fluid evaluations confirmed the technical viability of
the overall approach.
In aggregate, this project met its core objectives by establishing validated design tools,
de-risking key ORC technologies for CHP applications, and defining a credible pathway
toward commercialization of flexible, high-efficiency CHP systems. The results form a
strong foundation for continued development and deployment beyond the conclusion
of the DOE-funded effort.

Project Objectives and Accomplishments

The project was executed over three budget periods:

 • Budget Period 1 (BP1): Optimized ORC componentry and conducted cost-
 reduction studies, including high-temperature prototype development and
 thermodynamic model validation.
 • Budget Period 2 (BP2): Clean-sheet design of an optimized engine + ORC CHP
 system, component selection, and techno-economic analysis framework.
 • Budget Period 3 (BP3): Extended performance testing, working fluid validation,
 scalability analysis, and new unit fabrication and testing.

Budget Period 1 – Optimized ORC Components and Model Validation
BP1 focused on expanding the ORC operating envelope to higher temperatures and
pressures suitable for CHP applications. Key accomplishments include:

 • Design and construction of high-temperature ORC prototypes (4400B+ and
 6500B+ configurations).
 • Validation of a physics-based thermodynamic model (Torque 2.0) using test cell
 data.
 • Chemical compatibility assessment of alternative working fluids and lubricants.
 • Successful endurance operation of a 4400B system at a commercial site,
 generating valuable expander reliability and lubrication insights.
These efforts established a validated modeling and design foundation for subsequent
system optimization and scale-up.

Budget Period 2 – Engine + ORC System Design and Techno-Economic Analysis
BP2 activities centered on pairing ORC systems with reciprocating engines and
optimizing system-level performance:

 • Selection and modeling of ORC components
 • Evaluation of shell-and-tube versus brazed-plate heat exchangers for condenser
 and evaporator duties.
 • Clean-sheet design of a next-generation ORC (targeting up to ~250 kW gross
 output) capable of pairing with 1–10 MW engines.
 • Initial techno-economic analysis demonstrating pathways to meet DOE electrical
 and total CHP efficiency targets when ORC systems are integrated with large
 reciprocating engines.
Modeling results showed that ORC integration can materially increase electrical output
and total CHP efficiency while enabling flexible operation in response to grid signals.

Budget Period 3 – Performance Testing, Working Fluids, and Market Analysis
BP3 emphasized extended testing, working fluid validation, and commercialization
considerations:

 • Successful operation and thermodynamic model validation with low-GWP
 working fluids, particularly R1233zd(E), demonstrating good agreement between
 predicted and measured performance.
 • Identification and evaluation of R1366mzz(Z) as a potential high-temperature
 CHP fluid, though ultimately ruled out due to material compatibility concerns.
 • Development of batch modeling tools to evaluate ORC performance under
 varying ambient conditions, thermal availability, and multi-unit configurations.
 • Advancement of TEA methodologyusing real-world energy demand data, engine
 performance data, and CHP market scenarios.
Late-stage testing of the largest next-generation ORC prototype revealed limitations
related to pump net positive suction head (NPSH) requirements and condenser flooding.
While these issues prevented full model validation of that configuration within the
project timeframe, they provided clear design direction for future system refinements.

Key Challenges and Lessons Learned
 • Pump NPSH and condenser design: Large ORC systems require careful
 integration of pump selection, condenser configuration, and system elevation to
 avoid cavitation without sacrificing condenser performance.
 • Working fluid selection: Low-GWP fluids such as R1233zd(E) are viable
 replacements for R245fa, though material compatibility and lubrication
 strategies are critical.
 • System scalability: Positive-displacement expanders and modular component
 designs offer practical pathways to scale ORC systems across a wide range of
 heat inputs.
These lessons materially reduce technical risk for future ORC-CHP deployments.

Technical Outcomes
 • Validated high-temperature ORC modeling capability: A thermodynamic model was
 developed and validated against test cell data across an expanded operating envelope,
 including single- and dual-heat-source configurations. This model provides a reliable
 design and optimization tool for future ORC-CHP systems and materially reduces
 development risk.

 • Low-GWP working fluid transition: The project successfully identified and validated
 R1233zd(E) as a low-global-warming-potential replacement for R245fa. Experimental
 testing demonstrated good agreement between measured performance and model
 predictions, enabling immediate use of the validated fluid in future designs and
 commercial offerings.

 • Scalable ORC architecture: Component-level analysis and modeling demonstrated
 practical pathways to scale ORC systems across a wide range of thermal inputs using
 modular expanders, pumps, and heat exchangers while minimizing changes to working
 fluid piping and balance-of-plant complexity.

 • Next-generation ORC system design: A new ORC design targeting substantially higher
 electrical output per unit was completed and fabricated. Although full experimental
 validation was constrained late in the project, the design effort clarified critical system
 interactions—particularly pump NPSH and condenser behavior—that will guide future
 prototype refinements.

Impact on CHP Performance and Grid Flexibility
 • Improved electrical efficiency: Modeling and testing results show that ORC integration
 can increase the effective electrical efficiency of large reciprocating engine CHP systems
 to meet or exceed DOE targets at both rated and part-load conditions.

 • Enhanced total CHP efficiency: By converting otherwise unused waste heat into
 electricity while still delivering usable thermal energy, ORC-enabled CHP systems
 maintain high total system efficiency even during grid-responsive operation.

 • Flexible, grid-responsive operation: The project demonstrated, through modeling and
 system-level analysis, that ORC-equipped CHP systems can respond dynamically to load
 demands without sacrificing long-term efficiency or equipment integrity.

Commercial and Market Impact
 • Reduced technical and commercial risk: Validated models, proven vetted and
 implemented working fluid alternatives, and defined component architectures which
 significantly reduce uncertainty for future demonstration and deployment projects.

 • Clear pathway to commercialization: Techno-economic analyses indicate strong value
 propositions for flexible CHP systems in industrial, institutional, and district energy
 applications with variable thermal and electrical demands.

 • Foundation for follow-on demonstrations: The outcomes of this project position ORC-
 enabled CHP systems for near-term pilot and demonstration projects that can accelerate
 market adoption and private-sector investment.

Conclusions
This project successfully advanced the state of ORC integration with reciprocating
engine CHP systems through modeling validation, prototype development, and working
fluid alternative assessment. While not all late-stage prototype testing objectives were
completed within the project period, the work achieved its core technical goals and
delivered actionable insights that support continued development and
commercialization.
The results demonstrate that ORC-enabled flexible CHP systems can meet DOE
efficiency targets, support thermal load responsive operation, and provide a compelling
pathway for improving energy efficiency in industrial and distributed generation
applications.

Future Work
Future efforts will focus on:

 • Refinement of condenser and pump architectures to resolve NPSH-related
 limitations.
 • Continued endurance testing and validation of next-generation ORC prototypes.
These activities will build directly on the technical foundation established under this
DOE project.

Task 3.1 – Operation of Next Gen ORC with R245fa:
The Recipient will operate the prototype with R245fa to compare the new system design
with a fully characterized working fluid and its performance expectations.

Due to the phase out of higher GWP fluids, R245fa is becoming harder to find. Honeywell has
said they will not be making any more of this fluid. Because of that we see no reason to test the
larger machine with this fluid, all application for this new ORC will have to use one of the HFO
replacements – see Task 3.4. Therefore, our initial operation, and model validation (task 3.2) will
be done with R1233zd(E).

 The Next Gen ORC was put on the test cell and evaluated for several key parameters to
guide performance evaluation. One critical parameter to ORC operation is the refrigerant charge.
The ORC needs enough refrigerant to provide enough hydrostatic pressure at the inlet of the
working fluid pump to meet the NPSH requirements. However, relying on hydrostatic pressure
alone is not sufficient to satisfy the NSPH needed for the pumps operation. To combat this, the
subcooled working fluid in the condenser will also contribute to the NSPH. However, if there is
too much subcooled working fluid in the condenser, there is less heat transfer surface area for
condensation and hampering performance. The ideal working fluid charge is enough that the
pumps NPSH requirements are satisfied, but not so much that the performance drops. After initial
internal volume calculations, and some iteration with changing the refrigerant charge, a charge of
650 lbs was established. At this level, the pump ran well without cavitation, but the condenser
was filled up to about 40% with subcooled refrigerant. This amount of subcooling was found to
greatly impact the condenser, and the system performance, see details below in section 3.2.
 The next steps to improve system performance will be to install a booster pump before
the primary working fluid pump, to increase the NPSH into the primary working fluid pump
without having to sacrifice condenser surface area. This will hopefully improve performance and
reduce the working fluid charge. This was planned for as an option back during the design phase
– see Task 2.8 Design of “Next Gen ORC” System.

 Unfortunately, the addition of the booster pump to the Next Gen ORC system design did
not resolve the challenges with operation. The booster pump was not able to provide sufficient
pressure head to the primary working fluid pump, at all flow rates. We tried many different
methods and combinations of starting and speed, but every time the unit started and was getting
loaded the main pump would start cavitating. We suspect that there was not sufficient volume
between the booster pump and the stack pump. As the stack pump ramped up, it would overdraw
the flow rate coming from the booster pump. It is possible that a surge tank between the pumps
would help to mitigate the problem, but we did not have that option designed in.

To overcome the challenge of meeting the NPSH requirements for the working fluid pump, the
condenser will be elevated to increase the hydrostatic pressure on the pump inlet. This is the
simplest method and is sure to work. It has the added advantage of getting rid of the additional
parasitic load of the booster pump. Unfortunately, this is cumbersome from a packaging
standpoint, that is the reason we were trying to avoid it in the first place.

At this point there is not sufficient time left in the project to make this large of a change to the
prototype unit. We are not giving up on the idea of the larger ORC, but a much greater redesign
will be necessary to recognize the full potential of this larger expander.

Task 3.3 – Operation of Next Gen with Alternate Working Fluids:
The Recipient will select the best new and alternate refrigerant/or combinations that has
passed both the chemical compatibility and the longevity testing and test in the prototype
system.

We have decided to use one of our current ORC systems to test the new working fluids. This will
allow us to do the testing in parallel with the completion of the Next Gen ORC. The operational
data and model validation will be valid for all ORC’s, so we see no downside to conducting this
work prior to the completion of the larger ORC. Time permitting, we can still run the most
promising fluids / blends through the larger system after the baseline testing is complete.

We have developed a preliminary test plan for the new working fluids this can be reviewed in
APPENDIX IV – TEST PLAN FOR NEW WORKING FLUIDS (Major update to the test plan
this quarter) – We will start with the two pure fluids, and then move onto mixtures. We have
ordered R1233zd(E) and R1366mzz(Z) for our initial testing. We have completed the design of

new pipe manifolds that will accommodate the mass flow meter in our standard ORC, and the
fabrication will be completed early next quarter.

We have identified the first glide mixture that will be tested to see how the cycle behaves at
smaller cycle temperature differences. R471a is a commercially available refrigerant that meets
our criteria and could work by itself or be used to enhance the other new fluids.

We have modified the test ORC to include a mass flow meter on the refrigerant line between the
condenser and the feed pump. We have also added a flow meter to the lubrication line, see Figure
1 below. This will allow us to do a full mass balance on the refrigerant, and know how much is
actually going through the screw expanders. This will give us a much better idea of the real
isentropic expander efficiency, so that we can see how it changes with the new working fluids.

 Figure 1: Flow Meters installed on Test ORC

We have cleared test cell time in November so that this testing can start next quarter.

We have successfully run the test ORC with R1233zd(E). This involved a new lubrication oil, a
new feed pump to be compatible, and a modified lubrication system to work with the new oil. All
systems worked well; no problems were noted during the operation across the full operating range
of the smaller 75kW ORC. During operation the system was monitored closely for noise and
vibration that could be signs of poor lubrication. All parameters stayed within the baseline with
R245fa. The control system had to be updated with a new operating envelope that matches the
PT and saturation curve of the new fluid. We used the vibration meter to validate the edges of the
operating envelope to ensure that the controls would keep the unit in an appropriate operating
point with the new fluid.

Once this was completed and we were confident that the machine was operating safely, we
evaluated the performance across a range of conditions. We tested three different hot water
temperature (100, 110, 120°C). At each hot water point, we looked at two different cold water
temperatures. This provides a good initial map of the outputs for comparison to the

thermodynamic model (see details in Task 3.4). The Gross power outputs compares well with
R245fa, as shown below in Figure 2.

The other important factor is the amount of heat that the ORC consumed at these conditions. This
shows the conversion efficiency (thermal to electric) for the new working fluid. As expected, at
each point the new fluid required a little more energy to produce the power, so the conversion

NOTE 1: In the original test plan, we wanted to test two new pure fluids, and a mixture to look at
the impact of “glide” on ORC performance. However, all the mixtures we have identified would
only be beneficial at lower temperatures, therefore not as interesting for CHP operation. For that
reason, we are prioritizing the other pure fluid R1366mzz. We will not get to test the mixtures
within the remaining time on the project.

NOTE 2: This winter during a cold snap, the coolers that make the cold water for the test cell
froze and broke. We have been operating with a small cooler on a temporary basis while we wait
for the full-sized replacements. Delivery of these new coolers has been delayed until July.
Therefore, our test cell output is limited by the temporary cooler for heat rejection, and we will
not be able to operate above the 70kW shown above. This is unfortunate but still provides

verification of operation, and good initial model validation.

Task 3.6 – Modeling of Scaled System Components Without Changing the Working
Fluid Piping:
The Recipient will demonstrate the scalability of the system to operate on larger / different
heat sources while keeping the production cost down.

Each of the 4 major components could be changed to scale the system for different heat loads, the
expander, the feed pump, the evaporator, and the condenser. Since most of the production costs
and labor is in the fabricated pipe manifolds, this only makes sense if the components can be
changed with only minimal changes to the piping. From an optimization standpoint the expander
and feed pump are the two components with the greatest opportunity for scalability and cost.

Expander:
The same expander housing can be internally modified to change the trapped volume on the high-
pressure side. Since the expander is positive displacement, this changes the volumetric flow
through the system. Higher flowrates lead to more heat transferred and more power output given
the same source and sink conditions. However, if the site has limited thermal energy, lowering
the flowrate of the working fluid can make more efficient use of the available heat. This is the
easiest modification to make and requires no changes to the working fluid manifolds. The initial
testing will be done with the largest possible trapped volume, this corresponds to the highest
volumetric flowrate, and the largest heat load on the other components. This will help us define
the upper limit for this Next Gen ORC.

Feed Pump:
The feed pump must then be sized to support the flowrate that is set by the expander. If the
expander is changed for lower heat consumption – then a smaller feed pump can also be used, this
could represent significant cost savings. Luckly there are many pump options that would use the
same flange size and spacing, meaning no changes to the working fluid piping. Even if we went
to a smaller flange size, it would be very minimal changes to the working fluid piping. Another
driving factor in the size of the feed pump is the NPSHr. A lot of effort was spent looking for a
pump with minimal required NPSHr, see BP2 tasks 2.2 and 2.8. As we look towards optimizing,
we recognize that we might want to reconsider the idea of using subcooled fluid out of the
condenser to satisfy some of the NPSHr needed by the pump. Subcooling in the condenser can be
controlled via the liquid level in the condenser. We have decided to add a sight glass to the
condenser so that the liquid level can be monitored during our testing.

Evaporator:
The amount of surface area in the Evaporator could be reduced if the system was being used at a
lower output design point. The number of plates in the heat exchanger could be lowered in the
same frame size to reduce costs, without major changes to the working fluid piping. Since we are
currently using a pair of heat exchangers to achieve the thermal duty of evaporation, smaller heat
exchangers could be inserted and the only change to the piping would be to make a longer spool
piece between the units, as shown below in Figure 13. Getting heat exchangers with less surface
area (less plates in this case) would only be a small saving on the cost of the units. This will be
evaluated once we are getting test data, but it will likely not be a large savings and might not be
worth the effort.

 Figure 13: Smaller HX could be used for the evaporator with minimal changes to the piping.

Condenser:
The condenser would be difficult to change without major changes to the piping and the support
structure. Similar to the evaporator, reducing the surface area in the condenser to accommodate
smaller heat loads would be a relatively small cost savings. Additionally, having an oversized
condenser has the benefit of lowering the pressure drop of the post expansion vapor. At this time,
we do not think it would be beneficial to try and optimize the system by changing the condenser.

This analysis will involve estimating the ORC output with both changing ambient conditions

The batch tool is designed to evaluate the ORC performance over a range of cold water
temperatures, which fluctuate due to ambient temperatures, as well as with varying thermal
availability. Both ambient temperature and thermal energy available over the course of a period
of time are inputs to the tool, as well as the ORC configuration and setpoint. It will vary the hot

water temperature at a given flow rate to meet the thermal constraints at a given timestamp. The
output is the ORC performance, water outlet temperatures, and thermal energy consumed and
rejected. Figure 8 below shows an example output from the batch tool that calculates the ORC
output based on a year of temperature data from NOAA.

For the large CHP covered by the scope of the project it might be necessary to run one or more
ORC’s. To account for this, we have added the ability to do multiple units in series to the batch

temperature difference, and will hit this limit before the first machine in the series. This can be
observed in the output from the updated batch tool. Figure 9 below show two machines running

Task 3.8 – Longevity Testing of Mixtures That Pass the Compatibility Testing:
The Recipient will subject new fluids that pass chemical compatibility testing to a longevity
test. In this test, a small compressor will be loaded and run with the test fluid and oil for

Out parent company BITZER has continued to do studies with the new refrigerants in Germany.
This serves both our market, and their core business of refrigeration systems. One of the biggest
aspects of system longevity is the lubrication of the expander (or compressor in the case of
refrigeration). Our current lubrication system relies on a mixture of refrigerant and a non-
miscible oil. This combination of oil and refrigerant forms a colloidal mixture, and the expander
is well lubricated because the oil has a higher affinity for the meatal surfaces.

One challenge with the new refrigerants we are considering is most oils have at least partial
solubility in them. It does not look like we will be able to form the same kind of mixture to keep
the expander lubricated. To solve this, we are currently looking into using a separator between
the evaporator and the expander to separate the liquid oil from the refrigerant vapor. BITZER
already has experience with using a separator like this, so we will be adapting their design to our
system.

We have completed the design of the new lubrication system. This will take advantage of an oil
separator that is built into the high-pressure inlet on the expander. The gas oil mixture coming
out of the evaporator goes through this separator before getting into the high-pressure port
between the screws. Most of the oil will collect at the bottom of the expander housing. From
here it will be filtered, go through a flow switch, and be fed into the lubrication port on the side of
the expander. The expander pressure difference will drive the oil through the bearing chambers
and then out with the low-pressure vapor. BITZER has done extensive testing with this method
of lubricating the expanders in Germany. We will adopt this method on our refrigeration test
machine that will be used for tasks 3.3 and 3.4. The unit will be evaluated after all the operation
that will be done for model and performance validation.

We believe that this actual run time with an expander and the new fluids will provide better
longevity data than the original plan of building a test system using small compressors. The
bearing loading, and the lubrication pathways, are different on expanders compared to
compressors. Also, the separation method for the oil is completely different in a compressor.
Therefore, we are not going to build a small compressor test stand. Instead, we will rely on the
run time accumulated during the performance validation, combined with the experience gained by
BITZER Germany to determine the expected longevity of the expanders when using the new
working fluids.

All testing with both new fluids (tasks 3.3 and 3.4) was conducted using the new lubrication
method. We took additional pressure data around the filter assembly, to validate the new flow
switch that is being used in the lubrication lines. The pressure drop across the filters lined up
with the literature values for pressure vs flow and confirmed that the switch opened when no flow
was present. After the testing was completed – the expander was evaluated and we saw no signs
of premature wear on the bearings. In addition, we have a demo site running at the BITZER
factory that has been using the new refrigerant, and lubrication method. This has been evaluated
for vibration and noise, and so far is not showing any increased vibration that would indicate

bearing wear. All of these factors together give us a high degree of confidence in the new fluids,
with the new lubrication method. We have no additional concerns regarding the longevity of the
system with the new fluids.

Task 2.1 – Shell and Tube Condenser Evaluation:
The Recipient will design and test a shell and tube condenser in the test cell. This style of
condenser will be compared to the standard condenser to determine further improvements
in overall pressure drop, heat transfer, and cycle pinch points.

For this larger ORC we wanted to consider a shell and tube condenser, with cooling water on the
tube side and working fluid on the shell side. In this configuration a shell and tube heat
exchanger should have lower working fluid pressure drop, increasing the pressure difference that
is available for the expander. The conditions and design point for this evaluation are detailed
below in Task 2.2– Next Gen ORC Component Selection Based on Model: - Table 10:
Condenser design point. These are the same conditions use to evaluate the brazed plate heat
exchangers.

Bitzer Germany proposed several options for shell and tube condensers. The intriguing
characteristic of this SH&T condenser design is its fined outer tube surface that facilitates droplet
formation during condensing, and its patterned inner tube surface that creates turbulent water
flow to enhance the heat transfer performance. The performance of two SH&T condensers are
detailed below in Table 3.

 Table 3: Shell and Tube Condenser Options

Shown in the table above, both options have the same shell diameter of 0.508 m, but type 257 is
about twice in length compared to type 44, resulting the use of type 44 as a pair. Comparing to
brazed plate condenser, more than 50% reduction in pressure drop on the working fluid side is
achieved, potentially saving significant amount of pump work. An impressive estimation from
Bitzer shows that the liquid level at the bottom of the shell is only around 1.5 inch to provide 8 C
subcooling. It indicates that the sacrifice of heat transfer surface area, occupied by the condensed
liquid, is minimum while sufficient subcooling is still achieved. According to Bitzer, the only
performance restriction related to shell and tube configuration is the tube side velocity. It is

recommended to keep the velocity above 1.2 m/s to prevent fouling. However, opting a pair of
type 44 condenser with split fluid flow, the tube side velocity can drop below 1.2 m/s when
cooling water flow rate is less than 30 L/s. The ideal cooling water flow rate of 250B+ machine
ranges from 25 to 55 L/s. Imposing a 30 L/s lower limit would handicap the machine’s operating
envelop. Therefore, the single unit, type 257 condenser is preferred. There are some challenges
related to using a shell and tube condenser. Firstly, manifold design needs to be adjusted to
accommodate the 3.6-meter condenser length, making sure the center of gravity is well placed to
prevent toppling, and to fit the size of a standard shipping container. Furthermore, due to the use
of fined tubes, the heat transfer coefficient and surface area are difficult to deterministically
calculate. It is therefore difficult to make direct comparison between its performance and that of
existing brazed plate condenser, and to make judgement on how well the SH&T condenser would
perform after the integration. It is therefore necessary to experimentally validate the performance
of the shell and tube condenser at a variety of operating conditions.

Heat exchanger design specification
 For the standard machine configuration with single heat source, a flooded evaporator (or
a pair of flooded evaporators connected in parallel) is proposed to handle the de-subcooling, and
evaporating duty of the working fluid, as it provides great advantage in simplifying piping
structure and lowers the cost. An optional, dual-heat configuration is also considered here in case
two heat streams are available from customers. Thus, two heat exchangers connected in series are
mandatory with one handling the de-subcooling duty and the other accomplishing evaporating.
Torque 2.0 is utilized here to estimate appropriate design points for heat exchangers, under the

Pump selection
 The design specifications presented above includes the highest expected pressure and
flow rate combination, where a small 12-degree approach exists between the working fluid and
the hot water while the working fluid is at a high flow rate (23.3 kg/s) into an L type expander at
21.6 barg pressure. Therefore, such condition was used as a reference condition for pump
selection. Among all types of pumps, inline multi-stage pumps serve the purpose well. One
promising option from Grundfos is CRN 125-8 with an 8-stage propeller and 90 kW rated power
consumption. According to the pump curve, CRN 125 – 8 produces 23.5 barg of pressure at 23.3
kg/s, consuming 74 kW of electric power at a 90% pump efficiency. The pump provides enough
pressure at desired flow rate with decent amount of safety margin to overcome additional pressure
drop within heat exchangers and pipe manifold and consumes a reasonable amount of electric
power. Other pump types, and final selection will be evaluated in the coming weeks.
 After lots of discussions with other vendors, we have decided to go with the Grundfos
CRN 125-8 listed above. There are two drawbacks to this pump – First the FFKM o-rings that
we have been using on the smaller pumps are not available in this size. We have ordered the
pump with FKM o-rings and are requesting a sample of them for compatibility testing. We are
looking into aftermarket shaft seals in case the o-rings do not perform well. The second problem
is the NPSHr (Net Positive Suction Head required) for this pump. This unit requires about 11 feet
of liquid column to prevent cavitation. This is the main reason we kept looking for alternatives,
we are trying to keep the height down. See more detail on this topic and possible solutions in
Task 2.8 – Design Of “Next Gen ORC” System:.

 Our goal in the above analysis was to find the “worst” case scenario for the working fluid
pump. The pump selected would meet this set of conditions. However, now that we are
modeling more cases with the update tool we are finding that we are never actually required to
run at this “worst case”. The worst case is created with the largest expander volume ration is
coupled with the highest temperatures input temperatures. What we are finding in the real cases
is that when we have the highest inlet temperatures, we get much better results with a smaller
expander volume ration, this gives us much better thermal efficiency. This result was a little
surprising, and we have gone back to the pump curves to see if we could find a smaller pump that
would satisfy theses real world working conditions that we are seeing come up in our test cases.
Using a smaller pump would save a significant amount of money on the cost of the equipment
and would consume less power making the net output of the ORC higher. This would give us a
competitive advantage, so the exact details of the selection are considered proprietary and are
therefore not presented here. We can set up a webinar to review the details of the selection upon
request.

The Recipient will subject new fluids that pass chemical compatibility testing to a longevity
test. In this test, a small compressor will be loaded and run with the test fluid and oil for
approximately 1,000 hours. The compressor will then be evaluated for signs of wear or
material incompatibility.

Our parent company BITZER has started lab scale lubricity testing of new refrigerants and oil
mixtures. These tests involve measuring the force needed to rotate a shaft against “V” blocks
with the different amounts of force applied. No project funds are being applied to this work in
Germany. We will benefit from this testing because only fluid mixtures that pass will be tested in
our full scale unit.

The lab testing was delayed due to reduced staff and backlog at the lab. However, our testing has
finally started, the test apparatus has been constructed, and they have achieved a baseline for the
different refrigerants without oil. Not surprisingly the pure refrigerants all showed little to no
lubricity. We should start getting data in the coming weeks for the oil refrigeration mixtures.

Our initial intent for the inhouse portion of this testing was to use a small compressor and load it
to run with the new fluids. We started working on the design and could not come up with an
appropriate simulant using compressors. The test rig was becoming very complex and was

requiring similar hardware to what a full ORC would need. Additionally, there are concerns
about the actual heat and bearing loading of a compressor vs. an expander. They are similar, but
not the same, and we were concerned about getting good reliable load data from a compressor.

We have abandoned the idea of using a compressor test stand and are instead building a small
ORC out of some surplus heat exchangers. The design and most of the fabrication has been
completed for this small test ORC, as you can see blow in Figure 14. Using a small ORC has the
added benefit of being able to test the performance of these new fluids while we are determining
the longevity of the refrigerant oil mixture.

We are outfitting this test stand with mass flow meters so that the true flow rate going to the
expander will be known. This is an area of uncertainty in our calculations. When we were only

Now that we can model the performance of the Next Generation Prototype, we have been looking
hard at the cases where the cold side of the cycle is elevated to be useful for the building. In most
CHP applications that ElectraTherm has investigated, the cooling source temperature is around
65-80 oC to produce 85 – 100 oC water leaving the ORC for heating purposes. One big

pressure difference across the expander and higher potential of exceeding the pressure limit.

 Figure 24: Next Gen ORC Major Components

One challenge with this new unit will be supplying the NPSHr (Net Positive Suction Head
required) of this larger feed pump. The pump requires approximately 11 feet of liquid column to
prevent cavitation. This would make the overall assembly quite large and difficult to transport,
however not so large that it is unreasonable. We are moving forward with this pump knowing
that we can elevate the condenser to meet the NPSHr of the pump for the prototype. However,
we are also going to investigate ways to satisfy the NPSHr without the full liquid column. We
have come up with two methods that will be investigated / tested next quarter
1. Use the condenser to sub-cool the liquid refrigerant. If the cycle can be designed and run
 to maintain subcooled liquid at the condenser outlet this should lower the pressure
 required to satisfy the NPSHr of the feed pump. The point of the liquid column is to sub-
 cool the fluid at the pump inlet to prevent flashing to vapor (cavitation) in the low

 pressure created by the impeller. The liquid column increases the pressure at the pump
 inlet, but not the temperature, this creates the required amount of subcooling to prevent
 cavitation. If we can start with liquid at the condenser outlet that is already subcooled
 than we should need less liquid column. We will be modifying an old test unit to lower
 the condenser below the liquid column requirement for that unit’s feed pump. We will
 then test the system under different conditions to see if the are able to prevent cavitation
 by subcooling the fluid at the condenser outlet.
 1. We have done significant testing with a standard unit at our facility to investigate
 the possibility of using subcooled fluid to satisfy the NPSHr of the feed pump.
 While this is technically possible, we have found that there is a significant
 performance hit associated with this method. This work was primarily related to
 trying to minimize the footprint of our standard project, and beyond the scope of
 this project, however the findings apply to this topic. We have additional data on
 this topic that can be shared upon request but is considered proprietary and is
 therefore not included here. The result is that we will not be perusing utilizing
 subcooled fluid out of the condenser to satisfy the feed pump NPSHr. We will be
 designing the Next Gen ORC with sufficient gravity head, or using a booster
 pump as discussed below.
2. Adding a booster pump in series with the main pump. The booster pump would need to
 be rated for the same flow rate, but a much smaller head. The head generated by the
 booster pump only needs to be enough to satisfy the NPSHr of the main pump. We have

The first B plus machine is in a dual-heat configuration. The dual-heat configuration aims to
preserve exergy for the power plants by providing the ORC multiple heat streams at different

working fluid is also at the coolest point, and to allow high-temperature heat stream supplied
exclusively to evaporator for optimized working fluid pressure elevation before entering the
expander. The ability run in a dual heat configuration could become very important to CHP
application where we need to preserve the high temperatures for other thermal users.

 The modeling effort is carried on to which Torque 2.0 is further refined and expanded for
dual-heat configuration. The modeling of dual-heat stream configuration is similar in large to the
single-heat stream configuration, discussed earlier, deploying the same in-house developed
evaporator and condenser models to estimate outlet conditions (temperature, quality) based on
inlet conditions (temperature, flow rate, quality). Due to the decoupling between pre-heater and

evaporator, the additional pre-heater model is required to accommodate the extra heat source and
to estimate its effect on the cycle performance. The pre-heater model also utilizes the standard
𝜀𝜀 − 𝑁𝑁𝑁𝑁𝑁𝑁 method to calculate temperature variation on both sides of the heat exchanger. Thanks
to the fact that only single-phase heat transfer occurs within the pre-heater, no additional
boundary condition is required to complete the iterative thermodynamic calculation of the
Rankine Cycle. The modified process thus initiates, as well, with an initial guess of evaporator
inlet condition, followed by the iterative call to the evaporator model, condenser model, expander
model and to the additional pre-heater model till a converged evaporator inlet (or pre-heater
outlet) condition is found.
 The model is then validated against the test data, replicating the site condition from
Pohnpei. Figure 30 demonstrates comparison of key performance parameters between test results
and model predictions, including water outlet temperature from pre-heater, evaporator and
condenser, amount of thermal power exchanged within above three heat exchangers, the working
fluid pressure at the expander inlet/outlet, and the gross electric power output from the expansion.

temperature variation within three heat exchangers, yielding very similar amount of thermal
power exchanged, shown in Figure 2 (b). The small difference in thermal power consumption and
rejection seen in Figure 2 (b) could come from the slightly different method in calculating
thermal properties, including specific heat and density, adopted by the model, and some
additional heat loss during testing that is not incorporated in the model. Overall, the magnitude of
the difference is rather small, and does not impose noticeable disturbance to the cycle
performance and the electric power output estimation. With accurate temperature estimation, the

 The long-lasting challenge of modeling the ORC cycle emerges from the fact that each
individual component is interlocked with others that forms a floating loop with insufficient
boundary conditions to solve for the performance. The previous system performance model
(Torque) was developed empirically based on a large number of system operation tests below 120
℃. However, the development of the new B+ product line operates above 120 ℃ with a high
temperature limit of 150 ℃. Therefore, to ensure the accuracy of performance estimation outside
the previously tested range and reduce the dependency on large-quantity testing, a more physics-
based model (Torque 2.0) is developed.
 The development of Torque 2.0 employed a proprietarily innovative method, based on
ElectraTherm’s operational experience, to determine the necessary boundary conditions for
performance modeling of cycle components including the evaporator, expander, and condenser.
Models of the evaporator and condenser are also developed in-house, using standard 𝜀𝜀 − 𝑁𝑁𝑁𝑁𝑁𝑁
method, where outlet conditions (temperature, quality) are obtained based on inlet conditions
(temperature, flow rate, quality). The new expander model is semi-empirical where the
correlation between output shaft work and inlet/outlet pressure difference is established based on
non-ideal polytropic expansion. The non-ideality is accounted for by empirically obtained
polytropic exponent. Given an initial guess of evaporator inlet condition, the evaporator model,
condenser model and expander model run in sequence until a converged condition is found.
 Torque 2.0 is validated against test data, comparing gross power output and process fluid
exit temperatures from evaporator and condenser. Fig. 1 presents the comparison of gross power
between ones obtained from testing and ones from tuned/untuned Torque 2.0 estimation and
Smarter tool estimation. The Smarter tool is the packaged version of previous Torque model, used
by the sales team to provide a quick estimation on cycle operation and power output based on
conditions of hot/cold sources from clients. The limitation of Smarter tool is inherited from
Torque, where validity of prediction above 120 ℃ is unknown, and not adjustable without a large
set of testing to reproduce the empirical correlations. The untuned Torque 2.0 as a physics-based
model uses Nusselt number correlation in the open literature for plate heat exchanger to estimate
evaporator and condenser performance. However, coefficients of those correlations were
determined empirically for our specific heat exchangers. If the heat exchangers used on our final
B+ design perform slightly different that offsets the performance estimation. Therefore, the tuned
version of Torque 2.0 is introduced so that the coefficients in Nusselt number correlation are
altered slightly to match the predicted gross power closer with test data.

Fig. 1 – Gross power comparison between test data and tuned/untuned Torque 2.0 estimation and
 Smarter tool estimation

 As seen from Fig. 1, Smarter tool prediction closely matches the gross power output at
hot source inlet temperature below 100 ℃, followed by under-prediction till temperature reaches
130 ℃ , switching into over-prediction. The untuned Torque 2.0 shows slight underprediction at
below 100 ℃, overpredicts gross power as temperature increases, and ends with a close match at
above 120 ℃. Based on the temperature/power variation observed from untuned Torque 2.0, the
Nusselt number correlation is adjusted, yielding a tuned Torque 2.0 showing close match within
entire temperature range. The untuned Torque 2.0 provides confidence that essential thermo-
fluidic and thermodynamic performance are well modeled, leading to a closely followed trend in
power generation. The tuning practice improves the model further and demonstrates a valid
method in producing close-matching model prediction with very few test data points.
 With closely matching power generation prediction, prediction of process fluid exit
temperature of evaporator and condenser by Torque 2.0 is then compared with test data, shown in
Fig. 2. As expected, the model predicts temperature well, within 5 degrees of difference
throughout the entire temperature range. The close matching in both temperature and power
generation prediction indicates that the expander is modeled correctly, so that under specific
temperature with corresponding pressure, the shaft work is calculated accurately, yielding power
output estimation close to reality.

 Additional validation on the tuned model with high-power output test cases are shown in

APPENDIX IV – TEST PLAN FOR NEW WORKING FLUIDS

BACKGROUND
 Halogenated refrigerants, namely chlorofluorocarbons, first synthesized in 1928 by
Thomas Midgley, Jr. i This new chemistry, now marked as first-generation refrigerants, solved the
problem of the inherent flammability of natural refrigerants commonly used prior.
 As use of refrigeration, thus refrigerants, became more widespread, the consequences of
these incredibly stable chemicals became apparent. The environmental hazard these chemicals
pose was first publicized in 1974 by Molina and Rowland at the University of California, Irvine.
This landmark paper outlined the chlorine catalyzed destruction of ozone caused by photolytic
byproducts of these chemicals in the upper atmosphere. ii The Montreal Protocol on Substances
that Deplete the Ozone Layer, passed in 1987 and signed by 46 countries, required the steady
phase out of ozone depleting CFCs across the globe. This piece of legislation has been touted for
its success and universal ratification; the ozone has been repleted as CFC use diminished. iii
 The chlorine molecules in CFCs were determined to participate in the ozone depletion
reaction; in the next generation of refrigerants designed to replace CFCs, it was removed and
replaced by more fluorine molecules, creating hydrofluorocarbon (HFC) refrigerants. HFCs have
dominated the refrigerant market for the past 20 years,
 Although they are non-ozone depleting, HFCs act as powerful greenhouse gasses. They
are anticipated to contribute significantly to climate forcing iv, due in part to their stability and
long atmospheric lifetime. This prompted the Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol,
signed in 2016 and effective in 2019, which sets to cut the use of HFCs by 80% by 2050. v
 The Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC) uses refrigerants as a working fluid due to their
volatility at relatively low temperatures, high vapor pressures, and favorable heat transfer
properties. Consequently, the increasing regulations surrounding the sale of HFC refrigerants is of
vital interest to those employing ORC technology as it will impact both the longevity and cost of
the operation. The current refrigerant used in ElectraTherm ORCs is R245fa, identified by the
Kigali Amendment as a controlled substance. Identifying and employing a drop-in replacement
for HFCs is vital to continue deploying and maintaining operation of ORCs in the short term,
while optimizing their use creates new avenues for improving overall efficiency and performance
of the alternative working fluids.
 Next-generation refrigerants are primarily hydrofluroolefins (HFO), which are similar in
composition to currently fielded hydrofluorocarbons (HFC) except that they are unsaturated
molecules. The double bonds in HFOs react rapidly with hydroxyl radicals in the atmosphere,
vastly reducing their lifetime thus global warming potential, compared to HFCs vi .
 Unfortunately, the atmospheric breakdown products of some HFOs are problematic
themselves. Trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), a structural analog to acetic acid with all hydrogens
substituted by fluorine, can be produced by the degradation of chemicals that contain a -CF3
group. TFA is soluble in water and has garnered scrutiny due to its acidity and long lifetime in
aqueous systems vii. To avoid a potentially regrettable substitution, HFOs with known atmospheric
degradation pathways that lead to TFA formation will not be considered as viable alternatives.

OBJECTIVES
OBJECTIVE 1: Validate and implement drop-in alternative working fluid
 The primary objective for this test program is to identify and successfully implement a
drop-in replacement that is compatible with currently fielded equipment; or is compatible with
only minor adjustments to existing equipment. This replacement should comply with current and
impending regulations, avoid environmental health and safety concerns, have comparable
performance, and be compatible with existing ORC materials of construction.
1.a Identify potential alternatives
 The currently fielded working fluid, R245fa, has a critical temperature of 154.4 C. To
maintain compatibility with current operational limits, alternatives with similar critical points will
be considered as potential drop-ins. Only non-toxic and non-flammable chemicals will be
considered.
 Of the three commercially available HFOs that meet the selection criteria, R1224yd(Z) is
known to form TFA as it decomposes viii and will not be considered in this selection campaign.
 R1233zd(E) is the primary candidate for a drop-in replacement as it has the closest
reported performance to R245fa: able to achieve potentially higher cycle efficiencies, but yields
lower gross power outputs. ix
 An interesting alternative is R1336mzz(Z), as can be seen in the following figures, it has
lower vapor pressures than R245fa or R1233zd(E), it also has a lower boiling point and higher
critical temperature, which widens the potential range of operation for this fluid. For this reason,
R1336mzz(Z) will be considered less as a drop-in replacement and more an option for specific
applications. This could be of interest to CHP applications where higher grade heat is available
on the hot side of the cycle, and it is necessary to generating hotter water on the cold side of the
cycle for further use.
