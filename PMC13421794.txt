The Impact of Rurality on Durable Medical Equipment Access and Discharge Planning for Tracheostomy and Ventilation Care in Nebraska
Authors: Karissa Nichols, Kayley Anderson, Morgan Bintner, Teresa Rodriguez, Jana Wardian, Jayme R. Dowdall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective(s)Recognize how rurality impacts safe discharge planning and home management for complex airway patients. This study explores the distribution of durable medical equipment (DME) companies for patients with tracheostomies and/or ventilators, aiming to identify patterns in service access statewide.MethodsKey informant phone surveys assessed active DME companies statewide. DME companies were identified through hospital referral lists, discharge directories, and online searches. Data collected included service radius, respiratory supplies, and ventilation types. Geographic information system (GIS) mapping visualized coverage. Rural–urban status was classified using rural–urban commuting area (RUCA) codes.ResultsOf 16 companies identified, 6 were closed, leaving 50 active locations. Among these, 22% (n = 11) support invasive ventilation (average service radius 168.8 km [104.9 mi], range 80.5–402.3 km [50–250 mi]), 52% (n = 26) serve tracheostomy patients (average service radius 129.7 km [80.6 mi], range 68.9–289.7 km [42.8–180 mi]), and 88% (n = 44) offer noninvasive ventilation, such as CPAP/BiPAP (average service radius 116.5 km [72.4 mi], range 25.3–289.7 km [15.7–180 mi]). Among invasive ventilation locations, 18% (n = 2) are rural (RUCA 4–10) and 82% (n = 9) urban (RUCA 1–3). For tracheostomy, 46% (n = 12) are rural and 54% (n = 14) urban.ConclusionGeographic disparities limit DME access for rural patients requiring tracheostomy and invasive ventilation. Surgeons should be aware that patients may live over 1.5 h from a capable DME company. Presence of a DME company does not ensure a specific location offers specialized supplies or equipment. Realistic discharge planning is essential to ensure care continuity, reduce emergency department visits, hospital readmissions, and preventable complications.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Both adult and pediatric patients with tracheostomies rely on specialized durable medical equipment (DME) and home‐based healthcare services to safely transition from hospital to home. This care is resource‐intensive, requiring reliable access to ventilators, suction equipment, humidification systems, and other respiratory supplies, as well as ongoing support from skilled professionals such as respiratory therapists and nurses [1, 2, 3]. Access to these essential resources varies substantially by geographic location, insurance status, and workforce availability, often placing significant strain on caregivers and increasing the risk of fragmented care [4, 5, 6]. While pediatric and adult patients may differ in underlying diagnoses and care trajectories, DME services for tracheostomy and ventilatory support are typically delivered through shared regional infrastructure. Accordingly, this study evaluates system‐level access across both populations. Pediatric patients may nonetheless require additional caregiver coordination and discharge support.

Given the complexity and resource intensity of this care, the transition from hospital to home represents a particularly vulnerable period. Caregivers frequently report difficulty obtaining equipment, navigating insurance coverage, and securing consistent in‐home clinical support [7]. Inadequate access to DME, qualified personnel, or timely service delivery may lead to airway‐related adverse events, increased emergency department utilization, rehospitalization, and mortality [5, 8, 9, 10, 11]. These challenges may be amplified in rural communities, where provider density is lower, travel distances are greater, and specialized personnel are limited [7, 12].

Despite the critical role of DME providers in ensuring safe discharge and long‐term stability for patients with tracheostomies, there is limited published data describing the geographic distribution, service capacity, and accessibility of these providers—particularly in states with large rural populations [4]. Understanding the spatial availability of DME services is essential for identifying gaps in care, informing discharge planning, and guiding policy efforts to promote equitable access [13, 14].

Nebraska presents a relevant case study due to its large rural geography and reliance on a limited number of tertiary care centers concentrated in more urban areas. Mapping the distribution and service coverage of DME companies that provide tracheostomy and ventilation support may reveal geographic disparities that affect patient safety and discharge feasibility.

The objective of this study was to characterize the availability, service capacity, and geographic accessibility of DME providers offering tracheostomy and ventilation support across Nebraska, with the goal of informing discharge planning and identifying structural barriers to care in rural populations more broadly.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional study used key informant telephone surveys to assess active DME companies operating in Nebraska. The study focused on characterizing service availability and geographic accessibility relevant to discharge planning from Children's Nebraska and Nebraska Medicine. This study was reviewed and determined not to constitute human subject research; therefore, institutional review board (IRB) approval was not required.

Recruitment and Eligibility

Eligible participants were active DME companies operating within Nebraska. Companies were required to be reachable by telephone and willing to respond to survey questions. Companies that could not be contacted after three telephone attempts or did not respond to voicemail messages were considered inactive or inaccessible and were excluded.

The initial list of DME companies was obtained from a key informant involved in complex discharges at Children's Nebraska, the state's only full‐service pediatric tertiary care center. This list was cross‐referenced with companies identified by Nebraska Medicine, a large regional health network. Additional companies were identified through Internet‐based directories to enhance comprehensiveness. Cross‐referencing ensured that surveyed companies reflected those most likely to be involved in discharge planning from either institution. Although the initial list originated from pediatric discharge planning resources, the study evaluated DME company services for all patients requiring tracheostomy and/or ventilation support, including both adult and pediatric populations.

Data Collection

Each company was contacted by telephone up to three times. Companies not reached after a third unsuccessful attempt were excluded from the study. Survey responses were obtained from personnel with operational knowledge of respiratory services, typically a branch manager or supervising/head respiratory therapist. Initial phone operators were asked to direct calls to the most appropriate representative familiar with tracheostomy and ventilation services. A standardized questionnaire was administered to company representatives. Companies were first asked about general tracheostomy and ventilation services for adult patients and then whether pediatric patients were additionally served. Companies that described service areas using geographic boundaries (e.g., named towns or directional limits) were geocoded, and distances from the company's location to each boundary were calculated using QGIS (version 3.4). The mean of these distances was used as the effective service radius. For companies reporting service areas in travel time (hours), values were converted to miles using an assumed average driving speed of 60 mi/h, reflecting typical Nebraska road conditions. All responses were recorded in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Follow‐up calls were conducted as needed for clarification, and additional information was incorporated into the dataset.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize company characteristics, including the proportion serving pediatric patients, types of respiratory services offered, average service radius, staffing availability, and insurance acceptance. Given the descriptive and exploratory nature of this study, which aimed to characterize DME availability and geographic access rather than test predefined hypotheses, no formal power analysis was performed, and analyses were limited to descriptive statistics; and demographic and socioeconomic variables (e.g., race, ethnicity, and income) were not collected or analyzed.

Geographic accessibility and spatial distribution were analyzed using QGIS (version 3.4). Rurality was classified using rural–urban commuting area (RUCA) codes, a validated method incorporating population density and commuting patterns. This approach is particularly relevant in Nebraska, where approximately one‐third of residents live in rural areas, and most counties are nonmetropolitan [15, 16]. Results

Sixteen DME companies comprising 50 active service locations were identified across Nebraska. Three companies were identified through additional Internet‐based searches beyond the initial hospital referral list. Six locations were identified as closed and excluded from analysis.

Service Availability

Among the 50 active locations, 52% (26/50) provided tracheostomy support, 88% (44/50) provided NIV, and 22% (11/50) supported invasive ventilation. Average reported service radii differed by service type. Locations providing invasive ventilation reported the largest average service radius (168.8 km [104.9 mi]; range 80.5–402.3 km [50–250 mi]). Given that Nebraska spans approximately 430 mi east to west, the maximum reported service radius represents a substantial proportion of the state's total geographic width. Tracheostomy support locations reported an average service radius of 129.7 km [80.6 mi] (range 68.9–289.7 km [42.8–180 mi]), while NIV locations reported an average service radius of 116.5 km [72.4 mi] (range 25.3–289.7 km [15.7–180 mi]). Rural–Urban Distribution

Rural–urban distribution varied by service type (Table 2). Rural–urban status was classified using RUCA codes, where codes 1–3 represent urban areas and codes 4–10 represent rural areas. Among locations serving tracheostomy and NIV patients, 53% were in urban areas and 47% in rural areas. This disparity widened for invasive ventilation services, with 82% of locations in urban areas and 18% in rural areas.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a clear gradient in DME service availability and levels of respiratory support across Nebraska. While most surveyed locations provide NIV and over half support tracheostomy care, fewer than one quarter offer services for patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. This pattern likely reflects the escalating clinical, operational, and workforce demands associated with invasive ventilation relative to lower‐acuity respiratory support [3, 17, 18]. Invasive ventilation requires ventilator inventory management, advanced troubleshooting capacity, respiratory therapist oversight, and often coordination with skilled nursing services—resources that are more costly and labor‐intensive to sustain than those required for tracheostomy care without mechanical ventilation or NIV.

Prior literature has documented significant barriers to obtaining tracheostomy‐related DME, including insurance limitations, coverage gaps, and supply delays [4, 19]. Caregiver‐centered studies further emphasize the complexity of coordinating home‐based care and the vulnerability of families during the hospital‐to‐home transition [5, 7]. However, much of the existing work focuses on insurance and care coordination barriers, with less attention to the geographic distribution and structural capacity of DME providers themselves [20]. By mapping service locations, radii, and density across Nebraska, this study adds a spatial dimension to the understanding of access, suggesting that geographic supply constraints may represent an additional and underrecognized determinant of safe discharge. Similar geographic challenges may exist in other states with large rural populations, though the degree of disparity likely varies based on regional healthcare infrastructure, workforce distribution, and reimbursement environments [12, 14].

Although this study focuses on Nebraska, the challenges identified are likely not unique to this region. Rural areas across the United States experience similar barriers related to geographic distance, limited healthcare infrastructure, and reduced access to specialized services [21, 22]. In pediatric tracheostomy populations, social and geographic factors have also been shown to influence access to post‐discharge resources and care outcomes, highlighting the complexity of supporting technology‐dependent individuals [23, 24]. More broadly, disparities in access to home health and supportive services between rural and urban settings further underscore these structural challenges [25]. Together, these findings suggest that the barriers observed in Nebraska may reflect broader patterns in rural healthcare delivery, while the single‐state design provides detailed regional insight into DME access and discharge planning challenges.

The finding that locations providing invasive ventilation reported the largest service radii, extending up to 250 mi, likely reflects provider scarcity rather than expanded access. In rural states, large coverage areas may translate into longer travel times for equipment setup, maintenance, and emergency troubleshooting. For ventilator‐dependent patients, who are among the most medically fragile individuals discharged to home care, delays in equipment replacement or malfunction response carry potentially life‐threatening consequences [8, 9, 10]. Existing evidence links gaps in DME access and home respiratory support to airway‐related adverse events, rehospitalization, and increased healthcare utilization [4, 10]. When coupled with extended travel distances and limited local workforce availability, geographic strain within the DME system may further compound these risks.

Urban–rural distribution patterns reinforce this concern. Although tracheostomy and NIV services were relatively balanced between rural and urban locations, invasive ventilation services were disproportionately concentrated in urban centers. High‐resource respiratory programs require capital investment, stable patient volume, and access to specialized respiratory therapists—conditions more readily sustained in metropolitan areas [12, 14]. Rural providers may face smaller patient populations, reimbursement constraints, and workforce shortages that limit their ability to maintain invasive ventilation services. In a predominantly rural state, such concentration may influence discharge feasibility, prolong hospital stays while services are arranged, or require families to rely on distant providers with expansive coverage territories.

The geographic differences observed in this study should be interpreted within the context of regionalized delivery of complex pediatric care. Concentration of higher‐acuity services in higher‐resource centers is often appropriate to support specialized expertise, maintain clinical volume, and ensure workforce sustainability, and families of medically complex children may appropriately relocate or travel to access these services. These patterns therefore reflect inherent trade‐offs among access, resource allocation, and system efficiency rather than representing disparities in isolation. However, even when clinically appropriate, geographic concentration of services may introduce logistical and financial burdens for patients and families. Accordingly, efforts to improve access should consider both feasibility and broader healthcare priorities, recognizing that complete geographic parity in high‐acuity services may not be realistic within current system constraints.

The provider landscape is further shaped by instability within the DME network. During data collection, multiple service locations from a large national provider were identified as closed and excluded from analysis. These closures occurred across both rural and urban regions of the state, underscoring that provider sustainability may be influenced by broader market pressures, reimbursement structures, and workforce constraints rather than geography alone [4, 20]. In rural communities, the loss of a single location may substantially increase travel distances and concentrate demand among remaining providers. Even in urban areas, consolidation reduces redundancy within the system, limiting flexibility during periods of high demand or staffing shortages. The dynamic nature of the provider network highlights that geographic access to DME services is not static, further complicating discharge planning for medically complex patients.

Taken together, these findings suggest that access to tracheostomy and ventilation support is shaped not only by insurance approval or caregiver preparedness, but also by the spatial distribution, workforce capacity, and economic viability of DME providers [12, 26]. For patients requiring invasive ventilation, geographic and structural constraints may represent a hidden barrier to safe home‐based care. Recognizing DME infrastructure as a determinant of post‐discharge safety may inform policy strategies aimed at strengthening rural respiratory therapy workforce pipelines, aligning reimbursement with the demands of high‐acuity home ventilation, and developing regional coordination models that promote equitable access to essential respiratory support services.

Limitations

This study represents a cross‐sectional assessment of DME providers at a single point in time; provider availability may change as locations open or close. Data were obtained through structured telephone surveys and relied on self‐report, which may introduce reporting variability. Service radii were standardized using mileage conversions from travel time or geographic boundaries, which may not fully reflect real‐world travel conditions or operational constraints. In addition, geographic inclusion within a reported service radius does not guarantee timely equipment delivery, staffing capacity, or functional access at the time of need. Given the descriptive and exploratory design of this study, analyses were limited to descriptive statistics and were not intended to evaluate statistically significant differences between groups. In addition, demographic and socioeconomic variables, including race, ethnicity, and income, were not collected or analyzed and therefore could not be incorporated into assessments of geographic disparities in DME access. As this analysis was conducted within a single predominantly rural state, findings may not be fully generalizable to regions with different healthcare infrastructure, population density, or DME delivery systems.

Conclusion

In Nebraska, the availability of DME services for tracheostomy and ventilation varies substantially by level of respiratory support, with invasive ventilation services both less prevalent and more geographically concentrated than lower‐acuity care. Larger service radii and urban clustering suggest structural and workforce constraints that may limit equitable access for medically complex patients, particularly in rural communities. By mapping provider distribution and service capacity, this study highlights geographic infrastructure as a critical yet underrecognized determinant of safe hospital discharge and sustained home‐based respiratory care. Strengthening the stability, workforce capacity, and regional coordination of DME services may be essential to improving equitable access and supporting vulnerable patients transitioning from hospital to home.
