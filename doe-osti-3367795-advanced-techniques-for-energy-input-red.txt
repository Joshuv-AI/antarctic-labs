Executive Summary
 Gypsum wallboards are a ubiquitous construction material in the United States, and the
 production of these wallboards is an energy-intensive process. The work detailed in this report
 aimed to reduce the energy consumption of the gypsum wallboard drying process by ~65%
 by reducing the amount of excess water used in the early stages of board production. In
 gypsum wallboard production, gypsum is first converted into stucco, typically by direct gas-
 fired calcination. After that, water and additives are mixed in with the stucco to form a
 flowable slurry that is poured onto backing paper. A top layer of paper is added, and the stucco
 is given some time to react with the water to convert back into gypsum. The final board is
 then dried to remove all excess water. The amount of water required to keep the slurry fluid
 enough to be processible far exceeds the amount of water required for the stucco reaction.
 Reducing the water requirement for the slurry would require control of the particle size
 distribution, allowing the same fluidity at a lower water requirement, and, in turn, would
 reduce the overall water consumption of the process. Such reduction would also reduce the
 amount of energy required to dry the final wallboard product, as having to remove less water
 would require proportionally less energy.
 The approach this project focused on to achieve the reduction in excess water use and
 associated energy consumption was the use of dielectric heating, in particular focused on
 microwave and radio frequency (RF) sources. In support of this approach, we developed a
 model which can match experimentally measured moisture removal and calcination of
 gypsum in a RF oven. We also explored a wide design space to determine the practicality of
 generating disintegration-resistant stucco with dielectric calcination. And we also developed
 a slurry rheology model which, while it does not give direct quantitative predictions of yield
 stress or viscosity, can determine if a given particle size distribution (PSD) of gypsum or
 stucco particles at a particular water loading will produce a slurry that is more or less fluid
 than another PSD at the same water loading. Ultimately, we were unable to demonstrate a
 process that produced the disintegration-resistant stucco that would give an easy path to water
 reduction in the slurry formulation step. However, we were able to significantly drive forward
 research in the areas of dielectric calcination and slurry rheology. These efforts can also help
 unlock opportunities for water and energy reduction for wallboard production if an alternate
 method of producing non-disintegrating stucco is found to be commercially viable.
 The project was an excellent candidate to drive forward the innovation and adoption of
 technologies that can cost-effectively reduce energy consumption. The production of 1 ton of
 gypsum board requires ~445 kWh in drying energy, which represents ~2/3 of the overall
 energy consumption in the manufacturing process. The energy is usually produced by direct
 gas firing, leading to CO2 emissions. Some work had previously been done to produce stucco
 by dielectric heating, but there is limited study on the use of this stucco in wallboard
 production. Specifically, no work had been found which examined the water requirement of
 slurries formed with stucco produced by dielectric heating. The production of low-water-
 requirement stucco by other methods and control of the particle size distribution by milling
 and related technologies is mature. Nonetheless, this knowledge has not been applied to
 industrial wall board production because of the inability of current calcination techniques to
 produce high quality dehydrated particles at sufficient throughput.
 The planned approach was to use dielectric heating in producing disintegration-resistant
 stucco by nature of its gentler volumetric heating compared to the convective gas-fired
 incumbent technology. This stucco would have a lower water requirement due to the reduction
 Page 5 of 57

 in the amount of fine particles (<1μm) present, as fines are understood to have a large impact
 on the fluidity of a slurry. To study this calcination process, two dielectric calciners were
 commissioned: a lab-scale RF calciner was commissioned at Georgia Institute of Technology
 and a pilot-scale RF calciner was commissioned at Saint-Gobain. A model was developed that
 could reliably recreate the drying, heating, and calcination curves generated experimentally
 on the Prototype A calciner. The model incorporated the effects of dielectric heating, moisture
 diffusion, and external convection. Model parameters including the kinetics of calcination,
 the dielectric properties of gypsum and stucco, and moisture mobility were measured
 independently.
 A strong link was established between the particle size distribution and the rheology of
 the slurries. The typical test used for fluidity of a stucco slurry is the slump test. In this test, a
 hollow cylinder is filled with slurry and then lifted to allow the slurry to flow out through the
 bottom to form a shallow disk of stagnant fluid. The size and shape of the disk is determined
 by the yield stress and viscosity of the fluid. A tight correlation was found between the yield
 stress and viscosity of the stucco slurries; therefore, modeling either one would give a good
 correlation to the overall performance in the slump test. It is well understood that as the
 particle loading of any size particle increases, the fluidity of the slurry decreases. Fine
 particles, especially those at or below 1μm in diameter, have a disproportionately high impact
 on the fluidity. This impact can be characterized by adding an interaction distance to the
 particles. With the interaction distance added, the max particle packing can be calculated
 using the Funk-Dinger model. At higher max packing fraction, the slurry is more fluid (i.e.
 lower viscosity and yield stress).
 Tests were carried out on both prototype calciners to understand the impact of target
 sample temperature, air temperature, calcination duration, particle size, and humidity. The
 target sample temperature was found to be the most sensitive parameter in determining the
 amount of calcium sulfate hemihydrate (stucco) and calcium sulfate anhydrite produced from
 calcium sulfate dihydrate (gypsum), with higher temperatures yielding higher conversion
 fractions to stucco and a higher likelihood of over-calcination to anhydrite. Air temperature
 was found to impact the uniformity of the calcination, with air temperatures matched to target
 sample temperatures yielding the best uniformity. This effect was due to the interplay between
 convective heat transfer at the surface of the sample and the volumetric RF heating.
 Calcination time impacted the total conversion of gypsum into stucco, but it was not as
 impactful as sample temperature. The impact of particle size was studied by looking at
 calcination differences between gypsum with a median particle diameter of ~80μm compared
 to gypsum with a median particle diameter of ~2mm. It was found that for a temperature and
 duration where the 80μm sample had fully calcined, the ~2mm sample was not fully calcined.
 After calcination, the ~2mm gypsum/stucco blend was sieved to isolate particles above 3.4mm
 and below 0.85mm. It was found that the <0.85mm particles were significantly more calcined
 than the >3.4mm samples. Altogether, this indicated that despite the volumetric heating, large
 particles were more resistant to calcination. Humidity was studied by placing the gypsum in
 an enclosed container. Humidity was found to produce stucco that had lower specific surface
 area. All stucco produced was found to disintegrate readily under shear during the slurry
 formation step, resulting in slurries that had the same or higher water requirement. The only
 slurries that appeared to have disintegration resistance were those with a large fraction of
 gypsum, which does not disintegrate, remaining uncalcined. It was concluded that the non-
 disintegrating particles in the slurry were likely to be gypsum, with all the stucco particles
 present disintegrating as normal.

Introduction
 The project’s aim was to demonstrate energy savings of ~63% in gypsum drying by using
 a set of complementary technologies at the benchtop and pilot scales. Dielectric heating
 technology was applied as the driving energy source for calcination.
 Originally the project targeted microwave technology (MW), but we were unable to
 achieve the targeted calcination with MW. Instead, the sister technology, radiofrequency (RF)
 heating, was used to reliably deliver enough energy into the gypsum crystals to achieve
 complete calcination into stucco. Calcium sulfate is mostly transparent to MW and RF; thus,
 energy is transferred directly to water molecules within the gypsum structure, causing
 volumetric heating, a much more efficient process than convective heating. In addition to
 being more efficient, we expect that MW and RF heating of gypsum will lead to a
 preponderance of higher quality hemihydrate crystal and grain production, significantly
 reducing the associated energy requirements in subsequent drying steps. The production of
 non-disintegrating calcium hemihydrate particles with low water demand would unlock a new
 optimization lever of particle size distribution to further reduce water and energy demand.
 The technical baseline and proposed processes are shown in Figure 1.
 The critical success factors for the proposed technology are reduced energy and water
 consumption, and CO2 emissions. The expected metrics for the proposed process are
 compared with those of the baseline in Table 1.
 Production of higher quality calcium sulfate hemihydrate crystals using microwave
 energy had been demonstrated in literature1, although rigorous quantification of the benefits
 of microwave-driven dehydration compared to the baseline technology is still needed,
 including the impact on slurry fluidity. This project was expected to develop a lab-scale
 continuous or batch microwave calciner to produce high quality crystals with a reduction in
 water demand by ~50%, while advancing the technology towards commercialization.
 The effect of high-quality hemihydrate particle size distributions on the consistency of the
 stucco/water mix is known2, and technologies to crush mineral particles are mature.
 Nonetheless, this knowledge has not been applied to industrial wall board production because
 of the inability of current calcination techniques to produce high quality dehydrated particles
 at sufficient throughput, leaving the technology at a low readiness level. The project was
 expected to make significant progress on the particle size optimization through poly-disperse
 distributions and identification of the relevant milling equipment and operating conditions to
 enable optimal distributions and increase the technology readiness level by project end.

Background
 Gypsum has been used for thousands of years in building and construction applications,
 starting with the pyramids in ancient Egypt, to “Plaster of Paris” in the midcenturies, and to
 the interior walls of modern-day houses. Gypsum plaster boards are ubiquitous for
 Page 8 of 57

 construction as they are less labor- and time- intensive. North America is one of the largest
 gypsum plaster board users in the world, with roughly half of the worldwide annual
 production capacity of 7.9 billion square meters3. Manufacturing gypsum boards is a
 worldwide industry with a market worth of $22.5 billion in 20184, and expected to grow to
 $101 billion by year 20275. Saint-Gobain is a key player in gypsum board production (with
 more than 20% share of the US Gypsum Board market) with plants spread across several
 continents, and manufacturing experience and know-how dating back to more than a century.
 The wide adoption of gypsum plaster board as wall construction material is due to the fact
 that its raw form is easy to mine, can be produced synthetically, and is desirable for fire
 protection.6 However, the processes necessary to transform gypsum from its raw form into
 its final plasterboard form are extremely energy intensive. The state of the art in the industry
 involves mining gypsum particles and reducing their sizes by milling. The gypsum particles
 then undergo calcination, which converts the dihydrate form of calcium sulfate to
 hemihydrate. The calcination products are mixed with water to form a slurry so that the slurry
 can be poured on a conveyor belt and molded to the desired geometry. The slurry is eventually
 dried to form the final product. The production of 1 ton of gypsum board requires ~445 kWh
 in drying energy, which represents ~2/3 of the overall energy consumption in the
 manufacturing process. Most production facilities employ direct natural gas-fired calcination,
 an intense heating process that leads to disintegration-prone stucco. By using advanced
 techniques such as (MW) or radiofrequency (RF) calcination and by controlling the particle
 size distribution by milling, it was estimated the energy used for gypsum board drying could
 be reduced by up to 63% (Figure 2).

Results and Discussion

Task 1: Detailed MW calcination modeling
This task was focused on modeling of the various transport mechanisms and material
transformation occuring during microwave heating of gypsum. The team was expected to conduct
a comprehensive review of the modeling methodologies for coupled heat and mass transfer inside
a porous media under microwave radiation. Based on the findings, the pertinent model assumptions
were expected to be identified and a coupled heat and mass transfer model with MW based heat
source to be developed. Eventually, the model was intended to predict the temperature and
moisture content of gypsum samples at different stages of the MW heating process. On the other
hand, the team was expected to identify testing methodologies for predicting material
transformation during calcination process, and adapt them to microwave heating calcination. In
the end, the main objective of this task was to develop gypsum calcination models and use them
as a design tool to optimize the performance and evaluate the effects of various parameters
discussed in Task 3.

  Task 1.2: Material transformation methodology
 Milestone 1.2.1: Predict the material composition of the calcined product within ±25% of
 composition obtained experimentally
 Several methods have been investigated to identify the composition of calcined products.
 To date, x-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy, and differential scanning calorimetry
 (DSC) have been the most promising methods. Raman spectroscopy has resulted in the ability
 to identify two-component mixtures between gypsum, and calcium sulfate hemihydrate
 (stucco, either non-disintegrating or disintegrating), by using the full width half-mast
 (FWHM) method which measures the width of a Raman peak at the halfway point for the
 height of the peak. By measuring the FWHM value for a specific peak that is present in stucco
 and gypsum, these values can be compared and averaged to determine the composition of a
 mixture.

 Figure 6: FWHM Values from Raman Spectroscopy for Various Two-Component Mixtures

 As shown in Figure 4, gypsum (dihydrate) has the smallest FWHM for the measured peak
 while disintegrating stucco has the largest. This is because FWHM can indicate the degree of
 crystallinity/defects, meaning the higher the FWHM value, the lower the crystallinity/higher
 the crystal defects. For disintegrating stucco, the higher FWHM is expected as SEM images
 in Figure 5 show smaller particles with a less defined structure as compared to non-
 disintegrating stucco and gypsum8. However, because FWHM for one peak is the only value
 used to identify the differences in composition, this method is unable to distinguish three-

 Milestone 3.1.2: Establish standard operating procedure for the determination of stucco

 In gypsum wallboard manufacturing facilities, the processability of stucco slurries is
 measured by operators via the slump test depicted in Figure 17. In a slump test, operators are
 fully filling a steel ring of a given geometry with a stucco slurry sampled from the line. Upon
 lifting of the ring, the slurry mass slump and the diameter of the spread slurry is measured
 using a ruler. The diameter must lie within a given range for the slurry to be deemed
 processable. This method has the advantage of being quick (the slurry sets in less than a
 minute) and simple. Nonetheless, it only provides a fluidity index and its correlation with
 rheological parameters needs to be investigated. The team decided to use the slump test as the
 technique to assess slurry fluidity because of its industrial relevance.

 Figure 17: Schematic overview of the slump test.

 In the mixers of gypsum wallboard manufacturing facilities, the stucco particles partly

 stucco slurries in the lab subject the particles to a mechanical stress similar to that in industrial

 We found that the Krieger-Dougherty model was insufficient for our real particle
 distributions which have broad particle sizes and poor discretization into size bins. Instead,
 we developed a qualitative model that can predict fluidity, as measured by slump test. The
 foundation of this model is the understanding that yield stress and viscosity trend together as
 seen in Figure 61, and that yield stress and viscosity both trend very well with slump diameter
 as seen in Figure 62.

                                                   Page 50 of 57

 Figure 61: Viscosity vs Yield stress for stucco slurries

 Figure 62: Yield stress (left) and viscosity (right) vs slump diameter

 With these relationships established, we can then look for the drivers of just one of these
 quantities to predict all of them reasonably well. The driver we are most interested in for this
 project is the particle size distribution (PSD). The link between PSD and yield stress is the
 max packing fraction (Φmax) of the powder in the slurry. Specifically, increased Φmax leads to
 decreased yield stress at a given particle loading. Neuville, et al. found this to hold true in
 gypsum suspensions.13
 Using a previously developed proprietary software we can use the Funk and Dinger
 packing model14 to predict the maximum dry packing fraction from a continuous PSD.
 However, in the pursuit of relating to the yield stress, we must instead concern ourselves with
 the behavior of the particles in solution. To account for this difference, a fitting parameter, a,
 was introduced. a represents an interaction distance, inside of which, particles cannot
 approach one another. A 2D representation of the packing that would result from different
 values of a are shown below in Figure 63. Note that in the image, the packing fraction only
 considers the dark blue particles, not the volume created by the interaction distance.

 To implement this model, the particle size distribution as measured by laser diffraction is
 adjusted by adding the desired value of a to each particle size bin value. For example, if the
 value of a was 2 μm, the particles in the 20-25 μm bin would become particles in a 22-27 μm
 bin. Next, the packing density is calculated as normal using the Funk and Dinger model. This
 calculation over-estimates the packing fraction and must next be adjusted to account for the
 dead space in medium blue in Figure 63. To figure out how much the packing fraction should
 be reduced, we tabulate the ratio between the volume of the particles based on their “true”
 particle diameter and the “inflated” particle diameter across all size bins. Then, based on the
 particle counts in each bin, we take a weighted average of that ratio. Generally, this produces
 very low predicted packings for larger values of a, especially in PSDs that have a significant
 number of particles with diameter ≤ a.
 Figure 64 below shows measured slump diameter plotted against the predicted “packing”
 based on the after-shear particle size distribution. Packing is put in quotes to indicate that this
 is not the true maximum packing of the particles in solution, as evidenced by the extreme low
 values (less than 5% volume loading) that would be predicted by this model. Instead of
 treating the predicted packing as a quantitative value, we can think of it as a representation of
 how free the particles are to move in solution at any given experimental volume loading. With
 that interpretation in mind, we can look across different values of a for a trend that appears to
 be monotonically increasing; we expect that particles that move more freely will have a larger
 slump diameter. The plot with a 5 μm value for a shows a convincing monotonically
 increasing trend.

Key Achievements
 The first achievement was to demonstrate calcination of gypsum into stucco using
 radiofrequency heating for the first time, leading to the patent application US2024246855.
 We were able to demonstrate a range of conditions that could generate >95% conversion of
 gypsum into stucco. In many cases we could go further to generate anhydrite through RF
 heating.
 The second major achievement was the development of two dielectric heating models.
 The first model accurately predicted microwave drying data from literature. The second
 accurately predicted the temperature and calcination extent over time for the Prototype A
 calciner.
 The third achievement was establishing the ability to control particle sizes and use that to
 optimize the rheology of an inert slurry. We demonstrated the ability to take an arbitrary
 distribution of large particles and use milling and air jet sieving to control the PSD to match
 the baseline PSD of plant stucco. We also showed that for spherical and prismatic particles,
 we could control the rheology by adjusting the ratio of coarse and fine particles.
 The fourth achievement was to develop three models of slurry rheology. The first was an
 analytical model that linked median particle diameters to a predicted viscosity. This model
 worked well for model particles with narrow distributions. The second was a numerical
 simulation of the slump test which linked the viscosity vs. shear data to a predicted slump test
 diameter and slump height profile. This is appropriate for taking experimental viscosity data
 and using it to predict slump behavior, allowing us to use much smaller volumes of sample in
 our analysis. The third was a qualitative model that could predict which PSDs would lead to
 the largest slump diameters. This model is appropriate for using experimentally measured
 slurry PSD data, especially post-shear PSD data, to predict slump test performance.
 The fifth achievement was to analyze the impact on calcination of a variety of calcination
 conditions including air temperature, target sample temperature, calcination duration, gypsum
 particle size, and humidity. We specifically were able to measure the extent of calcination,
 the specific surface area of the stucco produced, the disintegration resistance (or lack thereof),
 and the rheological properties of the resulting slurries.
                                                 Page 55 of 57

 The sixth achievement was to demonstrate the impact of having even a small amount of
 non-disintegrating stucco on the water gauge of processible slurry. We used commercial non-
 disintegrating stucco blended with our baseline disintegrating stucco and found that even
 retaining a small amount of large particle could have a noticeable impact on the slump
 diameter.

Benefits Assessment
 RF heating is an electrified heating method that has a major benefit of reducing on-site
 carbon emissions in processes that would otherwise use direct gas firing. This also has the
 potential to reduce overall carbon emissions if the electric grid uses low- or no-carbon energy
 sources. RF heating is adept at removing liquid or adsorbed water from a system, and it is
 naturally self-limiting in its power output in many drying scenarios. When the water is fully
 dried out, the dielectric susceptibility of the material drops substantially, reducing the
 coupling of the RF waves to the dried material. This allows you to dry very fast without
 overheating the material.
 In this project we set out to generate disintegration-resistant stucco using RF as a gentle,
 volumetrically heating alternative to direct gas-fired calcination. We were unable to produce
 disintegration-resistant stucco, so there were no water savings in the slurry formulation step,
 and thus no energy savings to the downstream drying process.

Lessons Learned
 We learned that RF heating is extremely fast and efficient for the targeted removal of
 moisture. Once the water is evaporated, it is difficult to maintain constant temperature
 during the heating. Because the RF susceptibility of a material like stucco increases with
 increasing temperature, overheating by a small amount can quickly become overheating
 by a much larger amount if the electrode plate height is not adjusted. This effect is
 amplified in the long calcination times and when operating in batch mode.

Conclusions
 This project gained valuable insight into the process of radiofrequency calcination.
 Radiofrequency heating can be used as an effective calcination method for transforming
 gypsum into stucco without producing significant impurities or over-calcination into calcium
 sulfate anhydrite. The RF drying, heating, and calcination processes can be modeled in a
 single simulation, giving insight into the calcination time required at given heating conditions.
 Under high humidity conditions, the stucco resulting from RF calcination exhibits a lower
 specific surface area. None of the stucco produced shows disintegration resistance; any
 apparent disintegration resistance was concluded to be from an increase in the amount of
 gypsum remaining in the sample.
 This project also explored the relationship between particle size distribution and slurry
 rheology. It is well understood that as the particle loading of any size particle increases, the
 fluidity of the slurry decreases. Fine particles, especially those at or below 1μm in diameter,
 have a disproportionately high impact on the fluidity. This impact can be characterized by
 adding an interaction distance to the particles. With the interaction distance added, the max
                                               Page 56 of 57

 particle packing can be calculated using the Funk-Dinger model. At higher max packing
 fraction, the slurry is more fluid (i.e. lower viscosity and yield stress).
 The energy requirement for drying was not lowered because there were no water savings
 for any process-viable stucco samples. The energy requirement of the RF calcination was
 quite high at 46.7 kWh/kg, with most of the energy going to the heating of the air. Subtracting
 out the energy required to run the auxiliary functions like the display, fans, and heated air
 leaves an energy required for RF alone of 5.0 kWh/kg which is still significantly higher than
 the energy requirement typical of gas-fired calcination. Therefore, implementing RF
 calcination as it is presented here would increase the overall energy consumption of the
 process.

Future Work/Recommendations
 No commercialization is planned given the results described in earlier sections. RF is a
 promising technology for electrifying drying operations, but its calcination performance is not
 strong enough to consider commercialization at this time. It is suggested future work for RF
 heating should focus on drying applications.
