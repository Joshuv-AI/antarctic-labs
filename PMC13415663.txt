Letter to the Editor: C2 schwannoma resection
Authors: Atul Goel
Published: 2026

Sir,

I watched the video presentation on the surgical management of a large C2 schwannoma with great interest, as this subject has been a focus of my clinical and academic work for nearly four decades [1–4]. It was, however, disappointing to note that the authors did not acknowledge our contributions to this field [2–4].

The authors describe their tumor as being "extradural" with intracranial extension. Our studies have shown that the C2 ganglion is unique among the spinal ganglia. It is the largest spinal ganglion and is situated outside the spinal canal, posterior to the atlantoaxial articulation [23] In contrast, all other spinal ganglia are located within the spinal canal. We have demonstrated that C2 schwannomas arise from the C2 ganglion and are fundamentally extraspinal in origin. Furthermore, we have shown that both the extraspinal and intraspinal components of these tumors are located within the interdural space [23].

Recognition of the extraspinal origin and interdural location of C2 schwannomas has important surgical implications. The tumor can be adequately exposed through its extraspinal location without the need for bone removal. By remaining within the interdural plane and exploiting the soft, relatively avascular nature of the tumor, the vertebral artery can often be avoided, thereby eliminating the need for its exposure or control. Furthermore, the intraspinal extension of the tumor can be resected by working within the interdural space without opening the spinal dural compartment. Consequently, complete tumor excision can, in most cases, be accomplished without bone removal or laminectomy.

The location and pattern of extension of C2 schwannomas closely resembles those of trigeminal schwannomas. The Gasserian ganglion is the largest cranial sensory ganglion, and trigeminal schwannomas arise from its region. An understanding of the origin of these tumors, together with the dural anatomy and their interdural location, has important surgical implications [35]. By exploiting the interdural plane, the extent of bone removal can be minimized, the need for carotid artery exposure and control can often be avoided, and direct entry into the venous compartment of the cavernous sinus may not be necessary. In our original publication on this subject, we were the first to demonstrate that these tumors could be safely resected without performing a craniotomy [5].
