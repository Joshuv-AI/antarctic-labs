 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 1 SUMMARY INTRODUCTION
 The Spent Fuel and High-Level Waste Disposition program of the U.S. Department of Energy
 (DOE) Office of Nuclear Energy is conducting research and development on geologic disposal of
 used nuclear fuel (UNF) and high-level nuclear waste (HLW). The Geologic Disposal Safety
 Assessment (GDSA) Uncertainty and Sensitivity Analysis Methods work package supports the
 Generic Repository Disposal Strategies control account objective to strategically develop disposal
 concepts. The work package priority in FY26 is on geologic repository system modeling and
 analysis capability to address direct disposal of UNF in dual purpose canisters in shale host rock.
 This report describes specific Fiscal Year (FY) 2026 activities associated with the GDSA
 Uncertainty and Sensitivity Analysis Methods work package (SF-26SN01020105) and fulfills the
 level 4 milestone, Uncertainty Support of Argillite Case (M4SF-26SN010201051). This work was
 closely coordinated with the other Sandia National Laboratory Disposal Development work
 packages: the Argillite GDSA Conceptual Analyses Definition work package (SF-
 26SN01020304), the Argillite GDSA Analyses Implementation work package (SF-
 26SN01020305), and the PLFOTRAN Development work package (SF-26SN01020106).

 1.1 HIGH-LEVEL PURPOSE OF THIS WORK
 Performance assessment for underground geologic disposal of nuclear waste is an iterative
 process used to determine the safety -relevant features, events, and processes to be included in
 a performance assessment model. The purpose of the GDSA Uncertainty and Sensitivity Analysis
 Methods work package is to perform uncertainty and sensitivity analyses that support the
 development of conceptual and numerical models relevant to the disposal of UNF and HLW in
 argillite geological formations in the U.S. This report documents analyses performed in
 collaboration with the Argillite GDSA Analyses Implementation work package using a
 deterministic and stochastic numerical model to investigate the range of temperatures and
 pressures in the near-repository region associated with disposal of high -heat load waste
 packages in argillite formations. Very high temperature and pressure in an argillite repository was
 identified as a potential concern in LaForce et al. (2024). A highly refined near repository system
 model and range of uncertain parameters are described in this work, while the detailed results of
 the sensitivity and uncertainty analysis are presented in Section 4 of Swiler et al. (2025).
 The findings from this quarter waste package shale case have implications for the design and
 operational strategy of repositories for hot UNF in argillite formations. Understanding thermal and
 pressure dynamics is important for ensuring the long-term safety and stability of the repository.
 The potential for elevated temperatures and pressures necessitates careful consideration of
 engineered barrier system materials and configurations to ensure they can withstand these
 conditions. By providing a clearer picture of nearfield behavior under various thermal loads, the
 safety margins and performance expectations of the repository system can be better evaluated.

 1.2 FY26 ACCOMPLISHMENTS
 For the quarter waste package shale case, simulations were analyzed using a higher thermal
 output curve for the waste package than previously considered. This analysis demonstrated
 how the system behaves with increased heat and identified an issue for consideration with the
 current argillite case development; a hot repository leads to more code failures. The code
 failures are due to extremely high temperatures, not a code failure of PFLOTRAN. These results

 UNCERTAINTY AND SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS METHODS AND
 APPLICATIONS IN THE GDSA FRAMEWORK (FY2026)

 1 INTRODUCTION
 This report documents analysis of a quarter waste package shale case to assess the effects of
 different uncertainties on the temperatures and pressures within a shale repository. The analysis
 builds upon previous work (Swiler et al., 2025) but considers disposal of hotter waste packages.
 The influence of increasing the waste package thermal output on system behavior within shale is
 important for the argillite disposal concept strategies currently being developed within the
 Disposal Research and Development Campaign.
 This analysis utilized GDSA Framework as described in Swiler et al. (2025).

 2 QUARTER WASTE PACKAGE SHALE REFERENCE CASE
 Section 2 covers the sensitivity analyses performed for a shale near-repository model. Basurto et
 al. (2025) gives the full description of the quarter waste package shale case. This study simulates
 a single waste package with reflective boundary conditions in the north-south-east-west directions
 to simulate a waste package in the center of the repository region surrounded by waste packages
 of identical type. This reference case provides a highly resolved simulation of near-field behavior
 in the immediate vicinity of the waste package, focusing primarily on temperatures and pressures.
 Generic thermal output curves over time were created for the waste package. In Swiler et al.
 (2025), a 200-sample UQ/SA study was performed to assess the influence of uncertain input
 parameters on temperatures and pressures in the repository for a 4-kW generic thermal output
 curve. To assess the impact and feasibility of hotter waste packages (approximating the 99
 percent hottest waste package in inventory), this study performed a 200-sample UQ/SA study
 with a 7-kW thermal output curve at emplacement. All other aspects of the UQ/SA study are
 identical to the FY25 analysis.
 As in Swiler et al. (2025), 10 parameters were varied, which Table 2-1 describes. The parameters
 are uncorrelated. The shale permeability anisotropy ratio is the ratio of the vertical and horizontal
 entries of the shale permeability tensor. When the ratio is low, it indicates greater horizontal than
 vertical permeability (more anisotropic permeability). When the ratio is 1, the vertical and
 horizontal components of the permeability tensor are equal. The thermal conductivity anisotropy
 ratio is similarly defined.

 2.1 SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS RESULTS
 In contrast to the FY25 UQ/SA analysis where five of the 200 simulations failed to complete, in
 this study with the hotter waste package 24 simulations failed to complete. Of those 24 failures,
 18 failed with water equation-of-state errors, with temperatures at the waste package observation
 point reaching approximately the critical point of water, 373.946 °C. This means approximately 10
 percent of simulations reached infeasibly hot temperatures using the 7-kW thermal output curve.
 Therefore, relying solely on waste packages with a thermal output of around 7-kW and higher
 could lead to significant operational challenges for an Argillite repository. These elevated
 temperatures may exceed the critical limits of the surrounding geological formations, thereby
 compromising the integrity of the repository and potentially leading to failures in waste

September 30, 2026                                                                                              2

 containment and long-term safety. This finding corroborates design decisions implemented in the
 Argillite conceptual model to: (1) alternate high-heat waste packages (~8–9 kW thermal output)
 with cooler waste packages ( ≤ 1 kW thermal output) to avoid this overheating; and (2) design
 for higher thermal conductivities via a pedestal to rapidly distribute heat in the drift.
 Analysis of the parameter combinations driving these high-temperature failures indicates that
 thermal conductivity plays a key role. Figure 2-1 shows histograms of parameter values for
 successful runs and high-temperature failures. Note that high-temperature failures are
 concentrated towards smaller values of thermal conductivity parameters (TK_buffer_wet,
 TK_shale_drz_wet, and TK_ani_shale).

 Figure 2-1. Density histograms of parameter values for successful runs (blue) and high-
 temperature failures (black).

 Pairwise scatterplots in Figure 2-2 illustrate how parameter combinations influence reaching
 these infeasibly high temperatures. The first row of Figure 2-2 shows that interactions between
 thermal conductivity parameters appear to be influential on failure, indicating low values for at
 least two of the thermal conductivity parameters can lead to very high temperatures and thus
 failure in this context. This makes intuitive sense because low thermal conductivity traps heat
 traps in the vicinity of the waste package, allowing it to build up. Additionally, the second row of
 Figure 2-2 shows a combination of low permeability with low thermal conductivity for the disturbed
 rock zone (DRZ) and shale appears to be influential. This indicates a combination of thermal
 diffusive transport and advective transport of hot water away from the region surrounding the
 waste package is influential.

 Note, however, that some successful runs feature low combined thermal conductivity values.
 Figure 2-3 shows scatterplots for such cases, highlighting the parameter values for these
 successful runs in green. In contrast to the high-temperature failure runs, these successful low
 thermal conductivity runs have a larger shale thermal conductivity anisotropy ratio parameter
 (TK_ani_shale) combined with a higher shale permeability. These combine to result in greater
 effective conductivity and increased transport of heat away from the waste package, which result
 in lower (although still high) temperatures at the waste package.

 2.2 SUMMARY
 In summary, while the ranges of temperatures and pressure observed for the 7-kW thermal output
 study presented herein resulted in significantly higher temperatures at the waste package
 compared to the 4-kW thermal output study, and a much wider range of maximum pressures,
 dependence of these outputs on input parameters was largely the same. For the 7-kW thermal
 output study, approximately 10 percent of simulations reached the critical temperature of water
 and failed to complete.
