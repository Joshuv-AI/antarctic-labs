Simulating Pyrocumulonimbus Clouds Using a Multiscale Wildfire Simulation Framework
  Objective
  A pyrocumulonimbus cloud (or pyroCb) is a thunderstorm cloud that forms above intense a
  wildfires or volcanic eruptions when heat and moisture from the fire drive deep convection into
  the upper atmosphere. We developed a first-of-its-kind multiscale pyroCb simulation
  framework in DOE’s flagship Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM).

  Approach
  ● We built a wildfire simulation framework by extending the Regionally Refined Model (RRM)
 capability to a 3-km resolution over California (CARRM) and incorporating a plume rise
 model and a vertical water vapor transport scheme. b
  ● We developed a high-resolution (500 m, hourly) dataset of fire radiative power, emissions,
 and sensible heat flux using satellite observations. We integrated it into the framework to
 provide the surface boundary conditions.

  Results/Impacts
  ● Our simulation successfully reproduces many pyroCb features, including cloud height, c
 spatiotemporal evolution, and convective intensity for the 2020 Creek Fire in California (see
 Figure) and reveals vertical transport of ambient near-surface water vapor as a key factor.
  ● This work represents a significant step toward understanding how extreme wildfires can
 alter the atmosphere and potentially impact the stratosphere and the Earth system.

Simulating Pyrocumulonimbus Clouds
Using a Multiscale Wildfire Simulation
Framework
Science
Pyrocumulonimbus (pyroCb) clouds, driven by extreme fires under favorable meteorological
conditions, can inject smoke into the stratosphere at magnitudes comparable to those of
moderate volcanic eruptions, potentially altering the global radiative balance and atmospheric
composition. However, simulating pyroCb is particularly challenging in Earth system models.
Here, a DOE-funded team at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory and UC Irvine developed
a first-of-its-kind multiscale pyroCb simulation framework in DOE’s flagship Energy Exascale
Earth System Model (E3SM). The simulation successfully captures key pyroCb features as
observed during the 2020 Creek Fire in California.

Impact
The new E3SM wildfire simulation capability provides a foundation for future exploration of
pyroCb impacts at both regional and global scales. Furthermore, this modeling capability
supports a broad array of E3SM applications targeting extreme events characterized by physical
processes similar to those of wildfires.

Summary
Pyrocumulonimbus (pyroCb) clouds are powerful storm clouds created by extreme wildfires.
They can inject smoke and moisture high into the atmosphere, affecting air quality, weather,
and the Earth system. Most Earth system models struggle to represent these events because
they lack detailed wildfire–atmosphere interactions. In this study, we built a new approach
within the DOE’s flagship Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM) using a convection
permitting regionally refined mesh. Our method combines high-resolution satellite fire data
with a plume-rise model and a scheme that lifts near-surface moisture into the atmosphere.
Together, these processes make it possible for the model to generate deep wildfire-driven
storms. Sensitivity tests show that removing or simplifying any one of these processes greatly
weakens or prevents pyroCb formation. Using the 2020 Creek Fire in California, the model
reproduced the observed timing, height, and strength of these explosive clouds. We also
applied it to the 2021 Dixie Fire, which produced several pyroCb events under very different
conditions, with similarly strong results. Our findings show that wildfire-driven vertical
moisture transport is a key ingredient in these storms. This work is an important step toward

understanding how extreme wildfires can change the atmosphere and potentially impact the
stratosphere and the Earth system.
