Concentrating Solar Power
 (CSP) Update
• The European company Volateq has deployed drones to perform O&M-based
  inspections for a 100-MWac parabolic trough CSP plant. The ball joints of parabolic
  trough collectors require periodic inspection, and the company reported that the
  automated drones can do this twice as fast as manual inspections.
• Fraunhofer Chile researchers have designed, built, and tested a Fresnel mirror solar
  collector and crucible system that melts and recycles aluminum. Chile has some of
  the highest direct normal irradiance, and currently it exports most of its aluminum
  for recycling. The next steps include refining the design, lowering the costs, and
  increasing output.
• In late 2025, researchers in France published a new paper presenting an algorithm
  strategy to deliver a steady supply of industrial heat from CSP plants with thermal
  energy storage. The intermittent nature of solar energy can be a barrier to using CSP
  technology for high-temperature industrial processes with specific temporal
  requirements.                                                         National Laboratory of the Rockies | 11

 CSP Update (China)
 • In 2025, China connected 9 CSP projects totaling 0.9 GWac to the electrical grid, bringing the country’s cumulative installed CSP
 capacity to 1.7 GWac.
 – An additional 25 projects totaling 3 GWac were also under construction, and another 31 projects, totaling 4 GWac were
 planned.

 • In December 2025, China released a policy roadmap that targets 15 GWac of CSP by 2030 with costs comparable to coal-fired
 plants (lowering costs from $72/MWh to $40/MWh). In addition to R&D and other efforts to lower CSP costs, China plans to
 increase CSP competitiveness through larger projects, participation in enhancing grid-reliability programs, improved project
 planning and layout, and integrating projects with PV and wind.
 – To achieve the 15-GWac goal, the country would need to plan another 6 GWac of projects.

 – BNEF reported that China released a goal in 2023 for 9 GWac to begin construction by the end of 2025 and achieved half the
 target.

 Proposed Revision and Possible Impacts of Greenhouse Gas
 Protocol Scope 2
 • Greenhouse Gas Protocol (GHGP) is a set of standards/guidelines for measuring and reporting GHG emissions. In
 general, it is voluntary and adopted by entities engaged in emissions reduction efforts. It is legally required
 under some programs—for example, in the EU.
 • GHGP methods for reporting Scope 2 emissions (from energy use) include:
 – Locational uses average emissions intensity of energy delivered.
 – Market-based uses the emissions intensities of contracted supply (virtual PPAs, RECs).
 • Proposed updates (phased in, exemptions based on data availability and entity size):
 – Locational would require using the most precise, publicly available geographic emission intensities at the most
 granular temporal level available.
 – Market-based would require hourly matching to load and contracts for resources in the same grid as the load,
 with additional protections against double-counting.
 • GHGP is also considering a consequential accounting framework to model the emissions impact of investments
 separate from an entity’s own energy use (e.g., for a project on a different grid).
 • Potential impacts include:
 – More precise/accurate reporting (including revised progress); more additionality from investments
 – New markets for time and geography-specific energy attribute certificates
 – Increased complexity for adherence, less adoption (or increased abandonment) of GHGP.

 IRS Releases Guidance on
 Material Assistance
• On February 12, the IRS issued Notice 2026-15, which explains how taxpayers must determine if 45Y or 48E (tech-neutral PTC and ITC) qualified facilities
 and energy storage properties, or 45X eligible components (manufacturing production tax credit) include material assistance from a prohibited foreign
 entity (PFE).
 – The PFE restrictions were enacted under 2025’s HR1 and generally apply to manufactured products and projects starting in 2026. They largely
 restrict who can own a project and how much material can come from certain countries (e.g., China).
 – Taxpayers must calculate a project’s material assistance cost ratio (MACR), which is the percentage of costs attributable to equipment, parts, and
 material, not supplied by a PFE. The required percentage increases over time.
• The notice details how taxpayers should calculate MACR and provides three interim safe harbor tables:
 – Identification and cost percentage safe harbors: Taxpayers can use the domestic content bonus safe harbor tables issued from 2023 to 2025 to (1)
 identify manufactured products and their components or material that should be assessed for purposes of calculating MACR and (2) use the direct
 cost percentages published to determine MACR. Steel items as well as any items not listed within previous safe harbor tables are not evaluated for
 material assistance. Tables are only available for a subset of technologies and products and not any facility under the “incremental production rule.”
 – Certification safe harbor: Taxpayers may rely on certifications from suppliers for how much direct cost, if any, can be attributable to a PFE. The
 certifications must be signed under penalty of perjury, among other requirements.
• Other key elements of the notice include:
 – Supply chain tracing: Qualified facilities and storage properties do not need to worry about subcomponents, and 45X products only need to worry
 about directly purchased materials.
 – IP licensing: IP licenses with a PFE entered into after July 4, 2025, can result in violation of material assistance rules because PFE has “effective
 control” over company.
 – 80/20 rule: Facilities qualifying under the 80/20 rule (in which used components represent 20% or less of total value) need only evaluate new
 products and components.
• The IRS intends to issue additional notices and new safe harbor tables and has requested comments by the end of March 2026.
