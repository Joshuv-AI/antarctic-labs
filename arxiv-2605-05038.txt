Nonequilibrium Fluctuation–Response Theory in the Frequency Domain
Euijoon Kwon, Hyun-Myung Chun, Hyunggyu Park, Jae Sung Lee

Abstract
We develop a unified fluctuation–response theory in the frequency domain for nonequilibrium steady states governed by overdamped Langevin dynamics and Markov jump processes. The relation expresses the power spectrum of general observables exactly as a quadratic form of local responses measured at the same frequency, thereby extending static nonequilibrium fluctuation–response relations to finite frequencies. The decomposition is spatial for Langevin systems and edge-resolved for Markov jump processes, and applies uniformly to state-dependent observables, current-like observables, and their combinations. As consequences of the same identity, we derive frequency-domain response uncertainty relations, kinetic and thermodynamic uncertainty relations, the equilibrium fluctuation–dissipation theorem, and Harada–Sasa-type relations. Applications to stochastic networks and driven diffusive systems illustrate how the theory resolves fluctuation spectra into edge-wise contributions and reveals frequency-dependent tradeoffs between fluctuations, response, and dissipation.

Introduction

Relating the response of a system to a weak perturbation to its spontaneous fluctuations is a central theme in statistical physics. At equilibrium, this connection is expressed by the fluctuation–dissipation theorem (FDT)

Thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs) constrain current fluctuations by entropy production

In contrast to FDT-type relations, which establish equalities linking fluctuations to response, these results take the form of inequalities that bound fluctuations.
More recently, these ideas have been extended from fluctuation bounds to response bounds, showing that the sensitivity of nonequilibrium systems to kinetic and thermodynamic perturbations is itself constrained by fluctuations, dissipation, activity, and network topology

The most recent development is the emergence of fluctuation–response relations (FRRs) far from equilibrium. For Markov jump processes, static FRRs were first derived to express long-time current covariances as quadratic combinations of local responses
6
. This structure was subsequently generalized to state observables and mixed state–current correlations

;
45

17

7

In this work, we derive an FRR in the frequency domain for nonequilibrium steady states of both overdamped Langevin systems and Markov jump processes.
We show that the power spectrum of a broad class of observables, including state-dependent and current-like components, can be exactly reconstructed from local linear responses measured at the same frequency. The decomposition is spatial in the Langevin case, whereas for jump processes it is resolved at the level of edges.
This frequency-domain FRR provides not only a finite-frequency extension of static nonequilibrium FRRs, but also an explicit decomposition of the power spectrum in terms of local responses.

II
Main results

In this section, we state the central result of the paper: a unified FRR in the frequency domain for nonequilibrium steady states. The relation applies to both overdamped Langevin systems and Markov jump systems, and expresses the power spectrum of a broad class of observables in terms of local linear responses to impulsive perturbations. As we explain below, this relation should be regarded as the foundational relation of the paper, since the frequency-domain RURs, TURs, the equilibrium FDT, and Harada–Sasa-type relations all arise from it as consequences.

II.1
Minimal definitions

We consider a system in a nonequilibrium steady state and an observable
A(t)
, or more generally a vector of observables
\bm{\theta}(t)
. In both the overdamped Langevin and Markov jump settings considered here, the observables of interest consist of two parts: a
state-dependent
part, which depends on the instantaneous state of the system, and a
current-like
part, which depends on the instantaneous dynamical increments. The precise forms of these observables will be specified in Secs.
III
and
IV
.

To characterize fluctuations, we introduce the covariance notation

C_{A,B}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigl[\langle A(t)B(0)\rangle-\langle A\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}\langle B\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}\Bigr]e^{i\omega t}\,dt,

(1)

where
A(t)
and
B(t)
may be scalar- or vector-valued stochastic processes, provided that the product
A(t)B(0)
is well-defined. In particular, for a vector observable
\bm{\theta}(t)
, the power spectrum matrix is denoted by
C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)
.

To characterize responses, we consider local impulsive perturbations of a dynamical quantity
\phi_{k}(\bm{x})
, where
\bm{x}
denotes the state of the system.
The index
k
labels spatial components in the Langevin case and edges in the Markov jump case. In the Langevin setting,
\phi
represents one of
\bm{F}
(force),
\ln M
(log-mobility), or
T
(temperature), while in the Markov jump case it represents either the symmetric or antisymmetric part of a transition rate. For an impulsive perturbation applied at time
t=0
, we define the local frequency-domain response of an observable
A(t)
as

R^{A}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{x})}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta\langle A(t)\rangle}{\delta\phi_{k}(\bm{x},0)}e^{i\omega t}\,dt,

(2)

where the second argument of
\phi_{k}(\bm{x},0)
in the functional derivative specifies the time at which the perturbation is applied.

We also introduce notation for responses to global perturbations. For a force perturbation of the form
\bm{F}(\bm{x})\mapsto\bm{F}(\bm{x})+\epsilon\bm{\psi}(\bm{x})
, the corresponding response of an observable
A
is denoted by
R^{A}_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)
and can be expressed in terms of the local responses as

R^{A}_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)=\sum_{k}\int d\bm{x}\,\psi_{k}(\bm{x})\,R^{A}_{F_{k}(\bm{x})}(\omega).

(3)

Analogous expressions hold for global perturbations of
\ln M
and
T
in the Langevin setting, and for the corresponding global perturbations of transition rates in the Markov jump case.

II.2
Frequency-domain fluctuation–response relation

We now state the main result of the paper. This result has two realizations, one for overdamped Langevin dynamics and one for Markov jump dynamics, but the structure is the same in both cases: the fluctuation spectrum of an observable can be reconstructed from local linear responses measured at the same frequency.

For overdamped Langevin systems, the frequency-domain FRR takes the form

C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)=\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{z}\,R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\,\bigl[A^{\phi}(\bm{z})^{-1}\bigr]_{kl}\,\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{l}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger},

(4)

where
\phi\in\{\bm{F},\ln M,T\}
, and
A^{\phi}(\bm{z})
is a positive matrix determined by the steady state and the type of perturbation.
The superscript
\dagger
denotes Hermitian conjugate of vectors and matrices. Its explicit form is given in Sec.
III
.

For Markov jump systems, the corresponding relation is

C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)=\sum_{n>m}\frac{1}{A^{\phi}_{nm}}\,R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{nm}}(\omega)\,\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{nm}}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger},

(5)

where
\phi\in\{B,F\}
, the label
\phi_{nm}
refers to a local perturbation associated with the edge
m\leftrightarrow n
, and
A^{\phi}_{nm}
is a positive scalar weight determined by the steady state and by the type of perturbation. Its explicit form is given in Sec.
IV
.

Equations (
4
) and (
5
) constitute the central results of this work. In both continuous and discrete dynamics, they express the power spectrum as a quadratic form of local responses. The decomposition is spatial for Langevin systems and edge-resolved for Markov jump processes.
This representation makes explicit how local response contributions at different spatial locations or edges enter the spectrum.

II.3
Interpretation and significance

The relations (
4
) and (
5
) are finite-frequency fluctuation–response identities for nonequilibrium steady states.
Unlike inequalities relating fluctuations and response, they establish an explicit equality expressing the power spectrum at finite frequency as a quadratic combination of local linear responses.
Thus, the frequency-domain FRR provides a unified framework for relating spontaneous fluctuations to controlled perturbations far from equilibrium.

This result is structurally different from the equilibrium FDT. In equilibrium, fluctuations and responses are related by a direct proportionality
32
. Out of equilibrium, such a simple proportionality is generally lost. This loss, however, does not imply the absence of structure. Rather, the power spectrum remains exactly expressible as a weighted quadratic combination of local responses.

It is important to position the present result relative to recent literature. Nonequilibrium FRRs were first established for Markov jump processes in the static, long-time limit, initially for current observables
6
, and then extended to state observables and mixed state–current covariances
44
;
45
.
In parallel, a time-domain fluctuation–response theory has been developed for overdamped Langevin dynamics, which unifies long-time FRRs, finite-time fluctuation–response inequalities, and response uncertainty relations within a single hierarchical structure
13
. More recently, extensions to the frequency domain have also been developed,
but in the form of a finite-frequency inequality
17
or in a macroscopic Gaussian theory valid near stable fixed points
7
.
Such approaches either constrain the response relative to the spectrum without reconstructing it, or are valid only within a regime of weak fluctuations around stable deterministic states.
Against this background, the present result fills an important gap: it establishes a finite-frequency FRR that reconstructs the power spectrum through an equality at finite frequency without relying on a Gaussian approximation.
Finally, a finite-time fluctuation–response theory for nonautonomous jump processes developed in
5
expresses covariance (the frequency-integrated power spectrum) in terms of Fourier components of response functions. While it does not provide a frequency-resolved reconstruction of the power spectrum, it provides a complementary extension of fluctuation–response relations to nonstationary dynamics by lifting the steady-state restriction.

A further strength of the present result is its breadth across observables. The same structure applies to current-like observables, state-dependent observables, and their combinations.
This is natural in settings where both occupations and transport are described within a common formalism.
It also enables a direct interpretation of the power spectrum, by distinguishing contributions from local residence statistics, transport channels, and their interplay.

Finally, the significance of the frequency-domain FRR lies in the results it generates.
Once the exact identity is established, the frequency-domain RUR and related inequalities arise from applications of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality; KUR and TUR arise from specific perturbations; the equilibrium limit recovers the FDT; and the nonequilibrium remainder yields Harada–Sasa-type dissipation relations
25
;
26
;
24
. For this reason, the frequency-domain FRR should be regarded as the foundational relation of this work.
The hierarchy stemming from the frequency-domain FRR is illustrated in Fig.
1
.

III
Fluctuation–response theory in overdamped Langevin systems

In this section, we construct the frequency-domain FRR for overdamped Langevin systems. We begin by specifying the stochastic dynamics and the perturbations of interest, and then introduce the class of observables together with the empirical density and current fields. The central object of the construction is a frequency-domain excess propagator that determines both local responses and two-point covariances. Since both quantities can be expressed in terms of this excess propagator, this structure leads first to a local FRR for empirical density and current, and then, by linearity, to the FRR for general observables. Technical derivations are deferred to Appendix
A
.

III.1
Setup and notation

Throughout this section,
\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denote configuration vectors. We consider an
N
-dimensional overdamped Langevin system governed by

\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=M(\bm{x}(t))\bm{F}(\bm{x}(t))+\sqrt{2}\,B(\bm{x}(t))\circledast\bm{\xi}(t),

(6)

where
\circledast
denotes the anti-Itô product,
\bm{F}(\bm{x})
is the drift force,
M(\bm{x})
is the mobility matrix,
B(\bm{x})
is the noise-amplitude matrix, and
\bm{\xi}(t)
is Gaussian white noise with

\langle\bm{\xi}(t)\rangle=\bm{0},\qquad\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(7)

Equivalently, the same dynamics can be written in Itô form as

\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=\bm{v}(\bm{x}(t))+\sqrt{2}\,B(\bm{x}(t))\bullet\boldsymbol{\xi}(t),

(8)

where
\bullet
is the Itô product and
\bm{v}
is the effective drift defined as

\bm{v}(\bm{x})=M(\bm{x})\bm{F}(\bm{x})+\bigl[\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}D(\bm{x})\bigr]^{\mathsf{T}},

(9)

where
\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}}\equiv(\partial_{x_{1}},\cdots,\partial_{x_{N}})^{\mathsf{T}}
is the gradient operator written as a column vector and
D(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}B(\bm{x})B(\bm{x})^{\mathsf{T}}
.
The superscript
\mathsf{T}
denotes transposition of vectors and matrices.

For notational simplicity, we assume
M(\bm{x})
and
D(\bm{x})
are diagonal,

\displaystyle M(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\mu_{1}(\bm{x}),\dots,\mu_{N}(\bm{x})\bigr),

(10)

\displaystyle D(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(D_{1}(\bm{x}),\dots,D_{N}(\bm{x})\bigr),

and define the temperature matrix by

T(\bm{x})=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(T_{1}(\bm{x}),\dots,T_{N}(\bm{x})\bigr),

(11)

such that

D(\bm{x})=M(\bm{x})T(\bm{x})=T(\bm{x})M(\bm{x}).

(12)

We further assume that the dynamics admits a unique nonequilibrium steady state with probability density
\pi(\bm{x})
. The corresponding steady-state probability current is denoted by
\bm{j}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})
.
The theory can be readily extended to non-diagonal matrices.

We consider the response to small perturbations and their relation to steady-state fluctuations. To treat them on equal footing, we denote the perturbed quantity by
\phi
, with
\phi\in\{\bm{F},\ln M,T\}
. A local impulsive perturbation of the
k
th component of
\phi
is written as

\phi_{k}(\bm{x})\mapsto\phi_{k}(\bm{x})+\epsilon\,\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})\delta(t-s),

(13)

where
\epsilon
is infinitesimal. Local perturbations are the elementary building blocks of the FRR. Global perturbations are defined in (
3
) and used later when discussing RURs and related consequences. Perturbations of the mobility induce no response at equilibrium and therefore probe nonequilibrium features, since they modify only the characteristic time scale of the dynamics
33
;
13
;
21
;
22
. They are not reducible to conventional force perturbations.

III.2
Observables, empirical fields, and response functions

We introduce the observables and local fields entering the FRR. A general observable consists of a state-dependent part and a current-like part. For a single observable, we write

\theta(t)=\Lambda(\bm{x}(t))^{\mathsf{T}}\circ\dot{\bm{x}}(t)+g(\bm{x}(t)),

(14)

where the first term is current-like and the second term is state-dependent. The product
\circ
denotes the Stratonovich product. More generally, for a collection of
N_{O}
observables we write

\bm{\theta}(t)=L(\bm{x}(t))\circ\dot{\bm{x}}(t)+G(\bm{x}(t)).

(15)

Here,
L(\bm{x})
is an
N_{O}\times N
matrix-valued function and
G(\bm{x})
is an
N_{O}
-dimensional vector-valued function. This form includes purely state-dependent observables, purely current-like observables, and their combinations.

The local objects underlying (
15
) are the empirical density and empirical current,

\rho(\bm{x},t)\equiv\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}(t)),\qquad\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x},t)\equiv\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}(t))\circ\dot{\bm{x}}(t).

(16)

In terms of these fields, the observable vector reads

\bm{\theta}(t)=\int d\bm{x}\,\Bigl[G(\bm{x})\rho(\bm{x},t)+L(\bm{x})\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x},t)\Bigr].

(17)

Thus, the fluctuations and responses of
\bm{\theta}(t)
follow from those of
\rho(\bm{x},t)
and
\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x},t)
by integration with the weights
G
and
L
.

Using the notation in (
1
), we write the empirical-field covariances as

C_{\rho(\bm{x}),\rho(\bm{y})}(\omega)
,

C_{\rho(\bm{x}),\bm{\jmath}(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)
, and

C_{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x}),\bm{\jmath}(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)
,
and the observable-level power spectrum as

C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)
. Similarly, using the response notation in (
2
), we write the corresponding local responses as

R^{\rho(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)
,

R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)
, and

R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)
. The next two subsections show that the local covariance functions of the empirical fields can be written as quadratic forms of the corresponding local responses.

III.3
Excess propagator and local responses

The central object in the frequency-domain response theory for overdamped Langevin systems is an excess propagator. It encodes how the responses of the empirical density and current, induced by impulsive perturbations, propagate through the unperturbed steady-state dynamics.

We denote by
P(\bm{x},t|\bm{y},s)
the transition probability density of the unperturbed dynamics. It satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation

\partial_{t}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{y},s)=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{y},s),

(18)

where
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}
is the Fokker–Planck generator acting on the variable
\bm{x}
. We write

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}=-\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}},\qquad\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}=M(\bm{x})\bigl[\bm{F}(\bm{x})-T(\bm{x})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bigr].

(19)

The steady-state density is characterized by

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}\pi(\bm{x})=0.

(20)

The corresponding steady-state current is

\bm{j}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})=\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}\pi(\bm{x}).

(21)

The excess propagator is defined by
4

H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl[P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)-\pi(\bm{x})\bigr]e^{i\omega t}\,dt.

(22)

It measures the excess probability at
\bm{x}
relative to the steady state, following a localized initial condition at
\bm{z}
, at frequency
\omega
.

The excess propagator satisfies the identities

\int d\bm{z}\,H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\pi(\bm{z})=0,

(23)

\bigl(i\omega+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}\bigr)H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)=-\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})+\pi(\bm{x}),

(24)

and

\bigl(i\omega+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{z}}^{\dagger}\bigr)H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)=-\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})+\pi(\bm{x}).

(25)

These relations show that
H
plays the role of the steady-state projected resolvent of the Fokker–Planck dynamics. Their derivations are provided in Appendix.
A.2
.

We consider a local impulsive perturbation of
\phi_{k}(\bm{z})
as in (
13
). At the level of the Fokker–Planck generator, this perturbation reads

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}-\epsilon\delta(t)\,\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})\,\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\phi_{k},\bm{x}}\,,

(26)

where the operator
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\phi_{k},\bm{x}}
depends on the choice of the perturbed quantity (see Eqs. (
150
)–(
152
)). We define the associated local prefactor by

N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z})\equiv\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\phi_{k},\bm{z}}\pi(\bm{z}).

(27)

Its explicit form depends on whether
\phi
corresponds to the force, mobility, or temperature (see Eqs. (
163
)–(
165
)), but the local response structure is identical in all cases.

The local response of the empirical density reads

R^{\rho(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)=\bigl[N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z})\bigr]^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{\bm{z}}H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega).

(28)

Similarly, the local response of the empirical current reads

R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)=\mathcal{P}(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\,N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z}),

(29)

where

\mathcal{P}(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)=I\,\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})+\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{\mathsf{T}}H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega).

(30)

with
I
denoting the
N\times N
identity matrix.
Equations (
28
) and (
29
) show that both density and current responses are determined by the same excess propagator. The perturbation type enters only through the local prefactor
N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z})
.
The derivations of (
28
) and (
29
) are given in Appendix
A.1
.

III.4
Local fluctuation–response relation for empirical density and current

We show that the local covariance functions of the empirical density and current are determined by the same excess propagator that governs the local responses in Sec.
III.3
. This establishes the local form of the frequency-domain FRR.

A direct calculation of the steady-state two-time distribution gives

C_{\rho(\bm{x}),\rho(\bm{y})}(\omega)=H(\bm{x},\bm{y};\omega)\pi(\bm{y})+H(\bm{y},\bm{x};-\omega)\pi(\bm{x}).

(31)

The key step is the identity that rewrites the right-hand side of (
31
) as a quadratic form in
\nabla_{\bm{z}}H
:

\displaystyle C_{\rho(\bm{x}),\rho(\bm{y})}(\omega)

(32)

\displaystyle=2\int d\bm{z}\,\pi(\bm{z})\,\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{\mathsf{T}}H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\,D(\bm{z})\,\nabla_{\bm{z}}H(\bm{y},\bm{z};-\omega).

The proof of (
32
) is deferred to Appendix
A.4
.

Using (
30
), the mixed and current–current covariances can be written in parallel form

\displaystyle C_{\rho(\bm{x}),\bm{\jmath}(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)

(33)

\displaystyle=2\int d\bm{z}\,\pi(\bm{z})\,\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{\mathsf{T}}H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\,D(\bm{z})\,\bigl[\mathcal{P}(\bm{y},\bm{z};\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger},

and

C_{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x}),\bm{\jmath}(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)=2\int d\bm{z}\,\pi(\bm{z})\,\mathcal{P}(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\,D(\bm{z})\,\bigl[\mathcal{P}(\bm{y},\bm{z};\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}.

(34)

Equations (
32
)–(
34
) make clear that the local covariance functions are built from the same objects that appear in the response formulas (
28
) and (
29
).
The proofs of (
33
) and (
34
) are deferred to Appendix
A.3
.

To make this connection explicit, we define the matrix of local prefactors

N^{\phi}(\bm{z})=\bigl(N_{\phi_{1}}(\bm{z}),\dots,N_{\phi_{N}}(\bm{z})\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}},

(35)

and the associated perturbation weight matrix

A^{\phi}(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{2\pi(\bm{z})}\bigl[N^{\phi}(\bm{z})\bigr]^{\mathsf{T}}D(\bm{z})^{-1}N^{\phi}(\bm{z}).

(36)

The explicit forms of the matrices
A^{\phi}(\bm{z})
for
\phi\in\{\bm{F},\ln M,T\}
are given in Appendix
A.5
.
Combining (
28
) with (
32
) yields

\displaystyle C_{\rho(\bm{x}),\rho(\bm{y})}(\omega)

(37)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{z}\,R^{\rho(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\,\bigl[A^{\phi}(\bm{z})^{-1}\bigr]_{kl}\,R^{\rho(\bm{y})}_{\phi_{l}(\bm{z})}(-\omega).

Likewise, combining (
28
) and (
29
) with (
33
) and (
34
), we obtain

\displaystyle C_{\rho(\bm{x}),\bm{\jmath}(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)

(38)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{z}\,R^{\rho(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\,\bigl[A^{\phi}(\bm{z})^{-1}\bigr]_{kl}\,\bigl[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{y})}_{\phi_{l}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger},

and

\displaystyle C_{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x}),\bm{\jmath}(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)

(39)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{z}\,R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\,\bigl[A^{\phi}(\bm{z})^{-1}\bigr]_{kl}\,\bigl[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{y})}_{\phi_{l}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}.

Equations (
37
)–(
39
) constitute the local frequency-domain FRR for overdamped Langevin systems. They show that the local covariance functions of empirical density and current are given by integrals of products of local responses over the perturbation point
\bm{z}
, weighted by the inverse of the matrix
A^{\phi}(\bm{z})
.

III.5
Frequency-domain fluctuation–response relation for general observables

Given the local relations (
37
)–(
39
), the extension to general observables is immediate.
Since the observable vector
\bm{\theta}
in (
17
) is linear in the empirical density and current, the local response is given by

R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)=\int d\bm{x}\,\Bigl[G(\bm{x})\,R^{\rho(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)+L(\bm{x})\,R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\Bigr].

(40)

Similarly, the power spectrum matrix of
\bm{\theta}(t)
reads

\displaystyle C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)=\int d\bm{x}\,d\bm{y}\,\Big[

\displaystyle G(\bm{x})\,C_{\rho(\bm{x}),\rho(\bm{y})}(\omega)\,G(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}

(41)

\displaystyle+G(\bm{x})\,C_{\rho(\bm{x}),\bm{\jmath}(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\,L(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle+L(\bm{x})\,C_{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x}),\rho(\bm{y})}(\omega)\,G(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle+L(\bm{x})\,C_{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x}),\bm{\jmath}(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\,L(\bm{y})^{\mathsf{T}}\Big].

Substituting (
37
)–(
39
) into (
41
) and using (
40
), the integrations over
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
combine into the local responses of the observable
\bm{\theta}
. One obtains

C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)=\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{z}\,R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\,\bigl[A^{\phi}(\bm{z})^{-1}\bigr]_{kl}\,\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{l}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}.

(42)

Equation (
42
) is the frequency-domain FRR for general observables in overdamped Langevin systems. It is the Langevin realization of the relation stated in Sec.
II
; cf. (
4
).
Its meaning is transparent: the power spectrum is reconstructed from local responses to perturbations applied at each point in configuration space.

Equation (
42
) applies to purely state-dependent observables, purely current-like observables, and their mixtures. Moreover, the structure is independent of whether the perturbation acts on the force, the mobility, or the temperature; only the perturbation matrix
A^{\phi}(\bm{z})
differs. This completes the construction of the frequency-domain FRR for overdamped Langevin systems. The corresponding derivation for Markov jump systems follows the same logic, but the local structure is organized over discrete edges rather than continuous configuration space.

IV
Fluctuation–response theory in Markov jump systems

In this section, we construct the frequency-domain FRR for Markov jump systems. The logic parallels that of Sec.
III
, but the local structure is now organized over the edges of a transition network rather than over points in configuration space. We first specify the jump dynamics and the perturbations of interest.
We then introduce the relevant observables, expressed in terms of empirical state indicators and empirical currents.
The central object is a discrete excess propagator, which determines both local responses and two-point covariances. This structure leads first to a local FRR for empirical state indicators and empirical currents, and then, by linearity, to the FRR for general observables. Technical derivations are deferred to Appendix
B
.

IV.1
Setup and notation

We consider a continuous-time Markov jump process on a finite set of states
n\in\{1,2,\dots,S\}
. Let
p_{n}(t)
denote the probability of occupying state
n
at time
t
. The dynamics is governed by the master equation

\partial_{t}p_{n}(t)=\sum_{m(\neq n)}\bigl[W_{nm}p_{m}(t)-W_{mn}p_{n}(t)\bigr],

(43)

where
W_{nm}
is the transition rate from state
m
to state
n
.
Introducing the rate matrix
\mathcal{W}
with off-diagonal elements
[\mathcal{W}]_{nm}=W_{nm}
(
n\neq m
) and diagonal elements

[\mathcal{W}]_{nn}=-\sum_{m(\neq n)}W_{mn},

(44)

Eq. (
43
) can be written compactly as

\partial_{t}\bm{p}(t)=\mathcal{W}\bm{p}(t),

(45)

where

\bm{p}(t)=\bigl(p_{1}(t),\dots,p_{S}(t)\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}.

(46)

To separate the kinetic and entropic aspects of the dynamics, we parametrize the transition rates as

W_{nm}=\exp\!\left(B_{nm}+\frac{F_{nm}}{2}\right),

(47)

where
B_{nm}=B_{mn}
and
F_{nm}=-F_{mn}
. Here,
B_{nm}
is the symmetric part of the rate and controls the kinetic timescale of the transition, while
F_{nm}
is the antisymmetric part and encodes the thermodynamic bias associated with the transition. This decomposition is commonly used in recent nonequilibrium fluctuation–response theory for jump processes
6
;
33
;
44
;
45
.

We assume that the process is irreducible and admits a unique stationary distribution
\pi_{n}
, satisfying

\sum_{m}[\mathcal{W}]_{nm}\pi_{m}=0.

(48)

The stationary distribution is the discrete analog of the steady-state density in the Langevin setting. The nonequilibrium character of the stationary state is reflected in the steady-state current along the edge
m\leftrightarrow n
,

j_{nm}=W_{nm}\pi_{m}-W_{mn}\pi_{n},

(49)

which characterizes the net probability flow.
By constrast, the corresponding traffic
37
,

a_{nm}=W_{nm}\pi_{m}+W_{mn}\pi_{n},

(50)

quantifies the total transition rate along that edge, irrespective of direction.

We consider local perturbations of the edge parameters
B_{nm}
and
F_{nm}
. For a perturbation on a specific edge
k\leftrightarrow l
, we take

B_{kl}\mapsto B_{kl}+\epsilon\,\delta(t-s),

(51)

or

F_{kl}\mapsto F_{kl}+\epsilon\,\delta(t-s),

(52)

for kinetic or entropic perturbations, respectively.
These local impulsive perturbations are the discrete counterparts of the spatially localized perturbations introduced in Sec.
III
.
Global perturbations can also be considered, for example
\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}
, for which the response is obtained as a linear combination of the corresponding local edge responses, as discussed below.

IV.2
Observables, empirical fields, and response functions

We introduce empirical state indicators and currents as the discrete counterparts of the empirical stochastic fields in the Langevin setting. As in Sec.
III
, the goal is to treat state-dependent and current-like observables within a common framework.

For each state
n
, we define the state indicator

\eta_{n}(t)\equiv\delta_{X(t),n},

(53)

where
X(t)
denotes the state occupied by the system at time
t
. The variable
\eta_{n}(t)
takes the value
1
when the system is in state
n
and
0
otherwise. These indicators are the discrete analog of the empirical density field (
16
).

To describe transitions, let
N_{nm}(t)
denote the accumulated number of jumps from state
m
to state
n
up to time
t
. The corresponding empirical current on the edge
m\leftrightarrow n
is defined by

\jmath_{nm}(t)\equiv\dot{N}_{nm}(t)-\dot{N}_{mn}(t),

(54)

where the overdot denotes the time derivative.
This quantity is antisymmetric in
n
and
m
, and measures the instantaneous net jump current along the edge.

We consider a broad class of observables that are linear combinations of state indicators and empirical currents. For a single observable,

\theta(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{S}g_{n}\,\eta_{n}(t)+\sum_{n>m}\Lambda_{nm}\,\jmath_{nm}(t).

(55)

More generally, for a collection of
N_{O}
observables we write

\bm{\theta}(t)=G\,\bm{\eta}(t)+L\,\bm{\jmath}(t),

(56)

where

\bm{\eta}(t)=\bigl(\eta_{1}(t),\dots,\eta_{S}(t)\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}

(57)

is the state-indicator vector, and
\bm{\jmath}(t)
is the vector of empirical edge currents, whose components are indexed by unoriented edges with a fixed convention, such as
n>m
. The matrix
G
contains the coefficients of the state-dependent part, while
L
contains the coefficients of the current-like part.

The covariance notation introduced in Eq. (
1
) applies directly to the discrete empirical fields.
In particular, the two-point covariance functions are
C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{m}}(\omega)
,
C_{\eta_{n},\jmath_{kl}}(\omega)
, and
C_{\jmath_{nm},\jmath_{kl}}(\omega)
. The power spectrum matrix of the observable vector is
C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)
. Thus, as in the continuous case, the fluctuation-response theory of general observables reduces to that of the elementary empirical observables
\eta_{n}(t)
and
\jmath_{nm}(t)
.

We next define the corresponding local responses. Since the local perturbations act on edges, the relevant responses are those to perturbations of the symmetric and antisymmetric edge parameters
B_{kl}
and
F_{kl}
. For a local perturbation of the edge
k\leftrightarrow l
, the frequency-domain response of an observable
A
is

R^{A}_{B_{kl}}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta\langle A(t)\rangle}{\delta B_{kl}(0)}e^{i\omega t}\,dt,

(58)

and

R^{A}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta\langle A(t)\rangle}{\delta F_{kl}(0)}e^{i\omega t}\,dt.

(59)

The response of a general observable follows from linearity. In particular,

R^{\bm{\theta}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=G\,R^{\bm{\eta}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)+L\,R^{\bm{\jmath}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega),

(60)

and similarly for perturbations of
B_{kl}
. For a global perturbation of the antisymmetric part of the rates,

\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi},

(61)

the corresponding response is given by the linear combination of the local edge responses:

R^{A}_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)=\sum_{n>m}\psi_{nm}\,R^{A}_{F_{nm}}(\omega),

(62)

and analogously for perturbations of
\bm{B}
.

IV.3
Excess propagator and local responses

We introduce the discrete counterpart of the excess propagator used in Sec.
III.3
. As before, this object determines both the relaxation of the unperturbed dynamics and the local linear responses to impulsive perturbations.

The conditional probability of being in state
n
at time
t
, given that the system was in state
k
at time
0
, is

P(n,t|k,0)=\bigl[e^{\mathcal{W}t}\bigr]_{nk}.

(63)

We define the excess propagator by
4

H_{nk}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl([e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nk}-\pi_{n}\Bigr)e^{i\omega t}\,dt.

(64)

This quantity is the discrete analog of the excess propagator (
22
): it measures the excess occupation of state
n
relative to the stationary state, induced by a localized initial condition at state
k
, at frequency
\omega
.

The excess propagator satisfies the identities

\sum_{m}H_{nm}(\omega)\pi_{m}=0,

(65)

\sum_{m}\bigl(i\omega\delta_{nm}+\mathcal{W}_{nm}\bigr)H_{mk}(\omega)=-\delta_{nk}+\pi_{n},

(66)

and

\sum_{m}H_{nm}(\omega)\bigl(i\omega\delta_{mk}+\mathcal{W}_{mk}\bigr)=-\delta_{nk}+\pi_{n}.

(67)

These relations are the discrete counterparts of Eqs. (
23
)–(
25
). They show that
H_{nk}(\omega)
is the projected resolvent of the Markov generator. Derivations are given in Appendix
B.2
.

We first consider an entropic perturbation of the edge
k\leftrightarrow l
, given by
F_{kl}\mapsto F_{kl}+\epsilon\,\delta(t)
.
The corresponding response of the state indicator is

R^{\eta_{n}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta\langle\eta_{n}(t)\rangle}{\delta F_{kl}(0)}e^{i\omega t}\,dt.

(68)

A direct calculation gives

R^{\eta_{n}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=\frac{a_{kl}}{2}\Bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\Bigr].

(69)

For a kinetic perturbation,
B_{kl}\mapsto B_{kl}+\epsilon\,\delta(t)
, we finds analogously

R^{\eta_{n}}_{B_{kl}}(\omega)=j_{kl}\Bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\Bigr].

(70)

Thus the two responses are proportional
6
;
13
;
5
,

\frac{1}{j_{kl}}R^{\eta_{n}}_{B_{kl}}(\omega)=\frac{2}{a_{kl}}R^{\eta_{n}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega).

(71)

We next turn to the response of the empirical current. For a fixed edge
m\leftrightarrow n
, define

R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\delta\langle\jmath_{nm}(t)\rangle}{\delta F_{kl}(0)}e^{i\omega t}\,dt,

(72)

and similarly for
R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{B_{kl}}(\omega)
. These responses are expressed through the same excess propagator:

\frac{2}{a_{kl}}R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{j_{kl}}R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{B_{kl}}(\omega)

(73)

with

\displaystyle\frac{2}{a_{kl}}R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=\delta_{nk}\delta_{ml}-\delta_{nl}\delta_{mk}

(74)

\displaystyle+W_{nm}\Bigl[H_{mk}(\omega)-H_{ml}(\omega)\Bigr]-W_{mn}\Bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\Bigr].

The first term in (
74
) is the direct local contribution from the impulsive perturbation acting on the perturbed edge, while the remaining terms describe how the perturbation propagates through the network and contributes to the current on the observed edge.
The derivations of (
69
), (
70
), and (
74
) are given in Appendix
B.1
.

Equations (
69
)–(
74
) show that both the state and current response are determined by the same excess propagator
H_{nk}(\omega)
. In the state response, the relevant object is the difference
H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)
. In the current response, the same difference appears, but combined with transition rates in a way that reflects how excess occupation is converted into current along an edge.

IV.4
Local fluctuation–response relation for empirical state and current

We turn from local responses to fluctuations. As in Sec.
III.4
, the aim is to express the covariance functions of the elementary empirical observables in terms of the same local responses derived above. This yields the local form of the frequency-domain FRR for Markov jump systems.

A direct calculation yields the state–state covariance

C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{m}}(\omega)=H_{nm}(\omega)\pi_{m}+H_{mn}(-\omega)\pi_{n}.

(75)

The crucial step is a discrete identity that rewrites (
75
) as a quadratic form

\displaystyle C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{m}}(\omega)

(76)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>l}a_{kl}\Bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\Bigr]\Bigl[H_{mk}(-\omega)-H_{ml}(-\omega)\Bigr].

This identity is the discrete counterpart of Eq. (
32
). Its proof is deferred to Appendix
B.4
.

To express (
76
) in terms of response functions, we define the edge weights

A^{F}_{kl}=\frac{a_{kl}}{4},\qquad A^{B}_{kl}=\frac{j_{kl}^{2}}{a_{kl}}.

(77)

Using Eqs. (
69
) and (
70
), the state–state covariance can then be written as

C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{m}}(\omega)=\sum_{k>l}\frac{1}{A^{\phi}_{kl}}\,R^{\eta_{n}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)\,R^{\eta_{m}}_{\phi_{kl}}(-\omega),

(78)

where
\phi\in\{F,B\}
and
A^{\phi}_{kl}
is defined accordingly.

The same logic applies to the mixed state–current covariance. We obtain

C_{\eta_{n},\jmath_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}(\omega)=\sum_{k>l}\frac{1}{A^{\phi}_{kl}}\,R^{\eta_{n}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)\,R^{\jmath_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}_{\phi_{kl}}(-\omega),

(79)

and an analogous expression holds for the current–current covariance:

C_{\jmath_{nm},\jmath_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}(\omega)=\sum_{k>l}\frac{1}{A^{\phi}_{kl}}\,R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)\,R^{\jmath_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}_{\phi_{kl}}(-\omega).

(80)

Equations (
78
)–(
80
) constitute the local frequency-domain FRR for Markov jump systems.
The proofs of (
79
) and (
80
) are deferred to Appendix
B.3
.

Three features are worth emphasizing. First, the local FRR shows that fluctuations at frequency
\omega
are controlled by the system’s response at the same frequency to perturbations of individual edges. The spectrum thus acquires a direct edgewise interpretation. Second, the perturbation type enters only through the scalar weight
A^{\phi}_{kl}
, while the dynamical information is contained in the local responses themselves. Third, because the decomposition is organized over edges, the jump-process formulation naturally identifies which transition channels dominate a given spectral feature.

IV.5
Frequency-domain fluctuation–response relation for general observables

With the local FRRs established, the extension to general observables is immediate. As in Sec.
III.5
, both the response and the covariance of a general observable follow from combining the corresponding local quantities of the elementary empirical variables.
The combination is determined by the coefficients defining the observable.

Given the form (
56
) of the observable vector, its local response to a perturbation of the edge parameter
\phi_{kl}
is given by

R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)=G\,R^{\bm{\eta}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)+L\,R^{\bm{\jmath}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega),

(81)

where

R^{\bm{\eta}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)=\bigl(R^{\eta_{1}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega),\dots,R^{\eta_{S}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}},

(82)

and similarly for
R^{\bm{\jmath}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)
.

The covariance of
\bm{\theta}(t)
follows from (
56
):

\displaystyle C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)={}

\displaystyle G\,C_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\,G^{\mathsf{T}}+G\,C_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\jmath}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\,L^{\mathsf{T}}

(83)

\displaystyle+L\,C_{\bm{\jmath},\bm{\eta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\,G^{\mathsf{T}}+L\,C_{\bm{\jmath},\bm{\jmath}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\,L^{\mathsf{T}}.

Substituting (
78
)–(
80
) into (
83
) and using (
81
), we obtain

C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)=\sum_{k>l}\frac{1}{A^{\phi}_{kl}}\,R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)\,\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}.

(84)

Equation (
84
) is the frequency-domain FRR for general observables in Markov jump systems. It is the jump-process realization of the unified relation stated in Sec.
II
; cf. (
5
).

The interpretation of (
84
) is transparent in the network setting. The fluctuation spectrum is decomposed into contributions from the responses to perturbations of individual edges. The FRR provides an explicit edgewise interpretation of how the network dynamics shapes the fluctuation spectrum. As in the Langevin case, the relation applies uniformly to purely state-dependent observables, purely current-like observables, and their combinations. The distinction between kinetic and entropic perturbations enters only through the edge weight
A^{\phi}_{kl}
, while the dynamical information is carried by the local responses themselves.

This completes the frequency-domain FRR for Markov jump systems. Taken together, Secs.
III
and
IV
show that a common underlying structure governs both continuous and discrete nonequilibrium dynamics.

V
Consequences of the frequency-domain fluctuation–response relation

In this section, we derive the main consequences of the frequency-domain FRR. Rather than treating these results as separate statements, we show that they arise from a common structure. The RUR follows directly from the quadratic form of the frequency-domain FRR; the response KUR and TUR follow from specific perturbation choices;
the FDT together with Harada–Sasa-type relations arise as two reductions of the same identity, corresponding to equilibrium and nonequilibrium conditions, respectively. To keep the section focused on the physical content, detailed algebraic manipulations are deferred to Appendix
C
.

V.1
Frequency-domain response uncertainty relation

A direct consequence of the frequency-domain FRR is a bound on the response to an arbitrary global perturbation. This bound follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the quadratic response representation of the power spectrum. It provides a frequency-resolved trade-off between the magnitude of the response and the spontaneous fluctuations of the corresponding observable.

We first consider the overdamped Langevin case. For a global perturbation of
\phi\in\{\bm{F},\ln M,T\}
of the form
\phi_{k}(\bm{x})\mapsto\phi_{k}(\bm{x})+\epsilon\,\psi_{k}(\bm{x},t)
, the corresponding response of the observable vector
\bm{\theta}(t)
is given by

R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,\psi_{k}(\bm{x},\omega)\,R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{x})}(\omega).

(85)

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the FRR (
42
) yields (see Appendix
C.1
for details)

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)

(86)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,\psi_{k}(\bm{x},-\omega)A^{\phi}_{kl}(\bm{x})\psi_{l}(\bm{x},\omega),

For a single observable
\theta(t)
, this inequality reduces to

\displaystyle\bigl|R^{\theta}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr|^{2}

(87)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\theta,\theta}(\omega)\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,\psi_{k}(\bm{x},-\omega)A^{\phi}_{kl}(\bm{x})\psi_{l}(\bm{x},\omega).

The same logic applies to Markov jump systems.
For a global perturbation of either the symmetric or antisymmetric edge parameter,
\phi_{nm}\mapsto\phi_{nm}+\epsilon\,\psi_{nm}(t)
(
\phi\in\{B,F\}
), the corresponding response of the observable vector
\bm{\theta}(t)
is given by

R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)=\sum_{n>m}\psi_{nm}(\omega)R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{nm}}(\omega).

(88)

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to (
84
) yields (see Appendix
C.1
for details)

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)

(89)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n>m}A^{\phi}_{nm}\,|\psi_{nm}(\omega)|^{2}\;.

For a single observable
\theta(t)
, this reduces to

\bigl|R^{\theta}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr|^{2}\leq C_{\theta,\theta}(\omega)\sum_{n>m}A^{\phi}_{nm}\,|\psi_{nm}(\omega)|^{2}.

(90)

Equations (
86
) and (
89
) are the frequency-domain RURs. In both continuous and discrete dynamics, the response at frequency
\omega
is determined by two ingredients: the power spectrum of the observable at that frequency,
and a quadratic cost associated with the perturbation. The only difference lies in how locality is organized: over configuration space in the Langevin case, and over edges in the jump-process case.

V.2
Frequency-domain response kinetic and thermodynamic uncertainty relations

The bounds derived in Sec.
V.1
become more informative once the perturbation class is fixed.
In that case, the perturbation weight matrix
A^{\phi}
admits a clear physical meaning, leading to uncertainty relations governed by kinetic or thermodynamic quantities.

We begin with overdamped Langevin systems.
For a force perturbation,
\bm{F}(\bm{x})\mapsto\bm{F}(\bm{x})+\epsilon\,\bm{\psi}(\bm{x},t)
, the weight matrix follows from (
36
) using (
163
):

[A^{\bm{F}}(\bm{x})]_{kl}=\delta_{kl}\,\frac{\pi(\bm{x})\mu_{k}(\bm{x})}{2T_{k}(\bm{x})}.

(91)

Substituting (
91
) into (
86
) gives

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)

(92)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,\frac{\pi(\bm{x})\mu_{k}(\bm{x})}{2T_{k}(\bm{x})}|\psi_{k}(\bm{x},\omega)|^{2}.

Bounding the perturbation amplitude by
|\psi_{k}(\bm{x},\omega)|\leq\psi_{\max}
, the inequality (
92
) further reduces to the compact, albeit looser, bound:

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)

(93)

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{\max}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\langle\frac{\mu_{k}(\bm{x})}{2T_{k}(\bm{x})}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}.

This bound constitutes the frequency-domain extension of the response KUR for overdamped Langevin dynamics
13
, which was originally derived for Markov jump processes in the time domain
36
;
33
.
The finite-frequency fluctuation–response inequality found in
17
is recovered as a special case under separable perturbations, i.e.,
\bm{\psi}(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{q}\bm{g}_{q}(\bm{x})\phi_{q}(t)
, with spatial modes
\bm{g}_{q}(\bm{x})
and temporal amplitudes
\phi_{q}(t)
.

For linear Langevin systems with spatially uniform
M
and
T
, and a force
\bm{F}(\bm{x})=-A\bm{x}
, the power spectrum is known to be
C_{\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)=2(MA-i\omega I)^{-1}D[(MA+i\omega I)^{\mathsf{T}}]^{-1}

31
;
7
.
The response function to a homogeneous force perturbation
\bm{F}(\bm{x})\mapsto\bm{F}(\bm{x})+\epsilon\bm{\psi}
is readily obtained as
R_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}^{\bm{x}}(\omega)=(MA-i\omega I)^{-1}M\bm{\psi}
.
Thus, the response KUR is saturated in this case:

[R_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}^{\bm{x}}(\omega)]^{\dagger}C_{\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}^{\bm{x}}(\omega)=\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}M(2T)^{-1}\bm{\psi}
.
This saturation has been recently verified algebraically in
17
;
7
.
In contrast, the equality condition of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality [Eqs. (
271
) and (
279
)] shows that the saturation arises as a structural consequence of the frequency-domain FRR. This perspective not only explains the saturation of the response KUR in linear Langevin systems, but also provides a potential route to identifying saturation conditions for other response uncertainty relations and more general settings.

A second important choice is the mobility perturbation,
\ln M(\bm{x})\mapsto\ln M(\bm{x})+\epsilon\,\bm{\psi}(\bm{x},t)
.
In this case, the weight matrix is determined by the steady-state current, and (
86
) becomes

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\ln M\mapsto\ln M+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\ln M\mapsto\ln M+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)

(94)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,\frac{[j_{k}^{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})]^{2}}{2\pi(\bm{x})D_{k}(\bm{x})}|\psi_{k}(\bm{x},\omega)|^{2}.

Bounding the perturbation amplitude by
|\psi_{k}(\bm{x},\omega)|\leq\psi_{\max}
, this implies

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\ln M\mapsto\ln M+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\ln M\mapsto\ln M+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)

(95)

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{\max}^{2}\,\frac{\sigma}{2},

where

\sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,\frac{[j_{k}^{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})]^{2}}{\pi(\bm{x})D_{k}(\bm{x})}

(96)

is the entropy production rate.

V.3
Equilibrium limit: fluctuation–dissipation theorem

The frequency-domain FRR reduces to the equilibrium FDT when the steady irreversible currents vanish and the local response satisfies the reciprocity relation. In the present framework, this can be shown directly in the main text, while proofs of the reciprocity identities are deferred to Appendix
C.3
.

We begin with the overdamped Langevin case.
For force perturbations, combining (
29
), (
30
), and (
163
) yields

R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{z})}(\omega)=\mathcal{P}(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\,M(\bm{z})\pi(\bm{z}).

(107)

Appendix
C.4
shows that the current–current covariance can be rewritten as

\displaystyle C_{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x}),\bm{\jmath}(\bm{z})^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)=R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\,T(\bm{z})+T(\bm{x})\bigl[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{z})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{x})}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}

(108)

\displaystyle+\bigl[\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\bigr]\bm{j}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{z})^{\mathsf{T}}+\bm{j}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\bigl[\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{z}}H(\bm{z},\bm{x};\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}.

At equilibrium, the last two terms vanish since
\bm{j}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})=0
, and time-reversal symmetry enforces the reciprocity relation

\bigl[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}=\bigl[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{z})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{x})}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(109)

Therefore, for a spatially homogeneous temperature
T(\bm{x})=T

\bigl[C_{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x}),\bm{\jmath}(\bm{z})^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}=2T\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\right]_{\mathrm{eq}}.

(110)

This is the local frequency-domain FDT for empirical currents.
A current-like observable is a linear functional of the empirical current.
Multiplying by the weight functions
L(\bm{x})
and
L(\bm{z})
and integrating over
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
yields the observable-level FDT for current-like observables.
The proof of (
109
) is given in Appendix
C.3
.

The same logic applies to Markov jump systems. Appendix
C.4
shows that the local current–current covariance can be rewritten as

C_{\jmath_{nm},\jmath_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}(\omega)=R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}(\omega)+R^{\jmath_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}_{F_{nm}}(-\omega)+\Delta_{nm,n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega),

(111)

where
\Delta_{nm,n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega)
is a term proportional to the steady currents and therefore vanishes at equilibrium.
Its explicit definition is given in (
316
) and (
318

At equilibrium,

j_{nm}=0\qquad\text{for all }n,m,

(112)

and time-reversal symmetry yields the reciprocity relation

\bigl[R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}=\bigl[R^{\jmath_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}_{F_{nm}}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}.

(113)

Substituting (
113
) into (
111
) gives

\bigl[C_{\jmath_{nm},\jmath_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}=2\rm{Re}\,\bigl[R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}.

(114)

This is the equilibrium FDT for local edge currents in the Markov jump setting.
A current-like observable is a linear combination of the edge currents.
Multiplying (

\Lambda_{nm}
and
\Lambda_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}
and summing over all edges yields the observable-level equilibrium FDT.
The proof of (
113
) is given in Appendix
C.3

The equilibrium FDT is recovered when the current-dependent terms vanish. Away from equilibrium, these terms persist and encode the extent to which the equilibrium FDT is violated. We next show that this deviation can be expressed in a form related to dissipation.

V.4
FDT violation and Harada–Sasa relations

We isolate the current-dependent correction terms in the full nonequilibrium covariance–response identities (
108
) and (
111
). These terms quantify the violation of the FDT and are therefore responsible for the breakdown of the equilibrium reduction derived in Sec.
V.3
. Integrating them over frequency yields Harada–Sasa-type relations
25
;
26
.

We first consider the overdamped Langevin case. Equation (
108
) shows that the deviation from the equilibrium FDT is carried by the two terms proportional to the steady-state current. Under spatially homogeneous mobility and temperature,

M(\bm{x})=\mu I,\qquad T(\bm{x})=T,

(115)

integrating (
108
) over
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
yields the covariance–response identity for the velocity observable
\dot{\bm{x}}(t)
:

\displaystyle C_{\dot{\bm{x}},\dot{\bm{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)-T\,R^{\dot{\bm{x}}}_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon}(\omega)-T\,\bigl[R^{\dot{\bm{x}}}_{\bm{F}\mapsto\bm{F}+\epsilon}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}

(116)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}(\omega)+\mathcal{Z}(\omega)^{\dagger},

where the current-dependent contribution
\mathcal{Z}(\omega)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\omega)=\int d\bm{x}\int d\bm{z}\bigl[\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\bigr]\bm{j}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{z})^{\mathsf{T}}

(117)

The left-hand side of (
116
) thus quantifies the frequency-resolved violation of the equilibrium FDT for the velocity observable.

Using the identity

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\,\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl[\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})-\pi(\bm{x})\bigr],

(118)

the integrated correction term can be evaluated explicitly.
Taking the trace of the resulting matrix identity gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigl[C_{\dot{x}_{k},\dot{x}_{k}}(\omega)-2T\,\mathrm{Re}\,R^{\dot{x}_{k}}_{F_{k}\mapsto F_{k}+\epsilon}(\omega)\Bigr]\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}

(119)

\displaystyle=\mu\,\langle\dot{Q}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}-\bigl|\langle\dot{\bm{x}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}\bigr|^{2},

where

\langle\dot{Q}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}=\int d\bm{x}\,\bm{F}(\bm{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{j}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})

(120)

is the steady-state heat dissipation rate
50
.
Equation (
119
) is the Harada–Sasa relation, stating that the integrated violation of the FDT is determined by dissipation, up to the correction associated with the mean drift
25
;
26

A more general form can be obtained without assuming homogeneous mobility by introducing the current-like observable
\bm{w}(t)=M(\bm{x}(t))^{-1}\circ\dot{\bm{x}}(t)
. Applying the same procedure to the mixed covariance between
\bm{w}(t)
and
\dot{\bm{x}}(t)
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigl[C_{\dot{x}_{k},w_{k}}(\omega)-2T\,\mathrm{Re}\,R^{w_{k}}_{F_{k}\mapsto F_{k}+\epsilon}(\omega)\Bigr]\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}

(121)

\displaystyle=\langle\dot{Q}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}-\langle\bm{w}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}^{\mathsf{T}}\langle\dot{\bm{x}}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}.

This generalized Harada–Sasa relation was studied in
24
, where the inhomogeneity of the mobility arises from hydrodynamic interactions.

A similar structure arises for Markov jump systems. Equation (
111
) shows that the deviation from the equilibrium FDT is encoded in the correction term
\Delta_{nm,n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\omega)
, which vanishes only when the steady currents vanish.
To relate this edge-based microscopic identity to macroscopic physical observables, we reorganize the description in terms of cycle currents defined on a fundamental set of directed cycles
49
;
28
.
These cycle currents provide a minimal set of independent current-like observables, as symmetries and conservation laws render steady-state edge currents redundant
41
;
8
.
The corresponding thermodynamic forces defined on cycles, known as affinities, represent the physical driving forces that maintain the system out of equilibrium.
Together, they form the appropriate current–force pairs at the macroscopic level.
In particular, since edge currents are not independent, fluctuation–dissipation relations at equilibrium, as well as their violations away from equilibrium, are formulated in terms of these cycle-level observables
54
;
55
;
6
.
Motivated by this structure, we consider a directed cycle
\mathcal{C}
in the network, consisting of the edges

(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}),\,(\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{3}),\,\dots,\,(\mathcal{C}_{n_{\mathcal{C}}},\mathcal{C}_{1}).

(122)

The corresponding empirical cycle current is defined by

\jmath^{\mathcal{C}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{C}}}\jmath_{\mathcal{C}_{i+1},\mathcal{C}_{i}}(t),\qquad\mathcal{C}_{n_{\mathcal{C}}+1}\equiv\mathcal{C}_{1}.

(123)

Summing (
111
) over all pairs of edges in the cycle yields the corresponding covariance–response identity for the cycle current,

C_{\jmath^{\mathcal{C}},\jmath^{\mathcal{C}}}(\omega)=R^{\jmath^{\mathcal{C}}}_{\bm{F}_{\mathcal{C}}\mapsto\bm{F}_{\mathcal{C}}+\epsilon}(\omega)+R^{\jmath^{\mathcal{C}}}_{\bm{F}_{\mathcal{C}}\mapsto\bm{F}_{\mathcal{C}}+\epsilon}(-\omega)+\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\omega),

(124)

where the perturbation acts homogeneously on all edges belonging to the cycle, and
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\omega)
is the corresponding cycle-summed correction term.

The physical content of (

The Harada–Sasa-type relation complements the equilibrium FDT by relating the violation of the latter to dissipation in nonequilibrium steady states.

VI
Examples

In this section, we illustrate three complementary aspects of the frequency-domain FRR. First, in Markov jump networks, the relation provides an exact edge-resolved decomposition of the covariance spectrum, revealing which transition channels dominate fluctuations at a given frequency. Second, in a driven diffusive system, the frequency-domain response bounds show that different uncertainty relations become relevant in different spectral regimes. Third, in a linear Langevin system, the frequency-domain TUR can be analyzed in a controlled setting, where its spectral structure and asymptotic limits can be examined explicitly.

Edge-resolved spectral decomposition in Markov jump networks

A distinctive feature of the frequency-domain FRR for Markov jump systems is the exact decomposition of the covariance spectrum into contributions from individual edges. For a single observable

We first consider a unicyclic network,

E_{1}\overset{k_{1}^{+}}{\underset{k_{2}^{-}}{\rightleftarrows}}E_{2}\overset{k_{2}^{+}}{\underset{k_{3}^{-}}{\rightleftarrows}}E_{3}\cdots E_{N-1}\overset{k_{N-1}^{+}}{\underset{k_{N}^{-}}{\rightleftarrows}}E_{N}\overset{k_{N}^{+}}{\underset{k_{1}^{-}}{\rightleftarrows}}E_{1},

k_{i}^{+}=ke^{\mathcal{F}/N},\qquad k_{i}^{-}=k,

(130)

where
\mathcal{F}
is the cycle affinity. We consider both a state-dependent observable,

, and a current-like observable,
\theta(t)=\jmath_{21}(t)

The corresponding edgewise decompositions are shown in Fig.

\omega^{\ast}=2\pi j_{\mathrm{ss}}=\frac{2\pi k}{N}\bigl(e^{\mathcal{F}/N}-1\bigr).

This example illustrates the utility of the frequency-domain FRR. A conventional covariance spectrum reveals a characteristic frequency of the dynamics, but does not indicate which transition channels are primarily responsible. By contrast, the decomposition (

VI.2
Frequency-dependent response bounds in a tilted periodic potential

We next illustrate that the uncertainty relations arising from the frequency-domain FRR are not merely formal extensions of static bounds, but provide frequency-dependent constraints on measurable response. To this end, we consider an overdamped particle in a tilted periodic potential, shown in Fig.
4
. This model is a standard setting for nonequilibrium transport and giant diffusion
48
;
46
;
47
;
27
.

The dynamics is described by

\dot{x}(t)=\mu\bigl[f_{0}-U^{\prime}(x(t))\bigr]+\sqrt{2\mu T}\,\xi(t),

(133)

where
\mu
is the mobility,
T
is the temperature,
f_{0}
is the constant driving force, and
\xi(t)
is Gaussian white noise satisfying

\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(134)

The periodic potential is taken to be

U(x)=U_{0}\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi x}{L}\right),

(135)

with amplitude
U_{0}
and period
L
. The observable is the instantaneous velocity,
\dot{x}(t)
.

The covariance spectrum
C_{\dot{x},\dot{x}}(\omega)
and the response
R^{\dot{x}}_{f_{0}\mapsto f_{0}+\epsilon}(\omega)
are shown in Fig.
4

We first consider the frequency-domain response KUR associated with perturbing the driving force
f_{0}
.
A homogeneous force perturbation with
\psi(x,t)=1
reduces (
93
) to

C_{\dot{x},\dot{x}}(\omega)\geq\frac{2T}{\mu}\,\bigl|R^{\dot{x}}_{f_{0}\mapsto f_{0}+\epsilon}(\omega)\bigr|^{2}.

(136)

To quantify the tightness of this inequality, we define a quality factor

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{RKUR}}(\omega)=\frac{2T}{\mu}\,\frac{|R^{\dot{x}}_{f_{0}\mapsto f_{0}+\epsilon}(\omega)|^{2}}{C_{\dot{x},\dot{x}}(\omega)}.

(137)

The behavior of
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{RKUR}}(\omega)
is shown in Fig.
4

\omega
and saturates at high frequency. This trend reflects that the bound becomes tighter at higher frequencies, where the velocity response is dominated by the short-time effect of the force perturbation, before the dynamics explores multiple periods of the tilted potential.

We compare the response KUR with the frequency-domain TUR shown in Fig.
4

\ln\mu

C_{\dot{x},\dot{x}}(\omega)\geq\frac{2\langle\dot{x}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}^{2}}{\sigma},

(138)

where
\sigma
is the steady-state entropy production rate. We define the associated quality factor by

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{fTUR}}(\omega)=\frac{2\langle\dot{x}\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}^{2}}{\sigma\,C_{\dot{x},\dot{x}}(\omega)}.

(139)

As shown in Fig.
4

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{fTUR}}(\omega)
decreases monotonically with
\omega
, in contrast to
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{RKUR}}(\omega)
, and is tightest in the static limit
\omega\to 0
.

The contrast between Fig.
4
(c) and (d) highlights a key aspect of this example. The two bounds constrain different physical quantities. Equation (
136
) constrains the response of the velocity to a perturbation of the driving force, whereas Eq. (
138

In this model, the force-response bound becomes tighter toward higher frequencies, while the thermodynamic bound is strongest at low frequency, where steady-state drift dominates the spectrum.

From an experimental perspective, this example is particularly relevant, as both the velocity spectrum and the force response are directly accessible. Figure
4
thus goes beyond a model-specific numerical check, revealing how different uncertainty bounds become tight in distinct spectral regimes and thereby probe different aspects of the dynamics. The frequency-domain FRR and its resulting bounds thus provide a practical framework for interpreting response and fluctuation data in driven diffusive systems.

VI.3
Frequency-domain TUR in a linear Langevin system

We finally consider a linear Langevin system, for which all analytical expressions involved in the frequency-domain TUR are available. This example clarifies the structure of the frequency-domain TUR and show explicitly how the bound connects the low- and high-frequency regimes.

The dynamics is described by

\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=-A\bm{x}(t)+B\bm{\xi}(t),

(140)

where
A

B
is a constant noise-amplitude matrix, and

(141)

where

\frac{2\langle\theta\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}^{2}}{\sigma\,C_{\theta,\theta}(\omega)}\leq 1,

(142)

\sigma

\mathcal{Q}_{\text{fTUR}}=\frac{2\langle\theta\rangle_{\mathrm{ss}}^{2}}{\sigma\,C_{\theta,\theta}(\omega)}.

(143)

The behavior of
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{fTUR}}
is shown in Fig.

Even in a linear system, the frequency-domain TUR is therefore not characterized by a single preferred spectral regime. Instead, its tightness depends on the interplay between deterministic relaxation, noise, and the observable defined by the matrix
W

The linear setting makes the asymptotic content of the frequency-domain TUR transparent. In the limit
\omega\to 0

\omega\to\infty

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have developed a fluctuation–response theory in the frequency domain for nonequilibrium steady states. The central result is a frequency-domain FRR that expresses the power spectrum of general observables in terms of local linear responses at the same frequency. We have established this relation for both overdamped Langevin and Markov jump systems. The decomposition is formulated in configuration space in the Langevin case, while in the Markov jump case it is over transition edges.

The relation applies to state-dependent observables, current-like observables, and their combinations. It thus provides a unified framework for describing fluctuations of occupations and currents in continuous and discrete stochastic dynamics. Compared with earlier nonequilibrium FRRs formulated mainly for static or long-time covariances
6
;
13
, the present formulation applies directly at finite frequency.

We have further shown that several consequences follow from the frequency-domain FRR. By applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have obtained frequency-domain RURs, which reduce to KUR and TUR for specific perturbations. In the equilibrium limit, the FRR recovers the FDT, while away from equilibrium the remaining current-dependent terms yield Harada–Sasa-type relations.
These results show that these seemingly different relations arise as different consequences of a single underlying frequency-domain fluctuation–response structure.

The examples illustrate complementary aspects of the theory. For Markov jump networks, the edge-resolved form of the relation decomposes the power spectrum into contributions from individual transition channels. For driven Langevin systems, the frequency-dependent bounds show that different perturbation classes are relevant in different spectral regimes. These examples demonstrate how the theory serves not only as a formal identity but also as a practical tool for analyzing frequency-resolved fluctuation and response spectra.

Several directions remain open. First, the present formulation suggests an experimental route for reconstructing fluctuation spectra from local or controlled finite-frequency responses, especially in systems where direct spectral measurements and perturbative measurements are both accessible. Second, extending the exact frequency-domain relation to underdamped Langevin dynamics would broaden its applicability to inertial stochastic systems and molecular-scale transport. Third, it would be interesting to investigate whether analogous frequency-domain fluctuation–response structures can be formulated for open quantum systems, where response, noise, and dissipation are constrained by both stochastic and quantum effects.

Several directions remain open. First, an important direction is to test experimentally whether fluctuation spectra can be reconstructed from local or controlled finite-frequency responses, particularly in systems where both spectral and perturbative measurements are accessible. Second, extending the frequency-domain FRR to underdamped Langevin dynamics would broaden its applicability to inertial stochastic systems. Third, it is natural to ask whether analogous frequency-domain fluctuation–response structures can be formulated for open quantum systems, where response, noise, and dissipation are constrained by both stochastic and quantum effects.

A.1
Perturbed Fokker–Planck generator and local response formulas

In this subsection, we derive the local response formulas stated in Sec.
III
. We consider the overdamped Langevin dynamics

\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=M(\bm{x}(t))\bm{F}(\bm{x}(t))+\sqrt{2}\,B(\bm{x}(t))\circledast\bm{\xi}(t),

(144)

where
\circledast
denotes the anti-Itô product, and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}
is the Fokker–Planck generator given by

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}=-\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}},\qquad\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}=M(\bm{x})\bigl[\bm{F}(\bm{x})-T(\bm{x})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bigr],

(145)

We use the relation
\frac{1}{2}B(\bm{x})B(\bm{x})^{\mathsf{T}}=D(\bm{x})=M(\bm{x})T(\bm{x})
.
The steady-state distribution
\pi(\bm{x})
satisfies

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}\pi(\bm{x})=0,

(146)

and the corresponding steady-state current is

\bm{j}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})=\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}\pi(\bm{x}).

(147)

We consider a local perturbation of the
k
th component of
\phi\in\{\bm{F},\ln M,T\}
at the point
\bm{z}
and time
t=0
:

\phi_{k}(\bm{x})\mapsto\phi_{k}(\bm{x})+\epsilon\,\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})\delta(t).

(148)

At the level of the Fokker–Planck generator, the perturbation can be written as

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}-\epsilon\,\delta(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})\,\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\phi_{k},\bm{x}}\,,

(149)

where
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\phi_{k},\bm{x}}
depends on the perturbed quantity. In the diagonal setting adopted in the main text, these operators are

\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{F_{k},\bm{x}}=M(\bm{x})\,\bm{e}_{k},

(150)

\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\ln M_{k},\bm{x}}=\mu_{k}(\bm{x})\,\bm{e}_{k}\bm{e}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl[\bm{F}(\bm{x})-T(\bm{x})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bigr],

(151)

and

\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{T_{k},\bm{x}}=-M(\bm{x})\,\bm{e}_{k}\bm{e}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{\bm{x}}.

(152)

Here
\bm{e}_{k}
denotes the
k
th unit vector.

Assuming a steady-state initial condition, we expand the probability density as

p(\bm{x},t)=\pi(\bm{x})+\epsilon\,q_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{x},t;\bm{z})+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(153)

Substituting this expansion into the perturbed Fokker–Planck equation and retaining terms up to linear order in
\epsilon
, we obtain

\partial_{t}q_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{x},t;\bm{z})=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}q_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{x},t;\bm{z})-\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})\,\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\phi_{k},\bm{z}}\pi(\bm{z})\,\delta(t).

(154)

It is therefore natural to define

N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z})\equiv\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\phi_{k},\bm{z}}\pi(\bm{z}).

(155)

With this notation, the linearized equation becomes

\partial_{t}q_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{x},t;\bm{z})=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}q_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{x},t;\bm{z})-\delta(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})\,N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z})\,.

(156)

Solving the linearized equation by convolution with the unperturbed propagator
P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)
, we obtain

q_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{x},t;\bm{z})=\bigl[N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z})\bigr]^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{\bm{z}}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0),

(157)

where the derivative with respect to
\bm{z}
arises from integration by parts acting on the delta function.
Introducing the excess propagator

H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl[P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)-\pi(\bm{x})\bigr]e^{i\omega t}\,dt,

(158)

the response of the empirical density is given by

R^{\rho(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)=\bigl[N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z})\bigr]^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{\bm{z}}H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega).

(159)

The response of the empirical current follows from the linear relation
\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x},t)=\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}p(\bm{x},t)
:

\delta\langle\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x},t)\rangle=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})\,N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z})\,\delta(t)+\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}\,q_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{x},t;\bm{z}),

(160)

where the first and second terms arise from the perturbations of
\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}
and
p(\bm{x},t)
, respectively.
Taking the Fourier transform in time, we obtain

R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{z})}(\omega)=\mathcal{P}(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\,N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z}).

(161)

where

\mathcal{P}(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)=I\,\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})+\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\bm{x}}\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{\mathsf{T}}H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega).

(162)

Finally, in the diagonal setting adopted in the main text, the explicit local prefactors are given by

N_{F_{k}}(\bm{z})=\pi(\bm{z})\mu_{k}(\bm{z})\,\bm{e}_{k},

(163)

N_{\ln M_{k}}(\bm{z})=j_{k}^{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{z})\,\bm{e}_{k},

(164)

and

N_{T_{k}}(\bm{z})=-\mu_{k}(\bm{z})\,\partial_{z_{k}}\pi(\bm{z})\,\bm{e}_{k}.

(165)

The second identity follows from the definition of the steady-state current,

j_{k}^{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{z})=\mu_{k}(\bm{z})\Bigl[F_{k}(\bm{z})\pi(\bm{z})-T_{k}(\bm{z})\partial_{z_{k}}\pi(\bm{z})\Bigr].

(166)

Thus, once the perturbation-dependent vector
N_{\phi_{k}}(\bm{z})
is identified, both density and current responses are determined by the same excess propagator.

A.2
Properties of the excess propagator

In this subsection, we derive the identities for the excess propagator used in Sec.
III
. We recall its definition,

H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl[P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)-\pi(\bm{x})\bigr]e^{i\omega t}\,dt.

(167)

The propagator satisfies both the forward and backward Fokker–Planck equations,

\partial_{t}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0),

(168)

\partial_{t}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{z}}^{\dagger}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0),

(169)

together with

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z}),\qquad\lim_{t\to\infty}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)=\pi(\bm{x}),

(170)

where
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{z}}^{\dagger}
denotes the adjoint of the Fokker-Planck generator acting the initial position
\bm{z}
.

We first show that

\int d\bm{z}\,H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\pi(\bm{z})=0.

(171)

Using the stationarity of the steady-state distribution,

\int d\bm{z}\,P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)\pi(\bm{z})=\pi(\bm{x}),

(172)

and therefore

\displaystyle\int d\bm{z}\,H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\pi(\bm{z})

(173)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\omega t}\int d\bm{z}\,\bigl[P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)-\pi(\bm{x})\bigr]\pi(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=0.

We next show that

\bigl(i\omega+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}\bigr)H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)=-\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})+\pi(\bm{x}).

(174)

This follows by rewriting the integrand as a total time derivative and evaluating the boundary terms at
t=0
and
t\to\infty
:

\displaystyle\bigl(i\omega+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}\bigr)H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)

(175)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\omega t}\Bigl[i\omega\bigl(P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)-\pi(\bm{x})\bigr)+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{x}}P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\partial_{t}\Bigl(e^{i\omega t}[P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)-\pi(\bm{x})]\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\Bigl[e^{i\omega t}[P(\bm{x},t|\bm{z},0)-\pi(\bm{x})]\Bigr]_{t=0}^{t=\infty}

\displaystyle=-\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})+\pi(\bm{x}).

Similarly,

\bigl(i\omega+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{z}}^{\dagger}\bigr)H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)=-\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})+\pi(\bm{x}).

(176)

This follows by the same argument, using the backward Fokker–Planck equation.

Equations (
174
) and (
176
) imply that
H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)
is the resolvent of the Fokker–Planck operator projected onto the subspace orthogonal to the steady state.

B.1
Perturbed master equation and local response formulas

In this subsection, we derive the local response formulas stated in Sec.
IV
. The structure closely parallels Appendix
A.1
. We consider a local perturbation applied at time
t=0
on the edge
k\leftrightarrow l
(with
k>l
). The unperturbed dynamics is governed by

\partial_{t}\bm{p}(t)=\mathcal{W}\bm{p}(t),

(202)

with stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}
satisfying

\mathcal{W}\bm{\pi}=0.

(203)

The evolution under perturbation reads

\partial_{t}\bm{p}(t)=\bigl[\mathcal{W}+\epsilon\,\delta(t)\mathcal{K}_{\phi_{kl}}\bigr]\bm{p}(t),

(204)

where
\phi\in\{F,B\}
labels the type of perturbation.

An entropic perturbation
F_{kl}\mapsto F_{kl}+\epsilon\delta(t)

modifies the antisymmetric part of the rates as

\delta W_{kl}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}W_{kl}\delta(t),\qquad\delta W_{lk}=-\frac{\epsilon}{2}W_{lk}\delta(t),

(205)

which yields

\mathcal{K}_{F_{kl}}=\frac{W_{kl}}{2}\bigl(E_{kl}-E_{ll}\bigr)-\frac{W_{lk}}{2}\bigl(E_{lk}-E_{kk}\bigr).

(206)

Likewise, a kinetic perturbation
B_{kl}\mapsto B_{kl}+\epsilon\delta(t)

modifies the symmetric part of the rates as

\delta W_{kl}=\epsilon W_{kl}\delta(t),\qquad\delta W_{lk}=\epsilon W_{lk}\delta(t),

(207)

which yields

\mathcal{K}_{B_{kl}}=W_{kl}\bigl(E_{kl}-E_{ll}\bigr)+W_{lk}\bigl(E_{lk}-E_{kk}\bigr).

(208)

Here
E_{nm}
denotes the matrix with a single nonzero entry
1
at row
n
, column
m
.

Assuming the steady-state initial condition, we expand

\bm{p}(t)=\bm{\pi}+\epsilon\,\bm{q}_{\phi_{kl}}(t)+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(209)

Substituting into the perturbed master equation and retaining only terms linear in
\epsilon
, we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\bm{q}_{\phi_{kl}}(t)=\mathcal{W}\bm{q}_{\phi_{kl}}(t)+\delta(t)\mathcal{K}_{\phi_{kl}}\bm{\pi},\qquad\bm{q}_{\phi_{kl}}(0^{-})=\bm{0}.

(210)

We define the local prefactor vector by

\bm{N}_{\phi_{kl}}\equiv\mathcal{K}_{\phi_{kl}}\bm{\pi}.

(211)

as a discrete analogue of (
27
) for Langevin systems.
The explicit expressions for
\phi\in\{F,B\}
follow as

\bm{N}_{F_{kl}}\equiv\mathcal{K}_{F_{kl}}\bm{\pi}=\frac{a_{kl}}{2}\,(\bm{e}_{k}-\bm{e}_{l}),

(212)

and

\bm{N}_{B_{kl}}\equiv\mathcal{K}_{B_{kl}}\bm{\pi}=j_{kl}\,(\bm{e}_{k}-\bm{e}_{l}).

(213)

The linearized equation then takes the compact form

\frac{d}{dt}\bm{q}_{\phi_{kl}}(t)=\mathcal{W}\bm{q}_{\phi_{kl}}(t)+\delta(t)\bm{N}_{\phi_{kl}}.

(214)

Its solution is

\bm{q}_{\phi_{kl}}(t)=e^{\mathcal{W}t}\bm{N}_{\phi_{kl}}\qquad(t>0).

(215)

Since
\langle\eta_{n}(t)\rangle=p_{n}(t)
, the response of the empirical state indicator coincides with that of
p_{n}(t)
and is given by the
n
th component of
\bm{q}_{\phi_{kl}}(t)
. Defining

H_{nm}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl([e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nm}-\pi_{n}\bigr)e^{i\omega t}\,dt,

(216)

and using the relation
\sum_{m}(\bm{N}_{\phi_{kl}})_{m}=0
due to probability conservation, we obtain

R^{\eta_{n}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)=\sum_{m}H_{nm}(\omega)(\bm{N}_{\phi_{kl}})_{m}.

(217)

The explicit expressions for
\phi\in\{F,B\}
follow as

R^{\eta_{n}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=\frac{a_{kl}}{2}\bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\bigr],

(218)

and

R^{\eta_{n}}_{B_{kl}}(\omega)=j_{kl}\bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\bigr].

(219)

The two responses share a common factor and therefore are proportional to each other:

\frac{2}{a_{kl}}R^{\eta_{n}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{j_{kl}}R^{\eta_{n}}_{B_{kl}}(\omega)=H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega).

(220)

We next derive the response of the empirical current

\jmath_{nm}(t)=\dot{N}_{nm}(t)-\dot{N}_{mn}(t).

(221)

For edges different from the perturbed one, the expectation value in the perturbed dynamics is

\langle\jmath_{nm}(t)\rangle=W_{nm}p_{m}(t)-W_{mn}p_{n}(t),

(222)

and therefore the response is entirely determined by the response of
\bm{p}
.
When the observed edge coincides with the perturbed one, there is an additional instantaneous contribution from the explicit perturbation of the rates.
Hence the response of the empirical current is given by

R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)=\Gamma^{\phi}_{nm;kl}+W_{nm}R^{\eta_{m}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega)-W_{mn}R^{\eta_{n}}_{\phi_{kl}}(\omega),

(223)

where

\Gamma^{F}_{nm;kl}=\frac{a_{kl}}{2}\bigl(\delta_{nk}\delta_{ml}-\delta_{nl}\delta_{mk}\bigr),

(224)

and

\Gamma^{B}_{nm;kl}=j_{kl}\bigl(\delta_{nk}\delta_{ml}-\delta_{nl}\delta_{mk}\bigr).

(225)

Substituting (
218
) and (
219
) into (
223
), we obtain

\displaystyle R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=\frac{a_{kl}}{2}\Bigl[\delta_{nk}\delta_{ml}-\delta_{nl}\delta_{mk}+W_{nm}\bigl(H_{mk}(\omega)-H_{ml}(\omega)\bigr)-W_{mn}\bigl(H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\bigr)\Bigr],

(226)

and

\displaystyle R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{B_{kl}}(\omega)=j_{kl}\Bigl[\delta_{nk}\delta_{ml}-\delta_{nl}\delta_{mk}+W_{nm}\bigl(H_{mk}(\omega)-H_{ml}(\omega)\bigr)-W_{mn}\bigl(H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\bigr)\Bigr].

(227)

Equivalently,

\frac{2}{a_{kl}}R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{j_{kl}}R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{B_{kl}}(\omega).

(228)

Thus the perturbation type enters only through the amplitudes
a_{kl}/2
and
j_{kl}
, while the remaining dynamical dependence is governed by the same excess propagator.

B.2
Properties of the discrete excess propagator

We now derive several identities for the discrete excess propagator defined in (
216
). To this end, we use the following limiting behaviors:

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}[e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nk}=\delta_{nk},\qquad\lim_{t\to\infty}[e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nk}=\pi_{n}.

(229)

First, we show that

\sum_{m}H_{nm}(\omega)\pi_{m}=0.

(230)

Multiplying (
216
) by
\pi_{m}
and summing over
m
, we obtain

\sum_{m}H_{nm}(\omega)\pi_{m}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\omega t}\sum_{m}\bigl([e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nm}-\pi_{n}\bigr)\pi_{m}=0.

(231)

The last equality follows from stationarity,

\sum_{m}[e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nm}\pi_{m}=\pi_{n}.

(232)

Second, we show that

\sum_{m}\bigl(i\omega\delta_{nm}+\mathcal{W}_{nm}\bigr)H_{mk}(\omega)=-\delta_{nk}+\pi_{n}.

(233)

Using

\frac{d}{dt}[e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nk}=\sum_{m}\mathcal{W}_{nm}[e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{mk},

(234)

we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\bigl(i\omega\delta_{nm}+\mathcal{W}_{nm}\bigr)H_{mk}(\omega)

(235)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\omega t}\sum_{m}\Bigl[i\omega\delta_{nm}\bigl([e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{mk}-\pi_{m}\bigr)+\mathcal{W}_{nm}[e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{mk}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\partial_{t}\Bigl(e^{i\omega t}\bigl([e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nk}-\pi_{n}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-\delta_{nk}+\pi_{n}.

Finally, using

\frac{d}{dt}[e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nk}=\sum_{m}[e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nm}\mathcal{W}_{mk},

(236)

we analogously obtain

\sum_{m}H_{nm}(\omega)\bigl(i\omega\delta_{mk}+\mathcal{W}_{mk}\bigr)=-\delta_{nk}+\pi_{n}.

(237)

Equations (
230
)–(
237
) show that
H_{nk}(\omega)
is the resolvent of the Markov generator projected onto the subspace orthogonal to the stationary distribution.

B.3
Covariances of empirical state indicators and edge currents

We now derive the covariance formulas for the empirical state indicators and empirical edge currents. We begin with

\eta_{n}(t)=\delta_{X(t),n}.

(238)

For
t>0

\langle\eta_{n}(t)\eta_{m}(0)\rangle-\pi_{n}\pi_{m}=\bigl([e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nm}-\pi_{n}\bigr)\pi_{m},

(239)

whereas for

\langle\eta_{n}(t)\eta_{m}(0)\rangle-\pi_{n}\pi_{m}=\bigl([e^{\mathcal{W}(-t)}]_{mn}-\pi_{m}\bigr)\pi_{n}.

Taking the Fourier transform in time, we obtain

C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{m}}(\omega)=H_{nm}(\omega)\pi_{m}+H_{mn}(-\omega)\pi_{n}.

(241)

As shown in the next subsection, this can be equivalently expressed in the quadratic form

\displaystyle C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{m}}(\omega)

(242)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>l}a_{kl}\bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\bigr]\bigl[H_{mk}(-\omega)-H_{ml}(-\omega)\bigr].

Next, we decompose the empirical current (

\jmath_{nm}(t)=W_{nm}\eta_{m}(t)-W_{mn}\eta_{n}(t)+\zeta_{nm}(t),

(243)

where we define

\zeta_{nm}(t)=[\dot{N}_{nm}(t)-W_{nm}\eta_{m}(t)]-[\dot{N}_{mn}(t)-W_{mn}\eta_{n}(t)]\;.

(244)

A direct calculation gives

\langle\zeta_{nm}(t)\zeta_{kl}(0)\rangle=a_{nm}\bigl(\delta_{nk}\delta_{ml}-\delta_{nl}\delta_{mk}\bigr)\delta(t),

(245)

and therefore

C_{\zeta_{nm},\zeta_{kl}}(\omega)=a_{nm}\bigl(\delta_{nk}\delta_{ml}-\delta_{nl}\delta_{mk}\bigr).

(246)

We next compute the mixed covariance between the state indicator and the explicit noise part of the current.
The definition of
\zeta_{kl}(t)
and the Markov property yield

\langle\eta_{n}(t)\zeta_{kl}(0)\rangle=a_{kl}\bigl([e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nk}-[e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{nl}\bigr),

(247)

for
t>0
, which implies

C_{\eta_{n},\zeta_{kl}}(\omega)=a_{kl}\bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\bigr].

(248)

Likewise, exchanging the order of the variables and applying
t\to-t
(
\omega\to-\omega
in the Fourier domain), we obtain

C_{\zeta_{nm},\eta_{k}}(\omega)=a_{nm}\bigl[H_{km}(-\omega)-H_{kn}(-\omega)\bigr].

(249)

From (
243
), the mixed state–current covariance reads

C_{\eta_{n},\jmath_{kl}}(\omega)=W_{kl}C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{l}}(\omega)-W_{lk}C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{k}}(\omega)+C_{\eta_{n},\zeta_{kl}}(\omega).

(250)

Substituting (
241
) and (
248
) into (
250
), we obtain

\displaystyle C_{\eta_{n},\jmath_{kl}}(\omega)={}

\displaystyle W_{kl}\Bigl[H_{nl}(\omega)\pi_{l}+H_{ln}(-\omega)\pi_{n}\Bigr]-W_{lk}\Bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)\pi_{k}+H_{kn}(-\omega)\pi_{n}\Bigr]

(251)

\displaystyle+a_{kl}\bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\bigr].

Using (
242

a_{kl}[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)]=\sum_{k^{\prime}>l^{\prime}}a_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}[\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}-\delta_{kl^{\prime}}\delta_{lk^{\prime}}][H_{nk^{\prime}}(\omega)-H_{nl^{\prime}}(\omega)]\;,

(252)

we rewrite (
251
) as

\displaystyle C_{\eta_{n},\jmath_{kl}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}>l^{\prime}}a_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}[H_{nk^{\prime}}(\omega)-H_{nl^{\prime}}(\omega)]

(253)

\displaystyle\times\{\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}-\delta_{kl^{\prime}}\delta_{lk^{\prime}}+W_{kl}[H_{lk^{\prime}}(-\omega)-H_{ll^{\prime}}(-\omega)]-W_{lk}[H_{kk^{\prime}}(-\omega)-H_{kl^{\prime}}(-\omega)]\}

Similarly, the current–current covariance reads

\displaystyle C_{\jmath_{nm},\jmath_{kl}}(\omega)={}

\displaystyle W_{nm}W_{kl}C_{\eta_{m},\eta_{l}}(\omega)-W_{nm}W_{lk}C_{\eta_{m},\eta_{k}}(\omega)

(254)

\displaystyle-W_{mn}W_{kl}C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{l}}(\omega)+W_{mn}W_{lk}C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{k}}(\omega)

\displaystyle+W_{nm}C_{\eta_{m},\zeta_{kl}}(\omega)-W_{mn}C_{\eta_{n},\zeta_{kl}}(\omega)

\displaystyle+W_{kl}C_{\zeta_{nm},\eta_{l}}(\omega)-W_{lk}C_{\zeta_{nm},\eta_{k}}(\omega)+C_{\zeta_{nm},\zeta_{kl}}(\omega).

Substituting (
242
), (
246
), and (

\displaystyle C_{\jmath_{nm},\jmath_{kl}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}>l^{\prime}}a_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\{\delta_{nk^{\prime}}\delta_{ml^{\prime}}-\delta_{nl^{\prime}}\delta_{mk^{\prime}}+W_{nm}[H_{mk^{\prime}}(-\omega)-H_{ml^{\prime}}(-\omega)]-W_{mn}[H_{nk^{\prime}}(-\omega)-H_{nl^{\prime}}(-\omega)]\}

(255)

\displaystyle\times\{\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}-\delta_{kl^{\prime}}\delta_{lk^{\prime}}+W_{kl}[H_{lk^{\prime}}(-\omega)-H_{ll^{\prime}}(-\omega)]-W_{lk}[H_{kk^{\prime}}(-\omega)-H_{kl^{\prime}}(-\omega)]\}\;.

B.4
Proof of the discrete quadratic identity for the state–state covariance

In this subsection, we prove the quadratic representation (
242
). To this end, we first rewrite
H_{nm}(\omega)\pi_{m}
in terms of a Kronecker-delta function as

H_{nm}(\omega)\pi_{m}=\sum_{k}H_{nk}(\omega)\pi_{k}\delta_{km}.

(256)

Using the identity (
237
) at frequency
-\omega
, we rewrite the Kronecker-delta as

\delta_{km}=-\sum_{l}\bigl(-i\omega\delta_{lk}+\mathcal{W}_{lk}\bigr)H_{ml}(-\omega)+\pi_{m}.

(257)

Substituting (
257
) into (
256
) and using (
230
), we have

\displaystyle H_{nm}(\omega)\pi_{m}

\displaystyle=i\omega\sum_{k}H_{nk}(\omega)H_{mk}(-\omega)\pi_{k}

(258)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k,l}H_{nk}(\omega)\mathcal{W}_{lk}\pi_{k}H_{ml}(-\omega).

Separating the
l=k
term and using

\mathcal{W}_{kk}=-\sum_{l(\neq k)}W_{lk},

(259)

we find

\displaystyle H_{nm}(\omega)\pi_{m}=i\omega\sum_{k}H_{nk}(\omega)H_{mk}(-\omega)\pi_{k}

(260)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\neq l}H_{nk}(\omega)W_{lk}\pi_{k}\bigl[H_{mk}(-\omega)-H_{ml}(-\omega)\bigr].

Similarly,
H_{mn}(-\omega)\pi_{n}
is obtained by exchanging
n\leftrightarrow m
and
\omega\leftrightarrow-\omega
:

\displaystyle H_{mn}(-\omega)\pi_{n}=-i\omega\sum_{k}H_{nk}(\omega)H_{mk}(-\omega)\pi_{k}

(261)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\neq l}H_{mk}(-\omega)W_{lk}\pi_{k}\bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\bigr].

The state-state covariance is given by the sum of the two expressions (
260
) and (
261
), where the terms proportional to
i\omega
cancel.

The resulting expression can be rearranged as

\displaystyle C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{m}}(\omega)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq l}W_{lk}\pi_{k}\Bigl[2H_{nk}(\omega)H_{mk}(-\omega)-H_{nk}(\omega)H_{ml}(-\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)H_{mk}(-\omega)\Bigr].

(262)

The stationary condition

\sum_{l(\neq k)}W_{lk}\pi_{k}=\sum_{l(\neq k)}W_{kl}\pi_{l}.

(263)

implies

\sum_{k\neq l}W_{lk}\pi_{k}H_{nk}(\omega)H_{mk}(-\omega)=\sum_{k\neq l}W_{lk}\pi_{k}H_{nl}(\omega)H_{ml}(-\omega),

(264)

under relabelling

Accordingly, (

\displaystyle C_{\eta_{n},\eta_{m}}(\omega)

(265)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\neq l}W_{lk}\pi_{k}\bigl[H_{nk}(\omega)-H_{nl}(\omega)\bigr]\bigl[H_{mk}(-\omega)-H_{ml}(-\omega)\bigr].

Separating the sum into contributions with
k>l
and
k<l
, and exchanging dummy indices in the latter, yields (
242
).

Appendix C
Derivations of the consequences of the frequency-domain fluctuation–response relation

C.1
Derivation of the frequency-domain response uncertainty relation

In this subsection, we derive the frequency-domain RUR stated in Sec.
V.1
. This follows directly from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the quadratic response representation of the covariance matrix.

We begin with the overdamped Langevin case.
For an arbitrary vector
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{O}}}
, multiplying (
42
) from the left by
\bm{u}^{\dagger}
and from the right by
\bm{u}
gives

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\bm{u}

(266)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,\bm{u}^{\dagger}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{k}(\bm{x})}(\omega)\,[A^{\phi}(\bm{x})^{-1}]_{kl}\,\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{l}(\bm{x})}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}\bm{u}.

We define two functions

f_{k}(\bm{x},\omega)=\sum_{l=1}^{N}[A^{\phi}(\bm{x})^{1/2}]_{kl}\,\psi_{l}(\bm{x},\omega),

(267)

and

g_{k}(\bm{x},\omega;\bm{u})=\sum_{l=1}^{N}[A^{\phi}(\bm{x})^{-1/2}]_{kl}\,\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{l}(\bm{x})}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}\bm{u}.

(268)

that connect the response to a global perturbation
\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}
and the covariance via

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,g_{k}(\bm{x},-\omega;\bm{u})f_{k}(\bm{x},\omega)=\bm{u}^{\dagger}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega),

(269)

and

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,|g_{k}(\bm{x},\omega;\bm{u})|^{2}=\bm{u}^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\bm{u}.

(270)

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to (
269
) yields

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\bigl|\bm{u}^{\dagger}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr|^{2}

(271)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl[\bm{u}^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\bm{u}\bigr]\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,\psi_{k}(\bm{x},-\omega)\,A^{\phi}_{kl}(\bm{x})\,\psi_{l}(\bm{x},\omega).

where we used

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,|f_{k}(\bm{x},\omega)|^{2}=\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,\psi_{k}(\bm{x},-\omega)\,A^{\phi}_{kl}(\bm{x})\,\psi_{l}(\bm{x},\omega).

(272)

Assuming that
C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)
is invertible on the subspace considered, we choose

\bm{u}=C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega).

(273)

This yields

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)

(274)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}\int d\bm{x}\,\psi_{k}(\bm{x},-\omega)\,A^{\phi}_{kl}(\bm{x})\,\psi_{l}(\bm{x},\omega),

which establishes the frequency-domain RUR for overdamped Langevin systems.

We next consider Markov jump systems.
For an arbitrary vector
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{O}}
, multiplying (
84
) from the left by
\bm{u}^{\dagger}
and from the right by
\bm{u}
gives

\bm{u}^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\bm{u}=\sum_{n>m}\frac{1}{A^{\phi}_{nm}}\bm{u}^{\dagger}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{nm}}(\omega)\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{nm}}(-\omega)\bigr]^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{u}.

(275)

We define two objects

\displaystyle f_{nm}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{A^{\phi}_{nm}}\,\psi_{nm}(\omega),

(276)

\displaystyle g_{nm}(\omega;\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A^{\phi}_{nm}}}\,R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi_{nm}}(-\omega)^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{u},

that connect the response to a global perturbation
\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}
and the covariance via

\sum_{n>m}g_{nm}(\omega;\bm{u})^{*}f_{nm}(\omega)=\bm{u}^{\dagger}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega),

(277)

and

\sum_{n>m}|g_{nm}(\omega;\bm{u})|^{2}=\bm{u}^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\bm{u}.

(278)

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to (
277
) yields

\bigl|\bm{u}^{\dagger}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr|^{2}\leq\bigl[\bm{u}^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)\bm{u}\bigr]\sum_{n>m}A^{\phi}_{nm}|\psi_{nm}(\omega)|^{2},

(279)

where we used

\sum_{n>m}|f_{nm}(\omega)|^{2}=\sum_{n>m}A^{\phi}_{nm}|\psi_{nm}(\omega)|^{2}.

(280)

Choosing

\bm{u}=C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega),

(281)

we arrive at

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)\bigr]^{\dagger}C_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\omega)^{-1}R^{\bm{\theta}}_{\phi\mapsto\phi+\epsilon\bm{\psi}}(\omega)

(282)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n>m}A^{\phi}_{nm}|\psi_{nm}(\omega)|^{2},

which is the frequency-domain RUR for Markov jump systems.

C.2
Derivation of the frequency-domain thermodynamic uncertainty relations

In this subsection, we derive the frequency-domain TURs, (

C.3
Proof of the equilibrium reciprocity identities

In this subsection, we prove the reciprocity identities used in Sec.
V.3

We begin with the overdamped Langevin case.

Integrating this relation over

We consider the local current response to a force perturbation.
Using (
161

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\bigr]_{ij}=\mu_{i}(\bm{x})\pi(\bm{x})\delta_{ij}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{z})

(295)

\displaystyle+\mu_{i}(\bm{x})\mu_{j}(\bm{z})\bigl[F_{i}(\bm{x})-T\partial_{x_{i}}\bigr]\partial_{z_{j}}H(\bm{x},\bm{z};\omega)\,\pi(\bm{z}).

Using (
294
) and

\bigl[F_{i}(\bm{x})-T\partial_{x_{i}}\bigr]\pi(\bm{x})=0,

(296)

the second term becomes

-\mu_{i}(\bm{x})\mu_{j}(\bm{z})T\pi(\bm{x})\,\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{z_{j}}H(\bm{z},\bm{x};\omega).

(297)

The same expression is obtained upon exchanging
(i,\bm{x})\leftrightarrow(j,\bm{z})
. Therefore, the response function (
295

\bigl[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\bigr]_{ij}=\bigl[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{z})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{x})}(\omega)\bigr]_{ji}.

(298)

and, in matrix form,

\bigl[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{x})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{z})}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}=\bigl[R^{\bm{\jmath}(\bm{z})}_{\bm{F}(\bm{x})}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(299)

We now turn to Markov jump systems and prove the discrete reciprocity relation

\bigl[R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}=\bigl[R^{\jmath_{kl}}_{F_{nm}}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}.

(300)

For clarity, we proceed in the time domain.
The time-domain counterpart of the response function (
226
) reads

\displaystyle r^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{kl}}(t)=\frac{a_{kl}}{2}\Bigl[\bigl(\delta_{nk}\delta_{ml}-\delta_{nl}\delta_{mk}\bigr)\delta(t)

(301)

\displaystyle+W_{nm}\bigl(P_{mk}(t)-P_{ml}(t)\bigr)-W_{mn}\bigl(P_{nk}(t)-P_{nl}(t)\bigr)\Bigr],

where
P_{ab}(t)\equiv[e^{\mathcal{W}t}]_{ab}
.

At equilibrium, detailed balance implies

W_{ab}\pi_{b}=W_{ba}\pi_{a},\qquad\pi_{b}P_{ab}(t)=\pi_{a}P_{ba}(t),

(302)

which in turn yields

a_{ab}=2W_{ab}\pi_{b}=2W_{ba}\pi_{a}.

(303)

To prove the reciprocity (
300
), we examine how the terms in the response function (
301
) transform under the exchange
(nm)\leftrightarrow(kl)
at equilibrium.
The singular term proportional to
\delta(t)
is manifestly symmetric. For the regular part, the individual terms are not separately invariant, but are mapped into one another.
We next examine one of the regular terms
(a_{kl}/2)W_{nm}P_{mk}(t)
.
By repeated use of the detailed balance conditions (
302
) and (
303
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{a_{kl}}{2}W_{nm}P_{mk}(t)

\displaystyle=W_{kl}\pi_{l}\,W_{nm}P_{mk}(t)

(304)

\displaystyle=W_{mn}\pi_{n}W_{lk}P_{km}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{nm}}{2}W_{lk}P_{km}(t).

This reflects the balance between the trajectory
(l\to k\to\cdots\to m\to n)
and its time reversal
(n\to m\to\cdots\to k\to l)
.
The remaining three regular terms are handled in the same way:

\frac{a_{kl}}{2}W_{nm}P_{ml}(t)=\frac{a_{nm}}{2}W_{kl}P_{lm}(t),

(305)

\frac{a_{kl}}{2}W_{mn}P_{nk}(t)=\frac{a_{nm}}{2}W_{lk}P_{kn}(t),

(306)

and

\frac{a_{kl}}{2}W_{mn}P_{nl}(t)=\frac{a_{nm}}{2}W_{kl}P_{ln}(t).

(307)

Taken (
304
)–(
307
) together, the regular part of the response function (
301
) is as a whole symmetric under
(nm)\leftrightarrow(kl)
.
Thus, the full time-domain response (
301
) is symmetric under the exchange
(nm)\leftrightarrow(kl)
,

r^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{kl}}(t)=r^{\jmath_{kl}}_{F_{nm}}(t)\qquad(t\geq 0).

(308)

Taking the Fourier transform, we obtain

\bigl[R^{\jmath_{nm}}_{F_{kl}}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}}=\bigl[R^{\jmath_{kl}}_{F_{nm}}(\omega)\bigr]_{\mathrm{eq}},

(309)

which is the reciprocity identity used in Sec.
V.3
.
