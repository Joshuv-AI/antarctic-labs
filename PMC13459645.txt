Mechanisms and Research Progress of Leech in Diabetic Ulcer Fibrosis
Authors: Yanchun Wang, Xianglong Zheng, Huafa Que, Zhangren Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDelayed wound healing is a major complication of diabetes, with its core pathological process being pathological fibrosis driven by persistent inflammation, metabolic disorders and vascular dysfunction under hyperglycemia. This process involves pathological retention of inflammatory cells, abnormal expression of pro‐fibrotic factors, and excessive deposition of extracellular matrix. As a traditional Chinese medicine, leech possesses multiple pharmacological effects including anticoagulation, anti‐inflammation and improvement of microcirculation, exerting its therapeutic roles through regulating coagulation, modulating inflammatory responses, and enhancing vascular microcirculation. Simultaneously, leech can reduce epithelial‐mesenchymal transition and extracellular matrix deposition by synergistically inhibiting multiple key signalling pathways associated with fibrosis. Future research should focus on elucidating the bioactive components of leech and their synergistic regulatory networks across multiple signalling pathways, providing a theoretical reference for developing leech‐based anti‐fibrotic drugs.

Introduction

Diabetes is a systemic disease. Patients with long‐term persistent hyperglycemia may develop peripheral neuropathy and peripheral vascular disease in the lower extremities, among which delayed wound healing is a major complication of diabetes [1]. When a wound forms, its repair involves numerous cell types, cytokines, and molecular mediators, constituting a complex and highly coordinated physiological process typically divided into three sequential yet overlapping stages: haemostasis, proliferation, and remodelling [2]. Normally, the smooth progression of these three stages is key to ensuring normal wound healing. However, in diabetic ulcers, the hyperglycemic milieu simultaneously drives aberrant fibroblast activation and immune cell dysfunction through oxidative stress, AGE/RAGE and TGF‐β/Smad signalling pathways. Dysfunctional fibroblasts subsequently deposit excessive extracellular matrix (ECM), while impaired immune function leads to abnormal macrophage polarization and impaired inflammatory resolution, ultimately resulting in chronic non‐healing wounds and establishing a vicious cycle of persistent inflammation and fibrosis that delays wound closure [1]. In addition, patients with diabetic ulcers exhibit significant activation of the coagulation and complement systems, a tissue injury‐induced pathological state that is mutually reinforcing with ECM degradation and persistent inflammation [3]. This hypercoagulable and hyperinflammatory environment not only contributes to the development of diabetic ulcers but may also represent a critical factor underlying their refractory nature and fibrotic progression.

Leech, as a common animal‐derived traditional Chinese medicine, was first recorded in Shennong's Classic of Materia Medica. It has the effects of promoting blood circulation, removing blood stasis, and eliminating masses, and is commonly used to treat blood stasis syndrome and various diseases caused by it [4]. In recent years, studies have demonstrated that leech and its active constituents can ameliorate visceral fibrosis through TGF‐β/Smad, mTOR/HIF‐1α, and related signalling pathways [5, 6]. Given that these same pathways are central to diabetic ulcer pathogenesis, this suggests that leech may play a pivotal role in a broader spectrum of fibrotic pathologies. Clinically, medicinal leech therapy has been applied in free flap transplantation, particularly for relieving venous congestion and obstruction; its blood‐sucking decompression and anticoagulant effects can directly salvage compromised flaps [7]. Furthermore, it can facilitate the transition of wounds from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase by suppressing inflammatory signalling pathways [8]. Notably, this dual anticoagulant and anti‐inflammatory effect precisely corresponds to the pathological cascade in which hypercoagulability and hyperinflammation drive fibrosis in diabetic ulcers. In addition, some scholars have confirmed through network pharmacology and machine learning that leech plays a role in regulating immune receptor activity and affecting immune cell function in diabetes [9]. This also provides a theoretical basis for its capacity to modulate fibrotic progression by improving the immune microenvironment and rebalancing macrophage polarization. However, the specific molecular targets and mechanisms by which leech modulates fibrosis in diabetic wounds remain incompletely understood. Therefore, this review aims to integrate clinical evidence with experimental findings to discuss the molecular mechanisms underlying leech‐mediated intervention in diabetic ulcer fibrosis, thereby providing a theoretical reference for the development of targeted leech‐based anti‐fibrotic therapeutic strategies.

Pathological Basis of Fibrosis in Diabetic Wounds

Persistent Inflammatory Response

Acute inflammation is essential for pathogen eradication and initiation of the wound healing cascade. However, excessive inflammation in diabetic ulcers hinders healing and promotes the emergence of chronic wounds [10]. In diabetic ulcers, macrophage recruitment is diminished and their function is impaired, with the pro‐inflammatory phenotype failing to transition to an anti‐inflammatory phenotype, resulting in persistent inflammation. Concurrently, elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐6 and TNF‐α exacerbate the inflammatory response and establish a vicious cycle. Moreover, suppression of the PI3K‐AKT signalling pathway impairs VEGF‐mediated cellular proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, thereby obstructing the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase and compromising granulation tissue formation, ultimately leading to wound healing impairment [11]. IL‐6, a cytokine with both pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory properties, studies have shown that IL‐6 levels in diabetic foot skin are significantly elevated compared to normal skin, indicating that diabetic skin is chronically maintained in a state of low‐grade inflammation. When chronic wounds actually develop, the loss of dynamic IL‐6 responsiveness in macrophages results in sustained pro‐inflammatory effects rather than effective activation of fibroblast‐mediated ECM remodelling, leading to insufficient angiogenesis and impaired wound healing [12]. This has been confirmed in cutaneous wounds, where unregulated overproduction of IL‐6 markedly exacerbates local fibrosis with elevated circulating inflammatory factors, suggesting that sustained high expression of IL‐6 is a critical driver of fibrotic progression [13]. TNF‐α, as a major pro‐inflammatory cytokine, does not directly promote fibrotic collagen deposition. In diabetic ulcers, high concentrations of TNF‐α represent a hallmark of impaired transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase, resulting in compromised ECM deposition, disorganized collagen arrangement, and ultimately delayed healing [14]. Further mechanistic studies have revealed that TNF‐α disrupts the inherent scarless regenerative microenvironment by enhancing inflammatory infiltration, inducing IL‐1β expression, and promoting epidermal thickening, thereby shifting wound repair from a regenerative pattern to an inflammation‐dominated repair pattern, delaying healing and abrogating regenerative characteristics [15].

Metabolic Disorders as a Continuous Driver

Glucose metabolic disorder represents one of the principal driving factors of diabetic ulcer fibrosis. Under normal conditions, endogenous bioelectricity exists during wound repair but gradually attenuates and eventually disappears. Exogenous bioelectricity can promote angiogenesis through the PI3K pathway, modulate inflammatory cytokine balance via the NOS pathway, and regulate cellular proliferation and ECM remodelling through the Smad2/3 pathway. However, the hyperglycemic environment in diabetic wounds induces excessive ROS production, leading to local tissue hypoxia, downregulation of hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1 expression, and subsequent reduction in vascular endothelial growth factor and platelet‐derived growth factor levels, thereby impairing angiogenesis. Concurrently, advanced glycation end products (AGEs) are generated, disrupting the wound microenvironment. The disappearance of endogenous bioelectricity, combined with hyperglycemia‐induced pathological damage, collectively results in dysregulated repair gene expression, downregulation of the healing cascade, disorganized collagen fibre deposition, and aberrant ECM remodelling, ultimately leading to scar formation [16]. On the other hand, researchers have demonstrated in cellular experiments that high glucose upregulates APOE expression, thereby activating NF‐κB and JAK/STAT3 inflammatory signalling pathways, and inducing the expression of fibrotic markers including α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA), COL1A1, and COL3A1 in human skin fibroblasts. This promotes pro‐fibrotic phenotypic transformation while suppressing cellular proliferation and migration, ultimately resulting in fibrosis and healing impairment in diabetic foot ulcers [17].

Vascular Impairment

Vascular impairment is one of the key factors in fibrosis. In diabetic ulcers, vascular impairment can lead to significantly reduced blood perfusion in the ulcer area, causing tissue hypoxia and insufficient nutrient supply, thereby delaying wound healing [18]. Among these, HIF‐1α serves as a core regulator of angiogenesis, promoting neovascularization through VEGF induction. Researchers have demonstrated that transfer of a stabilized form of HIF‐1α to the margins of wounded skin in diabetic mice effectively improves cutaneous healing [19]. However, although the hypoxic environment in diabetic ulcers attempts to activate HIF‐1α, persistent inflammation accompanied by increased ROS and elevated MMP‐9 leads to excessive ECM degradation, thereby counteracting the pro‐healing effects of HIF‐1α. This renders HIF‐1α upregulation a functionally insufficient compensatory activation, subsequently disrupting the angiogenic scaffold, causing disorganized collagen deposition and driving the wound into a chronic state [20, 21]. Studies have shown that this ineffectively elevated HIF‐1αcan activate fibroblasts through the HIF‐1α/TSLP/TSLPR axis, suggesting that HIF‐1αmay participate in TSLP‐mediated pro‐fibrotic processes [22]. In chronic wounds, excessive collagen deposition can create a HIF‐α‐dominated hypoxic microenvironment within the wound, which in turn induces further TSLP production, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies fibrotic progression [23]. In summary, diabetic wound fibrosis arises from a vicious cycle of persistent inflammation, metabolic disorder, and vascular impairment. Hyperglycemia disrupts macrophage phenotypic transition, with aberrant elevation of IL‐6 and TNF‐α; IL‐6 directly drives fibrosis, while TNF‐α disrupts the regenerative microenvironment. Regarding metabolic disorder, high glucose induces ROS and AGEs, downregulating HIF‐1α expression and suppressing angiogenesis, while concurrently activating NF‐κB and JAK/STAT3 pathways through APOE, thereby promoting fibroblast expression of α‐SMA, COL1A1, and COL3A1 and facilitating pro‐fibrotic phenotypic transformation. Vascular impairment creates a hypoxic environment in which HIF‐1α is attempted to be activated; however, inflammation accompanied by elevated ROS and MMP‐9 renders this activation functionally insufficient, failing to effectively drive VEGF while instead excessively degrading ECM. Simultaneously, HIF‐1α activates fibroblasts through the TSLP/TSLPR axis via STAT3, and excessive collagen deposition exacerbates hypoxia, forming a positive feedback loop. These three factors mutually reinforce one another, ultimately impeding wound healing.

Anti‐Fibrotic Functions of Leech

Leech is a traditional Chinese medicine. Modern research shows it contains over 100 chemical components with different molecular weights, including proteins, peptides, amino acids, lipids, sterols, fatty acid esters and trace elements, possessing effects such as anticoagulation, anti‐inflammation and anti‐fibrosis [24]. The anti‐fibrotic effect of leech does not rely on a single component but is synergistically mediated by multiple mechanisms including anticoagulation, anti‐inflammation, and metabolic regulation.

Regulation of Coagulation

Plasminogen activator inhibitor (PAI) includes PAI‐1 and PAI‐2, with PAI‐1 playing the main role in regulating plasma fibrinolytic activity. Synthesized by various cells, PAI‐1 is the primary inhibitor of the fibrinolytic system and is closely related to the regulation of coagulation‐fibrinolysis balance and the development of thrombotic diseases [25]. Tissue‐type and urokinase‐type plasminogen activators are serine proteases that cleave inactive plasminogen into active plasmin. Plasmin is a broad‐spectrum proteolytic enzyme that can activate precursors of various MMPs, thereby degrading ECM [26]. Recent studies have shown that, in metabolic disturbances including type 2 diabetes, chronic elevation of PAI‐1 impairs the plasminogen/plasmin system, reducing ECM degradation and promoting fibrotic processes. Pharmacological inhibition of PAI‐1 accelerates wound healing and attenuates fibrosis, suggesting that PAI‐1‐mediated hypofibrinolysis may contribute to impaired tissue repair in diabetic conditions [27]. On the other hand, Ma Z et al. found that high glucose can induce fibroblast senescence accompanied by a significant increase in PAI‐1, and high PAI‐1 expression is synchronized with delayed healing in diabetic ulcer mouse models [28]. Furthermore, studies have revealed that excessive upregulation of PAI‐1 in diabetic ulcers serves as a critical driver of excessive fibrosis by suppressing fibrinolysis, promoting ECM deposition, and synergizing with hypoxia and EMT responses, resulting in scar hyperplasia, skin hardening, and compromised healing quality [29]. This indicates that high PAI‐1 can promote fibrosis in diabetic ulcers from a hypercoagulable perspective.

Hirudin, as a recombinant direct thrombin inhibitor, has been shown to exert its anticoagulant mechanism by binding to the fibrinogen recognition site of thrombin, preventing the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin, thereby indirectly alleviating fibrin‐mediated neuroinflammation [30]. This suggests that hirudin's regulation of PAI‐1 may provide a new strategy for treating fibrotic diseases. Studies have demonstrated that hirudin can significantly downregulate PAI‐1 expression at both the gene and protein levels. In a bleomycin‐induced pulmonary interstitial fibrosis rat model, hirudin reduced PAI‐1 content and activity in a dose‐dependent manner, decreased fibrin deposition, and accelerated collagen degradation, thereby exerting therapeutic effects [31].

In addition to directly affecting PAI‐1 expression and function, hirudin can also indirectly exert its effects by suppressing key upstream transcriptional activators. TGF‐β1, as a critical target in fibrosis, has been shown to upregulate PAI‐1 expression through the Smad pathway, thereby promoting impaired ECM degradation and facilitating fibrosis [32]. Hirudin has been demonstrated to significantly inhibit TGF‐β1 protein expression and downstream Smad2/3 phosphorylation in a synovial fibroblast model [33], and in a rabbit tendon fibroblast model, hirudin can also exert anti‐fibrotic effects by suppressing the TGF‐β1/Smads signalling pathway [34]. In summary, TGF‐β1 has been well established to be closely associated with the fibrotic progression of diabetic ulcers; therefore, this mechanism may also provide a plausible explanation for the potential therapeutic efficacy of hirudin in excessive fibrosis of skin and soft tissues such as diabetic ulcers.

Regulation of Inflammatory Response

The NLRP3 inflammasome is one of the most extensively studied inflammasomes, typically composed of NLRP3, ASC and pro‐caspase‐1. Upon activation, it participates in and amplifies inflammatory responses and fibrotic lesions by cleaving IL‐1β and IL‐18 precursors, inducing pyroptosis and so forth [35]. This has been validated in diabetic ulcers, where danger signals like hyperglycemia can continuously activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, causing Caspase‐1 activation and massive release of IL‐1β and IL‐18. These cytokines stimulate neutrophils to produce NETs while blocking the conversion of M1 macrophages to M2 macrophages, thereby perpetuating chronic inflammation [36]. Concurrently, NLRP3 exhibits synergistic effects with TGF‐β/Smad signalling. Studies have demonstrated that in chronic ulcers, NLRP3 collaborates with the TGF‐β/Smad pathway through the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β to promote myofibroblast activation and excessive collagen deposition. Simultaneously, established fibrotic tissue disrupts vascular drainage, causing hypoxia, and DAMP molecules released by hypoxia and tissue injury further activate NLRP3, thereby mutually amplifying inflammation and fibrosis and ultimately resulting in refractory chronic wounds [37]. In a skin ischemic wound model, a drug with hirudin as its primary active component significantly inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome expression and reduced the release levels of IL‐1β and IL‐18 [38]. This first confirms that hirudin can directly modulate NLRP3 inflammatory expression, while simultaneously providing a theoretical basis for its inhibition of fibrosis through this pathway. On the other hand, mitochondrial ROS dysfunction represents one of the important triggers for NLRP3 activation in diabetic ulcers [39]. Hirudin can reduce the β‐hydroxybutyrylation level of superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), restore normal SOD2 enzymatic activity, enhance its antioxidant function, thereby effectively decreasing mtROS generation, and subsequently suppress NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and activation [40]. Furthermore, impaired autophagic function is closely associated with sustained NLRP3 activation in diabetic models [41]. Long C et al. confirmed that hirudin alleviates fibrosis by inducing autophagy, which reduces α‐SMA and NLRP3 inflammasome‐related protein levels. This indicates the feasibility of hirudin regulating fibrosis via anti‐inflammatory pathways [42].

Metabolic Regulation

Sustained hyperglycemia drives non‐enzymatic glycation reactions, resulting in substantial formation and accumulation of AGEs in diabetic ulcer skin tissue; AGEs serve as direct products of metabolic disturbance [43]. AGEs activate the NF‐κB pathway through RAGE, leading to sustained release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines including TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6, and impeding the transition from M1 to M2 macrophages, thereby maintaining a persistent inflammatory state in the wound [44]. Furthermore, in diabetic wounds, AGEs can cross‐link with collagen and elastin within the extracellular matrix, rendering their structure rigid and resistant to degradation, thereby physically obstructing tissue remodelling and cellular migration [45]. This indicates that this “inflammatory stasis” state not only prolongs the inflammatory phase but also directly stimulates fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis. Researchers have reported that hirudin can reduce AGEs levels, exert antioxidant stress effects and thereby ameliorate peritoneal fibrosis, with this mechanism exhibiting a pronounced dose‐dependent pattern [46]. On the other hand, AKT1 is extensively involved in biological processes including cell growth, proliferation, metabolism, and apoptosis inhibition; in diabetic ulcers, moderate activation of AKT1 facilitates wound repair [47]. However, studies have indicated that persistent hyperglycemia can cause sustained phosphorylation and activation of AKT1, and this sustained activation leads to excessive collagen deposition and continuous myofibroblast activation, ultimately promoting scar fibrosis [48]. In pathological fibrosis models, hirudin has been demonstrated to exert anti‐fibrotic effects through direct inhibition of the AKT target [49]. On the other hand, research based on network pharmacology‐molecular docking analysis has predicted that hirudin may act on the AKT target, regulating ET‐1 synthesis through the AGE‐RAGE signalling pathway in diabetic complications, thereby improving microcirculation [50]. This suggests that diabetic ulcer fibrosis is not merely “excessive repair” but rather the combined consequence of hyperglycemia‐driven sustained AKT1 activation and AGEs cross‐linking. Hirudin, on one hand, reduces AGEs levels and inhibits their cross‐linking effects, while on the other hand, modulates the activation rhythm of AKT1, thereby disrupting the vicious cycle between “inflammatory stasis” and “fibrotic exacerbation” and achieving effective intervention against excessive fibrosis in diabetic ulcers.

Additionally, hyaluronidase is an enzyme initially extracted from the anterior part of medicinal leeches that can hydrolyze hyaluronic acid. It exerts anti‐fibrotic effects by reducing hyaluronic acid accumulation, inhibiting fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition [51]. Research indicates that combined use of hyaluronidase with insulin can significantly reduce pharmacokinetic parameters of insulin, making insulin action more stable [52]. This corroborates its potential indirect impact on blood glucose control and provides collateral evidence for its intervention in fibrosis.

Signalling Pathways Targeted by Leech in Intervening Diabetic Ulcer Fibrosis

TGF‐β/Smad Pathway

TGF‐β is a multifunctional cytokine family playing important roles in various physiological processes such as embryonic development and tissue repair. TGF‐β1 is one of the major subtypes in the TGF‐β family. Overexpression of TGF‐β1 promotes EMT and excessive ECM deposition, playing a major role in tissue fibrosis and tumours [53]. The first member of the SMAD family, Mad, was discovered in the 1990s during research on the decapentaplegic gene in Drosophila. Among them, SMAD1, SMAD2, SMAD3, SMAD5 and SMAD8 serve as receptor substrates for the TGF‐β family and are called receptor‐regulated SMAD proteins [54]. In diabetic ulcer fibrosis, aberrant sustained activation of the TGF‐β/Smad pathway represents a critical mechanism underlying excessive fibrosis [37]. Further studies have revealed that TGF‐β/Smad directly drives endothelial cells to undergo endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EndMT) by activating core transcription factors such as Snail and Twist, resulting in downregulation of endothelial junction proteins including CD31 and VE‐cadherin, while concurrently inducing acquisition of mesenchymal markers such as α‐SMA and vimentin. This ultimately leads to the transformation of endothelial cells into myofibroblast‐like cells with contractile and matrix‐secretory functions, contributing to excessive scar tissue deposition [55]. Scholars have verified through animal experiments that leech extract components with molecular weights greater than 10 kDa can significantly inhibit TGF‐β1 induced fibroblast proliferation, migration and differentiation, and reduce ECM deposition [5]. Furthermore, existing literature has confirmed that leech can reverse EndMT by inhibiting the TGF‐β/SMAD pathway [56]. This further reveals the direct link between leech and the TGF‐β/Smad pathway.

RhoA/ROCK Pathway

RhoA is a small GTPase belonging to the Rho family, involved in regulating various cellular functions including cytoskeletal reorganization, cell migration, microtubule dynamics, signal transduction and gene expression. Rho kinase (ROCK) is a downstream effector protein of RhoA. Currently, two known isoforms exist: ROCK1 and ROCK2, both expressed in endothelial cells. ROCK1 is mainly localized to the cell membrane, while ROCK2 primarily resides in the cytoplasm [57]. Studies show that activation of the RhoA/ROCK pathway can promote cell migration, proliferation and contraction, increase the expression of fibronectin, α‐SMA and so forth, and exacerbate fibrosis progression [58]. In diabetic ulcers, elevated glucocorticoid levels induce upregulation of Caveolin‐1 expression. Caveolin‐1 inhibits ArhGAP35, reducing RhoA inactivation, leading to increased RhoA‐GTP levels. Activated RhoA further activates its downstream target ROCK, promoting stress fibre formation, increasing cytoskeletal rigidity, thereby inhibiting keratinocyte migration and impeding wound healing [59]. This suggests that inhibiting the RhoA/ROCK pathway may be a potential strategy for treating abnormal fibrosis. Qin Yuchun et al. demonstrated that hirudin reduces RhoA and ROCK1 protein expression, decreases collagen fibre deposition, and improves cytoplasmic staining in fibrotic lung tissue [60]. This indicates that the RhoA/ROCK signalling pathway is one of the key targets through which hirudin exerts its anti‐fibrotic effects.

ERK1/2 Pathway

ERK1/2 are important members of the MAPK family, involved in regulating physiological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis. Their activation depends on the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK signalling cascade. After activation, they enter the nucleus to regulate transcription factor activity, thereby affecting gene expression. In diabetic ulcers, activated ERK1/2 phosphorylates SP1. Phosphorylated SP1 binds to the SP1 site on the FGFR2IIIb promoter, upregulating FGFR2IIIb expression. Since FGFR2IIIb is a signature receptor for keratinocytes, this mechanism indicates that moderate activation of ERK1/2 has the capacity to repair the epidermis [61].

However, sustained activation of ERK1/2 leads to abnormal fibrosis. Studies show that hyperglycemia‐activated ERK1/2 can enhance the expression of TGF‐β1/Smad3 and connective tissue growth factor, induce EMT in epithelial cells, promote ECM deposition and ultimately lead to fibrosis [62]. Hu et al. demonstrated that hirudin attenuates fibrosis by suppressing the PAR‐1‐mediated TGF‐β1 signalling pathway, resulting in reduced phosphorylation of downstream molecules Smad3 and ERK1/2, as well as decreased collagen I expression [63]. In summary, in diabetic ulcer fibrosis, TGF‐β/Smad serves as the primary pro‐fibrotic signalling pathway, driving the fibrotic process by inducing EndMT and stimulating excessive ECM deposition. RhoA/ROCK and ERK1/2 do not exist in isolation from TGF‐β/Smad; they constitute a complex regulatory network that collectively promotes fibrosis progression. ERK1/2 can serve as both an upstream activating signal and a downstream effector for TGF‐β/Smad, jointly participating in inducing EMT and ECM deposition. RhoA/ROCK activation promotes the expression of fibrotic markers, mainly playing a synergistic role with TGF‐β/Smad in fibrosis.

Clinical Application of Leech in Diabetic Ulcer Fibrosis

Medicinal leech therapy, or hirudotherapy, is one of the oldest documented medical interventions, with origins tracing back to approximately 3500 years ago in ancient civilizations, when live medicinal leeches were applied to affected areas to allow their mouthparts to pierce the skin and extract accumulated blood, thereby clearing hematomas and promoting repair of skin and soft tissues [64]. In modern microsurgery, leech therapy has become an important adjunctive measure for managing venous congestion following free flap transplantation and replantation of severed limbs. These procedures frequently encounter challenges of blood stasis and compromised blood supply, while bioactive substances in leech saliva, including hirudin, calin, destabilase and hyaluronidase, can prevent thrombus formation and maintain blood flow through anticoagulant effects, improve local circulation and tissue oxygenation through vasodilatory effects, and alleviate inflammatory responses and postoperative pain through anti‐inflammatory and anaesthetic properties, thereby enhancing the success rate of such operations [65]. In 2004, the US Food and Drug Administration formally approved medicinal leeches as a medical device for plastic and reconstructive surgery, marking their transition from traditional empirical medicine to evidence‐based medicine [66].

However, the pathological mechanisms of diabetic ulcers differ fundamentally from the acute venous congestion encountered in flap surgery. The latter represents a mechanical, reversible venous outflow obstruction, whereas the former exhibits chronic, multifactorial pathological characteristics, including persistent inflammation, neuropathy, impaired angiogenesis and metabolic dysregulation. Of particular importance, the refractory nature of diabetic ulcers is closely associated with wound fibrosis imbalance. Prolonged early inflammatory phase, aberrant fibroblast activation, and excessive extracellular matrix deposition result in compromised scar tissue formation and diminished wound contractility, ultimately driving the wound into a vicious cycle of ‘inflammation‐fibrosis’.

Although leech therapy in microsurgery primarily targets acute venous congestion, its successful resolution of microvascular thrombosis and inflammation in flap surgery provides clinical proof‐of‐concept for the antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory applications of leech in diabetic ulcer fibrosis. It is precisely based on this mechanistic convergence that since the 21st century, researchers have begun to explore the application of leech therapy in diabetic ulcer treatment, accumulating a series of clinical evidence. However, clinical evidence reported to date has been largely limited to sporadic case reports [67, 68], and although these observational cases have demonstrated preliminary healing phenomena, they lack concurrent controls and standardized outcome measures. Farzali et al. have identified from a mechanistic perspective that leech therapy may promote early diabetic ulcer healing through improved microcirculation, anti‐inflammatory effects, and mechanical debridement, wherein anticoagulant components such as hirudin can ameliorate local hypercoagulable states, and vasodilators such as histamine can increase local blood flow, creating preliminary conditions for granulation tissue formation. However, these mechanisms have not yet been directly validated against fibrosis‐specific indicators [69].

Summary and Future Perspectives

The occurrence of fibrosis in diabetic ulcers is a complex pathological process resulting from the interplay among three major factors: persistent inflammatory response, metabolic disorders and vascular occlusion. Its core mechanism lies in the pathological retention of inflammatory cells and the abnormal activation of the TGF‐β/Smad pathway under a high‐glucose environment, leading to excessive ECM deposition. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that leech and its active components may intervene in this fibrotic process through anticoagulation, anti‐inflammation, improvement of microcirculation, and regulation of multiple signalling pathways, including inhibition of EMT and reduction of collagen deposition. These findings have suggested potential mechanistic links supporting the therapeutic plausibility of leech‐based interventions in diabetic ulcer fibrosis.

Although leech shows good anti‐fibrotic and promoting healing potential in basic research, clinical treatment protocols for leech's anti‐fibrotic effects in diabetic ulcers are still relatively empirical without standardized protocols, lacking unified dosage standards and controlled treatment duration. Its clinical translation still faces challenges such as insufficient standardization and safety risks. Therefore, future research needs to explore the regulatory patterns of leech active components at different healing stages through studies on the active constituents of leech and the analysis of signalling pathway mechanisms, helping to reveal the molecular mechanisms of its multi‐pathway anti‐fibrotic effects.
