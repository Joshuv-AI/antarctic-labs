Conductive CNT network assisted BaTiO3 nanocomposites for advanced supercapacitor electrodes
Authors: Muhammad Arif, Samira Elaissi, Johar Zeb, Nasrullah Wazir, Kamran Shahzad, Amina Bibi, Junaid Riaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work presents the synthesis of a BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposite and thoroughly studies its application for developing advanced supercapacitor electrode applications. Structural and surface characteristics were investigated by using SEM, EDX, XRD, BET, and XPS techniques, confirming the successful incorporation of BaTiO3 nanoparticles into the conductive carbon nanotube (CNT) network, resulting in a composite with high surface area and enhanced electrical conductivity. The results revealed that the BaTiO3@CNTs composite exhibited significantly improved capacitance compared to its pure BaTiO3 and CNT components, attributed to the cooperative enhancement between the pseudocapacitive BaTiO3 and the conductive CNT framework. The asymmetric BaTiO3@CNT//AC device achieved a maximum energy density and a power density of 65.33 Wh kg−1 and 4200 W kg−1 (at 6 A g−1), respectively. After 12 000 charge–discharge cycles, the device retained 95.35% of its initial capacitance and provided a coulombic efficiency of 93%, underscoring its excellent cycling stability and prolonged lifespan. Considering these results, we believe that this work is significantly useful for the development of promising energy storage applications.

Introduction

Improvements in renewable energy technologies are tied directly to progress in energy storage systems.1,2 Among several technologies for renewable energy storage and conversion, supercapacitors (SCs) have attracted significant interest owing to their fast cycling performance, higher power density, and outstanding cycle durability.3 Thanks to the improvement of SCs, a new class of energy storage systems has appeared that provides both higher energy storage as well as greater power density than conventional batteries.4–6 The fast cycling time and improved safety of SCs have led to the development across several industrial sectors, including transportation, electrical power grids,7 military applications,8 consumer electronics,9 and infrastructure. SCs primarily fall into two types depending on the mechanisms governing charge storage, i.e., pseudocapacitors and EDLCs,6 the former stores energy through reversible, rapid faradaic reactions at the electroactive material's surface.

On the other hand, the latter stores energy by accumulating electrostatically charged particles at the interface of electrodes, made of carbon, such as graphene and carbon nanotubes (CNTs).7,8 This basic distinction underscores the distinct charge and discharge mechanisms of each capacitor type. In comparison, the energy densities of pseudocapacitors exceed approximately 10–100 times than those of EDLCs.9 The overall efficiency of SCs relies primarily on the characteristics of their electrode materials, offering excellent durability, cost-effectiveness, and high specific capacitance. Therefore, a major focus has been on developing inexpensive, high-performance electrode materials and reducing environmental harm.10,11 In recent years, the materials used for pseudocapacitive electrodes, including metal nitrides, metal oxides, and conducting polymers, have garnered considerable attention.12 However, these materials face certain drawbacks: metal nitrides need long, complicated synthesis procedures, resulting in sluggish ionic kinetics and fewer active sites, which lead to poor specific capacitance; conducting polymers exhibit rapid degradation in cyclic stability;17 and metal sulfides exhibit reduced electrical conductivity.13 To overcome these issues, investigations into alternative advanced materials, such as tetragonal hollandite tunnel-type structures,19 perovskites, and spinels, have shown promise.14,15

Perovskite-based metal oxides with ABO3-type structures are gaining significant interest owing to their wide range of oxidation states, favorable electronic structure, and enabling rapid faradaic redox processes.16 For example, LaMnO3 was reported primarily by Mefford and his co-workers as an efficient candidate for energy storage systems.17 Their work demonstrated that charge or energy storage occurs through oxygen intercalation, where anionic vacancies, such as oxygen defects and interstitial lattice positions, serve as active charge or energy storage sites during the charge–discharge process.18 When the pseudo capacitance is enhanced from the reversible intercalation and deintercalation of OH− species, resulting in these types of electrodes gaining a significantly wider electric potential windows of ∼1.8 V. Subsequently, several types of binary composite structures, such as SrFe1−xZrxO3−δ,19 Sr2CoMo1−xNixO6−δ,20 BaMnO3,21 and CNT@SrTiO3 nanocomposites,1 have also established highly efficient electrochemical performance.22 Accordingly, such perovskite-based metal oxides (ABO3) cannot only enhance energy or charge storage capacity but also retain higher electrical conductivity via synergistic integration.1,23–25 A study presented by Oliva and Chao Yin with their co-workers, in which they have incorporated CaLaCo (Ca2.9La0.1Co4O9) microplate units to graphene electrodes, resulting in about 70% improvement in its capacitive performance, corresponding to about an 80% decrease in the charge transport resistance (Rct) process.1,26 In related research, Undavalli Venkata Gopi investigated rGO-LaNiO3 composites and observed that LaNiO3 enables rapid charge-transfer kinetics, leading to a higher specific capacitance than rGO electrodes alone.27 These research studies emphasize the importance of integrating carbonaceous materials with metallic oxides compounds via oxygen intercalation to harness the synergistic benefits of both components.

In carbonaceous materials, CNTs have garnered significant scientific attraction and enabled several applications in SCs owing to their exceptional mechanical and thermal stability, greater electrical conductivity, and outstanding structural durability.28 Rapid progress has recently been made in the investigation of inorganic incorporation into carbonaceous composites for charge and energy storage, particularly those composed of CNTs and metal oxides.29,30 The integration of CNTs and metal oxides addresses both key shortcomings associated with lower applied potential and energy density in SC composites. Also, it develops their promising applications through improved kinetic performance. Li and co-workers demonstrated an electrostatic self-assembly technique to prepare FeMnO3 microspheres incorporated into a conductive CNTS network (FeMnO3-CNTS), which served in lithium-ion SCs as an anode material.31 This electrode exhibited excellent energy density and cyclability owing to synergistic interactions among its components. However, further improvement in kinetic performance is required, attributed to their weak coulombic interactions.32 Therefore, a suitable, novel metal oxide is selected to modify the CNT networks efficiently. Among several metal oxides, barium titanate (BaTiO3) has attracted significant interest as a useful electrode material, offering superior electrochemical properties, low cost, natural abundance, minimal toxicity, and environmental safety.33,34

In this work, a BaTiO3@CNT hybrid electrode has been prepared to address the inherent limitations of pristine BaTiO3, such as low surface area and low charge-transport capacity. Incorporating BaTiO3 nanoparticles into the interconnected CNT network yields a synergistic structure with enhanced electrolyte accessibility, superior electron transport, and abundant electroactive sites for charge storage. Furthermore, the conductive CNT frameworks provide a robust support matrix and structural buffering, which help accommodate volume changes during repeated cycle lifespan and enhance the electrode's structural stability. These synergistic effects render the BaTiO3@CNT nanocomposite with charge-transfer acceleration, low charge-transfer resistance (Rct), and high capacitive performance attributes that are attractive for promising applications in high-performance SCs.

Experimental details

Materials

Tetrabutyl titanate (Ti(OC4H9)4, 99%) and ammonia (NH3·H2O, 20%) worked as the titanate precursor; caustic baryta octahydrate (Ba(OH)2·8H2O, 99%), and sodium hydrate (NaOH, 98%) were combined with the titanate precursor to form BaTiO3 nanoparticles. Ferrocene (Fe(C5H5)2, 99%), sulfur (S, 98%), and NaOH were used as the starting materials for the synthesis of carbon nanotubes. Deionized (DI) water, ethanol (EtOH, 99%), and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, 99%) were used as the solvents during the synthesis steps. All materials were utilized in their commercially supplied form without any further purification steps.

BaTiO3 nanoparticles preparation

BaTiO3 nanoparticles with a cubic perovskite structure were prepared by a hydrothermal method using analytical-grade reagents. Initially, add 3.27 mg (0.01 mol) of tetrabutyl titanate (Ti(OCH2CH2CH2CH3)4) into 50 mL of ethanol (EtOH) with constant stirring until fully dissolved. Then add 20% ammonia at the adjusted pH of 8–10 to prepare the titanate precursor. In parallel to synthesizing barium hydroxide octahydrate (Ba(OH)2·8H2O) precursor, first dissolved 3785.53 mg (0.012 mol) of Ba(OH)2·8H2O into hot DI water, followed by the incorporation of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) at a concentration of 0.02 g L−1 to serve as a dispersing stabilizer and structure-directing template. These two synthesized precursors were mixed under continuous stirring while maintaining a constant EtOH : DI water ratio to obtain a white, homogeneous suspension. Next, 2 M NaOH served as a mineralizer, and the 100 mL polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) lined stainless-steel autoclave was used to keep the final mixture. The sealed autoclave was maintained at 200 °C for 48 hours to complete the hydrothermal process successfully. After cooling to room temperature, the final solution was obtained by filtration, washed thoroughly with EtOH and DI water, and finally dried at 80 °C. The grain size of the resulting BaTiO3 nano powders was approximately 68.8 nm, indicating a uniform, spherical structure and high crystallinity.

Preparation of carbon nanotubes (CNTs)

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) were synthesized via a straightforward hydrothermal method utilizing ferrocene as the carbon source and sulfur as the catalyst.35,36 Initially, 2 g of ferrocene and 4 g of sulfur were combined with 100 mL of a 10 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution, prepared using a 70 : 30 (v/v) ethanol-to-deionized water mixture. The resulting mixture was magnetically stirred for 30 minutes to obtain a homogeneous dispersion. After that, the dispersion was kept in an ultrasonic bath at room temperature for 30 minutes to sonicate, after which it was agitated again for another 30 minutes. Then we added the final homogeneous suspension to a 200 mL Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave. The sealed autoclave was kept in a standard electric oven at 200 °C for 24 hours to carry out a hydrothermal reaction. Upon reaction finished, the stainless steel autoclave was left to cool down at ambient temperature. We collected the final black solid product via filtration and subsequently washed it with ethanol, 0.1 M hydrochloric acid, and distilled water until it showed a neutral pH of 7. The final purified products were subjected to drying at 100 °C for 24 hours in an oven.

Preparation of BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposite

BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposites were synthesized via a hydrothermal method, as shown in Fig. 1. The as-synthesized BaTiO3 nanoparticles and CNTs served as the active component and the conductive framework, respectively. In the first step, CNTs were dispersed in DI water and ultrasonicated for about 45 minutes to obtain a stable, homogeneous suspension. In the second step, BaTiO3 nano powders were mixed with NaOH in a mortar and pestle, and a small amount of ethylene glycol was added to form a homogeneous paste. In the third step, the aqueous CNT was added dropwise (slowly) to the homogeneous paste while continuously grinding to ensure uniform distribution and close contact between the BaTiO3 nanoparticles and the CNTs. The resulting product was transferred to a stainless-steel autoclave lined with Teflon, and an additional about 50 mL of distilled water was added. The sealed autoclave was heated to 180 °C and held for 6 hours, allowing the CNTs to attach and be effectively coated onto BaTiO3 nanoparticles in hydrothermal conditions.

Synthesis route of BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposite.

The reaction was left to cool down at ambient temperature. We collected the final product and rinsed it repeatedly with both deionized water and EtOH for purification. Lastly, the material was desiccated at 60 °C for 24–36 h to obtain the BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposite powder. A connected CNT network improves electrical conductivity and efficient charge transport, while BaTiO3 remains the primary active material.

Electrode fabrication

A standard slurry-coating procedure was used to prepare all electrodes, ensuring consistency across the fabricated samples.37 In each sample formulation, conductive carbon black, polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) binder, and the active material were combined in a 10 : 10 : 80 ratio by weight. The resulting mixture was kept in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) under continuous stirring to obtain the homogenized slurry. The slurry obtained was then deposited onto a nickel foam current collector through the doctor-blade technique. The coated electrodes were then dried at 60–80 °C in a vacuum oven for 12 hours to ensure complete solvent evaporation. The electrodes were dried, then pressed gently to enhance adhesion of the active layer to the current collector and increase overall structural integrity. The active material was loaded at a mass rate of about 2–3 mg cm−2. For each pristine BaTiO3 and CNTs electrode, the corresponding active materials, BaTiO3 nano powders and CNTs, were used as the working materials. The BaTiO3@CNTs electrode was prepared with the BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposite as the active material. This standardized approach enabled a direct comparison of the electrochemical performance of the pristine materials and the nanocomposite.

Results and discussion

Fig. 2(a) demonstrates the XRD patterns of pure BaTiO3, CNTs, and BaTiO3@CNTs. Pure BaTiO3 exhibits sharp diffraction peaks at about 22°, 31°, 38°, 45°, 50°, 56°, 66°, 69°, 75°, and 79°, corresponding to the (100), (110), (111), (200), (210), (211), (220), (300), (310), and (311) planes, respectively, which match well with PDF card no. 31-0174 and confirm the formation of crystalline perovskite BaTiO3 without impurity phases. The CNTs show a large peak at 26° and a small peak at 43°, which are attributed to the (002) and (100) planes of graphitic carbon. The characteristic diffraction peaks of BaTiO3 are clearly retained in the BaTiO3@CNT composite, while the broad and partially overlapped CNT-related features confirm the successful incorporation of CNTs without altering the crystal structure of BaTiO3.38 The minor widening and decrease in peak intensities also indicate that the CNTs are well dispersed and that the two phases interact well at the interfaces. The BJH pore-size distribution of BaTiO3@CNTs is given in Fig. 2(b), with an average pore-diameter of 29.31 nm, indicating that it is a mesoporous material. The mesoporous structure can facilitate fast ion diffusion and efficient charge transfer, and thus BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposites are promising electrode materials for high-performance SCs.

Fig. 3 shows the FESEM images of pure BaTiO3, CNTs, and the BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposite at various magnifications. Fig. 3(a and b) depicts that pristine BaTiO3 is composed of highly packed, almost spherical, to slightly irregular, nanoparticles that are agglomerated into clusters. This morphology will expose many particle surfaces, providing active electrochemical sites. In Fig. 3(c and d), the CNTs exhibit a highly entangled, interconnected fibrous structure that supports their one-dimensional tubular morphology and conductive framework. This type of interwoven morphology is advantageous in transporting electrons and providing structural stability.39 Following the composite formation, the FESEM images of BaTiO3@CNTs in Fig. 3(e and f) show that the distribution and anchoring of the BaTiO3 nanoparticles to the surface of the CNT network are successful after the composite formation. The CNTs are not fully separated, and the BaTiO3 particles are bonded to the conductive matrix, forming a highly contacted hybrid structure. Such close interfacial contact between CNTs and BaTiO3 confirms the effective formation of the nanocomposite and is likely to promote charge transfer, electrolyte accessibility, and overall electrochemical activity. The porous, fibrous structure of CNTs, combined with the nanoparticulate structure of BaTiO3, renders the composite desirable for use as an SC electrode.

Fig. 4 shows the EDS spectrum and elemental mapping results of the BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposite. Fig. 4(a) shows the EDS spectrum, which proves the existence of the primary constituent elements of the composite, i.e., C, O, Ti, and Ba. The Ba, Ti, and O peaks are ascribed to the phase of BaTiO3, and the C signal is ascribed to the CNT framework. The emergence of these typical features confirms the effective preparation of the BaTiO3@CNTs hybrid material. The elemental mapping images (Fig. 4(b and f)) further indicate that these elements are distributed across the chosen area of interest. The relatively homogenous distribution of the mapped components suggests excellent compositional homogeneity and close interfaces between BaTiO3 and CNTs. This homogeneous distribution of elements has been shown to form continuous, high-conductivity pathways and increase the number of active sites, thereby enhancing charge transfer and electrochemical performance.

For comparative purposes, the CV behavior of the electrodes at various scan rates in the potential window of 0–0.5 V is presented in Fig. 6. The current response is progressively enhanced with increasing scan rate from 10 to 50 mV s−1 (Fig. 6(a–c)), showing that the electrochemical reversibility, ion diffusion, and charge propagation at the electrode/electrolyte interface are favorable. The preservation of the CV profile even at higher scan rates further demonstrates the electrodes' good rate capability and structural stability during repeated charge-storage processes.43 The CV profile in Fig. 6(b) is nearly rectangular, with no visible redox peaks, indicating electric double-layer capacitive (EDLC) behavior of the CNT electrode. This capacitance is predominantly attributed to the ionic adsorption and desorption of OH− and K+ at the highly conductive carbon surface, where charge storage occurs via non-faradaic processes without significant change in the oxidation state of the carbon atoms.44 The network of interconnected CNTs also acts as continuous electron-transport pathways and gives a huge accessible surface area, which allows for rapid charge transfer and diffusion of electrolyte ions. The CV curve of pristine BaTiO3 in Fig. 6(c) shows a quasi-rectangular shape with widened redox humps, which are characteristic of a combination of EDLC and pseudocapacitive behavior. The pseudo capacitance is primarily attributed to reversible surface redox reactions involving Ti species on the BaTiO3 surface.33 In the case of an alkaline electrolyte, partial reduction of surface Ti4+ to Ti3+ occurs during the electrochemical process via electron injection and K+ ion adsorption/intercalation, and the opposite oxidation process occurs during discharge.45 The surface “faradaic” reactions further increase charge-storage compared to electrostatic capacitance alone. The surface reaction that corresponds to this is:1BaTiO + xK+ + xe− ↔ KxBaTiO3or, more specifically, at Ti–O surface sites,2Ti4+–O + e− + K+ ↔ Ti3+–O − K

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were recorded at various scan rates of (a) BaTiO3@CNT, (b) CNT, and (c) BaTiO3, with (d) comparative CV profiles at 20 mV s−1.

The reversible adsorption and intercalation of alkaline ions coupled with partial Ti4+/Ti3+ surface transitions are schematized in these reactions. The overall capacitive performance of pristine BaTiO3, however, is reduced due to the material's relatively low conductivity, which limits electron mobility and thus the utilization of electroactive sites. The BaTiO3@CNT composite electrode in Fig. 6(a) exhibits the highest current response and the largest enclosed CV area among all electrodes, indicating its superior charge-storage capability. The synergic contribution of the conductive CNT framework and the electroactive BaTiO3 phase is responsible for the improved electrochemical functionality. The uniform distribution of BaTiO3 nanoparticles on the CNT network increases the number of electrochemically active sites and electrolyte accessibility. The close contact at the interface between the CNTs and BaTiO3 also facilitates rapid charge separation and transport, thereby promoting surface redox reactions. Moreover, the comparative CV curves (Fig. 6(d)) show a significant improvement in the capacitive response of BaTiO3@CNT compared to pure CNT and BaTiO3. The composite thus has its overall capacitance values contributed from the surface pseudocapacitive Ti4+/Ti3+ redox reactions and the EDLC behavior of CNTs. The electrochemical performance of the BaTiO3@CNT composite electrode is high due to the simultaneous presence of these charge-storage mechanisms, as well as enhanced electrical conductivity and ion pathways.

Shown in Fig. 7 is how BaTiO3, CNTs, and the hybrid BaTiO3@CNTs behave during charge–discharge cycling at multiple current densities. The charge–discharge profiles reveal that the BaTiO3@CNTs (Fig. 7(d)) nanocomposite exhibits optimal capacitance, with the longest discharge time among the electrodes. This high performance can be attributed to the synergistic effect between BaTiO3 (Fig. 7(a)) and CNTs (Fig. 7(b)), whereby the former provides a highly conductive network and fast electron movement, and the latter provides electroactive sites. The specific capacitance of the BaTiO3@CNTs, at 1 A g−1, is 450 F g−1, substantially exceeding those of pristine BaTiO3 (276 F g−1), and CNTs (100 F g−1). The capacitance of all electrodes decreases gradually with increasing current density (1–5 A g−1), a natural result of the electrolyte ions requiring more time to reach all active sites at higher current densities.46 Nevertheless, the BaTiO3@CNTs composite exhibits the highest capacitive performance across all current densities, with values of 416, 324, 240, and 188 F g−1 at 2, 3, 4, and 5 A g−1, respectively. In comparison, BaTiO3 shows 230, 187, 144, and 102 F g−1, while CNTs exhibit 92, 87, 83, and 79 F g−1 at the same current densities. These findings clearly indicate that the addition of CNTs can greatly improve the electrochemical performance of BaTiO3 by enhancing its conductivity, charge transfer, and active material utilization. Accordingly, the BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposite demonstrates the highest charge-storage capacity and the highest rate performance among the electrodes examined. In Fig. 7(d) the GCD curve of the composite electrode BaTiO3@CNT shows a clear IR drop at the transition of charge and discharge. The IR drop may be due to the equivalent series resistance (ESR) of the electrode system, comprising electrolyte, charge-transfer, and interfacial contact resistances.47 The voltage discontinuity is less evident in the case of the relatively low capacitance of pristine BaTiO3, due to its short discharge time, but is still present. The enhanced charge-storage function of the BaTiO3@CNT composite, on the other hand, leads to a more prominent IR drop. Additionally, the voltage drop can be attributed to interfacial polarization and contact resistance at the BaTiO3-CNT heterointerfaces. These behaviors have been observed in composite SC electrodes that enable multiphase interfaces, enhancing charge storage but also introducing interfacial resistive losses and polarization effects.48–50 Hence, the measured IR drop is due to the composite electrode's non-uniformity and does not adversely affect its excellent capacitive behavior. To address the effect of CNT concentration on the electrochemical performance, two different BaTiO3@CNT compositions (10% CNT–90% BaTiO3 and 30% CNT–70% BaTiO3) were systematically investigated. As shown in Fig. S1(a and b), the galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) curves demonstrate that the discharge time of the 30% CNT composite is significantly longer than that of the 10% CNT sample at all current densities, indicating improved charge storage capability. The enhanced performance of the higher CNT content sample is attributed to the formation of a more efficient conductive network, which facilitates faster electron transport and reduces internal resistance. In addition, CNTs provide a large surface area that promotes effective electrolyte ion adsorption, while BaTiO3 contributes to interfacial polarization, resulting in a synergistic effect. The calculated specific capacitance values (Fig. S1c) further confirm this trend, where the 30% CNT–70% BaTiO3 electrode exhibits a maximum capacitance of 316 F g−1 at 1 A g−1, significantly higher than 150 F g−1 observed for the 10% CNT composite. Even at higher current densities, the 30% CNT sample consistently shows superior performance, confirming improved rate capability due to optimized CNT content (Table 1).

Fig. 8 shows the electrochemical performance of the assembled BaTiO3@CNT//AC asymmetric supercapacitor device. The cyclic voltammetry profiles at various scanning values of 10 to 50 m V s−1 are of quasi-rectangular shape with minor distortion, which is a result of the joint contribution of the electric double-layer capacitance and pseudocapacitive behavior.51 The progressive increase in the current response with scan rate indicates good reversibility and rapid charge transport through the device. Fig. 8(b) displays the galvanostatic charge–discharge curves at current densities of 2–6 A g−1. As anticipated, the discharge time is lower at higher current densities because electrolyte ions do not have sufficient time to reach all active sites at higher charge–discharge rates. The highest discharge time is recorded at 2 A g−1, indicating that the greatest charge-storage capacity occurs at low current density. Fig. 8(c) presents the individual capacitance values obtained from the GCD traces. In results, the higher specific capacitance 237 F g−1 value was obtained at 2 A g−1, decreasing gradually to 204 F g−1 at 3 A g−1, 120 F g−1at 4 A g−1, 77 F g−1 at 5 A g−1, and 54 F g−1 at 6 A g−1. This is a common decrease, since higher current density slows ion diffusion, and not every electroactive site is fully utilized in the charge-storage process. However, the comparatively high capacitance of lower and moderate current densities indicates the good electrochemical characteristics of the developed device. Fig. 8(d) shows the Nyquist plot of the BaTiO3@CNT//AC device. The Nyquist plot exhibits a small high-frequency intercept and a limited semicircle, suggesting low internal resistance and reduced charge-transfer resistance at the electrode/electrolyte interface.52 Ion diffusion and capacitive behavior are reflected in the inclined line in the low-frequency region. These findings suggest that the device has reasonable electrical conductivity and effective charge-transfer properties, which contribute to its overall SC performance.

The electrochemical behaviour of the BaTiO3@CNT//AC asymmetric supercapacitor (ASC) was systematically evaluated, as presented in Fig. 9. The device exhibits excellent energy storage properties, including greater power capacity, higher energy density, and long cycling durability. The energy density gradually decreases with increasing current density (2–6 A g−1), a typical trend for SC arrays, since high current densities limit electrolyte-ion diffusion and, hence, prevent all electroactive sites from being utilized.53,54 However, its energy-storage performance remains relatively high at high charging-discharging rates, and the values are about 65, 60.8, 32.7, 26.0, and 17.5 Wh kg−1 at 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 A g−1, respectively. The energy density of the cell has decreased relatively slowly, suggesting good rate capability and efficient electron/ion transfer through the electrode structure. The power density values corresponding to the above currents are presented in Fig. 9(b) and show a progressive increase with current density, as the discharge process is faster at higher rates. The power density increases from around 1400 W kg−1 at 2 A g−1 to around 2100, 2800, 3500, and 4200 W kg−1 at 3, 4, 5, and 6 A g−1, respectively, showing the high-rate performance of this device. Moreover, the cycling stability result exhibited in Fig. 9(c) confirms the excellent electrochemical stability of the BaTiO3@CNT//AC ASC, which preserves almost 95.35% of its initial capacitance after 12 000 charge–discharge cycles, with a high coulombic efficiency of nearly 93%, indicating highly reversible charge-storage behavior with minimal energy loss. The excellent cycling durability is attributed to the strong interfacial interactions between BaTiO3 and CNTs, which maintain the electrode's structural integrity and ensure stable electron transport over long cycling. In summary, the findings indicate that the synergistic combination of the electroactive BaTiO3 and the highly conductive CNT framework significantly enhances the electrochemical performance of the ASC device, making it a promising material for advanced energy storage applications (Table 2).

Conclusion

In summary, a BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposite, comprising BaTiO3 nanoparticles incorporated into CNTs, was designed, successfully prepared, and tested for efficient SCs promising applications. The incorporation significantly increased electrical conductivity and provided an effective path for rapid ion and electron mobility. Electrochemical performance indicates that the nanocomposite electrode exhibits exceptional capacitance and a higher charge-storage capacity. A symmetric SC was fabricated using this BaTiO3@CNTs composite, which produced a higher energy density of 65.33 Wh kg−1 and power density of 1400 W kg−1 at a current density of 2 A g−1, and the power density increased further to 4200 W kg−1 at 6 A g−1, revealing excellent rate ability. Moreover, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy revealed effective ionic diffusion and lower internal resistance, indicating promising electrochemical performance of the BaTiO3@CNTs electrode. The cycling stability is prolonged, with approximately 96% of capacitance maintained after 12 000 charging-discharging cycles and a higher coulombic efficiency of about 95.3%, representing the remarkable reversibility of the studied electrode material. The enhanced electrochemical activity is attributed to the synergistic interaction between BaTiO3 and the higher-conductivity CNT network, which enhances charge transport, increases the electroactive area, and promotes rapid ionic diffusion in the electrolyte. Collectively, these results suggest that the BaTiO3@CNTs nanocomposite has robust potential as an electrode material for future advanced SCs that demand both high power and high energy, opening a promising direction toward highly efficient and robust energy storage systems.
