A deep learning framework for standardized interpretation of multiparameter cardiac ultrasound and disease classification
Authors: Zhihong Wen, Xiangpeng Liu, Yi Liu, Wenbo Tang, Kang An, Shengli Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEchocardiographic interpretation underlies a large share of cardiovascular diagnoses, yet specialist expertise remains unevenly distributed, and quantitative measurements show inter-observer variability of 15–17% that contributes to disagreement in borderline cases. We developed DeepCard, a multi-task deep learning system that produces standardized, reproducible interpretation of pre-measured echocardiographic parameters by jointly analyzing 39 quantitative measurements across 17 diagnostic tasks spanning valvular disease, ventricular dysfunction, and structural abnormalities. Trained on 400 patients, DeepCard reached 91% specificity for valvular assessment and 82% accuracy for ventricular evaluation, and reduced inter-observer interpretive variability for pre-measured parameters to 13.4%. On an independent external cohort of 102 patients from a separate institution, performance decreased by only 2.6%, indicating consistent generalization. By providing standardized interpretation of quantitative measurements, DeepCard may help clinicians achieve more consistent and reproducible cardiac assessment, particularly in settings where specialist availability is limited.Keywords: Deep learning, echocardiography, multi-task learning, cardiovascular diagnosis, attention mechanism, external validation, valvular disease, ventricular dysfunction, diagnostic standardization, artificial intelligence

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) continue to be one of the leading causes of death globally, with both incidence and mortality rates on the rise, posing a significant threat to global public health. According to a study by Borges et al.1 published in The Lancet, CVD was responsible for approximately 18.8 million deaths in 2024, accounting for 32.8% of all fatalities. Further research by Fang et al.2 indicates that in China, the mortality rate of CVD reaches 45.91%, significantly surpassing that of cancer and other diseases.

Echocardiography plays a crucial role in the diagnosis and evaluation of CVDs, offering clinicians a real-time, non-invasive method to assess cardiac structure and function.3 However, with the growing number of patients with CVD, the accurate interpretation and diagnosis using echocardiography face two major challenges: a shortage of skilled professionals and variability in diagnostic expertise. 40% of the global population lacks access to basic diagnostic tools, and 90% of low-income regions face a shortage of specialized medical professionals.4,5,6

Traditional echocardiographic practice is subject to two distinct layers of inter-observer variability: measurement variability, which arises from differences in caliper placement, frame selection, and acquisition technique and is typically reported at 15–17%, and interpretive variability, which reflects differences in how clinicians translate the same quantitative measurements into diagnostic conclusions. DeepCard operates exclusively on standardized, pre-measured parameters and is therefore designed to address the latter. By providing a consistent interpretive framework learned from expert-labeled data, it standardizes the mapping from quantitative measurements to multi-task diagnostic outputs, reducing inter-observer interpretive variability in diagnostic classification to 13.4% in our clinical validation setting.

Globally, echocardiographic diagnosis faces severe challenges. A multicenter study by Klassen et al.7 revealed that in resource-limited regions, only 30% of primary healthcare facilities are equipped with echocardiography devices, and less than 10% have physicians with the expertise to interpret the results. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 40% of the world’s population lacks access to basic diagnostic medical equipment, including cardiac ultrasound equipment. Even in developed countries, the shortage of specialized personnel is a widespread issue. For instance, the American College of Cardiology reports that the U.S. will face a shortfall of approximately 15,000 cardiologists by 2025.7 In Europe, a survey covering 31 countries revealed an average of only 20 professionals capable of echocardiographic diagnosis per 100,000 people, with significant variability among countries.6

This global shortage of specialized professionals and uneven distribution of medical resources have led to delayed diagnoses, increased misdiagnosis rates, and reduced treatment efficacy. Studies indicate misdiagnosis rates fluctuate between 15% and 25% depending on the setting and physician expertise.8 Figure 3 illustrates the relationship between physician age and diagnostic accuracy, demonstrating that diagnostic accuracy generally improves with clinical experience, further emphasizing the critical role of expertise in achieving reliable diagnostic outcomes.

The substantial dispersion of data points, particularly at the early-to-mid career stages, highlights the inter-observer variability inherent in manual echocardiographic interpretation, underscoring the clinical need for standardized AI-assisted diagnostic tools.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning technologies, has made significant strides in medical analysis,9,10 showing great potential in addressing many current challenges in echocardiographic diagnosis. AI has demonstrated remarkable achievements in enhancing diagnostic accuracy and improving efficiency. For instance, AI models achieved a remarkable accuracy rate of 91.2% in identifying 12 distinct cardiac pathologies, significantly outperforming the average accuracy of 79.4% attained by cardiologists.11 Lovell et al.12 demonstrated that an AI tool could reduce the average diagnosis time to just 20 ss, significantly faster than traditional methods. A multicenter study by Philips Healthcare confirmed that AI- assisted diagnosis significantly enhances inter-physician diagnostic consistency, with improvement in consistency rates from 68% to 89% with the support of AI.

Deep learning techniques have shown particular promise in several key areas.13,14,15,16,17,18 U-Net and its variants have demonstrated exceptional performance in medical image segmentation, with studies achieving dice coefficients above 0.90 in cardiac segmentation tasks.19,20 Madani et al.21 utilized U-Net for automated cardiac disease diagnosis, achieving an accuracy rate of 94.4%. In cardiac function parameter estimation, Kim et al.22 developed a deep learning model capable of automatically estimating left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), showing strong correlation with the reference standard of magnetic resonance imaging (correlation coefficient r = 0.94). Blaivas et al.23 introduced a machine learning algorithm that significantly reduced inter-observer variability in LVEF measurements from 10.8% to 7.6%, thereby substantially improving diagnostic consistency.

Multi-task learning and multi-input multi-output (MIMO) architectures have emerged as powerful approaches for comprehensive cardiac assessment.24,25,26 Mahdavi et al.27 proposed a multi-task learning framework capable of detecting multiple cardiac abnormalities simultaneously, achieving an accuracy of 87%. Jan et al.28 developed a MIMO model capable of simultaneously processing phonocardiogram and electrocardiogram signals, achieving an accuracy of 97.32% for abnormal heartbeat detection and 96.76% for murmur detection. Chen et al.29 developed a MIMO-based deep learning framework for comprehensive cardiac function assessment, demonstrating 95% accuracy in detecting various cardiac abnormalities including valve disorders and wall motion abnormalities.

Despite these encouraging results, current AI systems in echocardiographic diagnosis face three critical limitations that hinder their clinical adoption. First, most existing models focus solely on diagnosing a single task or disease—such as LVEF estimation,22,23 valve disease classification, or specific cardiomyopathy detection30,31—lacking the ability to provide a comprehensive assessment of overall cardiac function. This single-task focus may lead to misjudgment in complex cardiac cases or omission of important related pathological changes. Second, many studies rely on datasets of limited size and lack large-scale, multi-center validation,7,32 which may impact the generalizability of models in real-world clinical settings. Third, the interpretability and clinical applicability of models need improvement to enhance clinicians’ trust in AI-assisted diagnostic results, with most systems providing limited explanation of their diagnostic reasoning.

To address these limitations, we present deep learning for cardiac diagnosis (DeepCard), a post-measurement clinical decision support system designed for the standardized analysis of quantitative echocardiographic parameters. In its current implementation, the system interprets 39 specific measurements acquired by trained sonographers according to standard clinical protocols. Through adaptive feature extraction and dynamic weight adjustment technologies, DeepCard achieves intelligent analysis of 39 key cardiac measurement indicators across 17 diagnostic tasks. The system demonstrated exceptional performance in clinical validation, achieving a specificity of 0.91 in quantitative valvular regurgitation analysis and an accuracy of 0.82 in ventricular function assessment. Notably, in our clinical validation setting, the system reduced inter-observer interpretive variability in the classification of standardized pre-measured parameters to 13.4%, supporting its value in improving post-measurement diagnostic consistency. Here, the reported variability pertains to the interpretive stage, at which experienced readers can reasonably draw somewhat different diagnostic conclusions from the same quantitative measurements, rather than to the measurement stage itself. External validation using an independent cohort of 102 patients from the Sixth Medical Center of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital showed consistent performance, with minimal degradation compared to internal test results, demonstrating the model’s robustness and generalizability.

Results

We developed and rigorously validated DeepCard, a deep learning framework for comprehensive echocardiographic diagnosis, using datasets from two clinical centers. The development cohort consisted of 500 patients from the First Medical Center of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital, of which 400 were used for model training and 100 for internal testing. External validation was conducted on an independent cohort of 102 patients from the Sixth Medical Center. The system simultaneously analyzes 39 echocardiographic parameters to identify 17 cardiac conditions, spanning valvular disorders, ventricular dysfunction, chamber enlargement, and structural abnormalities.

Comparison of disease prediction metrics

For valvular regurgitation, the model achieved high discriminative ability, with sensitivity ranging from 0.82 to 0.87 and specificity from 0.89 to 0.91. Among these, mitral regurgitation demonstrated the best overall performance (sensitivity 0.87; specificity 0.91; F1 score 0.86).

In ventricular functional assessment, the model reached an accuracy of 0.82 for systolic dysfunction and 0.81 for diastolic dysfunction, with specificity exceeding 0.85 in both tasks. These results underscore the model’s robustness in detecting global myocardial impairment, a critical component of routine echocardiographic interpretation.

A closer examination of the confidence interval widths across tasks reveals a clinically meaningful pattern that has direct implications for deployment prioritization. High-prevalence valvular endpoints—including mitral valve regurgitation (MR) (sensitivity CI: 0.79–0.92), TR (sensitivity CI: 0.77–0.91), and aortic regurgitation (AR) (sensitivity CI: 0.78–0.91)—demonstrate relatively narrow confidence intervals, reflecting stable and reliable performance estimates that support their readiness for clinical deployment with a high degree of confidence. In contrast, several structural and lower-prevalence endpoints exhibit notably wider confidence intervals, including RVE (sensitivity CI: 0.61–0.78), left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) (sensitivity CI: 0.64–0.81), pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) (sensitivity CI: 0.62–0.79), pericardial effusion (PE) (sensitivity CI: 0.66–0.82), and ventricular septal defect (VSD) (sensitivity CI: 0.69–0.85). The wider intervals for these tasks reflect the inherent statistical limitations of smaller positive case counts, as detailed in Table S2, and indicate that performance estimates for these conditions carry greater uncertainty. From a clinical deployment perspective, these findings suggest a natural prioritization strategy: DeepCard’s outputs for high-prevalence valvular and ventricular endpoints can be applied with greater confidence as a standardized interpretive aid, while outputs for lower-prevalence structural conditions should be interpreted more cautiously and ideally used to flag cases for expert review rather than as standalone diagnostic conclusions.

Together, the findings highlight DeepCard’s capacity to deliver reliable, multi-parameter cardiac ultrasound interpretation across diverse cardiovascular conditions and independent clinical settings.

External validation demonstrates robust generalizability

To assess the real-world applicability and generalizability of the DeepCard framework, we conducted an external validation study using an independent cohort of 102 patients from the Sixth Medical Center of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital. This cohort was prospectively collected between April and July 2024 under identical echocardiographic acquisition protocols, ensuring methodological consistency with the development dataset.

The cohort exhibited distributions comparable to the training dataset, with a mean age of 52.3 ± 13.8 years, 58.8% male, and a hypertension prevalence of 56.9%. The alignment of key demographic variables across cohorts supports the validity of cross-center performance assessment and reinforces the model’s potential for broad clinical deployment.

Table 2 provides a comprehensive comparison of model performance between the internal test set and the external validation cohort. Overall, the model preserved strong and stable performance across the external dataset, with only modest decreases relative to internal testing. Mean performance differences were minimal, with sensitivity decreasing by 2.6% (0.77 → 0.75), precision by 2.7% (0.74 → 0.72), F1 score by 2.7% (0.75 → 0.73), and accuracy by 2.6% (0.77 → 0.75). These marginal differences highlight the model’s robustness to center-specific variability. The confidence intervals for external validation metrics are broadly comparable in width to those of the internal test set for high-prevalence endpoints, further confirming stable generalization performance. However, for lower-prevalence conditions—most notably VSD (sensitivity CI: 0.55–0.89), PE (sensitivity CI: 0.57–0.84), and RVE (sensitivity CI: 0.53–0.77)—the wider intervals reflect the limited number of positive cases available in the external cohort (Table S13) and underscore the need for larger multi-center external validation studies to consolidate performance estimates for these rarer conditions. For a full comparison including 95% confidence intervals for all external validation metrics, computed via bootstrap resampling (n = 1,000), please refer to Table S14. It is important to note that these aggregated metrics represent the mean across all 17 tasks, including structurally complex assessments (e.g., right ventricular enlargement) that intrinsically pose greater diagnostic challenges and historically show higher variability. Crucially, in core high-prevalence clinical categories—specifically valvular heart diseases and left ventricular function—the model sustained superior performance levels significantly above the global average, aligning with the “expert-level” accuracy reported in these specific sub-tasks.

The resulting task-wise feature attribution matrix (Figure 10) demonstrated that each diagnostic task exhibited a distinct, physiologically coherent attribution signature, rather than relying on a small set of generic global predictors. Across tasks, the model consistently emphasized anatomically and hemodynamically relevant domains—including left atrial remodeling, ventricular geometry, pulmonary artery dimensions, and right-heart congestion markers—aligned with known disease mechanisms.

Secondary contributions from left ventricular end-diastolic dimension (LVEDD), LVESD, and LVEF indicate that the model also incorporates downstream LV dilation and reduced contractility where present. Overall, the attribution profile suggests that DeepCard infers MR through its hemodynamic and structural consequences, rather than isolated Doppler parameters.

Discussion

We developed and validated DeepCard, a comprehensive multi-task deep learning framework for automated cardiac ultrasound diagnosis that addresses critical limitations of existing artificial intelligence approaches in echocardiographic analysis. Our system demonstrates robust performance across 17 diagnostic tasks encompassing valvular diseases, ventricular dysfunction, chamber enlargement, and structural abnormalities, achieving a high level of label-level agreement with experienced cardiologists on the same set of pre-measured quantitative echocardiographic parameters while substantially reducing inter-observer interpretive variability in the classification of standardized measurements to 13.4% in our clinical validation setting. Rigorous external validation on an independent patient cohort of 102 patients confirms the model’s generalizability beyond the training environment, and systematic benchmarking against established baseline methods validates the architectural design choices. These findings establish DeepCard as a clinically viable solution for standardizing cardiovascular diagnosis in settings where expert interpretation may be limited or unavailable.

The clinical significance of our multi-task learning approach extends beyond improved accuracy on individual diagnostic tasks. Traditional single-task models, while potentially achieving high performance on isolated objectives such as LVEF estimation or valve disease classification, fail to provide the holistic cardiac assessment that clinicians require for treatment planning. Previous studies have demonstrated that AI models focusing on single parameters, such as the work by Kim et al. showing a strong correlation between automated LVEF estimation and MRI measurements, provide valuable but incomplete diagnostic information. In contrast, our simultaneous evaluation of 39 quantitative parameters across 17 diagnostic tasks mirrors the comprehensive approach of expert echocardiographers, enabling detection of complex multi-pathology patterns that single-task models would miss. Cardiovascular patients frequently present with multiple concurrent pathologies requiring integrated assessment. A patient with dyspnea may simultaneously exhibit mitral regurgitation, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, and pulmonary hypertension, each requiring coordinated evaluation for accurate diagnosis and appropriate therapeutic intervention. Our comprehensive framework addresses this clinical reality by providing integrated multi-parameter assessment in a single inference, facilitating the holistic diagnostic reasoning that experienced cardiologists employ.

The reduction in inter-observer interpretive variability represents a clinically meaningful advance in diagnostic standardization, directly addressing a specific and well-recognized limitation of conventional echocardiographic practice. Importantly, the reported 13.4% figure refers specifically to variability in the interpretive classification of pre-measured quantitative parameters, namely the disagreement that arises when different clinicians derive different diagnostic conclusions from the same set of standardized measurements. This form of variability is conceptually distinct from measurement variability, that is, the 15–17% operator-dependent inconsistency associated with parameter acquisition itself, which DeepCard neither addresses nor claims to address. Nevertheless, reducing interpretive variability remains highly clinically relevant. Even when measurements are obtained under standardized protocols, diagnostic conclusions in borderline cases may still vary substantially across clinicians, particularly for conditions that require multi-parameter integration, such as diastolic dysfunction grading, pulmonary artery pressure estimation, and structural remodeling assessment. By learning a consistent mapping from standardized measurements to diagnostic outputs, DeepCard provides a reproducible interpretive framework that complements, rather than replaces, the measurement standardization achieved through protocol-based acquisition. While DeepCard is designed specifically to reduce downstream interpretive variability, variability at the upstream measurement stage remains an additional and clinically important source of inconsistency in echocardiographic practice. At this stage, Klassen et al. documented that even in well-equipped international hospitals, inter-observer variability in echocardiographic measurements can lead to diagnostic disagreement rates of 19–25%. Traditional echocardiographic measurements show substantial variability even among experienced operators because of differences in transducer positioning, cardiac phase selection, respiratory cycle effects, and subjective identification of anatomical landmarks. This upstream variability contributes directly to downstream diagnostic uncertainty, particularly for borderline measurements near diagnostic thresholds.

External validation on 102 patients from an independent medical center provides crucial evidence of model generalizability that is often lacking in published artificial intelligence studies. Many deep learning models demonstrate impressive performance on internal test sets but experience substantial performance degradation when applied to external data, a phenomenon well-documented in medical imaging literature. Our minimal performance degradation in external validation, with mean decreases of only 2.6% across all metrics, indicates that the model has learned generalizable features of cardiac pathophysiology rather than overfitting to institution-specific patterns or imaging artifacts. This robustness likely results from several design choices, including aggressive regularization through dropout and weight decay, extensive data augmentation simulating measurement variability, and multi-task learning, which encourages learning of shared representations useful across multiple diagnostic objectives. The external validation cohort’s similar but not identical disease distribution compared to the training cohort provides an appropriate test of generalizability, demonstrating that the model performs reliably across the range of pathologies encountered in routine clinical practice. The consistent performance across internal and external cohorts suggests that the model would maintain reliability when deployed in similar clinical environments with comparable imaging equipment and patient populations.

Our systematic benchmarking against a range of modality-consistent tabular baselines reveals a nuanced performance landscape that merits careful interpretation. Although DeepCard achieves the highest overall macro-F1 score among the evaluated methods, its margin over strong competitors such as FT-transformer (0.75 vs. 0.73) and MMoE (0.75 vs. 0.74) remains modest and should not be overinterpreted as a standalone justification for greater architectural complexity. A macro-F1 improvement of 0.01–0.02, while directionally favorable, is unlikely to be clinically decisive on its own. Meanwhile, gradient-boosted decision tree models such as XGBoost and LightGBM, which attain macro-F1 scores of 0.73 and 0.71, remain mature, interpretable, and extensively validated approaches for clinical decision support. In practice, these models may represent a more pragmatic choice in deployment scenarios where interpretability, robustness, and regulatory simplicity are prioritized, particularly for high-prevalence endpoints for which performance differences are minimal. These findings align with recent advances in multi-task learning architectures across medical domains, where task-specific optimization combined with shared feature learning has consistently shown superior performance compared to single-task models or simple multi-output extensions of standard architectures. However, a known challenge in multi-task learning is gradient interference, where conflicting updates between tasks can degrade performance.

It is also important to acknowledge that the benefits of multi-task training are not uniform across all endpoints. As shown in the paired significance analysis with false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Table S6), the improvements conferred by DeepCard’s multi-task learning (MTL) architecture over single-task learning are statistically robust for structural and lower-prevalence endpoints—including left atrial enlargement (LAE), RAE, left ventricular enlargement (LVE), RVE, LVH, IVST, PE, WMA, and VSD—where shared representation learning provides the greatest knowledge transfer benefit. In contrast, for several common endpoints such as MR, TR, AR, AS, PR, LVDD, LVSD, and PAP, the MTL improvements over well-tuned single-task models are modest and do not reach statistical significance after FDR correction (q > 0.05). This pattern is clinically interpretable: common, high-prevalence conditions are well-supported by sufficient task-specific training data even in isolation, and thus benefit less from shared representations. These findings suggest that in future work, a hybrid architecture—applying multi-task learning selectively to lower-prevalence and structurally complex endpoints while allowing greater task-specific independence for well-represented common conditions—may further optimize both statistical robustness and clinical reliability.

While DeepCard utilizes a hard-parameter sharing approach suitable for high-correlation tabular measurements, more advanced state-of-the-art methods have been proposed to mitigate these conflicts. For instance, Chen et al.33 introduced modular learners (Mod-squad) to design mixtures of experts for task-specific optimization, and recent research by Wu et al.34 has demonstrated the utility of multi-modal multi-task mixture of experts for patient-specific modeling. While our 39-parameter input currently benefits from shared representations, incorporating such modular or expert-based architectures represents a vital future direction to enhance the model’s robustness as we scale to more heterogeneous clinical datasets. The performance gains justify the additional architectural complexity and computational requirements, particularly given that inference times remain clinically acceptable for real-time diagnostic support.

The attention mechanism’s learned feature importance patterns, revealed through SHAP analysis, align closely with established clinical diagnostic criteria from American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association guidelines, enhancing model interpretability and clinical trust. For mitral regurgitation diagnosis, the model appropriately prioritizes mitral valve velocities, left atrial dimensions, and regurgitant jet characteristics with contribution values of 0.32, 0.25, and 0.24, respectively, which are precisely the parameters that cardiologists emphasize when evaluating mitral regurgitation severity according to current guidelines. For left ventricular systolic dysfunction assessment, the model correctly emphasizes ejection fraction, end-systolic volume, and fractional shortening. For wall motion abnormalities, the model learned to integrate multiple correlated parameters including regional wall motion scores, tissue Doppler velocities across multiple myocardial segments, and global strain measurements, demonstrating sophisticated multi-parameter integration consistent with expert clinical reasoning. This concordance between model-learned importance and expert clinical reasoning suggests that the model is learning clinically valid diagnostic patterns rather than exploiting spurious correlations in the training data. Such interpretability is essential for clinical adoption, as physicians are understandably reluctant to rely on black-box algorithms whose decision-making processes cannot be understood or validated. Our SHAP-based interpretability analysis provides the transparency necessary for clinical integration and regulatory approval.

Our findings have important implications for addressing global disparities in cardiovascular care access. As documented by the World Health Organization, approximately 40% of the global population lacks access to basic diagnostic equipment including echocardiography, and even in regions where equipment is available, shortage of trained specialists severely limits diagnostic capacity. The United States alone faces a projected shortfall of approximately 15,000 cardiologists by 2025, while European data show highly variable access with an average of only 20 echocardiography-trained professionals per 100,000 people. In resource-limited regions, primary healthcare facilities show particularly acute shortages, with only 30% equipped with echocardiography devices and less than 10% having physicians with expertise to interpret results. By providing automated interpretation that approaches expert-level accuracy, DeepCard could enable primary care physicians, emergency medicine providers, and non-specialist clinicians to perform and interpret echocardiographic examinations that would otherwise require referral to distant specialty centers. This capability is particularly valuable in low- and middle-income countries where CVD burden is rising rapidly but specialist availability is severely constrained. The system’s modest computational requirements, operating efficiently on standard clinical workstations without specialized hardware such as graphics processing units, facilitate deployment in resource-limited settings without requiring expensive hardware upgrades.

The model’s performance compares favorably to previously published AI systems for echocardiographic diagnosis while providing substantially broader diagnostic coverage. Madani et al. reported 94.4% accuracy for automated cardiac disease diagnosis across mixed pathologies, while our system achieves 91% overall accuracy across 17 specific diagnostic tasks with detailed severity grading. The slightly lower overall accuracy is counterbalanced by the greater granularity and clinical specificity of our multi-parameter assessment, which provides actionable information for treatment planning rather than broad disease categories. Our wall motion abnormality detection sensitivity of 80% and specificity of 84% is comparable to specialized deep convolutional neural networks reported in the literature, despite our model simultaneously handling 17 diagnostic tasks rather than focusing exclusively on wall motion analysis. This demonstrates that multi-task learning, when properly implemented with task-specific output heads and weighted loss functions, can achieve performance comparable to specialized single-task models while providing comprehensive assessment. The multi-task approach also confers practical advantages in clinical deployment, as a single integrated model is simpler to implement, maintain, and update than multiple specialized models.

The choice between DeepCard and simpler alternatives should be guided by the specific clinical context. DeepCard is most valuable in settings that require comprehensive, multi-endpoint reporting in a single inference pass, for example, busy tertiary care centers or resource-limited environments where a single operator must assess a broad spectrum of cardiac conditions simultaneously. In such scenarios, the reduced parameter footprint (∼15-fold fewer parameters than 17 separate single-task models), lower inference latency (∼2.8 m s vs. ∼40.8 m s per patient), and consolidated maintenance pipeline demonstrated in Table S7 offer meaningful operational advantages. Conversely, for highly specialized workflows focused on a single well-defined diagnostic question, such as a dedicated LVEF screening program, a well-tuned single-task model or gradient-boosted baseline may offer comparable performance on that specific endpoint with lower implementation complexity, as reflected in the endpoint-level benchmarking results of Table S10. From a computational resource perspective, DeepCard is designed to run efficiently on standard clinical workstations without specialized GPU hardware; however, in settings with extremely constrained infrastructure or strict requirements for model simplicity, conventional machine learning approaches remain a viable alternative for narrowly defined applications.

The integration of our system into clinical workflows could substantially reduce the time and expertise required for echocardiographic interpretation. Studies have documented that comprehensive echocardiographic interpretation by experienced cardiologists typically requires 15–20 min per study, while our system generates complete diagnostic reports in under 30 ss after parameter extraction. This efficiency gain, demonstrated by Lovell et al. showing AI tools reducing diagnosis time to 20 ss, could dramatically increase diagnostic throughput in busy clinical practices and emergency departments where rapid triage decisions are critical. Furthermore, the standardized reporting format generated by our system could improve communication between providers and reduce ambiguity in diagnostic reports, which has been identified as a source of medical errors and delayed treatment. Automated documentation also reduces the administrative burden on cardiologists, potentially allowing more time for direct patient care and complex case management.

Future research directions should address several important extensions of this work. Expanding the training dataset to include data from multiple ultrasound equipment vendors would enhance generalizability across the diverse echocardiography equipment landscape in real-world clinical practice. Ultrasound systems from different manufacturers may produce systematically different measurements due to differences in image processing algorithms and beamforming techniques, and transfer learning approaches or domain adaptation methods could enable a single model to perform robustly across equipment from multiple vendors. Incorporating advanced echocardiographic modalities including three-dimensional imaging, speckle-tracking strain analysis, and contrast echocardiography would provide access to additional diagnostic information beyond conventional two-dimensional and Doppler imaging. Global longitudinal strain, for example, has emerged as a sensitive marker of subclinical left ventricular dysfunction that precedes ejection fraction decline, and automated strain analysis could enhance early disease detection.

Implementing uncertainty quantification through Bayesian deep learning or ensemble approaches would provide calibrated confidence intervals for predictions, enabling clinicians to appropriately weight model recommendations based on prediction certainty. This is particularly important for rare conditions or cases with unusual presentations where the model has limited training experience. Developing automatic image quality assessment algorithms could identify examinations where image quality is insufficient for reliable automated diagnosis, triggering alerts for repeat imaging or manual expert review. Integration with electronic health record systems could enable longitudinal tracking of cardiac parameters over time, facilitating early detection of disease progression or treatment response. Temporal trends in parameters such as left ventricular dimensions, ejection fraction, or valve regurgitation severity could be automatically flagged when changes exceed predefined clinically significant thresholds.

Prospective clinical trials comparing patient outcomes with and without AI-assisted diagnosis are necessary to demonstrate clinical utility beyond technical accuracy. Such trials should assess not only diagnostic accuracy but also downstream effects on treatment decisions, resource utilization, patient satisfaction, and clinical outcomes including morbidity and mortality. The impact on diagnostic confidence and decision-making speed among non-specialist providers deserves particular attention, as these are the clinical contexts where automated interpretation could have the greatest impact. Cost-effectiveness analyses comparing AI-assisted diagnosis to current standard of care would inform healthcare policy decisions and facilitate evidence-based resource allocation. Extending the framework to pediatric echocardiography would require substantial modifications to account for age-dependent normal values, different disease prevalence patterns, and developmental cardiac anatomy, but would address an important unmet need given the even more severe shortage of pediatric cardiologists globally.

Limitations of the study

Our study has certain limitations that offer opportunities for future research. First, while our multi-center design provides valuable external validation, the data were derived from specific clinical centers. Future studies involving broader geographic regions and more diverse patient demographics would further confirm the model’s global generalizability. Second, the model was developed based on standardized imaging protocols using a single vendor’s equipment (Philips Affiniti 30). While this ensured high data consistency for initial validation, it remains unclear how the model’s performance transfers across different ultrasound vendors, probes, or less controlled acquisition settings. However, because DeepCard operates on standardized quantitative measurements rather than raw pixel data, it may inherently possess greater cross-vendor stability than image-based models. Future studies involving a wider array of ultrasound hardware will be essential to fully establish its interoperability in heterogeneous clinical environments. Third, while the current cohort size was sufficient to demonstrate robust model convergence, we acknowledge that the statistical power for certain rare diagnostic categories may be limited. We have addressed this by reporting task-specific prevalence and 95% CIs to provide a transparent assessment of model stability. Expanding the dataset in future iterations would further refine performance, particularly for rare or complex pathologies. Finally, the model utilized expert cardiologist consensus as the ground truth. While this approach aligns with standard clinical practice and ensures high relevance to real-world workflows, future investigations incorporating invasive hemodynamic data or multi-modality imaging could provide complementary validation standards.

Experimental model and study participant details

This retrospective observational study analyzed anonymized echocardiographic examination records from adult patients aged 18 years or older who underwent comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography at the First Medical Center of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital between January 2023 and March 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (No. S2025-751-01). Given the retrospective nature of the study using fully anonymized medical records with no patient contact or intervention, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee in accordance with local regulations and institutional policies. All patient identifiers were removed from the dataset prior to analysis to ensure complete anonymization and protect patient privacy.

The study population consisted of consecutive adult patients who underwent clinically indicated echocardiography as part of routine cardiovascular assessment. Patients were included if they met the following criteria: age 18 years or older at the time of examination, completion of a comprehensive transthoracic echocardiographic study including all standard imaging views and Doppler assessments, and availability of confirmed clinical diagnoses documented by board-certified cardiologists. Exclusion criteria included incomplete clinical documentation defined as missing data exceeding 20% of collected variables, presence of complex congenital heart disease requiring specialized imaging protocols, and poor image quality that precluded reliable quantitative measurements as determined by the performing sonographer.

A total of 500 patients from the First Medical Center met all inclusion criteria and comprised the primary study cohort. This cohort was randomly divided into a training set of 400 patients and an internal test set of 100 patients using stratified random sampling to maintain proportional representation of disease categories. Patient demographics included a median age of 51.0 years with interquartile range of 41.0–56.0 years, with 60.0% male participants. The cohort represented diverse cardiovascular pathology including valvular diseases in 35.2%, ventricular dysfunction in 28.6%, cardiac chamber enlargement in 24.8%, and other structural abnormalities in 11.4% of patients.

For external validation, an independent cohort of 102 consecutive patients was prospectively collected from the Sixth Medical Center of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital between April 2024 and July 2024 using identical inclusion and exclusion criteria. This external validation cohort had similar demographic characteristics with median age of 52.3 years, 58.8% male participants, and comparable distributions of cardiovascular risk factors and disease presentations, ensuring appropriate representation for generalizability assessment.

Method details

Echocardiographic data acquisition and standardization

All echocardiographic examinations in both the primary and external validation cohorts were performed using Philips Affiniti 30 ultrasound systems equipped with S5-1 phased-array transducers operating at center frequencies between 1 and 5 MHz with harmonic imaging capabilities. Examinations were conducted by certified cardiac sonographers with a minimum of 5 years of clinical experience, following standardized imaging protocols that adhered strictly to the current recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography and the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging. Each examination protocol included comprehensive two-dimensional imaging, color Doppler flow mapping, spectral Doppler interrogation, and tissue Doppler imaging. It is important to clarify that the 39 input parameters utilized by the DeepCard framework are obtained via manual measurement by certified cardiac sonographers following the standard recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE). DeepCard serves as a standardized interpretative layer that processes these measurements into multi-task diagnostic outputs to minimize subjectivity in clinical decision-making.

The standardized imaging protocol acquired views from multiple acoustic windows to enable comprehensive cardiac assessment. From the parasternal window, long-axis and short-axis views at the aortic valve, mitral valve, and papillary muscle levels were obtained. From the apical window, four-chamber, two-chamber, and three-chamber views enabled evaluation of all cardiac chambers and valves. Subcostal imaging provided assessment of the inferior vena cava and its respiratory variation. Doppler interrogation followed a systematic protocol to quantify blood flow velocities and pressure gradients. Pulsed-wave Doppler sampled mitral inflow velocities at the leaflet tips, while continuous-wave Doppler measured peak velocities across all cardiac valves. Tissue Doppler imaging quantified myocardial velocities at the mitral annulus. Quality control procedures ensured that each measurement was performed across three consecutive cardiac cycles with electrocardiographic gating to identify optimal cardiac phases.

Deep learning model architecture

The DeepCard model adopts a lightweight residual 1D-CNN architecture tailored for structured echocardiographic measurements. Because 1D convolutions impose an inductive bias over adjacent inputs, the ordering of tabular features is not arbitrary in our implementation. We therefore arrange the 39 standardized measurements into a fixed, clinically defined 1D sequence based on anatomical and functional relevance, such that neighboring features correspond to physiologically coupled parameters (e.g., grouping left-ventricular systolic indices, diastolic indices, valvular hemodynamics, right-heart/pulmonary pressure parameters, and structural findings into contiguous blocks). The full block-wise ordering scheme and index mapping of all 39 features are provided in Supplemental Table Sx to ensure reproducibility.

To verify that this design choice is meaningful rather than incidental, we performed a controlled feature-ordering ablation in which only the input sequence was changed while keeping the backbone and training settings identical. Specifically, we compared the baseline clinical ordering against within-block shuffling, block permutation, and full random permutation of the 39 features. The results show that preserving clinically meaningful local neighborhoods yields consistently stronger performance than fully randomized sequences, supporting that the 1D-CNN leverages local correlations among related measurements.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.11 with SciPy version 1.11.0 for hypothesis testing, Statsmodels version 0.14.0 for regression analysis, and scikit-learn version 1.3.0 for machine learning utilities. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p-value less than 0.05, with Bonferroni correction applied when conducting multiple related hypothesis tests to control family-wise error rate. Sample size of 500 patients for the primary cohort was determined to ensure at least 30 cases per class for the rarest diagnostic categories, satisfying requirements for reliable deep learning model training. Post-hoc power analysis confirmed greater than 80% power to detect clinically meaningful differences defined as effect sizes of at least 0.5 in diagnostic performance metrics. All performance metrics are reported with 95% confidence intervals computed using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. Specific sample sizes, definitions of central tendency, and dispersion measures are detailed throughout the results section and figure legends for each analysis.
