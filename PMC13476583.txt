Ecosystem settings and urbanization shape microbial communities and antibiotic resistance genes on coastal microplastics
Authors: Yinglin Wu, Ling Xie, Sijia Li, Juan Ye, Zhenchang Zhu, Ying Zhang, Feiyang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Habitat drives plastisphere composition with strong context dependence.•Urbanization boosts mangrove richness while beach responses stay weak.•Microplastics carried >1000 ARGs peaking in urban mangrove tidal flats.•Disturbance yields sparser modular networks and weakened interaction hubs.•Mangrove plastispheres showed denser bacteria–ARG co-occurrence patterns.

Introduction

Owing to the inadequate management of plastic waste by humans, approximately 1.4 million tons of wasted plastic yearly entering the world ocean (Sonke et al., 2025). Microplastic pollution (particles < 5 mm), derived from both direct anthropogenic release and environmental weathering fragmentation, pervades almost all marine ecosystems (Cottom et al., 2024; Thompson et al., 2024). These long-lived, anthropogenic substrates provide novel surfaces for microbial colonization and biofilm formation, thereby facilitating microbial attachment and community development (Amaral-Zettler et al., 2020). Over time, they give rise to distinct micro-ecosystems known as the “plastisphere” (Zettler et al., 2013). A comprehensive global review revealed that over 80% of microorganisms in the plastisphere differ from those in natural environments and exhibit altered metabolic functions related to nitrogen and organic compounds (Li et al., 2024a). The bacteria-mediated nitrification process on plastics even reaches 0.9–12 times that in the surrounding environment in estuarine (Su et al., 2024). Moreover, the fouling microplastics provide favorable ecological niches for pathogenic and antibiotic-resistant bacteria to colonize and thrive (Bowley et al., 2021). The plastisphere, as an artificial biotic interface, has attracted increasing attention because it may influence microbial ecological processes and act as a potential reservoir for microorganisms and resistance genes of environmental and public health concern.

Previous studies have identified 594 pathogenic species and plentiful of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) associated with microplastics in marine environments (Dong et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024a). In natural environments, microplastics may become biofouled and acquire organic matter or algal-derived chemical cues such as dimethyl sulfide (DMS), which may influence their detectability and ingestion risk for some marine organisms in a species- and context-dependent manner (Bowley et al., 2021; Procter et al., 2019). Microplastics carrying resistance genes and pathogens pose a significant potential threat to the health of wildlife and humans (Li et al., 2024b). Microorganisms that adhere to microplastics can disperse over significant distances, potentially leading to substantial impacts on marine ecosystems (Li et al., 2024a). It is urgent to deeply study microbiome and ARGs on microplastics in marine environment.

Human socio-economic is thriving along global coastlines (Jia et al., 2023; Mokhtari et al., 2025), where they primarily discharge land-based waste plastics into the ocean (Kaandorp et al., 2023; Strokal et al., 2023). Less dense floating plastics are carried by tidal currents and can become easily trapped upon reaching intertidal zones, particularly those containing vegetation (e.g., mangrove) (Ivar do Sul et al., 2014; Martin et al., 2019). The median abundance of microplastics in nearshore waters is more than 30 times greater than that found in offshore waters (Zhao et al., 2025). The coastal zone serves as a global hotspot for the distribution of marine microplastics. Special attention should be given to the characteristics of the "plastisphere" along coastlines worldwide.

The accumulation of microorganisms on microplastics in marine environments is intricately influenced by multiple environmental factors, and correlates with geographical locations across different marine regions (Bairoliya et al., 2024). Research on the plastisphere in coastal zones remains at a nascent stage, particularly regarding microplastics (Zhi Xiang et al., 2023). Based on the incubation experiment, microplastic-associated bacterial community in coastal seawaters were analyzed (Dudek and Neuer, 2023; Xu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022), and nitrogen metabolism on plastics in estuaries were comprehensively studied (Su et al., 2024, 2022). The previous research on marine plastisphere mainly focuses on plastics or microplastics in seawater, while studies in coastal habitats are relatively scarce. The biofilm community on marine plastics found along coastal beaches significantly differed from that in the surrounding water and sediment (Bairoliya et al., 2024; Basili et al., 2020). Additionally, hundreds of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were identified on discarded plastics in mangrove ecosystems (Sun et al., 2023a). Several studies have examined the relationships between microorganisms and microplastics; however, most of them were based on incubation tests by artificially produced particles (Li et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024). Microplastics in natural environments differ from synthetic ones: they are the outcome of long-term natural physical, chemical, and biological processes (natural aging). It is imperative to analyze the microbiome associated with microplastics and their health risks in coastal habitats. In addition to environmental factors, prior research has demonstrated that human activities significantly affect the characteristics of the plastisphere in river ecosystems (Li et al., 2022b, 2021; Yan et al., 2024). However, it remains unclear whether this phenomenon can occur with microplastics on coastal habitats.

Here, we investigate how ecosystem type (mangrove vs sandy beach) and coastal urbanization jointly shape microplastic-associated microbial communities and antibiotic resistance genes in tidal-flat sediments. To this end, we sampled mangroves and sandy beaches along a typical Chinese coastline (Leizhou Peninsula) and applied a shotgun metagenomic sequencing.

Materials and methods

Field sampling

Leizhou Peninsula is located in Southern China, encompassing the largest mangrove wetland in China and the longest sandy beach (Fig. 1A). Microplastics samples were collected in surface sediment in the field from four beaches. To distinguish the effects of ecosystem type (mangrove vs. sandy beach) and coastal human disturbance intensity (rural vs. urban), we applied a 2 × 2 full factorial sampling design (Table S1) to assess both main effects and their interaction. The four factor combinations corresponded to four beach-level sampling sites: S1 (mangrove–rural, n = 3), S2 (mangrove–urban, n = 4), S3 (sandy beach–rural, n = 3), and S4 (sandy beach–urban, n = 4). Each sampling site represented a beach area rather than a single fixed coordinate point, and samples within each site were collected across spatially separated sections to capture within-beach variability. These samples were therefore treated as within-beach spatial replicates. However, because each treatment combination was represented by one beach-level site, site identity and treatment combination were not independently replicated. Accordingly, between-group comparisons were interpreted as exploratory and site-specific. The field survey was conducted over the period from June 24 to July 1, 2023. The stainless-steel sieves (4-mesh and 16-mesh) were used to screen out particles measuring 1–5 mm. Using standardized visual identification protocols, candidate particles demonstrating matte surfaces, mechanical resilience, and low surface roughness were classified as putative microplastics following the removal of biological/organic debris and mineral interferents (Masura et al., 2015). Subsequently, individual particles were randomly selected and analyzed by micro-FTIR spectroscopy to confirm their polymer composition (see Supplementary Materials). Polymer identifications were accepted only when the spectral match exceeded 65%. and all of the analyzed particles were verified as microplastics. To preserve the in-situ characteristics of microplastic-associated microbial communities and ARGs, microplastic particles were not rinsed with sterile water prior to DNA extraction. We avoided rinsing because it could have removed loosely attached cells, extracellular DNA, and biofilm fragments, potentially leading to underestimation of microplastic-associated abundances and bias in community composition. Instead, in the intertidal zone, we hand-picked only visually clean plastic particles, defined as particles without visible mud coating, attached mineral grains, shell fragments, plant debris, or other macroscopic organic residues. For each sample, more than 5 mL microplastics were collected, so that any incidentally co-collected mineral or organic particles represented only a minor fraction and were negligible for downstream analyses. This procedure was used to minimize non-plastic interference while retaining the native structure of microplastic-associated microbial and ARG assemblages.

All analysts completed competency-based training in microplastic morphology recognition prior to sample processing. Microplastics of all shapes were categorized in the field using sterilized tools and instruments, immediately stored on dry ice, transported to the laboratory within 5 h, and then stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

DNA extraction and metagenomic sequencing

The method of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) was used to extract DNA of microplastic-associated microorganisms. The procedure was performed as follows: (a) Microplastic samples were lysed with lysozyme in a 1000 microliter CTAB solution at 65 °C; (b) Phenol (pH 8.0): chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) were added, followed by centrifuge at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the supernatant; (c) Isopropanol was added, followed by shaking the mixture vertically, and the solution was then allowed to precipitate at −20 °C; (d) The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was discarded, the pellet was washed twice with 1 ml of 75% ethanol, and the precipitate was collected; (e) Double distilled water was added to dissolve the DNA sample, and if necessary, the solution was incubated at 55–60 °C for 10 min to aid dissolution; (f) One microliter of RNase A was added to digest the RNA, and the solution was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min.

The Agilent 5400 was used to measure the concentration, integrity, and purity of DNA. Library preparation and metagenomic sequencing were conducted at Wekemo Technology Group Co., Ltd., located in Shenzhen, China. The NEB Next® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) was utilized to construct the library. The procedure was briefly summarized as follows: qualified DNA samples were fragmented to approximately 350 base pairs, underwent end repair, received a polyA tail, had sequencing adapters ligated, were purified, and underwent PCR amplification to finalize the library preparation process. PCR products were purified using the AMPure XP system, the insert size of the library was detected using the Agilent 2100, and the library concentration was quantified by real-time PCR. DNA library sequencing was then performed using the Illumina NovaSeq high-throughput sequencing platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads.

Metagenomic analysis

Quality control of data and removal of host sequences

We utilized the Illumina NovaSeq high-throughput sequencing platform for metagenomic sequencing, generating an average of 22,526,025 raw read pairs per sample, with a range of 20,848,873 to 23,853,506 raw read pairs. Raw sequencing data were processed using KneadData (The-Huttenhower-Lab, 2020), with Trimmomatic used for quality control (Bolger et al., 2014). This process included removing adapter sequences, eliminating low-quality sequences, filtering out potential human-derived contaminant sequences by aligning reads against the human reference genome GRCh38 using Bowtie 2, and performing quality control checks with FastQC to evaluate the efficiency and validity of these steps (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012; Rosenbloom et al., 2015). Finally, the proportion of cleaned sequences is between 90.32% and 96.28%.

Annotation of taxonomy and ARGs

We employed Kraken (version 2.0.7-beta), a state-of-the-art alignment tool based on k-mer analysis, to annotate and classify all valid sequences from each sample (Wood and Salzberg, 2014). The Bracken (version 2.0) algorithm was utilized to estimate the relative abundance of species in the sample (Lu et al., 2017). Use DIAMOND (version 5.0) (Buchfink et al., 2015) to align and annotate the quality-controlled and host-removed reads from each sample against the antibiotic resistance gene database CARD (version 4.0.2) (Alcock et al., 2023). Clean metagenomic reads were aligned against ARG reference sequences in the CARD database using DIAMOND. The gene abundance was calculated by TPM method ([Fragments Count / Gene Length] / Sum [Fragments Count / Gene Length]) (Kim et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2020). The raw sequencing data reported in this study have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) at the National Genomics Data Center, China National Center for Bioinformation / Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, under accession number CRA040531, and are publicly available at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa.

Statistical analysis

We used a unified statistical framework to assess diversity patterns, differential abundance, and network structure of microbiome and ARGs data while accounting for their compositional nature. Microbial taxonomic and gene-level feature tables were treated as compositional, with zero replacement using the count zero multiplicative method in the zCompositions (version 1.6.0) package (Palarea-Albaladejo and Martín-Fernández, 2015), closure, and centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation. β-Diversity was quantified using Aitchison distances and visualized by PCoA. Before PERMANOVA, PERMDISP was performed to assess homogeneity of multivariate dispersion. PERMANOVA was then used to evaluate the main and interaction effects of ecosystem (mangrove vs. sand) and human impact (rural vs. urban), with robustness checked against Bray–Curtis and Hellinger-based distances. α-Diversity (Observed richness, Chao1, Shannon, and Hill N1 for ARGs) was analyzed using two-way fixed-effects models including Ecosystem, Human, and their interaction, followed by planned contrasts within and between factor levels. Differential abundance of genera was assessed with DESeq2, whereas ARG relative abundances were modeled with limma on CLR-transformed data under parallel designs, yielding volcano plots for main and simple-effect contrasts. Genus–genus co-occurrence and genus–ARG association networks (putative host) were inferred from CLR-transformed relative abundances using sparse graphical models (graphical lasso with stability-based penalization), and key network metrics (connectivity, modularity, and centrality of putative keystone or putative host genera) were used to compare network organization across ecosystems and human–impact levels. Full model specifications, diagnostics, and sensitivity analyses are provided in the Supplementary Materials. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1, including data preprocessing, model fitting, significance testing, and visualization.

Results

Analysis of microbiome on microplastics

A total of 6 421 microbial taxa were identified as co-occurring on microplastics in surface sediments of the coastal zone. Given the wide variability in some relative-abundance values, data in this paragraph are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon test with Holm correction. Bacteria dominated the microbial community, accounting for 99.12% of the total abundance (IQR: 98.72–99.52%; P < 0.001), followed by Fungi (0.22%, 0.15–0.37%) and Archaea (0.18%, 0.08–0.09%) (Table S2). Among bacterial taxa, Pseudomonadota (Proteobacteria) was overwhelmingly dominant (85.31%, 79.74–90.15%; P < 0.001), followed by Bacteroidota (6.77%, 3.64–8.28%; P < 0.001; Fig. 1B). Among fungi, Ascomycota was the predominant phylum (59.70%, 51.59–73.27%; P < 0.001), followed by Basidiomycota (6.25%, 2.04–7.99%; P < 0.001). Among archaea, Euryarchaeota (40.54%, 10.22–85.19%) and Nitrososphaerota (47.57%, 8.37–78.13%) were the most prevalent phyla (P < 0.001).

Natural and anthropogenic influences on the plastisphere

PCoA of Aitchison distances (Fig. 2A–D) revealed clear community separation along the first two axes (PCoA1 = 29.2% and PCoA2 = 11.8% of total variance). Group centroids indicated distinct clustering by ecosystem type and, to a lesser extent, by human impact. At the 2 × 2 level, the separation among mangrove–rural, mangrove–urban, sand–rural, and sand–urban samples further suggested group-specific differences in community composition. The largest separation occurred between mangrove–urban and sand-rural groups (Euclidean distance ≈ 155.1). PERMANOVA detected a significant Ecosystem × Human interaction across all β-diversity metrics, indicating consistent results (Table 1). Dispersion tests revealed a modest four-group difference only for Aitchison distances (F = 4.80, P = 0.037), driven by greater heterogeneity in sand–urban samples compared to sand–rural (P = 0.019; mean distance to centroid 84.4 vs. 62.0). No significant dispersion differences were observed for single-factor contrasts (P > 0.13) or under Bray-Curtis or Hellinger distances (P > 0.20). Alpha diversity varied significantly across factors, with a clear Ecosystem × Human interaction for richness estimators (Fig. 2E–G). Type III ANOVA revealed significant effects on Observed richness (Ecosystem, P = 0.017; Human, P = 0.00044; interaction, P = 0.0072) and Chao1 (Ecosystem, P = 0.021; Human, P = 0.00050; interaction, P = 0.0076), whereas Shannon diversity responded only to ecosystem type (P = 0.036). Post hoc comparisons based on estimated marginal means showed that urban mangrove sites harbored greater richness than rural ones (Observed Δ = −1331, P = 0.00013; Chao1 Δ = −1499, P = 0.00015; contrasts coded rural − urban), whereas this pattern was absent on sandy shores. Within urban areas, mangroves supported higher diversity than sands (Observed Δ = 974, P = 0.00078; Shannon Δ = 0.665, P = 0.028; Chao1 Δ = 1087, P = 0.00092), with no difference under rural conditions. The pre-specified difference-in-differences contrast confirmed a stronger urbanization effect in mangroves (Observed Δ = −1054, P = 0.0072; Chao1 Δ = −1191, P = 0.0076). These results were robust to the inclusion of log (library size) as a covariate, and variance inflation factors were approximately 1.

At a BH–FDR threshold of 0.05, the Human main effect (urban vs. rural) yielded 182 significant genera (∼23.5%; net enrichment ≈ +28), whereas the Ecosystem main effect (sand vs. mangrove) identified 132 significant genera with an overall net depletion (−30). Simple-effect contrasts (Fig. 3B) showed that human influence was stronger within mangroves than within sands (185 vs. 126 significant genera), while ecosystem differences were more pronounced under rural than urban conditions (131 vs. 94). The interaction volcano (Fig. 3A) identified 89 genera (∼10.9%) with significant Ecosystem × Human effects, 46 of which were also supported by the LRT; among these, 36 showed positive interactions and 21 negative interactions. Representative positive-interaction taxa (stronger human effect in sands) included Methyloceanibacter, Moorena and Bythopirellula, whereas negative-interaction taxa (stronger human effect in mangroves) included Nonlabens, Algoriphagus and Cellulosimicrobium. Co-occurrence network analysis indicated differences in plastisphere microbial network topology across human-impact and ecosystem contexts. For the Human factor (Fig. 4A and B), urban networks were smaller and less connected than rural ones (63 vs. 79 nodes; 84 vs. 263 edges), with ∼50% lower density and longer path lengths (unweighted 5.12 vs. 2.99), suggesting reduced network cohesion. Clustering and assortativity also declined, while modularity increased (0.704 vs. 0.565) and hub dominance weakened (−58% in mean eigenvector centrality), indicating a shift toward more separated network modules. Positive associations became dominant (69%vs. 51%), whereas negative associations decreased markedly. For the Ecosystem factor (Fig. 4C and D), sand networks also exhibited lower density (0.047 vs. 0.051) and efficiency than mangroves, with similar clustering but reduced assortativity (−43%) and weaker hub connectivity. Slightly higher modularity and more negative edges suggested differences in network association structure between ecosystems. Together, these results indicate that human disturbance and ecosystem context were associated with changes in plastisphere co-occurrence network topology. Analysis of antibiotic resistance genes on microplastics

A total of 1280 antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were identified on coastal microplastics. The most abundant gene was rpoB2 (14.89 ± 6.01%), followed by Bado-rpoB-RIF (12.89 ± 5.31%), ugd (6.63 ± 2.82%), CRP (3.66 ± 2.84%), and MexF (2.10 ± 1.10%). Among ARG classes (Fig. 5A), multidrug resistance genes dominated (57.61 ± 7.80%), followed by β-lactam (8.97 ± 6.29%), peptide (7.72 ± 2.74%), aminoglycoside (6.15 ± 4.25%), tetracycline (4.09 ± 1.56%), and MLSB (3.24 ± 0.94%). In terms of resistance mechanisms (Fig. 5B), the most frequent non-mutually exclusive CARD mechanism annotations were antibiotic target alteration (37.58 ± 12.32%) and antibiotic efflux (37.22 ± 7.00%), followed by antibiotic target replacement (29.82 ± 11.19%) and antibiotic inactivation (18.03 ± 9.48%). Discussion

Context-dependent responses of coastal plastisphere communities to ecosystem setting and urbanization

Microplastics subjected to long-term exposure provide a novel substrate that facilitates colonization by marine organisms (Amaral-Zettler et al., 2020). The dominance of bacterial communities on coastal microplastics partly mirrored taxonomic profiles commonly found in natural seawater (Ge et al., 2025), suggesting potential ecological overlap or seawater-derived colonization. Pseudomonadota were identified as the dominant bacterial phylum, aligning with the overall conclusions drawn from the summarized data (Sun et al., 2023b). The high richness and diversity within this phylum may buffer against environmental variability, resulting in relatively minor differences across ecosystems and under varying levels of anthropogenic disturbance.

Multivariate analyses demonstrated a clear and robust separation of plastisphere assemblages between mangrove and sandy shores, accompanied by additional shifts along the rural–urban gradient. Previous work in inland river systems has also shown that urbanization can markedly alter microplastic-associated microbial communities; however, these studies did not evaluate whether such responses vary with underlying aquatic ecosystem type (Li et al., 2022b). Differences among ecosystems led to divergent responses to human pressure (Keck et al., 2025), and we detected a significant Ecosystem × Human interaction, indicating strong context dependence in how anthropogenic disturbance reshapes microplastic-associated microbiota. Mangrove tidal flats, with vegetation that attenuates water exchange and produces water-chemistry and redox conditions distinct from the more hydrodynamically connected sandy beaches (Brustolin et al., 2022), may partly account for the divergent responses of plastisphere microbial communities observed between the two habitats. Importantly, dispersion analyses confirmed that these differences primarily reflect shifts in community centroids rather than unequal within-group dispersion, indicating that observed patterns are not artifacts of variability but instead reflect deterministic, environment-filtered assembly processes.

Urban samples showed higher taxonomic richness than rural samples in mangroves, whereas no consistent urban–rural difference was observed on sandy shores. This pattern suggests that urbanization-related differences in α-diversity may vary depending on local habitat type. The weaker response of the Shannon index relative to richness further suggests that the richness increase observed in urban mangroves may have been driven mainly by rare taxa rather than by major shifts in dominant taxa. Urban mangroves are known to support the expansion of opportunistic and previously rare taxa under sustained anthropogenic disturbance (Al-Tarshi et al., 2024). Plastic debris further introduces durable surfaces and physicochemical conditions distinct from surrounding sediments and waters, creating additional microhabitats within these systems (Marques et al., 2023). Together, these factors suggest that urban mangroves provide expanded niche space for rare taxa, enabling their disproportionate enrichment within the plastisphere. The accompanying β-diversity patterns further indicated differences in plastisphere community composition among groups, as reflected by the separation of group centroids in the ordination space.

The volcano-plot analyses further demonstrate that both urbanization and ecosystem type influence plastisphere microbial communities, and that these effects are context dependent. Human disturbance exerts a stronger impact within mangrove systems, whereas ecosystem differences are more pronounced in rural settings. Interpretation of the interaction effects is best grounded in organismal traits rather than statistical patterns alone. Sandy beaches function as high-energy, permeable systems where well-oxygenated coarse sediments and wrack leachates supply abundant low-molecular-weight DOM (Dissolved Organic Matters) to mineral and plastic surfaces (Auer et al., 2025; Waska and Banko-Kubis, 2024), whereas mangrove sediments accumulate cellulose-rich detritus in waterlogged, reducing soils and export sulfur-rich, humic-like DOM (Iroshaka Gregory Marcelus Cooray et al., 2025). Within this contrasting template, genera with positive DID values—Methyloceanibacter, Moorena, and Bythopirellula—show stronger urban–rural contrasts on sandy shores, consistent with their reliance on labile DOM, phototrophic or biofilm-forming lifestyles, and strong surface-colonizing capacity. Conversely, negative DID genera, including Nonlabens, Algoriphagus, and Cellulosimicrobium, decline more sharply in mangroves, reflecting their specialization on polysaccharide- and cellulose-rich detrital niches and aligning with evidence that urban and industrial pressures preferentially erode organic-matter-degrading functions in mangrove sediments. Beyond these genus-specific responses, the accompanying expansion of low-abundance taxa in certain habitat–impact combinations suggest a broader restructuring of plastisphere communities. Functionally, such enrichment may widen metabolic capacity—particularly for hydrocarbon and xenobiotic degradation—and increase the prevalence of pollution-tolerant or antibiotic-resistant lineages, with implications for biogeochemical cycling and potential health risks.

Previous studies of microplastic plastispheres consistently show that plastic surfaces reorganize microbial interaction topology. In controlled experiments, Wu et al. (2019) reported that biofilms on microplastics—relative to those on cobbles and leaf litter—exhibited higher connectivity and shorter path lengths but lower modularity, approaching a more small-world–like configuration (Wu et al., 2019). At larger spatial scales, Li et al. (2022b) found that along a rural–suburban–urban gradient, co-occurrence patterns on field microplastics shifted from sparse associations to dense, tightly clustered sub-networks centered on a few host taxa, with these structures strengthening toward urban reaches (Li et al., 2022b). In soils, Zhu et al. (2022) showed that plastisphere networks were dominated by positive correlations and contained fewer negative (potentially antagonistic) links and weaker module boundaries than adjacent bulk soils (Zhu et al., 2022), whereas Shi et al. (2022) demonstrated across multiple soil types and polymers that microplastics consistently reduced network size, connectivity, module richness, and the number of keystone taxa, indicating a broad simplification of microbial interaction structure (Shi et al., 2022). In this context, our genus-level co-occurrence networks display a similar direction of structural change under human disturbance. Disturbed sites exhibited sparser co-occurrence networks, higher modularity, weaker hub connectivity, and fewer negative associations. These patterns indicate changes in network topology and microbial association structure under disturbed conditions, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of altered functional redundancy, trophic complementarity, or community recovery capacity. The absence of a significant Human × Ecosystem interaction further suggests that the direction of disturbance effects on network topology is broadly consistent across habitats, whereas ecosystem-specific contrasts are more likely to appear in the gradual loss of hub–hub organization and the emergence of more competitive, weakly coupled modules along the mangrove–beach continuum.

Coastal plastisphere communities showed clear habitat separation and context-dependent responses to human disturbance: urbanization drove marked richness gains and compositional shifts on mangroves, whereas effects on sandy shores were weaker, less consistent, and largely confined to low-abundance taxa. Differential-abundance and network analyses pointed to a shared disturbance signature—sparser, more modular interaction structures with weaker hubs—most pronounced in urban mangroves. Overall, these results suggest that plastisphere association patterns differed across human influence and ecosystem contexts, with mangrove plastispheres showing more pronounced network reorganization than sandy beach plastispheres.

Context-dependent responses of coastal plastisphere resistomes

Previous studies on ARGs associated with microplastics in aquatic environments have mainly used three approaches (Table 2): direct field sampling, in situ field incubation, and laboratory simulation. Detection has typically relied on qPCR with gene-specific primers targeting known ARGs, or on metagenomic sequencing of community DNA. In this study, we combined field sampling of tidal-flat microplastics from the Leizhou Peninsula with metagenomic analysis. Unlike the incubation-based designs that dominate the literature, our approach captures ARG patterns under long-term, natural exposure, during which microplastics experience extended interaction with ambient microbial communities and pollution inputs. More than one thousand distinct ARGs were identified on coastal microplastics, a richness broadly comparable to that reported for riverine microplastics in the Zuo River, Guangxi, using a similar field-based strategy (Li et al., 2022b). and clearly higher than values from incubation-based studies in other regions. Although some incubation-based studies have reported lower ARG richness, such cross-study comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because differences in study location, plastic type, exposure duration, sequencing depth, reference databases, and analytical pipelines may strongly influence the reported number of ARGs. These comparisons nevertheless highlight the value of field-based investigations for characterizing ARG profiles on microplastics under environmentally realistic conditions. The Aitchison-based PCoA indicates that ARG composition on microplastics is shaped jointly by habitat and human disturbance, with mangrove and sandy sites forming distinct clusters and a secondary rural–urban separation within each habitat. The combination of a significant Ecosystem × Human effect and similar within-group dispersion points to true shifts in compositional centroids rather than differences in heterogeneity. This two-level pattern is consistent with previous plastisphere studies in rivers and mangroves, where ecosystem context defines the baseline resistome and human activities impose a directional gradient. In the Haihe River, for example, microplastic biofilms carried ARG profiles distinct from cobbles and leaf litter under the same water column, highlighting plastic as a discrete habitat filter (Wu et al., 2019). Along an urbanization gradient in Nanning, field microplastics exhibited systematic shifts in ARG and virulence profiles from rural to urban reaches, accompanied by increasing composite risk scores (Li et al., 2022b). A virus-focused study in the Beilun River likewise showed that microplastic-associated viromes, including auxiliary resistance- and virulence-related genes, are transported and reshaped along the river continuum (Li et al., 2022a). These observations are consistent with our PCoA results: mangrove versus sand reflects ecosystem-level filtering of ARG pools, whereas the rural–urban axis reflects superimposed anthropogenic selection on that baseline.

The α-diversity and differential-abundance patterns further show that the expression of this anthropogenic gradient is strongly habitat dependent. Urbanization was associated with clear increases in ARG richness and evenness on mangrove microplastics, whereas responses on sandy shores were weak or inconsistent. Across 881 ARG features, a noticeable fraction responded to the Human main effect, almost entirely as increases in urban sites, while pure Ecosystem responses were rare; slice contrasts revealed that Human-responsive ARGs occurred on mangrove microplastics, with sandy shores showing no significant feature-level shifts and often lower ARG abundances under urban conditions. These results are in line with coastal plastisphere studies from southern China: in Zhanjiang mangroves, microplastics along a gradient from residential parks to aquaculture and protected stands exhibited stepwise increases in ARG burden, generally exceeding surrounding sediments (Sun et al., 2022); in a Xiamen mangrove reserve, microplastics in both rhizosphere and non-rhizosphere soils hosted higher loads and a broader spectrum of high-risk ARGs and virulence traits than bulk soils, with particularly strong enrichment and co-localization of resistance and mobility determinants in root-associated samples (Li et al., 2025). The Houxi River study further showed that population density and faecal markers tightly tracked ARG and mobile genetic element loads on plastics and leaves, but only certain substrates (e.g., PVC and some biodegradable polymers) expressed markedly elevated mobilome and resistome risks under strong urban pressure (Yan et al., 2024). Collectively, these lines of evidence indicate that ARG profiles on microplastics varied across anthropogenic-disturbance and habitat contexts. Urban mangrove samples showed relatively higher ARG diversity and stronger ARG-related differences than the other sampled site types, but these patterns should be interpreted cautiously given the limited site-level replication and the absence of sediment or seawater baseline data.

Host–ARG partitioning across ecosystems and urbanization gradients

After CLR transformation and adjustment for ecosystem and human-impact effects, the genus–ARG association networks showed that a limited number of genera were statistically associated with multiple ARGs, with predominantly positive associations. This pattern is consistent with previous plastisphere studies in which resistance is concentrated in a limited number of disturbance-tolerant or opportunistic taxa on plastics (Wu et al., 2019). However, these hub-like patterns should be interpreted cautiously because highly abundant or prevalent genera may generate more co-occurrence edges and the network analysis does not directly confirm physical host–ARG linkages. Along the human-impact gradient, the rural network is the most connected but relatively diffuse, with many genera each linked to a moderate number of ARGs, whereas the urban network has fewer edges but more pronounced hub genera, such as Roseovarius, Pseudodonghicola, and Nocardioides, that were statistically associated with multiple ARGs (Li et al., 2022b). Along the ecosystem axis, mangrove microplastics support a denser, more richly connected genus–ARG network with pronounced hubs (e.g. Granulosicoccus, Salipiger, Flavobacterium), while sandy shores exhibit a sparse network with weaker hubs (e.g. Rubricoccus, Poseidonia), in line with evidence that mangrove plastispheres act as hotspots for host–ARG coupling and sand plastispheres function as relatively low-response backgrounds (Li et al., 2025). The occurrence of Roseovarius and Nocardioides as prominent nodes in the urban mangrove network may be related to their potential physiological adaptability to disturbed coastal microhabitats. Some Roseovarius members have been reported to possess metabolic flexibility, including anaerobic growth, denitrification, and oxidation of hydrogen, sulfur, or thiosulfate, which may be advantageous in low-redox and sulfur-rich mangrove sediments (Slobodkina et al., 2022). Nocardioides has also been reported to tolerate low-nutrient conditions and to degrade several hard-to-degrade organic pollutants, suggesting potential adaptability to pollutant-influenced environments (Ma et al., 2023).

Together, these results show that co-occurrence patterns between bacteria and ARGs varied across different levels of human influence and ecosystem contexts. The urban network showed a more centralized association structure, whereas the rural network showed a more diffuse pattern of associations. Ecosystem context was also associated with differences in network connectivity and modular organization. Urban mangrove samples exhibited relatively stronger co-occurrence patterns involving ARGs in this study, highlighting the need for further empirical studies to verify whether similar patterns occur across broader coastal regions and environmental settings.

The more pronounced urbanization associated differences observed in mangrove samples may be related to the stronger retention capacity, environmental heterogeneity, and sediment micro-environments of mangrove habitats. Recent studies have shown that mangrove root systems and fine sediments can trap microplastics and other particles, increasing their residence time in urban influenced sediments (Cerri et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Urbanization can also reshape mangrove soil dissolved organic matter and associated geochemical conditions, including salinity, metals, pH, and soil organic carbon (Wu et al., 2024). In addition, mangrove sediments can contain diverse ARGs and MGEs (Mobile Genetic Elements), and the surrounding environment has been reported to influence ARG and MGE patterns on mangrove microplastics (Jiang et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2022). Beyond these factors, organic rich and fine grained mangrove sediments are often characterized by low redox conditions and strong sulfur related biogeochemical gradients, which may support microbial processes and taxa that differ from those in more oxic sandy beach sediments (Qian et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Microplastic biofilms may further provide localized surface microhabitats with microscale gradients of oxygen and organic substrates, while biofilm derived extracellular polymeric substances can modify microplastic surface properties and enhance pollutant retention (Bottura et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Jo et al., 2022; Moyal et al., 2023).

Limitations

This study was based on one beach level sampling site for each ecosystem and human influence combination, with within beach sampling used to capture local spatial variability. Future studies with multiple independent beaches in each category, together with matched sediment and seawater baselines, will help further test the generality of the observed plastisphere community and resistome patterns across broader coastal settings. To preserve native plastisphere biofilms, particles were not rinsed before DNA extraction. This approach minimized the loss of loosely attached cells and extracellular DNA, but may not fully exclude trace sediment- or mineral-associated DNA. Therefore, only particles with no visible sediment or organic debris were selected, and this potential source of bias is acknowledged as a methodological limitation. A further limitation is that microplastics were collected from supralittoral surface sediments after tidal retreat. Exposure to elevated temperature, desiccation, and other supralittoral conditions may have favored thermotolerant or stress-tolerant microorganisms, meaning that the detected communities may represent a surviving subset rather than the full in-situ plastisphere assemblage. This potential bias should be considered when interpreting the microbial community composition.

Conclusions

Microplastic pollution in coastal wetlands may amplify antimicrobial resistance risks by creating long-lived biofilm habitats that concentrate resistant microbes and genes at the land-sea interface. Across tidal flats, plastisphere communities and resistomes varied across habitat and urbanization contexts, showing context dependent patterns in microbial diversity, ARG profiles, and co-occurrence network structure. Microplastics in mangroves and sandy beaches hosted clearly distinct microbiomes, while urbanization associated differences were more evident in mangroves than in sandy beaches. In urban mangrove samples, microbial richness was higher and co-occurrence networks showed sparser and more modular structures. However, these network patterns should be interpreted as statistical association structures rather than direct evidence of altered functional redundancy or community resilience. Resistomes were highly diverse, with >1000 ARGs detected across all samples, and urban mangrove microplastics showed relatively higher ARG richness and evenness, whereas sandy beach resistomes showed weaker urbanization associated differences. A limited number of genera were statistically associated with multiple ARGs, especially in mangrove networks, but these associations do not confirm direct host ARG linkages. These findings highlight urban mangrove tidal flats as sites of potential concern for microplastic associated resistance and support habitat targeted monitoring of plastisphere resistomes in coastal antimicrobial resistance surveillance. Further field-based studies with broader site level replication and matched environmental baselines will be valuable for testing the generality of these patterns across coastal ecosystems.
