 Frequency and time multiplexing for multiphoton state generation
 Alex Fischer
                                                November 19th, 2025

 Abstract
 One of the primary challenges with photonic quantum information processing is that single-photon states
 are created and heralded probabilistically, rather than being created on demand. One solution to this problem
 is multiplexing, which is attempting probabilistic generation of a single photon at multiple locations, times, or
 frequencies, and switching one successfully generated photon into an output mode.
 Motivated by the development of frequency-based photonic quantum information processing, we consider
 multiplexing using frequency and time as our options to use to get many photon generation opportunities. We
 devise an approach for generating multiphoton states, with photons populating multiple frequency modes in
 the same spatiotemporal mode. This method uses a variable-length optical delays to manipulate the temporal
 mode of the photons, and spaced fiber Bragg grating (FBG) reflectors to jointly manipulate the frequency and
 temporal modes of the photons.
 Experimental progress toward implementing this multiplexing scheme is proceeding along 2 fronts, each with
 different ways to achieve the variable-length optical delay. First, we will implement this scheme using a free-space
 optical quantum memory with multiple discretely adjustable free-space delays. For this implementation method,
 I calculate multiphoton generation rates, accounting for loss, that are realistically achievable with commercially
 available hardware. This work will appear in a theory paper, currently in preparation.
 Second, we will implement this multiplexing scheme in an integrated manner: the variable-length optical
 delay will happen on-chip using a long-lifetime Q-switchable Fabry-Perot cavity. The joint manipulation of the
 frequency and temporal modes of the photons will still happen off-chip in fiber with FBG reflectors. I report on
 experimental progress in constructing integrated Q-switchable Fabry-Perot cavities.

1 Introduction
Photonics is a promising platform for quantum computing and other quantum information processing tasks due
to photons’ lack of interaction with matter and long lifetimes at room temperature. However, a major challenge
associated with photonic quantum information processing is that single-photon states, the building block states
that most photonic quantum information processing protocols start with, cannot be generated deterministically.
They can only be generated probabilistically.
 Since photonic quantum information processing requires single-photon state generation with precise knowledge
of what modes (spatial, temporal, or otherwise) the photons are in, the solution to this problem involves heralding
the generation of a single photon and multiplexing by probabilistically attempting photon generation in many
modes and using the result of one successful attempt. Heralding single-photon generation can be done by producing
entangled pairs of signal and idler photons with nonlinear optical processes such as spontaneous parametric down-
conversion or four-wave mixing, then detecting an idler photon to herald the existence of the corresponding signal
photon. Multiplexing can be done with various schemes to select photons in certain modes where photon generation
succeeded and move them into desired modes.
 Common schemes considered for multiplexing photons include temporal[KK19], spatial[CXR+ 13], and combined
spatial-temporal[BAM+ 16] multiplexing. Here, we consider frequency and time as our degrees of freedom with
which to multiplex. Our use of frequency as a multiplexing degree of freedom is motivated by the development of
frequency-encoded photonic qubits for photonic quantum information processing[LL17], which has some scalability
advantages over the standard spatial encoding of photonic qubits.
 We consider two operations available to jointly manipulate the temporal and spectral modes of photons we
generate. One is a switchable optical memory, and the other is frequency-dependent delays, from either chromatic
dispersion or wavelength division multiplexing with delay line optical fibers. The switchable optical memory we
consider has two modes: a ‘catch-and-release’ mode where any photons in the memory exit the memory and any
photons entering the memory become stored in it, and a ‘stay-the-same’ mode where any photons in the memory

 1

stay there, and any photons entering the memory pass through it. This memory only affects the temporal modes
of photons, independent of their frequency. The frequency-dependent delay, which could be implemented in various
ways including fiber Bragg gratings spaced along an optical fiber, chromatic dispersion, or wavelength division
multiplexing and delay lines, is the natural way to interact the spectral and temporal properties of the generated
photons.
 We have 2 plans to implement the switchable optical delay. The first, and simplest, method to implement
this delay is a commercially available device[AVGK22] with Pockels cells that switch light into and out of free
space delay paths. The second method, which we will develop at Sandia, is integrated Q-switchable Fabry-Perot
cavities. Integrated in this context means manufacturable on a chip at very small scales, on the same devices we
will manufacture waveguides, beamsplitters, and other devices that manipulate light on-chip.
 We consider photons probabilistically generated in discrete time bins (e.g., from a pulsed laser pump) such that
the time bin spacing is equal to the delay line length of the switchable quantum memory. We also consider discrete
frequency bins, which are necessary for the phase modulation and pulse shaper approach to gates with frequency-
encoded photonic qubits. This defines a grid of time and frequency bins, in which photons will be randomly
generated from an entangled photon source; see Fig. 2(b) for an example.
 Quantum information with frequency-encoded photonic qubits makes use of single photons in distinct frequency
modes but identical temporal modes, in order to implement gates via phase modulation and frequency-dependent
phase shifting[LL17]. Therefore, a multiplexing scheme for generating resource states for frequency-encoded photonic
quantum information processing should not just produce a single photon in one spectral-temporal mode, but instead
should produce multiple single photons in different spectral modes but the same temporal mode. This is the task
we focus on in this paper. An example of the state we are trying to produce is in Fig. 2(e).
 Gates with frequency-encoded photonic qubits become less demanding if the relevant single photons are in
spectral modes with minimal frequency spacing. This is because the frequency spacing of the photons determines
the frequency of the RF signal used to phase modulate the light during gates. Higher frequency RF signals are
harder to generate, and they place more demands on the electro-optic material used to construct the phase shifter.
So smaller frequency spacing makes it easier to do gates, especially in an integrated manner. The eventual trade-
off with small frequency spacing is that this upper bounds the linewidth of the photons, which lower bounds their
wavepacket length, which upper bounds the repetition rate of the multiplexing scheme and thus the whole computer.
 In Section 3, I explain our time-frequency multiplexing scheme, and give numerical results for multiphoton state
generation rates when we have access to a single adjustable-length optical delay. In Section 3.3, I explain how to
extend our multiplexing scheme to the case where we have multiple adjustable length optical delays, and we give
numerical results for multiphoton state generation rates with several configurations of multiple delays. In Section
4, I explain our plan to make this multiplexing scheme smaller and lower loss making the adjustable delay out of
integrated Q-switchable Fabry-Perot cavities.

2 Background
The standard dual-rail encoding of quantum information into photons consists of 2 modes the photons can occupy.
Typically these are spatial modes, such as 2 modes of adjacent waveguides. The |0⟩ state is then a single photon
in one of the waveguides, and the |1⟩ state is a single photon in the other waveguide. See Fig. 1(a). This is the
approach that PsiQuantum is using[Psi25].
 Typically the spatial modes of photons one uses are modes of µm-scale dielectric waveguides etched into a chip.
Silicon and silicon nitride are common choices of waveguide material due to their low loss in telecom wavelengths
and ease of manufacturing using established semiconductor manufacturing techniques.
 In this encoding, single-qubit gates take the form of linear optical operations composed of beamsplitters and
phase shifters. Applying a relative phase shift to the |0⟩ or |1⟩ waveguide applies a Z-rotation on the qubit. This
can be done by varying the refractive index of the material that makes up one of the waveguides. 2 options to do
this are by heating the waveguide, which changes the refractive index via the thermo-optic effect, or by applying an
electric field to a waveguide which includes some material that exhibits the Pockels effect: a refractive index that
depends on the electric field in the material. Thermo-optic phase shifters are slow, but they can be very low loss.
Electro-optic phase shifters can be fast, but they are lossier[Rud17].
    Beamsplitters mix the |0⟩ and |1⟩ states. With dielectric waveguides, beamsplitters are achieved not with the
partially reflective mirrors one would use in bulk optics, but by bringing the waveguides close together (µm-scale)
so the evanescent fields of the 2 waveguide modes overlap.
 Entangling gates are performed with entangling measurements called fusions[BR05]. These entangling mea-
surements only succeed with some non-unit probability. This introduces a challenge that one must adapt what

2.1 Frequency-based photonic quantum information processing
This dual-rail spatial mode encoding of quantum information has some advantages, but it is not the most efficient
use of chip space. Every qubit requires 2 waveguides. We can make more efficient use of chip space if we take
advantage of another degree of freedom available to us: frequency. All within one spatiotemporal mode of one
waveguide, we could have the |0⟩ state be a single photon state with one frequency, and the |1⟩ state be a single
photon state with another frequency, as in Fig. 1(b).
 But even this is not the most efficient use of space. Silicon waveguides can be extremely broadband, as silicon
has low absorption across near-to-mid IR. Using commonly available telecommunications hardware designed for
1550 nm practically limits one to a few THz or a few tens of THz of bandwidth. Doing gates in frequency space
with phase modulation[LL17] may practically limit one to several tens of GHz of bandwidth. Still, fitting tens of
qubits in one waveguide using tens of frequency bins is an appealing prospect. With 2n frequency bins available,
we can put n qubits into one waveguide, and save a factor of 2n space on chip. See Fig. 1(c).
 A con of storing quantum information in the frequency of photons is that gates that mix different frequency
states are more involved than the simple passive linear optical gates of dual-rail spatial mode encoding of quantum
information. Passive linear optics cannot change the frequency of its input light, so we cannot get a universal
set of gates out of passive linear optics and single-photon measurements, as is the case with dual-rail spatial mode
encoding of quantum information. However, active linear optics gets us a universal set of gates via phase modulation
and pulse shaping[LL17].

2.2 Sandia’s Error-corrected Photonic Integrated Qubits (EPIQ) project
Sandia has an ongoing project to develop a photonic quantum computing testbed, the Error-corrected Photonic
Integrated Qubits (EPIQ) project. Integrated in this context means that the photonic components are mass
manufactured, at a tiny (µm) scale, on chips using modern semiconductor fabrication techniques. Integrated
photonics is a well-established field that has branched off the integrated circuit industry, taking advantage of much
of the same semiconductor manufacturing technology. This technology enables the fabrication of not only electronic
components, but also optical components such as waveguides and semiconductor lasers. Integrated photonics’
primary applications are in the telecommunications industry, although it has found applications for other purposes
such as sensing and imaging[ZTZ+ 23].
 Sandia has expertise in, and manufacturing capability for, integrated photonics, especially silicon photonics.
Sandia’s Microsystems Engineering Science and Applications (MESA) center has many microfabrication tools, and
a dedicated process for silicon photonics manufacturing that it uses for the lab’s mission work. Sandia staff can
also use MESA for basic research purposes, which is what is happening for the EPIQ project.
 The EPIQ photonic quantum computing testbed currently under development will encode quantum information
in the frequency of photons, in addition to their spatiotemporal modes. We are targeting 10 GHz frequency spacing

2.3 The hard problem of photonic quantum computing: nondeterministic state prepa-
 ration
One of the main challenges with photonic quantum computation is that state preparation is nondeterministic.
One can’t create a |0⟩ or |1⟩ computational state (a single photon state in a fixed, known, spatial temporal and
spectral state) on demand. Instead, one creates entangled pairs of photons with some nonlinear optical process,
such as spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) or four-wave mixing (FWM). Measuring one photon of
the entangled pair (typically called the signal photon) heralds the existence of the other photon in the pair (typically
called the idler). SPDC and FWM do not occur with perfect efficiency, so one can only attempt photon generation
with some nonzero, non-unit probability.
 In order to do quantum computation, one needs to send many single photons into what is essentially a massive
interferometer full of many Hong-Ou-Mandel (HOM)-type interference sites. Any time 2 photons arrive at a
beamsplitter to interfere, they need to arrive at the beamsplitter at the same time in order to exhibit HOM
interference1 . This means we need to take our nondeterministic heralded single-photon sources and transform them
into a source with a deterministic photon arrival time. Transforming the output of many probabilistic heralded
single-photon sources into a (near-)deterministic single photon source is known as multiplexing in this context.
 For spatial-mode dual-rail encoding of single photons, multiplexing schemes generally involve systems of optical
switches that manipulate the temporal[KK19], spatial[CXR+ 13], or joint spatiotemporal properties[BAM+ 16] of
the photons.
 For frequency-based multiplexing of single-photon states, there are multiplexing schemes involving frequency-
conversion[HPH+ 20, JFC+ 18]. In Sandia’s EPIQ project, we want to use multiplexing to directly produce multi-
photon states instead of single-photon states. The multiphoton states we aim to produce have multiple photons all
in different spectral modes, but in the same spatiotemporal mode. Visually, we want to create states where every
spectral mode, or some fixed subset of spectral modes, in Fig. 1(c), has 1 photon. We will then use linear optical
transformations and adaptations based on single-photon measurement results to transform these multiphoton Fock
states into resource states we will use to do quantum computation.
 Existing frequency-based and spatiotemporal multiplexing schemes could be combined with optical add-drop
multiplexers to produce the kinds of states we want to produce. However, we are devising a new approach to
directly multiplex the specific multiphoton states we want, instead of multiplexing to produce many single-photon
states and combining them with (lossy) optical add-drop multiplexers.

3 Multiplexing with an adjustable-length delay
We want to create multiphoton states where we have single photons in multiple different spectral modes, all in the
same known, fixed spatiotemporal mode. We start with entangled photon pairs in random frequency and time bins.
Visually, we start with the states in Fig. 2(b), and we want to end up with the state in Fig. 2(e).
 We will pump our entangled photon-pair source with a pulsed laser. This creates a set of discrete time bins
where single photons may be generated. We also filter the frequencies of the entangled photons we generate, which
creates a set of discrete frequency bins where single photons may be generated. Hence the possible states we can
generate are single photons inhabiting some grid in frequency-time space, as in Fig. 2.
 By measuring the signal photons, we herald the existence of idler photons, which informs us which frequency
and time bins contain photons. This gives us states such as those in Fig. 2(c). From here, we must manipulate the
frequency and time bins of the photons to produce the states we want.

 Manipulating the time bins of the photons can be done with switchable optical delays. This is the standard way
to do temporal multiplexing[KK19]. However, optical switches are typically broadband. It is difficult to construct
a high-speed, low-loss optical switch that can operate on only one or a subset of 10GHz-spaced frequency bins.
Instead, we will use a broadband, high-speed, low-loss optical switch that couples into a fixed length optical delay
with delay time equal to the time bin spacing.
 Our first experimental implementation of this multiplexing scheme will use a commercial switchable free-space
optical delay to implement the switch and free-space optical delay shown in Fig. 2(a). This optical delay is from
a company called PhotonQueue, which spun out of research efforts to do temporal multiplexing for single-photon
generation[AVGK22, KK19]. This switchable delay, which is currently under construction at Sandia, will allow us
to delay photons in 15 ns multiples, as it contains a 15 ns free-space loop that light can be switched in and out of2 .
 We will use this switchable memory to produce the states in Fig. 2(d). To get from Fig. 2(c) to Fig. 2(d), we split
the time bins into ‘batches’, where each batch contains a set of time bins that we will use to create a single-photon
state for one particular frequency bin. We then change the state of the 2 × 2 switch so that it delays photons into
the fixed state of frequency and time bins shown in Fig. 2(d). We ignore irrelevant photons there.
 It is worth noting that the 2 × 2 switch has only 2 states:
 • A ‘catch-and-release’ mode where the switch moves any photons that are in the loop out of the loop to the
 right, and moves and photons that are coming in from the left into the loop.
 • a ‘stay-the-same’ mode where the switch keeps any photons in the loop in the loop, and lets any photons
 coming in from the left continue without entering the loop.
 So we cannot just pick up all the photons we want into the loop and dump them in one time bin, as we cannot
pick up a photon into the memory loop without dumping whatever photons are already in the memory loop.
 To transform from the state in Fig. 2(d) to the final desired state in Fig. 2(e), we need a frequency dependent
delay. We use fiber Bragg grating (FBG) reflectors for this. Fiber Bragg gratings are periodic refractive index
modulations inscribed in optical fiber that can act as narrow bandwidth reflectors. Just as a periodic structure of
dielectric layers with alternating refractive indices makes a high-reflectivity dielectric mirror, a periodic refractive
index modulation in optical fiber makes what is essentially a dielectric mirror embedded within the fiber. Low
amplitude refractive index modulations with many periods can act as a narrowband filter, that can selectively filter
one frequency bin out of the 10 GHz spaced frequency bins.
 Spacing these FBGs at regular intervals equal to half the length of a batch causes each successive frequency bin
to get delayed one more batch than the last, which transforms the state in Fig. 2(d) to the final desired state in
Fig. 2(e). An optical circulator outputs only the reflected light, which contains photons in different frequency bins
lined up in time.
 The high-level overview of our multiplexing scheme is that since a switchable frequency-dependent delay is not
feasible, we combine a switchable frequency-independent delay (the free-space loop) with a fixed, non-switchable
frequency-dependent delay (spaced FBGs and an optical circulator) to produce the adaptable frequency-dependent
delays we need.

3.1 Loss assumptions
When calculating multiphoton state generation rates that account for loss, we assume that each component in the
multiplexing scheme has some amount of loss that we base off of commercially available parts. The loss parameters
we assume are

 • Coupling loss. This is the loss associated with coupling light from the free-space delay loop into fiber.
 • Loss per switch. This is the loss associated with the switch that controls when light enters and exits the
 memory loop.
 • Loss per memory loop. This is the loss associated with one pass through the memory loop. We assume
 this loss is just that of 10 reflections off 99.999% reflectivity mirrors. We count the loss of 10 mirrors here,
 rather than the 4 depicted in Fig. 2(a), because practical constructions of a free space switchable optical
 delay[AVGK22] will use on the order of 1 reflection every light-nanosecond for compactness.
 • Fiber Bragg grating (FBG) transmission loss. This is the loss associated with light passing through a FBG.
 We assume 1%.

 • FBG reflection loss. This is the loss associated with light being reflected by a FBG. We assume 1%.
 • Loss per fiber delay timestep. This is the loss associated with light traveling one timestep in optical fiber. We
 assume 0.5 dB/km is the loss in polarization-maintaining fiber, and one timestep is 10 ns.
 • Circulator loss. Light passes through the circulator twice.

The loss values we use in modelling the multiphoton state generation rates are in Table 1. We do not consider
multiphoton errors, which could be mitigated by efficient number-resolving detectors for the signal photons.

As I have shown, implementing this multiplexing scheme with COTS hardware will result in large amounts of
loss. Engineering the components of this multiplexing scheme to have lower loss is critical to demonstrate that
it can eventually become a practical part of a large fault-tolerant quantum computer. Once we demonstrate this
multiplexing scheme with COTS hardware, we will lower the loss by developing integrated, lower loss versions of
the components.
 Another part of the EPIQ project that I am working on is our effort to make integrated Q-switchable Fabry-Perot
cavities that will be a smaller and lower loss optical storage device. This could replace the variable-length optical
delay in our multiplexing scheme. The variable-length optical delay we have ordered from PhotonQueue[AVGK22]
takes up a significant portion of an optics table and weights several hundred pounds. Integrated cavities could
perform the same function with a footprint on the order of square centimeters or smaller.

4.1 Background and plan
Our collaborators in Peter Rakich’s group at Yale have developed a process to fabricate arrays of high-reflectivity
curved mirrors. Fabry-Perot cavities constructed from one of these curved mirrors and a flat mirror have finesse
greater than 1 million, implying 3 × 10−6 loss per mirror reflection[JMM+ 22]. We are developing a way to switch
light into and out of these cavities in order to use them as variable-length optical storage devices.
 The finesse of a cavity is the ratio of its free spectral range (FSR) (distance between peaks in its transmission
spectrum) to its full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) linewidth. It measures how narrow the resonances are
relative to the distance between resonances. Another definition of finesse more useful in this context is

We plan to couple these cavities and waveguides by etching waveguides and grating couplers into the top layer of

 Both the flat and curved mirrors are dielectric mirrors coated with many alternating quarter wavelength layers

coated in thin layers on top of each other. The top layer is is a full wavelength of Ta2 O5 , which is 753 nm in
the material. We have developed a process that can etch 400 nm into this top Ta2 O5 layer, allowing us to pattern
waveguides, grating couplers, and other integrated photonic elements into these dielectric mirrors.
 By etching a grating coupler into the bottom mirror, we can change the cavity mode so that it includes some
field scattered into the top Ta2 O5 layer of the flat dielectric mirror. See 8 for a diagram of where this grating will
be etched and how it will affect the cavity mode. By etching waveguides into this layer, we can guide the light
being scattered out of this cavity. We plan to control the rate at which light switches out of the cavity by switching
whether the light scattered into or out of the cavity constructively or destructively interferes with itself. The exact
method by which we will do this is under development.
 The first set of flat dielectric mirrors with gratings etched so they will be aligned with the curved cavity mirrors,
as well as waveguides that route scattered light to the edge of the chip so we can measure the coupling between the
cavity and the waveguides, are currently being fabricated. I designed the pattern to be etched into this layer along
with Tarik Cigeroglu, a collaborator in Peter Rakich’s group.
 The flat mirror layer and curved mirror layer are made on separate, cm-scale chips. Bonding multiple layers
of cm-wide chips together while maintaining µm-scale optical alignment presents a novel heterogeneous integration
challenge, and we will have to develop an alignment and bonding procedure here at Sandia. These integrated cavity
devices will eventually have to be combined with the devices produced in Sandia’s silicon photonics process, which
is being used to make the devices that do single-photon generation, single-photon detection, and frequency-based
gates. Probably these devices will be connected with fiber, although it may be possible to fabricate the flat dielectric
stack mirrors on the same wafers as those from the silicon photonics process.
 This concept of etching patterns into the top layer of a high reflectivity dielectric mirror (currently 99.99%
reflectivity, and we are aiming for 99.999%) with curved mirrors that make cavities is an exciting new platform for
integrated photonics. Applications beyond quantum computing could include reflectivity-enhanced grating couplers,
and integrated reference cavities.

ideally with as low loss as possible. We make waveguides on this dielectric stack using a 400 nm etch into the 753 nm

 Ring resonators, like Fabry-Perot cavities, have resonant frequencies when the resonator length is an integral
multiple of the wavelength of light in the waveguide. Because the round-trip length of the microring resonators we
fabricate is small (several hundred µm), the resonance line widths are relatively large, on the order of a GHz, so we
can resolve the peaks with a scanning laser with 0.1 pm (0.0125 GHz) resolution. With this measurement method,
