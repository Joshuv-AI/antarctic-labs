Executive Summary
This report documents an electricity planning study for the community of New
Shoreham, Rhode Island (aka Block Island), conducted under the U.S. Department of
Energy (DOE) Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project (ETIPP) by a national
laboratory analysis team comprising staff from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory.
The national lab team was assisted by community development specialists at the Island
Institute, based out of Rockland, Maine.

Block Island is a 7-mile-long and 3-mile-wide island that sits 12 miles off the southern
coast of Rhode Island, covering 7,000 acres. The community has roughly 1,200 year-
round residents but swells to 15,000–20,000 in the summer. More than 43% of the land
mass on Block Island is preserved for open space (Block Island Tourism Council 2025).

Block Island is served by the Block Island Utility District (BIUD) dba Block Island Power
Company. Up until 2017, the island’s electricity was supplied by a fleet of on-island
diesel generators. However, the construction of the nation’s first offshore wind facility,
Block Island Wind Farm, allowed the island’s electric grid to be interconnected with the
mainland through National Grid’s Sea2Shore submarine cable (Block Island Power
Company 2025). Because the submarine cable from the mainland to Block Island and
from Block Island to the offshore wind farm were sized and installed specifically to
transmit electricity from the offshore wind farm to the ISO New England electric grid, the
owners of the wind farm (Ørsted) only allowed BIUD to import electricity to the island via
the cable. As a result, the utility’s ability to export any electricity from the island to the
mainland was restricted. In addition, the removal of the underground diesel fuel storage
tanks in 2019 meant that BIUD now relies almost exclusively on electricity generated off
the island. As a result, the community is now subject to the reliability of the submarine
cable. Any outage, regardless of how frequent it may occur, forces BIUD to quickly start
what are now its emergency backup diesel generators, as well as to procure and
schedule sufficient fuel deliveries to meet its limited fuel storage capabilities and to
generate enough electricity until the necessary repairs are completed on the cable to
restart it. Lastly, in response to the Block Island community’s priorities, the cooperative
utility has pursued over the past several years long-term power purchase agreements
with electricity suppliers who can either provide energy generated exclusively from
renewable resources or provide renewable energy credits to offset any energy
generated from fossil-fuel based power plants.

The Block Island community applied to ETIPP in May 2023. The community requested
technical assistance on the following two issues:

 1. To improve the community’s understanding of the current electricity system on
 the island.

 2. To perform a forward-looking assessment of the island’s electricity supply and
 demand opportunities that could help inform decision-making to address the
 community’s goals around efficiency, resilience, and affordability.

The application was accepted, followed by a series of conversations between BIUD,
Block Island community members, and the technical lab team to develop a scope of
work. The results included the following:

  • Energy Roadmap to 2040 development. The technical team will perform an
 assessment of viable on-island solar and wind resources, develop an electricity
 demand forecast for 2040 based on community priorities around the adoption of
 specific electricity-focused technologies, and analyze a number of different
 electricity supply scenarios, which all ensure on-island biofuel and solar resources
 can meet 100% of the peak demand for electricity per community priorities, and
 demand scenarios, which vary by the level of adoption of these electricity-focused
 technologies per community priorities, in 2040 illustrating key trade-offs associated
 with critical areas for decision and policymaking. The community and BIUD would
 then use the results to make more informed decisions about future on-island
 resource development and demand-side end use program development.
This report presents the inputs, assumptions, and outcomes of the Energy Roadmap to
2040. Based on the analysis contained in this report, the technical team identified the
following key takeaways for the community of Block Island in line with the broad areas
of interest conveyed during the technical team’s initial conversations with both BIUD
and the island’s year-round residents during public meetings:

  • Sufficiency. Block Island has more than enough developable land (see Figure ES-
 1) and rooftops for solar resources to meet 100% of the island’s peak demand for
 electricity in 2040. As a result, all modeled scenarios allow the community to meet
 its goal of supplying 100% of its annual electricity needs with a portfolio of on-
 island biofuel and solar resources and off-island renewable energy resources
 under purchased-power agreements, per stated community priorities.

To achieve these goals, the Block Island community and its cooperative utility would
need to be deliberate in their efforts to promote on-island solar development as well as
achieve highly aggressive on-island energy efficiency and electrification goals. In the
former case, BIUD and its stakeholders could consider issuing requests for proposals

1 Introduction
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Energy Technology Innovation Partnership
Project (ETIPP) works with coastal, remote, and island communities seeking to
transform their energy systems and increase energy resilience. DOE national
laboratories partner with regional organizations to provide technical assistance to
address selected communities’ energy needs.

This report documents an energy system planning study for the town of New Shoreham,
Rhode Island (a.k.a. Block Island), conducted under ETIPP technical assistance by a
national laboratory analysis team comprising staff from Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory. The national lab team was assisted by community development specialists
at the Island Institute based out of Rockland, Maine.

2 Block Island’s Future Energy Demand
Developing a robust energy roadmap requires understanding future electricity demand.
The demand forecast provides this understanding via a scenario-based evaluation of
the potential impacts of different adoption rates associated with various technologies on
Block Island’s energy consumption. This section explains how the technical team
developed the demand forecast, which serves as a critical input for the subsequent
techno-economic analysis.

The primary challenge in creating this forecast is to project future load in a way that
accounts for the key drivers of demand growth, particularly the adoption of energy
efficiency and electrification technologies. To address this challenge, the technical team
developed the following two scenarios reflecting different adoption levels of energy
efficiency and electrification technologies (see Section 5 for more information):

 1. Status-quo. Adoption rates are held constant at historical levels concerning
 energy efficiency and electrification technologies.

 2. Established goals. Adoption of energy efficiency and electrification technologies
 sufficient to meet state-level objectives in Rhode Island or aggressive national
 goals.

2.2 Demand Forecast Methodology
The technical team constructed the demand forecast using a composite, “building block”
methodology (Figure 1). This method systematically layers the incremental load impacts
of new technologies onto a baseline projection of growth within existing electricity
consumption. It also ensures a transparent analysis that allows for the clear attribution
of load growth to its constituent drivers.

 Figure 1. Building blocks of the demand forecast.

 Block Island’s Energy Roadmap to 2040 3

The core components of the forecast include:

  • Baseline load. This component represents existing electricity use, derived from
 historical BIUD system data. It is scaled through 2040 using utility-provided
 forecasts for seasonal peak demand growth.
  • Distributed solar photovoltaics (DPV). This component quantifies the impact of
 existing behind-the-meter solar generation, which reduces metered load when on-
 site solar electricity production can first be self-consumed. The output is modeled
 using the PVWatts tool to reconstruct the island’s true gross electricity consumption
    in 2024 (OpenEI 2014).
  • Transportation electrification (electric vehicle supply equipment [EVSE]). This
 component projects the future load from residential and public EV charging. The
 hourly load profiles are derived from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s
 (NREL’s) Transportation Energy & Mobility Pathway Options (TEMPO) model (Yip
    et al. 2023).
  • Building electrification, energy efficiency, and full home electrification. These
 components model the load impacts from the adoption of electric heat pumps, heat
 pump water heaters, and building envelope efficiency measures. The impacts are
 derived from NREL's ResStock model, a simulation tool that models the energy
 consumption of the U.S. housing stock (Wilson et al. 2021).
A key challenge was adapting the state- and national-level energy models to reflect the
unique characteristics of Block Island. Because of the limited availability of island-
specific data, this analysis used allocation factors and statewide data to localize the
model inputs. The assumptions made during this localization process directly affected
the validity of the resulting forecast and represents a primary source of uncertainty. This
section of the report documents this and other uncertainties as they can affect each
component of the demand forecast.

2.2.1 Baseline Electrical Load
The baseline electrical load forecast represents the projected evolution of electricity
demand from existing uses, exclusive of new electrification technologies. The technical
team developed the 8,760-hour load profile through the following multistep process:

 1. Establish reference year. The technical team used the 2024 hourly load profile,
 derived from BIUD’s metered data on electricity imports via the submarine cable
 as the initial reference. The team filled gaps in the hourly data, which may result
 from meter read errors or cable outages, using a linear interpolation method to
 create a complete dataset.

 2. Apply growth factors (2025–2035). For the period from 2025 to 2035, the
 technical team calculated growth factors using BIUD-provided forecasts for
 summer (July) and winter (February) peak demand. The team applied the
 projected relative change in the summer peak as a growth factor to all hours in
 the summer season (June–August), and applied the same methodology for the

 Block Island’s Energy Roadmap to 2040 4

 winter peak and winter season (December–February). This method scales the
 8,760-hour profile for each year while preserving the underlying seasonal and
 diurnal load shapes observed in the reference year data.

 3. Extrapolate growth (2036–2040). As utility-provided peak forecasts were not
 available beyond 2035, the technical team extrapolated the growth for the final 5
 years of the forecast period using a linear trend derived from the 2025–2035
 reference data.

 4. Incorporate residential customer growth. The technical team applied a
 compounding annual customer growth rate of 1.0% to the residential portion of
 the load. The team determined this rate from the observed average change in
 BIUD residential customer accounts from 2021 to 2023.

The forecast for baseline electrical load from 2025 to 2040 is essentially a linear
extrapolation of peak demand growth, which assumes that recent growth trends will
continue unabated. Alternative growth patterns (e.g., accelerating, decelerating, or S-
curve growth) could yield different long-term peak demand values and therefore
accelerate or delay the projected impacts from the demand forecast.

2.2.2 Distributed Solar Photovoltaics Load
Existing DPV systems produce energy that customers either consume behind the
meter, under BIUD’s previous net-energy metering (NEM) program, or inject directly into
the local electric grid, under the current buy-all-sell-all 1 (BASA) program. In the former
case, this self-consumed energy is not recorded as a utility sale; therefore, it represents
missing load that must be quantified and added back to the baseline to reflect the
island’s true total electricity consumption.

The DPV modeling process involved the following four steps:

 1. Simulate representative array. The technical team used a PVWatts simulation
 of a representative 1-kilowatt (kW) solar array located at 10 Beach Ave, New
 Shoreham, Rhode Island (OpenEI 2014). For more information on the inputs, see
 Appendix D.

 2. Scale to existing fleet. The resulting 1-kW hourly generation profile was then
 scaled to each of the 52 existing net energy metered installations on the island
 based on their specific system capacities, which total 208.1 kW, as provided to
 the technical team by BIUD.

 3. Develop representative residential load profile. To estimate DPV self-
 consumption, the technical team developed a representative residential load
 profile to compensate for the absence of granular hourly data. This data gap
 introduces uncertainty into this part of the analysis. The representative hourly
 load shape was created using an NREL ResStock baseline profile for the region,

 which was then scaled by the average electricity consumption for a typical Block
 Island residential customer (Wilson et al. 2021).

 4. Calculate self-consumption. For each hour, the estimated DPV generation was
 compared to the representative residential load profile. DPV output that was less
 than or equal to the customer’s load was classified as self-consumption. This
 self-consumption was then aggregated for all 52 systems and added back to the
 baseline load forecast for all years.

A key assumption in this analysis is that no new NEM systems are added after the
baseline year, which is consistent with BIUD’s current program plans (Block Island
Utility District 2025). Under the current BASA tariff, future DPV installations will export
all generated electricity to the grid using separate meters. This method prevents the
issue of additional missing load that could arise from new installations.

2.2.4 Transportation Electrification
The demand forecast includes the additional electrical load from residential level 1 and
level 2 chargers, as well as public direct current fast chargers. The technical team
created these load profiles using NREL's national TEMPO database, a comprehensive
U.S. transportation demand model that simulates vehicle ownership, travel behavior,
and charging patterns (Yip et al. 2023).

To align with the primary forecast scenarios, the technical team selected 2050 EV

2.2.5 Building Electrification and Energy Efficiency
The technical team identified load changes from residential building energy efficiency
and electrification technologies via a two-part process: (1) by quantifying the hourly
impact on load from each technology, and (2) by projecting the cumulative adoption of
each technology over the forecast horizon.

The hourly load impact for each technology (an increase for electrification or a decrease
for efficiency) is based on the ResStock model (Wilson et al. 2021). The technical team
created a unique 8,760-hour “delta load” shape, representing the change in
consumption from the ResStock baseline profile for each of the following technology
packages:

 • Heat pumps. Based on replacing a fuel heating system with an ENERGY STAR
 air-source heat pump that retains the old system for supplemental heating.

 • Heat pump water heater. The load impact of a heat pump water heater, isolated
 from a full home package.

 • Building envelope (light touch). Savings based on upgrading attic insulation to
 International Energy Conservation Code Residential 2021 standards and
 achieving a 30% reduction in air leakage.

 • Heat pumps and building envelope. A single package that bundles the heat
 pump and building envelope upgrades.

 • Full home electrification. A comprehensive package combining the heat pump
 and envelope measures with the electrification of all other major appliances (e.g.,
 clothes dryer, cooking range).

The technical team created adoption levels for these technology packages using
projections from two sources:

2.2.5.1 Example: Calculating the impact of heat pumps in the "status quo"
 scenario
To illustrate this process, consider the load impact from heat pumps in a single hour,
6:00-7:00 p.m. on a cold day in January for the year 2027.

 1. Determine the per-unit delta load. Using the load curves established earlier, for
 a representative home, installing a new heat pump increases electricity demand
 by about 3.0 kWh at this specific hour.

 2. Calculate cumulative adoptions. The status quo scenario projects that 8 new
 homes adopt heat pumps each year. The forecast begins in 2025. By the start of
 2027, adoptions from 2025 and 2026 have occurred. Therefore, the cumulative
 number of adopted heat pumps is: (8 adoptions in 2025) + (8 adoptions in 2026)
 = 16 cumulative adoptions.

 3. Calculate the total load impact. The total impact for this specific hour is the
 per-unit delta load multiplied by the cumulative number of adoptions. 3.0
 kWh/home × 16 homes = 48 kWh.

Thus, for this single hour in January 2027, the total additional load placed on the Block
Island grid from all heat pumps adopted under the status quo scenario is 48 kWh. This
same calculation is performed for all 8,760 hours of each year and for every measure to
build the complete, aggregated demand forecast for 2025 and 2040.

2.3 Key Assumptions for Load Projections
The demand forecast relies on a series of key assumptions that are critical to
interpreting the results. In addition to the specific modeling assumptions noted
previously, the following general assumptions apply:

 • Background efficiency/changes in historical trends. The forecast does not
 explicitly model changes in background energy efficiency or deviations from
 historical building trends that may result from evolving building codes or
 appliance standards.

 • Residential load share. It is assumed the relative share of total system load
 between residential and nonresidential customers remains constant throughout
 the forecast period in BIUD’s service territory, based on historical monthly
 averages from 2021 to 2023.

 • Weather data. Technology load impacts are simulated using a single 2018
 weather year, which may not represent the full range of potential weather
 conditions over the forecast horizon.

 • System operation. For forecasting purposes, the model assumes the submarine
 cable, on-island generation, and metered load are fully operational and do not
 simulate outages or other emergent grid conditions.

A critical validation of the methodology is whether it can accurately reflect Block Island’s
highly seasonal residential occupancy. The model addresses this through its
fundamental design. Because the incremental load from new heating technologies is
calculated as a function of the total residential system’s load, which inherently captures
the significant drop in winter demand, the added load from electrification is
proportionally smaller during low-occupancy winter months. The resulting reduced load
ensures the newly electrified load best represents the smaller year-round population on
Block Island.

2.5 Risks and Limitations
Uncertainties affect any long-term forecast and assessing the impact of such risks is
critical for interpreting the results and for future planning efforts. Risks and limitations of
each component of the forecast have been noted in their respective sections. Additional
key risks and limitations of this forecast include:

 • Geographic allocation of travel. This forecast makes no assumption about the
 impacts from travel and associated energy consumption that may differ from what
 was included in the baseline electrical load forecast.
 • Constant customer-class load share. The analysis assumes the proportional
 split between residential and nonresidential building load remains constant over
 time. Significant changes in the island’s commercial sector or housing patterns
 could alter this ratio, shifting the overall load profile. The effect of changing this
 assumption is additionally compounded because there is no modeling of
 commercial electrification in the demand forecast.
 • Constant year-round customer share. The analysis assumes the proportional
 split between summer-only vs. year-round electric utility customers remains
 constant over time. Significant changes in the island’s population of year-round
 residents would significantly alter the overall load profile.
 • Economic and market barriers. The high upfront cost of technologies like heat
 pumps is a significant barrier to adoption, particularly for low- and moderate-
 income households. The analysis does not model the impact of consumer
 economics, payback periods, or the availability of financing on adoption decisions,
 or the “information deficit” among consumers and installers regarding heating
 technologies.

 Block Island’s Energy Roadmap to 2040 13

In summary, this forecast should be interpreted as a rigorous analysis of the potential
for grid impacts if certain adoption pathways are realized, rather than a prediction that
they will be realized, or that their effects are certain. The energy profile of Block Island
in 2040 will depend as much on real-world barriers as it will on tackling the outcomes
identified in this report.

4 Techno-Economic Modeling Methodology
This section outlines the methodology of the techno-economic model that was used to
assess each scenario in this analysis. By integrating the demand forecast from Section
2 and the solar and wind energy resource assessment from Section 3, the model
estimates the resulting on-island generation and its associated costs in 2040. This
techno-economic analysis provides the basis for assessing the difference between
potential pathways for the Block Island electricity system.

For each scenario, the model determines the combination of on-island generation,
curtailments or energy storage, and imported power required to meet the projected
hourly demand. The following subsections describe each type of supply-side resource
and the core logic of the model.

4.1 On-Island Generation Sources
For each scenario, the model determines the combination of on-island generation,
curtailments or energy storage, and imported power required to meet the projected
hourly demand. This section describes each on-island supply-side resource within the
techno-economic model.

4.1.1 Biofuel Generation
In the spring of 2024, the technical team met with the BIUD team and the Town of New
Shoreham’s facilities manager regarding an effort by the town to develop a biofuel
facility at the transfer station. Given the timing of those discussions relative to the timing
of this analysis, the technical team agreed with the BIUD team’s assessment that it was
most appropriate to include this as an on-island supply-side resource in 2040.

Accordingly, the model includes a firm, dispatchable, on-island biofuel generator as a
resource available to serve a portion of the island’s electrical load. The technical team
based the levelized cost of energy on NREL’s 2024 Annual Technology Baseline (ATB)
midcase projection for biofuel resources in 2040, which is estimated at $159/MWh
(NREL 2024).

The model assumes the generator operates with a constant output of 75 kW for 20
hours each day, from 2:00 a.m. to 10:00 p.m. This operational profile is a fixed
assumption based on BIUD input regarding the on-island generator (Block Island Utility
District 2024).

The fixed 20-hour dispatch profile is a model simplification based on the information
provided by BIUD. In practice, a generator of this type would likely be dispatched based
on economic signals to provide grid services or capacity during periods of high demand
or low solar or wind resource output.

 Block Island’s Energy Roadmap to 2040 22

4.1.2 Solar Generation
The technical team generated hourly production profiles for all solar resources using
NREL’s PVWatts calculator (OpenEI 2014). Detailed assumptions and inputs into the
PVWatts calculator are available in Appendix C.

4.1.2.1 Net-Energy Metering Systems
NEM includes the 208.1 kWdc of existing behind-the-meter rooftop solar installations
operating under the now-closed NEM tariff. In the techno-economic model, the analysis
treats existing NEM systems as having zero marginal cost, as the utility does not incur a
direct fuel or power purchase cost for the energy they produce. This assumption does
not account for the administrative costs of the NEM program or any indirect grid support
costs.

Consistent with current BIUD program plans, the model assumes no new NEM systems
were added after 2024 (Block Island Utility District 2025).

4.1.2.2 Buy-All-Sell-All Distributed Solar
BASA includes new distributed solar installations operating under a BASA tariff, wherein
all generated electricity is exported to the grid. The total installed capacity is defined by
the adoption level in each scenario (e.g., sufficient to meet 30%, 50%, or 90% of the
2040 summer peak demand). The model assumes the compensation rate for BASA
resources is 81.38% of the total wholesale market purchase cost, a rate derived from
historical BIUD purchased power costs. For discussion of the market purchase cost
estimated in 2040, see Section 4.3.

The analysis includes existing BIUD-owned solar resources, which have a total capacity
of 94 kWdc. They receive the same compensation rate as all other BASA solar
installations.

Holding the BASA compensation rate constant as a flat percentage of long-term total
market purchase costs through 2040 is a simplifying assumption. Future tariff
structures, regulatory changes, or market conditions could alter this rate.

4.1.2.3 Community-Scale Solar
In each scenario, the analysis sizes the new, community-scale solar installations (i.e.,
installed capacity) to ensure 100% of summer peak demand is met by solar facilities
(i.e., BASA distributed-scale installations plus community-scale solar installations
equals 100% of summer peak demand). For example, if BASA distributed-scale solar
installations are set to provide 30 MW of a 100-MW summer peak demand, the model
would size community-scale solar installations at 70 MW.

The analysis bases the cost of community-scale solar on NREL’s 2024 ATB midcase
projection for distributed commercial-scale solar in 2040, which is $54/MWh (2022 U.S.
dollars). The analysis assumes community-scale solar projects are secured through
take-or-pay power purchase agreements (PPAs). Under these terms, the utility is

 Block Island’s Energy Roadmap to 2040 23

obligated to pay for all energy generated, including any that must be curtailed because
of system constraints.

4.2 Management of Excess Generation
Given the submarine cable’s inability to export power, the model incorporates one of
two strategies in each scenario to manage hours when on-island solar generation
exceeds local demand. This option represents a key decision for Block Island between
investing in capital assets (batteries) to maximize the use of on-island solar resources
versus accepting operational energy reductions (curtailment) to minimize upfront costs.

4.2.1 Battery Storage Systems (Odd-Numbered Scenarios)
These scenarios use the NREL ATB’s representative 4-hour lithium-ion battery, which
has a 7,200-kWh storage capacity, an 85% capacity factor, inverter/storage ratio of
1.67, and inverter loading ratio of 1.3. 7 (NREL 2024). 8

The number of batteries deployed is determined by the amount of battery capacity
needed to absorb the highest daily excess on-island generation projected for that
scenario. In each hour, the model charges the batteries with any on-island generation
that exceeds demand. The stored energy is then dispatched in subsequent hours when
on-island generation is insufficient to meet demand, thereby reducing the need for
market purchases via the submarine cable. This strategy maximizes the value of on-
island solar resources and enhances energy self-reliance.

The analysis derives the annualized capital cost from the ATB’s 2035 projection for total
installed cost (including maintenance, battery degradation, and standard grid integration
costs without local infrastructure upgrades), resulting in an estimated annual cost of
$173,465 per battery over a 15-year operational life. 9 This estimated annual battery cost
represents the primary cost associated with this strategy. Although this analysis focuses
on energy arbitrage, it does not quantify the additional economic value batteries could
provide through ancillary services or by enhancing resilience during submarine cable
outages.

Using a standardized, large-scale commercial battery is a modeling simplification
dictated by the available data in the ATB. This approach likely overestimates the capital

4.2.2 Curtailment (Even-Numbered Scenarios)
In scenarios without battery storage, the model curtails any on-island solar generation

expenditures, the model follows a curtailment priority based on contractual obligations. 10
It curtails BASA solar resources first, as the utility is not required to pay for nonexported

needed, the model then curtails community-scale solar resources.

owner but are not a direct expenditure for the utility. In contrast, curtailed energy from
community-scale solar systems represents a direct cost to the utility because of the
assumed take-or-pay contracts, which obligate payment for all generated energy
regardless of whether it is used. This distinction creates a direct financial incentive for
the utility to prioritize the curtailment of resources with more flexible payment terms.

This strategy avoids the high capital cost of batteries but results in wasted energy and
can create financial disincentives for solar developers. The model’s curtailment
hierarchy is based on assumed contract structures that may differ from those in future
PPAs.

4.3 Market Purchases
In all scenarios, market purchases via the submarine cable are the balancing resource
required to ensure Block Island’s grid reliability. When the combined output from on-
island generation and stored energy is insufficient to meet hourly demand, the model
procures the deficit from the mainland's wholesale electricity market. This resource is
especially critical for meeting demand during nighttime hours and periods of low solar
output.

The technical team based hourly market prices on NREL’s 2023 Cambium dataset,
which is a standard, publicly available source for long-term projections of the U.S.
electricity sector (Gagnon et al. 2024b). The analysis uses the Cambium price forecast
for the ISO New England region under the midcase scenario, which assumes moderate,
business-as-usual trends for future technology costs, fuel prices, and demand growth
(Gagnon et al. 2024a).

To create a blended wholesale cost more representative of BIUD's procurement
strategy, the technical team first segmented the hourly Cambium price into two
components: 1) long-term PPAs, and 2) spot market purchases. The technical team
then used the same ratio of costs amongst these components as was observed in
BIUD's 2024 portfolio, holding the relative number of PPA-to-spot kilowatt-hour
purchases constant at the 2024 level.

The use of the Cambium forecast for ISO-New England is a necessary simplification of
what otherwise would be predominantly PPAs. Unfortunately, as a regional forecast, the
Cambium forecast for this region serves only as a proxy for the price of power delivered
to Block Island and does not capture potential local transmission constraints, line
losses, or other delivery charges that could change the final cost.

Furthermore, the assumption that the utility’s procurement strategy (i.e., the PPA-to-
spot-market cost and purchase ratio) will remain static for 16 years is a significant
limitation. A future strategy with a higher reliance on the spot market could expose the
utility to greater price volatility while a strategy with more long-term PPAs could mitigate
price risk but potentially lock in costs that diverge from the market over time. These
factors introduce uncertainty into the absolute cost projections for all scenarios.

4.4 Discussion
The techno-economic framework described earlier was not designed to predict a single
outcome but rather provide a systematic basis for evaluating the trade-offs inherent in
Block Island’s potential energy futures and illuminating the consequences of key
strategic choices. For example, the framework directly compares the capital-intensive
strategy of investing in battery storage against the operational strategy of curtailing
excess on-island solar generation. Similarly, it assesses how different levels of
distributed solar adoption and demand-side electrification influence total system costs
and reliance on imported power. The resulting analysis, which leverages standardized
cost and performance data from NREL's ATB and Cambium datasets, enables a
structured comparison of the 12 scenarios defined in the following section.

5 Pathways to 2040: Identifying Scenarios
Through a collaborative process with BIUD and community stakeholders, the technical
team developed 12 distinct scenarios to analyze potential energy futures for Block
Island.

5.1 Key Scenario Dimensions
Each scenario represents a unique combination of assumptions across three key
dimensions: (1) electricity demand growth, (2) the mix of distributed solar PV vs.
community-scale solar PV, and (3) the strategy for managing excess on-island
generation.

5.1.1 Demand Growth
Each scenario includes one of two distinct policy pathways affecting the growth in the
demand for electricity:

  • Status quo. This pathway represents a future in which the community and its utility
 do not seek to overcome existing barriers to the on-island adoption of energy
 efficiency and electrification technologies. As a result, the annual adoption rates in
 2040 are consistent with historical data on technology adoption on Block Island
 from 2020 to 2023. These adoption rates are modest: two heat pump water heaters
 installs per year, eight air-source heat pump installs per year, five building
 envelope measure installs per year, and one full-home electrification install per
 year.
  • Established goals. This pathway represents a future driven by aggressive policy
 action that deliberately seeks to overcome existing barriers to adoption. The
 adoption rates for residential electrification technologies are synthesized from
 ambitious state and regional targets. As discussed earlier, the development of
 these rates was informed by high-level policy documents, including the Heating
 Sector Transformation in Rhode Island report and ISO New England's 2024
 Heating Electrification Forecast, which provide high-level projections of heat pump,
 water heating, and build efficiency upgrade rates necessary to meet state energy
 goals through 2050 (ISO New England Load Forecasting Committee 2024; Murphy
 and Weiss 2020). These rates are scaled back to estimate a 2040 adoption level.
 The resulting annual adoption rates are significantly higher than in the status quo
 scenario: 49 water heaters per year, 23 heat pumps per year, 17 building envelope
 measures per year, and 2 full-home electrifications per year. The adoption rate for
 this last technology in the established goals scenario is not based on direct
 historical data but is instead an implied metric derived from the adoption rates of
 the other measures. As a result, the adoption rate of full-home electrification
 systems is a particularly uncertain variable.
 The pool of eligible customers is consistent across both scenarios.

5.1.2 Distribution of Distributed Photovoltaic Adoption
Although all scenarios assume the adoption of sufficient on-island generation resources
to meet the projected maximum summer load in 2040, they differed in the mix between
distributed and community-scale solar. The scenarios model three levels of distributed
solar adoption, driven by new rooftop PV installations under BIUD’s BASA tariff.
Community solar capacity was then sized to meet the remainder of the summer peak
demand for electricity after considering electricity production from existing distributed
solar PV resources under NEM and BASA, in addition to the biofuel facility, as follows:

  • Low distributed solar adoption. Distributed solar meets 30% of summer peak
 demand.
  • Medium distributed solar adoption. Distributed solar meets 50% of summer peak
 demand.
  • High distributed solar adoption. Distributed solar meets 90% of summer peak
 demand.
5.1.3 Handling Excess Generation
The scenarios differ in their strategy for managing excess solar generation, given the
submarine cable’s back feed restrictions. These strategies are:

  • Curtailment (even-numbered scenarios). In these scenarios, any excess on-
 island solar generation is curtailed.
  • Battery storage (odd-numbered scenarios). In these scenarios, utility-owned
 battery resources are installed on the island to absorb and later reinject on-island
 excess solar generation onto BIUD’s electric grid.

6.2 Key Decision Points and Trade-Offs
6.2.1 Curtailment vs. Batteries
Because of the unique nature of the Block Island electricity grid, the community can
determine if it wants to promote utility (or third-party) investment in battery storage
resources or manage excess on-island electricity production through utility-controlled
curtailment in 2040. As shown in Figure 15, utility investment in battery storage is
predicted to increase costs to ratepayers in every modeled scenario as a result of
curtailment. However, those battery storage devices also increase the Block Island
community’s level of self-adequacy to off-island electricity disruptions (see Figure 12
and Figure 13). Such outages have their own costs that would be directly incurred by

 Block Island’s Energy Roadmap to 2040 35

customers. However, utility-controlled battery storage devices could mitigate some (or
all) of those costs to the collective benefit of the community. Alternatively, the
community and/or the utility could promote individual customer adoption of battery
energy storage systems. Although this approach would achieve greater personal
resilience to electricity disruptions, any BIUD programs that seek to induce customers to
use those battery systems in ways that directly and comprehensively support the
electric grid may not be as effective and efficient as utility-controlled battery energy
storage systems.

6.2.2 Community vs. Rooftop Solar Resources
Another key choice for the community of Block Island will be to determine how much to
promote the development of rooftop solar vs. community solar resources by 2040. As
shown in Figure 11, the latter has a real utility-incurred cost when curtailment is required
because of the take-or-pay terms in a typical PPA. This conflicts with the indirect cost of
curtailment associated with rooftop solar, in which the BIUD’s contractual arrangement
with all new solar installations requires the utility to only pay for exported electricity, not
for curtailed electricity. In addition, the utility typically has more control over the
development of community solar resources than rooftop solar. A utility usually issues an
RFP for community solar resources that identifies the preferred nameplate capacity and
location (at a minimum). In contrast, individual customers make investment decisions on
their own inclusive of a variety of factors that includes an economic assessment based
on the utility’s export compensation program.

6.2.3 Status Quo vs. Established Goals Electricity Demand Levels
A final key decision for the community of Block Island will be the degree to which it
wants to address barriers that have inhibited, or will limit, the adoption of various energy
efficiency and electrification technologies that would allow it to approach the established
goals demand forecast in 2040. Achieving all established goals will require up-front
BIUD expenditures to cover program administration costs as well as technology
adoption incentives in the years between now and then. However, according to
conversations with BIUD leadership, board members, and community members, past
efforts to promote adoption of these technologies have not been as successful as
anticipated. This outcome suggests that the community and BIUD may need to pursue
more deliberate and concerted efforts to overcome the nonfinancial barriers (e.g.,
limited workforce, principal-agent problems 14) that have likely kept adoption rates low. If
such efforts to overcome these obstacles are ultimately successful, then BIUD’s supply
rate component under the established goals set of scenarios are predicted to be
systematically lower than those under the status quo (see Figure 16). Although not
modeled, BIUD’s other volumetric energy rate components would likely also be lower in
2040, as the utility’s embedded (i.e., fixed or sunk) costs would be spread over a larger
electricity sales base.

6.2.4 Future of the Submarine Cable
The introduction of the submarine cable that interconnected Block Island to the broader

years remain on the PPA between the offshore wind farm and its offtakers. This

across the cable even if enough on-island resources exist to meet maximum demand in

7 Conclusions
Based on the analysis contained in this report, the technical team identified four key
takeaways for the community of Block Island. The following takeaways align with the
broad areas of interest conveyed during initial conversations with both BIUD and the
island’s year-round residents during public meetings:

 1. Sufficiency. Block Island has more than enough developable land and rooftops
 for solar resources to meet 100% of the island’s peak demand for electricity in
 2040. As a result, all modeled scenarios allow the community to meet its goal of
 supplying 100% of its electricity annual energy needs with a portfolio of on-island
 biofuel and solar resources and off-island renewable energy resources under
 purchased-power agreements, per stated community priorities.

 2. Self-reliance. Because of the variable nature and diurnal/seasonal patterns of
 solar electricity production that does not perfectly align with the timing and level
 of demand for electricity on Block Island, there is no modeled scenario that will
 allow the island to be 100% self-reliant on an annual electricity basis. Instead,
 on-island electricity resources could meet at most 45% of the island’s annual
 electricity needs, even if BIUD leveraged sufficient battery storage devices to
 ensure there were no on-island solar production curtailments.

 3. Self-adequacy. Because of the lack of alignment between on-island electricity
 production and demand coupled with the island’s inability to export excess-
 produced electricity to the mainland, there is no modeled scenario that will allow
 Block Island to be 100% resilient. Instead, on-island electricity resources are
 predicted to fully meet Block Island’s demand for electricity in at most 30% of the
 hours in 2040, even if BIUD leveraged sufficient battery storage devices to
 ensure there were no on-island solar production curtailments.

 4. Affordability. BIUD’s current electric supply costs are a little over 6.1 ¢/kWh.
 Achieving the established goals demand forecast and relying on curtailment to
 manage off-island export constraints creates the lowest modeled supply rate
 level (7.0 ¢/kWh); whereas achieving the status quo goal demand levels and
 leveraging batteries to manage excess on-island electricity production results in
 the highest modeled supply rate levels (10.1 ¢/kWh). Consequently, modeled
 supply costs across all scenarios in 2040 are 14%–65% higher than current
 supply costs, in real terms. Not accounted for in these calculations are any
 outage costs avoided because of the existence of utility-controlled battery
 storage systems for resiliency.

To achieve these goals, the Block Island community and its cooperative utility would
need to be deliberate in their efforts to promote on-island solar development as well as
achieve highly aggressive on-island energy efficiency and electrification goals. In the
former case, BIUD and its stakeholders could consider issuing requests for proposals
for community solar development, ideally in locations identified in this analysis, as its
deployment is more controllable than rooftop solar photovoltaic adoption. However,

 Block Island’s Energy Roadmap to 2040 38

without the procurement and management of utility-controlled battery storage devices,
BIUD will likely need to curtail some community solar energy output to ensure grid
stability. In this case, BIUD would still need to pay for the output (total solar integrated
onto the grid AND the total curtailed) because of typical contractual arrangements.
Achieving the established goals demand forecast would require addressing long-
standing barriers to the adoption of energy efficiency and electrification measures.

Appendix B. Block Island State Airport Special Use
Case
The Block Island State Airport (Federal Aviation Administration [FAA] designation: BID)
covers an area of 136 acres (55 hectares; 0.55 square kilometers) at an elevation of
108 feet (ft; 33 meters [m]) above mean sea level. It has one runway designated 10/28
with an asphalt surface measuring 2,502 by 100 ft (763 by 30 m). For the 12-month
period ending February 29, 2020, the airport had 17,014 aircraft operations, an average
of 47 per day (Federal Aviation Administration 2024).

The technical team identified the airport as a potentially viable site for a community
solar array given its substantial acreage, respective to total island acreage, and high
level of solar irradiation (i.e., limited to no shade). However, the technical team needed
to determine the feasibility of BID as a community-scale solar site. To that end, the
technical team first conducted a desktop review of BID using Google Earth to determine
possible siting locations within the airport perimeter, and take initial measurements of
the distances from those potential sites to areas commonly used by aircraft (e.g.,
runway, taxiway, apron), with the expectation that proximity to areas commonly used by
aircraft would be the limiting factor for community-scale site feasibility at BID. Based on
this initial analysis, the technical team identified three plots on the BID parcel conducive
to possible community-scale photovoltaic (PV) siting given their respective areas,
elevations, and apparent nonobstructive locations (See Figure B-1):

 • Site 1: Open field adjacent to the runway

 • Site 2: Open field adjacent to the aircraft taxiway and parking area

 • Site 3: Car overflow parking area.

 Block Island’s Energy Roadmap to 2040 50

 Figure B-1. Aerial view of Block Island State Airport, New Shoreham, Rhode Island, with the viable

The technical team then began a desktop search of any installed PV arrays on active
general aviation (GA) public use airports in the United States. Most of the airports found
by the technical team that had solar PV within the airport perimeter had large
geographic footprints, in sharp contrast to BID, and had the PV arrays located far away
from aircraft areas, which would be challenging given the small size and configuration of
the airport. However, the technical team was able to identify three airports that
appeared comparable to BID in that their solar arrays were in close proximity to areas
heavily used by aircraft (see Figure B-2 through Figure B-4). For example, PV arrays
were in the intermittent space between the taxiway and runway; or were located near
hard-surface areas regularly used by aircraft. Based on the assessment of these three
airports, the technical team’s initial determination of viable plots on the BID parcel
seemed reasonable.

The technical team next reviewed current FAA guidelines (Barrett et al. 2018) to
understand the siting restrictions, including setbacks from the runway, tarmac, and any
other airport sites and equipment designated as unsuitable for PV installation. The FAA
Solar Airport Guide indicated sufficient flexibility in siting PV at BID, which further
supported the reasonableness of the initially identified three plots.

The technical team then contacted the Obstruction Evaluation Group at the FAA who
confirmed that PV installations on active airports are permitted on a case-by-case basis.
In administering Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulation, the prime objectives of the
FAA are to promote: 1) air safety and 2) the efficient use of the navigable airspace
(Code of Federal Regulation 2016). To accomplish this mission, aeronautical studies
are conducted based on information provided by proponents on FAA Form 7460-1,
Notice of Proposed Construction or Alteration (Obstruction Evaluation Group 2025).

With a robust understanding of the relatively limited and flexible federal regulations and
guidance for locating a solar PV array at an active GA public-use airport, the technical
team reviewed the relevant state regulations to ensure no state restrictions existed that

 Block Island’s Energy Roadmap to 2040 54

would invalidate the viability of the site or of the three initially identified plots on the
parcel conducive for siting community-scale solar PV (Rhode Island Department of
State 2022). No hindrance or exemption of airports were found.

Next, the technical team contacted the BID owner/operator, the Rhode Island Airport
Corporation (RIAC), for further evaluation. RIAC is a semiautonomous subsidiary of the
Rhode Island Commerce Corporation, a quasi-public agency, and is responsible for
operation and maintenance of all GA airports in the state.

The RIAC’s initial assessment was that two of three locations identified by the technical
team would likely violate federal regulations that promoted air safety and the efficient
use of navigable airspace. Specifically, Site 2 is the aircraft overflow parking area, used
by aircraft during times when the airport is heavily trafficked and/or experiencing an
emergency. As such, it had to be left accessible. Additionally, Site 1 has aircraft
navigation aids (NAVAIDs) sited within the identified area. These electromagnetic
spectrum transceivers provide critical support to pilots trying to navigate their aircraft in
the dark or otherwise poor visibility conditions. NAVAIDs are generally placed in
unobstructed areas to ensure their broadcast signal is strong and accessible from any
direction. RIAC believed an extensive special analysis for electromagnetic interference
would be required to support approval to locate a community-scale solar PV array on
Site 1.

The technical team then conducted a further examination of the FAA’s Notice Criteria
Tool for construction projects on airport property that included example language
regarding NAVAID interference as a criteria for denial “...the structure will be in an
instrument approach area and might exceed part 77 Subpart C, your proposed structure
will be in proximity to a navigation facility and may impact the assurance of navigation
signal reception” (Obstruction Evaluation Group 2025).

Accordingly, the technical team decided to remove Site 1 and Site 2 from the list of
viable plots on the property. Site 3, the car overflow parking area, appeared to be the
only location that would neither interfere with air safety nor use of navigable airspace,
and would not require extensive and costly additional analyses to support approval by
state and federal regulators. A geospatial investigation of this area determined the total
viable acreage for community-scale solar to be 2.7 acres, and this was added to the
total acreage available on Block Island.

Appendix C. Wetlands and Buffer Zones
The Rhode Island Department of Environmental Management (RI DEM) filed new
administrative rules for the administration and enforcement of the Freshwater Wetlands
Act that took effect July 1, 2022 (Rules and Regulations Governing the Administration
and Enforcement of the Freshwater Wetlands Act 2022). RI DEM (or Coastal Resources
Management Council along the coast) has jurisdiction over projects or activities located
in freshwater wetlands or within a buffer zone around them (Coastal Resources
Management Council and State of Rhode Island Department of Environmental
Management 2008). Buffer sizes for various freshwater wetland resources (e.g., rivers,
streams, lakes, ponds, and wetlands) in various regions are described in the DEM fact
sheet (State of Rhode Island Department of Environmental Management 2022). New
Shoreham has land in River Region 1 and in drinking water supply watersheds
(Environmental Data Center 2024b). All nonexempt projects, including ground-mounted
solar arrays, planned within wetlands or their buffer zone, also known as a jurisdictional
area, must go through the freshwater wetlands permitting process during which the level
of alterations to a freshwater wetland are determined. If the project is determined to
represent significant alterations to a freshwater wetland resource, the landowner must
file an “application to alter a freshwater wetland” (Office of Water Resources 2008). As
of July 2024, 132 solar projects have engaged with the freshwater wetlands permitting
process, including projects representing no, insignificant, and insignificant alterations
that have been approved (State of Rhode Island Department of Environmental
Management 2025). 17

There is no authoritative map for the presence of wetlands used by RI DEM in the
regulatory process. Requests to determine the presence of jurisdictional areas and
verify freshwater wetland edges with a field survey are made at the early stages of the
permitting process. RI DEM does, however, suggest the use of the U.S. Fish and
Wildlife Service (USFWS) National Wetland Inventory (NWI), in addition to a few other
state resources for early-stage planning purposes (State of Rhode Island Department of
Environmental Management 2008; U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 2025b). When
compared with Spring 2023 Rhode Island orthoimagery on Block Island, the USFWS
NWI was determined to be the most accurate and identify the smallest wetlands
(Environmental Data Center 2023).

Wetlands in the USFWS NWI are categorized in accordance with a national
classification system, which are more detailed and specific than the wetland categories
used under the new Rhode Island rules and regulations (Cowardin et al. 1979; Federal
Geographic Data Committee 2019). Table B-1 represents how USFWS NWI wetlands
on Block Island were reclassified according to the Rhode Island freshwater wetlands
categories. No wetland features were recategorized as bogs, evergreen forested
swamps, swamps with rhododendron, or vernal pools. Rhode Island DEM’s rules also
stipulate that buffers for “wetland complexes where 1 or more differing wetland types or
subtypes are present within 50 feet inward of a wetland edge” should add 25 feet to the
