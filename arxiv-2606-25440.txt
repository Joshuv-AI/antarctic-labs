Dragon curves in Littlewood roots
Marcus Michelen, Oren Yakir

Abstract
. A Littlewood polynomial is a polynomial whose coefficients lie in { − 1 , + 1 } \{-1,+1\} . While the majority of roots of a Littlewood polynomial of large degree are near the unit circle, numerical experiments suggest that when plotting the roots of all Littlewood polynomials of a given large degree, striking fractal structures appear away from the unit circle. These fractals resemble the attractor of a certain iterated function system and are known as dragon curves . In this note, we provide a rigorous explanation of this phenomenon, along with an analysis of a random variant, saying that such fractal behavior is typical.

A
Littlewood polynomial
is a polynomial with coefficients in
\{-1,+1\}
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{n}
the set of all Littlewood polynomials of degree
n\geq 1
. Let
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,|z|<1\}
denote the open unit disk. In this paper, we study the (microscopic) structure of the set of all possible roots of Littlewood polynomials of large degree in the disk, namely

(1)

\mathcal{Z}_{n}=\Big\{\alpha\in\mathbb{D}\,:\,f(\alpha)=0\ \text{for some }f\in\mathcal{L}_{n}\Big\}\,,

as
n\to\infty
. We note that whenever
\alpha
is a root of the polynomial
f(z)\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
, then
\alpha^{-1}
is a root of the polynomial
z^{n}f(z^{-1})\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
. Hence, for our purposes, we lose no generality by restricting our attention to roots lying strictly within the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
.

Similarly, a
Littlewood series
is a Taylor series centered at
z=0
with coefficients in
\{-1,+1\}
, and we denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
the set of all Littlewood series. Any Littlewood series defines an analytic function in
\mathbb{D}
with unit radius of convergence. Let

\mathcal{Z}_{\infty}=\Big\{\alpha\in\mathbb{D}\,:\,f(\alpha)=0\ \text{for some }f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\Big\}\,.

Noting that for any
f\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
we have
f(z)(1-z^{n+1})^{-1}\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
shows that
\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{Z}_{n}\subset\mathcal{Z}_{\infty}
and that this inclusion is strict

It is not hard to check that

meaning that these holes get filled up as

A striking, much less understood feature in Figure

[
2

is bounded below in terms of

is approximately a deterministic fractal (when rescaled and rotated) known as a
dragon curve

|P_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|
is in fact generic by showing that such a lower bound occurs asymptotically almost-surely for all roots bounded away from the unit circle for random Littlewood power series. We now properly define the limiting shapes known as dragon curves.

Dragon curves

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}
we consider the pair of functions

\phi_{+}(w)=1+\alpha w\,,\qquad\phi_{-}(w)=-1+\alpha w\,.

Both maps
\phi_{\pm}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
are contractions since
|\alpha|<1
,
and by a classical theorem
[
6
, Theorem 2.1.1]
, there exists a unique non-empty compact set
D_{\alpha}\subset\mathbb{C}
such that

D_{\alpha}=\phi_{+}(D_{\alpha})\,\cup\,\phi_{-}(D_{\alpha})\,.

This attractor
D_{\alpha}
is known as the
dragon curve
associated with the point
\alpha
. Some pictures of dragon curves are given in Figure
2
below. In one sense, the connection between dragon curves and Littlewood series is pretty direct, as one can easily verify that

(2)

D_{\alpha}=\big\{f(\alpha)\,:\,f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\big\}\,.

We note that a simple self-similarity argument
[
6
, Section 2.2]
shows that the Hausdorff dimension of
D_{\alpha}
is always bounded above by
\log(2)/\log(1/|\alpha|)
, which in particular implies that
D_{\alpha}
has an empty interior for all
|\alpha|<2^{-1/2}
. On the other hand, Shmerkin and Solomyak
[
19
]
proved, among other things, that for almost every point in the annulus
\{2^{-1/2}<|\alpha|<1\}
the interior of
D_{\alpha}
is non-empty.

Main deterministic theorem

Given a polynomial
P_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
we will consider all possible ways to extend
P_{n}
to a full Littlewood series. This collection is parametrized by

via

G_{P_{n},f}(z)=P_{n}(z)+z^{n+1}f(z)\,.

We define the set of all possible extension roots as

(3)

R(P_{n})=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{D}\,:G_{P_{n},f}(z)=0\ \text{for some }\,f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\Big\}\,.

We say that
P_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
is the

th prefix

P_{n}
is the
n
th prefix of

P_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}P
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}

\{R(P_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}

\mathbb{D}
(with respect to set inclusion) so that

\big\{\alpha\in\mathbb{D}\,:\,P(\alpha)=0\big\}=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}R(P_{n})\,.

Definition 0
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{D}
be a compact set, and let
\kappa>0
. A Littlewood series

is said to be
\kappa

(with respect to

\alpha\in K

|P^{\prime}(\alpha)|\geq\kappa

For
n\geq 1
we denote the magnifying affine transformation around

by

(5)

T_{n,\xi}(z)=\frac{z-\xi}{\xi^{n+1}}\,.

Finally, recall that the Hausdorff distance between two non-empty subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{C}
is given by

(6)

d_{\mathrm{H}}(A,B)=\max\bigg\{\sup_{a\in A}\,\text{dist}\big(a,B\big)\,,\,\sup_{b\in B}\,\text{dist}\big(b,A\big)\bigg\}\,.

Here, as always, for a point
z\in\mathbb{C}
and a non-empty
A\subset\mathbb{C}
we set

\displaystyle\text{dist}(z,A)=\inf_{a\in A}|z-a|\,.

We are ready to state our main deterministic result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{D}
be a compact set and let
\kappa>0
, then there exists
r_{0}>0
so that the following holds. Let
P\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
be a
\kappa
-good Littlewood series and let
P_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
be its
n
th prefix. Suppose
\alpha\in K
so that
P(\alpha)=0
. Then there exists
\alpha_{n}\in K
such that
P_{n}(\alpha_{n})=0
and
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha
.
If we define

R_{n,{\tt loc}}=R(P_{n})\cap\mathbb{D}(\alpha,r_{0})

where
R(P_{n})
is given by (
3

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{H}}\Big(T_{n,\alpha_{n}}\big(R_{n,{\tt loc}}\big)\,,\,\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\Big)=0

where
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}=P^{\prime}(\alpha)^{-1}D_{\alpha}
, with
D_{\alpha}
the dragon curve at
\alpha
given by (
2
).

We note that our proof of Theorem

Remark

Our proof can be easily extended to show a version that does not explicitly require information about the limiting power series

The random model

There is a natural (uniform) probability measure one can place on
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
which is obtained by randomizing the coefficients. Indeed, let
(\varepsilon_{k})
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables such that

\mathbb{P}\big(\varepsilon_{k}=1\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\varepsilon_{k}=-1\big)=\frac{1}{2}\,.

We define the random Littlewood series
F
by

F(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}z^{k}\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\,.

Theorem 2
.

Almost surely the random Littlewood series
F
has no double zeros. In particular, almost surely for all compact
K\subset\mathbb{D}
we have that
F
is
\kappa
-good for all sufficiently small
\kappa>0
.

The result above shows that the conclusion of Theorem
1
can be applied to any (almost sure) realization of the random Taylor series
F
. We also note that Theorem
2
is a direct consequence of a more general theorem proved in our previous work
[
17
, Theorem 1.3]
which proves an analogous theorem for more general choices of random coefficients. For ease of readability, we provide a self-contained (and simplified) proof of Theorem
2
in Section
2

To take a step further and discuss the global scaling limit for all root extensions in the disk
\mathbb{D}
—as opposed to just looking at the small disk of radius
r_{0}
around a fixed root, as done in Theorem
1
—we need to introduce a coarser topology on closed sets than the one given by the Hausdorff metric.

The Fell topology

Denote by
\mathbf{CL}(\mathbb{C})
the collection of all non-empty closed subsets of
\mathbb{C}
. When discussing global scaling limits for (random) root extensions (see Theorem
3
below), we will require a notion of convergence for sequences in
\mathbf{CL}(\mathbb{C})
. One natural candidate would be the Hausdorff metric topology (
6
) which we used in Theorem
1
, but since the scaled set of roots can (and will) become unbounded as
n\to\infty
, we will require a more flexible notion of convergence.

For a sequence
\{A_{n}\}\subset\mathbf{CL}(\mathbb{C})
, we define the lower (inferior) and upper (superior) limits as

\displaystyle\text{Li}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,\text{dist}(z,A_{n})=0\Big\}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\text{Ls}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,\text{dist}(z,A_{n})=0\Big\}\,.

It is straightforward to check that
\text{Li}(A_{n})\subset\text{Ls}(A_{n})
. If these sets coincide, their common value is defined as the
Kuratowski limit
of
\{A_{n}\}
, and we shall denote this limit by

(8)

\operatorname*{Lim}_{n\to\infty}A_{n}=\text{Li}(A_{n})=\text{Ls}(A_{n})\,.

This convergence gives the topology on
\mathbf{CL}(\mathbb{C})
, known as the
Fell topology
, see
[
5
, Chapter 5]
. The Fell topology is coarser than the standard Hausdorff metric topology, as it lets elements of
A_{n}
escape to infinity without preventing convergence. For example, the sequence of sets
A_{n}=\{0,n\}
converges in the Kuratowski sense to
\{0\}
, although
d_{\mathrm{H}}(\{0\},\{0,n\})=n
.
When restricting to closed subsets contained (uniformly) inside a fixed compact domain in the plane, the Fell topology and the Hausdorff topology coincide.

Point process convergence

For a sequence of random signs
(\varepsilon_{k})
we consider

(9)

F(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}z^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad F_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}z^{k}\,,

as random elements of
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, respectively. For
f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
we denote by

G_{n,f}(z)=F_{n}(z)+z^{n+1}f(z)\,.

As before, the collection
\{G_{n,f}\}
parametrizes all possible extensions of the random polynomial
F_{n}
to the full Littlewood series. We now consider the random closed set of all possible roots

(10)

R_{n}=R(F_{n})=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{D}\,:\,G_{n,f}(z)=0\ \text{for some}\ f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\Big\}\,.

Recall that
T_{n,\alpha}(z)=\alpha^{-n-1}(z-\alpha)
. The next result shows that clusters of roots in
\mathcal{Z}_{\infty}
converge, in a probabilistic sense, to randomly scaled and rotated dragon curves. This gives a rigorous explanation as to why the dragon curves are visually present in Figure
1
.

Theorem 3
.

For
F_{n},F
given by (
9
), we have that

\Big\{\big(\alpha,T_{n,\alpha}(R_{n})\big)\,:\,\alpha\in\mathcal{Z}(F_{n})\Big\}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\Big\{\big(\alpha,\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\big)\,:\,\alpha\in\mathcal{Z}(F)\Big\}

in distribution, with respect to the vague topology on (tagged) point processes on
\mathbb{D}\times\textbf{CL}(\mathbb{C})
.

In words, Theorem
3
is saying that when zooming in around a typical point in Figure
1
, the local roots nearby arrange approximately along a scaled dragon curve centered at this point. Moreover, this description becomes increasingly accurate as the degree gets large. Additionally, note that Theorem
3
(and its deterministic counterpart Theorem
1
) apply even when
|\alpha|
is close to
1
. One can understand these results as saying that the dragon curves still appear in this case, there are simply sufficiently many roots—and the dragons are full-dimensional—that they appear as a smearing near the unit circle in Figure
1
rather than visually articulated fractals.

In the course of the proof of Theorem
3
, we establish a slightly stronger, almost sure, form of the convergence; see Section
3
for details. Since convergence in distribution is the more standard formulation in the point process literature, we have chosen to state the theorem in this form.

1.
Local convergence to dragon curves

Throughout this section, we will denote by
P_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
a Littlewood polynomial and by
P\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
a series such that
P_{n}
is always the
n
th prefix of
P
;
as such,
P_{n}\to P
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
as
n\to\infty
. A simple application of Hurwitz’s theorem (see e.g.
[
1
, Chapter 5]
) shows that convergence on compact sets is sufficient to deduce convergence of roots:

Fact 4
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{D}
be a compact set. Then for all
n
large enough,
P
and
P_{n}
have the same number of roots in
K
provided
P
has no roots on
\partial K
. Furthermore, if
\beta\in K
is a root of
P
with multiplicity
k\geq 1
, then for all
\varepsilon>0
small enough there exists
n
large enough so that
P_{n}
has exactly
k
roots in
\mathbb{D}(\beta,\varepsilon)
.

The next lemma (based on Rouché’s theorem) is the first main step towards the proof of Theorem
1
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\kappa>0
and
K\subset\mathbb{D}
compact. Suppose that
\alpha\in K
is such that
P(\alpha)=0
and
|P^{\prime}(\alpha)|\geq\kappa
. Then there exists
r_{0}>0
so that for all sufficiently large
n
and for all
f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
the function

G_{P_{n},f}(z)=P_{n}(z)+z^{n+1}f(z)

has a unique root in
\mathbb{D}(\alpha,r_{0})
. Furthermore, denoting this root by
\beta_{n,f}
, we have that

(11)

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}|\alpha-\beta_{n,f}|\leq\big(|\alpha|e^{1-|\alpha|}\big)^{n+1}\,.

Proof.

We will start the proof by showing (
11
), that is, that there exists a root of
G_{n,f}
in

\mathbb{D}\Big(\alpha,\big(|\alpha|e^{1-|\alpha|}\big)^{n+1}\Big)

for all
n
large enough. Indeed, let
r_{0}=C^{-1}\kappa
for some sufficiently large
C>0
. For any
\rho\in(0,r_{0})
and for any
z=\alpha+\eta
with
|\eta|=\rho
we have that

(12)

|P(z)|\geq\rho|P^{\prime}(\alpha)|-\rho^{2}\max_{z:\mathrm{dist}(z,K)\leq\rho}|P^{\prime\prime}(z)|\geq\rho\kappa-C^{\prime}\rho^{2}\geq\frac{\rho\kappa}{2}\,.

We also have that

\max_{|z-\alpha|=\rho}\big|G_{P_{n},f}(z)-P(z)\big|\leq\max_{|z-\alpha|=\rho}|z|^{n+1}|f(z)|\leq\big(|\alpha|e^{(|\alpha|-1)/2}\big)^{n+1}

for all
n
large enough. Combining this with (
12
) applied with
\rho=\big(|\alpha|e^{(|\alpha|-1)}\big)^{n+1}
we get that for all
n
large

\max_{|z-\alpha|=\rho}\big|G_{P_{n},f}(z)-P(z)\big|\leq\frac{\kappa}{2}\big(|\alpha|e^{(|\alpha|-1)}\big)^{n+1}\leq\min_{|z-\alpha|=\rho}|P(z)|\,.

Hence, by Rouché’s theorem,
P
and
G_{P_{n},f}
have the same number of roots in
\mathbb{D}\big(\alpha,\big(|\alpha|e^{1-|\alpha|}\big)^{n+1}\big)
. By assumption we have that
P(\alpha)=0
, so in particular there is a root
\beta_{n,f}
of
G_{P_{n},f}
which satisfies (
11
). To deduce uniqueness, we note that (
12
) also shows that
P
has a unique root in
\mathbb{D}(\alpha,r_{0})
(precisely at
\alpha
), and we observe that

\max_{|z-\alpha|=r_{0}}\big|G_{P_{n},f}(z)-P(z)\big|\leq C\big(|\alpha|+r_{0}\big)^{n+1}\leq\frac{\kappa r_{0}}{2}\leq\min_{|z-\alpha|=r_{0}}|P(z)|\,,

so another application of Rouché’s theorem shows that
P
and
G_{P_{n},f}
have the same number of roots in
\mathbb{D}(\alpha,r_{0})
, which means that
G_{P_{n},f}
has a unique root there.
∎

Before we turn to prove Theorem
1
, we record a simple claim that will be useful throughout.

Claim 6
.

Let
\alpha\in K
be such that
P(\alpha)=0
and
|P^{\prime}(\alpha)|\geq\kappa
. Then for all
n
large there exists
\alpha_{n}
with
P_{n}(\alpha_{n})=0
such that

|\alpha_{n}-\alpha|\leq C\kappa^{-1}|\alpha|^{n+1}

where
C=C(K)>0
depends only on the compact set
K\subset\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

Existence of a root
\alpha_{n}
of
P_{n}
in a small enough disk around
\alpha
is guaranteed in view of Fact
4
. To estimate
|\alpha-\alpha_{n}|
, we Taylor expand and see that

|P_{n}(\alpha)|\geq\big|P_{n}(\alpha_{n})+(\alpha-\alpha_{n})P_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha_{n})\big|-\max_{z\in K}|P_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)||\alpha-\alpha_{n}|^{2}\geq\frac{\kappa}{2}\,|\alpha-\alpha_{n}|

for all
n
large enough. On the other hand, we also have that

|P_{n}(\alpha)|=|P_{n}(\alpha)-P(\alpha)|\leq|\alpha|^{n+1}\sum_{k\geq 0}|\alpha|^{k}\leq C\,|\alpha|^{n+1}\,,

and the claim follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Recall that
R_{n,{\tt loc}}=R(P_{n})\cap\mathbb{D}(\alpha,r_{0})
are the roots of all possible extensions
\{G_{P_{n},f}\}_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}
which lie in a small neighborhood of
\alpha
. By Lemma
5
, this set consists precisely of the roots
\beta_{n,f}
, which are the (unique) roots of
G_{P_{n},f}
which satisfy (
11
).
We will show that

(13)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}\Big|T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(\beta_{n,f})+\frac{f(\alpha)}{P^{\prime}(\alpha)}\Big|=0

which in turn will imply that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{H}}\big(T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(R_{n,{\tt loc}}),\,\mathcal{D_{\alpha}}\big)=0
. Indeed, letting

\varepsilon_{n}=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}\Big|T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(\beta_{n,f})+\frac{f(\alpha)}{P^{\prime}(\alpha)}\Big|\,,

we get that for an arbitrary point
P^{\prime}(\alpha)^{-1}f(\alpha)\in\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
there exists the root
\beta_{n,-f}\in R_{n,{\tt loc}}
so that

\text{dist}\big(P^{\prime}(\alpha)^{-1}f(\alpha),T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(R_{n,\tt loc})\big)\leq\big|P^{\prime}(\alpha)^{-1}f(\alpha)-T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(\beta_{n,-f})\big|\leq\varepsilon_{n}\,.

Furthermore, since any point in
R_{n,\tt loc}
is a root
\beta_{n,f}
for some
f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
, similar reasoning shows that

\text{dist}\big(T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(\beta_{n,f}),\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\big)\leq\varepsilon_{n}\,.

Altogether, we get
d_{\mathrm{H}}(T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(R_{n,{\tt loc}}),\mathcal{D}_{\alpha})\leq\varepsilon_{n}
, so the theorem will follow once (
13
) is established.

For each
f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
, the relation
G_{P_{n},f}(\beta_{n,f})=0
implies that

(14)

P_{n}(\beta_{n,f})=-\beta_{n,f}^{n+1}f(\beta_{n,f})\,.

By (
11
) and writing
\gamma=(|\alpha|e^{1-|\alpha|})^{n+1}
we have that

\beta_{n,f}^{n+1}=\alpha^{n+1}\big(1+O(n\gamma/|\alpha|)\big)=\alpha^{n+1}(1+o(1))

as
n\to\infty
, where the
o(1)
term does not depend on
f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
. Furthermore, we have that

|f(\beta_{n,f})-f(\alpha)|\leq|\alpha-\beta_{n,f}|\cdot\max_{z\in K}|f^{\prime}(z)|\leq C_{K}|\alpha-\beta_{n,f}|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0

uniformly in
f
. Therefore, as
n\to\infty
, we may rewrite the right-hand side of (
14
) as

(15)

\beta_{n,f}^{n+1}f(\beta_{n,f})=\alpha^{n+1}f(\alpha)+o(|\alpha|^{n+1})\,.

On the other hand, by Claim
6
the sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}
with
P_{n}(\alpha_{n})=0
must have
\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{D}\big(\alpha,C\kappa^{-1}|\alpha|^{n+1}\big)
. Taylor expanding the left-hand side of (
14
), we get that

\displaystyle P_{n}(\beta_{n,f})

\displaystyle=P_{n}(\alpha_{n})+(\beta_{n,f}-\alpha_{n})P_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha_{n})+O\big(|\beta_{n,f}-\alpha_{n}|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=(\beta_{n,f}-\alpha_{n})P_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha_{n})\big(1+o(1)\big)=(\beta_{n,f}-\alpha_{n})P^{\prime}(\alpha)\big(1+o(1)\big)\,,

where the
o(1)
term depends only on
K
and
\kappa
. Plugging back into (
14
) and rearranging gives

T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(\beta_{n,f})=-\frac{\beta_{n,f}-\alpha_{n}}{\alpha_{n}^{n+1}}=-\Big(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha_{n}}\Big)^{n+1}\frac{f(\alpha)}{P^{\prime}(\alpha)}+o(1)=-\frac{f(\alpha)}{P^{\prime}(\alpha)}+o(1)\,,

with the
o(1)
term being uniform in
f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
and in the last equality we used Claim
6
. This proves the limit (
13
), completing the proof.
∎

2.
Almost sure no double zeros

Recall that
F(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}z^{k}
with
(\varepsilon_{k})
an i.i.d. sequence of random signs. In this section we prove Theorem
2
, which states that almost surely
F
has no double roots in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
.

Fix some
r\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
, and recall that
r\mathbb{D}=\{|z|\leq r\}
. It is not hard to show (see Claim
8
below) that any power series with coefficients in
\{-1,+1\}
has at most
C(r)<\infty
roots (including multiplicities) in the disk
r\mathbb{D}
. For
j\geq 1
and
\gamma>0
we define the events

(16)

\mathcal{E}_{r,j}(\gamma)=\Big\{\exists z\in r\mathbb{D}\,:\,F(z)=F^{\prime}(z)=\ldots=F^{(j)}(z)=0\,,\ \text{and}\ |F^{(j+1)}(z)|>\gamma\Big\}\,.

Lemma 7
.

For all
r<1
,
j\geq 1
and
\gamma>0
we have
\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{r,j}(\gamma)\big)=0
.

Assuming Lemma
7
for a moment, it is quite simple to complete the proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Since the events
\mathcal{E}_{r,j}(\gamma)
are monotonically increasing as
\gamma\downarrow 0
, Lemma
7
implies

(17)

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{r,j}(0)\big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists z\in r\mathbb{D}\,:\,F(z)=F^{\prime}(z)=\ldots=F^{(j)}(z)=0\,,\ \text{and}\ |F^{(j+1)}(z)|>0\Big)=0\,.

The union bound shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists z\in r\mathbb{D}\,:\,F(z)=F^{\prime}(z)=0\Big)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{r,1}(0)\big)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists z\in r\mathbb{D}\,:\,F(z)=F^{\prime}(z)=F^{\prime\prime}(z)=0\Big)\,.

Iterating the above
C(r)
times, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists z\in r\mathbb{D}\,:\,F(z)=F^{\prime}(z)=0\Big)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{C(r)}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{r,j}(0)\big)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists z\in r\mathbb{D}\,:\,F(z)=\ldots=F^{(C(r)+1)}(z)=0\Big)\,.

Combining (
17
) and Claim
8
, we see that all terms on the right-hand side vanish, and hence

\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists z\in r\mathbb{D}\,:\,F(z)=F^{\prime}(z)=0\Big)=0\,.

Finally, by taking a countable union of radii with
r\uparrow 1
, we conclude that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists z\in\mathbb{D}\,:\,F(z)=F^{\prime}(z)=0\Big)=0

which is what we wanted to show.
∎

We devote the remaining portion of the section to proving Lemma
7
. For fixed
j\geq 1
we note that

(18)

F^{(j)}(z)=\sum_{k=j}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}(k)_{j}z^{k-j}\,,

where
(k)_{j}=j!\binom{k}{j}
is the falling factorial.

Claim 8
.

For all
j\geq 0
,
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup{\infty}
and every choice of signs
h_{k}\in\{-1,+1\}
the number of roots (including multiplicities) in
r\mathbb{D}
of

h(z)=\sum_{k=j}^{m}h_{k}(k)_{j}\,z^{k-j}

is at most
C(r,j)<\infty
.

Proof of Claim
8
.

Recall Jensen’s formula
[
1
, Chapter 5]
for analytic functions. For all
R<1
we have

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|h(Re^{i\theta})|\,\frac{{\rm d}\theta}{2\pi}-\log|h(0)|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|<R\\
h(\alpha)=0\end{subarray}}\log\Big(\frac{R}{|\alpha|}\Big)\,.

Applying the formula with
R=r+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-r)<1
while using the fact that
\log|h(0)|=\log|j!|\geq 0
gives

\displaystyle\#\big\{\alpha\in r\mathbb{D}\,:\,h(\alpha)=0\big\}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\log\Big(\frac{R}{r}\Big)\Big)^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|<r\\
h(\alpha)=0\end{subarray}}\log\Big(\frac{R}{|\alpha|}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\log\Big(\frac{R}{r}\Big)\Big)^{-1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|h(Re^{i\theta})|\,\frac{{\rm d}\theta}{2\pi}\leq\Big(\log\Big(\frac{R}{r}\Big)\Big)^{-1}\log\Big|\sum_{k=j}^{\infty}k^{j}R^{k-j}\Big|<\infty\,.\qed

For
m\geq 1
we set

P_{m}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\varepsilon_{k}z^{k}\,,\qquad T_{m}(z)=F(z)-P_{m}(z)\,.

The next lemma is the key step in our analysis. Roughly speaking, it says that if
F
has a double root at
z_{0}\in r\mathbb{D}
which is not a triple root, then for
m
large there will be a nearby critical point of
P_{m}
on which
F
is atypically small.

Lemma 9
.

Let
r\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
,
j\geq 1
and
\gamma>0
be fixed and let
m\geq m_{0}(j,r,\gamma)
be large enough. On the event
\mathcal{E}_{r,j}(\gamma)
there exists
w\in(e^{1-r}r)\mathbb{D}
such that

P_{m}^{(j)}(w)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad|F^{(j-1)}(w)|\leq Cm^{2j}\big(e^{2(1-r)}|w|\big)^{2m}\,,

for some
C=C(r,j,\gamma)>0
.

Proof.

Let
z_{0}\in r\mathbb{D}
be a point such that

F^{(j-1)}(z_{0})=F^{(j)}(z_{0})=0\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad|F^{(j+1)}(z_{0})|>\gamma\,.

On the event
\mathcal{E}_{r,j}(\gamma)
, we know that such a point exists. We would like to apply Rouché’s theorem to get a root of
P_{m}^{(j)}
in a disk
\mathbb{D}(z_{0},t)
for some suitably chosen small
t>0
. Indeed, since
|\varepsilon_{k}|\leq 1
for all
k\geq 0
, we have that

\max_{\ell\leq j+2}\max_{|z|\leq r}|F^{(\ell)}(z)|\leq\max_{\ell\leq j+2}\sum_{k=\ell}^{\infty}k^{\ell}r^{k-\ell}<\infty.

At a point
z=z_{0}+\eta
with
|\eta|=t
, a Taylor expansion shows that

(19)

|F^{(j)}(z)|\geq|F^{(j)}(z_{0})+F^{(j+1)}(z_{0})\,\eta|-t^{2}\max_{|z|\leq e^{r-1}r}|F^{(j+2)}(z)|>t\gamma-t^{2}C(r,j)\,.

On the other hand, by taking

t=\gamma^{-1}C^{\prime}m^{j}(e^{r-1}|z_{0}|)^{m}\,,

for sufficiently large
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(r,j)
we get that for all
z=z_{0}+\eta

|T_{m}^{(j)}(z)|\leq\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}k^{j}(|z_{0}|+t)^{k-j}\leq C(r,j)\,m^{j}(e^{r-1}|z_{0}|)^{m}<|F^{(j)}(z)|\,,

where the last inequality holds by (
19
) for all
m
sufficiently large. Rouché’s theorem now guarantees a root
w\in\mathbb{D}(z_{0},t)
of
P_{m}^{(j)}=F^{(j)}-T_{m}^{(j)}
, since
F^{(j)}(z_{0})=0
. We note that

\big||w|-|z_{0}|\big|\leq t\leq|z_{0}|e^{r-1}

for all
m
sufficiently large. Finally, since
F^{(j-1)}(z_{0})=F^{(j)}(z_{0})=0
, another Taylor approximation shows that

|F^{(j-1)}(w)|\leq t^{2}\max_{|z|\leq re^{r-1}}|F^{(j+1)}(z)|\leq C(r,j,\gamma)m^{2j}(e^{r-1}|z_{0}|)^{2m}\leq C(r,j,\gamma)m^{2j}(e^{2(r-1)}|w|)^{2m}\,,

as desired.
∎

Proof of Lemma
7
.

To prove the lemma, we will make use of the classical solution of Erdős
[
11
]
to the Littlewood-Offord problem.
For all
N\geq 1
and for any
c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|c_{k}|\geq 1
we have

(20)

\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{k}c_{k}-\zeta\Big|\leq 1\Big)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}\,,

for some absolute constant
C>1
. We note that even weaker bounds, such as the one originally obtained by Littlewood and Offord
[
16
]
(with an extra
\log(N)
factor on the right-hand side of (
20
)), would also suffice for our purpose.
From (
20
) we can deduce that for all
m\geq 1
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and for all
z\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
we have

(21)

\max_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|T_{m}^{(j)}(z)-\zeta\big|\leq\delta|z|^{m}\Big)\leq C\bigg(\frac{\log(1/|z|)}{\log(1/\delta)}\bigg)^{1/2}.

for some absolute constant
C>1
.
Indeed, let
N=\lfloor\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1/\delta)/\log(1/|z|)\rfloor
and observe that for
k\in\{m+1,\ldots,m+N\}
we have

\delta^{-1}(k)_{j}|z|^{k-j-m}\geq\delta^{-1}|z|^{N}\geq 1\,.

We get that

\displaystyle\max_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|T_{m}^{(j)}(z)-\zeta\big|\leq\delta|z|^{m}\Big)

\displaystyle=\max_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}(k)_{j}z^{k-j}-\zeta\Big|\leq\delta|z|^{m}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\sum_{k=m+1}^{m+N}\varepsilon_{k}\big(\delta|z|^{m}\big)^{-1}(k)_{j}z^{k-j}-\zeta\Big|\leq 1\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:small_ball_bound}}}{{\leq}}\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}

which, by our choice of
N
, proves (
21
).

Denote by
\mathbf{Z}_{m,j,r}
the (random) collection of roots of
P_{m}^{(j)}
inside
(e^{r-1}r)\mathbb{D}
. With this notation, Lemma
9
implies that, for all
m
large enough,

(22)

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{r,j}(\gamma)\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists w\in\mathbf{Z}_{m,j,r}\,:\,|P_{m}^{(j-1)}(w)+T_{m}^{(j-1)}(w)|\leq C(r,j)\,m^{2j}(e^{2(r-1)}|w|)^{2m}\Big)\,.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{m}=\sigma(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m})
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the first
m+1
random signs. Note that
P_{m}
(and, hence, also
\mathbf{Z}_{m,j,r}
) is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{m}
, while
T_{m}
is independent of it. Furthermore, by Claim
8
we know that
|\mathbf{Z}_{m,j,r}|\leq C(r,j)
for all realizations of
P_{m}
. Thus, we can apply the law of total expectation together with a union bound, and get that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists w\in\mathbf{Z}_{m,j,r}\,:\,|P_{m}^{(j-1)}(w)+T_{m}^{(j-1)}(w)|\leq C(r,j)\,m^{2j}(e^{2(r-1)}|w|)^{2m}\Big)\\
=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists w\in\mathbf{Z}_{m,j,r}\,:\,|P_{m}^{(j-1)}(w)+T_{m}^{(j-1)}(w)|\leq C(r,j)\,m^{2j}(e^{2(r-1)}|w|)^{2m}\mid\mathcal{F}_{m}\Big)\Big]\\
\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{w\in\mathbf{Z}_{m,j,r}}\mathbb{P}\Big(|P_{m}^{(j-1)}(w)+T_{m}^{(j-1)}(w)|\leq C(r,j)\,m^{2j}(e^{2(r-1)}|w|)^{2m}\mid\mathcal{F}_{m}\Big)\Big]\\
\leq C(r,j)\,\max_{|w|\leq e^{r-1}r}\max_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(|T_{m}^{(j-1)}(w)-\zeta|\leq C(r,j)\,m^{2j}(e^{2(r-1)}|w|)^{2m}\Big).

It remains to note that the last term tends to
0
as
m\to\infty
. Indeed, plugging into (
21
) with
\delta=Cm^{2j}(e^{4(r-1)}|w|)^{m}
while noting that
e^{4(r-1)}|w|\leq e^{5(r-1)}r<1
, we get that

\max_{|w|\leq e^{r-1}r}\max_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}\Big(|T_{m}^{(j-1)}(w)-\zeta|\leq C(r,j)\,m^{2j}(e^{2(r-1)}|w|)^{2m}\Big)\leq C(r,j)\big(\log(1/\delta)\big)^{-1/2}\leq\frac{C(r,j)}{\sqrt{m}}\,.

In view of (
22
), we get that

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{r,j}(\gamma)\big)\leq\frac{C(r,j)}{\sqrt{m}}

for all
m
large enough, and hence
\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{r,j}(\gamma)\big)=0
as we wanted to show.
∎

3.
Point process convergence

In this section we complete the remaining details in the proof of Theorem
3
, which says that as
n\to\infty
the random closed sets

\Big\{\big(\alpha,T_{n,\alpha}(R_{n})\big)\,:\,\alpha\in\mathcal{Z}(F_{n})\Big\}

converge in distribution, with respect to the vague topology on point processes on
\mathbb{D}\times\textbf{CL}(\mathbb{C})
, to

\Big\{\big(\alpha,\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\big)\,:\,\alpha\in\mathcal{Z}(F)\Big\}\,.

Let
C_{c}(\mathbb{D}\times\mathbf{CL}(\mathbb{C}))
denote the space of continuous and compactly supported functions
g:\mathbb{D}\times\textbf{CL}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}
. To check the above convergence, we need to show that for any
g\in C_{c}(\mathbb{D}\times\textbf{CL})
we have that

(23)

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Z}(F_{n})}g\big(\alpha,T_{n,\alpha}\big(R_{n}\big)\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\ d\ }\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Z}(F)}g\big(\alpha,\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\big)

where the convergence is in distribution just for a sequence of random variables. In fact, since both
\{F_{n}\}
and
F
are coupled together by the same sequence of random coefficients, we will prove the stronger statement

(24)

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Z}(F_{n})}g\big(\alpha,T_{n,\alpha}\big(R_{n}\big)\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\ a.s.\ }\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Z}(F)}g\big(\alpha,\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\big)\,,

where the almost-sure (a.s.) convergence here is with respect to the sequence of random coefficients
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}
. Since almost-sure convergence implies convergence in distribution, we get that (
23
) follows, and it remains to show (
24
) for all
g\in C_{c}(\mathbb{D}\times\textbf{CL}(\mathbb{C}))
.

We first deal with almost-sure convergence of the roots themselves.

Claim 10
.

Let
F_{n}
and
F
be given by (
9
). Then almost surely for each compact set
K\subset\mathbb{D}
there exists a labeling of the roots

K\cap\mathcal{Z}(F)=\big\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad K\cap\mathcal{Z}(F_{n})=\big\{\alpha_{1,n},\ldots,\alpha_{m,n}\big\}

with
m<\infty
, so that for each
1\leq j\leq m
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{j,n}=\alpha_{j}\,.

Proof.

For each compact set
K\subset\mathbb{D}
we have

\sup_{z\in K}|F(z)-F_{n}(z)|\leq\sup_{z\in K}\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}|z|^{k}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\,.

Furthermore, Theorem
2
implies that almost surely
F
has no double roots in
K
, and the claim now follows from Hurwitz’s theorem (Fact
4
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
g\in C_{c}(\mathbb{D}\times\textbf{CL}(\mathbb{C}))
. By the discussion in the beginning of the section, it is enough to show (
24
). Denote by
\pi_{1}
projection onto the first coordinate and set
K=\pi_{1}(\mathrm{supp}(g))
. Since
K\subset\mathbb{D}
is a projection of a compact set, it is also compact. Claim
10
guarantees the almost-sure convergence of the roots of
F_{n}
to the roots of
F
in
K
, so the convergence (
24
) will follow once we show that for each
1\leq j\leq m

(25)

\operatorname*{Lim}_{n\to\infty}T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}(R_{n})=\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{j}}

in the Kuratowski sense (
8
). We also note that, by Claim
8
,
m
is bounded by a (non-random) constant which depends only on
K\subset\mathbb{D}
.
Recall that
R_{n}
is the random set (
10
) of possible root extensions, and that
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{j}}
is the randomly rescaled dragon curve

(26)

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{j}}=\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})}D_{\alpha_{j}}=\Big\{\frac{f(\alpha_{j})}{F^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})}\,:\,f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\Big\}\,.

Denote by

(27)

\delta=\min_{1\leq j\leq m}|F^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})|\,.

Theorem
2
implies that
\delta>0
almost surely. In particular, almost surely
F
is
\delta
-good in
K
, in the sense of Definition
0
. Theorem
1
then shows that there exists
r_{0}>0
so that for each
1\leq j\leq m
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{H}}\Big(T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}\big(R_{n}\cap\mathbb{D}(\alpha_{j},r_{0})\big),\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{j}}\Big)=0\,.

Since convergence in the Hausdorff metric topology implies convergence in the Fell topology (see
[
5
, Chapter 5]
for a simple proof), we get that

(28)

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{j}}\subset\text{Li}\,\Big(T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}\big(R_{n}\cap\mathbb{D}(\alpha_{j},r_{0})\big)\Big)\subset\text{Li}\,\big(T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}(R_{n})\big)\,.

Claim 11
.

Almost surely for all
j=1,\ldots,m
we have
\text{Ls}\big(T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}(R_{n})\big)\subset\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{j}}\,.

We provide the proof of Claim
11
below. To finish the proof of Theorem
3
, we note that we always have
\text{Li}\,\big(T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}(R_{n})\big)\subset\text{Ls}\,\big(T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}(R_{n})\big)
, and by combining (
28
) with Claim
11
we get that

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{j}}=\text{Li}\,\big(T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}(R_{n})\big)=\text{Ls}\,\big(T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}(R_{n})\big)=\operatorname*{Lim}_{n\to\infty}T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}(R_{n})

in the sense of Kuratowski. This proves (
25
), and since
g:\mathbb{D}\times\textbf{CL}(\mathbb{C})
is continuous, we get that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{m}g\big(\alpha_{j,n},T_{n,\alpha_{j,n}}(R_{n})\big)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}g\big(\alpha_{j},\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{j}}\big)

almost surely,
which proves (
24
). As we explained in the beginning of this section, this implies the point process convergence of Theorem
3
and we are done.
∎

Proof of Claim
11
.

To simplify notation, we drop the dependence on
1\leq j\leq m
, and show that for any sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}\subset K
such that
F_{n}(\alpha_{n})=0
and
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha
we have

(29)

\text{Ls}\big(T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(R_{n})\big)\subset\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\,.

We will use the “hit-and-miss” criterion for the Fell topology: we have that (
29
) holds if and only if for every compact set
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathcal{M}\cap\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}=\emptyset
there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
so that for all
n\geq n_{0}

\mathcal{M}\cap T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(R_{n})=\emptyset\,.

By unpacking the definition (
10
), this just means that for any
f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
and for any root
\xi
of
G_{n,f}(z)=F_{n}(z)+z^{n+1}f(z)
we have

(30)

T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(\xi)\not\in\mathcal{M}

for all
n\geq n_{0}
. In view of Lemma
5
, the proof of (
30
) splits into two cases:

Case I:
Suppose first that the root
\xi
is the unique root of
G_{n,f}
in the disk
\mathbb{D}(\alpha,r_{0})
. In the proof of Theorem
1
, we denoted this root by
\beta_{n,f}
, and showed that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}\Big|T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(\beta_{n,f})+\frac{f(\alpha)}{F^{\prime}(\alpha)}\Big|=0\,,

see (
13
). In particular, by (
26
) the above display shows that

(31)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}\,\text{dist}\big(T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(\xi),\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\big)=0\,.

Since both
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
are compact sets with
\mathcal{M}\cap\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}=\emptyset
, we also have that
\text{dist}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D}_{\alpha})>0
. Combining this with (
31
), we get that (
30
) holds for all
n
large enough.

Case II:

Suppose now that
\xi
is a root of
G_{n,f}
such that
|\xi-\alpha|\geq r_{0}
, for
r_{0}>0
given by Lemma
5
. Note that
r_{0}
depends on
\delta
given by (
27
), but we still have almost surely that
r_{0}>0
, which is all that we shall use. Claim
6
implies that

|\xi-\alpha_{n}|\geq|\xi-\alpha|-|\alpha-\alpha_{n}|\geq r_{0}-C\delta^{-1}|\alpha|^{n+1}\geq r_{0}/2

for all
n
large enough. In particular, we get that

|T_{n,\alpha_{n}}(\xi)|=|\alpha_{n}|^{-n-1}|\xi-\alpha_{n}|\geq\frac{1}{4}|\alpha|^{-n-1}r_{0}\,.

Since the right-hand side of the above display tends to infinity as
n\to\infty
, we see that for all
n
large enough (
30
) holds, as
\mathcal{M}
is a compact set.

All in all, we proved that (
30
) holds for every root of possible roots of
\{G_{n,f}\}_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}
, which in turn proved the inclusion (
29
) and we are done.
∎
