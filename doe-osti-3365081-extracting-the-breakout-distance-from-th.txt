 Abstract
 Enhanced Corner Turning (ECOT) experiments provide an important metric of performance of high explosive (HE)
 formulations. The breakout distance is a single scalar value that characterizes the corner turning efficiency of an HE.
 Extracting the breakout distance from the raw ECOT results, whether experimental or simulated, is a conceptually
 straightforward procedure which, however, is non-unique, especially in the presence of noise. More specifically, this
 procedure involves numerical smoothing and selecting particular values for parameters of this smoothing introduces
 human bias. In this work, we propose to use the Gaussian process regression to analyze ECOT results. This analysis
 involves the effective smoothing of the data, thus allowing for accurate extraction of the breakout distance. Most
 importantly, the parameters of this smoothing can be inferred from the ECOT data itself, rendering the approach
 effectively parameter-free and thus diminishing the human bias. An additional benefit of the Gaussian process regression,
 being a statistical inference method, is that not just the value of the breakout distance, but also its confidence interval
 can be extracted from the data. This report introduces the Gaussian process regression, as applied to ECOT, and
 demonstrates its usefulness by extracting the breakout distances for a selection of experimental and simulated data.

1 Introduction
Enhanced Corner Turning (ECOT) experiment [1] is an important tool for qualifying and quantifying the detonation
performance of high explosive (HE) formulations. The so called breakout distance Λ - coordinate at the acceptor surface
where the detonation wave (as opposed to the non-reactive shock) breaks out to the surface of the acceptor charge in
ECOT experiments - has been reported for many important HE formulations/lots [1, 2]. Mathematically, the breakout
distance is defined as an inflection point - zero of the second derivative - of the ECOT x-t trajectory. Because of the
inevitable presence of noise in experimental data, accurate estimations of the breakout distance can be challenging or,
at best, non-unique. The amount of noise can typically be expected to be lower in hydrodynamic simulations of ECOT.
However, some approaches to calibration of reactive burn models (RBM) might potentially want to rely on small changes
in the breakout distance resulting from small variations of RBM parameters. Under these conditions, accurate extraction
of the value of the breakout distance, and estimation of the confidence interval of this resulting value, is expected to be
important for both experiment and modeling. The present report proposes to apply the Gaussian process regression (GPR)
to do exactly that. As applied to an ECOT x-t trajectory, GPR performs effective smoothing of the data allowing for
analytical differentiation of the resulting smooth t-vs-x dependence. Finding the location (x coordinate) of the minimum
of the resulting analytical derivative gives the breakout distance Λ. Generally, a numerical smoothing procedure is non-
unique since the parameters of smoothing need to be selected somehow, potentially introducing human bias. In the case
of GPR, its parameters (i.e., effectively the parameters of smoothing) can be obtained from the data itself through the so
called hyperparameter optimization (see below). As a result, extracting the breakout distance from a given ECOT data
set {xi , ti } becomes a unique non-ambiguous procedure, producing a unique result for the breakout distance.
 Since the experimental/simulated data is represented by a finite number of (xi , ti ) pairs, and some amount of noise is
expected, the unique result should not be understood as a certain exact value of Λ. Instead, what can generally be expected
is a value of Λ with an associated confidence interval. A general smoothing procedure, followed by analytical/numerical
differentiation to find the inflection point, does not provide this confidence interval. The benefit of the GPR, as used
for ECOT analysis in the present work, is that, being inherently a statistical inference method, it naturally provides not
just the (mean value of) breakout distance, but actually the entire probability distribution of Λ at almost no additional
computational cost. This distribution can in turn be used to calculate the standard deviation, and thus estimate the
confidence interval of Λ.

2 Breakout Distance from Experimental Data
The experimental data, the ECOT x-t trajectory for the ECOT56 shot taken directly from Fig. 6 in Ref. [1], is shown by
black dots in Fig. 1(left). The breakout distance is defined as the location of the inflection point (minimum slope) for the
x-t data, and is observed to be around Λ ∼ 20 mm in the left panel. To extract this distance more accurately, one would
need to somehow evaluate the first derivative of time vs coordinate and then find where, in the proximity of x ∼ 20 mm,
this derivative is minimized. The problem with the direct numerical differentiation is that the original experimental x-t
data does already have some amount of noise, and numerical differentiation is inherently a noise-amplifying procedure.
Furthermore, the resolution with respect to x is ∆x ∼ 0.5 mm in Fig. 1(left), and we perhaps want to obtain Λ more
accurate than up to this ∆x. Under these conditions, it would be great to apply some kind of smoothing procedure to the
original data, and only then proceed with differentiation.
 The procedure we propose to use in the present work is the Gaussian process regression (GPR) [3]. GPR, as used in
this work, assumes that the ECOT x-t trajectory can be represented by a sum of a smooth function f (x) and Gaussian
noise with zero mean and ϵ2 variance

Parameters a and λ specify, respectively, the characteristic amplitude of f and length scale of variation of this function.
If λ is taken to be very small (≲ ∆x), then one can fit any experimental data with just f (x), effectively setting ϵ in Eq.
(1) to zero. However, we want f (x) to be smooth to describe only large-scale variations of the ECOT x-t trajectory, and
not the noise. Looking at the black dots in Fig. 1(left), such a scale can be assumed to be on the order of λ ∼ 5-10 mm.
The characteristic magnitude of f (x) can be taken as a ∼ 5 µs. In fact, Eq. (4) describes only a “smooth” part of the
prior, and the full prior - the one that samples over t(x) - contains an additional parameter, ϵ from Eq. (1). Inspecting
the experimental points visually, we conclude that the experimental noise cannot be larger than, very approximately,
ϵ ∼ 5 × 10−3 µs. Parameters a, λ, ϵ are all the parameters of the prior GP.2
 Once the GPR algorithm is implemented in Python, we first perform the hyperparameter optimization by varying the
values of parameters a, λ, ϵ to maximize Pprior (Dexp ) as described above, and using the very approximate values of these
parameters, postulated just above, as the starting point of numerical optimization. The resulting optimized parameter
values, rounded to three significant figures, are a = 2.75 µs, λ = 5.43 mm and ϵ = 1.67 × 10−3 µs. As is seen, these values
do not contradict our original rough estimates. To further illustrate the hyperparameter optimization, we add some extra
noise to the experimental signal, a zero-mean Gaussian noise with the amplitude of 0.02 µs. The experimental data with
this extra noise added is depicted by black dots in Fig. 1(right). The hyperparameter optimization for this modified data
set produces ϵ ≈ 0.023 µs, which is indeed very close to the noise amplitude we added. It is seen that the hyperparameter
optimization produces meaningful reliable results and renders the entire GPR procedure effectively parameter-free, thus
potentially diminishing human bias. Once the optimal values of hyperparameters are found, the posterior GP can be

 In addition to finding the mean and variance of the derivative posterior GP, one can sample this process directly, via
the corresponding multivariate Gaussian distribution, to produce an entire family of f ′ (x). Such a family is depicted by
dashed black lines in Fig. 3(left). The breakout distance can be calculated for each member of this family, thus producing
the entire distribution of Λ values, shown in Fig. 3(right). The standard deviation for this distribution is ≈ 0.05 mm
and so the complete answer for the breakout distance, extracted by the GPR from the experimental data depicted in
Fig. 1(left), is Λ = 19.22 ± 0.05 mm. Performing the same analysis for the “experimental” data in Fig. 1(right) produces
Λ = 18.27 ± 0.28 mm. As expected, more experimental noise results in larger Λ uncertainty.
 As an exercise, we also sample the original posterior GP, Eq. (2), to obtain the distribution of f (x), i.e., not f ′ (x)
as just above, and then differentiate each f (x) numerically. The result of this numerical differentiation is depicted in
Fig. 3(left) by thin gray lines. As is seen, even though each sample f (x) is smooth, its numerical derivative (central
difference) can already become numerically rather noisy, especially if the finite step size is small, h = 2 × 10−3 mm here.
This emphasizes the need to first analytically obtain the posterior GP for f ′ (x), and only then find Λ and its confidence
interval.

3 Breakout Distance from Simulations
We now turn to extracting the breakout distance from simulations. The approach to simulations is described in detail
in Ref. [5]. The EOS and AWSD model parameters for PBX-9501 (donor) and PBX-9502 (acceptor) are adopted from
Refs. [6] and [7], respectively. The only modification to the simulation setup is to add the so called “axial” PDV probes.
Specifically, there is an array of uniformly distributed “radial” PDV probes in simulations in Ref. [5] that are used
effectively as velocity triggers to catch the moment when the shock/detonation wave hits a point on the acceptor surface.
An alternative approach, added and tested in this work, is to have an “axial” PDV probe with its beam (i) parallel to the
axis of cylindrical symmetry of the system, and (ii) looking in the direction opposite to that of detonation propagation.

 A very important question is how ’real’ is the error bar assigned to the breakout distance by the GPR via evaluating
the standard deviation of Λ? Hydrodynamic simulations are numerically noisy and sometimes changing some physical
model parameters ever so slightly, or even changing some ’numerical’/’computational’ parameters like the number of CPUs
FLAG simulations run on can noticeably change the resulting ECOT trajectory and, subsequently, Λ. The former, i.e.,
small changes to physical model parameters, are especially important from the perspective of our desire to apply the
recently introduced surrogate model approach [8] to the ECOT breakout distance. In that approach, one is required to
perform multiple simulations each corresponding to slightly different values of AWSD parameters, and use the results of
such simulations to draw conclusions about large-scale dependence of various observables (e.g., steady detonation velocity,
breakout distance) on model parameters. Under these conditions, the natural question is how small the parameter variation
can be made before the difference in observable values corresponding to this variation is just numerical noise? Black dots
in Fig. 5 give the variation of the breakout distance with one of the AWSD parameters, k2 . Black and gray error bars
represent, respectively, ±σ and ±3σ confidence intervals, where σ is the standard deviation of Λ obtained with the help of
GPR. The red line is the linear fit through the black dots. As is seen, the difference between black dots and the linear fit,
attributed to the numerical noise, stays within ∼ σ-2σ. Therefore, the confidence interval, estimated by the GPR from,
importantly, a single ECOT x-t trajectory, can be used to estimate the uncertainty of the dependence of Λ on AWSD
parameters in practical simulations.

4 Conclusion
This work proposes to use the Gaussian process regression to analyze the results of experimental/simulated ECOT and,
in particular, to extract the breakout distance. The method, when combined with the hyperparameter optimization,
becomes effectively parameter-free, which should reduce the contribution of human bias into the final reportable results
of the ECOT analysis.
 The last question we are going to address here is how adequate the model, described by Eq. (1), is for fitting ECOT
x-t trajectories? Generally, it is a good idea to introduce as much prior knowledge (hence “prior”) to the GPR analysis
as possible [3]. Here, the knowledge we introduce to the prior model is limited to very general observations/expectations
that ECOT trajectories are rather smooth on the length scales of ∼ 1-10 mm except for the low-amplitude white (i.e., no
correlation between different xi points) noise. We believe the argument that the selected model is adequate is supported
by the following two observations:
 1. Before starting the actual calculations within this project, we were concerned that the hyperparameter optimization
 would drive the noise amplitude ϵ to zero, and the length scale λ to some small values on the order of the distance

Is it possible to improve the model by adding more prior knowledge to it? One possibility is to introduce more physics
to it by having, for example, the correct long-time asymptotic behavior. The steady detonation in the acceptor charge
should result in x − Dt = const at long times [1], and so the model in Eq. (1) can be substituted with

                                                   t(x) = t0 + D−1 x + f (x) + δ,                                         (6)

where t0 and D can be obtained from hyperparameter optimization, or by some other means. For example, the steady
detonation velocity in the acceptor, D, might be known from the diameter effect measurements [9]. Once this “prior”
asymptotics in the form of t0 + D−1 x is introduced, it will describe the bulk of the dependence of t(x) on x, f (x) will
be small and centered around 0 - presumably a more ideal situation for using a zero-mean GP to represent f (x), than
what we have throughout this work. However, this adds more “moving parts” (e.g., more hyperparameters to control)
into the analysis, and it is a priori unclear whether it will significantly improve the accuracy, in the form of the resulting
confidence interval of Λ, to justify it. This might be a matter of the future research.
 The main motivation for this study was to introduce a reliable and robust approach to extract the breakout distance
from the simulated ECOT - important for developing surrogate models [8]. However, the introduced GPR-based analysis
can also become valuable in analyzing experimental data.
