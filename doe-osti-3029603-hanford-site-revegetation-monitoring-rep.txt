 1.0 INTRODUCTION

Revegetation of remediated and disturbed areas on the Hanford Site is performed to support
U.S. Department of Energy, Hanford Field Office’s goal of meeting cleanup and revegetation
requirements mandated in the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and
Liability Act of 1980. Revegetation and monitoring activities are conducted in accordance with
DOE/RL-2011-116, Hanford Site Revegetation Manual, area-specific revegetation plans,
mitigation action plans, and memorandums of agreement.
This report summarizes monitoring in fiscal year (FY) 2025 of revegetated areas on the Hanford
Site. Monitoring of revegetation site was conducted by Mission Support Alliance, LLC in 2017
and was assumed by Hanford Mission Integration Solutions, LLC (HMIS) in 2021. HMIS
performs monitoring at revegetation sites to evaluate success of revegetation activities and
recommend supplemental revegetation activities as needed. This report contains data collected in
FY 2025 for 37 revegetated sites shown in Figure 1-1.
Monitoring data are used to evaluate vegetation composition at revegetated sites. Shrub density
and native canopy cover are measured and evaluated against predetermined success criteria.
Revegetation sites are considered successful if, after a minimum of 5 years, they have a native
climax shrub density of 240 plants per acre and total canopy cover of 25 percent or greater for
native plants as specified in DOE/RL-2011-116. Climax shrub species are representative of
mature shrub-steppe communities as opposed to successional shrubs representative of developing
communities. If the composition of revegetated sites is shown to meet these success criteria the
site is successful, and no further action is recommended. Sites that are not meeting the success
criteria after 5 years of monitoring receive recommendations for additional revegetation actions
and additional monitoring.
Section 2.0 describes the methods used for monitoring. Section 3.0 contains a summary of the
revegetation history, monitoring results, and management recommendations for each site and a
summary table of recommendations. Section 4.0 summaries the recommendations for each site.
Section 5.0 lists the references used in this report. Appendix A provides the revegetation
monitoring species list and Appendix B provides canopy cover data.

 2.0 METHODS

The scientific nomenclature used in this report follows plant names found in the Flora of the
Pacific Northwest: An Illustrated Manual (Hitchcock and Cronquist 2018). Appendix A provides
a list of plant species recorded during vegetation monitoring including scientific names, common
synonyms, common names, and the native/introduced/noxious status. FY 2025 revegetation
monitoring consisted of two quantitative measurements, used to evaluate success criteria.
Canopy cover of all plant species was measured using a series of plot frames. Native and non-
native canopy cover was summed using these measurements. Climax shrubs within a fixed
transect area were counted to measure shrub density (i.e., plants per acre). Climax shrubs used to
measure density include big sagebrush (Artemisia tridentata), spiny hopsage (Grayia spinosa),
and antelope bitterbrush (Purshia tridentata). Successional shrubs such as green rabbitbrush
(Chrysothamnus viscidiflorus) and rubber rabbitbrush (Ericameria nauseosa) and half shrubs
such as snow buckwheat (Eriogonum niveum) were not included in shrub density estimates.
Total species richness including species not detected in plot frames was counted to provide
additional context. Data was collected between April and May 2025.
Shrub density was evaluated using fixed transects monitored annually. Typically transects are
100-m in length with 3-m offsets to either side, with a total area of 600 m2. Shorter (25 to 75-m)
transects have been established at sites too small to support a 100-m transect. The number of live
climax shrubs within the established transect area was used to calculate shrub density per acre.
Survival of planted shrubs was measured along fixed transects for additional context, and may be
analyzed in the future to facilitate comparisons between revegetation methods. Additionally,
survival of the half shrub snow buckwheat and the forbs Munro’s globemallow (Sphaeralcea
munroana) and cushion fleabane (Erigeron poliospermus) were measured at representative sites
where plugs were planted. No analysis of this data is presented in this report but it may be used
in the future to facilitate comparisons between revegetation methods.
Frequency of occurrence and canopy cover measurements were obtained using the methods
described in Steppe Vegetation of Washington (Daubenmire 1970). Canopy coverage is defined
in Daubenmire (1970) as, “the percentage of ground surface included in the vertical projection of
a polygon drawn around the extremities of undisturbed foliage of a plant.” The plot frame
encompasses a 0.5-m2 area. The name and relative amount of ground cover for each rooted
species within the confines of the plot frame is documented for each plot-frame measurement.
The total vegetation can exceed 100 percent with this method due to species overlapping when
plot measurements are taken in densely vegetated areas.
Plot-frame measurements were collected and analyzed to estimate canopy cover for each species
present, at a rate of around 2.75 plot frames per acre. The number of plot frames per acre at a site
varied slightly depending on the size of the site, with smaller sites receiving more plot frames per
acre. For example, 10 frames were collected at the 100-F-26 site, which is 1.04 acres (9.63 plots
per acre) while 250 frames were measured at the 100-C-7 site, which is 122.43 acres (2.04 plots
per acre).
Species that were observed within a revegetated area but not detected in plot frames were
recorded as present and denoted as an “X” in the tables. Washington State noxious weed species
recorded in the monitoring areas are identified in the site monitoring result tables with their state

class designation (i.e., A, B, or C) (Washington State Noxious Weed Control Board [NWCB
2017]).
Ground cover type was factored into the vegetative cover calculation for each site. The
measurement “Unavailable space” used the Daubenmire method to calculate the percentage of
each site where vegetation was unable to grow. An example of this would be a large boulder or
area with large rocks or concrete pads within the revegetation area. The amount of space
considered unavailable is subtracted from 100 percent to represent the available growing space.
Vegetative cover is then divided by the available growing space to represent the actual vegetative
cover in a revegetation site. For example, if a site had 5 percent unavailable space and 20 percent

3.1    100-B/C AREA SITES

Seven sites were monitored for canopy cover and shrub survival in the 100-B/C Area in
FY 2025: 100-B-35, 116-B/C Misc., 100-B/C-CTA, 100-C-7, 116-C-5, 183-B, and C9-L4
(Figure 3-1).
All 100-B/C Area sites except C9-L4 were remediated to meet the objectives for interim closure
as established in DOE/RL-96-17 and in the Interim Action Record of Decision for the 100-BC-1,
100-BC-2, 100-DR-1, 10-DR-2, 100-FR-1, 100-HR-1, 100-HR-2, 100-KR-1, 100-KR-2,
100-IU-2, 100-IU-6, and 200-CW-3 Operable Units, Hanford Site, Benton County, Washington
(Interim Action ROD) (EPA 1999).
Original revegetation efforts at the 100-B/C Area sites planted before FY 2019 entailed
broadcast seeding at approximately 15 lb per acre with a mixture of native grasses, including
Sandberg bluegrass (Poa secunda), Indian ricegrass (Achnatherum hymenoides), bluebunch
wheatgrass (Pseudoroegneria spicata), squirreltail (Elymus elymoides), needle-and-thread grass
(Hesperostipa comata), and prairie junegrass (Koeleria macrantha) topped with a straw mulch
that was crimped into the soil surface. Shrub species, including big sagebrush, antelope
bitterbrush, and spiny hopsage, were transplanted on the sites at approximately 500 to 650 plants
per acre with a mix of approximately 75 percent big sagebrush, 15 percent antelope bitterbrush,
and 10 percent spiny hopsage. Additional revegetation details are provided in the site-specific
sections below.
The C9L4 site was an access road, revegetated in FY 2022 to meet revegetation requirements as
specified in ECR-2017-114, Ecological Clearance for Removal of the 13.8KV Riverland Feeder
Line (C9-L4) From Bonneville Power Administration’s Midway Substation to the 100/BC Area
on the Hanford Site.

3.8    200 AREA SITES

Three revegetation projects in the 200 Areas were monitored in FY 2025: L-525, L-419, and
L-789 (Figure 3-101).
In FY 2017, an export water line was installed, and the disturbed areas were revegetated. For
revegetation and monitoring purposes, the area was divided into two sites, the 200-West (L-840)
Export Water Pipeline site (monitoring completed FY 2024) and the 200-East (L-525) Export
Water Pipeline site. As per the Site-Specific Revegetation Plan for the Export Water Pipeline
(L-840) (MSA 2016) and the Site-Specific Revegetation Plan for the Export Water Pipeline
(L-525) (MSA 2017a), revegetation efforts entailed broadcast seeding with a mixture of native
grasses, including Sandberg bluegrass, Indian ricegrass, and needle-and-thread grass at
approximately 9.75 lbs. per acre combined with a native forb mix of a minimum of four species
at approximately 0.4 lbs. per acre. Broadcast seeding areas were topped with a straw mulch that
was crimped into the soil surface. Shrub species, including big sagebrush, antelope bitterbrush,
and spiny hopsage were transplanted on the sites at approximately 600 plants per acre with a mix
of approximately 66 percent big sagebrush, 17 percent antelope bitterbrush, and 17 percent spiny
hopsage.
In FY 2018, an export water line was installed between the 2901-Y Valve House and the
282-E Inlet Valve House in the 200 East Area. The L-419 pipeline is approximately 3.8 km in
length and is close and parallel to the L-525 Export Water Pipeline. As per the Site-Specific
Revegetation Plan for the Export Water Pipeline (L-419) (MSA 2017b), revegetation efforts
entailed broadcast seeding with a mixture of native grasses, including Sandberg bluegrass, Indian
ricegrass, and needle-and-thread grass at approximately 15.5 lb per acre. Native forb seed was also
broadcast across the site, including Munro’s globemallow, Carey’s balsamroot, shaggy fleabane,
slender hawksbeard (Crepis atribarba), snow buckwheat, and Douglas’ dustymaiden at a rate of
1.88 lb per acre. Shrub species, including big sagebrush, antelope bitterbrush, and spiny hopsage
were transplanted on the sites at approximately 600 plants per acre with a mix of approximately
66 percent big sagebrush, 17 percent antelope bitterbrush, and 17 percent spiny hopsage.
In FY 2022, the L-789 site was revegetated. The L-789 site was an electrical utility access road
in the 200 West Area that had been decommissioned. The site was revegetated following the
Site-Specific Revegetation Plan for Project L-789: Operation and Maintenance of Transmission
and Distribution System Utility Poles and Access Roads in the 200 West Area (HMIS 2021). The
site was broadcast seeded with a mixture of native grasses, including Sandberg bluegrass, Indian
ricegrass, needle-and-thread grass, squirreltail, and prairie junegrass) at a rate of approximately
12.0 lb PLS per acre. Forb species seeded included hoary tansyaster, stalked-pod milkvetch,
Carey’s balsamroot, Munro’s globemallow, Douglas’ dustymaiden, and common yarrow. Big
sagebrush was planted at a rate of 400 plants per acre and spiny hopsage was planted at a rate of
200 plants per acre.
