Executive Summary
We present the results of a study focused on the feasibility of using medium-voltage direct
current (MVDC) power distribution from wind power generation to electrolyzers for hydrogen
production. This approach using hybrid energy generation in an MVDC microgrid offers many
advantages, including possible improvements in efficiency, reliability, and installation cost
compared to a more typical state-of-the-art AC distribution configuration. It also eliminates the
need for transformers, which have been subject to price volatility and availability concerns.

This study highlights the practical feasibility of MVDC distribution networks for integrating
various energy sources, offering improved efficiency and reduced system complexity compared
to conventional AC-based solutions. Future work will focus on enhancing fault protection
strategies, scaling the system to larger renewable installations, and conducting hardware
implementation at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s Flatirons Campus.

This report shows that a DC collection and distribution system reduces the losses associated with
the electrical conversion/distribution process relative to a state-of-the-art AC approach,
improving overall efficiency by 5%. On the qualitative side, reducing the number of conversion
stages is likely to improve reliability, reduce capital investment cost, enable the use of simpler
control algorithms, and reduce the risk of instabilities and malfunctions.

1 Introduction
1.1 Approach
For this effort, we first performed a detailed study to determine challenges and requirements for
devices to be procured to allow for demonstration of such a distribution system at the National
Renewable Energy Laboratory’s (NREL’s) Flatirons Campus.

An essential decision was the selection of topology and the design of a DC/DC step-down
converter, which is presented in Section 4. LLC resonant converter topology allows high-
frequency isolation between input and output stages, which in turn allows for the connection of
multiple stages in series at an input where voltage is above 4 kV and in parallel at an output
where voltage is below 500V. This allows for the use of widely available low-voltage
components in a modular design. Additionally, LLC topology is perfect for this application
because the input-to-output voltage ratio is relatively stable in all practical operating points. The
unidirectionality of this application also allows further simplifications to the topology. The LLC
topology is commonly used in industry for various DC/DC converters requiring isolation.

Once the converter was designed, various models allowing for simulation of future system with
multiple levels of fidelity were developed. The MATLAB Simulink model is an offline
representation of the system, including switching level models of NREL’s second controllable
grid interface (CGI2) with its filters and DC/DC conversion with an electrolyzer model. It allows
for offline simulation of phenomena at sub-millisecond scale necessary for protection design, as
shown in Section 6. Additionally, it was used to cross-validate the quality of the Typhoon
hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) model. This model executes in real time, which allows us to use the
controller hardware-in-the-loop (CHIL) CGI2 replica to further increase model fidelity. The
Typhoon model consists of an exact replica of the CGI2 power hardware with the proposed DC
filter and DC/DC converter supplying the electrolyzer model. It is a very detailed representation
of the future Flatirons Campus medium-voltage DC (MVDC) system proposed in the next stage
of this project. The model allows us to demonstrate steady and dynamic operation, as shown in
Section 4, and to validate the behavior of the system during fault conditions, as shown in Section
6.

By performing numerous simulations using the models described above, we were able to
determine the requirements for power converters and the CGI2 DC filter and distribution
network that need to be procured. For example, we characterized CGI2 DC filters and cables’
extreme short-term current withstand threshold level to determine the proper sizing of these
components for safe shutdown in the event of fault conditions. Moreover, the system is designed
such that complex and less mature MVDC breaker technology is not necessary, as redundant
fast-acting protections of the CGI2 and electrolyzer were verified to safely stop the system in
case of extreme fault conditions. This document will serve as a reference for possible future
design.

As a last step, we propose a method of control for wind generation and electrolyzer operation
that ensures power balancing during high-variability wind scenarios. The method does not
require a central controller to communicate to all the assets but depends on each device’s

 1

measurements of input DC voltage, which translate into power dispatch decisions. In real-world
deployments, however, systems will be interconnected and central controllers will be available.
Having a low-level fail-safe backup control scheme in place will allow more reliable operation of
wind turbine connected electrolyzer system. The control scheme is explained in Section 5 along
with a description of wind turbine system simplification to allow for MVDC interface.
Additionally, the results of operating a 25-MW system consisting of two wind turbines and two
electrolyzers with weak grid interface are presented to demonstrate that this control scheme is
feasible for multiple generation and load devices with extreme variability. This model will be
used in the future together with the CGI2 and electrolyzer to demonstrate full system
performance even in the absence of an MVDC wind converter. By using power hardware-in-the-
loop (PHIL), one electrolyzer in the model will be replaced with the real system at NREL’s
Flatirons Campus using CGI2 as an interface between simulated and real power domain at the
MVDC terminal.

1.2 MVDC Power Distribution in Co-Located Wind and Electrolyzer
 Plant
There is growing worldwide interest in hydrogen production and use. Hydrogen is receiving
much attention as a vital resource for industries such as steel, ammonia, long-haul transportation,
shipping, and aviation (Gulli 2023). However, the production of hydrogen has been challenging
due to high costs and electrical demand (Saur 2008; European Commission 2023).

Multiple ways to reduce the cost of hydrogen generation are being studied (Eichman 2020). One
approach focuses on co-locating a large wind power plant with hydrogen production and
consumption or storage at remote locations where a strong grid connection may not be feasible
or available (Harrison 2009). Typical large wind turbine farms would be connected to a high-
voltage transmission system through medium-voltage AC collection and distribution systems
(CDS), as shown in Figure 1.

Typical wind turbines are designed to operate at low-voltage levels and consist of AC/DC and
DC/AC power converters and transformers to match output voltage to a medium-voltage CDS. In
a co-located system, the electrolyzer would be interconnected through AC CDS, thus requiring
an additional matching transformer at the AC/DC conversion stage. If the location is electrically
remote, installation of a large capacity of power converters may cause serious stability issues and
may require additional specialized solutions, such as flexible AC transmission systems and grid-
forming wind technology, which could additionally increase cost and reduce overall reliability.

The approach we explore here is to use an MVDC CDS (also referred to more simply as DC
CDS) to transfer power from the wind turbines, with the fewest power conversion stages, directly
to the electrolyzer, as shown on Figure 2. A system like this will be partially decoupled from the

level multiple times smaller than the capacity of the wind converters. Section 5 discusses a
scenario in which a 1-MW converter was deemed sufficient for a 25-MW wind system. Such a
small converter is easier to integrate with a transmission grid in remote locations. As an
alternative, the same system can be isolated from the grid completely by replacing the grid-
facing inverter with a relatively small battery energy storage system with the same rating of 1
MW and modest storage capacity sufficient to cover dynamic deficiencies and periods of standby
time with no wind generation.

A crucial argument for DC CDS is the potential to reduce the losses associated with the electrical
conversion/distribution process. Figure 3 shows all conversion stages of a state-of-the-art AC
CDS and proposed DC CDS with typical conversion efficiencies marked. AC CDS efficiency

 3

totals 88% while the proposed DC system offers 93% efficiency, an increase of 5 percentage
points in overall system efficiency. While this efficiency estimation is very rough estimation by
assuming typical conversion efficiencies, the efficiency improvement should allow for
significant reduction in the levelized cost of hydrogen production. Further work at Flatirons
Campus will focus on demonstrating this efficiency gain on real systems at the megawatt scale.

Additional benefits to a DC system are real but harder to quantify. Reducing the number of
conversion stages is likely to improve reliability and reduce capital investment cost. Potential
reliability is higher in a system with fewer components that can break down. Also, by reducing
the number of conversion stages, simpler control algorithms can be used that reduce the risk of
instabilities and malfunctions. Also, a reduced number of components can lead to lower capital
investment, especially in light of increased demand for and short supply of distribution
transformers that depend on rare earth materials (Moseman 2024).

1.3 Why MVDC?
Typical state-of-the-art electrolyzers operate at DC voltage below 500 V, and land-based wind
turbines are typically low-voltage devices with AC voltages ranging between 480 V and 690 V,
so it is natural to question, “Why not use low voltage for CDS?” Figure 4 helps illustrate this
problem better. It shows a system consisting of ten 2.5-MW wind turbines collecting all the
power to a centrally located DC switchgear installed near the electrolyzer and AC/DC grid
interconnection. Wind farms need clearance between the turbines; thus, the distance between
each wind turbine and the switchgear can easily exceed thousands of feet. Transferring 2.5 MW
of power at 1,000 volts direct current (VDC) would require conductors capable of carrying 2,500

 4

A. Using 750-kcmil 1 aluminum photovoltaic cables, 12 parallel runs would be needed (five for
each pole). Assuming current commercial cable prices, a single turbine interconnection would
cost $60,000 for each 1,000 feet, which poses significant capital investment cost. An advantage
of a low-voltage solution is that it would use components that are readily available, e.g., DC/DC
converters, DC breakers, disconnects, fuses. This is unfortunately not the case with MVDC,
which is not currently a common power distribution solution. The DC/DC converters and DC
breakers for medium voltage are not readily available or in early development stages. Electrical
standards for MVDC distribution are also not readily available.

 Figure 4. An example 25-MW wind-electrolyzer DC CDS
On the other hand, by using higher DC voltage, we can reduce cable costs. For the same reason,
AC CDS transformers are used for each turbine, and a medium voltage is typically selected for
CDS. Selection of appropriate DC voltage level is also an interesting topic. With higher voltage,
the costs of cables should be lower; on the other hand, the availability of equipment decreases
exponentially. An aluminum equivalent of a typical AC CDS at 2.5 MW at 13.8 kV would use
16 kilovolts direct current (kVDC):

    •    3 cables rated at 105 A at 13.8 kV AC ($6,000/1,000 ft)
    •    2 cables rated at 156 A at 16 kVDC ($4,500/1,000 ft).

1
 1 kcmil denotes 1,000 circular mils, a unit of measurement for the area of an electrical conductor with a circular
cross section

 5

This means that at 16 kVDC, the cost of cables would be similar to the cost of cables in an AC
distribution system. However, at 16 kVDC, the cost of DC/DC converters and protection system
can increase the capital investment cost to the point that the system is not affordable.

The system proposed at Flatirons Campus is rated at 5 kVDC, as it is believed that at this voltage
level there are commercial solutions available or prototypes nearing commercialization for
DC/DC converters, DC protection, and wind turbines. NREL has worked with multiple vendors
and is aware of others currently offering or developing medium-voltage DC/DC converters.
Multiple DC breaker solutions, e.g., used in the traction business, are available at 3.5 kVDC.
Also, there are Type 4 medium-voltage wind turbine power conversion systems readily available
from some vendors.

Additionally, 5 kVDC is a preferred solution for demonstration at Flatirons Campus because of
the abundance of experience the Flatirons Campus team has with this voltage level from working
with multiple medium-voltage drives and power converters and because of the availability of the
CGI2 DC interface, which is rated at 5 kVDC.

A demonstration at 5 kVDC would enable research into the fundamental concepts of MVDC
distribution and the challenges with step-up and step-down conversion and protection concepts.
The experience gained during that demonstration can later translate into a better understanding of
MVDC distribution systems at higher voltage levels above 10 kVDC or even up to 50 kVDC.
Above 50 kVDC, it may not be practical to implement the DC CDS, as these voltages are more
appropriate for long-distance transmission rather than distribution systems, and associated costs
rise exponentially.

2 Flatirons Campus Demonstration
2.1 Site Overview
NREL Flatirons Campus is home to the Advanced Research on Integrated Energy Systems
(ARIES) research platform and a wide variety of assets (Figure 5) that allow critical research in
integrated energy systems, future energy systems, and grid integration space at a multimegawatt
scale with an underground medium-voltage distribution system that mimics distribution systems
at similar power scale. Among Flatirons Campus assets, the most noticeable are a 1-MW
hydrogen system consisting of electrolyzer, fuel cell, storage, and corresponding power
converters; a solar photovoltaic array; wind turbines; a battery energy storage system; and two
CGIs. The first CGI, which was commissioned in 2013, has a 7-MW capacity at 13.2-kV output
voltage level. The second CGI, commissioned in 2024, has a 20-MW capacity (Figure 6). A
unique capability of CGI2 is that it supports 10-MW DC output at up to 5,000 VDC. CGI2 was
commissioned to expand AC research capability and to add DC research capability at Flatirons
Campus.

Along with CGI2, a controller hardware-in-the-loop (CHIL) version of CGI2 (CGI2 CHIL) was

3 MVDC Converter
3.1 Requirements Specification of the Converter
On the Flatirons Campus, the nominal DC bus voltage for an MVDC distribution network is
planned at 4,300 V, and the rated input voltage for the electrolyzer varies from 320 V to 450 V,
depending on the current level. As the electrolyzer degrades over its lifespan, its maximum
voltage is expected to increase to 500 V. To connect the electrolyzer to an MVDC distribution
network, a DC-to-DC step-down converter is required. To ensure optimal performance of the
electrolyzer, the converter must meet certain requirements, as listed in Table 1.

 Table 1. Requirements for DC-to-DC Step-Down Converter

 Item Values
 Electrolyzer maximum power (continuous) 1.25 MW
 Electrolyzer maximum power (10 s) 1.5 MW
 Nominal input voltage 4,300 V
 Operating input voltage range 4,100-4,500 V
 Output voltage range 300-500 V
 Output current range 0-3,000 A
 Power flow Unidirectional
 Efficiency >95%

Additionally, a wider range of DC voltage operation can be considered to optimize the converter
design, e.g., 4,100 V–4,500 V, so that the output current is proportional to the input voltage, as
demonstrated in Section 5. That may simplify design for certain topologies, as the input-to-
output voltage ratio range can be significantly reduced.

3.2 Available Topologies and Selection Process Based on
 Requirements
There are several DC-to-DC step-down converter topologies available, but not all of them are
suitable for the medium-voltage level due to a lack of available components meeting medium-
voltage specifications—such as the buck converter. The most common concept for MVDC
conversion is to use multiple series-connected inverters, which at the module level are low-
voltage-rated with multiple components readily available. For low-voltage outputs, these
modules will have to be connected in parallel at the output. That topology requires galvanic
isolation between module inputs and outputs, which excludes multiple simple DC/DC
topologies. A typical approach in the industry to achieve a step-down in the DC voltage level
with isolation is to use DC-to-AC followed by AC-to-DC conversion. Step-down is achieved in
the AC stage by using a high-frequency or low-frequency transformer. We have decided that the
resonant LLC converter topology may be the most suitable for our application because of its:

 1. Narrow range of input-to-output voltage ratio
 2. Unidirectional power flow

 12

 3. High-frequency isolation that allows series parallel operation
 4. Low losses of LLC resonant converter.
An LLC topology was used for the sake of the study; however, it is understood that once the
procurement process starts, another topology may be found to be optimal. The results shown here
can be used to specify converter requirements but do not dictate the exact topology to be used by
a vendor.

The resonant LLC converter topology can provide reliable and efficient voltage step-down
conversion from 4.3 kV to 540~320 V using low-voltage components by using three series-
connected modules at the input and paralleling them at the output. An example schematic of a
half-bridge LLC resonant converter and three-phase series-parallel-connected LLC resonant
converters is shown in Figure 12 and Figure 13, respectively.

 Figure 12. Half-bridge LLC resonant converter

Figure 13 shows only three modules, but in reality, multiple parallel converters may need to be
used due to the current limitation of high-frequency switching devices. For simplification of
modeling, no paralleling was used.

The main components of a resonant LLC converter are series inductors, capacitors, and shunt
inductors. As shown in Figure 13, transformers provide galvanic isolation between the primary
and secondary sides. This isolation allows us to flexibly configure grounding on the output side
and to use a series-parallel connection for the converter. Also, the transformer’s magnetizing
inductors act as a shunt inductor in the LLC circuit.

There are several advantages to the resonant LLC converter. It can provide a wide range of
output voltage with only minor modification to the switching frequency. Also, it can achieve
zero-voltage switching during its entire operation when properly designed. As the electrolyzer’s
input voltage level varies between 540 V and about 320 V, having a wide range of output voltage
levels with only small modification to the switching frequency will ensure the converter’s
interoperability with the electrolyzer. Additionally, since power flows in only one direction in
the electrolyzer, we can further simplify the LLC resonant converter topology.

4 Integration of Assets (Electrolyzer and Wind)
The goal of this research is to simulate electrolytic hydrogen production with a wind turbine. To

the electrolyzer can be achieved using an MVDC distribution system. By removing the
conversion stage and the transformer, we can improve both power and economic efficiency.

4.1 Wind Turbine Modification for DC Connection
To achieve MVDC connection of the wind turbine, there are some modifications that must be
made to the wind turbine model. First, the DC-to-AC conversion stage was removed. The wind
turbine will only have a DC link capacitor before being connected to an MVDC grid.

Additionally, a scaling of DC link voltage was necessary. The wind turbine model’s rated DC
link voltage was lower than the intended DC voltage level of an MVDC distribution system. It
was rated at 1.2 kV while the MVDC distribution system was rated at 4.3 kV. However, if the
DC voltage level changes, other components such as inductors and capacitors should also be
adjusted. For the purpose of demonstration and to reduce the complexity, instead of changing all
the parameters, the scaling of the DC link voltage was done within CHIL.

4.3 Power Generation/Consumption Control Scheme
There is growing interest in hydrogen production in areas that are either underserved by the
utility grid or in remote areas where there is no grid. To produce hydrogen from hybrid energy
systems rather than the grid, a control scheme must be implemented to regulate the electrolyzer’s
power consumption, ensuring that it operates in harmony with the hybrid energy system power
generation. To achieve this, we developed a communication-less power consumption control
scheme using an MVDC voltage level.

In this control scheme, the MVDC voltage level is monitored by each device in the system to
determine the power generation level of the wind turbine, and the output current is adjusted
accordingly. The electrolyzer will decide its power consumption level based on DC voltage at its
terminal. Once the electrolyzer’s DC voltage level reaches its maximum, signifying that it is
consuming its maximum power, a wind turbine will start to curtail its power production by
controlling its pitch angle. For this case, the grid converter is only responsible for DC voltage
regulation and auxiliary power to turbines and the electrolyzer. DC voltage regulation and
auxiliary power will only require a small amount of power. A small capacity converter that is
less than 10% of the total electrolyzer and the wind capacity will be enough to handle the
operation, which also decreases the cost of installation. This grid converter will act as a virtual
capacitor to allow drift on DC voltage. This entire process is accomplished by only looking at the
DC voltage level at each device in the system. Figure 40 illustrates such control.

  Figure 40. Grid-side converter control and wind vs. electrolyzer voltage droop control scheme
When the wind power generation is at 1.25 MW or above—the maximum capacity the
electrolyzer can consume—the current controller commands the electrolyzer to draw its full
capacity, supplying 3,000 A at 415 V to consume 1.25 MW. For the wind turbine, the control
will curtail the power generation to be at 1.25 MW, even if there is more wind available to
generate more power, minimizing the grid involvement. However, when power generation falls
below 1.25 MW, the electrolyzer’s power consumption is curtailed by limiting the output current
from the converter. The output current level is determined from the pre-characterized curves
based on DC voltage level, which is shown in Figure 40. Using this control scheme, electrolytic
hydrogen production directly from hybrid energy systems can be achieved without requiring a
communication protocol between systems. Initial simulation results after implementing this
control approach are shown in Figure 41.

 Figure 41. Effect of wind speed change on the electrolyzer power and the grid power

The wind turbine produces maximum power at a wind speed of 12 m/s. As shown in Figure 41,
as the wind speed reaches 12 m/s, the wind turbine starts to generate maximum power, and the

As soon as the wind speed exceeds 12 m/s, the turbine started to adjust the pitch angle of the

4.4 Scalability of the System (to 25 MW)
We believe that a single 1.25-MW capacity electrolyzer and a single 2.5-MW wind turbine are
insufficient to fully demonstrate real-world operation. Typically, wind turbines are deployed in a
cluster with total capacities in the tens of megawatts. A single electrolyzer would not be enough
to utilize all the power generated from such a setup. To fully harness wind-generated power,
multiple electrolyzer stacks are needed to scale up hydrogen production.

Although the electrolyzer at Flatirons Campus is only 1.25 MW, and the resonant LLC converter
we designed is also rated for 1.25 MW, both can be easily scaled by connecting multiple units in
series. To demonstrate this scalability, we expanded our testbed by incorporating multiple wind
turbines and electrolyzers, increasing the system’s overall capacity.

 First, we increased the capacity of a single wind turbine to 12.5 MW and added a second
identical turbine in RTDS. Similarly, we scaled the electrolyzer capacity to 12.5 MW and added
a second identical electrolyzer. To enhance the realism of our simulation, we also introduced a
30-minute wind profile captured at Flatirons Campus.

5 Protection
5.1 Protection Overview
Although we demonstrated that the proposed concept is a probable solution to produce
electrolytic hydrogen from hybrid energy systems, protection is still a concern for the DC
system. There are many readily available protection equipment options for AC systems, and it is
relatively easy to implement protection due to the nature of AC systems—AC crosses zero every
1/60 s. AC protection equipment, such as a breaker, can latch at zero current when there is a
fault, minimizing the energy dissipated. Unlike AC, DC will not reach zero during operation,
which means that when a fault happens, a lot of energy will be dissipated. Also, our test articles,
such as CGI2 and the resonant LLC converter, contain large capacitors that store a lot of energy.
Thus, it is important to investigate the possible impact of faults on the DC system.

For the fault cases, we mainly focused on two major fault types: pole-to-ground fault and pole-
to-pole fault. This study is specific to NREL’s Flatirons Campus and investigates the impact of
the fault on CGI2, the MVDC distribution system, and the electrolyzer, including the resonant
converter.

Because faults are extremely dynamic events, we first had to confirm the integrity of our real-
time simulation model at dynamic levels. To do so, we ran a Simulink-based model for the
aforementioned fault cases and cross-validated the results with real-time simulations. The initial
responses from CGI2 CHIL were nearly identical to those of the Simulink model. This indicates
the reliability of our CGI2 CHIL model. Next, we compared and analyzed the results of the two
fault cases from both Simulink and real-time simulations, as discussed below.

For all results presented here, the initial responses from real-time simulations are similar but do
not exactly match the responses from the Simulink simulation. This is because the CGI2 CHIL
model includes the CGI2 protection system, whereas the Simulink model does not. When a fault
occurs, the CGI2 controller detects it and activates the protection system, such as integrated gate-
commutated thyristor pulse removal. This protection system disconnects CGI2 from the fault,
protecting the internal circuit from any damage. Using CGI2 CHIL allows us to see exactly how
the fault detection and protection actions will be executed by the CGI2 controller.

5.2 Ground Fault Test
For the pole-to-ground fault test, we injected a fault on the load side before the resonant
converter (Figure 44). The fault was applied between the positive pole and the ground for 0.005
seconds. We applied the fault after the converter reached steady-state operation at full power.
After applying the fault, we examined measurements from various locations on the circuit. We
also conducted an identical test on the real-time simulation model using CGI2 CHIL. The fault
location and fault duration were as described above, and measurements were taken from identical
locations in the circuit.

5.4 Protection Summary
Analyzing the results from both offline and real-time simulations, we did not observe any
abnormal behavior in terms of current, voltage, or converter and inverter performance that could
cause severe damage to connected assets. The results demonstrated that our assets, CGI2 and the
resonant converters, can shut themselves down within 2–3 ms without exceeding realistic design
criteria, ensuring safe operation for both personnel and equipment.

Although the operation is sufficiently safe, the resonant converter was exposed to short-term
overvoltage during the pole-to-ground fault. Therefore, we must ensure that the converter
components have adequate overvoltage ratings. Additionally, it would be beneficial to
implement undervoltage and overvoltage detection mechanisms on the converter’s input voltage
side. During a fault, the converter does not need to remain operational, so it is ideal to shut it
down immediately upon detecting undervoltage or overvoltage to isolate both the converter and
the electrolyzer from the fault.

Beyond the converter, the DC filter is also exposed to stresses during faults. We observed that
for both fault cases, high fault currents between 4,000 A and 6,000 A flowed through the filter,
particularly through its capacitors. Using this information, we can select appropriate DC filter
capacitors that can withstand the observed fault currents as well as capacitor fuses to protect the
capacitors in the event of a more severe fault with even higher current levels.

Assuming the above considerations and recommended protections are implemented, we do not
identify a critical need for DC breakers or high-current DC fuses in this MVDC distribution
network configuration connecting CGI2 and the electrolyzer at the NREL Flatirons Campus.
However, other use cases involving additional assets, such as a battery storage system, may
require different protection devices.

6 Future Work
While the analysis in this report demonstrated that the proposed MVDC distribution system is a
probable solution for integrating the wind turbine and the electrolyzer, there are still areas that
require further research. Particularly, optimization of protection strategies and evaluation of their
application to different assets and configuration will be necessary for broader implementation.
For instance, this report only investigated basic fault cases. Although they are the most common
fault cases, it is necessary to investigate more complex fault cases such as high impedance fault
to properly design the protection system.

Additionally, our test bed is designed around Type 4 wind turbines, which are primarily used for
offshore applications. To expand the applicability of our proposed solution, it will be important
to incorporate other types of wind turbines. For example, Type 3 wind turbines, also known as
doubly fed induction generator wind turbines, are more commonly used for land-based
installations. Integrating Type 3 wind turbines will require further development to account for
their different topology.

Finally, validating the proposed solution with physical systems at NREL’s Flatirons Campus will
be essential. Once all necessary assets are commissioned, confirming that the observed
simulation behaviors align with those seen in the physical system’s operation will be critical for
ensuring the system’s reliability and effectiveness.

7 Conclusion
This report demonstrated the feasibility of an MVDC distribution network to connect hybrid
energy sources, such as wind, directly to electrolyzers for electrolytic hydrogen production. By
implementing a resonant LLC converter, we successfully achieved efficient DC voltage step-
down while ensuring reliable power distribution to the electrolyzer. The proposed control
scheme that does not depend on communication effectively regulated the power consumption of
the electrolyzer based on available power from the hybrid energy systems, minimizing grid
dependency; thus, it will ensure reliable hydrogen production.

By utilizing both Simulink models and the CGI2 CHIL real-time model, we verified the system’s
performance under various circumstances, including two critical fault scenarios. The results
confirm that the converter and CGI2 can operate safely without external protection equipment, if
correct components that withstand the stresses are used.

While the proposed solution shows significant promise, further research is needed to develop
better protection strategies that would cover more complex fault scenarios and expand
compatibility with different wind turbine topologies. Future work will also include validation of
the performance in a physical test bed at NREL’s Flatirons Campus.

By using the proposed solution combined with effective control strategies, this report presented a
scalable, more efficient, and more economical solution for hybrid energy systems providing
power for electrolytic hydrogen production.
