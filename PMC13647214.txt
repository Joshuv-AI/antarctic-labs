Microbiota-derived metabolites in hematopoietic regulation and disease: mechanisms, context, and therapeutic perspectives

Husni Farah, Aziz Kubaev, Malathi Hanumanthayya, Maryam Abdul Al-Hussein, Ahmed Faisal Mutee, Fotima Axmedova, Babamuratov Bekzod, Shefali

Abstract

Recent studies have shown that the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in the regulation of hematopoiesis due to the generation of a variety of metabolites. Metabolites generated by the gut microbiota, including Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), tryptophan derivatives, Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), and bile acids, significantly affect the differentiation and function of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, as well as immune cell differentiation and activity. The effects of these metabolites are achieved through several signaling pathways, including Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition by SCFAs, aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR)-dependent regulation of hematopoietic stem cell fate and myeloid commitment, and inflammasome signaling control by bile acids, among others. Of note, dysregulation of metabolite-mediated pathways has been shown to play a role in the development of several disorders such as anemia, hematologic or solid malignancies, Graft-vs.-host disease (GvHD), and cardiovascular diseases. In this review, we describe current findings on the mechanisms by which microbiota-derived metabolites influence hematopoietic processes and contribute to the development of hematological disorders, while also highlighting novel therapeutic approaches targeting microbiota–metabolite interactions.

Introduction

Recent research has highlighted the functional interactions between microbial products and host hematopoietic and immune systems, revealing that commensal and pathogenic microbes can shape hematopoiesis and immune regulation through distinct molecular pathways (; ). Importantly, microbial metabolites act not only on hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) but also on the bone marrow niche that includes mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs), endothelial cells, neurons, and glial cells (; ). These niche elements regulate HSC mobilization, self-renewal, and lineage commitment and can be profoundly influenced by microbial signals such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), lactate, bile acids, and tryptophan derivatives (; ; ). Thus, the microbiota exerts a dual level of control: directly on HSC biology and indirectly through shaping the supportive stromal microenvironment. Notably, certain commensal bacteria, such as Clostridium, Faecalibacterium, and Lactobacillus, generate SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, through fermentation of dietary fibers (). These SCFAs are key regulators of immune cell differentiation and have also been implicated in modulating HSC function via epigenetic and metabolic pathways (). For example, butyrate has been shown to inhibit the proliferation of intestinal stem cells in a Forkhead Box O3 (FoxO3)-dependent manner, suggesting a broader role in stem cell regulation, including within the bone marrow niche (). Lactate, another metabolite produced by commensal lactic acid bacteria, stimulates bone marrow MSCs to release stem cell factor (SCF), thereby promoting HSC expansion and hematopoietic regeneration (). This process is mediated via the Gi-coupled protein receptor 81 (GPR81), as evidenced by the significant reduction in bone marrow HSCs in GPR81-deficient mice (; ). In addition to these commensal metabolites, other microbial products, like phosphatidylcholine and γ-glutamylalanine, could be derived from more general microbial sources, like pathobionts, and have been shown to elicit type I interferon (IFN) responses that restore hematopoietic disturbance induced by microbial dysbiosis (; ). The mechanistic evidence linking microbiota-derived molecules to hematopoietic regulation varies substantially across experimental systems (). Evidence obtained from human HSPCs or clinical studies provides the strongest basis for inferring relevance to human hematopoiesis, whereas findings from murine models, ex vivo systems, or cultured cells should be interpreted as preclinical or mechanistic evidence.

Given the broad effects, dysregulated host–microbiota interactions have been implicated in multiple disease contexts (; ). Altered microbiota metabolite availability can impair erythropoiesis in anemia, exacerbate graft-vs.-host disease (GVHD), shape the tumor microenvironment in hematologic cancers, and contribute to cardiovascular and inflammatory disorders and infectious diseases (; ; ; ; ; ). Conversely, restoration of beneficial metabolite pathways through diet, probiotics, or microbial engineering offers promising therapeutic avenues (). An important component of microbiota–hematopoiesis communication is the capacity of the host to sense microbial organisms and their molecular signals during both homeostasis and infectious challenge (). Commensal microorganisms provide physiological cues that contribute to immune maturation and hematopoietic homeostasis, whereas pathobionts and infectious agents can generate inflammatory signals that activate innate immune pathways and promote emergency hematopoiesis. Thus, host–microbe and host–pathogen interactions provide an important mechanistic link between microbial ecology, microbial metabolites and products, innate immune sensing, hematopoietic adaptation, and disease. This review discusses the numerous mechanisms by which microbial metabolites influence hematopoietic function and the underlying mechanisms.

Microbiota, microbiota metabolites, receptors, and functions on the host

The gastrointestinal tract (GIT) harbors a diverse richness of organisms such as bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses, and bacteriophages referred to as the gut microbiota, an immense ecosystem comprising over 1015 microbial cells and more than 22 million microbial genes, surpassing the total number of human cells and genes, and encoding a vast array of enzymes capable of degrading dietary compounds that escape digestion by human enzymes, such as complex polysaccharides and dietary fibers () (; ). The gut microbiota generates a diverse range of metabolites with extensive biological activities (). In this review, the term “microbiota-derived metabolites” is used primarily for small molecules whose production or biotransformation is attributable, at least in part, to the gut microbiota (). These include metabolites directly synthesized by microorganisms, such as SCFAs and several tryptophan-derived compounds, as well as host-derived molecules that are subsequently modified by microbial enzymes, such as secondary bile acids (). However, not all microbiota-associated bioactive molecules are conventional metabolites. Lactate, and polyamines can originate from both host and microbial metabolism; therefore, their classification as microbiota-derived should be restricted to contexts in which a microbial contribution has been demonstrated or specifically implicated ().

Microbial metabolites are generally believed to have two main functions: serving as “fuels” or energy sources and acting as fundamental elements in metabolic processes (). Nevertheless, it has been demonstrated that certain metabolites can function as intracellular signaling molecules by regulating the activity of enzymes, such as histone deacetylases (HDACs), inhibiting enzymes in traditional feedback loops of metabolic pathways, or directly acting as signaling molecules by binding to nuclear hormone receptors, such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) and farnesoid X receptor (FXR) (also known as bile acid receptor) (). It is widely recognized that some microbiota metabolites have a strong effect on the generation of hormones and stimulation of the GIT by activating certain G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) (). Currently, as orphan GPCRs were being gradually identified and connected with specific chemicals, it became clear that several other compounds may be recognized and linked by selective GPCRs to initiate traditional signal transduction pathways (). For instance, the gut microbiota produces SCFAs and secondary bile acids (SBAs), whereas the host's intermediate metabolism produces circulatory metabolites such as lactate and ketone bodies (). These compounds are referred to as “signaling metabolites” ().

SCFAs have a role in maintaining immune balance in both the mucosal and systemic parts of the body (). SCFAs promote a state of balanced immunological response in the colon by activating regulatory T cells (Tregs) through a process that relies on the free fatty acid receptor 2 (FFAR2) (also known as GPR43) (). These interactions have a crucial role in the generation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine Interleukin (IL)-10 and in protecting against colitis in mouse models (). The anti-inflammatory effects of SCFAs have also been associated with the activation of the inflammasome in the cells that line the intestines (). Indoles, tryptamine, serotonin, and kynurenine are among the well-known substances that are produced when tryptophan is broken down (). Dietary tryptophan can create indoles derived from microbes, such as indole-propionic acid, indole-3-acetate, indole-3-aldehyde, tryptamine, and 3-methyl-indole (). These metabolites affect immunity, metabolism, behavior, and lifespan, in addition to other aspects of the host, through their interaction with the nuclear receptor known as the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). Bile acids impact the microbiota, which in turn affects host metabolism (). In mice, activation of FXR by the fexaramine agonist modifies the makeup of the gut microbiota and raises serum glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) and glucose tolerance (). Recent research has found a connection between bile acids and gut bacteria in the pathogenesis of colorectal cancer (CRC) (). Furthermore, spermine inhibits the generation of IL-1, macrophage inflammatory protein-1 (MIP-1) α, and MIP-1β in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (). Moreover, the development of intestinal and systemic immune cells is impacted by the daily treatment of either spermine or spermidine in rats from birth (). In summary, the microbiota, their metabolites, receptors, and host activities interact dynamically to form a complex ecosystem that is precisely regulated inside the human body.

Methodology for the literature review

The present review was conducted to comprehensively summarize the current evidence regarding microbial metabolites in hematopoietic regulation and disease. To ensure broad coverage of the available literature, a systematic bibliographic search was performed using the Web of Science, PubMed, and Google Scholar databases. The primary search keywords included metabolites generated by the gut microbiota, including SCFAs, tryptophan derivatives, TMAO, and bile acids, which have a considerable effect on the differentiation and functioning of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells as well as immune cell differentiation and activity. The reference lists of relevant publications were also carefully reviewed to identify additional studies that may not have been captured in the initial database search. No publication-year restrictions were applied, allowing us to include both foundational studies and recent advances in the field. English-language articles were preferentially considered, and both preclinical and clinical studies were included. Study selection was conducted in two stages: titles and abstracts were first screened for relevance, followed by a thorough full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles.

Microbial metabolites and hematopoietic niches: from fetal development to adult adaptation

Microbial signals in early life are critical to guide the development of HSPCs in bone marrow (). Maternal microbiome metabolites have been reported to pass through the placenta and into fetal circulation, which impacts fetal hematopoietic development (). In vivo experiments have also shown that microbial communities control the development of HSCs and HSPCs by modulating inflammatory signaling in their niche (). A balanced maternal microbiome is thus essential to modulate normal embryonic hematopoiesis and HSPC homeostasis (). Specific bacterial lineages were found to influence the regulation of the differentiation of HSPCs by modulating the production of neighboring cytokines around these progenitors (). Microbial metabolites, such as SCFAs, are also passed to the offspring via breast milk, where they influence the adjustment of immune maturation and inflammatory homeostasis (). In addition to this, the gut MAMPs get into systemic circulation and travel to the bone marrow, where they suppress the proliferation and lineage commitment of HSCs, multipotent progenitors (MPPs), and their derivatives (). Interruptions of maternal microbiota, e.g., dysbiosis, could hinder the process and make the neonatal bone marrow microenvironment different, thereby influencing the activity of progenitor cells (). Collectively, these discoveries shed light on how maternal microbiota signals control the immune system and hematopoietic development in early life ().

Systemic metabolites can either potentiate or suppress these emergency responses by acting directly on HSPCs or indirectly via niche cells and inflammatory mediators (; ). For instance, SCFAs are present in trace amounts in fetal direct circulation, but maternal nutrition and placental transfer can transfer SCFAs to the embryo (). SCFAs in adults regulate bone marrow HSCs indirectly by modulating stromal cytokine secretion and systemic inflammation (). Propionate and butyrate are capable of inhibiting inflammatory signaling and altering the balance in myelopoiesis vs. lymphopoiesis; propionate was implicated in influencing IL-17–producing innate T cell responses and in HDAC-dependent modifications of innate immune ontogeny, secondarily modulating HSC output (; ). SCFAs are thus able to inhibit unwarranted emergency myelopoiesis or, in a context-dependent fashion, facilitate lineage differentiation. Concentrations of fetal circulation bile acids are unique compared with adults; pathologic exposure (maternal cholestasis, bile acid dysregulation of metabolism) can interfere with fetal hematopoiesis via placental inflammation and oxidative stress (). Microbiota-derived second bile acids can gain systemic circulation and modulate marrow stromal function via FXR/G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) on niche cells and induce epithelial barrier breakdown and low-grade systemic inflammation that initiates emergency myelopoiesis (). Deoxycholic acid (DCA) specifically has been implicated in secondary reprogramming of hematopoiesis through epithelial stress and inflammation (; ). In summary, microbial metabolites offer systemic signals that influence hematopoiesis at every phase of life, from fetal ontogeny to adult immune competence ().

Microbial metabolites and their impact on resident immune cells across tissue barriers and metabolic organs

The human microbiota generates a vast array of metabolites that cross the gut lumen and are vital regulators of host immunity (). These metabolites control immune homeostasis not merely at the intestinal mucosa but at distant tissue barriers and organs such as the skin, lungs, liver, adipose tissue, and the CNS (). Tissue-resident immune cells like dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), γδ T cells, and regulatory T cells are especially responsive to these microbial signals (; ; ; ; ). Through regulation of their function, microbial metabolites are involved in immune tolerance, pathogen defense, and inflammation modulation.

The gut is a dense microbial community and a metabolic crosstalk hot spot (). Diet fiber fermentation releases SCFAs (acetate, propionate, and butyrate), promoting epithelial barrier function and regulating differentiation of regulatory T cells by epigenetic reprogramming and GPCR activation (). SCFAs also control DC and macrophage pro-inflammatory responses and induce a tolerogenic state (). Other metabolites provide further layers of regulation. Secondary bile acids, produced by microbial modification of primary bile acids, suppress T cell helper (Th) 17 reactions and modulate intraepithelial lymphocyte homeostasis (). In contrast, tryptophan catabolites are strong agonists for the AhR, enabling ILC3-mediated IL-22 production, which induces mucosal protection (; ).

The skin microbiota adds its own set of metabolites to the portfolio, such as indole derivatives, lipids, and SCFA-like molecules (; ). Indoles, the tryptophan metabolites of bacteria, activate AhR in keratinocytes and cutaneous T cells and increase antimicrobial peptide expression (). Microbial lipids reset skin-resident macrophage and Langerhans cell function, influencing wound healing and inflammatory thresholds (; ). Commensal bacteria-derived metabolites, at the same time, prime epidermal γδ T cells to facilitate a rapid tissue repair response after injury (; ). Besides, in spite of reduced microbial burden of the respiratory tract, immune control remains under the influence of local microbes and gut-derived metabolites transported via the circulation (). SCFAs, for example, augment alveolar macrophage phagocytic and immunomodulatory activity and suppress allergic and inflammatory reactions (). Aberrations in this metabolic signaling—always present with dysbiosis—have been associated with asthma and susceptibility to infection ().

Located downstream of the gut via the portal vein, the liver is constantly exposed to microbial metabolites (). Secondary bile acids are also important in regulating Kupffer cell activation and hepatocyte signaling via FXR and TGR5, shaping the organ's inflammatory phenotype (). Microbial products like acetate and ethanol also modulate hepatocyte metabolism and innate immune tone (). Perturbation of these interactions forms the underpinning for diseases like non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (). Additionally, adipose depots are currently recognized to be immunologically active organs where microbial metabolites have systemic actions (). SCFAs induce anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization and regulatory T cell accumulation, guaranteeing metabolic homeostasis (). In the end, microbial metabolites are pivotal in two-way communication along the gut–brain axis (). In the CNS, microbial metabolites have profound influences on resident immune cells. SCFAs trigger maturation and functional calibration of microglia to enable maximum surveillance and synaptic support (). Tryptophan-derived ligands, by activating AhR in astrocytes and microglia, inhibit neuroinflammation, a mechanism implicated in multiple sclerosis and its related disorders (). Lactate, a product of microbial fermentation, is neuroprotective through the strengthening of regulatory immune cycles in the CNS (). When the production of metabolites is changed, however, these beneficial interactions are disrupted, making one susceptible to psychiatric and neurodegenerative illness (). In total, microbial metabolites are promiscuous molecular signals that repeatedly report to resident immune cells of barrier surfaces, metabolic organs, and the nervous system. Elucidating these interactions not only enhances our appreciation for host–microbe symbiosis but also promises new therapies that exploit microbial metabolism to restore immune balance across the body.

Microbial metabolites on hematopoiesis

The interplay of microbial metabolites and hematopoiesis represents a key axis of host-microbe communication, influencing blood cell development via direct effects on HSCs and the bone marrow microenvironment (). These interactions are shaped not only by the type of metabolite but also by the taxonomic identity of the microbial producers—whether commensals, pathobionts, or overt pathogens (). This section outlines the role of specific microbial metabolites in regulating hematopoiesis, focusing on erythropoiesis as a representative lineage (, ).

Erythropoiesis and red blood cells

In contrast to pathogen-derived products, commensal-derived microbial metabolites such as butyrate, propionate, and acetate exert more homeostatic effects on erythropoiesis. In a study, conducted a study to assess the impact of SCFAs on erythropoiesis in both primitive and definitive erythroid precursor cells. These cells were obtained from transgenic mice with naturally high levels of SCFAs, as well as from wild-type mice and transgenic mice that contained the human β-globin gene locus. From a mechanistic standpoint, it has been shown that SCFAs have an impact on several intracellular processes and regulatory molecules (). These include cyclic nucleotides, cell-signaling kinases, transcription factors, mediators of apoptosis, corepressors, and histone deacetylase inhibitors (). The activation of signaling molecules by SCFA has been linked to erythropoiesis and the increase in expression of embryonic and fetal globin genes (). indicated that SCFAs stimulate the activation of embryonic and fetal β-type globin genes in EryD, which is a crucial objective in the treatment of β-globin gene abnormalities. This discovery provides the chance to examine SCFAs in a model of definitive erythropoiesis that is sensitive to SCFAs, not altered, and can be easily modified by transgenic modification. The current study investigated the impact of naturally occurring increases in SCFAs on the expression of globin genes in immature red blood cell precursors (). This might elucidate the difference in induced human fetal (gA vs. gG) or induced murine embryonic (bH1 vs. eY) globin gene expression found with SCFAs alone vs. SCFAs with ins/erythropoietin (). Collectively, these results highlight the prime significance of the microbial origin of the metabolites—commensal or pathogenic—since such a differentiation is responsible for making the metabolite foster erythroid development or compromising it through stimulation.

Lymphocytes

The most significant anti-inflammatory characteristic of SCFAs is their capacity to stimulate Tregs, which in turn reduces the activity of effector T cells. This impact is mediated by a variety of mechanisms: acetate and propionate, but not butyrate, boost the growth of pre-existing colonic Tregs (cTregs), while propionate and butyrate, but not acetate, promote the de novo differentiation of naïve T cells into Tregs (; ). Each of these roles works independently since the number of Tregs in germ-free mice, which is drastically decreased in the absence of therapy, may be restored by separately administering butyrate, acetate, or propionate (; ). The particular nature of each action for a distinct group of SCFAs reflects the underlying biochemical mechanism. Butyrate does not efficiently activate FFAR2, which is necessary to proliferate pre-existing cTregs (). Separately, butyrate has been demonstrated to increase Treg production by fostering an anti-inflammatory phenotype in macrophages and DCs via HCAR2 activation (). In addition to these mechanisms, discovered that exposing a CRC cell line to SCFAs may increase the generation of transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), facilitating the differentiation of naïve T cells into Tregs. SCFAs have been disclosed to encourage the generation of both effector and Tregs based on the immune-mediated setting (). B-cell function is also modulated by commensal-derived SCFAs (). In a study, assessed this discovery further, demonstrating that SCFAs boost antibody responses by modulating gene expression via HDAC inhibition. Intriguingly, they discovered that SCFAs affect the energy metabolism of B cells, promoting antibody synthesis.

Recent investigations have demonstrated the distinct impacts of bile acid derivatives—metabolized by specialized gut bacteria, including Clostridium scindens and Eubacterium spp.—on the differentiation of Tregs (). Isoallolithocholic acid (isoalloLCA) enhances Tregs development by generating mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) (mitoROS), resulting in enhanced Forkhead box protein P3 (FOXP3) expression and Tregs differentiation regardless of the microbiome structure (). IsoalloLCA stimulates the nuclear hormone receptor Nr4a1 (nuclear receptor subfamily 4, group A, member 1) at the molecular level, leading to the creation of a permissive chromatin structure in the promoter region of the transcription factor FOXP3 (). Through the process of microbial epimerization, isodeoxycholic acid (isoDCA), along with isoalloLCA, reduces the immunostimulatory effects on DCs by acting on the FXR. IsoDCA induces extrathymic differentiation of RAR-related orphan receptor gamma (RORγt)+ Tregs cells by promoting Foxp3 induction and Tregs differentiation in a CNS1-dependent manner (). The 3-oxoLCA interacts directly with RORγt, a crucial transcription factor that promotes the development of Th17 cells, to hinder the process of Th17 cell differentiation. The degree of severity of the murine acute enteritis mouse model was improved with the treatment of 3-oxoLCA (). These findings emphasize the regulatory potential of bile acid metabolites from specific commensal taxa capable of 7α-dehydroxylation and epimerization.

Tryptophan metabolites, primarily produced by Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Clostridium species, are crucial in the formation of ILC3 (). AhR activation is necessary for the generation of IL-22 in ILC3s, which is facilitated by AhR ligands derived from the microbiota (; ). Tryptophan metabolites have a role in the regulation of mucosal immunity by affecting the activity of the AhR. The diminished AhR function in AhR knockout mice was associated with a decrease in ILC3 and an exacerbation of inflammatory disorders (). Disrupting tryptophan metabolism in the gut microbiota leads to a decrease in IL-22 levels in the intestines. On the other hand, activating the AhR in type 3 ILC3 stimulates the production of IL-22. This process helps regulate the intestine's immunological response and protects the integrity of the intestinal barrier. AhR also significantly impacts the differentiation of ILC3s (; ). AhR ligands are necessary for the development of IL-22-producing ILC3s, particularly in the early period after birth (). Functionally, AhR is involved in the formation of ILC3 through its interaction with Runx3 and RORγt. Additionally, AhR regulates the expression of Notch and c-Kit (; ; ). Significantly, decreased AhR signaling may lead to changes in the cellular populations of ILC3 and ILC1. Furthermore, AhR may induce the production of IL-22 in Th17 cells ().

Monocytes

Bile acid metabolites play a crucial role in maintaining the tolerogenic phenotype of macrophages via the bile acid receptor TGR5 (GPBAR1) (). TGR5 can suppress the production of cytokines by macrophages after exposure to LPS. The presence of TGR5 deficiency in mice might potentially enhance the inflammation caused by LPS in the liver, while the ability of a TGR5 agonist to reduce the levels of inflammatory mediators could be nullified (). TGR5 can inhibit inflammation that is reliant on the NLRP3 inflammasome, as shown by studies (; ). Actually, the TGR5 ligands and SBAs DCA and LCA may naturally limit the activation of NLRP3 by promoting TGR5-cAMP-PKA-dependent ubiquitination of NLRP3 (; ). Increased amounts of cAMP inside the cells may trigger the phosphorylation and ubiquitination of NLRP3, preventing NLRP3-dependent inflammation and metabolic problems associated with NLRP3. Activation of TGR5 also stimulates the polarization of macrophages toward a tolerogenic phenotype, as shown in Macs (). The order of hierarchy for TGR5 activation is as follows: LCA > deoxycholic acid (DCA) > chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) > ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA)> cholic acid (CA) (). Furthermore, FXR plays a crucial role in preserving the tolegeronic phenotype of Macs, as shown by studies on FXR knockout mice (; ). Additionally, FXR serves as a significant inhibitory regulator of NLRP3 by interacting directly with NLRP3 and caspase-1 (). The transcription factor FXR is able to stabilize nuclear receptor corepressor 1 (NCOR1) complexes on the promoters of iNOS (nitric oxide synthase) and IL-1β genes, which are attracted to the promoters of these genes (). A number of genes that are associated with inflammation, including iNOS, TNFα, and IL-1β, are characterized by NCoR1 in their promoter regions. These promoter areas are connected to an NF-κB-responsive element. By activating SOCS3 (suppressor of cytokine signaling 3), CYP450 (cytochrome P450), and FGF19 (fibroblast growth factor 19), FXR can reduce inflammation. Additionally, SHP (Src homology-2 containing protein tyrosine phosphatase) can inhibit NF-κB, AP-1 (activator protein-1), and NLRP3 (). There is also a nuclear receptor known as PXR that binds to LCA (). There is a possibility that activation of PXR might reduce the expression of IL6, TNFα, and IL8 ().

SCFAs, particularly butyrate, produced by commensal bacteria such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, also modulate monocyte/macrophage activity (). In a study, discovered that having access to SCFAs in the body leads to anti-inflammatory effects via controlling the synthesis of cytokines and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) by human monocytes. They found that the presence of these fatty acids led to a spike in the production of PGE2, indicating a synergistic effect when the fatty acids were incubated with LPS (). When cells were exposed to SCFAs after being primed with LPS, there was a decrease in IL-10 production. Additionally, a dose-dependent relationship was established between the reduction of C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) production and the reported effects. There is a connection between each of these discoveries and the cytokine profile. The findings obtained from peripheral blood mononuclear cells were similar, with the added benefit of a reduction in the production of TNF-α and IFN-γ that was caused by LPS (). On the other hand, different research indicated that SCFAs had a pro-inflammatory impact on human peripheral blood mononuclear cells. It was noted that this event occurred either when SCFAs were present independently or, in rare instances, when they were coupled with TLR agonists (). However, it has also been demonstrated that butyrate may block the migration of RAW264.7 cells and rat thioglycolate-elicited macrophages triggered by LPS (). The findings of research that was carried out by , who conducted a study that demonstrated that butyrate has anti-inflammatory effects on bone marrow, indicated that butyrate has the ability to reduce inflammation in macrophages that are produced from bone marrow. To be more specific, they discovered that butyrate could inhibit the synthesis of nitric oxide (NO), IL-6, and IL-12p40 in response to LPS, whereas it had no impact on the production of TNF-α or the cytokine CCL2 (). Propionate and acetate, unlike butyrate, did not demonstrate the same anti-inflammatory characteristics (). The findings that were obtained from the in vitro incubation of macrophages that originated from the colonic lamina propria with butyrate and LPS were similar to the results that were seen after the oral administration of this SCFA (). In their study, established that the mechanism of action of butyrate includes the inhibition of HDACs. In summary, microbiota-derived metabolites—including tryptophan catabolites, bile acids, and SCFAs—profoundly influence monocyte/macrophage biology through receptor-mediated, transcriptional, and epigenetic mechanisms.

Polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMN)

Neutrophils, the most abundant PMNs, are critical first responders in host defense and are tightly regulated by microbial signals (; ). Microbiota-derived SCFAs are responsible for regulating several neutrophil functions (). They affect the recruitment of neutrophils by influencing the production of inflammatory chemicals, such as IL-17 and TNF-α (). It has been found that the activation of the GPR43 receptor on neutrophils by SCFA is responsible for controlling neutrophil chemotaxis (; ). Furthermore, synergistic fatty acids have the potential to influence the actions of neutrophils, including their capacity for phagocytosis as well as their ability to generate and release ROS and NO (). However, it seems that the apoptosis caused by butyrate and propionate is not dependent on SCFA receptors. Instead, it is dependent on caspase activation rather than the Gαi/o and Gq pathways in both activated and non-activated neutrophils (). The antibacterial effect of butyrate is thought to be caused by an increase in the recruitment of neutrophils (). Furthermore, it was shown that SCFAs were specific in their ability to inhibit the production of NO by neutrophils (). Butyrate was shown to be effective in preventing the movement of neutrophils and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) (). These findings provide insight into the relevance of certain environmental cues that affect SCFA-related signaling pathways and the actions that are triggered by those pathways. SCFAs have been under increased examination in recent years () due to the potential therapeutic applications they offer in the treatment of a variety of ailments, particularly those associated with the dysfunction of neutrophils, notably inflammatory and autoimmune disorders. Both the efficiency of butyrate treatment in limiting the recruitment of myeloid and lymphoid cells into the colonic mucosa and the participation of immune cell penetration in an inflammatory response throughout the evolution of colitis have been thoroughly investigated in earlier research ().

Bile acids, specifically SBA LCA, can inhibit the release of IL-1β by the NLRP3 inflammasome, which ultimately results in a decrease in the recruitment of neutrophils during the inflammatory process (). Furthermore, mice that are lacking the FXR, which is the receptor that detects bile acids, have been shown to have enhanced osteopontin generation from Natural killer T (NKT) cells in the liver (; ). This, in turn, leads to an increase in neutrophil-mediated liver damage in a model of autoimmune hepatitis (; ). Throughout inflammatory processes, AhR ligands that are produced from the microbiota boost the immune-suppressive capabilities of Tregs, which in turn have an indirect effect on neutrophil recruitment and activation (; ). Even though it has been hypothesized that AhR ligands formed from the host might inhibit chemotaxis in neutrophils during inflammatory processes, it is yet unclear if microbiota-derived AhR ligands can directly connect with neutrophils (; ). In summary, commensal-derived microbial metabolites—particularly SCFAs, bile acids, and tryptophan catabolites—act through well-defined receptors to modulate neutrophil activity, suppress inflammation, and fine-tune host defense mechanisms.

Dendritic cells

Both butyrate and propionate have been shown to inhibit the formation of DCs from murine bone marrow cells (). To be more specific, murine bone marrow cells that have been cultivated in the presence of granulocyte-macrophage GM-CSF and either butyrate or propionate have a reduced ability to differentiate into CD11c+ cells when compared to cells that have been cultured in the absence of SCFAs (). It has been discovered that similar outcomes occur during the process of differentiation of monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs) from human monocytes (; ). This particular phenotype is dependent on SLC5A8 and GPR109A, and it has been discovered that cells lacking either of these receptors exhibit enhanced Bone marrow-derived DC (BMDC) differentiation, although it is not completely recovered (). However, more comprehensive genome-wide transcription and chromatin accessibility studies are needed to fully comprehend the mechanisms at play (). It is believed that butyrate and propionate hinder in vitro DC differentiation by controlling transcription factors essential for the development of DCs. This is accomplished through the inhibition of HDAC. For the formation of DC, the research made use of GM-CSF rather than Flt3 ligand (Flt3L), which is an important point to note. DCs that are formed in these cultures are unique from one another in terms of their development and phenotype, with the former aligning more closely to inflammatory DCs and the latter aligning more closely to steady-state DCs (; ). There is also a lack of clarity about whether or not SCFA administration affects the formation of Flt3L-dependent DCs in vitro. This is a significant issue in relation to diets that are rich in fiber and the effect that they have on the immune system (). SCFAs, in contrast to circumstances that are steady-state, encourage the production of DC bone marrow progenitor cells in vivo when inflammatory conditions are present. Propionate treatment of mice that were exposed to house dust mites increased the number of common DC progenitors (CDPs) and macrophage-DC progenitors (MDPs), which in turn promoted the formation of moDCs (). When mice are given a diet that is rich in fiber, it has also been reported that the number of MDPs increases (). Influenza infection, on the other hand, leads to an increase in the production of Ly6C-monocytes, which are responsible for developing alternatively activated macrophages rather than DCs. Interestingly, both findings reveal a dependence on the expression of GPR41, even though the result of microbiota-altered hematopoiesis is greatly reliant on the setting.

Several mechanisms may be responsible for the effects of SCFA on DC activation. Firstly, the activation of moDCs in the presence of SCFA (e.g., butyrate) leads to reduced extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) (). This behavior is comparable to that of moDCs that have been activated in the presence of glycolytic inhibitors, which raises the potential that butyrate may control glycolysis. The metabolic alterations that are caused by butyrate have also been established in colonocytes and T cells; however, the effects of butyrate on DCs have not yet been investigated (; ; ). GPRs are the means via which SCFAs may affect DCs. However, it has been shown that butyrate has the ability to inhibit the translocation of NF-κB to the nucleus in murine colonocytes and BMDCs via the GPR109A pathway. In the presence of butyrate, DCs that were stimulated with LPS would exhibit a decreased production of proinflammatory cytokines, which might be attributed to the impairment of NF-κB activation (; ). Thirdly, it is well-established that SCFAs block HDACs, which allows them to control the acetylation of histones as well as the acetylation of transcription factors. There is an increase in the amount of histone acetylation in moDCs that have been incubated in the presence of butyrate and, to a lesser degree, propionate (). Impairment of DC activation, which is defined as a reduction in the upregulation of costimulatory molecules, is found when DCs are treated with HDAC inhibitors in a manner that is comparable to the findings reported when DCs are treated with butyrate (). Based on this, it seems that the suppression of HDAC that is produced by butyrate may also contribute to decreased DC activation. The findings indicate that the microbial metabolites of healthy individuals have a tendency to reduce the amount of classical DC activation and also show a clear need for future research into precisely how the metabolites interact with and influence DCs, with a focus on the impacts on DC transcriptional, signaling, and metabolic profiles.

Platelets

Context-dependent effects of microbial metabolites on hematopoiesis and immunity

An important consideration in interpreting microbiota-derived metabolites is that their biological effects are not necessarily fixed or universally beneficial or detrimental. Rather, metabolite activity is determined by a combination of concentration, chemical identity, cellular target, receptor expression, disease stage, microbial source, and host biological background (). Thus, the same metabolite can produce distinct—and occasionally opposing—responses depending on the cellular and inflammatory environment in which it acts (). This context dependency is particularly evident for SCFAs. Although acetate, propionate, and butyrate are widely recognized for promoting immune tolerance and regulatory T-cell differentiation, their effects vary according to the specific SCFA, exposure level, receptor availability, and responding cell type (). For example, acetate and propionate can promote expansion of pre-existing colonic Tregs, whereas propionate and butyrate can preferentially support de novo differentiation of naïve T cells into Tregs, demonstrating that even closely related SCFAs can engage distinct biological programs (; ). In contrast, studies using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells have reported that SCFAs can, under certain experimental conditions, exert pro-inflammatory effects, whereas butyrate suppresses inflammatory mediator production in bone-marrow-derived macrophages (; ; ). The cellular target and receptor repertoire represent another major determinant of metabolite activity. SCFAs can signal through FFAR2/GPR43 and other G protein-coupled receptors while also exerting receptor-independent epigenetic effects through HDAC inhibition (). Consequently, cells expressing different combinations or levels of these receptors may respond differently to an identical metabolite concentration. This principle is particularly evident in dendritic-cell biology, where butyrate and propionate can inhibit dendritic-cell differentiation in vitro, whereas propionate can increase dendritic-cell progenitors under inflammatory conditions in vivo. The distinction between GM-CSF-dependent inflammatory DCs and Flt3L-dependent steady-state DCs further illustrates how cellular developmental context can determine the response to the same metabolite (; ). Metabolite concentration and systemic availability also influence biological outcome (). Local concentrations within the intestinal lumen may differ substantially from those reaching the circulation and bone marrow, while dietary, microbial, metabolic, and disease-associated changes can markedly alter metabolite availability (). Therefore, observations obtained using pharmacological concentrations in vitro should not automatically be extrapolated to physiological exposure in vivo. Indeed, the manuscript indicates that dietary interventions can markedly increase SCFA availability, emphasizing that the magnitude of exposure is an important component of metabolite-mediated signaling.

Disease stage and inflammatory status provide an additional layer of regulation. During homeostasis, microbial metabolites may support immune tolerance, tissue integrity, and balanced hematopoiesis, whereas during infection, transplantation, or chronic inflammation, the same signaling pathways may participate in emergency hematopoiesis or alter lineage commitment (). For example, SCFAs can suppress excessive inflammatory responses but may also enhance the production of dendritic-cell progenitors during inflammatory challenge (). Similarly, microbial signals that are required for appropriate emergency myelopoiesis during infection may become pathogenic when excessive or chronically activated. These observations indicate that metabolite effects should be interpreted in relation to the pre-existing inflammatory state rather than considered intrinsically anti- or pro-inflammatory. The microbial source and metabolic context are also critical (). A metabolite should not be considered independently of the microbial community that produces it, because different taxa possess distinct metabolic capacities and generate different concentrations, combinations, and ratios of metabolites. The findings therefore distinguish commensal-derived metabolites from products associated with pathobionts or dysbiotic communities. This distinction is particularly evident in hematopoiesis: commensal-derived SCFAs can participate in homeostatic regulation, whereas LPS derived from facultative pathobionts can suppress bone-marrow erythropoiesis through TLR4/MyD88 signaling in erythroblastic-island macrophages (). Similarly, dysbiosis may simultaneously decrease protective metabolites and increase inflammatory microbial products, thereby changing the overall signaling balance rather than simply producing a deficiency of a single beneficial metabolite. Finally, host genetic and biological background may influence metabolite responsiveness by determining receptor expression, intracellular signaling competence, metabolic capacity, and the basal state of hematopoietic and stromal cells (; ). This is particularly relevant for metabolites that act through genetically regulated sensing pathways such as AhR, FXR, TGR5, FFARs, and TLRs. The functional importance of host sensing is illustrated by the observation that loss of AhR activity is associated with HSC exhaustion and dysregulated hematopoiesis, whereas TGR5 deficiency can enhance inflammatory responses to LPS (). Thus, inter-individual differences in host sensing pathways may contribute to variability in responses to the same microbial metabolite. Collectively, these observations support a context-dependent model of microbiota–metabolite signaling, in which the biological outcome is determined by the interaction among metabolite identity and concentration, microbial origin, receptor and cellular target, host genetic and metabolic background, disease stage, and the pre-existing state of the hematopoietic niche. Accordingly, microbial metabolites should not be classified simply as “beneficial” or “harmful.” Instead, their effects should be interpreted as conditional outputs of a dynamic host–microbiota system. This framework provides a more mechanistic explanation for why alterations in the same metabolite pathway may produce different phenotypes across hematological disorders and why therapeutic manipulation of microbial metabolites will likely require disease-, dose-, cell-, and patient-specific strategies.

Disease-specific mechanisms linking microbial metabolites to hematological-associated pathogenesis

The contribution of gut microbiota-derived metabolites to hematological disease is best understood as a context-dependent interaction between microbial metabolic output, host sensing pathways, the bone marrow microenvironment, and the intrinsic state of hematopoietic cells. Although dysbiosis and altered metabolite profiles are observed across several hematological disorders, the downstream consequences differ according to the metabolite involved, its concentration and systemic availability, the responding cell type, receptor expression, and the inflammatory or malignant state of the hematopoietic niche (). Microbial metabolites can therefore influence disease through either direct regulation of hematopoietic cells or indirect remodeling of the cellular and inflammatory environment in which hematopoiesis occurs. Taken together, these disease models demonstrate that the same microbiota–metabolite axis can produce distinct pathological outcomes. In anemia, inflammatory microbial products suppress erythropoiesis through niche inflammation, whereas SCFAs can exert more direct regulatory effects on erythroid precursors (). In GVHD, reduced butyrate availability and increased TMAO can contribute to pathology through different mechanisms involving epithelial integrity and macrophage–T-cell inflammatory circuits (; ). In hematological malignancies, microbial metabolites may influence malignant-cell survival, immune surveillance, inflammatory signaling, and treatment response (). Thus, disease phenotype is determined not simply by whether a metabolite is increased or decreased, but by the interaction between metabolite identity, receptor/signaling pathway, cellular target, hematopoietic lineage, and the pre-existing state of the bone marrow niche. In recent years, significant focus has been given to the relationship between the human microbiota and blood cells, which are regulated by the generation of metabolites in different disorders (; ). Microbiota metabolites as a therapeutic target in blood cell-associated diseases

The therapeutic implications of microbiota-derived metabolites in hematopoietic disorders should be interpreted cautiously because the majority of mechanistic and intervention studies remain preclinical. Although manipulation of microbial communities, dietary substrates, specific metabolites, microbial biosynthetic pathways, or host receptors has shown beneficial effects in experimental models, these findings do not necessarily establish efficacy in humans. Accordingly, metabolite-based interventions discussed in this review should be considered investigational or hypothesis-generating unless supported by clinical evidence. Translation will require careful assessment of metabolite exposure, tissue distribution, receptor engagement, microbial composition and metabolic capacity, disease stage, treatment context, and potential off-target effects. Clinical studies are therefore needed to determine whether mechanisms demonstrated in experimental models can be safely and reproducibly exploited for therapeutic benefit in patients with hematopoietic disorders. In this regard, emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role of the gut microbiota and its metabolites in shaping the progression and treatment outcomes of hematologic-associated diseases. The gut microbiota contributes to immune system modulation, metabolic regulation, and mucosal barrier integrity—all factors that can significantly influence cancer development and response to therapy (). In hematologic cancers such as MM, butyrate has been shown to promote Treg expansion while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-17, which is associated with disease progression (). In addition, butyrate particularly inhibits HDACs, which are associated with regulating cellular proliferation and the cell cycle. The growing appreciation of gut microbiota-derived metabolites in regulating hematopoietic cell development has prompted significant interest in leveraging microbiota-targeted interventions as a therapeutic strategy (). These interventions—ranging from dietary modulation and probiotics to postbiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)—aim to restore or enhance beneficial microbial functions and thereby influence hematopoietic homeostasis or disease outcomes (; ). However, translating these strategies into clinical practice is fraught with several challenges that merit consideration. One major limitation is the strain-specific effect of probiotic organisms (). Even within a single species, such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus or Bifidobacterium longum, strains can vary significantly in their capacity to colonize the gut, produce bioactive metabolites (e.g., SCFAs or indole derivatives), and interact with host immune cells (). This heterogeneity complicates the standardization of probiotic therapies and may contribute to inconsistent clinical outcomes (). Moreover, the host's baseline microbiota composition can influence colonization resistance, further adding to variability in treatment response.

Another critical consideration is the bioavailability and distribution of microbial metabolites (). While compounds like butyrate, propionate, or tryptophan catabolites can exert potent immunomodulatory effects in vitro and in murine models, their physiological concentrations in specific niches such as the bone marrow or thymus remain unclear (). Many microbial metabolites are rapidly absorbed, degraded, or metabolized in the gut, which limits their systemic availability and therapeutic efficacy (). Hence, strategies to enhance metabolite stability or develop targeted delivery systems—such as encapsulation techniques or engineered microbes—are actively being explored (; ). However, while microbiota-targeted therapies hold transformative potential for modulating hematopoiesis and treating hematologic disorders, their clinical translation requires careful consideration of microbial ecology, metabolite pharmacokinetics, and personalized host-microbiome interactions ().

Translational relevance and limitations of experimental evidence

Although experimental studies have provided substantial mechanistic insight into microbiota–metabolite regulation of hematopoiesis, the available evidence should be interpreted according to the experimental system in which it was generated. A considerable proportion of mechanistic data concerning direct effects of microbial metabolites on HSPCs, hematopoietic lineage commitment, and bone-marrow niche remodeling originates from mouse or other animal models and in vitro systems. These studies are essential for establishing causality and identifying molecular pathways but do not necessarily demonstrate that identical mechanisms operate quantitatively or qualitatively in humans. For example, experimental evidence indicates that AhR activation by microbial metabolites can alter HSC proliferation and lineage bias, whereas loss of AhR signaling can promote HSC exhaustion; however, the extent to which this pathway produces the same cellular effects in human HSCs remains incompletely defined (; ). In contrast, several aspects of the microbiota–hematopoiesis relationship have been supported by human clinical or observational evidence. Reduced intestinal microbial diversity in patients undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is associated with poorer clinical outcomes and mortality, supporting the clinical relevance of microbiome disruption in hematopoietic transplantation (; ). Similarly, clinical observations indicate that dietary fiber intake can increase hemoglobin levels in individuals with anemia and can be accompanied by increased SCFA, particularly butyrate, concentrations in patients with renal anemia (). These findings support a relationship between diet, microbial metabolism, and hematological status, but they do not establish that the observed hematological effects are mediated exclusively by SCFAs. Human studies have also provided evidence for metabolite-related hematological effects in specific disease settings. For example, butyrate-related interventions have been investigated in individuals with hemoglobinopathies, including studies examining γ-globin and fetal hemoglobin induction (). Nevertheless, the presence of a clinical response in selected patients should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that the endogenous microbiota-derived butyrate pathway regulates hematopoiesis through the same mechanisms demonstrated experimentally. Accordingly, it is important to distinguish clinical association, experimental causality, and translational validation. Associations between microbial taxa or circulating metabolites and disease outcomes in humans provide evidence of clinical relevance but cannot independently establish causality. Conversely, animal and cellular models can demonstrate causal relationships and identify receptor-, pathway-, or cell-specific mechanisms, but their findings require confirmation in human systems. Several mechanisms discussed in this review should therefore currently be regarded as preclinical or hypothesis-generating rather than clinically established. This is particularly true for proposed direct effects of specific metabolites on abnormal HSPCs and for metabolite-mediated remodeling of the bone-marrow niche in diseases such as myelodysplastic syndromes, where the causal contribution of individual microbial metabolites remains insufficiently defined. Future studies should prioritize longitudinal human cohorts, quantitative metabolomics, functional microbiome profiling, ex vivo studies using human HSPCs and bone-marrow niche cells, and prospective clinical trials to determine whether experimentally defined pathways are reproducible in patients. Thus, the current evidence supports a model in which the biological plausibility and mechanistic framework are strongly supported by experimental research, whereas the clinical relevance of several pathways is supported by human associations and selected clinical observations but remains incompletely validated at the mechanistic level. Explicitly separating these evidence levels is essential for translating microbiota-derived metabolite biology into reliable therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion and future directions

The interactions of microbiota metabolites and hematopoiesis have been identified as an interesting topic to study in terms of immune homeostasis regulation and hematopoietic function. Recent findings show that bacterial metabolites play a direct part in the regulation of the hematopoietic system by regulating proliferation, differentiation, and functions of immune cells. The increase in SCFA production caused by microbial fermentation due to dietary fiber promotes the generation of DC precursors in the bone marrow; however, at the same time, it lowers their ability to induce a Th2 reaction, thus preventing allergic airway inflammation. Moreover, SCFAs, for example, propionate, can be considered a therapeutic agent in autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus via modulation of megakaryocyte functions and regulation of inflammatory cytokine production. Besides the influence on hematopoiesis and inflammatory diseases, recent data highlight the involvement of microbiota metabolites in the regulation of anticancer immunity. Moreover, indoles and IPA, as metabolic products of the gut microbiota, contribute to the enhancement of cytotoxicity of γδ T cells that can be used for improvement of cancer immunotherapy. Thus, the existing data reveal the fundamental role of bacterial metabolites in the regulation of hematopoietic and immune system processes. Further investigation of the interaction between microbiota and hematopoiesis can help understand the molecular background of the interaction and identify new targets for drug development.

Of note, the complexity of the microbiota–metabolite–hematopoiesis axis necessitates approaches capable of resolving cellular, spatial, metabolic, and microbial heterogeneity simultaneously. Conventional microbiome profiling can identify associations between microbial taxa and hematological phenotypes, but taxonomic abundance alone cannot determine which microbial functions are active, which metabolites are produced, or which hematopoietic and niche cells respond to these signals. Emerging single-cell and multi-omics technologies therefore offer an important opportunity to move from descriptive associations toward cell-specific and mechanistically resolved models of microbiota–hematopoiesis communication. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and related single-cell multi-omic approaches could identify heterogeneous responses among HSPCs and mature hematopoietic populations following changes in microbial metabolites. By resolving transcriptional states at single-cell resolution, these approaches may reveal metabolite-responsive cellular subsets, lineage-specific signaling programs, and changes in differentiation trajectories that are obscured in bulk tissue analyses. This is particularly relevant because microbial metabolites can influence hematopoiesis both directly through HSPCs and indirectly through stromal, endothelial, macrophage, and other niche populations.

Metabolomics provides a complementary functional layer by enabling quantitative characterization of circulating and tissue-associated microbial metabolites. Measurement of SCFAs, tryptophan derivatives, bile acids, TMAO, lactate, and other microbial products alongside hematopoietic phenotypes could determine whether changes in microbial abundance are accompanied by corresponding alterations in metabolic output. This distinction is critical because dysbiosis can involve both depletion of potentially protective metabolites and accumulation of inflammatory microbial products. Integration of microbiome multi-omics with host molecular profiling could provide an even more comprehensive framework. Shotgun metagenomics can characterize the genetic potential of microbial communities, whereas metatranscriptomics and metabolomics can provide information regarding microbial pathway activity and actual metabolic output. When these datasets are integrated with single-cell transcriptomic, epigenomic, and clinical data, it may become possible to construct mechanistic links between a microbial pathway, its metabolic product, the host receptor or signaling pathway activated, and the specific hematopoietic or niche cell affected. These approaches may distinguish conserved mechanisms from patient-specific responses, identify metabolic pathways that are causally linked to hematopoietic dysfunction, and reveal biomarkers capable of predicting therapeutic response. Such an integrated strategy could shift the field from identifying “which bacteria are associated with disease” toward determining which microbial functions and metabolites act on which cells, within which anatomical niches, at which stages of disease, and with what biological consequences. This level of resolution will be essential for translating microbiota-derived metabolite biology into precise therapeutic strategies.
