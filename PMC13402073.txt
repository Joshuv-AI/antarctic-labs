Neuromuscular Electrostimulation and Microvascular Perfusion in Chronic Lower‐Limb Ulcers: A Systematic Review
Authors: Marie‐Therese Targett, Duncan Shirreffs Bain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImpaired microcirculation contributes to delayed healing in venous, arterial, diabetic and mixed‐aetiology ulcers. Activation of the leg muscle pump by intermittent neuromuscular electrostimulation of the common peroneal nerve has been proposed to improve microvascular perfusion. This review examines current evidence for neuromuscular electrostimulation effecting changes in microvascular flux in chronic lower‐limb ulcers of all aetiologies, as measured by Laser Speckle Contrast Imaging. Eligible studies were limited to human research evaluating the effects on chronic lower‐limb ulcers of any aetiology. Four eligible studies (total n = 44) were identified. In venous ulcers, neuromuscular electrostimulation increased wound bed flux by 28.5% (p < 0.05), and pulsatility by 126% (p < 0.05). In arterial leg ulcers, flux increased by 60.9% (p < 0.02) and pulsatility by 161% (p < 0.02). In mixed aetiology ulcers, wound bed flux increased by 38.2% (p < 0.05) and pulsatility by 214% (p < 0.05). In diabetic foot ulcers, flux increased by 40.2% (p < 0.01) and pulsatility by 406% (p < 0.01). Across all ulcer types combined, wound‐bed flux increased by 37.6% (p < 0.001) and pulsatility by 198% (p < 0.001). There is compelling evidence that intermittent neuromuscular stimulation of the common peroneal nerve enhances microcirculatory perfusion in the wound bed of chronic leg and foot ulcers. These consistent physiological improvements support its potential as an adjunctive therapy.

Introduction

Chronic lower‐limb ulcers, including venous leg ulcers (VLU), diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), arterial ulcers (ALU) and those of mixed aetiology (MAU), represent a persistent and costly challenge for health services worldwide [1, 2, 3]. These wounds are typically characterised by impaired microcirculation, local hypoxia and delayed healing, particularly in older adults and individuals with multiple comorbidities. As a result, they contribute significantly to prolonged patient morbidity, reduced quality of life and high healthcare expenditure [1, 2, 3].

Optimising tissue perfusion is a key therapeutic goal in promoting effective wound healing. Whilst compression therapy and physical activity can improve vascular flow, many patients with chronic wounds are unable to tolerate or participate in these interventions due to frailty, pain, immobility or vascular compromise. In this context, neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) offers a promising alternative. One hertz NMES of the common peroneal nerve has been shown to augment blood flow and velocity in the venous, arterial and microvascular systems [4, 5, 6], and to accelerate healing in VLU [7]. It achieves these effects by externally stimulating the lower‐limb muscle pump through hands‐free, non‐invasive electrical pulses requiring no active effort from the patient.

This review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (PROSPERO 2026 CRD420261324429, available from https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420261324429) and evaluates the current evidence on the effects of 1 Hz NMES of the common peroneal nerve on microvascular perfusion in chronic lower‐limb ulcers, as assessed by Laser Speckle Contrast Imaging (LSCI). It specifically explores whether 1 Hz NMES improves microvascular flux and pulsatility in the wound bed and periphery, across different ulcer types and discusses the physiological and clinical implications of these findings. This review has been reported according to PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines.

Methods

Search Strategy

A targeted literature search was conducted using the PubMed, EMBASE and Cochrane Central databases, using keywords: ‘NMES’ AND ‘chronic ulcers’ AND ‘LSCI’. No filters were applied. Eligible studies were limited to human research evaluating the effects of NMES on chronic lower‐limb ulcers of any aetiology, provided they used LSCI to measure changes in microvascular flux and/or pulsatility. Sources were last accessed on 1 April 2026. The search yielded four eligible studies, screened by two independent reviewers to meet the review inclusion criteria. Search strategy flowchart.

The four observational studies included in this review varied slightly in their design but shared several methodological features [8, 9, 10, 11]. All studies utilised within‐subject, repeated‐measures designs and employed LSCI to capture microvascular perfusion data before and during 1 Hz NMES intervention. Overall risk of bias in all four studies was assessed by ROBINS‐I to be low, and low risk for all six sub‐domains considered within ROBINS‐I, including low risk of confounding owing to the self‐controlled model, and the quantitative nature of the endpoints.

Total participant number across all studies was 44 chronic lower limb ulcer patients, with individual sample sizes ranging from 8 to 14. Most participants were older adults, reflective of the typical chronic wound population and had at least one major comorbidity such as diabetes, peripheral arterial disease or venous insufficiency. Outcomes sought from these studies comprised wound bed flux, wound periphery flux, wound bed pulsatility and wound periphery pulsatility.

In the Das et al. [11] study on VLU, participants had chronic ulcers that had not responded to compression therapy. The study by Bosanquet et al. [8] focused on patients with clinically diagnosed ALU, confirmed via ankle‐brachial pressure index and duplex scanning. For DFU, Bosanquet et al. [9] included patients with type 2 diabetes and peripheral neuropathy, including two with neuroischaemic ulcers, whilst Harding et al. [10] examined patients with MAU, combining features of both venous and arterial insufficiency.

All studies received ethical approval and informed consent was obtained from participants.

Intervention

Each study applied NMES using the Geko device (Firstkind Ltd, Dresbury, UK), a compact, wearable unit positioned over the common peroneal nerve (Figure 2). Delivering low‐frequency (1 Hz) electrical pulses, the device induces involuntary lower‐leg muscle contractions that enhance microvascular blood flow in a non‐invasive, hands‐free manner.

Application of the device for lower limb wound management, showing common peroneal nerve A, and location mark on device B.

The device is positioned over the common peroneal nerve (A), with the alignment marker (B) placed above the fibular head. Accurate anatomical placement ensures effective stimulation to activate the calf muscle pump. The device delivers NMES at 1 Hz to enhance blood flow and support wound healing. Control buttons on the device allow adjustment of the stimulation intensity setting, so that effective muscle contraction (verified by a visible twitch of the foot) can be achieved without excessive movement.

Stimulation was typically applied for 30–60 min in a single session. LSCI measurements were taken under controlled room temperatures to minimise external influence on skin perfusion. Whilst all studies reported significant improvements in perfusion parameters, none included an external control group or employed blinding. Instead, participants served as their own controls in within‐subject designs. This limits internal validity and increases the potential for observer bias, though the use of objective LSCI imaging helps reduce the risk of measurement bias.

Endpoints

LSCI is a validated, non‐invasive imaging technique used to assess changes in microcirculatory blood flow, particularly in areas where vessels are superficial [12]. It generates colour‐coded maps of tissue perfusion, enabling detailed visualisation of both wound‐bed and peri‐wound regions. This makes it a valuable method for evaluating the physiological effects of interventions such as NMES. Results

In all studies, the LSCI values for each subject with the device switched on were compared with LSCI values with the device switched off, over multiple cycles allowing for pair‐wise comparison. Statistical analysis by paired t‐test demonstrated significant improvements in both wound‐bed and peri‐wound perfusion across all studies: VLU: wound bed flux increased by 28.5% and peri‐wound flux by 37.1%; whereas wound bed pulsatility rose by 125.7% and peri‐wound pulsatility by 187.0% (all p < 0.05). MAU: wound bed flux increased by 38.2% and peri‐wound flux by 19.7%; whereas wound bed pulsatility rose by 214.1% and peri‐wound pulsatility by 122.5% (all p < 0.05). DFU (neuropathic): wound bed flux increased by 36.0% and peri‐wound flux by 92.2%; whereas wound bed pulsatility rose by 182.7% and peri‐wound pulsatility by 359.7% (all p < 0.01). ALU: wound bed flux increased by 60.9% and peri‐wound flux by 27.2%; whereas wound bed pulsatility rose by 392.0% and peri‐wound pulsatility by 161.1% (all p ≤ 0.02). Combined data (all aetiologies): wound bed flux increased by 37.6% and peri‐wound flux by 43.4%; whereas wound bed pulsatility rose by 197.7% and peri‐wound pulsatility by 223.6% (all p < 0.0005).

One study [11] differed from the others in the presentation of results. In all cases, a value for perfusion was calculated for each individual patient with and without NMES. This allowed a value for each patient to be calculated for the difference between with NMES and without NMES. For three studies, these data were aggregated by presenting the average (mean) of these differences. However, in the case of Das et al. [11], an average (mean) value was calculated for all patients with NMES and a corresponding mean for without NMES, and the mean difference was then presented as the difference of those two means. For the purpose of meta‐analysis, the original Das et al. data has been retrieved and reprocessed in this review to present mean difference in the same form. This gives a slightly different value for mean difference from that originally published, but does not affect the p value, as the paired t‐test is still conducted on the same data in the same way.

Although all four studies demonstrated significant improvements in microvascular perfusion, the magnitude of response varied by ulcer type. ALU showed the most pronounced increases in both flux (+60.9%) and pulsatility (+392.0%), consistent with greater baseline ischaemia. MAU showed more modest changes, particularly in peri‐wound flux (+19.7%), possibly due to combined arterial and venous dysfunction. DFU (neuropathic and neuroischaemic) had the highest peri‐wound flux increase (+103.8%), whilst VLU showed substantial pulsatility improvements (~170%), indicating that NMES can enhance perfusion even in the context of venous hypertension. Wide confidence intervals reported in some studies, such as for DFU wound bed pulsatility (+195.5% to +1359.4%), highlight inter‐individual variability and underscore the need for better patient stratification in future research.

Discussion

This review found that 1 Hz NMES of the common peroneal nerve consistently improved microcirculatory perfusion in chronic lower‐limb ulcers across a range of aetiologies. Statistically significant increases in both flux and pulsatility were observed. The inclusion of 95% confidence intervals in several studies enhances the reliability of the findings by indicating both the precision and the degree of variability in the observed effects. Across all studies, NMES enhanced wound‐bed and peri‐wound perfusion, with pulsatility increases ranging from 122% to 452% and flux increases from 19% to 92%.

The substantial increases in pulsatility reflect the NMES‐induced lower‐limb muscle contractions, which pump blood intermittently through small vessels. This might be expected to result in a rise in endothelial shear stress, stimulating the release of nitric oxide (NO), a key vasodilator. These effects mirror those seen with moderate exercise, potentially aiding perfusion restoration in ischemic tissues. Such NO‐mediated mechanisms are supported by evidence from vascular and exercise physiology literature [13, 14].

LSCI was an effective, non‐invasive method for visualising perfusion changes. Unlike Doppler or transcutaneous oxygen assessment, it provides non‐contact colour coded tissue perfusion images and allows precise quantification and mapping of both wound‐bed and peri‐wound perfusion in real time.

NMES may be especially valuable for patients who cannot tolerate compression or exercise therapies. Its wearable, hands‐free design makes it suitable for home or outpatient settings, with potential benefits for elderly or frail individuals.

Limitations

All studies were small‐scale and short term, without patient‐reported outcomes. Long‐term clinical effects such as healing rates, infections and recurrence were not addressed. Further study would be required to extrapolate the findings to clinical endpoints. Nonetheless, the consistent improvements across ulcer types, combined with the non‐invasive nature and physiological plausibility of NMES, support its further investigation and recommendation as an adjunctive therapy when standard of care is not achieving healing. Larger, randomised controlled trials or self‐controlled trials should assess its clinical efficacy, patient benefit and cost‐effectiveness.

Conclusion

Intermittent NMES (1 Hz) of the common peroneal nerve significantly enhances microvascular perfusion in and around the wound bed, in chronic lower‐limb ulcers across multiple aetiologies. These effects, quantified using LSCI, are likely to enhance tissue oxygenation, deliver improved nutrition and promote local conditions conducive to wound healing. NMES offers a physiologically plausible, non‐invasive intervention that may restore perfusion in ischaemic wounds.

Clinically, NMES provides a practical, hands‐free solution for patients unable to tolerate compression or exercise‐based therapies. Its ease of use supports deployment in both clinical and community settings, including among elderly or frail populations. Patients with arterial insufficiency or diabetic neuropathy may derive particular benefit.

To validate these findings and guide implementation, future research should focus on well‐designed, randomised controlled trials with standard‐care control groups (e.g., compression, exercise, usual dressings), larger study populations and long‐term follow‐up. Outcomes should include both clinical endpoints (healing, infection, recurrence) and patient‐reported measures (pain, mobility, quality of life). Cost‐effectiveness and integration with other wound therapies should also be evaluated.
