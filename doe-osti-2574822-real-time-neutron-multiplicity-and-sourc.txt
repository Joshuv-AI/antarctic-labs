 SUMMARY

Advancing neutron detection and analysis techniques for complex radiation environments

is an ongoing focus in nuclear instrumentation and monitoring. This proposal presents

research and development of a generalized real-time neutron monitoring and analysis

system, applicable to any detector capable of producing time-tagged neutron count data.

While the work is demonstrated using the Neutron Multiplication Analysis Detector

(NoMAD), a modular 15-tube helium-3 (He-3) array, due to its availability, spatial

resolution, and flexible deployment, the methods developed are extensible to other

systems, including organic scintillators and fast digital detectors. This research investigates

two complementary analytical techniques for real-time characterization of neutron-

emitting sources: neutron multiplicity estimation based on the Hage-Cifarelli formalism

and spatial localization using supervised machine learning applied to spatial count rate

patterns. These methods are designed to operate under dynamic, evolving conditions such

as fuel debris retrieval or reactor startup, where neutron-emitting material geometries may

be partially unknown or changing over time. By integrating statistical neutron emission

data with spatial localization, this research aims to develop and evaluate methods for real-

time neutron monitoring, source characterization, and material verification. Key

contributions include implementation of a low-latency data pipeline for continuous neutron

multiplicity analysis, development and validation of machine learning models for spatial

inference, and experimental evaluation of system performance under variable measurement

conditions. The outcomes are intended to support applications in nuclear safeguards,

verification, emergency response, and reactor startup.

 CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION

Neutron detection and analysis provide a direct means of probing neutron-emitting

materials and their behavior through measurements of neutron production, transport, and

interaction processes. Because neutrons are the drivers of fission chain reactions, their

emission patterns reflect the multiplication behavior of fissile material, providing

information about source strength, reactivity, and material configuration. Measuring the

rate and time correlations of neutron detections allows extraction of statistical parameters,

such as multiplicity moments, that characterize the underlying fission dynamics. This

approach is particularly valuable in environments where the material geometry is irregular,

evolving, or only partially known, as it enables real-time assessment of neutron emission

behavior beyond simple count rate measurements. By leveraging time-correlated neutron

data, neutron monitoring systems can provide a more complete picture of material behavior

under dynamic conditions.

 In fissile systems, neutron emission arises from a combination of spontaneous

fission, induced fission, and other neutron producing reactions, such as (α,n) reactions,

photoneutron production (γ,n), and (n,2n) threshold reactions. The relative contributions of

these processes depend on the isotopic composition, neutron multiplication, external

neutron sources, and surrounding materials. In fissile systems with low multiplication (keff

<< 1), Spontaneous fission may dominate neutron production, resulting in emission

patterns that are uncorrelated in time due to the independent nature of each fission event.

In contrast, as multiplication increases and keff approaches unity, induced fission

contributes a growing fraction of neutron production, leading to longer fission chains,

 1

sequences in which a neutron from one fission event induces subsequent fissions in a

cascading process, and greater temporal correlations between detected neutrons. These

correlations manifest as deviations from Poisson counting statistics in the distribution of

detected neutrons over time intervals1. Measuring and analyzing these time correlations

provides insight into the neutron multiplication behavior, neutron lifetime, and underlying

chain reaction dynamics of the system. Higher-order statistical analyses, such as the

variance-to-mean ratio (Feynman-Y), factorial moments, and normalized second moments,

quantify the degree of neutron correlation and are sensitive to changes in system

multiplication2.

 Figure 1. Top down schematic of the NoMAD detector

 Neutron monitoring systems have historically been designed to process data

retrospectively, aggregating detection events into time-averaged counts or apply statical

analyses after acquisition is complete. While highly effective, these approaches inherently

operate on data collected over fixed intervals, producing results that reflect a snapshot of a

system’s behavior at some point in the past rather than a continuous, time-resolved

evolution. In dynamic measurement environments, such as those encountered during fuel

debris retrieval, material handling, or configurations involving unknown or evolving

geometries, there is a need for systems capable of processing neutron detection data in real

 2

time, providing real-time estimates of neutron emission characteristics as material

conditions change. This research focuses on developing a real-time neutron monitoring and

analysis system that integrates both neutron multiplicity analysis and source localization

into a continuous data processing pipeline.

 Figure 2. Real-Time Multiplicity and Localization Analysis Pipeline

 To demonstrate the developed methodology, this research uses the Neutron

Multiplication Analysis Detector (NoMAD) due to its flexible geometry, spatial

segmentation, and availability of time-resolved count data. However, the analytical

framework is not specific to NoMAD. The real-time multiplicity analysis and machine

learning–based spatial localization methods are designed to be detector-agnostic and may

be applied to any system capable of producing list-mode neutron detection data. This

includes PSD-capable organic scintillators, silicon photomultiplier arrays, and other

digitized neutron detection systems. NoMAD serves as a well-characterized, accessible

platform for validating the approach.

 3

 A central analytical method employed in this neutron monitoring system is the Hage-

Cifarelli formalism3, a probabilistic model that describes the distribution of detected

neutron counts under conditions of correlated emission. The Hage-Cifarelli model extends

beyond the assumptions of purely Poisson or negative binomial statistics by explicitly

accounting for the effects of neutron multiplication on the counting distribution. Its use

enables estimation of key parameters—such as the mean and variance of neutron

multiplicity—directly from observed count data over finite time intervals. This formalism

is particularly well suited to environments where the neutron source strength and

multiplication may vary or be partially unknown, as it provides a framework that relates

observed count distributions to underlying physical processes without requiring explicit

knowledge of system geometry, material composition, or neutron transport paths. The

Hage-Cifarelli formalism achieves this by modeling neutron detection as a compound

Poisson process, in which each source event (e.g., a spontaneous or induced fission)

initiates a stochastic branching process resulting in a variable number of detected neutrons.

The formalism derives the probability distribution of observed counts by combining the

source emission statistics with the probability generating function of the branching process,

allowing the overall counting distribution to reflect both the initial emission rate and the

neutron multiplication factor. By fitting this probability model to experimental count data,

the underlying parameters of the multiplication process, such as the mean number of

neutrons per fission and the variance introduced by chain reactions, can be inferred even

when the system’s physical configuration is incompletely characterized.

 In addition to multiplicity analysis, this research develops a spatial localization

method that uses supervised machine learning to estimate the position of neutron-emitting

 4

sources based on spatial count rate data. The approach is demonstrated using data from the

NoMAD detector array, selected for its availability, spatial segmentation, and well-

characterized response. However, the method is not specific to NoMAD and can be applied

to other neutron detector arrays capable of providing spatially distributed count rate

measurements. A Random Forest regression model4 is trained to map spatial patterns of

count rates across the detector array to corresponding source coordinates, learning the

nonlinear relationships imposed by neutron transport, scattering, absorption, and detector

geometry. Each tree in the ensemble learns a set of decision rules that partition the feature

space based on localized variations in count rates, capturing the gradients induced by

proximity effects and shielding asymmetries. The model is trained on a dataset of simulated

or experimentally measured count patterns corresponding to known source positions,

allowing it to generalize predictions for new, unknown source configurations. By

incorporating spatial inference into the analysis pipeline, the system leverages both the

statistical information encoded in time-correlated neutron detections and the geometric

information embedded in the spatial distribution of count rates. This dual analysis enables

simultaneous estimation of neutron multiplicity parameters and source location, providing

a richer and more multidimensional characterization of neutron-emitting materials than

achievable through either multiplicity or localization analysis alone. The integration of

these methods supports dynamic monitoring in environments where both the source

intensity and position may evolve over time, offering the potential for real-time spatial and

temporal tracking of changes in neutron-emitting material configurations.

 By integrating both multiplicity analysis and spatial inference into a unified, real-

time data processing system, this research advances the capability to monitor neutron-

 5

emitting materials dynamically as conditions evolve. The system leverages the Hage-

Cifarelli formalism for continuous estimation of neutron multiplicity parameters alongside

a machine learning–based spatial localization model trained on detector count rate patterns,

enabling simultaneous characterization of both the temporal and spatial properties of

neutron sources. This real-time processing architecture allows the system to provide

continuously updated insights into neutron emission behavior, supporting interpretation of

changes in material configuration, moderation, or source strength over short timescales.

 The integration of statistical modeling, neutron transport physics, detector response,

and computational methods represents a convergence of complementary approaches,

combining theory and data-driven techniques to address the challenges of real-time neutron

monitoring. Experimental validation will play a critical role in this process, providing

empirical evidence of the system’s ability to capture both multiplicity parameters and

spatial localization across varied source configurations, geometries, and moderating

environments. Through systematic testing under controlled and perturbed conditions, the

performance of the analysis pipeline will be assessed in terms of accuracy, precision,

robustness, and sensitivity to key factors such as count rate, background, and detector

placement. These results will not only quantify the practical capabilities and limitations of

the system, but will also inform opportunities for refinement and deeper understanding of

the interplay between physical measurement conditions and analytical interpretation.

 Ultimately, this work seeks not only to develop a novel monitoring tool, but to

deepen the understanding of how statistical and spatial signatures of neutron emission can

be used together to better inform reactivity in dynamic, complex environments.

1.1 Technical Questions

This research aims to develop a real-time neutron monitoring and analysis system that

integrates neutron multiplicity analysis and spatial localization to characterize neutron-

emitting materials under dynamic conditions. To achieve this, the following technical

questions are addressed:

1. What are the theoretical and operational limits of real-time neutron multiplicity

 analysis using the Hage-Cifarelli formalism in dynamic measurement

 environments, particularly when applied to specific detector systems such as He-

 3 arrays? How can this framework be adapted to support continuous inference in

 systems with changing geometries or reactivities, and how might it be generalized

 or extended to other detector modalities in future implementations?

 This component will implement the Hage-Cifarelli formalism in a real-time data

 processing pipeline capable of estimating neutron multiplicity parameters from real-

 time list-mode detection data. A sliding-window to time-binned approach will be used

 to extract factorial moments and compute multiplicity parameters under dynamic

 conditions. The system will be evaluated using both simulated and experimentally

 acquired data reflecting a range of count rates, background levels, neutron source

 configurations, and neutron multiplication. Analysis will focus on identifying the

 conditions under which the formalism yields stable, interpretable results – particularly

 in the presence of fluctuating source strengths, evolving geometry, or varying neutron

 multiplication. Performance will be assessed in terms of accuracy, statistical

 uncertainty, temporal resolution, and resilience to deviations from model assumptions,

 7

 with the goal of defining the practical limits of real-time multiplicity monitoring in

 complex environments.

2. To what extent can supervised machine learning models, when applied to He-3

 detector arrays, infer source localization from spatial count rate distributions in

 environments with limited geometric knowledge—and how can these models be

 trained or adapted to generalize across diverse measurement scenarios and

 potentially other detector technologies?

 A machine learning model is being developed to infer neutron source location from

 spatially distributed detector count rates. Training data will be generated through a

 combination of simulation and controlled experimental measurements that vary

 conditions such as source position, count rate, and shielding/moderation. A Random

 Forest regression model will be trained to predict source coordinates from count rate

 features, with generalization evaluated using hold-out test configurations. Metrics for

 model performance will include localization error, robustness to environmental

 variation, and uncertainty quantification. Special emphasis will be placed on training

 strategies that account for the underlying physics of neutron transport, such as feature

 engineering that reflects inverse-square distance behavior or moderator effects. Success

 will be defined by the model’s ability to localize sources within defined spatial

 uncertainty bounds across a range of representative configurations.

3. Under what conditions does combining multiplicity and localization reduce

 ambiguity or uncertainty in neutron emission analysis, compared to using either

 method alone?

 8

 This portion of research explores whether the combined used of multiplicity and

 localization analysis improves the ability to infer spatial and statistical properties of

 neutron-emitting sources. Scenarios will be constructed where temporal correlations

 provide multiplicity signatures, and spatial gradients encode source position – each

 contributing partial information. The research will evaluate how the joint analysis

 resolves ambiguities such as distinguishing between two low-rate sources versus one

 higher-multiplying source or identifying changes in source location versus changes in

 reactivity. Quantitative comparisons will assess whether combining both methods

 reduces uncertainty in source location estimates, improves the stability of multiplicity

 measurements, and increases the system’s ability to detect changes in source position

 or neutron emission behavior in real time.

4. Under what measurement conditions do real-time multiplicity and localization

 methods fail or lose interpretability, and how can experiments be designed to

 probe these limits?

 Experimental validation will be conducted using nuclear material selected to represent

 a range of emission rates, geometries, and multiplications. Measurements will be

 acquired with a fixed detector array, with the source systematically moved to

 predefined positions and configurations. This data will be used to assess the system’s

 real-time performance in both multiplicity analysis and source localization, evaluating

 sensitivity to source strength, position, and environmental variation. Observed

 discrepancies between predicted and actual outcomes will inform refinement of both

 statistical and machine learning models. The goal is to use well-controlled experimental

 testbeds to identify practical limitations, validate system functionality, and build a

 9

 foundation for eventual deployment in more complex, heterogeneous, or operationally

 constrained environments.

 CHAPTER 2. TECHNICAL CHALLENGES

2.1 Real-Time Multiplicity Analysis for Dynamic Environments

To determine the theoretical and operational limits of real-time neutron multiplicity

analysis using the Hage-Cifarelli formalism, we aim to address several challenges related

to statistical modeling, system dynamics, and uncertainty propagation. The Hage-Cifarelli

model, originally developed for static or time-integrated data, describes detected neutron

count distributions as a compound Poisson process driven by stochastic fission chains3,5.

This project adapts the formalism for real-time use by applying it to time-binned count data

acquired from He-3 detector arrays using a sliding-window approach.

To further improve interpretability and stability under dynamic conditions, we will also

explore methods for incorporating temporal continuity into the analysis. Rather than

treating each sliding window independently, the system will track a rolling history of

previous parameter estimates and moment statistics. These prior estimates will serve as

empirical priors or baseline expectations for the current window, allowing for smoothing,

trend tracking, and the suppression of transient statistical noise. This adaptive approach is

particularly valuable in marginal-count regimes, where a single window may produce

unstable results, but a time series of estimates can reveal consistent patterns. The system

will be designed to weigh past and present information dynamically, balancing

 10

responsiveness to sudden changes with robustness against random fluctuations. Over time,

this continuous updating framework is expected to yield increasingly accurate and reliable

characterizations of the evolving neutron field, enabling not just real-time monitoring, but

also learning and refinement throughout an operation.

Within each analysis window, the system calculates the first several factorial moments of

the count distribution and uses these to estimate physical parameters such as the source

emission rate and the mean and variance of neutron multiplicity of the system. It also

computes classical metrics such as the Feynman-Y quantity and average count rate across

the gate width, which provide additional sensitivity to neutron correlations. The primary

technical challenge is ensuring that these estimates remain stable and interpretable under

conditions of low count statistics, which are typical for short time windows or low-flux

environments. The limits of moment stability will be assessed through experimental

datasets by varying the window size, count rate, and background level, thereby defining

the minimum conditions required for reliable inference.

A key innovation in this work is the development of a new uncertainty quantification

framework for real-time multiplicity analysis. In current implementations, uncertainties on

parameters are calculated by propagating the standard deviations of factorial moments

independently. While this approach captures basic statistical variation, it does not account

for the inherent correlations between moments, which can lead to conservative or inflated

uncertainty estimates, especially in low-count regimes. To improve upon this, the proposed

framework will estimate the full covariance matrix between factorial moments in each time

window. This additional structure will allow for more accurate propagation of uncertainty

into derived multiplicity parameters, reducing overestimation. For example, parameters

 11

such as the Feynman-Y value depend jointly on the first and second moments of the count

distribution. The total uncertainty in such parameters is governed not only by the variances

Including the covariance term enables tighter, more reliable confidence intervals and

Another significant challenge lies in recognizing when the assumptions of the Hage-

Cifarelli formalism no longer hold. The model assumes a single-dominant neutron emitting

source and stationary emission statistics, which may be violated by time-varying

moderation, geometry changes, or the presence of multiple sources3. This work will

implement real-time diagnostic checks, such a monitoring moment ratios, to identify when

the observed behavior deviates from that expected under the compound Poisson

framework. When such deviations occur, the system can flag the affected data for exclusion

or adaptive reinterpretation. These checks will be critical for defining the formalism’s

boundaries of validity and guiding real-time decisions in fielded systems.

While this effort is focused on He-3 arrays in the form of the NoMAD detector, the

formalism is fundamentally detector-agnostic. The Hage-Cifarelli method, the real-time

uncertainty estimation algorithm, and the adaptive windowing algorithms are applicable to

any neutron detection system capable of providing timestamped count data. Potential

 12

extensions include plastic scintillators with pulse shape discrimination (PSD) capabilities,

fast neutron time-of-flight systems, or silicon-based detection arrays. The NoMAD

detector is used in this work for the sake of availability, convenience, and prior calibration,

which allow for rapid experimental validation and comparison of analysis performance.

By addressing the core challenges of windowed multiplicity inference, real-time

uncertainty propagation, adaptive temporal modeling, and model validity monitoring, this

work will clarify the conditions under which the Hage-Cifarelli formalism can be trusted

in real time and provide the statistical tools necessary to interpret its results in evolving

nuclear environments.

2.2 Data-Driven Localization of Neutron Sources Using Spatial Count Patterns

Accurately estimating the position of nuclear material in near real-time may enhance

situational awareness during activities such as fuel debris removal, especially in settings

where shielding, moderation, or source geometry are unknown or evolving. Traditional

techniques for source localization, such as time-of-flight or collimated scanning, often

require bulky hardware, precise timing electronics, or rigid geometric constraints. These

limitations reduce their applicability in dynamic or cluttered field environments.

An alternative approach involves training a machine learning (ML) model to infer source

position directly from spatial count patterns recorded by a detector array. In this case,

localization is pursued using the NoMAD detector which continuously collects time-

resolved neutron count data across its channels. A Random Forest regression model, a type

of supervised ML algorithm, is trained to map these spatial count distributions to

corresponding source coordinates. Once trained, the model can be applied in real time to

 13

new count rate data, enabling rapid source localization without requiring prior knowledge

of geometry, shielding, or moderation.

Figure 3. Predicted vs. actual neutron source positions using trained Random Forest
 model. Residuals increase at the edges of the detector’s field of view.

Preliminary results demonstrate that the Random Forest regression (RFR) model can

localize a single, unmoderated neutron source with sub-centimeter accuracy under

controlled conditions, with source positions tested up to 57.7 cm from the detector face.

The model was initially trained on synthetic datasets generated using Monte Carlo N-

Particle (MCNP) simulations with PTRAC output files, which were subsequently

converted into LMX-format list-mode data to emulate realistic detector responses. These

synthetic datasets captured spatially varying count distributions for known source positions

within the NoMAD array’s field of view. Model predictions were validated against

experimental measurements using a Cf-252 spontaneous fission source. As shown in

 14

Figure 1, localization accuracy was highest when the source was positioned near the center

of the array’s field of view, with errors increasing toward the edges. This degradation is

attributed to reduced detector sensitivity in peripheral regions and diminished spatial

gradients at the array boundary.

To improve predictive performance, additional feature engineering techniques are applied.

Inter-tube count difference (“tube delta” features) are used to enhance spatial resolution,

while principal component analysis (PCA) helps reduce feature redundancy9.

Hyperparameter tuning and filtering strategies further improved the model’s stability10.

Final model configurations achieve an R2 of 0.83 in cross-validation, with most prediction

errors under 2 cm. However, a systematic bias near the edge positions suggests a need for

improved training coverage and detector modeling.

The primary technical objective moving forward is to extend this framework to

accommodate more complex scenarios. In particular, the ability to resolve and distinguish

between two neutron-emitting sources placed within the array’s field of view will be

assessed. Dual-source configurations introduce overlapping count rate signatures and

increase the difficulty of mapping input features to unique spatial outcomes. Training data

will be generated for a range of source separations, intensities, and feature-to-position

mappings, with dual-source experiments used to validate model generalization and

resolution limits.

The expected outcome of this effort will be a lightweight, ML-enabled localization system

capable of producing real-time position estimates from NoMAD array data. This system is

designed to function as part of a broader collaborative effort in neutron detection, analysis

 15

and simulation, providing spatial interpretation tools to support data-driven decision-

making during efforts like fuel debris removal operations.

2.3 Joint Application of Multiplicity Analysis and Spatial Inference

In select scenarios, using multiplicity analysis and spatial localization together may offer

complementary insights into changes in neutron emission behavior. For example, an

increase in count rate could result from a mobile source moving closer to the detector array,

while a shift in inferred multiplicity parameters—such as the mean number of neutrons per

fission or higher-order moments—may indicate changes in system reactivity or

moderation. While each analysis method performs well independently, comparing their

outputs may help resolve ambiguities in complex or evolving environments, such as

distinguishing between geometric movement and changes in neutron multiplication.

To support this, the system will be designed to allow source localization to be optionally

invoked alongside real-time multiplicity monitoring. When enabled, localization will

operate on a user-selected time window that can be aligned with the multiplicity analysis

but is not automatically triggered by it. This decoupled structure preserves user control and

reduces unnecessary computational load by allowing localization to be performed only

when needed.

Several technical challenges are anticipated in aligning these two analysis streams. First,

multiplicity metrics are computed over short, overlapping time windows, while localization

models may require longer integration periods to achieve stable position estimates.

Selecting appropriate and adaptive time bases for synchronization will require empirical

testing. Second, the two methods operate on different sensitivity scales: multiplicity

 16

responds to statistical features of neutron emission and fission chain behavior, while

localization relies on spatial asymmetries in count rates across the He-3 detector array.

Their responses to the same physical change—such as movement, shielding, or moderation

shifts—may differ in both magnitude and timing. Third, repeated use of the localization

model over short windows could increase data handling and computational latency,

necessitating strategies such as data caching or batch processing to maintain performance.

Finally, interpretive ambiguity may arise if the two methods yield diverging outputs—for

example, an increase in inferred multiplicity without an associated spatial shift. Such

scenarios are physically plausible given that multiplicity can be influenced by numerous

factors (e.g., moderation, reflection, geometry, or source configuration), even when the

source remains stationary. To address this, the system will incorporate basic diagnostic

checks and visual indicators, such as change-point detection on time series or automated

flags based on statistical thresholds, to help users assess whether changes in multiplicity

are accompanied by plausible spatial explanations or warrant further investigation.

To address these challenges, a modular integration strategy will be developed. Localization

will be callable on demand, using a window of count data corresponding to a selected

segment of the multiplicity analysis timeline. Multiple temporal synchronization schemes

will be evaluated to determine how localization accuracy varies with window size and

count rate. A series of synthetic and experimental test cases will be constructed to test

whether this combined capability improves system understanding in specific contexts.

These scenarios include a static source with varying moderation, a mobile low-count rate

source, and shielding or source removal events. Comparison metrics will include spatial

residuals, multiplicity estimate variance, and detection latency, with emphasis on whether

 17

joint access to both analyses improves situational awareness or reduces false conclusions.

No assumption is made that fusion will be universally beneficial; rather, the goal is to

evaluate whether combined interpretation is helpful in edge cases where either method

alone may yield incomplete information.

2.4 Experimental Validation and Deployment Considerations

 Figure 4. Two NoMADs collecting data on the BeRP ball

Experimental testing will be essential for evaluating the performance and limitations of the

real-time analysis system. While simulations support system development, empirical data

is necessary to capture detector-specific behavior and edge cases that are otherwise difficult

to model. The primary goal of these experiments is to assess how the system performs

under realistic measurement conditions, identify failure modes, and refine analysis

parameters based on actual outcomes.

Experiments will be conducted either at Los Alamos National Laboratory or the National

Criticality Experiments Research Center (NCERC), depending on facility access. NCERC,

 18

located at the Nevada National Security Site and operated by Los Alamos, maintains a

substantial inventory of special nuclear material (SNM) and supports a wide range of

nuclear security missions, including criticality safety research and training, nuclear

emergency response, nonproliferation, and experimental support for government agencies.

It is one of the few facilities capable of conducting both subcritical and critical experiments

with hands-on access to significant quantities of fissile material across diverse

configurations. These capabilities make NCERC an ideal testbed for validating system

performance in higher-multiplication environments. At Los Alamos, initial validation will

use a well-characterized Cf-252 source with the NoMAD detector. Cf-252 provides a

reproducible and widely studied spontaneous fission neutron source with known

multiplicity properties, making it well-suited for evaluating time-correlation and statistical

analysis methods under varying count rate and moderation conditions7. If experiments are

conducted at NCERC, additional neutron-emitting sources will be used, including fissile

material configurations such as the BeRP ball (a 4.5 kg sphere of alpha-phase plutonium)8.

These configurations will allow for testing in higher-multiplication regimes a provide a

larger range for system validation.

Across both sites, a series of experiments will be designed to span different operational

conditions. These include variations in source position, count rate, shielding and

moderation configurations. Scenarios will specifically target regions where either the

multiplicity or localization analysis is expected to struggle. For instance, with low-count

rates, spatial edge regions, or geometries that suppress observable neutron correlations.

Performance will be evaluated using a set of quantitative metrics, including localization

accuracy (e.g., spatial residuals), multiplicity estimate stability, time-to-detection for

 19

source movement or reactivity shifts, and consistency across repeated measurements. Data

collected during these experiments will also be used to benchmark the covariance-based

uncertainty propagation approach against empirical variances and to test model

responsiveness across different integration windows. Observed deviations—such as edge

effects, detector non-uniformity, or inconsistent behavior under changing moderation—

will inform model updates and guide user documentation.

2.7 Conclusion

Built around the NoMAD He-3 detector array, the system will implement the Hage-

Cifarelli formalism for continuous estimation of multiplicity parameters and apply a

machine learning model for source localization based on spatial count rate patterns.

Together, these tools aim to provide operators with timely, interpretable insights into both

the statistical and geometric behavior of neutron-emitting materials.

 21

The research will address four central technical questions, including the operational limits

of real-time multiplicity analysis, the generalization of spatial localization models, the

interpretive value of combining both methods in ambiguous cases, and how these tools

perform in experimental configurations reflective of real-world constraints. The system

will be validated through measurements conducted at the Los Alamos National Laboratory

and/or the National Criticality Experiments Research Center, using a range of sources—

beginning with Cf-252 and potentially including fissile material such as the BeRP ball.

These experiments are designed to test the system across a range of count rates, moderation

levels, geometries, and background conditions.

The analysis pipeline will be modular by design. Multiplicity analysis will run

continuously, while localization can be invoked on demand, allowing flexibility in

deployment and interpretation. This structure enables the system to adapt to evolving

measurement priorities and operational needs. Experimental data will be used to refine

windowing strategies, uncertainty propagation, and diagnostic logic, ensuring the outputs

are both technically rigorous and practically useful.

This work will produce a real-time neutron analysis capability with direct applications in

criticality safety, safeguards, and emergency response. More broadly, it will demonstrate

how physics-based statistical models and data-driven inference can be combined in an

operational setting to support complex decision-making in nuclear environments.
