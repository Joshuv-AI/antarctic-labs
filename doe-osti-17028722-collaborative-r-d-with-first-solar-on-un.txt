 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This CRADA modification #19 involves analyses on a variety of CdTe-PV related materials, test
 structures, andsolar cells produced at First Solar (FSLR) and/or NLR. Materials will be provided
 by FSLR, NLR, or by interleaving FSLR and NLR layers and processes. Joint polycrystalline
 device activities are focused on the characterization and improvement of performance limiting
 mechanisms including doping and the hetero-interface. A combination of growth,
 characterization, and theoretical modeling will be applied.

 This CRADA Modification #24 involves analyses on a variety of CdSe, CdSeTe, CdTe-PV
 related materials, test structures, and solar cells produced at First Solar (FSLR) and/or NLR.
 Materials will be provided by FSLR, NLR, or by interleaving FSLR and NLR layers and
 processes. Joint polycrystalline device activities are focused on the characterization and

 combination of growth, characterization, and theoretical modeling will be applied.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 Benefit to Participant: Identify performance-limiting attributes over a range of common and
 uncommon cadmium selenium telluride (CdSeTe) and cadmium selenide (CdSe) device
 structures. These fundamental studies will help the participant identify limiting mechanisms to
 advance the efficiency of commercial CdSe and CdSeTe solar technology and prescribe solution
 s to the manufacturing processes that not only increase efficiency but also reduce cost.

 Benefit to DOE: Success of this project will help enable CdTe solar technology to realize the
 DOE 2030 LCOE goal of $0.03 per KWh, and can enable future absorbers for novel single-
 junction and multijunction thin film technology.

 General Task Description: Apply synthesis and characterization to the study of a range of
 samples produced at FSLR and at NLR. All studies will include discussion with NLR personnel
 on the nature of the samples and in-depth analysis of results. Samples will include a range of sX
 and pX materials as well as p-n junctions. Dopants may be introduced into First Solar and NLR
 single- and poly-crystalline materials by various means and annealing processes may be explored
 to modify dopant activation both at First Solar and NLR. Work will also examine modifying
 interfaces on First Solar and NLR samples. The Colorado School of Mines (CSM) will also
 perform computational analysis as described in the subcontract to understand, screen, and down-
 select potential front contact and buffer materials during the course of the CRADA work.

 Objective: Make cadmium telluride (CdTe) solar materials and device structures in order to
 understand CdTe solar technology. Establish characterization methods to identify performance-
 limiting attributes over a range of common and uncommon CdTe device structures. Identify and
 enhance the electronic properties of p-type and n-type dopants in sX and pX CdTe and related
 ternary alloys.

 Timeframe: Initial estimates are provided below. Expect continuous engagement, with the
 understanding that the focus could shift.

 Task 1: Synthesis: CdSeTe solar cells and related processes and materials for doping and
 interface development, interleaving, cross validation, and technical transfer.

 Task 1 Results: A variety of Cd(Se,Te) films and interface layers were synthesized in the course
 of this project. A summary of the different types of films made and their intended purposes is
 given below.

 Graded Cd(Se,Te): Films of CST that were graded in Se and Te content and which are typical
 of the sort used in high performing production devices were produced in several different
 variants. Films of CST “as-grown” that had not yet been heat treated with cadmium chloride
 (CdCl₂)nare referred to as FS100, and were used to study process variations resulting from the
 CdCl₂ heat treatment. Films of CST that had already gone through CdCl₂ heat treatment at First
 Solar, referred to as FS200, were used in studies of back contact processing. Films of CST that
 had been fully finished into devices, referred to as FS300, were used to study a variety of issues
 including degradation, interactions of interface materials, and in studies that combined detailed
 modeling and characterization. Graded CST films were produced with both arsenic and
 phosphorus doping.

 Ungraded CST films: Films of CST that had uniform Se and Te throughout were synthesized.
    Film compositions included CdTe, Cd(Se0.1, Te0.9), Cd(Se0.2, Te0.8), Cd(Se0.3, Te0.7), Cd(Se0.4,
 Te0.6), and CdSe. Ungraded films which, in principle, have less complicated electro-optical
 characteristics, and were used for a variety of purposes including measurement of electrical and
 optical properties as a function of Se/Te ratio. These films have also been useful in
 understanding activation of Group V dopants as a function of Se/Te ratio and how interfacial
 defects of various types, form as a function of Group VI grading. Ungraded films were
 synthesized in both undoped and Group V-doped variants.

 Front buffer layers: Nanometer-scale buffer layers of various sorts are used as part of the tin
 oxide SnO₂-based transparent conducting oxide (TCO) stack in CST solar cells to modify band
 alignment, interfacial chemical reactivity, and device stability. In the course of this project, two
 main buffer layers were synthesized and examined both before and after being incorporated into
 devices: zinc magnesium oxide (Zn₁₋ₓMgₓO) and manganese oxide (MnOₓ).

 Back contact buffer layers: Back buffer layers are used also to modify band alignment,
 transport, and other aspects of CST solar cells. Buffer layers that were synthesized for study in
 this project include zinc telluride (ZnTe), aluminum gallium oxide (AlGaOₓ), and proprietary
 organic materials. Back contact buffer layers were synthesized at both First Solar and NLR. NLR
 ZnTe was nitrogen doped and grown by radio-frequency (RF) sputtering and will be described in
 a future publication.

 Back contacts: Back contacts, either metals or transparent conducting oxides, are used to carry
 current from devices, improve device performance by allowing bifaciality, and enhance device
 stability. In the course of this project, several different back contact layers were synthesized,
 including gold, (Au) molybdenum (Mo), and proprietary metal oxides.

 Analysis of nitrogen-doped zinc telluride (ZnTe:N) films grown at NLR

 We performed initial screening of the electrical properties of the N:Zn(Se,Te) film deposited at
 250 °C as a function of Se/VI ratio (Fig.0). The results indicated that the resistivity remains
 relatively constant up to Se/VI = 0.3, and then increases for higher selenium (Se) concentration.
 The measured Hall effect indicates a hole density in the 1017 - 1018 cm-3 range, with mobility of
    0.3-4.6 cm2/Vs.

 At a constant Se/VI of 0.3, van der Pauw resistances in Fig. 1 show that increasing substrate
 temperature reduces resistivity for 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 standard cubic centimeters per minute
 (sccm) nitrogen (N₂) flow rates. Fig. 2 shows that the greatest conductivity and hole
 concentrations at high Se/VI compositions are for a 370 °C growth temperature and 0.5 sccm N2
 flow: p of 1019 cm-3 for N:ZnSe0.45Te0.55, close to the reported result.1

 The improved hole concentration at high Se/VI coincides with the appearance of wurtzite phase
 peaks in X-ray diffraction (XRD) in addition to the zincblende peaks (Fig. 3), while the other
 growth conditions produce phase-pure zincblende (sphalerite). Stabilization of the Zn(Se,Te)
 wurtzite polymorph was previously correlated with variable range hopping conductivity, rounded
 grains, and voids, all indicating that trapped N2 gas pockets stabilize wurtzite via increasing
 surface area.1 It remains to be seen whether these microstructural changes are also present for
 these samples.

 We additionally find an inverse correlation between hole concentration and Hall mobility, a trend
 that holds for all N:Zn(Se,Te) film compositions, growth temperatures, and N2 flow rates (Fig.
 4). Together, Fig. 3 and 4 indicate that the increased hole concentrations at high Se/VI
 compositions require a highly disordered microstructure to precipitate the wurtzite structure,
 which greatly reduces mobility.

 Future work is needed to quantify the nitrogen content in the films, determine changes in their
 valence band position with the changing Se content, and identify growth conditions that favor
 wurtzite without the presumed mobility-reducing microstructural changes.

 Scanning probe characterization:

 We performed a large amount of characterization using atomic force microscopy (AFM)-based
 nanoelectrical probes including Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) and scanning spreading
 resistance microscopy (SSRM), which provided valuable feedback for revealing and
 understanding the devices in the perspective of nm-scale electrical properties. KPFM measures
 surface potential in ~30 nm spatial resolution and ~10 mV potential sensitivity. SSRM maps
 local resistivity in ~50 nm lateral and vertical resolutions and a wide range of resistance in 103-
 1014 W. We performed KPFM potential mapping on device cross-sections, measuring the
 external bias voltage (Vb)-induced voltage drop across the devices. The voltage drop by a
 reverse Vb approximates the device built-in potential which is dominated by doping and defect
 charge trapping (Fig. 4a); the voltage drop by a forward Vb maps equivalent resistance for
 current flowing. KPFM characterizes the nm-scale potential nonuniformity, potential barriers,
 and determining the junction locations. We have also used SSRM for mapping local resistance
 on the delaminated front interface (FI), back surface, and in the bulk by beveling into the bulk
 using ion-milling (Fig. 4b), directly reflecting the local carrier concentrations. The SSRM can
 also identify the doping type with reasonable doping levels by flipping the bias voltage polarity.
 The resistance shows significant nonuniformity in both lateral and vertical directions, with
 especially a high resistance region in <100 nm near the FI in most samples from the California
 Technology Center (CTC), which complements with the KPFM potential nonuniformity. Most
 recently, and in ongoing studies, we have been using SSRM to assess possible light-soaking
 induced hysteresis found in some devices.

 Fig. 4. In (a), KPFM potential imaging schematic on cross-section of device; 1(b) SSRM setup for
 local resistance imaging. KPFM measures voltage drop across the device by applying a external
 bias voltage, to avoid the effect of surface charges. SSRM measures the local spreading
 resistance by minimizing the probe/sample contact resistance using large contact force and large
 forward bis voltage.

 In the past 18 months, we performed the nano-electrical characterizations on 74 samples,
 obtaining results of 398 slides filled with dense data, and published one joint journal paper2 and
 one oral presentation in 2024 PVSC3. A brief summary on some of the samples are: (1) The
 doping and electrical potential on record devices are more uniform with As-doped device than P-
 doped device, exhibiting opposite defect trend by photoluminescence (PL) with the same record
 23.1% efficiencies of the two doping, which illustrates balanced defect chemistry/structure with
 the electrical nonuniformity. (2) KPFM potential imaging shows additional Cu doping and
 different CHT flux changed the junction location and uniformity and increase the carrier
 concentration. (3) Developed capability of in-situ illumination with SSRM resistance mapping
 and measured significant illumination-induced local current. (4) Found actual resistance increase
 on the film back side instead of targeted decrease by additional As doping step and
 cathodoluminescence (CL) treatments, giving impotent hints in back contact engineering. (5)
 The KPFM and SSRM results on samples of different Gr-V doping elements (As, P, N, Sb) and
 different Cl-treatment time reveals the various nonuniformity that needs further improvements.
 (6) Illustrate laser-treatment on back surface does not efficiently lower the workfunction for the
 back contact engineering by KPFM on back surface. (7) In a set of different Gr-V doping levels,
 SSRM on delaminated FI illustrates well-doped n-type in low and medium doping doses and
 low-doped p-type in high dose in the region right next to the FI. (8) KPFM on samples for
 junction engineering illustrates the junction locations at FI, at the different depths within the
 CdSeTe absorber, and at the back interface, which was further correlated with SSRM resistance
 imaging. (9) SSRM on the TCO side of delaminated FI shows a systematical trend of buffer
 layer resistance, with small differences that needs to be improved by increasing the buffer layer
 thickness for improving SSRM measurement. (10) SSRM on back surface of the CdSeTe films
 illustrates that the resistance with CHT using process 2 is one order of magnitude larger than
 using process 1, process 1 and 2 being proprietary First Solar processes.

 Correlative microscopies: Auger electron microscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and
 scanning spreading resistance microscopy.

 Scanning probe microscopic methods like SSRM and SKPFM can provide valuable details about
 electrical properties of materials and junctions, but one shortcoming involved with these
 techniques is that they do not yield information about structure and composition. Likewise,
 electron-based probes such as AES (Auger electron spectroscopy), EBSD (electron back scatter
 diffraction) do not providing information on doping and electrical properties. In work in other
 NLR projects, we have developed various means of performing correlative microscopies in
 which we are able to do AES, EBSD, and other types of microscopic measurements on the same
 micron-sized areas. An example of this type of data set is shown in Figure 5. These
 measurements were conducted on the absorber sides of Cd(Se,Te):As films that were
 thermomechanically cleaved from the transparent conducting oxide superstrates upon which they
 were grown.

 8

 Fig. 5. Correlative electron spectroscopy and scanning probe microscopy on Cd(Se,Te):As. The
 top row from left to right: scanning electron micrograph and a scanning spreading resistance
 micrograph. The bottom row show AES-measured selenium, tellurium, and cadmium maps of the
 same areas imaged by SSRM.

 The top row of Figure 5 is comprised of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) data and SSRM
 data, while the bottom row is AES-based measurements of selenium, tellurium, and cadmium
 concentrations in the same regions as the other data sets. Our current understanding of these
 correlative data sets is incomplete and is complicated by interpretation of the SSRM data in
 which contrast can depend on whether the material being studied is n-type, p-type, and how the
 surface has been prepared. Nonetheless, we have generally observed that in nominally uniform
 films, there are substantial lateral inhomogeneities in both resistance and in composition. Device
 modelers at both First Solar and at collaborating universities are now getting to the point where
 such experimental inhomogeneities can be used as input, with these state-of-the art models being
 able to provide guidance on the performance losses that are associated with the inhomogeneities.
 By eye, it can be seen in Figure 5 that high selenium/tellurium ratio appears to correlate with
 high resistivity, implying that higher selenium impedes activation of the Group V dopant used in
 these films. These results are motivating researchers at First Solar, NLR, and collaborating
 institutions to both measure, minimize, and quantify the effects of local compositional and
 electronic fluctuations in Cd(Se,Te) devices.

 Front interface band alignments by photoemission, inverse photoemission, and device
 modeling:

 We developed a new means of performing TCO-CST band alignments on fully completed
 devices and used the method on recent record efficiency cells. In brief, cells were made and
 characterized by transport measurements. Transport data were used in device simulations. The
 simulations yielded probable front interface band alignments. Next, direct measurement of front
 interface band alignments were done in a way not previously possible: devices were subjected to
 thermomechanical delamination that serves to expose the buried TCO-CST interface.4 UPS, X-
 ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and low energy inverse photoemission (LEIPS) were then
 performed on each side of the interface. Band positions calculated from the photoemission data
 were then compared to the independent results from modeling of the same devices. Results
 agreed well and will be reported on fully in a forthcoming manuscript.
