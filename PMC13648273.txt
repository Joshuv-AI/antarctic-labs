Symmetry and
Type of Peripheral Substituents Tune
the Architecture of Gold-Supported Fluoro Phthalocyanine Films

Abdul Azeez, Jonathan Pinnock, Usha Kalra, Kai Hansen, Sanskruti Brahmbhatt, Hemantbhai H Patel, Marius Pelmuş, Sergiu M Gorun, Alexander Y Fadeev

Abstract

A solution adsorption
process for the facile production
of films
of C–H bonds free, fluorinated metal–organic phthalocyanines
on gold is described. The effects of symmetry, electronic structures,
and steric bulkiness, systematically varied within a series of electron-deficient
perfluorinated zinc phthalocyanines, F
n
PcZn (n = 16, 40, 52, 64), have been modeled to
account for experimental observations. The models reveal the underlying
forces that contribute to Pc adsorptions and the structure of the
resulting solid-supported uniform, lyophobic monolayer surfaces. The
most energetic adsorption and the highest surface coverage, as revealed
by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), adsorption isotherms, and
contact angle measurements, occurred for n = 40 and
52 molecules that were arranged “edge-on-head-to-tail”,
whereas molecules with n = 16 and 64 favored a “face-down”
surface orientation. A structural model of surface coverage is proposed
based on variation in interfacial interactions, ascribed to symmetry-imposed
variations in molecular dipole moments and steric interactions as
main factors that dictate the structure and properties of the constructed
monolayers. The reported procedures and modeling might be used to
reveal the factors that dictate the structure and properties of other
solid-supported monolayers.

Introduction

Phthalocyanines (Pcs) are highly conjugated
macrocycles of interest
in several technical areas, including catalysis and photocatalysis,
dyes and pigments, information storage, conductive materials, etc.
Pcs thin films, as recently reported, are of particular interest for
sensors, energy conversion, information storage, nonlinear optics,
and transistors.
−

 From a compositional point of view, Pcs bearing aromatic fluorine
substituents have been considered advantageous based on (i) F-induced
shift of their films toward n-type semiconductivity, candidates for
organic field-effect transistors (OFETs), (ii) stabilization of film
morphology, and (iii) enhanced Lewis acidity of coordinated metals
that, in turn, enhances Pcs sensitivity toward electron-donor analytes
such as ammonia.
−

 We have developed further a previously introduced class of Pcs exhibiting
a mixture of aryl and iso alkyl fluorine substituents, resulting in
extreme Pc electron deficiency,
, to complete the
series of molecules containing 1, 2, 3, and 4 Pc quadrants containing
the same fluoro alkyl substituents set. For the less hindered molecules,
the formation of dimers in solution, relevant to the deposition procedure
we report here, has recently been confirmed by electronic spectroscopy
and magnetic circular dichroism studies, supported by DFT calculations.

The fluorinated materials are
chemically and thermally
robust and
exhibit interesting properties ranging from efficient singlet oxygen
production to aerobic catalysis to bactericide activity to photochromism
and single molecular magnetism.
−

 Due to the presence of a highly polar metal center surrounded by
the nonpolar peripheral groups, these molecules exhibited amphiphilic
properties and, somewhat counterintuitively, demonstrated reasonably
good solubility (∼10–4 M) in water–ethanol
mixtures containing up to 95–98% water by volume. The macrocyclic ring substituents largely determine
the assembly of these Pcs in the solid state, including in crystals.
Specifically, the bulky perfluoroisopropyl (R
f) groups disfavor stacking interactions, favoring single-site
catalysis, and thus preventing photoexcited state deactivation via
intermolecular interactions. The bulkiness also limits π–π
stacking interactions and thus has a major influence on the structure
of both crystalline and thin film materials constructed using the
[(R
f)
x
F
y
Pc]2– ligand series.

The formation of Pc thin films relies upon the Pcs deposition on
solid supports. Indeed, the assembly of macrocycles on well-defined
surfaces has attracted significant attention due to their rich potential
to create surface patterns with nanometer dimensions with specific
electronic, sensor, magnetic, optical, and catalytic functionality.
Recent reviews addressed the surface chemistry of Pcs and related
compounds, focusing on the self-assembly of the macrocycles at solid/vacuum
interface,
,
 as well as on the thermodynamics
and kinetics of their 2-D and 3-D thin film growth including on the
influence of deposition parameters (vapor vs solution deposition)
on the structure, morphology, and physical properties of the films. The nature of supports, not just that of the
Pcs, influences the properties of F
n
PcM
films: for example, on SiO2, F16PcCu forms polycrystalline
structures. On Au(111) and Au(100), H
m
F

PcMetal complexes
(n = 0, 4, 8, and 16; n + m = 16) adopt a flat-lying first layer due to the planarity
of the Pc and the aromatic F–induced increase in the interface
dipole, and thus interfacial charge transfer. Metal-specific core
hole screening effects at organic/metal interfaces were observed for
both H16PcZn­(II) (n = 0) and F16PcZn­(II) (n = 16) deposited on Au(100).

To observe clean intermolecular interactions
within a monolayer
and determine their role in molecular orientation on metal surfaces,
a closed-shell metal such as Zn­(II) must be used, while the Pc must
be designed to modulate, up to even prevent stacking interactions
and interactions between the surface and the coordinated metal or
the macrocycle π electronic cloud. Additionally, the Pc ring
must retain its planarity to avoid Pc-surface decreased distances
due to macrocycle doming or twisting. A precise and mild deposition
method was also desirable. In the work reported here, these conditions
were met by the progressive introduction of Rf groups (i-C3F7) at the Pc ring periphery, . The room temperature
equilibrium solution adsorption method was used as the deposition
technique. This deposition technique offered control in preparation
of surfaces with monolayer and submonolayer coverage, and had a distinct
advantage over frequently used vapor deposition,

 dip-, spin-,
and spray-coating techniques, by allowing

The effects of
point-group symmetry variability, electronic structure,
and steric bulkiness of aromatic and aliphatic perfluoro substituents
grafted on Pcs and thus present in their supported films are unknown,
making it worth investigating, especially as we consider the expected
chemical and thermal robustness of such films. The range of Pcs bearing
F substituents, , represented by the (Rf)

F

PcZn can be classified based on the

by R

An electron-rich Pc, comprising four t-Bu (t-C4H9) substituents, was also included
in our study, e. The electronic structure of the Pc-rings varies from electron-rich,
imparted by the t-C4H9 groups,
to progressively higher electron-deficient as z increases.
It should be noted that, due to the π back-bonding ability of
aromatic F, alkyl R

more electron-withdrawing. Thus, as the value of z increases, the replacement of F by R

of the R

The contributions of molecular symmetry, electronic
structures,

experimental observations and to reveal the underlying forces that
contribute to the adsorption process, molecular interactions, and
organization of adsorbed macrocycles on solid supports based on progressively
more complex ring-shaped macrocyclic platforms.

Materials
and Methods

Solvents were obtained from commercial
sources and used without
further purification, unless stated otherwise. Gold-coated wafers
(Sigma-Aldrich) were ∼100 nm thick layers of highly crystalline
Au(111) supported on Si(100). Prior to the adsorption of Pcs, the
gold wafers were cut into ∼1 × 1 cm2 pieces
and soaked for at least 2 h in a freshly prepared Piranha solution. Caution: Piranha solution, a 3:1 mixture of 98% sulfuric acid and
30% hydrogen peroxide, is a very strong oxidizer and should be handled
with great care. The wafers were then rinsed thoroughly with
DI water, followed by ethanol, and dried in an oven at 60 °C.
The resulting gold surfaces exhibited water contact angles below ∼5–10°,
indicating the absence of organic contaminants.

Zinc Phthalocyanines

Six zinc phthalocyanines were
used in this study. Zinc phthalocyanine H16PcZn (97%) and
zinc 2, 9, 16, 23 tetra-(tert-butyl) phthalocyanine
(t-Bu)4PcZn (96%) were purchased from
Sigma-Aldrich. The F
n
PcZn were synthesized
by the tetramerization of phthalonitriles in the presence of zinc
acetate using microwave irradiation, as described in our previous
work. The crystal structures of F16PcZn, F40PcZn, F52PcZn, and F64PcZn have been reported.
The molecular volumes of the Pcs (V
m, XRD) were determined from their unit cells obtained via single-crystal
X-ray diffraction (SCXRD). Void volumes were calculated with Mercury
package using a 1.2 Å spherical
probe and subtracting the void volumes from the unit cell volumes. The X-ray structure of (t-Bu)4PcZn has not been reported, but the structure of (t-Bu)4PcH2 was. For the estimation of the (t-Bu)4PcZn volume, the volume of the Zn coordination sphere, ZnN4, of H16PcZn was obtained
by removing all remaining atoms, calculating the void volume, and
subtracting it from the unit cell volume. The volume of ZnH2 of (t-Bu)4PcZn was calculated similarly,
and the difference between the 2 volumes was applied as a correction
factor to the molecular volume of (t-Bu)4PcH2. The calculated structure of (t-Bu)4PcZn was obtained by placing a Zn atom in the geometric center
of the 4 coordinating Ns, after removing the 2 central Hs. The molecular
volumes (V
m, vdw) of the Pcs were
also calculated from their structures optimized in ChemDraw, using
the Marvin View software. The molecular
cross-sectional areas (σ) were calculated for the “face-down”
Pc orientation using van der Waals atomic radii. The monolayer thicknesses
(t) in the “face-down” orientation
were calculated as t = V
m/σ separately
using V
m, vdw and V
m, XRD. The monolayer thickness in the “edge-on”
orientation was calculated as a linear dimension of the Pc square: t = √σ. The dipole moments of the metal-free
Pcs (μ) were calculated using the Marvin Sketch software. X-ray metrical parameters are reported with
estimated standard deviations, esd, errors, usually at the third decimal
place. The experimental, V
m, XRD cell
volume errors are thus typically small, for example 0.03% (∼5
× 10–4 nm3) for F64PcZn,
the relative error values being independent of the reported units. The V
m, vdw errors were computed by using the tabulated van der Waals atomic
radii that are used to calculate interatomic contacts by Mercury. These values do not include solid state effects,
dipolar interactions, H-bonding etc., which lead to “short-contacts”,
a term that indicates distances between atoms that are shorter than
the sum of their van der Waals radii. It is thus reasonable to consider
that experimental errors in V
m, XRD, which include the above effects and their associated extra errors,
exceed the errors in V
m, vdw. This
meant that the 0.03% error in V
m, XRD could also be considered as the upper boundary for errors in V
m, vdw. The molecular cross-sectional areas,
thickness and dipole moments were also obtained from the molecular
dimensions. The XRD errors in the bond-lengths of Pcs could be as
high as 0.5% and this value was taken as the upper limit error in
dipole moment and thickness calculations given their linear dependency
on bond-lengths values.

Solution Adsorption

The Pcs solutions
of 1, 10, 20,
50, 100, and 200 μM concentrations in anhydrous ethanol were
kept under an inert atmosphere. The highest concentration possible
for H16PcZn was 40 μM. Freshly cleaned gold wafers
were placed in contact with 5 mL of the Pc solution, equilibrated
for 12 h at 25 °C, removed from the solution and then dried at
60 °C for 10 min. Adsorption measurements were performed in duplicate.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

The XPS measurements
were performed using a Thermo Scientific spectrometer and Al Kα
microfocused radiation, spot size 400 μm, operating pressure
5 × 10–9 Pa, θ = 90° takeoff angle.
The binding energy scale was referenced to Au 4f7/2 peak
at 84.0 eV. The Avantage software and Gauss-Lorentz functions with
Shirley background were used for spectra analysis and curve fits.
The XPS measurements were repeated twice using duplicate adsorption
trials. The errors in the atomic percentages for Au, N, F, and Zn
were 2, 5, 7, and 20%, respectively. The errors in the binding energies
were 0.05 eV (for C 1s, N 1s and F 1s) and 0.08 eV for Zn 2p, respectively.

Contact Angles

Contact angles were obtained using a

Results
and Discussion

The Pcs, , have been selected to contain closed-shell
Zn­(II). Its lack of
ligand-field stabilization energy lowered the probability of tight
axial bonding of solvents that might interfere with the adsorption
of Pcs. The Zn center, however, exhibited variable Lewis acidity,
which increased with the numerical value of the parameter z, .

The Pc-rings’ effective symmetry was defined by considering
the Pc ring and only the first atom of any substituent. For example,
F16PcZn represented structurally the full effective symmetry,
while in the case of F40PcZn, the Pc ring was substituted
by 12 F and 4 C atoms. This structural abbreviation appeared reasonable
since the aliphatic CF3 groups of the i-C3F7 substituents may occupy different positions
due to the rotational disorder the i-C3F7, but their position had little relevance to the electronic
structure of Pcs. Unlike aryl fluorines that exhibit through-space
π-back-bonding and have σ withdrawing effects, aliphatic
fluorines lack the former but retain the latter. The magnitude of
the σ–effect, which is through-bond type, depends mainly
on the number of F and their position in an aliphatic group, R
f, (n chain vs iso structure,
for example), but not on the R
f rotational
position relative to the aromatic ring to which it is attached, a
topic reviewed recently.

Considering
the effective symmetry, and, in the absence of ring
doming or twisting, the F16PcZn, F40PcZn, F52PcZn, F64PcZn, and (t-Bu)4PcZn belonged to D4h, C2v, C2v, D4h, and C4h point groups, respectively.
The presence of a 4-fold axis resulted in centro-symmetry. The variation
in the volume of Pc substituents, and in their positions, resulted
in variations of molecular dimensions (cross-sectional area and thickness),
dipole moments, and possible orientations in the adsorbed state on
the gold surface. summarizes up the structural parameters of the zinc Pcs under study.

The changes in the Zn 2p region of the adsorbed
Pcs were structurally
dependent. For H16PcZn and F16PcZn, the Zn 2p
binding energy shifted to lower values vs bulk Pcs, by 0.37 and 0.20
eV, respectively. Such significant shifts argued for the specific
interactions between Zn and the gold surface, as opposed to π-π
stacking, observed in bulk crystalline state. Notably, for F40PcZn, F52PcZn, F64PcZn, and (t-Bu)4PcZn, the position of the Zn 2p peak remained the
same (within the error) as in the bulk Pcs, suggesting the absence
of Zn–Au interactions. This was attributed to the difference
in the molecular thickness of planar H16/F16PcZn and the Pcs containing bulky alkyl groups. Indeed, the van der
Waals radii of Zn2+ and Au were 0.146 and 0.17 nm, respectively, and their sum was ∼0.31–0.32
nm, in agreement with the 0.33–0.38 nm thickness of monolayers
of H16PcZn and F16PcZn in the “face-down”
orientation (), thus allowing van der Waals contacts between Zn metal center and
Au. Similar Zn 2p shifts were observed for H16PcZn and F16PcZn on a crystalline Au
(100) face, but the Pcs have been deposited under the UHV conditions,
not through solution adsorption equilibrium. The results of this work
and work suggested the existence of a
general Zn–Au wave functions overlap, independent of the Pc
deposition mode, Au surface structure, and Pc electronic structures,
as long as the “planar” (2D-type) Pc was used. In contrast,
the other four Pcs, with z > 1, i.e., those containing
multiple bulky t-C4H9 or i-C3F7 groups (3-D type), exhibited
an increase in the Zn–Au distance, thus disrupting the direct
Zn–Au wave functions overlap, rendering the Zn 2p core level
binding energies for (t-Bu)4PcZn, F40PcZn, F52PcZn, and F64PcZn statistically
indistinguishable from those of the bulk Pcs. Noteworthy, unlike the
Zn binding energy, the F 1s and N 1s core level peak binding energies
for both z = 0 (unencumbered, 2-D structures) and
z > 1 (sterically encumbered 3-D structures) were statistically
indistinguishable,
underscoring the specificity of Zn–Au contact, and the absence
of C, F, and N specific interactions with the metal surface.

The adsorption of F52PcZn and F40PcZn yielded

In principle, the larger values of thickness could also be associated

contact angle measurement, which was used to identify surface functionalities
as well as the uniformity and lyophobicity of the Pc surfaces.

The orientation of molecules appeared puzzling: why
did the molecules
with the lowest and highest numbers of bulky R
f groups, z = 0 and 4, respectively, adopt
the “face-down” configuration, while those of intermediate
bulkiness, z = 2 and z = 3, adopt
the “edge-on” one? To rationalize the observed differences,
the symmetry and the polarity of the molecules must be considered.
The special behavior of F40PcZn and F52PcZn,
the C2v complexes, was attributed to the absence of an
inversion center, present in the D4h complexes, which resulted
in the appearance of a permanent dipole moment, . Conceivably, dipole–dipole interaction
along with steric repulsion of the wedge-shaped molecules F40PcZn and F52PcZn drove the “edge-on-head-to-tail”
arrangement in the adsorbed state, with the antiparallel orientation
of molecules observed in their bulk solids, Figure S6. In the absence of a dipole moment (z =
0, 4), the strong Zn–Au (z = 0) or, in the
absence of Zn–Au interactions (z = 4), weak
van der Waals interactions resulted in the “face-down”
configuration. For the z = 2 and 3, i.e., F40PcZn and F52PcZn, respectively, the Zn–Au interactions
were largely absent, but the intermolecular dipole–dipole interactions
appeared as the next strongest force, and thus the determining one
in a monolayer film, resulting in the “edge-on” configuration.
In the absence of strong molecule–substrate or molecule–molecule
interactions (e.g., ionic, covalent, donor–acceptor, H-bonding,
etc.), the steric and dipole interactions can play the leading role
in the surface assemblies.
,
 The importance of dipole–dipole
interactions as a major factor determining the orientation of adsorbed
molecules and as a driving force for the assembly of surface superstructures
has been demonstrated for a wide variety of systems.
−

Surface coordination
of the metal introduces a dipole component
of n-dependent magnitude perpendicular to the aromatic
ring, resulting in a total molecular dipole moment that forms an n-dependent angle with the plane of the macrocycle. The
complex interplay between metal coordination strength and the magnitude/orientation
of the molecular dipolesall exacerbated by the presence of
F and dependent on the value of nrenders
ana priori prediction of the surface orientation
of fluorinated metal phthalocyanines difficult. Additionally, X-ray
structures reveal F---F intermolecular interactions below the sum
of their van der Waals radii 2.94 Å, suggesting nonbonding interactions
that favor long-range order and crystallinity. Taken together, these
data underscore the difficulty of defining a general, clear-cut structural
role for dipole moments.

Conclusions

The efficient deposition
of monolayers
of a series of metal–organic
phthalocyanines exhibiting a systematic variation of electronic and
steric properties on gold was achieved using a simple equilibrium
solution adsorption method. The variation of experimentally determined
XPS thicknesses, adsorption equilibrium constants, heats of adsorption,
and surface wettability (contact angles) varied congruently with Pcs
calculated intrinsic dipole moments, demonstrating its critical role
in the arrangement of the Pcs monolayer structures, .

The experimentally based modeling strategy and
the recognition
of the importance of dipole–dipole interactions for the surface
assembly of these molecules, following the resolution of forces that
determine the structure of Pcs thin films, might be used to provide
insights for other surface science studies.
