Integrating Presurgical Orthopedics into Cleft Lip Surgical Planning: A Surgeon-led Conceptual Framework
Authors: Daniela Y. S. Tanikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Presurgical infant orthopedics (PSIO) is an established component of cleft care aimed at attenuating deformity severity and optimizing nasolabial anatomy before primary repair. Traditionally, PSIO—most commonly nasoalveolar molding—has been implemented within multidisciplinary care pathways and considered separately from intraoperative anatomy and surgical decision-making. This conceptual separation may limit how presurgical modifications are interpreted and applied during surgery. We present a surgeon-led, hypothesis-generating conceptual framework that reframes PSIO as an anatomy-driven extension of surgical planning rather than an isolated preparatory phase. The aim of this framework is to integrate presurgical modification with intraoperative anatomical interpretation, allowing presurgical preparation and surgical correction to function as coordinated stages of a single reconstructive strategy. This framework is particularly applicable to simplified PSIO approaches that integrate nasal and labial molding with progressive maxillary segment mobilization, including lip taping, nasal elevators, and presurgical lip–alveolus–nose approximation. The innovation lies in establishing a structured feedback loop between presurgical modification and intraoperative anatomy. With contemporary surgical techniques allowing improved visualization of septal alignment, lower lateral cartilage configuration, and ligamentous attachments, presurgical effects can be interpreted intraoperatively to refine surgical reasoning in real time. This model does not seek to replace orthodontic expertise or multidisciplinary care; rather, it proposes a complementary framework that enhances continuity between treatment phases and supports more integrated surgical planning.

Takeaways

Question: How can presurgical infant orthopedics be more effectively integrated into surgical planning for cleft lip repair from a surgeon-led perspective?

Findings: This article presents a hypothesis-generating, surgeon-led conceptual framework based on cumulative clinical observation and intraoperative assessment. It describes recurrent anatomical patterns encountered during cleft lip repair and illustrates how presurgical orthopedic strategies may inform intraoperative decision-making, rather than functioning as an isolated presurgical intervention.

Meaning: Integrating presurgical orthopedics into surgical reasoning offers surgeons a practical framework to anticipate intraoperative anatomy and refine surgical planning.

PRESENTATION OF UNIQUE IDEA

Presurgical infant orthopedics (PSIO) is widely used to attenuate deformity severity and facilitate cheilorhinoplasty.1 Nevertheless, controversy persists regarding its true contribution to final aesthetic outcomes.2 A central reason is conceptual rather than technical: PSIO and surgery are often approached as distinct domains, coordinated by different providers and interpreted through partially independent evaluative frameworks.

As a result, the impact of presurgical modification may be difficult to contextualize within intraoperative anatomy and surgical decision-making. A technically successful presurgical phase may still be followed by suboptimal surgical outcomes, whereas excellent repairs may be achieved without PSIO. This dissociation has sustained a misleading debate framed as PSIO versus surgery, rather than PSIO with surgery.

We propose a reframing: PSIO should not be viewed solely as a generic severity-reduction step preceding cleft repair, but as a preparatory process whose clinical relevance depends on how presurgical changes translate into intraoperative anatomy and surgical decision-making. Achieving this alignment requires a structured interpretive process in which presurgical modification and intraoperative findings are considered together, allowing preparation and repair to function as interdependent stages of a single reconstructive strategy (Fig. 1).

Conceptual feedback loop integrating PSIO, intraoperative anatomical assessment, and surgical execution. Presurgical modulation prepares nasolabial and maxillary anatomy, whereas intraoperative findings guide anatomy-adapted surgical strategy and iterative refinement of PSIO planning.

This concept does not challenge orthodontic expertise or multidisciplinary care. Rather, it emphasizes shared anatomical objectives and introduces a framework in which surgical interpretation helps contextualize presurgical effects.

Within this perspective, simplified PSIO methods—such as lip taping, nasal elevators, and presurgical lip–alveolus–nose approximation (PLANA)—may particularly benefit from active surgeon involvement, especially in environments where laboratory-based appliances or frequent orthodontic adjustments are not readily available.3–6

Our pathway toward this model was pragmatic. In the absence of local access to nasoalveolar molding, a surgeon-directed protocol combining a nasal elevator and lip taping was introduced. Through longitudinal clinical application, a critical observation emerged: the effects of PSIO are frequently disproportionate across anatomical domains.

Presurgical treatment may significantly improve nasal morphology, whereas skeletal relationships remain partially uncorrected, or conversely may improve maxillary alignment with limited nasal remodeling.7,8 These disproportionate responses may create secondary imbalances—such as apparent nasal symmetry masking residual skeletal discrepancy, or skeletal alignment without adequate cartilage remodeling—that may only become evident during surgery.

Comparison of intraoperative findings in patients treated with and without PSIO further clarified how presurgical forces may influence cartilage configuration and surgical anatomy.

With refinement of surgical techniques, these observations gained greater significance. Adoption of foundation-based primary rhinoplasty with septoplasty, followed by nasal tip dissection through a modified Tajima incision, enabled direct intraoperative visualization of the lower lateral cartilage (LLC) after correction of the nasal foundation.

This exposure revealed patterned LLC distortions—varying according to cleft morphology—that were not always predictable from external appearance alone (Fig. 2). Through repeated intraoperative observation, characteristic patterns of LLC kinking associated with cleft morphology were identified and have been described elsewhere.9

Further intraoperative exploration revealed abnormal soft-tissue attachments along the dome and crural regions, leading to a new hypothesis: ligamentous architecture—particularly the Pitanguy ligament and the interdomal and intercrural ligaments—may contribute not only to postoperative instability or recurrence of nasal tip asymmetry but also to the primary deformity itself.

By adapting principles of preservation rhinoplasty to cleft repair, controlled ligament release and reconstruction allowed expansion of the nasal envelope, reduction of tip tension, and more predictable midline alignment of the LLCs under direct vision.9 (See Video 1 [online], which displays the intraoperative video demonstrating the integrated presurgical and surgical approach to unilateral cleft nasal tip correction through a modified Tajima incision. Together, 3 converging elements—(1) accessible surgeon-directed PSIO,3,4 (2) systematic intraoperative visualization of cartilage distortion patterns,9 and (3) emerging ligament-based mechanisms10—support the concept of surgical integration, in which PSIO and surgery are interpreted as a continuous anatomical strategy. The innovation does not lie in a new device or technique, but in redefining the interpretive relationship between presurgical modification, intraoperative anatomy, and surgical execution.

More recently, incorporation of PLANA further reinforced this framework.5,6 (See Video 2 [online], which demonstrates surgeon-directed customization of PSIO using PLANA in a complete bilateral cleft with a protruded and deviated premaxilla. DISCUSSION

Surgical integration addresses a fundamental limitation of conventional PSIO delivery: fragmentation of care. When presurgical orthopedics are conducted independently from surgical planning, the surgeon may benefit from reduced deformity severity but lacks a framework to interpret how presurgical forces relate to intraoperative anatomy and surgical outcomes.

Surgeon-led PSIO re-establishes continuity by aligning presurgical vectors with the reconstructive plan and by incorporating intraoperative findings into subsequent presurgical refinement (Fig. 1).

This feedback loop is clinically meaningful because intraoperative anatomy frequently reveals deformity components that cannot be reliably inferred from external inspection alone. Even clefts that appear mild preoperatively may harbor disproportionate skeletal imbalance, septal deviation, or clinically relevant LLC distortions that influence predictability and stability of the final result.

Conversely, presurgical treatment may effectively remodel nasal cartilages while leaving residual skeletal discrepancy.9 Without a structured intraoperative interpretive framework, these discrepancies may remain unrecognized.7,8

Within this integrated model, PSIO becomes an instrument for targeted, adaptive preparation rather than a uniform severity-reduction tool. Intraoperative observations—such as cartilage distortion patterns and septal relationships,9 and ligamentous tethering—can retrospectively validate presurgical vector choices and prospectively guide refinement of presurgical strategies.

This approach also enables a structured correlation between preoperative severity, presurgical intervention (including timing and indication), and intraoperative anatomical findings across patients with and without PSIO. Through this correlation, patterns of response can be identified, including complete, partial, or domain-specific cartilage remodeling. These observations provide a more objective basis to understand how different variables—such as initial deformity and timing of intervention—influence anatomical remodeling and surgical relevance, and to refine presurgical strategies accordingly.

Importantly, it is the enhanced anatomical understanding afforded by this integrated approach, rather than any single intervention, which allows inherent limitations to be recognized and anticipated in surgical planning.

The proposed framework is particularly relevant in low- and middle-income settings, where simplified PSIO methods reduce dependence on laboratory infrastructure and intensive orthodontic follow-up. Because these approaches may be performed by a single clinician in a standard outpatient setting, they may also reduce the logistical burden and cost of care for both families and institutions.

As expected for an Ideas and Innovations contribution, this concept arises from longitudinal, iterative practice and observational correlation rather than randomized trials. The framework therefore emphasizes anatomical interpretation and surgical reasoning rather than reliance on a fixed mechanical protocol.

This study is not intended to evaluate cost-effectiveness, adherence, or dropout rates of PSIO, but rather to provide a conceptual framework for interpreting its anatomical effects and their implications for surgical planning.
