I. Summary

1) Cumulative Progress: As part of Task 1, we have started by testing our modeling capabilities
by reproducing isothermal DFIT simulations presented in the literature. To start, we have been
working towards adjusting numerical parameters to reproduce DFIT tests with different fracture
geometries. Then, we have focused modeling the DFIT conducted at well 58-32 (cycle 4 in zone
2). Specifically, we have carefully analyzed the influence of the aperture-contact stress
relationship and investigated how two different laws affect model results. We have also
introduced thermal effects in the model and rerun the simulations to estimate the effect of
cooling on the DFIT response. Due to the small amount of fluid injected during a DFIT, thermal
effects seem to be moderate. Starting in 2025, we have switched the focus to the MiniFrac tests
conducted on the 16B wellin 2023, specifically on the MF2 Test Cycle 1, because its post-testing
image log shows clear induced fracture. Classic G-function analysis and post-closure behavior
analysis have been performed on the field test data to estimate the minimum horizontal stress and
bulk permeability. Modeling has been resumed in June that aims to reproduce the field test
pressure behavior by calibrating modeling parameters.
In Task 2, we have adapted GEOS phase-field formulation to be able to simulate near-wellbore
hydraulic fracture nucleation and propagation. We have devised a novel formulation that,
compared to other existing ones, incorporates rock strengths. To demonstrate the ability of our
phase field formulation to simulate both nucleation and propagation of hydraulic fractures we
have conducted several simulations that include heterogeneous distribution of the rock properties
and low-tensile strengths points on the wellbore surface as well as different in situ stress
conditions. The work was published in the International Journal for Numerical and Analytical
Method in Geomechanics. We utilized the phase field model to simulate near-wellbore fracture
nucleation under varying stress conditions. In alignment with the objectives outlined in the
Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO), we completed 63 phase field simulations to
systematically explore the relationship between near-well fracture patterns, in situ stress, and
wellbore orientation. Starting in 2025, the phase-field model has been used to simulate near-well
fractures generated during MiniFrac tests conducted on 16B well in 2023, because the post-
testing image log data are available. Meanwhile, the phase-field model have been employed to
study the near-well fracturing pattern of notched samples in the experiment.
In Task 3, we have built a model of the region surrounding well 16A and modeled the stage 3
stimulation. First, we calibrated numerical simulation parameters using known analytical
solutions, and we ran simulations to study the model sensitivity to various physical parameters.
MEQ data showed near-equal vertical and horizontal fracture growth, producing an almost
circular fracture despite Utah FORGE’s high stress gradient. Thus, we used our numerical model
to test whether stress “roughness” may explain this discrepancy by calibrating toughness
anisotropy parameters to match the observed growth patterns and comparing those results with
stress profiles from sonic logs of Well 16B. We also performed simulations with isotropic
toughness and the measured heterogeneous stress profile to evaluate whether the observed
fracture geometry can be attributed to the measured stresses. To address one of reviewer’s
comments on the “layered” stress heterogeneity in our Year 2 simulations, we have generated a

full 3D heterogeneous stress field based on the geostatistical Gaussian realization and
incorporated it to the simulation to see the variation of the resulting hydraulic fracture geometry
for Stage 3 stimulation.
In Task 4, laboratory experimentation, we have designed and built an experimental apparatus
specifically tailored to conduct true-triaxial block fracturing tests on high-temperature analogue
Utah FORGE granites under relevant Utah FORGE site conditions. The apparatus (1) can
perform tests on 6- and 8-inches cubic blocks at temperatures up to 200°C, inducing thermal
stresses because of temperature changes of approximately 150°C within a few seconds.
Additionally, it can apply three distinct stress regimes with a maximum attainable stress of 30
MPa; (2) incorporates a novel borehole injection system that improves cooling efficiency; (3)
allows for testing blocks with wellbores at various angles (up to 30) from the vertical direction);
(4) allows for the adjustment of multiple operational parameters; (5) Allows for testing blocks
with notched and non-notched boreholes of pressurized sections with different lengths.
Furthermore, we have defined an extended analysis of post-peak pressure behaviors, facilitating
post-test pressure analyses such as the G-function, step rate, and fracture reopening
measurements. Thus, the key components of in-situ stress estimation can be extracted and
validated through our experiments, providing a solid foundation for validating existing in-situ
stress estimation theories or proposing new ones. So far, the PITT team has effectively
conducted several hydraulic fracturing tests in both normal and reverse faulting stress conditions.
We are integrating computer vision techniques with traditional experimental fracture observation
methods such as multi-overcore/slicing and water-penetration fracture observation. Additionally,
the PITT team has also equipped the ELE International compression machine, which is now
prepared for conducting indirect tensile and fracture toughness tests. These tests aid in
characterizing how rock fabrics influence the resulting fracture patterns. Finally, we have
completed the required personnel training and gained access to Scanning Electron Microscopy
(SEM) and Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) for conducting more detailed characterization
and analysis of rock fabrics, as well as the examination of thermal and hydraulically induced
fracture patterns.

2) Quarterly Progress:

We have resumed the hydraulic fracturing modeling for Task 1 on DFIT modeling and Task 3 on
far-field hydraulic fracturing modeling. Task 2 on the phase-field modeling of near-well
hydraulic fractures now focuses on the numerical parameter study on notched samples, which are
also being tested in Task 4 experimental tests. In Task 4, drawing from prior experimental and
numerical data analyses, we conducted three notched test cases evaluated at room temperature.
These serve as benchmark cases to contrast with earlier unnotched experimental data. With the
presence of the notch, the generated fracture should more closely align with the predictions of
classical theory, which helps decouple and mitigate the complexities observed in the thermo-
hydro-mechanical coupling process by simplifying the fracture behavior to better match
theoretical expectations.

V. Task 3 - Infer stress roughness and gradient based on large-scale fracture patterns

Subtasks 3.1 and 3.2 - Conduct GEOS modeling of fracture growth during stimulations to infer
far-field stress and rock fabric.

Input Summary
 1. Planned Activities: None. We will focus on tasks 1 and 2.

 2. Actual Activities:
 To address GNG2 reviewer’s comment on the “layered” in situ stress profile, we have
 generated a full 3D heterogeneous stress field based on the well log data from 16B well,
 and applied it to the previous Stage 3 hydraulic fracture simulation. Fig. 3-1 shows the
 slice of the heterogeneous field at a specific x in the local coordinate. Fig. 3-2 presents
 the plots of the fracture growth in both horizontal and vertical directions for the previous
 simulation with “layered” stress field and the new simulation with full heterogeneous
 stress field. Note that both simulations have considered a scale dependent rock toughness,
 i.e., rock toughness increases with the fracture size. Compared with the previous
 simulation and field MEQ data, the new simulation results show a much slower and
 isotropic fracture growth in vertical and horizontal directions. It suggests that the case
 with heterogeneous stress field may need a rock toughness with smaller size dependency
 to match the field data, and also implies that the apparent size dependency of the rock
 roughness could be due to this intrinsic stress heterogeneity.

VI. Task 4 - Perform laboratory experiments to determine relevant rock properties and
validate and calibrate models

Subtask 4.4 - Perform fracturing experiments for stress interpretation

Input Summary
 1. Planned Activities:
 We plan to perform 2–4 notched horizontal well tests and compare them with the current data
 and the results predicted by the theoretical model. The main goal is to use the notched cases
 to demonstrate that the complex behaviors observed in our unnotched experimental data so
 far arise from the thermo-hydro-mechanical coupling process. With the presence of a notch,
 these complexities are expected to be mitigated and to more closely align with the predictions
 of classical theory.
 2. Actual Activities:
 In line with the planned activities, we performed three notched test cases at room
 temperature. As shown in Table 4-1, these notched cases include two different notch depths
 (2 mm and 4 mm) and two different lengths of the central pressurized area (2 inches and 0.5
 inches), which involved manufacturing a new set of injection tubing, as shown in Fig. 4-1.
 Based on the observations of these notched tests, it is found that under the current
 experimental setup, the fracture pattern is more prone to being influenced by the length of the
 pressurized area rather than the notch depths. Note that all the listed test cases were
 conducted under the same in-situ conditions, which are favorable for promoting transverse
 fracture growth (fracture growth perpendicular to the wellbore direction, thus also called
 transverse; longitudinal here means the fracture direction is along the wellbore direction).
 Under the relatively long pressurized length, increasing the notch depth from 2 mm to 4 mm
 does not change the favorable fracture pattern. However, by reducing the pressurized length
 from 2 inches to 0.5 inches, the observed fracture pattern near the wellbore is not perfectly
 horizontal but nearly horizontal with slight inclinations and branching phenomena, as shown
 in Fig. 4-2. More importantly, as the fractures propagate to the outer boundary of the block,
 they become shearing diagonal fractures, as shown in Fig. 4-3.

 4. Plans for next Quarter:
 We plan to perform 2–4 experiments, which will include one test with the conditions of a 4
 mm notch depth and a 0.5-inch pressurized length under zero horizontal minimum stress.
 This test case is designed to explore how the horizontal minimum stress influences the
 fracture initiation and propagation process. Currently, we have observed that with a 7 MPa
 horizontal minimum stress, the induced fracture is not perfectly horizontal when initiated,
 and as it propagates, it becomes more like shearing diagonal fractures. Zero horizontal
 minimum stress will further simplify the in-situ stress conditions and provide another
 perspective on how the fracture pattern is influenced by the horizontal minimum stress.
