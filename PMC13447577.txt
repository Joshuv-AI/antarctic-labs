Extracellular vesicles derived from fetal stem cells in early pregnancy modulate glycolysis and lipid metabolism to induce apoptosis in cancer cells
Authors: Jangho Lee, Kyoungshik Cho, Songrae Kim, Eunsil Cho, Hyejin Kook, Suman Kang, Junho Kim, Mi Hyeon Cho, Sanghee Nah, Seung Hae Kwon, Jin-Chul Kim, Hoibin Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Antitumoral FSC-EVs enriched in LDHB and PGC-1α were generated by a multistage ex vivo conditioning workflow.•Engineered FSC-EVs suppress lactate production, downregulated Pdk1, and shift to oxidative phosphorylation.•Metabolic rewiring elevates ROS, impairs NADPH balance, and triggers apoptosis.•In vivo treatment lowers tumor PDK1/FASN and suppresses xenograft growth.Keywords: Extracellular vesicle, Fetal stem cells, Metabolic reprogramming, Tumor microenvironment, Cancer therapy

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy, including immune checkpoint inhibitors and chimeric antigen receptor T cell therapies, represents a revolutionary advancement in oncology [1,2]. However, despite their remarkable efficacy in hematological malignancies, these treatments have shown limited therapeutic benefit in solid tumors, which constitute the majority of cancers [3,4]. One prevailing hypothesis for this discrepancy is the distinct metabolic environment characteristic of solid tumors, primarily governed by the "Warburg effect" [5]. Unlike normal cells that rely predominantly on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to generate adenosine triphosphate (ATP), most solid tumor cells preferentially metabolize glucose to lactate through aerobic glycolysis, even in the presence of sufficient oxygen [6]. This metabolic shift results in the accumulation and secretion of lactate and protons into the extracellular space, which significantly lowers extracellular pH and increases lactate concentration within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [7]. The acidic, lactate-rich TME has been shown to impair immune responses through mechanisms such as antibody degradation, decreased tumor antigen presentation, suppression of T- and natural killer (NK)-cell proliferation and cytotoxicity, reduced major histocompatibility complex class Ⅱ expression and antigen presentation in dendritic cells, induction of M2 macrophage polarization, and upregulation of programmed death-ligand 1 [8,9]. Supporting this, numerous studies have consistently demonstrated abnormally elevated expression of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), an enzyme that converts pyruvate to lactate, and suppression of lactate dehydrogenase B (LDHB), which catalyzes the reverse reaction, in solid tumors [10].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are particles released from cells and delimited by a lipid bilayer, carrying bioactive molecules such as proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and metabolites that mediate intercellular communication [11]. Accumulating evidence indicates that tumor cell–derived EVs influence cancer metabolism and promote tumor progression through mechanisms such as metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, and premetastatic niche formation [12]. In addition, EVs derived from TME-resident cells, including immune cells, tumor-associated fibroblasts, and stromal cells, contribute to TME metabolic reprogramming, leading to tumor cell proliferation, poor chemotherapeutic response, and suppression of antitumor immune activity [13].

However, reports on stem cells or stem cell–derived EVs that directly modulate cancer cell metabolism are limited. Most studies on mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), including those derived from adipose tissue, bone marrow, and the umbilical cord, have focused on their dual role in cancer progression, showing that MSC-EVs can either suppress or promote tumor growth depending on the context, through mechanisms such as immunomodulation, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling [14,15].

Research on fetal stem cells (FSCs) and their EVs (FSC-EVs) remains scarce. Early-gestation FSCs obtained from amniocentesis express markers of embryonic stem cells, hematopoietic stem cells, and MSCs, demonstrating highly dynamic proliferative, differentiative, and metabolic properties but lack tumorigenicity [[16], [17], [18]]. In a previous study [19], we demonstrated the potential of EVs containing primal immunoglobulins and complement proteins secreted from FSCs in early pregnancy for the prevention and treatment of various infectious diseases. These EVs were cultured in a novel ex vivo system designed to promote the secretion of natural antibodies. However, little is known about the effects of FSCs and their EVs on tumors.

To address this gap, we investigated the effects of FSC-EVs on cancer cell metabolism and the tumor microenvironment. To target the Warburg effect, we developed a novel culture system optimized to enhance the metabolism-modulating potential of FSC-EVs in solid tumors. Our study provides new insights into cancer therapeutic strategies and highlights the potential of FSC-EVs as a promising approach in cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

In vitro conditioning of stem cells for functional EV harvest

Cell cultures

Human FSCs were established using a previously described method from amniotic fluid (AF) obtained from a healthy pregnant woman at 15 weeks of gestation who underwent amniocentesis with written informed consent for tissue utilization. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Bioethics Committee of Stemmedicare Ltd. (SIRB-2024-APR-01). Human trophoblasts (TBCs; AcceGen, NJ, USA) were expanded in Human Trophoblast Medium (AcceGen) supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) in a hyaluronic acid (HA)-based culture matrix under in vivo-like temperature, pH, and circulation conditions, as described below. Human amniotic membrane–derived MSCs (AM-MSCs; ScienCell, CA, USA) were expanded in MSC Medium (ScienCell) supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Human AF–derived MSCs (AF-MSCs) were established as described previously [20] and expanded in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) low glucose (Welgene, Gyeongsan-si, Republic of Korea) supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin.

In vivo-like temperature, pH, and circulation conditions

In vivo-like culture conditions for inducing immune-tolerized cell lines were applied using the temperature variation, pH, and circulation parameters established in a previous study [19]. Briefly, temperature oscillation between 36 and 37 °C with a 5-day cycle and an acidic pH of 6.2–6.8, maintained by the HA matrix, were applied to induce the secretion of various pregnancy-related hormones, including human chorionic gonadotropin and progesterone. Simultaneously, a circulation condition with a 24-h cycle was implemented to facilitate signal transduction between the culture matrix and immune-tolerized cells, thereby promoting autocrine signaling of various soluble factors.

Cell differentiation

FSCs were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/cm2 in hepatogenic differentiation medium containing 40% MCDB 201 (Welgene) and 60% DMEM low glucose supplemented with 20 ng/mL hepatocyte growth factor (PeproTech, NJ, USA), 10 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (PeproTech), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for 14 days to obtain hepatoblast-like cells (HBLCs). Subsequently, HBLCs were cultured in hepatogenic maturation medium containing 40% MCDB 201 and 60% DMEM low glucose supplemented with 20 ng/mL hepatocyte growth factor, 20 ng/mL oncostatin M (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), 1 μM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich), 1x insulin–transferrin–selenium (Sigma-Aldrich), 1% human serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin for 7 days to obtain hepatocyte-like cells (HLCs).

EV preparation

Multistage ultrafiltration

To increase the efficiency of subsequent stage filtration and maximize EV yield, a multistage filtration method was applied as described previously [20]. Briefly, the cell culture supernatant, centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, was filtered twice through a 0.45 μm vacuum filter (Corning, NY, USA) to remove cell debris and apoptotic bodies completely, followed by two filtrations through a 0.22 μm vacuum filter (Corning).

AF/AM-MSCCO-EVs

An indirect co-culture system of AF-MSCs and AM-MSCs was established using a multiplate with polycarbonate membrane insert (Thermo Fisher Scientific). AF-MSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 in a 6-well plate, and AM-MSCs were seeded at the same density in a 0.4 μm pore insert. The cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 serum-free medium (Welgene) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere for 120 h to obtain conditioned medium. To remove cell debris, the conditioned medium was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was subjected to multistage ultrafiltration to obtain AF/AM-MSCCO-EVs.

itTBC-EVs

TBCs were subcultured 3–5 times in HA matrix containing AF/AM-MSCCO-EVs under in vivo-like temperature, pH, and circulation conditions to establish immune-tolerized trophoblast cells (itTBCs). itTBCs were seeded at a density of 2.0 × 104 cells/cm2 without any matrix and cultured in DMEM/F-12 K serum-free medium (Welgene) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 environment for 96 h to obtain conditioned medium. After centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, the supernatant was filtered through multistage ultrafiltration to obtain itTBC-EVs.

HLC-EVs

HLCs were seeded at a density of 2.0 × 104 cells/cm2 without any matrix and cultured in 40% MCDB 201 and 60% DMEM/F-12 serum-free medium at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 environment for 48 h to obtain conditioned medium. After centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, the supernatant was filtered through multistage ultrafiltration to obtain HLC-EVs.

itFSC-EVs

FSCs were subcultured 3–5 times in an HA matrix containing AF/AM-MSCCO-EVs and itTBC-EVs under in vivo-like temperature, pH, and circulation conditions to establish immune-tolerized FSCs (itFSCs). itFSCs were seeded at a density of 2.0 × 104 cells/cm2 without matrix and cultured in DMEM/F-12 serum-free medium at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere for 96 h to obtain conditioned medium. The conditioned medium was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was filtered through multistage ultrafiltration to obtain the iFSC-EV–enriched supernatant. This supernatant was then filtered and concentrated using tangential flow filtration with a 300 kDa cut-off hollow fiber filter (Repligen, MA, USA) to remove small non-vesicular particles and recover highly purified itFSC-EVs.

itAT-FSC-EVs

FSCs were subcultured 3–5 times in an HA matrix containing AF/AM-MSCCO-EVs, itTBC-EVs, and HLC-EVs under in vivo-like temperature, pH, and circulation conditions to establish immune-tolerized FSCs secreting EVs enriched in LDHB and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) for antitumor applications (itAT-FSCs). itAT-FSCs were seeded at a density of 2.0 × 104 cells/cm2 without matrix and cultured in DMEM/F-12 serum-free medium at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 environment for 96 h to obtain conditioned medium. The conditioned medium was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was filtered through multistage ultrafiltration to obtain the itAT-FSC-EV–enriched supernatant. This supernatant was further filtered and concentrated by tangential flow filtration using a 300 kDa cut-off hollow fiber filter (Repligen) to remove small non-vesicular particles and recover highly purified itAT-FSC-EVs.

EV characterization

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

The concentration and size distribution of EVs were analyzed using a NanoSight NS300 (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). All samples were diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to concentrations within the instrument’s optimal detection range (1 × 107–1 × 108 particles/mL). EVs were measured at camera level 13 (slider shutter: 1232; slider gain: 175). Videos were analyzed using NanoSight Software NTA 3.4 (Build 3.4.003) with a detection threshold of 5.

Flow cytometry

EVs were stained with anti-human CD63 PE, anti-human CD81 APC, and anti-human leukocyte antigen G (HLA-G) APC antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at room temperature. Unbound antibodies were removed using ExoPAS™ (Microgentas, Seoul, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. EVs were analyzed using a Novocyte Quanteon (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with reference beads of defined sizes (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data were processed using NovoExpress software v1.5 (RRID: SCR_024676).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of LDHA, LDHB, lactate dehydrogenase C (LDHC), and PGC-1α were measured using human ELISA kits (MyBioSource, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell death and apoptosis analysis

Cell cultures and treatment

Human colorectal cancer (HCT116; RRID: CVCL_0291), liver cancer (HepG2; RRID: CVCL_0027), and embryonic kidney (HEK293; RRID: CVCL_0045) cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were passaged at 70–80% confluence and treated with itAT-FSC-EVs or itFSC-EVs (500 μg/1 × 105 cells) for 6 or 24 h. For in vitro evaluation of EV efficacy, cells were switched to low-glucose, serum-free medium to better mimic the nutrient-deprived TME [21,22].

Cell viability assay

Cytotoxicity was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with EVs for 6 or 24 h. For N-acetylcysteine (NAC) rescue experiments, cells were pretreated with 2 mM of NAC (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h before EV treatment. MTT reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) was added and incubated for 2.5 h at 37 °C. The medium was removed, and formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (Sigma-Aldrich). Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer (SpectraMax ABS PLUS; Molecular Devices, CA, USA).

Holotomography-based morphological analysis

Cells treated with itAT-FSC-EVs were imaged using an HT-X1 holotomography system (Tomocube Inc., Daejeon, Republic of Korea) under 37 °C and 5% CO₂ using a stage-top incubator (Tokai Hit, Shizuoka, Japan). The system utilized low-coherence light (450 nm LED) and a 40 × motorized objective lens (NA 0.95). Illumination was modulated by a digital micromirror device with a condenser lens (NA 0.72; working distance 30 mm), and autofocusing was assisted by a 780 nm laser. Three-dimensional refractive index (RI) tomograms were acquired and reconstructed using TomoStudio X software (Tomocube Inc.), and morphological and physical parameters were quantified using TomoAnalysis (Tomocube Inc.).

Human apoptosis antibody array

Cell lysates were collected after itAT-FSC-EV treatment and prepared using 1 × Cell Lysis Buffer (2 × Cell Lysis Buffer diluted 1:1 with ddH2O) containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, IL, USA), and the samples were normalized prior to use. The prepared lysates were applied to a Human Apoptosis Array G1 (RayBiotech, GA, USA). All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Western blot

Cell lysates were prepared with RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitors (T&I Biotechnology, Chuncheon, Republic of Korea) and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Quantified protein lysates were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Primary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), including anti-B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) (1:1000), anti-Bcl-2 associated X (Bax) (1:1000), anti-cysteine-aspartic acid protease 3 (Casp-3) (1:1000), anti-poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) (1:1000), and anti-β-actin (1:1000), were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology), including anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000) and anti-mouse IgG (1:1000), were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Target protein bands were visualized using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and images were acquired with an iBright CL1500 imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, MD, USA; RRID: SCR_003070).

In vitro experiments for metabolic analysis

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using NucleoZol (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed using AccuPower® 2 × GreenStar™ qPCR Master Mix (Bioneer, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) on an Exicycler™ 96 instrument (Bioneer). mRNA levels were calculated by relative quantification using comparative threshold cycle (CT) normalized to β-actin expression. Primer sequences used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Lactate measurement

Lactate levels in the culture medium were measured using a Lactate Assay Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After treatment of HCT116 cells with EVs, the medium was replaced with phenol red– and pyruvate-free medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 30 min of incubation, the supernatant was harvested, and lactate levels were quantified according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Seahorse extracellular flux analysis

Glycolytic activity and mitochondrial respiration were assessed using Seahorse extracellular flux assays. At 2 h after treating cells seeded in Seahorse XF cell culture microplates with itAT-FSC-EV, glycolytic proton efflux rate and oxygen consumption rate were measured using the Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assay and Cell Mito Stress Test, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The samples were analyzed using a Seahorse XF Pro Analyzer (NFEC-2025–07–306,941; Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) at the Ewha Fluorescence Core Imaging Center. Data were analyzed using Seahorse Analytics web platform (Agilent Technologies).

Measurement of oxidative stress and mitochondrial function

To assess reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, cells were treated with 20 μg/mL 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. For NAC rescue experiments, cells were pretreated with 2 mM of NAC for 1 h before EV treatment. Fluorescence intensity was measured at excitation/emission wavelengths of 485/535 nm using a GloMax Discover Microplate Reader (Promega, WI, USA). The mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using a tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester perchlorate (TMRE) Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (Abcam). Harvested cells were incubated with 50 nM TMRE for 30 min at 37 °C. After washing, samples were analyzed using an Accuri C6 flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, NJ, USA). The NADP/NADPH ratio was quantified using a Colorimetric NADP/NADPH Assay Kit (Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total and reduced forms of NADP were measured, and the ratio was calculated.

Fluorescence imaging of mitochondria, neutral lipids, and EV uptake

For mitochondrial imaging, cells were incubated with MitoTracker Deep Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a final concentration of 100 nM for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Biosesang, Yongin-si, Republic of Korea) and counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Thermo Fisher Scientific). For neutral lipid staining, cells were first fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at room temperature. Fixed cells were then incubated with BODIPY 493/503 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a final concentration of 2 μM for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After washing with PBS, cells were counterstained with DAPI. For EV uptake analysis, itAT-FSC-EVs (1 × 1010 particles/mL) were fluorescently labeled with Aco-600™ (Acoerela, Singapore) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, EVs were incubated with a 10 μM Aco-600™ solution for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, and unbound dye was removed using Vivaspin Turbo 4 10 kDa centrifugal concentrators (Sartorius Corporation, NY, USA) at 3000 × g for 15 min. To rule out non-specific background signals from dye aggregates, a ‘dye-only’ control (without EVs) was processed in parallel under identical conditions. Aco-600™-labeled EVs were then added to HCT116, HepG2, and HEK293 cells. After 3 h of incubation, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min, and counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using a STELLARIS confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). MitoTracker and BODIPY signal intensity was quantitatively analyzed using LAS X software (Leica Microsystems).

In vivo tumor model and analysis

Mouse xenograft establishment and tumor growth monitoring

Seven-week-old BALB/c nude male mice (Nara-Biotec, Seoul, Republic of Korea; RRID: MGI_2,683,685) were housed in a controlled environment (temperature, 23 ± 2 °C; relative humidity, 55 ± 5%; 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Korea Basic Science Institute (KBSI; approval no. KBSI-IACUC-25–14). For the xenograft model, mice were injected subcutaneously with 5 × 106 HCT116 cells. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice received intravenous injections of itAT-FSC-EVs (400 μg/mouse) or PBS every 2 days for 2 weeks. Tumor volumes were measured using calipers.

Immunofluorescence staining of tumor tissues

Tumor tissues were harvested and immediately embedded in O.C.T. compound (Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan) for cryosectioning. Frozen sections were fixed with 4% PFA (Biosesang) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1; Novus Biologicals, CO, USA) and rabbit anti-fatty acid synthase (FASN; Novus Biologicals). After washing with PBS, sections were incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature with anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by nuclear staining with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Immunofluorescence images were captured using a STELLARIS confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using LAS X software (Leica Microsystems).

Results

Establishment of an ex vivo culture system inducing the secretion of FSC-EVs containing LDHB and PGC-1α to reverse Warburg effects

To investigate the effects of FSC-EVs on cancer metabolism and the tumor microenvironment, FSC-EVs were prepared using two methods. The first type, itFSC-EVs, was obtained from an ex vivo culture system that simulates the in vivo pregnancy environment, as established in our previous study [20]. The biological rationale for simulating this microenvironment is to replicate the physical, chemical, and physiological niche where trophoblasts continuously express and secrete HLA-G to evade maternal immune rejection, thereby ensuring the systemic immunological safety of the resulting HLA-G-bearing vesicles. However, to specifically target and reverse the highly glycolytic Warburg phenotype in solid tumors, we sought to further enhance the metabolism-modulatory capacity of our platform, which motivated us to develop a new method. The second type, itAT-FSC-EVs, was obtained under newly optimized culture conditions designed to increase the content of factors directly influencing cancer cell metabolism in itFSC-EVs. Specifically, LDHB and LDHC, which reduce lactate production in cancer cells, and PGC-1α, which maintains the intracellular homeostasis of LDHs, were selected as key factors for normalizing the tumor microenvironment characterized by high lactate concentration and low pH. To enhance the secretion of these factors in FSCs, we focused on hepatocytes, which are highly specialized in gluconeogenic metabolism and exhibit high levels of LDHB, LDHC, and PGC-1α expression in body tissues and can be differentiated from stem cells. In other words, a new ex vivo culture system was devised, as shown in Fig. 1, to obtain itAT-FSC-EVs by applying HLC-derived EVs, differentiated from FSCs, to the initial ex vivo culture system.

Schematic diagram of the pregnancy-mimicking ex vivo culture system for generating itAT-FSC-EVs enriched in metabolic regulatory cargo. In our previous study [20], we developed an ex vivo culture system that simulates the in vivo pregnancy environment and, for the first time, established immune-tolerized cell lines that continuously express and secrete HLA-G proteins, which protect the fetus from the maternal immune system. We also demonstrated that EVs secreted by these cell lines contain HLA-G and can be used as allogeneic EV treatments that do not trigger immune responses owing to major histocompatibility complex discrepancy. Based on this, we devised a novel ex vivo culture system to develop itAT-FSC-EVs enriched in LDHB and PGC-1α to reverse glycolysis in cancer cells, by applying mature hepatocyte-derived EVs to the existing ex vivo culture system that induces immune tolerance characteristics. (A) Module for inducing pregnancy-related hormone secretion promoting HLA-G transcription: to replace the neuroendocrine regulation of pregnancy hormones such as progesterone, which promote HLA-G transcription in vivo, a 5-day temperature cycle mimicking the 28-day female hormonal cycle and a 24-h vibration cycle simulating biorhythmic changes were applied [19]. (B) Module for inducing immune tolerance characteristics that continuously express and secrete HLA-G: to simulate the in vivo environment during pregnancy surrounded by the amniotic membrane and amniotic fluid, an HA-based ex vivo culture matrix containing AF/AM-MSCCO-EVs was developed. Additionally, itTBC-EVs were used to induce HLA-G expression and secretion in FSCs. (C) Module for differentiation and maturation of HLCs: to reverse glycolysis and lactate accumulation in cancer cells, HLC-EVs derived from matured HLCs, differentiated from FSCs, were applied to the immune-tolerance–inducing ex vivo culture system to promote EV secretion containing LDHB and PGC-1α. EVs obtained by applying only modules (A) and (B) are referred to as itFSC-EVs, whereas those obtained by applying all three modules (A), (B), and (C) to further enhance the metabolism-modulatory capacity of itFSC-EVs in solid tumors are referred to as itAT-FSC-EVs.

Characterization of EVs reveals distinct metabolic cargo

To assess the physical and molecular characteristics of the itFSC- and itAT-FSC-EVs used in this study, we first examined their morphology by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) and field-emission transmission electron microscopy (FE-TEM) (Fig. 2A). Both analyses revealed that the EVs had typical spherical shapes with similar size ranges, which was further confirmed by NTA, showing a particle size distribution of 100–200 nm (Fig. 2B). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed the presence of EVs (Fig. 2C). Both EV populations exhibited robust and equivalent surface expression of canonical tetraspanin markers (CD63 and CD81) and HLA-G, verifying their immunotolerant properties are preserved throughout the conditioning processes [20].

The images show similar sizes and shapes between both FSC-EVs. (B) NTA results of 100-fold–diluted itAT-FSC-EVs and itFSC-EVs (n = 3). itAT-FSC-EVs show slightly larger particle size and higher concentration distributions than itFSC-EVs. However, the cargo of both EVs differed dramatically. ELISA assays revealed that the metabolic regulatory proteins LDHB, LDHC, and PGC-1α were more abundant in itAT-FSC-EVs compared with itFSC-EVs, while LDHA was markedly lower in itAT-FSC-EVs than in itFSC-EVs (Fig. 2D). These findings indicate that itAT-FSC-EVs are selectively enriched in metabolic regulatory proteins that promote oxidative metabolism, while carrying significantly less LDHA – the principal enzyme of pyruvate-to-lactate conversion in glycolytic cancer cells. Consistent with this cargo profile, lactate production in HCT116 cells was further reduced in the itAT-FSC-EV–treated group compared with the itFSC-EV–treated group (Fig. 2E).

itAT-FSC-EV treatment selectively reduces cancer cell viability and induces apoptosis

Given the evidence that itAT-FSC-EVs may induce metabolic changes—and considering the increasing focus on cancer therapies targeting metabolic pathways [23,24]—we next sought to examine whether itAT-FSC-EVs exert anticancer effects through similar mechanisms. Because the biological activity of EVs requires their delivery to recipient cells, we first confirmed that itAT-FSC-EVs were readily internalized by HCT116, HepG2, and HEK293 cells, indicating that all three cell types were capable of receiving EV-derived signals (Fig. 3A). Importantly, cells treated with a 'dye-only' control exhibited no detectable fluorescence, confirming that these signals represent the specific internalization of intact EVs. We next examined the effect of itAT-FSC-EVs on cancer cell viability. HCT116 cell viability decreased significantly at 6 and 24 h after itAT-FSC-EV treatment compared with untreated controls, whereas HepG2 cells showed a significant decrease only at 24 h (Fig. 3B). In contrast, HEK293 cells showed no notable decrease in cell viability, indicating selective cytotoxicity of itAT-FSC-EVs toward cancer cells. We next treated HCT116 cells with varying concentrations of itAT-FSC-EVs and observed a concentration-dependent decrease in cell viability, with significant reductions observed only at higher concentrations (Fig. S1A). In contrast, itFSC-EVs exhibited relatively mild cytotoxicity. HCT116 cells showed decreased viability at 24 h but no change at 6 h and HepG2 cells exhibited weaker responses to itFSC-EVs, whereas HEK293 cells remained unaffected (Fig. 3C), highlighting the specificity of itAT-FSC-EVs in exerting anticancer effects.

To determine whether itAT-FSC-EV–treated cancer cells underwent apoptosis, we monitored morphological changes over time using holotomography imaging. Although control cells exhibited spreading and proliferation, itAT-FSC-EV–treated HCT116 cells displayed a rounded morphology and progressive shrinkage (Fig. 3D), characteristic hallmarks of apoptotic cell death [25]. Quantitative analysis further revealed that itAT-FSC-EV treatment decreased cell volume, surface area, and projected area while increasing sphericity, concentration, dry mass, and RI mean value (Fig. 3E, S1B) [26].

Next, we sought to confirm whether itAT-FSC-EV–mediated cell death involves apoptosis. Human apoptosis array analysis in HCT116 cells revealed increased caspase-3 (Casp3) and decreased X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP) expression 24 h after itAT-FSC-EV treatment (Fig. 4A, S2), suggesting activation of apoptotic pathways. Western blot analysis further supported these findings, showing decreased levels of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 and increased levels of pro-apoptotic Bax, cleaved Casp3, and cleaved PARP after 24 h (Fig. 4B, 4C). Although these apoptotic markers showed little change at the 6-h time point, these results indicate that itAT-FSC-EV treatment induces apoptosis in cancer cells, ultimately leading to cell death.

itAT-FSC-EVs suppress glycolysis and enhance mitochondrial respiration

To elucidate the metabolic mechanisms underlying itAT-FSC-EV–induced apoptosis, we examined key metabolic parameters. qRT-PCR revealed a marked downregulation of Pdk1, a key kinase regulating the switch between glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidation [27], in HCT116, HepG2, and HEK293 cells after itAT-FSC-EV treatment (Fig. 5A), with the most pronounced decrease observed in HCT116 cells. In HCT116 cells, Pdk1 levels were significantly reduced at both 6 and 24 h (Fig. S3A). These results are consistent with the observed reduction in lactate secretion following itAT-FSC-EV treatment (Fig. 2E), indicating suppressed glycolytic activity.

We next examined whether this molecular shift was accompanied by functional changes in cellular energy metabolism. itAT-FSC-EV treatment markedly reduced basal and compensatory glycolysis in HCT116 cells and, to a lesser extent, in HepG2 cells, whereas HEK293 cells showed no significant change (Fig. 5B, 5C). In parallel, oxygen consumption analysis showed that basal respiration and ATP-linked respiration were increased in HCT116 cells after itAT-FSC-EV treatment. HepG2 cells also showed an increase in basal respiration, while HEK293 cells exhibited limited changes in mitochondrial respiration (Fig. 5D, 5E). These findings indicate that itAT-FSC-EVs suppress glycolytic activity and promote a shift toward mitochondrial respiration.

These oxygen consumption data were further supported by an increase in mitochondrial membrane potential after itAT-FSC-EV treatment (Fig. S3B, S3C, S3D), indicating enhanced mitochondrial activity. We then examined whether this metabolic shift was accompanied by oxidative stress. ROS levels increased at 24 h in all cell types (Fig. 5F). Interestingly, in time-dependent measurements at 6 and 24 h, cancer cells (HCT116 and HepG2) showed a continuous increase in ROS, whereas normal cells (HEK293) showed an initial increase, followed by a decrease (Fig. 5G), implying selectively defective redox homeostasis in cancer cells.

To determine whether ROS accumulation contributed to the reduced viability induced by itAT-FSC-EVs (Fig. 3B), we treated cells with NAC, ROS scavenger. NAC co-treatment reduced EV-induced ROS accumulation in all three cell lines (Fig. S3E). Consistent with this effect, NAC restored cell viability compared with itAT-FSC-EV treatment alone (Fig. 5H). These results indicate that ROS accumulation contributes to itAT-FSC-EV-induced cytotoxicity.

itAT-FSC-EVs reduce mitochondrial biogenesis and impair redox balance in cancer cells

To investigate whether the increased mitochondrial respiration and sustained ROS accumulation observed after itAT-FSC-EV treatment were accompanied by changes in mitochondrial biogenesis and redox balance, we measured the expression of fatty acid synthesis-related genes. Among itAT-FSC-EV–treated cells, HCT116 and HepG2 cells showed substantial downregulation of fatty acid synthesis genes, including acetyl-CoA carboxylase (Acc), ATP-citrate lyase (Acly), Fasn, and carnitine palmitoyltransferase (Cpt), with HCT116 cells showing the most pronounced decrease (Fig. 6A). HCT116 cells were evaluated at 6 and 24 h, and gene expression decreased further over time (Fig. S4A). In contrast, HEK293 cells showed mixed responses, with slightly reduced expression of some genes and increased expression of Fasn.

To determine whether the transcriptional changes in lipid metabolism–related genes were associated with corresponding changes in cellular lipid content, cellular neutral lipid accumulation was evaluated using BODIPY staining. BODIPY fluorescence intensity decreased in HCT116 and HepG2 cells following itAT-FSC-EV treatment at 24 h, whereas no significant change was observed in HEK293 cells (Fig. 6B, 6C). A similar reduction was detected as early as 6 h in HCT116 and HepG2 cells but not in HEK293 cells (Figs. S4B, S4C).

Since lipid synthesis provides membrane components required for mitochondrial biogenesis and maintenance, we next examined whether the reduction in lipid accumulation was accompanied by changes in mitochondrial mass. MitoTracker staining showed that mitochondrial mass decreased only in HCT116 and HepG2 cells after itAT-FSC-EV treatment, with a greater reduction over time, whereas it remained unchanged in HEK293 cells (Fig. 6D, 6E, S4D, S4E).

Because NADPH produced by mitochondria is essential for redox homeostasis, we measured the NADP-to-NADPH ratio following itAT-FSC-EV treatment. In HCT116 and HepG2 cells, the ratio increased progressively from 6 h to 24 h (Fig. 6F, S4F), suggesting impaired NADPH production. In HEK293 cells, the ratio increased at 6 h but returned to baseline at 24 h, consistent with the ROS pattern, indicating preserved redox recovery mechanisms. These results collectively indicate that itAT-FSC-EV treatment reduced lipid accumulation and mitochondrial content while disrupting redox balance in cancer cells, thereby limiting their ability to adapt to increased mitochondrial respiration and oxidative stress.

Systemic itAT-FSC-EV administration suppresses tumor growth via metabolic reprogramming

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of itAT-FSC-EVs in vivo, itAT-FSC-EVs were intravenously injected into HCT116 xenograft–bearing mice. itAT-FSC-EV–treated mice exhibited significantly reduced tumor volumes compared with controls, without any notable changes in body weight (Fig. 7A, 7B, S5A), suggesting effective tumor suppression with minimal systemic toxicity. Immunofluorescence staining of tumor tissues further revealed decreased expression of PDK1 and FASN in the itAT-FSC-EV–treated group (Fig. 7C, 7D), supporting the notion that itAT-FSC-EV–mediated metabolic reprogramming underlies the observed antitumor effects.

Discussion

Cancer cells in solid tumors display intrinsic metabolic vulnerabilities that can be therapeutically exploited without relying on the immune context. A hallmark is their glycolytic preference, which diverts pyruvate away from the mitochondria, limits mitochondrial respiration, and disturbs the intracellular redox balance [28,29]. We postulated that converting this biased flux toward a more “normal-like” oxidative state could render cancer cells vulnerable. To this end, we developed novel antitumoral EVs (itAT-FSC-EVs) enriched for LDHB (which converts lactate to pyruvate) and PGC-1α (a master coactivator of mitochondrial gene expression, fatty-acid oxidation, and LDH isoform balance) [30,31]. itAT-FSC-EVs were manufactured through a multistage, in vivo-mimetic workflow designed to maximize functional cargo: (i) serial conditioning of FSC–adjacent lineages under temperature cycling, mildly acidic HA matrix pH, and 24 h circulation to induce immune-tolerized phenotypes; (ii) stepwise exposure to AF/AM-MSCCO-EVs, itTBC-EVs, and HLC-EVs; and (iii) expansion of itAT-FSCs that secrete EVs with elevated LDHB and PGC-1α. Functionally, LDHB delivery should drive lactate-to-pyruvate conversion, whereas PGC-1α promotes mitochondrial programs. Our results showed that itAT-FSC-EVs reduced lactate secretion and downregulated Pdk1, thereby promoting pyruvate uptake into mitochondria, increasing mitochondrial respiration, and generating excessive ROS. Concurrently, NADPH decreased and fatty acid metabolism and mitochondrial biogenesis were reduced, creating a state of heightened mitochondrial activity with impaired biosynthetic and redox support, ultimately triggering apoptosis in cancer cells but not in normal cells (Fig. 8). Essentially, itAT-FSC-EVs reprogrammed cancer metabolism toward a normal oxidative state and elicited tumor cell death.

Summary model of itAT-FSC-EV-mediated metabolic rewiring and cancer-selective apoptosis. itAT-FSC-EVs shift cancer cells from lactate-producing glycolysis toward mitochondrial oxidation, elevate ROS while lowering NADPH, and reduce fatty acid metabolism and mitochondrial biogenesis, ultimately leading to apoptosis.

Consistent with these reprogramming effects, our findings outline a stepwise mechanism triggered by EV internalization. Once delivered into the cancer cells, the highly enriched LDHB and LDHC drive the reverse conversion of lactate to pyruvate. This shifts the intracellular LDH equilibrium and reduces net lactate secretion. Meanwhile, Pdk1 expression is markedly downregulated. Since PDK1 inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase [27], its suppression allows pyruvate dehydrogenase activation, driving pyruvate into the TCA cycle. This increased mitochondrial entry of pyruvate elevates both the mitochondrial membrane potential and oxygen consumption [32,33]. This forced shift toward oxidative phosphorylation rapidly generates mitochondria-derived ROS and induces apoptosis within 24 h, as evidenced by cleavage of Casp3 and PARP. Consistent with a functional contribution of ROS to this response, NAC co-treatment attenuated EV-induced ROS accumulation and restored cell viability, suggesting that ROS is an important mediator of itAT-FSC-EV-induced cytotoxicity. Importantly, this response was cancer cell-selective. Normal cells exhibited a transient ROS increase at 6 h that resolved by 24 h, consistent with intact redox homeostasis and survival. In contrast, cancer cells failed to restore redox homeostasis, resulting in sustained ROS elevation and cell death.

To dissect this selectivity, we examined redox metabolism focusing on mitochondrial NADPH. In mitochondria, NADPH is generated primarily by nicotinamide nucleotide transhydrogenase and isocitrate dehydrogenase-2, which buffer ROS within the cell [[34], [35], [36]]. Despite the enrichment of PGC-1α-a well-known promoter of mitochondrial biogenesis- in itAT-FSC-EVs, recipient cancer cells uniquely exhibited coordinated suppression of lipogenic genes (Acc, Acly, Fasn, and Cpt). This appears to be a consequence of the severe redox stress induced by the forced metabolic shift. De novo lipogenesis is essential for providing the phospholipids required for mitochondrial biogenesis and cristae remodeling [[37], [38], [39]]. Thus, the suppression of these genes limits the structural substrates required, which aligns with the observed decrease in mitochondrial mass. This structural deficit likely exacerbates the impairment of mitochondrial NADPH generation, reflected by an increased cellular NADP/NADPH ratio. We infer that the combination of heightened oxidative phosphorylation-derived ROS and restricted mitochondrial synthesis places a dual stress on cancer cells, overwhelming their redox buffering capacity rather than allowing metabolic adaptation. In contrast, normal cells successfully adapt to this challenge; their redox equilibrium was restored by 24 h, consistent with preserved NADPH levels and mitochondrial mass.

While our results demonstrate a metabolic shift driven largely by LDHB and PGC-1α, EVs contain a highly complex cargo. Other undefined EV-associated molecules, including microRNAs or additional proteins, likely contribute to the downstream signaling in recipient tumor cells. Moreover, the exact molecular pathways connecting the delivered LDHB/PGC-1α complex to the transcriptional suppression of Pdk1 remain to be fully characterized. Further studies incorporating multi-omics profiling of itAT-FSC-EVs and their intracellular targets are needed to construct a complete map of this EV-mediated metabolic reprogramming.

In vivo xenograft experiments confirmed the mechanisms first observed under laboratory conditions. Systemic itAT-FSC-EV administration restrained tumor growth without apparent toxicity and reduced intratumoral PDK1 and FASN levels, consistent with glycolytic suppression and reduced lipogenesis in colorectal cancer cells. These findings indicate that the EV-mediated metabolic reset operates in tumors as well as in cell-based systems.

An increase in mitochondrial membrane potential concurrent with a decrease in mitochondrial mass may appear paradoxical. However, several studies support the plausibility of this pattern. Quality-control mitophagy can remove damaged organelles, yielding fewer mitochondria per cell but greater energetic efficacy. Hurtado et al. reported that pharmacological induction of mitochondrial biogenesis triggers selective mitophagy to prune less-fit mitochondria, leaving fewer yet more active organelles [40]. Similar reductions in mitochondrial content accompanied by heightened respiratory activity have been described in cancer and stress-adaptation models [41]. In mouse embryonic fibroblasts, optic atrophy 1-dependent cristae reorganization tightens the cristae architecture and enhances respiratory function without increasing mitochondrial abundance [42]. Further studies are required to clarify these underlying mechanisms.

In addition to its intrinsic cytotoxicity, lowering lactate production has broader implications. Excess lactate export from glycolytic tumors acidifies the tumor microenvironment and suppresses antigen presentation, T cell and NK cell function, and dendritic cell cytokine programs while promoting pro-tumoral myeloid polarization [43,44]. itAT-FSC-EVs reduce lactate output in vitro, which could lessen extracellular lactate accumulation in tumors and alleviate pH-dependent immunosuppression, thereby synergizing with immune checkpoint inhibitors or adoptive cell therapies [45,46]. Although we did not directly quantify intratumoral pH or immune reinvigoration, the observed reductions in PDK1/FASN and tumor growth support a model in which itAT-FSC-EVs kill cancer cells and condition a metabolically less suppressive niche—an avenue to be pursued in future studies.

Collectively, our findings establish that itAT-FSC-EVs are a powerful metabolic-rewiring therapeutic that shifts glycolytic dependence toward mitochondrial oxidative activity and induces redox stress leading to selective cancer cell death. Importantly, confirmation of these metabolic effects in tumor models highlights the translational potential of this approach, supporting itAT-FSC-EVs as a promising platform to normalize cancer metabolism and enhance the effectiveness of current anticancer treatments.
