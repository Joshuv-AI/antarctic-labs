Clinical and Radiological Features Suggestive of Mucopolysaccharidosis in Two Siblings From Sudan: A Case Series
Authors: Alaa Bella, Mumen Abdalazim Dafallah, Ashraf Yousif Fadlalla, Bothaina Faisal Elmardi, Yomna Mohialdin Alataya, Abdalla Abdelmahmoud Abdalla, Mohammed Almeldin Ahmed Ibrahim, Mawada Faisal Nugud, Salah Elamin Ahmed Babiker, Hiba faroug Al_amin, Safaa Ayoub Yousif Mohammed, Moawia Elbalal Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMucopolysaccharidosis should be suspected in patients presenting with multisystem involvement, including coarse facial features, skeletal abnormalities, and progressive organ dysfunction, particularly in resource‐limited settings where delayed diagnosis is common. Early clinical recognition is crucial, as timely diagnosis can facilitate appropriate supportive care, genetic counseling, ultimately improving quality of life.

Introduction

Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS) are a group of rare inherited lysosomal storage disorders caused by deficiencies of enzymes involved in the degradation of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs). The progressive accumulation of partially degraded GAGs within lysosomes results in multisystem involvement affecting the skeletal, cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological, ophthalmological, and otolaryngological systems [1].

Clinical manifestations vary widely; the common clinical features include coarse facial features, short stature, joint stiffness or contractures, skeletal deformities, hearing impairment, recurrent respiratory symptoms, and cardiac valve abnormalities [2].

Here, we report two cases (an 18‐year‐old male and his 13‐year‐old half‐sister) with mucopolysaccharidosis presenting with progressive musculoskeletal deformities and multisystem manifestations, highlighting the diagnostic challenges associated with late presentation of MPS. Delayed diagnosis remains a major challenge, particularly in resource‐limited settings where enzymatic and genetic testing may not be readily available.

To the best of our knowledge, this may represent one of the first reported case series of mucopolysaccharidosis from Sudan in the published medical literature, emphasizing the importance of increasing awareness and improving diagnostic capabilities for rare genetic disorders in under‐resourced settings.

Case Series

Case 1

Case History and Examination

An 18‐year‐old previously healthy male presented with a slowly progressive musculoskeletal illness that had evolved over approximately 3 years. His symptoms began insidiously with intermittent aching pain affecting multiple joints, initially involving the knees and elbows before gradually extending to the spine and small joints of the hands. Over time, the pain became persistent and progressively more severe, significantly limiting his daily activities. Movement consistently exacerbated the symptoms, while there was no clear history of morning stiffness or noticeable joint swelling.

During the 6 months preceding presentation, his condition deteriorated further, with progressive difficulty in walking that eventually resulted in loss of independent ambulation. Despite the chronicity and progression of symptoms, he denied any constitutional symptoms. There was no history of trauma or other significant systemic complaints.

Past medical history was unremarkable apart from prior treatment with disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drugs following an earlier presumptive diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis, with no significant clinical improvement. Family history was notable for a similar illness affecting multiple siblings, in addition to several early childhood deaths within the family. The family pedigree is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Because two affected daughters were born to the same father and two mothers, the father is considered an obligate heterozygous carrier, while each mother is presumed to be a heterozygous carrier Figure 1. Although the affected siblings share the same father and exhibit similar clinical and radiological features, they share only the paternal allele. Therefore, in the absence of enzyme assays or molecular genetic testing, it cannot be assumed that they have the same pathogenic variant or definitive MPS subtype. This further supports describing the diagnosis as clinically and radiologically suggestive rather than genetically confirmed.

Family pedigree of the patients (one father‐ two mothers) demonstrating a strong history of consanguinity, multiple affected and deceased siblings.

On examination, the patient appeared ill with marked short stature (height 140 cm, weight 35 kg, BMI 17.9 kg/m2). Coarse facial features, a depressed nasal bridge, prominent jaw musculature (Figure 2A) and impacted teeth were noted (Figure 2B).

(B) Dental abnormalities showing widely spaced mal‐aligned teeth with anterior open bite, irregular lower incisors, and unerupted teeth. (C) Clinical photograph of the patient demonstrating severe bilateral hand deformities with flexion contractures. (D) Clinical photograph of the patient demonstrating disproportionate shortening of the left lower limb and bilateral bony knees swelling. (E) Slit‐lamp examination demonstrating corneal clouding.

Musculoskeletal examination revealed symmetrical deformities of both hands with fixed flexion contractures involving multiple proximal interphalangeal joints (Figure 2C). Multiple firm, non‐tender periarticular swellings were present without clinical evidence of active synovitis. Bilateral bony enlargement of the knees and apparent shortening of the left lower limb were also observed (Figure 2D). Spinal examination demonstrated kyphosis.

Slit‐lamp examination revealed bilateral marginal corneal clouding (Figure 2E). Otological examination showed mild bilateral narrowing of the external auditory canals with intact tympanic membranes.

Methods (Investigations and Treatment)

Laboratory investigations demonstrated negative rheumatoid factor and anti‐cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies. Renal function tests, liver function tests, serum electrolytes, and viral screening for HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C were within normal limits.

Radiographic evaluation of the skull, spine, pelvis, knees, and hands demonstrated classical features of dysostosis multiplex (Figure 3A–E), including J‐shaped Sella turcica, vertebral, knee, and hand abnormalities. The constellation of clinical, ophthalmological, familial, and radiographic findings raised strong suspicion for mucopolysaccharidosis. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed no structural or valvular abnormalities.

(A) Skull radiograph shows J‐shaped Sella turcica. (B) Spine radiograph demonstrates vertebral abnormalities with anterior beaking and thoracolumbar deformity. (C) Pelvic radiograph reveals bilateral hip dysplasia with irregular femoral heads and acetabular deformity. (D) Knees radiograph demonstrates genu valgum and abnormal epiphyseal development. (E) Hand radiographs demonstrating characteristic skeletal abnormalities including proximal pointing of the metacarpals, joint space narrowing, flexion deformities, and irregular phalangeal architecture.

Case 2

Case History and Examination

A 13‐year‐old previously healthy female presented with a progressive deforming musculoskeletal disorder that had evolved over approximately 3 years. Her symptoms began with painful symmetrical swelling involving both hands, accompanied by bilateral knee pain. The pain was not associated with morning stiffness, with no preceding history of trauma.

Over time, she developed worsening hand deformities with increasing functional impairment, particularly affecting fine hand movements. Despite the progressive skeletal manifestations, she remained free of significant systemic complaints.

Clinical examination revealed findings comparable to those observed in her affected sibling. The patient appeared ill with short stature (height 132 cm, weight 27 kg, BMI 15.5 kg/m2) and coarse facial features, including a depressed nasal bridge and prominent jaw musculature (Figure 4A). Multiple impacted teeth were noted (Figure 4B).

(A) Clinical photograph of the patient demonstrating coarse facial features, including a depressed nasal bridge, and prominent jaw musculature. (B) Dental abnormalities showing delayed eruption, malalignment of teeth, and gingival hypertrophy. (C) Slit‐lamp examination demonstrating unilateral superior corneal pannus and corneal clouding.

Musculoskeletal examination demonstrated symmetrical deformities of both hands with claw‐hand configuration, along with multiple firm, non‐tender periarticular swellings in the absence of clinical evidence of active synovitis. Bilateral bony enlargement of the knees was also observed, and spinal examination revealed mild scoliosis.

The familial clustering of these characteristic skeletal manifestations further supported the suspicion of an inherited lysosomal storage disorder, consistent with mucopolysaccharidosis.

Ophthalmological assessment revealed unilateral superior corneal pannus and corneal clouding of the right eye (Figure 4C), in addition to bilateral papilledema. Otological examination revealed narrowing of the internal and external auditory canal.

Methods (Investigations and Treatment)

Radiographic evaluation of the skull, spine, chest, pelvis, knees, and hands demonstrated classical features of dysostosis multiplex (Figure 5A–D), including J‐shaped sella turcica, and characteristic vertebral, knee, and hand abnormalities.

(A) Skull radiograph shows J‐shaped sella turcica, and atlantoaxial subluxation. (B) Hand radiographs demonstrating clawing deformities of the fingers (bullet shaped). (C) Spine radiograph demonstrates mild thoracolumbar scoliosis. (D) Lower limbs radiograph demonstrating bilateral hip dysplasia with irregular femoral heads, shallow acetabula, deformity of the proximal femora, bilateral genu valgum with metaphyseal widening and irregular epiphyseal development.

Given the presence of papilledema and radiographic evidence of atlantoaxial instability, further evaluation with brain and cervical spine MRI was recommended; nevertheless, these could not be performed due to limited resources. The family was counseled regarding cervical spine precautions, including avoidance of excessive neck flexion, extension, and high‐impact activities, and informed of the potential anesthetic risks associated with atlantoaxial instability. Regular ophthalmologic follow‐up with serial funduscopic examinations was advised to monitor papilledema and visual function. Referral to a tertiary center with neurosurgical expertise was recommended for further evaluation and management whenever feasible.

Transthoracic echocardiography was revealed no structural or valvular abnormalities.

Discussion

Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS) are a heterogeneous group of inherited lysosomal storage disorders resulting from deficiency of enzymes responsible for the degradation of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs). Impaired enzymatic activity leads to progressive intracellular accumulation of GAGs, causing multisystem involvement affecting the musculoskeletal, ocular, cardiovascular, and respiratory systems. Although cardiovascular involvement is well described in MPS, echocardiographic evaluation in our patients was unremarkable highlighting possible phenotypic variability or early disease stage.

MPS comprise multiple types and subtypes classified based on specific enzyme deficiencies, with most forms inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, except for MPS II (Hunter syndrome), which is X‐linked [1].

The global prevalence of MPS is estimated at approximately 1 in 25,000 live births; however, the condition remains substantially underdiagnosed in low‐resource settings, particularly in Africa, where access to specialized metabolic testing is limited. This under‐recognition contributes to delayed diagnosis and inappropriate management [3, 4].

In this familial case series, both patients exhibited classical phenotypic features strongly suggestive of MPS, including coarse facial features, short stature, progressive joint contractures, and characteristic skeletal deformities. The presence of similar manifestations among siblings, along with a history of early childhood deaths, further supports an inherited metabolic disorder. The occurrence of disease in half‐siblings supports an autosomal recessive mode of inheritance, indicating that both mothers are carriers and that the father is also a carrier. Although uncommon, this pattern is plausible in populations with a high rate of consanguinity and should be highlighted in genetic counseling.

Musculoskeletal involvement was the predominant and most disabling feature in both patients. Progressive joint stiffness, deformities, and contractures resulted from GAGs deposition within cartilages, ligaments, and periarticular tissues, ultimately leading to significant functional impairment. Notably, both patients were initially misdiagnosed with inflammatory arthropathy and treated with disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drugs without clinical benefit. This highlights an important diagnostic pitfall, as MPS can mimic juvenile idiopathic arthritis or rheumatoid arthritis, but typically lacks inflammatory features.

Ophthalmologic and otolaryngologic manifestations further supported the diagnosis. Corneal clouding, observed in both patients, is a well‐recognized feature of several MPS subtypes, particularly MPS I and VI, and may lead to progressive visual impairment. The presence of papilledema in the younger sibling raises concern for possible increased intracranial pressure, which has been reported in MPS due to impaired cerebrospinal fluid dynamics [2]. This represents a critical safety issue, particularly given the known anesthetic and neurological risks in MPS patients.

Additionally, narrowing of the auditory canals reflects GAGs deposition within otologic structures and may predispose to conductive hearing loss [5]. Oral and dental findings of MPS are characteristically present. They include flattened alveolar ridges with hyperplastic gingiva, peg‐shaped teeth, delayed dental eruption, and malocclusions that are frequently observed [6].

Radiological evaluation played a pivotal role in establishing the diagnosis. Both patients demonstrated classical features of dysostosis multiplex, including J‐shaped sella turcica and characteristic vertebral and appendicular skeletal abnormalities. These radiographic findings, in conjunction with the clinical phenotype, provided strong clinical and radiological suspicion for MPS in the absence of confirmatory biochemical or genetic testing.

The absence of advanced diagnostic modalities, including urinary GAGs quantification, enzyme assays, and genetic testing, represents a major challenge in our setting. Similar limitations have been reported in other low‐resource countries, where diagnosis often relies heavily on clinical suspicion and radiological findings. This underscores the importance of increasing awareness among clinicians to recognize early phenotypic clues and avoid misdiagnosis [7, 8]. Dried blood spot enzyme assays, which can be sent to international reference laboratories, offer a practical diagnostic alternative. Several international programs offer free testing for patients in low‐resource settings, making this a viable option in similar contexts. Although definitive subtyping was not possible, the presence of corneal clouding in both patients and the involvement of a female sibling argue against X‐linked MPS II (Hunter syndrome) and make MPS III less likely. These findings are more consistent with autosomal recessive forms such as MPS I or MPS VI, which are therefore considered the most likely clinical subtypes.

MPS is associated with progressive morbidity, functional decline, and reduced quality of life. In our patients, delayed diagnosis resulted in advanced disease at presentation, with significant physical disability and loss of independence. Beyond the clinical burden, the disease imposes considerable psychosocial and economic challenges, particularly in resource‐constrained environments.

Although disease‐specific therapies such as enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) have shown benefit in slowing disease progression, these interventions remain inaccessible in Sudan due to high cost and lack of specialized centers. Consequently, management is largely supportive and requires a multidisciplinary approach, including physiotherapy, orthopedic care, and regular auditory and ophthalmologic assessment [9, 10].

This case series highlights the critical need for improved recognition of MPS in low‐resource settings and emphasizes the importance of considering lysosomal storage disorders in patients presenting with non‐inflammatory joint disease and characteristic skeletal abnormalities. Early diagnosis is essential to optimize outcomes, even in settings where definitive therapies are not readily available.

Conclusion

This case series highlights the substantial burden of mucopolysaccharidosis in resource‐limited settings, where delayed recognition and lack of diagnostic facilities may lead to advanced disease and severe disability. Early clinical suspicion, multidisciplinary care, improved diagnostic accessibility, and genetic counseling are essential, particularly in populations with high rates of consanguineous marriage.
