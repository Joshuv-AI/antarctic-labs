Synthesis and characterization of sustainable Zinc Oxide nanoparticles for waxy crude flow assurance application

Abubakar Aji, Hisham Khaled Ben Mahmud, Abdullah Abduljabbar, Ibnelwaleed A Hussein, Nik Ahmad Nizam Nik Malek, Victor Damilare Ola, Mysara Eissa Mohyaldinn

ABSTRACT
Wax precipitation in crude oil pipelines is a challenge to production. Current inhibitors raise concerns about toxicity and environmental impact. Hence, the need for sustainable alternatives arises. This study aims to explore the Vitex doniana-assisted synthesis, characterization, and application of zinc oxide nanoparticles as crude oil wax inhibitors. The nanomaterial was characterized using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy, Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry, X-Ray Diffraction, Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy, Energy-Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy, and Thermogravimetric Analysis. The application of 0.04 wt% zinc oxide was evaluated based on wax appearance temperature, rheological properties, and wax-inhibition efficiency. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy analysis on the ethanolic Vitex doniana leaf extract confirmed the presence of alcohols, phenolics, carbonyls, aromatics, proteins, and polyphenols responsible for nanoparticle reduction and stabilization of the nanoparticle. Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry of the extracts identified 18 distinct peaks in the organic profile, revealing a mixture of esters, aldehydes, acids, and heterocycles. The dominance of n‑hexane (34.9%) and glycidyl oleate (21.2%) highlights hydrocarbon and ester fractions as major components. X-Ray Diffraction analysis confirmed zinc oxide nanocrystals in a pure hexagonal wurtzite phase (a = 3.243 Å, c = 5.195 Å) with an average crystallite size of 36.1 nm and microstrain of 1.4 × 10 − ⁴, evidencing high crystallinity and suitability for optoelectronic and photocatalytic applications. Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy-Energy-Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy confirmed elemental composition of 64.3 wt% Zn, 20.6 wt% O, 15.1 wt% residual C, and trace N, evidencing high purity. Thermogravimetric Analysis indicated high thermal stability with minimal mass loss (1.308% at ~118.7 °C, 3.340% at ~227.9 °C, and 7.427% at ~391.8 °C; total mass loss < 12.1% up to ~800 °C). Rheological analysis showed a reduction in viscosity of up to 56.2% at 23 °C when crude oil was blended with zinc oxide nanoparticles. The Cross-Polarized Microscopy test showed that zinc oxide modified the wax structure, resulting in clustered morphology, suggesting a cocrystallization mechanism. The Arrhenius plot of viscosity showed that zinc oxide reduced the flow activation energy from 213.6 kJ mol − ¹ to 207.6 kJ mol − ¹, confirming the effectiveness of zinc oxide nanoparticles in improving crude oil flow by disrupting wax-crystal networks. These findings confirm the successful production of high-quality, thermally stable, and surface-functionalized zinc oxide nanoparticles, suitable for various industrial applications, especially waxy crude flow assurance.

Introduction Wax precipitation in crude oil pipelines presents significant flow assurance challenges, increasing operational expenses and production risks [ 1 ]. Waxy crude oils are prone to solidification under sub-ambient pipeline conditions, leading to wax deposits that restrict flow, reduce pipeline efficiency, and increase operational costs [ 2 ]. Conventional wax mitigation techniques, such as chemical pour point depressants and thermal or mechanical methods, are widely employed. However, they have several limitations, including high cost, energy-intensive operation, and potential environmental hazards associated with chemical use [ 3 ]. These drawbacks necessitate the development of alternative, environmentally benign materials that mitigate wax precipitation and deposition in a more sustainable manner. In recent years, the escalating demand for sustainable and environmentally benign technologies has propelled the field of green synthesis to the forefront of materials science [ 4 ]. This approach emphasizes the use of natural resources and eco-friendly methods for material production, minimizing reliance on hazardous chemicals typically associated with conventional synthesis routes [ 5 , 6 ]. On the other hand, nanomaterials, particularly metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs) such as Zinc oxide (ZnO), have attracted significant attention due to their diverse applications including optoelectronics, photocatalysis [ 7 ], electrochemical performance [ 8 ] and medicine [ 9 ]. However, traditional methods for synthesizing the NPs often involve toxic precursors and generate harmful byproducts, necessitating the exploration of cleaner alternatives [ 10 ]. Green synthesis is an environmentally friendly approach to synthesizing materials, including nanomaterials, using sustainable methods and minimizing hazardous chemicals. It involves using natural resources, such as plant extracts, microorganisms, and enzymes, as reducing and capping agents in the synthesis process. Among the various green synthesis strategies, the use of plant extracts has emerged as a promising approach. This biological mediation offers a cost-effective, scalable, and non-toxic route for nanoparticle (NP) fabrication [ 11 ]. Specifically, plants contain phytochemicals such as flavonoids, alkaloids, and terpenoids, which can act as reducing and stabilizing agents, and could be used in the synthesis of metal oxide nanocomposites, while the resulting nanomaterial can serve as an environmentally benign wax inhibitor, aligning with the goals of sustainable flow assurance [ 12 ]. This study focuses on the green synthesis of ZnO NPs utilizing the leaves extract of Vitex doniana (V. doniana) , as well as characterization, and application of the nanoparticles for crude oil wax inhibition. The choice of this plant is motivated by its traditional medicinal uses and the presence of bioactive compounds that can effectively mediate the synthesis process [ 13 ]. The V. doniana ( Fig 1 , SD1) is widely distributed in Africa, particularly in Ghana, Burkina Faso, Nigeria, and Cameroon [ 14 ]. It produces small, white flowers and spherical, green fruits that turn black-purplish when ripe. The fruits have a woody seed, an approximate height of 1.5−20 cm, and a width of 1.0–1.2 cm. Methodology The plant extract was analyzed using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) and Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS). At the time, the biosynthesized ZnO NPs were characterized using X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM), Energy-Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy (EDS), and Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) to evaluate their functional groups, crystalline structure, thermal stability, morphology, elemental composition, and purity. For quantitative particle size analysis, FESEM micrographs were further processed using a high-throughput Python-based workflow (SciPy, NumPy, and Pillow). For particle sizing, FESEM images were analyzed using a calibrated Python-based workflow with Gaussian normalization and threshold-based segmentation, followed by a distance-transform-based seed-detection algorithm to resolve agglomerated particles. As for the application studies, Cross-Polarized Microscopy (CPM) and rheology tests were also conducted to assess the efficacy of the NPs on waxy crude oil. Material and Instrument The reagents used were Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH), Zinc Nitrate [Zn(NO 3 ) 2 ], Ethanol (C 2 H 6 O), and the plant extract. The instruments used were a hot plate with mixer, a furnace, a KUBOTA Centrifuge, a Drying Oven, an Ultrasonic Homogenizer, FTIR (Brand: Perkin Elmer, Model: Frontier 01), GC-MS (Brand: Agilent, Model: GC 5877), XRD (Panalytical Model, brand Xpert3 Powder), TGA (Brand: Perkin Elmer, Model: STA6000) and FESEM (Zeiss brand, model V55). As for the software, Origin, and HighScore were used to analyze some of the results. A hot plate was used for controlled heating and mixing, while the furnace enabled high-temperature calcination for ZnO crystallization. The KUBOTA centrifuge ensured efficient NP separation, and the drying oven facilitated moisture removal. Analytical instruments (FTIR, GC–MS, XRD, and FESEM) were employed for structural, morphological, and compositional analysis, ensuring comprehensive characterization. A rheometer was used for the rheology test. Extraction of V. doniana Leaves Fresh leaves of V. doniana were collected on 13 May 2024 from the school premises of Nile University of Nigeria, Abuja, Nigeria. This was the late dry‑to‑early rainy season transition, a period known to influence phytochemical composition. Dr. Namla identified the plant from the Department of Biological Sciences, Nile University of Nigeria. The leaves were dried in the shade at room temperature to protect the phytochemicals from degradation by sunlight. Approximately 115 g of fresh V. doniana leaves yielded 100 g of leaf powder, based on the reported moisture content of 13.08% [ 24 ], giving a fresh‑to‑powder ratio of 1.15: 1. Once dried, the leaves were chopped and ground into a fine powder using a blender for extraction. The dried and ground leaves (100 g) of V. doniana were boiled in deionized water for 30 minutes at 100 °C, continuously stirred at 200 rpm using a magnetic stirrer, to extract the bioactive components. After boiling, the mixture was filtered to obtain the clear leaf extract. The filtrate was stored in the refrigerator for further use in the synthesis process [ 25 ]. Accurate FTIR results require a solvent-free sample to avoid solvent-induced peak overlap. Consequently, ethanol was employed to extract the phytochemicals using a Soxhlet extractor [ 26 ]. A 10g sample of V. doniana leaves was extracted for 6 hours until the color of the recycling solvent became pale, indicating complete extraction. The solvent was subsequently removed from the samples using a rotary evaporator, and the residue was dried in an oven to a constant weight before submission for the analysis [ 27 ]. Synthesis of ZnO NPs To synthesize ZnO NPs, 10 mL of aqueous extract of V. doniana was added to Erlenmeyer flasks and brought up to 50 mL with distilled water. It was then stirred for 10 minutes at 150 rpm at room temperature. A 50 g mass of Zn(NO 3 ) 2 was dissolved in the above extract solution. It was then stirred for 30 minutes at 150 rpm at room temperature. The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath at 70 °C for 10 min until a brown precipitate formed, then allowed to cool at room temperature under continuous stirring for 15 min. Freshly prepared 0.5 M NaOH solution (60 mL) was added under continuous stirring until the color changed and a milky precipitate formed. Green-synthesized ZNO NPs were purified by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 30 min. The final product suspension was dried at room temperature for 5 days, oven-dried at 120 °C for 4 h, and calcined in the furnace at 600 °C [ 28 ]. Characterization of Components Characterization plays a pivotal role in understanding the physicochemical properties of green-synthesized NPs, as these attributes directly influence their performance in targeted applications such as wax inhibition in crude oil systems. The efficiency of ZnO NPs in preventing paraffin wax precipitation is strongly dependent on their size, surface morphology, crystallinity, thermal stability, and surface functional groups, which collectively determine their dispersion ability, interaction with crude oil constituents, and capacity to modify wax crystallization behavior [ 29 ]. A combination of analytical techniques was therefore employed to comprehensively examine the synthesized material. FESEM provides insights into the surface morphology and particle size distribution, which are critical for nanoparticle-to-oil interface interactions. XRD analysis elucidates the crystalline structure and phase purity, parameters essential for correlating structural properties with wax inhibition efficiency. EDS complements this by confirming elemental composition. TGA assesses the thermal stability and decomposition patterns of ZnO NPs to ensure suitability for high-temperature pipeline environments. Meanwhile, FTIR and GC-MS are applied to the V. doniana leaf extract to identify phytochemicals and functional groups responsible for the reduction, stabilization, and surface modification of the NPs [ 30 ]. Together, these characterization tools provide critical parameters, including particle size, crystallinity, elemental composition, thermal stability, and bioactive functional groups, that establish the foundation for understanding V. doniana -mediated ZnO NPs as sustainable wax inhibitors. Application of ZnO to Crude Oil Accurate investigation of the interaction between crude oil and inhibitors necessitated proper sample preparation. One hundred grams (100g) of Dulang crude oil were measured into a beaker and subsequently preheated in an oven at 70 °C for 90 minutes (1.5 hours). This preheating step was essential to guarantee the complete dissolution of any wax that had previously crystallized within the oil [ 31 ]. Subsequently, 0.04 wt% [39] of the synthesized ZnO NPs were blended with the crude oil using an ultrasonicator [ 32 ]. The actual settings to disperse the nanomaterial are power = 500, T-Run = 10 minutes, T-On=5.0 s, T-Off=0.05 s, and temperature = 70 °C. This procedure ensured uniform dispersion of ZnO NPs within the crude matrix, enabling reliable evaluation of their influence on wax inhibition and flow behavior. The subsequent analyses focused on compositional characterization and rheological performance to determine the effectiveness of the nanoparticles. Results and discussion The compositional and rheological analyses were conducted to understand how ZnO NPs interact with the waxy components of Dulang crude oil. The results provide insight into the crude’s molecular composition, wax content, and flow characteristics, forming the basis for evaluating the nanoparticle’s inhibition efficiency. FTIR Evaluation of V. doniana Leaves Extract The FTIR spectrum of V. doniana leaf extract in Fig 3 (SD3) highlights the presence of several bioactive functional groups that are pivotal in the green synthesis of metal oxide NPs like ZnO. These functional groups function as reducing, stabilizing, and capping agents, facilitating NP formation and ensuring colloidal stability. 10.1371/journal.pone.0358989.g003 Fig 3 FTIR of V. doniana leaf extract illustrating major absorption bands. The FTIR spectrum of the V. doniana leaf extract exhibits several prominent absorption bands that reflect the presence of diverse phytochemical constituents responsible for its reducing and stabilizing abilities in NP synthesis. A broad and weak band observed in the region of 3400–3300 cm − ¹ corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations, typically associated with alcohols and phenolic compounds [ 33 ]. These groups are widely reported in plant extracts and are known to play critical roles in hydrogen bonding and metal ion reduction. A strong and sharp absorption in the 2920–2850 cm − ¹ region is indicative of C–H stretching of aliphatic −CH 2 and −CH 3 groups, confirming the presence of long-chain hydrocarbons and other organic metabolites [ 35 ]. A distinct and intense peak around 1730–1700 cm − ¹ is attributed to the C = O stretching of carbonyl groups, commonly found in aldehydes, ketones, esters, or carboxylic acids [ 36 , 37 ]. The strength of this band suggests a considerable abundance of carbonyl-containing biomolecules, which are often reported as effective capping and stabilizing agents during NP formation. Additionally, absorptions located between 1650 and 1550 cm − ¹ correspond to C = C stretching vibrations of aromatic rings and, in some cases, amide I and II bands arising from proteins or alkaloids. These compounds further contribute to reduction and stabilization processes [ 38 ]. The region between 1500 and 1000 cm − ¹ displays multiple peaks indicating the presence of C–O, C–N, and C–O–C stretching vibrations, typically associated with polyphenols, flavonoids, glycosides, and other oxygenated phytochemicals [ 36 , 37 ]. The complexity of this fingerprint region reflects the heterogeneous phytochemical profile of V. doniana . XRD evaluation of ZnO The crystalline structure of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles was examined using XRD, as illustrated in Fig 5 (SD5), to determine the phase composition and microstructural parameters. The sharp and intense peaks observed in the diffractogram indicate a high degree of crystallinity in the ZnO NPs. Notably, no secondary phases or impurity-related peaks were detected, suggesting that the synthesized ZnO is phase-pure. 10.1371/journal.pone.0358989.g005 Fig 5 XRD pattern of ZnO NPs showing sharp diffraction characteristic peaks. The observed diffraction peaks at 2θ = 31.83°, 34.50°, 36.33°, 47.65°, 56.73°, and 63.01° correspond to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), and (103) planes of ZnO, respectively. The dominant (101) reflection at 2θ = 36.33° is characteristic of the wurtzite ZnO phase, as confirmed in the literature [ 41 – 43 ]. The diffraction pattern confirmed a single-phase hexagonal wurtzite structure [ 43 ] with space group P6 3 mc. The lattice constants a and c for the hexagonal lattice system were determined using the hexagonal interplanar spacing equation ( equation 1 ) derived from Bragg’s law (2d sin θ = λ, where n = 1): 1 d 2 = ( 4 3 ) × [ ( h 2 + hk + k 2 ) a 2 ] + ( l 2 c 2 ) (1) where ‘d’ is the interplanar spacing, (h, k, l) are the Miller indices of the crystallographic planes, and ‘a’ and ‘c’ are the lattice constants. This yielded a = 3.243 Å and c = 5.195 Å, with a c/a ratio of 1.602, closely matching the standard wurtzite ZnO structure (a = 3.249 Å, c = 5.206 Å). Such consistency indicates structural integrity and the absence of secondary phases, suggesting a high-purity crystalline material, as displayed in equation (1) . The reference intensity ratio (RIR) is 5.64, indicating a moderate scattering profile in multiphase analysis. This structural data supports the assignment of ZnO to the ZnS(2H) polytype and forms the basis for its widespread use in optoelectronic, photocatalytic, and nanocomposite applications [ 44 ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that materials with high surface area and surface-active functionalities play a critical role in modifying wax crystallization and deposition. For instance, Silica and alumina NPs have been shown to effectively reduce the wax appearance temperature and modify wax crystal growth in oil systems. These NPs act as heterogeneous nucleation sites, leading to a delayed onset of wax crystallization, reduced wax deposition, and altered crystal morphology, typically resulting in smaller, more regularly shaped, and finely dispersed wax crystals [ 48 ]. The surface-bound carbon may contribute to steric hindrance and functional group diversity, enhancing np dispersibility and interfacial activity in hydrocarbon matrices [ 49 , 50 ], making such NPs highly effective for applications ranging from biomedical imaging to catalysis and emulsion stabilization [ 51 ]. The EDS mappings ( Fig 8 , SD8) from this dataset further confirm the spatial homogeneity and compositional depth of the green-synthesized ZnO NPs. Clear, distinct distributions of Zn (purple), C (red), O (green), and N (orange) throughout the scanned area indicate well-integrated elemental domains. The presence of oxygen is directly associated with the formation of the ZnO lattice, thereby confirming the presence of the oxide framework. The nitrogen signal, albeit faint, is particularly noteworthy, as it implies incorporation of nitrogenous organic compounds from the bio-precursor, potentially offering additional surface functionalities [ 52 ] or from the substrate -NO 3 used during the synthesis. This indeed contributes to the steric and electronic modulation at the particle surface. Such features can enhance binding with hydrocarbon chains and promote thermal dissipation; qualities essential for delaying wax nucleation in crude oil systems [ 53 ]. 10.1371/journal.pone.0358989.g008 Fig 8 EDS elemental mapping and analysis of bio-mediated ZnO NPs. In the first weight loss (80.00 °C to 118.71 °C, ΔY = 1.308%), the minor decrease corresponds to the desorption of physically adsorbed moisture and volatile organic remnants from the green synthesis using V. doniana extract, confirming the presence of biodegradable stabilizers and effective post-synthesis drying [69]. The second weight loss (118.71 °C to 227.90 °C, ΔY = 3.34%) can be ascribed to the partial decomposition of surface-bound hydroxyl groups, adsorbed oxygen species, and residual phytochemical capping agents. Hence, limited total mass loss (<12% up to ~800 °C) demonstrates that the biosynthesized ZnO NPs are predominantly inorganic, with only a modest fraction of bio-derived stabilizers. Conclusion This study establishes a sustainable and functionally effective pathway for wax mitigation via the green synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles using V. doniana leaf extract. The novelty of this work is twofold. For the first time, this study demonstrates the successful use of V. doniana phytochemicals as bio-reductants and stabilizing agents in the synthesis of phase-pure ZnO nanoparticles. The resulting particles exhibit circular, irregular, and hexagonal wurtzite structures (a = 3.243 Å, c = 5.195 Å), nanoscale crystallite size (~33.5 nm with mean particle size of 36.1 nm), and low lattice strain (1.4 × 10 − ⁴), confirming their high crystallinity and structural integrity. Second, it provides the first systematic evaluation of these bio-derived ZnO nanoparticles as wax inhibitors in a real waxy crude oil system. The synthesized nanoparticles exhibited favorable physicochemical properties, high thermal stability (total mass loss < 12.1% up to ~800 °C), and surface functionalization by residual phytochemicals, collectively enhancing interfacial activity. These attributes translated into measurable improvements in flow assurance, with a maximum viscosity reduction of 56.2% at 23 °C, a decrease in flow activation energy from 213.6 to 207.6 kJ mol − ¹, and a peak inhibition efficiency of ~56.2% within the wax crystallization window. Complementary cross-polarized microscopy confirmed a transition from needle-like wax structures to clustered morphologies, evidencing a cocrystallization-based inhibition mechanism. Despite these promising findings, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted at a single nanoparticle concentration (0.04 wt%), and thus does not capture concentration-dependent optimization or threshold behavior. Additionally, the experiments were performed under controlled laboratory conditions using a single crude oil type (Dulang crude), which may limit direct generalization to field-scale pipeline systems with varying compositions, pressures, and shear environments. Furthermore, long-term dispersion stability, potential nanoparticle agglomeration under dynamic flow conditions, and environmental impact assessments were not addressed and warrant further investigation. Therefore, the findings provide strong experimental evidence that plant-mediated ZnO nanoparticles can serve as environmentally benign and effective wax inhibitors, while also highlighting the need for extended parametric and field-scale validation to fully establish their industrial applicability.
