 ABSTRACT

The overall goal of this project is to develop a high-volume electrode electrolyte assembly (EEA)
production capability to significantly increase throughput of solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) manufacturing
and reduce the cost while maintaining the same level of performance. We pursued four approaches: (1)
defining key parameters of the lamination window and optimizing the process to control the properties
and performance of the EEA laminate; (2) scale-up of the process to demonstrate fabrication of >10 ft
EEA laminate; (3) using slot-die coating to increase the throughput of EEA fabrication to target >5 m/min
for the thick, anode layer; and (4) minimization of the anode thickness to reduce material cost. Results
accumulated throughout the execution of the project will create the following benefits:

  > 10 fold increase in manufacture speed of anode support layer (ASL)
  > 30% reduction in manufacture cost
  > tenfold reduction of material cost
  no loss in performance versus cells made using conventional manufacturing
Demonstrating the fabrication of > 10 ft of the EEA laminate, closes remaining technical gaps and paves
the way forward for the commercialization readiness of this technology.

The major project goals remaining to be met is:
  confirmation of the fabricated cell using this process demonstrates no loss in performance
 versus cells made using conventional manufacturing.

The cell processing and performance test role was the responsibility of project partner Redox, which
became non-responsive during the project implementation. Due to management changes in the company,
this goal had not been completed in the agreed upon timeline. ORNL initiated this portion of work and
intends to collaborate with a new CRADA partner to finish the project.

2.3 Q3: Rheology and microscopy studies were used to investigate the ASL, AFL and GDC slurries
regarding coating processibility and quality based on the solid wt% loading of the slurries. In this work,
the ASL coating thickness was minimized and the slurry effects on coating quality were evaluated on
doctor blade coatings, as well as the rheological properties of all three slurries, which contribute to a
stable coating processibility window in slot-die coating. Highlights of the major progress include:

 • Successfully developed slurry formulation for slot-die coating
 • Fabricated both defect-free AFL and GDC with a very thin thickness (>5 m/min)
 • Fabricated thick ASL via slot-die coating (>5 m/min)

In this quarter, we focused on evaluating the rheological properties of the ASL, AFL and GDC slurries
with various solid contents, which will help predict the coating window.

Slurry Formulation:

Slurries of the AFL, ASL and GDC were prepared according to the formulations provided by Redox.
Each formulation was prepared and tested with solid loadings of: 70 wt%, 65 wt%, 60 wt% and 55 wt%, a
reasonable composition of binder, plasticizer, and dispersing agents. The slurries were prepared in a 2-
step addition and 2 overnight mixing steps using a ball mill. Each mixing step was kept the same between
slurries.

Originally, slurries with a 75% solids content were targeted, however, only the ASL resulted in a
homogeneous mixture, therefore further dilution and mixing was carried out to reach 70% solids for all
three of the slurries, followed by coating each sample onto Mylar using the doctor blade method, and
rheological evaluation of each slurry, as well as microscopy evaluation of the quality of the final, dried
coatings.

Rheology Study:

 6

To evaluate the rheological behavior for the slurries at various levels of solid loading, a test method
(described below) was used in a concentric cylinder rheometer (Discovery HR-3, TA Instruments)
operated by TRIOS software. The instrument was calibrated, with the test cup equilibrated to 25 °C prior
to transferring each of the prepared slurry samples, volumetrically into the test cup.

Experimental Procedure:

 Step 1. Pre-shear of 5 s-1 applied for 60 s.
 – Provides consistent shear history between samples to reduce hysteresis effects.
 Step 2. Flow Ramp logarithmic ramp between 5 to 3500 s-1 over 5 min.
 – Measures stress, providing the samples viscosity as a function of shear rate.
 Step 3. Oscillation Time Sweep angular frequency (ω) of 100 rad·s -1 and oscillation strain (g) of
                0.1% for 15 min.
 – Rebuilds the fluid’s bridging flocculation network structure which is disrupted at high
 shear rates.
 Step 4. Amplitude Sweep ω = 100 rad·s-1, between g = 0.01-10%
 – Characterizes strength of network structure by measuring the dependency of G’ and G’’
 on strain.
 Step 5. Oscillation Time Sweep angular frequency (ω) of 100 rad·s -1 and oscillation strain g of
                0.1% for 5 min.
 – Shorter rebuild time required for amplitude sweep (less destructive than the flow ramp
 test).
 Step 6. Frequency Sweep between ω = 100-0.1 rad·s -1 at g = 0.1%.
 – Frequency sweep within LVR (linear viscoelastic region) provides insight into the degree
 of gelling in the suspension.

Flow Ramp Characterization: Non-Newtonian, Shear Thinning Behavior

In slot-die coating, a slurry with a low-shear-rate viscosity (LSV) and lower high-shear-rate viscosity
(HSV)is ideal. A high LSV promotes sharper edges while lower HSV values allow higher solids loading
and faster coating rates. As shown in Figure 8, all slurries showed shear thinning behavior, which was
more pronounced at higher solid contents. The viscosity trend follows: GDC>AFL>ASL, which is in
agreement with the surface areas of the active materials.

Figure 8 Dynamic viscosity curves indicating shear thinning behavior for all samples tested. The viscosity

Amplitude Sweep Experiment: Probing Liquid Network Strength

The strength of the liquid network was characterized by measuring dependency of G’ and G’’ on strain.
Within the LVR small micro cracks form, but network remains largely intact, outside of the LVR,
however, macro fractures of network begin, and a 5% drop in moduli indicates the end of LVR. As shown
in Figure 10, the storage modulus (G’) generally increases with increasing solid content. However, the G’
reduces when further increasing the solid content from 65% to 70% for the AFL and GDC. Figure 11
shows the phase angle, which reveals whether the slurries behave more like a solid or a liquid.

Figure 10 Amplitude sweep experiment showing data for the different solid loadings of the ASL, AFL
and GDC slurries. Storage (closed symbol) and loss (open symbol) moduli. The LVR for each condition
terminates at the marker on G’.

Doctor Blade Coatings: Quality Check Using Microscopy

Coatings of the AFL, ASL and GDC slurries were cast using the doctor blade method onto Mylar. The
coatings were dried in a ventilated enclosure within a dry room, at room temperature. Each dry film’s
thickness was measured and a Keyence VHX-1000 microscope was used to evaluate differences in
morphology between slurries with varying concentrations of solid loading. It was noted that after drying,
surface defects such as air entrapment and cracks became visible in certain samples.

Features such as grain structure, surface roughness, and surface defects were observed under varying
magnifications and using software assisted 3D surface analysis. After drying, certain defects can be
reduced by calendering to fill in gaps and cracks with binder, or formulation changes can be made to
increase polymeric binders that increase contact between the active materials. Figures 14-17 show the
image of the various coatings. For the ASL coating, lower solid content could result in cracks when
forming thick films. The AFL and GDC coatings seem similar. Some surface defects are observed which
likely due to trapped air.

Conclusion of Rheology and Microscopy Studies

Coatings were cast onto Mylar from the ASL, AFL and GDC slurries, using the doctor blade method.
Rheology experiments were used to evaluate the effect that solid loading has on processability of the
slurries. Microscopy was used to determine the quality of the dried coating and to investigate surface
defects that may be present in order to support optimizing EEA manufacture. Achieving a stable coating
parameter window in slot-die coating is not fully understood for non-Newtonian fluids. Furthermore, high
coating speeds and a high-quality product are paramount for economically manufacturing the EEA.

During this quarter, the project has achieved the following:
  Demonstrated coating of the 2 and three layers of: AFL/GDC, AFL/ASL and ASL/AFL/GDC via
 slot-die coating.
  Fabricated varying thickness of ASL/ASL via slot-die coating from 100 - 700 µm.
  Fabricated EEA with the minimal ASL thickness (~400 µm of the 2-layer ASL).

The progress made in this period demonstrated the manufacturing potential when compared to the reverse
order of coating which was reported in Q3. In the previous work, the thickest layer (ASL) was coated first
and then subsequent coatings were deposited on this template. Variations in surface roughness caused the
thin AFL layer (~18 µm) to have periodic areas of surface defects and also experienced some fluctuations
in AFL layer thickness. This effect is mitigated by deposition of equivalent thickness of the ASL layer

 13

onto the AFL and/or AFL/GDC as substrate. By reversing the order of coating, clear benefits have been
observed in consistent layer thickness and removal of surface defects throughout the coating process.

For future work requiring laminating the coated layers into a completed EEA, materials to be used have
been fabricated, which includes 10 ft of ASL/AFL and > 5 ft of ASL/ASL/AFL along with > 20 ft of

2.7 Q7: The processing window for lamination was determined by varying processing speed, separating
force and temperature. After determination of appropriate speed and force conditions on the Fenn May
Mill in the 4-High configuration, the screening of processing temperature proceeded at three levels,

 16

90°C, 110°C and 130°C, for calendering of the EEA in various configurations, including: lamination
layer-by-layer, 2-layer/2-layer and 3-layer/1-layer.

Previous efforts included the processing window at these temperatures as well as room temperature on the
BMF’s heated calender. As an alternative to this equipment which is unavailable due to maintenance, we
implemented a 2-stage process, with samples preheated at the set temperature until equilibration in a
convection oven, followed by lamination at 25 FPM (feet per minute) and 16,000 pounds of separating
force on the Fenn 4-High mill. The residence time of the sample heat treatment was 5 minutes, with the
desired temperature checked to be at equilibrium with a temperature reader.

The lamination configurations for the SOFC EEA stack (ASL/ASL/AFL/GDC) fabrication proceeded via
the following configurations: 1) single-layer buildup: ASL+ASL  ASL/ASL + AFL  ASL/ASL/AFL
+ GDC, and multiple-layer coatings laminated together: 2) ASL/ASL + AFL  ASL/ASL/AFL + GDC,
3) ASL/ASL + AFL/GDC and 4) ASL/ASL/AFL + GDC. Lamination of samples was carried out between
the silicone coated Mylar substrate on which they were coated, resulting in the samples never coming into
contact with the roller of the calendering equipment. The results demonstrated that excellent quality EEA
can be prepared via lamination at 90 °C, 25 FPM, and 16,000 pounds separating force, which was
achieved with a 6 to 9% reduction of the total EEA stack thickness.

As had been previously determined, limiting the amount of passes in the calender is preferred, with high
yield and less potential of introducing damage to the EEA laminate surface due to material slipping
during processing in the mill. For example, while processing in configuration 1 (layer-by-layer
lamination) provided excellent interface contact, it required three passes on the mill as opposed to the
other configurations, which limited introduction of processing defects. Surface imperfections may be
resolved during binder burn out, under high temperature firing, and this will be investigated in upcoming
work.

Visual inspection, optical microscopy and SEM imaging of the laminates were used to determine the level
of contact at the interface between the layers brought together via lamination. Processing conditions and
results are summarized in Table 3 , along with optical images of the fabricated materials. The yellow and
red rows in Table 3 were processed with thick ASL layers, while the green rows utilized thin ASL and the
reduction in thickness and percentage compression is provided in the table.

Conclusions: The optimal manufacturing processing in terms of quality of the EEA stack at this stage of
fabrication would be the layer-by-layer deposition via slot-die coating to form the ASL/ASL/AFL
assembly followed by lamination of a separately coated GDC electrolyte layer to create the EEA stack,
through a single pass of lamination at 90 °C. This allowed for effective EEA fabrication, mild processing
temperatures, avoided slot-die coating the thin (~10 μm) electrolyte layer, onto a pre-coated 3-layer
substrate, which is prone to surface defects, even at the micron scale, resulting in issues in slot-die coating
process.

 CONCLUSIONS

Roll-to-Roll Manufacturing of Solid Oxide Fuel Cells project has demonstrated that the slot-die coating
and subsequent lamination by continuous calendering is an effective way to manufacture the EEA for
SOFC economically and in large scale, paving the way for the mass production of the low-cost, high
quality SOFC and SOEC, which could potentially play an important role in securing American energy
security.
