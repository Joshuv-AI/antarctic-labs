Identification of N4-Acetylcytidine-Related Biomarkers in Adult Asthma: An Integrative Multi-Omics Study
Authors: Min Zhang, Hairong Lin, Yanmei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAdult asthma (AA) is a chronic respiratory disease that significantly affects morbidity and quality of life. N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) might have a crucial part in facilitating the occurrence and development of inflammatory diseases. The objective of this research was to find biomarkers associated with ac4C in AA, potentially paving the way for targeted therapies.MethodsThe data of AA were procured from a public database, and ac4C-related genes (ac4CRGs) were downloaded from the literature. Candidate genes were obtained by overlapping the results of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and ac4CRGs. We conducted two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis with ac4C-related candidate genes as exposures and adult asthma as the outcome; instrumental variables were selected from genome-wide association study (GWAS) data, and causal associations were validated via inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median and other MR methods, with heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy tested to guarantee the reliability of causal inferences. The identification of biomarkers was facilitated by Mendelian randomization (MR), machine learning, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and gene expression analyses. Of great significance was the employment of the nomogram to assess the diagnostic effectiveness of the biomarkers. Following this, immune infiltration and drug prediction analyses were performed. Single-cell analysis was performed to acquire cell types and identify key cells by biomarker expression. Ultimately, in vitro, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was utilized to measure the expression levels of biomarkers.ResultsMR analysis verified that MCM5, TRIP6 and PALM were protective factors for AA (OR<1, P<0.05), while PUM1 and NPEPL1 were risk factors (OR>1, P<0.05). MCM5, PUM1, TRIP6, PALM, and NPEPL1 were identified as biomarkers. The nomogram developed utilizing these biomarkers demonstrated a satisfactory capacity for differentiating among various sample types. Moreover, MCM5 demonstrated substantial positive correlation with B cells and CD8 T cells (r > 0.30, P < 0.05). Furthermore, valproic acid simultaneously affected the 3 biomarkers PUM1, TRIP6, and NPEPL1. Single-cell analysis revealed that alveolar epithelial cells and mononuclear macrophages were considered key cell types that played a critical role in AA. A marked decrease in the levels of the 5 biomarkers were observed in the asthma mice samples as compared to the control mice samples.ConclusionThis study preliminary identified MCM5, PUM1, TRIP6, PALM, and NPEPL1 as biomarkers for AA, offering valuable insights that could provide preliminary clues for future mechanistic and therapeutic studies.

Introduction

Asthma is a heterogeneous condition defined by airway hyperresponsiveness, chronic inflammation, and reversible airflow obstruction. It affects approximately 330 million individuals globally, with the adult prevalence rate rising by 15% to 20% over the past two decades.1 Although the ongoing development of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and novel biologic agents has significantly improved symptoms and prognosis for some patients, approximately 10% to 20% continue to exhibit refractory phenotypes, such as severe asthma and steroid-resistant asthma. These phenotypes significantly impair patients’ quality of life and bring about an economic burden.2 There is not a definitive or universal standard for diagnosing asthma; rather, the diagnosis is ultimately based on clinical judgment. At present, several physiological measurements and biomarkers are associated with asthma. However, none of these are exclusive to this disease. This situation gives rise to underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis, thereby causing suboptimal treatment for asthma. Consequently, the need to discover novel biomarkers is of utmost urgency.3

Ac4C is a distinct chemical modification present in both eukaryotic and prokaryotic organisms. It is commonly observed in various types of RNA, including tRNA, rRNA, and mRNA, and represents one of the more evolutionarily conserved forms of nucleic acid modification.4 NAT10 is currently the only known acetyltransferase identified in eukaryotes and has been shown to participate in the acetylation modification of histones, tRNAs, 18S rRNAs, and mRNAs.5 Studies have demonstrated that mRNA acetylation contributes to the progression of cancer and other diseases, whereas a reduction in this modification is associated with dysregulated cell cycles and heightened inflammatory responses.6–8 It is noteworthy that NAT10-mediated ac4C modification plays a significant role in inflammatory diseases. In autoimmune disorders, NAT10 modulates immune responses and inflammatory pathways via ac4C modification.9 In idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, NAT10 enhances the stability of TGFB1 and PDK4 mRNAs through ac4C modification, thereby promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition and facilitating disease progression.10

Additionally, some research has demonstrated that ac4C modification plays a role in the pathogenesis of various lung diseases, including pulmonary fibrosis, sepsis-induced lung injury, and lung cancers.10–12 Considering that asthma is characterized by chronic airway inflammation, immune dysregulation, epithelial damage, mucus hypersecretion, and airway remodeling, the regulatory mechanisms associated with NAT10/ac4C may be particularly pertinent to the pathogenesis of asthma. It has been reported that NAT10 can regulate protein translation through the acetylation of cytidine in mRNA.13 NAT10 - mediated ac4C modification can also regulate mRNA stability and translation, thereby influencing inflammatory signaling, immune cell activation, epithelial - mesenchymal transition, cell proliferation, and stress response.14,15 These biological processes closely coincide with the key pathological features of AA.10 Studies have demonstrated that NAT10 is involved in the functional regulation and metabolic remodeling of airway epithelial cells, and the knockout of NAT10 leads to significant alterations in mitochondrial function in lung epithelial cells.16 Moreover, airway epithelial cells and immune cells are central participants in the progression of AA, and post - transcriptional RNA modification dysregulation may affect the expression of genes involved in cytokine production, antigen presentation, epithelial repair, and airway remodeling.17 Therefore, exploring genes related to NAT10/ac4C in AA may offer disease - specific insights into the underlying molecular mechanisms of AA and assist in identifying new diagnostic or therapeutic biomarkers. However, direct evidence connecting ac4C modification to AA remains scarce, further emphasizing the necessity for systematic studies on ac4C - related genes in the context of this disease.

Traditional genome-wide association studies (GWAS) are capable of identifying genetic loci associated with the risk of asthma. Nevertheless, given the intricate polygenic inheritance background of asthma and the circumstance that numerous significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are situated within linkage disequilibrium regions, it is arduous to precisely ascertain the candidate genes with functional significance solely based on the results of GWAS associations. In this research, Mendelian randomization (MR) was employed to examine whether the genetically predicted expression of ac4C-related candidate genes is associated with the risk of adult asthma, thus transcending simple genetic associations and effectively rectifying the limitations of traditional GWAS. MR leverages genetic variation to investigate causal relationships between modifiable exposures and health outcomes, offering a robust alternative to randomized controlled trials when they are impractical or ethically challenging.18 However, MR establishes causality but does not elucidate cellular heterogeneity or molecular mechanisms in pathogenesis of disease. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), a revolutionary transcriptomic technique, enables comprehensive characterization of cellular states at single-cell resolution.19 This approach has uncovered organized cellular diversity in vivo and clarified functional identities of distinct cell populations, with applications in diseases including Crohn’s disease, colorectal cancer, and asthma.20–23 Integrating MR with scRNA-seq bridges population-level causal inference and single-cell mechanistic insights, identifying key pathogenic genes and their expression in specific cell subpopulations. This strategy enhances understanding of functional heterogeneity and provides a theoretical foundation for precision medicine. Therefore, this research integrated publicly accessible asthma transcriptome and single-cell datasets with MR analysis to identify potential ac4C-associated biomarkers in AA. Comprehensive bioinformatics approaches were applied to investigate underlying mechanisms, followed by single-cell analysis to annotate relevant cell types and assess biomarker expression, offering novel insights into ac4C functional mechanisms in AA and clinical implications.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

The data of AA were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE147878 dataset (platform: GPL10558) served as the training set, containing 60 AA and 13 control endobronchial biopsies; while the GSE137268 dataset (platform: GPL6104) was used for validation, comprising 12 severe AA and 15 control induced sputum samples. In addition, 10 AA endobronchial brush samples and 11 control endobronchial brush samples were included in the scRNA-seq data GSE193816 (platform: GPL20795). Finally, a total of 2118 genes related to N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C), namely ac4C-related genes (ac4CRGs), were retrieved from the literature (Supplementary Table 1). 24

In the Integrative Epidemiology Unit Open genome-wide association study (IEU OpenGWAS) database (https://www.gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), asthma was examined to obtain the corresponding GWAS data (downloaded on May 1, 2025). The selection of the AA GWAS dataset (finn-b-J10_ASTHMA) was based on 2 criteria: first, the number of diseases exceeding 1000, and second, the maximum number of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). GWAS data included 16,380,176 SNPs in 156,078 European samples (20,629 case patients and 135,449 controls).

Differential Expression Analysis

First, perform differential expression gene (DEG) analysis to identify genes with dysregulated expression between adult asthma and control samples, thus determining disease - related transcriptional changes. To detect differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the AA group and the control group, DEGs were carried out by employing the limma package (v 3.56.2)25 based on a training set for both groups of samples (adjust. P < 0.05). Moreover, A volcano plot of DEGs was generated using the ggplot2 package (v 3.5.1),26 with the top 10 most significantly up- and down-regulated genes (ranked by |log2 FC|) labeled. Subsequently, the heatmap for these genes was plotted with the ComplexHeatmap package (version 2.16.0).27

Identification, Functional Enrichment, and Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) Network of Candidate Genes

The ggvenn package (v 0.1.10)28 was used to identify the intersection between DEGs and ac4CRGs in AA, defining the overlapping genes as candidates. Subsequently, the clusterProfiler package (v 4.8.3)29 was employed to perform GO and KEGG analyses on these candidates (P < 0.05) to explore their associated biological functions and pathways. Notably, GO analysis encompassed biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories. Protein-protein interactions among candidate genes were then analyzed via the STRING database (https://string-db.org/, score > 0.4) and visualized using Cytoscape (v 3.10.3).30 The Degree scores of these genes were determined to represent the connectivity of their corresponding nodes in the network.

Screening of Instrumental Variables (IVs)

A MR analysis was employed to assess the causal effect of candidate genes (exposure) on asthma (outcome). There were 3 major assumptions in MR analysis: (1) the association assumption: a hypothesis proposed a significant association between IVs and exposure variables; (2) the independence assumption: there was independence between IVs and confounders; (3) the exclusivity assumption: the IVs could only affect the outcome through the exposure factor. The IVs were screened using the TwoSampleMR (v 0.6.8).31 Here were the criteria for filtering IVs: (1) SNPs significantly associated with the exposure factors (P < 5×10-8) were selected as IVs; (2) Linkage disequilibrium (LD) IVs needed to be excluded using clumping = TRUE, r2 = 0.001, kb = 10; (3) IVs with F-statistics < 10 were excluded (where R2 denotes the cumulative explained variance of SNPs, N the sample size, and K the number of SNPs); (4) Furthermore, we excluded exposure factors that had fewer than 3 SNPs.

MR Analysis

In the forward MR analysis, the 150 ac4C-related candidate genes obtained from the intersection of DEGs and ac4C-related genes were defined as exposures, and adult asthma was defined as the outcome. For each candidate gene, genetic variants significantly associated with gene expression were selected as instrumental variables (IVs). The outcome data were obtained from the adult asthma GWAS dataset finn-b-J10_ASTHMA in the IEU OpenGWAS database. The harmonise_data function in the TwoSampleMR package (v 0.6.8) was used to align effect alleles and sizes for evaluating the causal effect of exposure on outcome. This process ensured that the directions of the effect alleles were consistent between exposure factor and outcome data. MR methods included MR Egger,32 Weighted median,33 Inverse variance weighted (IVW),34 Simple mode,31 and Weighted mode.35 The most important of these methods was IVW. Exposure factors with P < 0.05 in IVW were selected as candidate exposure factors of potential relevance. The MR results were quantified using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to define exposure-outcome relationships, while visualization employed scatter plots (showing individual associations), forest plots (displaying pooled effect sizes and CIs), and funnel plots (assessing potential publication bias and heterogeneity).

We conducted a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the robustness of the MR findings using the TwoSampleMR package (v 0.6.8). We assessed heterogeneity with the mr_heterogeneity function (Q.pval > 0.05), evaluated horizontal pleiotropy with the mr_pleiotropy_test function (P > 0.05), and performed a leave-one-out analysis via the mr_leaveoneout function to iteratively examine the influence of each SNP.

Reverse MR analysis was further conducted to assess potential reverse causation. In this analysis, adult asthma was defined as the exposure, whereas the ac4C-related candidate genes were defined as outcomes. A non-significant IVW result in reverse MR suggested that the observed association in the forward MR analysis was less likely to be driven by reverse causality. The IVW method was used to estimate effects, considering P > 0.05 as non-significant. Genes meeting this threshold were defined as potential targets. The study was adherence to the STROBE-MR guidelines for reporting Mendelian randomization studies clearly and transparently.

Machine Learning, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curves, and Expression Validation

To further screen out the characteristic genes that contribute the most to the differentiation between asthma and the control group, the researchers conducted XGBoost and Support Vector Machine - Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) analyses on the training set (GSE147878). The XGBoost analysis of potential target genes was carried out with the xgboost package (v 2.1.1.1) (https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/R-package/index.html), with the classification tree parameters set to max_depth = 5 and eta = 1. XGBoost genes were selected based on their importance scores and corresponding rankings. Using the e1071 software package (v1.7–16) (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/e1071/index.html), using 5-fold cross-validation, the potential target genes were analyzed by the SVM-RFE algorithm, the importance and importance ranking of each gene were obtained, and the error rate and accuracy of each iteration combination were obtained. The lowest error rate was selected as the best combination, and the optimal SVM-RFE gene was screened. Furthermore, the intersecting genes identified by both algorithms were obtained using the ggvenn package (v 0.1.10). These intersection genes were designated as feature genes. Subsequently, to assess the diagnostic power of the GSE147878 and GSE137268 datasets in differentiating AA from controls, ROC curve analysis was performed using the pROC package (v 1.18.0).36 Candidate biomarkers were identified with an AUC > 0.7 and then subjected to Wilcoxon test validation (P < 0.05) in the GSE147878 and GSE137268 datasets, yielding only those with consistent and significant differential expression as final biomarkers.

Developing and Assessing a Nomogram

Within the scope of the training set, a nomogram model was developed. This model was supported by biomarkers and made use of the rms package (v 1.1) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=rms) to estimate the probability of the occurrence of AA. The nomogram’s predictive accuracy was assessed using the rms package (v 1.1) to generate a calibration curve and perform the Hosmer-Lemeshow (HL) test, with P > 0.05 indicating good predictive fit. With the rmda package (v 1.6),37 the decision curve analysis (DCA) was carried out (net benefit above 0 indicated good model predictions). The ROC curve, generated using the pROC package (v 1.18.0), was used to determine the clinical utility of the nomogram. An AUC greater than 0.7 for the diagnostic effect was regarded as a significant reference point.

Functional Similarity, GeneMANIA Network, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

We assessed the functional similarity of the biomarkers by computing their GO semantic similarity with the GOSemSim package (v 2.33.0),38 considering genes with a similarity score greater than 0.5 as functionally analogous. In an effort to make a more in-depth prediction of the interactions between biomarkers and their involved biological functions, a gene co-expression network was constructed using the GeneMANIA website (http://www.genemania.org/). To clarify the biological functions of biomarkers during the entire progression of AA, the GSEA was conducted. The reference gene set (c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt) was sourced from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). Spearman correlations between the biomarkers and all genes in the training set were computed with the psych package (v 2.4.6.26).39 Following that, the sorted genes were then arranged in descending order according to their respective coefficients. Genes were ranked by Spearman correlation coefficients to define the gene set for subsequent analysis. GSEA was then performed using the clusterProfiler package (v 4.8.3), with significance thresholds set at |normalized enrichment score (NES)| > 1, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25, and adjusted P-value < 0.05.

Immune Infiltration Analysis and Drug Prediction

Immune cell infiltration was characterized by quantifying 10 cell types40 in the training set using the immunedeconv package (v 2.1.0)41 and the quanTIseq algorithm, followed by inter-group comparison with the Wilcoxon test (P < 0.05). Next, correlations involving the significant immune cells and biomarkers were analyzed using the psych package (v 2.4.6.26) with |r| > 0.3 and P < 0.05.

To quantify the expression profiles of inflammatory markers and matrix remodeling-related genes in asthma, accurately classify immune phenotypes, and predict the response rate to biologic therapies, an immune and matrix scoring analysis was carried out. Stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were generated in the training set with the ESTIMATE package (v 1.0.13).42 Their Spearman correlations with biomarkers were then evaluated using the psych package (v 2.4.6.26), considering |r| > 0.3 and P < 0.05 as significant.

Furthermore, to explore potential drugs for treating AA, the Drug Signatures Database (DsigDB, http://dsigdb.tanlab.org/) was used to predict drugs targeting the biomarkers. A drug-biomarker network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software (v 3.10.3).

Treatment of the scRNA-Seq Data and Selection of Key Cells

The GSE193816 dataset was quality-controlled with the Seurat package (v 5.1.0)43 based on thresholds for nFeature_RNA, mitochondrial percentage, and nCount_RNA. Subsequently, data normalization and standardization were carried out via the NormalizeData function. Finally, the top 2000 HVGs were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function, and the 10 most variable genes from this set were designated for further study. RunPCA function of the Seurat package (v 5.1.0) was accordingly employed to conduct principal component analysis (PCA) based on these 2000 genes to select principal components (PCs) which could be used for subsequent analyses (P < 0.05). Next, unsupervised clustering of all cells was performed using the FindClusters function at a resolution of 0.9. The clustering results were then visualized using the UMAP algorithm. Subsequently, the marker genes provided in the literature44 and the CellMarker database (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/) was utilized to annotate the cell types of various clusters.

To enable a more comprehensive analysis of metabolic heterogeneity in cells from disease samples, this study employed the scMetabolism package (v 0.2.1) (https://github.com/wu-yc/scMetabolism) to model metabolic pathway activity using the GSE193816 dataset. By annotating with the KEGG metabolic pathway database, the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm was employed to construct a metabolic activity matrix, revealing functional differentiation among cells in pathways such as energy metabolism and amino acid metabolism. Statistical analysis based on the bimodal likelihood ratio test (P < 0.05, min.pct = 0.1, logfc.threshold = 0.25) further identified metabolic pathways with significantly different activation levels within the disease microenvironment. To comprehensively characterize the transcription factors (TFs) in each cell type, we performed SCENIC analysis using the pySCENIC package (v 0.12.1),45 thereby investigating the enrichment of TF-bound gene sets. In the initial phase, co-expression modules linking TFs to their potential target genes were constructed with GENIE3. Next, each co-expression module was analyzed using the RcisTarget database (https://resources.aertslab.org/cistarget/) to identify potential targets with significantly enriched TF binding motifs, thereby defining direct targets of TFs in different cells.

To identify key cells, the distribution of biomarkers in each cell type was analyzed with the Seurat package (version 5.1.0) using the dataset GSE193816. This approach also visualized the expression levels of biomarkers within each cell type. Cells exhibiting high expression of more than 2 biomarkers were classified as key cells.

Cell Communication and Pseudotime Analyse

Cell-cell communication in the scRNA-seq dataset was analyzed with the CellChat package (v 1.6.1),46 thereby characterizing the interaction networks involving annotated and key cell types, with a focus on specific ligand-receptor pairs. Concurrently, the transcriptional dynamics of key cells during state transitions were investigated through pseudotime analysis using the Monocle package (v 2.28.0).47 Cells from individual clusters were projected onto a multi-branched root, and a single-cell trajectory was constructed by determining the differentiation state of each cell with the orderCells function following dimensionality reduction and clustering. The expression changes of biomarkers throughout the pseudotime progression were also analyzed.

Establishment of the Allergic Asthma Mouse Model

10 female BALB/C mice (6–8 weeks old; Bestest Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were randomized (simple randomization) into two groups: (1) Normal group; (2) Asthma group. The temperature within the animal housing room was maintained at 22± 2°C, and the humidity was kept at 50 ± 10%.

A 12- hour light/12 - hour dark cycle was sustained. Each cage accommodated 5 mice, and polypropylene cages with wood chip bedding were employed. Mice were supplied with feed and sterile drinking water. They were allowed to acclimate for one week prior to the experiment. Asthma group was sensitized by i.p. injection of 100 μg ovalbumin (OVA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 2 mg aluminum hydroxide in 200 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) on days 0, 7, and 14. From day 21 to 56, mice were challenged daily with aerosolized OVA (2% in PBS) for 30 minutes (PBS for Normal group). All mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Lung tissues were collected 24 hours post-final challenge for RT-qPCR. This animal study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

For this research, tissue samples were procured from 5 asthma mice samples and 5 control mice samples. The total RNA of lung tissues was extracted and then reversely transcribed to cDNA. Subsequently, DNA amplification was performed with the use of a fluorescent PCR instrument. The relative expression of key gens was assessed via the 2-ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v 4.3.3). Inter-group comparisons utilized the Wilcoxon test, while RT-qPCR data were analyzed with the t-test, both applying a significance threshold of P < 0.05. In all figures, significance levels are indicated as: **** P < 0.0001, *** P < 0.001, ** P < 0.01, * P < 0.05, and ns P > 0.05.

Results

The 150 Candidate Genes Were Associated with ac4C in AA

Differential expression analysis indicated that 1167 DEGs existed between the AA group and the control group. Among these DEGs, 402 genes in the AA group were recognized as upregulated genes, while 765 genes were recognized as downregulated genes (Figure 1A and B). Moreover, the intersection of DEGs and ac4CRGs yielded 150 shared genes, which were defined as candidate genes (Figure 1C). GO analysis revealed significant enrichment across 354 distinct entries (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 3). Among them, 248 BPs like DNA replication were enriched, 49 CCs like PcG protein complex were enriched, and 57 MFs like DNA-binding transcription factor binding were enriched (Figure 1D). Besides, a total of 10 KEGG pathways were significantly enriched, such as polycomb repressive complex, ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, and cell cycle (P < 0.05) (Figure 1E). Next, a PPI network was built, which included 220 interaction relationships related to 149 candidate genes (No retrieval was made for 1 gene) (Figure 1F). Among these, the 5 genes with the highest Degree scores were BRD4, CHD4, YY1, WDR5, and PARP1, which exhibited significant interactions at the protein level. A Total of 26 Potential Target Genes Were Identified as Having a Causal Relationship with Asthma

In the MR analysis treating the 150 candidate genes as exposures and asthma as the outcome, 26 genes were revealed to have significant causal associations with asthma (Table 1). Among these, 9 genes (eg, PUM1, NPEPL1) exhibited risk-enhancing effects (OR > 1 and P < 0.05). In contrast, 17 genes (eg, MCM5, TRIP6, PALM) showed protective effects (OR < 1 and P < 0.05). These associations were visually confirmed through scatter plots, where the 9 risk-associated genes displayed positive regression slopes (eg, PUM1, NPEPL1), while the 17 protective genes demonstrated negative slopes (eg, MCM5, TRIP6, PALM) (Figure 2A and Supplementary Figure 1). Forest plot analysis with the IVW method showed consistently positive effect sizes (MR effect > 0) for all 9 risk-associated genes (eg, PUM1, NPEPL1) and negative effect sizes for the 17 protective genes (MR effect sizes < 0) (eg, MCM5, TRIP6, PALM) (Figure 2B and Supplementary Figure 2). The MCM5, PUM1, TRIP6, PALM, and NPEPL1 Were Identified as Biomarkers

An evaluation of the feature importance assessment results from the XGBoost algorithm was conducted, leading to the selection of 15 XGBoost genes (Figure 3A). When applying the SVM-RFE algorithm, the model demonstrated the lowest prediction error with an optimal variable count of 20. This configuration enabled the selection of 20 SVM-RFE genes from the potential target genes (Figure 3B). Subsequently, an intersection analysis was conducted on the genes identified by the previously mentioned machine learning algorithms. This process ultimately led to the identification of MCM5, BMP2, MAP2K5, PUM1, TRIP6, MTG2, HNRNPUL1, NPEPL1, PALM, and ZNF362 as feature genes across all algorithms (Figure 3C). The ROC analysis was performed, which revealed that the AUC values for HNRNPUL1, TRIP6, PUM1, BMP2, MCM5, PALM, ZNF362, and NPEPL1 exceeded 0.7 in GSE147878 and GSE137268 datasets (Figure 3D and E). The results indicated that these genes exhibited a satisfactory ability to differentiate between AA patients and healthy people, suggesting their potential diagnostic value. Therefore, these genes were pinpointed as candidate biomarkers. However, AUC performance alone was not considered sufficient for final biomarker selection. We further performed expression validation in both datasets, and only MCM5, PUM1, TRIP6, PALM, and NPEPL1 showed consistent and significant differential expression between adult asthma and control samples (Figure 3F and G). The Nomogram Model Based on the Biomarkers Was Found to Be a More Effective Tool for Predicting AA

A nomogram incorporating the identified biomarkers was developed to predict AA risk (Figure 4A). The calibration curve demonstrated strong concordance between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (P = 0.983, Figure 4B), and DCA demonstrated high clinical utility of the model (Figure 4C). The nomogram demonstrated an AUC value of 0.974, indicating a high degree of accuracy (Figure 4D). The results demonstrated that the nomogram achieved high predictive accuracy for AA progression. The research results show that this nomogram has excellent predictive ability for the progression of AA, but it needs to be confirmed in clinical cohorts, providing preliminary clues for the future development of therapeutic strategies targeting ac4C - related genes. Before obtaining such verification, this nomogram is only used as a preliminary exploratory tool and cannot be directly applied to clinical decision-making. Exploring the Interactions and Differentiation of AECs and MNPs

A detailed analysis of cell communication revealed that, overall, the number of interactions among the 6 annotated cell types was increased, and compared with the control samples, the strength of these interactions was enhanced in the AA samples (Figure 8A and B). In both the control and AA samples, the interaction and intensity between AECs and MNPs with CD8 T cells were found to be significant. Furthermore, the HLA-C-CD8A, HLA-B-CD8A, and HLA-A-CD8A signaling axes were identified as key mediators in the interactions between AECs and CD8 T cells, as well as MNPs and CD8 T cells, both in the AA and control group (Figure 8C and D). Pseudotime analysis of AECs differentiation uncovered a progressive trajectory from an early (dark blue) to a more mature (light blue) state, and the 6 subclusters could be roughly divided into 7 differentiation states, with subclusters 2 and 3 representing the earliest stages of differentiation (Figure 8E). In AECs, as the simulated time trajectory progressed, the MCM5 gene exhibited elevated expression levels at the end of differentiation. In contrast, the TRIP6 and PALM genes increased their expression during the middle stages of differentiation, followed by a decline toward the end. The PUM1 and NPEPL1 genes exhibited high expression levels during the early stages of differentiation, which subsequently decreased over time (Figure 8G). Pseudotime analysis of MNPs differentiation uncovered a progressive trajectory from an early (dark blue) to a more mature (light blue) state, and the 8 subclusters could be roughly divided into 3 differentiation states, with subcluster 5 representing the earliest stages of differentiation (Figure 8F). During this process, PUM1, TRIP6, and PALM genes exhibited high expression levels during the early stages of differentiation, which subsequently decreased over time. In contrast, the MCM5 and NPEPL1 genes increased their expression during the middle stages of differentiation, followed by a decline toward the end (Figure 8H). The findings suggested the potential involvement of biomarkers in different phases of AECs and MNPs differentiation in AA. RT-qPCR

To experimentally investigate the biomarker expression, we performed RT-qPCR in a mouse asthma model (Figure 9). The results showed that, compared with the control group, the expression levels of MCM5, PUM1 and NPEPL1 were significantly decreased in the asthma group (P < 0.05), suggesting that the transcriptional levels of these genes were altered in the experimental asthma model, supporting their association with the asthmatic inflammatory state. However, although the expression levels of TRIP6 and PALM were lower in the asthma samples, no significant differences were observed compared with the control samples. Nevertheless, the results of RT-qPCR were inconsistent with the expression results of the bioinformatics dataset study, which may be due to species differences, disease phenotype differences between human adult asthma and the OVA-induced mouse allergic asthma model, differences in sample sources, as well as differences in cell composition between human airway samples and whole mouse lung tissues. Further research is needed. Discussion

Asthma ranks as the second most prevalent chronic respiratory disorder globally, just behind chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). It impacts individuals across all age groups.3 Considering the profound influence of refractory asthma on patients’ quality of life and the utilization of healthcare resources, the discovery of new biomarkers related to asthma is of great clinical significance. N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification is a significant RNA epigenetic modification widely present in eukaryotic RNAs, playing a crucial role in regulating immune cell activation, inflammatory responses, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), cell proliferation and apoptosis, airway remodeling, and immune homeostasis.48 Based on bioinformatics analysis, this study investigated the potential of ac4CRGs as biomarkers for adult asthma (AA) and identified five genes—MCM5, PUM1, TRIP6, PALM, and NPEPL1—as promising candidate biomarkers. Subsequently, functional enrichment analysis was performed to explore the molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic pathways associated with these biomarkers. Furthermore, single-cell sequencing data were integrated to examine the expression profiles of these biomarkers in key cell types involved in AA progression, and it was verified through animal experiments, thereby providing new perspectives for AA diagnosis and clinical management.

In present study, we elucidated the roles of five ac4CRGs in AA through a comprehensive multi-level analytical framework. Transcriptomic analysis showed significant downregulation of all five genes in asthma patients. Mendelian randomization analysis further substantiated their causal associations with asthma susceptibility: PUM1 and NPEPL1 were identified as risk factors (odds ratio [OR] > 1), whereas MCM5, TRIP6, and PALM were associated with reduced risk (OR < 1). Animal experiments confirmed significantly lower expression of MCM5, PUM1, and NPEPL1 in asthma model mice compared to controls (P < 0.05). PUM1 and NPEPL1 are identified as potential risk factors. However, their expression levels decline in animal models, which might imply their stage - and context - dependent roles in asthma. The results of functional enrichment analysis for PUM1 and NPEPL1 indicate their association with respiratory electron transport and mitochondrial function. In the context of chronic asthma inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are frequently observed. Therefore, the down - regulation of these two genes in chronic asthma tissues does not necessarily mean the absence of their genetic risk effects; instead, it may reflect secondary changes or metabolic adaptive/compensatory responses subsequent to the onset of the disease. Even though the expression of TRIP6 and PALM showed a downward tendency, the differences did not achieve statistical significance. This might be attributed to constraints in sample size, variability among species, or the spatiotemporal characteristics of gene expression. This study represented the first systematic clarification of the roles of these genes in adult asthma, providing a new perspective for future research endeavors. Based on functional similarities, the five biomarkers were classified and analyzed within this framework according to three distinct categories.

MCM5 and TRIP6 are predominantly enriched in pathways associated with ciliopathies and primary cilia development. This indicates that these genes might be linked to the structural integrity of the epithelium and the repair of the airway epithelium in adult asthma.49,50 Moreover, research has demonstrated that the destruction of ciliated cells and the impairment of mucociliary clearance, as observed in severe asthma,51 along with the findings of this study that MCM5 is significantly enriched in “ciliopathies” and “primary cilium development”, and TRIP6 is significantly enriched in “primary cilium development” and “rRNA processing”, further imply that MCM5 and TRIP6 may have potential connections with the epithelium and cilia in asthma. As a core element of the DNA replication licensing complex, MCM5 participates in the proliferation and repair of epithelial cells, which are essential for airway remodeling.52 TRIP6 is a protein with LIM domains. Previous research has shown that the TRIP6/YAP signaling axis might be a crucial factor affecting airway remodeling in childhood asthma.53 Nevertheless, in this study, TRIP6 was recognized as a protective factor through Mendelian randomization analysis. Yet, its expression was reduced in adult asthma samples and exhibited a downward tendency in an asthma mouse model. Moreover, the difference in the RT-qPCR reaction of TRIP6 did not reach statistical significance. Thus, our research findings do not directly confirm a functional mechanism but imply that TRIP6 may participate in processes related to epithelial cells in adult asthma, such as ciliary function, epithelial repair, or transcriptional remodeling. The disparity in TRIP6 expression between childhood and adult asthma might be attributed to the fact that childhood asthma is more closely associated with airway smooth muscle hyperplasia, whereas adult chronic asthma is more prominently characterized by epithelial dysfunction and ciliary abnormalities. Additionally, the Mendelian randomization analysis from a genetic viewpoint reveals that the genetically high expression of TRIP6 is linked to a decreased risk of asthma, implying that it may exert a protective effect by preserving airway epithelial integrity and regulating ciliary function, which aligns with the descriptions in the published literature.54 Regarding MCM5, its down - regulation in adult asthma appears to be a protective mechanism, potentially accomplished by regulating the epithelial repair process and maintaining ciliary function. Hence, inadequate expression of MCM5 may intensify the pathological progression of asthma.

PUM1 and NPEPL1, both of which are significantly enriched in the “respiratory electron transport chain” pathway. Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are well-established contributors to the pathogenesis of asthma.55 PUM1 is an RNA-binding protein that regulates mitochondrial function and immune cell activation by modulating the stability and translational efficiency of target mRNAs.48,53 NPEPL1, a member of the metallopeptidase family, is implicated in the regulation of cellular metabolism. Notably, other members of this family, such as Neurolysin, have been demonstrated to participate in the assembly and regulation of mitochondrial respiratory chain supercomplexes,56 suggesting that NPEPL1 may similarly influence mitochondrial energy metabolism and thereby indirectly modulate airway inflammatory responses. Importantly, both genes were identified as asthma risk factors in MR analyses, with lower expression levels associated with increased disease risk. This finding, while seemingly paradoxical, may reflect a compensatory regulatory mechanism: under chronic inflammatory conditions, downregulation of mitochondrial activity could represent a cellular adaptive response to mitigate oxidative stress.

Unlike the other four genes, PALM is independently associated with “antigen processing and cross-presentation” and “proteasome degradation” pathways, indicating distinct functional properties. As a protein involved in PALMitoylation modification, PALM plays a role in regulating membrane protein localization and signal transduction.57 The proteasome degradation system, particularly the immunoproteasome, is critically involved in antigen processing and presentation. Evidence indicates that mice deficient in immunoproteasome components exhibit markedly increased eosinophilic inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness after being exposed to house dust mite allergens and rhinovirus.58 When exogenous allergens enter the body, antigen-presenting cells, which include dendritic cells and macrophages, capture and process antigens through mechanisms like endocytosis and phagocytosis. After that, they present antigenic peptides to T lymphocytes.59,60 In the context of asthma, this process facilitates the activation of Th2 cells, which secrete cytokines including IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, thereby promoting IgE production and allergic airway inflammation.61,62 Notably, MR analysis identified PALM as a protective factor, suggesting its potential to modulate the magnitude of Th2-type immune responses through regulation of antigen presentation efficiency. In summary, this research combined multi-level evidence to clarify the intricate functions of five ac4C-related genes in adult asthma. These genes were consistently downregulated at the transcriptomic level, yet exhibit divergent causal effects in Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis—two acting as risk factors and three as protective factors. This discrepancy suggested that altered gene expression may be a consequence rather than a primary driver of disease pathogenesis, highlighting the intricate regulatory mechanisms governing these genes across various cell types, disease stages, and physiological contexts. Analysis of immune cell infiltration revealed significant differences in five immune cell types between the AA and control groups, specifically B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, dendritic cells, and monocytes. Among these, the most significant positive correlation with B cells was demonstrated by monocytes. During antigen presentation to T cells, B cells receive co-stimulatory signals from T cells, which facilitate their differentiation and antibody production. Enhancing the antigen-presenting capacity of B cells may augment the receipt of such co-stimulatory signals, thereby promoting their differentiation into plasma cells and increasing antibody secretion.63 In asthma, IgE produced by B cells represents one of the key mediators initiating allergic reactions. Enhanced antigen-presenting capacity of B cells may lead to increased IgE secretion, thereby potentially exacerbating allergic symptoms.64–66 Notably, a distinct subset of B cells—IL-10-producing B cells—has been shown to suppress established airway allergic inflammation through the production of IL-10.67 However, IL-10 may also contribute to Th2-mediated immune responses and eosinophilic infiltration in allergic dermatitis, and has been associated with aggravated allergen-induced airway hyperresponsiveness.68 This functional duality indicated that therapeutic strategies targeting B cells must carefully modulate the balance among their subsets, rather than indiscriminately suppressing B cell activity. To gain a deeper understanding of the crucial cell types implicated in asthma, we carried out single-cell RNA sequencing analysis. The results revealed that airway epithelial cells (AECs) and mononuclear phagocytes (MNPs) constitute the central cellular components in adult asthma, consistent with the significant enrichment of monocytes observed in the immune infiltration analysis. In patients with asthma, AECs serve as a major origin of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, contributing to airway remodeling and excessive mucus production.69 Upon exposure to inhaled particulate matter, AECs release alarmins—such as TSLP, IL-33, and IL-25—which initiate allergic inflammatory responses characterized by eosinophilic infiltration.70,71 MNPs may contribute to antigen presentation and amplification of inflammation through interactions with B cells, as suggested by the correlation analysis described above.

Meanwhile, we predicted potential drugs targeting the identified biomarkers. The research findings indicated that valproic acid targets PUM1, TRIP6, and NPEPL1, whereas trichostatin A (TSA) and scriptaid target TRIP6, MCM5, and PUM1. Valproic acid is among the most effective and widely prescribed antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) globally.72 Previous studies have demonstrated that valproic acid (VPA) modulates immune responses by disrupting the Th1/Th2 balance and promoting the differentiation of regulatory T cells, and can also alleviate chronic allergic airway pathologies through mechanisms such as reducing acetylcholine-induced airway constriction, lowering innate inflammatory markers, and suppressing Th2 responses.73,74 Airway remodeling and airway hyperresponsiveness are key features of asthma pathology that are not fully addressed by current anti-inflammatory therapies. As a histone deacetylase inhibitor selective for class II enzymes, valproic acid (VPA) has been shown to attenuate structural airway remodeling and hyperresponsiveness, thereby supporting the potential therapeutic application of histone deacetylase inhibitors in asthma management.75 More strikingly, trichostatin A (TSA), another histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, modulated three biomarkers: TRIP6, MCM5, and PUM1. Notably, the association of MCM5 with immune cell infiltration revealed a potential cellular mechanism which may underlie the therapeutic efficacy of TSA. TSA acts by suppressing the expression of Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-5) and IgE, along with reducing T cell infiltration, to attenuate allergic airway inflammation. Immune infiltration analyses indicated that MCM5 exhibits a strong positive correlation with B cells and CD8+ T cells, findings that have been validated in a murine model of chronic allergic airway disease.76 TSA treatment markedly reduced total inflammatory cell and eosinophil counts in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, suppresses goblet cell hyperplasia and subepithelial collagen deposition, thereby attenuating airway hyperresponsiveness.77 Notably, TSA inhibits airway contraction by blocking intracellular calcium ion release in airway smooth muscle cells—a mechanism that is distinct from anti-inflammatory agents such as glucocorticoids78—thereby offering a complementary therapeutic approach for asthma management. Given its multifaceted mechanisms of action mediated through epigenetic regulation and its unique bronchodilatory effect, TSA represents great potential as a viable approach for the advancement of targeted therapies in the precise treatment of asthma.

Finally, an asthma mouse model was established. RT-qPCR showed that the expression of MCM5, PUM1, TRIP6, PALM, and NPEPL1 was significantly downregulated in the lung tissues of asthmatic mice versus controls. Notably, the downregulation of MCM5, PUM1, and NPEPL1 exhibited statistically significant differences. Although the decreased expressions of TRIP6 and PALM did not reach the level of statistical significance, a continuous downward trend was still observed in the asthma group. This suggested that these genes undergo transcriptional level changes in the experimental asthma model, supporting their association with the inflammatory state of asthma. These results further supported the involvement of ac4C RNA modification in the pathogenesis of asthma and provided novel molecular evidence for potential therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This study identified MCM5, PUM1, TRIP6, PALM, and NPEPL1 as potential ac4C-related biomarkers for AA through the integration of transcriptomics, Mendelian randomization, machine learning, immune infiltration, single-cell sequencing, and RT-qPCR analysis. It was found that these biomarkers may be closely associated with alterations in immune cell composition, epithelial cell dysfunction, and AA-related inflammatory processes. Consequently, they offered new reference bases for comprehending the pathogenesis of AA, enhancing clinical diagnostic methods, and optimizing treatment strategies. Nevertheless, this study still has the following limitations: Firstly, the conclusions of this study are primarily based on bioinformatics analysis and preliminary validation in mouse models. The specific molecular mechanisms through which the selected potential biomarkers exert their functions in AA have not been fully elucidated, and the RT-qPCR results are inconsistent with the expression in the dataset. Further clinical cohort studies are required to verify their functions and clinical relevance. Secondly, although potential drugs targeting these biomarkers have been predicted via bioinformatics methods, the actual efficacy of these drugs in clinical practice, as well as the safety and effectiveness of targeted therapeutic drugs, still require further exploration. Moreover, this study integrated multiple GEO datasets with different tissue sources and cell populations. The mouse OVA - induced model is not entirely consistent with human disease conditions. There may be batch effects and heterogeneity among these data, which can impact the universality of the results. Therefore, future studies should increase the sample size, offer more adequate data support, and concentrate on exploring the biological functions of potential biomarkers and their potential application value in AA treatment. Meanwhile, in vitro or in vivo functional experiments such as gene knockout/overexpression, signal pathway intervention, and NAT10 - specific inhibitor treatment can be tried to test the NAT10 - ac4C - MCM5 regulatory axis in human air - liquid interface culture systems, and further validate its clinical translational significance in human airway epithelial cells, induced sputum cells, or bronchial biopsy samples.
