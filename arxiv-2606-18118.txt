Programming with Chebfun. Case study: Richards equation Journal: Advances in Water Resources
Nicolae Suciu

Abstract
The Chebfun software system is a Matlab extension essentially based on representations of (piece-wise) smooth one-variable functions by expansions in Chebyshev polynomials. One of Chebfun’s attractive features is the ability to provide solutions to nonlinear boundary value problems (BVP) with accuracy close to the machine precision. This is done by the chebop class which provides automatic solutions by performing linearizations with a Newton method in function spaces of the nonlinear BVP, automatic differentiation, and using Fast Fourier Transform computations for the coefficients of the Chebyshev polynomials. A drawback of chebop automatic approach is the possible lack of convergence of the Newton method if the initial guess is not close enough to the exact solution. An explicit functional linearization done for each particular shape of the differential operator (i.e. without automatic differentiation) proves to be more robust than the chebop class and allows an enlargement of the range of convergence. Another alternative is the implicit L-scheme (quasi-Newton approach with derivatives replaced by suitable positive constants L), with a much simpler implementation and globally convergent. While chebop is the easiest way to solve the BVP, provided that it converges, the last two approaches largely overcome the convergence issues, yielding accurate solutions to a wide class of steady-state one-dimensional problems governed by Richards’ equation. Chebfun2 and Chebfun3, which at the current stage cannot solve BVPs, provide efficient tools for accuracy and convergence assessments of the non-steady solutions in one or two spatial dimensions obtained by classical discretization schemes.



1
Introduction

The Chebfun system is a free downloadable Matlab package enabling computing with functions similar to computing
with vectors aiming to “feel symbolic but run at the speed of numerics”. While “Chebfun” with capital C denotes the software, “chebfun” is a function of one veriable defined on an interval of the real axis. The operations with chebfun follow (almost) the same syntax used in operations with vectors in MATLAB, with commands for vectors overloaded by those acting on functions. The implementation of Chebfun is based on polynomial interpolation of smooth functions in Chebyshev points which, thanks to Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), finally results in expansions in Chebyshev polynomials
[
24
]
.

The basic theorem on Chebyshev series says that Lipschitz continuous functions have unique representations as Chebyshev series. That is, the series

f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}T_{k}(x),

where
T_{k}
is the
k
-th order Chebyshev polynomial, is absolutely and uniformly convergent and the coefficients
a_{k}
have a closed form in terms of
T_{k}

[
24
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Chebfun constructs approximations of a smooth function
f
as follows. First, it calculates the polynomial interpolant

f_{n}(x)\approx\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}T_{i}(x),

at
n=9
Chebyshev points
x_{i}=\cos(i\pi/n)
,
0\leq i\leq n
, and checks whether the Chebyshev coefficients are small enough. If this is not the case, the number of points is progressively increased until the norm
\|f-f_{n}\|
falls to the level of rounding error. Further, the length of the series is reduced by neglecting the terms deemed to be negligible. Finally, the resulting series is evaluated at
n=
length(f)
points via FFT to compute the Chebyshev coefficients defining the approximating chebfun
[
24
, Chap. 3 and 4]
.

The accuracy of the approximation depends on the degree of smoothness of the function
f
. For instance, it
f
is
\nu
-times differentiable, the approximation can achieve at most the algebraic accuracy
\|f-f_{n}\|=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\nu})

[
24
, Theorem 7.2]
. Instead, if (an only if)
f
is (piece-wise) analytic one achieves the geometric accuracy

\mathcal{O}(C^{-n}),\;\;C>1

[
24
, Chap. 8]
. In the cited reference, this is illustrated by the chebfun approximation of the Runge function
f(x)=(1+25x^{2})^{-1}
by a Chebyshev polynomial of degree
n
with the command
fn = chebfun(f,n+1)
. Computing the approximation for
n=200
shown in Fig.
2
takes about 0.5 seconds. The straight-line semi-log plot of the error shown Fig.
2
demonstrates the geometric accuracy of the chebfun approximation.

The geometric accuracy is not a special property of chebfun approximation, but also characterizes classical discretization methods. Finite element/volume/difference methods are usually characterized by algebraic error estimates,
\mathcal{O}\left(h^{\alpha\;}\right),\;h<1,\;\alpha>0
, with respect to the mesh size
h
. However, this is not similar to the algebraic accuracy with respect to
n
of chebfun approximations for differentiable functions described above. For every mesh refinement
\{h_{n}=h_{0}r^{-n}\;|\;r>1\}
the errors of the discretization methods can be recast as
\mathcal{O}(C^{-n}),\;C=r^{\alpha}>1
, which corresponds to the geometric accuracy.

The accuracy
\mathcal{O}(C^{-n})
can be thought of as a functional dependence of the error
e_{n}=\|f-f_{n}\|
. It enables the assessment of the convergence order of the sequence
\{e_{n}\}
according to the definition of the classical
C
-order
p

[
7
]
by verifying the existence of the finite limit

Q_{p}=\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e_{s+1}}{(e_{s})^{p}}=\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C^{-(n+1)}}{(C^{-n})^{p}}=\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow\infty}C^{n(p-1)-1}.

(1)

We see that a finite limit
Q_{p}\in(0,\infty)
can only be obtained if
p=1
. Since
C>0
,
Q_{p}=C^{-1}<1
, which corresponds to the linear convergence (as already indicated by the semi-log plot in Fig.
2
). We have thus come to the somewhat surprising conclusion that both Chebfun and discretization approaches exhibit geometric accuracy and linear C-order of convergence. This could be misleading, because the mesh refinement becomes prohibitive from computational point of view after a few steps
n
. Instead, Chebfun effectively achieves the machine precision in remarkably short computing times.

An attractive feature of Chebfun is its ability to solve one-dimensional nonlinear boundary value problems (BVP). This is achieved with the object class
chebop
introduced since Chebfun version 4, which encompasses the class of linear operators as a particular case. Chebop uses forward automatic differentiation to compute Frechét derivatives, constructs linearized BVPs, and provides fully automatic solutions for a wide range of nonlinear BVPs
[
4
]
. More specifically,
chebop
uses a variant of Newton’s method implemented for continuous functions, rather than vectors, where the Jacobian matrices are replaced by Frechét derivative operators. Following
[
4
]
, the procedure can be described as follows. Let’s start with the the nonlinear BVP

\displaystyle\Phi(u)=0

(2)

\displaystyle\alpha(u)|_{x=a}=0,\;\;\beta(u)|_{x=b}=0

(3)

associated to the nonlinear differential operator
\Phi

in the interval

under the boundary conditions formulated with the aid of the (possibly nonlinear) differential operators
\alpha
and
\beta
.
Generalizing the Taylor expansion used to derive the classical Newton method in finite dimensional spaces we start with the current iterate
u_{k}
and evaluate
\Phi(u_{k}+v)
, for a given perturbation

by the linearization
\Phi(u_{k})+\Phi^{\prime}(u_{k})v
, where the prime denotes the Frechét derivative. The Frechét differential
\Phi^{\prime}(u_{k})v=\Phi(u_{k}+v)-\Phi(u_{k})
is computed with a standard perturbation approach by retaining linear terms in

and dropping higher order terms. After replacing
\Phi(u)
by
\Phi(u_{k}+v)
in the differential equation (
2
) and proceeding in the same way with the boundary conditions (
3
) one obtains the update (or correction)

as the solution of the linearized problem

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(u_{k})v+\Phi(u_{k})=0

(4)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(u_{k})v|_{x=a}+\alpha(u_{k})|_{x=a}=0,\;\;\beta^{\prime}(u_{k})v|_{x=b}+\beta(u_{k})|_{x=b}=0.

(5)

By solving (
4
-
5

-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|u_{k}\|}\sqrt{\|\Phi(u_{k})\|^{2}+\|\alpha(u_{k})|_{x=a}\|^{2}+\|\beta(u_{k})|_{x=a}\|^{2}}/<\varepsilon

(7)

\displaystyle\mbox{{iter} > {maxIter}},

where

Comparing the two solution approaches, we can see that the explicit linearization is more robust than the automated
chebop
(actually, we found that it works for all the BVP problems considered in this study). Moreover, the explicit approach is more flexible. For instance, we can choose the stopping criterion, e.g. in terms of residuals of the differential operator. We also can easily specify the output: residuals of the solution
u
and of the update

leads to the failure of the convergence.

To cope with the convergence issues, we propose a new linearization approach called
L
-scheme. This consists of a formulation for continuous functions of the the implicit

-scheme used in classical discretization approaches. The latter is a stabilized Picard iteration
[
8
]
where the Jacobian is replaced by a suitably chosen positive parameter

[

]

4
-
5

and to perform an explicit linearization by expressing some nonlinear terms with the solution of the previous iteration. The second feature is the global convergence of the scheme, as it only depends on the choice of the stabilization parameter
L

In this paper, we illustrate the use of the automated

The last application of programming with Chebfun presented in this paper uses a similar coupling to evaluate orders of accuracy of the FD

Linearization approaches

Newton’s method is a prototypical linearization approach for nonlinear problems. Since the
L
-schemes can be thought of as quasi-Newton methods, we shall introduce them in the following by simplifying the Newton method while using its fixed-point structure.

Let us consider the problem of finding the zeros of the one variable function
f(x)
. Looking for an iterative method to solve
f(x)=0
, we denote by
x_{k}
the current approximation and solve the linearization obtained by the first-order Taylor expansion
f(x_{k+1})=f(x_{k})+f^{\prime}(x_{k})(x_{k+1}-x_{k}))=0
to obtain a new approximate solution,

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\frac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})}=F(x_{k}).

(9)

Hence, the scheme is equivalent to the fixed point iteration
x_{k+1}=F(x_{k})
and the solution
x^{*}
is a fixed-point of
F
,
x^{*}=f(x^{*})
. If
F
is
p
-times differentiable the convergence of the fixed-point iterations is ensured under the following conditions
[
13
, Corollary 8.8]
: the iteration
x_{k+1}=F(x_{k})
is locally convergent in a neighborhood
U(x^{*})
of the fixed point

\bullet

if\;\;p=1,\;\;F^{\prime}(x^{*})\neq 0,\;\;and\;\;|F^{\prime}(x^{*})|<1\;
, which implies

\displaystyle|x_{k+1}-x^{*}|\leq C|x_{k}-x^{*}|,\;\;C<1,

(10)

\bullet

if\;\;p>1,\;\;F^{\prime}(x^{*})=F^{\prime\prime}(x^{*})\ldots F^{(p-1)(x^{*})}=0,\;\;and\;\;F^{(p)}\neq 0,\;
which implies

\displaystyle|x_{k+1}-x^{*}|\leq C|x_{k}-x^{*}|^{p},\;\;C>0.

(11)

An explicit
L
-scheme may be constructed as a relaxation of the Newton scheme, with
f^{\prime}(x_{k})
in (
9
) replaced by a positive constant
L
. Let
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with a unique root
x^{*}
. Choosing
L>\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f^{\prime}(x)
, we define the explicit
L
-scheme by

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\sign(f^{\prime}(x_{k}))\frac{f(x_{k})}{L}=F_{L}(x_{k}).

(12)

The sign function in (
12
) ensures the condition

|F^{\prime}(x^{*})|=\left|1-\sign(f^{\prime}(x_{k}))\frac{f^{\prime}(x_{k})}{L}\right|<1

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
regardless of whether the derivative
f^{\prime}(x_{k})
is positive or negative. Thus, according to (
10
) the
L
-scheme converges linearly to
x^{*}
. Since the condition
|F^{\prime}(x^{*})|<1
is not restricted to a neighborhood of
x^{*}
, like for (
10
), the
L
-scheme is globally convergent.

As an illustration let us consider the equation
[
7
, Example 4.6]

\displaystyle f(x)=x+x^{2}+10x^{3}=0,\;\;with\;\;x^{*}=0

(13)

and the corresponding fixed-point function
F(x)=x-f(x)/f^{\prime}(x)
. Since
F^{\prime}(x^{*})=0
and
F^{\prime\prime}(x^{*})=2
the fixed point iteration converges and behaves as (
11
) with
p=2
. Moreover, it is shown
[

, Theorem 4.2]
that the sequence of errors converges with the classical C-order
p=2
, according to (
1

Application to steady-state infiltration in soils

Steady-state infiltration problems modeled by Richards equation provide a methodological framework for the determination of soil hydraulic properties and in advancing the knowledge of the soil physics
[
15
,
17
,
3
]
. Such investigations use numerical
[
18
,
25
,
1
]
as well as approximate analytical solutions to solve the nonlinear Richards equation
[
2
,
5
,
6
,
19
]
. Since Chebfun system provides solutions to BVP problems with practically the machine precision, it could be a valuable alternative approach in solving steady-state infiltration problems.

3.1
Problem formulation

Infiltration in soils is often modeled by the one-dimensional Richards’ equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\theta(\psi)-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[K(\psi)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\psi-z)\right]=f,

(15)

where
\psi(z,t)
is the pressure head expressed in length units,
\theta
is the volumetric water content,
K
stands for the hydraulic conductivity of the medium,
z
is the height oriented positively downward, and
f
is sink/source term. The steady-state unsaturated flow occurs when the water content
\theta
is still smaller than the porosity, possibly spatially variable, but time independent,
\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\theta(\psi)=0
. In steady-state case of one-dimensional unsaturated flows it is convenient to work with the pressure potential defined by the pressure head with opposite sign
[
11
]
. With these, the steady-state Richards equation (
15
) becomes

\frac{d}{dz}\left[K(\psi)\left(\frac{d\psi}{dz}+1\right)\right]=-f,

(16)

where
f
is a positive function modelling the root water uptake by plants. Note that when working with the pressure potential
-\psi
the water flux
q=-K(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}-1)
, expressed in terms of pressure head
\psi
, becomes
q=K(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}+1)
.

To close equation (
16
) we consider two model relationships between hydraulic conductivity and potential proposed by the Gardner
[
11
]
,

\displaystyle K(\psi)=a\exp(-c\psi),

(17)

\displaystyle K(\psi)=1/(1+\psi^{n}).

(18)

where
a,\;c,\;\mbox{and}\;n
are model constants. The closure (
17
) is the classical exponential Gardner’s closure further referred to as Gardner model. The second closure, (
18
), has been used in a couple of papers by Basha, Braddock, and Parlange
[
2
,
5
,
6
]
to derive exact solutions of the equation (
16
) and will be further referred to as Basha model.

3.2
Implicit
L
-scheme for Richards Equation

The
L
-scheme for Richards equation can be obtained as a relaxation of the modified Picard method proposed by
[
8
]
. The latter is derived from the backward Euler time discretization of (
15
) and a simple Picard iteration scheme, with iteration count denoted by
k
,

\frac{\theta^{j,k+1}-\theta^{j-1}}{\tau}-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[K^{j,k}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\psi^{j,k+1}-z)\right]=0,

followed by truncated Taylor expansion of
\theta^{j,k+1}
with respect to
\psi
about
\psi^{j,k}
,

\theta^{j,k+1}=\theta^{j,k}+\left.\frac{d\theta}{d\psi}\right|^{j-1,k}(\psi^{j,k+1}-\psi^{j,k}),

and ends up to a modified Picard scheme

\left.\frac{d\theta}{d\psi}\right|^{j-1,k}(\psi^{j,k+1}-\psi^{j,k})+\frac{\theta^{j,k}-\theta^{j-1}}{\tau}-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[K^{j,k}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\psi^{j,k+1}-z)\right]=0.

(19)

The relaxation consists of replacing the first term in (
19
) by the stabilization term

L(\psi^{j,k+1}-\psi^{j,k}),\;L>0
. In the steady-state case, the implicit
L
-scheme iteration reads

L(\psi^{j,k+1}-\psi^{j,k})-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[K^{j,k}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\psi^{j,k+1}-z)\right]=0.

(20)

The convergence of the
L
-scheme (
20
) has been proven in a variational formulation by exploiting the monotonicity of
\theta
and assuming common regularity conditions on
K
and the gradient of the solution
[
14
]
.

In the following, we construct the explicit Newton-Fréchet linearization (

) of the problem (

-

\displaystyle d\Phi

\displaystyle=(u+v)^{\prime\prime}-c(u+v)^{\prime}[(u+v)^{\prime}+1]-u^{\prime\prime}+cu^{\prime}(u^{\prime}+1)

\displaystyle=v^{\prime\prime}-c(2u^{\prime}+1)v^{\prime}.

In the same way, we compute the Fréchet differential of (
22
),

\displaystyle d\alpha

\displaystyle=a(u^{\prime}+v^{\prime}+1)-q\exp[c(u+v)]-a(u^{\prime}+1)+q\exp(cu)

\displaystyle=av^{\prime}-q\exp(cu)[\exp(cv)-1]

\displaystyle=av^{\prime}-q\exp(cu)cv,

where we use the linearization
\exp(cv)=1+cv
.
Finally, for the right boundary condition we have

d\beta=u+v-1-u+1=v.

According to (
4
-
5

is obtained by solving the linearization problem

\displaystyle v^{\prime\prime}_{k}-c(2u^{\prime}+1)v^{\prime}_{k}+u^{\prime\prime}-cu^{\prime}(u^{\prime}+1)=0,

\displaystyle av^{\prime}_{k}(0)-q\exp(cu(0))cv_{k}(0)+a[u^{\prime}(0)+1]-q\exp[cu(0)]=0,

\displaystyle v_{k}+u(2)-1=0.

u = u+v;

4
Exploring higher dimensions with Chebfun2 and Chebfun3

With the Cheffun2
[
20
]
and Cheffun3
[
12
]
extensions, currently in the implementation stage, we may try to look for possible applications to infiltration problems in higher dimensions.

Chebfub2 can solve linear partial differential equations defined on rectangular domains with the class
chebop2

[
21
]
. However, a Newton-Fréchet method for non-steady infiltration in one spatial dimension cannot be implemented because only the determinant of the Jacobian but not the matrix itself can be constructed in Chebfun2 (see ’help jacobian’ in Matlab command line). Trying to solve the problem with the implicit
L
-scheme by using
chebop2
, the code breaks down with the warning “Warning: Matrix is close to singular or badly scaled” and the error “Solution was unresolved on a 65 by 65 grid”. The issue is not unexpected because typing “help chebop2” in the command line we get the information “Warning: This PDE solver is an experimental new feature. It has not been publicly advertised”. As for Chebfun3, there are no solvers for linear problems.

The approach proposed in
[
9
]
uses Chebfun to solve steady-state problems in one spatial dimension and a FD scheme to perform the tine-stepping. Here, we propose a Chebfun-FD coupling to solve non-steady problems in both one and two spatial dimensions. A version of this coupling will be further used to evaluate orders of accuracy of the FD schemes.

4.1
Chebfun-FD coupling

The coupling between Chebfun2 and Chebfun3 with one- and two-dimensional FD schemes is constructed as follows. At fixed time level, the infiltration is solved by an explicit FD
L
-scheme. For the one-dimensional Richards equation (
15
) the explicit
L
-scheme is obtained by evaluating the second order operator in (
19
) from the last iterate
\psi^{j,k}
,

L(\psi^{j,k+1}-\psi^{j,k})-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[K^{j,k}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\psi^{j,k}-z)\right]+\frac{\theta^{j,k}-\theta^{j-1}}{\tau}=0.

(28)

The generalization of the scheme (
28
) to higher dimensions is straightforward. (Since the explicit
L
-schemes do not solve systems of algebraic equations, they can be up to ten times faster in solving non-steady problems (see
[
18
]
for implementation details and comparisons with an implicit scheme). In the following, the vector representing the FD solution is imported into Chebfun2 (or Chebfun3) by interpolation procedures
[
22
,
23
]
. The Chebfun code performs time and iteration steps, evaluates corrections, errors, and residuals and provide a new initial guess for the FD scheme. The coupling approach is summarized in Algorithm

.

The accuracy of the classical discretization schemes is characteized by the so called observed order of accuracy
[

\alpha=\ln\left(\frac{e_{k}}{e_{k+1}}\right)\;/\;\ln(2),\;\;k=1,2,\cdots,

(31)

where

L^{2}
norm
\|\cdot\|:=\sqrt{h}\|\cdot\|_{\ell^{2}}

4.3
Failure of modeling saturated-unsaturated transition in Chebfun

The Chebfun-FD coupling is illustrated in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
for unsaturated flow regime. The natural question is, does the coupling also work for saturated-unsaturated transitions? Since in saturated regime the conductivity
K
is constant and Richards equation degenerates into an elliptic equation, we can try the splitting

\Phi=\Phi^{-}+\Phi^{+}
, where
\Phi^{-}
and
\Phi^{+}
are differential operators corresponding to the parabolic Richards equation, operating in the unsaturated regime, and to the elliptic equation in saturated regime. Chebfun operators operate on the whole function
u
, unlike the finite difference operators which automatically change the form for

\psi\geq 0
and
\psi<0
. Therefore, we have to split
\psi
into its negative and positive part,
\psi^{-}=\max(0,-\psi)
,
\psi^{+}=\max(0,\psi)
, and export them as
chebfun
objects,
u^{-}
and
u^{+}
, as shown in Fig.
18
. With these, the splitting operator becomes

\Phi(u,u^{+},u^{-})=\frac{1-\sign(u)}{2}\Phi^{-}(u^{-})+\frac{1+\sign(u)}{2}\Phi^{+}(u),

(32)

where the
\sign
function prevents spurious oscillations that may appear when
\Phi^{-}
operates for
u\geq 0
and
\Phi^{+}
operates for
u<0
(see Fig.
18
).

The problem for the one-dimensional non-steady Richards equation is implemented in [Accuracy/Failure.m] by the following lines:

[p] = FDL_scheme(E,I); % FD solution for exact solution E and grid dimension I.
u=chebfun(p’,D,’equi’);
pm=max(0,-p); % negative part of p (unsaturated)
pp=max(0,p); % positive part of p (saturated)
up=chebfun(pp’,D,’equi’);
um=chebfun(pm’,D,’equi’);
phi_m = @(u) diff(u,2)+c*diff(u).*(diff(u)+1); % operator for unsaturated regime
phi_p = @(u) diff(u,2); % operator for saturated regime
phi = @(u,up,um) (1-sign(u))/2.*phi_m(-um)+(1+sign(u))/2.*phi_p(up);
residual=norm(phi(u,up,um));

Summarizing this section, we have seen that the Chebfun-FD coupling is a valuable tool for accuracy assessments of the classical discretization schemes for Richards equation, but it only works for the unsaturated flow regime.

5
conclusions

The main results of this study are summarized as follows.

\bullet
The automated
chbeop
class of Chebfun solves nonlinear steady-state BVP for Richards equation in one spatial dimension, to roughly machine precision, provided that a suitable initial guess which ensures the convergence of the Newton iterations is available.

\bullet
If
chbeop
fails, a well posed BVP can always be solved, within a wider range of initial iterates, by an explicit linearization of the nonlinear operator in function space based on Fréchet differential and the linear Chebfun solver, as well as by globally convergent
L
-schemes, quasi-Newton methods where the derivatives are replaced by suitably chosen positive numbers.

\bullet
The coupling of Chebfun2 and Chebfun3 with explicit
L
-schemes can be used to solve non-steady infiltration problems in one and two spatial dimensions and is mainly useful in assessing the accuracy of the classical discretization schemes.

\bullet
Because Chebfun fails to resolve the derivatives of the solution close to the inflection point, where the pressure head changes the sign, Chebfun-based applications are only possible for the unsaturated flow regime.
