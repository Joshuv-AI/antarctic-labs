Almost Regular Coverings of the Sphere: Realizability. I. Tetrahedral Case
Nikolai M. Adrianov, Elena M. Kreines

Abstract
We prove the realizability of genus- 0 0 branch data of the form ( 2 r , a ​ | 3 s , b ​ | 3 t , c ) (2^{r},a\mathop{|}3^{s},b\mathop{|}3^{t},c) and ( 2 r , a ​ | 3 s ​ | 3 t , b , c ) (2^{r},a\mathop{|}3^{s}\mathop{|}3^{t},b,c) , where a a , b b , c c are not divisible by 2 2 , 3 3 , 3 3 respectively. The proof uses an explicit combinatorial description of coverings of the sphere branched over 3 3 points via dessins d’enfants. As a corollary, we establish realizability for a broader class of branch data with more critical values.



1
Introduction

1.1
Partitions

For a positive integer
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a tuple

\lambda=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}),\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}=n,\quad a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}

is called a
partition
of
n
, written
\lambda\vdash n
. The number of parts
m
is the
length
of the partition, denoted
\ell(\lambda)
.

Parts are often assumed to be arranged in non-increasing order
a_{i}\geqslant a_{i+1}
, but we do not impose this convention here. When many parts are equal, we use exponential notation; for example,

(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{s},5,2,2)=(3^{s},5,2^{2}).

1.2
The Hurwitz Realizability Problem

Let
f:\,X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{C}})
be a branched covering of the Riemann sphere of degree
n
, branched over
q
points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{C}})
. The ramification over each point
x_{i}
defines a partition
\lambda_{i}\vdash n
, so we associate to
f
its branch datum

\pi(f)=(\lambda_{1}\mathop{|}\lambda_{2}\mathop{|}\ldots\mathop{|}\lambda_{q}),\qquad\lambda_{i}\vdash n,\quad\lambda_{i}\neq(1^{n}).

(1)

The genus
g=g(X)
of the covering is determined by the Riemann–Hurwitz formula:

\sum_{i=1}^{q}\ell(\lambda_{i})-(q-2)n=2-2g.

(2)

A collection of partitions
\pi
satisfying (
2
) for some integer
g\geqslant 0
is called a
branch datum of genus
g
.
A covering
f:\,X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{C}})
of genus
g
whose branch datum equals
\pi
is called a
realization
of
\pi
.
The Riemann–Hurwitz formula is a necessary but not sufficient condition for realizability. The realizability problem has a rich literature; we highlight the key paper of Edmonds, Kulkarni, and Stong
[
9
]
and the computational results of Zheng
[
26
]
. For a detailed survey of the Hurwitz problem, see
[
19
]
and the introduction to
[
14
]
. Necessary conditions for realizability of various special families of branch data have been found, and in some cases their sufficiency has been proved; see
[
3
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
23
,
25
]
.

In the present paper we prove the realizability of an extremal family of genus-
0
branch data, providing a general scheme for a combinatorial construction via dessins d’enfants that can be applied to prove realizability of other related families as well.

The main result of this paper is the following theorem. Since almost all ramification indices in the first three components of the branch data equal
2
,
3
, and
3
, we call such branch data
almost regular of
(2,3,3)
type
(the tetrahedral case). In subsequent papers we will prove analogous results for the octahedral
(2,3,4)
and icosahedral
(2,3,5)
types, and describe some new non-realizable families of genus-
0
branch data.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let a genus-
0
branch datum have one of the following forms:

\pi=(2^{r},a\mathop{|}3^{s},b\mathop{|}3^{t},c\mathop{|}\lambda_{4}\mathop{|}\ldots\mathop{|}\lambda_{q}),

(3a)

or

\pi=(2^{r},a\mathop{|}3^{s}\mathop{|}3^{t},b,c\mathop{|}\lambda_{4}\mathop{|}\ldots\mathop{|}\lambda_{q}),

(3b)

where
2\nmid a
,
3\nmid b
,
3\nmid c
, and
\lambda_{i}
are arbitrary partitions.
Then the branch datum
\pi
is realizable.

1.3
Dessins d’Enfants

A covering
\beta:\,X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{C}})
branched over exactly
q=3
points is called a
Belyi cover
(or
Belyi function
).

Choose any two of the three critical values, say
c_{0},c_{1}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{C}})
. The preimage of the arc connecting them yields a connected bicolored graph
\Gamma=\beta^{-1}[c_{0},c_{1}]
on the surface
X
such that the complement
X\setminus\Gamma
is a disjoint union of discs (faces). In other words, the pair
(X,\Gamma)
is a bicolored map on a compact oriented surface. The preimages of
c_{0}
are colored white, and those of
c_{1}
are colored black.

We call the bicolored map
(X,\Gamma)
a
dessin d’enfant
, a term coined by Grothendieck
[
8
]
.
The use of this term, rather than the more prosaic “map”, is motivated by a theorem of Belyi
[
4
]
: the surface
X
can be interpreted as a nonsingular complex algebraic curve defined over a number field (a finite extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
). Conversely, every such curve admits a Belyi function, so dessins d’enfants provide a complete combinatorial encoding of curves over number fields. Furthermore, there is a faithful action of the absolute Galois group
\mathrm{Gal}({\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}/{\mathbb{Q}})
on dessins d’enfants. In the present paper, however, we restrict ourselves to the combinatorial aspects and do not pursue arithmetic questions.
The theory of dessins d’enfants is a well-developed subject; for a detailed and accessible introduction we recommend the monograph
[
11
]
.

The degree of a vertex or face equals the ramification index of the covering at that point. The
degree of a vertex
is the number of edges incident to it. The definition of face degree requires some care. Each face of a dessin can be represented as a polygon with an even number of sides whose vertices alternate in color, so the
degree of a face
is defined as
half
the number of edges incident to it.

In the theory of dessins d’enfants, the collection of vertex and face degrees is traditionally called the
passport
. In this paper we use the term “passport” as a synonym for “branch datum of width
q=3
” when speaking of a dessin.

The ordered pair of critical values can be chosen in
6
ways (reflecting the action of
\mathrm{\bf S}_{3}
on the three critical points), so each Belyi cover can be depicted by
1
,
2
,
3
, or
6
different dessins. With a slight abuse of terminology, we say that these dessins are
dual
to one another.
The passports of dual dessins are obtained as all possible permutations of the passport components:

(\lambda_{1}\mathop{|}\lambda_{2}\mathop{|}\lambda_{3})
,

(\lambda_{2}\mathop{|}\lambda_{3}\mathop{|}\lambda_{1})
,

(\lambda_{3}\mathop{|}\lambda_{1}\mathop{|}\lambda_{2})
,

(\lambda_{1}\mathop{|}\lambda_{3}\mathop{|}\lambda_{2})
,

(\lambda_{3}\mathop{|}\lambda_{2}\mathop{|}\lambda_{1})
,

(\lambda_{2}\mathop{|}\lambda_{1}\mathop{|}\lambda_{3})
.

In this paper we restrict to the case
g(X)=0
, so we work with connected graphs embedded in the sphere. Via stereographic projection we may view them as planar graphs, but a further choice remains: which face (or vertex) to place at
\infty\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{C}})
. We usually take the face of largest degree as the “outer” face, though this is not uniquely determined when several faces share the maximum degree, and we may depart from this convention for other reasons.

Plane trees, i.e. dessins with a single face, form a special subclass of genus-
0
dessins. The corresponding Belyi functions are polynomials (so-called Shabat polynomials). For plane trees, the Hurwitz realizability problem has a trivial answer: every plane tree passport is realizable. This follows from Tutte’s explicit formula
[
24
]
for the number of plane trees with a given passport, and is also a special case of Thom’s result
[
23
]
on the realizability of branch data of polynomials.

The following theorem is the restriction of Theorem
1.1
to the case of three critical values.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every genus-
0
passport of the form

\pi=(2^{r},a\mathop{|}3^{s},b\mathop{|}3^{t},c),

(4a)

or

\pi=(2^{r},a\mathop{|}3^{s}\mathop{|}3^{t},b,c),

(4b)

where
2\nmid a
,
3\nmid b
,
3\nmid c
, there exists a dessin d’enfant realizing it.

In Section
3
we reduce Theorem
1.1
to the case
q=3
, and then, in Sections
4
and
5
, describe a combinatorial construction that, given a passport of the form (
4a
) or (
4b
), produces a dessin realizing it.

1.4
Uniqueness of the Dessin

For
q=3
it is natural to ask how many realizations a given passport admits.
The following conjecture is supported by our computer calculations.

Conjecture 1.4
.

For each passport in the statement of Theorem
1.3
, the realization as a dessin d’enfant is unique.

Thus, the families of dessins constructed in this paper extend results previously obtained for plane trees
[
1
]
and weighted trees
[
15
]
. Note the structural similarity between our dessins and the weighted trees of series
C
and
E
in the classification of weighted unitrees due to Pakovich and Zvonkin; see
[
15
]
or
[
2
, Ch. 5]
.

Although we do not compute the Belyi functions explicitly and do not address the action of the Galois group on dessins d’enfants, we remark that Conjecture
1.4
would imply that the corresponding Belyi covers can be defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Furthermore, one can show that these functions cannot be expressed as nontrivial compositions. Compositionally irreducible Belyi functions over
{\mathbb{Q}}
are interesting and rather rare objects.

Proofs without Words: Historical Remarks

2.1
Bhāskara: Behold!

A popular story, found in some histories of mathematics and widely repeated in mathematical folklore, has it that the Indian mathematician Bhāskara II (12th century AD) proved the Pythagorean theorem in his treatise Bijaganita using a single diagram accompanied by the single word “Behold!”; see
[
6
]
:

Arranging this square and the four triangles in a different way, they are seen, together,
to make up the sum of the square of the two sides. “Behold!” says Bhaskara,
without adding another word of explanation.

In fact, this is
something of an exaggeration: Bhāskara uses the word much as a modern author uses “see” to refer the reader to an illustrating figure; see
[
20
]
, p. 477, or
[
6
]
, p. 333, Appendix 11 of the Russian edition of 1910.

Nevertheless, visual proofs without words are immensely attractive, and the beautiful legend gives them a perfect setting.

2.2
Visual Proofs via Dessins d’Enfants

A striking example of a Behold!-style proof using dessins d’enfants was given by Pakovich and Zvonkin; see

such that

, was stated by Birch, Chowla, Hall, and Schinzel
[

]
in 1965; its proof was given by Stothers
[

]
in 1981 using group-theoretic arguments.

(a)
Take a plane tree with

leaves and

.

(b)

In fact, the solution of the

2.3
Bhāskara: Non-Figures

In another treatise, Līlāvatī, Bhāskara introduces the term “non-figure” to describe a nonexistent polygon whose sides fail to satisfy the (generalized) triangle inequality
[
21
]
, p. 188:

A straight-sided figure described in [over]-confidence
where the sum of [all] the sides except [one] is smaller
than or equal to [that] one side is to be known as a
“non-figure.” (verse 163)

This resonates remarkably with our interest in the Hurwitz realizability problem. Perhaps we should adopt the term “non-cover” for non-realizable branch data?

Paying homage to Bhāskara, in this paper we use the term “non-triangle” for a triple
(u,v,w)
that fails to satisfy the triangle inequality.

Reduction to Three Critical Values

Proposition 3.1
.

If the realizability of branch data of the form
(\ref{eq:passport-wide-233-a})
or
(\ref{eq:passport-wide-233-b})
holds for
q=3
, then it holds for all
q
.

Proof.

We argue by induction, with the case
q=3
as the base.
Let
q\geqslant 4
and assume every such passport of width
q-1
is realizable. Take an arbitrary genus-
0
passport
\pi
of the form (
3a
) or (
3b
).

Write
\lambda=\lambda_{q}=1^{m_{1}}\,2^{m_{2}}\,3^{m_{3}}\ldots
; then

n=m_{1}+2m_{2}+3m_{3}+\ldots\geqslant 2(m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}+\ldots)-m_{1}=2\ell(\lambda)-m_{1},

\ell(\lambda)\leqslant\frac{n+m_{1}}{2}.

Set
m:=r+\ell(\lambda)-n
. From the Riemann–Hurwitz formula,

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle=r+1+s+1+t+1-n+\sum_{i=4}^{q}(\ell(\lambda_{i})-n)\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\leqslant r+s+t+3-n+\ell(\lambda)-n={}

\displaystyle=m+s+t+3-n\leqslant m+\frac{2n-2}{3}+3-n,

so
m\geqslant(n-1)/3>0
. Moreover,
r=(n-a)/2<n/2
, and therefore

m<\frac{n}{2}+\frac{n+m_{1}}{2}-n=\frac{m_{1}}{2},

so the number of
1
’s in
\lambda
exceeds
2m
. Consequently, we can split
\lambda
into two parts:

\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}\cup(1^{2m}),\qquad\lambda^{\prime}\vdash(n-2m),\qquad\ell(\lambda^{\prime})=\ell(\lambda)-2m.

Consider the passport

\pi^{\prime}=(2^{m},(n-2m)\mathop{|}3^{s},b\mathop{|}3^{t},c\mathop{|}\lambda_{4}\mathop{|}\ldots\mathop{|}\lambda_{q-1}).

By the Riemann–Hurwitz formula,

\displaystyle 2-2g(\pi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=m+1+s+1+t+1+\sum_{i=4}^{q-1}\ell(\lambda_{i})-(q-3)n={}

\displaystyle=r+\ell(\lambda)-n+1+s+1+t+1+\sum_{i=4}^{q-1}\ell(\lambda_{i})-(q-3)n={}

\displaystyle=r+1+s+1+t+1+\sum_{i=4}^{q}\ell(\lambda_{i})-(q-2)n=2-2g(\pi).

Hence
g(\pi^{\prime})=g(\pi)=0
and, by the induction hypothesis,
\pi^{\prime}
is realizable. By the monodromy correspondence going back to Hurwitz
[
7
]
, there exist permutations
g_{i}\in\mathrm{\bf S}_{n}
,
i=1,\ldots,q-1
, such that

(1)

the cycle types match the passport
\pi^{\prime}
:

\bigl(c(g_{1}),\ldots,c(g_{q-1})\bigr)=\pi^{\prime};

(2)

their product is the identity:
g_{1}\ldots g_{q-1}=1
;

(3)

they generate a transitive subgroup of
\mathrm{\bf S}_{n}
.

Conjugating all
g_{i}
by a suitable element of
\mathrm{\bf S}_{n}
, we may assume that

g_{1}=(1,2,\ldots,2n-m)\cdot\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{m},

where
\omega_{i}
are disjoint transpositions not involving
1,2,\ldots,2n-m
.

From the definition
m=r+\ell(\lambda)-n
it is clear that
m\leqslant r
. Consider the passport
\pi^{\prime\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime}\mathop{|}2^{r-m},a\mathop{|}(n-2m))
. By the Riemann–Hurwitz formula its genus is zero:

\displaystyle 2-2g(\pi^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda^{\prime})+r-m+1+1-(n-2m)={}

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda)-2m+r-m+2-(n-2m)={}

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda)+r-m+2-n=2.

Thus
\pi^{\prime\prime}
is a plane tree passport and hence realizable: there exist permutations
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathrm{\bf S}_{n-2m}
with

c(h_{1})=\lambda^{\prime},\qquad c(h_{2})=(2^{r-m},a),\qquad h_{1}\cdot h_{2}=(1,2,\ldots,n-2m).

Set
h=h_{2}\cdot\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{m}
; then

c(h)=(2^{m})\cup(2^{r-m},a)=(2^{r},a),

h_{1}\cdot h=h_{1}\cdot h_{2}\cdot\omega_{1}\ldots\omega_{m}=g_{1}.

The tuple
(h,g_{2},\ldots,g_{q-1},h_{1})
defines a covering with passport
\pi
:

(1)

the cycle types match
\pi
;

(2)

the product is the identity:

h\cdot g_{2}\ldots g_{q-1}\cdot h_{1}\sim h_{1}\cdot h\cdot g_{2}\ldots g_{q-1}=g_{1}g_{2}\ldots g_{q-1}=1;

(3)

the permutations generate a group containing
g_{1},\ldots,g_{q-1}
, hence a transitive group.

This completes the proof of the proposition.

\square

4.4
The Construction Algorithm

We are now ready to describe the structure of the dessin in the general case. It turns out that the structure of a dessin with a passport of the form (
4a
) depends on the residue
n\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12)
and on certain relations among
a
,
b
, and
c
.

Write
a=2u+1
,
b=3v+1,2
,
c=3w+1,2
with
u,v,w\geqslant 0
.
By the symmetry of the tetrahedral case, we may assume
v\leqslant w
.

Depending on the residue of
n
modulo
12
, the Riemann–Hurwitz formula gives:

•

n\equiv 1,7\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12)

\Rightarrow

2=(r+1)+(s+1)+(t+1)-n=\frac{n-a}{2}+\frac{n-b}{3}+\frac{n-c}{3}-n+3=\frac{n-1}{6}-u-v-w+2,

u+v+w=\frac{n-1}{6}.

•

n\equiv 5,11\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12)

\Rightarrow

2=(r+1)+(s+1)+(t+1)-n=\frac{n-a}{2}+\frac{n-b}{3}+\frac{n-c}{3}-n+2=\frac{n-5}{6}-u-v-w+2,

u+v+w=\frac{n-5}{6}.

Consequently,
u+v+w
is even when
n\equiv 1,5\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12)
and odd when
n\equiv 7,11\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12)
. Define the “integer semiperimeter”

p=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{u+v+w}{2},&\text{for}\ n\equiv 1,5\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12);\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{u+v+w-1}{2},&\text{for}\ n\equiv 7,11\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12).\end{cases}

The construction splits into three cases according to whether the triangle inequalities hold:

(T)

Triangle case (triangle inequalities hold):
u\leqslant p
,
v\leqslant p
,
w\leqslant p
.

(NT-3)

Non-Triangle-3 case (third inequality fails):
w>p
.

(NT-2)

Non-Triangle-2 case (first inequality fails):
u>p
.

Remark 4.3
.

When
n\equiv 1,5\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12)
, the inequality
u\leqslant p
is trivially equivalent to
u\leqslant v+w
. When
n\equiv 7,11\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12)
, the inequality
u\leqslant p
is equivalent to
u+1\leqslant v+w
, but since
u+v+w
is odd, this is in turn equivalent to
u\leqslant v+w
. Thus, despite the non-standard definition of the “semiperimeter”
p
, the stated inequalities are equivalent to the usual triangle inequalities.

Remark 4.4
.

Our interpretation of
u
,
v
,
w
as side lengths of a triangle (or of a non-triangle) is formal. We use it to unify the construction of the dessin realizing a given passport. It would be interesting to find a deeper connection between the geometry of this triangle and the analytic properties of the corresponding Belyi functions.
