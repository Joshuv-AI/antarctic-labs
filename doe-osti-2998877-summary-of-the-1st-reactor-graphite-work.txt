Meeting Goals
 Graphite is widely used as moderator in many fission reactors and other criticality systems. The
understanding of its short- and long-term behavior in fission environments is crucial not only for the
operation and optimization of existing reactors but also for the efficient design and safe deployment of
future advanced reactors. It is thus of utmost importance to incorporate in nuclear data libraries
accurate evaluations of the interaction of thermal neutrons with graphite used in applications. This is
however a complex task as there are challenges in defining the detailed characterizations of the studied
material, appropriate modeling, and optimal validation suite. This will only be achieved by the concerted
effort of experts of all relevant areas. The objective of the 1st Reactor Graphite (ReGra) workshop is
precisely to bring together this expertise and define a path forward in the following points.
 Material characterization: Not only there are different types of graphite used in reactor
applications, with internal structure ranging from idealized crystalline to highly porous, but also
prolonged exposure to neutron radiation affects and changes the material structure. We aim to clearly
identify, from the application perspective, what material characteristics need to be described in order to
properly model graphite performance.
 Modeling: Once the material characteristics are identified, we aim to investigate the different
modeling approaches, and ideally identify preferred ones, from the physics perspective.
 Differential data: Models are only as good as their capability to reproduce experimental data. The
workshop should deliberate and define which differential data sets should be used to compare and
constrain the models. Also, in case of ambiguity or data gaps, we should define clear recommendations
for new experimental measurements.
 Validation: Ultimately, adoption of nuclear data libraries is intrinsically tied with their usability in
applications. Therefore, it essential to define which benchmarks or integral tests can and should be used
to assess the quality and performance of evaluations. This workshop should provide a set of
recommended benchmarks or integral tests to be used to assess and cross-compare different potential
candidate evaluations.
 Library recommendation: The final goal of this effort is to produce one single set of recommended
thermal neutron scattering law evaluations for reactor graphite to be eventually incorporated in the
next ENDF/B release. This recommendation is to be authored by all participants of the ReGra workshop.
Due to the complexity of the task, provided there are significant progress in the topics above, the 1st
ReGra workshop can still be considered successful even if a consensus on final library recommendation
is not achieved, leaving the final decision for a second future workshop.
 All the discussions, perspectives, approaches, findings and decisions were thoroughly documented
and are being released in the present laboratory report authored by all workshop participants.
 The ReGra workshop was planned and organized by the Cross Section Evaluation Working Group
(CSEWG) and Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL). The CSEWG process has always been about making
ENDF the best evaluated data file it can be via a collegial and collaborative process that has historically
been institution and participant agnostic – the only thing that matters about data evaluations is that
each one is better and more useful than the preceding evaluations. This process has worked
considerably well for the past 60 years. Part of the reason that ENDF has achieved such widespread
success and use is that, ultimately, the users trust the process. To avoid any potential disruption to this
process or erosion of the community’s trust, CSEWG and BNL strongly encourage the open, de-
personalized, collaborative, respectful, selfless, and constructive exchange of technical ideas.

Key Takeaways from the Workshop

Background
 Graphite is a complex, sometimes ordered and sometimes stochastic material whose neutronics
properties are important to understand. In this workshop, we aimed to layout a path forward for the
series of graphite evaluations in the ENDF library while abiding by the CSEWG principal of "First, do no
harm."
 The first known evaluations of graphite were in the very first edition of BNL-325 (1952). In the
1960’s, General Atomics greatly improved the evaluations. Although these improvements pre-date
ENDF/B-I, they were not included in a library until ENDF/B-III in 1973. It was known for some time that
the reactor graphite thermal scattering law (TSL) evaluations were not performing adequately in
criticality simulations (although some benchmarks may have issues in themselves), so the LEIP group at
NCSU adopted an ab-initio approach using their FLASSH code and created for the graphite files in
ENDF/B-VIII.0. More specifically, using ENDF-6 format nomenclature, Files 1,3,4 were included in
ENDF/B-VIII.0 and carried forward in ENDF/B-VIII.1. Files 2,4 were added in ENDF/B-VIII.1. The full suite
of graphite TSL evaluations in ENDF are:
 1. Crystalline graphite (incoherent approximation, 0% porosity)
 2. Crystalline graphite (+Sd correction, 0% porosity)
 3. Nuclear graphite (incoherent approximation, 10% porosity)
 4. Nuclear graphite (incoherent approximation, 20% porosity)
 5. Nuclear graphite (incoherent approximation, 30% porosity)
In the nuclear graphite cases, the LEIP group modeled porosity by randomly (& uniformly) removing
individual nuclei to reach the desired density. The LEIP group argues this approach conserves scattering
reaction rate and bulk neutron transport properties. Since the release of ENDF/B-VIII.1, the ORNL group
has suggested to adopt only option #2 arguing that large voids change macroscopic density but
otherwise do not impact TSLs because the crystalline order is maintained. The ORNL group argues that
these large voids manifest themselves through small angle neutron scattering (SANS). Whichever model
matches reality, what matters is what the neutron undergoes ("sees").
 This begs the question, in a reactor, what does a neutron see? The de Broglie wavelength of the
neutron determines the size of the patch of material which the neutron will scatter off. The
arrangement of nuclei in this patch determines the (elastic and inelastic) scattering cross-section. The
patch that the neutrons interact with may be complex, ordered, stochastic, or a combination of all of
these. For materials for which the concept of a phonon is applicable:
 - phonon density depends on material temperature.
 - phonons interact differently with different size/shape features.
The cross sections determine the mean free path (MFP) and the diffusion length. The MFP and the
macro-geometry of the system determine how many scatterings the neutron will experience before
leaving the system or being absorbed. The de Broglie wavelength depends on neutron temperature
(which corresponds to a dependency on a range of energies for a given temperature). To summarize,
the local material properties are coupled to neutron cross-sections in the long neutron wavelength
regime.
 Given this background, these are the emerging questions and the drivers for this meeting, aside
from the evaluator opinion differences noted above:
 - What is the impact of radiation damage on graphite performance?
 o Validation test coverage (considering temperatures and graphite types) is
 insufficient.

 - Do we understand the microstructure of (all the) graphite(s) to a sufficient degree for

Current Experimental Situation
Radiation damage effects: The ORNL team performed irradiations of graphite samples at HFIR inducing
9-30 dpas, with neutron fluences of ~13-41 x 10^25 n/m^2 and ~6-17 reactor cycles [1]. This led to two
observations:
 - The PDOSs of the irradiated samples preserve the main features of the un-irradiated
 graphite PDOS, even after 30 dpa irradiation at HFIR.
 - There is evidence of damage tolerance at elevated temperatures due to self-annealing
             above ~500K.
These observations should inform new high-temperature graphite moderated reactor designs. Also, it
has been indicated that there is no need to be overly concerned about the effect of radiation damage on
reactor graphite TSL evaluations at this time. Depending on where the graphite is in the reactor
(reflector or fuel), it may be changed out every cycle or stay in the reactor for the lifetime of the reactor.
For some SMR designs, we then expect ~0.1-1 dpa/year (depending on power density).

State of validation test cases: Establishing a validation basis for exiting or possible new graphite
evaluations is essential for quality control and user acceptance. To that end, users need official ENDF/B-
VIII.1 ACE files to do proper validation. Out of the full suite of graphite-moderated benchmarks, the
understanding is that: a) they cover a limited temperature range for cross section and code validation
for high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) analysis purposes; b) there are limited benchmark data
for radial power profiles (although this is more of a reactor physics question then a graphite question)
and c) graphite impurity characterization listed as one of the main contributors to uncertainty in
benchmark for HTTR.
 To address this, Catherine Percher did a careful consideration of ICSBEP, IRPhEP, SINBAD and
SFCOMPO databases and recommends the following benchmark cases for graphite TSL validation:
 - FUND-ORELA-ACC-GRAPH-PNSDT-001 (1 case)
 - LEU-COMP-THERM-060 (28 cases)
 - HEU-COMP-THERM-016 (6 cases)
Travis Greene also did such a study and recommends additionally:
 - IEU-COMP-THERM-008 (5 cases) - this was not included in Catherine's list because in the
 benchmark evaluation a regular lattice was used and some pebbles were cut-off at the wall
 interface, but otherwise the experiment was of high quality.
 - IEU-COMP-THERM-010 (HTR10-GCR-RESR-001) (1 case) (SCALE and MCNP®) - - this was not
 included in Catherine's list because the pebble height was not measured and was assumed,
 so the packing fraction is not well determined. Also the pebbles were not uniform.
 Some cases show troubling discrepancies between SCALE and MCNP results. Examples from T.
Greene's talk are IEU-COMP-THERM-010 (HTR10-GCR-RESR-001) and HTTR-GCR-RESR-001. Additionally,

 6

existing benchmark models were built with essentially homogenized graphite, potentially missing
important physics. Biases were developed using crystalline graphite and the biases may need to be
reassessed using alternative porosities.
 The HTR-PROTEUS cases are in the process of being re-evaluated, including a more sophisticated
model for the random pebble drop. Uncertainties are well characterized. Core 5 is useful for ND
validation already and the random code model (Core 4) is getting close to being ready. Up to 12 core
models may ultimately be eventually ready for validation. The old core 4 models with random pebble
loading do not appear useful for validation.
 Through informal private communications with A. Hawari, it has been indicated that JAEA is working
on a better HTTR benchmark (HTTR-GCR-RESR-001) as the old one is not good due to unknown bias and
poorly understood boron concentration. It was originally meant as an engineering test, not a
benchmark.
 Both DEIMOS and THETA will provide additional validation, once the benchmark specifications are
ready. Additional experiments are also coming up.

State of graphite differential and quasi-integral experiments: Among differential measurements, the
most informative are inelastic neutron-scattering experiments, which yield both the scattering function
S(α, β) and the derived phonon density of states (PDOS). For quasi-integral measurements, neutron-
transmission experiments provide the greatest insight. In addition to these, NIST ultracold neutron
transmission, Positron Annihilation Spectroscopy and neutron powder diffraction studies were
performed.
A series of measurements were conducted at the Spallation Neutron Source, measuring S(Q, E) at ARCS,
SEQUOIA and VISION, allowing for the extraction of the PDOS. The first SEQUOIA measurements [2]
enabled low resolution PDOS measurements, and these laid the foundation for the LEIP lab evaluations.
A second SEQUOIA measurement of nuclear graphite that was performed later [3]. Subsequent ARCS
measurements were much higher resolution and enabled high resolution extraction of the PDOS for a
variety of fresh and irradiated reactor-grade graphites [4,5]. Comparisons of calculated PDOS for
crystalline graphite are in good agreement with reactor grade measurements with some modest
differences after irradiation. These differences may need characterization/understanding and it is not
clear these differences impact criticality. Additionally, inelastic neutron scattering measurements of
S(alpha,beta) of six different grades of nuclear graphite, from different manufacturers and different
porosities were measured at VISION. All these measurements were described by DFT calculations [5,6].
We note that the various ENDF reactor grade porosity cases do not agree with ARCS data or specific heat
measurements. However, it was also argued that special care was needed to correctly extract the heat
capacity from the porosity TSL files and that would lead to an agreement with the measured heat
capacity. This statement still needs to be independently verified. Finally, the high precision ARCS
measurements were small sample measurements so that multiple scattering was absent. In a reactor,
there multiple scattering is an important effect that seems to be captured by the ENDF reactor graphites
even if the details appear incorrect.
 The complete set of transmission measurements on graphite were also summarized. All together
there were 51 datasets with an additional 3 more that are preliminary and not considered here. Of
these, only 5 of do not have SANS effects or have SANS corrected below the first Bragg edge: Palevsky
(1955), Egelstaff (1957), Shamasundar (1974), Steyerl (1974), and Zhou (2006) (an NCSU thesis
experiment). In addition, analysis of ISIS measurements performed by ORNL are in progress and these
include temperature dependent transmission measurements.

Additional Questions
SANS questions: Small Angle Neutron Scattering (SANS) is very sensitive to pores as neutrons scatter off
of pore edges and needs to be corrected carefully. It is clearly an experimentally observable feature and
must be corrected for in transmission measurements above and below the first the Bragg edge. Is this
something that should be managed by the transport code/system model rather than built into an
evaluation? OpenMC can simulate this effect through NCrystal SANS plugin and physics and appears to
be more of a bulk material phenomenon and should not be built into the TSL.

Grain size & Extinction questions: Can extinction notably affect thermal scattering of reactor-grade
graphite being considered for use in advanced reactors? If so, what is the most appropriate way of
treating graphite extinction and is use of an effective grain size appropriate? Does extinction need to be
accounted for in nuclear design methods used in design of graphite-moderated reactors? Extinction
should be handled by the transport codes and should be outside the scope of the evaluation discussion.
However, it is an issue for benchmarking although the effect is much smaller than the SANS effect noted
above.

Main Workshop Findings
Processing and data files: It was noted by several attendees that the proliferation of TSL files and the
correct processing of these files is a pain point. There is a desire for streamlined TSL files and TSL
processing files. Unfortunately, if there is no “one-size fits all” TSL, we will need individual TSLs to cover
all situations.
 Also, the question arose, how should we interpolate TSLs vs temperature? (this is more of a code
implementation question). Alternatively, we could develop a model code that generates TSLs on the fly,
similar to NCrystal. This would also help users with intermediate temperatures but that leads
potentially to evaluation file version chaos.
 Following on these two points, there was a request from users for more processing code training,
especially one that focuses on TSL. There already is such training for the SCALE code system at ORNL.
The NJOY and FUDGE trainings currently occur at the Nuclear Energy Agency in Paris and so there is a
market for training in the US. This training could run as a satellite workshop at an ANS or CSEWG
meeting. Virtual trainings are also possible, but not ideal.
 Finally, additional information is needed to support uncertainty quantification and cross section set
validation. This ideally would include covariance data for TSL evaluations and include elevated
temperatures.

Evaluation codes: Many members of CSEWG and elsewhere would like access to FLASSH. We
encourage making an initial public release of FLASSH.

Materials science: The detailed microstructure of graphite can have a significant neutronics impact.
Therefore, understanding this microstructure is key for modeling. X-ray studies can complement
measurements at the Spallation Neutron Source, but X-rays are not as penetrating so there is skepticism
that synchrotron work will help with bulk characterization. Nevertheless, we have several materials
science questions:
 - We are told that there is not much sample-to-sample variation, but none of us seriously
 believe it. The mechanical properties do not vary much between vendors, but neutrons are
 sensitive to details in the bulk

 8

 -  Radiation damage is a big question. On one hand, it introduces defects over several length
 scales. On the other hand, at high temperatures, the samples self-anneal, healing the
 damage. Therefore irradiation studies would help. Getting samples from the INL NSUF (PIE
 repo) can be a route. We will need irradiation history (both fluence and temperature) since
 the self-annealing effect is a high-temperature effect.
 - We will need samples produced from multiple vendors and/or different manufacturing
 techniques as these impact both the microstructure and impurities.
Much of our questions have been answered in literature, it would be helpful to have more appropriate
SME's at any follow-on workshops.

New differential experiments: We need new neutron transmission measurements, especially below
Bragg edge, which are the most important for reactor physics and bulk transport. These are important
physical constraints for all TSLs. Also, confirmation of the radiation damage study would be useful.
Finally, it is possible such data already exists in literature and that we are unaware of it, given the poor
coverage of TSL measurements in EXFOR.

Validation: With conflicting opinions about the correct approach to modeling graphite, and new user
needs, establishing a recommended suite of trustworthy validation benchmarks is essential. We
currently have a small set of cases, but it must be expanded. Two sets of benchmarks have revisions
pending (HTTR-GCR-RESR-001 and PROTEUS) and IEU-COMP-THERM-008 is a target of opportunity for
revision, specifically IEU-COMP-THERM-008 case 5. This case was not included in Catherine's list
because the benchmark evaluation a regular lattice was used and some pebbles were cut-off but
otherwise the experiment was high quality. Re-evaluating this benchmark is a target of opportunity for
DNCSH funding
 We need more benchmarks to provide a validation basis for novel reactor design (especially HTGRs),
including HALEU fueled, graphite moderated, TRISO fuel form. Validation for core design tools could
benefit from:
 - Studies with radial power shapes (closer to assembly-wise radial power shape)
 - Additional high temperature benchmarks
 - Additional burnup benchmarks.
 New types of integral tests can also help:
 - We request independent thick-target (how many MFP?) transmission measurements for
 validation. The question of MFP depends on whether the measurement is above or below
 the Bragg edge. will also resolve SANS questions.
 - Also, consider Pile Oscillation or other historical approaches as alternative to critical
 assemblies.
 - Single source graphite measurements could be a clean test and should be considered. These
 could be oscillators, PNDA variations or measurements of spatial flux.
 - We recommend (re)establishing a Pulsed-Neutron Slowing Down Time (PNSDT) capability
 which includes a furnace so that elevated temperature TSLs can be tested. This should
 include a neutron source with a ~1 microsecond pulse structure.
 In all benchmark graphite(s) impurities need to be carefully characterized. Understanding the time-
evolution of the impurities as a function of burn-up and specifying the impurities in terms of boron
equivalents is requested. These benchmarks should include different grades of nuclear graphite (reactor
vs. crystalline), irradiated graphite and monolithic samples of graphite which are many MFP thick.

 9

Finally, the samples should be produced from multiple vendors and/or different manufacturing
techniques.

 We recommend creating a shared repository of input decks and code so we can do cross-validation.
This could this be a CSEWG effort or a part of a WPEC subgroup. As this would focus on graphite, it
would be separate from the VANDAL project.

Do we need a follow-on workshop? New experiments are in progress, and they will likely lead to new
evaluations. Similarly, re-evaluation of benchmarks is likely to happen in a few years. So, the answer is
probably “yes” in 2-3 years. If we do have a follow-on meeting, we need to bring in more neutron
scattering experts and materials scientists, reactor physics methods experts and reactor designers. We
also strongly encourage the involvement and participation of members of the user community, material
scientists and other subject matter experts in future CSEWG meetings.

 Date: Tuesday, July 8, 2025

Qualifying Graphite for Reactor Applications: A Materials Perspective
 Speaker: Dominic Piedmont

 Overview: ASME standard required of all reactor grade materials prior commercial implementation
that requires characterization of properties:
 • Physical
 • Mechanical
 • Thermal
 • Impact of relevant reactor conditions/environmental factors (oxidation, irradiation, etc.)
The last material that achieved this was Alloy 617 in 2019 (first in 30 years). Currently no reactor
graphite grades are qualified. The qualification process is quite elaborate, and we are barely at the
materials characterization stage.
 Experimental characterization can be performed at a synchrotron light source. This would allow
structure-property correlation and understanding the compound nature of microstructure/properties.
It is imperative that modeling length scale corresponds to length scale of experimental measurement.
Multimodal characterization via synchrotron techniques could play a role.
 Nanoscale measurements with X-ray absorption can reach resolution as low as 0.002𝜇m. Will reveal
element-specific local structure, coordination number, interatomic spacing and short-range order. This
can help with model validation.
 Microscale measurements with X-ray tomography can reach resolution as low as 5𝜇m. Our NSUF
beamline has resolution down to 5 𝜇m, various mechanisms are available for contrast: Absorption,
diffraction, fluorescence, etc. Direct visualization of 3D morphology, structure, and density are also
possible. diffracting domain orientation mapping is possible too.
 Structure Property Correlations. In-situ capabilities allow for bulk/environmental changes to occur
while microscale properties are measured: High temperature (> 1000 °C), Vapor pressure,
Electrochemical. Loading Data is at length scales appropriate for engineering scale model for
initialization and verification.
 Conclusion: Reaching the summit requires clear communication, using the right tool for the job, and
focused attention. Models must be experimentally validated and are only as good as the real-world data
they represent. Synchrotron offers a methodology of initializing and/or validating computational models
across a range of length-scales and modalities.

NNL Perspective of Reactor-grade Graphite
 Speaker: Mike Zerkle

 Overview: Reactor-grade graphite has a complicated fabrication process dependent structure so
that it is effectively a stochastic media with a wide range of porosity and texture. Accurate
representation of thermal neutron scattering in a particular grade of reactor-graphite requires
understanding of
 • As-fabricated microstructure
 • Inelastic neutron scattering properties
 • Bulk neutron transport (neutron transmission) – the neutron mean free path is 2.5 – 2.6 cm
 • Well characterized integral experiment benchmarks for validation
The current ENDF-6/GNDS formats implicitly assume isotropically pure, oriented, infinitely small, fine
powders but many grades of reactor-grade graphite violate these assumptions.
 Coherent elastic thermal scattering is characterized by a strict relationship between neutron energy
and scattering angle. In a perfect crystal, there is only one scattering angle available for a given neutron
energy / lattice plane scattering event. However, if a neutron scatters multiple times in the same
perfect crystal to “rejoin” the initial direction of travel or does not change energy (elastic), then it would
appear as if the scattering event never occurred. This would decrease the observed cross section.
 In the ND-2025 conference, two presentations focused on extinction:
 • [Trainer] “Modeling the Extinction Effect in Beryllium Metal”
 • [Di Julio] “New thermal scattering libraries for beryllium metal and other polycrystalline
 materials including extinction effects”
This extinction effect is more severe for materials with larger grain sizes (no extinction effect for fine
powder)

 Figure 2: Beryllium metal (area-averaged grain size 8.5μm) [Trainer et al. 2025]

 14

 A paper by Egelstaff (1956) presented experimental results from 1952. These consisted of samples
of British reactor-grade graphite used for the Harwell and Windscale piles. Measurements were taken
to consider two different graphite extrusion directions. Results show a significant difference in cross
section due to grain structure but there was no characterization of grain-size distribution. These results
emphasize that extinction could affect thermal scattering in nuclear-grade graphite.
 Fine powder cross section generated using ENDF/B-VIII.0 crystalline graphite TSL uses structure
factors for pyrolytic graphite. There is an implicit assumption which is infinitely small randomly oriented
crystallites. Including extinction model following the work of Sabine [1985,1988] (see Fig. 2). In recent
work on Be metal, extinction models have been observed to overpredict severity of the extinction
effect. Use of an effective grain size has been proposed but does this effect also apply to reactor-grade
graphite?

 Figure 3: Impact of extinction models on the total scattering cross-
 section of graphite.

 Microstructure of reactor-grade graphite can vary wildly according to manufacturing process, etc.
and there is wide variation in grain size. Infinitely small grain size approximation historically assumed in
ENDF TSL evaluations may not apply for some of these reactor-grade graphites.

 Summarizing,
 • Reactor-grade graphite has a complicated fabrication process-dependent structure that can
 impact thermal neutron scattering
 • The extinction effect appears when multiple coherent elastic scattering events effectively
 ‘cancel each other out’. Occurs in regions of perfect crystals. Larger grains increase the
 severity of the effect. As grain size → 0 (i.e., approaching fine powder), extinction effect
 becomes negligible
 • Recent analysis of the extinction effect on beryllium metal has been considered. Existing
 extinction models have been shown to overpredict the effect. Use of an effective grain size
 has been proposed
 • Concern is this extinction may need to be a considered when interpreting graphite
 measurements
Can extinction notably affect thermal scattering of reactor-grade graphite being considered for use in
advanced reactors? What is the most appropriate way of treating graphite extinction? Is use of an

effective grain size appropriate? Does extinction need to be accounted for in nuclear design methods
used in design of graphite-moderated reactors? Suspect neutron transmission measurements of
reactor-grade graphite samples with well-characterized grain structure will be needed to inform
resolution of these questions

 Project Chrono was a much more advanced “random pebble placement” model. The DEM uses
 Newton’s laws of motion to determine the linear and rotational movement of particles. Used GPU-
 based (high performance compared to CPU equivalent). A major assumption in the DEM is that particles
 are permitted to have small amounts of intersection at points of contact. This intersection is then used
 to compute the normal and tangential (friction) forces between particles using a spring-dashpot
 mechanism. Another assumption is that all pebbles have the same density which is an average between
 fuel and moderator pebbles. To accelerate the simulation, pebbles are dropped as "sheets" into the
 vessel, with positions within the sheet randomly assigned.

 • Javier Ortensi – I don’t understand how you run this calculation to get phonon spectrum
 • Iyad Al-Qasir - I employed an ab initio lattice dynamics approach, where a large graphite
 supercell was constructed to capture long-range interatomic interactions between carbon
 atoms within the graphene layers. A single atom at a time was slightly displaced from its
 equilibrium position—within the harmonic limit—and the resulting forces on all atoms
 (known as Hellmann–Feynman forces) were calculated using density functional theory
 (DFT). With both the displacement amplitudes and the corresponding forces known, the
 interatomic force constants were determined. These force constants were then used to

Overview of historical and new transmission measurements of nuclear graphite
 Speaker: Chris Chapman

 Overview: How do pores contribute to total cross section? Small Angle Neutron Scattering (SANS) is
the manifestation of pores in nuclear graphite. This has been known in graphite since at least Egelstaff
(1957). History of potential confusion behind underlying cause e.g. Bowman (2008). The current
consensus is that SANS manifests from porosity (e.g. Petriw (2010), Gould (2010)).
 SANS is an effect based on the material characteristics (i.e., pores). Previous attempts have been
made to correct for SANS in total cross section measurements. If SANS is present, one of two things
needs to be done to make a faithful comparison between processed & experimental data: Experimental
corrections and SANS adjustment to processed data.
 SANS adjustments to processed data can be done IF material characteristics are well known but
need to be done with care. Minor changes in graphite sample can lead to significantly different SANS
contributions, so must be handled with EXTREME care. Searches in EXFOR reveal a variety of
experiments with and without SANS. Without SANS, good agreement between data and Sd crystalline
NCSU graphite evaluation. Of these SANS corrected data sets: Palevsky (1955) gave no sample
information, Egelstaff (1957) corrected for SANS, Zhou (2006) and Steyerl (1974) in good agreement.
With SANS, above the Bragg edge there is chaos as neutron scatters off of many separate surfaces.
 Conclusions:
• Many total cross sections are available for graphite
• SANS is a material effect which manifests from porosity in materials
• Careful considerations required when comparing processed ENDF files against experimental data
• Pyrolytic graphite best sample for comparing with inelastic scattering cross section data (due to lack
 of SANS)
• When SANS is not present (i.e., pyrolytic graphite), Sd shows best agreement with total cross section
 measurements
