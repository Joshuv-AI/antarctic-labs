3D Radiomic Texture Analysis of Quantitative Muscle MRI Enhances the Distinction Between Myotonic Dystrophy Type 1 and Charcot–Marie‐Tooth Neuropathy Type 1A: A Proof‐of‐Concept Study
Authors: Louise Iterbeke, Lotte Huysmans, Kobe Bamps, Ronald Peeters, Veerle Goosens, Frederik Maes, Patrick Dupont, Kristl G. Claeys
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDifferentiating myogenic from neurogenic neuromuscular diseases (NMDs) can be clinically challenging. While quantitative muscle MRI (qMRI) with proton density fat fraction (PDFF,%) quantifies fat replacement, it misses micro‐spatial patterns linked to underlying pathology. This study investigates whether qMRI with 3D radiomic texture analysis (TA) might improve differentiation between myogenic and neurogenic diseases, using myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) and Charcot–Marie‐Tooth neuropathy type 1A (CMT1A) as proof‐of‐concept models.MethodsThirty‐three adults with DM1, 33 with CMT1A, and 33 matched healthy controls were included. qMRI on a 3T Philips Achieva system using a 6‐point Dixon sequence generated PDFF(%) maps of the lower limbs, and a convolutional neural network performed 3D segmentation of 28 lower limb muscles. We extracted macroscopic features, including muscle volume, asymmetry, and disto‐proximal gradients, alongside micro‐spatial radiomic features (entropy, contrast, homogeneity) to quantify tissue heterogeneity.ResultsBoth patient cohorts exhibited higher PDFF(%) in all lower limb muscles compared to controls (p < 0.001). DM1 predominantly involved the posterior compartment, while CMT1A targeted the anterolateral compartment with significantly steeper disto‐proximal fat gradients (p < 0.05). TA revealed higher entropy and contrast, and lower homogeneity in CMT1A compared to DM1, reflecting a more reticular pattern of fat infiltration vs. the confluent pattern in DM1.ConclusionIn this proof‐of‐concept study, 3D radiomic texture analysis of PDFF(%) maps revealed distinct spatial patterns of fat replacement in DM1 and CMT1A. Integrating radiomic and conventional qMRI features may enhance the non‐invasive distinction between DM1 and CMT1A and warrants further investigation in a broader range of NMDs.

Introduction

Myopathies and hereditary peripheral neuropathies are heterogeneous genetic disorders with distinct pathomechanisms but overlapping clinical phenotypes characterized by progressive muscle weakness and atrophy [1, 2]. Electromyography (EMG) and serum creatine kinase (CK) levels are commonly used diagnostically but are not always conclusive, making differentiation challenging [3, 4]. Muscle biopsy can provide additional information but is invasive and not consistently discriminative. Definitive diagnosis often relies on molecular genetic testing, although the diagnostic rate is lower in certain hereditary peripheral neuropathies, such as distal hereditary motor neuropathies (dHMN) [5, 6]. Consequently, patients can experience prolonged diagnostic trajectories, delaying appropriate disease monitoring and access to emerging, disease‐specific therapies [4].

Muscle Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is a valuable non‐invasive tool in the diagnostic workup of neuromuscular disorders (NMDs), enabling characterization of disease‐specific patterns of muscle involvement [7, 8, 9]. Previous studies have described distinct micro‐spatial patterns of fat replacement in myogenic and neurogenic diseases. A reticular pattern of fat replacement and the presence of “muscle islands” was suggested to be characteristic of neurogenic diseases, whereas more homogeneous, moth‐eaten patterns were features of myogenic conditions [7, 8, 10]. However, the evaluation of these patterns relied on qualitative gradings, using observer‐dependent visual descriptions, and semi‐quantitative scoring systems such as the Mercuri scale [11].

While quantitative MRI (qMRI) with proton density fat fraction (PDFF, %) has been shown to be more sensitive than qualitative or semi‐quantitative scoring [12, 13], current qMRI analyses focus on macroscopic measures such as mean PDFF (%), muscle volume, and asymmetry [14, 15]. These measures already provide substantial information but may not fully capture the micro‐spatial pattern of fat replacement. Radiomic texture analysis addresses this limitation by extracting quantitative features that characterize micro‐spatial intensity distributions [16]. By quantifying the heterogeneity of tissue characteristics, it offers significantly higher sensitivity than standard visual assessments [17, 18]. Radiomic texture analysis has shown promise in skeletal muscle imaging for differentiating NMD subtypes [19, 20] and characterizing patterns of fat infiltration [15, 18, 21].

To move beyond qualitative descriptions of fat replacement patterns, we applied 3D radiomic texture analysis of PDFF (%) maps to determine whether micro‐spatial fat replacement patterns differentiate myogenic from neurogenic diseases. As comparative disease models, we selected Myotonic Dystrophy type 1 (DM1) and Charcot–Marie‐Tooth neuropathy type 1A (CMT1A), among the most prevalent myogenic and neurogenic conditions with predominantly distal involvement [22, 23], to assess both macroscopic and micro‐spatial muscle MRI features of the lower limbs.

Methods

Participants and Study Design

We included patients with genetically confirmed adult‐onset DM1 or CMT1A, and matched healthy controls. Patient eligibility criteria were (i) age ≥ 18 years; (ii) presence of distal lower limb muscle weakness; and (iii) ambulatory status; use of assistive devices was permitted. Individuals with contraindications to MRI were excluded from participation. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee Research UZ/KU Leuven (s67475;s67476) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. Age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and disease duration were recorded at the time of the MRI scan. Disease duration was defined as the interval between self‐reported symptom onset and study visit.

MRI Acquisition and Analysis

MRI data were acquired using a 3 T scanner (Achieva, Philips Medical Systems, The Netherlands). Participants were positioned supine, feet‐first. Axial T1‐weighted images of the lower limbs were acquired for anatomical reference (four stacks, TR 546 ms, TE 20 ms, 30 slices, 6 mm slice thickness, 1 mm interslice gap, FOV 455 × 301 mm2, voxel size 0.8 × 0.8 mm2). Muscle PDFF (%) was quantified using a 3D 6‐point Dixon sequence (TR/TE/ΔTE = 10/1.5/1.0 ms, flip angle 3°, 130 slices, slice thickness 2 mm, FOV 450 × 394 × 260 mm3, matrix 348 × 304 × 130, voxel size 1.2 × 1.2 × 2 mm3). Five image stacks encompassed proximal (anterior superior iliac spine to tibial plateau) and distal (patella to lateral malleolus) lower limbs. Dixon images were processed using an in‐house AI tool based on a convolutional neural network (CNN) [24]. Fat, water, in‐phase, and out‐of‐phase images were preprocessed to stitched PDFF (%) maps of the proximal and distal lower limbs. The CNN subsequently predicted 3D whole‐muscle segmentations of 28 individual lower limb muscles (18 proximal and 10 distal) [24]. Segmentations were visually inspected and corrected if necessary using ITK‐Snap. Mean PDFF (%) of each muscle and volume‐weighted mean for each muscle group were extracted using a MATLAB script (MathWorks, Natick, USA). In the distal lower limb, muscle groups were defined as anterolateral compartment (tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, and peroneus) and posterior compartment (medial and lateral gastrocnemius, soleus, tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, flexor hallucis longus, and popliteus). In the proximal lower limb, muscles were grouped into quadriceps (rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus intermedius, and vastus medialis), adductor (adductor longus, adductor brevis, and adductor magnus), and hamstring (biceps femoris long head and short head, semitendinosus, and semimembranosus).

MRI Macroscopic Features

Total muscle volume (cm3) was calculated by multiplying the segmented voxels by the voxel volume. Muscle volumes were normalized to the participants' height squared (cm3/m2) and standardized as Z‐scores relative to the matched healthy controls. Muscles with Z‐scores ≤ −2.0 or ≥ 2.0 were considered atrophic or hypertrophic, respectively [25]. To evaluate asymmetry in both legs in fat replacement, the mean PDFF (%) was extracted for the right and left leg independently. An absolute left–right PDFF difference ≥ 10% was considered asymmetric [13].

To evaluate the disto‐proximal fat distribution, axial slice coordinates were normalized to a relative scale of 0–100% (0% = proximal, 100% = distal) to account for inter‐individual differences in muscle length. Mean PDFF (%) was extracted for each axial slice along the anatomical z‐axis. A Generalized Additive Model (GAM) was used to generate a smoothed representation of the gradient [12]. The disto‐proximal gradient was then defined as the rate of change in PDFF (%) relative to the normalized length, calculated via linear regression.

MRI Micro‐Spatial Features

To evaluate the micro‐spatial texture in muscle fat replacement, 3D radiomic texture analysis features were extracted from the PDFF (%) maps using the PyRadiomics library [26]. Prior to extraction, MRI data underwent B‐spline interpolation to isotropic voxels (1.2 × 1.2 × 1.2 mm3) consistent with the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative guidelines [27, 28]. To ensure biological relevance, gray‐level discretization was performed using a fixed bin width of 1.0% PDFF (%). This resulted in 100 discrete intensity bins, capturing the full physiological range of fat infiltration (0–100%). Three Gray‐Level Co‐occurrence Matrix (GLCM) features (contrast, joint entropy, and homogeneity) were selected a priori for their correspondence to qualitative patterns previously described in NMDs. Specifically, contrast measures local intensity variation. High contrast identifies sharp transitions between preserved muscle fibers and localized fat deposits, serving as a marker for the spatial granularity [14, 15, 21]. Joint Entropy is a measure of the randomness (disorder). High entropy characterizes the micro‐spatial complexity and chaotic mixture of fat and muscle fibers [15, 18, 21]. Finally, homogeneity measures the local uniformity. Higher values represent a smooth, contiguous tissue texture where neighboring voxels have similar PDFF (%) levels [14, 15, 21]. All radiomic texture features were extracted from these quantitative PDFF (%) maps. The T1‐weighted images were used only for qualitative illustration.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). Continuous variables were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with pairwise post hoc comparisons performed via Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using Fisher's exact test. To evaluate differences in tissue texture, radiomic features were analyzed using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) models. Disease group was included as a fixed effect, and the mean PDFF (%) of the corresponding muscle was included as a covariate. Radiomic features were log‐transformed prior to modeling to satisfy assumptions. Results are reported as estimated marginal means ± standard error (SE). Associations between CTG repeat length and disease duration with mean PDFF (%), entropy, homogeneity, and contrast were assessed with Spearman rank correlations. All p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR). Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p < 0.05.

Results

Age and sex did not differ significantly between groups. BMI was significantly different between patients and healthy controls, but not between the DM1 and CMT1A groups (p = 0.4). Disease duration did not differ significantly between the patient groups (p = 0.1).

Fat Replacement, Asymmetry and Muscle Volume

Although all muscles showed statistically higher PDFF (%) than controls (Table 1), the mean PDFF (%) of individual muscles is provided in Table S1. Within the patient populations, the DM1 group exhibited significantly higher PDFF (%) in the soleus (46.3% vs. 15.3%, p < 0.001), gastrocnemius medialis (52.9% vs. 26.2%, p < 0.001), gastrocnemius lateralis (31.0% vs. 15.0%, p = 0.01), and flexor hallucis longus (39.5% vs. 20.1%, p < 0.001) compared to the CMT1A group. In the proximal lower limb muscles, the PDFF (%) of the vastus lateralis (21.4% vs. 9.0%, p = 0.01) and vastus intermedius (19.0% vs. 8.0%, p = 0.02) of the DM1 cohort was significantly higher compared to the CMT1A cohort (Table S1).

The distal lower limb muscles showed a high disease burden in both conditions. In DM1, 59 muscles (18%, from 23 patients) were atrophic, 19 (6%, from 11 patients) hypertrophic, and 42 (13%, from 20 patients) asymmetric. Similarly, in CMT1A, 62 muscles (19%, from 20 patients) were atrophic, 21 (6%, from 11 patients) hypertrophic, and 37 (11%, from 19 patients) asymmetric (Table 1). Compared with controls, 5 of 10 distal lower limb muscles showed significant atrophy in both DM1 and CMT1A (Figure 1). In CMT1A, the highest prevalence of atrophy was observed in the tibialis anterior (36%), tibialis posterior (27%), and extensor digitorum longus (24%). In contrast, DM1 showed the greatest atrophy in the gastrocnemius medialis (49%), flexor hallucis longus (46%), and soleus (33%) (Figure 1). Direct comparisons between patient groups revealed disease‐specific patterns in which the gastrocnemius medialis was more frequently atrophic in DM1 than in CMT1A (49% vs. 9%, p < 0.01) and the tibialis posterior was more frequently affected in CMT1A (27% vs. 0%, p < 0.01). Regarding asymmetry, CMT1A patients exhibited significantly higher rates in the gastrocnemius medialis (30% vs. 0%, p = 0.008) and peroneus longus (24% vs. 0%, p = 0.02) compared with controls (Figure 2). In contrast, the DM1 group showed no significant differences compared with either CMT1A or controls (Figure 2). In the proximal lower limb muscles, atrophy, hypertrophy, and asymmetry were infrequent in both patient groups. In DM1, 13 muscles (2%, from 8 patients) were atrophic, 13 (2%, from 6 patients) hypertrophic, and 42 (7%, from 15 patients) asymmetric (Table 1). In CMT1A, 18 muscles (3%, from 5 patients) were atrophic, 23 (4%, from 10 patients) hypertrophic, and 12 (2%, from 8 patients) asymmetric. No significant differences were observed between groups or relative to controls for these measures, except for the tensor fasciae latae, which showed significantly greater hypertrophy in CMT1A compared with DM1 (27% vs. 0%, p = 0.03) (Figures 1 and 2).

Disto‐Proximal Profiles of Muscle Fat Replacement

In distal lower limb muscles, CMT1A exhibited significantly steeper disto‐proximal gradients compared to healthy controls in 9 of 10 distal lower limb muscles (Table 2). In DM1, distal gradients were only significantly increased compared with controls in 4 of 10 distal lower limb muscles (Table 2). Comparisons between patient groups demonstrated significantly higher distal gradients in 4 of the 10 distal lower limb muscles in CMT1A compared to DM1, mainly in the anterolateral compartment (TA: 14.9% vs. 8.6%, p = 0.04; PER: 35.4% vs. 6.6%, p < 0.001; EDL: 20.9% vs. 3.2%, p < 0.001; FDL: 13.5% vs. 7.8%, p < 0.001). In the proximal lower limb muscles, CMT1A showed significantly higher disto‐proximal gradients than controls in 8 of 18 muscles (Table 2). Conversely, some proximal lower limb muscles in CMT1A showed significantly lower gradients compared with controls, indicating relatively greater proximal than distal fat replacement in 3 of the 18 muscles (Table 2). In DM1, significantly higher proximal gradients compared with controls were observed in 10 of 18 muscles (Table 2). Between‐group comparisons further showed that DM1 exhibited significantly higher disto‐proximal gradients than CMT1A in 4 proximal lower limb muscles (VI: 36.0% vs. 5.9%, p < 0.001; VM: 10.7% vs. 2.2%, p = 0.03; AB: 2.9% vs. −2.1%, p = 0.01; Gmin: 1.2% vs. −15.3%, p < 0.001).

Direct comparison between patient groups revealed a consistent directional pattern, with CMT1A exhibiting higher entropy and contrast and lower homogeneity than DM1 (Figure 4, Tables S2–, S4). In distal lower limb muscles, both entropy and contrast were significantly higher in CMT1A than in DM1, particularly in the gastrocnemius medialis (entropy: 9.7 vs. 8.8, p = 0.03; contrast: 107.8 vs. 68.2, p < 0.05) and tibialis posterior (entropy: 8.3 vs. 7.6, p < 0.01; contrast: 71.0 vs. 43.0, p < 0.01). Homogeneity was significantly lower in the extensor digitorum longus (0.989 vs. 0.991, p = 0.04), gastrocnemius medialis (0.988 vs. 0.991, p = 0.04), tibialis posterior (0.992 vs. 0.995, p < 0.001), and popliteus (0.991 vs. 0.993, p < 0.01).

A similar pattern was observed in proximal lower limb muscles (Figure 4, Tables S2–, S4). Entropy was significantly higher in CMT1A in the gracilis (7.8 vs. 7.5, p < 0.05), biceps femoris longus (8.0 vs. 7.6, p < 0.01), semitendinosus (8.3 vs. 7.6, p < 0.001), semimembranosus (8.7 vs. 8.1, p = 0.02), tensor fasciae latae (9.7 vs. 9.3, p = 0.04), gluteus maximus (9.3 vs. 8.9, p = 0.03), and gluteus minimus (8.8 vs. 8.1, p = 0.01). Additionally, contrast was higher in the biceps femoris longus (37.4 vs. 26.6, p = 0.03), semitendinosus (52.1 vs. 36.1, p = 0.02), and gluteus minimus (56.5 vs. 39.5, p < 0.05). Consistently, homogeneity was significantly lower in CMT1A in the sartorius (p < 0.001), biceps femoris brevis (p = 0.02), semitendinosus (p < 0.001), gluteus maximus (p = 0.02), and gluteus minimus (p < 0.001). No significant correlations were observed between CTG repeat length and any imaging‐derived parameter in the DM1 cohort, but longer disease duration correlated with higher mean PDFF (%) and with greater textural disruption (higher entropy and contrast, lower homogeneity) in multiple muscles. In CMT1A, correlations were directionally consistent but did not reach significance after correction for multiple comparisons (Table S5).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the integration of micro‐spatial qMRI features may significantly enhance the objective differential diagnosis of DM1 and CMT1A as proof of concept and warrants further investigations in other myogenic and neurogenic diseases. Although both DM1 and CMT1A showed higher PDFF (%) compared with matched controls, radiomic texture analysis revealed distinct micro‐spatial alterations in the two conditions. These findings indicate that the spatial distribution of fat replacement might provide diagnostic information. Furthermore, these micro‐spatial patterns were complemented by disease‐specific signatures in muscle involvement, asymmetry, and disto‐proximal gradients.

Radiomic Texture Analysis Captures Micro‐Spatial Differences Between Adult‐Onset DM1 and CMT1A

The micro‐spatial distribution of fat replacement in neurogenic conditions shows a reticular pattern that may progress to muscle islands, whereas myopathies demonstrate a more diffuse appearance [7, 8, 10]. Studies describing these patterns have relied on qualitative visual evaluation on T1‐weighted images. Esteller et al. reported a reticular pattern in neurogenic cohorts [10], and Payá et al. confirmed that this pattern strongly characterizes neurogenic disease in contrast to the more homogeneous appearance observed in myopathies [7]. Radiomic texture analysis offers a quantitative approach to capture these visual differences [14, 17]. Only one previous study applied texture analysis in CMT1A, demonstrating increased textural heterogeneity compared to healthy controls using T1‐weighted MRI [18]. However, radiomics features derived from conventional T1‐weighted or STIR imaging are inherently influenced by scanner‐ and protocol‐dependent intensity variations. Burian et al. [21] and Dieckmeyer et al. (2021) extended texture analysis to PDFF (%) images in healthy controls, validating PDFF‐based radiomics and showing that texture features reflect structural information beyond mean fat fraction [15, 21]. By performing radiomics on quantitative PDFF (%) maps, our approach provides a methodologically sound framework for quantitative texture analysis. Recent studies have highlighted the growing utility of machine learning and radiomics in evaluating NMDs. Nagawa et al. (2021) extracted radiomic features using machine learning from STIR images; their study successfully predicted disease subgroups and autoantibody status in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, offering a valuable, non‐invasive alternative to muscle biopsies [19]. Similarly, Wei et al. (2023) demonstrated that machine learning‐based radiomics could differentiate limb‐girdle muscular dystrophy R2 (LGMDR2) from immune‐mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) with significantly higher accuracy than standard visual MRI evaluations [20]. To our knowledge, no prior study has applied 3D PDFF‐based texture analysis to differentiate DM1 and CMT1A. In our study, both cohorts demonstrated increased entropy and contrast and reduced homogeneity compared with controls, reflecting disruption of normal muscle architecture. Notably, CMT1A exhibited significantly greater textural heterogeneity than DM1. This high‐entropy, high‐contrast profile is consistent with the reticular fat replacement pattern characteristic of neurogenic disease, whereas DM1 showed a more homogeneous texture, aligning with the diffuse myopathic infiltration. These findings are a proof of concept that PDFF‐based 3D radiomic texture analysis might objectify qualitative imaging patterns and capture complementary micro‐spatial information beyond conventional mean fat fraction assessment, supporting its potential role in objective disease differentiation.

Disto‐Proximal Gradients Quantify Length‐Dependent Pathology

The disto‐proximal fat gradients provided quantitative evidence of the length‐dependent pathology. CMT1A exhibited significantly steeper distal gradients across nearly all lower limb muscles compared to both controls and DM1. This is consistent with the established length‐dependent pathophysiology of CMT1A. Conversely, DM1 demonstrated higher disto‐proximal gradients in several proximal thigh muscles compared to CMT1A, reflecting a distinct, disease‐specific trajectory of fat infiltration that does not strictly adhere to the neurogenic length‐dependent model [29, 30]. However, it should be noted that both diseases studied have a distal‐predominant involvement, and CMT1A in particular is length‐dependent. This interpretation should therefore be limited to distal and length‐dependent disease models and not extrapolated to the full spectrum of neurogenic and myogenic disorders.

Disease‐Specific Signatures: Compartmental Involvement, Atrophy, and the Role of Asymmetry

Consistent with prior reports, DM1 predominantly affected the posterior distal compartment, whereas CMT1A mainly involved the anterolateral compartment, with relative sparing of proximal lower limb muscles in both groups [29, 31, 32, 33]. Importantly, these distributions reflect disease‐specific rather than inherently myogenic or neurogenic patterns. Other neurogenic conditions may preferentially involve the posterior compartment, and certain myopathies may show predominant anterolateral involvement [7, 10, 34]. This underscores the importance of integrating multiple qMRI features. Beyond the specific muscles affected, muscle volume and asymmetry analysis revealed further discriminatory features. Both patient groups exhibited significant distal atrophy, yet the specific muscles affected closely mirrored their respective fat replacement patterns, with profound atrophy in the anterolateral compartment for CMT1A and the posterior compartment for DM1. Additionally, our quantitative approach detected significant asymmetry in the gastrocnemius medialis and peroneus longus of the CMT1A cohort, whereas DM1 showed no significant difference in the individual muscles compared to controls. These findings suggest that evaluating the involvement of individual muscles is more informative for the differential diagnosis, as global measures can mask disease‐specific involvement. Payá et al. (2026) reported that asymmetric muscle involvement and hypertrophy were more common in distal myopathies than in hereditary neuropathies [7]. These differences reflect the specific disease models used in our study. While myopathies as a broader category, such as facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD), can present with asymmetry [13, 35], DM1 typically manifests with a relatively symmetric fat replacement [32]. Because macroscopic measures like asymmetry and atrophy can overlap between myogenic and neurogenic diseases, they are insufficient for the differentiation of neurogenic and myogenic diseases.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. DM1 and CMT1A are well‐characterized, genetically confirmed distal myogenic and neurogenic disorders, providing a robust framework for systematic MRI comparison. The use of the automated 3D segmentation pipeline enabled analysis of 28 individual muscles, and the inclusion of matched healthy controls provided reliable reference values.

However, the limitations of our study should be considered. First, DM1 and CMT1A were investigated as proof‐of‐concept models, and generalizability to other myogenic and neurogenic conditions remains to be established. Moreover, DM1 is more heterogeneous than CMT1A, and the muscle MRI phenotype in DM1 can vary accordingly [32]. Inclusion was therefore restricted to genetically confirmed adult‐onset DM1. Second, the modest sample size may have limited statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses, and larger cohorts are needed to confirm robustness. Third, in DM1, disease duration correlated with PDFF (%) and texture features. As the texture comparisons were adjusted for PDFF (%) and disease duration did not differ between cohorts, this does not account for the between‐group differences, but longitudinal studies are needed to confirm progression. Fourth, we utilized a hypothesis‐driven selection of three a priori GLCM features specifically chosen to mathematically quantify the reticular and diffuse visual patterns. Future studies with larger cohorts should evaluate additional feature classes, such as run‐length or size‐zone matrices. Finally, the texture features in patients with low or overlapping PDFF (%) values were not evaluated. Texture differences were nonetheless detected between patients and healthy controls, indicating a discriminative signal at low fat fractions. Dedicated analyses in early‐disease cohorts are required to confirm this in patients.

Conclusion

This proof‐of‐concept study demonstrates that quantitative muscle MRI captures complementary imaging features that may aid in distinguishing CMT1A from adult‐onset DM1. By quantifying the micro‐spatial patterns, 3D texture analysis might offer diagnostic information that extends beyond the traditional qualitative assessment. These micro‐spatial markers are complemented by macroscopic features, including disease‐specific patterns of muscle atrophy, asymmetry, and disto‐proximal gradients. Future studies should determine whether integrating radiomic and conventional qMRI features improves the non‐invasive differentiation of a wider range of neurogenic and myogenic disorders and evaluate their potential use in automated diagnostic pipelines.
