 ABSTRACT

To meet the materials performance demands of next-generation nuclear and high-temperature energy
systems, a new class of intermetallic-dispersive steels (IDS) has been designed through integrated
computational thermodynamics and alloy design strategies. The IDS alloys incorporate coherent L1₂ (γ′-
Ni₃Al) nanoprecipitates within an Fe–Ni–Cr austenitic matrix, engineered to achieve both high-
temperature strength and thermodynamic stability while suppressing the formation of detrimental δ-Ni₃Nb
and η-Ni₃Ti phases. Initial creep testing of Ti-IDS and TiTa-IDS alloys demonstrates rupture lives
comparable to or exceeding those of Inconel 718 and ODS steels, despite being fabricated through
conventional ingot metallurgy. Step-load creep tests identified a stress threshold near 300–350 MPa for
the onset of tertiary creep, and in-situ neutron diffraction experiments on Ti-IDS revealed clear load
partitioning between the matrix and γ′ precipitates: elastic strain is shared by both phases, while plastic
strain localizes in the matrix. These results confirm that stable γ′–matrix interfaces play a dominant role in
retarding dislocation motion and enhancing creep resistance.

In FY26, the program will conduct repeat creep rupture testing of TiTa-IDS at 650 °C/400 MPa, initiate
long-term rupture testing of TiNb-IDS, and perform TEM-based microstructural characterization to
elucidate dislocation–precipitate interactions and microstructural stability. Collectively, these efforts will
establish the mechanistic foundation for next-generation high-temperature structural materials based on
the IDS concept.

 1. Introduction

 All Generation IV nuclear reactor concepts are designed to operate at significantly higher
temperatures than current light-water reactors. This requires structural materials that can maintain
mechanical strength and resist irradiation-induced degradation under such conditions [1]. Ni-based
superalloys such as Inconel 718, 706, and PE16 have been proposed as cladding and duct materials for Gas-
Cooled Fast Reactors (GFR), Molten Salt Reactors (MSR), Supercritical Water Reactors (SCWR), and
Very High-Temperature Reactors (VHTR) owing to their superior high-temperature strength, corrosion
resistance, and creep performance compared with austenitic and ferritic-martensitic steels [2].

 The irradiation response of these alloys has been extensively studied under the U.S. Cladding and
Duct Program through fast-reactor experiments at 450–700 °C and 10–40 dpa [3]. Significant radiation-
induced ductility loss was observed in Inconel 718, 706, and PE16. In 718 and 706, post-irradiation
examinations revealed that embrittlement was primarily associated with the radiation-accelerated
precipitation of brittle intermetallic phases—δ-Ni₃Nb in 718 and η-Ni₃Ti in 706—along grain boundaries.
These thermodynamically stable phases form slowly under thermal conditions but are promoted under
irradiation due to enhanced defect-mediated diffusion. Their formation consumes the metastable γ″
strengthening phase, depletes the surrounding matrix of Nb or Ti, and introduces brittle interfaces that serve
as crack-initiation sites.

 Thermodynamic calculations, shown in Fig. 1(718) and (706), confirm the stability of δ and η
phases in their respective alloys. By contrast, PE16 does not form such detrimental intermetallics, though
it still experiences irradiation-induced embrittlement associated with helium bubble growth. This
mechanism is consistent with the more pronounced void swelling observed in PE16 relative to 718 and 706.
We hypothesize that the higher combined volume fraction of metastable γ′ + γ″ precipitates in 718 and 706
helps moderate helium bubble coarsening. The coherent γ″ phase, having a small lattice mismatch with the
fcc matrix and low interfacial energy, along with the slower diffusivity of Nb compared with Ti, favors the
formation of fine-scale precipitates that can trap helium atoms and delay grain-boundary bubble growth.

Fig. 1 Phase fraction vs temperature in 718, 706, PE16 and the proposed new IDS alloy strengthened by
L12 nanoprecipitate

 Therefore, to design advanced alloys that exhibit both excellent high-temperature mechanical
performance and irradiation tolerance at lower temperatures, two key design criteria must be incorporated:
(1) careful control of alloy chemistry to ensure that detrimental phases such as the δ-Ni₃Nb and η-Ni₃Ti
phases are not thermodynamically stable, and (2) promotion of a high volume fraction of fine, dispersed
particles to effectively manage helium. Beyond these two criteria, it is also desirable to minimize the Ni
content because Ni produces long-lived transmutation products under neutron irradiation. Building upon
our previous LDRD project [4~6], and through the application of computational thermodynamics and
integrated alloy-design strategies, we have developed a new family of L1₂ (γ′-Ni₃Al) intermetallic-
dispersive steels (IDS, in contrast to ODS) that satisfy these criteria and enable direct testing of the
proposed hypothesis [4].

 The selection of γ′-Ni₃Al as the dispersive phase is based on our finding that it has a strong
thermodynamic and kinetic tendency to form a high volume fraction of nanoscale precipitates in Fe–Ni-
based fcc matrices. Thermodynamic calculations for a representative IDS composition (Fig. 1d) show that
γ′-Ni₃Al is the only thermodynamically stable precipitate phase in this alloy—no detrimental δ-Ni₃Nb or η-
Ni₃Ti phases are predicted to form. The small interfacial energy between γ′-Ni₃Al and the matrix, combined
with Ta additions that retard diffusion, promotes the formation of fine, stable precipitates. The designed
mole fraction of γ′-Ni₃Al nanoparticles is comparable to or greater than the combined γ′ + γ″ fraction in
Inconel 718 and 706, providing excellent swelling resistance through a high number density of nanoscale
dispersions. An SEM image of the new alloy (Fig. 2) reveals precisely such a dense distribution of nanoscale
γ′-Ni₃Al precipitates.

Fig. 2 SEM microstructure and APT on a typical L12 precipitate strengthened IDS alloy showing a high
number density of uniformly distributed nanoscale ’-Ni3Al type precipitates in Fe-based FCC matrix,
meeting our design criteria.

 In addition to the optimized precipitate structure, the bulk Ni content of the IDS alloy is
substantially lower than that in 718, 706, or PE16. This reduction not only decreases helium production
from transmutation but also mitigates helium-induced embrittlement and swelling. Atom probe tomography
(APT) measurements across the precipitate–matrix interface (Fig. 2) indicate that the matrix composition
is approximately Fe-19Cr-19Ni—close to that of austenitic steels such as 316 (Fe-16–18Cr-10–14Ni).
Thus, the alloy can be viewed as a high-density dispersion of γ′-Ni₃Al nanoparticles within an austenitic
steel matrix. At temperatures below 550 °C, thermodynamic calculations predict the formation of an Fe-
rich bcc phase, consistent with the behavior of 316 steel. However, such a phase has rarely been observed
experimentally in 316 before or after irradiation, because the fcc-to-bcc transition requires long-range
diffusion and is therefore kinetically much slower than local precipitation reactions.

 Beyond its microstructural advantages, this IDS alloy family also offers significant manufacturing
benefits. The fine precipitates form naturally through the intrinsic thermodynamics and kinetics of the alloy
system, eliminating the need for external mechanical alloying or other particle-embedding processes. The
example alloy shown in Fig. 2 was fabricated by conventional ingot metallurgy followed by a two-step
solutionizing and aging heat treatment. This inherent process simplicity enables straightforward scale-up
and cost-effective production, in sharp contrast to the powder-metallurgy routes typically required for ODS
steels.

 Although the thermodynamic design and microstructural characterization confirm that the IDS
alloys achieve the intended phase stability and precipitation behavior, their practical applicability ultimately
depends on their high-temperature mechanical performance. Among all deformation modes, creep is the
most critical factor governing the long-term reliability of structural materials in advanced energy systems.
The sustained exposure to high temperatures and mechanical stresses can lead to time-dependent
deformation, precipitate coarsening, grain-boundary sliding, and cavity formation—all of which dictate the
alloy’s usable stress–temperature window. Therefore, systematic creep testing of the IDS alloys is essential
to evaluate their microstructural stability, strengthening mechanisms, and deformation kinetics under
service-relevant conditions. These studies will establish whether the finely dispersed γ′-Ni₃Al precipitates

can effectively stabilize dislocation structures and resist grain-boundary weakening over extended
timescales. The resulting creep life and rupture data will provide critical benchmarks for comparing IDS
alloys with ODS alloys, conventional Ni-based superalloys and ferritic–martensitic steels, thereby
validating their potential as high-temperature structural materials for next-generation reactor and power-
conversion applications.

 Next, we will report the creep test results of three IDS alloys: Ti, TiNb and TiTa IDS alloys. We
performed three types of creep test: 1) Creep rupture test for Ti and Ta IDS alloys at 800C/200Mpa,
700C/250Mpa and 650C/Mpa, respectively. (The test for TiNb IDS alloys will be performed in FY26 due
to the relocation of mechanical Lab); 2) Step-load creep test for Ti, TiNb and TiTa IDS alloys at 700C; 3)
In-situ creep test under neutron diffraction for the Ti IDS alloy at 700C/250Mpa, 700C/300Mpa and
700C/350Mpa, respectively.

 2. Experimental procedures

2.1 Creep-rupture tests

Dog-bone creep specimens with a gauge section of 2.03 mm × 2.03 mm × 12.7 were machined from the
developed IDS alloys. Creep-rupture test procedure followed ASTM E139. Creep tests were performed at
800 °C/200 MPa, 700 °C/250 MPa, and 650 °C/400 MPa for each of the IDS alloys, respectively.
Temperature was controlled with type K thermocouples, and one thermocouple was attached to the
specimen gauge section. Axial strain was measured using a high-temperature dual-LVDT system. Rupture
data were generated at 800 °C and 650 °C, while creep tests at 700 °C were interrupted at ~1900 h due to
scheduled laboratory maintenance. The interrupted tests are planned to be resumed and continued until
rupture.

2.2 Step-load creep tests

Step-load creep tests were performed using the same dog-bone specimen geometry on a MTS servo-
hydraulic mechanical testing system under load-controlled mode. Incremental stress loading was applied
via LabVIEW control software. Tests were conducted at 700 °C, and the temperature monitored and
controlled using type S thermocouples and a temperature controller. Axial strain was recorded using a high-
temperature extensometer attached to the gauge region. Each specimen was loaded sequentially in 50 MPa
increments, starting at 100 MPa and continuing through 450 MPa or till rupture. At each stress level, the
test was held for a minimum of 48 hours and maintained until a steady‐state creep deformation was
achieved. Creep strain was continuously monitored throughout the test until specimen fracture.

2.3 Creep tests under neutron diffraction

The in situ neutron creep test on Ti-IDS was conducted under constant temperature of 700 °C and three
different stress levels which are 250, 300, and 350 MPa. The sample was held at constant temperature and
stress for 22 hours. Different specimens were used for each stress level. The three different stress levels
were chosen based on the result of the step-load creep test to observe different creep mechanisms. The
macroscopic strain was measured using a high-temperature extensometer. The gauge dimension of the
specimen was 2 × 2 × 12.7 mm. The neutron beam size was 8 × 2 mm, with 5mm collimator. The heating
rate during ramping up from room temperature to 700 °C was 40 °C/min. The neutron data was collected
continuously with the following scheme: 5 minutes of data collection at room temperature in unloaded
condition, 17 minutes of temperature ramp up, 15 minutes of data collection at 700 °C in unloaded
condition, 22 hours of data collection during creep, 30 minutes of data collection during unloading.

 3. Experimental results

3.1 Creep-rupture tests

Initial creep tests of the newly developed IDS alloys demonstrate promising high-temperature strength
and stability. As shown in Fig. 2, both Ti-IDS and TiTa-IDS alloys exhibit well-defined primary and
secondary creep regimes followed by accelerated tertiary deformation near rupture. At 800 °C and 200
MPa, Ti-IDS shows a rupture life of approximately 53 h, while TiTa-IDS exhibits a comparable rupture
life of ~48 h, indicating consistent creep resistance among the two alloy chemistries at this elevated
temperature. When tested at 650 °C and 400 MPa, Ti-IDS sustains loading for ~1530 h before rupture,
reflecting good creep resistance at intermediate temperatures. In contrast, the TiTa-IDS specimen
experienced premature failure at ~298 h, likely due to localized defects or processing variability. A repeat
experiment is planned for FY26 to confirm the intrinsic creep behavior of the TiTa-modified alloy at this
condition. At 700 °C and 250 MPa, both alloys have reached ~1900 h in interrupted tests without failure,
demonstrating excellent long-term creep stability under moderate stress.

Fig. 3 Creep curves of Ti-IDS and TiTa-IDS alloys under different test conditions. Both alloys show
comparable rupture lives at 800 °C/200 MPa, while Ti-IDS exhibits superior performance at 650 °C/400
MPa. The TiTa-IDS sample at 650 °C failed prematurely. Interrupted tests at 700 °C/250 MPa reached
~1900 h without rupture, indicating excellent creep stability.

Figure 4 compares the creep-rupture results of Ti-IDS and TiTa-IDS alloys with literature data for
conventional ferritic–martensitic steels (HT9, D9[8]) and Ni-based superalloys (Inconel 718, 625[9,10])
as well as representative ODS alloys[7]. When plotted against the Larson–Miller parameter (LMP = T[log
t + 20]) and the temperature-compensated time parameter T(log t + 25), the IDS alloys exhibit rupture
strengths significantly higher than HT9 and D9 across all temperature regimes. The Ti-IDS alloy in
particular demonstrates stress–rupture performance comparable to Inconel 718 and approaching that of
advanced ODS steels, despite being fabricated through conventional ingot metallurgy. The TiTa-IDS
alloy follows a similar trend, although the data point at 650 °C/400 MPa corresponds to a premature
failure; a repeat test is planned to confirm intrinsic behavior. These results highlight the excellent high-

temperature strength and creep resistance of the IDS alloy family, validating the design concept of
achieving superior performance through γ′-Ni₃Al nanoprecipitate strengthening within an Fe-based
matrix.

Fig. 4 The creep-rupture results of Ti-IDS and TiTa-IDS alloys compared with literature data for
conventional ferritic–martensitic steels (HT9, D9[9]) and Ni-based superalloys (Inconel 718, 625[10,11])
as well as representative ODS alloys[8].

3.2 Step load creep tests

Step-load creep tests performed at 700 °C provide valuable insight into the stress-dependent deformation
characteristics of the IDS-Ti, IDS-TiTa, and IDS-TiNb alloys. As shown in the total strain curves (Fig. 5
top row), all three alloys display minimal strain accumulation at lower stresses (≤250 MPa), indicating
strong resistance to time-dependent deformation within the primary and steady-state creep regimes. A
distinct transition to accelerated strain occurs beyond approximately 300–350 MPa for IDS-Ti and IDS-
TiTa, while IDS-TiNb maintains lower total strain up to 400 MPa, suggesting enhanced creep resistance.
The corresponding minimum creep rate (MCR) plots (Fig. 5 bottom row) exhibit a sharp stress
dependence beyond the same threshold, with IDS-Ti showing the steepest increase in MCR above 300
MPa, followed by IDS-TiTa. In contrast, IDS-TiNb demonstrates a more gradual rise in creep rate,
consistent with its superior strength observed in the step-load tests. These results indicate that the TiNb-
modified IDS alloy possesses the highest resistance to dislocation creep at 700 °C, likely due to the
combined effects of solid-solution strengthening from Nb and stabilization of the fine γ′-Ni₃Al precipitate
distribution. Overall, the step-load data confirm that the IDS alloy family exhibits excellent creep strength
retention up to high stress levels, with composition-dependent variations in the threshold for the onset of
tertiary creep.

Fig. 5 Step-load creep test results for IDS-Ti, IDS-TiTa, and IDS-TiNb alloys at 700°C under stress levels
from 100 to 450Mpa at a step size of 50Mpa. Top row: total strain vs time; Bottom row: minimum creep
rate vs applied stress.

Figure 6 compares the minimum creep rates of the IDS-Ti, IDS-TiTa, and IDS-TiNb alloys with
representative oxide dispersion–strengthened (ODS) ferritic alloys, including J05-ODS and OFRAC,
tested at 700 °C. All three IDS alloys exhibit remarkably low minimum creep rates, comparable to or
lower than those of the advanced ODS steels, despite being produced through conventional ingot
metallurgy rather than powder-based dispersion methods. The IDS alloys maintain creep rates in the
range of 10⁻⁸–10⁻⁹ s⁻¹ up to applied stresses exceeding 300 MPa, indicating excellent creep resistance and
stable microstructural strengthening. The TiNb-IDS alloy shows the lowest overall creep rate among the
three variants, followed closely by TiTa-IDS and Ti-IDS, consistent with their relative performance in the
step-load tests. By contrast, the ODS alloys, particularly OFRAC, display higher creep rates at
comparable stress levels. This comparison demonstrates that the γ′-Ni₃Al nanoprecipitates in IDS alloys
can provide creep resistance equivalent to or surpassing that of traditional ODS steels, while offering the
added advantages of simpler fabrication and better scalability.

Fig. 6 The minimum creep rate of IDS alloys compared to representative ODS ferritic alloys, including
J05-ODS [12] and OFRAC[13], tested at 700 °C.

3.3 In-situ neutron diffraction test of Ti IDS alloy

Because of limited beam time, in-situ neutron diffraction creep experiments were conducted only on the
Ti-IDS alloy to directly probe lattice strain evolution during deformation. Figure 7 shows the macroscopic
creep curves collected at 700 °C under applied stresses of 250, 300, and 350 MPa. The specimens tested
at 250 MPa and 300 MPa exhibited slow, nearly linear strain accumulation for over 22 hours,
characteristic of steady-state creep dominated by dislocation climb within a stable precipitate–matrix
framework. In contrast, the 350 MPa sample failed after ~15 hours, accompanied by a rapid acceleration
in strain rate after an initial incubation period. The systematic increase in strain rate with applied stress
indicates a clear transition from steady-state to tertiary creep beyond ~325 MPa, consistent with the stress
threshold observed in step-load tests. These data establish a direct experimental link between applied
stress and time-dependent plasticity in the Ti-IDS alloy under service-relevant conditions.

Fig. 7 Creep curves of Ti IDS alloy collected at 700 °C under applied stresses of 250, 300, and 350 MPa

Figure 8 presents the phase-resolved strain evolution of the L1₂ (γ′-Ni₃Al) precipitate and the fcc matrix
obtained from Rietveld refinement of the in-situ neutron diffraction data at 250, 300, and 350 MPa. At
250 MPa, both phases exhibit negligible lattice strain, confirming elastic compatibility and uniform load
sharing between precipitate and matrix during early creep. At 300 MPa, the fcc matrix shows increasing
tensile lattice strain while the L1₂ precipitate remains largely elastic, suggesting the onset of differential
load partitioning and internal stress accumulation. The strain mismatch becomes pronounced at 350 MPa,
where the fcc lattice experiences substantial tensile distortion prior to rupture, whereas the L1₂
precipitates remain relatively undeformed.

Fig. 8 The phase-resolved strain evolution of the L1₂ (γ′-Ni₃Al) precipitate and the fcc matrix in Ti IDS
obtained from Rietveld refinement of the in-situ neutron diffraction data at 250, 300, and 350 MPa

The in-situ neutron diffraction results clearly reveal that the elastic strain is shared between the L1₂ (γ′-
Ni₃Al) precipitates and the fcc matrix during the early stages of creep, indicating coherent load transfer
and strong interfacial bonding between the two phases. This elastic compatibility persists through the
steady-state regime at lower stresses (≤300 MPa), reflecting the stability of the precipitate–matrix
architecture. However, at higher stress levels (e.g., 350 MPa), the fcc matrix exhibits a pronounced
increase in lattice strain while the L1₂ precipitates remain largely elastic, signifying the onset of localized
plastic deformation primarily within the matrix. This strain partitioning demonstrates that plastic creep
strain is predominantly accommodated by dislocation glide and climb in the fcc matrix, while the
coherent γ′ precipitates act as stiff, load-bearing obstacles that impede dislocation motion and maintain
structural integrity. The combination of shared elastic strain and matrix-confined plasticity underpins the
superior high-temperature creep resistance of the IDS alloy system, analogous to deformation behavior
observed in advanced Ni-based superalloys.

 4. Summary and Planned FY26 work

In FY25, significant progress was achieved in establishing the high-temperature creep performance and
deformation mechanisms of the newly developed intermetallic-dispersive steel (IDS) alloys. The Ti-IDS
and TiTa-IDS alloys demonstrated excellent creep strength at 700–800 °C, with rupture lives comparable
to or exceeding those of Inconel 718 and ODS steels, despite being fabricated through conventional ingot
metallurgy. Step-load creep tests further confirmed their stable deformation response and identified a

creep threshold near 300–350 MPa, above which accelerated tertiary creep occurs. In-situ neutron
diffraction experiments performed on the Ti-IDS alloy at 700 °C provided direct evidence of phase-
specific load partitioning: the elastic strain was shared between the L1₂ (γ′-Ni₃Al) precipitates and the fcc
matrix, while plastic deformation was primarily accommodated within the matrix. This finding confirms
that the coherent γ′ precipitates act as strong, elastic load-bearing obstacles that retard dislocation motion
and underpin the alloy’s excellent creep resistance.

In FY26, the research will focus on expanding and validating these findings. Specifically, (1) a repeat
creep rupture test of the TiTa-IDS alloy at 650 °C/400 MPa will be conducted to replace the premature
failure observed previously and confirm the intrinsic creep performance; (2) a long-term creep rupture test
for the TiNb-IDS alloy will be initiated to benchmark its performance relative to Ti- and TiTa-IDS; and
(3) TEM characterization will be performed on crept specimens to elucidate the underlying deformation
mechanisms. The TEM study will focus on dislocation–precipitate interactions, interface coherency,
precipitate coarsening behavior, and possible grain-boundary damage. Together, these efforts will provide
a mechanistic foundation for the design optimization of IDS alloys and establish their qualification
potential as next-generation structural materials for advanced high-temperature reactor applications.
