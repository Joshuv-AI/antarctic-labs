 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This project, funded by the US Department of Energy’s Solar Energy Technologies Office
(SETO), focused on the operation of microgrids as a coordinated network. The primary
objective, which was successfully achieved, was to develop both control strategies and
hardware solutions to support the resilient and efficient operation of networked microgrids with
high solar penetration. The work was structured around the following four main tasks:

  Development of distributed and scalable optimization algorithms for AC-coupled
 networked microgrids.

  Design and implementation of a novel DC interconnection hardware to enable precise
 power exchange between microgrids.

  Laboratory operational validation of the developed technologies using 480 V testbeds
 and commercially available hardware.

  Field operational validation of the complete solution in Adjuntas, Puerto Rico,
 interconnecting two kW-scale, split-phase microgrids of Casa Pueblo’s microgrids.

This project addressed multiple technical challenges across the domains of optimization,
control, hardware interconnection, and protection. One of its key contributions was delivering
tangible, real-world solutions for networking microgrids. In contrast to purely theoretical or
simulation-based work, this project included full-scale hardware operational validation both in
the lab and in the field.

The work conducted as part of this project—in collaboration with the University of Puerto Rico;
the University of Tennessee, Knoxville; the University of Central Florida; and Casa Pueblo—has
advanced the state of the art in networked microgrids. Key contributions include the
development of distributed control strategies, practical solutions for real-world implementation
challenges, and the introduction of a novel DC interlink approach for microgrid interconnection.
The project featured both laboratory and field validation using commercial off-the-shelf
components. The field deployment successfully validated that a group of microgrids can operate
in a coordinated manner, enabling precise power flow between systems and mutual support
during extreme events.

This project resulted in 15 journal publications and 15 conference papers; 5 graduate students
and 15 undergraduate students were supported. The codes of distributed optimization and
forecasting were made open-source through OSTI.gov for distributed optimization and
forecasting. All the publications are available in the ORNL-hosted project landing page.

The DC interlink with state-of-charge balancing control was operationally validated in Adjuntas
by interconnecting two real-world, 240 V split-phase microgrids. To the best knowledge of the
team, this represents the first operational validation of AC microgrids interconnected via DC-
interlinks.

As a culmination of this project, a follow-on grant was awarded to support the technology
transfer of the distributed optimization framework to a commercial microgrid controller, Stellar
Edge, developed by the California-based company New Sun Road.

 1. BACKGROUND

Networked microgrids offer a more efficient and resilient alternative to traditional individual
microgrids. While prior research has primarily focused on optimal energy transaction strategies
to achieve economic objectives12, the dynamic reconfiguration resiliency of networked MGs has
received limited attention in optimization and control frameworks. This research project found that
one of the most salient features of networked microgrids is their extended islanding time
compared with stand-alone microgrids, which allows supplying power to all or selected critical
loads for extended durations. Before this project, most existing studies on networked microgrid
controllers (MGCs) were largely theoretical, lacking field implementation and validation, which
significantly limits their practical applicability. This project addressed this gap by advancing the
resilient operation of networked MGs through real-world testing in a community-based
deployment.

The project addressed the key areas required for enabling networked microgrids, delivering both
hardware and software solutions. The work focused on three main technical pillars: (1) power
electronic controls using commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) inverters, ensuring practical and
scalable deployment; (2) microgrid orchestration and distributed optimization and control,
enabling coordinated operation across multiple independent systems; and (3) evaluation and
implementation of interconnection types, including both AC and DC solutions to support flexible
and reliable power sharing. In addition to these core areas, the project also developed machine
learning–based forecasting tools for solar generation and load demand. Furthermore, in
collaboration with project partners, protection algorithms were developed for microgrids, and a
dynamic boundary algorithm was developed to expand microgrids based on their generation
availability. These works are compiled in the project landing page. These approaches were
evaluated at the ORNL GRID-C facility using a multi-kilowatt, 480 V networked microgrid
platform. The team investigated key technical challenges, including interconnection transients
during microgrid coupling and the dynamic behavior of different configurations involving both
grid-forming and grid-following inverters. A particularly novel outcome was the development of a
DC interlink designed for interconnection of microgrids. This solution was successfully validated
in both a three-phase 480 V configuration and a split-phase 208 V implementation.

The proposed microgrid optimization strategy is based on the Alternating Direction Method of
Multipliers (ADMM) algorithm. ADMM is designed to solve convex problems by decomposing
them into smaller, more manageable sub problems. It combines the benefits of dual
decomposition and augmented Lagrangian methods, enabling efficient distributed computation
of large-scale problems. The ADMM approach offers key advantages such as preserving data
privacy, ensuring high scalability, and enabling multi-objective optimization. It can be used not
only to minimize overall system operating costs but also to improve network-level objectives
such as bus voltage balancing, power factor correction, and reduction of power losses. As part
of this project, the optimization problem was mathematically formulated and validated through
control hardware-in-the-loop (CHIL) simulations, which validated the resiliency and cost benefits
of coordinated microgrid operation. Specifically, the ADMM-based optimization was applied to
the Adjuntas microgrids, using real operational data to coordinate their actions and enhance
system resiliency. The effectiveness of the coordinated operation was confirmed by comparing

Through a comprehensive approach that integrates interconnection types, optimization, and

The field operational validation successfully interconnected two operational microgrids in
Adjuntas, Puerto Rico. One microgrid powers the facilities of the nonprofit organization Casa
Pueblo, whereas the other—the Plaza de la Independencia Energética microgrid (hereafter,
Plaza Energética)—serves five homes and a community resiliency hub. Each microgrid
operated with its own independent controller. Using the developed 35 kW DC interlink and the
microgrid orchestrator (MO) control, the two systems were interconnected to enable stable,
bidirectional power sharing. The operational validation of precise control of both active and

generation loss. Although this validation focused on SoC balancing, other use cases are
feasible through the optimal control developed during the project. For example, the system can
be adapted to prioritize power delivery to a specific microgrid with critical loads during
emergencies.

This project was executed from July 1, 2021, to March 31, 2025. The work progressed through
multiple phases, each building toward the goal of validating coordinated operation of networked
microgrids using commercially available technologies.

Year 1: Algorithm Development and Simulation. The initial focus was on developing
foundational algorithms for optimization and forecasting. These were implemented in Python
using open-source solvers. A key novelty was the development of an ADMM-based
decentralized optimization approach for networked microgrids, which has superior scalability
compared with the scalability of centralized strategies. This algorithm was integrated into real-
time simulation models of the Adjuntas microgrid system in a CHIL validation. Multiple HIL
scenarios were tested to validate interoperability under normal, storm, and emergency operating
modes.

Year 2: AC Microgrid Hardware Validation. The second year centered on validating the
networked microgrid architecture through physical hardware experiments. Two AC microgrids
were interconnected via industrial-grade switches. Energy storage inverters were configured in
both grid-forming and grid-following modes, allowing for comprehensive testing under real-world
conditions. These experiments represented one of the first operation validation of networked
microgrids using COTS inverters.

Year 3: DC Interlink Development and Field Deployment. In the third year, the project
developed a novel DC interlink solution consisting of two 35 kW converters in a back-to-back
configuration, which can operate as split-phase or three-phase. This design electrically
decouples the microgrids while enabling precise, bidirectional power flow. Subsequent efforts
focused on field operational validation: the DC interlink was deployed in Adjuntas, Puerto Rico,

Final Phase: Technology Transfer. The project concluded with technology transfer activities,
including the dissemination of results and a technical seminar delivered to the School of

resulted in the award of a technology transfer grant to collaborate with New Sun Road, a
California-based microgrid controller company.

 1.1 PROJECT OBJECTIVES

The primary objective of this US Department of Energy Solar Energy Technologies Office
(SETO) project, as defined in the original proposal, was to develop a suite of technologies to
enable the interconnection and coordination of microgrids. This objective, which has been
successfully achieved, was structured around three key focus areas:

  Technology development for microgrid interconnection using both AC and DC links.

  Hardware validation in ORNL COMMANDER network microgrid testbed at 480V
 commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) converters and protection systems.

  Development of centralized and decentralized optimization algorithms to perform optimal
 power flow across a network of microgrids.

  Implementation of AC and DC interconnection and control strategies based on
 commercially available hardware, ensuring practical applicability for real-world
 deployments.

Development of an MO to coordinate operation of local microgrids—As the primary
objective, the team developed a suite of technologies encompassing hardware, software, and
control systems. Two approaches were studied for interconnect microgrids: AC and DC
interconnect. The central innovation of this project—a DC interlink based on COTS inverters—
was validated in Adjuntas, Puerto Rico. This approach significantly simplifies the deployment of
networked microgrids. In addition to the hardware, the team also developed distributed
optimization algorithms3 capable of interfacing with local microgrid controllers. This latter
innovation led to a follow-up technology transfer grant in partnership with the US-based

and system configurability in a controlled laboratory environment before field deployment. For
this purpose, the research was validated using the AC-networked microgrid testbed at Oak
Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). AC interconnection of microgrids was evaluated in the
testbed, including an evaluation of synchronization and control using grid-forming and grid-

 2. PROJECT RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

As illustrated in Figure 2.1, the operation of network microgrids (NMGs) relies on multiple layers
of hardware and control. At the foundation lies power electronics, where primary-level controls
set microgrid voltage, frequency, and power flow. These controls are categorized as grid-
forming or grid-following, depending on their role within the microgrid. When interconnecting
microgrids, the first decision is how the energy storage inverter must be controlled, which
determines overall control and protection of the system. Above this layer is protection, which
has the role of ensuring that the networked microgrid can quickly detect and isolate electrical
faults while maintaining coordination to prevent the energy storage to trip, leading to a black
start. Importantly, protection schemes must be robust and effective even in the event of
communication loss. The interconnection layer defines how microgrids exchange power—either
through AC links (e.g., 480 V three-phase or 208 V split-phase) or DC interlinks. At the control
layer, basic control sequences and logic controllers establish the basic level of controllability of
the microgrid network. Optimization algorithms and forecasts are implemented to manage

 37771

power flow based on user-defined objectives, such as cost reduction, resilience, or generation
optimization. The highest layer involves ownership models, such as when interconnecting
microgrids are operated by different institutions (e.g., a university, community, or other entity).
When interconnecting these systems, privacy and control should be considered. Coordinating
such diverse systems requires secure, reliable communication infrastructure and cybersecurity
requirements.

This project addressed several key areas critical to the coordination of networked microgrids. To
study these aspects in depth, the team leveraged the ORNL networked microgrid testbed—a

right side of Figure 2.1. This testbed enabled detailed investigations into primary-level power
electronics control, synchronization, and communication requirements. It also provided a
controlled environment to validate the DC interlink before field deployment.

2.1 NETWORKED MICROGRID TESTBED

To address research questions related to the operation and coordination of networked
microgrids, use of a testbed capable of evaluating control strategies and system configurability
in a controlled laboratory environment before field deployment was essential. This section
presents the development and experimental results of ORNL’s AC networked microgrid testbed,

microgrids—and the state of the art—by serving as a platform for testing advanced control
strategies. Key components of the testbed include a scenario manager, local microgrid
controllers, and a networked microgrid controller. The scenario manager is responsible for
emulating real-world operating conditions. Each local controller manages the microgrid’s

With ac interlinks, determining the mix between grid-forming (GFM) and grid-following (GF)
controls and the settings is based on important factors such as reliability, flexibility of operation,
scalability, and design of the energy management systems (Figure 3.3). Operating individually
or in separate microgrids, each microgrid must have at least one GFM asset. In a consolidated
form as a network, at least one microgrid must have a GFM asset, whereas other assets can
operate in either GF or GFM (depending on the type of GFM). GF inverters are equipped with
Phase-looped Loop (PLL) that perform the synchronization to a valid ac source.

For many real-world microgrids, the GFM asset is represented by the energy storage systems
(ESS). The reliability of the network can be increased when choosing to operate all GFM-
capable assets in GFM. This facilitates power sharing through droop control. Droop control is
used in power systems to manage the sharing of load among multiple generators, or in
microgrids with multiple inverters which requires only local measurements of voltage, current,

 37771

and frequency to determine real and reactive power output. However, managing multiple
microgrids with GFM inverters also brings several challenges. Standalone island microgrid
operations to networked microgrids (by closing the interconnecting switch) can introduce system
oscillations and result in tripping of GFM assets. Voltage and frequency regulation are heavily
dependent on the accurate tuning of droop gains and virtual impedance. Large load transients
can also introduce significant voltage and frequency fluctuations, which might activate protection
mechanisms. The interlink protective device should be configured with maximum current
thresholds to safeguard against potential wire damage.

Critically, networking AC interlinks may require extensive data sharing and control access

should have control access to some local microgrid assets, typically the ESS to control power

GFM to GF. This is particularly challenging when dealing with multivendor ESS systems,
proprietary technology, or microgrid controllers from different vendors. For a real-world
application, implementing an MO controller would require collaboration between local microgrid
controllers to allow a higher-level controllability of their assets. Liability may become a hurdle
when integrating multiple controllers in a multiowner system. Although research in distributed
optimization may ease some of these difficulties, this application may be challenging in real
deployments because it requires co-optimization with local microgrid controllers, which may not
be supported by some microgrid controller vendors. Currently, no microgrid controllers apply
distributed optimization to allow interoperable microgrid systems.

These technical and ownership-related complexities can make AC coupled microgrids a
particularly challenging option for some networked microgrid deployment.

3.2 INTERLINKS

DC interlinks ease many of the technical and operational challenges encountered when
interconnecting AC microgrids (Figure 3.4). DC interlinks decouple through power electronics
the dynamics of independent microgrids; each microgrid can operate their ESS in voltage-
frequency (VF) control, ensuring stiff regulation of these magnitudes. The synchronization
transients are minimized because turning on/off the power electronics is smooth. The transition
process is notably straightforward. DC interlinks may allow for a straightforward solution to
make microgrids in a network interoperable. For the DC-interlink validated in the lab and in the
field, its peak efficiency is 97% per converter, or 94% total. This factor not only simplifies the
control strategy but also maintains data privacy because no changes are made to the local
controllers to accommodate networked operation capabilities. DC interlinks can allow
interoperability among microgrids owned and controlled by different entities. Through DC
interlinks, the MO controls power flow exchange based on an optimization, or logical controller,
which only requires limited information from each microgrid (e.g., SoC). The local microgrid
controllers react to the inter-microgrid power exchange (either surplus or deficit) by controlling
their local assets. With this approach, the controller does not have to interact directly with any of
the local microgrids in the network. This allows for a simpler solution for power exchange in
addition to solving the technical challenges of AC coupling microgrids, including synchronization
transients.

 5. MICROGRID ORCHESTRATOR DISTRIBUTED OPTIMIZATION

The MO general architecture is shown in Figure 5.1. The MO provides the pricing signal at the
point of connection to each of the microgrids in the network. The proposed microgrid
architecture takes advantage of the distributed local microgrid controller (LMC), which performs
local optimization to achieve the user goals for each microgrid. The orchestrator receives
information regarding the power imbalance in the local microgrids to decide the price and the
point of interconnection and the switching actions.

To maintain the advantages of the distributed architecture, this optimization scheme allows the
orchestrator and LMC to optimize separately. This method also allows each LMC to
independently schedule their operation, rather than an MO making this decision on their behalf.
During networked operation, the LMC has full control of the DERs within its own boundaries.
Here, each LMC will set the local objectives and issue setpoints to the local DERs. The MO only
has control over the controllable switch that networks the microgrids.

 PRICING ALGORITHM

For the microgrids’ islanded mode, the price of electricity is crucial for operation as a network of
multiple microgrids. This research proposes a decentralized approach where the LMCs are still
in control of the local microgrid assets and the MO has the task of determining the price of
electricity. This approach is robust; it is easily scalable to a larger number of microgrids. Another
advantage of this method is that the MO does not need to have information about the microgrid
assets and the microgrid topology. It only requires the power balance to calculate the price of
electricity. In comparison, a centralized approach would require full information of the microgrid
topology to determine the global optimal power flow for the network of microgrids. The

 37771

centralized approach introduces multiple practical challenges, including an extensive
communication network with all the DERs in the network of microgrids, information about the
microgrid topologies and any updates. Furthermore, the centralized approach is not scalable
because the optimization would become too complex owing to the number of microgrids added
in the network increase. For these reasons, this project uses a distributed approach for the MO
architecture to reduce the complexity of implementation and increase the scalability for
multimicrogrid systems.

The pricing algorithm captures the resiliency metrics of the microgrids, the primary one being
the amount of critical load served. The pricing mechanism indirectly influences the generation
mix in the microgrids and reduces load shedding and expensive sources such as diesel
generation. The pricing algorithm uses a per-phase load forecast to help decide an appropriate
price forecast for the next 24 h. Determining electricity prices is crucial for operation in islands of
a network of multiple microgrids. As shown in Figure 5.2, to determine pricing, the MO receives
only the power unbalance in each microgrid by phase. Next, the LMC introduces this price into
the optimization to minimize its cost of operation. Minimizing the cost of operation indirectly
reduces load shedding in the microgrids because power can be imported from other microgrids.
During a loss of communication between the LMC and the MO, the microgrids default to the
base state of independent microgrid operation, which makes this architecture reliable. This
approach maintains the privacy of each of the businesses in the microgrid because no detailed
information about their load profile is needed. The pricing process is iterative, the local
microgrids always act independently, and the LMC agrees to import or export power from or to
its boundaries based on the price of electricity at the point of connection. MO pricing influences
the demand of power in the microgrids, and the LMC includes this price in the optimization
scheme to minimize the local cost of operation.

 6. NETWORKED MICROGRIDS VIA DC INTERLINKS THREE-PHASE

DC interconnection represents a novel approach for interconnecting microgrids. DC
interconnections bring several advantages: (1) They decouple through power electronics the
dynamics of independent microgrids. (2) Each microgrid can operate its grid-forming inverters in
VF control mode, ensuring stiff regulation of these magnitudes. (3) Transients are eliminated
because turning on/off the power electronics is smooth. The efficiency of each conversion state
is 97%. Using DC interconnection (shown in Figure 6.1), the MO primarily adjusts the active
power (P) and reactive power (Q) of the interconnection, preserving the local controllers and
assets within each microgrid exactly as they would be in an independent connection. This factor
not only simplifies the control strategy but also maintains ownership because no changes are
made to the local controllers. Furthermore, the interconnection process in DC systems is
notably straightforward. Microgrids start as standalone islands in VF control and, when
additional power is required, the back-to-back power converters adapt the P and Q levels to
meet demand. This process is characterized by minimal transients, thanks to the current-
regulated nature of the converters.

Bidirectional DC rectifier converter

The DC rectifier is a four-quadrant, 38 kW, 480 V, three-wire power converter. Its main purpose
is to maintain stiff regulation of the DC-link voltage regardless of the direction of the power flow.
Although the manufacturer does not disclose control of the converter, the most typical controller
for this type of converter is voltage-oriented control (VOC). The current control loop regulates
the exchanged grid current, whereas the DC-voltage control loop regulates the DC voltage. The
implementation of both control loops is well reported in the scientific literature, and a great
number of technical solutions exist (e.g., Proportional-Intergral (PI) control, proportional-
resonant control, repetitive control).

Energy Storage Inverter Control

The PQ converter is a four-quadrant, 40 kW, 480 V, four-wire power converter. The main
purpose of this converter is to regulate active and reactive power regardless of the direction of
the DC power. A popular control strategy for this converter is VOC. The current control loop
regulates the exchanged microgrid current, while the power control loop regulates the active
and reactive power independently. The implementation of both control loops is well reported.

Start-up Sequence

The start-up sequence of the DC interlink must follow a sequence. The first converter to start up
is the DC-rectifier. This converter requires valid AC voltage, which is established by the local
GFM unit in the microgrid (e.g., ESS). Once the inverter detects valid voltage, the start-up
sequence is provided through Modbus TCP/IP commands to the DC-rectifier converter. The
converter goes to proprietary internal sequences: calibrate, charging, stand-by, and online.

 37771

Once the converter is online, the DC-link voltage is regulated to the user-specified voltage. The
PQ converter requires valid AC and DC voltage. The AC voltage must be established in the
second microgrid by the GFM unit. The DC voltage is established by the DC-rectifier following
the steps previously mentioned. Once online, the PQ converter receives commands from the
orchestrator, the higher-level controller. The PQ values can be determined by an optimization or
can be calculated to achieve SoC balancing, as will be shown in the experimental section.

6.1 EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS, DC INTERCONNECTION: HARDWARE RESULTS

The DC interlink was validated on ORNL’s networked microgrid testbed, which incorporates
distribution grade protection and switchgear, off-the shelf three-phase PV inverter, and ESS that
can be configured as three- or four-wire, supporting resource emulators, and microgrid
controllers. The local ESS is represented as a coupling of a DC source emulator and inverter,
and it can operate in GFM and GF control. In GF, grid assets follow IEEE 1547-2018. While in
GFM, the inverter has been programmed to operate in droop control with virtual impedance. A
diagram of the testbed and the equipment is shown in Figure 6.3, with Figure 6.3(b) and Figure
6.3(c) showing oscilloscope data of the DC interlink voltage (green), current (cyan), and Grid
Microgrid 1 voltage (blue). When a small capacitor (100 µF/1,000 V) was used, oscillations
appeared in the DC interlink. To eliminate these oscillations, a larger film capacitor (1,000
µF/1,000 V) was installed at the DC rectifier terminals. This adjustment effectively suppressed
the oscillations, enabling greater power transfer between the microgrids. A detailed analysis of
this behavior will be provided in future publications.

Figure 6.4(b) illustrates the inrush current observed during the startup of the power converters.
For the converter to initialize, a valid AC voltage must be present in the microgrid. Upon
detection, the converter connects either in GF mode or as a DC rectifier to regulate the DC bus
voltage. As shown, the peak inrush current reaches 8 A but decays rapidly, stabilizing at
approximately 2.5 A. In contrast, Figure 6.4(a) presents the inrush current associated with the
AC interconnection of two microgrids employing GFM converters. In this case, the peak inrush
current reaches 30 A and is followed by a sustained high current before settling. These results
indicate that the DC interconnect achieves a significantly lower inrush current and enables a
smoother transition during interconnection.

6.2 VALIDATION WITH DATA FROM COMMUNITY MICROGRID IN ADJUNTAS, PUERTO

Figure 6.5 presents the experimental validation of the proposed DC interlink, using as a case
study of two community-owned microgrids located in Adjuntas, Puerto Rico. The experimental
results in Figure 6.5 show the potential resiliency benefits of networking these systems using
the proposed DC interlink. Data for this experiment were collected directly from the field using
dataloggers and PV inverter communication interfaces. These field data were imported into the
ORNL network microgrid testbed and scaled down to match the power rating of the laboratory
setup.

Similar to the AC interconnect use case, we analyzed in this experiment a scenario where one
of the microgrids, identified as Pod 2, experiences a 40% loss in PV generation. This simulation
generation loss emulates generation that was affected in the aftermath of a hurricane. To
address this, power flow between the two microgrids is coordinated through a network microgrid
controller implementing an SoC balancing algorithm. This algorithm aims to minimize the SoC
difference between the two microgrids, thereby extending the islanding duration compared with
that of stand-alone operation. In the absence of this coordination, the affected microgrid would
rapidly deplete its battery because of the generation shortfall.

Networking the microgrids allows surplus PV generation from the healthy microgrid to be
exported to the affected one, maximizing the overall utilization of available renewable energy
resources. The results obtained in this figure are accelerated; the time axis is in seconds, and
3,500 s corresponds to a week in the real system. As shown, the interlink power flow is mainly
positive, as power from the microgrid with full capacity flows to the second microgrid to maintain
the SoC of both systems close to each other. Without interconnecting the microgrids, the PV in
the healthy microgrid would have to be curtailed to prevent the batteries from overcharging. By
exporting power to the second microgrid, the use of renewables is optimized. By networking
microgrids with the proposed DC interlink and control, the system can run in island for 1 week
with a minimum SoC of around 40%.

Figure 6.6 and Figure 6.7 show the voltage and frequency regulation of both microgrid systems
using DC and AC interlinks, respectively. A key advantage of interconnecting microgrids through
DC interlinks is that the grid-forming inverters in Microgrid 1 and Microgrid 2 can operate in VF
control mode. This configuration ensures stiff voltage control within the microgrids, thereby
improving power quality. In contrast, the voltage and frequency regulation with AC interlinks
depends on the droop curves and virtual impedance configuration of the grid-forming inverters.
Therefore, the level of voltage regulation, as validated in the experimental results shown of
figure 6.7, is not stiff, and the voltage and frequency deviations depend on the load and
generation. Figure 6.6 shows that with the DC-interlink the voltage and frequency is perfectly
regulated to 480V and 60Hz in both microgrids because the converters can remain in Voltage
Frequency (VF) control.

7.8 SOC BALANCING

The validation of the DC interlink involved networking the Casa Pueblo and Plaza Energética
microgrids, both operating in islanded mode. The test aimed to activate the SoC fairness
control, allowing the microgrid with the higher SoC to support the one with the lower SoC. To
begin, the Plaza Energética microgrid was seamlessly transitioned to islanded operation. Then,
half of its PV generation was intentionally deactivated to simulate damage to the solar array.
Three electric stoves were connected to increase the load and accelerate the discharge of the
100 kWh battery. Once the battery’s SoC dropped to 88%, the SoC fairness control was
enabled. As expected, the Casa Pueblo microgrid began exporting power to support the
adjacent microgrid. The battery at Plaza Energética was recharged to 94% by importing power
from the Casa Pueblo microgrid, successfully concluding the test. The tests results are shown in
Figure 7.13 through Figure 7.15.

 9. SIGNIFICANT ACCOMPLISHMENTS AND CONCLUSIONS

This report summarizes the work completed under the SETO-funded project #37771, “Resilient
Operation of Networked Community Microgrids with High Solar Penetration.” Through
experimental and field validation, the project successfully validated the operation of networked
microgrids using both AC and DC interconnects. A key objective was to ensure compatibility
with today’s technologies by employing COTS inverters and solutions that are interoperable with
commercial microgrid controllers.

The project addressed a wide range of technical areas, including distributed optimization,
hardware development, forecasting, protection schemes, and dynamic boundary management
for microgrids. The primary activities were validated at ORNL’s GRID-C COMMANDER
networked microgrid testbed, as well as through CHIL experimentation.

Field validation in Adjuntas, Puerto Rico, confirmed the practical benefits of the DC interlink,
which overcame challenges typically associated with AC interconnections. These include
achieving seamless integration, simplified interoperability and accurate control of both active
and reactive power.

Importantly, the project advanced the state of the art not only in a controlled laboratory setting
but also in real-world deployments. The networked microgrids validated clear advantages during
critical events, such as maintaining SoC balance when local generation assets were
compromised.

The proposed algorithms and hardware enable real-world microgrids to operate in a coordinated
network, supporting bidirectional power flow and precise, synchronized power exchange.

This project resulted in 15 journal publications and 15 conference papers; 5 graduate students
and 15 undergraduate students were supported. The codes of distributed optimization and
forecasting were made available open-source through OSTI.gov for distributed optimization and
forecasting. All the publications are available on the ORNL hosted project landing page.

The DC interlink with SoC balancing control was validated in Adjuntas by interconnecting two
real-world, 240 V split-phase microgrids. The first, Casa Pueblo, includes 14 kW DC of PV and a
40 kWh/8 kW energy storage system. The second, Plaza Energética, features 55 kW DC of PV
and a 100 kWh/24 kW storage system. This effort represents the first known operational
validation of networked microgrids using a DC interlink.

As a culmination of this project, a follow-on grant was awarded to support the technology
transfer of the distributed optimization framework to a commercial microgrid controller, Stellar
Edge, developed by the California-based company New Sun Road.

Lessons learned:

Challenges: At the start of the project, the objective was to interconnect two microgrids located
in the town square of Adjuntas. However, several challenges emerged that made this original
plan unfeasible:

  Participating Businesses: Initially, the interconnection was designed to pass through a
 set of participating businesses, all of them were adjacent making the interconnection
 feasible. However, during project development, the participating businesses changed.

 37771

 As a result, the proposed interconnection path would have required crossing six
 businesses that were no longer participating in the microgrid initiative, creating both
 logistical and legal complications.

  Physical and Cost Barriers: The new path required over 1,000 feet of cabling,
 significantly increasing costs. Utility poles in the area were not permitted to carry
 additional loads. Underground cabling would have required digging through public
 streets, which was both disruptive and prohibitively expensive.

  Interoperability and Warranty Issues: The existing energy storage systems installed in
 the town square had compatibility and warranty restrictions that made integration into a
 unified system technically and contractually infeasible. The team reached out to the
 vendor and the communication was unsuccessful for their participation in the project.

Mitigation and Adaptation: In response to these challenges, the team negotiated with the U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) SETO to relocate the field validation to Plaza de la
Independencia. This shift was carried out in collaboration with Casa Pueblo and allowed the
team to successfully complete the field validation in a realistic scenario. While the original plan
was to validate interconnection using three-phase systems (as validated in the lab), the new site
involved a split-phase configuration, which is commonly used in Puerto Rico. This adjustment
extended the impact of the research by enabling validation in both three-phase and split-phase
networked microgrids.

The challenges related to interoperability and integration ultimately led the team to design and
implement a first-of-its-kind DC-interlink solution for networked microgrids. This novel system
was successfully deployed in Adjuntas, allowing the project to meet its goals through an
innovative and adaptable approach.

 10. PATH FORWARD

The immediate path forward is the commercialization of the microgrid orchestrator. ORNL and
New Sun Road were awarded a technology transfer grant. Under this grant, New Sun Road will
commercialize the MO–foundational control technology enabling networked microgrids during
the SETO project. The MO is the control layer responsible for coordinating networked
microgrids, which increases system resilience, improves utilization of renewable energy
sources, delivers economic benefits, and extends the duration of islanding operations compared
with operating the microgrids independently. New Sun Road is a public benefit corporation with
a mission to accelerate communities’ access to renewable energy. Its commercially available
Stellar Microgrid OS cloud-based microgrid control platform is used in over 1,000 microgrids in
22 countries in applications including disaster relief, commercial and agriculture facilities,
telecommunications, and community- and utility-owned microgrids. Stellar provides fleet
management and analytics tools, on-site computing, and control with the Stellar Edge, as well
as optimized power and energy management using AI. In this project, New Sun Road will work
with ORNL researchers to incorporate the MO into its Stellar platform and validate and test the
control of networked microgrids. New Sun Road will leverage its customer base and experience
bringing new technology to market to execute a commercialization plan consisting of a market
assessment of customer types including the regulatory environment, developing a go-to-market

 37771

strategy, and engaging with prospective customers to secure a target of three paid pilot
networked-microgrid projects using the new MO features.
