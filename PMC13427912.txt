Far-red light enhances photosynthesis in the shade-adapted old forest lichen Lobaria pulmonaria
Authors: Knut Asbjørn Solhaug, Krisilnta Kaimakou, Dhanushka Niroshani Herath Ekanayake, Nieves Solera Guerrero, Yngvar Gauslaa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Far-red (FR) light (700–750 nm) is strongly enriched beneath forest canopies, but its impact on photosynthesis in shade-adapted lichens is not well understood. We investigated the role of FR light in photosynthesis of Lobaria pulmonaria, an oldforest cephalolichen. Spectral measurements showed that FR photons contributed up to 32% of the extended photosynthetic photon flux density (ePPFD; 400–750 nm) under the canopy, compared to only 11% in open areas. Gas exchange data showed that FR light alone supported moderate CO2 uptake, but photosynthetic rates increased markedly when 50–100% FR was combined with background red light (R), demonstrating the Emerson enhancement effect. When comparing absorbed photons, FR light was even more efficient than R. Chlorophyll fluorescence data indicated that conventional electron transport rate (ETR) calculations often overestimate photosynthetic activity under high FR, underscoring the need for improved methods. These results suggest that FR light is crucial for lichen growth and survival in shaded forests. We recommend using ePPFD sensors in ecological studies to more accurately quantify relevant light availability for photosynthetic organisms.

Introduction

Approximately half of the energy in sunlight falls within the visible spectrum, which is used for photosynthesis. It was early demonstrated by Emerson and Lewis (1942) and later by Emerson and Rabinowitch (1960) that far-red (FR) light (700–750 nm) can also contribute to photosynthesis, particularly through synergistic interactions with shorter wavelengths. McCree (1972), using monochromatic light, showed that quantum yield of photosynthesis dropped steeply with longer wavelengths than 700 nm. Based on the action spectrum by McCree (1972), the 400–700 nm wavelength range has long been used as a definition of photosynthetic active radiation (PAR). As a result, standard PAR sensors, which exclude wavelengths above 700 nm, likely underestimate the total light available for photosynthesis because the synergetically effect of FR is not included.

Far-red photons are more readily transmitted and reflected by plant leaves (Gates 1965; Holmes and Smith 1977; Hertel et al. 2011), enriching the far-red wavelengths beneath a canopy of green leaves light (Gates 1965; Ross et al. 1986). Consequently, far-red light may be especially important for photosynthetic organisms inhabiting shaded forest understories, compared to those in open areas.

Lobaria pulmonaria, a shade-adapted cephalolichen, is typically found in forests with long ecological continuity unaffected by major disturbance such as logging or fires (Rose 1976). It is highly sensitive to direct sunlight, especially when desiccated (Gauslaa and Solhaug 1996, 2000; Gauslaa et al. 2019). Nevertheless, the amount of light received during hydration periods strongly promotes lichen growth (Palmqvist et al. 2008; Palmqvist and Sundberg 2000; Borge and Ellis 2024). Notably, hydration often occurs during cloudy weather or before sunrise, when light availability is low and may limit lichen growth (Porter 1927; Gauslaa et al. 2006). In this context, the lower layers of the forest canopy, characterized by slow desiccation rates and enrichment in far-red light, may facilitate substantial photosynthesis in L. pulmonaria. This is consistent with the observed high lichen biomass in old forests (Fig. 1).

Recent advances in lighting technology, particularly the widespread adoption of light-emitting diodes (LEDs) in greenhouses and vertical farming, have spurred increased research in the photosynthetic role of far-red light. While far-red LEDs have primarily been used to regulate plant morphology and development via the phytochrome system, emerging research indicates that far-red light can also directly drive photosynthesis. Some studies suggest that far-red light can be as efficient as PAR (400–700 nm) on an incident photon basis (Zhen and Bugbee 2020), while others report similar efficiency on an absorbed basis (Jin et al. 2025). Consequently, the concept of extended photosynthetic active radiation (ePAR), encompassing wavelengths from 400 to 750 nm, has been proposed (Zhen and Bugbee 2020).

Although the signaling effects of far-red light on shade plants via the phytochrome system are well documented (see e.g., Gommers et al. 2013), the efficiency of far-red light in driving photosynthesis in wild shade plants, and particularly in forest lichens, remains understudied. In this study, we characterize the spectral distribution of light in a shaded forest habitat that supports L. pulmonaria and assess the efficiency of far-red light in promoting photosynthetic CO₂ uptake in its thalli. Finally, we discuss the implications of ePAR for light measurements in forest environments.

Materials and methods

Study species and sampling site

Thalli of Lobaria pulmonaria (L.) Hoffm. were collected on September 3, 2025 from the trunks of five oak (Quercus robur) trees in an old oak forest at Langangen, southeastern Norway (59°06′43″ N, 9°50′05″ E; 150 m a.s.l.). After collection, the samples were air-dried and stored at − 20 °C until the experiments commenced a few weeks later. All thalli were sprayed with deionized water and pretreated in hydrated state at 20 °C under low light (10 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1) for one day before photosynthesis measurements.

Spectral photon irradiance in the 350–850 nm range was measured directly on the oak stems using a SpectraPen Mini spectrometer (Photon Systems Instruments, Drásov, Czech Republic), with the sensor oriented at the same vertical inclination as the lichen thalli. Measurements were performed at midday of the collection date under overcast conditions, with green leaves present on the trees. The season and weather during the irradiance measurement were typical for good growth periods because (1) the sun’s height and thus light levels remained high, (2) air humidity was elevated due to transpiration from fully developed foliage, and (3) the overcast conditions provided light levels characteristic of those supporting photosynthesis in forest lichens. For comparison, a reference spectrum was recorded at a nearby open site under identical weather conditions. The PPFD and ePPFD at the open site was 213 and 260 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1, respectively, while at the lichen sites on the five oak stems, the values ± SE were 37 ± 10 and 53 ± 13 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1, respectively.

Spectral reflectance

Spectral reflectance and transmittance (350–2500 nm) of the upper surface of eight L. pulmonaria thalli were measured using a reflectance/transmittance integrating sphere (Spectral Evolution, Haverhill, MA, USA) connected to a spectrometer (Model RS-3500, Spectral Evolution, Haverhill, MA, USA).

CO2 uptake

Photosynthetic CO2 uptake was measured using a LI-6400XT infrared gas analyzer (LiCOR, Nebraska, USA) equipped with a bryophyte cuvette (6400–24 Bryophyte Chamber) with a transparent top. Illumination was provided by a red and far-red LED panel (ISL-150 × 150-FR, CCS Inc., Kyoto, Japan), controlled by a two-channel LED driver unit (ISC-201-2, CCS Inc., Kyoto, Japan). The LED panel delivered monochromatic red light (peak: 661 nm ; half maximum wavelength width: 23 nm) and far-red light (peak: 733 nm ; half maximum wavelength width: 32 nm), as illustrated in Fig. 2. Red and far-red photon irradiance were quantified using a SpectraPen Mini spectrometer (Photon Systems Instruments, Drásov, Czech Republic). All measurements were conducted at 20 °C with a CO₂ concentration of 420 ppm.

Chlorophyll measurement

Thallus discs (0.5 cm²) were cut using a cork borer and placed in Eppendorf tubes containing 1.5 mL DMSO saturated with MgCO3. Chlorophyll was extracted in darkness at room temperature for 24 h. Absorbance was measured at 649, 665, and 750 nm using a UV-2101PC spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Scientific Instruments, Kyoto, Japan). Contents of chlorophyll a and b were calculated using the equations of Wellburn (1994). The absorbance at 750 nm, which is not absorbed by chlorophyll, was subtracted from the absorbances at 649 and 665 nm to correct for minor absorbance errors caused by impurities as e.g. small particles in the solution.

Chlorophyll fluorescence

The effective quantum yield of PSII (ΦPSII) and the apparent electron transport rate (ETR) were measured with a PAM-2000 fluorometer (Heinz Walz GmbH, Effeltrich, Germany). Four light treatments were used: darkness, 50 µmol m− 2 s− 1 R, 50 µmol m− 2 s− 1 FR, and 50 µmol m− 2 s− 1 R + 50 µmol m− 2 s− 1 FR from the LED panel described above. ΦPSII in darkness (equivalent to Fv/Fm) was measured after 15 min of dark adaptation. ΦPSII under each light treatment was measured after 5–10 min of equilibration. Apparent ETR was calculated as ETRapp = ΦPSII x ePPFD x 0.5. This equation is based on the calculation of absolute ETR, given by the equation ETR = ΦPSII x I x A x FractionPSII, where ΦPSII is the operating efficiency of PSII, I is the incident light, A is the fraction of incident light absorbed, and FractionPSII is the proportion of incident light utilized by PSII (Baker 2008). In this study, we calculated ETRapp based on ePPDF. Because FR affects both the fraction of incident light absorbed and the FractionPSII, measuring ETRapp provides insights into the effects of FR on photosynthesis when compared with photosynthetic CO2 uptake.

Statistical analyses

Differences in net photosynthetic CO₂ assimilation between light treatments were analyzed using paired samples t-test in Jamovi v2.6 (The Jamovi Project). Normality was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Results

Thallus reflectance, transmittance and absorptance

The spectral distribution of the incident photon irradiance from R (630–690 nm) and FR (700–760 nm) LED sources exhibits peaks at 660 and 740 nm (Fig. 2). Analysis of the LED panel spectra in combination with the thallus absorptance spectrum (Fig. 3) revealed that L. pulmonaria thalli absorbed 91% of red light but only 34% of far-red light. This difference is primarily due to the strong reflectance and transmittance of FR wavelengths by the thalli (Fig. 3).

The spectral contrast between irradiance measured above (open site) and below the forest canopy at the lichen collection site was substantial (Fig. 4). Far-red (700–750 nm) photons constituted only 11% of the extended photosynthetic photon flux density (ePPFD, 400–750 nm) in the open, but this proportion increased to 32% beneath the forest canopy during the lichen collection day. By multiplying the irradiance spectra (400–750 nm) from both sites (Fig. 4) with the absorptance, transmittance, and reflectance spectra of L. pulmonaria (Fig. 3), the fractions of ePPFD absorbed, transmitted, and reflected for thalli illuminated from above were determined. Most visible light was absorbed, whereas less than half of the far-red light was absorbed. In the forest, a slightly lower fraction of far-red light was absorbed compared to the open site. This is because, in the forest, the far-red light is shifted towards longer wavelengths (Table 1; Fig. 4). The relative FR fraction of ePPFD at the open site on a cloudy day was 11%, likely due to absorption of FR by water in the clouds. In contrast, the FR fraction of ePPFD in direct sunlight on a clear day (May 7th ), measured with the same spectrometer, was 16%. Chlorophyll content

The chlorophyll content of the eight thalli used in the 33/16 R/FR treatment was 330 ± 22 mg m− 2, with a chl a/b ratio of 2.83 ± 0.05 (means ± SE).

Discussion

Light level requirements for L. pulmonaria

The PPFD ± SE at the collection sites on the overcast day when L. pulmonaria was sampled for this study was 17 ± 5% of the open site. This site likely provided optimal conditions for L. pulmonaria growth. According to Mafole et al. (2017) and Kann et al. (2025), the light compensation points are 23 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1, and the thalli reach light saturation at 130 and 150 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1, respectively, when measured at 20 °C and estimated with Photosyn Assistant v 1.1.2 (Dundee Scientific, Dundee, UK). During most active growth, temperatures are typically lower than 20 °C, which results in reduced dark respiration and, consequently, lower light compensation points.

In their natural habitats, lichens often undergo daily cycles of desiccation and rehydration. During these cycles, the hydration levels of both the photobiont and the mycobiont may influence photosynthesis and dark respiration differently. In addition, excessive water can lead to suprasaturation, which reduces CO2 diffusion and thereby limits CO2 uptake, while not affecting dark respiration to the same extent. In the present study, we aimed to measure photosynthetic light responses under optimal hydration conditions. Thalli were first sprayed with water and then kept at 100% relative humidity under low light. Immediately before CO2 uptake measurements, they were gently blotted with absorbent paper to avoid suprasaturation. Net photosynthesis in lichens reflects a complex interplay among light, temperature, and hydration. (Stanton et al. 2023).

Synergistic effect of red and far-red light on lichen photosynthesis

The Emerson enhancement effect (Emerson and Lewis 1942; Emerson and Rabinowitch 1960) is clearly demonstrated in L. pulmonaria, with the addition of far-red light to red light resulting in greater photosynthetic efficiency than far-red light alone (Fig. 7). Although supplementing red (R) light with an equivalent amount of far-red (FR) light produced a smaller increase in photosynthesis than the addition of R light alone (Fig. 7), calculation of efficiency based on light absorbed reveals a striking pattern.

The efficiency based on absorbed photons (quantum yield) of adding 16 µmol m− 2 s− 1 FR or R were 0.071 and 0.029 µmol CO2 µmol photon− 1 respectively, and the efficiences by adding 50 µmol m− 2 s− 1 FR or R were 0.036 and 0.016 µmol CO2 µmol photon− 1 respectively.

These results indicate that, while added FR is less efficient than R on an incident basis—consistent with the findings of Jin et al. (2025) it is substantially more efficient than R when evaluated per absorbed photon. The efficiency of far-red light observed in this study therefore falls between the high efficiency reported by Zhen and Bugbee (2020) and the findings of Jin et al. (2025), who propose that R and FR can be equally efficient on an absorbed basis. The higher efficiency of FR reported by Zhen and Bugbee (2020) can be explained by their measurement of canopy photosynthesis, where FR absorption is enhanced due to its greater penetration to lower leaves, whereas Jin et al. (2025) measured single leaf photosynthesis, which has lower FR absorptance. The efficiency of FR may be dose dependent. In our study, we used 50–100% FR compared with R light, whereas Zhen and Bugbee (2020) did not exceed 30% FR in addition to background white light. Increasing the FR fraction beyond a certain point may approach a limit where further excitation balance between PSII and PSI cannot be achieved.

Challenges in electron transport rate calculation with far-red enrichment

The chlorophyll fluorescence data underscore limitations of the conventional equation for estimating ETR = Abs × PPFD × ΦPSII × 0.5, as discussed by Baker (2008). One challenge is the inherent difficulty in accurately measuring absorptance in lichens compared to higher plants. However, the most critical issue lies in the use of the 0.5 factor, which assumes that absorbed photons are equally distributed between PSI and PSII.

Far-red light is predominantly absorbed by PSI, which shifts electron flow away from PSII, keeping PSII in a more open state and thereby increasing ΦPSII. This explains the elevated ΦPSII values observed under FR light (Table 2). Under these conditions, less than half of the absorbed photons are utilized by PSII, leading to a substantial overestimation of ETR when the standard formula is applied. This discrepancy is evident when comparing ETR values (Table 2) with direct gas exchange measurements (Fig. 6).

For example, the increase in photosynthetic CO₂ uptake observed when 50 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of FR light is added to 50 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of R light (Fig. 6) is much smaller than the corresponding increase in ETR. This discrepancy occurs because, under red light, more than half of the absorbed light is directed to PSII, potentially causing overexcitation. Adding FR light primarily excites PSI, reducing the proportion of photons absorbed by PSII to less than 50%. This draws electrons away from PSII and increases ΦPSII (Table 2), a phenomenon also observed in lettuce (Zhen et al. 2019).

These findings demonstrate that applying a fixed absorptance factor of 0.5 to calculate ETR is inappropriate for comparing ETR across light treatments with differing proportions of far-red light. In conclusion, ETR estimation based on chlorophyll fluorescence is not a reliable method for assessing photosynthesis under light conditions with varying R/FR ratios.

Regulation of excitation balance between PSII and PS1

Efficient photosynthesis relies on maintaining an optimal excitation balance between PSII and PSI. Light with wavelengths longer than 680 nm predominantly excites PSI (Chow et al. 1990). Plants can regulate this excitation balance through multiple mechanisms. Over long timescales, they may adjust the PSI/PSII-ratio to adapt to their light environment. For instance, in shaded environments enriched with far-red light, the PSI/PSII ratio is reduced (Hogewoning et al. 2012). In the short term, state transitions enable the dynamic redistribution of light-harvesting complexes between PSII and PSI, therby maintaining excitation balance (Allen 2003).

Many classical studies of photosynthesis under controlled conditions used identical spectral light quality in both low and high light treatments (Leong and Anderson 1984a, b). Consequently, these studies may not fully capture the distinction between sun and shade environments, as they did not account for differences in light spectral quality.

Acclimation to far-red light in shaded forest

Photosynthetic organisms inhabiting shaded environments enriched in FR light are likely to develop a photosynthetic apparatus optimized for efficient utilization of these wavelengths. The L. pulmonaria thalli examined in this study were collected from a forest environment with abundant FR light and thus were probably acclimated to maximize the use of FR light. Future studies should compare FR light utilization between plants and lichens adapted to high-FR shade sites and those acclimated to high light conditions with a lower proportion of FR light. Substantial variation in FR light utilization is likely to exist among different species and acclimation states.

Extended photosynthetic active radiation (ePAR)

As emphasized by Zhen et al. (2022), FR light should be included when quantifying light available for photosynthesis, especially for shade-adapted or acclimated organisms exposed to high FR light fractions. The substantial contribution of FR light to photosynthesis makes ePAR (400–750 nm) a more appropriate parameter than traditional PAR (400–700 nm) for assessing light availability. Relying solely on conventional quantum sensors, which measure only in the 400–700 nm range, will underestimate the irradiance available for photosynthesis in shaded environments beneath green canopies. Similarly, in horticultural lighting where FR is included, ePAR provides a better prediction of light available for photosynthesis (Zhen et al. 2021). We therefore endorse the adoption of the term “extended photosynthetic photon flux density” (ePPFD) when ePAR is measured on a photon basis.

Ecological implications of FR light for canopy lichens

Epiphytic lichens predominantly colonize the leafless lower and inner regions of tree canopies, as illustrated by L. pulmonaria growing on the lower trunks of oak trees (Fig. 1a) and on dead segments of spruce branches (Fig. 1b). The inner, leafless part of a tree typically supports the highest biomass of epiphytic lichens (Goward et al. 2024), suggesting that lichen thalli can maintain a positive C-balance under light levels too low to sustain tree foliage.

Continuous measurements of PPFD on the side of an oak trunk with the largest L. pulmonaria population revealed that, over the course of an entire year, PPFD exceeded 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for less than 30 h, and levels at or above 100 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ were rare during the leaf-on season (Gauslaa and Solhaug 2000). Lower spruce canopy branches supporting reasonable growth rates of transplanted hair lichens received PPFD levels as low as 30 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (Gauslaa et al. 2020), which is only slightly above the light compensation point for the thalli studied (Fig. 5). These observations suggest that the persistence and productivity of large lichen biomass in shaded canopies are enhanced by the enrichment of FR light beneath layers of leaves and conifer needles. Furthermore, these findings highlight the need to replace traditional PPFD sensors with ePPFD sensors in ecological studies of forest ecosystems, to more accurately assess the light available for photosynthesis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that FR light, which is abundant beneath forest canopies, significantly contributes to photosynthesis in the old forest lichen L. pulmonaria. Although FR alone is less efficient than R light on an incident basis, it becomes highly efficient on an absorbed photon basis, often exceeding the efficiency of R light. We observed a strong Emerson enhancement effect, whereby the combination of R and FR light resulted in higher photosynthetic rates than either wavelength region alone.

Chlorophyll fluorescence data showed that conventional methods for estimating ETR are unreliable under varying R/FR ratios, as they do not account for the unique absorption characteristics of FR. This underscores the need for improved approaches when measuring photosynthetic activity in environment with substantial FR light.

Importantly, our results suggest that capacity to utilize FR light helps explain the high biomass and persistence of lichens in shaded forest canopies, where traditional PPFD measurements underestimate the light actually available for photosynthesis. To accurately assess light conditions and better support ecological research and conservation of shade-adapted species, we recommend the use of ePPFD (400–750 nm) sensors in forest studies.
