Correlation inequalities for Schur positivity
Swee Hong Chan, Hong Chen, Igor Pak, Daniel Soskin

Abstract
. We generalize the Ahlswede–Daykin inequality (1978) to a Schur positive ADS inequality , which also contains the Lam–Postnikov–Pylyavskyy inequality (2007) as a special case. We then present a number of further generalizations and applications. Notably, we resolve Mihalcea’s conjecture on log-supermodularity of stable Grothendieck polynomials.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Foreword

The extensive use of correlation inequalities in combinatorics, probability and across the sciences
lets one forget the remarkable story behind these inequalities. The first
instances of such correlation inequalities were introduced in the 1960s,
independently by Harris
[
Har60
]
and Kleitman
[
Kle66
]
, to solve
very specific problems in percolation theory and in extremal combinatorics,
respectively.

The original proofs by induction had somewhat obscured the real depth
of the
Harris–Kleitman
(HK)
inequality
. What followed was a long series of
generalizations, all extending the inequalities and the inductive arguments
in the increasingly clever ways. Of these, the

Fortuin–Kasteleyn–Ginibre
(FKG)
inequality

[
FKG71
]
,
the
Holley inequality

[
Hol74
]
, the
Daykin inequality

[
Day77
]
,
and the
Ahlswede–Daykin
(AD)
inequality

[
AD78
]
, are
probably the best known. Further generalizations include
q
- and multivariate
correlation inequalities
[
Bjö11
,
Chr09
,
CP23a
,
LP07a
]
,
the
localization inequalities
by Lovász–Saks
[
LS06
]
,
multiple events versions
[
AK96
,
Gla24a
,
RS93
]
,
and most recently the
decision tree versions
[
Gla24b
,
Kern20
]
.

Let us emphasize a key feature of AD type generalizations of the HK inequality:
the
local-to-global principle
. The goal is no longer to establish positive
correlations between specific probability measures, but between measures
which satisfy certain natural “local” assumptions. These local properties hold
trivially for the probabilistic and counting applications in the Harris and Kleitman
setting. The “global” conclusions have a similar structure as local assumptions,
the proofs become streamlined, and the results turn out to be much stronger
due to flexibility of local assumptions.

In this paper we introduce a local-to-global principle for correlation inequalities
involving symmetric functions, where the inequalities are stated in terms
of
Schur positivity
. We replace the
log-supermodularity
assumption
in the AD inequality, with the celebrated

Lam–Postnikov–Pylyavskyy inequality
(
LPP
), where the
meet and join operations on subsets are defined as intersection and union of Young
diagrams.

Our main result is the
ADS inequality
(
ADS
),
a Schur positive generalization of the AD inequality, which similarly
has a number of important applications, see below.
Since ADS inequality contains the LPP inequality as a trivial special case,
our proofs are no longer inductive and involve large refinement into
what we call
Schur orchestra inequalities
(Theorem
5.2
).
The latter are then proved
by a technical arguments involving
Temperley–Lieb immanants
, a
technique introduced by Rhoades–Skandera
[
RS05
,
RS06
]
,
and further extended in
[
LPP07
,
SS25
]
(see also
[
AKOS25
,
NP25
,
NP26
,
PS26+
]
).

We now give some probabilistic and algebraic combinatorics
background, before proceeding to state the main results. The
interested reader familiar with the subject is encouraged to jump
ahead to
\lx@sectionsign
1.5
.

1.2.
Harris–Kleitman inequality

We start with Kleitman’s enumerative version of the

HK inequality

[
Kle66
]
, which is easiest to state.
A collection
\mathcal{U}\subset 2^{[n]}
of subsets of
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is called
up-closed
, if
B\in\mathcal{U}
for all
B\subseteq A
,
A\in\mathcal{U}
.
Then:

(HK)

|\mathcal{U}|\cdot|\cV|\,\leq\,|\mathcal{U}\cap\cV|\hskip 0.85355pt\cdot\hskip 0.85355pt2^{n}\hskip 0.85355pt,

for every two up-closed collections
\mathcal{U},\cV\subset 2^{[n]}
. Stated in
probabilistic terms, this is equivalent to

(1.1)

\mathbf{P}[A\in\mathcal{U}\cap\cV]\,\geq\,\mathbf{P}[A\in\mathcal{U}]\hskip 1.70709pt\cdot\hskip 1.70709pt\mathbf{P}[A\in\cV]\hskip 0.85355pt,

where the probability is over uniform random subsets of
\mathcal{U}
.
For example, for collections of edges of a complete graph
K_{r}\hskip 0.85355pt
,
the result implies:

(1.2)

\mathbf{P}[G~\text{is triangle-free and planar}]\,\geq\,\mathbf{P}[G~\text{is triangle-free}]\hskip 1.70709pt\cdot\hskip 1.70709pt\mathbf{P}[G~\text{is planar}]\hskip 0.85355pt,

where the probability is over uniform subgraphs
G
of
K_{r}\hskip 0.85355pt
.

In this notation, Harris’s version
[
Har60
]
, can be viewed as a probabilistic
generalization of Kleitman’s version (
HK
), where the probability measure on
collections is a product measure on
2^{[n]}
. For example, this implies
that (
1.2
) holds for the probability
\mathbf{P}_{p}
in the Erdős–Rényi
G(n,p)
random graph model.
1
1

1

To
quote Harris’s own characterization of the HK inequality,
“its truth seems obvious, but I have not been able to find a shorter
proof [..] nor have I seen the result stated elsewhere”
[

.

In its basic application, the HK inequality implies that the
critical probability

is independent on the vertex

, see e.g.
[

]
.
Here by
\mathbf{P}_{p}(x\leftrightarrow\infty)
we mean the probability that

infinite cluster (connected component).
Now, the idea is that for every two vertices
x,y\in V
,
we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{p}(x\leftrightarrow\infty)

\displaystyle>\hskip 1.70709pt0\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ \mathbf{P}_{p}(y\leftrightarrow\infty)\hskip 1.70709pt>\hskip 1.70709pt0\quad\ \ \text{for all \, $p\in(0,1]$, \, since}

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{p}(x\leftrightarrow\infty)

\displaystyle\geq\hskip 1.70709pt\mathbf{P}_{p}(x\leftrightarrow y\leftrightarrow\infty)\hskip 1.70709pt\geq_{\eqref{eq:HK}}\hskip 1.70709pt\mathbf{P}_{p}(x\leftrightarrow y)\cdot\mathbf{P}_{p}(y\leftrightarrow\infty).

For the case when

is a square lattice, Harris used the HK inequality
to prove that

[

]
.
Famously, Kesten
[

established the equality

twenty years later.

1.3.
Ahlswede–Daykin inequality

Skipping over many intermediate generalizations, we now state the

AD inequality
, also called the
four functions theorem
,
see e.g.
[
AS16
,
\lx@sectionsign
6.1]
. This result is central to this paper,
both as a technical theorem and as an inspiration.

Fix integers
\ell,w\geq 1
, and let
\cX=\cX^{(\ell,w)}
denote the set of integer sequences
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})\in\{0,1,\ldots,w\}^{\ell}
.
For
\lambda,\mu\in\cX
, define the
join
(
\vee
) and
meet
(
\wedge
) operations as follows:

\lambda\vee\mu\ :=\ \big(\max\{\lambda_{1},\mu_{1}\},\ldots,\max\{\lambda_{\ell},\mu_{\ell}\}\big),\qquad\lambda\wedge\mu\ :=\ \big(\min\{\lambda_{1},\mu_{1}\},\ldots,\min\{\lambda_{\ell},\mu_{\ell}\}\big).

Under these operations,
(\cX,\vee,\wedge)
forms a distributive lattice, cf. Remark
1.3
.

Theorem 1.1
(
AD inequality

[
AD78
]
).

Fix integers
\ell,w\geq 1
, and let
\cX=\cX^{(\ell,w)}
be as above.
Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\cX\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be four functions satisfying

(
AD-cond
)

\fa({\lambda})\hskip 1.70709pt\fb({\mu})\ \leq\ \fc({\lambda\vee\mu})\hskip 1.70709pt\fd({\lambda\wedge\mu})\qquad\text{ for all \hskip 1.70709pt$\lambda,\mu\in\cX$.}

Then:

(
AD
)

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\cX}\fa(\lambda)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\cX}\fb(\lambda)\bigg)\ \leq\ \bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\cX}\fc(\lambda)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\cX}\fd(\lambda)\bigg).

The assumption (
AD-cond
) is a four function generalization of

log-supermodularity
property

(1.3)

f(x)\hskip 1.70709ptf(y)\,\leq\,f(x\vee y)\hskip 1.70709ptf(x\wedge y)\quad\text{for all}\quad x,y\in\mathcal{L},

where
\mathcal{L}
is a distributive lattice. See, e.g.,
[
Bjö11
,
SW75
]
for a discussion
of the significance of this property across combinatorics.

There are numerous combinatorial applications of the AD inequality, ranging
from extremal combinatorics to combinatorial number theory and order theory,
see e.g. monographs chapters
[
And87
,
\lx@sectionsign
6]
,
[
Bol86
,
\lx@sectionsign
19]
,
and dedicated surveys
[
FS00
,
Win86
]
. Let us single out
the
XYZ inequality
for the numbers of linear extensions

[
She82
]
(see also
[
AS16
,
\lx@sectionsign
6.4]
and
[
West21
, Thm. 12.4.41]
),
and other related correlation inequalities reviewed in
[
CP23b
,
\lx@sectionsign
14.4]
.

As we mentioned earlier, the FKG inequality is an important special
case used throughout percolation theory, see e.g.
[
BR06
,
Gri99
,
Gri06
,
Wer09
]

and
\lx@sectionsign
12.1
.
Let us emphasize that there are cases when the FKG is insufficient and
the full power of AD inequality is needed, see e.g. a discussion in

[
FS00
]
, and notable examples in
[
FDS88
,
CP23a
]
.
Finally, note that the HK inequality (
HK
) follows from (
AD
)
by taking
w=1
,

\ell=n
,
\fc=\mathbf{1}
, and setting
\fa
,
\fb
,
\fd
to be indicator functions of
\mathcal{U}
,
\cV
and
\mathcal{U}\cap\cV
, respectively.

The following generalization of the
w=1
case of the AD inequality
is both curious and essential to our work. This result was first obtained
by Reuter
[
Reu87
]
. It was later rediscovered by Lovász–Saks
[
LS04
, Cor. 4]

as a special case of their family of localization inequalities, and most recently
rediscovered again in
[
CP23a
, Claim 6.3]
.

Theorem 1.2
(
RLS inequality
).

Let
k\geq 1
,
and let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:2^{[k]}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be functions satisfying (
AD-cond
).
Then:

(
RLS
)

\sum_{S\hskip 0.85355pt\subseteq\hskip 0.85355pt[k]}\fa(S)\fb(\overline{S})\ \leq\ \sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\fc(S)\fd(\overline{S})\hskip 0.85355pt,

where
\overline{S}:=[k]\setminus S
denotes the set complement.

Remark 1.3
.

By
Birkhoff’s representation theorem

[
Bir37
]
, every finite distributive lattice is isomorphic to a sublattice of a Boolean lattice
\text{{\rm B}}_{\ell}=\cX^{(\ell,1)}
.
While the AD inequality is typically stated for finite distributive lattices,
we use this equivalent formulation as it is better adapted to the content of this paper.

1.4.
Lam–Postnikov–Pylyavskyy inequality

Schur polynomials
are symmetric polynomials which play a central
role in algebraic combinatorics, representation theory and other areas.
They are defined as irreducible characters of
{\text{\rm GL}}(N,\mathbb{C})
, and their
stable limits (
Schur functions
) form
an orthonormal basis in the ring of symmetric polynomials with respect to the Hall inner product.
We refer to
[
Ful97
,
Mac95
,
Sag01
]
and
[
Sta99
, Ch. 7]
for
standard introductions to the area, connections to other area
and numerous applications.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of integer partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)
,
and let
\lambda\vee\mu
,
\lambda\wedge\mu
be the operations on
\mathcal{P}
given
by the union and intersection of the corresponding Young diagrams.
Denote by
\fs_{\lambda}:=\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)
the
Schur function
indexed by
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)
, in variables
\xb:=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
.
For symmetric functions
\ff
and
\fg
, we write
\ff\leqslant_{\fs}\fg
if
(\fg-\ff)
is
Schur positive
, i.e.,
a nonnegative linear combination of Schur functions. Schur positivity is a
fundamental property which remains largely mysterious in many cases, see e.g.
[
Oko03
,
Sta00
]
; see also
[
Pat19
]
for a brief introduction.

Theorem 1.4
(
LPP inequality

[
LPP07
, Thm. 5]
).

For all
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, we have:

(
LPP
)

\fs_{\lambda}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\mu}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fs_{\lambda\vee\mu}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\lambda\wedge\mu}

This is a remarkable inequality and an important tool in the area, see e.g.
[
KT21
,
\lx@sectionsign
8.2]
and
[
PPY19
,
\lx@sectionsign
4.7]
.
The LPP inequality is a part of the “log-concavity phenomena” championed in recent years
by June Huh and his coauthors, see
[
Huh18
,
Kal23
]
. Let us single out a closely related
log-concavity property of structure constants, see
[
HMMS22
,
Oko03
]
and
\lx@sectionsign
12.4
.

We refer to
[
LP07a
]
for a bijective proof of the
monomial positivity
version of (
LPP
), a weaker property which still implies the

evaluation positivity
LHS
\leq
RHS,
for all
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\hskip 0.85355pt
. See
[
CP23a
]
,
for a multivariate extension of the AD inequality which also implies
the monomial positivity version of (
LPP
),
and the extensive discussion of (
LPP
)
in the context of poset inequalities (cf.
[
CP24a
,
\lx@sectionsign
4.1]
).
See also Speyer’s recent breakthrough
[
Spe26
]
, where he resolves
a conjecture from
[
DP07
]
by using the AD inequality (
AD
),
thus giving a new proof of (
LPP
) along the way.

In
[
LPP07
]
, Lam–Postnikov–Pylyavskyy proved also the Okounkov,
FFLP, and LLT inequalities, conjectured in
[
Oko97
,
FFLP05
,
LLT97
]
, respectively.
Of these, the Okounkov inequality gives log-concavity of LR coefficients and
will play an important role later in the paper (see
\lx@sectionsign
3.2
).
Lam–Postnikov–Pylyavskyy also proved the following important generalization of (
LPP
)
for skew Schur functions.

Theorem 1.5
(
skew LPP inequality
,
[
LPP07
, Thm. 5]
).

For all skew shapes
\lambda/\mu
and
\nu/\rho
, we have:

(
skew LPP
)

\fs_{\lambda/\mu}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\nu/\rho}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fs_{(\lambda\vee\nu)/(\mu\vee\rho)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{(\lambda\wedge\nu)/(\mu\wedge\rho)}

Let us emphasize that Schur positivity in (
skew LPP
) is defined
in terms of nonnegative linear combinations of the ordinary Schur functions,
rather than skew Schur functions, and that the inequality coincides with
(
LPP
) on straight shapes.

1.5.
Main results

Our first main result is a Schur positivity version of the AD inequality.
We call it the
Ahlswede–Daykin–Schur
(ADS)
inequality
.

Theorem 1.6
(
ADS inequality
).

Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be functions satisfying

(
ADS-cond
)

\fa({\lambda})\hskip 1.70709pt\fb({\mu})\ \leq\ \fc({\lambda\vee\mu})\hskip 1.70709pt\fd({\lambda\wedge\mu})\qquad\text{ for all \hskip 1.70709pt$\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}$.}

Then we have:

(
ADS
)

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fa({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fb({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}\bigg)\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fc({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fd({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}\bigg)

Note that the assumptions (
AD-cond
) and (
ADS-cond
) coincide
on partitions, but the conclusions are much stronger.
We note that the evaluation positivity

x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\geq 0
version of (
ADS
) follows immediately
from (
AD
). The monomial positivity version of (
ADS
)
would follow from the result by the first and third author
[
CP23a
, Thm. 6.1]
.
This is an intermediate notion between evaluation positivity and Schur positivity
(see
\lx@sectionsign
2.1
).

One way to see the power of the ADS inequality, is to consider the case when functions

\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd
are indicators of
\lambda,\mu,\lambda\vee\mu,\lambda\wedge\mu
,
respectively. The AD inequality becomes trivial in this case, while the ADS inequality
coincides with the LPP inequality. Thus, one can think of (
ADS
) as an
advanced extension of (
LPP
).

We now turn to the skew version of the ADS inequality. As with the skew LPP, it coincides with the ADS inequality
on straight shapes:

Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

(

\fa(\lambda,\mu)\hskip 1.70709pt\fb(\nu,\rho)\ \leq\ \fc(\lambda\vee\nu,\mu\vee\rho)\hskip 1.70709pt\fd(\lambda\wedge\nu,\mu\wedge\rho)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{ for all }\hskip 9.24994pt\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho\in\mathcal{P}.

Then we have:

1.6.
Stable Grothendieck polynomials

We now present our main application,
to the celebrated
symmetric Grothendieck polynomials
.
These are nonhomogeneous symmetric polynomials introduced by
Lascoux and Schützenberger
[
LS83
,
Las90
]
, building on
their earlier work on
Schubert polynomials
. Originally
defined as representatives of
K
-theory classes of structure
sheaves of Schubert varieties, their geometric and algebraic properties
and significance are well understood by now, see e.g.
[
Buch02
,
B+08
]
.

The
stable Grothendieck polynomials

\{\fG_{\lambda}\}
are obtained in the stable limit of Grothendieck polynomials.
3
3

3

The
name is standard in the literature and somewhat misleading as these
are symmetric functions, and thus power series rather than polynomials.

They form a basis in the ring of symmetric functions, and were introduced
by Fomin and Kirillov in an algebraic context
[
FK94
,
FK96
]
.
We use the term
Grothendieck polynomials
when the context is clear.

It is hard to overstate the importance of Grothendieck polynomials
in modern algebraic combinatorics. We refer to
[
Buch05
]

for a brief introduction, to
[
Kir16
]
for an in-depth overview
of algebraic generalizations, and to
[
BKTY05
,
TY09a
]
for
combinatorial formulas and algorithms. We postpone the definitions
until
\lx@sectionsign
2.5
, and proceed to state the main application.

First, we modify Grothendieck polynomials to make them monomial positive:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\tG_{\lambda}(\textbf{{x}})\ :=\ (-1)^{|\lambda|}\hskip 1.70709ptG_{\lambda}(-\textbf{{x}})\hskip 0.85355pt.

Our main application is the following
LPPG inequality
, originally conjectured by Mihalcea
[

]
.

Theorem 1.8
(

).

For all partitions
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, we have:

\tG_{\lambda}\hskip 1.70709pt\tG_{\mu}\,\leqslant_{\fs}\,\tG_{\lambda\vee\mu}\hskip 1.70709pt\tG_{\lambda\wedge\mu}\hskip 0.85355pt.

Taking the lowest degree terms in (

) gives (

).
Let us emphasize that even the evaluation positivity was open until now.
Note also that we only prove the Schur positivity, while the

Grothendieck positivity
remains open:

(

, see also

For all partitions
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, we have:

Here by

is a nonnegative sum of
positive Grothendieck polynomials
\tG_{\nu}\hskip 0.85355pt
. This is a stronger property than

so one can think of Theorem

We show in Section

1.7.
Dual stable Grothendieck polynomials

Recall that Schur functions are self-dual w.r.t. the

Hall inner product

\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\mu}\rangle=\delta_{\lambda\mu}\hskip 0.85355pt
, while the stable
Grothendieck polynomials are not. The
dual stable Grothendieck polynomials

\{\fG_{\lambda}^{\ast}\}
form a basis in the ring of symmetric
functions
\Lambda
that is orthogonal dual to the stable Grothendieck polynomials,
and has its own interesting combinatorial properties.

This family of symmetric functions was defined by Lam–Pylyavskyy in
[
LP07b
]

from a Hopf algebraic point of view. We refer to
[
Gal17
,
Yel17
]
for further
background and combinatorial formulas. We postpone the definitions
until
\lx@sectionsign
2.6
. For now, we state a dual version of
Theorem
1.8
.

Theorem 1.10
.

For all partitions
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, we have:

\fG_{\lambda}^{*}\hskip 1.70709pt\fG_{\mu}^{*}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fG_{\lambda\vee\mu}^{*}\hskip 1.70709pt\fG_{\lambda\wedge\mu}^{*}.

Again, note that we only prove Schur positivity. The proof
is given in Section
10
. Along the way, we prove
log-supermodularity of the numbers of certain
elegant fillings

(Lemma
10.2
).

1.8.
Shadow Schur functions

Fundamentally, the reason our skew ADS inequality (Theorem
1.7
)
is applicable to the stable and dual stable Grothendieck polynomials, is because
there are well known combinatorial expansions of these families
in the basis of Schur functions. Unfortunately, proofs of both
Theorem
1.8
and
1.10
are somewhat
technical, obscuring the underlying philosophy.

To clarify these applications, we introduce a new family of
symmetric functions, where the applications of the
skew ADS inequality is straightforward.
Fix integers
k\geq\ell\geq 1
and denote by
\mathcal{P}^{\ell}
the set of partitions with at most
\ell
parts.
Define a
shadow Schur function
as a sum of Schur functions
of partitions with at most
k
rows, of which the first
\ell
rows are fixed:

(1.6)

\fS_{\lambda}^{(k,\ell)}\,:=\,\sum_{\mu\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\small{\text{\rm$\mathbb{Y}$}}(\lambda;k,\ell)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\mu}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell},\ \ \small{\text{\rm$\mathbb{Y}$}}(\lambda;k,\ell)\,:=\,\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{k}\hskip 1.70709pt:\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda_{1}=\mu_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell}=\mu_{\ell}\}.

This family contains the (usual) Schur functions for
k=\ell
, and is interesting in its own right.

In Section
3
, we state and prove both the usual and skew versions of log-supermodularity for shadow Schur functions, modeled after Theorems
1.8
and
1.10
above. We also present a roadmap for generalizations in this setting.

2.
Definitions and notation

2.1.
Basic notations

We use
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}=\{1,2,\ldots\}

to denote the sets of nonnegative and positive integers. Let

[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}=\{x\geq 0\}
.
We use
2^{X}
to denote the set of subsets of
X
.
The indicator function of a subset
A
is denoted
\mathbf{1}_{A}\hskip 0.85355pt
.

For an inequality
a\geq b
, the difference
(a-b)
is called the
defect
. For polynomials
f,g\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots]
,
we write
f\geqslant g
if the defect
(f-g)\geq 0
for all
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\geq 0
. We write
f\geqslant_{\fm}g
if
(f-g)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots]
. These properties are called the
evaluation

and the
monomial positivity
, respectively.

2.2.
Partitions

Throughout the paper, a
partition
is an infinite nonincreasing sequence

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)
with bounded support. We use
\ell(\lambda)
to denote the support size, i.e., the number of nonzero parts in
\lambda
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of partitions, and let
\mathcal{P}^{\ell}
denote the set of partitions with at most
\ell
nonzero parts.
These are not to be confused with
set partitions

X=B_{1}\sqcup B_{2}\sqcup\ldots

When writing partitions, we omit the infinite
tail of zeros, and write only the prefix with the support of the sequence,
so e.g.
(4,3,1,0,0,\ldots)
is written as
(4,3,1)
.
For convenience, we use both the sequence and word notation,
so for example
(4,3,1)
and
431
correspond to the same
partition.

Denote by
|\lambda|:=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\ldots
the
size
of the partition
\lambda
.
A
conjugate partition

\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime},\ldots)
is defined by
\lambda_{i}^{\prime}:=|\{j\hskip 1.70709pt:\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda_{j}\geq i\}|
.
As in the introduction, let

\lambda\vee\mu\ :=\ \big(\max\{\lambda_{1},\mu_{1}\},\max\{\lambda_{2},\mu_{2}\},\ldots\big),\qquad\lambda\wedge\mu\ :=\ \big(\min\{\lambda_{1},\mu_{1}\},\min\{\lambda_{2},\mu_{2}\},\ldots\big).

Clearly, these operations coincide with union and intersection of the corresponding
Young diagram, as defined in the introduction. Note that under these operations,

(\mathcal{P},\vee,\wedge)
forms an infinite distributive lattice.

Next, we define two operations on all partitions
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}
:

\big\lceil\tfrac{\lambda+\mu}{2}\big\rceil\ :=\ \big(\big\lceil\tfrac{\lambda_{1}+\mu_{1}}{2}\big\rceil,\big\lceil\tfrac{\lambda_{2}+\mu_{2}}{2}\big\rceil,\ldots\big),\qquad\big\lfloor\tfrac{\lambda+\mu}{2}\big\rfloor\ :=\ \big(\big\lfloor\tfrac{\lambda_{1}+\mu_{1}}{2}\big\rfloor,\big\lfloor\tfrac{\lambda_{2}+\mu_{2}}{2}\big\rfloor,\ldots\big).

Clearly, when all parts
(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})
are even, these two partitions coincide.

Finally, for partitions
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}
,
let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{2\ell}
be a partition obtained by sorting the
combined parts of
\lambda
and
\mu
in weakly decreasing order.
We define:

\sort_{1}(\lambda,\mu)\,:=\,(\nu_{1},\nu_{3},\ldots),\qquad\sort_{2}(\lambda,\mu)\,:=\,(\nu_{2},\nu_{4},\ldots).

Note also that this sorting operation is conjugate to the averaging operation
above.

2.3.
Young diagrams and Young tableaux

We refer to
[
Mac95
]
and
[
Sta99
, Ch. 7]
for standard definitions and notation
in algebraic combinatorics.
A
Young diagram
corresponding to partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)
is the set of squares

\big\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\hskip 1.70709pt:\hskip 1.70709pt1\leq j\leq\lambda_{i},\hskip 0.85355pt1\leq i\leq\ell(\lambda)\big\}.

By a mild abuse of notation, we use
\lambda
to also
denote the corresponding Young diagram, and refer to it as the
straight shape
.
Note that the Young diagram of a conjugate partition
\lambda^{\prime}
is obtained by the
x\leftrightarrow y
reflection.
We use
\varnothing
to denote empty Young diagram corresponding to the
\mathbf{0}
sequence.

Let
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots)
be a partition such that
\mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
. The difference of Young diagrams
is denoted by
\lambda/\mu
and called the
skew Young diagram
of shape
\lambda/\mu
, or simply the
skew shape

\lambda/\mu
.
We use
|\lambda/\mu|:=|\lambda|-|\mu|
to denote the number of squares in
\lambda/\mu
.

Let
A:\lambda/\mu\to\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
be a function which increases in rows and strictly
increases in columns. We think of
A
as a Young tableau with integers written
in squares of
\lambda/\mu
. Such
A
is called a
semistandard Young tableau
.
The set of such tableaux is denoted
\operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda/\mu)
. We use
\operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda/\mu,t)
to denote semistandard Young tableaux with entries
\leq t
.

The
weight
of a tableau
A\in\operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda/\mu)
is a sequence
\big(m_{1}(A),m_{2}(A),\ldots\big)
, where
m_{i}(A):=|A^{-1}(i)|
is the number
of
i
’s in
A
.
Kostka number

K_{\lambda,\mu}
is the number of

A\in\operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda)
of weight
\mu
.

2.4.
Schur functions

Skew Schur polynomial
is a symmetric polynomial associated with the skew shape
\lambda/\mu
and can be defined as

(2.1)

\fs_{\lambda/\mu}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\,=\,\sum_{A\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda/\mu,\hskip 0.85355ptn)}\hskip 1.70709ptz_{1}^{m_{1}(A)}\hskip 0.85355pt\cdots\hskip 1.70709ptz_{n}^{m_{n}(A)}\hskip 1.70709pt.

They form a linear basis
in the space
\Lambda_{n}
of all symmetric polynomials in
n
variables. Here we include
only combinatorial definitions, rather than the (more standard) determinantal definition.

Similarly,

skew Schur functions
are defined as

(2.2)

\fs_{\lambda/\mu}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots)\,=\,\sum_{A\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda/\mu)}\hskip 1.70709ptz_{1}^{m_{1}(A)}\hskip 1.70709ptz_{2}^{m_{2}(A)}\hskip 0.85355pt\cdots

They are the stable limits of Schur polynomials as
n\to\infty
. Note that Schur functions

\fs_{\lambda/\mu}
are homogenous of degree
|\lambda/\mu|
.

Recall that Schur functions
\fs_{\lambda}
corresponding to straight shape
\mu=\varnothing
form a linear basis in the
ring of symmetric functions

\Lambda=\varprojlim\Lambda_{n}
, where
\Lambda_{n}=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{S_{n}}
.
It is convenient to extend the definition of functions
\fs_{\lambda}
to arbitrary infinite integer sequences
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
.
We adopt the convention that
\fs_{\lambda}=0
when
\lambda
is not a partition.

The
Littlewood–Richardson coefficients
are the structure constants
of multiplication of Schur functions:

\fs_{\mu}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\nu}\ =\ \sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709ptc^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\lambda}\hskip 0.85355pt.

Taking the degrees shows that
c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}=0
unless
|\lambda|=|\mu|\hskip 0.85355pt+\hskip 0.85355pt|\nu|
.

2.5.
Grothendieck polynomials

An
increasing tableau
of skew shape
\lambda/\mu
is a function

A:\lambda/\mu\to\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
which is strictly increasing in
rows and down columns, and such that every entry in the
i
-th row is
restricted to
\{1,2,\ldots,i-1\}
. Denote by
{\text{\rm IT}}(\lambda/\mu)
the set of such tableaux, and let
\fg_{\mu,\lambda}:=|{\text{\rm IT}}(\lambda/\mu)|
be
the number of such tableaux (note the order reversal in the index).

Denote by
\widehat{\lambda}
the unique maximal partition satisfying
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}+i-1
for all
i\geq 1
.
Denote
\mathcal{R}(\lambda):=\big\{\mu\hskip 1.70709pt:\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda\subseteq\mu\subseteq\widehat{\lambda}\big\}
.
For a partition
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
, the
stable Grothendieck polynomial
is defined as

(2.3)

\fG_{\lambda}(\textbf{{x}})\,:=\,\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{R}(\lambda)}(-1)^{|\mu|-|\lambda|}\hskip 1.70709pt\fg_{\lambda,\mu}\hskip 0.85355pt\fs_{\mu}(\textbf{{x}})\hskip 0.85355pt.

These functions form a basis in the ring of symmetric functions
\Lambda
.
Note that just like Schur functions, the stable Grothendieck polynomials are formal power series in
variables
\textbf{{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
.

There are several other equivalent definitions of
\fG_{\lambda}
, and the definition
presented here is due to Lenart
[
Len00
, Thm. 2.8]
. For an alternative combinatorial
formula for
\fG_{\lambda}
in terms of set-valued tableaux, see
[
Buch02
,
\lx@sectionsign
3]

and
[
MPS21
, Cor. 3.11]
. Yet another combinatorial definition in terms of standardized
increasing tableaux is given in
[
McN06
]
in a more general context of
factorial
Grothendieck polynomials
.

As in (
1.4
), it will be convenient for us to define the following positive
version of stable Grothendieck polynomials:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\tG_{\lambda}(\textbf{{x}})\,:=\,(-1)^{|\lambda|}G_{\lambda}(-\textbf{{x}})\,=\,\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{R}(\lambda)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fg_{\lambda,\mu}\hskip 0.85355pt\fs_{\mu}(\textbf{{x}})\hskip 0.85355pt.

2.6.
Dual stable Grothendieck polynomials

An
elegant filling
of the skew shape
\lambda/\mu
is a semistandard Young tableau
A\in\operatorname{SSYT}(\lambda/\mu)
subject to the condition that every entry in row
i
belongs to the set

\{1,2,\ldots,i-1\}
, for all
i\geq 1
. We use
{\text{\rm EF}}(\lambda/\mu)
to denote the set of elegant fillings, and let
\fg^{*}_{\lambda,\mu}:=|{\text{\rm EF}}(\lambda/\mu)|
denotes the number of elegant
fillings of the skew shape
\lambda/\mu
.

For partition
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
,
the
dual stable Grothendieck polynomial
is defined as

\fG^{*}_{\lambda}(\textbf{{x}})\ :=\ \sum_{\mu\subseteq\lambda}\hskip 1.70709pt\fg^{*}_{\lambda,\mu}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\mu}(\textbf{{x}}).

These functions also form a basis in the ring of symmetric functions
\Lambda(\textbf{{x}})
.

3.
Shadow Schur functions

In this section we introduce a new family of
shadow Schur functions
which generalize the ordinary Schur functions. We then present extensions
of LPP and skew LPP inequalities, which follow easily from the ADS inequalities.
We then state without proof several other related inequalities for shadow Schur
functions.

3.1.
Shadow LPP inequality

Fix integers
k\geq\ell\geq 1
and note that
\small{\text{\rm$\mathbb{Y}$}}(\lambda;k,\ell)=\{\lambda\}
when
k=\ell
, or when
k>\ell
and
\lambda_{\ell}=0
. Recall the definition
(
1.6
) of shadow Schur functions given in the introduction.

Theorem 3.1
(
shadow LPP inequality
).

For all
k\geq\ell\geq 1
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}
, we have:

(sLPP)

\fS_{\lambda}^{(k,\ell)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fS_{\mu}^{(k,\ell)}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fS_{\lambda\vee\mu}^{(k,\ell)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fS_{\lambda\wedge\mu}^{(k,\ell)}.

Note that the original LPP inequality (
LPP
) is a special case of
(
sLPP
), where
k=\ell
.
We define
skew shadow Schur functions
as follows:

(3.1)

\fS^{(k,\ell)}_{\lambda/\mu}\ :=\ \sum_{\nu\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\small{\text{\rm$\mathbb{Y}$}}(\lambda;k,\ell),\hskip 1.70709pt\rho\in\small{\text{\rm$\mathbb{Y}$}}(\mu;k,\ell)}\fs_{\nu/\rho}\hskip 1.70709pt,

the sum over all skew shapes whose first
\ell
row lengths are specified by
\lambda
and
\mu
.
The following theorem extends (
sLPP
) to the skew setting:

Theorem 3.2
(
shadow skew LPP inequality
).

For all
\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}
, we have:

\fS_{\lambda/\mu}^{(k,\ell)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fS_{\nu/\rho}^{(k,\ell)}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fS_{(\lambda\vee\nu)/(\mu\vee\rho)}^{(k,\ell)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fS_{(\lambda\wedge\nu)/(\mu\wedge\rho)}^{(k,\ell)}.

Proof.

Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\mathcal{P}^{k}\times\mathcal{P}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be given by

\displaystyle\fa(\alpha,\beta)\

\displaystyle:=\ \mathbf{1}_{\{\alpha_{i}=\lambda_{i},\hskip 1.70709pt\beta_{i}=\mu_{i}\ \text{ for all }i\in[\ell]\}}

\displaystyle\fb(\alpha,\beta)\

\displaystyle:=\ \mathbf{1}_{\{\alpha_{i}=\nu_{i},\hskip 1.70709pt\beta_{i}=\rho_{i}\ \text{ for all }i\in[\ell]\}}

\displaystyle\fc(\alpha,\beta)\

\displaystyle:=\ \mathbf{1}_{\{\alpha_{i}=\max\{\lambda_{i},\nu_{i}\},\hskip 1.70709pt\beta_{i}=\max\{\mu_{i},\rho_{i}\}\ \text{ for all }i\in[\ell]\}}

\displaystyle\fd(\alpha,\beta)\

\displaystyle:=\ \mathbf{1}_{\{\alpha_{i}=\min\{\lambda_{i},\nu_{i}\},\hskip 1.70709pt\beta_{i}=\min\{\mu_{i},\rho_{i}\}\ \text{ for all }i\in[\ell]\}}

It is straightforward to verify that these functions satisfy (
skew ADS-cond
), and that

\displaystyle\fS^{(k,\ell)}_{\lambda/\mu}\ =\ \sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}^{k}}\fa(\alpha,\beta)\fs_{\alpha/\beta}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\fS^{(k,\ell)}_{\nu/\rho}\ =\ \sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}^{k}}\fb(\alpha,\beta)\fs_{\alpha/\beta}\,,

\displaystyle\fS^{(k,\ell)}_{(\lambda\vee\nu)/(\mu\vee\rho)}\ =\ \sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}^{k}}\fc(\alpha,\beta)\fs_{\alpha/\beta}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\fS^{(k,\ell)}_{(\lambda\wedge\nu)/(\mu\wedge\rho)}\ =\ \sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}^{k}}\fd(\alpha,\beta)\fs_{\alpha/\beta}\,.

The theorem now follows from the skew ADS inequality (Theorem
1.7
).
∎

3.2.
Further Schur positive inequalities

The following two inequalities were conjectured by Okounkov
[
Oko01
, p. 269]

and Fomin–Fulton–Li–Poon
[
FFLP05
, Conj. 2.7]
. They were also proved
by Lam–Postnikov–Pylyavskyy
[
LPP07
, Thm. 4]
.

Theorem 3.3
(
Okounkov inequality
).

For all
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, we have:

(Ok)

\fs_{\lambda}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\mu}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fs_{\left\lceil(\lambda+\mu)/2\right\rceil}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\left\lfloor(\lambda+\mu)/2\right\rfloor}.

Theorem 3.4
(
FFLP inequality
).

For all
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, we have:

(FFLP)

\fs_{\lambda}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\mu}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fs_{\sort_{1}(\lambda,\mu)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\sort_{2}(\lambda,\mu)}.

Lam–Postnikov–Pylyavskyy proved further Schur positive inequalities
which fall outside the scope of this paper. They also stated the

LPP conjecture
, see
[
DP07
, Conj. 1]
, which was
recently proved by Speyer
[
Spe26
]
. Somewhat surprisingly,
Speyer observed that his proof implies a general statement about
Schur positivity:

Theorem 3.5
(
Speyer
[
Spe26
, Rem. 4.19]
).

Let
f:\mathcal{P}^{\ell}\to\Lambda
such that
f(\lambda+\mathbf{1})=f(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}
,
and

f(\lambda)\hskip 0.85355ptf(\mu)\,\leqslant_{\fs}\,f(\lambda\vee\mu)\hskip 0.85355ptf(\lambda\wedge\mu)\quad\forall\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}.

Then:

f(\lambda)\hskip 0.85355ptf(\mu)\,\leqslant_{\fs}\,f\big(\big\lceil\tfrac{\lambda+\mu}{2}\big\rceil\big)\hskip 1.70709ptf\big(\big\lfloor\tfrac{\lambda+\mu}{2}\big\rfloor\big)\quad\forall\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}.

In particular, Speyer’s theorem shows that the LPP inequality (
LPP
)
implies both (
Ok
) and, after conjugation, the inequality (
FFLP
).
We conclude with a skew version of (
Ok
) which was also proved by
Lam–Postnikov–Pylyavskyy.

Theorem 3.6
(
skew Okounkov inequality

[
LPP07
, Thm 11]
).

For all
\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho\in\mathcal{P}
, we have:

\fs_{\lambda/\mu}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\nu/\rho}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fs_{\left\lceil(\lambda+\nu)/2\right\rceil/\left\lceil(\mu+\rho)/2\right\rceil}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\left\lfloor(\lambda+\nu)/2\right\rfloor/\left\lfloor(\mu+\rho)/2\right\rfloor}.

3.3.
New inequalities for shadow Schur functions

As a consequence of Theorem

, we obtain the shadow generalization of
the skew Okounkov inequality:

Theorem 3.7
(
shadow skew Okounkov inequality
).

For all
k\geq\ell\geq 1
and
\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}

\fS^{(k,\ell)}_{\lambda/\mu}\hskip 1.70709pt\fS_{\nu/\rho}^{(k,\ell)}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fS^{(k,\ell)}_{\left\lceil(\lambda+\nu)/2\right\rceil/\left\lceil(\mu+\rho)/2\right\rceil}\hskip 1.70709pt\fS^{(k,\ell)}_{\left\lfloor(\lambda+\nu)/2\right\rfloor/\left\lfloor(\mu+\rho)/2\right\rfloor}.

We state Theorem

without proof, since it closely
parallels that of Theorem
3.6
given in
[

]
,

,

Corollary 3.8
(

For all
k\geq\ell\geq 1
and

\fS_{\lambda}^{(k,\ell)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fS^{(k,\ell)}_{\mu}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fS^{(k,\ell)}_{\left\lceil(\lambda+\mu)/2\right\rceil}\hskip 1.70709pt\fS^{(k,\ell)}_{\left\lfloor(\lambda+\mu)/2\right\rfloor}.

Let us also mention that the FFLP inequality also holds for shadow Schur functions:

Theorem 3.9
(
shadow FFLP inequality
).

For all
k\geq\ell\geq 1
and

\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}
, such that

\fS_{\lambda}^{(k,\ell)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fS_{\mu}^{(k,\ell)}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fS_{\sort_{1}(\lambda,\mu)}^{(k,\ell)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fS_{\sort_{2}(\lambda,\mu)}^{(k,\ell)}.

By the argument as above, this inequality extends (
FFLP
).
We state this result without proof, since it closely parallels that
of a skew version of (
FFLP
) given in
[
LPP07
, Cor. 12]
.
Here we substitute the shadow Okounkov inequality (Corollary
3.8
)
for the original version (Theorem
3.3
).

Remark 3.10
.

We note that the condition
\lambda_{\ell}=\mu_{\ell}
in Theorem
3.9
,
which is absent in (

), is necessary for this generalization.
To see this, let
k=3
,
\ell=2
,
\lambda=(2,2)
, and
\mu=(1,1)
.
In this case,
\sort_{1}(\lambda,\mu)=\sort_{2}(\lambda,\mu)=(2,1)
, yet

\fS^{(3,2)}_{22}\fS^{(3,2)}_{11}\not\leqslant_{\fs}\fS_{21}^{(3,2)}\fS_{2,1}^{(3,2)}.

3.4.
Lower Schur functions

For a partition
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
, define
lower Schur functions

\displaystyle\fS_{\subseteq\hskip 0.85355pt\lambda}\ :=\ \sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{P},\,\mu\subseteq\lambda}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\mu}\hskip 0.85355pt.

It is easy to see that all results in this section continue to hold when
shadow Schur functions are replaced with lower Schur functions. The proofs are nearly identical;
we omit the details.

Note that lower Schur functions are perhaps more natural than shadow Schur functions.
However, in contrast with the latter, the former do not contain the ordinary Schur
functions as a special case. On the other hand, the corresponding log-supermodularity,
i.e. version of Theorem
3.1
in this case, does again generalize the LPP inequality by taking the maximal degree terms.

4.
Understanding the ADS inequality

4.1.
The role of Schur functions

It is natural to ask if Theorem
1.6
can be strengthened to the
following formal algebraic statement which omits Schur functions altogether.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{R}\langle\hskip 0.85355pt\ff_{\lambda}\hskip 1.70709pt:\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda\in\mathcal{P}\hskip 0.85355pt\rangle
be a commutative
ring. Suppose that there exists a partial order “
\preccurlyeq
” on
\mathcal{A}

that satisfies the log-supermodularity property

\ff_{\lambda}\hskip 1.70709pt\ff_{\mu}\,\preccurlyeq\,\ff_{\lambda\vee\mu}\hskip 1.70709pt\ff_{\lambda\wedge\mu}\quad\text{for all }\quad\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P},

and is closed under addition and multiplication by nonnegative scalars.
It is tempting to conjecture that these assumptions suffice to imply the inequality

(naive AD)

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fa({\lambda})\ff_{\lambda}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fb({\lambda})\ff_{\lambda}\bigg)\ \preccurlyeq\ \bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fc({\lambda})\ff_{\lambda}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fd({\lambda})\ff_{\lambda}\bigg).

Indeed, for Schur functions, (
naive AD
) and (
LPP
) immediately implies (
ADS
).

However, this naive generalization is false, as demonstrated by the following counterexample.
Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\mathcal{P}\to\{0,1\}
be given by the indicator functions

\fa\,:=\,\mathbf{1}_{\{221,22\}}\hskip 1.70709pt,\quad\fb\,:=\,\mathbf{1}_{\{311,31\}}\hskip 1.70709pt,\quad\fc\,:=\,\mathbf{1}_{\{321,32\}}\hskip 1.70709pt,\quad\fd\,:=\,\mathbf{1}_{\{211,21\}}\hskip 1.70709pt.

It is easy to verify that these functions satisfy (
AD-cond
).
The (
naive AD
) then states

\displaystyle(\ast)\qquad\ff_{221}\hskip 0.85355pt\ff_{311}\,+\,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ff_{221}\hskip 0.85355pt\ff_{31}}}\,+\,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ff_{22}\hskip 0.85355pt\ff_{311}}}\,+\,\ff_{22}\hskip 0.85355pt\ff_{31}\ \preccurlyeq\ \ff_{321}\hskip 0.85355pt\ff_{211}\,+\,{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\ff_{321}\hskip 0.85355pt\ff_{21}}}\,+\,{\ff_{32}\hskip 0.85355pt\ff_{211}}\,+\,\ff_{32}\hskip 0.85355pt\ff_{21}\hskip 0.85355pt.

Observe that the LHS contains the terms
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ff_{221}\ff_{31}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ff_{22}\ff_{311}}
.
In contrast, the only term on the RHS that dominates these under
\preccurlyeq
is
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\ff_{321}\ff_{21}}
. This provides a counterexample to (
naive AD
).

On the other hand, the inequality
(\ast)
does hold for Schur functions
\ff_{\lambda}=\fs_{\lambda}
. This is justified by the following relations:

\displaystyle\fs_{221}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{311}\,\leqslant_{\fs}\,\fs_{321}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{211}\hskip 0.85355pt,\ \ \quad\fs_{22}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{31}\,\leqslant_{\fs}\,\fs_{32}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{21}\hskip 0.85355pt,\ \ \quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\fs_{221}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{31}}\hskip 1.70709pt+\hskip 1.70709pt{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\fs_{22}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{311}}\,\leqslant_{\fs}\,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\fs_{321}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{21}}\hskip 1.70709pt+\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{32}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{211}.

Here the first two inequalities are immediate consequences of (
LPP
).
By contrast, the third inequality is an special case of a new family of
Schur inequalities, which we call the
Schur duet inequalities

(Corollary
5.3
).

4.2.
Losing Schur functions

It is natural to ask whether (
AD
)
is a special case of (
ADS
).
While we are not aware of such implication, we can deduce
it from the more general (
skew ADS
),
as follows.

By Birkhoff’s representation theorem
[
Bir37
]
, every finite
distributive lattice is isomorphic to a sublattice of a Boolean lattice, cf. Remark
1.3
.
Therefore, to prove the full
AD inequality, it suffices to establish it for the special case where the
underlying lattice is the Boolean lattice
\text{{\rm B}}_{\ell}=2^{[\ell]}
.
For a subset
E\in 2^{[\ell]}
, let
\lambda:=\lambda(E)=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
be a partition given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\ :=\ \begin{cases}\ell-i+1&\text{ if }\ i\in E,\\
\ell-i&\text{ if }\ i\notin E.\end{cases}

Let
\mu:=(\ell-1,\ell-2,\dots,1,0)
.
Note that
\lambda/\mu
consists of a collection of disjoint boxes.
Therefore,
\fs_{\lambda/\mu}(1,0,0,\ldots)=1.

Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\text{{\rm B}}_{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be four functions
satisfying the assumptions (
AD-cond
) in the AD inequality.
Define function
\fa^{\prime}:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

\fa^{\prime}(\nu,\rho)\ :=\ \begin{cases}\hskip 1.70709pt\fa(\lambda,\mu)&\text{ if }\ \nu=\lambda(E)\ \text{ for some }\ E\in 2^{[\ell]}\ \text{ and }\ \rho=\mu,\\
\hskip 1.70709pt0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let functions
\fb^{\prime},\fc^{\prime},\fd^{\prime}:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be defined analogously.
It is straightforward to verify that these four functions
\fa^{\prime},\fb^{\prime},\fc^{\prime},\fd^{\prime}
satisfy (
skew ADS-cond
).
Also note that,

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu,\rho\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709pt\fa^{\prime}(\nu,\rho)\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\nu/\rho}(1,0,0,\ldots)\ =\ \sum_{E\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt2^{[\ell]}}\hskip 1.70709pt\fa(E),

with analogous identities holding for
\fb^{\prime},\hskip 0.85355pt\fc^{\prime}
and
\fd^{\prime}
.
By the Schur positivity in (
skew ADS
), evaluating both sides
at
(1,0,0,\dots)
gives

\bigg(\sum_{E\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt2^{[\ell]}}\fa(E)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{E\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt2^{[\ell]}}\fb(E)\bigg)\ \leq\ \bigg(\sum_{E\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt2^{[\ell]}}\fc(E)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{E\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt2^{[\ell]}}\fd(E)\bigg).

This is the desired AD inequality on
\text{{\rm B}}_{\ell}\hskip 0.85355pt
.

5.
Orchestra inequalities

In this section we introduce the
AD cone
and the
orchestra inequalities
,
generalizing the
duet inequalities
. This is the main technical tool we need to
prove Schur positive inequalities.

5.1.
Ahlswede–Daykin cone

Fix a positive integer
\ell
.
Let
\mathscr{E}
and
\mathscr{F}
be two disjoint copies of
[\ell]:=\{1,2,\ldots,\ell\}
.
Formally, we may regard
\mathscr{E}:=[\ell]\times\{0\}
and
\mathscr{F}:=[\ell]\times\{1\}
to distinguish them.
However, to avoid making the notation overly heavy, we will refer to elements of
\mathscr{E}
simply by the index
i
and elements of
\mathscr{F}
by the index
j
.
Throughout this paper, we use the disjoint union symbol
\sqcup
to emphasize that elements are distinguished by their origin, even when they share the same numerical value.

Consider a function
\mathcal{O}:2^{\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
For every set partition
\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{t}\}
of
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
,
we say that
\mathcal{O}
satisfies an
orchestra inequality
, if

(Orch)

\sum_{H\hskip 0.85355pt\subseteq\hskip 0.85355pt[t]}\hskip 1.70709pt\mathcal{O}\bigg(\bigsqcup_{h\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355ptH}\hskip 1.70709ptB_{h}\bigg)\ \geq\ 0.

Here, the sum runs over all
2^{t}
subsets of
[t]=\{1,\dots,t\}
.
4
4

4

We
use the term “orchestra” because it aggregates the value of the function
\mathcal{O}

over every possible subset of partition part, akin to how an orchestra’s collective
output is defined by the combination of all its individual sections.

Define
Ahlswede–Daykin
(AD)
cone

\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
as the set of functions satisfying
orchestra inequalities for all set partitions of
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
.

Example 5.1
.

For
\ell=2
, the set
\mathcal{E}\sqcup\mathcal{F}
has 4 elements,
resulting in 15 possible partitions. The structure of the orchestra
inequality depends entirely on the number of blocks in the chosen
partition. There are the four possible cases:

Case 1. One-block partition:
\{\mathcal{E}\sqcup\mathcal{F}\}
:

There is 1 such partition. The inequality aggregates
2
terms:

\mathcal{O}(\varnothing)+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E}\sqcup\mathcal{F})\geq 0.

Case 2. Two-block partitions:
\{B_{1},B_{2}\}
:

There are 7 such partitions. For each, the inequality aggregates

2^{2}
terms:

\mathcal{O}(\varnothing)+\mathcal{O}(B_{1})+\mathcal{O}(B_{2})+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E}\sqcup\mathcal{F})\geq 0.

Case 3. Three-block partitions:
\{B_{1},B_{2},B_{3}\}
:

There are 6 such partitions. For each, the inequality aggregates

2^{3}
terms:

\mathcal{O}(\varnothing)+\mathcal{O}(B_{1})+\mathcal{O}(B_{2})+\mathcal{O}(B_{3})+\mathcal{O}(B_{1}\sqcup B_{2})+\mathcal{O}(B_{1}\sqcup B_{3})+\mathcal{O}(B_{2}\sqcup B_{3})+\mathcal{O}(\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F})\ \geq\ 0.

Case 4. Four-block partition:
\{B_{1},B_{2},B_{3},B_{4}\}
:

There is 1 such partition, where every element is its own block.
The inequality aggregates
2^{4}
terms:

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\mathcal{O}(\varnothing)+\mathcal{O}(B_{1})+\mathcal{O}(B_{2})+\mathcal{O}(B_{3})+\mathcal{O}(B_{4})

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(B_{1}\sqcup B_{2})+\mathcal{O}(B_{1}\sqcup B_{3})+\mathcal{O}(B_{1}\sqcup B_{4})+\mathcal{O}(B_{2}\sqcup B_{3})+\mathcal{O}(B_{2}\sqcup B_{4})+\mathcal{O}(B_{3}\sqcup B_{4})

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(B_{1}\sqcup B_{2}\sqcup B_{3})+\mathcal{O}(B_{1}\sqcup B_{2}\sqcup B_{4})+\mathcal{O}(B_{1}\sqcup B_{3}\sqcup B_{4})+\mathcal{O}(B_{2}\sqcup B_{3}\sqcup B_{4})+\mathcal{O}(\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F})\ \geq\ 0.

Intuitively, the definition of this cone captures the essential inequalities
derivable from the constraints (
AD-cond
) in the Ahlswede–Daykin Theorem
1.1
.
Note that the cone definition incorporates both
\mathscr{E}
and
\mathscr{F}
to accommodate
the
skew
version of the ADS inequality.
If we are only aiming for the straight version,
the cone definition can be restricted only to
\mathscr{E}
.

5.2.
Schur orchestra inequality

For convenience, we extend the definition of skew Schur functions
\fs_{\lambda/\mu}
to arbitrary vectors
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
.
We adopt the convention that
\fs_{\lambda/\mu}:=0
if either
\lambda
or
\mu

is not a partition, or if
\mu\not\subseteq\lambda
.

For
E\subseteq\mathscr{E}
and
F\subseteq\mathscr{F}
,
let

(5.1)

\overline{E}\ :=\ \mathscr{E}\setminus E,\quad\overline{F}\ :=\ \mathscr{F}\setminus F\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{E\sqcup F}\hskip 0.85355pt:=\hskip 0.85355pt\overline{E}\hskip 0.85355pt\sqcup\hskip 0.85355pt\overline{F}\hskip 0.85355pt.

The following result gives a mechanism to lift the real-valued inequalities in (
Orch
)
to statements about Schur positivity.

Theorem 5.2
(
Schur orchestra inequality
).

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
and let
\mathcal{O}
be a function that satisfies (
Orch
).
For subsets
E\subseteq\mathscr{E}
and
F\subseteq\mathscr{F}
, let
\lambda(E)=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
and
\mu(F)=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{\ell})
in
\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, where

(5.2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\ :=\ \begin{cases}\alpha_{i}+\gamma_{i}&\text{ if }\ i\in E,\\
\alpha_{i}&\text{ if }\ i\notin E,\end{cases}

and

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}\ :=\ \begin{cases}\beta_{j}+\delta_{j}&\text{ if }\ j\in F,\\
\beta_{j}&\text{ if }\ j\notin F.\end{cases}

Then

(S-Orch)

\sum_{E\subseteq\mathscr{E},\hskip 1.70709ptF\subseteq\mathscr{F}}\mathcal{O}\big(E\sqcup F\big)\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\lambda(E)/\mu(F)}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\lambda(\overline{E})/\mu(\overline{F})}\ \geqslant_{\fs}\ 0.

The sum in (
S-Orch
) can be viewed as a sum over the finest partition of
\mathscr{E}\cup\mathscr{F}
as in Example
5.1
, Case 4. A generalization of (
S-Orch
) involving other partitions of
\mathscr{E}\cup\mathscr{F}
is discussed in
\lx@sectionsign
7.7
. The present formulation suffices for all subsequent applications in this paper.
In particular, Theorem
5.2
implies the following corollary.

Corollary 5.3
(
Schur duet inequality
).

Let
\lambda:=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
and
\mu:=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})
be nonnegative integer vectors of length
3
such that
\lambda\supseteq\mu
.
Then

\fs_{(\mu_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{(\lambda_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})}\,+\,\fs_{(\lambda_{1},\mu_{2},\lambda_{3})}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{(\mu_{1},\lambda_{2},\mu_{3})}\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \fs_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})}\,+\,\fs_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\mu_{3})}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\lambda_{3})}.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
5.2
as follows. Let
\ell=3
, and

\alpha\leftarrow\mu
,
\beta\leftarrow(0,0,0)
,
\gamma\leftarrow\lambda-\mu
,
\delta\leftarrow(0,0,0)
.
Then, for
E\subseteq\mathscr{E}
and
F\subseteq\mathscr{F}
, we have
\lambda(E)=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
where

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\ :=\ \begin{cases}\lambda_{i}&\text{ if }\ i\in E,\\
\mu_{i}&\text{ if }\ i\notin E,\end{cases}

and
\mu(F)=(0,0,0)
.
Define a function
\mathcal{O}:2^{\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\mathcal{O}(E\sqcup F)\ :=\ \begin{cases}1&\text{ if }E=\{1,2,3\}\text{ or }\ E=\{1,2\},\\
-1&\text{ if }E=\{2,3\}\text{ or }\ E=\{1,3\},\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that function
\mathcal{O}
does not depend on
F
in this case.
It is straightforward to verify that the function
\mathcal{O}
satisfies
the orchestra inequalities (
Orch
), and that (
S-Orch
) gives the desired inequality.
∎

6.
Temperley–Lieb algebra and immanants

In this section we give a quick review of
Temperley–Lieb immanants
,
which we use to prove orchestra inequalities in the next section.

6.1.
Temperley–Lieb algebra

For a fixed
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
, define
Temperley–Lieb algebra

\mathrm{TL}_{k}(\xi)
by the basis
t_{1},\dotsc,t_{k-1}
, subject
to the relations

\displaystyle t_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\xi t_{i},

\displaystyle\text{for }\ i=1,\dotsc,k-1,

\displaystyle t_{i}t_{j}t_{i}

\displaystyle=t_{i},

\displaystyle\text{if }\ |i-j|=1,

\displaystyle t_{i}t_{j}

\displaystyle=t_{j}t_{i},

\displaystyle\text{if }\ |i-j|\geq 2.

We refer to
[
DG26
]
for a friendly historical survey and many helpful references.

The basis elements of
\mathrm{TL}_{k}(\xi)
can be represented by planar diagrams on the vertex set
\cV\sqcup\cW
,

(6.1)

\displaystyle\cV:=\{1,2,\ldots,k\}\quad\text{and}\quad\cW:=\{k+1,k+2,\ldots,2k\},

where we use the symbol
\sqcup
to emphasize that the union is disjoint.
Each basis element
\kappa
corresponds to a perfect matching of these
2k
points via
k
non-intersecting strands. From now on these
2k
points form two rows of
k
points in each. The points in the first row are increasing from
1
to
k
, and points in the second row are decreasing from
2k
to
(k+1)
.
Equivalently, this says that there are no edge pairs
\{a,b\}
and
\{c,d\}
satisfying the crossing condition
a<c<b<d
,
see e.g.
[
TL71
]
. We denote the set of these basis elements (or equivalently,
planar diagrams
) by
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.
Note that the cardinality of
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is given by the
Catalan number

{\text{\rm Cat}}(k)=\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}
.

An example of generators of
\mathrm{TL}_{4}(2)
is shown in Figure
6.1
, and remaining basis elements in
{\mathcal{B}}_{4}
are shown in Figure
6.2
, giving
{\text{\rm Cat}}(4)=14
elements in total.

6.2.
Temperley–Lieb immanants

Following Littlewood
[
Lit40
]
and Stanley
[
Sta00
]
,
the
immanants
are polynomials in matrix entries defined
as follows.

Fix
k\geq 1
and let
S_{k}
be symmetric group on
k
elements.
Given a function
f:S_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and a
k\times k
matrix
X=(x_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
, the
f
-immanant
of
X
is the polynomial

(6.2)

\Imm_{f}(X)\ :=\ \sum_{w\in S_{k}}\hskip 1.70709ptf(w)\hskip 1.70709ptx_{1,w_{1}}\cdots x_{k,w_{k}}\hskip 1.70709pt.

From now on, let
\xi=2
. In this case, there is a natural surjection

(6.3)

\displaystyle\sigma\hskip 1.70709pt:\hskip 1.70709pt\mathbb{C}[S_{k}]\rightarrow\TL_{k}(2)\ \ \ \mbox{ with }\ \ s_{i}\mapsto t_{i}-1,\quad i\in[k-1],

where
s_{i}=(i,i+1)
is an adjacent transposition.
Since
(\kappa)_{\kappa\in{\mathcal{B}}_{k}}
forms a basis of
\TL_{k}(2)
, for each
\kappa\in{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
,
define a function
f_{\kappa}:S_{k}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(6.4)

\displaystyle\sigma(w)\hskip 1.70709pt:=\hskip 1.70709pt\sum_{\kappa\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt{\mathcal{B}}_{k}}\hskip 1.70709ptf_{\kappa}(w)\hskip 1.70709pt\kappa\hskip 0.85355pt.

Following
[
RS05
,
RS06
]
, the
Temperley–Lieb immanant
of a matrix
X
,
denoted
\Imm_{\kappa}(X)
, is defined as

(6.5)

\Imm_{\kappa}(X)\ :=\ \Imm_{f_{\kappa}}(X)\ =\ \sum_{w\in S_{k}}\hskip 1.70709ptf_{\kappa}(w)\hskip 1.70709ptx_{1,w_{1}}\hskip 1.70709pt\cdots\hskip 1.70709ptx_{k,w_{k}}\hskip 0.85355pt.

6.3.
From diagrams to multisets

Fix
\ell\geq 1
and set
k:=2\ell
. Let
\cM=\{m_{1}\leq\dots\leq m_{k}\}
and
\cN=\{n_{1}\leq\dots\leq n_{k}\}
be multisets of size
k
with maximum multiplicity
2
. For
M\subset\cM
and
N\subset\cN
, let
\overline{M}:=\cM\setminus M
and
\overline{N}:=\cN\setminus N
. We extend this notation to unions by
\overline{M\sqcup N}:=\overline{M}\sqcup\overline{N}
. Throughout this paper, we restrict our attention to subsets
M
and
N
of size
\ell
, such that
M,N,\overline{M}
and
\overline{N}
are
simple
, i.e., contain no repeated elements.

Let
\kappa\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
be a Temperley–Lieb diagram on the vertex set
\cV\sqcup\cW
.
We identify the multiset
\cM
with
\cV
, and
\cN
with
\cW
,
via a bijection
\psi\colon\cM\sqcup\cN\to\cV\sqcup\cW
.
This bijection is defined by

(6.6)

\psi(x)\ :=\ \begin{cases}i&\text{if }x=m_{i}\in\cM,\\
2k+1-j&\text{if }x=n_{j}\in\cN.\end{cases}

For any subset
V\sqcup W\subseteq\cV\sqcup\cW
, we define its complement by
\overline{V\sqcup W}:=(\cV\setminus V)\sqcup(\cW\setminus W)
.
Note that the map
\psi
commutes with the complement operation; that is, for any subset
M\sqcup N\subseteq\cM\sqcup\cN
,

\psi(\overline{M\sqcup N})\ =\ \overline{\psi(M\sqcup N)}.

Example 6.1
.

Our running example is by the following multisets of size 6:

\cM\ =\ \{2,4,7,8,10,11\},\qquad\cN\ =\ \{1,2,3,3,5,6\}.

Now let
M\subseteq\cM
and
N\subseteq\cN
be given by

M\ =\ \{2,10,11\}\ =\ \{m_{1},m_{5},m_{6}\},\qquad N\ =\ \{3,5,6\}\ =\ \{n_{4},n_{5},n_{6}\}.

Notice that

\overline{M}\ =\ \{4,7,8\}\ =\ \{m_{2},m_{3},m_{4}\},\qquad\overline{N}\ =\ \{1,2,3\}\ =\ \{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\},

so
M
,
N
,
\overline{M}
,
\overline{N}
are all simple subsets.
Then, applying
\psi
gives:

\displaystyle\psi(M\sqcup N)\

\displaystyle=\ \{1,5,6\}\sqcup\{13-6,13-5,13-4\}\

\displaystyle=\ \{1,5,6,7,8,9\},

\displaystyle\psi(\overline{M\sqcup N})\

\displaystyle=\ \{2,3,4\}\sqcup\{13-3,13-2,13-1\}\

\displaystyle=\ \{2,3,4,10,11,12\}.

6.4.
Compatible diagrams

We say that a diagram
\kappa
is
compatible
with
(\cM,\cN)
,
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

All duplicate labels in
\cM
are paired:
for every index
i
such that
m_{i}=m_{i+1}
, the vertices
i
and
(i+1)
are connected by an edge.

(ii)

All duplicate labels in
\cN
are paired:
for every index
j
such that
n_{j}=n_{j+1}
, the vertices
(2k+1-j)
and
(2k-j)
are connected by an edge.

Let
K\subseteq\cM\sqcup\cN
be a subset such that the intersections
K\cap\cM
and
K\cap\cN
are both simple subsets of size
\ell
.
We say that
\kappa
is
compatible
with
K
, if it is compatible with

Proof of orchestra inequalities

In this section, we introduce the definitions and auxiliary lemmas needed for the proof of the Schur orchestra inequality, and we conclude by proving the inequality itself. The main idea of the proof is to show that the left-hand side of (

7.1.
Admissible subsets

In this subsection we collect various lemmas that will be used in the proof of Theorem
5.2
.
We need to set up the following notations.
Let
\ell\geq 1
and

\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
be
as in the proposition, and are fixed throughout the rest of this section.

Recall that
\mathscr{E}
and
\mathscr{F}
are two disjoint copies of
[\ell]
.
We say that
E\subseteq\mathscr{E}
is
admissible
if both
\lambda(E)=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
and
\lambda(\overline{E})=(\lambda^{\circ}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\circ}_{\ell})
defined in (
5.2
), are partitions.
Similarly, we say that
F\subseteq\mathscr{F}
is
admissible
if both
\mu(F)=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{\ell})
and
\mu(\overline{F})=(\mu^{\circ}_{1},\ldots,\mu^{\circ}_{\ell})
defined in (
5.3
), are partitions.
Finally, we call a union
E\sqcup F\subseteq\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}

admissible
if its components
E
and
F
are individually admissible.
We denote by
\mathcal{A}
the collection of all admissible subsets of
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
.

It is straightforward to verify that
(\mathcal{A},\vee,\wedge)
forms a distributive lattice, where the lattice operations
\vee
and
\wedge
are given by set union and intersection, respectively.
We assume without loss of generality that there exists at least one admissible set, as otherwise the LHS of (
S-Orch
) vanishes and the proposition holds vacuously.

Lemma 7.1
.

The empty set
\varnothing
and the total set
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
are both admissible.
In other words, the vectors
\lambda(\mathscr{E})=\alpha+\gamma
,
\lambda(\varnothing)=\alpha
,
\mu(\mathscr{F})=\beta+\delta
,
\mu(\varnothing)=\beta
are all partitions.

Proof.

Let
E\sqcup F
be an admissible set, which exists by assumption.
By the definition of admissibility, the complement
\overline{E}\sqcup\overline{F}
is also admissible.
Observe that

\displaystyle(E\sqcup F)\vee(\overline{E}\sqcup\overline{F})

\displaystyle=(E\cup\overline{E})\sqcup(F\cup\overline{F})=\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F},

\displaystyle(E\sqcup F)\wedge(\overline{E}\sqcup\overline{F})

\displaystyle=(E\cap\overline{E})\sqcup(F\cap\overline{F})=\varnothing.

Since the family of admissible sets is a lattice (and thus closed under join and meet), it follows that
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
and
\varnothing
are admissible.
∎

We now characterize admissibility in terms of an equivalence relation on elements of
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
.

Definition 7.2
(
Admissibility equivalence relation
).

We first define an equivalence relation
\sim_{\mathscr{E}}
on the set
\mathscr{E}
, by setting
i\sim_{\mathscr{E}}i+1
.,
for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
satisfying

\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{i+1}+\gamma_{i+1},

and then taking the reflexive and transitive closure of this condition.
Note that this condition identifies indices
i
and
i+1
that must share the same membership status in
E
to prevent the partition condition from failing in either
\lambda(E)
or
\lambda(\overline{E})
.
Similarly, we define an equivalence relation
\sim_{\mathscr{F}}
on the set
\mathscr{F}
by setting
j\sim_{\mathscr{F}}j+1
for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
satisfying

\beta_{j}<\beta_{j+1}+\delta_{j+1},

and taking the reflexive and transitive closure.
Finally, we extend these to a single equivalence relation
\sim
on
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
by taking the disjoint union of
\sim_{\mathscr{E}}
and
\sim_{\mathscr{F}}
.

Lemma 7.3
.

A subset
E\sqcup F\subseteq\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
is admissible if and only if it is a union of equivalence classes of
\sim
.

Proof.

The forward implication (
\Rightarrow
) is immediate. We focus on the reverse implication (
\Leftarrow
).
Let
E\subseteq\mathscr{E}
be a union of equivalence classes of
\sim_{\mathscr{E}}
.
We will show that
\lambda(E)
is a partition, i.e.,
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
.
Clearly, the argument for
\lambda(\overline{E})
is identical by the symmetry.

First, suppose that
i\sim_{\mathscr{E}}i+1
. By the definition of
E
as a union of equivalence classes, it follows that either
\{i,i+1\}\subseteq E
or
\{i,i+1\}\cap E=\varnothing
. We have:

•

If
\{i,i+1\}\subseteq E
, then

\lambda_{i}=\alpha_{i}+\gamma_{i}\geq\alpha_{i+1}+\gamma_{i+1}=\lambda_{i+1}\hskip 1.70709pt,

where the inequality holds because
\lambda(\mathscr{E})=\alpha+\gamma
is a partition (by Lemma
7.1
).

•

If
\{i,i+1\}\cap E=\varnothing
, then

\lambda_{i}=\alpha_{i}\geq\alpha_{i+1}=\lambda_{i+1}\hskip 1.70709pt,

where the inequality holds because
\lambda(\varnothing)=\alpha
is a partition (by Lemma
7.1
).

Next, suppose that
i\not\sim_{\mathscr{E}}i+1
. By the definition of the relation
\sim_{\mathscr{E}}
, the failure of the relation implies the strict inequality condition does not hold, so we must have:

\alpha_{i}\geq\alpha_{i+1}+\gamma_{i+1}.

Consequently,
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}
regardless of whether
i
or
i+1
are in
E
, as the minimum possible value of
\lambda_{i}
, which is
\alpha_{i}
, is at least the maximum possible value of
\lambda_{i+1}
, which is
\alpha_{i+1}+\gamma_{i+1}
.

This confirms that
E
is admissible. By an analogous argument, if
F\subseteq\mathscr{F}
is a union of equivalence classes of
\sim_{\mathscr{F}}
, then
\mu_{j}\geq\mu_{j+1}
for all
j
, ensuring that

is admissible.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

E\sqcup F\subseteq\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
be an admissible set. Then
\kappa
is compatible with
E\sqcup F
if and only if every edge of
\kappa
connects a vertex in
\Psi(E\sqcup F)

to a vertex in
\overline{\Psi(E\sqcup F)}
.

Proof.

The lemma is an immediate consequence of Lemma
7.8
and the definition
of compatibility given in

.

Lemma 7.10
.

Every Temperley–Lieb diagram compatible with an admissible set
E\sqcup F
is also compatible with
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
, i.e.

\Theta(E\sqcup F)\ \subseteq\ \Theta(\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}).

The proof of this lemma is adapted from the argument in
[
LPP07
, Thm. 11]
.

Proof.

Let
\kappa\in{\mathcal{B}}_{2\ell}
be any diagram compatible with
E\sqcup F
, and let
\{a,b\}
be any edge of
\kappa
, with vertices
a<b
in the set
\cV\cup\cW=[4\ell]
.
Let
U:=\{u_{1}<\cdots<u_{2\ell}\}:=\Psi(E\sqcup F)
.
It follows from Lemma
7.9
that we can without loss of generality assume that
a=u_{i}
and
b:=\overline{u_{j}}
for some
i,j\in[2\ell]
.
Since no edges of
\kappa
can cross the edge
\{a,b\}
,
the elements of
U\cap[a+1,b-1]
are matched with the elements of
\overline{U}\cap[a+1,b-1]
.
Let

t\ :=\ |U\cap[a+1,b-1]|\ =\ |\overline{U}\cap[a+1,b-1]|.

By Lemma
7.9
, it suffices to show that one of
a,b
lies in
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{2\ell}\}
and the other lies in
\overline{P}=\{\overline{p_{1}},\ldots,\overline{p_{2\ell}}\}
. Suppose to the contrary that this is not the case.
There are now two possibilities:
First suppose that both
a,b
lie in
P
.
This implies that

a=p_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad b=p_{j}\hskip 1.70709pt.

By using the inequalities
\overline{p_{i}}<p_{i}
and
\overline{p_{j}}<p_{j}
, this implies

(7.5)

\overline{u_{i}}\ =\ \overline{a}\ <\ a\ =\ u_{i}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad u_{j}\ =\ \overline{b}\ <\ b\ =\ \overline{u_{j}}\hskip 0.85355pt.

We now estimate
t
in two ways.
First note that

\displaystyle\big|U\cap[a+1,\overline{b}-1]\big|\ \leq\ t-1,

because the interval
[a+1,\overline{b}-1]
does not contain
\overline{b}=u_{j}
, but the interval
[a+1,{b}-1]
does, as a consequence of (
7.5
).
On the other hand, we also have

\big|U\cap[a+1,\overline{b}-1]\big|\ =\ \{u_{i+1},u_{i+2},\ldots,u_{j-1}\}\ =\ j-i-1.

Hence we have

(7.6)

t-1\ \geq\ j-i-1.

Now note that

\displaystyle\big|\overline{U}\cap[\overline{a}+1,{b}-1]\big|\ \geq\ t,

as a consequence of (
7.5
).
On the other hand, we also have

\big|\overline{U}\cap[\overline{a}+1,{b}-1]\big|\ =\ \{\overline{u_{i+1}},\overline{u_{i+2}},\ldots,\overline{u_{j-1}}\}\ =\ j-i-1.

Hence we have

(7.7)

t\ \leq\ j-i-1,

which contradicts (
7.6
), as desired.
The case that both
a,b
lie in
\overline{P}
is analogous.
This completes the proof.
∎

7.5.
Compatibility equivalence relation

Let
\kappa\in{\mathcal{B}}_{2\ell}
be a Temperley–Lieb diagram compatible with
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
.
We define an equivalence relation
\sim_{\kappa}
on the set
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
via the following construction.

Definition 7.11
(
Compatibility equivalence relation
).

Let
G_{\kappa}
be the graph obtained by adding the
2\ell
edges
\{p_{i},\overline{p_{i}}\}
for all
1\leq i\leq 2\ell
, to the diagram
\hskip 0.85355pt\kappa
.
Since the original edges of
\kappa
also form a perfect matching between
P
and
\overline{P}
(by Lemma
7.9
), the graph
G_{\kappa}
is the union of two perfect matchings.
Consequently,
G_{\kappa}

decomposes into a collection of disjoint even cycles.

The equivalence relation
\sim_{\kappa}
is defined by whether the vertices in
P
corresponding to specific indices belong to the same connected component (cycle) in
G_{\kappa}
. Explicitly:

\circ
for
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathscr{E}
, let
i\sim_{\kappa}i^{\prime}
if and only if
p_{\ell+1-i}
and
p_{\ell+1-i^{\prime}}
belong to the same component,

\circ
for
j,j^{\prime}\in\mathscr{F}
, let
j\sim_{\kappa}j^{\prime}
if and only if
p_{\ell+j}
and
p_{\ell+j^{\prime}}
belong to the same component.

\circ
for mixed elements
i\in\mathscr{E}
and
j\in\mathscr{F}
,
i\sim_{\kappa}j
if and only if
p_{\ell+1-i}
and
p_{\ell+j}
belong to the same component.

Example 7.12
.

Let
\ell=3
, let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
be as in Example
7.5
, and let
E,F
be as in Example
7.7
.
Consider the diagram
\kappa\in\Theta(E\sqcup F)
in the LHS of Figure
7.2
.
In this example, the relation
\sim_{\kappa}
has three equivalence classes, namely

\{(1,0),(2,0)\},\qquad\{(3,0),(1,1),(3,1)\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\{(2,1)\},

where recall our convention that
\mathscr{E}=[\ell]\times\{0\}
and
\mathscr{F}=[\ell]\times\{1\}
.

.
(a) A diagram
\kappa
that is compatible with
E\sqcup F
, where
E,F
are as in Example
7.12
. White nodes indicate elements in
\Psi(E\sqcup F)
. (b) the graph
G_{\kappa}
, where the additional edges are colored in green.

Lemma 7.13
.

Let
\kappa\in{\mathcal{B}}_{2\ell}
be a Temperley–Lieb diagram compatible with
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
, and let
E\sqcup F\subseteq\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
be an admissible set.
Then
\kappa
is compatible with
E\sqcup F
if and only if the set
E\sqcup F
is a union of equivalence classes of
\sim_{\kappa}
.

Proof.

Let
U:=\{u_{1}<\cdots<u_{2\ell}\}:=\Psi(E\sqcup F)
.
We first prove the
(\Leftarrow)
direction.
Since
\kappa
is compatible with
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
,
it follows from Lemma
7.9
that
every edge of
\kappa
connects
p_{i}
to
\overline{p_{j}}
for some
i,j\in[2\ell]
.
It then follows from the definition of
G_{\kappa}
that
p_{i}
and
p_{j}

belongs to the same connected component of
G_{\kappa}
.
On the other hand, since
E\sqcup F
is a union of equivalence classes of
\sim_{\kappa}
,
it follows that either both
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
lie in
U
, or both
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
lie in
\overline{U}
.

In the first case, it follows from (
7.3
) that
p_{i}
lies in
U
, while

\overline{p_{j}}
lies in
\overline{U}
.
In the second case, it follows from (
7.3
) that
p_{i}
lies in
\overline{U}
,
while
\overline{p_{j}}
lies in
{U}
.
Since the edge was chosen arbitrarily, it follows that every edge of
\kappa
connects a vertex in
U
to a vertex in
\overline{U}
.
By Lemma
7.9
, this implies that
\kappa
is compatible with
E\sqcup F
.

We now prove the
(\Rightarrow)
direction:
We proceed by proving the contrapositive.
Suppose that
E\sqcup F
is not a union of equivalence classes of
\sim_{\kappa}
.
This implies that there exists an equivalence class of
\sim_{\kappa}
containing two elements with different membership statuses in
E\sqcup F
.
It then follows that there exist
i,j\in[2\ell]
such that
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
belong to the same connected component of
G_{\kappa}
, yet
p_{i}\in U
while
p_{j}\in\overline{U}
.

Since
G_{\kappa}
is a bipartite graph with parts
P
and
\overline{P}
, it follows that
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
must be connected by a path of even length in
G_{\kappa}
, where every vertex at an even position along the path belongs to
P
.
It follows that along this path, there exists a pair of vertices at distance 2 such that one belongs to
P\cap U
and the other to
P\cap\overline{U}
. By substituting the original endpoints with this pair, we may assume without loss of generality that
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
are at distance
2
in
G_{\kappa}
.
This implies that either
\{p_{i},\overline{p_{j}}\}
or
\{\overline{p_{i}},p_{j}\}
is an edge in
\kappa
. By symmetry, we assume the former.
On the other hand, since
p_{i}\in U
and
p_{j}\in\overline{U}
, it follows from (
7.3
) that
p_{i}
and
\overline{p_{j}}
both belong to
U
.
Consequently, by Lemma
7.9
, we have
\kappa
is not compatible with
E\sqcup F
, which gives us the desired contrapositive.
∎

8.
Proof of the ADS inequality

In this section, we establish the skew ADS inequality (Theorem
1.7
),
which we use to derive the ADS inequality (Theorem

8.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

Expanding the product in (
skew ADS
) and grouping the summands, we have:

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{\lambda,\hskip 0.85355pt\mu\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\fc({\lambda},\mu)\fs_{\lambda/\mu}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda,\hskip 0.85355pt\mu\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\fd({\lambda},\mu)\fs_{\lambda/\mu}\bigg)\ -\ \bigg(\sum_{\lambda,\hskip 0.85355pt\mu\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\fa({\lambda},\mu)\fs_{\lambda/\mu}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda,\hskip 0.85355pt\mu\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\fb({\lambda},\mu)\fs_{\lambda/\mu}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad=\ \sum_{\lambda,\hskip 0.85355pt\mu,\hskip 0.85355pt\nu,\hskip 0.85355pt\rho\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\big(\fc({\lambda},\mu)\fd(\nu,\rho)\ -\ \fa({\lambda},\mu)\fb(\nu,\rho)\big)\fs_{\lambda/\mu}\fs_{\nu/\rho}

\displaystyle\quad=\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\alpha,\hskip 0.85355pt\beta,\hskip 0.85355pt\gamma,\hskip 0.85355pt\delta)\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\lambda,\hskip 0.85355pt\mu,\hskip 0.85355pt\nu,\hskip 0.85355pt\rho)\end{subarray}}\hskip 1.70709pt\big(\fc(\lambda,\mu)\fd(\nu,\rho)\hskip 1.70709pt-\hskip 1.70709pt\fa(\lambda,\mu)\fb(\nu,\rho)\big)\fs_{\lambda/\mu}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\nu/\rho}.

where the outer sum is over all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
with finite support,
and the inner sum is over all
(\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho)\in\mathcal{U}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)
, where

\mathcal{U}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)\ :=\ \big\{\lambda,\mu,\nu,\rho\in\mathcal{P}\hskip 1.70709pt:\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda\vee\nu=\alpha+\gamma,\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda\wedge\nu=\alpha,\hskip 1.70709pt\mu\vee\rho=\beta+\delta,\hskip 1.70709pt\mu\wedge\rho=\beta\big\}.

Now, define
\Phi_{\fs}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)\hskip 1.70709pt:=\hskip 1.70709pt\Phi_{\fs}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta;\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd)
to be the inner sum

(8.1)

\Phi_{\fs}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)\ :=\ \sum_{\lambda,\hskip 0.85355pt\mu,\hskip 0.85355pt\nu,\hskip 0.85355pt\rho\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{U}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)}\big(\fc(\lambda,\mu)\fd(\nu,\rho)\hskip 1.70709pt-\hskip 1.70709pt\fa(\lambda,\mu)\fb(\nu,\rho)\big)\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\lambda/\mu}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\nu/\rho}.

To prove Theorem
1.7
, it suffices to show that each
\Phi_{\fs}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)
is Schur positive, which we state as Theorem
8.1
below. This completes the proof. ∎

Theorem 8.1
(
LSS inequality
).

Let
\ell\geq 1
,
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
,
let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be four functions satisfying (

), and let
\Phi_{\fs}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)
be defined by (

).
Then we have:

(LSS)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\fs}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)\ \geqslant_{\fs}\ 0.

Note that Theorem
8.1
is a Schur positive generalization
of the
Lovász–Saks inequality

[
LS06
, Thm. 8]
,
of which the RLS inequality (Theorem
1.2
)
is an easy corollary. Although somewhat technical to state,
the LSS inequality (
LSS
) is the most powerful inequality
we have in this paper.

8.2.
Proof of Theorem
8.1

The following argument combines the RLS inequality
(Theorem
1.2
) and the Schur orchestra inequalities
(Theorem
5.2
), to obtain the result.

Let
\ell
,
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
be as in Theorem
8.1
, and set
k:=2\ell
.
Let
\mathscr{E}
and
\mathscr{F}
be two disjoint copies of

.
Note that
2^{\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}}
forms a finite distributive lattice with set union as the join and set intersection as the meet.
Recall the definitions of
\lambda(E)
and
\mu(F)
for
E\subseteq\mathscr{E}
and

given in (
5.2
) and (

).
Recall the definition of admissible subsets from
\lx@sectionsign
7.1
.

We define the function
\mathcal{O}:2^{\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(E\sqcup F)\ :=\ \fc\big(\lambda(E),\mu(F)\big)\cdot\fd\big(\lambda(\overline{E}),\mu(\overline{F})\big)\,-\,\fa\big(\lambda(E),\mu(F)\big)\cdot\fb\big(\lambda(\overline{E}),\mu(\overline{F})\big)

if
E\sqcup F
is admissible, and let
\mathcal{O}(E\sqcup F):=0
otherwise.
We now verify that this function
\mathcal{O}
satisfies the orchestra inequalities (
Orch
).

Let

\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{t}\}
be a set partition of
\mathscr{E}\sqcup\mathscr{F}
.
We define the functions
\fa^{\prime},\fb^{\prime},\fc^{\prime},\fd^{\prime}\colon 2^{[t]}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
as follows. For any subset

, let

E_{H}\hskip 1.70709pt:=\hskip 1.70709pt\bigcup_{h\in H}(B_{h}\cap\mathcal{E})\quad\text{and}\quad F_{H}\hskip 1.70709pt:=\hskip 1.70709pt\bigcup_{h\in H}(B_{h}\cap\mathcal{F}).

We define
\fa^{\prime}\colon 2^{[t]}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\fa^{\prime}(H)\,:=\,\begin{cases}\fa\big(\lambda(E_{H}),\mu(F_{H})\big)&\text{if }\ \ \lambda(E_{H}),\mu(F_{H})\in\mathcal{P},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The functions
\fb^{\prime}
,
\fc^{\prime}
and
\fd^{\prime}
are defined analogously.

We now check that
\fa^{\prime},\fb^{\prime},\fc^{\prime},\fd^{\prime}
satisfies (
AD-cond
), i.e., for all

(8.3)

\fa^{\prime}(G)\fb^{\prime}(H)\ \leq\ \fc^{\prime}(G\vee H)\fd^{\prime}(G\wedge H).

Note that (

\lambda(E_{G}),\mu(F_{G}),\lambda(E_{H})
or

This implies that the vectors

and

are also partitions. Indeed, observe that

Since
\mathcal{P}
is closed under join and meet operations

the resulting vectors are partitions. Analogous arguments apply to the other vectors.
Thus in this case, (

) reduces to checking that

\displaystyle\fa\big(\lambda(E_{G}),\mu(F_{G})\big)\hskip 1.70709pt\cdot\hskip 1.70709pt\fb\big(\lambda(E_{H}),\mu(F_{H})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\ \fc\big(\lambda(E_{G})\vee\lambda(E_{H}),\mu(F_{G})\vee\mu(F_{H})\big)\hskip 1.70709pt\cdot\hskip 1.70709pt\fd\big(\lambda(E_{G})\wedge\lambda(E_{H}),\mu(F_{G})\wedge\mu(F_{H})\big),

which follows from the assumption (
skew ADS-cond
).
This completes the verification of (

).

We can now apply the RLS inequality (Theorem
1.2
) to
\fa^{\prime},\fb^{\prime},\fc^{\prime},\fd^{\prime}
.
to obtain:

(8.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{H\subseteq[t]}\fc^{\prime}(H)\fd^{\prime}(\overline{H})\hskip 1.70709pt-\hskip 1.70709pt\fa^{\prime}(H)\fb^{\prime}(\overline{H})\ \geq\ 0.

When

is admissible, the corresponding term in the sum above are equal to

\displaystyle\fc^{\prime}(H)\fd^{\prime}(\overline{H})\hskip 1.70709pt-\hskip 1.70709pt\fa^{\prime}(H)\fb^{\prime}(\overline{H})

\displaystyle\quad=\ \fc\big(\lambda(E_{H}),\mu(F_{H})\big)\fd\big(\lambda(\overline{E_{H}}),\mu(\overline{F_{H}})\big)\hskip 1.70709pt-\hskip 1.70709pt\fa\big(\lambda(E_{H}),\mu(F_{H})\big)\fb\big(\lambda(\overline{E_{H}}),\mu(\overline{F_{H}})\big)

Similarly, when

is not admissible, we have:

\displaystyle\fc^{\prime}(H)\fd^{\prime}(\overline{H})\hskip 1.70709pt-\hskip 1.70709pt\fa^{\prime}(H)\fb^{\prime}(\overline{H})\ =\ 0\ =\ \mathcal{O}\big(\bigsqcup_{h\in H}B_{h}\big).

Plugging these terms into summation (

), gives:

\displaystyle\sum_{H\subseteq[t]}\mathcal{O}\big(\bigcup_{h\in H}B_{h}\big)\ \geq\ 0.

In other words, this shows that

satisfies the orchestra inequalities (

Proof.

9.3.
Ahlswede–Daykin–Grothendieck inequality

We now give an alternative proof of Theorem
1.8
, via a different

Theorem 9.4
(
ADG inequality
).

Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be functions satisfying (

Then we have:

(ADG)

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709pt\fa({\lambda})\hskip 1.70709pt\tG_{\lambda}(\xb)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709pt\fb({\lambda})\hskip 1.70709pt\tG_{\lambda}(\xb)\bigg)\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \bigg(\sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709pt\fc({\lambda})\hskip 1.70709pt\tG_{\lambda}(\xb)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709pt\fd({\lambda})\hskip 1.70709pt\tG_{\lambda}(\xb)\bigg).

Proof.

Definition (
1.4
) implies that

\sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709pt\fa({\lambda})\hskip 1.70709pt\tG_{\lambda}(\xb)\ =\ \sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709ptf_{a}(\lambda)\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\lambda}(\xb),

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}f_{a}(\lambda)\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}f_{b}(\lambda)\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\bigg)\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \bigg(\sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}f_{c}(\lambda)\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}f_{d}(\lambda)\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\bigg).

and further explore these variations. Alas, we do not live in that world.
The details are available upon request.

11.1.
Schur measures

The ADS inequality (Theorem
1.6
) can be further generalized
to positivity results for functions on Schur measures. Namely,
let
\xb:=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
and
\textbf{{y}}:=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)
be sets of variables, and let

\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)
and
\fs_{\mu}(\textbf{{y}})

denote Schur polynomials in these sets of variables.
Recall that
\Lambda(\xb)\otimes\Lambda(\textbf{{y}})
has a basis
\mathcal{D}:=\{\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\fs_{\mu}(\textbf{{y}})\hskip 1.70709pt:\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}\}
.
For
f,g\in\Lambda(\xb)\otimes\Lambda(\textbf{{y}})
, we write
f\geqslant_{\otimes}g
if
(f-g)
has non-negative coefficients when expanded in
\mathcal{D}
.

Theorem 11.1
(
Ahlswede–Daykin inequality for Schur measures
)
.

Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be four functions satisfying (
AD-cond
).
Then we have:

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fa({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\fs_{\lambda}(\textbf{{y}})\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fb({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\fs_{\lambda}(\textbf{{y}})\bigg)\,\leqslant_{\otimes}\,\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fc({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\fs_{\lambda}(\textbf{{y}})\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fd({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\fs_{\lambda}(\textbf{{y}})\bigg).

The proof of this theorem is completely analogous to that of the ADS
inequality (Theorem
1.6
), following from a consecutive application
of Theorem
7.14
and Theorem
5.2
. We omit the details.

The theorem has a natural application to the setting of
Schur measures
.
Fix two vectors
\textbf{{{a}}}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)
,
\textbf{{{b}}}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\infty}
with finite support.
The corresponding
Schur measure
on
\mathcal{P}
, introduced in
[
Oko01
]
, is defined by

\displaystyle\Pb_{\textbf{{{a}}},\textbf{{{b}}}}[\lambda]\ :=\ \frac{1}{Z_{\ab,\bb}}\fs_{\lambda}(\ab)\fs_{\lambda}(\bb)\hskip 0.85355pt,

where
Z_{\textbf{{{a}}},\textbf{{{b}}}}
is given by the
Cauchy identity
(see, e.g.,
[
Mac95
,
Sta99
]
):

Z_{\textbf{{{a}}},\textbf{{{b}}}}\ :=\ \sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\lambda}(\textbf{{{a}}})\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\lambda}(\textbf{{{b}}})\ =\ \prod_{i,j\geq 1}\frac{1}{1-a_{i}b_{j}}\hskip 1.70709pt.

The following result by Baslingker–Krishnapur–Madiman shows
that the marginal distribution of each part is log-concave:

Theorem 11.2
(
[
BKM26
, Thm. 5]
).

For all
i\geq 1
and
k\geq 1
and any
\ab,\bb\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\infty}
,

\Pb_{\ab,\bb}[\lambda_{i}=k+1]\hskip 1.70709pt\Pb_{\ab,\bb}[\lambda_{i}=k-1]\ \leq\ \big(\Pb_{\ab,\bb}[\lambda_{i}=k]\big)^{2}.

By Theorem
11.1
, we can strengthen the inequality
in Theorem
11.2
to Schur positivity:

Theorem 11.3
.

For all
i\geq 1
and
k\geq 1
, we have:

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P},\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda_{i}=k+1}\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\fs_{\lambda}(\textbf{{y}})\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P},\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda_{i}=k-1}\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\fs_{\lambda}(\textbf{{y}})\bigg)\ \leq_{\otimes}\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P},\hskip 1.70709pt\lambda_{i}=k}\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\fs_{\lambda}(\textbf{{y}})\bigg)^{2}.

The proof of Theorem
11.3
proceeds analogously to the proof of
the Okounkov inequality (Theorem
3.3
) given in
[
LPP07
]
,
but with Theorem
11.1
playing the role of the ADS inequality
(cf. Corollary
3.8
).
We omit the details.

11.2.
Klartag–Lehec inequality

We now consider the following
Klartag–Lehec
(KL)
inequality
,
which was first discovered as a discrete version of a result in convex geometry,
see a discussion in
[
MM24
]
. It can be viewed as version of the AD inequality
(Theorem
1.1
), where the log-supermodularity assumption is replaced by log-concavity.

Theorem 11.4
(
KL inequality

[
KL19
, Thm. 1.4]
).

Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be functions satisfying

(KL-cond)

\fa(\lambda)\,\fb(\mu)\ \leq\ \fc(\left\lceil(\lambda+\mu)/2\right\rceil)\hskip 1.70709pt\fd(\left\lfloor(\lambda+\mu)/2\right\rfloor).

Then we have:

(KL)

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}}\fa({\lambda})\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}}\fb({\lambda})\bigg)\ \leq\ \bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}}\fc({\lambda})\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}}\fd({\lambda})\bigg).

It is natural to ask whether this inequality has a Schur positive version:

Conjecture 11.5
.

Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\mathcal{P}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be functions with finite support,
satisfying (
KL-cond
). Then we have:

(KLS)

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}}\fa({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}}\fb({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}\bigg)\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}}\fc({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}}\fd({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}\bigg).

We checked the conjecture computationally, on all partitions of length at most
3

and size at most
10
.
The conjecture is best understood in the context of discrete convex analysis,
see
[
Mur03
]
, by comparing it with Speyer’s framework in
[
Spe26
]
. There,
Speyer presents four successively stronger
positivity properties that happen to be equivalent for single real functions

[
Spe26
, Thm. 4.4]
, but not for single Schur positivity for symmetric functions

[
Spe26
, Rem. 4.19]
. In this context, Speyer’s Theorem
3.5

says that log-supermodularity implies log-concavity for single symmetric functions.

It would be interesting to investigate if Speyer’s framework extends to
four functions inequalities, i.e. whether (
ADS
) is stronger
than (
KLS
)? What makes Conjecture
11.5
so challenging,
is that it is neither weaker nor stronger than the AD inequality.
If true, the conjecture would imply Schur differential log-concavity
(Conjecture
11.7
below). It is also worth noting that the KL inequality
(Theorem
11.4
) admits a unified proof via the AD inequality
[
HKS21
]
.
This lends further credence to the conjecture (cf.
\lx@sectionsign
12.8
).

11.3.
Differential operators

Let
\xb=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
and
\textbf{{z}}=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots)
be two sets of variables. Let
\Lambda(\xb)[[\textbf{{z}}]]
denote the ring of
formal power series in variables
z
, with coefficients in the ring of
symmetric functions
\Lambda(\xb)
.
For a sequence
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
, define the
differential operator

\partial^{\lambda}
with respect to
z
, by

\partial^{\lambda}\ :=\ \prod_{i\geq 1}\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}\right)^{\lambda_{i}}.

We denote by
\eb_{i}
the
i
-th standard unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
.

Differential operators provide a natural framework for the theory of
stable

and
Lorentzian polynomials
, see, e.g.,
[
BBL09
,
BH20
]
. They
act as fundamental operations that preserve the underlying geometric
properties. Motivated by these connections, we define the

normalized Schur generating function
by

(11.1)

\mathfrak{S}(\textbf{{z}})\ :=\ \sum_{\lambda\hskip 0.85355pt\in\hskip 0.85355pt\mathcal{P}}\,\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\,\prod_{i\geq 1}\frac{z_{i}^{\lambda_{i}}}{\lambda_{i}!}\ \in\hskip 1.70709pt\Lambda(\xb)[[\textbf{{z}}]]\hskip 0.85355pt.

Theorem 11.6
(
Schur differential total positivity
).

For all sequences
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}_{\geq 0}
,
\textbf{{z}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\infty}
with finite support, and for all distinct
i,j\geq 1
, we have:

(STP)

\big(\partial^{\lambda+\eb_{i}}\mathfrak{S}(\textbf{{z}})\big)\hskip 1.70709pt\big(\partial^{\lambda+\eb_{j}}\mathfrak{S}(\textbf{{z}})\big)\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \big(\partial^{\lambda+\eb_{i}+\eb_{j}}\mathfrak{S}(\textbf{{z}})\big)\hskip 1.70709pt\big(\partial^{\lambda}\mathfrak{S}(\textbf{{z}})\big).

Without Schur positivity, the inequality
in (
STP
) is known in the literature as

multivariate total positivity
of order
2
, see e.g.
[
KR80
]
.
When the inequality is reversed, it is known as the

Rayleigh property
, see e.g.
[
Brä15
]
.
The proof of Theorem
11.6
follows the same argument as the
proof of Theorem
3.1
,
except that the four functions are modified to keep track of the
weight
\prod_{i\geq 1}({z_{i}^{\lambda_{i}}})/{\lambda_{i}!}
.
We omit the details.

Conjecture 11.7
(
Schur differential log-concavity
).

For all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}_{\geq 0}
,
\textbf{{z}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\infty}
with finite support and any
i\geq 1
,

(SLC)

\big(\partial^{\lambda+2\eb_{i}}\mathfrak{S}(\textbf{{z}})\big)\hskip 1.70709pt\big(\partial^{\lambda}\mathfrak{S}(\textbf{{z}})\big)\ \leqslant_{\fs}\ \big(\partial^{\lambda+\eb_{i}}\mathfrak{S}(\textbf{{z}})\big)^{2}.

Unfortunately, our approach via the skew ADS inequality (Theorem
1.6
)
no longer applies for certain ranges of parameters. Nevertheless,
there is a good reason to believe that Conjecture
11.7
holds (cf.
\lx@sectionsign
12.7
).
First, without Schur positivity, the inequality holds for evaluations
at
\xb=\ab
, for all finitely supported
\ab\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\infty}
.
Indeed, this follows by combining the KL inequality (Theorem
11.4
)
and the LPP inequality (Theorem
1.4
). Second, relatedly, Conjecture
11.7

follows from Conjecture
11.5
, the Schur positive version of the KL inequality.

11.4.
Supersymmetric Schur functions

As before, let
\xb:=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
and
\textbf{{y}}:=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)
be two sets of variables.
We say that a symmetric function
p(\xb,\textbf{{y}})\in\Lambda(\textbf{{x}})\otimes\Lambda(\textbf{{y}})
is
supersymmetric
, if under the substitution
x_{1}=t
,
y_{1}=-t
,
the resulting series is independent of
t
. We denote by
\Lambda(\xb/\textbf{{y}})
the ring of supersymmetric polynomials in
\xb
and
y
. See
[
Mac92
,
\lx@sectionsign
6]

for an overview.

For a partition
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
, the
supersymmetric Schur function

\fs_{\lambda}(\xb/\textbf{{y}})
, defined by Berele and Regev
[
BR87
, p. 152]
, is given by

\fs_{\lambda}(\xb/\textbf{{y}})\ :=\ \sum_{\mu\subseteq\lambda}\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\mu}(\xb)\hskip 1.70709pt\fs_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(\textbf{{y}}),

Note that we recover the classical Schur functions
\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)
by setting
\textbf{{y}}=\mathbf{0}
.

Supersymmetric Schur functions
\{\fs_{\lambda}(\xb/\textbf{{y}})\}
form a basis for the ring
\Lambda(\xb/\textbf{{y}})
, see
[
Ste85
]
.
For elements
f,g\in\Lambda(\xb/\textbf{{y}})
, we write
f\geqslant_{\oslash}g
if
(f-g)
nonnegative coefficients when expanded in this basis.
The ADS inequality (Theorem
1.6
) has the following generalization to supersymmetric functions.

Theorem 11.8
(
supersymmetric ADS inequality
).

Let
\fa,\fb,\fc,\fd:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be functions satisfying (
AD-cond
).
Then we have:

(11.2)

\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fa({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}(\xb/\textbf{{y}})\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fb({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}(\xb/\textbf{{y}})\bigg)\ \leqslant_{\oslash}\ \bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fc({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}(\xb/\textbf{{y}})\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\fd({\lambda})\fs_{\lambda}(\xb/\textbf{{y}})\bigg).

Proof.

Write the defect of (
11.2
), i.e. the difference of two sides, in the basis
\{\fs_{\lambda}(\xb/\textbf{{y}})\}
as

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709pt\ff_{\lambda}\cdot\fs_{\lambda}(\xb/\textbf{{y}})\hskip 0.85355pt.

From above, it suffices to show that
\ff_{\lambda}\geq 0
, for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
.
Substituting
\textbf{{y}}\leftarrow{\mathbf{0}}
into (
11.2
), gives the inequality
(
ADS
), which
is Schur positive by Theorem
1.6
.
On the other hand, this is also equal to

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\hskip 1.70709pt\ff_{\lambda}\cdot\fs_{\lambda}(\xb)\hskip 0.85355pt.

Thus, all
\ff_{\lambda}
’s are nonnegative, as desired.
∎

12.
Final remarks and open problems

12.1.
Correlation inequalities

From the ocean of other correlation inequalities in probability and
statistical physics, let us single out the
Griffiths inequality

[
Gri67
]

for the Ising model, and the
van den Berg–Kesten
(BK)
inequality

[
BK85
]
for events occurring with disjoint evidence. These
inequalities are also proved by induction and have a number of
advanced generalizations, see
[
KS68
,
Gin70
,
GHS70
]
and
[
BCR99
,
Smy13
,
Rei00
]
,
respectively.
When the inductive approach fails, such as for the monotonicity of percolation

[
GPZ25
,
\lx@sectionsign
8.4]
, establishing the inequality becomes a major challenge.
See also
[
Lig85
]
for applications of correlation inequalities
to other problems in statistical physics.

12.2.
Equality conditions

There is long ongoing effort to characterize the equality conditions
for more and more general correlation inequalities, from HK inequality
to the FKG inequality, and all the way to the AD inequality, see

[
DKW79
,
Beck90
,
MR94
,
AK95
]
. The problem was ultimately resolved in a
forthcoming paper by the first and third authors
[
CP26+
]
,
which also characterized equality condition of the skew LPP and skew
Okounkov inequalities.

It would be an interesting and difficult challenge to characterize
equality conditions for the ADS inequality, as even the equality conditions
for the RLS inequality remain open. Note that there is no reason
these equality conditions should have a tractable characterizations,
since many inequalities do not, see
[
IP22
,
CP24b
,
CP24c
]
.

12.3.
Combinatorial interpretations

In a forthcoming paper
[
PS26+
]
, the last two authors used
a generalized Littlewood–Richardson rule by Nguyen–Pylyavskyy
[
NP25
]
,
to give a combinatorial interpretation for the defect
\delta(\lambda,\mu,\nu)
of Schur multiplicities in (
LPP
):

\delta(\lambda,\mu,\nu)\,:=\,c^{\lambda}_{\mu\vee\nu,\hskip 0.85355pt\mu\wedge\nu}\hskip 1.70709pt-\hskip 1.70709ptc^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}\hskip 1.70709pt.

This resolved the open problem in
[
Pak22
,
\lx@sectionsign
7.4]
.

It would be interesting to extend the result to the defect of Schur multiplicities
of (
ADS
). Note that one cannot use our proof of ADS inequality as a black
box, since it uses (
AD
), among other ingredients. However, the defect of the
AD inequality
does not
have a combinatorial interpretation, understood
as being in
#P
, under certain complexity assumptions
[
Gla25
, Ch. 6]
.
In a positive direction, the defect of the HK inequality (
1.1
) does
have a combinatorial interpretation
[
Pak22
, Prop. 5.8]
.

12.4.
Representation theory aspects

In
[
Oko01
,
Oko03
]
, Okounkov studied log-concavity of Kostka numbers and
LR coefficients from representation theoretic point of view. Although his most
ambitious conjecture was disproved in
[
CDW07
]
, the LPP inequality
(Theorem
1.4
) and the M-convexity for Kostka numbers
[
HMMS22
]

can be viewed as special cases. One can follow Okounkov’s approach to
restate (
ADS
) in the language of certain
S_{n}
-modules being
submodules of others; we avoid the temptation.

Let us also mention a conjecture by Gui
[
Gui25
, Conj. 5.1]
,
generalizing the Okounkov inequality (
Ok
) to the reduced
(stable) Kronecker coefficients. By the result in
[
AS20
]
,
this conjecture can be interpreted as log-concavity for
a certain basis of symmetric functions.

12.5.
Speyer’s theorem

In
[
Spe26
]
, Speyer proved a Schur positive inequality conjectured
previously by Lam–Postnikov–Pylyavskyy, see
[
DP07
]
.

Speyer’s inequality
extended (
LPP
)
in the direction not covered in this paper.
Curiously, (
AD
) is a key technical tool in the proof of Speyer’s theorem.
It would be interesting to find a skew version of this result.
7
7

7

After this
paper was written, we learned of a forthcoming paper
[
LN26+
]
which proves a
skew version of Speyer’s inequality.
Even better would be to obtain a
four functions version of his inequality, see below.

12.6.
Skew stable Grothendieck polynomials

It would be interesting to find a skew version of supermodularity of
Grothendieck polynomials (Theorem
1.8
)
for the definition by Buch
[
Buch02
]
. As emphasized previously,
the polynomials used in Theorem
9.3
are a
simplified variant tailored specifically to our proof,
rather than the conventional definition.
One potential approach to proving this general skew inequality would be to
combine the skew ADS inequality with
combinatorial formulas for skew stable Grothendieck polynomials
developed by Chan–Pflueger
[
CP21
]
.

12.7.
Lorentzian Schur positivity

In view of discussion in
\lx@sectionsign
11.3
, it would be interesting
to develop Schur positive analogues of stable and Lorentzian polynomials.
This could allow one to translate the wealth of existing intuition and
techniques from the geometry of polynomials directly into the realm of
Schur positivity.
This could lead to many new Schur positive inequalities,
potentially resolving Conjecture
11.7
.

12.8.
Four functions style discrete convex analysis

Continuing the discussion above and in
\lx@sectionsign
11.2
, it would be interesting
to develop a comprehensive study of the discrete convex analysis on distributive
lattices in the style of (
AD
), where convexity conditions such as
log-supermodularity (
1.3
) is substituted with four functions conditions
such as (
AD-cond
). A good roadmap would be four conditions in
[
Spe26
, Rem. 4.19]
.
One can view both (
AD
), (
RLS
) and (
KL
) as basic result
in this theory, all leading to their Schur positive analogues: (
ADS
) in Theorem
1.6
,
(
LSS
) in Theorem
8.1
, and (
KLS
) in Conjecture
11.5
.
