Anatomical Considerations. ? The two innominate veins
begin behind the sternal ends of the clavicles on either side and
unite behind the sternal end of the first costal cartilage to form
the superior vena cava which extends downwards to the level
?f the upper margin of the third costal cartilage near the
sternum. The length of the cava is 7 to 7*5 cm. Its axis at its
upper end is slightly anterior to that of the aorta, but lower down
it gradually passes backwards so as finally to lie to the right of,
and slightly posterior to, the aorta. I he ascending aorta slightly
overlaps its lower half, hence one might expect that an aneurysm
at the junction of the ascending and transverse parts of the
a?rta would push the superior cava outwards and slightly
forwards, whereas an aneurysm developing about the middle or
lower parts of the ascending aorta would push it outwards and
backwards. This does not work out in practice, as in only one
out of six cases in which the cava was anterior to the aneurysm
the latter had developed at the junction of ascending and
transverse parts of the aorta. In the remainder the aneurysms
had developed from the right posterior wall of the ascending
a?rta lower down and resulted from disease of the wall.
The aortic opening is 3 to 3*5 cm- from the termination
?f the cava, being on a level with the tricuspid, so that the
whole depth of the right auricle intervenes between these two
orifices. Hence an aneurysm localised to the commencement
of the aorta, and extending to the right, would tend to compress
the right auricle rather than the superior cava.
In the recoil of diastole the greatest strain falls on the right
interior quadrant of the aorta just under cover of the right

The azygos major enters the superior cava from behind
above its mid point, dividing it into an upper part measuring
about 3 cm. and a lower part measuring about 4 cm. The
orifice of the azygos is at about the level of the upper attach-
ment of the pericardium over the aorta. An aneurysm of the
aorta, in pushing the cava outwards and backwards, would at
the same time compress the azygos and interfere directly with
the circulation in that vessel.
In the cases to be cited, it is important to remember that
almost invariably the superior vena cava has been stretched and
compressed, so that its length may become as great as twice the
normal, and its relations to right auricle, azygos, and innominates
greatly altered. Hence, indications of positions, e.g., of a perfora-
tion, as so many inches or centimetres from the right auricle, or
from the innominate veins, or artery, or from the aortic opening,
give little clear information as to its actual situation relative to
these important structures, and lack of appreciation of such
alterations resulting from stretching of the cava has diminished
the value of many of the reports.
Both the azygos major, which receives the intercostal veins
from the right side of the thorax, and the hemiazygos, which
joins it and receives blood from the lower left intercostal veins,
originate in the ascending lumbar veins or occasionally in
branches of inferior vena cava or in renal veins. Obstruction
to the circulation through the azygos major accounts for the
lower extension of the congestion and oedema on the (back)
posterior aspect of the trunk, as compared with that on the
front, where the swelling is usually sharply limited to the level
of the diaphragm or costal margin. Moreover, obstruction will
also give rise to back pressure in the bronchial veins with con-
sequent congestion and cedema of the lungs.
The anastomoses through which a collateral circulation
between the two venae cavae develops are deep and superficial.
The deep anastomoses are formed by the azygos and hemi-
azygos to the inferior cava, and through the ascending lumbars
to the common iliacs, partly by way of the phrenic veins to
suprarenals and renals. The superficial anastomoses are formed
by the intercostals, lateral thoracic and iliac circumflex veins

Dr William Hunter, in the latter half of the eighteenth
century, was the first to describe traumatic arterio-venous
aneurysm. This condition has thus been well recognised for
a long time, and, in these latter days, the Great War has
enabled surgeons to become well acquainted with the accident:
whereas spontaneous arterio-venous aneurysm may be regarded
as a comparatively rare condition. James Syme (83) was the
first to describe its occurrence in the case of a young man,

In the present communication I shall deal chiefly with cases
in which an aneurysm of the ascending portion of the thoracic
aorta has communicated spontaneously either with the left
innominate vein or with the superior vena cava. In my second
case, the aneurysm partly obliterated the lower part of the
superior vena cava, leaving two narrow channels, anterior and
posterior, the rupture occurring into the right auricle just below
the caval opening; the case, clinically, resembling those in
which the perforation was higher up.

Onset and Symptoms.?The important early indications of
a spontaneously occurring communication between aneurysm
and venous trunk are dyspnoea, cyanosis, and swelling, the
two last limited to the upper parts of the body. The usual
history given is that, following the perforation, dyspnoea and
lividity have developed rapidly, or in some cases suddenly,
and that these are followed after a shorter or longer interval
by oedema of the livid areas. In a few cases all three have
developed rapidly, the probable explanation being that the
perforation has occurred in such a position that the escaping
arterial stream has been directed so as to cause a rapid damming
back of the venous return in the innominates, and (or) in the
azygos; the post-mortem data thus confirming the clinical
statements. In other cases, however, the patient's statements
as to rapidity of onset have been perhaps too readily accepted
without verification. In the first case I report, the patient
said the oedema and cyanosis developed suddenly, but as a
matter of fact they had been gradually increasing for years,
and when perforation took place, further increase was rapid,
and it is highly probable that this was the correct history
in many other of the cases reported. In a few cases there

venous area or the increase of peripheral resistance in that area.
