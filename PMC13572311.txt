Distinct Representations of Irrelevant Emotional Information in the Visual Network Are Associated With Psychopathology in Youth
Authors: Yen-Chu Lin, Qingyang Meng, Angelica Lopez-Tucker, Janya Utkarsh, Fin Sterner, May I. Conley, Lena J. Skalaban, Richard Watts, Dylan G. Gee, Arielle Baskin-Sommers, B.J. Casey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescence is a period of heightened risk for psychopathology. Recent studies suggest shared neural correlates across psychiatric disorders in brain networks involved in emotional and cognitive processes. However, the visual network—crucial for extracting and processing emotional information—has received less attention. Here, using a transdiagnostic approach, we focus on the visual network to investigate how youth with psychopathology represent and differentiate between emotional face categories during a cognitively demanding task, for which emotional content is irrelevant.MethodsWe used data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, Release 4.0 (ages 11–12 years; N = 4952 after quality control). Symptoms of psychopathology were based on the Child Behavior Checklist. Participants performed an emotional n-back task in the scanner. Representational similarity analysis was performed to examine dissimilarities in neural representations between emotional categories in functional brain networks.ResultsGreater dissimilarity in neural representations between emotional categories in the visual network was associated with worse cognitive performance and more psychopathology for the high memory load condition (p < .001). This association was evident early in visual processing, as demonstrated in the primary visual cortex (p < .001), and predicted symptoms a year later.ConclusionsUsing a transdiagnostic approach, we showed that the distinctiveness in neural representations of task-irrelevant emotional information in the visual cortex is positively correlated with psychopathology. Together, our findings suggest the importance of the visual network and early visual areas in understanding neural mechanisms of emotional processing in youth with psychopathology.

Emotional processing involves emotional sensation and perception, which can in turn influence our thoughts, attention, and actions. Adolescence is a time of heightened sensitivity and reactivity to emotional information (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9). It is also a time of increased risk for psychiatric disorders (10,11). A core component of mental health problems is an alteration in emotional processing that can influence how individuals perceive, attend, and react to emotional information, which can in turn compromise cognitive abilities (12, 13, 14). However, how youth with psychopathology visually perceive their emotional world remains less clear. In the current study, we focus on the visual network to investigate how the distinctiveness of neural representations of visual emotional information within individuals relates to their cognitive performance and symptoms of psychopathology.

During adolescence, heightened sensitivity and reactivity to emotional information has been linked to an increased risk of psychopathology (12,13,15). Many symptoms of psychopathology in adolescents have been associated with heightened vigilance to emotional information, including symptoms related to anxiety (16, 17, 18) and depression (18, 19, 20). Cognitive studies using a variety of visual stimuli and measurements, including eye-tracking techniques, emotional face labeling, and response latencies to visual targets, found attentional biases toward emotional information among individuals with mental illness, even when the information was irrelevant to the task at hand (21, 22, 23). Other studies have found blunted or a lack of attentional bias to emotional information in individuals with conduct disorder (24,25) and schizophrenia (26). These alterations in cognitive performance may reflect interference in the interpretation of, or distraction by, emotional cues. In fact, impairment in emotional recognition has been associated with both internalizing and externalizing disorders in young people, including major depressive disorder (27), bipolar disorder (28,29), borderline personality disorder (30), conduct disorder (31), and oppositional defiant disorder (32). Together, these studies are consistent with prior work suggesting that altered emotional processing is a transdiagnostic feature of psychopathology (12).

Given the centrality of altered emotional processing across mental health disorders, a large neuroimaging literature has focused on the involvement of limbic circuitry in the risk for and presence of specific and overlapping psychiatric conditions (33, 34, 35, 36, 37). Less attention has been given to the visual system despite its importance in basic visual processing of emotional cues. A growing body of evidence has begun to associate altered activity and functional connectivity in visual regions with different mental health disorders, including autism (38), major depressive disorder (39, 40, 41, 42, 43), bipolar disorder (44), and conduct disorder (45), suggesting the importance of the visual network across psychiatric disorders (46). Recent studies have identified transdiagnostic alterations in brain networks that are associated with general psychopathology (47, 48, 49). Specifically, altered functional connectivity in the visual network has been associated with a transdiagnostic general psychopathology (p) factor in adolescents (48,50,51). Heightened connectivity between the visual network and other emotional processing regions and networks also has been linked to general psychopathology (41,48). Alterations in the representation of emotional information in the visual network and early visual areas—both key in integrating low-level visual information—may contribute to impairments in higher-level cognitive function in emotional contexts that present across different forms of psychopathology.

An analytic approach gaining traction in the field of individual differences in neural representations of stimuli is representational similarity analysis (RSA). Unlike univariate analysis, which asks where in the brain activity differs between condition A and condition B, RSA is a multivariate analysis that asks how different types of information (e.g., emotional categories) are represented differently within a brain area in an individual. By directly comparing individual responses, one can get a more complete picture of the structure of representations of information (52). Studies using this technique have shown that individual dissimilarity in neural representations between types of information is associated with individual differences in perception, emotion, and behavior (53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59). These studies suggest that RSA is an advantageous tool for examining how youth with mental health problems represent and differentiate categories of emotions differently from those without—a central question of this study.

In the current study, we used a transdiagnostic approach to examine how youth with mental health problems visually represent and differentiate their emotional world, focusing on visual processing areas. Using the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study imaging and behavioral data, we tested how neural dissimilarity between emotional categories of information within an individual related to their cognitive performance and psychopathology. We probed the distinctiveness of individuals’ neural representations between emotional face categories (fearful, happy, and neutral) using RSA and its association with cognitive performance during an emotional n-back task, for which emotional content was task-irrelevant. That is, the identity of a face—not the emotion of the face—was task-relevant. We hypothesized that more distinct neural representations of task-irrelevant emotional face categories in the visual network would be associated with worse cognitive performance and more psychopathology in youth.

Methods and Materials

Participants

Participants were 11- to 12-year-old youths who were part of the 4.0.1 data release of the ABCD Study, with 4952 of 10,414 youths having the measures of interest (DOI: 10.15154/1523041) and passing quality control (Supplemental Methods and Table S1). We selected the data for this study at a later time point when the youths were older, rather than at baseline when ABCD Study’s youths were 9- to 10-years old, to increase the sample of youths with mental health problems, given the increase in psychiatric disorders during adolescence.

Measures

Symptoms of Psychopathology Measure

The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) (60), a parent questionnaire about the youth’s externalizing and internalizing behaviors, was used to measure the number of mental and behavioral problems. The CBCL measures a youth’s behavior over the past 6 months based on 113 items. A total problems score was calculated by summing all the problem items and used as the psychopathology measure. To investigate the associations between neural dissimilarity and individuals’ symptoms of psychopathology, we used the raw total problems score instead of the T score to preserve the individual differences without group normalization.

Emotional n-Back Task

The ABCD in-scanner emotional n-back task (Figure 1) was used to engage both cognitive and emotional processes (61). The task includes 2 runs of 8 blocks, each block with 10 trials for a total of 160 trials. In each run, there were 4 blocks of the 0-back (low load) and 4 blocks of the 2-back (high load) conditions. Blocks of trials consisted of happy, fearful, and neutral faces (62,63) as well as place stimuli (64, 65, 66). Participants were instructed to match the face identity of a target face presented at the beginning of a block of trials for the 0-back condition or to match the face identity of a face presented 2 trials back for the 2-back condition (Figure 1). Because emotional face categories were blocked, the identity—not the emotion—of the face was relevant for completing the task. Neuroimaging Acquisition and Preprocessing

The task-based functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data were collected following the method described by Casey et al. (61). Across all 21 ABCD sites, 3 platforms —Siemens Prisma, Philips, and GE 750 3T scanners—were used. Functional images were acquired using a multiband gradient echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with a standard adult-size multichannel receive coil. Parameters included 800 ms TR, 30 ms TE, 52° flip angle, isotropic voxel size 2.4 mm3, 60 slices acquired in the axial plane, and multiband EPI with a slice acceleration factor of 6. Additional detailed acquisition parameters are reported in previous ABCD Study articles (61,67).

Data were preprocessed as Hagler et al. (67) described and preprocessing was performed by the ABCD Data Analysis, Informatics & Resource Center. Preprocessing steps included 1) head motion correction using 3dvolreg in AFNI, 2) B0-distortion correction using the reverse polarity method, 3) nonlinearity distortion correction using gradient wrap correction, and 4) between-scan motion correction by cubic interpolation resampling using a midsession scan as reference. Mutual information was used for automated registration between the spin echo, B0 calibration scans, and T1-weighted structural images. Rigid-body transformation was used for the alignment between fMRI and T1-weighted images.

Analysis

Behavioral Analysis

We used a linear mixed-effects model in the lme4 package (68) in R (version 4.3.1) to test for main effects and interactions of memory load (0- and 2-back), emotional face category (happy, fear, and neutral), and CBCL total problems score on the dependent variable of mean accuracy during the n-back task. Mean accuracy was calculated separately by memory load and emotional face category. The model included data collection site and family relatedness nested within site as random-effect covariates and age at interview and sex assigned at birth as fixed-effect covariates.

Imaging Analysis

Processing of neuroimaging data was followed by a surface-based, HCP (Human Connectome Project)–style MRI processing pipeline [for details, see Hagler, et al. (67)] . For each participant, a general linear model (GLM) was run using AFNIs 3dDeconvolve (69) on the time series of all voxels extracted for each FreeSurfer brain parcellation (70). GLM-predicted task-based activation for each block of the study convolved with a hemodynamic response function. This GLM included predictors for 0-back and 2-back fearful face, happy face, and neutral face and place conditions. Regressors included motion estimates to account for head movement (x-, y-, and z translations and rotations) (57). Volumes with >0.9 mm framewise displacement were excluded from analyses, as well as the neighboring volumes (71,72). First-level analyses were smoothed at 5-mm full width at half maximum of the Gaussian kernel. Second-level analyses calculated mean response across runs of the task for each participant.

Using a region of interest (ROI)–based approach at the individual participant level, GLM beta coefficients in each vertex were sampled into surface space using FreeSurfer’s mri_vol2surf [for details, see Hagler et al. (67)]. Average time courses were calculated for cortical surface-based ROIs using FreeSurfer’s anatomically-defined parcellations (73,74) and subcortical ROIs (75).

We defined the visual network based on Yeo’s 7 functional networks (75). These functional networks are surface-based vertex-level data that map to regions defined in Schaefer et al. (76) with 400 parcellations (77). Each parcellation is associated with one of the 7 functional networks (visual, limbic, dorsal attention, ventral attention/salience, default, frontoparietal/control, and somatomotor networks). To interrogate how early in the visual network hypothesized effects arise, we identified the early visual area of the primary visual cortex (V1) using an anatomical approximation of V1 defined by the Glasser atlas (78) given no available retinotopic data.

Representational Similarity Analysis

To examine dissimilarities between neural representations of emotional face categories, we used RSA (52) with beta contrasts during the emotional n-back task. For each emotional face category, beta contrasts (in each vertex) were estimated using the linear beta coefficient contrasts of fixation and one of the 3 emotional categories by condition. Weighted averages of beta contrasts of the 2 runs were used (67). In each parcellation, we estimated the dissimilarity between all pairwise comparisons between the 3 emotion categories using their beta contrasts for each individual participant (Figure 2). Pearson correlation was used and subtracted from 1 to estimate dissimilarity. Emotional category pairwise comparisons included happy versus fearful, happy versus neutral, and fearful versus neutral faces. In each functional network, we reported the average dissimilarities across all parcellations assigned to the network. Associations of Neural Dissimilarity With Cognitive Performance and Psychopathology

To test for associations between neural dissimilarity and cognitive performance, we treated neural dissimilarity between emotional face category comparisons (happy vs. fearful, happy vs. neutral, or fearful vs. neutral faces) as our independent variable and average accuracy of emotional faces in the 0- or 2-back condition as our dependent variable, controlling for age, sex, overall task accuracy, and random effects for relatedness nested within site in the model. Overall task accuracy was calculated across all stimuli (faces and places) by condition. To investigate the association between neural dissimilarity of emotional categories and psychopathology, we treated neural dissimilarity as the independent variable and CBCL total problems score as the dependent variable. Fixed-effect covariates included sex and age, and random effects included relatedness nested within site. Supplemental analyses used the same model to test for associations between neural dissimilarity and each of the 8 CBCL subscales (Supplemental Methods and Figure S1). Results

Behavioral Results

The mixed-effect model showed main effects of memory load (β = −0.0482, p < .001), emotional face category (β = −0.0211, p < .001), and CBCL total problems score (β = −0.0004, p < .001) on the dependent variable of mean accuracy and a significant interaction between load and emotional face category (β = 0.0113, p < .001) (Figure 3 and Table S2). Mean accuracy was lower in the 2-back condition, the negative relative to the neutral emotional face category, and for higher total problems scores. The interaction between memory load and emotional face category revealed lower accuracy in the 2-back condition for negative relative to neutral emotional faces. Imaging Results

Neural Dissimilarity of Emotional Categories in Functional Networks

To investigate the relevance of the visual network in the dissimilarity in neural representations of the emotional face categories, we first examined the effect size of this network in relation to the other functional networks (75). The parcellations of the 7 functional networks and their effect sizes, based on R2marginal values, are shown in Figure 4. Our results showed that in both low (0-back) and high (2-back) load conditions, the visual network (and the limbic network) showed the largest effect sizes, indicating the importance of the visual network in distinct representations between emotional categories.

Association of Neural Dissimilarity With Cognitive Performance

We investigated the association between neural dissimilarities and mean accuracy on the n-back task separately for the 0- and 2-back conditions. The mixed-effects model controlled for overall mean accuracy across all stimuli (faces and places). Our results showed greater neural dissimilarity in the visual network (β = −2.46 × 10−3, SE = 7.46 × 10−4, p < .001) was associated with lower mean accuracy in the 2-back, but not the 0-back, condition (p = .83). A similar pattern was observed for the limbic network for the 2-back condition (β = −1.43 × 10−3, SE = 5.08 × 10−4, p < .01). To interrogate how early in visual processing the effects emerged in the visual network, we examined the associations in V1, where a similar effect was observed (β = −1.40 × 10−3, SE = 6.10 × 10−4, p < .05) (Figure 5 and Table S3).

Association of Neural Dissimilarity With Psychopathology

Our mixed-effects models testing the association between individuals’ neural dissimilarity of emotional categories and CBCL total problem scores showed no significant difference in neural dissimilarity between the 3 emotional categories (happy vs. fearful, happy vs. neutral, and fearful vs. neutral) regardless of load. There was also no association of neural dissimilarity and CBCL total problems score in the 0-back condition for either the limbic or visual network (Figure 6A and Table S4). However, for the 2-back condition, dissimilarity was positively associated with CBCL total problems score in the visual (β = 1.5900, SE = 0.4211, p < .001) but not the limbic (β = 0.2214, SE = 0.3115, p = .477) network (Figure 6B and Table S4). Neural dissimilarity in V1 was associated with CBCL total problems score regardless of load (for 0-back: β = 0.6979, SE = 0.3326, p < .05; for 2-back, β = 1.3810, SE = 0.3463, p < .001) (Figure 6 and Table S4). Supplemental analyses investigated whether individuals’ neural dissimilarity of emotional categories was associated with specific or overlapping CBCL subscales (Figure S1). All but one subtype—somatic complaints—showed an association with neural dissimilarity in the visual network, V1, or both (Table S5).

We tested whether neural dissimilarities in this network and region mediated the association between cognitive performance during the 2-back and CBCL total problems score. The mediation analysis showed only partial mediation (Supplemental Methods and Figure S2). Additional analyses tested whether neural dissimilarity in the limbic and visual networks and V1 predicted total problems scores 1 year later. We found only the visual network and V1 predicted symptoms a year later (Supplemental Methods and Table S6).

Finally, because the ABCD sample was not recruited as a clinical sample (80), we tested whether our results held for those youths with clinically relevant symptoms. A group-level analysis comparing participants with clinically relevant CBCL T scores (60) that were ≥63 with an age-, sex-, and site-matched control sample (n = 264 for each group) was performed. While we observed associations in the visual and limbic networks, the most robust associations were in the visual network (Supplemental Methods, Figure S3, and Table S7).

Discussion

The current study examined how youth with psychopathology visually process their emotional world. Specifically, we investigated whether youth with emotional and behavioral problems represent and differentiate between task-irrelevant emotional categories differently from those without these problems. We found that more distinct neural representations of task-irrelevant emotional information in the visual network and V1—crucial for extracting and processing sensory and emotional information—were associated with worse cognitive performance, more psychopathology, and predicted symptoms 1 year later. These findings emphasize the importance of early visual areas in emotion processing as a transdiagnostic component in youth with psychopathology (12).

Consistent with our hypothesis, we showed that greater neural dissimilarity between task-irrelevant emotional categories was associated with diminished working memory performance in youths. This association was observed in the limbic and visual networks and V1, but only during the high cognitive load (2-back) condition. This negative association between neural dissimilarity and cognitive performance may reflect interference due to distraction or confusion by the irrelevant emotional information, especially in cognitively demanding situations. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating susceptibility of cognitive performance to interference from emotional information during adolescence (5, 6, 7,9,16,81,82).

Consistent with the existing literature, cognitive performance was also associated with psychopathology. Specifically, youths with more emotional and behavioral problems performed worse on the emotional n-back task, regardless of memory load or emotional category. This finding is consistent with the literature—a core component across many mental health disorders affecting youth is a dysregulation in emotional processing that can influence how an individual perceives, attends, or reacts to emotional information (12, 13, 14). As such, our findings of diminished cognitive performance in youth with more emotional and behavioral problems may reflect distraction or confusion associated with the emotional content that interferes with their performance, especially in the demanding high memory load condition.

A key finding of our study was the association of neural dissimilarity in the representation of emotional categories in the visual network and V1 with psychopathology. Dissimilarity in these regions also predicted symptoms a year later. These associations, together with the behavioral findings, suggest that youth with psychopathology may allocate greater visual resources to irrelevant emotional information, especially in cognitively demanding situations. It is important to note that overall n-back task performance was treated as a covariate in our analysis, underscoring the role of visual emotional processes in our results. Moreover, V1 is known to be crucial for face processing (83) and is important in extracting and encoding basic features, such as the corners of the mouth, that convey emotional information in our stimuli (84,85). The current and prior evidence of discrete and enhanced brain activity and functional connectivity in the visual cortex in youth with various forms of psychopathology (39, 40, 41,43,44,48) highlights a role of altered visual cortical functioning in general psychopathology.

Explanations for the robust association between an early visual processing region and psychopathology in youth may be related to cortical or subcortical modulation of the visual pathway by emotional stimuli. Specifically, there is evidence that the corticogeniculate pathway plays an important role in what information we process (86). Specifically, the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus not only provides feedforward input to the V1 but also receives feedback from the cortex. Cortical input to the lateral geniculate can bias what information is transmitted to V1 based on a given context or focus (e.g., food cues when hungry). Other information is filtered out. Thus, cortical input to and from the lateral geniculate can alter what visual cues we process. In the current study, the visual cortex may be biasing attention to salient emotional faces. However, emotional information was irrelevant to the task demands—instead, the facial identity was key. As such, those youths with few, if any, emotional and behavioral problems may have been less biased toward processing irrelevant emotional information than those with these problems. An alternative explanation involves subcortical inputs from the amygdala, a critical region for processing emotional information, directly to V1, which could modulate visual processing (83,87). Individuals with psychopathology may have increased connectivity between these regions that would potentially enhance amygdala modulation of the neural representation of visual emotional information in V1 (83,88, 89, 90, 91). While the current study cannot differentiate between these possible explanations or how they may be interacting, it raises important directions for future research in identifying specific neural mechanisms underlying the association between V1 and psychopathology.

Limitations

It is important to consider potential limitations of this study when interpreting the results. First, our findings are based on a task where emotional information was irrelevant to the task itself. That is, the n-back task required detection of repeats in facial identity, not emotional facial expression. Future studies using fMRI tasks where emotional information is relevant to the task demands (e.g., facial emotion recognition task) may further constrain our interpretations, but such fMRI tasks are not currently available in the ABCD Study. Regardless, the finding that even representations of irrelevant emotional information were associated with both diminished cognitive performance and increased psychopathology highlights the significance of visual emotional processing in youth with emotional and behavioral problems. Second, our measure of the V1 region was based on an anatomical atlas and not on retinotopic mapping as the latter is not available in the ABCD Study. Additional studies using retinotopic mapping to identify early visual areas would strengthen the current findings. Third, the ABCD sample is not a clinical sample, and the CBCL is not a diagnostic measure, so research is needed to assess whether our findings generalize to specific clinical samples. Fourth, our primary analyses were cross-sectional and did not provide directionality of effects. We did find that neural dissimilarity in the visual network and V1 predicted symptoms a year later. Finally, previous work has shown alterations in the interactions between functional networks in individuals with psychopathology (41,43,48,50,92, 93, 94), highlighting the importance of examining both between- and within-network differences in future work.

Conclusions

Using a transdiagnostic approach, this study examined whether youth with symptoms of psychopathology visually process their emotional world differently based on distinct neural representations in the visual cortex. Although a large neuroimaging literature has focused on limbic circuitry in the risk for psychiatric disorders in youth given a common component of emotional dysregulation across them, this study highlights the importance of early visual processing areas. Specifically, we found that more distinct neural representations of emotional categories in the visual network and in an early visual processing area—crucial for extracting and processing sensory and emotional information—were associated with worse cognitive performance and more psychopathology. Together, our findings suggest that emotional facial cues, even when irrelevant to a situation, can significantly influence how one perceives, attends, and reacts to such cues and importantly underscore the role of early visual areas in these processes and risk for psychopathology in youth.
