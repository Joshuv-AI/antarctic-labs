Universality in Deep Neural Networks: An approach via the Lindeberg exchange principle
Filippo Giovagnini, Sotirios Kotitsas, Marco Romito

Abstract
. We consider the infinite-width limit of a fully connected deep neural network with general weights, and we prove quantitative general bounds on the 2 2 -Wasserstein distance between the network and its infinite-width Gaussian limit, under appropriate regularity assumptions on the activation function. Our main tool is a Lindeberg principle for Deep Neural Networks, which we use to successively replace the weights on each layer by Gaussian random variables.



1.
Introduction

Neural networks have become nowadays the central engines of modern technology, driving the most recent scientific breakthroughs, as well as reshaping how we work and create. While their empirical success is now well established, there is still a significant amount of work to be done to achieve a complete theoretical understanding of their properties. Randomly initialized networks are just as vital, acting as both a practical ”blank slate” that allows neurons to learn distinct features and a theoretical gold mine for researchers studying the mathematical foundations of how deep learning actually works.

The main subject of this paper is the analysis of fully connected deep neural networks, where weights and biases are random variables, and the interest is in their behavior as the widths of the hidden layers tend to infinity. In this regime, random neural networks are expected to display Gaussian behavior: under the standard mean-field scaling of the weights, the output of the network converges to a Gaussian process whose covariance is determined recursively by the architecture and the activation function. This infinite-width description provides a tractable probabilistic model for wide networks and has become a basic tool in the analysis of random neural networks.

Neal’s seminal work
[
18
]
laid the groundwork for the subject by proving the convergence of randomly initialised shallow wide fully connected networks to Gaussian processes. Deep networks were later covered by
[
17
,
10
,
22
,
21
]
, with weaker assumptions
[
14
]
, or with a functional perspective in
[
7
]
.

Further results focused on quantitative bounds for convergence of randomly initialised networks with Gaussian weights. The first quantitative proof of convergence, with Gaussian initialised weights, was given in
[
5
]
, in Wasserstein-2 distance. The role of the depth-to-width ratio in the convergence to the neural network Gaussian process was introduced in
[
15
]
. Further results of quantitative convergence were obtained in
[
2
]
, with total variation and Wasserstein-1 distances. Improved convergence rates were obtained in
[
12
]
for Wasserstein-2 distance, and later in
[
19
]
for Wasserstein-
p
distances. A recent extension to the total variation metric is given in
[
8
]
. Quantitative convergence to Gaussian processes in the infinite width limit turns out to be stable also for trained shallow neural networks
[
1
]
.

[
14
]
provides the first proof of universality of the neural network Gaussian process, namely of convergence of randomly initialised networks with
non-Gaussian
weights. The proof in
[
14
]
is qualitative, earlier quantitative results of convergence were obtained in
[
11
,
16
]
. More refined rates of convergence in Wasserstein-1 are given in
[
3
]
, and improved in
[
4
]
and
[
9
]
. We refer to
[
9
]
for a detailed literature review on the subject, with overviews on both Gaussian and non-Gaussian weights.

The main goal of this paper is to study quantitative rates of convergence for universality of random neural networks. More precisely, we prove explicit bounds on the distance between the output of a finite-width network and its infinite-width Gaussian limit in the
2
-Wasserstein distance. Our approach is based on the Lindeberg exchange principle. Roughly speaking, the weights of the network are successively replaced by Gaussian weights with matching first and second moments and the cumulative error along this switching procedure is carefully estimated. The additional difficulty is that a naive application of the classical Lindeberg method leads to dimension-dependent bounds that are too large in our setting. The key point is that the recursive structure of the network provides additional smoothing, which can be exploited to obtain better estimates.

In contrast to the recent results
[
4
]
and
[
9
]
, which focus on the 1-Wasserstein, total variation or convex metrics under Lipschitz assumptions on the activation function, our work establishes quantitative bounds in the 2-Wasserstein distance. While our approach necessitates higher regularity for the activation function – specifically
C^{3}_{b}
in the presence of biases and even higher-order derivatives for the zero-bias case – we obtain a convergence rate of approximately
O(n^{-1/4})
(
n
is layers width), which is more robust than
[
4
]
, although is slower than the rate obtained in
[
9
]
. This provides a stronger metric of convergence than the
W_{1}
results in
[
4
]
and
[
9
]
, while requiring only finite third moments for the hidden layer weights. Additionally, in the presence of Gaussian biases we are able to recover bounds in the total variation and
1-
Wasserstein distance establised in
[
9
]
, with optimal rates.

2.
The setup and main results

We begin by introducing the model.

Definition 2.1
.

Fix
L\in\mathbb{N}
and
n_{0},\dots,n_{L+1}\in\mathbb{N}
. A
fully connected deep neural network
is a function

z^{(L+1)}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}}

defined recursively by

(2.1)

z^{(\ell)}=\begin{cases}W^{(1)}x+b^{(1)},&\ell=1,\\
W^{(\ell)}\sigma(z^{(\ell-1)})+b^{(\ell)},&\ell=2,\dots,L+1.\end{cases}

The matrices
W^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times n_{l-1}}
are called
weights
, and the vectors
b^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}
are called
biases
. Here
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the activation function, and for a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we write

\sigma(x)=(\sigma(x_{1}),\dots,\sigma(x_{d}))\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The integer
n_{0}
is the input dimension and
n_{L+1}
is the output dimension of the network. We refer to
z^{(L+1)}
as the output of the network.

Following
[
12
,
15
]
, we consider
random
neural networks, in the sense that the weights and biases are random. We study the behavior of the random output
z^{(L+1)}
as the widths of the hidden layers tend to infinity, that is, as
n_{1},\dots,n_{L}\to\infty
.

To obtain a non-trivial limit, one needs to impose some assumptions on the weights and biases.

Assumption 2.2
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
with mean zero, variance
1
, and finite third moments. For
\ell=2,\dots,L+1
, we choose the weights as

(2.2)

w^{(\ell)}_{i,j}\sim\sqrt{\frac{C_{W}}{n_{\ell-1}}}\cdot\mu,

where
C_{W}>0
, and all these weights are sampled independently. For
\ell=1
, the weights
w_{i,j}^{(1)}
are all independent Gaussian random variables, independent also of the weights in the subsequent layers, with mean
0
and variance
C_{w}/n_{0}
.

The assumptions on the biases are

Assumption 2.3
.

The biases are Gaussian and i.i.d.. In particular

(2.3)

b^{(\ell)}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,c_{b}I_{n_{l}\times n_{l}}),

where
c_{b}>0
.

Under these assumptions, the infinite-width limit of the network is well understood
[
14
]
: as
n_{1},\dots,n_{L}\to\infty
, the output converges to a Gaussian process. In particular

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
L,n_{0},n_{L+1},r\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed, and assume that
\sigma
is polynomially bounded
to order
r
in the sense of
[
12
, Definition
2.1
]
. Under
Assumptions
2.2
and
2.3
, the process

x\mapsto z^{(L+1)}(x)

converges weakly in
C^{r-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}},\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}})
to a Gaussian process

x\mapsto{\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x),

whose
n_{L+1}
components are i.i.d. and whose covariance structure is defined recursively by

K^{(\ell+1)}(x,y)=\begin{cases}c_{b}+C_{W}\mathbb{E}[\sigma({\mathcal{Z}}^{(\ell)}(x))\sigma({\mathcal{Z}}^{(\ell)}(y))],&\ell\geq 1,\\[3.00003pt]
c_{b}+\dfrac{C_{W}}{n_{0}}x\cdot y,&\ell=0.\end{cases}

The same convergence is true when the DNN
z^{(L+1)}
has zero biases and weights satisfying
Assumption
2.2
. The limiting Gaussian process is defined recursively as above, with
c_{b}=0
.

Our main focus is on the speed of convergence of
z^{(L+1)}
to
{\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}
in suitable probability metrics. In the case of Gaussian weights, one can use techniques from Gaussian analysis and Stein’s method to derive quantitative estimates in total variation and in
p
-Wasserstein distance; see, for instance,
[
12
,
8
]
. For general weight distributions with sufficiently many moments, quantitative limit theorems in total variation and
1
-Wasserstein distance were obtained in
[
9
]
. Here we are interested in obtaining bounds in the
2
-Wasserstein distance, and we consider two distinct cases: when the network has zero biases and when the network has biases satisfying
Assumption
2.3
. In the first case, the main result is the following estimate.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\sigma\in C_{b}^{3\cdot 2^{L-1}}(\mathbb{R})
, and consider a DNN of depth
L
with weights as in
Assumption
2.2
and zero biases. Let
{\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x)
be the zero-bias Gaussian process from
Theorem
2.4
, and assume that

K^{(\ell)}(x)
is invertible for
\ell=1,\ldots,n_{L+1}
. Then

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(z^{(L+1)}(x),{\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x)\bigr)\leq C\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L}}}+\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{k}}}\Bigr)^{1/4}\biggr),

for some constant
C=C(L,n_{L+1},\sigma)
.

Observe that
Theorem
2.5
requires rather strong regularity assumptions on the activation function
\sigma
. Nevertheless, many activation functions of interest satisfy these hypotheses, for example, the tanh, the arctan or the sigmoid activation function.
The reason for the regularity assumptions is outlined in
Section
3
. On the other hand, if the neural network has Gaussian biases, we can leverage the extra smoothing obtained by them to dramatically reduce the regularity required by the activation function. In particular, our second main result is the following theorem

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
z^{(L+1)}(x)
be the output of a DNN with weights and biases satisfying
Assumptions
2.2
and
2.3
respectively. Moreover, assume that
\sigma\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
and that

K^{(\ell)}(x)
is invertible for
\ell=1,\ldots,n_{L+1}
. Then for some constant
C=C(L,n_{L+1},\sigma)

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(z^{(L+1)}(x),{\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x)\bigr)\leq C\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L}}}+\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{k}}}\Bigr)^{1/4}\biggr),

where now
{\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x)
is the biased version of the Gaussian process from
Theorem
2.4
.

The Gaussianity of the biases is essential in the proof of this improved result, just as it is in the proof of
Theorem
2.4
. Indeed, since the biases are not scaled to zero as the widths diverge, their distribution has a non-trivial effect on the limiting infinite-width process. This is also the reason why the weights
W^{(1)}
need to be Gaussian. The same point is made in
[
14
]
.

Our proof is based on the Lindeberg replacement principle: we successively replace the weights of the network by Gaussian weights with matching first and second moments, controlling the error at each step. This reduces the problem to the case of Gaussian weights, where quantitative results from
[
12
]
can be applied. We refer to
Section
3
for a detailed discussion of the argument. This replacement is captured in the following weak comparison principle, which can be viewed as a Lindeberg principle for deep neural networks.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
z^{(L+1)}
and
\tilde{z}^{(L+1)}
be two DNNs in the sense of
Definition
2.1
, with the same widths, with zero biases, and with an activation function
\sigma\in C^{3\cdot 2^{L-1}}_{b}
. Assume that the weights in
z^{(L+1)}
satisfy
Assumption
2.2
and
\tilde{z}^{(L+1)}
has Gaussian weights with matching first and second moments. Then

\Bigl|\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(\tilde{z}^{(L+1)}(x))]\Bigr|\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{L}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{k}}},

for all
F\in C^{3\cdot 2^{L-1}}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}})
.
If the DNNs
z^{(L+1)}
,
\tilde{z}^{(L+1)}
also have the same biases satisfying
Assumption
2.3
, and an activation function
\sigma\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
, then the same bound is true for all
F\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}})
with a constant of the form
C(L,n_{L+1,\sigma})\|F\|_{\infty}
. If instead,
F\in C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}})
, the constant is of the form
C(L,n_{L+1},\sigma)\|\nabla F\|_{\infty}
.

Finally,
Theorem
2.7
also yields, by a standard duality argument, a weaker but more direct bound on
1-
Wasserstein distance. When there are no biases the regularity requirements on the test function are so strong, that we will get a worse estimate than the one we can get from
Theorem
2.5
, since
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\leq{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
. When there are biases however,
Theorem
2.7
yields a bound in the total variation distance and the
1-
Wasserstein distance with optimal rates in
n_{1},\ldots,n_{L}
:

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
z^{(L+1)}(x)
be the output of a DNN with weights and biases satisfying
Assumptions
2.2
and
2.3
and with activation function
\sigma\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
. Assuming that

K^{(\ell)}(x)
is invertible for
\ell=1,\ldots,n_{L+1}
, we have

\max\{d_{TV}\bigl(z^{(L+1)}(x),{\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x)\bigr),\,{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\bigl(z^{(L+1)}(x),{\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x)\bigr)\}\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{L}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{k}}},

where
d_{TV}
denotes the total variation distance.

3.
Idea of the proof

As mentioned in the introduction, our proof is based on the Lindeberg switching principle. The general result, in the context of the sums of i.i.d. random variables, is contained in the following result

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
({\mathbf{X}}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of independent random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume that, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the coefficients of
{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
are i.i.d. with zero mean, variance
\sigma_{i}^{2}
and absolute third moments equal to
m_{3}^{(i)}
. Also, let
({\mathbf{Y}}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, be a sequence of independent Gaussian vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that the coefficients of
{\mathbf{Y}}_{i}
are all independent and have matching first and second moments to the corresponding coefficients of
{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
. Finally, let
F\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
. Then

\biggl|\mathbb{E}\biggl[F\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{X}}_{i}\biggr)\biggr]-\mathbb{E}\biggl[F\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{Y}}_{i}\biggr)\biggr]\biggr|\lesssim C_{F}\cdot d^{3}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{3}^{(i)},

where the implied constant depends only on universal constants and

C_{F}:=\max\left\{||\partial^{3}_{i,j,k}F||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,:i,j,k=1,\dots d\right\}.

The proof is classical and is based on a Taylor expansion of
F
up to the third order. Due to the moment-matching condition on the random variables, only the third-order terms survive, which are then estimated crudely using the fact that
F\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

This argument also produces the factor
d^{3}
on the right-hand side of the above bound, which points to the difficulty in applying this proposition to our case. To see it more clearly, first consider the output
z^{(L+1)}(x)
with weights
W^{(\ell)}
satisfying
Assumption
2.2
.

Definition 3.2
.

For
K=-1,\ldots,L-1
, we define
z^{(L+1;L-K)}(x)
to be the output of a DNN with weights
\tilde{W}^{(\ell)}
defined as

\tilde{W}^{(\ell)}=\begin{cases}W^{(\ell)},\quad\text{if}\quad l=1,\ldots L-K,\\
G^{(\ell)},\quad\text{if}\quad l=L-K+1,\ldots,L+1,\end{cases}

where
G^{(\ell)}
is a
n_{\ell}\times n_{\ell-1}
random matrix with independent, mean-zero Gaussian coordinates, with second moment equal to
C_{W}/n_{\ell-1}
. These are also independent from
(W^{(\ell)})_{\ell\in\{1,\ldots L+1\}}
.

In other words,
z^{(L+1;L-K)}(x)
is the output of the DNN
z^{(L+1)}
, but where we switched the weights of the layers
L-K+1,\ldots,L+1
to Gaussian weights with second moments matching the moments of the weights of the original DNN.

To estimate some distributional distance of
z^{(L+1)}(x)
from its limit, we first can estimate

\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F({\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x))],

where
{\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x)
is the Gaussian process as in
Theorem
2.4
. Using the DNN with the switched layers
z^{(L+1;L-K)}(x)
we may write the above difference as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F({\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x))]=

\displaystyle\sum_{K=-1}^{L-2}\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K-1)}(x))]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;1)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F({\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x))],

where we used the convention that
z^{(L+1;L+1)}(x)=z^{(L+1)}(x)
.
Observe that, by definition, the weights of
z^{(L+1;1)}(x)
are all Gaussian, and as such, we can bound the difference

\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;1)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F({\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x))],

using tools from Gaussian calculus. We are left now with estimating the differences

\bigg|\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K-1)}(x))]\bigg|,

for
K=1,\ldots,L-1
. When
K=0
, we can condition on
z^{(L)}(x)
and apply
Proposition
3.1
to get a bound of the form

|\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(\tilde{z}^{(L+1;L)}(x))]|\lesssim C_{F}\cdot n_{L+1}^{3}\cdot\frac{1}{n_{L}^{3/2}}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}\sigma((z^{(L)}(x))_{i})^{3}\biggr].

A bound of this form also appears in
[
14
]
in Lemma
2.5
. Under our assumption,
\sigma
is bounded and as such we obtain

|\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(\tilde{z}^{(L+1;L)}(x))]|\lesssim n_{L+1}^{3}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L}}}.

The problem arises when we want to apply
Proposition
3.1
to deeper layers. Indeed, for
K>0
, by conditioning on
z^{(L-K)}(x)
, and then applying
Proposition
3.1
we get a bound of the form

(3.1)

\bigg|\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K-1)}(x))]\bigg|\lesssim n_{L-K+1}^{3}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L-K}}},

which goes to
0
only if
n_{L-K}\gg n_{L-K+1}^{6}
. As such, to get a better bound, we must exploit the recursive structure of the network.

Let us look at the case
K=0
. We need to bound

\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-1)}(x))].

Both networks have Gaussian weights at layer
L+1
, and they have matching weights up to layer
L-1
. Conditioning at the layer output
z^{(L-1)}
yields

\mathbb{E}\left[F(z^{(L+1;L)}(x))\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[F(G^{(L+1)}\sigma(W^{(L)}\sigma(z^{(L-1)}(x))))\bigg|z^{(L-1)}(x)\right]\right],

and

\mathbb{E}\left[F(z^{(L+1;L-1)}(x))\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[F(G^{(L+1)}\sigma(G^{(L)}\sigma(z^{(L-1)}(x)))\bigg|z^{(L-1)}(x)\right]\right],

where we recall that the weights
G^{(L+1)}
,
G^{(L)}
are Gaussian. To compare these last two quantities, we must apply the Lindeberg switching principle to

W^{(L)}\sigma(z^{(L-1)}(x)),

and replace
W^{(L)}
by
G^{(L)}
with a small error. The point is that, after conditioning on
z^{(L-1)}(x)
we are looking at a very specific function of the weights
W^{(L)}=(w^{(L)}_{i,j})_{i=1,\dots n_{L+1},j=1,\dots n_{L}}
, which is given by

{\mathcal{F}}_{L}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{G^{(L+1)}}[F(G^{(L+1)}\sigma(x))],

with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L}}
.
It turns out that, when we follow the proof of the classical Lindeberg principle to this function, we can use this Gaussian averaging in the definition of
{\mathcal{F}}_{L+1}
along with the scaling implicit in
G^{(L+1)}
to get an extra decay in
n_{L}
which will ’beat’ the factor
n_{L}^{3}
in (
3.1
) and will lead to a better bound than the generic one in
Proposition
3.1
.

The situation for
K\geq 1
is a bit more complicated. Indeed, in that case, we can condition on
z^{(L-K)}(x)
, to get a function of the weights we want to ’switch’ and can be defined recursively in
K
as

{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(x)=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}(G^{(L-K+1)}\sigma(x))],

with
{\mathcal{F}}_{L,-1}(x):=F(x)
. The issue here is that for
K\geq 1
,
{\mathcal{F}}_{L-K}
is a function of
n_{L-K}
variables which will introduce an extra diverging factor in our estimates. Luckily, the same idea as before still works: using the fact that
{\mathcal{F}}_{L-K}
is an appropriate average of
F
composed with Gaussian random variables, we will be able to get an extra decaying factor in
n_{L-K}
times higher derivatives of
{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}
. We then continue recursively to get a bound that does not contain a diverging factor in the inner widths. We refer to
Theorem
4.1
for more details.

We point out that the recursion hinted at in the previous paragraph is the reason that we need a high regularity assumption on
\sigma
. If, on the other hand, we include Gaussian biases, we can run this recursion much more effectively by estimating third derivatives of
{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}
by an extra decaying factor in
n_{L-K}
times third derivatives of
{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}
. As such, we only need
\sigma\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
in this case. For more details, look at
Theorem
4.2
.

The previous arguments can be used to prove
Theorem
2.7
, which can be seen as a Lindeberg principle for fully connected deep neural networks. To get a bound for the Wasserstein distance, we will argue as follows. First, we make use of classical theorems bounding the

4.
Lindeberg switching trick

In this section, we prove the Lindeberg principle in the context of DNNs, i.e., prove
Theorem
2.7
. We split our main results into two cases: zero biases and Gaussian biases.
In the case of zero biases, the main technical estimate is contained in the following Theorem:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
z^{(L+1)}(x)
be the output of a DNN with
L
hidden layers in the sense of
Definition

2.1
. Assume that
\sigma\in C^{3\cdot 2^{L-1}}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
, where
\sigma
is the activation function, and that the weights are centered with finite third moment. Then for
F\in C^{3\cdot 2^{L-1}}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}})
, we have

\bigg|\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K-1)}(x))]\bigg|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L-K-1}}}{,\quad\forall K\in\{-1,\dots,L-2\}\,,}

where
z^{(L;L-K)}(x)
is as in
Definition
3.2
.

Proof.

For
K=-1
, we may use the usual Lindeberg exchange principle,
Proposition
3.1
. For
K=0,\ldots,L-2
we inductively define the following functions
{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{L-K}}\to\mathbb{R}

(4.1)

{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(x)=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}(G^{(L-K+1)}\sigma(x))],\qquad K\geq 0,

with
{\mathcal{F}}_{L,-1}\equiv F
.

With this definition, we can write

\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K)}(x))]=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(W^{(L-K)}\sigma(z^{(L-K-1)}(x)))]\,,

and

\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K-1)}(x))]=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(G^{(L-K)}\sigma(z^{(L-K-1)}(x))))]\,,

If
\sigma\in C^{3\cdot 2^{L-1}}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
and
F\in C^{3\cdot 2^{L-1}}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}})
then
{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}\in C^{3\cdot 2^{L-1}}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{L-K}})
. To estimate

(4.2)

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K-1)}(x))]\bigg|=

\bigg|\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(W^{(L-K)}\sigma(z^{(L-K-1)}(x)))]-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(G^{(L-K)}\sigma(z^{(L-K-1)}(x)))]\bigg|\,,

we will estimate

(4.3)

\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(W^{(L-K)}\sigma(y))]-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(G^{(L-K)}\sigma(y))]\,

uniformly in
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L-K-1}}
. The latter follows the basic steps of the proof of the classical Lindeberg switching principle closely, see for example
[
23
]
.

We define the random vectors
{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
,
{\mathbf{Y}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L-K}}
,
i=1,\ldots,n_{L-K-1}
with components

(4.4)

{\mathbf{X}}_{i}(j)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L-K-1}}}w_{i,j}^{(L-K)}\sigma(y_{j}),\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{Y}}_{i}(j)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L-K-1}}}g_{i,j}^{(L-K)}\sigma(y_{j}).

With this notation we can rewrite (
4.3
) as

(4.5)

\biggl|\mathbb{E}\biggl[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-K-1}}{\mathbf{X}}_{i}\biggr)\biggr]-\mathbb{E}\biggl[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-K-1}}{\mathbf{Y}}_{i}\biggr)\biggr]\biggr|\,,

To estimate this, we define

(4.6)

{\mathbf{S}}_{n_{L-K-1};K}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-K-1}-m}{\mathbf{X}}_{i}+{\mathbf{Y}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+2}+{\mathbf{Y}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+3}+\dots+{\mathbf{Y}}_{n_{L-K-1}}.

Since,

\biggl|\mathbb{E}\biggl[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-K-1}}{\mathbf{X}}_{i}\biggr)\biggr]-\mathbb{E}\biggl[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-K-1}}{\mathbf{Y}}_{i}\biggr)\biggr]\biggr|\leq

\sum_{m=1}^{n_{L-K-1}}|\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}({\mathbf{S}}_{n_{L-K-1};k}+{\mathbf{X}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1})]-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}({\mathbf{S}}_{n_{L-K-1};m}+Y_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1})]|\,,

it is sufficient to bound the distance

(4.7)

|\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}({\mathbf{S}}_{n_{L-K-1};m}+{\mathbf{X}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1})]-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}({\mathbf{S}}_{n_{L-K-1};m}+Y_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1})]|\,.

From Taylor, we get

{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})={\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{a=1}^{n_{L-K}}\partial_{a}{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(\mathbf{x})h_{a}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b=1}^{n_{L-K}}\partial^{2}_{a,b}{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(\mathbf{x})h_{a}h_{b}+R(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{h})\,.

where

R(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{h})=\sum_{a,b,c=1}^{n_{L-K}}{\mathcal{R}}_{a,b,c}(\mathbf{x})h_{a}h_{b}h_{c}\,,

with

{\mathcal{R}}_{a,b,c}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{2}\partial^{3}_{a,b,c}{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{h})dt\,.

Now, since the components of
{\mathbf{X}}_{n_{L-K-1-m+1}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}_{n_{L-K-1-m+1}}
have matching first and second moments, (
4.7
) is bounded above by

\mathbb{E}[|R({\mathbf{S}}_{n_{L-K-1};k};{\mathbf{X}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1}))|]+\mathbb{E}[|R({\mathbf{S}}_{n_{L-K-1};k};{\mathbf{Y}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1}))|].

Assuming that

(4.8)

\sum_{a,b,c=1}^{n_{L-K}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\partial_{a,b,c}{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(x)|\lesssim 1,

it is straightforward to estimate these terms. For example recalling (
4.4
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|R({\mathbf{S}}_{n_{L-K-1};m},{\mathbf{X}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1})|]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{a,b,c=1}^{n_{L-K}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\partial_{a,b,c}{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(x)|

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{\mathbf{X}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1}(a){\mathbf{X}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1}(b)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1}(c)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n_{L-K-1}^{3/2}}\,,

where we used Hölder’s inequality and the fact that
\sigma
is bounded.
Similarly, using the identity

\mathbb{E}[|Y|^{p}]=\mathbb{E}[|Y|^{2}]^{p/2}2^{p/2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{p+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

where
Y
is a Gaussian mean zero random variable with variance
1
, we get the same bound for
\mathbb{E}[|R({\mathbf{S}}_{n_{L-K-1};m},{\mathbf{X}}_{n_{L-K-1}-m+1})|]
, which implies that (
4.7
) is
O(1/n_{L-K-1}^{3/2})
. Summing over
m
implies that (
4.3
) is
O(1/\sqrt{n_{L-K-1}})
, uniformly in
y
. This proves that (
4.2
) is
O(1/\sqrt{n_{L-K-1}})
.

Therefore, we are left to show (
4.8
). Recalling (
2.4
), it is sufficient to show that

(4.9)

\Lambda_{3}({\mathcal{F}}_{L,K})\lesssim 1.

We do this via a recursion on
K
. We will show

(4.10)

\Lambda_{r}({\mathcal{F}}_{L,K})\lesssim\sum_{j=2}^{2r}\Lambda_{j}({\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}),

for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\sigma\in C^{r}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
{\mathcal{F}}_{L,-1}=F
, we have
\Lambda_{r}(F)\lesssim 1
, for all
r=1,\ldots,3\cdot 2^{L-1}
, we can conclude.

Recalling the recursive definition in (
4.1
), we can write

{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(x)=P_{q_{K}(x)/2}{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}(0)=:\Phi(q_{K}(x)/2),

where
P_{t}=e^{t\Delta}
and
\Phi(t):=P_{t}{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}(0)
and

(4.11)

q_{K}(x):=\frac{C_{W}}{n_{L-K}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-K}}\sigma(x_{i})^{2}.

Indeed, we have
{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(x)=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}(\sqrt{q_{K}(x)}Z)]
, where
Z
is an
n_{L-K+1}-
dimensional vector with independent Gaussian entries of mean zero and variance
1
.

Now, we observe that

(4.12)

\frac{d^{k}}{d^{k}t}\Phi(t)=\mathbb{E}[\Delta^{k}{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}(\sqrt{t}Z)].

Now, we have

(4.13)

\partial_{a_{1:r}}{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{d^{k}}{d^{k}t}\Phi(q(x))\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k}}\prod_{B\in\pi}\partial_{a_{B}}q(x),

where we used the Faá di Bruno formula
[
13
]
. We recall that
\Pi_{k}
denotes the set of partitions of
\{1,\ldots,r\}
into
k
parts, and
\partial_{a_{B}}:=\prod_{i\in B}\partial_{a_{i}}
. Recalling (
4.11
), we see that, for
B=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{|B|}\}

\partial_{a_{B}}q(x)=\frac{C_{W}}{n_{L-K}}\frac{d^{|B|}}{dx^{|B|}}(\sigma(x_{a_{i_{1}}})^{2})\mathbb{1}_{\{a_{i_{1}}=a_{i_{2}}\ldots=a_{i_{|B|}}\}}.

since
\sigma\in C_{b}^{2r}(\mathbb{R})
, this identity implies that

(4.14)

\biggl|\sum_{a_{1:r}=1}^{n_{L-K}}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k}}\prod_{B\in\pi}\partial_{a_{B}}q(x)\biggr|\lesssim 1.

Now, by plugging (
4.12
) to (
4.13
), summing over

, and using the above estimate we have

\Lambda_{r}({\mathcal{F}}_{L,K})\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sup_{x}\mathbb{E}[|\Delta^{k}{\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}(\sqrt{q(x)}Z)]\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{2r}\Lambda_{k}({\mathcal{F}}_{L,K-1}),

which concludes the proof.

Remark 4.3
.

The previous theorems compare the output of a DNN with Gaussian weights in the last
k
layers to the output of a DNN with Gaussian weights in the last
k+1
layers. For our purposes, we will also need to estimate

\bigg|\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L;L-K)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L;L-K-1)}(x))]\bigg|.

As usual the problem is that
z^{(L;L-K)}(x)
is an
n_{L}-
dimensional vector, and that
n_{L}\to\infty
. It is clear that in this case, there is no hope of estimating the above quantity uniformly in
n_{L}
, for general
F
. Luckily, there is no need. As an application of
Theorem
3.3
below, we will only need to consider functions
F
that depend on a fixed number of components of
z^{(L;L-K)}(x)
. For such functions, we can apply
Theorem
4.1
to obtain

(4.25)

\bigg|\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L;L-K)}_{i_{1}}(x),\ldots,z^{(L;L-K)}_{i_{\alpha}}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L;L-K-1)}_{i_{1}}(x),\ldots,z^{(L;L-K-1)}_{i_{\alpha}}(x))]\bigg|\leq C\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L-K-1}}},

where
i_{1},\dots i_{\alpha}
are some fixed indices and
C=C(L,\alpha,\sigma)
some constant.

Now,
Theorem
2.7
can be proved by a simple telescoping sum trick, as described in
Section
3
:

Proof of
Theorem
2.7
.

We can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F({\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x))]=

\displaystyle\sum_{K=-1}^{L-2}\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;L-K-1)}(x))]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;1)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F({\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x))].

Applying
Theorem
4.1
in the case of zero biases or
Theorem
4.2
in the case of Gaussian biases, yields that the sum in the above equation is bounded above by
C\sum_{K=0}^{L-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L-K}}}
. From
[
12
, Theorem
3.3
]
, we have

\mathbb{E}[F(z^{(L+1;1)}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[F({\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x))]\lesssim\sum_{K=0}^{L-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{L-K}}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

We follow the sketch described in

Proof.

For

\mu_{\omega}:=\mathcal{L}(X\mid{\mathcal{F}})(\omega),\qquad\nu_{\omega}:=\mathcal{L}(Y\mid{\mathcal{F}})(\omega).

Choose a probability kernel

\mu_{\omega}
and
\nu_{\omega}

\pi

\pi(A):=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{\omega}(A)],\qquad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Then
\pi
is a coupling of
\mathcal{L}(X)
and
\mathcal{L}(Y)
. Hence

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}(X,Y)^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{2}\,d\pi(x,y)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{2}\,d\pi_{\omega}(x,y)\Bigr].

Since
\pi_{\omega}
is optimal,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{2}\,d\pi_{\omega}(x,y)={\mathcal{W}}_{2}(\mu_{\omega},\nu_{\omega})^{2}

for a.e.
\omega
. This gives the claim.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

If
z^{(L+1)}(x)
is the output of a DNN with zero biases, weights satisfying
Assumption
2.2
and a bounded activation function, we have

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(z^{(L+1)}(x),z^{(L+1;L)}(x)\bigr)\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{n_{L+1}}{n_{L}}},

where
z^{(L+1;L)}(x)
is as in
Definition
3.2
. The same bound is true if
z^{(L+1)}(x)
has Gaussian biases, according to
Assumption
2.3
.

Proof.

We only prove the case of zero biases, as the case of Gaussian biases is identical.

First, recall a one-dimensional Wasserstein estimate, obtained by specializing a theorem of Bonis
[
6
, Theorem 1]
.
If
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
is a sum of independent centered real-valued random variables and
G
is Gaussian with the same variance as
S_{n}
, then

(5.1)

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}(S_{n},G)^{2}\lesssim\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}|^{4}]}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]}.

The implicit constant depends only on the universal constant in
[
6
]
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
z^{(L)}(x)
, and set

a_{i}:=\sqrt{\frac{C_{W}}{n_{L}}}\,\sigma\bigl(z_{i}^{(L)}(x)\bigr),\qquad i=1,\dots,n_{L}.

Then, conditionally on
{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
, the coefficients
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{L}}
are
deterministic.

For each
r=1,\dots,n_{L+1}
, write

z_{r}^{(L+1)}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}a_{i}\,w^{(L+1)}_{r,i}\,,\qquad z_{r}^{(L+1;L)}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}a_{i}\,g^{(L+1)}_{r,i}\,,

where
(w^{(L+1)}_{r,i})_{r,i}
are i.i.d. with law
\mu
specified in
Assumption
2.2
, and

(g^{(L+1)}_{r,i})_{r,i}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables, all
independent of
{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
.

Set

v:=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}a_{i}^{2}=\frac{C_{W}}{n_{L}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}\sigma\bigl(z_{i}^{(L)}(x)\bigr)^{2}.

If
v=0
, then
a_{i}=0
for all
i
, and therefore

z^{(L+1)}(x)=z^{(L+1;L)}(x)=0

almost surely conditionally on
{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
. Thus the conditional

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
-distance is zero.

Assume now that
v>0
. Fix
r\in\{1,\dots,n_{L+1}\}
, and define

X_{r,i}:=a_{i}\,w^{(L+1)}_{r,i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n_{L}.

Conditionally on
{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
, the variables
X_{r,i}
are independent,
centered, and satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}\mathbb{E}[X_{r,i}^{2}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}]=v.

Moreover,

z^{(L+1)}_{r}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}X_{r,i}.

Let
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, independent of everything else. Applying (
5.1
) (conditionally on
{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
), we obtain

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1)}_{r}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}),\mathcal{L}(\sqrt{v}\,G\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L})\bigr)^{2}\lesssim v^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}\mathbb{E}[|X_{r,i}|^{4}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}]\lesssim\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}a_{i}^{4}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}a_{i}^{2}}.

Since
z_{r}^{(L+1;L)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v)
, this gives

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1)}_{r}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}),\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1;L)}_{r}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L})\bigr)^{2}\lesssim\frac{C_{W}}{n_{L}}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}\sigma\bigl(z_{i}^{(L)}(x)\bigr)^{4}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}\sigma\bigl(z_{i}^{(L)}(x)\bigr)^{2}}\,\mathbb{1}_{\{\sum_{i}\sigma(z_{i}^{(L)}(x))^{2}>0\}}.

Now observe that, conditionally on
{\mathcal{F}}_{L}
, the coordinates

(z_{1}^{(L+1)}(x),\dots,z_{n_{L+1}}^{(L+1)}(x))
are independent, and the coordinates
(z_{1}^{(L+1;L)}(x),\dots,z_{n_{L+1}}^{(L+1;L)}(x))
are
also independent. Hence

\mathcal{L}\bigl(z^{(L+1)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}\bigr)=\bigotimes_{r=1}^{n_{L+1}}\mathcal{L}(z_{r}^{(L+1)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}),\qquad\mathcal{L}\bigl(z^{(L+1;L)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}\bigr)=\bigotimes_{r=1}^{n_{L+1}}\mathcal{L}(z_{r}^{(L+1;L)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}).

Taking the product of optimal couplings in each coordinate, we get

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}),\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1;L)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L})\bigr)^{2}\leq\sum_{r=1}^{n_{L+1}}{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(z_{r}^{(L+1)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}),\mathcal{L}(z_{r}^{(L+1;L)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L})\bigr)^{2}.

Therefore

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}),\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1;L)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L})\bigr)^{2}\lesssim\frac{n_{L+1}\,C_{W}}{n_{L}}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}\sigma\bigl(z_{i}^{(L)}(x)\bigr)^{4}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L}}\sigma\bigl(z_{i}^{(L)}(x)\bigr)^{2}}\,\mathbb{1}_{\{\sum_{i}\sigma(z_{i}^{(L)}(x))^{2}>0\}}.

Since
\sigma
is bounded, on the event where the denominator is positive,

\frac{\sum_{i}\sigma(z_{i}^{(L)}(x))^{4}}{\sum_{i}\sigma(z_{i}^{(L)}(x))^{2}}\leq\|\sigma\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Thus

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}),\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1;L)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L})\bigr)^{2}\lesssim\frac{n_{L+1}}{n_{L}}.

Finally,
Lemma
5.1
yields

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(z^{(L+1)}(x),z^{(L+1;L)}(x)\bigr)^{2}\lesssim\mathbb{E}\Bigl[{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L}),\mathcal{L}(z^{(L+1;L)}(x)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{L})\bigr)^{2}\Bigr]\lesssim\frac{n_{L+1}}{n_{L}}.

This proves the claim.
∎

Before moving on with the proof of
Theorem
2.5
, we need an additional lemma, which quantifies the rate of convergence of a DNN with Gaussian weights to its infinite width limit. We will only need this for special observables of the layer
L
. In particular, we have the following lemma

Lemma 5.3
.

Fix
\alpha\in\{1,2,3,4\}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
.
Assume that the infinite-width covariances are non-degenerate on the singleton input set
\{x\}
in the sense of
[
19
]
.
Let

\Phi_{\alpha}(u_{1},\dots,u_{\alpha}):=\prod_{r=1}^{\alpha}\sigma(u_{r})^{2}.

Then there exists a constant
C_{\alpha,x}<\infty
, independent of the widths
n_{1},\dots,n_{L-1}
, such that for every
\alpha
-tuple
(i_{1},\dots,i_{\alpha})
of pairwise distinct indices in
\{1,\dots,n_{L}\}
,

(5.2)

\left|\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Phi_{\alpha}\bigl(z^{(L;1)}_{i_{1}}(x),\dots,z^{(L;1)}_{i_{\alpha}}(x)\bigr)\Bigr]-\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Phi_{\alpha}(G_{1},\dots,G_{\alpha})\Bigr]\right|\leq C_{\alpha,x}\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\frac{1}{n_{k}},

where
G_{1},\dots,G_{\alpha}
are i.i.d. centered Gaussian random variables with variance
{\mathcal{K}}^{(L)}(x)
.

Proof.

Since
\sigma\in C_{b}^{3\cdot 2^{L-1}}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
, the function
\Phi_{\alpha}
is bounded and Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{\alpha}
.
Fix pairwise distinct indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{\alpha}
, and consider the random vector

U_{I}(x):=\bigl(z^{(L;1)}_{i_{1}}(x),\dots,z^{(L;1)}_{i_{\alpha}}(x)\bigr).

Because the rows of each Gaussian weight matrix are i.i.d.,
U_{I}(x)
has the same law as the output at layer
L
of a fully Gaussian network of depth
L
, evaluated at the single input
x
, with output dimension
\alpha
.
The corresponding Gaussian-process limit is the vector
(G_{1},\dots,G_{\alpha})
, whose coordinates are independent and distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,{\mathcal{K}}^{(L)}(x))
.

Applying
[
19
, Theorem 4.2]
with
p=1
, singleton input set
X=\{x\}
, fixed output dimension
\alpha
, and layer
\ell=L
, we obtain

{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\bigl(U_{I}(x),(G_{1},\dots,G_{\alpha})\bigr)\leq C_{\alpha,x}\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\frac{1}{n_{k}}.

Finally, by Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality,

\left|\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{\alpha}(U_{I}(x))]-\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{\alpha}(G_{1},\dots,G_{\alpha})]\right|\leq\|\Phi_{\alpha}\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\,{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\bigl(U_{I}(x),(G_{1},\dots,G_{\alpha})\bigr),

which gives (
5.2
).
∎

Proof of
Theorem
2.5
.

From
Lemma
5.2
, it suffices to bound

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}({\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x),z^{(L+1;L)}(x))
. From
Theorem
3.3
, we have

(5.3)

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}({\mathcal{Z}}^{(L+1)}(x),z^{(L+1;L)}(x))\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(K)}}\,\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\|A-K\|_{HS}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}+\frac{2^{3/2}d^{1/4}}{\lambda(K)^{3/2}}\,\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\|A-K\|_{HS}^{4}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Here,

A=\frac{C_{W}}{n_{L}}\left\lVert\sigma(z^{L}(x))\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\text{I}_{n_{L+1}},

which is the variance of
z^{(L+1;L)}(x)
conditioned on
z^{(L)}(x)
, and
{\mathcal{K}}^{(L+1)(x)}
is as in
Theorem
2.4
.

We have

\displaystyle\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\frac{C_{W}}{n_{L}}\left\lVert\sigma(z^{L}(x))\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\text{I}_{n_{L+1}}-{\mathcal{K}}^{(L+1)}(x)\right\rVert_{HS}^{p}\right]$\mbox{}

The only thing left to prove is the following elementary lemma, which we used in the above proof

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
be even, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be bounded real-valued random variables.
Set

\overline{Y}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i},\qquad m:=\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}].

Then there exists a constant
C_{p}
, depending only on
p
and
\sup_{i}\|Y_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}
, such that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\overline{Y}_{n}-m|^{p}\bigr]\leq C_{p}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{p}\left|\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\alpha}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\prod_{r=1}^{\alpha}Y_{i_{r}}\Bigr]-m^{\alpha}\right|.

Proof.

Since
p
is even,
|\overline{Y}_{n}-m|^{p}=(\overline{Y}_{n}-m)^{p}
.
By the binomial formula,

(\overline{Y}_{n}-m)^{p}=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{p}\binom{p}{\alpha}(-m)^{p-\alpha}\,\overline{Y}_{n}^{\alpha}.

Taking expectations gives

\mathbb{E}[(\overline{Y}_{n}-m)^{p}]=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{p}\binom{p}{\alpha}(-m)^{p-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}[\overline{Y}_{n}^{\alpha}].

Since

\mathbb{E}[\overline{Y}_{n}^{\alpha}]=\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\alpha}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\prod_{r=1}^{\alpha}Y_{i_{r}}\Bigr],

and
\mathbb{E}[\overline{Y}_{n}^{0}]=1=m^{0}
, we may rewrite

\mathbb{E}[(\overline{Y}_{n}-m)^{p}]=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{p}\binom{p}{\alpha}(-m)^{p-\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\alpha}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\prod_{r=1}^{\alpha}Y_{i_{r}}\Bigr]-m^{\alpha}\right),

because

\sum_{\alpha=0}^{p}\binom{p}{\alpha}(-m)^{p-\alpha}m^{\alpha}=(m-m)^{p}=0.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}[|\overline{Y}_{n}-m|^{p}]\leq\sum_{\alpha=0}^{p}\binom{p}{\alpha}|m|^{p-\alpha}\left|\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\alpha}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\prod_{r=1}^{\alpha}Y_{i_{r}}\Bigr]-m^{\alpha}\right|.

The term
\alpha=0
vanishes identically, and since the
Y_{i}
are bounded,
|m|
is bounded by
\sup_{i}\|Y_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
This yields the claim.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of
Theorem
3.3

Here we prove
Theorem
3.3
. The proof is inspired by
[
19
]
, and a version of it has been proven in
[
8
]
.

Remark B.1
.

Let
X
be a centered random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a
\sigma
-field such that, conditionally on
{\mathcal{F}}
, the law of
X
is Gaussian with covariance matrix
M
.
Then
X
has the same law as
\sqrt{M}\,N
, where
N\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}(0,I_{d})
is independent of
M
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

Since the value of
W_{2}
is bounded above by the cost of any coupling, we may realize
F
and
G
on the same probability space using the same Gaussian vector
N
.
Then

W_{2}(F,G)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|\sqrt{A}\,N-\sqrt{K}\,N\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{K}\|_{HS}^{2}.

Set

\lambda:=\lambda(K)>0,\qquad E:=\{\|A-K\|_{op}\leq\lambda/2\}.

We split

\mathbb{E}\|\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{K}\|_{HS}^{2}=T_{1}+T_{2},

where

T_{1}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{K}\|_{HS}^{2}\,\mathbb{1}_{E}\right],\qquad T_{2}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{K}\|_{HS}^{2}\,\mathbb{1}_{E^{c}}\right].

On the event
E
, a standard perturbation bound for the matrix square root gives

\|\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{K}\|_{HS}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\|A-K\|_{HS}.

Therefore

T_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}\|A-K\|_{HS}^{2}.

On
E^{c}
, the Powers–Størmer inequality implies

\|\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{K}\|_{HS}^{2}\leq\|A-K\|_{S_{1}}\leq\sqrt{d}\,\|A-K\|_{HS}.

Since on
E^{c}
,

\|A-K\|_{HS}\geq\|A-K\|_{op}>\frac{\lambda}{2},

it follows that

\mathbb{1}_{E^{c}}\leq\left(\frac{2\|A-K\|_{HS}}{\lambda}\right)^{3}.

Hence

T_{2}\leq\frac{8\sqrt{d}}{\lambda^{3}}\mathbb{E}\|A-K\|_{HS}^{4}.

Combining the bounds for
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, we obtain

W_{2}(F,G)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}\|A-K\|_{HS}^{2}+\frac{8\sqrt{d}}{\lambda^{3}}\mathbb{E}\|A-K\|_{HS}^{4}.

Taking square roots and using
\sqrt{x+y}\leq\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}
concludes the proof.
∎
