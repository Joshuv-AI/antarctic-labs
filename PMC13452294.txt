Patient and oncologist expectations of functional prognosis among older adults with acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Marielle Jensen-Battaglia, Soroush Mortaz-Hedjri, Rachel Blumberg, Emily Oh, Cait Fallone-Sharma, Ying Wang, Laura Nafis, Jason Mendler, Rachel Rodenbach, Eric Huselton, Jane Liesveld, Joanne Terhune, James Terhune, Emily M. Agree, Robert C. Block, Christopher L. Seplaki, Supriya G. Mohile, Kah Poh Loh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Older adults with AML have different expectations compared to their oncologist regarding how their physical function will change.•Future research is needed to understand the clinical impact of discordant expectations and how to support communication about functioning.

Introduction

Older adults with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) face challenges in making treatment decisions due to the sudden onset of disease, potential travel to an unfamiliar medical center to receive final diagnosis, and the need to begin treatment quickly.1,2 Patients may feel shocked by their diagnosis, uncertain of their options, and have difficulty processing information presented by their oncologist.2 Whether older adults with AML understand their prognosis is particularly important in the context of treatment decisions, given their greater risk for treatment-related toxicities and worse survival outcomes relative to younger adults.3, 4, 5, 6 Unfortunately, patient understanding of chance for survival or cure (prognostic understanding/concordance) is often poor, with at least half of the patients with AML having different expectations from their oncologist.4,7,8 Beyond survival, an important but sometimes overlooked aspect of prognosis is functional prognosis, for example how patients can expect their ability to complete daily activities will change in response to their disease and treatment. This is particularly salient for older adults with cancer, most of whom prioritize quality of life and physical function over survival when making decisions about their cancer care.9, 10, 11, 12 Although regulatory bodies such as the US Food and Drug Administration now recommend including patient reported outcomes including physical function in clinical trials of cancer treatments, historically this was rare, which may make communicating functional prognosis challenging for both patients and oncologists.13,14

Functional prognosis may be especially important for older adults with AML who are at high risk for rapid (although potentially short term) declines in function related to chemotherapy and stem cell transplant.15,16 Mobility-related physical function (eg, standing, walking) is a clinically relevant aspect of function which is feasible to assess and modifiable in many cases, as well as representing a “sentinel” decline that occurs at relatively younger ages and often precedes need for assistance with self-care tasks.17, 18, 19, 20 Changes in mobility are also a patient-centered outcome closely tied to ability to stay in one’s own home throughout the aging process, or “age in place,” an important aging-related goal for most older adults in the United States.21 Unfortunately, most older adults do not have a plan for increased services and supports to remain in (or return to) their homes in the event of a functional decline.22 Understanding their functional prognosis may allow older adults to proactively build these valued plans.23 Overall, there is a need to understand and optimize communication about functional prognosis between older adults with AML and their oncologists.24

There is a lack of evidence regarding patient and oncologist expectations of functional prognosis among older adults with cancer, which may be of particular importance for older adults with AML at risk for functional decline. To our knowledge, there are no studies which compare patient and oncologist expectations of physical function, which may have significant implications for treatment decision-making. We address this gap using data from older adults with AML participating in a randomized clinical trial of a communication support tool. Our primary objective was to describe patient and oncologist expectations about functional prognosis, and secondary objectives were to estimate the agreement between patient and oncologist expectations and explore differences by study arm.

Materials and methods

Study design

We included patients participating in the University of Rochester-Geriatric Oncology Assessment for Acute Myeloid Leukemia (UR-GOAL-2; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05335369), a pilot randomized controlled trial evaluating the preliminary efficacy of a patient-centered communication tool on shared decision-making among older adults with AML. Development of the intervention has been previously described.25 Patients were aged ≥60 years and enrolled between June 2022 and March 2025 from the Wilmot Cancer Institute/University of Rochester Medical Center. All patients were newly diagnosed with AML and had not yet started treatments.26 After enrollment, patients were randomized to the intervention or usual care study arm. Patients in both arms had information collected regarding geriatric vulnerabilities, information preferences and prognostic awareness. In the intervention arm, patients additionally watched a disease-specific education video, and both patients and their oncologists received a report summarizing results (geriatric vulnerabilities, preferences, and awareness) before clinical encounters in which treatment decisions were discussed.25 Primary outcomes and patient/oncologist expectations of functional prognosis were evaluated 1 to 4 weeks after the last treatment decision-making encounter. This study received ethical approval from the University of Rochester Research Subjects Review Board (approval no. 00007102), and all participants provided informed consent.

Measures

We captured patient demographic and clinical characteristics, patient preference for aging in place, and patient and oncologist expectation of functional prognosis. A full list of analytic variables, their measurement, and associated references is provided in supplemental Table 1.

Oncologist and patient demographic and patient clinical characteristics

Oncologist and patient demographics (including gender) were obtained by self-report. Patient clinical characteristics were abstracted from the medical record by study staff. Cancer treatment was characterized into intensive (inpatient “7+3” cytarabine and anthracycline such as daunorubicin), less intensive (primarily outpatient venetoclax and/or hypomethylating agents, such as azacitidine), or supportive care (infusions but no cancer-directed therapy). Assessments of patient geriatric vulnerabilities included objective physical function (short physical performance battery or virtual short physical performance battery), cognition (Mini-Cog), depression (Geriatric Depression Scale-15) and self-reported physical function (Katz activities of daily living, older Americans Resources Survey instrumental activities of daily living). Validated cut scores were used to define presence of geriatric syndromes where appropriate (supplemental Table 1).

Patient preference for aging in place

Patients rated how important it is for them to remain in their own home as they grow older (most important, very important, somewhat important, or not important). This question uses wording from the 2002 Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research Community Partnerships for Older Adults Survey to allow comparison with a representative sample of older adults in the United States.27

Patient and oncologist expectation of functional prognosis

Patients self-reported whether they expected their mobility to get worse, stay the same, or improve over (1) the next 6 months, and (2) the next 1 year. Each patient’s treating oncologist also rated their expectations for the patient’s mobility over the same timeframes. These timeframes were chosen to reflect distinctions in patient expectations for response to initial treatment vs their long-term expectations for living with their disease.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

We report descriptive statistics (percentages, means, ranges, and standard deviation [SD]) of the study sample of demographic and clinical characteristics and expectations for functional prognosis overall and by study arm. Concordance between patient and oncologist expectations is reported descriptively. Because patients who anticipate they will stay the same or improve while their oncologist believes their function will worsen may be unable to plan for this decline and at risk for adverse outcomes,22 we also explored this specific type of “clinically meaningful” discordance. We further describe concordance by initial treatment type but a priori do not compare this by study arm or generate analytic statistics for these subgroups due to small cell sizes.

Analytic statistics

For binary measures of agreement (ie, clinically meaningful discordance), we compare proportions across study arms using the χ² or Fisher exact tests. Patient and oncologist agreement incorporating all response levels was analyzed using a prevalence- and bias-adjusted κ statistic. This represents the proportion of agreement beyond that expected by chance while avoiding bias from circumstances in which 1 level of the measure is much more common than the others.28 In addition, the rating scale for expectation of future function is ordinal: (1) get worse, (2) stay the same, (3) improve. We assert that a circumstance in which the patient expects their function to improve but the oncologist expects it to worsen represents a greater magnitude of disagreement than the same scenario in which the oncologist expects function to remain the same. The weighted κ allows the greatest disagreement (1 vs 3) to contribute 0 weight, whereas lesser disagreement (1 or 3 vs 2) contributes 50% of the weight of observations in which patients and oncologists have the same rating. Consistent with others, we consider κ <0 as disagreement only, 0 to 0.20 as no agreement, 0.21 to 0.29 as minimal agreement, 0.40 to 0.59 as weak agreement, 0.60 to 0.79 as moderate agreement, 0.80 to 0.90 as strong agreement, and 1.0 as perfect agreement.29 We report these κ values overall and by study arm. We used nonparametric bootstrap resampling, calculating the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the difference via the percentile method, with P values based on the SD of the bootstrap distribution. Missing data were evaluated and reported in the participant inclusion flow. Because this was an exploratory secondary analysis, patients with missing data were dropped from the analytic sample.

Because treating oncologists could have multiple patients enrolled in the study, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis to explore whether differences in patient and oncologist agreement by study arm were dependent on treating oncologist. Because of the sample size and distribution of patient and oncologist expectations, we limited this to reporting the distribution of patients in each study arm by oncologist, and descriptive statistics of patient and oncologist discordance by study arm at the oncologist level for oncologists with ≥10 enrolled patients.

All P values were from 2-sided tests with α = 0.05, and analyses were completed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC).

Oncologist characteristics, descriptive results

The 77 patients reflected in Table 1 received care from 8 oncologists. On average, oncologists were aged 43.7 years (SD, 12.7; range 36.0-70.4). Half of the oncologists reported being female (n = 4 [50.0%]), and all reported non-Hispanic ethnicity. White race was the most common (n = 7 [76.9%]) followed by Asian (n = 1 [12.5%]).

Concordance

Descriptive overlap between patient and oncologist expectations is shown as a bubble plot in Figure 2. Exact concordance between patients and oncologists is reflected by the circles on the counterdiagonal (from lower left to upper right). For both the 6-month and 1-year timeframes, ∼32% of patients had expectations that matched those of their oncologist, with the remaining 68% discordant. There were some differences by study arm. Specifically, clinically meaningful discordance was more common in the usual care arm (56% at 6 months, 44% at 1 year) than in the UR-GOAL arm (27% at 6 months, 36% at 1 year). This difference was statistically significant for the 6-month time point (P = .010) but not 1 year (P = .520). We also describe concordance by initial treatment. At 6 months, 27.6% of patients initiating intensive chemotherapy expected their function to stay the same, and a further 17.2% expected it to improve while their oncologist expected it to worsen (44.8% clinically meaningful discordance). This was slightly better among those initiating less-intensive chemotherapies, in which 35.6% of patients either expected their function to stay the same (22.2%) or improve (13.3%) while their oncologist expected it to worsen. Exact concordance was low among both groups (intensive, 31.0%; less intensive, 33.3%) for this time point. At 1 year 38.5% of patients selecting intensive chemotherapy remained clinically meaningfully discordant vs 41.0% of those on less-intensive chemotherapy, whereas exact concordance was unchanged for the 2 groups (supplemental Figures 1 and 2).

Expectations of physical function, analytic results

Figure 4 displays the agreement between patient and oncologist expectations for 6 months and 1 year as bias-corrected weighted κ and 95% CIs. Overall values were κ = 0.110 in the entire sample for 6 months and 0.080 for 1 year, with 95% CIs including 0 and negative values, suggesting no or very slight agreement between patient and oncologist ratings. This conclusion was consistent for both study arms and both time points. Patients and oncologists in the UR-GOAL arm demonstrated numerically, although not statistically significantly, higher κ for both time points than those in usual care (P = .495 and P = .456 respectively). Sensitivity analysis

Oncologists were not randomized by study arm, therefore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to explore whether differences by study arm were independent of the treating oncologist. First, we evaluated the distribution of patient study arm by oncologist and found that all but 1 oncologist had patients randomized to both study arms (this oncologist only had 1 patient included in the analysis). The top 2 recruiting oncologists had sufficient numbers of patients and distribution of expectations to support comparisons by study arm (33 and 16 patients, respectively) at the oncologist level. For both oncologists, more of their patients in the usual care arm were discordant (oncologist 1: 13 [72%] usual care, 7 [47%] UR-GOAL; oncologist 2: 3 [60%] usual care, 3 [27%] UR-GOAL), although these differences were not statistically significant (Fisher exact P = .169 and P = .300, respectively).

Discussion

Our prospective study of older adults with newly diagnosed AML provides an initial characterization of patient and oncologist expectation of functional prognosis for this population. We found oncologists were much more likely to expect patients to decline in the next 6 months or 1 year than patients themselves (>50% of oncologist ratings vs 16%-20% of patient ratings). Across both time points and study arms, patient and oncologist agreement on functional prognosis was lacking (κ < 0.2). In addition, 40% of patients demonstrated clinically meaningful discordance by expecting their function would stay the same or improve, whereas their oncologist expected a decline. This has potential clinical significance, because 94% of patients felt it was important to age in place, and worsening function may make this more difficult. Although this was an exploratory outcome for this clinical trial, these preliminary findings lay the groundwork for future studies to understand the determinants and health effects of patient and oncologist concordance in functional prognosis. This, in turn, may inform targeted use of resources to prevent actual decline, an important application that was outside the scope of this study.

Most patients in our sample expected their function to stay the same (6 months, 42%; 1 year, 31%) or improve (6 months, 43%; 1 year, 49%). There are limited descriptive data characterizing expectations of future function among older patients with cancer initiating new treatments, and our study makes a novel contribution in this area. However, adults with cancer receiving inpatient rehabilitation anticipate improvements in their self-care related physical function and quality of life by the time of discharge.31,32 It may be that those newly diagnosed and initiating cancer treatment expect, on average, that treatment will improve their health and their function along with it. In the general population, older adults qualitatively identify physical functioning as an important aging-related expectation33 and are able to identify health circumstances (eg, hospitalization) that may affect their function.34 However, openly anticipating decline before it occur may be difficult for some older adults who view functional decline as a negative part of the aging process.23 Our analysis showed patient expectations for function are discordant from those of their oncologist 68% of the time. Prior work among adults with hematologic malignancies found 61% had discordant expectations with their oncologist regarding the chance of cure before consultation, 50% after consultation with their oncologist.8 In our study, discordance in functional prognosis was even more prevalent, although all patients had at least 1 clinic visit with their oncologist before being asked about their expectations. The factors contributing to this discordance are likely complex. For instance, patients may not have discussed their function with their oncologist. With regard to survival-related prognosis, only 60% of oncologists discuss this with most of their patients, and 1 in 5 never readdress prognosis after that initial discussion.35 Barriers to discussing prognosis include uncertainty due to the remitting/relapsing nature of the disease course, possibility of cure even with advanced disease (eg, stem cell transplant), and often rapid decline after treatment failure.36,37 These same factors doubtless make communication about functional prognosis challenging during clinical encounters, which are often scheduled on short notice and focused on initiating urgently needed cancer treatments. We provide initial evidence that discordant expectations of functional prognosis are: (1) common for patients and their oncologists and (2) similar to or greater in magnitude than discordance in chance of cure.

Our exploratory analysis also suggests that patients receiving a communication intervention are less likely to demonstrate clinically meaningful discordance from their oncologist in expectations of physical function. This may have several explanations. First, the UR-GOAL intervention includes a geriatric assessment of physical function shared with the oncologist in a report of UR-GOAL tool results before the treatment decision-making clinical encounter. It is possible that this increased oncologist awareness of physical function directly, leading to more discussion of functioning during the clinical encounters. Second, patient-ranked treatment priorities including changes in physical function or quality of life were also part of the UR-GOAL report. Patient and oncologist discussion of these treatment priorities may have indirectly led to a conversation about how treatment choices would affect patient function. Lastly, the UR-GOAL tool may have improved overall quality of the treatment discussions, fostering an interactional communication style and an environment that encourages patients or caregivers to ask and clarify expectations in general, including those related to physical function.38 Future qualitative analysis of transcripts from these clinical encounters is needed to provide insight into the potential mechanisms.

Our work stops short of showing any health implications of patient/oncologist discordance in expectation of functional prognosis. The literature on survival and cure-related discordance has shown discordant patients have increased risk for hospitalization and decreased use of hospice services at end of life, therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize discordance in expectations of physical function may have clinical significance.39, 40, 41, 42 We show that older adults with newly diagnosed AML value aging in place to a similar extent as older adults in the general US population and suggest discordance between patient and oncologist expectations may limit patient ability to proactively plan for declines in their physical function and age in place.43 However, we acknowledge limitations to this assertion. Although patients desire honest prognostic information, survival-related prognostic understanding has been associated with depression, anxiety, and worse quality of life for patients with advanced cancers in the time period immediately following diagnosis.44,45 Although these negative outcomes may be moderated by coping strategies, optimistic health beliefs may have potential benefits. For example, patient expectation of treatment-related toxicities is associated with more severe patient-rated toxicities, even among those who have never previously received cancer treatment.46 Conversely, patients who expect improvements in their symptoms (eg, low back pain) are more likely to report those improvements, even after accounting for other factors.47 Among older adults, those who have more negative expectations about growing older tend to report worse functioning over time compared with otherwise similar individuals.48 Therefore, future research is needed to generate empirical data regarding the clinical impacts of patient/oncologist discordance in functional prognosis.

These findings should be understood in the context of their limitations. First, this is an exploratory analysis of a randomized controlled trial that was not designed or powered to detect differences by study arm with regard to our novel measure of patient and oncologist expectation of functional prognosis. In addition, missing data on functional prognosis were more common among those with worse health. Therefore, our results reflect a relatively more robust patient population compared to those seen in clinical practice. Second, with regard to patient and oncologist concordance, we did not compare expectations with actual change in physical function; therefore, it is possible that either the oncologist, patient, or both were inaccurate in their expectations. Although there is evidence that pretreatment rehabilitation clinician ratings of future patient functional outcomes are similar to actual function at discharge,31 few adults with cancer receive cancer-specific rehabilitation.49, 50, 51 Therefore, as in our study, oncologists are the most likely source of information about future function for patients, and future research is needed to determine the accuracy of expectations. Third, we were limited in our ability to analyze patient and oncologist expectations by initial treatment due to the small number of patients in our study who received best supportive care (n = 2). In our sensitivity analysis, we show that clinically meaningful discordance is similar for patients with intensive and less-intensive chemotherapies as their initial treatment, and not substantially different for short term (6 months) vs longer term (1 year) expectations. However, the clinical impact of functional prognosis may vary by initial treatment. For example, a patient receiving best supportive care who anticipates their function will improve in the next year may be at greater risk for future unmet needs than a patient who has chosen intensive chemotherapy and anticipates improvement in the same timeframe. Lastly, although patient characteristics were well balanced by study arm, there may be differences in patient and oncologist agreement by treating oncologist. Although we show a similar direction of effect in favor of the UR-GOAL intervention for patients of 2 of our treating oncologists, our small sample size and skewed distribution of patient and oncologist expectations limit our ability to explore this further.

Conclusions

Our study provides an initial description of a novel construct, patient and oncologist expectation of functional prognosis. Given the high risk for declines in function among older adults with AML, characterizing this construct is an important first step in a line of research that will ultimately encompass determinants and clinical impacts of this concordance. Our preliminary evidence of a positive effect on patient/oncologist concordance from the UR-GOAL communication intervention suggests that it is possible to improve alignment between patient and oncologist expectations of functional prognosis. However, it remains critical to generate empirical evidence associating discordance with clinically meaningful health outcomes (eg, unplanned hospitalizations and nursing home admissions) to motivate interventions to improve communication and reduce discordance. In addition, we should leverage qualitative data from clinical encounters to understand to what extent patients and oncologists discussed functional prognosis. This will help delineate whether discordance stems from a lack of information shared or information understood, and the mechanism by which the UR-GOAL tool may support discussions of patient function. An ongoing multisite clinical trial of the UR-GOAL intervention (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06697600) will collect data to address these important research gaps and improve the quality of cancer care for older adults with AML.
