Proof of Thomassen’s Conjecture on Highly connected subgraphs with large chromatic number
Achintya Raya Polavarapu

Abstract
. We prove that g ⁡ ( k , m ) ≤ max ⁡ ( m + 2 ​ k − 2 , 3 ​ k + 1 ) g(k,m)\leq\max(m+2k-2,\,3k+1) for all k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 and m ≥ 2 m\geq 2 , establishing the 1983 conjecture of Thomassen [ 7 ] that g ⁡ ( k , k + 1 ) ≤ 3 ​ k + 1 g(k,k+1)\leq 3k+1 . 1.
Introduction

For integers
k\geq 1
and
m\geq 2
, define
g(k,m)
to be the least integer
n\geq 1
such that every graph with chromatic number at least
n
contains a
(k+1)
-connected subgraph with chromatic number at least
m
. Thus
g(k,2)
is simply the least
n
such that every graph with chromatic number at least
n
contains a
(k+1)
-connected subgraph,
g(1,m)=\max(m,3)
for all
m\geq 2
, and the conjecture of Thomassen
[
7
]
asserts that
g(k,k+1)\leq 3k+1
for all
k\geq 1
.

Conjecture 1.1

(Thomassen
[
7
]
, 1983)
.

For every integer
k\geq 1
, every graph with chromatic number more than
3k
contains a
(k+1)
-connected subgraph with chromatic number more than
k
.

The conjecture originated as a theorem with an incorrect proof. The study of
g(k,m)
was initiated by Alon, Kleitman, Saks, Seymour, and Thomassen
[
1
]
, who sought a correct substitute and established

\max(m+k-1,2k+1)\leq g(k,m)\leq\max(m+10k^{2},100k^{3}+1).

The upper bound was subsequently improved by Chudnovsky, Penev, Scott, and Trotignon
[
3
]
to

g(k,m)\leq\max(m+2k^{2},2k^{2}+k+1),

and by Penev, Thomassé, and Trotignon
[
6
]
to

g(k,m)\leq\max(m+2k-2,2k^{2}+1).

Most recently, Girão and Narayanan
[
4
]
proved that
g(k-1,k)\leq 7k+1
, and Nguyen
[
5
]
refined their argument to
obtain

g(k,m)\leq\max\!\left(m+2k-2,\left\lceil\!\left(3+\tfrac{1}{16}\right)k\right\rceil\right),

making a step closer to Conjecture
1.1
. In this paper we prove the exact bound, thereby establishing Conjecture
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all integers
k\geq 1
and
m\geq 2
,

g(k,m)\leq\max(m+2k-2,3k+1).

In particular,
g(k,k+1)\leq 3k+1
for all
k\geq 1
.

Proof strategy

Our proof follows the framework of Nguyen
[
5
]
, which works with minimal
C
-inextensible graphs equipped with good templates. After a sequence of reductions, one is left with a family of stable residual pieces
Y_{i}
, each carrying an available-color list
L_{i}
, and the proof requires assigning each piece a distinct color from its list. Nguyen handles this by establishing a uniform lower bound
|L_{i}|\geq|I|
for all
i
, which suffices for a greedy assignment but forces the palette size to exceed
3k
by a factor of
\frac{1}{16}
.

Our key observation is that greedy assignment requires more than necessary. By Hall’s theorem, a system of distinct representatives exists if and only if every subfamily
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
|\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{A}}L_{i}|\geq|\mathcal{A}|
. We prove this Hall condition directly by showing that no Hall obstruction can exist at any size, via a case analysis on
|\mathcal{A}|
. Large obstructions are ruled out by a structural result already present in Nguyen’s proof; medium-sized obstructions are ruled out by the global inequality
\sum x_{i}<kt^{\prime}
inherited from the cost bound on the template; and small obstructions are ruled out by a direct list-size estimate that requires only
|C|\geq 3k
. Working with the exact condition for distinct representatives rather than a sufficient one is what closes the
\frac{1}{16}
gap.

Organization

Section 2 recalls the template framework and the minimal inextensibility machinery of Nguyen. Section 3 introduces admissible light decompositions and isolates the recoloring budget that motivates the Hall analysis. Section 4 carries out the Hall-feasibility argument and derives Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Templates and minimal obstructions

We begin by recalling the template framework (see
[
6
,
4
]
for more information) that converts the global statement about highly chromatic graphs into a local obstruction problem. Following Nguyen, the right objects are minimal
C
-inextensible graphs equipped with good templates: once one passes to such a minimal obstruction, the coloring constraints are encoded by a bounded-cost template, while the structural conclusion of Nguyen’s connectivity theorem remains available. This reduces the proof of the exact Nguyen–Thomassen bound to a chromatic lower bound for minimal
C
-inextensible graphs.

Let
G
be a graph and let
C
be a finite set of colors.

Definition 2.1

(Template)
.

A
C
-template is a triple

T=(S,c,F),

where
S\subseteq V(G)
,
c:S\to C
is a proper coloring of
G[S]
, and for every
v\in V(G)\setminus S
there is a forbidden set
F(v)\subseteq C
.

A
C
-coloring
\varphi
of
G

respects

T
if

\varphi|_{S}=c\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varphi(v)\notin F(v)\quad\text{for every }v\notin S.

For
X\subseteq V(G)\setminus S
, define its forbidden weight by

w(X)=\sum_{v\in X}|F(v)|.

The cost of a template is

\operatorname{cost}_{k}(T)=k|S|+\sum_{v\in V(G)\setminus S}|F(v)|.

Definition 2.2

(
C
-inextensible)
.

A graph
G
is
C
-inextensible if there exists a
C
-template
T=(S,c,F)
such that

\operatorname{cost}_{k}(T)<2k^{2},\qquad|F(v)|\leq k\quad(v\notin S),

and no proper
C
-coloring of
G
respects
T
.

Lemma 2.3

(Empty template)
.

If
|C|=\chi(G)-1
, then
G
is
C
-inextensible.

Proof.

Take
S=\varnothing
and
F(v)=\varnothing
for every
v\in V(G)
. A respecting coloring is simply a proper coloring of
G
using colors from
C
. This would imply
\chi(G)\leq|C|=\chi(G)-1
, impossible.
∎

We use the following consequence of Nguyen’s template framework.

Lemma 2.4

(Good template principle, Nguyen
[
5
]
)
.

Let
G
be
C
-inextensible. Among all witnessing templates, choose one with
|S|
maximal. Then every uncolored vertex satisfies

|F(v)|\leq k-1.

A template satisfying the conclusion of Lemma
2.4
will be called good. We also use Nguyen’s connectivity theorem for minimal inextensible graphs.

Theorem 2.5

(Nguyen
[
5
]
)
.

Let
H
be an induced subgraph minimal with respect to being
C
-inextensible. Then
H
is
(k+1)
-connected. Moreover,

|H|>|C|-k+1.

3.
Reduction to an exact decomposition criterion

Before turning to the final Hall argument, it is useful to isolate the abstract recoloring mechanism that underlies the proof. If, after partially coloring the graph, the remaining vertices can be partitioned into light stable pieces whose total number is within the budget
2k-|S|+1
, then each piece has a large available color list and the template can be completed. Although the final proof in Section 4 works directly with Nguyen’s residual reduction rather than by invoking this criterion formally, the present section explains why the conclusion should be viewed as a controlled list-coloring problem on stable pieces.

Definition 3.1

(Admissible light decomposition)
.

Let
T=(S,c,F)
be a good
C
-template on
H
. An admissible light decomposition consists of:

(i)

a set
U\subseteq V(H)\setminus S
colored by a proper coloring using
q
colors from
C\setminus c(S)
and respecting all forbidden lists on
U
;

(ii)

a partition of
V(H)\setminus(S\cup U)
into stable pieces

Q_{1},\dots,Q_{R};

(iii)

the lightness condition

w(Q_{j})<k\qquad(1\leq j\leq R).

We call the decomposition
exact
if

R+q\leq 2k-|S|+1.

Lemma 3.2

(Recoloring lemma at palette size
3k
)
.

Let
T=(S,c,F)
be a good
C
-template with
|C|\geq 3k
. Suppose there is an admissible light decomposition with parameters
q,R
satisfying

R+q\leq 2k-|S|+1.

Then the template can be extended to a full respecting
C
-coloring.

Proof.

The set
U
is already colored using
q
colors outside
c(S)
. Let
C^{\prime}
be the colors not used on
S
and not used in the coloring of
U
. Since
c
is proper, we have
|c(S)|\leq|S|
, and therefore

|C^{\prime}|\geq|C|-|c(S)|-q\geq 3k-|S|-q.

For each leftover piece
Q_{j}
, define its available list

L(Q_{j})=C^{\prime}\setminus\bigcup_{v\in Q_{j}}F(v).

Since
w(Q_{j})<k
, we have

|L(Q_{j})|\geq|C^{\prime}|-w(Q_{j})>3k-|S|-q-k.

As all quantities are integral,

|L(Q_{j})|\geq 2k-|S|-q+1.

The exactness condition gives

R\leq 2k-|S|-q+1.

Thus every leftover piece has a list of at least
R
available colors. For every subfamily
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{R}\}
, we have

\left|\bigcup_{Q_{j}\in\mathcal{A}}L(Q_{j})\right|\geq\max_{Q_{j}\in\mathcal{A}}|L(Q_{j})|\geq R\geq|\mathcal{A}|,

so Hall’s condition holds. Hence, by Hall’s theorem (see, for example, Bondy and Murty
[
2
]
), one may choose distinct colors

\gamma_{j}\in L(Q_{j}),\qquad 1\leq j\leq R.

Since each
Q_{j}
is stable, color every vertex in
Q_{j}
with
\gamma_{j}
. The colors are distinct across pieces, avoid the colors used on
S
and
U
, and avoid every forbidden list. Hence we obtain a full
C
-coloring respecting

T
.
∎

Theorem 3.3

(Reduction to exact decomposition)
.

Suppose that the following statement holds for every minimal
C
-inextensible graph
H
with a good template
T=(S,c,F)
and
|C|\geq 3k
:
if

\chi(H)\leq|C|-2k+2,

then
H
admits an exact admissible light decomposition. Then

g(k,m)\leq\max(m+2k-2,3k+1)

for all
k,m
.

Proof.

Let
G
satisfy

\chi(G)\geq\max(m+2k-2,3k+1).

Set
|C|=\chi(G)-1
. Then
|C|\geq 3k
, and by the empty-template lemma
G
is
C
-inextensible. Let
H\subseteq G
be an induced subgraph minimal with respect to
C
-inextensibility. By Lemma
2.4
,
H
has a
good inextensibility witness. If

\chi(H)\leq|C|-2k+2,

then the hypothesis gives an exact admissible light decomposition of
H
, and the recoloring lemma produces a respecting
C
-coloring of
H
, a contradiction. Therefore

\chi(H)\geq|C|-2k+3=\chi(G)-2k+2.

Since
\chi(G)\geq m+2k-2
, this gives

\chi(H)\geq m.

By Theorem
2.5
, the graph
H
is
(k+1)
-connected. Thus
H
is a
(k+1)
-connected subgraph of chromatic number at least
m
, as required.
∎

Although Theorem
3.3
is not invoked in the proof of our main theorem, it isolates the recoloring budget
2k-|S|+1
that motivates the Hall analysis in Section 4. In the proof of Theorem
4.10
we work directly with Nguyen’s residual pieces rather than first packaging them as an exact admissible light decomposition.

4.
Residual Hall obstructions

We now carry out the final step of the argument inside Nguyen’s reduction. After the preliminary recoloring stages, all uncolored vertices lie in a family of stable residual pieces, each equipped with a list of colors that remain legal for that piece. Nguyen’s original proof finishes this stage by a pointwise numerical estimate that requires extra slack in
|C|
. Our contribution is to replace that last numerical step with a Hall-feasibility analysis of the residual lists: we show that every putative Hall obstruction is incompatible with Nguyen’s global budget bounds, thereby allowing Hall’s theorem to complete the coloring at the exact threshold.

We now record the part of Nguyen’s proof of
[
5
, Lemma 4.1]
that will be used in the Hall analysis.

Lemma 4.1

(Reduction package from Nguyen
[
5
]
)
.

Let
H
be a minimal
C
-inextensible graph with a good template
T=(S,c,F)
and fix a partition

V(H)=S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\chi}

of
V(H)
into stable sets. For each
i\in[\chi]
, set

P_{i}:=S_{i}\setminus S,\qquad p_{i}:=\left\lfloor\frac{w(P_{i})}{k}\right\rfloor,\qquad p:=\sum_{i=1}^{\chi}p_{i},\qquad t:=2k-|S|,\qquad t^{\prime}:=t-p.

Then Nguyen’s Lemma 4.5 and the subsequent reduction in his proof of Lemma 4.1 produce the following objects.

(i)

Integers
q_{i},t_{i}
with

0\leq q_{i}\leq p_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{i}=q_{i}+t_{i}\qquad(i\in[\chi]).

(ii)

Subsets
P_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq P_{i}
and partitions

X_{i}:=P_{i}\setminus P_{i}^{\prime}=P_{i1}\cup\cdots\cup P_{i,t_{i}+1}\qquad(i\in[\chi]),

where each
P_{ij}
is stable and satisfies

w(P_{ij})<k.

(iii)

A proper coloring
c_{1}
of

S^{1}:=S\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{\chi}P_{i}^{\prime}

respecting the template, such that at most
q_{i}
colors are used on
P_{i}^{\prime}
.

(iv)

Quantities

x_{i}:=w(X_{i})-t_{i}k\qquad(i\in[\chi])

satisfying

0\leq x_{i}<k\qquad(i\in[\chi])

and

\sum_{i=1}^{\chi}x_{i}<kt^{\prime}.

(This inequality follows from the cost bound
\operatorname{cost}_{k}(T)<2k^{2}
together with the definitions of
p
,
t
,
t^{\prime}
, and
x_{i}
; see Nguyen
[
5
]
, proof of Lemma 4.1.)

(v)

Index sets

I_{0}:=\{i\in[\chi]:t_{i}=0\},\qquad I_{2}\subseteq[\chi]\setminus I_{0},

where
I_{2}
is produced by Nguyen’s greedy selection procedure (see
[
5
]
, proof of Lemma 4.1): indices are added to
I_{2}
one at a time as long as
\bigcup_{i\in I_{2}}X_{i}
can be colored, extending
c_{1}
, with at most
\sum_{i\in I_{2}}t_{i}
additional colors while respecting the template, and the procedure terminates when no further index can be added without violating this budget. We then set

I_{1}:=[\chi]\setminus(I_{0}\cup I_{2}),\qquad I:=I_{0}\cup I_{1}.

(vi)

An extension
c_{2}
of
c_{1}
to

S^{2}:=S^{1}\cup\bigcup_{i\in I_{2}}X_{i}.

(vii)

For each
i\in I_{1}
, a minimum-weight part
Y_{i}
among
P_{i1},\dots,P_{i,t_{i}+1}
, and an extension
c_{3}
of
c_{2}
to

S^{3}:=S^{2}\cup\bigcup_{i\in I_{1}}(X_{i}\setminus Y_{i})

that uses at most
t_{i}
colors on
X_{i}\setminus Y_{i}
. For
i\in I_{0}
, we set

Y_{i}:=X_{i}.

If we write

y_{i}:=w(Y_{i})\qquad(i\in I),

then each
Y_{i}\subseteq S_{i}
is stable, each
y_{i}<k
, and the only uncolored vertices after the coloring
c_{3}
are the residual pieces
Y_{i}
for
i\in I
. Their residual lists are

L_{i}:=C\setminus\left(c_{3}(S^{3}\setminus S_{i})\cup\bigcup_{v\in Y_{i}}F(v)\right)\qquad(i\in I),

where
c_{3}(S^{3}\setminus S_{i})
denotes the set of colors used by
c_{3}
on vertices of
S^{3}\setminus S_{i}
. Nguyen’s calculation also gives

|c_{3}(S^{3}\setminus S_{i})|\leq 2k-t^{\prime}-|S\cap S_{i}|-p_{i}\qquad(i\in I).

Finally, we set

s_{1}:=\sum_{i\in I_{1}}t_{i}.

For
i\in I_{0}
, one has
x_{i}=y_{i}
.

Lemma 4.2

(Nguyen’s Claim 4.8, restated)
.

In the notation of Lemma
4.1
, we have

x_{i}\geq t_{i}\bigl(|C|-3k+t^{\prime}+s_{1}+|S\cap S_{i}|+q_{i}\bigr)\qquad(i\in I).

Lemma 4.3

(Nguyen’s Claim 4.10, restated)
.

In the notation of Lemma
4.1
, assume furthermore that
\chi(H)\leq|C|-2k+2
. Then there exists
J\subseteq I
with
I_{1}\subseteq J
,
|J|\leq|C|-2k+1
, and
|L_{i}|\geq|I|
for every

i\in I\setminus J
.

Definition 4.4

(Hall-feasible family)
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a family of stable pieces, each equipped with an available-color list
L(Q)
. We say that
\mathcal{Q}
is
Hall-feasible
if for every subfamily
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
,

\left|\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}L(Q)\right|\geq|\mathcal{A}|.

A family that is not Hall-feasible will be called
Hall-bad
.

Standing assumptions for the Hall analysis

Throughout the rest of this section, we work under the following standing assumptions. Let
H
be a minimal
C
-inextensible graph with a good template
T=(S,c,F)
, and assume

|C|\geq 3k-1.

We are in the contradiction regime of Nguyen’s chromatic lemma, namely

\chi(H)\leq|C|-2k+2.

Write

|C|=3k-1+d

with
d\geq 0
. We fix a partition

V(H)=S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\chi}

into stable sets and apply Lemma
4.1
. Thus all objects

p_{i},p,t,t^{\prime},q_{i},t_{i},X_{i},x_{i},I_{0},I_{1},I_{2},I,Y_{i},L_{i},s_{1}

are those produced by that reduction package. In particular,

0\leq x_{i}<k,\qquad\sum_{i}x_{i}<kt^{\prime},

and Nguyen’s Claims 4.8 and 4.10 are available in this setting.

We begin by excluding large Hall obstructions. In this range,
4.3
already forces one residual class to have a list large enough to contradict Hall-badness immediately.

Theorem 4.5

(No large Hall obstruction)
.

Assume
|C|=3k-1+d
with
d\geq 0
. There is no Hall-bad family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq I
with
|\mathcal{A}|\geq k+d+1
.

Proof.

We use Lemma
4.3
, which gives a set
J\subseteq I
with
I_{1}\subseteq J
,
|J|\leq|C|-2k+1
, and
|L_{i}|\geq|I|
for every
i\in I\setminus J
. Since
|C|=3k-1+d
, we have

|C|-2k+1=k+d,

and therefore
|J|\leq k+d
.

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that
\mathcal{A}\subseteq I
is Hall-bad with
|\mathcal{A}|\geq k+d+1
. Since
|J|\leq k+d
, the family
\mathcal{A}
is not contained in
J
, so there exists

i\in\mathcal{A}\setminus J.

For this index
i
, Lemma
4.3
gives
|L_{i}|\geq|I|
. Because
\mathcal{A}\subseteq I
, we also have
|\mathcal{A}|\leq|I|
. Therefore

\left|\bigcup_{h\in\mathcal{A}}L_{h}\right|\geq|L_{i}|\geq|I|\geq|\mathcal{A}|,

contradicting the assumption that
\mathcal{A}
is Hall-bad.
∎

The remaining difficulty lies in the middle range, where a Hall-bad family is too small for the large-obstruction argument to apply but may still contain indices from
I_{1}
. The following numerical lemma is the main new technical ingredient that controls this regime.

Lemma 4.6

(numerical lemma)
.

Let
k,d,r,t^{\prime}
be integers with
d\geq 0
,
3\leq r\leq k+d
, and
1\leq t^{\prime}\leq r-d-2
. Set
A:=k+d-r+t^{\prime}
and
M:=r-d-1-t^{\prime}
. Let
b\geq 1
,
a+b=r
, and
1\leq t_{j}\leq M
for
1\leq j\leq b
. Put
T:=\sum_{j=1}^{b}t_{j}
. Then

aA+\sum_{j=1}^{b}\max\bigl\{t_{j}(t^{\prime}+T+d-1),\,A+t_{j}(t_{j}-M)\bigr\}\geq kt^{\prime}.

Proof.

Since
\sum_{j}\max\{U_{j},V_{j}\}\geq\max\{\sum_{j}U_{j},\sum_{j}V_{j}\}
, it suffices to show that at least one of

D_{1}:=aA+T(t^{\prime}+T+d-1)-kt^{\prime},\qquad D_{2}:=aA+\sum_{j=1}^{b}\bigl(A+t_{j}(t_{j}-M)\bigr)-kt^{\prime}

is nonnegative. Set
n:=k+d-r\geq 0
, so
A=n+t^{\prime}
and
r=M+d+t^{\prime}+1
. Using
a=r-b
,

(1)

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=n(M+d+1-b)+T(T+d-1)+t^{\prime}(T+d-b),

(2)

\displaystyle D_{2}

\displaystyle=n(M+d+1)+dt^{\prime}+\sum_{j=1}^{b}t_{j}(t_{j}-M).

For the Case
b\leq M+d+1
: The first term of (
1
) is nonnegative. Since
T\geq b
, we have
T+d-b\geq d\geq 0
, and
T(T+d-1)\geq 0
since
T\geq 1
. Hence
D_{1}\geq 0
.

For the Case
b>M+d+1
: Assume
D_{1}<0
. Since
T\geq b
and
t^{\prime}\geq 1
, the term
t^{\prime}(T+d-b)\geq 0
may be discarded from (
1
), giving
n(b-M-d-1)>T(T+d-1)
. Set
B:=b-M-d-1>0
, so
n>T(T+d-1)/B
and hence

n(M+d+1)>\frac{T(T+d-1)(M+d+1)}{B}.

We claim
(T+d-1)(M+d+1)\geq(M-1)B
. Indeed,
T\geq b=M+d+1+B
gives
T+d-1\geq M+2d+B
, so

(T+d-1)(M+d+1)\geq(M+2d+B)(M+d+1)=(M+2d)(M+d+1)+B(M+d+1)\geq B(M-1),

where the last step uses
(M+2d)(M+d+1)\geq 0
and
(M+d+1)-(M-1)=d+2\geq 0
. Therefore

(3)

n(M+d+1)>T(M-1).

Since
t_{j}\geq 1
, we have
t_{j}(t_{j}-M)=t_{j}^{2}-Mt_{j}\geq t_{j}-Mt_{j}=-t_{j}(M-1)
, and summing over
j
gives
\sum_{j}t_{j}(t_{j}-M)\geq-T(M-1)
. Substituting (
3
) into (
2
),

D_{2}>T(M-1)+dt^{\prime}-T(M-1)=dt^{\prime}\geq 0.

Thus one of
D_{1},D_{2}
is nonnegative in every case.
∎

We now use the numerical lemma to rule out middle-range Hall obstructions that meet
I_{1}
. This is the heart of the parametric strengthening, since it is precisely here that Nguyen’s original proof required additional slack.

Theorem 4.7

(Exclusion of
I_{1}
-indices)
.

Assume
|C|=3k-1+d
with
d\geq 0
. Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq I
be a Hall-bad family with
3\leq|\mathcal{A}|\leq k+d
. Then
\mathcal{A}\cap I_{1}=\varnothing
.

Proof.

Set
r:=|\mathcal{A}|
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is Hall-bad,
|\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{A}}L_{i}|<r
, so in particular
|L_{i}|\leq r-1
for every
i\in\mathcal{A}
. Recalling that

L_{i}=C\setminus\!\left(c_{3}(S^{3}\setminus S_{i})\cup\bigcup_{v\in Y_{i}}F(v)\right),

Nguyen’s bound
|c_{3}(S^{3}\setminus S_{i})|\leq 2k-t^{\prime}-|S\cap S_{i}|-p_{i}
together with
|C|=3k-1+d
gives

|L_{i}|\geq(3k-1+d)-(2k-t^{\prime}-|S\cap S_{i}|-p_{i})-y_{i}=k-1+d+t^{\prime}+|S\cap S_{i}|+p_{i}-y_{i}.

Combining with
|L_{i}|\leq r-1
yields

(1)

y_{i}\geq k+d-r+t^{\prime}+|S\cap S_{i}|+p_{i}\qquad(i\in\mathcal{A}).

Suppose for contradiction that
\mathcal{A}\cap I_{1}\neq\varnothing
. Write
B:=\mathcal{A}\cap I_{1}
and
A_{0}:=\mathcal{A}\cap I_{0}
, with
b:=|B|\geq 1
and
a:=|A_{0}|
, so
a+b=r
.

Lower bounds on
x_{i}
:

For
i\in A_{0}
: since
t_{i}=0
we have
X_{i}=Y_{i}
and
x_{i}=y_{i}
, so (
1
) (dropping the nonnegative terms
|S\cap S_{i}|
and
p_{i}
) gives

(2)

x_{i}\geq k+d-r+t^{\prime}=:A.

For
i\in B
: since
Y_{i}
is a minimum-weight piece among the
t_{i}+1
parts of
X_{i}
, we have
(t_{i}+1)y_{i}\leq w(X_{i})=x_{i}+t_{i}k
, hence
x_{i}\geq(t_{i}+1)y_{i}-t_{i}k
. Substituting (
1
) and using
p_{i}=q_{i}+t_{i}\geq t_{i}
and
|S\cap S_{i}|\geq 0
, we obtain

(3)

x_{i}\geq k+d-r+t^{\prime}+t_{i}(t^{\prime}-r+d+t_{i}+1).

Setting
T:=\sum_{i\in B}t_{i}
and applying Lemma
4.2
with
|C|=3k-1+d
and
T\leq s_{1}
,

(4)

x_{i}\geq t_{i}(t^{\prime}+T+d-1)\qquad(i\in B).

Range of
t^{\prime}:

Since
0\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}<kt^{\prime}
, we have

(5)

t^{\prime}\geq 1.

For
i\in B
, the lightness condition
y_{i}<k
gives
y_{i}\leq k-1
. Combining with (
1
) and
p_{i}=q_{i}+t_{i}\geq 1
(since
i\in I_{1}
) yields
r-1\geq d+t^{\prime}+|S\cap S_{i}|+p_{i}\geq d+t^{\prime}+1
, so

(6)

t^{\prime}\leq r-d-2.

If
r\leq d+2
then (
6
) contradicts (
5
), so
r\geq d+3
. Moreover
t_{i}\leq p_{i}\leq r-d-1-t^{\prime}
.

Set
A:=k+d-r+t^{\prime}
and
M:=r-d-1-t^{\prime}
. Then (
2
) reads
x_{i}\geq A
for
i\in A_{0}
, and since
t^{\prime}-r+d+t_{i}+1=t_{i}-M
, (
3
) reads
x_{i}\geq A+t_{i}(t_{i}-M)
for
i\in B
. The constraints (
5
) and (
6
) verify the hypothesis
1\leq t^{\prime}\leq r-d-2
of Lemma
4.6
. Combining the lower bounds above with (
4
) and applying that lemma gives
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}}x_{i}\geq kt^{\prime}
. But
\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}<kt^{\prime}
and all
x_{i}\geq 0
, so
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}}x_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}<kt^{\prime}
, a contradiction. Hence
\mathcal{A}\cap I_{1}=\varnothing
.
∎

Once the
I_{1}
-indices have been excluded, the remaining middle-range obstruction is an
I_{0}
-only family. In that case the argument becomes simpler, because the residual weight parameter agrees with the surplus parameter.

Theorem 4.8

(No middle-size
I_{0}
-only Hall obstruction)
.

Assume
|C|=3k-1+d
with
d\geq 0
. There is no Hall-bad family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq I_{0}
with
3\leq|\mathcal{A}|\leq k+d
.

Proof.

Suppose such
\mathcal{A}
exists and set
r:=|\mathcal{A}|
. Since
\sum x_{i}<kt^{\prime}
and all
x_{i}\geq 0
, we have
t^{\prime}\geq 1
. Arguing as in
Theorem
4.7
, Hall-badness and
t_{i}=0
for
i\in I_{0}
give
x_{i}=y_{i}\geq k+d-r+t^{\prime}
for all
i\in\mathcal{A}
, hence

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}}x_{i}\geq r(k+d-r+t^{\prime}).

It remains to show
r(k+d-r+t^{\prime})\geq kt^{\prime}
, equivalently
r(k+d-r)+(r-k)t^{\prime}\geq 0
. If
r\geq k
this is immediate since
r\leq k+d
. If
r<k
, then
y_{i}\leq k-1
and (
1
) force
t^{\prime}\leq r-d-1
; if
r\leq d+1
this contradicts
t^{\prime}\geq 1
, so
r\geq d+2
and

r(k+d-r)-(k-r)t^{\prime}\geq r(k+d-r)-(k-r)(r-d-1)=k(d+1)-r>0.

Hence
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}}x_{i}\geq kt^{\prime}
, contradicting
\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}<kt^{\prime}
.
∎

With the residual Hall theorem in hand, Nguyen’s chromatic lemma can be completed at the exact threshold. The only change from Nguyen’s proof is that the final slack-based argument is replaced by Hall’s theorem on the residual family.

Theorem 4.9

(Residual Hall feasibility above the exact threshold)
.

Assume
|C|=3k-1+d
with
d\geq 1
. Then the residual family
(Y_{i})_{i\in I}
is Hall-feasible.
That is, for every
\mathcal{A}\subseteq I
,

\left|\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{A}}L_{i}\right|\geq|\mathcal{A}|.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}\subseteq I
is Hall-bad and set
r:=|\mathcal{A}|
. Since
\sum x_{i}<kt^{\prime}
and all
x_{i}\geq 0
, we have
t^{\prime}\geq 1
. Nguyen’s bound gives
|L_{i}|\geq d+t^{\prime}+|S\cap S_{i}|+p_{i}\geq d+t^{\prime}\geq 2
for all
i\in I
, ruling out
r=1
. For
r=2
, Hall-badness forces
|L_{i}\cup L_{j}|=1
, contradicting
|L_{i}|\geq 2
. For
3\leq r\leq k+d
, Theorem
4.7
gives
\mathcal{A}\subseteq I_{0}
, contradicting Theorem
4.8
. For
r\geq k+d+1
,
Theorem
4.5
gives a contradiction. Hence no Hall-bad family exists.
∎

The final step is now formal. We combine the exact chromatic lemma for minimal
C
-inextensible graphs with Nguyen’s connectivity theorem to obtain the desired bound for
g(k,m)
.

Theorem 4.10

(Exact version of Nguyen’s chromatic lemma)
.

Let
H
be a minimal
C
-inextensible graph with a good template
T=(S,c,F)
. If
|C|\geq 3k
, then
\chi(H)\geq|C|-2k+3
.

Proof.

Suppose not, and let
\chi:=\chi(H)\leq|C|-2k+2
. Write
|C|=3k-1+d
with
d\geq 1
. Choose a partition
V(H)=S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\chi}
of
V(H)
into stable sets, set
P_{i}:=S_{i}\setminus S
, and define

p_{i}=\left\lfloor\frac{w(P_{i})}{k}\right\rfloor,\qquad p=\sum_{i}p_{i},\qquad t=2k-|S|,\qquad t^{\prime}=t-p.

Apply Lemma
4.1
. This produces the sets
X_{i}
, the parameters
q_{i},t_{i},x_{i}
, the index sets
I_{0}
,
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
,
I=I_{0}\cup I_{1}
, the intermediate colorings
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
,
c_{3}
on
S^{1}\subseteq S^{2}\subseteq S^{3}
, and the residual pieces
(Y_{i})_{i\in I}
with residual lists
(L_{i})_{i\in I}
. By construction, the only uncolored vertices after
c_{3}
are the stable sets
Y_{i}
for
i\in I
.

At the final residual-coloring step, instead of Nguyen’s final numerical argument, we apply Theorem
4.9
, which gives that the family
(Y_{i})_{i\in I}
is Hall-feasible:

\left|\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{A}}L_{i}\right|\geq|\mathcal{A}|\qquad(\mathcal{A}\subseteq I).

By Hall’s theorem, there exist pairwise distinct colors
\gamma_{i}\in L_{i}
for each
i\in I
. Color every vertex of
Y_{i}
by
\gamma_{i}
. This extends
c_{3}
to a proper
C
-coloring of
H
: each
Y_{i}\subseteq S_{i}
is stable and the colors
\gamma_{i}
are distinct for distinct
i
, so there is no conflict among the residual pieces themselves; since
\gamma_{i}\notin c_{3}(S^{3}\setminus S_{i})
, no vertex of
Y_{i}
conflicts with any already-colored vertex outside
S_{i}
; there are no edges from
Y_{i}
to already-colored vertices inside
S_{i}
because
S_{i}
is stable; and
\gamma_{i}\notin F(v)
for
v\in Y_{i}
because
\gamma_{i}\in L_{i}
by definition.

Therefore the coloring extends to a proper
C
-coloring of all of
H
respecting the template
T=(S,c,F)
, contradicting
C
-inextensibility of
H
. Hence
\chi(H)\geq|C|-2k+3
.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

The remaining step is now formal: we combine the exact chromatic lemma for minimal
C
-inextensible graphs with Nguyen’s connectivity theorem to obtain the desired bound for
g(k,m)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
G
satisfy

\chi(G)\geq\max(m+2k-2,3k+1).

Set

|C|=\chi(G)-1.

Then
|C|\geq 3k
, and by Lemma
2.4
together with the empty template lemma, every induced subgraph minimal with respect to being
C
-inextensible admits a good inextensibility witness. Let
H\subseteq G
be such a minimal induced
C
-inextensible subgraph. By Theorem
4.10
,

\chi(H)\geq|C|-2k+3=\chi(G)-2k+2.

Since
\chi(G)\geq m+2k-2
, we obtain

\chi(H)\geq m.

By Theorem
2.5
, the graph
H
is
(k+1)
-connected. Thus
H
is a
(k+1)
-connected subgraph of chromatic number at least
m
, as required.
∎

Remark
.

The bound
|C|\geq 3k
in Theorem
4.10
is sharp for the present method. The Hall-feasibility argument breaks down at
|C|=3k-1
, i.e.
d=0
, already at the smallest possible obstruction size: when
d=0
the list size lower bound gives only
|L_{i}|\geq t^{\prime}\geq 1
, and the inequality
|L_{i}|\geq d+t^{\prime}\geq 2
used to rule out Hall-bad pairs in the proof of Theorem
4.9
requires
d\geq 1
strictly. Thus any improvement of the upper bound on
g(k,k+1)
below
3k+1
would require either a fundamentally different approach to the residual coloring step, or a refinement of the reduction package that produces residual lists with additional structure not captured by the present framework. We also note that the best known lower bound
g(k,k+1)\geq 2k
, due to
[
1
]
, leaves open the question of whether
g(k,k+1)=3k+1
exactly; closing this gap would require a new extremal construction.
