 Executive Summary
 Problem Statement and Research Questions
 Standard residential construction practices can be subject to the inefficiencies of
 multiple trades, products, technologies, and systems, resulting in a fragmented market
 with little concern for improving energy efficiency. The team from GTI Energy (GTI),
 Dvele Inc., and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) investigated high-performance
 modular home construction to reduce energy use for new homes and transform current
 construction practices. The Transformative Efficiency and Automation in Modular
 Homes (TEAMH) project integrated emerging envelope technologies with modular
 manufacturing innovation and automation. The intent was to investigate cost-effective
 solutions for producing new factory-built homes with a high level of energy efficiency
 and quality.

 The primary research question for the project was: Which automation enhancements
 and advanced envelope technologies can be incorporated to achieve 50% modeled
 energy savings over International Energy Conservation Code (IECC) 2018 site-built
 homes at no additional life cycle cost and a minimal first cost premium when ramped
 into volume production? The integration challenge for this project was to integrate
 emerging production technologies, like modular housing and factory automation, with
 emerging envelope technologies to reach first costs similar to conventional construction.

 Objectives
 The TEAMH project sought to develop a scalable solution for producing homes with
 20%–50% energy savings and a low cost premium relative to site-fabricated single-
 family home construction. The specific project objectives were to: (1) define efficiency
 measures and modular home manufacturing processes capable of achieving 20%–50%
 site energy savings over IECC 2018 code-minimum homes, (2) construct and
 commission a high-performance assembly using the cost- and performance-optimized
 modular home measures and production process, and (3) demonstrate production
 efficiency gains of implementing automation in light gauge steel (LGS) construction
 compared to traditional wood-framed construction.

 Methodology
 The project team started by defining and specifying the high-performance envelope
 technologies for energy savings analysis. The early designs included a combination of
 blown-in and batt insulation in between the framing (“cavity insulation”); exterior
 continuous insulation in the roof, exterior walls, and floor; and very low air leakage rates
 arising from liquid applied air barrier technology. During the factory demonstration and
 fabrication of automation-assisted LGS and wood-framed construction, we used batt
 insulation as cavity insulation. In addition, we included a vacuum insulation panel (VIP)-
 based assembly for consideration as an emerging envelope technology. We applied
 these insulation and air barrier technologies to wood-framed and LGS construction for
 whole-building energy modeling.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation              vii

 We performed laboratory evaluations of envelope materials and assemblies to support
 the energy modeling work. Laboratory evaluations included both small- and large-scale
 testing of envelope materials and assemblies. Small-scale tests in heat flow meter
 apparatuses (HFMAs) were used to measure the thermal performance of VIPs and VIP
 assemblies. We tested multiple VIPs of different dimensions for their center-of-panel
 thermal resistance and evaluated the “edge effects” in VIP assemblies.

 We performed large-scale testing in a guarded hot box to measure the effective R-
 values of multiple full-scale exterior wall assemblies. These wall assemblies were built
 using wood framing and LGS and contained different combinations of cavity insulation
 and exterior continuous insulation.

 We used EnergyPlus® to perform whole-building energy modeling to evaluate the
 energy benefits of the upgraded envelope assemblies over IECC 2018 compliant homes
 across 12 climate zones. The IECC 2018 models were based on U.S. Department of
 Energy prototype residential building models and were built to match the physical
 geometry of three modular home designs. The following three modular home designs by
 Dvele were considered for energy modeling:

 • Angora: 750-ft2 U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development style
 manufactured home

 • Mills: 1,800-ft2 modular home

 • Roberts: 400-ft2 manufactured home.

 All modeled homes were assumed to be all-electric.

 A key project attribute was the evaluation of advanced industrial and manufacturing
 methods to improve the construction efficiency of modular homes. Dvele designed a
 digitized, collaborative design software platform that integrates into a robotized
 production process, translating building information modeling into robotics assembly
 language. Furthermore, to partially automate the construction process, Dvele
 commissioned truss and panel machines to produce LGS structural members in the
 required numbers and with the specified dimensions. Further, the LGS machines punch
 the necessary holes for assembly, routing cables, and so on, which saves labor time
 and cost in the latter stages of construction.

 To quantify the benefits of automated construction, we performed a factory
 demonstration to compare the construction times of typical wood-framed and
 automation-assisted LGS walls. The wood-framed wall was constructed using traditional
 manual construction methods, with no automation assistance. The LGS walls were
 created using the structural members produced by the LGS machines. We implemented
 a time-and-motion study to evaluate the production efficiency gains via automation. Key
 steps for each framing process were defined and followed during the demonstration. A
 key requirement of the real-time construction comparison was that all processes were

 performed sequentially to enable accurate time recording. For consistency, the same
 two-person crew was used to assemble all wood and LGS walls.

 We also investigated the impact of high-performance envelope measures and
 automation-assisted modular construction on the eventual cost of the high-efficiency
 homes and compared the costs against site-built IECC 2018 compliant homes. The
 construction cost analysis work included: (1) a review of the technical literature and
 construction databases to gather cost and sales price information on site-built homes,
 (2) Dvele’s time tracking data for construction labor, and (3) price projections of Dvele’s
 current and future costs based on their plans for scaling, improving production efficiency
 via increased automation, and leveraging economies of scale with higher production
 volume. A major challenge we faced was finding accurate construction costs for
 conventional wood-framed, site-built homes, especially those conforming to IECC 2018
 standards and of a similar design/footprint as the Dvele modular homes. Finding
 granular data differentiating the costs of the construction and envelope measures from
 the overall cost of the homes was also a challenge.

 Conclusions
 The TEAMH project evaluated the potential cost and energy benefits of combining
 emerging envelope technologies with automation-assisted modular home
 manufacturing, as compared to site-built residential homes meeting the IECC 2018
 standards. The key emerging envelope technology that was considered was a VIP with
 fiberglass core material. Building energy modeling of multiple modular home designs
 indicated that the upgraded envelope assemblies can yield heating energy savings of
 up to 50% and cooling energy savings of up to 34% compared to IECC 2018 standards.

 Dvele implemented multiple upgrades to their manufacturing capabilities, including
 partial automation of LGS construction. A manufacturing demonstration showed a
 reduction in the time to manufacture the automation-assisted LGS walls by 24%–46%
 compared to a baseline wood-frame construction using traditional methods. Dvele is
 planning further manufacturing process improvements, including additional production
 automation and robotics, use of standard modular home designs, and increased
 production volume. The combination of these measures is expected to significantly
 reduce the cost of Dvele’s future modular homes compared to their current offerings.
 Based on Dvele’s internal cost projections, assuming increasing levels of automation,
 production capacity, and economies of scale, the future cost of a 1,200-ft2 high-
 performance and above-code modular home is estimated to be about $149–$179 per
 square foot, which is similar to the average cost of residential homes in the western
 region of the United States in 2017–2021.

 1 Introduction
 1.1 Problem Statement
 Standard residential construction practices can be subject to the inefficiencies of
 multiple trades, products, technologies, and systems, resulting in a fragmented market
 with little concern for improving energy efficiency. The team from GTI Energy (GTI),
 Dvele Inc., and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) investigated high-performance
 modular home construction to reduce energy use for new homes and transform current
 construction practices. The Transformative Efficiency and Automation in Modular
 Homes (TEAMH) project integrated emerging envelope technologies with modular
 manufacturing innovation and automation. The intent of this project was to investigate
 cost-effective solutions for producing new factory-built homes with a high level of energy
 efficiency and quality.

 Although off-site construction methods like panelized walls and modular systems were
 introduced in the U.S. market in the 1950s, this industry segment has not been able to
 achieve market growth, energy savings, or significant cost reductions. Further, highly
 efficient buildings continue to be the exception rather than the norm. Additional costs for
 research and validation or premium performance features, especially ones that do not
 improve aesthetics, present a significant barrier. Yet advanced construction methods,
 along with more resilient and energy-efficient materials and systems, are critical to
 increasing the competitiveness of U.S. construction businesses and their workforce
 (DOE 2019).

 The TEAMH project sought to investigate which automation enhancements and
 advanced envelope technologies can be incorporated to achieve 50% modeled energy
 savings over International Energy Conservation Code (IECC) 2018 site-built homes at
 no additional life cycle cost and a minimal first cost premium when ramped into volume
 production. The integration challenge for this project was to converge emerging
 production technologies, like modular housing and factory automation, with emerging
 envelope technologies to reach first costs similar to those of conventional construction.

 1.2 Objectives
 The TEAMH project sought to develop a scalable solution for producing homes with
 20%–50% energy savings and low cost premiums relative to site-fabricated single-
 family home construction. The specific project objectives included:

 1.3 Research Questions
 The primary research question for the project was: Which automation enhancements
 and advanced envelope technologies can be incorporated to achieve 50% modeled
 energy savings over IECC 2018 site-built homes at no additional life cycle cost and a
 minimal first cost premium when ramped into volume production? Note that when this
 project was initiated, IECC 2018 was the latest code in effect. Since then, IECC 2021
 has been released. The targeted energy efficiency metrics in this project are expected
 to yield significant savings compared to even the 2021 code, albeit lower than
 compared to the 2018 code.

 The specific project objectives can be summarized under two major categories:

 1. Modular home production automation

 a. Develop a fully digitized and collaborative design software package to
 integrate into the robotized production process—translating building
 information modeling (BIM) into robotics assembly language.

 b. Define hybrid production practices using human and automated robotic
 labor, leveraging the efficiencies and strengths of each.

 c. Perform time-and-motion studies of pre- and post-automation
 manufacturing processes to assess the effectiveness and impact of
 manufacturing automation on production efficiency and cost.

 2. Energy efficiency measures, including a high-performance envelope design

 a. Define efficiency measures, including envelope assembly design(s)
 incorporating vacuum insulation panels (VIPs), that are capable of
 achieving 50% energy savings over IECC 2018 compliant site-built
 homes.

 b. Construct and commission a high-performance assembly using the cost-
 and performance-optimized modular home measures and production
 process. The envelope measures will be tested in the laboratory and
 incorporated in whole-building energy models to evaluate the energy
 savings potential.

 2 Methodology
 The following sections describe the work performed under the different high-level tasks:

 • Energy modeling to estimate the savings from the upgraded building envelope
 designs compared to IECC 2018.

 • Laboratory testing of insulation materials and test wall assemblies.

 • Factory demonstration and fabrication of multiple test walls to demonstrate the
 efficiency gains from the implementation of BIM and factory automation.

 • Construction cost data analysis and evaluation of embodied carbon based on
 data from selected home modules created by Dvele.

 It is important to note that the aforementioned activities were performed iteratively and
 not in a specific chronological order. A key implication is that envelope details changed
 across the different activities, based on the findings of the project and Dvele’s evolving
 plans for their modular homes. The building energy modeling was one of the earlier
 tasks, and Dvele’s initial envelope specifications were used, which included both cavity
 insulation and exterior continuous insulation for all wall and roof assemblies. The factory
 demonstration built multiple wood and light gauge steel (LGS) test walls, all of which
 contained cavity insulation. These test walls were shipped to ORNL for thermal
 performance testing. However, in the period between the test wall fabrication and
 testing at ORNL, a decision was made to remove the cavity insulation from the LGS
 walls and test them with exterior insulation alone, based on discussions related to
 thermal performance and the cost implications of implementing cavity insulation in LGS
 walls.

 The following sections contain all pertinent details related to the different tasks and
 associated envelope details.

 2.1 High-Performance Envelope Specifications
 The team started by compiling the different envelope technologies that were already
 being implemented or were planned to be used by Dvele in their modular home
 offerings. Dvele’s construction includes a combination of blown-in and batt insulation
 between structural framing (“cavity insulation”) and rigid foam or exterior continuous
 insulation in the roof, exterior walls, and floor. Dvele also uses liquid applied air barrier
 technologies that can enable very low air leakage rates.

 thermal performance of building envelopes at a much smaller thickness than traditional
 foam insulation. The smaller thickness is attractive to modular home builders, who build
 to the maximum outer dimensions of home modules based on transportability and can

 2.2.1 Heat Flow Meter Testing
 The laboratory evaluation started with measurements of fiberglass VIPs in ORNL’s heat
 flow meter apparatus (HFMA). Multiple VIPs and VIP assemblies were obtained from a
 commercial vendor for testing. The intent was to evaluate the thermal performance
 characteristics of the VIPs, including edge effects, i.e., higher heat flows along the VIP
 edges and joints. These characteristics were intended to be used in updated energy
 modeling of the modular homes with VIP-based envelope systems.

 Figure 1 shows a typical HFMA. The HFMA consists of two independently temperature-
 controlled plates that sandwich a flat specimen and impose a temperature differential
 across the specimen. By measuring the heat flow across the center of the specimen via
 embedded heat flux transducers (HFTs) and a built-in specimen thickness
 measurement, the thermal conductivity and resistance of the specimen can be
 measured. The HFMA can also be used to measure the heat flow across nonuniform
 specimens, which can be used in numerical modeling and analytical evaluation of the
 specimens.

 2.4 Automated Modular Construction
 2.4.1 Software- and Automation-Assisted Construction
 Dvele designed a digitized, collaborative design software platform that integrates into a
 robotized production process, translating BIM into robotics assembly language. One
 objective of this project was to evaluate improvements in production efficiency and
 reduction in construction costs via automation. As a part of the automation process,
 Dvele commissioned truss and panel machines to produce LGS structural members in
 the required numbers and with the specified dimensions. Further, the LGS machines
 punch the necessary holes for assembly, routing cables, etc., which saves labor time
 and cost in the latter stages of construction.

 Figure 11 and Figure 12 show the LGS machines used to create the framing members
 for exterior walls, interior partition walls, and roof and floor truss assemblies. These
 machines were selected to create framing components with zero waste and to
 significantly reduce the time and cost associated with constructing the structure.

 A key requirement of the real-time construction comparison was that all processes were
 to be performed sequentially to enable accurate time recording. The same two-person
 crew was used to assemble all wood and LGS walls. The team also planned to capture
 any rework/resizing needed and to track the extra time/labor cost.

 Exterior insulation was not added during the construction demonstration. Once built, the
 demonstration wall assemblies were shipped to ORNL for hot box testing, with the
 exterior insulation to be installed on the wall sections after arrival at ORNL. Part of the
 reasoning was that, because each wall would contain a different exterior continuous
 insulation system for the purposes of the current project, it would not be appropriate to
 compare the addition of the exterior insulation across the different walls. In general, if
 the same exterior insulation were applied to both the wood-framed and LGS walls, they
 would be expected to require similar time and labor.

 2.5 Construction Cost Analysis
 A key project goal was to evaluate the impact of high-performance envelope measures
 and automation-assisted modular construction on the eventual cost of the high-
 efficiency homes and to compare the costs against site-built IECC 2018 compliant
 homes. The construction cost analysis work consisted of the following:

 • We reviewed the technical literature and construction databases to gather cost
 and sales price information for site-built homes, with a specific focus on
 construction costs related to envelope framing and insulation.

 • We analyzed Dvele’s time tracking data for construction labor: Initially, Dvele
 implemented a “smart factory” system, which laid the foundation for their
 production and time tracking efforts. However, Dvele has transitioned to a system
 called Trunk, which allows them to track various processes, identify potential
 bottlenecks, and support the production floor teams. Trunk also has a live
 tracking capability and provides real-time visibility into all activities on the
 production floor through an online platform called Watchtower.

 • Dvele prepared a summary of price projections for their current and future costs
 based on their plans of scaling, improving production efficiency via increased
 automation, and leveraging economies of scale with higher production volume.
 These projections include breakdowns of the total sales price of a modular home
 into major categories and include projections for both materials and labor. The
 price estimates were used to compare the cost of Dvele’s automation-assisted
 LGS modular homes with that of site-built homes.

 A major challenge faced by the project team was finding accurate construction costs for
 conventional wood-framed site-built homes, especially those conforming to IECC 2018
 standards and of a similar design and footprint as the Dvele modular homes. Another
 challenge was finding granular data differentiating the costs of the construction and
 envelope measures from the overall cost of the homes.

 The project team was able to find limited construction cost data from the literature and

 2.6 Embodied Carbon Analysis
 The embodied carbon analysis of Dvele’s manufacturing method considered two main
 categories: framing and insulation. The building’s main structural elements changed
 from dimensional lumber to sheet roll steel as a result of the new automation processes.
 Dvele also changed the insulation approach to the building modules, eliminating batt
 insulation between studs and adding more external insulation.

 Using data from the time analysis study, Dvele building model files, and additional Revit
 plugins, the team was able to make direct comparisons between the embodied carbon
 of manufacturing assemblies using traditional softwood lumber and structural steel, as
 well as sample several versions of insulation methodologies. Referencing the standard
 size test walls built during the time-and-motion study as a baseline, the team compared
 different build types and their respective embodied carbon intensities. The team also
 analyzed the effects of framing and insulation changes on a full-building embodied
 carbon value, based on a single model bill of materials and architectural drawings. A
 Revit plugin, One Click LCA (One Click LCA 2018), was used to calculate the embodied
 carbon of various framing and insulation materials. This tool references an internal
 catalog of environmental product declarations (EPDs) to evaluate the embodied carbon
 of the respective construction materials. The embodied carbon values are based on an
 aggregate library of various material types and their associated EPDs.

 It is important to note that these values consist of life cycle stages A1–A3 (cradle-to-
 gate methodology), representing only the material-production-related emissions
 (Gibbons et al. 2022). These calculations do not include emissions related to material
 shipping, use, or end of life, all of which impact the final amount of embodied carbon
 and the sustainability of the finished building. Life cycle stages beyond A3 dramatically
 increase the variability of the carbon calculations, as these life cycle stages are
 influenced by elements outside the manufacturer’s control and, for that reason, were
 outside the scope of this analysis.

 Increasing the overall R-value from 33.3 to 41.7 h⋅ft2⋅°F/Btu is expected to have a
 diminished energy savings benefit. A study by Kosny et al. (2010) included whole-
 building energy modeling of a single-story residential building in climate zone 3A that
 showed rapidly diminishing benefits of increased exterior wall insulation beyond about
 R30. Similar diminishing benefits of increased exterior wall insulation were observed by
 Mora et al. (2012). Although the R-value threshold for diminishing returns will vary
 based on the building and climate conditions, available literature indicates that beyond a
 certain point, adding more insulation does not yield meaningful energy savings.

 Hence, the decision was made to remove the cavity insulation from the LGS test walls
 to test whether an acceptable R-value could be achieved without cavity insulation.
 Omitting cavity insulation is expected to reduce installation costs (labor and materials)
 and streamline the manufacturing process. Cavity insulation installation is a labor-
 intensive process that cannot easily be automated because the insulation must be

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                         21

 finessed around wiring and other bodies internal to the wall. Furthermore, in case of
 LGS walls, the effective R-value of cavity insulation is expected to be de-rated by >50%
 (AISI 2021), and its impact on overall thermal performance will be limited. The
 combination of potential installation cost savings, lower effective R-value of cavity
 insulation, and reaching the point of diminishing returns with R-value makes it a
 reasonable approach to eliminate cavity insulation from the LGS walls.

 3.3 Energy Modeling Results
 3.3.1 Heating and Cooling Load Calculations
 The initial whole-building energy modeling work focused on estimating the heating and
 cooling loads as a function of the different envelope characteristics in the three Dvele
 home designs: Angora, Mills, and Roberts. In the models, the outdoor weather
 conditions were based on typical meteorological year (TMY) weather data, available
 from the National Solar Radiation Database (NREL 2025). The indoor conditions were
 based on the DOE prototype residential building models (DOE 2025). The heating and
 cooling set points were assumed to be 72°F and 75°F, respectively, which are typical for
 residential buildings. The Dvele and VIP home models included mechanical ventilation
 to account for the very tight envelope via energy recovery ventilators, which were
 considered in the heating/cooling load calculations and energy analyses. The energy
 recovery ventilators were modeled as enthalpy-based heat recovery objects in the ideal
 loads air system. Additionally, the air infiltration models were adjusted to represent a 0.6
 ACH50 home, in accordance with Dvele’s specifications.

 In addition to the upgraded envelope scenarios listed in Table 11, an additional scenario
 was modeled following the hot box tests of the LGS test walls. This additional scenario
 was based on Dvele 3, but with the external wall R-value revised to 30.5 h⋅ft2⋅°F/Btu to
 match the hot box test results of the LGS2 and LGS3 test walls (see Table 7). This
 scenario, designated as Dvele 3b, assumes no cavity insulation and only external
 insulation. The Dvele 3b case was modeled only for the Mills home design, the largest
 modular home design that was modeled. The calculated loads and savings were similar
 to the Dvele 3 and VIP options. The 3b case was not modeled for the Angora and
 Roberts designs.

 3.3.2 Energy Savings and Emission Reduction Calculations
 The purpose of the energy simulation is to predict the heating, ventilation, and air
 conditioning (HVAC) load and corresponding energy usage for multiple design options.
 The ideal load air system was used in EnergyPlus models to calculate cooling and
 heating loads to ensure that the thermostat set point is met at all hours, which allows a
 fair comparison among the various design options. However, the ideal load air system
 does not use detailed HVAC models to calculate energy use. Therefore, we calculated
 the hourly cooling and heating coefficient of performance (COP) of the HVAC systems
 for each climate zone using the IECC 2018 compliant DOE prototype residential
 building models.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                           35

 Another reason for selecting the ideal load air system was that directly modeling
 predefined HVAC systems for buildings with different envelope configurations can result
 in different numbers of hours when each building does not meet the desired thermostat
 set points, commonly known as “unmet load hours.” This could lead to varying indoor
 temperatures or varying magnitudes of unmet load hours for the buildings with the
 different envelope configurations and corresponding differences in HVAC energy use.
 Therefore, when predefined HVAC is used in building models with different envelope
 configurations, or any energy efficiency and load reduction options, the energy savings
 can be artificially impacted by not having identical indoor temperatures between the
 different buildings. This potential issue was resolved by using the ideal load air system,
 which ensured that the buildings always met the thermostat set points and that the
 estimated HVAC energy usage was impacted solely by the differences in the envelope
 configurations, not by different unmet load hours among the various buildings.

 It is likely that there will be hours when the IECC 2018 model building does not require
 cooling or heating but the other building models (Dvele 1–3 and VIP) do need cooling or
 heating. When HVAC equipment is “off” in IECC 2018 models (i.e., there are no heating
 or cooling loads), HVAC COP cannot be calculated. To resolve this issue and get a
 common set of COP values, we calculated hourly COPs for the DOE prototype building
 models. Next, regression methods were developed to calculate hourly HVAC COP
 values for the entire year as a function of outdoor air temperature for each climate zone,
 which could then be applied to the IECC 2018, Dvele 1–3/3b, and VIP models. Four
 regression methods—linear, gradient boosting, random forest, and voting regressor—
 were evaluated using the following procedure:

 1. Calculate cooling and heating COPs for hours when the DOE prototype
 residential building model requires cooling or heating.

 2. Calculate regression equations to predict the COPs as a function of outdoor air
 temperature and relative humidity.

 a. Linear regression fits a linear model with coefficients to minimize the
 residual sum of squares between the observed targets in the dataset.

 b. Gradient boosting builds an additive model in a forward stage-wise
 fashion; it allows for the optimization of arbitrary differentiable loss
 functions.

 c. A random forest is a meta-estimator that fits a number of classifying
 decision trees on various subsamples of the dataset and uses averaging
 to improve the predictive accuracy and control overfitting.

 d. A voting regressor is an ensemble meta-estimator that fits several base
 regressors. Then it averages the individual predictions to form a final
 prediction. It randomly selected 80% of existing COP data as the training
 dataset and the remaining 20% of the data as the validation dataset in the
 reference cases when HVAC was on for regressions.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                36

 3. Substitute the missing hourly COPs from the DOE prototype residential building
 model with the COPs calculated from regression equations.

 4. Use the hourly COPs to convert cooling and heating loads to cooling and heating
 energy use for all energy models (IECC, Dvele 1–3, and VIP).

 The gradient boosting method yielded the lowest mean squared error between the
 EnergyPlus data and the equation-based COP values. Hence, the heating and cooling

 with weather data from the year 2012. It should be noted that the energy modeling to
 estimate the building loads was generated using TMY weather data, representing
 weather patterns from 1976–2005. This discrepancy is noted but could not be readily
 corrected due to the lack of emissions data prior to 2012.

 used the long-run marginal emission rate (LRMER) with the metric name “lrmer_co2” to
 capture the hourly kg/MWh emissions of CO2e. The LRMER represents the induced or
 avoided emissions resulting from a change in electric demand, incorporating both
 operational and structural consequences.

 LGS construction methodology removes two steps in the manufacturing process: (1)
 cutting framing pieces to size, and (2) drilling holes in the frame assembly. In this
 instance, drilling holes in the wood-framed wall was time-consuming, requiring manual

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation                                      51

 measurements and the use of power tools. Omitting these steps can significantly reduce
 the construction time and reduce potential safety hazards associated with the saws,
 hole saws, and manual drilling. It is noted that, during this demonstration, cavity
 insulation was added to both LGS walls. The cavity insulation from the LGS walls was
 removed before the hot box testing at ORNL.

 The following additional observations were made:

 1. Lumber sorting and sizing took, on average, 10 min, whereas LGS loading and
 printing took an average of 5 min.

 2. The largest time savings difference between the two construction methodologies
 was achieved from the “printed” segments having pre-punched holes for
 assembly and interior running of cables or pipes. In the standard wood-frame
 construction, it took approximately an additional 20 min to drill these holes into
 the wall frames.

 a. The workers needed to spend time replacing the drill bits, including time to
 search for suitable alternate bits, while drilling holes in the wood framing.
 The stopwatches were partially paused during the time it took for the
 workers to search for the drill bits.

 3. Along with the time savings benefit, the automated LGS methodology removed
 several physical dangers from the construction process. The LGS printers shear
 the individual pieces to size, eliminating the need for workers to use a saw to cut
 lumber to size, and the automated punching of holes eliminates the need for drills
 and multiple drill bits.

 4. LGS construction has the additional step of setting up frame supports for building
 the assembly, but this step did not have a time penalty, as it could be carried out
 concurrently with printing the LGS pieces.

 The analysis is missing time values for a couple of steps that might impact the overall
 time analysis.

 1. The time taken to produce Revit drawings for the model segments was unknown,
 but it is a common step for both wood and LGS walls and is not expected to
 impact the findings.

 2. The time requirement for converting the Revit drawing into machine language for
 the LGS printing machines was not known. Per feedback from Dvele, originally,
 once the drawing files were uploaded for a new module, there was a 1-h review
 process to ensure that the specifications were loaded appropriately. But this 1-h
 review was associated with an entire building or module, so the “review” period
 would be much shorter for the relatively small wall section that was built for the
 demonstration. Recently, Dvele has implemented an automated translation
 process from Revit to machine language, and the manual reviews are no longer
 needed.

 4 Summary, Conclusions, and Future Work
 The TEAMH project evaluated the potential cost and energy benefits of combining
 emerging envelope technologies with automation-assisted modular home manufacturing
 as compared to site-built residential homes meeting the IECC 2018 standard. The key
 emerging envelope technology that was considered was a VIP technology with
 fiberglass core material.

 The project approach included laboratory-testing-informed building energy modeling.
 We performed energy modeling of multiple modular home designs across 12 climate
 zones. The modular homes were modeled using the IECC 2018 envelope specifications
 as the baseline. Additional models considered multiple combinations of wall and roof
 assemblies with upgraded insulation systems being considered by Dvele, as well as one
 scenario with VIPs in both the wall and roof assemblies. The energy modeling results
 indicated that the upgraded envelope assemblies can yield heating energy savings of
 up to 50% and cooling energy savings of up to 34% compared to IECC 2018 standards.

 Dvele implemented multiple upgrades to their manufacturing capabilities. This included
 designing a digitized, collaborative design software platform that integrates into a
 robotized production process, translating BIM into robotics assembly language. Dvele
 also commissioned multiple truss and panel machines to partially automate the
 production of LGS structural members with pre-punched holes. A demonstration
 comparing the construction of a wood-framed wall following the traditional method and
 multiple steel-framed walls using automation assistance was performed. The objective
 of this demonstration was to quantify the efficiency gained from incorporating
 automation equipment into the construction process. Each step in the construction of
 the wood and steel walls was identified and logged. All the walls were constructed and
 assembled sequentially by the same two-man crew. The build times of the test walls
 were compared on a total and step-by-step basis. The time savings analysis showed a
 reduction in manufacturing time of the steel walls by 24%–46% compared to the
 baseline wood-frame construction.

 One of the project goals was to evaluate the cost premium of the automation-assisted,
 high-performance modular construction compared to the IECC 2018 site-built
 construction. Dvele is planning further manufacturing process improvements, including
 additional production automation and robotics, use of standard modular home designs,
 and increased production volume. The combination of these measures is expected to
 significantly reduce the cost of Dvele’s future modular homes compared to their current
 offerings.

 Based on Dvele’s internal cost projections, which assume increasing levels of
 automation, production capacity, and economies of scale, the future cost of a high-
 performance and above-code 1,200-ft2 modular home is estimated to be about $149–
 $179 per ft2. For comparison, based on census data, average cost of residential homes
 in the western region of the United States in 2017–2021 was in the range of $140–$170
 per ft2.

U.S. Department of Energy | Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation          66

 The project team performed high-level embodied carbon analysis of the test walls and
 sample home modules built by Dvele. As expected, LGS construction has significantly
 higher embodied carbon than wood construction. On a unit mass basis, LGS
 construction was estimated to have 28.4 times the embodied carbon as wood. Using
 LGS with 100% recycled steel content can reduce this ratio to about 6.6. The embodied
 carbon analysis only considered the embodied carbon associated with the production of
 LGS and wood framing, without assuming any waste. Traditional wood-framed
 construction is expected to generate some waste, whereas Dvele’s LGS construction
 processes seek to drastically reduce or even eliminate waste. Furthermore, energy use
 and embodied carbon related to automation-assisted LGS construction and traditional
 wood-framed construction also need to be considered; however, they could not be
 pursued in this project due to a lack of appropriate data.

 Finally, to better evaluate the energy and cost benefits of high-performance modular
 homes built using advanced construction practices, we need improved and more
 granular data collection from site-built homes. To enable appropriate comparison, we
 need accurate material and labor cost estimates for construction and insulation
 materials and associated installation labor costs for site-built homes. In addition,
 knowledge of the as-built energy performance of site-built homes will help develop
 better estimates of the operational energy cost savings from high-performance modular
 homes.
