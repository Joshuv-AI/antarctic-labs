Management of high-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer: a real-world study in North America, Europe and Japan
Authors: Jens Bedke, Anthony Eccleston, Mia Unsworth, Julia Brinkmann, Jane Chang, Abin Koshy, Laure Manuel, Jacob Skilling, Emilia Biondi, Elliott Brown, Caitlin Ford, Gary Steinberg, Yair Lotan, Yohann Loriot, Oscar Rodriguez Faba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionNon-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) accounts for approximately 75% of newly diagnosed bladder cancers and is particularly prevalent in men. Guidelines stratify patients into risk categories based on disease characteristics as patients often recur or progress to muscle-invasive disease. Most guidelines recommend transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) followed by up to 3 years of Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) therapy for high-risk (HR) patients. For very HR patients or those who recur after BCG, radical cystectomy (RC) is recommended. This study aimed to describe the profile of individuals with HR-NMIBC, alongside treatment patterns and outcomes across Europe, North America, and Japan.MethodsData were extracted from the Adelphi Disease Specific Program for 1,864 patients initially diagnosed with HR-NMIBC and 273 with very HR-NMIBC.ResultsFirst-line treatment was mainly intravesical BCG, however, less than half of patients received the guideline recommended maintenance BCG and none received RC. Second-line treatment options were similar to first-line, with RC usage still rare.ConclusionWhile TURBT+BCG was shown to be the current standard of care, the underutilization of full guideline recommended treatment alongside the relatively high number of recurrences highlights the need for novel therapeutic approaches, either in combination with BCG therapy, or as alternative first-line treatments.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the ninth most common cancer globally, and the sixth most common for males [1] and the median age of diagnosis is 73 years [2]. Bladder cancer also represents the thirteenth highest cancer-related mortality amongst all patients, and the ninth highest amongst males [3]. Tobacco smoking is the primary cause of bladder cancer, and accounts for approximately 50% of cases, while occupational exposure to aromatic amines, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and chlorinated hydrocarbons accounts for about 10% of all cases [4]. Given the role of external factors and the epidemiology of bladder cancer, the incidence varies across regions and countries [1].

Non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) accounts for approximately 75% of newly diagnosed bladder cancer [5]. NMIBC is primarily confined to the urothelial lining or submucosa of the bladder (stages Ta, T1, and carcinoma in situ [CIS]) [6]. Common symptoms at diagnosis include hematuria, frequent/painful urination and lower back/pelvic pain. Since 90% of patients are over the age of 55 years, comorbidities are frequent and should also be considered when making treatment decisions [7].

The survival prognosis for patients with NMIBC is relatively favorable, with the cancer-specific survival in high-risk disease ranging between 70% and 85% at 10 years. Patients diagnosed with low-grade disease have a higher 10-year survival rate compared with patients with high-grade disease [8]. The rates of recurrence and progression to MIBC are important surrogate endpoints for NMIBC prognosis, as these are major determinants of long-term outcome. Depending on disease status, rates of recurrence have been reported to be as high as 78% within 5 years, and rates of progression to muscle-invasive or metastatic disease up to 45% [9]. However, NMIBC is a clinically heterogeneous group of cancers with a wide range of recurrence and progression probabilities that depend on several clinical, pathologic, and behavioral factors [4,8]. As a result of the heterogeneity seen in NMIBC, professional societies, including the American Urological Association (AUA) and the European Association of Urology (EAU), have adopted evidence-based guidelines, with a risk-stratification system classifying NMIBC to either low-risk, intermediate-risk, high-risk, and some very high-risk [10].

The EAU guidelines recommend that patients are stratified into high- or very high-risk groups based on risk factors of cancer progression and recurrence. These factors include tumor stage, grade, size, focality, presence of CIS, and response to intravesical treatments [4]. Additional risk factors for risk stratification are age ≥70 years old at initial diagnosis, multiple papillary tumors, and a tumor disease diameter >3 cm, which help differentiate patients at high- and very high-risk [4].

Patients stratified as high-risk include all patients with high-grade (HG)/G3 T1 tumors without carcinoma in-situ (CIS) (except those in the very high-risk group); all patients with CIS (except those in the very high-risk group); any patients with low-grade (LG)/G2 Ta or G1 T1 tumors without CIS who have all 3 additional risk factors; patients with HG/G3 Ta or LG T1 tumors without CIS with at least 2 additional risk factors; and patients with G2 T1 tumors without CIS and at least 1 additional risk factor (Supplementary Table 1). Patients in the very high-risk category include all patients with HG/G3 Ta tumors and CIS who have all 3 additional risk factors present; all G2 T1 with CIS with at least 2 additional risk factors; all HG/G3 T1 with CIS with at least 1 additional risk factor; and any HG/G3 T1 tumors without CIS who have all 3 additional risk factors present (Supplementary Table 1).

High-risk NMIBC (HR-NMIBC) has a relatively high propensity for recurrence and progression to muscle-invasive disease. The presence of CIS is associated with an increased risk of invasive disease, including increased cancer progression rates and worse cancer-specific outcomes. If untreated, 50% of patients with CIS tumors will progress to muscle invasive disease within 5 years. If treated, patients diagnosed with CIS tumors have a 30–40% risk of progression within 10 years [7]. For patients with NMIBC, treatment is directed at reducing recurrences and preventing progression to a more advanced stage. Patients with HR-NMIBC are commonly managed using transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) which removes all visible lesions from the bladder and is also used to carry out random biopsies.

The standard of care treatment for HR-NMIBC after TURBT is generally well aligned across the treatment guidelines [11]. In general, the preferred treatment post-TURBT is adjuvant intravesical immune therapy with Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG), which is also an independent risk factor for recurrence after TURBT in patients with NMIBC. It is possible that certain systemic adverse effects are related to traumatic catheterization and absorption of BCG, which is why it is not recommended to start BCG before 14 days following TURBT [12]. The guidelines recommend six weekly treatments of BCG induction followed by maintenance therapy [4]. There are slight differences in guideline recommendations related to maintenance BCG: the AUA recommends 3 years of maintenance BCG for high-risk patients, whereas the EAU suggests 1–3 years of maintenance therapy [11]. BCG has become the standard of care based on a 35% reduction in 5-year recurrences [11].

Despite the effectiveness of BCG nearly 40% of patients will experience disease recurrence and/or progression [11]. Heterogeneity in the duration of BCG treatment in real-world clinical practice owing to a variety of factors such as tolerability or shortages may contribute to this rate of recurrence. Patients treated with 1–3 years of maintenance BCG have a varied prognosis for both time to first recurrence and time to progression and death, based on tumor stage and grade [13]. Moreover, compliance with NMIBC guidelines has been shown to be low worldwide. Rates of compliance with BCG have been declining over time. Compliance is as high as 85–98% for the 6 week schedule of induction therapy, then drops to 53–83% for the 12 month maintenance period, then to as low as 0–36% over 3 years of maintenance therapy [14]. Currently, cystectomy is recommended for patients who recur despite BCG, but there are several FDA and non-FDA approved bladder sparing options. For patients that express very high-risk features at diagnosis, cystectomy is recommended as first-line cancer management due to the high risk for progression to a more advanced stage [7]. However, many patients are either medically unfit for surgery, or choose not to undergo such an invasive procedure due to its profound impact on quality of life [10,15].

BCG remains the gold standard treatment for patients with intermediate-and high-risk NMIBC yet, despite being the standard of care, patients still experience high rates of recurrence or progression. Moreover, as countries continue to experience BCG shortages and patients vocalize a desire to avoid radical cystectomy, more effective strategies to effectively treat NMIBC are needed. These current challenges presented to clinicians demonstrate that there remains a high unmet need for enhanced treatment options for patients. The desire is for more effective strategies that would allow patients to preserve their bladder, while providing durable disease control through prevention or delays in disease recurrence and progression [16].

New immunotherapies including monoclonal antibodies (mAB) specific for human programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) have now shown promising results in BCG-unresponsive patients [17]. Preclinical studies in preclinical models and tumors from patients with HR-NMIBC suggest that exposure to BCG is associated with increased PD-L1 expression [18]. Since enhanced PD-L1 expression might contribute to the immune escape mechanism in bladder cancer cells, the combination of PD-(L)1 inhibition with BCG may be expected to improve therapeutic efficacy. Sasanlimab, a humanized, mAB specific for human PD-1 that blocks the interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1/PD-L2, in combination with BCG induction and maintenance, has been shown to be superior to BCG monotherapy in prolonging event-free survival in patients with BCG-Naïve HR-NMIBC [16].

Aim of the study

The objective of this multinational, observational study was to aid an understanding of the clinical and demographic profile of patients with HR-NMIBC. In addition, another objective was to document treatment patterns and associated outcomes in real-world clinical practice across a number of differing geographic regions.

Methods

Study design

Data were extracted from the Adelphi NMIBC Disease Specific Program (DSP™), a cross-sectional retrospective survey of physicians (urologists and medical oncologists) and patients with HR-NMIBC presenting in a real-world clinical setting between January and June 2023 in North America (USA and Canada), Europe (France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and the United Kingdom [UK]) and Japan. DSPs are large, multinational, observational surveys collecting information on real-world clinical practice, designed to identify current disease management and patient- and physician-reported disease impact [19–21].

A geographically representative sample of physicians were eligible to participate in the DSP provided they were personally responsible for treatment decisions and management of at least five patients with HR-NMIBC per month. Physicians were recruited to participate by local fieldwork agents, following completion of a short screening questionnaire; the data collection setting was secondary urological oncology services (public or private hospitals, clinics, or offices). Physicians completed a patient record form (PRFs) for up to 8 consecutive patients with HR-NMIBC through review and abstraction from existing patient clinical records, and through the judgment of the respondent physician, which is considered consistent with decisions made in routine clinical practice.

Patients were eligible for inclusion provided they were 18 years of age or older with a confirmed diagnosis of HR-NMIBC as defined by EAU criteria at least 12 months before data collection and were not involved in clinical trials. Additionally, patients were stratified as high- or very high-risk NMIBC at their initial NMIBC diagnosis according to EAU definitions (Supplementary Table 1). The PRF contained detailed questions on patients’ demographics, clinical assessments, treatment history, and clinical outcomes.

This manuscript was developed in adherence to STROBE guidelines.

Data analysis

As the primary objective of the survey was descriptive (i.e., no priori hypotheses specified), and the sample size was determined by the duration of the survey period. Therefore, formal sample size calculations were not applicable and were not performed. Continuous data were expressed as means and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables, summarized by frequencies and proportions. Missing data were not imputed, such that the base of patients for analysis could vary from variable to variable. Analysis was conducted in IBM® SPSS® Data Collection Survey Reporter Version 7.5.1 or later (International Business Machines Corp., New York, USA).

Ethical considerations

The DSP complies with all relevant market research guidelines and legal obligations. Data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association guidelines [22]. The survey was performed in full accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection, including the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act 1996 [23] and Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act legislation [24]. This survey was reviewed by the Pearl Institutional Review Board and was given exempt status based on the methodology used (protocol number AG9392). The DSP is non-interventional and employs solely retrospective data collection, and no identifiable protected health information was extracted during the course of the study.

Results

Survey results were provided by 364 physicians (165 medical oncologists, 199 urologists). Three quarters of these physicians worked in a hospital setting (Supplementary Table 2). Physicians reported a mean of 37% of their caseload being HR – NMIBC patients at the time of data collection. Physicians provided information on a total of 2,137 patients with NMIBC. According to EAU definitions, 1,864 (87%) patients were categorized as HR-NMIBC and the remaining 273 (13%) defined as very HR-NMIBC at their initial NMIBC diagnosis.

The majority of NMIBC patients (75%) were male, 89% of those with a known ethnicity (n = 1,597) were white Caucasian, with a mean (SD) age of 70.8 (9.0) years (Table 1). Approximately 20% of patients were in full- or part-time employment and 79% were current or ex-smokers. The clinical characteristics of patients included in this analysis are considered representative of the general HR-NMIBC population. In general, the characteristics of patients with HR-NMIBC were similar to patients with very HR-NMIBC (Table 2). Physician-reported patient symptoms at time of data collection included hematuria, frequent need to urinate, and other urinary disorders. The majority of patients presented with comorbidities, which included diabetes without chronic complications, COPD, and peripheral vascular disease. Approximately 30% of patients had no known comorbidities. Interestingly, more patients from Japan (53%) reported no comorbidities compared to approximately 25% in the other regions. The mean Charlson Comorbidity Index was 2.8, and approximately half of patients (51%) had an ECOG status score of 1–2. At the time of the initial NMIBC diagnosis, 10% of patients were diagnosed with CIS only tumors, 8% had CIS + T1 tumors, 3% had CIS + Ta tumors, and the majority (79%) had papillary only tumors with 73% HG T1 and 6% HG Ta stage (Table 2). Patients’ tumor status at the time of data collection was also recorded, where it was indicated that 15% of patients had CIS only tumors, 6% had CIS + T1 tumors, 3% had CIS + Ta tumors, and the majority (76%) had papillary only tumors. HR-NMIBC tumor staging was similar across regions both at diagnosis and at reevaluation. At initial diagnosis, differences were observed between HR-NMIBC and very HR-NMIBC. 11% of HR-NMIBC had CIS only tumors, 1% CIS + T1, 3% CIS + Ta, and 85% papillary only, compared with patients in the very HR-NMIBC cohort, none of whom had CIS only, 56% CIS + T1, 4% CIS+ Ta, and 40% papillary only tumors.

The most common first-line treatment after NMIBC diagnosis was TURBT (79%) with the majority (62%) receiving intravesical therapy following TURBT and the remainder (17%) receiving TURBT alone (Table 3). An additional 11% received intravesical therapy alone and no patients underwent an upfront radical cystectomy. The majority of patients received BCG (62%) as part of their first-line treatment plan, with 52% receiving TURBT plus BCG, 1% receiving another type of surgery plus BCG, and the remaining 9% receiving BCG alone. Almost two-thirds of all patients who underwent TURBT also received BCG. Treatment patterns were similar in both HR-NMIBC and very HR-NMIBC groups as well as across geographic regions. A few patients (10%) received chemotherapy as their first-line option. Discussion

This study, conducted in North America, Europe, and Japan, aimed to assess the treatment patterns, outcomes, and burden of disease in patients with HR-NMIBC. International guidelines suggest that patients at high risk of tumor progression should undergo TURBT to remove the tumor followed by intravesical BCG therapy [4,8,25]. The real-world data included in these analyses indicate that approximately 80% of patients received the recommended TURBT as their first-line treatment option. Moreover, the majority of these patients received subsequent intravesical therapy after TURBT, although twice the number of patients treated in Japan received TURBT without intravesical therapy compared with Europe and North America. This may be attributed to a more conservative approach toward treatment-related toxicity, frequent use of reduced-dose or shortened maintenance BCG schedules, and the widespread use of the Tokyo-172 BCG strain [26], which has contributed to the development of local treatment strategies. In addition, concerns about tolerability in elderly patients and recurrent BCG shortages have also influenced clinical practice in Japan. Recent real-world Japanese data have shown that many patients receive less than 1 year of BCG maintenance, substantially shorter than standard EAU/AUA recommendations [27].

Expert guidelines recommend intravesical BCG induction and at least 1 year of maintenance following TURBT for patients with HR-NMIBC. Our data indicate that BCG maintenance therapy was used less than recommended by the guidelines, with fewer than half of patients receiving both BCG induction and maintenance. Our data suggest that the level of adherence to BCG induction therapy is generally high. Not only did patients from Japan more frequently receive only TURBT but of those who did receive BCG therapy, approximately half received only BCG induction. Patients treated in Europe had the greatest likelihood of receiving both BCG induction and maintenance compared with Japan and North America. The heterogeneity of treatment data observed in this real-world study demonstrates the regional variations in treatment choices irrespective of guideline treatment recommendations.

Despite the reported global lack of BCG, almost two-thirds of patients received BCG in the first-line setting. Moreover, patients continued to receive BCG in later lines of treatment. This suggests that the BCG shortage may not have affected treatment choice. It is possible that the shortage did create concerns in isolated regions or specific institutes. It may have also impacted length of maintenance.

International guidelines also recommend that radical cystectomy should be considered for select patients, in particular patients with BCG-unresponsive tumors [4,8]. In our survey, no patients diagnosed with either HR-NMIBC or very HR NMIBC had a radical cystectomy. The preferred treatment option for most patients was TURBT plus intravesical therapy. This highlights the clinical reality of the patients’ demand to avoid cystectomy and to preserve the bladder, if possible. Real-world data to date suggest that cystectomy carries a relatively high risk of complications without providing a significant overall survival benefit over BCG [28,29].

We observed that approximately one-quarter of patients with HR-NMIBC experienced recurrence following BCG therapy, one-fifth of whom experienced two or more recurrences. This aligns with the published literature on recurrence rates in BCG treated HR-NMIBC, using the EAU criteria [30]. The high frequency of recurrence, despite many patients receiving standard of care therapy, and pembrolizumab usage ranging from 4% at first-line to 8% following initial recurrence, indicates that the current recommended treatment options are not adequate for HR-NMIBC patients. It is also possible that the recurrences are due to the lack of 1–3 years of BCG treatment recommended in the guidelines.

The optimal treatment strategy for patients failing BCG therapy remains unclear. In general, treatment decision patterns across Europe, North America, and Japan were similar. A systematic literature review of the efficacy of salvage therapies used in patients with NMIBC recurrence after failing BCG therapy demonstrated heterogeneous long-term data with a response rate of around 50% at 3 months [15]. The high number of recurrences and the lower than desired response rates in the second-line setting highlights the need for novel, more effective first-line therapeutic approaches in combination with BCG therapy or alternative treatments in first-line that are more effective.

Limitations

We acknowledge some limitations of this international survey. The DSP is based on a pseudo-random sample of physicians or patients and, while minimal inclusion criteria governed the selection of physicians, their participation was influenced by their willingness to complete the survey. To minimize bias in patient selection, physicians were asked to provide data for a consecutive series of eligible patients. Patient eligibility and classification was based on the professional judgment of the respondent physician and not using a diagnostic checklist; however, it is considered representative of the physician’s real-world classification of their patients.

It should also be noted that the survey was designed to facilitate an understanding of real-world clinical practice, and thus physicians could only report on data available at the time of the consultation. Therefore, this represents the evidence they had when making any clinical treatment and other management decisions at that consultation. The point-in-time design of this survey therefore prevented any conclusions about causal relationships; however, identification of significant associations was possible.

Finally, it is recognized that patients participating may not reflect the general HR-NMIBC population since the DSP only includes patients who are consulting with their physician, and it is therefore recognized that patients who were visiting physicians more often are more likely to have been included in the study. This study, nevertheless, involved a high number of physicians, working in different settings, across different geographical regions, thereby ensuring that the sample is likely to be representative of the overall population of patients with HR-NMIBC.

Conclusion

While TURBT plus BCG remains the current standard of care for patients with HR-NMIBC, there remains a substantial proportion of patients whose disease recurs within 1 year. Moreover, the majority of patients do not receive the guideline-recommended course of BCG therapy. The proportion of patients who do not receive the recommended 3 years of BCG maintenance, due to high recurrence rates or poor tolerability, highlight the need for novel HR-NMIBC treatment interventions that can reduce the serious risk of recurrence and disease progression, improve patients’ functioning, and restore their quality of life.
