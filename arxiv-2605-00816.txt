On the largest sum-free subset of the lattice cube
Peter Keevash, Jeck Lim

Abstract
We determine the limiting density of the largest sum-free subset of the lattice cube { 1 , 2 , … , n } d \{1,2,\ldots,n\}^{d} for all d d , thus resolving the natural conjecture that it is constructed by two appropriate hyperplane slices.



1
Introduction

A subset
S
of an abelian group is called
sum-free
if there are no
x,y,z\in S
such that
x+y=z
. The aim of this paper is to determine the density of the largest sum-free subset of the lattice cube
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}^{d}
. For notational convenience, we denote by
[n]
the set
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, and we will be studying sum-free subsets of
[n+1]^{d}
. If
S\subset[n+1]^{d}
is the largest sum-free subset, then we would like to determine the value

c_{d}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|S|}{n^{d}}.

For
d=1
, it is folklore that
c_{1}=1/2
. The problem of determining
c_{d}
for
d\geq 2
was posed by Aydinian
[
2
, Problem 10]
(in the case
d=2
) and Cameron
[
1
]
, and is also featured in a list of 100 open problems by Green
[
5
, Problem 6]
. The case
d=2
was resolved by Elsholtz and Rackham
[
4
]
, with
c_{2}=3/5
. Recently, Lepsveridze and Sun
[
8
]
resolved the cases
d=3
and
d=4
.

In general, it is believed that the slice

\left\{x\in[n+1]^{d}\;\middle|\;u\leq\sum_{i}x_{i}<2u\right\}

is optimal, where
u
is chosen to maximize the size of the slice. To quantify
u
, it is convenient to consider the continuous version in the limit
n\to\infty
.

Let
\mathbf{1}_{d}
denote the all ones vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If the context is clear, we just write
\mathbf{1}
. For
u\in[0,d]
, let
S_{d}(u):=\left\{x\in[0,1]^{d}\;\middle|\;u\leq\sum_{i}x_{i}<2u\right\}
. Let
u_{d}^{*}
be the value of
u
that maximizes
\Vol(S_{d}(u))
, and let
c_{d}^{*}
be the maximum volume
\Vol(S_{d}(u_{d}^{*}))
.

Note that the set
\left\{x\in[n+1]^{d}\;\middle|\;u_{d}^{*}n\leq\sum_{i}x_{i}<2u_{d}^{*}n\right\}
is sum-free and has asymptotic density
c_{d}^{*}
, thus we have the easy bound
c_{d}\geq c_{d}^{*}
. The conjecture is that
c_{d}=c_{d}^{*}
for all
d
, which was known up to
d\leq 4
. We prove this conjecture for all
d
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
S\subseteq[n+1]^{d}
be sum-free. Then
|S|\leq c_{d}^{*}n^{d}+O(n^{d-1})
.

Our proof follows the same approach as Lepsveridze and Sun
[
8
]
. Their idea begins with a simple but key inequality relating the sizes of one-dimensional fibers of
S
, then rewrites the problem of bounding
|S|
as a linear program. It turns out that this linear program is sufficient to obtain the optimal bound for
|S|
. To solve this linear program (or rather, to obtain a good upper bound), it is enough to construct feasible weights for the dual linear program. The construction of such weights essentially amounts to showing that the uniform distributions on certain slices of the cube are jointly mixable.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}
be distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})
is
jointly mixable
if there exists a coupling
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})
with marginals
X_{i}\sim\mu_{i}
for all
i
such that
X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3}=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{1}]+\mathbb{E}[\mu_{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\mu_{3}]
almost surely.

Definition 1.3
.

For
r\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
L_{d}(r):=\left\{x\in[n+1]^{d}\;\middle|\;\sum_{i}x_{i}=r\right\}
. For
r\in\mathbb{R}
, let
L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(r):=\left\{x\in[0,1]^{d}\;\middle|\;\sum_{i}x_{i}=r\right\}
. For any set
L
, finite or of non-zero finite measure, denote by
U(L)
the uniform distribution on
L
.

Lepsveridze and Sun reduced Theorem
1.1
to the following joint mixability result (and another weaker but more complicated coupling which we ultimately avoid).

Conjecture 1.4
.

For
d\geq 2
, the triple

U(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(u_{d}^{*})),\ U(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(u_{d}^{*})),\ U(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(d-2u_{d}^{*}))

is jointly mixable.

They are able to explicitly construct such a coupling for
d=3,4
, then obtain a discretised version of the coupling.

The main contribution of this paper is the following general joint mixability result in the discrete setting, which implies the above conjecture by taking suitable limits.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
r,s,t
be nonnegative integers with
r+s+t=dn
. Then the triple

U(L_{d}(r)),\ U(L_{d}(s)),\ U(L_{d}(t))

is jointly mixable.

We will also briefly discuss the problem of determining the size of the largest sum-free subset of convex sets besides the hypercube
[0,1]^{d}
.

2
Joint mixability

We will prove Theorem
1.5
by induction on
d
. There is nothing to prove for
d=1
, since the sets
L_{1}(r),L_{1}(s),L_{1}(t)
are singletons. We first prove the base case
d=2
, which is equivalent to the following result by Perchet, Rigollet, and Le Gouic
[
9
]
. The continuous version was also proved by Wang and Wang
[
10
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
9
, Proposition 15]
)
.

Let
r,s,t
be nonnegative integers. Then
(U([r+1]),U([s+1]),U([t+1]))
is jointly mixable if and only if
r+s+t
is even and
r,s,t
satisfy the triangle inequality, i.e.
r+s\geq t
,
s+t\geq r
, and
t+r\geq s
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
for
d=2
.

By projecting onto the first coordinate, the joint mixability of
(U(L_{d}(r)),U(L_{d}(s)),U(L_{d}(t)))
is equivalent to the joint mixability of intervals

[\max(0,r-n),\min(n,r)],[\max(0,s-n),\min(n,s)],[\max(0,t-n),\min(n,t)].

By translating the intervals so that they start at zero and applying Lemma
2.1
, we see that the joint mixability of the above intervals is equivalent to the numbers

\min(n,r)-\max(0,r-n),\min(n,s)-\max(0,s-n),\min(n,t)-\max(0,t-n)

satisfying the triangle inequality and having even sum. The even sum is easy to verify since
\min(n,r)-\max(0,r-n)\equiv r\pmod{2}
and
r+s+t=2n
, so it suffices to check the triangle inequality. By symmetry, it suffices to show that

\min(n,r)-\max(0,r-n)+\min(n,s)-\max(0,s-n)\geq\min(n,t)-\max(0,t-n).

(1)

Recall that
r+s+t=2n
. If
r,s\leq n
, then (
1
) is equivalent to
r+s\geq\min(n,2n-r-s)-\max(0,n-r-s)
, which is true since
r+s=n-(n-r-s)\geq\min(n,2n-r-s)-\max(0,n-r-s)
. If one of
r,s
is greater than
n
, say
r>n
, then since
r+s+t=2n
, we have
s,t<n
. Hence, (
1
) is equivalent to
2n-r+s\geq t=2n-r-s
, which is true.
∎

We now assume
d\geq 3
. To find a coupling
(X,Y,Z)
on
L_{d}(r)\times L_{d}(s)\times L_{d}(t)
with
X+Y+Z=n\mathbf{1}
, we will find a suitable coupling
(X_{1},Y_{1},Z_{1})
on the first coordinates, then use the induction hypothesis to couple the remaining coordinates. In order for this to yield a uniform marginal on
L_{d}(r)
, the law of
X_{1}
should be the law of the first coordinate of
U(L_{d}(r))
. The coupling
(X_{1},Y_{1},Z_{1})
is obtained by setting up certain linear equations, then applying Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem (or just the Perron–Frobenius theorem) to find a solution.

For
p=(i,j,k)\in[n+1]^{3}
, let

r_{p}:=r-i,\quad s_{p}:=s-j,\quad t_{p}:=t-k.

The support of the coupling on the first coordinates is

A_{d}(r,s,t):=\left\{p=(i,j,k)\in[n+1]^{3}\;\middle|\;i+j+k=n,\ 0\leq r_{p},s_{p},t_{p}\leq(d-1)n\right\}.

It is straightforward to check that
A_{d}(r,s,t)
is non-empty. By our induction hypothesis, for each
p\in A_{d}(r,s,t)
, there exists a coupling
(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y},\tilde{Z})
on
L_{d-1}(r_{p})\times L_{d-1}(s_{p})\times L_{d-1}(t_{p})
with
\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y}+\tilde{Z}=n\mathbf{1}_{d-1}
. Take one such coupling, and let
\nu_{p}
be the law of the first-coordinate triple
(\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{Y}_{1},\tilde{Z}_{1})
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
p\in A_{d}(r,s,t)
, the measure
\nu_{p}
is supported on
A_{d}(r,s,t)
.

Proof.

Let
(x,y,z)
be in the support of
(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y},\tilde{Z})
, then the support of
\nu_{p}
is the set of possible values of
(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})
. Since
x\in L_{d-1}(r_{p}),y\in L_{d-1}(s_{p}),z\in L_{d-1}(t_{p})
and
x+y+z=n\mathbf{1}_{d-1}
, we have
x_{1}+y_{1}+z_{1}=n
. Furthermore,
x_{1}\leq r_{p}\leq r
and

x_{1}=r_{p}-x_{2}-\cdots-x_{d-1}=r-i-x_{2}-\cdots-x_{d-1}\geq r-(d-1)n.

Thus,
0\leq r-x_{1}\leq(d-1)n
. Similarly,
0\leq s-y_{1}\leq(d-1)n
and
0\leq t-z_{1}\leq(d-1)n
. Hence,
(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})\in A_{d}(r,s,t)
.
∎

Let
\mathcal{P}(A_{d}(r,s,t))
denote the space of probability distributions supported on
A_{d}(r,s,t)
, which can be viewed as a simplex with
|A_{d}(r,s,t)|
vertices. Let
T:\mathcal{P}(A_{d}(r,s,t))\to\mathcal{P}(A_{d}(r,s,t))
be the map given by

T(\kappa):=\sum_{p\in A_{d}(r,s,t)}\kappa(p)\nu_{p}.

By Lemma
2.2
,
T(\kappa)
is a probability distribution supported on
A_{d}(r,s,t)
, so
T
is a well-defined map. Note that
T
is linear, hence continuous, and
\mathcal{P}(A_{d}(r,s,t))
is convex and compact. Thus, by Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem, there exists a fixed point of
T
. Take a fixed point
\kappa
, so we have

\kappa=\sum_{p\in A_{d}(r,s,t)}\kappa(p)\nu_{p}.

(2)

We will show that
\kappa
has the desired marginal distributions. Let
N_{d}(r)=|L_{d}(r)|
, so that
N_{d}(r)>0
whenever
0\leq r\leq dn
, and
N_{d}(r)=0
otherwise. Counting the size of
L_{d}(r)
by iterating over the first coordinate, we have

N_{d}(r)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}N_{d-1}(r-i).

(3)

The law of the first coordinate of
U(L_{d}(r))
is given by

q_{d,r}(u):=\frac{N_{d-1}(r-u)}{N_{d}(r)},

whenever
0\leq r\leq dn
and
0\leq u\leq n
. For all other triples
(d,r,u)
, we set
q_{d,r}(u)=0
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
0\leq r\leq dn
and every
u\in[n+1]
we have

q_{d,r}(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}q_{d,r}(i)q_{d-1,r-i}(u).

(4)

Proof.

The right-hand side is equal to

\sum_{i}\frac{N_{d-1}(r-i)}{N_{d}(r)}\cdot\frac{N_{d-2}(r-i-u)}{N_{d-1}(r-i)}=\frac{1}{N_{d}(r)}\sum_{i}N_{d-2}(r-i-u),

where the sum is over
i
such that
0\leq i\leq n
and
0\leq r-i\leq(d-1)n
. Note that if
r-i<0
or
r-i>(d-1)n
, then
N_{d-2}(r-i-u)=0
. Thus, the right-hand side is equal to

\frac{1}{N_{d}(r)}\sum_{i=0}^{n}N_{d-2}(r-i-u)=\frac{N_{d-1}(r-u)}{N_{d}(r)}=q_{d,r}(u),

where the first equality follows from (
3
).
∎

Let
q^{*}
be the law of the first coordinate of
\kappa
, that is,

q^{*}(u)=\sum_{p=(i,j,k)\in A_{d}(r,s,t):i=u}\kappa(p).

Our aim is to show that
q^{*}=q_{d,r}
. Note that the first coordinate of
\nu_{p}
has the same law as the first coordinate of
U(L_{d-1}(r-i))
, which is
q_{d-1,r-i}
. Then by (
2
),
q^{*}
satisfies

\displaystyle q^{*}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=(i,j,k)\in A_{d}(r,s,t)}\kappa(p)q_{d-1,r-i}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j,k:(i,j,k)\in A_{d}(r,s,t)}\kappa(i,j,k)q_{d-1,r-i}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}q^{*}(i)q_{d-1,r-i}(u).

Thus,
q^{*}
satisfies the same equation (
4
) as
q_{d,r}
in Lemma
2.3
. To show that they must be the same, observe that (
4
) says that
q_{d,r}
is a stationary distribution of the Markov chain with transition matrix
M(i,u)=q_{d-1,r-i}(u)
. The state space of this Markov chain is the set

J:=\left\{i\in\mathbb{Z}\;\middle|\;0\leq i\leq n,0\leq r-i\leq(d-1)n\right\}.

Note that for
i,u\in J
, we have
M(i,u)>0
if and only if
0\leq r-i-u\leq(d-1)n
.
In particular,
M(i,u)>0
if and only if
M(u,i)>0
,
so we can represent connectivity in the chain by an undirected graph
G
on
J
.
If
r\leq(d-1)n
, then
0\in J
and
M(i,0)>0
for all
i\in J
, so the Markov chain is ergodic, as
G
is connected and not bipartite (due to the loop at
0
). If
r>(d-1)n
, then
n\in J
and
M(i,n)>0
for all
i\in J
since
d\geq 3
, so the Markov chain is also ergodic in this case. In either case, the Markov chain is ergodic, so it has a unique stationary distribution, which must be
q_{d,r}=q^{*}
. Thus, the law of the first coordinate of
\kappa
is
q_{d,r}
. Similarly, the law of the second coordinate of
\kappa
is
q_{d,s}
and the law of the third coordinate of
\kappa
is
q_{d,t}
.

We are now ready to prove the joint mixability of
U(L_{d}(r)),U(L_{d}(s)),U(L_{d}(t))
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
for
d\geq 3
.

Let
\kappa
be as above. Sample
(i,j,k)
from
\kappa
, then
i
has the law
q_{d,r}
,
j
has the law
q_{d,s}
and
k
has the law
q_{d,t}
. By our induction hypothesis, there exists a coupling
(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y},\tilde{Z})
on
L_{d-1}(r-i)\times L_{d-1}(s-j)\times L_{d-1}(t-k)
with
\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y}+\tilde{Z}=n\mathbf{1}_{d-1}
, where
\tilde{X},\tilde{Y},\tilde{Z}
are uniform on their respective sets. Set

X=(i,\tilde{X}),\ Y=(j,\tilde{Y}),\ Z=(k,\tilde{Z}).

We claim that this gives the required coupling. Indeed,
i+j+k=n
and
\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y}+\tilde{Z}=n\mathbf{1}_{d-1}
, so
X+Y+Z=n\mathbf{1}_{d}
. It suffices to verify that
X\sim U(L_{d}(r))
, since the argument for
Y,Z
is identical. By definition, the law of the first coordinate of
U(L_{d}(r))
is
q_{d,r}
, which is the same as the law of
i
. Conditioned on
i
, the law of the remaining coordinates of
X
is uniform on
L_{d-1}(r-i)
, which is the same for
U(L_{d}(r))
. Therefore,
X
is uniform on
L_{d}(r)
.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

For
d\geq 1
and
r,s
nonnegative integers with
r+s\leq dn
, there exists a coupling
(X,Y,Z)
on
L_{d}(r)\times L_{d}(s)\times L_{d}(r+s)
with
X+Y=Z
, with marginals
X\sim U(L_{d}(r))
and
Y\sim U(L_{d}(s))
and
Z\sim U(L_{d}(r+s))
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
1.5
by taking
t=dn-r-s
, and observing that if
Z^{\prime}\sim U(L_{d}(t))
, then
Z=n\mathbf{1}-Z^{\prime}\sim U(L_{d}(r+s))
.
∎

3
Slice estimates

Recall that
u_{d}^{*}
is the value of
u
that maximizes
\Vol(S_{d}(u))
. Let
L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(r):=\left\{x\in[0,1]^{d}\;\middle|\;\sum_{i}x_{i}=r\right\}
, the
r
-slice of the hypercube
[0,1]^{d}
, whose volume is normalised so that
\int_{0}^{d}\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(r))dr=\Vol_{d}([0,1]^{d})=1
.
u_{d}^{*}
is a root of the derivative of
\Vol(S_{d}(u))
, which is

\frac{d}{du}\Vol(S_{d}(u))=\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(u))-2\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(2u)).

Thus
\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(u_{d}^{*}))=2\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(2u_{d}^{*}))
. Note that there is a unique solution to the above equation in
(0,d/2)
. Indeed, there are no solutions for
u\in(0,d/4]
since
\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(2u))>\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(u))
. For
u\in(d/4,d/2)
,
\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(u))
is strictly increasing while
\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(2u))
is strictly decreasing. Checking the endpoints, we see that there is a unique solution
u_{d}^{*}\in(d/4,d/2)
.

Let
f_{d}(x)=\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(x))
, then
f_{d}
is the probability density function of the sum of
d
independent uniform
[0,1]
random variables. In particular,
f_{d}
is supported on
[0,d]
, is symmetric about
d/2
and has a unique maximum at
d/2
. The main estimate we need is the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

The following inequalities hold for
d\geq 5
.

1.

f_{d-1}(2u_{d}^{*}-1)\geq 2.01f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*}-1).

2.

0.99f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*})\geq 2f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*}-1)+4f_{d-1}(2u_{d}^{*}).

3.1
Heuristics

Let us first give some non-rigorous heuristics justifying the above inequalities for large
d
, and also obtain estimates for
u_{d}^{*}
. Recall that
u_{d}^{*}
is the unique solution to
f_{d}(u)=2f_{d}(2u)
, for
u\in[0,d/2]
. Since
f_{d}(d/3)=f_{d}(2d/3)
, we expect
u_{d}^{*}
to be close to
d/3
.

Using large deviation estimates, we can estimate the behaviour of
f_{d}
near
d/3
. Let
X
be a uniform random variable on
[0,1]
. Let
M(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]=\frac{e^{\lambda}-1}{\lambda}
be the moment generating function of
X
, and let
K(\lambda)=\log M(\lambda)
be the cumulant generating function of
X
. Let
K^{*}(x)=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}(\lambda x-K(\lambda))
, the Fenchel–Legendre transform of
K
. Then by standard large deviation estimates (which can be proven using Cramer’s theorem
[
3
, Theorem 2.2.3]
and log-concavity), for fixed
x\in[0,1]
, we have

\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{d}\log f_{d}(xd)=-K^{*}(x).

To continue with our heuristics, let us assume that

\log f_{d}(xd)=-dK^{*}(x).

(5)

To analyse the behaviour of
f_{d}
near
d/3
, let
\sigma\approx-2.149126...
be the unique solution to
K^{\prime}(\sigma)=1/3
, then
K^{*}(1/3)=\sigma/3-K(\sigma)
and
(K^{*})^{\prime}(1/3)=\sigma
. Differentiating (
5
) with respect to
x
at
x=1/3
, we have

\frac{f_{d}^{\prime}(d/3)}{f_{d}(d/3)}=-\sigma,

that is to say, for small (constant-sized)
z
, we have
f_{d}(d/3+z)\approx f_{d}(d/3)e^{-\sigma z}
. Writing
u_{d}^{*}=d/3+\alpha
and using
f_{d}(u_{d}^{*})=2f_{d}(2u_{d}^{*})
, we have

e^{-\sigma\alpha}\approx 2e^{2\sigma\alpha},

giving
\alpha\approx-\log 2/(3\sigma)\approx 0.107508
. Now we have the following estimates.

\displaystyle f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*})

\displaystyle=f_{d-1}(d/3+\alpha)\approx f_{d-1}(d/3)e^{-\sigma\alpha}\approx 1.260f_{d-1}(d/3),

\displaystyle f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*}-1)

\displaystyle=f_{d-1}(d/3+\alpha-1)\approx f_{d-1}(d/3)e^{-\sigma(\alpha-1)}\approx 0.147f_{d-1}(d/3),

\displaystyle f_{d-1}(2u_{d}^{*}-1)

\displaystyle=f_{d-1}(d/3-2\alpha)\approx f_{d-1}(d/3)e^{2\sigma\alpha}\approx 0.630f_{d-1}(d/3),

\displaystyle f_{d-1}(2u_{d}^{*})

\displaystyle=f_{d-1}(d/3-2\alpha-1)\approx f_{d-1}(d/3)e^{\sigma(2\alpha+1)}\approx 0.0734f_{d-1}(d/3).

These estimates do indeed satisfy the lemma with plenty of room to spare. We also have the estimate
u_{d}^{*}\approx d/3+0.107508
.

3.2
Log-concavity

To make the above estimates rigorous, we will exploit the log-concavity of
f_{d}
in order to prove explicit bounds on
f_{d}^{\prime}(x)/f_{d}(x)
near
d/3
.

Definition 3.2
.

A distribution on
\mathbb{R}
with probability density function
f:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is
log-concave
if
\log f
is a concave function.

Fact 3.3
.

If
X,Y
are independent log-concave random variables, then
X+Y
is also log-concave.

We will need the following bound on the location of the mode of a unimodal distribution in terms of its mean and variance.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a random variable on
\mathbb{R}
with probability density function
f
. Suppose
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu,\Var(X)=\sigma^{2}
, and
a\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
f
is increasing on
(-\infty,a)
and decreasing on
(a,\infty)
. Then

|a-\mu|\leq\sqrt{3}\sigma.

Proof.

By scaling and translating
X
, we may assume that
\mu=0
and
\sigma=1
. By symmetry, we may further assume that
a\geq 0
, and our aim is to show that
a\leq\sqrt{3}
. Consider the magic polynomial

g(x)=(x+a)(x-a/3).

Then
g(x)\leq 0
for
x\in[-a,a/3]
and
g(x)>0
everywhere else. The main property of
g
is that

\int_{-a}^{a}g(x)dx=0.

Using nonnegativity and unimodality of
f
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(X)]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)f(x)dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-a}^{a/3}g(x)f(x)dx+\int_{a/3}^{a}g(x)f(x)dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-a}^{a/3}g(x)f(a/3)dx+\int_{a/3}^{a}g(x)f(a/3)dx

\displaystyle=f(a/3)\int_{-a}^{a}g(x)dx=0.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}+(2a/3)X-a^{2}/3]=\sigma^{2}-a^{2}/3.

This gives
a\leq\sqrt{3}\sigma
, as required.
∎

Remark
.

The constant
\sqrt{3}
is tight, as witnessed by the uniform distribution on
[-1,1]
, which has mean
0
, variance
1/3
, and
a=1
.

3.3
Proof of the slice estimates

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Let
X
be the uniform random variable on
[0,1]
, then
f_{d}
is the probability density function of the sum of
d
independent copies of
X
. Since the probability density function of
X
is the indicator function on
[0,1]
, which is log-concave,
f_{d}
is also log-concave by Fact
3.3
. In particular,
f_{d}^{\prime}(x)/f_{d}(x)
is a decreasing function of
x
. Let
M(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]=\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}
be the moment generating function of
X
.

For
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, let
X_{\sigma}
be the exponential tilt of
X
, which is the random variable with probability density function

f(x;\sigma)=\frac{e^{\sigma x}}{M(\sigma)}f(x).

This is indeed a probability density function since

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x;\sigma)dx=\frac{1}{M(\sigma)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\sigma x}f(x)dx=1.

Let us compute the mean and variance of
X_{\sigma}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\sigma}]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xf(x;\sigma)dx=\frac{1}{M(\sigma)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xe^{\sigma x}f(x)dx=\frac{M^{\prime}(\sigma)}{M(\sigma)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{\sigma}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{2}f(x;\sigma)dx=\frac{1}{M(\sigma)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{2}e^{\sigma x}f(x)dx=\frac{M^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)}{M(\sigma)}.

Therefore,

\Var(X_{\sigma})=\mathbb{E}[X_{\sigma}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{\sigma}]^{2}=\frac{M^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)}{M(\sigma)}-\left(\frac{M^{\prime}(\sigma)}{M(\sigma)}\right)^{2}.

Let
Y_{\sigma}
be the sum of
d
independent copies of
X_{\sigma}
, and let
f_{d}(\cdot;\sigma)
be the probability density function of
Y_{\sigma}
. Since
f(\cdot;\sigma)
is log-concave,
f_{d}(\cdot;\sigma)
is also log-concave by Fact
3.3
.

Claim 3.5
.

We have

f_{d}(x;\sigma)=\frac{e^{\sigma x}}{M(\sigma)^{d}}f_{d}(x).

Proof.

We argue by induction on
d
. For
d=1
, this is true by definition. For
d\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle f_{d}(x;\sigma)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{d-1}(x-y;\sigma)f(y;\sigma)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{\sigma(x-y)}}{M(\sigma)^{d-1}}f_{d-1}(x-y)\cdot\frac{e^{\sigma y}}{M(\sigma)}f(y)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\sigma x}}{M(\sigma)^{d}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{d-1}(x-y)f(y)dy=\frac{e^{\sigma x}}{M(\sigma)^{d}}f_{d}(x).

∎

We would like to show that
f_{d}^{\prime}(x)/f_{d}(x)
is approximately
-\sigma^{*}
for
x
close to
d/3
, where
\sigma^{*}=-2.149...
is the unique solution to
M^{\prime}(\sigma^{*})/M(\sigma^{*})=1/3
. By log-concavity,
f_{d}^{\prime}/f_{d}
is decreasing in
x
. We will show a lower bound on
f_{d}^{\prime}(x)/f_{d}(x)
for
x
slightly above
d/3
, and an upper bound for
x
slightly below
d/3
. For our application, we are aiming for the lower and upper bounds of
\sigma_{1}:=-3
and
\sigma_{2}:=-1.6
respectively.

Let
\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}[X_{\sigma_{i}}]
and
s_{i}^{2}=\Var(X_{\sigma_{i}})
for
i=1,2
. We have the following approximate values:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle\approx 0.280938,\quad s_{1}\approx 0.236580,

\displaystyle\mu_{2}

\displaystyle\approx 0.372030,\quad s_{2}\approx 0.271405.

We have
\mathbb{E}[Y_{\sigma_{i}}]=d\mu_{i}
and
\Var(Y_{\sigma_{i}})=ds_{i}^{2}
. Since
f_{d}(\cdot;\sigma_{i})
is log-concave, it is unimodal, so by Lemma
3.4
, we have
f_{d}^{\prime}(x;\sigma_{i})\leq 0
for
x\geq d\mu_{i}+\sqrt{3d}s_{i}
, and
f_{d}^{\prime}(x;\sigma_{i})\geq 0
for
x\leq d\mu_{i}-\sqrt{3d}s_{i}
. Therefore, for
x\geq d\mu_{i}+\sqrt{3d}s_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq f_{d}^{\prime}(x;\sigma_{i})=\frac{e^{\sigma_{i}x}}{M(\sigma_{i})^{d}}(\sigma_{i}f_{d}(x)+f_{d}^{\prime}(x))

\displaystyle\implies\frac{f_{d}^{\prime}(x)}{f_{d}(x)}

\displaystyle\leq-\sigma_{i}.

Similarly,
f_{d}^{\prime}(x)/f_{d}(x)\geq-\sigma_{i}
for
x\leq d\mu_{i}-\sqrt{3d}s_{i}
. Thus, we have

-\sigma_{2}\leq\frac{f_{d}^{\prime}(x)}{f_{d}(x)}\leq-\sigma_{1}

for
x\in[d\mu_{1}+\sqrt{3d}s_{1},d\mu_{2}-\sqrt{3d}s_{2}]
. In particular, this implies that for
d\mu_{1}+\sqrt{3d}s_{1}\leq x\leq y\leq d\mu_{2}-\sqrt{3d}s_{2}
, we have

e^{-\sigma_{2}(y-x)}\leq\frac{f_{d}(y)}{f_{d}(x)}\leq e^{-\sigma_{1}(y-x)}.

(6)

For
d\geq 200
, the interval
[d\mu_{1}+\sqrt{3d}s_{1},d\mu_{2}-\sqrt{3d}s_{2}]
contains
[d/3-1,d/3+1]
, so (
6
) holds for all
x,y\in[d/3-1,d/3+1]
. For
5\leq d<200
, we directly verify the lemma by numerically computing the relevant quantities, see Appendix
A
.

We now estimate the value of
u_{d}^{*}
. Let
u_{d}^{*}=d/3+\alpha
, then
f_{d}(u_{d}^{*})=2f_{d}(2u_{d}^{*})
implies that

\frac{f_{d}(d/3+\alpha)}{f_{d}(2d/3+2\alpha)}=\frac{f_{d}(d/3+\alpha)}{f_{d}(d/3-2\alpha)}=2.

(
6
) then gives

\displaystyle e^{-3\sigma_{2}\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq 2\leq e^{-3\sigma_{1}\alpha}

\displaystyle\implies 0.077016

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\leq 0.144406.

Note that a priori (
6
) may not apply since we do not know the value of
\alpha
yet. However, the above estimates show that
f_{d}(d/3+0.5)/f_{d}(d/3-1)>2
. Then, the uniqueness of
\alpha
implies that
\alpha<0.5
, so (
6
) does apply.

Recall that the lemma is the following two inequalities.

1.

f_{d-1}(2u_{d}^{*}-1)\geq 2.01f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*}-1).

2.

0.99f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*})\geq 2f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*}-1)+4f_{d-1}(2u_{d}^{*}).

In our computations below, we only evaluate
f_{d-1}(x)
for
x\in[(d-1)/3-1,(d-1)/3+1]
, so (
6
) holds.

For (1), we have

\displaystyle\frac{f_{d-1}(2u_{d}^{*}-1)}{f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*}-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{d-1}(2d/3+2\alpha-1)}{f_{d-1}(d/3+\alpha-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{d-1}(d/3-2\alpha)}{f_{d-1}(d/3+\alpha-1)}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\sigma_{2}(1-3\alpha)}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\sigma_{2}}/2\approx 4.953032/2>2.01.

For (2), we have

\displaystyle\frac{2f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*}-1)}{f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*})}+\frac{4f_{d-1}(2u_{d}^{*})}{f_{d-1}(u_{d}^{*})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2f_{d-1}(d/3+\alpha-1)}{f_{d-1}(d/3+\alpha)}+\frac{4f_{d-1}(2d/3+2\alpha)}{f_{d-1}(d/3+\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2f_{d-1}(d/3+\alpha-1)}{f_{d-1}(d/3+\alpha)}+\frac{4f_{d-1}(d/3-2\alpha-1)}{f_{d-1}(d/3+\alpha)}

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{\sigma_{2}}+4e^{\sigma_{2}(3\alpha+1)}

\displaystyle\leq 0.961800<0.99.

∎

4
Weights for the dual program

Let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
be the projection that forgets the last coordinate. For a subset
S\subseteq[n+1]^{d}
and a point
x\in[n+1]^{d-1}
, define the fiber
S(x):=\left\{y\in[n+1]\;\middle|\;(x,y)\in S\right\}
. We begin with the following key observation from Lepsveridze and Sun
[
8
]
.

Lemma 4.1

(
[
8
, Lemma 2.3]
)
.

Let
S\subseteq[n+1]^{d}
be sum-free. Then for every
x,y,z\in[n+1]^{d-1}
with
x+y=z
, we have

|S(x)|+|S(y)|+|S(z)|\leq 2(n+1).

(7)

Finding an upper bound for the size of a sum-free set
S
then amounts to solving the linear program

maximize

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in[n+1]^{d-1}}|S(x)|

subject to

\displaystyle|S(x)|+|S(y)|+|S(z)|\leq 2(n+1)\text{ for all }x,y,z\in[n+1]^{d-1}\text{ with }x+y=z,

\displaystyle 0\leq|S(x)|\leq n+1\text{ for all }x\in[n+1]^{d-1}.

An upper bound for the optimal value is given by a feasible solution to the dual linear program. We state this as follows for motivation, but it is not used in the proof:

minimize

\displaystyle 2(n+1)\sum_{x,y,z}w(x,y,z)+(n+1)\sum_{x}b(x)

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{y,z:x+y=z}w(x,y,z)+\sum_{y,z:y+x=z}w(y,x,z)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{y,z:y+z=x}w(y,z,x)+b(x)\geq 1\text{ for all }x\in[n+1]^{d-1},

\displaystyle w(x,y,z)\geq 0\text{ for all }x,y,z\in[n+1]^{d-1}\text{ with }x+y=z,

\displaystyle b(x)\geq 0\text{ for all }x\in[n+1]^{d-1}.

As observed by Lepsveridze and Sun, the existence of joint mixability of the distributions

U(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(u_{d}^{*})),\ U(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(u_{d}^{*})),\ U(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(d-2u_{d}^{*}))

gives a feasible weight function
w
for the dual program, resulting in the optimal bound for the primal program as we will see later.

Let
m=\left\lfloor u_{d}^{*}n\right\rfloor
, and consider the partition of the cube
[n+1]^{d-1}
into the following five sets.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in[n+1]^{d-1}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{1}^{T}x\in[0,m-n)\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in[n+1]^{d-1}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{1}^{T}x\in[m-n,m]\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in[n+1]^{d-1}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{1}^{T}x\in(m,2m-n)\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in[n+1]^{d-1}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{1}^{T}x\in[2m-n,2m]\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in[n+1]^{d-1}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{1}^{T}x\in(2m,(d-1)n]\right\}.

Lemma 4.2
.

The following estimates hold for sufficiently large
n
.

1.

Let
r\in[0,m-n)
and
s\in[\frac{(d-1)n}{2},2m-n)
. Then
|L_{d-1}(s)|\geq 2|L_{d-1}(r)|
.

2.

Let
r\in[0,m-n)
,
s\in(m,\frac{(d-1)n}{2}]
, and
t\in(2m,(d-1)n]
. Then
|L_{d-1}(s)|\geq 2|L_{d-1}(r)|+4|L_{d-1}(t)|
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
and unimodality of
f_{d-1}
, we have for
r\in[0,m-n)
and
s\in[\frac{(d-1)n}{2},2m-n)
that

\displaystyle\Vol_{d-2}(L_{d-1}^{\mathbb{R}}(s/n))

\displaystyle\geq\Vol_{d-2}(L_{d-1}^{\mathbb{R}}(2u_{d}^{*}-1))

\displaystyle\geq 2.01\Vol_{d-2}(L_{d-1}^{\mathbb{R}}(u_{d}^{*}-1))

\displaystyle\geq 2.01\Vol_{d-2}(L_{d-1}^{\mathbb{R}}(r/n)).

Since
|L_{d-1}(x)|=n^{d-2}\Vol_{d-2}(L_{d-1}^{\mathbb{R}}(x/n))+O(n^{d-3})
, this implies (1) for sufficiently large
n
. A similar argument gives (2).
∎

Let
\Omega:=\left\{(x,y,z)\in(\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{D})\cup(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C})\cup(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{E})\;\middle|\;x+y=z\right\}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists a weight function
w(x,y,z)
supported on
\Omega
satisfying the following. For
x\in[n+1]^{d-1}
, let
W(x):=\sum_{y,z}w(x,y,z)+\sum_{y,z}w(y,x,z)+\sum_{y,z}w(y,z,x)
.

1.

w(x,y,z)\geq 0
for all
(x,y,z)\in\Omega
.

2.

For every
x\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
W(x)\leq 1
.

3.

We have

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{E}}|W(x)-1|=O(n^{d-2}).

Proof.

We will split
w
into three parts
w=w_{1}+w_{2}+w_{3}
, where
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
are supported on
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
, and
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{E}
respectively. Analogously define
W_{1},W_{2},W_{3}
for
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
.

Construction of
w_{1}
. By Corollary
2.4
, there exists a coupling
(X_{1},Y_{1},Z_{1})
on
L_{d}(m)\times L_{d}(m)\times L_{d}(2m)
with
X_{1}+Y_{1}=Z_{1}
, with marginals
X_{1}\sim U(L_{d}(m))
,
Y_{1}\sim U(L_{d}(m))
, and
Z_{1}\sim U(L_{d}(2m))
. Under the map
\pi
, we obtain a coupling
(\pi(X_{1}),\pi(Y_{1}),\pi(Z_{1}))
on
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{D}
with
\pi(X_{1})+\pi(Y_{1})=\pi(Z_{1})
, where the laws of
\pi(X_{1})
and
\pi(Y_{1})
are uniform on
\mathcal{B}
, and the law of
\pi(Z_{1})
is uniform on
\mathcal{D}
.

Recall that
\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(u_{d}^{*}))=2\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(2u_{d}^{*}))
. Thus, since
m=\left\lfloor u_{d}^{*}n\right\rfloor
, we have

|L_{d}(m)|=n^{d-1}\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(u_{d}^{*}))+O(n^{d-2}),\quad|L_{d}(2m)|=n^{d-1}\Vol_{d-1}(L_{d}^{\mathbb{R}}(2u_{d}^{*}))+O(n^{d-2}).

Hence, we have
|\mathcal{B}|=|L_{d}(m)|=2|L_{d}(2m)|+O(n^{d-2})=2|\mathcal{D}|+O(n^{d-2})
. Define
w_{1}
by

w_{1}(x,y,z):=|\mathcal{D}|\cdot\mathbb{P}((\pi(X_{1}),\pi(Y_{1}),\pi(Z_{1}))=(x,y,z)).

Since
\pi(X_{1}),\pi(Y_{1})
are uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{B}
, we have
W_{1}(x)=2|\mathcal{D}|/|\mathcal{B}|=1+O(n^{-1})
for
x\in\mathcal{B}
, since
|\mathcal{B}|=\Theta(n^{d-1})
. Since
\pi(Z_{1})
is uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{D}
, we have
W_{1}(x)=|\mathcal{D}|/|\mathcal{D}|=1
for
x\in\mathcal{D}
. Furthermore,
W_{1}(x)=0
everywhere else.

Construction of
w_{2}
. Let
1\leq r<m-n-1
and

s=\left\lfloor\frac{3m-n-r}{2}\right\rfloor,\quad t=r+s.

In other words, as
r
ranges from
m-n-2
down to
1
,
s
ranges from
m+1
to
m+\left\lfloor\frac{m-n-1}{2}\right\rfloor
with each value attained at most twice, and
t
ranges from
2m-n-1
down to
m+\left\lfloor\frac{m-n+1}{2}\right\rfloor
with each value attained at most twice. Therefore, each value in
[m+1,2m-n-1]
is attained at most twice by
s,t
combined.

By Corollary
2.4
, for each
r
, there exists a coupling
(X_{2}^{(r)},Y_{2}^{(r)},Z_{2}^{(r)})
on
L_{d-1}(r)\times L_{d-1}(s)\times L_{d-1}(t)
with
X_{2}^{(r)}+Y_{2}^{(r)}=Z_{2}^{(r)}
, with uniform marginals. Define
w_{2}
by

w_{2}(x,y,z):=|L_{d-1}(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)|\cdot\mathbb{P}((X_{2}^{(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)},Y_{2}^{(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)},Z_{2}^{(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)})=(x,y,z)),

which is supported on
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
. For
x\in\mathcal{A}
with
0<\mathbf{1}^{T}x<m-n-1
, we have

W_{2}(x)=|L_{d-1}(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)|\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{2}^{(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)}=x)=1.

For
x\in\mathcal{C}
,
\mathbf{1}^{T}x
is attained by
s
or
t
at most twice. So there are at most two values of
r
, say
r_{1},r_{2}
, such that
x
is in the support of
Y_{2}^{(r_{i})}
or
Z_{2}^{(r_{i})}
. For each such
r_{i}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(Y_{2}^{(r_{i})}=x)
or
\mathbb{P}(Z_{2}^{(r_{i})}=x)=1/|L_{d-1}(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)|
. Thus, for
x\in\mathcal{C}
, we have

W_{2}(x)=\sum_{i}\frac{|L_{d-1}(r_{i})|}{|L_{d-1}(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)|}.

Construction of
w_{3}
. This is similar to the construction of
w_{2}
. Let
2m+2\leq t\leq(d-1)n
and

r=\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor,\quad s=t-r=\left\lceil\frac{t}{2}\right\rceil.

In other words, as
t
ranges from
2m+2
to
(d-1)n
,
r
ranges from
m+1
to
\left\lfloor(d-1)n/2\right\rfloor
with each value attained at most twice, and
s
ranges from
m+1
to
\left\lceil(d-1)n/2\right\rceil
with each value attained at most twice. Therefore, each value in
[m+1,\left\lceil(d-1)n/2\right\rceil]
is attained at most four times by
r,s
combined. We will assume
n
is even for simplicity.

By Corollary
2.4
, for each
t
, there exists a coupling
(X_{3}^{(t)},Y_{3}^{(t)},Z_{3}^{(t)})
on
L_{d-1}(r)\times L_{d-1}(s)\times L_{d-1}(t)
with
X_{3}^{(t)}+Y_{3}^{(t)}=Z_{3}^{(t)}
, with uniform marginals. Define
w_{3}
by

w_{3}(x,y,z):=|L_{d-1}(\mathbf{1}^{T}z)|\cdot\mathbb{P}((X_{3}^{(\mathbf{1}^{T}z)},Y_{3}^{(\mathbf{1}^{T}z)},Z_{3}^{(\mathbf{1}^{T}z)})=(x,y,z)).

Split
\mathcal{C}
into two parts

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in\mathcal{C}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{1}^{T}x\in(m,\tfrac{(d-1)n}{2}]\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in\mathcal{C}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{1}^{T}x\in(\tfrac{(d-1)n}{2},2m-n)\right\}.

w_{3}
is supported on
\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{E}
. For
x\in\mathcal{E}
with
\mathbf{1}^{T}x>2m+1
, we have

W_{3}(x)=|L_{d-1}(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)|\cdot\mathbb{P}(Z_{3}^{(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)}=x)=1.

For
x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\mathbf{1}^{T}x
is attained by
r
or
s
at most four times, say when

. Thus, for

W_{3}(x)=\sum_{i}\frac{|L_{d-1}(t_{i})|}{|L_{d-1}(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)|}.

w=w_{1}+w_{2}+w_{3}
and

. Clearly

\Omega

For
x\in\mathcal{A}
with
0<\mathbf{1}^{T}x<m-n-1

. If

or

, then

.

x\in\mathcal{B}
, we have
W(x)=W_{1}(x)=1+O(n^{-1})
.

For
x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, we have

W(x)=W_{2}(x)+W_{3}(x)=\frac{\sum_{i}|L_{d-1}(r_{i})|+\sum_{i}|L_{d-1}(t_{i})|}{|L_{d-1}(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)|}\leq 1

(2), since there are at most two different

, each in the range
(1,m-n-1)
, and there are at most four different

, each in the range
(m+1,(d-1)n]
.

For
x\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
, we have

W(x)=W_{2}(x)=\frac{\sum_{i}|L_{d-1}(r_{i})|}{|L_{d-1}(\mathbf{1}^{T}x)|}\leq 1

by Lemma
4.2
(1). This verifies condition (2) of the lemma.

For
x\in\mathcal{D}
, we have
W(x)=W_{1}(x)=1
.

For
x\in\mathcal{E}
with
\mathbf{1}^{T}x>2m+1
, we have
W(x)=W_{3}(x)=1
. If
\mathbf{1}^{T}x=2m+1
, then
W(x)=0
. Putting all together, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{E}}|W(x)-1|

\displaystyle=O(n^{-1})|\mathcal{B}|+\sum_{x:\mathbf{1}^{T}x=0,m-n-1}1+\sum_{x:\mathbf{1}^{T}x=2m+1}1

\displaystyle=O(n^{d-2})+|L_{d-1}(0)|+|L_{d-1}(m-n-1)|+|L_{d-1}(2m+1)|

\displaystyle=O(n^{d-2}),

verifying condition (3) in the lemma as required.
∎

Let
S^{*}:=\left\{x\in[n+1]^{d}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{1}^{T}x\in[u_{d}^{*}n,2u_{d}^{*}n)\right\}
. Then
S^{*}
is sum-free, and
|S^{*}|=c_{d}^{*}n^{d}+O(n^{d-1})
. It was observed in
[
8
]
that for
S^{*}
, the inequality (
7
) is essentially tight for every
(x,y,z)\in\Omega
.

Lemma 4.4

(
[
8
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

For every
(x,y,z)\in\Omega
, we have

|S^{*}(x)|+|S^{*}(y)|+|S^{*}(z)|\geq 2(n+1)-1.

Furthermore, for every
x\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
|S^{*}(x)|=n+1
.

With our weight function
w
, the proof of Theorem
1.1
is identical to that of
[
8
]
, which we rephrase here.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
S\subseteq[n+1]^{d}
be sum-free. By Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
4.4
, we have

|S(x)|+|S(y)|+|S(z)|-(|S^{*}(x)|+|S^{*}(y)|+|S^{*}(z)|)\leq 1

(8)

for every
(x,y,z)\in\Omega
. Furthermore, since
S^{*}(x)=n+1
for all
x\in\mathcal{C}
, we have

|S(x)|-|S^{*}(x)|\leq 0\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{C}.

(9)

Let
w
and
W
be as in Lemma
4.3
. Taking
w(x,y,z)
times (
8
) over all
(x,y,z)\in\Omega
and taking
1-W(x)
times (
9
) over all
x\in\mathcal{C}
and summing, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in[n+1]^{d-1}}W(x)(|S(x)|-|S^{*}(x)|)+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}(1-W(x))(|S(x)|-|S^{*}(x)|)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{3}\sum_{x\in[n+1]^{d-1}}W(x)

\displaystyle=O(n^{d-1}).

The left-hand side is equal to

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{E}}W(x)(|S(x)|-|S^{*}(x)|)+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}(|S(x)|-|S^{*}(x)|).

By Lemma
4.3
, the first sum is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{E}}(|S(x)|-|S^{*}(x)|)+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{E}}|W(x)-1|\cdot\left\lvert|S(x)|-|S^{*}(x)|\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{E}}(|S(x)|-|S^{*}(x)|)+O(n^{d-2})\cdot O(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{E}}(|S(x)|-|S^{*}(x)|)+O(n^{d-1}).

Putting all together, we have

\sum_{x\in[n+1]^{d-1}}|S(x)|-|S^{*}(x)|\leq O(n^{d-1}),

which implies that
|S|=\sum_{x}|S(x)|\leq\sum_{x}|S^{*}(x)|+O(n^{d-1})=c_{d}^{*}n^{d}+O(n^{d-1})
, as required.
∎

5
Sum-free subsets of other convex shapes

Theorem
1.1
implies that the largest sum-free subset of the hypercube
[0,1]^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is of the form
\left\{x\in[0,1]^{d}\;\middle|\;1\leq L(x)<2\right\}
, for some linear functional
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. More generally, subsets of the form

\left\{x\in[0,1]^{d}\;\middle|\;\left\lfloor L(x)\right\rfloor\equiv 1\pmod{3}\right\}

are also sum-free. It is natural to ask whether such subsets are optimal for other convex shapes besides the hypercube. It does not hold in full generality, at least for large
d
, as shown by the following example.

Proposition 5.1
.

For sufficiently large
d
, there exist a bounded convex set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
not containing the origin and a sum-free subset
S\subseteq K
such that for any linear functional
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\Vol(S)>\Vol(\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;\left\lfloor L(x)\right\rfloor\equiv 1\pmod{3}\right\}).

Proof sketch.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small. Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
be a regular
(d-1)
-dimensional simplex of volume 1, centred at the origin. Let
P_{1}
be a copy of
P
placed on the hyperplane
\left\{x_{1}=1-\varepsilon\right\}
, and let
P_{2}
be a translate of
-P_{1}
, placed on the hyperplane
\left\{x_{1}=2+\varepsilon\right\}
. Let
K
be the convex hull of
P_{1}\cup P_{2}
, then
K
is a bounded convex set not containing the origin, and let
S=K\setminus 2K
, which is sum-free.

Let
L
be a linear functional. Observe that the sum-free subset
\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;1\leq x_{1}<2\right\}
has volume
(1-O_{d}(\varepsilon))\Vol(K)
. Thus, for the subset corresponding to
L
to beat this, it suffices to consider the set
T=\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;1\leq L(x)<2\right\}
bounded by a single strip. Indeed, if
K
intersects more than one of the strips in
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;\middle|\;\left\lfloor L(x)\right\rfloor\equiv 1\pmod{3}\right\}
, say it intersects
\left\{1\leq L(x)<2\right\}
and
\left\{4\leq L(x)<5\right\}
, then from the convexity of
K
, there exists a constant
c_{d}>0
such that

\Vol(\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;2\leq L(x)<4\right\})\geq c_{d}\Vol(\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;1\leq L(x)<2\text{ or }4\leq L(x)<5\right\}).

It follows then that
\Vol(\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;\left\lfloor L(x)\right\rfloor\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}\right\})\geq c_{d}\Vol(\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;\left\lfloor L(x)\right\rfloor\equiv 1\pmod{3}\right\})
.

We will show that
\Vol(S)>\Vol(T)
, or equivalently,
\Vol(K\setminus S)<\Vol(K\setminus T)
. On one hand,
K\setminus S=K\cap 2K
, whose volume is approximately
2\varepsilon\Vol(P\cap(-2P))
for small
\varepsilon
. It can be shown that
\Vol(P\cap(-2P))
is exponentially small in
d
.

On the other hand, suppose without loss of generality that
L(x)=a_{1}x_{1}+a_{2}x_{2}
, with
a_{1},a_{2}\geq 0
. If
a_{1}\leq 1
, then
K\setminus T
contains the set
\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;L(x)<1\right\}\supseteq\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;x_{1}<1,x_{2}<0\right\}
. The volume of the latter set is approximately
\varepsilon\Vol(\left\{x\in P_{1}\;\middle|\;x_{2}<0\right\})
. Grünbaum’s inequality
[
6
]
says that

\Vol(\left\{x\in P_{1}\;\middle|\;x_{2}<0\right\})\geq\left(\frac{d-1}{d}\right)^{d-1}\Vol(P_{1})>c\Vol(P_{1})

for some absolute constant
c>0
, for any convex body
P_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
with centre of mass at the origin. If
a_{1}>1
, then
K\setminus T
contains the set
\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;L(x)\geq 2\right\}\supseteq\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;x_{1}>2,x_{2}>0\right\}
, whose volume is approximately
\varepsilon\Vol(\left\{x\in P_{2}\;\middle|\;x_{2}>0\right\})>c\varepsilon
. In either case, we have
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Vol(K\setminus T)>c
is absolutely bounded from below, while
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Vol(K\setminus S)
is exponentially small in
d
. Thus,
\Vol(K\setminus S)<\Vol(K\setminus T)
for sufficiently large
d
.
∎

Nevertheless, it is possible that this holds for
d=2
, even just considering the first strip
1\leq L(x)<2
.

Problem 5.2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded convex set not containing the origin. Is it true that the largest sum-free subset of
K
is of the form
\left\{x\in K\;\middle|\;1\leq L(x)<2\right\}
for some linear functional
L:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
?
