The effect of GIP and GLP-2 on bone turnover in children with cerebral palsy or spinal muscular atrophy
Authors: Charlotte B Christiansen, Kirsa Skov-Jeppesen, Marie M Naume, Sille Myrup, Astrid Høj, Lærke S Gasbjerg, Jens J Holst, Christina E Hoei-Hansen, Mette C Ørngreen, Mette M Rosenkilde, Bolette Hartmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren with cerebral palsy (CP) and spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) are at high risk of low bone mineral density, fractures, and low muscle mass. The gut-derived hormones glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) reduce bone resorption in adults, but their effects in pediatric populations with CP and SMA are unknown.MethodsIn this preliminary study, 3 children (1 CP and 2 SMA) underwent 3 interventions: (1) oral liquid mixed-meal ingestion, (2) subcutaneous GIP+GLP-2 administration, and (3) placebo. Plasma levels of GIP, GLP-2, CTX (bone resorption), P1NP (bone formation), and parathyroid hormone (PTH) were measured over 240 minutes.ResultsThe liquid mixed-meal significantly reduced CTX levels, indicating suppressed bone resorption, while exogenous GIP+GLP-2 administration had no significant effect on CTX or P1NP. PTH levels decreased after GIP+GLP-2 administration but were unaffected by the liquid mixed-meal.ConclusionIn these studies, exogenous GIP and GLP-2 unexpectedly did not modulate bone turnover in our children with CP or SMA. The study highlights the need for age- and disease-specific investigations into the gut–bone axis in pediatric populations.

Cerebral palsy (CP) and spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) are 2 distinct neurological and neuromuscular disorders that significantly affect motor function in children. CP is a systemic neurological disease caused by early brain injury, while SMA is a genetic neuromuscular disorder affecting motor neurons (1, 2). Complications including low muscle mass, reduced mobility, and low bone mineral density (BMD) are seen in both conditions, placing them at increased risk of fractures (3-5). Fractures may result in significant pain, complete immobility, and progressive functional decline, underscoring the importance of proactive strategies to support skeletal health in these individuals (6, 7).

In healthy children, bone remodeling is a dynamic and essential process that not only facilitates growth but also shapes developing bones and repairs skeletal microdamage. Unlike in adults, where remodeling is slower and primarily serves to maintain bone integrity through a balance between osteoclastic resorption and osteoblastic formation, bone remodeling in children is closely linked to their rapid growth and development (8-10). Although the mechanisms are less thoroughly studied in pediatric populations, evidence suggests that bone remodeling follows a circadian rhythm in children as it does in adults (11-13). This rhythm is influenced by nutrient intake and the secretion of gut-derived hormones including glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2), which are released by enteroendocrine cells into the circulation after meals (14-17).

In adult humans, both GIP and GLP-2 have beneficial effects on bone by significantly reducing bone resorption, as reflected by up to 50% decreased circulating levels of the resorption marker carboxy-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX-I) after parenteral administration (18-26). Co-administration of the 2 hormones results in an additive reduction in CTX (27). GIP may also stimulate bone formation, as evidenced by increased levels of procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP). This anabolic effect is likely mediated via GIP receptor (GIPR) signaling in osteoblasts (21, 27).

These findings highlight the gut–bone axis as a promising therapeutic target in the management of metabolic bone diseases. However, circulating levels of GIP and GLP-2 have not yet been investigated in pediatric populations with CP or SMA, and the potential association between bone loss and altered gut hormone signaling remains unexplored. In 3 children diagnosed with CP or SMA, we examined the circulating bone remodeling markers in response to nutrient intake (expected to elicit endogenous secretion of GIP and GLP-2) as well as to subcutaneous administration of exogenous GIP and GLP-2 or placebo.

Materials and methods

Ethics

The study was approved by the Scientific Ethical Committee of the Capital Region of Denmark (H-16047626) and registered with the Danish Data Protection Agency (SUND-2017-21) and ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07254988). Oral consent was obtained from all participants, and both oral and written consent were provided by at least one legal parent prior to inclusion.

Study participants

Children (both male and female) aged 10-17 years with CP or SMA attending the Department of Paediatrics and Adolescent Medicine at Rigshospitalet were invited to participate in the study. Inclusion of children was essential, as compromised bone health in individuals with CP or SMA originates already during childhood, coinciding with the critical period of bone development. Additionally, eligible participants were required to have a disease severity that markedly impaired ambulation, necessitating wheelchair use for most of the day. Exclusion criteria included use of anti-resorptive medications, confounding illnesses, pregnancy, allergy toward the components given and Hgb < 6.5 mmol/L.

Experimental design

The study protocol comprised 3 visits, each separated by at least 1 week. On the first visit, participants underwent an oral liquid mixed-meal test (Nutricia Nutridrink (150 kcal/100 mL), 3.3 mL/kg body weight). Study days 2 and 3 were randomized to include either subcutaneous injections of GIP and GLP-2 (1 mL containing 100 µg GIP+1 mL containing 400 µg GLP-2) or placebo (2 × 1 mL isotonic saline). Participants were blinded to the order of injections (placebo or GIP+GLP-2). Randomization sequences were generated via http://www.randomization.com

Participants fasted from 11 Pm the night before each visit and arrived fasting at the research facility at the hospital the following morning. Upon arrival, they were placed in a hospital bed, and a catheter was inserted into a cubital vein for serial blood sampling. A local anesthetic cream or EMLA patch was applied prior to both intravenous catheter placement and subcutaneous injections.

Following a 20-minute rest period, baseline blood samples were collected at t = −10 and −5 minutes. At t = 0 minutes, participants received either the liquid mixed-meal or the assigned injections (administered as two bolus injections in the periumbilical region). Subsequent blood samples were drawn at t = 15, 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, and 240 minutes postadministration. At the same time points blood pressure and heart rate were monitored using a standard digital blood pressure monitor, while blood glucose was measured using a FreeStyle Precision Neo blood sugar monitor.

Peptide formulation

Human GIP(1-42) was synthesized by PolyPeptide Group (Strasbourg, France), and human GLP-2(1-33) by Bachem (Bubendorf, Switzerland), both with a purity exceeding 97%. Peptides were dissolved in a 50 mM sodium bicarbonate buffer (pH 8.5) containing 0.5% human serum albumin and 9 mg/mL NaCl. Final concentrations were 100 µg/mL for GIP and 400 µg/mL for GLP-2, prepared by the Capital Region Pharmacy (Herlev, Denmark). Vials were stored at −20 °C. The placebo consisted of isotonic saline (NaCl 9 mg/mL).

Laboratory analyses

Whole blood was collected into prechilled EDTA tubes containing in addition dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (valine pyrrolidide; final concentration 0.01 mmol/L; kindly provided by Novo Nordisk). Samples were centrifuged for 10 minutes at 4 °C to obtain plasma.

Plasma concentrations of CTX, P1NP, and parathyroid hormone (PTH) were quantified using an automated immunoassay and chemiluminescence techniques on the IDS-iSYS Multi-Discipline Automated System (CTX: Cat# IS-3000, RRID:AB_2910613; PINP: Cat# IS-4000, RRID:AB_2936077; PTH: Cat# IS-3200, RRID:AB_2935763; Immuno Diagnostic Systems, Frankfurt, Germany) (21, 28).

Plasma concentrations of total GIP and intact GLP-2 were determined using in-house radioimmunoassays, employing antibody #80867 (RRID:AB_2892194) for total GIP and antibody #92160 (RRID:AB_2943622) for intact GLP-2. Detailed assay procedures have been described previously (29, 30).

Statistical analysis

The primary endpoint of the study was CTX. CTX, P1NP, and PTH are expressed in percent of baseline (mean of t = −10 and t = −5 minutes), while GIP and GLP-2 are expressed in pmol/L. All results are presented as mean ± SEM. For analysis of CTX, P1NP, and PTH responses either the incremental or decremental area under the curve (iAUC/dAUC) (collectively referred to as the net area under the curve [netAUC]) was calculated with y = 100% as baseline. Differences in AUCs were analyzed by a 1-way ANOVA for repeated measurements using Tukey post hoc evaluation. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA, www.graphpad.com). P-values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

Peptide hormones

After subcutaneous injection of GIP and GLP-2, plasma levels increased with a peak value of 152 ± 69 pmol/L for total GIP at t = 30 minutes (from 11 ± 3 pmol/L at baseline) and a peak value of 1250 ± 208 pmol/L for intact GLP-2 at t = 60 minutes (from 20 ± 9 pmol/L at baseline). The liquid mixed-meal induced a GIP response peaking at t = 30 minutes of 70 ± 12 pmol/L from basal levels of 10 ± 2 pmol/L (Fig. 1A). During the liquid mixed-meal test, basal GLP-2 secretion was 9 ± 3 pmol/L and highest concentration was measured to 24 ± 3 pmol/L at t = 180 minutes (Fig. 1B), consistent with the more distal localization of GLP-2 secreting L-cells (31). Plasma levels of GIP and GLP-2 were unaffected by the placebo injection.

Plasma concentrations of total GIP (A) and intact GLP-2 (B) were measured in 3 children with CP or SMA on 3 separate study days. Circle: ingestion of a liquid mixed meal. Square: subcutaneous injection of GIP and GLP-2. Triangle: placebo. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, n = 3.

Bone biomarkers

At baseline, CTX levels were comparable between all 3 visits: 1.2 ± 0.4 (meal test), 1.0 ± 0.4 (GIP+GLP-2), and 1.2 ± 0.5 (placebo) ng/mL. Basal levels of P1NP were also comparable between visits; 416 ± 171 (meal test), 447 ± 176 (GIP+GLP-2), and 522 ± 261 (placebo) ng/mL and the same applies for PTH; 16 ± 3 (meal test), 20 ± 2 (GIP+GLP-2), and 21 ± 1 (placebo) pg/mL.

After ingestion of the liquid mixed-meal, bone resorption, measured as CTX, decreased to 63 ± 8% of basal levels at t = 90 minutes. CTX netAUCs were significantly different between the liquid mixed-meal and the placebo injection (P = .002). Unexpectedly, CTX levels remained unaffected after subcutaneous injection of GIP and GLP-2, and netAUCs were not significantly different between placebo and the GIP and GLP-2 injections (P = .544; Fig. 2A).

Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and netAUCs were statistically evaluated by repeated measures 1-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey post hoc evaluation, n = 3.

Bone formation, measured as plasma levels of P1NP, was similar on all 3 visits, and there was no significant difference in the netAUCs between any of the visits (Fig. 2B).

Administration of GIP and GLP-2 decreased PTH levels to 49 ± 14% of basal levels at t = 30 minutes. However, the netAUCs of the placebo injection and the GIP and GLP-2 administration were not significantly different, presumably due to the low number of study participants. After ingestion of the liquid mixed-meal, PTH levels were similar to those observed for placebo (Fig. 2C).

Blood glucose, blood pressure, and heart rate

An expected increase in blood glucose from 4.8 ± 0.4 mmol/L to a peak value of 6.2 ± 0.4 mmol/L was observed upon ingestion of the liquid mixed-meal, while blood glucose levels were stable during GIP and GLP-2 administration as well as placebo (data not shown).

No difference in heart rate or blood pressure was found during any of the visits (data not shown).

Discussion

This preliminary study investigated the effects of GIP and GLP-2 on bone turnover in 3 children with CP and SMA. Contrary to findings in adult populations, we observed no significant reduction in bone resorption following subcutaneous administration of GIP and GLP-2. As exogenous GIP and GLP-2 administration showed no effect on CTX levels, and further recruitment proved difficult, the trial was terminated earlier than planned.

The lack of effect is surprising, given the robust evidence from previous studies in adults —both healthy (19, 21, 26, 27) and with conditions such as hypoparathyroidism (32) or type 2 diabetes (28)—that consistently demonstrate that GIP and GLP-2 reduce bone resorption, while GIP also stimulates bone formation. However, the absence of a similar response in children with CP or SMA suggests that the gut–bone axis may function differently in pediatric populations or in the context of these diseases. The lack of significant changes in P1NP levels further supports the notion that exogenous GIP and GLP-2 may not exert the same anabolic or regulatory effects on bone metabolism in these children as previously seen in adults. It is, however, important to emphasize that this study only included 3 participants, and caution must be taken regarding interpretation of the data.

Ingestion of the liquid mixed-meal resulted in a normal increase in blood glucose accompanied by increased endogenous GIP and GLP-2 levels and a significant reduction in CTX levels. These meal responses are considered normal as when compared with healthy children (16, 17), but slightly lower than those observed in healthy adult populations (22, 33, 34). Interestingly, the intact meal response was observed despite the lack of effect from exogenous hormone administration. Another unexpected finding was that the liquid mixed-meal did not decrease PTH as previously seen (22), whereas exogenous GIP and GLP-2 lowered PTH levels consistent with previous studies in adults (20, 22, 28). GLP-2 (unlike GIP) is believed to exert its effect on bone in a PTH-dependent manner (32). These discrepancies raise questions about the mechanisms underlying postprandial bone resorption suppression in children. It is possible that other nutrient-related signals or gut-derived factors, beyond GIP and GLP-2, are responsible for this effect in children. Alternatively, the pharmacokinetics or receptor sensitivity to these hormones may differ in pediatric populations with growing bone, particularly those with neuromuscular disorders and neurological systemic diseases. However, to establish the impact of endogenous GIP and GLP-2 on bone, experiments utilizing GIP and GLP-2 receptor antagonists are needed. This has been done in adults where infusion of the GIP receptor antagonist GIP(3-30)NH2 upon meal consumption attenuated the endogenous GIP-induced suppression of CTX (22, 35).

CP and SMA are frequently associated with a high prevalence of gastrointestinal (GI) complications, including dysphagia, gastroesophageal reflux, delayed gastric emptying, and constipation (36, 37). The presence of chronic GI dysfunction and malnutrition may influence the enteroendocrine system and, thus, also the interplay between the GI system and bone metabolism. Notably, studies in colectomized adults have demonstrated a lack of bone resorption inhibition following exogenous administration of GLP-2, underscoring the necessity of an intact and functional intestine for the hormone's osteoprotective effect (38). Given the elevated fracture risk and impaired BMD observed in children with CP and SMA, it is essential to elucidate the mechanisms underlying gut–bone interactions for the development of targeted interventions.

While the small sample size and heterogeneity of the cohort limit the generalizability of our findings, the results nonetheless provide valuable insights. Nutrient intake suppressed bone resorption in 3 children with CP or SMA whereas injections of GIP and GLP-2 were ineffective; however, future studies utilizing GIP and GLP-2 receptor antagonists are needed to elucidate whether this effect is mediated by endogenous GIP and GLP-2. PTH levels decreased upon exogenous GIP and GLP-2 administration, despite the unexpected lack of a PTH response to nutrient intake. The lack of effect of exogenous GIP and GLP-2 on CTX and P1NP questions translation of gut hormone-based therapies for bone health to pediatric populations and calls for age- and disease-appropriate investigations into the gut–bone axis.
