EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This project focused on developing and analyzing a method that could be employed by operational
nuclear facilities to evaluate their CAS operators and determine a probability of assessment for each
operator. This method is agnostic of alarm communication and display system (AC&D), sensors and
cameras in place in the field, and any other technical security measures. This security-measure-
agnostic method enables operational facilities to adopt it to determine the effectiveness of their
alarm station operators.

1. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
Probability of assessment (P A ) plays a significant role in the overall effectiveness of a physical
protection system (PPS) and is a key piece of the system effectiveness (P E ) calculation used to
determine whether a PPS is effective at defending against the design basis threat (DBT). The first step
in the process for determining P E is to calculate the probability of detection, which is found by
multiplying the probability of sensing (P S ) by the probability of assessment.

The probability of interruption (P I ) is determined by both sensor characteristics and the likelihood that
the alarm station operator correctly determines the cause of an alarm and can assist in dispatching
the correct response to an alarm cause. The probability of interruption is the likelihood that the
response force can arrive in a timely manner to interrupt the adversary act of completing a theft or
sabotage. Probability of interruption is the cumulative probability of detection from the first sensing
opportunity to the critical detection point (CDP). The CDP is the last sensing opportunity in which an
alarm can be assessed and the response force be dispatched to interrupt the adversary force. This can
be seen in the following equation:

Once the probability of interruption is calculated, the probability of neutralization (P N ) must be
determined. The probability of neutralization evaluates the probability that the response force is able
to effectively neutralize the adversary force before they can achieve their goal of theft or sabotage.

determining the overall system effectiveness in a performance-based PPS approach. This can be seen

can be determined, as shown in the following equation:

As the equations in this section indicate, the alarm station operator’s ability to accurately assess
alarms plays a significant role in the overall effectiveness of the PPS. Other factors of alarm station
operator performance that impact the overall PPS effectiveness include the time for assessment and
time for communication to the response force. Once an alarm is generated in the central alarm station
(CAS), the PPS timeline starts. The PPS time includes the time it takes for the alarm to be assessed, the
time for the alarm to be communicated to the response force, the deployment time of the response
force, and the time it takes the response force to arrive to a location where they can interrupt the
adversary force before they complete their act of theft or sabotage at a nuclear facility. Figure 1
demonstrates this timeline.

“Assessment of nurses interventions in the Management of Clinical Alarms in the critical care unit,
Kenyatta National Hospital, a cross sectional study.” 1
 Alarms in the critical areas are an important component of most of the machines as
 they alert nurses on the change in the patients’ condition. Most patients in the critical
 care units cannot speak for themselves hence cannot pinpoint when their condition
 changes. It is therefore important to assess the nurses’ interventions when managing
 clinical alarms. The purpose of this study was to assess interventions employed by
 nurses in the management of clinical alarms in the care of patients in the Critical Care
 Unit (CCU), Kenyatta National Hospital (KNH). A descriptive cross sectional study was
 carried out in the month of June 2014 where 87 nurses were recruited as study
 respondents. KNH/ University of Nairobi (UoN) Ethics and Research committee
 approved the research. A structured self administered questionnaire was used to
 collect data. The questionnaire contained some questions in a Likert scale in relation to
 the actions the nurses would take in the management of clinical alarms and some on
 whether policies on alarm management existed in the hospital, if they filled alarm

“A Framework to Assess Alarm Fatigue Indicators in Critical Care Staff.” 2
 This article examines work-related and personality factors that could influence health
 providers in experiencing alarm fatigue. The purpose of this study is to provide a basis
 to determine factors that may predict the potential of alarm fatigue in critical care
 staff. A questionnaire-based survey and an observational study were conducted to
 assess factors that could contribute to indicators of alarm fatigue. Factors included
 patient-to-staff ratio, criticality of the alarm, priority of different tasks, and
 personality traits. The study was conducted at an eight-bed ICU in a mid-size hospital
 in Montana. Data was collected for six day shifts and six night shifts involving 24
 critical care professionals. Within each 12-hour shift, six 15-minute intervals were
 randomly generated through work sampling for 6 days; a total of 1,080 observations
 were collected. Alarm fatigue was assessed with the subjective workload assessment
 technique and Boredom, Apathy, and Distrust Affects, which were measured through
 validated questionnaires. The Big Five Personality model was used to assess
 personality traits.Work factors including task prioritization, nurse-to-patient ratio, and
 length of shifts were associated with indicators of alarm fatigue. Personality traits of
 openness, conscientiousness, and neuroticism were also associated. We recommend
 assessing personality traits for critical care staff to be aware of how their
 individualities can affect their behavior towards alarm fatigue. We also recommend an
 examination of alternative strategies to reduce alarm fatigue, including examining the
 use of breaks, work rotation, or shift reduction.

“Impact of Alarm Fatigue on the Work of Nurses in an Intensive Care Environment – A Systematic
Review.” 3
 In conditions of intensive therapy, where the patients treated are in a critical
 condition, alarms are omnipresent. Nurses, as they spend most of their time with
 patients, monitoring their condition 24 h, are particularly exposed to so-called alarm
 fatigue. The purpose of this study is to review the literature available on the
 perception of clinical alarms by nursing personnel and its impact on work in the ICU
 environment. A systematic review of the literature was carried out according to the
 guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis
 (PRISMA) protocol. The content of electronic databases was searched through, i.e.,
 PubMed, OVID, EBSCO, ProQuest Nursery, and Cochrane Library. The keywords used in
 the search included: “intensive care unit,” “nurse,” “alarm fatigue,” “workload,” and
 “clinical alarm.” The review also covered studies carried out among nurses employed
 at an adult intensive care unit. Finally, seven publications were taken into
 consideration. Data were analyzed both descriptively and quantitatively, calculating a
 weighted average for specific synthetized data. In the analyzed studies, 389 nurses
 were tested, working in different intensive care units. Two studies were based on a
 quality model, while the other five described the problem of alarms in terms of
 quantity, based on the HTF (Healthcare Technology Foundation) questionnaire.
 Intensive care nurses think that alarms are burdensome and too frequent, interfering
 with caring for patients and causing reduced trust in alarm systems. They feel
 overburdened with an excessive amount of duties and a continuous wave of alarms.
 Having to operate modern equipment, which is becoming more and more advanced,
 takes time that nurses would prefer to dedicate to their patients. There is no clear
 system for managing the alarms of monitoring devices. Alarm fatigue may have serious
 consequences, both for patients and for nursing personnel. It is necessary to introduce
 a strategy of alarm management and for measuring the alarm fatigue level.

“Decision-making and response strategies in interaction with alarms: the impact of alarm
reliability, availability of alarm validity information and workload.” 5
 Responding to alarm systems which usually commit a number of false alarms and/or
 misses involves decision-making under uncertainty. Four laboratory experiments
 including a total of 256 participants were conducted to gain comprehensive insight
 into humans' dealing with this uncertainty. Specifically, it was investigated how
 responses to alarms/non-alarms are affected by the predictive validities of these
 events, and to what extent response strategies depend on whether or not the validity
 of alarms/non-alarms can be cross-checked against other data. Among others, the
 results suggest that, without cross-check possibility (experiment 1), low levels of
 predictive validity of alarms ( ≤ 0.5) led most participants to use one of two different
 strategies which both involved non-responding to a significant number of alarms (cry-
 wolf effect). Yet, providing access to alarm validity information reduced this effect
 dramatically (experiment 2). This latter result emerged independent of the effort
 needed for cross-checkings of alarms (experiment 3), but was affected by the workload
 imposed by concurrent tasks (experiment 4). Theoretical and practical consequences of
 these results for decision-making and response selection in interaction with alarm
 systems, as well as the design of effective alarm systems, are discussed.
Numerous studies in medical research have examined the impact of nuisance alarm rates (NAR) on
healthcare staff, particularly nurses, and the frequency of alarms encountered during shifts. This
phenomenon can lead to delayed responses to alarms and, in some instances, a complete disregard
for alarms that are frequently recognized by medical personnel as nuisance alerts. Additionally,
patients may inadvertently trigger alarms when there is no genuine need for them.
The first medical study evaluated in the literature review found a statistically significant relationship
between the age of medical staff and their response to alarms. It was found that older, more
experienced personnel exhibited a lower response rate to alarms, suggesting that seasoned staff may
be more inclined to dismiss alarms due to their familiarity with nuisance or false alarms from certain
patients or devices. This observation can be paralleled in the security field, where experienced
security officers may also overlook alarms, having developed a keen sense of when a false or nuisance
alarm is likely, based on their extensive experience at a site.
The second medical study evaluated in the literature review explored additional factors contributing
to alarm fatigue, including task prioritization and shift length. The findings indicated that as the
number of tasks increases, the likelihood of ignoring or inadequately assessing alarms also rises.
Similarly, longer shifts correlate with a higher propensity to overlook alarms. This is relevant to

1.1.2. Security Related Literature
“Human Factors in Security.” 6
 Physical security systems (PSS) and humans are inescapably tied in the current physical
 security paradigm. Yet, physical security system evaluations often end at the console
 that displays information to the human. That is, these evaluations do not account for
 human-in-the-loop factors that can greatly impact performance of the security system,
 even though methods for doing so are well-established. This paper highlights two
 examples of methods for evaluating the human component of the current physical
 security system. One of these methods is qualitative, focusing on the information the
 human needs to adequately monitor alarms on a physical site. The other of these
 methods objectively measures the impact of false alarm rates on threat detection.
 These types of human-centric evaluations are often treated as unnecessary or not cost
 effective under the belief that human cognition is straightforward, and errors can be
 either trained away or mitigated with technology. These assumptions are not always
 correct, are often surprising, and can often only be identified with objective
 assessments of human-system performance. Thus, taking the time to perform human
 element evaluations can identify unintuitive human-system weaknesses and can
 provide significant cost savings in the form of mitigating vulnerabilities and reducing
 costly system patches or retrofits to correct an issue after the system has been
 deployed.
“Human reliability assessment under uncertainty – towards a formal method.” 7
 Humans are and will remain one of the critical constituents of a technological system.
 The study of human factors is a broad domain with equally varying applications.
 Furthermore, with the advent of new technologies in safety-critical systems there is
 always a need to ensure system safety and reliability in accordance with increasingly
 demanding certification standards. Human reliability is a cause of concern as hardware
 becomes increasingly reliable and relatively human error is rising in its share of
 causing an accident. Human Reliability Analysis (HRA) provides a way to quantify the
 risk associated with a human. This paper presents a discussion on the development of a
 HRA model for the domain of transportation, rail transport in particular. Railway
 specific human factors studies are analyzed to identify safety relevant factors in order

“HABA-MABA and High Consequence Facility Security— Understanding the Human/Machine Trade
Space in Security System Design & Performance.” 8
 Physical protection systems (PPS)—typically the most recognizable element of securing
 high consequence facilities (HCF)—often utilize humans as critical decisionmakers for
 nearly all data created by the PPS. Yet, research in systems design asserts humans as
 one of the largest sources of uncertainty and variability in complex system
 performance. Further, physical security personnel are often a significant HCF
 operations cost. In response, HCF security solutions are trending toward “designing
 out” the human. To ensure adequate HCF protection, such technocentric design
 approaches must fully consider the impact of removing humans from security system
 operations. Better understanding the trade space between performance, efficiency,
 effectiveness, and resilience in human-driven versus machine-driven security solutions
 is necessary when designing—and implementing—successful PPS. Thus, the goal of this
 work is to initiate a broader discussion on how to balance human and technological
 components—and optimize overall sociotechnical system performance— within HCF
 physical security systems.

 sociotechnical context. Through application of the Systems Context Lenses, critical
 Performance Shaping Factors(PSFs) influencing human performance can be identified

 resources in an MLN. This paper summarizes the benefit of a tiered-lens approach on a
 use case of a multilayered network model of NPP security, including demonstrating

2. METHOD DEVELOPMENT
The method developed for this analysis focuses on evaluating individual operators based on the
systems they encounter, specific procedures for each type of alarm, and the environmental conditions
present during the testing period. The initial phase of model development involved identifying the
objectives of the testing method and the corresponding matrix to be utilized. The outlined goals for
this method and test matrix are as follows:
 o Demonstrate that the probability of assessment is not equal to one
 o Create a test matrix applicable across various technical security measures
 o Assess potential differences based on operator backgrounds
 o Identify technical features that may influence the probability of assessment
 o Develop a test matrix that can be used to evaluate changes focused on improving the
 probability of assessment for alarm station operators
 o Construct a test matrix that accounts for alarms encountered in operational facilities,
 including nuisance, false, incursion, and routine maintenance alarms
 o Understand the factors affecting the time required for assessment and the probability of
 assessment for CAS operators
 o Examine how the probability of assessment, assessment time, and communication time impact
 the overall performance of a PPS
The primary objective of this work is to establish that the overall probability of an alarm station
operator is not equal to 1 (one). As discussed in the foundational mathematics and equations in
Section 1 of the report, the probability of assessment plays a crucial role in determining the overall
probability of detection, which in turn affects the probability of interruption and the effectiveness of
the PSS. If the probability of assessment is less than 1 (one), it may indicate a reduction in the overall
effectiveness of the system.
Creating a test matrix that is independent of specific technical security measures will enable its
application across a variety of operational nuclear facilities, each with unique systems in place. These
facilities may utilize different exterior and interior sensors, video display configurations, pre- and
post-recorded video feeds, automatic video call-up systems, and various camera types. Such diversity
necessitates a test matrix that can adapt to different operational environments, ensuring broad
applicability.
While this study did not focus on human factors or performance, it did evaluate two operators with
distinct backgrounds and expertise in CAS operations. One operator possesses a background in
technical security measures, cybersecurity, video management systems, and alarm communication and
display (AC&D) systems. The other operator, a former protective force leader at a Category-I nuclear
facility, has extensive experience within a CAS, including dispatching responders and directing
response strategies. This comparison allowed the study to identify potential nuances in how different
backgrounds may influence further testing.
Additionally, this study aimed to identify technical measures that could impact the probability of
assessment. Although these factors were not specifically examined during testing, they may affect an

 19

operator's ability to accurately assess alarms, as well as the time taken to make a correct assessment
and communicate the alarm's cause to the response force leader or other relevant organizations.
Technical measures of interest include pre- and post-recorded video, camera contrast ratios, the
positioning of closed-circuit television (CCTV) cameras, camera resolutions, resolution of CAS operator
monitors, and the types of sensors deployed in the field.
The developed test matrix will facilitate the evaluation of changes to determine whether they enhance
or diminish the probability of assessment for alarm station operators. Emerging technologies, such as
video motion detection (VMD) and video analytic cameras, are gaining traction in the security
community. These innovations may help reduce or filter nuisance and false alarms, serving as effective
sensors. By establishing a benchmark for the performance characteristics of CAS operators, the impact
of these emerging technologies on operator performance can also be assessed. Furthermore,
advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have been considered as
potential solutions for minimizing nuisance and false alarms. By creating a framework to evaluate the
probability of assessment, these tools can be integrated, and their effects on alarm station operators
can be analyzed.
The method and test matrix developed account for the various alarms encountered in an operational
CAS. Discussions with CAS operators and technical security team members revealed four primary
causes of alarms: nuisance alarms, false alarms, day-to-day or maintenance alarms, and incursion
alarms. The method and test matrix are designed to generate and gather information on all these
alarm types. The following definitions are used for the aforementioned alarms:
 o Nuisance Alarm: An alarm triggered by an identifiable cause that is not related to the facility's
 operation or genuine threats.
 o False Alarm: An alarm that is triggered but cannot be attributed to a specific cause.
 o Day-to-Day or Maintenance Alarm: An alarm resulting from activities such as maintenance,
 performance testing, operational testing, and other known causes.
 o Incursion Alarm: An alarm indicating unauthorized individuals in a restricted area, attempts by
 insiders to access prohibited locations, violations of two-person rules, or incursions by
 external adversaries.
Based on these alarm sources, the test method and matrix will evaluate the impact of alarm types and
their frequency on the probability of assessment, assessment time, and communication time. This
analysis is crucial for understanding how these alarm types affect the performance of alarm station
operators.
Finally, the developed test method and matrix support the evaluation of how the probability of
assessment influences the overall probability of interruption and, consequently, system effectiveness.
This aspect of the test plan and matrix development will provide valuable insights for operational
nuclear facilities and their CAS operators.

The test bed used for the development and testing of the matrix is located at the Nuclear Security
Technology Center (NSTC) at Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia). This facility houses operational
nuclear security technologies and systems that have been used and are set up similarly to the original
security system design when the facility housed Category-I nuclear material. Using the facility’s

Figure 2 shows the exterior intrusion detection system installed along the protected area perimeter at
the test facility. The exterior intrusion detection system includes an isolation zone of 50 feet between
an outer nuisance fence and an inner protected area fence. The isolation zone is equipped with
overlapping sensors that include microwave sensors, active infrared sensors, and a vibration sensor

the live video of the area where the alarm has been generated.

3.1. SIMULATED ADVERSARY INCURSION SCENARIO
A CAS operator plays a critical role in the effectiveness of a PPS and is crucially important during a real
nuclear security event. To analyze this, the testing methodology described herein included a simulated
incursion into the MRA of the testbed. To facilitate this simulated adversary incursion a set time was
chosen by the individuals running the exercise, and the CAS operators were not informed that the
simulated incursion would take place.
The simulated adversary incursion occurred at the western perimeter of the protected area at the
testbed facility. At the start of this simulated incursion event, performance testing of a microwave
sensor was occurring under normal operational circumstances of the facility. This was deemed
credible by the test controllers, as real adversary groups may have knowledge of testing activities and
use this time to attack the facility while alarms are being generated from the testing and reporting in
the CAS. During the time of the incursion event, the CAS also received nuisance alarms in a different
sector of the perimeter. The CAS operator was communicating these nuisance alarms to technical
security who was in the field assisting with the performance testing of microwave sensors in the
northern part of the perimeter. At the time the incursion alarm was generated through the microwave
sensors and fence vibration sensor at the western point, a nuisance alarm was also generated at the
northern portion of the facility.
The simulated adversary then began to move toward the main entrance of the building that houses
the MRA. The adversary entered the building, generating another alarm from the BMS on the entry
door. Once inside the area, the PIR sensor outside of the interim storage vault alarmed as well. The
final alarm the CAS operator received was the BMS protecting the door into the interim storage vault.
While this scenario-specific data was captured in the aggregate data collected during testing (rather
than individually during this event), it was noted that the overall assessment time and communication
time was much longer compared to nuisance and false alarms that were generated during testing. The
time it took for alarm assessment of the initial incursion took approximately 180 seconds, with a
communication time of 20 seconds. Many factors contributed to this lengthy assessment and
communication time, including ongoing nuisance alarms, actual performance testing alarms and
performance testing activities, and the incursion alarms coming into the facility.
The simulated adversary incursion generated an assessment time approximately 69 seconds longer
than the average time for assessment of incursion alarms that were generated in the overall testing
method.
This simulated adversary incursion scenario highlights the importance of alarm prioritization and CAS
operator training when determining and assessing alarm causes at a facility. Alarm prioritization for
CAS operators could have led to a reduction in the total assessment time, as the CAS operator may
have realized a new alarm was coming into the facility at a location close to the target and in a
portion of the perimeter that was not receiving nuisance alarms or undergoing active performance
testing.

3.2. ADDITIONAL CONSIDERATIONS
During this test series human factor analysis and studies were not involved, as this study focused on
developing a testing methodology, testing matrix, and collecting and analyzing the resulting data.
Additionally, this data was collected on an operational PPS in a testbed environment and does not

 32

consider the wide array of technical features that may be present at operational nuclear facilities or
the differences in other security technologies that may be used at those facilities.
Many human factors may have an impact on the overall operator performance in an alarm station. The
following factors have been identified as having a role in CAS operator performance. Observations for
these human factors were made based on the test plan and test matrix used during this testing:
 • Cognitive Load: Operators must process a large amount of information quickly. High cognitive
 load can lead to errors, so it's important to design systems that minimize unnecessary
 complexity.
 o As demonstrated during the testing, when CAS operators are presented with numerous
 simultaneously occurring alarms and operational practices, metrics such as assessment
 time and communication time are negatively impacted. This is an example of how
 cognitive load could affect CAS operator performance.
 • Situational Awareness: Maintaining awareness of the environment and understanding the
 status of the entire AC&D system is critical. CAS operators must be cognizant of sensors or
 cameras undergoing maintenance, sensors or cameras that are down for repair, and sensors
 and cameras that have trouble during various times of day.
 • Stress and Fatigue: The nature of the job can be stressful, especially during security events,
 high alarm loads, exercises, etc. Fatigue can impair performance, so implementing regular
 breaks and monitoring workload is essential.
 o During the testing, both CAS operators expressed being tired and stressed when
 experiencing high alarm loads. This is also accompanied by times when no alarms were
 generated in the CAS. These extremes lead to large ramp-up and ramp-down times for
 the CAS operator. Depending on the facility and the systems being utilized, these
 factors may impact the overall performance of the CAS operators.
 • Communication Skills: Effective communication is vital for coordinating responses to alarms.
 o Communication procedures may vary from facility-to-facility, and therefore, play a
 role in the overall communication time from a CAS operator to the response force. In
 this testing, the CAS operator with years of experience in an operational CAS provided
 clear and concise communication, while the less experienced operator was less
 concise in their communication of alarm causes. These communication skills can be
 improved with training and proper communication procedures, both of which can be
 implemented at sites.
 • Decision-Making: Operators often need to make quick decisions based on incomplete
 information.
 o During the testing, some decisions made by the operator were due to a lack of
 understanding of both procedures and the scenario they were facing. For example, in
 the simulated incursion scenario discussed previously, the CAS operator was unable to
 quickly decide whether an alarm occurred at the perimeter in a sector that was not
 actively experiencing performance testing or nuisance alarms. Therefore, the operator
 decided this alarm may have been an nuisance alarm and therefore, less of a priority.
 This may have been due to other alarms being generated at the time and the high

 33

 stress and workload the operator was experiencing. This may show that these human
 factors are not independent but may be related, and therefore, one may have an
 impact on the other. Training in decision-making processes and providing decision
 support tools can enhance CAS operator effectiveness.
• Ergonomics: The physical setup of the workstation can impact operator performance.
 Ergonomic designs can reduce physical strain and improve comfort, leading to better focus
 and efficiency.

4. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
One conclusion from this effort is that the equation for evaluating probability of assessment may need
to be reevaluated based on lessons learned from this testing. The equation for probability of
assessment is restated as follows:

                           𝑃 𝐼 = 1 ― [(1 ― 𝑃 𝑑1 ) ∗ (1 ― 𝑃 𝑑1 ) ∗ ..((1 ― 𝑃 𝑑𝐶𝐷𝑃 )

This formula may be correct if, and only if, the probabilities of all the detection points are
independent. From this experiment, and from the calculation of the probability of assessment, it is
clear that these detection points are not independent. From the adversary incursion time, as stated in
a previous section, it took a total of 180 seconds to process one alarm, while it took a shorter time on
a different alarm on the same attack path. Additionally, the CAS operator incorrectly assessing at least
one of the alarms on the attack path. Based on this, if probability of assessment is not equal to 1
(one), then probability of detection as a whole is decreased. More significantly, the attack path
detection points are not independent. The incursion scenario shows that detection and assessment
took longer when alarms occurred further away from the target, and assessment took less time when
the adversary was closer to the target. This indicates there is a higher priority for CAS operators to
assess alarms closer to the target area, which is the logical choice, but it disproves the equation used
to calculate P I . Ultimately, the above equation is very simple and relies on independence, which is
almost virtually unattainable in any context, but especially in this one. Overall, with this data set
alone and without further study, it would be difficult to create a new equation and verify it without
subsequent and further SME opinion.
Based on the test plan, analysis, and factors identified above, some conclusions and recommendations
can be drawn from this work. The first conclusion is that overall probability of assessment for a CAS
operator may not be equal 1 (one) for all sites, and this could have an impact on the overall
effectiveness analysis conducted for a PPS. While this is an underlying assumption, this test series
allowed the team to determine the probability of assessment for the CAS operators during a defined
test window. This does not provide a holistic testing sample for all operational conditions at this test
facility, but it does start to identify the probability of assessment for a CAS operator.
The second conclusion from this analysis is the effect that successive alarms have on CAS operators,
especially in the event of a facility incursion. This testing showed that successive alarms increase the
overall assessment and communication time for the CAS operator. It was also shown that most
incursion alarms will send successive alarms to the CAS operator. Additionally, successive alarms such
as nuisance and performance testing alarms could significantly impact the time for assessment and
communication of a real incursion alarm to the response force.
While the data from this testing was collected using a testbed facility, it indicates that these tests may
be beneficial for operational sites to conduct to (1) better understand the performance of their CAS
operators and (2) better quantify CAS operator assessment and communication times. Conducting this
analysis at an operational site will allow that facility to capture overall performance of its CAS
operators. This performance data can then be used in tabletop exercises and combat simulations to
evaluate the performance of the PPS. Additionally, this data will improve facility understanding of CAS
operator assessment and communication times, which will support incorporation of these higher

 35

fidelity timelines into the overall PPS analysis to ensure that the response force can interrupt and
neutralize an adversary.
This testing methodology and sample test matrix show a way for probability of assessment to be
analyzed, nuisance alarm rates and false alarm rates to be determined, and assessment and
communication times to be determined. Researchers aiming to produce technologies that can reduce
nuisance and false alarm rates, improve probability of assessment, or decrease assessment times may
benefit from this approach. The team ensured this test methodology was technology- and security-
measure-agnostic, and as a result, it could be utilized to evaluate the impact of new technologies or
systems introduced into an operating environment and help to determine if those new technologies or
systems actually improve CAS operator performance.
The CAS operators’ assessment and communication time was critical for this study and the data
analyzed. These times can be impactful to the deployment and arrival time of responders to a
potential security event. This study showed through the testbed and test plan that the assessment
time for an actual incursion into the facility is much longer than that for nuisance and false alarms
generated onsite. This increase in time for incursion alarms over the average assessment time may
impact overall response timelines. For example, if the PPS at a facility is designed for a 6-minute
response to an 8-minute adversary attack timeline and uses the average assessment and
communication time for all alarms, then the response team will arrive at the designated location at 6
minutes and 50 seconds. However, if only the incursion alarm assessment and communication time is
used, then the response team will arrive at the designated location at 8 minutes and 19 seconds. This
would require the PPS to be reconfigured, assessment time to be reduced, communication time to be
reduced, or the response plan to be modified to be able to interrupt the adversary before 8 minutes.
By conducting these tests, facilities will gain more insight into modifications that could be made to
improve the overall effectiveness of the PPS.

4.1. LINKS TO NRC IDHEAS FRAMEWORK
The NRC IDHEAS-ECA model is based on identifying human failure events (HFEs) and what causes
HFEs. 11 These functions are defined below, and the sub bullets identify how they apply for a CAS
operator.
 1. Detection: noticing cues or gathering information in the work environment
 a. Detection in a PPS is initially achieved with sensors or personnel in the field. In this
 study, sensors in the field provide initial detection, which is the first phase of
 identifying a potential threat in a PPS. In this case, sensors operate as the detection
 function (which a CAS operator relies on) in the IDHEAS-ECA framework.
 2. Understanding: the integration of pieces of information with a person’s mental model to make
 sense of the scenario or situation
 a. In the IDHEAS-ECA model, understanding correlates to the CAS operator’s ability to
 discern why a sensor may have alarmed (e.g., weather, intruder, faulty sensor, etc.).
 For a CAS operator, understanding is determined by their ability to understand the

11   https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1707/ML17073A041.pdf

 36

 system (nuisance causes, false alarm causes, access control issues, system issues, etc.)
 and the limitations of the assessment technology deployed (e.g., CCTV cameras) that
 may impact their ability to assess the cause of an alarm.
 3. Decision-making: includes selecting strategies, planning, adapting plans, evaluating options,
 and making judgements on qualitative information or quantitative parameters
 a. In the IDHEAS-ECA framework, decision-making correlates to how a CAS operator
 assesses an alarm cause and who they report the alarm cause to once it has been
 logged in the AC&D system. Decision-making for a CAS operator will be based on the
 facility’s policies and procedures and the response plan to alarms at various locations
 for the facility. For example, if a nuisance alarm can be confirmed as a nuisance by the
 CAS operator, it may not need to be confirmed or communicated to a response force
 team leader. However, if an alarm is caused by a person, the policy or procedure may
 require communication to the response force team leader. For a CAS operator,
 decision-making is determined by the cause of the alarm and the procedure for
 responding to a specific alarm in a specific location.
 4. Action execution: the implementation of the decision or plan to change some physical
 component or system
 a. In the IDHEAS-ECA framework, action execution focuses on the final action a CAS
 operator would take. If the action required is to communicate alarm cause to the RTL
 and report the alarm cause in the AC&D system, this would be the final action
 execution for that task. If the action does not require communication to a response
 force team leader, then the action execution would be the final log of alarm cause into
 the AC&D system.
 5. Inter-team coordination: focuses on how various teams interact and collaborate on a critical
 task
 a. In the IDHEAS-ECA framework, inter-team coordination correlates to the continuous
 feedback from a CAS operator to an RTL, the security shift supervisor, or the technical
 security department. Each of these individuals or groups has a coordination role to
 resolve failures based on their jurisdiction and overall role in the PPS. For example, if
 an alarm is caused by a person who may be an intruder and an intruder continues to
 progress through the facility, the CAS operator may be required to communicate with
 the response team lead about the intruder’s position and other alarms the intruder
 may have set off along their pathway to a target location. If a sensor continues to
 experience nuisance alarms or false alarms, the CAS operator may have to coordinate
 with the security shift supervisor and the technical security department to determine
 how to best handle a faulty sensor with high alarm rates and communicate the cause
 of the alarms from the perspective of the alarm station operator.
The IDHES-ECA provides a set of five cognitive failure modes (CFMs) to model failure of a critical task.
These CFMs are described below; sub bullet a discuss how these CFMs apply to CAS operators and a
PPS strategy for responding to security events, and sub bullet b discusses observed ways in which
these CFMs may apply to CAS operators and the impact on the overall PPS.
 1. CFM1: failure of detection

 37

 a. In a PPS, specifically for CAS operators, CFM1 is determined by the probability of
 detection for the sensors that must be bypassed by an adversary. Probability of
 detection for sensors is based on the sensor phenomenology, the environment,
 correct installation, and correct application of a sensor.
 b. During testing, detection was conducted both by sensors in the field and by
 dispatching personnel if the cause of an alarm could not be assessed by the CAS
 operator. This is a normal operating procedure that is followed by many nuclear
 facilities. Because detection can now be achieved both by technologies and people,
 this introduces another potential failure to detection for CAS operators.
2. CFM2: failure of understanding
 a. For a CAS operator, CFM2 would occur when the CAS operator does not properly
 assess the cause of an alarm and records the wrong cause (e.g., recording an incursion
 alarm as a false alarm).
 b. During testing this was noted to occur for the alarm station operators that were
 conducting the testing. At some points, alarms were not reported correctly due to not
 following instructions and as well as not recording the cause of an alarm that entered
 into the CAS. Not reporting the cause of an alarm can occur for many reasons
 including CAS operator neglect or forgetfulness. This can also lead into additional
 CFMs, which are defined in the following paragraphs. However, during testing, an
 alarm was recorded as nuisance when the alarm was actually false, or the alarm was
 not recorded correctly according to shift supervisor and facility requirements. This
 shows that CAS operators may fail in understanding during operations, and this lapse
 in understanding may indicate a larger overall problem. A lack of understanding during
 a nuclear security event may cause correct information to be lost to the response
 team and cause the response team to be less effective at responding to the nuclear
 security event.
3. CFM3: failure of decision-making
 a. For a CAS operator, CFM3 would occur if the CAA operator makes the wrong decision
 about who to report alarm cause(s) to (e.g., the alarm station operator reporting an
 incursion alarm to the technical security department instead of the response team
 lead would be a failure). Another example is an alarm station operator failing to report
 multiple nuisance alarms in one sector of the perimeter to the security shift
 supervisor.
 b. During testing, failure of decision-making was noticed less frequently than a failure of
 understanding occurred. Decision-making was often quickly executed by the operators
 used during this testing. Decision-making failures witnessed during testing occurred in
 relation to the number of nuisance alarms and false alarms that were reported within
 the system. During the testing, operators were asked to contact the technical security
 department about nuisance alarms if a sensor had alarmed three times within an hour.
 This would alert the technical security team of issues with sensors at the facility.
 During testing, this procedure was not always followed, and the CAS operator did not
 always communicate nuisance alarms to technical security.

 38

 4. CFM4: failure of action execution
 a. For a CAS operator, CFM4 would occur if they are not able to properly communicate
 alarm causes to the correct individual/responsible authority to act for an alarm being
 generated. Other occurrences include improper and/or incorrect alarm recording to
 the AC&D system.
 b. During this testing, this failure method did not show very often. When this failure
 method did show, it was a result of CFM2. The failure of action execution from a CAS
 operator could occur because of failure to understand; the CAS operator is also a
 potential active insider at the facility who is ignoring alarms or failing to communicate
 the cause of alarms to a responsible authority.
 5. CFM5: failure of inter-team coordination
 a. For a CAS operator, CFM5 would occur if the operator is not able to properly
 communicate alarm situations to the response force team lead during a potential
 incursion or cannot properly communicate nuisance and false alarms to the security
 shift supervisor or technical security department.
