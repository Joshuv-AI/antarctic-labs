Abstract
. We study the Cauchy problem for inhomogeneous evolution equations with time-dependent, potentially degenerate, and unbounded coefficients. A key feature of our work is allowing the principal coefficients to undergo arbitrary blow-up at both the initial and terminal times.

1.
introduction

Singular coefficients in partial differential equations are typically characterized by discontinuity, unboundedness, or degeneracy.
The investigation of second-order PDEs featuring such coefficients is a classical and extensive area of mathematical research, tracing its origins to the seminal works of Picone, Tricomi, Keldyš, Sobolev, and Fichera.
For a comprehensive treatment of these classical results, Oleǐnik and Radkevič’s literature (
[
26
]
) remains a foundational reference.

The field continues to see vigorous activity, with numerous authors exploring various classes of singularities within a framework of weighted spaces (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
18
,
16
,
11
,
7
,
1
,
9
,
25
,
12
,
10
]
and the references therein).

Despite this rich literature, a prevailing limitation in contemporary studies is the standard assumption of local integrability.
Even when coefficients exhibit blow-up behavior near the boundaries or the initial time, they are generally required to remain locally integrable.
Intuitively, severe growth—such as exponential blow-up—is incompatible with non-zero boundary or initial data.
However, a crucial insight is that well-posedness can still be recovered even if the coefficients undergo rapid, unrestricted growth near the initial time, provided that the initial condition is strictly zero.
This observation provides the central motivation for the present paper: we aim to establish that the well-posedness of second-order evolution equations can be rigorously affirmed even in the presence of strongly non-integrable coefficients.

To formalize our theory, we consider the following Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+b^{i}(t)u_{x^{i}}(t,x)+c(t)u(t,x)+f(t,x),

\displaystyle u(0,x)=0,\quad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d},

(1.1)

where the spatial dimension
d\in\mathbf{N}
and the terminal time
T>0
are fixed constants.
Throughout this paper, we adopt the Einstein summation convention, where repeated indices imply summation over
i,j=1,\dots,d
.
The coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
,
b^{i}(t)
, and
c(t)
are real-valued Lebesgue measurable functions defined on the interval
(0,T)
.
We impose a degenerate ellipticity condition on the principal part,
i.e.

a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\geq\lambda(t)|\xi|^{2}\quad\text{for all }t\in(0,T)\text{ and }\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d},

where
\lambda(t)\geq 0
is a Lebesgue measurable function.
No additional regularity conditions are imposed, nor is
\lambda(t)
assumed to be strictly positive.
Consequently, our framework naturally accommodates cases where the equation becomes irregular or degenerate on sets of positive measure.
Furthermore, we merely assume that the coefficients are locally integrable inside the open interval
(0,T)
.
Specifically, for any
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<T
, we have

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(|a^{ij}(s)|+|b^{i}(s)|+|c(s)|\right)\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

Crucially, this assumption permits the coefficients to exhibit extreme singularities—including exponential blow-up—at the boundaries
t=0
and
t=T
.
Under these hypotheses, we demonstrate that for a given inhomogeneous source term
f
, the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) admits a unique weak solution
u
.
If we further assume that the zero-order coefficient
c(t)
is integrable near the initial time, namely
\int_{0}^{t}|c(s)|\mathrm{d}s<\infty
for all
t\in(0,T)
, we can establish the following maximal regularity estimate for any
p,q\in(1,\infty)
and
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|u_{xx}(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{q/p}\mathrm{e}^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}w(\alpha(t))\lambda(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim_{d,p,q,w}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|f(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{q/p}\mathrm{e}^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}w(\alpha(t))(\lambda(t))^{1-q}\mathrm{d}t,

where
w
represents a one-dimensional Muckenhoupt weight (
cf.
Definition
2.19
) and
\alpha(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)\mathrm{d}s
.

Physically, allowing for such strong singularities significantly broadens the modeling capabilities of the equation.
Singularities in
a^{ij}(t)
can describe diffusion processes with extreme temporal fluctuations, representing phases of instantaneous or highly rapid propagation.
Similarly, a blow-up in the advection term
b^{i}(t)
captures intervals where transport completely dominates the system’s dynamics.
Finally, an unbounded
c(t)
can model explosive reaction kinetics, leading to sudden, unbounded growth or decay.
Because our framework accommodates singularities precisely at the initial time
t=0
, it is exceptionally well-suited for capturing the initial bursts of rapidly evolving phenomena, such as explosive chemical reactions or heat conduction with extreme initial conductivity variations.

The presence of unbounded coefficients introduces substantial difficulties in establishing the existence, uniqueness, and stability of solutions, thereby demanding rigorous mathematical treatment. Analyzing parabolic PDEs with such singular coefficients is inherently challenging and typically relies on specialized methodologies, such as the introduction of tailored weight functions to control the singularities.
Historically, however, these approaches impose a critical restriction: the coefficients are prohibited from blowing up faster than the reciprocal of the distance to the boundary (cf.
[
4
]
).
We emphasize that our framework fundamentally relaxes this constraint. Specifically, to guarantee the existence and uniqueness of a weak solution, our coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
and
b^{i}(t)
(
i,j=1,\dots,d
) are entirely exempt from the standard bound:

|a^{ij}(t)|+|b^{i}(t)|\lesssim\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{T-t},\quad t\in(0,T).

Likewise, the zero-order coefficient
c(t)
is not required to satisfy the analogous condition:

|c(t)|\lesssim\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{T-t},\quad t\in(0,T).

In fact, we impose absolutely no restrictions on the asymptotic behavior of these coefficients as they approach the initial and terminal times.
As previously noted, they are permitted to exhibit singularities that grow faster than any exponential function.
To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to establish well-posedness while allowing the coefficients’ blow-up rates to strictly exceed the reciprocal of the distance to the temporal boundaries.

A natural extension of equation (
1.1
) would involve coefficients that depend on both time and space, formulated as:

u_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t,x)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+b^{i}(t,x)u_{x^{i}}(t,x)+c(t,x)u(t,x)+f(t,x),

u(0,x)=0,\quad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}.

However, establishing well-posedness for such spatially dependent equations in
L_{p}
-spaces is highly non-trivial. For instance, even for a simplified spatial operator of the form:

\displaystyle a^{ij}(t,x)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+b^{i}(t,x)u_{x^{i}}(t,x)+c(t,x)u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\Delta u(t,x)-d\frac{x^{i}}{|x|^{2}}u_{x^{i}}(t,x)-\lambda u(t,x),

where
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
, well-posedness in
L_{p}
-spaces breaks down (as demonstrated in
[
24
, Example 2.1]
).
This implies that generalizing our framework to include spatially dependent coefficients would require imposing stringent structural conditions on their spatial behavior.
Because our primary objective is to investigate the unique effects of strongly non-integrable temporal singularities, we leave this complex generalization outside the scope of the current work.
Identifying the precise spatial assumptions necessary to accommodate such coefficients remains a promising avenue for future research.

2.
Setting and main results

To lay the mathematical groundwork, we direct readers seeking foundational context to Krylov’s comprehensive text
[
23
]
.

The primary challenges—and consequently, the significance—of our results arise directly from the singularities within the equation’s coefficients. To emphasize the novelty of our results, we begin by detailing our core assumptions regarding these terms. Throughout this work, we assume that all coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
,
b^{i}(t)
, and
c(t)
(for
i,j=1,\dots,d
) are real-valued, measurable functions defined on the open interval
(0,T)
.

Assumption 2.1

(Degenerate ellipticity and local integrability)
.

The principal coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
satisfy the following conditions:

(i)

Degenerate ellipticity:
The coefficient matrix
(a^{ij}(t))
is non-negative definite, meaning

\displaystyle a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\geq 0\quad\forall t\in(0,T)~\text{and}~\forall\xi=(\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{d})\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

(2.1)

(ii)

Inside Local integrability:
The components
a^{ij}(t)
are locally integrable inside
(0,T)
, such that

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\max_{i,j}|a^{ij}(r)|\mathrm{d}r<\infty\quad\text{for all $0<s<t<T$},

where

\displaystyle\max_{i,j}|a^{ij}(t)|=\max\{|a^{ij}(t)|:i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\}.

Assumption 2.2
.

The first-order coefficients
b^{i}(t)

(i=1,\ldots,d)
are locally integrable inside
(0,T)
. Specifically,

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\max_{i}|b^{i}(r)|\mathrm{d}r<\infty\quad\text{for all $0<s\leq t<T$}.

Assumption 2.3
.

The zero-order coefficient
c(t)
is locally integrable inside
(0,T)
, satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}|c(r)|\mathrm{d}r<\infty\quad\text{for all $0<s\leq t<T$}.

Next, we define a specific class of locally integrable functions on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
, which provides the foundation for the rigorous formulation of our weak solutions.
A real-valued measurable function
f
, defined almost everywhere on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
, is said to be locally integrable if

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|<r}|f(s,x)|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}s<\infty

for all
t\in(0,T)
and
r>0
.
Note that
f(t,x)
need not be evaluated precisely at
t=0
.
However, we explicitly utilize the half-open interval
[0,T)
rather than the open interval
(0,T)
to emphasize that the time integration extends down to zero.
This prevents any ambiguity with the preceding assumptions, where the coefficients were only required to be locally integrable strictly inside
(0,T)
.

Definition 2.4

(Weak solution)
.

Let
u
and
f
be locally integrable functions on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
We say that
u
is a weak solution to the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) if, for every test function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, the following integral identity holds for all
t\in(0,T)
:

\displaystyle(u(t,\cdot),\varphi)_{L_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(u(s,\cdot),a^{ij}(s)\varphi_{x^{i}x^{j}}-b^{i}(s)\varphi_{x^{i}}+c(s)\varphi\right)_{L_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}(f(s,\cdot),\varphi)_{L_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s,

(2.2)

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}
denotes the standard spatial inner product in
L_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, given by

(u(t,\cdot),\varphi)_{L_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}:=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}u(t,x)\varphi(x)\mathrm{d}x.

When necessary, we may refer to
u
as a weak solution to (
1.1
) with data
f
to explicitly emphasize the associated inhomogeneous term.

Remark 2.5
.

Since this paper avoids the use of general distribution-valued functions, our definition of a weak solution relies entirely on the standard
L_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
inner product.
As a result, the source term
f
must be locally integrable on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
to ensure that the final integral in (
2.2
) is well-defined.
Throughout the remainder of this work, it is implicitly assumed that any weak solution
u
and its corresponding source data
f
satisfy this local integrability requirement, without needing to restate it.
Furthermore, this integral formulation remains robust even when the coefficients fail to be integrable near the initial time.
This is because a solution
u
with a zero initial condition decays rapidly enough to counteract the singularities of the coefficients.

Remark 2.6
.

We now recall a standard PDE technique for simplifying the principal coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
.
The degenerate ellipticity condition (
2.1
) does not intrinsically require the leading coefficient matrix
A(t):=(a^{ij}(t))
to be symmetric, meaning we cannot immediately apply spectral decomposition.
To resolve this and facilitate element-wise matrix analysis, we replace
A(t)
with its symmetric part. It is straightforward to verify that
u
is a weak solution to (
1.1
) if and only if it is a weak solution to the symmetrized equation:

u_{t}(t,x)=\frac{a^{ij}(t)+a^{ji}(t)}{2}u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+b^{i}(t)u_{x^{i}}(t,x)+c(t)u(t,x)+f(t,x),

u(0,x)=0,\quad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}.

Thus, we may assume without loss of generality that
A(t)
is symmetric for all
t\in(0,T)
.
We have rigorously verified that this standard convention remains valid even for the highly singular coefficients considered in our framework.
Assuming symmetry provides two fundamental analytical advantages:

•

Square Root Matrix:
It guarantees the existence of a unique positive semi-definite square root matrix, denoted
\sqrt{A}(t)
, satisfying
\sqrt{A}(t)\sqrt{A}(t)=A(t)
.

•

Spectral Lower Bound:
It allows us to identify a non-negative function
\lambda(t)
on
(0,T)
—termed a spectral lower-bound of
A(t)
—that satisfies the quadratic form inequality:

\lambda(t)|\xi|^{2}\leq a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\quad\text{for all }t\in(0,T)\text{ and }\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

Typically,
\lambda(t)
is defined as the smallest eigenvalue of
A(t)
.
However, a complication arises if this eigenvalue is not integrable near the initial time
t=0
(i.e., if
\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\lambda(t)\mathrm{d}t=\infty
for some
T^{\prime}
).
We can circumvent this by introducing a truncated lower bound using a positive constant
M
.
Setting
\lambda(t)\wedge M:=\min\{\lambda(t),M\}
, the ellipticity bound still holds:

(\lambda(t)\wedge M)|\xi|^{2}\leq a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\quad\text{for all }t\in(0,T)\text{ and }\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

Crucially, this truncated function is locally integrable down to
t=0
, meaning:

\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}(\lambda(t)\wedge M)\mathrm{d}t<\infty\quad\text{for all }T^{\prime}\in(0,T).

Consequently, under Assumption
2.1
, we may assume without loss of generality that
A(t)
possesses a spectral lower-bound that is locally integrable on
[0,T)
.
This integrability property plays a pivotal role in the proofs of Theorem
2.23
and Theorem
2.29
.

To simplify our presentation throughout the remainder of the paper, we introduce the notation

\mu_{a,b,c}(t)=\left(\max_{i,j}|a^{ij}(t)|+\max_{i}|b^{i}(t)|+|c(t)|\right).

With this established, we now state our first main result, which establishes the existence of a weak solution to equation (
1.1
).

Theorem 2.7

(Existence of a weak solution)
.

Suppose
f
is locally integrable on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
and let
p\in[1,\infty]
.
Under Assumptions
2.1
–
2.3
, if the following supplementary integrability conditions are satisfied:

(i)

\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\max_{i,j}|a^{ij}(r)|\mathrm{d}r<\infty

for all
0<s<t<T
,

(ii)

\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s<\infty

for all
t\in(0,T)
,

(iii)

\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t<\infty

for all
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

then there exists a weak solution
u
to equation (
1.1
), which obeys

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s

as well as

\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{a,b,c}(s)\|u(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{a,b,c}(s)\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{r}^{s}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\|f(r,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

for any
t\in(0,T)
.

The proof of this theorem relies on techniques from stochastic calculus. To avoid overwhelming the reader with the extensive mathematical machinery of Itô calculus upfront, we defer the rigorous proof to a later section (see Theorem
3.10
).

Remark 2.8
.

In Theorem
2.7
, the implication
(ii)\implies(iii)
fails because the coefficients are unbounded. Conversely, the reverse implication
(iii)\implies(ii)
is also false, owing to the fact that the coefficients are permitted to be arbitrarily degenerate.

We now turn our attention to the uniqueness of weak solutions to equation (
1.1
).
Although the hypotheses on the source term
f
could technically be relaxed if we were only concerned with uniqueness, discussing uniqueness is vacuous without first ensuring existence.
Therefore, we restrict our focus to function classes where both the existence and uniqueness of a weak solution are simultaneously guaranteed.
For
p\in(1,\infty)
, the
L_{p}
-spaces are reflexive Banach spaces and thus benefit from robust functional analytic properties.
In contrast, the boundary cases
p=1
and
p=\infty
lack reflexivity, introducing notorious technical challenges when handling
L_{p}
-valued functions.
Because these extreme cases are both mathematically demanding and of profound theoretical interest—especially since existence has already been established by the preceding theorem—we choose to first tackle the highly intriguing case of
p=1
.

Theorem 2.9

(Existence and uniqueness of a weak solution in
L_{1}
)
.

Suppose
f
is locally integrable on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
and that Assumptions
2.1
–
2.3
hold.
Assuming further that conditions (i)–(iii) of Theorem
2.7
are met for
p=1
, equation (
1.1
) possesses a unique weak solution
u
belonging to the class of functions defined by the following four properties

(i)

(Spatial Integrability) For any
t\in(0,T)
,

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty.

(ii)

(Weighted Fourier Integrability) For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
, the following integral is finite:

\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\|\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

where
\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)]
denotes the spatial Fourier transform defined by
\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-ix\cdot\xi}u(t,x)\mathrm{d}x
.

(iii)

(Temporal Continuity in Frequency) The map
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)
is continuous on
[0,T)
for almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

(iv)

(Initial vanishing condition) For almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and all
t\in(0,T)
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\exp\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[u(\varepsilon,\cdot)](\xi)=0.

Additionally, this unique solution
u
satisfies

\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{a,b,c}(s)\|u(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{a,b,c}(s)\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{r}^{s}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\|f(r,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

for any
t\in(0,T)
.

For the sake of exposition, we postpone the proof of this theorem. It will be restated as Theorem
4.11
alongside its complete proof.
For
p\in(1,\infty)
, we can also establish both the existence and uniqueness of a weak solution within a slightly different function class.
The corresponding theorem is stated below.

Theorem 2.10

(Existence and uniqueness of a weak solution in
L_{p}
)
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and suppose
f
is a locally integrable function on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
. Assume that Assumptions
2.1
–
2.3

.
Then, there exists a unique weak solution

to equation (

) within the class of functions satisfying

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{\delta}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|u(\delta,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}=0,

and the weighted integrability constraint

Moreover, for any

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{a,b,c}(s)\|u(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}|u(t,\cdot)|_{\mathrm{L}p(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{s}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}|f(s,\cdot)|{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s.

Definition 2.11
.

For
p\in(1,\infty)
, we let
\mathrm{AC}_{0,loc}([0,T);L_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
denote the space of all locally integrable functions
u
on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
that satisfy the following four conditions:

(i)

For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
, the
L_{p}
-norm of
u
is uniformly bounded over
[0,T^{\prime}]
, meaning that

\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|u(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/p}<\infty.

(ii)

For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
, the mapping
t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)
is an absolutely continuous function from
[0,T^{\prime}]
into
L_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
That is, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that for any finite collection of pairwise disjoint subintervals
(s_{i},t_{i})\subset[0,T^{\prime}]
, we have

\sum_{i}(t_{i}-s_{i})<\delta\implies\sum_{i}\|u(t_{i},\cdot)-u(s_{i},\cdot)\|_{L_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\varepsilon.

(iii)

For almost every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
, the temporal mapping
t\mapsto u(t,x)
is a real-valued absolutely continuous function on
[0,T^{\prime}]
.

(iv)

The function vanishes at the initial time in the
L_{p}
-sense, namely

\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}=0.

Remark 2.12
.

Assume
p\geq 1
. Absolutely continuous functions taking values in
\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
possess several significant characteristics.
One primary property is that the absolute continuity of an
\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
-valued function on an interval is directly tied to its norm.
Specifically, the mapping
t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)
is absolutely continuous on
[0,T^{\prime}]
if and only if its real-valued norm
t\mapsto\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}
is absolutely continuous on that same interval.
Furthermore, when
p>1
, the space
\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
possesses the Radon–Nikodým property.
Because of this, any absolutely continuous
\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
-valued function
u
defined on
[0,T^{\prime}]
can be expressed via an integral representation:

u(t)=u(0)+\int_{0}^{t}u_{t}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\quad\forall t\in[0,T^{\prime}].

Here, the equality is understood in the
\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
-sense, and
u_{t}(s)
represents the Fréchet derivative.
If we consider
u
as a function of both time and space on
[0,T^{\prime}]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
, this representation implies that for any given time
t\in[0,T^{\prime}]
, the equation
u(t,x)=u(0,x)+\int_{0}^{t}u_{t}(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}s
holds for almost every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
It is important to note that this pointwise equation does not automatically guarantee that the scalar function
t\mapsto u(t,x)
is absolutely continuous on
[0,T^{\prime}]
for almost every
x
.
To achieve this, a straightforward and sufficient requirement is simply that
t\mapsto u(t,x)
is continuous on
[0,T^{\prime}]
for almost every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
When this continuity is combined with the integral equation, it guarantees that
t\mapsto u(t,x)
is indeed an absolutely continuous real-valued function.
Consequently, the formal definition of the space
\mathrm{AC}_{0,\mathrm{loc}}([0,T);\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
for
p\in(1,\infty)
can be simplified.
The standard condition
(iii)
can be replaced with the less demanding requirement that the mapping
t\mapsto u(t,x)
is continuous on
[0,T^{\prime}]
for almost every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and for all
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
.
For further extensive details on these properties, one can refer to literature such as
[
13
]
, specifically Lemma 1.3.7 and Theorem 2.5.12.

Because singularities in the coefficients complicate the standard analysis of solutions and data in traditional
\mathrm{L}_{p}
-spaces, we address this issue by utilizing weighted
\mathrm{L}_{p}
-spaces instead.
Furthermore, since the coefficients depend entirely on time, the corresponding weights are also defined strictly in terms of the temporal variable.

Definition 2.13

(Temporal weighted
\mathrm{L}_{p,q}
-spaces)
.

To formalize our functional framework, assume
p,q\in[1,\infty]
and let
\mu
represent a non-negative measurable weight function defined almost everywhere on the interval
(0,T)
.
We introduce the space
\mathrm{L}_{q,p,loc}((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu(t)\mathrm{d}t)
to denote the collection of all measurable functions
f
defined
a.e.
on
(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
that satisfy a specific local integrability condition.
Specifically, for any intermediate time
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
, the associated weighted mixed norm must be finite:

\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{q,p}((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu(t)\mathrm{d}t)}:=\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{q}((0,T^{\prime}),\mu(t)\mathrm{d}t;\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))}<\infty.

Explicitly, this norm evaluates to

\begin{cases}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\|f(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right]^{1/q}<\infty&\text{if }q\in[1,\infty),\\
\inf\left\{M:\mu(\{t:\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}>M\})=0\right\}<\infty&\text{if }q=\infty,\end{cases}

where we utilize the measure notation
\mu(\mathrm{d}t)=\mu(t)\mathrm{d}t
.
For the standard unweighted scenario where
\mu(t)=1
a.e. on
(0,T)
, we streamline the notation by dropping the measure.
In this case, we simply write
\mathrm{L}_{q,p,loc}((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d})
for the space itself and
\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{q,p}((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d})}
for its corresponding norm.

Remark 2.14
.

Let
\mu
be a non-negative measurable function defined almost everywhere on
(0,T)
, and let
m(\mathrm{d}t)
represent the standard Lebesgue measure on
\mathbf{R}
.
When equivalence classes are formed based on equality
\mu
-almost everywhere, the space
\mathrm{L}_{q,p,\mathrm{loc}}((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu(t)\mathrm{d}t)
constitutes a Banach space.
However, a complication arises if the weight
\mu(t)
is zero on a set of positive Lebesgue measure, as two functions being equal
\mu
-almost everywhere does not guarantee they are equal
m
-almost everywhere.
Because of this discrepancy, if we define our equivalence classes using
m
-almost everywhere equality, the expression
\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{q,p}((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu(t)\mathrm{d}t)}
functions strictly as a semi-norm rather than a true norm.
To eliminate any potential confusion between these two interpretations, we explicitly reject the
\mu
-almost everywhere convention.
Consequently, for the remainder of the paper, we treat the space
\mathrm{L}_{q,p,\mathrm{loc}}((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu(t)\mathrm{d}t)
as a topological vector space induced by this semi-norm, and we emphasize that every reference to “almost everywhere” or “a.e.” applies exclusively to the standard Lebesgue measure.
Nonetheless, to guarantee the uniqueness of limits, we implicitly pass to the associated quotient space whenever taking limits is required.

Assumption 2.15
.

We assume the coefficient
c(t)
is locally integrable on the interval
[0,T)
, meaning that for every

\int_{0}^{t}|c(s)|\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

Assumption
2.15
imposes a tighter condition compared to Assumption

By operating under this more stringent hypothesis, we can identify a highly precise functional class and derive straightforward criteria for the well-posedness of the problem.
Furthermore, it naturally yields a sharper upper bound for the temporal supremum of

Remark 2.17

Muckenhoupt weights are fundamental tools in both harmonic analysis and the theory of partial differential equations, making the study of weighted estimates for solutions and data a standard practice.
However, as noted earlier, traditional Muckenhoupt estimates are incompatible with our current framework. Instead, we must consider adapted estimates that can handle singular coefficients.
Furthermore, because incorporating spatial weights introduces significant technical complexity, we defer their analysis for now and focus exclusively on one-dimensional Muckenhoupt weights applied to the time variable.
While comprehensive details on the properties of these weights can be found in literature such as
[
14
]
, we will limit our current discussion to recalling their formal definition and providing a fundamental example.

Definition 2.19

(Muckenhoupt’s weight)
.

For any
p\in(1,\infty)
, the notation
\mathrm{A}_{p}(\mathbf{R})
designates the set of all non-negative, locally integrable functions
w
defined on
\mathbf{R}
that satisfy the Muckenhoupt condition

[w]_{\mathrm{A}_{p}(\mathbf{R})}:=\sup_{-\infty<a<b<\infty}\left(\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}w(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\left(\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}w(t)^{-1/(p-1)}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{p-1}<\infty.

A direct consequence of this finiteness condition is that
w(t)>0
almost everywhere on
\mathbf{R}
.

Remark 2.20
.

A straightforward and notable example of an
\mathrm{A}_{p}(\mathbf{R})
-weight is the absolute value function raised to a restricted power.
Specifically, it is a well-established result that the function
|t|^{\beta}
belongs to the Muckenhoupt class
\mathrm{A}_{p}(\mathbf{R})
provided the exponent satisfies the condition
-1<\beta<p-1
(
cf.

[
14
, Example 7.1.7]
).

Next, we introduce Sobolev derivatives via a weak formulation. While multiple approaches exist for defining these derivatives, we deliberately adopt this specific method.
We acknowledge that a comprehensive review of these elementary concepts may seem overly exhaustive to some readers.
However, we have chosen to present them in full due to the non-standard nature of our weighted Sobolev spaces.
Furthermore, within this generalized framework, the Sobolev derivatives emerge as locally integrable functions, demanding a level of rigorous treatment that is not typically found in conventional literature.

Definition 2.21

(Sobolev derivative)
.

Let
\alpha
be a multi-index and suppose
v
is a locally integrable function on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
, meaning that for any finite radius
r>0
, the following holds:

\int_{|x|<r}|v(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

We define a locally integrable function
v^{\alpha}
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
to be the Sobolev derivative (or weak derivative) of
v
with respect to the multi-index
\alpha
if it satisfies the following integration-by-parts relation for every test function
\varphi\in\mathrm{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
:

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}v(x)D^{\alpha}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}v^{\alpha}(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

It is straightforward to verify that this Sobolev derivative is unique up to a set of measure zero.
That is, if
v_{1}^{\alpha}
and
v_{2}^{\alpha}
are both Sobolev derivatives of
v
for the same multi-index
\alpha
, then
v_{1}^{\alpha}(x)=v_{2}^{\alpha}(x)
almost everywhere on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
We standardly denote this unique equivalence class by
D^{\alpha}v
.
For lower-order derivatives where
|\alpha|=1
or
2
, we adopt conventional subscript notation, utilizing
v_{x^{i}}
and
v_{x^{i}x^{j}}
instead of
D^{\alpha}v
(for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
).
Correspondingly,
v_{x}
represents the gradient vector, and
v_{xx}
denotes the Hessian matrix composed of these second-order Sobolev derivatives.
Because weak mixed partial derivatives commute (
v_{x^{i}x^{j}}=v_{x^{j}x^{i}}
), it immediately follows that the Hessian matrix
v_{xx}
is symmetric.

Definition 2.22

(Temporal weighted Sobolev spaces)
.

Let
p,q\in[1,\infty)
and assume
\mu
is a non-negative, almost-everywhere defined measurable function on the interval
(0,T)
.
We define the space
\mathrm{H}^{2}_{q,p,loc}((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu(t)\mathrm{d}t)
to be the set of all measurable functions
u
on
(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
that exhibit finite weighted integrability up to their second-order spatial weak derivatives.
Explicitly, a function
u
belongs to this space if, for every truncation time
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
, its corresponding norm is finite:

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{2}_{q,p}((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu(t)\mathrm{d}t)}

\displaystyle:=\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\left(\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}+\|u_{x}(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}+\|u_{xx}(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\right)\mu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right]^{1/q}<\infty.

In this notation,
u_{x}(t,\cdot)
and
u_{xx}(t,\cdot)
denote the spatial gradient vector and the spatial Hessian matrix, respectively, which are assembled from their Sobolev partial derivatives.

We now establish a maximal
\mathrm{L}_{p}
-regularity estimate for equation (
1.1
).

Remark 2.24
.

Generally, the integral in Theorem
2.23
should not be viewed as a standard Lebesgue integral. Because
\lambda(t)
may vanish on a set of positive measure, the expression must be interpreted as an improper integral, as formally defined in (
2.3

This distinction explains why we provide the integral in its expanded form within the estimates rather than using the shorthand norm notation associated with weighted
\mathrm{L}_{q,p}
-spaces; the latter typically presumes a standard Lebesgue measure that might not be applicable here.

To demonstrate the scope and generality of our results, we present two primary examples.
These cases focus on the leading coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
and serve as straightforward yet powerful illustrations of the diverse types of singularities accommodated by our framework.

Example 2.25
.

A straightforward illustration is provided by the leading coefficients
a^{ij}(t)=\left(t^{\beta_{1}}+(T-t)^{\beta_{2}}\right)\delta^{ij}
for
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbf{R}
. In this expression,
\delta^{ij}
represents the Kronecker delta, which is equal to
1
when
i=j
and
0
otherwise.

Example 2.26
.

It is straightforward to verify that our coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
may exhibit exponential growth, as we impose no integrability constraints at the initial or terminal times.
For example, the coefficients defined by

a^{ij}(t)=\left(\exp\left(\mathrm{e}^{1/t}\right)+\exp\left(\mathrm{e}^{1/(T-t)}\right)\right)\delta^{ij}

are consistent with the requirements of Assumption
2.1
.

Our analysis will further explore the existence and properties of strong solutions for (

The specific definition of a strong solution used in this study is provided below.

Definition 2.27

(Strong solution)
.

Let
f
be a locally integrable function on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
and let
u
be a weak solution to (
1.1
).
Suppose that for almost every
t\in(0,T)
and all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}

u(t,\cdot)

u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,\cdot)
and
u_{x^{i}}(t,\cdot)

u

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(a^{ij}(s)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,x)+b^{i}(s)u_{x^{i}}(s,x)+c(s)u(s,x)+f(s,x)\right)\mathrm{d}s.

(2.9)

Remark 2.28
.

According to Definition
2.27
, a strong solution

to (
1.1

While one might assume that any strong solution naturally satisfies the weak formulation without requiring explicit enforcement, proving this directly from the integral identity (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|<r}\left(|a^{ij}(s)||u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,x)|+|b^{i}(s)||u_{x^{i}}(s,x)|+|c(s)||u(s,x)|\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}s<\infty,

(2.10)

When this holds for every

and

In our framework, however, the presence of significant singularities means this local integrability cannot be guaranteed.
Consequently, to ensure validity, we must explicitly include the weak solution property as a foundational requirement in the definition of a strong solution.

Theorem 2.29

(Well-posedness of a strong solution)
.

Let
p,q\in(1,\infty)
,
f\in\mathrm{L}_{1,p,loc}\left((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\right)

w\in\mathrm{A}_{q}(\mathbf{R})
.
Suppose that Assumption
2.1
, Assumption
2.2
, and Assumption
2.15
hold.
Additionally, assume that for all
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\left(w(\alpha(t))\lambda(t)\right)^{-1/(q-1)}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\left(w(\alpha(t)+\delta t)(\lambda(t)+\delta)\right)^{-1/(q-1)}\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}^{q}\mathrm{e}^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}w(\alpha(t))(\lambda(t))^{1-q}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}^{q}\mathrm{e}^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}w(\alpha(t)+\delta t)(\lambda(t)+\delta)^{1-q}\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

\displaystyle\lambda(t)|\xi|^{2}\leq a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\quad\text{a.e.}~t\in(0,T)~\text{and}~\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

Then there exists a strong solution

\mathrm{AC}_{0,loc}\left([0,T);\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\right)

\mathrm{L}_{1,p,loc}\left((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right),

\mathrm{L}_{\infty,p,loc}\left((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}t\right),

and

\mathrm{H}^{2}_{q,p,loc}\left((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu_{c,\lambda}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle\mu_{c,\lambda}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}w(\alpha(t))\lambda(t).

Moreover, the solution

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\right)\leq\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1,p}\left((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}t\right)},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}_{q,p}\left((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}|c(t)|\mathrm{d}t}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{q,p}\left((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)},

\displaystyle\|u_{t}\|^{q}_{\mathrm{L}_{q,p}\left((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu_{c,\lambda}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)}+\|u\|^{q}_{\mathrm{H}^{2}_{q,p}\left((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mu_{c,\lambda}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,p,q,w}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}^{q}\mathrm{e}^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}w(\alpha(t))(\lambda(t))^{1-q}\mathrm{d}t,

and

\displaystyle\|u_{t}\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1,p}\left((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,p,q,w}\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\left(w(\alpha(t))\lambda(t)\right)^{-1/(q-1)}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}^{q}\mathrm{e}^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}w(\alpha(t))(\lambda(t))^{1-q}\mathrm{d}t.

(2.13)

Similarly, the proof of Theorem
2.23
is provided in Section
6
.

Remark 2.30
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
2.29
, it is straightforward to verify that any strong solution
u
is also a weak solution in the sense of Theorem
2.10
.
Consequently, the uniqueness of the strong solution within the class defined by Theorem
2.29
follows directly from Theorem
2.10
.

Remark 2.31
.

Since the solution
u
belongs to the class
\mathrm{AC}_{0,loc}([0,T);\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
, the term
u_{t}
in (
2.13
) is well-defined as an
\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
-valued Fréchet derivative.
Furthermore, one can readily verify that

coincides with the Sobolev derivative by applying the fundamental theorem of calculus in conjunction with integration by parts.

Remark 2.32
.

Condition (
2.11
) is imposed to ensure the well-posedness of the strong solution in Theorem
2.29
. Clearly, (
2.11
) is satisfied if all coefficients are bounded and
a^{ij}(t)
is uniformly elliptic; that is, there exists a constant
\kappa\in(0,1)
such that

\kappa|\xi|^{2}\leq a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}|\xi|^{2}\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\text{ and }t\in(0,T).

Furthermore, condition (
2.11
) guarantees the local integrability of the coefficients on
[0,T)
. To see this, let
\delta>0
and
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
. By applying Hölder’s inequality and a straightforward change of variables, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\left(w(\alpha(t)+\delta t)(\lambda(t)+\delta)\right)^{-1/q}\left(w(\alpha(t)+\delta t)(\lambda(t)+\delta)\right)^{1/q}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\left(w(\alpha(t)+\delta t)(\lambda(t)+\delta)\right)^{-1/(q-1)}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{q-1}{q}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\int_{0}^{\alpha(T^{\prime})+\delta T^{\prime}}w(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/q}.

Letting
\delta\downarrow 0
, it follows that for any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\left(w(\alpha(t))\lambda(t)\right)^{-1/(q-1)}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{q-1}{q}}.

Consequently, the local integrability of the coefficients on
[0,T)
, namely that

\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

is a necessary requirement to establish the existence and uniqueness of the strong solution to (
1.1
) in Theorem
2.29
.

Although the local integrability of the coefficients on
[0,T)
is a prerequisite for applying our theorem, our condition is robust enough to capture compelling, non-trivial scenarios well beyond standard uniform ellipticity. We provide an illustrative example below to demonstrate this.

Example 2.33
.

Let
-\infty<a_{1}\leq a_{2}<1
.
We investigate existence and uniqueness of a strong solution to the following simple equation:

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)=t^{-a_{1}}u_{x^{1}x^{1}}(t,x)+t^{-a_{2}}u_{x^{2}x^{2}}(t,x)+f(t,x),

\displaystyle u(0,x)=0,\qquad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{2}

(2.14)

We establish the existence of a unique strong solution to (
2.14
) in a weighted
\mathrm{L}_{p,q}
-space, provided
f
satisfies an appropriate condition.
For the special case where
a_{1}=a_{2}
and
f
is smooth, this result is already known (see,
e.g.
,
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
).
Recalling Remark
2.20
and the notation from Theorem
2.29
, we set

A(t)=(a_{ij}(t))=\begin{bmatrix}t^{-a_{1}}&0\\
0&t^{-a_{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

For all
t\in(0,T)
, we define
\lambda(t)=t^{-a_{1}}
,
\alpha(t)=\frac{1}{1-a_{1}}t^{1-a_{1}}
,
b^{i}(t)=c(t)=0
, and the weight
w(t)=t^{\beta}
with
-1<\beta<q-1
.
Then, for any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
, the integrability condition

\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\left(w(\alpha(t))\lambda(t)\right)^{-1/(q-1)}\mathrm{d}t<\infty

is equivalent to

\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}t^{\frac{-a_{2}q-(1-a_{1})\beta+a_{1}}{q-1}}\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

Clearly, for any
q\in(1,\infty)
, we can ensure that the exponent satisfies

\frac{-a_{2}q-(1-a_{1})\beta+a_{1}}{q-1}>-1

by choosing
\beta
sufficiently close to
-1
.
Therefore, applying Theorem
2.29
with the corresponding condition on

, we conclude that there exists a unique strong solution to (
2.14
) that satisfies all the estimates detailed in the theorem.

Remark 2.34
.

The non-zero initial value problem associated with (
1.1
) is generally ill-posed because the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
blow up near the initial time
t=0
. To briefly illustrate this, consider the non-zero initial value problem:

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)u_{x^{i}x^{i}}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}

\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}.

Formally, the solution
u
can be represented either via convolution with a fundamental solution,

u(t,x)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}p(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)\mathrm{d}y,

or through a stochastic representation,

u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\left[u_{0}\left(x+\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{A(s)}\mathrm{dB}_{s}\right)\right].

Here,
\mathrm{B}_{t}
denotes a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion,
\sqrt{A(s)}
is the positive semi-definite matrix satisfying
\sqrt{A(s)}\sqrt{A(s)}=A(s)=(a^{ij}(s))
, and the heat kernel is given by

p(t,x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\exp\left(-\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\int_{0}^{t}a^{ij}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right)\right](x).

Since the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
fail to be integrable near the initial time
t=0
, both the deterministic integral
\int_{0}^{t}a^{ij}(s)\mathrm{d}s
and the stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{A(s)}\mathrm{dB}_{s}
generally diverge.
This phenomenon is illustrated concretely in Examples
2.25
and
2.26
.
To provide better intuition for why equation (
1.1
) is ill-posed when the initial condition
u_{0}
is non-zero, we can look at a simplified scenario.
For convenience, let’s restrict the problem to one spatial dimension (
d=1
) and assume that the coefficient
a(t)
has a divergent integral over the interval:

\int_{0}^{T}a(s)\mathrm{d}s=\infty.

Suppose
u
is a weak solution to the following initial value problem:

u_{t}(t,x)=a(t)u_{xx}(t,x)

u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad\text{where }(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}.

Assume that the mapping
x\mapsto u(t,x)
belongs to
L_{p}(\mathbf{R})
for some
p\in[1,2]
.
By utilizing mollifiers, we can assume without loss of generality that
u
is spatially smooth and qualifies as a strong solution.
This allows us to express the solution in its integral form:

u(t,x)=u_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)u_{xx}(s,x)\mathrm{d}s.

Applying the spatial Fourier transform to both sides yields a first-order ordinary differential equation with respect to time for each frequency
\xi
:

\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)=-a(t)\xi^{2}\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)\quad\text{for all }(t,\xi)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}.

If we solve this ODE starting from an arbitrary base time
t_{0}\in(0,T)
, we get

\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)=\mathcal{F}[u(t_{0},\cdot)](\xi)\exp\left(-|\xi|^{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}a(s)\mathrm{d}s\right)\quad\text{for all }t\in(0,T).

Now, taking the limit as
t
approaches
0
from above, we find

\mathcal{F}[u_{0}](\xi)=\mathcal{F}[u(t_{0},\cdot)](\xi)\exp\left(|\xi|^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}a(s)\mathrm{d}s\right)=\infty.

Because the integral of
a(s)
diverges, the exponential term blows up to infinity. In order to prevent the initial data
\mathcal{F}[u_{0}](\xi)
from evaluating to infinity, it forces the condition
\mathcal{F}[u(t_{0},\cdot)](\xi)=0
.
Since the choice of
t_{0}
was arbitrary, the solution must be identically zero (
u=0
), which consequently means
u_{0}
must also be zero. Therefore, the problem cannot be well-posed for any non-zero initial condition.

3.
Itô calculus for existence of a solution

Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathrm{P})
be a probability space equipped with a filtration
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
satisfying the usual conditions; that is, each
\mathscr{F}_{t}
is a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathscr{F}
, the filtration is right-continuous (
\mathscr{F}_{t}=\bigcap_{s>t}\mathscr{F}_{s}
), and
\mathscr{F}_{0}
contains all
\mathrm{P}
-null sets.
We assume the existence of a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion (or Wiener process)
\mathrm{B}_{t}=(\mathrm{B}^{1}_{t},\ldots,\mathrm{B}^{d}_{t})
that is adapted to
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, meaning
\mathrm{B}_{t}
is
\mathscr{F}_{t}
-measurable for every
t\geq 0
.
For a comprehensive background on Itô calculus, we refer readers to standard texts such as
[
15
,
20
,
22
,
27
]
.
However, we emphasize that our current framework does not require the full generality of Itô integrals with predictable or progressively measurable random integrands.
Instead, our analysis is strictly confined to Itô integrals with deterministic integrands.
For any deterministic
d\times d
-matrix-valued function
\sigma(t)=(\sigma^{ij}(t))
satisfying

\int_{0}^{\infty}|\sigma(t)|^{2}\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

the Itô stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(t)\mathrm{dB}_{t}
is well-defined as an element of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d})
via the Itô isometry.
This integral is an
\mathbf{R}^{d}
-valued random variable whose
i
-th component is given by the scalar Itô integral
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma^{ij}(t)\mathrm{dB}^{j}_{t}
.
Furthermore, if
\sigma
is locally square-integrable such that

\int_{s}^{t}|\sigma(r)|^{2}\mathrm{d}r<\infty\quad\text{for all }0<s<t<T,

we define the truncated Itô integral for
0<s\leq t<T
as

\int_{s}^{t}\sigma(r)\mathrm{dB}_{r}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{(s,t)}(r)\sigma(r)\mathrm{dB}_{r}.

By Doob’s martingale inequality, this stochastic integral admits a continuous modification. Specifically, for any fixed
s\in(0,T)
and almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, the sample path mapping
t\in[s,\infty)\mapsto\int_{s}^{t}\sigma(r)\mathrm{dB}_{r}(\omega)
is a continuous trajectory.
The primary methodological tool in our analysis is Itô’s formula, which requires functions to be twice continuously differentiable.
Therefore, our strategy is to first establish the definition of a classical solution and apply Itô’s formula directly to it.
Following standard techniques in the literature, we will then construct our general weak solutions by approximating them with sequences of these classical solutions.

Definition 3.1

(Classical solution)
.

Let
u
be a locally integrable function on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
We say that
u
is a classical solution to (
1.1
) if for all
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(a^{ij}(s)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,x)+b^{i}(s)u_{x^{i}}(s,x)+c(s)u(s,x)+f(t,x)\right)\mathrm{d}s.

(3.1)

Here
u_{x^{i}x^{j}}
and
u_{x^{i}}
are classical derivatives,
i.e.

\displaystyle u_{x^{i}}(t,x)=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{u(t,x+he_{i})-u(t,x)}{h},

where
e_{i}
is the standard basis vector in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
whose
i
-th coordinate is 1 and the other coordinates are zero.

Remark 3.2
.

It is necessary to clarify a subtle distinction between our concept of a classical solution and the conventional definition, particularly regarding differentiability in the temporal variable.
Suppose
u
is a classical solution to (
1.1
) according to our definition. Because (
3.1
) guarantees that
u
is absolutely continuous with respect to
t
, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus applies in the temporal variable.
Consequently, the Lebesgue differentiation theorem ensures that
u
is differentiable with respect to
t
almost everywhere on
(0,T)
, yielding

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+b^{i}(t)u_{x^{i}}(t,x)+c(t)u(t,x)

(3.2)

for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
However, without assuming that the coefficients are continuous, we generally cannot expect this equation to hold for every
t\in(0,T)
.
Because the coefficients are highly general, the right-hand side of (
3.2
) may not satisfy Darboux’s property (the intermediate value property for derivatives).
By Darboux’s theorem, this implies that no function
u
can possess an everywhere-defined time derivative that satisfies (
3.2
) pointwise on all of
(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
Therefore, our formulation in Definition
3.1
represents the highest level of regularity achievable given the inherent irregularity of the coefficients.
For this reason, it is thoroughly justified to refer to it as a classical solution to (
1.1
).

Remark 3.3
.

Even when considering a classical solution
u
to (
1.1
), it is impossible to guarantee that the classical derivatives
u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)
and
u_{x^{i}}(t,x)
exist uniformly for every
t\in(0,T)
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
This limitation arises mainly because the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
and
b^{i}(t)
are permitted to vanish on certain subsets of
(0,T)
, which potentially disrupts the regularity of the solution.

Remark 3.4
.

Suppose that for all
t\in(0,T)
and
r\in(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|<r}\left(|a^{ij}(s)||u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,x)|+|b^{i}(s)||u_{x^{i}}(s,x)|+|c(s)||u(s,x)|\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

Due to the local integrability, any existing classical derivatives coincide with their corresponding Sobolev derivatives. Consequently, every classical solution
u
to (
1.1
) is automatically a strong solution. Furthermore, as discussed in Remark
2.28
, any such classical solution simultaneously qualifies as a weak solution.

Recall that the function defined by

\displaystyle u(t,x):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(s,x+X_{s,t}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}s

(3.3)

typically constitutes a classical solution to (
1.1
), where
X_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}b(r)\mathrm{d}r+\int_{s}^{t}\sigma(r)\mathrm{dB}_{r}
,
\sigma(r)=\sqrt{2}\sqrt{A(r)}
,
b(t)=\left(b^{1}(t),\ldots,b^{d}(t)\right)
, and
A(r)=(a^{ij}(r))
(
cf.

[
20
,
17
,
2
]
).

However, our framework allows all coefficients to be unbounded or even non-integrable, a rigorous justification is required to show that expression (
3.3
) still provides a classical solution to equation (
1.1
).

Theorem 3.5

(Existence of a classical solution)
.

Assume that Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
are satisfied.
Furthermore, suppose the leading coefficients satisfy the following local integrability condition for any
0<s\leq t<T
,

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\max_{i,j}|a^{ij}(r)|\mathrm{d}r<\infty.

(3.4)

Let
f
be a locally integrable source function on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
that fulfills the following regularity and weighted integrability criteria:

(i)

(Spatial Smoothness) For every
t\in(0,T)
, the spatial mapping
x\mapsto f(t,x)
is twice continuously differentiable.

(ii)

(Zeroth-Order Integrability) For any truncation time
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}|c(t)|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(iii)

(First-Order Integrability)
For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
, the double integral involving the drift coefficients and the gradient is finite:

\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}|b^{i}(t)|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f_{x^{i}}(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(iv)

(Pointwise Gradient Bound)
The spatial gradient is essentially bounded in time, meaning
\|f_{x}(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty
for almost every
t\in(0,T)
.

(v)

(Second-Order Integrability) For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
, the double integral involving the diffusion coefficients and the Hessian is finite:

\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}|a^{ij}(t)|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

Under these conditions, the function
u
explicitly defined in (
3.3
) constitutes a classical solution to equation (
1.1
).

Proof.

We divide proof into several parts.

I. Applying Itô’s Formula and the Martingale Property

To begin, let
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and fix a time
s\in(0,T)
such that the spatial gradient is essentially bounded

\|f_{x}(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty.

By applying Itô’s formula to the exponentially weighted process, we obtain the following expansion for any
t\in[s,T)
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}f\left(s,x+X_{s,t}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}f\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+f(s,x)+\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}b^{i}(r)f_{x^{i}}\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}f_{x^{i}}\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\sigma^{ij}(r)\mathrm{dB}^{j}_{r}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}a^{ij}(r)f_{x^{i}x^{j}}\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\mathrm{d}r.

(3.5)

Based on condition (iv) and estimate (
3.4
), we can naturally bound the quadratic variation of the stochastic integral

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\left|\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}f_{x^{i}}(s,x+X_{s,r})\sigma^{ij}(r)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f_{x}(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{2\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\max_{i,j}|a^{ij}(r)|\mathrm{d}r<\infty.

Because this integral is finite, the stochastic integral qualifies as a true martingale, which guarantees that its expected value vanishes,
i.e.

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}f_{x^{i}}\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\sigma^{ij}(r)\mathrm{dB}^{j}_{r}\right]=0.

II. Deriving the Expected Value Equation

Taking the expectation of both sides of (
3.5
) and utilizing Fubini’s theorem to commute the expectation with the time integrals, we arrive at the following relation.
By assumptions
(i)\sim(v)
in the theorem, for almost
s\in(0,T)
and for all
t\in[s,T)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(s,x+X_{s,t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=f(s,x)+\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}b^{i}(r)\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{x^{i}}\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}a^{ij}(r)\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{x^{i}x^{j}}\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}r.

(3.6)

III. Integration and Recovering the Classical Solution

For any fixed
t\in(0,T)
, the identity in (
3.6
) remains valid for almost every
s\in(0,t]
. By integrating both sides from
0
to
t
with respect to
s
, and invoking Fubini’s theorem a second time to swap the order of integration, we find

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(s,x+X_{s,t}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}f(s,x)\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}b^{i}(r)\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{x^{i}}\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}a^{ij}(r)\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{x^{i}x^{j}}\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}f(s,x)\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}c(r)\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}b^{i}(r)\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{x^{i}}\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}a^{ij}(r)\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{x^{i}x^{j}}\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(a^{ij}(r)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(r,x)+b^{i}(r)u_{x^{i}}(r,x)+c(r)u(r,x)+f(r,x)\right)\mathrm{d}r.

(3.7)

This sequence of equalities demonstrates that
u
is a classical solution to equation (
1.1
).

IV. Rigorous Justification of Fubini’s Theorem

To make this derivation completely rigorous, we must formally justify our use of Fubini’s theorem by verifying that the corresponding absolute integrals are finite. Applying Tonelli’s theorem alongside our initial assumptions on
f
, we can bound the terms as follows

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}|c(r)\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(s,x+X_{s,r}\right)\right]|\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}\left|c(r)\right|\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left|c(r)\right|\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}r<\infty,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}\left|b^{i}(r)\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{x^{i}}(s,x+X_{s,r})\right]\right|\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|b^{i}(r)\right|\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\|f_{x^{i}}(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}r<\infty,

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}\left|a^{ij}(r)\mathbb{E}\left[f_{x^{i}x^{j}}(r,x+X_{s,r})\right]\right|\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\left|a^{ij}(r)\right|\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\|f_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}r<\infty.

Finally, the initial source integral
\int_{0}^{t}f(s,x)\mathrm{d}s
is guaranteed to be well-defined because

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}|f(s,x)|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

Since all of these bounding integrals are finite,
u(t,x)
is rigorously well-defined for all
(t,x)\in(0,T)
as established by the relationships in (
3.7
).

Remark 3.7
.

We now clarify why conditions
(iii)
and
(iv)
are stated separately in the preceding theorem.
Assuming

holds, we have

|b^{i}(t)|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f_{x^{i}}(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s<\infty\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in(0,T).

Corollary 3.9

Supremum Bound

Proof.

We proceed by a standard regularization argument using a Sobolev mollifier.

(Derivative Bounds) For any

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(u^{(\varepsilon)}(s,\cdot),a^{ij}(s)\varphi_{x^{i}x^{j}}-b^{i}(s)\varphi_{x^{i}}+c(s)\varphi\right)_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left(f^{(\varepsilon)}(s,\cdot),\varphi\right)_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}ds\quad\text{a.e.}~t\in(0,T).

Finally, we pass to the limit as

. Utilizing the strong convergence properties of the mollifiers alongside the integrability conditions on

:

\displaystyle(u(t,\cdot),\varphi)_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(u(s,\cdot),a^{ij}(s)\varphi_{x^{i}x^{j}}-b^{i}(s)\varphi_{x^{i}}+c(s)\varphi\right)_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left(f(s,\cdot),\varphi\right)_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}ds\quad\text{a.e.}~t\in(0,T).

This confirms that
u
is indeed a weak solution to (
1.1
), thereby completing the proof of the primary statement.

It remains only to verify the additional statements regarding the solution’s bounds and temporal regularity.
First, establishing the supremum bound in (
3.13
) is straightforward; this inequality follows as a direct and immediate consequence of combining (
3.10
) with the local integrability condition (
3.12
).
Next, we establish the absolute continuity of the map
t\mapsto\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}
.
By utilizing the generalized Minkowski inequality in conjunction with the translation invariance of the
\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
-norm, we can bound the variation of the norm between any two times
0<s<t<T^{\prime}<T
:

\displaystyle\left|\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}-\|u(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{r}^{t}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(r,\cdot\right)\right]\mathrm{d}r\right\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}-\left\|\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{r}^{s}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(r,\cdot\right)\right]\mathrm{d}r\right\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left(\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}-\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{s}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\right)\right|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\left\|f\left(r,\cdot\right)\right\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{s}|c(\rho)|\mathrm{d}\rho}\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\left\|f\left(r,\cdot\right)\right\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}r.

The upper bound is easily controlled because the integrals of locally integrable functions are absolutely continuous.
From this, it immediately follows that the temporal mapping
t\mapsto\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}
is also absolutely continuous on
[0,T^{\prime}]
.

Remark 3.11
.

Suppose
u
is the weak solution established in Theorem
3.10
.
As demonstrated in the theorem’s proof, its Sobolev mollification
u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)
acts as a classical solution to equation (
1.1
) corresponding to the regularized source term
f^{(\varepsilon)}
.

Remark 3.12
.

Having established existence, it is natural to address the uniqueness of the solution.
However, the classical solution
u
derived in Theorem
3.5
does not possess sufficient regularity for a direct application of Itô’s formula.
A standard uniqueness proof using Itô’s formula demands that the solution belong to
C^{1,2}([0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d})
—requiring continuous differentiability once in time and twice in space.
Because our solution lacks a guaranteed continuous time derivative
u_{t}
, a rigorous application of Itô’s formula is precluded.
To overcome this limitation, the subsequent sections will introduce alternative strategies to prove uniqueness.

4.
Fourier transform method for uniqueness of a weak solution

In this section, we establish the uniqueness of the solution by utilizing a classical approach based on the Fourier transform.
This approach is highly beneficial when the inhomogeneous term is an
\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
-valued function for
p\in[1,2]
.
We begin by briefly recalling the foundational definitions and properties of the Fourier transform.
For any function
f\in\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, its Fourier transform is given by

\mathcal{F}[f](\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-ix\cdot\xi}f(x)\mathrm{d}x,\quad\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

Directly from this definition, we obtain the uniform bound

\|\mathcal{F}[f]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}.

Furthermore, Plancherel’s theorem guarantees that

\|\mathcal{F}[f]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}=\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\quad\forall f\in\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\cap\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d}).

Due to the completeness of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, this equality allows the Fourier transform to be uniquely extended as an isometry on
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
By interpolating between these
\mathrm{L}_{1}
and
\mathrm{L}_{2}
estimates using the Riesz–Thorin theorem, the Fourier transform can be extended to a bounded operator from
\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})+\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
into
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})+\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
Most notably, for any
p\in[1,2]
, we obtain the Hausdorff–Young inequality

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}[f]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p/(p-1)}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{d(2-p)}{2p}}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\quad\forall f\in\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),

(4.1)

where we adopt the convention
1/0:=\infty
.
Consequently, for any
p\in[1,2]
and
f\in\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}[f]
is well-defined as a function in the conjugate space
\mathrm{L}_{p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with
p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}
.
In particular, this guarantees that
\mathcal{F}[f]
is locally integrable.
We refer the reader to
[
14
]
for a comprehensive treatment of these details.
Relying on these properties in conjunction with a suitable approximation scheme, we arrive at the following identity.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p\in[1,2]
,
f\in\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, and
\varphi\in\mathrm{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
Then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}f(x)\varphi(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}[f](\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}[\varphi](\xi)}\mathrm{d}x.

We are now in a position to establish a fundamental lemma that guarantees the uniqueness of a solution
u
to (
1.1
) within a specific function class, provided the source term
f
belongs to
\mathrm{L}_{1,p}((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d})
for
p\in[1,2]
.

Throughout this section, the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
,
b^{i}(t)
, and
c(t)
are considered merely measurable functions unless otherwise specified.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume
p\in[1,2]
and let
f\in\mathrm{L}_{1,p}((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
Suppose
u
is a locally integrable function on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
that serves as a weak solution to (
1.1
).
We further impose the following three regularity and integrability conditions on
u
:

(i)

(Spatial
\mathrm{L}_{p}
-Bound) For any
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty.

(ii)

(Weighted Fourier Integrability) For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\|\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

(4.2)

where
p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}
denotes the Hölder conjugate of
p
(adopting the convention
1/0:=\infty
).

(iii)

(Temporal Continuity in Frequency) For almost every frequency
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the temporal mapping
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)
is continuous over the interval
[0,T)
.

Under these assumptions, the Fourier transform of the solution satisfies the following integral identity for almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and all
t\in[0,T)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathcal{F}[u(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s.

(4.3)

Proof.

Leveraging the Fourier estimate (
4.1
) alongside our assumptions regarding
u
and
f
, one can readily verify that both sides of (
4.3
) are continuous with respect to
t\in(0,T)
for almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
Consequently, it suffices to prove that for any fixed
t\in(0,T)
, the following equality holds for almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathcal{F}[u(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s.

(4.4)

We organize the remainder of the proof into several distinct parts.

I. Transitioning to the Frequency Domain

By the standard definition of a weak solution, for any
t\in(0,T)
and test function
\varphi\in\mathrm{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}u(t,x)\varphi(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}u(s,x)\left(a^{ij}(s)\varphi_{x^{i}x^{j}}(x)-b^{i}(s)\varphi_{x^{i}}(x)+c(s)\varphi(x)\right)\mathrm{d}xds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}f(s,x)\varphi(x)\mathrm{d}xds.

Applying Lemma
4.1
and utilizing the standard properties of the Fourier transform (which convert spatial derivatives into algebraic multipliers), we rewrite this equation in the frequency domain. This yields the integral identity:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}[\varphi](\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}[u(s,\cdot)](\xi)\overline{\left(a^{ij}(s)\mathcal{F}[\varphi_{x^{i}x^{j}}](\xi)-b^{i}(s)\mathcal{F}[\varphi_{x^{i}}](\xi)+c(s)\mathcal{F}[\varphi](\xi)\right)}\mathrm{d}\xi ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)]\overline{\mathcal{F}[\varphi](\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}[u(s,\cdot)](\xi)\left(\overline{-\left(a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\mathcal{F}[\varphi](\xi)-\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}\mathcal{F}[\varphi](\xi)+c(s)\mathcal{F}[\varphi](\xi)\right)}\right)\mathrm{d}\xi ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)]\overline{\mathcal{F}[\varphi](\xi)}\mathrm{d}xds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathcal{F}[u(s,\cdot)](\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}[\varphi](\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)]\overline{\mathcal{F}[\varphi](\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi ds

(4.5)

for all
t\in(0,T)
and
\varphi\in\mathrm{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

II. Constructing the Approximation

To extract the pointwise equality, we fix a symmetric test function
\psi\in\mathrm{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that
\psi(y)=\psi(-y)
and normalized to satisfy
\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\psi(y)\mathrm{d}y=(2\pi)^{d/2}
.
For any scaling parameter
\varepsilon>0
, we define the rescaled function

\psi^{(\varepsilon)}(y)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\psi(y/\varepsilon).

III. Applying Fubini and Fourier Inversion Theorems

We substitute the specific shifted test function
\phi=\psi^{(\varepsilon)}(x-\cdot)
into (
4.5
). Recalling that
\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}(x-\cdot)](\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi\cdot y}\psi^{(\varepsilon)}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y
, and justifying the swap of integration order via Fubini’s theorem (permitted by condition (
4.2
)), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}(\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}(x-\cdot)](\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathcal{F}[u(s,\cdot)](\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}(x-\cdot)](\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)]\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}(x-\cdot)](\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}\int_{0}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathcal{F}[u(s,\cdot)](\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}](\xi)}ds\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)]\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}](\xi)}ds\mathrm{d}\xi.

By applying the Fourier inversion theorem, we can strip away the outer integral against
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}
to establish a pointwise equality valid for almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}(x-\cdot)](\xi)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathcal{F}[u(s,\cdot)](\xi)ds\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}](\xi)}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)]ds\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}](\xi)}.

IV. Passing to the Limit

Finally, we analyze the behavior as the scaling parameter vanishes. Observe that
\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi^{(\varepsilon)}](\xi)}=\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi](\varepsilon\xi)}
.
Because of our initial normalization, the value at the origin is strictly
\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi](0)}=1
.
By evaluating along a sequence
\varepsilon=1/n
and taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, the multiplier
\overline{\mathcal{F}[\psi](\varepsilon\xi)}
converges to 1.
This limiting process directly yields the desired identity (
4.4
).

\Box

Remark 4.3
.

The pointwise requirement in condition
(i)
of Lemma
4.2
could technically be relaxed to hold merely for almost every
t
, such that

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty.

Nevertheless, because the solutions we examine are inherently continuous in time, this subtle theoretical distinction is of little practical consequence in our context.

Proposition 4.4

(Representation of a weak solution)
.

Let the hypotheses of Lemma
4.2
be satisfied.
Furthermore, assume that the following initial asymptotic condition holds for almost every frequency
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and all
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\exp\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[u(\varepsilon,\cdot)](\xi)=0.

(4.6)

Under these conditions, the Fourier transform of the solution admits the following explicit integral representation for almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and all
t\in[0,T)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(r)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+ib^{i}(r)\xi^{i}+c(r)\right)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s.

(4.7)

Proof.

To facilitate clarity, we organize the proof into three parts.

I. Establishing the Frequency-Domain ODE

By invoking Lemma
4.2
, we know that for almost every frequency
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the following integral equation holds for all
t\in(0,T)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+ib^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathcal{F}[u(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s.

(4.8)

By isolating the integration over the interval
[\varepsilon,t]
, we can rewrite this for any
0<\varepsilon\leq t<T
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}[u(\varepsilon,\cdot)](\xi)+\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathcal{F}[u(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s.

Now, fix a specific
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
such that both the limit condition (
4.6
) and the integral representation above are satisfied. Because
\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)
is expressed as the integral of locally integrable functions, it is absolutely continuous on
[0,T^{\prime}]
for any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
. Differentiating with respect to
t
yields a first-order linear ordinary differential equation valid for almost every
t\in(0,T)
:

\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)]_{t}(\xi)=\left(-a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(t)\xi^{i}+c(t)\right)\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)+\mathcal{F}[f(t,\cdot)](\xi).

II. Applying the Integrating Factor

To solve this differential equation on the interval
[\varepsilon,T^{\prime}]
, we introduce the corresponding integrating factor. Because the exponent is absolutely continuous, the product

\exp\left(-\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

is also absolutely continuous on
[\varepsilon,T^{\prime}]
and differentiable for almost every
t\in[\varepsilon,T)
. Applying the product rule of differentiation directly yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\exp\left(-\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[f(t,\cdot)](\xi)\quad\text{a.e.}~t\in[\varepsilon,T).

III. Integration and Taking the Limit

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we integrate this derivative from
\varepsilon
to
t
and obtain

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}[u(\varepsilon,\cdot)](\xi)+\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\exp\left(-\int_{\varepsilon}^{s}\left(-a^{ij}(r)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(r)\xi^{i}+c(r)\right)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s.

Multiplying both sides by the inverse integrating factor isolates
\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)
,
i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(s)\xi^{i}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[u(\varepsilon,\cdot)](\xi)

\displaystyle+\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(r)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(r)\xi^{i}+c(r)\right)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)](\xi)\mathrm{d}s.

Finally, we take the limit as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. Invoking the vanishing initial condition (
4.6
), the first term on the right-hand side drops out entirely. This directly yields the target representation (
4.7
), completing the proof.

\Box

Remark 4.5
.

Formal manipulation of (
4.7
) suggests that
u(t,x)
can be represented through the inverse Fourier transform:

u(t,x):=\mathcal{F}_{\xi}^{-1}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(r)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(r)\xi^{i}+c(r)\right)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathcal{F}f(s,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}s\right](x).

Despite its validity as a distribution, the local integrability of
u
remains uncertain—especially in the presence of degenerate coefficients
a^{ij}
.
To ensure we obtain a solution with the required functional properties, we depart from the transform method and adopt a stochastic approach based on Itô’s stochastic calculus to achieve a rigorous construction.

As a corollary of this integral representation, the uniqueness of the weak solution to (
1.1
) is readily established.

Corollary 4.6

(Uniqueness of a weak solution)
.

Let
p\in[1,2]
and assume
f
is locally integrable on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
A weak solution
u
to equation (
1.1
) is unique within the class of functions satisfying the following four criteria:

(i)

(Spatial Integrability) For any
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty.

(ii)

(Weighted Fourier Integrability) For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\|\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

(4.9)

where
p^{\prime}
is the Hölder conjugate of
p
, i.e.
p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}
and
\frac{1}{0}:=\infty
.

(iii)

(Temporal Continuity in Frequency) For almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the mapping
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)
is continuous on
[0,T)
.

(iv)

(Initial Vanishing Condition) For almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and all
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\exp\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[u(\varepsilon,\cdot)](\xi)=0.

Proof.

Suppose
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are two weak solutions to (
1.1
) that both reside in the previously defined function class.
By the linearity of the operator, their difference
u:=u_{1}-u_{2}
is a weak solution to the following homogeneous problem

\begin{cases}u_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+b^{i}(t)u_{x^{i}}(t,x)+c(t)u(t,x),&(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\\
u(0,x)=0.\end{cases}

Since
u
also satisfies the class conditions, Proposition
4.4
implies that for a vanishing source term
f\equiv 0
, the Fourier transform must vanish,
i.e.

\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)=0\quad\text{for almost every }(t,\xi)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}.

Applying the Fourier inversion theorem, we conclude that

u(t,x)=0\quad\text{for almost every }(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}.

Thus,
u_{1}(t,x)=u_{2}(t,x)
in the sense of locally integrable functions on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
, establishing the uniqueness.

\Box

Next, for the case
p=1
, we examine a set of sufficient conditions that satisfy the hypotheses of Corollary
4.6
.
This allows us to derive new criteria that do not explicitly involve Fourier transforms.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
f
be a locally integrable function on
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
A weak solution
u
to equation (
1.1
) is unique within the class of functions that satisfy the following four criteria:

(i)

(Uniform Local Bound) For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty.

(4.10)

(ii)

(Weighted Integrability) For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(iii)

(Pointwise Temporal Continuity) For almost every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the mapping
t\in(0,T)\mapsto u(t,x)
is continuous on
[0,T)
.

(iv)

(Initial Vanishing Condition) For all
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\exp\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\mu_{a,b,c}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right)\|u(\varepsilon,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}=0.

Proof.

Let
u
be a weak solution to (
1.1
) satisfying the aforementioned conditions.
To establish uniqueness, it is sufficient to verify that
u
fulfills the criteria of Corollary
4.6
,
i.e.

(i)

For any
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty.

(ii)

For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\|\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

(iii)

For almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the mapping
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)
is continuous on
[0,T)
.

(iv)

For almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\exp\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[u(\varepsilon,\cdot)](\xi)=0\quad\forall t\in(0,T).

We omit the specific details of this verification, as they follow directly from the Fourier estimate (
4.1
) and the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem.

Remark 4.8
.

We observe that in the case
p=1
, the mapping
\xi\mapsto\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)
is continuous for all
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
However, this continuity is generally lost when
p\in(1,2]
.
As a result, for this higher
p
range, the relaxed conditions derived in Corollary
4.7
do not appear sufficient to replace the original assumptions of Corollary
4.6
.

Remark 4.9
.

The function class defined in Corollary
4.7
for the uniqueness of weak solutions to (
1.1
) is not necessarily optimal.
While Corollary
4.6
demonstrates that this class can be broadened by utilizing the Fourier transform, Corollary
4.7
remains valuable because it provides criteria independent of spectral analysis.
Nevertheless, even without relying on the Fourier transform, further expansion of this class is possible; specifically, the uniform bound in (
4.10
) is not strictly required.
Although (
4.10
) is typically used to ensure the temporal continuity of
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)
, this requirement can be bypassed by leveraging the inherent continuity of the
\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
-Fourier transform alongside the structural link between
u
and
f
.
Following the introduction of a preliminary lemma, we provide a rigorous formulation of this result in Theorem
4.11
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
p\in[1,2]
and
f\in\mathrm{L}_{1,p,loc}((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d})
, and suppose that Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
hold.
Furthermore, assume that the source term
f
satisfies the integrability condition

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(4.11)

Define the function
u
via the probabilistic representation:

u(t,x):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(s,x+X_{s,t}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}s,

where the stochastic process
X_{s,t}
is given by
X_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}b(r)\mathrm{d}r+\int_{s}^{t}\sigma(r)\mathrm{dB}_{r}
with
\sigma(r)=\sqrt{2A(r)}
,
b(t)=(b^{1}(t),\ldots,b^{d}(t))
, and
A(r)=(a^{ij}(r))
. Then, for each
t\in(0,T)
, the Fourier transform of
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(r)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+ib^{i}(r)\xi^{i}+c(r)\right)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathcal{F}\left[f\left(s,\cdot\right)\right](\xi)\mathrm{d}s\quad\text{a.e.}~\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}.

(4.12)

In the specific case where
p=1
, this identity (
4.12
) is valid for all
(t,\xi)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Although this proof is highly technical, it can be decomposed into a clear, logical sequence. The primary objective is to introduce an approximate solution using spatial truncation, apply the Fourier transform by exploiting the properties of Gaussian processes, and ultimately take the limit as the truncation parameter approaches infinity.

I. Defining the Truncated Approximation

First, we localize the source term by cutting it off outside a ball of radius
n
.
For any natural number
n\in\mathbf{N}
and variables
(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
, we define the truncated function

f^{n}(t,x):=f(t,x)1_{|x|<n}

Using this, we define an approximate solution
u^{n}(t,x)
via the stochastic representation

u^{n}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{n}\left(s,x+X_{s,t}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}s.

II. Justifying the Fourier Transform

To apply the Fourier transform to
u^{n}
, we must ensure it is sufficiently integrable. By applying Hölder’s inequality, we can bound the
\mathrm{L}_{1}
-norm of the truncated function by the
\mathrm{L}_{p}
-norm of the original function
f
,
i.e.

\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t\lesssim_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

Because this integral is finite, Fubini’s theorem permits us to apply the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
directly to both sides of our definition for
u^{n}
, passing the transform inside the expected value
\mathbb{E}
, that is,

\mathcal{F}[u^{n}(t,\cdot)](\xi)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}[f^{n}\left(s,\cdot+X_{s,t}\right)](\xi)\right]\mathrm{d}s.

III. Applying Gaussian Process Properties

The shift in the spatial variable by the stochastic process
X_{s,t}
translates to a phase shift in the Fourier domain. Taking the expectation of this phase shift yields the characteristic function of the Gaussian process:

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}[f^{n}\left(s,\cdot+X_{s,t}\right)](\xi)\right]=\mathcal{F}[f^{n}(s,\cdot)](\xi)\exp\left(-\int_{s}^{t}a^{ij}(r)\mathrm{d}r\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{t}b^{i}(r)\mathrm{d}r\xi^{i}\right)

Substituting this back into our equation for
\mathcal{F}[u^{n}(t,\cdot)](\xi)
, we obtain an exact analytical expression for the Fourier transform of the approximate solution:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u^{n}(t,\cdot)](\xi)=\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(r)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+\mathrm{i}b^{i}(r)\xi^{i}+c(r)\right)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathcal{F}\left[f^{n}\left(s,\cdot\right)\right](\xi)\mathrm{d}s.

(4.13)

This equality holds almost everywhere for
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

IV. The Limit Procedure (
n\to\infty
)

To find the representation for true solution, we must take the limit to both sides of (
4.13
) as
n\to\infty
. We measure the convergence in the
\mathrm{L}_{p^{\prime}}
-space using the generalized Minkowski inequality and standard Fourier estimates.
The distance between the approximate and true solutions in the Fourier domain is bounded by the distance between
f^{n}
and
f
in the spatial domain:

\|\mathcal{F}[u^{n}(t,\cdot)]-\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f^{n}\left(s,\cdot\right)-f\left(s,\cdot\right)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s

When setting up the integral bound for the difference, we rely on the degenerate ellipticity condition (
a^{ij}(r)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\geq 0
).
This ensures that the exponential term related to the diffusion matrix is bounded by 1, allowing us to drop it from the inequality:

\|\mathcal{F}[u^{n}]-\mathcal{F}[u]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|\mathcal{F}[f^{n}(s,\cdot)]-\mathcal{F}[f(s,\cdot)]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s

Because
f^{n}\to f
in
\mathrm{L}_{p}
, the right-hand side goes to zero as
n\to\infty
.
Thus, the limiting case satisfies (
4.12
).

V. Continuity of the Result for the case
p=1

Finally, we establish the continuity of the resulting limiting function with respect to the frequency variable
\xi
.
This is achieved by demonstrating that
u
has a bounded
\mathrm{L}_{1}
-norm.
By applying the generalized Minkowski inequality alongside the probabilistic representation and (
4.11
), we obtain

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r\right)\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

The finiteness of this
\mathrm{L}_{1}
-norm ensures that the Fourier transform is continuous in
\xi
for any
t\in(0,T)
.
Consequently, the relation (
4.12
) is valid for all
(t,\xi)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

For the reader’s convenience, we restate Theorem
2.9
here.

Theorem 4.11

(Existence and uniqueness of a weak solution)
.

Let
f
be a locally integrable function
[0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
hold.
Additionally, assume the following conditions:

(i)

For any
0<s<t<T
,

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{r}c(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho}\max_{i,j}|a^{ij}(r)|\mathrm{d}r<\infty.

(ii)

For any
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

(iii)

For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

Then there exists a unique weak solution
u
to (
1.1
) in the class satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

For almost every

,

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty.

(ii)

For any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\|\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(4.14)

(iii)

For almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the mapping
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)
is continuous on
[0,T)
.

(iv)

For almost every

,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\exp\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[u(\varepsilon,\cdot)](\xi)=0\quad\forall t\in(0,T).

Moreover, the solution
u
satisfies the estimates that for all

,

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s

and for all
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

If additionally the coefficient
c(t)
is locally integrable on

, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}|c(t)|\mathrm{d}t<\infty\quad\forall T^{\prime}\in(0,T),

then the mapping

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{s}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s.

Proof.

Given the complexity of the proof, we break it down into the following sequential parts.

I. Existence and Established Bounds

Theorem
3.10
provides the existence of a weak solution

This solution

obeys the following integral bounds:

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{a,b,c}(s)\|u(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s

\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{s}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s.

II. Verifying the Uniqueness Class

As established in Corollary

(i)
through
(iv)
.
Thus, to conclude the proof, it is sufficient to demonstrate that this specific solution
u
falls into the required uniqueness class.
This means verifying that
u
satisfies all four of the following properties:

(i)

For any
t\in(0,T)

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty.

(ii)

For any

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\|\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)]\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(iii)

For almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

t\mapsto\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

[0,T)

(iv)

For almost every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\exp\left(\int_{\varepsilon}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+c(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{F}[u(\varepsilon,\cdot)](\xi)=0\quad\forall t\in(0,T).

3.10

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u(t,\cdot)](\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\left(-a^{ij}(r)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+ib^{i}(r)\xi^{i}+c(r)\right)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathcal{F}\left[f\left(s,\cdot\right)\right](\xi)\mathrm{d}s.

(4.17)

) and (

and

5.
Elementary calculus approach for uniqueness of a weak solution

In this section, we present an energy estimate tailored to our framework.
While this estimate is derived using fundamental calculus tools—namely, integration by parts and the fundamental theorem of calculus—the singular nature of the coefficients requires every step to be handled with meticulous care.
Nevertheless, for
p\in(1,\infty)
, this classical energy estimate proves to be remarkably robust, even in such highly singular settings.

Lemma 5.1

(A priori estimate for a weak solution)
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and suppose that Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
hold.
Assume that
u
is a weak solution to (
1.1
) with
f
such that for all
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{\delta}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|u(\delta,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}=0,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty,

(5.2)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)\|u(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(5.3)

Then for all
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|u(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\leq p\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{p\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|u(s,\cdot)\|^{p-1}_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\|f\left(s,\cdot\right)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s.

(5.4)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{p\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|u(s,\cdot)\|^{p-1}_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\|f\left(s,\cdot\right)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

(5.5)

To enhance readability, we organize the proof into four logical parts.

I. Mollification and Classical Formulation

Let
\varphi\in\mathrm{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be a standard non-negative mollifier such that
\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\mathrm{d}x=1
.
For any
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
and
(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
, we define the spatial convolutions:

u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}u(t,y)\varphi^{(\varepsilon)}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y

and

f^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}f(t,y)\varphi^{(\varepsilon)}(x-y)\mathrm{d}y.

By the standard properties of convolutions, for any
d
-dimensional multi-index
\alpha
, the spatial derivatives satisfy the following bounds:

\displaystyle\|D^{\alpha}_{x}u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}

(5.6)

and

\displaystyle\|D^{\alpha}_{x}f^{(\varepsilon)}(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}.

(5.7)

Because of this smoothing effect, for each
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
,
u^{(\varepsilon)}
becomes a classical solution to the regularized equation

\displaystyle u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}a^{ij}(s)u^{(\varepsilon)}_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,x)\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}f^{(\varepsilon)}(s,x)\mathrm{d}s\quad\forall(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}.

(5.8)

Consequently, for any
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, equation (
5.8
) yields the pointwise bound

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}|u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)|\leq\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}|a^{ij}(s)u^{(\varepsilon)}_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,x)|\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}|f^{(\varepsilon)}(s,x)|\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

(5.9)

II. Transformation and Application of the Chain Rule

To handle the lower-order coefficients, we introduce a transformed function. For
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
and
0<\delta\leq t<T
, we define:

\displaystyle v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{\delta}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}u^{(\varepsilon)}\left(t,x-\int_{\delta}^{t}b(s)\mathrm{d}s\right).

(5.10)

For each fixed
\delta
and
x
, the map
t\mapsto v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)
is absolutely continuous on
[\delta,T^{\prime}]
for all
T^{\prime}\in(\delta,T)
. Applying the chain rule, we obtain the derivative with respect to
t
for almost every
t\in(\delta,T)
:

\displaystyle v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}_{t}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-c(t)v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{\delta}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}\left(u^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}\left(t,x-\int_{\delta}^{t}b(s)\mathrm{d}s\right)-b^{i}(t)u^{(\varepsilon)}_{x^{i}}\left(t,x-\int_{\delta}^{t}b(s)\mathrm{d}s\right)\right)

\displaystyle=a^{ij}(t)v_{x^{i}x^{j}}^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)+\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{\delta}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}f^{(\varepsilon)}\left(t,x-\int_{\delta}^{t}b(s)\mathrm{d}s\right).

Furthermore, the mapping
t\mapsto|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p}
is also absolutely continuous on
[\delta,T^{\prime}]
. Applying the chain rule once more yields:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p}

\displaystyle=p|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p-2}v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)a^{ij}(t)v_{x^{i}x^{j}}^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)

\displaystyle\quad+p|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p-2}v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{\delta}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}f^{(\varepsilon)}\left(t,x-\int_{\delta}^{t}b(s)\mathrm{d}s\right).

III. Integration and the Energy Bound

By integrating from
\delta
to
t
via the fundamental theorem of calculus, we have for all
(t,x)\in[\delta,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p}

\displaystyle=|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(\delta,x)|^{p}+p\int_{\delta}^{t}\left(a^{ij}(s)|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(s,x)|^{p-2}v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(s,x)v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,x)\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+p\int_{\delta}^{t}|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(s,x)|^{p-2}v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(s,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{\delta}^{s}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}f^{(\varepsilon)}\left(s,x-\int_{\delta}^{s}b(r)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=|u^{(\varepsilon)}(\delta,x)|^{p}+p\int_{\delta}^{t}\left(a^{ij}(s)|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p-2}v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,x)\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+p\int_{\delta}^{t}|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(s,x)|^{p-2}v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(s,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{\delta}^{s}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}f^{(\varepsilon)}\left(s,x-\int_{\delta}^{s}b(r)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathrm{d}s.

Next, we integrate with respect to
x
over
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
By applying Fubini’s theorem, integration by parts, and the degenerate ellipticity of
a^{ij}(t)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|u^{(\varepsilon)}(\delta,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-p(p-1)\int_{\delta}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left(\lambda(s)|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p-2}|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}_{x}(s,x)|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+p\int_{\delta}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(s,x)|^{p-2}v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(s,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{\delta}^{s}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}f^{(\varepsilon)}\left(s,x-\int_{\delta}^{s}b(r)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|u^{(\varepsilon)}(\delta,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+p\int_{\delta}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(s,x)|^{p-2}v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(s,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{\delta}^{s}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}f^{(\varepsilon)}\left(s,x-\int_{\delta}^{s}b(r)\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}s.

(5.11)

The use of Fubini’s theorem is justified by equations (
5.2
), (
5.3
), (
5.5
), (
5.6
), and (
5.7
), guaranteeing the necessary joint integrability.
Additionally, integration by parts requires careful handling for

since the map
x\mapsto|x|^{p}
is not strictly twice differentiable at the origin; however, this procedure remains valid, and we refer the reader to
[

, Lemma 2.17]
for a rigorous justification.

IV. Passing to the Limits

Substituting the definition of
v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}
from (
5.10
) back into (
5.11
) and applying Hölder’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-p\int_{\delta}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|v^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|u^{(\varepsilon)}(\delta,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+p\int_{\delta}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-p\int_{\delta}^{s}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|u^{(\varepsilon)}(s,\cdot)\|^{p-1}_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\|f^{(\varepsilon)}\left(s,\cdot\right)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s.

Multiplying through by the exponential factor gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{p\int_{\delta}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|u^{(\varepsilon)}(\delta,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+p\int_{\delta}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{p\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|u^{(\varepsilon)}(s,\cdot)\|^{p-1}_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\|f^{(\varepsilon)}\left(s,\cdot\right)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s.

Taking the limit as
\delta\downarrow 0
and utilizing (
5.1
) alongside (
5.6
), the initial value term vanishes, leaving:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\leq p\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{p\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mathrm{d}r}\|u^{(\varepsilon)}(s,\cdot)\|^{p-1}_{\mathrm{L}p(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\|f^{(\varepsilon)}\left(s,\cdot\right)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s.

Finally, by taking the limit as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and invoking standard properties of Sobolev mollifiers, we recover the desired energy estimate (
5.4

III. Proving Estimate (

We split the proof of (
2.8

•

Step 1:
(The Locally Integrable Case)

If we assume that both the diffusion matrix
a^{ij}

Step 2:
(The General Case)

When the coefficients lack local integrability near the initial time, an approximation method is required.
While we can safely truncate the drift vector point-wise at a threshold

To circumvent this, we rely on the spectral decomposition
A(t)=Q(t)\Lambda(t)Q(t)^{\top}

Assuming the eigenvalues satisfy
\lambda_{1}(t)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}(t)

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}(t)&0&0&\cdots&0\\

–

–

Spectral Truncation: We construct a truncated eigenvalue matrix

\displaystyle\Lambda_{M}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}(t)\wedge M&0&0&\cdots&0\\

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&\lambda_{d}(t)\wedge M.\end{bmatrix}

We then reconstruct the approximated diffusion matrix as

This method ensures that

and
b_{M}^{i}
, the necessary estimates continue to hold.
A critical point is that the constants in these estimates rely strictly on our initial assumptions, maintaining complete independence from the truncation parameter
M
.
For the sequence of solutions
\{u^{M}\}
, this uniform bound guarantees both relative weak compactness and the Cauchy property within the appropriate weighted spaces.
Ordinarily, extracting a weakly convergent Cauchy subsequence would be enough to finish the proof using weighted norm approximation.
However, the original source term
f
lacks the spatial regularity required to rigorously take the limit.
We overcome this by executing the full truncation procedure on an auxiliary system driven by a mollified source term
f^{(\varepsilon)}
.
By applying analogous relative weak compactness and Cauchy criteria to the smoothed solutions
u^{(\varepsilon)}
, we can rigorously justify the limit passage and finalize the proof.

\Box

Proof of Theorem
2.29
.

For the sake of clarity, we have structured the proof into several separate stages, as is typical.

I. The Objective

Building upon Theorem
2.23
, the remainder of the proof reduces to verifying (
2.13
) and confirming that
u
is a strong solution to (
1.1
).
Once
u
is established as a strong solution, (
2.13
) follows immediately from its definition and the a priori estimates provided in Theorem
2.23
.
Therefore, our primary objective is now to demonstrate that
u
is a strong solution by utilizing a mollified approximation.

II. Applying Sobolev Mollifiers

We return to the Sobolev mollifiers
u^{(\varepsilon)}
and
f^{(\varepsilon)}
that were utilized in the proof of Theorem
2.23
.
As noted in Remark
3.11
, these regularized functions satisfy the following integral representation :

\displaystyle u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(a^{ij}(s)u^{(\varepsilon)}_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,x)+b^{i}(s)u^{(\varepsilon)}_{x^{i}}(s,x)+c(s)u^{(\varepsilon)}(s,x)+f^{(\varepsilon)}(s,x)\right)\mathrm{d}s.

III. Convergence Analysis

Let
\varepsilon_{n}
be a sequence of positive numbers such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=0
.
We must analyze the convergence of each term in the integral equation as
n\to\infty
:

•

Convergence of
u^{(\varepsilon)}
:

By extracting an appropriate subsequence based on (
2.12
), the left-hand side term
u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)
converges to
u(t,x)
for all
t
and for almost every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
. This is guaranteed by the following bound:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}\|(u^{(\varepsilon_{n})}-u)(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\right)\leq\|f^{(\varepsilon_{n})}-f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{1,p}\left((0,T^{\prime})\times\mathbf{R}^{d},\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}t\right)}.

•

Convergence of the Source Term:

The integral of the source term,

\int_{0}^{t}f^{(\varepsilon)}(s,x)\mathrm{d}s,

converges straightforwardly due to our baseline assumption that

f\in\mathrm{L}_{1,p,loc}\left((0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\right).

•

Convergence of the Differential Operators:

We can bound the remaining integral, which involves the coefficients and spatial derivatives, by invoking the generalized Minkowski inequality, Hölder’s inequality, and the a priori estimates established in Theorem
2.23
.
This approach leads to the following upper bound:

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\left(a^{ij}(s)u^{(\varepsilon_{n})}_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,\cdot)+b^{i}(s)u^{(\varepsilon_{n})}_{x^{i}}(s,\cdot)+c(s)u^{(\varepsilon_{n})}(s,\cdot)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left(|a^{ij}(s)|\|u^{(\varepsilon_{n})}_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}+|b^{i}(s)|\|u^{(\varepsilon_{n})}_{x^{i}}(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}|c(s)|\|u^{(\varepsilon_{n})}(s,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\mu_{a,b,c}(t)^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\left(w(\alpha(t))\lambda(t)\right)^{-1/(q-1)}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|f^{(\varepsilon_{n})}(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{q/p}\mathrm{e}^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}w(\alpha(t))(\lambda(t))^{1-q}\mathrm{d}t.

IV. Concluding the Strong Solution

To finalize the proof, we apply the above convergence logic to the Cauchy sequences. By evaluating the differences
u^{(\varepsilon_{n})}-u^{(\varepsilon_{m})}
and
f^{(\varepsilon_{n})}-f^{(\varepsilon_{m})}
(rather than just
u^{(\varepsilon_{n})}
and
f^{(\varepsilon_{n})}
) for any
n,m\in\mathbf{N}
, we can rigorously pass to the limit. This confirms that
u
is indeed a strong solution to (
1.1
) corresponding to the source term
f
.

\Box

7.
Discussions and Open problems

While Corollary
4.6
and Theorem
5.2
successfully establish the uniqueness of weak solutions to (
1.1
) within classes of
\mathrm{L}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
-valued functions for
p\in[1,\infty)
, a natural subsequent question is whether this uniqueness extends to bounded functions.

Open problem 7.1
.

Is a weak solution to (
1.1
) unique within a class of
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
-valued functions?

Itô calculus appears essential for establishing well-posedness in the
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
-setting.
For instance, the existence of such a weak solution is constructed using Itô’s formula, as shown in the proof of Theorem
3.10
.
However, extending this stochastic approach to prove uniqueness is hindered by the low regularity of the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
and
b^{i}(t)
, as highlighted in Remark
3.12
.
Furthermore, we cannot rely on the classical maximum principle for uniqueness; the irregularity of the coefficients prevents the mollified approximations
u^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)
from residing in the classical space
C^{1,2}([0,T)\times\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

Our next inquiry focuses on the behavior of the solution near the initial time when the zero-order coefficient is highly singular.
Specifically, we want to know if regularity estimates analogous to (
2.8
) in Theorem
2.23
can be derived even when
c(t)
is not locally integrable near
t=0
(i.e., when
\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}|c(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t=\infty
for some
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
).

Open problem 7.2
.

Can we establish meaningful regularity estimates for a weak solution to (
1.1
) when
c(t)
fails to be locally integrable near the initial time?

Current estimates, such as (
2.8
), depend heavily on the exponential integrating factor
\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s}
.
Consequently, the failure of local integrability invalidates this approach, dictating the need for a fundamentally different class of regularity bounds.
At present, we have no preliminary hypotheses regarding what structural form these alternative estimates might take.
