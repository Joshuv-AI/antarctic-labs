EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This Storage Field Development plan presents the Storage Complex characterization results,
construction, monitoring, operational plans, and costs associated with the proposed Prairie State
Energy Campus - Carbon Capture and Storage (PSEC-CCS) project in Washington County,
Illinois, near Marissa. The proposed storage complex, known as the St. Peter-Everton Storage
Complex, comprises the St. Peter Sandstone and Everton sandstone reservoirs, with the Maquoketa
Shale serving as the primary confining unit and the Knox Group as lower confining unit. The
lowermost Underground Source of Drinking Water (USDW) identified for the site is the
Pennsylvanian section.
Geologic characterization of the St. Peter-Everton Storage Complex at the Prairie State Generating
Company (PSGC a.k.a. PSEC-CCS) site was performed under the Illinois Storage Corridor
CarbonSAFE Phase III project, which also prepared Underground Injection Control (UIC) Class
VI permit applications to construct two injection wells; the permit applications were submitted to
PSGC. A characterization well, Lively Grove #1 (LG #1), was drilled to collect site-specific data
that was used to prepare the UIC Class VI applications.
The proposed buildout for the PSGC site includes (1) two injection wells (I2E and I3E), (2) six
Injection Zone Monitoring (IZM) wells in three phases; two IZM well will be installed in the St
Peter Sandstone and Everton Formation during each phase, (3) two above confining zone (ACZ)
monitoring wells, one of which will be used to monitor the lowermost USDW, (4) capture and
compression facilities, and (5) transportation facilities, i.e., pipelines. A pre-operational testing
program was proposed in the Class VI permit application and will be employed at the site pending
approval. Additional pre-injection (baseline), syn-injection, and post-injection monitoring and site
care procedures will be followed by site operator to ensure that injection activities are protective
of human health and the environment.
The evaluation of the geologic setting indicates that the St Peter Sandstone and the Everton
Formation at the site are sufficiently deep, thick, porous and permeable. The Maquoketa Group at
the site is of sufficient thickness, lateral continuity, and has low enough permeabilities to serve as
the primary confining zone. The site affords additional containment with over 600 feet of dense,
carbonate-rich strata, consisting of the Joachim Dolomite, Platteville Group, and Trenton
Limestone. Both the Maquoketa Group and New Albany Shale confining units are present in the
characterization well LG #1 and are situated between the top of the St Peter Sandstone and the
Pennsylvanian age strata, which is the identified lowermost USDW. The dense dolostones of the
Knox Group underlying the Everton Formation will act as an impermeable lower boundary for the
Everton injection zones.
Ninety-three wells have been identified as penetrating the confining units within the estimated
Area-of-Review (AoR), of which 85 wells penetrate the primary confining unit and four wells
reach the storage formation.
Seismic reflection data indicate all interpreted faults transecting the storage and confining units
within the AoR are over three miles from the modeled CO2 plume. Within the AoR, there are no
identified fault conduits for CO2 to migrate out of the storage reservoirs and into the shallower
stratigraphy above the confining interval.

INTRODUCTION
This Storage Field Development Plan was created to guide the development of a carbon dioxide
(CO2) storage field site for the Prairie State Energy Campus - Carbon Capture and Storage project
(PSEC-CCS) project (a.k.a. Prairie State Generating Company [PSGC] site) near of Marissa,
Illinois, in Washington County. This document details the (1) characterization effort performed by
the Illinois Survey Geological Survey (ISGS) during the Phase III CarbonSAFE project known as
the Illinois Storage Corridor and (2) construction and monitoring plans for the capture, transport,
and secure storage of up to 20 million tonnes of CO2 over 20 years.
Detailed plans developed for the PSEC-CCS project’s UIC Class VI submittals are included with
this submission. They include the following, referred to herein by name: Area-of-Review (AoR)
and Corrective Action Plan, Testing and Monitoring Plan, Quality Assurance and Surveillance
Plan, Injection Well Plugging Plan, Emergency and Remedial Response Plan, Injection Well Plan,
and Narrative sections: Geomechanical and Petrophysical Information, Seismic History, and
Injection and Confining Zones.
Details on the method to establish the project AoR per U. S. EPA guidelines are provided in this
document and the included PSEC-CCS UIC Class VI Area-of-Review and Corrective Action Plan.

FIELD CHARACTERIZATION RESULTS
The PSEC-CCS project involves the capture, transport, and storage of carbon dioxide (CO2) from
the Prairie State Generating Station (PSGS) coal-fired electricity generation plant situated near
Marissa, Illinois. The captured CO2 will be transported via a 6-mile pipeline to injection wells for
geological storage in the St Peter Sandstone and Everton Formation. The PSGS and the adjacent
Lively Grove coal mine, which provides the coal feedstock, constitute the Prairie State Energy
Campus (PSEC) also known as the Prairie State Generating Company (PSGC) site. The PSGS and
a planned CO2 capture unit will annually emit approximately 8.125 metric tonnes (Mt) of CO2
associated with the production of electricity.
Two CO2 injection wells are currently planned for PSEC-CCS project, with the capability of
storing up to 20 Mt of CO2 within 20 years or longer. Construction of the CO2 capture and
compression unit for the PSGC-CCS project is expected to commence after PSGC selects a third-
party site operator of the PSEC-CCS project referred herein as PROJECT OWNER, that will own,
operate, and be the permit holder of the injection wells.
A characterization well, Lively Grove #1 (LG #1), was drilled in 2022 to evaluate the geologic
formations at the PSGC site and which provides site-specific data used within the permit
submissions. The supporting documentation was prepared in accordance with the U.S.
Environmental Protection Agency’s (US EPA’s) UIC Control Program for Carbon Dioxide
Geologic Sequestration Wells (The Geological Sequestration [GS] Rule, codified in Title 40 of the
Code of Federal Regulations [40 CFR 146.81 et seq.]).
The LG #1 well was drilled as part of a US DOE-sponsored CarbonSAFE Phase III project, the
Illinois Storage Corridor, which is managed by the Illinois State Geological Survey (ISGS) of the
University of Illinois. The ISGS led the first US demonstration of CCS via the Illinois Basin –
Decatur Project (IBDP) at the Archer Daniels Midland (ADM) facility in Decatur, IL. This project
led to the first Underground Injection Control (UIC) Class VI permit approvals for saline storage;

the IBDP site and the subsequent commercial injection project, Illinois Industrial Carbon Capture
& Storage (IL-ICCS), are both located at ADM in Decatur, IL. Upon completion of the data
acquisition, LG #1 was plugged and abandoned.

SITE CHARACTERIZATION

St Peter– Everton Storage Complex
The term “storage complex” is employed in this document to describe the geologic system that
includes the storage reservoir and the confining strata. The proposed St. Peter-Everton Storage
Complex at Prairie State Energy Campus (PSEC) consists of the following components: (1) a
storage reservoir with substantial sandstone intervals in the lower Everton Formation and St. Peter
Sandstone, separated by dense dolomite intervals in the upper Everton Formation; (2) a regional
primary seal in the Maquoketa Group, characterized by its thick, pure shale intervals; and (3)
intermediate strata of the Joachim Dolomite, Platteville Group, and Trenton Limestone. The
stratigraphic relationships among these formations are illustrated in Figure 1.
Stratigraphic layers were identified and located based on downhole wireline logs and regional
geological knowledge. The units have a broad areal extent, extending beyond the boundaries of
the study area. Figure 2 presents a cross-section of the Ordovician strata that constitute the St.
Peter-Everton Storage Complex, spanning from the Borowiak #1 well in Washington County to
the Hinton #7 well in Champaign County, a distance of approximately 154 miles northeast. The
cross-section also reveals that the reservoirs become too shallow to be considered potential storage
reservoirs (e.g., less than approximately 2,625 feet deep, or 800 meters, as per US DOE data from
2012)) as one progresses northeastward into central and north central Illinois.
A characterization LG #1 well was drilled six miles north of PSEC to characterize the St. Peter-
Everton Storage Complex. LG #1 was drilled to a total depth of 5,888 feet (1,794 meters) measured
depth (MD), reaching the Precambrian basement. The formation tops for LG #1 are presented in
Table 1.

Injection and Confining Zone Details
Depth, areal extent, and thickness
The St. Peter Sandstone and Everton Formation are the storage reservoirs for PSEC-CCS project
site. The St. Peter Sandstone (upper injection zone) is 119 feet (36 m) thick and occurs at 3,589
feet (1,094 m) measured depth (MD) at the LG #1 well. The top of the dolomitic portion of the
Everton Formation occurs at 3,708 feet (1,130 m) MD and is 77 feet (23 m) thick, while the
sandstone portion of the Everton Formation, the lower injection zone, occurs at 3,785 feet (1,154
m) MD and is 86 feet (26 m) thick at the LG #1 well. The Joachim Dolomite, the Platteville Group,
and the Trenton Limestone form the overlying strata separating the storage reservoirs from the
primary confining zone (Figure 3). The primary confining zone and seal for the PSEC
sequestration site, the Maquoketa Group, is 142 feet (43 m) thick and occurs at 2,820 feet (860 m)
MD in the LG #1 well. Selected formation thickness and measured depth, along with average
porosity, permeability, and total shale thickness values obtained from LG #1 log analyses.

Injection Zones

Everton Formation
At LG #1, the Everton Formation is 160.7 feet (48.9 m) thick and is divided into two distinct
intervals (Figure 4): the upper portion is fine-grained, massive dolomite with interbedded
argillaceous dolomite and thin laminations of shale, and the lower portion (injection zone) is
sandstone, with well sorted and well rounded, fine to medium-sized grains. The cross-plot log
analysis of Pe versus RHOZ demonstrates that the data points can be segregated into two groups:
pure sandstone with a porosity range of 5-16% and dolomite with a porosity range of 3-15%
(Figure 5).

Everton Formation – sandstone interval (lower injection zone): Analytical results of eighteen
RSWC samples from the formation show that the lower portion of the Everton Formation has good
reservoir potential, with an average porosity of 13.1% up to a maximum of 17.3%, an average
permeability of 189 mD up to a maximum of 489.2 mD, and average grain density of 2.64 g/cc
(Table 3, Table 6).
Everton Formation – dolomite interval: Overlying the lower sandstone, the dolomite section of
the Everton can be differentiated in geophysical logs and the cross-plot analysis of Pe versus
RHOZ (Figure 5). The porosity range (3-15%) in the dolomite interval is a result of the presence
of shale and clay minerals, predominantly illite, and is therefore not considered to be effective
porosity. This upper portion of the formation consists primarily of argillaceous dolomite and thin
intervals of shale.

The St. Peter Sandstone (upper injection zone) overlies the Everton and is 119 feet (36 m) thick
at LG #1 (Figure 3) where its top occurs at 3,589 feet (1,094 m). Approximately 25.51 feet (8 m)
of FDC was recovered from the interval 3,646 to 3,711 feet (1,111 to 1,131 m) plus 14 RSWC.
The St. Peter core samples are light tan in color and exhibit sub-rounded and well-sorted grain
structures, with grain sizes ranging from fine to medium.
The results obtained from the ELAN method and the cross-plot analysis of Pe versus RHOZ
(Figure 5) indicate that the St. Peter Sandstone is composed purely of sandstone. The gamma ray
(GR) values, which are less than 20 API, confirm the absence of clay minerals or shale intervals
in the formation. From log analyses, St Peter Sandstone has a porosity range of 5-20%.

Eleven RSWC and ten FDC samples from the St. Peter Sandstone at LG #1 were analyzed (Table

of 87 mD. Although the RSWC and FDC samples were not obtained from the same depths, the
FDC samples display a wider range of porosity and permeability compared to the RSWC samples,
with porosity reaching a maximum value of 17.0% and permeability reaching a maximum value
of 408 mD (Table 5 and Table 6).
The average porosity of the RSWC samples was found to be 13.0% (Table 6), while the average

K90) of the FDC samples was 242.2 mD (Table 5), and the samples displayed high levels of

WWDW #1 Well Testing
The PSEC Underground Injection Control (UIC) Class I WWDW #1 well is used as a wastewater
disposal well in the St. Peter Sandstone, approximately 5.5 miles south-southwest of LG #1 and
6.5 miles west of the planned injection wells; WWDW #1 step rate test and pressure fall-off test
results in the St. Peter Sandstone are summarized below.
Step Rate Testing (SRT) was conducted on the PSEC Class I injection well, WWDW #1, October
17 – 18, 2018 (SCS Engineers, 2018). The SRT was performed prior to conducting the external
mechanical integrity test (MIT) and pressure fall-off test. The SRT was performed by injecting
fresh water from the on-site raw water storage pond at successively higher rates and pressures.
Wellhead injection rates were held constant during each test interval of approximately one hour to
allow pressure in the injection zone to stabilize. Six steps from approximately 82 gallons per
minute (gpm) to 492 gpm were completed for this SRT. While conducting the step rate test, a
mechanical failure in one of the pumps caused a shutdown of that pump just after increasing the
pump rate from 328 gpm to 410 gpm, at which point a backup pump continued to inject water into
the well. Consequently, the injection rate and pressures dropped briefly while the injection rate on
the backup pump gradually increased to 410 gpm.

 11

The following conclusions are drawn from the analysis of the fall-off test data (SCS Engineers,
2020):
 • The well is completed in an injection zone with an apparent average permeability
 of 32 mD based on a net thickness of 150 ft (4,775 md-ft) as determined by straight
 line analysis on the infinite-acting homogeneous model.
 • Skin is -2. A negative mechanical skin factor generally indicates enhanced flow
 from the well into the reservoir and establishes initial reservoir conditions prior to
 injection.
 • The negative skin reduced the injection pressure by 313 psi.
 • The well is stimulated (negative skin) with an injection efficiency of 52 percent.
 • The lateral extent that the reservoir test has covered is 945 feet. Based on these test
 results no reservoir stimulation work is recommended for this well.
Based on the results of the initial 2018 fall-off test, the reservoir parameters have been determined
as required by the UIC permit and this well was recommended for wastewater injection.

Overlying Strata

Trenton Limestone (Galena Group)
The Trenton is composed of pure limestone and has a thickness of 104 ft (31.7 m) at LG #1. The
GR values throughout the formation are less than 25 API, except for 13 ft (4 m) in the lower part
of the Trenton where the GR value reaches 80 API, which suggests the presence of argillaceous
limestones in that interval (Table 6). The porosity of the formation ranges from 0.1 to 8%, as
estimated from geophysical logs.

Platteville Group
The Platteville is primarily composed of pure limestone, with limited intervals of argillaceous and
shaly limestone. The top of the Platteville Group is 3,066 feet (934 m) deep, and the formation is
391 feet (50 m) thick at LG #1. The cross-plot of Pe vs. RHOZ indicates that the maximum porosity
for limestone intervals is less than 5% but deviations from the limestone line can result in higher
porosity due to the presence of clay minerals. However, these porosities are not considered to be
effective porosities.

Joachim Dolomite
The top of the Joachim Dolomite occurs at 3,457 ft (1,054 m) MD in LG #1 and the formation is
133 feet (41 m) thick. The uppermost member of the Ancell Group, the Joachim has the potential
to serve as a restrictive unit to the vertical movement of CO2 for the Everton-St. Peter Storage
Complex. The Joachim overlies the St. Peter Sandstone in the AoR. Based upon sedimentary
structures and ichnofacies, the Joachim is thought to have been deposited in peritidal to shallow
subtidal environments and it contains layers of anhydrite and shaly dolomite (Figure 11Figure 11).
Anhydrite is generally known to be an effective barrier to fluid migration due to its ductile nature.
In addition, thin limestone intervals are scattered throughout the formation.

Confining Zone

The Maquoketa Group is the primary seal at the PSGC site with a total thickness of 141.7 ft (43.1
m) measured in the LG #1 well, and 134.5 feet (41 m) estimated from logs as consisting of pure
shale. The gamma ray values range from 100 to 150 API, except for a 7-foot interval between
2,929 to 2,936 feet (892.7 to 894.9 m), which is comprised of argillaceous dolomite (Figure 7).
The dominant clay minerals present are illite and mixed layer clays (Figure 7). The NPHI and
DPHI porosity values are high due to the presence of bound water between the clay mineral layers
(Figure 7). However, given that the formation primarily consists of shale, its effective porosity and
permeability are very low.
Eight FDC samples from the Maquoketa were analyzed. Of these samples, four were procured
from pure shale intervals, while the remaining four were obtained from the argillaceous sandy
dolomite interval. The grain density of the pure shale samples was determined to be 2.86 g/cc, with
an average porosity of 6.1% and a permeability value of 1.24 mD. The vertical permeability of all
the samples was found to be less than 0.001 mD. Conversely, the samples collected from the
argillaceous sandy dolomite interval exhibited porosity values that were less than 2% and an
average permeability of 0.3 mD. Notably, all these samples exhibited vertical permeabilities less
than 0.001 mD, with one exception: a sample that displayed a vertical permeability of 318 mD,

 13

which was attributed to a conductive fracture. These analyses indicate that the Maquoketa Group
is an excellent confining unit for the St. Peter-Everton Storage Complex. Further detailed Mecury
Injection Capillary Pressure (MICP) analyses described below confirm the petrophysical and
whole core analyses.

Detailed MICP data were used to calculate a range of minimum to maximum scCO2 column heights
based upon a range of CO2 contact angles and mercury (Hg)/air threshold pressures, to provide
insight into seal capacity and leakage potential.
As a typical adjunct to MICP evaluations, porosity (fraction), Swanson permeability (mD), and
pore throat radius in microns are also reported (Table 7). Porosity and permeability values derived
from MICP substantiate the ranges of values determined from the full core analyses (Table 8).
Seal capacity relative to CO2 is another way to evaluate caprock efficacy. Three MICP evaluations
were utilized to assess seal capacity (supercritical [sc] CO2 column heights) (Table 9). A 20%

Bedrock Aquifers and Lowermost Underground Source of Drinking Water

 15

Beneath the unconsolidated deposits is a sequence of Paleozoic rocks thousands of feet thick. The
lower portion of the rock sequence contains thick sandstones and limestone aquifers filled with
heavy brines. The upper portion is dominated by Pennsylvanian age shales with thin layers of coal,
limestone, and sandstone that generally do not yield more than 10 gallons per minute to a well
(Illinois Technical Advisory Committee on Water Resources, 1967). Water quality in the shallow
bedrock is variable and often contains very high concentrations of dissolved minerals (solids).
Near the Mississippi River the bedrock surface map (Figure 8) shows Mississippian-age
sandstones and limestones rising out of the Illinois Basin.

Pennsylvanian
Water-yielding Pennsylvanian sandstones occur in the central part of Washington County, ranging
in depths from 70 feet (21 m) in the northeast to over 500 feet (152 m) in the central part of the
county.
The few wells within the AoR that pump from bedrock produce from the upper part of the
Pennsylvanian Mattoon Formation, which is characterized by thin beds of sandstone, limestone,
and coal within thicker beds of shale. Most of the production comes from thin beds of sandstone
and limestone generally less than 15 feet (4.6 meters) in thickness. Deeper Pennsylvanian
formations (Bond, Shelburn-Patoka, Carbondale, and Tradewater) have potential water-producing
units, but it is unknown how productive they might be, nor are data readily available to estimate
net thickness of producing zones. Overall, the gross thickness of the Pennsylvanian in the area is
roughly 400 to 500 feet (122 to 152 meters) and thickens rapidly towards the east, toward the
depocenter of the Illinois Basin. Through the AoR, the top of the Pennsylvanian occurs at depths
of <25 to 150 feet (7.6 to 46 meters).

Mississippian
Mississippian rocks are well represented in the area and contain numerous water-yielding
formations, however, the Mississippian rocks are not considered to be a USDW in the AoR; a swab
sample from the LG #1 well in the Mississippian Salem Limestone yielded a salinity of 46,000
mg/L total dissolved solids (TDS). The Mississippian rocks are well known in the area for having
numerous oil-yielding units. Within the AoR, the gross thickness of the Mississippian is roughly
1,500 to 1,700 feet (457 to 518 meters), and it occurs from 550 to 715 feet (167 to 218 meters)
below ground surface.
No wells within the AoR draw water from the Mississippian or deeper formations.

Ordovician – St Peter Sandstone
The St. Peter Sandstone is a quartz-rich sandstone formation found throughout most of Illinois and
it is a major source of drinking water in northern Illinois and Wisconsin, where it is encountered
at much shallower depths.
In the LG #1, the St. Peter Sandstone is present from 3,589 to 3,708 feet deep; a St. Peter Sandstone
drill stem test in the same well produced a fluid sample with a salinity of 50,890 mg/L TDS. In

 17

addition, the nearby WWDW #1 well in the southwest part of the PSGC plant site is being used
for wastewater disposal into the St. Peter Sandstone, and a fluid sample from this well yielded
58,100 mg/L.

Lowermost USDW

Based on the lack of differentiation by formation in the Pennsylvanian water samples, the base of
the Pennsylvanian section (approximately 825 feet (251 m) deep in PSGC’s WWDW #1 disposal
well is considered as the lower boundary for the lowermost USDW at the proposed storage site.

The Ordovician Maquoketa Group is a laterally continuous impermeable confining layer situated
above the St. Peter. The top of the Maquoketa is at 2,820 feet (860 m) deep, and the formation is
142 feet (43 m) thick in the LG#1 well.

General Geologic History
The PSEC-CCS project is sited within the Illinois Basin, an intracratonic basin that formed during
the early Paleozoic. The Illinois Basin (Figure 9) is bordered by a series of prominent structures
(Kolata and Nelson, 1990), including the Kankakee Arch to the northeast, Cincinnati Arch at the
east, Mississippi River Arch at the northwest, Ozark Dome to the southwest, and Nashville Dome
to the southeast. The New Madrid Rift System, including the Rough Creek Graben and Reelfoot
Rift, extends into the southernmost part of the Basin.
Precambrian crystalline rocks form the basement of the Illinois Basin. The oldest sedimentary
rocks in the Basin are the Middle Cambrian Mt. Simon Sandstone, which contains the injection
zone for this project, and the Argenta sandstone, a stratigraphic unit that underlies the Mt. Simon
in the central part of the basin (Freiburg et al., 2015). The Mt. Simon system was deposited over
much of the current North American midcontinent in depositional environments including fluvial,
eolian, and marginal marine (Freiburg et al., 2014; Reesink et al., 2020). Precambrian topography
exerted substantial control over both the thickness and lithology of Mt. Simon deposits, including
shedding the feldspars that helped create high porosity arkosic zones.
The Mt. Simon Sandstone is conformably overlain by the Eau Claire Formation. The Eau Claire
is the basal unit of the Knox Group. The Knox Group is a thick succession of mostly marine,
dominantly carbonate (limestone and dolomite) formations with secondary siltstone, shale, and
sandstone components. The Knox Group was deposited across a large swath of the proto-North
American continent during the Cambrian and Ordovician (Fritz et al., 2012).
Post-Knox deposits of the Ancell, Platteville, and Galena Groups (mostly carbonate formations
and the lowermost USDW) are overlain by the Maquoketa Group, a primarily shaly-silty sequence
that contains impermeable strata serving as primary confining zones to the storage site. After
Maquoketa deposition, a thick succession of Silurian through Pennsylvanian rocks (dominantly
carbonates and shales, with lesser amounts of sandstone) was deposited in the central Illinois area.
These units include the Devonian-Mississippian New Albany Shale that is a secondary confining
zone at the PSEC-CCS project site. The shallowest bedrock units in the PSEC-CCS project area
are Pennsylvanian (the lowermost USDW) in age and are covered by a few hundred feet of much
younger, unconsolidated sediments.

Geologic Structures and Tectonic History
The Illinois Basin has been affected by three major tectonic episodes during the Phanerozoic Eon,
including Rodinia-related rifting; widespread compressional (reverse) faulting during the
assembly of the supercontinent Pangea in the late Paleozoic; and extensional (normal) faulting
during the Mesozoic related to Pangea’s breakup (Denny et al., 2020).

The orientations of the other faults discussed in this section have some uncertainty. Faults need to
be observed on at least two seismic lines to properly interpret fault orientation on 2D seismic, and
all the faults discussed in this report are visible on only one seismic line. However, some
observations suggest the faults are dominantly oriented north-south. All faults in the AoR have
been observed on seismic lines that are oriented east-west, except for one fault observed on 2022
Line AO7. If faults were oriented close to an east-west orientation, faults observed on east-west
seismic lines would show a much shallower angle than observed due to apparent-dip effects. Since
none of the faults observed on east-west seismic lines have a shallow dip, they are assumed to be
closer to a north-south orientation than an east-west direction. Additionally, the fault mapped on
2021 Line 5 shown in Figure 11 likely has a north-south orientation, as discussed above, and this
fault can be considered an analog to the other faults mapped in this area, reflecting similar geologic

 28

stress and fault timing. Therefore, the assumption is that the dominant orientation of the mapped
faults in this area is approximately north-south.
The fault observed on 2021 Line 5 has a maximum extent of just under 3 miles, which is the
distance between 2022 Lines AO3 and AO4 directly north and south of the fault (Figure 10). The
fault is not observed on either of these lines; therefore, the fault must die out before reaching those
two lines. The other faults mapped in this area likely have similar or smaller extents to that fault.
The overall 2D line spacing is between 1.5 and 3 miles, and no fault is observed on more than one
seismic line, which implies the mapped faults die out and do not reach adjacent lines. Because of
this observation, the extents of the mapped faults are likely similar to or smaller than the fault
observed on 2021 Line 5.
Tectonic Stability
Faults that come up out of the Precambrian and die out within the Cambrian-Ordovician Knox
Group (interval between the Everton Formation and Davis Formation) likely have been inactive
since Ordovician time. However, there could have been some reactivation and formation of similar
faults resulting from deformation in the Illinois Basin related to the Ancestral Rocky Mountains
orogeny in Mississippian to Pennsylvanian time (McBride and Nelson, 1999).
The larger faults that extend shallower than the Maquoketa Group are also likely related to the
same deformation caused by the Ancestral Rocky Mountains orogeny. None of the mapped faults
extend to the surface and die out within Silurian to Pennsylvanian strata. Structures associated with
this tectonic event are from Mississippian to Pennsylvanian time (Nelson, 2010; McBride and
Nelson, 1999). These faults have likely been inactive since that time.
As for modern seismicity, this part of southern Illinois does have periodic but generally small
measurable earthquakes. However, there has been only one measurable event within 20 miles of
the PSGC facility, which was due to an explosion (USGS Earthquake Catalog
https://earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/search/). For more information on seismicity, see the
SEISMICITY section.

Seismic History
In Illinois, seismic frequency and intensity are most prominent in the southern part of the state
(Figure 19) where multiple seismic zones are located: The New Madrid seismic zone (NMSZ), the
Wabash Valley seismic zone (WVSZ), the Saint Genevieve seismic zone (SGSZ), and the Rough
Creek Graben (RCG). McBride et. al. (1997) investigated the potential relationship between
basement structures that appeared in a high-quality seismic reflection profile and a magnitude 5.5
earthquake that occurred in southern Illinois (Hamilton County) in 1968. The 1968 event was the
20th century's largest magnitude earthquake in the southern Illinois region (McBride et al 1997).

Reservoir (Storage) Units and Modeling Approach
Static reservoir model
The development of the static reservoir model is predicated upon a comprehensive dataset,
encompassing seismic surveys, well logs, core data, and other pertinent information. This
assemblage of data is crucial to the accurate representation of the subsurface reservoir
characteristics, notably the structural and petrophysical properties of the storage and confining
units.
The model is 45 × 45 miles (72.4 × 72.4 km) laterally and includes the St. Peter Sandstone, Everton
Formation, the overlying Joachim, Platteville, Trenton, and Maquoketa, and the underlying
Shakopee and Oneota (Table 12).

 36

The model is centered over LG #1, and the reservoir properties are heterogenous laterally and
vertically. The cell thickness is variable and ranges from 1 to 35 ft but averages 2.5 ft within the
St. Peter and Everton. There are 182 layers in the model, of which 113 are within the St. Peter and
Everton formations. The model has 181 ×181 × 182 cells, each cell is 1,320 × 1,320 ft (402 × 402
m) areally with a local grid refinement of 3:1 within a 10.25 × 10.25 mile area around LG #1,
WWDW #1, and the CO2 injection wells (I2S_North and I3E_West) wells. The two CO2 injection
wells are horizontal wells located on the same pad.
The LG #1 (i.e., site-specific) data used to determine porosity and permeability include laboratory
core measurements, geophysical logs (neutron porosity, density porosity, nuclear magnetic
resonance, and resistivity), and drill stem test (DST) data. Laboratory measurements, log
calculations, and well test measurements of porosity and permeability are summarized in Table
13, Table 14, and Table 15. These data will be augmented by laboratory measurements of core
samples and geophysical logs collected from any additional injection or monitoring wells drilled
for this project.
This model forms the foundational basis for subsequent dynamic simulations and represents a key
component of the Class VI permit application process.

Dynamic Model
Boundary Conditions
The top and bottom of the model are no-flow boundaries. The four sides of the Maquoketa Group
are closed. The four sides of the St. Peter Sandstone and Everton Formation are modeled as
infinite-acting (open) by attaching an infinite-acting Carter-Tracy analytical aquifer.

Initial Conditions
The initial state of the model was defined with four essential parameters, i.e., formation
temperature, formation pressure, brine density, and brine salinity. The average formation
temperature was set at 98°F, based on borehole temperature logs. The formation pressure was
determined as 1,583 psi. The fluid density was calculated to be 64.5 lb/ft³, considering the
reservoir’s salinity, pressure, and temperature. Finally, the reservoir salinity was measured as

 38

58,100 ppm from the LG #1 brine chemistry analysis. These values, all corresponding to an
elevation of 5,435 feet MSL, form the foundational input for the dynamic simulations (Table 9).

Area-of-Review Calculation
As indicated by U. S. EPA, “The boundaries of the AoR are based on simulated predictions of the
extent of the separate-phase (i.e., supercritical, liquid, or gaseous) plume and pressure front” (U.
S. EPA, 2013). The AoR is the greater of either the maximum areal extent of CO2 plume or
pressure front, or a combination of the two, over the duration of a storage project.
The pressure front is defined by the extent of the threshold differential pressure (∆pcrit), the
minimum pressure increase in the injection zone that initiates fluid flow from the injection zone
into the deepest underground source of drinking water (USDW) through a notional conduit (White
et al. 2019; Nicot et al., 2009). The approach for estimating the minimum pressure in this study
assumes fluid density in the wellbore to be uniform and equal to the fluid density in the injection
zone (Birkholzer et al., 2011).

Injection Facilities
The injection facilities at PSEC-CCS project site will include a distribution system that delivers
CO2 to injection wells from installed pipeline and consist of piping, valves, injection wellhead,
CO2 metering and control facilities.
The pipeline will be connected to two horizontal injection wells, on the same well pad. A short
lateral from the mainline will route CO2 to the injection well pad. The lateral will have an actuated
valve at the mainline or at the well location to provide isolation of the injection well from the
pipeline system. Flow meters will be installed to measure CO2 flow rate for each injection well.
The flow meter at the well has dual purpose for both pipeline leak detection and for measuring the
quantity of CO2 sequestered into the formation. A breakout spool between the surface piping and
the injection well will be installed for ease of access for any downhole activities that are required
during the life of the well. A back pressure valve may be installed downstream of the flow meter
or the wing valve on the wellhead may be used for this purpose.
No equipment to boost the pressure of the CO2 is planned for the injection locations. Based on the
reservoir parameters, the pressure of the CO2 from the pipeline will be sufficient for injection into
the formation. A small electrical load will be required to operate actuated valves, instrumentation,
and communication devices at the injection well pad. Power could be supplied by the grid or from
a solar array with battery backup.

Piping and Valves
Pipe, valves, and fittings associated with the laterals will be consistent in design with the mainline.
The yield and wall thickness for each line will exceed design pressure requirements of the system
(2,500 psig). Where necessary, material will be selected to minimize differences in wall
thicknesses for transitions diameter. All materials shall conform to the applicable API, ASTM,
MSS or ASME minimum requirements.
Valves in the process flow to the injection well will include an automated valve for isolation at the
injection well location, manual valves for isolation of the meter run, and a back pressure valve for
startup operation of the system. For automated valves, operation will be confirmed at least once
per year to ensure the close signals and position signals work and the valve operates properly.
Operation of an automated valve might occur if the CO2 product entering the pipeline was out of
specification, due to high or low pressure upset, or if there was an integrity event along the pipeline.
No pig launcher or receivers are planned for the laterals associated with the injection wells due to
the short lengths of piping.

Metering and control (including control skids)
The flow of CO2 into the pipeline from each CO2 source end and at each injection location will be
measured so a continuous mass balance can be done on the pipeline system. Typical flow
measurement consists of a Daniels type orifice meter tube or a Coriolis meter. The specific design
and size of the meter tube will depend on flow conditioning devices and the flow capacity at each
location. Each measurement tube will be equipped with pressure and temperature instrumentation
and isolation valves for maintenance. The flow computer with the meter will be able to record data
locally and connect to a Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) module so data can be transmitted

 57

to a control center. If an orifice meter is used, it is important for the flow computer to have good
property set information and good density correlation for the pressure/temperature range of the
CO2 stream.

Pressure control
The control focus of the pipeline is on the balance of flow and pressure throughout the system. A
control center will monitor the operation of the pipeline to ensure the required pressure at injection
wells is met and will coordinate with CO2 sources to increase pressure into the pipeline as needed.
The pressure of the fluid in the pipeline will be measured at each CO2 source entering the pipeline,
at mainline valve stations along the pipeline, and at each injection well to provide a pressure profile
for the pipeline system. Pressure will be measured with an indicating transmitter that will display
the value locally and transmit the value to a PLC module at the control center. Rosemount and
Siemens offer transmitters that are standard in the industry, but there are others that are similar.
Pressure in the pipeline will be maintained in dense phase for normal operation. A back pressure
control valve will be located at each injection well to be used when starting flow or pressuring up
the formation. Under normal operations the back pressure valve is expected to be fully open. Flow
measurement into and out of the system will be measured and monitored continuously and the
operator will be alerted if significant variations in flow rate occur.

Testing and Monitoring Plan
This Testing and Monitoring Plan describes how the PROJECT OWNER will monitor the site

Deep Monitoring
Pre-Injection Phase
The Testing and Monitoring Plan describes how Project owner will monitor the Project site
pursuant to 40 CFR 146.90. The data acquired by the monitoring and testing procedures will be
used to demonstrate that injection wells are operating as planned, that the CO2 plume and pressure
front are evolving as predicted, and that there is no endangerment to underground sources of
drinking water (USDW). Additionally, the monitoring and testing data will be used to validate and
refine geological models and simulations used to forecast the distribution of the CO2 within the
storage zone, support AoR re-evaluations, and to demonstrate non-endangerment. Results of the
testing and monitoring activities described in the Plan may trigger action according to the
Emergency and Remedial Response Plan.
The Testing and Monitoring Plan will utilize direct and indirect monitoring technologies that will
monitor:
 • Injectate composition to demonstrate that it is consistent with the permit
 • Corrosion of well materials and components
 • To determine whether CO2 or brine has migrated Above the Confining Zone
 • USDW groundwater quality
 • Well integrity over the injection phase of the project

 58

 • Near well-bore environment using pressure fall-off testing
 • Development of the CO2 plume and pressure front in the storage formation over time

Injection operations will be monitored using a range of techniques and methods. Continuous
recording devices will monitor wellhead injection pressure, temperature, and flow rate. A Coriolis
flow meter, which measures mass flow rate directly, or an orifice meter which measures flow
volume with a calculated mass flow rate, will be installed on the injection line at surface.
The annular pressure between the tubing and the injection casing strings and the annular fluid
volumes also will be monitored on a continuous basis. These data will be linked into a supervisory
control and data acquisition (SCADA) system to record the operations data, control injection rates,
or initiate system shutdown, if needed. The SCADA system can also be used to adjust the volume
of annular fluid, and thereby pressure, in the annular space to meet the operational and regulatory
objectives. Pressure and temperature will be measured continuously using pressure gauges to
establish a wellhead-to-packer pressure correlation. This correlation can be used to calculate the
injection pressure at the reservoir (perforated interval) at any time using the wellhead and
downhole pressure data. The reservoir pressures and temperatures will also be used to calculate
the injection rate at the reservoir, and the injection volumes will be used to update the
computational models at regular intervals throughout the injection phase of the project (AoR and
Corrective Action Plan).
Pre-operational logging and testing (See Narrative) will establish baseline mechanical integrity of
the injection wells. External mechanical integrity will be monitored continuously using distributed
temperature sensors (DTS) mounted to the exterior of the injection well casing and cemented into
place. External mechanical integrity will be confirmed through annual logging and compared back
to baseline logging data to identify deflections from that could indicate fluid flow behind the casing
(40 CFR 146.90 (e)). Annual testing will include oxygen activation logging, temperature logging
(wireline or DTS), or noise logging.
Monitoring wells will be used to evaluate the plume and pressure front development in the
injection zone, and to assure containment and protection of USDWs. The ACZ wells will be
located on the injection pad.

Oxygen Activation Log
A wireline tool is deployed to activate oxygen by emitting high-energy neutrons from a neutron
source. The activated isotopes emit gamma radiation which is measured by the wireline tool.
Gamma ray measurements are used to calculate water flow direction and velocity. If water flow
outside of the casing is detected it could indicate the potential loss of external mechanical integrity.
To minimize false positives, a calibration will be performed and measurements will be confirmed
at several nearby depths and/or under a minimum of three varying injection rates.
Temperature Log
Temperature logging detects leaks by measuring temperature anomalies due to fluid movement
adjacent to the well bore. Fluid leaks from the wellbore are typically at different temperatures
compared to native fluids. Temperature logs are run after the well has been shut-in long enough
for temperature effects to dissipate, leaving a relatively simple temperature profile (typically ~36
hours). While the absolute gradients may differ due to injection history, the relative profiles should
be consistent. If there has been a leak of fluid out of the well, there may be anomalous heating or
cooling effect as compared to the baseline or another log. Gradient variation due to lithologic
changes are expected. Distributed fiber sensing or electric wireline deployed temperature
measurement devices can be used and should be of sufficient resolution and sufficiently calibrated
to detect changes.
Pulsed Neutron Monitoring
Pulsed neutron logging detects leaks by measuring changes in the capture cross section of the
fluids and gases in the pore space of the rock using a wireline tool that emits neutrons which are
slowed to a thermal velocity through elastic and inelastic collisions with the nuclei of the
environment’s elements and ultimately captured. These interactions are sensitive to fluid type and
saturation changes in the formation and in the casing-formation annulus. Therefore, pulsed neutron
measurements can be used to monitor the formation fluids as well as identify mechanical integrity
problems. The pulsed neutron Sigma (Σ) is the thermal neutron capture cross section or the rate at
which thermal neutrons are captured by the formation matrix and fluids. The capture cross section

Annulus Pressure Test Procedures for Injection Well
After the injection wells are completed, including the installation of tubing, packer, and annular
fluid, a test of the well’s internal mechanical integrity will be performed by conducting an annular
pressure test (Standard Annulus Pressure Test (SAPT)). This is a short-term test wherein the fluid
in the annular space between the tubing and casing is pressurized, the well is shut-in, and the
pressure of the annular fluid is monitored for leak-off. EPA Region 5 (EPA, 2008) requires
comparison of the pressure change throughout the test period to 3 percent of the test pressure (0.03
x test pressure). If the annulus test pressure decreases by this amount or more, the well has failed
to demonstrate internal mechanical integrity. If the annulus pressure changes by less than 3 percent
during the test period, the well has demonstrated internal mechanical integrity. If the well does not
meet this requirement, the tubing and packer may need to be removed from the well to determine
the cause of the leak. EPA Region 5 guidance (EPA, 2008) for conducting the test will be consulted
when performing this test.
The general procedure for the test is summarized from EPA 2008 and includes:
 1. The tubing/casing annulus (annulus) will be filled with liquid. Temperature stabilization of
 the well and annulus liquid is necessary prior to conducting the test. This will be achieved
 by filling the annulus with liquid and either ceasing injection or maintaining stabilized
 injection (i.e., continuous injection at a constant rate and constant injection fluid
 temperature) before and through the test.
 2. No unapproved substances will be added to the annulus liquid.

 63

 3. After stabilization, the annulus will be pressurized to a surface pressure of no less than 300
 psig. A positive pressure differential between the pressure in the annular space and the
 injection tubing pressure of at least 100 psi will be maintained throughout the entire
 annulus (from the top of the packer to the surface). Specific gravity differences between
 liquids in the annulus and the tubing should be accounted for when determining the
 appropriate test pressure.
 4. Following pressurization, the annular system will be isolated from the source of pressure.
 The annulus system will remain isolated for a period of no less than 60 minutes.
During the active CO2 injection phase, internal mechanical integrity will be continuously
monitored by the well annular pressure maintenance and monitoring system. After the SAPT test
period has been completed, the valve to the annulus will be opened and liquid returns from the
annulus observed and measured.

Injection and Pressure Fall-Off Test Procedures
Baseline pressure fall-off tests (PFO) will be conducted as described in the Testing and Monitoring
Plan and in the Pre-Operational Testing Plan. The objective of the testing is to periodically monitor
for changes in the near well bore environment that would impact injectivity or cause injection
pressures to increase (U. S. EPA, 2013). Baseline pressure fall-off testing will be performed in
each injection well.
A PFO test has a period of injection followed by a period of no-injection or shut-in. Normal
injection will be used during the injection period preceding the shut-in portion of the falloff tests.
However, if the rate causes relatively large changes in bottomhole pressure, the rate may be
decreased. A minimum of one week of relatively continuous injection will precede the shut-in
portion of the falloff test. The PFO data will be measured using a downhole gauge sampling at 5-
second intervals. The gauges may be those used for day-to-day data acquisition, or a pressure
gauge conveyed via wireline. Surface or downhole gauges will be used to inform test duration.
The shut-in period of the falloff test will be adequate to assure that enough pressure transient data
are collected to calculate the average pressure. Quantitative analysis of the measured data is used
to estimate formation characteristics, including transmissivity, permeability, and a skin factor. The
measured parameters will be compared to those used in site computational modeling and AoR
delineation.

Injection operations, discussed in the subsequent section, will be monitored using a range of

Infrastructure Monitoring
Data will be measured, recorded, and transmitted from each injection well site to a central control
center (this may be through fiberoptic or point to point tower communication). It is expected that
there will be one control center for the pipeline, but a mirror image of the data may be available at
multiple sites. The data collected will be managed with a supervisory control and data acquisition
(SCADA) system. The SCADA system will be configured to process the data in real time and

 70

continually compare the pipeline operation to expected values to identify a potential leak or line
integrity issue.
Pressure and CO2 flow will be the primary variables that are monitored within the SCADA system.
A material balance of the pipeline system will be done continuously (input – output) and there will
be a difference that is within an acceptable range based on normal operational experience of the
pipeline. The SCADA system will be designed to identify when the system operation falls outside
of the normal parameters and will alarm control room operations.
Monitoring of pressure will also be done with the SCADA system. Because the pressure measured
down the pipeline will vary based on flow rate and other conditions, there will be a correlation of
expected pressure drop based on CO2 flow. If the pressure measurement falls outside of normal
parameters, the SCADA system will alarm control room operations and further investigation
would be required.
The SCADA system will also manage and monitor the actuation of shut down valves on the
pipeline and the operation of back pressure valves on each well. Certain events or pressure/flow
conditions would cause a valve to shut and isolate a segment or area of the pipeline. Specifics of
how the SCADA system operates equipment will be determined in the detailed engineering phase
of project.
Field inspection of pipeline operations will be done on a periodic basis. A visual inspection is often
the best method to identify a small leak on a pipeline system. Visual inspection will include riding
or flying the right-of-way quarterly and daily operation of meter stations and/or pump stations.

Proposed injection procedures for the project injection wells incorporates short-term maintenance,

The primary operational constraint would be imposed by potential limitation of permitted injection

However, the maximum (surface) injection pressure will not exceed 90% of the fracture pressure.

Contingency plans will be in place to identify situations where potential plant and/or process upset
conditions may occur and take appropriate measures which are protective to the local area and the
environment by shutting in the wells and monitoring their pressure falloff. Operational
contingency plans for all injection wells include potential downtime periods when annual injection
well testing, maintenance, well service, and stimulation occur. These plans include the following:
 • Annual testing
 • Monitoring downhole and on surface
With two permitted injection wells, one well would normally be operational while one well is
tested or serviced for maintenance.
The availability of both wells and adhering to proper operations practices, including regular well
maintenance and service, will reduce most injection well down-time and should eliminate the
unlikely occurrence of one or both wells being simultaneously unavailable for use. In the unlikely
event that both wells are temporarily unavailable or are out of commission, the CO2 may be vented
to the atmosphere for that limited period until operations and injectivity are re-established.

Description of Plugging Procedure
Notifications, Permits, and Inspections
The PROJECT OWNER will notify the regulatory agency at least 60 days before plugging the
well and provide updated Injection Well Plugging Plan along with relevant design updates, cement
formulations, and necessary certification documents as appropriate. Plugging plans will be
initiated with the approval of UIC Program Director.

Plugging Procedures - Narrative
Upon completion of the project, or at the end of life of the I3E West injector, the well will be
plugged and abandoned to meet the requirements of 40 CFR 146.92. The plugging procedure and
materials will be designed to prevent any unwanted fluid movement, to resist the corrosive aspects
of carbon dioxide/water mixtures, and to protect any USDWs. Any necessary revisions to the well
plugging plan to address new information collected during logging and testing of the well will be
made after construction, logging, and testing of the well have been completed. The final plugging
plan will be submitted to the UIC Program Director for approval.

Based on bottomhole pressure measurements, the well will be flushed with buffer fluid and
corrosion inhibitor. The selected buffer fluid composition will be aimed at minimizing corrosion
risk over the PISC period. A minimum of two tubing volumes will be injected without exceeding
fracture pressure. An external MIT will be conducted prior to plugging as outlined in Table 33. If
a loss of mechanical integrity is discovered, the well will be repaired prior to proceeding with the
plugging operations. Detailed plugging procedures are provided below.

The entire well casing will be cemented to surface at the time of construction and will not be
retrievable at abandonment. After the injection is terminated permanently, the injection tubing and
packer will be removed. After the tubing and packer are removed, buffer fluid compatible with
injectate and formation fluid will be injected to establish a static equilibrium to prevent CO2 flow
out of the injection zone. Once the pressure control is accomplished, a permanent bridge plug made
with corrosion-resistant alloy will be placed in liner to isolate the injection zone. Next, balanced-
plug placement method will be used to plug the remainder of the well per the schedule outlined in
Table 33 and illustrated in Figure 35. The cement(s) formulated for plugging will be compatible
with the CO2 stream. The exact cement formulation and required certification documents will be
submitted to the regulatory agency with the well plugging plan prior to conducting the operation.
The operator will report the wet density and will retain duplicate samples of the cement used for
each plug. All the casing strings will be cut off at least 3 ft below the surface, below the plow line.
A blanking plate with the required permit information will be welded to the top of the cutoff casing.

Detailed plugging procedures:

1. The regulatory agency will be notified at least 60 days before plugging the well and provide an
updated plugging plan, if applicable. Review historic MIT data and remedial work.

 74

2. Mobilize rig onto I3E West and rig up. All CO2 pipelines will be marked, locked, and tagged
out and noted with rig supervisor prior to rig up.

3. Conduct an operations review and safety meeting.

4. Record bottom hole pressure (BHP) from down hole gauge and calculate kill fluid density.

5. Open all valves on the vertical run of the tree and check pressures and flow.

6. Pressure test the pump and line to 2,500 psi (or 1.10 maximum reservoir pressure). Fill tubing
with kill weight brine (as determined by bottom hole pressure measurement). Bleeding off tubing
occasionally may be necessary to remove all air from the system. Test casing annulus to 1,000 psi
and monitor. If there is pressure remaining on tubing, rig to pump down tubing and inject two
tubing volumes of kill weight brine. Monitor tubing and casing pressure for 1 hour. If the well is
dead, then nipple up blowout preventers (BOPs). Monitor casing and tubing pressures.

7. If the well is not dead or the pressure cannot be bled from the tubing, rig up slickline and set a
plug in the lower profile nipple below packer. Circulate tubing and annulus with kill weight fluid
until the well is dead. After the well is dead, nipple down the tree, nipple up blow-out preventers
(BOPs), and perform a function test on the BOPs. BOPs should be appropriately sized single pipe
rams on top and blind rams in the bottom ram for tubing. Pressure test pipe rams and blind rams
to 250 psi low and the greater of maximum working pressure or 110% reservoir pressure for the
high test. Test annular preventer to 250 psi low and 70% maximum working pressure high. Test
all full open safety valves (TIWs), BOPs choke and kill lines, and choke manifold to 250 psi low
and the greater of maximum working pressure or 110% reservoir pressure for the high test.

NOTE: Make sure the casing valve(s) are open during all BOP tests. After testing BOPs pick up
tubing string and unlatch seal assembly from seal bore. Rig up slick line and lubricator and remove
X-plug from well. Pump via lubricator or wing valve and circulate until the well is dead.

8. Pull out of hole with tubing and control lines laying it down as tubing is removed.

NOTE: Ensure that the well is over-balanced by a minimum of 100 psig so there is no backflow
due to swabbing and there are always at least 2 well control barriers in place.

9. Pull the seal assembly, pick up a tubing work string, and run in hole with the packer retrieving
tools. Latch onto the packer follow manufactures recommendations for unsetting the packer and
pull out of hole laying down same. Next, confirm well mechanical integrity by performing one of
the permitted external mechanical integrity tests presented in Table 1. Contingency: If unable to
pull seal assembly, rig up electric line and make cut on tubing string just above packer.

NOTE: Cut must be made above packer at least 5-10 ft MD. If unable to pull the packer, pull the
work string out of the hole and proceed to the next step. If problems are noted, update the cement
remediation plan (if needed), and confer with the agency prior to plugging operations.

 75

10. TIH with work string to total depth (TD) with bit and scraper. Monitor fluid displacement to
keep the hole full at all times. Circulate the well and prepare for cement plugging operations. TOH.

11. Isolate the horizontal section of the well by placing permanent bridge plug made with
corrosion-resistant alloy approximately 100 ft above the top of injection zone at 4,710 ft MD. Place
approximately 860 ft of CO2 resistant cement (Evercrete) over bridge plug in 7.0-inch liner and
9.625-inch casing. Balanced plug placement method will be used to set the plug using a slurry of
density 15.8 ppg with a yield of 1.11 ft3/sack. Pull out of plug and reverse circulate tubing. After
the plug has been set, pull tubing from the well and shut in for 12 hours. Trip in hole with tubing
and tag cement top. Pull tubing back out of the well.

12. Pump 1,950 ft of gel spacer (composition TBD) to fill the 9.625-inch casing before additional
cement plugs are set. Place permanent bridge plug approximately 100 ft above the kickoff point at
1,900 ft MD to prevent cement plug from migrating. Next, mix and spot 300 ft of balanced plug
as described in Figure 1. Using a density of 15.8 ppg slurry with a yield of 1.11 ft3/sack,
approximately 115 sacks of Class G cement will be required. Pull out plug 10 stands above
calculated TOC and reverse circulate tubing at least 2X. Leave well static until cement hardens
and tag calculated top to confirm setting depth. Top off if needed. Round trip tubing to drift if
needed.

13. Pump 1034 ft of gel spacer (composition TBD) to fill the casing before setting cement plug in
shallower depth to protect the Underground Source of Drinking Water (USDW). Place permanent
bridge plug approximately 200 ft below the surface casing at 566 ft MD. Next, mix and spot 566
ft of balanced plug in 9.625-inch casing (approximately 208 sacks Class G mixed at 15.8 ppg with
yield 1.16 ft3/sack). Pull out of plug and reverse circulate tubing. Pull out plug 10 stands above
calculated TOC and reverse circulate tubing at least times (2X). Leave well static until cement
hardens and tag calculated top to confirm setting depth. Top off if needed. POOH, nipple down
BOPs and cut all casing strings below plow line (minimum 3 ft below ground level or per local
policies/standards and other identified requirements). Lay down all work string, etc. Rig down all
equipment and move out. Clean cellar to where a plate can be welded with well name onto lowest
casing string at 3 ft, or as per permitting agency directive.

NOTE: Actual cement volume will depend upon actual weight of the casing within the plugged
zone as well as the length of plug set as determined during the plugging operation.

14. The procedures described above may be modified during execution as necessary to ensure a
plugging operation that protects worker safety as well as all identified USDWs. Any significant
modifications due to unforeseen circumstances will be described in the Plugging report. Plugging
report will be submitted within 60 days after plugging is completed and include:

 • Pumping charts and all lab information.
