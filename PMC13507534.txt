Molecular characterization of Vibrio cholerae strains from clinical samples during the 2025 epidemic in the Republic of the Congo
Authors: Tarcisse Baloki Ngoulou, Pembe Issamou Mayengue, Nicole Prisca Makaya Dangui Nieko, Felix Koukouikila-Koussounda, Doria Moise Kaya-Ongoto, Dachel Aymard Eyenet Boussam, Léadisaelle Hosanna Lenguiya, Jordy Exaucé Demboux Lyelet, Isaac Samuel Onyankouang, Rancia Colombe Diakouka Diambalou, Reiche Golmard Elenga, Fabien Kangoula-Dia-Kikoudi-Kia-Louzala, Marianne Mouko, Christoffer Mounkala, Diane Dibantsa, Wivine Salva Mouellet, Igor Louzolo, Ghislain Dzeret Indolo, Louis Regis Dossou-Yovo, Fabien Roch Niama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Emergence of a highly virulent Vibrio cholerae O1 Ogawa clone in Congo Brazzaville.•Whole-genome sequencing revealed a direct link with Nigerian strains.•Multiresistance profile including the genes catB9, dfrA1, AlmG, and varG.•Confirmed presence of major virulence factors ctxA/B, tcpA/B, ToxT, and zot.•Call for cross-border coordination and mass vaccination.

Introduction

Cholera is an acute and highly contagious infectious disease that causes severe vomiting and profuse diarrhea, leading to severe dehydration and even death in affected individuals if left untreated, with serious repercussions for public health [1]. It is caused by toxin-producing bacteria belonging to serogroups O1 and O139 of the species Vibrio cholerae (V. cholerae). The first epidemic began in 1817, followed by pandemics in 1829, 1852, 1863, 1881, 1889, and 1961, with the latter continuing to this day [2,3]. Global spread patterns have varied, although most of Asia, Africa, Europe, and the Americas have been affected at one time or another [4,5]. Regular and increasingly frequent resurgences of cholera epidemics now pose a major public health challenge throughout the world, particularly in resource-limited countries. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recorded more than 2.6 million cases of cholera over the past two decades, resulting in more than 60,000 deaths, with a particularly high burden of resurgence in sub-Saharan Africa [6]. In fact, a cholera surveillance network in Africa supported by the WHO and the Global Task Force for Cholera Control reported in 2023 a worrying resurgence of cholera worldwide, with alarming increases in at least 30 countries. The African Region recorded the highest number of outbreaks, with 17 countries affected, reflecting the serious situation regarding the disease [7]. More specifically, preliminary data from mid-December 2023 revealed alarming figures: 59,114 cases and more than 1771 deaths in Malawi, 38,879 cases and 153 deaths in Mozambique, 1330 cases and 34 deaths in Zambia, 47,028 cases and 390 deaths in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), 11,290 cases and 249 deaths in Zimbabwe, 29,399 cases and 428 deaths in Ethiopia, 80 cases and 40 deaths in Uganda, 348 cases and 1 death in South Sudan, 21,252 cases and 508 deaths in Cameroon, and 21 cases and 5 deaths in Republic of Congo [6,7].

The situation continues to evolve, with 190 suspected cases and no deaths in the Central African Republic reported during the fourth epidemiological week of 2025, and 40,487 suspected cases and 1102 deaths reported in the DRC between January and July 2025 [8]. In the Republic of Congo (RoC), several suspected cases were reported in the Ile Mbamou health district in the department of Brazzaville and in the Mossaka health district in the department of Congo-Oubangui during early July 2025. On July 10, 2025, in response to these reports, the Ministry of Health and Population, in collaboration with the WHO country office, dispatched a rapid response team to the affected areas to conduct a thorough investigation of the situation and collect biological samples for laboratory analysis. On July 24, 2025, three samples were received at the National Public Health Laboratory for bacteriological diagnosis, and two tested positive for V. cholerae by culture. These results led to the official declaration of a cholera epidemic in the RoC on July 26, 2025. In this article, we report the results of the bacteriological and molecular analyses of strains isolated from suspected cases.

Methods

Identification of suspected cases and collection of sociodemographic and clinical information

The WHO standard definition for cholera was used to identify suspected cases in health centers in the affected departments. Forms for collecting sociodemographic (age, sex, and geographic origin) and clinical (symptoms, date of onset of signs, and hydration status) information were completed by health personnel at the time of sampling. Each form was linked to the patient's code to ensure anonymity and data traceability.

Type, period, and setting of the study

The outbreak of the cholera epidemic was followed by a cross-sectional study conducted over a period of a three-month period from July to September 2025. Four departments of the country were included: Brazzaville, Congo-Oubangui, Plateaux, and Pointe-Noire. The selection of these areas was based on the notification of confirmed cases. Samples were collected from local health facilities and sent to the National Public Health Laboratory for bacteriological and molecular analysis.

Sample collection and transport

Diarrheal stool samples from patients were collected aseptically using clearly labeled sterile containers. A small amount of fecal matter was also collected by inserting a swab 2-3 cm deep into the rectum and rotating it. The swab was then inserted into the bottom of the tube containing the transport medium (Cary-Blair). Finally, the tightly closed tube was placed in a zip-lock bag and transported together with the stool container in a cooler within 2 hours of collection or within 48 hours when stored in the transport medium.

Bacterial culture

Stool or rectal swab samples were seeded directly on agar containing Thiosulfate-Citrate-Bile-Saccharose (TCBS) and incubated at 37°C for 18-24 hours. At the same time, the same samples were first enriched in peptone water and subsequently cultured in TCBS agar at 37°C for 18-24 hours.

Phenotypic identification

After culture on TCBS agar, yellow, round, smooth, and generally large colonies suspected of being V. cholerae were identified using conventional bacteriological methods (Gram staining and oxidase test) and biochemical methods using the analytical profile index 20E from BioMérieux, France (urea, indole, and sugar fermentation). Vibrio cholerae antisera Polyvalent Inaba and Ogawa kits, Antisera O139 “Bengal” (Deben Diagnostics Ltd), and Difco™ Vibrio cholerae Antiserum Poly (BD Company) were used to confirm the serogroups and serotypes of the suspected strains. The slide agglutination test using Poly O1 and O139 antisera for the identification of V. cholerae strains belonging to serogroups O1 and O139 was performed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

The procedure involved placing a drop of specific antiserum (Poly O1, Ogawa, Inaba, and O139) on a slide, followed by the addition of a bacterial colony. The mixture was mixed using circular motions, and the slide was then rotated for one minute. Visible agglutination was then observed. A positive reaction with the Poly antiserum indicated a suspected V. cholerae O1 strain, whereas reactions with Ogawa or Inaba antisera enabled determination of the serotype. Agglutination with the O139 antiserum confirmed the O139 serogroup. The absence of agglutination with all tested antisera suggested the presence of a non-O1/non-O139 strain or another Vibrio species.

Antibiotic sensitivity testing

The standard Mueller-Hinton agar diffusion method was used to perform the strain sensitivity test, as recommended by the Antibiogram Committee of the French Society of Microbiology [9]. The antibiotics tested were ciprofloxacin, doxycycline, tetracycline, azithromycin, trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole, ampicillin, and nalidixic acid.

Genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from 15 strains of V. cholerae using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's recommendations. The DNA extracts obtained were stored at -20°C until use.

Library preparation and sequencing with the Illumina DNA Prep kit

Genomic DNA extracted from the colonies was subjected to library preparation using the Illumina DNA Prep kit, strictly following the manufacturer's recommendations. The addition of specific adapters and the simultaneous fragmentation of DNA was made possible by enzymatic fragmentation. The fragments thus generated were then amplified by polymerase chain reaction, during which unique dual indexes were incorporated to ensure multiplexing of the samples. The libraries were purified using magnetic beads, normalized to obtain an equimolar concentrations, and combined into a single pool. The final mixture, including a 1% PhiX internal control, was loaded into an iSeq 100 Reagent v2 cartridge (300 cycles). Sequencing was performed in paired-end mode, ensuring adequate read depth for subsequent genomic analyses.

Bioinformatic data analysis

The raw sequencing data were first subjected to quality control using FastP and then analyzed using the PeGas tool, which integrates several assembly and annotation modules. To identify antimicrobial resistance genes and known virulence factors, the genomes were screened using the Abricate tool integrated into the pipeline with curated databases. The filtered reads were aligned to the V. cholerae O1 El Tor N16961 reference genome using burrows-wheeler aligner and then processed with SAMtools. The consensus sequences for V. cholerae were generated using the command-line tool BCFtools, with the sequences corresponding to chromosomes 1 and 2 of strain N16961 (GenBank accession numbers: CP028827.1 and CP028828.1) used as the reference genome. The whole-genome assembly data of V. cholerae isolated from the National Center for Biotechnology Information database were selected for multiple sequence alignments. The selected genomes were obtained from isolates recovered during cholera epidemics in Africa during 2018-2024. A selection of 51 background sequences from Uganda, Malawi, Zimbabwe, the DRC, Cameroon, and Nigeria was included in the analysis. Sequence alignment was performed using Parsnp version 1.7.4, and the phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE version 3.0.1 for Mac, with the most appropriate substitution model determined using the maximum likelihood approach and 1000 boostrap replicates. The generated tree was edited using the Interactive Tree of Life online tool.

Statistical analyses

The main descriptive statistical measures (mean, quartiles, minimum, and maximum) and group comparisons using Fisher's exact test were performed using R software (version 4.3.1).

Molecular typing of serogroup and serotype

The analyses performed confirmed that the isolated strains were V. cholerae of serogroup O1. Analysis of the typing genes revealed the presence of the wbeT gene, which is essential for assigning the strain to the Ogawa serotype. All identified strains belonged to the O1 biovar El Tor N16961 and were of the Ogawa serotype.

Phylogenetic analysis was performed on seven isolates with sequences showing good coverage (>70%). The resulting phylogenetic tree demonstrated that African strains of V. cholerae collected during recent epidemics in Africa between 2018 and 2024 were closely related (Figure 1). Several well-defined clades were observed, particularly among isolated from the DRC, between isolated from Malawi and Zambia, within Uganda, and between isolates from Nigeria and Cameroon. These groupings were strongly supported by bootstrap values. Virulence genes

The virulence genes ctxA, ctxB, tcpA, tcpB, torN, toxT, torR, zot, Ace, and hlyA were identified in all V. cholerae strains.

Discussion

Sociodemographic analysis of the 57 suspected cases recorded in this study revealed a mean age of 32.09 years. The majority of cases involved individuals aged 15-49 years (52.63%), followed by those aged 50 years or older (22.81%). These findings are consistent with the observations made by Siamalube et al. [10], who pointed out that young and middle-aged adults are often the most affected by cholera in sub-Saharan Africa due to their increased mobility and exposure to poor sanitary conditions. The distribution by gender showed almost equal numbers of men (49.12%) and women (50.88%), with no significant difference. This balanced distribution has been found in the study conducted by Mbewe et al. [11] where men and women had a similar exposure to risk factors in Zambia

Genome sequencing revealed V. cholerae O1 biovar El Tor N16961 and serotype Ogawa. A study conducted in South Korea in 2021 on 601 V. cholerae genomes showed that 504 belong to serogroup O1 and 19 were structural variants of O1, named O1 var. The same study also highlighted that the Ogawa serotype is assigned to V. cholerae strains based on the presence of a key marker called the wbeT gene, which codes for a methyltransferase [12] and was also identified in the genomes of the analyzed strains in the present study. Similar findings were also reported by Leekitcharoenphon et al. [13], who reported the presence of V. cholerae strains belonging to the O1 serogroup, El Tor variant, in stool samples from the DRC. However, a study from Bangladesh reported the predominance of the Inaba serotype of V. cholerae between 2020 and 2021, with respective rates of 91% and 96% [14].

Phylogenetic analysis showed high genetic proximity (divergence <0.000001) with high bootstrap values (100), which rules out the possibility that the epidemic was caused by an ancient strain or an evolving indigenous lineage in the RoC. Instead, these findings indicate local transmission within an epidemic cluster without evolutionary diversification, suggesting a very recent introduction, probably from Nigeria, because no sequences from 2025 originating from other countries bordering the RoC are currently available in public databases (GenBank and European Nucleotide Archive).

The results of this study do not represent an isolated event or a spontaneous emergence from the local aquatic environment but rather fit within the overall dynamics of the seventh El Tor pandemic in sub-Saharan Africa [15]. At the regional level, the phylogenetic tree shows a close genetic relationship, characterized by a very low number of single-nucleotide variants between our strains and those from Nigeria, confirming a common evolutionary origin. Historically, West Africa, and Nigeria in particular, has served as a major epidemic hub and endemic hotspot from which stable directional waves of V. cholerae spread, especially from west to east toward Central Africa. Weill et al. [15] suggested that rapid cross-border spread, whether direct (from person to person) or indirect (through fecal contamination of the environment), is mainly driven by human factors such as migration flows, regional trade, and fishing activities. In the tree topology, this human link is reflected by an almost perfect genetic match between isolates collected in different geographic areas but connected by major communication routes. Our observations align with the model of Weill et al. [15], who demonstrated the rapid spread of epidemic sub-lineages across Central Africa via the Congo River and its tributaries, confirming that population movements play a greater role than local aquatic environmental reservoirs. Nevertheless, these human movements are linked to very specific local vulnerabilities. Girotto et al. [16] showed that the population's limited access to drinking water and sanitation, combined with rising temperatures, constitutes critical infrastructure and environmental factors that maintain cholera endemicity and increase the epidemic risk throughout sub-Saharan region. This spread dynamic also coincides with a critical change in antimicrobial resistance profiles: since 2000, almost all strains collected in sub-Saharan Africa have exhibited an imported multi-drug resistant profile [15]. Our results fit this overall pattern, while also showing that resistance to certain specific antibiotics can be acquired locally.

The genes CatB9 and dfrA1 were present in the RoC strains analyzed in the present study. Recently, a study conducted in the DRC in May 2024 on the draft genomic sequencing of 4 V. cholerae strains linked to the cholera outbreak in Kamituga, South Kivu, revealed the presence of the genes CatB9, dfrA1, floR, Sul2, and aph(6)-Id, which are responsible for resistance to chloramphenicol, trimethoprim, florfenicol, sulfamethoxazole, and streptomycin, respectively [13]. It is worth noting that some of these genes were absent in the present study, which could be explained by genomic plasticity and the fact that antimicrobial resistance evolves over time, often varying among regions and countries. A previous report in the DRC showed that the loss of antibiotic sensitivity occurs over the years and that V. cholerae can acquire resistance to multiple antibiotics during this process [17]. Analysis of the genome of our V. cholerae strains revealed the presence of the genes catB9, catB9_1, and catA4, which are involved in resistance to phenicols. Although this resistance was not tested phenotypically, the molecular detection of these determinants aligns with the findings of Lee et al. [12], who, using the VicPred prediction tool, showed that 65% of V. cholerae strains carried the catB9 gene.

Although the dfrA1_8 gene was detected, V. cholerae strains showed phenotypic susceptibility to sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (SXT). This finding differs from the observations of Jubyda et al. [14] in Bangladesh, where a 97% resistance rate was reported, which was closely associated with the SXT mobile genetic element rather than with the dfrA1_8 gene alone. As Rashed et al. [18] reported, the dfrA1_8 gene is carried by this SXT element, which simultaneously integrates sulfonamide resistance genes (sul2). The absence of the intSXT gene suggests that the dfrA1_8 determinant is located on a genetic locus independent of the complete SXT integrative and conjugative element, preventing the co-selection of sulfonamide resistance and allowing sulfamethoxazole to maintain its inhibitory activity through synergy, thereby explaining the observed susceptibility phenotype.

The presence of the tet(J) and tet(J)_1 genes, which encode resistance to tetracycline and doxycycline via an efflux system, in one strain did not correlate with phenotypic resistance to these antibiotics. Recently, Sebastian et al. [19] reported, in California, genotypic and phenotypic discordance in Vibrio spp. for the tet(34) and tet(35) genes, both of which are involved in tetracycline resistance. The reason for this discordance was that gene expression fluctuated depending on the environment, suggesting that these genes may be poorly expressed or nonfunctional. In Austria, Lepuschitz et al. [20] found that genotypic and phenotypic results in non-O1/non-O139 V. cholerae associated with human infections were consistent, suggesting they were influenced by genotypic and phenotypic traits. All V. cholerae strains identified in the present study were phenotypically resistant to ampicillin and nalidixic acid. However, the molecular detection results were not consistent with these findings, as no genes involved in resistance to these drugs were detected. A study by Das et al. [21] on resistance mechanisms in V. cholerae revealed that, in the absence of resistance genes, V. cholerae may exhibit antimicrobial resistance through efflux systems.

According to Gupta et al. [22], the presence of biofilms in V. cholerae O139 prevented the entry of certain molecules, such as ampicillin, which resulted in resistance to them. Our results could also be explained by the presence of the dfrA1 gene, which causes resistance not only to ampicillin and nalidixic acid but also to tetracycline [23].

In addition, several virulence genes were identified in V. cholerae, including the major virulence genes ctxA/ctxB, tcpA/tcpB, torN/toxT, torR, zot, Ace, and hlyA. Jubyda et al. [14] discovered that the CtxA, tcpA, Ace, and Zot genes were present in V. cholerae strains, confirming their pathogenicity. The presence of these genes supports the observation that all strains are capable of producing cholera toxin and colonizing the intestinal tract, which may explain their role in the severity of cholera infections observed.

Given the persistence of seventh pandemic clones and the demonstrated genomic connections between Nigeria's endemic hotspots and our 2025 isolates from the RoC, it is crucial to move away from purely national surveillance strategies. Cholera control programs in the sub-region need to synchronize their epidemiological databases in real time and incorporate standardized genomic tracking to detect the movement of new sub-lineages across borders at an early stage. Intervention efforts, whether through improving drinking water access infrastructure, sanitation, or oral vaccination campaigns, should no longer be implemented uniformly but instead strategically targeted toward human mobility hubs and island or coastal communities living along the Congo-Oubangui river system, which acted like a real 'highway' for transmission during this epidemic crisis.

Conclusion

This study confirms that the 2025 cholera epidemic in the Republic of the Congo was caused by V. cholerae O1 biovar El Tor, serotype Ogawa, characterized by distinct repertoire of virulence genes and a specific resistance profile. Genomic sequencing allowed the precise identification of virulence and resistance determinants and underscored the urgency of systematic molecular surveillance. Furthermore, the homogeneity of resistance profiles, combined with maintained sensitivity toward doxycycline and ciprofloxacin, favorably guides treatment protocols. However, the emergence of additional genes in certain strains demonstrates the genetic plasticity of the pathogen. Overall, this study improves our understanding of local dynamics and calls for strengthening diagnostic and genomic capacities in order to more effectively contain future resurgences.
