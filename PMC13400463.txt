Analysis of Human Placental Tissues Using Gas Chromatography High Resolution Accurate Mass Spectrometry to Characterize Exposure to a Range of Semivolatile Organic Compounds
Authors: Michelle Misselwitz, Nicholas J. Herkert, P. Lee Ferguson, Heather M. Stapleton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Measuring chemical exposures during fetal development is critical to understanding the environmental influences on chronic disease risk. The placenta, in direct contact with the fetus throughout gestation, offers a vital matrix for assessing fetal chemical exposures. However, analytical challenges remain due to its heterogeneous composition and the need to detect trace levels of diverse chemical classes. This study aimed to develop and validate a robust analytical method to quantify and screen for environmental contaminants in human placental tissue using QuEChERS-based extraction that was optimized for high recovery and reproducibility while minimizing matrix interference. Analysis was performed using gas chromatography–high resolution mass spectrometry (GC-HRMS) with isotope dilution for targeted quantitation of 119 semivolatile organic compounds (SVOCs). Method performance was assessed using spiked placental samples and a NIST-certified reference material. The validated method was applied to 50 placental samples collected from a North Carolina pregnancy cohort (Project HOPE 1000). The optimized method displayed acceptable recoveries (50–150%) for up to 75% of target analytes across four spike levels ranging from 0.1 to 10 ng/g wet weight (ww). A total of 72 compounds were detected in at least one placental sample, including several brominated flame retardants, organophosphate esters, polychlorinated biphenyls, pesticides, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and phthalates. This validated GC-HRMS method effectively quantifies a broad range of environmental contaminants in placental tissue while balancing throughput, sensitivity, and versatility and can be used to support exposome research in pregnancy cohorts.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12403-026-00791-y.

Introduction

Fetal development is a critical timepoint where environmental stressors, such as chemical exposure, can have a cascading effect on lifetime health and disease risk (Heindel et al. 2015). Measuring exposure to environmental chemical contaminants during this vulnerable period is therefore critical to elucidating this connection. During pregnancy, women are ubiquitously exposed to mixtures of environmental contaminants present in indoor air, food and other consumer and personal care products, where exposure can occur via inhalation, diet, and even dermal absorption. The fetus is then subsequently exposed in-utero through transfer across the placenta. These contaminants include legacy pollutants that have long been phased out but continue to persist and bioaccumulate, for example organochlorine pesticides (e.g. DDT) and polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs), in addition to replacement chemicals that may or may not be as toxic as their predecessors, such as organophosphate ester-based flame retardants (OPEs). And yet other chemicals may be classified as emerging chemicals with an incomplete understanding of exposures and toxicities. The magnitude of maternal-fetal transfer is influenced by the chemicals physiochemical properties, metabolism, and binding affinities to active transporters in the placenta (Zhang et al. 2021).

Traditional biomonitoring methods during the prenatal period typically use biofluids such as maternal blood, urine, or cord blood as a proxy for fetal exposure. However, the placenta has emerged as an important biomatrix to characterize the chemical exposure of the developing fetus, and for some chemicals, may provide a more precise measure of fetal exposure compared to maternal blood or urine due to toxicokinetic considerations. Measurement of chemical metabolites is often used to assess exposure; however not all metabolic pathways are fully understood, and several chemicals may metabolize to a common metabolite. Analysis of environmental chemicals in the placenta is advantageous because the tissue is in direct contact with the fetus throughout gestation, accumulates chemicals over time, is noninvasive, commonly disposed of as medical waste, and offers a substantial amount of tissue for analysis (Contini et al. 2023). The health and function of the placenta is crucial to the health of the fetus as it functions as the respiratory, immune, endocrine, excretory, and metabolic systems for the developing fetus. These systems are dynamic throughout pregnancy and respond to maternal stressors. Adverse stressors, such as chemical exposure, may impact the health of the pregnancy and predispose individuals to poorer health outcomes later in life (Thornburg and Marshall 2015). For example, exposure to organophosphate esters (OPEs) during pregnancy has been associated with higher odds of preterm birth (Oh et al. 2024; Gan et al. 2023; Hoffman et al. 2018). Several chemicals such as brominated flame retardants (BFRs) (Eick et al. 2024), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) (Lignell et al. 2013), phthalates (Stevens et al. 2023; Hwa et al. 2022), pesticides (Lin et al. 2023), polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) (Sewor et al. 2024; MoghaddamHosseini et al. 2023) and OPEs (Lian et al. 2025; Lapehn et al. 2025) have all shown adverse toxicological effects. Importantly, no single chemical or chemical class is responsible for all environmentally related diseases (Pearce et al. 2021; Mitro et al. 2015; Govarts et al. 2025). Therefore, this research aims to measure a broad suite of environmental chemicals in the human placenta.

The analytical challenges of measuring environmental chemicals in the placenta are numerous. The placenta is considered more challenging to analyze compared to other biological matrices to assess fetal exposure (e.g., cord blood, human milk) due to its heterogenous nature (Needham et al. 2011). Extracting and purifying samples for chemical analysis must strike a balance between efficiently isolating target xenobiotic chemicals with a broad range of physical-chemical properties while minimizing interference from co-extracted endogenous substances. Additionally, the instrumentation used in the analysis must be sensitive enough to detect levels as low as parts-per-trillion. To overcome this challenge, current biomonitoring programs typically measure a selective subset of environmental chemicals. This approach eliminates potential interferences (endogenous and exogenous chemicals) through meticulous sample cleanup, ensuring a high degree of quantitative certainty. Unfortunately, this does not provide a complete measurement of all environmental chemicals, which misrepresents the chemical load of the developing fetus and leads to underestimation of exposure. For example, targeted analysis of persistent organic pollutants (POPs) often fractionates the non-polar target compounds and discards the polar fraction (e.g., phenols, OPEs) during cleanup.

The other analytical approach to biomonitoring is a non-targeted, or non-selective methodology. This approach employs minimal sample preparation to reduce chemical biasing and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) to detect chemical features found in the placenta. Non-targeted analysis requires extensive post analysis data reduction techniques to filter the large number of chemical features detected in samples (Herkert et al. 2024). Furthermore, non-targeted analysis includes both endogenous and exogeneous chemicals that can be difficult to differentiate, especially when endogenous chemicals are often several orders of magnitude higher in concentration than xenobiotic chemicals. Annotation of chemical features is a time-consuming process where the confidence of structure assignment is scored on multiple lines of evidence (Koelmel et al. 2022). Confirmed identification of a chemical feature is limited to the availability of chemical standards.

While non-targeted analysis is essential for exposome research which aims to assess the totality of environmental exposures starting with the prenatal period it can be difficult to compare results across studies due to the lack of quantitative information. This research aimed to develop an extraction and analysis method to detect environmental chemicals in the placenta utilizing gas chromatography with high resolution mass spectrometry (GC-HRMS) to simultaneously support targeted quantitative analysis and non-targeted screening. Targeted semivolatile organic compounds (SVOCs) in this study have LogKow values ranging from 0.8 to 13.6 and included compound classes such as BFRs, OPEs, PAHs, pesticides, phthalates and others. Selection of extraction solvents, cleanup methods and instrument parameters were evaluated to optimize the reproducibility, recovery and accuracy of the targeted analytical method, being broad enough to support non-targeted screening in the future. The optimized method was then applied to 50 placental samples collected as part of a prospective pregnancy cohort recruited from 2018 to 2025 in central North Carolina, USA.

Materials and Methods

Analytical Standards

All solvents used for the analysis were HPLC-grade or better. Standards were purchased from Wellington Laboratories, Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, and AccuStandard. A native bulk mixed analyte standard (100–200 ng/mL) was prepared in acetonitrile for spiking experiments and calibration curves. It included 19 BFRs, 25 PAHs, 13 PCBs, 2 Phenols, 19 Pesticides, and 33 OPEs. For a complete list of standard names please see Table S1. The calibration was prepared by diluting the mixed spike standard, with levels at 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10, 25, 50 and 100 ng/mL. To each calibration level 10 ng of a mass labeled internal standard mix was prepared consisting of four labeled BFRs, two labeled PCBs, two labeled OPEs and two labeled pesticides, and five labeled PAHs (see Table S1). A recovery calibration curve consisting of two mass labeled BFRs, five PAHs, two OPEs and one phthalate was prepared at 200%, 100%, 50% and 25% internal standard recovery levels (see Table S1).

Sample Preparation

Method development was performed on anonymous placental tissues donated as part of a pilot study conducted in our lab (Ruis et al. 2023). The anonymous placental samples were removed from the − 20 °C freezer, allowed to slightly thaw, pooled, then homogenized in a 300 mL pre-cleaned glass food processor. The homogenized placental tissues were weighed (either 10 g–5 g portions) and aliquoted into 50 mL glass centrifuge tubes.

Original Method

The first extraction method examined was based on our prior study (Leonetti et al. 2016), in which the homogenized placental tissue was freeze-dried for 24 h prior to extraction. After the tissues were dried, three of the replicate samples were spiked with 50 ng of the mixed native standard and one sample was not. The samples were sonicated with 3 fresh portions of 10 mL hexane: dichloromethane (1:1 v/v) for 15 min. After each sonication, the tube was centrifuged, and the resulting supernatant was combined in another clean glass centrifuge tube, for 30 mL total. The combined extracts were concentrated to ~ 1 mL using a ThermoScientific SpeedVac. The extracts were purified using 8 g prepared Florisil dry packed in a glass column. The Florisil was prepared by cleaning with solvent, baking overnight at 450 °C, then de-activated with 2.5% LC-MS water by weight. Approximately 1 cm of baked Na2SO4 was added to the top of the Florisil column. The column was conditioned with 25 mL ethyl acetate and 25 mL hexane: dichloromethane (1:1). The column remained wet while the sample was loaded to the top, rinsing the centrifuge tube with fresh solvent two times to ensure complete transfer. The sample was eluted with 40 mL hexane followed with 40 mL ethyl acetate. The eluate was collected in a 250 mL Turbovap glass vial then concentrated and solvent exchanged into hexane with a final volume of 1 mL. The extracts were filtered with a 0.45 μm nylon syringe filter. Prior to analysis the resulting extracts were spiked with an internal standard mixture containing mass labeled compounds from each target compound class. This method was also repeated using hexane: acetone (1:1 v/v) in place of hexane: dichloromethane (1:1).

QuEChERS Method

For the QuEChERS extraction method, we compared two different extraction solvents, hexane: acetone (1:1 v/v) and acetonitrile. A 5 g portion of the pooled homogenized anonymous placenta was weighed into a 50 mL glass centrifuge tube. Five samples were fortified at 1 ng/g with the mixed standard, three samples remained unfortified. 20 mL of extraction solvent was added to the tissue sample and sonicated for 1 h. One packet of QuEChERS original ultrapure extraction salts containing 4 g MgSO4 and 1 g NaCl (Restek) were added to each sample and immediately shaken vigorously by hand for 30 s to ensure thorough distribution of the magnesium sulphate. The samples were sonicated for an additional 15 min prior to centrifugation for 10 min. The supernatant was removed and placed in a clean 50 mL centrifuge tube. The extracts were concentrated to ~ 2 mL in the SpeedVac. Subsequent purification methods were selected based on the extraction solvent. The hexane: acetone extract was cleaned using a 2 g Florisil solid phase extraction cartridges (SPE) (Restek). The cleanup method was scaled down (2:8) from the original cleanup method described above. The acetonitrile extracts were cleaned using a 500 mg PSA (primary, secondary, amine) SPE cartridge (Restek). The SPE cartridge was prepared by adding 6 mL of acetone to the top of the cartridge, eluted dropwise, and dried under vacuum for 5 min. Approximately 1 cm of baked MgSO4 was added to the top of the prepared SPE cartridge. The concentrated extract was added to the top of the bed and an additional ~ 2 mL of acetonitrile was used to rinse the centrifuge tube to ensure complete transfer of analytes onto the cleanup column. The extract was gravity eluted dropwise. When no more drips were being captured, a weak vacuum was applied to capture any remaining extract. Both SPE eluents (Florisil and PSA) were further concentrated using to ~ 0.5 mL using an N-evap, syringe filtered and spiked with the internal standard mixture prior to analysis.

Lipid Determination

Lipid content was determined using gravimetric analysis. An additional 0.5 g of placental tissue was weighed into a 12 mL glass centrifuge tube, freeze dried for 24 hrs., extracted with 3 × 1 mL portions of hexane: acetone and evaporated to dryness for gravimetric lipid determination. Lipophilic compounds such as BFRs, PCBs and p, p’-DDE are presented in lipid adjusted units.

Instrumental Analysis

Analyses were performed using the Thermo Scientific Orbitrap gas chromatograph with high resolution accurate mass spectrometer (GC-HRMS). The QExactive GC-HRMS operated in electron impact (EI) ionization mode was used to quantify all compounds except the high molecular weight brominated flame retardants which were quantified on the Exactive GC-HRMS operated in negative chemical ionization mode (NCI) (see Table S1). 1). The QExactive was equipped with a non-coated analytical guard column connected to a Restek Rxi-SVOC column using an SGE µ-union connector. The instrument was operated simultaneously in scan and timed-selected ion monitoring (t-SIM) mode. Eight timed windows were set up to monitor 3–5 selected masses to target BFRs, PCBs and OPEs (see Table S2). The Exactive instrument operated in NCI mode was equipped with a customized 10 m x 0.25 mm ID, 0.10 μm Agilent DB-5 MS column (a 30-meter column was purchased and cut into 3 × 10 m portions). Data Analysis

Analytical peaks were manually reviewed using the ThermoScientfic Tracefinder software 5.1. The raw data were exported into a .csv file. R studio was used to create an R markdown (RMD) file (Supplement 2) to calculate method detection limits (MDL), detection frequencies, and create an export report file with blank subtracted calculated concentrations. The R markdown uses three input files (1) raw datafile exported directly from Tracefinder, (2) meta datafile that has sample weights and other sample specific information, and (3) peak file that groups the compounds into compound classes. The RMD file links the input files to the output files and serves as a consistent and reproducible collection of scripts to calculate and report instrument data. Two output files are generated. The first is an html or pdf data analysis overview that includes any sample or analyte specific notes, quality control parameters (MDLs, ISTD recovery, QC standard stability) and summary data (min, med, max, detection frequency) (Supplement 2). The second output file is an excel data file with reported chemical concentrations that have been blank-subtracted, and weight-corrected. These data will be shared among other researchers; therefore, data consistency, reproducibility, and findability are key considerations when archiving data for later use.

The MDL is calculated according to our laboratory’s standard operating procedures. First the instrument detection level (IDL) is determined as the lowest detected calibration point for each compound. Two different approaches were used to determine the average blank value dependent on whether the target analyte is present or not in corresponding lab processing blanks. Any compounds that were not detected in the lab blanks are imputed by generating a random number between 0 and the IDL. This produces variation across the blank replicates. The average lab blank (LBAVG) value is calculated for each compound and subtracted from the sample amount reported in the raw data file. The MDL is calculated as 3 times the standard deviation of all blanks included in that processing batch (up to 10). If the blank subtracted amount is greater than the MDL the value is normalized to sample mass and reported, values < MDL and > 0 after blank subtraction are flagged but used for summary statistics to reduce left censoring (George et al. 2021).

Method Validation

The selected method was validated using replicate spiked anonymous placental tissue at 0.1 ng/g (n = 3), 0.5 ng/g (n = 7), 1 ng/g (n = 3) and 10 ng/g (n = 3). Non-spiked placental samples (n = 3) served as matrix blanks. In addition, we evaluated the accuracy of the method using a Standard Reference Material, SRM2974a – Organics in Freeze-Dried Mussel Tissue (Mytilus edulis) from NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology). Triplicate samples of 1 g each of the NIST SRM2974a with 5 mL LC-MS water were taken through the method. All samples were spiked with mass labeled internal standards just prior to instrumental analysis. An internal standard calibration curve was used to calculate raw concentrations. Percent recovery was calculated for each compound (% Recovery = calculated amount/spike amount * 100). For the NIST samples, the spike amount was replaced with the certified reference value. Acceptable recoveries followed the generally accepted limit of 50–150% for environmental health data (Udesky et al. 2019). Reproducibility was evaluated using percent relative standard deviation of the spike replicates (%RSD = Standard Deviation/Mean *100). Results with an RSD < 25% were considered acceptable.

The validated method was then applied to 50 placental samples each dissected into maternal and fetal portions, collected as part of the Project HOPE 1000 (Health Outcomes related to Pregnancy and early-life Exposures) prospective cohort study. This cohort is part of the Duke’s Children’s Health and Discovery Initiative based in Durham, North Carolina. Pregnant women were recruited at Duke University Health clinics in the first or second trimester. Participants were surveyed on their health, diet, potential environmental exposures, and socioeconomic status during study visits each trimester. All participants provided informed consent. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the Duke University School of Medicine.

Results and Discussion

Method Development

A previously published method was successfully used in our lab to quantify brominated flame retardants in human placenta (Leonetti et al. 2016). Modification of this method was necessary to expand the target analyte list, reduce the use of hazardous solvents, and minimize sample analysis time. The expansion of the target analyte list was to better reflect the wide range of SVOCs that are of human health concern and to support non-targeted analyses in the future (Hammel et al. 2020; Levasseur et al. 2021; Samon et al. 2023).

Original Method

As pesticides, phthalates and OPEs were on the expanded target list, and they are susceptible to acid-catalyzed degradation, the extract purification method was shifted to a Florisil method in place of the previously published acidified silica cleanup. The original method used a 1:1 mix of hexane: DCM. In addition, we wanted to evaluate the extraction efficiency of hexane: acetone due to the greater toxicity of DCM. Side by side evaluations at 50 ng/g fortification show very similar distributions of analyte recoveries across the compound classes (Fig. S1). There were no statistically significant differences in percent recovery between hexane: acetone and hexane: DCM, with 75% and 71% of the compounds within the acceptable percent recovery range respectively. These results indicated that switching to hexane: acetone for subsequent experiments would be acceptable and result in the use of less toxic solvents.

The original method also involved multiple labor- and solvent- intensive steps, which prompted the need for alternative approaches with higher throughput to support research that includes analysis of hundreds of samples. To reduce sample processing time and solvent usage, several solid phase extraction (cSPE) cartridges and dispersive (dSPE) cleanup approaches were evaluated following sonication extraction of freeze-dried tissues. These approaches included a 2 g Florisil cSPE (Restek), the Florisil cartridge with a layer of either z-sep sorbent (Supelco) or PSA (Restek) on top of the cartridge bed, and a carboprep/PSA (Restek) combination cartridge. The Florisil SPE had low recoveries of BFRs and the PSA + carboprep SPE resulted in significantly lower recoveries of PAHs. Out of all of the cleanup approaches evaluated the Zsep + Florsil SPE combination had the best overall recoveries across the different compound classes with the exception of pesticides which resulted in slightly lower recoveries compared to the other approaches (Fig. S2). Reducing the sample size to improve cleanup effectiveness was considered, however sample detectability and representativeness were deemed a higher priority.

QuEChERS Method

An alternative method was evaluated based on the QuEChERS (Quick, Easy, Cheap, Effective, Rugged, and Safe) sample preparation approach. The QuEChERS approach is a robust and efficient method for extracting multiple compound classes from complex matrices. The salt-assisted liquid-liquid extraction approach has been proven to be well-suited for a diverse range of analytes, including pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and environmental contaminants. The method is relatively high-throughput and has been successfully adapted for the analysis of biological tissues in environmental studies (Cloutier et al. 2017; Ninga et al. 2022; Haimovici et al. 2016; Baduel et al. 2015; Santana-Mayor et al. 2023). Two extraction solvents were evaluated, hexane: acetone (1:1) and acetonitrile. The typical extraction solvent used in QuEChERS (acetonitrile) is amenable to both gas chromatography and liquid chromatography, which further broadens the potential scope of analysis. One immediate benefit to utilizing a QuEChERS extraction is the elimination of the freeze-dry step, as the method was developed for high-water (> 70%) content fruits and vegetables. Placental samples were weighed pre and post freeze-drying to determine that the samples were on average ~ 80% water. As one of the primary concerns of utilizing acetonitrile was the effectiveness of extraction for high molecular weight lipophilic compounds, experiments were conducted at 1 ng/g spike level. The solvent to tissue ratio was increased from 2:1 to 4:1 and the samples were sonicated for 1 h prior to salt addition to further increase the extraction effectiveness of QuEChERS as recommended by Haimovici et al. (2016). In traditional QuEChERS based extractions a 1 mL aliquot of the 10 mL extract is taken through the cleanup procedure and on to analysis. As the levels of chemical contaminants in the placenta are often in the sub ng/g level, this step was altered and the entire extract was concentrated to ~ 2 mL prior to cleanup.

The extract cleanup step was selected based on the extraction solvent. A normal phase 2 g Florisil cSPE cleanup, scaled 2:8 from the original sonication extraction method was used for the non-polar (i.e., normal) hexane: acetone extracts. A weak anion exchange cSPE column (PSA – primary, secondary amine) was used for the more polar acetonitrile extracts (i.e., reverse phase). The acetonitrile extraction with PSA cleanup outperformed the hexane: acetone with Florisil cSPE cleanup with 73% of the compounds recovered in the acceptable range compared to only 28% (Fig. 2). In addition to better analyte recoveries, the acetonitrile based QuEChERS extraction had better reproducibility compared to hexane: acetone as 71% of the analytes had less than 25% RSD for the spike replicates (n = 5), compared to only 29% of the analytes had acceptable reproducibility for the hexane: acetone. The hexane: acetone based QuEChERS extraction was more aggressive (as observed in gravimetric analysis) and resulted in more endogenous compounds in the final matrix. This hindered instrumental analysis as noted by irregular peak shapes and non-detected peaks in the presence of abundant matrix background. The acetonitrile based QuEChERS extraction approach with a simple PSA SPE cartridge balanced efficiently extracting the analytes of interest without an overwhelming amount of matrix interferences. In addition, as the PSA cSPE method does not require any elution solvents it reduces both processing time and solvent usage. The sample preparation method required 3 days of processing for 24 samples and only 26 mL of solvent per sample. As the number of compounds that were acceptably recovered in the QuEChERS acetonitrile-based method was equal to that of the original hexane: acetone sonication method and it saved more than a day of processing time and 130 mL of solvent per sample, it was chosen as the final method (Fig. S3) and applied to the 50 HOPE 1000 placental tissues.

Instrumental Analysis

The ThermoScientific GC Orbitrap High Resolution Mass Spectrometer (GC-HRMS) was used to quantify 119 semivolatile environmental chemicals (see Table S1). Instrument parameters were optimized to minimize the MDLs for target chemicals while maintaining full data acquisition across multiple orders of magnitude. Full spectrum data acquisition generates thousands of chemical features per sample and can be screened for potential spectral library matches to support non-targeted analyses in the future. The screening data provides additional context in fetal exposure patterns. It was desirable to have a non-selective sample preparation approach so that non-target screening of metabolites and other potential emerging contaminants of interest could be monitored. However, this places an additional burden on the mass spectrometer to measure trace level contaminants in the presence of abundant co-extracted matrix. QuEChERS extraction with PSA cleanup resulted in extracts that were clean enough for at least 100 sample injections prior to a loss of signal and requiring instrument maintenance, as monitored through QC vial injections. For the EI instrumentation (Q-Exactive) a 5 m x 0.25 mm guard column improved the peak shape of the early eluting peaks (especially with acetonitrile extraction solvent) and helped with column maintenance in between batches as it reduced retention time shifts. The Q-Exactive HRMS utilizes a curved linear ion trap (C-trap) to accumulate and focus ions prior to injection into the orbitrap mass analyzer. While this configuration produces high resolution (60,000) with high mass accuracy (< 2 ppm) spectral data, the Orbitrap has a limited ion capacity. Exceeding the Orbitrap ion capacity can result in false negative detection for analytes of interest that coelutes with high abundance ions from matrix interferences. This phenomenon was observed during method development at the lowest spike level of 0.1 ng/g (0.5 ng on-column) for 29 of the 119 target compounds spanning multiple chemical classes (PCBs, BFRs and OPEs) (Fig. 3).

To address this problem, timed windows of selected ion monitoring (t-SIM) were collected simultaneously with the full mass acquisition to alleviate the ion trap overload which increased the detectability of target analytes in the presence of an abundance of matrix. The instrument alternates each data point between full mass acquisition and acquisition of selected ions timed around the compounds of interest. Optimization of the isolation window and multiplex (MSX) parameters were important to balance data acquisition speed and eliminating (or reducing) the Orbitrap capacity limitation in the presence of abundant matrix. Isolation windows of 2 m/z, 4 m/z, and 8 m/z were evaluated for the selected compounds of interest at the 0.1 ng/g spike level. The isolation window sets the target ion at the center of the acquisition window so that an isolation window of 8 m/z would collect +/- 4 m/z values around the target ion. Peak areas were greatest in full scan mode followed by 8 m/z isolation window, 4 m/z isolation window and finally the 2 m/z isolation window. Calculated concentrations followed the trend of 8 m/z > 4 m/z > full scan > 2 m/z. Therefore, the isolation window was set to 8 m/z. As the target compounds of interest have quantitation ions that are greater than the coeluting matrix by more than 20 m/z, increasing the isolation window to 8 m/z did not compromise the selectivity of the t-SIM approach. Multiplexing the t-SIM windows improved the data acquisition speed, resulting in more points across the peak than collecting each selected m/z window individually. In this case, 3–5 selected m/z windows are accumulated in the c-trap before being injected into the orbitrap mass analyzer. As this is being done by rapidly alternating between full scan and t-SIM, grouping selected masses for compounds that elute near each other produces less scan events, thus increasing the number of data points acquired per peak. Setting up the dual acquisition modes allowed for the detection of 10 compounds of interest that were not detected in full scan mode and increased the calculated concentration (and therefore % recovery) of an additional 19 compounds at the 0.1 ng/g spike level (Table S2).

While most of the target compounds are measured via GC-EI-HRMS, the high molecular weight BDEs are analyzed using GC-NCI-HRMS. The high molecular weight BDEs require a shorter GC column (10 m compared to 30 m) to minimize on-column thermal degradation, especially for BDE 209. Instrument detection limits for both ionization methods were similar for tribrominated through pentabrominated diphenyl ethers. However, there was a clear divulgence at the hexabrominated diphenyl ether, BDE 153, with IDLs of 0.05 ng and 0.1 ng for NCI and EI respectively. The use of t-SIM wasn’t necessary for GC-NCI-HRMS as the softer chemical ionization produces less ions and thus the Orbitrap capacity did not impact target analyte detectability.

Method Validation

Method precision and recovery were validated using placenta replicates spiked with either 10 ng/g (n = 3), 1.0 ng/g (n = 3), 0.5 ng/g (n = 7) and 0.1 ng/g (n = 3) using a prepared analyte standard solution. These values were selected as they cover the median values of our target compounds commonly found in placental tissue (Contini et al. 2023). As there are no certified reference materials of placenta to measure accuracy, and spike recoveries may not fully evaluate the true extraction of chemical contaminants inherent in samples, a matrix proxy was selected. The NIST standard reference material of freezedried mussel tissue was selected due to the comparable lipid content to placentas and certified values for BDEs, PAHs, PCBs and pesticides included in our target compound list. Method accuracy was assessed using NIST SRM 2974a by calculating the recovery based on the reported certified value in the Certificate of Analysis (COA). A majority (89%) of the certified compounds had an accuracy between 50 and 150% of the COA values (Fig. S4). Levels of analytes found in the SRM spanned 3 orders of magnitude ranging from 0.2 ng/g to over 200 ng/g.

Recoveries for the 4 matrix spike levels are shown in Fig. 4; error bars represent the standard deviation of the replicates. At the 10 ng/g and 1 ng/g spike level 75% of the target analytes had acceptable recoveries. At the lower spike levels (0.5 ng/g and 0.1 ng/g) 71% and 54% (respectively) of analytes were within the 50–150% recovery range. Of these, about 25% were in the more conservative 80–120% recovery range. Most compounds that dropped out of the acceptable range at the lowest spike level were due to lab background levels. This was especially apparent for phthalates. Reproducibility was very good across all spike levels as 92% of all compounds had < 25% relative standard deviation (RSD) of the replicates. A closer inspection across the compound classes revealed that the method performed well for the broad range of compounds targeted. The recoveries of BFRs and PCBs were very reproducible (< 15% RSD) across all spike levels, except for BDE 209 that had more variability both within the replicates and across the spike levels. This highlights the ability of the QuEChERS method to effectively extract lipophilic compounds in a lipid-rich matrix such as placental tissue. For PAHs, there appears to be a relationship between method performance and molecular weight. The lighter PAHs had higher recovery values, but also higher variability, where the heavier PAHs had lower recovery values but better reproducibility. The pesticides and phthalates had the most variability across the individual compounds. As mentioned previously, the phthalates suffered from lab background contamination and therefore resulted in a high MDL. Steps such as using glassware instead of plastic when possible or solvent rinsing when unavoidable (cSPE tubes) were taken to reduce potential background interference.

The validated method was used to analyze 50 placental samples that were dissected into maternal and fetal portions and analyzed separately (total n = 100)collected as part of a prospective pregnancy cohort (Project HOPE 1000). Out of 119 target compounds, 72 were detected at least once and 8 were detected in at least 50% of the placenta tissues (Table 2). Legacy chemicals such as BDEs, PCBs and organochlorine pesticides (OCPs) are persistent, bioaccumulate, prevalent in the food chain and therefore often detected in human placenta. PCB 153 was the most frequently detected congener followed by PCB 118 and 138. PCB 118 was more frequently detected on the maternal side of the placenta (49% compared to 37%). The most commonly detected chemical in all the samples was p, p’-DDE, an OCP and major metabolite of dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT), detected in 100% of all samples with a max concentration of 192 ng/g lw. These values are in the same range as previously reported concentrations in human placental tissue (Contini et al. 2023). BDE 153, BDE 183, and BDE 47 were the most detected BDEs, however BDE 209 was the most abundant congener. Trends for BDEs continue to decline. Concentrations of BDE 47 and 153 were lower than a previously published study with placentas from women recruited in the same region, highlighting the decreasing concentrations over the past 10 years (2011 vs. 2021) (Leonetti et al. 2016). A more recent analysis of PBDEs in placental tissue had lower median levels as reported here but concentrations followed the same pattern as observed in this study where BDE 209 > BDE 153 > BDE 183 > BDE 47 (Liu et al. 2024). Concentrations of BDEs were generally higher on the fetal side of the placenta which is consistent with a previous study in our laboratory that observed the same trend.

As BDE 47 is analyzed in both EI and NCI modes and had equivalent IDLs, comparisons were made between the two analyses to determine which instrument platform performs better for the HOPE 1000 samples. BDE 47 was detected in 36% of the samples in both EI and NCI mode with generally good agreement of calculated amounts across the instrument platforms with an average relative percent difference (RPD) of 13% (+/- 25). In 50% of the samples both ionization methods resulted in < MDL. However, in 14% of the samples the NCI detected BDE 47, and the EI did not. It was concluded that NCI would be used to report values for BDE 47. A similar analysis was performed for the other BFRs that are included in both analyses. Except for BDE 28, all other BFRs had better detectability in NCI mode compared to EI.

Decabromodiphenyl ethane (DBDPE) was detected in 60% of the samples and was the most common alternative BFR detected and had a median concentration of 3.7 (maternal) and 3.8 (fetal) ng/g lw. DBDPE is an alternative flame retardant for similar applications to BDE 209 (Covaci et al. 2011). Commercial application of DBDPE has continued to increase while BDE-209 has been phased out since 2013(Shen et al. 2019). As these samples were collected after the phase out date (2021–2024) it is not surprising that the replacement DBDPE is more highly detected. No other reports for levels of DBDPE in human placenta have been published. This is concerning as the production volume and use continues to rise and there is evidence that DBDPE exposure can cause placental damage (Xie et al. 2025).

Organophosphate esters (OPEs) are widely used in consumer products for their flame retardant and plasticizer capabilities. Although prenatal exposure to OPEs has been linked to adverse birth outcomes (Gan et al. 2023) and alterations of the placental transcriptome (Lapehn et al. 2025), only one other study has directly measured OPEs in human placenta (Ding et al. 2016). Tris(2-butoxyethyl) phosphate (TBoEP) was detected more frequently in maternal tissue compared to fetal tissue (73% and 63%) with median concentrations of 0.422 ng/g and 0.397 ng/g respectively. The second most frequently detected OPE, tri-n-butyl phosphate (TnBP) were detected evenly on both sides of the placenta (37% and 39%). The levels previously reported in Ding et al., (2016) are significantly higher than this study, likely due to the different study areas, including proximity to e-waste recycling areas in the previous study. Although thought to be quickly metabolized, the detection of several OPEs in the human placenta highlights the need for further testing of OPEs in human tissues.

Nonylphenol (NP) and 4-tert-octylphenol (4tOP) are endocrine disrupting environmental chemicals that are not widely reported in placenta tissue, yet has shown to impact birth weight and neurodevelopment (Hu et al. 2025; Lv et al. 2016). In the HOPE 1000 cohort, 4tOP was detected more frequently on the fetal side of the placenta (73% fetal and 61% maternal) but had a similar median concentration across tissue types (0.04 ng/g). The max concentration of 1.16 ng/g was an order of magnitude higher than previously reported in placenta (Du et al. 2019). NP, analyzed here as a mixture of isomers were only detected in overall 29% of samples but had a max concentration of 801 ng/g. This is again an order of magnitude higher than previously reported (Huang et al. 2014). As these chemicals are not widely reported in placental tissue it is unclear if the differences in concentrations are regional, temporal, or in the case of NP measurement differences of the measured isomers.

The target phthalates and phthalate alternatives were not detected in more than 50% of the samples. However, this is not surprising as phthalates are quickly metabolized and excreted in urine. Dimethyl phthalate (DMP), and diisobutyl phthalate (DiBP) had the highest max concentrations of the phthalates detected, 2800 ng/g, and 2810 ng/g respectively. The terepthlate DEHT (di(2-ethylhexyl) terephthalate), often used as a replacement for DEHP (di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate) had a higher detection frequency (21% compared to 9%) and higher max concentration (1810 ng/g compared to 2.99 ng/g). As phthalates are commonly used as plasticizers in medical supplies (Wang and Kannan 2023), it is impossible to untangle the potential for contamination of the sample post birth and potential exposure to the developing fetus through the placenta. However, the occurrence of DEHP again highlights the need to monitor both legacy chemicals and their replacements to see how this shift in exposures may alter health outcomes.

Conclusions

This study establishes a validated analytical method capable of quantifying and screening a wide range of environmental contaminants in human placental tissue. The sample preparation approach balances precision and accuracy with sensitivity and throughput, a necessity for large cohort studies. The data analysis workflow using R markdown establishes consistency in evaluating quality parameters and reporting results for future research. Several chemical classes were detected in the HOPE 1000 pregnancy cohort, many with known or suspected toxicological effects, highlighting the placenta as a valuable matrix for characterizing fetal exposure. Additionally, the non-targeted screening capabilities of the GC-HRMS method allows further inspection of chemical features detected in placental tissue which provides a complimentary analysis to the quantitative targeted data presented here. This method provides a critical tool for measuring early-life chemical exposures and enables future research on how these exposures impact birth outcomes.
