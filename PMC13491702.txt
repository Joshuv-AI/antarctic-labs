Soil flooding suppresses Fusarium wilt in watermelon by modulating soil chemical properties and microbial community richness
Authors: HuiFang Lv, Ying Zhang, LingLing Wang, Zulan Ou, CongSheng Yan, HuanXin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soil flooding is a well-documented agricultural practice for disease control, but the underlying mechanisms are still poorly understood. The objective of this study was to investigate the effect of soil flooding on watermelon growth, soil chemical properties, enzyme activities, and the diversity and richness of microbial community structure, and to examine the relationships between these factors and wilt incidence. The results showed that soil flooding reduced the population of FON and Fusarium wilt incidence, promoted plant growth, increased soil AP and AK contents, increased UA and ALPA activities, but decreased soil SOM and AN contents (P < 0.05). Bacterial diversity and richness were significantly higher, whereas fungal diversity and richness were significantly lower under soil flooding (P < 0.05). The microbial community structure was significantly altered by soil flooding. The relative abundance of bacterial phyla such as Actinobacteriota, Chloroflexi, and Bacteroidota increased, whereas that of the dominant fungal phylum Ascomycota decreased. Compared with continuous cropping, soil flooding increased the relative abundance of potentially beneficial microbes like Geobacillus, Bacillus, Nocardioides, and Penicillium (P < 0.05). In addition, the relative abundance of potentially pathogenic fungi such as Fusarium was reduced on day 30 after soil flooding (P < 0.05). Soil AK and AN influenced both bacterial and fungal microbial communities. The SEM analysis revealed that the indirect pathways from bacterial and fungal community richness, AK, and AN to watermelon Fusarium wilt were the primary drivers of disease incidence. In conclusion, soil flooding improves soil chemical properties and rebuilds a healthy soil microbiota, thus effectively suppressing watermelon Fusarium wilt.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05469-8.

Introduction

Watermelon (Citrullus lanatus L.) is an important global crop and one of the most widely consumed fresh fruits. Driven by economic interests, intensive and high-intensity watermelon cultivation has become increasingly widespread in China. Fusarium wilt, caused by the soil-borne fungus of Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. niveum (FON), is one of the most severe diseases influencing the sustainable production of watermelon [1]. Once FON invades the soil, it is particularly difficult to remove, as the pathogen can survive for many years [2]. Several methods are commonly used to suppress this soil-borne Fusarium wilt, including soil sterilization, grafted seedlings, crop intercropping, disease-resistant varieties, and chemical control [3], but most of them have not generated consistent and effective results. Therefore, it is urgently necessary to develop effective strategies to enhance disease suppression.

In addition to direct pathogen suppression, the improvement of soil biochemical properties is a central approach for preventing both pathogen growth and occurrence of Fusarium wilt [4]. Soil nutrients are well known to be essential for the growth and development of both plants and microorganisms, as well as important factors in plant–disease interactions [5]. Soil pH is an important indicator of soil physicochemical properties and significantly affects crop growth and microbial activity [6]. Alterations of K, N, and organic matter have been reported to suppress Fusarium wilt in many crops [7–8]. Improved K nutrition in plant tissue has been associated with phenolics that inhibit sporulation of several pathogens and induce plant resistance [9]. The longer waterlogging lasts, the more pronounced its negative impact on soil nutrients [10].

Soil enzymes, primarily derived from microorganisms, plant and animal residues, and root exudates, serve as the principal catalysts governing soil biochemical processes. They play an indispensable role in organic matter decomposition, nutrient turnover, energy transformation, and the maintenance of overall environmental quality [11–12]. For example, the decline in soil urease and alkaline phosphatase activities is often associated with impaired nutrient conversion and reduced soil fertility [13], which can affect crop growth [6]. Soil enzyme activities are highly sensitive to environmental perturbations and changes in soil management practices [14]. Soil flooding substantially influences the activities of phosphatase and urease, with consequent effects on nutrient transformation processes [15]. Previous studies have shown that increased soil enzyme activity inhibits FON growth, leading to a reduced incidence of watermelon Fusarium wilt [16]. Therefore, elucidating the relationships among flooding regimes, enzyme activity, and soil properties is essential for developing effective strategies to suppress Fusarium wilt of watermelon.

Soil microbial communities play an important role in maintaining soil health and suppressing plant diseases [17]. Increased rhizosphere microbiome diversity in peanut reduced the incidence of root rot at maturation [18]. In addition, increasing studies has demonstrated that soil water content affects the activity and community structure of soil microorganisms [19]. For instance, prolonged submergence at 20 °C for 24 days has been reported to significantly influence the forest soil microbial community [20], and summer flooding has been shown to effectively reduce the incidence of cotton Verticillium wilt, an effect associated with a marked decline in the population density of the pathogen Verticillium dahliae [21]. These findings suggest that flooding-induced shifts in the microbial community may represent a viable pathway for suppressing soil-borne pathogens, a hypothesis that requires further validation in watermelon production.

The summer post-harvest fallow period of watermelon provides a natural window for flooding treatment, as elevated temperatures synergize with soil submersion to accelerate the inactivation of soilborne pathogens [22]. Building on previous reports that reductive soil disinfestation alters soil properties and microbial communities [23], we hypothesized that soil flooding would reshape the microbial community and improve soil biochemical properties, thereby suppressing watermelon Fusarium wilt. To test this hypothesis, we examined the changes in FON abundance, watermelon growth, soil biochemical properties, and microbial communities following flooding treatment, and further assessed their interrelationships with disease suppression. The overarching goal of this study is to establish a theoretical framework and technical guidance for flooding-mediated soil microecological improvement and disease management, while contributing to the sustainable use of soils in protected cultivation systems.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The watermelon (Citrullus lanatus L., variety 97103), a cultivar highly susceptible to Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. niveum (FON), was employed as the experimental plant material. The seeds were obtained from the Zhengzhou Fruit Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS).

Experiment design

The experiment was carried out in a plastic greenhouse at the experimental station of AnHui Academy of Agricultural Sciences (117°14′ E, 31°53′ N), Hefei, China, from March to July 2023. The plastic greenhouse had been under continuous watermelon cropping for four years and exhibited Fusarium wilt before the experiment. Soil properties were as follows: pH 6.93 (soil: water = 1:2.5); organic matter 57.48 g kg− 1; alkaline hydrolyzable N 182.52 mg kg− 1; available P 137.85 mg kg− 1; available K 751.36 mg kg− 1. Soil flooding treatment was conducted from July 7 to August 7, 2022. The experiment included three treatments: (1) continuously cropped watermelon soil (CK); and (2) continuously cropped watermelon soil was subjected to flooding with the water level maintained 2–3 cm above the soil surface. The irrigated soils were covered with plastic film (transparent, 80-µm thickness; Hefei Jingcheng Plastic Co., Ltd., Hefei, China) and incubated with temperatures within the range 40–60 °C for 15 days, the soil was air-dried and then used for autumn watermelon cultivation (F15); and (3) continuously cropped watermelon soil was subjected to flooding with the water level maintained 2–3 cm above the soil surface. The irrigated soils were covered with plastic film (transparent, 80-µm thickness; Hefei Jingcheng Plastic Co., Ltd., Hefei, China) and incubated at 40–60 °C for 30 days (F30). Each treatment comprises of three replicates, for a total area of 28 m² per replicate. Each plastic greenhouse contained three ridges (1 m wide), with a single planting row per ridge. The planting density was set at a row spacing of 1.0 m and a plant spacing of 0.4 m.

The watermelon seedlings at the four true-leaf stage were transplanted into plastic greenhouses (4 m wide, 28 m long), with three replicates per treatment and 70 seedlings per replicate (3 replicates × 3 treatments × 70 plants). The soils for all three treatments were sourced from the aforementioned post-treatment soils used for autumn watermelon cultivation in 2022. Additionally, the soils were fertilized with organic fertilizer at a rate of 3000 kg ha⁻¹, containing ≥ 2 × 10⁸ CFU g⁻¹ of effective viable bacteria, ≥ 50% organic matter, ≥ 1% amino acids, and ≥ 6% total N + P₂O₅+K₂O, along with K₂SO₄-based compound fertilizer at 200 kg ha⁻¹, with a total nutrient content of ≥ 51% (N: P₂O₅:K₂O = 26:10:15). Both fertilizers were supplied by SiErTe Fertilizer Industry Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China). The soils were irrigated weekly throughout the experimental period.

Eighty days after transplanting, soil samples were collected. From each replicate, twenty plants of similar growth status and vigor were randomly selected (3 treatments × 3 replicates × 20 plants), and the loosely adhering soil from the root systems was carefully removed. The tightly adhering soil was collected, homogenized, and pooled to constitute a single biological replicate, with three such replicates established per treatment. Subsequently, each sample was divided into two portions: one portion was allocated for analysis of soil chemical properties, enzyme activities, and FON population density, while the other portion was immediately frozen at − 80 °C for subsequent DNA extraction.

Assessment of the incidence of wilt disease and plant dry biomass

Wilt disease incidence was evaluated at 80 days post-transplanting, calculated as the percentage of infected plants relative to the total number of plant in each replicate [24]. For biomass measurement, 20 uniformly growing plants were randomly selected from each replicate. The sampled plants were first oven-treated at 105 °C for 30 min to inactivate tissue enzymes, and then dried at 70 °C to constant weight using a forced-air oven (DHG-9070 A, Shanzhi Instrument and Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The dry matter weight was subsequently recorded with an electronic balance.

Measurement of soil chemical properties

This study investigated soil pH, organic matter (SOM), alkali-hydrolyzed nitrogen (AN), available potassium (AK), and available phosphorus (AP) as key chemical properties. The measurements were performed according to the protocols established by Bao et al. [25]. Soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 (w/v) soil : water suspension. Soil organic matter (SOM) was quantified via the dichromate oxidation method with external heating. Soil available nitrogen (AN) was quantified via the alkaline diffusion technique. Soil available phosphorus (AP) was extracted with sodium bicarbonate and analyzed via the molybdenum-antimony anti-colorimetric technique. Soil available potassium (AK) content was determined via ammonium acetate extraction followed by flame photometric analysis.

Measurement of soil enzyme activities

The study determined the enzymatic activities of soil urease, dehydrogenases, alkaline phosphatase, and acid phosphatase following Guan’s protocol [26]. The sodium phenol-sodium hypochlorite colorimetric assay was used to determine soil urease activity, expressed as NH₃-N released per gram of soil per day (µg g⁻¹ soil 24 h⁻¹). The 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) reduction method was employed to determine soil dehydrogenases activity, reported as µg triphenylformazan (TPF) per gram of soil per 6 h (µg TPF g⁻¹ soil 6 h⁻¹). Using disodium p-nitrobenzene phosphate as the substrate, soil acid phosphatase activity was measured spectrophotometrically at 410 nm and expressed as phenol µg g⁻¹ soil 24 h⁻¹. The disodium phenyl phosphate colorimetric assay was employed to quantify soil alkaline phosphatase activity (phenol µg g⁻¹ soil 24 h⁻¹ unit). A SpectraMax M2 Multi-Detection Microplate Reader (Meigu Molecular Instruments, USA) was used for all spectrophotometric analyses.

Soil DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and Illumina sequencing

The OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02, Omega Bio-tek, USA) was used to extract total genomic DNA from 0.5 g CK, F15, and F30 soil samples. DNA quality and integrity were evaluated with a NanoDrop NC2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with integrity further confirmed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. FON populations were quantified with FON-1/FON-2 primers [27]. The V3-V4 region of bacterial 16 S rRNA genes was amplified with primers 338 F/806R [28].The fungal ITS rRNA gene was amplified with ITS5F/ITS2R primers [29]. The RT-PCR was performed in a 20 µL reaction volume comprising 10 µL of 2× SYBR real-time PCR mix, 0.5 µL of each primer, 2 µL DNA template, and 7 µL of ddH₂O. The amplification program consisted of an initial 5 min denaturation at 98 °C; followed by 25 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 53 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. FON populations were quantified according to the protocols outlined in references [30–31]. The 16 S rRNA and ITS amplicons were sequenced by PERSONALBIO Ltd. (Shanghai, China) on an Illumina MiSeq PE250 platform, with three biological replicates per treatment for both RT-PCR and sequencing analysis.

Sequence data analysis

Bioinformatic analysis of the microbiome sequencing data was conducted with QIIME2 platform (version 2019.4) [32]. Raw sequencing data were initially demultiplexed using the Demux plugin to assign reads to individual samples based on barcode sequences, and primer sequences were subsequently removed from both forward and reverse reads using the Cutadapt plugin [33]. Low-quality sequencing reads (length < 50 bp, quality score ≤ 20) were discarded during the initial quality control (QC) step. Data processing was performed using the DADA2 plugin [34], encompassing error-based denoising, paired-end merging (≥ 12-bp overlap), and chimera removal via the consensus method against reference databases, ultimately generating single-nucleotide-resolution amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic annotation was performed using the following reference databases: SILVA Release 132 for 16 S rRNA gene sequences and UNITE Release 8.0 for ITS regions.

Statistical analysis

QIIME2 was used to analyze sequence data, yielding alpha diversity metrics including Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou indices from the ASV table. Beta diversity was assessed using the Jaccard dissimilarity matrix [35], and the resulting dissimilarity matrices were visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) [36]. Shared and unique ASVs were visualized via a Venn diagram generated with the “VennDiagram” R package (v.3.20) [37]. Correlations between soil parameters and microbial community structure were analyzed using the Mantel test. Using the Biozeron Cloud Platform, redundancy analysis (RDA) was applied to assess the association between microbial community structures and biochemical parameters [38]. A structural equation model (SEM) was fitted using Amos 26.0 (AMOS IBM, United States) to evaluate the direct and indirect effects of bacterial and fungal communities, FON population, and chemical properties on wilt disease incidence. Model fit was assessed using a non-significant chi-square test (p > 0.05), χ²/df (< 7), a high goodness-of-fit index (GFI > 0.95), and a root mean square errors of approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), following the recommended cutoffs [39]. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 19.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, United States). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test was used for multiple comparisons at p < 0.05. Bar graphs were generated using OriginPro 2021 (OriginLab Corp., USA), with data expressed as means ± standard error (SE).

Watermelon wilt disease incidence and FON population are shown in Fig. 1. At eighty days after transplanting, the disease incidence was 52.78% in the continuously cropped soil (CK), but it was significantly (P < 0.05) lower at 25 and 5.56% in the flooded soil (F15 and F30), respectively (Fig. 1A).

Similarly, the FON population showed a significant difference among the CK, F15, and F30 treatments. For the three treatment soils, the highest FON population was detected in the rhizosphere soil of watermelon in the CK treatment (up to 4.52 × 104 copies g− 1 soil). The population was significantly (P < 0.05) lower in the F15 and F30 treatments, at 3.07 × 104 and 2.77 × 104 copies g− 1 soil, respectively (Fig. 1B).

In this study, the growth of watermelon plants was compared across three treatments CK, F15, and F30 (Fig. 1C). The results revealed the dry biomass of watermelon plants was significantly (P < 0.05) higher in the F15 and F30 treatments compared to CK control. However, no significant differences in dry biomass were observed between the F15 and F30 treatments.

Soil chemical properties

In this study, five soil chemical indices in the rhizosphere soil of watermelon were investigated (Fig. 2). The soil SOM and AN content in the F15 and F30 treatments were significantly (P < 0.05) lower compared with the CK treatment (Fig. 2B and C). On the contrary, the contents of AP and AK in the F15 and F30 treatments were significantly (P < 0.05) higher compared with the CK treatment (Fig. 2D and E). However, no significant difference in soil pH was observed among the CK, F15, and F30 treatments (Fig. 2A).

Using the Illumina MiSeq platform, we identified amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) across 38 phyla, 107 classes, 245 orders, 376 families, and 645 genera. A total of 3407, 3843, and 3981 ASVs were detected in the CK, F15, and F30 treatments, respectively (Fig. 4B).

Eight phyla had relative abundances (RA) > 1% in the bacterial communities (Fig. 4C). These phyla represented more than 96.88% of all bacterial sequences. Among them, Proteobacteria was the dominant phylum, accounting for 32.75, 31.78, and 26.07% of the bacterial community in the CK, F15, and F30 treatments, followed by Firmicutes (29.80, 14.64, and 23.83%) and Actinobacteriota (16.37, 28.58, and 19.49%), respectively. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to identify phyla that were significantly enriched or depleted in the flooding treatments compared to the control (Fig. S1A). The relative abundance of Firmicutes and Patescibacteria declined significantly in the F15 and F30 treatments compared with the CK treatment. Conversely, the F15 and F30 treatments exhibited a significant increase in the relative abundance of Actinobacteriota, Chloroflexi, Bacteroidota, and Myxococcota compared to CK treatment. However, the relative abundance of Acidobacteriota and Verrucomicrobiota did not differ significantly among treatments. Additionally, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria did not differ significantly between the CK and F15 treatments, but both were significantly higher than in the F30 treatment. The relative abundance of Gemmatimonadota was significantly lower in the CK and F15 treatments than in the F30 (Fig. S1A).

At the genus level, the dominant bacterial genera (RA > 1%) were Tuberibacillus, Chujaibacter, Geobacillus, Bacillus, Gitt-GS-136, Longimicrobiaceae, Nocardioides, Mycobacterium, Rhodanobacter, and Thermoactinomyces (Fig. 4D). Notably, the relative abundance of Tuberibacillus, Chujaibacter, and Thermoactinomyces significantly declined, while Geobacillus, Bacillus, Gitt-GS-136, Longimicrobiaceae, Nocardioides, and Mycobacterium significantly increased in the F15 and F30 treatments compared to the CK. The relative abundance of Rhodanobacter did not differ significantly among treatments (Fig. S1B).

High-throughput sequencing classified the fungal amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) into 9 phyla, 27 classes, 63 orders, 125 families, and 195 genera. Among these ASVs, 532, 442, and 441 were detected in the CK, F15, and F30 treatments, respectively (Fig. 5B).

At the phylum level, Ascomycota was the dominant fungal phyla in the watermelon rhizosphere soil, accounting for more than 90.47%, which decreased significantly in the F15 and F30 compared with the CK (Fig. 5C). In all three treatments, the relative abundances of Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, Rozellomycota, Aphelidiomycota, Mortierellomycota, Mucoromycota, and Olpidiomycota were below 1%, with Olpidiomycota absent in the F15 and F30 (Fig. S2A). At the genus level, the dominant genera in the rhizospheric soil under the CK, F15, and F30 treatments were Penicillium, Ovatospora, Myceliophthora, Trichocladium, Fusarium, Trichoderma, Botryotrichum, Emmonsiellopsis, Aspergillus, and Phialemonium (Fig. 5D). Moreover, the relative abundance of Ovatospora, Myceliophthora, and Botryotrichum significantly declined, while Penicillium significantly increased in the F15 and F30 treatments compared with the CK treatment. The abundance of Trichocladium, Trichoderma, and Emmonsiellopsis exhibited no significant differences among treatments. Interestingly, the abundance of Fusarium in the F30 treatment was 1.55- and 2.07-fold lower compared with the CK and F15, respectively (Fig. S2B).

Soil microbial co-occurrence network

Analysis of co-occurrence networks in rhizosphere soil under CK, F15, and F30 treatments revealed that soil flooding modified the interaction patterns among microbial phyla. The bacterial species correlation network in the F15 treatment soil was more complex than those in the CK and F30 treatments, while all three networks showed positive correlations (Fig. 6A and C). With 196 nodes and 452 edges, the F15 treatment soil had more nodes and edges than CK and F30 treatment soils, indicating more interconnected relationships among bacterial species (Table S1). However, the fungal species correlation network in the CK treatment exhibited greater complexity than those in the F15 and F30 treatments, while all three treatments exhibited a positive correlation between nodes and edges (Fig. 6D and F). Specifically, the numbers of nodes and edges in the microbial community of the CK (108.0 and 181.00) rhizosphere soil were higher than those in the F15 (88.00 and 132.00) and F30 (79.00 and 115.00) treatments, indicating more interconnected relationships among fungal species in the CK treatment (Table S2).

Effect of soil biochemical indexes on microbial communities

The Mantel test revealed a significant overall correlation between soil biochemical indexes and the microbial community at the genus level. This study revealed that soil pH, AP, AK, AN, SOM, and ACPA had significant effects on the bacterial community in the CK treatments, whereas in the F15 and F30 treatments, only AK and AN significantly influenced the bacterial communities (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, the fungal community in the F15 and F30 treatments was significantly affected by soil AP, AK, AN, SOM, and ACPA, whereas in the CK treatment, it was significantly affected by AP, AK, SOM, UA, and ACPA(Fig. 7B).

Redundancy analysis (RDA) was further performed to identify the soil biochemical properties that influenced the microbial community (Fig. 8). Among these soil biochemical indexes, AP (r2 = 0.733, p = 0.024), AK (r2 = 0.977, p = 0.002), AN (r2 = 0.869, p = 0.005), SOM (r2 = 0.868, p = 0.022), UA (r2 = 0.806, p = 0.038), and ACPA (r2 = 0.837, p = 0.011) significantly influenced bacterial community structure (Fig. 8A and C), while AP (r2 = 0.696, p = 0.043), AK (r2 = 0.790, p = 0.017), AN (r2 = 0.886, p = 0.003), SOM (r2 = 0.942, p = 0.003), and UA (r2 = 0.926, p = 0.001), and ACPA (r2 = 0.845, p = 0.006) significantly affected fungal community composition (Fig. 8B and D), indicating that AP, AK, AN, SOM, UA, and ACPA played a key role in shaping both bacterial and fungal communities.

Discussion

Soil flooding controlled watermelon Fusarium wilt

Watermelon wilt disease, caused by soil-borne pathogens, is a devastating condition in global watermelon production, resulting in substantial annual losses in yield and economic revenue. Several studies have shown that flooding measures can effectively control soil-borne pathogens, such as Fusarium species [40]. Reducing pathogen population density is one of the key measures for controlling soil-borne diseases [41]. This study demonstrated that the incidence of watermelon Fusarium wilt was significantly reduced in the F15 and F30 treatments compared to the CK treatment (Fig. 1A), and this reduction was consistent with a concomitant decrease in FON population density (Fig. 1B). The results indicate that Fusarium wilt incidence may be suppressed in waterlogged soil, which is consistent with previous findings [42]. These data support soil flooding could serve as a cost-effective strategy for mitigating continuous cropping-induced Fusarium wilt in watermelon production.

Soil flooding promoted pepper germination and seedling growth, presumably by reducing soil nutrient accumulation [43]. In line with this, our results revealed that the soil flooding-induced promotion of watermelon growth (Fig. 1C) is likely attributable to the reduction in SOM and AN levels (Fig. 2B and C).

Soil biochemical properties change due to soil flooding

During greenhouse crop cultivation, excessive fertilizer application reduces nutrient utilization efficiency. As a result, nutrients rapidly accumulate in the soil over a short period, causing severe soil health issues such as secondary salinization and nutrient imbalance [44]. The reductions in SOM and AN contents observed in the F15 and F30 treatments (Fig. 2B and C) likely result from soil flooding, which alleviates the accumulation of organic matter and alkali-hydrolyzed nitrogen. A high rate of N fertilizer application resulted in increased severity of Fusarium wilt in banana [45], and our study further revealed a negative correlation between soil AN levels and disease incidence (Fig. 9). Interestingly, the F15 and F30 treatments exhibited higher contents of AP and AK (Fig. 2D and E). Soil phosphorus dynamics were altered to promote phosphate dissolution and increase available phosphorus content by flooding [46], while ferrous ions released during reduction reactions facilitated the liberation of exchangeable potassium, converting it into a plant-available form [47]. Adequate phosphorus supply is critical for sustaining plant health [48]. The enhanced AP levels observed in our study likely contribute to maintaining plant vigor. Overall, our findings suggested that soil flooding optimizes soil nutrient availability by reducing excess nitrogen while enhancing phosphorus and potassium availability, a process that may benefit plant health.

Soil enzyme activity serves as a vital indicator of soil health, reflecting alterations in metabolic processes and nutrient cycling dynamics induced by agricultural management practices [49]. Soil urease and phosphatase play pivotal roles in the cycling of nitrogen and phosphorus, critically influencing nutrient availability and uptake in terrestrial ecosystems [50–51]. In this study, the elevated urease activity following soil flooding (Fig. 3A) is likely attributable to enhanced nitrogen demand by watermelon plants, a pattern consistent with previous findings [52]. Similarly, the increased ALPA activity (Fig. 3D) corresponded with elevated soil available phosphorus (AP) levels, supporting the role of phosphatase in organic phosphorus mineralization. Acid phosphatase activity, primarily derived from plant roots and soil microorganisms [53], decreased significantly after soil flooding (Fig. 3C), potentially reflecting shifts in microbial community composition or root exudation patterns. Additionally, studies have shown that increased soil enzyme activity contributes to the suppression of soil-borne diseases [16]. In summary, high rhizosphere levels of AP, AK, UA, and ALPA were correlated with enhanced watermelon plant growth and reduced wilt disease incidence. Furthermore, our study provides a foundation for establishing a more systematic soil enzyme indicator system and for elucidating the intrinsic relationships among soil enzyme activities, microbial communities, and disease incidence.

Microbial community composition changes due to soil flooding

Soil microbial diversity is widely recognized as a key indicator of soil quality [54]. ‌A fungal-dominated microbial community may be more susceptible to disease invasion [41]. Strauss et al. showed that the higher bacterial diversity observed after the flooding treatment may be a factor in the suppression of pathogenic microorganisms [55]. Similarly, previous studies have demonstrated that soils with low fungal diversity and high bacterial diversity can effectively suppress the Fusarium wilt spread [56–58]. These results are consistent with the findings of our study. In this study, the F15 and F30 treatments significantly reduced fungal community diversity and richness while increasing bacterial counterparts relative to CK (Tables 1 and 2). The observed shift from a fungal- to a bacterial-dominated community in the watermelon rhizosphere soil may be associated with an increased disease-suppressive capacity [59].

Soil microbial community structure is a key determinant of plant health and can also serve as an indicator of soil condition [60]. Our study showed that the dominant bacterial phyla in the watermelon rhizosphere were Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteriota (Fig. 4C), which is consistent with previous reports [61]. Notably, the relative abundances of Actinobacteriota, Chloroflexi, and Bacteroidota were significantly increased under the F15 and F30 treatments (Fig. S1A). Various bacterial members can antagonize soilborne pathogens through antibiosis, competition, and induced systemic resistance in plants [62]. Members of the phylum Actinobacteriota are mostly saprophytic in soil, where their beneficial populations play important roles in the decomposition of organic matter and humus and in the production of diverse antibiotics [63]. Studies have demonstrated that Actinobacteriota serve as the primary microbial predictors of fungal pathogen inhibition [64]. Chloroflexi are capable of degrading cellulose and maintaining stable soil carbon levels [65]. Bacteroidota are one of the important indicators of soil health and possess potential for biological control [66]. Additionally, the relative abundances of Firmicutes and Patescibacteria in the F15 and F30 treatments were significantly lower than those in the CK treatment (Fig. S1A). Many members of the phylum Firmicutes are aerobic or facultative anaerobic bacteria, which may be one reason for their reduction. Proteobacteria, which typically flourish in nutrient-rich conditions, declined markedly after the flooding treatment, suggesting alleviation of secondary salinization. Gemmatimonadetes are considered a keystone microbial group, playing a vital role in organic carbon soil cycling owing to their metabolic strategies [67]. The relative abundance of Gemmatimonadota increased in the F30 treatment, suggesting that this phylum exhibits high adaptability to waterlogged conditions. Acidobacteriota play an important role in the degradation of plant residues [68]. However, no significant variation was observed in the relative abundance of Acidobacteriota among the CK, F15, and F30 treatments, indicating a high degree of stability for this phyla in soil (Fig. S1A). Increases in the abundance and diversity of soil bacterial communities are associated with enhanced ecosystem stability [69]. These observed changes indicate that the flooding-induced enrichment of Actinobacteriota, Chloroflexi, and Bacteroidota may contribute to improved soil quality and disease suppression. At the genus level, the genus Geobacillus includes aerobic and facultatively anaerobic bacilli, many of which possess the remarkable ability to degrade harmful substances [70]. Bacillus have been widely reported to contribute to disease suppression through antagonism against soil-borne pathogens [71]. Nocardioides are closely related to the degradation of insecticides and antimicrobials [72]. Our results revealed a significant increase in the relative abundance of Geobacillus, Bacillus, and Nocardioides in both the F15 and F30 treatments (Fig. S1B). This finding is consistent with the report of Zenova et al. [73], who attributed bacterial predominance in flooded soils to their superior flood tolerance over other microorganisms. Beneficial microorganisms in the rhizosphere can mediate plant growth under submerged conditions by triggering specific stress responses [74]. Our results suggest that soil flooding altered the bacterial community, particularly by enriching potentially beneficial taxa such as Bacillus—known suppressors of soil-borne pathogens [75]. These changes may contribute to enhanced soil health and reduced incidence of Fusarium wilt of watermelon after soil flooding [76].

Fungal community structure, in composition and diversity, is fundamental to ecosystem balance [77]. Ascomycota was identified as the dominant fungal phylum in the watermelon rhizosphere (Fig. 5C); however, its relative abundance was significantly reduced by the F15 and F30 treatments (Fig. S2A). Ascomycota, a large and diverse fungal phylum, includes many plant pathogens, such as Fusarium [78]. A previous study has demonstrated that the reduction in Ascomycota abundance was correlated with lower Fusarium wilt incidence [79]. Thus, the flooding-induced reduction in Ascomycota abundance may partly account for the decreased disease incidence. At the genus level, the relative abundance of Penicillium increased in the F15 and F30 treatments, suggesting a potential enhancement of beneficial microbial populations (Fig. S2B). Penicillium is known for producing Penicillins, and certain strains can serve as biocontrol agents to suppress plant diseases [80]. Additionally, the relative abundances of Ovatospora, Myceliophthora, and Botryotrichum decreased, which may have contributed to the decrease in fungal community diversity and richness (Fig. S2B). As a soil-borne fungal pathogen, Fusarium species can induce a wide range of diseases in plants [81–82]. The reduction in pathogenic microorganism abundance plays a crucial role in suppressing soil-borne diseases [83]. Interestingly, the relative abundance of Fusarium increased in the F15 treatment but decreased in the F30 treatment, suggesting that a 30-day flooding period is critical for effective pathogen suppression. We propose that soil flooding effectively improves soil quality by reducing plant pathogenic microorganisms (e.g., Fusarium) and increasing beneficial microorganisms (e.g., Penicillium), which may be a key factor in suppressing watermelon wilt.

Linkage of soil biochemical properties and microbial communities in response to Fusarium wilt

Microorganisms are highly responsive to environmental changes and play a critical role in plant-soil interactions [84]. The findings of this study indicate that bacterial community structure was potentially influenced by AK and AN, while fungal community structure was also affected by AP, AK, AN, SOM, and ACPA (Figs. 7 and 8). Previous research has demonstrated that AK, AN, and SOM strongly influence microbial community structure [85]. This study confirmed that soil chemical properties and soil enzymes not only significantly affected both bacterial and fungal communities but also helped explain their consequent impacts on soil health.

The bacterial correlation networks in the F15 and F30 treatments were more complex than those in the CK soil, while the opposite was true for the fungal network (Fig. 6 and Tables S1 and 2). This suggests that the closely interconnected fungal species in the CK treatment have developed strong pathogenicity induced by pathogenic fungi [86]. Conversely, the more tightly connected bacterial species in the flooded soils exhibited enhanced tolerance to external stress [87], which may contribute to the suppression of pathogenic fungal invasion and the improvement of overall soil quality, thereby enhancing the resistance of watermelon plants to Fusarium wilt.

To assess the effects of the richness index and chemical indices on watermelon Fusarium wilt incidence, a structural equation model (SEM) was constructed. After model refinement, the final model pathways are shown in Fig. 9A, and the corresponding standardized total effects are summarized in Fig. 9B. The results showed that the direct paths from bacterial and fungal community richness to wilt incidence were not significant. Rather, wilt incidence was indirectly influenced by SOM, FON, DW, AK, and AN (Fig. 9A), a pattern that is generally consistent with previous reports [88]. Notably, negative total effects on watermelon Fusarium wilt incidence were observed for bacterial community richness (total effect = -0.886) and AK (total effect = -0.562) (Fig. 9B), suggesting that higher bacterial richness and AK content are associated with reduced disease incidence. Conversely, positive total effects were observed for AN, fungal community richness, and FON [56–58]. Thus, the reduction in watermelon Fusarium wilt incidence under soil flooding in our study can be attributed to indirect pathways involving changes in soil AK, AN, and microbial community composition.

Conclusion

In this study, we have shown that soil flooding reduced the incidence of watermelon Fusarium wilt, altered soil biochemical properties, and changed the diversity and richness of bacterial and fungal communities. Furthermore, we found that soil flooding reshaped rhizospheric microbial community in watermelon, enriching beneficial bacteria and decreasing harmful fungi, which may be the main reason for suppressing soil-borne wilt diseases. Additionally, the Mantel test and RDA revealed that AK and AN were the key factors associated with the structures of both bacterial and fungal communities. Soil AN, fungal community richness, and FON positively affected the incidence of watermelon Fusarium wilt under soil flooding, whereas bacterial community richness and soil AK had a negative effect. Therefore, in actual production, attention should be paid to maintaining the stability of the rhizospheric soil microecology of watermelon to suppress Fusarium wilt.
