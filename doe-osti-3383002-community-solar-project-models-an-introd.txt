Introduction
• This resource is a collection of visual aids and descriptions to help explain the basic financial and administrative
 relationships underpinning common community solar (CS) project models.
• This resource consolidates information on CS models provided in other sources with differing detail, audiences,
 and topical focuses (e.g., financial relationships, electricity flow, customer type).
• The graphics in this resource depict the flow of electricity from generation to consumption and the financial
 exchanges between the CS project owner, subscribers, and distribution utility. Community solar models vary
 based on ownership (e.g., third party, utility), customer type (e.g., multifamily, single household), and solar array
 location (e.g., on-site, off-site).
• This resource provides an introductory explanation of how CS can function. For example, this resource was used
 to establish a baseline understanding of CS during a workshop engaging diverse energy stakeholders in the early
 planning stage of state CS program rules.
• References and complementary resources are listed on slide 22, and the Appendix on slides 23 through 25
 contains links to project model case studies.

Community Solar Terms
This deck uses terms that may not be familiar to users new to the solar power industry but are commonly used in community solar project proposals and other
documents.

• Behind the meter vs. in front of the meter: Behind-the-meter systems are located at the customer site and provide energy directly to the property, whereas in-
 front-of-the-meter systems serve the grid as a whole rather than specific end users.

• Energy off-takes: The entities that purchase the electricity generated by the solar development project.

• Flow of electricity: The pathway the electrons take to reach the end user.

• Host/hosting: The owner of the physical location of the solar installation; can be an owner of the installation or just the owner of the site.

• Net metering: A billing mechanism that allows owners of solar panels or other renewable systems to export excess electricity to the utility grid in exchange for
 credits on their utility bills.

• Ownership: Entity that owns both the physical assets and the energy produced by the solar project.

• Project developer: Person or entity that finances, builds, and often operates a solar project.

• Power purchase agreement (PPA): A long-term contract between the solar project owner and buyer of the electricity.

• Solar installation: Physical assets that compose the solar project, including panels, inverters, and racking.

• Subscribers: A subscriber is a household or entity that generally contributes to the project costs and in return receives a share of the electrical or financial
 benefits generated. Each model has a different subscriber role, some of which have a variable definition, such as receiving benefits without direct financial
 contribution or participating without actually receiving project electricity.

Utility-Owned Community Solar
Ownership and hosting: In a utility-owned community solar project, the utility finances, hosts, and operates
 the community solar installation. As such, the utility pays the initial setup cost and the ensuing
 operation and maintenance (O&M) costs.*
Subscribers: The subscribers are exclusively utility rate-paying customers who opt in (or don’t opt out) of the
 utility’s community solar program. The utility is responsible for subscriber marketing and acquisition.*
Subscription payment: The utility collects payment from the CS subscribed utility ratepayer, who then
 receives a bill credit or discount on their bill at a rate set by the community solar or net-metering rules.
Flow of electricity: The community solar installation is hosted by the utility, and the energy generated
 is integrated into the utility’s energy mix and transported to the customer via the utility’s established
 distribution grid.
Case Studies: Florida: Florida Power & Light SolarTogether; Texas: Austin Energy Community Solar

* The utility may choose to procure or outsource project components, such as project development, O&M, and customer management.

Third-Party Owned | Consolidated Billing
Ownership and hosting: A third party finances and operates the community solar project and the ensuing
 O&M costs.
Subscribers: Subscribers are customers of the utility that has approved interconnection of the project to its
 distribution grid, in the service territory where the project is located. The third party is generally
 responsible for subscriber marketing and acquisition for the CS project.
Subscription payment: The third party is also responsible for coordination with the utility, who implements
 subscription cost collection for the third party and subscriber credit allocation. The subscriber receives
 a single bill from the utility.
Flow of electricity: The community solar installation is connected to the utility’s distribution grid, and the
 energy generated is integrated into the utility’s energy mix and transported to the subscriber via the
 utility’s established distribution grid.

Case Studies: New York: NY-Sun & Solar For All; New Jersey: Community Solar Energy Program

Third-Party Owned | Duel Billing
Ownership and hosting: A third party finances and operates the community solar project and the ensuing
 O&M costs.
Subscribers: Subscribers are customers of the utility that has approved interconnection of the project to its
 distribution grid, in the service territory where the project is located. The third party is generally
 responsible for subscriber marketing and acquisition for the CS project.
Subscription payment: The third party is responsible for charging the subscriber for subscription costs via a
 nonutility bill and then must coordinate with the utility to allocate the appropriate credits on the
 subscriber’s utility bill.
Flow of electricity: The community solar array is connected to the utility’s distribution grid, and the energy
 generated is integrated into the utility’s energy mix and transported to the subscriber via the
 distribution grid.

Multifamily Housing Owned | Master Metered
Ownership and hosting: The multifamily facility owner finances and operates the community solar
 installation and hosts the installation on-site. As such, the facility owner pays the initial setup cost and
 the ensuing O&M costs.
Subscribers: The subscribers are residents of the housing facility; however, not all residents have to take
 part in the project.
Subscription payment: Residents, acting as subscribers, may or may not be responsible for paying a fee to
 receive the benefits of the solar system. The facility owner will collect subscription fees where
 applicable and distribute the financial savings from the solar system to the participating residents
 directly or indirectly via financial or nonfinancial benefits.
Flow of electricity: The electricity generated is first used on-site as in the case of any other behind-the-
 meter solar system, and in cases of excess generation, electricity is exported to the utility.
Case Study: District of Columbia: Enterprise Community Development

Multifamily Housing Third Party | Master Metered
Ownership and hosting: A third party finances and operates the community solar installation, setting up a
 lease or other financial mechanism with the multifamily facility owner to locate the project on-site. The
 third-party developer pays the initial setup cost and the ensuing O&M costs, which are recovered
 through the lease or other partnership agreement with the building owner.
Subscribers: The subscribers are residents of the housing facility; however, not all residents have to take
 part in the project.
Subscription payment: Residents, acting as subscribers, may or may not be responsible for paying a fee to
 receive the benefits of the solar system. The facility owner will collect these subscription fees where
 applicable and distribute the financial savings from the solar system to the participating residents
 directly or indirectly via financial or nonfinancial benefits.
Flow of electricity: The electricity generated is first used on-site as in the case of any other behind-the-
 meter solar system, and in cases of excess generation, electricity is exported to the utility.
Case Studies: Maryland: Aigburth Vale, California: Valle de Las Brisas

Multifamily Housing Owned | Net Metering
Ownership and hosting: The property owner finances and operates the community solar installation and
 hosts the installation on-site behind a central meter. As such, the facility owner pays the initial setup
 cost and the ensuing O&M costs.
Subscribers: The subscribers are tenants/residents of the property; however, not all residents have to take
 part in the project.
Subscription payment: Subscribers pay their electricity bill separately from the main meter along with a
 subscription fee to the property owner. Energy generated by the system is distributed to multiple
 meters via the system design passing savings directly to the tenants without utilizing virtual net
 metering.
Flow of electricity: The electricity generated is first used on-site as in the case of any other behind-the-
 meter solar system, and in cases of excess generation, electricity is exported to the utility.
Case Study: Mississippi: Belhaven Residential Apartment Homes

Community Benefit Funds
Ownership and hosting: A community member (usually an organization) finances and operates the
 community solar project and the ensuing O&M costs. The host could also be a partner in a project
 owned by a third party rather than the owner (e.g., leasing space to a solar developer).
Subscribers: There are no subscribers in the traditional sense of paying directly for a product or service;
 however, community members of any type, regardless of their utility affiliation or location, may
 participate in the project’s costs and benefits. In most cases, subscribers have a connection with the
 owner or host as a member or affiliate, such as a house of worship.
Subscription payment: Subscribers have the option to pay into the project separate from their utility bill but
 are not always required to do so to receive benefits. The host then converts a portion of the project’s
 financial benefits into a transferable, nonelectrically associated, monetary form that is distributed to
 subscribers.
Flow of electricity: The host continues normal electrical exchanges with the utility using on-site generated
 electricity and exporting excess to the grid. No electricity “flows” to nonhost participants.

Case Studies: Mississippi: Mendenhall Bible Church; Michigan: North End Woodward Community Coalition

Cooperative Ownership
Ownership and hosting: The cooperative (co-op), comprising its members, using membership funds and
 other sources will finance, own, and operate projects sited either in front of or behind the meter.
 • If in front of the meter, the energy off-taker (host or otherwise) will pay for energy from the
 project.
 • If behind the meter, the host’s solar energy rate is often lower than their utility rate, reducing their
 energy bills from the utility. If behind the meter, the host may be, but does not have to be, a co-op
 member.
Subscribers: Subscribers are the co-op members.
Subscription payment: Co-op members are inherently CS subscribers and in return receive benefits from
 project revenues via co-op dividend payments.
Flow of electricity: If in front of the meter, energy flows to the grid and is delivered to the off-taker virtually,
 and if behind the meter, the energy serves the host’s load first and then exports to the grid like any
 other net metered project.
Case Study: Behind the Meter – California: People Power Solar Cooperative
