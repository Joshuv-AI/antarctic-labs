Abstract
Water is an essential natural resource for the survival of living beings and the development of societies; however,
increasing natural and anthropogenic pressures have contributed to the progressive deterioration of its quality. In this
context, the objective of this study was to evaluate the water quality of the Chone River by applying the National
Sanitation Foundation Water Quality Index (ICA-NSF), with the aim of determining its level of contamination and
establishing its potential implications for the various uses of this water resource. To this end, five sampling points
(P1–P5) located in the watershed associated with Chone, Bolívar, Tosagua, and Junín were analyzed, considering two
sampling depths (30 and 60 cm). The calculation of the ICA-NSF incorporated nine variables: dissolved oxygen, fecal
coliforms, pH, biochemical oxygen demand (BOD₅), temperature change, phosphates, nitrates, turbidity, and total
solids, applying the index’s classic weighting structure. The values obtained ranged from 31.4 to 66.2, with a
predominance of the medium and poor quality categories. Point P1 exhibited the best relative conditions at both
depths, while P3 recorded the highest degree of deterioration, primarily associated with high concentrations of
nutrients, BOD₅, turbidity, and microbiological contamination. The comparison between depths revealed minor
variations between 30 and 60 cm, suggesting a relatively homogeneous distribution of deterioration throughout the
water column. In conclusion, the results indicate a water system under pressure from organic pollution and nutrient
enrichment, with potential effects on ecological integrity and restrictions on the most sensitive uses of the resource,
highlighting the need to strengthen strategies for monitoring, control, and management of water quality in the Chone
River basin.
Keywords: ICA-NSF; Water quality; Nutrients; Turbidity, Microbiological contamination.

1. Introduction
Water is a natural resource essential for the survival of living beings and the development of societies (Campos,
2019). This resource is not only vital for human consumption but also for economic, agricultural, and industrial activities (Pan
American Health Organization [PAHO], 2024). However, globally, factors such as population growth, uncontrolled
urbanization, industrial and agricultural pollution, and the effects of climate change have placed increasing pressure on water
resources, significantly degrading their quality. This poses a serious threat to public health, given that approximately 1.7 billion
people consume water contaminated with fecal matter (World Health Organization [WHO], 2023).
According to the World Water Assessment Programme, more than 80% of the wastewater generated worldwide is
discharged into water bodies without prior treatment, thus posing a direct threat to aquatic ecosystems and public health
(World Water Assessment Programme [WWAP], 2017). This situation has led to an alarming decline in freshwater quality,
which is now considered one of the major global environmental challenges (United Nations Educational, Scientific and
Cultural Organization [UNESCO], 2023). In Latin America, millions of people are exposed to health risks from consuming
water contaminated with physical, chemical, and biological agents (PAHO, 2024).
In Ecuador, water resources face increasing pressure from domestic, agricultural, and industrial discharges that
compromise their quality (Cárdenas Novillo, 2020). Durán et al. (2020) also highlight the impact of these activities on multiple
watersheds across the country. According to the 2024 report on Environmental-Economic Information in Municipal GADs,
56.7% of untreated wastewater disposal sites discharged into rivers and 35.8% into streams (Instituto Nacional de Estadística y
Censos [INEC], 2024). In the Chone River basin, located in Manabí, Solórzano & Quiroz (2021) documented that agriculture,
livestock farming, and urban growth affect water quality.
Recent research has identified high concentrations of nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus in the waters of the
Chone River. According to the study by Véliz & Seni (2022), total phosphorus levels range from 1.71 to 3.81 mg/L, while total
nitrogen ranges from 5.88 to 14.00 mg/L, thus exceeding the limits established for unrestricted use, which indicates

Given the need for tools that allow for a comprehensive assessment of the condition of water bodies, the Water

This study aims to assess the water quality of the Chone River by applying the Water Quality Index (WQI), to
determine its level of pollution and its impact on the various uses of this water resource.

2. Methodology
An experimental field study was conducted (Risemberg et al., 2026a) using a quantitative approach (Risemberg et al.,
2026b; Pereira et al., 2018) and simple descriptive statistics, including column charts, line charts, data classes, absolute
frequency values (counts), and mean values (Shitsuka et al., 2014a; Shitsuka et al., 2014b). The study was conducted on the
Chone River in the province of Manabí (Figure 1). Its watershed primarily encompasses the cantons of Chone, Bolívar,
Tosagua, and Junín. This river originates in the western foothills of the Andes Mountains and flows southwest until it empties
into the city of Bahía de Caráquez. This tributary plays a fundamental role in the agricultural, economic, and ecological
development of the area, as it is one of the province’s main waterways.

Assessment of the Current State and Site Survey
The assessment of the current state of the Chone River began with an on-site survey aimed at identifying the main
anthropogenic activities with a potential impact on water resource degradation. The study includes direct measurements of
critical parameters such as turbidity, suspended solids (SS), total solids (TS), pH, phosphates, biochemical oxygen demand
(BOD₅), temperature, fecal coliforms, nitrates, and dissolved oxygen (DO). These measurements are linked to the river’s flow
regime, a determining factor in the dynamics and health of aquatic ecosystems (Lara and Vélez, 2022). The monitoring stations
were georeferenced using a portable GPS receiver, allowing for the precise mapping of strategic sites in spatial analysis tools
such as Google Earth for subsequent territorial analysis (Orrala De La Cruz, 2024).
Water Quality Assessment
The ICA-NSF synthesizes multiple physicochemical and microbiological variables into a single value ranging from 0
to 100. Its original structure was proposed by Brown et al. (1970) and remains one of the most widely used tools for the
integrated assessment of surface waters. The index was calculated using a weighted aggregation of quality sub-indices (Qi)
obtained from the method’s standard rating curves, according to the general expression ICA-NSF = Σ(Qi·Wi) (Coronel and
Jaramillo, 2022; Quiroz et al., 2017).
Nine parameters were used in this study: dissolved oxygen (% saturation), fecal coliforms, pH, BOD₅, temperature
change (ΔT), phosphates, nitrates, turbidity, and total dissolved solids (TDS). The standard weights of the method were
adopted: DO = 0.17; fecal coliforms = 0.16; pH = 0.11; BOD₅ = 0.11; temperature = 0.10; phosphates = 0.10; nitrates = 0.10;
turbidity = 0.08; total solids = 0.07 (Uddin et al., 2021; Gupta and Gupta, 2021; Kumar et al., 2024).
It should be noted that the database reports phosphates and TDS, whereas the original formulation of the index was
based on total phosphorus and total solids. This substitution is common in applied practice, although it introduces a source of
methodological uncertainty that must be explicitly stated when interpreting the results. The classification adopted was: 91–100
excellent, 71–90 good, 51–70 average, 26–50 poor, and 0–25 very poor.

Site P1 had the highest ICA-NSF values at both depths, with 66.2 at 30 cm and 58.7 at 60 cm, placing it in the
“moderate” category. This pattern indicates that, although the site maintains relatively better conditions compared to the other
sites, certain parameters limit the achievement of higher water quality. Factors contributing to this result included low
concentrations of nitrates, phosphates, and fecal coliforms, as well as a moderate BOD₅ at 30 cm (3.30 mg/L). However, the
decrease in the index at 60 cm is related to the increase in BOD₅ and temperature variation, which indicates a lower prevalence
of optimal conditions at depth.
On the other hand, points P2, P3, and P5 fell into the “poor” category at both depths, indicating a significant
deterioration in water quality. Point P3 recorded the lowest average value in the study (32.1), primarily associated with high
concentrations of nitrates and phosphates, as well as high levels of fecal coliforms at 30 cm. These results indicate a significant
influx of organic matter and nutrients, possibly related to wastewater discharges or diffuse pollution processes. Likewise, high
turbidity and total dissolved solids negatively contributed to the index, reducing environmental quality conditions at this point.
In contrast, point P4 showed intermediate quality values, with indices of 38.9 and 36.7. This result was related to high
nutrient concentrations—especially phosphates and nitrates—as well as considerable BOD5 and turbidity values. Although
fecal coliform concentrations were moderate, the combination of the other parameters significantly reduced the ICA-NSF
value.

According to Table 4 of Ministerial Agreement 097-A, regarding acceptable quality criteria for the preservation of
aquatic and wildlife in freshwater and marine environments, the results obtained at the various monitoring sites along the
Chone River showed variable trends among the analyzed parameters.
pH values ranging from 7.92 to 8.21 remained within the permissible limits established by regulations for freshwater
and saltwater, indicating favorable chemical conditions for the maintenance of aquatic ecosystems and the development of
hydrobiological organisms. Likewise, dissolved oxygen showed saturation percentages ranging from 108.4% to 142.9%, far
exceeding the minimum values required by regulations—which set >80% for freshwater and >60% for saltwater—reflecting
adequate oxygenation capacity in the water body and favorable conditions for the respiration and survival of aquatic biota.
Regarding nitrate concentration, the values obtained ranged from 1.80 to 14.0 mg/L. According to regulations, the
permissible limit for freshwater is 13 mg/L, indicating a slight noncompliance at point P3 at a depth of 60 cm, where a
concentration of 14.0 mg/L was recorded. However, all values obtained remained below the limit established for saltwater.
This isolated increase is associated with the input of nutrients from agricultural runoff, domestic discharges, and/or the
decomposition of organic matter.
On the other hand, BOD5 concentrations ranged from 3.30 to 65.0 mg/L. According to Table 3 of Ministerial
Agreement 097-A, the permissible limit for freshwater is 20 mg/L; however, points P2, P3, and P5 exceeded this value, with
P2 at depths of 30 cm and 60 cm standing out in particular, with concentrations of 65.0 mg/L and 57.0 mg/L, respectively.
These results indicate a high load of biodegradable organic matter in certain sections of the river, a situation that may cause
disturbances in the ecological balance, an increase in decomposition processes, and potential impacts on aquatic fauna.
Regarding the parameters of phosphates, total dissolved solids (TDS), turbidity, and fecal coliforms, Table 3 of
Ministerial Agreement 097-A does not establish specific permissible limits for the preservation of aquatic life; however, the
high concentrations of phosphates and turbidity observed at several points suggest the presence of sediment, nutrient, and
organic matter inputs into the water body—factors that may contribute to eutrophication and the progressive deterioration of
the river’s environmental quality.

4. Discussion
The results show a pattern of moderate to severe water quality deterioration. It is worth noting that no sampling site
achieved the “good” or “excellent” categories; on the contrary, three of the five sites remained in “poor” condition at least at

one of the depths. This distribution suggests sustained anthropogenic disturbance rather than an isolated, one-time event. In
quantitative terms, the recorded ICA–NSF values were 46.40 (30 cm) and 45.04 (60 cm), confirming an overall unfavorable
water quality. These findings differ from those reported by Vera and Zambrano (2025) for the Canuto River, where “good”
water quality was observed, with ranges between 77.5 and 81.9. However, the data align with the findings of Das (2026), who
notes that the recurrence of “unsuitable” or “very poor” levels typically stems from anthropogenic activities such as the
discharge of industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, and domestic wastewater.
The persistently low values at P2, P3, and P5 are consistent with water bodies subject to organic inputs, fine sediment
transport, and nutrient enrichment—a combination typically associated with domestic discharges and runoff from developed
areas. The most compromised site was P3, with an average of 32.10, which consistently places it in the “poor” category. This
pattern is consistent with its combination of elevated nitrate levels (12.5–14.0 mg/L), very high phosphate levels (10.3–12.9
mg/L), BOD₅ exceeding 22 mg/L, and the highest recorded level of fecal coliforms at the surface (240 NMP/100 mL). From an
ecological perspective, this convergence of pressures implies an environment prone to trophic imbalances, increased biological
oxygen demand, and a loss of suitability for recreational use or water supply without robust treatment.
Regarding nutrient dynamics, Yap et al. (2020) argue that nitrate is essential for the growth of aquatic plants and
phytoplankton; however, Haldar et al. (2020) warn that high concentrations can lead to plant mortality and cause respiratory
problems in humans. On the other hand, Al-haen et al. (2026) and Shah et al. (2025) note that, although phosphates are less
mobile than nitrate, their transport into surface water bodies is a key factor in eutrophication processes. Similarly, the presence
of phosphates may result from the application of organic fertilizers, whose concentration typically decreases as soil depth
increases. The authors state that in their study, well depths ranged from 5 to 15 m. Missaoui et al. (2026) assert that the
elevated levels recorded are attributable to the intensive and prolonged agricultural use of phosphate fertilizers.
P1 showed the best relative condition at both depths, although it remained at only an average quality level. This
indicates that the system retains some areas with comparatively less impact, but they are not free from pressure. The
simultaneous presence of turbidity near 200 NTU and TDS levels exceeding 740 mg/L confirms that even the most favorable
site still operates under a significant load of suspended and dissolved solids, which limits the interpretation of a genuine
recovery of the water body. Atuhaire et al. (2026) argue that higher turbidity levels can be attributed to high biological activity,
including the decomposition of organic matter and runoff from agricultural fields into water bodies. For their part, authors such
as Chaves (2025) and Silva et al. (2015) state that suspended or dissolved matter in water—typically composed of minerals,
metals, and salts—has a detrimental effect on the quality of water bodies because it causes turbidity and promotes heat
absorption, thereby increasing the temperature.
The variation in depth was minimal. The overall average decreased only slightly from 46.40 to 45.04, and the specific
variation ranged from -7.5 to 2.7 units. This behavior suggests sufficient vertical mixing to distribute the pollutant load
throughout the water column. In terms of management, this means that the deterioration should not be interpreted as a
disturbance restricted to the surface layer, but rather as a condition affecting the system as a whole.
From a methodological standpoint, the ICA-NSF is particularly useful due to its ability to integrate heterogeneous
variables into a single, comprehensible scale; however, recent literature cautions that its performance depends on the
consistency between the original parameters and the surrogates used in real-world datasets. Therefore, in this study, the
interpretation focuses on the comparative and ecological value of the index, rather than on an absolute normative reading. Even
with this caveat, the signal of deterioration is robust, as multiple critical parameters point in the same direction. Gil et al.
(2018) indicate that an ICA study can serve as a guide for local authorities in making appropriate decisions to implement
corrective measures.

5. Conclusion
It is concluded that the water body is in a state of critical degradation, with an ICA–NSF ranging from 31.4 to 66.2,
falling predominantly into the “moderate” to “poor” quality categories. The deterioration is particularly severe at point P3,
where the high concentration of nutrients and organic matter indicates a persistent source of contamination. The consistency of
these results at depths of 30 and 60 cm confirms that the disturbance affects the entire water column, which validates the
hypothesis of sustained anthropogenic pressure. Given this scenario, it is imperative to implement a comprehensive
management plan that includes rigorous control of discharges and runoff management systems to halt the progression of water
quality deterioration.
