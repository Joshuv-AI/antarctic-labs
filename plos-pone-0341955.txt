Sensitivity analysis of annual energy production and associated economic losses due to external wake effects on offshore wind clusters: A case study of the Shinan offshore wind cluster in southwest sea of Korea

Geonhwa Ryu, Dohee Lee, Jungho Kim, Suji Han, Jun Yong Lee, Young-Gon Kim, Chae-Joo Moon

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the quantitative effects of external wake interactions and inter-farm spacing within a large-scale offshore wind cluster, focusing on the Shinan Cluster in Korea. As the country moves forward with government-led site development under the forthcoming Offshore Wind Special Act, the spatial clustering of multiple wind farms raises concerns about potential energy yield reductions and associated revenue losses caused by external wake effects. Using energy yield assessment simulations, a hypothetical 390 MW offshore wind farm was modelled adjacent to the Shinan offshore wind cluster. A sensitivity analysis was conducted across 12 scenarios by varying cluster completion levels (30%, 60%, and 100%) and inter-farm spacing distances (0 km, 1 km, 3 km, and 5 km). The results indicate that increasing the spacing between farms from 0 km to 3 km and 5 km reduced external wake losses from 8.0% to 5.2% and 4.3%, respectively, compared to the no-spacing case. For project developers, this translates into reductions in annual electricity revenue losses of approximately KRW 15.8 billion (3 km) and KRW 13.0 billion (5 km), based on a 2.5 REC scenario. These findings provide empirical evidence supporting the establishment of minimum inter-farm spacing guidelines to mitigate revenue loss and potential stakeholder conflicts within designated offshore wind cluster zones. The study offers actionable insights for policymakers and developers to promote sustainable offshore wind cluster design under Korea’s evolving regulatory framework.

Introduction As global efforts to combat climate change and strengthen energy security accelerate, offshore wind power has emerged as one of the most promising renewable energy sources [ 1 , 2 ]. Europe, led by Denmark, Germany, and the United Kingdom, has already accumulated extensive experience in operating offshore wind farms with capacities of several tens of gigawatts. More recently, large-scale offshore wind clusters have been actively developed and financed across East Asia, underpinned by ambitious national siting and development plans in countries such as China [ 3 – 5 ]. In Korea, the government has set forth the goal of achieving 18.3 GW of onshore and offshore wind capacity by 2030 under the 11th Basic Plan for Long-Term Electricity Supply and Demand. The national energy transition strategy increasingly prioritizes offshore wind deployment, with large-scale cluster developments planned and promoted in regions such as Shinan, Yeonggwang, and Gunsan in the southwestern seas [ 6 ]. Spatial development models for offshore wind farms in Korea can be classified into three categories: (1) the individually-developed sites led by private sector initiatives, (2) the cluster-type zones led by local governments that identify and plan sites exceeding 40 MW, and (3) the government-designated planned sites established under the legal framework of the forthcoming Offshore Wind Special Act [ 7 , 8 ]. The planned-site model represents a top-down national approach where optimal sites, permitting processes, and spatial coordination are managed by the central government. In contrast, the cluster-type zones function as a bottom-up model, initiated by local governments emphasizing community acceptance and regional participation. Designation as a cluster-type zone provides several policy advantages. It grants eligibility to request joint grid connection facilities from the Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO), offers partial permitting support from central authorities, and may include financial incentives of up to 0.1 Renewable Energy Certificate (REC) credits per MWh for power generated within the zone. The resulting benefits can be redistributed to local governments and communities. These incentives enhance regional co-benefits while improving efficiency in grid connection, permitting, and environmental management. Since 2020, the Ministry of Trade, Industry, and Energy (MOTIE) and the Korea Energy Agency have implemented the “Public-Led Offshore Wind Development Support Program,” assisting local governments such as Gunsan, Boryeong, Taean, Shinan, Yeosu, Incheon, Pohang, and Goheung in developing large-scale offshore wind projects [ 8 , 9 ]. Consequently, 1.4 GW in Jeonbuk and 3.2 GW in Shinan were officially designated as offshore wind cluster zones by MOTIE in 2025. Other local governments, including Incheon and Boryeong, are also pursuing cluster designation in coordination with public–private working groups ahead of the Offshore Wind Special Act’s enforcement. Despite these developments, current domestic regulations lack explicit technical criteria for determining the optimal spacing between individual offshore wind farms and do not provide guidelines for mitigating external wake losses which is a critical factor that influences both energy yield and developer profitability. While Korea’s policy framework emphasizes quantitative expansion through large-scale cluster or planned-site development [ 10 , 11 ], discussions concerning qualitative issues such as inter-farm energy losses, financial and economic impacts of external wakes, and conflict management among developers remain limited. There is also growing recognition of the need to define appropriate inter-farm spacing standards and spatial allocation criteria as government-led development expands. Experience from mature offshore wind markets highlights the importance of addressing these challenges. In Europe, for instance, external wake interactions between large-scale clusters and neighbouring wind farms have led to capacity factor reductions, revenue losses, and even legal disputes among project stakeholders. Cases such as the Hornsea development in the United Kingdom have brought to light compensation claims, spacing policy debates, and liability issues linked to wake-induced losses [ 8 ]. Furthermore, the legal dispute between Iberdrola and RWE concerning the East Anglia 2 and Five Estuaries projects, as well as the request to halt the Awel y Môr project due to insufficient pre‑assessment and mitigation of external wake losses, are also closely related to these “wind theft” issues [ 8 ]. Existing studies have demonstrated that neighbouring‑farm wakes can persist over tens of kilometres offshore, reduce capacity factors of downwind projects, and in some cases give rise to disputes over liability and compensation. However, most of this work focuses on the North Sea and other European waters, and very few studies quantify how such cluster‑scale wakes translate into long‑term AEP and financial metrics under the specific regulatory and market conditions of emerging offshore wind markets such as Korea. To address this research gap, this study introduces a hypothetical 390 MW offshore wind farm within the Shinan Offshore Wind Cluster to quantitatively evaluate the effects of external wakes and inter-farm spacing. A total of 12 sensitivity scenarios were analysed, combining variations in cluster operation ratios (30%, 60%, and 100%) and inter-farm spacing distances (0 km, 1 km, 3 km, and 5 km). The analysis quantifies both the energy yield reduction caused by external wakes and the corresponding economic loss, taking into account Korea’s revenue structure under the System Marginal Price (SMP) and REC mechanisms. Given the ongoing legal and institutional evolution of offshore wind policy, these results provide practical reference data for governments, regulators, and developers seeking to establish spatial design criteria for planned offshore wind zones. By objectively quantifying energy losses and economic impacts, the study underscores the necessity of introducing minimum spacing and wake management guidelines within clustered offshore wind developments. Such insights can contribute to preventing policy disputes, promoting sustainable industry growth, and supporting evidence-based planning for future large-scale offshore wind expansion in Korea. Literature review: External wake effects and inter-farm spacing External wake effects As offshore wind clusters and government-led planned sites continue to expand, the issue of external wake interactions among adjacent wind farms is becoming increasingly significant [ 12 – 14 ]. In general, a wake refers to a flow phenomenon characterized by velocity deficit and enhanced turbulence that forms downstream of a wind turbine as it extracts kinetic energy from the wind [ 15 ]. The wake released from upstream turbines can negatively influence the performance of downstream turbines, leading to reduced power generation efficiency [ 16 ]. This phenomenon occurs not only within a single wind farm (internal wakes) but also between neighbouring farms within cluster zones, where its impact is amplified as farm sizes increase and spatial layouts become denser. Wake effects typically manifest as diminished wind velocity, increased turbulence intensity, and subsequent energy losses, all of which ultimately reduce the annual energy production (AEP) and financial performance of downstream assets [ 17 – 20 ]. Therefore, quantifying external wake effects and identifying optimal inter-farm spacing are essential for developers, policymakers, and regulators during the design and planning stages of offshore wind clusters. Policy and spatial planning frameworks for inter-farm spacing in offshore wind clusters Inter-farm spacing serves as a key spatial design parameter for large-scale offshore wind developments organized through clustered or government-planned siting schemes. Adequate spacing between neighbouring wind farms helps minimize generation losses due to external wakes, ensures long-term energy efficiency, and reduces the risk of conflicts among project developers. Inadequate spacing can lead to wake-induced efficiency decline, economic loss, and even legal disputes between adjacent projects [ 21 – 23 ]. To mitigate such risks, many countries have established policies or engineering-based guidelines governing inter-farm spacing. In the United Kingdom, for instance, the Round 4 Offshore Wind Leasing guidelines specify a minimum distance of 7.5 km between new projects and at least 5 km from existing adjacent projects [ 8 ]. These regulations aim to minimize wake-induced energy losses and ensure safe marine navigational corridors. “Taiwan’s Offshore Wind Power Development Rules” legally mandate a minimum spacing of 1.2 km between offshore wind farms. In contrast, countries such as Germany, the Netherlands, and Denmark have not set fixed numerical criteria for inter-farm spacing. Instead, spacing and layouts are determined within the context of Marine Spatial Planning (MSP) processes, which consider navigational safety, environmental preservation, and other competing ocean uses in an integrated manner. Currently, Korea lacks explicit legal criteria concerning the minimum spacing between offshore wind farms to mitigate external wake effects. The Offshore Traffic Safety Assessment Guidelines prescribe separation distances based primarily on navigational safety defined as six times the length of the largest passing vessel plus 500 m and 0.3 nautical miles [ 24 ]. These criteria prioritize maritime safety rather than energy production efficiency or wake loss mitigation. Considering the nation’s transition toward government-led planned sites and large-scale cluster-type developments, there is an urgent need to establish a formal regulatory framework for inter-farm spacing. Incorporating international policy best practices and scientific research findings into Korean spatial planning policies would help prevent energy yield and revenue losses due to external wakes. Moreover, such institutionalization would promote sustainable industry growth and facilitate equitable coordination among offshore wind developers in the long term. The turbine layout was based on the open-source 15 MW reference wind turbine model developed by the IEA [ 25 – 27 ], which was applied to both the cluster-type zones and the hypothetical project to closely replicate real-world development scenarios. The hypothetical project was designed using a higher density criterion of roughly 9.0 MW/km², resulting in a total capacity of 390 MW to emphasize wake effects. Considering the prevailing wind direction in the southwest sea (northwest–north-northwest), the hypothetical project wind farm was positioned as far downstream as possible relative to the cluster, to conservatively assess maximum wake impacts. Wind resource input data Vortex MAPS mesoscale modelling data was utilized as the primary wind resource input. Vortex MAPS, based on the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) [ 28 ] numerical weather model, is specifically designed for regional wind resource assessment and leverages reanalysis datasets such as ERA5 to generate long-term averages for wind speed, turbulence intensity, and vertical/horizontal profiles over periods exceeding 20 years with a horizontal resolution of 1 km [ 29 ]. For this analysis, Vortex MAPS provided long-term wind resource characteristics including mean wind speed, direction, temperature, and turbulence intensity for each candidate site. Most notably, Vortex MAPS serves as an alternative data source reflecting long-term climate characteristics, making it suitable for spatial distribution and vertical profile analysis during the early design stages of large offshore wind cluster developments. Wake models Jensen wake model Commonly known as the Park model, the Jensen wake model is an analytical framework that linearly simplifies and predicts far-wake wind speed deficits occurring downstream of wind turbines (see Equation 1 ) [ 30 , 31 ]. The model is based on the assumption that the low-speed, high-turbulence zone formed behind a turbine due to energy extraction diffuses linearly with distance, resulting in gradual wind speed recovery. C t refers to the thrust coefficient, and k is the wake decay constant (WDC). For offshore conditions, a WDC value of 0.04–0.06 is typically applied; this study adopts a representative value of 0.04. D denotes the rotor diameter of the turbine. The combined velocity deficit at turbine i due to multiple upstream turbines is then obtained using the root‑sum‑square superposition method as below: δ i = ∑ j ∈ u ( i ) δ j → i 2 (3) where u ( i ) denotes the set of all turbines located upstream of turbine i for the considered wind direction. The effective hub‑height wind speed at turbine i is thus given by: U i = U 0 ( 1 − δ i ) (4) The Jensen model is widely used in commercial software as a default wake model, valued for its computational simplicity and flexible parameterization, making it applicable across both offshore and onshore contexts. However, its limitations include inadequate representation of turbulence intensity changes, inter-turbine interactions in large arrays, nonlinear wind speed distribution, and atmospheric effects. Consequently, it is known to have accuracy limitations and a tendency to underestimate wake losses in practical wind farm applications. TurbOPark wake model Unlike the Jensen model, which assumes linear wake expansion, the TurbOPark (Turbulence Optimised Park) model accounts for wake expansion deceleration as turbulence generated within the wake dissipates with increasing distance [ 32 – 35 ]. TurbOPark complements the physical interpretation of inter-turbine interactions by more accurately reflecting nonlinear effects such as wind speed deficits, turbulence intensity, and thermal impacts when multiple wakes overlap. This approach enables the prediction of induced losses not only for single turbine wakes but also for complex aggregated scenarios within large wind farms [ 36 – 39 ]. In this study, the TurbOPark model as implemented in the WindPRO PARK module is used to represent far‑wake development and inter‑farm interactions. The model retains the top‑hat assumption of the Jensen/Park approach but links the wake expansion rate to the local turbulence intensity, allowing the wake to recover faster in highly turbulent conditions while remaining persistent in low‑turbulence offshore environments. The formulation of the TurbOPark model builds upon the Jensen framework while introducing probabilistic and weighting methods to quantify the cumulative wake effects from multiple turbines downstream [ 40 ]. This model is particularly suitable for simulating external wake effects in large clusters, although it may conservatively estimate the magnitude and persistence of offshore external wake influences, potentially resulting in conservative annual energy yield predictions. Selection of wake models for external wake assessment For the analysis of external wake losses in offshore wind cluster developments, both the Jensen and TurbOPark wake models were selected, and an ensemble modelling approach was adopted by equally weighting the results from each model (weighting factor of 0.5). This methodology accounts for the inherent bias tendencies of each model: the Jensen model’s tendency to oversimplify wake expansion and thus underestimate wake losses, and TurbOPark’s propensity to overestimate losses by capturing nonlinear cumulative wake effects. To address these contrasting characteristics, model outputs were independently derived and then integrated with equal weights to arrive at the final loss estimate. Meanwhile, the Eddy Viscosity (EV) wake model was excluded from consideration, as it requires detailed atmospheric turbulence and flow field data not available during the early-stage, pre-feasibility assessment targeted in this project. The EV model is subject to considerable uncertainty in the absence of ample site measurements and detailed flow parameters, and its computational complexity limits practicality for large offshore wind cluster early-phase reviews [ 40 , 41 ]. Consequently, it was excluded from the model shortlist. Results and discussion Core assumptions for energy yield assessment The P50 energy yield represents the 50th percentile of long-term average energy production, meaning there is a 50% probability that actual generation will meet or exceed the predicted value. The P50 figure is routinely used for project feasibility evaluations, profitability analyses for investors, and credit/bond assessments by financial institutions, often serving as the ‘Best Case’ scenario during feasibility studies [ 6 ]. More conservative risk evaluations such as P75 and P90 percentiles are also commonly calculated, but P50 remains the technical and economic decision-making baseline. The blue shading reflects the magnitude of wind speed deficit attributable to wake effects, with darker blue regions indicating a deficit of up to 0.9 m/s relative to free-stream conditions. As in Fig 5 , Case 3 exhibits the most extensive and severe velocity deficits, illustrating substantial wake-induced wind speed reduction when turbine arrays are densely packed without spatial separation. Increasing inter-farm spacing from 0 km to 5 km progressively mitigates these wake interactions, as the additional distance allows greater wake recovery and dispersion before encountering subsequent turbines. This spacing improvement is manifested by weakening green and blue regions in Figs 5 and 6 for Cases 6, 9, and 12, indicating higher hub-height wind speeds and smaller wake-induced deficits. Fig 7 further illustrates the cross-sectional spatial variation of wind speed deficit across the Shinan cluster. It is evident that the magnitude of the difference between the free-stream wind speed and the waked wind speed progressively increases toward the central region of the cluster. This trend is attributable to the cumulative effect of overlapping wakes generated by upstream turbine arrays, which become more pronounced as the wind progresses into the denser core of the cluster. Furthermore, a clear distinction is observed among the different spacing scenarios: as the inter-farm spacing increases from Case 3 to Case 12, the severity of the wake-induced wind speed reduction is markedly alleviated. This finding demonstrates that greater spacing between wind farm sub-clusters effectively mitigates the adverse impact of wake losses on the available wind resource, thereby reducing the expected negative effect on annual energy production. The observed phenomenon where tighter spacing exacerbates wake-induced wind speed deficits can be primarily attributed to the physics of wake formation and recovery in clustered wind farm environments. Turbulent wakes generated behind wind turbines lead to a low-momentum region with reduced wind speeds and heightened turbulence, which slowly recovers with downstream distance due to turbulent mixing and ambient flow entrainment. When wind farms are positioned with minimal separation, these wakes overlap and interact, producing cumulative deficits that reduce available wind energy for downstream turbines, thereby impacting energy yield. This trend demonstrates that longer horizontal separations allow more room for wake recovery, substantially mitigating external wake impacts. When considering the cluster operational ratio, with no specified inter-cluster distance, external wake losses increased sharply with higher ratios: 3.7% (30%), 5.0% (60%), and 8.0% (100%), compared to the Base case without external wakes. Correspondingly, P50 capacity factor differences reached up to 2.5% between scenarios. Using the fixed P90/P50 ratio of 0.90 without independent uncertainty analysis, the estimated P90 capacity factor for each scenario was approximately 3.0% lower than the P50 value. In Case 3, compared to the Base case, the capacity factor suffered a 2.2% reduction and P90 annual energy yield decreased by roughly 8.0% due to external wakes. Overall, the results indicate that greater completion and operational rates of neighbouring offshore projects, as well as narrower distances between clusters, can significantly increase the annual energy yield losses attributable to external wakes. Offshore wind clusters within close geographic proximity demand careful consideration of external wake effects during yield assessments. Additionally, composite factors including the construction status, operational period, and turbine types of adjacent clusters should be thoroughly incorporated during the design and operational planning phases. External wake losses should be recognized not only for their impact on annual energy production, but also for their broader negative influence on project financial and contractual stability. Economic loss simulation results SMP and REC scenarios To estimate the economic impact of external wake losses for a hypothetical offshore wind project in the Shinan cluster, both the System Marginal Price (SMP) and Renewable Energy Certificate (REC) values were considered. RECs certify the environmental value of electricity produced from renewable sources and are issued to large-scale producers under Korea’s Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS), enabling them to fulfil renewable energy supply obligations. In fixed-price contract competitive bidding, long-term (20-year) contracts are awarded based on the combined price of SMP and REC (or REC price adjusted by weighting factors), negotiated between power producers and obligated suppliers. According to the revised RPS management guidelines (July 2021), coastal offshore wind is assigned a base REC weight of 2.0 (tidal flat or seawall), while offshore wind receives a base weight of 2.5, with final composite weightings determined by site-specific factors (such as transmission distance and water depth). Project revenue is calculated by multiplying actual energy output by the combined SMP and REC prices; any reduction in output (due to external wake losses, for example) directly reduces economic returns. Therefore, evaluating economic loss sensitivity through combinations of SMP and varying REC weights is a critical tool for assessing project feasibility and financial security in offshore wind development. This study analysed changes in annual energy revenues, under different SMP + REC scenarios, to quantify long-term economic impacts attributable to external wake effects. Reference values were drawn from the ‘2025 Korea Offshore Wind Fixed-Price Contract Competitive Bidding Announcement (first half)’, which set an upper price limit for land-based projects at 176,565 KRW/MWh (SMP + 1 REC). The reference SMP for land was 86,350 KRW/MWh, yielding a REC price of 90,215 KRW. Economic loss sensitivity was assessed for several REC weights (2, 2.5, 3.5, and 4.5). These scenarios cover basic weights (coastal: 2, offshore: 2.5) and the maximum expected REC weighting for deep water, remote floating wind clusters, such as Ulsan, which could reach 4.5 based on local depth and transmission distance. In Section 4.3.2, economic losses are compared for each combination of SMP and REC unit price and weighting factor, quantifying the annual energy yield impacts of external wake losses and providing concrete evidence for domestic offshore wind developers to support project feasibility decisions. This is especially important as the trend toward large, closely clustered offshore wind zones can lead to direct conflicts of interest between multiple developers. Thus, policy and regulatory responses including compensation frameworks, minimum inter-project distance rules, and the adoption of technical mitigation measures are essential subjects for further discussion and implementation. Conclusion and implications This study quantitatively analysed the impact of inter-cluster distance and external wake effects on energy yield and project economics, using the Shinan offshore wind cluster as a case study amid expanding government-led offshore wind cluster development. Sensitivity analysis across 12 scenarios showed that increasing the inter-cluster spacing from 0 km to 1 km, 3 km, and 5 km reduced external wake-induced energy losses from –8.0% to –6.6%, –5.2%, and –4.3%, respectively, with corresponding annual revenue loss reductions of up to 14.66 billion KRW (comparing Cases 3 and 12 at REC 3.5). These results highlight the need for policy measures such as minimum separation guidelines, which can mitigate profit loss and minimize potential disputes between project developers. Currently, Korea’s offshore wind permitting and design guidelines lack institutional consideration for external wake effects. However, as large-scale cluster development becomes reality, coordinating interests and establishing compensation frameworks among developers is becoming increasingly important. Based on the findings, the study proposes three key legal and institutional improvements: First, mandatory submission of external wake impact documentation. In the UK, cumulative impact assessments (CIA) are required for offshore wind projects [ 45 ]: these evaluate the overlapping impacts of individual projects on the environment and local communities, considering past, present, and planned activities. By systematically stating external wake impact assessment requirements following Europe’s precedent objective information would support fair review, permitting, and resolution among developers, and help prevent legal or commercial disputes [ 46 , 47 ]. For example, requiring government-mandated assessments when other permitted or operating projects exist within a specified radius (e.g., 20–30 km) would improve transparency, accountability, and reliability. Second, expanding R&D and developing domestic standards and models for objective quantification of external wakes and their cumulative impact. Enhancing research projects, field data sharing, and the creation of advanced predictive models at the national level would empower stakeholders to assess losses accurately and reduce conflict risk during site selection, contributing to robust industry confidence. Third, establishing minimum inter-cluster distances at the planning stage. Instituting clear minimum separations between offshore wind farms is critical for managing external wakes, ensuring economic viability, and building trust in the market. Insufficient separation exacerbates cumulative wakes, energy losses, and turbine fatigue, while well-defined rules support coexistence with local communities and various maritime interests. Reviewing international standards (see Section 2.2) and this study’s sensitivity results would help define a comprehensive Korean offshore wind separation policy from the outset, fostering sustainable national sector growth. According to Tamarindo, a global renewable energy network, offshore wind projects facing legal or commercial disputes over external wake impacts in the UK are expected to exceed 20 GW of capacity by 2025. Economic compensation for losses from external wakes is a likely issue in Korea’s clustered offshore wind market. Preventive strategies require sufficient project spacing, active cluster control, and, at the policy design stage, follow-up research on uncertainty, risk assessment, and scenario-based financial impact analysis under various siting and meteorological conditions. This paper provides an empirical foundation for a sustainable Korean cluster-based offshore wind planning model and supports the design of fair, transparent stakeholder frameworks, offering strategic insights for both academic research and future offshore wind policy and development. Limitations and future work This study quantitatively assessed the impact of external wake effects on energy production and economic performance, focusing on high-resolution mesoscale modeling of meteorological conditions, operational ratios of individual wind farms, and inter spacing scenarios within the Jeollanam-do Shinan offshore wind cluster zone. Nevertheless, the research presents several limitations and potential directions for further development. First, the numerical modeling results are inherently dependent on the specific meteorological and wind conditions of the Shinan offshore region such as annual mean wind speed, prevailing wind direction, and turbulence intensity. Therefore, the spatial patterns and magnitude of external wake losses may differ under distinct atmospheric environments or in other offshore areas. Future research should comprehensively evaluate the sensitivity of wake effects to variations in meteorological parameters, including atmospheric stability, boundary-layer structure, and vertical wind speed profiles. Such investigations are essential to reduce the uncertainty between simulated outcomes and actual conditions. Continuous comparison and validation between measured power production data and model results under diverse environmental conditions are required to ensure the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Second, while increasing inter-farm spacing can effectively mitigate external wake losses, it inevitably introduces drawbacks such as inefficient use of marine space, additional infrastructure requirements (e.g., transmission and grid connection), and higher capital expenditures associated with auxiliary civil works. Hence, a quantitative assessment is needed to identify the trade-off between the energy gains achieved from wake loss reduction and the economic costs arising from expanded spatial and infrastructure demands. In particular, attention should be given to potential diminishing returns, where the reduction of wake-induced energy loss becomes marginal beyond a certain separation distance. Deriving the optimal spacing range that maximizes overall techno-economic and societal benefits represents a crucial task for both policy formulation and industry practice. Third, to enhance the sustainability and international competitiveness of large-scale offshore wind cluster developments in Korea, it is essential to establish a national framework for minimum inter-farm spacing and external wake impact assessment that reflects key influencing factors such as hub-height-dependent wake expansion characteristics, cluster radius, and cumulative operational years. This effort requires the acquisition of empirical wake validation datasets and the development of predictive and compensatory models through coordinated national R&D programs. Furthermore, policymakers should recognize the importance of applying differentiated criteria and location-specific optimization to ensure that such standards deliver strategic and equitable benefits across regional and industrial contexts.
