Search for electroweak scale dijet resonances in pile-up collisions at s = 13 \sqrt{s}=13 TeV with the ATLAS detector


Abstract
A search for dijet resonances in the mass range of 100–250 GeV is presented using proton–proton collision data recorded by the ATLAS experiment at the Large Hadron Collider at a centre-of-mass energy of 13 TeV. Conventional searches for hadronic resonances in the sub-TeV regime are heavily constrained by high jet trigger thresholds required to manage the overwhelming rate of Standard Model multijet background processes described by quantum chromodynamics. To overcome this limitation, this analysis uses a novel strategy that separately reconstructs multiple proton–proton interactions per bunch crossing, known as pile-up collisions. The dataset was collected by the ATLAS experiment using single-electron and single-muon triggers in 2016–2018 corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 1.30 pb -1 , which represents the effective luminosity of pile-up collisions recorded alongside triggered events. This constitutes the first application of pile-up collisions to probe low dijet mass scales. No significant excess is observed above the background expectation and exclusion limits are set for a generic Gaussian model and a simplified dark matter model featuring an axial-vector mediator with coupling to quarks.

Introduction

Many extensions to the Standard Model (SM) predict the existence of new particles that couple to quarks and gluons, including excited quarks
[
1
]
, leptophobic
Z^{\prime}
and coloron resonances
[
2
,
3
]
, and axion-like particles
[
4
]
. The hadronic decays of such particles produced in proton–proton (
\mathit{pp}
) collisions would manifest as an excess of events in the invariant mass spectrum of pairs of jets above a smoothly falling background dominated by multijet processes described by quantum chromodynamics (QCD). Consequently, the search for dijet resonances has been a key part of the Large Hadron Collider (LHC)
[
5
]
physics programme.
Historically, searches at the LHC targeted heavy resonances in the multi-TeV regime
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. In this mass range, the decay particles from the posited resonance are highly energetic and straightforward to select using standard jet triggers.

Probing resonances below the TeV scale, however, presents significant experimental challenges. In this regime, the production rate of background SM QCD dijet events becomes prohibitive, significantly exceeding the storage bandwidth available to the experiments. To circumvent this limitation, recent analyses by the ATLAS
[
12
]
and CMS
[
13
]
collaborations have employed alternative strategies. One such approach involves storing only trigger-reconstructed objects with reduced information for subsequent offline analysis, in order to lower the data transfer rate
[
14
,
15
]
. Alternatively, searches target the associated production of resonances with a high transverse momentum (
p_{\text{T}}
) initial-state radiation (ISR) object corresponding to a jet or photon which is used to trigger on the collision
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. While highly effective, these approaches have their intrinsic limitations: storing trigger objects means an analysis cannot rely on offline event reconstruction, whereas ISR-based searches suffer from reduced acceptance and require specific topological assumptions about the signal.

In this paper, a novel search strategy is presented that exploits a unique dataset whereby multiple
\mathit{pp}
collisions (pile-up) are reconstructed separately in each bunch crossing recorded by ATLAS during the Run 2 data-taking period
[
20
]
; this dataset has been demonstrated to be trigger-unbiased. By reconstructing dijet events from these pile-up collisions, which are recorded simultaneously alongside triggered events, the low-mass regime can be accessed while retaining full offline reconstruction capabilities. This enables, for the first time, a search for dijet resonances in pile-up collisions.
Pile-up collisions are available only for the subset of LHC events
selected
by the trigger system, therefore the effective integrated luminosity is reduced compared to the total luminosity
processed
by the ATLAS trigger system and considered for a traditional trigger-based analysis.
Despite the reduced luminosity, the dataset offers competitive reach for signatures with a high production cross section and a low (jet) trigger acceptance, such as low-momentum multijet events, for which the trigger prescales are often very high
[
21
]
.
Other strategies to collect trigger-unbiased data can produce datasets with the same physical properties but with much reduced statistical power.
For example, the pile-up dataset has a factor of 30 higher luminosity than data collected in the ATLAS ZeroBias stream
[
21
]
.

The results of the analysis are interpreted in the context of SM
W/Z
production, a simplified
s
-channel dark matter model involving a leptophobic axial-vector mediator,
Z^{\prime}
, and a generic Gaussian signal in the mass range 100–250 GeV.
A model is chosen in which the
Z^{\prime}
is allowed to decay only into a quark pair, as the dark matter particle mass is assumed to lie significantly above the kinematically accessible phase space.
No assumptions are made for the final states of the Gaussian resonances, which can also include gluon jets.

The probed invariant mass region is already covered by previous searches targeting low-mass resonances, namely the dijet ISR photon
[
17
]
search. This earlier search relied on a boosted topology, where the dijet resonance is reconstructed as a single large-radius jet and selections on jet substructure are used to identify
p_{\text{T}}
-balanced two-prong decays in order to suppress the QCD background. These substructure selections make such searches less sensitive to models in which the decay is not balanced, for example in dark QCD scenarios where part of the shower is invisible
[
22
]
. In contrast, the resolved dijet strategy used in this analysis does not rely on substructure selections and therefore retains sensitivity to such signatures. Sensitivity to dark QCD depends on model parameter choices: some parameter combinations result in wide resonances that are similar to the larger width options of the
Z^{\prime}
resonances searched for in this result. In addition, this search has unique sensitivity to resonances that couple only to gluons: gluon–gluon initial states produce negligible photon ISR at leading order because gluons are electrically neutral, reducing strongly the sensitivity of the dijet ISR photon search to gluon-initiated production.

14
and Section

It consists of an inner tracking detector surrounded by a thin superconducting solenoid, electromagnetic and hadronic calorimeters,

The muon spectrometer (MS) comprises separate trigger and

The field integral of the toroids ranges between
2.0

Three layers of precision chambers, each consisting of layers of monitored drift tubes, cover the region
|\eta|<2.7

These are complemented in the innermost layer of the endcap region by cathode-strip chambers, providing precision tracking in the region
1.3<|\eta|<2.7
.
The muon trigger system covers the range
|\eta|<2.4
with resistive-plate chambers in the barrel region, and thin-gap chambers in the endcap regions.

Events were selected by the first-level trigger system implemented in custom hardware,

3
Data and simulated samples

The analysis makes use of
\mathit{pp}
collision data collected by ATLAS in 2016–2018. The data are selected using single-electron and single-muon triggers
[
25
,
24
]
, with a 26 GeV
p_{\text{T}}
requirement at the HLT. These triggers are used as they correspond to a significant fraction of the total trigger rate and because the single-lepton signature is relatively easy to identify and match to a given collision vertex.

In this paper the full detector readout following an HLT accept signal is referred to as a recorded bunch crossing (RBC); the term
event
is instead used to refer to the detector signals associated with a physics process generated by a single
\mathit{pp}
collision.
Each RBC contains on average 34 independent
\mathit{pp}
collisions, of which 60% can be reconstructed as separate primary vertices
[
27
]
.
In the context of dijet resonances, the difference between
\mathit{pp}
collisions and primary vertices is reconstruction-efficiency-based not physics-origin-based, thus the two terms will often be used interchangeably, highlighting the conceptual goal of reconstructing every
\mathit{pp}
collision.
Reconstructed primary vertices can be further classified as being either a triggering primary vertex (TPV) or a pile-up primary vertex (PPV): TPVs are defined as vertices associated with the
\mathit{pp}
interaction(s) that produced the electron and/or muon signature(s) that triggered the recording of the bunch crossing, while all other reconstructed primary vertices are labelled as PPVs.
The pile-up dataset is obtained by processing each RBC, identifying and removing the TPV(s) from that RBC, and analysing the remaining PPVs. Primary vertex reconstruction is described in

4
sec:ObjReco

, while the identification of the TPV and its removal are described in more detail in

5
sec:EvSel

.

The pile-up reconstruction procedure yields a recorded dataset corresponding to an effective integrated luminosity of 1.30
pb
-1
. The integrated luminosity of the pile-up dataset is obtained by considering the combined rate of data
recorded
using the single-electron and single-muon triggers as a fraction of the total
delivered
bunch crossing rate, following the procedure described in Ref.
[
20
]
. This results in the pile-up dataset integrated luminosity being roughly (30 MHz delivered)/(300 Hz recorded) =
10^{5}
times smaller than the integrated luminosity of the delivered dataset, which is measured by the LUCID–2 detector.

Monte Carlo (MC) samples of signal and background events are used to optimize the analysis selection and to interpret the results. When using simulated event samples, the assumption is that the generated process of interest would constitute an individual
\mathit{pp}
collision vertex in the pile-up dataset after all quality selection procedures detailed in Ref.
[
20
]
.
These samples are therefore generated using the standard ATLAS simulation pipeline, without extra requirements on the presence of leptons for the trigger selections or TPV removal.

The main benchmark model is a simplified leptophobic
Z^{\prime}
signal, generated according to recommendations in Refs.
[
28
,
29
]
. The signal is assumed to be produced in the
s
-channel with axial-vector interactions. The simplified model is described by two parameters: the mediator mass,
m_{Z^{\prime}}
, and its coupling to the quarks,
g_{q}
. A grid of signal samples is generated, including the following points:
m_{Z^{\prime}}=80,100,150,250\,\text{GeV}{}
and an equal coupling to all quarks of
g_{q}\in[0.2,1.0]
, with a spacing of 0.2.
The intrinsic width of the
Z^{\prime}
is accounted for at all times, and is found to be non-negligible for
g_{q}
larger than roughly 0.2, leading to wider resonances for higher
g_{q}
values.
Couplings with
g_{q}>1.0
are not considered as the generated resonance width-to-mass ratio exceeds 50%.
The signal samples neglect any interference effects between the
Z^{\prime}
and the SM
Z
boson.
The
Z^{\prime}
decays to
u,d,s,c,b
quark flavours. Decays of the
Z^{\prime}
into the dark matter candidate are assumed to be negligible, with the dark matter coupling set to
g_{\chi}=1
and the mass fixed at
m_{\chi}=10\penalty\ \text{TeV}
, making such decays kinematically forbidden.

The signal samples are generated using the
MadGraph
2.9.16
[
30
]
generator at leading order (LO) in perturbative QCD with the
NNPDF2.3lo

[
31

. The events are interfaced with

[
33

EvtGen
1.7.0

[
34

is used to model the decays of heavy flavour quarks.

Simulated QCD multijet samples are employed to obtain the detector resolution binning for the invariant mass fit and for signal-to-background optimisation studies. Multijet samples were generated using

35

The renormalisation and factorisation scales were set to the geometric mean of the squared transverse masses of the two outgoing particles in the matrix element,
p^{\text{hat}}_{\text{T}}=\sqrt{(p_{\text{T},1}^{2}+m_{1}^{2})(p_{\text{T},2}^{2}+m_{2}^{2})}

NNPDF2.3lo

[

was used in the matrix-element (ME) generation, the parton shower, and the simulation of the multi-parton interactions. The A14 set of tuned parameters was used.

The generated signal and background events are passed through detector simulation

6
Object reconstruction

Reconstructing jets from pile-up collisions relies heavily on the capabilities of charged particle (track) reconstruction and particle flow objects (PFOs). First, good track reconstruction performance is instrumental in reconstructing different pile-up collisions as distinct clusters of tracks (primary vertices) in a given RBC. Furthermore, electron and muon tracks need to be associated with a given primary vertex with a very high accuracy in order to correctly identify and remove the TPV and thus obtain an unbiased pile-up dataset. Finally, these tracking capabilities are critical for the assignment and reconstruction of jets from different primary vertices.

This section describes the reconstruction of standard detector signatures in ATLAS, namely tracks, particle showers in the calorimeters, and primary vertices. These are used as building blocks in the reconstruction of physics objects such as electrons, photons, muons and jets from all primary vertices to obtain a global interpretation of a given RBC.

The reconstructed dataset contains overlapping signatures from both the triggering event and pile-up collisions. The final analysis selections demonstrated to produce a trigger-unbiased dataset are further described in

7
sec:EvSel

.

7.1
Standard object reconstruction

Tracks are reconstructed in the ID by fitting a trajectory model to tracker hits
[
27
]
. Tracks with
p_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{track}}>500\,\text{MeV}
are used as inputs for an iterative primary vertex reconstruction procedure
[
40
]
. The reconstructed primary vertices are sorted by decreasing
\sum\left(p_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{track}}\right)^{2}
. Vertices that are considered in this analysis are required to have at least two associated tracks.

Particle showers are reconstructed as clusters of energy deposits in the calorimeters
[
41
]
. These topo-clusters are formed by a topological clustering algorithm that identifies seed cells which have an energy at least four times the noise threshold. The algorithm iteratively expands around the seed cell by including any additional cells with an energy at least twice the noise threshold. As a final stage, any remaining neighbouring cells are added.

PFOs are obtained by combining tracks and topo-clusters. The algorithm attempts to match tracks to calorimeter clusters. The energy expected to be deposited by the charged particle is calculated and used to judge whether the topo-cluster is compatible with the charged particle hypothesis, or whether additional neutral energy is present. The expected energy is then subtracted on a cell-by-cell basis from the matching topo-clusters, and any remaining energy indicative of neutral particles is left to form additional topo-clusters. The tracks are classified as charged PFOs, while the surviving topo-clusters are referred to as neutral PFOs. Charged and neutral PFOs are used as inputs to jet reconstruction in the particle flow algorithm, described in detail in Ref.
[
42
]
.

Electron candidates are obtained by combining the information from the ID and the electromagnetic calorimeter
[
43
]
. Electrons are required to satisfy
p_{\text{T}}>7\,\text{GeV}
and
|\eta|<2.5
. Two types of electron candidates are considered.
Loose
electrons are required to satisfy the medium likelihood-based identification working point
[
44
]
and are used for trigger matching and TPV identification. The inputs to the likelihood include measurements from the tracking system, the calorimeter system, and quantities that combine both tracking and calorimeter information.
Tight
electrons, on the other hand, are required to pass the tight likelihood-based identification working point and a tight isolation requirement.
Tight
electrons are used for an overlap removal procedure with jets.

Photons are reconstructed from clusters in the electromagnetic calorimeter
[
43
]
. Photons are required to have
p_{\text{T}}>7\,\text{GeV}
and
|\eta|<2.37
, and are not considered if they fall within the transition region between the barrel and endcap calorimeters
(
1.37<|\eta|<1.52
). They are required to satisfy the tight identification criteria for the lateral and longitudinal shower shape and the tight isolation working point
[
44
]
. In general, photons cannot be easily associated with a specific primary vertex to the required level of precision. Photons are only used in the procedure to remove overlaps with jets.

Muon candidates are reconstructed by combining the hits from the ID and the muon spectrometer
[
45
]
. Muons are considered if they are successfully reconstructed via combined, stand-alone, or segment-tagged algorithms, satisfy minimal quality criteria and have
p_{\text{T}}>7\,\text{GeV}
and
|\eta|<2.5
. Similar to electrons, two kinds of muon candidates are defined, differing in their isolation requirements.
Loose
muons, which are used for TPV identification, are required to pass a track-based isolation requirement
p_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{varcone30}}/p_{\mathrm{T}}<0.07
. This requirement calculates the ratio of the total transverse momentum of surrounding tracks within a cone of
\Delta R=0.3
(excluding the muon’s own momentum) and helps suppress muons with a second high-momentum track nearby. This criterion was found to help reduce differences in TPV identification between electron and muon triggers, likely due to reducing an inherent difference between such triggers in regards to heavy-flavour hadron decays: muon decays are more likely to pass a muon trigger, while electron decays are unlikely to pass an electron trigger due to the large surrounding hadronic component.
Tight
muons, which are only used for overlap removal with jets, are required to satisfy a tight particle-flow-based isolation requirement.

The
loose
electron and muon
p_{\text{T}}
thresholds are set significantly below the single-lepton trigger threshold (26
GeV
) to maximize the rate of TPV identification and removal described in Section
9
. The
loose
offline lepton collections are required to be matched to the corresponding object at the HLT via a
\Delta R<0.1
requirement.

7.2
Pile-up jet reconstruction

To reconstruct jets from pile-up collisions, the standard ATLAS jet reconstruction algorithm is extended to all primary vertices. This procedure is described in detail in Ref.
[
20
]
and is summarized below. The anti-
k_{t}
algorithm
[
46
]
with a radius parameter of
R=0.4
is used to reconstruct jets using PFOs as inputs using the
FastJet

[
47
]
software package. The algorithm is run once for each reconstructed primary vertex. Only charged PFOs within
|(z_{0}-z_{\mathrm{PV}})\sin\theta|<1\,\mathrm{mm}
are used for each vertex, where
z_{0}
is the longitudinal position of the track at the point of closest approach in the transverse plane and
z_{\mathrm{PV}}
is the primary vertex position along the beam axis. The association is exclusive, meaning that if there are multiple vertices satisfying this criterion, the charged PFO is only considered for the vertex that minimizes the longitudinal separation. The same set of neutral PFOs, origin-corrected to point to the current primary vertex, is used inclusively across all vertex iterations, as neutral PFOs carry no particular vertex interpretation. Since the jet clustering is run independently for each vertex, several jets are typically reconstructed for a given region of the calorimeter, corresponding to the same neutral PFOs adjusted corresponding to different vertex interpretations.

Jets from pile-up collisions are calibrated following the standard ATLAS procedure. This consists of several MC-based corrections that correct the average jet response to the particle-level scale, followed by in situ calibrations that account for differences between data and simulated events
[
48
]
. Wherever applicable, the calibration is modified to take into account the current vertex under consideration (which may not be the vertex with the highest
\sum(p_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{track}})^{2}
as in the standard ATLAS jet reconstruction), but is otherwise identical. In Ref.
[
20
]
the calibration was found to produce compatible jet energy scale responses between different PVs in simulated events.

Due to its combinatorial nature, the modified jet reconstruction procedure produces a large number of redundant jets. The jet-vertex tagger (JVT)
[
49
]
is used to select jets that are consistent with their vertex reconstruction hypothesis: charged tracks pointing at the jet must primarily originate from the vertex in question, and the ratio of the jet’s charged activity to its total energy must roughly correspond to hadronic showering expectations. This significantly reduces the number of jets that do not have significant charged activity associated with the current vertex of interest.

Pile-up jets that satisfy
p_{\text{T}}>17\,\text{GeV}
,
|\eta|<2.0
and the JVT requirement are retained for analysis.
Overlaps between jets and other physics candidate objects are considered.
Any jet found within
\Delta R<0.2
of a
tight
electron is removed.
Any jet with less than three tracks associated to it found within
\Delta R<0.2
of a
tight
muon, or if a muon inner-detector track would be clustered into the jet, is removed. Finally, any jet found within
\Delta R<0.4
of a photon is removed.

These requirements impose a stringent vertex interpretation for most pile-up jets, but uncommon cases of overlapping showers from differing primary vertices remain. The procedure for addressing these jet–jet overlaps is described in

8
sec:EvSel

.

9
Analysis selections

The final dataset used in this search is obtained after a series of sequential selections applied on the reconstructed pile-up data, first at the object level as discussed in

10
sec:ObjReco

, then at the RBC level and finally at the primary vertex level, closely following the selections used in Ref.
[
20
]
.

RBCs are required to pass either the single-electron or single-muon trigger requirement, and a veto is applied for RBCs with errors identified in the Tile, LAr or SCT detectors
[
50
,
51
]
.
In addition, RBCs with jets arising from sources other than
\mathit{pp}
collisions are removed via Loose jet cleaning criteria
[
52
]
. It is required that a TPV can be found, as described in detail in Ref.
[
20
]
, by matching an offline loose lepton to its triggering counterpart and requiring that the offline lepton can be uniquely associated to a primary vertex.
If either of these conditions fails, and the collision of origin for the triggering lepton cannot be found, the entire RBC is vetoed.

The primary vertices in the surviving RBCs are examined next.
First, in order to ensure that the analysed PPVs in the RBC remain unbiased by the trigger, the TPV is vetoed together with any primary vertices within
\left|\Delta z(\mathrm{TPV,PV})\right|<2\mathrm{\ mm}
.
The remaining PPVs are required to have at least two jets with
p_{\text{T}}>17\,\text{GeV}
and
|\eta|<2.0
matched to them; these two leading jets form the dijet system used in the analysis.
The robustness of the TPV subtraction procedure is checked by comparing the dijet invariant mass spectrum between RBCs selected by electron and muon triggers; the two spectra were found to be compatible within the statistical precision of the dataset.

In order to reduce contributions from overlapping high-momentum signals originating in different
\mathit{pp}
collisions, the two leading jets for each PPV are required not to overlap with other jets from a different PPV within the RBC.
This requirement is achieved by means of a geometrical isolation criterion: jets from different PPVs are considered to overlap if they are within
\Delta R=0.4
of each other.
The isolation requirement exceptionally lowers the jet
p_{\text{T}}
threshold from 17
GeV
to 15
GeV
and increases the detector acceptance from
\left|\eta\right|<2.0
to
\left|\eta\right|<2.4
.
This conservative choice is made to ensure that jets are not mistakenly qualified as isolated because the jet they overlap with happens to be just past the kinematic or geometric threshold used, thereby better capturing stochastic overlaps near analysis thresholds.
If such an overlap is identified, only the affected PPV is removed from the analysis, while other PPVs in the same RBC that satisfy the isolation requirement are retained.
More details on this isolation criterion can be found in Ref.
[
20
]
. An additional jet cleaning selection is imposed to reject PPVs in which either of the two leading jets deposits more than half of its energy in the Tile Calorimeter gap and cryostat scintillators, located in a narrow Tile gap region from
1.0<|\eta|<1.6
. Such jets are susceptible to large calibration corrections and noise fluctuations due to the limited active material in this region, and were observed to degrade the background modelling.
Additionally, a requirement that the timing
2
2

2

The jet timing is calculated as the energy-squared-weighted sum of the timing of the constituent clusters, which in turn is the energy-squared-weighted sum of the timing of the cells within each cluster.
of the two leading jets be within 10 ns of the nominal bunch-crossing time is applied in order to reduce contributions from out-of-time pile-up.
Finally, the two leading jets in each PPV are required to satisfy the condition
\left|y^{*}\right|=\frac{\left|y_{1}-y_{2}\right|}{2}<0.8
, where
y_{1}
(
y_{2}
) is the rapidity of the leading (subleading) jet. This selection enhances the presence of resonant signal-like topologies while suppressing contributions from the main background, SM multijet processes, which increases with
\left|y^{*}\right|
due to
t
-channel poles at leading order. The threshold was found to be the optimal selection for maximal Poisson significance (
S/\sqrt{B}
) for the models targeted in this search, estimated with
Z^{\prime}

Methodology

11.1
Resonance search

The invariant mass range explored in this analysis is dominated by QCD multijet background, which is modelled using a smoothly-falling function, with small additional expected resonant contributions from hadronic decays of SM
W
and
Z
bosons. Inclusive observations of the hadronic
W/Z
dijet signal at a hadron collider were previously reported by the UA2 Collaboration
[
53
,
54
]
. Producing simulated event samples for inclusive
W
and
Z
bosons with hadronic decays, possibly in association with other jets, and at NLO accuracy with state-of-the-art MC generators, was found to be challenging due to divergences in collinear and infrared regimes. Instead, these resonances are modelled with Gaussian templates, justified since the detector resolution of the
W
and
Z
resonances exceeds their intrinsic resonance widths.
The final template used,
f_{W/Z}
, is the sum of two Gaussians, one centred at
m_{W}=80
GeV and the second one at
m_{Z}=91
GeV.
A value of
\sigma=15\mathrm{\ GeV}
is considered for both resonances, estimated from the simulated MC event sample for a
Z^{\prime}
boson with mass of 80 GeV, as the experimental resolution does not change significantly over that mass range.

An overall normalisation factor is included for both Gaussians, with a relative normalisation factor applied to the template for the
Z
boson to account for the difference in cross section and branching ratio to quarks between both resonances
[
55
]
. The lack of a precise estimate limits the ability to disentangle potential BSM signals near the
W/Z
resonance, and therefore the BSM interpretations of the search are only performed for masses above 100 GeV.

The first step in the analysis is to study the compatibility of the data with the background-only hypothesis, arising from SM multijet processes (Section
11.2
), where the procedure for quantifying the degree of compatibility must be defined in advance of looking at the data. If the
\chi^{2}

p
-value of the background-only fit is smaller than 0.05, the
W/Z
template is added to the background model to account for the contribution of hadronic SM
W/Z
processes. Due to being near the start of the considered
m_{jj}
spectrum, any SM
W/Z
contribution may either show up as a local excess at
m_{W/Z}
or may bias the start of the fit, thereby creating tension later in the
m_{jj}
spectrum. The spectrum is then refit using this new background model.

If the
\chi^{2}

p
-value is still smaller than 0.05, the
BumpHunter
algorithm
[
56
,
57
]
is subsequently used to identify local excesses as a proxy for resonance-like behaviour present in the data. It performs a scan of all possible adjacent windows in the fit mass range, and evaluates the consistency of the observed yields with Poisson fluctuations over the expected number of events.
The background-only fit is repeated after removing the highest local excess identified by
BumpHunter
, where a new
\chi^{2}

p
-value of 0.05 or higher indicates compatibility with the hypothesis of a single resonance on top of the smoothly falling background.
In this case, there remains an ambiguity: the original fit tension may have been related to the SM
W/Z
contribution being large enough to affect the background model description, the presence of a local excess, or a combination of both effects.
The relevance of the SM
W/Z
contribution is therefore tested by removing the
W/Z
template from the background model and retaining the same
BumpHunter
window exclusion; in case the
\chi^{2}

p
-value remains above 0.05, the
W/Z
template is dropped from the background model to simplify the background description, otherwise it is retained.
After the inclusion or exclusion of the
W/Z
template in the background model has been fixed, the
BumpHunter
excess is studied in the context of the
Z^{\prime}
scenario discussed in Section
3
.

If the fit of the background function to the data yields a
\chi^{2}

p
-value of 0.05 or higher, the background-only hypothesis is not rejected. In this case, exclusion upper limits on the cross section (
\sigma
) times acceptance times efficiency (
A
) times branching ratio to jets (
\mathrm{B}
),
\sigma\times A\times\mathrm{B}
, are set for the SM and BSM resonance interpretations. The upper limits are set for the
W/Z
resonance, described as a double-Gaussian template, as well as for two signal scenarios: the
Z^{\prime}
models discussed in Section
3
and Gaussian-like dijet resonances for masses above 100 GeV. Only signals with resonance masses between 100 and 250 GeV are considered in the upper limit computation, in order to ensure that the majority of the resonance shape is contained within the fit range used for the background estimate, introduced in detail in Section
11.2
.

Templates for benchmark points in the
Z^{\prime}
scenario are generated with MC simulation, as discussed in Section
3
. The signal templates are fitted with a double-sided Crystal Ball (DSCB) function
[
58
]
, and the resulting parameterization is subsequently employed in the signal-plus-background fits.
The mean of the Gaussian bulk was found to be shifted downwards by around 6–7% compared to the generated
Z^{\prime}
mass.
The granularity of the mass grid for a given coupling
g_{q}
is increased with additional templates created by performing an interpolation between the available event samples, following a procedure similar to the one discussed in Ref.
[
59
]
. For the Gaussian signal scenarios, templates built with a Gaussian distribution with mean equal to the resonance mass are used. In this case, the relative width of the distribution, representing the total width including both the intrinsic resonance width and the detector resolution, is considered at values of 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% of the resonance mass. As the relative detector mass resolution improves with increasing mass, the lower bound of 10% is chosen to correspond to the resolution at the highest mass point considered. The upper bound of
25\%
is fixed by fit considerations, as above this width fitting the signal component becomes increasingly more difficult and thus the signal sensitivity starts to drop rapidly.

11.2
Background estimate

The non-resonant background in this search corresponds to SM multijet processes.
Due to the large statistics in the pile-up dataset in the targeted mass range, producing simulated samples for multijet processes with enough statistical power is computationally unfeasible.
Instead, the invariant mass of the two leading jets of the selected PPVs,
m_{jj}
, is fit with a family of functions that preserve the smoothly falling behaviour expected from SM multijet processes:

f_{N}(x)=p_{1}(1-x)^{p_{2}}x^{\sum_{i=3}^{N}p_{i}\log^{(i-3)}(x)},

(1)

where
x\equiv m_{jj}/\sqrt{s}
and
N
is a fixed number of parameters.
Such functions were found to provide appropriate background estimates in a number of dijet and multijet resonance searches by the ATLAS and CMS collaborations
[
60
,
8
,
7
,
11
,
10
,
9
,
18
,
14
]
.
In this analysis, versions of the functional form with three to nine free parameters were tested.

An additional turn-on term is included in the description to account for deviations from the behaviour described by Equation
1
at the beginning of the
m_{jj}
spectrum due to jet
p_{\text{T}}
threshold effects and reconstruction inefficiencies.
The chosen functional form to fit the data is thus

f_{\textrm{fit}}(x,N)=\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{\frac{-(x-x_{0})}{k}}}\right)f_{N}(x),

(2)

where
x_{0}
is the point where the turn-on function reaches 50% efficiency, and the parameter
k
controls the steepness of the turn-on curve. Both the
x_{0}
and
k
parameters are allowed to float freely in the fit.
The data are divided into narrow 0.5 GeV equidistant bins as an approximation to an unbinned fitting procedure. This bin width is much smaller than the detector mass resolution, which ranges from approximately 12 to 18 GeV over the mass range relevant to the analysis.
The fit is then performed using a framework based on the
RooFit

[
61
]
and
RooStats

[
62
]
codes.
The
m_{jj}
range used in the fit spans from 55 to 300 GeV.
After the fit, the data are rebinned into mass-dependent bin sizes that reflect the detector resolution, as estimated with dijet MC simulated event samples.
The rebinned data are also used as input in deriving the fit
p
-values in the
BumpHunter
algorithm.

A number of cross-checks are performed on the data, including spurious signal and signal injection tests, to ensure the robustness of the fitting procedure for fixed
N
in describing the background and its ability to mask potential signals present in the data following the recommendations given in Ref.
[
63
]
.
First, pseudodata are created by fitting the data with a
f_{\mathrm{fit}}(x,N+1)
function and sampling from it the
m_{jj}
distribution multiple times, applying Poisson fluctuations that match the statistical uncertainties observed in data.
The fit with
f_{\mathrm{fit}}(x,N+1)
is required to have a
\chi^{2}

p
-value higher than 0.05 to ensure the compatibility of the data with the background-only hypothesis.
This ensures the pseudodata have realistic statistical power while containing no resonant structure.

In the spurious signal tests, signal-plus-background fits are performed to the pseudodata distributions, where the background component is given by
f_{\textrm{fit}}(x,N)
.
Two options for the signal hypothesis are used:
Gaussian-like resonance templates under different mass hypotheses within the fit range and widths between 10 and 25% of the mass provide model-independent evaluation of bias in the signal extraction, and simulated
Z^{\prime}
signal templates under the different mass and coupling
g_{q}
hypotheses discussed in Section
3
.
The spurious signal test checks whether the extracted amount of signal is compatible with the background-only distribution used to generate the pseudodata, revealing potential biases in the fitting procedure.
For a given signal hypothesis, a signal yield is extracted from each pseudodata distribution.
The mean
S_{\text{spur}}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{\text{spur}}
of the distribution of signal yields are required to fulfil the condition
S_{\text{spur}}/\sigma_{\text{spur}}<0.5
for all signal hypotheses.

In the signal injection test, signal templates are added to the pseudodata distributions to evaluate whether the amount of signal present in the data can be extracted without bias.
Both Gaussian shapes and
Z^{\prime}
signal templates are used, as well as the
W/Z
double Gaussian template, injected with a given cross-section such that the significance
S/\sqrt{B}
is in the range 0-5.
The number of signal
S
and background
B
events are estimated in each test as the contribution of each process to a full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) window around the peak of the injected signal.
The mean
S_{\mathrm{inj}}
of the extracted signal yield distributions is required to be within 0.5
\sigma_{\mathrm{inj}}
of the injected signal yield for all signal hypotheses, with
\sigma_{\mathrm{inj}}
being the standard deviation of the extracted signal yield distribution.

The final fitting procedure applied to the full dataset uses a function of the form
f_{\textrm{fit}}(x,N=5)
to describe the observed
m_{jj}
spectrum in the pile-up dataset.
This number was chosen by performing an
F
-test
[
63
]
to the functions that pass all spurious signal and signal injection tests in a statistically reduced dataset of about one fifth of the total luminosity of the pile-up data, constructed by randomly selecting one in every five RBCs from the dataset with the requirements discussed in Section
9
.
This was done to ensure the chosen function would be optimal when describing a dataset with negligible signal contribution, containing mostly background events.
The validity of this choice to use a sub-dataset to characterise the fit quality was studied by repeating the same set of tests using a roughly
1/35^{\mathrm{th}}
dataset, where the results were cross-checked on the one-fifth dataset.

12
Systematic uncertainties

A number of experimental sources of uncertainty are considered in the interpretation of the analysis under different signal hypotheses.
Systematic uncertainties in the jet energy scale and resolution are evaluated on a jet-by-jet basis on the simulated
Z^{\prime}
samples discussed in

13
sec:DataMC

.
An ensemble of 43 nuisance parameters are considered, following the description in Ref.
[
48
]
.
The impact on the signal templates is estimated by varying each source of uncertainty up and down by one standard deviation.
The nominal and varied
m_{jj}
distributions are fitted with a DSCB function, and the impact of each systematic variation on the mean and width of the distribution is applied as a systematic uncertainty.
The largest jet-energy-related sources of uncertainty on the mean of the signal are the jet flavour composition and response, with differences up to 2 GeV.
The impact of the width of the signal templates is negligible, with the largest contributions equally coming from the jet flavour composition and response, which remain at the sub-GeV level.

An uncertainty on the procedure used to adjust the simulated pile-up conditions to match those in recorded data, as applied to the simulated
Z^{\prime}
samples, is derived by varying the total inelastic cross-section used in the determination of the distribution of the number of expected collisions in simulation. The resulting alternative weights are applied to simulated events and the effect on the final observables is taken as a systematic uncertainty.

Uncertainties in the signal yield extraction due to the background modelling are accounted for by including the amount of spurious signal as part of the systematic uncertainty prescription.
For each signal hypothesis, a separate signal component with equal shape is implemented, with maximum amplitude
0.5\sigma_{\mathrm{spur}}
and constrained by Gaussian terms in the likelihood.

As discussed in Ref.
[
20
]
, the uncertainty on
the luminosity of the collected pile-up dataset is 0.83%.
It is applied as an uncertainty on the normalisation of the extracted signals from the fit, and is found to be negligible with respect to the other sources of experimental uncertainty.

Electrons and muons are important to the reconstruction of the pile-up dataset: they are the means by which TPV(s) are identified and thus how trigger bias is removed.
This is of relevance solely to data, where the impact of using a triggered dataset must be mitigated; in simulation, there is no such concern.
Typical lepton uncertainties related to differences in their reconstruction and identification between data and simulation are thus not applied, as leptons are never used in simulation.
The efficiencies related to the use of leptons in the TPV removal process, namely lepton reconstruction, offline-online matching, and vertex identification, are estimated directly in data using the full analysis dataset and the uncertainties on those efficiencies are found to be negligible.
This factorisation assumes that vertices are sufficiently uncorrelated so that the jets used in the analysis are not impacted by the presence (or absence) of a TPV, which was demonstrated to be a valid assumption in Ref.
[
20
]
.

2
, are found to be
x_{0}=43.7\pm 0.2
GeV and

\chi^{2}

Given no significant excess over the background prediction is found, the background model is accepted.
The analysis proceeds to search for two signal scenarios:
Z^{\prime}
signals, as discussed in Section
3
, and Gaussian-like dijet resonances, both for masses above 100 GeV.
Upper limits on
\sigma\times A\times B
, the product of the cross section, acceptance times efficiency, and branching ratio to jets, are then set for both of these signal scenarios at 95% CL.

Signal-plus-background fits are performed for the
W/Z
as well as for each individual hypothesis for both signal scenarios.
For the
Z^{\prime}
and Gaussian signals, the systematic uncertainties discussed in Section
12
are introduced as nuisance parameters constrained with Gaussian terms in the likelihood. For the
W/Z
interpretation, the corresponding spurious signal systematic uncertainties are included in the fit and propagated to the derived cross section limits.

Similarly to the procedure followed in Ref.
[
14
]
, the parameters related to the background estimate are left to float in the signal-plus-background fits to take into account the functional form flexibility and its interplay with the signal template considered.
A modified frequentist approach
[
64
]
is used to derive upper limits at 95% CL with the
\mathrm{CL}_{\mathrm{S}}

[
65
]
technique for each signal scenario.
The best fit for the
W/Z
template normalisation is consistent with zero, thus the analysis does not have sensitivity to observe the
W/Z
signal.
The expected (assuming no
W/Z
signal) and observed 95% confidence level upper limits on the yield of the
W
and
Z
boson template are:

N_{\text{exp}}^{95\%\,\text{CL}}=(7.1^{+2.8}_{-2.0})\times 10^{4},\quad N_{\text{obs}}^{95\%\,\text{CL}}=5.8\times 10^{4}.

(3)

These limits are compared to the expected contribution from SM
W
and
Z
bosons to the analysis after selections. The production cross sections for each process as measured by ATLAS are
\sigma(pp\rightarrow W)=190.1\mathrm{\ nb}
and
\sigma(pp\rightarrow Z)=60.18\mathrm{\ nb}

[
66
]
, which for the luminosity of the pile-up dataset, and after considering the acceptance of the analysis selections in the relevant mass region, yields a total expected contribution of
3.9\times 10^{4}
events. While the SM
W
and
Z
bosons are not observed in this result, the expected exclusion limit of
1.8\times\sigma_{\mathrm{SM}}^{W/Z}
is at a level that requires investigating these processes.

It is possible that hadronic
W
and
Z
boson decays are reconstructed at different masses than the values assumed in the
W/Z
template. Both theoretical and experimental effects can shift the peak position: for example, interference between the QCD multijet background and
W/Z
production process can lead to few-GeV effects
[
67
,
68
,
54
,
69
]
, and various jet-related reconstruction effects can also shift the mass peak.
Due to the very large width of the template, such shifts in the peak position are not expected to have a significant impact on the result.
In order to confirm this, a fit using an alternative
W/Z
template obtained by shifting the mean of both Gaussians down by 7% is performed to investigate the impact of a possible downward shift of the reconstructed peak positions; this is done following the results seen using
Z^{\prime}
simulated samples as mentioned in Section
11.1
. The resulting limits for the shifted template are found to be compatible with the nominal results. This cross-check also covers the impact of interference effects between the QCD multi-jet background and
W/Z
production, which are expected to result in a downward shift of a few GeV.

The lack of evidence for the presence of the
W/Z
mass peak results in a poorly constrained
W/Z
template, which when combined with a BSM signal shape leads to fit instability.
The
W/Z
template is therefore not included in the background fit when conducting BSM signal interpretations.

15
Conclusion

This paper presents a search for low-mass dijet resonances in a novel dataset comprising pile-up collisions, obtained using single-lepton triggers and corresponding to 1.30 pb
-1
of data recorded by the ATLAS detector at the LHC.
The data do not show significant excesses over the Standard Model background prediction.
Two models are used in order to extract 95%
\mathrm{CL}
limits on new physics phenomena.
For simplified dark matter
Z^{\prime}
models, masses between 135 and 185 GeV are excluded for quark couplings
g_{q}=0.6
, and the lowest excluded coupling is
g_{q}=0.28
at a
Z^{\prime}
mass of around 150 GeV.

For signal scenarios that produce Gaussian-like bumps in the dijet mass spectrum, upper limits on
\sigma\times A\times\mathrm{B}
range between 1.5 nb and 15 nb, depending on the mass, for resonances with a 10% relative width, and increasing with the width.
Even though Standard Model resonances produced by hadronic decays of
W
and
Z
bosons are within the mass range explored, they are not observed. Upper limits at 95% CL are derived on their yield, and the achieved sensitivity lies near the threshold required for their detection.
This analysis complements the ATLAS low-mass search programme by providing an inclusive search with full object reconstruction and without assumptions on the presence of initial-state radiation.
