 ABSTRACT
 Griffin is a Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) based reactor multiphysics
analysis application, jointly developed by Argonne and Idaho National Laboratories under the DOE-NE
NEAMS program. This fiscal year, capabilities for reactivity and sensitivity evaluation using perturbation
methods were implemented and verified. The First Order Perturbation Method (FOPT) was employed to
compute reactivity worth resulting from small perturbations in input parameters, while the Generalized Per-
turbation Theory (GPT) was used to evaluate sensitivities of a range of response types, including reaction
rate ratio, k-eigenvalue, neutron generation time, and effective delayed neutron fraction. These pertur-
bation methods enable users to quantify how response quantities change due to a perturbation in a input
parameter without explicitly performing an additional transport simulation for each perturbed state. In par-
ticular, the GPT formulation accounts for indirect effects arising from flux changes by solving generalized
inhomogeneous equations, for which a Neumann series-based iterative solution method was developed and
implemented in Griffin. The implemented reactivity and sensitivity evaluation capabilities were verified us-
ing two test problems: an infinite homogeneous system and a two-dimensional hexagonal core. The results
showed excellent agreement with reference solutions obtained by a direct method based on finite difference
approximation as well as GPT-based results from the PERSENT code, confirming the accuracy of both re-
activity and sensitivity evaluations. Additionally, preliminary uncertainty quantification (UQ) results were
obtained by combining the sensitivity values computed using GPT and external covariance data, demon-
strating that the implemented sensitivity results can be reliably used for uncertainty calculations. To further
demonstrate the generality and practical strength of the implementation, the sensitivity evaluation capability
was successfully applied to the Empire microreactor with a geometrically complex design that poses signif-
icant modeling challenges. The results confirm that Griffin enables sensitivity evaluations even for irregular
and highly heterogeneous reactor configurations, thereby establishing a foundation for UQ applications in
advanced reactor designs and analyses.

 1. Introduction

 Accurate evaluation of how response quantities vary with changes in input parameters is essential for

ensuring reactor safety and supporting various operational assessments, as this process quantifies how un-

certainties in model inputs including nuclear cross section data propagate throughout the system. The most

straightforward way to calculate changes in a response to a perturbation is the direct method, which re-

quires additional transport simulations for each perturbed state for comparison to the unperturbed solution.

However, the direct approach becomes significantly impractical when assessing responses with respect to

multiple input parameters due to the large number of required simulations. To overcome these limita-

tions, adjoint-based perturbation methods, such as First-Order Perturbation Theory (FOPT) and Generalized

Perturbation Theory (GPT) [3, 4], have been widely adopted and implemented in several codes including

PERSENT [1]. These approaches eliminate the need for additional transport solutions at perturbed states.

Instead, they relate changes in input parameters to changes in the response quantities using only the trans-

port solutions at the base (unperturbed) state. In particular, GPT enables efficient sensitivity evaluations for

a range of responses beyond reactivity by accounting for both direct effects and indirect effects. The indirect

effects arising from flux changes are captured by solving generalized inhomogeneous equations.

 The objective of this study is to implement FOPT-based reactivity worth calculation and GPT-based sen-

sitivity calculation capabilities within Griffin [5], enabling users to perform these analyses deterministically

within a high-fidelity framework and eventually quantify uncertainties across various reactor types. Griffin

is a MOOSE-based deterministic reactor physics code developed jointly by Argonne and Idaho National

Laboratories under the DOE-NE Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program.

It is applicable to a wide range of advanced reactor types, including pebble- or prismatic-type high tem-

perature reactors, molten salt reactors, fast reactors, and a variety of microreactors. Unlike many other

conventional deterministic codes that rely on structured meshes and are limited in handling irregular or

highly heterogeneous configurations, Griffin supports unstructured mesh to address modeling challenges,

making it readily applicable for analyzing complex designs. As a result, developing reactivity, sensitiv-

ity, and uncertainty evaluation capabilities within Griffin provides a robust and extensible framework for

performing these analyses across diverse reactor types.

 One of the most significant contributions of this work, therefore, is that it extends the applicability of

practical deterministic sensitivity and uncertainty evaluations to general reactor types and geometries, in-

 1

cluding highly heterogeneous and irregular configurations, whereas such evaluations for reactor cores with

complex geometry have traditionally been performed using Monte Carlo methods. In addition to implement-

ing and verifying FOPT and GPT capabilities, this work also presents preliminary uncertainty quantification

(UQ) results based on computed sensitivity vectors, and demonstrates the applicability of the sensitivity

evaluation capability to the Empire microreactor [6], a compact system with significant geometrical com-

plexity. These results highlight not only the accuracy and robustness of the implementation but also its

practical strength and generality for advanced reactor designs and analyses.

 Previous research [7] demonstrated GPT-based sensitivity evaluation within Griffin under the MOOSE

framework [8], using a Preconditioned Jacobian-Free Newton Krylov (PJFNK) solver with PETSc libraries

[9]. However, that work is only implemented with the diffusion approximation. In contrast, this work intro-

duces a new numerical solution scheme for SN (discrete ordinate) transport equations, using the Neumann

series method to solve the generalized inhomogeneous equations. Furthermore, the scope of responses is ex-

tended beyond k-eigenvalue and reaction rate ratio to include neutron generation time, and effective delayed

neutron fraction, etc.

 3. Reactivity Evaluation Based on First Order Perturbation Theory

 The capability to evaluate reactivity change induced by perturbations introduced within the core is cru-

cial for both nuclear reactor safety analysis and reactor design optimization. To enable this, a first order

perturbation theory (FOPT) capability has been implemented in Griffin to compute reactivity worth effi-

ciently without requiring a separate transport calculation for the perturbed state.

 In the current implementation, three types of perturbations are supported: macroscopic cross sections,

microscopic cross sections, and isotopic number densities. Regardless of the perturbation type, these three

cases ultimately lead to changes in macroscopic cross sections of relevant reaction types, which are then

reflected into changes in the transport operators. For example, perturbations to microscopic cross sections or

number densities are processed by reconstructing the corresponding perturbed macroscopic cross sections.

Indirect perturbations, such as the microscopic cross section changes arising from temperature changes, are

not yet included in this capability.

 For a given base macroscopic cross section Σ, microscopic cross section σ , and isotopic number density

N, the perturbed macroscopic cross section for a specific reaction type and isotope is defined as follows

depending on the perturbation type and the given corresponding multiplicative factor fα :

 • Macroscopic cross-section perturbations: Σ̂ = f Σ · Σ

 • Microscopic cross-section perturbations: Σ̂ = N · σ̂ = N · ( fσ · σ )

 • Isotopic number density perturbations: Σ̂ = N̂ · σ = ( fN · N ) · σ

Once the perturbed macroscopic cross sections are defined, they are directly used to compute the changes in

operators that appear in the transport equation.

 5. Preliminary Works on Uncertainty Quantification

 This section describes a preliminary work on uncertainty quantification (UQ), which quantifies the un-

certainties in a response of interest arising from uncertainties in nuclear data, specifically microscopic cross

sections in this work. As a primary application of the sensitivity capability is UQ, we use sensitivities

computed by Griffin using its GPT implementation to evaluate these uncertainties.

 Uncertainty calculation is currently performed offline with a prototype MATLAB post-processing script

as a preliminary measure to confirm the validity of the workflow, but this capability will be fully integrated

into Griffin, including parsing of covariance data, automatic alignment of reaction and isotope keys between

given sensitivity vector and covariance data, and reporting.

 The uncertainty calculation follows the standard first-order formulation called the sandwich rule:

 √
                                                u=    sT · D · s,                                          (69)

where s is the sensitivity vector that includes sensitivities of a target response with respect to microscopic

cross sections specified with isotope, reaction type, and energy group, and D is an external covariance

matrix given by the user. For this study, the COMMARA-2.0 covariance library [10], which is based on

 21

the ENDF/B-VII.0 library, is used. It provides cross-section uncertainties and correlation matrices between

different cross sections based on the 33-group energy structure.

 The uncertainty calculation procedure is straightforward given the sensitivities from Griffin and the

COMMARA covariance matrix. First, identify the cross sections for which sensitivities are available, then

extract and load the corresponding COMMARA covariance matrices. Subsequently, compute the response

uncertainty using Eq. (69) with breakdown by isotope, reaction type, and energy group.

 Results are provided as 1) a breakdown by isotope, reaction type, and energy group, 2) a breakdown

by isotope and reaction type, and 3) a total uncertainty. Breakdowns are computed and shown only for iso-

topes and reaction types that are present in the sensitivity vector and defined consistently in the covariance

data. For instance, COMMARA provides separate covariance for elastic and inelastic scattering, whereas

Griffin does not split scattering into these sub-reactions. Therefore, the uncertainties by scattering are not

evaluated. Likewise, Griffin treats capture via its sub-reactions such as (n, γ), (n, α), (n, p), and so on,

rather than explicitly defining a single total capture, while COMMARA provides covariance data for the

total capture. Therefore, exact capture breakdown are limited unless total capture is determined only by one

sub-reaction. These are definitional differences rather than issues in numerical accuracy of UQ implemen-

tation. Accordingly, in the verification studies comparing against PERSENT reference solutions in Section

6.4, we focus on the common subset of isotopes and reactions, which are consistently represented in both

codes and covariance data. This is the initial step of UQ efforts. Future work will include full integration

of the UQ capability into Griffin, extension coverage to additional covariance sources and responses, and

comprehensive verifications.

 7. Application to Empire Microreactor Benchmark

 One of the significant contributions of this work is that it enables sensitivity analyses and uncertainty

quantification without being limited by reactor type or geometrical complexity, as long as the target problem

can be modeled for Griffin calculation. Griffin is designed to handle unstructured meshes and multiple

spatial discretizations, which allows the sensitivity evaluation capability to be applied to various reactor

configurations.

 To demonstrate this generality of the sensitivity calculation capability, it was applied to the 2D Empire

microreactor core [6]. This model was selected because its geometry is significantly complex, including

explicit representation of control drums. These features make it difficult to analyze with traditional struc-

tured deterministic core analysis codes, which typically rely on regular geometry and simplified spatial

meshing. As a result, sensitivity and uncertainty calculations for such problems are mostly performed using

Monte Carlo codes. Successfully applying the deterministic sensitivity evaluation capability to this reactor

therefore highlights both its robustness and practical strength.

 The radial configuration of the Empire core used in this work is illustrated in Figure 21. The detailed

description of the core geometry can be found in the references [6, 13]. Since the objective of this study

is to verify the applicability of the sensitivity evaluation capability rather than to produce high fidelity

transport solutions, we simplified the material compositions. The model includes 21 isotopes and uses 6-

group microscopic cross sections, yielding the eigenvalue of 1.35209. The upper energy boundaries of each

group are provided in Table 35.

dominant. For U-238 capture, negative sensitivities are confirmed, with the peak located in group 4 (4 eV -

spatial sensitivity distributions in group 4 for U-235 fission and U-238 capture cross sections. Sensitivities

are evaluated at each element in fuel where those isotopes are present. These distributions demonstrate that

the sensitivity magnitude is highest near the interior of the core, where neutron fluxes are concentrated in

that group. These results are physically consistent and align with the expectations based on neutron transport

behavior. It confirms that the sensitivity evaluation capability implemented in Griffin through this work is

not only physically correct but also applicable to advanced reactor systems with complex geometry, assuring

its practical value.

 8. Conclusions and Future Work

 This work implemented both FOPT-based reactivity evalaution and GPT-based sensitivity evaluation

capabilities in Griffin. The FOPT method enables efficient estimation of reactivity worth due to small per-

turbations in macroscopic cross sections, without requiring explicit perturbed transport calculations. The

GPT framework further extends this capability to evaluate sensitivity coefficients for a range of response

types, including reaction rate ratio, k-eigenvalue, neutron generation time, and effective delayed neutron

fraction. This method efficiently captures indirect effects induced by variations in fluxes, through gener-

alized functions, for which a Neumann series-based iterative solution method was newly developed and

implemented in Griffin.

 The FOPT and GPT capabilities in Griffin were verified using two sets of test problems: an infinite

homogeneous problem and a 2D hexagonal core problem. The computed reactivity worth and sensitivity

results showed excellent agreement with both finite difference approximations and the FOPT and GPT

results obtained with PERSENT. In particular, spatial distributions of sensitivities confirmed that the direct

effect dominates in the response region, while minor indirect contributions are correctly captured outside

the response region due to global flux changes. These results demonstrate the accuracy and robustness of

the perturbation and sensitivity implementations in Griffin, establishing a solid basis for extending their use

to future UQ applications.

 In addition, this work presents preliminary UQ results based on sensitivity vectors computed using GPT

in Griffin. These results confirm that the implemented sensitivities can be reliably used to propagate un-

certainties in nuclear data to those in response parameters. Furthermore, the applicability and generality of

the sensitivity evaluation capability were demonstrated through its application to the Empire microreactor

model. Despite its geometrical complexity, which poses challenges to conventional deterministic codes,

Griffin successfully evaluated group-wise and spatial sensitivities as well as the global value in a determin-

istic manner, highlighting Griffin’s practical strength in performing sensitivity analyses and enabling their

use in UQ for advanced reactor analyses.

 Future work will focus on extending and improving the implemented capabilities in the following as-

pects:

 • Enhancement of performance by enabling the diffusion acceleration for solving singular generalized

 equations with Richardson iteration.

 50

• Extension of support to additional discretization schemes, including HFEM-PN and CFEM-Diffusion,

  beyond the current DFEM-SN scheme.

• Extension of the generalized function solution capability from scalar-flux- to angular-flux-based form,

  enabling more rigorous and accurate evaluation of indirect sensitivity effects through angle-dependent

  generalized functions.

• Support for additional reaction types in sensitivity evaluations, including scattering cross sections and

  fission spectra.

• Extension of support to additional Griffin material types that utilize microscopic cross section data,

  such as SelfShieldingNeutronicsMaterial (online cross section generation), beyond

  MicroNeutronicsMaterial, on which this work is currently based.

• Full integration of the UQ capability into Griffin, enabling automated evaluation using either online-

  computed sensitivity vectors or user-specified sensitivity data, along with input covariance data.

• Comprehensive verification and application of UQ, including extended response types and a broader

  set of test problems.

• Extension of reactivity and sensitivity evaluations to indirect perturbations, such as those induced by

  temperature changes affecting cross sections.

• Applications of the implemented capabilities to various reactor types and configurations.
