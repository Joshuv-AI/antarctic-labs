1 Introduction
1.1 Project Background

The Los Alamos Neutron Science Center (LANSCE) is located at Technical Area 53 (TA-53) at Los
Alamos National Laboratory in Los Alamos, New Mexico. LANSCE is driven by an 800 mega electron
volt (MeV) proton accelerator that delivered first beam in 1972. The LANSCE accelerator is unique in that
it accelerates both H- (to full energy of 800 MeV) and H+ ions (up to 100 MeV currently but has accelerated
high-power H+ beam to 800 MeV in the past) and supports five separate experimental areas that operate
simultaneously with each having different timing and beam current requirements. Many of the LANSCE
accelerator Front End components date back to its original commissioning in 1972, including the ion
sources, Cockcroft-Walton (CW) generators, and the Drift Tube LINAC (DTL) which accelerates the beam
up to 100 MeV.

The purpose of the LANSCE Accelerator Modernization Project (LAMP) is to replace and modernize
multiple accelerator systems and components to improve beam delivery, reliability and availability. LAMP
modernization will improve system maintainability and prepare the facility for operation through 2050. The
scope of modernization currently includes the LANSCE Front End (up to 100 MeV). This scope includes
modernizing the two ion sources, replacing the two CW generators with a modern Radio Frequency
Quadrupole (RFQ), and replacing the DTL which is exhibiting severe degradation and has significantly
exceeded its expected lifespan. Modernization of accelerator systems beyond the 100 MeV beam energy
are not within the currently approved LAMP scope; however, end-to-end modeling through the entire
accelerator including the Proton Storage Ring (PSR) will need to be performed to verify the project’s
performance requirements for all five experimental areas.

LAMP will be executed as a congressional line-item project under DOE Order 413.3b [1] for the National
Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA). The design will follow the Program Requirements Document
(PRD) for LAMP [2] and other requirements that flow down from the PRD.

1.2 Document Scope

LAMP is using the requirement management software Visure [3], in which approved requirements are
placed under configuration control within the database. The purpose of this system-requirements database
is to maintain a central repository of requirements, organize the requirements, and identify a minimum set
of key requirements for designing, constructing, and fielding of the LAMP system. More details about
requirements management are available in LAMP-SE-PLAN-001; Systems Engineering Management Plan
(SEMP) [4].
LAMP Level 0 and Level 1 requirements have been reviewed and approved in Visure database. To allow
the stakeholders to review these requirements without needing access to the database, LAMP Level 0 and
Level 1 requirements were exported from Visure and presented in this document.

2 Requirements Overview
2.1 Requirements Hierarchy

Requirements for the Project are organized into three levels. Figure 1 illustrates the requirements hierarchy
for the Project, with explanations provided as follows. Note that the subsystems in Figure 1 are shown for
illustrative purposes only, and that the final system architecture may differ. Present document has LAMP
Level 0 and Level 1 requirements exported from requirements management database in Visure. Level 2
requirements are under development.

Level 0 Requirements
The highest, Level 0, includes requirements found in the NNSA’s PRD for LAMP [2].
Level 0 requirements are ‘parents’ to Level 1 requirements, i.e. Level 0 requirements flow down (or, are
decomposed) into Level 1 requirements.

Level 1 Requirements
The next category of requirements, Level 1, are the Accelerator Physics Requirements, which flow down
from the Level 0 Requirements.
These physics requirements typically specify the performance requirements for each operational mode of a
major system, such as beam current, energy, timing structure, etc. Level 1 requirements specify how a
system needs to perform and what criteria the system needs to meet to satisfy the parent Level 0
requirements. The document provides physics specifications that are generally used as a starting point in
engineering design.
Level 1 requirements are ‘children’ of Level 0 requirements, and in general, are ‘parents’ to Level 2
requirements.

Level 2 Requirements
The functional and design requirements for the major subsystems of the Front End are collected at Level 2,
along with the Interface Control Documents (ICDs). Level 2 requirements describe the required behavior
of a subsystem or component, and what is needed by the end user. These requirements outline the
constraints, assumptions, technical requirements, and requested properties of inputs and outputs.
Requirements at levels 2 (and 3, if needed) include (a) requirements directly derived from higher levels (i.e.
from their parent requirements) and (b) design-specific requirements that must be captured to ensure the
higher-level requirements can be met.

Level 2 Requirements also incorporate requirements derived from Operational Concepts for the Front End
system and its subsystems, as well as requirements derived from or informed by other documents or parts
of the Project such as Removal and Installation (Figure 1, bottom right).

Interface Control Documents (ICDs) describe how two (or more, as needed) adjacent systems connect
physically, electronically, electrically, and functionally. ICDs are used to describe the boundaries or
endpoints of one specific system with respect to another system, the physical interface between the two,
and the limits of responsibilities for the two systems. The ICDs are implemented in Level 2 Requirements
(and Level 3 if needed), as shown in Figure 1, bottom left.

2.2 Requirements Identification Process

The goal of the requirements identification process is to provide authoritative documented criteria to the
Project team, engineers, and designers for use during design, fabrication/procurement, logistics planning,
installation, and commissioning. Requirements at each hierarchical level define the acceptable limits of
performance and/or functional capabilities of the corresponding system.

LAMP team is using LAMP-PM-FORM-002; Requirements Input Form [5], to gather input for drafting
requirements. Once the information in the form is reviewed by the requirements owner and stakeholders,
SEM adds the requirement to requirements management database in Visure, for further review and
approval.

Requirements must be defined such that they are clear and unambiguous, and actionable in the design
process. The following requirement characteristics will ensure ease of communication and successful
conformance:

 • Achievable - the requirement must be technically achievable given the present state of the
 art, specified schedule, and allocated budget.

 • Verifiable - the requirement or specification must be defined in a way that can be readily
 verified by objective measurement, test, or analysis.

 • Clear - the requirement or specification must be unambiguous, have one and only one
 obvious interpretation, and met solely by the system for which the requirement is written.

 • Complete - the set of requirements and specifications must define all information necessary
 to design a system that performs in compliance with Project functional needs and interfaces with
 connected systems readily.

 • Consistent - any given requirement must not conflict with any other requirement.

Aspects of a Requirement

The following aspects are used in the process of defining a requirement. Each action must be completed
to fully define a requirement, although they need not be carried out in the order they appear here.:

 • Identify - describe the requirement and check that it is achievable, clear, complete, and
 consistent.

 • Flow down - determine where in the hierarchy a requirement belongs (generically, system,
 subsystem, component); see Figure 1 for reference.

 • Establish verification - describe the method and procedure by which the requirement will
 be verified. This verification process may include but is not limited to measurement,
 analysis, third-party certification, functional demonstration, inspection, similarity, testing,
 or some combination of these.

 • Validate - check that the requirement has been correctly derived and all characteristics are
 evident.

 • Traceability - identify the ‘parent’ of the requirement at the next higher level. The result of
 this identification process is a well-formed requirement that is clearly defined, which can
 then be used to justify the rationale for the cost and risk associated with the design that is
 developed to satisfy the requirement.

2.3 Requirements Description

Project requirements are documented and managed using the LAMP requirements management software
database. Each requirement entry includes several components (known as ‘attributes’ in Visure database),
listed below.

 1. Code: Requirement identification number (ID), such as LAMP_L0.xx for Level 0
 requirements and LAMP_L1.xx for Level 1 requirements.
 2. Name: Short name for a requirement.
 3. Description: Text of the specified requirement.
 1. All requirements use a SHALL statement.
 2. Guidance statements, which are not obligatory but may be listed together with the
 requirements, use a SHOULD statement.
 3. Certain statements and values listed in the requirements may have a TBR (To Be
 Reviewed) or TBD (To Be Determined) designation. This is not unusual during the
 conceptual design stage, however all TBRs and TBDs need to be resolved at later
 design stages.
 4. Parents and Children (where <- denotes parent requirements, and -> denotes children
 requirements): defines requirements traceability, i.e. flow-down from parents (higher-level
 requirements such as level 0) to children (lower-level requirements such as level 1 and level
 2).
 5. Requirement Type
 1. Type can be one of the following: Functional, Operations, Performance, Codes and
 Standards, Safety, or Interface. See Table 1 for more details.
 6. Rationale for the requirement: Justification explaining why a requirement is needed, with
 additional information to help understand the requirement, source of any numerical values
 stated in the requirement, etc.
 7. Assumptions (such as scope limitations, constraints, environmental conditions, available
 capacity for power or cooling, etc.)
 8. Requirement Verification Method (see Section 0 for more details)
 1. Each requirement specified therein shall be verified with one or more of the following
 verification methods: Analysis, Certification, Demonstration, Inspection, Similarity,
 and/or Test
 9. Requirement Verification Method Details
 10. Requirement Owner (name). Owner should also determine the primary WBS (work
 breakdown structure) where specific requirement resides.
 11. Requirement Owner's role

2.4 Requirements Verification and Validation

The Systems Engineering process [4] shall ensure that the system meets the scientific, functional, and
performance needs by verifying the design and implementation against the requirements. During the
requirements definition process, the method of verification for each requirement shall be identified. Each
requirement and associated verification method shall be assigned an owner prior to or during the
Requirements Review. Methods used for verification may include Analysis, Certification, Demonstration,
Inspection, Similarity, and/or Test. Each requirement specified herein shall be verified with one or more
of the following verification methods:

Analysis
The verification of a requirement using models, simulations, calculations, and testing equipment. Analysis
allows one to make predictive statements about the typical performance of a product or system based on
the confirmed analysis results of a sample set or, by combining the outcome of individual analyses, to
conclude something new about the product or system.

Certification
Certification is applicable when there is a known standard or specification that is either nationally or
internationally recognized that the product or system has been stated to meet by a certifying body.

Demonstration
The manipulation of a product or system, as designed, to verify that the results are as planned or expected.
Typically, the verifications demonstrated are based on direct, in-person observation of the activity
conducted by the Design Authority or customer during an equipment or process walkdown and the results
are generally documented. Examples of demonstration-based verification may include activities such as
verifying the range of an installed gage, exercising physical safety interlocks, or checking calibration labels.

Inspection

The nondestructive examination of a product or system, using one or more of the five senses, and
documentation of the results.

Similarity

If a product or service is close enough in design to a previously designed product or service, i.e., a heritage
design, the current product or service is verified by previous requirements.

Test

The verification of a product or system using a controlled and predefined series of inputs, data, or stimuli
to ensure the product or system will produce a very specific and predefined output, as defined by
requirements.
Verification by test method includes exercising equipment or processes to a test plan or approved procedure,
with a formal report generated after the activity. The testing itself may or may not be directly observed by
the Design Authority or customer, but the test report is submitted as objective evidence supporting
validation of the requirement. Examples of test-based verification may include thermal, shock, vibration,
pressure, or load limit testing; chemical composition testing; or metrology reports for calibrated equipment.

2.5 Requirements Verification Products

Verification products are the deliverables of the verification efforts and are listed as follows.

The Verification Cross Reference Matrix (VCRM) ensures that every requirement has an assigned
verification method, and recorded proof that the requirement is satisfied. VCRM is developed based on the
requirements approved by the Project leadership team. For LAMP, the VCRM is built into the requirements
management database in Visure. The Systems Engineering Manager (SEM) develops and maintains a
database (currently in Visure) identifying the method of verification (analysis, certification, demonstration,
inspection, similarity, testing, or some combination of these) for each requirement.
Level 0 and Level 1 requirements, exported from Visure database and shown in Table 2 and Table 3, contain
verification method and verification details for each requirement, hence the VCRM information is
embedded in these tables. Verification details can reference other documents such as verification plans and
verification reports for individual requirements, or items such as drawings, line diagrams, CAD models,
interface control documents, etc. The VCRM (embedded in the requirements database) will be used to track
the requirements verification efforts, including verification artifacts and requirements owner/responsible
technical leads. The VCRM may be combined with another document, such as the Verification Plan(s), or
stand alone.

All requirements will have traceability up to the highest requirement level, i.e. to Level 0. Table 2 and
Table 3 have the traceability info in the Parents and Children column. Later in this document, we include a
separate traceability matrix for Level 0 and Level 1 requirements, in Table 4. Since Level 2 requirements
are still under development, we opted not to show traceability from Level 1 to Level 2 in the present
document.

The Verification Plan and Verification Report shall be detailed documents providing the final
verification method to prove the substantiation of the associated requirement. During the conceptual and
preliminary design phase, the owner of each requirement is responsible for generating the verification plan
for their assigned requirements. During and following the final design phase, the owner of each requirement
is responsible for generating the Verification Report for their assigned requirements. For the Project, the
system technical leads are the default requirements owners, but they may delegate responsibility for specific
requirements to other Project team members who are supporting the related systems. The VP may be
combined with another document, such as the VCRM, or stand alone. Verification details field in the
database may be sufficient in early design stages, but later in the design development, verification details
need to point to specific documents such as plans and reports.
Final Product Validation: During final requirements validation the Project will confirm that the final
product fulfills its intended use when operated in its intended environment. The Project will conduct final
product validation with any qualification or acceptance testing leading into commissioning and validation
of the Key Performance Parameters (KPPs) before Transition to Operations (TTO). Higher level
(sub)system qualification evidence may be used to demonstrate that KPPs have been met, or additional
activities may be conducted to collect objective proof of verification for the KPPs.
