 Executive summary

 The STARFIRE hub leverages foundational science and technology (S&T) research and
integrated plant modeling tools to advance Inertial Fusion Energy (IFE). S&T breakthroughs achieved
in each thrust are incorporated into an integrated modeling tool to assess and prioritize remaining
gaps to bridge for IFE to become viable. Scientific highlights span high-gain target design, diode-
pumped solid-state lasers, and innovative target manufacturing. Key highlights include the
investigation of shock propagation through various foam structures among other limiting factors of
compression of IFE target, exploration of novel 3D printing techniques such as light-sheet additive
manufacturing for 2-photon polymerization foams, and diode-pumped solid-state laser
architectures required for several IFE approaches. In addition, the STARFIRE team has established
two public-private working groups to address laser diode supply chain issues and test advanced
laser diode configurations. Finally, a lite version of the integrated modeling tool has been developed
to facilitate outreach and engage with private companies. STARFIRE’s management structure has
been designed to ensure agile resource allocation, robust performance assessment, and alignment
with the broader fusion energy mission. STARFIRE’s breakthroughs are disseminated to the
community via reports, presentations, and publications, while agreements with private companies
are set in place to protect proprietary information.

Introduction
 The Science & Technology Accelerated Research for Fusion Innovation & Reactor Engineering
(“STARFIRE”) Hub aims to tackle foundational Science and Technology (S&T) challenges applicable
to the entire Inertial Fusion Energy (IFE) community, as described in the basic research need report
[Ma, 2022]. STARFIRE relies on constant back-and-forth between research to advance core challenge
areas and the validation of an integrated plant modeling tool. The integrated plant modeling tool
evaluates the significance of the advances made in each technical area while also serving as a guide
to direct and prioritize the foundational research needed for Inertial Fusion Energy.
 The organization of our hub reflects this intent; it leverages its members’ expertise in
four S&T thrust area, and one workforce development thrust area listed below:
I. High-gain plant-compatible target designs
II. Diode-Pumped Solid-state Lasers (DPSSL) and optics for IFE
III. Target Manufacturing and Engagement
IV. IFE plant design and assessment framework
V. Workforce development and IFE ecosystem stewardship
 The first three thrust areas focus on advancing in technologies required to go beyond the
ignition results obtained on the National Ignition Facility and produce energy. Namely, target designs
need to be assessed for expected gain, by using gain projections anchored in experimental results,
and ignition robustness using reduced order models that can be incorporated in Artificial Intelligence
and Machine learning models. In parallel, DPSSL architecture designs for IFE drivers and a road
mapping effort in collaboration with industry to address supply chain issues with diode
manufacturing are conducted.
 Advances made in the first three areas are incorporated in an integrated plant model, which
constitutes the goal of the fourth thrust. Such a model is essential to our effort. It provides a techno-
economic analysis of a power plant, allowing for comparison between different technologies and to
identifying key gaps in S&T. The assessment can only be useful if the tool is validated and versatile
enough to handle many technologies. Therefore, the incorporation of as many approaches and
technologies as possible and the thorough benchmarking against existing designs is the main priority
of the thrust.
 Finally, the STARFIRE hub is helping develop the workforce of the future for IFE through
partnerships with leading universities and innovative new curriculum development and
implementation.

Scientific Achievements
I. High-gain plant-compatible target designs
This research thrust focuses on resolving the remaining target physics issues related to high energy
gains, developing energy scaling laws and exploring high-gain target designs for IFE. Two IFE target
physics topics are explored in depth: limitations in implosion convergence/compression and the
hydrodynamics of wetted foam targets. High-gain target designs include wetted foam IFE targets and
low adiabat implosions with laser imprint mitigation for direct drive.

II. Diode-Pumped Solid-state Lasers and optics for IFE
We list below the highlights for this thrust with respects to the three main goals.

 Towards our goal of developing inertial fusion energy (IFE) laser architecture concepts
based on near-term available technology, we have made significant progress on several fronts.
First, we completed the definition of driver requirements, documenting these results in a
comprehensive report that synthesizes input from 20 leading laser and laser-fusion experts. These
findings are actively informing updates to our driver designs and are being prepared for a future
technical publication. In parallel, we developed and evaluated IFE laser concepts using laser
energetics and optical ray tracing simulations. Our analyses indicate that the DOE BRN target of
multi-kilojoule (up to 10 kJ), 10 Hz, 10% efficiency operation is achievable; however, the cost-
effectiveness as a function of output energy and efficiency remains under investigation. To address
this, we have focused our scope and coordinated closely with the IPM efforts in Thrust 4. This work
has resulted in four conference presentations, with additional technical papers in preparation.
Additionally, we initiated a collaborative effort between LLNL and Fraunhofer ILT to cross-validate
laser simulation codes, formalized through a CRADA finalized in July 2025. Initial results, using
simplified physics models, show excellent agreement between the codes.
 Towards our goal of assessing the robustness of neutron-irradiated final optics to optical
damage, we have completed the fabrication, pre-irradiation characterization, and irradiation of test
optics. The optics were irradiated at LBNL with neutron doses ranging from 8 x 10^7 n/cm² to 5.5 x
10^12 n/cm² and neutron energies between 10 and 40 MeV, consistent with the conditions
established during LLNL’s 2011-3 LIFE project. The irradiated samples are expected to be safe to
handle and ready for post-irradiation characterization in November or December 2025.
 Towards our goal of addressing cost and capacity constraints on laser diode (LD) pumps,
we have made several key advancements. We completed a first spiral assessment and Pareto
analysis of LD costs, representing consensus among Leonardo, TRUMPF, and LLNL, which led to
specific R&D recommendations for near-term cost reduction in a proposal to DOE’s FIRE call. To
further inform our cost and capacity road mapping, we are developing a ‘factory model’ for our next
spiral, which will be presented at Photonics West (PW) in January 2026. Additionally, we formed a
laser diode technology working group with broad industry participation—seven institutions—to
address critical LD technology topics. Since its inception in July 2025, the group has focused on

III. Target Manufacturing and Engagement
 Two-photon polymerization (2PP) additive manufacturing (AM) systems can produce high-
quality by plotting individual points with a focused ultrafast laser.
However, that approach is not suitable for the high target consumption
rates of IFE. Instead, 2PP printing systems utilizing micro devices to
print thousands of points simultaneously are the leading candidate to
address this challenge. Large-scale printing has been demonstrated at
a system at UC Santa Barbara; a 5 mm scale block of log stack foam
(see Figure 4) was printed with a Large Field-of-View (LFOV)
microscope objective.

designs of 2PP systems in the literature. Zemax optical analysis software was used to assess the

 The team at LLNL is developing a lightsheet-based Volumetric Additive Manufacturing
(Lightsheet VAM) approach for target fabrication. We have been improving the printing resolution by
better optical design (using a thinner, Bessel beam lightsheet) and optimized resin formulation. The
first version of Lightsheet VAM achieved a 5 µm-thick lightsheet over a 1 cm2 area using Bessel beam
scanning. The second-generation printing system features two wavelengths coming from the same
side of the cuvette. This modification will provide easier diagnostic capabilities and better scalability.
On the photochemistry front, the team developed an automated two-wavelength screening system
that investigates laser power & dosage range for single wavelength and two wavelength curing
pathways. We built a digital twin model of the printing process to capture the reaction-diffusion
kinetics during two-wavelength photopolymerization, which is validated by our resin screening
results. We currently focus on using the digital twin model to enable grayscale correction during
printing to increase part fidelity and inform further resin formulation optimization.
 The UCLA team has focused on various topological designs of foam for IFE targets. This
includes strutted simple cubic (SC), body-centered cubic (BCC), face-centered cubic (FCC), and
gradient heterostructured designs (wherein the local foam density varied from a relative density of
5% inside to 25% outside or vice versa). We applied fused deposition modeling (FDM) additive
manufacturing to print carbon foams according to our gradient design and conducted mechanical
 testing. The gradient foams exhibited strong stiffness variation,
 depending on the gradient direction. We worked closely with LLNL
 partners to improve materials design and fabrication speed for fusion
 targets made by the 2PP processes. A Ph.D. student (Jasmine Yang) is

IV. IFE plant design and assessment framework
 Significant improvements have been made in advancing integrated systems modeling for
inertial fusion energy (IFE) plant design. Central to these efforts is the Integrated Process Model
(IPM) tool. It consists of a simulation-guided framework developed over the past fifteen years as part
of LLNL’s work in IFE. This tool has been benchmarked against relevant experiments focused on a
DPSSL indirect-drive approach. Current and upcoming efforts and collaborations are aiming to
evolve the IPM into a comprehensive optimization tool across various IFE approaches. Incorporating
both technical and economic considerations helps to inform critical design decisions for risk
retirement prior to large-scale deployment and highlight key S&T gaps. By enabling detailed
optimization of drivers, targets, chambers, fuel cycles, and economic factors, the IPM supports the
development of robust, cost-effective, and scalable IFE plant designs. Systems modeling tools such
as the IPM will be essential in guiding future critical decisions, ultimately saving both time and
resources in the pursuit of the world’s first fusion power plants.
 Recent progress has centered on key advancements to the IPM. The model has been updated
with improved gain-scaling curves for various driver power to reflect recent National Ignition Facility

 Document Name 8

(NIF) ignition shots. In conjunction with Thrust 2, reduced-order laser modeling has been integrated,
leveraging results from LLNL’s high-fidelity AmpSim laser modeling software to enhance the
representation of driver performance. Originally developed in Excel, an initial port of the IPM into
Python has been completed, with the Python version serving as the primary platform for future work.
Additionally, the IPM was recently presented at the International Fusion Science and Applications
(IFSA) conference in September 2025, generating strong international collaborative interest.
 In response to the 2023 FESAC Facilities call, LLNL proposed LD-FIRST (Laser-Driven Fusion
Integration Research and Science Test Facility) as an initial reference architecture for IPM
development. LD-FIRST serves as a baseline “stress test” for the IPM, ensuring comprehensive
and self-consistent analysis across all critical subsystems. The initial LD-FIRST point design,
optimized using the IPM and complementary systems modeling techniques, features a DPSSL
helium gas-cooled driver, dry-wall chamber, indirect-drive targets, liquid lithium alloy blanket, and
aims to close the fuel and power cycles with target gain of 20. Moreover, the LD-FIRST platform
provides flexibility for introducing meter-scale samples into chamber for accelerated neutron
damage testing. Parallel and iterative development of the LD-FIRST point design has ensured a
comprehensive and self-consistent IPM framework.
 In parallel to the IPM development, a simplified tool, the Generalized Economics Model
(GEM) aiming to support outreach, education, and high-level collaboration within the fusion
community was developed. The GEM is currently being leveraged as part of academic outreach
events including IFE summer and winter schools through Thrust 5. The tool is scheduled for broad
release within a couple of months at the requests of private companies and academic collaborators.
 Looking forward, the modular, collaboration-focused Python IPM framework seen in figure 7,
will evolve to be AI-ready by integrating subsystem models using machine-learned surrogates,
such as chamber multi-physics and fuel cycle/tritium accountancy. Advanced optimization
techniques, such as inverse optimization under constraints are also considered. The model is being
broadened to consider a wide array of IFE options, including various driver technologies (excimer
lasers, DPSSLs, pulsed power), drive schemes (direct drive, fast ignition, indirect drive), chamber
configurations (solid wall, thick liquid walls, wetted walls), and operational regimes spanning single-
shot yield to high repetition rates. This expansion is driven by the increasing number of collaborators
with other IFE-STAR hubs, universities (through senior design projects) and private companies
providing the expertise necessary to develop the subsystems. Specific collaborations include
working with STARFIRE partner EX-Fusion and the IFE-STAR CoLOR Hub to build out a direct-drive
and fast-ignition capability in the IPM. A publication is planned for early 2026, detailing current and
future IPM capabilities. Continued refinement of LD-FIRST and other reference architectures will
ensure the model remains aligned with the latest technological developments. Finally, we
incorporate advanced AI-driven capabilities in the IPM with. Taken together, these efforts will
culminate in the delivery of a premier techno-economic systems modeling capability, supporting
DOE FES and its fusion energy mission.

Management
 The Hub operates with a management structure designed to balance technical expertise
and effective coordination. Leadership is provided by the hub lead and her deputy, who oversee
reporting, resource allocation, and strategic direction. Technical leadership is provided by each
thrust lead and organized around two pillars: fundamental research and integrated design.
 Strategic planning and adaptive management are informed by quarterly highlights submitted
to the deputy lead, as well as semi-annual full team meetings that bring together all members to
review progress, align on goals, and adjust priorities as needed. Regular meetings are held, with
additional ad-hoc discussions among technical experts to organize, conduct work and address
emerging issues. A good example of the built in collaboration across thrusts is the experimental
campaign at OMEGA studying shock propagation through foams. The experiment relies on target
manufactured by experts in Thrust 3 while the analysis is conducted by members of the design Thrust
1. This ensures target development and design are collaborating amongst STARFIRE.
There is even further integration withing the larger IFE ecosystem as the principal investigator of the
OMEGA campaign is part of an LDRD SI on advanced IFE concepts at LLNL synergistic with STARFIRE.
 The Hub maintains strong connections to the broader IFE program at LLNL via its
integration in the recently launched Livermore Institute for Fusion Technology and the wider

 11

research ecosystem, enabling efficient re-allocation of resources in response to evolving project
scopes. This adaptability has been successfully demonstrated by STARFIRE redirecting some of its
funding to print 2PP foams in support of the aforementioned LDRD SI. Added support from STARFIRE
helped General Atomics develop and use the 4π 2PP printing station to produce targets which is
synergistic with the Thrust 3 STARFIRE goals.

Collaboration with the private sector
 The STARFIRE hub’s work supports the private sector with both direct collaboration and the
dissemination of its results through publications and presentations at conferences. Some examples
of direct engagement with the private sector have been discussed in the science highlights and we
provide two detailed examples here.
 STARFIRE helped fund the LLNL hosted the HEC-DPSSL 2025 workshop on March 25th – 27th.
The workshop assembled many of the world’s most experienced institutes, research groups, and
companies involved in DPSSL system and component R&D. The meeting comprised over 75
members from 28 organizations and 7 countries who gathered to review technology scaling,
challenges, and prospects. The last day of the workshop was dedicated to a special session on HEC-
DPSSL Scaling for Inertial Fusion Energy with presentations from SC-FES, universities, national
laboratories, and private companies. This exchange helped inform public partners of expectations
and needs from private partners. It translated into Thrust 2 efforts, adding members to the laser diode
technology working group.
 STARFIRE contributed to the Mod/Sim ecosystem workshop that engaged with industry.
This project aims to develop a plan for a robust public-private collaboration ecosystem for Inertial

Fusion Energy (IFE) modeling and simulation to accelerate US leadership in fusion energy. Developed
through extensive stakeholder engagement with national laboratories, private IFE companies, AI
firms, and universities, the initiative responds to urgent industry needs for simulation capabilities

collaboration plan including governance models, secure data/code exchange mechanisms,
capability development priorities, data standards for interoperability, and detailed implementation
schedules. This foundation will support the dual goals of achieving first-to-market fusion energy on
the grid while building a sustainable domestic fusion industry for long-term energy security and
competitive advantage.
 These collaborations rely on the various mechanisms at LLNL’s disposal, from Cooperative
Research and Development Agreements (CRADA), Strategic Partnership Projects (SPP), Non-
Disclosure Agreements (NDA), and Memoranda of Understanding (MOU). The appropriate
mechanism for each partnership can be identified during the first discussion with the
companies. The cost/capacity working group within Thrust 2 provides a great example of how
STARFIRE handles proprietary information from private companies. Information shared among LEI,
LLNL and TRUMPF is treated as business proprietary. It is protected by treating it as Controlled
Unclassified Information and using the appropriate markings: CUI//PROPIN. STARFIRE operates
under NDAs to hold that information confidential therefore ensuring a controlled and productive
exchange of information between the partners. Whilst the details of the technical discussions will
not be made public, they are being used to craft deliverables and a road map that will be
disseminated to benefit the public.
