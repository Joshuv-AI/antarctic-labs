Harnessing Latent Dirichlet Allocation-based topic modeling to unveil the focus areas in Ayush healthcare in India
Authors: Tina Dutta, Abhinav Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The exponential growth of unstructured data has presented new opportunities for leveraging computational techniques to uncover meaningful insights in diverse domains, including healthcare. In the context of India's pluralistic medical ecosystem, the Ministry of Ayush plays a central role in shaping policies related to traditional systems such as Ayurveda, Yoga, Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, and Homeopathy. Despite the increasing prominence of AYUSH, especially post-COVID-19, there has been limited scholarly engagement with its policy evolution through a data-driven lens. This study applies Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), a topic modeling technique, to analyze annual reports published by the Ministry of Ayush from 2012 to 2023. The research aims to (i) identify recent focus areas in alternate healthcare policy, (ii) track temporal shifts in strategic priorities, and (iii) uncover underrepresented themes in institutional discourse. The findings reveal evolving trends such as increased emphasis on research, integration with mainstream health systems, digital initiatives, and global promotion. The study identifies four critical dimensions that remain underrepresented within Ayush policy discourse: the positioning of Ayush philosophy as a frontline public health framework, the articulation of an Ayush-centric health system paradigm, the programmatic integration of mental health, and the systematic incorporation of artificial intelligence beyond foundational digital health initiatives, thereby identifying areas requiring greater strategic attention and reform. By introducing topic modeling into Indian healthcare policy research, this paper fills a key methodological gap and offers empirical insights into the policy trajectory of Ayush. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first application of LDA to Ayush's policy literature.

Introduction

It is estimated that more than 80% of current data is unstructured, encompassing formats like text, audio, images, and video, with a large portion conveyed in rich and sometimes ambiguous natural language [1]. Focusing on the healthcare domain, this unstructured data comprises clinical notes, annual reports, patient's feedback and reviews, institutional documents and other data that if analyzed effectively can converge towards valuable insights into public health trends and institutional focus areas. Topic modeling is one such powerful tool that can be used to identify and extract the hidden latent themes from the large textual dataset [2,3]. Various topic modeling techniques have been developed to suit specific types of data, including short texts, long-form documents, correlated topics, and data with complex structural relationships [4]. Among all these diversified topic modeling techniques, LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation) has emerged as one of the robust topic models revealing the thematic structures within large collection of documents. LDA has proven effective in policy research, healthcare discourse analysis, and government document mining [5,6]. LDA is a probabilistic framework that not only facilitates the identification of latent topics but is also well suited for a variety of complex applications [7].

Despite its widespread adoption across various domains, the application of LDA in the context of India's healthcare research has been limited. India's healthcare system is characterized by its pluralistic nature, wherein multiple medical traditions coexist, including modern allopathic medicine and diverse systems of traditional medicine such as Ayurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, and Homeopathy-collectively governed under the Ministry of Ayush. Established in 2014 (after evolving from the earlier Department of Indian Systems of Medicine and Homeopathy formed in 1995), the Ministry of Ayush was tasked with developing, regulating, and promoting alternate systems of medicine in India [8]. The Ayush philosophy is grounded in the One Health approach that offers holistic perspectives and practices that engage with the interdependence of human well-being, animal health, and ecological sustainability [9,10]. Amid rapid social and economic development, India has undergone a major epidemiological transition marked by a rising burden of non-communicable diseases, prompting the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW) to strengthen health policies, notably through the Ayushman Bharat Mission (2018), a holistic and pioneering initiative that significantly integrates and benefits the Indian systems of medicine, including Ayush [11]. The Ayush system emphasizes preventive healthcare through lifestyle modification, dietary regulation, Yoga, and rejuvenative therapies, including natural antioxidants and immunomodulators, gaining increasing global attention among health scientists (ibid). Ayurveda offers a holistic framework for mental health by emphasizing Sattva and Tridosha balance, employing interventions such as Medhya Rasayanas, Panchakarma, lifestyle and dietary regulation, and yoga-meditation practices, with emerging evidence supporting its role as a complementary approach to managing common mental health conditions [12]. The resurgence of interest in Ayush, especially during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, has further emphasized its relevance in public health discourse and policy [13]. Despite the growing policy significance of the Ayush sector, there exists a critical gap in scholarship concerning systematic, data-driven analysis of the Ministry's evolving priorities and discursive patterns over time. Existing literature largely concentrates on specific systems, interventions, or clinical efficacy, often overlooking broader institutional narratives and long-term policy evolution [14]. The Ministry of Ayush, India, publishes voluminous annual reports, a rich repository of unstructured textual data, which can be harnessed to generate useful policy-specific insights. In light of this, this paper applies LDA based topic modeling to annual reports published by the Ministry of Ayush between 2012 and 2023 to address the following three research objectives:1.To identify and analyze major thrust areas in alternate healthcare in India in recent years;2.To examine the temporal evolution and shifts in Ayush policy priorities over the past decade; and3.To highlight underrepresented or missing dimensions in the Ministry's discursive focus.

Literature review

Alternate healthcare in India

India's healthcare system is undergoing significant changes, with a growing focus on alternative medicine alongside conventional biomedicine. As per National Sample Survey 2014, around 6.9% of outpatient care patients had utilized Ayush services [15]. Nearly half of non-communicable disease patients in India use both allopathic and alternative medicine, with medicinal herbs/biological-based medicine being the most common form of alternative medicine used [16]. Despite 40-70% of Indians using multiple healthcare systems, preferring allopathy for emergencies and others for chronic or preventive care, 97% of the national health budget goes to allopathy, leaving only 3% for the remaining seven systems, limiting integration and innovation [17]. Addressing this gap, the National Health Policy (NHP) 2017 advocates for mainstreaming AYUSH within a pluralistic healthcare system [18]. Over the past decade, the Ayush sector has expanded significantly through increased budgetary support, institutional growth, integration into public healthcare, and policy backing under the National Health Policy 2017 [19]. Despite notable progress, such as the rollout of AYUSHman Arogya Mandirs, co-located facilities, and standard-setting initiatives, persistent challenges in access, financing, and supply chains remain, hindering seamless integration and universal health coverage (ibid.). Also, in most societies, including India, an implicit medical hierarchy places allopathy above traditional, complementary and alternative systems of medicine (TCAM), and TCAM's growing adoption of allopathic tools in diagnosis, research, and education often clashes with its distinct epistemology, requiring separate research paradigms [20]. In recent years, Ayurveda is gaining global attention for its holistic approach, but faces challenges in aligning with contemporary scientific paradigms [21].

Recent researches on Ayush in India focus on efforts to integrate traditional medicine into modern healthcare and research frameworks, such as in cancer research [22] and diabetes management [23]. Between 2009 and 2020, most Ayush clinical trials were in Ayurveda (2054), followed by Siddha (635), Yoga (408), Unani (366), and Homoeopathy (169), with interventional studies dominating and a focus on non-communicable diseases reflecting Ayush's strengths [24]. The Ministry of Ayush has launched several digital initiatives to improve information systems, research databases, and academic resources for traditional medical systems [25]. However, the scope and volume of research on alternate healthcare in India remain significantly limited compared to mainstream health research.

Topic modeling as a tool for social science research

Topic modeling is a technique used to extract latent hidden topics within the texts. It is a probabilistic approach where the raw filtered and preprocessed texts constitute a corpus, further divisible into multiple documents. The main idea is to conceptualize the entire text dataset as documents, topics and words. Topic modeling, particularly using Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), is a powerful text mining technique for discovering latent semantic structures in document collections [26]. It has been widely applied in various fields, for e.g. Silva et al. (2021) applies topic modeling tool in 111 software engineering papers published during 2009-2020, showing that LDA-based methods dominate in topic modeling research, and topics are typically named manually [27]. A number of studies have used LDA-based topic modeling to identify major themes from newspapers [28,29]. Shakeel et al. (2018) propose a novel topic modeling framework for the Urdu language, by combining pre-processing techniques, LDA, and Gibbs sampling [30]. LDA and other techniques of textual analysis have emerged as a valuable tool in corporate and business research as well. Textual analysis has been applied on annual reports of Chinese companies between period 2006-2018 to examine the relationship between conciseness, complexity of financial disclosure and market reaction [31]. On a similar line, LDA has been used for the thematic analysis of annual reports of corporate firms operating in Borsa, Istanbul [32]. Kushkowski et al. (2020) explores the major topics covered under corporate governance research using LDA [33].

Topic modeling has gained prominence as a tool for healthcare research as well. LDA-based topic modeling has been used for tracing the evolution of research topics in social health domain [34] and in health journals in Indonesia [6]. Applying LDA to patient's interview data Li et al. (2019) [35], analyze personal goals of 211 patients undergoing bladder cancer surgery, revealing changing priorities from pre-surgery concerns about surgery and recovery to post-surgery goals of regaining normalcy and enjoying life. Using LDA Geletta et al. (2019) [36], analyze clinical trial data from ClinicalTrials.gov and predict trial terminations by identifying patterns in unstructured narrative descriptions. Cho et al. (2019) [37] uses LDA on news articles to understand the pattern of topics in the field of women's health. LDA has been used to identify hidden themes in clinical notes of admitted patients [38], thematic classification of the clinical reports [39], thematic analysis of patient's and caregivers' feedbacks [40], and to uncover latent topics within online physician reviews [41]. Different topic models have been applied on tweets for a correlation mining on Twitter Health Data [42]. Zakkar and Daniel (2021) analyze 367,573 patient stories on the Care Opinion platform using text mining techniques, identifying 16 topics related to healthcare experiences and finding that more than 55% of stories about certain services had negative sentiments, providing insights for quality improvement [43]. Despite various challenges in topic modeling approach [44], LDA-based topic modeling turns out be quite efficient in analyzing and interpreting voluminous health-related text data.

This paper aims to harness the potential of text-mining techniques to analyze the annual reports of the Ministry of Ayush, India, with the objective of identifying recent thrust areas, tracing the evolution of the ministry's focus over the past decade, and uncovering the overlooked domains within the field.

Data

The dataset utilized for this research paper comprises of the annual reports for the last decade i.e. from 2012 to 13 to 2022-23 (except 2018-19 and 2021-22)1 published by the Ministry of Ayush, Government of India. A total of nine annual reports, which are available in pdf format at the website of the Ministry of Ayush (https://ayush.gov.in/#!/archivereports) are downloaded and channeled for pre-processing. The annual reports are well structured and ideal for topic modeling as they provide comprehensive data across diverse healthcare themes, enabling effective identification of key trends, policies and development areas. The analyses solely focused on the textual components of the annual reports, excluding any visual elements such as photographs, logos etc.

Data preprocessing

Annual reports from the Ministry of Ayush were collected in PDF format and converted to text using Python's PyPDF2 library. The extracted text was saved as a JSON file for further processing. In the preprocessing stage, tokenization was performed, where the text was split into individual words or tokens for analysis. This was followed by lemmatization, converting words to their root forms (e.g., “swimming” to “swim”). Using the NLTK library, stop words-common words with little analytical value-were removed. Finally, domain-specific stop words, i.e., frequently occurring terms specific to AYUSH that could dominate the model due to repetition rather than thematic relevance, were excluded to avoid skewing the topic modeling results. Domain-specific stop words are identified after reviewing the reports. These are common recurring words in all the reports and upon removing them, other words, with greater thematic value could appear as high frequency words. This ensured a more accurate identification of latent themes. Topic modeling using LDA

The preprocessed data is analyzed by LDA algorithm using the python Genism library [32] to extract the hidden topics. Given a corpus that consists of D documents, T topics and W filtered words, the LDA model assumes that each document is a mixture of topics and each topic as a probability distribution over words [7]. Suppose d denotes a specific document, z denotes a topic and w denotes a word, then LDA model defines two conditional probability distributions, p(z|d) as the probability of a topic z in a document d and p(w|z) as probability of a word w in topic z. The distribution over topics for each document is drawn from Dirichlet prior with parameter. Similarly, the word distribution of topics shares common Dirichlet prior with hyperparameter η. Provided the parameters α and η, for any document d, the parameter θd of multinomial distribution over T topics is derived from Dir(θd|α) and for topic t, the parameter βt of a multinomial distribution over W word is derived from Dir(βt|η).

For an LDA model, the joint distribution over the topic mixture θ, the word mixture β, the topic assignment z and the observed words w for the entire corpus can be expressed as,(1)p(α,η)=∏d=1Dp(α)(∏n=1Ndp(θd)·p(zdn,β))·∏t=1Tp(η)

Inference in LDA involves computing the posterior distribution over the hidden topic structures, i.e., estimating p(β,θ,z|w). This posterior reveals how documents relate to different topics and how topics are expressed through vocabulary.

In this study, the pre-processed annual reports-i.e., the filtered text for each year-constitute the corpus, which was segmented into documents of 1000 words each. Each segment was treated as a separate document for analysis, resulting in a variable number of documents per year depending on report length. To perform LDA, it was first necessary to determine the optimal number of topics, as topic modeling is an unsupervised method with no predefined topic count. For this, coherence scores were calculated across a range of topic numbers (5 to 35), and the count yielding the highest coherence score was selected as optimal. A higher coherence score indicates that the top words within a topic are more logically and meaningfully related, making the topic easier to interpret [42]. There is practically no cut-off for coherence scores. The higher is better. Generally, coherence score<0.30 is unacceptable. For illustration, Fig. 2 presents the coherence scores for different topic counts in the 2015-16 annual report.Fig. 2Optimal Number of Topics vs Coherence Score for the annual report 2015-2016.

Optimal Number of Topics vs Coherence Score for the annual report 2015-2016.

After confirming optimum number of topics, the LDA model was applied to extract the top 30 terms/words (in terms of frequency of the words) associated with each topic, along with their respective weightage. To ensure reliability and stability, the LDA model was applied to the preprocessed corpus across five independent runs with different random initializations. In each run, the top 30 words per topic were extracted based on topic-word probabilities. A consensus-based approach was then used, retaining only those words that appeared in at least two of the five runs to reduce stochastic variation and hyperparameter sensitivity (this approach is adopted from Ref. [40]). The resulting topic keywords are therefore considered stable, reproducible, and representative of the underlying thematic structure of the corpus. These terms were then analyzed to interpret and assign meaningful labels to the underlying latent topics.

Labelling and validity of the extracted topics

Based on the terms extracted under each topic for a given annual report, topic identification was carried out through an independent manual review process as illustrated by Melnikov et al. (2024) [40]. To establish the interpretive validity of the LDA-generated topics, a structured expert-based manual review process was conducted. Two domain experts from healthcare field independently examined each topic using the corresponding set of the top 30 representative keywords derived from the LDA model. During the initial phase, experts assessed the internal semantic coherence of each keyword set and examined the contextual relevance of these keywords by systematically locating their occurrences within the corresponding policy document for the relevant year.

In the second phase, each expert independently assigned provisional thematic labels to the topics based on the dominant policy narratives and conceptual patterns emerging from the keyword-context mapping. This process ensured that topic labelling was grounded not only in statistical output but also in substantive textual evidence. Following the independent assessments, a consensus-building discussion was undertaken to reconcile any discrepancies in interpretation and to finalize topic labels. Consensus was achieved through deliberation on keyword meanings, contextual usage, and alignment with established policy constructs. Finally, to further validate the labelled topics, the experts manually identified and extracted explicit textual occurrences corresponding to each theme from the policy documents. These verified instances of topic manifestation were compiled and documented in the appendix table (Table-A1). For e.g., for the year 2019-20, topics such as international cooperation through MoUs and WHO engagement, pharmacopoeial standardization via PCIM&H and PLIM, regulatory oversight under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, academic expansion coupled with pharmacovigilance initiatives, and cross-cutting concerns of gender equity and administrative governance, all of these are explicitly documented in the AYUSH Annual Report (2019-20).

The occurrence of terms like “MoU” (Memorandum of Understanding), “cooperation”, “reference”, “exchange”, and “certification” underscores the sector's emphasis on forging formal bilateral and multilateral collaborations with foreign governments, research institutions, and global health bodies. Terms such as “regulatory”, “rules”, “authority”, and “standards” highlight the increasing policy focus on standardization and harmonization with international regulatory frameworks to enhance the credibility and acceptance of AYUSH products in overseas markets. The inclusion of “cosmetics”, “manufacturing”, and “raw” materials further indicates a trade-oriented outlook, focusing on the production and export of herbal products and traditional formulations. Similarly, Topic 2 (as depicted in figure-3b), comprising 18% of the tokens, includes terms such as “vidyapeeth” (such as Rashtriya Ayurveda Vidyapeeth), “campus”, “university”, and “delivery” suggesting the involvement of academic and institutional networks in health promotion and education dissemination. The term “rashtra” (nation) and “bharat” (India) point to national-level efforts, while “systems” and “availability” emphasize the focus on making healthcare services accessible. Words like “public”, “force”, “reach”, “periphery”, and “access” indicate outreach to marginalized populations, emphasizing the goal of inclusivity in healthcare communication and service provision. Furthermore, terms like “program”, “platform”, “model”, and “practices” suggest the structured and institutionalized nature of awareness campaigns and field-based interventions. The presence of “gram” (village), “unit”, “block”, and “zone” further reinforces the rural orientation and grassroots-level focus of these initiatives. Together, these terms portray a theme centered on increasing awareness, ensuring wide delivery of services, and engaging communities through structured programs. Thus, the reviewers label Topic-2 as “Health Awareness & Outreach Programs.”

Evolution and shifts in Ayush policy priorities (2012-2023)

Reviewing the topics of Table-2 over the study period unfolds four broad phases in the evolution of AYUSH policy.

Phase-I: System Building and Regulation (2012-13 to 2014-15): In the initial years, the primary thrust was on establishing regulatory frameworks and formalizing traditional medicine systems within public health. The dominant topics extracted during this phase centered around institutional training (e.g. “To ensure availability of quality education and training to Ayush Doctors/Scientists/Teachers … [Report 2012-13]”; Training Programme for Teachers: One training programme has been conducted on 25-26 July, 2014 at Ahmedabad for Ayurveda teachers for upgrading these clinical skills. … [Report 2014-15]”), development of standardized curricula (“Priority to research covering clinical trials, pharmacology, toxicology, standardization and study of pharmaco-kinetics in respect of identified areas of strength. … [Report 2012-13]; “The Indian Medicine Central Council (Minimum Standards of Education in Indian Medicine) (Amendment) Regulations, 2013 for Ayurveda. … [Report 2012-13]”), drug quality assurance (“Department of AYUSH has a Drug Control Cell to administer regulatory and quality control provisions for Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani and Homoeopathy (ASU&H) drugs. … [Report 2014-14]”; “The research areas include clinical research, drug standardization research, survey and cultivation of medicinal plants … [Report 2014-15]”, and early-stage community health initiatives (“AYUSH facilities have been set up in Primary Health Centres, Community Health Centres and District Hospitals.…[Report 2012-13]”; “The Department of AYUSH organized a National Campaign for popularizing use of Homoeopathy for health of mothers and children..…[Report 2013-14]” “Monthly Workshops and Regional Language Workshops on Naturopathy & Yoga are conducted in NIN with emphasis to women's health.…[Report 2013-14]”). This period reflected the Ministry's foundational efforts to integrate AYUSH more systematically into national healthcare structures.

Phase-II: Expansion and Diversification (2015-16 to 2016-17): A notable broadening of policy focus is observed during this period. Alongside continued efforts in academic strengthening and regional training, there was a visible push toward policy formulation (“The Ministry is responsible for policy formulation, development and implementation of programmes for the growth, development and propagation of Ayurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha and Homoeopathy. … [Report 2016-17]”and institutional expansion (“Council has undertaken ‘SwasthyaRakshan Program’ (SRP) through its 11 Research Institutes by adopting five villages/urban cities near to the Institutes [Report 2016-17].”. This phase also introduced themes related to international trade (“International Cooperation”; “Memorandum of Understanding (MoU)”; “International activities” … [Report 2015-16]), export of AYUSH products (“To facilitate availability of raw drugs, which are authentic and contain essential components as required under pharmacopoeial standards to help improve quality of drugs, for domestic consumption and export, …[Report 2015-16]”; “Estimated Exports of Herbal Raw Drugs, including Extracts has been estimated 1,34,500 MT in 2014-15.. … [Report 2016-17]”) and the initiation of strategic collaborations (“Signing MoU between PCIM&H and United States Pharmacopoeia (USP). … [Report 2016-17]”. Topics during these years suggest a growing ambition to both diversify programmatic goals and increase institutional visibility.

Phase-III: Thematic Complexity and Targeted Interventions (2017-2018): The 2017-18 period marked a high point in thematic diversification, with topic modeling revealing up to ten distinct thematic areas in a single year. This included not only core areas like infrastructure, training, and drug regulation (“Research covering clinical trials, pharmacology, toxicology and standardization is accorded priority.” … [Report 2017-18]), but also emphasizing the eminent role of herbal products and medicinal plants (“India's herbal sector is representing about 8500 licensed herbal units, thousands of extractors or semi processors, export houses and cottage-level units of herbal based products, millions of folk healers and household users of thousands of herbal raw drugs.”; “… India is the world's second largest exporter of medicinal plants after China and both the countries together produce more than 70% of the total global demand for herbal products.” … [Report 2017-18], regional language accessibility (“e-charak supports 7 languages viz. Phase-IV: Consolidation and Global Positioning (2019-20 to 2022-23): The most recent phase reflects a consolidated strategic orientation, with recurring emphasis on international cooperation, scientific research infrastructure, and mass outreach programs. Topics such as international agreements, branding and promotion of AYUSH globally, and integration with wellness tourism and preventive healthcare (including naturopathy and yoga) dominated this period. This phase also emphasized creating public awareness through nationwide campaigns and enhancing regulatory alignment with international standards. Underrepresented dimensions in Ayush policy discourses

While the thematic analysis of Ayush annual reports from 2012 to 2023 reveals a wide array of programmatic focus areas, several critical dimensions remain underrepresented or insufficiently addressed within formal policy documents. These gaps highlight potential blind spots in the Ministry's policy narratives and suggest areas for further development and strategic investment. Four major underrepresented areas are outlined here:1.Ayush philosophy as a frontline public health framework: Across Ayush annual reports from 2012 to 2023, references to the philosophical foundations of Ayush systems, such as holistic health, balance, preventive care, and harmony with nature are consistently present. However, these references are predominantly descriptive, serving to contextualize institutional mandates, historical evolution, or conceptual frameworks. The LDA-based topic modeling did not identify a distinct theme centered on the application of AYUSH philosophical principles as frontline responses to modern-day ailments, including lifestyle-related disorders, non-communicable diseases, mental health concerns, and emerging public health challenges. Manual review indicates that while Ayush philosophical principles are operationalized through activities such as clinical trials and research initiatives aimed at establishing efficacy and effectiveness, these efforts are seldom positioned within the policy discourse as the principal or frontline solutions to contemporary health challenges. Instead, such initiatives are largely presented as isolated research endeavours rather than as part of an overarching, philosophy-driven public health response. This gap relates to the application of AYUSH philosophy in public health problem-solving.2.Ayush-Centric Health System Paradigm: The holistic and preventive orientation of Ayush systems allows for an alternative conceptualization of healthcare in which biomedical interventions function as condition-specific tools within a broader health paradigm informed by Ayush philosophy. However, the LDA-based analysis of Ayush annual reports indicates that such a paradigmatic reframing does not emerge as a coherent theme within policy discourse. Instead, manual review traces positioning of Ayush as a complementary or adjunct system to biomedicine, embedded within existing biomedical infrastructures. For e.g. the latest 2022-23 report states “… establishment of advanced/specialized Ayush medical health unit in reputed Ayush and Allopathy institutions both in Government and Non-Government sector … [Report 2022-23]”. This asymmetrical framing reflects a broader conceptual underrepresentation, wherein Ayush is rarely positioned as the foundational health framework guiding prevention, wellness, and long-term care, with biomedicine playing a supportive, episodic role. The absence of this Ayush-centric paradigm from policy narratives suggests that while philosophical grounds for such a framing exist, they remain weakly articulated at the level of health system design and governance. This gap relates to the positioning of Ayush within the architecture of the national health system.3.Integrative mental health within AYUSH policy framework: Despite the growing national and global emphasis on mental health, the LDA-based topic modeling of Ayush annual reports (2012-2023) did not yield a distinct or coherent topic related to mental health. This absence in the model outputs does not imply a complete lack of mental health related content in the reports; rather, it reflects the limited and fragmented representation of mental health within the Ayush policy discourse. A detailed manual review revealed that references to mental health were largely confined to research-oriented sections, predominantly in the context of collaborative studies and academic activities involving the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS). For e.g. “Studying the role of Yoga in correcting GABA neurotransmitter deficit in moderate to severe depressive patient … [under ongoing projects at NIMHANS, Report 2019-20]”; “Knowledge, attitude, practice (KAP) and barriers for a referral to Yoga among the psychiatrists, neurologists, and neurosurgeons in India-A survey”; “Psycho-neuro-endocrinological markers of stress and the response to a Yoga-based intervention in first degree relatives of schizophrenia patients (FDRS)”; “Effect of Yoga on mirror neuron activity among patients with depression: A transcranial magnetic stimulation study” … [Under completed projects at NIMHANS, Report 2022-23]”. These references show that mental health is acknowledged as a subject of scientific inquiry in majority of the annual reports, but it is not articulated as a programmatic or policy priority within the Ayush framework. While Ayush systems such as Ayurveda, Yoga, Unani, Siddha, and Homoeopathy possess established therapeutic concepts relevant to mental well-being, these are not systematically reflected in policy narratives related to mental health promotion, prevention, or treatment.4.Artificial Intelligence (AI)-enabled Ayush systems: While digital health initiatives such as mobile applications, online portals, and the Ayush Grid are consistently reported across multiple Ayush annual reports, artificial intelligence-based applications did not emerge as a distinct topic in the LDA analysis of reports from 2012 to 2023. A manual review indicates that explicit references to artificial intelligence appear primarily in the 2022-23 report, largely in the context of international deliberations and exploratory discussions under the World Health Organization-International Telecommunication Union (WHO–ITU) Focus Group on Artificial Intelligence for Health. AI remains at a conceptual and consultative stage, rather than a fully articulated policy or implementation domain within AYUSH. This underrepresentation underscores the scope for future policy consolidation, particularly in translating international consultations and technical discussions into concrete AI-enabled applications for Ayush research, diagnostics, decision support, and public health surveillance.

Conclusion

This study employed a natural language processing-based topic modeling approach, supplemented by systematic manual review, to examine policy discourses in alternate healthcare in India, as documented by the Ministry of Ayush. Specifically, it aimed to (i) identify recent policy thrust areas, (ii) assess shifts in policy focus over the past decade, and (iii) uncover underrepresented dimensions within the policy landscape. A key contribution of this study lies in its novel application of text mining techniques, used here for the first time to systematically analyze government documentation in the context of Ayush policy. The methodology proved to be highly effective for processing large volumes of unstructured text and extracting latent thematic patterns (as triangulated by manual review of the policy documents), demonstrating both scalability and relevance for broader policy research. Recent studies combining LDA with qualitative policy analysis show that topic modeling can systematically identify dominant frames, thematic shifts, and emerging policy narratives that may be difficult to detect through manual analysis alone. For instance, the application of LDA to local government climate strategy documents in Australia has illustrated how topic modeling can be used to assess the diffusion and persistence of the “climate emergency” policy frame across jurisdictions and over time [46].

In terms of substantive findings, the study offers critical insights into emerging priorities, historical shifts, and neglected domains within Ayush policy. Through triangulation of model outputs with close textual examination, the study identifies four critical dimensions that remain underrepresented within Ayush policy discourse: the positioning of Ayush philosophy as a frontline public health framework, the articulation of an Ayush-centric health system paradigm, the programmatic integration of mental health, and the systematic incorporation of artificial intelligence beyond foundational digital health initiatives. Importantly, these gaps do not reflect a lack of philosophical foundations, research activity, or exploratory engagement, but rather a limited translation of these elements into sustained, cohesive, and strategically articulated policy narratives. The findings suggest that Ayush philosophy remains predominantly descriptive rather than operationally foregrounded in public health problem-solving; mental health is acknowledged primarily through research collaborations rather than policy-led initiatives; artificial intelligence is discussed as an emergent possibility rather than an implementation domain; and integration is framed largely as the inclusion of Ayush within biomedical infrastructures rather than as a reimagining of health system paradigms. The identification of underrepresented dimensions points to opportunities for policy consolidation, conceptual clarity, and strategic expansion. The study calls for a more proactive, integrative, and future-oriented approach, one that harmonizes global aspirations with local relevance, and merges scientific validation with Ayush's holistic health philosophy. Such an approach will be essential for enabling Ayush to make a meaningful contribution to India's evolving public health landscape and to position itself effectively within the global wellness economy.

Limitations of the study

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of certain methodological constraints inherent to computational text analysis. The use of LDA is well established in policy analysis for identifying latent thematic structures across large textual corpora. In the present study, LDA was employed as an exploratory and descriptive tool to examine longitudinal shifts in policy emphasis within Ayush ministry documents. While LDA abstracts textual data into probabilistic topic distributions, the study deliberately complemented automated outputs with systematic expert validation and policy-document cross-referencing, thereby strengthening interpretability and substantive relevance. Consequently, the identified topics should be understood as dominant policy frames and thematic emphases, rather than exhaustive representations of policy intent. Model outcomes in topic modeling are influenced by preprocessing decisions and parameter selection. To address this, multiple model specifications were evaluated using coherence diagnostics, and the final model was selected based on a convergence of quantitative coherence trends and qualitative expert consensus. This multi-step validation approach reduces arbitrariness and enhances analytical stability. Furthermore, the analysis focuses on official policy texts, which are authoritative sources of governmental intent and strategic direction. The study does not attempt to infer implementation effectiveness or program-level outcomes, as its objective is confined to mapping shifts in policy discourse and institutional priorities over time. Finally, the study does not claim causal inference. Instead, it provides a systematic, replicable mapping of evolving policy focus, offering an empirical foundation upon which explanatory and evaluative research may build.

Scope for future research

Future research can build upon the present study in several meaningful ways. First, advanced topic modeling approaches, such as Structural Topic Models (STM), Dynamic Topic Models (DTM), or embedding-based models (e.g., BERTopic), may be employed to better capture temporal evolution, document metadata, and semantic relationships within AYUSH policy discourse. Second, integrating mixed-methods approaches, combining computational text analysis with in-depth qualitative policy analysis, interviews, or ethnographic methods could provide richer contextual interpretation and strengthen substantive policy insights. Third, future studies may expand the corpus to include parliamentary debates, budget speeches, implementation guidelines, media coverage, and stakeholder reports, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of policy formulation, communication, and execution. Fourth, linking topic trends with external indicators, such as funding patterns, program coverage, research outputs, or public health indicators could facilitate evaluative and impact-oriented analyses of Ayush policy priorities. Finally, comparative analyses across ministries, states, or countries could help situate Ayush policy trajectories within broader national and global health policy frameworks, including the One Health and integrative medicine paradigms.
