A comparative study of n-3 PUFA enrichment, antioxidative status, and underlying mechanism of Pekin ducks fed different dietary oil sources
Authors: Yanhong Guo, Zitao Zhao, Lei Zhuang, Qimeng Wang, Junting Cao, Yongbao Wu, Zhiguo Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of dietary supplementation with soybean oil (SO), flaxseed oil (FO), and rubber seed oil (RSO) on systemic fatty acid composition, antioxidant status, and hepatic lipid-metabolism-genes expression in Pekin ducks. A total of 144 one-day-old Pekin ducks were randomly allotted to three groups and fed diets containing SO (control), FO, or RSO for six weeks. The results showed that dietary FO and RSO supplementation reduced the liver index and abdominal fat index of Pekin ducks (P < 0.05). Furthermore, FO and RSO promoted the enrichment of n-3 PUFA and reduced the n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio in plasma and liver (P < 0.05). In terms of lipid composition and antioxidant capacity, RSO exhibited more pronounced effects. Specifically, RSO significantly decreased plasma levels of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), as well as hepatic TG content (P < 0.05). Moreover, RSO significantly reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in both plasma and liver, while enhancing the activities of plasma superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), as well as hepatic SOD activity. In addition, compared with SO, both FO and RSO increased the hepatic expression of CAT, ApoA-1, and LCAT, while decreasing the expression of PPARα, PPARγ, DGAT2, and SCD1 in Pekin ducks (P < 0.05). Overall, RSO enriched n-3 PUFA and reduced the n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio in Pekin ducks, and also demonstrated superior lipid-lowering and antioxidant effects compared with FO. These findings suggest that RSO has potential as a dietary lipid source in duck production and as an ingredient for functional feed development.Keywords: Antioxidant, Lipid metabolism, n-3 PUFA, Pekin duck, Rubber seed oil

Introduction

Dietary oil is a primary energy source in poultry diets, and different oil sources exert distinct effects on poultry growth, development, and fat metabolism. A growing number of studies have highlighted the critical role of n-3 PUFA in cardiovascular disease prevention (Watanabe and Tatsuno, 2020) and nervous system development (Yu et al., 2021). Furthermore, the lipid-lowering effects of n-3 PUFA are particularly prominent. Studies have shown that n-3 PUFA activates peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) and upregulates genes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation, thereby promoting fatty acid breakdown and utilization and reducing substrate availability for triglyceride synthesis (Larter et al., 2008; Tapia et al., 2014). Concurrently, n-3 PUFA suppresses sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP-1c) activity, leading to the downregulation of key lipogenic enzymes such as fatty acid synthase (FAS) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), thus reducing de novo lipogenesis (Hernández-Rodas et al., 2017). Moreover, n-3 PUFA exhibits significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. For instance, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) can specifically activate the Nrf2 pathway (Starcevic et al., 2023) or inhibit the IKK/NF-κB pathway to exert its anti-inflammatory effects (Yang et al., 2013).

N-6 PUFA also plays a dual role in promoting growth and development as well as preventing cardiovascular disease (Penumetcha et al., 2012). However, excessive intake of n-6 PUFA can exacerbate inflammation (Selmin et al., 2021). Therefore, maintaining an optimal dietary n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio is crucial. Previous studies have shown that an n-6/n-3 ratio of 5:1 improved metabolic markers and downregulated inflammation-related gene expression in obese mice (Gutierrez-Guerra et al., 2025), whereas an n-6/n-3 ratio exceeding 20:1 increased serum oxidative stress markers in rats (Yang et al., 2016). Similarly, in meat ducks, dietary n-6/n-3 ratios of 6:1 or 2:1 were associated with lower serum lipid levels (Li et al., 2017). Collectively, these findings indicate that a lower n-6/n-3 ratio can promote growth performance and reduce blood lipids and inflammation, although the optimal ratio varies among species.

Rubber seed oil (RSO), constituting 40 %–50 % of seed weight, is rich in PUFA and contains significantly higher levels of linolenic and linoleic acids compared with palm oil or coconut oil (Fernando et al., 2019). Notably, its linolenic acid content of 17–22 % qualifies RSO as a typical n-3 PUFA-enriched plant oil. It has been reported that dietary supplementation with RSO in laying hens can reduce the cholesterol content and increase n-3 PUFA levels in eggs (Lu et al., 2021; Wen et al., 2019). However, current research on RSO has predominantly focused on biofuel production, and few studies have evaluated its application in animal feed or nutrition. Therefore, this experiment aimed to investigate the effects of RSO relative to soybean oil (SO) and flaxseed oil (FO) on the growth performance, fatty acid composition, antioxidant status, and lipid metabolism of Pekin ducks.

Materials and methods

This animal procedures in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Feed Research Institute Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (IFR-CAAS-20210901).

Experimental design, diets, and management

A total of 144 one-day-old male Pekin ducks were randomly divided into three groups, with four replicates per group and 12 ducks per replicate. The ducks were housed in pens (length 1500 mm, width 1000 mm, height 600mm), with 12 ducks each pen. There was no significant difference in the initial body weight of ducks among the groups (P > 0.05), and all replicates were evenly distributed across different locations in the house to minimize positional effects. Throughout the experimental period, ducks had ad libitum access to feed and water. The brooding temperature was maintained at 32 ± 2 °C until 7 days of age and then gradually decreased to 25 ± 2 °C by 21 days of age. The ambient humidity was maintained between 60 % and 65 %.

The experimental diets were formulated to meet the nutrient requirements for growing ducks and consisted of starter phase (d 1-21) and grower phase (d 22-42). The experimental diets were formulated based on a corn-soybean meal basal diet according to the Nutritional Requirements of Meat-Type Duck (GB/T45103-2024) and supplemented with 1 % SO (control diet), 1 % FO, or 1 % RSO. All diets were formulated to have similar nutrient composition and differed only in the oil source (Table 1). Fatty acid composition of experimental diets was analyzed and the results are shown in Fig. 1.

Data collection and sample preparation

At the end of the trial, two ducks from each replicate were randomly selected after a 6-h fast. Blood samples were collected into tubes containing sodium heparin, and plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and stored at −20 °C. Abdominal fat and liver were collected and weighed to calculate the relative tissue weights (% of live BW). Samples of liver tissue were collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Plasma biochemical and immunity parameters

The plasma concentrations of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), as well as the activities of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), were measured using a spectrophotometric method with an automatic analyzer (Hitachi 7080, Tokyo, Japan) and corresponding commercial kits (Maccura, Chengdu, China). Liver samples were freeze-dried to determine dry matter content. Protein content was determined using a Dumas Nitrogen analyzer (FOSS DumatecTM 8000, Hilleroed, Denmark). Total fat content was determined using the Soxhlet extractor method. The tissue samples were ashed in a muffle furnace for 4 h to determine ash content.

Fatty acid composition

The experimental diets and liver samples were freeze-dried, ground, and passed through a 40-mesh sieve. The fatty acid composition of the experimental diets, plasma, and liver samples was analyzed by gas chromatography (Agilent 7890A Series Systems, with FID detector) according to the method described in previous research (Ma et al., 2015). The column was J&W 112-88A7 (100 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm), and 1 μL of the sample was injected with the carrier gas (N2) onto the column by an autosampler at a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. The oven temperature program was set as follows: 120 °C (held for 1 min) to 180 °C at 15 °C/min (held for 10 min), then to 210 °C at 3 °C/min (held for 8 min), then to 230 °C (10 min) at 5 °C/min. Prior to analysis, 25 µL of methyl nonadecanoate (TCI>99.0 %) was added to both the mixed standard solution and the samples as the internal standard. Fatty acids in samples were identified and quantified by comparison with fatty acids standards from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, USA). Saturated fatty acids, monounsaturated fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids, total n-3 and n-6 PUFA, and n-6/n-3 ratios were calculated after fatty acids analysis. The results were shown as milligrams per gram of diets or tissues, and milligrams per milliliter of plasma.

Plasma and liver antioxidant status

A 10 % tissue homogenate was prepared by homogenizing 0.5 g of tissue under ice-cold conditions in 4.5 mL of normal saline. The homogenate was subsequently centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. The antioxidant parameters, including malondialdehyde (MDA) content and the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), were measured in plasma and liver homogenates using commercial kits obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from liver samples using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Dalian, China) according to the instructions. The concentration and purity of the total RNA were determined using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vantaa, Finland). The OD260/280 ratios of the liver RNA samples ranged from 1.8 to 2.1. cDNA was synthesized using TransScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Real-time PCR was performed using a ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a CFX96™ Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The mRNA expression levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to β-actin. The final expression level of each target gene was presented as a percentage of the SO treatment group. The primer sequences of the detected genes are shown in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed by the GLM procedure of SAS9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). When dietary treatment was significant (P < 0.05), Student's t test was used to test the significance of differences between treatments. The probability level of P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Carcass traits

Carcass traits serve as key indicators for evaluating animal growth and development. The abdominal fat percentage was significantly reduced in the RSO group, and the liver index was significantly decreased in both the FO and RSO groups (Table 3, P < 0.05). Consistent with previous studies, increasing n-3 PUFA levels in diets has been shown to reduce liver weight in broilers (Huo et al., 2019). Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that n-3 PUFA can significantly reduce lipid deposition, which may contribute to the reduction in organ weight (Kumar et al., 2019). These findings are also consistent with our previous study, in which dietary RSO supplementation dose-dependently reduced the liver index and abdominal fat deposition in Pekin ducks (Zhao et al., 2023; Zhuang et al., 2025). Given the differences in the utilization of different oils by ducks, these results suggest that dietary RSO had a more pronounced effect on reducing liver and abdominal fat deposition in Pekin ducks.

Plasma and liver biochemical indicators

Plasma biochemical parameters directly reflect the nutritional and health status of animals. In clinical studies, elevated blood lipid levels have been found to increase the risk of cardiovascular diseases, such as coronary heart disease and atherosclerosis (Tortosa-Caparros et al., 2017). As shown in Table 4, plasma TG and TC levels in the RSO group were significantly reduced (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the hepatic TG content was significantly reduced in both the RSO and FO groups (P < 0.05), and importantly, the reduction was more pronounced in the RSO group. N-3 PUFAs are well established to reduce lipid synthesis and deposition, while also enhancing antioxidant capacity and immune function (Xu et al., 2025). Similarly, n-6 PUFA has been shown to modulate lipid metabolism and support cardiovascular health. However, excessive intake of n-6 PUFA has been found to upregulate lipogenic genes, leading to lipid accumulation, and may interfere with the absorption and utilization of n-3 PUFA (Selmin et al., 2021). In contrast to previous findings, both FO and RSO exhibited varying degrees of lipid-lowering effects under the experimental conditions used in this study. We hypothesize that this outcome may be related to the dietary n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio and the specific animal breed. For instance, one study reported that in Cherry Valley ducks, the highest plasma TG levels occurred at an n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio of 2:1, whereas the lowest fat content in liver and muscle was observed at a ratio of 6:1 (Li et al., 2017). Similarly, in pigs, a dietary n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio of 1.5:1-5:1 resulted in lower serum levels of TG, TC, and LDL-C. These results indicate that the RSO diet, which had an n-6/n-3 ratio of 4.7:1, demonstrated a stronger capacity to regulate lipid metabolism and reduce lipid deposition than the FO diet in Pekin ducks.

Aminotransferases play an important role in maintaining normal metabolism and are mainly distributed in the liver and other tissues. They are released into the blood only when tissue damage or cell rupture occurs. Therefore, elevated aminotransferases activities in the blood are important markers of liver damage (Han et al., 2019). The anti-inflammatory effects of n-3 PUFA have been widely reported. Existing evidence suggests that PUFA can modulate immune responses through one or more major molecular mechanisms, including alterations in membrane composition and function, reductions in eicosanoid production, and changes in cytokine biosynthesis (Fritsche, 2006). In this study, plasma ALT activity was significantly reduced in the RSO group (Table 4, P < 0.05), indicating that RSO exerts a beneficial effect of RSO on liver cell integrity and function. Although dietary FO showed a trend toward reducing plasma ALT activity, this reduction was not statistically significant (P ˃ 0.05). In addition, dietary supplementation with FO or RSO increased plasma IgG levels, while IgM levels were significantly increased only in the RSO group (P < 0.05). Given that humoral immunity is a critical defense mechanism against pathogen invasion and that immunoglobulin are key components of humoral immunity, these results suggest that RSO may enhance humoral immune function in Pekin ducks. In our previous study, RSO showed anti-inflammatory properties in LPS-induced RAW 264.7 macrophages, as evidenced by decreased IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels and gene expression, as well as increased IL-10 level and expression. These effects may be attributed to the high levels of phenols and flavonoids in RSO (Liu et al., 2022). Together, these findings support that dietary fatty acid manipulation may regulate cytokine production and enhance humoral immunity, although the effects of n-3 PUFA in diseased animals remain controversial.

Fatty acid composition

Numerous studies have demonstrated that the fatty acid profiles of animal tissues can be effectively modulated through dietary manipulation. For instance, a high-α-linolenic acid rapeseed oil diet significantly increased n-3 PUFA levels in the muscle of Micropterus salmoides (Li et al., 2025). Similarly, the replacement of sunflower oil with linseed oil and fish oil in broilers diets significantly increased n-3 PUFA levels and reduced the n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio in muscle (Kalakuntla et al., 2017). In this study, dietary supplementation with FO and RSO significantly elevated the levels of ALA, DPA, DHA, and total n-3 PUFA in plasma and liver (Table 5, Table 6, P < 0.05). However, the tissue enrichment capacity of RSO was lower than that of FO, which may be attributed to its lower ALA content. Simultaneously, both FO and RSO significantly reduced the n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio (P < 0.05). The decrease in the ratio resulted from an increase in total n-3 PUFA levels concomitant with a decrease in total n-6 PUFA levels. Notably, FO treatment exhibited a more pronounced effect compared to RSO treatment. In the absence of the lack of dietary n-3 long-chain PUFA (LC-PUFA), tissues deposition of n-3 LC-PUFA, such as EPA, DPA, and DHA, is mainly derived from the conversation of dietary ALA. This process is catalyzed by Δ-6 and Δ-5 desaturases, elongases, and peroxisomal β-oxidases (Gutiérrez et al., 2019). The synthesis of n-6 LC-PUFA in vivo also requires the participation of these enzymes. In the present study, n-6 LC-PUFA levels decreased as n-3 LC-PUFA levels increased in both plasma and tissue. Specifically, DHA, the end product of n-3 PUFA metabolism, was highest in the FO group, whereas C22:5N6, the end product of n-6 PUFA metabolism, was highest in the SO group. Since Δ-6 and Δ-5 desaturases are the rate-limiting enzymes in LC-PUFA synthesis, n-3 and n-6 PUFA compete for these enzymes in vivo. Previous studies have shown that diets enriched in n-3 PUFA can increase the expression of desaturase-related genes, such as members of the FADS and ELOVL families (Li et al., 2017). In our study, FO and RSO promoted the conversion of dietary ALA to n-3 LC-PUFA, thereby enhancing the deposition of these fatty acids in tissues. Therefore, dietary n-3 PUFA content is a key factor influencing n-3 LC-PUFA deposition in tissues, and ALA-rich RSO may serve as a dietary lipid source to enhance n-3 PUFA status in ducks.

Plasma and liver antioxidant capacity

The combined effects of intensified metabolism and heat production during later growth stages, compounded by high-density intensive farming conditions, can predispose poultry to oxidative stress (Wu et al., 2019). Oxidative stress is characterized by a disruption of redox balance, leading to the overproduction and accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which disrupt normal physiological and biochemical functions in poultry. MDA is a primary end product of lipid peroxidation initiated by ROS attack on cell membranes. Consequently, MDA levels in plasma and tissues provide a reliable marker of cellular oxidative damage, indicating both the extent of lipid peroxidation and the load of systemic ROS (Yang et al., 2010). Animals possess a comprehensive antioxidant enzyme system. SOD is an important antioxidant enzyme that catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide anions (O2-), generated by electron leakage during mitochondrial electron transport, into H2O2. Key isoforms include Cu/ZnSOD in the cytoplasm and MnSOD in the mitochondria (Surai, 2016). The antioxidant system can scavenge H2O2 through a variety of pathways. H2O2 can be directly decomposed into H2O and O2 by CAT, or reduced to H2O2 by GSH-Px using GSH as an electron donor, thereby generating oxidized glutathione (GSSG) (Surai et al., 2019). This process is an important mechanism by which the antioxidant system scavenges ROS and maintains redox balance in the body. A previous study reported that milk replacer containing n-3 PUFA increased GSH-Px and SOD activities in calf serum and reduced MDA levels, thereby improving ROS-scavenging capacity (Śpitalniak-Bajerska et al., 2020). In the present study, CAT and GSH-Px activities were increased in FO and RSO groups (Table 7, P < 0.05), and RSO decreased plasma MDA levels (P < 0.05). In the liver, dietary FO and RSO significantly reduced MDA levels, whereas RSO significantly elevated SOD activity (P < 0.05). In our previous research, we observed that RSO exhibited strong ROS-scavenging activity and increased the expression of antioxidant enzyme-related genes by activating Nrf2 signal pathway in cells (Liu et al., 2022). These results indicate that dietary supplementation with FO or RSO improved the antioxidant capacity of Pekin ducks, and that RSO may contain antioxidant substances that make it slightly more effective than FO in scavenging ROS.

Hepatic related-genes mRNA expression level

Fig. 2 shows the expression levels of metabolism-related genes in the liver of ducks fed the different dietary oils. Compared with the SO group, dietary FO and RSO supplementation decreased the mRNA expression of PPARα, PPARγ, DGAT2, SCD1, ABCA1, and RXRα genes, while increasing the relative mRNA expression of APOA1 and LCAT in duck liver (P < 0.05). Compared with the FO treatment, RSO further decreased PPARα and PPARγ expression and increased APOA1 expression (P < 0.05). PPARα and PPARγ are key transcription factors involved in lipid metabolism. PPARα primarily promotes fatty acid oxidation, whereas PPARγ regulates the uptake, storage, and esterification of free fatty acids in the liver (Dossi et al., 2014). We observed reduced PPARα and PPARγ mRNA expression in FO and RSO groups (Fig. 2B, C), which was consistent with the above results. This reduction may be associated with the lower TG levels after FO and RSO supplementation (O'Reilly et al., 2020). DGAT2 catalyzes the final committed step in TG synthesis and contributes substantially to TG production. Conversely, SCD serves as a key desaturase that generates monounsaturated fatty acids from their saturated precursors (Chitraju et al., 2019). Previous studies have shown that n-3 PUFA-rich diets decreased the expression of lipogenic genes, including DGAT2 and SCD1 (Yang et al., 2021), which is consistent with our finding that FO and RSO reduced DGAT2 and SCD1 expression in this study (Fig. 2I, J). In addition, the lower dietary saturated fatty acid content may also have contributed to the decreased SCD1 expression. High-density lipoprotein (HDL) promotes reverse cholesterol transport (RCT), facilitating the clearance of excess cholesterol from extrahepatic tissues to the liver. ABCA1 mediates the efflux of free cholesterol from cells to APOA1, leading to the formation of nascent HDL. LCAT subsequently esterifies cholesterol, promoting HDL maturation, Furthermore, whereas ABCG1 further facilitates cholesterol efflux to mature HDL-C (Pizzini et al., 2017). ABCA1 transcription is regulated by LXR/RXR response elements, and activated LXR forms a heterodimer with RXR, which binds to the ABCA1 promoter to promote transcription (Uehara et al., 2007). Our results showed that RXRα expression decreased in the FO and RSO groups compared with SO treatment (Fig. 2D). ABCA1 gene expression was decreased in the FO group (Fig. 2G), and a similar decreasing trend was observed in the RSO group. These results suggest that FO and RSO may regulate ABCA1 expression through the LXR/RXR pathway. Consistent with these results, researchers observed that unsaturated fatty acids suppressed ABCG1 gene expression through a mechanism mediated by the binding of the LXR/RXR heterodimer to its promoter (Uehara et al., 2007). Although ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux may be reduced in the FO and RSO groups, FO and RSO may promote HDL-C maturation, as reflected by the increased expression of APOA1 and LCAT (Fig. 2A, F), with the effect being more pronounced in the RSO group than in the FO group.

Meanwhile, we found that FO and RSO increased the mRNA expression of antioxidant-related genes, including SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px, in the liver (Fig. 2K-M). However, FO and RSO did not increase hepatic CAT and GSH-Px activities, and the T-AOC showed a declining trend (Table 7). One possible explanation is that the increased deposition of n-3 PUFA in tissues promoted their incorporation into membrane phospholipids. Due to their high susceptibility to oxidation, this incorporation may increase the sensitivity of plasma and tissue membranes to lipid peroxidation, consequently leading to a greater antioxidant demand. For example, El-Bahr reported that 4 % FO supplementation in broiler diets decreased SOD activity and GSSG levels, while increasing MDA and 8-OHdGD levels compared with 2 % FO supplementation. These changes were accompanied by reduced antioxidant capacity in PUFA-rich tissues, including the liver and breast muscle (El-Bahr et al., 2021). On the other hand, a bidirectional relationship exists between oxidative stress and inflammation, whereby a reduction in the inflammatory response can mitigate oxidative stress (Wu et al., 2021). Studies have shown that the increase in n-3 PUFA percentage relative to n-6 PUFA can benefit the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capacity by avoiding n-6 PUFA synthesis in the liver (Chen et al., 2015). Overall, despite the limited changes in antioxidant enzyme activities, the reduced hepatic MDA levels indicate that both FO and RSO alleviated ROS-induced lipid peroxidation, suggesting an overall improvement in hepatic oxidative status.

Conclusion

Taken together, dietary RSO supplementation increased n-3 PUFA levels in plasma and tissues and improved immune status in Pekin ducks. More importantly, RSO demonstrated a potent ability to reduce lipid deposition and enhance antioxidant capacity. These effects may be associated with the downregulation in TG synthesis-related genes and the promotion of reverse cholesterol transport. These findings provide a basis for further evaluation of RSO as a potential dietary lipid source in duck production and as a candidate ingredient for functional feed development.
