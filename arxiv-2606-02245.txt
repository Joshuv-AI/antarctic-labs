When Knowledge Is Not Free: Cost-Aware Evidence Selection in Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Mingyan Wu, Han Yang, Omer Ben-Porat, Yftah Ziser

Abstract
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) typically assumes that external knowledge is free, but many high-quality sources are paywalled, licensed, restricted, or otherwise costly to access. We introduce cost-aware RAG, a setting where retrieved evidence is assigned access-cost tiers and systems must answer under an explicit evidence-access budget. We instantiate this setting by augmenting MS MARCO v2.1 with access-friction tiers and evaluate budgeted evidence selection across general-domain and domain-specific QA benchmarks. Our results show that static selection is brittle: no fixed selector uniformly dominates, and larger budgets do not reliably improve answer quality, even when costly evidence is domain-matched. We then study agentic cost-aware RAG, where an LLM decides when to retrieve, which tier to access, and when to stop. Agents show strong promise as adaptive evidence-acquisition controllers, but their behavior remains highly model- and task-dependent. These findings suggest that cost-aware evidence acquisition is a central challenge for the next generation of RAG systems. All code and data are available at https://github.com/Mignonmy/Cost-Aware .



1
Introduction

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems are usually evaluated as if external knowledge were freely and uniformly accessible. In practice, however, many high-quality sources are restricted, licensed, paywalled, or otherwise costly to access, including academic journals, clinical references, professional databases, licensed code repositories, and proprietary business resources. This makes retrieval not only a relevance-ranking problem, but also a resource-allocation problem: a system must decide which evidence is worth acquiring under a limited access budget. This access cost is distinct from commonly studied RAG constraints such as computation time, context length, or token budgets
6
;
23
;
13
. Those constraints limit how much evidence can be processed once it is available; access costs determine which evidence can be obtained in the first place. Cost-aware RAG therefore requires estimating the value of evidence relative to its cost, rather than optimizing retrieval relevance or generation quality alone.

We study this setting by introducing a cost-aware RAG evaluation framework that augments MS MARCO v2.1 with discrete access-cost tiers. These tiers serve as coarse proxies for access friction, distinguishing freely accessible sources, general open-web sources, and higher-friction professional or restricted sources. As shown in Figure
1
, each evidence item has an access cost, and the system must select evidence under an explicit budget before passing it to the generator. We evaluate this framework on general-domain QA benchmarks, including HotpotQA, Natural Questions (NQ), and TriviaQA, and domain-specific medical QA benchmarks, including MedQA-US and MMLU-Med. For the medical setting, we augment the high-cost tier with domain-matched textbook evidence, allowing us to test whether costly specialized sources help when relevant knowledge is concentrated in higher-friction resources. We compare static evidence-selection strategies, including cost-agnostic top-
k
, relevance-only selection, cost-aware greedy selection, knapsack-based optimization, and redundancy-aware methods.

Our results show that cost-aware RAG does not admit a single fixed selection rule. No static selector uniformly dominates across datasets, budgets, and backbone models, and larger access budgets do not reliably improve answer quality. Since the generator receives a bounded evidence context, increasing the budget mainly changes the composition of the selected evidence, allowing low-cost passages to be replaced by higher-cost ones. Even in the medical setting, where costly evidence is domain-matched, a more expensive evidence mix is not necessarily more useful. These findings motivate an adaptive formulation. Rather than assigning every query the same budget and applying a fixed selector, a cost-aware system should decide during inference whether more evidence is needed, whether higher-cost evidence is worth acquiring, and when the current context is sufficient. We therefore study an agentic formulation of cost-aware RAG, where an LLM acts as an evidence-acquisition controller. Agents can substantially improve the performance–cost tradeoff with some backbones, but their behavior remains model- and task-dependent, showing that current LLMs are still imperfect cost-sensitive evidence controllers.

Contributions.

We make three contributions:
(i) We introduce a cost-aware RAG evaluation setting by augmenting MS MARCO v2.1 with discrete access-cost tiers that model relative access friction.
(ii) We benchmark static budget-constrained evidence-selection strategies across general-domain and domain-specific QA benchmarks, showing that no fixed selector uniformly dominates and that larger access budgets do not reliably improve answer quality.
(iii) We study agentic cost-aware evidence acquisition in per-query and shared-budget settings, showing that agentic frameworks are promising for cost-aware RAG while revealing that current LLMs remain unreliable cost-sensitive evidence controllers.

2.1
Cost-annotated corpus.

We build on the MS MARCO v2.1 document collection
14
, which consists of web-sourced passages with associated queries and source URLs.
We keep the original queries unchanged, and augment each evidence item with an access-cost label inferred from its originating domain. These costs are intended to model relative access friction rather than literal monetary prices. Specifically, each source domain is assigned to a discrete cost tier, summarized in Table
1
, ranging from open community resources to general open-web content and higher-friction curated, professional, or restricted sources. This construction enables budget-constrained retrieval and evaluation while preserving the original MS MARCO evidence and supervision.

2.2
Domain distribution analysis.

We first analyze the source-domain distribution of the MS MARCO v2.1 segment corpus. The corpus contains 113,520,750 segments from 1,035,806 unique domains, and the distribution is highly skewed. The most frequent domain,
en.wikipedia.org
, contributes 7,006,110 segments, corresponding to 6.17% of the corpus, while the top 30 domains together account for 19,178,928 segments, or 16.89% of all segments. This skew motivates a domain-level annotation strategy: a relatively small number of high-coverage domains accounts for a substantial fraction of the corpus, while most domains occur only in the long tail.

2.3
LLM-based domain classification.

Since manually labeling more than one million domains is impractical, we annotate the highest-coverage domains using an LLM-as-judge procedure. We select the top 20,000 domains by segment count, which together cover 82,604,646 segments, or 72.77% of the full corpus. Given the tier definitions in Table
1
, GPT-4.1-mini assigns each domain to an access-friction tier based on the domain identity and its likely accessibility characteristics. To avoid forcing arbitrary assignments, the model is also allowed to abstain when the domain cannot be confidently classified; abstentions are rare in practice (around 0.3%). The initial annotation used a four-way rubric separating curated or professional sources from high-friction restricted sources. Under this rubric, the top 20,000 domains were classified as follows: 1,904 domains in Tier 0 (9.52%), 14,350 in Tier 1 (71.75%), 2,715 in Tier 2 (13.58%), and 1,031 in Tier 3 (5.16%). Because the two highest-friction classes are relatively sparse, we merge the original Tiers 2 and 3 into the final Tier 2 used in our experiments. This yields a three-tier structure that preserves the distinction between free, low-cost, and costly evidence while making the highest-cost tier sufficiently populated for evaluation.

2.4
Long-tail imputation and final tier distribution.

The remaining domains form a large long tail with low individual coverage. Rather than querying the LLM for every rare domain, we impute their tiers by sampling from the empirical tier proportions observed among the annotated top 20,000 domains. After propagating domain labels to all segments and merging the two highest-friction tiers, the final MS MARCO-only corpus contains 36,547,305 Tier 0 segments (32.19%), 62,632,643 Tier 1 segments (55.17%), and 14,340,802 Tier 2 segments (12.64%), as shown in Table
2
. In all budgeted retrieval experiments, these tiers are mapped to costs of 0, 1, and 4, respectively.

3.3
Budgeted Retrieval Protocol

For each query
q
, we retrieve a candidate set of passages and select a subset to provide to the generator under an evidence-access budget
B
. Each candidate passage
d_{i}
is associated with a relevance score
v_{i}
, estimated by the similarity score between the passage and the query, and an access cost
c_{i}
, determined by its source cost tier. Unless otherwise stated, the generator receives at most 30 pieces of evidence per question. When the budget is exhausted before reaching this evidence limit, the remaining context slots can be filled with the highest-ranked Tier-0 evidence, since these passages incur no additional access cost. We evaluate answer quality as a function of the available budget, measuring how different selection strategies trade off relevance, cost, and redundancy.

3.4
Baselines and Evidence Selection Methods

Baselines

We compare cost-aware selection against two standard baselines. The
vanilla LLM
baseline answers without retrieved evidence. The
top-
k
baseline provides the generator with the top-
k
retrieved passages, ignoring access costs. These baselines separate the effect of retrieval itself from the effect of budgeted evidence selection.

Budgeted selectors

We evaluate five budgeted evidence-selection methods:

•

Relevance-only
ranks passages by relevance and selects the highest-scoring passages that fit within the budget.

•

Greedy cost-aware
ranks passages by cost-normalized relevance, prioritizing high predicted utility per unit cost.

•

Knapsack
formulates selection as a 0/1 knapsack problem:

\max_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{i}v_{i}x_{i}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{i}c_{i}x_{i}\leq B,

(1)

where
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether passage
d_{i}
is selected. This directly maximizes predicted relevance under the budget constraint, while assuming independent passage utilities.

•

Redundancy-aware knapsack
extends the knapsack objective with a pairwise redundancy penalty:

\max_{\mathbf{x}}\sum_{i}v_{i}x_{i}-\eta\sum_{i<j}s_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{i}c_{i}x_{i}\leq B,

(2)

where
s_{ij}
denotes the similarity between passages and
\eta
controls the strength of the redundancy penalty. This encourages the selector to spend budget on complementary rather than near-duplicate evidence. The
\eta
is 0.2.

•

Maximum Marginal Relevance (MMR) with cost penalty
performs sequential set-aware selection. Given the selected set
S
, each remaining passage is scored as

m_{i}(S)=\alpha r(q,d_{i})-(1-\alpha)\max_{d_{j}\in S}s(d_{i},d_{j})-\gamma c_{i},

(3)

where
r(q,d_{i})
is the query–passage relevance score,
s(d_{i},d_{j})
is passage similarity, and
\gamma
controls the cost penalty.
\alpha
is 0.7 and
\gamma
is 0.1.
This method balances relevance, diversity, and access cost at each selection step.

4
Results

We first ask whether cost-aware evidence selection can be solved by a fixed budgeted selector. The results suggest not: across datasets, budgets, and backbone models, no single rule consistently dominates, and increasing the evidence-access budget often fails to improve performance. These findings motivate a more adaptive formulation, where the system decides how much to spend and which evidence tier to access on a per-query basis.

No fixed selector uniformly dominates.

Figure
2
shows that the best selector depends strongly on the dataset, budget, and backbone model. With Qwen3-8B, Greedy is best on HotpotQA and NQ, reaching 40.90 and 45.19 F1 at
B=15
, while Knapsack is best on TriviaQA and Redundancy on MedQA. On MMLU, several methods reach the best score of 84.00 at different budgets. The pattern changes with Llama3.1-8B-Instruct: Relevance is best on HotpotQA and TriviaQA, Knapsack on NQ, and Redundancy on MedQA. Thus, the relative ranking of selectors is unstable: the same objective can be competitive in one setting and suboptimal in another.

More budget is not necessarily better.

Since the generator receives a fixed evidence window, increasing
B
mainly changes the composition of the selected evidence rather than the amount of context. The relevant question is therefore whether a more expensive evidence mix improves answer quality. It does not reliably do so. Across the five selectors and five datasets, none of the 25 Qwen3-8B curves is monotonically non-decreasing over budgets
\{0,5,10,15,20,25,30\}
, and the same holds for Llama3.1-8B-Instruct. Moreover, only 6 out of 25 Qwen3-8B curves and 4 out of 25 Llama3.1-8B-Instruct curves achieve their best finite-budget score at
B=30
. This remains true even in the domain-specific benchmarks, where the high-cost tier includes domain-matched medical evidence. With Qwen3-8B on MMLU-Med, Relevance reaches 84.00 F1 at
B=5
but drops to 79.00 at
B=30
, while Greedy reaches 84.00 at
B=10
and
B=25
but drops to 78.00 at
B=30
. On MedQA, Relevance and Knapsack also peak before the largest budget. Thus, even when costly evidence is plausibly useful, simply allowing more of it into the context is not enough.

Budget changes evidence composition differently across domains.

Figure
3
helps explain the non-monotonic trends. In the open-domain setting, represented by NQ, larger budgets do not necessarily lead to substantially greater use of the highest-cost tier; many methods continue to rely on lower-cost evidence. This suggests that general-knowledge questions can often be answered from open or low-cost sources. In contrast, for MedQA, where the costly tier contains domain-specialized medical evidence, higher budgets often lead selectors to use more high-cost evidence. The value of expensive evidence is therefore task-dependent: conservative policies may miss useful specialized evidence, while aggressive policies may waste budget when low-cost evidence is sufficient.

Reducing the number of evidence passages is not sufficient.

As shown in Table
12
and
13
,
the cost-agnostic top-
k
baseline controls for context size by providing only the
k
highest-ranked passages, without considering access cost. If the weakness of fixed selectors were simply that they include too many passages, then some fixed
k
should perform consistently well. The results do not support this explanation. Top-
k
sometimes benefits from using fewer than 30 passages, but no value of
k
is consistently strong across datasets or models. With Qwen3-8B, the best top-
k
scores on HotpotQA and NQ are 37.67 and 40.09 F1, below the best budgeted-selector scores of 40.90 and 45.19 F1. With Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, top-
k
also falls short on HotpotQA and NQ. Thus, shorter contexts alone do not explain the gains; the harder problem is deciding which evidence is worth including and from which cost tier.

Implication: cost-aware RAG requires adaptive allocation.

Taken together, these results show that cost-aware evidence selection is difficult to reduce to a single fixed rule. The best selector varies across datasets, models, and budgets; larger budgets do not reliably help; and the usefulness of costly evidence depends on the domain and query. This motivates a sequential formulation in which the system assesses the evidence already acquired, decides whether it is sufficient, and determines whether additional or higher-cost evidence is worth the expense. We therefore turn to an agentic formulation of cost-aware RAG, where evidence acquisition is treated as an online decision process: the model must decide not only what to retrieve, but also when to spend, where to spend, and when to stop.

Agentic Cost-Aware Evidence Acquisition

The results in Section
4
suggest that fixed budgeted selectors are too brittle for cost-aware RAG. We therefore study an LLM-based retrieval agent as a testbed for adaptive evidence acquisition. At inference time, the agent must decide whether to retrieve, which cost tier to access, and when the accumulated evidence is sufficient to answer. This setting is more flexible than static selection, but it also introduces new failure modes: the agent may stop prematurely, spend too aggressively, or retrieve from an inappropriate tier. We therefore view agentic RAG not as a complete solution, but as a probe of whether current LLMs can act as cost-sensitive evidence controllers.

Design.

We instantiate the agent with a ReAct-style loop
22
. At each step, the agent observes the question, collected evidence, remaining budget, and available tiers with their costs. It then chooses between two actions:
Retrieve
, which specifies a tier and returns one passage from that tier ranked by query similarity, or
Answer
, which terminates the loop (see Figure
4
). The agent uses the same corpus, retriever, and cost tiers as the fixed-selector experiments; only the acquisition policy changes. The prompt instructs the agent to obey the budget and answer only when the evidence is sufficient or no useful retrieval is affordable. The full prompt is shown in Table
4
. Unless otherwise stated, we use a per-query budget of
B=20
with the same two backbones as before, Qwen3-8B and Llama3.1-8B-Instruct. The budget is an upper bound; we report the average realized cost. We also evaluate a
shared-budget
variant in which the agent receives batches of five questions and allocates a total budget of 100 before answering. This diagnostic setting tests whether the model can identify, at the workload level, which questions are likely to require costly evidence. Additional details are provided in Appendix

.

Per-query agents can be efficient evidence controllers.

Figure
5
compares per-query agents with fixed budgeted selectors. The main result is that agentic acquisition can often match or exceed fixed selectors while using far fewer passages and much lower realized cost. This improvement is not explained by shorter contexts alone: as shown in Section
4
, cost-agnostic top-
k
selection does not consistently perform well. The clearest gains come from Qwen3-8B. On NQ, the agent reaches 49.93 F1 with only 0.50 average cost and 2.47 retrieved passages, outperforming all fixed selectors while spending far less than the high-budget settings. On TriviaQA, it reaches 66.73 F1 with 0.52 cost and 2.60 passages, and on MMLU-Med it reaches 85.00 F1 with 2.90 cost and only 1.14 passages. On MedQA, it obtains 68.00 F1 with 5.08 cost and 1.28 passages, competitive with the best fixed selectors despite using a much smaller evidence context. These results suggest that the agent is not simply retrieving more evidence; rather, it often stops after acquiring a small amount of useful evidence.

Agentic cost awareness is model- and task-dependent.

Agentic control is not automatically cost-aware. HotpotQA remains difficult: with Qwen3-8B and
B=20
, the agent reaches only 30.34 F1, below the best fixed selector. This suggests that multi-hop questions may require more than repeated retrieval with the original query, such as query decomposition, sub-question planning, or targeted query rewriting.
Backbone choice also matters
. Llama3.1-8B-Instruct can achieve competitive scores, but often by spending much more. With
B=20
, it reaches 46.77 F1 on NQ using 13.47 average cost and 12.54 passages, and 68.40 F1 on TriviaQA using 11.53 cost and 11.53 passages. On MedQA, it reaches 60.00 F1 with 18.39 cost, and on MMLU-Med it reaches only 72.00 F1, substantially below the best fixed selectors. In contrast to Qwen3-8B, the Llama agent often treats the available budget as a resource to consume rather than a constraint to manage. Thus, the key challenge is not only choosing the right tier, but also estimating when the current evidence is sufficient.

Budget sensitivity reveals evidence-sufficiency failures.

A cost-aware agent should not spend more merely because more budget is available. We therefore vary the per-query budget on NQ and MedQA, covering both open-domain and domain-specific settings. Figure
6
shows that increasing the budget has limited effect on final F1, but substantially changes spending behaviour. Qwen3-8B exhibits relatively stable stopping behaviour. Increasing the budget from
B=20
to
B=30
changes its average cost from 0.50 to 1.06 on NQ and from 5.08 to 4.72 on MedQA, while F1 changes from 49.93 to 45.20 and from 68.00 to 69.00. In contrast, Llama3.1-8B-Instruct spends much more as the budget increases, without corresponding gains: from
B=20
to
B=30
, its cost rises from 13.47 to 15.54 on NQ and from 18.39 to 27.01 on MedQA, while F1 drops from 46.77 to 42.89 and from 60.00 to 56.00. These trends indicate that evidence-sufficiency estimation is a central bottleneck for agentic cost-aware RAG. Representative trajectories are provided in Appendix
C

and a corresponding case study comparing this behaviour across both models is presented in Appendix
D
.

Shared-budget allocation probes workload-level planning.

We next evaluate shared-budget allocation as a diagnostic for workload-level
planning. Unlike the per-query setting, the agent receives a batch of questions
and must decide in advance which ones deserve more budget. For Qwen3-8B, this
improves the performance–cost tradeoff on most datasets: compared with the
per-query
B=20
agent, it reduces cost and passage usage on HotpotQA,
TriviaQA, MedQA, and MMLU-Med while improving or maintaining F1. For example,
TriviaQA improves from 66.73 to 70.27 F1 while cost drops from 0.52 to 0.33;
MedQA improves from 68.00 to 71.00 F1 while cost drops from 5.08 to 4.38; and
MMLU-Med maintains 85.00 F1 while reducing cost from 2.90 to 2.56. However,
the benefit is not uniform: on NQ, Qwen spends more while performing worse, and for Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, lower cost and passage usage often come with lower
F1. Thus, shared-budget allocation can help when the model estimates question
difficulty well, but it also introduces a new failure mode: the agent must
predict before retrieval which questions are worth spending on.

Takeaway.

Agentic frameworks naturally fit cost-aware RAG: each retrieval decision can be
conditioned on the question, the evidence collected so far, and the remaining
budget. This adaptivity can improve the performance–cost tradeoff when the
model recognizes that a small amount of evidence is sufficient. However,
current agents remain unreliable cost-sensitive controllers: their behaviour
varies with the backbone, task structure, and evidence-sufficiency judgments.
Cost-aware RAG therefore highlights a capability underexplored in standard RAG:
deciding whether additional evidence is worth its access cost.

Most RAG work assumes that retrieved evidence is freely accessible

7
Conclusion

We introduced cost-aware RAG, where evidence carries source-dependent access
costs and retrieval becomes a budgeted acquisition problem. Across open-domain
and domain-specific QA, fixed selectors are brittle: no rule consistently
dominates, and higher budgets do not reliably improve answer quality. Agentic
RAG offers a promising alternative by deciding when to retrieve, which tier to
access, and when to stop, but current agents remain highly model- and
task-dependent.

8
Limitations

Simulated access costs.

Our access-cost tiers are proxies for relative access friction rather than direct measurements of monetary cost, licensing restrictions, latency, or institutional access. We assign costs at the domain level and map them to a small number of discrete tiers, which enables large-scale evaluation but cannot capture variation within the same source.

Automatic tier annotation.

Our cost annotations rely on automatic domain classification and long-tail imputation. The LLM judge may misclassify some domains, and imputed labels for rare domains may not reflect their true access properties. These errors could affect the apparent value of high-cost evidence and the behavior of cost-aware selectors.

Evaluation scope.

Our evaluation is limited to five QA benchmarks and sampled subsets. This allows controlled comparisons across selectors, budgets, and backbones, but leaves open how stable the conclusions are on larger samples, additional domains, and different answer formats. The medical benchmarks should be viewed as domain-specific QA testbeds rather than evidence of clinical readiness.

Simplified retrieval environment.

The agent retrieves from a fixed candidate pool using the original question and receives one passage at a time from a selected tier. It does not perform query rewriting, sub-question decomposition, source verification, or multi-query search, which likely limits performance on multi-hop questions.

Zero-shot agent control.

Our agentic controller is evaluated in a zero-shot prompting regime with two open-weight backbones. The results show that agentic cost-aware RAG can be efficient, but stopping and spending behavior remains highly model-dependent. We therefore view the agentic results as a diagnostic of current LLMs as cost-sensitive evidence controllers, rather than as a fully optimized policy.

We evaluate performance using Token-Level F1 (F1) and Exact Match (EM).
While the F1 score measures the trade-off between coverage and redundancy by allowing partial matches, EM imposes a stricter criterion, requiring the model’s output to perfectly match the reference answer.
If the benchmark provides multiple reference answers, we adopt the highest score.
For each benchmark and method, token-level F1 is first computed for each question and then averaged over the 100 sampled questions. We compute the 95% confidence interval for the mean F1 using a normal approximation, where the standard error is estimated from the sample variance of question-level F1 scores.
Values in the tables are reported as the mean F1

Case Studies: Agents’ Cost-aware Decision Making

We illustrate how the agent processes tasks. In the first case, as shown in Table
6
,
the agent first identifies that the question requires external evidence about a specific historical election and therefore begins with tier 0, the free retrieval source. When the first retrieved passage fails to provide information about the target year,
it continues retrieving from tier 0, since the needed fact is likely available in encyclopedic sources. The second retrieval provides sufficient evidence that Eliot Cutler finished ahead of Libby Mitchell in Maine’s 2010 gubernatorial election, allowing the agent to answer correctly.
This example shows that the agent performs tier-aware evidence acquisition. It keeps retrieving when evidence is insufficient, avoids unnecessarily expensive sources, and reaches the correct answer.

In the second case, as shown in Table
7
, the agent handles a question that requires specific and up-to-date factual evidence about Mount Ruapehu’s most recent eruption.
The agent first retrieves from tier 0, since the question asks for a factual answer that may be available in free community resources.
However, the initial passages only provide historical information, rather than the exact date of the latest eruption.
After multiple unsuccessful attempts from tier 0 and tier 1, the agent decides to explore a higher-cost tier, since cheaper sources have not yet provided the required evidence and the remaining budget is still sufficient. Although the tier 2 retrieval also fails to directly answer the question, the agent does not stop prematurely. Instead, it continues retrieval and eventually finds a useful tier 0 passage that explicitly states the last eruption date as 25 September 2007.
This case shows that the agent can adaptively balance cost and evidence quality.
It incurs a total cost of 7 and switches across all three tiers, but eventually retrieves the necessary passage and stops retrieving once sufficient evidence is found.

In the third case, as shown in Table
8
, the agent handles a domain-specific medical question and correctly identifies tier 2 as the appropriate source for professional medical knowledge. Since the retrieved passages do not fully specify the protocol for this situation, the agent remains uncertain and continues searching until the budget is exhausted. With no remaining budget, the agent then combines the incomplete evidence with its own parametric knowledge and reasoning to make a correct final decision.
This case shows that the agent can identify the appropriate knowledge tier, understand the trade-off between task difficulty and budget, and continue evidence acquisition when confidence is insufficient.

We further demonstrate how the agent performs flexible budget allocation under the shared-budget setting. As shown in Table
9
, the agent first estimates the domain, difficulty, and likely retrieval tier of each question. It assigns smaller budgets to questions that can likely be answered by public or low-cost sources, while reserving larger budgets for questions that require more specific historical or scientific knowledge. The agent then iteratively adjusts the allocation until the total budget exactly matches the shared budget constraint. This case shows that the agent can reason about the relative difficulty of multiple questions and distribute the available budget accordingly.

We further compare cost-aware behaviours across different backbone models. As shown in Table
10
and Table
11
, both agents identify the question as requiring domain-specific medical knowledge and therefore select tier 2 for retrieval. However, they differ substantially in when to stop retrieving. The agent with Qwen3-8B retrieves only one tier 2 passage, recognizes that the evidence is sufficient, and stops with a total cost of 4.
In contrast, the agent with Llama3.1-8B-Instruct repeatedly retrieves from tier 2 despite already having relevant evidence, and only answers after exhausting the full budget of 40.
This comparison suggests that cost-aware behaviour is strongly influenced by the backbone model.
