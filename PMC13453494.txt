Introduction

Syringa oblata Lindl. (S. oblata), a plant of the Syringa genus in the Oleaceae family, is widely cultivated in northern China, Japan, and Korea [1], [2]. In Harbin, China, S. oblata has been designated as the city flower, with over 1.3 million trees planted. The S. oblata leaves are often consumed as tea with economic value and possess potential economic and health-promoting value. [1]. However, current research has primarily focused on its flowers and essential oils [3], [4], [5], with limited attention given to the development and utilization of S. oblata leaves. Although S. oblata leaves are abundant, they are often regarded as agricultural by-products, resulting in the underutilization of valuable biomass resources and potential environmental burdens. The leaves are rich in various bioactive constituents, including lignans, iridoids, triterpenoids, and flavonoids [6], [7], and recent studies have shown that the leaves possess antibacterial, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective activities [1], [8]. However, polysaccharides from S. oblata leaves remain largely underexplored and underutilized.

Natural polysaccharides are a class of biomacromolecules widely distributed in plants, fungi, algae, and microorganisms [9]. Owing to the structural diversity, biocompatibility, biodegradability, and modifiable functional groups, polysaccharides have attracted broad attention in biomedical research, drug delivery, biosensing, environmental remediation, and the development of functional biomacromolecular materials [10], [11], [12], [13]. For instance, Wen et al. developed Enteromorpha polysaccharides-based hydrogels that could efficiently adsorb heavy metal ions [14]. Zhu et al. developed polysaccharide-based film prepared by using persimmon polysaccharides and sodium alginate with the synthesized silver nanoparticles, had excellent antibacterial activities against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Aspergillus niger [15]. In addition, polysaccharide-derived hydrogels, prepared either from polysaccharides alone or by integrating them with synthetic polymers, have emerged as promising materials for wastewater remediation [16], [17].

Efficient extraction of polysaccharides while preserving their functionality has become a critical focus of current research. Hot water extraction is still the most widely used method. However, it is often constrained by low efficiency, prolonged extraction time, and high energy consumption [18]. In plant tissues, polysaccharides are closely associated with cell wall components such as cellulose, hemicellulose, proteins, and other cell wall components, forming a complex and dense network that acts as a natural barrier, thereby restricting their release during conventional extraction [19], [20]. Therefore, it is necessary to explore more efficient extraction methods and suitable green solvent systems for polysaccharide recovery.

Due to the strong protective capacity of NADES on the molecular structure of polysaccharides, it is beneficial for maintaining the products original physiological and antioxidant activities, and enhancing functionality [21]. For instance, Cao et al. reported that the yield of Lycium barbarum polysaccharides obtained using DESs was 2.4 times higher than that from water extraction. Moreover, at the same concentration, LBPs-D also showed better performance than LBPs-W in promoting apoptosis recovery, together with higher SOD and T-AOC activities, indicating stronger antioxidant potential [22]. Similar results were also reported by Yao et al., further confirming the bioactivity-preserving advantages of DES extraction [23]. Therefore, employing NADES for polysaccharide extraction not only aligns with the principles of green chemistry but also offers a promising strategy for the high-value utilization of functional polysaccharides.

The extraction yield of polysaccharides is strongly influenced by process parameters, and the relationships among these factors are often nonlinear and complex. As a result, traditional linear models may not perform well when multiple variables are involved. In recent years, the advancement of artificial intelligence, particularly machine learning (ML), has been increasingly applied to address this problem. ML has shown great potential in fields such as biomanufacturing, waste valorization, materials science, drug discovery, and pollutant treatment [24], [25], [26]. Multiple models, including linear regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), have been widely applied in energy and environmental monitoring, food safety and quality control, and materials chemistry. For example, Hu et al. employed nonlinear predictive control combined with ML to achieve sustainable crop production, increasing the utilization efficiency of renewable energy from 4.7% to 127.5% [27]. Kandpal et al. used decision tree (DT) model to predict the biochar yield and higher heating value generated from biomass pyrolysis, achieving an R2 of 0.86 on the test set, and further applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to evaluate the contribution of individual feature to the prediction results [28]. Sun et al. applied an RF model for data-driven optimization of cellulose and hemicellulose transformation into high-value chemicals [29]. These studies demonstrate that ML not only improves predictive accuracy but also identifies key influencing factors through interpretability analysis, providing new insights for the efficient extraction of natural products and the high-value utilization of resources.

To address the growing demand for the green, economically viable, and environmentally sustainable utilization of polysaccharide resources, this study proposes a data-driven strategy for eco-friendly polysaccharide extraction and efficient resource utilization (Fig. 1). Exemplified by polysaccharides of S. oblata leaf residues, NADESs in combination with ultrasound-assisted extraction were employed, and the performance of various ML models in predicting S. oblata leaf polysaccharides yield was systematically evaluated. SHAP analysis was applied to achieve both global and local interpretability, thereby elucidating key extraction parameters and their interactions influencing the yield. This approach not only enhanced extraction efficiency and preserved the functional properties of polysaccharides but also offered a feasible pathway for the high-value utilization of underutilized plant biomass and waste materials. Extraction conditions were further optimized based on model predictions, validating the high predictive accuracy and practical applicability of the integrated ML model in polysaccharide extraction processes. Furthermore, the structural characteristics and biological activities of S. oblata leaf polysaccharides were investigated, and DFT was employed to provide molecular-level insights into the extraction mechanism. With ongoing technological advancements, this green extraction strategy holds great promise for broad application in the comprehensive utilization of agricultural by-products, the circular economy, and sustainable manufacturing, thereby advancing functional polysaccharide research and industrialization.

Materials and methods

Choline chloride, 1,3-propandiol, isopropanol, and FeSO4·7H2O were purchased from Beijing InnoChem Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Citric acid, carbamide, and n-butanol were purchased from Tianjin Tianli Chemical Reagents Ltd (Tianjin, China). 1,4-Butanediol and 2,2′-Azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Ultrasound-assisted NADESs extraction of polysaccharides from S. Oblata leaf residue

The S. oblata leaf residue obtained from previous ethanol extraction in our lab were dried at 40 °C and subsequently subjected to NADES-based polysaccharide extraction. To screen the NADES solvents, polysaccharide extraction was performed under identical conditions using different NADESs, and the NADES affording the highest polysaccharide yield was selected. Total sugar content was measured using the phenol–sulfuric acid method [31]. Subsequently, the polysaccharides extracted with the optimal solvent (NADES-6) were further purified and characterized. After ethanol precipitation and lyophilization, the polysaccharides were purified using the sevag method, followed by dialysis and freeze-drying, to obtain purified S. oblata leaf residue polysaccharide (SOLP).

Dataset Construction

By varying the input variables, including NADES to S. oblata leaf residue ratio (DSR) (mL/g), NADES water content (%), ultrasonic time (min), and ultrasonic power (W), experimental data were collected from NADES-based extraction of S. oblata leaf residue polysaccharides. A dataset was subsequently constructed, in which the polysaccharide yield served as the target variable. The primary dataset was established based on a total of 238 data points.

Predicting polysaccharide yield using machine learning models

First, the pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) was calculated to evaluate the correlations among the input variables and to assess potential multicollinearity, thereby identifying key influencing factors. Subsequently, multiple ML models were constructed using Python (scikit-learn), including LR, SVM, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), DT, RF, and XGBoost, to predict polysaccharide yield. Model performance was visualized using matplotlib to illustrate the fitting and generalization performance on both training and test sets. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%). In addition, SHAP analysis was employed to provide interpret the model predictions and to quantify the contributions of individual features to the outputs.

Characterization of NADES

The FT-IR spectra of choline chloride, ethanedioic acid, and NADES-6 were recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with KBr pressing tablet method using an FTIR-8400S spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan).

The 1H NMR spectra of choline chloride, ethanedioic acid, and NADES-6 were recorded on a Bruker-Avance II spectrometer (400 MHz).

UV and FT-IR spectra analysis

The UV–Vis spectrum of SOLP was recorded in the range of 200–400 nm using a UV-1601 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The FT-IR Spectrum of SOLP was recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with KBr pressing tablet method using an FTIR-8400S spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan).

Dynamic light scattering analysis and Congo red test

The particle size and zeta-potential of SOLP were analyzed with dynamic light scattering (DLS) at room temperature [26]. The triple-helix conformation of SOLP was evaluated using a Congo red assay [32]. Briefly, SOLP was dissolved in distilled water to obtain a final concentration of 1 mg/mL and mixed thoroughly with Congo red solution. Subsequently, the mixture was then treated with NaOH solutions at varying concentrations (0–0.5 mol/L). After incubation at room temperature, the maximum absorption wavelength of the reaction system was recorded.

Thermal analysis

The thermal stability of SOLP was determined with a Mettler TGA2 thermogravimetric analyzer, and the TGA and DTG curves of SOLP were obtained [33].

Rheological analysis

The rheological characteristic of SOLP was determined with TA DHR 10 hybrid rheometer, equipped with a cone-plate (40 mm diameter, 1 mm gap, 1◦ angle) at 25 ℃ [33]. The viscosity and conductivity of NADES-6 (51.4% water content) and the corresponding simple aqueous solutions (51.4% water content) are determined with TA DHR 10 hybrid rheometer (at shear rate of 1 s−1) and TDS&EC meter, respectively.

Monosaccharide composition analysis

The monosaccharide composition analysis of SOLP was performed according to previously method with slight modifications [31]. Briefly, SOLP was hydrolyzed with 3 mol/L TFA, then reacted with PMP and NaOH solution at 70 °C for 1 h, and subsequently neutralized with HCl. The resulting derivatives were analyzed using HPLC-DAD equipped with a Cosmosil 5C18-PAQ column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) at 30 °C, with a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min and detection at 245 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 83% acetonitrile and 17% (0.08 mol/L) phosphate buffer (pH 6.85).

Scanning electron microscopy analysis of SOLP

The surface morphology of SOLP was observed using SU8000 scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Hitachi, Ltd, Tokyo Metropolis, Japan).

Results and discussion

Screening of NADES

Plant polysaccharides are tightly bound within the complex matrix of the primary cell wall, which is composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, lignin, proteins, and other non-polysaccharide substances. This intricate structure limits the efficiency of conventional hot water extraction methods [19]. Acidic or alkaline solvents can interfere with the hydrogen bonds between polysaccharides and cell wall components, thereby facilitating polysaccharide release [38], [39]. However, harsh acidic or alkaline conditions may result in glycosidic bond hydrolysis and may also lead to environmental pollution [40].

To address these limitations, NADES, characterized by high safety and biocompatibility, were introduced in this study as alternative green extraction media. A total of 12 different NADESs were designed (Table 1). The physicochemical properties of NADESs largely depend on the types and molar ratios of HBAs and HBDs. Differences in viscosity, swelling behavior, charge polarity, and hydrogen-bonding interactions among various NADES systems result in distinct extraction efficiencies, providing high flexibility in solvent design [19]. Under the same extraction conditions, this study compared the extraction yields obtained using 12 NADESs. The results showed that NADES-6, composed of choline chloride and ethanedioic acid at a molar ratio of 1:2, produced the highest extraction yield of S. oblata leaf residue polysaccharides (Fig. S1). Compared with NADESs with near-neutral pH values and alcohol-based NADESs exhibiting relatively alkaline properties, acidic NADESs generally produced higher yield. The acidic NADESs may promote the dissolution and release of acidic polysaccharides by weakening the hydrogen-bonding network in the plant cell wall and potentially chelating metal ions [41]. For NADES-6, the abundant hydrogen-bond network of the system may facilitate strong interactions between NADES components and polysaccharide hydroxyl groups [42]. These interactions may replace the original hydrogen bonds between polysaccharide chains, thereby weakening intermolecular interactions. As these hydrogen bonds are disrupted, the originally compact polysaccharide aggregates gradually become loosened, reducing intermolecular cohesion and facilitating the release of polysaccharides from the plant cell matrix [43]. In addition, the NADES may also disrupt the cell wall matrix, and its strong hydrogen-bonding capability may endow it with high solvation capacity, which is beneficial for polysaccharide solubility and diffusion efficiency [19]. Therefore, the NADES-6 system was selected as an experimental acidic NADES system for subsequent machine learning modeling, extraction process optimization, structural characterization, and mechanism investigation.

Characterization of NADES

FT-IR analysis

FT-IR was employed to analyze the characteristic absorption bands and hydrogen-bond formation of NADES-6. As shown in Fig. 2B, compared with pure choline chloride (HBA) and ethanedioic acid (HBD), several new characteristic peaks appeared in the FT-IR spectrum of NADES-6. The absorption bands at 3600–3000 cm−1 were assigned to –OH stretching vibrations [44]. The observed blue shift of peaks relative to the individual components indicates changes in the hydrogen-bonding environment and thereby confirming the formation of hydrogen bonds within the NADES system [45].

1H NMR analysis

Hydrogen bonding can alter the chemical environment of hydrogen atoms and consequently affect their chemical shifts in the 1H NMR spectrum. Thus, 1H NMR was applied to investigate the molecular structure and intermolecular interactions within the NADES. As shown in Fig. 2C, no new peaks were detected in the 1H NMR spectrum of NADES-6, indicating that its formation was dominated by physical process rather than chemical reaction, which is consistent with the general formation mechanism of NADES. In addition, slight shift of signal compared with the individual components further confirmed the occurrence of hydrogen-bond interactions, supporting the synthesis of NADES [44].

Prediction of S. Oblata leaf residue polysaccharide yield using machine learning models

PCC analysis

In the extraction of polysaccharides using NADESs, the DSR, water content, ultrasonic power, and ultrasonic time were the key parameters affecting polysaccharide yield. Additionally, these variables served as key input features for ML models, which facilitated a deeper understanding of yield variations and their underlying mechanisms. The data obtained under different extraction conditions were analyzed in relation to the four variables. In multivariate analysis, the PCC analysis is commonly used to assess the linear correlation between two variables, with values ranging from −1 to 1. A PCC value close to 1 indicates a strong positive correlation, whereas a value close to −1 reflects a strong negative correlation. The value near 0 suggests a weak or no linear correlation. In this study, an absolute PCC value above 0.6 was regarded as a strong correlation. As shown in Fig. 3, the PCC between DSR and water content was 0.78, indicating a strong positive relationship between these two variables.

Predicting polysaccharide content with machine learning models

In this study, multiple ML models, including LR, SVM, LightGBM, DT, RF, and XGBoost were employed to predict the polysaccharide yield from S. oblata leaf residue and to evaluate the predictive performance of each model. The model performance was intuitively assessed by comparing predicted values with actual values. As shown in Fig. 4 and Fig. 5, the comparison between predicted and actual polysaccharide yields clearly reflects the predictive accuracy of the models. Except for the LR (R2 = 0.581) and SVM (R2 = 0.658), all other models achieved higher R2 values, which may be attributed to the significant nonlinear characteristics of the dataset that could not be effectively captured by linear methods. The LightGBM, DT, RF, and XGBoost models significantly improved prediction accuracy (R2 > 0.80), further supporting the existence of nonlinear relationships among the variables. Moreover, the R2 values of DT, RF, and XGBoost models in both the training and test sets exceeded 0.90 (RMSE < 0.45), with only minor differences between them, indicating good generalization ability. The R2 values of all the models are summarized in Table 2. The optimal extraction conditions predicted by the models were as follows: NADES water content of 51.4%, DSR of 42.9 mL/g, ultrasonic time of 41.7 min, and ultrasonic power of 250 W. And the predicted yield of 45.52%. Among the nonlinear models, DT, RF, and XGBoost outperformed LightGBM, with XGBoost demonstrating the best overall predictive performance.

From the perspective of the extraction mechanism, water content is an important factor affecting the extraction efficiency of NADES systems. Previous studies have shown that the high viscosity of NADESs is one of the major obstacles limiting their extraction applications because it restricts mass transfer between the sample matrix and the extraction phase. This high viscosity is generally associated with the extensive hydrogen-bonding network formed between the HBA and HBD components. The addition of an appropriate amount of water can decrease the viscosity of the extraction medium, enhance mass transfer, and improve extraction efficiency [46]. However, excessive water may also competitively participate in hydrogen-bonding interactions, thereby weakening the original HBA-HBD hydrogen-bonding network. Therefore, based on the optimization results and the water content, the 51.4% water content in this study may reflect a comprehensive balance among NADES component interactions, system viscosity, and ultrasound-assisted mass transfer efficiency.

To further investigate this issue, this study measured the viscosity and conductivity of the NADES-6 system containing 51.4% water and compared it with simple aqueous solution of its individual components. As shown in Fig. S2, the NADES-6 system with water content of 51.4% still showed marked differences in viscosity and conductivity compared with simple aqueous solution of its individual components. These results indicate that NADES-6 with 51.4% water did not behave merely as a simple aqueous solution of its individual components, but still retained certain NADES physicochemical characteristics. The extraction performance of this system may result from the combined effects of interactions among NADES components, regulation of viscosity by water molecules, and ultrasound-enhanced mass transfer and cell-wall disruption. In addition, previous studies have shown that NADES systems with water contents above 50% have also been used for natural product extraction [47], [48], [49], [50].

Assessment of input variable importance

SHAP analysis enables intuitive quantification of importance of input variables by calculating the marginal contribution of each feature during prediction. To reveal the differing roles of input features in prediction, SHAP analysis was employed to evaluate feature importance. As shown in Fig. 6A-C, the feature ranking results from the RF, DT, and XGBoost models consistently indicated that DSR ranked first among all variables, which is consistent with the PCC analysis results. In the XGBoost model, the contributions of DSR, NADES water content, ultrasonic power, and ultrasonic time were 58.57%, 21.31%, 7.29%, and 12.82%, respectively. The markedly higher importance of DSR compared with other variables suggests that polysaccharide yield was predominantly depended on the DSR. It is noteworthy that SHAP global analysis primarily highlights feature importance ranking and variable interactions but may be limited in reflecting sample-specific nonlinear patterns and higher-order interactions. Therefore, SHAP local analysis (Fig. 6D) was further applied to assess both the relative importance of variables and the directional influence of each variable on the model output. The x-axis represents SHAP values, the color scale indicates feature values, and each row represents to an input variable. The results showed that DSR was in the upper region of the density scatter plot, indicating that it had the dominant influence on the system. Moreover, DSR feature values were generally positively correlated with the SHAP values, indicating that higher DSR values corresponded to higher predicted polysaccharide yields. Ultrasonic time ranked second, with extended ultrasonic time exerting a positive effect on yield. In all models, DSR was consistently identified as the most important factor affecting polysaccharide yield, a finding corroborated by both PCC analysis and SHAP global and local interpretations.

The variables of driving polysaccharide yield prediction based on SHAP analysis. (A-C) The global analysis of input variables with RF, DT, and XGBoost model. (D) The local analysis of input variables based on SHAP analysis.

The heatmap (Fig. S3) clearly illustrates the contributions of input variables to polysaccharide yield and the fluctuations of predicted values (x) across all samples. Overall, several samples exhibited (x) values below the mean. Notably, the trend of (x) closely corresponded to the SHAP values of the DSR, displaying a decline in the middle of the samples followed by a gradual recovery. This pattern indicates that the DSR was the main factor influencing the yield. When the SHAP value of the DSR was negative, the predicted yield generally lower than the mean. In such cases, adjusting other variables such as ultrasonic time, ultrasonic power, or NADES water content could partially mitigate the negative impact of the DSR, thereby enhancing the overall prediction performance.

Structural characterization of SOLP

Preparation and purification of SOLP

Validation experiments were conducted under the optimized NADES conditions (NADES water content = 51%, DSR = 43 mL/g, ultrasonic time = 42 min, and ultrasonic power = 250 W) resulted in a polysaccharide yield of 44.0%, closely matching the values predicted by the model. After NADES extraction and ethanol precipitation, the total S. oblata leaf residue polysaccharides were isolated. The SOLP was obtained after the removal of proteins using Sevag method and dialysis for subsequent analysis. It should be noted that although the Sevag method is a traditional deproteinization method widely used in laboratory-scale polysaccharide research [51], it relies on organic solvents and may cause environmental burdens and issues related to organic waste disposal, thereby reducing the overall greenness of the polysaccharide preparation process. This represents a current limitation of this study. In future process optimization and scale-up studies, alternative deproteinization methods, such as enzymatic treatment [52], palygorskite adsorption [53], resin adsorption [54], and magnetic chitosan microsphere method [55], could be considered as substitutes for the Sevag method to reduce or avoid the use of organic solvents. These improvements may further enhance the practical application potential of the strategy proposed in this study for the high-value utilization of natural polysaccharides from underutilized plant resources and agricultural by-products, as well as for sustainable resource development.

Ethanol precipitation is a commonly used post-treatment step for polysaccharide separation, and its main function is to precipitate macromolecular polysaccharides from the extract. In contrast, NADES components, including ethanedioic acid and choline chloride, are more likely to remain in the ethanol–water liquid phase and can be partially separated from the polysaccharide precipitates during centrifugation. The subsequent dialysis step further takes advantage of the substantial molecular-weight difference between polysaccharides and ethanedioic acid, thereby further reducing the potential residues of ethanedioic acid in the final polysaccharide sample. For future applications, additional post-treatment strategies, such as resin adsorption or membrane electrodialysis, could be introduced to reduce and recover residual NADES components [43], [56], [57], [58].

UV and FT-IR analysis

UV spectrum of SOLP (Fig. 7A) demonstrated that absorption in the 260–280 nm range was almost absent, which indicated that proteins and nucleic acids were almost absent. The FT-IR spectrum of SOLP was showed in Fig. 7B. A peak at 3423.8 cm−1 was observed, which was associated with O-H stretching vibrations. The signal at 2919.3 cm−1 corresponded to the C-H stretching in the glucopyranose ring. The absorbance at the 1720.7 cm−1 and 1605.0 cm−1 were attributed to the C=O stretching vibrations, and the signal at 1413.7 cm−1 was related to C-H bending. Furthermore, the peaks at 1237.2 cm−1, 1081.3 cm−1, and 1022.4 cm−1 were attributed to the C-O, C-O-C, and pyranose rings vibrations. The 834.2 cm−1 and 894.9 cm−1 showed the presence of α-glycosidic and β-glycosidic linkage [30], [32], [59].

Dynamic light scattering analysis

DLS was used to determine the particle size and zeta potential of SOLP. The results showed that SOLP exhibited a diameter of approximately 1102.7 ± 55.4 nm and a zeta potential of −27.1 ± 1.9 mV (Fig. 7C), indicating a negatively charged surface with electrostatic stability. The negative zeta potential suggests the presence of ionizable anionic groups on the polysaccharide surface, implying that SOLP may contain uronic acid residues.

Congo red analysis

To determine whether the polysaccharide chains exhibit a triple-helix structure, the Congo red method was employed to analyze SOLP under different alkaline conditions. Compared with the Congo red blank control, the maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) of the SOLP-Congo red complexes gradually decreased with increasing sodium hydroxide concentration, and no significant red shift was observed (Fig. S4), which suggest that SOLP does not exhibit a typical triple-helix structure.

Thermal analysis and rheological analysis

As shown in Fig. 7D, the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves of SOLP exhibit a typical three-stage weight-loss behavior. In the first stage, with a weight loss of approximately 11.45%, occurs between 30 and 200 °C, mainly due to the evaporation of absorbed and bound water. Accordingly, a broad and weak weight-loss rate peak appears at around 80 °C on the DTG curve. The second stage represents to the major thermal degradation process, with a weight loss of about 50.06% occurring between 200 and 500 °C. A pronounced DTG peak appears at approximately 250 °C, indicating the primary thermal decomposition and cleavage of the polysaccharide backbone. At higher temperatures (500–700 °C), a further slower weight loss of approximately 11.96% was observed, which may be related to the further decomposition or carbonization of the residual char. At 800 °C, the residual mass is approximately 26.53%.

The change in the apparent viscosity of SOLP with shear rate is shown in Fig. 7E. As the shear rate increased, the apparent viscosity decreased markedly, showing typical shear-thinning (non-Newtonian) behavior. In the low shear rate region (< 20 s−1), polysaccharide chains were strongly entangled. Under shear, the chains gradually oriented along the flow direction and became less entangled, leading to a rapid decrease in viscosity. As the shear rate increased further, chain orientation became more stable and most intermolecular entanglements were disrupted. As a result, internal flow resistance and a gradual leveling-off of the apparent viscosity.

The Fig. 7F illustrates the variations in the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of SOLP with angular frequency. As the angular frequency increased, both G′ and G″ increased indicating enhanced elastic and viscous responses at higher frequencies. Notably, G′ remains consistently higher than G″ over the entire frequency range, suggesting that the polysaccharide solution exhibits elastic-dominated behavior. This result implies the presence of physical entanglements or a weak transient network structure within the system, and the mechanical response of the solution shows pronounced frequency dependence.

SEM is a widely used technique for morphological characterization of polysaccharides, as it provides high-resolution images of surface morphology. As shown in Fig. 8C-D, the polysaccharide exhibited an irregular lamellar morphology with non-uniform sizes, a slightly rough surface, and a multilayer stacking structure.

Density functional theory analysis

The formation of NADES is driven by hydrogen-bonding interactions between HBAs and HBDs. They are capable of forming strong hydrogen bonds with polysaccharides, thereby promoting the relaxation and disruption of the cell wall structure and significantly enhancing the solubility and dispersibility of polysaccharides in the solvent. Ultrasound-assisted extraction can further damage plant cell walls and accelerate solvent penetration and polysaccharides release, thus improving the extraction efficiency of polysaccharides.

To elucidate the molecular interaction mechanisms between NADES and polysaccharides, DFT calculation was performed to evaluate the binding energies and identify the potential binding sites between polysaccharides and NADES. The main components (galacturonic acid, galactose, and rhamnose) were selected as simulated substances. DFT calculations were carried out to compare the binding energies and bond lengths between NADES and galactose, NADES and galacturonic acid, NADES and rhamnose. The interaction systems of H2O and galactose, H2O and galacturonic acid, H2O and rhamnose served as controls. The most stable geometric structures before and after interaction, along with the corresponding bond lengths (Å) and binding energy changes (ΔE), are showed in Fig. 9 and Fig. S5.

DFT analysis of the hydrogen bonds interactions and binding energies between galactose (A), galacturonic acid (B), and rhamnose (C) with NADES (choline chloride-ethanedioic acid).

The results showed that among the NADES-polysaccharide binding modes, galactose exhibited the strongest interaction with NADES, with a binding energy of −27.44 kcal/mol. This interaction was mainly associated with hydrogen bonding within the NADES system. Similarly, galacturonic acid and rhamnose have consistent binding trends. The binding energies are −20.70 and −17.23 kcal/mol, respectively, and both formed relatively stable hydrogen-bonded structures.

These interactions were mainly attributed to hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, which facilitated the association between polysaccharide chains and NADES, and improved polysaccharide solubility and extraction efficiency. In addition, the binding energies between NADES and galacturonic acid, galactose, and rhamnose were significantly higher than those between H2O and the corresponding monosaccharides (ΔE = -11.35, −13.18, and −13.07 kcal/mol, respectively). These results demonstrated that NADES-6 has a stronger molecular affinity and stability in facilitating polysaccharide dissolution and extraction, which provide theoretical support for the NADES-assisted ultrasonic extraction.

Antioxidant activity results analysis

As shown in Fig. 10, the ABTS and hydroxyl radical scavenging activities of SOLP were assessed. The results demonstrated that S. oblata leaf residue polysaccharides extracted using ultrasound-assisted NADES exhibited strong antioxidant activity. At a concentration of 2 mg/mL, the ABTS radical scavenging activity reached 70.8%, and the hydroxyl radical scavenging activity was 57.1%. The IC50 values for ABTS and hydroxyl radical scavenging were 0.941 mg/mL and 1.46 mg/mL, respectively. The antioxidant activity of polysaccharides is associated with structural characteristics, including monosaccharide composition and others [37]. Previous studies have demonstrated that pectic polysaccharides enriched in GalA generally exhibit stronger antioxidant activity [60], which is consistent with our study and may be due to its higher GalA residue content [61].

Antioxidant activities of SOLP. (A) ABTS radical scavenging activity. (B) Hydroxyl radical scavenging activity.

The biological activities of polysaccharides are generally closely related to the structural characteristics [37], such as monosaccharide composition and uronic acid content. Previous studies have shown that different extraction methods may influence the antioxidant activity of polysaccharides by altering their structure [62]. For example, compared with traditional water extraction, NADES extraction has been reported to not only improve the extraction yield of polysaccharides, but also enhance the antioxidant activities of the polysaccharides from Lycium barbarum residue and okra [41], [63]. These findings suggest that NADES systems may be favorable for releasing or enriching polysaccharide fractions with stronger antioxidant potential. Previous studies have shown that the antioxidant activity of pectic-like polysaccharides may be associated with structural features such as GalA residue content and the proportion of homogalacturonan (HG) domains. For example, compared with WLBP-A3-c, WPOP-A-c exhibited higher scavenging activities against DPPH, ABTS, and hydroxyl radicals, which may be related to its higher GalA and HG domain contents [64]. In addition, the GalA content of crude polysaccharides from Fructus Mori was higher than that of the fraction MFP1P, and the crude polysaccharides also exhibited stronger antioxidant activity. This further suggests that GalA content may be one of the important structural factors affecting the antioxidant capacity of polysaccharides [65].

In this study, SOLP was mainly composed of GalA (44.43%), Gal (30.41%), and Rha (13.49%), along with Glc (5.98%), Ara (2.82%), and a small proportion of GlcA (2.87%). The relatively high total uronic acid content, including GalA and GlcA, suggests that SOLP exhibits acidic polysaccharide characteristics. Considering its high contents of GalA, Gal, and Rha, SOLP may possess acidic pectic-like structural features. The strong ABTS radical scavenging activity of natural polysaccharide has been reported to be associated with its uronic acid content. This may be related to the interaction of uronic acid with hydrogen atom of the anomeric carbon of the polysaccharides. This interaction promotes the availability of hydrogen, which can subsequently participate in radical quenching. In addition, previous studies have suggested that polysaccharide fractions with relatively high rhamnose content may be associated with stronger hydroxyl radical scavenging activity [66].

SOLP also contained a relatively high proportion of Gal. Previous studies based on pearson correlation analysis have shown a significant association between Gal content and the antioxidant capacity of polysaccharides, suggesting that Gal-rich structures may also be involved in their antioxidant activity [67]. Furthermore, although different polysaccharide fractions may contain similar monosaccharide types, variations in their molar ratios may lead to differences in antioxidant activity. This suggests that the antioxidant activity of polysaccharides is not determined solely by the presence of specific monosaccharides, but is also closely associated with the relative proportions of different monosaccharide residues [68].

In conclusion, the ABTS and hydroxyl radical scavenging activities of SOLP may not be determined by a single factor. Rather, they may result from the combined contributions of multiple structural features, including GalA enrichment, high uronic acids content, relatively high proportions of Gal and Rha, and acidic pectic-like structural characteristics.

Although this study established a machine learning-assisted NADES extraction strategy for SOLP and investigated its extraction process and potential activity through structural characterization, DFT calculations, and antioxidant activity evaluation, several limitations remain. Therefore, further optimization and improvement are needed in future studies. First, the safety and practical application boundaries of the NADES system need to be further clarified. Although NADES-6 exhibited high extraction efficiency for polysaccharides, ethanedioic acid may still raise potential safety concerns. Therefore, future studies should further optimize post-treatment strategies, such as resin adsorption or membrane electrodialysis, and determine the residual levels of ethanedioic acid in the final polysaccharide. Second, the greenness of this study is mainly reflected in the NADES-based ultrasound-assisted extraction stage, whereas the subsequent Sevag deproteinization process still involves the use of organic solvents. Therefore, greener deproteinization and purification methods, such as enzymatic treatment, palygorskite adsorption, resin adsorption, and magnetic chitosan microsphere method, should be considered in future studies to further improve the environmental compatibility of the overall polysaccharide preparation process. Third, the antioxidant activity of SOLP was mainly evaluated using ABTS and hydroxyl radical scavenging assays. In future studies, methylation analysis and 1D/2D NMR spectroscopy could be combined to further elucidate the structure–activity relationship of SOLP. In addition, other antioxidant evaluation systems could also be introduced to more comprehensively clarify the antioxidant mechanism of SOLP. Fourth, the generalizability of the machine learning models remains limited. The models established in this study were mainly based on S. oblata leaf residues and a specific NADES extraction system. Although the models showed good predictive performance, the dataset size was relatively limited. Therefore, future studies should further expand the experimental dataset by incorporating more types of plant materials, NADES combinations, and extraction conditions, which may help improve the stability, generalizability, and transferability of the models. Finally, process scale-up and economic feasibility have not yet been systematically evaluated. The NADES extraction system established and validated in this study was mainly developed at the laboratory scale. Therefore, future studies should include pilot-scale experiments, energy-consumption evaluation, solvent recycling assessment, and cost analysis to evaluate the practical application potential of this strategy for larger-scale polysaccharide extraction and the high-value utilization.

Conclusion

In this study, machine learning was combined with experimental validation to systematically investigate the effects of NADES on the extraction efficiency, structural characteristics, and bioactivities of S. oblata leaf residue polysaccharides. Based on ultrasound-assisted NADES extraction, a green and efficient polysaccharide extraction strategy was established, with SOLP used as a case study, thereby significantly enhancing polysaccharide extraction efficiency and predictive accuracy. By constructing multiple ML models, it was found that nonlinear models significantly outperformed the linear model, with XGBoost achieving the highest accuracy. Variable importance analysis indicated that the DSR, water content, ultrasonic power, and ultrasonic time had major effects on polysaccharide extraction. Both SHAP and PCC analyses consistently confirmed that DSR was the dominant factor influencing extraction yield and revealed key interactions among variables, thereby enabling prediction and optimization of extraction conditions. Furthermore, DFT calculations revealed that NADES-6 interacts with galactose, galacturonic acid, and rhamnose through hydrogen bonding, with binding energies markedly higher than those of traditional solvents, exploring the molecular mechanisms of the enhanced polysaccharide extraction efficiency of NADES. Experimental validation further demonstrated that SOLP are acidic polysaccharides mainly composed of Rha, GlcA, GalA, Glc, Gal, and Ara. Furthermore, the polysaccharides showed distinct rheological behavior, including clear shear-thinning characteristics and good viscoelasticity. Morphological analysis revealed irregular lamellar stacked structures, while functional evaluation confirmed the ABTS and hydroxyl radical scavenging activities.

This approach provides new insights into the high-value utilization of underused plant resources and agricultural by-products. Moreover, by combining green chemistry with ML, this study establishes a generalizable methodological framework that provides support for polysaccharide-based functional materials, the valorization of agricultural by-products, the advancement of the circular economy, and the development of environmentally friendly manufacturing.
