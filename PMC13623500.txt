Sanicula fengjiensis (Apiaceae), a New Species From China Revealed by Integrative Morphological and Molecular Evidence

Hui‐Min Li, Ji‐Min Liao, Xu‐Dong Ma, Chun‐Feng Song

Abstract

Sanicula fengjiensis (Apiaceae), a new species discovered in Fengjie County, Chongqing Municipality, southwestern China, is described and illustrated. It is morphologically most similar to 
S. caerulescens
 in having pseudo‐racemose inflorescences, but differs in having predominantly white petals (vs. blue to purple) and distinctive fruit ornamentation, with prickles absent, the ribs basally fused into a thin layer, and the valleculae occasionally bearing sparse scales or tubercles (vs. fruits sparsely to densely covered with short, straight spinous bristles). Quantitative morphological analyses further distinguished S. fengjiensis from 
S. caerulescens
 and the phylogenetically proximate S. lamelligera and S. orthacantha. In particular, the maximum height of fruit‐surface appendages in S. fengjiensis (0.033–0.101 mm) showed no overlap with the observed ranges of the other three species. The complete plastomes of four sampled accessions of S. fengjiensis were all 155,501 bp in length and exhibited the typical quadripartite structure. Plastome and nrDNA phylogenies based on complete plastome sequences and combined ITS and ETS sequences, respectively, consistently recovered the sampled accessions of S. fengjiensis as a clade, although its relationships with other Sanicula species differed between the two datasets. In addition to the extant population in Fengjie, a historical herbarium specimen collected from Yichang, Hubei Province, in 1963 is attributable to the new species. The combined morphological and phylogenetic evidence supports the recognition of S. fengjiensis as a distinct species.

Introduction

Sanicula L. (Apiaceae, subfamily Saniculoideae) comprises ca. 42 taxa and is widely distributed from East Asia to North America (Van Wyk et al. ; Li et al. ). The genus has long been regarded as taxonomically complex due to its considerable morphological variation in rhizomes, leaves, inflorescences, and fruits, and it has been considered a relatively primitive lineage within subfamily Saniculoideae (Shan and Constance ; Hiroe ; Calviño et al. ; Wen et al. ; Xie et al. ). Over the past two decades, only four species and one variety have been newly described in the genus (Kamelin et al. ; Xie et al. ; Kenny et al. ; Song et al. ). Meanwhile, seven species and one variety have been reduced to synonymy (Li et al. , ; Li and Song ; Li, Zhou, et al. ; Li, Zhu, et al. ). China is one of the major centers of distribution for Sanicula, harboring ca. 16 species (Li et al. , ; Li and Song ; Song et al. ; Li, Zhou, et al. ; Li, Zhu, et al. ).

In taxonomically complex plant groups, taxa may remain overlooked or misidentified when morphological characters provide insufficient resolution (Duminil and Di Michele ; Bebber et al. ; Goodwin et al. ), particularly when available specimens lack the reproductive structures required for reliable identification (Sullivan and Nazaire ). Fruit morphology provides important diagnostic characters for species identification and delimitation in Sanicula (Shan and Constance ; Pryer and Phillippe ; Chen et al. ; Li et al. ). Recent studies have increasingly incorporated both plastome and nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) data to provide complementary phylogenetic evidence for species delimitation (Shen et al. ; Zhang et al. ). Therefore, integrating detailed morphological examination of numerous specimens, particularly of reproductive characters, with molecular phylogenetic evidence can help distinguish overlooked taxa from intraspecific variants and identify previously misidentified specimens.

During our botanical expeditions to Fengjie County, Chongqing Municipality, southwestern China, from 2022 to 2024, we encountered an unusual population of Sanicula that exhibited morphological features resembling 
S. caerulescens
 Franch., a species widely distributed in various regions of China and Vietnam (Li et al. ). These plants shared pseudo‐racemose inflorescences with 
S. caerulescens
, a key characteristic of the species. However, upon further examination, we discovered that these plants differed significantly in fruit morphology, a crucial diagnostic feature in Sanicula. These morphological differences led us to hypothesize that the Fengjie population represented a previously overlooked taxon rather than an intraspecific variant of 
S. caerulescens
. Subsequent plastome and nrDNA phylogenetic analyses further supported the distinctiveness of the Fengjie population, while the nrDNA phylogeny indicated particularly close relationships with S. lamelligera Hance and S. orthacantha S. Moore. These results prompted us to extend the morphological comparison beyond 
S. caerulescens
 to include these phylogenetically proximate species.

During our examination of herbarium material, we also identified a historical specimen collected from Yichang, Hubei Province, in 1963 that was morphologically consistent with the Fengjie plants, particularly in its diagnostic fruit characters (Figure ). However, no extant plants have yet been relocated at the historical Yichang locality. We therefore treat this specimen as a historical herbarium record rather than evidence of a confirmed extant population. Here, we describe this previously overlooked taxon as Sanicula fengjiensis based on morphological evidence and plastome and nrDNA (ITS and ETS) phylogenetic analyses. We further compare it with the morphologically similar 
S. caerulescens
 and the phylogenetically proximate S. lamelligera and S. orthacantha, and clarify its phylogenetic placement and currently documented distribution.

Materials and Methods

Morphological Comparison

For morphological comparison, we examined physical specimens or high‐resolution images of specimens of Sanicula caerulescens from 64 herbaria, including CDBI, NAS, PE, and WUK (Li et al. ). We also observed living plants from five representative populations of 
S. caerulescens
 (Li et al. ), four representative populations of S. orthacantha (Li et al. ), three representative populations of S. lamelligera (Li and Song ) and the type population of S. fengjiensis in Fengjie county, Chongqing Municipality. The type population of S. fengjiensis was repeatedly observed over two consecutive years to assess the stability of its diagnostic morphological characters.

We also examined a historical herbarium specimen collected in 1963 from the vicinity of Huanglingmiao in Yichang, Hubei Province. To evaluate the current occurrence of S. fengjiensis in this area, we conducted targeted field surveys in Yichang in 2021, 2023, and 2024, including searches around Huanglingmiao and nearby habitats considered potentially suitable on the basis of the locality information recorded on the specimen label.

Living individuals of S. fengjiensis collected from the type population were transplanted to and cultivated at the Institute of Botany, Jiangsu Province and Chinese Academy of Sciences for repeated morphological observations under cultivation. The morphological description of S. fengjiensis was based on observations of herbarium specimens and living plants in the wild and in cultivation.

Morphological comparisons focused on inflorescence morphology, involucral‐bract morphology, calyx teeth, and fruit morphology. Ten quantitative characters were included in the statistical analyses: inflorescence length, number of primary branches, terminal capitate umbel diameter, involucral bract length and width, calyx tooth length and width, fruit body length and width, and maximum height of fruit‐surface appendages. Fruit‐surface ornamentation was additionally recorded as a qualitative character (e.g., prickly, scaly, tuberculate, or nearly smooth) but was not included in the PCA. Quantitative characters were measured from fully developed structures. Where multiple homologous structures were available from an individual, repeated measurements were averaged and the individual mean was used as the statistical unit. The number of inflorescences examined per individual, individual‐level measurements, and summary statistics for each taxon are provided in Tables and .

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using individual‐level mean values for the ten quantitative morphological characters. Pairwise correlations among characters were first assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients to evaluate potential redundancy among variables. Prior to PCA, all variables were centered and scaled to unit variance to account for differences in measurement units and numerical ranges. Because no extensive collinearity was detected and the correlated characters represented biologically distinct morphological features, all ten quantitative characters were retained in the PCA. PCA was conducted using the prcomp function in R v.4.5.3. Qualitative characters, including fruit‐surface ornamentation, were excluded from the PCA and evaluated separately in the morphological comparison (Table ).

Phylogenetic Reconstruction

Plant Materials, DNA Extraction, Plastome Sequencing, and NRDNA Amplification

Four individuals of Sanicula fengjiensis were sampled from the type population in Fengjie County for molecular analyses, including plastome sequencing and nuclear ribosomal ITS and ETS sequencing. Fresh and healthy leaf tissues were collected in the field and dried in silica gel. Voucher specimens were deposited in the Herbarium of the Institute of Botany, Jiangsu Province and Chinese Academy of Sciences (NAS), and the voucher numbers are listed in Table .

Total genomic DNA was extracted from silica‐dried leaf tissues using a modified CTAB method (Doyle and Doyle ). DNA integrity and purity were evaluated using 1% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), respectively, and DNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer.

For plastome sequencing, high‐quality DNA libraries were constructed and sequenced by Wuhan Benagen Tech Solutions Company Limited (Wuhan, China). Genome‐skimming sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq PE150 platform, generating 150‐bp paired‐end reads with an insert size of approximately 350 bp. The resulting sequencing reads were used for plastome assembly.

The nuclear ribosomal ITS and ETS regions were amplified separately by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). ITS was amplified using ITS5 (5′‐GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG‐3′) and ITS4 (5′‐TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC‐3′) (White et al. ), whereas ETS was amplified using 18S‐ETS (5′‐ACTTACACATGCATGGCTTAATCT‐3′) and Umb‐ETS (5′‐GCGCATGAGTGGTGAWTKGTA‐3′) (Baldwin and Markos ; Logacheva et al. ). The PCR products were purified and bidirectionally sequenced using the Sanger method by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. Forward and reverse reads were assembled and manually checked using Geneious Prime v.2020.0.5. In total, we newly generated 21 ITS and 20 ETS sequences for Sanicula, including four ITS and four ETS sequences from the four individuals of S. fengjiensis. These sequences were deposited in Zenodo and their accession numbers are provided in Table .

For phylogenetic analyses, the four newly generated plastomes of S. fengjiensis were combined with 15 complete plastomes retrieved from NCBI GenBank, including 16 plastomes of Sanicula and three plastomes of Eryngium (Table ).

Complete Chloroplast Genomes Assembly and Annotation

Clean sequencing reads were assembled de novo using the GetOrganelle pipeline (Bankevich et al. ; Langmead and Salzberg ; Jin et al. ). Bandage v.5.6.0 (Wick et al. ) was used to visualize and manually inspect the assembly results. Chloroplast genome annotation was performed using PGA (Qu et al. ). Start and stop codons, as well as intron–exon boundaries, were manually corrected in Geneious Prime 2020.0.5 (Kearse et al. ). Circular genome maps were generated using OrganellarGenomeDRAW v.1.3.1 (Greiner et al. ). The annotated chloroplast genomes were deposited in Zenodo.

Phylogenetic Analysis

To reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships within the genus Sanicula, 19 plastomes, 22 ITS sequences, and 21 ETS sequences, including the outgroup, were analyzed. Three Eryngium species were selected as outgroups for the plastome dataset, and one Eryngium species was selected as the outgroup for the ITS and ETS datasets, based on the phylogenetic framework proposed in a previous study (Calviño and Downie ). The plastome, ITS, and ETS datasets were aligned separately using MAFFT v7.221 (Katoh and Standley ).

Maximum‐likelihood analyses of the plastome, ITS, and ETS datasets were conducted separately using IQ‐TREE (Nguyen et al. ; Stamatakis and Kozlov ) with automatic substitution model selection and 1000 bootstrap replicates to evaluate nodal support. The analyses were run on the CIPRES Science Gateway platform (Miller et al. ). BI analyses were conducted in MrBayes on XSEDE v3.2.7a (Huelsenbeck and Ronquist ), with models selected under the Bayesian model choice setting implemented in MrBayes (nst = 6, rates = gamma).

Furthermore, the ITS and ETS gene trees were summarized to infer the ASTRAL tree (Mirarab et al. ). The final phylogenetic trees were visualized using Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) v6 (Letunic and Bork ).

Results and Discussion

Morphology and Ecology

Sanicula fengjiensis was morphologically most similar to 
S. caerulescens
, particularly in having pseudo‐racemose inflorescences. It could, however, be distinguished from 
S. caerulescens
 by its usually unbranched or only sparsely branched inflorescences composed predominantly of terminal capitate umbellules, mostly white petals (vs. blue to purple), and fruits lacking spinous prickles. In S. fengjiensis, the fruit‐surface appendages were strongly reduced, with the ribs basally fused into a thin layer and the valleculae occasionally bearing sparse scales or tubercles, whereas the fruits of 
S. caerulescens
 were sparsely to densely covered with short, straight spinous bristles that were basally fused into a thin sheath (Figures and ).

Given that the nrDNA ASTRAL tree (Figure ) placed S. fengjiensis in a clade with S. lamelligera and S. orthacantha, these two phylogenetically proximate species were additionally included in the morphological comparison. Both species could also be readily distinguished from S. fengjiensis by their markedly different inflorescence branching patterns, together with differences in other floral and fruit characters summarized in Table . Quantitative analyses of 10 morphological characters across the four species further supported the distinctiveness of S. fengjiensis. The first two principal components accounted for 49.8% of the total morphological variation (PC1 = 28.2%; PC2 = 21.6%) (Figure ; Tables and ). Among the measured characters, maximum fruit‐surface appendage height and terminal capitate umbel diameter showed the strongest diagnostic differentiation of S. fengjiensis from the other three species.

Maximum fruit‐surface appendage height (T10) showed particularly pronounced differentiation among species (Kruskal–Wallis test, BH‐adjusted p < 0.001, ε
2 = 0.525; Figure ). In S. fengjiensis, this character ranged from 0.033 to 0.101 mm, with no overlap with the observed ranges of 
S. caerulescens
 (0.253–0.625 mm), S. lamelligera (0.152–0.447 mm), or S. orthacantha (0.265–0.800 mm) (Figure and Table ). Terminal capitate umbel diameter (T3) also differed significantly among species (one‐way ANOVA, BH‐adjusted p < 0.001, η
2 = 0.378; Figure ). Although the overall observed ranges showed some overlap, the interquartile range of S. fengjiensis was completely separated from those of the other three species (Figure ). The number of primary inflorescence branches (T2) provided additional differentiation from S. lamelligera and S. orthacantha, although it did not significantly distinguish S. fengjiensis from 
S. caerulescens
 (Figure ). In contrast, several other quantitative characters showed considerable interspecific overlap and therefore had lower diagnostic value when considered individually.

Taken together, the quantitative differences and qualitative characters of the inflorescences, petals, calyx teeth, and fruit surface distinguished S. fengjiensis from both the morphologically similar 
S. caerulescens
 and the phylogenetically proximate S. lamelligera and S. orthacantha. Detailed morphological comparisons among the four species were provided in Table .

According to available distributional records, the four species also differed geographically and ecologically. 
S. caerulescens
 was widely distributed in China (Chongqing, Guangxi, Hubei, Hunan, Sichuan, and Yunnan) and Vietnam (Hà Giang), occurring mainly at elevations of 400–1700 m (Li et al. ). S. lamelligera was widely distributed in central, eastern, southern, and southwestern China and southern Japan, where it occurred in forests on mountain slopes and along ravines and streams at elevations of 450–1600 m (Li and Song ). S. orthacantha was widely distributed in China (Anhui, Chongqing, Fujian, Gansu, Guangdong, Guangxi, Guizhou, Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Taiwan, Yunnan, and Zhejiang) and Vietnam, occurring mainly on forested mountain slopes and along ravines and streams at elevations of 600–2140 m (Li et al. ). At the time of this study, S. fengjiensis was documented only from Chongqing and Hubei (Figure ), with known extant populations occurring at relatively low elevations of approximately 100–200 m on mountain slopes.

Characteristics of Plastomes and Phylogenetic Analysis

The complete plastomes of four Sanicula fengjiensis accessions were all 155,501 bp in length (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19202452; A representative plastome map is shown in Figure ). All four plastomes exhibited the typical quadripartite structure, comprising a large single‐copy (LSC) region, a small single‐copy (SSC) region, and two inverted repeat regions (IRa and IRb) (representative plastome characteristics are shown in Table ).

Phylogenetic analyses based on plastome and combined ITS and ETS datasets consistently recovered the sampled accessions of S. fengjiensis as a distinct clade, although its relationships with other Sanicula species differed between the two datasets. In the plastome phylogeny, the four sampled accessions of S. fengjiensis formed a strongly supported clade (BS = 100, PP = 1.00; Figure ). This clade was resolved as sister to a larger lineage containing S. rugulosa Diels, 
S. brevispina
 (H.Boissieu) B.N. Song & X.J. He, S. orthacantha, S. lamelligera, and 
S. caerulescens
, although support for this relationship was relatively low (BS = 62, PP = 0.60).

In the ASTRAL tree inferred from ITS and ETS gene trees, the four accessions of S. fengjiensis also formed a clade (LPP = 0.87; Figure ). This clade was placed with S. orthacantha, although their immediate relationship received low support (LPP = 0.42), and the resulting lineage was grouped with S. lamelligera with higher support (LPP = 0.87). This broader lineage was sister to the clade comprising the three sampled populations of 
S. caerulescens
 (LPP = 0.81).

The plastome and nrDNA datasets therefore recovered different placements of S. fengjiensis, indicating topological discordance between the plastome and nrDNA phylogenies. The underlying cause of this discordance cannot be determined from the present data alone. Nevertheless, the sampled accessions of S. fengjiensis consistently formed a coherent clade in both analyses. Together with the diagnostic morphological differences from 
S. caerulescens
, S. lamelligera, and S. orthacantha, these results supported the recognition of S. fengjiensis as a distinct species.

Taxonomic Treatment

Sanicula fengjiensis H.M. Li & C.F. Song, sp. nov. (Figures and ).

Type

CHINA. Chongqing municipality: Fengjie County, Lianhua village, along roadsides, in forest understories, and on stony slopes, 30.91767418° N, 109.54895994° E, 180.74 m alt., 6 June 2024, LHM1594 (fl., holotype NAS00722724!, isotype NAS00722725!).

Diagnosis

Sanicula fengjiensis is morphologically similar to 
S. caerulescens
 in having pseudo‐racemose inflorescences, but differs in having usually unbranched or sparsely branched inflorescences, predominantly white petals (vs. blue to purple), and fruits lacking spinous prickles, with the ribs basally fused into a thin layer and the valleculae occasionally bearing sparse scales or tubercles (vs. fruits sparsely to densely covered with short, straight spinous bristles that are basally fused into a thin sheath). It is also readily distinguished from the phylogenetically proximate S. lamelligera and S. orthacantha by its much less extensively branched inflorescences and distinctive fruit‐surface ornamentation (Table ).

Description

Perennial. Rhizome, oblique rootstock, bearing fibrous roots. Stems 1–8, slender and erect, 18–28 cm tall. Basal leaves 7–24, long petiolate; petioles 1.5–13 cm long; blade glabrous adaxially and abaxially, 1–7 cm long, 1–7.5 cm wide, cordate suborbicular, ternate, segments distinctly petiolate; median segments obovate to ovate‐lanceolate, distinctly shallowly trilobed, petiolate; lateral segments obliquely ovate, bilobed to the middle or only notched or rarely entire; margins irregularly crenulate serrate with spinose teeth. Cauline leaves absent or nearly absent; degenerating into sheathing, trisect or undivided, bract‐like, ca. 2.0 mm long, subsessile or sessile. Inflorescence rarely branched, pseudo‐racemose, consisting of capitate umbellules only, terminal; lower umbels fascicled, sessile or short‐pedunculate; involucrate bracts lanceolate to ovate, acuminate, 2–3 mm long; terminal umbels pedunculate, with 2–10 rays, rays 4.8–6.0 mm long; involucellate bracteoles 2–6, linear to lanceolate, 0.7–1.1 mm long. Staminate flowers 4–6 per umbellule; pedicels ca. 2 mm long; petals mainly white. Fertile flowers 1 per umbellule, sessile; calyx teeth 4–8, linear to lanceolate, acute, 0.6–1.2 mm long; styles 2.0–3.6 mm long, recurved. Mericarps globose to ellipsoid, ca. 1.8 mm long, 1.0–1.8 mm broad, along ribs fused at base into a thin layer; valleculae occasionally bearing sparse scales or tubercles; prickles absent; mericarps dorsally flattened, orbicular in cross‐section.

Phenology

Flowering from late March to early May. Fruiting from late March to early June.

Etymology

The epithet “fengjiensis” is derived from the type locality, Fengjie County, Chongqing Municipality, China. The Chinese name given is Feng Jie Bian Dou Cai (奉节变豆菜).

Distribution and Habitat

Sanicula fengjiensis is currently known from its extant population at the type locality, that is, Lianhua Village in Fengjie County, Chongqing Municipality, China, and from a historical herbarium record collected from Yichang City, Hubei Province, China (Figure ). It grows along roadsides, in forest understories, and on stony slopes at altitudes of 100–200 m.

Preliminary Conservation Status

Based on extensive examinations of herbarium specimens and our field surveys, Sanicula fengjiensis is rare in its type locality and adjacent areas. To date, only one extant population and one historical locality are known: one observed in the wild and one identified from specimen records. As its full distribution range, population dynamics, and potential threats remain unclear, we tentatively assess this species as Data Deficient (DD) according to the IUCN Red List Categories and Criteria (IUCN Standards and Petitions Committee ).
