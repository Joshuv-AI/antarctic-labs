Prime Quadruplets and Jump Conditions on Arithmetic Functions
Himaghna Roy Choudhury, Shicheng Wei

Abstract
. We provide progress on the characterization of composite integers n n that satisfy the jump conditions φ ⁡ ( n + 12 ) = φ ⁡ ( n ) + 12 \varphi(n+12)=\varphi(n)+12 and σ ⁡ ( n + 12 ) = σ ⁡ ( n ) + 12 \sigma(n+12)=\sigma(n)+12 simultaneously. While it is known that prime quadruplets ( p , p + 2 , p + 6 , p + 8 ) (p,p+2,p+6,p+8) generate solutions n = p ⁡ ( p + 8 ) n=p(p+8) , the complete characterization remains an open conjecture. We prove that this characterization is complete when n n and n + 12 n+12 are both squarefree semiprimes, and that no solutions can be composed of a single prime power. Furthermore, a complete search up to 10 12 10^{12} resulted in no counterexamples to the conjecture. If this conjecture is proven true, and there are infinitely many such solutions, then it can be proved that there are infinitely many prime quadruplets.

1.
Introduction

A classic subject to study in number theory is the behavior of arithmetic functions, such as
\varphi
, Euler’s totient function, and
\sigma
, the sum-of-divisors function, over shifted intervals. For instance, characterizing the solutions to
\sigma(n)=\sigma(n+1)
remains a well-known open conjecture (see Guy
[
4
, Section B13]
). Another equation, namely
\sigma(n+2)=\sigma(n)+2
, has solutions when
n
and
n+2
are twin primes, but composite solutions still exist
[
4
, Section B13]
. Other equations may inquire about the relationships between the two arithmetic functions, such as
\varphi(m)=\sigma(n)
, which can be shown to have infinite solutions if there are infinite twin primes.

In this paper we study a simultaneous jump condition for both arithmetic functions. More specifically, in the 2004 arXiv preprint
Prove or Disprove: 100 Conjectures from the OEIS

[
5
]
, R. Stephan wrote the following as Conjecture (33):

(1)

\bigl\{\text{composite }n\;\big|\;\varphi(n+12)=\varphi(n)+12\;\wedge\;\sigma(n+12)=\sigma(n)+12\bigr\}\;\Longrightarrow\;n\equiv 64\pmod{72}.

The residue
64\pmod{72}
is a typographical error. It is easy to verify that the smallest solution to the jump conditions is
n=65
. Stephan also confirms this when he originally proposed the
65\pmod{72}
result in the OEIS database under sequence
A056777

[
2
]
. In addition, in the same sequence entry, Jud McCranie conjectured that all solutions satisfying the jump conditions can be completely characterized by prime quadruplets, that is, prime tuples of the form
(p,p+2,p+6,p+8)
. We formulate this into a conjecture below.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Every composite integer
n
satisfying both
\varphi(n+12)=\varphi(n)+12
and
\sigma(n+12)=\sigma(n)+12
is of the form
n=p(p+8)
, where
(p,p+2,p+6,p+8)
is a prime quadruplet with
p\geq 5
.

Notice that if Conjecture
1.1
holds, then Stephan’s corrected residue claim follows as a corollary, and can be easily shown with the Chinese Remainder Theorem. Additionally, Conjecture
1.1
effectively bridges these jump conditions about
\varphi
and
\sigma
with the Hardy–Littlewood prime
k
-tuple conjecture for
k=4

[
1
]
. If Conjecture
1.1
is true and it can be shown that there are infinitely many solutions to these jump conditions, then it would imply infinitely many prime quadruplets.

In this paper, we prove that the prime quadruplet construction gives every solution in the case where
n
and
n+12
are squarefree semiprimes, and that no solutions
n
can be of a single prime power.

We assume throughout that
n\geq 4
is composite. The functions
\varphi
and
\sigma
are extended to
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
in the usual way. Recall that they are multiplicative, where
\varphi(p)=p-1
,
\sigma(p)=p+1
for primes
p
, and
\varphi(pq)=(p-1)(q-1)
,
\sigma(pq)=(p+1)(q+1)
for distinct primes
p
and
q
.

2.
Characterization in the Semiprime Case

We now show that within the class of squarefree semiprime solutions, the prime quadruplet construction gives a complete characterization.

Definition 2.1
.

A
prime quadruplet
is a
4
-tuple of primes in the form of
(p,\,p+2,\,p+6,\,p+8)
. The smallest primes
p
of prime quadruplets form the OEIS sequence
A007530
, beginning with
5,11,101,191,

821,1481,1871,2081,\ldots

[
3
]
. The Hardy–Littlewood prime
k
-tuple conjecture, which remains open, predicts that there are infinitely many
[
1
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose
n=pq
and
n+12=p^{\prime}q^{\prime}
are both products of two distinct primes (with
p<q
and
p^{\prime}<q^{\prime}
). Then both jump conditions

\varphi(n+12)-\varphi(n)=12,\hskip 20.00003pt\sigma(n+12)-\sigma(n)=12

hold if and only if
p\geq 5
,
q=p+8
, and
\{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\}=\{p+2,\,p+6\}
. That is,
(p,\,p^{\prime},\,q^{\prime},\,q)=(p,\,p+2,\,p+6,\,p+8)
is a prime quadruplet.

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
) Using
\varphi(rs)=(r-1)(s-1)
and
\sigma(rs)=(r+1)(s+1)
for distinct primes
r
and
s
, we get

\displaystyle\varphi(n)

\displaystyle=(p-1)(p+7)=p^{2}+6p-7,

\displaystyle\varphi(n+12)

\displaystyle=(p+1)(p+5)=p^{2}+6p+5,

\displaystyle\sigma(n)

\displaystyle=(p+1)(p+9)=p^{2}+10p+9,

\displaystyle\sigma(n+12)

\displaystyle=(p+3)(p+7)=p^{2}+10p+21.

By subtracting, we get
\varphi(n+12)-\varphi(n)=12
and
\sigma(n+12)-\sigma(n)=12
from just polynomial identities in terms of
p
.

(
\Rightarrow
) For any squarefree semiprime
rs
with
r
and
s
distinct primes,

\varphi(rs)=(r-1)(s-1)=rs-(r+s)+1,\hskip 20.00003pt\sigma(rs)=(r+1)(s+1)=rs+(r+s)+1.

Applying this to
n=pq
and
n+12=p^{\prime}q^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\varphi(n)

\displaystyle=pq-(p+q)+1

\displaystyle=n-(p+q)+1,

\displaystyle\varphi(n+12)

\displaystyle=p^{\prime}q^{\prime}-(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime})+1

\displaystyle=(n+12)-(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime})+1,

\displaystyle\sigma(n)

\displaystyle=pq+(p+q)+1

\displaystyle=n+(p+q)+1,

\displaystyle\sigma(n+12)

\displaystyle=p^{\prime}q^{\prime}+(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime})+1

\displaystyle=(n+12)+(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime})+1.

Subtracting the first from the second and the third from the fourth:

\displaystyle\varphi(n+12)-\varphi(n)

\displaystyle=\bigl[(n+12)-(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime})+1\bigr]-\bigl[n-(p+q)+1\bigr]=12+(p+q)-(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\sigma(n+12)-\sigma(n)

\displaystyle=\bigl[(n+12)+(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime})+1\bigr]-\bigl[n+(p+q)+1\bigr]=12+(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime})-(p+q).

Both expressions equal
12
if and only if

(2)

p+q=p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}.

Now, let
S=p+q=p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}
. Then,
p,q
are the roots of
X^{2}-SX+n=0
, and
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
are the roots of
X^{2}-SX+(n+12)=0
. The discriminants are

u^{2}:=S^{2}-4n=(q-p)^{2}\geq 0,\hskip 20.00003ptv^{2}:=S^{2}-4(n+12)=(q^{\prime}-p^{\prime})^{2}\geq 0,

so

(3)

u^{2}-v^{2}=48.

We now seek integer pairs
(u,v)
with
u>v\geq 0
satisfying (
3
). We first write
48=(u-v)(u+v)
, which leads us to notice that
u-v
and
u+v
must have the same parity, since their sum
2u
is even.

If both factors are odd, their product is odd, which contradicts
48
being even. If both are even, then let
u-v=2a
and
u+v=2b
with
a<b
and
ab=12
. The factorizations
(a,b)\in\{(1,12),\,(2,6),\,(3,4)\}
yield

(u,v)=(a+b,\,b-a)\in\{(13,11),\,(8,4),\,(7,1)\}.

We can verify them directly:
7^{2}-1^{2}=48
,
8^{2}-4^{2}=48
,
13^{2}-11^{2}=48
. These are the only integer solutions with
u>v\geq 0
.

Now recall
S=p+q
. We consider separate cases for whether
p=2
:

Case 1:
p,q
both odd.
Then
S=p+q
is even, and
u=q-p
must satisfy
S-u=2p
(even), forcing
u
even. Of the three solutions, only
(u,v)=(8,4)
has an even
u
. Hence

q-p=8,\hskip 10.00002ptq^{\prime}-p^{\prime}=4.

Combined with
p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}=p+q=2p+8
, this gives us a linear system in
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
:

p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}=2p+8,\hskip 20.00003ptq^{\prime}-p^{\prime}=4.

Adding and subtracting yields
2q^{\prime}=2p+12
and
2p^{\prime}=2p+4
respectively. Hence

p^{\prime}=p+2,\hskip 20.00003ptq^{\prime}=p+6.

Since
p,\,p^{\prime},\,q^{\prime},\,q
are prime by hypothesis, we know that
(p,p+2,p+6,p+8)
is a prime quadruplet. In addition,
p\geq 5
, because the smallest prime quadruplet starts at
p=5
(if
p=3
, then it requires
3,5,9,11
all to be prime, but
9=3^{2}
is not).

Case 2:
p=2
.
Then
n=2q
is even, so
n+12=p^{\prime}q^{\prime}
is also even. Since
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
are both prime and
2
is the only even prime, either
p^{\prime}
or
q^{\prime}
must be
2
. By the convention
p^{\prime}<q^{\prime}
, we have
p^{\prime}=2
, which leads to

q^{\prime}=\frac{n+12}{2}=\frac{2q+12}{2}=q+6.

Condition (
2
) gives
2+q=2+q^{\prime}
, i.e.,
q=q^{\prime}=q+6
, which is impossible. (Alternatively,
\varphi(2q)=q-1
and
\varphi(2(q+6))=q+5
, with difference
6\neq 12
.) So this case yields no solutions.

This exhausts the cases.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Theorem
2.2
proves that all semiprime solutions come from prime quadruplets. The remaining problem now is to demonstrate that no other composites besides semiprimes can satisfy the same simultaneous jump conditions. Below we provide a brief proof for Stephan’s observation mentioned in the introduction, conditional on Conjecture
1.1
.

Corollary 2.4

(to Conjecture
1.1
)
.

If
n=p(p+8)
where
(p,p+2,p+6,p+8)
is a prime quadruplet with
p\geq 5
, then
n\equiv 65\pmod{72}
. Consequently, if Conjecture
1.1
holds, all composite solutions to the jump conditions must satisfy
n\equiv 65\pmod{72}
.

Proof.

We use the Chinese Remainder Theorem with
72=8\cdot 9
.

Modulo
8
.
Since
p
is an odd prime,
p^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{8}
. Therefore

n=p(p+8)\equiv p\cdot p=p^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{8}.

Modulo
9
.
Since
p
is a prime and
p\geq 5
, we know
p\not\equiv 0\pmod{3}
. Similarly,
p+2
being prime with
p\geq 5
gives us
p\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. Therefore

p\equiv 2\pmod{3},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{i.e.,}\hskip 10.00002ptp\equiv 2,\,5,\,\text{or }8\pmod{9}.

For each possible residue, we compute
n=p(p+8)\bmod 9
:

\displaystyle p\equiv 2:\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle p(p+8)\equiv 2\cdot 10\equiv 2\cdot 1\equiv 2\pmod{9},

\displaystyle p\equiv 5:\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle p(p+8)\equiv 5\cdot 13\equiv 5\cdot 4\equiv 20\equiv 2\pmod{9},

\displaystyle p\equiv 8:\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle p(p+8)\equiv 8\cdot 16\equiv 8\cdot 7\equiv 56\equiv 2\pmod{9}.

Hence,
n\equiv 2\pmod{9}
in all cases.

CRT.
We seek
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{72}
with
x\equiv 1\pmod{8}
and
x\equiv 2\pmod{9}
. Using the CRT, we obtain that
x\equiv 65\pmod{72}
.
∎

3.
Non-Existence of Single Prime Power Solutions

We contribute partial progress to Conjecture
1.1
by proving that the case where
n
is a power of a single prime yields no solutions. We do this by first eliminating the possibility of small primes
p=2
and
p=3
, then proving that for all
p\geq 5
,
n+12
must be composite and squarefree. Thus
n+12
must have either exactly two distinct prime factors or three or more distinct prime factors. We finish the proof by creating a contradiction for both cases.

Theorem 3.1
.

No prime
p
and integer
k\geq 2
satisfy
\varphi(p^{k}+12)=\varphi(p^{k})+12
.

Proof.

Recall that for a prime power
p^{k}
,
\varphi(p^{k})=p^{k-1}(p-1)=p^{k}-p^{k-1}
. So we wish to show that

\varphi(p^{k}+12)=p^{k}-p^{k-1}+12

has no solutions.

First let
n=p^{k}
and
N=p^{k}+12
. We consider separate cases for when
p=2
,
p=3
, or
p\geq 5
.

Case 1:

p=2
. Then
n=2^{k}
and
N=2^{k}+12=4(2^{k-2}+3)
. If
k=2
, then
n=4
and
N=16
. But since
\varphi(16)=\varphi(4)+12
simplifies to
8=2+12
, which we know this is false. Thus
k\geq 3
in this case. Then, let
m=2^{k-2}+3
. Notice that since
k\geq 3
,
m
is always odd. Since
4
and
m
are guaranteed to be coprime, we can use the multiplicative property of Euler’s totient function:

\varphi(N)=\varphi(4)\cdot\varphi(m)=2\varphi(m).

Recall that for any integer
m
,
\varphi(m)\leq m-1
. Hence we can write

\varphi(N)=2\varphi(m)\leq 2(m-1)=2(2^{k-2}+2)=2^{k-1}+4.

However, recall that since the beginning, we wanted

\varphi(N)=\varphi(n)+12=\varphi(2^{k})+12=2^{k-1}+12.

Using substitution we can obtain

2^{k-1}+12\leq 2^{k-1}+4,

which is clearly false. Thus,
p\neq 2
.

Case 2:

p=3
. Then
n=3^{k}
and
N=3^{k}+12=3(3^{k-1}+4)
. Since
k\geq 2
, we know
m:=3^{k-1}+4
is an integer. We can now see that
m\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, meaning it is coprime to
3
. So we can use the multiplicative property of Euler’s totient function:

\varphi(N)=\varphi(3)\cdot\varphi(m)=2\varphi(m).

We wish that
\varphi(N)=\varphi(3^{k})+12=2\cdot 3^{k-1}+12
. Substituting gives

\displaystyle 2\varphi(m)

\displaystyle=2\cdot 3^{k-1}+12

\displaystyle\varphi(m)

\displaystyle=3^{k-1}+6.

Recall that for any integer
m
,
\varphi(m)\leq m-1
. Hence

\varphi(m)=\varphi(3^{k-1}+4)\leq 3^{k-1}+3.

Substituting gives

3^{k-1}+6\leq 3^{k-1}+3,

which is also clearly false. So
p\neq 3
.

Case 3:

p\geq 5
. We first show that
N
must be composite.

Assume
N
to be prime. Then
\varphi(N)=N-1=p^{k}+11
. Recall that we wish
\varphi(N)=p^{k}-p^{k-1}+12
. Using substitution, we get

p^{k}+11=p^{k}-p^{k-1}+12,

which simplifies to

p^{k-1}=1.

This means either
p=1
or
k=1
, both of which contradict our initial assumptions. Thus,
N
must be composite.

We now do a simple substitution to the equation
\varphi(N)=p^{k}-p^{k-1}+12
using
p^{k}=N-12
, giving us

\varphi(N)=N-12-p^{k-1}+12,

which simplifies to

(4)

N-\varphi(N)=p^{k-1}.

We must next show that
N
must be squarefree. We assume for contradiction that
N
is not squarefree. Then there exists some prime
r
such that
r^{2}\mid N
. Note that if
r^{2}\mid N
, then
r\mid N
and
r\mid\varphi(N)
. So
r\mid(N-\varphi(N))
. From Equation (
4
), we can substitute to get
r\mid p^{k-1}
, which implies
r=p
because
r\neq 1
. So
p^{2}\mid N
. But since
k\geq 2
, we also know
p^{2}\mid p^{k}
. Because
N=p^{k}+12
, it must be that
p^{2}\mid 12
. This strictly implies that
p=2
, which contradicts
p\geq 5
. So
p^{2}\nmid N
, hence
N
is squarefree.

Now that we know
N
is a composite squarefree integer, we have two cases.
N
has either exactly two distinct prime factors, or
N
has three or more distinct prime factors.

Subcase 3.1
:
N
has exactly two distinct prime factors. Let us call them
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, where
r_{1}<r_{2}
. We already know that
N
is squarefree, so we can say
N=r_{1}r_{2}
.

For any number
N
with two distinct prime factors, we can use the standard formula of Euler’s totient function to write

\varphi(N)=(r_{1}-1)(r_{2}-1)=N-r_{1}-r_{2}+1.

Substituting into (
4
), we get

\displaystyle N-(N-r_{1}-r_{2}+1)

\displaystyle=p^{k-1}

\displaystyle r_{1}+r_{2}-1

\displaystyle=p^{k-1}

\displaystyle r_{2}

\displaystyle=p^{k-1}+1-r_{1}.

To force a contradiction, we now wish to minimize
N
. Using Euler’s totient function formula, we can see that
\varphi(N)<N\left(1-\frac{1}{r_{1}}\right)
. Rearranging gives
\frac{\varphi(N)}{N}<1-\frac{1}{r_{1}}
. From Equation (
4
) we can get
\varphi(N)=N-p^{k-1}
. Hence, we can substitute this into the inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{N-p^{k-1}}{N}

\displaystyle<1-\frac{1}{r_{1}}

\displaystyle 1-\frac{p^{k-1}}{p^{k}+12}

\displaystyle<1-\frac{1}{r_{1}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{1}}

\displaystyle<\frac{p^{k-1}}{p^{k}+12}

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle>p+\frac{12}{p^{k-1}}.

Since
\frac{12}{p^{k-1}}
is strictly positive,
r_{1}
is strictly greater than
p
. Because both are odd primes, it follows that
r_{1}\geq p+2
and
r_{2}\geq p+4
.

Now that we have an expression for
r_{2}
in terms of
r_{1}
, we can write

N=r_{1}(p^{k-1}+1-r_{1}).

For fixed
p
and
k
, this is a quadratic in terms of
r_{1}
. For
r_{1}<\frac{p^{k-1}+1}{2}
,
N
is always increasing. Because
r_{1}<r_{2}=p^{k-1}+1-r_{1}
, we know
2r_{1}<p^{k-1}+1
. So it is indeed true that
r_{1}<\frac{p^{k-1}+1}{2}
, which means
r_{1}
lies on the increasing part of the function for
N
. If we wish to minimize
N
, then we also need to minimize
r_{1}
. Therefore,
r_{1}=p+2
. We substitute this into the expression for
N
:

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\geq(p+2)(p^{k-1}+1-(p+2))

\displaystyle p^{k}+12

\displaystyle\geq(p+2)(p^{k-1}-p-1)

\displaystyle p^{k}+12

\displaystyle\geq p^{k}-p^{2}-p+2p^{k-1}-2p-2

\displaystyle p^{2}+3p+14

\displaystyle\geq 2p^{k-1}.

Assume first that
k=2
. We know previously that
r_{2}=p^{k-1}+1-r_{1}
, so here we have
r_{2}=p+1-r_{1}
. We also know that
r_{1}\geq p+2
and
r_{2}\geq p+4
. Now we can substitute to get
p+1-(p+2)\geq p+4
. This simplifies to
p\leq-5
, which is impossible. So
k\neq 2
.

If
k=3
, then
2p^{k-1}=2p^{2}
. So

\displaystyle p^{2}+3p+14

\displaystyle\geq 2p^{2}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq p^{2}-3p-14.

It is easy to verify that for
p\geq 5
, the only prime solution is
p=5
. If
p=5
, then
N=5^{3}+12=137
, a prime number, which contradicts
N
being composite. So
k\neq 3
.

If
k\geq 4
, then
2p^{k-1}\geq 2p^{3}
. However, notice that since
p\geq 5
, we have
p^{3}\geq 3p^{2}
. So
2p^{3}\geq 6p^{2}
. We can substitute this and get

\displaystyle p^{2}+3p+14

\displaystyle\geq 6p^{2}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq 5p^{2}-3p-14

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq(5p+7)(p-2).

Since
p
is always positive, the only way for
(5p+7)(p-2)\leq 0
to hold is if
p\leq 2
. But this contradicts
p\geq 5
, hence
k
cannot be greater than or equal to
4
. This exhausts all possible

, and thus this subcase yields no solutions.

Subcase 3.2:

N
has three or more distinct prime factors. Recall that
N
is proven to be squarefree.

From Equation (
4
), we can divide by
N=p^{k}+12
to get

1-\frac{\varphi(N)}{N}=\frac{p^{k-1}}{p^{k}+12}.

Since
p^{k}+12>p^{k}
, we know
\frac{p^{k-1}}{p^{k}+12}<\frac{p^{k-1}}{p^{k}}=\frac{1}{p}
. So

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\varphi(N)}{N}

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{p}

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi(N)}{N}

\displaystyle>\frac{p-1}{p}.

The Euler’s totient function formula tells us

\frac{\varphi(N)}{N}=\prod_{r\mid N}\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)

for distinct primes
r
. Call
r_{1},r_{2},
and
r_{3}
the three smallest prime factors of
N
, where
r_{1}<r_{2}<r_{3}
. Recall that we established in the last subcase that
r_{1}\geq p+2
and
r_{2}\geq p+4
. We can write the following inequality:

\frac{\varphi(N)}{N}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{p+2}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p+4}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{r_{3}}\right)=\frac{p+1}{p+2}\cdot\frac{p+3}{p+4}\left(1-\frac{1}{r_{3}}\right).

Substituting this into the inequality
\frac{\varphi(N)}{N}>\frac{p-1}{p}
gives

\frac{p+1}{p+2}\cdot\frac{p+3}{p+4}\left(1-\frac{1}{r_{3}}\right)>\frac{p-1}{p}.

By isolating
1-\frac{1}{r_{3}}
and then expanding, we get

1-\frac{1}{r_{3}}>\frac{(p-1)(p+2)(p+4)}{p(p+1)(p+3)}=1+\frac{p^{2}-p-8}{p^{3}+4p^{2}+3p}.

Notice that since
p\geq 5
,
p^{2}-p-8
is strictly positive. So we can say
1-\frac{1}{r_{3}}>1
, which reduces to
\frac{1}{r_{3}}<0
. This is impossible, so this subcase has no solutions.

This exhausts all the cases and subcases.
∎

, and
\sigma(n+12)
, and tested both gap conditions. The search returned exactly 166 solutions. Every solution is congruent to
65
modulo
72
, which supports Stephan’s corrected observation. Every solution is also a squarefree semiprime
n=p(p+8)
, where
(p,p+2,p+6,p+8)
is a prime quadruplet. The complete list of solutions is given in Appendix
A
. The existing OEIS entry
A056777

[
2
]
has records up to
1{,}006{,}475{,}609
. We expand the search range by approximately
1{,}000
times.

Algorithmic Method

A flat linear sieve allocates arrays of size
N
. However, this is approximately
240\,\mathrm{GB}
at
N=10^{10}
. For
N=10^{12}
, we use a segmented sieve to avoid excessive memory usage. Using the sieve of Eratosthenes, we first precomputed approximately
78{,}000
small primes up to
\sqrt{N}\approx 10^{6}
. Then, the range
[2,N)
is processed in smaller blocks of size
B\approx 5\times 10^{5}
. For each block
[\ell,\ell+B)
, we use three arrays of length
B
to track the remaining cofactor, the running totient value
\varphi
, and the running sum of divisors
\sigma
.Each small prime
p
sieves its multiples within the block. It divides out the full
p
-power and multiplies in the corresponding
\varphi
and
\sigma
factors. Any residual factor greater than
1
after all small primes is a large prime, handled directly. A number
n
is prime if and only if
\varphi(n)=n-1
. Total memory is
O(B+\pi(\sqrt{N}))\approx 12\,\mathrm{MB}
, independent of
N
, and total work is
O(N\log\log N)
. For the
10^{12}
search, the algorithm was parallelized across 32 cores via OpenMP and executed on an AWS
c7i.8xlarge
instance, completing in
5536\,\mathrm{s}
.
