Path integral quantization of null bosonic strings with Carroll-Weyl ghosts
and Sourav Maji

Abstract
We revisit the path integral quantization of the null bosonic string from the viewpoint that all local gauge symmetries of the Carrollian worldsheet must be gauge fixed before the quantum theory is defined. In the tensile-string construction the b ​ c bc ghosts are the Faddeev-Popov determinant for fixing Diff × Weyl \mathrm{Diff}\times\mathrm{Weyl} . In the ILST null string this logic gives the BMS b ​ c bc system. However, a Carrollian worldsheet admits an additional volume-preserving Carroll-Weyl scaling, whose Hamiltonian generator is C 3 = P ⋅ X C_{3}=P\cdot X . Keeping this scaling as a genuine local gauge symmetry adds one more Faddeev-Popov row. The correct ghost system is therefore a b ​ c ​ s bcs system: the BMS b ​ c bc ghosts plus a scalar ghost s s and scalar antighost b s b^{s} for Carroll-Weyl scaling. We derive the revised path integral, the b ​ c ​ s bcs -ghost action, its residual symmetry equations, mode expansion, and its relation to the extended BMS algebra. The result changes the BRST complex and the anomaly problem: the usual D = 26 D=26 check based only on the old BMS b ​ c bc ghosts is a partially gauge-fixed calculation, while the Carroll-Weyl covariant quantum theory must include the s , b s s,b^{s} sector.

2.1
Faddeev-Popov determinant in tensile string theory

We begin with the Faddeev-Popov determinant in tensile string theory construction, because the point of the present paper is a direct
application of the same rule to a Carrollian gauge symmetry. The Euclidean

Z[\hat{g}]=\int\frac{[dX\,dg]}{\mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{Diff}\times\mathrm{Weyl})}\exp[-S_{X}[X,g]],\qquad S_{X}=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int_{M}\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,g^{1/2}g^{ab}\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X_{\mu}.

(2.1)

The gauge slice is

, and the Faddeev-Popov identity is written as following

1=\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}(g)\int[d\zeta]\,\delta(g-\hat{g}^{\zeta}),

(2.2)

as in
[
17

Here

2.2

Z[\hat{g}]=\int[dX]\,\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}(\hat{g})\,\exp[-S_{X}[X,\hat{g}]].

(2.3)

Near the identity, with
\delta\sigma^{a}
the vector generating diffeomorphisms and

\delta\omega
the Weyl parameter, the metric variation decomposes as

\delta g_{ab}=(2\delta\omega-\nabla_{c}\delta\sigma^{c})g_{ab}-2(P_{1}\delta\sigma)_{ab},

where

(P_{1}\delta\sigma)_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{a}\delta\sigma_{b}+\nabla_{b}\delta\sigma_{a}-g_{ab}\nabla_{c}\delta\sigma^{c}\right).

(2.5)

The Weyl part fixes the trace of

maps a vector into a traceless
symmetric tensor. This is why the ordinary tensile antighost

is traceless. Replacing the

(2.6)

This is the ghost determinant.
Equivalently, using tracelessness,

S_{g}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,\hat{g}^{1/2}b_{ab}\hat{\nabla}^{a}c^{b}.

(2.7)

In conformal coordinates this becomes

S_{g}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}z\,\left(b_{zz}\partial_{\bar{z}}c^{z}+b_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}\partial_{z}c^{\bar{z}}\right),

(2.8)

so

has weight
-1

[

There are two lessons we will use below. First, the ghost system is determined by the complete set of

is enlarged, the Faddeev-Popov operator is enlarged and the ghost complex changes.

3
Carroll-Weyl gauged null string

3.1
The two Carroll-Weyl scalings

We briefly review the two Carroll-Weyl scalings of a null-string worldsheet, following

[
19
]
. In an ordinary Lorentzian or Euclidean two-dimensional
geometry, the metric
\gamma_{ab}
admits the usual Weyl rescaling

\gamma_{ab}\ \rightarrow\ e^{2\phi(\sigma)}\gamma_{ab}.

(3.1)

This rescaling changes the local scale of the metric while leaving the coordinate system
unchanged. The important point emphasized in
[
19
]
is that the
Carrollian worldsheet of a null string has a richer geometric structure. Instead of a
non-degenerate metric, the intrinsic data are described by the Carrollian triple

\left(\ell_{a},\ v^{a},\ n_{a}\right),

(3.2)

where
v^{a}
is the kernel vector,
n_{a}
is the clock one-form normalized by

v^{a}n_{a}=1,

(3.3)

and
\ell_{a}
is the spatial one-form defining the degenerate spatial metric

h_{ab}=\ell_{a}\ell_{b}.

(3.4)

The associated two-dimensional volume form is

\epsilon_{ab}=n_{a}\ell_{b}-n_{b}\ell_{a}.

(3.5)

Because the Carrollian structure separates the temporal and spatial directions, one can
rescale the clock sector and the spatial sector independently. Thus one has

n_{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{\chi_{t}(\sigma)}n_{a},\qquad v^{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{-\chi_{t}(\sigma)}v^{a},

(3.6)

together with

\ell_{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{\chi_{s}(\sigma)}\ell_{a}.

(3.7)

The normalization condition
v^{a}n_{a}=1
is preserved by the first transformation. The
derived geometric objects then transform as

h_{ab}=\ell_{a}\ell_{b}\ \rightarrow\ e^{2\chi_{s}}h_{ab},

(3.8)

and

\epsilon_{ab}=n_{a}\ell_{b}-n_{b}\ell_{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{\chi_{t}+\chi_{s}}\epsilon_{ab}.

(3.9)

It is useful to decompose these two independent scalings into two special combinations.
The first is the symmetric combination

\phi:=\chi_{t}=\chi_{s}.

(3.10)

This is the Carrollian analogue of the ordinary Weyl rescaling, because both the volume
form and the spatial metric scale in the standard way

n_{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{\phi}n_{a},\qquad v^{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{-\phi}v^{a},\qquad\ell_{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{\phi}\ell_{a},

(3.11)

and therefore

\epsilon_{ab}\ \rightarrow\ e^{2\phi}\epsilon_{ab},\qquad h_{ab}\ \rightarrow\ e^{2\phi}h_{ab}.

(3.12)

For this reason, this transformation may be called the
volume-modulating

Carroll-Weyl scaling.

In two dimensions one may choose a basis in which the degenerate metric takes the form

h_{ab}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&h\end{pmatrix}.

(3.13)

Then the vector density

\mathcal{V}^{a}:=\sqrt{h}\,v^{a}

(3.14)

is invariant under the
\phi
-scaling. This object is the Carrollian analogue of

\sqrt{-\gamma}\gamma^{ab}
in the Polyakov formulation. Under worldsheet
diffeomorphisms it transforms as a vector density of weight
1/2
,

\delta_{\xi}\mathcal{V}^{a}=\xi^{b}\partial_{b}\mathcal{V}^{a}-\mathcal{V}^{b}\partial_{b}\xi^{a}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{b}\xi^{b})\mathcal{V}^{a}.

(3.15)

This should be compared with the transformation of the ordinary Polyakov combination

\sqrt{-\gamma}\gamma^{ab}
,

\delta_{\xi}\left(\sqrt{-\gamma}\gamma^{ab}\right)=\sqrt{-\gamma}\left(\nabla^{a}\xi^{b}+\nabla^{b}\xi^{a}+(\nabla_{c}\xi^{c})\gamma^{ab}\right),

(3.16)

where
\xi_{a}=\gamma_{ab}\xi^{b}
and
\nabla_{a}
is the covariant derivative associated with

\gamma_{ab}
.

The second and more intrinsically Carrollian combination is obtained by taking

\chi:=\chi_{t}=-\chi_{s}.

(3.17)

This gives

n_{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{\chi}n_{a},\qquad v^{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{-\chi}v^{a},\qquad\ell_{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{-\chi}\ell_{a}.

(3.18)

Consequently,

\mathcal{V}^{a}\ \rightarrow\ e^{-2\chi}\mathcal{V}^{a},\qquad h_{ab}\ \rightarrow\ e^{-2\chi}h_{ab},

(3.19)

whereas the volume form is left invariant

\epsilon_{ab}\ \rightarrow\ e^{\chi-\chi}\epsilon_{ab}=\epsilon_{ab}.

(3.20)

This is therefore a
volume-preserving Carroll-Weyl scaling
. It has no direct
counterpart in an ordinary Lorentzian worldsheet geometry, where the metric and the
volume form are tied together by non-degeneracy. The existence of this second scaling is
one of the central geometric observations of
[
19
]
. It is precisely
this extra Carrollian scaling option that motivates the gauged Carroll-Weyl formulation
of the null string and ultimately leads to the additional gauge structure absent in the
standard tensile-string Polyakov description.

3.2
Null string action and the constraints

The Carrollian worldsheet admits a second Weyl-type scaling not present in Lorentzian geometry

[
19
]
. In the normalization used in this paper the volume-preserving
Carroll-Weyl transformation acts as

\delta_{\chi}X^{\mu}=\chi X^{\mu},\qquad\delta_{\chi}V^{a}=-\chi V^{a}.

(3.21)

The ILST action is not invariant under (
3.21
) for generic
\chi(\tau,\sigma)
. The
standard remedy is to introduce a Carroll-Weyl connection
W_{a}
and define

D_{a}X^{\mu}=\partial_{a}X^{\mu}+W_{a}X^{\mu},\qquad\delta_{\chi}W_{a}=-\partial_{a}\chi.

(3.22)

Then

\delta_{\chi}(D_{a}X^{\mu})=\chi D_{a}X^{\mu},

(3.23)

and the gauged null-string action

S_{\mathrm{CW}}[X,V,W]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,V^{a}V^{b}D_{a}X\cdot D_{b}X=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,(\mathcal{D}X)^{2}

(3.24)

is invariant under local Carroll-Weyl transformations. Here

\mathcal{D}X^{\mu}\equiv V^{a}D_{a}X^{\mu}=V^{a}\partial_{a}X^{\mu}+\mathcal{W}X^{\mu},\qquad\mathcal{W}\equiv V^{a}W_{a}.

(3.25)

Only the contraction
\mathcal{W}
enters the action.

The combined infinitesimal diffeomorphism plus Carroll-Weyl transformations are

\displaystyle\delta_{\eta}X^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\xi^{a}\partial_{a}X^{\mu}+\chi X^{\mu},

(3.26)

\displaystyle\delta_{\eta}V^{a}

\displaystyle=-V^{b}\partial_{b}\xi^{a}+\xi^{b}\partial_{b}V^{a}+\frac{1}{2}V^{a}\partial_{b}\xi^{b}-\chi V^{a},

(3.27)

\displaystyle\delta_{\eta}W_{a}

\displaystyle=\xi^{b}\partial_{b}W_{a}+W_{b}\partial_{a}\xi^{b}-\partial_{a}\chi.

(3.28)

Under
\chi
alone,

\delta_{\chi}(\mathcal{D}X^{\mu})=\delta_{\chi}(V^{a}D_{a}X^{\mu})=-\chi V^{a}D_{a}X^{\mu}+\chi V^{a}D_{a}X^{\mu}=0,

(3.29)

so the Carroll-Weyl invariance of (
3.24
) is manifest. Under diffeomorphisms,

\mathcal{D}X^{\mu}
transforms as a density of weight
1/2
, so
(\mathcal{D}X)^{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma
is
diffeomorphism invariant.

The equations obtained by varying the auxiliary gauge fields are constraints. Define the canonical
momentum density

P_{\mu}\equiv\mathcal{D}X_{\mu}.

(3.30)

Varying
V^{a}
gives

\delta_{V}S_{\mathrm{CW}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,\delta V^{a}\,D_{a}X\cdot P,

(3.31)

so

P\cdot D_{a}X=0,\qquad a=0,1.

(3.32)

Varying
W_{a}
gives

\delta_{W}S_{\mathrm{CW}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,P\cdot X\,V^{a}\delta W_{a},

(3.33)

so the Carroll-Weyl constraint is

C_{3}\equiv P\cdot X=0.

(3.34)

After using
P\cdot D_{a}X=P\cdot\partial_{a}X+\mathcal{W}P\cdot X
, the full constraint set may be written as

C_{a}\equiv P\cdot\partial_{a}X=0,\qquad C_{3}\equiv P\cdot X=0.

(3.35)

In the gauge
V^{a}=(1,0)
,
\mathcal{W}=0
, one has
P_{\mu}=\dot{X}_{\mu}
, and the three independent
constraints are

C_{1}=P^{2}=0,\qquad C_{2}=P\cdot X^{\prime}=0,\qquad C_{3}=P\cdot X=0.

(3.36)

The third one is the new ingredient relative to the older ILST path integral.

4
Revised path integral and the
bcs
-ghost determinant

4.1
Gauge conditions

The unfixed Carroll-Weyl covariant path integral is formally

Z_{\mathrm{CW}}=\int\frac{DX\,DV\,DW}{\mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{Diff}\ltimes\mathrm{CW})}\exp\{iS_{\mathrm{CW}}[X,V,W]\}.

(4.1)

The semidirect product notation emphasizes that Carroll-Weyl scalings do not form an inert spectator
factor: diffeomorphisms act on the local scaling parameter by pullback, and the constraint generated by
the scaling has non-trivial brackets with the BMS constraints. A correct Faddeev-Popov treatment must
therefore divide by
\mathrm{Diff}\ltimes\mathrm{CW}
, not only by the diffeomorphism subgroup.

The gauge group is now generated by

\eta^{A}=(\epsilon^{0},\epsilon^{1},\lambda),

(4.2)

where
\lambda
is the Carroll-Weyl parameter. We fix the gauge by imposing

G^{0}=V^{0}-1=0,\qquad G^{1}=V^{1}=0,\qquad G^{s}=\mathcal{W}=V^{a}W_{a}=0.

(4.3)

The last condition is the diffeomorphism-invariant Carroll-Weyl gauge used in

[
19
,
20
]
. In the temporal gauge it is simply
W_{0}=0
.
The component of
W_{a}
orthogonal to
V^{a}
does not enter (
3.24
); it only contributes an
overall decoupled volume factor and will be suppressed below.

The gauge-preserving equations already reveal the physical change. Setting

\delta G^{0}=\delta G^{1}=\delta G^{s}=0
gives

\partial_{0}\epsilon^{1}=0,\qquad\partial_{0}\lambda=0,\qquad\partial_{0}\epsilon^{0}=\partial_{1}\epsilon^{1}-2\lambda.

(4.4)

Thus the residual transformations are labelled by three functions of the spatial circle

\epsilon^{1}=f(\sigma),\qquad\lambda=\chi(\sigma),\qquad\epsilon^{0}=\tau\big(f^{\prime}(\sigma)-2\chi(\sigma)\big)+g(\sigma).

(4.5)

The old BMS residual symmetry is recovered by setting
\chi=0
. The extra function
\chi

is precisely the residual Carroll-Weyl scaling. This is why the ghost equation below will contain

\partial_{0}c^{0}-\partial_{1}c^{1}+2s=0
, rather than the old BMS equation

\partial_{0}c^{0}-\partial_{1}c^{1}=0
.

Equivalently, the three gauge functions change linearly as

\begin{pmatrix}\delta G^{0}\\[2.0pt]
\delta G^{1}\\[2.0pt]
\delta G^{s}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{0}\epsilon^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{1}\epsilon^{1}-\lambda\\[2.0pt]
-\partial_{0}\epsilon^{1}\\[2.0pt]
-\partial_{0}\lambda\end{pmatrix}.

(4.6)

This already displays the three independent infinitesimal gauge directions that must be divided out in
the path integral. The first row is especially important: the same gauge condition
V^{0}=1
is moved
both by time reparametrizations and by Carroll-Weyl rescalings, which is the origin of the off-diagonal

-1
entry in the Faddeev-Popov matrix.

The Faddeev-Popov identity is

1=\int D\epsilon^{0}D\epsilon^{1}D\lambda\,\delta(G^{0,\eta})\delta(G^{1,\eta})\delta(G^{s,\eta})\det\mathcal{M},

(4.7)

where

\mathcal{M}^{A}{}_{B}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\left.\frac{\delta G^{A,\eta}(\sigma)}{\delta\eta^{B}(\sigma^{\prime})}\right|_{\eta=0},\qquad A,B\in\{0,1,s\}.

(4.8)

Here
G^{A,\eta}
denotes the gauge-fixing function
G^{A}
evaluated after a gauge transformation generated by
\eta^{B}=(\epsilon^{0},\epsilon^{1},\lambda)
. Infinitesimally,

G^{A,\eta}=G^{A}+\delta_{\eta}G^{A},\qquad\delta_{\eta}G^{A}=\mathcal{M}^{A}{}_{B}\,\eta^{B}\equiv\delta G^{A}.

(4.9)

The functional delta functions
\delta(G^{A,\eta})
enforce the gauge conditions by selecting the representative on each gauge orbit satisfying
G^{A}=0
.

After inserting (
4.7
), the path integral on the gauge slice is

Z_{\mathrm{CW}}=\int DX\,\det\mathcal{M}\,\exp\!\left\{\frac{i}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,\dot{X}^{2}\right\},

(4.10)

up to the decoupled volume coming from the component of
W_{a}
orthogonal to
V^{a}
.

4.2
The three-row Faddeev-Popov operator

Using (
3.27
), evaluated on
V^{a}=(1,0)
, we find

\displaystyle\delta V^{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{0}\epsilon^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{1}\epsilon^{1}-\lambda,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\delta V^{1}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{0}\epsilon^{1}.

(4.12)

The third gauge condition is slightly more instructive. Since
\mathcal{W}=V^{a}W_{a}
,

\delta\mathcal{W}=(\delta V^{a})W_{a}+V^{a}\delta W_{a}.

(4.13)

On the gauge slice
V^{a}=(1,0)
,
V^{a}W_{a}=0
, this gives

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle=(\delta V^{1})W_{1}+\delta W_{0}

\displaystyle=(-\partial_{0}\epsilon^{1})W_{1}+\left(W_{1}\partial_{0}\epsilon^{1}-\partial_{0}\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{0}\lambda.

(4.14)

The dependence on the decoupled spatial component
W_{1}
cancels, as it must.

We now extract the operator
\mathcal{M}
directly. By definition, the Faddeev-Popov operator is the
linear map from gauge parameters to gauge-condition variations

\delta G^{A}=\mathcal{M}^{A}{}_{B}\eta^{B},\qquad\eta^{B}=(\epsilon^{0},\epsilon^{1},\lambda),\qquad G^{A}=(G^{0},G^{1},G^{s}).

(4.15)

In components this says

\displaystyle\delta G^{0}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{0}{}_{0}\epsilon^{0}+\mathcal{M}^{0}{}_{1}\epsilon^{1}+\mathcal{M}^{0}{}_{s}\lambda,

(4.16)

\displaystyle\delta G^{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{1}{}_{0}\epsilon^{0}+\mathcal{M}^{1}{}_{1}\epsilon^{1}+\mathcal{M}^{1}{}_{s}\lambda,

(4.17)

\displaystyle\delta G^{s}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{s}{}_{0}\epsilon^{0}+\mathcal{M}^{s}{}_{1}\epsilon^{1}+\mathcal{M}^{s}{}_{s}\lambda.

(4.18)

Comparing (
4.16
)–(
4.18
) with
(
4.6
), or equivalently with
(
4.11
), (
4.12
), and (
4.14
), gives row by row

\displaystyle\delta G^{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{0}\epsilon^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{1}\epsilon^{1}-\lambda

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{M}^{0}{}_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{0},\qquad\mathcal{M}^{0}{}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{1},\qquad\mathcal{M}^{0}{}_{s}=-1,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\delta G^{1}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{0}\epsilon^{1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{M}^{1}{}_{0}=0,\qquad\mathcal{M}^{1}{}_{1}=-\partial_{0},\qquad\mathcal{M}^{1}{}_{s}=0,

(4.20)

\displaystyle\delta G^{s}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{0}\lambda

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{M}^{s}{}_{0}=0,\qquad\mathcal{M}^{s}{}_{1}=0,\qquad\mathcal{M}^{s}{}_{s}=-\partial_{0}.

(4.21)

Thus, ordered as
(G^{0},G^{1},G^{s})
and
(\epsilon^{0},\epsilon^{1},\lambda)
, the Faddeev-Popov
operator is

\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{0}&\frac{1}{2}\partial_{1}&-1\\
0&-\partial_{0}&0\\
0&0&-\partial_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.22)

The old BMS
bc
operator (
2.21
) is the upper-left block. The third column and
third row are the path-integral imprint of Carroll-Weyl scaling.
For example,
\mathcal{M}^{0}{}_{s}=-1
comes from the Carroll-Weyl variation

\delta_{\lambda}(V^{0}-1)=-\lambda
, while
\mathcal{M}^{s}{}_{s}=-\partial_{0}
comes from

\delta_{\lambda}(V^{a}W_{a})=-\partial_{0}\lambda
. These two entries are what distinguish the
Carroll-Weyl covariant determinant from the old two-row BMS determinant. A longer derivation of each
row is given in appendix
A
.
Since
\mathcal{M}
is triangular, its formal determinant is

\det\mathcal{M}=\det\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{0}\right)\det(-\partial_{0})\det(-\partial_{0}).

(4.23)

Although the determinant of the triangular Faddeev-Popov operator
\mathcal{M}
depends only on its diagonal entries, the ghost action depends on the full operator. The off-diagonal terms
\mathcal{M}_{01}
and
\mathcal{M}_{0s}
encode how a single gauge orbit moves through the gauge slice, namely that
G^{0}
is affected by both spatial diffeomorphisms and Carroll-Weyl transformations. Upon exponentiation, this gauge-orbit mixing is precisely what generates the local BMS ghost couplings
c^{1}\partial_{1}b^{0}
and the new Carroll-Weyl mixing
s\,b^{0}
, to be discussed in the next subsection.

4.3
Exponentiation and the
bcs
-ghost action

Introduce fermionic ghosts

c^{A}=(c^{0},c^{1},s),

(4.24)

where
s
is the Carroll-Weyl ghost, and antighosts

b_{A}=(2b^{0},2b^{1},b^{s}).

(4.25)

The factors of
2
in the first two antighosts are the same normalization that converts the old
two-row determinant into the standard BMS
bc
-ghost action
[
5
]
. The determinant is exponentiated by
the Grassmann Gaussian identity. For a finite-dimensional matrix
M^{A}{}_{B}
,

\det M=\int\prod_{A}db_{A}\,dc^{A}\,\exp\!\left[-b_{A}M^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}\right],

(4.26)

up to an overall normalization fixed by the ordering of the Berezin measure. The only ambiguity is the overall sign fixed by the orientation of the Berezin measure; since this sign is field independent, it is absorbed into the normalization of the path integral. A short proof of this Grassmann identity, including the measure convention, is given in Appendix
F
.

In a Lorentzian worldsheet
path integral we want the full gauge-fixed integrand to be of the form
\exp(iS)
. Therefore we write

\exp\!\left[-\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,b_{A}\mathcal{M}^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}\right]=\exp\!\left[i\left(-i\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,b_{A}\mathcal{M}^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}\right)\right].

(4.27)

Equivalently, the factors of
i
may be absorbed into a constant phase of the Grassmann determinant,
which is independent of the dynamical fields. With this convention,

\det\mathcal{M}=\int Db^{0}Db^{1}Db^{s}Dc^{0}Dc^{1}Ds\,\exp\{iS_{bcs}\},

(4.28)

and the ghost action is obtained from

iS_{bcs}\sim-i\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,b_{A}\mathcal{M}^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}.

(4.29)

Here
\sim
means equality up to field-independent determinant normalizations and the common
worldsheet factor
1/(2\pi)
, which is restored in (
4.38
). Equation
(
4.29
) is simply the Lorentzian rewriting of the Grassmann determinant
(
4.26
).
Substituting (
4.22
) gives, row by row,

\displaystyle-b_{A}\mathcal{M}^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}

\displaystyle=-2b^{0}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{0}c^{0}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{1}c^{1}-s\right)-2b^{1}(-\partial_{0}c^{1})-b^{s}(-\partial_{0}s)

\displaystyle=b^{0}\partial_{0}c^{0}-b^{0}\partial_{1}c^{1}+2b^{0}s+2b^{1}\partial_{0}c^{1}+b^{s}\partial_{0}s.

(4.30)

Thus the pre-integration-by-parts form is

\displaystyle-b_{A}\mathcal{M}^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}

\displaystyle=b^{0}\partial_{0}c^{0}-b^{0}\partial_{1}c^{1}+2b^{0}s+2b^{1}\partial_{0}c^{1}+b^{s}\partial_{0}s.

(4.31)

Here the cylinder is the closed-string worldsheet

\Sigma_{\mathrm{cyl}}=[\tau_{i},\tau_{f}]\times S^{1},\qquad\sigma\sim\sigma+2\pi,\qquad\int_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{cyl}}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma=\int_{\tau_{i}}^{\tau_{f}}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\sigma.

(4.32)

All fields are periodic around
S^{1}
. In deriving the local action we either fix the variations at

\tau_{i},\tau_{f}
or take fields with compact support in time, so total
\tau
-derivatives and total

\sigma
-derivatives do not contribute. Because the ghosts and antighosts are Grassmann odd,

\int_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{cyl}}}b\,\partial_{a}c=\int_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{cyl}}}c\,\partial_{a}b
for two
odd fields
b,c
. Therefore integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,b^{0}\partial_{0}c^{0}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,c^{0}\partial_{0}b^{0},

(4.33)

\displaystyle-\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,b^{0}\partial_{1}c^{1}

\displaystyle=-\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,c^{1}\partial_{1}b^{0},

(4.34)

\displaystyle 2\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,b^{1}\partial_{0}c^{1}

\displaystyle=2\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,c^{1}\partial_{0}b^{1},

(4.35)

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,b^{s}\partial_{0}s

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,s\partial_{0}b^{s},

(4.36)

\displaystyle 2b^{0}s

\displaystyle=-2sb^{0}.

(4.37)

Thus the gauge-fixed matter plus ghost action is

S_{\mathrm{gf}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,\left[\dot{X}^{2}+i\left(c^{0}\partial_{0}b^{0}-c^{1}\partial_{1}b^{0}+2c^{1}\partial_{0}b^{1}+s\partial_{0}b^{s}-2sb^{0}\right)\right].

(4.38)

Equation (
4.38
) is the central result of the path-integral revision. The first three ghost
terms are the BMS
bc
system. The last two terms are forced by the Carroll-Weyl determinant.
The kinetic term
s\partial_{0}b^{s}
comes from fixing
\mathcal{W}=0
, and the algebraic mixing

-2sb^{0}
comes from the fact that
V^{0}
itself scales under Carroll-Weyl transformations.
Appendix
B
gives a more detailed component derivation of
(
4.38
), including the Grassmann signs and the role of the off-diagonal entries of

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{CW}}
.

5
Equations of motion and mode expansion of the
bcs
system

We now derive the free equations of motion of the
bcs
system directly from (
4.38
).
The matter part is

S_{X}^{\mathrm{gf}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,\dot{X}^{2}.

(5.1)

Its variation is

\delta S_{X}^{\mathrm{gf}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,\dot{X}\cdot\partial_{0}\delta X=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,\ddot{X}\cdot\delta X,

(5.2)

where the cylinder boundary conditions of (
4.32
) have been used. Hence the
matter equation is

\ddot{X}^{\mu}=0,

(5.3)

as in the ordinary ILST gauge-fixed action.

For the ghosts, write

\mathcal{L}_{bcs}=i\left(c^{0}\partial_{0}b^{0}-c^{1}\partial_{1}b^{0}+2c^{1}\partial_{0}b^{1}+s\partial_{0}b^{s}-2sb^{0}\right).

(5.4)

Varying the ghosts
c^{0},c^{1},s
, which appear without derivatives, gives the antighost equations

\displaystyle\partial_{0}b^{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{1}b^{0}-\partial_{0}b^{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{0}b^{s}-2b^{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(5.5)

respectively. Varying the antighosts
b^{0},b^{1},b^{s}
and integrating by parts on

\Sigma_{\mathrm{cyl}}
gives the ghost equations

\displaystyle\partial_{0}c^{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{0}s

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{0}c^{0}-\partial_{1}c^{1}+2s

\displaystyle=0.

(5.6)

The last equation is precisely the ghost version of the gauge-preserving condition

\delta V^{0}=0
in (
4.11
); the new
s
term is not optional.
Appendix
C
gives the same variation with every sign displayed.

We solve these equations on the same cylinder. Since
\ddot{X}^{\mu}=0
, the embedding is linear in

\tau
. Expanding the two arbitrary
\sigma
-dependent functions in Fourier modes gives

\displaystyle X^{\mu}

\displaystyle=x^{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}p^{\mu}\tau+\frac{i}{2}\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{n}\left(A^{\mu}_{n}-in\tau B^{\mu}_{n}\right)e^{-in\sigma},

(5.7)

where the normalization has been chosen so that the canonical brackets take the standard tensionless
form. The equations
\partial_{0}c^{1}=0
and
\partial_{0}s=0
imply

\displaystyle c^{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}c_{n}e^{-in\sigma},

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}s_{n}e^{-in\sigma}.

(5.8)

The remaining ghost equation reads

\partial_{0}c^{0}=\partial_{1}c^{1}-2s=\sum_{n}(-inc_{n}-2s_{n})e^{-in\sigma}.

(5.9)

Integrating once in
\tau
gives

\displaystyle c^{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\left(\tilde{c}_{n}-in\tau c_{n}-2\tau s_{n}\right)e^{-in\sigma}.

(5.10)

Similarly,
\partial_{0}b^{0}=0
gives

b^{0}=\sum_{n}\tilde{b}_{n}e^{-in\sigma}.

(5.11)

The equation
\partial_{0}b^{1}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{1}b^{0}
gives

\displaystyle b^{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}(b_{n}-in\tau\tilde{b}_{n})e^{-in\sigma},

(5.12)

and
\partial_{0}b^{s}=2b^{0}
gives

\displaystyle b^{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}(r_{n}+2\tau\tilde{b}_{n})e^{-in\sigma}.

(5.13)

Collecting the ghost modes,

\displaystyle c^{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}c_{n}e^{-in\sigma},

(5.14)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}s_{n}e^{-in\sigma},

(5.15)

\displaystyle c^{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\left(\tilde{c}_{n}-in\tau c_{n}-2\tau s_{n}\right)e^{-in\sigma},

(5.16)

\displaystyle b^{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\tilde{b}_{n}e^{-in\sigma},

(5.17)

\displaystyle b^{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}(b_{n}-in\tau\tilde{b}_{n})e^{-in\sigma},

(5.18)

\displaystyle b^{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}(r_{n}+2\tau\tilde{b}_{n})e^{-in\sigma}.

(5.19)

The new terms in
c^{0}
and
b^{s}
are paired:
s
deforms the temporal diffeomorphism ghost, and

b^{0}
sources the Carroll-Weyl antighost.

Canonical quantization gives

[x^{\mu},p^{\nu}]=i\eta^{\mu\nu},\qquad[A^{\mu}_{m},B^{\nu}_{n}]=2m\,\eta^{\mu\nu}\delta_{m+n,0},

(5.20)

and

\{b_{n},c_{m}\}=\delta_{m+n,0},\qquad\{\tilde{b}_{n},\tilde{c}_{m}\}=\delta_{m+n,0},\qquad\{r_{n},s_{m}\}=\delta_{m+n,0}.

(5.21)

The algebraic term
-2sb^{0}
does not change the equal-time brackets because it contains no

\tau
-derivative. It does, however, change the Hamiltonian evolution:
s
enters the solution for

c^{0}
, and
b^{0}
enters the solution for
b^{s}
. This is the canonical trace of the same
off-diagonal Faddeev-Popov entry that produced the mixing term in the action. For a detailed derivation of the oscillator algebra (
5.21
), see Appendix
D
.

6
Extended BMS constraints and BRST structure

The gauge-fixed action (
4.38
) still carries the constraints generated by the three residual
gauge symmetries. For the matter fields the constraints are (
3.36
). Their smeared
forms are

C_{1}[f]=\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\,f(\sigma)C_{1}(\sigma),\quad C_{2}[g]=\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\,g(\sigma)C_{2}(\sigma),\quad C_{3}[k]=\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\,k(\sigma)C_{3}(\sigma).

(6.1)

Using the canonical Poisson brackets of
X^{\mu}
and
P_{\mu}
, one obtains the closed algebra

[
20
]

\{X^{\mu}(\sigma),P_{\nu}(\sigma^{\prime})\}=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}\,\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}).

(6.2)

The genuinely new bracket can be derived in one line:

\displaystyle\{C_{1}[f],C_{3}[g]\}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\,f(\sigma)g(\sigma^{\prime})\{P^{2}(\sigma),P(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot X(\sigma^{\prime})\}

\displaystyle=-2\int\mathrm{d}\sigma\,f(\sigma)g(\sigma)P^{2}(\sigma)=-2C_{1}[fg].

(6.3)

Thus
C_{3}
is not central: it rescales
C_{1}
, exactly as expected from

\delta_{\lambda}P_{\mu}=-\lambda P_{\mu}
,
\delta_{\lambda}X^{\mu}=\lambda X^{\mu}
. The remaining
brackets follow by the same distributional manipulations and give

\displaystyle\{C_{1}[f],C_{1}[g]\}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\{C_{1}[f],C_{2}[g]\}

\displaystyle=C_{1}[fg^{\prime}-f^{\prime}g],

(6.4)

\displaystyle\{C_{1}[f],C_{3}[g]\}

\displaystyle=-2C_{1}[fg],

\displaystyle\{C_{2}[f],C_{2}[g]\}

\displaystyle=C_{2}[fg^{\prime}-f^{\prime}g],

(6.5)

\displaystyle\{C_{2}[f],C_{3}[g]\}

\displaystyle=C_{3}[fg^{\prime}],

\displaystyle\{C_{3}[f],C_{3}[g]\}

\displaystyle=0.

(6.6)

With modes

L_{n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\sigma\,e^{-in\sigma}C_{2}(\sigma),\quad M_{n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\sigma\,e^{-in\sigma}C_{1}(\sigma),\quad S_{n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\sigma\,e^{-in\sigma}C_{3}(\sigma),

(6.7)

this becomes the extended BMS algebra

\displaystyle\{L_{m},L_{n}\}

\displaystyle=i(m-n)L_{m+n},

\displaystyle\{L_{m},M_{n}\}

\displaystyle=i(m-n)M_{m+n},

\displaystyle\{M_{m},M_{n}\}

\displaystyle=0,

(6.8)

\displaystyle\{L_{m},S_{n}\}

\displaystyle=inS_{m+n},

\displaystyle\{M_{m},S_{n}\}

\displaystyle=-2M_{m+n},

\displaystyle\{S_{m},S_{n}\}

\displaystyle=0.

(6.9)

The
L,M
subalgebra is BMS

. The

modes form a weight-one current under

, the Carroll-Weyl

The BRST charge must therefore contain three ghost families. A compact way to state the minimal
charge is to collect the constraints into
T_{A}=(L,M,S)
, the ghosts into

\mathsf{c}^{A}=(c,\tilde{c},s)
, and the antighost modes into

. If the quantum extended algebra is written as

[T_{A}{}_{m},T_{B}{}_{n}]=f_{AB}{}^{C}(m,n)\,T_{C}{}_{m+n}+K_{AB}(m)\delta_{m+n,0},

(6.10)

then the minimal BRST charge has the BFV form

Q_{\mathrm{BRST}}=\sum_{n,A}\mathsf{c}^{A}_{-n}T_{A}{}_{n}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,n}f_{AB}{}^{C}(m,n):\mathsf{c}^{A}_{-m}\mathsf{c}^{B}_{-n}\mathsf{b}_{C,m+n}:+Q_{\mathrm{intercept}}.

(6.11)

Here

denotes the possible quantum central extensions of the extended BMS–Carroll-Weyl algebra, while

represents the normal-ordering (intercept) contributions that may be required to restore BRST nilpotency at the quantum level.

For the

and

\displaystyle Q_{\mathrm{BRST}}^{2}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad K_{AB}=0\quad\text{for every sector of the extended algebra,}

(6.13)

\displaystyle\text{up to the standard intercept shifts.}

7
Conclusions and future directions

In this paper, we show that the path integral of the tensionless bosonic string must follow the complete Carrollian gauge orbit. When we keep the volume-preserving Carroll-Weyl scaling as a local symmetry, the temporal gauge
V^{a}=(1,0)
no longer fixes the gauge completely. We must also fix the Carroll-Weyl connection by imposing
V^{a}W_{a}=0
. The three gauge conditions

G^{A}=(V^{0}-1,\ V^{1},\ V^{a}W_{a})

then define the three-row Faddeev–Popov operator
\mathcal{M}
in (
4.22
). Exponentiating this full operator gives the
bcs
-ghost action (
4.38
). We recover the BMS
bc
system only after deleting the Carroll-Weyl row and column. Thus the BMS
bc
system describes a partially gauge-fixed theory, not the fully Carroll-Weyl covariant null string.
The central term in the new ghost action is

S_{bcs}\supset-\frac{i}{\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma\,s\,b^{0}.

This term is important because it is not a removable decoration. It is the local path-integral memory
of the fact that
V^{0}
transforms under Carroll-Weyl scaling. Equivalently, it is the ghost-level
reflection of the extended constraint algebra in which the Carroll-Weyl generator
S_{n}
has a
non-trivial bracket with the supertranslation generator
M_{n}
. Without this term the equations of
motion would incorrectly reduce to the old BMS ghost equations, the residual gauge algebra would be
represented incompletely, and the BRST operator would miss the ghost that enforces
P\cdot X=0
.

The same point affects anomaly and critical-dimension calculations. The
D=26

based on the BMS
bc
system tests only the
L_{n},M_{n}
part of the residual algebra
[
5
]
. The fully
gauged theory must test the matter plus
bcs
realization of the extended BMS algebra. A complete
quantum consistency analysis should compute all possible central terms in the
L
-,
M
-, and

S
-sectors, determine the normal-ordering constants in the extended BRST charge, and impose nilpotency in the enlarged Hilbert space. Only after this computation is done can one assign a final critical
dimension or intercept condition to the Carroll-Weyl covariant tensionless bosonic string.

There are several immediate directions, some of which will be developed in forthcoming work
[
7
]
. First, one can construct the complete stress tensor of the
bcs
system, including the improvement terms generated by the algebraic mixing
-2sb^{0}
, and use it to compute the quantum extended BMS algebra explicitly. Second, one can develop the corresponding BRST cohomology of physical states and vertex operators, together with the ghost-number selection rules associated with the
(s,r)
ghost pair. Third,
one can build the corresponding null-string scattering measure. In that problem the
bcs
action
is expected to control the insertions needed to divide by residual BMS-Carroll transformations, just as
the ordinary
bc
ghosts control conformal Killing zero modes and moduli in the tensile Polyakov
string. Fourth, the relation to ambitwistor strings and scattering equations

[
4
,
3
,
16
]
can be revisited with the Carroll-Weyl ghost
included from the beginning.

Another interesting direction is the Carroll-Weyl covariant quantization of
tensionless
p
-branes. While critical-dimension conditions have recently
been obtained from the residual worldvolume symmetry algebra in a fixed partial gauge

[
6
]
, it remains to understand how these results
are modified when the full Carroll-Weyl gauge symmetry and its associated
BRST structure are incorporated.

The analysis of this paper can be exteneded to tensionless superstrings, with worldsheet fermions
\psi^{\mu}
and local supersymmetry parameters
\epsilon^{\alpha}
. The bosonic analysis nevertheless gives a clear structural lesson: before introducing the BMS superghost sector, one must first enlarge the purely bosonic gauge sector itself. In a Carroll-Weyl covariant superstring formulation, the standard ghost content should therefore be extended schematically as

(bc)\oplus(\beta\gamma)\quad\longrightarrow\quad(bcs)\oplus(\beta\gamma\delta).

Here
\beta,\gamma
denote the usual superghosts associated with local worldsheet supersymmetry, while the additional field
\delta
schematically represents the super-Weyl partner required by the supersymmetric completion of Carroll-Weyl symmetry. Thus the
bcs
system derived here should be regarded as the minimal bosonic backbone on which any future Carroll-Weyl covariant quantization of tensionless superstrings must be built.

This appendix isolates the derivation of the ghost action from the determinant. The Faddeev-Popov

(B.3)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}c)^{s}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{0}s.

(B.4)

(B.7)

)–(
B.4

\Sigma_{\mathrm{cyl}}

\displaystyle\{b^{1}(\sigma),c^{1}(\sigma^{\prime})\}

\displaystyle=\pi\,\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}),

(D.7)

\displaystyle\{b^{s}(\sigma),s(\sigma^{\prime})\}

\displaystyle=2\pi\,\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}).

(D.8)

Notice that the factor of
\pi
instead of
2\pi
in (
D.7
) arises from the factor
2
multiplying
c^{1}\partial_{0}b^{1}
in the kinetic term.

We now substitute the mode expansions

\displaystyle c^{1}(\tau,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}c_{n}e^{-in\sigma},

(D.9)

\displaystyle s(\tau,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}s_{n}e^{-in\sigma},

(D.10)

\displaystyle c^{0}(\tau,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}(\tilde{c}_{n}-in\tau c_{n}-2\tau s_{n})e^{-in\sigma},

(D.11)

\displaystyle b^{0}(\tau,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\tilde{b}_{n}e^{-in\sigma},

(D.12)

\displaystyle b^{1}(\tau,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}(b_{n}-in\tau\tilde{b}_{n})e^{-in\sigma},

(D.13)

\displaystyle b^{s}(\tau,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}(r_{n}+2\tau\tilde{b}_{n})e^{-in\sigma}.

(D.14)

Using

\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{k}e^{-ik(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime})},

(D.15)

we first consider the pair
(b_{n},c_{n})
. Substituting
(
D.9
) and (
D.13
) into (
D.7
) gives

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,n}\{b_{m},c_{n}\}e^{-im\sigma}e^{-in\sigma^{\prime}}=\pi\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}e^{-ik\sigma}e^{ik\sigma^{\prime}}.

(D.16)

Matching Fourier coefficients yields

\{b_{m},c_{n}\}=\delta_{m+n,0}.

(D.17)

Next consider the pair

(\tilde{b}_{n},\tilde{c}_{n})
. Using
(
D.6
), (
D.11
) and (
D.12
) we obtain

\sum_{m,n}\{\tilde{b}_{m},\tilde{c}_{n}\}e^{-im\sigma}e^{-in\sigma^{\prime}}=\sum_{k}e^{-ik\sigma}e^{ik\sigma^{\prime}}.

(D.18)

The terms proportional to
c_{n}
and
s_{n}
do not contribute,
since they belong to different canonical sectors. Therefore

\{\tilde{b}_{m},\tilde{c}_{n}\}=\delta_{m+n,0}.

(D.19)

Finally, using (
D.8
), (
D.10
) and (
D.14
),

\sum_{m,n}\{r_{m},s_{n}\}e^{-im\sigma}e^{-in\sigma^{\prime}}=\sum_{k}e^{-ik\sigma}e^{ik\sigma^{\prime}}.

(D.20)

The
\tilde{b}_{n}
contribution in (
D.14
) again drops out
because it belongs to a different canonical pair. Hence

\{r_{m},s_{n}\}=\delta_{m+n,0}.

(D.21)

Combining (
D.17
), (
D.19
) and (
D.21
) gives the
oscillator algebra

\{b_{n},c_{m}\}=\delta_{m+n,0},\qquad\{\tilde{b}_{n},\tilde{c}_{m}\}=\delta_{m+n,0},\qquad\{r_{n},s_{m}\}=\delta_{m+n,0}.

(D.22)

This reproduces the mode algebra quoted in (
5.21
). The

-dependent terms appearing in

and

modify the
solutions of the equations of motion but not the symplectic form,
since they arise from the Hamiltonian evolution generated by the
mixing term

and do not introduce additional kinetic

Appendix E
Semidirect products and the Carroll-Weyl gauge group

In this appendix we explain why the gauge group of the Carroll-Weyl covariant null string is written as

\mathrm{Diff}\ltimes\mathrm{CW},

rather than the direct product

\mathrm{Diff}\times\mathrm{CW}.

The distinction is important because it is reflected directly in the
constraint algebra and in the structure of the Faddeev-Popov
determinant.

Direct and semidirect products

Suppose that a theory possesses two symmetry groups
G
and
H
.
If the two groups act independently, the full symmetry group is the
direct product

G\times H

(E.1)

At the infinitesimal level this means that generators belonging to the
two sectors commute,

[T_{G},T_{H}]=0

(E.2)

A semidirect product arises when one subgroup acts nontrivially on the
other. In this case the full symmetry group is written

G\ltimes H

(E.3)

The generators no longer commute. Instead,

[T_{G},T_{H}]\sim T_{H}

(E.4)

showing that transformations generated by
G
rotate or mix the
generators belonging to
H
.

A familiar example is the Euclidean group

ISO(d)=SO(d)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{d}

(E.5)

The translation generators
P_{i}
transform under rotations
J_{ij}
,

[J_{ij},P_{k}]=\delta_{jk}P_{i}-\delta_{ik}P_{j}

(E.6)

Thus rotations act nontrivially on translations, and the symmetry group
is a semidirect product rather than a direct product.

Semidirect products in gauge theories

The same phenomenon appears in local gauge theories whenever one gauge
transformation acts on the parameter of another gauge transformation.

Consider two local gauge symmetries with parameters
\xi
and

\lambda
. If under a
\xi
-transformation the second parameter
changes according to

\delta_{\xi}\lambda\neq 0

(E.7)

then the two gauge sectors are not independent. Acting with a

\xi
-transformation modifies a subsequent
\lambda
-transformation.

At the infinitesimal level one finds

[\delta_{\xi},\delta_{\lambda}]=\delta_{\delta_{\xi}\lambda}

(E.8)

which is again a transformation belonging to the
\lambda
-sector.
The gauge group therefore acquires the structure of a semidirect
product.

The essential criterion is simple: whenever one gauge transformation
acts nontrivially on the parameter of another gauge transformation, the
full gauge group is generically a semidirect product.

Diffeomorphisms and Weyl symmetry in the Polyakov string

The Polyakov action is invariant under worldsheet diffeomorphisms and
Weyl transformations,

\delta_{\xi}g_{ab}=\nabla_{a}\xi_{b}+\nabla_{b}\xi_{a}

(E.9)

and

\delta_{\omega}g_{ab}=2\omega g_{ab}

(E.10)

The Weyl parameter
\omega(\sigma)
is a local scalar function on the
worldsheet. Under a diffeomorphism generated by
\xi^{a}
,

\delta_{\xi}\omega=\xi^{a}\partial_{a}\omega

(E.11)

Consequently,

[\delta_{\xi},\delta_{\omega}]g_{ab}=2(\xi^{c}\partial_{c}\omega)g_{ab}

(E.12)

which may be rewritten as

[\delta_{\xi},\delta_{\omega}]=\delta_{\xi\cdot\partial\omega}

(E.13)

Thus diffeomorphisms act nontrivially on local Weyl transformations.
Strictly speaking, the local gauge group is therefore

\mathrm{Diff}\ltimes\mathrm{Weyl}

(E.14)

In practice this distinction is rarely emphasized. After conformal
gauge fixing the Weyl factor is removed through the trace part of the
metric variation, and the resulting Faddeev-Popov determinant is
represented solely by the usual
bc
-ghost system. Weyl symmetry does
not introduce an additional independent first-class constraint in the
physical phase space.

Diffeomorphisms and Carroll-Weyl symmetry in the null string

The Carroll-Weyl covariant null string possesses local
diffeomorphisms together with local Carroll-Weyl transformations.
The latter act on the embedding coordinates as

\delta_{\chi}X^{\mu}=\chi X^{\mu}

(E.15)

The Carroll-Weyl parameter
\chi(\tau,\sigma)
is again a local
worldsheet scalar. Under diffeomorphisms,

\delta_{\xi}\chi=\xi^{a}\partial_{a}\chi

(E.16)

Applying the commutator to the embedding coordinates gives

[\delta_{\xi},\delta_{\chi}]X^{\mu}=(\xi^{a}\partial_{a}\chi)X^{\mu}

(E.17)

Equivalently,

[\delta_{\xi},\delta_{\chi}]=\delta_{\xi\cdot\partial\chi}

(E.18)

Hence the Carroll-Weyl subgroup is acted upon nontrivially by
diffeomorphisms, and the gauge group takes the form

\mathrm{Diff}\ltimes\mathrm{CW}

(E.19)

Unlike the Polyakov string, however, the Carroll-Weyl symmetry is
generated by an independent first-class constraint,

C_{3}=P\cdot X

(E.20)

The Carroll-Weyl sector is therefore a genuine part of the physical
gauge algebra rather than merely a redundancy of auxiliary worldsheet
variables.

Constraint algebra interpretation

The semidirect-product structure is reflected directly in the
constraint algebra. The mode generators satisfy

\{L_{m},S_{n}\}=in\,S_{m+n}

(E.21)

where
L_{m}
generate superrotations and
S_{n}
generate
Carroll-Weyl transformations.

This relation has the same mathematical form as the Euclidean-group
commutator

[J,P]\sim P

(E.22)

The
S_{n}
generators transform nontrivially under the action of

L_{m}
and therefore do not form an independent commuting sector.

Furthermore,

\{M_{m},S_{n}\}=-2M_{m+n}

(E.23)

showing that Carroll-Weyl transformations act nontrivially on the
supertranslation generators as well.

The constraint algebra therefore realizes the semidirect-product
structure directly at the Hamiltonian level.

Implications for the Faddeev-Popov construction

The importance of the semidirect-product structure is that the
Carroll-Weyl symmetry cannot be treated as a spectator gauge factor.
Since
C_{3}=P\cdot X
is an independent first-class constraint, the
path integral must divide by the full gauge orbit generated by

\mathrm{Diff}\ltimes\mathrm{CW}

(E.24)

The resulting Faddeev-Popov operator therefore contains three gauge
directions rather than two. This enlargement introduces the additional
ghost-antighost pair
(s,b_{s})
and leads to the
bcs
-ghost system
derived in Section
4
.

From this viewpoint, the off-diagonal entries of the Faddeev-Popov
operator and the mixing term
sb_{0}
are the path-integral
manifestation of the semidirect-product structure of the gauge group.

Appendix F
Grassmann proof of the fermionic determinant identity

In this appendix we prove the finite-dimensional Grassmann identity

\det M=\int\prod_{A}db_{A}\,dc^{A}\,\exp\!\left[-b_{A}M^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}\right],

(F.1)

up to an overall convention-dependent normalization of the Berezin measure.
Here
M^{A}{}_{B}
is an
N\times N
matrix, and
b_{A},c^{A}
are independent
Grassmann variables,

b_{A}b_{B}=-b_{B}b_{A},\qquad c^{A}c^{B}=-c^{B}c^{A},\qquad b_{A}c^{B}=-c^{B}b_{A}.

(F.2)

We choose the orientation of the Berezin measure such that

\int\prod_{A}db_{A}\,dc^{A}\,c^{1}b_{1}c^{2}b_{2}\cdots c^{N}b_{N}=1.

(F.3)

A different ordering changes the result only by an overall sign independent
of
M
.

Using the Grassmann anticommutation relation, the exponent can be written as

-b_{A}M^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}=c^{B}M^{A}{}_{B}b_{A}.

(F.4)

Since the Grassmann variables are nilpotent, the exponential terminates

\exp\!\left[-b_{A}M^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{1}{k!}\left(c^{B}M^{A}{}_{B}b_{A}\right)^{k}.

(F.5)

The Berezin integral is nonzero only for the term containing all
N

variables
c^{A}
and all
N
variables
b_{A}
. Hence only the
k=N
term
contributes

\displaystyle\int\prod_{A}db_{A}\,dc^{A}\,\exp\!\left[-b_{A}M^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}M^{A_{1}}{}_{B_{1}}\cdots M^{A_{N}}{}_{B_{N}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\int\prod_{A}db_{A}\,dc^{A}\,c^{B_{1}}b_{A_{1}}\cdots c^{B_{N}}b_{A_{N}}.

(F.6)

By definition of the Berezin integral, the last integral is completely
antisymmetric separately in the upper indices
B_{i}
and in the lower indices

A_{i}
. With the convention (
F.3
), one has

\int\prod_{A}db_{A}\,dc^{A}\,c^{B_{1}}b_{A_{1}}\cdots c^{B_{N}}b_{A_{N}}=\epsilon^{B_{1}\cdots B_{N}}\epsilon_{A_{1}\cdots A_{N}}.

(F.7)

Substituting this into (
F.6
), we obtain

\int\prod_{A}db_{A}\,dc^{A}\,\exp\!\left[-b_{A}M^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}\right]=\frac{1}{N!}\epsilon_{A_{1}\cdots A_{N}}\epsilon^{B_{1}\cdots B_{N}}M^{A_{1}}{}_{B_{1}}\cdots M^{A_{N}}{}_{B_{N}}.

(F.8)

The right-hand side is precisely the standard epsilon-symbol expression for
the determinant

\det M=\frac{1}{N!}\epsilon_{A_{1}\cdots A_{N}}\epsilon^{B_{1}\cdots B_{N}}M^{A_{1}}{}_{B_{1}}\cdots M^{A_{N}}{}_{B_{N}}.

(F.9)

Therefore,

\det M=\int\prod_{A}db_{A}\,dc^{A}\,\exp\!\left[-b_{A}M^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}\right],

(F.10)

with the Berezin-measure orientation chosen as in
(
F.3
).

Equivalently, for a different ordering of the Berezin measure, one obtains

\int\prod_{A}db_{A}\,dc^{A}\,\exp\!\left[-b_{A}M^{A}{}_{B}c^{B}\right]=\pm\det M.

(F.11)

The sign is independent of
M
and can be absorbed into the normalization of
the path-integral measure. This is the finite-dimensional origin of the
Faddeev–Popov representation of a determinant by fermionic ghosts.
