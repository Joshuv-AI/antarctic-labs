 ABSTRACT
Accurate, independently verified geospatial data is essential for automated calibration, validation, and
operational decision-making. This study evaluated the positional accuracy of Vexcel Imaging™’s Ultra-
Cam® Osprey imagery (7.5 cm GSD) using globally distributed Continuously Operating Reference Sta-
tions (CORS) as independent control. Despite manufacturer claims of 15 cm horizontal accuracy, residual
errors were consistently one to two orders of magnitude larger, with no subset of imagery meeting preci-
sion thresholds. These discrepancies cannot be explained by normal photogrammetric or environmental
factors and raise concerns about the reliability of the imagery for high-precision tasks. The results demon-
strate that Vexcel imagery, in its current form, is unsuitable for workflows requiring rigorous spatial accu-
racy or automated verification. At the same time, the reproducible validation framework developed in this
study establishes a scalable method for assessing commercial imagery, ensuring that future products can be
independently and objectively verified before operational adoption.

 1. ACCURACY ASSESSMENT FRAMEWORK
The positional accuracy of orthoimagery is influenced by both sensor calibration and the quality of ground
control and elevation models applied during photogrammetric processing (Mikhail, Bethel, and McGlone
2023; U.S. Geological Survey 2023). Commercial products, such as those produced by Vexcel Imaging
(Vexcel Imaging 2022), advertise 15cm absolute accuracy; however, there is no published independent
verification of the Vexcel’s claimed accuracy.
The Continuously Operating Reference Station (CORS) network, operated under multiple national and
regional agencies and standardized through the International GNSS Service (IGS) and affiliated bodies,
such as the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) provides an ideal reference dataset
(Blewitt, Hammond, and Kreemer 2016; Altamimi, Métivier, and Collilieux 2017). CORS stations are
permanent Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) installations with positional accuracies on the order
of millimeters to centimeters (Houston 2016). By selecting a geographically diverse set of stations, this
study reduces bias and enables evaluation of imagery under varying acquisition environments, including
urban versus rural, flat versus mountainous terrain, and coastal versus inland settings (Blewitt, Hammond,
and Kreemer 2016).

Residual error is quantified as the two-dimensional Euclidean distance between the published International
Terrestrial Reference Frame (ITRF)-aligned positions of CORS antennas and their corresponding observed
positions in Vexcel imagery (Vexcel Imaging 2022). This measure captures both systematic and random
components of positional error.

 2. METHODOLOGY
2.1 CORS SELECTION AND QUALITY CONTROL
An initial set of 100 CORS stations was identified across multiple continents to maximize geographic di-
versity (Blewitt, Hammond, and Kreemer 2016). Stations included both urban and remote installations,
sourced from the NOAA CORS network (United States), the IGS network (Europe and Australia), and
regional networks in Australia and Japan (Altamimi, Métivier, and Collilieux 2017). Stations were consoli-
dated into a master spreadsheet, from which random samples were drawn. After preliminary inspection, 50
sites were retained for analysis.

2.2 COORDINATE FRAMEWORK
CORS site coordinates were originally referenced in ITRF2020 (epoch 2010). All coordinates were trans-
formed into ITRF2008 (epoch 2018) to align with Vexcel imagery specifications (Altamimi, Métivier, and
Collilieux 2017). Transformations to the imagery-projected coordinate reference system were performed
using seven-parameter Helmert transformations (Hofmann-Wellenhof, Lichtenegger, and Wasle 2017), en-
suring consistency across imagery and ground control points.

2.3 DATA SELECTION AND INGESTION
Vexcel ultra-high-resolution aerial imagery with a ground sample distance (GSD) of 7.5 cm was utilized
(Vexcel Imaging 2022). The archive spans over 35 countries and is acquired using the Vexcel UltraCam
Osprey sensor mounted on fixed-wing aircraft platforms. Analysis was performed on raw, unrectified im-
age frames together with their adjusted exterior orientation (EO) parameters to avoid distortions or interpo-
lation artifacts introduced during orthorectification (Zhang et al. 2023).
Imagery was accessed programmatically via the Vexcel Oriented Image Services Query Application Pro-
gramming Interface (API). CORS site coordinates referenced in ITRF2008 were converted into Well-
Known Text (WKT) format for API ingestion. The API returned raw raster frames in .png format and
associated metadata files in .json format. Raster images were batch-converted into baseline .tif files us-
ing IrfanView to ensure compatibility with standard photogrammetric workflows (Hernandez et al. 2018).
Adjusted EO parameters were extracted directly from the JSON metadata. Each record included the im-
age identifier, focal length, raster dimensions, and associated camera parameters (camera ID, sensor posi-
tion X, Y, Z, and orientation angles Omega, Phi, Kappa). These parameters were parsed, structured, and
sorted to facilitate automated error analysis and consistency checks across acquisition campaigns, support-
ing scalable calibration and validation workflows (Zhang et al. 2023; Mikhail, Bethel, and McGlone 2023).

2.4 RESIDUAL MEASUREMENT
Control points, imagery, and EO data were imported into Cardinal System’s VR Air Trig software. For
the 50 retained sites, manual point selection enabled precise sub-pixel identification of antenna locations
in the imagery. Using EO parameters, known CORS ground coordinates were projected into image space
(ground to image) to allow direct comparison with the measured pixel positions. Residuals were then com-
puted as Euclidean distances between imagery-identified and EO-projected coordinates in image space
(Hernandez et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2023). This integration of manual verification with automated EO-
based projection supports both accuracy and reproducibility in assessing sensor calibration performance
(Mikhail, Bethel, and McGlone 2023).

Site positions in the imagery were independently measured by multiple individuals to ensure consistency
and reduce observer bias. Published photographs, Google Street View imagery, and Google Earth data
were used as supplementary sources for cross-verification of the measurements.
Residuals were evaluated using multiple visualization and statistical approaches. Scatter plots of residuals
in image x- and y-directions quantified both magnitude and directional error. Histograms of residual mag-
nitudes provide a visualization of the probability distribution. Quiver plots visualized both magnitude and
direction of residual vectors, revealing potential systematic errors in camera calibration or datum trans-
formation (Zhang et al. 2023; Hernandez et al. 2018). Measured and projected points were overlaid with
arrows representing individual residuals to visually assess spatial consistency (Mikhail, Bethel, and Mc-
Glone 2023).
Together, these statistics and visualizations supported both global and localized evaluations, demonstrating
the value of automated calibration and validation for reproducible assessment of Vexcel’s 7.5 cm imagery
against independent geodetic control (Vexcel Imaging 2022; Zhang et al. 2023).

The scatter plot shows the residuals in the X and Y directions of point selections performed between the
author and multiple individuals. Most stations cluster near the origin, indicating strong agreement in point
selection. Site HNLC00USA shows larger residuals, highlighting an instance where selections differed sig-
nificantly. The overall spread provides insight into the consistency and potential bias of the point selection
process.

The scatter plot provides a spatial view of residuals directly in pixel units, allowing assessment of both the
magnitude and directional distribution of errors. While some clustering near the origin is observed, even
these “closest” points are hundreds of pixels off and therefore unusable for automatic sensor calibration or
accuracy verification. The plot also makes clear the range and dispersion of errors: many points are widely
scattered, demonstrating that large deviations are common and not confined to a few outliers.

The histogram provides a visualization of the distribution of residual magnitudes by counting observations

The residual vector plot was included to ease investigation of systematic error. It displays the magnitude,
orientation, and position of errors in the image space. Note, that the magnitude of the vectors doesn’t have
strong correlation with distance from the center of the image–which may have indicated an issue with ori-
entations or focal length. We observed that most of the longest vectors have strong x-components, but we
offer no interpretation because of that pattern is not easily attributable to an error source and there are no-
table exceptions (large error vectors with strong y components).

 4. CONCLUSION
Despite manufacturer claims, Vexcel’s controlled imagery data product is not ready for use in automated
calibration or validation. Accuracy was assessed by observing CORS GNSS antennas in the raw frame im-
ages, and residuals were consistently at least 100 times larger than the stated accuracy specification. These
residuals are far too large to be explained by atmospheric effects, lens distortion, or similar photogrammet-
ric factors. Misidentification of antennas could account for some of the error; however, while it is impossi-
ble to rule out every identification blunder, it is clear that not every-or even most-points were misidentified.
Similarly, coordinate system conversion errors could have produced larger discrepancies, but transforma-
tions were verified using multiple libraries and online conversion tools, making it highly unlikely that all
were incorrect. Given that even basic direct image georeferencing would be expected to produce substan-
tially smaller errors than those observed, the most plausible explanation is database or API errors. These
findings suggest that Vexcel imagery, in its current form, should not be used for high-precision mapping or
automated workflows. We recommend that Vexcel implement independent verification procedures and pro-
vide explicit positional uncertainty metadata. Finally, the reproducible framework developed in this study
enables scalable quality assessment of commercial imagery and can guide future evaluations of sensor ac-
curacy and data reliability.
