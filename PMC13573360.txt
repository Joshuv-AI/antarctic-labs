Construction and validation of a nomogram model incorporating the systemic immune-inflammation index for preoperative deep vein thrombosis risk in patients with traumatic lower limb fractures
Authors: Ailing Yang, Yamin Feng, Yang Shen, Sitong Liu, Huaiping Zhao, Hongli Guo, Tiantian Ren, Xiaoli Guan, Zhihong Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to explore the risk factors for preoperative deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in patients with traumatic lower-extremity fracture (TLEF), analyze the correlation and predictive efficacy of the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) in DVT development, and establish a corresponding nomogram prediction model.MethodThis study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 950 patients who underwent surgery for TLEF at our hospital between May 2022 and April 2024. The enrolled patients were randomly divided into the training group (665 cases) and the validation group (285 cases) at a 7:3 ratio. We performed univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses in the training dataset to screen independent risk factors for preoperative DVT in TLEF patients. R Studio was used to construct the nomogram model. The goodness-of-fit test and ROC curve were adopted to evaluate the efficacy of the model.ResultsAge, fracture site, SII, D-dimer and APTT were identified as independent predictive factors for preoperative DVT. The nomogram exhibited good agreement with the ideal model in both the training and validation datasets.ConclusionsThe nomogram prediction model for preoperative DVT risk in TLEF patients was developed by incorporating SII and other risk factors. It can assist clinical practitioners in early assessment of DVT development.Keywords: Traumatic lower extremity fractures, Deep vein thrombosis, Risk prediction model, Systemic immune-inflammation index

Introduction

Traumatic lower extremity fractures (TLEF) are fractures resulting from external trauma. According to the involved anatomical sites, TLEF can be divided into femoral fractures, tibial fractures, patellar fractures, foot fractures and other types [1]. Patients with TLEF often present with a hypercoagulable state and vascular endothelial injury. In addition, fractured limbs usually require immobilization, which puts these patients at high risk of developing lower extremity deep vein thrombosis (DVT) [2].DVT is a severe complication commonly observed in orthopedic patients with TLEF, and it may cause local pain and walking dysfunction [3]. Relevant clinical data show that the incidence of DVT after traumatic lower extremity fractures ranges from 10% to 60% [4–6]. Progressive DVT may lead to serious complications such as pulmonary embolism, post-thrombotic syndrome and even limb amputation [7], all of which greatly affect patient prognosis. Moreover, thrombus migration increases the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE), a major cause of morbidity and mortality in orthopedic patients [8].

Studies have reported that without effective thromboprophylaxis, the incidence of DVT reaches 30%–50% among fracture patients undergoing orthopedic surgery [9]. For patients with femoral fractures, the preoperative and postoperative DVT rates are as high as 90% and 84%, respectively [10]. Therefore, early preoperative prediction and identification of DVT in TLEF patients are of great clinical significance.

Research on prediction models for deep venous thrombosis (DVT) in patients with lower limb fractures is still in the exploratory stage. A major controversy lies in the inconsistent risk factors adopted across different studies. Zheng et al. [9] found that elevated D-dimer, fibrinogen, fibrin degradation products and platelet counts were independent risk factors for preoperative DVT in patients with lower limb fractures. By contrast, Wang et al. [10] reported that VTE events, time from injury to DVT screening, BMI, peripheral vascular disease and lower albumin level, as well as elevated D-dimer, were associated with preoperative deep vein thrombosis among elderly patients with intertrochanteric femoral fractures. In addition, another study conducted by Wang et al. [11] demonstrated that the Caprini score, D-dimer and fibrinogen (FBG) were independent risk factors for VTE in patients with lower limb fractures.

Most of the above studies focused on conventional clinical indicators. Apart from these routine parameters, inflammatory molecules and immune cells are important contributors to thrombosis, and systemic inflammation increases the risk of deep venous thrombosis [12]. Composite hematological markers of inflammation and immunity are useful for perioperative risk assessment and prognostic stratification in diverse clinical settings [13–15]. As a comprehensive indicator of systemic inflammation and local immune responses, the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII = platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count) is closely associated with various types of thrombosis [16].Similarly, studies incorporating inflammatory and immune-related markers into thrombosis prediction models for lower limb fractures have adopted different combinations of risk factors [12, 17, 18].Accordingly, this study aimed to explore the predictive performance of SII combined with conventional clinical risk factors for DVT in patients with lower limb fractures, so as to provide a practical and widely applicable reference for clinical risk assessment.

Materials and methods

Participants

This retrospective cohort study analyzed the clinical data of 950 patients with TLEF who were hospitalized at the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University from May 2022 to April 2024.

The study was conducted following Helsinki Declaration and gained approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University (2023 A-091). Inclusion criteria encompassed: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) underwent surgical treatment for TLEF; (3) no history of hematological diseases; (4) complete and accurate clinical data without recording errors. The exclusion criteria included: (1) a personal history of venous thrombosis; (2) presence of mental, language, or consciousness disorders; (3) combined multiple traumatic injuries. According to the ratio of 7:3, the cases were randomly divided into the training group (665 cases) and the validation group (285 cases).

Data collection and definitions

Relevant clinical data were retrospectively extracted from the patients’ electronic medical records, including baseline demographics: age, gender, body mass index (BMI); comorbidities and medical history: hypertension, diabetes mellitus, history of malignant tumors, smoking history, drinking history; medication use: anticoagulant use; injury characteristics: fracture site (femur, patella, tibia and fibula, ankle), injury mechanism (high/low energy trauma); Routine laboratory parameters on admission: white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count (N), lymphocyte count (L), monocyte count (M), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), platelet (PLT), glucose (Glu), potassium (K), sodium (Na), chlorine (Cl), calcium (Ca), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), indirect bilirubin (IBIL), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C), prothrombin time (PT), prothrombin activity (PTA), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), fibrinogen (FIB), thrombin time (TT), D-dimer (D-D), fibrinogen degradation products (FDP); outcome indicator: DVT diagnostic outcomes. For patients with multiple medical records, only the most recent admission data were included in the analysis.Systemic Immune - Inflammation Index (SII) = platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count (PLT × N/L).

Diagnosis of DVT

The diagnosis of DVT was made in accordance with the Common Venous Disease Diagnosis and Treatment Specification (2022 Edition) [19], formulated by the Venous Group of the Vascular Surgeons Branch of the Chinese Medical Association. All patients underwent venous Doppler ultrasonography, and the diagnostic criteria were defined as follows: (1) Solid echoes of varying intensities were observed within the venous lumen, partially or completely occluding the vascular cavity; (2) The venous lumen failed to collapse completely or exhibited partial collapse under probe compression; (3) In cases of complete deep venous embolism, color Doppler and pulsed Doppler detected no blood flow signals at the lesion site, as well as the proximal and distal segments of the vessel, and no enhanced blood flow was observed following distal limb compression; (4) In cases of partial deep venous embolism, pulsed Doppler revealed a continuous blood flow spectrum at the non-embolized vascular segment without respiratory fluctuation. Color Doppler showed no intraluminal filling flow, while a small amount of blood flow could be detected in some patients after distal limb compression.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 was used for general statistical analysis. The normality of all continuous variables was verified using the Shapiro‑Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared via independent‑samples t‑test, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed using the Mann‑Whitney U nonparametric test. Categorical variables were presented as a number and a percentage, and between-group diferences were examined by Chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Prior to model development, all enrolled patients were randomly allocated into a at a 7:3 ratio for split‑sample external validation. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate binary logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for preoperative deep venous thrombosis (DVT) in patients with traumatic lower extremity fractures, while R 4.3.4 was applied for nomogram construction and model validation.

The concordance index (C‑index) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) were calculated to evaluate model discrimination, alongside model sensitivity and specificity. AUC values of 0.5–0.7, 0.7–0.9, and ≥ 0.9 were defined as poor, moderate, and excellent discriminatory ability, respectively [20]. The Hosmer‑Lemeshow (H‑L) goodness‑of‑fit test was performed to assess model calibration, in which P > 0.05 indicated favorable consistency between model‑predicted probabilities and actual clinical outcomes. Calibration curves were further plotted to visually confirm the agreement between predicted DVT risks and observed DVT incidence. Meanwhile, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to calculate the net clinical benefit of the model at different threshold probabilities, further verifying the clinical application value of the prediction model. In addition, bootstrap internal validation with 1000 resamples was performed to evaluate the stability, discrimination and calibration of the prediction nomogram [21]. All statistical analyses were two‑tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

This study included a total of 950 patients, with a mean age of 52.47 ± 18.72 years. The overall incidence of preoperative DVT was 24.7% (n = 235). The training dataset consisted of 665 patients, among whom 169 developed preoperative DVT, corresponding to an incidence of 25.4%. The validation dataset included 285 patients, with 66 cases of preoperative DVT and an incidence of 23.1%.

Predictive nomogram development

The collected data was randomly sampled in a 7:3 ratio and divided into training and validation groups. The training group data was used to construct the nomogram, as shown in Fig. 1 (Femur fracture = 0, Patella fracture = 1, tibiofibular fracture = 2, Ankle fracture = 4).The ROC curve of the training group was plotted to validate the predictive performance of the nomogram (Fig. 2A). The results showed that the area under the curve (AUC) of the model was 0.786 (95% CI: 0.741–0.832), and the C-index was 0.777, with a sensitivity of 74.4% and a specificity of 70.6%. Internal validation was further performed using the validation dataset. The corresponding AUC was 0.774 (95% CI: 0.704–0.845), and the C-index was 0.778, with a sensitivity of 70.3% and a specificity of 81.4% (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

The present study included 950 patients with TLEF, among whom 235 cases developed preoperative DVT, with an incidence of 24.7%. This result is consistent with the findings reported by Zheng et al. [9], highlighting the importance of strengthened preoperative thromboprophylaxis for TLEF patients. Given the dynamic progression of DVT, identifying preoperative risk factors and implementing individualized risk stratification are critical for screening high-risk populations, which can substantially optimize early risk warning and facilitate targeted prophylactic interventions [22]. Accordingly, we established a predictive nomogram based on age, fracture site, SII, D-dimer, and APTT. The nomogram yielded AUC values of 0.786 in the training cohort and 0.774 in the validation cohort, demonstrating satisfactory discriminatory efficacy for predicting preoperative DVT in patients with TLEF. The H–L goodness-of-fit test confirmed good calibration of the model, with the predicted DVT probabilities closely consistent with the actual observed incidence.Importantly, all predictive factors incorporated in this nomogram are routine clinical and laboratory indicators obtained upon admission, which are easily accessible and low-cost. Clinicians can rapidly complete risk scoring based on patients’ baseline admission data to accurately screen patients at high risk of DVT. This allows high-risk individuals to receive targeted confirmatory examinations and timely thromboprophylaxis, effectively reducing the risk of missed preoperative DVT diagnosis. Independent of advanced imaging equipment and sophisticated laboratory tests, this model is particularly suitable for primary medical institutions with limited resources. It can efficiently identify patients at high preoperative DVT risk following lower extremity fractures and serves as a simple and effective evaluation tool for standardized risk screening and individualized prevention of preoperative DVT in clinical practice.

Age serves as a crucial predictive factor for preoperative DVT in patients with TLEF. The possible reason is that advancing age induces structural and functional deterioration of the vascular wall, including decreased vascular elasticity and impaired endothelial function. These changes increase blood viscosity and disrupt coagulation balance, thereby promoting thrombus formation [23, 24]. Additionally, aging leads to progressive declines in immune and metabolic functions. Abnormal inflammatory responses and immune dysfunction further exacerbate vascular endothelial injury and accelerate thrombogenesis, ultimately increasing the incidence of preoperative DVT [25, 26]. The present findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that DVT risk increases with advancing age [27, 28]. For instance, an epidemiological study by Stein et al. reported that the DVT incidence was approximately 160 per 100,000 in patients aged 20–69 years, whereas the rate rose sharply to 655 per 100,000 in patients aged 70 years and older [29]. As age is an uncontrollable risk factor, clinicians should pay close attention to elderly TLEF patients in clinical practice, perform standardized preoperative DVT risk assessment, and deliver individualized targeted prophylaxis to reduce the incidence of preoperative DVT.

Fracture site is another vital independent predictive factor for preoperative DVT in patients with TLEF. The present study revealed distinct preoperative DVT incidences across different fracture sites: femoral fractures exhibited the highest DVT rate of 35.7% (99/277), followed by peri-knee fractures at 27.9% (17/61), tibiofibular fractures at 23.8% (38/160), and foot and ankle fractures at merely 9.0% (15/166). Multivariate analysis further confirmed that fracture site serves as an independent risk factor for preoperative DVT in TLEF patients. Accordingly, clinical DVT prevention and intervention strategies should be comprehensively formulated according to specific fracture locations. These findings are consistent with previous clinical studies. Wang et al. [30] retrospectively analyzed 159 femoral fracture cases and 59 patellar fracture cases and reported a high preoperative DVT incidence of 40.2% in patients with femoral fractures. Zhang et al. [31] reviewed 152 cases of tibial shaft fractures and identified a preoperative thrombosis incidence of 26.9%. Furthermore, Ma et al. [32] retrospectively enrolled 537 patients with foot fractures, among whom 28 cases developed preoperative DVT, yielding a low incidence of 5.2%. Collectively, these external data corroborate our results, demonstrating that the preoperative thrombosis incidence increases with the proximal shift of the fracture site. Therefore, clinicians should fully consider fracture site during preoperative DVT risk assessment, with particular focus on high-risk fracture types. Standardized DVT prevention and perioperative management should be prioritized for patients with femoral fractures. Routine preoperative anticoagulation and individualized anticoagulant strategies tailored to different fracture sites are essential to effectively reduce the overall incidence of preoperative DVT in TLEF patients.

SII is also an important independent predictive factor for preoperative DVT in TLEF patients and has attracted extensive clinical attention in recent years. Compared with conventional single inflammatory biomarkers, SII is a composite indicator integrating platelet count, neutrophil count, and lymphocyte count. Specifically, platelets reflect systemic thrombotic tendency, neutrophils represent the severity of inflammatory responses, and lymphocytes indicate the status of host immune function. Although inflammation and thrombosis are relatively independent physiological processes, emerging evidence suggests close biological interactions between these two pathological mechanisms [33]. The crosstalk between inflammatory activation and thrombotic events has been widely verified in various clinical conditions [34]. A retrospective cohort study by Zeng et al. [12] adopted SII, BMI, and NLR to evaluate preoperative DVT risk in elderly patients with femoral intertrochanteric fractures and validated that SII is a powerful predictive indicator for DVT occurrence. Moreover, SII has also demonstrated favorable predictive value for postoperative thrombosis after knee arthroplasty [35]. Although the exact molecular mechanism underlying the association between elevated SII and preoperative DVT in TLEF patients remains unclear, the present study provides robust clinical evidence for the potential correlation between systemic immune-inflammatory status and preoperative DVT development in TLEF populations.

Prolonged APTT serves as a protective factor against preoperative DVT, whereas shortened APTT increases the risk of preoperative DVT in TLEF patients. The possible reason is that APTT is an essential indicator reflecting the functional status of the endogenous coagulation pathway. Coagulation dysfunction and clinical administration of anticoagulant agents can elevate APTT levels, while hypercoagulable states and thrombotic predisposition contribute to decreased APTT values. Reduced APTT accelerates the synthesis and release of endogenous clotting factors, aggravates blood hypercoagulability, and consequently increases the incidence of preoperative DVT. Consistent with the present results, Xiong et al. [36] found that patients with thrombosis exhibited significantly shorter APTT compared with non-thrombotic individuals, and that elevated APTT within the normal range acted as a protective factor against preoperative DVT in patients with knee osteoarthritis. Similarly, Li et al. [37] also verified that prolonged APTT exerts a protective effect on lower extremity DVT formation and exhibits moderate diagnostic value for DVT screening. Therefore, clinicians should closely monitor preoperative APTT levels in TLEF patients. Timely pharmacological regulation and targeted prophylactic interventions should be performed for patients with decreased APTT to reduce the occurrence of preoperative DVT.

As a classic and critical laboratory indicator, D-dimer also serves as an important independent predictive factor for preoperative DVT in TLEF patients. D-dimer is a specific degradation product of cross-linked fibrin during fibrinolysis, and its serum concentration directly reflects thrombotic activity and dynamically characterizes the balance between coagulation and fibrinolysis. Elevated D-dimer levels are closely correlated with increased blood viscosity and enhanced hypercoagulability, rendering it a reliable noninvasive biomarker for thrombotic diseases [38, 39]. Once DVT develops, massive intravascular fibrin polymer accumulation activates sustained fibrinolytic responses, generates large amounts of D-dimer, and consequently leads to markedly elevated serum D-dimer levels. During DVT progression, the systemic coagulation state shifts toward hypercoagulability, which shortens thrombin activation time, disrupts coagulation-fibrinolysis homeostasis, and further promotes thrombus formation and progression [40]. Nevertheless, although D-dimer possesses high sensitivity for DVT screening, its clinical specificity is relatively poor. Multiple confounding conditions, including traumatic fracture, malignant tumors, infection, and advanced age, can significantly increase D-dimer levels, thereby limiting its standalone predictive value in elderly populations [41, 42]. Therefore, relying solely on D-dimer for early preoperative DVT prediction is insufficient. Combined assessment with other independent risk factors, such as age and SII, is required to optimize predictive performance and improve the accuracy of preoperative DVT risk stratification in TLEF patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, all participants were enrolled from a single medical center, which may cause selection bias. Further multicenter, large-sample prospective studies are needed to perform external validation, improve the external validity of the prediction model and the reliability of the research findings. Second, the optimal cut-off value of SII was not explored in the present study, which will be investigated in our future research. Third, only admission biochemical data were analyzed in this study. Given that biochemical markers fluctuate dynamically over time, serial changes in these indicators may better reflect preoperative DVT risk. Future studies may adopt a longitudinal design to collect biochemical data at multiple time points. Analyzing the dynamic trends of relevant biomarkers can achieve more accurate evaluation of preoperative DVT risk in TLEF patients and further improve the efficacy of clinical risk prediction. Lastly, potential confounding indicators, such as prior surgical history and long-term bedridden status, will be incorporated into subsequent studies to further optimize confounding factor analysis.

Conclusions

In this study, age, fracture site, SII, D-dimer, and APTT were identified as independent predictive factors for preoperative DVT in TLEF patients. The risk prediction model and corresponding nomogram constructed in this study can efficiently assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients and implementing timely prophylactic interventions to reduce the incidence of preoperative DVT.
