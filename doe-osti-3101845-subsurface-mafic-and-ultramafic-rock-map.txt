Project Objectives and Outcome Summary
Achieving durable, gigaton-scale carbon dioxide (CO₂) removal will require the development of
storage strategies that are both permanent and scalable. Among the available options, carbon
mineralization in mafic and ultramafic rocks offers a uniquely robust pathway for long-term CO₂
sequestration, converting CO₂ into thermodynamically stable carbonate minerals. While surface
exposures of these lithologies have been extensively studied, particularly in ophiolitic and
volcanic settings, the potential for carbon mineralization in the subsurface remains
comparatively underexplored at national scale. This gap limits the ability to assess storage
capacity, accessibility, and deployment pathways for mineralization-based carbon management
in the United States.
The SubMAP-CO₂ project (Subsurface Mafic and Ultramafic Rock Mapping and Analysis for
Carbon Mineralization) addresses this critical need by systematically identifying, characterizing,
and evaluating subsurface mafic and ultramafic rock formations across the conterminous United
States. Funded by the U.S. Department of Energy under Funding Opportunity Announcement
DE-FOA-0002614 (Carbon Management, Area of Interest 4: Carbon Storage Technology) and
supported by Grant DE-FE0032249 (July 1, 2023–December 31, 2025), this effort represents
the first coordinated attempt to develop a comprehensive, three-dimensional national framework
for assessing the suitability of subsurface (ultra)mafic rocks as permanent CO₂ sinks.

A central goal of SubMAP-CO₂ is to move beyond surface-based geological inventories by
integrating subsurface data derived from well penetrations, core descriptions, and geophysical
interpretations. These data are used to map the distribution, geometry, and volume of mafic and
ultramafic rock bodies at depth, providing quantitative constraints on potential storage capacity.
Complementary laboratory experiments conducted on representative core and outcrop samples
quantify carbonation reactivity and reaction kinetics, linking lithology, mineralogy, and alteration
state to CO₂ uptake potential. Together, these datasets enable a more realistic assessment of
mineralization feasibility under subsurface conditions. We also carried out a source-to-sink
analysis that integrates geologic suitability with proximity to industrial CO₂ sources,
infrastructure, land and water use, and deployment feasibility. Applying these constraints
reduces the accessible storage area by more than 80%. The most favorable opportunities are
concentrated in relatively small, spatially discrete regions with strong source–sink alignment,
particularly in the central-northern U.S. near the Great Lakes and along parts of the East Coast.
While potential exists along portions of the West Coast, distance to major CO₂ sources remains
a limiting factor.

In addition to carbon storage, the data generated through SubMAP-CO₂ are relevant to broader
subsurface resource questions, including critical mineral systems and emerging interest in
geologic hydrogen associated with mafic and ultramafic rocks.

All data products developed under this project are disseminated through the GeoMAFIC publicly
accessible web portal at https://maps.beg.utexas.edu/geomafic/), designed to facilitate
transparency, reuse, and collaboration. The portal provides interactive maps of subsurface mafic
and ultramafic rock units and ultramafic outcrop samples, locations of wells intersecting

Abstract
Mafic and ultramafic rocks are increasingly recognized as critical components of strategies to
address energy security and climate change. Despite their importance, detailed knowledge of
their distribution, composition, and three-dimensional geometry remains limited in the United
States—especially in the subsurface, where large volumes of these rocks could be utilized for
carbon mineralization, geologic hydrogen, and critical mineral resources. The GeoMAFIC portal
is designed to disseminate these data through a publicly accessible web platform
(https://maps.beg.utexas.edu/geomafic/), facilitating transparency, reuse, and collaboration. The
portal provides interactive maps of subsurface mafic and ultramafic rock units and ultramafic
outcrop samples, locations of wells intersecting (ultra)mafic basement, detailed petrographic
and geochemical datasets, volumetric and storage potential estimates, and results from
carbonation experiments. By making these data openly available, GeoMAFIC aims to accelerate
research, inform future pilot and demonstration projects, and support the deployment of
commercial-scale operations targeting mafic and ultramafic lithologies in the United States.

Introduction
Mafic and ultramafic rocks are increasingly recognized as critical components of strategies to
address energy security and climate change. These lithologies are enriched in divalent cations
such as Fe²⁺, Mg²⁺, and Ca²⁺, as well as other metals essential for clean-energy technologies
such as Ni, Co, platinum group elements (PGE), and Cu. In addition to serving as important
sources of critical minerals, they provide a unique geologic medium for carbon dioxide (CO₂)
sequestration through carbon mineralization and for the generation of natural hydrogen (H₂), a
clean and renewable energy resource (Gislason et al., 2010; Mayhew et al., 2013; Blondes et
al., 2019; Zgonnik et al., 2019; Kelemen et al., 2020;; Reich and Simon, 2024, Templeton et al.,
2024).
Carbon mineralization in mafic and ultramafic rocks offers a durable and permanent mechanism
for CO₂ storage by converting CO₂ into stable carbonate minerals such as magnesite and
calcite (Oelkers et al., 2008; Zhang and DePaolo, 2017; Kelemen et al., 2019; Raza et al.,
2023). This process is driven by reactions between CO₂ and divalent cations released during
mineral dissolution, for example:

 5

 Mg₂SiO₄ (olivine) + 2CO₂ → 2MgCO₃ (magnesite) + SiO₂ (quartz)
In parallel, water–rock interactions during serpentinization promote the oxidation of Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺,
generating hydrogen gas:
                                   3FeO + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
This triple capacity—as a medium for permanent CO₂ storage, a source of hydrogen, and a
source of critical minerals—positions mafic and ultramafic rocks as key targets for integrated
energy and carbon management systems (McCollom and Bach, 2009; Albers et al., 2022;
Jackson et al., 2024).
Despite their importance, mafic—and especially ultramafic—rocks in the subsurface of the
United States remain poorly characterized. Assessing their potential for carbon mineralization,
natural hydrogen generation, and critical mineral resources requires detailed knowledge of their
distribution, composition, and three-dimensional geometry. Although surface outcrop exposures
provide useful mineralogical and structural insights, they are often weathered and may not
accurately reflect subsurface conditions where these processes occur. Robust evaluation
therefore depends on approaches that can resolve subsurface composition, geometry,
volumetric extent, and petrophysical properties.
At the national scale, major data gaps persist. Previous efforts (e.g., Krevor et al., 2009;
Blondes et al., 2019) established an important framework for mapping mafic and ultramafic
rocks, but subsurface ultramafic units remain poorly constrained due to limited drilling and
sparse sampling. This uncertainty limits reliable estimates of CO₂ storage capacity, hydrogen
generation potential, and critical mineral prospectivity.
To address this gap, we conducted a national-scale assessment of mafic and ultramafic rocks
within the uppermost 5 km of the U.S. continental crust. This work involved compiling a
database of historical well penetrations into basement lithologies, with emphasis on mafic and
ultramafic rocks; mapping and estimating the volume of prospective units using aeromagnetic
data; and collecting and analyzing core and outcrop samples for mineralogical and geochemical
characterization. Laboratory carbonation experiments were used to quantify carbon uptake
potential in representative ultramafic samples.
All results are being made publicly available through GeoMAFIC, an open-access platform that
compiles spatial, petrographic, mineralogical, geochemical, and volumetric data for mafic and
ultramafic units across the United States. By integrating these datasets with experimental
constraints on carbonation kinetics and reactivity, GeoMAFIC enables more realistic estimates
of CO₂ storage potential. The platform also supports evaluation of natural hydrogen generation
and critical mineral resources, providing a unified framework for multi-resource assessment.
Data products are accessible through an interactive web portal
(https://maps.beg.utexas.edu/geomafic/), promoting transparency, reproducibility, and
collaboration. By extending existing geologic frameworks into the subsurface and grounding
them in direct observations and measurements, GeoMAFIC establishes a foundation for
identifying and prioritizing targets for future pilot, demonstration, and commercial-scale
deployment of carbon mineralization, hydrogen production, and critical mineral extraction in the
United States.
Below we describe the content and different components of the interactive GeoMAFIC
database.

Methodology
Outcrop and Subsurface Sample Suite
Outcrop samples for this study were collected across a range of ultramafic terranes in the
western and northeastern United States, with a primary focus on California and the Pacific
Northwest, and additional coverage in Texas, New York, and Arizona. The suite was designed to
capture variability in protolith composition and structural setting, with an emphasis on relatively
unaltered materials.
In Washington, sampling targeted dunite and peridotite from Bellingham and the Leavenworth
area, as well as sites on Vashon Island and the San Juan Islands. These samples are
predominantly serpentinized peridotites. In Oregon, sampling near Brookings focused on the
Josephine ophiolite and associated coastal exposures (Harper, 1984). California constitutes the
largest component of the dataset and spans several tectonic provinces. Samples from the Coast
Ranges are largely serpentinite and serpentinized peridotite of the Franciscan Complex (Bailey,
1964; Shervais et al., 2004). In the Klamath Mountains, sampling focused on major ultramafic
bodies, including the Trinity ophiolite (Quick, 1981; Carter and Quick, 1987). In the Sierra
Nevada foothills, comparatively fresh dunite and peridotite preserve visible olivine and
orthopyroxene with limited alteration. Additional samples from the eastern margin of the Central
Valley represent more isolated ultramafic occurrences. In Texas, serpentinite samples include
both foliated and massive varieties of the Coal Creek serpentinite, alongside a less
serpentinized sample from Badu Hill. In upstate New York, a suite of carbonated serpentinites
captures advanced stages of alteration. In Arizona, a serpentinized pyroxenite from Cemetery
Ridge represents a more pyroxene-rich ultramafic protolith.
A central objective of this project was to expand the characterization of subsurface mafic and
ultramafic lithologies in the U.S. Accordingly, sampling targeted these rock types within the
Midcontinent Rift in Iowa and Texas, with additional basement samples collected in Tennessee.
In Iowa, samples were obtained from multiple drill sites (Sharp, Finnegan, and C-1 through C-5)
provided by the Iowa Geological Survey, representing a wide range of mafic to ultramafic
compositions. New thin sections were prepared for analysis. The Sharp and Finnegan cores are
dominated by basalt and altered basalt, consistent with volcanic or shallow intrusive crust. In
contrast, the C-1 through C-5 suite includes more compositionally diverse intervals, such as
pyroxenite, cyclic and massive dunite, lamprophyre–pyroxenite dike material, gabbro, and
anorthosite. In Texas, the dataset is derived from the Nellie No. 1 borehole, which penetrated a
mafic to ultramafic layered intrusion in the Permian Basin, and consists of cuttings spanning
depths of approximately 5,850 to 18,500 ft. A total of ~250 thin sections previously described by
Kargi (1993) and Kargi and Barnes (1995) were reimaged and analyzed for this study. In
Tennessee, sampling focused on crystalline basement rocks from the LADD #1 and Carthage
basement wells, provided by the Tennessee Geological Survey. These samples comprise
gabbroic to troctolitic lithologies representative of mafic intrusive complexes, collected from
depths of approximately 4,300 to 7,700 ft.

Full thin-section photomicrographs
Full thin-section photomicrographs of all samples were acquired at the Bureau of Economic
Geology’s e-MAGE Lab using a Zeiss Axio Imager A2 microscope. High-resolution
photomicrographs were collected in both plane-polarized light (PPL) and cross-polarized light
(XPL). To capture the entire thin section, an automated motorized stage acquired a grid of
overlapping image tiles to ensure spatial continuity. These frames were subsequently integrated
into seamless, high-resolution mosaics using the ZEN software stitching module, which
corrected for vignetting and aligned the tiles into a single large-format composite for detailed
petrographic analysis. Each image consists of approximately 120 to 130 stitched tiles.

X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)
Mineralogical analysis of a select suite of samples was conducted using a Bruker D8 Advance
X-ray diffractometer at the Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences, Jackson School of
Geosciences. Powdered samples were prepared by grinding rock specimens to a fine
consistency to ensure homogeneity and eliminate grain-size effects, following standard
protocols to avoid contamination. Raw diffraction patterns were processed using automated
mineralogy identification features within the MDI JADE software suite.

SEM-EDS
Elemental maps and semi-quantitative mineral chemical analyses of selected samples were
performed using a Zeiss Sigma field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) at the e-
MAGE Lab. The instrument is equipped with a GEMINI column, a five-axis motorized stage, and
an Oxford X-Max 50 silicon drift detector (SDD) for energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS), and was operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV.
Semi-quantitative mineralogical assessment of primary phases was conducted using EDS, with
elemental quantification and phase identification performed in Oxford Aztec software by
correlating stoichiometric ratios with backscattered electron (BSE) intensity. Large-area image
mosaics, consisting of 60–100 individual tiles, were acquired at 150×–250× magnification.
These mosaics integrate secondary electron (SE), BSE, and EDS signals to map textural and
compositional relationships. Semi-quantitative mineral compositions were obtained for
representative minerals in each sample using spot analyses.

 14

Modal Mineralogy
We developed an in-house, Python-based workflow to derive modal mineralogy from scanning
electron microscope (SEM)–based energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) maps,
integrating high-resolution compositional imaging with supervised machine learning
classification.
SEM-EDS mapping was first conducted on each sample following the methods described above
to generate spatially resolved datasets that capture both textural and chemical variability at the
pixel scale. Mineral classes were then defined based on elemental compositions and
characteristic EDS spectra. Using a graphical user interface, representative regions within each
image were manually delineated, and polygons were assigned to specific mineral phases to
serve as training data for the classification algorithm.
A supervised machine learning model was subsequently trained on these labeled datasets and
applied to the full SEM-EDS maps. The algorithm classifies each pixel according to its
mineralogical signature, enabling automated and consistent identification of mineral phases
across the entire image. The workflow produces classified images in which mineral phases are
visualized as distinct, color-coded domains. These outputs, along with intermediate diagnostic
plots, allow for iterative validation and refinement of the training dataset to ensure accurate
phase discrimination. Finally, modal mineralogy is quantified by calculating the relative
abundance of each mineral phase based on pixel counts normalized to the total mapped area.
The resulting dataset provides a robust estimate of mineral proportions for a representative area
within each sample, derived directly from high-resolution SEM-EDS maps.

Whole-rock geochemistry
Major, minor, and trace element compositions were determined by SGS laboratories using a
combination of borate fusion wavelength-dispersive X-ray fluorescence (WD-XRF) and sodium
peroxide fusion followed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-
OES) and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Major and minor element
oxides were analyzed by WD-XRF on homogenized glass disks prepared by lithium
tetraborate/lithium metaborate fusion of dried, pulverized samples. Loss on ignition (LOI) was
determined separately by gravimetric analysis at 1000°C and incorporated into matrix
corrections.
Trace and selected major elements were analyzed on representative powdered aliquots using
sodium peroxide fusion, followed by dissolution in HNO₃ and analysis by ICP-OES and ICP-MS.
This method provided concentrations for a 55-element suite, including major, minor, and trace
constituents, as well as critical metals relevant to carbon mineralization, hydrogen generation,
and resource assessment. Analytical quality control included the routine use of blanks,
duplicates, certified reference materials, and calibration checks in accordance with ISO/IEC
17025-accredited laboratory procedures.
Due to its relevance for hydrogen generation from rocks, ferrous iron content was determined by
potassium dichromate titration. Samples were digested at high temperature using sulfuric and
hydrofluoric acids. Residual HF was converted to fluoroboric acid by the addition of boric acid
immediately following digestion. The samples were then rapidly cooled, and ferrous iron was
titrated with a standardized potassium dichromate solution, oxidizing Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺. The endpoint
was determined visually using barium diphenylamine sulfonate as an external indicator. Ferrous
iron is reported as FeO, calculated by multiplying measured Fe²⁺ concentrations by a factor of
1.286.

Well Penetrations
In addition to extensive sample collection, we compiled a database of wells that penetrate
basement rocks, with particular emphasis on identifying cores and wells intersecting subsurface
mafic and ultramafic lithologies.
Most of the well data incorporated into the GeoMAFIC portal were provided by state geological
surveys. In states where lithologic information is available, datasets were obtained directly from
these surveys and are based on core, cuttings, and well reports, providing the most reliable
constraints on basement composition. In states lacking direct lithologic descriptions, well
penetration depths were compared with existing basement geologic maps; wells were correlated
with mapped basement units, and depths were classified according to interpreted lithology.
Finally, thirteen states lack sufficient well control or publicly available data, precluding
meaningful characterization of basement penetrations in those areas.

Volumetric estimates from gravity and aeromagnetic data
We used Bouguer gravity and magnetic data to estimate the volumes of high-susceptibility
bodies in the subsurface of the United States, focusing on nine high-priority regions. Most of the
data used in this study are publicly available through the USGS (e.g., Kucks, 1999; Bankey et
al., 2002; Bankey, 2006; Ravat et al., 2009ep). In two cases, we incorporated non-public
datasets: one for the Pecos region in West Texas, including a high-spatial-resolution dataset
licensed from Getech Pvt Ltd., and another consisting of a high-resolution PACES gravity
dataset for California provided by the California Geological Survey.

3D visualization of high-susceptibility (HS) bodies
To visualize the distribution and volumetric characteristics of mafic and ultramafic bodies in the
subsurface across the United States, we developed an ArcGIS-based 3D web portal that
integrates geophysical data, volumetric modeling, and interactive visualization tools.
The resulting 3D models from the magnetic inversion were exported and incorporated into an
interactive web platform. The portal was developed using a beta version of the ArcGIS Maps
SDK for JavaScript and allows users to explore each locality dynamically by rotating, zooming,
and selecting individual bodies to retrieve associated spatial and volumetric information (Figures
3 and 4). Volumetric outputs generated using SimPEG and Python workflows were imported into
the 3D environment as point clouds through a multi-step conversion process (Figure 5). Each
high-susceptibility body is visualized in three dimensions within its geological context.

Carbon mineralization experiments
Core plugs measuring 8 mm in diameter were prepared from 18 different outcrop samples. For
each sample, five replicate cores were produced, resulting in a total of 90 core samples. CO₂-
saturated water was generated using deionized (DI) water carbonated with a SodaStream
device at a concentration of six pumps per liter.

 19

Each core sample was placed into a glass vial sealed with a rubber cap, and 6 mL of the CO₂-
saturated water was added at room temperature. The sealed vials containing both fluid and rock
were then transferred to an oven maintained at 90°C to initiate the reaction. The first set of
samples was retrieved after 24 hours. Fluid extraction was performed using a needle inserted
through the rubber cap, allowing sampling without exposing the system to atmospheric
conditions.
Extracted fluids were filtered using Cole-Parmer PTFE chromatography syringe filters with a
pore size of 0.45 µm and a diameter of 25 mm to remove suspended particles. A small aliquot of
the filtered fluid was used to measure pH with a micro-pH analyzer. The remaining filtered fluid
was immediately transferred into helium-flushed glass vials to minimize oxidation, and
subsequently acidified with 0.1 mL of 16 M HNO₃ to preserve dissolved species for analysis.
Following fluid extraction, the solid samples were removed from the vials and gently rinsed with
DI water to prevent the formation of secondary precipitates during drying. The rinsed solids
were then left to dry overnight under ambient conditions before being archived in individual
plastic containers.
To maintain CO₂ availability throughout the experiment, the remaining samples were periodically
replenished with CO₂ gas on days 1 and 3 by injecting gas through the vial septa using a
syringe and needle. Additional sample sets were retrieved after 4, 12, 19, and 27 days, and the
same fluid extraction, filtration, and preservation procedures were applied.
Dissolution rates were assessed by analyzing fluids collected at 1, 4, 12, 19, and 27 days from
the start of the experiment. To ensure that magnesium and iron remained in solution, a strict
extraction and analytical protocol was implemented. Retrieved aqueous samples were filtered to
remove particulates and any precipitates larger than 0.45 µm. The filtered fluids were
transferred into helium-filled vials to prevent oxidation, as helium provides an inert atmosphere
that minimizes unwanted chemical reactions. Immediate acidification with nitric acid ensured
dissolution of target elements and stabilization of the samples prior to analysis.
Upon removal from the reaction vessels, rock samples were rinsed with DI water to prevent the
precipitation of carbonate or other mineral phases during evaporation.

Carbon uptake calculations
To estimate the scalability of the carbonation experiments, we developed a methodology to
quantify the extent of carbonation in different rock types by comparing dissolved magnesium
concentrations in the fluid between peak (maximum) values and post-plateau conditions,
following Lacinska et al. (2017). In that study, the potential for carbon uptake via magnesium
carbonate precipitation was assessed using dissolved Mg concentrations, with the difference
between peak and final Mg values serving as a proxy for the amount of Mg removed from
solution. It was assumed that 50% of this difference represented the fraction of magnesium
effectively available for mineralization, accounting for potential losses and incomplete reaction
pathways.
Using this available magnesium, theoretical precipitation of three carbonate minerals—
magnesite (MgCO₃), hydromagnesite (Mg₅(CO₃)₄(OH)₂·4H₂O), and nesquehonite
(MgCO₃·3H₂O)—was calculated based on stoichiometry and molecular weights. The number of
moles and total mass of each mineral that could form were determined, along with the
corresponding CO₂ uptake per mole of mineral produced. These values were then scaled to a
reference fluid volume of 100,000 liters to approximate a realistic system and enable
comparison across samples. Final carbon uptake potential was expressed both as total CO₂

sequestration mass and as CO₂ uptake per ton of rock, providing a comparative framework for
evaluating mineralization potential across lithologies and mineral assemblages. Among the
three minerals, magnesite contains the highest proportion of CO₂ per unit mass because it is
anhydrous and more thermodynamically stable. However, it is also less likely to form under low-
temperature, near-surface conditions. In contrast, hydromagnesite and nesquehonite
incorporate significant amounts of water into their crystal structures, resulting in lower CO₂
uptake per unit of mineral produced but potentially greater feasibility under surface conditions.
For our study, calculations were based on the maximum magnesium concentrations measured
in fluids at 90 °C after 27 days of reaction. A true plateau was not reached within the
experimental duration, likely due to continued Mg undersaturation at the end of the run.
Because the reactions had not reached completion, our estimates assume that 100% and 50%
of the peak dissolved magnesium in the fluid is eventually sequestered in carbonate form,
respectively.

Carbon mineralization upscaling estimates
To assess feasible carbon storage areas in the United States, we applied seven different
estimation methods to calculate the CO₂ storage potential at various representative sites. These
methods follow Steinthorsdottir et al. (2024) and were adapted or derived from previously
published approaches, including volume-based estimates (Gislason et al., 2010; Mitchinson et
al., 2020, 2024), CO₂ dissolution capacity (Lu et al., 2022), experimentally derived reaction rates
(Lacinska et al., 2017; Vanderzee et al., 2019; Wynands and Dipple, 2022), and natural
analogues (Steinthorsdottir et al., 2022). For all calculations, the available rock volume of each
rock body was estimated based on the volumetric calculations obtained from our geophysical
methods.
Each method incorporates a series of assumptions regarding subsurface parameters, including
the thickness of the target rock body, bulk rock density, porosity, the type and abundance of
carbonate minerals expected to form, and the specific mineralogical composition of the
mafic/ultramafic body. To ensure comparability among the six approaches, these assumptions
were standardized as much as possible. Except for the method adapted from Gislason et al.
(2010), all calculations used a standard serpentinite density of 2800 kg/m³, consistent with
moderately to highly serpentinized rock (Mitchinson et al., 2020; Cutts et al., 2021). However,
serpentinite densities can vary from approximately 2550 to 3200 kg/m³, depending on the
degree of serpentinization and carbonate alteration (Toft et al., 1990; Cutts et al., 2021).
Mineral-specific stoichiometric formulas were used in all estimates to calculate theoretical CO₂
uptake capacities.
Method 1a (modified from Gislason et al., 2010)
Estimates CO₂ storage based on a serpentinite body with 2% porosity, assuming only 10% of
that pore volume is eventually filled with nesquehonite or hydromagnesite. This method
captures a conservative scenario for in-situ carbonation via mineral precipitation.
Method 1b (modified from Gislason et al., 2010)
Extends Method 1a by assuming a higher porosity of 6% and complete (100%) filling of pore
space with carbonate minerals, representing an upper-bound potential for mineral trapping
within the rock.
Method 2a (modified from Lu et al., 2022)
Calculates storage potential based on labile magnesium release in dissolution experiments at
0.1 bar pCO₂, with hydromagnesite as the expected product. The rock composition modeled is
serpentinized dunite, using experimental data to estimate reactivity and mineralization potential.

 21

Method 2b (modified from Lu et al., 2022)
Follows the same approach as 2a but applies it to serpentinized harzburgite, accounting for its
different mineralogical composition. This method highlights variability in reactivity depending on
rock type and degree of serpentinization.
Method 3 (modified from Steinthorsdottir et al., 2022)
Uses field observations of naturally carbonated serpentinites (ophicarbonates) that contain
sequestered CO₂ in the form of carbonate minerals (0.82-5.56 wt% CO2). These analogues
provide insight into long-term natural mineralization processes in ultramafic rocks.
Method 4 (modified from Vanderzee et al., 2019)
Applies a stoichiometric conversion from labile MgO content (assumed at 2.3 wt.%) to potential
CO₂ sequestration as hydromagnesite. This method uses a generalized factor to scale the
storage potential to rock volume and mass.0.0563 GtCO2 per km3 of serpentinite
Method 5 (modified from Mitchinson et al., 2020)
Draws from laboratory reactivity experiments where CO₂ was injected into serpentinite mine
tailings (e.g., from the Baptiste deposit). The method estimates carbonation rates under

Based on direct CO₂ injection into intact serpentinite cores, this method simulates idealized in-
situ mineralization conditions and represents a maximum bound for potential reactivity and
carbon storage in whole-rock systems. ~30 kg of CO2 per ton of serpentinite.
Method 7 (based on this study’s experimental results; assumes 1 kg CO2 trapped per ton of
rock). This method takes into account the approximate volume of a rock body calculated based
on our geophysical methods and assumes a carbon uptake of ~1 kilograms of CO₂ per ton of
rock through the formation of magnesite.

Carbon Mineralization Estimates
Carbon mineralization experimental results
Analyses of aqueous fluids following reaction of the 18 selected rock samples show a rapid
increase in Mg concentrations within just a few hours, likely due to dissolution of olivine and
serpentine in most cases (Figure 25). In general, Si concentrations did not change significantly
throughout the experiments, suggesting that the contribution of Mg and Fe from serpentine and/or
relict olivine was probably limited.
Carbonate minerals began precipitating on the surface of core plugs when the fluid pH reached
approximately 7–7.5, about 12 to 19 days after the experiments began. Mg concentrations in the
fluid subsequently decreased or plateaued. Fe concentrations dropped rapidly after four days,
leading to the precipitation of iron oxides on the walls of the glass vials. A decrease in Ni and Co
concentrations in the fluids after four days indicates precipitation and potential incorporation into
the newly formed carbonates and/or oxides.
After reaction, carbonated samples were examined using stereomicroscopy and Raman
spectroscopy—a non-destructive method requiring only small amounts of material—to
qualitatively characterize mineral precipitates formed during carbonation reactions. Samples were
lightly rinsed with deionized (DI) water to prevent additional carbonate precipitation due to
evaporation. Because the experiments were conducted at relatively high temperature (90 °C), all
observed carbonate phases are magnesite rather than hydrous phases such as nesquehonite,
dypingite, or hydromagnesite, which are stable at lower temperatures.

 34

experiments run at 90⁰C. Different colors indicate different rock samples. Mg elemental
concentrations in the fluid phase were used in the calculations below.

Carbonation potential based on experimental results
For each sample, following Lacinska et al. (2017), the difference between peak and end Mg
concentrations was used to estimate the amount of Mg available for precipitation as solid
carbonate (Table 4). Table 4 accounts for this by reporting the concentration of Mg consumed
during precipitation, scaling the values to a fixed fluid volume of 100,000 L, and calculating the
corresponding amount of carbonate mineral formed. Each mineral phase has distinct
stoichiometry and molecular weight, which controls both the mass of mineral produced and the
associated CO₂ sequestration capacity.
Among the three minerals considered, magnesite contains the highest proportion of CO₂ per
unit mass because it is anhydrous and thermodynamically stable. However, it is also less likely
to form under low-temperature, near-surface conditions. In contrast, hydromagnesite and
nesquehonite incorporate significant amounts of water into their crystal structures, resulting in
lower CO₂ uptake per unit mass of mineral, but potentially greater likelihood of formation under
surface conditions. For example, sample IP23-04 shows the highest carbon uptake potential
across all phases, reaching approximately 1 kg CO₂ per ton of rock as magnesite, with slightly
lower values for hydromagnesite and nesquehonite.
Under our experimental conditions, we estimate that approximately 0.2 to 1 kg of CO₂ per ton of
rock was sequestered in solid form after 27 days. This calculation assumes that roughly half of
the dissolved magnesium is ultimately incorporated into carbonate minerals. Based on the total
CO₂ input, approximately 0.002 g CO₂ was trapped out of the ~0.002 g initially introduced into
the system (assuming 6 mL of DI water saturated with CO₂), corresponding to an apparent
trapping efficiency of 100%. This indicates that the assumption that half of the carbon input is
mineralized within one month is likely an overestimation, and that significantly longer reaction
times are required for complete uptake.
If instead all Mg released at peak conditions is assumed to be fully converted into carbonate
minerals, then approximately 0.4 to 2 kg CO₂ per ton of rock could be sequestered after 27 days
(Table 12). However, this also implies an unrealistically high conversion efficiency, as it would

 35

require more CO₂ to be incorporated than was initially available in the system (up to ~0.004 g
CO₂ sequestered from ~0.002 g introduced). This further suggests that the assumption of
complete Mg-to-carbonate conversion is not physically realistic under the experimental
conditions.
In comparison, Lacinska et al. (2017) reported approximately 30.8 kg CO₂ per ton of
serpentinized material sequestered after ~6.5 months, corresponding to ~3% carbonation. This
represents a higher total capture than observed in our experiments, but a lower apparent
efficiency in terms of conversion rates over time. We note, however, that our experiments ran for
only up to 27 days, whereas their system was allowed to react for several months. Given
additional time, similar or higher carbonation extents may have been achieved. Notably, their
peak dissolved Mg concentrations were reached after approximately three months, compared to
the one-month duration of our experiments.
Finally, we suggest that higher CO₂ uptake may have been achieved if experiments had been
conducted at 50 °C instead of 90 °C. Lower temperatures increase CO₂ solubility in the fluid
phase, thereby increasing the availability of dissolved CO₂ for reaction and potentially
enhancing overall carbonation efficiency.

the Mg dissolved at peak conditions was trapped as magnesite, hydromagnesite, and
nesquehonite. Bottom: Carbon uptake estimates for 18 different representative samples
assuming all of the Mg dissolved at peak conditions was trapped as magnesite,

Carbonation potential based on 3D volumes and experimental results
The calculated carbon sequestration potential across the various High Susceptibility (HS)
bodies reveals significant variability, heavily dependent upon the methodological approach
employed (see Methodology section) (Table 5). Comparing the seven distinct calculation
methods, it is evident that Method 6 (assumes ~30 kg of CO2 per ton of serpentinite
sequestered) consistently yields the highest carbon uptake estimates across nearly all regional
bodies. For the California B3 body, which comprises a substantial 108,509 km3, Method 6
suggests a capacity of 7,534,864.96 Mt CO2 sequestered as hydromagnesite and 9,357,816.16
Mt CO2 as nesquehonite. In contrast, Method 1a (assumes 2% porosity and only 10% of that
pore volume is eventually filled with nesquehonite or hydromagnesite) functions as the most
conservative baseline, yielding values of 24,781.99 Mt CO2 Hmgns and 17,220.25 Mt CO2 Nesq
for the same formation. Method 7, developed in this study and based on an assumption of ~1 kg
CO₂ sequestered per ton of rock, generally yields lower estimates within the range of results
produced by the different methods. It typically ranks second after Method 1a, highlighting the
conservative nature of this approach. The discrepancy of several orders of magnitude between
methods underscores the critical necessity of establishing standardized reaction kinetics and
mineralogical efficiency definitions when modeling mineral carbonation capacity.
The geographical distribution of these potential sites demonstrates a strong correlation between
the total geological volume of the HS body and its net carbon sequestration capacity. The
California HS bodies, specifically California B3, represent the primary candidates for large-scale
sequestration efforts due to their massive scale. In the Midwest, Minnesota HS2 (10,050 km3)
represents a significant secondary prospect, with Method 6 estimating a capacity of 866,712 Mt
CO2 Hmgns and 697,872 Mt CO2 Nesq. These figures rank competitively alongside the major
California bodies and far exceed the smaller Alaska sites. For example, the Alaska HS1 body (8
km3) yields a much more modest potential of 13.55 Mt CO2 Hmgns under Method 1a and
$689.92 Mt CO2 Hmgns under Method 6.
These estimates underscore that geological volume is the primary control on total carbon
storage capacity, largely independent of the specific method employed. This is precisely why the
type of volumetric estimates carried out in this study are so critical.
While these figures provide a valuable theoretical ceiling for carbon sequestration, it is essential
to discuss the realism of these estimates within a practical engineering context. Many of the
methodologies used in this analysis, particularly those generating the most optimistic results,
operate under the assumption that 100% of the geological body becomes mineralized. In situ,
achieving such complete carbonation is highly unlikely due to physical and chemical limitations,
including limited rock permeability, the passivation of mineral surfaces that prevents further
reaction, and the logistical difficulty of ensuring uniform fluid contact throughout the entire rock
mass.
A more realistic scenario suggests that perhaps only 10% of the body may be accessible or
reactive under standard injection conditions. Adjusting the expectations from 100% to 10%
mineralization would reduce these reported potential values by one order of magnitude. While
such an adjustment significantly lowers the absolute numbers, the relative hierarchy of the sites
remains unchanged; the large-volume bodies in California and Minnesota would still provide the
most robust targets for carbon removal. This downward adjustment highlights that while the
geological potential is immense, the actual net carbon removal will be governed by the
efficiency of the carbonation process, emphasizing the need for advanced injection strategies to
maximize the reactive fraction of these reservoirs.

Conclusions
This work presents a national-scale synthesis of mafic and ultramafic systems in the upper ~5
km of the continental crust, developed to better constrain their role in subsurface resource
systems. While carbon mineralization is one important application, the broader objective of this
project is to establish a unified geoscientific framework that can support multiple energy and
resource pathways, including in-situ CO₂ storage, natural hydrogen generation and
accumulation, and critical mineral exploration.
To achieve this, we compiled and integrated a diverse set of datasets, including historical well
penetrations into basement rocks, regional geophysical interpretations (with emphasis on
aeromagnetic data), mapped distributions of mafic and ultramafic units, and volumetric
estimates of subsurface rock packages. These spatial datasets are complemented by new
mineralogical and geochemical analyses of core and outcrop samples, as well as laboratory
experiments that quantify reactivity and carbonation potential of representative lithologies.
Together, these datasets provide a multi-scale characterization of both the distribution and the
reactivity of key rock types across the United States.
A key outcome of this study is the development of a flexible, multi-method evaluation framework
that links geological volume, geochemistry, and experimentally derived reactivity to subsurface
resource potential. When combined with a source-to-sink assessment incorporating industrial
emitters, infrastructure, and surface constraints, the results highlight that viable opportunities
are spatially concentrated in discrete and highly favorable regions rather than broadly
distributed. In particular, areas in the Central-North region near the Great Lakes and parts of the

 40

eastern United States emerge as priority zones due to favorable geology and strong source–
sink connectivity, while other regions remain limited by logistical or spatial mismatches.
Beyond carbon storage applications, the same integrated datasets provide a foundational
platform for additional subsurface resource evaluation. Mafic and ultramafic systems are also
key components in models of natural hydrogen generation and migration, and they host or
influence the distribution of a range of critical minerals relevant to the energy transition. By
unifying these perspectives within a single spatial and geochemical framework, this study
enables cross-disciplinary exploration strategies that were previously difficult to implement at
national scale.
All datasets, results, and model outputs are made publicly available through the GeoMAFIC web
portal (https://maps.beg.utexas.edu/geomafic/). The platform is designed as a dynamic and
extensible resource, allowing continuous updates, refinement, and expansion as new data
become available. This open framework invites ongoing contributions from the scientific
community and stakeholders, ensuring that the dataset evolves into a shared national
infrastructure for evaluating subsurface opportunities across carbon management, hydrogen
systems, and critical mineral resources.

Outcrop samples
Washington ultramafic outcrops
Bellingham, WA – Twin Sisters Peridotite
The Twin Sisters Peridotite, located in the North Cascades near Bellingham, WA, is a well-
preserved ultramafic massif primarily composed of dunite with minor harzburgite. The high
olivine content has led to its local designation as the Twin Sisters dunite, though some areas
contain enough low-Ca pyroxene to classify as harzburgite. The basal peridotites are dominated
by antigorite, a high-temperature serpentine polytype, distinguishing them from nearby
peridotites near Leavenworth, WA, and Brookings, OR.
The Twin Sisters Peridotite likely represents a section of upper mantle peridotite, emplaced
tectonically onto the North American margin during Jurassic–Cretaceous accretionary events. It
exhibits well-developed foliation and lineation, consistent with deformation in the mantle prior to
emplacement. Shear zones within the massif indicate tectonic transport along fault systems,
likely through obduction. Its characteristics are similar to forearc peridotites found in the
Franciscan Complex and other subduction-related terranes.

Figure 1: Outcrop samples from the Bellingham WA field localities.
Leavenworth, WA – Ingalls Peridotite
The Ingalls Peridotite Complex, located near Leavenworth, WA, is a significant ultramafic body
composed of harzburgite, dunite, and pyroxenite, interpreted as a dismembered ophiolite
sequence. It forms part of the Ingalls Tectonic Complex, a mélange of oceanic and continental
rocks accreted during Mesozoic subduction along the western North American margin.
Some areas contain chromite-rich layers, possibly representing former melt conduits.
Serpentinization is present, primarily along fault zones and fractures, reflecting interaction with
hydrothermal fluids during and after emplacement. The complex exhibits intense deformation,

 42

shearing, and fault-bounded contacts with surrounding metasedimentary and metavolcanic
rocks, indicative of incorporation into a regional accretionary wedge.

Fidalgo Complex, WA
Vashon Island
Cobbles of serpentinized peridotite found on Vashon Island beaches likely originate from the
Fidalgo Ophiolite. Exposed on Fidalgo Island in northwestern Washington, this ophiolite
represents a well-preserved remnant of oceanic lithosphere that was accreted to the North
American margin as part of the Western Mélange Belt.

 43

The Fidalgo Ophiolite preserves a complete ophiolitic sequence, including residual mantle rocks
such as harzburgite and dunite, which represent mantle material left after partial melting. These
ultramafic rocks are overlain by gabbroic and dioritic intrusions interpreted as the former lower
oceanic crust, and a sheeted dike complex indicative of seafloor spreading processes. The
uppermost portion is represented by pillow basalts formed from basaltic magma extruded onto
the seafloor.
Geochemical and structural evidence suggests that the Fidalgo Ophiolite formed in a Mesozoic
island-arc setting, likely within a forearc or back-arc environment, before being tectonically
emplaced onto the continental margin during subduction-related accretion. The complex exhibits
significant deformation, with folded and faulted contacts, and has undergone low- to medium-

Oregon Ultramafic Outcrops
Brookings, OR – Josephine Peridotite
The Brookings area, near the Oregon–California border, encompasses several ultramafic bodies
in the Klamath Mountains, including the prominent Josephine Peridotite and additional massifs
extending north along the coast and southeast into more remote parts of the range.
The Josephine Peridotite is a large, well-preserved ultramafic body that forms a key section of
the Josephine Ophiolite, an ophiolitic complex emplaced during the Late Jurassic (~155 Ma) in
a suprasubduction-zone, forearc setting (Harper, 1984). The peridotite is dominated by
harzburgite and dunite, with lesser lherzolite, representing highly depleted residues of mantle
melting. Partial melting of these peridotites produced basaltic magmas for the overlying
ophiolitic crust.
The complex also contains podiform chromite deposits (high-Cr chromitite lenses), gabbroic and
pyroxenitic dikes, and shear zones, reflecting melt–rock interactions, pathways of mantle melt
migration, and deformation during tectonic emplacement. Geochemical evidence, including the
highly depleted nature of the mantle and boninitic affinities in associated volcanic rocks,
supports formation in a forearc rather than mid-ocean ridge setting.
During the Late Jurassic, the ophiolite was tectonically thrust onto the North American margin,
emplaced as part of a larger ophiolitic nappe and subsequently incorporated into the Klamath
Mountains. Regional deformation, including faulting, folding, and shearing, accompanied its
accretion. The peridotites have undergone serpentinization, with olivine and pyroxene altered to
serpentine minerals, magnetite, and brucite. This alteration is localized along fractures and
shear zones, reflecting fluid infiltration during and after emplacement.

California ultramafic outcrops
Coast Ranges – Franciscan Complex
In the Coast Ranges, including the Diablo and Santa Lucia ranges, the sampled ultramafic rocks
are dominantly associated with the Franciscan Complex and occur within a classic subduction-
related mélange (CSP24-22A, CSP24-25, CSP24-26, CSP24-30, CSP24-41, CSP24-44,
CSP24-45, CSP24-47) (Bailey, 1964; Irwin, 1977; Shervais et al., 2004). The lithologies are
primarily serpentinite and serpentinized peridotite, commonly exhibiting extensive alteration and
strong deformation fabrics. Outcrops are pervasively serpentinized and often exhibit well-
developed foliation and evidence for folding and shearing, consistent with incorporation into an
accretionary prism. These rocks frequently contain carbonate and chrysotile veins, sometimes
reaching centimeter scale, and are often exposed in fault-bounded settings or as blocks within a
sheared matrix alongside mafic and sedimentary components.
The structural and mineralogical characteristics indicate significant fluid–rock interaction during
subduction and exhumation. Anastomosing fault zones, block-in-matrix textures, and sharp
lithologic contacts all point to a dynamic environment of mechanical mixing and metasomatism.
Klamath Mountains
In the Klamath Mountains of northern California, the samples are derived from large, relatively
coherent ultramafic bodies that are less tectonically disrupted than those of the Coast Ranges
(CSP24-10, CSP24-12, CSP24-13, CSP24-15, CSP24-18, CSP24-19) (Lipman, 1964). These
include serpentinized peridotites, dunites, and olivine-rich peridotites, notably including units
such as the Trinity peridotite (ophiolite) (Carter and Quick, 1987). Field descriptions emphasize
the presence of kilometer-scale ultramafic masses, locally massive and compositionally uniform,
though variably serpentinized.

 46

Unlike the Franciscan assemblages, many of these rocks preserve primary igneous textures
and mineralogy, including abundant olivine with subordinate orthopyroxene and locally talc or
antigorite. Banded peridotite and clear spatial relationships with nearby plutonic bodies, such as
contacts with the Slinkard pluton, further support an origin in obducted mantle sections or
ophiolitic complexes. Although serpentinization is present, it is often incomplete, allowing for
direct observation of protolith characteristics.
This combination of scale, preservation, and variable alteration makes the Klamath ultramafic
bodies particularly important for evaluating both critical mineral potential and large-volume
carbon mineralization capacity.
Western Sierra Nevada Foothills
Samples from the western Sierra Nevada foothills represent smaller ultramafic bodies
embedded within accreted terranes along the Sierran margin (CSP24-02, CSP24-03, CSP24-
06). These include relatively fresh dunite and peridotite with visible olivine and orthopyroxene
and comparatively limited serpentinization. Field notes highlight fresh exposures with smooth
weathering surfaces and only minor alteration, in contrast to the more extensively serpentinized
rocks of the Coast Ranges (Bailey, 1964; Saleeby, 1982).
These ultramafic bodies likely represent fragments of ophiolitic mantle or ultramafic cumulates
that were accreted during arc-related tectonism. Their relatively unaltered state provides an
important reference for protolith composition and physical properties, offering a baseline against
which more altered systems can be compared. As such, they are valuable for understanding
intrinsic reactivity and for calibrating models of carbon mineralization and fluid–rock interaction.

Arizona ultramafic outcrop
Cemetery Ridge
The ultramafic rock sample from Cemetery Ridge is associated with the Orocopia Schist. The
Orocopia Schist is a metamorphic rock unit found in southwestern Arizona and southeastern
California, consisting primarily of metapelitic and metaigneous rocks. It is closely related to other
subduction-related schists in the region, such as the Pelona and Rand Schists, and provides
valuable insights into the tectonic evolution of the southwestern margin of the North American
plate.
The Orocopia Schist is composed mainly of quartz-mica schists derived from subducted
sedimentary rocks, metabasites (amphibolites and greenschists) likely representing altered
oceanic crust, and rare ultramafic rocks, possibly fragments of mantle material. The unit
experienced high-pressure, low-temperature metamorphism, reaching conditions characteristic
of the blueschist to greenschist facies. Mineral assemblages including glaucophane, lawsonite,
garnet, phengite, and epidote indicate subduction-related metamorphism at depths of
approximately 30–40 km.
The Orocopia Schist represents subducted material from the Late Cretaceous (~75–60 Ma) that
was subsequently exhumed during the early Cenozoic. It is interpreted as part of a Franciscan-
type subduction complex, where sedimentary and oceanic crustal material was scraped off the
downgoing Farallon Plate and incorporated into the subduction zone.
Tectonically underplated beneath the North American plate, the Orocopia Schist was later
exhumed along major low-angle detachment faults during regional extension in the Paleogene.
This exhumation is associated with the Laramide Orogeny and the onset of Basin and Range
 extension.

Upstate New York ultramafic outcrops
Yellow Lake Serpentinite (Upstate New York)
The Yellow Lake Serpentinite is a small but geologically significant ultramafic body located in St.
Lawrence County within the Adirondack Highlands of upstate New York. It consists primarily of
serpentinite, most likely after peridotite or harzburgite. The serpentinite is dominated by
antigorite, with accessory minerals including magnetite, chromite, and talc.
The Yellow Lake Serpentinite is interpreted as a remnant of an ophiolitic sequence likely tied to
arc-continent collision or obduction processes that occurred during the Grenville orogeny,
approximately 1.1 billion years ago. This orogenic event was responsible for assembling much
of the continental crust in the region and deeply buried and metamorphosed existing lithologies.
Metamorphic overprinting on the serpentinite suggests a retrograde history, where original high-
temperature minerals such as olivine and pyroxene were replaced by serpentine group minerals
during hydration at lower temperatures. This transformation reflects deep crustal fluid activity
during or following the Grenville orogenic events. Structurally, the Yellow Lake Serpentinite
occurs as a tectonic lens or slice embedded within the high-grade metamorphic terrane of the
Adirondack Highlands, which is composed largely of gneisses and amphibolites. Its boundaries
are typically sheared or tectonic in nature, suggesting emplacement along major fault zones or
shear zones during regional deformation.
The Yellow Lake Serpentinite is exposed close to outcrops of the Pyrites Complex on the
northwestern side of the Adirondack Massif, a region known as the Adirondack Lowlands. The
Pyrites Complex is part of the Grenville Province and has been interpreted as an ophiolite
complex dated at 1200–1284 Ma (Chiarenzelli et al., 2014, 2019). The surface exposures do not
cover a large area, but a gravity anomaly in the Lowlands extends from Pyrites (the town) to the
southwest for over 10 km indicating substantial amounts of high-density rocks at depth.

Texas ultramafic outcrops
Coal Creek Serpentinite – Central Texas
The Coal Creek Serpentinite is a tabular-shaped, south-dipping body approximately 6 km and
500 m-2.3 km wide located within the Grenvillian Llano Uplift in central Texas. The Llano Uplift

 50

consists of multiply deformed, Mesoproterozoic metasedimentary, metavolcanic and
metaplutonic rocks metamorphosed at amphibolite (transitional to eclogite/granulite facies)
facies and intruded by late syn- to post-tectonic granites. Deformation and metamorphism
occurred during continent-continent (and arc) collision at ~1150-1120 Ma (Mosher, 1998).
During Grenville-age collisional orogenesis, the Coal Creek Serpentinite was structurally
imbricated with an ensimatic arc that formed exotic to the rest of the Llano Uplift. The
serpentinite most likely represents arc basement that was tectonically emplaced onto the margin
of Laurentia during arc-continent or continent-continent (with arc intermediate) collision between
~1150-1120 Ma.
The serpentinite is intruded by numerous thin metabasic dikes with metasomatic alteration
zones along the margins. The serpentinite is mainly composed of lizardite with secondary veins
of chrysotile. Magnetite, tremolite, talc, chlorite, and relict chromite in layers are accessory
minerals. Two types of serpentinite are distinguished: massive and foliated. Massive
serpentinite is mainly composed of lizardite mesh texture after olivine and pyroxene and minor
metamorphic amphibole. Rare samples contain olivine, orthopyroxene, and antophyllite,
suggesting the body was originally harzburgite (Garrison, 1979; 1981a and b).
The serpentinite records a complex history of multiple serpentinizations, polyphase
metamorphism, and deformations (Gillis, 1989). Gillis and Mosher (1988) concluded that the
serpentinite was tectonically emplaced as a harzbugite and partially to completely serpentinized
during emplacement. The unit then underwent regional metamorphism and deformation along
with the country rock, during which it was deserpentinized. During emplacement of the
surrounding granites, static metamorphism resulted in growth of metamorphic anthoplyllite and
replacement of higher temperature minerals by talc. Disequilibrium assemblages and structural
fabrics allow the P-T-t path to be estimated. At peak conditions, estimated pressures range
between 6.4 and 12 kb and temperatures between 685⁰C and 810⁰C. Temperatures between
710⁰C to 750⁰C at pressures of 8-10 kb, however, are most consistent with the rest of the uplift.
Later serpentinization (post-Grenville) most likely occurred due to interaction with meteoric
water when the ultramafic body was exposed near or at the surface.

Midcontinent Rift System (MRS) – Iowa
The Midcontinent Rift System (Keweenawan Rift) is a ~2,000 km-long failed continental rift in
central North America that formed about 1.1 billion years ago when the North American craton
began to split during the Mesoproterozoic (van Schmus and Hinze, 1985; Cannon et al., 1989;
Allen et al., 2006). The rifting never progressed to full ocean formation, leaving thick
accumulations of basaltic lava, gabbro, and granite intrusions, now exposed mainly around Lake
Superior and buried elsewhere beneath younger sedimentary rocks.
The rift is interpreted as a failed triple-junction system, possibly linked to hotspot-driven
upwelling and crustal doming near Lake Superior, with two main arms extending southwest
(through Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa, Nebraska, and into Kansas and possibly Oklahoma) and
southeast (into Michigan and potentially farther into the central and eastern United States), and
a third failed arm extending north into Ontario (the Nipigon Embayment). Rift development
overlapped with and was likely modified by compressional forces from the Grenville Orogeny,
which contributed to its eventual failure after roughly 15–22 million years.
Rift evolution occurred in two main stages. First, crustal extension created fault-bounded basins
(grabens) that filled with thick basaltic flows and later water, forming lakes that accumulated
organic-rich sediments (Lower Red Clastics), with potential as petroleum source rocks and
reservoirs. Second, a shift to regional compression uplifted parts of the rift (forming horsts),
triggering erosion and deposition of younger sediments (Upper Red Clastics) in adjacent basins,
much of which has since been removed by later erosion.
The rift in Iowa
In the Lake Superior region, rocks associated with the MRS are well exposed along the north
shores of Minnesota and Ontario and across Michigan’s Keweenaw Peninsula (Anderson,
2006). Clastic deposits are also visible in parts of Wisconsin and Michigan’s Upper Peninsula.
One prominent unit, the organic-rich Nonesuch Shale, even seeps oil into a copper mine in
northern Michigan. Farther south, however, across southern Minnesota, Iowa, and beyond, rift-
related rocks are buried beneath younger sedimentary cover. Their presence is identified
through geophysical methods, particularly gravity and magnetic surveys, which highlight the
dense volcanic core as gravity highs and the surrounding sediment-filled basins as gravity lows.
Geophysical and seismic data indicate that the rift enters Iowa near the Minnesota border
before bending westward into Nebraska. Modeling of these data reveals a complex structure,
with sediment-filled basins flanking a central horst in Iowa and reaching depths greater than
16,000 feet.
Interest in the rift’s resource potential led to exploratory drilling in the 1980s, when three deep
test wells were completed: the Texaco Poersch #1 in Kansas, the Amoco M.G. Eischeid #1 in
Iowa, and the Terra-Patrick #1 in Wisconsin. The deepest of these, the Eischeid #1 well,
reached 17,851 feet and intersected nearly 15,000 feet of rift-related sedimentary rocks. These
included both Upper and Lower Red Clastic sequences, with the Lower Red containing roughly
600 feet of organic-rich black shale comparable to Michigan’s Nonesuch Formation.

along fractures.
Both samples are igneous, gabbroic rocks classified as troctolite, composed primarily of
plagioclase and olivine. The upper sample is compositionally similar to the lower one but shows
clear evidence of alteration.
The lower sample is a well-developed cumulate rock consisting of calcic plagioclase (75%),
olivine (14%), clinopyroxene (6%), serpentine (2%), biotite (1%), and opaque minerals (2%).
The olivine occurs as clustered grains with triple junctions, indicating crystallization as
glomerocrysts alongside larger plagioclase crystals. Partial alteration of olivine to serpentine is
evident, along with abundant opaque oxides along grain boundaries. Some larger opaque
minerals—likely magnetite—are euhedral and locally rimmed by biotite.
The upper sample was originally similar to the lower sample but has undergone significant
cataclastic deformation and deuteric alteration. It now consists of plagioclase (69%), fine-
grained aggregates of green amphibole and serpentine (24%), clinopyroxene (2%), biotite (1%),
opaque minerals (4%), and trace olivine. Former olivine grains are largely replaced by
amphibole coronas where they were in contact with plagioclase, a typical feature of troctolites.
Alteration has progressed to the point that most olivine cores are completely replaced by
amphibole, serpentine, magnetite, and hematite. Hematite is confined to these altered cores
and likely accounts for the red coloration observed in hand sample. A single relict olivine grain
remains, preserved by a protective rim of clinopyroxene. Plagioclase is cloudy and exhibits a
mortar texture indicative of cataclastic deformation. The higher abundance of opaque minerals
reflects the formation of secondary magnetite during alteration.

Nellie No. 1 well – West Texas
The North American Royalties Company Nellie No. 1 well penetrated ~4,460 m of Precambrian
mafic and ultramafic rocks beneath ~1,400 m of Phanerozoic sediments (Hoover et al., 1985;
Keller et al., 1989, 2019). The Nellie intrusion is a thick, layered mafic to ultramafic body
exceeding 4,420 meters in thickness, with a radiometric age of approximately 1,163 Ma. Rock
types vary sharply with depth and include norite, pyroxenite, gabbronorite, hornblende gabbro,
gabbro, anorthosite, harzburgite, and lherzolite (Kargi, 1993; Kargi and Barnes, 1995). Norite is
the dominant lithology, although gabbro and hornblende gabbro are locally prevalent, with
occasional olivine-rich layers.
The crystallization sequence follows the order of olivine, orthopyroxene, plagioclase +
clinopyroxene, and hornblende. Mg# values, defined as 100 Mg/(Mg + Fe), range from 76.3 to
85.8 in olivine, 56.7 to 84.9 in orthopyroxene, 62.5 to 90.3 in clinopyroxene, and 52.4 to 82.8 in
amphibole. These values vary with depth and show abrupt reversals, suggesting periodic
injections of new mafic magma into the system. Based on evidence for repeated basaltic
magma influx, Kargi and Barnes (1995) defined eleven cyclic units, which are further grouped
into three megacyclic units.

 62

The high orthopyroxene content and Fe-enrichment trends within the cyclic units suggest that
the parental magmas were subalkaline and tholeiitic. Plagioclase compositions in equilibrium
with olivine range from An65 to An46, which argues against an arc-related magma source.
Although the intrusion is broadly coeval with Keweenawan magmatism and the emplacement of
diabasic dikes in western North America, it differs in important details from both. However, its
composition and geophysical characteristics are consistent with formation in an extensional
tectonic setting.
A collection of ~250 thin sections from the Nellie No. 1 well was made available for this study by
Texas Tech University.

Subsurface Volumetric Estimates of
Ultramafic and Mafic Rocks in the
Livengood Terrane, Central Alaska:
Implications for Carbon Mineralization
By Rama Chandrudu Arasada, Shuvajit Bhattacharya, and Estibalitz Ukar
Acta Geophysica, In Review

Abstract
Mafic and ultramafic rocks are sources of critical minerals and also provide a medium to securely
store carbon. The objective of this study is to delineate the geometry and assess the volumes of
the ultramafic rocks in the Livengood Terrane in central Alaska by analyzing high-resolution
aeromagnetic, petrophysical, and borehole datasets. We analyzed magnetic anomalies using the
3D inversion tool with constraints from previous outcrop and petrophysical data. High-resolution
aeromagnetic anomaly map reveals that the elongated high magnetic signatures spread from
northeast corner to southwest corner of the study area and associated with Cambrian Cascade
ophiolite group assemblage of Livengood Terrane. 3D magnetic inversion results show three
high-susceptibility bodies (HS-1 to HS-3) with a magnetic susceptibility values range of 0.03-0.055
SI, and their collective volume is estimated to be ~12 km3. The estimated in-situ carbon
mineralization derived from laboratory and stoichiometric methods indicates this region's realistic
capacity of approximately ~154.45-843.6 Mt CO2. These finding highlights first-order target areas
in the Livengood Terrane for initial assessment of carbon mineralization potential. Further, this
study demonstrates that geophysical inversion of magnetic data can help to guide site selection
and development for sustainable carbon mineralization initiatives.

Geophysical mapping of subsurface mafic
and ultramafic rocks of the Pecos Mafic
Intrusive Complex, West Texas:
Implications for CO2 mineralization and
hydrogen generation in the Permian Basin
By Rama Chandrudu Arasada, Shuvajit Bhattacharya, Estibalitz Ukar, Gabriel Pasquet
Geophysics, In Review

Abstract
Mafic and ultramafic rocks play a crucial role in carbon sequestration and clean energy systems
due to their capacity for long-term storage of carbon dioxide through carbon mineralization, and
their potential for natural hydrogen generation. This study presents a comprehensive
geophysical mapping of the distribution of layered mafic-ultramafic rocks within the Pecos Mafic
Intrusive Complex (PMIC) in the Permian Basin, West Texas, based on the integrated analysis
of gravity, magnetic, and borehole datasets. Qualitative interpretation of high-resolution Bouguer
gravity and magnetic anomaly maps reveals three distinct anomaly features striking
approximately north-to-south. Forward gravity modeling shows that basement depths below the
PMIC range from 1.4 to 4.65 km. Statistical analysis of 7,089 susceptibility samples yields P90,
P50, and P10 percentile values of 0.043 SI, 0.0075, and 0.0004 SI, respectively. The estimated
P90, P50, and P10 volumes of high-susceptibility geobodies, interpreted as mafic and ultramafic
rocks and extending to depths of up to 3 km, are approximately 3,305 km³, 9,105 km³, and
13,700 km³, respectively. Conservative estimated carbon sequestration capacities are estimated
at approximately 0.9 to 28 (P90), 2.5 to 78 (P50) and 3.8 to 118 gigatons of CO2 (P10
threshold). Conservative carbon sequestration capacities for these volumes are ~0.9–28 Gt CO₂
(P90), ~2.5–78 Gt CO₂ (P50), and ~3.8–118 Gt CO₂ (P10). Assuming 10% olivine-rich lithology,
total hydrogen generation potentials through serpentinization for the P90, P50, and P10
scenarios are ~0.511.49, ~1.431.67, and ~2.147.69 Gt H₂, respectively. Compared to typical
sequestration capacities within saline aquifers, these values are substantial, highlighting the
PMIC as a promising target for long-term CO₂ storage. In addition, the hydrogen generation
potential underscores the potential presence of additional geologic resources in the area
beyond conventional oil and gas.

Access-Constrained Deployment of in-situ
Carbon Mineralization in Mafic and
Ultramafic Rocks in the U.S.
By Ramon Gil Egui, Edwina Owusu-Adjapong, Estibaliz Ukar
Environmental Science & Technology, In Preparation

Abstract
Mafic and ultramafic rock formations across the United States present significant geological
potential for in-situ CO₂ mineralization, offering secure and permanent carbon storage through
stable carbonate formation. However, the deployability of this technology at scale is constrained
by a complex mix of environmental, socioeconomic, and infrastructural factors, which are often
overlooked in assessments that focus solely on geological suitability. This study builds on recent
geological assessments of mafic-ultramafic resources (USGS 2019, 2025) and introduces a
constraint-aware framework that explicitly integrates economic, environmental, jurisdictional,
demographic, and infrastructural considerations into site screening for accessible and feasible
in-situ carbon mineralization. We apply exclusion criteria based on competition from low-cost
saline CO₂ storage areas, proximity to proposed CCS transport infrastructure, federal land-use
restrictions, population density, water-stressed regions, and proximity to protected habitats and
water sources. By evaluating the alignment of emission sources with candidate rock formations,
this approach identifies spatial clusters where geology, access, and emissions converge.
Results indicate that more than 80% of geologically favorable zones are excluded for
commercial deployment under current constraints, with competition from nearby saline
formations and proximity to trunk CCS pipelines being the most significant limiting factors.
Source-to-sink analysis highlights the Upper Midwest cluster as the highest-priority area,
combining large mineral storage capacity with high industrial emissions. Secondary priority
regions include Ohio–Kentucky–West Virginia–Indiana, driven by the nation’s highest emissions,
and Kansas–Missouri, notable for exceptional storage potential despite lower emissions.
Although the West Coast contains large volumes of highly reactive rocks, opportunities there
are limited by a lack of major emissions sources. The identified clusters provide actionable
guidance for pilot projects, infrastructure investment, and policy planning, offering a realistic
framework for the large-scale deployment of in-situ CO₂ mineralization across the United States.
