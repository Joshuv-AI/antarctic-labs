EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The U.S. rail sector is a cornerstone of the United States’ freight transportation system, responsible
for moving approximately 40% of long-distance freight ton-miles each year (AAR, 2025). Freight
demand is projected to grow over time (BTS, 2017) thereby necessitating the investigation of
alternative fuels that can sustain operational efficiency while reducing emissions and cost impacts.
At the same time, it is important to explore alternative fuels as new sources of energy that can
increase energy security and resilience for this sector.

This report investigates the feasibility and compatibility of seven alternative fuels in the U.S. rail
sector, focusing on biofuels and fuels from other renewable energy sources. The study evaluates
alternative fuel pathways, considering their technical, economic, and environmental aspects to
provide stakeholders with a comprehensive understanding of potential solutions.

ES.1 ECONOMIC AND LIFECYCLE ANALYSIS

Detailed technoeconomic and lifecycle analyses of alternative fuel pathways confirm that
renewable fuels can be cost competitive with crude diesel while providing lifecycle and tailpipe
benefits. For example, renewable diesel produced from sewage sludge via hydrothermal
liquefaction (HTL) offers a competitive minimum fuel selling price ($3.48 per diesel-gallon
equivalent [DGE] without fuel incentives) and significant lifecycle emission reductions (-35 g
carbon dioxide equivalents [CO2e] MJ-1). In comparison, diesel costs approximately $2.76 per
gallon (national average in 2024, adjusted to 2020 dollars) and emits about 88 gCO2e MJ-1.

Federal and state incentive programs may further improve the economic compatibility of
alternative fuel pathways. Renewable fuels also provide considerable reductions in criteria air
pollutants (such as sulfur oxides [SOX] and particulate matter with a diameter less than 2.5 um
[PM2.5]). However, emerging e-fuels like e-methanol and e-ammonia face higher costs due to the
price of renewable hydrogen.

This analysis highlights the economic and environmental tradeoffs associated with each fuel
pathway.

ES.2 ENGINE COMPATIBILITY AND MODIFICATIONS

This report explores the efficiency, compatibility, and emissions of using biofuels in locomotive
engines. Renewable diesel and biodiesel can be used with no modifications to minimal
modifications, which makes them attractive options for immediate implementation. Fuels like
methanol and ethanol are cost competitive but would require significant engine changes (for
example, new dual-fuel combustion systems that would require additional fuel injectors and fuel
system material compatibility considerations), which presents both technical challenges and
opportunities for innovation. To implement alternative fuels, it is crucial to understand the engines
that use them.

ES.3 LEVELIZED COST IMPLICATIONS AND OPERATIONAL CONSIDERATIONS

When we analyze the levelized cost of operation for various biofuel mixes, we can see that
biodiesel and renewable diesel blends are a cost-effective option that would require minimal
engine modifications. In contrast, pure biodiesel and other non-drop-in fuels may incur higher
operational costs because they would require engine modifications and additional infrastructure
support. These findings underscore the need for strategic planning to balance fuel costs with
lifecycle and operational benefits.

ES.4 SCALABILITY AND INFRASTRUCTURE DEVELOPMENT

The scalability of alternative fuels is contingent upon regional resource availability and existing
infrastructure. Waste-based fuels provide immediate benefits but are limited in capacity, while
biomass and green hydrogen present long-term opportunities. Strategic planning is essential to
align fuel supply with demand and to develop infrastructure that supports large-scale adoption.

ES.5 HEALTH AND SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS

The adoption of alternative fuels necessitates an understanding of potential environmental health
and safety implications. Ammonia, in particular, poses significant toxicity concerns, and would
require careful management to mitigate risks. However, renewable diesel should be handled like
traditional diesel. Alternative fuels have the potential to reduce lifecycle air pollutants and criteria
emissions, but risk assessments may be needed to ensure safe implementation and avoid
operational hazards.

ES.6 FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Coordinated efforts are needed across railroads, owners-operators, engine manufacturers,
alternative fuel producers, and researchers to diversify energy sources and strengthen energy
security in the rail sector. To overcome economic barriers and support adoption, it is crucial to
strengthen federal and state cooperation, invest in infrastructure, scale up the volumes of available
alternative fuel technologies, and foster stakeholder engagement. Continued research,
development, and demonstrations are needed to improve efficiency and reduce the costs associated
with engine modifications and alternative fuel production. In addition, more research and
technology demonstrations are required to produce a higher volume of alternative fuels using
domestic feedstocks to increase overall energy security.

In conclusion, this report provides a comprehensive assessment of alternative fuels for the rail
sector, offering insights into their potential to satisfy consumer demands, tap into additional energy
sources, and improve the economics and efficiency of rail movement. Table ES-1 summarizes the
primary economic and lifecycle results.

1 INTRODUCTION

1.1 CURRENT STATUS OF THE U.S. RAIL SECTOR

The transportation sector is the one of the largest fuel consumers and the largest contributor to
carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) emissions in the United States, accounting for approximately
29% of total U.S. emissions in 2022 (EPA, 2022). Within this sector, rail transportation plays a
critical role in freight movement, offering a more fuel-efficient alternative to road transport.
According to the Association of American Railroads (AAR, 2022), railroads move about 40% of
the nation’s total long-distance freight ton-miles. Despite their efficiency, U.S. railroads still rely
heavily on diesel-powered locomotives, which contribute to air pollution, energy vulnerability,
and limited options for customers.

In recent years, the U.S. rail sector has made progress in improving fuel efficiency and reducing
emissions on a ton-mile basis. As freight demand is projected to grow significantly, there is an
urgent need to address the environmental impacts of rail transportation to meet near-term and long-
term regulatory emission reduction goals and to improve local air quality.

1.2 RATIONALE FOR USING BIOFUELS

The U.S. rail sector requires exploring viable alternatives to conventional diesel fuel for energy
security, air pollution reduction, and diversification of fuel choice perspectives. Alternative fuels,
particularly biofuels, offer promising solutions for several reasons:

 • Compatibility with Existing Infrastructure: Several biofuels can be used in current
 locomotive engines with minimal modifications, enabling a more straightforward and cost-
 effective transition.

 • Immediate Impact: Biofuels can reduce lifecycle emissions in the near term, without
 waiting for the locomotive fleet to turnover and to be replaced with new propulsion and/or
 energy conversion technologies.

 • Renewable Resources: Biofuels are produced from renewable feedstocks such as
 agricultural residues and energy crops, contributing to energy security and diversifying our
 domestic energy sources

 • Reduced Criteria Air pollutant (CAP) Emissions: Advanced biofuels and other alternative
 fuels can, in some cases, also reduce the CAP emissions.

 • Support for Rural Economies: Biofuel production can stimulate economic growth in rural
 areas through feedstock cultivation and processing.

 • Divert Organic Waste Streams: Biofuel production can reduce the landfilling of human,
 animal, and other organic wastes; this reduces costs for their disposal while also generating
 a revenue stream.

Biofuels can be a solution, providing immediate emission reductions and energy securities while
other technologies, such as electrification and hydrogen fuel cells, continue to develop and mature.

in the U.S. rail sector, including renewable diesel (RD), biodiesel (BD), bio-oils, ethanol,

2 COSTS AND LIFECYCLE EMISSIONS FOR ALTERNATIVE FUEL PATHWAYS

2.1 FUEL PATHWAYS

This study investigates 16 production pathways for generating seven alternative fuel options
relevant to the U.S. rail sector: RD, BD, bio-oils, ethanol, methanol, DME, and ammonia. These
fuels are derived from first-generation biomass (such as corn and soybean), organic wet waste
(including sewage sludge and animal manure), cellulosic biomass (like woody biomass and corn
stover), and renewable hydrogen. Brief descriptions of all pathways are provided in this section.
Detailed descriptions and block flow diagrams are provided in Huang et al. (2025).

Commercial technologies such as transesterification (Bland et al., 2015) and hydroprocessed esters
and fatty acids (HEFA), both at a technology readiness level (TRL) of 9, are considered for the
production of BD and RD, respectively (Xu et al., 2022). Soybean oil and used cooking oil (UCO)
are considered as the feedstocks for these pathways. The corresponding pathways are:

 • Soybean–transesterification–BD,
 • UCO–transesterification–BD,
 • Soybean–hydroprocessing–RD,
 • UCO–hydroprocessing–RD.

Here, the first term represents feedstock, followed by conversion technology, and then the final
product type.

Organic wet waste, including sludge and manure, can be efficiently converted into biocrude
through hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) technology (Snowden-Swan et al., 2022a). This HTL
biocrude can potentially be used as a liquid fuel blend or further upgraded to RD via
hydroprocessing. In this study, a centralized upgrading facility is used for HTL pathways. Key
technology components of HTL, such as feedstock preparation, pumping, heating, and the HTL
reactor, have reached a TRL level of 6, while current research and development (R&D) focuses
on solid separation and aqueous-phase treatment. Pathways considered in this study include:

 • Sewage sludge–HTL–RD,
 • Swine manure–HTL–RD, and
 • Sewage sludge–HTL–biocrude.

Both indirect and direct liquefaction are considered for cellulosic woody biomass (represented by
a 50% clean pine and 50% forest residue blend in this study). In the indirect routes, biomass is first
converted to syngas via gasification (with a TRL around 6–8) (Zhu et al., 2011). After cleanup and
reforming, the refined syngas can be converted into (1) RD via the Fischer-Tropsch (FT) process,
(2) methanol through methanol synthesis, or (3) DME via methanol dehydration. The
corresponding pathways in this study are:

 • Woody biomass–gasification–methanol,
 • Woody biomass–gasification–DME, and
 • Woody biomass–FT–RD.

 5

On the other hand, ex-situ catalytic fast pyrolysis (CFP) (with a TRL around 6) can convert
biomass directly into liquid fuel (Tan et al., 2021). In this process, biomass is decomposed into
solid and vapor-phase products in a fast pyrolysis reactor. The vapor phase is then upgraded into
liquid CFP bio-oil in an ex-situ catalytic vapor upgrading reactor filled with Pt/TiO2 catalyst. Like
HTL biocrude, CFP bio-oil can potentially be used as a liquid fuel blend or upgraded into RD via
hydroprocessing. In this study, CFP bio-oil is co-processed at an existing petroleum refinery.
These pathways are, respectively:

 • Woody biomass–CFP–bio-oil and
 • Woody biomass–CFP–RD.

The ethanol fuel pathways encompass a well-established dry mill technology, with a TRL of 9, to
convert corn starch into ethanol, alongside a less mature corn stover to ethanol technology
(McAloon et al., 2000; Humbird et al., 2011), respectively:

 • Corn starch–fermentation–ethanol and
 • Corn stover–fermentation–ethanol.

Currently, most of the ethanol produced in the United States comes from corn via fermentation.
Further R&D is needed to enhance the conversion efficiency of lignocellulosic biomass to ethanol,
thereby reducing the cost disparity between these two pathways.

The two e-fuel pathways use mature conversion technologies. They are:

 • Renewable H2–Haber-Bosch (HB)–green ammonia and
 • Renewable H2–reverse water gas shift (RWGS)–e-methanol

Ammonia is generated from renewable hydrogen and nitrogen using the commercially available
HB process (Lee et al., 2022). To produce e-methanol, syngas is first produced from renewable
hydrogen and captured CO2 from a point source via a RWGS, then directed to methanol synthesis
(Zang et al. 2021).

2.2 TECHNOECONOMIC ANALYSIS (TEA)

The TEA was performed with consistent economic assumptions based on the U.S. Department of
Energy’s (DOE’s) Bioenergy Technologies Office guidelines (Snowden-Swan et al., 2022b). Mass
and energy balances and equipment cost for selected pathways are derived from existing process
models or literature, as described in Section 2.1. Subsequently, a discounted cash-flow analysis
was conducted to determine the minimum fuel selling price (MFSP), which must achieve a 10%
internal rate of return and a net present value of zero. All costs were adjusted to 2020 U.S. dollars,
and MFSPs were expressed in dollars per diesel gallon equivalent (DGE).

Note that the selected fuel pathways use various conversion technologies with differing TRLs and
feedstocks, and raw materials, which results in varying levels of uncertainty in the modeled MFSPs
presented in Section 2.2.2. For fair comparison, technoeconomic uncertainty in this study was
quantified for the selected pathways based on their state-of-technology as of 2024. The lower or

 6

upper bound of the analysis results reflects a possible range based on current technological
performance, which is subject to change with continuous R&D.

The key TEA model inputs selected for this study were utility prices, the most significant variable
cost contributors, process performance, and capital costs. Five-year historical data were used to
define utility, feedstock, and raw material prices.

Uncertainties related to process performance and capital costs are heavily influenced by the TRLs
of the conversion pathways. Process models for pathways with a TRL greater than 5 are expected
to be more accurate, resulting in smaller deviations for fuel yield (±5% versus ±10%) and utility
consumptions (±10% versus ±20%). The uncertainties of capital cost also decrease with increasing
TRL, as high TRL technologies will have a more well-defined capital project and have been proven
by piloting or commercial practices (+15%/-10% for TRLs >8; +30%/-20% for TRLs 5–7; and
+50%/-30% for TRLs <5).

2.2.1 Modeled MFSP

Figure 2-1 shows the modeled MFSP in dollars per DGE for the 16 selected alternative fuel
pathways. It is important to note that the MFSP shown here does not account for potential incentive
programs available at the federal and state levels. Technology and market uncertainties as well as
impacts of incentive programs on MFSP are discussed in Sections 2.2.2 and 2.2.3.

As mentioned in Section 2.1, BD and RD from soybean oil are currently available in the U.S. fuel
market. The modeled MFSPs for BD and RD from soybean oil are $3.51 and $4.03 per DGE ($3.26
and $3.85/gallon), respectively. These are comparable to the U.S. BD price of $3.08–3.73/gallon
and the California RD price of $3.10–3.76/gallon in 2020 (DOE, 2025). Modeled MFSPs of other
emerging RD pathways range from $3.47 to $4.65 per DGE. Among these emerging pathways,
RDs from woody biomass CFP and wet waste HTL pathways have relatively lower MFSPs due to
their significant byproduct credits and savings from avoided waste treatment tipping fees.

The modeled MFSPs for CFP bio-oil and HTL biocrude are considerably lower than those for CFP
RD and HTL RD, primarily because they do not require the upgrading step for bio-oil or biocrude.
Notably, the biocrude generated from the sewage sludge HTL pathway has the lowest MFSP of all
alternative fuel pathways, $2.05 per DGE. However, the properties of CFP bio-oil and HTL
biocrude are also considerably different from petroleum-derived diesel, which limits their potential
as drop-in fuel for locomotives and their allowable blending ratio. The tradeoff between the cost
of hydroprocessing and developing drop-in fuel such as RD is apparent in the MFSP.

Methanol and DME produced from biomass gasification exhibit similar MFSPs, $3.15 and $2.99
per DGE, respectively, because the gasification step has a significant influence on the overall
production cost. The MFSP for corn stover ethanol is $4.71 per DGE. This is considerably higher
than that of corn ethanol, $3.69 per DGE, due to the lower yield of ethanol from corn stover, higher
chemical consumptions and higher capital costs.

2.2.3 Analysis of Incentive Programs Affecting the Future Market for Alternative Fuels in
 the Rail Sector

The future market for alternative fuels in the rail sector is significantly shaped by a complex
interplay of existing incentive programs, market dynamics, and associated risks. Understanding
current supportive programs is crucial for effective financial planning and decision-making, as
well as for managing related risks. Key government programs that promote biofuel usage include
federal initiatives like the Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) from the U.S. Environmental Protection
Agency (EPA) and 45V product tax credits (PTCs) for clean hydrogen from the Inflation
Reduction Act (IRA). In addition, state-level programs such as California’s Low-Carbon Fuel
Standard (LCFS) and Clean Fuel Standards in Oregon and Washington further promote the
production of alternative fuels and diversify energy used in rail transportation.

For biofuels, the RFS Renewable Identification Numbers (RIN) system is designed to monitor
compliance, facilitate credit trading, and establish guidelines for waivers. The prices of these
waivers are given in ethanol equivalent gallons and influenced by fuel type, chosen timeframe,
production technology, and feedstock. The price of LCFS credits is specified for 1 metric ton (MT)
of avoided emissions, which depends heavily on the difference in CO2e emissions between
baseline fuel and alternative fuel.

Potential risks and uncertainties associated with incentive programs include fluctuations in market
supply and demand, uncertainties regarding feedstock availability for specific biofuel pathways,
and potential regulatory changes. To best predict the credits from various federal- and state-level
incentive programs, the median value of the credits in the past 5 years was used as the baseline.
The 25th and 75th percentiles of the past 5-year credit profile served as upper and lower limits,
respectively.

For e-fuels, RFS is not applicable. Fortunately, PTCs under the IRA’s 45V program for hydrogen
production can apply if hydrogen with low CO2e is used as the feedstock. The value of PTC ranges
from $0.60 to $3.00 per kilogram of H2. Please see Huang et al. (2025) for details about the RINs,
PTCs, and LCFS for each pathway.

Figure 2-3 illustrates how existing federal incentive programs affect the modeled MFSP.
Specifically, the median D5 RIN can reduce the MFSP of BD and RD pathways by $1.59–1.79
per DGE. Similar RIN credits of $1.63 per DGE can be obtained for woody biomass to methanol
and DME. In contrast, the RIN credits for the corn starch (D6 RIN credit) and corn stover ethanol
(D3 RIN credit) pathways are $0.56 and $3.97 per DGE, respectively. A $1 per kilogram H2 PTC
could potentially lower the MFSP of the two e-fuel pathways by about $1.04 per DGE.

2.3 LIFECYCLE ANALYSIS (LCA)

The goal of the LCA is to evaluate the environmental impacts of alternative fuel use in freight
locomotives, estimating CO2e emissions and CAP emissions and comparing them to Tier 4 line-
haul diesel locomotive emission standards. The LCA practice complies with ISO 14040 standards
and uses Argonne R&D GREET (Greenhouse gases, Regulated Emissions, and Energy use in
Technologies) 2023 model as the life cycle database. The scope of this study covers the well-to-
wheel (WTW) or well-to-pump (WTP) lifecycle, including the feedstock, conversion process, fuel
storage and transportation, and combustion phases for locomotives.

Notably, lifecycle emissions include contributions from the combustion phase. These emissions
are calculated stoichiometrically based on the carbon and sulfur content of the alternative fuels.
However, because testing data is limited for the combustion phase in Tier 4 locomotive, other
CAPs—NOX, and PM—are reported only for the WTP stage of the alternative fuel pathways. For
CO2e, CO2, CH4, N2O, VOC (volatile organic compounds), and CO are included. For SOX, sulfur
percentage in the fuel has been used to calculate combustion related SOX emissions. Detailed
lifecycle system boundaries, allocation methods, and combustion assumptions can be found in
Huang et al. (2025).

2.3.1 Lifecycle CO2e Emissions

WTW emissions range from 41 to 53 g CO2e/MJ for the pathways evaluated in this study (Figure
2-5). Most of these pathways reduced WTW CO2e emissions by 80% compared to fossil-based
diesel (88 g CO2e/MJ), except soybean-based biodiesel, renewable diesel, and corn starch-based

 12

ethanol. Corn starch to ethanol was the only pathway that was unable to provide at least 50%
emission reduction. Land use change emissions were included for those pathways that use soybean,
corn starch, and corn stover as the feedstocks. The land use change datasets are derived from the
Calculator for Land Use and Land Management Change from Biofuels Production (Kwon et al.,
2021).

The ethanol pathway from corn starch via fermentation has the highest emissions, 53 g CO2e/MJ.
Next is RD from soybean through hydroprocessing, and BD from soybean through
transesterification, with 34 and 27 g CO2e/MJ, respectively. Feedstock, conversion phase, and land
use change emissions are the main contributors to the impacts of the three pathways with the
highest emissions (corn starch). The three pathways with the lowest emissions are RD from swine
manure and sewage sludge and biocrude from sewage sludge, which are -41, -35, and -34 g
CO2e/MJ, respectively. By diverting these waste feedstocks, CH4 emissions associated with
conventional waste management were avoided, and that is included as avoided emissions credits
for these fuels (see Figure 2-5).

2.3.2 Lifecycle SOX Emissions

Lifecycle SOX emissions range from -0.0002 to 0.6856 g SOX/MJ for the evaluated pathways, with
an average of 0.01 g SOX/MJ for ultra-low-sulfur diesel as the baseline. All RD pathways, except
RD from woody biomass with a Pt/TiO2 catalyst, have SOX emissions that are comparable to or
lower than the baseline. Methanol from woody biomass via gasification, RD from woody biomass
via CFP (Pt/TiO2), and DME from woody biomass gasification have higher SOX emissions: 49,
40, and 37 mg SOX/MJ, respectively. For these pathways, SOX mainly derives from process
emissions during the conversion phase because the final fuel has no sulfur and the sulfur content
in the feedstock is removed during the conversion process. As such, the SOx emissions are not
expected at the tailpipe of the locomotive but should be evaluated during the fuel production
process. Any SOx emission strategy from these fuels needs to be implemented in the refineries
where the fuel is produced. Emission control strategies could include feedstock hydro-
desulphurization and selective catalytic reduction of the stack gas in the refinery.

Ammonia from renewable H2, BD and RD from UCO has low SOX emissions since sulfur content
is low in these fuels, -0.2, 4.4, and 3.5 mg SOX/MJ, respectively.

RD from wastes such as manure and sludge avoids SOX emissions because the manure and sludge
are used as biofuel instead of undergoing business-as-usual waste treatment. However, sewage-
sludge-based biocrude via HTL had the highest SOX emissions due to the fuel’s high sulfur content
(1.2 wt.%). RD from sludge and manure had only 10 ppm of sulfur. Desulfurization through
hydrotreatment will be necessary for biocrude pathway to decrease SOX emissions to the ultra-
low-sulfur diesel baseline (see Figure 2-6).

2.3.3 WTP NOX Emissions

There was a lack of testing datasets and high uncertainty surrounding combustion phase NOX and
PM emissions for alternative pathways in Tier 4 locomotives. Therefore, our study only
considers WTP NOX and PM emissions—not full WTW emissions. WTP NOX emissions range
from -0.016 to 0.17 g NOX /MJ across these alternative fuel pathways, compared to the crude diesel
WTP emission baseline of 0.018 g NOX/MJ. WTP NOX contributions mainly stem from feedstock
farming practices, process emissions, and energy use (e.g., electricity, natural gas, and coal). For
woody biomass, nitrogen fertilization results in high NOX emission during biomass production.
About 84% and 89% of all emissions in the woody biomass to biooil and RD pathway,
respectively, comes from the feedstock production/preprocessing stage. As such, NOX emissions
for these alternative fuels are not expected at the tailpipe of the locomotive and should be evaluated

 15

upstream during fuel production. Any NOX emission strategy from these fuels needs to be
implemented in the refineries where the fuel is produced. Emission control strategies could include
selective catalytic reduction of the stack gas or using low-NOX burners in the refinery.

All pathways—except those from UCO-based BD and RD, sludge, and e-ammonia—exceed the
WTP crude diesel baseline. The pathways with the highest WTP NOX emissions are bio-oil from
woody biomass via CFP (Pt/TiO₂), and methanol/DME from woody biomass via gasification.
Their emissions are 0.173, 0.116, and 0.088 g NOX /MJ, respectively. Woody biomass
pretreatment and process emissions during gasification produce higher NOX. In contrast, biocrude
and RD from sludge via HTL (-0.016 and -0.015 g NOX /MJ, respectively) have the lowest NOX
emissions because their feedstock emissions are relatively low and because they avoid emissions
by diverting the waste from conventional waste management (see Figure 2-7).

2.3.5 MFSP versus CO2e Emissions

Overall, the chosen alternative fuel pathways could reduce emissions by 43–144% compared to
conventional diesel use. However, these alternative fuels have higher MFSPs than conventional
diesel, ranging from 8% to 173%, with the exception of biocrude from sewage sludge via HTL,
which has a lower MFSP than diesel.

 18

In general, although e-fuel pathways have a good emission reduction ratio (84–98%), they still
require higher MFSPs because these technologies are still in the early stages of development (e.g.,
e-MeOH and e--ammonia) with a low TRL of 3. RD and biocrude from sewage sludge via HTL

This study underscores the tradeoffs between the economic and environmental impacts of different
fuel pathways in the freight rail industry. While waste-based fuel pathways offer both economic
advantages and lower environmental impacts, the limited availability and distributed nature of
waste feedstocks pose challenges for scaling up biorefinery infrastructure. On the other hand,
alternative fuels such as electro-ammonia from green hydrogen, RD or bio-oil from woody
biomass via CFP, and DME from woody biomass gasification can also reduce emissions by at least
85% and decrease CAPs relative to emission standards. Furthermore, green hydrogen and woody
biomass are scalable because their feedstock is more abundant, making them attractive options for
biorefinery development. However, the MFSP of e-fuels like electro-ammonia and electro-
methanol remain higher, potentially limiting their economic feasibility for commercialization,

even with the inclusion of LCFS credits. Future research should explore additional alternative fuel
pathways and diverse feedstocks to offer a more comprehensive assessment of TEA and LCA,
considering scalability and practical applications in freight rail locomotives.

3 RAIL ENGINE EFFICIENCY, COMPATIBILITY, AND EMISSIONS

In the United States, the freight rail sector is primarily powered by diesel-electric locomotives,
which employ diesel engines to power an electric generator that sends electricity to motors
powering multiple axles of the locomotive. The engines that power these locomotives are large,
four-stroke diesel engines. An example is the 12-cylinder Wabtec Evolution, which is a 45,000-hp,
turbocharged V12 locomotive engine used for long-haul freight rail applications. Each cylinder of
this engine has a displacement of 15.7 L, and a 250-mm bore. In comparison, Class-8 truck engines
have a total displacement of 13–16 L and have nominally 130–140-mm bores. The locomotives
used in freight rail applications across North American Class-1 railroads are all diesel engines and
are designed to maximize efficiency and minimize criteria emissions using ultra-low sulfur diesel
with a high cetane number (cetane number is a measure of a fuel to show how easily any fuel will
auto ignite in diesel engines).

The EPA sets the U.S. emissions regulations for emissions from newly manufactured and
remanufactured locomotives (EPA, 2016). Line-haul locomotives are long-living assets that have
useful lifespans of 30 years or more with regular engine rebuilds. Industry feedback has indicated
that drop-in replacements and fuel system retrofits for alternative fuels are of interest to meet
regulatory requirements globally and customer demands, in addition to OEM (original equipment
manufacturer) efforts to design next-generation locomotives.

This project considers seven alternative fuels for use in the freight locomotive sector. These fuels
range from drop-in replacements for the incumbent ultra-low-sulfur diesel used in current diesel
engine systems to fuels that would require significant engine modifications. Alternative fuels such
as methanol and ethanol have a near-zero cetane number; instead, they have high research octane
numbers (a measure of resistance to auto-ignite or knock in a spark-ignited engine).

Types of combustion systems and ability to retrofit existing locomotive engines in the near term
to help meet the sector’s emission reduction goals are described in Huang et al. (2025). A 2030
design case was summarized for near-term uses of alternative fuels in locomotives, in which the
first consideration went to fuels that are compatible with current diesel engine fueling and
combustion systems (also referred to as drop-in fuels) or can be used with retrofit solutions. For
modern turbocharged, direct-injection 4-stroke diesel engines, the fuels of interest in this project
can be grouped based on whether they are drop-in replacements. Drop-in or near-drop-in
replacements require no—or minimal—retrofit technologies to store the fuel in existing diesel
tanks, use the existing high-pressure fuel delivery system (high-pressure fuel pumps and high-
pressure direct injectors), and are compatible with the combustion geometry of the piston and
quiescent in-cylinder flow fields.

Dual-fuel techniques use a direct injection of a high-cetane fuel as the ignition source for a
premixed charge of another fuel that can either be port-injected (a port fuel injector located in the
intake manifold or upstream) or have two direct injectors. The commercial implementation of dual-
fuel natural gas locomotive uses a port injection, diesel ignition strategy. There is a limit to how
much diesel displacement can be achieved with a port fuel injection dual fuel strategy, because the
fuel displaces air coming into the cylinder, which is needed to provide oxygen to the combustion
event.

 21

Spark ignition for volatile, high-octane fuels (ethanol, methanol etc.) is not currently implemented
in commercial freight locomotives, but its use is well established for light-duty and medium-duty
vehicles. Prechamber spark-ignition is widely used in stationary power applications and more
recently as pre-commercial technology for vehicles from companies like MAHLE and others.

The conventional diesel combustion strategy requires no changes or minimal changes to the fueling
system and represents the most straightforward path to implementing alternative fuels. Dual-fuel
strategies require some level retrofit technologies for the engine and the onboard fuel systems to
implement. The simplest dual-fuel retrofit strategy is to keep the direct injection diesel fueling
system in place and modify the intake manifold to incorporate port fuel injectors. This also requires
the addition of a second fuel tank and fueling system. With this port-injection dual-fuel strategy,
the diesel combustion system can be left in place for backwards compatibility if the locomotive
needs to refuel at a depot that does not carry the alternative fuel.

Other dual-fuel systems including using a high-pressure direct injection system or two direct
injectors would require a redesign of the cylinder head and the addition of a second high-pressure
fuel delivery system. Dual-fuel strategies offer a range of flexibility specific combustion strategies
and amounts of diesel displacement. Spark ignition technologies would require a more substantial
retrofit, including redesigning the cylinder head to account for the spark system, optimizing the
in-cylinder combustion geometry, compression ratio, and cylinder flow characteristics to best
match the fuel for highest efficiency and lowest possible emissions.

Spark ignition engines with low-cetane (high-octane) fuels are traditionally not used in high-torque
applications such as line-haul locomotives, because engine knock at high loads can damage the
engine and severely impact its useful lifespan. Very high-octane fuels like natural gas are often
used with spark ignition or pre-chamber spark ignition system for stationary power. However,
most commercial implementations for heavy-duty and rail have used diesel ignition dual-fuel
engines that mitigate the end-gas autoignition knock behavior seen in spark ignition systems.

Figure 2-11 summarizes the retrofit applications for the alternative fuels studied in this project.
These alternatives have a wide range of properties that dictate how well suited they are for use as
diesel fuel replacements, what additional storage and fuel delivery considerations are needed, and
whether a secondary ignition source is needed. Bio-oils have moderate cetane numbers, which is
a measure of how easily fuel will auto-ignite in diesel engines; higher cetane numbers are better.
The efficiency and emissions for the near-drop-in replacements of BD and RD are the most well
understood, and for this project are taken from GREET.

Many alternative fuels lack efficiency data and emissions factors for the engines specific to this
sector. Additional R&D is needed to understand the maximum diesel replacement for dual-fuel
operation with low-cetane alternative fuels such as ethanol, methanol, and ammonia, and the use
of bio-oils as a diesel replacement. Ethanol, methanol, and ammonia have low cetane ratings (or
high octane, which is a measure of fuel’s resistance to knocking during combustion). These fuels
higher octane rating will have trouble in compression ignition engines. A solution to this is using
these fuels along with pilot fuel in a dual fuel engine.

Previous dual-fuel research has assessed dual-fuel natural-gas/diesel solutions for locomotive four-
stroke engines. For example, Klingbeil et al. (2020) showed that dual-fuel engines are somewhat

less efficient than the diesel-fuel baseline for simple retrofits and potential replacement limits
below 60%, but that better than 90% replacement and diesel-like efficiency was possible with
additional engineering. For these fuel solutions to be used in the North American market, they
must meet the same emissions standards as the current diesel engine fleet.

3.1 RENEWABLE DIESEL AND BIODIESEL

Both RD and BD are well suited for use as alternative for high-cetane direct-injection fuel (or pilot
fuel) for dual-fuel combustion systems. They would also retain fuel useful operational ability if
the second fuel was unavailable. RD has recently been approved for use as a diesel replacement
by many diesel-engine OEMs. This includes rail use because its physical and chemical properties
are very similar to those of diesel fuel. The properties of BD are similar enough to diesel fuel for
BD to be used as a direct diesel replacement without any fuel system modifications by some
OEMs. Others do not yet warrant the use of 100% BD (B100) because there are some differences
in materials compatibility and emissions controls compatibility.

 23

3.2   BIO-OIL

Bio-oils from CFP and other processes are not suitable for use as drop-in diesel fuel without further
upgrading help with corrosivity, viscosity, and other properties. Some studies have looked at
blending bio-oil with distillate fuels and related miscibility concerns. However, to date those
studies have focused primarily on the large two-stroke engines used in marine vessels. Bio-oils
have a moderate to high cetane value. However, they would require a redesigned fuel delivery
system, fuel injector, and perhaps combustion geometry to be able to use bio-oil in a 4-stroke
engine. Not much work has not been done to understand the emissions implications of using non-
upgraded bio-oil—which would be expected to be much worse than other bio-derived diesel
fuels—or how well it would work as a pilot fuel.

3.3 ETHANOL AND METHANOL

Ethanol and methanol are flammable low-cetane (high-octane) alternative fuels that have higher
volatility and lower lubricity than diesel fuel. They have been widely used neat or in blends with
gasoline in multiple spark-ignition transportation applications. More recently, the number of
methanol-fueled ships in the marine sector has increased.

Ethanol and methanol are both well suited for use as the primary fuel in a dual-fuel strategy. They
have been demonstrated for use in port-fuel injection applications. Some challenges for rail
applications include the lower energy of methanol, to a lesser extent the larger fuel tanks ethanol
would require achieving the diesel equivalent range, and different safety designs needed for fuel
storage systems with flammable fuels. Methanol also has a very high latent heat of vaporization;
this requires engineering solutions for port fuel injection.

Methanol and ethanol dual-fuel engines have been demonstrated in both locomotive engines and
marine engines; the findings of these demonstrations are relevant for locomotive engines
(Argonne, 2025; Atchison, 2022; Soltic et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2018). Both fuels are relevant for
spark ignition, but there have not been many studies with knock implications for larger bore
engines. Cummins has shown that high-power-density spark ignition engines are possible with
ethanol, but they do require additional engineering compared to a retrofit (Green Car Congress,
2014).

3.4 DIMETHYL ETHER

DME is a high-cetane alternative fuel that has similar working fluid properties to propane. As such,
DME is usually stored as a liquified gas at its own vapor pressure, similar to propane. DME has a
high cetane number and has been shown to be a low-/no-sooting fuel; this is attractive for use in
older emissions control systems and for additional optimization of exhaust gas recirculation and
combustion strategies for NOX control in all combustion systems.

Since DME’s working properties are so different from those of diesel fuel, the fuel injection system
and the fuel storage and delivery system require significant changes that do not allow for easy
backward compatibility with diesel fuel. DME’s lower energy content by volume also requires

 24

larger tanks than diesel. Tanks are similar to those for used for propane, which requires a different
configuration than a liquid diesel tank.

3.5 AMMONIA

Anhydrous ammonia (NH3) is classified as flammable but has the lowest flame speed (7 cm/s
compared to 58 cm/s for gasoline or 36 cm/s for methanol) and the highest ignition energy of any
alternative fuel under consideration. It also has an extremely low cetane number (very high-octane
rating).

Ammonia is widely used as a chemical and working fluid but has not achieved commercial success
as an alternative fuel. There is active research on ammonia. Its low flame speed and high ignition
energy make it problematic for use in conventional spark-ignition systems and the high octane
makes it unsuitable as a drop-in compression ignition.

Ammonia’s working properties are nearly identical to those of propane, which means liquid
ammonia can result from compressing the gas to a few bars for storage and transportation.
Ammonia has significant corrosivity challenges, as well as lubricity challenges, which require
different fuel injection systems than those for other fuels that have similar working properties.

Propane-like vapor compression properties also require the use of a propane-style tank. The lower
energy density of ammonia requires a larger tank compared to traditional diesel. In addition, the
toxicity of anhydrous ammonia requires different handling considerations than other fuels.

Ammonia can be used in dual-fuel applications and work is ongoing to understand the NOX and
N2O tradeoffs with efficiency.

4 LEVELIZED COSTS OF LOCOMOTIVE ENERGY SYSTEMS

This section evaluates the costs associated with the use of biofuels in freight rail. While RD can
serve as a 100% drop-in replacement for diesel, BD blends above 25% (B25) require engine
modifications or fuel additives. Section 4.1 first analyzes the fuel cost of biofuel mixes by
estimating the levelized cost of operation (LCO), and Section 4.2 takes a deeper look at a case
study of intermodal freight operation between Chicago and Los Angeles. Section 4.3 discusses
the infrastructure and operational implications beyond fuel cost for non drop-in fuels.

4.1 FUEL COST ANALYSIS OF LOCOMOTIVE ENERGY SYSTEMS USING
 BIOFUELS

While some biofuel options (e.g., RD) can use existing infrastructure with no major modifications
to locomotive engines, some emerging biofuel options (e.g., methanol, ammonia) may require
additional engine modification and/or infrastructure support. In our preliminary analysis, we
focused on the fuel cost of each biofuel. We assume the biofuels are either blended with RD
directly or used in a dual-fuel configuration with a specific mix ratio. For each biofuel option, the
mix ratio was selected based on the review of existing literature.

Some biofuels (for example, methanol and ethanol) can be combined with diesel in multiple

4.3 LCO: INFRASTRUCTURE AND OPERATIONAL IMPLICATIONS

While Sections 4.1 and 4.2 focused on the direct fuel costs associated with biofuel options, it is
also essential to consider the broader LCO that encompasses the infrastructure needed to store and
distribute fuels, the operational adjustments required for locomotives, and the implications for
emissions controls and efficiency. The following subsections discuss these three areas,
infrastructure needs, operational implications, and emissions controls and efficiency, to highlight
how non-drop-in fuels may influence both costs and day-to-day performance in the freight rail
sector.

Infrastructure Needs

Non-drop-in fuels impose additional infrastructure requirements that extend beyond a simple
substitution for conventional diesel. One of the main considerations is fuel storage and handling.
Because fuels such as methanol and ethanol have lower energy densities, they require significantly
larger storage volumes to deliver the same energy output, which affects the design and capacity of
both onboard locomotive tanks and refueling depots. In addition, some of these fuels have
chemical properties that demand specialized materials and enhanced safety measures to prevent
leaks and ensure compatibility with storage systems. Meeting these needs often translates into
additional capital expenditure, either for retrofitting existing facilities or building new
infrastructure. Refueling infrastructure also presents challenges. Establishing a reliable supply
chain for non-drop-in fuels requires new distribution networks and strategically located refueling
stations, which can be costly and time-consuming to implement. Moreover, the use of specialized
equipment and procedures can lengthen refueling times, potentially disrupting train schedules and
reducing overall operational efficiency.

Operational Implications

Beyond infrastructure, the adoption of non-drop-in fuels carries operational implications for
locomotives themselves. These impacts relate to the extent of retrofitting required, as well as
potential effects on maintenance and reliability:

 • Retrofitting Requirements: Non-drop-in fuels often necessitate significant engine
 modifications to ensure compatibility and optimal performance. These modifications can
 include changes to fuel injection systems, combustion chambers, and emissions control
 technologies.
 • Maintenance and Reliability: The use of alternative fuels can affect engine wear and
 maintenance schedules. For instance, BD’s solvent properties may lead to increased filter
 clogging, requiring more frequent maintenance and potentially impacting reliability.

 31

Emissions Controls and Efficiency

The use of alternative fuels also has implications for emissions control technologies and overall
efficiency. In some cases, these adjustments are necessary to maintain compliance with air quality
standards:

 • Additional Emissions Equipment: Certain fuels may produce higher levels of specific
 emissions, necessitating enhanced emissions control systems. For example, fuels with
 higher NOX emissions might require selective catalytic reduction systems, which use urea
 as a reducing agent.
 • Efficiency Considerations: The integration of additional emissions controls and the use of
 less energy-dense fuels can place an efficiency burden on train operations. This may result
 in increased fuel consumption and operational costs, affecting the overall economic
 viability of non-drop-in fuels.

 32

5 SCALING UP BIOFUEL USE: REGIONAL RAIL ENERGY DEMAND, BIOMASS
FEEDSTOCK AVAILABILITY, AND ENVIORNMENTAL BENEFITS

Argonne’s Bioeconomy AGE and R&D GREET models, and Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s
(ORNL’s) BILT model were used to analyze the benefits and tradeoffs associated with the scale
up of alternative locomotive fuels and to understand the sufficiency of biomass feedstocks to meet
freight rail demand considering competing uses. These models were used to estimate direct and
indirect emission reductions, capital funding requirements, construction resource requirements,
CAP emissions, water consumption, and energy use with the production and use of these fuels.

These results show the amounts of key materials and resources required for scale up by certain
points in time (e.g., 2030, 2040, 2050). The U.S. freight rail system was also analyzed to
understand specific applications and routes, and to identify promising opportunities for specific
alternative fuels. Section 5.1 describes regional resource availability for biofuel production.
Section 5.2 characterizes regional freight rail energy use and identifies potential alternative fuel
corridors. Section 5.3 describes the scale-up analysis combining biofuel production and demand
for the freight rail sector.

5.1 REGIONAL RESOURCE AVAILABILITY FOR BIOFUEL PRODUCTION

US DOE 2023 Billion Ton study dataset (DOE, 2024) was used to produce feedstock supply curves
and ORNL’s BILT model (Oyedeji et al., 2025) was used to aggregate regional feedstock to
conversion facilities. This analysis quantified the economic availability of biomass resources for
rail fuels using the Policy Analysis System model (ORNL, 2019) for agricultural residues and
energy crops, the Forest Sustainability and Economic Assessment Model (ORNL, 2019) for
forestland resources, and waste resource assessments that are ongoing. The comprehensive
analysis used the BILT model to quantify spatially explicit allocation of biomass resources to
biorefineries for rail applicable biofuels given the TEAs and LCAs from the project.

The BILT model, which ORNL developed for the DOE, is an accounting and optimization model
that tracks the entire biomass-to-end-use supply chain, including biomass production,
transportation, and processing for potential feedstocks, pathways, and end uses. This model solves
a series of carbon avoidance costs for specific routes and allows an analysis of routes and end-uses
that will result in resource competition. A differentiating feature of BILT is the spatial resolution
of inputs and outputs which include the distribution of biomass, infrastructure, and siting of
potential facilities for different parts of the biomass, and biofuel/bioproduct conversion process.
Details of the decision variables and process of BILT model can be found in (Oyedeji et al., 2025).
This model operates by solving a series of mixed-integer programming problems to minimize net
carbon emission or total system cost. Based on the LCA/TEA inputs, the model provides results
for optimized biorefinery location, movement of feedstock, and total carbon abated. The locations
that BILT considers for facility siting are provided via an OR-SAGE framework. OR-SAGE
considers several constraints to determine regions of feasibility for siting.

For this analysis, BILT was run using a 50-mile plant spacing for the baseline results. For
comparison, the model was also run using a 75-mile spacing as a sensitivity analysis. This
parameter applies to facilities that could be built in the future. Some of the analyses used existing
 33

facilities. The facilities that process the soybeans into soybean oil already exist, and data about
them were gathered from the National Oilseed Processors Association website (NOPA, 2025). The
bio-oil generated via the CFP pathway and biocrude from the HTL pathway were assumed to be
transported to and hydrotreated at existing petroleum refineries. The locations for existing
petroleum refineries were retrieved from the Energy Information Administration’s U.S. Energy
Atlas (2024).

The analysis was run with a minimum facility usage factor of 0.9, meaning that a facility would
have to process at least 90% of its nameplate feedstock processing capacity to be sited. This is a
strict utilization requirement. Realistically, a refinery would run approximately 330 days a year
(Kinchin, 2020) and will strive to maintain 60% of its nameplate capacity.

The analysis does not account for existing facilities, other than the existing petroleum facilities
and soybean processing facilities already noted. This removes the complication of interacting with
the existing use of facilities. The main output of a BILT analysis is a carbon avoidance cost curve.
The results that are discussed in this section are primarily from the 90% of maximum CO 2 stage.
Moving from avoiding 90% to avoiding 100% of the maximum carbon avoidance level results in
a large increase in the number of facilities that are constructed. This is expected since the model
needs to utilize 100% of the feedstocks and it achieves carbon minimization by minimizing

5.2 CHARACTERIZATION OF REGIONAL FREIGHT RAIL ENERGY USE AND
 IDENTIFICATION OF POTENTIAL ALTERNATIVE FUEL CORRIDORS

ORNL’s Freight Analysis Framework (FAF) (BTS, 2017), the North American Rail Network
(NARN) (BTS, 2025), and Carload Waybill Sample (CWS) data (STB, 2025) was employed to
analyze the U.S. freight rail system for specific applications and routes to identify promising
opportunities for specific alternative fuels. These results were used to help understand
opportunities and tradeoffs for fuel selection based on trip characteristics and rail fuel demand
centers. They can also be used in the future to help screen for potential environmental health and

 45

safety hotspots. This analysis combined a review of rail routing, commodity movements, and
potential fueling locations to identify potential early adopters and initial fuel distribution.

Section 5.2 focuses on understanding fuel consumption, and route characteristics to develop
strategies for the implementation of alternative fuels in the rail industry.

5.2.1 Datasets for Regional Freight Rail Energy Use and Corridor Identification

This analysis uses data from three primary sources: FAF (USDOT, 2017), CWS (STB, 2025), and
EIA’s Distillate Fuel Oil Sales Dataset (EIA, 2025).

FAF (USDOT, 2025) contains crucial data on the U.S. freight rail system. It is a shipper-based
dataset that provides information on the origin and destination of goods from the shipper’s
perspective, but it does not specify the exact routes taken.

Similarly, CWS (STB, 2025) gives a comprehensive picture of the freight rail activity. It is a
carrier-based dataset that offers a more detailed view of rail carriers and routes. However, CWS
has confidentiality restrictions to protect commercial interests. It is available in two versions:

 • The Public Use Waybill Sample (PUWS) provides origin-destination (OD) movements
 only at the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) level, with around half of the tonnages
 suppressed to protect confidentiality.

 • The Confidential Carload Waybill Sample (CCWS) offers more granular information,
 including individual movements at the county level with railroads and interchange
 locations, but it cannot be directly used due to confidentiality concerns. However, the
 CCWS has been valuable for validating the county-level OD movements.

The Distillate Fuel Oil Sales Dataset (EIA 2025) offers annual data on the sale and distribution of
distillate fuel oil by state. The data is available up to the year 2020.

Apart from OD movements, this analysis also relies on the rail freight network data. The NARN
published by the Bureau of Transportation Statistics, provides a detailed geographic representation
of the railway infrastructure across North America.

5.2.2 Data Limitations and Methodology for the Freight Rail Analysis

While FAF (USDOT, 2017) provides a shipper-based outlook on commodity movements between
origin and destination, the intermediate stops or transfer locations are unknown. With the addition
of PUWS (STB, 2025), a carrier-based movement, a single OD record from FAF could be broken
down to multiple records but only at an aggregated level. Hence, to address the limitations in both
datasets, a combined dataset was created. This dataset merged county-level OD movements from
FAF with BEA-level data from PUWS. Specifically, BEA-level marginal totals were sourced from
PUWS only in instances where total flows between BEA regions exceeded FAF totals. For flows
where FAF totals were higher, it was assumed that PUWS flows were suppressed and therefore
not updated in combined dataset. Once the marginal totals were obtained, iterative proportional

 46

fitting was employed to disaggregate the dataset to county level. This statistical technique updates
the county-level data by ensuring consistency with the known totals from the combined dataset.

After estimating county-level OD movements for rail freight, the future movements were projected
by applying FAF growth factors specific to each Standard Classification of Transported Goods
(SCTG) code (USDOT, 2017). SCTG is the federal standard for classifying freight shipments in
the US. These growth factors, derived from the FAF, proportionally increase or decrease projected
freight flows based on anticipated changes in commodity demand and transportation patterns. This
approach allows for more realistic projections of future rail freight movements by considering the
unique growth trajectories for each commodity.

Further, a multistep process was employed to estimate the total ton-miles of freight transported by
rail in each state. First, county-level OD movements were integrated into the rail network. This
involved identifying the nearest node in the network to each county’s centroid, effectively
connecting the shipment data to the physical rail infrastructure. Next, using a shortest path
algorithm, the route between each OD pair was estimated, assigning all tons between the origin
and destination counties to that specific route. The final tons transported on each rail segment, or
link, were calculated by summing all the tons assigned to that link from various OD movements.
Finally, the ton-miles for each state were determined by multiplying the total tons transported on
each rail segment within the state by the segment’s length.

To streamline the analysis and maintain a focus on the U.S. system, the routing process was limited
to tracks within the NARN designated as “Main Line” or “Major Industrial Lead,” and excluding
rail tracks from Canada and Mexico (BTS, 2025). In addition, the network was only segmented at
interchanges or nodes connecting different states, simplifying the analysis while ensuring the
accurate representation of interstate freight flows.

5.2.3 Estimation of Distillate Fuel Oil Use by Rail by State

To estimate state-level distillate fuel oil consumption by rail, ton-mile data were used as an
independent variable. The known railroad distillate fuel oil consumption data from the EIA for
2017–2020 served as the response variable. Multiple regression models, including linear, ridge,
lasso, decision tree, and support vector regression, were evaluated to identify the most accurate
predictive model. The model with the highest cross-validation score was ultimately selected to
forecast future distillate fuel oil consumption by state.

5.2.5 Findings and Gap Analysis

Analyzing ton-miles offers valuable insights into freight rail energy use, but this metric alone is
insufficient for a detailed feasibility assessment of alternative fuels, primarily due to significant
gaps in publicly available data. The key data gaps hindering this assessment include:

 • Limited data on refueling depot locations: Although the data on state-level fuel
 consumption by rail is available, further disaggregation is difficult as data on existing
 refueling depots is limited and often kept private by rail companies. This information gap

 50

 makes it challenging to strategically locate alternative fueling infrastructure, thereby
 impeding the development of a comprehensive transition strategy.

 • Lack of location-specific data on current biofuel availability: While there is some use of
 drop-in biofuels for the rail sector, it is difficult to ascertain the availability of biofuels (for
 example, BD and RD) at these locations. This gap makes it difficult to assess the existing
 infrastructure for these alternative fuels and understand the current capacity for renewable
 fuel use in the rail sector. Additionally, other fuels of interest lack established refueling
 infrastructure at these depots, and additional analysis needs to be performed on the costs
 and implications of additional refueling infrastructure and potential options for near-term
 implementation.

 • Detailed information on commodity chemicals unknown: Although the OD movements
 provide a two-digit commodity code that categorizes the commodity into 42 different
 groupings, they are not detailed enough to identify different chemicals such as methanol,
 ethanol, ammonia, biomass, hydrogen, captured CO2, or e-fuels. These chemicals hold
 potential as alternative fuels for the rail industry. These data are needed to estimate the
 potential for mode shifts for freight based on changes to costs and emissions.

Furthermore, the FAF (USDOT, 2017) or PUWS dataset (STB, 2025) is not granular enough to
ascertain the location itself. Identifying areas where certain commodities are readily available
could encourage their adoption as rail fuels and contribute to emissions reduction efforts. This
level of data would be needed to understand the potential future scenarios because certain
commodity customers may value low-emission transport enough to offset the costs of being an
early adopter. For example, the potential shift of long-haul truckloads to rail or to rail-truck
multimodal operations could produce a significant reduction in emissions.

By analyzing ton-mile data and focusing on high-demand corridors, promising opportunities for
alternative fuel implementation can be identified. However, developing a truly effective strategy
requires moving beyond this metric. For instance, ton-mile estimates alone cannot pinpoint
specific demand centers for fuel, as a railroad’s refueling decisions are influenced by numerous
complex operational variables. Therefore, addressing the specific data gaps to alternate fuel
availability, refueling infrastructure, and commodity chemical locations is crucial for developing
a comprehensive and actionable plan for understanding the role of alternative fuels in the freight
rail sector.

5.3 SCALE-UP ANALYSIS

In assessing the benefits and tradeoffs associated with the scale up of alternative locomotive fuels,
the Argonne bioeconomy air emissions, greenhouse gas emissions, and energy consumption
(AGE) model was adopted (Oke et al., 2022; Rogers et al., 2017). Bioeconomy AGE is a scenario-
based model that quantifies the energy and environmental impacts of biotechnology deployment
at scale. For this study, Bioeconomy AGE brings together the alternative fuel production by
location from the BILT model (Section 3.1), the rail fuel demand by state from FAF (Section 3.2),
and the LCA data (Section 2.3) to project the potential benefits of scaling up biofuel use in the rail
sector. The metrics of focus include CO2e emissions, CAP emissions, water consumption, and

 51

energy use. In addition, the biofuel production by location was aggregated from the BILT model
into state-level and regional-level supply. Likewise, the state-level rail diesel fuel demand from
FAF was aggregated into regional-level demand. These were combined to analyze how regional
level biofuel supply compares to demand and potential impact on fuel adoption. The analysis uses
the U.S. Census Bureau’s four regions—Northeast, Midwest, South, and West—each with the following
divisions: Northeast (New England, Middle Atlantic); Midwest (East North Central, West North Central);
South (South Atlantic, East South Central, West South Central); and West (Mountain, Pacific) (U.S. Census
Bureau, 2025).

5.3.2 Environmental Benefits and Tradeoffs

To assess the potential benefits and tradeoffs of scaling up biofuel use in the rail sector, we
considered three diesel-biofuel blending cases: RD100, BD100, and BD20+RD80. Although
RD100 and BD20+RD80 can serve as a drop-in replacement for diesel, BD100 would require
engine modifications or fuel additives. All three of these biofuel blending cases substantially
reduced rail-sector emissions compared to conventional diesel (Figure 5-18). CO2e emissions
decreased by 32.9, 29.7, and 33.5 million MT in the RD100, BD100, and BD20+RD80 cases,
respectively. This is equivalent to reductions of 76%, 69%, and 77% compared to conventional
diesel use.

Emissions decreased more in the RD100 and BD20+RD80 cases compared to the BD100 case.
This is because, for these two cases, RD is produced from different feedstocks and pathways with
various lifecycle CO2e emissions impacts. RD from swine manure and sewage sludge have
negative lifecycle CO2e emissions, which contributes to the reduction in overall emissions
compared to the BD case. However, the limited supply of these resources limits their scalability
potential.

Overall, the BD20+RD80 case has the lowest lifecycle CO2e emissions. BD from soybeans has
lower lifecycle CI than RD from soybeans (Figure 2-5). This accounts for the slightly higher
emissions reduction in BD20+RD80 compared to RD100.

6 ENVIRONMENTAL HEALTH AND SAFETY

When considering new fuels for implementation, it is important to understand and account for the
environmental health and safety implications for the identified fuels, both positive and negative.
The year 1 activities for this project identified that ammonia posed the greatest risks for human
populations due to its toxicity compared to all other fuels under consideration. Those efforts
focused specifically on two significant externalities for biofuel processing facilities: risk of
chemical accident from ammonia processing facilities and traffic increases from feedstock
shipments to ethanol and BD processing facilities. The results of that modeling highlighted the
importance of release modeling with human population data and suggested that certain sites have
a higher level of risk associated with their location, related to local population density. This type
of analysis can help eliminate high risk sites from consideration. In addition to the potential danger
posed by such a spill, the damage could harm public acceptance of biofuel facilities and of
emerging fuels that can help with future cost reductions and resiliency. A recent report by
Kolodziejczyk on the environmental and health issues of ammonia for transportation applications
summarized the health and safety concerns and ammonia safety requirements (Kolodziejczyk,
2021). A thorough summary was completed for these same alternative fuels for the marine sector
by Kass et al. (2021) for the U.S. DOT Maritime Administration.

7 CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

The assessment of alternative fuels for the U.S. freight rail sector reveals that many biofuel
pathways offer meaningful reductions in CI and improvements in air quality relative to
conventional diesel. However, challenges remain in achieving cost competitiveness and ensuring
broad engine compatibility across the rail fleet. To fully realize the potential of these fuels, future
efforts should focus on optimizing production economics, validating emissions and energy
performance through real-world demonstrations, and conducting rigorous engine compatibility
testing. The following key areas have been identified for further investigation: advanced
conversion technologies (Section 7.1), engine compatibility and performance (Section 7.2), fuel
blends and optimization (Section 7.3), TEA (Section 7.4), lifecycle and environmental impact
assessments (Section 7.5), feedstock availability and supply chain logistics (Section 7.6),
technological integration and systems analysis (Section 7.7), regional fuel demand analysis
(Section 7.8), and scale-up analysis (Section 7.9).

7.1 ADVANCED CONVERSION TECHNOLOGIES

More R&D is needed to explore innovative conversion technologies that can improve the
efficiency and cost-effectiveness of producing alternative fuels. This includes developing new
catalysts and processes for converting biomass, organic waste, and renewable hydrogen into viable
fuel options. Emphasis should be placed on scaling up and demonstrating promising technologies,
such as HTL and CFP, to commercial levels.

7.2 ENGINE COMPATIBILITY AND PERFORMANCE

Most of the seven fuel categories across sixteen pathways studied in this effort have little
performance data from end-use testing on rail engines. RD and BD are drop-in fuels (or nearly
drop-in) for existing diesel engines. A high-level screening was completed for the other fuels, some
of which would require dual-fuel operation, and to determine whether the fuel would be a
promising main or pilot fuel. Ethanol and methanol are not suitable for use as a replacement for
diesel fuel, but they could be used in dual-fuel applications with additional modifications to the
engine and fuel system.

Further studies and demonstrations are needed to understand the compatibility of alternative fuels
with existing locomotive engines and to provide emissions factors for these types of studies.
Additional research is needed on developing advanced engine technologies such as dual-fuel
systems and retrofitting options while maintaining performance and emissions standards.

7.3 FUEL BLENDS AND OPTIMIZATION

Exploring and demonstrating optimal fuel blends that balance environmental benefits with
operational feasibility is essential. Research should investigate the effects of different biofuel
blends on engine performance, emissions, and maintenance requirements. This includes studying

 63

the long-term impacts of using BD blends, RD, and other non-drop-in fuels in various locomotive
configurations.

7.4 TECHNOECONOMIC ANALYSIS (TEA)

Among the 16 selected alternative fuel pathways, biocrude from sewage sludge HTL has the lowest
estimated MFSP without incentives. However, it cannot be directly used as a drop-in fuel for
existing locomotive engines. The estimated MFSP of all pathways producing RD, a drop-in fuel,
ranges from $3.47 to $4.65 per DGE without incentives. Existing incentive programs may further
decrease the estimated MFSP and improve the economic feasibility of alternative fuel production.
Note that the TEA conducted in this work was based on standardized economic assumption for a
Nth-plant application, which are subject to several uncertainties. An "Nth-plant" refers to a
chemical plant that replicates a design already validated by prior operations, thereby typically
offering reduced risks and capital costs compared to a first-of-a-kind facility.

A detailed business case study is crucial to thoroughly assess whether producing alternative fuels
is economically viable in a specific region or project. This study should consider the unique
characteristics of local feedstocks and various financial projections. Furthermore, the analysis
should incorporate key economic indicators like net present value, internal rate of return, and
payback period, because these provide a more practical perspective for industrial adoption. For
technologies that are still in the early to mid-stages of development (low- to mid-level TRLs), the
TEA should be regularly revised to reflect improvements in performance over time and to
minimize uncertainties.

7.5 LIFECYCLE AND ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT ASSESSMENTS

Most of the renewable fuel options could provide 80% CO2e emission reduction, compared to the
conventional diesel used in the freight rail sector. Future studies can look into other agricultural
waste feedstocks such as cotton stalk or other oilseed feedstocks such as carinata, camelina, and
canola. While WTP lifecycle SOX emissions are estimated and presented in this study, a significant
knowledge gap remains in the pump-to-wheel SOX emissions.

Future research should focus on estimating combustion related emissions of both carbon dioxide
and CAPs for more robust lifecycle assessment results. In other words, comprehensive lifecycle
assessments should be validated with demonstrations to confirm the environmental impacts of
alternative fuels across their entire supply chain.

7.6 FEEDSTOCK AVAILABILITY AND SUPPLY CHAIN LOGISTICS

The biomass resource data from the DOE 2023 Billion Ton study (DOE, 2024) was used to provide
insights into the supply curves (availability and cost) of feedstocks to produce the alternative fuels
studied here. The use of this dataset allows regional supply and demand analyses at a county level
granularity across the United States. The BILT model was then used to optimize biorefinery
facility siting to use these biomass resources with given constraints on spacing between plants and
other economic or location constraints. The combination of the biomass resource data and the

 64

BILT modeling allowed the project to quantify spatially explicit allocation of biomass resources
to biorefineries for rail applicable biofuels given the TEAs and LCAs from the project. Insights
from this work include information about the size of biorefineries needed for the different
pathways and locations of biorefineries that could be supported by regional feedstock distributions.
Identifying renewable and scalable feedstock sources through studies like this are key to
supporting large-scale fuel production.

This analysis did not account for existing refueling depots or facilities other than the existing
petroleum facilities and soybean processing facilities and only optimized for new sites. Additional
research could better link existing facilities. Additional research is also needed to link the demand
side to biomass pricing, and to understand the tradeoffs for the pathways that compete for the
biomass resources and facilities. For example, many of the pathways investigated here rely on the
same feedstock and others could be implemented simultaneously (e.g. corn to ethanol + stover to
ethanol + woody to RD + soybean to RD). Additional research would be valuable in understanding
which combination of pathways most benefits a set of objectives.

Biofuels require multiple preprocessing steps such as drying, densifying, chemical pretreatment,
and esterifying. The impacts of preprocessing hub for feedstocks before sending them to refineries
should be analyzed. Depending on the presence or absence of the preprocessing units, the cost of
biofuel production could be significantly different.

7.7 TECHNOLOGICAL INTEGRATION AND SYSTEMS ANALYSIS

Future research should explore the integration of alternative fuels into existing rail systems,
considering both technical and operational aspects of integration. This includes analyzing systems
to evaluate the impacts of fuel transitions on rail infrastructure, operations, and maintenance.
Understanding these interactions will help identify potential barriers and opportunities for
seamless integration.

7.8 REGIONAL FUEL DEMAND ANALYSIS

This study analyzed the U.S. freight rail system to understand regional freight rail energy use and
identify potential alternative fuel corridors. Due to data limitations in FAF and CWS, a combined
dataset was created using an iterative proportional fitting statistical technique to estimate county
level-freight movements. These movements were routed on the North American Rail Network
using the shortest path algorithm to calculate link-level tonnages, state-level ton-miles, and future
flows.

These analyses identified corridors with high tonnage concentration, and those projected to have
increasing tonnages. However, the study found significant data limitations, including a lack of
detailed information on existing refueling depot locations, location-specific biofuel availability,
and sufficient granularity in commodity and location data (like specific chemicals at precise
locations). These limitations pose challenges for developing a comprehensive alternative fuel
transition strategy, and they warrant additional research.

 65

7.9 SCALE-UP ANALYSIS

The scale-up analysis evaluated the potential benefits and tradeoffs of scaling up biofuel use in the
U.S. rail sector. The analysis used the Bioeconomy AGE model, which integrates data about
alternative fuel production, rail fuel demand, and lifecycle assessments. Scaling up biofuel use in
the U.S. rail sector—particularly RD and BD, which are derived mainly from soybeans and waste
feedstocks—has strong potential to meet or exceed rail diesel demand by 2035. RD-HEFA is the
dominant near-term pathway, while woody and herbaceous biomass offer longer-term
opportunities. This transition could reduce CO2e emissions by around 69–77% compared to
conventional diesel, although SOX, particulate matter and NOX emissions may increase in some
cases. However, increased water consumption is a significant environmental tradeoff due to the
high water intensity of biofuel production, especially from soybean-based pathways.

Regional variations in biofuel supply and demand, feedstock availability, and infrastructure
logistics will influence cost and adoption feasibility, highlighting the need for more detailed
analysis at a granular level to fully assess biofuel implementation feasibility on specific rail routes.
There is also a need for further research to optimize water consumption in biomass conversion
processes due to the increased water use associated with scaling up biofuel production.

APPENDIX B. FUEL PATHWAYS DESCRIPTION

B.1 SOYBEAN/UCO | TRANSESTERIFICATION | BIODIESEL

Transesterification is a commercialized technology (TRL = 9) for biodiesel production. In the
United States, most biodiesel (BD) is produced from vegetable oil and animal fats. Some low-
value feedstocks, such as used cooking oil (UCO) and distillers corn oil (DCO) are becoming
prevalent but will require additional feedstock treatment steps (Xu et al., 2022).

In this study, TEA was conducted for BD produced from soybean oil to represent the current state
of technology. In this process, refined soybean oil, methanol, and sodium methylate are fed to the
reactor to be converted to fatty acid methyl ester (FAME). The reactor output is sent to a gravity
decanter to be separated into FAME phase and glycerin phase. The FAME phase is sent to a second
reactor to complete the transesterification reaction. The FAME product is washed with water and
then distilled under vacuum to produce the final BD product. Glycerin is sold as a byproduct that
is widely used in wound and burn treatment.

B.2 SOYBEAN/UCO | HYDROPROCESSING | RD

Hydroprocessed esters and fatty acids (HEFA) fuel is another commercially available biofuel
(TRL = 9). The HEFA process uses the same feedstock as the BD process (Xu et al., 2022).
Similarly, soybean oil is selected as the feedstock in this TEA study. Here, soybean oil is heated
and fed to a fixed-bed catalytic reactor with H2 to produce n-paraffins as well as H2O, CO2, light
hydrocarbons, and naphtha. The gas phase product is fed to a H2 recovery unit to separate the
hydrogen and recycle it back to the reactor. The hydrocarbon phase is sent to a distillation column
to be separated into light hydrocarbons, naphtha, and RD.

B.3 WOODY BIOMASS | FT | RD

In this process, woody biomass (50% clean pine, 50% forest residue blend) is dried and sent to an
indirectly heated gasifier followed by tar reformer (Tan et al., 2015) to generate syngas. Then
syngas is sent to the gas cleanup unit to remove ash, CO2 and adjust H2 to CO ratio in the syngas
to about 2:1 for downstream synthesis. The clean syngas is sent to a fixed bed Fischer-Tropsch
(FT) reactor filled with cobalt-based catalyst. The FT reactor products include light gases and FT
liquid, which are separated via a series of flash tanks. The FT liquid is then upgraded in
hydrotreating and/or hydrocracking unit to produce gasoline and diesel-range fuel blendstocks.

B.4 SEWAGE SLUDGE | HTL | BIOCRUDE

In this process, the slurry sludge is converted into biocrude at a non-catalytic reactor operated
under subcritical water status (20 MPa, 350°C), while aqueous-phase product, gas, and solid are
generated as byproducts. The HTL reactor products are separated via filter, flash tank, and
decanter. The HTL aqueous phase product has higher ammonia and chemical oxygen demand
(COD), a measure of the amount of oxygen required to chemically oxidize organic compounds in

 B-1

B.5 WOODY BIOMASS | CFP (PT/TIO2) | BIO-OIL

Ex-situ catalytic fast pyrolysis (CFP) technology using a Pt/TiO2 catalyst is selected to represent
the most recent state of technology for CFP-based renewable diesel. In this process, biomass from
a feedstock handling unit is sent to a fast pyrolysis reactor with heat supplied by hot sand. The
solid phase product contains mainly ash, char, and sand, which is sent to the char combustor. From
there, hot sand is circled back to the pyrolysis reactor to heat the endothermic reaction. The vapor-
phase product is sent to the ex-situ catalytic vapor upgrading reactors. The upgraded pyrolysis
vapor is sent to the CFP product condensation unit to be separated into organic liquid CFP bio-oil
and aqueous wastewater stream. Valuable oxygenated coproducts are generated with additional
recovery and purification equipment.

B.6 SEWAGE SLUDGE | HTL | RD

This study assumes a centralized HTL biocrude upgrading plant, with the capacity of processing
110 dry tons of waste feedstocks per day and producing 2,700 barrels per stream day (BPSD) of
biofuel blendstocks. The HTL biocrude upgrading process mainly includes hydrotreating and
hydrocracking to remove the heteroatoms in the biocrude. Specifically, the biocrude feed is first
pumped to 10.5 MPa, mixed with compressed hydrogen, and preheated to the hydrotreater reactor
temperature of 400°C. The hydrotreating process can convert biocrude oxygen, nitrogen, and
sulfur into CO2 and water, ammonia, and hydrogen sulfide, respectively. The hydrotreater effluent
is cooled to 25°C to separate the gas and water. The resulting hydrocarbons are then fractionated
into lights, naphtha, diesel, and heavy oil. Heavy oil combined with hydrogen is then hydrocracked
at 400°C and 7 MPa to increase diesel yield. Hydrogen is produced onsite via steam reforming of
the upgrading off-gas and purchased natural gas.

B.7 SWINE MANURE | HTL | RD

The conversion process and plant scale are the same as RD: sludge HTL, but high-moisture-content
swine manure is used. Swine farms produce solid waste at a much slower rate than the typical
wastewater treatment plant. It is reported that 90% of swine farms in the United States can generate
<2 dry ton of recoverable swine manure per day, per farm. Thus, swine manure collection and
transportation cost would be more expensive than the wastewater sludge. HTL refineries, co-
located with a large wastewater treatment plant, can potentially receive credits due to the avoided
disposal cost at $40/wet ton sludge. The swine manure cost at the gate of the HTL plant is assumed
to be zero in the analysis, assuming potential avoided disposal cost to farms and transportation
cost offset the swine manure collection and transportation cost to support the 110 tons per day
HTL plant scale.

B.8 WOODY BIOMASS | CFP (PT/TIO2) | RD

The bio-oil produced from biomass CFP technology is sent to an existing petroleum refinery with
hydrotreating and hydrocracking capabilities to be upgraded into renewable hydrocarbon fuel

B.9 WOODY BIOMASS | GASIFICATION | METHANOL

Similar to the FT pathway, biomass is first converted to syngas in a gasifier, followed by tar
reforming, amine-based syngas cleanup and steam reforming (Tan et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2009).
The clean syngas, with a desired H2/CO ratio of 2, is then compressed and sent to the methanol
synthesis reactor filled with Zn-Cu catalyst to produce crude methanol, which is purified by
distillation to generate high purity methanol. Due to the relatively low per-pass conversion of the
methanol synthesis reaction, unconverted syngas is recycled back to the reactor to boost overall
conversion.

B.10 WOODY BIOMASS | GASIFICATION | DME

DME is conventionally produced via methanol dehydration (Zhu et al., 2009). In this process, the
semi-purified methanol (about 98%) produced from biomass gasification is used as the feed of the
DME synthesis reactor. In the reactor, methanol is dehydrated over 𝛾-Al2O3 catalyst. The reactor
outlet contains DME, water, and unreacted methanol, which are separated via distillation. The
unreacted methanol is recycled back to the reactor.

B.11 CORN STARCH | FERMENTATION | ETHANOL

Corn to ethanol is a well-established process and majority of ethanol in United States is produced
from corn via fermentation. Ground corn is added along with recycle water, alpha-amylase,
ammonia, and caustic/lime. The stream is further cooled to 60°C before saccharification where
glucoamylase is added with sulfuric acid for pH control. The effluent from saccharification is
cooled before feeding to four continuous cascade fermenters where yeast is fed. Exhaust gas from
the fermenters is vented to the vent scrubber. At the end of the fermentation train, the fermented
beer and scrubber bottoms are collected.

The effective ethanol concentration in the fermentation train is 10–13 vol %. Distillation and
molecular sieve adsorption are used to recover and concentrate ethanol from the raw fermentation
beer and produce 99.5% ethanol. The heated beer is then fed to the beer column, which removes
residual carbon dioxide and as little ethanol as possible overhead. Ethanol is removed as a vapor
side draw from the beer column and fed directly to the rectification column. Overhead from the
rectification column is fed to the molecular sieve unit. Adsorption removes 95% of the water and
a small portion of ethanol. The 99.5% pure ethanol vapor is cooled by heat exchange against
regenerated condensate and finally condensed and pumped to offsite storage.

The beer column bottoms stream, which contains all the unfermented, nonvolatile components, is
known as whole stillage. After heat interchange, the whole stillage is sent to distillers’ dried grains
recovery as a coproduct.

B.12 CORN STOVER | FERMENTATION | ETHANOL

Corn stover is lignocellulosic agricultural waste that is available in large quantities in the United
States. The process adopted in this work is based on the National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s
lignocellulose feedstock to ethanol technology (Humbird et al., 2011), using co-current dilute acid
pre-hydrolysis of the lignocellulosic biomass with simultaneous enzymatic saccharification of the
remaining cellulose and co-fermentation of the resulting glucose and xylose to ethanol.

Specifically, the stover is first pretreated to hydrolyze hemicellulose to soluble sugars with the
help of dilute sulfuric at 190°C. About 90% of the hemicellulose is converted to xylose. Then
simultaneous saccharification and co-fermentation of the hydrolysate slurry is carried out in a
series of continuous anaerobic fermentation trains where enzymes and other nutrients are added.
Ethanol concentration is 4–5% after saccharification and fermentation.

In the final step, distillation and molecular sieve adsorption are used to recover ethanol from the
raw fermentation beer and produce 99.5% ethanol. The beer column bottoms stream, containing
water, lignin, and other organics, is sent to a series of triple effect evaporators. The resulting slurry
is filtered and wet lignin from the filters may be combusted for generated electricity as a co-
product.

B.13 RENEWABLE H2 | RWGS | E-METHANOL

In this process, methanol is produced from renewable H2 and CO2 captured from point sources
through a two-step pathway. In the first step, H2 and CO2 with 1:1 molar ratio is fed to a reverse
water gas shift (RWGS) reactor, where CO2 is partially converted to CO. The unconverted CO2 is
separated into an acid gas removal unit using Selexol technology and recycled back to the RWGS
reactor. The syngas from RWGS, with a H2:CO ratio about 2:1, is then sent to the methanol
synthesis and purification unit to produce high purity methanol similar to that in the methanol:
biomass gasification pathway.

B.14 RENEWABLE H2 | HB | GREEN AMMONIA

In this process, ammonia is produced from renewable hydrogen via water electrolysis and nitrogen
from air separation via cryogenic distillation. To obtain high-purity nitrogen, dry air is compressed
to 6–7 MPa and precooled via heat transfer between the warm feeding air and the outgoing cold
air. Then precooled air goes through an expansive valve into a cryogenic heat exchanger where
the air is cooled to -184°C. Then a distillation column is employed to separate the air components
based on the boiling points. As a result, the pure oxygen from the distillation column will be treated
as a co-product.

APPENDIX C. RESULTS SUMMARY BY FUEL

C.1 BIODIESEL (BD)

C.1.1 Production Pathways and Cost

In this study, we examine the biodiesel (BD) pathway using soybean oil and used cooking oil
(UCO) through commercially available transesterification technology. As illustrated in Figure 3-1,
the modeled minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) for BD derived from soybean oil is $3.51 per
diesel gallon equivalent (DGE), with approximately 85% of this cost attributed to feedstock.

When substituting soybean oil for the less-expensive UCO, the feedstock contribution to the MFSP
decreases by about $1.12/DGE. However, capital expenditures (CAPEX) and fixed operating costs
increase by $0.75/DGE and $0.95/DGE, respectively, because the smaller plant scale is limited by
UCO availability.

Overall, the modeled MFSP for a 44-million -lb/year UCO BD plant is approximately $0.60/DGE
higher than that of a typical soybean oil BD plant with an annual production capacity of 220 million
lb. In addition, the MFSP of BD from UCO is subject to higher uncertainties because the market
price of UCO varies more significantly.

C.1.2 Lifecycle Emissions

BD from soybean oil and UCO generate 27 and 15 g CO2e/MJ. This is equivalent to 69% and 83%
emission reductions compared to conventional diesel use in freight rail. Because UCO is the waste
feedstock, the feedstock phase only includes UCO collection and transport to the biorefinery
facility. In contrast, the feedstock phase for soybean oil includes soybean farming, soybean oil
refinery, transportation and distribution stages. It is worth noting that land use change emission
also contributes 9.3 g CO2e/MJ to BD from the soybean oil pathway, which is equivalent to 34%
of its total lifecycle emissions. The land use change emissions are derived from Carbon Calculator
for Land Use and Land Management Change from Biofuels Production (CCLUB) model
(Argonne, 2020).

C.2 RENEWABLE DIESEL (RD)

C.2.1 Production Pathways and Cost

For RD pathways, we examine the commercially available HEFA technology using soybean oil
and UCO, along with emerging RD technologies such as HTL converting wet wastes. We also
studied RD production from woody biomass with CFP and FT technologies. The MFSPs for these
six RD pathways range from $3.47 to $4.65 per DGE. Analyzing the MFSP breakdowns for these
RD pathways reveals that each technology has distinct cost drivers. In the HEFA pathway,

 C-1

feedstock costs account for approximately 81% of the total RD cost when using soybean oil and
substituting soybean oil with UCO can reduce the RD cost by $0.14/DGE.

For the HTL pathways, potential savings from avoided wet waste disposal fees and regional waste
availability have a significant impact on the cost of RD derived from HTL. For instance, avoiding
sewage sludge disposal costs of $178 per dry ton could reduce the HTL-derived RD cost by
$2.23/DGE. Woody biomass cost makes up nearly 40% of the RD cost, while capital expenditure
(CAPEX) and operational expenditure (OPEX) are comparable for both the woody biomass to RD
via FT and CFP processes. Nonetheless, the electricity byproduct from the CFP pathway leads to
a lower RD cost compared to the woody biomass FT pathway. In addition, pathways that use
woody biomass as the feedstock have slightly lower uncertainties in the modeled MFSP.

C.2.2 Lifecycle Emissions

Renewable diesel (RD) made from soybean oil and UCO via hydroprocessed esters and fatty acids
(HEFA) emits 34 and 18 g CO2e/MJ with 62% and 79% reductions, respectively, compared to
conventional diesel. RD and biocrude from waste feedstock via hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL)
could make the carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) emissions negative (-41 to -34 g CO2e/MJ).
Approximately 88 and 240 g CO2e could be avoided from treating 1 wet ton of sludge and swine
manure in anaerobic digestion process, respectively, which leads to CH4 avoidance by diverting
wet wastes from conventional waste management to fuel production.

Carbon sequestration happens in aqueous treatment after HTL producing solids to store carbon
and reduces part of the emissions. RD from woody biomass via catalytic fast pyrolysis (CFP) and
Fischer-Tropsch (FT) processes have adequate emission reduction performance, ranging from 8.5
to 16 g CO2e/MJ with reductions of 90–82%. However, because both methods require pretreating
woody biomass through gasification, considerably higher criteria air pollutant emissions are
produced compared to other RD pathways.

C.3 METHANOL/DIMETHYL ETHER (DME), ETHANOL, AND E-FUELS

C.3.1 Production Pathways and Cost

Other alternative fuels examined in this study include methanol and DME produced from woody
biomass via gasification, ethanol derived from corn starch and corn stover through fermentation,
and e-methanol and e-ammonia generated from green hydrogen. The cost of methanol and DME
from woody biomass is relatively low compared to all other pathways, ranging from $3.00 to
$3.15/DGE. As illustrated in Figure 3-1, the cost breakdown for both woody biomass-derived
methanol and DME is similar: feedstock accounts for approximately 45% of the total cost, while
CAPEX and OPEX represent about 22% and 33%, respectively.

In contrast, the MFSP for the two e-fuel pathways is the highest among all options, primarily due
to the elevated cost of green hydrogen, which contributes approximately $6.28 to $6.58/DGE. In
addition, the CAPEX and OPEX for the e-methanol pathway are about $0.39 and $0.63/DGE
higher, respectively, than those for the e-ammonia pathway.

C.3.2 Lifecycle Emissions

Methanol and DME have good emission reduction performance for 7.4–14 g CO2e/MJ, with 92–
83% reduction. E-ammonia from renewable H2 has the best emission reduction performance of all
