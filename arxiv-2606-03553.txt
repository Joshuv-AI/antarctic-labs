A Robust Optimization Approach to Sparse Principal Component Analysis
David Vävinggren, Francis Bach, André M. H. Teixeira, Dave Zachariah, Antônio H. Ribeiro, [0.8em] Uppsala University, Sweden

Abstract
While principal component analysis (PCA) is a fundamental tool for dimensionality reduction, its dense representations make it ill-suited for high-dimensional data. Existing methods address this by promoting sparsity through explicit ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -penalties, but these are not obvious to tune due to the unsupervised nature of the task. In contrast, we propose Adversarial PCA (AdvPCA), which leverages robust optimization to achieve sparsity by optimizing the reconstruction objective against bounded, worst-case latent space perturbations. We show that this formulation admits a closed-form reduction, leading to a practical iterative algorithm that alternates between adversarial linear regression-style updates for the sparse encoder and orthogonal updates for the decoder. By theoretically characterizing the solution, we derive a data-adaptive parameterization that allows the algorithm to perform effectively out of the box. We validate these claims through numerical experiments on synthetic and real-world genomics data.

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a standard technique for reducing the dimensionality of data
[

34

3
Background

Principal component analysis.
PCA plays a central role in machine learning as a standard dimensionality reduction technique. PCA (
1
) aims to find linear transformations
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
such that the reconstruction error over the data is minimized in the least squared sense. Specifically, given
k\leq d
,
B^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
compresses the input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
into a lower-dimensional representation
z=B^{\top}x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
(approximately) recovers the original input from its compressed counterpart by reconstructing
x
as
\tilde{x}=Az=AB^{\top}x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Because
\tilde{x}=Az
,
\tilde{x}
is constrained to the
k
-dimensional subspace defined by the columns of
A
(see Figure
1
). An important characteristic of PCA is that any optimal solution to (
1
) must have matrices
A,B
that coincide
[
37
, ch. 23.1]
, which implies that the solution is an orthogonal projection. This makes the subsequent solution simple and very elegant. To be precise, let
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a set of
n
zero-mean datapoints in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be the corresponding data matrix. Then, PCA is solved simply by letting the columns of
A

and

B
be the
k
leading eigenvectors of
X^{\top}X
, or equivalently, the
k
leading right-singular vectors of the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
X
.

Thresholding.
The issue with PCA is that the solution is notoriously dense
[
24
]
, which only makes it suitable in data-rich settings (
n>d
). To circumvent this, a common approach is to force the lower-dimensional representations to be sparse combinations of the input variables. Perhaps the simplest way of achieving this (which typically serves as a baseline) is through thresholding: solve PCA and decouple
A
and
B
by thresholding
B
according to
B_{ij}:=B_{ij}\mathds{1}\{\left\lvert B_{ij}\right\rvert\geq\varepsilon\}\;\forall(i,j)\in[d]\times[k]
.

Sparse PCA.
A more sophisticated approach to enforce sparsity in
B
is to extend (
1
) with
\ell_{1}
-penalties and make use of the Lasso. This is the core idea behind the Sparse PCA algorithm
[
51
]
:

\min_{A,B}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert x_{i}-AB^{\top}x_{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}\quad\text{subject to}\quad A^{\top}A=I_{k},

(3)

which in essence is a
regularized
reconstruction problem where
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

\forall j\in[k]
are hyperparameters controlling the sparsity, and
A=[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}]
and
B=[\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}]
are both matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
. By solving (
3
), an approximate solution to PCA can be achieved with the added benefit of sparse input combinations. The full objective of Sparse PCA also includes an additional sum of ridge penalties that ensure uniqueness, but they are excluded in (
3
) for simplicity.

Adversarial linear regression.
Adversarially trained linear regression has been shown to be a viable alternative to traditional Lasso and ridge regression, exhibiting similar regularization behavior while enjoying favorable properties
[
35
,
34
]
. A defining feature of the formulation is that the inner maximization can be solved in closed-form, leaving a convex problem with an explicit regularization term. To be precise, adversarial linear regression satisfies

\min_{\beta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{\left\lVert\Delta x_{i}\right\rVert\leq\delta}\left(y_{i}-\beta^{\top}(x_{i}+\Delta x_{i})\right)^{2}=\min_{\beta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left\lvert y_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\right\rvert+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{*}\right)^{2},

(4)

where
\smash{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{*}}
denotes the dual norm of
\smash{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
and
\smash{\delta\geq 0}
. Indeed, comparing (
4
) to a traditional method such as the Lasso, with objective

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\right)^{2}+\lambda\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}

for
\smash{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}
, the main connection is clear: the norm-penalty is now
inside
the square. Another feature is that the type of penalty directly depends on the norm defining the perturbation set. Lastly, and perhaps most importantly, a key advantage of the adversarial formulation is that it has been shown to attain near-oracle performance for a choice of
\delta
independent of the data noise level. This is analogous to the square-root Lasso
[
3
]
.

4
Proposed Formulation

Building on the adversarial linear regression framework, we propose a min-max formulation that naturally induces sparsity by optimizing the reconstruction objective against worst-case perturbations. This is formulated as an extension of the original PCA formulation (
1
), where the objective is augmented with adversarial perturbations acting directly in the latent space:

\min_{A,B}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underset{r_{i}\in\Omega_{\delta}}{\text{max}}\left\lVert x_{i}-A(B^{\top}x_{i}+r_{i})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad A^{\top}A=I_{k},

(5)

where
A=[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}]
and
B=[\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}]
are both matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
. We define
\Omega_{\delta}
to be an axis-aligned hyperrectangle (or weighted
\ell_{\infty}
-norm) in the latent space, given by

\Omega_{\delta}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left[-\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1},\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}\right]\subset\mathbb{R}^{k},

(6)

where
\{\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k}\}
is a set of nonnegative adversarial radii. While this choice of set may initially appear unmotivated, subsequent sections will demonstrate its necessity for the algorithm’s efficacy. For now, we summarize the motivation with (i) perturbations in the latent space in combination with the axis-aligned geometry of the set (product of intervals) decomposes (
5
) into
independent adversarial linear regression problems
, making the inner maximum solvable in closed-form; and (ii) the
\ell_{1}
-norm is what promotes sparsity in
\beta
, and comes from the relationship between input and latent space perturbations. We refer to Section
5
for a detailed discussion on this manner.

4.1
Closed-Form Reformulation

By design, the geometry of
\Omega_{\delta}

1

Let

5
Relationship with Input Space Perturbations

We establish the relationship between input and latent space perturbations to motivate our specific choice of
\Omega_{\delta}
. We provide intuition behind perturbing the latent representations, demonstrating that unlike input space perturbations, this approach yields a formulation consistent with ordinary PCA.

The equivalence in one dimension.
Typically, adversarial perturbations are introduced directly in the input space, constrained by an upper bound on the norm. For the case of linear regression, as shown in (
4
), this amounts to augmenting the standard objective with a local maximization:

\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\right)^{2}\xrightarrow{\text{robust}}\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\color[rgb]{0.3359,0.707,0.9141}\max_{\left\lVert\Delta x_{i}\right\rVert\leq\delta}}\left(y_{i}-\beta^{\top}(x_{i}+{\color[rgb]{0.3359,0.707,0.9141}\Delta x_{i}})\right)^{2}.

(11)

To simplify the analogy to PCA, we consider a special case of the PCA objective (
1
) where
k=1
, meaning the matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
reduce to vectors
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The corresponding, perhaps most standard way of introducing adversarial perturbations in PCA would then be given by

\min_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert x_{i}-\alpha\beta^{\top}x_{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\xrightarrow{\text{robust}}\min_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\color[rgb]{0.3359,0.707,0.9141}\max_{\left\lVert\Delta x_{i}\right\rVert\leq\delta}}\left\lVert x_{i}-\alpha\beta^{\top}(x_{i}+{\color[rgb]{0.3359,0.707,0.9141}\Delta x_{i}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(12)

Mimicking the linear regression case, the target input is left undisturbed and the adversary is only allowed to act on the input being reconstructed. To connect this standard input space formulation in (
12
) to the general latent AdvPCA objective in (
5
), let
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)
denote the inner maximum of (
12
) for a single sample
x
. Through Proposition
3
, we establish that this natural way of perturbing in the input space is exactly equivalent to perturbing in the latent space.

3
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left\lVert\alpha\right\rVert_{2}=1
. Further, let
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)
denote the worst-case adversarial reconstruction loss. Then, for an adversarial radius
\delta\geq 0
and conjugate exponents
p,q\geq 1
satisfying
1/p+1/q=1
, the following expressions for
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)
are equal:

(a)
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)=\max_{\left\lVert\Delta x\right\rVert_{p}\leq\delta}\left\lVert x-\alpha\beta^{\top}(x+\Delta x)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
.  (Input space perturbation)

(b)
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)=\max_{|r|\leq\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}}\left\lVert x-\alpha(\beta^{\top}x+r)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
.  (Latent space perturbation)

(c)
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)=\left\lVert x-\alpha\alpha^{\top}x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left(|\beta^{\top}x-\alpha^{\top}x|+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}\right)^{2}
.  (Closed-form maximum)

Additionally, if
\alpha=\beta
is imposed, then
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)=\left\lVert x-\beta\beta^{\top}x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\delta^{2}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}^{2}
.

Parts (a) and (b) establish the core equivalence; the substitution
r=\beta^{\top}\Delta x
projects the adversary into a single dimension and Hölder’s inequality
[
38
, ch. 9]
then translates the norm constraint. This bounds the latent perturbation as
|r|=|\beta^{\top}\Delta x|\leq\|\Delta x\|_{p}\|\beta\|_{q}\leq\delta\|\beta\|_{q}
and shows the intuition behind letting
\Omega_{\delta}
be a product of independent intervals
[-\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q},+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}]
(a hyperrectangle). Part (c) subsequently evaluates this maximum in closed form, yielding an objective convex in
\beta
where the adversarial radius
\delta
explicitly dictates the amount of regularization. This behavior is most apparent in the special case where
\alpha=\beta
. Finally, to promote sparsity in
\beta
, we set
q=1
in the proposed formulation (
5
), inducing an
\ell_{1}
-norm penalty. By conjugate exponents, this mathematically corresponds to defending against an
\ell_{\infty}
-norm adversary in the original input space.

Inconsistency when scaling to multiple dimensions.
A defining feature of PCA is that the solution stays consistent across multiple values of
k
. To be precise, solving PCA for
k
components and then another time for
k+1
components (using the same data), the first
k
components of the two solutions should be consistent with each other. Now, consider generalizing part (a) of Proposition
3
to multiple dimensions. For
k>1
, the vector
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is extended to the matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
meaning the adversary becomes a vector
r=B^{\top}\Delta x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Thus, because
\Delta x
maps to all
k
dimensions of the latent representation, the optimal adversarial direction for
\Delta x
will generally depend on
all

k
columns of
B

simultaneously
. Hence, the solution of Adversarial PCA would change with
k
, which is not in line with ordinary PCA. Instead, by perturbing in the latent space, independence between components is kept intact meaning solving for different
k
has no impact on the solution.

6
Properties of the Solution

Many structural properties of adversarial linear regression naturally extend to Adversarial PCA. To isolate this behavior, we examine the step in the solver when
A

k

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\lvert\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\rvert+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}\right)^{2},

(13)

where we drop the subscript
j
for notational convenience since all
k

\widehat{\beta}\in\argmin_{\beta}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)
denote the resulting estimator. The behavior of the estimator is governed by the adversarial radius
\delta\geq 0

4

characterize its behavior by quantifying two extremes of the regularization path. Proposition
4

\ell_{1}

4
.

Assume

8
Conclusion

We propose a robust optimization approach to sparse PCA that naturally induces sparsity and is well-suited for high-dimensional problems. Hyperparameter tuning in unsupervised learning remains an important challenge in modern data science. The adversarial formulation provides a valuable perspective with favorable theoretical properties to guide the parameterization, showing promising results out of the box. Our work is best understood in the context of its limitations. AdvPCA solves a min-max problem that is not concave-convex, restricting global theoretical guarantees. We underline that the nonconvexity of the outer problem also applies to Sparse PCA
[
51
]
. Furthermore, establishing the exact decay properties in Proposition
6
warrants further study to better understand how the radius
\delta
dictates the solution. A promising avenue for future work is extending the framework to kernel PCA using techniques similar to
[
34
]
, as well as exploring other norms, e.g., structured PCA via the nonconvex
\ell_{1/2}
-norm
[
21
]
. Finally, analyzing the adjusted total variance is also another important avenue for future work.

A.1
PCA and Eigenvector Inconsistency

PCA is a linear dimensionality reduction technique that exploits an important property of high-dimensional data: dimensions are often correlated meaning it is typically concentrated near a lower-dimensional manifold. This manifold is not necessarily linear, but PCA finds the best rank-
k
linear approximation to it. Once the subspace is found the data can be projected onto it, which reduces the dimension while preserving as much information as possible.

Figure A.1
:
Plot illustrating the eigenvector inconsistency showcased in (
A.1
). Here,
\langle u_{1},v_{1}\rangle=0
means that the estimate
v_{1}
is orthogonal to
u_{1}
. Hence, the estimate is as good as a random guess and provides no information about
u_{1}
for low signal strength
\tau_{1}
.

PCA hinges on the assumption that the dimension of the data
d
is sufficiently small in relation to the number of datapoints available,
n
. The high-dimensional setting poses issues that have been studied extensively over the years.
23
give an orientation into these phenomena, and labels them
eigenvalue spreading
,
eigenvalue bias
and
eigenvector inconsistency
. Perhaps least recognized of the three is the eigenvector inconsistency, which refers to the fact that leading eigenvectors in high-dimensional PCA can be inconsistent estimates when
d
grows proportionally with
n
.

We assume the data
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are drawn i.i.d. from an underlying distribution
p_{x}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with zero mean and covariance
\Sigma
. The sample covariance matrix is then given by
\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}
. The traditional formulation of large sample theory assumes
d
to be fixed and
n
to be large; in this setting it is well-known that
\widehat{\Sigma}
estimates
\Sigma
consistently. For example, if the data is assumed to be i.i.d., then
\widehat{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
as
n\to\infty

[
46
]
. We write the population covariance as

\Sigma=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\tau_{\ell}u_{\ell}u_{\ell}^{\top}=UTU^{\top},

where
U
is a
d\times d
orthogonal matrix whose columns
\{u_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d}
are the eigenvectors of
\Sigma
and
T
is a diagonal matrix containing the corresponding eigenvalues. By convention and without loss of generality, the eigenvalues are arranged in decreasing order such that
\tau_{1}\geq\tau_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\tau_{d}
. In the same fashion, we write the sample covariance matrix as

\widehat{\Sigma}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\xi_{\ell}v_{\ell}v_{\ell}^{\top}=V\Xi V^{\top},

where we use the same convention as for the population. The asymptotic result for the covariance matrix presented above now extends to its decomposition; if
d
is considered fixed, one can show that
\xi_{\ell}\to\tau_{\ell}
and
v_{\ell}\to u_{\ell}

\forall\ell=1,\dots,d
as
n\to\infty
. However, if we no longer consider
d
as fixed and instead let
d
grow proportionally with
n
, the eigenvector inconsistency presents itself.

For the leading eigenvector,
23
show that

\langle u_{1},v_{1}\rangle^{2}\to\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-\nu/(\tau_{1}-1)^{2}}{1+\nu/(\tau_{1}-1)},&\tau_{1}>1+\sqrt{\nu},\\
0,&\tau_{1}\in[1,\,1+\sqrt{\nu}],\end{cases}

(A.1)

when
d/n\to\nu>0
. This result is plotted in Figure
A.1
. Broadly speaking, this result shows that the leading eigenvalue (corresponding to the signal strength along the leading eigenvector) needs to be large enough to match
d
. If
\tau_{1}
remains fixed and
d
and is allowed to grow, the inconsistency properties can only get worse.

A.2
The Spiked Covariance Model

In the Sparse PCA literature, it is common to study models under simpler classes of covariance matrices. Two common ones are the spiked Wigner model and the spiked Wishart model, e.g., considered in
[
10
]
. These allow for analysis and tractable derivations.

In this work, we use the spiked covariance model from
[
46
, ch. 8.2.2]
. Let
z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
w_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
be i.i.d. noise and
\beta_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the true (unknown) spike direction. Moreover, let
\tau
define the spike strength, which is a quantity similar to the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR). Then, let
x_{i}=\sqrt{\tau}z_{i}\beta_{*}+w_{i},\;i=1,\dots,n
, which means
x_{i}
has zero mean and true covariance matrix
\Sigma_{*}=\tau\beta_{*}{\beta_{*}}^{\top}+I_{d}
. By construction, this setup means that
\beta_{*}
is the unique maximal eigenvector of
\Sigma_{*}
with eigenvalue
1+\tau
. All other eigenvalues are located at 1, meaning
\tau
is the eigengap.

To accommodate arbitrary eigenvalue spectra and multiple spike directions in our empirical evaluations, we generalize the rank-1 model to a full spectral formulation. Let
D=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
be a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be an orthogonal matrix whose columns encode the corresponding eigenvectors. For a generalized
k
-spiked model, we configure the spectrum by setting the top
k
eigenvalues to
\lambda_{j}=1+\tau_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,k
, where
\tau_{j}
are the respective eigengaps, and the remaining
d-k
ambient eigenvalues to
1
. The orthogonal matrix
V
can then be instantiated to inject arbitrary, potentially sparse, spike directions into the ambient space.

To draw a finite-sample data matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with the exact population covariance
\Sigma_{*}=VDV^{\top}
, we first sample a latent noise matrix
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
whose entries are drawn i.i.d. from a standard normal distribution,
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The observed data matrix is then constructed via the transformation

X=ZD^{1/2}V^{\top}.

(A.2)

By construction, the
i
-th sample (corresponding to the
i
-th row of
X
) is formed by the linear combination
x_{i}=VD^{1/2}z_{i}
, where
z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the
i
-th row of
Z
. Because
z_{i}
has an identity covariance matrix, this affine transformation guarantees that each sample has zero mean and exactly recovers the prescribed true covariance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=VD^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[z_{i}z_{i}^{\top}]D^{1/2}V^{\top}

\displaystyle=VDI_{d}V^{\top}

\displaystyle=VDV^{\top}=\Sigma_{*}.

This generative procedure explicitly connects our theoretical covariance assumptions to the practical simulation of our datasets, allowing independent control over the geometric structure of the eigenvectors in
V
and the signal-to-noise ratios in
D
.

. (
Right
) Without smoothing, using
\epsilon=0
.

To implement the smoothing, we cannot simply update
A\leftarrow\epsilon A+(1-\epsilon)A_{\mathrm{upd}}
. Because the set of orthogonal matrices forms a curved manifold, taking a linear step pushes the matrix off the constraint surface into the ambient space, violating the strict requirement that
A^{\top}A=I_{k}
. To address this issue, we allow the step into the ambient space but also follow it up by projecting the smoothed matrix back onto the nearest valid point on the manifold of orthogonal matrices
[
13
]
. Finding the closest orthogonal matrix to our smoothed target
A_{\mathrm{smooth}}
is formulated as the orthogonal Procrustes problem

\min_{A^{\top}A=I}\lVert A-A_{\mathrm{smooth}}\rVert_{F}^{2}.

We solve this Procrustes problem in the exact same way as when updating
A
, i.e., using the singular value decomposition; see Appendix
B.2
.

A.5
The Subgradient

We compute the subgradient of the Adversarial PCA risk (
13
), as this will be used in the proofs later on. Consider the minimization of the risk,

\argmin_{\beta}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)=\argmin_{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\lvert\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\rvert+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}\right)^{2},

where we minimize over
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as
g_{i}(\beta)=\lvert\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\rvert+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}
, so that the risk
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)
is given by

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(\beta)^{2}.

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}
is convex in
\beta
since
g_{i}
is convex and nonnegative, but it is not differentiable everywhere because of the absolute value. For convex functions however, the notion of subgradients naturally extends derivatives into the space of non-differentiable functions.

Given a convex function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to be a subgradient of
f
at
\beta
if

f(\beta^{\prime})\geq f(\beta)+\langle v,\beta^{\prime}-\beta\rangle\quad\text{for all $\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

The set of all subgradients of
f
at
\beta
is called the subdifferential, and is a convex set denoted
\partial f(\beta)
. If
f
is differentiable at
\beta
, then this consists of a single element.

In case of the absolute value function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
f(\beta)=\lvert\beta\rvert
, the subdifferential is given by

\partial\lvert\beta\rvert=\begin{cases}\{+1\}&\beta>0,\\
\{-1\}&\beta<0,\\
[-1,+1]&\beta=0.\end{cases}

We use this to derive an expression for the subdifferential of
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)
, which is the set of all subgradients. To start,

\partial\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)=\partial\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(\beta)^{2}=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(\beta)\partial g_{i}(\beta),

where
\partial g_{i}(\beta)=\partial\lvert\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\rvert+\delta\partial\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}
. Here, we apply the result for
\lvert\cdot\rvert
, which gives

\partial\lvert\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\rvert=\underbrace{\begin{cases}\{+1\}&\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}>0\\
\{-1\}&\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}<0\\
[-1,+1]&\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}=0\end{cases}}_{s_{i}}\;\partial\left(\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\right)=s_{i}(-x_{i}),

and

\partial\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}=z,\quad\text{where}\quad z_{j}=\begin{cases}\{+1\}&\beta_{j}>0\\
\{-1\}&\beta_{j}<0\\
[-1,+1]&\beta_{j}=0\end{cases}\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\dots,d.

We therefore have

\partial\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\lvert\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\rvert+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}\right)(-s_{i}x_{i}+\delta z)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}.

By noting that
\lvert\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\rvert s_{i}=\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}
, this can be simplified to

\frac{1}{2}\partial\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)=\{X^{\top}X\left(\beta-\alpha\right)\}+\left\lVert X(\beta-\alpha)\right\rVert_{1}\delta z-\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}X^{\top}s+n\delta^{2}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}z.

Proof of Proposition

Proof.

We denote the adversarial PCA objective as

\mathcal{L}(A,B)=\underset{r\in\Omega_{\delta}}{\text{max}}\;\left\lVert x-A(B^{\top}x+r)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Expanding the norm gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert x-A(B^{\top}x+r)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-A(B^{\top}x+r))^{\top}(x-A(B^{\top}x+r))

\displaystyle=x^{\top}x-x^{\top}A(B^{\top}x+r)-(B^{\top}x+r)^{\top}A^{\top}x+(B^{\top}x+r)^{\top}\underbracket{A^{\top}A}_{=I_{k}}(B^{\top}x+r)

\displaystyle=x^{\top}(I_{d}-2AB^{\top}+BB^{\top})x+2r^{\top}(B-A)^{\top}x+r^{\top}r

\displaystyle=x^{\top}\left[(B-A)(B-A)^{\top}+I_{d}-AA^{\top}\right]x+2r^{\top}(B-A)^{\top}x+r^{\top}r.

Letting

, where we note

, gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert x-A(B^{\top}x+r)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=e^{\top}e+x^{\top}x-x^{\top}AA^{\top}x+2r^{\top}e+r^{\top}r,

and hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(A,B)

\displaystyle=e^{\top}e+x^{\top}x-x^{\top}AA^{\top}x+\underset{r\in\Omega_{\delta}}{\text{max}}\;\left\{r^{\top}r+2r^{\top}e\right\}.

If we let
\Omega_{\delta}
be the hyperrectangle given by
\Omega_{\delta}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left[-\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1},\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\underset{r\in\Omega_{\delta}}{\text{max}}\;\left\{r^{\top}r+2r^{\top}e\right\}

\displaystyle=\underset{|r_{j}|\leq\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}}{\text{max}}\;\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\{r_{j}^{2}+2r_{j}e_{j}\right\}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\;\underset{|r_{j}|\leq\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}}{\text{max}}\left\{r_{j}^{2}+2r_{j}e_{j}\right\}

since we have
k
separable constraints and
k
separable objective functions. The maximization is now solvable directly:

\displaystyle\underset{|r_{j}|\leq\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}}{\text{max}}\left\{r_{j}^{2}+2r_{j}e_{j}\right\}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{r_{j}^{2}+2r_{j}e_{j}}\smash{\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$\Big|_{r_{j}=+\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}}$}}\quad\text{if $e_{j}\geq 0$}\\[10.76385pt]
{r_{j}^{2}+2r_{j}e_{j}}\smash{\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$\Big|_{r_{j}=-\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}}$}}\quad\text{if $e_{j}<0$}\\

\displaystyle=\delta_{j}^{2}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}^{2}+2\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}|e_{j}|.

So

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(A,B)

\displaystyle=x^{\top}x-x^{\top}AA^{\top}x+e^{\top}e+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\delta_{j}^{2}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}^{2}+2\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}|e_{j}|

\displaystyle=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\left\lVert A^{\top}x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(|e_{j}|+\delta_{j}\left\lVert\beta_{j}\right\rVert_{1}\right)^{2}

leaving us with the final expression for
\mathcal{L}(A,B)
. Note that the perturbation maximizing the objective corresponds to

. Also note that
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\left\lVert A^{\top}x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert x-AA^{\top}x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
since

.

∎

Proof.

(A.3)

We start by showing the equivalence between input and latent space perturbations.

Proof.

We define

\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)=\underset{\left\lVert\Delta x\right\rVert_{p}\leq\delta}{\mathrm{max}}\left\lVert x-\alpha\beta^{\top}(x+\Delta x)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Making the variable substitution
\beta^{\top}\Delta x=r
and using Hölder’s inequality
[
38
, ch. 9]
, namely

\left\lvert r\right\rvert=|\beta^{\top}\Delta x|\leq\left\lVert\Delta x\right\rVert_{p}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}\leq\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q},

gives

\boxed{\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)=\underset{\left\lVert\Delta x\right\rVert_{p}\leq\delta}{\mathrm{max}}\left\lVert x-\alpha(\beta^{\top}x+\beta^{\top}\Delta x)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\underset{\left\lvert r\right\rvert\leq\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}}{\mathrm{max}}\left\lVert x-\alpha(\beta^{\top}x+r)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\;.

To complete the proof, we must verify that the upper bound of Hölder’s inequality is tight for any conjugate pair
p,q\geq 1
. By the definition of the dual norm (see e.g.
[
5
, app. A.1.6]
)

\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}=\sup_{\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{p}\leq 1}\beta^{\top}\nu,

there always exists a vector
\nu
satisfying
\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert_{p}\leq 1
that maximizes the inner product such that
\beta^{\top}\nu=\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}
. By scaling this vector to utilize the full adversarial budget, we construct the worst-case perturbation
\Delta x_{\star}=\delta\nu
. This guarantees strict feasibility, as
\left\lVert\Delta x_{\star}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\delta
, and achieves exactly
\beta^{\top}\Delta x_{\star}=\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}
. By symmetry, the lower bound
-\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}
is also attainable. Consequently, the inner product
r=\beta^{\top}\Delta x
can span the entire interval
[-\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q},\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}]
, confirming the equivalence of the optimization domains.

∎

Isolating terms dependent on
\Delta x

\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-(\alpha^{\top}x)^{2}+e^{2}+\underset{|r|\leq\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}}{\mathrm{max}}\{r^{2}+2re\},

which is maximized by

if

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-(\alpha^{\top}x)^{2}+e^{2}+\begin{cases}\delta^{2}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}^{2}+2\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q}e&\text{if }e\geq 0\\

\displaystyle=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-(\alpha^{\top}x)^{2}+(|e|+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{q})^{2}

leaving us with our final expression for

. Note that the perturbation maximizing the objective corresponds to

Proof of Proposition

This proof is adapted from
[
35
, thm. 1]
.

The following lemma is used to prove Proposition
4
. We start by proving this.

1
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be full row rank. The minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator then admits the following dual problem:

\min_{X\alpha=X\beta}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}=\max_{\left\lVert X^{\top}\nu\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\nu^{\top}X\alpha.

Furthermore,
\widehat{\beta}
and
\hat{\nu}
are primal and dual optimal, respectively, if and only if
X^{\top}\hat{\nu}\in\partial\left\|\smash{\widehat{\beta}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}
.

Proof.

We study the primal problem

\min_{X\alpha=X\beta}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}.

We first note that we have a feasible problem (
\beta=\alpha
trivially feasible) with a convex objective function subject to an affine constraint. Therefore, Slater’s condition is satisfied and strong duality holds (see e.g.
[
5
, ch. 5.2.3]
).

The following is standard duality theory, with notation from
[
5
, ch. 5]
. The Lagrangian of the primal problem is given by

L(\beta,\nu)=\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}+\nu^{\top}(X\alpha-X\beta)

where
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the dual variable. The Lagrange dual function is then given by

\displaystyle g(\nu)=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L(\beta,\nu)=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}+\nu^{\top}(X\alpha-X\beta)\right\}

\displaystyle=\nu^{\top}X\alpha+\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}-(X^{\top}\nu)^{\top}\beta\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(a)}}}{{=}}\nu^{\top}X\alpha-\sup_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{(X^{\top}\nu)^{\top}\beta-\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}\right\},

where we in (a) use that
\inf_{x}\{f(x)\}=-\sup_{x}\{-f(x)\}
holds for an arbitrary function
f
. By the definition of the conjugate of a function, see e.g.
[
5
, ch. 3.3]
, we identify that the sup is a conjugate:

\sup_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{(X^{\top}\nu)^{\top}\beta-\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}\right\}=\left\lVert X^{\top}\nu\right\rVert_{1}^{*}.

Further, the conjugate of this
\ell_{1}
-norm is given by

\left\lVert X^{\top}\nu\right\rVert_{1}^{*}=\begin{cases}0&\left\lVert X^{\top}\nu\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1,\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

So for the Lagrange dual function
g(\nu)
to be bounded, we have the implicit constraint
\left\lVert X^{\top}\nu\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
. Using this, we can formulate the dual problem:

\boxed{\sup g(\nu)=\sup_{\left\lVert X^{\top}\nu\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\nu^{\top}X\alpha}\;.

Finally, to establish the optimality condition, we rely on the KKT conditions (see e.g.
[
5
, ch. 5.5.3]
). Since strong duality holds, the primal optimal
\widehat{\beta}
must minimize the Lagrangian evaluated at the dual optimal
\hat{\nu}
. This means the zero vector must be in the subdifferential of
L(\beta,\hat{\nu})
with respect to
\beta
, evaluated at
\widehat{\beta}
:

\boxed{0\in\partial\left\|\smash{\widehat{\beta}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}-X^{\top}\hat{\nu}\Rightarrow X^{\top}\hat{\nu}\in\partial\left\|\smash{\widehat{\beta}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}}\;.

∎

We now move on to prove Proposition
4
.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{\beta}
be the minimum
\ell_{1}
-norm interpolator
\argmin_{X\alpha=X\beta}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}
and let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

\widehat{\beta}
minimizes the Adversarial PCA risk (

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\lvert\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\rvert+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}\right)^{2},

if and only if

where
\hat{\nu}
is the solution to the convex problem
\max_{\left\lVert X^{\top}\nu\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\nu^{\top}X\alpha

To prove the theorem, we make use of the subdifferential of the risk (see Appendix
A.5
), satisfying

\frac{1}{2}\partial\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)=\{X^{\top}X(\beta-\alpha)\}+\left\|\smash{X(\beta-\alpha)}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}\delta z-\delta\left\|\smash{\beta}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}X^{\top}s+n\delta^{2}\left\|\smash{\beta}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}z.

Evaluating the subdifferential at the interpolator

means we have
X\widehat{\beta}=X\alpha

\frac{1}{2}\partial\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\widehat{\beta})=-\delta\left\|\smash{\widehat{\beta}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}X^{\top}\partial\left\lVert 0\right\rVert_{1}+n\delta^{2}\left\|\smash{\widehat{\beta}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}\partial\left\|\smash{\widehat{\beta}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}.

Because
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}
is convex,
\widehat{\beta}
is a global minimizer if and only if
0\in\partial\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\widehat{\beta})
. For the zero vector to be in this set, there must exist some specific subgradient
\hat{s}\in\partial\left\lVert 0\right\rVert_{1}
and some
\hat{z}\in\partial\left\|\smash{\widehat{\beta}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}
such that

0=-\delta\left\|\smash{\widehat{\beta}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}X^{\top}\hat{s}+n\delta^{2}\left\|\smash{\widehat{\beta}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}\hat{z}.

Assuming
\widehat{\beta}\neq 0
, we equivalently have

X^{\top}\hat{s}=n\delta\hat{z}.

From Lemma
1
, we know that the specific subgradient
\hat{z}\in\partial\left\|\smash{\widehat{\beta}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{1}
at optimality is determined by the dual solution as
\hat{z}=X^{\top}\hat{\nu}
. Substituting this yields

X^{\top}\hat{s}=n\delta X^{\top}\hat{\nu}.

By assumption,
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with
n<d
has full row rank, which means its transpose
X^{\top}
has full column rank. Consequently,
X^{\top}
has a trivial null space, allowing us to drop it from both sides of the equation and obtain

\hat{s}=n\delta\hat{\nu}.

By definition, any element
\hat{s}\in\partial\left\lVert 0\right\rVert_{1}
must satisfy
\left\|\smash{\hat{s}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. Therefore, for
\widehat{\beta}
to be the global minimizer, we must have

\left\lVert n\delta\hat{\nu}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1,

which, because
n
and
\delta
are strictly non-negative, rearranges to the required bound

\boxed{\delta\leq\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert\hat{\nu}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{-1}}\;.

Conversely, if
\delta>(n\left\lVert\hat{\nu}\right\rVert_{\infty})^{-1}
, we would have

\left\lVert\hat{s}\right\rVert_{\infty}=n\delta\left\lVert\hat{\nu}\right\rVert_{\infty}>1,

which violates the bound required for
\hat{s}
to be a valid element of the subdifferential
\partial\left\lVert 0\right\rVert_{1}
. This means no such subgradient exists,
0\notin\partial\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\widehat{\beta})
, and therefore
\widehat{\beta}
is not the global minimizer.

∎

Proof.

We want to prove that the zero solution
\widehat{\beta}=0
minimizes the Adversarial PCA risk (
13
), given by

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\lvert\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}\rvert+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}\right)^{2},

if and only if

To prove the proposition, we use the subdifferential of the risk; the complete derivation can be found in Appendix
A.5
. The subdifferential of the risk satisfies

\frac{1}{2}\partial\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)=\{X^{\top}X\left(\beta-\alpha\right)\}+\delta\left\lVert X(\beta-\alpha)\right\rVert_{1}z-\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}X^{\top}s+n\delta^{2}\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}z.

Because
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}
is convex,
\widehat{\beta}=0
is a global minimizer if and only if
0\in\partial\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(0)
. Evaluating the subdifferential at
\beta=0
simplifies the expression significantly, yielding the set

\frac{1}{2}\partial\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(0)=-\{X^{\top}X\alpha\}+\delta\left\lVert X\alpha\right\rVert_{1}\partial\left\lVert 0\right\rVert_{1}.

For the zero vector to be in this set, there must exist some specific subgradient
\hat{z}\in\partial\left\lVert 0\right\rVert_{1}
such that

0=-X^{\top}X\alpha+\delta\left\lVert X\alpha\right\rVert_{1}\hat{z}.

Solving for
\hat{z}
then gives

\hat{z}=\frac{1}{\delta\left\lVert X\alpha\right\rVert_{1}}X^{\top}X\alpha,

and taking the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm on both sides gives

\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{1}{\delta\left\lVert X\alpha\right\rVert_{1}}\left\lVert X^{\top}X\alpha\right\rVert_{\infty}.

From the definition of the subdifferential of the absolute value function (see Appendix
A.5
), any element
\hat{z}\in\partial\left\lVert 0\right\rVert_{1}

\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1

\delta=\frac{\left\lVert X^{\top}X\alpha\right\rVert_{\infty}}{\left\lVert X\alpha\right\rVert_{1}},

then
\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}=1
and subsequently

\beta=0

\delta>\frac{\left\lVert X^{\top}X\alpha\right\rVert_{\infty}}{\left\lVert X\alpha\right\rVert_{1}}

since this implies
\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}<1
. However, if

\delta<\frac{\left\lVert X^{\top}X\alpha\right\rVert_{\infty}}{\left\lVert X\alpha\right\rVert_{1}},

then
\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}>1
, meaning no such subgradient
\hat{z}\in\partial\left\lVert 0\right\rVert_{1}

\beta=0

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}(\beta)

X
when
\alpha

k\leq d
components and let the SVD of

,

, and

Consider the
j
th subproblem where
\alpha

j
th right singular vector, namely
\alpha=v_{j}

The denominator can similarly be written as

where

denotes the

By substituting these back into the expression for

\boxed{\delta_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{\left\lVert X^{\top}X\alpha\right\rVert_{\infty}}{\left\lVert X\alpha\right\rVert_{1}}=\frac{\sigma_{j}^{2}\left\lVert v_{j}\right\rVert_{\infty}}{\sigma_{j}\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{1}}=\sigma_{j}\frac{\left\lVert v_{j}\right\rVert_{\infty}}{\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{1}}}\;.

∎

\{z_{i}w_{ij}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a set of independent, zero mean, subexponential random variables for a constant
c_{1}>0
depending only on the subexponential norm of
z_{i}w_{ij}
. For small deviations from the mean
(t\leq\sqrt{n})
, we get a Gaussian tail bound, and for large deviation
(t\geq\sqrt{n})
, we get heavier subexponential tails.

Now, by using the properties of the union, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j\in[d]}\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}w_{ij}\right\rvert\geq t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\left\{\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}w_{ij}\right\rvert\geq t\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}w_{ij}\right\rvert\geq t\right),

and we can then apply (
A.6
) to each individual term. First, assuming
t\leq\sqrt{n}
, we get

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j\in[d]}\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}w_{ij}\right\rvert\geq t\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}2e^{-c_{1}t^{2}}=2de^{-c_{1}t^{2}},

which means

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j\in[d]}\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}w_{ij}\right\rvert\leq t\right)\geq 1-2de^{-c_{1}t^{2}}.

By letting
de^{-c_{1}t^{2}}=\gamma
for
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
, we get

\max_{j\in[d]}\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}w_{ij}\right\rvert\leq\sqrt{\frac{\ln{(d/\gamma)}}{c_{1}}}

with probability at least
1-2\gamma
. This holds as long as
n\geq\frac{\ln{(d/\gamma)}}{c_{1}}
.

If we use the same reasoning but instead assume the second case, i.e.,
t\geq\sqrt{n}
, we get

\max_{j\in[d]}\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}w_{ij}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\ln{(d/\gamma)}}{c_{1}\sqrt{n}}

with probability at least
1-2\gamma
, where we have let
de^{-c_{1}t\sqrt{n}}=\gamma
. This holds as long as
n\leq\frac{\ln{(d/\gamma)}}{c_{1}}
.

Thus, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\boxed{2\left\lVert\sqrt{\tau}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}w_{i}\right){\beta_{*}}^{\top}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2\sqrt{\tau}\left\lVert\beta_{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}\begin{cases}\begin{cases}\frac{\ln{(d/\gamma)}}{c_{1}n}&n\leq\frac{\ln{(d/\gamma)}}{c_{1}}\\
\sqrt{\frac{\ln{(d/\gamma)}}{nc_{1}}}&n\geq\frac{\ln{(d/\gamma)}}{c_{1}}\end{cases}\end{cases}}

with probability at least
1-2\gamma
and for a constant
c_{1}>0
.

$\ast$⃝$\ast$⃝$\ast$⃝:

For the third and final term, we note that the matrix
I_{d}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}
now provides a mixture between $\ast$⃝ and $\ast$⃝$\ast$⃝, with the important property that all entries of the matrix are sub-exponential with zero mean. By denoting

W=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}-I_{d}

the diagonal elements are given by

W_{jj}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{ij}^{2}-1,

i.e., what we had in $\ast$⃝, and the off-diagonal elements are given by

W_{jk}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{ij}w_{ik},\quad j\neq k

which is what we dealt with in $\ast$⃝$\ast$⃝. By the same reasoning as before, and using
\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{j,k}\left\lvert W_{jk}\right\rvert
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j,k}\left\lvert W_{jk}\right\rvert\geq t\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{j,k}\left\{\left\lvert W_{jk}\right\rvert\geq t\right\}\right)\leq\sum_{j,k}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert W_{jk}\right\rvert\geq t\right).

Again using
[
45
, rem. 2.9.4]
then gives

\displaystyle\boxed{\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{\infty}=\left\lVert I_{d}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}w_{i}^{\top}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\begin{cases}\frac{2\ln{(d/\sqrt{\gamma})}}{c_{2}n}&n\leq\frac{2\ln{(d/\sqrt{\gamma)}}}{c_{2}}\\
\sqrt{\frac{2\ln{(d/\sqrt{\gamma})}}{nc_{2}}}&n\geq\frac{2\ln{(d/\sqrt{\gamma})}}{c_{2}}\end{cases}}

for a constant
c_{2}>0
.

We conclude that using the spiked covariance model, treating
\tau
,
\gamma
and
\left\|\smash{\beta_{*}}\vphantom{\text{X}}\right\|_{\infty}
as constants, with high probability we have

\boxed{\left\lVert\Sigma_{*}-\widehat{\Sigma}\right\rVert_{\infty}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\ln{d}}{n}}}\;.

∎

B.7
Proof of Algorithm
2

The algorithm we use was proposed by
32
. We outline the main steps of the proof and refer the reader to this paper for a more detailed description.

Proof.

We want to solve

\displaystyle\argmin_{\beta}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(|\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}|+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}\right)^{2}

(A.7)

As outlined already, by solving this subproblem, (
8
) is given by the sum of the individual solutions and thus the ”fix
A
, solve for
B
” part is completed. Note here that we drop the
j
-subscript for simplicity. We denote the summand $\ast$⃝:

\argmin_{\beta}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underbrace{\left(|\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}|+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}\right)^{2}}_{\text{\textcircled{$\ast$}}}.

We now use something that is sometimes referred to as the
\eta
-trick (see e.g
[
2
]
):

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{1}^{2}=\underset{\mathbf{\eta}\in\Delta_{d}}{\text{min}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\frac{w_{\ell}^{2}}{\eta_{\ell}},

where the set
\Delta_{d}
is a simplex. If we think of
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{1}^{2}
as
$\ast$⃝
, then we get

\displaystyle\text{\textcircled{$\ast$}}=\underset{\mathbf{\eta}^{(i)}\in\Delta_{d+1}^{(i)}}{\text{min}}\left\{\frac{(\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i})^{2}}{\eta_{0}^{(i)}}+\delta^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\frac{\beta_{\ell}^{2}}{\eta_{\ell}^{(i)}}\right\}.

This is uniquely minimized at

\displaystyle\eta_{0}^{(i)}=\frac{|\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}|}{|\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}|+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}},\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{\ell}^{(i)}=\frac{\delta|\beta_{\ell}|}{|\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i}|+\delta\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{1}}.

If we set

\displaystyle w_{i}:=\frac{1}{\eta_{0}^{(i)}},\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{\ell}:=\delta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\eta_{\ell}^{(i)}},

we can write (
A.7
) as

\displaystyle\argmin_{\beta}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\alpha^{\top}x_{i}-\beta^{\top}x_{i})^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\gamma_{\ell}\beta_{\ell}^{2}.

(A.8)

(
A.8
) is a weighted ridge regression problem that can be solved using standard methods. To summarize, when we include the minimization over
\mathbf{\eta}
, we get a joint problem over
\beta
and
\mathbf{\eta}
. Since the minimization over
\mathbf{\eta}
is uniquely given in closed-form, we can iterate between fixing
\beta
and
\mathbf{\eta}
and solve for one at a time until convergence.

∎
