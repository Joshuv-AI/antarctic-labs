1 Executive Summary

This project studied one of the central problems in modern theoretical physics: how the exact rules
of quantum mechanics are encoded in black holes and in gravitational path integrals. Black holes
appear to erase information, while quantum mechanics says that information must be preserved.
During the award period, the project used solvable models of quantum gravity, especially Jackiw-
Teitelboim gravity, the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev model, double-scaled matrix integrals, and related tools
from quantum chaos, to make this conflict mathematically precise and to resolve parts of it in
controlled settings.
The main accomplishments were advances in the understanding of wormholes, factorization, late-
time spectral statistics, and the microscopic structure behind apparently smooth semiclassical grav-
itational answers. The work showed that wormhole saddles by themselves are not the whole story
in fixed-coupling theories; additional saddles are required to recover factorization. It also developed
a convergent genus expansion for the late-time plateau, clarified subleading effects needed for uni-
tarity, extended the mathematical control of low-dimensional gravity path integrals, and built new
connections between gravitational observables, random matrix theory, and quantum chaos. Work
carried out during the award period also fed directly into a small number of papers first posted in
October and November 2025, including papers on three-dimensional gravity, subsystem complexity,
and de Sitter state counting. These results strengthened the conceptual bridge between quantum
gravity, quantum information, random matrix theory, and many-body chaos. Because this award
supported basic theoretical research rather than technology demonstration, economic feasibility is
not the relevant metric; the public benefit lies in improved scientific understanding, broad dis-
semination through public preprints and peer-reviewed publications, and training and coordination
within a high-impact research community.

2 Original Goals and Scope

The original proposal organized the research into three main tasks:

1. Task 1: Averaged discreteness from wormholes. Test whether replica wormholes require
 an ensemble interpretation, determine how unitarity is preserved, understand the plateau in the
 spectral form factor, and apply these lessons to three-dimensional gravity.

2. Task 2: Quantum gravity beyond geometry. Go beyond smooth wormhole averages and
 identify the degrees of freedom or mechanisms responsible for the fine-grained discreteness of a
 single quantum theory.

3. Task 3: Applications to cosmology. Transfer the lessons learned from black holes and
 wormholes to de Sitter space and quantum cosmology.

The central hypothesis throughout the project was that solvable models could reveal how the dis-
creteness of black-hole microstates appears in gravitational calculations, and that wormholes would
be a useful but incomplete part of that story.

3 Actual Accomplishments Compared with the Project Goals

Task 1: Averaged discreteness from wormholes

This task saw substantial progress and produced multiple direct outcomes. Wormholes without
averaging clarified the role of wormholes in fixed-coupling theories, showing that additional “half-
wormhole” saddles are essential for factorization and sharpening the ensemble interpretation pro-
posed in the original grant. Subleading Weingartens studied the subleading structures needed for
unitarity in Haar-like averages and found corresponding effects in JT gravity and Brownian SYK.
A convergent genus expansion for the plateau directly addressed the late-time plateau in the spec-
tral form factor, giving a controlled expansion with nonzero radius of convergence. The original
3d gravity objective also saw meaningful related progress during the award period, including work
with student collaborators on random-matrix behavior and non-decaying contributions in 3d pure
gravity. That thread continued immediately after the formal end date and contributed to Cusps in
3d gravity (posted October 22, 2025), which is therefore discussed below as a late-appearing output
rooted in award-period research.

Task 2: Quantum gravity beyond geometry

This task also saw strong progress. Rather than finding a single simple replacement for the wormhole
picture, the work identified several concrete structures that go beyond naive semiclassical averag-
ing. A symmetry algebra in double-scaled SYK isolated a deformation of the JT gravity algebra
and identified discrete states associated with an Einstein-Rosen bridge. Scramblon loops studied
interactions of the collective mode responsible for chaos and showed regimes where the simplest
single-mode picture breaks down. Bulk thimbles dual to trace relations analyzed unstable saddles
and their Lefschetz thimbles in a half-BPS AdS/CFT setting, providing a distinct example of how
subtle nonperturbative structures encode information not visible in a naive semiclassical treatment.
Related award-supported work by postdocs also explored bit models of replica wormholes, the ro-
bustness of the double-cone geometry, and the nonperturbative Hilbert space of JT gravity. Work
begun during the award period also continued into Growth and collapse of subsystem complexity
under random unitary circuits (posted October 21, 2025), which extends the project’s quantum-
information connections.

Task 3: Applications to cosmology

The cosmology component remained more exploratory during the award period and was not the main
source of in-period publications. This was an intentional shift in emphasis rather than a project
failure. The field evolved rapidly after the proposal was written, and the most productive path
was to first sharpen the black-hole and wormhole foundations using fixed-coupling factorization,
matrix-integral methods, and low-dimensional gravity. That refocusing improved the rigor and
impact of the core black-hole program and positioned the group to carry similar questions into
cosmology in follow-on work. This is reflected in On the phase of the de Sitter density of states
(posted November 3, 2025), a closely related follow-on paper growing out of award-period efforts on
de Sitter and Euclidean gravitational path integrals.

4 Project Activities, Approaches, and Scientific Results

The project was primarily analytical and theoretical. Its recurring strategy was to attack concep-
tual problems in quantum gravity using solvable models where exact or semiclassically controlled
calculations are possible and where different viewpoints can be compared directly: gravitational
path integrals, random matrix theory, SYK-type many-body systems, and methods from quantum
chaos and moduli-space geometry.

1. Replica wormholes, factorization, and black-hole information

The original proposal emphasized replica wormholes as probes of the underlying discreteness of
black holes. During the award period, the project clarified both the power and the limitations of
this picture.
Wormholes without averaging showed that wormhole saddles can persist even when disorder cou-
plings are fixed, but that they must be supplemented by additional saddles that are highly sensitive
to the microscopic couplings. This was an important conceptual result because it explained how
factorization is restored in a single theory while leaving wormhole physics visible in averaged de-
scriptions.
Subleading Weingartens then addressed the subleading structures required by exact unitarity. In
both JT gravity with matter and Brownian SYK, the work identified off-shell or nearly off-shell con-
figurations that account for corrections analogous to subleading terms in Haar integrals. Together,
these papers refined the original ensemble-based hypothesis of the proposal into a more complete
picture in which wormholes are part of the answer but not the full answer.
Firewalls from wormholes explored a dramatic nonperturbative implication of wormhole physics:
the tunneling of a very old black hole to a white-hole/firewall configuration through baby-universe
emission. Although this was not framed exactly as in the original subtask on the evaporating
black-hole S matrix, it directly addressed late-time black-hole dynamics and the consequences of
nontrivial wormhole topologies for black-hole information.

2. Spectral statistics, plateau physics, and low-dimensional gravity

The original proposal highlighted the spectral form factor plateau as a particularly sharp test of
microscopic discreteness. This topic became one of the clearest success stories of the award period.
A convergent genus expansion for the plateau proposed a precise late-time formula for the spectral
form factor with a genus expansion that has a nonzero radius of convergence. The paper also
connected this expansion to the semiclassical theory of periodic-orbit encounters, giving a concrete
bridge between matrix-integral control and older ideas from quantum chaos.
Sphere and disk partition functions in Liouville and in matrix integrals sharpened the understanding
of low-dimensional gravity partition functions by matching Liouville and matrix-integral calcula-
tions and showing, in particular, that the sphere partition function in JT gravity is divergent. A
Mirzakhani recursion for non-orientable surfaces extended geometric recursion methods to a set-
ting relevant for non-orientable topologies and orthogonal random-matrix ensembles. These results
expanded the mathematical toolbox available for the study of gravity path integrals and topological
expansions.

On the proposal’s 3d gravity objective, the award period produced related progress on random-
matrix behavior and non-decaying contributions in 3d pure gravity through work with student
collaborators, even though that thread matured more slowly than the original timetable suggested.
That effort fed directly into the later paper Cusps in 3d gravity, which proposed a renormalized
treatment of divergences in the sum over topologies by including cusped hyperbolic manifolds as
counterterms.

3. Beyond the ensemble: discrete structure, chaos, and emergent algebra

Several outputs addressed the proposal’s second major theme: going beyond smooth ensemble-
averaged observables to identify the structures that govern individual theories or more microscopic
descriptions.
A symmetry algebra in double-scaled SYK identified a deformed symmetry algebra acting on the
chord Hilbert space of the double-scaled SYK model. The work connected this algebra to near-
horizon symmetries and to discrete states moving in an Einstein-Rosen bridge, giving a concrete
framework for studying how microscopic discreteness survives or is reorganized in semiclassical
limits.
Scramblon loops studied self-interactions of the mode that mediates the butterfly effect in large-N
chaotic systems. The paper showed that loop effects can invalidate the single-scramblon approxi-
mation in some regimes well before the leading contribution becomes order one, clarifying when the
simplest effective description of chaos fails.
Bulk thimbles dual to trace relations extended the project’s impact into adjacent areas of holography
and quantum information by analyzing unstable saddles and Lefschetz thimbles associated with
giant graviton physics and trace relations. This reinforced the broader goal of linking gravitational
reasoning to quantum-information-theoretic structure. A closely related late-appearing continuation
was Growth and collapse of subsystem complexity under random unitary circuits, which studied how
subsystem complexity evolves and then collapses in chaotic dynamics and compared those results
to holographic expectations.

4. Community synthesis and dissemination

Two Snowmass community reports were produced during the award period: Snowmass White Paper:
Quantum Aspects of Black Holes and the Emergence of Spacetime and TF1 Snowmass Report:
Quantum gravity, string theory, and black holes. These were not original research articles in the
same sense as the papers above, but they were significant public products of the project environment.
They synthesized recent progress, articulated open problems, and helped coordinate the broader U.S.
high-energy theory community around black holes, holography, and quantum gravity.

6 Problems Encountered and Departures from Planned Methodol-
 ogy

No administrative or resource problem prevented progress on the core scientific program. The main
changes were scientific and strategic:

 • The field moved unusually quickly after the original proposal was written, especially because of
 the rapid development of replica-wormhole ideas. As a result, the most productive route was not
 to execute the proposal exactly in sequence, but to focus first on precise issues of factorization,
 subleading saddles, genus expansions, and fixed-coupling effects.

 • One important scientific lesson was that a simple wormhole-only picture is insufficient in a fixed
 microscopic theory. Additional saddles, off-shell effects, and more refined algebraic structures
 are needed. This was not a setback; it was one of the main discoveries of the award period.

 • The cosmology/de Sitter component remained longer-term and was not developed into a compa-
 rable body of publications during the award period. The impact of this departure was positive
 overall, because concentrating on the black-hole side of the program produced a more rigorous
 and influential set of results.

Networks and collaborations fostered

The award supported a broad collaboration network spanning quantum gravity, string theory,
and quantum information. Representative coauthors during the award period included Phil Saad,
Stephen Shenker, Shunyu Yao, Zhenbin Yang, Rahul Mahajan, Chao-Ming Yan, Shreya Vardhan,
and others, with collaborations distributed across Stanford and external institutions. The award
also helped support postdoctoral researchers Andreas Blommaert, Henry Maxfield, and Yiming
Chen. The Snowmass reports further contributed to national-scale community coordination.

Techniques, methods, and other research products

Key conceptual and technical products of the award include:

 • The identification of half-wormholes and related fixed-coupling saddles needed for factorization.

 • Subleading Weingarten-type structures in JT gravity and Brownian SYK relevant for exact
 unitarity.

 • A convergent genus expansion for the late-time plateau in spectral statistics.

 • Extended recursion and moduli-space methods for non-orientable surfaces.

 • Algebraic control of double-scaled SYK and its near-horizon / wormhole interpretation.

 • New treatments of divergent sums over topologies in three-dimensional gravity using cusped
 manifolds.

9 Conclusion

During the period of performance, this award produced a coherent body of work on the quantum
structure of black holes, wormholes, and related solvable models. The project met its central
scientific goal of sharpening how microscopic discreteness appears, or fails to appear, in semiclassical
gravity. The strongest outcomes came in the clarification of wormholes versus factorization, the
development of controlled late-time expansions, the extension of low-dimensional gravity tools, and
renewed progress on three-dimensional gravity. A few papers first posted in October and November

2025 show that this research momentum continued immediately after the formal end of the award
and that the award-period work was still feeding directly into new results. Although the cosmology
portion remained less developed within the award window, the foundational advances made here
significantly strengthened the conceptual basis for future work in that direction.
