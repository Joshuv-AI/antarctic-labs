ABSTRACT
This work examined the impact of large loads on utility distribution system models using the
Sandia-developed open-source software DREAMS. It was shown that hosting capacity varies with
location and changes after any asset is added to, or removed from, a system. Despite the tested
models having similar rated voltages and other characteristics, their thermal and voltage constrained
hosting capacity varied over 2 MW.
The addition of a 3-phase balanced constant power large load with power factor of 1.0 exhibited
non-linear reductions to all voltage constrained hosting capacities. The reductions to thermal
constrained hosting capacity from a load with similar characteristics was more linear, related to
the size of the added load, and did not impact all model buses. Co-located capacitors were shown
to accommodate demand that was beyond the baseline voltage constrained hosting capacity limits,
however, the costs and benefits from this approach were found to not be 1:1 and required additional
available thermal capacity.

1. INTRODUCTION

Planning for load growth is a common practice among electric utilities and system operators.
Recently, anticipation of large loads related to data centers, increased manufacturing, concentrated
electric vehicle charging, and other factors have led to an increase in interconnect requests that may
overwhelm system operators [2]. While large loads are often assumed to connect to high voltage
systems (transmission) due to power requirements [3], there are situations where a medium voltage,
or distribution system level (1 kV to 30 kV phase to neutral), connection may appear more feasible
for a variety reasons.
Regardless of voltage level connection, large loads typically require some form of technical analysis
to identify and address system impacts. Hosting Capacity (HC) analysis is a type of study that
aims to describe the existing ability of a system to accommodate new assets before operational
violations are reported. Often, two types of HC are calculated: Voltage Constrained HC (VCHC)
and Thermal Constrained HC (TCHC). VCHC is the amount of an asset that can be added before
voltage violations occur, and TCHC is the amount of an asset that can be added before thermal, or
equipment overloading, occurs.
HC is impacted after any additional asset is added to, or removed from, a system with effects that
are unique to each particular circuit and location. As such, a workflow involving batch processing,
or repeatedly calculating HC is required. This work utilized DREAMS [4] (an open-source Python
code package that can calculate VCHC and TCHC using OpenDSS [1] models) to calculate HC
results, demonstrate impacts of adding loads above known HC limits, and simulate a VCHC
mitigation approach using a co-located capacitor. While each system model is anonymous and
unique, identified characteristics of large load impacts on distribution systems may be informative
to any region dealing with similar interconnection requests.

3. HOSTING CAPACITY METHODOLOGY

Generally, to identify the demand HC of a system bus, a load is added to the model at that location
and increased in magnitude until a violation is reported. For this work, the TCHC violation
threshold was any modeled element over 100% rated value, while the VCHC was defined as any
bus voltage outside of the 0.95 - 1.05 VPU range. Snapshot powerflow solutions were used as time
series data was not available for all models. The nodal HC algorithm used to calculate VCHC and
TCHC (included in [4]) is depicted in Figure 3-1.
From a high level, for each bus of interest, a trivially small load is added to initialize a lower HC
bound. This non-violation hosting capacity asset value is referred to as non_vhc in the diagram. If
there is no violation reported, that small load is replaced with a very large load to induce a system
violation. This larger load value that causes a system violation is treated as the upper bound of the
HC range and referred to as vhc in Figure 3-1.
From there, the next asset value to test is calculated as the mid-point of the known HC range, which
is defined as [non_vch, vch]. Using the new asset value, the model is solved again. If there was
no system violation, the lower HC bound is updated with the recently tested asset value, and if
there was a violation reported, the upper bound of the HC is updated. The process of calculating
the mid-point, testing the new asset value, and replacing the appropriate bound is repeated until
the HC range span is less than a defined threshold (15 kW in this work). After which point, the
lower, non-violation inducing asset size is accepted as the HC for that location. The entire process
is repeated for all buses of interest.

6. RESULT INTERPRETATIONS

All tested feeders, despite similar substation kV, reported different baseline HC ranges and limiting
types of HC. This was due to the different system characteristics of each model. Except in cases
where there were locations with zero HC, the limiting HC value was often near the end of each
feeder, or furthest away from the substation. Additionally, VCHC results appeared generally non-
linear while TCHC were more linear. The relatively low TCHC reported for Feeder 4 was likely
due to a limiting in-line element near the substation that impacted all downstream elements.
Adding an acceptably sized load to a system impacted the VCHC of all other locations, but not all
TCHC results. This was likely due to the load location being on a different branch of the feeder.
VCHC impacts were more noticeable ‘upstream’, or closer to the substation, than ‘downstream’ of
the added load. Load impacts to VCHC were non-linear, while TCHC impacts were more linear
and related to the added load size.
The marginally unacceptable load was mitigated with a capacitor equal to load (PF of 0.71 leading),
while the significantly unacceptable load required a much large capacitor (resulting in a 0.45 leading
PF). This showed that VCHC benefits from added capacitance is not 1:1.
Further, the TCHC impact from adding a 2 MVAR capacitor to the 2 MW load was reported as
somewhere between -50 kW and 50 kW (Figure 5-10), while adding a 8 MVAR capacitor to the
4 MW load reduced TCHC by roughly 3 MW (Figure 5-15). Which highlights that TCHC costs
from added capacitance is not 1:1.

7. CONCLUSION

This work showed how DREAMS could be used to run nodal hosting capacity on distribution
feeders as well as explore the impacts from possible mitigation approaches.
Computed VCHC and TCHC results from similar feeders were found to have different charac-
teristics. However, common characteristics of HC impact from additional load were identified.
Specifically, large load addition was shown to have system wide non-linear impacts to VCHC,
while TCHC impacts were more linear and may not affect all system buses. Additionally. TCHC
impacts were found to be more related to the added load size while VCHC impacts tended to be
larger than the added load upstream of interconnection and generally smaller downstream of asset
interconnection.
Simulations showed how VCHC was increased with additional capacitors due to their ability to
increase local voltage, but required more thermal bandwidth to supply increased reactive power
flow. The benefits and costs associated with capacitor mitigation were found to not be 1:1.
Finally, this work showed that not only is the immediate HC result of value for identifying acceptable
system connections, but also post-asset-connection HC to account for known variation from model
behavior or to anticipate other operating margins.
Future work may involve using DREAMS to perform quasi-static time-series HC simulations that
utilize interpolation to reduce the number of required model solutions.
