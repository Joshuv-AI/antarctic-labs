 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The overarching goal of the voucher project is to evaluate the suitability of NexTC materials as
 functional layers in perovskite photovoltaic technology. There are several different perovskite
 device configurations that are being pursued for commercialization. Most are superstrate
 configuration (light enters the device through the glass substrate) and can be subdivided into two
 categories: PIN and NIP. At minimum, PIN devices have a transparent conductor (typically
 Indium Tin Dioxide [ITO] or Fluorine-Doped Tin Oxide [FTO]) and hole transport layer (HTL)
 deposited on glass before the perovskite is deposited. Alternatively, NIP devices have a
 transparent conductor and electron transport layer (ETL) deposited before the perovskite is
 deposited. NexTC produces several inks that can be deposited to replace or modify the
 transparent conductor and/or the ETL or HTL, depending on the device. NLR will produce PIN
 and NIP perovskite devices on substrates that have been coated by NexTC and compare to NLR-
 standard devices that perform among the best for research labs across the world. The work will
 ultimately expose NexTC products to the up-and-coming perovskite PV industry.

 Summary of Research Results:

 The overall goal of the research is to provide an initial evaluation of NexTC materials for
 application in metal halide perovskite (MHP) solar cells. Metal halide perovskite solar cells are
 constructed in numerous architectures. We have chosen three representative stacks to reflect the
 most popular architectures being pursued for commercialization (Figure 1). There are two single-
 junction architectures, PIN stack and NIP stack. In the PIN stack, NexTC’s indium tin oxide
 (ITO) will be explored on the hole collection side of the device as the current-collecting
 electrode. For the NIP, NexTC’s tin oxide (SnO2) will be applied as the electron transport layer.
 The third architecture is the two-terminal tandem, where NextTC’s material will serve as a
 recombination layer between a silicon absorber and metal halide perovskite layer. To address the
 viability of the three architectures, five tasks were devised.

 The red box in each stack highlights where the NexTC material is being applied. The tandem
 architecture is simplified to show which layers were explored in this work.

 Task 2: Electrochemical Characterization

 NLR will evaluate samples that will be coated with SnO2, electrochemical characterization to
 evaluate selectivity of the contact to transport electrons and block holes

 Task 2 Results:

 Hole blocking was an important characteristic of an electron transport layer in an MHP solar cell.
 The goal of this work was to evaluate the hole-blocking selectivity of the SnO2 layer. NLR had
 developed a technique dedicated to evaluating transport layers based on electrochemistry where
 the sample was immersed in an electrolyte with a Fe2+/Fe3+ with a 0.5 M KCl supporting
 electrolyte (Figure 2).

 Fig. 2. Diagram of electrochemical cell setup used to evaluate hole-blocking of NexTC SnO2 layer.

 The sample formed a semiconductor-electrolyte junction with band-bending induced in the SnO2
 (Figure 1a). Control measurements of the ITO without SnO2 yielded a cyclic voltammogram
 with the classic duck-shaped curve of a reversible redox couple (Figure 1b). There was no
 blocking of electrons or holes in this case. In contrast, there was a sharp cathodic onset observed
 when SnO2 was coated on the ITO (Figure 1b).

 Fig. 3. (a) Diagram illustrating the semiconductor-electrolyte junction. (b) Current density as a
 function of potential relative to Silver/Silver Chloride (Ag/AgCl) for three different SnO2 samples
 and an ITO control.

 4

 We compared two NexTC samples to a standard sample fabricated at NLR. The NLR sample is
 composed of tin oxide (SnOx), where x varies depending on oxidation state and manufacturing
 conditions, nanoparticles (NPs) because this composition is common in the MHP literature, and
 has been vetted in many other projects at NLR. The NLR NP sample exhibits hole blocking
 behavior indicated by limited current as positive voltages. The 16.7 nm SnO 2 samples obtained
 from NexTC show slightly more anodic current, indicating poorer hole-blocking. This likely
 indicates the presence of pinholes in the film since there is also a peak close to 0 V, which likely
 corresponds to the ITO beneath the SnO2. In contrast, the thicker samples (48.8 nm) that were
 annealed at lower temperatures show a near-ideal response, with negligible anodic current and a
 sharp onset at ~0.75 V.

 Task 3: Optical and electrical characterization

 NLR will evaluate samples coated with ITO or SnO2 will be characterized using UV-vis
 spectroscopy to evaluate optical transmission of the samples. For ITO on glass and Si, the sheet
 resistance will be evaluated using 4-point probe measurements.

 Task 3 Results:

 The goal of this task was to have NLR evaluate the optical and electrical properties of samples
 coated with ITO or SnO2 characterized using ultraviolet-visible-near-infrared (UV-vis-NIR)
 spectroscopy to evaluate optical transmission of the samples. For ITO on glass and silicon (Si),
 the goal was to evaluate sheet resistance four-point probe measurements. However, ITO samples
 were not received, so sheet resistance measurements were not performed.

 We compared two NexTC samples to a standard sample fabricated at NLR and an ITO control
 sample. The NLR sample was composed of SnOx NPs. UV-vis-NIR transmission spectra of the
 control ITO sample exhibited a maximum transmission of ~90% at 500 nm with a tail at higher
 wavelengths due to free carrier absorption of the ITO. Other features in the spectrum were due to
 thin film interference.

 For ideal solar cell applications, the SnO2 layer should not have introduced any reflection or
 parasitic absorption (reduced transmission), which would have decreased the current of the
 device. Application of SnO2 modified the transmission spectra due to new interfaces that
 introduced additional interference but no significant decrease in transmission (Figure 4). This
 was true for the NLR NP control sample as well as the NexTC samples, though interference
 effects changed the structure of the spectrum. For instance, the peak transmission was shifted to
 lower wavelengths for the 48 nm sample, relative to ITO. Transmission also increased at high
 energy. One noticeable difference was the decrease in free carrier absorption when the SnO2 was
 annealed at 500 °C. The higher annealing temperature likely degraded the ITO below by
 reducing the number of free carriers. Overall, however, the optical quality of the SnO2 samples
 was high.

 Task 4: Perovskite device fabrication and characterization

 NLR will evaluate ITO/glass samples coated by SnO2 at NexTC will be coated with remaining
 layers to form NIP perovskite PV devices. Glass samples coated with ITO at NexTC will be
 coated with the remaining layers to form PIN perovskite PV devices. Devices will be tested
 using currentvoltage characterization (reverse/forward sweeps and stabilized power output) with
 six or more individual pixels for each dependent variable for statistical confidence. Control
 devices with all layers deposited by NLR will be fabricated for comparison to devices with
 NexTC coatings. If characterization of Si surface coatings are continuous and conformal, 2-
 terminal tandem devices will be fabricated on the ITO-coated Si and characterized the same way
 single-junction devices are tested.

 Task 4 Results:

 The goal of this task was to integrate NexTC materials into device stacks that were completed
 and measured by NLR. NLR evaluated ITO/glass samples coated with SnO2 at NexTC where
 NLR deposited the remaining layers to form NIP perovskite PV devices. Glass samples coated
 with ITO at NexTC would have been coated with the remaining layers at NLR to form PIN
 perovskite PV devices. However, ITO samples were not received.

 NIP devices were tested using illuminated current density-voltage characterization
 (reverse/forward sweeps) with six or more individual pixels for each dependent variable for
 statistical confidence. Control devices with all layers deposited by NLR were fabricated for
 comparison with devices with NexTC coatings. Control devices exhibited rectifying behavior
 and a curve shifted away from the origin, indicating collection of photocurrent (Figure 5).
 Devices exhibited open-circuit voltages as high as 1 V and short-circuit current as high as 20 mA
 cm-2. Champion devices showed power conversion efficiency of 16%.

 In contrast, NIP devices fabricated with SnO2 layers from NexTC showed little rectification, no
 photovoltage, and high negative currents (Figure 6). Each of these attributes pointed to shorts in
 the device where the SnO2 layer did not protect the ITO from coming in contact with other layers
 in the device.

 Task 5: Coating quality characterization

 NLR will evaluate ITO samples coated on silicon, optical microscopy and profilometry will be
 used to identify pinholes and evaluate large-area conformal coverage (1> mm scale). Atomic

 mm).

 Task 5 Results:

 The goal of this task was to evaluate the possibility of NexTC’s ITO to serve as a recombination
 layer in a silicon/MHP tandem architecture. NLR evaluated ITO samples coated on silicon, and
 optical microscopy and profilometry were used to identify pinholes and evaluate large-area
 conformal coverage (greater than 1 mm scale). Atomic force microscopy and scanning electron
 microscopy were used to evaluate smaller areas (less than 1 mm).

 This task was not completed entirely as planned since samples coated on silicon were not
 received. Instead, NLR obtained ITO precursor ink from NexTC. It was assumed that spin-
 coated films of ITO would not conformally coat textured Si, so NLR performed spray-coating,
 which was a method more capable of conformally coating a non-planar surface. Rather than
 coating on textured Si, initial trials were performed on glass with the intention of moving to
 textured Si if films were successful.

 A spray-coating study was performed with different solvents to determine if conformal films
 could be achieved. All spray-coating runs were performed in a nitrogen (N₂)-purged glovebox at
 a temperature of 300 °C to decompose the incident precursor with a pump rate of 0.05 mL min⁻¹,
 ink concentration of 0.015 M, and N₂ flow rate of 6 L min⁻¹. Optical microscopy was performed
 on samples deposited from different inks. Both n-methylpyrrolidone (NMP, Figure 7a) and
 dimethylformamide (DMF, Figure 7b) produced films with roughness that exceeded 100 µm.
 Combining both solvents yielded a similar film (Figure 7c). However, when combining DMF
 with a more volatile solvent such as acetonitrile (ACN), the film roughness was reduced. Though
 small particles could be observed, the film quality was more continuous and may have pointed to
 a way forward for this method.
