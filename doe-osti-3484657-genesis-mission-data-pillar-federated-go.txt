A. Executive Summary
The Genesis platform’s mission is to enable consistent, auditable, and policy-compliant cross-
laboratory sharing, stewardship, access, review, and lifecycle management of data assets across
participating U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) and National Nuclear Security Administration
(NNSA) National Laboratories and their contractors. Genesis is designed to support discovery,
governed access, and lifecycle management of data assets in a federated environment, while
preserving the authority of the originating site for all substantive legal, security, and governance
determinations
Genesis serves a broad community of stakeholders across DOE and NNSA, including laboratory staff
and contractors, data owners, stewards, custodians, and generators, as well as legal, contracts,
records management, technology transfer, and cybersecurity teams. Its user base also encompasses
governance boards and working groups, scientific and technical reviewers, and individuals involved
in AI and computational workflows. Each of these groups plays a distinct and interdependent role
within the governance process—spanning data creation, stewardship, review, rights and
compliance evaluation, and ultimately the approval and provisioning of governed access—ensuring
that data assets are responsibly managed, discoverable, and usable across the federated Genesis
platform.
Genesis provides a comprehensive set of capabilities that collectively enable secure, interoperable,
and policy-aligned data governance across DOE and NNSA laboratories. Its federated governance
framework supports cross-site and cross-organizational data sharing while clearly distinguishing
among ownership, stewardship, rights, records, and sharing authority. Through robust metadata
management, Genesis implements a shared metadata standard and data card structure that enable
consistent, machine-actionable descriptions of data assets to support discovery, interoperability,
and lifecycle tracking. Policy-as-code automation further strengthens governance by enabling
scalable, automated controls such as metadata validation, access-control enforcement, rights and
agreement checks, and audit logging. The platform preserves originating-site authority through an
incremental review model that applies only the additional governance steps needed for federated
sharing. It also ensures compliance and auditability by enforcing site-specific and federal
requirements and maintaining clear logging and escalation pathways for ambiguous or non-
compliant conditions. Finally, Genesis promotes continuous improvement by incorporating lessons
learned and stakeholder feedback to iteratively refine governance processes, metadata standards,
and policy logic.
This framework was developed through tri-laboratory collaboration under the Genesis Mission
Data Pillar. Although it reflects the current tri-lab Genesis implementation environment, it is
intended to serve as a broader governance reference model applicable across DOE and NNSA
laboratories, plants, sites, and related organizations, subject to local authority, mission-specific
needs, and evolving enterprise governance direction.
The governance structures described in this document represent the current working model and
should be understood as evolving. As Genesis matures and as DOE and NNSA governance bodies

continue to develop, the scopes, roles, interfaces, escalation paths, and decision forums described
here may be refined or revised.

B. Purpose
The Genesis Mission Data Pillar Federated Governance Framework defines the end-to-end
framework for governing the sharing, stewardship, access, review, and lifecycle management of
data assets1 across participating DOE and NNSA National Laboratories and their M&O contractors.
Its purpose is to enable consistent, auditable, and policy-compliant cross-laboratory sharing while
preserving the authority of the originating or otherwise responsible site for all substantive legal,
security, and governance determinations.
This process is designed to support discovery, cross-site sharing, governed access, and lifecycle
management of data assets in federated DOE and NNSA environments. It records, normalizes, maps,
and enforces governance-relevant metadata and constraints, but it does not replace site-specific
legal, security, review-and-release, records, or policy processes. Genesis does not itself determine
classification, declassification, CUI designation, export-control status, ownership, contractual rights,
records status, intellectual property rights, or publication/release approval. Those determinations
remain with the responsible classification officials, legal and contracts offices, records-management
authorities, technology-transfer offices, export-control authorities, privacy authorities, and other
designated site officials.
The governance process also distinguishes between ownership, contractual rights in data, custody
or stewardship, records status, and sharing authority. These concepts are often conflated in
collaborative settings, particularly in multi-laboratory work. Genesis must support those
distinctions in metadata and workflow, but no action in Genesis, such as registering a dataset,
attaching a data card, or provisioning access, should be interpreted as changing ownership, rights,
or obligations established by law, contract, or approved site process.
For datasets used in AI-related workflows, this governance process applies to the dataset metadata,
provenance, lineage, use restrictions, quality indicators, and AI-use suitability conditions associated
with the data asset. It does not, by itself, govern application-level AI system behavior except insofar
as that behavior depends on governed data assets, produces derived governed outputs, or triggers
governance controls tied to the data.
This framework was developed by the tri-laboratory Genesis Mission collaboration as part of the
Genesis Data Pillar effort. While its immediate implementation context reflects current tri-lab
Genesis work, the framework is intended to support broader applicability across DOE and NNSA
laboratories and sites where cross-organizational governance, governed sharing, metadata
consistency, and federated access control are needed.

C. Scope
This Governance Process applies to all cross-site and cross-organizational data-sharing activities
conducted under the Genesis Mission Data Pillar. It covers the full lifecycle of governed data assets,
including raw datasets, processed datasets, derived data products, time-series data, simulation
outputs, metadata packages, training datasets, evaluation datasets, data services, and related
dataset-level documentation.
The process governs the metadata, workflows, approvals, controls, and enforcement mechanisms
necessary to support compliant data sharing across participating organizations. This includes
metadata validation, review routing, classification and control metadata ingestion, access and
sharing restrictions, agreement verification, cross-laboratory interoperability mapping,
provisioning, audit logging, lifecycle updates, and continuous review. It also includes the
identification and recording, for governance purposes, of rights- and obligation-related metadata
that may affect sharing. Such metadata may include whether an asset was first produced under a
DOE or other governing contract, whether it incorporates background or third-party content,
whether it is subject to delivery obligations, whether it may constitute a federal or contractor
record, and whether legal, records, export-control, publication, or technology-transfer reviews are
required.
This process should not be interpreted as a complete enterprise governance framework for all DOE
systems and assets. It is a mission data governance process specifically intended to support the
data-related activities of Genesis while aligning, where appropriate, with broader DOE-level
metadata, repository, and policy expectations.
Although the current implementation emphasis is the Genesis Mission and its participating tri-lab
environment, the governance patterns, metadata structures, workflow logic, and access-control
principles described here are intended to be extensible across the broader DOE and NNSA
laboratory and site ecosystem, subject to local authority and future enterprise governance
decisions.

C.1 DOE-Level Genesis Platform Governance Framework
The Genesis Mission Data Pillar Governance Process should be understood within a broader DOE-
level Genesis Platform and Ecosystem Governance Framework. At full maturity, that broader
framework would establish enterprise direction for the governance of datasets, code, models,
workflows, agents, documentation, platform services, and supporting infrastructure across the
Genesis ecosystem. The DOE-level framework would provide common direction on ownership
metadata, access control expectations, provenance and lineage, lifecycle management, review
obligations, repository interoperability, and minimum metadata requirements.
Within such a framework, governance functions as the control plane for Genesis. It determines how
assets are described, reviewed, shared, protected, and audited across federated DOE and NNSA
environments. Governance metadata would provide the structure needed to support repository and
discovery interoperability, while preserving the local authority of participating sites and
organizations over the underlying decisions that affect data sharing.

Rights Model
The decision-rights model articulates how governance authority is distributed across DOE, NNSA,
and participating sites within the federated Genesis framework. At the enterprise level, DOE
establishes policy authority by setting minimum governance standards, defining enterprise
metadata requirements, establishing cross-laboratory interoperability expectations, and
determining the boundaries for policy-as-code automation. NNSA holds mission-specific
governance authority, interpreting DOE’s enterprise direction for NNSA needs, approving NNSA-
level exceptions, and elevating mission-related issues that require enterprise adjudication.
Individual sites retain full authoritative review authority for all determinations involving
classification, CUI, export control, intellectual property and legal constraints, records obligations,
and release approval responsibilities that cannot be overridden by DOE or NNSA governance
bodies. Meanwhile, the Genesis Platform and its Governance Teams exercise operational authority
by implementing workflows, validating metadata, managing issue escalation, and coordinating
governance activities across the federated platform. Together, these elements define a layered,
federated model consistent with DOE/NNSA governance recommendations.
The governance relationships and decision-rights allocations presented here should be understood
as the current working representation, not as a permanently fixed final structure. As DOE-level,
NNSA-level, and Genesis-specific governance bodies continue to develop, the exact organizational
boundaries, forum responsibilities, and escalation routes may evolve. The decision-rights model

The DOE governance role described here is intended to reflect enterprise-level authority over
standards, interoperability requirements, and policy-as-code boundaries. The NNSA Data
Governance Board, in turn, exercises decision-making for NNSA-specific mission needs but does not
override DOE-level-policy or the authoritative review conducted at individual sites. Each
participating site remains the definitive authority for all internal determinations—such as
classification, CUI, UCNI, export control, legal rights, records obligations, intellectual property and
licensing considerations, and release decisions—and these determinations cannot be superseded by
DOE or NNSA bodies. Finally, Genesis operational teams are responsible for implementing
governance rules and workflows, but they do not adjudicate or reinterpret governance decisions
established by DOE, NNSA, or the originating sites
A conflict-resolution pathway is therefore necessary to reflect the federated governance principles
in the DOE/NNSA framework. Under this model, conflicts arising at the local or site level, such as
issues related to classification, CUI, export control, intellectual property, or records, are resolved by
the originating site’s authoritative review bodies. When a disagreement spans multiple laboratories
or concerns mission-specific governance matters, it is escalated to the NNSA Data Governance
Board for adjudication within the NNSA mission context. Enterprise-level disputes or
disagreements between NNSA and DOE are elevated to the DOE-level governance forum,. Technical
disputes involving metadata, validation rules, or workflow logic are handled by the Genesis Data
Pillar Governance Team and only escalate upward when policy authority rather than operational
clarification is required.

D. NNSA-Level Genesis Platform Governance
Structure
The NNSA-level Genesis governance structure is the mission-specific implementation layer within
the broader DOE context. It exists to develop standards, coordinate stewardship, resolve issues, and
implement the governance process across participating NNSA-aligned laboratories and their
collaborating organizations. It should be understood as part of a layered governance structure
rather than as an isolated or self-sufficient authority.
At the mission level, the NNSA Data Governance Board provides governance direction for NNSA-
aligned Genesis data governance matters, including mission-specific standards, exceptions, and
implementation questions. Supporting it is the NNSA Data Management Working Group, which
develops process drafts, metadata expectations, implementation recommendations, and policy
proposals while coordinating with broader Genesis activities.
The Genesis Mission Data Pillar Governance Team acts as the central execution and coordination
layer. It translates governance direction into operational workflows, metadata requirements,
stewardship processes, and implementation guidance. It also coordinates with the Data Ecosystem
and Stewardship, Model, and Infrastructure teams to ensure that data-related governance remains
connected to repository, platform, and downstream AI or workflow concerns.

To ensure alignment across the federated governance structure, any conflict between an NNSA Data
Governance Board (DGB)-approved policy, standard, or governance decision and an existing site
policy, contractual requirement, or authoritative review process must be resolved through an
escalation pathway that preserves site authority and complies with DOE and NNSA directives. When
such a conflict arises, the originating site’s authoritative determinations, including classification,
CUI, export control, legal and contractual rights, records obligations, and release decisions, take
precedence and cannot be superseded by DGB decisions. In these cases, the issue should first be
elevated to the NNSA DGB for clarification and possible adjustment to the mission-level standard. If
the conflict stems from enterprise-level requirements or cannot be reconciled at the mission level,
the matter is then escalated to the DOE-level governance forum for adjudication.

E. Roles and Responsibilities
The governance process depends on a distributed set of roles that together support ownership,
stewardship, review, access control, rights analysis, records determination, legal agreement
verification, and technical enforcement. In some cases, one individual or organization may fill more
than one role; however, the responsibilities themselves remain conceptually distinct and should not
be collapsed.
Participating sites are not required to create new organizational roles solely for Genesis governance.
Instead, each site should map the Genesis governance roles, such as Data Owner, Data Steward, Data
Custodian, Contractual Rights Assessor, Classification Authority, and others, to the closest
equivalent existing roles within the site’s established governance, legal, classification, cybersecurity,
and records-management structures. This approach respects longstanding site authorities and

 11

processes, avoids unnecessary reorganization, and ensures that Genesis governance responsibilities
integrate smoothly with existing operational, legal, and compliance frameworks. If a site identifies a
gap where no comparable role exists, the site may designate an appropriate individual or office to
fulfill the responsibility, but such decisions remain under site authority. This ensures that Genesis
governance remains consistent with each site’s policies, contractual frameworks, and review
processes, while still supporting the cross-laboratory alignment required for federated governance
across DOE and NNSA.

F. Governance Process Workflow
Genesis Mission governance operates through a federated review model. Each laboratory performs
its own complete internal sensitivity, security, legal, and review process before the data asset enters
Genesis for cross-laboratory governance. Genesis then consumes the authoritative labels and
governance metadata from the originating site and applies only the incremental responsibilities
needed to support federated sharing, discovery, and access. These incremental responsibilities
include metadata validation, interoperability mapping, cross-site sharing checks, partner-tier or
audience checks, and routing to additional review only when the new sharing context introduces
new governance implications, Figure 3.

 Figure 3. Genesis Platform and Governance Framework

 14

The governance workflow begins with internal authoritative review, through which the originating
site determines classification, CUI, UCNI, export-control, IP, privacy, records, and release status as
applicable. Once those determinations are made, the data remain protected in an approved source
environment while the Data Steward confirms that the metadata package is complete. Where cross-
laboratory sharing introduces additional concerns—such as a new external partner, foreign-
national involvement, a new target environment, or unresolved inheritance of restrictions from
parent assets—incremental review is triggered before the asset proceeds further.
After rights, records, and agreement implications have been assessed and any necessary
agreements verified, the Data Card and associated metadata are submitted to the Discovery Engine.
The Discovery Engine validates metadata completeness and consistency, maps authoritative labels
into the shared interoperability schema, and supports the determination of the asset’s
discoverability and access eligibility. Access is then provisioned into approved environments only if
all metadata, approvals, agreements, and access conditions are in place. All actions are logged. Later
changes in labels, agreements, release status, rights, stewardship, or lineage require corresponding
updates in Genesis so that the metadata remain synchronized with authoritative site status.

G. Metadata, Shared Data Card, and Labeling
Requirements
Metadata is the control plane for Genesis-governed data assets. Every data asset entering Genesis
must have sufficient metadata to support discovery, governed use, access review, stewardship,
interoperability, and lifecycle tracking. The detailed metadata standard, including category
structure, required and conditional metadata fields, Discovery Engine ingest expectations, shared
data card alignment, and controlled vocabulary structures, is maintained in the standalone
document Genesis Mission Data Pillar Metadata Standards, August 2026. References in this
framework to metadata standards should be understood as references to that standalone
document.The shared data card remains the principal structured metadata artifact used to express
the Genesis metadata standard in author-facing and machine-validatable form. The card is intended
to support multiple capabilities, including discovery, accessibility, interoperability, reuse, governed
use, AI-related use, and repository integration. It is not owned by any one program or platform.
Rather, it is intended to support multiple DOE-aligned initiatives, including ModCon, Office of
Science, ASC, and Genesis-related implementations.
Sensitivity and governance metadata carried in Genesis should preserve authoritative source
markings while also representing governance-relevant constraints such as export control, privacy,
rights, release status, records status, and review provenance through structured fields. This allows
Genesis to support machine validation and governed sharing without erasing source truth or
overriding site authority.

H. Data Ownership, Rights, Stewardship, and
Records Framework
A sound governance process must distinguish clearly among ownership, contractual rights,
stewardship, records obligations, and sharing authority. These concepts overlap in practice but are
not interchangeable.
Ownership refers to the underlying legally recognized rights or title in the asset. Contractual rights
in data refer to the government or sponsor’s rights under applicable contracts, grants, CRADAs, or
other instruments. Background and third-party data may remain subject to their original rights and
restrictions even when integrated into a larger project. Stewardship and custody refer to
responsibility for metadata quality, access coordination, storage, and lifecycle management, but do
not by themselves imply legal ownership. Records status is determined separately and may
introduce retention, access, preservation, or disposition obligations.
Sharing authority therefore depends on the combined effect of sensitivity, rights, agreements,
records status, and approved use conditions. Genesis metadata and workflows must preserve these
distinctions. No single metadata field or workflow state in Genesis should be interpreted as
collapsing them into one.

 18

I. Policy & Agreement Compliance
Compliance with site-specific and federal requirements remains with the originating or otherwise
responsible organization. Genesis records and enforces the existence and result of those
determinations for governance purposes, but it does not re-adjudicate them.
This includes compliance with classification, CUI, UCNI, export control, privacy, records obligations,
intellectual property restrictions, STI/public release requirements, and agreement-based
conditions such as DUAs, CRADAs, MOUs, NDAs, and licenses. It also includes ensuring that external
environments, cloud services, and vendor or partner systems do not receive access or use rights
beyond those explicitly approved. Where vendors or external services are involved, agreements
should address data rights, retention, deletion, portability, and restrictions on using non-public data
to train vendor commercial models without explicit authorization.
Because the shared data card is also meant to support broader data management and sharing plan
expectations, the governance process should treat compliance not merely as a local internal matter,
but as part of the metadata and workflow logic needed to support repository deposit, controlled
access, reproducibility, and public release where appropriate.

J. Escalation & Enforcement
Genesis governance must support clear escalation and enforcement pathways. If metadata are
missing, inconsistent, unresolved, or otherwise not sufficient to support a governance decision, the
workflow should halt or route to human review. Examples of such stop conditions include missing
classification basis, absent agreement coverage, unresolved export or partner restrictions,
incomplete rights or records determinations, missing required review, or unapproved
environments.
Genesis itself does not resolve these disputes or ambiguities. It enforces the workflow logic that
prevents sharing from proceeding until the necessary corrections or authoritative decisions have
been made. Non-compliance may therefore result in return for correction, workflow suspension,
denial of provisioning, revocation of access, or escalation to the responsible site or governance body.

K. Federated Labeling & Incremental Review
Model
The federated review model is central to Genesis governance. In this model, each site performs its
own full authoritative review and labeling, and Genesis ingests the resulting metadata for cross-
laboratory use. Genesis applies only incremental responsibilities needed to support discovery,
partner-tiering, metadata interoperability, and cross-lab sharing checks. This preserves source
authority, avoids duplicative review, and reduces unnecessary operational burden.
The same logic applies to rights, records, and agreement metadata. Where those determinations
have already been made by the site, Genesis records and enforces them. Where a new cross-

 19

laboratory sharing context raises new questions—such as a new partner class or a new
environment—Genesis routes the issue for incremental review rather than attempting to make the
determination itself.
This federated model is directly aligned with the updated RBAC/ABAC framework, which similarly
treats Genesis as an orchestration and enforcement layer that evaluates metadata, roles, and
attributes but does not replace authoritative site decisions.

L. Continuous Improvement & Amendment
The governance process is intended to improve over time through measured use, periodic review,
and structured incorporation of lessons learned. Metrics such as metadata completeness, request
volume, turnaround time, returned submissions, exception volume, lineage quality, and cross-
laboratory interoperability issues should be reviewed on a recurring basis. The relevant governance
bodies should use these observations to refine processes, metadata requirements, validation logic,
controlled vocabularies, and implementation guidance.
Annual review of the process should be conducted, and more frequent updates should occur
whenever changes in DOE, NNSA, or site-level authorities require them. Suggestions for improving
the shared data card, shared metadata schema, or governance process should continue to be
accepted, and such suggestions may be incorporated into later versions as the shared system
evolves.

M. Document Management
This Governance Process and its appendices should be managed as a coordinated, version-
controlled documentation set. Changes to authorities, metadata standards, sensitivity handling,
rights fields, validation logic, controlled vocabularies, or implementation assumptions should be
propagated consistently across the governance process, shared data card, metadata schema,
metadata dictionary, and related guidance. The Genesis Teams site or other designated document-
management system should maintain the authoritative change history and distribution of updates.

N. Policy-as-Code Requirements for Governance
Automation Implementation
To support scalable governance, federated sharing, automated workflow routing, and future agentic
or AI-assisted operations, Genesis should implement governance controls using policy-as-code
wherever the relevant rules are sufficiently structured, approved, and traceable to authoritative
metadata. In this context, policy-as-code refers to the machine-readable and machine-evaluable
representation of governance rules, metadata requirements, access conditions, review triggers, and
environment restrictions. Its purpose is to enable governance decisions to be enforced consistently,
rapidly, and transparently across systems and workflows as seen in Figure 4.

 20

Governance requirements should be structured as a traceable hierarchy of policy, control objectives,
and control. In this model, a policy expresses the governing intent, a control objective defines the
compliance outcome to be achieved, and a control defines the specific enforceable check, decision
rule, validation, restriction, or obligation implemented in Genesis. Automated decisions, exceptions,
and violations should be traceable to the applicable control objective and control identifier.

A request is evaluated using validated identity, attribute, and governance metadata inputs.
Depending on the governance boundary, Genesis may either render the decision directly or route
the request to an originating-site decision service. The resulting decision outcome is logged to
support auditability, traceability, and incident review.
Policy-as-code does not replace the governance process itself. It does not independently classify
information, designate CUI, determine export-control status, resolve ownership or rights questions,
or create waivers outside approved governance pathways. Rather, it operationalizes decisions that
have already been made by the appropriate authorities and expresses those decisions in a form that
can be enforced programmatically. Metadata are therefore the canonical input layer for automated
governance evaluation.
This section applies to the governance implementation for Genesis data assets, metadata,
workflows, services, platforms, and access-control mechanisms wherever governance decisions
may be evaluated or enforced programmatically. It includes metadata validation, access-control
enforcement, rights and agreement checks, environment restrictions, delegated decision making,

and governance controls for automated or agentic workflows that request, transform, summarize,
retrieve, route, or share governed data.

N.1. Core Principle: Originating-Site Authority Remains Primary
Originating-site authority remains the governing source of truth for the data it contributes to
Genesis. Automated Genesis services must not override, weaken, reinterpret, or bypass site-
authoritative determinations. They may enforce only those permissions, restrictions, labels,
conditions, and controls that have been supplied by the originating site or otherwise approved
through the Genesis governance process.
Where an automated policy outcome conflicts with an originating-site determination, the
originating-site determination governs unless superseded by an authorized governance authority.
Genesis should therefore be understood as an orchestration and enforcement layer, not as a
substitute source of authority.

N.2. Foundational Requirements
All governance-relevant determinations that Genesis is expected to enforce must be represented as
machine-readable metadata using the shared metadata standard defined in the appendices. At a
minimum, this includes, where applicable, classification status, CUI category and dissemination
controls, export-control basis, rights and intellectual property restrictions, records-management
constraints, agreement-based restrictions, approved partner classes, approved environments,
approved purposes of use, derivation or aggregation indicators, review status, review authority, and
any expiration, re-review, or conditional-use limits.
These metadata should use structured fields, controlled vocabularies, and standardized values
wherever possible so that policy evaluation can be deterministic and reproducible. Free-text fields
may still be used for explanatory context, but free text alone should not serve as the sole basis for
automated policy enforcement unless it has been translated into an approved structured control.
Genesis governance implementation should treat metadata as the canonical decision input layer for
automated policy evaluation. Before a data asset, derived product, workflow, or access request is
evaluated for sharing or use, the required metadata must be checked for completeness, syntactic
validity, controlled vocabulary compliance, consistency across fields, current review status,
authoritative source attribution, and the validity of any review or expiration dates. If required
metadata is missing, stale, inconsistent, ambiguous, or not machine-readable, automated approval
must not proceed. In such cases, the request should either be routed for human review or returned
for metadata correction.
Machine-readable governance metadata and evaluable policy logic shall be mapped to defined
control objectives and discrete controls so that each automated decision can be traced to the
specific control basis being enforced. Where conditional approvals or obligations apply, the
corresponding control identifiers shall also be recorded so that downstream enforcement,
monitoring, and audit review remain consistent.

authorization attributes, required training, need-to-know basis, agreement coverage, approved
purpose of use, approved destination environment, classification status, CUI status, export-control
basis, rights restrictions, records obligations, derivative-data sensitivity, and any applicable site-
specific overlays.

N.3. Deterministic, Delegated, and Bounded Decisioning
Automated decisions should be deterministic and reproducible based on the policy version in effect
at the time of decision, the metadata values evaluated, the requester attributes used, the action
requested, the destination or execution environment, and the decision service or rule engine
invoked. If a delegated originating-site decision service is involved, that delegated response must
also be part of the recorded decision basis. Genesis should therefore be able to reconstruct the basis
for any automated decision during audit, incident review, dispute resolution, or governance lifecycle
review.
Where an originating site exposes an authoritative automated decision service capable of evaluating
sharing, access, export, environment, partner, or agreement conditions, Genesis should support
federated or delegated decision making. In such cases, Genesis may submit a decision request to the

 23

originating-site system and receive an authoritative result such as approval, denial, conditional
approval, request for additional metadata, or requirement for human review. Delegated site
decisions should be treated as authoritative within the scope defined by the originating site. Genesis
must not infer approval from silence, timeout, service failure, or incomplete delegated response. In
all such cases, the workflow should fail closed or route to human review.
Policy-as-code must not be used to change classification status, assign or remove CUI designations
without approved authority, override export-control determinations, waive records requirements,
alter intellectual property restrictions, expand agreement rights, approve access beyond
originating-site constraints, permit use for an unapproved purpose, move data into an unapproved
environment, or bypass required human review. Automation may enforce, route, validate, or flag
such conditions, but it should not replace authoritative review unless that authority has explicitly
approved machine execution of the relevant decision.

N.5. Policy Lifecycle Management
All machine-readable governance policies should be versioned. Each automated decision should
record the policy version used at the time of evaluation. Historical versions must be retained as
necessary to support audit, reproducibility, incident review, and governance oversight.
Policies used for automated governance enforcement should be approved through the Genesis
governance process before deployment. Changes to policy should be subject to documented change
control, including a policy owner, rationale, effective date, version identifier, affected workflows or
data classes, testing requirements, rollback strategy, and communication plan. High-impact policy
changes—particularly those affecting classification, CUI, export, rights enforcement, partner access,
or site authority—should require review by the Genesis Data Pillar Governance Team, affected
originating sites, and, where applicable, the NNSA Data Governance Board.
Policy logic should be tested against representative scenarios before deployment. Testing should
include approved cases, denied cases, conditional approvals, escalation cases, incomplete metadata
cases, conflicting metadata cases, cross-laboratory sharing cases, environment restriction cases,
agentic workflow cases, and delegated-site-decision cases. Policy logic should not be deployed for
automated enforcement until testing demonstrates that it operates within approved governance
boundaries.
Policies that are obsolete, superseded, or no longer approved should be deprecated or retired
through documented lifecycle management. Retired policies should not govern new decisions, but
they may need to be retained to support reconstruction of historical decisions.

N.7. Human Review and Escalation
Automation must include a safe fallback to human review. Human review is required whenever
metadata are missing or ambiguous, metadata conflict with other metadata or site information,
policy logic cannot produce a deterministic outcome, a new or unapproved partner class is involved,
foreign-national or export/CUI concerns are unresolved, agreement scope is unclear, a derived or
aggregated data product may alter sensitivity, the requested environment is unapproved, site policy
requires manual review, an exception is requested, or an automated system fails or returns an
incomplete result.
Escalation paths should identify the appropriate responsible authority, which may include
originating-site reviewers, Genesis Data Pillar Governance Team members, subject-matter
authorities, or the NNSA Data Governance Board, depending on the nature of the issue.

N.9. Interoperability and Federation Requirements
To support cross-laboratory operation, policy-as-code in Genesis should be federated and
interoperable. This requires common metadata fields and vocabularies, shared decision-outcome
semantics, consistent reason codes, defined interfaces for delegated decisioning, support for site-
specific policy constraints, compatibility across Genesis platform services and laboratory systems,
and the ability to evaluate cross-lab requests without ad hoc manual coordination wherever the
rules are already clear and approved.
Federation should not require sites to surrender authority over their data. Instead, it should allow
originating-site determinations to be expressed and enforced consistently across Genesis
workflows.

operationally required. However, performance objectives must not override governance
requirements. If timely decisioning cannot be achieved without compromising compliance,
traceability, or source authority, the request should be delayed, denied, or escalated as appropriate.

N.13. Limitations
Policy-as-code is an implementation mechanism for governance. It does not replace the governance
process itself. It must not replace classification review, CUI designation, export-control review,
records-management authority, intellectual property review, agreement negotiation or
interpretation where human judgment is required, originating-site approval, governance-board
oversight, or required human review for high-risk, ambiguous, or exceptional cases.
Its proper role is narrower: to enable approved governance determinations to be enforced
consistently, rapidly, and transparently across Genesis systems while remaining bound by
authoritative metadata, approved policy logic, originating-site authority, and applicable law,
regulation, policy, contract, and agreement terms.

Appendix A: Classification, Sensitivity, and Control
Marking Reference

This appendix provides a concise reference for the principal classification, sensitivity,
dissemination, and control marking types that may apply to Genesis-governed data assets. Its
function is to support cross-laboratory interoperability by helping users distinguish between
authoritative source determinations and Genesis interoperability overlays. It is informational and
does not replace originating-site review, official markings, or the legal and regulatory requirements
that govern handling and dissemination.
Authoritative determinations originate at the site level. Classification, CUI, export-control, privacy,
and related restrictions must be established through the responsible site’s internal processes.
Genesis does not make these determinations. Instead, it consumes and records the resulting
metadata and applies them to cross-laboratory discovery, access, and sharing workflows.
Multiple control regimes may apply to the same asset simultaneously. A dataset or related asset may
be subject to classified handling, export-control restrictions, proprietary restrictions, records
obligations, dissemination controls, or multiple categories of CUI at the same time. Genesis
metadata must preserve all applicable conditions necessary to support compliant sharing and
enforcement.
Legacy labels such as OUO and predecessor site-specific designations should not be treated as
stand-alone current control categories for new cross-laboratory governance decisions. Before such
assets are shared across sites, the originating organization should determine the appropriate
current control basis through its authorized processes. Genesis may record both the original legacy
label and the current interpreted control representation, but the latter is the governance-relevant
basis for current sharing workflows.
Genesis interoperability tiers remain overlays only. They support cross-laboratory discovery, access
coordination, and enforcement but do not replace official classification levels, CUI categories, or
source-authoritative markings.

Appendix B: Shared Data Card Alignment and
Requirements

The shared data card is the principal structured metadata artifact used to express the metadata
standard in author-facing and machine-validated form. It is intended to serve multiple capabilities,
including discovery, accessibility, interoperability, reuse, governed use, AI-related use, and
repository integration. It is not owned by any one program or platform. Rather, it is a metadata
artifact intended to support multiple DOE-aligned initiatives, including ModCon, Office of Science,
ASC, and Genesis-related implementations.
The current merged data card work reflects a decision to organize the card around intended
capabilities and validation annotations rather than forcing all authors through a single monolithic
structure. This capability-based organization allows cards to vary in required content depending on
whether they are being created for draft registration, governed sharing, publication, AI use, or
repository integration. At the same time, the card preserves a common core needed for dataset
discovery and governance.
The merged data card established several key requirements that this appendix must reflect. These
include use of the intended_capabilities block to drive validation; use of field annotations such as
discoverable_required, governance_required, and ai_use_required; support for a domain_metadata
extension block; replacement of the earlier sensitivity block with the revised source-of-truth
sensitivity and governance metadata structure; and explicit recognition that a public/open reuse
license is not always the sole governing instrument for a dataset.
The shared data card also needs to support DOE data management and sharing plan expectations. It
should therefore contain enough metadata to support validation and replication of published
results, repository selection and deposit, public access where appropriate, provenance, and justified
sharing limitations where sharing cannot be fully open. This makes the card useful not only for
internal governance but also for broader scientific data management and sharing workflows.

Appendix C: Policy Barriers and Lab-Specific
Considerations

C.1 Purpose
This appendix summarizes the principal policy barriers and implementation differences that affect cross-
laboratory data sharing under Genesis. Its purpose is to identify recurring friction points that must be
addressed through harmonization, workflow design, metadata standardization, or governance guidance
to support scalable, consistent, and policy-compliant sharing across participating organizations.
The examples documented here are drawn from the current tri-laboratory Genesis implementation
environment and are intended to be illustrative rather than exhaustive. Comparable or additional
barriers may exist across other DOE laboratories, plants, sites, user facilities, and partner environments.
Genesis will continue to identify and address opportunities to reduce such barriers across the broader
DOE and NNSA ecosystem as governance, metadata, access, and collaboration practices mature.
Genesis operates on a federated review model. Each laboratory remains responsible for its own
authoritative classification, CUI, export-control, legal, records, and release determinations. Genesis
consumes those determinations as metadata and performs only the incremental governance functions
needed to support cross-site discovery, access, and sharing. This model depends on a sufficient degree of
harmonization in metadata semantics, partner treatment, review triggers, and environment controls.

C.3 Lab-Specific Notes
 • The barriers described above manifest differently across participating laboratories,
 reflecting each site’s unique governance structures, review practices, collaboration tools,
 and operational environments.
 • While these variations can introduce friction, they also highlight opportunities for continued
 alignment and shared improvement across the broader Genesis ecosystem.
 • Across laboratories, commonly observed themes include:
 • External Collaboration Tools: Many sites are refining policies for external Git hosting
 platforms, issue trackers, and other collaborative development tools. Establishing clearer,
 consistently applied guidance and shared review pathways can streamline collaboration and
 reduce duplicative approvals.
 • Review Workflows and Transfer Processes: Laboratories may employ size-based transfer
 triggers or maintain overlapping workflows for software, export-controlled content, or data
 release. Transitioning toward more risk-based triggers and harmonized review paths can
 reduce cycle time while maintaining compliance.
 • Agreement and Vendor Interactions: Collaboration agreements, vendor acceptance
 processes, and data-use instruments sometimes interact in complex ways. Laboratories are
 actively identifying opportunities to simplify and better align these requirements—
 particularly in scenarios involving combined code, data, and external services.
 • Cloud and External Processing: Sites may differ in how they evaluate short-duration or
 low-risk compute tasks in external environments. Developing proportionate review
 pathways helps ensure that approved external workflows remain accessible while
 preserving necessary protections.
 • Metadata, Access Control, and Interoperability Semantics: Although many laboratories are
 closely aligned in several key governance areas, ongoing harmonization of metadata
 semantics, access-control logic, and federated governance practices continues to strengthen
 cross-site collaboration.
Overall, these variations reflect healthy differences in site missions, legacy systems, and governance
models. The Genesis Mission Data Pillar Governance Team works collaboratively with participating
laboratories to identify these areas, prioritize alignment, and incorporate improvements into shared
metadata standards, governance workflows, and implementation guidance.

C.4 Governance Implications
The barriers documented in this appendix do not imply that a single uniform policy must replace all
local practices. Rather, they identify areas in which Genesis requires shared metadata semantics,
clearer review boundaries, common partner taxonomy, more explicit treatment of mixed-sensitivity
and derived assets, and proportionate governance of environments and external processing.
These barriers should not be interpreted as unique to the tri-labs, nor as a complete inventory of
DOE-wide issues. Instead, they provide a current example set to inform future harmonization efforts

across DOE and NNSA laboratories and sites. Additional site-specific and mission-specific barriers
are expected to emerge as broader implementation proceeds.

and incorporate corresponding harmonization actions into shared governance processes, metadata
