4 Executive Summary
 The overarching goal of the proposed project is to develop a simple, energy-saving, high-
selectivity technology for efficiently converting rare-earth oxides (REOs) to rare-earth metals (RE-
metals) using microwave plasma under low temperature and inexpensive H2 and N2. Nd2O3 and
CeO2 are the representative REOs used for the microwave plasma reduction process. University
of Wyoming (UW) has completed the microwave plasma based REOs to RE-metals setup. The
UW team started Nd2O3 reduction with N2 plasma. The MKS AX2600 series microwave plasma
system can be divided into two sections: the plasma generator and the microwave generator.
Typically, plasma performance is inferred by measuring the applied pulse voltage.

 The University of Wyoming (UW) team started the reduction of rare earth metal oxide
(CeO2) with N2 microwave plasma using different voltages for different time intervals. X-ray
diffraction (XRD) reveals that all the samples treated with N2 plasma maintain the crystalline
planes of the cubic fluorite structures of CeO2 treated with N2 plasma. In addition, the Raman
spectroscopy confirms the fluorite structured CeO2 treated with nitrogen plasma. Moreover, the
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) demonstrates the presence of interstitial nitrogen (Nint)
species, which may relate to N-O bonds and adsorbed nitrogen for the CeO2 treated with N2
plasma. Based on these results, it is revealed that N2 plasma is not suitable for the reduction of rare
earth oxide. Then, the University of Wyoming (UW) team initiated the reduction of rare earth
metal oxide (CeO2) using H2 microwave plasma. XRD revealed that there was no change in the
crystalline structure at the beginning of the treatment. However, for CeO2 treated with hydrogen
plasma for 33-52 hours), new peaks appear beside those related to CeO2, indicating the formation
of another crystalline phase. TGA analysis demonstrates that treatment of CeO2 with hydrogen
plasma has no influence on thermal stability. Also, the N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm reveals
that there is no significant change in the surface area after treating CeO2 with hydrogen plasma.
To confirm the influence of H2 plasma in other rare earth oxides, Nd2O3 is treated with hydrogen
plasma using a hydrogen flow rate of 1500 ml/min at a voltage equal to 5V. Also, at the beginning
of the reduction of Nd2O3 with hydrogen plasma, there is no influence on the crystalline structure,
as demonstrated by the XRD. However, for the sample Nd2O3 treated with hydrogen plasma for
32 hours, new peaks beside the peaks of Nd2O3 started to appear. As the time of treatment with
hydrogen plasma increases, additional peaks start to appear beside the peaks of Nd2O3 revealing
change in the crystalline structure. Some of these peaks which have values close to (28.4, 29.4,

 8

30.5, 32.3, 36.6, 42.1, 48.9, 50.08, 55.1, 59.05, 59.64, 60.68, 62.5, 63.25) may be related to
elemental Nd. Also, it can be related to other lower oxidation states of Nd2O3 like (Nd2O4, Nd2O2,
NdO2, NdO). (According to data from the Rigaku database).

 The use of hydrogen to reduce rare earth oxides has many advantages as hydrogen is
renewable, and it does not produce harmful emissions in the production of the metal. Also, it avoids
the use of carbon and the emission of CO2. This agrees with the DOE goal for enhancing energy
efficiency in both processes and on-site steam and power generation and reducing the carbon
footprint of energy sources and feedstocks by incorporating lower-carbon fossil energy and
integrating low-fossil carbon sources like clean hydrogen.

 Access to rare earth elements, crucial components in numerous technologies, is important,
so one of the aims is to produce rare earth on a commercial scale. The largest commercially
available microwave generator that was found was 100 kW. This could be used for the scale-up.
If we assume that it is possible to obtain complete (or significant) conversion, then scaling up from
0.5 grams of neodymium oxide in 238 hours to kilograms per hour is very difficult. The next
logical step is a larger-scale batch test to reduce the required time, power, and hydrogen usage.
Making this a continuous process would be very difficult due to the vacuum conditions and the
fact that it is a heterogeneous (gas-solid) chemical reaction. A 100-kW power supply, operated at
80 kW, would be a scale-up of about 1,700 times. This might be used to treat a kilogram of
Neodymium oxide to produce 0.9 kilograms of Nd. If so planned, the system should be able to
vary power, pressure, temperature, and hydrogen flow rate. If successful, this might provide the
information needed for preliminary flow sheet development and capital and operating cost
estimates.

5 Introduction
 Microwave plasma is defined as a gas ionized by means of a high-frequency
electromagnetic field. The term "ionized" means that an electron is not bound to an atom or
molecule so that it becomes a positively charged ion. One of the characteristics of plasma is that it
can produce very high concentrations of energetic and chemically active species such as electrons,
ions, atoms and radicals, excited states, photons of different wavelengths, and high concentrations
of the species may be out of thermodynamic equilibrium. Electrons are heated by the alternating
electric field, which produces the energy to produce the ionization and excitation of neutral atoms
and molecules. The energy the electrons receive is lost when they collide with the heavy particles,
and the temperature between them can be balanced. However, there are cases in which the
temperature of the electrons significantly exceeds that of the heavy particles; these are called non-
thermal plasmas. Non-thermal plasmas are generally generated at low pressures or lower power
levels or in different types of discharge systems. One of the main advantages of non-equilibrium
plasma is the high energy efficiency for generating atomic hydrogen, excited hydrogen molecules,
and other active species that stimulate the reduction process of gas and solid oxides at low
temperatures.

 Rare Earth metals and their compounds find uses in various fields like high-performance
magnets, magnetic refrigeration, catalysis, luminescent materials, chemical sensors, high-
temperature superconductors, nuclear reactors, and medicine due to their unique physical and
chemical properties arising from the unpaired 4f electronic configuration. Rare-earth metals are
highly reactive. Performing reactions with rare-earth metals in a controlled manner is still difficult.
On the one hand, the surface area of the bulk metals is small, and crushing them into pieces is still
difficult owing to their hardness and sensitivity. On the other hand, the surface of rare-earth metals
is usually passivated by hydroxides, oxides, and/or carbonates, which kinetically inhibit reactions
at low temperatures. These passivation layers naturally cause impurities in chemical syntheses.
Different procedures have been applied to produce rare earth metals, such as (a) reduction of
anhydrous rare earth chlorides or fluorides. (b) reduction of rare earth oxide, and (c) fused salt
electrolysis of rare earth chlorides or oxide fluoride mixtures. Rare earth oxides are highly stable,
and their metal reduction is very difficult. The fluoride reduction process and the lanthnothermic
direct reduction process are technically perfect reduction processes for rare earth metal

 10

preparation. However, these processes are expensive due to the high cost of chemicals, expensive
reactors, and the batch nature of the process.

 There are several advantages to applying hydrogen as a reductant. and fuel in metals
production. Hydrogen is renewable and does not produce harmful emissions in metal production.
From a thermodynamic perspective, there are several metals (e.g., W, Mo, Ni, Co, Cu, Pb, Zn, and
others) that can be generated through hydrogen reduction of their oxides, however, only limited
commercial metals production utilizing hydrogen as a reductant currently exist, i.e., only for the
production of selected refractory metal powders (W, Mo), nickel, cobalt, and iron. Hydrogen is a
weaker reductant than carbon at high temperatures, and the endothermic nature of hydrogen
reduction of oxides means that the whole energy balance in existing reactors needs to be revisited.

 Beyond the multiple advantages of microwave-assisted organic synthesis in batch-type
reactors, a severe limitation is a difficulty of scaling up this technology to a production scale level
since the benefits achieved on a small scale are lost. The restricted penetration depth of microwave
radiation into absorbing materials is another limitation; due to the apparent limitations of large-
scale batch microwave processing, recent efforts have also focused on performing microwave
chemistry under continuous flow conditions. or using single-mode or multimode microwave
instruments [8]. Some advantages of this are shown below:

6 Background
 Hydrogen plasma (H-plasma) is commonly generated based on H2 feedstock. High-energy
electrons strike inelastically with hydrogen molecules in the plasma and transfer their energy to
the latter, which produces excited hydrogen species and ions ((H, H+, H2+, H3+) with a significantly
higher reduction ability than molecular hydrogen [1]. Gibbs' free energy calculations confirm that
monoatomic hydrogen (H) can readily reduce many oxides compared to molecular hydrogen due
to the elimination of the dissociation step in the process. H-plasma can act as a reducing agent for
oxides. Several metal oxides, like ZnO and TiO2, deliver strong M-O bonds [2]. Therefore, the as-
obtained O-H groups cannot be reduced further into H2O, and H-species will be binding on their
surface. Such an H-abundant layer also leads to the passivation of the material surface [3]. This
phenomenon is known as hydrogenation. When exposing indium tin oxide (ITO), F-doped tin
oxide (FTO), and indium zinc oxide (IZO) films into H-plasma, both ITO and FTO were reduced
to corresponding metal, while IZO was not reduced to elemental Zn [4]. Similarly, with H-plasma
treatment, the surface of TiO2 can only be partially reduced to Ti 3+, along with the abundance of
O-H groups on its surface.

 The oxides of rare earths such as samarium, cerium, and neodymium have many important
applications, including high-efficiency phosphors and catalysts. They show good catalytic
properties in several reactions, including ammonia synthesis and methane oxidative coupling [5].
The practical metallurgical methods employed in the commercial production of rare earth metals
include metallothermic reduction and electrowinning. Metallothermic reduction involves complex
thermochemical techniques utilizing energy-intensive reductants. This method can be applied to
produce nearly all rare earth metals. Herein, the reduction of Nd2O3 by hydrogen plasma will be
studied. Plasma utilizations are becoming mainly applied in metallurgical processes, including
metal extraction. This technique may have advantages, such as being environment-friendly and
not generating secondary waste.

 Thermodynamics and kinetics are potential paths of the production process. The reaction
of interest is the reduction of metal oxide (MO) by hydrogen plasma. Hydrogen gas is supplied in
molecular form to the site in the plasma where metal oxide reduction takes place. Plasma changes
molecular hydrogen gas into atomic, ionic, or vibration-activated forms and other excited forms.

7 Results and Discussion
7-1 Material preparation, establishment of microwave plasma setup, evaluation of the
factors affecting REOs to RE-metals conversion, and producing 99.5%-Purity RE-metals.
 University of Wyoming (UW) has completed the microwave plasma based REOs to RE-
metals setup. The experimental system shown in Fig. 3 consists of a flow control system, a
microwave plasma delivery system, and an outlet gas analyzer with a data acquisition unit.
Reduction of REOs starts with introducing N2 flow (1), which is measured by a mass flow meter
(4 and 4’). The MKS AX2600 series microwave plasma system can be divided into two sections:
the plasma generator and the microwave generator. Typically, plasma performance is inferred by
measuring the applied pulse voltage and current. When the plasma generator starts, the high-power
supply charges the pulse to form capacitors (7). The capacitors discharge rapidly into the reactor
path through a thyratron, giving the short voltage-rise time required. The wave signals can be
displayed on the screen of the frequency generator (10). Basically, two types of current pulses can
be derived from the waveform. In detail, the first pulse occurs during the rising part of the voltage
pulse, representing the current through the reactor caused by the charge released by the capacitors;
the second current pulse occurring during the falling part of the voltage pulse is due to the presence
of electrons and ions, indicating the formation of intense plasma. Pulsing of the voltage and current
allows energy to be deposited in the gas at a high density. The area under the product of the pulsed
voltage and discharge current, therefore, represents the input electrical energy into the reactor. The
gas then enters the microwave reactor chamber, where the proposed reduction of REOs is
continuously carried out. The pressure within the reactor is maintained at 1-8 torr using a pressure
controller (6, 6’) and a dry vacuum pump (15). A back-pressure regulator (13) is employed at the
outlet, assuring gas pressure consistency throughout the process. The gaseous products and
unreacted ingredients are then monitored online using an outlet gas analyzer (16). A data
acquisition unit (17) is connected to the outlet gas analyzer to collect, calculate, and display data
results.

 Fig. 4 depicts the XRD analysis for CeO2 samples before and after N2 plasma at different
times and at the same voltage (5V). The diffraction peaks at 2θ around 28.5, 33.1, 47.6, 56.1, 59.4,
69.7, 76.8, 79.1, and 88.70 can be ascribed to the (111), (200), (220), (311), (222), (440), (331),
(420), and (422) crystalline planes of the cubic fluorite structure of the pure CeO2 (JCPDS no. 04-
0593) [5]. The XRD peaks of CeO2 treated with N2 plasma for 1, 2, 3, and 5 hours at 7V also match
well with pure CeO2. However, for the CeO2 treated with nitrogen plasma for 8 hours, an additional
peak at 2θ around 30.98 can be related to the (111) crystalline plane of cerium nitride. This
indicates that treatment of CeO2 with N2 plasma results in nitrogen doping (Firstly when the
nitrogen concentration is small. This doped nitrogen is interstitial or adsorbed nitrogen. With the
increase in nitrogen content, the substitutional doped nitrogen is formed.).

 To understand the change of the oxidation state of CeO2 samples prior to and after N2 and
H2 treatments, XPS analysis was carefully performed by prolonging the integration time. The
primary C 1s core level was employed to correct the binding energies. XPS is highly accurate for
detecting the low concentrations of the measured elements. For CeO2, the XPS spectra of Ce3d
gave six signals with binding energies of 882.6 eV, 888.4 eV, 898.6 eV, 901.3 eV, 907.4 eV, and
916.5 eV (Fig. 5). All these peaks can be attributed to Ce (IV). In CeO2, the peaks at 901.3 eV,
907.4 eV, and 916.5 eV are assigned to 3d3/2. The peaks at 882.6 eV, 888.4 eV, and 898.6 eV can
be ascribed to 3d5/2.

7-2-3 Metastable Phase Analysis
 To better understand the predicted reduction process, a more holistic approach was
emphasized for the remainder of the project. Two perspectives dominated our understanding:
 Simplified Model: A hydrogen atom collides with neodymium oxide, leading to
 either an Indian or oxidative removal. The subsequent step involves
 recombination.
 Realistic Model: This model offers a more nuanced picture. Within plasma,
 there's a series of dynamic interactions: ionic impacts, hydrogen implantation,
 secondary electron emission, material recycling, long-range material transport,
 film growth, bubble formation, etc. A comparative study with NASA's research
 on plasma erosion prevention on rocket surfaces offered a potential strategy:
 enhancing erosion effects for increased reaction efficiency.
 The realistic situation is depicted in Fig. 27.

 It became clear that the reaction could vary substantially between different pressures, as
plasma temperature is defined for high temperatures due to the increased rate at which particles
collide. Additionally, plasmas are known to travel in the E x B direction for externally applied
electronic (E) and magnetic (B) fields. By increasing pressure local to the exposed material,
plasma oxide reaction speed is expected to increase substantially, while local products such as
unionized H2O are free to diffuse into the remainder of the reaction chamber, limiting
recombination rates, which would halt Nd production.
 Following some discussion and referring to Fig. 28, we considered the reaction sequence:
Nd2O3, NdO, Nd2O, Nd in which metastable products form a reaction sequence during the
surface converting to a sequence of sub-oxides. This contrasts our previous treatments, where we
emphasized the direct decomposition of Nd2O3 via hydrogen plasma.
 Specifically, we investigated NdO, a metastable phase of neodymium oxide. A
preliminary study suggests that NdO can emerge as an intermediate product during certain
reactions, particularly during the reaction between Indio and ionic hydrogen to yield neodymium

 As the project end neared, radical changes to the experimental methodology become

 1. Predicting experimental yield based on reactor parameters such as power
 input, plasma rate, and pressure. This is a simple analysis that would take many runs of

 2. Directly forecasting spectroscopy data from reactor parameters. This is
 substantially enhanced by enhancing the model with additional data from external

 The primary model considered was the direct spectroscopy data prediction, which would
be most effective implemented as a conditional generative adversarial neural network (GAN)

8-3 Technology transfer and outreach (Commercialization)
8-3-1 Scaling batch process to continuous flow
 Unfortunately, using traditional batching, microwave chemistry is difficult to scale to
larger volumes, mainly due to penetration depth issues: volumetric heating is genuinely effective
only on a comparatively small scale. Although impressive progress has been made in recent
years in translating small-scale microwave chemistry from the lab (ml) scale to a larger "kilolab"
scale format using dedicated batch microwave instrumentation, it is obvious that this technology
will not be able to generate industrially relevant quantities of product (> 100 t/year) in an
economically viable way.
 Using single mode or multimode microwave instruments, several successful examples of
microwave-assisted continuous flow synthesis on a g or kg scale using various formats have been

8-3-3 Multipolar plasmas for uniform processing physics, design and performance

 Numerous mechanisms can be used to generate hydrogen plasma; however, the focus of
this study is on microwave-driven plasmas, specifically using an electron cyclotron resonance
(ECR) source. Recent developments include compact source designs with a static magnetic field;
this provides high power efficiency and, more importantly, the ability to scale the plasma using
array configurations of individual plasma sources (Sigeneger, Ellis, Harhausen, Lang, & H van
Helden, 2022) [17]

 The plasma is excited by a commercial microwave ECR source, this source consists of a
solid-state microwave generator and a compact microwave launcher with coaxial geometry
allows up to 200 W of microwave power to be coupled into a plasma in a pressure range of 3 to
10 Pa. The excitation frequency is variable in the 2.4 to 2.5 GHz range, which guarantees a
minimized reflected microwave power for a given operating condition.

 Since it seems difficult or impossible to produce uniform plasmas in the square meter
range using an array of linear plasma sources, an alternative solution is to extend the distribution
concept applied with DECR sources to the end and produce these uniform plasmas. of two-
dimensional networks of elemental sources. This objective can be achieved with multidipolar
plasmas whose principles are described below: (Lacoste, Lagarde, Béchu, Arnal, & Pelletier,
2002) [18]

 • Uniform low-pressure plasmas are produced from a network of independent
 plasma elemental sources held at ECR.

 • This concept requires the distribution of microwave power over the elementary
 sources of plasma arranged in the network. These plasma sources can be powered with
 microwaves, either from as many independently controlled microwave supplies, or from as
 many independent microwave circuits after division of the microwave power delivered by a
 single high power microwave supply. Each microwave power transmission line includes a
 circulator with its corresponding load to absorb the reflected energy, a tuner to match the
 impedance, a matched vacuum feed, and a coaxial line.

 • In a single elementary source, the plasma is produced by fast electrons, trapped in
 the field of a magnetic dipole, e.g. a permanent magnet, acting as a magnetron-like
 configuration, and accelerated at ECR by microwaves.

 49

 Once the principles of multidipolar plasmas have been established, the difficulty lies,
firstly, in realizing an elementary plasma source and, secondly, in assembling many of these
plasma sources according to a two-dimensional network.

10 Conclusions
 XRD demonstrates that the long-term treatment of H2 plasma changes the crystalline
structure. This change indicates the formation of other crystalline structures that can be related to
different intermediates. Some of these peaks which have values close to (28.4, 29.4, 30.5, 32.3,
36.6, 42.1, 48.9, 50.08, 55.1, 59.05, 59.64, 60.68, 62.5, 63.25) may be related to elemental Nd.
Also, it can be related to other lower oxidation states of Nd2O3 like (Nd2O4, Nd2O2, NdO2, NdO).
(According to data from the Rigaku database). Thermal gravimetric analysis and the analysis of
adsorption-desorption isotherms reveal that treatment with hydrogen plasma has no influence on
thermal stability and surface area.

 This study has made substantial advancements in the theoretical modeling of plasma
reactions. These advancements include the conceptualization of the plasma reaction as a
thermodynamic process, with two distinct approaches: one considering it as a static process,
focusing solely on temperature, and the other as a dynamic process, incorporating changes in
temperature over time. Furthermore, a more comprehensive model was developed, providing a

 53

holistic treatment that integrates all relevant elements of the process. This approach was
complemented by the application of machine learning techniques, offering novel insights into
plasma reaction dynamics. However, it is important to note a limitation encountered in the
experimental phase. The plasma reactor used in this study did not achieve the temperatures
predicted as necessary for initiating the reduction of neodymium oxide. Despite this, the theoretical
models developed remain valid and provide a strong foundation for future research. These models
not only retain their theoretical integrity but also suggest a promising pathway for the plasma
reduction of rare earth elements, specifically through the adjustment and optimization of reaction
conditions to achieve the requisite temperatures. With further validation, these techniques could
provide a scalable solution to meet the rising global demand for vital rare earth materials.

 The key to scaling an established microwave plasma system is to upgrade the microwave
plasma generator, depending on resources, which can be upgraded in two ways:

 • couple multiple microwave generators to a single reactor chamber

 • develop single unit microwave sources with an output power of > 100 kW
 at lower frequencies.

 For material processing, particularly for the reduction of metal oxides, it is important to
analyze the kinetics and thermodynamics of the reaction that is generated, depending on the
compound that is being treated, by-products can be generated that intervene in the efficiency of
the process, the same occurs when the working gas is ionized, active species are generated that
may or may not contribute to the reaction. It has been shown that the effect of pressure and
energy can produce variation in the active species and therefore affect the reaction. Interestingly,
the working pressures in the plasma microwave at "low pressures" exhibit values less than 1 torr,
so it would be necessary to evaluate these two parameters by reducing the pressure as much as
possible (<1 torr) or, if necessary, increase the energy supplied. The flow of hydrogen is also
necessary to be studied based on constant energies.

 Although the aforementioned works show a great efficiency of continuous flow
microwave reactors, the scalability of a Batch process to maintain optimal conditions achieved,
these processes are applied mainly to organic synthesis processes, many of the investigations
found are related to the treatment of this type of material, on the other hand, although it works at
high pressure and relatively low temperatures, this could be used for the reduction of metal

oxides, however the reaction that is intended to be generated is a plasma-solid reaction, which
does not occur in this type of process, it would be interesting to find a solvent that can be used as
a reducing medium for metal oxides and in this way take advantage of this technique and apply it
to inorganic reduction reactions, however no work has been found that have conducted this type
of study.

 It can be concluded that this novel method of implementing units configured with
multiple sources can achieve uniformity and high levels of plasma density since according to
(Grotjohn, Liske, Hassouni, & Asmussen, 2005) the scaling of the microwave power as the

method called Aura wave it can be achieved high powers and likewise plasma uniformity to treat
