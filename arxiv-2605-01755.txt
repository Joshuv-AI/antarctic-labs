From the Volterra type Lyapunov functions of Rahman-Zou towards a competitive exclusion partition property for rank one models
Rim Adenane, Florin Avram, Andrei-Dan Halanay

Abstract
Fall, Iggidr, Sallet, and Bonzi—see also Earn and McCluskey (2025) and [ 5 ] —have studied a class of bilinear epidemic models with rank-one next-generation matrix (NGM), in which the right Perron eigenvector yields an explicit endemic equilibrium (EE), while the left Perron eigenvector provides Lyapunov functions at both the disease-free equilibrium (DFE) and the EE. Together with the graph-theoretic method of Shuai and van den Driessche (2013), these results establish strong threshold theorems: when the DFE loses stability, a unique EE emerges and becomes globally asymptotically stable, with the transition certified by an explicit global Lyapunov function. A striking extension was obtained by [ 17 ] , who proved analogous results for a two-strain rational rates (saturating) model with multiple boundary equilibria. In this setting, stability is relayed across equilibria with increasing infection levels, inducing a partition of parameter space into regions where exactly one equilibrium is certified to be globally stable by an explicit Lyapunov function. We refer to this as the competitive exclusion partition property (CEPP). We show that these results are governed by a common Perron–Volterra mechanism: the Lyapunov functions are sums of a Volterra entropy on resident variables and a linear functional on invaders, weighted by left Perron eigenvectors of transversal Jacobians. A balance identity eliminates higher-order coupling terms, reducing stability certification to the recursive evaluation of invasion numbers on the siphon lattice. For a two-strain model with concave increasing incidence, we prove the CEPP completely, providing explicit global Lyapunov functions at all four equilibria (Theorem 4 ). For n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 strains, the same mechanism works, as long as all the strains involve only one compartment. Beyond that, we also obtain global Lyapunov functions at all equilibria for models with one irreducible rank one strain, and one (or more) scalar strains in Theorem 5 ; whether further extensions are possible remains an open problem. We have provided an algorithmic implementation in the Mathematica package EpidCRN ( https://github.com/florinav/EpidCRNmodels ). The method organizes the dynamics via the minimal siphon lattice, recursively computes Perron eigenvectors of transversal Jacobians, and constructs candidate Lyapunov functions for all equilibria, producing a parameter partition into regions with a unique locally stable equilibrium and, for n = 2 n=2 , explicit global stability certificates.

Why CRN methods.

The first characteristic of most models in mathematical epidemiology (RS),
ecology, and immuno-virology is that they are positive ODEs (or stochastic models
whose mean field is a positive ODE). This means that important phenomena occur
when trajectories reach the boundary of the positive orthant, and that the notion
of invariant boundary faces is crucial. While boundary faces have been studied in
classical ODE theory, the systematic exploitation of simplifications arising for
positive ODEs was initiated by CRN works, see notably
[

Beyond the polynomial case, but assuming a stoichiometric representation, crucial

transversal Jacobians on siphons have the Metzler/cooperativity property. This property further ensures the existence of
left and right Perron eigenvectors which, in all cases we have studied, provide Lyapunov

1.1
Short review of some Volterra type Lyapunov function results in ME and CRN

Since Volterra, Lyapunov functions for positive ODEs
z^{\prime}=f(z)
have been often constructed
using the Bregman divergence:

D_{i}(z_{i}):=z_{i}-z_{i}^{*}-z_{i}^{*}\,\ln\frac{z_{i}}{z_{i}^{*}}=z_{i}^{*}G(u_{i})\geq 0,\qquad u_{i}:=\frac{z_{i}}{z_{i}^{*}},G(u)=u-1-ln(u)

associated with
x\mapsto x\ln x
.
“Candidate Lyapunov functions” include often terms of the form:

\mathcal{L}(z):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}D_{i}(z_{i}),\qquad l_{i}>0.

(1)

Note that
D_{i}^{\prime}=(1-u_{i}^{-1})z_{i}^{\prime}
yields the explicit formula:

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}\!\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{u_{i}}\Bigr)f_{i}(z).

(2)

Unconditional GAS under complex balance and strong endotacticity in chemical reaction network.

Classical CRNT
[
14
]
proves
unconditional
global asymptotic stability (GAS) of complex-balanced equilibria within their stoichiometric compatibility class,
for arbitrary positive rate constants
, by means of the Volterra–Horn–Jackson Lyapunov function

\mathcal{L}(z)=\sum_{i}\bigl(z_{i}\ln(z_{i}/z_{i}^{*})-z_{i}+z_{i}^{*}\bigr)

(see also
[
1
]
for an extension of weakly reversible, single
linkage mass-action ODEs,
and see
[
11
]

for an extension to strong endotactic networks).
Note here the Lyapunov argument is purely structural: once complex balancedness or strong endotacticity hold, the construction of the Lyapunov function does not require any computation, beyond that of a positive steady state.

Conditional GAS in mathematical epidemiology models.

In

The first method, for proving the global stability of the disease-free equilibrium (DFE), used a Lyapunov function which combined a Volterra function in the variable(s) which are unconditionally positive, and a linear function in the “infection/siphon/possibly zero” variables

[

Below, we extend the results of
[

consequently, a competitive exclusion partition of the parameter space.

Two-strain scalar models with increasing and concave incidence

Consider the two-strain ODE

\begin{cases}s^{\prime}=\Lambda-\mu s-\beta_{1}sf_{1}(i_{1})-\beta_{2}sf_{2}(i_{2}),\\[2.84526pt]
i_{1}^{\prime}=\beta_{1}sf_{1}(i_{1})-v_{1}i_{1},\\[2.84526pt]
i_{2}^{\prime}=\beta_{2}sf_{2}(i_{2})-v_{2}i_{2},\end{cases}\qquad v_{j}>0.

(3)

We assume

\begin{cases}\text{\emph{(A1)}}&f_{j}\in C^{1}([0,\infty)),\quad f_{j}(0)=0,\quad f_{j}^{\prime}(0)=1,\quad x>0\Rightarrow f_{j}(x)>0,\qquad j=1,2,\\[2.84526pt]
\text{\emph{(A2)}}&\mu\leq\min\{v_{1},v_{2}\},\\[2.84526pt]
\text{\emph{(A3)}}&f_{j}(i)\leq i,\qquad i\geq 0,\quad j=1,2,\\[2.84526pt]
\text{\emph{(A4)}}&f_{j}\text{ is increasing and concave on }\mathbb{R}_{+}.\end{cases}

When strictness of equality sets is needed below, we assume that the relevant
incidence function is strictly concave away from the origin; otherwise the
LaSalle argument below still gives convergence because the
s
-equation
removes the possible equality rays.

Lemma 1

(a forward invariant compact set)
.

The region

\Gamma=\{(s,i_{1},i_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}:\ s+i_{1}+i_{2}\leq\Lambda/\mu\}

is forward invariant.

Proof.

The positive orthant is forward invariant. Moreover,

(s+i_{1}+i_{2})^{\prime}=\Lambda-\mu s-v_{1}i_{1}-v_{2}i_{2}\leq\Lambda-\mu(s+i_{1}+i_{2}),

by
(A2)
. Hence
s+i_{1}+i_{2}\leq\Lambda/\mu
is preserved.
∎

(A2) could be weakened to the non-explicit condition of existence of a compact invariant set.

Lemma 2

(Normalization preserves monotonicity and concavity)
.

For every
\bar{\imath}>0
, define

F_{j,\bar{\imath}}(z):=\frac{f_{j}(\bar{\imath}z)}{f_{j}(\bar{\imath})}.

Then
F_{j,\bar{\imath}}
is increasing and concave on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Moreover

g_{j}(i):=\frac{f_{j}(i)}{i},\qquad g_{j}(0):=f_{j}^{\prime}(0)=1,

is continuous and nonincreasing on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. If
f_{j}
is strictly concave, then

g_{j}
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

The first claim follows by affine reparametrization and multiplication by the
positive constant
1/f_{j}(\bar{\imath})
.

For
0<i<j
, concavity and
f_{j}(0)=0
give

f_{j}(i)=f_{j}\!\left(\frac{i}{j}j+\left(1-\frac{i}{j}\right)0\right)\geq\frac{i}{j}f_{j}(j).

Hence

\frac{f_{j}(i)}{i}\geq\frac{f_{j}(j)}{j}.

The limit at zero is
f_{j}^{\prime}(0)=1
, giving continuity at zero. Strict concavity
gives strict monotonicity.
∎

Equilibria and reproduction functions.

The DFE is

P_{0}=(s_{0},0,0),\qquad s_{0}=\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}.

The DFE next-generation matrix is diagonal:

K(P_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}R_{1}&0\\
0&R_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}s_{0}{\mathcal{R}}_{1}&0\\
0&s_{0}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\mathcal{R}}_{j}:=\frac{\beta_{j}}{v_{j}}.

(4)

We write

R_{j}(s):=s{\mathcal{R}}_{j}=\frac{\beta_{j}s}{v_{j}},\qquad R_{j}:=R_{j}(s_{0}).

If
P_{1}=(s_{1},i_{1}^{*},0)
and
P_{2}=(s_{2},0,i_{2}^{*})
exist, the invasion numbers are

R_{2}^{P_{1}}:=R_{2}(s_{1})=\frac{\beta_{2}s_{1}}{v_{2}},\qquad R_{1}^{P_{2}}:=R_{1}(s_{2})=\frac{\beta_{1}s_{2}}{v_{1}}.

(5)

Transversal Jacobians.

At the boundary equilibria,

J^{\perp}(P_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}(R_{1}-1)&0\\
0&v_{2}(R_{2}-1)\end{pmatrix},

J^{\perp}(P_{1})=v_{2}(R_{2}^{P_{1}}-1),\qquad J^{\perp}(P_{2})=v_{1}(R_{1}^{P_{2}}-1).

Thus the sign of each transversal eigenvalue is the sign of the corresponding
invasion number minus one.

(Canonical example: Michaelis–Menten incidence)

The Michaelis–Menten choice

satisfies

Theorem 2

(Existence of
P_{1},P_{2}
)
.

Under

, the strain–

P_{1}=(s_{1},i_{1}^{*},0),\qquad i_{1}^{*}>0,

Proof.

At a boundary equilibrium

\beta_{1}s\,g_{1}(i)=v_{1},\qquad\mu s+v_{1}i=\Lambda.

Thus

The first factor is strictly decreasing and

is nonincreasing, hence

Moreover

\phi(0)=\beta_{1}s_{0}-v_{1}=v_{1}(R_{1}-1),\qquad\phi(\Lambda/v_{1})=-v_{1}<0.

Therefore a positive solution exists iff

∎

Remark 3

(IVT + monotonicity template)

The proof uses a recurring pattern: reduce the equilibrium equations to a

Lemma 3

(Invasion numbers are below the corresponding basic numbers)

If

R_{2}^{P_{1}}<R_{2}.

Hence

Since

Assume

If

F(s^{*})=0.

Setting

i_{j}^{*}:=i_{j}(s^{*}),\qquad j=1,2,

gives the unique coexistence equilibrium.

It remains to mention the mixed case. Suppose, for instance, that

g_{1}\equiv 1
and
g_{2}
is strictly decreasing. Then coexistence forces

s^{*}=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{R}}_{1}}.

The condition
R_{1}^{P_{2}}>1
gives

s_{2}>\frac{1}{{\mathcal{R}}_{1}}=s^{*},

so the equation for strain
2
determines a unique positive
i_{2}^{*}
. Then
the balance equation

\Lambda-\mu s^{*}-v_{2}i_{2}^{*}=v_{1}i_{1}^{*}

determines a unique positive
i_{1}^{*}
. The other mixed case is symmetric.
Thus sufficiency and uniqueness also hold when one strain is linear.
∎

2.3
GAS-CEP extension of Rahman-Zou with concave
incidence

Lemma 4

(Entropy bracket)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be increasing and concave, with

F(0)=0,\qquad F(1)=1.

Then, for all
x,y>0
,

B(x,y):=G\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)+G\!\left(\frac{xF(y)}{y}\right)-G(F(y))+G(y)\geq 0,

where
G(u)=u-1-\ln u
. Moreover,

B(x,y)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=1,\qquad F(y)=y.

Proof.

Write

B(x,y)=G\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)+G\!\left(\frac{xF(y)}{y}\right)+\bigl(G(y)-G(F(y))\bigr).

The first two terms are nonnegative since
G\geq 0
. The last term is
nonnegative because concavity and normalization give

G(F(y))\leq G(y).

Thus
B(x,y)\geq 0
.

If
B(x,y)=0
, each of the three nonnegative terms must vanish, so

x=1,\qquad\frac{xF(y)}{y}=1,

hence
F(y)=y
. The converse is immediate.
∎

Theorem 4

(GAS–CEP extension of Rahman–Zou
[
17
]
with concave incidence (t:GAS))
.

Assume that for each resident strain at the target equilibrium, either

\text{(a) }f_{j}\text{ is strictly concave on }(0,\infty),\quad\text{or}\quad\text{(b) }f_{j}(i)=i.

(both cases ensure:

F_{j}(y)=y\;\Longrightarrow\;y=1,

which is needed below.
Put

G(u)=u-1-\ln u\geq 0.

Under assumptions
(A1)–(A4)
, define

V_{0}=s_{0}G\!\left(\frac{s}{s_{0}}\right)+i_{1}+i_{2},

V_{1}=s_{1}G\!\left(\frac{s}{s_{1}}\right)+i_{1}^{*}G\!\left(\frac{i_{1}}{i_{1}^{*}}\right)+i_{2},

V_{2}=s_{2}G\!\left(\frac{s}{s_{2}}\right)+i_{2}^{*}G\!\left(\frac{i_{2}}{i_{2}^{*}}\right)+i_{1},

and

V_{*}=s^{*}G\!\left(\frac{s}{s^{*}}\right)+i_{1}^{*}G\!\left(\frac{i_{1}}{i_{1}^{*}}\right)+i_{2}^{*}G\!\left(\frac{i_{2}}{i_{2}^{*}}\right).

Then the following hold.

(i)

If
R_{0}:=\max\{R_{1},R_{2}\}<1
, then
P_{0}
is globally
asymptotically stable on
\Gamma
.

(ii)

If
R_{1}>1
and
R_{2}^{P_{1}}<1
, then
P_{1}
is globally
asymptotically stable on
\Gamma
.

(iii)

If
R_{2}>1
and
R_{1}^{P_{2}}<1
, then
P_{2}
is globally
asymptotically stable on
\Gamma
.

(iv)

If
R_{2}^{P_{1}}>1
and
R_{1}^{P_{2}}>1
, then
P_{*}
is globally
asymptotically stable for all interior initial conditions.

Consequently, away from the nonhyperbolic threshold surfaces, the four open
CEP regions

\{R_{1}<1,R_{2}<1\},\qquad\{R_{1}>1,R_{2}^{P_{1}}<1\},\qquad\{R_{2}>1,R_{1}^{P_{2}}<1\},\qquad\{R_{2}^{P_{1}}>1,R_{1}^{P_{2}}>1\}

have global attractors
P_{0},P_{1},P_{2},P_{*}
, respectively.

Proof.

The proof is case-by-case. The point is that the resident terms give exact
entropy brackets, while invader terms are estimated using

0\leq f_{j}(i_{j})\leq i_{j}.

No exact identity of the form

\beta_{j}s_{E}f_{j}(i_{j})-v_{j}i_{j}=v_{j}(R_{j}(E)-1)i_{j}

is used unless
f_{j}(i_{j})=i_{j}
.

1. The DFE
P_{0}
.

Let

P_{0}=(s_{0},0,0),\qquad s_{0}=\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}.

Then

\dot{V}_{0}=\left(1-\frac{s_{0}}{s}\right)s^{\prime}+i_{1}^{\prime}+i_{2}^{\prime}.

Substituting the equations gives

\dot{V}_{0}=-\mu\frac{(s-s_{0})^{2}}{s}+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\bigl(\beta_{j}s_{0}f_{j}(i_{j})-v_{j}i_{j}\bigr).

Since

R_{j}=\frac{\beta_{j}s_{0}}{v_{j}}

and
f_{j}(i_{j})\leq i_{j}
,

\beta_{j}s_{0}f_{j}(i_{j})-v_{j}i_{j}=v_{j}\bigl(R_{j}f_{j}(i_{j})-i_{j}\bigr)\leq v_{j}(R_{j}-1)i_{j}.

Thus, if
R_{0}<1
,

\dot{V}_{0}\leq-\mu\frac{(s-s_{0})^{2}}{s}+\sum_{j=1}^{2}v_{j}(R_{j}-1)i_{j}\leq 0.

Equality implies
s=s_{0}
and
i_{1}=i_{2}=0
. Hence the largest invariant set in

\{\dot{V}_{0}=0\}
is
\{P_{0}\}
. LaSalle’s invariance principle gives GAS of

P_{0}
.

2. The boundary equilibrium
P_{1}
.

Let

P_{1}=(s_{1},i_{1}^{*},0).

Use the equilibrium identities

\Lambda=\mu s_{1}+v_{1}i_{1}^{*},\qquad v_{1}i_{1}^{*}=\beta_{1}s_{1}f_{1}(i_{1}^{*}).

Set

x=\frac{s}{s_{1}},\qquad y=\frac{i_{1}}{i_{1}^{*}},\qquad F(y)=\frac{f_{1}(i_{1}^{*}y)}{f_{1}(i_{1}^{*})}.

Then

\dot{V}_{1}=\left(1-\frac{1}{x}\right)s^{\prime}+\left(1-\frac{1}{y}\right)i_{1}^{\prime}+i_{2}^{\prime}.

A direct calculation gives

\dot{V}_{1}=-\mu\frac{(s-s_{1})^{2}}{s}-\beta_{1}s_{1}f_{1}(i_{1}^{*})\,B(x,y)+\beta_{2}s_{1}f_{2}(i_{2})-v_{2}i_{2},

where

B(x,y)=G\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)+G\!\left(\frac{xF(y)}{y}\right)-G(F(y))+G(y).

By Lemma
4
,

B(x,y)\geq 0.

The invader term is estimated, not identified:

\beta_{2}s_{1}f_{2}(i_{2})-v_{2}i_{2}=v_{2}\bigl(R_{2}^{P_{1}}f_{2}(i_{2})-i_{2}\bigr)\leq v_{2}(R_{2}^{P_{1}}-1)i_{2}.

Therefore, if
R_{2}^{P_{1}}<1
,

\dot{V}_{1}\leq-\mu\frac{(s-s_{1})^{2}}{s}-\beta_{1}s_{1}f_{1}(i_{1}^{*})B(x,y)+v_{2}(R_{2}^{P_{1}}-1)i_{2}\leq 0.

We identify the equality set. Equality implies

s=s_{1},\qquad i_{2}=0,\qquad B(x,y)=0.

Since
x=1
, Lemma
4
gives

F(y)=y.

On this set, the
s
-equation gives

0=s^{\prime}=\Lambda-\mu s_{1}-\beta_{1}s_{1}f_{1}(i_{1})=v_{1}i_{1}^{*}-\beta_{1}s_{1}f_{1}(i_{1}).

Using

v_{1}i_{1}^{*}=\beta_{1}s_{1}f_{1}(i_{1}^{*}),

we obtain

f_{1}(i_{1})=f_{1}(i_{1}^{*}).

Since
f_{1}
is strictly increasing,
i_{1}=i_{1}^{*}
. Thus the largest invariant
subset of
\{\dot{V}_{1}=0\}
is
\{P_{1}\}
, and LaSalle gives GAS of
P_{1}
.

3. The boundary equilibrium
P_{2}
.

The proof is symmetric. At

P_{2}=(s_{2},0,i_{2}^{*}),

set

x=\frac{s}{s_{2}},\qquad y=\frac{i_{2}}{i_{2}^{*}},\qquad F(y)=\frac{f_{2}(i_{2}^{*}y)}{f_{2}(i_{2}^{*})}.

Then

\dot{V}_{2}=-\mu\frac{(s-s_{2})^{2}}{s}-\beta_{2}s_{2}f_{2}(i_{2}^{*})\,B(x,y)+\beta_{1}s_{2}f_{1}(i_{1})-v_{1}i_{1},

with
B(x,y)\geq 0
. Moreover,

\beta_{1}s_{2}f_{1}(i_{1})-v_{1}i_{1}\leq v_{1}(R_{1}^{P_{2}}-1)i_{1}.

Thus
R_{1}^{P_{2}}<1
gives
\dot{V}_{2}\leq 0
. Equality forces

s=s_{2},\qquad i_{1}=0,\qquad i_{2}=i_{2}^{*}.

Therefore the largest invariant subset of
\{\dot{V}_{2}=0\}
is
\{P_{2}\}
,
and LaSalle gives GAS of
P_{2}
.

4. The coexistence equilibrium
P_{*}
.

Let

P_{*}=(s^{*},i_{1}^{*},i_{2}^{*}).

Use

\Lambda=\mu s^{*}+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\beta_{j}s^{*}f_{j}(i_{j}^{*}),\qquad v_{j}i_{j}^{*}=\beta_{j}s^{*}f_{j}(i_{j}^{*}).

Set

x=\frac{s}{s^{*}},\qquad y_{j}=\frac{i_{j}}{i_{j}^{*}},\qquad F_{j}(y_{j})=\frac{f_{j}(i_{j}^{*}y_{j})}{f_{j}(i_{j}^{*})}.

Then

\dot{V}_{*}=-\mu\frac{(s-s^{*})^{2}}{s}-\sum_{j=1}^{2}\beta_{j}s^{*}f_{j}(i_{j}^{*})\,B_{j}(x,y_{j}),

where

B_{j}(x,y_{j})=G\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)+G\!\left(\frac{xF_{j}(y_{j})}{y_{j}}\right)-G(F_{j}(y_{j}))+G(y_{j}).

Again
B_{j}(x,y_{j})\geq 0
, hence

\dot{V}_{*}\leq 0.

We identify the equality set. Equality implies

s=s^{*},\qquad F_{j}(y_{j})=y_{j},\qquad j=1,2.

On the equality set, invariance requires
i_{j}^{\prime}=0
for
j=1,2
. Since

s=s^{*}
,

0=i_{j}^{\prime}=\beta_{j}s^{*}f_{j}(i_{j})-v_{j}i_{j}.

Using the equilibrium identity

v_{j}i_{j}^{*}=\beta_{j}s^{*}f_{j}(i_{j}^{*}),

we obtain

\frac{f_{j}(i_{j})}{i_{j}}=\frac{f_{j}(i_{j}^{*})}{i_{j}^{*}}.

Equivalently,

g_{j}(i_{j})=g_{j}(i_{j}^{*}),\qquad g_{j}(i):=\frac{f_{j}(i)}{i}.

By the monotonicity of
g_{j}
, this implies

i_{j}=i_{j}^{*}.

Thus the largest invariant subset of
\{\dot{V}_{*}=0\}
is
\{P_{*}\}
.

When

R_{2}^{P_{1}}>1,\qquad R_{1}^{P_{2}}>1,

the boundary equilibria
P_{0},P_{1},P_{2}
are acyclic and repelling for interior
orbits in the sense of the persistence theorem. Hence interior trajectories
are uniformly persistent away from
\partial\Gamma
. Applying LaSalle on the
compact persistent interior set gives convergence to
P_{*}
for all interior
initial conditions.

Finally, the existence equivalences

P_{1}\text{ exists}\iff R_{1}>1,\qquad P_{2}\text{ exists}\iff R_{2}>1,

and

P_{*}\text{ exists}\iff R_{2}^{P_{1}}>1\text{ and }R_{1}^{P_{2}}>1

give the four CEP regions and their corresponding global attractors.
∎

Remark 4

(Threshold surfaces)
.

The theorem is stated on the open hyperbolic regions. On threshold surfaces,
for example
R_{2}^{P_{1}}=1
, the Lyapunov derivative is no longer strict in the
transversal direction. Those nonhyperbolic cases require a separate analysis.

Perron–Volterra ansatz and obstruction

At
E_{A}
, set

u=\frac{s}{s_{A}},\qquad y_{j}=\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}^{*}},\qquad\alpha_{j}^{A}=\frac{\ell_{j}w_{j}/v_{Aj}}{{\mathcal{R}}_{A}},\qquad\bar{y}_{A}=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\alpha_{j}^{A}y_{j}.

The standard ansatz

V_{A}=s_{A}G(u)+\sum_{j=1}^{2}a_{j}^{A}x_{j}^{*}G(y_{j})+c_{B}^{\top}z

(11)

has derivative

\dot{V}_{A}=T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4},

(12)

where

T_{1}=-\mu s_{A}\frac{(u-1)^{2}}{u}\leq 0,

T_{2}=\xi_{A}\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{u}\right)(1-u\bar{y}_{A})+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\alpha_{j}^{A}\left(1-\frac{1}{y_{j}}\right)(u\bar{y}_{A}-y_{j})\right]\leq 0,

T_{3}=(R_{B}(s_{A})-1)c_{B}^{\top}V_{B}z\leq 0\qquad\text{in }\Omega_{A},

and

T_{4}=s\phi_{B}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2}\frac{\alpha_{j}^{A}}{y_{j}}-1\right).

The sign of
T_{2}
follows from weighted AM–HM. The term
T_{4}
has no fixed
sign and is the obstruction.

4.4
Reduced test and numerical evidence

Set
u=1
,
y_{2}=1
, and
z_{1}=z_{2}=\tilde{z}
. Then

\dot{V}_{A}^{\rm red}(y_{1},\tilde{z})=C_{1}\left(1-\frac{1}{y_{1}}\right)+\tilde{z}\left(C_{2}\frac{1-y_{1}}{y_{1}}-C_{3}\right),

with explicit positive constants
C_{i}
. For
y_{1}\ll 1
and
\tilde{z}
large,

\dot{V}_{A}^{\rm red}>0.

Thus
V_{A}
is not a Lyapunov function.

For example, with

\Lambda=1,\quad\mu=\frac{1}{4},\quad\ell=\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right),\quad w=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right),\quad V_{A}=\diag(1,2),\quad V_{B}=\diag\left(\frac{3}{2},\frac{5}{2}\right),

one has
R_{A}>R_{B}>1
. Grid scans show
\dot{V}_{A}>0
on a large subset, while
numerical integration suggests convergence to
E_{A}
from tested initial data.
Thus the failure is in the ansatz, not necessarily in the dynamics.

4.5
Augmented candidate

Define

V_{A}^{\delta}=V_{A}+\delta\sum_{k=1}^{2}a_{k}^{B}z_{k}^{*}G(z_{k}/z_{k}^{*}),\qquad a_{k}^{B}=\frac{\ell_{k}w_{k}/v_{Bk}}{{\mathcal{R}}_{B}}.

Numerically, the choice
\delta=1
removes all observed positive values of

\dot{V}_{A}^{\delta}
. This suggests that a cross-equilibrium Lyapunov function,
using data from both
E_{A}
and
E_{B}
, is the correct object for the two-block
problem.

4.6
Conclusion and open problems

For the symmetric
2\times 2
–
2\times 2
rank-one model:

•

LAS–CEP holds by the ordering of
R_{A}
and
R_{B}
;

•

the standard Perron–Volterra ansatz fails because of
T_{4}
;

•

numerical trajectories suggest GAS may still hold;

•

the augmented candidate
V_{A}^{1}
is the next object to test analytically.

The main open problems are:

1.

prove GAS of
E_{A}
in
\Omega_{A}
, or construct a dynamical counterexample;

2.

prove or disprove
\dot{V}_{A}^{1}\leq 0
globally;

3.

extend the analysis to arbitrary
n_{A},n_{B}\geq 2
;

4.

identify the algebraic condition replacing scalar cancellation in the
multi-block case.

5
Multi-strain algorithmic implementation on the siphon lattice

5.1
Perron–Volterra Lyapunov structure

This section formulates the common structure behind the explicit Lyapunov
functions used in the proved cases of this paper. In these cases the Lyapunov
function on a siphon face has the form

V_{\Sigma}=H_{\Sigma}(y_{\Sigma})+(\ell^{\Sigma})^{\top}x_{\Sigma},

where
H_{\Sigma}
is a Volterra entropy on resident variables and

(\ell^{\Sigma})^{\top}x_{\Sigma}
is a Perron-weighted linear functional on invading
variables.

Theorem 6

(Local Perron–Volterra Lyapunov function near a siphon face)
.

Consider a positive ODE near a boundary equilibrium
E_{\Sigma}=(0,y_{\Sigma}^{*})
,
written as

x_{\Sigma}^{\prime}=(A_{\Sigma}+\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}(x_{\Sigma},y_{\Sigma}))x_{\Sigma},\qquad y_{\Sigma}^{\prime}=g_{\Sigma}(x_{\Sigma},y_{\Sigma}),

where
A_{\Sigma}
is Metzler and Hurwitz and
\ell^{\Sigma}\gg 0
is a left Perron
vector for
A_{\Sigma}
. Assume there exists a Volterra function
H_{\Sigma}
such
that

\nabla H_{\Sigma}(y_{\Sigma})\cdot g_{\Sigma}(x_{\Sigma},y_{\Sigma})\leq-c_{\Sigma}|y_{\Sigma}-y_{\Sigma}^{*}|^{2}+d_{\Sigma}|x_{\Sigma}|^{2}

near
E_{\Sigma}
. Then, for all sufficiently small
\lambda>0
,

\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}^{\lambda}=\lambda H_{\Sigma}(y_{\Sigma})+(\ell^{\Sigma})^{\top}x_{\Sigma}

is a strict local Lyapunov function near
E_{\Sigma}
.

Proof.

The linear functional
(\ell^{\Sigma})^{\top}x_{\Sigma}
decreases strictly along

x_{\Sigma}^{\prime}=A_{\Sigma}x_{\Sigma}
. The remainder is higher order near

E_{\Sigma}
, while the resident estimate controls
H_{\Sigma}
. Choosing

\lambda
and the neighborhood sufficiently small gives strict negativity away
from
E_{\Sigma}
.
∎

5.2
Perron-based construction of the CEP partition

The same spectral object, the left Perron eigenvector of the transversal
Jacobian, has two roles:

1.

it determines invasion or exclusion through the sign of the dominant
transversal eigenvalue;

2.

it supplies the linear invader coefficients in the Lyapunov function.

The lattice walk starts at the DFE node and descends by removing unstable
invaders. At each node
\Sigma
it computes the face equilibrium, transversal
Perron data, invasion quantities, and the candidate Perron–Volterra function.

5.3
Lattice data and CEP walk

At each node
\Sigma
the required data are:

E_{\Sigma}\in\mathrm{relint}(\mathcal{F}_{\Sigma}),\qquad R_{\sigma}(E_{\Sigma})=\rho(K_{\sigma}(E_{\Sigma})),\qquad V_{\Sigma}=H_{\Sigma}(y_{\Sigma})+(\ell^{\Sigma})^{\top}x_{\Sigma}.

The CEP walk is:

1.

start with the DFE node;

2.

compute all outgoing invasion numbers;

3.

if all are at most one, stop and select the current face equilibrium;

4.

otherwise remove a dominant invader and continue.

It terminates in at most
n
steps. In generic parameter regions the dominant
invader is unique; on tie surfaces, branching or a tie-breaking convention is
needed.

5.4
Certification and theorem coverage

The certification step is a theorem-level global statement only when a facewise
inequality
\dot{V}_{\Sigma}\leq 0
has been proved. In this paper that includes:

•

the two-strain concave Rahman–Zou model;

•

the diagonal singleton extension;

•

the scalar
+
rank-one block model.

For general matrix models, Theorem
6
gives only a local certificate.
The two-block rank-one example above shows why a global certificate may fail
for the standard ansatz.

5.5
Symbolic CEP partition

Symbolically, the terminal node
\Sigma
is described by inequalities

R_{j}(E_{\Sigma})<1\quad(j\in\Sigma),\qquad R_{k}(E_{\Sigma\cup\{k\}})>1\quad(k\notin\Sigma).

For scalar singleton systems this symbolic partition is a GAS partition. For
general matrix blocks it is a local/LAS partition unless a global Lyapunov
certificate is available.

Remark 5

(Perron-alignment diagnostic)
.

A useful test for whether the standard Perron–Volterra ansatz can close at a
face
E_{\Sigma}
is the following.

Let the transversal block have the form

x_{\Sigma}^{\prime}=A_{\Sigma}(y)x_{\Sigma}-V_{\Sigma}x_{\Sigma},

and let
\ell_{\Sigma}\gg 0
be a left Perron vector of the frozen transversal
Jacobian at
E_{\Sigma}
. Compute the family of row vectors

\ell_{\Sigma}^{\top}A_{\Sigma}(y)

as
y
ranges over the resident face.

If these row vectors all lie in a fixed one-dimensional cone, i.e.

\ell_{\Sigma}^{\top}A_{\Sigma}(y)=q_{\Sigma}(y)\psi_{\Sigma}^{\top}

for some fixed
\psi_{\Sigma}\geq 0
and scalar
q_{\Sigma}(y)\geq 0
, then the
production term is Perron-aligned and the standard ansatz has a chance to close.

This condition is automatically satisfied in the scalar case and in the
single rank-one block case

A_{\Sigma}(y)=q_{\Sigma}(y)w_{\Sigma}\ell_{\Sigma}^{\top},

because

\ell_{\Sigma}^{\top}A_{\Sigma}(y)=q_{\Sigma}(y)(\ell_{\Sigma}^{\top}w_{\Sigma})\ell_{\Sigma}^{\top}.

If the vectors
\ell_{\Sigma}^{\top}A_{\Sigma}(y)
span dimension larger than one,
then the standard ansatz

H_{\Sigma}(y)+\ell_{\Sigma}^{\top}x_{\Sigma}

should not be expected to reduce the invader contribution to a single signed
invasion term.

5.6
Conclusion and open directions

The algorithmic CEP picture rests on:

1.

entropy contraction on resident variables;

2.

Perron weights for transversal invaders;

3.

recursive relay on the siphon lattice.

These ingredients yield explicit global Lyapunov functions in the proved cases.
The first obstruction appears when two non-scalar blocks interact through the
same susceptible equation, producing the mixed remainder isolated in
(
12
). Controlling that remainder, or replacing the ansatz by a
cross-equilibrium Lyapunov function, is the main open direction.
