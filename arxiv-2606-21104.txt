On the Weak Right Order of Right-Angled Coxeter Systems
Harrison Gimenez

Abstract
. Let ( W , S ) (W,S) be a Coxeter system, and let w ∈ W w\in W . Let [ 1 , w ] := { x ∈ W ∣ x ≤ R w } [1,w]:=\{x\in W\mid x\leq_{R}w\} where ≤ R \leq_{R} denotes the weak right order of ( W , S ) (W,S) . The element w w is said to have the ancestor property if there is a unique non-trivial involution of maximal length in the set [ 1 , w ] [1,w] . The ancestor property was first defined by Hart and Rowley in [ 4 ] where they conjectured that all non-identity elements in a finite Coxeter system have the ancestor property. In an arbitrary Coxeter system ( W , S ) (W,S) , we show that the ancestor property holds for any non-identity fully commutative element (see [ 6 ] for the definition of a fully commutative element). In particular, since any element of a right-angled Coxeter system is fully commutative, we show that the ancestor property holds for all non-identity elements of a right-angled Coxeter system. Lastly, we also provide an axiomatization of right-angled Coxeter systems as reflection systems with a reflection cocycle that obeys a certain property called the meet intersection condition .

be a Coxeter system. Let

Proposition 2.18

. Therefore, any non-trivial involution of maximal length in
[1,w]
must be unique, and hence
w
satisfies the ancestor property.
∎

Proposition 2.22
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system, and let
w\in W
be fully commutative. Let
q\in\mathcal{H}(w)
. Consider the principle labeled order ideal
I(q)\subseteq\mathcal{H}(w)
. Let
x\in W
be the unique element such that
x\leq_{R}w
and
\mathcal{H}(x)\cong I(q)
(Proposition
2.10
). Then there is a well-defined reflection
t\in T
such that:

t=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k-1}s_{k}s_{k-1}\dots s_{2}s_{1}

where
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
is some reduced expression for
x
.

Proof.

Consider two reduced expressions for
x
:
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
and
(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{k})
. Hence, we have that
s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k}=x=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{k}

q\in\mathcal{H}(x)\cong I(q)
is the unique maximal element of
\mathcal{H}(x)
, it follows that
D_{R}(x)
is a singleton set (Proposition
2.8
part (1)), and hence
s_{k}=r_{k}
. But if
s_{k}=r_{k}
and
s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k}=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{k}
, we conclude that
s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k-1}=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{k-1}
. Hence:

s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k-1}s_{k}s_{k-1}\dots s_{2}s_{1}=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k-1}r_{k}s_{k-1}\dots s_{2}s_{1}

Hence,
t:=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k-1}s_{k}s_{k-1}\dots s_{2}s_{1}=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{k-1}r_{k}r_{k-1}\dots r_{2}r_{1}

Proposition 2.23
.

Let
(W,S)
be a right-angled Coxeter system, and let
w\in W
be any element (note that
w
is fully commutative since we are working in a right-angled Coxeter system). Then there is a bijection between the set of principle labeled order ideals of
\mathcal{H}(w)
and the set
N(w)
given by the following map:

I(q)\longmapsto t\in N(w)

where
t\in T
is the reflection given in Proposition
2.22
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.22
, let
x\leq_{R}w
such that
\mathcal{H}(x)\cong I(q)
, and let
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
be a reduced expression. Then
t=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k-1}s_{k}s_{k-1}\dots s_{2}s_{1}
. First, note that a quick calculation shows that
tx=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k-1}
, and hence
\ell(tx)<\ell(x)
. Therefore,
t\in N(x)
. But note that because
x\leq_{R}w
, Proposition
2.20
implies that
N(x)\subseteq N(w)
. Therefore,
t\in N(w)
, and this shows that the codomain of the map
I(q)\longmapsto t
lies in
N(w)
. We just need to show that the map
I(q)\longmapsto t
is a bijection between the set of principle labeled order ideals of
\mathcal{H}(w)
and the set
N(w)
.

Let
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(w)
. Let
t_{1},t_{2}\in N(w)
be the reflections corresponding to
I(q_{1})
and
I(q_{2})
respectively. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\leq_{R}w
such that
\mathcal{H}(x_{1})\cong I(q_{1})
and
\mathcal{H}(x_{2})\cong I(q_{2})
. Let
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}_{1}=(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{m})
and
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}_{2}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n})
be reduced expressions. Then:

t_{1}=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{m-1}r_{m}r_{m-1}\dots r_{2}r_{1}

and

t_{2}=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{n-1}s_{n}s_{n-1}\dots s_{2}s_{1}

I claim that the map
I(q)\longmapsto t
is injective. To prove this, suppose that
t_{1}=t_{2}
. Note that
t_{1}=t_{2}
implies that the simply reflections
r_{m}
and
s_{n}
are conjugate. But by the exercise at the end of Section 5.3 of
[
5
]
, two simple reflections are conjugate if and only if there exists a path of edges whose labels are odd integers
\geq 3
in the Coxeter diagram of
(W,S)
. Since
(W,S)
is a right-angled Coxeter system, no edges with odd labels
\geq 3
occur in the Coxeter diagram of
(W,S)
. Hence, we deduce that
r_{m}=s_{n}
. But note that
r_{m}
and
s_{n}
are the
S
-labels of
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
respectively. Hence,
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
have the same
S
-label
s:=r_{m}=s_{n}
. If
q_{1}=q_{2}
, then
I(q_{1})=I(q_{2})
, and we conclude that the map is injective. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
q_{1}\neq q_{2}
. Note that because
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
have the same
S
-label
s
, it follows from Proposition
2.11
that either
q_{1}<_{w}q_{2}
or
q_{2}<_{w}q_{1}
. Without loss of generality, let us assume that
q_{1}<_{w}q_{2}
. But because
q_{1}<_{w}q_{2}
, we deduce that
I(q_{1})\subsetneq I(q_{2})
. Because
I(q_{1})\subsetneq I(q_{2})
, where
\mathcal{H}(x_{1})\cong I(q_{1})
and
\mathcal{H}(x_{2})\cong I(q_{2})
, it follows from Proposition
2.12
that
x_{1}<_{R}x_{2}
. But since
x_{1}<_{R}x_{2}
, we may assume that
r_{i}=s_{i}
for
i=1,2,\dots,m
and that
m<n
. Hence,

\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}_{2}^{\ast}=(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{m},s_{m+1},\dots,s_{n})

is a reduced expression for
x_{2}
and

t_{2}=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{m}s_{m+1}\dots s_{n-1}s_{n}s_{n-1}\dots s_{m+1}r_{m}\dots r_{2}r_{1}

But note that because
t_{1}=t_{2}
, we have that

x_{2}=t_{1}t_{2}x_{2}=t_{1}t_{2}r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{m}s_{m+1}\dots s_{n}

=t_{1}r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{m}s_{m+1}\dots s_{n-1}

=r_{1}r_{2}\dots\widehat{r_{m}}s_{m+1}\dots s_{n-1}

where the
\widehat{r_{m}}
denotes omission of
r_{m}
. But the conclusion
x_{2}=r_{1}r_{2}\dots\widehat{r_{m}}s_{m+1}\dots s_{n-1}
is a contradiction, since we have produced an expression for
x_{2}
whose length is strictly less than the length of the reduced expression
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}_{2}^{\ast}
. Thus, we must have that
I(q_{1})=I(q_{2})
. This proves that the map
I(q)\longmapsto t\in N(w)
is injective.

To see that the injective map
I(q)\longmapsto t\in N(w)
is actually a bijection, note that the set of principle labeled order ideals of
\mathcal{H}(w)
has cardinality
|\mathcal{H}(w)|
, and
|\mathcal{H}(w)|=\ell(w)
. It is a general fact that
|N(w)|=\ell(w)
. Thus, since the set of principle labeled order ideals of
\mathcal{H}(w)
has the same finite cardinality as the set
N(w)
, and since the map
I(q)\longmapsto t\in N(w)
is an injection between these two sets, we conclude that the map is a bijection

∎

Proposition 2.24
.

Let
(W,S)
be a right-angled Coxeter system, and let
w\in W
. Let
I(q)\subseteq\mathcal{H}(w)
denote a principal labeled order ideal. Let
t\in N(w)
denote the reflection corresponding to
I(q)
by Proposition
2.23
. Let

t=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k-1}s_{k}s_{k-1}\dots s_{2}s_{1}

be as in Proposition
2.22
where
x\leq_{R}w
,
\mathcal{H}(x)\cong I(q)
, and
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
is some reduced expression. Then

\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k-1},s_{k},s_{k-1},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})

is a reduced expression.

Proof.

Consider
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
. Note that since
\mathcal{H}(x)\cong I(q)
,
s_{k}
is the
S
-label of the unique maximal element
q\in I(q)
. Thus, by Proposition
2.8
, it follows that
D_{R}(x)=\{s_{k}\}
. Since
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
is reduced, applying any braid relation to
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
still results in a reduced expression. But since we are working in a right-angled Coxeter system, the only braid relations are the commutation relations. Note that if
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}^{\ast}=(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{k})
is
any
reduced expression for
x
, then
r_{k}=s_{k}
,
r_{k-1}\neq s_{k}
, and
m(r_{k-1},r_{k})=\infty
. In particular, since
m(r_{k-1},r_{k})=\infty
, it follows that one can never commute the
(k-1)
-th and
k
-th entries of
any
reduced expression for
x
. Since
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}^{-1}=(s_{k},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})
is also a reduced expression, one can conclude using similar reasoning that one can never commute the first and second entries of
any
reduced expression for
x^{-1}
.

Now suppose for the sake of contradiction that

\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k-1},s_{k},s_{k-1},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})

failed to be a reduced expression. Note that by Theorem 3.3.1 of
[
1
]
, it follows that there exists a sequence of braid-moves on
t
that allows for a nil-move to occur. Since we are working in a right-angled Coxeter system, the only braid moves are commutation relations. But since
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
occurs as a prefix of
t
, it follows that one can never commute the
(k-1)
-th and
k
-th entries of
t
. Similarly, since
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}^{-1}=(s_{k},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})
is a reduced expression that occurs as a postfix of
t
, it follows that one can never commute the
k
-th and
(k+1)
-th entries of
t
. Hence, commutation-moves can only occur on the prefix
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k-1})
or on the postfix
(s_{k-1},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})
of
t
. But since there exists a sequence of commutation-moves on
t
that allows for a nil-move to occur, it follows that a nil-move will occur in
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k-1})
or in
(s_{k-1},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})
after some sequence of commutation-moves. But this would imply that
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k-1})
or
(s_{k-1},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})
fails to be a reduced expression, which would then imply that
x
or
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{x}}^{-1}
would fail to be a reduced expression, which is a contradiction. Hence,
t
must be reduced.
∎

Proposition 2.25
.

Let
(W,S)
be a right-angled Coxeter system. Let
t\in T
. Suppose that

\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{1}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots s_{k-1},s_{k},s_{k-1},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})

and

\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{2}=(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{k-1},r_{k},r_{k-1},\dots,r_{2},r_{1})

are both palindromic reduced expressions for
t
. Then
s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k}=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{k}
.

Proof.

Note that since
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{1}
and
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{2}
are palindromic expressions for the same element
t
, it follows that
r_{k}
and
s_{k}
are conjugate simple reflections. But in a right-angled Coxeter system, two simple reflections are conjugate if and only if they are equal. Hence,
r_{k}=s_{k}
. More generally, in
any
palindromic expression for
t
, the simple reflection in the exact middle entry must be equal to
s:=r_{k}=s_{k}
. Since we are dealing with palindromic reduced expressions, one can use similar reasoning as in the proof of the previous proposition to conclude that a commutation-move can never occur between the
(k-1)
-th and
k
-th entries of
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{1}
and
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{2}
. Similarly, a commutation-move can never occur between the
k
-th and
(k+1)
-th entries of
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{1}
and
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{2}
. But since
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{1}
and
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{2}
are both reduced expressions for
t
, it follows that there exists a sequence of commutation-moves that transforms
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{1}
to
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{2}
. But when a commutation-move occurs on
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{t}}_{1}
, it must occur either on the prefix
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots s_{k-1})
or on the postfix
(s_{k-1},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})
. Hence,
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots s_{k-1})
and
(s_{k-1},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})
can be transformed via commutation-moves to
(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{k-1})
and
(r_{k-1},\dots,r_{2},r_{1})
respectively. Thus,
s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k-1}=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{k-1}
, and since
r_{k}=s_{k}
, we deduce that

s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k}=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{k}

∎

Proposition 2.26
.

Let
(W,S)
be a right-angled Coxeter system. Let
x,y\in W
. Let
x\wedge y
denote the meet of
x
and
y
in the weak right order of
(W,S)
. Then
N(x\wedge y)=N(x)\cap N(y)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.20
, we have that
N(x\wedge y)\subseteq N(x)
and
N(x\wedge y)\subseteq N(y)
. Hence,
N(x\wedge y)\subseteq N(x)\cap N(y)
. We just need to show the reverse inclusion.

Let
t\in N(x)\cap N(y)
. Since we are working in a right-angled Coxeter system,
x
and
y
are fully commutative. Thus, since
t\in N(x)
and
t\in N(y)
, one can apply Proposition
2.23
and Propostion
2.22
to construct principal labeled order ideals
I(q_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{H}(x)
and
I(q_{2})\subseteq\mathcal{H}(y)
such that
t
corresponds to
I(q_{1})
and
I(q_{2})
. Let
z_{1}\leq_{R}x
and
z_{2}\leq_{R}y
be such that
\mathcal{H}(z_{1})\cong I(q_{1})
and
\mathcal{H}(z_{2})\cong I(q_{2})
. Let

\textbf{\lx@text@underline{z}}_{1}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{m})

and

\textbf{\lx@text@underline{z}}_{1}=(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{n})

denote reduced expressions. Then:

t=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{m-1}s_{m}s_{m-1}\dots s_{2}s_{1}

and

t=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{n-1}r_{n}r_{n-1}\dots r_{2}r_{1}

By Proposition
2.24
, the expressions

(s_{1},s_{2},\dots s_{m-1},s_{m},s_{m-1},\dots,s_{2},s_{1})

and

(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{n-1},r_{n},r_{n-1},\dots,r_{2},r_{1})

are both reduced (and hence
m=n
). By Proposition
2.25
, one deduces that
z_{1}=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{m}=r_{1}r_{2}\dots r_{n}=z_{2}
. Let
z:=z_{1}=z_{2}
. Since
z\leq_{R}x
and
z\leq_{R}y
, we have that
z\leq_{R}x\wedge y
. Thus,
N(z)\subseteq N(x\wedge y)
. But note that
t\in N(z)\subseteq N(x\wedge y)
. This shows that
N(x)\cap N(y)\subseteq N(x\wedge y)
, and thus we have established that
N(x\wedge y)=N(x)\cap N(y)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Suppose
(W,S)
is a right-angled Coxeter system. Then Theorem
1.4
and Proposition
2.26
imply that
N(w)=\{t\in T\mid\ell(tw)<\ell(w)\}
is the unique reflection cocycle associated to
(W,S)
and that
N:W\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(T)
satisfies the meet intersection condition.

Suppose now that
(W,S)
is a reflection system with reflection cocycle
N:W\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(T)
that satisfies the meet intersectio condition. Note that Theorem
1.4
implies that
(W,S)
is a Coxeter system. Let us suppose for the sake of contradiction that
(W,S)
failed to be a right-angle Coxeter system. This would imply that there exists some pair of simple reflections
r,s\in S
such that
m(r,s)\in\mathbb{N}
and
m(r,s)\geq 3
. Consider the standard parabolic dihedral subsystem
(W_{\{r,s\}},\{r,s\})
. Note that the weak right order of a standard parabolic subsystem naturally embeds as an order ideal of the weak right order of the overall Coxeter system. Hence, if
N:W\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(T)
has the meet intersection property on all of
(W,S)
, then
N
should have the meet intersection property when restricted to
(W_{\{r,s\}},\{r,s\})
. Let
m:=m(r,s)
. Since
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
(W_{\{r,s\}},\{r,s\})
is a finite dihedral Coxeter system. Let
w_{0}\in W_{\{r,s\}}
denote the longest element. Consider
w_{0}r
and
w_{0}s
. Since
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\geq 3
, it follows from a quick calculation that
N(w_{0}r)\cap N(w_{0}s)=T^{\ast}\setminus\{r,s\}
where
T^{\ast}:=\bigcup_{w\in W_{\{r,s\}}}w\{r,s\}w^{-1}
. But note that there cannot exist any
z\in W_{\{r,s\}}
such that
N(z)=T^{\ast}\setminus\{r,s\}
. This is because
N(z)
must contain some simple reflection, but
T^{\ast}\setminus\{r,s\}
does not contain any simple reflection. This is a contradiction, and therefore
m(r,s)\in\{2,\infty\}
for all distinct
r,s\in S
. This proves that
(W,S)
is a right-angled Coxeter system.

∎
