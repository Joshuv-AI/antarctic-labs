Assessing pesticide compatibility with entomopathogenic fungi for biological control: evidence from in vivo and in vitro assays
Authors: Islam Bouketta, Meelad Yousef-Yousef, Pablo Lozano González, Enrique Quesada-Moraga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntegrating entomopathogenic fungi (EPF) into crop protection programs requires ensuring their compatibility with insecticides, herbicides, and fungicides to enable simultaneous applications and reduce operational costs. Compatibility is typically assessed by incorporating pesticides into growth media and measuring toxicity through germination, growth, and sporulation (Biological Index). However, field applications mainly expose conidia directly to pesticides, potentially leading to mismatches between laboratory and real-world outcomes. Here, we evaluated the compatibility of 5 fungicides (copper oxychloride, fosetyl + proamocarb, potassium phosphonate, difenoconazole, and fluopyram + tebuconazole) and 4 herbicides (diflufenican, oxyfluorfen, MCPA, and glyphosate) with 2 virulent EPF strains (Metarhizium brunneum and Beauveria bassiana) selected for efficacy against olive fruit fly, Bactrocera oleae (Rossi) (Diptera: Tephritidae), comparing the conventional medium-amendment approach (MAA) with a direct conidial-pesticide mixture method (CPM), and relating both to in vivo biological activity. For M. brunneum, only one of 4 herbicides was compatible using MAA, whereas CPM classified all 4 as compatible. Of the 5 fungicides, MAA classified 3 as compatible and 2 as toxic, while CPM classified 3 as compatible and 2 as moderately toxic. In vivo bioassays using adults of the tephritid model Mediterranean fruit fly, Ceratitis capitata (Wiedemann) indicated that CPM results aligned more closely with observed efficacy. B. bassiana showed similar results, with lower virulence and fungicide compatibility, and CPM again better reflecting in vivo biological activity. Overall, results suggest that CPM may provide a more realistic approach than MAA for assessing EPF compatibility with agrochemicals and could facilitate EPF integration into crop protection.

Introduction

Within crop protection, Integrated Pest Management (IPM) offers a framework for sustainable intensification by combining strategies that curb pesticide use while maintaining crop productivity and safeguarding ecosystem health (Zhou et al. 2024, 2025). Alongside advances in agronomic and genetic methods and in precision agriculture, biological pest control involving the use of natural enemies such as predators, parasitoids, and entomopathogenic microorganisms occupies a central role as a tool for the effective implementation of IPM strategies (Stenberg et al. 2021, Maurya et al. 2022, Zhou et al. 2024). Entomopathogenic fungi (EPF) are the only entomopathogens with a contact mode of action that begins with conidial adhesion to the cuticle, followed by germination, penetration, and subsequent invasion of the target insect’s hemocoel (Vega et al. 2012, Quesada Moraga 2020, Quesada-Moraga et al. 2023). Moreover, the use of EPF has garnered considerable attention not only due to their efficacy but also to their multifunctionality in agricultural ecosystems (Quesada-Moraga et al. 2023). Entomopathogenic ascomycetes of the genus Beauveria, Metarhizium, Akanthomyces, and Cordyceps have been commercially developed as mycoinsecticides, mainly with EPF conidia constituting the active ingredient, for the management of a wide array of arthropod pests in agricultural, forestry, greenhouse, domestic, and storage environments (Jaronski and Mascarin 2017, Mascarin et al. 2019, Jiang and Wang 2023). More specifically, these fungi are being developed for the control of the olive fruit fly throughout the Mediterranean region (Yousef et al. 2017, 2018, Mantzoukas et al. 2024).

The integration of biological and chemical control measures was first proposed in 1944 in an article published in Nature (Ripper 1944); initially, biological control was conceived as a complementary tool to chemical control, which was then the predominant pest management strategy (Ripper et al. 1949). In recent years, the combined and/or simultaneous use of chemical and biological control methods has gained increasing importance within IPM programs, in compliance with regulations throughout the world promoting the sustainable use of plant protection products and prioritizing non-chemical measures (Zhou et al. 2024). In this context, the increasing application of EPF in IPM programs presents several challenges such as selecting environmentally competent fungal strains together with effective mass production and formulation strategies to mitigate the deleterious effects on the fungal inoculum of the key abiotic factors, temperature, relative humidity (RH), and UV-B (Quesada-Moraga 2020).

However, in integrated crop protection, it is not only essential to combine different strategies targeting the primary pest organism, but also to jointly implement measures for the control of insects, diseases, and weeds. This becomes particularly important when working with entomopathogenic microorganisms, such as EPF. In addition to optimizing their combined application with other pest-control tools—such as chemical insecticides or alternative bioinsecticides—it is also necessary to assess their compatibility with other plant protection products, including fungicides and herbicides. The simultaneous use of EPF with plant protection products may negatively affect fungal viability, spore germination, and overall efficacy (Khun et al. 2021, Garcia-Riaño et al. 2022, Rakes et al. 2024). Certain fungicides can inhibit fungal growth, while some herbicides may alter soil properties and interfere with spore dispersal (Roberti et al. 2017, Mäder et al. 2024). Therefore, compatibility studies are essential to determine whether EPF can be co-applied with other plant protection products without compromising their bio-efficacy, and to design integrated strategies that ensure both efficacy and economic feasibility (Fiedler and Sosnowska 2017, Yousef et al. 2018, Lumînare et al. 2022, Zhang et al. 2026).

According to IOBC standards, assessing the compatibility of pesticides with EPF in the growth medium is recommended because it represents a worst-case scenario (Coremans-Pelseneer 1994). In contrast to conidia, which possess a resistant chitinous cell wall, the actively growing mycelium constitutes the fungus’s most physiologically sensitive stage (Coremans-Pelseneer 1994). If the product is non-toxic at this stage, its safety under field conditions can be confidently assumed (Coremans-Pelseneer 1994). Conversely, the opposite outcome may lead to a clear false negative: potential toxicity could exclude the product from being simultaneously used with the EPF, even if direct mixing of conidia with the pesticide—or even in vivo assays—would otherwise indicate their compatibility. Moreover, most of the previous research conducted to date based on the IOBC standard has relied primarily on in vitro assays assessing fungal growth or sporulation in the presence of agrochemicals (Poprawski and Majchrowicz 1995; Tkaczuk et al. 2004). Consequently, a substantial list of fungicides (Shah et al. 2009, Reddy et al. 2018, Johnson et al. 2020, Shoeb et al. 2021, Erdoğan and Sağlan 2023) and herbicides (Yousef et al. 2015) have been discarded due to their toxicity to EPF based on the in vitro growth medium protocol. Indeed, there are very few works of direct mixing of EPF conidia with the plant protection products (Lopes et al. 2011, da Silva et al. 2013, Garcia-Riaño et al. 2022), and even less including complementary in vivo assessments (Yousef et al. 2018, Rakes et al. 2024), highlighting the overall caution against the joint use of herbicides and fungicides with EPF due to the possibility of false negatives; namely, a compatibility issue detected in culture medium that is not confirmed either when the product is mixed with conidia or in the in vivo assessment.

The present research aimed to evaluate the compatibility of widely used fungicides and herbicides with Metarhizium brunneum EAMa 01/58-Su and Beauveria bassiana EABb 01/33-Su strains—previously selected for their virulence against the olive fruit fly, Bactrocera oleae (Rossi) (Diptera:Tephritidae)—using both the conventional medium-amendment method and a direct conidial-pesticide mixture approach, and to identify which method best approximates in vivo results.

Materials and Methods

Entomopathogenic Fungi Strains and Insects

Two EPF strains belonging to 2 species were used: M. brunneum strain EAMa 01/58-Su and B. bassiana strain EABb 01/33-Su (Table 1). Both strains are currently under development as mycoinsecticides against various insect pests, such as the meadow spittlebug Philaenus spumarius L. (Hemiptera: Aphrophoridae) and the cotton leafworm Spodoptera littoralis (Boisduval) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), with particular emphasis on the olive fruit fly B. oleae (Garrido-Jurado et al. 2020, Yousef-Yousef et al. 2023). To provide inoculum for the experiments, the strains were subcultured from slant cultures stored on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) in Petri dishes and incubated for 15 d at 25 ± 1 °C in darkness.

Adults of Mediterranean fruit fly Ceratitis capitata (Diptera: Tephritidae) used as tephritid model pest species were obtained from the colony at the Department of Agronomy, University of Cordoba (Spain). The colony has been maintained in our laboratory for more than 20 years and is annually refreshed with individuals emerging from field-collected infested fruits. Flies were reared in an environmental growth chamber at 25 ± 1 °C, 60% to 70% RH, and a 16:8 h light-dark photoperiod. Adult flies were provided with water and a standard artificial diet consisting of 40 g of protein hydrolysate (ICN Biomedicals, Aurora, OH) and 10 g of sucrose (Panreac, Barcelona, Spain). Adult flies were maintained in mesh cages (30 × 30 × 43 cm). Colony maintenance followed the procedure described by Quesada-Moraga et al. (2008).

Herbicides and Fungicides

To study the compatibility of EPF with herbicides and fungicides, commercial formulations of pesticides were used, 4 to 5 active ingredients from each category were selected from the database of registered plant protection products of the Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries, and Food (https://servicio.mapa.gob.es/regfiweb).

Among the herbicides, 4 compounds were selected, each representing a major herbicide mode of action group as classified by the Herbicide Resistance Action Committee: synthetic auxin herbicides (Group 4), EPSPS inhibitors (Group 9), PPO inhibitors (Group 14), and carotenoid biosynthesis inhibitors (Group 12).

Regarding fungicides, at least one commercially available compound was selected to represent each of the 4 major mode-of-action categories defined by the Fungicide Resistance Action Committee: group active on Botrytis spp. (Ascomycota: Helotiales) and similar fungi (7); group active on oomycetes (P7); group active on powdery mildews and many other fungi (3); and groups that have multisite activity (M1).

All products were prepared at the manufacturer’s recommended field rate. Final concentrations were calculated based on this rate and a standard spray volume of 1,000 liter/ha for fungicides and 400 liter/ha for herbicides, following common practices for olive orchard. These concentrations were consistently used in both in vitro and in vivo bioassays (Table 2).

Compatibility Assessment of EPF with Herbicides and Fungicides

The compatibility of EPF with herbicides and fungicides was evaluated through 2 in vitro assays to determine their effects on germination, radial mycelial growth and conidial production. For each assay, 5 replicates were used per treatment per trial, with a single Petri dish serving as the experimental unit. Both experiments were conducted twice as independent temporal trials using fresh fungal inoculum, resulting in a total sample size of n = 10 per treatment after combining trials. In addition, an in vivo assay was conducted to validate the compatibility outputs under a real scenario.

In Vitro Experiment for Compatibility

In vitro 1: Incorporation of pesticides into the culture medium (MAA)

In this experiment, the compatibility of EPF with pesticides was assessed by incorporating the recommended field application rate of each pesticide directly into SDA medium. The medium was autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min and allowed to cool to approximately 48 °C before pesticide incorporation. Commercial pesticide formulations were then aseptically added to the cooled medium using a sterile micropipette at the calculated field-equivalent concentration and thoroughly mixed for homogeneous distribution. The final volume of each pesticide added corresponded to the concentration required to obtain the recommended field rate, as indicated in Table 2. Approximately 20 ml of the pesticide-amended medium was poured into sterile 90 mm Petri dishes and allowed to solidify in a laminar flow hood at room temperature for approximately 30 min before inoculation.

In vitro 2: Mixture with conidial suspension (CPM)

In this experiment, the pesticides were mixed with the conidial suspensions of both EPF, simulating tank-mixing scenarios. For that, conidial suspensions were obtained by scraping the surface of sporulated cultures using a sterile spatula and suspending the inoculum in a sterile aqueous solution containing 0.1% Tween 80. To obtain a homogeneous, the suspension was sonicated for 5 min and filtered through several layers of sterile cheesecloth to remove mycelial mats. Conidial concentration was determined by Malassez chamber (Marienfeld Laboratory Glassware, Lauda-Koenigshofen, Germany) and standardized to 1 × 107 conidia/ml. The recommended field rate of each pesticide was added to 10 ml of the prepared conidial suspension, and the mixture was agitated in a rotary shaker for an hour at 25 ± 1 °C. This assay was designed to evaluate tank-mix compatibility between pesticides and EPF when both components are mixed in the spray tank prior to application. Therefore, it does not simulate droplet deposition or drying processes that may occur on plant surfaces after spraying.

Evaluation of Germination, Radial Growth, and Conidial Production

For both in vitro assays, germination was assessed by placing 4 drops, each of 25 µl conidial suspension (1 × 107 conidia/ml), on the corresponding media. In the incorporation into growth medium method (MAA), drops of pesticide-free conidial suspension were placed onto SDA previously amended with pesticides (treatment) or onto unamended SDA (control). In the mixture with conidial suspension method (CPM), drops of conidia-pesticide mixtures (treatment) or the conidial suspension without pesticide (control) were inoculated onto SDA without pesticide. In all cases, drops were covered with coverslips and incubated in a standard laboratory incubator (Memmert GmbH, Schwabach, Germany) in the dark at 25 ± 1 °C. A single evaluation of conidial germination was performed exactly 24 h post-inoculation. Germination was observed under a microscope (Motic BA400, China) at 40× magnification, counting 100 conidia per drop. A conidium was considered germinated when the germ tube exceeded the conidial length.

To evaluate radial growth, when incorporating the pesticide into the culture medium, the Petri dishes were inoculated by transferring 5 mm diameter fungal culture discs taken from a single 4-day-old actively growing colony previously cultured on SDA into the center of each dish using a sterile cork borer. Control dishes containing SDA without pesticide were prepared under the same conditions for comparison. In contrast, in the mixture with the conidial suspension method, 2 µl of the conidial suspension–pesticide mixture was placed at the center of SDA Petri dishes (90 mm diameter) without pesticide using a sterile micropipette. Petri dishes inoculated with pesticide-free conidial suspension served as controls. For both assays, all dishes were sealed with Parafilm and incubated in a standard laboratory incubator in the dark at 25 ± 1 °C. The radial growth was recorded every 2 d for 10 d by averaging 2 perpendicular colony diameters on each Petri dish using a digital calliper. Growth inhibition was determined using the formula: I = ((C − T)/C) × 100, where I is the percentage of growth inhibition, C is the control colony diameter, and T is the treated one. The fungal growth inhibition obtained from each treatment was classified using an index based on Hassan’s (1989) toxicity index as: harmless if reduction in radial mycelial growth was <50%, slightly harmful for 50% to 79%, moderately harmful for 80% to 90%, and harmful for >90%.

After 10 d of incubation, conidial production in both assays was determined by scraping the surface of the same fungal colonies used for the radial growth assessment into a sterile 15-ml Falcon tube containing 10 ml of 0.1% Tween 80 solution. The tube was then vortexed for 5 min to obtain a homogeneous suspension.

Biological Index

The toxic effect of each pesticide on the fungi was assessed using the Biological Index (BI) formula proposed by Rossi-Zalaf et al. (2008): BI = [47 × VG + 43 × SP + 10 × GER]/100. In this formula, values of vegetative growth (VG), sporulation (SP), and germination (Ger) were given in relation to control (100%), where toxicity grades will be as follows: 0 to 41 = Toxic; 42 to 66 = Moderately toxic, and >66 = Compatible.

In Vivo Compatibility of Herbicides and Fungicides with EPF

This experiment aimed at evaluating the efficacy of both EPF when mixed with the pesticides to control C. capitata adults by measuring insect mortality during the bioassay. The experiment followed a completely randomized design with 4 different treatments: (i) a control group treated with a sterile aqueous solution containing 0.1% Tween 80, (ii) an EPF treatment consisting of a fungal conidial suspension (1 × 108 conidia/ml), (iii) a pesticide treatment (recommended field rate), and (iv) a combined treatment where the fungal conidial suspension (1 × 108 conidia/ml) was mixed with the corresponding pesticide before application. C. capitata adults were cold anaesthetized before treatment to facilitate manipulation. A Potter spray tower (Burkard Manufacturing Co. Ltd, Rickmansworth, United Kingdom) was used to apply the treatments, ensuring a precise and uniform deposit of 1.54 ± 0.06 mg/cm2 at 0.7 bars of pressure. The experimental unit consisted of a group of 10 flies, which were treated with 1 ml of the corresponding solution and transferred to a methacrylate cage (8 × 8 × 6 cm; Resopal, Alcalá de Henares, Madrid, Spain). Each cage featured a circular ventilation hole (2 cm diameter) covered with mesh fabric with an approximate mesh size of 1 mm. Treatments were replicated 3 times per trial, and the entire bioassay was conducted in 2 independent temporal trials using a fresh fungal suspension and a new generation of C. capitata adults. This resulted in a total sample size of n = 60 flies per treatment.

The bioassay was conducted under controlled environmental conditions of 25 ± 1 °C, 60% to 70% relative humidity, and a 16:8 (light:dark) photoperiod. To maintain the flies, a liquid diet was provided every 48 h, consisting of 0.1 g hydrolyzed protein and 0.4 g sucrose dissolved in 1.5 ml of distilled water, supplied via a dedicated feeding tube inside each cage. Mortality was recorded daily for 12 d, considering individuals dead when they showed complete immobility and no response to gentle physical stimulation. Dead flies were removed from the cages using a fine brush to prevent the escape of live individuals and to avoid horizontal transmission of fungal infection. To confirm fungal infection, all cadavers recovered from each treatment were surface sterilized in 1% sodium hypochlorite for one minute and rinsed 3 times with sterile water. The sterilized cadavers were then placed on sterile wet filter paper moistened with 1 ml of sterile distilled water inside Petri dishes, sealed with Parafilm, and incubated at 25 ± 1 °C to assess fungal outgrowth (Quesada-Moraga et al. 2006).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2. Analyses were conducted separately for M. brunneum and B. bassiana. In all experiments, treatment was included as a fixed effect and experimental trial as a random effect to account for temporal replication.

Radial growth (colony diameter, mm) and sporulation were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs). Sporulation data were log10-transformed prior to analysis to meet model assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity. Conidial germination and insect mortality, expressed as proportions, were analyzed using generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with a binomial distribution and logit link function. The significance of treatment effects was assessed using F-tests for LMMs and likelihood ratio tests (χ2) for GLMMs. When significant treatment effects were detected (α = 0.05), pairwise comparisons among treatments were performed using Tukey-adjusted contrasts. Model assumptions were verified by visual inspection of residual plots.

Mean survival times were estimated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and compared among treatments using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. These analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States, 2017). Agreement between the in vitro compatibility classifications obtained from the MAA and CPM assays and the in vivo outcomes was evaluated using a weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ). Because compatibility outcomes were ordinal (compatible, moderate, toxic), quadratic weights were applied to account for the ordered nature of the categories. Kappa values were interpreted according to standard benchmarks: <0.20 slight agreement, 0.21 to 0.40 fair agreement, 0.41 to 0.60 moderate agreement, 0.61 to 0.80 substantial agreement, and >0.80 almost perfect agreement.

The complete raw dataset for radial growth, conidial germination, and sporulation measurements across all experimental replicates is provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S4. Adjusted means and corresponding 95% CIs derived from the fitted mixed-effects models are reported in Supplementary Tables S5 and S6.

Results

In Vitro Experiment for Compatibility

Germination

When herbicides were evaluated using the MAA, germination of M. brunneum and B. bassiana was significantly reduced ( χ2 = 4,492; df = 4; P < 0.001 and χ2 = 3,779; df = 4; P < 0.001, respectively), particularly by MCPA and glyphosate. Glyphosate completely inhibited germination, whereas diflufenican did not cause any significant difference compared to the control in either species (Fig. 1). In the CPM, M. brunneum showed high germination rates when mixed with oxyfluorfen, with no significant differences compared to the control, while MCPA significantly reduced germination ( χ2 = 189.27; df = 4; P < 0.001). For B. bassiana, germination was significantly affected by all herbicide treatments ( χ2 = 83.96; df = 4; P < 0.001), although the overall effect was milder than in the MAA (Fig. 1).

Effect of herbicides on germination of M. brunneum and B. bassiana, showing A, B) incorporation into culture medium (MAA) and C, D) mixture with conidial suspension (CPM). Mean values (% ±SE) followed by different letters are significantly different according to the Tukey post hoc test (P < 0.05).

Regarding fungicides in the MAA, conidial germination of both fungi was significantly affected (M. brunneum: χ2 = 4,147; df = 5; P < 0.001 and B. bassiana: χ2 = 4,702; df = 5; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Difenoconazole completely inhibited germination, while fluopyram + tebuconazole and copper oxychloride markedly reduced it in both species. In contrast, fosetyl + propamocarb showed no significant effects on germination. In the CPM, difenoconazole and fluopyram + tebuconazole significantly reduced germination rates in both fungi (M. brunneum: χ2 = 350.92; df = 5; P < 0.001; B. bassiana: χ2 = 2,355; df = 5; P < 0.001), whereas the other fungicides maintained high germination levels (Fig. 2).

Mycelial Growth

The herbicide effects on M. brunneum and B. bassiana varied depending on the type of exposure, MAA or CPM. In MAA, diflufenican had no significant effect on the growth of either fungus compared to the control. However, glyphosate and oxyfluorfen significantly reduced fungal growth, while MCPA completely inhibited growth in both M. brunneum (F = 148.84; df = 4.44; P < 0.001; R2 = 0.844) and B. bassiana (F = 214.66; df = 4.44; P < 0.001; R2 = 0.91) (Fig. 3).

Mycelial growth of M. brunneum and B. bassiana 10 d after exposure to herbicides using 2 application methods: A, B) incorporation into culture medium (MAA) and C, D) mixture with conidial suspension (CPM).

In CPM, diflufenican, glyphosate, and oxyfluorfen did not significantly affect M. brunneum growth, whereas MCPA significantly inhibited growth (F = 8.85; df = 4.45; P < 0.001; R2 = 0.998) (Fig. 3). In contrast, no significant differences in B. bassiana growth were observed among the herbicide treatments under this exposure method (F = 2.72; df = 4.44; P > 0.05; R2 = 0.992) (Fig. 3). Among fungicides, difenoconazole and fluopyram + tebuconazole significantly affected fungal growth for both fungi under all assay conditions (M. brunneum: F = 63.07; df = 5.53; P < 0.001; B. bassiana: F = 26.37; df = 5.53; P < 0.001) (Fig. 4), with complete inhibition observed in the MAA. According to Hassen’s classification, diflufenican, copper oxychloride, fosetyl + propamocarb, and potassium phosphonate were considered harmless (I < 21%) to M. brunneum in the MAA, whereas glyphosate and oxyfluorfen were slightly harmful (I = 50.1% and I = 57.7%, respectively). MCPA, difenoconazole and fluopyram + tebuconazole were classified harmful (I = 100%) with both fungal species (Table 3). However, in the CPM, all herbicides and fungicides were classified harmless for both fungal species (I < 46%) (Table 3).

Conidial Production

Conidial production of M. brunneum and B. bassiana was significantly affected by herbicides and fungicides, depending on the exposure method. For MAA, M. brunneum conidial production significantly decreased with oxyfluorfen, MCPA, and glyphosate compared to the control (F = 1,156; df = 4.44; P < 0.001), while diflufenican had no significant effect (Fig. 5). For B. bassiana, significant differences were observed under the same conditions (F = 790.8; df = 4.44; P < 0.001); diflufenican and glyphosate reduced conidial production, MCPA completely inhibited it, and oxyfluorfen had no significant impact (Fig. 5). In the CPM, neither fungus showed significant differences in conidial production compared to controls (M. brunneum: F = 2.9; df = 4.44; P > 0.05; B. bassiana: F = 1.07; df = 4.44; P > 0.05) (Fig. 5). Regarding fungicides, difenoconazole and fluopyram + tebuconazole completely inhibited conidial production of both fungi in MAA (M. brunneum: F = 6,989; df = 5.53; P < 0.001; B. bassiana: F = 88.65; df = 5.53; P < 0.001). In contrast, copper oxychloride did not significantly reduce conidial production in both fungi (Fig. 6). However, in the CPM, potassium phosphonate and fluopyram + tebuconazole significantly reduced conidial production in M. brunneum, though the effect was less severe (F = 22.38; df = 5.53; P < 0.001) (Fig. 6). As for B. bassiana, significant reductions were observed with copper oxychloride and difenoconazole (F = 5.17; df = 5.53; P < 0.001), whereas fosetyl + propamocarb, potassium phosphonate and fluopyram + tebuconazole showed no significant effects (Fig. 6).

Biological Index

In MAA, the BI demonstrated that diflufenican was the only compatible herbicide with M. brunneum (BI = 67.1). In contrast, all herbicides were compatible when applied in CPM (BI > 76) (Table 4). Within the fungicides, difenoconazole and fluopyram + tebuconazole were toxic in MAA (BI < 8) but showed only moderate toxicity in CPM (BI = 52.7 and BI = 42.3, respectively), while all other fungicides remained compatible under both conditions (BI > 69) (Table 4). On the contrary, B. bassiana exhibited a different response to the same treatments. The herbicide oxyfluorfen was the only compatible in MAA (BI = 91.4), while MCPA was classified as toxic (BI = 5.6). All herbicides were considered as compatible in CPM (BI > 69) (Table 4). Most fungicides were moderately toxic in MAA, except for difenoconazole and fluopyram + tebuconazole were classified as toxic (BI < 3). In the CPM, fosetyl + propamocarb and potassium phosphonate were classified as compatible (BI > 70), while copper oxychloride, difenoconazole, and fluopyram + tebuconazole were moderately toxic (Table 4).

In Vivo Compatibility of Herbicides and Fungicides with EPF

The M. brunneum and B. bassiana strains were sprayed onto newly emerged C. capitata adults either alone or in a mixture with each herbicide and fungicide. Significant differences were observed in the percentage of insect mortality when the EPF were applied in combination with herbicides (χ2 = 119.36, df = 9, P < 0.001 for M. brunneum and χ2 = 50.87, df = 9, P < 0.001 for B. bassiana) (Fig. 7). Mortality was high for M. brunneum (87%) and B. bassiana (58%) when used alone, while it was significantly reduced only when M. brunneum was combined with MCPA. In contrast, the herbicides glyphosate, oxyfluorfen and diflufenican did not affect the virulence of either strain (Fig. 7).

Discussion

In integrated crop protection, direct contact between fungicides and herbicides and EPF can occur during standard pesticide application practices, not only due to residues in soil or canopies, or spray drift (Larsen et al. 2019), but particularly through co-application (Gandini et al. 2020). A common case of co-application when allowed by regulations is tank mixing, entailing the preparation of plant protection products directly in the spray tank or containers shortly before application, which improves management efficiency by reducing costs, soil compaction, and the number of field operations (Gandini et al. 2020). Regarding EPF, selecting compatible plant protection products is essential to preserve the viability and infectivity of fungal propagules, thereby ensuring their effective performance as biocontrol agents and preventing detrimental chemical interactions.

According to the now probably outdated IOBC recommendations, developed when the use of mycelial fragments (blastospores) was considered a promising strategy for inclusion as the active ingredient in mycoinsecticides (Stephan and Zimmermann 1998) (today the focus is more on microsclerotia and conidia), the preferred approach was to mix the pesticide with the fungal growth medium (Coremans-Pelseneer 1994). However, the only certainty this procedure provides is that, in cases of compatibility, this worst-case scenario allows the products to be used without in vivo testing (Coremans-Pelseneer 1994). As our results demonstrate, this method is risky: varying degrees of incompatibility can lead to the unnecessary exclusion of fungicide or herbicide combinations with EPF that are, in fact, compatible, as revealed not only by the CPM but also by in vivo studies.

Selecting a substantial number of active ingredients with different Fungicide Resistance Action Committee (7, P7, 3, and M1) and Herbicide Resistance Action Committee (4, 9, 12, and 14) modes of action allowed us to corroborate some observations reported by other authors using the MAA. This was the case for MCPA and glyphosate of known negative impact on EPF in MAA, especially for B. bassiana (Yousef et al. 2015), probably because this herbicide interferes with the synthesis of essential amino acids in fungi, affecting their development and infection capacity (Druille et al. 2013), whereas there are also reports on glyphosate compatibility with B. bassiana (Garcia-Riaño et al. 2022). Such differences may be related not only to variation among fungal strains but also to formulation characteristics, since pesticide toxicity can also be influenced by inert ingredients, adjuvants, and oil-based carriers that ­modify the chemical stress experienced by fungal propagules (Morjan et al. 2002; Lopes et al. 2011; Diao et al. 2021; Khun et al. 2021). It is also known that the toxicity of MCPA is a harmful herbicide for the soil microbial communities (Tejada et al. 2010, Rodríguez-Morgado et al. 2014, Mäder et al. 2024). These 2 herbicides illustrate the contrast between the toxicity observed in MAA and the compatibility shown in CPM, with the in vivo assays demonstrating that the high incompatibility indicated by MAA under worst-case scenarios does not translate to reality, as even the least compatible herbicides caused reductions in C. capitata mortality rates below 20%. Although the reduction of the fungal outgrowth rates could compromise the recycling of the fungus and its persistence in the ecosystem (Rakes et al. 2024). Frome a practical perspective the decision to accept a slight reduction in mortality when combining EPF with herbicides should be based on agronomic and economic considerations within IPM programs. The remaining herbicides, showing asymmetric effects on M. brunneum and B. bassiana, were compatible in vivo, supporting the potential value of the CPM strategy under the experimental conditions evaluated. Indeed, the compatibility of tank mixing of the M. brunneum strain with oxyfluorfen at 24% has already been shown as highly compatible (Yousef et al. 2018), acknowledging that 2 of the products may incur some activity loss depending on pest management decision-making.

However, the case of fungicides further highlights the contrast between the data provided by MAA and CPM experimental strategies, with CPM appearing to better approximate the in vivo results obtained in this study. This interpretation is further supported by the agreement analysis between methods, which indicated a closer correspondence between CPM and the in vivo outcomes than between MAA and the in vivo results. The strong inhibitory effect in MAA of triazoles such as difenoconazole and tebuconazole affecting ergosterol biosynthesis in soil fungi, disrupting cell membrane integrity and drastically reducing their viability, has been previously reported (da Silva et al. 2013, Reddy et al. 2018, Johnson et al. 2020, Garcia-Riaño et al. 2022), together with the compatibility to both EPF species of copper oxychloride, potassium phosphonates, and fosetyl + propamocarb (Khan et al. 2012, Challa and Sanivada 2014). Moreover, the in vivo findings could even be considered surprising, as they suggest that the potential incompatibility of EPF with fungicides appears to have limited impact under the experimental conditions evaluated. Indeed, in the bioassay with C. capitata, the combination of the fungi with the fungicides did not significantly reduce mortality, suggesting good functional compatibility under practical conditions and indicating that results obtained using the MAA do not necessarily reflect the outcomes observed in vivo (Shah et al. 2009, Jaronski 2010, Roberti et al. 2017). This discrepancy between laboratory toxicity and fungal performance under more complex environmental conditions has also been reported by Bruck (2009), who observed that several fungicides strongly inhibited conidial germination and mycelial growth of M. anisopliae in vitro but did not significantly reduce fungal persistence in soil environments. Similarly, field studies have shown that EPF can remain effective in cropping systems where pesticides are routinely applied, as demonstrated by Maniania et al. (2003), who reported effective control of Thrips tabaci Lindeman (Thysanoptera: Thripidae) in onion fields despite the presence of fungicides used for disease management.

The present research also highlights the importance of selecting not only the appropriate method for evaluating compatibility, but also the index, with a preference for the BI as far as it combines different parameters such as germination, growth, and sporulation and avoid considering a product toxic according only to the effect on the EPF growth (toxicity) and highly compatible as revealed by the BI, that provides a comprehensive view of the pesticide’s impact on EPF, allowing for the differentiation between highly toxic, moderately compatible, and safe products for combined use with these microorganisms.

The results obtained in this study have important implications for integrating EPF and plant protection products into sustainable agricultural strategies. Firstly, it has been shown that using compatible herbicides and fungicides can be safely co-applied with EPF, reducing operational costs, soil compaction, and the number of field operations. Secondly, the compatibility data obtained for EPF and the selected pesticides contribute to improving the technical recommendations for biopesticides’ use under field conditions. Thirdly, the 2 in vitro methods applied offer complementary perspectives: MAA simulates the effect of residual pesticide presence, while CPM better reflects practical application scenarios. When combined with in vivo testing, these approaches provide a robust framework for evaluating the functional compatibility and performance of biological control agents when integrated with chemical crop protection strategies. Therefore, our results suggest that the IOBC-guided procedure could potentially be complemented with the compatibility tests based on conidia–pesticide mixtures, as this strategy my help to reduce false negatives compatibility assessments. Nevertheless, broader validation including multiple fungal strains, pesticides formulations, and field trials would be required before such modification could be formally recommended.

These finding reveal that strains EAMa 01/58-Su and EABb 01/33-Su could be co-applied with the selected herbicide products in olive crops for the control of B. olea. Although the laboratory bioassays were conducted using C. capitata as a model tephritid species, this species is widely used in laboratory studies to assess the virulence and compatibility of EPF. Therefore, the results provide a useful indication of potential compatibility, although further validation under field conditions with B. oleae would be desirable to confirm efficacy in olive orchards.
