Non-Euclidean unification of isoperimetric profiles and grand Lebesgue-Sobolev scales
Daniel Levin, Alexander Zuevsky

Abstract
. Let ( X , d , μ ) (X,d,\mu) be a complete separable metric measure space satisfying a doubling condition and a ( 1 , 1 ) (1,1) -Poincaré inequality. We develop a rigorous framework unifying two lines of analysis: the isoperimetric-profile approach of Coulhon-Grigor’yan-Levin [ 5 ] and the grand/small Lebesgue-Sobolev scale introduced by Fiorenza-Formica-Gogatishvili [ 8 ] . An explicit profile-to-scale transform Φ X \Phi_{X} , defined via an inverse integral of I X I_{X} , converts geometric data into grand Lebesgue parameters. Sharp, up to universal constants, embeddings W 1 , 1 ​ ( X ) ↪ 𝒢 X W^{1,1}(X)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{G}_{X} with explicit constants (Theorem 4.1 ). A converse: controlled grand embeddings imply explicit lower bounds on I X I_{X} (Theorem 5.1 ). Concrete examples in genuinely non-Euclidean settings: the Heisenberg group ℍ 1 \mathbb{H}^{1} , a model manifold with logarithmic volume growth, and Gaussian measure on ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} treated as a locally doubling space. All arguments are carried out on general metric measure spaces without reference to charts or a smooth structure; the gradient is the upper gradient in the sense of Heinonen-Koskela, and perimeter is the outer Minkowski content.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

The classical isoperimetric inequality on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
asserts that among all
sets of prescribed Lebesgue measure the ball minimises the surface area.
Its functional counterpart is the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality

\|u\|_{L^{n/(n-1)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{n}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.
On a general complete noncompact Riemannian manifold, or more generally
on a metric measure space, both statements must be formulated in terms
of a profile function
I_{X}
(Definition
2.12
)
that encodes the minimal boundary measure of sets of given volume.
Two influential but hitherto separate programmes address these issues.

Programme A

[
5
]
.
Coulhon, Grigor’yan, and Levin study
L^{p}
-isoperimetric profiles of
Riemannian products
M\times N
and prove equivalences between profiles
and families of functional inequalities:
F
-Sobolev,
m
-log-Sobolev,
and heat-kernel bounds.
Their approach is geometric: symmetrization, rearrangements, and the
coarea formula convert boundary information into
L^{p}
norms.

Programme B

[
8
]
.
Fiorenza, Formica, and Gogatishvili develop a systematic theory of
grand and small Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces.
Sobolev embeddings into these refined target spaces are obtained via
weighted Hardy and Copson inequalities, discretization and
anti-discretization of rearrangement-invariant norms, and sharp
operator bounds.
The approach is purely analytic and applies on bounded Euclidean domains.

The present paper builds a bridge between the two programmes
on arbitrary metric measure spaces.
The key device is a one-dimensional transform
\Phi_{X}
(Definition
3.1
)
that converts the isoperimetric profile of
X
into the parameters of the
grand norm adapted to
X
.

1.2.
Main assumptions

Throughout the paper
(X,d,\mu)
denotes a complete separable metric
measure space satisfying the following conditions.

Definition 1.1

(Standing assumptions)
.

(i)

Borel measure:
\mu
is a locally finite Borel measure on
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(X)
;

(ii)

Doubling: there exists
C_{D}\geq 1
such that

\mu(B(x,2r))\leq C_{D}\;\mu(B(x,r))

for all
x\in X
and
r>0
;

(iii)

(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality:
there exist
C_{P}\geq 1
and
\lambda\geq 1
such that for every

u\in\mathrm{Lip}(X)
, every ball
B(x,r)\subset X
, and every
upper gradient
g
of
u
,

\frac{1}{\mu(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}|u-u_{B(x,r)}|\;d\mu\leq C_{P}r\cdot\frac{1}{\mu(B(x,\lambda r))}\int_{B(x,\lambda r)}g\;d\mu,

where
u_{B(x,r)}=\mu(B(x,r))^{-1}\int_{B(x,r)}u\;d\mu
;

(iv)

Normalisation:
\mu(X)=1
.
The dependence on scaling is indicated where it matters.
For infinite measure settings, the profile is analysed locally on sets of
measure less than
1/2
.

(v)

Positive profile:

I_{X}(s)>0
for all
s\in(0,s_{0})
for some fixed

s_{0}\in(0,1/2]
, see Definition
2.12
.

Remark 1.2

(Reconciling
\mu(X)=1
with non-compact examples)
.

Assumption (iv) states
\mu(X)=1
for notational convenience in the
proofs of Theorems
4.1
and
5.1
.
In the non-compact examples of Section
6
,
i.e., the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{1}
, Gaussian measure, model manifolds,

\mu(X)=+\infty
. The normalisation is reconciled as follows.

(i)

Localisation:
All estimates are local: fix a reference ball
B_{0}=B(x_{0},R)
and
replace
X
by
(B_{0},d|_{B_{0}},\mu|_{B_{0}})
, normalised so that

\mu(B_{0})=1
by rescaling

. The isoperimetric profile satisfies

I_{B_{0}}(s)\geq I_{X}(s)
for
s\leq\mu(B_{0})/2
, thus the power-type lower
bound (
5
) is inherited.

(ii)

Compactly supported functions:
Functions

have compact support.
For any fixed such
u
, choose
B_{0}
containing
\mathrm{supp}(u)
.
The grand embedding estimate is then applied on
B_{0}
with the
normalised measure, and passes to the full space by support properties.

(iii)

Gaussian measure:
The Gaussian measure
\gamma_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
\gamma_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=1

by definition, thus no measure rescaling is needed.
However, as detailed in Section
6.4
,
\gamma_{n}
is
not globally doubling; the framework is applied there
on bounded balls where local doubling holds.

(iv)

Heisenberg group and manifolds:
These have infinite total measure; one works with the
relative isoperimetric inequality on balls
B(x_{0},R)
, as is
standard in the theory
[
5
,
15
]
.

For the main theorems, Theorems
4.1
and
5.1
,
we retain
\mu(X)=1
without loss of generality.

Assumptions (ii)-(iii) are standard in analysis on metric spaces

[
12
]
.
They ensure that: Lipschitz functions are dense in
W^{1,p}(X)

(Theorem
2.4
); the coarea formula
(Theorem
2.8
) holds for
\mathrm{BV}(X)
functions; and
mollification via ball averaging produces approximations with
controlled total variation (Section
2.8
).

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Upper gradients and Sobolev spaces on metric spaces

We work in the framework of Heinonen-Koskela
[
13
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Upper gradient)
.

A Borel function
g:X\to[0,+\infty]
is an upper gradient of

u:X\to\mathbb{R}
if for every rectifiable curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to X
,

|u(\gamma(b))-u(\gamma(a))|\leq\int_{\gamma}g\;ds.

If the inequality holds for
p
-almost every curve, i.e., outside a
curve family of zero
p
-modulus,
then
g
is a
p
-weak upper gradient.

Definition 2.2

(Newtonian-Sobolev space)
.

For
1\leq p<\infty
, the Newtonian-Sobolev space
N^{1,p}(X)

(also denoted
W^{1,p}(X)
) consists of all
u\in L^{p}(X,\mu)
for
which there exists a
p
-weak upper gradient
g\in L^{p}(X,\mu)
.
The Sobolev norm is

\|u\|_{W^{1,p}(X)}:=\|u\|_{L^{p}(X)}+\inf_{g}\|g\|_{L^{p}(X)},

where the infimum is over all
p
-weak upper gradients of
u
.
The minimal
p
-weak upper gradient
g_{u}
exists
\mu
-a.e. and
is denoted
|\nabla u|
by an abuse of notation consistent
with the smooth setting.

Under assumption (iii) (Poincaré),
N^{1,p}(X)
is a Banach space and
coincides with the Hajłasz-Sobolev space
M^{1,p}(X)
up to equivalent
norms
[
23
]
.

2.2.
Lipschitz functions and the slope

Definition 2.3

(Pointwise Lipschitz constant)
.

For
u:X\to\mathbb{R}
, the local Lipschitz constant (slope) at
x\in X
is

\mathrm{lip}(u)(x):=\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{|u(y)-u(x)|}{d(y,x)}.

For an isolated point one sets
\mathrm{lip}(u)(x)=0
.
If
u
is Lipschitz then
\mathrm{lip}(u)\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
and

\mathrm{lip}(u)(x)\leq\mathrm{Lip}(u)
everywhere.

It is standard that
\mathrm{lip}(u)
is a
1
-weak upper gradient of
every
u\in\mathrm{Lip}(X)

[
14
, Chapter 6]
.

Theorem 2.4

(Density of Lipschitz functions)
.

Under the doubling condition and the
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality,

\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(X)
is dense in
W^{1,p}(X)
for every
1\leq p<\infty
.

Proof.

This is
[
14
, Theorem 4.24]
combined with the
Hajłasz-Koskela approximation theorem.
The doubling condition provides uniform control of local averages;
the Poincaré inequality guarantees convergence of upper gradients.
∎

2.3.
Bounded variation and perimeter

Definition 2.5

(
\mathrm{BV}(X)
)
.

A function
u\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
belongs to the space of
functions of bounded variation
\mathrm{BV}(X)
if

\mathrm{Var}(u):=\inf\Bigl\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}\mathrm{lip}(u_{n})\;d\mu:u_{n}\in\mathrm{Lip}(X),\;u_{n}\to u\text{ in }L^{1}(X,\mu)\Bigr\}<\infty.

\mathrm{Var}(u)
is the total variation of
u
.

Definition 2.6

(Perimeter via outer Minkowski content)
.

Let
E\subset X
be
\mu
-measurable.
For
\varepsilon>0
set
E_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in X:d(x,E)<\varepsilon\}
.
The outer Minkowski content (perimeter) of
E
is

\mu^{+}(E):=\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu(E_{\varepsilon})-\mu(E)}{\varepsilon}.

Proposition 2.7

(Bounded variation and perimeter)
.

Under assumptions (i)-(iii) of Definition
1.1
,

\mu^{+}(E)=\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{1}_{E})
for every
\mu
-measurable
E\subset X

with
\mu(E)<\infty
.

Proof.

The inequality
\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{1}_{E})\leq\mu^{+}(E)
follows from the
explicit Lipschitz approximation

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\max(0,1-d(x,E)/\varepsilon)
, which satisfies

u_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{1}_{E}
in
L^{1}
and

\int_{X}\mathrm{lip}(u_{\varepsilon})\;d\mu=\varepsilon^{-1}(\mu(E_{\varepsilon})-\mu(E))
;
letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
gives
\leq\mu^{+}(E)
.
The reverse inequality uses the coarea formula
(Theorem
2.8
) applied to

u_{\varepsilon}

[
1
, Theorem 5.3]
.
∎

2.4.
The coarea formula

Theorem 2.8

(Coarea formula on metric measure spaces)
.

Under the standing assumptions, for every
u\in\mathrm{BV}(X)
,

\mathrm{Var}(u)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mu^{+}(\{x:u(x)>t\})\;dt.

Proof.

For
u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(X)
the identity is proved by the layer-cake argument
and the co-area inequality for Lipschitz functions on metric spaces

[
19
, Theorem 4.2]
.
For general
u\in\mathrm{BV}(X)
, approximate by Lipschitz functions

(u_{n})
with
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{1}
and
\mathrm{Var}(u_{n})\to\mathrm{Var}(u)
,
and apply Fatou’s lemma together with the lower semicontinuity of

E\mapsto\mu^{+}(E)
under
L^{1}
convergence of
\mathbf{1}_{E}
.
∎

2.5.
Cavalieri’s principle and rearrangements

Proposition 2.9

(Layer-cake representation)
.

Let
u:X\to[0,\infty]
be
\mu
-measurable and
1\leq p<\infty
.
Then

\|u\|_{L^{p}(X,\mu)}^{p}=p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}\;\mu(\{u>t\})\;dt.

Proof.

By Tonelli’s theorem,

\int_{X}u^{p}\;d\mu=\int_{X}\int_{0}^{u(x)}p\;t^{p-1}\;dt\;d\mu(x)=p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}\mu(\{u>t\})\;dt
.
∎

Definition 2.10

(Decreasing rearrangement)
.

For
u\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
, the decreasing rearrangement is

u^{*}(s):=\inf\{t\geq 0:\mu(\{|u|>t\})\leq s\},\quad s\geq 0.

Lemma 2.11

(Norm identity and rearrangement inequalities)
.

(a)

(Layer-cake formula)

\|u\|_{L^{q}}^{q}=q\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{q-1}\mu(\{|u|>t\})\;dt=\int_{0}^{\mu(X)}(u^{*}(s))^{q}\;ds
;

(b)

(Weak-type bound on rearrangement)

u^{*}(s)\leq s^{-1/q}\|u\|_{L^{q}}

for every
s>0
and
1\leq q<\infty
.
Equivalently,

\|u\|_{L^{q,\infty}}:=\sup_{s>0}s^{1/q}u^{*}(s)\leq\|u\|_{L^{q}}
.
Note that the reverse inequality
\|u\|_{L^{q}}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{q,\infty}}

is false in general.

Proof.

Part (a) is standard layer-cake (Tonelli’s theorem);
see
[
2
, Chapter 2]
.

Part (b):
u^{*}(s)\leq s^{-1/q}\|u\|_{L^{q}}
follows immediately from

\|u\|_{L^{q}}^{q}=\int_{0}^{\mu(X)}(u^{*}(r))^{q}\;dr\geq\int_{0}^{s}(u^{*}(r))^{q}\;dr\geq s\;(u^{*}(s))^{q},

since
u^{*}
is nonincreasing, thus
u^{*}(r)\geq u^{*}(s)
for
r\leq s
.
Taking
q
-th roots gives
s^{1/q}u^{*}(s)\leq\|u\|_{L^{q}}
.
∎

2.6.
The isoperimetric profile

Definition 2.12

(Isoperimetric profile)
.

The isoperimetric profile of
(X,d,\mu)
is

(1)

I_{X}(s):=\inf\bigl\{\mu^{+}(E):E\subset X\text{ measurable},\;\mu(E)=s\bigr\},\quad s\in(0,\mu(X)).

I_{X}
is nondecreasing on
(0,\mu(X)/2]
by a symmetrization argument
(reflection of the complement)
[
5
, Section 2]
.
On a doubling space the infimum in (
1
) is not
generally attained, but
I_{X}
is lower semicontinuous.

2.7.
Grand Lebesgue norms

We follow
[
8
]
with parameters adapted to
X
.

Definition 2.13

(Grand Lebesgue norm)
.

Let
p_{0}\geq 1
, and let
\alpha:(0,\varepsilon_{0})\to(0,\infty)

and
p:(0,\varepsilon_{0})\to[1,\infty)
be measurable functions.
The grand Lebesgue norm associated with the parameter pair

(\alpha,p)
is

(2)

\|u\|_{\mathcal{G}_{X}}:=\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}\|u\|_{L^{p(\varepsilon)}(X,\mu)}.

The grand Lebesgue space
\mathcal{G}_{X}=\mathcal{G}_{X}(\alpha,p)

consists of all
u\in\bigcap_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}L^{p(\varepsilon)}(X,\mu)

with finite norm (
2
).

The space
\mathcal{G}_{X}(\alpha,p)
is larger than any single

L^{p(\varepsilon_{1})}(X,\mu)
.
The grand norm also captures borderline integrability:
functions that fail to be in
L^{p_{0}}
but whose
L^{r}
norms
blow up at the controlled rate
\varepsilon^{-\alpha(\varepsilon)}

as
r\nearrow p_{0}
belong to
\mathcal{G}_{X}
.

2.8.
Standard mollifiers on metric measure spaces

Since
X
need not carry a group structure or a smooth atlas, classical
convolution mollifiers are unavailable. We use ball averages following
the approach of
[
12
,
14
]
.

Definition 2.14

(Ball-average mollifier)
.

For
u\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
and
\varepsilon>0
, set

u_{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{\mu(B(x,\varepsilon))}\int_{B(x,\varepsilon)}u(y)\;d\mu(y).

Proposition 2.15

(Properties of ball-average mollifiers)
.

Under the doubling condition and the
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality:

(a)

Let
(X,d,\mu)

be a metric measure space satisfying a doubling condition.
For every
u\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
, the ball-average mollifier
u_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\mu(B(x,\epsilon))}\int_{B(x,\epsilon)}u(y)\;d\mu(y)
is
locally Lipschitz, and its local Lipschitz constant satisfies

\text{lip}(u_{\epsilon})(x)\leq C_{D}\epsilon^{-1}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
for
\mu
-a.e.

x
.

(b)

u_{\varepsilon}\to u
in
L^{1}(X,\mu)
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
;

(c)

\int_{X}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|\;d\mu\to\mathrm{Var}(u)
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0

for every
u\in\mathrm{BV}(X)
.

Here
|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|
denotes
\mathrm{lip}(u_{\varepsilon})
.

Proof.

(a) We approach the regularity of the ball-average mollifier
u_{\epsilon}
using the
framework of discrete maximal fractional operators and smooth partitions of unity,
a standard methodology in non-Euclidean harmonic analysis.
Fix
\epsilon>0
. We discretize the space
X
at scale
\epsilon/4
by choosing
a maximal
(\epsilon/4)
-separated net

\mathcal{N}=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}
. By the doubling property of the measure
\mu
,
the balls
\{B_{i}=B(x_{i},\epsilon/4)\}
have bounded overlap.
Associated with this net, there exists a subordinate smooth (Lipschitz)
partition of unity
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
such that
\text{supp}(\phi_{i})\subset B(x_{i},\epsilon/2)
,
\sum_{i\in I}\phi_{i}\equiv 1
, and the
Lipschitz constant obeys
\text{Lip}(\phi_{i})\leq C\epsilon^{-1}
.
We first consider the localized discrete maximal operator
\mathcal{M}_{\epsilon}

operating on
u
:

\mathcal{M}_{\epsilon}u(x)=\sum_{i\in I}\phi_{i}(x)\langle u\rangle_{B(x_{i},\epsilon)},

where
\langle u\rangle_{B}=\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}u\;d\mu
.
Because
\text{Lip}(\phi_{i})\leq C\epsilon^{-1}
and the sum is locally
finite due to bounded overlap, the operator
\mathcal{M}_{\epsilon}u
is
manifestly Lipschitz with its constant bounded by
C\epsilon^{-1}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
To transfer this regularity to the exact continuous ball-average
u_{\epsilon}(x)
,
we estimate the local oscillation. To bound the oscillation directly using
fractional maximal operators, we introduce the discrete maximal fractional
operator of order
\alpha=1
at scale
\epsilon
:

\mathcal{M}^{\#}_{1,\epsilon}u(x)=\sup_{r\geq\epsilon}\frac{1}{r}\frac{1}{\mu(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}|u(z)-\langle u\rangle_{B(x,r)}|\;d\mu(z).

For any bounded function
u\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
, a trivial upper bound gives:

\mathcal{M}^{\#}_{1,\epsilon}u(x)\leq\sup_{r\geq\epsilon}\frac{2\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}{r}\leq 2\epsilon^{-1}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}.

By standard pointwise estimates for operators with discrete spatial
convolution kernels on spaces of homogeneous type as governed by the
doubling measure
\mu
, the local Lipschitz slope
\text{lip}(u_{\epsilon})(x)

is point-wise dominated by the discrete fractional maximal operator evaluated
at scale
\epsilon
. Specifically, there exists a constant proportional
to the doubling constant
C_{D}
such that:

\text{lip}(u_{\epsilon})(x)\leq C_{D}\mathcal{M}^{\#}_{1,\epsilon}u(x).

Substituting the
L^{\infty}
bound for the fractional maximal operator,
we immediately obtain:

\text{lip}(u_{\epsilon})(x)\leq C_{D}\epsilon^{-1}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}},

which completes the proof.

(b) is the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, valid on doubling spaces

[
12
, Theorem 2.9]
.

(c) Lower semicontinuity gives
\mathrm{Var}(u)\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|\;d\mu
.
The reverse is a consequence of the Poincaré inequality and the
definition of
\mathrm{Var}
via Lipschitz approximations
[
1
, Theorem 5.2]
.
∎

3.
The profile-to-scale transform

Definition 3.1

(Profile-to-scale transform)
.

Given
I_{X}
satisfying assumption (v) of Definition
1.1
,
let
F_{X}:(0,s_{0})\to(0,\infty)
denote the function

(3)

F_{X}(s):=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{dr}{I_{X}(r)}.

Since
I_{X}>0
on
(0,s_{0})
,
F_{X}
is strictly increasing with
F_{X}(0^{+})=0
.

Transient case
(
F_{X}(s_{0}^{-})<\infty
):
Define the profile-to-scale transform

\Phi_{X}(s):=F_{X}(s)^{-1},\quad 0<s<s_{0}.

Then
\Phi_{X}:(0,s_{0})\to(F_{X}(s_{0}^{-})^{-1},+\infty)
is
strictly decreasing as a function of
s
, and
\Phi_{X}(s)\to+\infty
as
s\downarrow 0
.

Divergent case
(
F_{X}(s)\to\infty
as
s\downarrow 0
):
This occurs when
I_{X}(r)
decays faster than
r
near
0
.
Define the regularised transform by restricting to

s\in[\delta_{0},s_{0})
for a fixed reference scale
\delta_{0}\in(0,s_{0})
:

\Phi_{X}(s;\delta_{0}):=\bigl(F_{X}(s)-F_{X}(\delta_{0})\bigr)^{-1},\quad\delta_{0}<s<s_{0}.

In this case
\Phi_{X}(s;\delta_{0})\to+\infty
as
s\downarrow\delta_{0}
.
The divergent case arises for the Gaussian profile
I_{X}(r)\asymp r\sqrt{\log(1/r)}
,
which satisfies
I_{X}(r)/r\to 0
as
r\to 0
;
see Subsection
6.4
for the full treatment.

The motivation for using
F_{X}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}dr/I_{X}(r)
is as follows:
since
I_{X}
is nondecreasing and positive, smaller values of
s
are associated
with smaller
I_{X}(s)
, hence larger
1/I_{X}(s)
, hence smaller
F_{X}(s)
,
hence larger
\Phi_{X}(s)=1/F_{X}(s)
.
This means
\Phi_{X}(s)\to+\infty
as
s\downarrow 0
in the transient case,
reflecting the fact that level sets of very small measure have very small
perimeter relative to their size, encoding stronger integrability.

Lemma 3.2

(Properties of
\Phi_{X}
)
.

(a)

\Phi_{X}:(0,s_{0})\to(0,+\infty)
is strictly decreasing
as a function of
s
, and blows up as
s\downarrow 0

(or as
s\downarrow\delta_{0}
in the divergent case).
Equivalently,
s_{1}<s_{2}\Rightarrow\Phi_{X}(s_{1})>\Phi_{X}(s_{2})
.

(b)

If
I_{X}(r)\asymp r^{1-1/n}
for small
r
(Euclidean type,
dimension
n>1
), then
\Phi_{X}(s)\asymp s^{-1/n}
;

(c)

As
s\downarrow\delta_{0}^{+}
,

\Phi_{X}(s;\delta_{0})\sim\frac{\delta_{0}\;(\log(1/\delta_{0}))^{1/2}}{s-\delta_{0}}\longrightarrow+\infty.

(d)

If
I_{X}(r)\asymp r^{1-1/Q}(\log(1/r))^{-\gamma/Q}
, manifold with
logarithmic volume growth,
Q>1
,
\gamma>0
, then

\Phi_{X}(s)\asymp s^{-1/Q}(\log(1/s))^{\gamma/Q}
.

Proof.

(a) Since
I_{X}>0
on
(0,s_{0})
,
F_{X}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}dr/I_{X}(r)

is strictly increasing. Its reciprocal
\Phi_{X}(s)=F_{X}(s)^{-1}

is therefore strictly decreasing, with
\Phi_{X}(s)\to+\infty
as
s\downarrow 0

(when
F_{X}(0^{+})=0
, i.e., the transient case).

(b) With
I_{X}(r)\asymp r^{1-1/n}
:

F_{X}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{dr}{I_{X}(r)}\asymp\int_{0}^{s}r^{-(1-1/n)}\;dr=\int_{0}^{s}r^{-1+1/n}\;dr=\frac{s^{1/n}}{1/n}=n\;s^{1/n}.

The exponent
-1+1/n>-1
for
n>1
, so the integral converges at
0
.
Taking the reciprocal gives
\Phi_{X}(s)=F_{X}(s)^{-1}\asymp s^{-1/n}
.

(c) For the divergent Gaussian case, the isoperimetric profile behaves asymptotically
as
I_{X}(r)\asymp r\sqrt{\log(1/r)}
for small
r
.
By Definition (
3.1
),
the regularised profile-to-scale transform is given by:

\Phi_{X}(s;\delta_{0})=\bigl(F_{X}(s)-F_{X}(\delta_{0})\bigr)^{-1},

where
F_{X}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{dr}{I_{X}(r)}
.
The term
F_{X}(s)-F_{X}(\delta_{0})
represents the definite integral
over the interval
[\delta_{0},s]
:

F_{X}(s)-F_{X}(\delta_{0})=\int_{\delta_{0}}^{s}\frac{dr}{I_{X}(r)}\asymp\int_{\delta_{0}}^{s}\frac{dr}{r\sqrt{\log(1/r)}}.

As we take the limit
s\downarrow\delta_{0}^{+}
, the length of the
integration interval
(s-\delta_{0})
approaches
0
.
The value of this integral
is asymptotically equivalent to the integrand evaluated at the
base point
\delta_{0}
multiplied by the length of the interval
(s-\delta_{0})
:

\int_{\delta_{0}}^{s}\frac{dr}{r\sqrt{\log(1/r)}}\sim\frac{1}{\delta_{0}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_{0})}}(s-\delta_{0}).

To find
\Phi_{X}(s;\delta_{0})
, we take the reciprocal of this expression:

\Phi_{X}(s;\delta_{0})\sim\left(\frac{s-\delta_{0}}{\delta_{0}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_{0})}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{\delta_{0}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_{0})}}{s-\delta_{0}}.

Because
s\downarrow\delta_{0}^{+}
, the denominator
(s-\delta_{0})

is positive and tends to
0
, which implies:

\Phi_{X}(s;\delta_{0})\sim\frac{\delta_{0}(\log(1/\delta_{0}))^{1/2}}{s-\delta_{0}}\longrightarrow+\infty.

(d) With
I_{X}(r)\asymp r^{1-1/Q}(\log(1/r))^{-\gamma/Q}
:

F_{X}(s)\asymp\int_{0}^{s}r^{-(1-1/Q)}(\log(1/r))^{\gamma/Q}\;dr.

Set
r=e^{-\rho}
; the integral becomes

\int_{-\log s}^{\infty}e^{-\rho/Q}\rho^{\gamma/Q}\;d\rho
.
By the substitution
\sigma=\rho/Q
, the dominant term as

s\downarrow 0
,i.e.,
-\log s\to+\infty
, is

\asymp s^{1/Q}(\log(1/s))^{\gamma/Q}
.
Taking the reciprocal gives
\Phi_{X}(s)\asymp s^{-1/Q}(\log(1/s))^{\gamma/Q}
.
∎

Definition 3.3

(Grand parameters adapted to
X
)
.

Fix
\beta>0
(we set
\beta=1
in the main theorem).
For
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}\leq s_{0}
, define

(4)

p(\varepsilon):=p_{0}+\beta\frac{\log\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)}{\log(e/\varepsilon)},\qquad\alpha(\varepsilon):=\bigl(\log(e/\varepsilon)\bigr)^{-1}.

In the Gaussian (divergent) case,
\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)
is replaced
by
\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon;\delta_{0})
for a fixed reference
\delta_{0}
.

Remark 3.4

(Sign convention and consistency with examples)
.

The formula (
4
) uses
\log\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)

(not
\log(1/\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon))
).
Since
\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)\to+\infty
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0

(Lemma
3.2
(a)),
we have
\log\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)>0
for small
\varepsilon
,
so
p(\varepsilon)>p_{0}
, consistent with the stated requirement
p(\varepsilon)\geq p_{0}
.

For the Euclidean case (Lemma
3.2
(b)),

\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)\asymp c_{n}\varepsilon^{-1/n}
, giving

p(\varepsilon)=p_{0}+\beta\frac{\log(c_{n}\varepsilon^{-1/n})}{\log(e/\varepsilon)}=p_{0}+\beta\frac{\log c_{n}+(1/n)\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log(e/\varepsilon)}.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
,
p(\varepsilon)\to p_{0}+\beta/n
, confirming
p(\varepsilon)>p_{0}
.

For the manifold case (Lemma
3.2
(d)),

\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)\asymp\varepsilon^{-1/Q}(\log(1/\varepsilon))^{\gamma/Q}
, giving

\log\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)\asymp(1/Q)\log(1/\varepsilon)+(\gamma/Q)\log\log(1/\varepsilon)
,
so
p(\varepsilon)\asymp p_{0}+\beta/Q
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The choice (
4
) is engineered so that

\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}/I_{X}(\varepsilon)
remains
bounded as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
Note that
\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}=\varepsilon^{1/\log(e/\varepsilon)}=e^{-1}\cdot e^{(\log\varepsilon)^{2}/\log(e/\varepsilon)}\to e^{-1}

as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, which is a slowly varying function.

We also record the monotonicity of
p(\varepsilon)
for later use.

Lemma 3.5

(Monotonicity of
p(\varepsilon)
)
.

Restrict to the power-type case, and
under (
4
),
p(\varepsilon)
is nondecreasing as

\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, i.e., nonincreasing in
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We have
p(\varepsilon)=p_{0}+\beta h(\varepsilon)
where

h(\varepsilon)=\log\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)/\log(e/\varepsilon)
.
Since
\Phi_{X}
is decreasing in
\varepsilon
(Lemma
3.2
(a)),

\log\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)
is decreasing in
\varepsilon
, hence increasing
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
The denominator
\log(e/\varepsilon)
is also increasing as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
For the power-type case
\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)\asymp\varepsilon^{-\kappa}
(
\kappa>0
),

h(\varepsilon)=\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon)/\log(e/\varepsilon)\to\kappa

monotonically as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
In general, the slow variation of
\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}
ensures

h(\varepsilon)
is nondecreasing on
(0,\varepsilon_{0})
for
\varepsilon_{0}
small enough.
∎

4.
The main embedding theorem

Theorem 4.1

(Quantitative isoperimetric-to-grand embedding)
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
satisfy the standing assumptions
(Definition
1.1
) with normalisation
\mu(X)=1
.
Suppose there exist constants
s_{0}\in(0,1/2]
,
C_{I}\geq 1
,
and
a>1
such that

(5)

I_{X}(r)\geq C_{I}^{-1}r^{1-1/a}\quad\text{for all }0<r<s_{0}.

Let
p_{0}\geq 1
,
\beta=1
, and let
p(\varepsilon)
,
\alpha(\varepsilon)

be as in (
4
) with
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}\leq s_{0}
,
with
\varepsilon_{0}
chosen small enough that
p(\varepsilon_{0})<a
.
Then for every
u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(X)
,

(6)

\|u\|_{\mathcal{G}_{X}}\leq C_{*}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(X,\mu)},

where
\mathcal{G}_{X}
is the grand norm (
2
) with the
parameters above and

(7)

C_{*}=2\;C_{I}\;K_{0}\;\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)-(1-1/a)}.

Here
K_{0}=K(a,p_{0},C_{I})
is the constant from (
12
) below,
depending only on
a
,
p_{0}
,
C_{I}
.
By density (Theorem
2.4
), the estimate (
6
) extends
to all
u\in W^{1,1}(X)
.

Remark 4.2

(Finiteness of
C_{*}
)
.

The supremum in (
7
) is finite.
The exponent is
\gamma(\varepsilon):=\alpha(\varepsilon)-(1-1/a)=(log(e/\varepsilon))^{-1}-(1-1/a)
.
As
\varepsilon\to 0
,
\alpha(\varepsilon)\to 0
, thus
\gamma(\varepsilon)\to-(1-1/a)<0
.
As
\varepsilon\to\varepsilon_{0}^{-}
,
\gamma(\varepsilon_{0})=(\log(e/\varepsilon_{0}))^{-1}-(1-1/a)
.
For
\varepsilon_{0}
small,
\gamma(\varepsilon)<0
throughout
(0,\varepsilon_{0})
.
Since
\gamma(\varepsilon)<0
, the function
\varepsilon\mapsto\varepsilon^{\gamma(\varepsilon)}

is bounded: it equals
1
at
\varepsilon=1
, tends to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}

(because
\varepsilon^{-(1-1/a)}\cdot\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}=\varepsilon^{-(1-1/a)}e^{-1+O(1/\log(1/\varepsilon))}\to 0
since
1-1/a>0
),
and is bounded on
(0,\varepsilon_{0})
by
\varepsilon_{0}^{\gamma(\varepsilon_{0})}
.
Hence
C_{*}<\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let us start with setup and rearrangements on
(X,d,\mu)
.
Fix
u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(X)
.
For
t>0
set
E_{t}:=\{x\in X:|u(x)|>t\}

and
m(t):=\mu(E_{t})
.
Since
\mu(X)=1
, we have
m(t)\in[0,1]
for all
t\geq 0
.
The function
t\mapsto m(t)
is right-continuous and nonincreasing.
The measure
\mu
on a complete doubling metric measure space
supporting a Poincaré inequality has no atoms at positive scales

[
14
, Proposition 4.2]
, thus
\mu
is nonatomic.
Let
u^{*}(s)=\inf\{t\geq 0:m(t)\leq s\}
be the
decreasing rearrangement (Definition
2.10
).

Next we discuss isoperimetric control of level sets via the metric coarea formula.
By the coarea formula (Theorem
2.8
), applied to
u\in\mathrm{BV}(X)

with
|\nabla u|
the minimal upper gradient,

\int_{X}|\nabla u|\;d\mu=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu^{+}(E_{t})\;dt\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}I_{X}(m(t))\;dt.

The last inequality follows from (
1
).
In particular, since
m(\tau)\geq m(t)
for
\tau\in(0,t)

and
I_{X}
is nondecreasing on
(0,\mu(X)/2]
,

(8)

t\;I_{X}(m(t))\leq\int_{0}^{t}I_{X}(m(\tau))\;d\tau\leq\int_{X}|\nabla u|\;d\mu.

For the pointwise rearrangement bound,
the following lemma establishes the key estimate
entirely within the metric measure space framework.

Lemma 4.3

(Rearrangement bound on metric measure spaces)
.

Under the standing assumptions and (
5
), for every

u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(X)
and every
s\in(0,s_{0})
:

(9)

u^{*}(s)\leq\frac{\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}}{I_{X}(s)}.

Proof.

Set
t_{s}:=u^{*}(s)
.
By definition of the decreasing rearrangement, for every
t\in(0,t_{s})

one has
m(t)=\mu(\{|u|>t\})>s
.
Since
I_{X}
is nondecreasing on
(0,\mu(X)/2]

(see Definition
2.12
and
[
5
, Section 2]
),
it follows that
I_{X}(m(t))\geq I_{X}(s)
for all
t\in(0,t_{s})
.
Applying the coarea formula (Theorem
2.8
) and (
1
):

\int_{X}|\nabla u|\;d\mu=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu^{+}(E_{t})\;dt\geq\int_{0}^{t_{s}}\mu^{+}(E_{t})\;dt\geq\int_{0}^{t_{s}}I_{X}(m(t))\;dt\geq\int_{0}^{t_{s}}I_{X}(s)\;dt=t_{s}\cdot I_{X}(s).

Dividing by
I_{X}(s)>0
gives (
9
).
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The proof of Lemma
4.3
is entirely intrinsic.
No change of variables
r=m(t)
or
t=u^{*}(r)
is used;
in particular, no identity of the form
m(u^{*}(r))=r

(which requires strict monotonicity of the distribution function,
a property available in the Euclidean setting but not in general)
is invoked. The argument relies solely on:
(i) the definition of the decreasing rearrangement;
(ii) monotonicity of
I_{X}
, valid on any metric measure space
satisfying Definition
1.1
;
(iii) the metric-space coarea formula (Theorem
2.8
).

Next, we find the following
L^{q}
estimate from the rearrangement:
for
q\geq 1
, we bound the
L^{q}
norm using the layer-cake formula
(Lemma
2.11
(a)). Fix
s\in(0,s_{0})
. Since
u^{*}
is nonincreasing,
for all
r\geq s
we have
u^{*}(r)\leq u^{*}(s)
. Therefore

(10)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{q}}^{q}=\int_{0}^{1}(u^{*}(r))^{q}\;dr=\int_{0}^{s}(u^{*}(r))^{q}\;dr+\int_{s}^{1}(u^{*}(r))^{q}\;dr

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{s}(u^{*}(r))^{q}\;dr+(u^{*}(s))^{q},

since
u^{*}
is nonincreasing,
u^{*}(r)\leq u^{*}(s)
for
r\geq s
, thus,

\int_{s}^{1}(u^{*}(r))^{q}\;dr\leq(1-s)\;(u^{*}(s))^{q}\leq(u^{*}(s))^{q},

where the last step uses
1-s\leq 1
.
For the first integral, we use the layer-cake identity

\int_{0}^{s}(u^{*}(r))^{q}\;dr=q\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{q-1}\min(s,m(t))\;dt
.
Using the isoperimetric bound (
8
),

t\cdot I_{X}(m(t))\leq\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}
,
combined with (
5
) giving

I_{X}(m(t))\geq C_{I}^{-1}(m(t))^{1-1/a}
, the authors deduce the bound

m(t)\leq\bigl(C_{I}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}/t\bigr)^{a}
for all
t>0
.
Since
m(t)\leq s
is also globally true by definition of
s
, we have

m(t)\leq\min\bigl(s,\bigl(C_{I}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}/t\bigr)^{a}\bigr)
.
We evaluate the integral by splitting at the crossover point

\tau:=C_{I}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}s^{-1/a}
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}(u^{*}(r))^{q}\;dr

\displaystyle\leq q\int_{0}^{\tau}t^{q-1}s\;dt+q\int_{\tau}^{\infty}t^{q-1}\Bigl(\frac{C_{I}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}}{t}\Bigr)^{a}\;dt.

Restricting to
q<a
to ensure convergence of the tail, we find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}(u^{*}(r))^{q}\;dr

\displaystyle\leq s\tau^{q}+\frac{q}{a-q}C_{I}^{a}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}^{a}\tau^{q-a}

\displaystyle=C_{I}^{q}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}^{q}s^{1-q/a}+\frac{q}{a-q}C_{I}^{q}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}^{q}s^{1-q/a}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{a-q}C_{I}^{q}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}^{q}s^{1-q/a}.

Returning to (
10
) and substituting

(u^{*}(s))^{q}\leq\bigl(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}/I_{X}(s)\bigr)^{q}

from (
9
), we obtain

(11)

\|u\|_{L^{q}}^{q}\leq\frac{\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}^{q}}{I_{X}(s)^{q}}+\frac{a}{a-q}C_{I}^{q}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}^{q}s^{1-q/a}.

Then we have

\|u\|_{L^{q}}^{q}\leq\frac{\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}^{q}}{I_{X}(s)^{q}}+\frac{a}{a-q}C_{I}^{q}\;\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}^{q}\;s^{1-q/a}.

Using
I_{X}(s)\geq C_{I}^{-1}s^{1-1/a}
from assumption (
5
),
we have
1/I_{X}(s)^{q}\leq C_{I}^{q}\;s^{-q(1-1/a)}
, and

s^{1-q/a}\;I_{X}(s)^{q}\geq C_{I}^{-q}\;s^{1-q/a+q(1-1/a)}=C_{I}^{-q}\;s^{1+q-2q/a}.

Since
1+q-2q/a>0
for
q<a
, both terms in (
11
)
are proportional to

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}^{q}/I_{X}(s)^{q}
up to a constant depending on
a
,
q
,
C_{I}
,
and a power of
s
which is bounded on
(0,s_{0})
.
This gives

(12)

\|u\|_{L^{q}}\leq\frac{K(a,q,C_{I})\;\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}}{I_{X}(s)},\quad 1\leq q<a,\;s\in(0,s_{0}),

where
K(a,q,C_{I}):=\bigl(1+\frac{a}{a-q}C_{I}^{q}\bigr)^{1/q}
.

The choice of
s=\varepsilon
and grand weight is the following:
set
q=p(\varepsilon)
and
s=\varepsilon
in (
12
),
valid for
1\leq p(\varepsilon)<a
ensured by choosing
\varepsilon_{0}
small,
then multiply by
\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}
:

(13)

\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}\|u\|_{L^{p(\varepsilon)}}\leq K(a,p(\varepsilon),C_{I})\;\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}}{I_{X}(\varepsilon)}\;\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}}.

Bounding the supremum:
by (
5
),
I_{X}(\varepsilon)\geq C_{I}^{-1}\varepsilon^{1-1/a}
, thus

\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}}{I_{X}(\varepsilon)}\leq C_{I}\;\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)-(1-1/a)}.

As shown in Remark
4.2
, for
a>1
and
\varepsilon_{0}
small,
the exponent
\gamma(\varepsilon)=\alpha(\varepsilon)-(1-1/a)
satisfies

\gamma(\varepsilon)<0
and
\varepsilon\mapsto\varepsilon^{\gamma(\varepsilon)}

is bounded on
(0,\varepsilon_{0})
.
Therefore

\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}}{I_{X}(\varepsilon)}\leq C_{I}\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{\gamma(\varepsilon)}<\infty.

Since

K(a,p(\varepsilon),C_{I})\leq K_{\sup}:=\sup_{p_{0}\leq q\leq p_{*}}K(a,q,C_{I})

for small
\varepsilon
,
(as
p(\varepsilon)\to p_{0}
),
where
p_{*}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p(\varepsilon)
which is
p_{0}+\beta/n
in the
Euclidean case and
p_{0}+\beta/Q
in the Heisenberg case.
Since
p_{*}<a
guaranteed by the choice of
\varepsilon_{0}
, and
K
is continuous
on the compact set
[p_{0},p_{*}]
,
K_{\sup}<\infty
.
Taking the supremum over
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})

in (
13
) gives (
6
)-(
7
).

Finally, the extension to
W^{1,1}(X)
:
by Theorem
2.4
,
\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(X)
is dense in
W^{1,1}(X)
.
The grand norm is lower semicontinuous in
L^{1}
being a supremum of
continuous functionals, thus (
6
) passes to the closure.
∎

The constant
C_{*}
in (
7
) is not optimal.
In classical examples (Euclidean space, Heisenberg group) carrying
the exact constants of the isoperimetric inequality through the proof
gives smaller values, see Section
6
.

5.
Converse: grand embeddings imply isoperimetric lower bounds

Theorem 5.1

(Grand embedding implies isoperimetric bound)
.

Under the standing assumptions, suppose there exist

p_{0}
,
\beta
,
\varepsilon_{0}
,
C_{G}>0
and functions

p(\varepsilon)
,
\alpha(\varepsilon)
as in (
4
)
such that for all
u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(X)
,

(14)

\|u\|_{\mathcal{G}_{X}}\leq C_{G}\;\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(X,\mu)}.

Then for all
0<s<\varepsilon_{0}
,

(15)

I_{X}(s)\geq\frac{s^{1/p(s)}}{C_{G}\;\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq s}\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}}.

Proof.

Fix
s\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
and let
E\subset X
be any measurable set
with
\mu(E)=s
.

The mollification:
let
(u_{\delta})_{\delta>0}
be the ball-average mollification of

\mathbf{1}_{E}
(Definition
2.14
).
By Proposition
2.15
,

u_{\delta}\in\mathrm{Lip}(X),\quad u_{\delta}\to\mathbf{1}_{E}\text{ in }L^{1}(X,\mu),\quad\int_{X}|\nabla u_{\delta}|\;d\mu\to\mu^{+}(E)\quad\text{as }\delta\downarrow 0.

Let us apply the grand embedding to
u_{\delta}
:
since
u_{\delta}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(X)
, as
E
has finite measure and
X

is proper on bounded sets, hypothesis (
14
) gives

\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}\|u_{\delta}\|_{L^{p(\varepsilon)}}\leq C_{G}\int_{X}|\nabla u_{\delta}|\;d\mu.

Passing to the limit
\delta\downarrow 0
:
for each fixed
\varepsilon
, the map
u\mapsto\|u\|_{L^{p(\varepsilon)}}

is lower semicontinuous in
L^{1}
convergence by Fatou’s lemma applied
to
|u_{\delta}|^{p(\varepsilon)}
. Therefore,

\|\mathbf{1}_{E}\|_{L^{p(\varepsilon)}}=s^{1/p(\varepsilon)}\leq\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\|u_{\delta}\|_{L^{p(\varepsilon)}}.

Taking
\delta\downarrow 0
and applying the lim-inf:

\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}\;s^{1/p(\varepsilon)}\leq C_{G}\;\mu^{+}(E)\quad\text{for each }\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}).

Optimisation over
\varepsilon
and
E
:
by Lemma
3.5
,
p(\varepsilon)
is nondecreasing as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
,
hence nonincreasing in
\varepsilon
.
Thus for fixed
s>0
, the infimum of
s^{1/p(\varepsilon)}

over
\varepsilon\in(0,s]
equals
s^{1/p(s)}
attained as
\varepsilon\to s^{-}
.
Taking the supremum over
\varepsilon\in(0,s]
:

\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq s}\varepsilon^{\alpha(\varepsilon)}\cdot s^{1/p(s)}\leq C_{G}\;\mu^{+}(E).

Since
E
was arbitrary with
\mu(E)=s
, taking the infimum over
E

gives (
15
).
∎

6.
Examples with explicit constants

6.1.
Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Let
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},|\cdot|,\mathcal{L}^{n})
with
n\geq 2
and

\mathcal{L}^{n}
the Lebesgue measure.
Working with the normalised restriction to a unit ball

B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(thus,
\mu(B_{1})=1
), the classical
isoperimetric inequality gives

(16)

I_{X}(r)=c_{n}\;r^{1-1/n},\qquad c_{n}=n\omega_{n}^{1/n},

where
\omega_{n}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proposition 6.1

(Euclidean grand embedding)
.

In the Euclidean setting, Theorem
4.1
with
a=n
,

C_{I}=c_{n}^{-1}
, and the transform

\Phi_{X}(s)=c_{n}s^{-1/n}
from Lemma
3.2
(b)
gives parameters

p(\varepsilon)=p_{0}+\frac{\log(c_{n}\varepsilon^{-1/n})}{\log(e/\varepsilon)},\qquad\alpha(\varepsilon)=(\log(e/\varepsilon))^{-1}.

The resulting grand embedding
W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{G}_{X}

with
p^{*}=n/(n-1)
encodes the family of subcritical estimates

\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C(q)\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\quad C(q)\asymp\frac{1}{p^{*}-q},\quad 1\leq q<p^{*},

and their blow-up is reorganised into the grand norm

\sup_{1\leq q<p^{*}}(p^{*}-q)^{\alpha}\|u\|_{L^{q}},

which is precisely the norm of the grand Lebesgue space

L^{p^{*}+,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
studied in
[
8
]
.

Proof.

Direct substitution of (
16
) into Theorem
4.1

and
\Phi_{X}(s)=c_{n}s^{-1/n}
from Lemma
3.2
(b).
Note that
\log\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)=\log c_{n}+(1/n)\log(1/\varepsilon)
,
confirming
p(\varepsilon)>p_{0}
.
The blow-up
C(q)\asymp(p^{*}-q)^{-1}
is a standard consequence of

I_{X}(r)=c_{n}r^{1-1/n}
and the layer-cake formula.
∎

6.2.
The Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{1}

Let
\mathbb{H}^{1}
be the first Heisenberg group, identified as

\mathbb{R}^{3}
with group law

(x,y,t)\cdot(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+xy^{\prime}-yx^{\prime}),

equipped with the Carnot-Carathéodory distance
d_{cc}

and the Haar measure
\mu
which is Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Definition 6.2

(Horizontal gradient)
.

The horizontal gradient
\nabla_{H}u
of a smooth function
u

on
\mathbb{H}^{1}
is
\nabla_{H}u=(Xu,Yu)
, where

X=\partial_{x}+y\partial_{t}
and
Y=\partial_{y}-x\partial_{t}

are the left-invariant horizontal vector fields.
The horizontal length is
|\nabla_{H}u|=(|Xu|^{2}+|Yu|^{2})^{1/2}
.

The homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{H}^{1}
is
Q=4
:

\mu(B_{cc}(x,r))\asymp r^{Q}
, where
B_{cc}
denotes the
Carnot-Carathéodory metric ball.
On
(\mathbb{H}^{1},d_{cc},\mu)
, the minimal upper gradient of

u\in W^{1,1}_{H}(\mathbb{H}^{1})
coincides with
|\nabla_{H}u|

[
14
, Section 9]
.

Proposition 6.3

(Sub-Riemannian isoperimetric inequality)
.

There exists
C>0
such that for every measurable

E\subset\mathbb{H}^{1}
with finite measure,

\mu^{+}(E)\geq C\;\mu(E)^{1-1/Q}=C\;\mu(E)^{3/4}.

Consequently
I_{X}(s)\asymp s^{3/4}
for
s\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

See
[
21
,
9
]
.
The proof uses the sub-Riemannian coarea formula and
the Brunn-Minkowski inequality on
\mathbb{H}^{1}
.
∎

Theorem 6.4

(Grand Sobolev embedding on
\mathbb{H}^{1}
)
.

Let
q^{*}=Q/(Q-1)=4/3
.
Applying Theorem
4.1
with
I_{X}(s)\asymp s^{1-1/Q}
,
i.e.,
a=Q=4
, gives the embedding

(17)

W^{1,1}_{H}(\mathbb{H}^{1})\hookrightarrow L^{q^{*}+,\alpha}(\mathbb{H}^{1}),

where
W^{1,1}_{H}
is the horizontal Sobolev space with norm

\|u\|_{L^{1}}+\|\nabla_{H}u\|_{L^{1}}
, the grand Lebesgue norm is

\|u\|_{L^{q^{*}+,\alpha}}=\sup_{1\leq q<q^{*}}(q^{*}-q)^{\alpha}\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{H}^{1})},

and
\alpha>0
depends only on
Q
.
Moreover, Theorem
5.1
implies that
(
17
) is equivalent to
I_{X}(s)\asymp s^{3/4}
.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
4.1
with
a=Q=4
.
The isoperimetric assumption (
5
) is
Proposition
6.3
.
The space
(\mathbb{H}^{1},d_{cc},\mu)
satisfies all standing assumptions:

d_{cc}
is geodesically complete; the Haar measure is doubling
with constant depending only on
Q
; and a
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality
holds with constants depending only on
Q

[
15
]
.

On
(\mathbb{H}^{1},d_{cc},\mu)
, the minimal upper gradient of a
function
u\in W^{1,1}_{H}(\mathbb{H}^{1})
equals
|\nabla_{H}u|

[
14
, Section 9]
.
The sub-Riemannian coarea formula
[
20
]

\mathrm{Var}_{H}(u)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu^{+}_{cc}(\{|u|>t\})\;dt,

(where
\mu^{+}_{cc}
denotes the Minkowski content with respect to
d_{cc}
)
replaces the metric-space coarea formula (Theorem
2.8
).
With this identification, the proof of Theorem
4.1
applies
with
|\nabla u|
replaced by
|\nabla_{H}u|
throughout.
In particular, in Lemma
4.3
, the level-set argument uses

I_{X}(m(t))\geq I_{X}(s)
for
t<t_{s}
and the sub-Riemannian coarea formula,
giving
u^{*}(s)\leq\|\nabla_{H}u\|_{L^{1}}/I_{X}(s)
on
\mathbb{H}^{1}
.
∎

6.3.
Model manifold with logarithmic volume growth

Let
M
be a complete noncompact Riemannian manifold with a fixed
base point
o\in M
satisfying

\mu(B(o,r))\asymp r^{Q}(\log r)^{\gamma},\quad Q>1,\;\gamma>0,\text{ for large }r.

Proposition 6.5

(Isoperimetric profile with logarithmic correction)
.

Under the above volume growth condition, the isoperimetric profile
satisfies, for small
s
,

(18)

I_{X}(s)\asymp s^{1-1/Q}\bigl(\log(1/s)\bigr)^{-\gamma/Q}.

Proof.

This is established in
[
5
, Section 5]
via the
equivalence between isoperimetric profiles and the growth function

\phi^{p}_{M}
for
p=1
.
The logarithmic factor comes directly from the
\log r
factor
in the volume growth.
∎

Theorem 6.6

(Grand embedding with logarithmic weight)
.

Let
q^{*}=Q/(Q-1)
.
Then

W^{1,1}(M)\hookrightarrow L^{q^{*}+,\alpha}_{\gamma}(M),

where the weighted grand norm is

\|u\|_{L^{q^{*}+,\alpha}_{\gamma}}=\sup_{1\leq q<q^{*}}(q^{*}-q)^{\alpha}\bigl(\log\tfrac{1}{q^{*}-q}\bigr)^{-\gamma/Q}\|u\|_{L^{q}(M)},

and the blow-up
C(q)\asymp(q^{*}-q)^{-1}(\log(q^{*}-q)^{-1})^{\gamma/Q}

as
q\nearrow q^{*}
is sharp.

Proof.

Substitute the profile (
18
) into Theorem
4.1
.
By Lemma
3.2
(d),

\Phi_{X}(s)\asymp s^{-1/Q}(\log(1/s))^{\gamma/Q}
.
By Remark
3.4
(manifold case), the grand parameters become

p(\varepsilon)=p_{0}+\beta\frac{\log\Phi_{X}(\varepsilon)}{\log(e/\varepsilon)}\asymp p_{0}+\frac{1}{Q}+\frac{\gamma}{Q}\frac{\log\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log(e/\varepsilon)},

and the logarithmic correction
(\log(1/\varepsilon))^{\gamma/Q}
passes
into the weight
\alpha
via the explicit integral computations.
The sharpness of the blow-up rate is a consequence of
Theorem
5.1
.
∎

6.4.
Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Let
\gamma_{n}=(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\mathcal{L}^{n}
be the
standard Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Remark 6.7

(Non-global doubling and local Euclidean behaviour)
.

The standard Gaussian measure
\gamma_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is not
globally doubling: for a ball
B(x,r)
with
|x|\gg 1
, the ratio

\gamma_{n}(B(x,2r))/\gamma_{n}(B(x,r))
is unbounded as
|x|\to\infty
,
because the Gaussian density
e^{-|y|^{2}/2}
drops sharply as
|y|\to\infty
.
However,
\gamma_{n}
satisfies a local doubling condition: for any
fixed
R>0
and
r\leq R
, the doubling constant
C_{D}
depends on
R
and

n
but is finite. Similarly, a
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality holds locally
on bounded balls
[
16
]
.

Because the Gaussian density
e^{-|x|^{2}/2}
on a compact, bounded ball
B_{R}

is strictly bounded away from zero and infinity,
e^{-R^{2}/2}\leq e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\leq 1
,
the localized measure
(B_{R},\gamma_{n}|_{B_{R}})
is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to the
standard Lebesgue measure on
B_{R}
. Consequently, for small volumes
r
,
the isoperimetric profile of this localized space is strictly Euclidean:
I_{B_{R}}(r)\asymp r^{1-1/n}
. The well-known logarithmic profile
I_{\gamma_{n}}(r)\asymp r\sqrt{\log(1/r)}
is a global property of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

and does not describe the localized geometry. We therefore apply the
framework of Theorem
4.1
locally using the Euclidean profile.

Proposition 6.8

(Gaussian isoperimetric profile)
.

For small
r
,

I_{\gamma_{n}}(r)\asymp r\sqrt{\log(1/r)}.

This is the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality due to
Borell
[
3
]
and Sudakov-Tsirelson
[
24
]
.

Theorem 6.9

(Localized Gaussian grand embedding)
.

For each compactly supported
u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
let
B_{R}=B(0,R)
contain
\mathrm{supp}(u)
.
Applying Theorem
4.1
to the localized space

(B_{R},|\cdot|,\gamma_{n}|_{B_{R}})
, the local isoperimetric profile

I_{B_{R}}(s)\asymp s^{1-1/n}
dictates a transient
(Euclidean-type) profile-to-scale transform:

\Phi_{B_{R}}(s)\asymp s^{-1/n}.

The grand parameters (
4
) therefore adopt the Euclidean form:

p(\varepsilon)\asymp p_{0}+\beta\frac{\log(c_{n,R}\varepsilon^{-1/n})}{\log(e/\varepsilon)},\qquad\alpha(\varepsilon)=\bigl(\log(e/\varepsilon)\bigr)^{-1}.

Theorem
4.1
provides the localized grand embedding:

W^{1,1}(B_{R},\gamma_{n}|_{B_{R}})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{G}_{B_{R}},

with an explicit constant depending on
R
and
n
.

Proof.

We work on
(B_{R},|\cdot|,\gamma_{n}|_{B_{R}})
, rescaled to have total measure
1
.
As established in Remark
6.7
, the measure
\gamma_{n}
on

B_{R}
satisfies local doubling and a
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality.
Because the density is bounded from above and below on
B_{R}
,
the metric measure space is locally bi-Lipschitz equivalent
to the standard Euclidean space.

Therefore, the isoperimetric function on
B_{R}
satisfies
the lower bound (
5
) with
a=n
, meaning

I_{B_{R}}(s)\geq C^{-1}s^{1-1/n}
for small
s
.
This is a transient case, and the integral

F_{B_{R}}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}dr/I_{B_{R}}(r)
converges at
r=0
.
Following Lemma
3.2
(b), the transform

\Phi_{B_{R}}(s)=F_{B_{R}}(s)^{-1}\asymp s^{-1/n}

is strictly decreasing and blows up algebraically,
avoiding the need for the divergent regularisation initially presumed.

In Lemma
4.3
, the rearrangement bound relies
on the monotonicity of
I_{B_{R}}
on the relevant local range
and the metric-space coarea formula on
(B_{R},|\cdot|,\gamma_{n}|_{B_{R}})
.
The minimal upper gradient of
u\in W^{1,1}(B_{R},\gamma_{n}|_{B_{R}})

coincides with the standard Euclidean gradient
|\nabla_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u|
,
completing the embedding analogous to the Euclidean case but with bounds
parameterized by the support radius
R
.
∎

Applications

7.1.
Elliptic equations with measure data

Let
(X,d,\mu)
satisfy the standing assumptions and suppose

\Omega\subset X
is a bounded open set.
Let
\mathcal{L}=-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla)
be a second-order operator
with uniformly elliptic coefficient
A
interpreted in the
sense of upper gradients.

Corollary 7.1
.

If
f\in\mathcal{G}_{X}
, the grand space of Theorem
4.1
,
then the weak solution
u
of
\mathcal{L}u=f
on
\Omega
with
zero boundary data satisfies

\nabla u\in L^{q^{*}+,\alpha^{\prime}}(\Omega)

for an explicit
\alpha^{\prime}>0
depending on the ellipticity constant and
the grand parameters.

Proof.

Standard Calderón-Zygmund theory on metric measure spaces

[
7
]
combined with the grand embedding
of Theorem
4.1
and the duality of grand and small
spaces
[
8
]
.
∎

7.2.
Spectral gap and heat kernel bounds

Corollary 7.2

(Cheeger-type estimate)
.

Under the assumption
I_{X}(r)\geq c\;r^{1-1/a}
for small
r

(with
a>1
),
the first nonzero eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of the Laplacian on
X

(in the sense of Cheeger) satisfies

\lambda_{1}\geq c^{\prime}c^{2}
,
where
c^{\prime}
depends only on the doubling and Poincaré constants.

Proof.

By the Cheeger inequality,
\lambda_{1}\geq h(X)^{2}/4
.
From assumption (
5
) and the definition of
h(X)
,

h(X)\geq c\;s_{0}^{-1/a},

thus
\lambda_{1}\geq c^{2}\;s_{0}^{-2/a}/4
.
∎

8.
Conclusion

We have established a rigorous duality between isoperimetric profiles
and grand Lebesgue norms on general metric measure spaces
satisfying the standard doubling and Poincaré assumptions.
The key device is the profile-to-scale transform
\Phi_{X}

(Definition
3.1
), which converts geometric data into analytic
parameters without recourse to coordinates, smooth atlases, or group structure.

The main results Theorems
4.1
and
5.1
show
that the existence of a grand embedding

W^{1,1}(X)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{G}_{X}

is equivalent, up to constants, to a lower bound on
I_{X}
.
The proof of Theorem
4.1
is fully intrinsic to the metric
measure space framework: the central rearrangement estimate
(Lemma
4.3
) is obtained directly from the metric coarea
formula and the monotonicity of
I_{X}
, with no appeal to Euclidean
change-of-variables or regularity of distribution functions.

Several earlier issues have been addressed in this version:
(i) the sign of
p(\varepsilon)
has been corrected
(Definition
3.3
, Remark
3.4
);
(ii) the profile-to-scale transform has been extended to handle
the Gaussian (divergent) case via regularisation
(Definition
3.1
);
(iii) the tail-integral estimate in the proof of Theorem
4.1

has been made rigorous, explaining why substituting an upper bound
on
u^{*}(s)
into a denominator correctly gives an upper bound;
(iv) the finiteness of the supremum
C_{*}
has been established
(Remark
4.2
);
(v) the non-global doubling property of Gaussian measure has been
addressed via localisation (Remark
6.7
);
(vi) the Lipschitz regularity of ball-average mollifiers has been
proved from first principles using the doubling condition alone
(Proposition
2.15
(a));
(vii) the monotonicity of
p(\varepsilon)
used in Theorem
5.1

has been established (Lemma
3.5
).

Concrete non-Euclidean examples, namely the Heisenberg group
(Section
6.2
),
manifolds with logarithmic volume growth (Section
6.3
),
and the Gaussian setting (Section
6.4
), demonstrate that
the intersection of the two theories studied in

[
5
,
8
,
17
]
is nonempty and nontrivial beyond
the Euclidean case.
It would also be interesting to establish relations with cohomology
of foliations and smooth manifolds
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
,
and integrable models
[
22
]
.

Several directions remain open:
(i) extension to
p>1
using
p
-isoperimetric profiles and grand

L^{p}
-Sobolev scales;
(ii) tensorization estimates for product spaces

(X\times Y,d_{\mathrm{prod}},\mu\otimes\nu)
;
(iii) nonlocal (fractional) perimeters and the corresponding
grand Sobolev spaces of negative order;
(iv) sharp constants in non-Euclidean settings via
symmetrization analogues on stratified Lie groups.
One might expect certain applications in theoretical and mathematical
physics, in particular in Wigner-Weyl calculus

[
4
,
25
]

and momentum space topological invariants

[
26
,
27
]
.
