Plancherel Identities for unbounded subsets of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}
Piyali Chakraborty, Dorin Ervin Dutkay

Abstract
. We present a class of pairs of subsets of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} for which the Fourier transform, when restricted to these subsets, is an isometric isomorphism, and thus the Plancherel identity is satisfied. The sets are invariant under translations by dual full-rank lattices.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
47F05,47A10,42A38,52C22

1.
Introduction

Fuglede’s conjecture originated in a question posed by Irving Segal concerning the partial differential operators

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}

defined on an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The question asks: under what conditions do these operators admit commuting self-adjoint extensions on
L^{2}(\Omega)
? In
[
4
]
, Fuglede proved that for finite-measure domains
\Omega
with some regularity restrictions, there are such extensions if and only if there are orthogonal bases of exponential functions
\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
for
L^{2}(\Omega)
, where

e_{\lambda}(x)=e^{2\pi i\lambda\cdot x}.

He also proved that even for some nice domains like the disk or a triangle, this condition is
not
satisfied. It is satisfied, of course, by the unit cube, with the classical Fourier series.

Definition 1.1
.

A measurable set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure is called
spectral
if there exists a set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the family of exponential functions

\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

forms an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\Omega)
,
e_{\lambda}(x)=e^{2\pi i\lambda\cdot x}
. In this case,
\Lambda
is called a
spectrum
for
\Omega
.

We say that
\Omega

tiles

\mathbb{R}^{d}
by translations if there exists a set
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the translations
\{\Omega+t:t\in\mathcal{T}\}
form a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
up to measure zero. The set
\mathcal{T}
is then called a
tiling set
for
\Omega
, and we say that
\Omega

tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the set

\mathcal{T}
.

If only the condition that the sets
\Omega+t
,
t\in\mathcal{T}
are mutually disjoint (up to measure zero) is satisfied, (so the union
\cup_{t}(\Omega+t)
is not necessarily the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), then we say that
\Omega

packs
with
\mathcal{T}
.

Fuglede then formulated his conjecture:

Conjecture 1.2
.

[Fuglede’s Conjecture]

A measurable subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure is spectral if and only if it tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by translations.

In the same paper, Fuglede proved that his conjecture is true when the spectrum or the tiling set is a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite measure and let
A
be some invertible
d\times d
matrix. Then
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the lattice
A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
if and only if
\Omega
has spectrum the dual lattice
(A^{T})^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

The conjecture was later shown to be false in dimensions
d\geq 3

[
13
,
3
]
, but it is true for convex domains
[
9
]
.

In
[
11
]
, Steen Pedersen extended Fuglede’s result by removing both the regularity condition and the finite-measure assumption. Naturally, the definition of a spectral set had to be adapted for infinite-measure domains (since
e_{\lambda}
is not square-integrable in that case).

Definition 1.4
.

For a function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, define its classical Fourier transform as

\hat{f}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-2\pi it\cdot x}\,dx,\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable and let
\mu
be a positive Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that
(\Omega,\mu)
is a
spectral pair
if:
(1) for each
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)
, the continuous function
t\mapsto\hat{f}(t)
satisfies
\int|\hat{f}|^{2}\,d\mu<\infty
; and
(2) the mapping
f\mapsto\hat{f}
from
L^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
into
L^{2}(\mu)
is isometric and has dense range.

This map then extends by continuity to an isometric isomorphism

\mathscr{F}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\mu).

The isometry property yields a Plancherel identity obtained by taking the Fourier transform of functions in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, restricting it to the support of the measure
\mu
and integrating the absolute value squared of this restriction of the Fourier transform against the measure
\mu
.

The set
\Omega
is called
spectral
if there exists a measure
\mu
such that
(\Omega,\mu)
forms a spectral pair;
\mu
is then called a
pair measure
or a
dual measure
for
\Omega
. When
\Omega
has finite measure, this definition coincides with the earlier one
[
11
, Corollary 1.11]
, and the pair measure
\mu
is the counting measure on the spectrum
\Lambda
.

Denote by
\textup{Leb}_{\Omega}
the Lebesgue measure restricted to a subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In
[
1
]
, we presented a class of unbounded sets in
\mathbb{R}
that are spectral:

Theorem 1.5
.

[
1
, Theorem 1.8]

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}
and let
a\in\mathbb{N}
,
a\geq 2
. Then
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,1,\dots,a-1\}
if and only if
\Omega
is a spectral set with pair measure
\mu
equal to a renormalized Lebesgue measure on
\left[-\frac{1}{2a},\frac{1}{2a}\right]+\mathbb{Z}
,
\mu=a\textup{Leb}_{\left[-\frac{1}{2a},\frac{1}{2a}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}
.

In this paper we extend this theorem to higher dimensions. Our main results are

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
be a bounded open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite measure, let
\Lambda_{0}
be a discrete subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
A
be some invertible
d\times d
matrix. Assume in addition that

(1.1)

The set
\Omega_{0}
packs with the lattice
A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

and

(1.2)

\lambda_{0}\cdot Ak\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda_{0}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, equivalently
\Lambda_{0}\subseteq(A^{T})^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\Omega_{0}
has spectrum
\Lambda_{0}
.

(ii)

The set

\Omega=\Omega_{0}+A\mathbb{Z}^{d}

is spectral with pair measure

\mu:=\frac{|\det A|}{|\Omega_{0}|}\textup{Leb}_{(A^{T})^{-1}[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}+\Lambda_{0}}.

(iii)

For the set
\Omega=\Omega_{0}+A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the restriction of the Fourier transform

L^{2}(\Omega)\ni f\to\hat{f}|_{(A^{T})^{-1}[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}+\Lambda_{0}}\in L^{2}(\mu)

is an isometry.

As a fairly easy corollary, we obtain a generalization of Theorem
1.5
:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
A
be an invertible
d\times d
integer matrix, and let
\mathcal{R}
be a complete set of representatives
\operatorname{mod}A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Assume in addition that the sets
\Omega_{0}
packs with the lattice
A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and define the set

\Omega:=\Omega_{0}+A\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathcal{R}
.

(ii)

\Omega_{0}
tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and hence
|\Omega_{0}|=1
.

(iii)

\Omega
is spectral with pair measure

\mu:=\frac{|\det A|}{|\Omega_{0}|}\textup{Leb}_{(A^{T})^{-1}[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}+\mathbb{Z}^{d}}.

(iv)

The Fourier transform
f\to\hat{f}
, from
L^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)
into
L^{2}(\mu)
(with
\mu
as above) extends to an isometry from
L^{2}(\Omega)
into
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Note that our sets
\Omega
are invariant under translations by elements from the lattice
A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
; in other words, any element of the lattice
A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a period for
\Omega
(see Definition
2.2
). In section
4
, we show that, in dimension 1, if a set
\Omega
tiles with a finite set, then
\Omega
has to be periodic (Theorem
4.1
). If
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,1,\dots,a-1\}
for some integer
a\geq 2
, then
a
is a period for
\Omega
(see the Proof of Theorem
1.5
). Also, if the cardinality of the tiling set
\mathcal{T}
is a prime number
p
, and the greatest common divisor of the elements of
\mathcal{T}
is 1, then
\Omega
has period
p
(Theorem
4.3
).

The paper is structured as follows: in Section
2
, we introduce some definitions, notations, and some lemmas needed for the proofs of our main results; see in particular Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
2.8
, which might be of independent interest. In Section
3
, we present the proofs of our main results. In Section 4, we show that, in dimension 1, the subsets of
\mathbb{R}
that tile with a finite set have periodicity properties. In Section
5
, we present some examples, and we make connections to fundamental domains of lattices and self-affine tiles.

2.
Preliminaries

We begin with some definitions and notations.

For a measurable set
\Omega_{0}
,
|\Omega_{0}|
denotes its Lebesgue measure. For a finite set
F
,
|F|
denotes its cardinality.

For a finite or countable set
F
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
\Sha_{F}
the Dirac comb on
F
, i.e., the counting measure on
F
.

When
F
is finite, we say that
F
has spectrum
\Lambda
if the measure
\Sha_{F}
has spectrum
\Lambda
, see Definition
2.3
below.

For a Lebesgue measurable set
E
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
\textup{Leb}_{E}
the restriction of the Lebesgue measure to the set
E
.

For two finite or countable sets
F_{1},F_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we use the notation
F_{1}\oplus F_{2}
to indicate that each point
\lambda
in
F_{1}+F_{2}
can be written
uniquely
as
\lambda=f_{1}+f_{2}
with
f_{1}\in F_{1}
and
f_{2}\in F_{2}
.

If
\Omega
is a measurable subset in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{T}
is discrete, we write
\Omega\oplus\mathcal{T}
to indicate that the sets
\Omega+t
,
t\in\mathcal{T}
are disjoint, up to measure zero. In other words, for a.e.,
y\in\Omega+\mathcal{T}
there exist
unique

x\in\Omega
and
t\in\mathcal{T}
such that
y=x+t
.

For a full-rank lattice
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a
fundamental domain
is a measurable subset
F
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the lattice
\Gamma
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega
be measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{T}
a discrete subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that
\Omega_{0}

tiles
\Omega
by
\mathcal{T}
if
\Omega_{0}\oplus\mathcal{T}=\Omega
(up to Lebesgue measure zero).

Let
B_{0}
and
B
be subsets of a countable Abelian group
G
and
\mathcal{T}\subseteq G
. We say that
B_{0}

tiles

B
by
\mathcal{T}
if
B_{0}\oplus\mathcal{T}=B
.

Definition 2.2
.

A subset
B
in a countable Abelian group
G
is called
periodic
if there exists an element
g\in G
,
g\neq e
such that
g+B=B
; in this case
g
is called a
period
for
B
. The periods of
B
together with identity element
e
form a subgroup of
G
called the
subgroup of periods of
B
.

A measurable subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called
periodic
if there exists an element
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
p\neq 0
, such that
\Omega+p=\Omega
, up to measure zero. If
A
is a
d\times d
invertible real matrix, we say that
\Omega
has period lattice
A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
if
Ak
is a period for
\Omega
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
.

For a measurable subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some countable subset
\Gamma
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we say that
\Omega
is
\Gamma
-invariant
if
\Omega+\gamma\subseteq\Omega
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
, up to measure zero.

For an invertible
d\times d
integer matrix
A
, a
complete set of representatives (or residues)
\operatorname{mod}A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, is a set
\mathcal{R}
contained in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, there exists a unique
r\in\mathcal{R}
such that
k-r\in A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Let
A
be an invertible
d\times d
real matrix. We say that two measurable subsets
E
and
F
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
congruent

\operatorname{mod}A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, if there exist partitions
\{E_{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
of
E
, and
\{F_{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
of
F
such that

F_{k}=E_{k}+Ak,\quad(k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}),

up to measure zero.

For a measurable set
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a positive Radon measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,\mu}
the restriction the map from
L^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)
into
L^{2}(\mu)
, defined by the Fourier transform

\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,\mu}f=\hat{f},

as long as it is well-defined.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mu
be a finite positive Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that
\mu
is a
spectral measure
, if there exists a subset
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the family of exponential functions

\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

forms an orthogonal basis for the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mu)
. In this case,
\Lambda
is called a
spectrum
for the measure
\mu
.

The next lemma is well known, see e.g.
[
5
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
\mu
is a finite positive Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with total measure
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})=m
, then
\mu
has spectrum
\Lambda
if and only if

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\hat{\mu}(t+\lambda)|^{2}=m^{2}.

Proof.

Suppose
\Omega
has a spectrum
\Lambda.
Then

\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mu)
. Applying the Parseval identity to the function
e_{-t}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we obtain

m=\|e_{-t}\|^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\left\langle e_{-t}\,,\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}e_{\lambda}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right|^{2}

=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i(t+\lambda)\cdot u}d\mu(u)|^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\hat{\mu}(t+\lambda)\right|^{2}

and therefore

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\hat{\mu}(t+\lambda)\right|^{2}=m^{2}.

Conversely, let

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\hat{\mu}(t+\lambda)|^{2}=m^{2}.

Substitute
t=-\lambda_{0}
and since
\hat{\mu}(0)=\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})=m
, we get that
\hat{\mu}(-\lambda_{0}+\lambda)=0
for
\lambda\neq\lambda_{0}
, and this proves that the exponentials
e_{\lambda}
and
e_{\lambda_{0}}
are orthogonal. Also,
\lvert\lvert e_{-t}\rvert\lvert^{2}=m
and

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\left\langle e_{-t}\,,\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}e_{\lambda}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i(t+\lambda)\cdot u}d\mu(u)|^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\hat{\mu}(t+\lambda)\right|^{2}=m,

which shows that

\|e_{-t}\|^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\left\langle e_{-t}\,,\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}e_{\lambda}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right|^{2}.

On the right hand side we have the norm squared of the projection of
e_{-t}
onto the closed span of the functions
\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
. This shows that
e_{-t}
is in this closed span. By the Stone-Weierstrass theorem and by approximation by continuous compactly supported functions, the functions
e_{-t}
span the entire space
L^{2}(\mu)
. It follows that
\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
also span the entire space
L^{2}(\mu)
and therefore they form an orthogonal basis for it.

∎

The next lemma shows that we can convolute a spectral measure with a spectral finite set to obtain a new spectral measure whose spectrum is the direct sum of the two spectra.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
F
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and suppose the measure
\Sha_{F}
has spectrum
\Lambda_{1}
. Let
\mu_{2}
be a finite positive Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with spectrum
\Lambda_{2}
. Assume in addition that

(2.1)

f\cdot\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{Z},\mbox{ for all }f\in F\mbox{ and }\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{2}.

Then the convolution measure
\Sha_{F}\ast\mu_{2}
has spectrum
\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}
.

Proof.

Note first that, for
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{2}
, with (
2.1
),

\hat{\Sha}_{F}(t+\lambda_{2})=\sum_{f\in F}e^{-2\pi if\cdot(t+\lambda_{2})}=\sum_{f\in F}e^{-2\pi if\cdot t}=\hat{\Sha}_{F}(t).

Using Lemma
2.4
we compute:

\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda_{1}}\sum_{\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{2}}\left|\widehat{\Sha_{F}\ast\mu_{2}}(t+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})\right|^{2}

=\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda_{1}}\sum_{\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{2}}\hat{\Sha}_{F}(t+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})|^{2}|\hat{\mu}_{2}(t+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})|^{2}

=\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda_{1}}|\hat{\Sha}_{F}(t+\lambda_{1})|^{2}\sum_{\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{2}}|\hat{\mu}_{2}(t+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})|^{2}

=\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda_{1}}|\hat{\Sha}_{F}(t+\lambda_{1})|^{2}\mu_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}=|A|^{2}\mu_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}=(\Sha_{F}\ast\mu_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{2}.

This proves that
\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2}
is a spectrum for the convolution.

To show that the sum
\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}
is direct, assume now that
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}^{\prime}+\lambda_{2}^{\prime}
for some
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2}^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{2}
,
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\neq(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})
. Take
t=-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
in the previous sequence of equalities. Then, the sum on the left of the equalities above, contains the terms

|\widehat{\Sha_{F}\ast\mu_{2}}(t+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})|^{2}+|\widehat{\Sha_{F}\ast\mu_{2}}(t+\lambda_{1}^{\prime}+\lambda_{2}^{\prime})|^{2}=2|\widehat{\Sha_{F}\ast\mu_{2}}(0)|^{2}=2|\Sha_{F}\ast\mu_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})|^{2},

and this yields a contradiction.

∎

The next two lemmas show that translation of a pair measure is another pair measure for the same set, and if we dilate a spectral set, then the dilated set has spectrum the contraction of the pair measure with the inverse transpose of the dilation matrix.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mu
be a positive Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the translation measure

T_{t}\mu(E)=\mu(E+t),\mbox{ for Borel subsets $E$ of $\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

Let
\Omega
a measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If the Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,\mu}
extends to an isometric/onto map between
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
L^{2}(\mu)
, then so does
\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,T_{t}\mu}
. In particular, if
\mu
is a pair measure for
\Omega
then
T_{t}\mu
is also a pair measure for
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that, for a Borel set
E
,

\int\chi_{E}\,dT_{t}\mu=T_{t}\mu(E)=\mu(E+t)=\int\chi_{E+t}\,d\mu=\int\chi_{E}(x-t)\,d\mu(x),

and so

\int f\,dT_{t}\mu=\int f(x-t)\,d\mu(x),\quad(f\in L^{1}(T_{t}\mu)).

Define the operator
\tilde{T}_{t}
from
L^{2}(\mu)
to
L^{2}(T_{t}\mu)
by

\tilde{T}_{t}h(x)=h(x+t),\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},h\in L^{2}(\mu)).

This is an isometric isomorphism.

Define the modulation operator
\mathscr{M}_{t}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
:

\mathscr{M}_{t}f=e_{-t}f,\quad(f\in L^{2}(\Omega)).

This is also an isometric isomophism.

A simple computation shows that

\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,T_{t}\mu}\circ\mathscr{M}_{t}=\tilde{T}_{t}\circ\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,\mu},

i.e., the following diagram is commutative:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\mu)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(T_{t}\mu)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{M}_{t}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,\mu}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,T_{t}\mu}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{T}_{t}}

It follows that
\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,T_{t}\mu}
is isometric/onto if
\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,\mu}
is.

∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mu
be a positive Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
M
be an invertible
d\times d
real matrix. Define the dilation measure
D_{M}\mu
by

\int g\,dD_{M}\mu=\frac{1}{|\det M|}\int g((M^{T})^{-1}x)d\mu(x),\mbox{ whenever the function }x\to g((M^{T})^{-1}x)\mbox{ is in }L^{1}(\mu).

Let
\Omega
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If the Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,\mu}
extends to an isometric/onto map between
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
L^{2}(\mu)
, then so does
\mathscr{F}_{M\Omega,D_{M}\mu}
. If
\Omega
is spectral with pair measure
\mu
, then
M\Omega
is spectral with pair measure
D_{M}\mu
.

Proof.

Define the operator
D_{M}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(M\Omega)
by

D_{M}f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\det M|}}f(Mx),\quad(x\in M\Omega,f\in L^{2}(\Omega)).

D_{M}
is an isometric isomorphism, as can be seen by a change of variable.

We also denote by
\tilde{D}_{M}
the operator from
L^{2}(\mu)
to
L^{2}(D_{M}\mu)
, defined by

\tilde{D}_{M}h(x)=\sqrt{|\det M|}h(M^{T}x),\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},h\in L^{2}(\mu)).

A quick computation shows that

\mathscr{F}_{M\Omega,D_{M}\mu}\circ D_{M}=\tilde{D}_{M}\circ\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,\mu},

i.e., the diagram is commutative:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(M\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(\mu)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(D_{M}\mu)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D_{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,\mu}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F}_{M\Omega,D_{M}\mu}}

The next lemma shows that if some renormalized Lebesgue measures are in duality (actually just the isometry property is enough), and the pair measure has a tiling property, then the normalization constant

is completely determined by the tiling property, and it has to be equal to the cardinality of the tiling set

.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\Omega
and
\tilde{\Omega}
be measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

some positive constant. Let
\mu
be
c\cdot\textup{Leb}|_{\tilde{\Omega}}
. Assume that the Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,\mu}
extends to an isometry from

to

. Assume in addition that
\tilde{\Omega}
tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a finite set

Proof.

By Lemma
2.6
, we have that
\mathscr{F}_{\Omega,T_{-f}\mu}
also extends to an isometry, for any

. Note that
T_{-f}\mu=c\textup{Leb}|_{\tilde{\Omega}+f}
.

Take now some non-zero function

\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\hat{\varphi}|^{2}=\sum_{f\in F}\int_{\tilde{\Omega}+f}|\hat{\varphi}|^{2}=\sum_{f\in F}\frac{1}{c}\int|\hat{\varphi}|^{2}\,dT_{-f}\mu

\mathcal{T}_{t}
,

∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose
\Omega
is a Lebesgue measurable set which tiles
\mathbb{R}
with a subset

\mathbb{Z}
which has the following properties:
|B|=p
prime,
0\in B

\gcd(B)=1
. Then
B
is a complete set of representatives modulo
p
and
\Omega
has period
p
.

Proof.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Denote

\mathcal{T}_{t}=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:t+n\in\Omega\}.

As we saw in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we have that
B\oplus\mathcal{T}_{t}=\mathbb{Z}
.

We need a lemma (see also
[
14
, Theorem 2]
):

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
B\oplus\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{Z}
,
|B|=p
prime,
0\in B\cap\mathcal{T}
,
\gcd(B)=1
. Then
\mathcal{T}=p\mathbb{Z}
and
B
is a complete set of representatives modulo
p
.

Proof.

We know from
[
10
]
that
\mathcal{T}
has to be periodic and let
N
be its minimal period. Then
\mathcal{T}=C\oplus N\mathbb{Z}
and
B\oplus C=\mathbb{Z}_{N}
,
0\in C
, for some finite set
C\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
.

We use the following theorem:

Theorem 4.5
.

[
12
, page 69]

If
B\oplus C=\mathbb{Z}_{N}
and
|B|=p^{\lambda}
for some prime number
p
and some
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, then either
B
or
C
is periodic.

Since
|B|=p
is prime, by Theorem
4.5
, it follows that either
B
or
C
is periodic in
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. However
C
cannot be periodic, because that would contradict the fact that
N
is the minimal period of
\mathcal{T}
. Thus
B
has to be periodic. Let
H
be the subgroup of periods of
B
, in
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. Then
B
is a union of cosets of
H
and therefore the order of
H
has to divide the cardinality of
B
, which is
p
. Thus
|H|=|B|=p
and, since
0\in B
,
B=H
. But, the only subgroup of order
p
of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
is
\{0,N/p,2N/p,\dots,(p-1)N/p\}
, and so
B=\{0,N/p,2N/p,\dots,(p-1)N/p\}
modulo
N
. But then, all elements in
B
are divisible by
N/p
, and since
\gcd(B)=1
, we must have
N=p
. Finally, this implies that
|C|=1
and so
C=\{0\}
, therefore
\mathcal{T}=p\mathbb{Z}
. Since
B\oplus p\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}
, it follows that
B
is a complete set of representatives modulo
p
.
∎

Consider now

, so
0\in\mathcal{T}_{t}
. With Lemma
4.4
, we obtain that
\mathcal{T}_{t}=p\mathbb{Z}
. This means

for all

. This shows that
\Omega+p\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\Omega
and the conclusion follows.
∎

Example 5.1
.

Here is an example of a set
\Omega
which tiles by the complete set of representatives modulo 2
\mathcal{R}=\{0,3\}
, but is not invariant under addition by 2,
\Omega+2\neq\Omega
,

\Omega=(0,3)+6\mathbb{Z}.

We can apply Theorem
1.6
with
\Omega_{0}=(0,3)
with spectrum
\Lambda_{0}=\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{Z}
and
A=6
. We obtain that
\Omega
is spectral with pair measure
2\textup{Leb}_{[-\frac{1}{12},\frac{1}{12}]+\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{Z}}
.

Note that this set
\Omega=(0,3)+6\mathbb{Z}
does not have as pair measure the renormalized Lebesgue measure on
[-1/4,1/4]+\mathbb{Z}
, as
\Omega^{\prime}=(0,1)+2\mathbb{Z}
does (Theorem
1.5
), even though both these sets tile
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,3\}
.

Indeed, if we take the characteristic function
\chi_{(0,1)}
and its translation
\chi_{(1,2)}
, we have
\left\langle\chi_{(0,1)}\,,\,\chi_{(1,2)}\right\rangle=0
. Assuming that the Fourier transform is isometric from
L^{2}(\Omega)
into
L^{2}(2\textup{Leb}_{[-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}]+\mathbb{Z}})
, we would get that

\left\langle\hat{\chi}_{(0,1)}\,,\,e^{-2\pi it}\hat{\chi}_{(0,1)}\right\rangle=0
.

We have

\hat{\chi}_{(0,1)}(t)=e^{-\pi it}\sinc(\pi t)=e^{-\pi it}\frac{\sin\pi t}{\pi t},\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}).

Since
\mathbb{Z}
is a spectrum for
(0,1)
, with Lemma
2.4
we have that

1=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\hat{\chi}_{(0,1)}(t+k)|^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sinc^{2}(\pi(t+k)),\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}).

Then

0=\left\langle\hat{\chi}_{(0,1)}\,,\,e^{-2\pi it}\hat{\chi}_{[0,1]}\right\rangle=\int_{[-1/4,1/4]+\mathbb{Z}}e^{2\pi it}\sinc^{2}(\pi t)\,dt=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{-1/4}^{1/4}e^{2\pi i(t+k)}\sinc^{2}(\pi(t+k))\,dt

=\int_{-1/4}^{1/4}e^{2\pi it}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sinc^{2}(\pi(t+k))\,dt=\int_{-1/4}^{1/4}e^{2\pi it}\,dt=\frac{e^{2\pi i/4}-e^{-2\pi i/4}}{2\pi i}\neq 0,

a contradiction. Thus
2\textup{Leb}_{[-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}]+\mathbb{Z}}
is not a pair measure for
(0,3)+6\mathbb{Z}
.

We generalize the idea in Example
5.1
into a lemma, which gives a necessary condition for a measure to be a pair measure for a set
\Omega
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a spectral measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with pair measure of the form
\nu_{0}\ast\Sha_{\Lambda_{0}}
where
\nu_{0}
is a finite positive Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Lambda_{0}
is a countable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assume in addition, that there is a subset
E
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite measure and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the following properties:

(i)

Both
E
and
E+a
are subsets of
\Omega
.

(ii)

The set
E
has spectrum
\Lambda_{E}
, and
\Lambda_{E}
tiles
\Lambda_{0}
with a finite set
F
.

(iii)

\lambda_{E}\cdot a\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
\lambda_{E}\in\Lambda_{E}
.

Then

\frac{|E\cap(E+a)|}{|E|^{2}}=\hat{\nu}_{0}(a)\sum_{f\in F}e^{-2\pi ia\cdot f}.

Proof.

We have

|E\cap(E+a)|=\left\langle\chi_{E+a}\,,\,\chi_{E}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left\langle\hat{\chi}_{E+a}\,,\,\hat{\chi}_{E}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\nu_{0}\ast\Sha_{\Lambda_{0}})}=\int e^{-2\pi ia\cdot x}|\hat{\chi}_{E}(x)|^{2}\,d\nu_{0}\ast\Sha_{\Lambda_{0}}(x)

=\int\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{0}}e^{-2\pi ia\cdot(t+\lambda)}|\hat{\chi}_{E}(t+\lambda)|^{2}\,d\nu_{0}(t)=\int\sum_{f\in F}\sum_{\lambda_{E}\in\Lambda_{E}}e^{-2\pi ia\cdot(t+f+\lambda_{E})}|\hat{\chi}_{E}(t+f+\lambda_{E})|^{2}\,d\nu_{0}(t)

=\int\sum_{f\in F}e^{-2\pi ia\cdot(t+f)}\sum_{\lambda_{E}\in\Lambda_{E}}|\hat{\chi}_{E}(t+f+\Lambda_{E})|^{2}\,d\nu_{0}(t)

but, with Lemma
2.4
,
\sum_{\lambda_{E}}|\chi_{E}(t+f+\lambda_{E})|^{2}=|E|^{2}
, so we get further

=|E|^{2}\int\sum_{f\in F}e^{-2\pi ia\cdot(t+f)}\,d\nu_{0}(t)=|E|^{2}\sum_{f\in F}e^{-2\pi ia\cdot f}\int e^{-2\pi ia\cdot t}\,d\nu_{0}(f)

=|E|^{2}\hat{\nu}_{0}(a)\sum_{f\in F}e^{-2\pi ia\cdot f}.

The conclusion follows.

∎

Example 5.6

A particularly appealing class of examples of sets

is associated to a expansive

(expansive means all eigenvalues have absolute value strictly greater than 1), and a set of digits
\mathcal{D}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
|\mathcal{D}|=|\det R|

that satisfies the equality

R(T)=\bigcup_{d\in\mathcal{D}}(T+d),

which is given explicitly by

T(R,\mathcal{D}):=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}R^{-k}d_{k}:\mbox{ all }d_{k}\in\mathcal{D}\right\}.

The set
T(R,\mathcal{D})
is called a
self-affine tile
if it has positive Lebesgue measure.

Associated to an integral pair
(R,\mathcal{D})
, is the smallest
R

\mathbb{Z}[R,\mathcal{D}]
of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that contains the difference set
\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{D}

\mathbb{Z}[R,\mathcal{D}]=\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{D},R(\mathcal{D}),\dots,R^{d-1}\mathcal{D}].

A pair

is called
primitive
if
\mathbb{Z}[R,\mathcal{D}]=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{D}
is called a
primitive digit set
for
R
.

The study can be reduced to primitive digit sets: there is another integral self-affine tile
\tilde{T}=T(\tilde{R},\tilde{D})
with
(\tilde{R},\tilde{D})
primitive and
0\in\tilde{D}
, such that for some integer
d\times d
matrix
B
(whose columns form a basis for
\mathbb{Z}[R,\mathcal{D}]
) with
\det B\neq 0
, and some
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

T=B(\tilde{T})+v.

A primitive digit set is called
standard
if it forms a complete set of residues (
\operatorname{mod}R
).

Theorem 1.1 in
[
8
]
states that:

Theorem 5.7
.

Every integral self-affine tile
T
coming from a standard digit set tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by some lattice
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

We can construct our examples for Theorem
1.6
as follows: let
\Omega_{0}:=T(R,\mathcal{D})=T
for a standard digit set. Let
\tilde{\Gamma}
be any full-rank sublattice of
\Gamma
; let
A
be a matrix such that
A\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\tilde{\Gamma}
. Since
\Omega_{0}
tiles with the lattice
\Gamma
, it packs with the lattice
\tilde{\Gamma}
. Also, by Fuglede’s Theorem
1.3
,
\Omega_{0}
has spectrum
\Lambda_{0}=\Gamma^{*}
, the dual lattice of
\Gamma
,

\Gamma^{*}=\left\{\gamma^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\gamma^{*}\cdot\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}\mbox{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma\right\}=(A^{T})^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Since
\tilde{\Gamma}\subseteq\Gamma
we also have
\lambda_{0}\cdot\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda_{0}=\Gamma^{*}
, and
\tilde{\gamma}\in\tilde{\Gamma}
.

Note also that
|\det A|
is the index of
\tilde{\Gamma}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
[\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\tilde{\Gamma}]
. As,
\Omega_{0}
tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\Gamma
, using Lemma
2.9
, we get that
|\Omega_{0}|=[\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\Gamma]
. Thus

\frac{|\det A|}{|\Omega_{0}|}=\frac{[\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\tilde{\Gamma}]}{[\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\Gamma]}=[\Gamma:\tilde{\Gamma}].

Thus, we can apply Theorem
1.6
and obtain that the set
\Omega=\Omega_{0}+\tilde{\Gamma}
is spectral with pair measure

[\Gamma:\tilde{\Gamma}]\textup{Leb}_{(A^{T})^{-1}[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}+\Gamma^{*}}

Note that, if we take
\mathcal{R}
to be a complete set of representatives of
\tilde{\Gamma}
in
\Gamma
, then

\Omega\oplus\mathcal{R}=\Omega_{0}\oplus\tilde{\Gamma}\oplus\mathcal{R}=\Omega_{0}+\Gamma=\mathbb{R}^{d},

so
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\mathcal{R}
. In particular, if
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then Theorem
1.7
can be used.
