 Neodymium and Dysprosium Quantification
Rare Earth Elements (Nd, Pr, Dy) are primarily embedded in
bonded permanent magnets used in rotating equipment and
precision actuators in servers and storage drives of IT equipment.

 • Servers, storage devices, and networking equipment
 are the primary IT hardware categories driving critical
 mineral demand in data centers.
 • Within servers, rare earth magnets may be found in Source: 2016 LBNL Data Center Report
 cooling fans, Hard Disk Drives (HDD), motors, and
 liquid-cooling pumps, while semiconductors and power
 electronics rely on materials such as gallium and
 copper.
 • Storage systems can vary significantly between HDD-
 heavy architectures and Solid State Drives (SSD)-based
 systems, with hyperscale AI facilities increasingly
 shifting toward SSDs for performance and reliability.

The mix of IT equipment varies by data center type, workload, and server architecture, which ultimately
influences the material intensity per MW or per server deployed.

 Gallium Nitride (GaN) Power Electronics
 Drive Limited Near-Term Demand

• Ga demand from data centers is primarily tied to gradual adoption of GaN power electronics in server PSUs and
  rack-level power shelves.

• Traditional silicon Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor (MOSFETs) remain dominant through 2029,
  but GaN gains share in high-efficiency, high-power-density AI systems.

• Estimations could be reasonably higher if wider adoption of GaN in facility-side UPS systems, rectifiers and more
  aggressive rack-level power architectures.

 Staggering Copper Demand from U.S. Data Centers Growth
• Copper demand for US Data Centers is
  expected to rise with AI-driven infrastructure
  growth, reaching roughly 0.6-1.1 million MT by
  2028, based on LBNL’s data center deployment
  scenarios.

• U.S. currently mines 1.1 million MT of Cu
  annually but has only two primary copper
  smelters and relies on imports for roughly half of
  refined copper. Deficits are covered by imports
  from Chile, Canada and Peru.

• Global refined copper demand is 28 million MT
  as of 2025 and could grow by 40% by 2040.
  Copper mine supply alone may meet less than
  70% of projected 2035 demand. (USGS 2025,

 Copper Estimates in Data Center Servers

• AI server architectures can contain 5–10X
  more copper per server than conventional
  systems, driven primarily by liquid cooling
  and high-current power electronics rather
  than Printed Circuit Boards (PCB) copper.
 Server cooling is a copper hotspot
• Advanced liquid cooling, cold plates,
  Voltage Regulator Module (VRMs), PSU
  windings, and internal busbars become
  dominant copper drivers in hyperscale AI
  systems.

• Copper demand scales faster than server
  count alone and is increasingly linked to AI
  rack power density and cooling strategy. Low and high ranges reflect variation in board count, PSU sizing, VRM density, GPU count,
 cooling design, and use of copper-rich cold plates or busbars. AI servers show the widest range
 because copper content scales strongly with accelerator density and cooling strategy.

 Still Copper Growth is Primarily driven by Sector Growth
 Per MW IT Rather than servers alone

• AI servers increase rack-level copper
intensity, but facility-level copper demand
remains dominated by electrical distribution
and balance-of-plant infrastructure → higher
node power density compresses the server-
side Cu intensity per MW IT.
• Reduction in wiring at rack/server level is
partly offset by heavier electrical distribution
equipment (larger busbars, more transformer
capacity, more cooling).
• Therefore, future copper demand is driven
more by total deployed MW and sector
growth than by AI servers alone.

AI data centers are dramatically more copper-intensive per node and per rack, but on a per annual-average MW basis,
facility-level copper intensity is only modestly above conventional air-cooled halls, and future high-voltage architectures
could further reduce copper density.

 Supply Chain Bottlenecks for Meeting U.S. Copper Demand

U.S. has currently roughly 48 million MT of copper reserves, while
mining only ~1.1 million MT per year (USGS 2026). Proven
reserves could theoretically provide several decades of domestic
supply.

• U.S. exported nearly 957,000 MT of copper scrap to China or its
  intermediaries due to lack of domestic smelting → Losing nearly
  580,000 MT of high-purity copper after refining.
• U.S. lacks enough domestic facilities to process low-grade,
  mixed, contaminated, or alloyed scrap into refined copper
  cathode.
• New investments are needed to develop advanced copper-
  sorting technologies from complex waste streams,
  pyrometallurgical and hydrometallurgical facilities for recovering
  copper, and refining capacity for impure copper into cathode-
  grade copper.

  Copper ore availability is not the primary constraint for the U.S.; smelting, refining, scrap processing,
  value recycling, and downstream manufacturing capacity are the true bottlenecks. National Laboratory of the Rockies | 12

Integrated Decision Support For Critical Minerals Supply Chains
The Benchmarking Life Cycle Environmental, Economic, and Social Metrics
for Critical and Advanced Minerals and Materials (BLEECAMTM) tool is an
integrated decision-support platform that enables U.S. Department of
Energy and industry to evaluate, optimize, and de-risk the deployment of
materials, processes and emerging technologies in critical mineral supply
chains across all life cycle stages.
• BLEECAMTM integrates dynamic supply chain optimization, life cycle
 assessment, technoeconomic- and life cycle costing analysis, and
 social life cycle assessment in a single platform.

• Evaluates the impacts of emerging materials and technologies before
 large-scale deployment, including new processing routes, recycling
 pathways, substitutions, and domestic supply chain expansion.

• Designed to inform decisions across sectors including energy
 generation, data centers, and grid infrastructure.

• Current applications include rare earths, permanent magnets, gallium,
 copper, AI data centers, grid infrastructure and recycling pathways.

 Key Takeaways
• AI data centers are becoming a major new source of demand for copper, rare earths,
 gallium, batteries, and power electronics.

• Copper is likely the dominant near-term bottleneck because demand is driven more by
 power delivery, cooling, transformers, and grid infrastructure than by servers alone

• The main U.S. challenge is not resource availability, but limited refining, recycling,
 transformer manufacturing, and midstream processing capacity.

• Higher-voltage systems, liquid cooling, aluminum substitution, and better power
 electronics can reduce copper intensity, but not eliminate the need for major copper
 buildout.

• Recycling, reuse, domestic processing, and allied manufacturing will all be needed to
 meet future demand.

• BLEECAM shows that under a Dy supply shock, the fastest near-term response is allied
 expansion, U.S. recycling, and a temporary strategic reserve of finished magnets.

• Labor risks can be reduced at relatively low cost through sourcing decisions, while
 emissions reductions require advanced process technologies rather than rerouting alone.
