LINEAR STABILITY OF THE LAMB-CHAPLYGIN DIPOLE
FRANCESCO PIO NUMERO AND PAOLO VENTURA

Abstract




Abstract.
We describe the linearized dynamics near the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole, a classical traveling solution of the two-dimensional Euler equations. Exploiting the Hamiltonian structure of the system together with its symmetries, we identify all possible sources of linear instability. For general perturbations in
L^{1}\cap L^{p}
,
p>2
, growth can occur only through two explicit mechanisms triggered by: (i) a nonzero circulation on the core of the dipole, and (ii) a nontrivial component along the generalized eigenvectors associated with the eigenvalue
0
. In particular, we completely classify the spectrum and the Jordan chains of the operator associated with the linear dynamics. Both mechanisms hint for a nonlinear dynamics that may drift along the symmetry-generated family of traveling dipoles without moving away from it.

1
Introduction

We consider the two-dimensional Euler equation for an incompressible, inviscid fluid, in vorticity formulation

\partial_{t}\omega+\mathrm{div}(u[\omega]\omega)=0,\qquad t\geq 0,\,\,x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(1.1)

where
\diver(v_{1},v_{2}):=\partial_{x_{1}}v_{1}+\partial_{x_{2}}v_{2}
.
The velocity
u[\omega]
of the fluid is determined by the usual Biot-Savart Fourier multiplier as follows

u[\omega]:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[-\frac{\mathrm{i}\,\xi^{\perp}}{|\xi|^{2}}\mathcal{F}[\omega](\xi)\right],\quad\xi^{\perp}:=(-\xi_{2},\xi_{1}),

(1.2)

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}[f]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)e^{\mathrm{i}\,x\cdot\xi}{\rm d}x
.

We are interested in the stability of (
1.1
) near traveling solutions, namely profiles that evolve in time by pure translation and without changing shape. We thus introduce the traveling frame

\begin{cases}x^{\prime}=x+\mathbf{c}t,\quad\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
t^{\prime}=t.\end{cases}

In these variables, equation (
1.1
) becomes

\partial_{t}\omega=-\mathrm{div}((u[\omega]+\mathbf{c})\omega)\,.

(1.3)

It is a classical result of Yudovich
[
26
]
that, for initial data

\omega_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the Cauchy problem
for (
1.1
) (equivalently for (
1.3
)) admits a unique global solution in the same class. The existence argument can be extended to initial data in the broader
\omega_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
p>2
.

A well-known steady solution of (
1.3
) is the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole, given by –in polar coordinates–

\omega_{eq}(r,\theta)=\begin{cases}AJ_{1}(kr)\sin(\theta)\quad{\rm{in}}\ {\rm D},\\
0\quad{\rm{in}}\ \mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{\rm D},\end{cases}\quad{\bf c}_{eq}:=(1,0),

(1.4)

where
{\rm D}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|x|\leq 1\}
is the unit disk and
A:=\frac{2k}{J_{0}(k)}
. Here
J_{i}=J_{i}(r)
,
i\in\{0,1,\dots\}
, are the Bessel functions of the first kind and
k=3.8317\dots
is the first nontrivial zero of
J_{1}
. We observe that, inside the unit disk
\rm D
, one has the functional relation

\omega_{eq}=-k^{2}\psi_{eq},

(1.5)

where
\psi_{eq}
is the stream function of the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole, determined (up to constants) by the relation

\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{eq}=u_{eq},\quad u_{eq}:=u[\omega_{eq}]+{\bf c}_{eq},

(1.6)

with
u_{eq}
being the physical velocity field of the dipole. In formulas

\psi_{eq}(r,\theta)=\begin{cases}-\frac{A}{k^{2}}J_{1}(kr)\sin(\theta)\quad r\leq 1\\
(\frac{1}{r}-r)\sin(\theta)\quad r>1.\end{cases}

(1.7)

Figure 1
:
Streamlines of the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole shown in black. The red and blue regions represent the positive and negative vorticity intensities, respectively.

The Lamb–Chaplygin dipole introduced in (
1.4
) traces back to
the classical works of Chaplygin
[
8
]
and Lamb

[
15
]
; see also
[
19
]
for a historical account. More recently, it has attracted renewed attention by the fluid community.
On one hand, its orbital stability was proved by Abe and Choi

[
1
]
under symmetry, sign and moment assumptions, and later refined
in
[
2
,
16
]
. On the other hand, it has gained a primary role
as building block in the sharp convex-integration construction of
Brué, Colombo, and Kumar
[
6
]
.

A natural question is whether the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole is stable under
arbitrary perturbations in the class
w_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{p}
,
p>2
, with the distance between the perturbed
solution and the steady state measured in the
L^{2}
norm. The present paper takes a step toward answering this question, which, to the best of our knowledge, remains open in such a general setting.

As usual, our starting point in the stability analysis of system (
1.3
) is the study of the linear evolution of the initial datum
\omega_{eq}+\epsilon w_{0}
, with
\epsilon
sufficiently small to discard the terms of order
O(\epsilon^{2})
.
By linearizing (
1.3
) at its steady solution
(\omega_{eq},{\bf c}_{eq})
one obtains the linear system

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}w(t)={\mathcal{L}}_{eq}w(t),\\
w(0)=w_{0},\end{cases}\quad{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}w:=-\diver(u_{eq}w)-\diver(u[w]\omega_{eq}),

(1.8)

with
\omega_{eq}
in (
1.4
) and
u_{eq}
in (
1.6
).

Our first main result is the following

Theorem 1
.

Let
w_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
p>2
, and
\mu:=|{\rm D}|^{-1}\int_{\rm D}w_{0}
be the average of
w_{0}
on the unit disk. The solution
w\in C^{0}\big(\mathbb{R},L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\big)
of the linear problem (
1.8
)
grows at most quadratically in time: there exist
C>c>0
, independent from
w_{0}
, such that

c|\mu|t^{2}-C\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}t\leq\|w(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\big(\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}t+|\mu|t^{2}\big),\quad\text{ for every }t>0.

(1.9)

Moreover there exist initial data
w_{0}
,
with
\mu=0
, for which
\|w(t)\|_{L^{2}}
grows linearly in time.

We emphasize that such a precise polynomial-growth estimate is remarkable in its own right. For an arbitrary generator
{\mathcal{L}}
, eigenvalues with positive real part provide an immediate mechanism for exponential growth of the semigroup
e^{t{\mathcal{L}}}
. However, the absence of such eigenvalues, or even more detailed spectral information, is in general not sufficient to control the semigroup evolution:
Renardy’s PDE counterexample shows that, even for a hyperbolic equation, linear stability cannot in general be inferred from the spectrum alone
[
24
]
. The separation between spectral information and semigroup growth is already apparent in the celebrated Gearhart–Prüss theorem on Hilbert spaces
[
12
,
23
]
(see
[
10
]
for a short elementary proof). Their result shows that subexponential growth of the semigroup is equivalent to a uniform resolvent estimate along the imaginary axis, a requirement much stronger than merely locating the spectrum in the closed left half-plane.
Here we conclude much more than a subexponential growth estimate: we provide a quadratic-in-time bound, whose leading-order
t^{2}
contribution is generated only by the circulation of the initial datum. Moreover, the starting functional setting of our analysis is not Hilbertian. As observed in
[
9
]
, the linear evolution of a semigroup may behave very differently in
L^{1}
and in
L^{2}
, even when the spectrum of the generator is unchanged.

The derivation of estimate (
1.9
) relies on a precise description of the linearized dynamics.
Since the vorticity
\omega_{eq}
of the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole is supported on the unit disk, the linear evolution in (
1.8
) separates neatly into two parts.
Outside the unit disk the perturbation is simply transported by the dipole
flow. Inside the unit disk
{\rm D}
, the initial datum evolves according to a forced linear system:
its homogeneous part is governed by a closed operator

{\mathscr{L}}:{\rm Dom}({\mathscr{L}})\subseteq L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})\to L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
,
while the forcing term is proportional to the circulation of
w_{0}
in
{\rm D}
. Here
L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
is the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions on
{\rm D}
with zero average and

\mathscr{L}:=-(u_{eq}\cdot\nabla)({\rm Id}+k^{2}\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}),

(1.10)

where
\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}
is the inverse Laplace operator with suitable boundary conditions on
\partial{\rm D}
descending from the analysis of the evolution outside the disk, see Section
3.2
. We will be able to convert
the spectral properties
of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}
in (
1.10
), including its Jordan chains, into quantitative information on the dynamics of (
1.8
). The next theorem provides a complete description of this spectral structure.

Before presenting the statement of the theorem, let us recall that a Jordan chain of length
m\geq 2
associated with an eigenvalue
\lambda
of
{\mathscr{L}}
is a set of the form

\{f_{m-1},f_{m-2},\dots,f_{0}\}\subset{\rm Dom}({\mathscr{L}}),

(1.11)

with
{\mathscr{L}}f_{0}=\lambda f_{0}
,
f_{0}\neq 0
, and
{\mathscr{L}}f_{j}=\lambda f_{j}+f_{j-1}
for every
j=1,\dots,m-1
. We also recall that, if
\Re(\lambda)\geq 0
, such a structure entails a growth mechanism for the linear system
\dot{w}={\mathscr{L}}w
. For example, if
\{f_{1},f_{0}\}
is a Jordan chain of length
2
associated with
\lambda
, then
w(t)=e^{t\lambda}(f_{1}+tf_{0})
is a growing solution with initial datum
w(0)=f_{1}
.

Our second main result is the following

Theorem 2
.

Let
{\mathscr{L}}
be the operator introduced in (
1.10
). Then:

1.

{\mathscr{L}}
is spectrally stable, namely

\sigma_{L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})}({\mathscr{L}})\subseteq\mathrm{i}\,\mathbb{R}
, and each of its nonzero eigenvalues is semisimple.

2.

{\mathscr{L}}
is linearly unstable. It has exactly two (up to linear combinations) Jordan chains of length
2
associated with
0
.

Let us comment on Theorems
1
-
2
.

1.

We are able to characterize completely the linear dynamics near the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole, without restricting ourselves to any particular symmetry class of initial data as done in prior literature. The estimate that we show in (
1.9
) is sharp, in the sense that the only sources of growth for a general initial datum
w_{0}
under the linear system (
1.8
) are:

•

a nonzero average
\mu\neq 0
on the disk
{\rm D}
, which gives rise to a quadratic-in-time detachment from the original equilibrium;

•

a nontrivial component along one of the two Jordan chains in the generalized kernel of
{\mathscr{L}}
, yielding a linear-in-time drift.

The proof we present shows as by-product that, without one of the above elements, the evolution
e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}}w_{0}
remains bounded in time, cfr. Lemma
13
.

2.

The mild linear instability that we obtain is a direct consequence of the symmetries preserved by Euler equations. Indeed, as we will show in Section
2
, the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole appears inside a continuum of traveling solutions. The linear growth reflects the possibility for an initial datum to evolve by drifting along this ensemble of steady states. In particular, as we prove in Lemma
3
, the unstable generalized eigenvectors correspond to infinitesimal rotations/amplifications of the Lamb dipole and generate spatial translations of the solution. The latter will then move away from the original steady state at constant speed, while keeping invariant distance with respect to the manifold of Lamb dipoles. On the other hand, as we show in the end of Section
2
, a nontrivial circulation on the unit disk gives rise, under the evolution of the full Euler equations, to an explicit rototranslation of the Lamb dipole. The linearized dynamics retains a trace of this behavior, as reflected in the quadratic-in-time growth displayed in (
1.9
).

3.

Our linear analysis
fits naturally with the previous stability results for the Lamb–Chaplygin
dipole by clarifying the linear mechanisms underlying them. In particular, the
odd-symmetry assumption in
[
1
,
2
]
eliminates both
the average over the unit disk and the generalized unstable direction associated with infinitesimal rotations, namely the obstructions whose absence underlies the
orbital stability mechanism modulo horizontal shifts developed in those works.

The spectral-stability result recently appeared in

[
16
]
, and comparable with point
1
of Theorem
2
, moves in a similar direction.
In that paper, the
authors study the spectrum of the linearized operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
in (
1.8
) directly and
prove the absence of unstable eigenvalues by relying on the general index theory
for Hamiltonian PDEs developed by Lin and Zeng
[
18
]
.
This powerful
framework, designed for a broad class of Hamiltonian problems, is implemented after realizing the operator on suitable weighted
subspaces of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, which provide the additional decay needed to handle
the Biot–Savart operator on the whole plane; see also

[
3
, Remark B.4.1]
.

We also mention the recent work of Wang
[
25
]
, where a truncated
version of the Lamb–Chaplygin dipole is studied as a steady solution of the
Euler equation in a fixed disk, and orbital stability is proved by variational
methods.
in Wang’s disk setting, the truncation fixes the vortical region and removes the
modulation mechanism associated with changes of the amplitude of the
full Lamb–Chaplygin family.

Let us outline the strategy of our proof.

We begin by obtaining, in Section
2
, the generalized eigenvectors of the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
in (
1.8
) associated with
0
by differentiating the symmetries of system (
1.3
). This is the standard mechanism
by which continuous families of equilibria generate neutral directions for the linearized operator. In the present traveling-wave setting, however, some symmetries also change the traveling velocity of the Lamb–Chaplygin dipole; differentiating along these parameters therefore produces generalized eigenvectors, rather than genuine kernel elements. This is exactly the strategy followed in
[
20
]
to obtain, starting from the underlying symmetries of the water waves system, the Jordan chains associated with
0
for Stokes waves that play an essential role also in the proof of the long-wave spectral picture in
[
4
,
5
]
.

The viewpoint that we adopt in Sections
3
is dynamical rather than
purely spectral, in contrast with
[
16
]
. We do not treat
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
as an operator to be analyzed directly
through its spectrum, since, as observed by the authors of the latter paper, in the natural
L^{2}
-setting this operator is not
closed. Instead, we interpret
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
as the generator of a strongly continuous
semigroup and consider the linear evolution associated with
arbitrary initial data in the vorticity class

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
p>2
. Exploiting the compact support of the
vorticity of the Lamb–Chaplygin dipole, we reduce the relevant part of the
dynamics to a suitable
L^{2}
-closed operator
{\mathscr{L}}
on the unit disk. This approach avoids
the use of weighted
L^{2}
spaces and, at the same time,
characterizes completely the sources of growth in the linear evolution, which are shown to
arise solely from the circulation of the initial datum and from the Jordan chains found in Section
2
.

The rest of our proof, presented in Section
3.3
, relies entirely on the Hamiltonianity of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}={\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{H}}
, cfr. (
3.16
), which is itself a remnant of the
Hamiltonian structure of the two-dimensional Euler equations, see
[
21
]
. This point of
view is in line with the previous stability analyses in
[
1
,
2
,
16
]
,
and has been fully exploited in
[
7
]
to study the stability of Taylor-Green vortices. The key point is that the generalized eigenvectors presented in Section
2
already
exhaust the maximal number of generalized unstable directions allowed by the Hamiltonian structure. This rules out both eigenvalues with positive real part
and nontrivial Jordan chains associated with nonzero imaginary eigenvalues. The only substantial computation in the proof of Theorem
2
is the verification of this last point, carried
out in Lemmata
6
and
11
.

Section
3.4
combines the spectral information obtained in the proof of Theorem
2
with the dynamical argument needed to prove Theorem
1
. The main difficulty is that the standard Hamiltonian energy argument cannot be applied directly. Such an argument would require embedding
{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}})
in an
{\mathscr{L}}
-invariant closed subspace
Y
on which
{\mathcal{H}}
is coercive.
One can define
Y
in a standard way as the
L^{2}
-orthogonal to the Jordan chains of
{\mathscr{L}}^{*}=-{\mathcal{H}}{\mathcal{J}}
constructed as the image through
{\mathcal{H}}
of those of
{\mathscr{L}}
. In our problem, one readily finds
one
Jordan chain for
{\mathscr{L}}^{*}
, in contrast with the
two
obtained for
{\mathscr{L}}
in Section
2
, see Remark
8
. This is a usual obstruction in the non-normal infinite-dimensional setting, where the Jordan structure of an operator need not mirror that of its adjoint. Consequently,
{\mathcal{H}}
is not coercive on
Y
and the standard stability argument does not apply.

Nevertheless, we will be able to show that
{\mathcal{H}}
is positive definite on the non-closed subspace
{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}}^{2})
. This is still sufficient to conclude that the evolution of every initial datum in
{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}})
remains bounded in time. As a consequence, a general initial datum in
L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
can grow at most linearly in time. This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

We now pass to the analysis of the symmetries of
(
1.3
) and of the Jordan chains generated by them.

2
The family of Lamb-Chaplygin dipoles

The Lamb-Chaplygin dipole presented in (
1.4
) is not an isolated traveling solution of (
1.1
), since it belongs to a five-parameter family of steady states of (
1.3
) arising from the symmetries of the system. In this section we present the construction of this family of steady solutions of (
1.3
) and its aftermaths on the linear operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
in (
1.8
).

Let us define the vector field
F(\omega,\mathbf{c}):=-\mathrm{div}((u[\omega]+\mathbf{c})\omega)\,
, corresponding to the right hand side of (
1.3
). As one can readily see by inspection, the function
F
in (
1.3
) is invariant under the following transformations, for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
:

(i)

horizontal translation
:
F(\omega(x_{1}+\alpha,x_{2}),\mathbf{c})=F(\omega,\mathbf{c})(x_{1}+\alpha,x_{2})
;

(ii)

vertical translation
:
F(\omega(x_{1},x_{2}+\alpha),\mathbf{c})=F(\omega,\mathbf{c})(x_{1},x_{2}+\alpha)
;

(iii)

rotation
:
F(\omega(R_{\alpha}x),R^{T}_{\alpha}\mathbf{c})=F(\omega,\mathbf{c})(R_{\alpha}x)
for every rotation
R_{\alpha}:=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha)&-\sin(\alpha)\\
\sin(\alpha)&\cos(\alpha)\end{pmatrix}
;

(iv)

amplification
:
F(\alpha\omega(x),\alpha\mathbf{c})=\alpha^{2}F(\omega,\mathbf{c})(\alpha x)
;

(v)

dilation
:
F(\omega(\alpha x),\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{c})=F(\omega,\mathbf{c})(\alpha x)
, if
\alpha\neq 0
.

When applied to the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole, horizontal and vertical translations represent rigid spatial shifts along the
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
axes, respectively, leaving the internal structure, size, and translation velocity
{\rm{\bf c}}
unaffected. On the other hand, symmetries
(iii)
-
(v)
change the traveling velocity of the dipole. More precisely, a rigid rotation pivots the geometric structure and rotates the direction of propagation of the the dipole by the same angle, whereas the dipole propagates
\alpha
times faster across the plane under an
\alpha
-amplification of the vorticity. Finally, a spatial dilation as in
(v)
induces an inverse
\alpha^{-1}
rescaling of the traveling velocity.

To characterize the entire family of equilibria, we introduce the following notation.
Let
\mathcal{G}
be the group of transformations generated by arbitrary compositions of the operations
(i)
-
(v)
. Any element
\tau\in\mathcal{G}
acting on the steady state
(\omega,\bf c)
can be uniquely represented by a set of parameters
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{5})
,
\alpha_{5}\neq 0
, such that

\tau_{\bm{\alpha}}\omega(x):=\alpha_{4}\,\omega\left(\alpha_{5}R_{\alpha_{3}}x+\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right),\qquad\gamma_{\bm{\alpha}}{\bf c}:=\frac{\alpha_{4}}{\alpha_{5}}R_{\alpha_{3}}^{T}\mathbf{c}.

(2.1)

It is straightforward to notice that if
F(\omega,{\bf c})=0
, i.e.
(\omega,\bf c)
is an equilibrium, then
F(\tau_{\bm{\alpha}}\omega,\gamma_{\bm{\alpha}}{\bf c})=0
. This property naturally establishes a continuous family of equilibria whose elements will simply be referred to as Lamb-Chaplygin dipoles in the sequel.

By linearizing (
1.3
) at its steady solution
(\tau_{\bm{\alpha}}\omega_{eq},\gamma_{{\bm{\alpha}}}{\bf c}_{eq})
one obtains the linear system

\partial_{t}w={\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\alpha}}w\,,\quad{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\alpha}}w=-\diver(u_{{\bm{\alpha}}}w)-\diver(u[w]\omega_{{\bm{\alpha}}})\,.

(2.2)

where
\omega_{\bm{\alpha}}:=\tau_{\bm{\alpha}}\omega_{eq}
and
u_{\bm{\alpha}}:=u[\tau_{\bm{\alpha}}]+\gamma_{\bm{\alpha}}{\bf c}
.
It is straightforward to observe that

{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\alpha}}:=\alpha_{4}^{2}\;\tau_{\bm{\alpha}}\circ{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}\circ\tau_{\bm{\alpha}}^{-1},

(2.3)

with
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
in (
1.8
).
In view of (
2.3
) the linear evolution near any Lamb-Chaplygin dipole is completely determined by studying the semigroup generated by
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
.

We now present the elements of the generalized kernel of
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
associated with symmetries
(i)
-
(v)
.

Lemma 3
.

The following functions

f_{1}^{\rm odd}:=\frac{2k}{J_{0}(k)}J_{1}(kr)\sin(\theta){\bf 1}_{{\rm D}},\quad f_{1}^{\rm ev}:=\frac{2k}{J_{0}(k)}J_{1}(kr)\cos(\theta){\bf 1}_{{\rm D}},

(2.4)

fulfill

{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}\,f_{1}^{\rm odd}=f_{0}^{\rm odd},\quad{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}\,f_{1}^{\rm ev}=-f_{0}^{\rm ev},

(2.5)

where
f_{0}^{\rm odd}
and
f_{0}^{\rm ev}
are the following kernel elements of
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}

f_{0}^{\rm odd}:=J_{2}(kr)\sin(2\theta){\bf 1}_{{\rm D}},\quad f_{0}^{\rm ev}:=\frac{k^{2}}{J_{0}(k)}\big[J_{0}(kr)+J_{2}(kr)\cos(2\theta)\big]{\bf 1}_{{\rm D}}\,.

(2.6)

Proof.

We will use the family of symmetries introduced in Section
2
.
Let
s
be a real number parameterizing one of those invariant transformations, and let
(\omega_{s},\mathbf{c}_{s})
be the related Lamb-Chaplygin dipole. By differentiating
F(\omega_{s},\mathbf{c}_{s})=0
with respect to
s
at
s=\bar{s}
, where
\omega_{\bar{s}}=\omega_{eq}
, one obtains the identity

D_{\omega}F(\omega_{eq},{\bf c}_{eq})[\left.\partial_{s}\omega_{s}\right|_{s=\bar{s}}]+D_{\mathbf{c}}F(\omega_{eq},{\bf c}_{eq})[\left.\partial_{s}\mathbf{c}_{s}\right|_{s=\bar{s}}]=0,

(2.7)

namely

{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}(\left.\partial_{s}\omega_{s}\right|_{s=\bar{s}})=\left.\partial_{s}\mathbf{c}_{s}\right|_{s=\bar{s}}\cdot\nabla\omega_{eq},

(2.8)

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
is the linearized operator in (
1.8
).
For horizontal and vertical translations we have
\bar{s}=0
and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}\,f_{0}^{\rm odd}={{\mathcal{L}}}_{eq}\,f_{0}^{\rm ev}=0,\quad\text{ where }f_{0}^{\rm odd}=\partial_{x_{1}}\omega_{eq},\quad f_{0}^{\rm ev}=\partial_{x_{2}}\omega_{eq}.

(2.9)

Indeed, in polar coordinates,

\partial_{x_{1}}\omega_{eq}=A\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)\left(J_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}(kr)k-\frac{1}{r}J_{1}(kr)\right),

and

\partial_{x_{2}}\omega_{eq}=AJ_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}(kr)k\sin^{2}(\theta)+\frac{1}{r}AJ_{1}(kr)\cos^{2}(\theta).

By classical trigonometric identities and recurrence formulas for Bessel functions in
[
13
, Appendix B, Section B.2]
, one obtains the explicit formulas (
2.6
).
For the amplitude rescaling we have
\bar{s}=1
and

{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}\,f_{1}^{\rm odd}=\mathbf{c}_{eq}\cdot\nabla\omega_{eq}=\partial_{x_{1}}\omega_{eq}=f_{0}^{\rm odd},\text{ where }f_{1}^{\rm odd}:=\omega_{eq}\,,

whence the formula for
f_{1}^{\rm odd}
in (
2.4
) descends from (
1.4
).
Finally, for the rigid rotation we have
\bar{s}=0
and

{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}\,f_{1}^{\rm ev}=-(\mathbf{c}_{eq}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla)\omega_{eq}=-\partial_{x_{2}}\omega_{eq}=-f_{0}^{\rm ev},\text{ where }f_{1}^{\rm ev}:=\partial_{\theta}\omega_{eq},

and one obtains the explicit formula for
f_{1}^{\rm ev}
in (
2.4
).

Remark 4
.

By considering the dilation symmetry in
(v)

F(\omega_{s},{\bf c}_{s})=0

{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}

rJ_{0}(kr)\sin(\theta){\bf 1}_{\rm D}

It is interesting to notice that any perturbation of the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole
\omega_{eq}

X_{p}:=L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

p>2
, can be decomposed into another Lamb-Chaplygin dipole
\tau_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}\omega_{eq}
plus a
stabilized
perturbation, namely a perturbation that is orthogonal to the two generalized eigenvectors of
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}
. Let us state the result as follows.

Lemma 5
.

For every
f\in X_{p}
, there exists an element
{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
(specifically, a combination of a rotation and an amplification) and a function
g\in X_{p}\cap\mathrm{span}\{\tau_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}f_{1}^{\rm{odd}},\tau_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}f_{1}^{\rm{ev}}\}^{\perp}
such that

\omega_{eq}+f=\tau_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}\omega_{eq}+g.

Proof.

Given
f\in X_{p}
, let

\beta^{\rm odd}:=\frac{\langle f,f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle}{\|f_{1}^{\rm odd}\|_{L^{2}}}
and
\beta^{\rm ev}:=\frac{\langle f,f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle}{\|f_{1}^{\rm ev}\|_{L^{2}}}.

We have

\omega_{eq}+f=\omega_{eq}+\beta^{\rm odd}f_{1}^{\rm{odd}}+\beta^{\rm ev}f_{1}^{\rm{ev}}+g,

where
g:=f-\beta^{\rm odd}f_{1}^{\rm{odd}}-\beta^{\rm ev}f_{1}^{\rm{ev}}
. Moreover, by (
2.4
),

\omega_{eq}+\beta^{\rm odd}f_{1}^{\rm{odd}}+\beta^{\rm ev}f_{1}^{\rm{ev}}=(1+\beta^{\rm odd})\omega_{eq}+\beta^{\rm ev}\partial_{\theta}\omega_{eq}.

We look for parameters
s
and
\delta
such that
(1+\beta^{\rm odd})\sin(\theta)+\beta^{\rm ev}\cos(\theta)=\delta\sin(\theta+s)
. By applying the standard trigonometric addition formulas and solving the resulting two-dimensional system, we obtain

\delta:=\sqrt{(1+\beta^{\rm odd})^{2}+(\beta^{\rm ev})^{2}},\qquad s:=\arctan\left(\frac{\beta^{\rm ev}}{1+\beta^{\rm odd}}\right),

and define
{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}:=(0,0,s,\delta,1)
.
To conclude we observe that
\mathrm{span}\{f_{1}^{\rm{odd}},f_{1}^{\rm{ev}}\}=\mathrm{span}\{\tau_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}f_{1}^{\rm{odd}},\tau_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}f_{1}^{\rm{ev}}\}
.
∎

We conclude this section by pointing out that the other source of linear instability in Theorem
1
, namely the average of the initial datum
w_{0}
on the unit disk, is also an artifact of linearizing around the single equilibrium
\omega_{eq}
rather than considering stability with respect to the whole family of traveling solutions. Indeed, if one takes
as initial datum for the full Euler equations in (
1.1
)

\omega_{in}:=\omega_{eq}+\mu\mathbf{1}_{D},\qquad\mu\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mu\neq 0,

with initial traveling velocity
{\bf c}_{eq}=(1,0)
,
then the corresponding solution is given exactly by

\omega(t)=\tau_{\bm{\alpha}(t)}(\omega_{eq}+\mu\mathbf{1}_{D}),\quad\bm{\alpha}(t):=\big(-\tfrac{2}{\mu}\sin\big(\tfrac{\mu t}{2}\big),\tfrac{2}{\mu}\big(1-\cos\big(\tfrac{\mu t}{2}\big)\big),-\tfrac{\mu t}{2},1,1\big),

with traveling velocity

{\bf c}(t)=\gamma_{\bm{\alpha}(t)}{\bf c}_{eq}=R_{\mu t/2}{\bf c}_{eq}.

In other words, the perturbation
\mu\mathbf{1}_{D}
does not generate a
genuine instability of the nonlinear dynamics: it merely adds a rigid
rotation inside the disk, so that the solution remains for all times on the
orbit of the family of traveling Lamb–Chaplygin dipoles.

We now turn our attention to the dynamics generated by the linearized flow (
1.8
).

3
Linear dynamics near the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole

In this section, we continue our study of the linear system in (
1.8
) by shifting the focus from the spectral properties of
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
to the reduction of the problem to the internal dynamics of the disk. This leads to the definition of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}
introduced in (
1.10
). The analysis of its spectral properties, together with an abstract argument, culminates in the proof of Theorem
2
. The spectral picture will then be combined with a dynamical argument to obtain suitable growth estimate and complete the proof of Theorem
1
.

3.1 Reduction to the unit disk

Let us consider the system (
1.8
). The first term of
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
is the classical transport operator which generates a strongly continuous semigroup on
X_{p}=L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
p>2
, whereas the second one is a bounded operator on the same class. Indeed, for every
w\in X_{p}
one has
u[w]\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
(cfr.
[
3
, Remark 1.0.2]
) and, by inspection,
\nabla\omega_{eq}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
By
[
11
, Chapter III, Theorem 1.3]
we conclude that
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
generates a strongly continuous semigroup on
X_{p}
.

To study the linear stability of the Lamb-Chaplygin dipole, we first observe that the dynamics can be rigorously restricted to the unit disk
{\rm D}
. We split a solution
w(t)\in X_{p}
of
\partial_{t}w(t)={\mathcal{L}}_{eq}\,w(t)
into
w(t)=w_{ext}(t)+w_{int}(t)
. Here we define
w_{ext}
as the solution of

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}w_{ext}(t)=-u_{eq}\cdot\nabla w_{ext}(t),\\
w_{ext}(0)=w(0)(1-\mathbf{1}_{{\rm D}}),\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
\mathbf{1}_{{\rm D}}
is the characteristic function of the unit disk.
In view of the streamlines of the velocity flow
u_{eq}
, see Figure
1
,
w_{ext}
remains spatially supported outside the unit disk. We define
w_{int}(t):=w(t)-w_{ext}(t)
,
and observe that, since
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}\,w_{ext}=-u_{eq}\cdot\nabla w_{ext}
,

\partial_{t}w_{int}=\mathcal{L}_{eq}\,w-(u_{eq}\cdot\nabla)w_{ext}=\mathcal{L}_{eq}\,w_{int}.

(3.2)

We claim that
{\rm{supp}}(w_{int}(t))\subseteq{\rm D}
for every
t\geq 0
. Indeed, let
r(t):=w_{int}(t)-\mathbf{1}_{{\rm D}}w_{int}(t)
, where
\mathbf{1}_{{\rm D}}
is the indicator function of the unit disk. We have

\partial_{t}r(t)=\mathcal{L}_{eq}\,w_{int}-\mathbf{1}_{{\rm D}}\,\mathcal{L}_{eq}\,w_{int}=-u_{eq}\cdot\nabla r(t)

where the last identity descends from the fact that
u_{eq}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{1}_{{\rm D}}=0
since
\nabla\mathbf{1}_{{\rm D}}
is a distribution supported on
\partial{\rm D}
and directed along its normal vector
\mathbf{n}
, whereas
u_{eq}
is tangential to
\partial{\rm D}
(the boundary of the disk is a streamline for the flow). By uniqueness of the solution for the transport equation arising from
r(0)=0
, we have that
r(t)
vanishes identically. This proves our claim.

We observe that, as a consequence of the divergence Theorem, the total circulation
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}w(t){\rm d}x
is constant in time. Moreover, since
w_{ext}
is governed by the transport equation, its circulation is preserved at any time. We conclude that

\int_{{\rm D}}w_{int}(t,x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}w(t,x)\,{\rm d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{\rm D}}w_{ext}(t,x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{{\rm D}}w(0,x)\,{\rm d}x

is constant as well.
Let us consider
w_{int}(t)=\widetilde{w}(t)+\mu\ \mathbf{1}_{{\rm D}}
, where
\mu:=|{\rm D}|^{-1}\int_{{\rm D}}w_{int}(0)\,{\rm d}x
and

\widetilde{w}
has zero average. By (
3.2
),

\partial_{t}\widetilde{w}=-\diver(u_{eq}\widetilde{w})-\diver(u[\widetilde{w}]\omega_{eq})-u[\mu\ {\bf 1}_{\rm D}]\cdot\nabla\omega_{eq}={\mathcal{L}}_{eq}\widetilde{w}-\frac{\mu}{2}f_{1}^{\rm ev},

(3.3)

with
f_{1}^{\rm ev}
in (
2.4
). Indeed, by solving the Poisson problem
\Delta\psi=\mu\ \mathbf{1}_{{\rm D}}\ {\rm in}\ \mathbb{R}^{2}
with
|\nabla\psi|
goes to
0
as
|x|\to+\infty
,
we find that the general solution inside the unit disk is
\psi=\frac{\mu}{4}|x|^{2}+C
. Thus,
u[\mu]=\nabla^{\perp}\psi=\frac{\mu}{2}x^{\perp}
on
{\rm D}
. Moreover, since
x^{\perp}\cdot\nabla=\partial_{\theta}
in polar coordinates, we have
u[\mu]\cdot\nabla\omega_{eq}=\frac{\mu}{2}\partial_{\theta}\omega_{eq}=\frac{\mu}{2}f_{1}^{\rm ev}
by (
2.4
).

3.2 The homogeneous problem

We now restrict our analysis to the homogeneous part of (
3.3
). The linear evolution of an initial data supported on
{\rm D}
with
zero average
is determined by

\partial_{t}w=-u_{eq}\cdot\nabla w-\nabla^{\perp}\omega_{eq}\cdot u^{\perp}[w]=-u_{eq}\cdot\nabla w+k^{2}u_{eq}\cdot u^{\perp}[w],

(3.4)

since, by the functional relation (
1.5
), we have

\nabla^{\perp}\omega_{eq}=-k^{2}u_{eq}.

(3.5)

By the incompressibility condition, we know that
u^{\perp}[w]
is minus the gradient of a scalar function
\psi
solving

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=w\qquad{\rm{in}}\ {\rm D},\\
\Delta\psi=0\qquad{\rm{in}}\ {\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{\rm D}},\end{cases}

(3.6)

with
|\nabla\psi|(x)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
. The function
\psi_{ext}:=\psi|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{\rm D}}
is harmonic and with gradient decaying at infinity. Thus, in polar coordinates,

\psi_{ext}(r,\theta)=\zeta\ln(r)+\psi_{0}+\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\psi_{m}r^{-|m|}e^{\mathrm{i}\,m\theta}\,,

(3.7)

for some complex coefficients
\zeta
and
\psi_{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
w
is average free, one has

0=\int_{\rm D}w(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\rm D}\Delta\psi(x)\,{\rm d}x=\oint_{\partial{\rm D}}\partial_{r}\psi(\sigma)\,{\rm d}\sigma=2\pi\zeta,

(3.8)

where the last identity follows by differentiating (
3.7
) on

\partial{\rm D}
and integrating in
\theta
, since all the
non-zero Fourier modes have zero average. Hence
\zeta=0
.

As a consequence, at the boundary of the unit disk,
\psi_{ext}
fulfills the relation

(\partial_{r}\psi_{ext})(1,\theta)=-|D_{\theta}|\psi_{ext}(1,\theta),

where
|D_{\theta}|
is the Fourier multiplier

|D_{\theta}|(e^{im\theta}):=|m|e^{im\theta}.

(3.9)

Thus, (
3.6
) is reduced to the following Poisson equation with Robin boundary condition

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=w\qquad&{\rm{in}}\ {\rm D},\\
\partial_{n}\psi=-|D_{\theta}|\psi\qquad&{\rm{on}}\ \partial{\rm D}.\end{cases}

(3.10)

Let
\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}
denote the operator that associates with
\omega\in L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
the unique (up to a constant) solution of (
3.10
). By the previous passages we have

u^{\perp}[w]=-\nabla\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}w\,.

(3.11)

Finally,
we write (
3.4
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w=-u_{eq}\cdot\nabla w-k^{2}u_{eq}\cdot\nabla(\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}\,w)=-(u_{eq}\cdot\nabla)({\rm Id}+k^{2}\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1})w={\mathscr{L}}w,

(3.12)

where
{\mathscr{L}}
is the operator introduced in (
1.10
).

We end this section by endowing
L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
with an explicit basis given by eigenfunctions of the self-adjoint and compact operator
\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}
.
Let
m\geq 0
be an integer. We recall that the
m
-th Bessel function of the first kind
J_{m}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as the solution of the following ODE

t^{2}J_{m}^{\prime\prime}(t)+tJ_{m}^{\prime}(t)+(t^{2}-m^{2})J_{m}(t)=0,

(3.13)

so that, for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

\Delta[J_{m}(\alpha r)e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\,m\theta}]=-\alpha^{2}J_{m}(\alpha r)e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\,m\theta}
. Let
\alpha\neq 0
,
by (
3.10
),
w:=J_{m}(\alpha r)e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\,m\theta}
is such that
\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}w=-\alpha^{-2}w
if and only if
\psi:=-\alpha^{-2}w
fulfills
\partial_{n}\psi=-|D_{\theta}|\psi
. The latter condition boils down to

\alpha J^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha)+mJ_{m}(\alpha)=0
.
By recalling the well-known relation
xJ_{m}^{\prime}(x)=xJ_{m-1}(x)-mJ_{m}(x)
, we conclude that
f
is an eigenfunction of
\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}
if

\alpha J_{m-1}(\alpha)=0\,,

(3.14)

i.e
\alpha\in\{\pm j_{m,n}\}_{n\geq 1}
where
j_{m,n}
is the
n
-th strictly positive root of
J_{m}(x)
.
One has
w\in L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
since

\displaystyle\int_{\rm D}w(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\rm D}\Delta\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}w(x)\,{\rm d}x=\oint_{\partial{\rm D}}\partial_{n}\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}w(x)\,{\rm d}\sigma=\oint_{\partial{\rm D}}\partial_{n}\psi(\sigma)\,{\rm d}\sigma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{PoissonRobin}}}{{=}}-\oint_{\partial{\rm D}}|D_{\theta}|\psi(\sigma)\,{\rm d}\sigma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{D-to-N}}}{{=}}0\,.

Let us observe that each eigenfunction associated with a negative value of
\alpha
is a multiple of another one associated with
|\alpha|
because of the parity properties of the Bessel functions.
In conclusion,
thanks to the Hilbert-Schmidt theorem, the following set forms a basis of
L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})

\mathcal{B}:=\{J_{0}(j_{1,n}r)\}_{n\geq 1}\cup\{J_{m}(j_{m-1,n}r)e^{\mathrm{i}\,m\theta}\}_{m\geq 1,n\geq 1}.

(3.15)

We are now in a position to study the linear system (
3.12
) generated by the operator
{\mathscr{L}}
in (
1.10
).

3.3 The Hamiltonian operator
{\mathscr{L}}

In this section we conclude our proof of Theorem
2
, by a complete analysis of the spectral properties of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}
governing (
3.12
).

The operator
{\mathscr{L}}
in (
1.10
) is
Hamiltonian
in the sense of
[
7
, Definition 1.4]
, namely it decomposes into

{{\mathscr{L}}}={\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{H}},\qquad{\rm{with}}\qquad\mathcal{J}:=-u_{eq}\cdot\nabla,\qquad{\rm{and}}\qquad\mathcal{H}:={\rm Id}+k^{2}\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1},

(3.16)

where:

(i)

the operator
\mathcal{J}:{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{J})\subset L_{0}^{2}({\rm D})\rightarrow L_{0}^{2}({\rm D})
, with
{\rm Dom}(\mathcal{J}):=\left\{f\in L_{0}^{2}({\rm D}):\mathcal{J}f\in L_{0}^{2}({\rm D})\right\}
, is densely defined, closed and skew-adjoint;

(ii)

the operator
\mathcal{H}:L_{0}^{2}({\rm D})\rightarrow L_{0}^{2}({\rm D})
is bounded and self-adjoint.

In particular, the operator
{\mathscr{L}}
is closed with
{\rm Dom}({\mathscr{L}}):={\rm Dom}(\mathcal{J})
.

We are interested in all possible initial data
w_{0}\in L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
such that the evolution
e^{t{\mathscr{L}}}w_{0}
grows in time.
A first answer to the problem is provided by classifying
every
unstable generalized eigenfunction
of
{\mathscr{L}}
. We recall that
f\in\cap_{m=1}^{\infty}{\rm Dom}({\mathscr{L}}^{m})
is a generalized eigenfunction associated with an eigenvalue
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
if, for some integer
m\geq 1
, one has

({\mathscr{L}}-\lambda)^{m}f=0\,.

(3.17)

We shall call such an
f

unstable
if (cfr.
[
7
, p. 13]
)

\text{\it either}\quad({\bf A})\ \ \Re\,\lambda\neq 0\quad\text{\it or}\quad({\bf B})\ \ \Re\,\lambda=0\text{ and }m\geq 2\,.

Indeed, one can readily prove that
\|e^{t{\mathscr{L}}}f\|_{L^{2}}
will grow exponentially fast in the first case, and polynomially fast in the second case.

Suppose
m\geq 1
is the first integer for which (
3.17
) holds. If
m=1
then
f_{0}:=f\neq 0
is an actual eigenvector of
{\mathscr{L}}
. On the other hand if,
m\geq 2
, we can associate with
f
the eigenvector
f_{0}:=({\mathscr{L}}-\lambda)^{m-1}f\neq 0
. In both cases, we have

f_{0}\in{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}})\quad\text{and}\quad\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{0},f_{0}\rangle=0,

(3.18)

as deduced, e.g., in the first lines of the
Alternative proof
of
[
7

.

The self-adjoint operator
\mathcal{H}={\rm Id}+k^{2}\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}
is diagonal in the basis
\mathcal{B}
in (
3.15

\mathcal{H}(J_{0}(j_{1,n}r))=\left(1-\frac{k^{2}}{j_{1,n}^{2}}\right)J_{0}(j_{1,n}r),\quad\mathcal{H}(J_{m}(j_{m-1,n}r)e^{\mathrm{i}\,m\theta})=\left(1-\frac{k^{2}}{j_{m-1,n}^{2}}\right)J_{m}(j_{m-1,n}r)e^{\mathrm{i}\,m\theta}.

(3.19)

The kernel
{\rm K}
of
{\mathcal{H}}
and its maximal negative invariant subspace
{\rm N}
are respectively given by

{\rm{K}}={\rm{span}}\{J_{0}(kr),\ J_{2}(kr)\cos(2\theta),\ J_{2}(kr)\sin(2\theta)\},\quad{\rm{N}}={\rm{span}}\{J_{1}(j_{0,1}r)\cos(\theta),\ J_{1}(j_{0,1}r)\sin(\theta)\}.

(3.20)

In particular the functions
f_{0}^{\rm odd}
and
f_{0}^{\rm ev}
in (
2.6
) lie in the kernel
{\rm K}
.
To prove (
3.20
) we observe that, in view of (
3.19
), any nonpositive direction of
{\mathcal{H}}
corresponds to an integer

such that

j_{m-1,n}\leq k=j_{1,1}.

By definition

j_{m-1,1}<j_{m-1,2}<j_{m-1,3}<\dots

and, by properties of the Bessel functions’ roots, for every integer

one has

j_{0,n}<j_{1,n}<j_{2,n}<j_{3,n}<\dots

(3.21)

Thus, to conclude (

By general properties of Hamiltonian operators, see e.g.
[

, formula (

) entails that the operator
{\mathscr{L}}
has at most two linearly independent unstable eigenvectors.
In our case, however, the negative directions of
{\mathcal{H}}
are not related to some unstable direction of
{\mathscr{L}}
, but are absorbed by the Jordan chains generated by the symmetries listed in Section
2
that we described in Lemma
3
. This property is presented in the following result.

Lemma 6
.

The functions
f_{1}^{\rm odd}
and
f_{1}^{\rm ev}
in (
2.4
) satisfy

\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle_{L^{2}({\rm D})}=\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm ev},f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle_{L^{2}({\rm D})}<0
.

Proof.

We compute
\frac{J_{0}(k)}{2k}\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}f_{1}^{\rm odd}=g(kr)\sin(\theta)
where

t^{2}g^{\prime\prime}(t)+tg^{\prime}(t)-g(t)=\frac{t^{2}}{k^{2}}J_{1}(t)\ \text{ for }t\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }\ kg^{\prime}(k)=-g(k).

One verifies that
g(t)=k^{-2}(\tfrac{1}{2}J_{1}^{\prime}(k)t-J_{1}(t))
.
It follows that

\frac{J_{0}(k)}{2k}{\mathcal{H}}(f_{1}^{\rm odd})=\frac{k}{2}J_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}(k)r\sin(\theta).

Then

\displaystyle\frac{J_{0}(k)^{2}}{4k^{2}}\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle_{L^{2}({\rm D})}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{k}{2}r^{2}J_{1}^{\prime}(k)J_{1}(kr)\sin^{2}(\theta)\,{\rm d}r\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi k}{2}J_{1}^{\prime}(k)\int_{0}^{1}r^{2}J_{1}(kr)\,{\rm d}r=\frac{\pi}{2k^{2}}J_{1}^{\prime}(k)\int_{0}^{k}z^{2}J_{1}(z)\,{\rm d}z,

where in the last step we performed the change of variable
z=kr
. By the recurrence formulas for Bessel functions in
[
13
, Appendix B, Section B.2]
, we have the following identities

\int_{0}^{k}z^{2}J_{1}(z)\,{\rm d}z=k^{2}J_{2}(k),\qquad J_{1}^{\prime}(k)=J_{0}(k),\qquad J_{2}(k)=-J_{0}(k).

We conclude that
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle=-2\pi k^{2}<0
. The computation for
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm ev},f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle
is identical.
∎

The above lemma justifies the following characterization.

Corollary 7
.

The Jordan chains
\{f_{1}^{\rm odd},f_{0}^{\rm odd}\}
and
\{f_{1}^{\rm ev},f_{0}^{\rm ev}\}
of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}
introduced in Lemma
3
do not admit any extension, in the sense that there does not exist
f_{2}\in L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
such that
{\mathscr{L}}f_{2}=f_{1}^{\rm odd}
or
{\mathscr{L}}f_{2}=f_{1}^{\rm ev}
.

Proof.

Indeed one would have
0\neq\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{H}}f_{2}\rangle=-\langle f_{0}^{\rm odd},{\mathcal{H}}f_{2}\rangle=0
, which is a contradiction. The same holds in the other case.
∎

Remark 8
.

Canonically, one can construct Jordan chains for
{\mathscr{L}}^{*}=-{\mathcal{H}}{\mathcal{J}}
as
\{{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},{\mathcal{H}}f_{0}^{\rm odd}\}
and
\{{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm ev},{\mathcal{H}}f_{0}^{\rm ev}\}
. However, since
f_{0}^{\rm odd}
and
f_{0}^{\rm ev}
belong to the kernel of
{\mathcal{H}}
, this construction just provides two kernel elements
g_{0}^{\rm odd}:={\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd}
,
g_{0}^{\rm ev}:={\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm ev}
of
{\mathscr{L}}^{*}
. Only the last one gives rise to a nontrivial Jordan chain. Indeed, setting
g_{1}^{\rm ev}(r):=\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}k^{2}
one has
{\mathscr{L}}^{*}g_{1}^{\rm ev}={\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm{ev}}
by inspection. On the contrary, since
f_{1}^{\rm odd}
lies in
{\rm Ker}({\mathcal{J}})
, one readily obtains that there does not exist any
g_{1}^{\rm odd}\in L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
such that
{\mathscr{L}}^{*}g_{1}^{\rm odd}={\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd}
.

As anticipated in the introduction, such a vector
g_{1}^{\rm odd}
would have allowed us to define the subspace
Y:=\{g_{0}^{\rm odd},g_{1}^{\rm odd},g_{0}^{\rm ev},g_{1}^{\rm ev}\}^{\perp}
being closed,
{\mathscr{L}}
-invariant and supporting
\langle{\mathcal{H}}\cdot,\cdot\rangle
coercively. The argument below circumvents this obstruction by using a different invariant structure.

In light of Lemma
6
,
we define the auxiliary closed
subspaces
\tilde{Z}\subset Z\subset L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
given by

\displaystyle Z:=\{f\in L^{2}_{0}({\rm D}):\langle{\mathcal{H}}f,f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}f,f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle=0\},

(3.22)

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}:=\{f\in Z:\langle{\mathcal{H}}f,h\rangle=0\text{ for every }h\in{\rm Ker}({\mathscr{L}})\}\,.

Let us list their main properties.

(i)

Since
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{odd},f_{1}^{odd}\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{ev},f_{1}^{ev}\rangle\neq 0
and
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle=0
, one readily obtains that

L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})={\rm span}\{f_{1}^{\rm odd},f_{1}^{\rm ev}\}\oplus Z.

(ii)

One has
{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}})\subseteq\tilde{Z}\subset Z
. Indeed, if
f={\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{H}}g
then

\langle{\mathcal{H}}f,f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle=-\langle{\mathcal{H}}g,{\mathscr{L}}f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle=-\langle{\mathcal{H}}g,f_{0}^{\rm odd}\rangle=-\langle g,{\mathcal{H}}f_{0}^{\rm odd}\rangle=0,

and similarly
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f,f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle=0
. Moreover, for every
h
in
{\rm Ker}({\mathscr{L}})
,
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f,h\rangle=-\langle{\mathcal{H}}g,{\mathscr{L}}h\rangle=0
.

(iii)

The operator
\mathcal{H}
is positive semi-definite on
Z
. Indeed, any
f\in Z
such that
\langle\mathcal{H}f,f\rangle<0
would be a third negative direction for
{\mathcal{H}}
linearly independent from
f_{1}^{\rm odd}
and
f_{1}^{\rm ev}
, in contradiction with (
3.20
).

A direct consequence of property
(iii)
of
Z
is the following

Lemma 9
.

Let
f\in Z
be such that
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f,f\rangle=0
, then
{\mathcal{H}}f=0
.

Proof.

The quadratic form
q:L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})\to\mathbb{R}
given by
q(f):=\langle\mathcal{H}f,f\rangle
, which is positive semi-definite on
Z
, attains a minimum at
f
when constrained on the set
\{g\in Z:\|g\|_{L^{2}}=\|f\|_{L^{2}}\}
.
By the Lagrange multiplier method,
f
will be a stationary point of the following functional

J(g,\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{H}g,g\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{0}(\|g\|^{2}-\|f\|^{2})-\mu_{1}\langle\mathcal{H}g,f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle-\mu_{2}\langle\mathcal{H}g,f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle,

whose gradient with respect to
g
at
g=f
gives

\mathcal{H}f-\mu_{0}f-\mu_{1}\mathcal{H}f_{1}^{\rm odd}-\mu_{2}\mathcal{H}f_{1}^{\rm ev}=0.

Since, by hypothesis,
\langle\mathcal{H}f,f\rangle=0
and
f\in Z
, the inner products of the left-hand side above with the functions
f
,
f_{1}^{\rm odd}
and
f_{1}^{\rm ev}
respectively give
\mu_{0}=\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=0
. Thus
{\mathcal{H}}f=0
.
∎

We also obtain the following

Corollary 10
.

The operator
{\mathcal{H}}
is coercive on
Z\cap{\rm K}^{\perp}
, namely there exists
c>0
such that

\langle{\mathcal{H}}f,f\rangle\geq c\|f\|^{2},\quad\text{for every }f\in Z\cap{\rm K}^{\perp}.

(3.23)

Proof.

By contradiction, let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset Z\cap{\rm K}^{\perp}
, with
\|f_{n}\|=1
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be such that
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{n},f_{n}\rangle\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
. Up to subsequences,
f_{n}
converges to some
f\in Z\cap{\rm K}^{\perp}
as
n\to+\infty
weakly in
L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
. By (
3.16
),

0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to+\infty}}{{\longleftarrow}}\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{n},f_{n}\rangle=1+k^{2}\langle\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}f_{n},f_{n}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to+\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}1+\langle\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}f,f\rangle\geq\|f\|^{2}+\langle\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}f,f\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}f,f\rangle,

(3.24)

where we used that
\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}f_{n}
converges strongly to
\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}f
as
n\to+\infty
because
\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}
is a compact operator. If follows that
\langle Hf,f\rangle=0
and, by Lemma
9
,
f\in{\rm K}
. Thus
f=0
, and by (
3.24
),
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{n},f_{n}\rangle\to 1
as
n\to+\infty
, which is a contradiction.
∎

One has
\overline{{\rm Ran}({\mathcal{J}})}\cap{\rm K}={\rm span}\,\{f_{0}^{\rm odd},f_{0}^{\rm ev}\}

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The operator
{\mathscr{L}}
in (
1.10
) is a relatively compact perturbation of the skew-adjoint operator
{\mathcal{J}}
. Thus the set
\sigma_{L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})}({\mathscr{L}})\setminus\mathrm{i}\,\mathbb{R}
is contained in the pure-point spectrum of
{\mathscr{L}}
. Regarding the latter, let
f
be an unstable generalized eigenfunction of
{\mathscr{L}}
associated with an eigenvalue
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
. As done in (
3.18
), we can construct an eigenfunction
f_{0}\in{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}})
such that
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{0},f_{0}\rangle=0
. By property
(ii)
of
Z
, we have
f_{0}\in Z
and, by Lemma
9
,
\lambda=0
. Thus
any unstable generalized eigenfunction of
{\mathscr{L}}
is associated with
0
. This concludes the proof of (
1
).

Let us consider the point (
2
). By Corollary
7
, we already have two Jordan chains of length
2
in the generalized kernel of
{\mathscr{L}}
. Let us suppose that another Jordan chain of arbitrary length, ending as
\{\dots,f_{1},f_{0}\}
with
{\mathscr{L}}f_{0}=0
and
{\mathscr{L}}f_{1}=f_{0}
, appears in the generalized kernel. Then, as for the proof of the first point,
f_{0}\in Z
and
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{0},f_{0}\rangle=0
, implying
f_{0}\in{\rm Ran}({\mathcal{J}})\cap{\rm Ker}({\mathcal{H}})
. By Lemma
11
we obtain that
f_{0}=\alpha f_{0}^{\rm odd}+\beta f_{0}^{\rm ev}
for some
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}
. As a consequence
f_{1}=\alpha f_{1}^{\rm odd}+\beta f_{1}^{\rm ev}
and the Jordan chain we are considering is just a linear combination of the two in Corollary
7
.
∎

3.4 Linear stability

We now focus on the proof of Theorem
1
. A first step is a weak separation result between the space
{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}})\subseteq\tilde{Z}
in (
3.22
), to which all trajectories are parallel, and the kernel
{\rm K}
of the operator
{\mathcal{H}}
, near which the Hamiltonian structure of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}
is insufficient to control the linear evolution.

Lemma 12
.

One has
{\mathscr{L}}(\tilde{Z})\cap{\rm K}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
{\rm K}\ni f_{0}={\mathscr{L}}f_{1}
for some
f_{1}\in{\tilde{Z}}
. In particular,
f_{0}
lies in
\overline{{\rm Ran}({\mathcal{J}})}\cap{\rm K}
and, by Lemma
11
, there exist
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f_{0}=\alpha f_{0}^{\rm odd}+\beta f_{0}^{\rm ev}
. In view of (
2.5
),

f_{1}=\alpha f_{1}^{\rm odd}-\beta f_{1}^{\rm ev}+h,\quad h\in{\rm Ker}({\mathscr{L}}).

(3.25)

One readily obtains that
\beta=0
in (
3.25
). Indeed, by (
3.22
),

0=\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1},f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle=-\beta\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm ev},f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle+\langle{\mathcal{H}}h,f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle.

(3.26)

Let us observe that
f_{1}^{\rm ev}={\mathcal{J}}g
, with
g(r):=-\tfrac{1}{2}{r^{2}k^{2}}{\bf 1}_{{\rm D}}
, by inspection.
As a consequence,

\langle{\mathcal{H}}h,f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}h,{\mathcal{J}}g\rangle=-\langle{\mathscr{L}}h,g\rangle=0.

(3.27)

Since, by Lemma
6
,
\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm ev},f_{1}^{\rm ev}\rangle\neq 0
, we conclude from (
3.26
)-(
3.27
) that
\beta
vanishes. We now prove that
\alpha=0
too. Again, by (
3.22
) and (
3.25
),

\begin{cases}0=\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1},f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle=\alpha\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle+\langle{\mathcal{H}}h,f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle,\\
0=\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1},h\rangle=\alpha\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},h\rangle+\langle{\mathcal{H}}h,h\rangle.\end{cases}

(3.28)

As a consequence

\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{decomposition}}}{{=}}\alpha\big(\alpha\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle+\langle{\mathcal{H}}h,f_{1}^{\rm odd}\rangle\big)+\big(\alpha\langle{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}^{\rm odd},h\rangle+\langle{\mathcal{H}}h,h\rangle\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{la1}}}{{=}}0.

By Lemma
9
we obtain that
{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}=0
. Thus,
0={\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{H}}f_{1}=\alpha f_{0}^{\rm odd}
, namely
\alpha=0
and
f_{0}
vanishes.
∎

As a corollary, let
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\tilde{Z}
be a bounded sequence and suppose that
{\mathscr{L}}g_{n}
converges to some function
h\in{\rm K}
weakly in
L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
. Then, up to subsequences,
g_{n}
tends to some
g_{\star}\in\tilde{Z}
weakly in
L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
. Since
{\mathscr{L}}
is a closed operator,
{\mathscr{L}}g_{\star}=h
and, by the previous lemma,
h=0
.

Relying on the latter result we show that no initial datum in
{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}})
yields linear growth.

Lemma 13
.

For every
f\in{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}})
one has
\sup_{t\geq 0}\|e^{t{\mathscr{L}}}f\|_{L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})}<+\infty
.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction and suppose that there exists a sequence of times
t_{n}\geq 0
such that
\|e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f\|\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
.
Let
h_{n}:={e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f}/{\|e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f\|}
. Up to a subsequence,
h_{n}
converges to some
h\in\tilde{Z}
weakly in
L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
. We claim that the convergence is automatically strong and
h\in K
. Indeed, by the conservation of the energy
{\mathcal{H}}
along the trajectories of the semigroup,
\langle{\mathcal{H}}e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f,e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}f,f\rangle
, and, dividing by
\|e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f\|^{2}
, we get
\langle{\mathcal{H}}h_{n},h_{n}\rangle\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
. On the other hand, since
\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}
is a compact operator,
\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}h_{n}\to\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}h
as
n\to+\infty
strongly in
L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
and, by property
(iii)
of
\tilde{Z}\subset Z
,

\langle{\mathcal{H}}h_{n},h_{n}\rangle=\|h_{n}\|^{2}-\langle k^{2}\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}h_{n},h_{n}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to+\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}1-\langle k^{2}\Delta_{D\!N}^{-1}h,h\rangle\geq\langle{\mathcal{H}}h,h\rangle\geq 0.

Since
\langle{\mathcal{H}}h_{n},h_{n}\rangle\rightarrow 0
, we have
\|h\|=1
and
h\in K
by Lemma
9
. By the Radon-Riesz property of the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{0}({\rm D})
,
h_{n}\to h
as
n\to+\infty
strongly. This concludes the proof of the claim. Since
{\mathscr{L}}g=f
,

h_{n}=\frac{f+{\mathscr{L}}\tilde{g}_{n}}{\|e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f\|}=o(1)+{\mathscr{L}}\tilde{g}_{n},\quad\text{with }\tilde{g}_{n}:=\frac{e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}g-g}{\|e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f\|}=\frac{{\mathscr{L}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}e^{s{\mathscr{L}}}g\,{\rm d}s}{\|e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f\|}\in{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}})\subseteq\tilde{Z}.

(3.29)

We
split
\tilde{g}_{n}=k_{n}+g_{n}
, where
k_{n}\in{\rm K}\cap\tilde{Z}
and
g_{n}\in{\rm K}^{\perp}\cap\tilde{Z}
. It follows that
{\mathscr{L}}g_{n}={\mathscr{L}}\tilde{g}_{n}
and, by (
3.23
) and exploiting again the conservation of
{\mathcal{H}}
along trajectories,

\displaystyle c\|g_{n}\|^{2}\leq\langle{\mathcal{H}}g_{n},g_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}(\tilde{g}_{n}-k_{n}),(\tilde{g}_{n}-k_{n})\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{H}}\tilde{g}_{n},\tilde{g}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{hnid}}}{{=}}\frac{\langle{\mathcal{H}}e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}g,e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}g\rangle}{\|e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f\|^{2}}+o(1)=\frac{\langle{\mathcal{H}}g,g\rangle}{\|e^{t_{n}{\mathscr{L}}}f\|^{2}}+o(1)\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to+\infty.

We have thus found a sequence
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\tilde{Z}
such that
{\mathscr{L}}g_{n}=h_{n}+o(1)
converges to
h\in{\rm K}
, with
\|h\|=1
. By the corollary result of Lemma
13
we conclude that
h=0
, which is a contradiction.
∎

In view of the previous result, one is able to prove that a general initial datum in
L_{0}^{2}({\rm D})
may grow at most linearly in time. Indeed, we know that this estimate is sharp, since, for example,
e^{t{\mathscr{L}}}f_{1}^{\rm odd}=tf_{0}^{\rm odd}+f_{1}^{\rm odd}
.

Lemma 14
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for every
f\in L_{0}^{2}({\rm D})
,

\|e^{t{\mathscr{L}}}f\|\leq C(1+t)\|f\|\qquad{\rm{for\,every}}\,t\geq 0.

(3.30)

Proof.

By the uniform boundedness principle, it suffices to show (
3.30
) with a constant
C
depending on
f
.
For every integer
n\geq 1
,
we write

e^{n{\mathscr{L}}}f-f=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}e^{j{\mathscr{L}}}(e^{{\mathscr{L}}}f-f)
,
with
e^{{\mathscr{L}}}f-f={\mathscr{L}}\int_{0}^{1}e^{s{\mathscr{L}}}f{\rm d}s\in{\rm Ran}({\mathscr{L}})
. By Lemma
13
and since
e^{{\mathscr{L}}}-{\rm Id}
is a bounded operator, one has a positive constant
C_{f}>0
such that

\|e^{n{\mathscr{L}}}f-f\|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}C_{f}\|e^{{\mathscr{L}}}f-f\|\leq nC_{f}\|f\|.

(3.31)

Let
t>0
and split
t=n+r
with integer
n\geq 0
,
r\in[0,1)
. By (
3.31
) we conclude

\|e^{t{\mathscr{L}}}f\|\leq\|e^{r{\mathscr{L}}}f\|+\|e^{r{\mathscr{L}}}(e^{n{\mathscr{L}}}f-f)\|\leq C_{f}(1+t)\|f\|,

as desired.
∎

We conclude our exposition with the following

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let us now consider the evolution
w(t)
of an initial datum
w_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
p>2
, under the strongly continuous semigroup generated by
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}
in (
1.8
). As described in Section
3.1
, we have
w(t)=w_{ext}(t)+w_{int}(t)
, with
w_{ext}
given by the pure transport equation (
3.1
) and
w_{int}(t)=\widetilde{w}(t)+\mu{\bf 1}_{{\rm D}}
where
\mu
is a constant term and the zero-average profile
\widetilde{w}
evolves according to the forced linear equation (
3.3
). By Duhamel’s formula,

\widetilde{w}(t)=e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}}\widetilde{w}_{0}(0)-\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s){\mathcal{L}}_{eq}}f_{1}^{\rm ev}{\rm d}s,

Since, by Lemma
3
,
{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}f_{1}^{\rm ev}=-f_{0}^{\rm ev}\in{\rm Ker}({\mathcal{L}}_{eq})
, we have
e^{(t-s){\mathcal{L}}_{eq}}f_{1}^{ev}=f_{1}^{\rm ev}-(t-s)f_{0}^{\rm ev}
. In view of Section
3.2
the term
e^{t{\mathcal{L}}_{eq}}\widetilde{w}(0)
coincides with
e^{t{\mathscr{L}}}\widetilde{w}(0)
, hence

\widetilde{w}(t)=e^{t{\mathscr{L}}}\widetilde{w}(0)-\frac{\mu}{2}tf_{1}^{\rm ev}+\frac{\mu}{4}t^{2}f_{0}^{\rm ev}.

(3.32)

By (
3.30
),

\|e^{t{\mathscr{L}}}\widetilde{w}(0)\|\leq C(1+t)\|\widetilde{w}(0)\|
, for every
t\geq 0
, and we conclude the estimate (
1.9
).
∎
