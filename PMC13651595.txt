ICARION: A Modular
Framework for Ion Trajectory Simulation
in Electric Fields and Neutral Gas Environments

Christoph Schaefer, Juraj Jašík, Patrik Španěl

Abstract

Ion transport in electric fields and neutral gases underlies
the
operation of ion mobility spectrometers, mass spectrometers, and coupled
IMS-MS instruments. Numerical modeling of such systems requires consistent
treatment of field-driven motion, stochastic ion-neutral collisions,
and reactions across different pressure and field regimes. Many IMS-MS
instruments operate in transitional regimes, requiring explicit particle-based
treatment of collisions. Here, we present ICARION (Ion Collision And
Reaction IntegratiON), an open-source, modular, particle-based simulation
framework for reproducible simulations of ion transport, collisions,
and reactions. ICARION is intended for mechanistic studies of ion
dynamics, exploration of new concepts in IMS, MS, and related techniques,
and rapid and reproducible screening of operating parameters and geometries
for instrument development and optimization. Its modular architecture
combines various selectable field, collision, and reaction models
with structured configuration and data processing. Multiple instrument
domains with different electric fields, geometries, and environmental
conditions can be seamlessly combined in a single simulation of coupled
IMS-MS instruments. This work validates ICARION rather than introducing
new physical models. Validation includes collisional thermalization,
bimolecular reaction kinetics, ion transport in homogeneous electric
fields validated with established tools, and coupled multidomain simulations.
Across these cases, ICARION reproduces the expected behavior of the
selected physical models and enables continuous simulations across
domains differing by orders of magnitude in pressure and field time
scales. While externally generated field maps can be integrated as
needed, a planned internal field solver will enable the calculation
of electric fields directly from the user-defined electrode configurations.
The modular architecture also facilitates the integration of additional
physical models.

Introduction

Ion transport in electric fields and neutral
gases underlies the
operation of a wide range of analytical instruments, including ion
mobility spectrometers (IMS), radio-frequency (RF) ion traps, and
hybrid IMS-MS systems.
,
 Depending on pressure and electric
field strength, ion motion spans distinct transport regimes. At very
low pressures, where the mean free path is much greater than the characteristic
electrode dimensions, ion trajectories are nearly collision-free and
deterministic. At high pressures, frequent collisions lead to diffusive
transport that can be described statistically in terms of ensemble-averaged
drift and diffusion.
−

 In contrast, many modern IMS-MS platforms operate
in an intermediate-pressure regime, where the ion mean free path becomes
comparable to the electrode dimensions. In this regime, individual
ion-neutral collisions must be treated explicitly using stochastic,
particle-based Monte Carlo approaches,
,
 as effective
drag forces or purely statistical descriptions no longer accurately
capture the underlying physics.

Modern mass spectrometers typically
consist of multiple instrument
regions, such as drift cells, ion guides, and RF trapping or filtering
devices. In numerical simulations, these regions can be treated as
separate instrument domains, each characterized by its own environmental
conditions (pressure, temperature, and gas composition) and electric
field configuration. Ions therefore traverse a sequence of domains
that may differ strongly in their physical conditions, ranging from
highly collisional drift cells to nearly collision-free trapping or m/z analysis stages. Accurate modeling
of such instruments requires simulation approaches that consistently
describe the ion motion across heterogeneous domains.

In low-field
IMS, the collision cross section (CCS) obtained from
the measured mobility using the fundamental equation of ion mobility
corresponds to the first-order collision integral Ω(1,1). This collision integral is obtained
by integrating the momentum transfer efficiency induced by collisions
over the impact parameters and averaging over the relative orientations
of ions and neutrals to obtain the collision-energy-dependent momentum
transfer cross section, followed by a transport-weighted averaging
over the distribution of ion-neutral relative speeds. Therefore, the
CCS is a transport quantity that depends on the neutral gas, the ion-neutral
interaction potential, and the temperature, and is not a purely geometric
area.

Numerical ion trajectory simulation is therefore an essential
tool
for instrument development, exploration of new concepts in MS, and
data interpretation,
,
 enabling quantitative prediction
of ion mobilities, transmission efficiencies, and reaction kinetics
under experimentally relevant conditions.
−

 While statistical and analytical transport models
provide valuable insights into equilibrated ion transport conditions,
realistic instrument geometries and intermediate-pressure transport
conditionswhere mean free paths are comparable to electrode
dimensionsrequire particle-based simulations that explicitly
resolve both ion-neutral collisions and electric fields.
−

Established simulation environments address these requirements
from a complementary perspective. General-purpose ion-optics tools
such as SIMION provide highly accurate
electrostatic field calculations and deterministic trajectory propagation
and are widely regarded as a reference standard for ion-optical simulations.
Collisional processes can be incorporated through dedicated collision
models and user-defined extensions; however, these capabilities are
typically configured at the level of individual simulation setups
and are not embedded in a unified, reproducible workflow that explicitly
targets coupled transport-reaction problems across heterogeneous pressure
regimes.

Particle-based drift simulation frameworks such as
IDSimF
,
 or ITSIM focus
on stochastic ion transport
and reaction kinetics and provide explicit particle-based descriptions
of ion-neutral collisions. In principle, IDSimF can be extended to
simulate complex or multidomain instrument configurations. In practice,
however, such coupled simulations require substantial custom code
development, as multidomain behavior cannot be expressed solely through
declarative configuration but must be implemented explicitly by the
user.

Recently, IMoS 2 has further
advanced
microscopic transport calculations by propagating ions through a local
neutral gas environment and explicitly evaluating ion-neutral interactions
from interaction potentials. This approach enables a detailed description
of transport properties, field-dependent mobility, rotational dynamics,
and ion heating, and can explicitly account for interactions with
multiple gas particles simultaneously. However, its primary purpose
is the extraction of transport properties rather than the full instrument-level
simulation of ion trajectories across complete instrument domains
or IMS-MS architectures with explicit geometries, boundary conditions,
ion losses, and domain transitions.

As a result, simulations
of coupled IMS-MS systems spanning drift,
trapping, and m/z analysis domains
often rely on bespoke code extensions, tool chaining, or simplifying
assumptions, which complicate reproducibility and systematic validation
across transport regimes.

Ion Collision And Reaction IntegratiON (ICARION) was developed for three complementary
applications: mechanistic
studies of ion motion in electric fields under the influence of collisions
and reactions, the exploration of new concepts in IMS, MS, and related
techniques, and rapid, reproducible screening of operating parameters
and geometries for instrument development and optimization. It combines
deterministic ion motion in electric fields with discrete collision
and reaction events and supports continuous propagation across multiple
instrument domains with different geometries, fields, and gas environments
without requiring a manual transfer or reinitialization of the ion
ensemble.

Its modular architecture allows for the selection
of alternative
physical descriptions (e.g., different collision models) and facilitates
the integration of new models through stable interfaces. The current
implementation offers analytical field descriptions and various selectable
collision and reaction models, which are sufficient for many mechanistic
simulations and rapid screening (see Section S3). Field maps and transport parameters generated by external tools
can additionally be incorporated, e.g., when realistic, nonideal electric
fields are required. The same interfaces also facilitate the integration
of an internal numerical field solver, which is currently under development,
to calculate electric fields from user-defined electrode geometries
in future versions of ICARION without requiring restructuring of the
instrument definitions, simulation engine, or data workflow.

The source code is available via GitHub (see Software Availability
Statement), and the archived software release is available on Zenodo. Prebuilt executable packages for Windows and
Linux are provided via the GitHub repository, allowing users to run
ICARION without compilation.

The present work does not aim to
introduce new ion-transport or
reaction models. Instead, it focuses on the systematic validation
of this unified, particle-based simulation framework that combines
deterministic ion optics, stochastic ion-neutral collisions, and chemical
reactions within a single reproducible numerical architecture. Emphasis
is placed on transport regimes relevant to modern IMS-MS instrumentation,
where ions traverse multiple instrument domains characterized by distinct
pressures, field configurations, and time scales. The framework is
validated using theoretical reaction kinetics and statistical speed
distributions at equilibrium, for which independent reference solutions
are available. Ion transport in a drift region with a uniform field
is compared with the established ion trajectory simulation tools IDSimF
and SIMION using comparable collision models that apply the same collision
physics and match in terms of the input parameters and operating conditions.

Design philosophy, execution workflow, and configuration concepts
are summarized in the next sections to provide context, while detailed
implementation aspects are documented in the SI. This article therefore provides a validated and extensible framework
for particle-based trajectory simulations of ion transport and chemistry
in IMS-MS systems, together with openly available software and benchmark
data sets to support its use and further development by the IMS-MS
community.

Design Philosophy and Development Goals

Design Goals

ICARION was designed as a universal particle-based
simulation framework for coupled ion transport and chemical reactions
across heterogeneous IMS-MS environments.

ICARION is not primarily
intended as a standalone tool for predicting transport parameters
such as ion mobility or collision cross sections. Rather, the implemented
collision models serve to simulate ion trajectories and thus propagate
ion ensembles at the instrument level in the presence of electric
fields and gas environments. The required level of physical detail,
particularly in the collision model and in the description of the
electric field, depends on the intended application. Computationally
efficient, physically justified approximations are useful for rapid
screening and simulations of large ion ensembles, whereas more detailed
collision models based on explicit ion-neutral interaction potentials
are required when ion transport depends strongly on collision energy
or specific ion-neutral interactions. Likewise, field maps become
relevant when nonideal electric fields must be taken into account.

A central design principle of ICARION is the modularity of physical
models and numerical components. Field descriptions, collision models,
reaction kinetics, force contributions, and numerical integrators
are treated as interchangeable modules that can be combined consistently
without modification of the core simulation engine. This approach
enables the use and comparison of alternative physical models under
identical numerical conditions and facilitates the implementation
of new physical models through the use of stable interfaces. If necessary,
externally generated field maps and transport parameters can be used
as inputs, while a future internal numerical field solver can be integrated
via the same interfaces.

The framework was evaluated with respect
to numerical consistency,
transparency, and reproducibility. The predictive accuracy of a given
simulation depends on the assumptions made, the parametrization, the
scope of applicability of the selected physical models, and the numerical
settings, and must therefore be evaluated with regard to the intended
application. To meet reproducibility and transparency as central design
goals, all simulation parameters are either defined directly in structured
configuration files or are derived from them. Defaults are clearly
specified and documented in accordance with a strict single-source-of-truth
(SSOT) philosophy, while all outputs and metadata are recorded in
a standardized, self-describing format. This ensures that simulation
results can be reproduced deterministically and independently of the
execution environment while maintaining full transparency of the physical
and numerical assumptions. In practice, this is implemented via declarative
JSON-based input configurations and standardized HDF5 output files, which together encode the parameters and
metadata required for reproducible simulations.

Execution Workflow

The general execution workflow of
an ICARION simulation is shown in . The SimulationEngine module acts as the
central coordinator for all simulation components. Consistent with
a strict separation of concerns, it does not encode any instrument-
or model-specific logic but orchestrates the execution of independently
defined physical and numerical modules. All simulation settings are
initialized from the validated configuration prior to execution, including
descriptions of the domains, physical models, numerical integrators,
and output handlers.

During execution, the simulation advances in discrete
time steps.
At each step, active ions are processed independently: optional space-charge
contributions are updated according to the selected model, stochastic
collision and reaction events are sampled, and forces from electric
and magnetic fields are evaluated. Collisional effects are treated
either explicitly via stochastic sampling, particularly for intermediate
pressures, or implicitly via an ensemble-averaged drag force, particularly
for high-pressure simulations, depending on the selected collision
model. Subsequently, ion trajectories are propagated by using the
selected numerical integrator. Boundary conditions and domain transitions
are enforced after each propagation step. Output is buffered and written
at user-defined intervals to ensure consistent temporal sampling while
minimizing I/O overhead. The simulation terminates when either the
maximum simulation time is reached or no active ions remain, after
which the final output and metadata are written.

Materials and Methods

All simulations were performed
using the ICARION simulation framework
(v1.0.2 production code). The exact simulation
commit (40210bfe) is recorded in the output metadata; no production
code relevant to the simulations was changed between this commit and
the final v1.0.2 release (0f5dfce3). The code was compiled against
the required libraries (HDF5 1.10.10 and JSON libraries (jsoncpp 1.9.5
and nlohmann_json 3.11.3)), the GNU Compiler Collection (GCC) 13.3.0,
and executed on a workstation running Windows Subsystem for Linux
(WSL). Parallel execution employed OpenMP for shared-memory multithreading.
No GPU backends were used in this study. The exact Git commit, the
configuration files, the random number generator (RNG) seed values,
and relevant build information are recorded in the output data, which
is available in the associated data set stored in the National Data
Repository.

Simulation setups were
fully defined in structured JSON configuration
files that specified domain geometries, neutral gas properties, electric
field parameters, numerical integration settings, and the selected
physical models. All configuration files, raw simulation outputs,
postprocessing scripts, and auxiliary input files required to reproduce
the validation cases are also provided in the associated data set.

All simulations presented in this work use the built-in analytical
models for the instrument-specific idealized electric fields. Consequently,
no externally generated electric field maps are required to reproduce
the simulations presented here. For the HSS simulations, the externally
generated transport data consist of the CCS values derived from MobCal-MPI
2.0, as explained below. The GR-HSS simulations
also use the molecular geometries obtained from the quantum chemical
calculations described below.

Ion Species and Structural Data

All simulations were
conducted for three representative small ions commonly encountered
in gas-phase ion–molecule reaction and ion mobility studies:
hydronium (H3O+), protonated pentanal, and protonated
2,6-di-tert-butylpyridine (2,6-DTBP). These species
span a modest but relevant range of m/z and CCS typical for volatile compounds (see ), without extending into the macromolecular
regime. They were selected as representative test cases rather than
as a limitation of the framework: ICARION can also be applied to the
ion transport of larger molecular or macromolecular ions, provided
that the corresponding species parameters are defined. All species
metadata were specified through the centralized Species Database JSON
file.

Applied Hard-Sphere Collision Models

The two validation
cases that focus on thermalization from collisions and ion mobility
transport use two stochastic elastic hard-sphere collision models:
an effective, isotropic single hard-sphere scattering model (HSS)
and a geometry-resolved, single-contact hard-sphere scattering model
(GR-HSS), as schematically shown in . A detailed explanation of both models is
given in the SI (Section S3). Briefly,
both models sample collision events from a Poisson process. In the
following, σ
event denotes the collision
event cross section, which is used in each time step to calculate
the instantaneous collision rate according to 
kcoll=Nσevent|v⃗rel|
, where N is the neutral
number density and 
|v⃗rel|
 is the sampled ion-neutral relative speed.
The Cartesian velocity components of neutral collision partners are
drawn from the corresponding thermal normal distributions, resulting
in a Maxwellian velocity distribution for the neutral ensemble, as
detailed in the SI (Section S3).

In the HSS model, a single value is used as σ
event, and an accepted event is treated
by sampling an
isotropic relative velocity direction after the collision in the center-of-mass
reference frame, which is consistent with other Monte Carlo methods
for hard spheres. The model requires
neither an explicit ion geometry nor an ion-neutral interaction potential
and therefore provides a computationally efficient approximation for
rapid screening and large ion ensembles. In the GR-HSS model, σ
event is determined from the projected
area of an ion orientation selected at random from a precomputed set
of three-dimensional orientations, which is consistent with established
geometry-based approaches using molecular projected areas.
−

 If an event is accepted, the same orientation is used to identify
the first contact between the neutral and the atom-centered hard-sphere
representations of the ion, using a consistent set of van der Waals
radii from Mantina et al., followed by
a single elastic specular reflection at the local three-dimensional
contact normal. Secondary contacts and multiple scattering, as in
the exact hard-sphere scattering (EHSS) model, are not included.

Both HSS and GR-HSS treat collisions
as specular and elastic and
conserve the total translational kinetic energy of the ion-neutral
pair. Diffuse re-emission, thermal accommodation, and transfer of
translational energy to rotational or vibrational degrees of freedom
are not taken into account. Another collision model not implemented
in this work, diffuse hard-sphere scattering (DHSS),
,
 represents a different physical description in which nonspecular
re-emission and energy accommodation can be incorporated. Such processes
are not included in the HSS and GR-HSS implementations considered
here.

The scope of the present work is a fundamental validation
of the
ICARION v1.0.x framework. Stochastic reaction sampling and thermalization
from collisions are evaluated on the basis of theoretical reaction
kinetics and statistical velocity distributions at equilibrium, respectively.
Ion mobility transport and computational performance are compared
with the established ion trajectory simulation tools IDSimF and SIMION,
both of which implement a comparable effective single-hard-sphere
approximation to ensure consistent collision physics under matched
input parameters and operating conditions.

ICARION v1.1.0 introduced
a collision model based on explicit ion-neutral
interaction potentials to account for the dependence of elastic scattering
on collision energy. However, its application and physical validation
are beyond the scope of this work. Inelastic collisions are currently
not included in any of the implemented collision models, and their
implementation would require a separate model extension. The modular
ICARION architecture is designed to enable such extensions without
modifying the surrounding instrument, field description, or the remaining
data handling framework.

Quantum Chemical and Collision Cross Section Calculations

Three-dimensional molecular geometries and electrostatic partial
charges for all three ions were obtained from density functional theory
(DFT) with the ORCA 6.0 software package. Geometry optimizations and vibrational frequency calculations were
conducted using the ωB97X-D3 functional with the def2-TZVPP basis set. The
RIJCOSX approximation was employed for
the evaluation of hybrid exchange, and all calculations used tight
self-consistent field and geometry convergence criteria. CHELPG partial charges were extracted using a cubic
grid with 0.1 Å spacing and a sampling radius of 3.0 Å.
The resulting optimized molecular geometries were stored in the ICARION
JSON geometry format and are included in the associated data set along
with ORCA input and output files.

Optimized geometry and charge
distributions were exported to MobCal-MPI 2.0, which was used to calculate the CCS under low-field conditions
in helium at 298 K. The resulting CCS values were used as the collision
event cross section σ
event in the
HSS simulations. These values are determined using the trajectory
method, in which the momentum transfer cross section is calculated
as a function of ion-neutral relative speed, then averaged over all
considered orientations, and finally transport-weighted averaging
is performed over the relative speed distribution. Nevertheless, σ
event values used in both hard-sphere
models remain independent of the instantaneous collision energy. The
use of these constant values for Poisson sampling is part of the effective
single-sphere approximation in the HSS model.

Thermalization from Stochastic Collisions

The ability
of the stochastic collision models implemented in ICARION to reproduce
thermal equilibrium with a neutral gas was assessed under field-free
conditions for H3O+, protonated pentanal, and
protonated 2,6-DTBP.

Simulations were conducted over a pressure
range from 0.2 to 2000 Pa in decadic steps and at background temperatures
of 200, 300, 400, 700, and 1000 K. For each combination of ion species,
pressures, and temperatures, 10,000 ions were initialized from an
isotropic thermal velocity distribution at 0.1 K and propagated under
field-free conditions without any gas flow. The simulation duration
was chosen to correspond to 30, 100, and 140 mean collision times
for H3O+, protonated pentanal, and protonated
2,6-DTBP, respectively, ensuring that steady-state thermal equilibrium
was reached within the statistical uncertainty.

Both the effective
single-sphere HSS model and the geometry-resolved
GR-HSS model were evaluated. The purpose of this comparison is to
verify whether the ions in both collision models reach an expected
thermalized state. It is explicitly not intended to present either
model as a general, correct microscopic description of the ion-neutral
collision.

To quantify thermalization under the field-free conditions
considered
here, the instantaneous translational temperature of the ion ensemble
was calculated from its mean kinetic energy as shown in (). Here, ⟨υ
2(t)⟩ denotes the ensemble-averaged squared ion
speed at time t with ion mass m
ion. Since neither an electric field nor bulk gas flow is present,
the mean ensemble velocity remains close to zero, and T
ens(t) describes the transient relaxation
of the ion ensemble toward the ambient gas temperature. It should
not be interpreted as the field-dependent effective temperature used
in two-temperature theory.

the normalized ensemble translational temperature T

evaluated at 0.2 and 2000 Pa. For each ion, the low- and high-pressure

are primarily governed by the number of ion-neutral collisions. The

To further

Equilibration times obtained with the HSS and GR-HSS
collision
models are closely correlated and of the same order of magnitude across
the full pressure–temperature grid ((d)). In the present benchmark, GR-HSS yields
systematically shorter equilibration times than HSS. The equilibration
times reported here are obtained from exponential fits to the translational
temperature relaxation curves. Across all ions and conditions, τ
HSS/τ
GR–HSS is typically in the range of approximately 1.14–1.32. Complete
pressure–temperature grids and additional equilibration statistics
(τ
95, τ
99, and the average number of collisions required for equilibration)
are available in the associated data set.

This systematic offset
between the two collision models is primarily
explained by the different σ
event values used in Poisson sampling. In HSS, the MobCal-MPI output was
used as the single σ
event value.
In the geometry-resolved model, σ
event corresponds to the projected area of the sampled orientation (see
“” and Section S3). The mean σ
event values in GR-HSS are approximately
1.24, 1.25, and 1.17 times larger for H3O+,
protonated pentanal, and protonated 2,6-DTBP, respectively, than the
corresponding σ
event values in HSS.
The larger σ
event value in the GR-HSS
model may seem counterintuitive, since the CCS derived from MobCal-MPI,
which is used as σ
event in HSS simulations,
includes contributions from long-range ion-neutral interactions, whose
contribution to scattering generally decreases with increasing relative
speed. However, the CCS from MobCal-MPI and the σ
event values derived from GR-HSS are not identical physical
quantities and are derived from different scattering models. The former
is a transport collision integral derived from an explicit ion-neutral
interaction potential, while the latter is a geometric projected area.
Another cause of the observed difference is the independent parametrization
of the GR-HSS contact geometry. In ICARION, the element-specific atom-helium
contact distances used to search for the first ion-neutral contact
are determined by adding the respective van der Waals radii reported
by Mantina et al. This provides a single,
internally consistent literature-based set of parameters without ion-specific
fitting to calculated or experimental ion mobility or CCS data. The
resulting hard-sphere size therefore does not correspond to the finite
repulsive interaction used in MobCal-MPI. The observed differences
consequently reflect both the different physical quantities and the
independent GR-HSS parametrization. Thus, the two collision models
should not be interpreted as alternative predictions of the same CCS.

The different σ
event values nevertheless
lead directly to different sampled collision event rates between HSS
and GR-HSS and thus capture the trend and order of magnitude of the
observed equilibration time ratios. Additional contributions may arise
from stochastic sampling uncertainty and residual uncertainty in the
relaxation fits, as supported by control simulations with different
RNG seed values (see Section S5 in the SI for more details). Briefly, these control
simulations yield standard deviations of approximately 0.01–0.04
across seed values for a given ion and fixed condition. The error
bars in (d)
represent the median value of 0.025 of these calculated standard deviations
to estimate the statistical uncertainty. These show that the differences
in the equilibration time ratios between the various conditions can
be largely explained by stochastic sampling uncertainty and the residual
uncertainty in the relaxation fits. A mechanistic control simulation
for protonated 2,6-DTBP, in which the σ
event in the HSS simulation was set to the mean GR-HSS value,
reduced the ratio of equilibration times to 1.019 ± 0.015. This
supports the interpretation that, under the conditions studied, the
observed differences in equilibration times are dominated by differences
in collision rates, with minor residual contributions from the different
collision physics.

Reaction Kinetics

To validate the stochastic sampling
of ion–molecule reactions along ion trajectories, a bimolecular
proton transfer from H3O+ to a neutral molecule
M, forming MH+, was simulated under pseudo-first-order
conditions. The purpose of this benchmark is to verify that the probabilistic
reaction handling implemented in ICARION reproduces the expected temporal
evolution of ion populations for well-defined reaction rates.

The reaction was treated as irreversible and bimolecular, with three
fixed model rate coefficients of k
1 =
1 × 10–9cm3/s, k
2 = 2 × 10–9cm3/s, and k
3 = 3 × 10–9cm3/s, representative of fast proton transfer reactions. Simulations
were conducted at a background temperature of 300 K and a pressure
of 400 Pa in helium, with the neutral M mixing ratio set to 1 ppm,
thereby ensuring pseudo-first-order conditions with respect to the
ion population.
,

Reaction events were sampled
stochastically by using a Poisson
process. At each time step, the probability of a reaction occurring
was computed from the reaction rate coefficient (see Section S3 for details). When a reaction event was accepted,
the reacting ion species was updated through the centralized species
database, including mass, charge state, collision cross section, and
reduced ion mobility, before propagation continued.

shows the
temporal evolution of the model H3O+ and MH+ ion populations for the three different reaction rate coefficients.
In all cases, the decay of the reagent ion population follows an exponential
behavior, while the product ion population increases correspondingly.
The simulated ion counts are in excellent agreement with the analytical
solution of the corresponding first-order rate equation, demonstrating
correct probabilistic sampling of reaction events along ion trajectories.
The final ion populations obtained at the end of the simulation are
consistent with the expected reaction extent under the chosen conditions.
For a reaction rate coefficient of 3 × 10–9 cm3/s, the simulated ion population yields approximately
70% MH+ product ions after 4 ms, which is in good quantitative
agreement with the analytical prediction based on the effective pseudo-first-order
rate coefficient.

These results confirm that the reaction handling
in ICARION reproduces
correct ensemble-averaged reaction kinetics for bimolecular ion–molecule
reactions. This provides a validated basis for coupled transport-reaction
simulations, which are known to affect arrival time distributions
in ion mobility experiments.

Ion Transport in Drift Cells

In the next series of
simulations, ion transport in uniform electric fields was investigated
to validate stochastic particle-based ion mobility simulations and
to benchmark ICARION against established trajectory simulation tools.
Simulations were conducted in a cylindrical drift cell operated with
helium as the neutral gas at 300 K. The drift cell had an effective
length of 100 mm and an inner diameter of 10 mm. Pressures ranging
from 20 to 2000 Pa were investigated, covering a regime in which stochastic
ion-neutral collisions remain computationally feasible while spanning
a broad range of collision frequencies. Reduced field strengths of
3, 5, 7, 8.5, and 10 Td were applied as representative IMS operating
conditions in the range of low-to-moderate reduced field strengths.

Simulations were conducted for protonated pentanal in helium. Ions
were initialized with thermal velocity distributions at 300 K and
propagated through the drift cell until they exited the domain. Drift
times were extracted from the simulated arrival time distributions
and converted to reduced mobility.

Ion transport simulations
were conducted using the two stochastic
collision models implemented in ICARION and already employed in the
thermalization validation case: the isotropic HSS model and the geometry-resolved
GR-HSS model. For cross-code benchmarking, ICARION-HSS results were
compared to isotropic single-sphere simulations conducted with IDSimF
(HS) and with SIMION (HS1), widely regarded as a reference standard
for ion trajectory simulations. In all isotropic single-sphere simulations,
collision cross sections were matched to identical effective values
to ensure a fair numerical comparison. Further details on the simulation
configurations are provided in Section S4.

(a)
shows
the reduced ion mobility as a function of pressure for representative E/N values of 3 and 10 Td. At moderate
and higher pressures, particularly above 200 Pa, ICARION-HSS, IDSimF,
and SIMION yield consistent ion mobilities across the investigated
field range. Deviations between the three implementations typically
remain below 1% under these equilibrated conditions, confirming numerical
agreement of stochastic collision handling and trajectory propagation
across the three independent simulation frameworks. The reduced ion
mobilities of 10.10 to 10.32 cm2/(V s), determined from
the simulated arrival times using the HSS model, agree well with the
MobCal-MPI value of 10.17 cm2/(V s), which is expected
since σ
event in the HSS model was
parametrized based on the MobCal-MPI value. Nevertheless, this agreement
further demonstrates the internal consistency of the HSS implementation
and confirms that it reproduces the expected transport behavior for
a given input value of σ
event.

At lower pressures, systematic deviations in the
outputs of the
three tools became apparent. While SIMION in particular yields higher
apparent mobilities, ICARION-HSS and IDSimF remain in closer agreement,
as shown in (b) and (c). These deviations reflect the transition toward an ion
transport regime in which the actual axial velocity is not fully equilibrated
over the entire finite drift time, rather than numerical inconsistencies
between the simulation tools. Because of the low collision frequencies
under these conditions, the equilibration time can become a substantial
fraction of the observed drift time. The extracted arrival time, and
thus the inferred apparent ion mobility, therefore retains sensitivity
to the initial ion velocity distribution (see Section S6). The observed differences between the simulation
tools can be attributed to small differences in the initial velocity
distributions and the ion initialization conditions. These results
demonstrate numerical agreement among ICARION, IDSimF, and SIMION
under equilibrated conditions when comparable collision models and
matched input parameters are used. The remaining deviations at low
pressure reflect intrinsic nonequilibrium transport effects rather
than differences in the underlying numerical implementations.

The GR-HSS model yields systematically lower reduced ion mobilities
than the isotropic HSS model across the investigated pressures and
reduced electric field strengths. The ion mobility ratio K
0,HSS/K
0,GR–HSS is
approximately 1.24–1.27, which agrees with both the ratio of
equilibration times discussed above and the ratio of σ
event. For protonated pentanal in helium, the mean value
of σ
event in GR-HSS is 1.25 times
larger than the corresponding HSS value. As in the validation case
of thermalization from collisions, we performed control simulations
to assess statistical uncertainty and the effect of using identical σ
event values (see Section S6). These results again support the interpretation
that, under the conditions studied here, the observed difference in
ion mobility is dominated by differences in σ
event, with only a small residual contribution from the
different collision physics. Thus, the lower ion mobility in the GR-HSS
model can be attributed primarily to the higher collision event rate.
We emphasize again that the larger σ
event determined with GR-HSS reflects the parametrization using the consistent
data set of van der Waals radii from Mantina et al., which, while it may limit predictive accuracy, avoids ion-
or data-specific empirical fitting.

A complete data set of reduced
ion mobilities for all settings
and control simulations is available in the associated data set.

Multidomain Simulation: IMS-Quadrupole Coupling

To
validate ICARION’s ability to simulate ion transport across
coupled instrument domains, a multidomain IMS-quadrupole configuration
was simulated for H3O+, protonated pentanal,
and protonated 2,6-DTBP. The multidomain setup consists of a drift
cell (100 mm length, 10 mm radius) operated under collisional conditions
(10 mbar helium, 298 K), followed by an RF quadrupole (100 mm length,
4 mm characteristic radius) operated as a mass filter at low pressure
(10–6 mbar), as illustrated in . This configuration represents
a minimal yet representative IMS-MS coupling scenario spanning collisional
and nearly collision-free transport regimes. From a practical standpoint,
the coupled simulation is defined by adding only a second domain block
to the configuration, while all physical models and numerical settings
remain unchanged.

In the first domain, ions are separated according
to their mobilities
in helium under a uniform electric field. Upon exiting the drift cell,
ions enter the second domain, which acts as a quadrupole mass filter,
where ion motion is predominantly governed by time-dependent RF and
DC electric fields. The two domains differ by 7 orders of magnitude
in pressure and by the nature of the governing electric fields.

(a) shows
the arrival time distributions at the domain boundary for the three
ion species, illustrating the ion mobility separation achieved prior
to domain transition. The three ion packets reach the domain boundary
at distinct mean arrival times of approximately 90, 171, and 251 μs
for H3O+, protonated pentanal, and protonated
2,6-DTBP, respectively. Ions enter the quadrupole sequentially according
to these residence times in the drift cell, resulting in temporally
separated ion packets at the domain boundary.

To illustrate the coupling of the two domains and
the reproducibility
of the particle-based simulations, three independent simulations were
conducted with identical IMS conditions but different quadrupole mass
filter settings, each tuned to selectively transmit only one of the
three species. (b) shows representative ion trajectories across both domains for
these simulations. In the drift cell, trajectories exhibit diffusive
motion superimposed on directed drift, characteristic of stochastic
ion-neutral collisions. Upon entering the quadrupole domain, trajectories
transition smoothly to an RF-driven oscillatory motion with negligible
collisional effects.

For all of the simulations, the same RNG
seed value was used. Therefore,
the corresponding ion trajectories in the drift domain are identical
across the three simulations, reflecting identical stochastic collisions
under the identical IMS conditions. This illustrates the deterministic
reproducibility of the stochastic simulation workflow when the input
configuration and the RNG seed value are fixed. The trajectories diverge
only in the quadrupole domain because of the different RF and DC voltages
applied in the simulations. The multidomain results demonstrate that
ICARION can simulate the motion of individual ions across multiple
instrument domains within a single simulation run. Each domain defines
its own electric field, gas environment, and boundary conditions,
while ions retain continuous trajectories across the domain interfaces.
Hence, ICARION enables particle-based modeling of coupled IMS-MS instruments
spanning collisional and nearly collision-free transport regimes without
the manual transfer or reinitialization of ion ensembles between separate
simulations.

Performance Benchmark

To evaluate its computational
performance, ICARION was benchmarked against IDSimF and SIMION on
an AMD Ryzen 9 7950X CPU with 64 GB RAM. The benchmark simulated the
ion mobility transport of protonated pentanal through a 20 mm drift
region with a uniform electric field by using the HSS collision model.
The simulations were performed in helium at 300 K and 200 Pa with
a reduced electric field strength of 10 Td. The physical and numerical
input parameters were closely matched among the ICARION, IDSimF, and
SIMION. Minimal output settings were used to reduce the I/O overhead
while still obtaining information about the final state and reproducibility.

Ensembles containing 1000, 5000, 10,000, and 50,000 ions were simulated.
ICARION and IDSimF were evaluated with 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 CPU threads
at a fixed time step of 0.6 ns. For each configuration, five independent
runs were performed, preceded by one warm-up run to eliminate initialization
effects. The median wall-clock times were used to calculate speedup
compared with single-thread execution. SIMION was evaluated in single-thread
mode using adaptive integration with T.Qual set to
3 and a maximum time step of 0.6 ns. Since the time-step control,
runtime measurement, and output handling differ from those of ICARION
and IDSimF, the SIMION runtime values are considered descriptive rather
than strictly equivalent.

(a) compares
the runtime in single-thread mode with that from the thread configuration
that provides the lowest median runtime among the tested numbers of
threads. ICARION and IDSimF exhibit similar runtimes for 1000 ions,
while ICARION requires a shorter runtime for larger ensembles. When
considering the fastest multithreaded configuration, ICARION consistently
achieved shorter runtimes than those of IDSimF and SIMION. The fastest
ICARION simulations used eight threads for all of the ensemble sizes.
For IDSimF, four threads were fastest up to 10,000 ions, and eight
threads were fastest at 50,000 ions. (b) shows the parallel speedup of the ICARION
simulations. With eight threads, ICARION achieves speedups of approximately
2.8, 4.2, 4.5, and 5.7 for 1000, 5000, 10,000, and 50,000 ions, respectively.
Increasing the number of threads to 16 did not result in any further
reduction in runtime, indicating saturation in the shared-memory scaling.
This benchmark does not isolate the respective contributions of memory
access, scheduling, synchronization, or other parallel overheads.

The maximum memory usage is summarized in . In the fastest configuration,
ICARION’s
maximum resident set size (RSS) for the ensemble sizes under investigation
increased from 22.0 to 170.9 MiB, compared to 18.0 to 81.4 MiB for
IDSimF. Thus, ICARION’s shorter runtimes for the larger ion
ensembles were accompanied by higher memory usage.

Overall, the results show that the modular ICARION
framework remains
computationally feasible for the ion ensemble sizes examined and benefits
from shared-memory parallelization. This benchmark refers to the specific
hardware, software versions, and simulation settings used here and
is not intended as a general performance evaluation of the three tools.

Current Implementation and Future Directions

While
the present validation focuses on collision models, reaction kinetics,
ion transport in drift cells, and a coupled IMS-quadrupole configuration,
the scope of ICARION is not restricted to ion mobility simulations
or to the examples presented in this work. The same domain-based architecture
can also be applied to other ion optics and mass spectrometry devices,
including linear ion traps, SIFT, Orbitraps, FT-ICR, and time-of-flight
regions, using available analytical field descriptions, imported field
maps, and configurable time-dependent voltage waveforms. ICARION therefore
provides a universal framework for particle-based ion trajectory simulations
beyond ion mobility alone.

The stochastic hard-sphere collision
models implemented in version v1.0.2, as used in this work, provide
computationally efficient runtime descriptions for ion trajectory
simulations in multidomain instruments. Starting with version v1.1.0,
ICARION also offers a collision model based on explicit ion-neutral
interaction potentials. However, a detailed validation of this model
is beyond the scope of this work. Future work could focus on parametrizing
the effective GR-HSS contact distances using reference scattering
data to more closely connect the geometric model with the collision
model based on interaction potentials. The currently available collision
models do not account for diffuse re-emission, thermal accommodation,
or transfer of translational energy to rotational, vibrational, or
other internal degrees of freedom. They therefore represent an intermediate
level of physical description that avoids empirical energy accommodation
models while remaining computationally feasible for larger ion ensembles,
in contrast to fully atomistic molecular dynamics simulations.

A major future direction of development is the integration of an
internal numerical field solver into ICARION trajectory simulations.
This will enable the calculation of electric fields in the same framework
using user-defined electrode geometries rather than relying on analytical
expressions or externally generated field maps. Complete coupled IMS-MS
instruments, as well as user-defined trapping, focusing, ion-guiding,
or mass-analysis stages, could then be defined within the same infrastructure
for trajectories, collisions, reactions, configurations, and outputs
without requiring manual transfer of ion ensembles between separate
tools or simulation runs. This would also support the automated optimization
of electrode geometries, voltages, pressures, time sequences, or gas
compositions using objective evaluation metrics such as transmission,
resolving power, confinement, energy distributions, or reaction yields.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduce ICARION, a modular
and reproducible
simulation framework for particle-based ion transport across heterogeneous
IMS-MS instruments. Rather than proposing new microscopic interaction
models, this study demonstrates that deterministic electric fields,
stochastic ion-neutral collisions, and chemical reactions can be treated
consistently within a single execution framework that covers multiple
pressure and electric field ranges. The implemented models reproduce
the expected behavior in thermalization from collisions, bimolecular
reaction kinetics, ion transport simulations validated using established
simulation tools, and an example of a coupled IMS–quadrupole
system.

The particle-based approach also resolves nonequilibrium
transport
when the collisional relaxation times become comparable to residence
times in the instrument. The low-pressure drift simulations demonstrate
how the obtained apparent ion mobility can maintain sensitivity to
ion initialization under conditions of slow equilibration. A key strength
of ICARION is its ability to simulate coupled instrument domains within
a single simulation by consistently applying the same physical models
under strongly varying conditions. The multidomain example with IMS
and quadrupole demonstrates that ion mobility separation and RF-based
mass filtering can be combined seamlessly while preserving continuous
trajectories across significantly different pressure and electric
field ranges without the need to manually transfer ion distributions
between separate simulations or perform sequential postprocessing.
This reflects the architectural requirements of modern, multistage
IMS-MS instrumentation.

ICARION is primarily intended as a framework
for simulating ion
trajectories to propagate ion ensembles at the instrument level in
electric fields and gaseous environments, rather than as a standalone
tool for predicting collision cross sections or ion mobilities. Its
modular architecture supports mechanistic investigations of ion dynamics,
the exploration of new concepts in IMS, MS, and related techniques,
and the rapid and reproducible screening of operating parameters and
geometries for instrument development and optimization. Furthermore,
it enables the future implementation of an internal numerical field
solver without changing the general architecture or the underlying
physical models.

ICARION is released as open-source software
together with fully
reproducible configuration and analysis workflows. By providing both
prebuilt executable releases for users who wish to run simulations
directly and the full source code for users who wish to inspect, modify,
or extend the framework, we aim to facilitate adoption, transparency,
and further development within the IMS-MS research community.

The data underlying
this study are openly available in the National Data Repository at
DOI: 10.48700/6t3q1-es569. These data include all simulated data
sets shown in this work, together with the configuration files required
for full reproducibility, all analyzed data, and all used pre- and
postprocessing scripts.
