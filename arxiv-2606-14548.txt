Machine Learning-Accelerated Time Integration of Plasticity Models
Nasrin Talebi, Magnus Ekh, Knut Andreas Meyer

Abstract
Finite element simulations of structures with nonlinear material behavior require advanced material models to provide accurate predictions. However, the computational costs of these models can be high, as they solve coupled differential algebraic equations at each integration point, in each equilibrium iteration, in every time step. In this study, we propose a machine learning-based framework to accelerate these computations by explicitly calculating the state variable updates with neural networks, enabling large time steps with low computational costs. The neural networks operate on invariants, and the necessary and sufficient evolution directions are determined analytically based on the training data. Furthermore, the proposed framework enforces exact fulfillment of the plastic consistency condition. To evaluate the proposed framework, a prototype model with the von Mises yield criterion and nonlinear kinematic hardening is chosen. Only 10 cycles of multiaxial proportional loading are used to generate the training data. After evaluating the proposed framework in material point simulations, we incorporate it into finite element simulations to evaluate its accuracy and computational efficiency in a boundary value problem. The results from both material point and finite element simulations show a very promising numerical performance of the neural network-based time integrator. It provides very good accuracy and numerical stability, as well as a noticeable gain in computational time for a single strain increment per load segment.



Keywords
Cyclic plasticity modeling, Explicit time integration, Machine learning, Neural networks, Finite element simulations

Introduction

To accurately predict the mechanical response of structural components in finite element (FE) simulations, advanced nonlinear material models are essential across a wide range of engineering applications. Some examples include the investigation of rolling contact fatigue in railway rails
[
30
,
41
,
15
,
42
]
, geotechnical and soil simulations
[
23
]
, and sheet metal forming processes
[
44
]
.
Despite their predictive capabilities, nonlinear constitutive models remain computationally costly within the FE framework, since a set of Differential Algebraic Equations (DAEs) needs to be solved in every integration point in the FE mesh, for every iteration in each time step of the simulation. Therefore, efficient material model algorithms are crucial for computational efficiency. Explicit forward Euler integration of the DAEs results in fast material models, but requires very small time steps to be numerically robust
[
39
]
. Implicit backward Euler integration is often preferred due to its unconditional stability, which allows for larger time steps. However, many time steps are still required per loading cycle, and a nonlinear equation system must usually be solved iteratively. The challenge is particularly severe in cyclic simulations where no stabilized behavior is obtained
[
24
,
43
]
. To accelerate simulations with nonlinear material models, this study proposes a machine learning (ML) based explicit time integrator that accurately integrates the DAEs while taking much larger time steps than existing methods.

Recent studies have demonstrated that employing ML algorithms, such as Neural Networks (NNs), can reduce the computational cost of constitutive modeling, especially when integrated into FE frameworks; see, e.g., the reviews on machine learning in constitutive modeling by Fuhg et al.
[
12
]
and Dornheim et al.
[
8
]
. In early ML-based constitutive models, NNs were trained to approximate the mapping from strain, strain history, or state variables to stress or state variable increment
[
18
,
1
,
20
]
. In general, these approaches have low predictive accuracy beyond the training regime, as they do not account for the underlying physics
[
29
]
.

To address this, several approaches have been proposed in the literature to satisfy physical principles in a weak or strong form. Inspired by the idea of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
[
35
]
, Haghighat et al.
[
17
]
proposed a PINN-based constitutive modeling framework in which violations of elastoplastic inequality constraints are penalized in the loss function. In
[
11
]
, the introduced physics-informed framework adhered to the second law of thermodynamics during training by penalizing violating analytical expressions for hypoplasticity. Weber et al.
[
46
]
extended the idea of constrained NNs in
[
45
]
to rate-independent plasticity by enforcing physical constraints, such as material tangent symmetry and material stability, directly via error terms in the loss function. Despite their impressive ability to describe material behavior, these approaches do not guarantee physical constraints by construction. Instead, these constraints are only weakly enforced in the loss function during training. In contrast, Meyer and Ekre
[
32
]
proposed a framework in which Feed Forward Neural Networks (FFNNs) are embedded directly into the evolution equations for internal variables. Independent of the training, this formulation inherently satisfies physical requirements, such as thermodynamical consistency and objectivity. Using an alternative approach, Fuhg et al.
[
13
]
introduced a thermodynamically consistent framework based on isotropic and nonlinear kinematic hardening potentials.

ML-based approaches have recently offered a promising route to accelerate the numerical integration of plasticity models, in which the local solution of nonlinear evolution equations at each integration point dominates the computational cost. The following provides elaboration on some relevant studies from the literature focused on this objective. Zhang and Mohr
[
47
]
reformulated the classical return-mapping algorithm for von Mises plasticity using nonlinear isotropic hardening with explicit integration in an approximate manner. They employed an NN to estimate the tangent stiffness without imposing a priori assumptions
about the yield surface, flow rule, or hardening law.
In
[
22
]
, during plastic loading, the stress integration algorithm was replaced with an NN in principal stress space, employing plane stress von Mises plasticity with nonlinear isotropic hardening under monotonic and cyclic loading. Further, they extended the approach to anisotropic plasticity for sheet metal forming applications
[
10
]
. Dettmer et al.
[
7
]
presented a framework for the stress update procedure that separates the state variable update from stress prediction, using state and response NN, respectively. The NNs were trained and evaluated based on cyclic data from uniaxial elastoplasticity with linear isotropic hardening, a uniaxial elastoplastic damage model, and plane strain elastoplasticity with tensor components used as input and output features. Several PINN-type surrogate models have been investigated in literature for bypassing the need for solving the nonlinear system of equations at the material point level. Examples include the work of Eghbalian et al.
[
9
]
, who proposed a PINN-based surrogate model to replace an elastoplastic material model using incremental data for monotonic loading in principal stress space. Moreover, Rezaei et al.
[
36
]
adopted an unsupervised PINN-based approach to train a surrogate model for accelerating the incrementation of elastoplastic material models. They evaluated the methodology on a one-dimensional plasticity model with nonlinear isotropic hardening and on a three-dimensional interface damage model for cracking behavior. In contrast to purely surrogate-based formulations, Li et al.
[
26
]
introduced a PINN-driven framework that circumvents only the local stress integration in an elastoplastic soil constitutive model under cyclic loading. Aiming to fulfill the consistency condition during plastic loading, Zhang
[
48
]
presented an unsupervised learning framework in which an NN is leveraged to predict the stress increment accurately during plastic loading in each strain increment. This objective was achieved by minimizing a loss function that incorporates the residuals of the yield function and the stress increment.
Recently, Malleval et al.
[
31
]
proposed using NNs to approximate the solution to a scalar nonlinear constitutive equation at each integration point for an elastoviscoplastic material model.

The core idea of this study is to propose an NN-based explicit time integration algorithm that enables robust integration over very large strain increments within a physics-encoded framework.
The framework is designed such that loading and unloading conditions are fulfilled by construction. Training data are generated from highly resolved reference solutions obtained using a plasticity model with nonlinear kinematic hardening under multiaxial proportional loading. To ensure frame-independent predictions and reduce the dimensions of the NNs, the models are formulated with invariants as input features, combined with evolution directions inferred from the training data. Furthermore, the consistency condition during plastic loading is enforced in the framework by introducing a correction factor.
Finally, we embed the proposed framework into FE examples to assess its numerical performance and computational efficiency for boundary value problems under different strain increment sizes.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we present the general formulation for explicit ML-based time integration of a generic rate-independent plasticity model. This is followed by the description of the adopted prototype model and the corresponding formulation of the ML-based time integration scheme in Section
3
. We explain the considered FFNNs and the training data generation strategy in Section
4
. Finally, in Sections
5
and
6
, we discuss and summarize our findings.

2
ML-accelerated time integration of a generic plasticity model

In this section, we first present the generic formulation of a thermodynamically consistent material model in a small-strain setting, followed by a description of the time integration schemes for this model. Finally, we propose an explicit ML-accelerated time integrator for solving the evolution equations of state variables.

The notation for different tensors is as follows: Second-order tensors are boldface, e.g.
\boldsymbol{t}
, fourth-order tensors are boldface and upright, e.g.
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}
. Sets of variables are written with blackboard bold, e.g.
\mathbb{T}
. Finally,
\bullet^{\mathrm{dev}}:=\bullet-\mathrm{tr}(\bullet)\boldsymbol{I}/3
denotes the deviatoric part of the tensor
\bullet
.

2.1
Generic small-strain plasticity model

The starting point for a generic small-strain, rate-independent, plasticity model is the Helmholtz’s free-energy function,
\varPsi
, that depends on the total strain,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, and the set of
N_{\rm{s}}
state variables,
\mathbb{S}=\{{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}_{1},\ {\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}_{2},\ \cdots,\ {\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}_{N_{\mathrm{s}}}\}
,

\displaystyle\varPsi=\varPsi\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\mathbb{S}\right)

(1)

The stress,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and the (hardening) variables,
\mathbb{A}=\{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}_{1},\ {\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}_{2},\ \cdots,\ {\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}_{N_{\mathrm{a}}}\}
, are defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\mathbb{S})=\frac{\partial\varPsi}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\mathbb{S})=-\frac{\partial\varPsi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}

Next, a yield function,
\varPhi\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\mathbb{A}\right)
, that defines the elastic domain
(\varPhi<0)
and the plastic domain
(\varPhi=0)
, is formulated. By introducing the plastic multiplier,
\dot{\lambda}
, the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) loading/unloading conditions are expressed as

\varPhi\leq 0,\quad\dot{\lambda}\geq 0,\quad\varPhi\,\dot{\lambda}=0

(2)

The state variables only evolve during plastic loading, which are described by the generic evolution laws

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}_{i}=\dot{\lambda}\,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{f}}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\mathbb{S}),\ \mathbb{A}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\mathbb{S})\right)

(3)

2.2
Integration of a generic small-strain plasticity model

In an incremental strain-controlled algorithm, the role of a material model is to calculate the current stress,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and the updated state variables,
\mathbb{S}
. Here, we assume that the strain increment (from time
{\vphantom{t}}{}^{n}t
to
t
),
\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-{\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, is linear on a material point level, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\theta)={\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\theta\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}

(4)

for
\theta\in[0,1]
within the time step.
1
1

1

For higher order time integration on the global finite element level,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
may vary non-linearly during the increment, but such higher order space-time finite element formulations are not considered herein.
For a given
\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, the standard procedure is to assume an elastic response and calculate the trial stress

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{tr}}:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},{\vphantom{\mathbb{S}}}^{n}\mathbb{S})

(5)

The assumption is true if
\varPhi^{\mathrm{tr}}:=\varPhi(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{tr}},{\vphantom{\mathbb{A}}}^{n}\mathbb{A})<0
, resulting in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{tr}}
and
\mathbb{S}={\vphantom{\mathbb{S}}}^{n}\mathbb{S}
; otherwise, the material model must solve the set of DAEs in Equations
2
and
3
. Different time integration schemes,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}_{i}^{\mathrm{TI}}
, can be used to approximate the solution to Equation
3

\displaystyle\Delta\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}_{i}^{\mathrm{TI}}({\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ \Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\,{\vphantom{\mathbb{S}}}^{n}\mathbb{S})

(6)

For implicit algorithms,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}_{i}^{\mathrm{TI}}
can typically not be described analytically, but requires solving a nonlinear equation system iteratively
[
38
]
.
In explicit algorithms, upon transition from elastic to plastic behavior, the intersection of the trial stress path with the yield surface is identified to determine the plastic part of the applied strain increment
[
40
,
33
]
. This introduces the elastic limit stress,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\rm lim}
, as

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\rm lim}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\left({\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}{}^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+s\,\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon},{\vphantom{\mathbb{S}}}^{n}\mathbb{S}\right)

(7)

where the scalar variable
s\in[0,1]
is determined from
\varPhi(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\rm lim},{{\vphantom{\mathbb{A}}}{}^{n}\mathbb{A}})=0
.
During this elastic substep, no evolution of state variables occurs as
\varPhi<0
. Accordingly,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}_{i}^{\mathrm{TI}}
only acts on the plastic loading part as

\displaystyle\Delta\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}_{i}^{\mathrm{TI}}({\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+s\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ [1-s]\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ {\vphantom{\mathbb{S}}}^{n}\mathbb{S})

(8)

For the remaining strain increment, the KKT condition (Equation
2
) is replaced with
\varPhi=0
, which, coupled with Equation
3
, form the DAEs to be solved to calculate
\Delta{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}_{i}
. The solution for the increment of the plastic multiplier,
\Delta\lambda
, is typically obtained using an iterative procedure for implicit integration, whereas it can be obtained analytically for explicit integration from
\varPhi(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\mathbb{A})=0
. This makes explicit time integrators numerically efficient, but they require small time steps to ensure numerical stability.

2.3
ML-accelerated integration of a generic small-strain plasticity model

The idea of this paper is to use ML to find an explicit time integrator,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}^{\mathrm{ML}}
, that can handle large strain increments with good accuracy and numerical stability. Specifically, we propose to use NNs to formulate
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}^{\mathrm{ML}}
for each state variable,
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}
, as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(

{\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}{}^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+s\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ [1-s]\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ {\vphantom{\mathbb{S}}}^{n}\mathbb{S})=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\mathcal{NN}_{{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}},\,1,\,k}(\mathbb{I})\frac{\partial I_{k}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{tr}}}+\mathcal{NN}_{{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}},\,2,\,k}(\mathbb{I})\frac{\partial I_{k}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{lim}}}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{a}}}\mathcal{NN}_{{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}},\,j+2,\,k}(\mathbb{I})\frac{\partial I_{k}}{\partial{\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{j}}\right]

(9)

where
\mathcal{NN}
are scalar outputs of an NN, and
\mathbb{I}=\{{I}_{1},\ {I}_{2},\ \cdots,\ {I}_{N}\}
is a set of suitable invariants that depends on
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{tr}}
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{lim}}
, and
{\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}}{}^{n}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{j}
. The choice to express the NNs in invariants eliminates coordinate system dependence and lowers their dimensions.

When applying the proposed time integration scheme, the increments of the state variables are computed as

\Delta\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}=c\,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}^{\mathrm{NN}}

(10)

where we introduce the correction factor,
c
, to ensure that
\varPhi(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\mathbb{A})=0
is satisfied exactly during plastic loading.

3
Prototype model: Plasticity model with kinematic hardening

This section first presents the prototype plasticity model, which will be used to train and evaluate the proposed NN-based time integration scheme. This is followed by a detailed description of the NN-based integration.

3.1
Prototype plasticity model

Considering the generic formulation of a small-strain plasticity model explained in Section
2.1
, the prototype plasticity model assumes linear isotropic elasticity and adopts kinematic hardening.
The set of state variables for this model is
\mathbb{S}=\{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm{p}},\,\boldsymbol{b}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}
is the plastic strain and
\boldsymbol{b}
is the state variable associated with kinematic hardening. The free-energy density function,
\varPsi
, is formulated as

\displaystyle\varPsi\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\,\mathbb{S}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,\left[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}\right]:\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}^{\mathrm{e}}:\left[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}\right]+\frac{1}{3}\,H_{\rm{kin}}\,\boldsymbol{b}:\boldsymbol{b}

(11)

where
H_{\rm{kin}}
is the kinematic hardening modulus. Isotropic elasticity is considered such that the elasticity tensor,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}^{\mathrm{e}}
, is formulated as

\quad\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}^{\rm{e}}=2\,G\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}^{{}^{\mathrm{dev}}}+K_{\rm{b}}\,\boldsymbol{I}\otimes\boldsymbol{I}\quad\text{where}\quad K_{\mathrm{b}}=\frac{E\,G}{3\left(3\,G-E\right)}

(12)

G
and
E
are the shear and Young’s moduli, respectively.

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}^{{}^{\mathrm{dev}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}-\boldsymbol{I}\otimes\boldsymbol{I}/3
is the 4th order deviatoric identity tensor, where
\boldsymbol{I}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}
are the second- and fourth-order identity tensors.
The stress,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and the (hardening) variables,
\mathbb{A}=\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{p}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\}
, are obtained as

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{\partial\varPsi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}^{\mathrm{e}}:\left[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}\right],\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\rm p}=-\frac{\partial\varPsi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm p}}=\boldsymbol{\sigma},\quad{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=-\frac{\partial\varPsi}{\partial\boldsymbol{b}}=-\frac{2}{3}\,H_{\rm kin}\,\boldsymbol{b}

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\rm p}
is the plastic stress and
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
the back-stress.
The plasticity model adopts the isotropic von Mises yield function, which is formulated as

\varPhi\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\mathbb{A}\right)=f^{\mathrm{vM}}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{dev}}_{\mathrm{red}}\right)-Y_{0},\quad f^{\mathrm{vM}}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{dev}}_{\mathrm{red}}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{dev}}:\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{dev}}}\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{dev}}={\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}^{\mathrm{dev}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}

(14)

Y_{0}
is the initial yield stress. The evolution of the plastic strain,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}
, follows the associative flow rule

\dot{\boldsymbol{{\epsilon}}}^{\rm{p}}=\dot{\lambda}\,\frac{\partial\varPhi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\dot{\lambda}\,\boldsymbol{\nu}\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{\nu}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\,\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{dev}}}{\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{dev}}\right|\right|}

(15)

The evolution of the back-stress,
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, is assumed to follow the Armstrong-Frederick kinematic hardening law
[
2
]
as

\dot{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}=-\frac{2}{3}\,H_{\rm{kin}}\,\dot{\boldsymbol{b}}\quad\text{where}\quad\dot{\boldsymbol{b}}=\dot{\lambda}\left[-\boldsymbol{\nu}+\frac{3}{2}\,\frac{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{{\beta}_{\infty}}\right]

(16)

{{\beta}_{\infty}}
is the saturation value of the back-stress. The material model parameter values are presented in Table
1
, which are inspired by those calibrated against experimental data for isotropic R260 pearlitic rail steel
[
42
]
. The prototype material model, integrated with the implicit Backward Euler time integration algorithm to solve the set of coupled DAEs in Equations
2
,
15
, and
16
, is employed for training data generation and performance evaluation of the prototype model, integrated with the NN-based integration scheme in material point simulations; see Sections
4.2
and

respectively for further details.

3.2
NN-based integration of the prototype plasticity model

When integrating the prototype plasticity model with the proposed explicit NN-based time integration scheme described in Section
2.3
, for a given
\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, we first calculate
\varPhi^{\mathrm{tr}}=\varPhi({\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}:\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon},{\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta})
. If
\varPhi^{\mathrm{tr}}<0
, the loading is elastic such that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{tr}}
,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}={\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}
, and
\boldsymbol{b}={\vphantom{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{b}
. Otherwise, if
\varPhi^{\mathrm{tr}}\geq 0
, we need to determine if the first part of the load step is elastic. If the material response at time
{\vphantom{t}}{}^{n}t
was elastic, i.e.,
{\vphantom{\varPhi}}{}^{n}\varPhi=\varPhi({\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\sigma},{\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta})<0
, this will be the case as illustrated in the left part of Figure
1
. However, even if
{\vphantom{\varPhi}}{}^{n}\varPhi=0
, the first part could be elastic due to a load direction change, see the right part of Figure
1
. We detect this case by evaluating if
\varPhi
decreases (i.e., becomes negative since the previous value was zero) at the beginning of the strain increment, specifically

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\varPhi({\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}:[\theta\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}],{\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial\theta}\right|_{\theta=0}\leq 0

(17)

If either case is satisfied, we compute the plastic part of the applied
\Delta\boldsymbol{\epsilon}

s\in[0,1]
is determined such that

\displaystyle f_{s}=\frac{3}{2}\left|\left|{\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{dev}}}}{}^{n}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{dev}}+s\,\left[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{tr,\,\,dev}}-{\vphantom{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{dev}}}}^{n}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{dev}}\right]-{\vphantom{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right|\right|^{2}-Y_{0}^{2}=0

(18)

which can be solved analytically.

4.1
Feed-Forward Neural Networks (FFNNs)

We use fully connected FFNNs to formulate
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}^{\mathrm{NN}}
through

\mathcal{NN}_{\,\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i},\,1}
-
\mathcal{NN}_{\,\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i},\,3}
for each state variable,
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}
.
A fully connected FFNN establishes a mapping from an input vector, in our case
\hat{\mathbb{J}}
, to an output vector, in our case
\mathcal{NN}_{\,\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i},\,1}
-
\mathcal{NN}_{\,\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i},\,3}
, by propagating information through
m
hidden layers. Each hidden layer
l
computes its output
\underline{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l}
using an activation function
a_{l}
as
\underline{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l}=a_{l}\left(\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{l}\,\underline{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l-1}+\underline{\boldsymbol{b}}_{l}\right)
,
where
\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{l}
and
\underline{\boldsymbol{b}}_{l}
are trainable weight matrices and bias vectors associated with layer
l
. In this study, the activation function for all hidden layers is chosen as
a_{l}={\rm{tanh}}(x)
.
The considered FFNNs consist of
m=8
hidden layers with 8 neurons and are trained over 30000 epochs. The FFNNs’ weights and biases are randomly initialized before being optimized with the gradient-based Rectified Adam (RAdam) optimizer
[
28
]
in the PyTorch library
[
34
]
, using a learning rate of
10^{-4}
. Although the selected hyperparameters were determined through trial and error to provide satisfactory training performance, further improvements in training efficiency may be possible through automated hyperparameter optimization; see
[
21
]
.

To facilitate the optimization of the network parameters, i.e., weights and biases, during training
[
16
]
, and to prevent vanishing gradients caused by large input values to the activation function,
{\rm{tanh}}(x)

[
37
]
, scaling has been introduced as described in Section
3.2
.

Figure 3:
Schematic illustration of the inputs and outputs of the NNs, which are embedded in Equations
28
and
29
.

In Equations
28
and
29
, we use one FFNN to formulate the increment of each state variable,
\Delta\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}
, as illustrated in Figure
3
. Each FFNN produces three scalar outputs denoted by
\mathcal{NN}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i},1}-\mathcal{NN}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i},3}
. The reason for considering separate FFNNs rather than a single FFNN with six outputs is to keep the networks smaller, thereby improving training efficiency and better training performance.
After using the outputs of the FFNNs in Equations
28
and
29
to predict
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}^{\mathrm{NN}}
, the mean squared error type loss,
{\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}}
, can be calculated as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{data}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{data}}}\left[\left[{{\Delta\hat{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}_{i}}}\right]_{j}^{\rm{target}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{g}}}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right]^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{p}}\,\left|\left|\boldsymbol{p}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}\right|\right|_{2}^{2}

(36)

Here,
N_{\mathrm{data}}
denotes the total number of training data samples, and
\left[{{\Delta\hat{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}_{i}}}\right]^{\rm{target}}
is the target state variable increments, normalized with
\mathtt{SF}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}_{i}}
.
We apply L2 regularization to enhance the generalizability of the FFNNs
[
19
,
5
]
. This is done by augmenting the loss function with a differentiable penalty term corresponding to the square of the L2-norm of the network parameters
\boldsymbol{p}
, where
\lambda_{\rm{p}}=10^{-6}
.

A single deviatoric multiaxial pulsating strain path is generated over 10 loading cycles, each discretized into 400 time steps; see Figure

3

Concluding remarks

In this contribution, we have presented an NN-based framework to accelerate the time integration of DAEs in rate-independent nonlinear constitutive models.
The key features of the framework are

•

Exact encoding of the separation between elastic and plastic responses.

•

State variable updates formulated by neural networks with invariants as inputs.

•

Pre-identification of sufficient and necessary evolution directions based on the training data.

•

Exact fulfillment of the plastic consistency condition.

We have chosen a prototype material model with the von Mises yield function and nonlinear kinematic hardening. Under pulsating proportional multiaxial loading, we have generated training data for the FFNNs using the prototype model, integrated with the implicit backward Euler time integration algorithm.
While only 10 loading cycles were used to generate training data, the proposed NN-based time integration scheme gave very good accuracy for material point simulations.
Even when taking a single strain increment per half-cycle for 100 loading cycles, it achieved good predictions.
Furthermore, the framework’s performance has been evaluated in cyclic finite element (FE) simulations. The von Mises stress predictions were accurate, with a maximum error of less than
2\%
of the maximum stress over 100 cycles when using a single displacement increment per half-cycle. Moreover, the NN-based time integration is roughly 3 times faster than the reference FE model when taking a single displacement increment per half-cycle.
