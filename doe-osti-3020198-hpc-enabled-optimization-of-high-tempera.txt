B. Project Scope

This project was a collaborative effort between Lawrence Livermore National Security, LLC
(LLNS) as manager and operator of Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and
Materials Sciences, LLC, to develop a technology for design and optimization of heat
exchangers using powerful desktop and laptop computers. The project was originally designated
as a 12-month project, and consisted of 3# major tasks and the following 8# major deliverables:

 1) CFD models of 3D heat exchangers based on existing and new geometry.
 2) Validation against experimental data provided by MSC and published in the literature.
 3) CFD models of 3D unit cells based on TPMS.
 4) Surrogate models capable of delivering the gradients of the homogenized properties
 with respect to the parametrization.
 5) 3D design methodology using TO algorithms.
 6) Conventional reference and topology optimized designs.
 7) 3D optimized designs stored in a 3D printer build format.
 8) Verification of the improved performance.

All of the deliverables for this project were successfully completed with two no-cost time
extensions.

A No-Cost Time Extension letter was executed on September 22, 2021, to extend the term of the
project for an additional 12 months, to ensure enough time to complete the theoretical
development necessary for executing the tasks and hiring qualified staff. A second No-Cost Time
Extension letter was executed on September 6, 2022, to extend the term of the project for an
additional nine months, to complete the HPC simulations necessary for producing and validating
the deliverables of the project.

This project combines recent advances in topology optimization-based design, high-performance
computing (HPC), and additive manufacturing (AM) technology to develop high-pressure and
temperature heat exchanger (HEX) concepts with improved performance and reduction in
volume. The goal is to overcome the current design and economic limitations of conventional
manufacturing methods.

The design of thermal energy transfer systems is usually based on long-developed engineering
intuition or trial-and-error approaches. The geometries utilize relatively simple forms and
patterns to simplify the analysis and the production. However, as already demonstrated in several
pilot projects, the uncomplicated layouts are not optimal and fail to perform well for fully
coupled processes, in which standardized rules cannot capture the complexity of the interactions.
Therefore, a design methodology, such as topology optimization, which allows complete
geometric freedom and considers the holistic behavior of the system, significantly improves
efficiency. Topology optimization is an iterative design process which aims to find a material
distribution in a design domain by minimizing an objective and satisfying a set of constraints.
The process has gained huge popularity in solid mechanics applications and recently was
extended to fully coupled realistic large-scale 3D thermal fluid designs with examples shown in
Figure 1.

Updates of the design are based on the gradients of the objective and the constraints. Every
iteration requires the solution of a forward-state problem in the form of a fully coupled set of
partial differential equations describing the physics of the problem. The optimization process
requires the utilization of fine meshes to resolve the smallest design details, which necessitates
the solution of large systems of equations, ultimately excluding desktop computers from the
computational setup. Currently, the only feasible way to execute these optimization problems
within a reasonable amount of time is to use parallel computations. For smaller problems (Figure
2), the time for a single forward simulation is reduced to several hours. Thus, a full optimization
requiring hundreds of forward and adjoint simulations requires a significant computational
resource not available to even some of the biggest design companies.

 left and right) vs. the extruded 2D design (bottom left) can be clearly observed by checking the
 temperature distribution on the top plates. Instead of distributed flow channels though the 2D design
 volume, 3D topology optimization creates intriguing swirl-like flow patterns and mixes the cold and
 the heated regions of the fluid. b) 3D view of an optimized design for Re=5000 (top) together with
 streamlines of the flow (bottom) colored by the temperature [K] and velocity magnitude [m/s].

Complete freedom in the design space requires a discretization of the entire design domain,
resolving the physics down to the smallest length scale. Thus, a common approach to reduce the
computational cost is to restrict the design to be a spatially periodic microstructure, as shown in
Figures 2. In these cases, the design is built by repetitive cells with matching topology along the
periodic cell boundaries and internally spatially varying parameters such as wall thickness or
internal cell connectivity. In this work, the focus is on the so-called Triply Periodic Minimal
Surfaces (TPMS) (Gyroid structure, Schwarz-D/P) [1], as previous parametric studies suggest an
improvement of performance between 40% and 120% [2]. However, the methodology applies to
all kind of unit cells. For example, our studies utilize Octet-truss structures (Figure 4) and simple
cylinders and balls embedded in the unit cells. A main benefit of such design strategies is the
relatively easy enforcement of design constraints. An overhang constraint for 3D manufacturing
can be enforced directly on the topology of the unit cell, and subsequent design and optimization
phases can focus solely on the wall thickness. Even though the utilization of repetitive unit cells
significantly simplifies the design problem, the computational requirements are still extremely
high.

CRADA TC02373 Page 4

The focus of this work is to leverage HPC resources to develop a methodology that reduces
computational complexity by employing homogenization. The homogenization relies on a
separation of length scales. This assumption allows for the extraction of macroscopic properties
from the analysis of a single period unit cell. The basic idea can be explained using the heat
exchanger design shown in Figure 2. The design consists of an infill of periodic patterns. Instead
of resolving every small detail, several highly detailed smaller problems are solved once for a
single repetitive cell to identify homogenized properties representing the behavior on the global
(macro) level. The original state equations (Navier-Stokes) are employed for the repetitive cell,
and the macro system is modeled with the help of simplified Darcy-like equations combined with
heat transfer. The homogenized properties are stored, and the calculations do not need to be
repeated for subsequent design of HEXs utilizing these unit cells. The idea is demonstrated in
Figure 3.

C. Technical Accomplishments

The specific technical accomplishments were:
 1) Developed general 2D and 3D fluid flow solver for flow though geometries with arbitrary
 complexity.
 2) Expanded the applications of the homogenization theory and developed the necessary
 software tools for modeling the homogenized behavior.
 3) Developed physically consistent machine learning (ML) models for flow and heat
 transfer though unit cells with arbitrary geometry.
 4) Developed validated homogenized models.
 5) Developed discretization and software solvers for the global homogenized system.
 6) Developed the software for topology optimization.

The development of detailed CFD models started with the implementation of fully resolved
Comsol models. The utilization of commercial software significantly limited the scalability of
the simulations. For example, Comsol allowed runs only on up to 36 cores on Quartz, LLNL,
which provided a single flow simulation of a single unit cell in 30-40 minutes. Such execution
time is far from satisfactory for simulating the training data necessary for the ML training.
Switching to Ansys Fluent or StarCCM+ did not improve the solution time. In addition to the
poor scalability, all commercial packages require well-defined meshes to accurately resolve the
complex boundary of the geometry. The latter was a significant obstacle to automatizing the data
generation and the simulation process. A detailed investigation by a summer student hired on the
project demonstrated clearly that the time necessary for generating different realizations of even
simple geometries, such the Octet-Truss structure shown in Figure 4, more than triples the total
time for a single simulation, ruling out the utilization of any available commercial package.

 simulations have been obtained with the developed software.

Therefore, we implemented a fluid flow solver based on the so-called Brinkman penalization
approach [26] to resolve the above-mentioned bottleneck. The implementation is based on
existing development - the so-called Navier miniapp in the MFEM software library [8]
developed at LLNL. The solver is scalable to thousands of processes on current DoE CPU and
GPU HPC machines and has been utilized for simulating fully resolved fluid flow simulations of
complete heat sinks/exchangers, as the one shown in Figure 2, as well as unit cell CFD models
shown in Figure 4. The solver was validated against fully resolved flows around body-fitted
geometry obtained with Comsol (Figure 5) and against experimental results available in the
literature (Figure 7). The developed software will be committed to the MFEM repository and
will be utilized in future projects on the subject.

The next step focused on homogenization and upscaling the local simulations. Two common
upscaling approaches can be identified in the literature – the so-called Darcy flow model and its
nonlinear empirical extensions [3,4,5]. The Darcy flow model assumes a linear relation between
the averaged velocity and pressure gradients in the unit cell. It is valid for creeping flows and
cannot accurately represent faster flow regimes. The so-called Forchheimer extension requires
empirical parameter fitting and has a limited range of applicability. Other possible practical
empirical extensions, like Kozeny–Carman, Ergun, and Burke-Plummer, are valid only in limited
velocity ranges, for particular geometries of the unit cells, and with the assumption of isotropic
behavior. Therefore, after careful study focused on finding a general model applicable to a wide
range of fluid velocity ranges and arbitrary cell topologies, we decided to deploy a recently
developed homogenization theory for nonlinear filtration laws [6]. The theory is based on
rigorous mathematical proofs and covers a wide range of fluid flow regimes. However, a major
limitation is the lack of explicit analytical relation between the cell-averaged pressure gradients
and the fluid velocity. An attempt to utilize polynomial expansions is presented in [7]. The
application, however, is limited due to several expansion coefficients and difficulties with the
enforcement of the positive definiteness of the Jacobian with regards to the relation between the
averaged pressure gradient and the velocity.

velocity vector, we decided to utilize a fully implicit model based on the so-called universal
approximation [9,10], i.e., we used machine learning (ML) to discover the constitutive relation
based on data points in the velocity-pressure space. We generated pairs of pressure gradients and
corresponding velocity vectors for a selected unit cell topology by simulating fully resolved flow
through the cell with the already developed fluid mechanics solvers. The wall thickness was
selected as an additional parameter, which was entered as input to the ML model in order to
make the constitutive relation suitable for topology optimization. The resulting ML model
provided an excellent representation of the constitutive relation (Figure 6); however, similar to
the polynomial relation [7], its Jacobian is not always positive definite. The latter introduces non-
physical behavior in the conservation partial differential equation (PDE), which models the
global averaged fluid velocity distribution and the pressure within the design domain. To
circumvent the above issue, we directly modeled the Jacobian of the constitutive relation by
representing it as a finite difference (FD) approximation between two neighbor data points [11].
The linear interpolation between the data points required a dense set of data points to decrease
the interpolation error. An alternative is the utilization of integral representation [12,13]. In both
the FD approximation case and the integral representation case, the cost of evaluating the
constitutive relation is increased slightly. However, a consistent physical behavior is enforced
strictly on the computational model.

Another essential aspect which should be mentioned is the cost of data generation. For 2D
models, a solution for a single-unit cell can be obtained in 1-2 minutes on 36 cores node (2x Intel
Xeon E5-2695). The considered pressure-velocity range needs several thousand data points to
obtain a reliable ML model (Figure 6). Increasing the dimension to 3D, however, increases the
number of data points by an order of magnitude, from ten thousand to one hundred thousand.
Such a significant increase in the number of unit cell simulations combined with an order of
magnitude increase in the computational cost per unit cell puts a considerable weight on the cost
of generating the training data. Therefore, an alternative feasible direction requiring additional
research is the utilization of experiments for data generation or a combination of experiment and
computational modeling.

The unit cell ML model was validated using experimental data from the literature (Figure 7) and
body-fitted CFD results using Comsol (Figure 5). As can be seen in Figure 7, the ML model
matches the experimental results for a particular direction. Furthermore, it can represent the
anisotropic behavior of the fluid flow, i.e., the relation between the pressure gradient and the
velocity vector depends on the direction of the flow relative to the unit cell orientation. The ML
model follows the experimental results for the considered pressure-velocity range for directions
aligned with the cell sides. However, for flow directions aligned with diagonals of the cell, the
ML relation differs significantly, especially in the fast flow regime. This deviation is expected as
the geometry is not isotropic, and exact numerical homogenization will result in an anisotropic
permeability tensor even in the linear Darcy regime.

Validation against experimental results found in the literature [28] was also performed, with
results presented in Figure 8. A major issue with all published experimental results is the
boundary condition effect, i.e., the number of unit cells is relatively small, and the boundaries

CRADA TC02373 Page 9

applied to the experiment affect the flow in the bulk of the volume. Thus, the relation between
average pressure gradient and velocity is valid only for the tested configuration and cannot
represent the general constitutive behavior in a design constructed by many unit cells. The effect
was confirmed numerically by varying the boundary conditions on four super-cell sides and
using configurations from 4x4x4 to 10x10x10 unit cells.

We used a 2D array of cylinders with spatially varying radii to validate the model for spatially
varying cell configurations, as shown in Figure 9. As it can be clearly observed, the
homogenized model provides an excellent representation of the flow in the bulk of the
computational domain. The averaged flows obtained with homogenized and fully resolved
models differ close to the inlet and outlet. The latter is expected as the homogenization is
mathematically valid for an infinite number of repetitive unit cells and requires correction around
the boundaries of the domain. The correction can be obtained through an additional unit-cell ML
model trained precisely for the enforced boundary conditions, as shown in [14].

On a global level, the fluid flow is modeled by a homogenized PDE, a simple conservation law
with a non-linear constitutive relation between pressure gradients and velocity. The Jacobian of
the constitutive relation is obtained by ML and is guaranteed to be positive definite by
construction. However, the Jacobian is not symmetric. Thus, for high-speed flows, the
asymmetric part of the Jacobian results in equivalent advection, which might require stabilization
of the discretized system of equations. Our implementation is based on continuous Galerkin
FEM. Better conservation properties and a more stable discretized set of equations can be
obtained by deploying discontinuous Galerkin discretizations, which is a subject of future
research. The solution time of the global homogenized system is several seconds, which enables
the optimization process on a small desktop or laptop computer.

The homogenized model for the convective heat transfer follows a similar approach as the fluid
flow homogenization described earlier. For incompressible fluid flow, the global homogenized
PDE is a linear diffusion equation with a non-symmetric diffusion tensor [21,22]. The tensor
depends on the fluid velocity distribution within the unit cell. Thus, the unit cell homogenization
problem can be solved only with the local unit cell fluid flow problem. The above should be
carefully considered in future studies aiming to replace the local unit cell solutions with
experimental measurements.

Several topology optimization [23] problems were formulated and solved in order to exemplify
the homogenization theory assisted by ML techniques. For fluid flow optimization, the selected
objective was to minimize the power dissipation in the design domain subject to a volume
constraint on the fluid-occupied domain with the topology-optimized design shown in Figure 11.
For heat transfer, a typical objective is to minimize the thermal compliance subject to constraints

Finally, the manufacturability of the obtained design was validated by Materials Sciences, LLC.
The design was transferred directly to the in-house software developed at Materials Sciences,
LLC. The transfer was performed through a sequence of 2D slices generated directly from the
FEM discretization in MFEM. The approach allows for the 3D printing of designs with arbitrary
complexity if the printer resolution can represent the pixelized 2D slices. The above is a huge
step toward automatizing the manufacturing process, as it removes the necessity of explicit
geometry representation and the cumbersome generation of STL (stereolithography) CAD format
files. Furthermore, the direct generation of 2D slices represents exactly the optimized geometry
and removes the approximation error usually imposed by the linear triangular surface
representation in the STL file format.

D. Expected Economic Impact

The project enables the design and fabrication of advanced HEX outperforming currently
available designs with respect to heat transfer, weight, compactness, geometric flexibility, and
robustness. The above advantages will enable a significant decrease in energy used by domestic
gasoline and electric ground transportation vehicles, while offering the potential to extend the
range of the emerging electric vertical take-off (eVTOL) vehicles. Further, the competitiveness
of US manufacturing will be enhanced with energy savings from the heat transfer process and
from the actual device production in terms of material and production time. The adaptability of
the design through optimization provides replacement of existing installations with improved, or
if necessary, an exact match of the existing performance.

For example, automotive air conditioning consumes up to 30% of the fuel for internal
combustion engine cars and can reduce the battery range by up to 40% for electric vehicles [15,
16, 17]. Reducing the temperature difference between the ambient and the condensing
temperature can save up to 30% of the compressor power and increase the coefficient of
performance by 45%. Additional improvements (up to 70% of power savings) can be achieved
through new components in the cooling system and combinations of the different strategies and
elements. An essential requirement for all these methods is the need for compact and lightweight
HEX designs - the main impact of this work. The estimate for a twice lower temperature
difference between the condensing and the outdoor temperature, achievable with topology-
optimized heat exchangers, would increase the battery range for electric cars by 13% and reduce
the fuel consumption by 10% for combustion engine cars. When applied to all light-duty vehicles
and projected on a national scale, such a modification would result in 9.2% gasoline savings in
the United States [18].

CRADA TC02373 Page 13

Additionally, emerging eVTOL vehicles show great potential for everyday transport at reduced
energy relative to electric vehicles. Some eVTOLs claim to be twice as efficient as commercially
available electric vehicles at double the transport speed [19]. However, take-off and landing
require much more power than turning the wheels of a typical road vehicle. Consequently,
eVTOL batteries must be able to discharge at rates roughly 10 times faster than the batteries in
electric road vehicles [20]. This rapid discharge produces an enormous amount of heat that
require eVTOL to have specialized cooling systems to preserve both reliability and safety. This
is a crucial difference between electric road vehicles and electric aircraft: Designers of trucks and
cars do not have any need to radically improve either their power output or their cooling systems,
as this would add cost without improving performance. A future realization has the potential for
a 50% decrease in domestic transportation energy use, relative to current electric road vehicles.

D.1 Specific Benefits

Benefits to DOE

This project provides an important extension of LLNL’s design and optimization capabilities to
tackle energy related problems. LLNL’s background in development, modeling and simulations
of coupled fluid/thermal/solid mechanics problems is extended to include multiple fluid/solid
phases combined with ML. These novel capabilities will open new directions to tackle fully
coupled problems by reducing the computational requirements. The developed methodology
provides a solid foundation for approaching designs with similar physics in a timely manner, i.e.,
transport problems involving several phases/constituents. With the new development 1) we can
create new physically consistent homogenization models capable of representing fast flow through
porous media, 2) we reduce the pre- and post-processing cost for arbitrary complex geometries,
and 3) we can immediately deploy new ML models to similar non-linear physical behavior, such
as elasto-plastic optimization problems, coupled thermo-mechanics and coupled multi-fluid
reactors.

Benefits to Industry

The main benefit to the industry is the newly developed capability for design and analysis of
porous structures without the need for large scale HPC machines. By designing a suitable
experiment, the methodology can be extended further (subject to additional research) to avoid the
expensive data generation for training of the unit cell ML. The workflow results in
manufacturable designs without any manual intervention or postprocessing steps, allowing for
direct transfer to any additive manufacturing machine without an explicit geometry
representation. The lack of necessity for explicit geometry allows manufacturing of extremely
complex structures, further pushing the boundaries of this relatively new manufacturing
technology.
