Qualitative assessment of the clinical meaningfulness of MDS-UPDRS part III items in early-stage Parkinson's
Authors: Sophie Cleanthous, Roger A Barker, Milton Biagioni, David T Dexter, Tanya Simuni, Thomas Morel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDevelopers of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) for early-stage Parkinson's are in dialogue with regulators to identify the relative merits of the Movement Disorder Society – Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) for detecting clinically meaningful changes in patients’ daily functioning during early-stage disease. We report the first qualitative assessment of MDS-UPDRS part III items’ clinical meaningfulness for use in DMT pivotal trials.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 40 patients and 20 movement disorder neurologists. Evidence was synthesized using an interpretation framework to evaluate items according to four appraisal components.ResultsBased on synthesized evidence for each appraisal component: Patient feedback indicated all items were at least somewhat meaningful to patients; 12/18 items were classified as “highly meaningful” given their linkage to motor/daily activities functioning. See Interactive Supplement .4/18 items were endorsed by >90% of neurologists as clinically relevant in early-stage Parkinson's (“highly relevant”).Evidence from neurologists was supportive of 6/18 items in terms of their usefulness for capturing disease progression (“highly useful”).Neurologist feedback suggested response option distinction/assessment specificity is inconsistent across items.Together, qualitative evidence endorsed the value of items 3.3 Rigidity, 3.4 Finger tapping, 3.5 Hand movements, 3.6 Pronation-supination, 3.7 Toe tapping, and 3.8 Leg agility across all four appraisal components.ConclusionsA subset of limb-related rigidity/bradykinesia items may be most clinically meaningful for detecting changes in early-stage Parkinson's. Introduction

Parkinson's is one of the fastest-growing neurological diseases and the third leading cause of neurological disease burden in the global ageing population. 1 The development of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) that can slow or halt functional decline and delay loss of quality of life and independence in the early stages of disease, by treating the underlying pathobiology of Parkinson's, is a key unmet need.

With numerous promising DMT candidates under investigation to treat people with early-stage Parkinson's, 2 there is a need for a sensitive and meaningful clinical outcome assessment (COA) to serve as a primary efficacy outcome measure in this target population. 3 As reflected in regulatory guidance, this COA not only needs to be responsive to changes indicative of disease progression, but must also reflect how patients feel or function in day-to-day life (e.g., ability to perform relevant tasks or functions impacted by the disease) to ascertain the unequivocal clinical benefit of the therapeutic intervention.4–6

The Movement Disorder Society – Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) is the current gold standard in clinical practice for measuring Parkinson's severity, including motor features rated by clinicians (part III), and motor (part II) and non-motor (part I) features reported by patients. Originally developed without consideration of disease stage, the MDS-UPDRS was mostly developed and validated in patients with moderate- to late-stage Parkinson's. 7 With the shift towards DMT intervention earlier in the disease continuum, bespoke analyses evaluating the ability of MDS-UPDRS parts II and III to assess the earliest features of Parkinson's have demonstrated psychometric limitations (e.g., floor effects, suboptimal scale-to-sample targeting, disordered thresholds for some items).8,9 In the context of selecting a primary clinical efficacy endpoint for DMT pivotal trials, these findings suggest that some MDS-UPDRS items may not be sufficiently sensitive to detect disease progression in early-stage Parkinson's. 3

Both quantitative and qualitative evidence is required to inform the selection of a suitable COA that translates into a robust, reliable, patient-centered, and interpretable primary endpoint to assess disease progression and DMT benefit in clinical trials for this population. While insights from movement disorder neurologists are key to discern the utility of a COA in clinical trial settings, feedback from those living with Parkinson's must also be sought to endorse its meaningfulness to patients, aligned with regulatory guidance. 6 The meaningfulness of MDS-UPDRS items to people with early-stage Parkinson's – in other words, the extent to which the concepts captured by each item are linked to individuals’ overall daily life (how it affects their ability to feel, function, and survive) – remains to be confirmed.10–12

To this end, and building upon previous quantitative data,8,13,14 we report a novel qualitative assessment of the clinician-reported MDS-UPDRS part III items, crucially from the perspective of both people living with Parkinson's and movement disorder neurologists.

Methods

Objectives and study design

The overarching aim of this study was to assess the extent to which MDS-UPDRS part III items reflect a clinically meaningful COA in the context of DMT pivotal trials targeting early-stage Parkinson's. Evidence across four research questions was collected from people with early-stage Parkinson's and movement disorder neurologists and synthesized to inform this overall objective.

Evidence collected from individuals living with early-stage Parkinson's

1. Meaningfulness to patients: People living with early-stage Parkinson's were asked to verbalize to what extent the movement or motor sign assessed by each MDS-UPDRS part III item relates to aspects of disease they consider meaningful and impacts on their activities of daily living (i.e., how it affects their ability to feel, function, and survive).

Ethical approval

The study protocol, informed consent form and all participant-facing documents were submitted to Western Institutional Review Board – Copernicus Group (WCG IRB) for review in the US (tracking ID: 20222732) and subsequently local ethics boards for individual research sites: WCG IRB and Mass General Brigham IRB. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

People aged 40–85 years with idiopathic Parkinson's diagnosed by a movement disorder specialist within the past 2 years were recruited from two clinical sites in the United States (US). The study recruited only participants with Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) stage ≤2.

Movement disorder neurologists were identified based on clinical expertise, with the aim of recruiting equal numbers of participants across two groups: 1) clinically experienced key opinion leaders in movement disorders, and 2) principal/sub-principal investigators with ≥3 years’ experience of administering the MDS-UPDRS. See Supplementary Methods for more information.

Qualitative analysis

All interviews were recorded, transcribed verbatim and coded manually using ATLAS.ti, in line with a structured codebook and analysis guide developed to match the research objectives. Coding with ATLAS.ti was used to summarize the interview transcripts; however, further interpretation was conducted separately by the research team at the analysis stage. No artificial intelligence features were used.

Concept elicitation data were coded using detailed line-by-line inductive and deductive coding and analyzed thematically,15–17 before they were synthesized using standard analytical techniques for conceptual model development. 10 These data were consolidated with findings from a previous phase of this research initiative, describing a conceptual model of the patient experience in early-stage Parkinson's. 10 The revised (consolidated) conceptual model was used as an anchor to assess the meaningfulness of each MDS-UPDRS part III item from the patient perspective. Cognitive debriefing data were coded thematically using structured multiple-level pre-set codes capturing spontaneous or probed feedback in relation to each of the four research questions.

Interpretation of clinical meaningfulness through evidence synthesis

To achieve the study's overarching aim, an interpretation framework was developed to synthesize the collected evidence, by defining different levels of evidence available for each of the four specific research questions (or “appraisal components”; Figure 2). Considering the nature of qualitative research methods, no a priori cut-offs or frequency benchmarks were set. Instead, the specific criteria within the framework were refined through relative comparison of the available evidence, in consultation with clinical and regulatory experts/research collaborators (Figure 2).

Interpretation framework of qualitative evidence.

Two independent research team members (SC, VS) assessed the categorization of evidence for each item across all four appraisal components, in line with the framework, and subsequently appraised the extent to which the overall evidence supported an item's clinical meaningfulness in the context of DMT pivotal trials in early-stage Parkinson's, resulting in three item classifications: Items for which the qualitative findings support their potential value to assess disease progression (where evidence was supportive across the majority of the four appraisal components).Items for which the qualitative findings did not support their potential value to assess disease progression (where evidence on at least one of the four appraisal components was not supportive).Items for which qualitative evidence was mixed (where evidence was discordant across all four appraisal components).

Results

Participant characteristics and demographics

A total of 40 people with early-stage Parkinson's and 20 movement disorder neurologists were interviewed (Table 1). People living with Parkinson's had a mean age of 68 years, were diagnosed a mean of 1 year prior to interview, and the majority had H&Y stage 2 disease (n = 30 [75%]). Approximately half were receiving symptomatic treatment (n = 21 [53%]) while the rest were treatment-naïve.

Consolidated conceptual model of patient experience

Previous phases of this work identified key aspects related to the lived experience of people with early-stage Parkinson's according to feedback from patients (including patient experts), caregivers, and movement disorder neurologists, which were categorized in a conceptual model as three core domains: Motor Symptoms/Functions, Non-Motor Symptoms/Functions, and Impacts. 10 Consolidated with evidence gathered in the current study, we present the fourth iteration of this conceptual model (Figure 3).

Consolidated conceptual model of the patient experience in early-stage Parkinson's. This fourth iteration of a conceptual model of the patient experience consolidates feedback gathered from interviews with people living with early-stage Parkinson's (n = 208), patient experts (n = 9), relatives of people with early-stage Parkinson's (n = 9) and movement disorder neurologists (n = 29), including participants from the current study, across 8 years of research. 10

The current evidence from people living with early-stage Parkinson's further endorsed the comprehensiveness of the previous model. 10 Additionally, feedback gathered from interviewed neurologists informed further granularity and refinement of the concept categorization; specifically, the distinction between motor/non-motor signs and symptoms and motor/non-motor functioning, as well as the distinction between impacts that are more proximal and tangible to daily activities functioning versus impacts on psychosocial functioning (Figure 3). 10

Clinical meaningfulness of MDS-UPDRS part III items

Meaningfulness to people living with early-stage Parkinson's

When asked to verbalize to what extent the movement or motor sign assessed by each MDS-UPDRS part III item relates to aspects of disease they consider meaningful and impacts on their activities of daily living (i.e., how it affects their ability to feel, function, and survive), participants with early-stage Parkinson's provided examples of some degree of impact on their lives for every item, indicating that all items are to some extent “meaningful”. However, both the types of impact as well as the volume of suggested examples of impact varied across the items, as illustrated in the Interactive Supplement .

To infer item meaningfulness as per the interpretation framework (Figure 2), the contextualized examples presented by participants during the interviews were summarized and categorized based on the consolidated conceptual model (Figure 3). Using this approach, twelve items were classified as “highly meaningful” (Table 2) as they were linked to multiple examples of current impact on aspects of the patient experience, including motor and/or daily activities functioning. Two items were classified as “meaningful”, as whilst they were linked to examples of current impacts on motor and/or daily activities functioning, there were fewer of them and they were less diverse than for “highly meaningful” items. Finally, four items fell into the “less meaningful” category, as they were linked to singular or no examples of impact on motor and/or daily activities functioning. Exemplar items from each category – specifically, 3.3 Rigidity (“highly meaningful”), 3.7 Toe tapping (“meaningful”), and 3.2 Facial expression (“less meaningful”) – are presented in Table 3. The concepts associated with each item, with relevant exemplar quotations, are illustrated in the Interactive Supplement .

Clinical relevance from the perspective of movement disorder neurologists

All MDS-UPDRS part III items have some degree of clinical relevance in early-stage Parkinson's according to movement disorder neurologists’ feedback, with four items being endorsed by the vast majority (>90%) of neurologists and hence categorized as “highly relevant” according to the interpretation framework (Table 2; Figure 4). Seven items were categorized as “relevant” as their clinical relevance in early-stage Parkinson's was endorsed by 51%–90% of interviewed neurologists. Two items were categorized as “less relevant”, based on endorsement by a minority of interviewed neurologists (20%–50%), whilst one item was endorsed by <20% and thus categorized as “not relevant”; specific feedback indicated that the motor features associated with these items would be minor or absent in patients with early-stage disease (Supplementary Table S1). Four items (related to tremor) were classified as “relevance linked to phenotype”, as endorsement from neurologists was dependent on disease phenotype (Table 2; Supplementary Table S1).

Usefulness for capturing disease progression

An item's usefulness for capturing disease progression was inferred based on: 1) whether there was direct feedback from interviewed neurologists endorsing its usefulness for making progression-related clinical decisions; and 2) the percentage of neurologists who identified the item when asked which MDS-UPDRS part III items were the most indicative of disease worsening/progression (Figure 2). The combination of identification by >20% of neurologists in response to the latter question (Figure 5), together with explicit endorsement of usefulness for making progression-related clinical decisions (Supplementary Table S2), led to the categorization of six items as “highly useful” (Table 2). Two items were classified as “useful”, as whilst they were explicitly endorsed as useful for making progression-related clinical decisions, they were identified by ≤20% of neurologists as among the most indicative of disease worsening/progression. Seven items were categorized as “less useful”, as they had no/mixed feedback in relation to their usefulness for making disease progression-related clinical decisions, but were identified by ≥10% of neurologists as being among the items most indicative of disease worsening/progression. Three items were classified as “not useful”, as their usefulness was not endorsed by neurologists’ feedback; in parallel, they were identified by <10% of neurologists as among the most indicative of disease worsening/progression. Response option distinction/specificity of assessment

To evaluate this appraisal component, neurologists were asked to describe to what extent they considered the five-level response categories for each item to be distinct and easy to select from. Items were categorized depending on the degree of issues associated with the distinction and specificity of their assessment, as defined in the interpretation framework (Figure 2). Based on this, there were “no consistent issues identified” for one item, feedback for which was limited to patient comorbidities and the effort required to perform the assessment (Table 2; Supplementary Table S3). Nine items had “some issues identified” with feedback solely related to difficulty rating/assessing them. Eight items had “consistent issues identified” across multiple areas defined in the interpretation framework, supported by feedback from >2 interviewed neurologists. “Extensive issues” were not identified for any item. Synthesis of the qualitative evidence on MDS-UPDRS part III items’ clinical meaningfulness

Overall, the qualitative evidence collected from people living with early-stage Parkinson's and movement disorder neurologists across all appraisal components endorsed the potential value of items 3.3 Rigidity, 3.4 Finger tapping, 3.5 Hand movements, 3.6 Pronation-supination, 3.7 Toe tapping, and 3.8 Leg agility to assess disease progression in the context of DMT pivotal trials targeting early-stage Parkinson's (Table 2).

Items not endorsed by the qualitative evidence were 3.11 Freezing of gait, 3.15 Postural tremor of hands, 3.16 Kinetic tremor of hands, 3.17 Rest tremor amplitude, and 3.18 Rest tremor constancy (Table 2). The remaining items had mixed evidence in terms of their potential value to assess disease progression in the context of DMT pivotal trials in this population (Table 2).

Discussion

Availability of a sensitive and clinically meaningful primary endpoint is an essential unmet need for DMT trials in early-stage Parkinson's. Extensive recent dialogue between regulatory agencies and developers of DMTs for early-stage Parkinson's has focused on identifying the relative merits of MDS-UPDRS part II or part III as primary endpoints to detect clinically meaningful changes in patient daily functioning during DMT registrational trials, and the potential of innovative endpoint constructs, including sub-scores comprising a subset of items that may better reflect outcomes that matter most to patients. Our new study, the first of its kind to undertake a qualitative assessment of the MDS-UPDRS part III with people living with early-stage Parkinson's and movement disorder neurologists, suggests that a subset of items covering rigidity and bradykinesia (items 3.3–3.8) are the most clinically meaningful items, with documented links to impact on patient motor functioning and daily activities in addition to potential for capturing disease progression in the context of DMT pivotal trials. This corroborates findings from previous qualitative studies, in which bradykinesia and rigidity were frequently identified as central to the patient experience of early-stage Parkinson's, and supports the possible development of a sub-score based on these items.10,11,18,19

Seven additional items were flagged during the current study as potentially relevant but displaying either inherent issues or discordant supportive evidence (i.e., items 3.1 Speech, 3.2 Facial expression, 3.9 Arising from chair, 3.10 Gait, 3.12 Postural instability, 3.13 Posture, and 3.14 Global spontaneity of movement). For instance, items relating to gait and global bradykinesia, although highly meaningful to patients, presented response option issues that challenged their utility in the context of DMT pivotal trials. In addition, item 3.11 Freezing of gait was not endorsed by the qualitative evidence when considered across the four appraisal components. While items relating to postural instability and gait disturbance (3.10–3.13) assess key features that define progression in more advanced disease, they have a floor effect 8 ; in combination with our findings, this suggests they may not be sensitive or meaningful enough in early-stage Parkinson's.

The potential value of items 3.15–3.18 (relating to tremor) to assess disease progression in early-stage Parkinson's was also not endorsed by the qualitative evidence gathered in our study. Although most tremor-related items were highly meaningful to patients, they were not endorsed due to evidence from neurologists indicating variable relevance in early-stage Parkinson's and a lack of usefulness for capturing disease progression. Tremor severity is considered to be distinct from non-tremor manifestations of Parkinson's, possibly having a different neural basis to rigidity/bradykinesia features.20,21 It has been proposed that the MDS-UPDRS part III tremor items should be considered separately to non-tremor items, due to their different domain-specific patterns in relation to disease severity/progression, highlighting the value of a potential sub-score.21,22

These qualitative takeaways fully align with findings from a parallel stream of research, in which we explored the clinical meaningfulness of MDS-UPDRS part III items in early-stage Parkinson's from a quantitative perspective, using psychometric methods. 13 In that study, using different methods in a separate cohort of people with early-stage Parkinson's, a sub-score covering the same limb-related bradykinesia/rigidity items (3.3–3.8) demonstrated good measurement properties, suggesting that it could capture change in the context of DMT trials targeting disease progression in this population. 13 Still, a gap remained at the mildest end of the severity continuum for these features. 13

Looking forward, the development of composite endpoints that combine clinician-rated assessments (e.g., MDS UPDRS part III or a relevant subset) with novel patient-reported outcome instruments that directly capture the earliest lived experience, and objective digital measures (e.g., of motor activity), represents an attractive strategy to assess disease progression in a comprehensive manner. While any discordance between the individual components would warrant further investigation, conversely, when all components point in the same direction, the totality of evidence would support that a clinically meaningful treatment benefit has been achieved. This approach is particularly valuable for Parkinson's, where no validated biomarker of progression exists, the clinical phenotype is highly heterogeneous, and no single COA fully captures the multidimensional nature of disease trajectory.

Overall, this extensive and innovative qualitative research, incorporating the perspectives of both people living with Parkinson's and neurologists, will contribute to evidence-based decision-making on the most viable COA to assess disease progression in early-stage Parkinson's. The learnings from this research constitute a powerful contribution to the ongoing precompetitive initiatives, led by the Critical Path for Parkinson's Consortium and the Michael J. Fox Foundation for Parkinson's Research, to inform the definition of viable primary endpoint constructs for DMT pivotal trials in early-stage Parkinson's.23–25 To ultimately make a decision regarding a primary clinical efficacy endpoint for this population, rigorous evaluation of consolidated qualitative and quantitative (e.g., item-level responsiveness) evidence for existing and novel COAs, including data from interventional clinical trials, will be required.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this research is the inclusion of both people living with early-stage Parkinson's (clinically defined with the input of clinical experts) and movement disorder neurologists, with large numbers of participants in each group for a qualitative cognitive debriefing study. 26 This collaboration with people living with early-stage Parkinson's reflects current efforts to evaluate concepts of interest that are the most relevant to patients’ daily functioning, as recommended by regulatory bodies, to underpin patient-focused drug development for early-stage Parkinson's.

Given the MDS-UPDRS part III is not intended to be a self-reported outcome, feedback from people living with early-stage Parkinson's should be interpreted with this in mind. People living with early-stage Parkinson's included in this study were highly educated, located in the US only, and there was a lack of ethnic diversity, despite significant recruitment efforts to address this. Due to the heterogeneity of people living with Parkinson's, the results of this study alone may therefore not be representative of the global Parkinson's community. Another limitation was that due to the semi-structured nature of the interview guides, some questions were not addressed to all participants (particularly those for neurologists related to response option/specificity of assessment issues). Given the qualitative nature of the methods and generated evidence, it was not possible to assess the items using any pre-specified objective criteria; however, a systematic approach was taken to develop and refine the interpretation framework, involving expert collaborators with different research backgrounds.

Conclusions

This is the first study of its kind to evaluate the clinical meaningfulness of MDS-UPDRS part III items in early-stage Parkinson's from a qualitative perspective. Its findings suggest that a subset of items covering limb-related rigidity and bradykinesia may be the most meaningful and clinically relevant for detecting disease progression in the context of DMT pivotal trials in early-stage Parkinson's. These conclusions are strengthened by the combination of perspectives from both people living with early-stage Parkinson's and movement disorder neurologists, with synthesized evidence showcasing the meaningfulness of these items on patients’ lives, as documented by their linkage to impacts on motor and daily activities functioning, as well as their clinical relevance, usefulness for assessing disease progression, and response option distinction/assessment specificity according to neurologists. Overall, our findings consolidate previous qualitative and quantitative evidence and will inform the ongoing debate and decision-making across the Parkinson's community regarding options for sensitive, clinically meaningful, and interpretable primary clinical efficacy endpoints for future DMT pivotal trials in early-stage Parkinson's.
