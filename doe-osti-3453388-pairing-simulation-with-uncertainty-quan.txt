ABSTRACT
The inspiration for the report is to attempt to replicate results of an exploding wire test for
validation. I am attempting to determine if the experiment or if the simulations were causing an issue
in the accuracy of the results. The methods that will be applied will be a sensitivity using Correlation
Coefficient and Uncertainty Analysis to determine if there is a detectable reason for why there is a
difference in experimental data and simulations. The replication of the results was successful
however it was not found to be the reasoning behind the differences.

1. INTRODUCTION
Consistently predicting the behaviors and characteristics of gold under dramatic changes in electrical
loading is a key aspect of designing effective systems for aerospace, energy devices, and hardware
development. Without the ability to accurately characterize gold underload it can lead to
uncertainties and compatibility regarding the best applications of the material which is key to have
confident simulation that are certain in their results. In a recent report, [1], comparisons of models
for exploding wires were compared to experiment in a validation exercise for a range of materials,
including gold. In that report, it was observed that experimental and model results were not in
agreement, especially peak voltage, and time of peak, for gold, with observed disagreements of up to
40%. The only uncertainties considered in that study were associated with experimental
conditions, for example, initial voltage for the experiment. These differences indicate that the
actively used model may not fully capture the physical behavior of gold under the experimental
loading conditions. In this work, we investigate whether other sources of uncertainty in the model
can explain this disagreement between experiment and model. We will employ uncertainty
quantification and sensitivity analysis, applied to material model parameters, to propagate and study
the importance of different modeling choices.

2. BACKGROUND
A 2025 report by Carnes et al. [1] was released investigating exploding wire tests submerged in water
for Gold, Copper, Nickel and Aluminum, in which a selected material was placed between two
electrodes in order to complete a circuit. Then upon the preparations being completed a burst of
electricity was sent through the circuit in order to determine how much power is needed to explode
the wire. The experiment was also simulated in ALEGRA-NG to validate the results of the
experiment. The quantities of interest [QoI] in the experiment were the following: Global max
voltage, time of max voltage, local max current, time of max current, current at max voltage, global
max power, and time of max power, and energy density at time of max power. When the
experimental data and the simulations were compared a majority of the graphs and figures were in
agreement on the results. However, it was noted that in figures relating to Gold the simulations and
results were not in agreement with each other.

3. METHODS
Uncertainty Quantification [UQ] is an area within mathematics to identify, distinguish, and reduce
uncertainties in computational modeling and real-world problems. The purpose of this aspect of
math is to explain how parameters, uncertainties, and simplified aspects of a condition are used
together to determine the impact on the output or results. This combined with Sensitivity Analysis
[SA], an approach that utilizes how variations of input parameters, will affect the output to
determine which input parameters will have the most significant impact on the results. These
methods are what allow for the user to assess the credibility of the results and determine which
parameters to focus on. Here, we apply UQ and SA to investigate why the gold simulations and
experimental results from the report were not in agreement with each other hence the primary focus
of this analysis is to determine possible sources behind the significant difference between the results.
This is important to have consistency between simulation and experimental results and providing
direction for future work investigating sources of inconsistency.

Dakota was used to generate the samples and manage the workflow while ALEGRA-NG was used
to run the exploding wire simulations. The analysis was completed by using the nominal values from
the report for each of the 3 initial voltages being 1000 V, 2000 V, and 3000 V. This was then put
into the workflow to output 60 samples of data per voltage case, totaling to 180 total simulations.
The varied parameters were firing resistance, firing inductance, firing capacitance, and initial voltage.
The uncertainty bounds defined the maximum allowed variation of each sampled parameter from its
nominal value. The bounds were ±8% for firing resistance, ±3% for firing capacitance, ±2.5% for
firing inductance, and ±1% for initial voltage. Wire diameter and wire length were held constant
during the Dakota sampling study while the voltage and circuit parameters were varied. Although
they were not varied in the sample set, they had associated acceptable input tolerances of ±0.01%
for wire diameter and ±5% for wire length. Dakota selected input-parameter values within the
allowed tolerance ranges. For each sampled input set, ALEGRA-NG produced the corresponding
simulation outputs which were max power, current at max voltage, energy density at max power,
time at max power, cpu, time at max current, max voltage, max current, time at max voltage. Python
post-processing scripts were then used to organize the results into three types of plots: time-history
plots, input-output correlation coefficient plots, and comparisons of peak voltage from the shot-data
files.

4. RESULTS
The goal of the analysis was to validate the results presented in the SAND report [1], confirm
replicability, and to identify which input parameter most strongly influenced the simulation output.
The validation of the graphs was promising due to replicating the time history graphs used
throughout the SAND report to confirm the replicability of the simulations. The time histories
plotted include both current and voltage as function of time, as shown in Figure 2. The time history
differences for voltage as a function of time were 40% in the Figure 1 and as initial voltage increased
the trend in the time history figures gradually decreased to 23% different when compared to the
simulations in Figure 3. The results of the time history graphs were replicated and were in agreement
based on the overall curve, shape, and time of peak response. Next, a sensitivity analysis was done to
determine which input parameters had the most significant influence on the simulation outputs.
Three graphs were produced from the Dakota workflows that produced Correlation Coefficients
(CCs) for each of the initial voltages’ parameters which are shown in figures 4 a, b, and c. The
parameter ranges used in the Dakota workflow included the nominal experimental values as
reference points.
From the produced CCs from the Dakota workflow the individual values were then averaged
together to then compare with Figure 5-3 Sobol’s Sensitivity from [1]. A Sobol value is a part of
sensitivity analysis that reveals which input contributes most to the output. The CCs averaged values
were compared to the Sobol values in a qualitative manner since it was done to identify the
significant parameters. The comparison showed a significant disagreement for wire length in the
maximum-power response and wire diameter in the time-at-maximum-current response. This
indicates that the two methods did not rank these parameters similarly in terms of overall influence
which may likely be due to the parameters having a more significant influence over the results than
the original report documented. Aside from these differences, the remaining parameters showed
similar relative rankings between the Dakota CC results and the Sobol sensitivity results. The
comparison revealed the most influential parameter for max voltage was wire length and the most
influential parameter for time at max voltage was firing inductance. A notable trend of the 2
influential parameters was that as initial voltage increased the influence only increased. Then the
samples produced with the nominal parameters were then filtered by python code to filter the peak
voltage values of each of the sample plots. It was then all the peak voltage values from the data
outputs were plotted into a histogram of blue being the voltages from the simulation and black
being peak voltage from the shot data. It revealed that even in replication the simulation data and
experimental data are in disagreement regarding peak voltage by 20% difference. Hence needing to
investigate what could be causing the difference in the figure.

 9

5. DISCUSSION
The trend shown in the Max Voltage histogram reveals that as the initial voltage increased, the
percent difference between the simulation and experimental data decreased from 40% to 23%. The
trend suggests that there may be a parameter missing from the model inputs that is limiting the
accuracy of the model. However, this still leaves a significant discrepancy, indicating that the model
is not acceptable for predicting peak voltage effectively. This limitation could prevent the user from
preparing adequately for an expected peak voltage when gold is selected as the material in the
experiment or simulation. Future work will be needed to investigate the missing or inaccurate data
that could be a potential input into the model to improve accuracy.

6. CONCLUSION
The goal of this experiment was to replicate and validate the results of the workflow. The outputs of
the workflows were found to be replica table. This indicated that the process can consistently
reproduce results under the tested parameters. However, the accuracy of the peak voltage
predictions still requires improvement. Future work would focus on determining whether additional
parameter inputs would improve the accuracy in predicting peak voltage.
