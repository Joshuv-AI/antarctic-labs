Dual Randomness From Spatial and Temporal Variations in Stochastic Zinc Gallate Nanoclusters for PUF and TRNG Co‐Driven Image Generation

Seungme Kang, Wangmyung Choi, Jueun Lee, Yeong Kwon Kim, Byung Chul Jang, Wonjun Shin, Hocheon Yoo

Abstract

We propose a novel dual‐randomness framework that integrates physically unclonable functions (PUFs) and true random number generators (TRNGs) within a single nanomaterial platform. In contrast to conventional approaches that treat PUFs and TRNGs as separate entities, our platform integrates both entropy sources within a single stochastic nanomaterial system, enabling parallel and co‐localized operation. The mixed zinc gallate (ZGO) nanoclusters (NCs) present stochastic properties in which size‐dependent variations influence device resistance, capacitance, and noise characteristics. These variations lead to unique device signatures, while time‐dependent noise fluctuations serve as a TRNG source. The proof as a PUF is confirmed by the difference in position through the measurement of the work function, capacitance, and impedance. The device‐derived TRNG bitstreams from 20 devices exceeded the acceptance threshold of 0.01 for all 11 reported NIST criteria. By integrating the stochastic characteristics arising from the spatially dependent and time‐dependent properties from mixed ZGO NCs, the dilemma of a single device is solved. To demonstrate hardware‐derived latent‐vector modulation, experimentally measured current traces from the mixed‐ZGO devices were converted into a 512‐dimensional latent vector and supplied to a pretrained StyleGAN2‐AFHQv2 generator.
A dual‐randomness hardware platform is demonstrated by integrating PUF and TRNG functionalities into mixed‐ZGO nanoclusters. By leveraging spatial and temporal stochasticity, the mixed‐ZGO NCs device enables both unique identification and random signal generation. The extracted entropy is applied to a StyleGAN2‐AFHQv2 framework, generating various and unpredictable animal images.

Introduction

Physical unclonable functions (PUFs) have emerged as a critical technology for hardware security, adopting inherent process variations to generate unique and unclonable identifiers [, , ]. Conventional PUF implementations predominantly rely on complementary metal‐oxide‐semiconductor (CMOS) silicon‐based architectures, such as static random access memory (SRAM) [, ], dynamic random access memory (DRAM) [, ], NAND‐flash memory [, ], or via‐based designs [, ], where random process variations in semiconductor fabrication define the device‐specific response. These methods exploit random process variations occurring during semiconductor fabrication to generate unique challenge‐response pairs (CRPs). While effective, these circuit‐based approaches are constrained by their reliance on predefined semiconductor structures and suffer from circuit complexity and accordingly, high‐cost fabrication process.

To address these limitations, recent advancements in material‐based PUFs have explored employing intrinsic morphological variations in materials to generate randomness at a fundamental level [, , ]. Rather than circuit‐based approaches, these methods utilize stochastic variations arising from material synthesis, as follows: blending multiple materials [, ], structural heterogeneity [, , ], defect‐induced randomness [, , ], electronic disorder at the interface employing co‐evaporation techniques [], integrating particles [, ] or optical materials [, , ], and employing atomic‐scale disorder in two‐dimensional materials [, ], grain boundaries, and charge trap sites [, , ]. These techniques introduce low‐level physical disorder: material‐induced stochasticity that is inherently uncontrollable and unpredictable, thereby enabling PUF implementations that offer higher security and resilience against physical attacks. In addition to its effectiveness, material‐based randomness eliminates the need for complex circuit designs, reducing fabrication costs and expanding PUF applicability beyond conventional silicon‐based methodologies.

Compared to the above‐mentioned PUFs, which generate finite sets of CPR based on structural randomness, true random number generators (TRNGs) exploit temporal variations to produce an endless stream of unpredictable yet uncontrollable outputs [, , ]. TRNGs leverage dynamic fluctuations in physical properties—such as thermal noise [, ], phase jitter [], photonic fluctuation [, ], or electronic shot noise []—that evolve continuously over time, enabling significantly expanded entropy sources compared to static PUFs. This temporal dependency allows TRNGs to surpass the inherent information constraints of PUFs, generating truly stochastic sequences that are not limited by a finite device architecture. By maintaining high entropy across time‐dependent processes, TRNGs generate graphically secure random numbers with extreme unpredictability, enabling boundless cryptographic randomness beyond traditional finite models [, , ].

Integrating PUF‐based spatial randomness and TRNG‐based temporal randomness into a single platform represents a transformative advancement in hardware security and generative AI. By means of these two distinct sources of randomness, our dual‐randomness framework enables diverse latent vector sampling in generative adversarial networks (GANs). The structural randomness of the PUFs ensures the generation of unique and device‐specific image categories, while the temporal randomness introduced by TRNGs provides dynamic, fine‐grained control over image details, such as subtle facial expressions and micro‐movements. The core challenge in realizing such a system lies in the necessity for a co‐designed approach that spans material selection and device engineering. Different from conventional semiconductor‐based randomness sources that depend on predefined circuit‐level variations, our method strategically engineers intrinsic material heterogeneity to induce stochastic yet controllable entropy across (i) spatial (PUFs) and (ii) temporal (TRNGs) domains.

Here, as introduced above, we demonstrate a dual‐randomness security device that combines PUF‐based structure randomness with TRNG‐driven temporal variations by using mixed‐zinc gallate (ZGO) nanoclusters (NCs). This work presents a unified entropy platform that co‐localizes PUF and TRNG functionalities within a single nanomaterial system, offering simultaneous access to spatial and temporal randomness in a compact architecture. Our approach induces morphological variations of mixed‐ZGO NCs, causing a 10 × 20 array to exhibit diverse current variation within a 104‐scale range at −1 V. This impacts the imaginary component of impedance, capacitance, and noise fluctuations, generating unique PUF identifiers. The PUF functionality achieves an inter‐cell Hamming distance (HD) of 46.21%, inter‐device HD of 50.40%, intra‐HD of 0.20%, and an average entropy of 0.83, while the TRNG functionality exhibits a uniformity of 49.89%, an inter‐HD of 51.33%, an average entropy of 0.91, and a bit‐aliasing of 49.45%, with device‐derived bitstreams from 20 independent devices meeting all 11 reported NIST criteria. Electrical analysis confirms spatial PUF randomness and temporal TRNG randomness, while long‐term measurements demonstrate stability over 91 days for the PUF and 237 days for the TRNG. To evaluate latent‐space controllability, we performed two separate demonstrations: a two‐dimensional MNIST proof‐of‐concept using a simple GAN and a 512‐dimensional animal‐image inference using a pretrained StyleGAN2‐AFHQv2 generator. In both demonstrations, the mixed‐ZGO measurements supplied hardware‐derived latent inputs, whereas image synthesis was performed by the trained or pretrained software generator. For the animal‐image demonstration, the pretrained StyleGAN2‐AFHQv2 model was used with a 512‐dimensional latent input. Each animal category—including cats, dogs, foxes, lions, and wolves—from the pretrained StyleGAN2‐AFHQv2 occupies a distinct region within the latent space [, ]. Based on the proposed dual‐randomness security device, where each device provides a unique current output, we can selectively sample latent vectors from precise latent‐space regions associated with desired animal categories, achieving targeted generation previously unattainable with purely random sampling approaches. The integration of temporal stochastic noise introduces subtle intra‐category variations, such as realistic shifts in facial expressions, eye positions, and fur textures. By synergistically combining PUF characteristics with TRNG noise into a unified ZGO NC‐based security platform, our approach effectively addresses the longstanding challenge in GAN‐driven imagery—simultaneously enabling deterministic category selection and fine‐grained visual diversity, significantly advancing the realism, security, and controllability of generative technologies. The integration of two distinct entropy sources within a single platform provides a hardware‐derived latent‐modulation interface for pretrained generative models.

Results and Discussion

Based on the stochastic properties of the mixed‐ZGO NCs, two functionalities can be realized: PUF and TRNG (Figure ). The device‐to‐device variation in the array composed of mixed‐ZGO NCs leads to different current levels across cells depending on their location, which can be utilized to implement PUFs. This property means that different sizes of random numbers can be generated in parallel for each zone of the device. On the other hand, transient current changes show stochastic behavior in which the current continuously fluctuates over time. Defects and material imperfections in the device capture and emit carriers, resulting in stochastic output currents [, ]. This time‐domain stochastic behavior can be utilized for TRNG applications. The measured device‐current traces are converted into hardware‐derived latent inputs and supplied to a pretrained image generator, which performs the image‐synthesis computation. The mixed‐ZGO NCs devices can use multiple input sources (position‐specific multi‐random noise) that can be classified. Using the position‐dependent current traces as hardware‐derived latent inputs, the pretrained generator synthesized multiple animal images. Each type of animal image is transformed into a variety of different appearances. Accordingly, the demonstrated application should be interpreted as hardware‐derived latent‐vector modulation for pretrained image synthesis rather than direct image generation by the mixed‐ZGO device.

The device fabrication process is as follows: The ZGO NCs synthesized with different reaction times were mixed and then spin‐coated onto n‐Si (Figure ). The mixed‐ZGO NCs deposited on the n‐Si are randomly distributed, forming a rough surface that partially exposes the substrate. After deposition of dinaphtho[2,3‐b:2', 3'‐f]thieno[3,2‐b]thiophene (DNTT), the DNTT film covers the n‐Si and ZGO NCs as shown in Figure . Then, the top electrode Ti was deposited (Figure ). The scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of the sequentially formed ZGO NCs, DNTT, and Ti clearly demonstrate the morphology and grain size differences of each material. These differences are also indirectly reflected in the elemental distributions on the film surface. The elemental distribution of each material in the film stacked with all materials was confirmed using energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Figure ). Overall, the ‘Ti’ is densely distributed on the surface. In contrast, the ‘Zn’ and ‘Ga’ elements in the mixed‐ZGO NCs do not completely cover the surface and exhibit low density. Similarly, the ‘S’ elements in the DNTT exhibit a low distribution. These surface distribution characteristics are also observed in the SEM cross‐sectional images. As shown in Figure , mixed‐ZGO NCs did not completely cover some n‐Si surfaces. On the other hand, DNTT covers the entire ZGO NCs without exposing the n‐Si surface (Figure ). The surface of devices fabricated through Ti deposition remains rough in Figure . The surface characteristics of such device films can cause irregular electrical characteristics for the following two reasons. The rough surface causes differences in carrier transport distance in the vertical direction, which increases carrier recombination. Second, as mentioned above, the device undergoes two conditions in which the DNTT covers the mixed‐ZGO NCs, causing depletion width differences and affecting the electrical properties.

As demonstrated in Figure , DNTT was classified into two distinct junctions due to the partial deposition of mixed‐ZGO NCs. When the electron‐rich n‐Si contacts the hole‐rich DNTT, carrier diffusion occurs due to the difference in the majority carrier concentration between the semiconductors, resulting in a p‐n junction with a depletion region as illustrated in band structure 1 in Figure . In band structure 2, with mixed‐ZGO NCs presented between n‐Si and DNTT, a W
F difference at the n‐n junction of the mixed‐ZGO NCs/n‐Si interface leads electrons from mixed‐ZGO NCs to diffuse into n‐Si, forming a Schottky junction (Figure ). Therefore, the ZGO NCs of small or large scale scattered on the n‐Si form different junctions. This causes different carrier transport paths, which in turn results in different conductance differences by device location (Figure ). At the DNTT/mixed‐ZGO NCs interface, a p‐n junction forms through the diffusion of majority carriers from each semiconductor, corresponding to band structure 1. Compared to band structure 1, band structure 2 exhibits an overall reduction in electrical conductivity with a particularly notable improvement in rectification characteristics (Figure ). In band structure 1, a depletion region at the DNTT/n‐Si interface expands under reverse bias, resulting in rectification. However, a small valence band difference of 0.19 eV facilitates leakage current, leading to poor rectifying performance (Figure ). In band structure 2, the wide band gap of mixed‐ZGO NCs (4.88 eV) offers a significant energy barrier of 2.21 eV at the DNTT/mixed‐ZGO NCs interface, effectively blocking holes and greatly enhancing rectification under reverse bias (Figure ). Additionally, the Schottky barrier at the mixed‐ZGO NCs/n‐Si interface contributes to lower conductance in forward bias compared to band structure 1. Based on these characteristics, the device exhibits device‐to‐device variations due to locally dominant junctions that are irregularly formed by the morphological characteristics of the mixed‐ZGO NCs, leading to variations in electrical conductivity.

The reaction time difference revealed that the synthesized ZGO NCs exhibited small and large NCs (Figure ), which were demonstrated by X‐ray diffraction (XRD) (Figure ). Each of the synthesized ZGO NCs show peaks at 30.3° (220), 35.7° (311), 43.4° (400), 53.8° (422), 57.4° (511), 63.0° (440), 71.5° (620), and 74.5° (533) positions (JCPDF:38‐1240), which all correspond to the spinel type of ZGO. The small‐ZGO NCs contain an impurity peak corresponding to zinc hydroxide (Zn(OH)2), but they are negligibly weak compared to the peaks of ZGO. This phenomenon can be explained by the thermal decomposition of urea during the synthesis of ZGO NCs, which follows the reaction []:

The generated hydroxyl (OH−) ions bond with Zn2+ to form Zn(OH)2. Due to insufficient reaction time, a small amount of Zn(OH)2 remains in the small ZGO NCs, but these peaks are negligibly weak compared to those of ZGO. The synthesized ZGO nanoparticles are covered with OH− groups [], which are precipitated from NH4+ ions generated by the thermal decomposition of urea []. With longer reaction times, the concentration of NH4+ ions increases, which attracts more hydroxyl‐covered ZGO nanoparticles and facilitates the formation of larger ZGO NCs (Figure ). To investigate the size of ZGO NCs, we performed additional measurements of the chemical characteristics. As shown in Figure , the measured pH increases from 8.84 to 9.18 under different reaction times, which is consistent with the increased concentration of NH4+ ions described above. To further clarify the influence of reaction time on the surface charge of ZGO NCs, the Zeta potentials of the dispersions of small and large ZGO NCs were measured (Figure ). The small ZGO NCs exhibit a limited amount of positively charged NH4+ ions relative to the surface OH− groups on ZGO nanoparticles, which accounts for their slightly negative electrostatic potential of −6.65 mV. In contrast, the large ZGO NCs are sufficiently compensated by abundant positively charged NH4+ ions for the surface OH− groups, resulting in a positive potential of +27.99 mV. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis was performed to investigate the effect of NH4+ concentration on particle size distribution (Figure ). The average particle sizes (± standard deviation) of the small and large ZGO NCs were confirmed to be 137.5 ± 36.8 and 178.6 ± 36.3 nm, respectively, demonstrating that the NC size can be effectively tuned by the concentration of NH4+ ions during the synthesis process. The size‐dependent conditions of their ZGO NCs induce morphological differences when deposited on n‐Si. The deposition of small‐ZGO NCs forms a relatively uniform thickness formation and exposes a large area of n‐Si surface (Figure ). In contrast, large‐ZGO NCs are more densely aggregated with ZGO NCs, resulting in a relatively smaller exposed area of n‐Si surface compared to small‐ZGO NCs and exhibiting inhomogeneous thickness (Figure ). When each ZGO NCs are mixed at a 1:1 ratio and deposited onto the n‐Si surface, the morphological characteristics of the two ZGO NCs are reflected and result in the irregular exposure of the n‐Si surface (Figure ). These results are also reflected in the roughness of the surface. Figure shows atomic force microscopy (AFM) mapping images of surface roughness under different ZGO NCs conditions. Similar to the SEM cross‐section image, the small‐ZGO NCs exhibit the lowest roughness. Meanwhile, when the large‐ZGO NCs and mixed‐ZGO NCs are deposited, the surface is rougher compared to the small‐ZGO NCs device (root‐mean square average roughness: 13.2 nm (small‐ZGO NCs), 17.5 nm (large‐ZGO NCs), and 16.4 nm (mixed‐ZGO NCs). When comparing the surface roughness mapping images of the large‐ZGO NCs and mixed‐ZGO NCs, it can be observed that the mixed‐ZGO NCs have relatively more regions with lower roughness locally. The morphological differences caused by the formation conditions of ZGO NCs influence the junctions between materials, leading to various electrical characteristics. Devices based on small‐ZGO NCs generally exhibit high current levels above 10−5 A (Figure ), whereas those based on large‐ZGO NCs show a low current level below 10−6 A (Figure ). Interestingly, the devices fabricated with a mixture of different ZGO NCs exhibit an irregular distribution of the two current levels (Figure ). As illustrated in Figure , this occurs because the mixing of small‐ and large‐ZGO NCs results in the random exposure of the n‐Si surface. It is due to the selective formation of DNTT/n‐Si junctions (band structure 1) and DNTT/ZGO NCs/n‐Si junctions (band structure 2). Consequently, areas with large‐ZGO NCs exhibit low currents (band structure 2 dominates), while regions with small‐ZGO NCs show high currents (band structure 1 dominates).

The mixed‐ZGO NC device can provide not only spatial stochasticity but also temporal stochasticity. Figure shows time‐domain current fluctuations measured five times from a single device. These discrete current fluctuations arise from stochastic trapping/de‐trapping processes. For visual illustration of the current‐to‐bit conversion, the measured current was divided into ‘0’ and ‘1’ states using a single threshold of 14.32 μA, producing the 30 × 30 binary map shown in Figure . This single‐threshold binary representation is distinct from the quantile‐based 3‐bit encoding protocol used to generate the bitstreams for the NIST statistical evaluation. The illustrative binary codes exhibit a uniformity of 49.89%, an inter‐HD of 51.33%, an average normalized binary Shannon entropy of 0.91 for the encoded output, and a bit‐aliasing value of 49.45% (Figure ). This output‐based Shannon entropy metric is not a raw‐source min‐entropy estimate. These values are close to their ideal values and support the temporal randomness of the device‐derived binary outputs.

To quantitatively describe the uniqueness identification capability of PUFs, inter‐cell HD and entropy were calculated (Figure ). The extracted security code at −1 V achieves a 46.21% inter‐cell HD and an average entropy of 0.83, indicating that the security code generated from the PUF device is irregular and exhibits ideal uniqueness. To further evaluate the reliability of the PUF responses, repeated‐read measurements were performed using a 10 × 10 PUF device array. The PUF keys were extracted from the current values at −1 V over 10 repeated measurements using the same threshold of −2.59 × 10−8 A (Figure ). The threshold was fixed at the median current value obtained from the first sweep. Most of the repeated measurements reproduced the reference PUF key without bit errors, and only two measurements exhibited a single‐bit mismatch. To quantify the repeated‐read reliability, the intra‐HD was calculated by comparing each PUF key obtained from the repeated measurements. Accordingly, the mean and standard deviation of the intra‐HD were 0.20% and 0.42%, respectively, corresponding to a PUF reliability of 99.8% (Figure ). These low intra‐HD values indicate stable PUF key extraction during repeated‐read operations.

We re‐measured the current after 91 days to verify the long‐term stability of the proposed PUF device. As a result, a slight current decrease was observed after 91 days of air exposure (Figures and ). This decrease was observed uniformly across all devices and showed a consistent trend in the initial state shape. As shown in Figures , the mapped PUF key based on the current obtained at −1 V after 91 days does not mismatch with the initial state, showing a bit error of 0%. The inter‐HD and entropy values ​​of the PUF key extracted after 91 days remain the same as those in the initial state, at 46.21% and 0.83, respectively (Figure ). Through this analysis, we verify the long‐term stability of PUF. In addition, we have performed tests to evaluate the environmental stability of PUF. Figure shows the variation in the PUF characteristics with temperature. We measured a 4 × 4 size device under four different temperature conditions: room temperature (25°C, RT), 40°C, 60°C, and 80°C. The I–V curves exhibited temperature‐dependent behavior, which may be attributed to thermally activated carrier transport in silicon. Accordingly, to account for the temperature‐induced shift in the average current level, the threshold was adjusted for each condition. The detailed threshold values are provided in the Experimental Section. Figure shows the PUF characteristics measured under different humidity conditions. The current showed a slight increase with increasing humidity. This increase may be associated with the adsorption of water molecules on the exposed device surface, which could modify the surface charge states and carrier transport characteristics []. Accordingly, the threshold was adjusted for each humidity condition to account for the humidity‐induced shift in the average current level, with the detailed threshold values provided in the Experimental Section. As a result, the 4 × 4 PUF keys remained unchanged at approximately 60%, 81%, and 90% RH, indicating that no bit errors occurred within the tested humidity range. For the NIST statistical evaluation, transient‐current traces from 20 devices were sampled at 3200 samples s−1 and converted into bitstreams using the quantile‐based 3‐bit encoding protocol described in the Experimental Section and Supporting Information. Briefly, the absolute current fluctuation, d(t) = |I(t) – median(I)|, was divided into eight quantile‐defined intervals using seven trace‐specific quantile boundaries at cumulative probabilities of 1/8, 2/8, ..., and 7/8. The interval labels from 0 to 7 were encoded as 000 to 111 and concatenated in chronological order. With no temporal decimation (D = 1), the bitstream generation rate was 9600 bits s−1, calculated from the sampling rate of 3200 samples s−1 and 3‐bit encoding assigned to each current sample. This rate is determined by the sampling and encoding procedures and represents the encoded‐bit throughput rather than the entropy generation rate of the physical noise source. Accordingly, each 60 000‐bit sequence corresponded to 20 000 current samples and 6.25 s of data. The quantile boundaries were determined before bit encoding and were not adjusted based on the NIST results. No additional filtering or post‐processing was applied. The proposed PUF‐TRNG dual randomness security device addresses the challenge in hardware‐based generative systems of achieving both high entropy and deterministic control in latent vector sampling. Conventional TRNGs provide the stochasticity necessary for high entropy but do not offer control over the mean of the latent vector, making it difficult to target specific regions in the latent space. In contrast, PUFs offer device‐specific deterministic signatures; however, they yield only a finite set of codes, which limits their application for continuous sampling. By integrating spatially dependent deterministic offsets that originate from locally irregular mixed‐ZGO NCs with time‐dependent TRNG noise, the proposed device resolves this dilemma. This dual randomness strategy not only preserves high entropy but also allows controlled modulation of the latent vector mean, thereby enabling selective sampling within the latent space. Recent studies have reported various approaches for integrating PUF and TRNG function within a single hardware platform by exploiting different forms of intrinsic device randomness. Generally, PUFs primarily rely on static device‐to‐device variations arising from fabrication processes or structural randomness, whereas TRNGs utilize dynamic stochastic variations, such as temporal or cycle‐to‐cycle fluctuations within individual devices. Accordingly, various integration strategies have been explored, including SRAM arrays, circuit‐level integration, vertically integrated devices, and memristor arrays. In this study, the mixed‐ZGO NC platform implements both functions using the same simple two‐terminal device architecture. Current variations arising from the spatial distribution of ZGO NCs are utilized for PUF operation, while temporal current fluctuations serve as the entropy source for TRNG operation. This approach enables the extraction of both static and dynamic randomness without requiring additional device integration for the two functions. Furthermore, multiple devices with distinct fluctuation characteristics can be utilized as independent TRNG sources, enabling the demonstration of a multi‐source TRNG application. Table compares recently reported integrated PUF‐TRNG studies based on key security parameters, including uniformity, inter‐HD, entropy, long‐term stability, and NIST results. PUFs and TRNGs require distinct entropy characteristics, which has prompted several studies to employ additional readout circuits capable of simultaneously extracting both metrics [, ]. In comparison, our work realizes a combined PUF‐TRNG within a single platform by exploiting the intrinsic entropy sources of the device, without resorting to complex circuit configurations. Furthermore, the PUF and TRNG were evaluated under various environmental conditions, including long‐term stability, thermal stability, and humidity variation for the PUF, and temperature variation and vacuum conditions for the TRNG, while successfully maintaining randomness (Figure ). We believe that these results highlight the strength and distinctiveness of our work.

Conclusion

In summary, we present a dual‐randomness security device that integrates PUFs and TRNGs using mixed ZGO nanoclusters. The distribution of large and small NCs induces spatial variations in electrical properties, enabling device‐to‐device differences in conductance and thus PUF functionality. Measurements across a 10 × 20 array reveal clear spatial variation, supported by capacitance, impedance, and work function analyses at multiple locations. These spatial features yield an inter‐cell HD of 46.21%, inter‐device HD of 50.40%, intra‐HD of 0.20%, and an average entropy of 0.83. Temporal current fluctuations provide the basis for TRNG operation. The single‐threshold binary mapping used for the illustrative randomness analysis yields a uniformity of 49.89%, an inter‐HD of 51.33%, an average entropy of 0.91, and a bit‐aliasing value of 49.45%. Separately, quantile‐encoded 60 000‐bit sequences from 20 devices exceeded the acceptance threshold of 0.01 for all 11 reported NIST criteria without additional filtering or post‐processing. The reported statistical tests and normalized binary Shannon entropy characterize the encoded output but do not constitute a raw‐source min‐entropy assessment or certification of cryptographic suitability. The mixed‐ZGO measurements provide spatial offsets and temporal fluctuations for stochastic latent‐vector modulation. The MNIST study serves as a separate two‐dimensional proof‐of‐concept, while the animal images were generated by a pretrained StyleGAN2‐AFHQv2 model using 512‐dimensional latent vectors constructed from two measured device traces. Thus, the device performs hardware‐derived latent modulation rather than direct image generation.

Experimental Section/Methods

Materials

Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COOH)2∙2H2O, ≥98%), gallium nitrate hydrate (Ga(NO3)3∙xH2O, 99.9%) and urea (CH4N2O, ≥98%), chloroform (CHCl3, ≥99%), DNTT (C22H12S2, >99%), were purchased by Sigma–Aldrich (USA). Ethanol (C2H6O, anhydrous, 99.8%) was supplied by Alfa Aesar (South Korea). 2‐propanol (C3H8O, ≥99.5%) was obtained from DUKSAN (South Korea). Titanium pellet (Ti, 99.995%) was purchased from iTASCO (South Korea).

Synthesis of Small‐ and Large‐ZGO NCs

Size variation ZGO NCs were synthesized using a one‐pot hydrothermal process. 0.05 mol zinc acetate dihydrate and 0.1 mol gallium nitrate hydrate were dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water. The mixture was stirred until homogeneous and then added to 6 g of urea. The solution was stirred for 10 min and transferred to a glass bottle with a cap. The size of the ZGO NCs depends on the reaction time at 90°C. When the solution is reacted for 24 h, smaller ZGO NCs are formed, whereas a 36 h reaction results in larger ZGO NCs. After cooling to room temperature, the ZGO NCs solution was collected and washed with 2‐propanol via centrifugation.

Preparation of Coating Solutions

Small‐ and large‐ZGO NCs were dispersed in a co‐solvent of chloroform and ethanol in a 3:1 volume ratio with a concentration of 20 mg∙mL−1. Mixed‐ZGO NCs solution was prepared by mixing the small‐ and large‐ZGO NCs solution in a 1:1 volume ratio.

Fabrication Process

We prepared n‐doped Si substrates. n‐Si substrates cleaning was ultrasonicated in ethanol for 10 min and in 2‐propanol for 10 min, and then dried on a hot plate at 100°C. The synthesized small‐ZGO NCs, large‐ZGO NCs, and mixed‐ZGO NCs were spin‐coated. It was spin‐coated for 30 s under the conditions of 3000 rpm and annealed at 120°C for 1 h. After that, DNTT of 20 nm was deposited at a rate of 0.2–0.4 Å∙s−1 through a thermal evaporator. Finally, the top electrode Ti was formed to a thickness of 70 nm through the e‐beam evaporator.

Characterization

The I–V characteristics of ZGO NCs device were obtained using a Keithley 4200 semiconductor parameter analyzer at room temperature. The ambient RH was monitored using a commercially available digital thermo‐hygrometer (CAS, T023, Korea). The top view and cross‐sectional images of the device for comparison by ZGO NCs size were analyzed using SEM (S‐4700, Hitachi). The crystal structures and size of the small and large ZGO NCs synthesized were analyzed using XRD (SmartLab, Rigaku) and DLS (ELS‐8000, Otsuka Electronics, Osaka, Japan). The elemental distribution of the film is observed through EDS mapping. pH of solution synthesized small and large ZGO NCS were meawured with pH meter (ST3100‐F, OHAUS, New Jersey, USA). Zeta potentials of the dispersions of small and large ZGO NCs were characterized by Zeta potential analyzer (ELS‐8000, Otsuka Electronics, Osaka, Japan). The roughness of ZGO NCs of different sizes was confirmed using AFM (Park NX10, Park Systems). ZGO and DNTT were measured for their optical bandgaps using UV–vis spectrophotometry (Lambda 750, PerkinElmer) to determine the absorbance at different wavelengths and using the Tauc plot. The HOMO and LUMO levels of DNTT were obtained by calculating the E
Cut‐off and E
V,Max values gained from UPS (AXIS SUPRA, Kratos, Inc.) analysis.

LFN Analysis

LFN characteristics of two‐terminal ZGO NCs‐based devices were systematically analyzed according to NCs size. Noise measurements were conducted using a low‐noise current preamplifier (SR570) connected to a dynamic signal analyzer (Agilent 35670A) to accurately capture and characterize LFN signals from the samples. Note that bias voltage is also applied using SR570A. Each sample, differentiated by NCs size, was individually measured under identical conditions to determine size‐dependent noise behavior by varying the read biases. For samples containing mixed NCs sizes, LFN measurements were performed at multiple localized positions. This allowed for detailed mapping of defect distributions across different areas of the sample. Such positional noise analysis provided insight into the spatial heterogeneity of defects related to NCs size variation within the ZGO films.

PUF Bit‐Generation Procedure

PUF bit values of ‘0’ and ‘1’ were divided based on the current values extracted at −1 V. The threshold was arbitrarily set to the median current value at which the ratio of ‘0’ and ‘1’ in the bitstream was 50%. In the case of 10 repeated measurement data, the same threshold value (−2.59 × 10−8 A) was applied to all 10 sets of bit values. To account for the temperature‐induced shift in the average current level, the threshold was adjusted for each temperature condition (RT: −7.55 × 10−8 A, 40°C: −2.02 × 10−7 A, 60°C: −2.75 × 10−7 A, and 80°C: −4.99 × 10−7 A). To account for the humidity‐induced shift in the average current level, the threshold was adjusted for each humidity condition (60% RH: −2.26 × 10−7 A, 81% RH: −2.30 × 10−7 A, and 90% RH: −2.35 × 10−7 A).

TRNG Bit‐Generation Procedure

Transient‐current traces were sampled at 3200 samples s−1. For the original trace‐specific encoding, the median current of each trace I(t) was calculated, and the absolute fluctuation amplitude was defined as d(t) = |I(t) – median(I)|. The empirical distribution of d(t) was divided into eight quantile‐defined intervals using seven quantile boundaries at cumulative probabilities of 1/8, 2/8, ..., and 7/8. These quantile boundaries were calculated before bit encoding for each measured trace and were used as deterministic digitization thresholds; they were not adjusted or optimized based on the NIST test results. Each of the eight intervals was assigned an integer label from 0 to 7, and each label was converted into a 3‐bit binary word: 0 = 000, 1 = 001, ..., and 7 = 111. The resulting 3‐bit words were concatenated in chronological order to generate the TRNG bitstream. Thus, each current sample was converted into a 3‐bit word. No temporal decimation was applied (D = 1). Accordingly, the bitstream generation rate was 9600 bit s−1, calculated as 3200 samples s−1 × 3 bits sample−1. This value represents the encoded‐bit throughput determined by the sampling and encoding procedures and should not be interpreted as the entropy rate of the physical noise source. A 60 000‐bit sequence corresponded to 20 000 current samples and 6.25 s of data. No additional post‐processing, including Von Neumann correction, XOR whitening or mixing, digital filtering, or error‐correction coding, was applied.

For the independent chronological calibration‐test analysis, each of the three device 9‐1 transient‐current traces containing 22 222 samples was divided before any statistical calculation. The first 2222 samples, corresponding to approximately 10% of each trace, were used exclusively for calibration. The calibration median, I_cal, med, was calculated from this segment, and the absolute calibration fluctuations, d_cal(t) = |I(t) − I_cal, med|, were used to determine seven empirical quantile boundaries at cumulative probabilities of 1/8, 2/8, ..., and 7/8. The calibration median and all seven quantile boundaries were then frozen and applied without modification to the remaining 20 000 test samples. No median, threshold, or quantile boundary was recalculated using the test segment. Each test sample was assigned to one of the eight intervals using the frozen calibration parameters and encoded as a 3‐bit binary word from 000 to 111. The words were concatenated chronologically to produce a 60 000‐bit test sequence for each environmental condition. This procedure was applied to the device 9‐1 transient‐current traces measured at −1 V under ambient, vacuum, and 60°C conditions. The resulting test‐set eight‐bin occupancies are presented in Figure .

MNIST Latent‐Space Proof‐of‐Concept

The image generation simulation method involves mapping experimentally measured transient currents from dual‐randomness security devices onto the latent space of a trained GAN model for MNIST image synthesis. Device‐specific mean current outputs, ranging between 5 and 30 μA, are normalized to latent coordinates (Z

By carefully selecting device pairs based on their deterministic offsets, it is possible to target specific latent space regions, enabling reproducible generation of desired digit categories while maintaining controlled variability in image features. Detailed procedures and analyses are provided in Supporting Information (Figure ). Hardware‐derived latent inputs were benchmarked against a software i.i.d. standard‐normal baseline (PRNG‐iid) under matched sample counts, truncation (ψ = 0.7), and Linear/Rank/Qmatch mappings. For each condition, comparative FID and KID estimates were calculated from the InceptionV3 feature representations of 96 synthesized images and a matched reference set of 96 images. The reference images were generated using the same pretrained StyleGAN2‐AFHQv2 generator with independent 512‐dimensional i.i.d. standard‐normal latent vectors, z∼N(0,1), rather than being sampled directly from the AFHQv2 real‐image dataset. The generator, truncation parameter, preprocessing, sample size, and image‐synthesis settings were held identical within each comparison. Accordingly, the reported values quantify relative distributional differences from the software‐Gaussian latent baseline and should not be interpreted as conventional dataset‐level FID or KID scores against real AFHQv2 images. Under the evaluated conditions, the hardware‐derived latent inputs showed broadly comparable metric values to the PRNG‐iid baseline in Figure , and the corresponding latent‐space distributions were compared by PCA in Figure . This two‐dimensional MNIST workflow was used only as a conceptual demonstration of latent‐space controllability and was separate from the 512‐dimensional StyleGAN2‐AFHQv2 animal‐image inference.
