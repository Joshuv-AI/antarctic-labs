ABSTRACT
A fracture study of coupon-scale composite cylinders with embedded defects was conducted with an
objective to assess and validate a modeling approach using two available cohesive material models.
The study included experimental and simulation evaluations of initiation of crack growth and
progression. Interrupted thermal experiments used acoustic emissions monitoring to identify the
onset of crack progression during each cooling interval and ultrasonic scanning provided images of
defect growth. Verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification (VVUQ) processes were
performed in the assessment of the simulation predicted temperature at which crack propagation
begins (quantity of interest). The Sobol sensitivity analysis identified the hoop direction elastic
modulus in the carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP) plies as the most influential parameter for
simulations using both cohesive models, accounting for at least 70% of the variation in the
temperature at crack propagation. The UQ temperature range for the Tvergaard-Hutchinson model
was higher (more conservative) than the experimental acoustic measurement indicators of crack
progression, while the temperature range for the Thouless-Parmigiani model enveloped the
experimental data points for the primary defect size of 0.75 x 1 in. The simulations could not
capture the stable crack growth indicated in the experiments. This is likely due to the models’
inability to represent anisotropic fracture toughness attributed to the structure of the orthotropic
fiber weave in a woven composite laminate.

1. INTRODUCTION

Residual stresses develop in composite structures during the manufacturing process primarily due to
differences in the composite materials’ coefficients of thermal expansion as well as polymer cure
shrinkage effects. These residual stresses can induce delamination failure in a composite structure
where the bond between adjacent laminae fails, and the layers separate [1, 2]. In addition, composite
structures may contain defects such as foreign debris, incomplete bonds, or bubbles following their
fabrication. Such defects may interact with residual stresses to precipitate delamination as the
composite structure is subject to increasing temperature changes relative to its stress-free
temperature. Cohesive zone models with cohesive surface elements and corresponding cohesive
traction-separation (T-S) relations are often used to model delamination when the location of a
fracture is known a priori.

The objective of this work is to assess and validate a simulation modeling approach using two
cohesive material models available in the finite element software Sierra/SM [3]. The modeling
approach aims to predict the onset and evolution of crack progression in a composite cylinder
structure with a prescribed defect. The modeling approach uses the concept of a stress-free
temperature for the composite structure to account for the residual stresses developed during the
composite curing process [4, 5]. A large number of input parameters are required to define the
constitutive and cohesive models of the materials. It can be difficult to fully characterize all the
properties given constraints to budgets and facilities; further, composite material properties have
inherent variability attributed to variability of manufacturing by different operators affecting certain
characteristics like void content, fiber volume fraction, ply thickness, material aging, etc. The effects
of material property variability are assessed with sensitivity analysis methods to identify the most
influential parameters on the fracture behavior of the composite structure. Experimental thermal
testing is conducted on composite cylinder structures with a prescribed defect of varying size.
Acoustic emissions (AE) recordings and ultrasonic tomography (UT) scanning techniques are
employed to characterize crack initiation and defect size growth. Modeling predictions are compared
with experimental results in an assessment of the simulation modeling approach.

The report is organized as follows: Section 2 discusses the experimental testing setup and results.
Section 3 outlines the computational model setup and verification, validation, and uncertainty
quantification (VVUQ) methods performed. Section 4 contains the computational modeling results;
these are broken out into a fracture onset study and a crack progression study. Section 5 summarizes
key results and points to future work.

2. EXPERIMENTAL TESTING SETUP AND RESULTS

2.1. Specimen Manufacturing
The specimens were manufactured by the Electroplating Laboratory at SNLCA (08344) and the
Lightweight Structures Laboratory (08445). Thin-walled 6061-T6 aluminum cylinders were
purchased with an outer diameter of 7 in., a length of 6 in., and a wall thickness of 0.125 in. The
cylinders were then phosphoric acid anodized (PAA), and the outside surface was primed using EW-
5005 primer within 24 hours after anodization. Each unit had two GFRP plies and then two CFRP
plies laid up symmetrically on the outside surface of the aluminum cylinder for a total of four plies
[2G/2C]. The symmetry for the two plies of like material was to reduce any additional flexural
stresses due to the mismatch in CTE between the warp and weft faces of each 8-harness satin weave
fabric ply (0/90 weave). Between the two GFRP plies, a 0.0005 in. thick PTFE film defect was
placed of varying size with the 1 in. dimension oriented in the hoop direction and the 0.5, 0.625, or
0.75 in. dimension placed in the axial direction. The film was cut using a Zünd (Zünd Systemtechnik
AG, Altstätten, Switzerland) automated ply cutter and measured prior to insertion. Each piece of
film was 1 in. wide, but the as-cut axial dimensions were 0.5, 0.64, and 0.76 in. This is a small
difference compared to the analysis, which was run on the unit. Two units were produced for each
defect size. The units were vacuum bagged individually using perforated release ply on the outer
CFRP surface and a layer of breather to help distribute the vacuum. The units were then cured in an
autoclave from ASC Process Systems (ASC Process Systems, Valencia, CA) to the manufacturer
specified cure cycle which is a one-step cure that is a dwell at 310°F (155°C) for four hours.

2.2. Ultrasonic Tomography (UT) Scanning
Ultrasonic scanning is a common non-destructive evaluation technique that is used to determine
whether disbonds or delaminations exist within a composite or hybrid structure. Acoustic waves are
transmitted into the surface of the part by piezoelectric transducers, which emit a certain frequency
and collect the reflected waves, via pitch-catch transducers. The results can be visualized using A-
scans, B-scans, or C-scans. A-scans are the waveform of what the transducer is reading, the time of
flight of the reflected signal is correlated to the depth of the defect or the back face of the part. The
B-scan is a cross-section of the part that is parallel to the direction of the signal’s flight where one
dimension of a defect and its depth within the part can be seen. The C-scan uses gates on the A-scan
to narrow a band which is related to a certain depth of the part. This can be thought of as a cross-
section in the plane of the scanned area. The C-scan, when gated properly, can show the size and
shape of the defect in the plane of the scanned area. In this study, the C-scan was used primarily to
visualize changes in defect size and shape.

The scans were performed using a Mistras (Mistras Group, Princeton Junction, NJ) system using a
MHz transducer. The cylinders are submerged in water and rotated on a turntable 360°. Once a full
rotation is complete, the transducer is moved axially, and it is rotated back. These units are scanned
both from the outer composite face and from the inner aluminum face. A typical C-scan of the
defect is shown in Figure 1 below.

 Figure 1. C-scan of 1 in. x 0.5 in. defect scanned from the aluminum side

The size of the defect is then measured using ImageJ [6]. Similar scans are performed after each
temperature ramp to track the growth in the size of the defect. Wrinkles are present in the scans,
which can cause some issues if they are present near the defect. Thankfully, only one of the units
showed a wrinkle near the defect.

2.3. Thermal Testing
A total of 6 samples consisting of carbon-fiber wrap around an aluminum cylinder were thermally
cycled for total of 11 tests. Samples were cooled to temperatures as low as -70°C. After
manufacture, the six units were hand carried to SNLNM to ensure they were not exposed to sub-
ambient temperatures. As some of the predicted defect initiation temperatures were between room
temperature and freezing, this was critical. Shipping or storage in checked baggage with a
temperature recorder could detect whether the units saw cold temperatures, but at that point the test
would be ruined, and the parts would have to be remanufactured. After baseline UT scanning and
attachment of AE sensors, 01527 personnel placed them in a Thermotron EC 193 (Thermotron
Industries, Holland, MI), attached thermocouples to the inner aluminum and outer composite
(Figure 2). The part was then ramped down to the specified dwell temperature at a rate of 1°C/min,
held for one hour, and then ramped back to room temperature at a rate of 1°C/min (Figure 3).

2.4. Acoustic Emissions (AE) Setup
Validating the model’s onset of defect growth prediction was the ultimate goal of this study. It was
impractical to cool the samples with many runs where they are brought to colder and colder
temperatures and back to room temperature for ultrasonic scanning to determine when the defect
grew. In earlier testing in the INF114/251-HT replacement, the infusion resin cracking was
observed and detected by use of strain gages mounted to the outer aluminum ring. A similar
approach was attempted as the analysis showed unstable crack growth at the initiation temperature.
This should have released a large enough stress wave that a signal could be read on the strain gages.
Two strain gages were bonded to the surface approximately 2 in. away from the defect, and data was
collected at a rate of 5 kHz on each channel. While the strains of the unit from thermal contraction
were measured, no stress wave was detected in the strain gage signals. Upon later inspection, the
defect growth was not an unstable event and the energy release upon growth was relatively small.
Accelerometers were also bonded to the surface in hopes of capturing the stress wave, but the noise
level produced by the fan in the environmental chamber produced too much noise. As popping
could be heard during these initial tests, AE monitoring was chosen as a potential method for
capturing the onset of crack growth.

AE monitoring is a common non-destructive testing technique used to quantify the formation of
damage during laboratory testing and large-scale structural monitoring. When damage occurs in a
material, like the formation of cracks, elastic waves are generated that propagate through the
material. Structures are instrumented with a number of piezoelectric transducers that convert these
elastic waves to voltage signals for processing and recording. By employing multiple sensors, the
source of the energy can be located in the structure to map where damage is occurring. For the 6
manufactured samples thermally cycled in SNLNM, each sample was instrumented with 6–8 AE
sensors to monitor defect growth.

In this study, we utilized a Mistras Express 8 based AE monitoring system (Mistras Group,
Princeton Junction, NJ). This is an 8-channel system that monitors, filters, and records in real time.
The system utilizes trigger-based acquisition where the system is continuously monitored; when the
incoming signal crosses a user-defined threshold, the system records and stores the associated

 14

waveform. This is defined as a hit. The system monitors multiple channels, and when multiple hits
happen across channels, the system will attempt to group multiple hits into a location based on
dimensions and velocities of the structure. Associated hits that can be located are defined as one
event. The system has a broad range of frequency recording, from 10 kHz to 1 MHz. The system
used a 2/4/6 preamplifiers in line between the sensors and Mistras system to amplify the incoming
signal, set to 60 dB preamplification for these tests. A combination of sensors was used for testing.
Nano30 sensors and Wsα sensors (Mistras Group) were applied to the exterior carbon fiber and
interior aluminum surfaces. Nano30 sensors were directly affixed to the carbon fiber surface using
cyanoacrylate adhesive. Wsα sensors were affixed to the carbon fiber surface using skived PTFE film
tape and acoustic coupling gel. Wsα sensors were also affixed to the aluminum surface using
cyanoacrylate adhesive. Aluminum waveguides were used to match the radius of curvature of the
aluminum cylinder to the 0.75 in. diameter Wsα sensors. Waveforms were bandpass filtered to 100–
1000 kHz and recorded at 10 MSamples per sec for a total duration of 1000 µsec. The initial
threshold for recording was set to 25 dB but reprocessed with a higher threshold value of 45 dB to
eliminate electrical noise from the environmental chamber. The data was then reprocessed with a
lower threshold of 26 dB to increase the accuracy of first arrival picks to improve event location.
Events were picked using an anisotropic velocity structure of 1.3566*106 in./s in the circumferential
direction and 9.717*105 in./s in the axial direction.

The temperature of the onset of crack growth was extracted from cumulative hit count plots vs.
temperature Figure 4. As the cooling rate was held constant, the slope prior to the dwell temperature
for each test is related to the acoustic emission hit rate. When that slope became noticeable, the
defect is assumed to be under stable crack growth. The hit rate remains small during the initial cool
until it begins to grow, then it dies down during the dwell as the crack has arrested, some additional
signs of defect growth exist during the dwell but disappear as the part is heated back to ambient.
This may suggest that there is additional creep crack growth due to a relatively constant level of
thermal strain energy, the current model cannot capture this phenomenon.

To plot the experimental results in temperature vs. defect area space, the UT scanning data was
combined with the AE data as shown in Figure 5 for each of the six composite cylinders tested. The
plot shows results for all defect size cases; the subsequent plots show the results for each defect size

probable onset of crack growth or delamination. Each UT small circular marker indicates the defect
size measured following a temperature ramp test to the corresponding temperature. The dashed
lines connect the UT scan markers and AE markers, with AE markers always located at the most
recently measured defect area.

3. COMPUTATIONAL MODEL SETUP

3.1. Geometry and Boundary Conditions
The composite cylinder consists of a 6in.-long aluminum cylinder substrate with an inner diameter
of 7 in. and wall thickness of 0.125 in. (3.175 mm). The outer composite contains two inner GFRP
plies with warp fibers oriented in the hoop direction corresponding to the material 1-direction (weft
fibers in axial direction corresponding to the material 2-direction) and two outer CFRP plies with
the same orientation. Each GFRP ply is modeled with a thickness of 0.01284 in. (0.3262 mm), and
each CFRP ply is modeled with a thickness of 0.02015 in. (0.5119 mm). Figure 7 shows the general
model geometry, including defect location and composite cross-section layup. The model is
partitioned in the hoop and axial directions so that a defect may be placed at the mid-length of the
cylinder. The defect dimensions are variable in the axial length direction and fixed to a 1.0 in. (25.4
mm) width in the hoop or circumferential direction. For the initial VVUQ assessment a defect
length of 0.75 in. (19.05 mm) was considered. Additional defect length sizes were subsequently
assessed. Three symmetry boundary conditions in the three x-, y-, z- coordinate directions are used
to reduce the size of the model as shown in in Figure 8. The symmetry condition BC3 implies a
second defect would be located on the opposite side of the cylinder. A simulation without BC3 was
run and confirmed identical results with or without BC3; cohesive behavior and stress
concentrations remain near the defect and do not extend near the BC3 plane. The structure is
subjected to isothermal cooling from an assumed stress-free temperature of 418 K (145 °C) down to
173 K (-100 °C) at a rate of 0.25 K/min as shown in Figure 9.

3.3. Analysis Software

3.3.1.     Sierra/SM
The finite element simulations were completed using Sierra/SM, a 3D Lagrangian code suitable for
implicit, quasi-static analyses [3]. A nonlinear conjugate gradient solver is used to iteratively find
equilibrium solutions at each time step within a user-defined tolerance. For computational savings,
the Aluminum, CFRP plies, and GFRP plies were simulated with 8-noded hexahedral elements with
the default element formulation of volume integration with single-point Gaussian quadrature.
Default settings also include an hourglass stiffness of 0.05 to stop the formation of zero energy
‘hourglass’ modes while not affecting any global modes. Meshes were constructed with Cubit, a
toolkit for creating 2- and 3D geometries and meshes. Cohesive element blocks were inserted to
replace side sets between ply blocks with Exomerge, a tool for pre- and post-processing exodus type
files.

3.3.2. VVUQ Methods
A mesh convergence study is implemented to verify the FEA approach provides converging
solutions for the target output quantity of interest (QOI). This is achieved following Richardson’s
extrapolation method [12], an error estimation technique to approximate a high-order estimate of a
continuum (exact) solution using three lower order discrete solutions.

The sensitivity analysis procedure implemented consists of four steps and uses the Dakota toolkit to
manage the sampling workflow and compute statistics [13]. See reference papers by Nelson and
Hanson for more detail regarding these methods applied to composite structures [5, 4]. In the first
step, incremental Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) with at least three levels of sampling is performed
until convergence of the standard deviation of the output QOI is achieved, using the Richardson’s
extrapolation. This check identifies a sufficient sample size to minimize the effect of a given sample
set on the statistical analysis. Ideally, convergence of the skew is also achieved; however, the required
number of samples for skew convergence is often intractable. LHS evenly distributes samples over
the parameter space to ensure entire parameter space is represented.

In the second step, a Gaussian process (GP) surrogate is built with the previous LHS training data.
The fitness of the surrogate is assessed with 4-fold cross validation, where the sample set is divided
into four groups, and each group is held out for validation once with the other three groups used for
training. The surrogate fitness metric evaluated is a root mean squared error; the error should be
small relative to the mean QOI scale.

In the third step, the GP surrogate is sampled hundreds of thousands of times in seconds to
perform a variance-based decomposition sensitivity analysis, which is also often called a Sobol
sensitivity analysis. The computed sensitivity indices indicate how much a parameter contributes to
the overall variance of the simulation output QOI. The Main index indicates the fraction of variance
in the output QOI attributed to each input variable parameter individually. The Total index indicates
the fraction of variance in the output QOI due to an input variable parameter and its interactions
with other input variables.

In the fourth step, an uncertainty quantification performed for the output QOI. A reduced input
parameter set that includes the most influential parameters as identified with the sensitivity analysis

 25

is sampled to build another GP surrogate. Then, the confidence interval for the output QOI is
computed.

4. COMPUTATIONAL MODEL RESULTS

4.1. Fracture Onset Study
The objective of this section is to assess and compare the TH and TP models in predicting initial
cohesive failure. A VVUQ assessment of the modeling approach to determine the temperature at
which crack propagation begins with each of the two different cohesive zone material models is
completed. For the TH cohesive material model, this crack propagation temperature occurs when
the output variable lambda_max, an indicator of the progression through the T-S relation, reaches 1,
which represents full separation or failure of the cohesive element. For the TP model, a similar
output variable fraction_of_failure with the same [0,1] range is used. The primary focus is on a
cylinder with a 0.75 in. by 1.0 in. (19.05 mm by 25.4 mm) defect where the 0.75 in. length is along
the cylinder length direction and the 1.0 in. width is in the cylinder hoop direction. Following the
VVUQ assessment for the targeted defect size, other defect sizes are explored to understand sizing
trends. Later, the crack progression regime after the initial first cohesive failure will be investigated.

4.1.1. Solution Verification: mesh convergence study and time step resolution
The mesh convergence study on the temperature at crack propagation (output QOI) was conducted
following Richardson’s extrapolation method [12] using three levels of mesh refinement. The coarse
mesh has one element through each ply thickness, the medium mesh has two elements through each
ply thickness, and the fine mesh has 4 elements through each ply thickness. An aspect ratio of 2:2:1
is used such that the in-plane elements lengths are twice the element height. This information and
the resulting number of mesh nodes are listed in Table 6. Figure 11 shows the three levels of mesh
refinement. Simulations were run on the three meshes with the TH and TP cohesive material
models. The simulation results and estimated errors via Richardson extrapolation are listed in Table
6. The estimated exact (continuum) temperature at crack propagation for the TH and TP models are
293.2 K and 280.7 K, respectively. The errors for the coarse mesh are 0.426% for the TH model and
4.926% for the TP model. The associated p-values give an indication of the rate of convergence in
the QOI. The p-value of 10.3 for the TH model indicates much faster convergence as the mesh is
refined compared to the TP model’s p-value of 0.48. A common rule of thumb in selecting a mesh is
to aim for less than 5–10% error. Here, we select the coarse mesh for further sensitivity analysis to
balance computational cost and accuracy. This selection retains four elements across the cohesive
process zone, which was checked visually (Figure 12). Turon et al. found for typical carbon and glass
fiber composites, the length of the cohesive zone is less than 1-2mm and at least three elements
through the process zone are needed to sufficiently represent the tractions in the cohesive zone [14].

In addition to spatial visualization, time step resolution was analyzed at the element level. The
element traction and normalized separation (lambda_max) was tracked over time and plotted as T-S
relations. Figure 13 shows representative element T-S relations for simulations with the TH cohesive
model (top) and the TP cohesive model (bottom). In these plots the circle markers correspond to
each time step and show that the full triangular peaks of the T-S relations prescribed by the TH and
TP models are captured, even with adaptive time stepping active. Several individual element T-S
relations were checked, and all confirmed the same general behavior capturing the triangular T-S
shape. For the transverse T-S relation for the TP model, the plot captures the triangular peak but
does not complete the full transverse T-S relation because the normal T-S relation is completed first
and drives the cohesive fracture. The final time step increment sizes for the TH and TP models
when the first T-S relationships complete are on the order of 1–20 seconds; with the prescribed
cooling rate of 0.25 K/min., the final time step increments correspond to temperature changes of
less than 0.1 K when the T-S relations complete. This gives additional confidence in using the coarse
mesh for the analysis of initial crack propagation via cohesive failure.

4.1.2. Sensitivity Analysis Study
The sensitivity analysis for the simulated temperature at which first cohesive failure occurs was
performed following the procedure outlined in the computational model setup section. In the first
step, incremental LHS was conducted to identify a sufficient sample size for construction of a GP
surrogate. Sampling was conducted over a large parameter space including 22 normally distributed
and 28 uniformly distributed uncertain material parameters from Table 1, Table 2, and Table 3.
Table 7 lists convergence results for the standard deviation of the QOI temperature at first cohesive
failure using Richardson’s extrapolation. For the TH model, the maximum estimated error was
approximately 2.9% for 100 samples. For the TP model, the maximum estimated error was higher at
approximately 7.3%. A common rule of thumb is to aim for less than 5% error, but the choice
depends on the particular details of an analysis, including factors like how the analysis will be used,
risk tolerance, computational resource constraints, available experimental testing, among others.

In the third step, the variance-based decomposition sensitivity analysis was conducted using the GP
surrogate models trained on 400 LHS each. The TH model surrogate and TP model surrogate were
each sampled over 500,000 times to calculate main and total effect indices for all of the input
parameters: 49 input parameters for the model using the TH cohesive material model and 50
parameters for the model using the TP model. Table 9 and Table 10 list the main and total index
values for input parameters that have main and total index values over 1%. For both the TH and TP
models, the same seven input parameters account for at least 1% of the variance in the output QOI
temperature at crack propagation. In both cases, the variance in the 11-direction (hoop) elastic
modulus in the CFRP material accounted for over 70% of the variance in the temperature at crack
propagation. The main and total index values for each input parameter are very similar, which
indicates that there is very little coupling effect between input parameters. The total index value
should be larger than the main index for each parameter since it includes individual and coupling
terms. However, for a couple parameters the total index value is slightly smaller than the main index
value; this is likely due to numerical rounding within the sampling and index calculation process.
Qualitatively, the relative influence of the top parameters in unaffected.

Figure 14 shows the predicted temperatures for the initial crack propagation, indicated by first CZE
failure, for the TH and TP models for different defect length sizes and two mesh sizes. The TH

that neither model provides valid predictions for small scale defects. Together these results indicate
that the simulations are missing some important physical mechanisms present in the experimental
results. One possibility is the effect of modeling the defect with sharp edges where the cohesive
elements have zero thickness. Geometric variability was not investigated but could be an important
factor. Other sources of error could be incorrect uncertainty bounds on composite material
properties, which could happen with different curing processes, fiber volume fraction, porosity, etc.
between the tested material and that in the composite cylinders. Model form error is another
possible error source; the TH model has a combined normalized separation and fixed mode ratio
while the TP model keeps normal and tangential modes independent; perhaps a different model
form with variable coupling would better capture the experimental results.

The objective of this section is to explore the post-onset-of-fracture regime to determine differences

4.2.1. Defect Area Growth Calculation for Simulations
To plot simulated prediction comparisons to the experimental data, a method for calculating
simulation defect area is needed. Figure 16 shows a comparison of options for calculating simulation
defect area as a function of temperature. The surface integral method ‘surfint’ performs integration
of lambda_max values extrapolated to nodes over each cohesive element surface. The other
approach to calculate the defect area is to take the summation of all lambda_max values and
multiply it by the initial area of a cohesive element, this approach is denoted with ‘Lmax’ in the plot
legend. The ‘Lmax’ method gives a larger defect area compared to that of the ‘surfint’ method as the
temperature is reduced. The two additional ‘low_thresh’ curves use a lower threshold on the
lambda_max metric to reduce the contribution of partially ‘failed’ elements or elements that have
not mostly completed progression through the T-S relation. The threshold admits elements with
lambda_max values between 0.98 and 1. When this lower threshold is applied, the surface integral
‘surfint’ method and the ‘Lmax’ method coincide; the curves take on a shape with a very sharp
upturn increase in defect area at approximately 5 °C. The threshold approach ensures only
approximately fully separated cohesive elements are accounted for in the simulated defect area
representation. The previous non-threshold curves converge to the same steep threshold curve. This
shows how the ‘partial’ area contribution has less effect as the defect grows. The ‘partial’ nature of
the approach also obfuscates the true first failure of a cohesive element in the model, the key
quantity of interest in the previous section. The temperature at first cohesive element failure

 35

coincides more closely with the thresholded approach curves. For these reasons, the plots in the
remaining sections, except Section 4.2.5, use the low threshold surface integral method.

step of approximately 1e-5 sec for the T-S relation to complete for element 10x10.

4.2.5. Effect of Initial Slope of Traction-Separation Relations on Defect Growth
 Prediction
An additional consideration investigated for the usability assessment of the two cohesive models
with orthotropic composite materials pertains to the prescriptive form of the T-S relationship. The
initial slope of the cohesive T-S relationship ties the two adjacent materials, in this case the two
GFRP plies, together before separation occurs. By matching the initial slope (stiffness) of the T-S
relationship for the cohesive elements to the characteristic crack-tip element stiffness of the adjacent
solid element material, one may reduce noise and provide smoother crack progression calculations
[17, 18]. For the boundary value problem considered here, a lambda value of 0.0129 provides these
matching conditions, following from the expression 𝜆' = 𝜎/Δ⁄𝐸( 𝑑"! where 𝜎/ is the peak traction
and 𝑑"! the normal length scale for the cohesive model, and Δ is the element length and 𝐸( the
applicable elastic moduls of the adjacent solid GFRP material. Table 13 lists the predicted
temperature at first cohesive failure for simulation with three different lambda values. The lambda
value of 0.0025 was the baseline mean value for the initial sensitivity study for the onset of crack
propagation; this value gives the steepest initial slope for the T-S relation in Table 13. Increasing the
lambda value reduces the initial slope of the T-S relation. The simulation with a lambda value of 0.01
provides the closest case to the matching slope case (lambda = 0.0129); this results in a very modest
increase in the temperature at first cohesive failure of 0.3 K. The simulation with a lambda value of
0.025 represents a case with a softer slope; in this case, increasing the baseline lambda value by 10x,
increases the temperature at first cohesive failure by 1.2 K. Recall that in Section 4.1 for the
sensitivity study of parameters on the onset of crack propagation, the lambda value was sampled

 47

from uniform uncertain distribution with ±20% bounds on a mean value of 0.0025; lambda was not
found to be a significantly influential parameter for the temperature at first cohesive failure. In the
sensitivity study, the initial slope of the T-S relation was also varied via the normal and tangent
length scales, where larger length scales give softer initial slopes. These length scale parameters were
found to be significantly influential and to account for approx. 2-10% of the variance in the
temperature at first cohesive failure, behind the dominant influential parameter of the hoop
direction elastic modulus in the outer CFRP layers. Figure 24 shows the temperature vs. defect area
plots for simulations with the different lambda values. Increasing lambda provides a smoother
transition to the asymptotic increase in defect area. All three curves meet in the asymptote at
approximately 278 K or 5 °C. While increasing the lambda value provides a smoother accumulation
of defect growth, the simulations remain unstable and unable to capture the stable growth and arrest
indicated in the experiments.

5. CONCLUSION
A fracture study of coupon-scale composite cylinders with embedded defects was conducted with an
objective to assess and validate a modeling approach using two available cohesive material models.
The study included experimental and simulation evaluations of initiation of crack growth and crack
progression during applied cooling of the composite cylinders.

Key takeaways from the crack onset study include:

 • A comprehensive VVUQ analysis for the modeling approach with the TH and TP cohesive
 models for a single initial defect length size of 0.75 in. was performed and gave uncertain
 ranges for the temperature at initial crack propagation. The crack initiation temperature
 range for the TH model was higher (more conservative) than the experimental acoustic
 measurement indicators of crack progression. The crack initiation temperature range for the
 TP model was lower than that of the TH model and just enveloped the experimental data
 points.
 • The E11 parameter of the CFRP material, the elastic modulus in the material 1-direction
 aligned with the hoop direction of the cylinder, was identiﬁed as the most inﬂuential model
 parameter aﬀecting the temperature at initial crack propagation and accounted for over 70%
 of the variance in the crack propagation temperature. It is important to accurately represent
 this material property to build conﬁdence in using model predictions.
 • Mesh size convergence and time step resolution studies were evaluated to ensure that
 element size and time step size were suﬀiciently small to capture the cohesive T-S
 relationship spatially and temporally. It is recommended to perform this type of solution
 veriﬁcation for any boundary value problem simulation.
 • For the portion of the study that evaluated additional defect length sizes from 0.5 in. to 1.0
 in., the TH model consistently predicted temperatures for crack propagation that were
 higher than the predictions from the TP model for all defect sizes. This is likely due to the
 way the TH model combines normal and tangential modes while TP keeps the modes
 independent
 • Both experiments and simulations captured the general trend of crack propagation
 commencing at lower temperature for smaller initial defect sizes. The TH model predictions
 were conservative for the 0.75 in. and 0.625 in. defects, while the TP model predictions were
 non-conservative. For the smallest defect size of 0.5 in., both models give non-conservative
 predictions, i.e. lower temperatures for initial crack propagation compared to experimental
 data indicators.
 • Overall, the TH model can be used for conservative crack onset predictions in similar
 simulation for defects larger than 0.5 in. in length. Neither model should be used for
 predictive simulation of defects with lengths smaller than 0.5 in. without additional testing
 and experimental validation.

Key takeaways from the crack progression study include:

 • While experimental cooling results indicated arrestable crack growth, simulations in the crack
 progression regime showed rapid unstable crack growth leading to structural failure in all
 defect size cases for both TH and TP models

 49

 • A mesh size convergence and time step resolution study indicated that the coarse mesh
 deemed suﬀicient for the crack initiation metric was not suﬀicient for the crack progression
 regime. The ﬁner ‘medium’ mesh with two elements through each ply thickness was selected
 for further comparison with experimental results as it balanced computational cost and
 better captured of the prescribed T-S curves; however, it could not fully capture the
 prescribed T-S curves, speciﬁcally the ﬁnal softening regime even with very small time steps.
 • The simulations’ inability to capture the stable crack growth indicated in the experiments
 may be due to the models’ purely isotropic fracture toughness representation. Triki et al. and
 others found that resistance to crack growth varied with ply angle for woven laminates [15,
 16]. In this study, as the crack front expands into a rounded shape, crack resistance in the
 defect corner region would correspond to higher resistance (45 degree) where it should take
 more energy to propagate the crack.
 • Overall, simulations should not be used for predictive decisions in the propagation or
 growth regime of cracks in similar composite structures. Instead, experimental testing should
 be relied upon to characterize crack growth.

In conclusion, the comparison of experimental and simulation results of this work suggests that the
TH model is appropriate for conservative analyses of crack onset for moderately large flaws with
lengths greater than 0.5in. and areas greater than 0.5 in.2 for similar hybrid GFRP-CFRP structures
under similar conditions and temperature ranges. The TP model was found to give generally non-
conservative predictions for the temperature at first fracture metric and is not recommended. For
smaller flaws, the available TH and TP models do not produce validated predictions, and
experimental testing should be employed for decision making activities. In the post-onset-of-fracture
or crack growth regime, neither the TH nor the TP model has been shown to capture experimentally
observed stable crack growth; thus, the models are not validated and should not be used for analyses
in this crack growth regime for similar hybrid composite structures.

In future work, a damage model could be used to incorporate any induced softening due to
intralaminar damage while the interlaminar delamination remains modeled with cohesive surface
models. This might influence the stability of the crack growth. With the Sierra/SM LAMÉ
elastic_orthotropic_frp_modular_damage model, the interlaminar delamination can be represented
with the material model without the use of cohesive elements and cohesive T-S models [19]. This
would provide a useful comparison to the modeling effort in this report. Future work could also
investigate the development of an anisotropic cohesive model with fracture properties GIC and GIIC
as functions of material orientation following the work in Triki et al. that showed interlaminar
fracture toughness varying with fiber orientation [15]. Mesoscale fracture studies including the weave
structure of the composite plies could elucidate small scale fracture mechanisms that may produce
aggregate anisotropic fracture behavior at the laminate scale.
