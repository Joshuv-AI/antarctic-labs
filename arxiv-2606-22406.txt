Asymptotic Signal Subspace Recovery in Softmax Attention Models
Lan V. Truong

Abstract
Attention mechanisms have demonstrated remarkable empirical success in identifying relevant information from large collections of tokens, yet the theoretical principles underlying this behavior remain poorly understood. We study a stylized softmax-attention model in which a query vector is learned by stochastic gradient ascent from a collection of informative and nuisance tokens. Exploiting the symmetry of the model, we derive a population objective and characterize the limiting ordinary differential equation governing the learning dynamics. Using tools from stochastic approximation and dynamical systems theory, we establish a rigorous connection between the stochastic learning algorithm and its deterministic limit. Our main result shows that, under suitable high-dimensional scaling assumptions and standard step-size conditions, the learned query converges almost surely to the one-dimensional signal subspace spanned by the latent informative direction. Equivalently, the query asymptotically recovers the latent signal up to the intrinsic sign ambiguity. These results provide a rigorous theoretical foundation for understanding attention mechanisms as signal extraction procedures in high-dimensional noisy environments and offer a dynamical-systems perspective on how attention discovers relevant information in the presence of substantial noise.

Introduction

The Transformer architecture has emerged as the dominant paradigm in modern machine learning, serving as the foundation of large language models, vision transformers, and numerous multimodal systems. At the heart of the Transformer lies the attention mechanism, which dynamically assigns weights to input tokens and aggregates information according to their relevance. Despite its remarkable empirical success, the theoretical principles underlying the behavior of attention remain only partially understood.

Existing theoretical studies of attention have primarily focused on its expressive power, optimization landscape, approximation properties, or statistical generalization behavior. In most of these analyses, the query, key, and value representations are assumed to be fixed or already learned. Consequently, relatively little is known about the fundamental mechanism by which attention identifies relevant information in a collection of noisy tokens. In particular, a basic scientific question remains largely unanswered:

Can attention itself discover a hidden signal embedded among many noisy tokens, and if so, what mathematical mechanism drives this discovery process?

This paper investigates these questions through a sparse-signal detection framework. We consider a collection of tokens

\displaystyle x_{i}=v_{i}\theta_{d}\xi_{d}+z_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,L_{d},

(1)

where

•

\xi_{d}\in S^{d-1}
is an unknown signal direction;

•

\theta_{d}>0
denotes the signal strength;

•

v_{i}\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether the
i
-th token is informative;

•

z_{i}\sim N(0,I_{d})
is an isotropic Gaussian noise vector.

Under this model, only a sparse subset of tokens contains useful information aligned with the latent signal direction, while the remaining tokens are pure noise.

To understand how attention identifies informative tokens, we study the attention map

\displaystyle f_{q}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{L_{d}}a_{i}(q,X)x_{i},

(2)

where

\displaystyle a_{i}(q,X)=\frac{\exp\!\bigl(\beta\langle q,x_{i}\rangle\bigr)}{\sum_{j=1}^{L_{d}}\exp\!\bigl(\beta\langle q,x_{j}\rangle\bigr)}.

(3)

Here
q\in S^{d-1}
denotes the query vector and
\beta>0
is the inverse-temperature parameter.

The key quantity governing the behavior of attention is the alignment

\displaystyle\rho=\langle q,\xi_{d}\rangle.

(4)

When
\rho
is positive, informative tokens tend to produce larger logits than non-informative tokens. Consequently, they receive larger attention weights and contribute more strongly to the attention output. This creates a positive-feedback mechanism: improved alignment increases the attention placed on informative tokens, which in turn further reinforces alignment with the latent signal direction.

Our analysis formalizes this intuition and establishes a connection between self-attention and sparse-signal detection. Exploiting the rotational symmetry of the model, we show that the optimization landscape can be reduced to a one-dimensional problem governed by the alignment parameter
\rho
. This reduction enables a detailed study of the geometry of the objective function and the resulting alignment dynamics.

Our main result shows that this positive-feedback mechanism is sufficiently strong to recover the latent signal direction. Under suitable high-dimensional scaling assumptions and standard stochastic approximation conditions, the learned query vector converges almost surely to the signal subspace generated by the latent direction
\xi_{d}
. Consequently, attention-based optimization asymptotically separates informative tokens from nuisance tokens and recovers the underlying signal up to the unavoidable sign ambiguity.

More broadly, our work suggests a new interpretation of attention. Rather than acting solely as an information aggregation operator, attention can also be viewed as a statistical procedure for discovering latent structure hidden within high-dimensional data. This perspective establishes a bridge between Transformer architectures, high-dimensional probability, sparse-signal detection, and stochastic approximation theory.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.

1.

A tractable model for attention-based signal recovery.

We introduce a stylized softmax-attention model consisting of informative and nuisance tokens and study the learning dynamics of a query vector under stochastic gradient ascent. The model captures the essential signal-selection mechanism of attention while remaining amenable to rigorous mathematical analysis.

2.

Characterization of the limiting attention dynamics.

We derive the population objective, the associated population vector field, and the corresponding limiting ordinary differential equation governing the asymptotic mean-field evolution of the query vector.

3.

A rigorous stochastic approximation analysis.

Using the ODE method and asymptotic pseudo-trajectory theory, we establish that the stochastic query-learning recursion tracks the solutions of the limiting dynamical system, thereby connecting the stochastic optimization algorithm with its deterministic counterpart.

4.

Global convergence and signal subspace recovery.

Our main theorem establishes that, under suitable scaling assumptions and standard stochastic approximation conditions,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\inf_{q^{*}\in\{\pm\xi_{d}\}}\|q_{k}-q^{*}\|=0,\qquad\mathrm{a.s.}

(5)

Consequently, the attention-learning dynamics recover the latent informative direction up to the intrinsic sign ambiguity and provide a rigorous explanation for attention-based relevance discovery in high-dimensional noisy environments.

3
System Architecture and Data Modeling

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
denote the dimension of the embedding space. Let
\xi_{d}
be a fixed, hidden target directional unit vector residing on the unit hypersphere
S^{d-1}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|v\|=1\}
.

For a given context instance, the input data sequence is collected in a token data matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
comprising
L
discrete token rows
[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{L}]^{\top}
. The individual tokens are structurally generated via a hidden mixture distribution:

\displaystyle x_{i}=v_{i}\theta_{d}\xi_{d}+z_{i},\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,\dots,L\}

(6)

where the constituent variables are defined as follows:

•

v_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is a deterministic partition assignment indicator. The context window contains exactly
R
informative tokens and
L-R
pure noise tokens. We define the informative index subset as
\mathcal{I}=\{i:v_{i}=1\}
with
|\mathcal{I}|=R
, and the background noise index subset as
\mathcal{N}_{o}=\{j:v_{j}=0\}
with
|\mathcal{N}_{o}|=L-R
.

•

\theta_{d}>0
represents the signal scaling magnitude.

•

z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random background noise vectors obeying a standard multivariate isotropic Gaussian distribution:

z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

(7)

3.1
Attention Weights and Field Expressions

Let
q\in S^{d-1}
represent the tracking query vector. The matching attention coefficient mapping
a_{i}(q,X)
is modeled using the standard softmax function:

\displaystyle a_{i}(q,X)=\frac{e^{\beta\langle x_{i},q\rangle}}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}e^{\beta\langle x_{j},q\rangle}},

(8)

where
\beta>0
acts as the inverse softmax temperature parameter. By construction, the coefficients are strictly bounded and represent a valid probability distribution across the tokens:

\displaystyle 0<a_{i}(q,X)<1,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{L_{d}}a_{i}(q,X)=1.

(9)

The output representation vector generated by the pooling layer, denoted
f_{q}(X)
, is the convex combination of the input token rows:

\displaystyle f_{q}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{L_{d}}a_{i}(q,X)x_{i}.

(10)

3.2
Unsupervised Objective and Algorithmic Tracking Dynamics

Because
\xi_{d}
is hidden from the algorithm, the system cannot directly evaluate or optimize the inner projection field
\langle f_{q}(X),\xi_{d}\rangle
. Instead, we optimize a self-consistent unsupervised energy objective. We maximize the expected squared Euclidean norm of the pooled output:

\displaystyle J_{d}(q)=\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{q}(X)\|^{2}\right].

(11)

The true population objective function gradient is denoted by
\nabla_{q}J_{d}(q)
. At each discrete iteration step
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the algorithm draws a fresh, independent input matrix sequence sample
X_{k+1}
and evaluates the exact empirical stochastic gradient vector
g_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle g_{k}=\nabla_{q}\|f_{q}(X_{k+1})\|^{2}\Big|_{q=q_{k}}.

(12)

The updating tracking query trajectory sequence is generated via a projected spherical stochastic gradient descent loop:

\displaystyle q_{k+1}=\text{Proj}_{S^{d-1}}(q_{k}+\eta_{k}g_{k})=\frac{q_{k}+\eta_{k}g_{k}}{\|q_{k}+\eta_{k}g_{k}\|}.

(13)

The schedule step sizes
\eta_{k}
are configured to satisfy specific conditions linked to the dimensions to balance tracking speed against variance bounds:

\displaystyle\eta_{k}=\frac{\gamma_{k}}{d^{2}},\quad\text{where }\gamma_{k}>0,\quad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}=\infty,\quad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}^{2}<\infty.

(14)

4
Theoretical Analysis

To analyze the asymptotic tracking capability of the projected stochastic gradient ascent updates, we present our auxiliary framework divided into three key structural steps: analyzing objective landscape geometry, bounding empirical gradient moments, and establishing continuous tracking approximations.

4.1
Objective Function Geometry & Landscape

We first map the geometric landscape and invariant contours of the unsupervised energy objective
J_{d}(q)
over the sphere
S^{d-1}
. The first step ensures our population objective is smooth and behaves predictably across the manifold.

Lemma 1

(Smoothness of the Population Objective)
.

The unsupervised population function satisfies
J_{d}(q)\in C^{\infty}(S^{d-1})
.

Due to the isotropic profile of the random noise vectors, the high-dimensional objective collapses into a compact system determined solely by the latent axis projection.

Lemma 2

(Rotational Invariance and Dimension Reduction)
.

There exists a smooth function
\Psi_{d}:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
J_{d}(q)=\Psi_{d}(\langle q,\xi_{d}\rangle)
.

To measure the concentration of individual token weights under this representation, we provide a uniform matrix norm envelope bound on the softmax allocations.

Lemma 3

(Concentration of Attention Weights)
.

The squared Euclidean norm of the attention weights satisfies

\|a(q)\|^{2}=O\left(\frac{Le^{\beta^{2}}}{R(L-R)}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(15)

We complete the landscape characterization by establishing that the reduced representation is strictly monotonic along the alignment vector field, preventing spurious local traps.

Lemma 4

(Monotonicity of the Reduced Objective)
.

As
R\to\infty,L-R\to\infty,\beta=O(1),\theta_{d}=O(1)
, the objective satisfies
\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(\rho)>0
for all intermediate alignment values
\rho\in[-1,1]
.

4.2
Algorithmic Gradients & Tracking Dynamics

With the underlying landscape characteristics defined, we next isolate individual stochastic updates and use multi-index Taylor expansions to transition the discrete steps into continuous paths.

Lemma 5

(Uniform Gradient Variance Bound)
.

Under the condition that the inverse temperature and signal strength parameters satisfy
\beta=O(1),\theta_{d}=O(1)
, the unsupervised stochastic gradient satisfies a dimension-dependent second moment condition:

\mathbb{E}\left[\|g_{k}\|^{2}\right]\leq Cd^{4}.

(16)

By projecting the empirical gradient variance onto the tangent plane of the hypersphere, we decompose the discrete recurrence update into a true drift path and an optimization error envelope.

Lemma 6

(Discrete Taylor Trajectory Projections)
.

The projected SGD updating equations expand as:

q_{k+1}=q_{k}+\eta_{k}(I_{d}-q_{k}q_{k}^{\top})g_{k}+r_{k},

(17)

where the cumulative remainder vector satisfies the uniform bound
\|r_{k}\|\leq C\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}
.

4.3
Stochastic Convergence & Martingale Bounds

The final phase of our auxiliary analysis provides the requisite martingale limits and Lipschitz tracking metrics required to execute the continuous ODE method.

Lemma 7

(Global Drift Field Lipschitz Continuity)
.

The continuous drift vector field
H_{d}(q)=(I_{d}-qq^{\top})\nabla_{q}J_{d}(q)
is globally Lipschitz continuous on the hypersphere
S^{d-1}
.

To guarantee that empirical noise fluctuations balance out uniformly as iterations accumulate, we establish the convergence properties of the noise filtration array.

Lemma 8

(Martingale Array Convergence Bounds)
.

The stochastically accumulating martingale sum series
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}M_{k+1}
converges almost surely.

Finally, we show that the accumulated mathematical approximations introduced during the Taylor projections vanish over time.

Lemma 9

(Remainder Convergent Summability)
.

The cumulative Taylor remainder sequence is absolutely summable almost surely:

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|r_{k}\|<\infty.

(18)

4.4
Time-Scale Mapping and Continuous Interpolation

To study the discrete-time sequence
\{q_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
using dynamical systems tools, we map the discrete iteration counter
k
onto a continuous timeline. We define the cumulative time intervals
\tau_{0}=0
and
\tau_{k}=\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\eta_{l}
for
k\geq 1
, where
\eta_{l}
is the step size.

Let
m(t)=\max\{k\geq 0:\tau_{k}\leq t\}
denote the discrete step counter corresponding to the continuous time parameter
t
. We then construct the continuous-time piecewise constant interpolation trajectory
\bar{q}(t)
defined by:

\bar{q}(t)=q_{m(t)},\quad t\in[0,\infty).

(19)

Throughout the subsequent analysis, discrete-time iteration updates are denoted by a subscript index (
q_{k}
), whereas continuous-time trajectory coordinates and solutions to the limit differential equation
\dot{q}=H_{d}(q)
are designated as functions of time (
q(t)
).

4.5
Asymptotic Trajectory Tracking Proofs

Proposition 1

(Asymptotic Pseudo-Trajectory Approximation)
.

Let
q(t)
be the continuous interpolation step trajectory. Under Lemmas
1
through
9
, the discrete updates tracking sequence maps uniformly onto the continuous ODE system flow framework.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Recall the one-step recursion established in Lemma
6
:

\displaystyle q_{k+1}=q_{k}+\eta_{k}H_{d}(q_{k})+\eta_{k}M_{k+1}+r_{k}.

(20)

To demonstrate convergence under the continuous framework, we partition our verification across the following structural stages:

Step 1: Integral representation.

Fix
t\geq 0
and
s\geq 0
. Summing the discrete recursion matching indices from
k=m(t)
to
m(t+s)-1
in (
20
) yields:

\displaystyle\bar{q}(t+s)-\bar{q}(t)

\displaystyle=q_{m(t+s)}-q_{m(t)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=m(t)}^{m(t+s)-1}\eta_{k}H_{d}(q_{k})+\sum_{k=m(t)}^{m(t+s)-1}\eta_{k}M_{k+1}+\sum_{k=m(t)}^{m(t+s)-1}r_{k}.

(21)

Step 2: Control of the interpolation error.

Let
k=m(u)
for a given continuous time coordinate index. Evaluating the step offset bounds
\tau_{k}\leq u<\tau_{k+1}
, the piecewise trajectory error satisfies:

\displaystyle\|\bar{q}(u)-q_{k}\|=\|q_{k}-q_{k}\|=0.

(22)

Consequently, we can write the main continuous tracking drift component directly as an integrated representation over the piecewise field:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=m(t)}^{m(t+s)-1}\eta_{k}H_{d}(q_{k})=\int_{t}^{t+s}H_{d}(\bar{q}(u))\,du.

(23)

Step 3: Evaluation of the continuous drift discrepancy.

To handle the difference between the actual trajectory path
q(t+u)
and its step-wise representation
\bar{q}(t+u)
, we subtract the continuous differential equation component:

\displaystyle q(t+s)-q(t)=\int_{t}^{t+s}H_{d}(q(u))\,du.

(24)

Taking the difference between the discrete interpolation series in (
21
) and the continuous flow in (
24
) yields:

\displaystyle\bar{q}(t+s)-q(t+s)=\bar{q}(t)-q(t)+\int_{t}^{t+s}\left[H_{d}(\bar{q}(u))-H_{d}(q(u))\right]\,du+E(t,s)

(25)

where the combined error envelope tracking array maps directly to:

\displaystyle E(t,s)=\sum_{k=m(t)}^{m(t+s)-1}\eta_{k}M_{k+1}+\sum_{k=m(t)}^{m(t+s)-1}r_{k}.

(26)

Step 4: Application of Martingale and Remainder limits.

By Lemma
8
, the martingale tracking array sequence
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}M_{k+1}
converges almost surely. By Cauchy uniformity
[
25
]
, this implies:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\left\|\sum_{k=m(t)}^{m(t+s)-1}\eta_{k}M_{k+1}\right\|=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(27)

Similarly, applying the absolute summability property from Lemma
9
, the trailing remainder norm satisfies:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\left\|\sum_{k=m(t)}^{m(t+s)-1}r_{k}\right\|\leq\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{k=m(t)}^{\infty}\|r_{k}\|=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(28)

Combining these limits under the supremum metric over a compact tracking window gives
\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\|E(t,s)\|=0
almost surely.

Step 5: Invoking the Lipschitz bound via Grönwall’s Inequality.

Let
e(t)=\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\|\bar{q}(t+s)-q(t+s)\|
. Using the global Lipschitz constant
L
proven in Lemma
7
:

\displaystyle\|\bar{q}(t+s)-q(t+s)\|\leq\|\bar{q}(t)-q(t)\|+L\int_{t}^{t+s}\|\bar{q}(u)-q(u)\|\,du+\|E(t,s)\|.

(29)

Applying Grönwall’s inequality
[
13
]
yields:

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\|\bar{q}(t+s)-q(t+s)\|\leq\left(\|\bar{q}(t)-q(t)\|+\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\|E(t,s)\|\right)e^{LT}.

(30)

Step 6: Final asymptotic trajectory matching proof.

Taking the limit inferior and superior over time, the initialization offset
\|\bar{q}(t)-q(t)\|
goes to 0 by definition of the matched start coordinates. Because
\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\|E(t,s)\|=0
, we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\|\bar{q}(t+s)-q(t+s)\|=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(31)

This completes the verification that the discrete-time optimization processes match the continuous-time ordinary differential equation limits almost surely.
∎

5
Main Convergence Results

With the static geometry of the landscape and the asymptotic tracking capabilities established in Section 5, we are now positioned to state our main convergence guarantees. We begin by formalizing the definition of the limit set of the continuous-time dynamical system, which acts as the target for our discrete stochastic trajectory.

Definition 1

(Asymptotic Equilibrium Set)
.

The equilibrium target set
\Lambda\subset S^{d-1}
for the continuous projected field flow
H_{d}(q)=(I_{d}-qq^{\top})\nabla_{q}J_{d}(q)
is defined as the set of vanishing drift configurations:

\Lambda=\left\{q\in S^{d-1}:H_{d}(q)=0\right\}.

(32)

By leveraging the monotonicity property established in Lemma
4
, we prove that this equilibrium set consists exclusively of the true latent signal direction and its exact inverse, showing that no spurious local maxima or saddle points can attract the optimization path inside the sphere.

Theorem 1

(Global Convergence to the Signal Subspace)
.

Let
\{q_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
be the sequence of query vectors generated by the projected stochastic gradient ascent update scheme on the hypersphere
S^{d-1}
. Assume the step-size sequence satisfies the standard Robbins-Monro conditions:

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}^{2}<\infty.

(33)

Furthermore, assume the system operates in the high-dimensional token scaling regime where
R\to\infty
,
L-R\to\infty
, and the inverse temperature and signal strength parameters satisfy
\beta=O(1),\theta_{d}=O(1)
. Then, the sequence of learned queries converges almost surely to the isolated latent signal subspace:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\inf_{q^{*}\in\{\pm\xi_{d}\}}\|q_{k}-q^{*}\|=0,\qquad\text{a.s.}

(34)

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let

\displaystyle H_{d}(q)=(I_{d}-qq^{\top})\nabla J_{d}(q)

denote the limiting drift field, and consider the ODE

\displaystyle\dot{q}=H_{d}(q).

(35)

By Lemma
1
,
H_{d}
is continuous on the compact manifold
S^{d-1}
.

We first characterize the equilibrium set of the ODE. Along any solution
q(t)
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}J_{d}(q(t))

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla J_{d}(q(t)),\dot{q}(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla J_{d}(q(t)),(I_{d}-q(t)q(t)^{\top})\nabla J_{d}(q(t))\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\|(I_{d}-q(t)q(t)^{\top})\nabla J_{d}(q(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|H_{d}(q(t))\|^{2}\geq 0.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}J_{d}(q(t))=0\quad\text{if and only if}\quad H_{d}(q(t))=0.

(36)

Hence,
J_{d}
is a strict Lyapunov function for the flow.

Now, by Lemma
2
and Lemma
4
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla J_{d}(q)=\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(\rho)\,\xi_{d},\qquad\rho=\langle q,\xi_{d}\rangle,

(37)

and

\displaystyle\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(\rho)>0,\qquad\rho\in[-1,1].

(38)

Hence, the equilibrium condition becomes

\displaystyle\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(\rho)\,(I_{d}-qq^{\top})\xi_{d}=0,

(39)

which implies

\displaystyle(I_{d}-qq^{\top})\xi_{d}=0.

(40)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\xi_{d}=qq^{\top}\xi_{d}=\langle q,\xi_{d}\rangle q=\rho q.

(41)

Taking norms gives

\displaystyle 1=\|\xi_{d}\|=|\rho|\,\|q\|=|\rho|,

(42)

and therefore

\displaystyle\rho=\pm 1.

(43)

Consequently, the equilibrium set is

\displaystyle\Lambda=\{\xi_{d},-\xi_{d}\}.

(44)

Since
J_{d}
is a strict Lyapunov function, every internally chain transitive invariant set of the flow is contained in
\Lambda

[
6
]
.

Now, by Proposition
1
, the affine interpolation of the stochastic iterates forms an asymptotic pseudo-trajectory of the ODE. Hence, by the Kushner–Clark theorem
[
17
,
6
]
, the limit set
\omega(\{q_{k}\})
is almost surely an internally chain transitive invariant set of the flow generated by
H_{d}
.

Since the only internally chain transitive invariant sets are the equilibria
\pm\xi_{d}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\omega(\{q_{k}\})\subseteq\{\pm\xi_{d}\}\qquad\text{a.s.}

(45)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\inf_{q^{\ast}\in\{\pm\xi_{d}\}}\|q_{k}-q^{\ast}\|=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(46)

This completes the proof.
∎

As an immediate structural consequence of Theorem
1
, we can guarantee that the empirical softmax attention weights concentration profile converges to a clean, deterministic indicator mapping. This confirms that the model asymptotically ignores the high-dimensional background noise.

Corollary 1

(Asymptotic Attention Partition Filtering)
.

Under the global convergence guarantees of Theorem
1
, if the parameter scalings satisfy
\beta=O(1)
,
\theta_{d}=O(1)
and the relative token distribution obeys
\frac{R}{L-R}\to\infty
as
R\to\infty
and
L-R\to\infty
, the total attention weight allocated to the background noise partition vanishes almost surely:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}a_{j}(q_{k},X)=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(47)

Consequently, the entire attention allocation mass concentrates within the informative token partition:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}a_{i}(q_{k},X)=1\qquad\text{a.s.}

(48)

Remark 1

(Separation of Convergence and Attention Concentration)
.

We highlight a fundamental structural distinction between the requirements for geometric query convergence (Theorem
1
) and absolute partition filtering (Corollary
1
). The optimization process requires only
R\to\infty
and
L-R\to\infty
because isotropic noise cancels out in expectation within the gradient drift field, allowing
q_{k}
to successfully track the signal direction. However, at the equilibrium point, the static softmax distribution requires the stronger relative density condition
\frac{R}{L-R}\to\infty
to collectively drown out the noise partition when individual signal components are weak (
\beta\theta_{d}=O(1)
).

Proof.

Recall that under the inverse temperature parameter
\beta>0
, the attention weight
a_{m}(q_{k},X)
assigned to an arbitrary token
x_{m}
at step
k
is given by the softmax function:

\displaystyle a_{m}(q_{k},X)=\frac{\exp(\beta\langle q_{k},x_{m}\rangle)}{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{I}}\exp(\beta\langle q_{k},x_{l}\rangle)+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}\exp(\beta\langle q_{k},x_{n}\rangle)}.

(49)

By Theorem
1
, the learned query sequence converges almost surely to the isolated signal subspace, meaning
\lim_{k\to\infty}q_{k}=q^{*}\in\{\pm\xi_{d}\}
a.s. Without loss of generality, assume
q^{*}=\xi_{d}
.

We now apply the continuous mapping theorem to analyze the total attention mass allocated to the background noise partition,
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}a_{j}(q_{k},X)
, as
t\to\infty
:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}a_{j}(q_{k},X)=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}\exp\left(\beta\langle\xi_{d},z_{j}\rangle\right)}{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{I}}\exp\left(\beta\theta_{d}+\beta\langle\xi_{d},z_{l}\rangle\right)+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}\exp\left(\beta\langle\xi_{d},z_{n}\rangle\right)}.

(50)

To evaluate this fraction under the partitioned scaling regime, we divide both the numerator and the denominator by the total sum over the nuisance elements,
\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}\exp\left(\beta\langle\xi_{d},z_{n}\rangle\right)
. This simplifies the total mass expression to:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}a_{j}(q_{k},X)=\frac{1}{\exp(\beta\theta_{d})\cdot\frac{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{I}}\exp\left(\beta\langle\xi_{d},z_{l}\rangle\right)}{\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}\exp\left(\beta\langle\xi_{d},z_{n}\rangle\right)}+1}.

(51)

Because
z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
are independent and identically distributed, their projections along the fixed signal vector
\xi_{d}
are i.i.d. scalar Gaussians
\langle\xi_{d},z_{i}\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. By the Kolmogorov Law of Large Numbers, as the partition sets grow large (
R\to\infty
and
L-R\to\infty
), the empirical means converge to their identical expected values
\mathbb{E}[e^{\beta\langle\xi_{d},z\rangle}]=e^{\beta^{2}/2}
almost surely:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{I}}\exp\left(\beta\langle\xi_{d},z_{l}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}e^{\beta^{2}/2},

(52)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L-R}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}\exp\left(\beta\langle\xi_{d},z_{n}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}e^{\beta^{2}/2}.

(53)

Evaluating the ratio of these two sums under their respective dimensions gives:

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{I}}\exp\left(\beta\langle\xi_{d},z_{l}\rangle\right)}{\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}\exp\left(\beta\langle\xi_{d},z_{n}\rangle\right)}=\frac{R\cdot\left(e^{\beta^{2}/2}+o(1)\right)}{(L-R)\cdot\left(e^{\beta^{2}/2}+o(1)\right)}=\frac{R}{L-R}(1+o(1))\qquad\text{a.s.}

(54)

By the high-dimensional token scaling and concentration criteria defined in Lemma
3
, the relative density parameter ratio
\frac{R}{L-R}
grows unboundedly large as the systems expand (
\frac{R}{L-R}\to\infty
). Substituting the asymptotic behavior in (
54
) back into the total mass fraction in (
51
) yields:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}a_{j}(q_{k},X)=\frac{1}{\exp(\beta\theta_{d})\cdot\infty+1}=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(55)

Consequently, because the sum of all attention weights must equal 1, the total mass of the informative partition matches:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{i}(q_{k},X)=1-\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}a_{j}(q_{k},X)=1-0=1\qquad\text{a.s.}

(56)

This completes the proof.

∎

7.1
Proof of Lemma
1

Let
q
be an arbitrary vector in the embedding domain. Consider the joint functional mapping:

\displaystyle(q,X)\mapsto a_{i}(q,X)=\frac{e^{\beta\langle x_{i},q\rangle}}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}e^{\beta\langle x_{j},q\rangle}}.

(57)

This mapping is a composition of linear products and exponential terms. Since the denominator is a sum of positive exponentials, it is strictly positive for all bounded realizations of
X
and
q
, preventing singularities. Thus, the mapping is smooth jointly in
q
and
X
.

We calculate the exact partial derivative with respect to a query coordinate
q_{k}
. From (
57
) we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial a_{i}(q,X)}{\partial q_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta x_{ik}e^{\beta\langle x_{i},q\rangle}\sum_{j=1}^{L}e^{\beta\langle x_{j},q\rangle}-e^{\beta\langle x_{i},q\rangle}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\beta x_{jk}e^{\beta\langle x_{j},q\rangle}}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{L}e^{\beta\langle x_{j},q\rangle}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\beta x_{ik}a_{i}(q,X)-\beta a_{i}(q,X)\sum_{j=1}^{L}a_{j}(q,X)x_{jk}

\displaystyle=\beta a_{i}(q,X)\left(x_{ik}-f_{q}(X)_{k}\right).

(58)

By induction, any arbitrary multi-index derivative of order
\alpha
, denoted
\partial_{q}^{\alpha}a_{i}(q,X)
, can be expressed as a finite sum of algebraic products of polynomials in the token coordinates and products of attention weights:

\displaystyle\partial_{q}^{\alpha}a_{i}(q,X)=\sum_{m}P_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{L})\prod_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}a_{j}(q,X),

(59)

where each
P_{m}
is a multivariate polynomial mapping. Because the softmax weights are bounded within the unit interval (
0<a_{j}<1
), from (
59
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{q}^{\alpha}a_{i}(q,X)\right|\leq\sum_{m}|P_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{L})|\leq C_{\alpha}\left(1+\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{m_{\alpha}}\right).

(60)

where
m_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}
depends on the order
|\alpha|
.

Now, consider the pooled attention output function
f_{q}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(q,X)x_{i}
. Its derivative satisfies:

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{q}^{\alpha}f_{q}(X)\right\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left|\partial_{q}^{\alpha}a_{i}(q,X)\right|\cdot\|x_{i}\|\leq C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\left(1+\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{m_{\alpha}+1}\right).

(61)

The unsupervised population objective function is
J_{d}(q)=\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{q}(X)\|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle f_{q}(X),f_{q}(X)\rangle\right]
. Applying the product rule, the partial derivatives of the integrand are bounded by the polynomial envelope of the token inputs:

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{q}^{\alpha}\left(\langle f_{q}(X),f_{q}(X)\rangle\right)\right|\leq C_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\left(1+\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{2m_{\alpha}+2}\right).

(62)

To differentiate under the expectation operator, we must verify that the polynomial envelope is integrable. Because each token
x_{i}=v_{i}\theta_{d}\xi_{d}+z_{i}
is a shifted standard Gaussian vector, its norm components possess finite moments of all orders. Specifically, using Gaussian tail properties, the maximum norm across
L
variables satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{k}\right]\leq 2^{k}\left(\theta_{d}^{k}+\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|z_{j}\|^{k}\right]\right)<\infty,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

(63)

Since the integrand derivatives are bounded by an integrable function independent of
q
, the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem permits the exchange of differentiation and expectation to all orders
[
25
]
. Hence,
J_{d}(q)\in C^{\infty}(S^{d-1})
.

7.2
Proof of Lemma
2

Let
U\in O(d)=\{M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:M^{\top}M=I_{d}\}
be an arbitrary orthogonal transformation matrix that leaves the hidden signal vector invariant:

\displaystyle U\xi_{d}=\xi_{d}.

(64)

We evaluate the statistical distribution of the rotated input token matrix
UX=[Ux_{1},Ux_{2},\dots,Ux_{L}]^{\top}
. Applying the linear transformation to each token model equation:

\displaystyle Ux_{i}=v_{i}\theta_{d}U\xi_{d}+Uz_{i}=v_{i}\theta_{d}\xi_{d}+Uz_{i}.

(65)

Because the noise component
z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is an isotropic multivariate Gaussian vector, its probability density function is invariant under orthogonal rotation. Thus,
Uz_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,UI_{d}U^{\top})=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. Since the indicators
v_{i}
and the signal direction
\xi_{d}
are unchanged, the joint distribution of the transformed token matrix matches the original matrix:

\displaystyle(Ux_{1},Ux_{2},\dots,Ux_{L})\overset{d}{=}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{L}).

(66)

Now, we evaluate the attention weights under a rotated query
Uq
and rotated data inputs
UX
:

\displaystyle a_{i}(Uq,UX)=\frac{e^{\beta\langle Ux_{i},Uq\rangle}}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}e^{\beta\langle Ux_{j},Uq\rangle}}.

(67)

Since
U
is orthogonal,
U^{\top}U=I_{d}
, which implies
\langle Ux_{i},Uq\rangle=\langle x_{i},q\rangle
. Substituting this back to (
67
) yields:

\displaystyle a_{i}(Uq,UX)=\frac{e^{\beta\langle x_{i},q\rangle}}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}e^{\beta\langle x_{j},q\rangle}}=a_{i}(q,X).

(68)

Using the identity (
68
), we express the pooled output function under rotation:

\displaystyle f_{Uq}(UX)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(Uq,UX)(Ux_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(q,X)Ux_{i}=U\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(q,X)x_{i}\right)=Uf_{q}(X).

(69)

Now, we evaluate the unsupervised population objective function at the transformed query vector
Uq
:

\displaystyle J_{d}(Uq)=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\|f_{Uq}(X)\|^{2}\right].

(70)

Since the expectation is taken over the entire distribution of the token matrix, and the distribution is invariant under rotation (
X\overset{d}{=}UX
), we change variables to
UX
:

\displaystyle J_{d}(Uq)=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\|f_{Uq}(UX)\|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\|Uf_{q}(X)\|^{2}\right],

(71)

where the second equality in (
71
) follows from (
69
).

The vector norm is invariant under orthogonal matrix operations (
\|Uv\|^{2}=v^{\top}U^{\top}Uv=\|v\|^{2}
). Therefore, from (
71
) we obtain:

\displaystyle J_{d}(Uq)=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\|f_{q}(X)\|^{2}\right]=J_{d}(q).

(72)

This equality holds for any orthogonal transformation
U
that fixes
\xi_{d}
. By the transitive action of the stabilizer subgroup

\mathrm{Stab}(\xi_{d})=\{U\in O(d):U\xi_{d}=\xi_{d}\}\cong O(d-1)

on the unit sphere of
\xi_{d}^{\perp}
, any two vectors

q_{1},q_{2}\in S^{d-1}
satisfying

\langle q_{1},\xi_{d}\rangle=\langle q_{2},\xi_{d}\rangle

can be mapped to one another by an orthogonal transformation
U\in O(d)
such that

U\xi_{d}=\xi_{d},Uq_{1}=q_{2}

(see, e.g., Lee
[
19
]
). Thus,
J_{d}(q)
is constant on the contours where
\langle q,\xi_{d}\rangle
is constant. This guarantees the existence of a unique smooth scalar mapping
\Psi_{d}:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
J_{d}(q)=\Psi_{d}(\langle q,\xi_{d}\rangle)
.

Proof of Lemma
3

The squared Euclidean norm of the full attention weight vector is the sum of the squared weights across both partitions:

\displaystyle\|a(q)\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(q,X)^{2}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{i}(q,X)^{2}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}a_{j}(q,X)^{2}.

(73)

Let
\rho=\langle q,\xi_{d}\rangle
. We decompose each background noise vector
z_{i}
into a component aligned with the query vector
q
and an orthogonal component residing in the subspace
q^{\perp}
:

\displaystyle z_{i}=G_{i}q+z_{i}^{\perp},\quad\text{where }G_{i}=\langle z_{i},q\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad z_{i}^{\perp}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}-qq^{\top}).

(74)

We evaluate the exact inner product projections
\langle x_{i},q\rangle
for the tokens based on their partition identity. For informative tokens (
i\in\mathcal{I}
):

\displaystyle\langle x_{i},q\rangle=v_{i}\theta_{d}\langle\xi_{d},q\rangle+\langle z_{i},q\rangle=\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i},\quad G_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(75)

For noise tokens (
j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}
):

\displaystyle\langle x_{j},q\rangle=0\cdot\theta_{d}\langle\xi_{d},q\rangle+\langle z_{j},q\rangle=G_{j}^{\prime},\quad G_{j}^{\prime}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(76)

We isolate the deterministic signal scaling factors from the random components in the informative partition sum
A_{\rho}
:

\displaystyle A_{\rho}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e^{\beta(\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i})}=e^{\beta\theta_{d}\rho}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e^{\beta G_{i}},

(77)

where
G_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The terms
e^{\beta G_{i}}
are independent and identically distributed log-normal ran
dom variables. Their theoretical expected value is given by the moment-generating function of a standard normal distribution:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\beta G_{i}}\right]=e^{\beta^{2}/2}.

(78)

By the Strong Law of Large Numbers, the empirical sample average converges in probability to this expected value as the number of informative tokens grows (
R\rightarrow\infty
):

\displaystyle\text{lim}_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{R}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e^{\beta G_{i}}=e^{\beta^{2}/2}\qquad\text{a.s.}.

(79)

From (
77
) and (
79
) we have

\displaystyle A_{\rho}=R\cdot e^{\beta\theta_{d}\rho+\beta^{2}/2}\left(1+o(1)\right)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(80)

We apply the same analysis to the noise partition sum
B=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}e^{\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}
. The terms
e^{\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}
are also i.i.d. log-normal variables with the same expected value:
\mathbb{E}[e^{\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}]=e^{\beta^{2}/2}
. Since the number of noise tokens is
L-R
, the sample average satisfies:

\displaystyle\text{lim}_{L-R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{L-R}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}e^{\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}=e^{\beta^{2}/2}\qquad\text{a.s.}\implies B=(L-R)\cdot e^{\beta^{2}/2}\left(1+o(1)\right)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(81)

Now, observe that for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
,

\displaystyle 0<a_{i}(q,X)=\frac{e^{\beta(\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i})}}{A_{\rho}+B}\leq\frac{e^{\beta(\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i})}}{A_{\rho}},

(82)

which leads to

\displaystyle a_{i}(q,X)^{2}\leq\frac{e^{2\beta(\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i})}}{A_{\rho}^{2}}.

(83)

Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{i}(q,X)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{e^{2\beta(\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i})}}{A_{\rho}^{2}}

(84)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{e^{2\beta(\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i})}}{(R\cdot e^{\beta\theta_{d}\rho+\beta^{2}/2}\left(1+o(1)\right))^{2}}

(85)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\beta^{2}}R}\bigg[\frac{1}{R}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e^{2\beta G_{i}}\bigg](1+o(1))\qquad\text{a.s.}.

(86)

The terms in both sums are independent log-normal random variables. Applying the Strong Law of Large Numbers, these sample averages converge in probability to their theoretical expected values:

\displaystyle\text{lim}_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{R}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e^{2\beta G_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{2\beta G_{i}}\right]=e^{(2\beta)^{2}/2}=e^{2\beta^{2}}\qquad\text{a.s.}.

(87)

From (
86
) and (
87
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{i}(q,X)^{2}\leq\frac{e^{\beta^{2}}}{R}(1+o(1))\qquad\text{a.s.}.

(88)

Similarly, for each
j\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
we have

\displaystyle 0<a_{j}(q,X)=\frac{e^{\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}}{A+B}\leq\frac{e^{\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}}{B},

(89)

which leads to

\displaystyle a_{j}(q,X)^{2}\leq\frac{e^{2\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}}{B^{2}}.

(90)

Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{0}}a_{j}(q,X)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{e^{2\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}}{B^{2}}

(91)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{0}}\frac{e^{2\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}}{((L-R)\cdot e^{\beta^{2}/2}\left(1+o(1)\right))^{2}}

(92)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\beta^{2}}(L-R)}\bigg[\frac{1}{(L-R)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{0}}e^{2\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}\bigg](1+o(1))\qquad\text{a.s.}.

(93)

The terms in both sums are independent log-normal random variables. Applying the Weak Law of Large Numbers, these sample averages converge in probability to their theoretical expected values:

\displaystyle\text{plim}_{L-R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{L-R}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{0}}e^{2\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{2\beta G_{j}^{\prime}}\right]=e^{(2\beta)^{2}/2}=e^{2\beta^{2}}.

(94)

From (
93
) and (
94
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{0}}a_{i}(q,X)^{2}\leq\frac{e^{\beta^{2}}}{L-R}(1+o(1))\qquad\text{a.s.}.

(95)

We substitute these limits back into Equation
73
:

\displaystyle\|a(q)\|^{2}\leq\frac{e^{\beta^{2}}}{R}(1+o(1))+\frac{e^{\beta^{2}}}{L-R}(1+o_{P}(1))\leq\frac{Le^{\beta^{2}}}{R(L-R)}(1+o(1))\qquad\text{a.s.}.

(96)

It follows that

\displaystyle\|a(q)\|^{2}=O\left(\frac{Le^{\beta^{2}}}{R(L-R)}\right).

(97)

This concludes our proof of Lemma
3
.

7.4
Proof of Lemma
4

Before proving Lemma
4
, we prove the following result.

Lemma 10
.

Let

\displaystyle S_{L}(\rho,X)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}^{2}(q,X),

(98)

where

\displaystyle a_{i}(q,X)=\frac{\exp(\beta y_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}\exp(\beta y_{j})},

(99)

and

\displaystyle y_{i}=\theta_{d}v_{i}\rho+G_{i},

(100)

with
G_{1},\ldots,G_{L}
i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables.
Assume that

\displaystyle S_{L}(\rho,X)\to 0\qquad\text{a.s.}

(101)

for every
\rho\in[-1,1]
.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\mathbb{E}[S_{L}(\rho,X)]\longrightarrow 0.

(102)

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle F_{L}(\rho)=\mathbb{E}[S_{L}(\rho,X)].

(103)

Since

\displaystyle y_{i}=\theta_{d}v_{i}\rho+G_{i},

(104)

we may write

\displaystyle F_{L}(\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{L}}S_{L}(\theta_{d}v_{1}\rho+g_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}v_{L}\rho+g_{L})\varphi(g)\,dg,

(105)

where

\displaystyle\varphi(g)=(2\pi)^{-L/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|g\|^{2}}{2}\right)

(106)

denotes the standard Gaussian density on
\mathbb{R}^{L}
.

Since the softmax map is smooth,
S_{L}
is continuously differentiable with respect to the coordinates
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{L})
. Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{L}}{\partial y_{k}}=2\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial y_{k}}.

(107)

Using the softmax derivative formula

\displaystyle\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial y_{k}}=\beta a_{i}(\delta_{ik}-a_{k}),

(108)

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{L}}{\partial y_{k}}

\displaystyle=2\beta\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}^{2}(\delta_{ik}-a_{k})

(109)

\displaystyle=2\beta\left(a_{k}^{2}-a_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}^{2}\right)

(110)

\displaystyle=2\beta\bigl(a_{k}^{2}-a_{k}S_{L}\bigr).

(111)

Since

\displaystyle 0\leq a_{k}\leq 1,\qquad 0\leq S_{L}\leq 1,

(112)

it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial S_{L}}{\partial y_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta\left(a_{k}^{2}+a_{k}S_{L}\right)

(113)

\displaystyle\leq 4\beta a_{k}.

(114)

Hence

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}S_{L}(\theta_{d}v\rho+g)\right|

\displaystyle=\theta_{d}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{L}v_{k}\frac{\partial S_{L}}{\partial y_{k}}\right|

(115)

\displaystyle\leq 4\beta\theta_{d}\sum_{k=1}^{L}v_{k}a_{k}

(116)

\displaystyle\leq 4\beta\theta_{d}.

(117)

Therefore the derivative is dominated by the integrable constant

\displaystyle 4\beta\theta_{d}.

(118)

The Dominated Convergence Theorem
[
25
]
yields

\displaystyle F_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\theta_{d}\sum_{k=1}^{L}v_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial S_{L}}{\partial y_{k}}\right].

(119)

Substituting the expression for
\partial S_{L}/\partial y_{k}
,

\displaystyle|F_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta\theta_{d}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{L}v_{k}\left(a_{k}^{2}+a_{k}S_{L}\right)\right]

(120)

\displaystyle=2\beta\theta_{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{L}v_{k}a_{k}^{2}\right]+2\beta\theta_{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{L}v_{k}a_{k}\right)S_{L}\right]

(121)

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta\theta_{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{L}a_{k}^{2}\right]+2\beta\theta_{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{L}a_{k}\right)S_{L}\right]

(122)

\displaystyle=2\beta\theta_{d}\mathbb{E}[S_{L}]+2\beta\theta_{d}\mathbb{E}[S_{L}]

(123)

\displaystyle=4\beta\theta_{d}\mathbb{E}[S_{L}].

(124)

Since

\displaystyle 0\leq S_{L}\leq 1

(125)

and

\displaystyle S_{L}(\rho,X)\to 0\qquad\text{a.s.},

(126)

the Dominated Convergence Theorem
[
25
]
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{L}]\longrightarrow 0.

(127)

Consequently,

\displaystyle|F_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)|\leq 4\beta\theta_{d}\mathbb{E}[S_{L}]\longrightarrow 0.

(128)

Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\mathbb{E}[S_{L}(\rho,X)]=F_{L}^{\prime}(\rho)\longrightarrow 0.

(129)

This completes the proof.
∎

Now, let us return to prove Lemma
4
. Denote by
\rho=\langle q,\xi_{d}\rangle
. Then, for all
i\in[L]
we have

\displaystyle y_{i}:=\langle q,x_{i}\rangle=v_{i}\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i},\qquad G_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(130)

The cumulative attention weight mass concentrated on the informative partition is given by:

\displaystyle\alpha(q):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{i}(q,X)=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e^{\beta\langle x_{i},q\rangle}}{\sum_{k=1}^{L}e^{\beta\langle x_{k},q\rangle}}=\frac{A_{\rho}}{A_{\rho}+B},

(131)

where

\displaystyle A_{\rho}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e^{\beta(\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i})},\quad B=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}e^{\beta G_{j}^{\prime}},\qquad G_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1),G_{j}^{\prime}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(132)

We expand the attention pooled representation vector
f_{q}(X)
by separating it into informative and noise partitions:

\displaystyle f_{q}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{i}(q,X)(\theta_{d}\xi_{d}+z_{i})+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{o}}a_{j}(q,X)z_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{i}(q,X)\right)\theta_{d}\xi_{d}+\sum_{k=1}^{L}a_{k}(q,X)z_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha(q)\theta_{d}\xi_{d}+\sum_{k=1}^{L}a_{k}(q,X)z_{k}.

(133)

From (
133
), we have:

\displaystyle\|f_{q}(X)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha(q)\theta_{d}\xi_{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}z_{i},\,\alpha(q)\theta_{d}\xi_{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{L}a_{j}z_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha(q)^{2}\theta_{d}^{2}\|\xi_{d}\|^{2}+2\alpha(q)\theta_{d}\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}\langle\xi_{d},z_{i}\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{L}a_{i}a_{j}\langle z_{i},z_{j}\rangle.

(134)

Since
\|\xi_{d}\|^{2}=1
, taking the expectation both sides of (
134

\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\rho)=\theta_{d}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(q)^{2}\right]+2\theta_{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(q)\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}\langle\xi_{d},z_{i}\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{L}a_{i}a_{j}\langle z_{i},z_{j}\rangle\right].

(135)

Because the noise components
z_{k}
have zero mean and are independent of each other, the cross-terms simplify. Differentiating the core informative signal component
\alpha(q)=\frac{A_{\rho}}{A_{\rho}+B}
with respect to
\rho
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\alpha(q)}{\partial\rho}=\frac{A_{\rho}^{\prime}B}{(A_{\rho}+B)^{2}}.

We evaluate the derivative of the partition sum
A_{\rho}
:

\displaystyle A_{\rho}^{\prime}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e^{\beta(\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i})}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\beta\theta_{d}e^{\beta(\theta_{d}\rho+G_{i})}=\beta\theta_{d}A_{\rho}.

(137)

Substituting this back into the derivative of
\alpha(q)
gives:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\alpha(q)}{\partial\rho}=\beta\theta_{d}\frac{A_{\rho}B}{(A_{\rho}+B)^{2}}.

(138)

We calculate the derivative of the squared mass
\alpha(q)^{2}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\alpha(q)^{2})}{\partial\rho}=2\alpha(q)\frac{\partial\alpha(q)}{\partial\rho}=2\beta\theta_{d}\frac{A_{\rho}^{2}B}{(A_{\rho}+B)^{3}}>0.

(139)

Observe that since

a.s., we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\partial(\alpha(q)^{2})}{\partial\rho}\bigg|=2\beta\theta_{d}\frac{A_{\rho}^{2}B}{(A_{\rho}+B)^{3}}\leq 2\beta\theta_{d}.

(140)

Hence, by Dominated Convergence Theorem
[
25
]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(q)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial\alpha(q)^{2}}{\partial\rho}\right].

(141)

It follows from (
135

\displaystyle\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(\rho)

\displaystyle=2\beta\theta_{d}^{3}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{A_{\rho}^{2}B}{(A_{\rho}+B)^{3}}\right]+\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(q,X)^{2}\right]

(142)

\displaystyle=2\beta\theta_{d}^{3}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{A_{\rho}^{2}B}{(A_{\rho}+B)^{3}}\right]+\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{L}(q,X)\right].

(143)

Now, by Lemma
3
we have

\displaystyle S_{L}(q,X)=O\left(\frac{Le^{\beta^{2}}}{R(L-R)}\right),\qquad\text{a.s.}

(144)

Hence,
S_{L}(q,X)\to 0
a.s. if

and
\beta=O(1)

10
that

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{L}(q,X)\right]=0.

(145)

On the other hand, since

a.s., it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{A_{\rho}^{2}B}{(A_{\rho}+B)^{3}}\right]>0.

(146)

From (
143
), (
145
), and (
146
), we conclude that
\Psi_{d}^{\prime}(\rho)>0
for all
\rho\in[-1,1]
under the condition that

and
L-R\to\infty
. This concludes our proof of Lemma
4
.

7.5
Proof of Lemma
5

The empirical stochastic gradient is the derivative of the squared output norm with respect to the query vector:

\displaystyle g_{k}=\nabla_{q}\|f_{q}(X)\|^{2}=2\left[\nabla_{q}f_{q}(X)\right]^{\top}f_{q}(X).

(147)

The partial derivative of the pooled output function with respect to the query coordinates is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{q}f_{q}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}x_{i}\left(\nabla_{q}a_{i}(q,X)\right)^{\top}.

(148)

Substituting the partial derivative of the attention weights derived in the proof of Lemma
1
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{q}a_{i}(q,X)=\beta a_{i}(q,X)\left(x_{i}-f_{q}(X)\right).

(149)

This allows us to write the gradient tensor as:

\displaystyle\nabla_{q}f_{q}(X)=\beta\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(q,X)x_{i}(x_{i}-f_{q}(X))^{\top}.

(150)

We substitute (
150
) back into (
147
) and obtain:

\displaystyle g_{k}

\displaystyle=2\beta\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(q,X)(x_{i}-f_{q}(X))x_{i}^{\top}\right]f_{q}(X)

\displaystyle=2\beta\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(q,X)\langle x_{i},f_{q}(X)\rangle(x_{i}-f_{q}(X)).

(151)

By bounding the norm of
g_{k}
using the triangle inequality and the properties of convex combinations (
\sum a_{i}=1
), from (
151
) we have

\displaystyle\|g_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(q,X)|\langle x_{i},f_{q}(X)\rangle|\cdot\|x_{i}-f_{q}(X)\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}(q,X)\|x_{i}\|\cdot\|f_{q}(X)\|\left(\|x_{i}\|+\|f_{q}(X)\|\right).

(152)

Since
f_{q}(X)
is a convex combination of the tokens, its norm is bounded by the maximum token norm:

\displaystyle\|f_{q}(X)\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}a_{i}\|x_{i}\|\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|.

(153)

Substituting (
153
) into (
152
) simplifies it to a cubic expression:

\displaystyle\|g_{k}|\leq 2\beta\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|\cdot\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|\cdot\left(2\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|\right)=4\beta\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{3}.

(154)

Squaring this expression yields:

\displaystyle\|g_{k}\|^{2}\leq 16\beta^{2}\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{6}.

(155)

We compute the expectation of this upper bound. Each token is modeled as
x_{j}=v_{j}\theta_{d}\xi_{d}+z_{j}
, where
z_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. Using the
C_{r}
inequality:

\displaystyle\|x_{j}\|^{6}\leq 32\left(\theta_{d}^{6}\|\xi_{d}\|^{6}+\|z_{j}\|^{6}\right)=32\left(\theta_{d}^{6}+\|z_{j}\|^{6}\right).

(156)

This bounds the maximum token norm by the maximum of the noise vector norms:

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{6}\leq 32\theta_{d}^{6}+32\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|z_{j}\|^{6}.

(157)

The squared norm of a standard Gaussian vector,
\|z_{j}\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}z_{jk}^{2}
, follows a chi-squared distribution with
d
degrees of freedom (
\chi^{2}_{d}
). Its sixth moment scales quadratically with the dimension:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|z_{j}\|^{6}\right]=d(d+2)(d+4)=\mathcal{O}(d^{3}).

(158)

By applying standard Gaussian concentration inequalities for the maximum of
L
independent chi-squared variables, the expectation is bounded by the individual moment scaling:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|z_{j}\|^{6}\right]\leq C^{*}d^{3}.

(159)

Because the objective is unsupervised, the gradient contains higher-order polynomial terms. Incorporating this into the full expectation bounds the stochastic gradient by the fourth power of the dimension:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|g_{k}\|^{2}\right]\leq 16\beta^{2}\cdot 32\left(\theta_{d}^{6}+C^{*}d^{3}\right)\leq Cd^{4},

(160)

where (
160
) follows from the assumption that
\beta=O(1)
and
\theta_{d}=O(1)
.
This completes the proof.

Proof of Lemma
6

Let
u_{k}=q_{k}+\eta_{k}g_{k}
be the unnormalized update vector at step
k
. The next query vector is defined by normalizing
u_{k}
:

\displaystyle q_{k+1}=\frac{u_{k}}{\|u_{k}\|}.

(161)

We compute the squared norm of
u_{k}
:

\displaystyle\|u_{k}\|^{2}=\langle q_{k}+\eta_{k}g_{k},\,q_{k}+\eta_{k}g_{k}\rangle=\|q_{k}\|^{2}+2\eta_{k}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle+\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}.

(162)

Since
q_{k}\in S^{d-1}
, we have
\|q_{k}\|^{2}=1
. This simplifies (

) to:

\displaystyle\|u_{k}\|^{2}=1+2\eta_{k}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle+\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}.

(163)

Now, observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\eta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|g_{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq Cd^{4}\sum_{k}\eta_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=Cd^{4}\sum_{k}\frac{\gamma_{k}^{2}}{d^{4}}

\displaystyle=C\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle<\infty.

(164)

Hence, for each fixed
\epsilon>0
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t}\mathbb{P}(\eta_{k}\|g_{k}\|>\epsilon)<\infty.

(165)

This means that
\eta_{k}\|g_{k}\|\to 0

[
8
]
.

We apply a second-order Taylor expansion to the normalization scalar function
h(x)=(1+x)^{-1/2}
, where
x=2\eta_{k}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle+\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}

(1+x)^{-1/2}=1-\frac{1}{2}x+\frac{3}{8}x^{2}+\mathcal{O}(x^{3}).

(166)

Substituting
x
into the expansion yields:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|u_{k}\|}

\displaystyle=\left(1+2\eta_{k}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle+\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\left(2\eta_{k}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle+\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}\right)+\frac{3}{8}\left(2\eta_{k}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle+\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}\right)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta_{k}^{3}\|g_{k}\|^{3})

\displaystyle=1-\eta_{k}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\eta_{k}^{2}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta_{k}^{3}\|g_{k}\|^{3})

\displaystyle=1-\eta_{k}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}).

(167)

Multiplying both sides of (
167

u_{k}

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(q_{k}+\eta_{k}g_{k})\cdot\left(1-\eta_{k}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2})\right)

where the residual error vector

(169)

\displaystyle\|r_{k}\|\leq\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}+C^{\prime}\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}=C\eta_{k}^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}.

(170)

Rewriting the primary updating term using the projection matrix
(I_{d}-q_{k}q_{k}^{\top})g_{k}=g_{k}-q_{k}\langle q_{k},g_{k}\rangle
yields:

\displaystyle q_{k+1}=q_{k}+\eta_{k}(I_{d}-q_{k}q_{k}^{\top})g_{k}+r_{k},

(171)

which completes the proof.

7.7
Proof of Lemma
7

The drift vector field is defined as
H_{d}(q)=(I_{d}-qq^{\top})\nabla_{q}J_{d}(q)
. Since the projection operator function
M(q)=I_{d}-qq^{\top}
is a polynomial mapping, its derivatives are bounded on the compact sphere
S^{d-1}
. Therefore, to prove that
H_{d}
is globally Lipschitz, it suffices to show that the gradient of the population objective,
\nabla_{q}J_{d}(q)
, has a uniformly bounded derivative (Hessian matrix) on the sphere:

\displaystyle\sup_{q\in S^{d-1}}\|\nabla_{q}^{2}J_{d}(q)\|_{\text{op}}<\infty,

(172)

where
\|\cdot\|_{\text{op}}
denotes the matrix operator norm.

We differentiate the gradient vector expression
\nabla_{q}J_{d}(q)=\mathbb{E}\left[2\left[\nabla_{q}f_{q}(X)\right]^{\top}f_{q}(X)\right]
with respect to
q
. Applying the product rule inside the expectation:

\displaystyle\nabla_{q}^{2}J_{d}(q)=2\mathbb{E}\left[\left[\nabla_{q}^{2}f_{q}(X)\right]^{\top}f_{q}(X)+\left[\nabla_{q}f_{q}(X)\right]^{\top}\left[\nabla_{q}f_{q}(X)\right]\right].

(173)

From the derivative bounds established in the proof of Lemma
1
, the first derivative of the pooled output function satisfies:

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{q}f_{q}(X)\|\leq C_{1}\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{2}.

(174)

Differentiating a second time yields a bound on the second derivative tensor:

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{q}^{2}f_{q}(X)\|\leq C_{2}\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{3}.

(175)

We substitute these spatial derivative bounds into the Hessian matrix formula in (
173
):

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{q}^{2}J_{d}(q)\|_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla_{q}^{2}f_{q}(X)\|\cdot\|f_{q}(X)\|+\|\nabla_{q}f_{q}(X)\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(C_{2}\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{3}\right)\cdot\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|\right)+\left(C_{1}\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left[C_{2}\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{4}+C_{1}^{2}\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=C_{3}\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{4}\right].

(176)

Since the tokens are shifted Gaussian vectors, their fourth moments are finite:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|x_{j}\|^{4}\right]\leq C_{4}d^{2}<\infty.

(177)

Because this upper bound is a constant independent of the query vector
q
, the Hessian matrix norm is uniformly bounded across the entire sphere:

\displaystyle\sup_{q\in S^{d-1}}\|\nabla_{q}^{2}J_{d}(q)\|_{\text{op}}\leq C_{5}<\infty.

(178)

This proves that
\nabla_{q}J_{d}(q)
is globally Lipschitz. Combining this with the Lipschitz continuity of the spherical projection matrix ensures that the combined drift field
H_{d}(q)
is globally Lipschitz.

7.8
Proof of Lemma
8

Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma(q_{0},X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
be the natural filtration tracking the optimization history. We isolate the stochastic sampling fluctuations by defining the martingale difference sequence:

\displaystyle M_{k+1}=(I_{d}-q_{k}q_{k}^{\top})\left(g_{k}-\mathbb{E}[g_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\right).

(179)

By construction, its conditional expectation given the historical filtration is zero:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=(I_{d}-q_{k}q_{k}^{\top})\left(\mathbb{E}[g_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[g_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\right)=0.

(180)

This confirms that
S_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\eta_{k}M_{k+1}
is a valid vector-valued martingale.

To prove almost sure convergence using the Martingale Convergence Theorem, we must show that the sum of the conditional variances is finite. We evaluate the squared norm of the martingale difference:

\displaystyle\|M_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq\|I_{d}-q_{k}q_{k}^{\top}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\cdot\|g_{k}-\mathbb{E}[g_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\|^{2}\leq 1\cdot\|g_{k}-\mathbb{E}[g_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\|^{2}.

(181)

Taking the conditional expectation and expanding the variance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|M_{k+1}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|g_{k}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]-\|\mathbb{E}[g_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\|g_{k}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}].

(182)

From (
181
) and (
182
), by computing the total expected variance across all iterations, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\eta_{k}M_{k+1}\|^{2}\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|M_{k+1}\|^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|g_{k}\|^{2}\right].

(183)

On the other hand, by Lemma
6
we have (
\mathbb{E}[\|g_{k}\|^{2}]\leq Cd^{4}
). It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|M_{k+1}\|^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}^{2}\cdot Cd^{4}.

(184)

Since (
\eta_{k}=\frac{\gamma_{k}}{d^{2}}
), from (
184
) we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k}}{d^{2}}\right)^{2}\cdot Cd^{4}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{k}^{2}}{d^{4}}\cdot Cd^{4}=C\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}^{2}.

(185)

On the other hand, by the setup conditions, the scalar step sequence is square-summable (
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}^{2}<\infty
). Therefore, the total variance of the martingale is finite:

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[\|S_{n}\|^{2}]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\eta_{k}M_{k+1}\|^{2}\right]\leq C\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}^{2}<\infty.

(186)

Since it is an
L^{2}
-bounded martingale, the Vector Martingale Convergence Theorem
[
11
]
guarantees that the random series
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}M_{k+1}
converges to a finite limit almost surely.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|r_{k}\|\right]\leq C_{2}d^{4}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k}}{d^{2}}\right)^{2}=C_{2}d^{4}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{k}^{2}}{d^{4}}=C_{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}^{2}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|r_{k}\|\right]\leq C_{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}^{2}<\infty.

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|r_{k}\|<\infty
