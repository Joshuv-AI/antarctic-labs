Emergency departments (EDs) and hospitals are increasingly sites of interaction between healthcare professionals and federal immigration authorities, including U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement and U.S. Customs and Border Protection. Recent shifts in federal enforcement policy, including the rescission of long‐standing limitations on immigration enforcement actions in or near healthcare facilities [1], have intensified these encounters. Patients now more frequently arrive in custody, accompanied by immigration enforcement officers or contracted guards who may assert control over communication, decision‐making, or access to patients. Some individuals are brought to EDs for medical clearance after injuries sustained while arrested, while others present with acute illness or exacerbations of chronic conditions that developed or worsened in detention. This paper seeks to add evidence, clinical context, and operational accuracy to a topic already circulating in public discourse.

The scale of immigration detention has increased substantially this past year. An estimated 290,000 individuals were arrested in 2025, with planned meteoric expansion of detention capacity in the coming year [2]. That same year, 32 deaths were reported in U.S. immigration custody, the highest number recorded in more than two decades [3]. Preliminary reports suggest that mortality trends in 2026 will exceed those observed previously [4]. As of the writing of this paper, at least 22 people have died while in immigration custody, and 10 of these deaths occurred in a medical setting. Together, these data highlight the intersection between detention conditions and downstream healthcare utilization.

These circumstances introduce challenges for maintaining standard clinical practice. Clinicians may encounter situations in which patient confidentiality, informed consent, and independent medical judgment are difficult to operationalize in the presence of immigration enforcement officers. In addition to impacts on patient care, these encounters may contribute to clinician moral injury and distress, particularly when emergency medicine (EM) clinicians feel pressured to compromise patient privacy, deviate from standard practices, or navigate conflicts between enforcement priorities and patient care priorities. Uncertainty regarding institutional policy and the appropriate limits of immigration enforcement authority may further exacerbate tensions related to liability and compliance. For patients, disruptions in care continuity and delays in treatment may increase the risk of adverse outcomes.

In response to these urgent concerns, the National Immigration Law Center (NILC), a leading advocacy organization in U.S. immigration since 1979, has developed Advocating On Behalf of Patients in ICE Custody: A Resource for Health Care Providers and Medical Staff , a national resource guide that outlines practical considerations for clinicians and healthcare institutions caring for patients in immigration custody [5]. In this commentary, we build on this resource to provide practical, bedside‐oriented guidance for EM clinicians when interacting with immigration enforcement officers or caring for patients in immigration custody. We examine the implications of immigration enforcement within healthcare settings while articulating a path forward that protects patient rights, clinical integrity, and the ethical foundations of medical practice.

The following questions highlight clinical questions that EM clinicians may have when caring for patients in immigration custody or interacting with immigration enforcement officials in hospital settings. These scenarios are intended to provide practical guidance grounded in relevant legal frameworks (Table 1). This is not meant to serve as legal advice; rather, the goal is to equip clinicians with knowledge to navigate complex clinical encounters that maintain patient‐centered care. Clinicians should interpret these within the context of institutional policies and applicable local and state regulations.

Question 1: If immigration enforcement officers are in the hospital with a patient, can they talk to another patient? Am I required to share information if they ask me about another patient?

If immigration enforcement officers are accompanying a detained patient to a healthcare facility, they can view anything in plain sight, ask questions of people present in the spaces where they have permission to be, and even arrest people, because officers are legally permitted to question and arrest anyone they believe does not have permission to be in the U.S. or who may have committed certain immigration violations [17]. This presence of the officers in healthcare settings raises important ethical and legal considerations, and it is the responsibility of the hospital to establish clear boundaries for officers to prevent them from accessing other patients or their protected health information (PHI). Like care provided for any patient, clinicians and hospitals have the responsibility to protect PHI under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) [18]. This obligation does not extend to immigration officers. If an immigration officer asks for information or would otherwise be in a position that could compromise a clinician's obligation, hospitals can assert their constitutional rights under the Fourth Amendment based on a reasonable expectation of privacy. Invoking this legal right can also limit the officer's presence to that which is necessary for the patient in their custody, lessening the impact on other patients' care.

When it comes to physical locations, hospitals should clearly delineate private spaces from those that are accessible to the public because immigration enforcement officers may freely enter public hospital spaces and speak to patients, staff, or others. When it comes to records, an immigration enforcement officer may also read documents, including other patient records, that are in plain view without a warrant. So hospitals should be mindful of, e.g., a track board listing assignments and locations for the day with patients' names.

Notably, there are law enforcement and public safety exceptions to HIPAA which have limited applications and would not generally apply to patients in immigration custody without other circumstances, so hospitals should seek legal counsel prior to acting upon this exception [19]. Ultimately, the disclosures are permissible, not mandatory, so a hospital may still decline to provide records without a valid judicial warrant, even if deemed a permissible disclosure under an exception to HIPAA.

If immigration enforcement officers ask clinicians for information regarding additional information about a patient not in their custody, any person has the right to remain silent, so a best practice for HIPAA compliance is to decline to answer and contact hospital legal counsel for further guidance prior to any sharing of protected health information.

Question 2: Can a clinician call the family of a patient in immigration custody? Can a patient in immigration custody call their family or trusted representative with the help of a clinician?

Generally, yes—a clinician can call the family of a person who is in immigration custody both to inform their family and, if necessary, to make health decisions on their behalf. Clinicians should not assume they are not allowed to call family; they should work with officers to prioritize patient care.

First, patients have the federal right to have family or other representatives informed and involved in their medical care. Otherwise, immigration authorities do not inform families that a person has been transferred to a hospital, and the online detainee locator system [9] will also not show that someone is at a hospital.

While an immigration enforcement officer has custodial authority over a patient in immigration custody and can limit the person's interactions, they do not have legal authority over the hospital staff. During medical encounters external to the facility, Performance Based National Detention Standards (PBNDS) and the National Detention Standards (NDS), which govern the day‐to‐day operation of detention centers where immigrants are detained, maintain that immigration enforcement officers should defer to the hospital's standard rules and procedures concerning seriously ill, injured, and dying patients, including contacting next of kin [10]. (PBNDS apply to dedicated facilities holding only immigrant detainees, while NDS apply to non‐dedicated facilities that also hold local or state inmates.) Therefore, immigration enforcement officers' authority does not supersede and should defer to policy enforcing federal health regulations that require hospitals to notify family or other representatives upon their admission to the hospital if the patient wishes [8].

Second, immigrant detainees are in civil—not criminal—detention. Civil detainees are generally entitled to more considerate treatment than criminal detainees [20], and restrictions on visitations or phone calls could be found to be unnecessarily punitive or a violation of a person's constitutional rights [21]. As such, hospital protocols or restrictions for patients in criminal custody should not be automatically applied to these patients, as they may cause unnecessary further hardships for patients and families, and could expose the hospital to civil litigation liability.

In practice, such situations require careful discussion. Clinicians can advocate for patients if communication is relevant to the patient's care, but they should expect possible resistance from immigration enforcement officers who may default to prohibiting family contact under their custodial authority. However, in balancing not interfering with an immigration enforcement officer's duties and providing humane patient care, clinicians should know that federal regulations and immigration authorities' own standards support the right to contact family.

Question 3: Can a clinician ask an immigration enforcement officer to leave the room during a physical examination?

Yes, clinicians can ask immigration enforcement officers to leave the room during a physical examination. For the provider, discussing the patient's private health information with a third party present would violate the patient's HIPAA rights. Although an officer is not bound by HIPAA, the conclusion that they should leave the room is also supported by the National Commission on Correctional Health Care (NCCHC) guidelines [11], which develop healthcare standards and accredit correctional facilities, including many detention facilities. ICE's PBNDS direct detention facilities to strive to achieve and maintain accreditation by NCCHC, thereby incorporating NCCHC standards into expectations for medical care in immigration detention facilities.

Practically, immigration enforcement officers may be unaware of these guidelines and may insist on always remaining with a patient brought for treatment, as federal regulations require them not to leave individuals in their custody unattended [22]. However, there are other ways officers can meet this obligation, so clinicians can and should request that immigration enforcement officers leave the room in the context of medical visits, and especially when surveillance interferes with provision of medical care or violates patients' privacy rights. Clinicians can, e.g., suggest immigration enforcement officers wait at a designated spot in the hallway where they may maintain visual contact of the patient without being close enough to hear conversations.

Question 4: What happens if a patient is unable to make medical decisions for themselves?

Regarding medical decision‐making on behalf of a patient who is incapacitated or otherwise not competent to make health care decisions (medical surrogacy) [23], presentations of immigrant detainees in extremis after a period of medical neglect are not uncommon due to the scarcity of medical care resources available in immigrant detention centers. There are also documented incidents over the years of immigration authorities taking a patient to a hospital who is dying and “releasing” them from immigration custody once admitted [24].

If a patient is unable to make medical decisions for themselves, the best practice is to follow state laws dictating the process by which patients' family or representatives must be involved in making decisions on behalf of the patient.

The clinical reality and legal framework of medical decision‐making for such patients may not align in practice, and discussions with immigration enforcement officers may be required to remind them of required duties and state laws. Medical surrogacy laws often require contacting next of kin or, if unavailable, reaching out to the hospital ethics team for assistance in decision‐making.

While it may be necessary for immigration enforcement officers to make administrative decisions about a detained patient's care, such as placing a guard on duty or getting supervisory approval to take off handcuffs, decisions regarding medical treatment are not within their purview.

Question 5: Can immigration enforcement officers force a hospital to discharge a patient who is not medically stable?

No, clinical decision‐making is the responsibility of the treating clinician and thus immigration enforcement officers do not have the authority to dictate medical care or disposition. The National Detention Standards are clear that a hospital assumes medical decision‐making authority once a chronically, critically, or terminally ill patient is admitted. Arguably, this includes safe discharge decisions [14].

Within detention centers, there is a sub‐agency tasked with providing medical care and overseeing medical care of people in their custody. This agency is called the Immigration and Customs Enforcement Health Service Corps (IHSC) [25], and its staff is required to stay up to date about the status of a patient in immigration custody who is transferred to a hospital. Clinicians should be aware that IHSC staff may relay instructions to immigration enforcement officers, but they should not supersede clinicians' medical care decisions.

In addition to federal obligations under the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act (EMTALA) to evaluate and provide stabilizing treatment to any individual with an emergency medical condition, several state hospital laws prohibit unsafe discharge and reinforce that the treating clinician is responsible for medical decision‐making. A patient cannot be discharged until medically stable, and if requests are made to do so, it is important to refer to EMTALA guidance and escalate to hospital legal counsel [26].

Question 6: Will immigration enforcement officers continue a patient's medical treatment in detention?

It depends. Individuals who are discharged back to immigration detention are entitled to ongoing medical care. Detained individuals should undergo intake screening, chronic disease management, access to medications, and emergency care [27]. However, a growing body of evidence—including congressional investigations, Government Accountability Office reports, and Department of Homeland Security Office of Inspector General inspections—has documented substantial concerns regarding adherence to these standards, and many reports indicate highly variable to no care provided [28, 29, 30]. Peer‐reviewed studies have consistently documented deficiencies in medical care and oversight, including cases of preventable death.

For clinicians discharging patients, it is important to note that discharge does not necessarily result in continuation of medical care and necessary follow‐up. Notably, a patient who will be transferred to a detention facility will depend on the facility's medical staff to provide them with any medication prescribed by a clinician. Detention policies prohibit detainees from holding onto their medication, even if provided by a provider. Instead, the transporting officer holds the medication and is responsible for turning it over to medical personnel at the facility. Only if immigration authorities are releasing a detained patient from their custody will a patient physically control their own medication. Overall, clear documentation of ongoing medical needs and, if possible, direct communication of care plans with the medical staff at the facility can assist with ongoing treatment. If the immigration enforcement officer does not provide the contact information for medical staff at the facility, clinicians can use the local immigration authority field office contact information to request this information.

Question 7: What documentation practices should I follow when documenting clinical care and interactions with immigration enforcement officers?

When documenting interactions with immigration enforcement officers, clinicians should consider documenting if and how interactions may have affected patient care, communication, and disposition. For example, whether immigration enforcement officers were present during the clinical exam (e.g., privacy limitations) and considerations for discharge planning (e.g., communication with medical staff at transferring immigration detention facilities). If relevant to clinical care, documenting names and roles of immigration enforcement personnel should be recorded as well.

When documenting clinical care with patients in immigration custody, confirming appropriate patient identifiers—name, date of birth, next of kin, A‐Number (“Alien Registration Number”)—should be confirmed independently rather than relying on information provided from immigration enforcement officers. If the patient was injured while in immigration custody, objectively document injuries and include photographs if clinically appropriate and, if relevant, document worsening of conditions while in custody. As with all patients, the use of a medical interpreter for patients who speak a language other than English is required. Immigration enforcement officers should never be used as medical interpreters.

Furthermore, clinicians should be careful about both questioning and documentation of patients in immigration custody. A patient may withhold critical information if asked questions that they fear could impact their immigration case, and a clinician can build trust by only asking and documenting clinically necessary information. For example, a clinician should avoid asking or documenting where a person was born or the circumstances of their entry into the country, even if a patient's place of birth or immigration status is considered PHI in their state [6].

Question 8: How can I approach my administration to develop guidance for clinical care for individuals who are in immigration custody?

Discussing in advance and collaborating on policies that prioritize patient care and hospital care can be beneficial both to medical staff working conditions and a hospital's legal obligations. Statutory requirements for policy guiding hospital interactions with immigration enforcement will vary by state; some states require hospitals to have immigration enforcement guidance [6, 15]. Referencing state‐mandated requirements and proffering model policy can be helpful to explain the importance of institutional policy development—prior to immigration enforcement interaction, if possible. While implementation specifics will vary, it is necessary to address several key points with a multidisciplinary stakeholder group: Hospital zoning, centralized authority, standardized response procedures, care of patients in immigration custody [31], and ongoing education for frontline staff.

Hospital zoning is an important aspect of preparation for immigration enforcement. Defining public vs. nonpublic areas is vital to set boundaries with immigration enforcement officers. Certain areas of the hospital are badge access‐only, and the availability of certain hospital access points to immigration enforcement officers will vary more based on function than physical layout.

Centralized authority is a key requirement to determine who in hospital leadership will respond to immigration enforcement officers' requests; these individuals should have access to legal counsel and administrative resources.

Standardized response procedures when interacting with immigration enforcement officers are important to provide guidance to frontline staff and establish contact expectations to the hospital chain of command.

Hospital guidelines for care of patients in immigration custody are often by default identical to general guidelines for care of patients in criminal custody. However, it is imperative to remember that regardless of an individual's interactions with the criminal legal system, if a patient is in the custody of an immigration enforcement officer, they are in civil custody. Thus, general hospital guidelines for care of detained patients should not automatically apply to patients in immigration custody.

Lastly, ongoing education and training for clinicians and staff with routine instruction on institutional procedures and applicable legal and ethical considerations should be in place before immigration enforcement encounters occur.

Discussion

Now more than ever, considerations of immigrant patient treatment are highly relevant to EM clinicians. Heightened immigration enforcement activity has led to growing fears among staff and patients seeking care in hospital settings, leading to delays in presentation, avoidance of care, and challenges in clinical decision‐making and disposition. Patients may perceive that hospitals are no longer safe spaces, which can be the unfortunate reality. Further, patients may be more inclined to seek guidance from healthcare staff when navigating interactions with immigration enforcement officers. At the same time, the expansion of immigration detention has resulted in more patients presenting to the ED in immigration custody, often with complex or advanced medical conditions. Developing institutional guidance before these encounters occur can reduce uncertainty and ensure patient care remains consistent with ethical, legal, and professional standards.

Beyond operational challenges, these encounters can contribute to moral injury among EM clinicians. When immigration enforcement activities interfere with responsibilities related to patient privacy, informed consent, and medical decision‐making, clinicians may feel conflicted between their ethical obligations and requests from immigration enforcement officers. This is especially true when EM clinicians may feel they are unable to provide the same standard of care for all patients. Clear hospital and institutional policies, education, and readily available legal and ethics consultation can help with navigating these encounters. We hope that this discussion and guidance will be a useful resource for EM clinicians, as well as protective for all immigrants in healthcare spaces, including patients, providers, and staff. Hospitals should not by default agree to requests or demands by immigration enforcement officers that interfere with or impact medical care. In many cases, healthcare institutions have legal guidance that will protect decisions aligned with patient‐centered care.

Ultimately, EM clinicians are increasingly being asked to navigate the intersection of immigration enforcement and medical care. While clinicians cannot control immigration policy, we can uphold ethical and professional obligations to provide equitable, safe, and patient‐centered care regardless of immigration status. Hospitals and health systems should proactively develop institutional guidance that protects patient privacy, preserves clinical independence, and clarifies communications with immigration enforcement officers. Clinicians and health systems also must navigate prioritizing patient care while avoiding conflicts with immigration authorities that may lead them to be less likely to bring ailing immigrant detainees to seek healthcare in the future. When caring for immigrant patients, we can be immigration status‐agnostic to provide patients with the same standard of care as any other patient who walks into triage.

For clinicians who want to take the next step to prepare their institution to treat immigrants in immigration custody, question eight outlines practical considerations for developing policies that prepare hospitals and staff for navigating encounters. Such guidance should address hospital access, communication, care of patients, documentation practices, hospital‐designated interpreter services, and staff education.

Summary

As EM clinicians increasingly encounter patients in immigration custody, navigating patient encounters can be challenging, especially in the absence of clear hospital policy. Here, we answer clinical questions with relevant legal background to help provide guidance when encountering these likely scenarios (summarized in Table 2). These considerations are especially relevant to EM clinicians who are the first and potentially only clinicians to interact with immigrant detainees brought in by immigration enforcement.

No. Clinical decision‐making and disposition decisions remain the responsibility of the treating clinician. Immigration enforcement officers do not have the authority to dictate medical care, disposition, or override medical judgment.
