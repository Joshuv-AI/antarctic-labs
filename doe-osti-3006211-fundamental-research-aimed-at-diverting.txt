Abstract:
Photosystems are incredible biological machines that use sunlight to drive the conversion of carbon
dioxide to sugar. The amount of sunlight available for photosynthesis sometimes exceeds the
amount of energy plants can use. This excess energy has to be safely dissipated through non-
productive biological processes. The ultimate goal of this project is to understand whether we can
utilize that otherwise unused excess energy. In our previous work, we showed that, in principle, it
is possible to attach a catalyst to photosystem I and generate H2 using light. Our current strategy
is to genetically fuse parts of the photosystem I complex with a recently discovered oxygen-
tolerant [FeFe] hydrogenase. Our rationale is that such chimeric proteins may potentially result in
the natural incorporation of the photosystem I-hydrogenase link using the inherent genetic
machinery of the cell. In this project, we aim to verify that light-driven hydrogen production in
this construction is possible. Throughout the project, we designed nanoconstructs that showcase
the plausibility of this technology, at least in vitro. We take advantage of these constructs to
investigate details of the coupling between photosystem I and a H2-producing enzyme called
[FeFe] hydrogenase. This part of the project reveals details of the electron transfer between
photosystem I and the attached hydrogenase, providing information that can lead to new strategies
for improved biological photocatalysis. We also researched efficient and robust tethering of the
[FeFe] hydrogenase to photosystem I in cyanobacteria. This work will highlight successful design
strategies to guide the future development of photosynthetic biohybrids. Uncovering the principles
governing the utilization of otherwise unusable energy significantly further our understanding of
cyanobacterial photosynthesis. The work proposed establishes the feasibility of diverting excess
energy under high light conditions to orthogonal enzymatic pathways and set design rules for
efficient utilization of such a strategy for scientific and industrial applications in biosensing,
renewable energy, and high-value chemicals production. The work addresses the DOE-BES
Photosynthetic Systems program goal to develop a multidimensional understanding of
photosystems that would provide specific metrics that instruct strategies for improving biological
photosynthesis and for guiding the future development of bioreactors and biomimetic energy
systems.

Introduction:
Photosystems I (PSI) is an incredibly durable machine that, in cooperation with Photosystem II
(PSII), efficiently converts sunlight, CO2, and H2O into chemical bond energy. Our project stems
from the fact that photosynthesis saturates at an irradiation intensity below the solar maximum
available in the interval between several hours before and after the solar meridian. At high light
intensities (above 200 mol m-2s-1) downstream metabolic processes such a CO2 fixation cannot
keep up with the amount of NADPH and ATP generated by the photosystems. Non-photochemical
quenching mechanisms are present in the cell to prevent the evolution of harmful reactive oxygen
species (e.g. 1O2). As a result, the excess energy primarily converts to heat. In such organisms as
Chlorella, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Cr), and
Synechococcus sp. PCC 7002, photosynthesis saturates
at less than 500 mol photons m-2s-1, which is well
below the maximum available solar irradiation of about
2,000-2,500 mol photons m-2s-1. Is it possible to
utilize that unused energy to produce chemical bonds
instead of heat? We hypothesized that we could do just
that by wiring enzymatic systems directly to the
photosystem's electron-transport chain.
[FeFe] Hydrogenases (H2ases) can catalyze the
reduction of H+, thereby releasing H2. Because of their
extremely high turnover rates (>10,000 s-1), these
enzymes have the ability to direct a substantial amount Figure 1. Linking PSI with an O2-tolerant
 [FeFe]H2ase for H2 production using excess energy
of the excess energy generated by the photosystems into
H2 production. Consequently, a coupling of a [FeFe]H2ase to PSI is an attractive strategy for
bioengineering phototrophs (such as cyanobacteria) as cost-efficient and environmentally friendly
biohydrogen producers (see Figure 1). In the preliminary work, initiated by the John Golbeck,
former PI of this project, we sought to wire [FeFe]H2ase directly to the distal FB [4Fe-4S] cluster
of PSI, located in the PsaC stromal subunit. For this purpose, a surface-located Cys14 residue
coordinating the FB cluster of PsaC (S. 7002) and a surface-located Cys97 residue in the distal FeS
cluster of the [FeFe]H2ase from Clostridium acetobutylicum (CaHydA) were both changed to a
Gly residue, thereby opening binding sites for a thiol-terminated organic molecule (e.g. aliphatic
dithiols). We have illustrated that when cytochrome c6 (Cyt c6) is cross-linked with PSI, the
PSIPsaCC14G-1,8-octanedithio-CaHydA1C97G construct is able to produce H2 at a staggering rate of
2200±460 μmol of H2 per mg Chl−1 h−1 (an electron throughput of 105 ± 22 e− PSI−1s−1) which
exceeds the estimated electron flux from the O2-splitting PSII of 1640±100 μmol e− mg Chl−1 h−1
(85 e− RCPSII−1s−1). The results show a high efficiency of the coupling strategy, resulting in an
electron throughput well above the nominal electron flow generated by the photosystems.

● As a apart of the effort to find better electron accepting enzymes to be coupled to PS I, we have
isolated an [FeFe]H2ase from Clostridium beijerinkii (CbHydA1) that exhibits unexpected
tolerance to O2. The bulk of the characterization was performed on the recombinant CbHydA1
expressed in E. coli (Rosetta DE3 strain). We utilized a method of reconstituting the H-cluster with
a synthetic precursor [Fe2(-adt)(CO)4(CN)2]2− where adt = (SCH2)2NH (hereafter [2Fe]H), which
allows for higher protein yield as we omit maturation factor expression. Our IR spectroscopic
measurements showed that on air the enzyme enters an inactive state and converts back to the
active state when O2 is replaced with 1 atm H2 (see Figure 3). Our spectroelectrochemical
experiments revealed that the process of inactivation can be reproduced anaerobically by a simple
1e– oxidation. Subsequently, the enzyme can be
reactivated by a 1e– reduction. The electrochemical
experiments also reveal that the inactivation/reactivation
potential is less than 100 mV above the operating potential
of the H2ase, which attests to the efficiency of the
inactivation process at mildly oxidizing conditions. Based
on the results obtained we were able to narrow down the
mechanism of inactivation to two most likely possibilities.
In either of the proposed scenarios, facile oxidation of the
H-cluster triggers binding of an exogenous ligand to the
open coordination site (either hydroxide or a nearby Cys
side chain), thereby blocking the O2 binding. This work
was published in JACS in 2020.

● Based on the research above, we hypothesize that PSI will have to infrequently expend one
electron to reduce the H-cluster in the nanoconstruct to keep O2-tolerant [FeFe] hydrogenase
active. To test this idea, we researched the possibility of generating an active fusion protein that
would allow to natively couple H2ase to PSI through one of the PSI’s stromal subunits. For this
project, we opted for a PsaE-based strategy, taking inspiration from the early work by Ihara et al.
on coupling a [NiFe] H2ase to PSI. We have made significant progress in engineering a CbHydA1-
PsaE chimeric protein. We exposed the CbHydA1-PsaE fusion to carbon monoxide and verified
by FTIR that the enzyme is indeed in the EPR active Hox-CO state. Consequently, we performed a
back-to-back EPR investigation of the Hox-CO spectra in the fusion and the wild-type enzymes.
There was a clear difference in the spectral composition of the EPR spectra obtained despite
running the experiments side-by-side under identical conditions (see Figure 6A). While

CbHydA1WT showed a 2:1 ratio between two isoforms, the CbHydA1-PsaE demonstrated a 9:1
ratio, highlighting a dramatic shift in the preference for isoform 1. We then hypothesized that this

We also performed a detailed characterization of the PSIΔPsaE:CbHydA1-PsaE nanoconstruct by
time-resolved optical spectroscopy. The time-resolved absorption at 830 nm reports on the
reduction of P700•+ after a 7-ns, single-turnover actinic laser flash at 532 nm. P700•+ reduction can
occur from either a reduced acceptor in the electron transfer chain (termed charge recombination),
or an exogenous electron donor (ascorbate, DCPIP, Cyt c6, etc.). The study demonstrates a
noticeable change in the charge recombination kinetics without PsaE. PSIΔPsaE variant exhibited a
three-fold increased lifetime of the P700•+-[FA/FB]− charge recombination state. Also, we observed
the appearance of a faster P700•+-reduction phase, indicative of charge recombination from an
upstream cofactor. The latter observation indicates that PsaE may act as a stabilizing agent, and FA
or FB may have been more prone to degradation in the PSIΔPsaE variant than in PSIWT. The addition
of CbHydA1-PsaE fusion results in different P700•+-reduction kinetics (PSIΔPsaE:CbHydA1-PsaE
nanoconstruct), which vary based on the sample conditions. Building a nanoconstruct with a holo-
CbHydA1-PsaE under an inert atmosphere (ultra-pure Helium gas) results in a mix of PSIWT-like
kinetics and a long-lived P700•+ phase (>1s). The latter indicates a forward electron transfer from
[FA/FB]− to the H2ase domain, so the reduction of P700•+ is only possible by an external electron

donor (ascorbate). Intriguingly, when we use 3%H2/97%N2 atmosphere for the experiments on
PSIΔPsaE:holo-CbHydA1-PsaE, the kinetics of P700•+ reduction becomes much more complex,
indicating the existence of multiple charge recombination pathways. Most surprisingly, we did not
observe a long-lived P700•+ phase that would indicate a loss of electrons to H2 evolution. We
attribute such complex behavior to a "backfire" mechanism, in which H2ase uptakes H2 and can
act as an electron donor to the FA/FB pair. This experiment indicates that the chosen construct can
reasonably efficiently donate electrons from the H2ase domain back to the PSI electron-transfer
pathway. The work was published in PNAS in 2024.

● Partially supported by this funding was the research into alternative [FeFe] hydrogenases that
can operate under oxidizing conditions, with the goal of finding better candidates for PSI-H2ase
coupling. The major thrust of this research was the investigation of an H2-dependent H2O2
reductase that contains a highly unusual combination of metallocofactors, including rubredoxin,
rubrerythin, F-cluster, and H-cluster binding domains. While the enzyme did not exhibit traits
characteristic of O2 tolerance, this system demonstrated exceptional stability in the presence of
high concentrations of hydrogen peroxide. Unfortunately, the near-complete bias of this enzyme
towards hydrogen oxidation made it incompatible with being an electron acceptor for the purposes
of this research. Nonetheless, this system demonstrated the high tunability of the [FeFe] H2ase
domains. Furthermore, the mere presence of such a system suggests the existence of a novel, yet
to be explored, oxidative stress response pathway in bacteria that utilizes hydrogen (via
hydrogenase) as a source of reducing equivalents for the reduction of H2O2. This work was
performed in collaboration with Dr. Paul W. King and published in JACS in 2025 acknowledging
a partial support of the work by this funding.

Summary: 1–14
The project demonstrates the plausibility of modifying PSI to afford a diversion of photosynthetic
energy to non-native electron-accepting pathways. The project has been instrumental in
uncovering viable [FeFe] H2ases as building blocks of a robust hybrid photosynthetic H2-
producing. Using the knowledge we acquired over the span of this project, we were able to develop
a functional framework that can act as a test bed for future optimization and generation of viable
biological systems as well as to further investigate the electron transfer processes of PSI
complexes. All aims addressed key fundamental unknowns associated with coupling enzymes to
PSI in cyanobacteria. With this project, we gained broader understanding of the ability of PSI to
divert energy that would be lost to NPQ to an orthogonal metabolic pathway. Therefore, our
research provides a steppingstone that opens doors for research into generating functional cell,
which will need to consider a multitude of bioengineering factors (outside the scope of this
project).
