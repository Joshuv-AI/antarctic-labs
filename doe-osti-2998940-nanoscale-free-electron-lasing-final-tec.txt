Executive Summary
Optical control of electron dynamics and energy structure can be leveraged for tailoring classical
and quantum light sources. Previous work was primarily limited to Transmission Electron
Microscopes (TEMs). Our work under this program sought to enable full time and energy control
of electrons in SEMs for exploring the physics of attosecond electron dynamics and light
generation at the nanoscale. The motivation behind this work was to understand these
fundamental processes important to free-electron lasing at the nanoscale using sub-relativistic
electrons.

Major Goals
The overarching goal of this program was to study super-radiant optical emission resulting from
the interaction of sub-relativistic free electrons (1-30 keV) and nanostructured surfaces. These
super-radiant processes are akin to free-electron lasing, but occur within nanoscale regions of
space and without the need for magnetic undulators. Our aim was to provide insights that
subsequent researchers and engineers could use to develop highly tunable, compact, and
efficient laser-like free-electron sources of light.

The efforts to understand seeded and self-amplified super-radiance require the ability to send
trains of bunched electrons by nanostructured gratings with nanometer-scale alignment
precision. To do this, our primary task was the development of: (1) a free-electron testbed
system; and (2) modeling tools for simulating super-radiant emission dynamics resulting from
sub-relativistic electrons interacting with nanoscale structures.

Experimental Tasks (MIT)
 ●​ Develop novel instrumentation and tools for studying the sub-optical-cycle interaction
 dynamics between passing free electrons, nanostructures, and generated photons
 ○​ Optically driven electron energy modulators and demodulators for precise
 sub-optical-cycle (sub-femtosecond) pre-bunching of electrons
 ○​ Electron spectrometers with sufficient resolution (sub eV) to retrieve electron
 spectra for reconstructing the passing electron bunching dynamics with
 sub-femtosecond resolution
 ○​ Nanostructured interaction structures such as 100-nm pitch gratings for
 generating and out-coupling light from super-radiant electron-photon interaction
 processes
 ●​ Understand super-radiance in the seeded regime by exciting nanostructured surfaces
 such as nanoscale grating structures with pre-bunched electrons having varying
 bunching factors to induce and examine the underlying physical mechanisms of
 super-radiance at the nanoscale. We will seek to understand

 ○​ the distributed feedback processes between the passing electron and generated
 photons that lead to further electron bunching and enhanced, super-radiant
 optical emission
 ○​ how the electron bunching changes in time and space as the electrons pass over
 the nanostructured surfaces
 ●​ Leverage our newly obtained understanding of these physical mechanisms to inform
 nanostructured grating designs and electron injection schemes that more efficiently
 obtain super-radiant optical emission from nanostructured surfaces and improve the
 brightness and wavelength tunability of the outgoing radiation
 ●​ Determine beam parameters needed to excite self-amplified super-radiance where there
 is no pre-bunching of the electrons.
 ●​ Experimental demonstration of super-radiance in the self-amplified regime whereby the
 electrons are not pre-bunched before interaction
 ●​ Development of a suite of analytical and numerical modeling tools aimed at
 understanding super-radiance in the seeded and self-amplified regimes.

Modeling Tasks (University of California, Davis)
The primary responsibility of the team at the University of California, Davis for this project
involved modeling. The modeling effort was critical in supporting the experimental investigations
supported by this program and in unraveling the complete ultrafast dynamical processes
underlying nanoscale, free-electron lasing. This modeling effort could be divided into two main
tasks:

 Task M1: Seeded Super-Radiance Parameter Space
 In this task, we planned to use semi-analytical and numerical models to comprehensively
 examine the parameter space associated with seeded, super-radiant Smith-Purcell
 emission. Specifically, our modeling sought to determine the expected radiation emission
 from pre-bunched electron beams of differing bunch shape, duration, and energy content.
 These models were intended to use measurements made in our seeded super-radiance
 experiments and provide predictions and guidance for our efforts in the spontaneous
 super-radiance experiments.

 Task M2: Super-Radiance Dynamics and Threshold
 In this task, we aimed to explore the transition from seeded, super-radiant Smith-Purcell to
 spontaneous, self-amplified super-radiant emission. In this task, we planned to extend our
 semi-analytical model to account for the complete time dynamics of picosecond-duration
 electron bunches, and we sought to explore the conditions required to transition into the
 self-amplified regime. Similar to Task M1, we planned to additionally incorporate fully
 numerical, particle-in-cell simulations to support our reductive semi-analytical models.
 Additionally, in this task, we intended to also explore fully analytical approaches to
 understanding the super-radiant transition and reducing the complex, ultrafast dynamics into
 a simple set of parameters to guide future experiments in this regime.

Accomplishments
Experimental Effort – MIT

The scope of this two-year project was primarily focused on instrumentation and infrastructure
development with a final goal of performing initial experiments in testing superradiance in our
custom system. In parallel with this instrumentation effort, the team at UC Davis developed
modeling tools that are critical for guiding our infrastructure development and will be essential to
interpret and understand our experimental results.

This program was essential in establishing a novel free-electron testbed within an SEM capable
of sub-eV spectroscopic resolution, as well as femtosecond optical triggering and device
interaction. While the shortened time frame and delays in procuring certain elements of the
apparatus did not allow us to get as far as we had hoped in the study of super-radiance, we will
be able to leverage the infrastructure developed in this program for follow-on studies of free
electrons with light and matter.

Simulation Effort – University of California, Davis
As noted above, in our two-year program, we had two major modeling tasks: First, we aimed to
use semi-analytical and numerical models to comprehensively examine the parameter space
associated with super-radiant Smith-Purcell emission. Specifically, we sought to determine the
expected radiation emission from electron beams of differing properties and from materials with
different geometries. Second, we aimed to study the dynamics of super-radiance and the
threshold associated with this phenomenon. Over the course of our initial program, we made
significant progress on these two goals and uncovered other interesting areas of exploration, as
we will elaborate on below.

In our studies of the parameter space associated with super-radiant Smith-Purcell, we
developed extensive frequency- and time-domain simulation tools. In the frequency-domain, we
built a numerical tool capable of calculating the output radiation power and spectral content
generated from a single electron passing over a periodic structure. The structure can have
arbitrary geometry and can be composed of an arbitrary material. We validated this tool
quantitatively by comparison to theoretical predictions for Cerenkov radiation and qualitatively
by comparison to intuitive expressions for Smith-Purcell radiation from metallic gratings.
Furthermore, we extended this frequency domain tool to study inverse Smith-Purcell radiation,
or PINEM-like interactions. Specifically, we modified our numerical platform to calculate the
PINEM coupling factor, g, for arbitrary periodic structures composed of arbitrary geometries.
Beyond these frequency-domain models, we have also developed time-domain approaches to

studying Smith-Purcell radiation. In particular, we have developed a numerical, particle-in-cell
(PIC) solver to analyze the radiation produced from an electron traveling near a photonic
coupling structure. The PIC solver is ideal for studying super-radiant effects since it incorporates
the effect of the electron on the electromagnetic field as well as the effect of the electromagnetic
field back on the electron. Such back-action of the field on the electron is essential to
understand bunching dynamics.

Beyond studies of the Smith-Purcell radiation parameter space, we also explored the dynamics
of super-radiance and the super-radiance threshold. In this work, we have leaned on our
time-domain, PIC solver. This solver has enabled us to begin to explore how and when the
radiated field begins to shape the radiating electron's energy structure such that the radiation
process is affected. There is much work to do in this area, and the computational demands of
these numerical studies have led us to begin to develop analytical and semi-analytical models
as well. On the analytical side, we have been working on a model that can predict the
spontaneous emission from a classical current pulse of arbitrary shape traveling near a photonic
coupling structure. This model is a time-domain version of conventional, steady-state models
used in vacuum electronics, and this model can help us validate and understand the basic
predictions of our PIC solver. Furthermore, we have also made significant progress in building a
coupled system of governing equations for the basic super-radiant Smith-Purcell process.
These equations take a form similar to the coupled pendulum equations from free-electron laser
theory, with small modifications. We have been exploring numerical solutions to this analytical
system of equations and comparing our results to the predictions from our PIC model. Overall,
there have been exciting modeling developments, but there is much work still to be done.

Impact

Training and professional development provided
This program partially supported two postdoctoral associates and three graduate student RAs.
The students and postdocs gained hands-on experience designing, building, and implementing
free-electron tools in combination with ultrafast optical sources. Additionally, the students and
postdocs developed their understanding of the theoretical underpinnings of free-electron-photon
interactions as well as enhanced their skills with numerical electromagnetics.

The cross-disciplinary nature of the project in particular provided the team members with a
unique opportunity to develop skills and interact with experts across different fields. This was
further enhanced by the collaboration with UC Davis under our sub-award. At MIT and Davis,
we held bi-weekly seminars and journal club meetings that offered the opportunity to research,
present, and learn about relevant sub-disciplines such as ultrafast optics, free-electron device
physics, and more recent research combining both areas that are highly relevant to this work.
The technical and hands-on skills provided by working on this program could easily translate to
projects outside of this program. Through conference presentations, manuscript development,

and reporting efforts, the students and postdocs contributing to this program also developed
important communication skills.

Impact on Principle Disciplines
Super-radiant optical emission processes from the interaction of electrons and
micro/nanofabricated gratings have been debated greatly in the literature. While there have
been efforts that have claimed to have observed it, these findings have been disputed. Only
recently have the tools emerged to understand the intricate physical details of this process
in-situ, as they entail interactions between light and micro/nanostructures that occur over very
short distances (nano- to micrometers), and over very short timescales (atto- to femtoseconds).

The tools we developed under this program take an important step toward the understanding of
sub-femtosecond electron bunching techniques (originally developed for photo-induced
near-field electron microscopy) to study: (1) the influence of sub-cycle electron bunching on the
super-radiant optical radiation from free electrons interacting with nanostructures; and (2) the
underlying dynamics critical to super-radiant emission, such as how the distributed feedback
mechanisms reshape the passing electrons leading to greater photon emission. We anticipate
that with further follow-on effort, we will be able to leverage the resources developed through
this program to excite and study these interactions.

Impact on Other Disciplines
Free-electron interactions with nanostructures are now used in a variety of applications,
including electron microscopy and correlated electron-optical spectroscopy (like electron energy
loss spectroscopy). Such tools have been instrumental in improving our understanding of the
optical and electronic properties of nanoscale systems, and could be used to provide ever
higher spatial and temporal resolution in such applications.

By building on the tools we have developed and using them to understand super-radiant
interactions and low-energy free-electron lasing, for instance, engineers would be able to design
highly tunable, compact optical sources capable of spanning a myriad wavelength ranges, in
particular those that are difficult to access with conventional techniques such as the
mid-infrared. Currently, such sources are highly effective in the mm-wave spectral region (for
example using traveling wave-tube devices) and are favored for their high-power output and
tunability. Our work would enable the extension of such techniques, and thus their advantages,
to provide alternative sources in the optical domain.

Infrastructure Development
This modified SEM unit serves as infrastructure for our lab, and potentially for others at MIT to
use through collaboration. We are continuing experiments and plan further publications that

leverage the instrumentation developed under this program. Through this ongoing open
scientific effort, others will be able to learn how to incorporate the infrastructure approaches we
have developed into their work leading to follow-on research even outside of our group, being a
benefit to the entire community.

Aside from this experimental infrastructure, we are also continuing the development of custom
simulation software for full three-dimensional modeling of super-radiant free-electron-driven
radiation from nanostructured elements. Through follow-on publications and presentations, we
will detail these efforts contributing to the wider simulation and software infrastructure for
applications beyond basic research.

Technology Transfer
Our work is the first to apply approaches developed for attosecond free-elctron science to the
study of super-radiant smith purcell radiation. We are in the initial stages of patenting the
electron spectrometer approach we have developed, and will continue to pursue publication of
work building off of the infrastructure developed in this program. We are confident these
ongoing efforts will lead to further technology development and transfer to teams in industry and
academia wanting to leverage SEM tools for free-electron spectroscopy and excitation of novel
interactions between electrons and light mediated by nanostructures.

Societal Impact

We have developed a variety of tools that can be leveraged for follow-on research to understand
ultrafast interactions between light and electrons mediated by nanostructures. This could lead
to follow-on studies that inform the development of novel optical sources that are compact and
highly tunable. These sources could have a widespread impact on society in the form of new
technological capabilities. For instance, as improved optical sources for optical ranging (e.g. for
self-driving vehicles), chemical detection and remote sensing (e.g. for environmental polutants),
or myriad other areas where optical sources have already had profound impacts on society.
