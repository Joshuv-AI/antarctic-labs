The Pareto Frontier of Randomized Learning-Augmented Online Bidding
Mathis Degryse, Imrane Zaakour, Christoph Dürr, Spyros Angelopoulos

Abstract
Online bidding is a classical problem in online decision-making, with applications in resource allocation, hierarchical clustering, and the analysis of approximation algorithms. We study its randomized learning-augmented variant, where an online algorithm generates a sequence of random bids while leveraging predictions from an oracle. We provide analytical upper and lower bounds on the optimal consistency C C as a function of the robustness R R , which match when R ≥ 2.885 R\geq 2.885 , effectively closing the gap left by previous work. The key technical ingredient is the notion of a bidding function , a novel abstraction that provides a unified framework for the design and analysis of randomized bidding strategies. We complement our theoretical results with an experimental application of randomized bidding to the incremental median problem, demonstrating the applicability of our algorithm in practical clustering settings.

Introduction

In several sequential decision-making settings, the algorithm relies on increasing a quantity until it reaches a level that guarantees success, without knowing that threshold in advance. For instance, a scheduler may gradually increase the runtime allocated to a job until it finishes, or an algorithm may successively guess larger values of an unknown optimum until feasibility is reached. In such settings, all unsuccessful choices contribute to the total cost, hence the performance of the algorithm depends on how quickly it reaches the threshold without excessive overshooting.

Online bidding

[
20
]
provides a simple abstraction of this fundamental tradeoff. In this problem, a player submits an increasing sequence of
bids
until one of them reaches or exceeds an unknown threshold
u
, and pays the sum of all bids made up to that point. The objective is to design a bidding strategy whose total cost is as small as possible relative to the unknown threshold. Formally, in the deterministic setting, the player produces an increasing sequence

X=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and given a threshold

\operatorname{cost}(X,u)=\sum_{j\leq i}x_{j}\text{ for $i$ such that }x_{i-1}<u\leq x_{i}.

The

\operatorname{nc}(X,u)=\operatorname{cost}(X,u)/u

Under the standard framework of

We consider a natural oracle which provides the algorithm with a prediction

\operatorname{cons}(X)=\operatorname{nc}(X,\hat{u}),\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{rob}(X)=\sup_{u}\operatorname{nc}(X,u).

A natural goal is to design algorithms that achieve the optimal tradeoff between consistency and robustness; in the learning-augmented literature, such algorithms are often called

Preliminaries

We say that a bidding algorithm is
R
-robust and
C
-consistent if its robustness is at most
R
and its consistency at most
C
. In our analysis, we will typically minimize the consistency, given a robustness requirement
R
. Note that
R
must be at least the optimal randomized competitive ratio, namely at least
e
.

Note that the normalized cost of a sequence is invariant under multiplication of the bids, a property that follows from the fact that the bidding sequences are bi-infinite. Formally, we have
\operatorname{nc}(\delta X,\delta u)=\operatorname{nc}(X,u)
for any
\delta>0
, where
\delta X:=(\delta x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
. This implies that for any bidding sequence
X^{\prime}
, we can find a scaled sequence
X
such that

\operatorname{cons}(X)=\inf_{u}\operatorname{nc}(X,u)\ \text{ and }\ \operatorname{rob}(X)=\sup_{u}\operatorname{nc}(X,u),

(2)

which provides a convenient expression that we will be using in the remainder of this work.

Given
R\geq e
, we define

\underline{w}(R):=-\frac{1}{W_{-1}(-1/R)},\quad\overline{w}(R):=-\frac{1}{W_{0}(-1/R)},

(3)

where
W_{0}
and
W_{-1}
denote the principal and lower real branches of the Lambert function. We will use the shorthanded versions
\overline{w},\underline{w}
when
R
is clear from context.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
3
, we introduce the framework of bidding functions, establish its equivalence with bidding sequences, and illustrate the analysis of several classes of algorithms. Section
4
presents and analyzes our main algorithm, while in Section
5
we prove the corresponding lower bounds using a linear programming approach. Section
6
concludes with a numerical comparison of algorithms that incorporates prediction error.
Finally, in Section
7
, we present an experimental application of randomized online bidding to the incremental median problem.

Bidding Functions : a Framework for Bidding Sequences

In this section, we introduce the concept of a
bidding function
that is instrumental in our analysis of the problem. Let
B:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a nondecreasing function such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}B(t)=0,\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}B(t)=+\infty
and for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
\int_{-\infty}^{t}B(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
is finite.
We call the domain of
B
the set of
reference points
.
We define the randomized bidding sequence
induced by

B
as
X_{B}\mathrel{:=}(B(i+\lambda))_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
where
\lambda
is chosen uniformly at random in
\left[0,1\right]
.

In order to express the normalized cost of
X
as a function of
B
, we introduce the concept of
normalized mass

\tilde{B}
, defined by

\tilde{B}(t)\mathrel{:=}\int_{-\infty}^{t+1}\frac{B(x)}{B(t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

In addition, we define the
work function

w_{B}
, which will be useful in the analysis of our algorithms. This function represents the expected sum over all bids up to the threshold
B(t)
, normalized by this threshold.

w_{B}(t)\mathrel{:=}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{B(x)}{B(t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

When
B
is clear from the context, we will sometimes use the shortcut
w_{t}:=w_{B}(t)
.
To be able to relate
\tilde{B}
to
\operatorname{nc}(X_{B})
, we define the
generalized inverse
of
B
. Formally
B^{-}(u)
is the infimum over all
t
such that
B(t)\geq u
, see
[
24
]
.

With the above definitions established, we can prove a strong connection between
\operatorname{nc}(X_{B},u)
and
\tilde{B}
.

Theorem 1
.

For every
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
we have

\displaystyle\inf_{u}\operatorname{nc}(X_{B},u)=\inf_{t}\tilde{B}(t),\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{u}\operatorname{nc}(X_{B},u)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\tilde{B}(t).

theorem

1
allows us to use bidding functions explicitly in the analysis. Hence, we can restate the consistency and robustness expressions (
2
) in terms of
\tilde{B}(t)
as

\displaystyle\operatorname{cons}(B)\mathrel{:=}\inf_{t}\tilde{B}(t),

\displaystyle\operatorname{rob}(B)\mathrel{:=}\sup_{t}\tilde{B}(t).

(4)

As an example, the exponential bidding function
B(t)=e^{t}
induces the sequence
(e^{i+\lambda})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
where
\lambda\sim U[0,1]
which has optimal robustness
e
as shown in
[
20
]
. As a different example, the randomized algorithm of
[
5
]
is induced by the exponential of a piecewise linear function, as we will discuss shortly. As a last example, a piecewise constant function such as
B(t)=2^{\lfloor t\rfloor}
induces the doubling sequence that has optimal deterministic robustness equal to 4
[
13
]
. These examples illustrate that bidding functions capture a broad spectrum of classical bidding strategies, and in fact this connection is complete:
every
randomized bidding sequence is induced by some bidding function.

Theorem 2
.

For every randomized sequence
X
, there is a bidding function
B
such that the consistency (respectively, robustness) of
X
is upper bounded by the consistency (respectively, robustness) of
B
.

In Sections
4
and
5
, we leverage

to obtain near-tight consistency–robustness tradeoffs. While these results require more intricate bidding functions, several interesting structural insights already arise from considering relatively simple ones. These examples help illustrate the main ideas and provide intuition for the more general techniques developed later. In particular, we consider the following classes of bidding functions:

\bullet
Class
\cal E
is comprised of all exponential bidding functions. This class contains, for instance, the
e
-robust bidding function
B(t)=e^{t}
of
[
20
]
. This warm-up class induces sequences of constant normalized cost, and reveals a connection between
R
-robustness and the expressions
\overline{w},\underline{w}
, as defined in Section

\bullet
Class
\cal D
contains all bidding functions
B
that are exponential within each half-open interval of the form
[i,i+1)
for

, and satisfy, in addition, that

is constant. This class captures the bidding sequences studied in
[
5
]
. Our analysis allows us to obtain exact, closed-form expressions of the consistence–robustness tradeoffs, which was left open in
[

]
.

\bullet
Class
\cal I
contains all

. We also prove that the tradeoff improvement becomes quantifiably more pronounced as

We refer to the Appendix
A.1
for the detailed discussion, with statements and proofs of results. We note that the class
\cal D
consists of functions that are

\cal I
is comprised by linear

. Together, these classes illustrate that achieving Pareto-optimality requires bidding functions with genuinely nontrivial structure.

Application to the Incremental Medians Problem

In this section, we conduct an experimental study of the application of randomized bidding to the incremental median problem, a classical online clustering problem. Previous work on this problem has been purely theoretical; we provide the first experimental results, in the context of a learning-augmented setting.

Problem statement
Incremental medians is closely related to the well-studied

with distance function
d
. In its simplest variant, the goal is to choose a set
F\subseteq V
of
k

facilities
. Each point
v\in V
is assigned to its closest facility, which incurs cost defined as

\operatorname{cost}(F)\mathrel{:=}\sum_{v\in V}\min_{f\in F}d(v,f).

The quality of a solution is measured by the
approximation ratio
, defined as
\operatorname{cost}(F)/\operatorname{cost}(F_{k})
, where
F_{k}\subseteq V
denotes an optimal set of
k
facilities minimizing the cost. For a randomized algorithm producing a random set
F
, we consider the expected value of
\operatorname{cost}(F)
divided by the
\operatorname{cost}(F_{k}^{*})
.

The
incremental median problem
introduces an additional constraint: the algorithm does not know in advance how many facilities it will be allowed to choose, and must therefore construct the solution sequentially, without the possibility of replacing previously selected facilities. Formally, a solution to the incremental median problem is a total order on
V
. We denote by
F_{k}
the first
k
facilities in that order, and by
\operatorname{cost}(F_{k})/\operatorname{cost}(F_{k}^{*})
the approximation ratio of the algorithm for a specific value of
k
.

Algorithms
Incremental medians was introduced under the name
online median problem
in
[
34
]
, and its connection to the online bidding problem was studied in
[
20
]
. It was shown therein that, given a
\beta
-approximation algorithm for the
k
-median problem and a
\gamma
-competitive algorithm for the online bidding problem, one can obtain a
2\beta\gamma
-approximation algorithm for the incremental median problem. In Appendix
E
we extend this connection to the learning-augmented setting, with a prediction
\hat{k}
on the allowable number of facilities to choose. We show that any
R
-robust and
C
-consistent bidding algorithm yields an algorithm for incremental median that is
2\beta C
-consistent, and
2\beta R
-robust. We also address technical details related to the implementation.

Datasets
For our experimental setup, we use the road network of the Isle of Man from the
OpenStreetMap
project
[
36
]
. Restricting to its largest connected component yields a graph on a vertex set
V
of size 5340, where vertices correspond to geographic locations on the island. In this setting, facilities may represent, for example, mailboxes or electric charging stations, while the cost reflects the quality of service provided across the island. Edges correspond to road segments, and their weights represent traversal times. For the purpose of this study, we make all edges bidirectional by assigning to each undirected edge the minimum arc weight among both orientations. This connected edge-weighted graph induces a metric on
V
, where distances are defined by shortest-path lengths.

8
Conclusion

We presented algorithms that provably achieve a near-optimal consistency–robustness tradeoff for randomized learning-augmented bidding, effectively closing the gap in previous work. A very small gap for
R\leq 2/\ln 2
remains, which we believe can be further narrowed by allowing more resources to the
computational lower bound. Central to our analysis is a novel function-based framework that maps bidding sequences to bidding functions and enables both the positive and impossibility results.
Beyond online bidding, this methodology extends naturally to closely related problems such as searching for a hidden target on the infinite line
[
13
]
, as shown in
[
5
]
. We also aim to extend our approach to more challenging sequencing problems such as
contract scheduling

[
38
]
, where the optimal randomized competitive ratio remains unknown even in the classical setting without predictions, making it a natural candidate for function-based analysis techniques.

A.1
Analysis of Bidding Function Classes
\cal E
,
\cal D
,
\cal I

A.1.1
Class
\cal E

An exponential bidding function
B
is defined by
B(t)=e^{t/w}
, for some parameter
w>0
and for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The normalized cost of a linear function is invariant with respect to the reference point. In particular, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\tilde{B}(t)=\tilde{B}(0)=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{1}e^{x/w}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{e^{0}}=we^{1/w}.

It is interesting to know for which values of
w
the bidding function is
R
-robust. For this purpose, we observe that the ratio
we^{1/w}
is convex in
w
, which holds since its second derivative
e^{1/w}w^{-3}
is positive for
w>0
. In addition its limit at
0
from above is
\infty
, as is its limit at
\infty
. As a result, we have
we^{1/w}\leq R
, for all
w\in\left[\underline{w},\overline{w}\right]
where
\underline{w},\overline{w}
are the two solutions of the equation
we^{1/w}=R
.
Note that for the extreme case
R=e
, we have
\underline{w}=\overline{w}=1
.

A.1.2
Class
\cal D

Bidding functions in class
\cal D
are defined by two values
\ell,h\geq 0
with
\ell+h>0
, and have the form

B_{\ell,h}(t)=\exp\left(\lfloor t\rfloor\cdot(\ell+h)+\left[t\right]\cdot\ell\right),

where
\left[t\right]\mathrel{:=}t-\lfloor t\rfloor
denotes the fractional part of
t
. When it is clear from context, we will omit the subscripts
\ell,h
in the notation. We note that this class induces sequences equivalent to those studied in
[
5
]
. In this section we give analytical results about the robustness–consistency tradeoff of these functions.

Note that the parameter
\ell
allows to tune the amount of randomness in the sequence. In particular, of
\ell=0
, then the resulting sequence is deterministic.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\ell,h\geq 0
such that
\ell+h>0
. We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{rob}(B_{\ell,h})

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 1^{-}}\tilde{B}_{\ell,h}(t)=\frac{(e^{\ell}-1)}{\ell}\frac{e^{\ell+h}}{(e^{\ell+h}-1)}e^{h}

\displaystyle\operatorname{cons}(B_{\ell,h})

\displaystyle=\tilde{B}_{\ell,h}(0)=\frac{(e^{\ell}-1)}{\ell}\frac{e^{\ell+h}}{(e^{\ell+h}-1)}.

Proof.

Since bidding functions of class
\cal D
satisfy that
B(t+1)/B(t)
is constant, the normalized mass is
1
-periodic. For this reason, we can restrict the analysis to the interval
[0,1)
. We first compute
w_{B}(0)
.

By periodicity of the normalized mass,
w_{B}(0)=w_{B}(1)
, furthermore, if
\ell>0
, then
w_{B}(1)=\frac{w_{B}(0)B(0)+\frac{e^{\ell}-1}{\ell}}{B(1)}
. Thus we have
w_{B}(0)=\frac{e^{\ell}-1}{\ell(e^{\ell+h}-1)}
, which for convenience we denote
w
.

Next we compute

\displaystyle\tilde{B}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{wB(1)+\int_{1}^{t+1}B(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{B(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{we^{\ell+h}+e^{\ell+h}(\frac{e^{t\ell}-1}{\ell})}{e^{t\ell}}

\displaystyle=e^{\ell+h}\left(\frac{1}{\ell}+e^{-t\ell}\left(w-\frac{1}{\ell}\right)\right).

Since
\ell+h>\ell
, we have
w<1/\ell
so this function is increasing in
[0,1)
. We finally obtain the equalities

\operatorname{rob}(B)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 1^{-}}\tilde{B}(t)=e^{h}(w+\frac{e^{\ell}-1}{\ell})=we^{\ell+2h},\quad\operatorname{cons}(B)=we^{\ell+h}

.

We note that the limits for
\ell\rightarrow 0
of these expressions give
\operatorname{rob}(B)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 1^{-}}\tilde{B}(t)=\frac{e^{2h}}{e^{h}-1}
and
C=\frac{e^{h}}{e^{h}-1}
, as in the deterministic case.
∎

The obtained formulae for the robustness and consistency of bidding functions from class
\cal D
make it possible to analyse the tradeoff they induce.

Theorem 14
.

Let
1<C\leq e
. Let
R\mathrel{=}\min_{\ell,h}\left\{\operatorname{rob}(B_{\ell,h})|\operatorname{cons}(B_{\ell,h})\leq C\right\}
be the optimal robustness among
C
-consistent bidding functions from class
D
.

•

If
1<C\leq\frac{3}{2}
, then
R=\frac{C^{2}}{C-1}
, which is achieved by a deterministic bidding sequence.

•

If
\frac{3}{2}\leq C\leq e
, then
R=\frac{\ell C^{2}e^{-\ell}}{\ell C+1-e^{\ell}}
for
\ell
being the unique non negative root of

1-C\ell^{2}-\ell-e^{\ell}(1-2\ell).

Proof.

From the proof of
lemma

13
we have
R=we^{\ell+2h}
and
C=we^{\ell+h}
for
w=\frac{e^{\ell}-1}{\ell(e^{\ell+h}-1)}
. First we express
R
in terms of
C
and
\ell
only. We have

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\ell}-1}{\ell}\frac{e^{\ell+h}}{e^{\ell+h}-1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle Ce^{\ell+h}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\ell}-1}{\ell}e^{\ell+h}+C

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle e^{h}\left(e^{\ell}C-\frac{e^{\ell}-1}{\ell}e^{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=C

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle e^{h}(\ell C-(e^{\ell}-1))

\displaystyle=\ell Ce^{-\ell}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle e^{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell Ce^{-\ell}}{\ell C+1-e^{\ell}}.

Hence
R=Ce^{h}=\frac{\ell C^{2}e^{-\ell}}{\ell C+1-e^{\ell}}
. For fixed
C
, we want to find the value of
\ell
which minimizes
R
. We have

\partial_{\ell}R=\frac{C^{2}e^{-\ell}\ell^{2}}{(C\ell-e^{\ell}+1)^{2}}\frac{1-C\ell^{2}-\ell-e^{\ell}(1-2\ell)}{\ell^{2}}.

For this product of two fractions, observe that the first fraction is always positive. Hence the sign of
\partial_{\ell}R
equals the sign of the second fraction, which we denote by
F(C,\ell)
. Using a series expansion, we obtain

F(C,\ell)=\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}\frac{3+2i}{(i+2)!}\ell^{i}-C.

We immediately obtain the following :

•

F
is strictly increasing along
\ell
.

•

F(C,0)\geq 0
if and only if
C\leq\frac{3}{2}
.

•

If
C>3/2
, then
F
is negative on
[0,\ell^{*})
and positive on
(\ell^{*},+\infty)
, where
\ell^{*}
is the unique root of
F
.

Hence we have that, if
C>3/2
, then the optimal robustness is reached for the unique
\ell
satisfying
F(C,\ell)=0
. Else, it is reached by the deterministic case
\ell=0
.
∎

A.1.3

\cal I

A bidding function

\cal I

(B(t))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
for fractional reference points

and B(

In this section we use an equivalent representation based on a bi-infinite sequence of exponential

, which allows us to express

B(t)=\exp\left([t]\cdot\ell_{\lfloor t\rfloor}+\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor t\rfloor-1}\ell_{i}&\text{if }t\geq 0\\
\displaystyle-\sum_{i=\lfloor t\rfloor}^{-1}\ell_{i}&\text{if }t<0\end{cases}\right).

The following theorem is the consequence of the lower bound in
lemma

18
and the matching upper bound in
lemma

19
.

Theorem 15

Let

R

\cal I
which minimizes its consistency. Let

(\ell_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be its associated sequence of slopes.

•

If
R\geq R_{0}
, then
\operatorname{cons}(B)=\underline{w}+1
.

•

If
R\leq R_{0}
, let
\ell^{*}
such that

\operatorname{cons}(B)=\underline{w}+\frac{e^{\ell^{*}}-1}{\ell^{*}}

For the analysis it will be important to bound the work

at every integer reference point

Lemma 16

If a linear continuous bidding function

is

-robust, then for every

. Moreover,
\ell_{i}\leq 1/\underline{w}
for every

Proof.

Let
(\ell_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence of slopes,
B
the associated bidding function and
(w_{B}(i))_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}

The above Lemma formalizes an intuitive idea: if the work is too high at some point, then the robustness requirement cannot be met in the immediate future. Conversely, if the work is too low, it means the sequence is not dense in the past, hence the robustness requirement must have been broken at some point.

Lemma 22
.

Let
a\geq 1
and
R\geq e
. The smallest root in modulus of
X^{a}-aR(X-1)
is its unique real root
\rho\in(1,1+1/a)
. Moreover,
\rho
is a root of multiplicity
1
.

Proof.

We first prove that
\rho
exists. Let
f(x)=x^{a}-aR(x-1)
, we have
f(1)=1
and

f(1+\frac{1}{a})=(1+\frac{1}{a})^{a}-R\leq e-R<0.

Hence
\rho
exists; we now show uniqueness of
\rho
. Indeed,
f^{\prime}(x)=a(x^{a-1}-R)
is negative on
[1,1+1/a]
. Since
f^{\prime}
is non-zero on this interval,
\rho
is of multiplicity
1
.

It remains to verify that there does not exist any root of smaller modulus. Suppose, by way of contradiction, that there exists a root
\eta=v\rho
with
|v|\leq 1
and
v\neq 1
. We have

v^{a}\rho^{a}=aR(v\rho-1).

Using that
\rho^{a}=aR(\rho-1)
, we obtain the following

\displaystyle v^{a}=\frac{v\rho-1}{\rho-1}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow v^{a}-1=\frac{\rho(v-1)}{\rho-1}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\frac{v^{a}-1}{v-1}=\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}.

The above can be rewritten as
\sum_{i=0}^{a-1}v^{i}=\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}
. Note that left hand side has modulus at most
a
by the triangular inequality
(|v^{i}|\leq 1,\forall i)
, and the right hand side has modulus at least

\rho\in[1,1+1/a]
