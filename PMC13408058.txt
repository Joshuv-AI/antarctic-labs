Serum albumin-enhanced screening: A triage strategy and predictive model for OGTT in community-managed patients with coronary heart disease
Authors: Chumin Ni, XianWu Zhao, Xueying Liu, Kaixun Zhao, Weidai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with coronary heart disease (CHD) are at high risk of undiagnosed diabetes. Reliance on standard fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) may lead to underdiagnosis, highlighting the need for optimized screening strategies.MethodsUsing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cycles 2007–2016, we analyzed 287 patients with CHD and 8,904 non-CHD controls without pre-existing diabetes. A two-tier protocol was implemented, consisting of initial FPG/HbA1c screening followed by OGTT. A prediction model was derived to identify individuals at high risk for OGTT-dependent diabetes.ResultsPatients with CHD had a 3.4-fold higher weighted prevalence of newly diagnosed diabetes than controls (22.1% [95% CI, 16.5%–28.8%] vs. 6.5% [95% CI, 5.8%–7.2%]). Initial FPG/HbA1c screening missed 32 of 64 diabetes cases (50.0%), which were detectable only by OGTT. The albumin-enhanced model (HbA1c + FPG + albumin) demonstrated robust discriminatory capacity (AUC 0.823) and provided significant incremental value over the HbA1c + FPG model, with an integrated discrimination improvement of 0.0611 and net reclassification improvement of 0.1562 (both P<0.05).ConclusionSerum albumin is an adjunctive biomarker that enhances prediction. Our model may provide an effective triage strategy for targeting OGTT in high-risk individuals, enhancing screening efficiency.

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major risk factor for coronary heart disease (CHD) and is independently associated with adverse clinical outcomes.1–3 Effective diabetes management is essential for reducing coronary events and improving long-term prognosis.4,5 Current guidelines recommend incorporating comprehensive metabolic profiling as part of standard CHD evaluations in cardiology care. 6

In the resource-limited community healthcare setting, a practical dilemma exists. While the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) is the most sensitive method for diabetes diagnosis, its operational complexity (requiring fasting, timed samples, and prolonged clinic visits) limits its widespread use for population screening.6,7 Consequently, reliance on the more convenient fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is common. However, this reliance may lead to significant underdiagnosis, particularly in high-risk groups like patients with CHD.8,9 Thus, a critical need is to identify patients with normal FPG/HbA1c results who are nonetheless at high risk for OGTT-dependent diabetes. The discovery of routine biomarkers for this task would enable feasible and cost-effective targeted OGTT screening in community practice.

This study aims to address these gaps by: (1) quantifying the proportion of diabetes missed by FPG/HbA1c and detectable only by OGTT in a community-managed CHD population; and (2) developing and validating a biomarker-based prediction model to optimize patient selection for confirmatory OGTT testing.

Method

Study design and data source

This retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed data from five consecutive cycles (2007–2016) of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which evaluates the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population. 10 All NHANES procedures were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. 11 We conducted an analytical simulation of a sequential screening strategy to better reflect real-world clinical workflows. The two-tier sequential screening strategy was implemented as follows: (1) First-tier screening: Measurement of FPG and HbA1c; (2) Second-tier confirmatory testing: OGTT for individuals who tested negative in the first tier.

Study population

From an initial pool of 50,588 NHANES participants, we applied the following exclusion criteria: (1) age under 20 years; (2) pregnancy; (3) prior diagnosis of diabetes; and (4) missing data for FPG, HbA1c, or OGTT. Finally, 287 participants with CHD and 8,904 non-CHD controls were included in the analysis.

Key variables and definitions

In accordance with the American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines and using available data from the NHANES database, diabetes was defined as meeting any of the following criteria 6 : (1) FPG ≥7.0 mmol/L; (2) 2-hour plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L during a standardized OGTT; (3) HbA1c ≥6.5%.

OGTT protocol

Eligible participants (fasting ≥9 hours and not taking diabetic medications) ingested a 75 g glucose solution; venous blood samples were drawn at 0 and 120 minutes (±15 minutes). To ensure the accuracy of the OGTT, individuals with hemophilia, those who declined phlebotomy, or those who failed to consume the entire glucose solution within the specified time frame were excluded from the study.

Coronary heart disease

In the NHANES questionnaire, trained health professionals conducted interviews. Participants were asked, “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have coronary heart disease?”. Thus, CHD status was identified based on self-reported medical history rather than adjudicated clinical records.

Covariates

Variables known to be risk factors for CHD and diabetes were included as covariates in the analyses to address potential confounding. These included demographic characteristics, history of hypertension, lipid profiles, smoking status, body mass index, waist circumference, arm circumference, alanine aminotransferase, serum albumin, creatinine, insulin resistance index (homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance [HOMA-IR], calculated as fasting insulin [μU/mL] × fasting glucose [mmol/L]/22.5), 12 daily physical activity, 13 and other relevant parameters.

Statistical analysis

To account for the complex survey design of NHANES and guarantee nationally representative estimate, appropriate sampling weights, stratification, and clustering variables were used in all descriptive statistics and prevalence calculations. Continuous variables were presented as survey-weighted means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Group comparisons were conducted using survey-weighted linear regression. For categorical variables, survey-weighted percentages with 95% CIs were reported, and group differences were assessed using survey-weighted Chi-square tests. Variable selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression for preliminary feature selection. Variables retained by LASSO were subsequently assessed for multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all variables meeting the criterion of VIF < 10, and were then entered into a multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify significant predictors for the final model.

For precise prediction, a prediction model incorporating selected variables was developed and internally validated using bootstrap resampling with 500 repetitions. Model performance was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC), calibration (calibration plots and Hosmer-Lemeshow test), and clinical utility (decision curve analysis). A nomogram was developed to convert the prediction model into a practical scoring system for individualized risk assessment. A Shiny-based interactive web application was developed to help integrate the dynamic nomogram into clinical practice. The incremental predictive value of serum albumin beyond the conventional model (FPG + HbA1c) was further evaluated using the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification improvement (NRI). IDI reflects improvement in overall discrimination, whereas NRI assesses whether the addition of serum albumin improves risk reclassification for OGTT-defined diabetes. All analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.2). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Supplementary analysis

To address the practical concern that FPG requires planned fasting assessment and may be difficult to operationalize in opportunistic community screening, we performed a supplementary analysis simulating a non-fasting screening pathway. Among the 287 CHD participants without previously diagnosed diabetes in the primary cohort, HbA1c alone was first used to identify overt diabetes at the initial screening stage. Participants not classified as diabetic by HbA1c then entered a second-tier analysis in which OGTT positivity was used as the reference outcome. Within this HbA1c-negative subgroup, the discriminatory performance of HbA1c alone, serum albumin alone, and a combined HbA1c+albumin model for identifying OGTT-positive cases was evaluated using ROC curves and the AUC. Exploratory triage cutoffs based on ROC analysis were established utilizing the Youden index. The performance of this simplified non-fasting strategy was then contrasted with that of the primary FPG+HbA1c+albumin model.

Results

Diabetes screening in CHD vs non-CHD groups

To mirror real-world clinical practice, a two-tier glycemic screening strategy was implemented in participants without a prior diabetes diagnosis. The diabetes detection rate was significantly higher in the CHD group than in the non-CHD group. Specifically, within the CHD cohort (n=287), initial screening with FPG and HbA1c identified 32 patients with diabetes (11.1%). Subsequent OGTT testing of the 255 initially screen-negative individuals revealed an additional 32 cases (12.5%), yielding a total prevalence of 22.3%. In contrast, among 8,904 non-CHD individuals, only 5.3% were positive in the first tier, with OGTT detecting an additional 3.6%. Consequently, in unweighted analyses, newly diagnosed diabetes was identified in 64 of 287 participants (22.3%) in the CHD group and 774 of 8,904 participants (8.7%) in the non-CHD group. After applying survey weights, the estimated prevalences were 22.1% (95% CI, 16.5%–28.8%) and 6.5% (95% CI, 5.8%–7.2%), respectively, representing a 3.4-fold higher prevalence in the CHD group. Diagnostic performance of different screening methods

Importantly, the initial FPG and HbA1c screening demonstrated limited diagnostic accuracy for diabetes in the CHD population, capturing only 32 out of 64 total diabetes cases (50.0%). This suggests that half of the newly diagnosed diabetes cases required OGTT confirmation.

Characteristics of OGTT-dependent diagnosis individuals

Among the 255 patients with CHD who underwent OGTT following a negative first-tier screen, 32 were diagnosed with diabetes exclusively detected by OGTT, while 223 tested negative. To optimize the targeted use of OGTT, we aimed to identify predictors of a positive OGTT result in this subgroup. Variable selection was first performed using LASSO regression (with lambda.min), which identified nine potential predictors: sex, race, FPG, HOMA-IR, HbA1c, HDL-C, ALT, albumin, and sufficient physical activity (Figure 2). Multicollinearity assessment confirmed no significant issues, as all selected variables had variance inflation factor (VIF) values below 10. These variables were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression analysis. After adjusting for confounders, three significant predictors were identified: FPG (OR = 5.11, 95% CI: 2.08-12.55), HbA1c (OR = 4.26, 95% CI: 1.16-15.64), and serum albumin (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.09-1.52).Figure 2.Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis for variable selection in the OGTT-dependent diabetes prediction model.Footnotes: (a) Cross-validation plot for optimal lambda selection. Serum albumin as a novel predictive biomarker

Higher serum albumin levels were positively associated with a positive OGTT result, establishing it as a novel predictive biomarker. The optimal diagnostic cutoff, determined by the Youden index, was 42.52 g/L. At this threshold, albumin provided an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.61–0.78), with a sensitivity of 78% and a specificity of 58%.

Development and validation of a prediction model

Based on the three identified predictors (FPG, HbA1c, and albumin), a clinical prediction model was developed. The model demonstrated excellent discrimination with an AUC of 0.823 (95% CI: 0.745-0.895), significantly surpassing the performance of individual predictors (Figure 3(a)). To validate model stability and assess potential overfitting, we conducted 1,000 bootstrap resampling iterations. The performance of the prediction model was rigorously evaluated. The calibration curves (Figure 4) showed good agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities, which was further confirmed by a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p=0.83). Internal validation with 500 bootstrap iterations revealed minimal optimism (0.015), indicating robust model performance without overfitting. Contribution of serum albumin to model performance

To assess the improvement gained by incorporating serum albumin, the performance of a model with conventional glycemic markers (FPG and HbA1c) was compared with that of the albumin-enhanced model. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) increased from 0.797 to 0.829. This improvement was further supported by net reclassification and integrated discrimination indices (categorical NRI=0.1562, p <0.05; p <0.05; IDI=0.0611, p <0.05). Supplementary analysis for an opportunistic non-fasting screening scenario

In a supplementary analysis designed to simulate opportunistic screening without FPG, HbA1c alone identified 15 of the 287 CHD participants as having diabetes at the first tier. Among the remaining 272 participants with HbA1c below the diagnostic threshold, 39 were OGTT-positive and 233 were OGTT-negative. For identifying OGTT-positive diabetes in this subgroup, HbA1c alone showed modest discrimination (AUC 0.687, 95% CI 0.585–0.789), albumin alone also retained predictive value (AUC 0.658, 95% CI 0.573–0.743), and the combined HbA1c+albumin model achieved the highest discrimination among the non-FPG approaches (AUC 0.728, 95% CI 0.628–0.829). Exploratory ROC-derived triage thresholds for the single markers were 5.95% for HbA1c and 42.5 g/L for albumin. At these thresholds, HbA1c yielded a sensitivity of 51.3% and a specificity of 84.1%, whereas albumin yielded a sensitivity of 71.8% and a specificity of 58.2%. These findings suggest that when fasting glucose cannot be feasibly implemented, the combination of HbA1c and albumin may still provide useful triage information for selective OGTT use, although its discrimination remained lower than that of the full FPG+HbA1c+albumin model in the primary analysis.

Discussion

The high prevalence of undiagnosed diabetes in patients with CHD represents a critical gap in preventive cardiology, particularly within resource-conscious community healthcare settings. Our study confirms a substantially higher burden of newly diagnosed diabetes in community-managed patients with CHD compared to the general population, with a detection rate 3.4 times greater. Crucially, 32 of 64 newly diagnosed diabetes cases (50.0%) were identifiable only by the OGTT, underscoring the limitation of relying solely on FPG and HbA1c for screening in this high-risk group. Consistent with this, prior studies have also demonstrated that reliance on FPG and HbA1c alone fails to diagnose a substantial proportion of diabetes cases in high-risk groups, such as individuals with obesity.7,8,14 These findings necessitate a paradigm shift towards more effective triage strategies for the utilization of the OGTT. In response, we developed and validated a serum albumin-enhanced prediction model to better identify patients with CHD who are most likely to benefit from confirmatory OGTT testing.

The novel role of serum albumin in predicting OGTT-dependent diabetes

The main finding of our study is that FPG, HbA1c, and serum albumin were identified as independent predictors. The most novel aspect is the identification of serum albumin as an independent predictor of OGTT-dependent diabetes in patients with CHD. This indicates serum albumin not merely as a traditional marker of nutrition, but as a potential indicator of a specific metabolic phenotype characterized by preserved fasting glucose homeostasis alongside compromised postprandial regulation. While lower serum albumin is a well-recognized prognostic marker in patients with diabetes and CHD, our finding of a positive association with OGTT positivity presents an apparent paradox, suggesting a more complex relationship in the early dysglycemic state.15,16 We propose several interconnected mechanisms that may explain this relationship. First, in individuals with preserved hepatic synthetic function, higher serum albumin may reflect adequate metabolic and nutritional reserve. This reserve could mitigate fasting hyperglycemia through efficient hepatic glucose uptake, thereby maintaining normal FPG and HbA1c levels, while mechanisms regulating postprandial insulin sensitivity or incretin response remain compromised, leading to OGTT-dependent diabetes.17,18 Second, as the primary carrier for free fatty acids, serum albumin may influence the fatty acids transport flux, which predominantly affect skeletal muscle glucose disposal after a meal, thus creating a dissociation between fasting and post-load glucose metrics.19–22

Complementary diagnostic characteristics and model utility

The clinical prediction model integrating FPG, HbA1c, and serum albumin demonstrated excellent discriminatory capacity (AUC 0.823), alongside good calibration and superior net clinical benefit. The model incorporates three biomarkers that serve complementary pathophysiological roles: FPG primarily indicates hepatic glucose production, HbA1c reflects chronic glycemic exposure, and albumin may serve as a marker of metabolic and inflammatory status.23,24 Notably, the inclusion of serum albumin provided a statistically and clinically meaningful improvement over the model based solely on FPG and HbA1c, as confirmed by the NRI, IDI, and DCA. This enhancement underscores the unique and additive predictive contribution of serum albumin. To address the disparity between model development and its application at the point of care, we have developed an interactive, web-based dynamic nomogram. This online tool enables clinicians to instantly obtain personalized probabilities of OGTT-dependent diabetes. To our knowledge, this is the first predictive model for OGTT-dependent diabetes specifically within a community-managed CHD population.

Clinical implications: Towards a feasible two-tier triage strategy

Current guidelines recommend diabetes screening in cardiovascular patients using FPG and HbA1c, with OGTT reserved for ambiguous cases, primarily due to its operational complexity.4,6–8 Our findings provide robust evidence for the essential role of the OGTT in the management of community-based populations with CHD, while also recognizing the challenges associated with implementing universal testing. Therefore, we propose a pragmatic, two-tier, albumin-enhanced triage strategy: (1) Universal first-tier screening with FPG and HbA1c to diagnose obvious cases; (2) Risk-stratified second-tier OGTT for all first-tier negative patients, using the albumin-enhanced prediction model (readily applied via the online nomogram) to calculate individual risk, thereby guiding the selective and efficient use of OGTT. This strategy optimizes resource allocation by targeting OGTT to those with the highest pre-test probability, thereby increasing diagnostic yield and cost-effectiveness.

An important practical consideration is that incorporating FPG into a screening pathway may be cumbersome in high-risk community populations, because it requires planned attendance in the fasting state. To address this issue, we performed a supplementary analysis simulating a non-fasting screening scenario. In this setting, both HbA1c and albumin retained discriminatory value for OGTT-positive diabetes, and their combination performed better than either marker alone, supporting the incremental contribution of albumin even when FPG is unavailable. However, the simplified HbA1c+albumin approach remained inferior to the full FPG+HbA1c+albumin model, indicating that FPG still adds clinically useful information when fasting assessment is feasible. Accordingly, the full model (HbA1c + FPG + albumin) may be preferred for structured community screening programs, whereas an HbA1c+albumin-based approach may serve as a pragmatic alternative for opportunistic screening encounters in which fasting sampling cannot be arranged.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

A key strength of this study is the innovative repurposing of nationally representative NHANES data to simulate a real-world, stepwise community screening workflow. Specifically, this approach helps bridge the evidence gap between hospital-based studies and community practice. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, CHD status in NHANES was determined from self-reported medical history rather than adjudicated clinical records, which may have introduced recall bias and disease misclassification. Therefore, it is advisable to exercise caution when interpreting the findings. Although this approach may partly reflect the way medical history is obtained in community-based practice, further validation in cohorts with clinically confirmed CHD is still needed. Second, the lack of external validation in an independent external cohort remains an important limitation and should be addressed before broad clinical implementation. Nevertheless, the multistage probability sampling design employed by NHANES supports the representativeness of the study population. Third, the observational and cross-sectional design of the data limits the ability to draw causal inferences about the relationship between albumin and dysglycemia. Future mechanistic studies are necessary to determine whether albumin directly affects postprandial glucose metabolism or acts as a proxy for other underlying metabolic processes.

Conclusion

In summary, reliance on standard FPG/HbA1c testing fails to identify approximately half of diabetes cases among patients with CHD, underscoring the necessity of OGTT for definitive diagnosis. Our study suggests serum albumin as a new, accessible biomarker to improve diabetes prediction. The validated albumin-enhanced model and online tool offer a practical, cost-effective way to identify high-risk individuals for OGTT, improving diagnostic efficiency in community cardiology.
