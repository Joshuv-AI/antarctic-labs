Bionic intelligent responsive barbed microneedles synergistically remodel the microenvironment for the treatment of refractory pigmentation disorders (melasma)
Authors: Hao Ding, Erhao Zhang, Yubin Wang, Chunyi Guo, Yixin Sun, Xinyu Zhang, Ye Shu, Yicheng Zhi, Lu Wang, Dianhe Yu, Yi Sun, Chenyang Ye, Sufan Wu, Ji Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinically refractory melasma remains difficult to treat because it is driven by a multidimensional pathological microenvironment that includes melanocyte hyperactivity, aberrant vascular growth, oxidative stress and chronic inflammation. Single-pathway therapies therefore often produce incomplete or transient responses. To remodel this microenvironment more broadly, we developed a bioinspired, stimulus-responsive co-delivery microneedle platform (TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs). Inspired by the backward-curved barbs on the tick hypostome, the microneedles were engineered with outward-facing barbs to improve anchoring within skin tissue. Chemically, adipose-derived stem cell exosomes (ADSC-Exos) acted as bioactive crosslinking nodes. They were coupled to the HAMA-PBA matrix through ROS-sensitive dynamic boronate ester bonds, while tranexamic acid (TXA) was physically co-encapsulated. After insertion into ROS-rich melasma-like lesions, oxidative cleavage of carbon-boron bonds accelerated matrix degradation and promoted on-demand release of both payloads. Mechanistically, this platform produced an asymmetric but coordinated blockade of the pathological microenvironment: ADSC-Exos mainly scavenged local ROS, including ·OH, whereas TXA preferentially disrupted abnormal capillary networks. Together, they suppressed melanogenesis through the MITF/TYRP1 cascade and shifted macrophages from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype towards a tissue-reparative M2 phenotype. In a UV/progesterone-induced mouse model of melasma validated by bulk RNA sequencing, the system produced multidimensional microenvironmental regulation and visible depigmentation. This bioinspired, spatiotemporally responsive co-delivery microneedle platform integrates complementary and synergistic therapeutic actions and offers a translational strategy for complex, multifactorial hyperpigmentary disorders.Keywords: Biomimetic microneedles, ROS-Responsive biomaterials, Exosomes, Tranexamic acid, Transdermal drug delivery, Hyperpigmentation

Introduction

Melasma is a common acquired facial hyperpigmentary disorder that is difficult to clear completely. It typically presents as symmetric yellow-brown to dark-brown macules and patches on both cheeks [[1], [2], [3]]. Its global prevalence is approximately 1%, but the incidence may exceed 30% among women of reproductive age with darker skin phototypes (Fitzpatrick types III-V) [4,5]. Because melasma directly affects facial appearance and relapses readily, it imposes a substantial psychological burden. Its pathogenesis has not been fully defined, although several contributing factors are well established, including familial susceptibility, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, visible light, pregnancy and exogenous hormone use [1,3,4]. Oxidative stress is also involved. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) is usually reduced in melasma, whereas malondialdehyde (MDA) is commonly increased [6,7]. Histologically, melasma is characterised by increased melanin within the basal layer, often together with melanocyte hyperactivity or dysfunction. Melanosomes are transferred to keratinocytes, resulting in epidermal hyperpigmentation [8,9]. Several signalling pathways that stimulate melanogenesis have been identified. The cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)-mediated pathway, for example, is central to melanogenic regulation. UV radiation activates the cAMP cascade, leading to increased expression of tyrosinase (TYR), tyrosinase-related protein 1 (TYRP1) and dopachrome tautomerase (DCT), which ultimately promotes melanin synthesis [10,11]. Recent single-cell transcriptomic and molecular studies of skin have shifted the view of refractory acquired facial hyperpigmentation, including melasma, away from a purely melanocyte-centred disease. Melasma is increasingly understood as a disorder of the dermal microenvironment [12], shaped by a cycle of melanocyte hyperactivity, abnormal dermal angiogenesis, persistent local inflammation and UV- or visible light-induced oxidative stress [13]. Traditional topical monotherapies directed at a single pathway, such as tyrosinase inhibition alone, therefore often have limited efficacy and high relapse rates. These limitations highlight the clinical need for multi-targeted and coordinated treatments that remodel the hyperpigmentary microenvironment rather than only suppress melanin synthesis.

Current mainstream treatments for melasma include topical agents such as hydroquinone and retinoids, oral tranexamic acid and energy-based procedures [1,14,15]. Each approach has important limitations. Conventional topical hydrogels or creams are restricted by the dense stratum corneum, which prevents efficient transdermal delivery of macromolecules and hydrophilic actives and leads to low bioavailability at deeper dermal targets [16,17]. Direct intradermal injection bypasses this barrier, but pain reduces patient adherence and free drugs are rapidly metabolised within tissue, limiting sustained microenvironmental regulation [18,19]. Tranexamic acid (TXA), a classical plasmin inhibitor, can interfere with interactions between melanocytes and keratinocytes. It also has potential to attenuate abnormal capillary networks and limit melanocyte migration by protecting the basement membrane [1,4]. Yu Jeong Bae and colleagues reported that TXA may inhibit E2-induced melanogenesis through the cAMP-PKA-CREB-MITF axis, supporting its depigmenting activity [20,21]. TXA is therefore considered one of the more promising drugs for melasma because it inhibits melanin synthesis and transport and may support barrier recovery. However, TXA does not intrinsically scavenge intracellular ROS effectively, leaving upstream oxidative triggers insufficiently addressed. Oral TXA can be effective, but long-term administration may cause gastrointestinal discomfort and carries potential coagulation-related risks, including thrombosis [1,22,23]. Energy-based treatments may disrupt local homeostasis when energy delivery is poorly controlled, and topical agents can aggravate inflammation-prone skin, potentially inducing post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) [1,4,24]. A safe, minimally painful delivery system that crosses the skin barrier and provides targeted, intelligent and sustained intervention in the complex melasma microenvironment is therefore needed.

Human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (ADSC-Exos), which contain bioactive lipids, proteins and nucleic acids, have emerged as immunomodulatory agents and intrinsic antioxidants [25,26]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have been shown to contribute to skin anti-ageing, tissue regeneration and wound repair [[27], [28], [29]]. Previous studies have reported that stem cell-conditioned medium (CM), mainly derived from mesenchymal stem/stromal cell cultures, can exert anti-ageing, anti-wrinkle and depigmenting effects in skin [30,31]. The MSC secretome in MSC-CM contains soluble proteins and extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes. Compared with other MSC sources, adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) are readily available and secrete many cytokines associated with skin repair and anti-ageing [32,33]. The therapeutic effects of ADSCs are largely mediated by paracrine factors, especially exosomes [34,35]. Compared with parental ADSCs, exosomes have lower immunogenicity, better stability and greater convenience for storage [36]. Exosomes are generated through the endosomal pathway and arise from fusion of the plasma membrane with multivesicular bodies containing intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) [34,35]. Once released into the extracellular space, these ILVs are referred to as exosomes. They are enclosed by a lipid bilayer, are typically 30-200 nm in diameter, and contain proteins, genetic material such as messenger RNA (mRNA) and microRNA (miRNA), and metabolites [37]. MSC-derived exosomes are promising next-generation cell-free therapeutics for skin repair and regeneration [34,35,38]. Exosomes derived from adipose tissue MSCs have been reported to alleviate atopic dermatitis by restoring the skin barrier and reducing inflammation [39]. They can also induce collagen type I and elastin synthesis in human dermal fibroblasts in vitro [40]. Although keratinocyte-derived exosomes have been reported to regulate melanogenic homeostasis [41], evidence supporting the skin-brightening effects of ADSC-Exos or other MSC-derived exosomes remains limited.

We therefore reasoned that co-delivery of TXA and ADSC-Exos could establish a complementary and synergistic therapeutic paradigm. ADSC-Exos would preferentially neutralise ROS and promote anti-inflammatory macrophage polarisation, whereas TXA would target vascular abnormalities. Together, these actions could suppress melanogenesis and regulate the disease microenvironment more broadly. However, precise co-delivery of a hydrophilic small molecule (TXA) and nanoscale biological macromolecules (ADSC-Exos) into the dense dermis is severely limited by the stratum corneum [42]. Microneedle (MN) technology provides a transdermal delivery platform that can address this barrier. MNs penetrate the stratum corneum in a minimally invasive and nearly painless manner, creating microchannels that deliver therapeutics directly to the epidermal basal layer and superficial dermis [43,44]. Conventional MNs, however, can detach from mobile skin and usually lack microenvironment-responsive release, reducing local bioavailability [45]. Recent advances in smart biomaterials have expanded MNs from simple transdermal delivery tools into multifunctional platforms capable of spatially separated loading, local microenvironmental regulation and tissue-interactive therapy. Spatially partitioned MNs, for example, can integrate therapeutics with different functions and release requirements in distinct regions [46], while hydrogel MNs can deliver drugs locally and modulate pathological skin microenvironments [47]. At the same time, bioinspired adhesive hydrogels and tissue-interlocking microstructures have provided important design principles for improving adhesion and retention in dynamic skin [48]. These developments support the integration of structural anchoring, local delivery and microenvironment-responsive therapy for complex skin diseases. Nature offers an elegant solution for anchoring to skin. The hypostome of the tick Ixodes ricinus contains stepped, multilayered, backward-curved barbs that minimise insertion resistance while maximising interlocking force within host tissue, enabling firm long-term attachment [[49], [50], [51]]. Inspired by this architecture, we designed a bioinspired MN array with outward-facing barbs. We also drew on ROS-responsive hydrogels used in oxidative stress-driven diseases, such as tumours and chronic wounds, where smart gels can provide on-demand release and consume local pathological ROS [[52], [53], [54]]. The microneedle matrix was built from phenylboronic acid (PBA)-modified methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA-PBA), which was designed to exploit the abnormal oxidative microenvironment in melasma for targeted and coordinated depigmenting therapy. HAMA itself has good biocompatibility and supports skin hydration [55], while the introduced PBA groups are sensitive to lesion-associated microenvironmental cues, including elevated ROS [56]. This responsiveness allows the MNs to degrade and release payloads according to local pathological signals after insertion, offering more localised and sustained delivery than conventional hydrogels or injections. ADSC-Exos were dynamically coupled to the HAMA-PBA matrix through ROS-sensitive boronate ester bonds that interact with cis-diols enriched on exosomal surface glycoproteins [57,58]. In this design, ADSC-Exos function not only as therapeutics but also as structural crosslinking nodes.

Although TXA, exosome therapy, microneedle-assisted delivery and bioinspired anchoring MNs have each been applied to skin-related diseases, their isolated use may be insufficient for the complex and relapse-prone microenvironment of melasma. TXA can attenuate plasmin-related melanogenic and angiogenic signalling, but its local retention and lesion-adaptive release remain limited. Exosomes have anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and tissue-reparative functions, yet their transdermal delivery and sustained local retention in skin are constrained by the stratum corneum and insufficient tissue residence. Conventional dissolving MNs can enhance intradermal delivery, but they usually lack active anchoring and microenvironment-responsive release. Previous barbed MN designs have mainly focused on mechanical fixation and improved delivery efficiency, with limited use in coordinated regulation of oxidative stress, vascular abnormalities, inflammatory imbalance and immune dysregulation associated with hyperpigmentation [59,60]. Here, we describe the development of a tick mouthpart-inspired, ROS-responsive, dual-drug MN platform (TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs) for spatiotemporal remodelling of the melasma microenvironment (Fig. 1). The microneedles are not designed to rely on a single anti-pigmentary mechanism. Instead, they improve local retention and lesion-responsive release while coordinating the regulation of oxidative stress, angiogenesis, inflammation-associated immune imbalance and melanogenesis. After TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs are inserted into ROS-rich melasma skin, oxidative cleavage of carbon-boron (C-B) bonds disrupts exosome-mediated crosslinks, accelerates matrix degradation and enables targeted release of TXA and ADSC-Exos. Using MNT-1 human melanoma cells as an in vitro model of melanin deposition, we hypothesised that this platform would provide efficient co-delivery through the physical advantages of MNs and would further remodel the pathological microenvironment through a complementary mechanism involving HAMA-PBA-responsive release, ADSC-Exo-mediated improvement of the ROS microenvironment and TXA-mediated anti-angiogenic activity. We also performed in vivo studies in a UV/progesterone-induced C57BL/6 mouse model validated by bulk RNA sequencing and systematically evaluated the platform's complementary effects (anti-angiogenesis and ROS scavenging) and synergistic effects (anti-melanogenesis and macrophage polarisation remodelling). Macrophages are important regulators of tissue homeostasis and pathological microenvironment remodelling. Increasing evidence from biomaterial-based strategies shows that modulating macrophage activation and polarisation can influence inflammatory signalling, extracellular matrix remodelling, vascular reconstruction and tissue repair. These findings provide an important framework for understanding how TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs may regulate macrophage-associated immunity and remodel the local microenvironment [61]. This nature-inspired and stimulus-responsive MN co-delivery system provides a promising strategy for treating complex, multifactorial hyperpigmentary skin diseases.

Results

Isolation, characterisation and validation of ADSCs and ADSC-Exos

To obtain high-quality therapeutic cargo for MN delivery, we first isolated and cultured adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs). In vitro morphological observation (Fig. 2A) showed that ADSCs adhered tightly and displayed the typical spindle-shaped, fibroblast-like morphology. The CCK-8 proliferation curve (Fig. 2B) further showed a characteristic S-shaped growth profile. After a short adaptation phase on days 1-2, ADSCs entered a robust logarithmic phase on days 3-5, with exponential growth in cell viability, followed by a plateau phase on days 6-7 due to contact inhibition. These results confirmed good self-renewal and in vitro expansion capacity. Flow cytometry (Fig. 2C) showed high mesenchymal lineage purity, with strong expression of the canonical markers CD90 (95.06%) and CD105 (94.71%) and minimal expression of the haematopoietic stem cell marker CD34 (4.71%). Under lineage-specific induction, ADSCs differentiated successfully into osteoblasts, indicated by dense calcium nodule formation (Fig. 2D), and adipocytes, indicated by intracellular lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 2E), confirming retained multilineage differentiation potential. We then collected ADSC-derived exosomes (ADSC-Exos) from conditioned medium by ultracentrifugation. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed intact lipid bilayer vesicles with the typical saucer- or cup-shaped morphology (Fig. 2F). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed a narrow and monodisperse size distribution, with a main peak at approximately 145.6 nm and a yield of 7.3 × 1010 particles/mL (Fig. 2G), consistent with the expected exosome size range. Western blot analysis (Fig. 2H) confirmed the molecular identity and purity of ADSC-Exos. Compared with parental ADSC lysates, the extracted exosomes were enriched in the tetraspanins CD9, CD63 and CD81 and in the luminal protein TSG101. Importantly, the endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin was abundant in cell lysates but nearly absent from the exosome samples, excluding substantial contamination by intracellular organelle debris. Together, these characterisation results confirmed the successful isolation of high-purity and biologically active ADSC-Exos, providing the basis for their integration into the ROS-responsive MN system.

Preparation and comprehensive characterisation of TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA microneedles

Bioinspired architecture and mechanical performance of the Co-delivery microneedles

The engineering workflow for preparing drug-loaded bilayer MNs is shown in Fig. 3A. To reduce premature detachment, and to mimic the tick hypostome as an efficient tissue-anchoring organ, we designed a stepped array of outward-growing barbs. Macroscopic and optical microscopy images (Fig. 3B) confirmed high-fidelity replication of the mould and showed a checkerboard arrangement of alternating long and short needles. This alternating-height design was used to overcome the trampoline effect of skin during insertion and to buffer variations in manual pressure. The long MNs contained two layers of outward-facing barbs for firm anchoring in the dermis, whereas the short MNs contained a single barb layer to target the epidermal basal layer. This stepped structure established a three-dimensional physical anchoring system and enabled spatially layered delivery to the basal epidermis and dermis, which matches the multilayer pathological distribution of melasma. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) further showed the geometric uniformity of the barbed array and sharp needle tips (Fig. 3C). High-magnification SEM images of the needle surface (Fig. 3D) showed that blank HAMA-PBA MNs had a relatively smooth surface, whereas TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs contained crystalline and clustered surface features, consistent with dense incorporation of the two therapeutic cargos within the polymer matrix. Mechanical strength is required for reliable penetration of the stratum corneum. Force-displacement curves (Fig. 3E) showed that TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs had substantial mechanical compliance during compression and did not undergo abrupt brittle fracture. The array tolerated nearly 80 N of compression, exceeding the threshold required to pierce human skin. In addition, incorporation of PBA groups increased matrix stiffness compared with pure HAMA MNs (Fig. S1A). We next assessed skin penetration using mouse skin and trypan blue staining. A highly ordered micropore matrix matching the MN geometry was observed (Fig. 3F, upper panel). Subsequent Masson-Fontana histological staining (Fig. 3F, lower panel) confirmed that the barbed MNs penetrated the epidermis and reached the dense dermal matrix, thereby opening transdermal channels for drug delivery.

ROS-responsive degradation, drug release and chemical verification

The HAMA-PBA matrix was designed to undergo accelerated disassembly under oxidative stress. Mass-loss analysis (Fig. 3H) showed that TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs degraded faster than TXA-only MNs in a simulated ROS environment containing 0.03% H2O2 as an accelerated oxidative challenge [62]. This accelerated disassembly was attributed to ROS-triggered cleavage of dynamic covalent crosslinks. Initially, PBA groups on the HAMA backbone formed cyclic boronate ester bonds with cis-diols enriched on ADSC-Exo surface glycoproteins, such as CD9 and CD63. These bonds served as auxiliary structural crosslinks. Under oxidative conditions, PBA-associated oxidation-sensitive bonds were cleaved, reducing effective crosslink density and accelerating matrix disassembly. Irreversible oxidation of C-B bonds further disrupted structural nodes, leading to swelling and matrix collapse. This mechanism was visualised by three-dimensional confocal fluorescence tracking of FITC-labelled MNs (Fig. 3G), where ADSC-Exo-loaded MNs rapidly collapsed within 48 h under oxidative stress. Macroscopic degradation of alizarin red-loaded gels similarly showed rapid material dissociation and dye release in 0.03% H2O2 (Fig. S1B). To avoid non-specific inference of exosome release from total protein content, we quantified extracellular vesicles in the MN release medium by NTA. Release media from TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs were collected at predefined time points in PBS or 0.03% H2O2, and particle concentration and mode size were measured by NTA. Cumulative particle release was calculated relative to the total number of particles obtained after complete dissolution of an equivalent number of MN patches. Oxidative conditions markedly promoted exosome release. Compared with PBS, the 0.03% H2O2 group showed a higher cumulative particle release ratio at each time point, reaching 94.2% at 144 h, whereas the PBS group reached only 54.4% (Fig. 3I). HPLC analysis also showed faster cumulative TXA release under oxidative conditions, with 90.72% release at 144 h in 0.03% H2O2 compared with 50.94% in PBS. This trend was consistent with NTA-based particle release and indicates that the PBA-associated oxidation-sensitive network undergoes accelerated disassembly under strong oxidative stimulation, promoting synchronized release of TXA and extracellular vesicles. NTA was also used to analyse samples collected at different release times in PBS and 0.03% H2O2 and to record changes in particle mode size and mean size (Fig. 4F). Particle signals within the extracellular vesicle size range were detected throughout the release period in both environments. As release proceeded, the mode size and mean size generally remained within 90-150 nm, indicating no obvious large-scale particle aggregation during release.

To convert the TXA formulation concentration in the MN precursor solution into a dosing parameter with practical relevance, we quantified TXA loading in 50 mM TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs. Based on the geometry of the MN array, needle volume and 50 mM TXA precursor concentration, the theoretical TXA loading in the needle region of each patch was approximately 36.89 μg. This corresponded to a theoretical dose of approximately 17.57 μg/cm2 over the MN-covered area. Each MN patch contained 225 needles, including 113 tall needles containing approximately 0.263 μg TXA per needle and 112 short needles containing approximately 0.064 μg TXA per needle. The average theoretical loading was approximately 0.164 μg TXA per needle. Local tissue concentrations after MN release in skin are described in the subsequent in vivo experiments.

Finally, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used to verify the chemical composition of the composite MNs (Fig. 3J). In addition to the characteristic amide bands of the HAMA backbone, the appearance of a B-O bending vibration near 1374 cm−1 confirmed successful PBA grafting. After TXA loading, characteristic absorption peaks corresponding to protonated amino N-H bending (1541 cm−1) and carboxyl C-O stretching (1261 cm−1) became evident. Incorporation of ADSC-Exos and TXA also caused shifts and broadening of O-H and C=O peaks, such as those near 3274 cm−1 and 1608 cm−1, indicating hydrogen bonding and dynamic crosslinking interactions within the network. These spectral features collectively confirmed successful assembly of the HAMA-PBA-TXA-Exo quaternary structure without disrupting the structural integrity of the therapeutic cargos. Detailed FITC analysis is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Short-term storage stability and performance of TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs

Because dynamic boronate ester networks and ADSC-derived extracellular vesicles may be affected by temperature and humidity, we evaluated the short-term stability of TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs under different storage conditions. MN patches were stored for 14 days at 4 °C in the dark under dry conditions, at 25 °C in the dark under dry conditions, or at 25 °C with 75% relative humidity (RH). SEM was used to assess changes in barbed MN morphology after storage. As shown in Fig. 4A, MNs stored at 4 °C under dry conditions retained an intact and regular needle outline, sharp tips and complete lateral barbs, with no obvious collapse, bending or surface damage. This indicates that low-temperature dry storage helps maintain overall MN geometry. At 25 °C under dry conditions, the needle bodies and barbs remained recognisable and largely intact, but mild granular deposition or surface roughening was observed, suggesting some surface morphological change during room-temperature dry storage. Under 25 °C and 75% RH, the needles became blunted, swollen and collapsed, and the originally sharp tips and layered barbs were largely lost, showing that high humidity markedly disrupts the three-dimensional structural integrity of the MNs. We then evaluated mechanical performance, skin anchoring, TXA chemical stability, exosomal particle characteristics and biological function. Compression force-displacement curves showed that MNs stored at 4 °C in the dark and dry state retained high mechanical strength after 14 days, with a maximum compression force of approximately 80 N and no evident decrease compared with freshly prepared MNs. MNs stored at 25 °C in the dark and dry state showed a mild decrease in mechanical strength, whereas those stored at 25 °C and 75% RH showed the most pronounced softening and loss of mechanical performance (Fig. 4B). Consistently, the maximum pull-out force after storage was highest in the 4 °C dark dry group and was markedly reduced in the 75% RH group (Fig. 4C), suggesting that humidity exposure may weaken MN stiffness and barb-mediated tissue interlocking. HPLC quantification showed that TXA retention was highest under 4 °C dark dry storage (95.64 ± 0.7979%), decreased to 90.31 ± 1.447% under 25 °C dark dry storage, and further decreased to 70.73 ± 1.28% under 25 °C and 75% RH (Fig. 4D). These results indicate that moisture and elevated temperature can accelerate TXA loss or matrix-associated degradation in TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs.

We next used NTA to evaluate the characteristics of extracellular vesicle particles released from stored MNs. Compared with freshly prepared MNs, the 4 °C dark dry group retained a relatively high particle recovery concentration and a more concentrated size distribution. The 25 °C dark dry group showed a moderate decrease in particle concentration and a rightward shift in size distribution. The 25 °C and 75% RH group showed a marked decrease in particle concentration, with further increases in mode size and mean size (Fig. 4E), suggesting vesicle aggregation, fusion or structural instability under high humidity. To assess retained exosome function after storage, we used DCFH-DA to measure the ability of released components to suppress H2O2-induced intracellular ROS in HaCaT cells. The 4 °C dark dry group still markedly reduced intracellular ROS fluorescence, with an effect close to that of freshly prepared MNs. Antioxidant activity partially decreased after 25 °C dark dry storage and was most clearly impaired in the high-humidity group (Fig. 4G and H). In HUVEC tube formation assays, MNs stored at 4 °C in the dark and dry state still effectively inhibited vascular network formation, whereas this effect was weakened after storage at 25 °C and 75% RH (Fig. 4I and J). Together, these results show that 4 °C dark dry storage preserves the mechanical performance, barb anchoring capacity, TXA retention, exosomal particle characteristics and key biological functions of TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs over 14 days. In contrast, high humidity damages structural stability, drug retention and functional activity, indicating that this composite MN formulation should be protected from hot and humid environments.

To directly verify the contribution of the barbed structure to skin anchoring, we prepared non-barbed MNs with the same material composition, array density and basic needle dimensions as the barbed MNs. An ex vivo skin pull-out test was used to quantify mechanical anchoring. Barbed and non-barbed MNs were inserted into ex vivo skin under identical conditions, pulled out vertically at a constant speed, and force-displacement curves were recorded (Fig. 4K). Compared with non-barbed MNs, barbed MNs showed higher resistance during pull-out and a significantly increased maximum pull-out force of approximately 2.65 N, more than threefold higher than that of non-barbed MNs (p < 0.0001; Fig. 4L). These results show that outward-facing barbs enhance mechanical interlocking between MNs and skin tissue, thereby improving anchoring in ex vivo skin.

To further verify the structural contribution of ADSC-derived exosomes within the HAMA-PBA network, we compared the rheological properties of four materials: HAMA, HAMA-PBA, Exo@HAMA and Exo@HAMA-PBA. All groups were prepared with the same polymer concentration and crosslinking conditions. The HAMA group served as the base network control, HAMA-PBA was used to evaluate the effect of PBA modification on mechanical properties, Exo@HAMA was used to exclude simple physical filling by exosomes, and Exo@HAMA-PBA was used to assess network reinforcement when PBA and exosomes were both present.

Time-sweep measurements showed that all four materials maintained relatively stable storage moduli (G′) throughout the 600 s test, indicating structural stability under the measurement conditions. Exo@HAMA-PBA consistently showed the highest G′, remaining near 3.0 × 104 Pa and significantly exceeding HAMA-PBA, Exo@HAMA and HAMA (Fig. 4M). Compared with HAMA, PBA incorporation alone clearly increased the elastic modulus, whereas exosome addition alone produced only a modest increase. Notably, the G′ of Exo@HAMA-PBA was markedly higher than that of HAMA-PBA, and the increase was greater than that produced by exosome addition to the HAMA matrix. Frequency-sweep measurements confirmed this trend: across 0.1-100 rad s−1, Exo@HAMA-PBA maintained the highest G′ and showed a stable elastic response as frequency increased (Fig. 4N). These results indicate that exosome incorporation further improves storage capacity and effective crosslinking in the PBA-modified network. Considering that exosomal membrane glycoproteins are rich in cis-diol structures, these findings support the involvement of exosomes in HAMA-PBA network formation through multivalent dynamic interactions with PBA groups.

In vitro validation of ADSC-Exo-mediated melanogenesis inhibition, ROS scavenging and immunomodulation

Efficient cellular internalisation and biocompatibility

For Exos released from MNs to exert therapeutic effects, they must first be internalised by target cells in the dermal-epidermal microenvironment. To model this release-uptake cascade in vitro, we used a Transwell co-culture system (Fig. 5A). After co-incubation with PKH26-labelled ADSC-Exos (red), confocal microscopy (Fig. 5B) showed abundant red fluorescence in the perinuclear regions of both THP-1 macrophages and MNT-1 cells, confirming efficient exosome uptake. Establishing the safety of the delivery vehicle was also essential. Live/dead staining and CCK-8 proliferation assays in HaCaT keratinocytes (Fig. 5C and D) showed minimal cytotoxicity even when MNs were loaded with different exosome concentrations (1, 5 and 10 × 109 particles/mL). Cell viability remained above 99% (Fig. S2A), and normal proliferation kinetics were not disturbed, supporting the biocompatibility of this platform for transdermal use.

Upstream blockade through potent and broad ROS scavenging

Because UV-induced excess ROS is a key upstream trigger of melanocyte hyperactivity and chronic inflammation in melasma, we first evaluated the antioxidant capacity of released Exos. DCFH-DA green fluorescence showed that ROS levels in H2O2-treated HaCaT cells were reduced by Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5H). Multiple radical-scavenging assays further confirmed broad intrinsic antioxidant activity against ·OH, ·O2−, ABTS and DPPH radicals (Fig. 5I and Fig. S2B). This ROS-scavenging effect showed a clear marginal plateau at the 5Exo concentration.

Downstream effects on melanogenesis and the inflammatory microenvironment

After effective attenuation of upstream oxidative stress, we examined downstream effects on the melanogenic and inflammatory axes. In the melanogenesis assay, macroscopic observation of MNT-1 cell pellets (Fig. 5E) showed dose-dependent pigment lightning, with a plateau in the 5Exo group. Quantitative biochemical analysis confirmed that 5Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs significantly inhibited intracellular tyrosinase activity (Fig. S2C) and total melanin content (Fig. 5F), with no further meaningful reduction at the 10Exo dose. At the molecular level, western blot analysis (Fig. 5G and Fig. S2D) showed downregulation of MITF and TYRP1, supporting suppression of the melanogenic signalling cascade. Because persistent inflammation contributes to melasma recurrence, we used LPS and IFN-γ to stimulate THP-1 macrophages towards a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. ADSC-Exo-loaded MNs induced clear phenotypic reprogramming. As shown by qRT-PCR (Fig. S2E) and ELISA (Fig. 5K), treatment reduced M1-associated markers (CD80, CD86, TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6) and increased M2-associated anti-inflammatory markers (CD163, CD206, IL-4 and IL-10). Flow cytometry further confirmed this M1-to-M2 shift (Fig. 5J), showing a decrease in the CD86+/CD206- M1 subset and a corresponding increase in the CD86-/CD206+ M2 subset. Consistent with the antioxidant and anti-melanogenic results, immunomodulatory activity also reached a plateau at 5 × 109 particles/mL. Balancing therapeutic efficacy against the diminishing return at higher dose, we selected 5Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs for subsequent in vivo evaluation.

In vitro optimisation of TXA concentration and evaluation of multitarget therapeutic mechanisms

Biosafety analysis and formulation optimisation of TXA-loaded MNs

To define the pharmacological mechanisms of TXA delivered through the MN platform, we established a Transwell co-culture system using relevant cell types, including MNT-1, HaCaT and THP-1 cells (Fig. 6A). Identifying an appropriate therapeutic window that balances anti-melanogenic efficacy with biosafety was a key prerequisite. CCK-8 assays (Fig. 6B and Fig. S3A) and live/dead staining (Fig. S3B and C) showed concentration-dependent and cell type-specific effects of TXA-loaded MNs (10, 25, 50 and 100 mM) on MNT-1, HaCaT, THP-1, HUVECs and NHEMs. In normal skin-resident cells, including HaCaT keratinocytes and THP-1 macrophages, MNs loaded with 10-50 mM TXA showed good biocompatibility, with cell viability maintained above 95%. At the highest concentration (100TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs), non-specific cytotoxicity appeared, reducing HaCaT viability below 90%. In contrast, TXA produced a clear dose-dependent inhibitory effect in the target MNT-1 melanoma cells and in normal human epidermal melanocytes (NHEMs). MNT-1 and NHEM viability decreased as TXA concentration increased and fell below 50% in the 100TXA@HAMA-PBA MN group. This selective sensitivity suggests that TXA strongly suppresses hyperactive melanocytic cells, a desired effect in melasma therapy.

To achieve a balanced formulation that maximised targeted suppression of melanocytic cells while avoiding the broader cytotoxicity observed at 100 mM, we selected 50TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs as the optimal and safe formulation threshold for subsequent mechanistic studies.

Dual effects on melanin-producing MNT-1 cells: inhibition of melanogenesis and pathological migration

In refractory melasma, hyperactive melanocytes not only overproduce melanin but also display abnormal motility, which may contribute to basement membrane disruption and downward melanin deposition into the dermis, known as pigmentary incontinence. Scratch-wound assays (Fig. 6C and D) and Transwell migration assays (Fig. 6E and F) showed dose-dependent slowing of MNT-1 cell motility after TXA treatment. This finding is consistent with the core mechanism of TXA as a plasmin inhibitor. By inhibiting plasmin-mediated proteolytic degradation of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, TXA may help stabilise the basement membrane and restrict pathological downward migration of melanocytic cells.

Macroscopically, MNT-1 cell pellets treated with TXA-loaded MNs showed dose-dependent depigmentation that plateaued at 50TXA (Fig. 6I). Quantitative analysis showed that 50TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs inhibited intracellular tyrosinase activity (Fig. S3D) and total melanin synthesis (Fig. 6J), with efficacy comparable to that of 100TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs. At the molecular level, marked downregulation of MITF and TYRP1 further supported the anti-melanogenic effect (Fig. 6K and Fig. S4F).

Remodelling of the dermal microenvironment: anti-angiogenic and immunomodulatory effects

Melasma is increasingly regarded as a photoageing-related disorder characterised by prominent vascular proliferation and low-grade chronic inflammation. Because plasmin is a potent pro-angiogenic activator [63], we evaluated the anti-angiogenic potential of TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs in HUVECs. The in vitro tube formation assay (Fig. 6G) showed that TXA treatment disrupted endothelial capillary-like networks, significantly reducing node number (Fig. 6H), mesh number and total capillary length (Fig. S4E), with maximal inhibition at 50 mM and 100 mM TXA. To exclude the possibility that inhibition of HUVEC tube formation by TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs was caused only by non-specific cytotoxicity, we assessed HUVEC viability under the same treatment conditions used for the tube formation assay. CCK-8 results showed no significant decrease in HUVEC viability after treatment with 50 mM TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs compared with the control group (Fig. S3A). Live/dead staining further showed that HUVECs in all groups were mainly Calcein-AM-positive viable cells, with only rare PI-positive dead cells (Fig. S3B). These results suggest that 50 mM and even 100 mM TXA MN formulations did not produce obvious HUVEC cytotoxicity under the current conditions, and that the inhibition of vascular network formation was unlikely to be explained simply by reduced cell viability.

After excluding obvious cytotoxicity, we investigated the potential mechanism by which TXA inhibits abnormal angiogenesis. uPA is an important upstream activator of plasminogen-to-plasmin conversion, while VEGFA drives endothelial migration, tube formation and abnormal vascular growth. Western blotting showed that uPA and VEGFA protein expression in HUVECs decreased after TXA@HAMA-PBA MN treatment (Fig. S4A–C). We also measured plasmin activity after TXA@HAMA-PBA MN treatment. Compared with the control group, plasmin activity was significantly reduced in all TXA@HAMA-PBA MN groups (Fig. S4D). These results indicate that TXA-loaded MNs affect angiogenesis-related molecular expression and inhibit plasmin-related activity at the functional level. Together with the tube formation results, these findings suggest that TXA-loaded MNs can attenuate uPA-associated plasminogen activation and VEGFA-mediated angiogenic responses without markedly compromising HUVEC viability, thereby inhibiting abnormal vascular network formation.

We also evaluated immunomodulatory activity in THP-1 macrophages prestimulated with LPS and IFN-γ. ELISA quantification of culture supernatants (Fig. 6L) showed that TXA progressively inhibited secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6) and increased release of anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-4 and IL-10). Consistent with these secretion profiles, qRT-PCR (Fig. S4G) confirmed phenotypic reprogramming. TXA-loaded MNs reduced expression of M1 markers (CD80 and CD86) and promoted polarisation towards an M2 tissue-reparative phenotype (CD163 and CD206). The anti-angiogenic and immunomodulatory effects both plateaued at 50 mM TXA. These in vitro mechanistic results support the use of 50TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs to regulate the vascular and inflammatory dermal microenvironment associated with melasma.

Comprehensive in vitro validation of synergistic and complementary mechanisms of TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs

Synergistic and complementary remodelling of the melasma microenvironment by dual-loaded MNs

To validate the rationale and advantages of co-delivery, we systematically compared dual-loaded TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs with corresponding single-drug MNs across the major pathological features of melasma. TXA mainly contributed anti-inflammatory, anti-melanogenic and anti-angiogenic effects, whereas ADSC-Exos contributed anti-inflammatory, anti-melanogenic and antioxidant (anti-ROS) functions (Fig. 7A). We first assessed their shared synergistic anti-melanogenic mechanism. Macroscopic observation of MNT-1 cell pellets (Fig. 7B) and quantitative biochemical assays showed that TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs and Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs each inhibited tyrosinase activity (Fig. S5A) and melanin synthesis (Fig. 7C) to some extent, while dual-drug MNs produced the greatest pigment reduction. Western blot analysis (Fig. 7D and Fig. S5B) confirmed this effect at the molecular level, with the combined treatment producing the strongest suppression of TYRP1 and MITF expression. To improve the physiological relevance of the in vitro findings and verify the effects of different loading strategies on melanogenesis, we also compared the anti-melanogenic activity of TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs, Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs and TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs in low-passage primary normal human epidermal melanocytes (NHEMs). After co-culture of primary melanocytes with the corresponding MN patches, cell pellets were observed, and tyrosinase activity and intracellular melanin content were measured. Macroscopic observation showed that TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs and Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs both lightened cell pellets compared with the control group, whereas TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs produced the lightest pellets, indicating stronger inhibition of pigmentation in primary melanocytes (Fig. S5C). Functional quantification further showed that both TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs and Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs reduced tyrosinase activity and melanin content to varying degrees. The TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN group produced the strongest inhibition of both endpoints and outperformed either single-loaded MN group (Fig. S5D and E).

These results indicate that TXA and ADSC-Exos each exert anti-melanogenic effects in primary human melanocytes, while dual-loaded MNs produce stronger overall pigment suppression. This finding is consistent with the MNT-1 results and further supports the complementarity of TXA and ADSC-Exos in melanogenesis inhibition. It also indicates that the anti-melanogenic activity of dual-loaded MNs is not limited to a melanoma cell model. Subsequent experiments clarified the asymmetric complementarity of the two agents. In cellular mechanotransduction and motility assays, phalloidin staining (Fig. 7E) showed that TXA-containing formulations (TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs and TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs) mainly drove MNT-1 cell rounding and actin stress fibre disassembly, thereby directly restricting pathological migration. Similarly, in HUVEC tube formation assays (Fig. 7F,G and Fig. S5F), TXA-loaded MNs disrupted endothelial capillary-like networks, whereas Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs had little effect on this anti-angiogenic endpoint. In contrast, the role of ADSC-Exos was most apparent in oxidative stress assays. DCFH-DA fluorescence (Fig. 7H and I) and radical-scavenging assays (Fig. 7J and Fig. S5G) showed that TXA alone produced moderate ROS reduction, whereas ADSC-Exos exerted stronger intrinsic antioxidant activity. Co-delivery in TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs produced the greatest neutralisation of radicals, including ·OH, ·O2−, ABTS and DPPH. These results indicate that ADSC-Exos compensate for the limited antioxidant capacity of TXA and act with TXA to attenuate upstream oxidative triggers. Finally, we assessed their immunomodulatory synergy in LPS- and IFN-γ-prestimulated THP-1 macrophages. Because both TXA and ADSC-Exos have anti-inflammatory properties, the combined formulation produced a stepwise phenotypic shift. ELISA (Fig. 7L) and qRT-PCR (Fig. S5H) showed a transition from a pro-inflammatory M1 state, with decreased TNF-α, IL-6, CD80 and CD86, towards an anti-inflammatory M2 state, with increased IL-10, IL-4, CD163 and CD206. As expected, TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs induced the most pronounced immunomodulatory response. Flow cytometry confirmed this synergistic polarisation effect (Fig. 7K). Overall, TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs integrated shared anti-melanogenic and immunomodulatory effects with asymmetric complementary functions, namely Exo-dominant ROS scavenging and TXA-dominant anti-angiogenic activity, enabling broad regulation of the complex melasma microenvironment.

In vivo efficacy and microenvironmental remodelling of TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs in a mouse model of melasma

In vivo treatment, local drug exposure and macroscopic efficacy of TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs in a mouse model of melasma

To evaluate the short-term therapeutic effects and early microenvironmental regulation of TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs in melasma-like lesions, we established an in vivo workflow that included model induction, treatment intervention and endpoint analysis (Fig. 8A). After successful model establishment, mice received the corresponding MN treatment and were assessed macroscopically and histologically on days 7 and 14 after treatment. C57BL/6 mice were subjected to a 28-day combined induction protocol consisting of daily UV irradiation and intramuscular progesterone injection, which generated stable and pathophysiologically relevant melasma-like pigmentation. On the day of successful model establishment (defined as day 0), digital photographs were obtained and lesional skin was collected for Masson-Fontana staining. To validate the pathological relevance of the model at the molecular level, dorsal skin tissues from melasma-model and healthy control mice were collected for bulk RNA-seq analysis. The volcano plot (Fig. 8B) showed broad upregulation of melanogenesis-related genes in the model group, including Tyr, Tyrp1, Mlana, Slc45a2, Slc24a4, Pmch and Gjb6. Consistently, Gene Ontology enrichment analysis (Fig. 8C) showed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were enriched in melanin biosynthetic and metabolic processes and in melanosome or pigment granule components. These findings confirmed activation of pigmentation mechanisms in the model and supported its relevance to human melasma-like pathology.

After 14 days of MN treatment, macroscopic observation (Fig. 8D) and quantitative image analysis (Fig. 8E) showed that dual-loaded TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs produced the most pronounced lesion lightning, with skin colour approaching baseline levels. To evaluate local skin exposure to TXA after MN administration, dorsal skin from the treated area was collected at 12, 24, 48 and 72 h after MN application, and TXA content was measured. As shown in Fig. 8F, TXA was detectable in the treated skin at all time points. Skin TXA content peaked 24 h after MN application at 169.5 ± 11.27 μg/g tissue and then gradually decreased, while remaining detectable at 72 h (54.75 ± 3.775 μg/g tissue). These results indicate that the 50 mM TXA formulation not only shows ROS-responsive release in vitro but also provides sustained local drug release within the MN-covered skin region. To directly assess whether the melasma-like lesions exhibited enhanced oxidative stress, H2O2 levels in dorsal skin homogenates were quantified using the Amplex Red Hydrogen Peroxide Assay. Compared with healthy skin, H2O2 levels were significantly increased in the melasma model group (Fig. 8G). This result is consistent with the oxidative stress profile of melasma, including decreased SOD and increased MDA, and further confirms biochemically that the UV/progesterone-induced melasma model has a local oxidative stress microenvironment.

Both single-drug MNs reduced basal-layer melanin deposition to some extent, but dual-loaded MNs produced the greatest reduction, supporting synergistic anti-melanogenic activity in vivo. To further determine how MN treatment affected the melanogenic programme in melasma-like lesions, we measured MITF and TYRP1 protein expression in skin tissue. Compared with healthy controls, the melasma model group showed increased MITF and TYRP1 expression, indicating activation of melanogenesis-related signalling. After MN treatment, MITF and TYRP1 expression decreased to varying degrees, with the strongest downregulation in the TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN group (Fig. 9J and Fig. S6C). The non-responsive and non-barbed groups also partially reduced MITF and TYRP1 expression, but the improvement was smaller than that observed with TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs. Clinically refractory melasma represents a complex pathological cascade driven not only by melanocyte hyperactivity but also by abnormal angiogenesis, chronic inflammation and oxidative stress. Our in vivo immunofluorescence results reflected the asymmetric complementarity observed in vitro. For pathological angiogenesis, CD31 staining (Fig. 9B, E) showed that TXA-loaded MNs exerted stronger anti-angiogenic effects than ADSC-Exo-only MNs, reducing the expanded vascular signal in the superficial dermis. Because abnormal vascular activation in melasma lesions is closely associated with plasmin-related signalling, we further measured uPA and VEGFA protein expression in skin tissue. Western blotting showed that uPA and VEGFA expression increased in the melasma model group and decreased after MN treatment. TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs produced the strongest inhibition of uPA and VEGFA, whereas the non-responsive and non-barbed groups produced only partial reductions (Fig. 9I and Fig. S6A). Downregulation of uPA and VEGFA was consistent with reduced CD31 signal, indirectly supporting attenuation of plasminogen activation-associated signalling and related pro-angiogenic responses. For macrophage polarisation, CD86/CD206 staining (Fig. 9C, F) identified ADSC-Exos as the main driver of inflammatory microenvironment regulation. ADSC-Exo-containing MNs reduced the M1 pro-inflammatory marker CD86 and increased the M2 tissue-repair marker CD206. To further evaluate changes in the inflammatory microenvironment in vivo, we measured TNF-α protein expression and multiple inflammatory cytokines in skin tissue. Western blotting showed increased TNF-α expression in the melasma model group, while all MN treatment groups showed varying degrees of reduction, with the largest decrease in the TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN group (Fig. 9I and Fig. S6B). ELISA further showed that IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α levels were increased in melasma-model skin, whereas IL-4 and IL-10 were decreased. TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN treatment significantly reduced IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α and largely restored IL-4 and IL-10 levels (Fig. 9K). Together with CD86/CD206 immunofluorescence, these results suggest that TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs reduce local pro-inflammatory signalling and promote an immune microenvironment more favourable for tissue homeostasis.

We also quantified oxidative stress markers in skin tissue (Fig. 9G and H). Combined TXA/Exo MN treatment reduced accumulation of the lipid peroxidation product MDA and restored the antioxidant enzyme SOD towards physiological levels. Although single-drug TXA and ADSC-Exos showed comparable effects on the MDA/SOD balance, their combination produced broader correction of the oxidative microenvironment. Together, these in vivo findings indicate that TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs integrate the anti-vascular and anti-melanogenic precision of TXA with the immunomodulatory and antioxidant effects of ADSC-Exos. This multi-target co-delivery platform produced strong therapeutic effects and may provide a translational strategy for local melasma treatment.

Independent contributions of the ROS-responsive matrix and barbed structure to in vivo efficacy

To distinguish the contributions of the ROS-responsive matrix and the barbed structure to in vivo efficacy, we included two platform-specific control groups in the melasma model. The non-responsive MN group retained the barbed structure and TXA/Exo dual-loading design but used a HAMA matrix without PBA instead of HAMA-PBA. The non-barbed MN group retained the HAMA-PBA responsive matrix and dual-loading design but lacked lateral barbs on the needles. Macroscopic skin images showed that both the non-responsive and non-barbed groups produced some depigmentation compared with the untreated melasma model group. However, the barbed TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN group showed the greatest improvement in lesion colour and brown pigmentation (Fig. 8D). Consistently, Masson-Fontana staining showed the least epidermal and basal-layer melanin deposition in the TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN group, whereas the non-responsive and non-barbed groups showed only partial improvement (Fig. 9A–D).

CD31 immunofluorescence further showed that CD31-positive vascular signals in the superficial dermis were markedly reduced in the TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN group, indicating stronger suppression of abnormal vascular activation. In contrast, the non-responsive and non-barbed groups improved relative to the model group but retained higher CD31-positive areas than the complete platform group (Fig. 9B–E). These findings suggest that ROS-responsive release and barb-mediated local retention may both contribute to sustained TXA-mediated regulation of the lesional vascular microenvironment.

In the macrophage-associated immune microenvironment, the TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN group showed the most pronounced decrease in CD86-positive signal and increase in CD206-positive signal, indicating suppression of pro-inflammatory macrophage-associated phenotypes and enhancement of repair-associated macrophage phenotypes (Fig. 9C). The non-responsive and non-barbed groups also showed some decrease in CD86 and increase in CD206, but the magnitude was smaller than that observed with the complete platform (Fig. 9F).

Thus, the barbed TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN platform produced the strongest effects on pigment reduction, abnormal vascular signalling and macrophage-associated microenvironmental regulation. The partial efficacy of the non-responsive and non-barbed controls indicates that local dual-drug delivery itself has therapeutic activity. The additional benefit observed with TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs supports the involvement of both the ROS-responsive matrix and the barbed anchoring structure in enhancing in vivo treatment efficacy.

To systematically evaluate the in vivo biosafety of the TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN system, major metabolic and immune organs, including heart, liver, spleen and kidney, were collected after the treatment period for H&E staining and histopathological analysis (Fig. S6D). Histology showed that the macroscopic and microscopic structures of all examined organs remained intact, with no clear pathological injury. Myocardial fibres were regularly arranged, without cardiomyocyte hypertrophy or necrosis. Hepatic lobules were well defined, with radially arranged hepatic cords and no evidence of steatosis or congestion. The spleen showed clear boundaries between white pulp and red pulp and typical lymphoid follicle morphology, with no evidence of systemic immune abnormality. In the kidney, the major excretory organ, glomerular capillary tufts were normal, Bowman capsules were intact, renal tubular epithelial cells showed no degeneration, necrosis or detachment, no pathological casts were present in the tubular lumen, and no inflammatory cell infiltration or fibrosis was observed in the interstitium. Lung tissues in all groups maintained largely intact alveolar architecture and septal morphology, without obvious alveolar collapse, interstitial thickening, inflammatory infiltration, haemorrhage or necrosis. These histological findings indicate that this smart responsive transdermal MN delivery system has good in vivo biocompatibility and did not cause detectable off-target effects or systemic organ toxicity while exerting local therapeutic effects. To further assess potential systemic toxicity, key serum biochemical indicators of liver and kidney function were quantified at the end of the treatment period (Fig. S6E). Liver function markers included alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and kidney function markers included blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CREA). Serological analysis showed no statistically significant differences in these biomarkers between any MN-treated group and healthy controls (P > 0.05). All quantitative values remained within the physiological reference range for C57BL/6 mice. Within the 14-day observation period, no obvious histological abnormalities were observed in major organs, and ALT, AST, BUN and CREA showed no significant between-group differences, supporting preliminary short-term in vivo biosafety under the current dosing regimen.

Discussion

Melasma is not caused only by increased melanogenesis. It is a complex skin microenvironmental disorder involving oxidative stress, abnormal vascular activation, low-grade chronic inflammation and local immune imbalance. These pathological processes interact and can reinforce one another. A single tyrosinase inhibitor, or a strategy that only improves transdermal delivery, is unlikely to provide durable and comprehensive efficacy. Local treatment for melasma therefore needs to deliver anti-pigmentary drugs effectively while also coordinating regulation of oxidative, vascular, inflammatory and melanogenic components of the pathological microenvironment.

Previous studies have shown that TXA, exosome therapy, microneedle-assisted delivery and bioinspired anchoring structures each have therapeutic potential in skin. However, these strategies have often been developed as separate interventions. TXA-loaded MNs mainly improve local exposure to an anti-pigmentary drug. Exosome therapy is limited by the stratum corneum, skin penetration efficiency and uncertain local retention. Barbed MNs have generally been investigated for mechanical fixation and tissue interlocking, with less emphasis on combining lesion-responsive release with multidimensional microenvironmental regulation. The TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs developed here integrate barb-mediated mechanical anchoring, oxidative disassembly of the HAMA-PBA network, and complementary delivery of TXA and ADSC-derived extracellular vesicles within one local delivery platform. This design is not a simple addition of functions. It aims to improve local retention, promote drug release under oxidative conditions, and simultaneously intervene in melanogenesis, vascular abnormalities, inflammatory imbalance and oxidative stress, thereby regulating the pathological microenvironment of melasma more comprehensively.

In this study, H2O2 levels were increased in skin homogenates from the melasma model, further supporting oxidative stress as an important component of the pathological microenvironment. However, H2O2 levels measured in tissue homogenates should not be interpreted as the instantaneous local H2O2 concentration to which the MN matrix is exposed in vivo. H2O2 in tissue is subject to continuous generation, clearance, regional compartmentalisation and metabolism, and the measured values can be influenced by sampling time, tissue processing, extraction efficiency and the detection system. Therefore, the 0.03% H2O2 used in the in vitro degradation and release experiments was not intended to precisely mimic the absolute physiological concentration in melasma lesions. Rather, it served as an accelerated oxidative challenge to test whether the PBA-associated network could undergo sensitive cleavage, matrix disassembly and payload release under strong oxidative stimulation. The release differences observed between PBS and 0.03% H2O2 primarily demonstrate oxidation-stimulus responsiveness of the MNs and should not be directly equated with their true in vivo release kinetics. Future studies should use lower H2O2 concentration gradients, release systems that better approximate the skin tissue environment, and local drug concentration-time analyses to clarify the relationship between lesional redox status and drug release behaviour.

Platform-specific control experiments further suggested that the in vivo efficacy of the complete MN system was not determined solely by local delivery of TXA and ADSC-derived extracellular vesicles. Both non-responsive and non-barbed MNs partially improved pigmentation, vascular-related signals and macrophage-associated markers, indicating that local dual-drug delivery itself had therapeutic activity. However, the barbed TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN platform produced stronger overall effects on melanin reduction, MITF/TYRP1 downregulation, inflammatory cytokine improvement, uPA/VEGFA suppression and CD86/CD206-associated immune microenvironmental regulation. These findings support additional contributions from both the ROS-responsive matrix and the barbed structure. The HAMA-PBA network disassembled more rapidly in the oxidative lesion environment, thereby promoting local release of TXA and exosomes. The barbed structure may extend effective contact between MNs and lesion tissue by enhancing mechanical interlocking and patch stability. Together, these two design elements may allow the therapeutic payloads to more fully regulate oxidative, inflammatory, vascular and melanogenic networks in melasma.

Mechanistically, decreased MITF and TYRP1 expression was consistent with reduced melanin deposition on Masson-Fontana staining, indicating that the platform can suppress melanogenesis-related programmes in vivo. Decreased pro-inflammatory factors, including TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, together with improved IL-4 and IL-10 profiles and altered CD86/CD206 signals, support regulation of local low-grade chronic inflammation and macrophage-associated immune status. Decreased uPA and VEGFA expression, together with weaker CD31-positive vascular signals, suggests that the system may attenuate plasminogen activation-associated signalling and downstream abnormal angiogenic responses. Importantly, these results mainly represent associated pathway changes at the treatment endpoint. They do not fully establish causal order among mechanisms or precisely distinguish the relative contributions of TXA and exosomes at each pathological step. Future work should incorporate direct plasmin activity assays, cell-specific interventions, local drug exposure analysis and spatially resolved mechanistic studies to establish a more complete chain of action.

Several limitations remain. First, the in vivo treatment and observation period was 14 days. The current results mainly demonstrate short-term improvement of melasma-like lesions and early microenvironmental regulation by this MN platform. They do not show whether the platform can prevent repigmentation, maintain long-term lesion homeostasis or produce permanent microenvironmental remodelling. Melasma is chronic and relapse-prone. Whether pigmentation, vascular activation and inflammatory imbalance recur after treatment withdrawal requires longer post-treatment follow-up. Future studies should include observations on day 28, day 42 or later after treatment, combined with repeated UV/hormonal stimulation or rechallenge models, to assess whether melanin deposition, MITF/TYR-related proteins, angiogenic signals, oxidative stress and inflammatory factors rise again. Local skin reactions after repeated dosing, major-organ histology and coagulation-related indicators should also be included in long-term safety assessments.

Second, clinical translation of the composite MN system depends not only on therapeutic performance but also on quality stability during manufacturing, transport and storage. TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs contain a humidity-sensitive hydrogel network, dynamically rearrangeable PBA-associated groups and ADSC-derived exosomes that are sensitive to temperature and environmental stress. The short-term stability results indicate that storage at 4 °C, protected from light, dry and sealed, can preserve mechanical performance, barb anchoring capacity, TXA retention, exosome-like particle recovery, size characteristics, and antioxidant and anti-angiogenic function. However, the 14-day storage observation provides only preliminary evidence of short-term stability and cannot be treated as a formal shelf life or industrial quality standard. Long-term and accelerated stability studies are still needed. Future work should systematically evaluate packaging barriers against moisture and oxygen, sterilisation compatibility, transport vibration, batch consistency, retention of exosome markers and function, and MN sterility. For the exosome component, lyophilisation, protectant addition and buffer optimisation may be key directions for improving long-term stability.

In summary, TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs combine structural retention, oxidative microenvironment-responsive release and dual-payload synergy to improve pigmentation and regulate the early pathological microenvironment in melasma-like lesions over a short treatment period. Although their value for preventing repigmentation or relapse, ensuring long-term safety and enabling scalable translation remains to be validated, this platform provides a potentially useful design strategy for local integrated treatment of refractory hyperpigmentary diseases.

Conclusion and perspectives

The clinical recalcitrance of melasma arises from its multidimensional pathology. It is not merely a local pigmentary disorder, but a deeper disturbance of the dermal microenvironment marked by persistent oxidative stress, pathological angiogenesis and chronic inflammation. Traditional monotherapies often fail to provide durable relief because they focus mainly on melanin synthesis and do not sufficiently address the abnormal microenvironmental triggers. In this study, we designed a smart responsive dual-payload co-delivery platform, TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs, for local melasma therapy. The in vitro and in vivo results indicate that the platform works through asymmetric synergy. The ROS-responsive HAMA-PBA framework enables on-demand release aligned with the local oxidative microenvironment. Once released, TXA and ADSC-derived exosomes act through complementary routes. TXA mainly regulates cellular mechanotransduction and vascular remodelling, reducing expanded endothelial vascular networks and limiting downward migration of melanocytic cells. Exosomes act as bioactive mediators that attenuate ROS-related stress and promote macrophage polarisation from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype towards a tissue-reparative M2 phenotype. Together, these effects shift a pro-melanogenic pathological microenvironment towards a more homeostatic and repair-oriented state, producing depigmenting effects in a mouse model of melasma.

Future clinical translation of this smart MN system requires validation through longer follow-up, recurrence models and formulation studies. Advanced high-throughput approaches, such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), should be used to map cell-cell communication networks within the remodelled dermal microenvironment and to clarify deeper epigenetic and transcriptomic regulatory mechanisms. This bioinspired and stimulus-responsive transdermal delivery strategy may provide a translational therapeutic option for refractory melasma and a broader technical framework for microenvironment-driven skin and inflammatory diseases.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

The Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People's Hospital approved the experimental procedures involving female C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks old) and the isolation of ADSC-Exos from human adipose tissue (approval number 20250422249541).

Isolation, culture and identification of human adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs)

Human adipose tissue samples were obtained from healthy donors undergoing liposuction, with informed consent from the donors and ethical approval from Zhejiang Provincial People's Hospital (IRB). Collected adipose tissue was washed repeatedly with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Servicebio, China) containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Meilun, China) to remove erythrocytes and anaesthetic solution. The tissue was minced and digested enzymatically with 0.1% type I collagenase (Solarbio, China) for 60 min at 37 °C with gentle continuous shaking. An equal volume of α-MEM (Alpha Minimum Essential Medium; Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma, USA) was added to terminate digestion. The suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, and the stromal vascular fraction (SVF) pellet was collected. After erythrocyte lysis buffer (Solarbio, China) treatment and PBS washing, isolated cells were resuspended in complete medium consisting of α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and then seeded into culture flasks. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The medium was changed every 2-3 days, and passage 3-5 (P3-P5) cells were used for subsequent exosome isolation. The morphology of isolated human ADSCs was routinely observed and recorded using optical microscopy. To further validate the ADSC immunophenotype, specific surface markers were analysed by flow cytometry. Briefly, collected cells were washed and incubated in the dark with fluorophore-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against human surface markers, including the mesenchymal stem cell-positive markers CD90 and CD105 and the haematopoietic stem cell-negative marker CD34 (BioLegend, USA). Data were then acquired using a flow cytometer and analysed to determine cell purity. To confirm multilineage differentiation potential, ADSCs were seeded at defined densities and cultured in human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell osteogenic or adipogenic induction medium (Oricell, China), with media changed according to the manufacturer's instructions. At the end of the respective induction periods, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Alizarin Red S staining was then performed to detect calcium nodule deposition and confirm osteogenic differentiation, while Oil Red O staining was used to visualise intracellular neutral lipid droplets and confirm adipogenic differentiation.

Extraction and purification of ADSC exosomes (ADSC-Exos)

To collect high-purity ADSC-Exos, ADSCs were cultured until approximately 80% confluent and washed three times with sterile PBS to remove residual conventional FBS. Cells were then cultured for 48 h in α-MEM supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted FBS. Conditioned medium (CM) was collected and subjected to serial differential centrifugation at 4 °C to remove impurities. The CM was first centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to remove dead cells, then at 2000 × g for 20 min to remove cell debris, and then at 10,000 × g for 30 min to remove large extracellular vesicles and apoptotic bodies. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile membrane. Exosomes were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 90 min at 4 °C. To improve purity and remove co-precipitated proteins, the exosome pellet was resuspended in pre-cooled PBS and washed by ultracentrifugation again at 100,000 × g for 70 min. Purified ADSC-Exos were finally resuspended in a small volume of pre-cooled PBS and stored immediately at −80 °C until use. Particle size distribution and concentration of isolated ADSC-Exos were measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using a ZetaView nanoparticle tracking analyser (PMX-120, Particle Metrix, Germany). Before sample measurement, the instrument was calibrated using standard 100 nm polystyrene beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Fremont, CA, USA). ADSC-Exo samples were diluted appropriately in particle-free PBS to fall within the optimal detection range. Exosome concentration (particles/mL) and size distribution were recorded and analysed using ZetaView NTA software. Each sample was measured using the built-in automated batch process mode to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. The ultrastructure of isolated ADSC-Exos was characterised by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). To verify the exosome-specific protein expression profile, western blotting was performed to analyse the positive exosome markers CD9 (Proteintech, China), CD63 (Proteintech, China), CD81 (Abcam, UK) and TSG101 (Proteintech, China), as well as the negative endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin (Abcam, UK).

Preparation and characterisation of TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA microneedles

Preparation of precursor solutions

LAP (0.025 g; EFL, China) was dissolved in 10 mL PBS in an amber glass bottle to prepare a standard photoinitiator solution (0.25%, w/v). The mixture was heated in a 40-50 °C water bath for 15 min and vortexed three times to ensure complete dissolution. The required mass of HAMA (EFL, China) or HAMA-PBA (EFL, China) was then dissolved in the LAP stock solution to prepare 10% (w/v) HAMA precursor solution and 5% (w/v) HAMA-PBA precursor solution, respectively. The mixtures were stirred at room temperature in the dark for 0.5-1 h. After complete dissolution, precursor solutions were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 2-3 min to remove bubbles and sterilised through a 0.22 μm sterile syringe filter before use.

Design parameters of the dual-height microneedle mould

Custom polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) micromoulds were used to prepare MN patches. The mould contained a dual-height, staggered 15 × 15 array, with an overall base area of 14.5 mm × 14.5 mm. Tall MNs were 800 μm in length, with a base diameter of 400 μm and a horizontal barb extension width of 90 μm. Short MNs were 500 μm in length, with a base diameter of 250 μm and a horizontal barb extension width of 70 μm. The two MN types were arranged in a checkerboard pattern at a constant centre-to-centre spacing of 750 μm.

Two-step fabrication of drug-loaded bilayer microneedles

Drug-loaded MN patches were prepared using custom dual-height PDMS micromoulds and a two-step centrifugation-assisted micromoulding method. To prepare therapeutic formulations, tranexamic acid (TXA; Solarbio, China) and ADSC-Exos were added individually or together to 5% HAMA-PBA precursor solution to obtain three different needle-tip layer mixtures: TXA@HAMA-PBA, Exo@HAMA-PBA and co-loaded TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA. To optimise therapeutic efficacy and assess dose-dependent effects, defined concentrations of ADSC-Exos or TXA were mixed into the HAMA-PBA gel precursor matrix to prepare multiple drug-loaded MN formulations. In the exosome-only MN groups, ADSC-Exos were uniformly mixed into the matrix at final concentrations of 1 × 109, 5 × 109 and 10 × 109 particles/mL and were designated Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs, 5Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs and 10Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs, respectively. In the TXA-only MN groups, TXA was dissolved in the matrix at 10, 25, 50 and 100 mM and designated 10TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs, 25TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs, 50TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs and 100TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs, respectively. The mixed gel precursor solutions were then cast into PDMS moulds and crosslinked under UV light to form core-shell structured MN arrays. During fabrication, 100 μL of the specified drug-loaded HAMA-PBA solution (needle-tip layer formulation) was introduced into the PDMS mould under vacuum (EFL). The mould was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to ensure complete filling of the needle-tip microcavities and then initially crosslinked under UV light (365 nm) for 30 s. To form a mechanically robust backing layer, 300 μL of pure 10% HAMA solution (backing-layer formulation) was poured onto the mould. Centrifugation and UV crosslinking were then repeated. Finally, bilayer MN samples were dried overnight at room temperature in a vacuum desiccator. Fully dried MN patches were carefully peeled from the mould using tweezers, sealed and stored at 4 °C protected from light until use.

Morphological and mechanical characterisation of microneedles

The macroscopic appearance of MN patches was recorded using a digital camera (Canon, Japan). For microscopic morphology, MN arrays were imaged under bright-field mode using an optical microscope (Nikon, Japan). To further assess ultrastructural details, MN patch surfaces were sputter-coated with gold/platinum and imaged by scanning electron microscopy (ZEISS GeminiSEM 360, Germany).

The mechanical strength of MNs was evaluated using a universal testing machine (Instron 3366, USA). MN patches were placed flat on the testing platform, and a stainless-steel probe compressed the MN array vertically downward at a constant speed of 0.02 mm/min. Force-displacement curves were recorded continuously, and the array compression force at a specified deformation or at the endpoint of testing was recorded.

Ex vivo skin pull-out force test of microneedles

Fresh ex vivo mouse dorsal skin was flattened and fixed on the testing platform. MN patches were pressed vertically into the skin and maintained for 60 s to ensure adequate insertion and tissue contact. The MN patches were then pulled out vertically at a constant speed of 0.02 mm/min using a universal testing machine (Instron 3366, USA), while force-displacement curves were recorded continuously. The maximum pull-out force of each sample was recorded to evaluate the mechanical anchoring capacity of MNs in skin. Five independent samples were tested per group.

Ex vivo mouse skin penetration assay

To assess skin insertion ability, dorsal ex vivo skin from C57BL/6 mice was collected, and subcutaneous fat was carefully removed. MN patches were gently pressed onto the ex vivo skin and maintained for 5 min. The punctured skin was then stained with 0.4% trypan blue for 15 min to visualise the microchannel array. After patch removal, punctured skin tissue was immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded and vertically sectioned. Skin sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Masson-Fontana and observed under an optical microscope to visualise microchannel formation, penetration depth and the affected skin layers.

In vitro ROS-responsive degradation of microneedles

The degradation profile of MNs was assessed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; Leica STELLARIS 5, Germany) and mass-loss assays. For morphological degradation, FITC-labelled TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs and TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs were immersed in physiological buffer simulating an ROS microenvironment (0.03% H2O2). At predefined time points (0, 12, 24 and 48 h), MNs were removed and scanned by CLSM (Leica STELLARIS 5, Germany) using Z-stacks. Layered images were reconstructed into three-dimensional models to monitor progressive decreases in MN height and volume.

For degradation kinetics based on mass loss, pre-weighed lyophilised MN patches (W0) were immersed in 0.03% H2O2 solution and incubated at 37 °C. At defined time points, patches were removed, gently rinsed with deionised water, lyophilised and weighed accurately using an analytical balance (Wt). The remaining mass percentage was calculated as Wt/W0 × 100%.

In vitro ROS-responsive drug release profiles

A vertical Franz diffusion cell system was used to evaluate the in vitro release kinetics of TXA and ADSC-Exos from MN patches. TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN patches were carefully pressed onto a porous synthetic membrane and then fixed between the donor and receptor compartments of the diffusion cell. The donor compartment was kept open to simulate environmental exposure, while the receptor compartment was filled with PBS (pH 7.4) with or without 0.03% H2O2 to simulate ROS-rich pathological and physiological microenvironments, respectively. The system was maintained at 37 °C, approximating physiological skin temperature, under constant magnetic stirring (300 rpm). At predefined time points (0, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120 and 144 h), a fixed volume of receptor medium (1 mL) was withdrawn for quantitative analysis and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium at the same temperature to maintain sink conditions. TXA release concentration was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; WATERS e2695, USA), and NTA was used to measure cumulative ADSC-Exo release.

Chemical structure characterisation (FTIR analysis)

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used to confirm successful matrix synthesis and subsequent drug incorporation. Lyophilised samples of HAMA MNs, HAMA-PBA MNs, Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs, TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs and TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs were analysed using an FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS20, USA) over a wavenumber range of 4000-400 cm−1 to compare characteristic functional group changes among the five materials.

Short-term storage stability evaluation of TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs

Freshly prepared TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MNs were stored for 14 days at 4 °C protected from light under dry conditions, at 25 °C protected from light under dry conditions, or at 25 °C and 75% relative humidity. After storage, unstored fresh MNs were used as controls to evaluate mechanical performance, skin anchoring capacity, TXA retention, exosomal particle characteristics and functional activity. Compression force-displacement curves were recorded using the mechanical testing method described above, and the maximum compression force was measured. The maximum pull-out force of stored MNs in ex vivo skin was evaluated using the pull-out method described above. To assess TXA retention, stored intact MN patches were fully dissolved in PBS, TXA content was quantified using the HPLC method described above, and TXA retention after storage was calculated using the total TXA content measured in fresh MNs as 100%.

To evaluate exosomal particle characteristics and functional retention after storage, equivalent numbers of stored MN patches were fully dissolved or released in 5 mL PBS. The resulting supernatant was defined as the MN patch extract. NTA was then used as described above to measure the concentration, mode size and mean size of extracellular vesicle-like particles in the extract. The DCFH-DA assay was used to evaluate the ability of MN patch extracts from different storage conditions to suppress H2O2-induced intracellular ROS. The HUVEC tube formation assay was used to evaluate the anti-angiogenic function of MN patch extracts from different storage conditions. All extracts used for functional comparison were prepared using the same number of MN patches, extraction volume and treatment ratio.

Rheological testing

A rotational rheometer (Haake Mars40, Germany) was used to evaluate the viscoelastic properties of different hydrogel materials. Four materials were prepared: HAMA, HAMA-PBA, Exo@HAMA and Exo@HAMA-PBA. All groups used the same polymer concentration (10%, w/v) and the same photocrosslinking conditions. Exosome-containing groups received equivalent amounts of ADSC-derived extracellular vesicles. After crosslinking, materials were immediately transferred between parallel plates of the rheometer for testing. Strain sweeps were first performed to determine the linear viscoelastic region. Time sweeps were then performed at constant strain and angular frequency for 600 s, and the storage modulus (G′) was recorded. Frequency sweeps were performed at fixed strain over an angular frequency range of 0.1-100 rad s−1. All tests were performed at 4 °C. Three independent replicates were performed per group.

Theoretical estimation of TXA loading in the microneedle patch

Assuming a standard two-step casting process in which the drug is mainly concentrated in the needle shafts, the calculation is as follows. The MN bodies were approximated as cones for volume estimation, and the TXA precursor solution was 50 mM, equivalent to 7.86 μg/μL:

TXA molar concentration: 50 mM = 50 mmol/L = 0.05 mol/L.

Molecular weight (MW) of TXA: 157.2 g/mol.

Mass concentration of the drug solution: 0.05 × 157.2 = 7.86 g/L = 7.86 mg/mL = 7.86 μg/μL.

MN array: 15 × 15 = 225 microneedles, arranged in an alternating checkerboard pattern with 113 tall microneedles and 112 short microneedles.

Base area: 14.5 mm × 14.5 mm = 210.25 mm2 = 2.10 cm2.

(Because the barb volume is irregular and relatively small, we used a standard conical approximation for the loading estimate and stated this assumption in the response. In the classical two-step centrifugation/vacuum casting process, the drug is concentrated in the needle tips, whereas the backing layer is drug-free.)

Single-microneedle volume and drug loading:

Tall microneedle: radius r = 200 μm (0.2 mm), height h = 800 μm (0.8 mm).

Volume Vtall = 1/3 × π × r2 × h = 1/3 × 3.14 × 0.22 × 0.8 ≈ 0.0335 mm3 (that is, 0.0335 μL).

Drug loading per tall microneedle = 0.0335 μL × 7.86 μg/μL ≈ 0.263 μg.

Total drug load per patch and dose per unit area:

Cell culture

The four cell types used in this study, HaCaT, HUVECs, MNT-1 and THP-1, were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). All complete media were supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Meilun, MA0110, China). HaCaT cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Meilun, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma, USA). HUVECs were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Meilun, MA0215, China) supplemented with 10% FBS. MNT-1 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS. THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol (MedChemExpress, USA). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Uptake of ADSC-Exos by THP-1 macrophages or human melanoma cells (MNT-1)

To assess cellular uptake of exosomes, ADSC-Exos were first labelled with the red exosome tracer dye PKH26 (Solarbio, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions before being encapsulated into HAMA-PBA MNs, designated PKH26-ADSC-Exos@HAMA-PBA MNs. The MNs were then incubated in the corresponding cell culture medium for 24 h to allow exosome release. Exosome-enriched conditioned medium was collected and used to culture MNT-1 and THP-1 cells. After 6 h of co-incubation, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with cell fixative, stained for the cytoskeleton with FITC-labelled phalloidin (Actin-Tracker Green-488; Beyotime, China), and counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio, China). Cellular uptake of PKH26-labelled ADSC-Exos (red fluorescence) was observed and imaged using CLSM (Leica STELLARIS 5, Germany).

Live/dead cell viability assay

Cell viability and cytotoxicity were assessed by live/dead staining using a Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, C2015S, China). MNT-1 and HaCaT cells were seeded separately into the lower chambers of 12-well Transwell plates at a density of 1.2 × 105 cells/well and cultured until adherent. Cells were then assigned to experimental groups according to the study design. Transwell inserts were placed into the wells, and the corresponding MNs were placed in the upper chambers and fully immersed in serum-free medium for co-incubation. After the designated treatment time, cells in the lower chambers were washed with PBS. Calcein/PI working solution was prepared according to the kit instructions and incubated with cells at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Live cells (green fluorescence) and dead cells (red fluorescence) were then observed and imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus APX100, Japan).

In vitro cytotoxicity evaluation based on a transwell Co-culture system (CCK-8 assay)

Three human cell types, MNT-1 melanoma cells, HaCaT keratinocytes and THP-1 monocytes/macrophages, were used to evaluate the in vitro biocompatibility and cytotoxicity of MN patches. Cell viability was quantified in the Transwell co-culture system using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, China). MNT-1, HaCaT and THP-1 cells were seeded into the lower chambers of 24-well Transwell plates (0.4 μm pore size; Corning, 3422, USA) at 5 × 104 cells per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2 to allow attachment. THP-1 cells were induced to differentiate before treatment as required. Different MN formulations were then placed in the upper chambers of the Transwell system, allowing released components to diffuse freely through the porous membrane into the lower-chamber medium. Cells cultured in fresh medium without MNs served as blank controls. After co-culture for the specified time, inserts containing MNs were removed. The lower-chamber medium was discarded, and cells were washed twice with PBS. Fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent (100 μL per well) was added, and the plate was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Absorbance, expressed as optical density (OD), was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Tyrosinase activity assay

Tyrosinase is a key enzyme in melanin synthesis and catalyses oxidation of L-DOPA to dopachrome, which has a characteristic absorbance peak at 475 nm. Intracellular tyrosinase activity was measured using a tyrosinase activity assay kit (Boxbio, AKAM010C, Beijing, China). Collected MNT-1 cells were resuspended in the extraction solution provided with the kit and disrupted by ultrasonication on ice (20% power, 3 s sonication with 10 s intervals, repeated 30 times). Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the crude enzyme supernatant was collected. A 100 μL aliquot of supernatant was mixed thoroughly with 900 μL freshly prepared reaction reagent I in a cuvette. Absorbance at 475 nm was recorded accurately at 10 s and 190 s using a spectrophotometer at 37 °C. Tyrosinase activity was calculated from the absorbance change (ΔA).

Melanin content assay

Intracellular melanin content was quantified using an Amplite Fluorimetric Melanin Assay Kit (AAT Bioquest, 11310, USA). This high-sensitivity assay uses a specific substrate that reacts with melanin to generate a fluorescent product, with fluorescence intensity proportional to melanin concentration in the sample. According to the manufacturer's instructions, 50 μL cell lysate was transferred to a black 96-well microplate, and 50 μL signal enhancer was added and mixed thoroughly. After incubation for 30-60 min at room temperature in the dark, fluorescence intensity was measured with a fluorescence microplate reader at excitation/emission wavelengths of 470/550 nm, with a 515 nm cut-off. Melanin concentration in each sample was calculated using the standard curve provided with the kit.

Intracellular ROS scavenging assay in HaCaT cells

To evaluate the effects of TXA and ADSC-Exos on intracellular ROS levels in HaCaT cells, a DCFH-DA ROS probe (Solarbio, D6470, China) was used. Experimental groups included a blank control group, a high-ROS group in which HaCaT cells were treated with 1 mM H2O2 for 24 h, and treatment groups in which cells were treated with 1 mM H2O2 for 24 h and then co-incubated with MNs in Transwell inserts for 12 h. DCFH-DA fluorescence intensity, representing intracellular ROS levels in HaCaT cells, was recorded using CLSM (Leica STELLARIS 5, Germany).

In vitro radical-scavenging assays

The in vitro radical-scavenging capacity of different MN formulations was systematically assessed using commercial colorimetric assay kits. MN patches from each group were fully dissolved in an appropriate buffer to obtain sample extracts. ABTS radical-scavenging capacity was measured using a total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) colorimetric assay kit (Elabscience, E-BC-K219-M, China). Hydroxyl radical (·OH), DPPH radical and superoxide anion (·O2−) scavenging capacities were quantified using the corresponding assay kits (H931091-50 T/EA, Macklin, China; Elabscience, E-BC-K807-M, China; and Elabscience, E-BCK001-M, China, respectively). All assays were performed strictly according to the manufacturers' instructions. Absorbance of each reaction mixture was recorded at the specified wavelength for each kit using a microplate reader, and radical-scavenging rates were calculated accordingly.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was used to measure mRNA expression levels of M1 macrophage markers (CD80 and CD86) and M2 macrophage markers (CD206 and CD163). Total RNA was extracted from treated THP-1 macrophages using an EZ-press RNA Purification Kit (EZBioscience, B0004D, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (EZBioscience, USA). The resulting cDNA was amplified on an ABI 7500 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (EZBioscience, A0001, USA), with a final reaction volume of 20 μL. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in the table.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to quantify secretion of polarisation-related cytokines and assess the immunomodulatory capacity of MNs. THP-1 cells were seeded into the lower chambers of 6-well Transwell plates and induced to differentiate into macrophages using PMA (MedChemExpress, HY-18739, USA). Cells were then stimulated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; MedChemExpress, HY-D1056, USA) and 20 ng/mL interferon-γ (IFN-γ; MedChemExpress, HY-P7025, USA) to induce an M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype. MN patches were then placed in the upper chambers for continuous co-culture. After 24 h of treatment, culture supernatants from the lower chambers were collected and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. Levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10 and IL-4) in clarified supernatants were quantified using human-specific ELISA kits (Solarbio, China) strictly according to the manufacturer's instructions. Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm, and cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated in parallel.

Flow cytometry

After THP-1 cells were co-cultured with MNs in the Transwell system for 3 days, THP-1 macrophages were collected and analysed using an Agilent NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies, USA). To assess macrophage polarisation, a multicolour flow-cytometric gating strategy was used to quantify cell subsets based on surface marker profiles. Within the initial gate, M1 macrophages were defined and quantified as CD80+/CD206− cells, whereas M2 macrophages were identified as CD80−/CD206+ cells.

Western blot

Cells were cultured and treated according to the experimental design. Total protein was extracted from cell samples and protein samples from mouse skin using standard procedures. After separation by SDS-PAGE, proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010, USA) and blocked with 5% skim milk. Primary antibodies included MITF (Cell Signalling Technology, 12590 T, USA), TYRP1 (Cell Signalling Technology, 58193 T, USA), TNF-α (Proteintech, 17590-1-AP, China), uPA (Proteintech, 17968-1-AP, China) and VEGFA (Proteintech, 81323-2-RR, China). GAPDH (Affinity, AF7021, China) was used as the internal reference protein. Secondary antibodies included goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (Abcam, ab205718, UK) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (Abcam, ab205719, UK). Signals were detected using an Ultrasensitive ECL Kit (Oriscience, PD202, China) and captured with a multifunctional fluorescence and chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP, USA). Band intensities were analysed using ImageJ software.

Cell scratch assay

MNT-1 cells were divided into control and MN treatment groups, including HAMA-PBA MNs, 10TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs, 25TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs, 50TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs and 100TXA@HAMA-PBA MNs. When cells in the lower chambers of Transwell plates reached 95% confluence, a 200 μL pipette tip was used to scratch the cell monolayer to simulate a wound, and Transwell inserts were placed into the wells. The corresponding MNs were placed in the upper chambers and immersed in serum-free DMEM. Blank control wells received serum-free DMEM without MNs. Cell migration was imaged using an optical microscope (Nikon, Japan) at 0 and 48 h after scratching.

Transwell cell migration assay

MNT-1 cells were assigned to experimental groups according to the study design. A single-cell suspension of MNT-1 cells was prepared in serum-free medium and added to the upper chambers of Transwell inserts. The corresponding MNs were placed in the lower chambers and immersed in medium containing 2% FBS for 48 h of co-incubation. At the end of incubation, cells that had migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with cell fixative and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, G1062, China). Images were acquired using an optical microscope (Nikon, Japan), and cell migration was quantified using ImageJ software.

Tubular network formation assay

HUVECs were divided into experimental groups according to the study design. Matrigel (Corning, 356234, USA) was diluted 1:1 with culture medium. Diluted Matrigel (80 μL) was added to the lower chambers of 12-well Transwell plates and incubated for 30 min to allow gelation. Cells were then resuspended in serum-free DMEM and seeded onto Matrigel at 1.2 × 105 cells/well. Transwell inserts were placed into the wells, and the corresponding MNs were placed in the upper chambers and fully immersed in serum-free DMEM. Images were acquired at 12 h using an optical microscope (Nikon, Japan), and tubular structures were analysed using the angiogenesis plugin in ImageJ.

Cytoskeleton imaging

MNT-1 cells were seeded onto glass coverslips placed in the lower chambers of 24-well Transwell plates. After cell attachment, treatments were applied according to the experimental design. Transwell inserts were placed into the wells, and the corresponding MNs were placed in the upper chambers and fully immersed in serum-free medium for 24 h of co-incubation. After treatment, cells on coverslips were fixed with cell fixative, stained with Rhodamine-phalloidin (Solarbio, CA1610, China) to label the cytoskeleton, and counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio, S2110, China). Cytoskeletal structure and cell morphology were then imaged and analysed by CLSM.

Plasmin activity assay

Plasmin activity was measured using a Plasmin Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK617, USA). After HUVECs were co-incubated with MNs for 24 h, culture supernatants were collected. According to the kit instructions, samples were mixed with the specific plasmin substrate working solution and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Absorbance was then read at the wavelength recommended by the kit using a microplate reader. Plasmin activity was calculated from the plasmin standard curve and normalised to total cellular protein concentration. Results are expressed as relative plasmin activity. Four independent biological replicates were included per group.

Animal experiments

Establishment of a mouse model of melasma

Female C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks old) were acclimatised for one week. Before model induction, dorsal hair was gently removed using an electric shaver and depilatory cream. To establish the melasma model, mice received daily irradiation with a UV phototherapy device (Sigma, SH2B, China) for 40 min and simultaneous intramuscular progesterone injection at 20 mg/kg/day. This combined induction protocol was maintained for 28 days, until obvious and stable dark hyperpigmented patches formed on the dorsal skin and Masson-Fontana sections showed melanin granule deposition in the basement membrane region and dermis, indicating successful model establishment.

Quantification of local TXA in mouse skin

To evaluate local tissue exposure to TXA after MN administration, melasma-model mice received one 50 mM TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN patch on a fixed dorsal region. At 12, 24, 48 and 72 h after application, MN patches were removed, and the skin surface was gently wiped with pre-cooled PBS to remove residual MN material and drug that had not entered the skin. Full-thickness skin tissue from the MN-covered area was then collected using an 8 mm skin biopsy punch, and subcutaneous fat was carefully removed before wet weight measurement. Skin tissue was homogenised in pre-cooled extraction solution. Homogenates were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and analysed by HPLC to determine TXA content.

Measurement of H2O2 in mouse dorsal skin homogenates

To quantify local oxidative stress in melasma-like lesions, H2O2 levels in mouse dorsal skin homogenates were measured using a MyQubit Amplex Red Peroxide Assay Kit (Invitrogen, A22188) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After model induction, dorsal skin from healthy control and melasma-model mice was collected. Residual subcutaneous fat and attached blood were carefully removed, and tissue wet weight was recorded immediately. For each sample, 30 mg skin tissue was homogenised on ice in 300 μL pre-cooled 1 x reaction buffer or PBS without reducing agents. Homogenates were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected for detection. All sample-processing steps were performed protected from light. To ensure that samples fell within the quantitative range of the kit, skin homogenate supernatants were serially diluted. Each assay tube received 100 μL sample supernatant and 100 μL freshly prepared Amplex Red/HRP working solution, followed by incubation at room temperature in the dark for 5 min. Amplex Red/UltraRed stop solution (40 μL) was then added to terminate the reaction. Fluorescence was measured using a Qubit fluorometer according to the manufacturer's instructions. H2O2 concentration was calculated from the standard curve and normalised to total protein concentration measured by the BCA assay. Final results are expressed as nmol H2O2 per mg total protein.

In vivo microneedle treatment and macroscopic efficacy assessment

On the day of successful model establishment, mice were randomly assigned to the healthy control group, melasma model group, TXA@HAMA-PBA MN group, Exo@HAMA-PBA MN group, TXA/Exo@HAMA non-responsive MN group, TXA/Exo@Non-barbed HAMA-PBA MN group and complete TXA/Exo@HAMA-PBA MN group. The corresponding MN patch was applied to the dorsal hyperpigmented area by gentle thumb pressure for 5 min and fixed with a transparent waterproof dressing. To assess short-term efficacy after MN treatment, macroscopic lesion appearance was recorded before treatment (day 0) and on days 7 and 14 after treatment, followed by relevant histological and molecular analyses. Day 14 was defined as the short-term treatment endpoint for evaluating pigmentation improvement and early pathological microenvironmental regulation. Macroscopic fading of pigmentation was monitored longitudinally, and ImageJ software was used for quantitative analysis by calculating grayscale values of the lesion region relative to day 0 and to the healthy control group.

Histological and immunofluorescence analyses

On days 7 and 14 after treatment, representative mice from each group were euthanised by carbon dioxide, and dorsal skin tissue from the treated area was immediately collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and paraffin-embedded. Tissue sectioning, haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson-Fontana staining for melanin deposition, and immunofluorescence (IF) staining of skin sections for CD31 (angiogenesis), CD86 (M1 macrophages) and CD206 (M2 macrophages) were performed by Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Melanin clearance and the relative mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CD31, CD86 and CD206 were quantified uniformly using ImageJ software.

In vivo assessment of skin oxidative stress

To evaluate the ability of MNs to remodel the oxidative microenvironment in vivo, skin tissue collected on day 14 was weighed accurately and mechanically homogenised in cold PBS. Tissue homogenates were centrifuged to collect supernatants. Levels of the lipid peroxidation product malondialdehyde (MDA) and activity of the antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase (SOD) in skin tissue were quantified using commercial MDA and SOD assay kits (Solarbio, China) strictly according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Systemic biosafety evaluation

To comprehensively evaluate systemic biosafety of MN treatment in vivo, blood samples and major organs were collected from mice on day 14. Serum was isolated by centrifugation, and key biochemical indicators of liver and kidney function, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), were measured using an automated biochemical analyser. Major organs, including heart, liver, spleen, kidney and lung, were harvested, fixed, sectioned and stained with H&E to examine potential pathological changes, necrosis or inflammatory infiltration. These analyses were performed by Servicebio (Wuhan, China).

Bulk RNA sequencing

RNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from skin tissue samples (n = 3 for both melasma and control groups) using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quality and integrity were assessed using an Agilent 5300 Bioanalyser, and RNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies). Only high-quality RNA samples meeting the following criteria were used for library construction: concentration ≥20 ng/μL, total RNA >1 μg and RNA quality number (RQN) > 4.5.

RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep Ligation protocol (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Messenger RNA was isolated by poly(A) selection using oligo(dT) beads and fragmented into approximately 300 bp fragments. Double-stranded cDNA was then synthesised, with dUTP incorporated into the second strand to maintain strand specificity. After end repair, A-tailing and adaptor ligation, the second strand was digested using UDG enzyme. Final libraries were amplified by PCR and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus/DNBSEQ T7 series sequencer using paired-end 150 bp reads.

Quality control and read mapping

Raw paired-end reads were trimmed and quality-controlled using fastp (version 0.19.5) [64] to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality reads. The resulting clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 [65] in orientation mode. Mapped reads from each sample were then assembled using StringTie [66] with a reference-based approach.

Transcriptomic profiling and differential expression analysis

Gene expression levels were quantified as transcripts per million reads (TPM), and gene abundances were estimated using RSEM. Differential expression analysis between melasma and control groups was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.48.2) [67]. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) if they met the statistical thresholds of adjusted p value (padj) < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change >3. Differential expression results were visualised as a volcano plot using the R package ggplot2 (version 4.0.2).
