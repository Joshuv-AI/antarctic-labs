Final Technical Report

 Project Title: Deep Learning Methods for Symbolic Calculations in HEP

 Principal Investigator: Nobu Okada
 Institution: University of Alabama
 DOE Award Number: DE-SC0023713
                           Project Period: 11/01/2022 – 01/15/2026

Abstract
This project develops machine learning methods to accelerate symbolic calculations in high-energy
physics. Using sequence-to-sequence transformer models inspired by advances in natural language
processing, we constructed symbolic AI frameworks to predict computationally expensive quantities
such as squared amplitudes and cross-sections for Standard Model and Beyond the Standard Model
processes relevant to the Large Hadron Collider and its high-luminosity upgrade. Over the course
of the project, we demonstrated high accuracy in predicting results across QED, QCD, and elec-
troweak calculations, and explored extensions to longer symbolic expressions and matrix element
computations. These results establish a scalable approach to symbolic computation in high-energy
physics, with potential applications to precision calculations and matrix element–based searches
for new physics at current and future collider experiments.

Research Objectives
The objective of this project was to develop machine learning methods to accelerate symbolic
calculations in high-energy physics. In particular, the project aimed to leverage advances in natural
language processing and sequence-to-sequence transformer models to predict analytical expressions
for squared amplitudes, cross-sections, and related quantities for Standard Model and Beyond the
Standard Model processes relevant to the Large Hadron Collider and its high-luminosity upgrade.
 A central goal was to establish a scalable symbolic AI framework capable of handling increasingly
complex scattering processes, including those involving quantum electrodynamics (QED), quantum
chromodynamics (QCD), and electroweak (EW) interactions. The project also aimed to develop
physically meaningful data representations and training strategies to enable accurate and eﬃcient
symbolic computation using machine learning techniques.
 More broadly, the objective was to demonstrate that modern deep learning approaches can
provide a viable and eﬃcient alternative to traditional symbolic computation methods, thereby
enabling faster theoretical predictions for current and future high-energy physics experiments.

Work Performed
During the project period, we developed machine learning frameworks for symbolic calculations in
high-energy physics, focusing on the prediction of squared amplitudes from scattering amplitudes
using sequence-to-sequence transformer models.
 A major component of the work involved constructing physically meaningful datasets for Stan-
dard Model processes, including quantum electrodynamics (QED), quantum chromodynamics (QCD),
and electroweak (EW) interactions. Amplitude and squared-amplitude expressions were generated
using established symbolic computation tools and then processed into tokenized sequences suitable
for machine learning models. Special attention was given to preserving the physical structure of the
expressions, including index contractions and momentum dependence, while avoiding artificial sim-
plifications. In particular, external particles were restricted to on-shell states to eliminate redundant
configurations and prevent data leakage, resulting in more realistic and challenging datasets.
 We developed custom tokenization schemes based on the atomic components of quantum field
theory expressions, including matrices, indices, momenta, and particle labels. These tokenization
strategies enabled eﬃcient representation of symbolic expressions while retaining the necessary in-
formation for accurate reconstruction. To address the challenge of long sequence lengths, especially
in QCD processes, we implemented techniques such as decomposition of squared amplitudes into
additive terms, allowing the model to generate complex expressions in a structured and scalable
manner.
 Transformer-based encoder-decoder models were trained to map amplitude expressions to squared-
amplitude expressions across a range of scattering processes with diﬀerent numbers of final-state
particles. Separate models and training configurations were developed for QED, QCD, and EW
datasets, with careful optimization of model architecture, sequence length limits, and training
procedures.
 In addition to model development, we performed systematic benchmarking of model perfor-
mance across diﬀerent physics processes and levels of complexity. The work included the evalu-
ation of sequence-level accuracy, analysis of performance as a function of expression complexity,
and comparison with previous approaches. These eﬀorts established a consistent framework for
evaluating symbolic machine learning methods on physically meaningful problems in high-energy
physics.

Results and Discussion
The research carried out in this project progressed from initial feasibility studies of applying machine
learning to symbolic calculations in high-energy physics to the development of a scalable and
physically realistic framework for predicting squared amplitudes. Early work demonstrated that
sequence-to-sequence transformer models could successfully learn mappings between amplitude and
squared-amplitude expressions for relatively simple processes, establishing a proof of concept for the
application of modern deep learning techniques to symbolic computation in high-energy physics.
These initial studies guided subsequent developments in dataset construction, model design, and
evaluation strategies.
 Building on this foundation, we developed transformer-based symbolic AI frameworks capable

of handling a wide range of Standard Model processes, including quantum electrodynamics (QED),
quantum chromodynamics (QCD), and electroweak (EW) interactions. The models achieved high
token-level accuracy and strong performance in reproducing full symbolic expressions, particu-
larly for processes of moderate complexity. For QED processes, which involve relatively compact
expressions, the models achieved near-perfect accuracy in many cases. Electroweak processes also
exhibited strong performance, demonstrating the ability of the models to generalize across a diverse
set of interactions.
 In contrast, QCD processes presented significant challenges due to longer sequence lengths,
increased combinatorial complexity, and the presence of color structures. While the performance
for these processes was reduced compared to QED and EW cases, the results provide important
insight into the limitations of current transformer architectures and highlight key challenges for
future development of symbolic machine learning methods.
 A major outcome of this project is the development of physically realistic datasets and evaluation
strategies. By removing oﬀ-shell external particles and preserving detailed index structures, the
datasets avoid artificial simplifications and data leakage that can otherwise lead to overestimated
model performance. As a result, the reported accuracies provide a more reliable assessment of the
ability of machine learning models to learn physically meaningful symbolic relationships.
 To address the challenge of long symbolic expressions, particularly in QCD processes, we devel-
oped techniques such as decomposition of squared amplitudes into additive terms. This approach
enables the model to generate complex expressions in a structured and scalable manner, improving
its applicability to realistic high-energy physics calculations.
 Overall, the results demonstrate that machine learning provides a viable and promising approach
to symbolic computation in high-energy physics. The framework developed in this project serves as
both a practical tool for accelerating calculations and as a benchmark for evaluating future advances
in symbolic machine learning. These developments open new opportunities for applying artificial
intelligence to precision calculations and data analysis in current and future collider experiments.

Other Outputs and Contributions
This project contributed to the training and development of graduate students in the interdisci-
plinary area of machine learning and high-energy physics. In particular, Victor Baules, a graduate
student in particle physics theory, and Eric Reinhardt, a graduate student in the CMS high-energy
physics experimental group, were supported as research assistants and played key roles in the
development of symbolic machine learning frameworks, including dataset construction, model de-
velopment, and performance evaluation. Victor Baules completed his Ph.D. in December 2025,
with this project forming a significant component of his dissertation research.
 The composition of the research team, combining expertise in theoretical and experimental high-
energy physics, enabled the development of machine learning approaches that are both theoretically
grounded and relevant for collider applications.
 The project also involved collaboration with researchers working on related machine learning
methodologies. In particular, collaborative eﬀorts with P. R. Dinesh contributed to the explo-
ration of novel neural network architectures relevant to symbolic learning, complementing the main
objectives of the project.
 More broadly, the project fostered interdisciplinary collaboration between theoretical and ex-
perimental high-energy physics and machine learning, contributing to the development of new
approaches at the interface of physics and artificial intelligence. In addition, the project produced
datasets, model architectures, and software tools for symbolic calculations, providing a foundation
for future research and potential community use.

Summary
In this project, we developed machine learning methods for symbolic calculations in high-energy
physics, focusing on the use of sequence-to-sequence transformer models to predict squared ampli-
tudes from scattering amplitudes. Over the course of the project, we established a scalable symbolic
AI framework and demonstrated its eﬀectiveness across Standard Model processes, including quan-
tum electrodynamics, quantum chromodynamics, and electroweak interactions.
 A key achievement of this work is the development of physically realistic datasets and evaluation
strategies that avoid artificial simplifications and data leakage, enabling a more reliable assessment
of machine learning performance on symbolic tasks. The results demonstrate that modern deep
learning techniques can successfully learn complex symbolic relationships and provide a viable
approach to accelerating theoretical calculations in high-energy physics.
 Looking forward, an important direction for future research is the extension of these meth-
ods to more complex calculations, including the incorporation of higher-order eﬀects such as loop
corrections. Developing machine learning frameworks capable of handling such calculations would
significantly expand the applicability of symbolic AI in high-energy physics. Additional future work
includes improving model architectures for handling long symbolic sequences and exploring broader
applications to precision calculations and collider data analysis.
 Overall, this project establishes a foundation for the application of machine learning to symbolic
computation in high-energy physics and opens new avenues for integrating artificial intelligence into
theoretical and phenomenological studies.
