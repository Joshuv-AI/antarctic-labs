Pork and salmon diets differentially modulate lipid oxidation along the gastrointestinal tract in rats, with limited effects on gut microbiota
Authors: Núria Elias Masiques, Jo De Vrieze, Yannick Gansemans, Dieter Deforce, Filip Van Nieuwerburgh, Stefaan De Smet, Thomas Van Hecke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRed meat consumption has been associated with less favorable health outcomes, whereas fish intake is often considered beneficial. These differences may partly relate to variations in fatty acid composition and heme iron content, which can influence oxidative processes during digestion and thereby affect intestinal and systemic responses. This study investigated the effects of pork- and salmon-based diets, differing primarily in fatty acid profile and heme iron content but matched for macronutrient composition, on oxidative stress, gut microbiota, fermentation metabolites, and inflammation in rats. The pork-based diet supplied 2.3-fold more SFA and 14-fold more heme iron than the salmon-based diet, which in turn provided 14-fold more n-3 PUFA. Consumption of pork significantly increased propanal (+81%), hexanal (17-fold), and 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (27-fold) in stomach contents, and elevated thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS, +45%) in plasma. In contrast, salmon consumption raised TBARS in duodenal mucosa (+19%) and C-reactive protein (+30%) in plasma. Only subtle diet-related changes were observed in gut microbiota composition and fermentation metabolites, with no difference in fecal calprotectin. A small number of discriminant taxa were identified, including Clostridioides and Muribaculum in salmon-fed rats and Eubacterium fissicatena, Sellimonas, and Dielma in pork-fed rats, while valerate levels were higher in pork-fed rats. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that no individual genes remained significant after correction for multiple testing. Overall, pork and salmon diets differentially modulated lipid oxidation along the gastrointestinal tract, whereas their effects on gut microbiota were limited.Keywords: Gastrointestinal digestion, Lipid oxidation, Aldehydes, Polyunsaturated fatty acids, Heme iron, Rat model

Introduction

Meat and fish are important components of an omnivorous diet, providing high-quality protein, essential vitamins and minerals. Red meat, derived primarily from mammalian species, such as beef, pork and lamb, is distinguished by its higher heme iron content compared to white meat or fish. This classification requires careful consideration, as heme iron levels can vary substantially depending on the specific muscle type within a species (Keeton and Dikeman, 2017). Aside from heme iron, muscle foods of animal origin also differ in their fatty acid profiles. For example, salmon contains more polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), with a profile dominated by n-3 PUFA, whereas pork has lower PUFA levels that are primarily of the n-6 type. Epidemiological studies suggest that fish consumption may have protective effects against colorectal cancer (Aglago et al., 2020; Caini et al., 2022), whereas red and processed meat are associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer and various chronic diseases (Demeyer et al., 2016; Hill et al., 2024). Seafood is considered an essential part of a healthy diet, yet evidence for its benefits over terrestrial meat in preventing obesity, insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes remains inconsistent (Liaset et al., 2019). Hence, it is essential to identify specific dietary components contributing to these different physiological outcomes between muscle sources (De Smet and Vossen, 2016).

Oxidative stress, resulting from an imbalance between pro-oxidants and antioxidants, is a critical pathway for health and disease. The fatty acid composition of muscle foods is a key determinant of oxidation during in vitro gastrointestinal digestion (Steppeler et al., 2016). Heme iron, particularly abundant in red meat, may further stimulate oxidative reactions, but its pro-oxidant effect during digestion has been reported to be secondary to the influence of the fatty acid profile (Van Hecke et al., 2019). Oxidation of n-6 PUFAs leads to the formation of 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (HNE) and hexanal (HEX), whereas n-3 PUFAs are the primary substrates for the formation of 4-hydroxy-2-hexenal (HHE) and propanal (PROP). Malondialdehyde (MDA), a non-specific lipid oxidation product, can originate from the oxidation of both PUFA types. In vitro gastrointestinal digestion of pork has previously been shown to result predominantly in the formation of HNE and HEX, whereas salmon digestion yields higher levels of HHE, PROP and TBARS (Steppeler et al., 2016; Van Hecke et al., 2019, 2024). These oxidation products are partially absorbed into the bloodstream, with hydroxy-alkenals, such as HHE and HNE, in particular showing dual biological effects. At low levels, they may trigger adaptive antioxidant defenses, whereas exposure to higher levels can induce oxidative stress (Ishikado et al., 2013; Nègre-Salvayre et al., 2017). The threshold for toxic effects is lower for HNE than for HHE, indicating that n-6 PUFA-derived oxidation products may exert harmful effects at lower concentrations (Riahi et al., 2010).

Beyond oxidative stress, the gut microbiota plays a critical role in intestinal health by metabolizing dietary components and interacting with the gut mucosa. Heme iron has been implicated in gut microbiota alterations, promoting the growth of mucin-degrading Akkermansia muciniphila and sulfate-reducing bacteria, such as Desulfovibrionaceae, which produce metabolites such as hydrogen sulfide (H2S) or carbon disulfide (CS2) (Ijssennagger et al., 2015; Van Hecke et al., 2019, 2021). Hydrogen sulfide is hypothesized to disrupt the intestinal mucin barrier by reducing its disulfide bonds, potentially exposing colonocytes to harmful bacteria and toxins, leading to inflammation and oxidative stress (Ijssennagger et al., 2016). Long-chain PUFA, abundant in fish, fulfil essential physiological roles, e.g., as structural components of phospholipids in key biological membranes and as precursors to prostaglandins and other lipid mediators (Martín-Olmedo & and Jurado-Fasoli, 2025). Accumulating evidence from animal model studies suggests that the interplay between gut microbiota, n-3 PUFA, and the immune system supports the maintenance of intestinal health (Costantini et al., 2017). A higher n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio was seen in inflamed mucosa from ulcerative colitis patients, which correlated with the severity of inflammation (Pearl et al., 2014). Fang et al. (2022) attributed beneficial effects to EPA on experimental colitis in mice by elevating the abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila and reducing the abundance of Proteobacteria. These findings underscore the need for an in-depth understanding of how n-3 PUFAs influence microbial community composition and function.

Previous studies have compared the effects of fish proteins versus red or white meat, eggs, milk, or soy proteins on gut microbiota in rodents (Sivixay et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2015), as well as salmon versus cod (Bratlie et al., 2021), or lean seafood (e.g., cod or scallop) versus non-seafood proteins (meat, dairy, eggs) in humans (Schmedes et al., 2019). However, to our knowledge, no controlled dietary intervention has directly compared red meat and fatty fish consumption with respect to oxidative stress and gut health. In the present rat feeding study, it was hypothesized that inherent differences in fatty acid composition (n-6 vs. n-3 PUFA) and heme iron content in red meat (pork) and fatty fish (salmon) would differentially influence oxidative stress, gut microbiota composition, fermentation metabolites, and inflammatory responses, with salmon consumption expected to exert more favorable effects.

Material and methods

Chemicals

Standards for volatile analysis, carbon disulfide [180173], indole [I3408], p-cresol [C85751], dimethyl disulfide [471569] and phenol [33517]; and for oxidation-related compounds, including glutathione [G1404], HNE-DMA [H9538], HEX [115606] and PROP [64409] were purchased from Merck (Diegem, Belgium). The HHE [32060] was purchased from Sanbio B.V. (Uden, the Netherlands).

Experimental diets

Lean pork (mixed muscles) and lard were purchased as fresh as possible from a local meat retailer. Salmon loin (with and without skin) was purchased from a local supermarket, selecting the freshest available products based on packaging date. Meat and fish samples were manually chopped and minced in a grinder (Omega T-12), equipped with a 10 mm plate. The crude fat content (ISO 1444–1973) of pork and salmon was determined to equilibrate the fat content between diets. Lard was chopped and minced as previously described, and was manually added to pork and homogenized at a proportion of 15% meat weight. Culinary salt (2% w/w) was added to the meat and fish. Next, meats were packed in vacuum bags in equal weights and cooked in the oven until a core temperature of 70 °C was reached. Meats were cooled at room temperature and stored at 4 °C. The next day, meats (65% of the diet) were mixed with the other ingredients to obtain the experimental diets (Table 1). Diets were vacuum packed in daily portions (±120g) and stored at −20°.

Rat feeding study

The rat feeding experiment was conducted in accordance with the principles of laboratory animal care and the Belgian law on the protection and welfare of animals. The experiment was approved by the Ghent University Ethical Committee (ECD 21-68). Twenty male Sprague-Dawley rats (±200 g) (Janvier Laboratories, Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France) were housed by two rats per cage during an adaptation period of one week, in environmental conditions of 22.0 ± 0.6 °C, 75 ± 5% humidity and 15 h daylight. During this adaptation period, rats were fed a standard laboratory diet (Ssniff R/M−N pellets) (Ssniff, Soest, Germany) and water was provided ad libitum. After the adaptation period, rats were randomly assigned to the experimental diets, which were provided fresh each day and offered ad libitum. Body weight and feed intake were monitored every 2 days at cage level. On day 17, rats were individually housed for fecal collection during 24h. At the end of the experiment (21 days on experimental diets), rats were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane gas, followed by blood collection by cardiac puncture into heparin tubes until death occurred. Plasma and red blood cells (RBC) were immediately separated by low-speed centrifugation and divided in different aliquots. Organs (brain, colon mucosa, duodenal mucosa, heart, kidney, liver) and fat deposits (mesenteric and retroperitoneal) were carefully rinsed with 0.9% NaCl solution, weighed and divided into aliquots. The colon length was measured, and the tissue was subdivided. The colon mucosa of the proximal half was collected and stored as such for the analysis of oxidation parameters, whereas the colon mucosa of the distal half was stored separately for the analysis of the transcriptome. One cm in the middle of the colon was stored in a 4% aqueous formaldehyde solution for histology. All aliquots were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Chemical analysis of meats & diets

Contents of dry matter (ISO 1442–1973), crude protein (ISO 937–1978), crude fat (ISO 1444–1973) and crude ash (ISO 5984–2002) were determined in the pork and salmon diets. Lipids were extracted using chloroform/methanol (2:1, v/v), and subsequently, fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) were prepared using methanolic NaOH (0.5 M) and methanolic HCl (1:5, v/v). The FAMEs were analyzed by gas chromatography (HP6890, Agilent Technologies, Brussels, Belgium) (Raes et al., 2001). Nonadecanoic acid (C19:0) was used as an internal standard to quantify the fatty acids. Levels of total SFA (sum of C8:0, C10:0, C12:0, C14:0, C15:0, C16:0, C17:0, C18:0, C20:0 and C22:0), MUFA (sum of C14:1, C16:1, C17:1, c9-C18:1, c11-C18:1, and C20:1), n-3 PUFA (C18:3n-3, C20:3n-3, C20:4n-3 and C20:5n-3), ALA (C18:3n-3), long chain (LC) n-3 PUFA (sum of C20:4n-3, C20:5n-3, C22:5n-3 and C22:6n-3), n-6 PUFA (C18:2n-6, C18:3n-6, C20:2n-6, C20:3n-6 and C20:4n-6), LA (C18:2n-6) and LC n-6 PUFA (sum of C20:2n-6, C20:3n-6, C20:4n-6, C22:4n-6, C22:5n-6) were calculated. Heme iron was determined spectrophotometrically in the pork and salmon samples, and subsequently calculated for the diets (Hornsey, 1956).

Oxidative stress and oxidation parameters

Concentrations of protein carbonyl compounds (PCC) in diets and stomach contents were determined spectrophotometrically following reaction with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine according to Ganhão et al. (2010). Levels of free HNE, HEX, HHE and PROP in diets and stomach contents were detected by HPLC-fluorescence (Agilent 1200 Series, Waldbronn, Germany) following their derivatization with cyclohexanedione, as described by (Van Hecke et al., 2017a). For the analysis of TBARS and GSH-Px activity, phosphate buffer (pH 7, 50 mM) was added to the frozen tissues in a 1:5 ratio (w/v). Samples were homogenized by Ultra-Turrax, and centrifuged (10 min, 12.000g, 4 °C). The supernatant was filtered through glass wool and transferred into different aliquots for immediate determination of GSH-Px activity and TBARS. The measurement of GSH-Px activity in plasma and organ extracts occurred by the oxidation of NADPH, whereby one unit of GSH-Px activity was defined as the amount of extract needed to oxidize 1 μmol NADPH/min at 25 °C (Hernández et al., 2004). The total (free + bound) TBARS fraction was measured in diets, stomach contents, plasma, and tissue extracts, by measuring the absorbance at 532 nm following the reaction with 2-thiobarbituric acid and quantified with a standard curve with 1,1,3,3-tetramethoxypropane, as described by Grotto et al. (2007). The concentrations of glutathione in the red blood cell (RBC) fraction were determined by HPLC using γ-glutamyl glutamate as an internal standard (Degroote et al., 2012). Alpha-tocopherol in the diets was extracted using ethanol and hexane and quantified by reversed-phase HPLC with UV detection at 292 nm (Claeys et al., 2016).

Microbiota composition in the colon

DNA extraction of colonic contents of rats was performed by means of bead beating using a PowerLyzer (Qiagen, Venlo, the Netherlands) and phenol/chloroform extraction (Vilchez-Vargas et al., 2013). The DNA extract was sent to LGC Genomics GmbH (Berlin, Germany) for Illumina amplicon sequencing of the V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene of the bacterial community on a MiSeq platform with V3 chemistry using primers 341F (5′-CCT ACG GGN GGC WGC AG -3′) and 785Rmod (5′-GAC TAC HVG GGT ATC TAA KCC-3′). The total number of bacteria in rat feces was quantified by qPCR (De Vrieze et al., 2015). The DADA2 R package was used to process the amplicon sequence data according to the pipeline tutorial (Callahan et al., 2016). In a first quality control step, primer sequences were removed and reads were truncated at a quality score cut-off (truncQ = 2). Additional filtering was performed to eliminate reads containing any ambiguous base calls or reads with high expected errors (maxEE = 2,2). After dereplication, unique reads were further denoised using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm (DADA) error estimation algorithm and the selfConsist sample inference algorithm (with the option pooling = TRUE). The obtained error rates were inspected and after approval, the denoised reads were merged. Finally, the ASV table obtained after chimera removal was used for taxonomic assignment using the Naive Bayesian Classifier and the DADA2-formatted SILVA v138 (Quast et al., 2012). The raw FASTQ files of the rat experiment have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (Accession number PRJNA1254479).

Fecal and cecal volatile organic compounds

Volatile organic compounds in rat feces were analyzed as described earlier (Elias Masiques et al., 2025). In brief, volatiles were extracted using a solid-phase microextraction (SPME) and separated and detected by a gas chromatography-mass spectrophotometer (GC–MS) (Thermo, Finnigan). Peaks were integrated for area quantification by targeting the quantifier ion as follows (m/z): indole 117; phenol 94; x-cresol 108; carbon disulfide 76; methanethiol 48; dimethyl disulfide 94; dimethyl trisulfide 126. Results were expressed as area under the curve (AUC) per g feces. Branched-chain fatty acids (iso-butyrate and iso-valerate) and short-chain fatty acids (acetate, propionate, butyrate and valerate) in cecal contents of rats were measured by gas chromatography (HP 7890A, Agilent Technologies, Diegem, Belgium), equipped with a flame ionization detector and a Supelco Nukol capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Sigma-Aldrich, Diegem, Belgium) as described previously (Gadeyne et al., 2016). In brief, a 10% formic acid solution containing 2-ethyl butanoic acid as internal standard was added to the cecal content. Following centrifugation (22.000g at 4 °C), the supernatant was filtered and transferred into a glass vial, followed by injection on the GC. Ammonia was measured spectrophotometrically at 625 nm in the same supernatant of the cecal content, with a calibration curve of NH4Cl following the reaction with phenol, sodium nitroprusside, sodium hydroxide and sodium hypochlorite (Chaney and Marbach, 1962).

C-reactive protein, calprotectin and low-density lipoprotein

Quantification of plasma C-reactive protein (CRP) (RAB0097, Merck, Diegem, Belgium), fecal calprotectin (S100A8/S100A9, Sanbio KR6936, Uden, The Netherlands) and plasma low-density lipoprotein (LDL) (E-EL-R0579, Elabscience, Texas, USA) were performed using commercial ELISA kits and measured on a microplate reader (Infinite M Nano, Tecan, Grödig, Austria), according to manufacturers’ instructions.

Histology

Colonic tissue samples were fixed in a formaldehyde solution and processed in an automatic tissue processor under standard conditions (Shandon, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). In brief, tissues were cut into small and homogeneous sections to be dehydrated and embedded in paraffin wax. Next, the embedded tissue was trimmed and mounted onto microscope slides. Histological staining was performed on paraffin-embedded colonic sections with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining to assess crypt depth and number of goblet cells per crypt. A minimum of four sections of colon tissue were assessed, with at least 20 well-oriented crypts evaluated per animal.

Transcriptomics

Total RNA was extracted from colonic mucosal tissues using the Total RNA Fatty and Fibrous Tissue kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, California, USA) in an RNase-free environment. RNA quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, whereas RNA integrity was evaluated using the Experion automated electrophoresis station with the Experion™ RNA StdSens kit (Bio-Rad, Temse, Belgium). The RNA was stored in a −80 °C freezer until shipment to NxtGent (Ghent University, Ghent, Belgium) for sequencing. Two samples were excluded due to low quality of the RNA, checked using the Agilent DNF-472 HS RNA (15 nt) Kit on a Fragment Analyzer 5200 system (Agilent). Sequencing libraries were constructed using the QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq V2 Library Prep Kit FWD for Illumina (Lexogen) and the UMI Second Strand Synthesis Module for QuantSeq FWD (Illumina) and sequenced as single-read 75 on an Aviti device (Element Biosciences). The unique molecular identifier (UMI) and spacer were removed using UMI-tools (v1.1.6) (Smith et al., 2017). Raw sequencing reads were inspected using FastQC (v0.12.1) for their quality and length (Wingett and Andrews, 2018). Samples were screened for potential contamination using FastQ Screen (v0.15.3). Adaptor trimming was done using cutadapt (v4.9) with added filtering of reads containing ambiguities or not passing the phred score threshold of 20 (Martin, 2011). The quality of the remaining reads was checked using FastQC. Trimmed reads were aligned on the rat reference genome (Rattus_norvegicus.mRatBN7.2, ENSEMBL release 113) using the splice-aware STAR (v2.7.11b) mapper (Dobin et al., 2013). UMI-based deduplication of mapped reads was done with UMI-tools (v1.1.6). Feature counting at the gene level was done using rsem-calculate-expression (RSEM v1.3.1) (Li and Dewey, 2011).

Statistical analysis

Feed intake was recorded per cage and analyzed using the cage as the experimental unit, with feeding day as the repeated factor. For graphical presentation, cage-level feed intake was divided by two and expressed as estimated feed intake per rat per day. Body weight was analyzed using a repeated-measures ANOVA with individual rats as the experimental unit and feeding day as the repeated factor. Normality and homogeneity of variance were determined by Shapiro-Wilk and Levene's test, respectively. When the data met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, a mixed model ANOVA procedure was used with the fixed effect ‘diet’ and the random factor ‘euthanasia day’. When data normality and homogeneity of variance was violated, an independent samples Kruskal-Wallis test with pairwise comparisons was performed, with ‘diet’ as independent variable. For all statistical tests, p ≤ 0.05 was considered significant.

Differences in colonic bacterial communities were identified using LEfSe (linear discriminant analysis effect size) (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/galaxy) between dietary treatments as the main class under the following conditions: 1) alpha values for the factorial Kruskal–Wallis test among classes, and for the pairwise Wilcoxon test among subclasses were < 0.05; 2) the threshold on the logarithmic LDA score for discriminative features was set to 2.0, and 3) the strategy for multi-class analysis was all-against-all (Segata et al., 2011). The α-diversity of the microbiota was assessed by determining the Shannon and Inverted Simpson indexes. The β-diversity, by using Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity metric, and Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) to test statistical significance, using the Rstudio packages vegan (Oksanen et al., 2013) and ggplot (Wickham, 2016). Spearman correlation analysis was performed to explore the associations between discriminant bacterial genera identified through LEfSe. P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, using the Rstudio packages corrplot (Wei and Simko, 2010) and circlize for the Chord diagram (Gu et al., 2014).

For transcriptomic data, statistical analyses were done in R (v4.4.1) using the edgeR (v4.2.2) package for differential gene expression (Robinson et al., 2010). The preferred and more robust quasi likelihood model including ‘euthanasia day’ as batch effect was used. Pairwise comparison at the gene level was done using the F-test for a more reliable error rate. Correction of the p-values for repeated testing (PAdj) was done with the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Gene-level fold changes were used for pathway enrichment analysis based on KEGG pathways, using the GAGE (v2.54.0) package (Luo et al., 2009). Resulting P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Results

Diet composition

The dry matter, crude protein, crude fat and crude ash contents were very similar between the two experimental diets, as intended (Table 1). The main differences between the diets lay in their fatty acid profile and the contents of heme iron and α-tocopherol. The pork-based diet contained 2.3-fold higher SFA levels, whereas the salmon-based diet had nearly 2-fold higher total PUFA content, particularly n-3 PUFA (14-fold higher), but also slightly higher n-6 PUFA (+18%). Consequently, the n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio was 12-fold higher in the pork diet. As expected, the heme iron content was markedly greater in the pork diet (14-fold higher). In contrast, α-tocopherol levels were 3-fold higher in the salmon diet.

Animal performance

Rats fed the salmon diet exhibited a lower feed intake, particularly on the first day of the trial (−14%), but this difference levelled off during the following days, resulting in a modest overall reduction in total feed intake (−3%; Fig. 1). Nevertheless, final body weight was not altered by the diets. Interestingly, the brain weight was higher in rats consuming the salmon diet compared to those on the pork diet (+8%, p = 0.049). No significant differences were observed in the weight of other organs or adipose tissue deposits (Table S1).

Average feed intake and body weight of rats during the feeding period, and brain weight at the end of the trial.

Dietary and gastrointestinal oxidation products and oxidative stress markers

The salmon diet contained 5-fold higher PROP levels and 27% higher TBARS, whereas HEX levels were 5-fold higher in the pork diet, and HNE was only detected in the pork diet (Table 1). The PROP levels in stomach contents were 32- and 3-fold higher in rats consuming pork and salmon, respectively, compared to their corresponding diets (Fig. 2), whereas HHE levels were 2-fold increased in the stomach of pork-fed rats, and 30% in salmon-fed rats, relative to the dietary levels. Similarly, the stomach contents of rats on the pork diet contained 4- and 10-fold greater levels of HEX and HNE, respectively, compared to their diets. In contrast, HEX levels in the gastric contents of rats fed the salmon diet remained similar to their dietary levels, while HNE levels were detected at low levels (Table S4). Consequently, gastric HEX (62.5 vs. 3.75 μg/g) and HNE (4.79 vs. 0.18 μg/g) concentrations were 17- and 27-fold higher, respectively, in pork-fed than in salmon-fed rats (both p = 0.001). Interestingly, PROP levels were also higher in the stomach contents of rats on the pork diet (+81%, p = 0.017), despite being higher in the salmon diet before consumption. Although TBARS levels were not different in the stomach content between treatments, higher levels were found in the duodenal mucosa of the salmon-fed group (19%, p = 0.039). In contrast, rats fed the pork diet had higher TBARS levels in plasma (+45%, p = 0.002). The GSH-Px activity in heart tissue was higher in the pork group (+8%, p = 0.038), whereas a lower activity was seen in kidney (−10%, p = 0.039), compared to the salmon group. No significant differences were observed in HHE or TBARS levels and GSH-Px activity in other organs, nor in PCC levels in the stomach contents (Table S4).

Significant levels of n-3 (PROP) and n-6 (HNE, HEX) oxidation aldehydes in the diet and in stomach content, and oxidative stress markers in duodenum, plasma, heart and kidney tissues.

Colon microbiota composition

The microbial community of the colon contents is illustrated in Fig. 3. No significant differences were observed in bacterial density (p = 0.684), α-diversity metrics (Shannon index, p = 0.54; Inverted Simpson, p = 0.593 or β-diversity (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity metric: PERMANOVA R2 = 0.843, p = 0.568) (Fig. 3B). Overall, the colonic microbiota composition was similar between dietary groups. The predominant phyla were Bacteroidota (median abundances of 38% pork vs. 36% salmon), followed by Verrucomicrobiota (29% in both groups) and Firmicutes (23% pork vs. 29% salmon). Desulfobacterota was less abundant (4% pork vs. 3% salmon) (Fig. 3C). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) identified five bacterial taxa at very low relative abundances that significantly differed between diets (Fig. 3D). The Eubacterium fissicatena (median 0.0% (range 0.00% - 1.95%) in pork vs. not detected in the salmon group, LDA = 3.49, p = 0.030), Sellimonas (median 0.12% (range 0.00% - 0.38%) pork vs. median 0% (range 0.00% - 0.08%) salmon, LDA = 3.06, p = 0.002), and Dielma (median 0.03% (range 0.00% - 0.16%) pork vs. not detected in the salmon group, LDA = 2.70, p = 0.005) were more discriminant in the pork-fed group, whereas Clostridioides (median 0.13% (range 0.00% - 0.63%) salmon vs. median 0% (range 0.00% - 0.13%) pork, LDA = 3.35, p = 0. 007) and Muribaculum (median 0.03% (range 0.00% - 0.21%) salmon vs. median 0% (range 0.00% - 0.03%) pork, LDA = 2.78, p = 0.014) were discriminant for the salmon-fed group. Correlation analysis revealed six significant associations among the identified significant bacteria taxa (Fig. 3E–Table S2). In brief, the strongest positive correlation was between Dielma with Eubacterium fissicatena (r = 0.737, p = 0.002) and with Sellimonas (r = 0.573, p = 0.017). The strongest negative correlations were between Clostridioides with Dielma (r = −0.690, p = 0.004) and Sellimonas (r = −0.619, p = 0.012).

Consistent with the microbial composition, only minor differences were observed in bacterial fermentation metabolites. Plasma CRP levels were significantly higher in the salmon-fed group (+30%, p = 0.037), whereas fecal calprotectin (p = 0.648) and plasma LDL levels (p = 0.186) were not significantly altered by diet (Fig. 5).

Histology

No significant differences were seen for the crypt depth (252 vs. 253 μm; SEM = 1.91; p = 0.358) or goblet cell number per crypt (28.3 vs. 31.0; SEM = 5.17; p = 0.545) in colonic tissue of rats fed the pork or salmon diet, respectively.

Transcriptomics

In the initial analysis, 762 genes were differentially expressed between diets at the nominal level (p < 0.05). However, no genes remained significant after Benjamini–Hochberg correction (PAdj > 0.05). An exploratory KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was nevertheless performed using the fold changes of all detected genes to identify potential biological processes that may warrant further investigation (Fig. 6). This exploratory analysis indicated enrichment of immune-related pathways in the pork-fed group, including Th1, Th2, and Th17 cell differentiation, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, primary immunodeficiency, and the intestinal immune network for IgA production. In contrast, the salmon-fed group showed enrichment of pathways related to metabolism (e.g., thermogenesis, protein digestion and absorption, mineral absorption, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease), as well as neurodegeneration-related and cardiac function pathways. Discussion

Pork and salmon diets differentially modulate gastrointestinal lipid oxidation and associated oxidative stress responses

The higher TBARS and PROP levels in the salmon-based diet compared to the pork-based diet are consistent with the well-known susceptibility of fish muscle to lipid oxidation during storage, largely driven by fish hemoglobin and high n-3 PUFA content (Aranda IV et al., 2009). In contrast, the higher levels of HEX and HNE in the pork diet may reflect the combined effects of a higher heme iron content and a fatty acid profile characterized by a higher n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio. This interpretation is in line with previous findings showing that TBARS and PROP formation is associated with LC n-3 PUFA, whereas HEX and HNE are more strongly related to the n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio rather than absolute n-6 PUFA levels (Van Hecke et al., 2019).

Gastric TBARS were not affected by the dietary treatment. Instead, pork-fed rats exhibited higher levels of PROP, HEX, and HNE in stomach content, suggesting enhanced in situ aldehyde formation during the gastric phase. Gastric lipid oxidation is promoted by low pH and the presence of dissolved oxygen, conditions that promote lipid peroxidation, particularly in the presence of catalytic heme iron (Gorelik et al., 2008; Van Hecke et al., 2017b). The higher heme iron content in pork may therefore drive Fenton-like reactions and the formation of reactive oxygen species, resulting in increased aldehyde formation during the gastric phase despite the lower n-3 PUFA availability in the diet. Although propanal is generally considered a marker of n-3 PUFA oxidation, its absolute formation is influenced by both substrate availability and the intensity of the oxidative environment. Under highly pro-oxidative gastric conditions, enhanced oxidation may therefore result in increased propanal formation despite a lower n-3 PUFA content. Conversely, the 3-fold higher levels of α-tocopherol and likely presence of other antioxidants, such as astaxanthin (Bell et al., 2000; Kenmogne-Domguia et al., 2012), potentially mitigated oxidative reactions in the stomach of salmon-fed rats. However, this protective effect may be overridden during intestinal digestion, where the release of highly unsaturated fatty acids, such as EPA and DHA, provides substrates that are particularly susceptible to peroxidation. Indeed, during in vitro gastric digestion of beef and salmon, HHE levels were comparable, but increased significantly from gastric to intestinal phase, with higher levels observed in salmon than in minced beef (Steppeler et al., 2016). In accordance, we have previously observed higher levels of n-3 PUFA oxidation products in various fish species compared to a variety of meat digests after the small intestinal phase (Steppeler et al., 2016; Van Hecke et al., 2019; Van Hecke and De Smet, 2021). This suggests that enhanced intestinal oxidation may occur in vivo in salmon-fed rats, potentially contributing to the increased TBARS levels observed in the duodenal mucosa of these rats. Similar increases in fecal TBARS and urinary MDA have been reported in rats following consumption of fish-based diets (Steppeler et al., 2017) or fish oil combined with heme iron (Guéraud et al., 2015).

Whether these compartment-specific oxidative processes translate into systemic effects depends on the extent to which lipid oxidation products are absorbed during digestion. The absorption of lipid oxidation products in the bloodstream of rats following gastrointestinal formation has been demonstrated following the consumption of turkey meat (Gorelik et al., 2008). Likewise, the consumption of oxidized n-3 PUFA leads to intestinal absorption of reactive aldehydes such as HHE, which can induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses both locally and systemically (Awada et al., 2012). In line with this, the increased TBARS levels observed in the duodenal mucosa of salmon-fed rats may reflect enhanced intestinal oxidative processes. The higher plasma CRP levels observed in the salmon-fed group may indicate a modest systemic inflammatory response. However, despite these increased intestinal oxidative markers in the salmon-fed group, plasma TBARS were higher in pork-fed rats, while fecal calprotectin did not differ between groups, indicating that the relationship between oxidative and inflammatory markers remains unclear. It is plausible that the higher levels of TBARS observed in pork-fed rats may stimulate antioxidant defense mechanisms, such as the increased GSH-Px activity in the heart. However, the absence of a consistent response across tissues, including the opposite trend observed in the kidneys, indicates that these effects are likely tissue-specific and complicates a straightforward interpretation of systemic antioxidant responses.

Salmon and pork diets induce only subtle changes in gut microbiota, metabolites, and host responses

In the present study, no clear shifts in the whole microbial community and gut metabolites were observed between rats fed the salmon or pork diets. While LEfSe identified 5 taxa discriminant between the groups, these were found in low abundances, with no clear biological interpretation. This is in line with previous work in whole-food models, where minimal microbiota changes were reported in mice fed seafood-rich vs. lean meat-rich diets, with only modest shifts in Bacteroides spp. and Robinsoniella spp. (Holm et al., 2016). In contrast, studies using isolated protein fractions have reported more pronounced differences. For instance, Zhu et al. (2015) compared the impact of extracted proteins from beef, pork, chicken, and salmon on the gut microbiota composition in rats, and found that Lactobacillaceae were more abundant in salmon-fed rats (36%) compared to pork-fed rats (19%), while Ruminococcaceae were more abundant in the pork group (27%).

Similarly, studies investigating dietary fat composition have reported inconsistent effects on gut microbiota. Fish oil (vs. lard) increased the relative abundance of Proteobacteria and Desulfovibrio spp. in both cecal and colonic contents of rats, alongside elevated inflammatory markers in the colon (Li et al., 2017). On the other hand, n-3 PUFA supplementation to a beef-rich diet helped restore the gut microbial balance by inhibiting the growth of potentially pathogenic bacteria, such as Desulfovibrio spp. and Escherichia–Shigella (Park et al., 2023). Fjære et al. (2019) found that dietary fat content and voluntary exercise had a stronger influence on gut microbiota than the source of protein itself, though certain taxa, such as Desulfovibrionaceae, were more abundant in salmon-fed mice than in those fed beef.

Consistent with the limited effects observed for the gut microbiota and fermentation metabolites, transcriptomic analysis of the colonic mucosa also provided limited evidence for diet-dependent transcriptional differences. Exploratory KEGG pathway enrichment analysis suggested enrichment of immune-related processes in the colon tissue of pork-fed rats, whereas salmon-fed rats showed enrichment of metabolic and other biological pathways. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as they are based on pathway enrichment despite the absence of significant differentially expressed genes after multiple-testing correction, and should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Larger studies may be required to detect more subtle transcriptional differences between dietary groups. Notably, these observations were not accompanied by differences in fecal calprotectin or histological markers, suggesting limited effects on intestinal inflammation under the conditions tested.

In conclusion, pork and salmon intake differentially affected lipid oxidation along the gastrointestinal tract. While pork consumption promoted gastric and plasma lipid oxidation, the elevated n-3 PUFA content in salmon was associated with increased duodenal lipid oxidation. Salmon-fed rats also exhibited higher plasma CRP levels, whereas fecal calprotectin remained unchanged. Despite these differences in lipid oxidation, the colon microbiota composition and associated metabolites exhibited only subtle differences between diets.
