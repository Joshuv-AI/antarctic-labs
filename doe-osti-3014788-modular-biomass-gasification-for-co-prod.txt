Executive Summary
The overall goal of this project was to demonstrate a novel process-intensified and modular
Combined Hydrogen, Heat and Power (CH2P) production technology, targeting commercial
scales of 5-to-10-megawatt electric (MWe) equivalent of hydrogen (H2). The process technology
integrates a novel adaptation of the steam-iron process to produce high purity H2 from
gasification of biomass and biomass blends with a compression-ready carbon dioxide (CO2)
stream, enabling significant progress towards achieving $1 per kilogram (kg) cost for clean H2.

The process addresses the challenges to small-scale modular H2 production by:

 • Use of a novel iron-based material with multi-functionality (oxygen carrier material,
 OCM) that combines H2 production and CO2 separation (process intensification).
 • Adopting a commercially available small-scale gasification system. For this project, the
 low cost, modular bed gasification system patented i by Singularity Energy Technologies
 (SET) and known as the SMART Gasifier was evaluated as the source of synthesis gas
 (syngas).
 • Completing a preliminary heat mass balance (HMB) integration of the gasification
 process and synthesis gas conversion process for biomass feed rates of 50 to 100
 tonnes/day (tpd).

Figure 1 is a block flow diagram of the process showing oxygen-blown gasification of the
biomass sunflower hulls.

Figure 1. Schematic of CH2P Process

 1

Syngas from the gasification of biomass (sunflower hulls was the biomass selected for model
development) is fed sequentially to both CH2P-A and B vessels (Figure 1), both loaded with the
OCM enriched in iron oxides. Both vessels contain a Syngas to Hydrogen (S2H) reactor and a
Syngas to Carbon Dioxide (S2C) Reactor, and the inlet syngas is first fed to the S2H reactor.
Each step is designed to operate in a semi-batch mode; gases are switched between vessels A and
B.

Partially oxidized syngas leaving the S2H reactor of the CH2P-A vessel, is oxidized in the S2C
reactor of the CH2P-B vessel producing CO2 and water. Steam and air are used to oxidize the
S2H and S2C producing H2 and heat respectively. The S2H and S2C reactors in each vessel are
thermally coupled to ensure autothermal operation of the process.

This project focused on evaluating the following technical objectives to facilitate development of
the CH2P process:
 • Demonstrate that the OCM can produce high purity H2 and compression-ready CO2 and
 can manage trace syngas impurities effectively (Task 2 and 4).
 • Characterize the OCM cyclic stability, phase transformations and the inhibition potential
 of syngas trace impurities (sulfur, chloride and volatile organic compounds) on
 performance (Task 2).
 • Test a novel integrated laboratory unit that will convert a biomass fuel feed to synthesis
 gas under oxygen-blown operation and convert the raw synthesis gas to high purity H2
 using the OCM (Task 4).
 • Demonstrate and identify key operating conditions of the next generation oxygen-blown
 SMART gasifier technology (Task 3).
 • Perform a preliminary techno-economic analysis (TEA) of the process, including
 evaluation of H2 purity, preliminary process HMB, and equipment sizing to identify a
 pathway towards $1/kg H2.

Two OCMs were evaluated for use in the S2H reactor – a perovskite based OCM known as P-
OCM and a patentediv hematite-based OCM known as FEH31. The FEH31 OCM was developed
under the Department of Energy funding agreement FE0031534. ii The active ingredient of the P-
OCM is a strontium-doped lanthanum ferrite perovskite, La0.6Sr0.4FeO3 (LSF641).

LSF641 was successfully manufactured using both a wet chemistry method (Pechini method)
and a mechanical mixing method. The Pechini method achieved higher purity LSF641 while the
mechanical method used lower cost raw materials. Performance testing of the LSF641 powders
and P-OCM pellets established it was not suitable for the S2H reactor given its rate-limited water
splitting performance but showed promise for use in the S2C reactor. Consequently, FEH31 was
selected for use in both the S2H and S2C reactor. Task 2 provides more details on the
manufacturing and evaluation of LSF641.

Parametric and extended testing was completed for FEH31 with simulated syngas (Task 2) and
actual syngas (Task 4) to determine the optimal operating conditions including, operating

 2

temperatures, kinetics, mass transfer and cyclic life. The following recommendations and
conclusions were determined from testing:
 • Optimal operating temperature for the CH2P process is 740 – 850°C.
 • The S2H reactor should be designed as a fluidized bed to improve mass transfer rates.
 • The H2 production capacity of the FEH31 was established as 2 – 4 kg H2 per tonne OCM.
 • Minimizing the extent of reduction of FEH31 (minimize formation of pure iron) will
 extend the cyclic life of the OCM.
 • Correctly sizing the S2H reactor is critical to achieving high purity production of H2 and
 CO2.
 • No additional tar clean-up is needed when tar concentrations in the syngas is less than 5
 mg/Nm3.
 • Future testing with a higher fidelity experimental set-up is recommended to fully quantify
 extended cyclic performance of the OCM with actual syngas.

A HMB was completed for a facility producing 160 kilograms per hour (kg/h) of H2 from
sunflower hulls at an 85% capacity factor (CF). ASPEN Plus ® simulation software was used to
complete the HMB. The design adopted was based on the process summarized in Figure 1. A
combined heat and power gas engine was included in the design and the partially oxidized
syngas leaving the S2H reactor was split between both the gas engine and the S2C reactor. This
configuration ensured production of steam for the S2H oxidation step and power for auxiliary
needs. The design also included a heat recovery steam generator (HRSG), recuperators and
coolers for condensing steam and cooling the hot exhaust streams.

The plant performance summary is included below in Table 1. In addition to the 160 kg/h of H2
and 400 kg/h of CO2, net power of 1.4 MW electricity is produced by the gas engine along with
steam for drying purposes. The design assumes the facility will be built next to a sunflower hull
source with an operational life of 30 years. The facility’s size of 100 tpd corresponds to less than
20% of North Dakota sunflower hull production in 2020. The gasifier island will consist of two
50 tpd SMART gasifiers with preliminary cost estimates provided by SET.

and the sale of low-pressure steam (~$0.1 million). With the tipping fee and sales revenue, the
O&M costs of the facility are fully covered. see Table 3.

Recommendations for Future Work

The activities completed during this award advanced the CH2P concept from a technology
readiness level (TRL) 2 to TRL 4. For further maturation of the technology to a higher TRL, the
following activities are recommended:
 • Thermal Integration: Rigorous heat transfer modelling coupled with experimental studies
 to validate the thermal coupling between S2H and S2C reactors and ensure autothermal
 operation.
 • Impurity Testing: Higher fidelity testing to assess the long-term impact of trace
 impurities in syngas on OCM degradation.
 • Reactor Design: Future designs should prioritize fluidized beds and ensure reactors are
 appropriately sized to maintain purity of product gases.
 • Front-End Engineering Design (FEED): Completion of above activities will justify
 completion of a FEED to update the TEA of the process.

The Technology Readiness Assessment (TRA) system evaluated by the TMP was the CH2P
system, defined as a semi-batch system for converting syngas to H2, CO2 and power in a two-

Three critical technology elements (CTE) were identified for the CH2P system:
 • The Syngas to Hydrogen (S2H) reactor concept, proposed for conversion of the syngas
 to H2 in a two-step process. In the first step, syngas is partially oxidized to CO2 and
 water, by reduction of the selected OCM. In the second step, H2 is produced by the

 • The Syngas to Carbon Dioxide (S2C) reactor concept, proposed for complete
 conversion of the partially oxidized syngas leaving the S2H system during step 1 of the

 the S2H reactor was successfully demonstrated using laboratory scale equipment
 advancing this CTE to a TRL 4.
 • Thermal Integration of the S2H and S2C CTE units. A HMB and preliminary reactor
 design of the S2H and S2C reactors was completed using ASPEN Plus® simulation

Task 2 – Development and Evaluation of Novel Oxygen Carrier Materials:

This task focused on the development (Subtask 2.1) and evaluation (Subtask 2.2 and 2.3) of two
OCM’s for use in CH2P reactors – the FEH31 OCM and the P-OCM. The FEH31 is a patented
OCMiv developed under a previous Department of Energy funding agreement (FE0031534ii)
with hematite (Fe2O3) as the active ingredient. The active ingredient of the P-OCM is a
strontium-doped lanthanum ferrite perovskite, La0.6Sr0.4FeO3 (LSF641).

P-OCM and FEH31 were planned for use in the S2H reactor, while only FEH31 was planned for
the S2C reactor. During evaluation and testing, it was determined the P-OCM was not suitable
for the S2H and only the FEH31 was evaluated for the S2H reactor.
The FEH31 is a patentediv low-cost and recyclable OCM which is manufactured using a
mechanical-mixing, high temperature calcination method. Existing FEH31 material was screened
to a particle size ranging from 0.8 to 2mm and characterized using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to obtain a baseline composition and morphology of the
material prior to evaluation in Subtasks 2.2. 2.3 and Task 4.

 9

The novel perovskite P-OCM, manufactured using the LSF641 perovskite was proposed with the
expectation of achieving higher syngas conversion to H2, as demonstrated by de Leeuwe et al., iii
who reported 95% conversion, exceeding thermodynamic limitations for iron-only oxidation
with steam. Manufacturing of the P-OCM was successfully completed in Subtask 2.1 and the
material evaluated in Subtask 2.2.

LSF641 Synthesis via Pechini Method: The Pechini Method is a modification of the solution gel
(sol-gel) method. It involves the formation of metal complexes aided by an esterification
reaction, which occurs between a carboxylic acid like citric acid and a polyalcohol like ethylene
glycol. The citric acid also serves as a chelating agent. When compared to the sol-gel method,
the esterification reaction helps to delay the decomposition of organics and promote the growth
of a polymer network.

Tar Sampling

The European Tar Protocol (ECN-C-06-046) x was adopted as the tar sampling method for
biomass gasification following a literature review. For the impinger train, six impinger bottles of
250 ml were used, each containing an isopropanol solution of 100 ml. Impinger bottles 1, 5, and
6 contained 6mm diameter glass beads. Impingers 1, 2, 3, and 4 were placed in a hot bath at
40ºC, while impingers 5 and 6 were placed in a cold bath at a temperature of -20ºC using a
chiller. Utilizing both hot and cold baths is key to trap operation: at higher temperatures, the
isopropanol evaporates and condenses in the chilled impingers promoting condensation of the tar
vapors.

Immediately after sampling, the contents of the impinger bottles were decanted into a designated
storage bottle (Figure 75). These samples were maintained at a controlled temperature below 5°C

 67

to ensure their integrity prior to filtration and analysis by gas chromatography with flame
ionization detector (GC-FID).

the carrier gas.

Subtask 4.3 – Integrated System Testing

Initial shakedown testing focused on validating the standalone performance of the S2H unit
under actual gasification conditions using pressed sunflower hulls, gasified for approximately 70
minutes total. During this period, syngas was diverted to the S2H reactor, where it was initially

The operating procedure adopted is summarized below:
 1. The gasifier was operated until steady state at a solids feed rate ranging from 0.2 – 0.5
 lb./hr. Oxidant flow rates totaled 1.1 SLPM. The lower flow was due to the high RP of
 the syngas determined in Task 3.
 2. The syngas produced was analyzed using an ARI Laser Gas Analyzer and vented.
 3. Reduction: all the syngas was diverted to the CH2P for a set period to reduce the OCM
 (reduction step). The duration varied from half a minute to 8 minutes across the 50 tests.
 4. At the end of reduction, the syngas was diverted back to the analyzer and/or vent, and the
 CH2P unit isolated for regeneration (oxidation step).
 5. The isolated CH2P unit was connected to the gas analyzer and purged with N2 prior to the
 start of oxidation.
 6. Oxidation: The S2H reactor was first regenerated with steam to produce H2. When the
 H2 peaked, the steam was turned off and the S2H purged with N2.
 7. Next the S2C was regenerated with air until O2 breakthrough, except for Cycle 41 to 50
 where the S2C reactor was first oxidized with steam to confirm syngas slip from S2H,
 before oxidation with air.
 8. At breakthrough, the next cycle resumed from step 3.

A summary of all 50 tests is provided below:

Cycle 0 – 20: The S2H and S2C reactors were initially loaded with 25 grams of FEH31 each.
During the reduction step, the CO and H2 exiting the S2C reactor was greater than 0%, implying

 81

the CH2P system was undersized and incomplete syngas conversion was occurring. Meanwhile,
H2 was successfully produced during each oxidation half-cycle.

Cycle 21 – 40: To address poor conversion in the S2C reactor, the OCM loading was increased
to 50 g. An increase in CO2 exiting the CH2P reactor was observed, but the concentration of CO
and H2 was still significant (> 5 vol.%). It was determined that the amount of FEH31 OCM
loaded in the S2H is still too small and the RP of syngas entering the S2C is higher than 0.65
(see Figure 33, RP higher than region above operating box will produce Fe) and converting the
Hematite to Wüstite and iron. Because Magnetite reduction by CO/H2 is equilibrium controlled,
any significant reduction of Magnetite in the S2C makes it challenging to produce high purity
CO2. To mitigate this, the reduction duration was dropped to 30 seconds during cycling.
However, because the integrated system has a gas residence time of over 1 min and can be
considered a well-mixed reactor, the 30-second reduction duration created challenges with gas
purity analysis due to back-mixing.

Cycle 41 – 50: For these cycles, the OCM in the S2H was increased to 39 g, and a N2 purge
included for Cycle 50 during the reduction step to minimize back mixing effects. With these
changes the CO2 purity (N2-free basis) reached a high of 97% with less than 3% of CO and H2
combined, see Figure 91. Per the results, the CO2 met the purity target set for the experiments,
however, future development with a higher fidelity system is needed to ensure no CO and H2 is
present in the CO2 product.

The schematic of the process is shown in Figure 96 with two reactor vessels – 1A and 1B, each
containing and S2H and S2C reactor. Operation of the CH2P is defined by the following steps:

 • The cold inlet syngas is preheated by hot partially oxidized syngas leaving the S2H
 reactor in the first vessel (1A).

 • In vessel 1A, during syngas oxidation in the S2H, air is fed to the S2C reactor to provide
 the heat for the endothermic syngas reduction.

 • The partially oxidized and cold syngas leaving the S2H is then split; a portion is sent to a
 gas engine to make power and steam for the process, while the balance is fed to the S2C
 reactor of the second vessel (1B) where it is fully converted to CO2.

 • Steam is produced by the gas engine and superheated by the hot H2/steam mix leaving the
 S2H (1B).

 • In vessel 1B, steam is fed to the S2H reactor to make the H2 during complete oxidation of
 the syngas.

 • At the end of a cycle, the gases are switched from vessel 1A to 1B.

TEA Summary

Completion of the TEA marked the completion of Deliverable B and provided a clear pathway
toward the DOE’s target of $1/kg for clean hydrogen, while highlighting significant
dependencies:
 • Feedstock and Tipping Fees: The Levelized Cost of H2 (LCOH) is estimated at $2.0/kg
 with a tipping fee of $51/tonne for sunflower hulls, but this jumps to $3.40/kg without
 such fees.
 • CAPEX Dominance: Capital expenditures account for 70% of the LCOH. The $1/kg goal
 would require reducing CAPEX from the estimated $25 million to approximately $16
 million through grants or value engineering.
 • Carbon Capture: The system achieves a carbon capture efficiency of 48%, with the use of
 sunflower hulls. A complete life cycle analysis (LCA) is required to determine the net
 carbon footprint of the produced H2.
