 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The American-Made FLoating Offshore Wind ReadINess (FLOWIN) Prize provides the
 Participant with a voucher technical support provided by DOE national laboratories, such as the
 Contractor. The primary goals include providing insights for the INO/INOC floating offshore
 wind platform on 1) an evaluation of the workforce requirements for the full-scale production
 and deployment; and 2) an assessment of domestic supplier capabilities for the main components
 US regional and national supply chain constraints and gaps.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Uses the laboratory’s core competencies
 • Enhances U.S. competitiveness by utilizing DOE developed intellectual property and/or
 capabilities.

 Summary of Research Results:

 NLR assessed the workforce considerations and supply chain considerations for the INO/INO15
 technology, a floating semi-submersible offshore wind substructure. This technical assistance
 was in support of the FLoating Offshore Wind ReadINess (FLOWIN) Prize Phase 2 and 3
 submissions.

 As part of the analysis, NLR researchers evaluated the workforce needs for the full-scale
 production and deployment of the INO/INOC technology to help to inform workforce
 takeaways. The workforce needs for processes were evaluated at four California locations,
 including San Diego (fabrication), Long Beach and Oakland (assembly) and Humboldt County
 (integration). The analysis focused on the feasibility to train relevant occupations across these
 processes using local training programs. Additional workforce analysis was conducted on the
 Port of Long Beach across all phases of INO/INOC deployment including pre-
 development/development, floating substructure manufacturing, pre-assembly/assembly,
 construction and installation, port operations, and operations and maintenance. In addition, San
 Diego was a focus for manufacturing workforce needs. A method was developed to evaluate
 workforce risk levels based on the key metrics such as workforce availability, transportation
 access, existing transferrable skills in offshore wind, and existing education and training program
 availability. Workforce development plans that highlighted key occupational need and training
 information were created for Technip Energies USA, Inc. to share with key stakeholder groups.

 For the supply chain analysis, NLR conducted interviews with companies including steel
 distributors, forges, foundries, ports, large component fabricators, subcomponent fabricators, and
 secondary suppliers. Insights included port requirements, the barriers and opportunities to
 establish new ports, and the barriers and opportunities to reduce final assembly time. In addition,
 information was provided on the existing West Coast fabricators and whether they are currently
 capable of fabricating subcomponents for floating offshore wind energy platforms as well as
 domestic manufacturers capacities for steel and secondary components.

 Task 1: Assessment, Identification, and Solutions for Utilizing a Domestic Supply Chain

 The Contractor shall complete an assessment of the capacity to supply materials, provide
 services, and meet fabrication requirements for the INO15 technology. The Contractor shall
 specifically identify the capacity to provide materials (e.g., steel) and manufacture the INO15
 using a domestic fabricator. The Participant shall provide technical and engineering designs
 specific to the INO15 technology to support the Contractor in assessing potential material and
 fabrication companies. The scope includes identifying potential suppliers of materials across the
 United States but the fabrication of sub-blocks proximate to a geographic location of interest for
 final assembly. As funding allows additional regions across the United States may be considered.
 Work activities under this Task 1 include:

 • Identify the major components, sub-blocks, parts, and materials in the INO15 hull and
 their technical specifications.
 • Identify suppliers with the potential to provide steel and indirect materials.
 • Identify businesses with the potential to fabricate the INO15 FOWT.
 • Consider the services and logistics for final assembly, including turbine integration and
 • installation.
 • Assess the supply chain gaps and constraints for domestic production.
 • Propose potential solutions to resolve the known gaps in the domestic supply chain.

 NLR provided Technip Energies USA, Inc. with 1) an assessment of domestic supplier
 capabilities for the main components of their floating offshore wind platform design and 2)
 analyzed US regional and national supply chain constraints and gaps. The Technip design has
 flat panel and tubular features, uses welded connections, and can utilize a sub-block approach for
 modularity. The columns for these FOW platforms are a tubular design and pre-welded flat panel
 connections. Pontoons and deck beams are flat panel with widths that range depending on the
 component. As part of this analysis, NLR conducted 13 interviews with companies including
 steel distributors, forges, foundries, ports, large component fabricators, subcomponent
 fabricators, and secondary suppliers. This assessment was documented in a report to Technip
 Energies USA, Inc. Key insights from this supply chain assessment include:

 • Final assembly ports are the key infrastructure barrier standing in the way of unlocking
 the domestic assembly and component fabrication for steel-based FOW platforms.
 • There will likely be a need for additional development of 100+ acre port facilities given
 that demand for floating offshore wind deployment on the West Coast.
 • There are numerous hurdles to consider when attempting to establish a new port on the
 West Coast that could be used for FOW platform assembly, such as lengthy and complex
 permitting processes.
 • This design utilizes welded connections which could result in increased assembly times
 due to weld time, finishing, and inspection when compared to designs featuring pinned,
 bolted, or flanged connections primarily.

 3

 • Final assembly time dictates the number of concurrent builds that will be needed to
 deliver one platform per week. There are multiple opportunities to streamline/reduce the
 amount of time needed, including integrating learnings that are achieved through the
 fabrication of multiple orders, and the early development and implementation of process
 lanes that utilize automated procedures.
 • Domestic fabrication of subcomponents for this design can likely be achieved today
 through a coordinated approach that utilizes the capacity of existing fabrication resources
 in and out of the West Coast region.
 • Flat panel designs align with current West Coast capabilities because of the region’s
 experience with shipbuilding. This type of design can take advantage of the cutting
 capabilities of steel distributors who can deliver cut-to-order steel (i.e., subcomponent
 assembly kits) which can be used to reduce fabrication time.
 • Tubular designs that are fabricated on the West Coast will likely require additional
 equipment investments since many West Coast fabricators have limited, if any, rolling
 capabilities.
 • Column fabrication is a unique process that requires significant welding given the
 component’s size and the need for reinforced steel. This fabrication process is typically
 compared to shipbuilding which does align with current regional capabilities.
 • Interviewees stated that domestic steel mills are essentially capable of producing any
 standard of steel necessary (including European standards or equivalent), although it may
 cost more if the platform design calls for a less common steel grade.
 • Steel (welding) and paint trades, but equipment (crane and SPMT) operators, welding
 inspectors, naval architects, and other skilled tradespeople will be needed. Interviews
 indicated that facilities could need between 500 and 1,000+ workers, depending on
 component design and onsite activities.
 • To optimize designs and processes, account for lead times and workforce training, and
 encourage capital investments, interviewees expressed the need for early and consistent
 engagement between potential ports, fabricators, and suppliers.

 Task 2: INO15 fabrication workforce considerations

 The Contractor shall complete an assessment of the workforce requirements for the full-scale
 production of the INO15 technology. The Participant shall share knowledge on the production
 processes and requirements to assist in the workforce analysis. The scope includes fabrication
 and assembly of the INO15 hull and the workforce to install the project.

 • Estimate the direct workforce to fabricate the INO15 hull.
 • Provide indirect job requirement estimates from supply chain utilization.
 • Estimate the workforce to install the INO15 hull.
 • Identify the necessary skill sets and credentials for installation and fabrication workers.
 • Assess the training programs and initiatives needed, with a focus on increasing diversity,
 and benefiting marginalized and historically underrepresented communities near a
 geographic location of interest for final assembly.

 Technip Energies contracted with NLR to evaluate the workforce requirements and training
 feasibility for the fabrication, assembly, and integration processes of the INO15 technology. A
 high-level workforce needs assessment based on the Task 2 scope was conducted in four
 California locations including San Diego (fabrication), Long Beach, Oakland (assembly), and
 Humboldt County (integration). This assessment was achieved through:

 • Providing local demographics metrics for each process as it pertains to workforce
 availability.
 • Collecting and creating a registered apprenticeship program training list for each
 geographic location of interest.
 • Compiling a database of skills and certifications required for skilled trades occupations in
 each process.
 • Validating the number of workers needed.

 NLR employed a workforce assessment method to gather localized data for each location of
 interest and analyzed it to identify key insights, which were shared with Technip Energies USA
 Inc. to support their future workforce development initiatives. The locations were selected based
 on discussion and selection by Technip Energies USA Inc. based on their investigation of
 feasible process areas. Technip Energies USA Inc. also provided NLR with technology design
 specifications and processes documents that informed the selection of worker types and training
 needs for each process. To understand each location’s current education and training landscape,
 information on the relevant education and training programs was compiled into a database.
 Insights into the current demographic, economic, education and workforce metrics for each
 community were also provided to guide possible investments in education and training program
 development and inform targeted recruitment and hiring efforts. Finally, NLR completed an
 occupation and training alignment, identifying the specific occupations and certifications
 required for each process. With this information, NLR evaluated the feasibility of aligning the
 certifications and training available through existing programs with those needed for fabrication,
 assembly, and integration processes.

 As a result of this analysis, it was found that there were varying levels of feasibility to train the
 necessary skilled trades occupations identified at each location. Understanding each area’s
 strengths and weaknesses is crucial to developing and refining strategic plans to develop a
 workforce for floating offshore wind energy. Insights include:
 San Diego (fabrication)

 • It was indicated in the workforce assessment that 33% of the skilled trades occupations
 identified for the fabrication process did have existing registered apprenticeship programs
 that could feasibly train needed occupations with limited adjustments to the current
 offerings.
 • Welders were identified as the primary occupation needed in this process.
 • Occupations identified as not feasible to train through existing education programs
 include machine operating welder, rigger, banksman, and SPMT or crawler crane
 operator.
 • Apprenticeship Readiness Programs and RAPs located within communities could be
 potential partners in building up a workforce for Technip Energies, while also allowing
 for economic benefits to return to communities.

 7

 Long Beach (assembly)

 • Long Beach has a robust foundation of existing support from local stakeholder groups,
 areas such as creating new registered apprenticeship programs and partnering with local
 economic and workforce development boards require attention and intentional effort.
 • It was determined that there is potential to develop a qualified workforce for assembly in
 Long Beach; however, further work to specialize the workforce for floating offshore
 wind is still required.
 • Approximately half of the necessary occupations for the assembly process in Long Beach
 could be supported through the existing education and training programs.
 • Training programs for electricians have good representation in the area.
 • Four identified occupations lacked a program to fill the workforce including rigger,
 banksman, welder foreman, and rigger foreman.

 Oakland (assembly)

 • Registered apprenticeship programs did not cover a wide range of occupations, indicating
 there will need to be additions to the existing education and training programs for skilled
 trades positions.
 • Through the occupational alignment, it was found that approximately half of the
 necessary occupations for the assembly process could not be supported through the
 existing education and training programs.
 • Occupations without support from existing programs include SPMT or crawler crane
 operator, rigger, banksman, heavy lift supervisor, main crane operator, auxiliary crane
 operator, certification surveyor, forklift operator, blaster and painter foreman, and rigger
 foreman.

 Humboldt County (integration)

 • Significant investments in workforce development programming will be required to
 leverage a local workforce from Humboldt County for floating offshore wind energy
 development.
 • As of 2024, there are no registered apprenticeship programs within Humboldt County.
 However, there is a 2-year public college –College of the Redwoods – that does have
 programs relevant for training a floating offshore wind energy workforce.
 • Sourcing a local workforce from Humboldt County may pose a challenge due to
 workforce and training program availability.

 Mod 1, Task 1: Determine Workforce Risk Level for INO/INOC Technology

 1.1 The Participant shall select one location and provide the required information for the
 Contractor to determine the risk level at that location across the phases.

 1.2 The Contractor shall determine the risk level for workforce readiness for each phase of the
 INO/INOC technology deployment, including the substructure design, production, installation,
 maintenance, and operation phases for the INO/INOC technology. Workforce risk is defined as
 the ability to hire from an existing labor market, transition workers with similar skillsets, or train
 a workforce using existing or scalable training programs near a specific location. The Contractor
 shall conduct the assessment at an occupational level, highlighting workforce strengths and gaps
 for floating offshore wind energy substructure deployment. This assessment will evaluate how
 labor unions, community colleges, universities, and other training providers can supply different
 worker types and the associated risk and readiness. Work activities under this Task 3 include:

 • Identify all the occupations across the INO/INOC deployment phases.
 • Compile Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) and Census data on workforce.
 • Conduct at least five interviews to assist with workforce risk level determination.
 • Develop a tool to calculate the risk rating of workforce readiness.
 • Determine the risk rating of workforce readiness (i.e. low, medium, high) at a phase
 level.
 • Provide key insights that factor into the risk rating.

 Utilizing existing workforce data and insights from Task 2, NLR researchers analyzed the
 workforce risk – or the ability to hire from an existing labor market, transition workers with
 similar skillsets, or train a workforce leveraging existing or scalable training programs – for each
 phase of the INO/INOC technology’s deployment at one location, including the substructure
 design, production, installation, maintenance, and operation. The Port of Long Beach, California
 was chosen as the focus of this comprehensive study, as it will potentially be a site for the
 fabrication, assembly, and integration of the INO/INOC technology. San Diego, California was
 also considered as for this study; however, only the workforce risk of a fabrication site for the
 area was determined.

 A novel methodology was developed to support the calculation of the workforce risk for floating
 offshore wind energy in California. For this assessment, workforce risk was defined as the ability
 to hire from an existing labor market, transition workers with similar skillsets, and train a
 workforce using existing or scalable training programs near a specific location.

 9

 The workforce risk calculator was developed to provide a way to compare between multiple
 areas’ ability to support the development of offshore wind at a singular point in time. A direct
 outcome of this technical assistance was support in the creation of a new index, the Workforce
 Readiness Index which was developed as a national tool that assesses and compares energy
 sector workforce readiness. The approach is documented in The Workforce Readiness Index: A
 Local and Regional Assessment Tool for Energy Sector Preparedness report (McDowell, 2025).

 To conduct the workforce risk level for the INO/INOC technology, multiple quantitative and
 qualitative resources were compiled and analyzed to evaluate the workforce risk related to
 floating offshore wind related phases of development. Highly detailed information was provided
 to Technip Energies USA Inc. to better inform their future workforce development across
 floating offshore wind energy phases. These resources include:

 • An education and training database focused on identifying institutions within a one-hour
 commuting radius of the Port of Long Beach and San Diego.
 • A workforce risk calculator that comparatively evaluates multiple labor, economic,
 transportation, and skill related indicators to calculate the current workforce risk at a
 county level.
 • Key takeaways from local stakeholder interviews that provided focused insights on
 workforce development initiatives, challenges, and opportunities.
 • A desktop analysis of industries related to floating offshore wind that leverages similar
 workers.

 Key insights on workforce risks for the Port of Long Beach include:

 • Based on the assessment, Los Angeles County was determined to have a very low risk
 ranking regarding its ability to support all phases of floating offshore wind deployment
 from a workforce perspective.
 • Los Angeles County was calculated to have an above average number of education and
 training programs, and specialization for occupations relevant to the floating offshore
 wind industry.
 • Los Angeles County, especially outside of the city center, was noted to have a high
 commute time and limited access to public transportation, both of which may pose a
 barrier when trying to attract a workforce to the Port of Long Beach.

 10

 Key insights on workforce risks for the San Diego include:

 • San Diego County was evaluated for its workforce risk to support the fabrication of the
 floating substructure sub-assembly blocks and compared to the nation and state has a
 very low workforce risk ranking.
 • Transportation access, workforce availability, and program availability were noted as
 areas where there could be opportunities to improve.
 • General Dynamics NASSCO, a company that has a long history of shipbuilding, could
 lead the fabrication of Technip Energies sub-block fabrication in San Diego. NASSCO
 has an in-house welder training program.

 Local stakeholder interviews were also conducted to better understand ongoing and future
 workforce development initiatives, challenges, and opportunities related to floating offshore
 wind development in the area. Six semi-structured interviews were conducted to validate
 calculator results, and assumptions on local workforce risks near the Port of Long Beach.
 Interviews were conducted with Port of Long Beach and Pier Wind, California Workforce
 Development Board and Department of Labor, Cal Maritime, Ironworkers Local 416.

 Regarding floating offshore wind deployment (across pre-development/development,
 substructure block fabrication, block assembly into hull and turbine integration (at port),
 installation (offshore), operation and maintenance, and port operation), the Port of Long Beach
 area was found to have a low risk as it pertains to its foundation for workforce development.

 However, through the assessment conducted, several areas for workforce improvement were
 identified:

 • Risk 1: Lack of qualified welders for floating offshore wind and competition from
 adjacent industries.
 • Risk 2: Absence of Global Wind Organization (GWO) training.
 • Risk 3: Uncertainty around timing of programs development and workforce demand.
 • Risk 4: Lack of awareness on floating offshore wind job role opportunities.

 Potential risk mitigation strategies to address the identified challenges include:

 • Strategy 1: Build awareness of floating offshore wind energy job opportunities among
 youth populations in the area.
 • Strategy 2: Communicating technology-specific training needs with training providers.
 • Strategy 3: Implement floating offshore wind energy education and certifications into
 local training programs.
 • Strategy 4: Gather key stakeholders to address future workforce training gaps.

 11

 As floating offshore wind energy continues to develop off the coast of California, the industry
 will need a properly trained and adequately sized workforce. While the workforce risk for both
 Los Angeles and San Diego County was determined to be low, the areas will still encounter
 challenges when deploying floating offshore wind energy in the coming years.

 Mod 1, Task 2: Complete Location Specific Workforce Development Plan

 The Contractor shall complete a workforce development plan for the location identified in Task
 3, detailing the job requirements and strategies on how to recruit and hire workers, with an
 emphasis placed on engaging diverse population and marginalized communities. A particular
 focus will be given to occupations with phases that a medium or high-risk level determined in
 Task 3, or occupations prioritized by the Participant. The workforce development plan will
 identify connections to existing or scalable training partners (e.g. registered apprenticeship
 programs, labor unions, community colleges, or universities) and create a toolkit that the
 Participant could provide useful information on the skillsets, certifications, safety, or other
 workforce needs for the INO/INOC substructure technology to those providers. Work activities
 under this Task 4 include:

 • Identify job requirements for the deployment phases for the INO/INOC technology.
 • Identify location-specific opportunities to transition and recruit a workforce.
 • Identify key existing or scalable training partners to source workers.
 • Interview at least five local or regional workforce practitioners to collect information on
 nearby workforce development.
 • Create a detailed plan on where to recruit and hire workers from communities near the
 chosen location
 • Create a resource to provide education and training programs and understanding of the
 skills, certification, and workforce needs for at least 5 critical occupations, identified as
 high risk or of critical importance.

 Based on this workforce risk analysis (Mod 1, Task 1) the team also completed a workforce
 development plan for the location of interest, detailing the job requirements and strategies on
 how to recruit and hire workers, identifying connections to existing or scalable training partners
 (e.g. registered apprenticeship programs, labor unions, community colleges, or universities).

 The occupations that were determined to be of high-risk or high priority in Los Angeles County
 are: welder, rigger, stevedore, industrial truck and tractor operator, specialist coating technician,
 SPMT operator, crane and tower operator. High-risk or high priority occupations are defined as
 those that are highly specialized and in demand across multiple phases of deployment without
 sufficient training programs in the area of interest.

 12

 Workforce development plans for the high-risk or high priority in Los Angeles County
 occupations were shared with Technip Energies USA Inc. These documents includes information
 describing the occupations, occupation projections, labor demand increase estimates, licenses
 needed, skills needed, potential certifications needed, and the primary labor unions involved for
 the occupations. Information about Technip Energies and the INO/INOC was also included
 along with how the occupations would be used by this company in the deployment of floating
 offshore wind energy. These documents are a toolkit that Technip Energies could provide
 interested parties with useful information on the skillsets, certifications, safety, or other
 workforce needs for the INO/INOC substructure technology.

 Task 3: Supply Chain Advisement

 3.1 The Participant will request a supply chain advising meeting at least two weeks prior to the
 desired meeting date and provide a list of questions prior to the meeting. Advising topics under
 this Task 3 could include:

 • Providing updates on assembly and fabrication yards on US coasts.
 • Tracking port development on the West Coast and across U.S. coasts.
 • Share information on U.S. manufactured steel supply.
 • Share learnings from other DOE-sponsored supply chain research.

 3.2 The Contractor shall advise the Participant on critical supply chain issues specific to
 floating offshore wind energy and the INO/INOC substructure. The objective of these advising
 meetings is to share Contractor knowledge gained from research projects that can readily be
 shared and not covered by a non-disclosure agreement. The Contractor will meet with the
 Participant at least four times for a one-hour virtual advising meeting. The Contractor will
 identify relevant and knowledgeable staff members to support the request.

 Technip Energies USA, Inc. determined the most effective way to support Task 3 was to review
 the information reported under Task 1 and provide any known updates. Two NLR staff
 researchers reviewed the report submitted to Technip Energies USA, Inc. in April 2025, and
 shared insights including:

 • California Energy Commission’s Offshore Wind Energy Waterfront Facility
 Improvement Program awarded $43 million to Long Beach, Humboldt, Richmond,
 Oakland, and San Luis.
 • Multiple interviewees indicated that they believed domestic steel mills could adequately
 supply the needs of FOW platform fabricators. A non-exhaustive list of domestic steel
 plate producers was provided; many companies can produce steel plates with
 characteristics that are suitable for offshore wind energy monopiles.
