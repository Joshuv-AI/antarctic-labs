1. What are the major goals of the project?
The GROWTH-MSI program was designed to broaden participation in High Energy Physics
(HEP) by building a new research and workforce-development pathway for undergraduate
students at Minority Serving Institutions (MSIs) in Northern California. The project had two
primary goals:

PG-1: Create a 1.5-year traineeship program for undergraduates from six local
universities.
The six participating universities were CSU Stanislaus, Sonoma State University, San Francisco
State University, CSU East Bay, Fresno State University, and the University of California,
Merced. The traineeship was designed to provide students with structured preparation for HEP
research, paid research and development time, local and external mentorship, an Introduction to
Particle Physics course, weekly HEP seminars, summer research placements, and graduate-
school or workforce preparation. The original proposal emphasized that many students at these
MSIs face barriers to HEP participation, including limited access to HEP courses, limited local
HEP faculty, lack of a peer cohort, and inadequate financial support.

PG-2: Develop HEP research infrastructure at CSU Stanislaus and Sonoma State
University.
A second major goal was to strengthen the long-term HEP research capacity of two MSI-based
investigators: Prof. Wing To at CSU Stanislaus and Prof. Alexandra Miller at Sonoma State
University. The project sought to support faculty research development, build undergraduate
research pipelines, establish HEP course offerings, strengthen connections with DOE
laboratories and R1 research institutions, and make both institutions more competitive for future
HEP research funding.

The program collaborated with five DOE or research sites: Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory,
UC Berkeley, and UC Santa Barbara.

2. What was accomplished under these goals?
Accomplishments under PG-1: Crea8on of the GROWTH-MSI traineeship

The GROWTH-MSI program successfully created and implemented a 1.5-year traineeship
model for undergraduate students from MSIs in Northern California. Two cohorts of trainees
were recruited, supported, mentored, and placed into HEP research experiences.

Cohort 1

Cohort 1 consisted of seven trainees from four local universities. The trainees participated in the
program from January 2024 to May 2025. During the first spring semester of the traineeship,

Cohort 1 students participated in an Introduction to Particle Physics course offered through CSU
Fully Online by CSU Stanislaus. They also attended weekly HEP seminars and were matched
with DOE research mentors from LBNL for summer research. Most continued research work
after the summer and presented results at the American Physical Society Global Physics Summit
in March 2025.

Of the seven Cohort 1 trainees, three entered graduate programs, two were still completing their
bachelor’s degrees at the time of reporting, and one entered the private-sector workforce.

Three Cohort 1 students were from Sonoma State University. Two of the Sonoma State Cohort 1
trainees are now enrolled in Physics Ph.D. programs, one at UC Davis and one at Texas Tech.
The third Sonoma State Cohort 1 trainee entered the workforce.

Cohort 2

Cohort 2 consisted of twelve trainees from six participating universities. These students began
the GROWTH-MSI traineeship in January 2025. They received scholarship or stipend support,
began working with their local mentors, attended weekly HEP seminars, and enrolled in the
Introduction to Particle Physics course offered through CSU Fully Online by Sonoma State
University.

Cohort 2 students were matched with DOE research mentors from SLAC, LBNL, and LLNL for
summer research placements from June 9, 2025, to August 15, 2025. They continued research
work after the summer, and seven presented results at the American Physical Society Global
Physics Summit in March 2026. Six Cohort 2 trainees were accepted into graduate programs, and
two applied to the APS Bridge Program.

Two Cohort 2 students were from Sonoma State University. One completed summer research at
Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, and the other completed summer research at
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. One of these Sonoma State trainees will begin a
Physics Ph.D. program at Michigan Technological University, and the other will begin a Physics
Master’s program at San Jose State University.

Across both cohorts, five Sonoma State students participated in the traineeship. Four of the five
Sonoma State trainees continued into graduate study in physics, and the fifth entered the
workforce. These outcomes represent a significant strengthening of the HEP pathway for
Sonoma State students.

Mentorship and student support

The traineeship used a dual-mentorship structure. Each trainee was matched with a local MSI
mentor and a DOE laboratory or research-institution mentor. The local mentor supported the
trainee’s academic progress, course planning, graduate-school preparation, and campus-based
advising. The research mentor guided the trainee’s HEP research project and provided access to
the broader HEP research community.

Prof. Miller and Prof. To worked as a team in organizing all major aspects of the traineeship.
This included recruitment, mentor matching, coordination with research mentors, support for the
weekly HEP seminar series, and student advising. At Sonoma State, Prof. Miller served as the
local mentor and graduate-school advisor for all five Sonoma State trainees.

Accomplishments under PG-2: HEP research infrastructure and faculty
development

The project also made significant progress toward developing HEP research infrastructure at
CSU Stanislaus and Sonoma State University.

At CSU Stanislaus, the PI and students continued developing HEP research connected to the Eos
Hybrid Neutrino Detector Demonstrator. The Stanislaus research group took a leading role in the
development, installation, and commissioning of the Muon Veto System for Eos. A data-analysis
server purchased through the project was installed at CSU Stanislaus and used heavily for data
analysis and simulations for the Eos experiment. Results from the Muon Veto System were
presented by the PI at the APS 2025 meeting, and student analysis results were also presented at
APS 2025.

At Sonoma State University, the grant supported Prof. Miller’s development as a theoretical
high-energy physicist working in quantum gravity and related areas. During the grant period,
Prof. Miller completed two scholarly works, both of which are currently undergoing peer review.
The first, A Conformal Field Theory Primer in D ≥ 3, coauthored with SSU students, Andrew M.
Evans and Aaron Russell, introduces foundational concepts and methods in conformal field
theory and is written primarily for upper-division undergraduate physics majors, master’s
students, Ph.D. students, and readers with undergraduate physics preparation (arXiv). The
second, Quantum chaos and pole skipping in two-dimensional conformal perturbation theory,
coauthored with Curtis T. Asplund, Sebastian Fischetti, and David M. Ramirez, presents new
research on pole skipping in two-dimensional field theories perturbed away from conformality
and compares the resulting skipped poles with the butterfly velocity obtained from a holographic
gravitational dual (arXiv).

The grant also enabled Sonoma State to build durable educational infrastructure in HEP through
the creation of a new Introduction to Particle Physics course. This course is especially significant
because particle physics is not typically offered at small primarily undergraduate MSI physics
programs. The course will continue to be available at Sonoma State beyond the grant period,
providing a sustainable pathway for future students to gain formal preparation in HEP. Ideally,
the course will primarily be offered through CSU Fully Online, so that students across all 23
CSUs will be able to take advantage.

3. What opportuni;es for training and professional
development did the project provide?
The GROWTH-MSI program provided several major forms of training and professional
development.

Introduc8on to Par8cle Physics course

The Introduction to Particle Physics course was a central component of the traineeship. It gave
students formal academic preparation in HEP before or during their research placements. The
course was offered in Spring 2024 by CSU Stanislaus and in Spring 2025 by Sonoma State
University. Across these offerings, 38 students took the course.

At Sonoma State, Prof. Miller was fully responsible for creating the course. This included
bringing the course through the Sonoma State curricular approval process, choosing a textbook,
recording lecture videos, writing homework assignments, and writing exams. The Sonoma State
version of the course was fully online rather than HyFlex, and the course content was created
from scratch. The fully online format allowed students from multiple institutions to participate
through CSU Fully Online or related enrollment mechanisms. The students included not only
CSU students, but also students from UC Berkeley and UC Merced.

The course was integral to the traineeship. Most undergraduate physics majors do not have
formal coursework in particle physics, and the students’ research placements at national
laboratories were significantly enhanced by the background provided in the course.

Weekly HEP seminar series

The program organized a weekly GROWTH-MSI HEP seminar series. Seminars were offered
online for trainees and participants across the consortium. The seminars were open to the broader
community and typically drew 15–20 attendees. Speakers included potential DOE research
mentors, who introduced their research areas, described their paths into DOE science, and
highlighted research and internship opportunities available through GROWTH-MSI and beyond.

The seminar series played several important roles. It exposed trainees to the breadth of HEP
research, helped students identify potential research mentors, created a shared intellectual
community across institutions, and allowed students from small MSI programs to interact
regularly with scientists from national laboratories and R1 universities.

Summer research placements

Both cohorts completed summer research placements with mentors from DOE laboratories and
partner research institutions. These placements gave trainees direct experience in HEP research

environments, including experimental HEP, particle theory, detector development, dark matter
research, neutrino physics, and collider-related projects.

For Sonoma State students, the summer research placements were especially impactful. The five
Sonoma State trainees worked at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory or Lawrence
Livermore National Laboratory. These experiences helped students build research skills, develop
professional networks, and prepare competitive graduate-school applications.

Graduate-school and career prepara8on

The project provided graduate-school preparation through local mentorship and structured
workshops. The Graduate School Workshop Series was offered monthly during the Fall 2024
and Fall 2025 semesters, when many applications were due. The workshops helped trainees
identify graduate programs, find research groups aligned with their interests, understand the
application process, and revise research and personal statements.

At Sonoma State, Prof. Miller provided graduate-school advising to all five Sonoma State
trainees. Four of the five Sonoma State trainees entered or will enter graduate programs in
physics, demonstrating the effectiveness of the local advising and research-preparation model.

4. How have the results been disseminated to
communi;es of interest?
The results of the GROWTH-MSI program were disseminated through conference presentations,
student research presentations, course development, seminar activities, and scholarly
publications.

Conference presenta8ons

The project produced multiple presentations at the American Physical Society Global Summit in
March 2025 and March 2026. In March 2025, Alexandra Miller gave an invited talk titled
Collaborations Between Emerging Research and R1 Institutions: A Success Story!. In that same
year, Wing To gave a talk discussing the first cohort of the program. Cohort 1 students also gave
poster presentations of their research at the meeting. Two Sonoma State students received Top
Presenter awards. In march 2026, Wing To presented an overview of the program titled Three
Years of GROWTH-MSI: An Undergraduate Research & Workforce Development Program in
High Energy Physics. Student presentations included work on dark matter calibration
simulations, the Eos Demonstrator Muon Veto System, SUSY particle event prediction for future
colliders, the ATLAS ITk Inner System Upgrade for the HL-LHC, SiPM array characterization
for a dual-phase XeNeu time projection chamber, the SPLENDOR experiment, and hardware
research and development for the nEXO experiment.

These presentations disseminated both the scientific work completed by trainees and the broader
workforce-development model of the GROWTH-MSI program.

Scholarly products from Sonoma State

Prof. Miller completed two scholarly works during the project period. Both are currently under
peer review and available on the arXiv.

The first is A Conformal Field Theory Primer in D ≥ 3, coauthored with Andrew M. Evans and
Aaron Russell. This work contributes to theoretical HEP education by making conformal field
theory more accessible to advanced undergraduate and graduate students. (arXiv)

The second is Quantum chaos and pole skipping in two-dimensional conformal perturbation
theory, coauthored with Curtis T. Asplund, Sebastian Fischetti, Alexandra Miller, and David M.
Ramirez. This work advances research in quantum chaos, conformal perturbation theory, and
holography. (arXiv)

Course and seminar dissemina8on

The Introduction to Particle Physics course created through the project is a lasting educational
product. At Sonoma State, the course was developed as a fully online offering, with recorded
lectures, homework assignments, exams, and a complete course structure. Because the course
was approved through the Sonoma State curriculum process, it can continue to be offered after
the end of the grant.

The weekly HEP seminar series also disseminated HEP research to students and faculty across
multiple MSIs. The seminars brought DOE-relevant research into institutions where students
otherwise had limited access to HEP researchers and national laboratory scientists.

5. What was the impact of the project?
The GROWTH-MSI program had substantial impact on students, faculty, participating
institutions, and the broader HEP workforce-development pipeline.

Impact on students

The program gave undergraduate students from MSIs access to a structured HEP pathway that
included coursework, paid research time, mentoring, seminars, summer placements, and
graduate-school advising. Many of these students would not otherwise have had access to formal
HEP coursework or national laboratory research experiences.

Across the two cohorts, the program supported nineteen trainees. Cohort 1 included seven
trainees, while Cohort 2 included twelve trainees. The program produced strong graduate-school
outcomes, including multiple students accepted into Ph.D. and master’s programs.

At Sonoma State, the impact was especially strong. Five students participated, four entered or
will enter graduate programs in physics, and one entered the workforce. These outcomes
significantly strengthened the HEP trajectory for Sonoma State students.

Impact on Sonoma State University

The project was transformative for HEP at Sonoma State. It enabled Prof. Miller to build
collaborations with national laboratories, mentor students into DOE laboratory research
experiences, develop a formal particle physics course, and strengthen the visibility of HEP
within the department.

The particle physics course is a particularly important institutional outcome. Its existence at
Sonoma State is unique for a small primarily undergraduate MSI physics program, and the
course will continue to be offered in the future. This creates a sustainable educational pathway
for future students interested in HEP, quantum field theory, particle physics, astrophysics,
cosmology, or related areas.

The grant also supported Prof. Miller’s research productivity in quantum gravity, conformal field
theory, and quantum chaos. The completion of two peer-review-stage scholarly works during the
award period demonstrates progress toward the project’s goal of strengthening HEP research
infrastructure at Sonoma State.

Impact on CSU Stanislaus

At CSU Stanislaus, the project supported student and faculty involvement in the Eos Hybrid
Neutrino Detector Demonstrator, including work on the Muon Veto System. The project also
supported installation and use of a data-analysis server, which has been used for simulations and
analysis connected to Eos.

Impact on the HEP community

The project created a multi-institutional HEP consortium connecting MSIs, DOE laboratories,
and R1 universities. This consortium helped address structural barriers faced by students at small
MSIs, including isolation from the HEP community, lack of local HEP course offerings, and
limited access to research mentors.

The project also demonstrated that a relatively small amount of targeted support can produce
significant outcomes when paired with structured mentoring, paid research time, formal
coursework, and national laboratory partnerships.

6. What challenges or barriers were encountered?
The GROWTH-MSI program addressed several known structural barriers for students and
faculty at MSIs. These included the limited availability of HEP courses, the small number of
HEP faculty at individual institutions, the lack of a local HEP peer cohort, and the difficulty of
connecting students at primarily undergraduate institutions to DOE laboratory research.

Some challenges were logistical. Coordinating recruitment, coursework, seminars, mentor
matching, summer placements, local advising, and graduate-school support across six institutions
and multiple national laboratories required substantial organization by the two PIs. The program
also had to support students across multiple institutional calendars, registration systems, and
research-site requirements.

The fully online Sonoma State Introduction to Particle Physics course addressed one of these
logistical challenges by allowing students from multiple campuses to participate remotely. The
course required substantial up-front development, including curriculum approval, lecture
recording, assignment creation, and exam preparation, but it now provides a sustainable model
for future offerings.

7. What do you plan to do aDer the current repor;ng
period?
All of the funds originally allotted to Sonoma State were used. However, there was remaining
funding at CSU Stanislaus. Wing To, the awardee at Stanislaus requested a no-cost extension to
use remaining funds to continue the GROWTH-MSI project. Planned activities included
continued support for the CSU Stanislaus PI’s research on the Eos Hybrid Neutrino Detector
Demonstrator, continued support for the Sonoma State PI’s quantum gravity research and student
mentoring, and support for up to three additional trainees during Summer 2026, Fall 2026, and
Spring 2027, until remaining funds are exhausted.

At Sonoma State, additional future plans include continuing to offer the Introduction to Particle
Physics course, maintaining collaborations with national laboratory researchers, mentoring
students in HEP and quantum gravity research, and using the infrastructure developed through
GROWTH-MSI to pursue future external funding.

The broader GROWTH-MSI consortium has established a model that can be continued or
expanded in future proposals. The program demonstrated that MSI students can successfully
engage in DOE-relevant HEP research when provided with structured coursework, paid research
support, local mentorship, national laboratory mentorship, and a cohort-based intellectual
community.

10. Summary
The GROWTH-MSI program successfully created a new HEP traineeship pathway for
undergraduate students from MSIs in Northern California and strengthened HEP research
infrastructure at CSU Stanislaus and Sonoma State University. The program supported two
cohorts of trainees, created formal particle physics course offerings, connected students with
national laboratory mentors, supported summer research placements, provided graduate-school
preparation, and produced strong graduate-school and workforce outcomes.

For Sonoma State University, the program had a particularly lasting impact. It supported five
Sonoma State trainees, four of whom entered or will enter graduate programs in physics. It
enabled the creation of a new fully online Introduction to Particle Physics course that will
continue beyond the grant. It strengthened Prof. Miller’s supported scholarly work in conformal
field theory, quantum chaos, and quantum gravity. More broadly, the project increased the
visibility of HEP at Sonoma State and created a sustainable pathway for future students to
engage in DOE-relevant research.

The project demonstrates the effectiveness of combining coursework, paid research, mentoring,
cohort-building, and national laboratory partnerships to broaden participation in HEP and
strengthen research capacity at MSIs.
