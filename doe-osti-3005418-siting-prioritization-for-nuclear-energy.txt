 ABSTRACT

For industry, commerce, service, and residential consumers, affordable access to sufficient energy
resources is essential for prosperity. Energy policy decisions drive siting of significant infrastructure that
may affect numerous stakeholders in different ways. This literature review investigates current research
on energy justice in modeling frameworks as a component of informed decision-making, with an
emphasis on nuclear energy infrastructure siting. The purpose of this review is to identify prevailing
themes, theoretical frameworks, methodological approaches, and knowledge gaps within the existing
literature. Of particular interest is the integral consideration of deployment strategy economics, resource
utilization, and geospatial considerations. The review highlights several areas where consensus exists,
such as the more developed scope of distributional equity, as well as points of ongoing debate, including
the use of AI in modeling. The analysis reveals significant gaps in the literature related to policy decision-
making and suggests directions for future research, such as collecting and analyzing empirical data,
refining policy considerations, and theoretically refining modeling strategies. These efforts can optimize
the risk/benefit balance for all stakeholders to pursue increased energy availability.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Society is facing significant challenges surrounding energy production and distribution. Climate change,
energy poverty, security of supply, and access to the necessary raw materials are all increasing global
concerns. Climate change driven by carbon emissions from fossil fuel use is making the world less
livable, and the greatest impacts are felt by the poorest communities (Teshome, 2024). Increasing access
to energy provides the opportunity to increase standards of living and lower poverty rates (Ritchie, 2019).
Expanded deployment of clean, carbon-free energy, such as nuclear energy, increases the quality of life
for people around the world without increasing the risk of climate change. Further consideration should
be given to making the most effective use of the limited global supplies of raw materials needed for
energy production.

These overlapping challenges and goals should be addressed in a way that minimizes negative impacts on
the environment and disadvantaged communities. Energy justice is a framework established within social
science literature, which can be applied to guide and assess proposed solutions to these challenges.
Energy justice aims to ensure that both the advantages and disadvantages of energy systems are
distributed equitably across members of a community or society, fostering the overall well-being of
society (Sovacool and Dworkin, 2015; Jenkins et al., 2016). However, energy justice alone does not
consider all aspects of energy policy or energy siting decision-making.

Currently, few policy-level decision-making models are available that integrate energy justice impacts
along with technical, economic, resource management, and regulatory considerations. Few, if any,
comprehensive quantitative decision-making models exist for energy infrastructure siting that incorporate
an energy justice framework. A new type of model is needed for consistent and effective policy decision-
making that incorporates technical requirements, economic realities, and stakeholder needs for the most
effective siting of nuclear and other energy infrastructure.

 2. BACKGROUND

The term energy justice was coined in the early 2000s, born out of the climate justice and environmental
justice movements. It originally was used by nongovernmental organizations and groups to apply the
challenges of climate and environment to energy policy. Later, the concept was explored further in
academic works (Galvin, 2020).

 1

The three core tenets of energy justice are distributional, procedural, and recognition (Romero-Lankao,
2021). Distributional justice describes the societal allocation of the benefits and drawbacks in energy
systems. Procedural justice relates to how decisions are made: for example, community stakeholders are
frequently excluded from decisions that directly affect them. Recognition justice elevates the importance
of identifying and considering all relevant stakeholder groups, particularly marginalized communities that
are often left out of energy decision-making discussions (Lee & Byrne, 2019). Figure 1 shows the
connection.

Although energy justice is a nascent field of study, the concept has been carried forward by advocates and
has largely influenced energy regulation in recent years. State governments, utility companies, and federal
agencies under the Biden administration adopted equity and environmental justice screening tools (Hanus
et al., 2024). Current efforts focus on understanding the problem, extent, historical context, and so on, and
looking at ways to improve outcomes, primarily by evaluating the energy justice impacts of infrastructure
projects.

Incorporating energy justice principles also allows for deployment of energy infrastructure according to a
more reliable time frame than with historical methods. In a study of utility-scale renewable energy
projects in the United States, nearly half of projects that encountered community resistance were
ultimately cancelled (Susskind et al., 2022). That is, engaging communities and stakeholders up front may
take some additional time, but the costs are relatively low compared with the costs of legal challenges,
delays, or potential cancellation during construction or development.

Interest in energy justice has steadily increased from the early 2000s, with the number of research and
review articles reaching just over 400 papers in 2024 as seen in Figure 2.

Although the vast majority (e.g., more than 75%) of these articles are in social science disciplines, a small
number of them (e.g., fewer than 10%) are engineering articles. Of these engineering articles, themes

energy infrastructure siting; additional study in this area provides important insights and opportunities to
improve siting prioritization and decision-making models.

A more detailed discussion of quantitative models is provided here, along with limits of the existing
models and a discussion of system-wide approaches to energy justice in modeling and methodology
development. Some novel modeling approaches are discussed, along with the advantages and drawbacks
of using AI and machine learning models to develop energy justice methodologies. Finally, a spatial
approach to energy justice is discussed, along with gaps in the available studies and how they can be
addressed.

 3. ENERGY SYSTEMS AND MODELING LITERATURE

Societal perspectives on energy systems have shifted significantly since the beginning of the
environmental movement in the 1970s. These perspective shifts have changed the focus of optimization
models through the decades (Spittler et al., 2019). In the 1970s, the oil crisis affected the daily lives of
Americans for years, leading to an intense focus on the potential shortage of fossil fuels, energy security,
and stability of energy supply. The 1980s showed increased interest in sustainability and efficiency
measures; discussions on climate change especially began with the formation of the United Nations
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) in 1988.

In the 1990s, there was an increasing focus on climate change and the understanding that energy was a
key part of this developing issue. Research in these areas was integrated. The 2000s saw a continuing
focus on this area, as well as the consideration of poverty and disadvantaged communities regarding
access to energy and climate change impacts. The intergovernmental work in this area, through
organizations such as the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC),
accelerated over the decade.

 3

In the 2010s, the interfaces among energy, climate, sustainable development, and the environment
became increasingly complex. Critical minerals and sustainable mining were discussed in the context of
renewable energy and other energy use. The need for quick action to address climate change, while not
increasing detrimental environmental effects, became a source of tension in negotiations. Discussions of
the need for “deep decarbonization” were initiated. All these areas of research and international
negotiations have continued into the 2020s, with an explosion of research on the topic. Energy is one of
the top climate issues, with an increasing focus on a “just transition” and a global acceptance of the need
to transition away from fossil fuels in a way that does not disadvantage developing economies. A
continued focus on wind and solar energy is being balanced by an interest in energy storage, as well as in
other low-carbon energy sources such as hydrothermal, nuclear, and green hydrogen. Figure 3 shows the
broad progression of this development, with further details in Table 1.

Common model types are bottom-up, which require simplification on the system side, and top-down,
which require simplification on the individual impact side. Bottom-up models start from individual
components, such as household appliance electricity use, and aggregate estimated system demand. Top-
down models begin with a total system value, such as electricity consumption in a region, and then
estimate individual demands and requirements. The errors introduced by the required simplifications of
each model type limit their broad applicability.

Hybrid models attempt to account for these issues by combining features from the bottom-up and top-
down approaches. For example, a hybrid model may predict future energy demand based on bottom-up
cost aggregation combined with a top-down policy implementation change, such as taxes. Hybrid models
add complexity that can make their use and interpretation difficult; however, they can be used to address
multiple questions, balancing the limitations.

Many other assessment models exist, typically to address a specific question. These have limited
applicability to broader, systematic questions. A specific class of these models are integrated assessment
models, which can be the most useful for analyzing climate change and energy impacts in a cohesive way.

All energy models must balance numerous (often diverging) goals and while prioritizing optimizing
varying political goals, such as increasing social equity and the wise use of economic and environmental
resources (Spittler et al., 2019). Depending on how equity is incorporated into modeling, energy
production and access to energy in some sectors may be increased, through large facilities near cities,
while the overall energy distribution becomes more disparate and inequitable between urban and rural
communities (Nock et al., 2020). Few models incorporate energy justice in policy-making (Chu &
Cannon, 2021; Goforth, 2023), but even simplified energy system models can help to improve decision-
making (Spittler et al., 2019). These models can also provide increased access to preemptive information
on how decisions will affect stakeholders and communities as societies continue toward a more equitable
energy economy while addressing carbon emissions and climate change. Most efforts toward energy
justice focus on outreach and public education, particularly within historically disadvantaged
communities (Chu & Cannon, 2021), but additional policy options are also possible.

3.1 QUANTITATIVE MODELS

Quantitative work in the existing energy justice literature is somewhat limited, particularly in
methodological development areas. This scarcity is relatively expected since this topic has primarily
developed as a conceptual framework, only more recently moving into application-based research. To
facilitate this change, this work should be moved into engineering spaces such as clean energy, climate

 7

change, and energy justice continue to develop within the broader public narrative. This shift can be
accomplished through interdisciplinary teams that include modeling experts with a spectrum of expertise
from social science to engineering (Goforth, 2023).

An exception is the work of Heleno et al., (2022), which incorporates energy justice into a quantitative
decision-making framework. This study focused on the outcomes of policy interventions on increasing
energy justice by using distributed energy systems, with a focus on reducing energy burden for low-
income households. The authors maintained that existing quantitative works only studied examples of
energy injustice at the time of data collection and are not useful for predicting optimal policies to increase
energy justice. Their model aimed to maximize the benefits of energy system investments; however, with
a focus only on distributed renewable energy systems, the need for carbon-free baseload energy is
neglected. A similar example is the study by O’Shaughnessy et al. (2024), who completed an after-the-
fact quantitative analysis of the impact of distributed renewable energy on community equity.

Another approach is to apply quantitative parameters for an energy transition away from fossil fuel
sources, including access, consumption, essential services requirements, and prices. Other key criteria are
energy access, energy democracy, and energy poverty (Rios-Ocampo et al., 2025). These key criteria are

3.2 LIMITS OF EXISTING MODELS

In addition to the lack of quantitative models, most existing models typically only focus on one of the
three main tenets of energy justice—distributional justice. This emphasis is not unexpected since
distributional justice is perhaps the simplest and most objective factor of energy justice. Vågerö &
Zeyringer (2023) examined how energy justice is included in existing energy system optimization models.
The authors found that when energy justice is included, it is overwhelmingly distributional justice.
Additionally, this inclusion is only cursory, as it does not meet the definition of justice; the models
attempt to optimize for equal spatial access to energy. Equity, process, and recognition are rarely
considered in the models investigated. Furthermore, although many models have been developed, none
seem to have been broadly adopted (Vågerö & Zeyringer, 2023). This limits the usefulness of any
particular model and may be because none of the models have broadly incorporated all tenets of energy
justice.

Whereas Vågerö & Zeyringer (2023) focused on system optimization models, Lonergan et al. (2023) took
a broader look at different types of models that incorporate energy justice. Lonergan et al. (2023) noted
that a majority (i.e., 59%) of models focus on the operational phase, primarily on energy distribution in
combination with distributional justice. Fewer studies of commissioning (i.e., 40%) were identified, and
only one (i.e., 1%) examined decommissioning aging power (fossil fuel) plants. To address climate
change through a just energy transition, fossil fuel plants must be decommissioned while low-carbon
energy sources are increasingly applied to efficiently use available resources. In a world with growing
energy needs, the focus may necessarily shift to enabling accelerated deployment of low-carbon sources
such as nuclear energy. This shift highlights the need to further develop decision-making models for new
energy infrastructure, as well as decommissioning.

Considering other models that involve justice factors, ambiguity exists in how the energy justice tenets
used are defined (Jodoin et al., 2024). A typical definition of availability is “sufficient energy resources of
high quality.” Affordability is evaluated by a percentage of household income, which is also broad and
potentially problematic. Transparency, accountability, and sustainability are also terms that are frequently
used in energy justice research but are difficult to define. These concepts must be properly
operationalized for further study (Jodoin et al., 2024).

Many existing models fail to include all major sources of energy, most notably excluding nuclear energy.
Nuclear energy is a key source of highly available, carbon-free baseload energy generation, so its
exclusion from models focused on a just transition is notable. For example, the optimization model
developed by Gladkykh et al. (2021) is incomplete because it does not consider nuclear energy at all, in
historical or future states, in centralized electricity generation, or decentralized electricity generation.
Although the focus of the model is Sub-Saharan Africa, this omission is in direct conflict with the reality
that multiple African nations either have or are considering building large-scale and/or distributed nuclear
energy resources (World Nuclear News, 2024). As noted in Table 4 (Gladkykh et al., 2021), renewable-
only grid development is prohibitively expensive. Inclusion of nuclear energy in these scenarios would
provide a more realistic look at the cost of a fully decarbonized energy infrastructure.

3.3 SYSTEM-WIDE ISSUES

A key factor to consider is that addressing climate change via decarbonization does not necessarily lead to
addressing energy justice or energy inequities (Skare et al., 2024). Therefore, justice and equity must be
directly considered in decision-making models.

Multiple authors note that energy justice concerns cannot be addressed without challenging the status quo
of existing energy systems (Gladkykh et al., 2021). Optimization models show that more distributed
energy systems, rather than the existing centralized ones, increase energy justice (Lonergan et al., 2023).
In the study of Sub-Saharan Africa, Gladkykh et al. (2021) found that the status quo scenario with large-
scale generation does not support energy equity, as 10% of rural communities remain without sufficient
power. The scenarios that considered distributed generation provide greater equity between urban and
rural households but are significantly more expensive. With a baseline of fossil fuel generation, pollution
levels also increase as equity is increased through distributed generation at smaller budget levels, such as

 10

using diesel generators. Both pollution and equity are reduced as regions move toward more centralized
generation facilities (Nock et al., 2020). The use of low-carbon baseload energy generation, such as
nuclear, hydropower, and geothermal, to backfill distributed wind and solar capacity can limit the
pollution impacts of distributed generation and allow for climate goals to be reached in a just and
equitable way.

Another factor is the interconnectedness of the energy system. By prioritizing investment in distribution
rather than generation, more of the population (particularly rural communities) gains access to energy
(Nock et al., 2020). However, the available energy may be limited by a lack of generation facilities.
Energy systems are difficult to implement equitably since generation occurs in interspersed large blocks,
and low budgets lead to limited implementation (Nock et al., 2020).

However, to move toward a cleaner energy economy with lower reliance on fossil fuels, massive amounts
of energy will be needed to electrify new markets, including transportation, industry, and data centers,
and to produce clean fuels for markets that cannot be electrified. These increasing needs necessitate an
increase of both large, centralized power sources, and distributed energy generation for communities.

Relatedly, Gladkykh et al. (2021) suggest that there is a minimum amount of energy required to meet
human needs, and anything above this is a secondary priority. However, in addition to the noted
increasing energy needs, having increased access to energy results in an increased standard of living. It is
unclear if a limit to this exists, especially as the world focuses on decarbonization.

3.4 ECONOMIC MODELING APPROACHES

The traditional Western optimization modeling approach is to minimize cost while meeting a set of
minimum criteria, such as for distribution, generation, reliability, and so on. This approach can result in a
“catalog of options” for addressing energy inequity, with options purchased within a budget without
considering the overall system (Chu & Cannon, 2021). An alternative approach, particularly useful in
developing economies, is to maximize benefits while using a set budget constraint (Nock et al., 2020). An
analog to this is observed in developing nations where loans may not be available, and houses are built as
money is available—bricks are purchased and installed one by one, rather than saving up for the purchase
of a complete house. This novel modeling approach can be especially useful in developing economies
where resources are not sufficient to meet all current energy needs; infrastructure can be developed over
time to eventually satisfy energy needs while making progress when resources and budget are available
(Nock et al., 2020). This approach should be explored in more developed economies, as well, to
determine the impact on energy justice outcomes.

3.5 MODELING DECARBONIZATION POLICIES

A broad and robust modeling community is investigating the potential options and scenarios for
decarbonization, domestically and globally. The Energy Modeling Forum 37 study looked at approaches
to decarbonizing North America by 2050 to reach current government policies and initiatives (Browning
et al., 2023). This broad sensitivity study compiled 16 models with varying approaches. It found broad
agreement on the ability to decarbonize the electricity sector, but only one of the models predicted that the
United States could reach its goal to achieve a carbon-free electricity sector by 2035 (Browning et al.,
2023). This issue is further complicated by the unknown extent to which other sectors, such as
transportation, buildings, and industry, will be electrified and add additional strain to this demand. These
complications demonstrate a disconnect between stated policies for decarbonization and technical ability
to meet these goals, according to modeling. This disconnect must be addressed through better integration
of policymaker needs with technical analysis and modeling outputs.

3.6 AI AND MACHINE LEARNING MODELS

Another novel approach is to use machine learning and algorithms to assess equity. AI has been used for
modeling and predictions and could be expanded to more effective use to improve decision-making
(Fischer-Abaigar et al., 2024). AI and machine learning require very large quantities of data for the most
accurate predictions. In small samples, random variation in the data may be exacerbated and can lead to
inaccurate assessment as algorithms attempt to incorporate these small variations (Rajput et al., 2023).
However, the accuracy across data sets depends on the variation within the sample. “Well-behaved”
samples, where data are more consistent, produce greater accuracy with a smaller sample size (Rajput et
al., 2023).

Because the data for energy justice outcomes are limited, predictive capability may be limited at this time.
Also, a bias exists in publishing outcomes, where negative outcomes are assessed and analyzed in the
literature, but positive outcomes are typically not studied with academic rigor. An example is Diablo
Canyon, a nuclear plant in California that was slated for shutdown but was successfully kept open through
community pressure and support (Gearhart, 2024). Publishing has been limited regarding the positive
lessons learned and outcome of the Diablo Canyon effort, and nothing on this subject was published in
the peer-reviewed academic space. These factors limit the use of AI and machine learning for energy
justice modeling, particularly related to energy infrastructure. Where these approaches are used, best
practices, shown in Table 5, should be considered.

As mentioned, the required sample size for use of AI and machine learning algorithmic assessment varies
depending on the type and variation of the training data (Rajput et al., 2023). In the case of energy justice,
the limited—and potentially skewed—sample data need to be carefully considered and addressed.
Historic forms of discrimination through siting, land use, pollution, and waste can be further magnified
through machine learning. A model can be accurate and useful for one community while increasing
injustice for others (Rajput et al., 2023). Therefore, the methods, models, and outcomes must be
considered at each stage of development to ensure that the outputs are properly pursuing the desired
outcomes.

Although the best practices for addressing latent inequities in the existing data must be considered, the
data for machine learning or AI in this research context are insufficient for appropriate use. Power plants
are not numerous enough, and the decision-making data about siting are not necessarily available,
especially for older units that were constructed prior to the digital age when records may not be as
accessible, and the regulatory environment may have been different.

3.7 SPATIAL APPROACH TO ENERGY JUSTICE

An emerging area for energy justice research is in spatial assessment. Although spatial assessment and
modeling are commonplace, they typically do not incorporate energy justice frameworks in a robust way,
if at all. Thus, this method is a good candidate for future advancement of the field (Urkidi Azkarraga &
Gurrutxaga, 2024).

A methodology for qualitative spatial modeling and assessment for electrical/energy sources is the Oak
Ridge Siting Analysis for Power Generation Expansion (OR-SAGE). This tool uses Geographical
Information Systems (GIS) data layers on a region- or nation-wide scale to find suitable siting locations
for energy infrastructure, shown in Figure 4. Some of the considerations can include access to water, rail
systems, grid interconnections, and distance from population centers (Belles et al., 2012).

 Figure 4. Applying OR-SAGE methodology for US siting (Belles et al., 2012).

Since its creation more than a decade ago, OR-SAGE has been adapted for many applications in many
locations. Efforts are being made to use this methodology for spatial assessment of energy justice, but
these activities focus on energy burden and accessibility on a city-wide scale. A region-specific effort is
the CalEnviroScreen tool that enables comparison of the environmental risk factors for different census
tracts in California. This tool is used to identify communities that have access to additional funding, or
locales that are required to have additional environmental planning and mitigation activities in place
(Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment, 2023).

The main challenges faced in energy planning when considering energy and environmental justice on a
spatial scale are how the problem is framed, which social and technical aspects to consider, what the
proper size or scale of planning focus should be, and how the public should be engaged for participation
in the process (Urkidi Azkarraga & Gurrutxaga, 2024). Although this approach is becoming more
common, it often focuses exclusively on the technical parameters and does not consider community needs

 13

and values (Wang et al., 2022). Spatial planning is a multifaceted challenge, including technical, project,
and political implications. Environmental, social, and infrastructure needs must be considered. How we
undertake spatial planning must be investigated further since it is the basis of many of the factors of
distributive and/or procedural injustice in energy (Urkidi Azkarraga & Gurrutxaga, 2024).

Two studies in the application of energy justice on a spatial planning dimention were reviewed in further
detail. The first (Urkidi Azkarraga & Gurrutxaga 2024) looked at the Basque region of Spain, where a
government plan for wind and solar development was issued and received a significant amount of media
attention. Urkidi Azkarraga & Gurrutxaga (2024) performed a qualitative media study of 225 articles,
selected from more than 1,500 articles from various news outlets. From the coded media articles, which
included quotes from citizens, a list of additional exclusion areas was developed and mapped over the
original government exclusion areas using GIS layers. Through this process, a type of spatial energy
justice methodology was developed, shown in Figure 5.

3.8 GAPS IN EXISTING KNOWLEDGE

Because energy justice is a nascent field, there are many gaps in the literature. Out of the methodology-
based papers reviewed, only one looked at a quantitative assessment of energy justice. Currently, no
quantitative models examine energy generation. Although significant data on energy justice-related
considerations have been generated, few studies provided clear input for decision-making by
policymakers. Spatial approaches are also rarely used to investigate energy justice outcomes, and very
few studies include nuclear energy in justice research. Future research will need to address this gap with a
study of energy justice using a spatially based approach that focuses on energy generation.

Focusing specifically on the two identified spatial analysis papers, issues were identified that need to be
addressed. For the Basque study (Urkidi Azkarraga & Gurrutxaga, 2024), the new exclusion areas were
not mapped back to the original government plan; see Figure 6. This mapping would identify if gaps
existed in the qualitative assessment (i.e., if concerns were only identified in areas where new
development was planned). Second, the study did not identify if concerns existed from other segments of
the population that did not have access to the voice of the media or similar publishing.

Similarly, in the Taiwan study (Wang, Dodd, and Ko, 2022), the prioritization survey data were based on

community participants, so the data should be considered carefully. Also, the study was only completed
for a small agricultural region of Taiwan and only considered solar energy development. To be effective,
the framework must be applicable to diverse geographic areas and a broader spectrum of energy sources.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

To enable better decision-making for energy by policymakers, the relationship between access to energy
and quality of life must be noted. Economies with increased access to and use of energy have improved
standards of living (Nock et al., 2020). This is shown in Figure 7, where Human Development Index
(HDI) represents standard of living, and the use of energy indicated by per-capita energy consumption.
On a per-capita basis, the best-fit trend indicates increasing standards of living as use of energy increases.
To achieve even greater improvements, increased communication and collaboration via interdisciplinary
research in energy can help to bring about improvements beyond the scope of any individual discipline.

A combined qualitative and quantitative approach should be developed to assess both technical
engineering and community priorities when siting energy infrastructure. This interdisciplinary approach
using hybrid qualitative and quantitative decision-making tools for energy policy will incorporate both
social and technical data. This method will allow for more robust, repeatable decision-making tools for
policymakers and energy providers. Such tools are important because energy is a complex system that has
huge effects on the daily lives of everyone in every community. Having access to sufficient energy
resources at a price that does not introduce an undue energy burden is essential for prosperity.

This type of methodology can create a foundation for future interdisciplinary work that has a spatial
component and requires siting of infrastructure. Beyond energy, this study could be extended for siting of
industrial, commercial, or residential facilities. It could also be used to plan green spaces to improve
communities and to improve other land and resource utilization outcomes, or to assess safety and security
implications.
