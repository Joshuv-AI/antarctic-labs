Bounds on Spectral Gaps for Non-Reversible Markov Chains with Applications to Temporal Difference Learning
Andrew Lamperski

Abstract
This work is motivated by the analysis of temporal difference algorithms, where stability can be guaranteed by bounding the eigenvalues of an associated matrix derived from a, typically non-reversible, Markov kernel. We generalize the existing sufficient conditions for stability and show that the associated eigenvalues can be bounded in terms of the Dirichlet spectral gap of the Markov kernel. We derive a collection of methods for showing that non-reversible Markov chains have positive spectral gaps. We show that if a Markov chain has positive absolute spectral gap, then it has a positive Dirichlet spectral gap. In the case of discrete-time linear Gaussian systems, we give explicit bounds for both Dirichlet and absolute spectral gaps. Additionally, we present an example of a Markov chain which is V V -uniformly geometrically ergodic but has zero Dirichlet spectral gap.

Introduction

Spectral properties of operators induced by Markov kernels are commonly studied as a means to analyze convergence to stationary distributions
[
4
,
8
,
2
]
. Spectral gaps, in particular, can be used to quantify convergence speed.

When the Markov chain is reversible, spectral gaps can be expressed in terms of an associated Dirichlet form. However, when the Markov chain is non-reversible, the Dirichlet form and associated spectral gap can be defined
[
5
]
, but their connection to the spectrum of the original operator and convergence properties is less obvious. Related notions, such as absolute spectral gaps, can give more precise information about convergence for non-reversible Markov chains
[
17
]
.

While other spectral gap notions may be more useful for analyzing convergence for non-reversible Markov chains, the spectral gap definition via Dirichlet forms naturally arises in the analysis of reinforcement learning methods
[
20
,
27
]
. We show concretely how the eigenvalues of a matrix arising in the analysis of temporal difference learning methods,
[
16
,
28
]
, can be bounded in terms of the Dirichlet spectral gap for (typically non-reversible) Markov chain. Bounds on the eigenvalues of this matrix, in turn, utilized for quantitative convergence guarantees for actor-critic reinforcement learning. Often, however, the bounds are assumed rather than proved
[
30
,
31
,
11
]
. The connection to reinforcement learning motivates the main results on this paper, which gives a collection of methods for proving that the Dirichlet
spectral gap is positive and giving positive lower bounds.

The main contributions of the work are as follows. We generalize existing conditions for showing that eigenvalues for matrices arising in temporal difference learning have negative real parts. Existing conditions require that the underlying Markov chain is
V
-uniformly geometrically ergodic. Our results show that irreducibility suffices. We show, further, that the real-parts of the eigenvalues of the corresponding matrices can be bounded in terms of the (Dirichlet) spectral gap of the corresponding Markov operator. We give general conditions for positive spectral gaps based on density functions of the Markov kernel. Then we show how to derive quantitative bounds on spectral gaps via comparison methods. In particular, we show how uniform geometric ergodicity and absolute spectral gaps can lead to bounds on Dirichlet spectral gaps. We analyze the particular case of linear Gaussian systems, as it commonly arises in control problems, and give explicit bounds on Dirichlet and absolute spectral gaps in terms of the original system matrices. Finally, we construct an example of a
V
-uniformly geometrically ergodic Markov chain which has zero Dirichlet and zero absolute spectral gap, thus showing that
V
-uniform geometric ergodicity is not sufficient to imply a positive Dirichlet spectral gap.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
gives background on Markov operators and defines the spectral gap properties studied in the paper. Section
3
describes the connection with spectral gaps and temporal difference learning.
Sufficient conditions for positive spectral gaps are given in Section
4
. The example of a
V
-uniformly geometrically ergodic Markov chain with zero Dirichlet spectral gap is given in Section
5
. Conclusions are given in Section
6
.

. Random variables are denoted as bold symbols, e.g.

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]

Markov Operators and Spectral Gaps

In this section,
P
denotes a Markov kernel with invariant probability measure
\mu
over measure space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the state space and
\mathcal{B}
is a corresponding
\sigma
-algebra over
\mathcal{X}
.

We review some required concepts from the operator-theoretic interpretation of Markov chains. For more details, see
[
8
]
.

Let
L^{2}(\mu)
denote the set of square integrable functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
with inner product defined by

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bar{f}(x)g(x)\mu(dx).

(1)

The Markov kernel,
P
, can be viewed as an operator over
L^{2}(\mu)
with action defined by

Pf(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}P(x,dy)f(y).

The measure,
\mu
, can be viewed as a linear functional over
L^{2}(\mu)
with action defined by

\mu f=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dx)f(x).

The adjoint of
P
with respect to the inner product from (
1
) is given by

P^{\star}f(x)=\frac{d\mu_{f}}{d\mu}(x),

where
\mu_{f}
is the complex measure defined by

\mu_{f}(A)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dx)f(x)P(x,A).

for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
.

The operator,
P^{\star}
, can also be interpreted as a Markov kernel defined by

P^{\star}(x,A)=P^{\star}\mathds{1}_{A}(x),

where
\mathds{1}_{A}
is the indicator function of the set
A
:

\mathds{1}_{A}(x)=\begin{cases}1&x\in A\\
0&x\notin A.\end{cases}

Furthermore,
\mu
is an invariant measure for
P^{\star}
.

We say that
P
is
reversible
with respect to
\mu
if
P=P^{\star}
. Using the terminology of
[
10
]
, we call
P_{a}:=\frac{1}{2}(P+P^{\star})
the
additive reversibilization
of
P
.

Using the terminology of
[
25
]
, the
Dirchlet form
associated with
P
and
\mu
is defined by:

\mathcal{E}(f,g)=\mathrm{Re}\left(\langle f,(I-P)g\rangle\right),

(2)

where
\mathrm{Re}
denotes the real-part.
(We use a slightly different definition from
[
25
]
for consistency with our definition of the inner product.)

In particular, direct calculations show that:

\mathcal{E}(f,f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dx)P(x,dy)|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}=\left\langle f,\left(I-\frac{1}{2}(P+P^{\star})\right)f\right\rangle

(3)

Let
\mathrm{var}(f)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dx)|f(x)|^{2}-\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dx)f(x)\right|^{2}
denote the variance of
f
. In the terminology of
[
25
,
4
]
, we define the
spectral gap
of
P
by

\mathrm{gap}(P)=\inf\left\{\frac{\mathcal{E}(f,f)}{\mathrm{var}(f)}\middle|0<\mathrm{var}(f)<\infty\right\}.

(4)

This notion of spectral gap goes by various names such as the
optimal Poincaré constant

[
9
]
or the
first Dirichlet eigenvalue

[
4
]
.

We will discuss in Section
3
how the definition of spectral gap from (
4

A common alternative spectral gap notion is the
absolute spectral gap
, which is examined in
[
8
,
17
]
. To define the absolute spectral gap, let
L_{0}^{2}(\mu)
denote the subspace of

of functions,

, and let
\mathrm{spec}(P|L_{0}^{2}(\mu))
be the spectrum of

restricted to the subspace

. Then the absolute spectral gap of

is defined by:

\mathrm{absgap}(P)=1-\sup\{|\lambda||\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(P|L_{0}^{2}(\mu))\}.

Lemma
4.4
in Subsection
4.2
shows that
\mathrm{absgap}(P)>0
implies that
\mathrm{gap}(P)>0
. Example
Example
below in this section shows that the converse does not hold.

Other spectral gap notions include the pseudo-spectral gap
[
23
,
29
]
and the iterated Poincaré gap
[
3
,
13
]
.

We say that
P
admits a kernel density with respect to
\mu
if there is a function,
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
:

P(x,A)=\int_{A}\mu(dy)K(x,y).

When
P
admits a kernel density with respect to
\mu
, the actions of
P
and
P^{\star}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle Pf(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dy)K(x,y)f(y)

\displaystyle P^{\star}f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dy)K(y,x)f(y).

In this case,
P
is reversible if
K(x,y)=K(y,x)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
.

When
\mathcal{X}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\mathcal{B}=2^{\mathcal{X}}
, we identify

with the corresponding
n\times n

\mu
with the corresponding

vector. If
\mu_{i}>0

, then
P
admits a kernel density with respect to
\mu
, which is given by
K_{ij}=P_{ij}/\mu_{j}
, and
P^{\star}
corresponds to the matrix with
P^{\star}_{ij}=K_{ji}\mu_{j}=\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}P_{ji}\mu_{j}
. Setting
D=\mathrm{diag}(\mu)
, the expressions for
K
and
P^{\star}
can be expressed in matrix form as:

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=PD^{-1}

\displaystyle P^{\star}

\displaystyle=D^{-1}P^{\top}D

In this case,
P
is reversible if and only if
P=D^{-1}P^{\top}D
.

Example
.

This example gives a finite-state Markov chain with
\mathrm{absgap}(P)=0
and
\mathrm{gap}(P)=2>0
. Thus, in contrast to Lemma
4.4
,
\mathrm{gap}(P)>0
does not imply that
\mathrm{absgap}(P)>0
. Let

P=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

This defines an irreducible, reversible (but periodic) Markov chain, and the unique invariant distribution is

\mu=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Direct calculations give:

\displaystyle\mathrm{spec}(P|L_{0}^{2}(\mu))=\{-1\}\implies\mathrm{absgap}(P)=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{gap}(P)=2.

In this example, the difference between spectral and absolute spectral gaps reflects a basic difference in their definitions. Namely, when restricted to reversible operators, the (Dirichlet) spectral gap measures the distance of the spectrum from
1
(aside from the eigenvalue at exactly
1
), where the absolute gap measures the deviation of the spectrum from all of the unit circle.

For Markov chain matrices which are both reversible and aperiodic, only a single eigenvalue (at
1
) could occur on the unit circle and both the (Dirichlet) spectral gap and the absolute spectral gap would be positive.

An elementary lemma needed in the proofs is:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
P
be a Markov kernel with invariant probability measure
\mu
.
If
P
is irreducible, then
P_{a}:=\frac{1}{2}(P+P^{\star})
must be irreducible as well.

Recall that
P
is irreducible if the state space has an accessible small set, which we denote by
C\in\mathcal{B}
. By accessible, we mean that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, there is a number

such that
P^{n}(x,C)>0
. But then
C
must also be accessible for
P_{a}
, since
P_{a}^{n}(x,C)\geq\frac{1}{2^{n}}P^{n}(x,C)
. By small, we mean that there is a non-zero non-negative measure,
\nu
, and a positive integer
m
such that for all
x\in C
and all
B\in\mathcal{B}
we have
P^{m}(x,B)\geq\nu(B)
. (Theorem 9.2.15 of
[
8
]
shows that we can take
\nu(B)=\delta\mu(B)
for some
\delta>0
.) Then, as before, we have
P_{a}^{m}(x,B)\geq\frac{1}{2^{m}}P^{m}(x,B)\geq\frac{1}{2^{m}}\nu(B)
. So,
C
is small for
P_{a}
as well.
∎

The following elementary lemma relates the definition of the spectral gap from (
4
) to the spectrum of the additive reversibilization.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
P
is irreducible, then

\mathrm{gap}(P)=1-\sup\left(\mathrm{spec}(P_{a})\setminus\{1\}\right)

The definition from (
4
) can be expressed equivalently as

\mathrm{gap}(P)=\inf\{\mathcal{E}(f,f)|f\in L_{0}^{2}(\mu),\>\|f\|=1\}=1-\sup\{\langle f,P_{a}f\rangle|f\in L_{0}^{2}(\mu),\>\|f\|=1\}.

A standard result from operator theory, see e.g. Theorem 22.A.19 of
[
8
]
, implies that

\sup\{\langle f,P_{a}f\rangle|f\in L_{0}^{2}(\mu),\>\|f\|=1\}=\sup\>\mathrm{spec}(P_{a}|L_{0}^{2}(\mu)).

Since
P_{a}
is irreducible, Proposition 2.1.2 of
[
8
]
implies that
P_{a}
has an eigenvalue of multiplicity
1
at
\lambda=1
, and the corresponding eigenvectors are constants. The spectral theorem for bounded self-adjoint operators implies that

\mathrm{spec}(P_{a})\setminus\{\mathsf{1}\}=\mathrm{spec}(P_{a}|(\mathrm{span}\{\mathsf{1}\})^{\perp})=\mathrm{spec}(P_{a}|L_{0}^{2}(\mu)),

where the second equality follows because
L_{0}^{2}(\mu)
is the orthogonal complement of the space of constant functions. The result now follows.
∎

3
Motivation from Temporal Difference Policy Evaluation

Temporal difference (TD) policy evaluation is one of the main components of actor-critic algorithms, a common family of algorithms from reinforcement learning
[
26
]
. The main results of this section generalize existing sufficient conditions for the asymptotic approximation of the policy evaluation method
TD(\lambda)
to be stable. They also show how bounds on the corresponding matrix can be given in terms of spectral gaps.

Let
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function of the state space and let
\mathbf{x}_{k}
be a sequence of states generated by the Markov kernel,
P
. Let
\mu_{k}
be the distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{k}
. In the typical setting of average-cost reinforcement learning, it is assumed that there is a unique invariant distribution,
\mu
, such that
\mu_{k}
converges to
\mu
.

Let
\bar{c}=\mu c
. The
marginal value function
is given by:

V(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(c(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\bar{c}\right)\middle|\mathbf{x}_{0}=x\right]

and satisfies:

V=c-\mathsf{1}\bar{c}+PV

where
\mathsf{1}
is the constant function taking the value of
1
.

The
policy evaluation
problem involves estimating
V
, and temporal difference methods estimate
V
directly from observations of the Markov chain. When the state space,
\mathcal{X}
, is large or infinite, it is often impossible to compute
V
exactly. In the most basic setting, we fix a collection of “features",
\phi_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and search for parameters
\theta^{i}
such that:

V(x)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}(x)\theta^{i}=:\phi(x)^{\top}\theta.

A common temporal difference algorithm, known as
\mathrm{TD}(\lambda)
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}_{k}+\alpha_{k}(c(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\mathbf{J}_{k})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\left(c(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\mathbf{J}_{k}+\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}-\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\right)\mathbf{z}_{k}

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathbf{z}_{k}+\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})

where
\mathbf{J}_{k}
is an estimate of
\bar{c}
,
\alpha_{k}>0
is a sequence of step sizes, and
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

We will focus on the case that
\lambda\in[0,1)
. The case of
\lambda=1
requires alternate techniques outside the scope of the paper. See
[
16
]
.

As discussed in
[
28
]
, under standard stochastic approximation step size assumptions,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}=\infty
,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty
, and
\lambda\in[0,1)
, the values of
(\mathbf{J}_{k},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
from
\mathrm{TD}(\lambda)
behave, asymptotically, like solutions to the differential equation:

\displaystyle\frac{dJ_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\bar{c}-J_{t}

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=b-gJ_{t}+A\theta_{t},

where

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}(x)&\cdots&\phi_{n}(x)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

\displaystyle P^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{i}P^{i+1}

\displaystyle b_{i}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi_{i},c\rangle

\displaystyle g_{i}

\displaystyle=\mu\phi_{i}

\displaystyle A_{ij}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi_{i},(P^{(\lambda)}-I)\phi_{j}\rangle.

Note that in the case that
\lambda=0
, the algorithm simplifies, since we can take
\mathbf{z}_{k}=\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k})
. Furthermore, in this case,
P^{(0)}=P
.

If
A
is Hurwitz (i.e. all its eigenvalues have negative real parts), then the differential equation has a unique, asymptotically stable equilibrium at
\begin{bmatrix}\bar{c}\\
A^{-1}(g\bar{c}-b)\end{bmatrix}
. In this case,
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{J}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\end{bmatrix}
will converge to this equilibrium.

A variety of papers have derived sufficient conditions for
A
to be Hurwitz under different assumptions. The case of finite state spaces and
\lambda\in[0,1)
is examined in
[
28
]
, the case that
\mathcal{X}
is a Polish space,

is
V
-uniformly geometrically ergodic, and
\lambda\in(0,1]
is examined in
[
16
]
.

The following lemma shows that when
\lambda\in[0,1)

. It will be used to give sufficient conditions for

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

be a Markov kernel and

2.

If

If

4.

If

\mathcal{E}_{P^{(\lambda)}}(f,f)\geq(1-\lambda)\mathcal{E}_{P}(f,f)

5.

\mathrm{gap}(P^{(\lambda)})\geq(1-\lambda)\mathrm{gap}(P)
.

Note that for

Proposition 3.1

Let

.
Let

\displaystyle A_{ij}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{spec}(P_{a})}(1-\lambda)\langle w,E(d\lambda)w\rangle

Now, since

\langle w,E(\mathrm{spec}(P_{a})\setminus\{1\})w\rangle=\langle E(\mathrm{spec}(P_{a})\setminus\{1\})w,E(\mathrm{spec}(P_{a})\setminus\{1\})w\rangle=0,

Furthermore, since

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\left(E(\mathrm{spec}(P_{a})\setminus\{1\})+E(\{1\})\right)w

\displaystyle=E(\{1\})w,

which implies that

is in the range space of

implies that the range space of

Proposition 3.2
.

Let

be a Markov kernel with invariant probability distribution

and

\displaystyle S_{ij}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle-\overline{(\mu\phi_{i})}(\mu\phi_{j}).

Let

\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{\star})\preceq-(1-\lambda)\mathrm{gap}(P)\gamma I.

(6)

In particular, the eigenvalues of

-(1-\lambda)\mathrm{gap}(P)\gamma
.

If the following conditions hold:

1.

\mathrm{gap}(P)>0

2.

the functions

are linearly independent,

3.

the constant function,

Let

by

. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}v^{\star}(A+A^{\star})v

\displaystyle\leq-(1-\lambda)\mathcal{E}_{P}(w,w)

Now, assume that

If, furthermore,

, the bound satisfies

4
Sufficient Conditions for Positive Spectral Gaps

Proposition
3.2
shows that a key condition to get
A
Hurwitz is that the Markov chain has non-zero spectral gap. In this section, we describe a collection of sufficient conditions that ensure a positive spectral gap.
Subsection
4.1
shows that
P
has a positive gap if it admits a kernel which is sufficiently well-behaved. This result, in particular, suffices to prove that irreducible Markov chains over finite state spaces have positive spectral gaps. Subsection
4.2
shows how to derive bounds on spectral gaps by comparison of one Markov operator with another. In particular, the results show that uniformly geometrically ergodic Markov chains have positive spectral gaps. Subsection
4.3
gives bounds on spectral gaps in the commonly studied case of linear Gaussian systems.

4.1
Kernel Conditions

The following proposition gives a sufficient condition for a positive spectral gap which is often satisfied in practice. Below,
\mu\otimes\mu
denotes the product measure of
\mu
over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
. In particular, it will be satisfied for Markov chains on finite state spaces.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
P
be an irreducible Markov kernel with invariant probability measure,
\mu
. If
P
admits a kernel density with respect to
\mu
,
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that
K\in L^{2}(\mu\otimes\mu)
, then
\mathrm{gap}(P)>0
.

For compact notation, let
P_{a}=\frac{1}{2}(P+P^{\star})
denote the additive reversibilization of
P
. Then
P_{a}
admits a kernel density with respect to
\mu
,
K_{r}
defined by
K_{r}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(K(x,y)+K(y,x))
. The assumption that
K\in L^{2}(\mu\otimes\mu)
implies that
K_{r}\in L^{2}(\mu\otimes\mu)
, as well.

So, we have that

P_{a}f(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dy)K_{r}(x,y)f(y)

(7)

A classical result shows that if

can be represented in the form of (

), with
\mu
a positive measure and

, then

is a compact operator. See, e.g. Proposition 4.7 of
[
6
]
.

It follows that the only possible accumulation point of
\mathrm{spec}(P_{a})
is at
0
. It follows that
\sup\left(\mathrm{spec}(P_{a})\setminus\{1\}\right)<1
, and so, Lemma
2.2
implies the result.
∎

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
P
be an irreducible Markov kernel with invariant probability distribution
\mu
over a finite set
\mathcal{X}
.
If
\mu(\{x\})>0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then
\mathrm{gap}(P)>0
.

If
\mu(\{x\})>0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then
P
admits
a kernel density,
K(x,y)=P(x,\{y\})/\mu(\{y\})
, and
K\in L^{2}(\mu\otimes\mu)
since the space is finite.
∎

4.2
Comparison Conditions

In this subsection, we show how
\mathrm{gap}(P)
can be bounded below in terms of properties of an auxiliary Markov chain or probability measure. In particular, Lemma
4.4
shows that if
\mathrm{absgap}(P)>0
then
\mathrm{gap}(P)>0
, while Proposition
4.1
shows that if
P
is uniformly geometrically ergodic, then
\mathrm{gap}(P)>0
.

The following is an extension of a classical comparison lemma (proved for reversible chains on a finite state space in
[
19
]
) for general Markov kernels. Closely related comparison theorems for non-reversible finite-state Markov chains are given in
[
5
]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
P
and
Q
be Markov kernels over
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
with respective invariant probability distributions
\mu_{P}
and
\mu_{Q}
. If

•

\mu_{P}\ll\mu_{Q}

•

\mu_{Q}\ll\mu_{P}

•

there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
P(x,A)\geq\epsilon Q(x,A)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and all
A\in\mathcal{B}
,

then

\mathrm{gap}(P)\geq\frac{\epsilon\mathrm{gap}(Q)}{\left\|\frac{d\mu_{P}}{d\mu_{Q}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{Q})}\left\|\frac{d\mu_{Q}}{d\mu_{P}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{P})}}.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{P}
and
\mathcal{E}_{Q}
be the respective Dirichlet forms for
P
and
Q
, and let
\mathrm{var}_{P}
and
\mathrm{var}_{Q}
be the respective variance functionals. The absolute continuity conditions imply that the Radon-Nikodym derivatives
\frac{d\mu_{P}}{d\mu_{Q}}
and
\frac{d\mu_{Q}}{d\mu_{P}}
exist. Furthermore,
\mu_{P}
and
\mu_{Q}
have the same sets of measure zero, and so
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{Q})}=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{P})}
for
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
.

For measurable
v:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{Q}(v,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu_{Q}(dx)Q(x,dy)|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu_{P}(dx)\frac{d\mu_{Q}}{d\mu_{P}}(x)Q(x,dy)|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left\|\frac{d\mu_{Q}}{d\mu_{P}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{P})}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu_{P}(dx)P(x,dy)|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left\|\frac{d\mu_{Q}}{d\mu_{P}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{P})}\mathcal{E}_{P}(v,v).

Using that
\mu_{P}v=\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[|v(\mathbf{x})-y|^{2}]
, where
\mathbb{E}_{P}
is the expectation with respect to
P
gives:

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}_{P}(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu_{P}(dx)|v(x)-\mu_{P}v|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu_{P}(dx)|v(x)-\mu_{Q}v|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu_{Q}(dx)\frac{d\mu_{P}}{d\mu_{Q}}(x)|v(x)-\mu_{Q}v|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{d\mu_{P}}{d\mu_{Q}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{Q})}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu_{Q}(dx)|v(x)-\mu_{Q}v|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{d\mu_{P}}{d\mu_{Q}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{Q})}\mathrm{var}_{Q}(v).

Combining the bounds shows that if
\mathrm{var}_{P}(v)>0
, then
\mathrm{var}_{Q}(v)>0
and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{E}_{P}(v,v)}{\mathrm{var}_{P}(v)}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{\left\|\frac{d\mu_{P}}{d\mu_{Q}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{Q})}\left\|\frac{d\mu_{Q}}{d\mu_{P}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{P})}}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(v,v)}{\mathrm{var}_{Q}(v)}

Minimizing both sides over
v
gives the result.
∎

In many cases, it is easier to work with the sampled chain,

Lemma 4.3
.

Let

In particular, if

\mathrm{gap}(P)>0
.

Let
P_{a}=\frac{1}{2}(P+P^{\star})
be the additive reversibilization of

with respect to
\mu

\mu
is invariant for

,

, and

For all

P_{a}^{m}(x,A)\geq\frac{1}{2^{m}}P^{m}(x,A).

Now, since

The spectral mapping theorem shows that

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

4.3
Linear Gaussian Systems

Now we use Lemma

Let
P
be the Markov operator corresponding to the linear Gaussian of the form:

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=F\mathbf{x}_{k}+G\mathbf{v}_{k}

(12)

where
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and and
\mathbf{v}_{k}

0
and covariance

The main result of this section, Proposition

Let
\prec
and
\preceq
denote the positive definite and positive semi-definite partial orders, respectively. Let
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denote the spectral norm for matrices.
The following elementary lemma is required for the main results of the subsection.

Lemma 4.7
.

Say that the system
(F,G)
is controllable and all eigenvalues of

have magnitude less than
1
.

1.

P
is irreducible.

2.

The unique invariant distribution for

, is a Gaussian with mean

S=GG^{\top}+FSF^{\top}.

3.

For all integers

4.

For

S
also satisfies the Lyapunov equation:

S=W+ASA^{\top}.

5.

If
\tilde{A}=S^{-\frac{1}{2}}F^{m}S^{\frac{1}{2}}

\|\tilde{A}\|_{2}<1

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}F^{i}GG^{\top}(F^{i})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}F^{i}GG^{\top}(F^{i})^{\top}+F^{m}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}F^{i}GG^{\top}(F^{i})^{\top}\right)(F^{m})^{\top}.

\tilde{A}=S^{-\frac{1}{2}}AS^{\frac{1}{2}}

. The Markov kernel in this subsection is a simplified version of the Markov kernel from

. The Markov kernel from
[

]
was used in

]
to give an example of a Markov kernel which is

-uniformly geometrically ergodic, but has
\mathrm{absgap}(P)=0
. By Example
Example
, having
\mathrm{absgap}(P)=0
does not necessarily imply that
\mathrm{gap}(P)=0
. In contrast, by Lemma

, if we can show that

\mathrm{absgap}(P)=0

Proposition 5.1
.

There is a Markov chain on a countable state space which is
V
-uniformly geometrically ergodic, but has
\mathrm{gap}(P)=\mathrm{absgap}(P)=0
.

The state space for the Markov chain is

\mathcal{X}=\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{a=1}^{\infty}\{(a,1),(a,2),\ldots,(a,a)\}.

For simpler notation, we identify
P(x,\{y\})
with
P(x,y)
and identify
\mu(\{x\})
with
\mu(x)
when
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
. The Markov kernel is given by:

P(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&x=y=0\\
2^{-a-1}&x=0,y=(a,a)\\
1&x=(a,b),y=(a,b-1)\textrm{ with }1<b\leq a\\
1&x=(a,1),y=0\textrm{ with }a\geq 1\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

and its invariant probability measure is

\mu(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&x=0\\
2^{-a-2}&x=(a,b)\textrm{ with }1\leq b\leq a.\end{cases}

Here
P
is irreducible and aperiodic, and
0
is a recurrent atom. In particular,
\{0\}
is a petite set. We will show that for any
\rho\in(1,\sqrt{2})
that
P
is
V
-uniformly geometrically ergodic with

V(x)=\begin{cases}\rho^{b}&x=(a,b)\textrm{ with }1\leq b\leq a\\
1&x=0.\end{cases}

Direct calculation shows that
P
and
V
satisfy the drift inequality:

PV(x)\leq\rho^{-1}V(x)+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rho}{2-\rho}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{0\}}(x)\textrm{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X}.

Furthermore,

\|V\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rho^{2}}{2-\rho^{2}}.

So,
P
is
V
-uniformly geometrically ergodic for some
V\in L^{2}(\mu)
.

Now we turn to showing that
P_{a}=\frac{1}{2}(P+P^{\star})
has a sequence of eigenvalues (with respect to
L^{2}(\mu)
) which converges to
1
.

Since
\mu(y)>0
for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
, we have that
P
admits a kernel density given by
K(x,y)=P(x,y)/\mu(y)
. The corresponding adjoint kernel is given by
P^{\star}(x,y)=K(y,x)\mu(y):

P^{\star}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&x=y=0\\
2^{-a-1}&x=0,y=(a,1)\\
1&x=(a,b),y=(a,b+1)\textrm{ with }1\leq b<a\\
1&x=(a,a),y=0\textrm{ with }a\geq 1\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If we order the states like
0,(1,1),(2,1),(2,2),\ldots,(n,1),\ldots,(n,n),\ldots
, then
P_{a}=\frac{1}{2}(P+P^{\star})
can be represented by the infinite matrix:

P_{a}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{2^{2+2}}c_{2}^{\top}&\frac{1}{2^{3+2}}c_{3}^{\top}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2^{n+2}}c_{n}^{\top}&\cdots\\
1&0&0&0&\cdots&0&\cdots\\
\frac{1}{2}c_{2}&0&\frac{1}{2}M_{2}&0&\cdots&0&\cdots\\
\frac{1}{2}c_{3}&0&0&\frac{1}{2}M_{3}&&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{2}c_{n}&0&0&&&\frac{1}{2}M_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}

where

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}

\displaystyle M_{n}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&1\\
0&1&0&\\
&&&\ddots\\
&&&&0&1\\
&&&&1&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

So, if
v
is an eigenvector of
M_{n}
such that
\lambda v=M_{n}v
and
c_{n}^{\top}v=0
, then
\tilde{v}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
v\\
0\end{bmatrix}
(with appropriately sized zeros) is an eigenvector of
P_{a}
with
P_{a}\tilde{v}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\tilde{v}
. If
\frac{\lambda}{2}\neq 1
, then we must have
\mu\tilde{v}=0
, since

\mu P_{a}\tilde{v}=\mu\tilde{v}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\mu\tilde{v}\implies\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\mu\tilde{v}=0.

Furthermore, since
\tilde{v}
has only finitely many non-zero values, it must have
\|\tilde{v}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\infty
.

We will show, in particular, that for all
k\geq 2
, there is an eigenvector
v_{k}
of
M_{2^{k}-1}
such that
M_{2^{k}-1}v_{k}=\lambda_{k-2}v_{k}
and
c_{2^{k}-1}^{\top}v_{k}=0
, where
\lambda_{k-2}
is defined by the recursion:

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\lambda_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2+\lambda_{i}}.

If
0\leq\lambda_{i}<2
, the recursion satisfies
\lambda_{i}<\lambda_{i+1}<2
. So,
\lambda_{i}
must converge to a fixed point of the recursion in the interval
[0,2]
, and the only such fixed point is
2
. Thus, the corresponding sequence of eigenvectors of
P_{a}
, denoted by
\tilde{v}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
v_{k}\\
0\end{bmatrix}
, satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{P}(\tilde{v}_{k},\tilde{v}_{k})}{\mathrm{var}(\tilde{v}_{k})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\langle\tilde{v}_{k},(I-P_{a})\tilde{v}_{k}\rangle}{\|\tilde{v}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{k-2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

So, if such eigenvalues and eigenvectors exist, we must have
\mathrm{gap}(P)=0
, and then Lemma
4.4
implies
\mathrm{absgap}(P)=0
as well.

To construct the required eigenvectors, we will derive several properties of the eigenvectors of
M_{n}
for

. For consistency of the analysis, we set
M_{1}=0\in\mathbb{R}
.

Define a sequence of polynomials,
q_{i}
by:

\displaystyle q_{0}(s)

\displaystyle=1

(20a)

\displaystyle q_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=s

(20b)

\displaystyle q_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=sq_{i-1}(s)-q_{i-2}(s)\textrm{ for }i\geq 2.

(20c)

Throughout this proof,
s
will denote the general variable for the polynomials constructed.

Manipulation of the equation
M_{n}v=\lambda v
shows that the characteristic polynomial of
M_{n}
is
q_{n}
and

is an eigenvector of
M_{n}
if and only if it has the form

v=z\begin{bmatrix}q_{0}(\lambda)\\
q_{1}(\lambda)\\
\vdots\\
q_{n-1}(\lambda)\end{bmatrix}

(21)

where
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
M_{n}
and

is a non-zero scalar. Note that
q_{0}(\lambda)=1
, so that

as long as

. In particular, when choosing an eigenvector, we can set

.

To find the desired eigenvalues and eigenvectors, we will also utilize the following auxiliary sequence of polynomials:

\displaystyle r_{0}(s)

\displaystyle=s

\displaystyle r_{i+1}(s)

\displaystyle=r_{i}(s)^{2}-2\textrm{ for }i\geq 0.

The existence of the desired eigenvalue/eigenvector pairs is proved from the following claims:

1.

For

2.

The roots of

are given by
\hat{\lambda}_{i,0},\cdots,\hat{\lambda}_{i,2^{i}-1}
which are defined recursively by:

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{0,0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{i+1,2j}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2+\hat{\lambda}_{i,j}}\textrm{ for }j=0,\ldots,2^{i}-1

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{i+1,2j+1}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{2+\hat{\lambda}_{i,j}}\textrm{ for }j=0,\ldots,2^{i}-1

3.

If
q_{n}(\lambda)=0
, then
q_{n+i}(\lambda)=-q_{n-i}(\lambda)
for

.

Before proving the claims, we show how they can be used to get the desired eigenvalue/eigenvector pair: Claim

implies that any root of

must also be a root of
q_{2^{k-1}-1}
and
q_{2^{k}-1}
. Comparing the recursions for

shows that

for

. In particular,

. Then
q_{2^{k}-1}(\lambda_{k-2})=0

. Using that

v_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}q_{0}(\lambda_{k-2})\\

-q_{0}(\lambda_{k-2})\end{bmatrix}.

In particular, we have

.

Now we prove Claim
1
. At

, the formula holds since:

To prove the claim, assume inductively that the formula from the claim holds at some

q_{2^{k+1}-1}(s)=q_{2^{k}-1}(s)r_{k}(s).

(22)

To prove (

\displaystyle q_{n}(s)=q_{i}(s)q_{n-i}(s)-q_{i-1}(s)q_{n-i-1}(s)

The case of

If for

Thus, to prove (

(24)

The proof of (

\displaystyle r_{n+1}(s)=r_{1}(r_{n}(s))=r_{1}(r_{i-1}(r_{n-(i-1)}(s)))=r_{i}(r_{n+1-i}(s))=r_{i}(r_{n+1-i}(s)).

6
Conclusion

This paper gives methods to compute bounds on (Dirichlet) spectral gaps for non-reversible Markov chains, motivated by analysis of temporal difference algorithms. The current results give general approaches to computing spectral gap bounds and specific bounds for the case of linear Gaussian systems.
Further bounds for spectral gaps of Markov chains are certainly possible. For example,
[
5
]
gives bounds on the spectral gap for finite-state Markov chains via a variation on Cheeger’s inequality. Future work will examine the interplay between stability for nonlinear stochastic systems and spectral gap bounds, as well as continuous-time variations.
