Executive Summary
The EDGE project, supported by the U.S. Department of Energy Geothermal Technologies Office
under award DE-EE0008793, established a data-driven framework for improving the efficiency,
cost-effectiveness, and reliability of geothermal well drilling. The project focused on developing
scalable data infrastructure, advanced machine learning and probabilistic models, and integrated
analytics tools to support continuous drilling optimization. A central objective was to reduce
geothermal drilling costs by up to seventy percent while minimizing the risk of well failure through
predictive diagnostics and adaptive planning.
Over the project period, a comprehensive data repository was designed and deployed,
incorporating records from over one hundred geothermal wells across varied geological settings.
This repository supported both structured and unstructured data and adhered to FAIR data
principles, enabling provenance tracking, quality control, and standardized metadata. The project
introduced automated ingestion pipelines and a cloud-hosted platform that facilitated access to
raw, processed, and derived datasets. This infrastructure served as the foundation for model
development and analysis.
Machine learning workflows were developed to predict key drilling metrics including rate of
penetration, non-productive time, and total drilling costs. Self-organizing maps and dimensionality
reduction methods were used to uncover operational patterns and outliers, while supervised
learning algorithms such as random forests and deep neural networks were applied to forecast
performance outcomes. The models were validated on heterogeneous datasets from both U.S.
and Icelandic fields, demonstrating variable but significant predictive accuracy. The results
indicated that finer temporal resolution, inclusion of lithological data, and consistency in
operational annotations could substantially improve model performance. The project also
implemented process mining techniques to reconstruct state-transition models from drilling event
logs. These models enabled the identification of deviations from optimal workflows and provided
insights into recurring failure modes. Analysis of non-productive time highlighted the impact of
equipment failures, geological challenges, and human factors, offering opportunities for targeted
mitigation strategies.
The EDGE Dashboard was developed as a web-based expert system integrating data
visualization, model outputs, and user-driven queries. It provided an accessible interface for
operators to explore historical data, evaluate predicted outcomes, and compare drilling scenarios.
Initial feedback from project partners suggested that the dashboard could serve as a foundation
for more advanced advisory and optimization tools.
Overall, the EDGE project demonstrated the feasibility and value of applying modern data science
techniques to geothermal drilling. It delivered a set of interoperable tools and models that can
support more efficient, lower-risk well development. The findings point toward a viable path for
transitioning from advisory analytics to semi-autonomous drilling systems, contingent on
continued collaboration, expanded datasets, and field validation. The project results have
immediate relevance for drilling operations, data management practices, and future geothermal
R&D efforts aimed at achieving reliable, cost-competitive geothermal energy at scale.

Overview
The DOE-funded project DE-EE0008793, "Toward Drilling the Perfect Geothermal Well" (2019–
2022), referred to hereafter as the EDGE project, is an international research coordination
network supported by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Geothermal Technologies Office.
The project is focused on developing machine learning strategies to improve geothermal drilling
efficiency, with particular emphasis on cost reduction and early identification of well failures
(Figure 1). The EDGE research team includes three U.S. Universities, a DOE National Laboratory
and four Geothermal and Oil and Gas companies from several countries (Iceland, Norway, USA).
The aim of the project is to use data from different geothermal fields to develop a continuous
optimization framework for geothermal drilling. The resulting optimization scheme will be able to
continuously improve as new data are ingested into the database. The project is structured in two
yearlong phases with specific tasks focusing on data collection and management, model
development and drilling optimization, and identification and mitigation of well failures.

time identification of data gaps, and generation of actionable insights to support drilling optimization, cost
reduction, and early well failure detection and mitigation. The overall goal is to accelerate progress toward
data-driven, autonomous geothermal well design and operation.

Background
Optimizing geothermal well drilling requires an integrated approach that combines modern data
infrastructure, machine learning (ML), and advanced optimization strategies. This background
section provides an overview of the current state of the art in three key areas that are central to
the project. First, it examines recent advances in geothermal data management, including cloud-
based infrastructure, standardized schemas, and FAIR data principles. Second, it reviews the
application of data analytics and ML for improving drilling performance, highlighting emerging

 1

techniques for prediction, anomaly detection, and interpretable modeling. Third, it discusses
modern approaches to geothermal drilling optimization, with a focus on algorithmic decision
support, real-time control, and the progression toward autonomous drilling systems. Collectively,
these developments define the technical context in which this project operates. The final
subsection illustrates how it contributed to innovations in geothermal science and engineering.

Geothermal Data Management

Geothermal drilling data are often heterogeneous (e.g., time-series sensor readings, stratigraphic
data, daily reports, cost logs, etc.). Modern optimization efforts have therefore invested in data
platforms that can aggregate, standardize, and securely share these datasets. However, simply
collecting data is not enough and data products must be curated and served to analytics and ML
workflows in a consistent format. Recent developments have further advanced the data platform
landscape for geothermal. For instance, DOE’s Geothermal Data Repository (GDR) has been
evolving into a more powerful resource to support ML projects. In 2023–2024, NREL and partners
introduced data lakes and automated data pipelines in the GDR to handle high-volume, streaming
drilling data and efficient management of big data assets. Cloud-based data lakes allow users to
access and query large drilling datasets without complex and lengthy downloads, and they
facilitate easier data ingestion directly from field sources. The DOE is also pushing for data
standardization across geothermal projects. Examples of this effort include the development
standard schemas for time-series drilling data, adoption of industry standards like PRODML for
well logs, and requiring comprehensive metadata. Standardization greatly enhances data
interoperability and reusability, aligning with FAIR principles by making it easier for different
scientists, engineers (and ultimately, AI agents) to use each other’s data without ad-hoc
reformatting.
In industry, geothermal operators are beginning to leverage cloud-based drilling data platforms
(e.g., adaptations/extensions of oil and gas digital solutions) to centralize well data from planning
through completion. The net result is that geothermal drilling analysis is increasingly supported
by modern data infrastructure: cloud databases, real-time data streaming, and consortium-based
data sharing agreements. These platforms ensure that sophisticated ML/AI models and
optimization algorithms have reliable, rich data to learn from, and they lower the barrier for new
analytics by treating data as a well-managed asset rather than an afterthought.
Data Analytics and Machine Learning for Geothermal Drilling Performance

Recent years have seen significant adoption of advanced ML techniques to analyze and optimize
geothermal drilling. Modern ML models are being used to predict key performance metrics such
as rate of penetration (ROP), non-productive time (NPT), drilling problems (e.g. stuck pipe, lost
circulation), and even costs. Researchers have explored a wide range of algorithms (from
ensemble tree methods to deep neural networks) to capture the complex, nonlinear relationships
in drilling data. For example, a 2022 study at the Utah FORGE site applied both random forest
regressors and neural networks to predict ROP, finding that a tuned random forest could explain
~84% of the variability in field ROP In addition to regression models, recent work has emphasized
classification and anomaly detection via ML. Real-time drilling incident detection systems have
been prototyped using systematic feature engineering and ML to predict when hole problems are

 2

likely to occur. This field study in New Zealand demonstrated that machine learning could
successfully predict hole-related NPT incidents even under challenging geothermal conditions
(e.g. total lost circulation), allowing proactive mitigation. Notably, decision tree-based models
achieved strong performance in distinguishing normal vs. problematic drilling states. Such
capabilities represent an evolution beyond the primarily post hoc analyses of the past – enabling
drilling teams to anticipate failures and adjust parameters in real time.

Given the high stakes of well decisions, there is interest in interpretable ML (e.g., feature
importance, SHAP analyses) to identify which factors (weight on bit, rotations per minute, mud
properties, etc.) drive a predicted outcome. Some recent approaches train explainable ensemble
models to ensure that engineers can trust and understand the recommendations. Moreover,
hybrid physics-ML models are being explored, where domain knowledge (e.g. drilling physics
equations or rock mechanics) is embedded into the learning process to improve model robustness
on limited data. Overall, the trend from 2022 to 2025 has been to leverage state-of-the-art data
science including deep learning, transfer learning, and even federated learning to extract more
value from the relatively sparse datasets in geothermal drilling. Recent research efforts introduced
a transformer deep learning model to improve ROP prediction by capturing temporal
dependencies in drilling parameters. And looking ahead, researchers are investigating federated
learning as a privacy-preserving way for multiple operators to collaboratively train models on their
combined drilling data without directly sharing sensitive data. Federated approaches will be critical
to enable sector-wide ML models that learn from a much larger pool of geothermal wells while
respecting proprietary concerns.

Geothermal Drilling Optimization

Complementing the predictive models, advanced optimization algorithms are being applied to
drilling program planning and real-time control. A core aim of these approaches is to determine
the optimal setpoints and practices that maximize ROP and minimize costs or risks for a given
well scenario. Traditional drilling engineering relied on heuristic or physics-based calculations to
choose parameters like weight on bit (WOB), rotary speed, and mud properties. Now, data-driven
optimization approaches use the outputs of ML models or probabilistic models to search for the
best decisions. Data-driven optimization marks a shift from purely experience-based planning to
evidence-based, computational planning. Contemporary efforts have embraced multi-objective
optimization algorithms to balance competing goals (e.g. drilling speed versus tool wear or risk).
Researchers have used evolutionary algorithms and integer programming for well design
optimization, such as choosing casing depths, bit sequences, or trajectory that minimize cost
while meeting safety constraints. Moreover, some drilling optimization systems integrate real-time
sensor feedback with control algorithms using reinforcement learning or adaptive control
techniques. In the oil & gas sector, automated drilling control systems use real-time data to adjust
WOB and bit RPM to maintain optimal drilling efficiency (e.g. minimizing mechanical specific
energy). The geothermal industry is beginning to adopt similar technology for closed-loop
optimization during drilling. While true reinforcement learning for autonomous geothermal drilling
is still in early research, the groundwork is being laid. These approaches illustrate the expanding
scope of optimization: not only planning the initial drilling program optimally, but continually

 3

updating the plan as conditions change and even automating on-the-fly decisions to approach a
“perfect” drilling process.

Comparison of project outcomes with the current state-of-the-art
The EDGE project was an innovative effort that encapsulated many of the techniques in use today.
It pioneered the integration of ML, data analytics, and expert systems for geothermal well
optimization, producing the EDGE dashboard, a cloud-based tool providing data-driven guidance
for drilling programs. The EDGE project’s approach can be seen as a foundation upon which
current state-of-the-art methods have expanded. Similarities between EDGE and current
approaches include the central role of ML models to inform decision-making, the use of diverse
algorithms (from random forests to neural networks and advanced AI methods) to predict drilling
outcomes, and the emphasis on continuous improvement of recommendations as more data are
gathered. Both EDGE and later efforts recognize the importance of comprehensive data
management: EDGE built a federated data repository to aggregate multi-operator data, reflecting
FAIR data values, and this philosophy is continued and amplified by initiatives like the GDR
improvements. Additionally, the inclusion of process mining and probabilistic modeling in the
EDGE project signaled a forward-looking mindset that is consistent with recent trends. In fact,
many of the subsequent cutting-edge developments (e.g., NLP for drilling logs, real-time incident
prediction, etc.) were already foreshadowed by EDGE’s comprehensive scope.
That said, there are clear advancements beyond the scope of the original project that have
emerged in the past couple of years. One major difference is the move toward real-time and
closed-loop optimization. EDGE’s expert system was primarily designed as an advisory tool that
could recommend optimized plans or parameters, which human drillers would then implement.
Current efforts are pushing this further by integrating ML predictions directly into drilling control
systems for real-time adjustments (an approach common in advanced oil & gas operations, now
translating into geothermal). Another advancement is the increasing sophistication of AI/ML
models themselves. While EDGE employed state-of-the-art techniques available up to 2022 (e.g.
self-organizing maps, dual-path deep networks, random forests), recent projects have begun
experimenting with architectures like transformers for sequential sensor data and advanced
ensemble methods for greater accuracy. There is also a stronger focus on model interpretability
and uncertainty quantification now, ensuring that AI-driven recommendations can be explained
and trusted in high-risk drilling decisions. Data sharing mechanisms have also evolved. The
EDGE consortium relied on a central shared database maintained by PNNL. By contrast, the
notion of federated learning and decentralized data collaboration is gaining traction, potentially
enabling broader industry participation beyond a single consortium. This could address one
limitation of the original project: the sensitivity of proprietary data. Federated approaches let
companies benefit from collective learning without handing over raw data, thus possibly involving
more partners and more wells than EDGE’s 113-well dataset. Moreover, the sector now benefits
from larger volumes of open data (through GDR data lakes and other FAIR repositories) than
were readily available a few years ago, which can enhance model generality beyond what EDGE
could achieve with its initial dataset. In summary, the DOE’s "Drilling the Perfect Geothermal Well"
project introduced a comprehensive data-science-driven paradigm for geothermal well
optimization – leveraging ML models, an expert dashboard, process analysis, and curated data.

Current state-of-the-art approaches build upon this foundation with incremental and sometimes
transformative improvements: more advanced algorithms (deep learning and hybrid models),
real-time deployment of optimization, novel techniques like process mining fully integrated, and
improved data platforms enabling wider collaboration. The trajectory from EDGE to today shows
a clear progression toward smarter, faster, and more autonomous geothermal drilling optimization.
The similarities affirm the soundness of the original project’s vision, while the differences and new
capabilities highlight how the field is continuously advancing – moving ever closer to the ideal of
“drilling the perfect geothermal well” with minimal cost, time, and risk.

Objectives
The overarching objectives of the project were to significantly reduce geothermal drilling costs,
targeting up to a 70 percent reduction, and to lower the risk of well failure through the development
of an integrated, data-driven optimization framework. To achieve these goals, the project focused
on three core areas: data infrastructure, intelligent modeling, and continuous improvement. First,
the project emphasized the design and deployment of a robust data collection and management
system capable of integrating diverse geothermal drilling datasets. This included both structured
and unstructured data such as time-series sensor readings, lithological logs, daily operations
reports, and cost records. The system was designed to ensure data quality, maintain provenance,
and comply with FAIR data principles to support transparency, traceability, and reuse. Second,
the project applied advanced data analytics and machine learning methods to model drilling
performance, forecast failure modes, and inform operational decisions. Third, the project
implemented a continuous optimization loop based on machine learning, allowing drilling program
parameters to be updated dynamically as new data were incorporated. This adaptive framework
was designed to support real-time learning and iterative refinement, enabling more efficient, cost-
effective, and resilient geothermal well development.

Technical Approach
The project implemented a comprehensive, scalable framework to address key data, modeling,
and adoption challenges in geothermal drilling optimization. The technical approach integrated
sustainable data infrastructure, advanced machine learning (ML) pipelines, and deployment of
user-facing tools to enable actionable insights from complex, heterogeneous well datasets.
Emphasizing traceability, reproducibility, and compliance with DOE data standards, the team
leveraged a hybrid cloud/on-premises architecture, state-of-the-art ML methods, and a modular
dashboard interface to support end-to-end analysis and decision-making. Each component of the
approach was designed with scalability and technology transfer in mind, ensuring practical
relevance to operators and paving the road toward long-term sustainability beyond the project
performance period.

Results and Discussion
The project’s technical execution was structured around four core activities that collectively
advanced the goal of improving geothermal drilling efficiency through cost reduction and early
failure identification. The first activity, Data Management and Data Repository, focused on
building a scalable infrastructure to support ingestion, standardized curation, and secure access
to diverse geothermal datasets. This included the implementation of metadata standards,
automated pipelines, and provenance tracking to ensure FAIR compliance and long-term usability.
The second activity, Data Analytics and Modeling, involved the development of supervised and
unsupervised machine learning models for predicting drilling outcomes such as rate of
penetration, non-productive time, and total well cost. The third activity, Well Failure Analysis,
emphasized the identification and classification of common failure modes (e.g., bit wear, lost
circulation) using predictive and root-cause analysis techniques. Finally, Enabling Further Data
Analytics and Cost Reduction addressed the integration of project outputs into a user-facing
expert system designed to facilitate industry adoption and promote continued analytics-driven
cost optimization. The following sections present outcomes from each activity, including technical
achievements, performance metrics, and insights into implementation challenges and
opportunities for future expansion.

Activity 2: Data Analytics and Modeling
Data-driven approaches are key for modeling and optimizing the operation of geothermal wells.
However, data collected at different drilling sites are usually not standardized and contain missing
or erroneous values which hinder the development of reliable models that can be used across a
broad range of conditions and geological contexts.
Project collaborators led the analysis of data to develop the well optimization system. The data
analysis process started by applying feature selection techniques to identify, from the set of
variables identified during the data mining process, the most relevant features for model
development. Filter and wrapper approaches were used for feature selection and ranking

Data Analytics
Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) was conducted to assess the structure, quality, and operational
variability of geothermal drilling datasets from U.S. and Icelandic sources. The analysis focused
on identifying statistical distributions, unit inconsistencies, outliers, and missing values, while also
providing guidance for data preprocessing and feature engineering. Dimensionality reduction
methods such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor
Embedding (t-SNE) were applied to uncover patterns in high-dimensional spaces. To further
explore temporal and operational dynamics, Self-Organizing Maps (SOMs) were used to cluster
drilling regimes and trace well-specific trajectories through operational states, enabling visual
interpretation of drilling behavior across geological contexts. In parallel, process mining
techniques were employed to reconstruct operational workflows from event logs, revealing state
transitions, identifying non-productive time (NPT) patterns, and quantifying deviations from
expected operation sequences. Together, these EDA approaches established a robust foundation
for predictive modeling and operational optimization.
Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)
EDA US Dataset. Analysis of the database revealed two primary sources of drilling data: the
dailybit and dailydrill tables. The dailybit table contains drilling and bit-related metrics averaged
over the lifespan of each drill bit, while the dailydrill table provides drilling parameters averaged

wells. All measurements are reported in U.S. customary units.

Self-Organizing Map Analysis
The Self-Organizing Map (SOM) is an unsupervised learning algorithm that maps high-
dimensional data onto a lower-dimensional grid while preserving topological relationships. Each
SOM unit represents a reference vector (codebook) that adjusts during training based on
neighborhood-based competition, producing both vector quantization and an ordered, reduced
representation of the input space.
SOMs are particularly useful for visualizing complex datasets. Each unit on the map corresponds
to an n-dimensional weight vector, where n matches the dimensionality of the input data. The grid
can be structured as rectangular or hexagonal, and its size determines the resolution of the
mapping. The algorithm's component planes (c-planes) show the distribution of each variable

 19

across the SOM, making it possible to interpret feature correlations and spatial patterns. Clusters
emerge as groups of neighboring units with similar codebook vectors. These can be detected
visually or enhanced using clustering algorithms like K-means. New data can be projected onto
the trained map, and time-dependent processes can be visualized as trajectories across the grid,
enabling the identification of dynamic regimes or system transitions. This makes SOMs a powerful
tool for both static and temporal pattern recognition in high-dimensional data.
We apply the SOM methodology to data provided by ISOR corresponding to the operation of
drilling wells located in different locations across Iceland. The data collected for these wells
includes the drilling features listed in Figure 11. After a data preprocessing stage that included
filtering erroneous and incomplete data followed by normalization and scaling, the set of 17
features were used to train a self-organizing map using a hexagonal lattice without periodic
boundary conditions.

Process Mining
Operational data on drilling and geothermal well performance are routinely collected by plant
operators as discrete event logs, which capture sequences of events and their precise timestamps
(Table 3). Analyzing these logs enables the reconstruction of process models that help identify
deviations from standard operating conditions and provide early warnings of potential faults.
Historically, event log analysis has relied on probabilistic models such as Markov chains, where
the process is represented as a set of states with defined transition probabilities. In this
framework, each entry (i,j) in the transition matrix specifies the likelihood of moving from state i to
state j, with transitions dependent solely on the current state.
Recently, process mining has emerged as a powerful framework for deriving insights from event
logs. Process mining combines algorithmic techniques and domain knowledge to reconstruct and
analyze process models directly from operational data. Its three main capabilities include:
 • Process discovery, which generates models directly from logs without prior assumptions;
 • Conformance checking, which compares observed behavior against predefined models to
 identify deviations; and
 • Performance analysis, which overlays process models with temporal or cost data to detect
 bottlenecks and inefficiencies.
Algorithms such as the alpha miner, heuristic miner, and inductive miner are commonly used to
generate interpretable models from raw event data.

Machine Learning Models
Before starting the development and test of any ML model, the choice of the variables that might
be considered as target variables (i.e., features that are related to well drilling performance) as
well as the definition of the well performance were thoroughly discussed. The project team has
identified four main criteria that should guide the definition of drilling performance: i) minimization

 26

of both drilling cost and drilling time; ii) comparison between scheduled and un-scheduled non-
productive time; iii) well usability and long-term durability; iv) injectivity or productivity success.
Following these criteria, the project team choose three main targets: the Rate of Penetration
(ROP), the non-productive time (NPT) and the drilling costs. For each of these targets specific
models were developed and will be discussed separately in the following sections.
Modeling Rate of Penetration
The rate of penetration (ROP) is one of the most significant parameters for evaluating drilling
efficiency and is widely adopted as an optimization target in the drilling industry (Alali et al., 2020).
Its appeal lies in its widespread availability and its strong correlation with total working hours, and
hence, overall drilling cost. As a result, ROP serves as a reliable proxy for assessing drilling
performance.
To model ROP, a Random Forest (RF) algorithm was applied independently to the two datasets
(United States and Iceland) due to their differences in feature sets and sampling rates. Prior to
modeling, both datasets underwent extensive preprocessing. Records with null or zero values in
either the ROP or weight on bit (WOB) columns were excluded, as they typically indicate non-
drilling activities. Remaining null or zero values in other fields were retained but flagged using a
binary mask. This mask, a matrix with the same dimensions as the dataset, marks the presence
of missing or invalid entries with a 1 and all others with a 0. This enables the model to account for
the original data quality during training. To mitigate overfitting, each dataset was partitioned into
training and testing subsets with an 80:20 split ratio. Model accuracy was assessed using the root
mean squared error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination (R²).
Random Forest is a non-parametric, supervised learning algorithm that builds an ensemble of
decision trees through recursive partitioning of the feature space. While individual decision trees
are capable of modeling nonlinear relationships, they tend to suffer from high variance and
overfitting. Random Forest addresses these issues through bootstrap aggregation and random
feature selection. Bootstrap resampling ensures that each tree is trained on a unique subset of
the data, while random selection of input features at each decision node promotes diversity
among trees. This ensemble approach reduces model variance and improves generalization
performance. The RF model is primarily controlled by three hyperparameters: the number of trees
in the forest, the size of the feature subset used at each split, and the size of the bootstrap sample.
In this study, RF models were implemented using Python's scikit-learn library, with default settings
for all hyperparameters.
ROP Models for US data. To prevent data leakage in the prediction of Rate of Penetration (ROP),
features directly derived from drilling time (BitHrs, ReportHrs) and footage (ReportFootage,
BitFootage) were excluded from the set of predictors, since ROP is defined as the ratio between
drilled footage and the corresponding drilling time. Figure 17a presents a cross plot comparing
predicted versus measured ROP values on the test dataset using the Random Forest (RF) model.
The model achieved an R² score of 0.56 and a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 6.07 ft/hr.
These metrics suggest that the RF model captures some underlying structure in the data but
exhibits limited predictive accuracy. The feature importance plot shown in Figure 17b highlights
the relative influence of input variables on the model's predictions. Among all considered features,

 27

BitWOBAvg and BitDiam emerge as the most influential, indicating that weight on bit and bit
diameter are critical drivers of ROP in the trained model.

Figure 17. ROP prediction with Random Forest on US dataset. a) cross plot of predicted versus actual
ROP values; b) Feature importance plot.

The obtained accuracy suggests that the actual predictors might not be sufficient to fully
characterize the relationship between the drilling parameters and the resulting ROP. The lack of
information regarding rock type and rock strength might be crucial, because starting with the same
predictors, different ROP can be achieved when drilling rocks with different rock strength. On the
other hand, the low prediction accuracy might not be related to the number and kind of features
but to the sampling frequency. Indeed, having a daily average data might probably smooth out
the complex relationship between the ROP and its predictors. To address this problem, new
features, such as lithological descriptions, were integrated into the ML models and well data with

Modeling Non-Productive Time
Non-Productive Time (NPT) is arguably the most critical metric for assessing the success or
failure of a geothermal drilling operation. The broad range of contributing factors, including
mechanical failures, formation-related issues, and procedural delays, makes it impractical to
develop a single predictive model that encompasses all possible NPT scenarios. A more effective
approach is to address individual causes of NPT separately. In this project, the analysis focused
on modeling tripping and cementing operations, which are frequently associated with elevated
NPT and have a significant influence on overall drilling performance and cost.
Modeling Trip Operations. Tripping operations are among the most time-intensive activities during
well drilling. Accurately forecasting the likelihood of tripping on the following day would enable
drillers to proactively implement mitigation strategies, particularly when tripping events are
unexpected or undesirable.

Probabilistic Modeling and Optimization
Probabilistic modeling based on Bayesian networks was used to represent the complex
relationships among drilling parameters while accounting for uncertainty and missing data. Given
the heterogeneity of geothermal drilling data—which includes both categorical variables such as
geological context and continuous variables such as operational parameters—a hybrid Bayesian
network model was implemented to handle mixed data types. The model was developed using
the bnlearn package in R, which supports learning conditional probability structures from data.
To enable reliable model construction, a comprehensive preprocessing step addressed the
presence of missing values in the EDGE datasets. Multivariate imputation was performed using
the mice package in R, with random forest models trained to capture dependencies among
operational features. These models were then used to impute missing entries, resulting in a
completed dataset that preserves the original statistical relationships (Figure 30). This imputed
dataset was added to the EDGE data repository to support ongoing and future modeling efforts.
A comparison of distributions between observed and imputed values confirmed consistency
across all variables with missing data.

Activity 3: Well Failure Analysis
The analysis of well failure modes was performed alongside the broader effort to optimize
geothermal well drilling using machine learning techniques. After applying and validating the ML
algorithms on the integrated drilling dataset, the project team focused on identifying failure
signatures based on both data-driven patterns and expert knowledge. Domain experts
established a comprehensive set of well failure criteria, which included mechanical issues such
as casing collapse, casing leaks, and casing connection failures; integrity problems such as loss
of formation integrity and cementing failure; operational hazards such as loss of well control; and
performance-based failures such as the inability to achieve target production or injection rates.
The presence of stimulation activities was also recorded as a potential indicator of well
performance issues.
Using these expert-defined criteria, the dataset was labeled to distinguish between successful
and unsuccessful wells. ML classification models were then trained to predict well outcomes and
identify the most relevant operational and contextual parameters associated with failures (see
NPT and cost models in Activity 2). These models facilitated automated sorting of wells into
success and failure categories and enabled the identification of dominant failure modes for each
case. Model outputs were subjected to additional evaluation by experts, who compared the
predicted failure modes with known operational challenges and lessons learned from field
experience. This process allowed not only the refinement of failure mode definitions but also the
identification of recurring patterns and root causes that could inform future mitigation strategies.
The integration of expert insight and ML-driven pattern recognition provided a systematic
approach to diagnosing well failures and offered a foundation for developing proactive risk-
reduction strategies.
In addition to the models developed in Activity 2, additional analysis and modeling activities
focused on quantifying bit degradation in real time and constructing a robust methodology to

 43

support data-driven tripping decisions. This approach aligned with the broader project goal of
optimizing rate of penetration (ROP) while reducing costly and unnecessary bit replacements.
The strategy for bit degradation analysis was built upon a two-pronged metric system that
leveraged surface-recorded drilling data: (1) a bit wear metric (BWM) and (2) a tooth wear metric
(TWM). The bit wear metric was derived from principles linking mechanical input (e.g., weight on
bit and rotary speed) to rate of penetration, essentially estimating cutter wear based on sliding
distance and changes in depth of cut. In contrast, the tooth wear metric, originally developed by
Bourgoyne and Young, used geometric and operational parameters to track the progressive
dulling of roller cone teeth over time.
To validate and apply these metrics, historical data from 41 geothermal wells (drilled within the
same granodiorite formation in the western United States) were filtered to focus on high-quality
runs using 12¼-inch roller cone insert bits. The metrics were computed using electronic drilling
recorder (EDR) data sampled every 10 seconds. To ensure reliability, noisy or inconsistent data
were smoothed using robust local regression techniques, preserving meaningful trends while
filtering out outliers and low-quality readings.
The analysis demonstrated that real-time tracking of the bit wear metric alone was insufficient due
to its partial dependence on ROP, which could also be affected by lithology, hole cleaning, or
suboptimal drilling parameters. By integrating the tooth wear metric, which independently
estimates cutter dulling, researchers could disambiguate bit-related performance loss from other
confounding factors.
The implementation of the tooth wear model to 5 wells, totaling 46 runs (excluding the training
well) shows that the acceptance rate is at 93.5% for the inner cutter prediction and 73.9% for the
outer cutter prediction. Figure 32 illustrates the end-of-run tooth wear prediction against the actual
tooth wear. The satisfactory performance of the model is partly due to the fact that the wells
chosen are within close proximity to each other, with distance between wells being in the range
of 1 to 2 miles. Additionally, it is worth re-emphasizing that the runs used in the learning and
predicted wells have been filtered to be only the runs within the granodiorite formations. Thus,
while the approach to predict the end-of-run teeth dull state is expected to be generally applicable,
new constants must be deduced and applied for different lithologies drilled.

(right). The cells highlighted in green indicate regions where the predictions are accepted while the cells in

Bit runs were then evaluated and classified into three categories: (i) premature bit pulls, where
bits were removed despite minimal wear; (ii) overdue trip outs, where bits were run too long and
exhibited severe wear; and (iii) optimal pulls. For instance, a premature trip was flagged when the
bit wear metric showed elevated values, but the tooth wear remained within acceptable thresholds
(e.g., ≤ 2/8 dull), suggesting that the trip could have been avoided with improved parameter tuning.
Conversely, overdue trips were identified by extreme rises in bit wear metric values (e.g., gains
exceeding 10,000 rev·klbf/ft within a single stand) and tooth dull values of ≥ 7/8, often
accompanied by additional damage such as cone failure or lost teeth.
To operationalize these findings, a decision-making flowchart was developed. This "bit pull
criterion" incorporated thresholds derived from the dataset and recommended real-time corrective
actions (e.g., adjusting weight on bit or checking for hole cleaning issues) before committing to a
trip.
The purpose of the bit pull criterion is to provide the drilling crew with a set of instructions that can
help them decide whether the bit run should be terminated, and what actions could be attempted
before pulling out the bit, such that premature bit pulls and overdue trip outs can be avoided. The
insights from various cases, most of which were discussed in the Results section, were used to
construct a comprehensive bit pull criterion, as shown by the flow chart in Figure 33. This criterion
takes advantage of bit wear and tooth wear metric tracking in real time, allowing drillers to first
attempt corrective actions before confidently terminating a bit run.
Several checkpoints were incorporated in the bit pull criterion to ensure that whenever drillers
observe performance degradation, it is truly caused by the bit wearing off. The first checkpoint is
to check if the bit wear behavior qualifies as an ‘extreme case’. Thresholds derived from the use
cases in the dataset are used to define such an extreme case. It is defined as a significant gain
in the bit wear metric while drilling one stand. The next checkpoint examines if an initial alert is
encountered, which is based on insights gathered from the cases with premature bit pulls. As
discussed in the previous subsection, adjusting the drilling parameters would be a prudent action
to take to ensure that the rise in bit wear metric (and concurrently the drop in ROP) is not due to
bit damage. Furthermore, the bit pull criterion checks for high bit damage potential, which
intuitively checks if the bit wear and the tooth wear metrics both indicate high potential of damage.
Lastly, a checkpoint to assess the need for hole cleaning is included as an additional step if
adjusting the drilling parameters fails to remediate the initial alert. This checkpoint is still loosely
defined due to the lack of runs with clear hole cleaning issues. Finally, an important feature of the
bit pull criteria is a check to assess whether the target depth is reachable even with the damaged
bit before deciding to trip out. The additional check is incorporated as it might be more time
consuming to trip out and replace the bit than to simply continuing drilling with the low-performing
damaged bit to reach the target depth.
It should be noted that the thresholds used to define each checkpoint in the bit pull criterion are
based on the insights studied from only a limited number of historical bit runs. However, as more
wells are drilled and more runs are analyzed, the thresholds used to define each checkpoint can
be updated accordingly. With proper integration of a data collection process and a well-
established data infrastructure, such a parameter updating process can be streamlined and
automated.

Activity 4: Enabling Further Data Analytics and Cost Reduction
Drilling cost reduction remains a critical objective in geothermal well development, and substantial
opportunities exist to lower total well delivery time and cost through targeted data analytics.
Achieving this requires not only the systematic collection of appropriate data but also the
application of rigorous analysis techniques, both in real time and during post-well evaluations.
When executed properly, data-driven strategies can narrow the gap between actual performance
and the technical limit, addressing both Invisible Lost Time (ILT) and Non-Productive Time (NPT).

 46

Key data elements necessary for cost reduction analysis are often dispersed across multiple,
heterogeneous sources. At present, there is no standardized reference for the specific types of
data that are most impactful in reducing geothermal well construction costs. As a result, efforts to
establish best practices for geothermal drilling data collection and utilization are of high strategic
importance.
As part of this task, a technical report was produced that identifies and organizes critical data
required for geothermal drilling analytics. The report draws upon methodologies and practices
proven in oil and gas (O&G) drilling operations and adapts them for the geothermal context. This
resource was intended to support future applications of machine learning and real-time analytics
by defining the minimum viable dataset necessary to reduce ILT, minimize NPT, and drive
performance toward the technical limit.

Accomplishments and Conclusions
Over the course of the project, significant progress was made in advancing geothermal well
optimization through data-driven methods. During the first year, the project team concentrated on
the foundational task of data collection, compiling records from more than 100 geothermal wells
operated by various companies across a range of geothermal fields. This effort culminated in the
creation of a curated data repository that not only stores raw and processed data, but also
includes computational code, analysis workflows, and initial predictive models. To assess the
quality and usability of the collected data, the team performed extensive exploratory data analysis
(EDA). This process focused on identifying structural and quality-related issues such as missing
values, outliers, systematic errors, and internal correlations. Based on the insights gained from
EDA and preprocessing, a subset of the dataset was selected for testing and comparing different
machine learning approaches, ultimately identifying the most promising strategies for predictive
modeling.
The second year of the project focused on building and validating predictive models capable of
forecasting well operation parameters, estimating non-productive time (NPT), and projecting
drilling costs. These models were integrated into a multi-objective optimization framework
designed to identify optimal drilling parameters that simultaneously minimize NPT and total cost.
Beyond optimization, the team also applied machine learning techniques to investigate the root
causes of well failures. This analysis enabled the development of data-informed mitigation
strategies to reduce the occurrence and severity of such failures in future geothermal drilling
campaigns. Overall, the well failure analysis in this project exemplified a systematic, data-driven
approach to bit run optimization. By combining empirical modeling, real-time monitoring, and
historical validation, the team established a foundation for predictive analytics in geothermal
drilling that can be extended to automated rig control systems. This methodology not only aids in
failure prevention but also provides actionable insights for future well planning and operational
risk reduction.

hosting was not required. Unfortunately, due to the lack of continuation funding, the system was
shut down at the end of 2023.

Path Forward
The progress achieved by the EDGE project provides a strong foundation for further development
of intelligent geothermal drilling systems, but realizing their full potential would depend on
continued investment and collaboration. If future funding were secured, the project could expand
its focus toward operational deployment of real-time optimization and control systems. The current
EDGE Dashboard functions as a proof-of-concept advisory platform offering retrospective
analytics and predictive insights. With additional support, this capability could evolve into a semi-
autonomous decision-support system capable of interfacing with high-frequency sensor streams
and providing adaptive control recommendations during active drilling. Such an extension would
require close collaboration with field operators to validate performance, ensure safety, and refine
system responsiveness under varying geological and operational conditions.
Future work might also prioritize the implementation of federated learning approaches to address
persistent challenges in data access and generalizability. The centralized data-sharing model
developed under the EDGE project proved effective within the bounds of the consortium, but
broader participation from industry partners would likely require decentralized, privacy-preserving
methods. Federated learning could enable cross-operator model training without exposing
sensitive raw data, thus expanding the training dataset while maintaining compliance with
intellectual property constraints. Demonstrating this capability in a controlled deployment could
be a key milestone in attracting additional contributors and improving model robustness.
Another area with significant potential for advancement concerns the continued standardization
and enrichment of geothermal drilling datasets. Although the EDGE data infrastructure
incorporated basic structured metadata, cost categories, and lithology descriptors, many of these
elements remain inconsistent across operators. Further funding could support the development
of shared taxonomies for lithological classification, harmonized cost coding systems, and
automated routines for quality assurance and data integration. These enhancements would
reduce the manual effort required for model development and accelerate the translation of new
data into actionable insights. In particular, the integration of higher-frequency drilling data and the
inclusion of more complete failure mode annotations would likely improve model accuracy and
enable finer-grained optimization.
To demonstrate the practical value of these technologies, targeted field deployments could be
undertaken to evaluate the effectiveness of the EDGE platform under real-world conditions. Wells
selected across diverse geological settings could serve as testbeds for model-driven decision
support, allowing comparisons between baseline operational practices and those informed by the
platform’s predictive and prescriptive capabilities. These deployments would also provide an
opportunity to assess user experience, operator trust in model recommendations, and the
system’s impact on non-productive time and cost overruns. Results from such studies could
inform broader technology adoption and guide the development of standards for digital drilling
optimization in the geothermal sector.
If supported, future efforts could also explore the application of transfer learning and domain
adaptation techniques to improve model generalization across wells with differing sensor
resolutions, lithologies, and operational protocols. Current results suggest that model

 49

performance is highly sensitive to site-specific data characteristics. By enabling the adaptation of
pre-trained models to new contexts using limited local data, these techniques would extend the
applicability of EDGE-developed tools and promote broader usage beyond the original dataset.
Collectively, these future directions would position the EDGE framework as a leading platform for
geothermal drilling optimization, capable of supporting both immediate operational decisions and
long-term learning across the industry. Achieving this vision would require sustained investment,
active industry engagement, and a coordinated effort to align technological innovation with real-
world deployment needs.

Project Team and Roles
The project was led by Oregon State University (OSU), which provided overall coordination and
oversight, with Principal Investigator Adam Schultz responsible for managing deliverables and
inter-institutional collaboration. The data management infrastructure, including the hybrid
cloud/on-premises data repository and ingestion workflows, was developed under the leadership

 50

of Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL), drawing on their expertise in scalable data
architectures and scientific data stewardship. Stanford University directed the machine learning
model development and optimization strategies, contributing advanced methodologies in both
supervised and unsupervised learning tailored to geothermal well datasets. AltaRock Energy,
Equinor, and Iceland GeoSurvey (ISOR) provided deep domain expertise in geothermal drilling
operations and failure analysis, ensuring that the machine learning approaches were grounded in
practical operational knowledge. University of Texas at Austin was added as partner during the
second year contributing to well failure analysis and modeling. The multidisciplinary composition
of the team enabled integration of geoscience, engineering, and data science to address the
complex problem of geothermal drilling optimization.
