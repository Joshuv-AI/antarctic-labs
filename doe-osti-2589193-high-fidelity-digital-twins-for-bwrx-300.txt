Public Executive Summary

Advanced nuclear reactors (AR) have focused on improving economic competitiveness by
addressing the main challenges typical of large LWR designs, such as construction cost and
schedule overruns. Much less attention has been given, however, to the operation and maintenance
(O&M) costs that are important once the reactor has been constructed and is under operation.
While U.S. nuclear operators have decades of experience operating LWRs, this body of experience
is not directly translatable to AR systems with different physics, operating temperatures,
degradation modes, fuel cycles, etc. Economic viability dictates that AR developers and
prospective owners must climb up the AR learning curves with urgency in order to attain
operational efficiency promptly, being able to address different maintenance paradigms and being
able to operate more flexibly by varying the dispatch of their electrical output, which may require
more operational actions and maintenance considerations.

This project supports the advancement and demonstration of novel predictive‐maintenance and
model‐based fault‐detection techniques through digital‐twin technology for the BWRX-300
advanced reactor. The BWRX-300, developed by GE Hitachi Nuclear Energy (GEH), is a
designed-to-cost, 300 MWe water-cooled, natural-circulation small modular reactor intended to
provide flexible energy generation that is cost-competitive with natural-gas-fired plants. The
collaborative effort leverages GEH’s operational experience and customer input to inform cost-
minimization strategies across design, operation, and maintenance; GE Research’s probabilistic
machine-learning tools and digital-twin algorithms for real-time inference; and MIT’s high-fidelity
physics models, which resolve localized thermal and mechanical states in critical reactor
components.

The project demonstrates the incorporation of high-fidelity physics models as the main engine of
physics-based inference within the context of a digital twin. The digital twins can fuse observation
data from a physical twin and provide knowledge from which superior O&M decisions may be
made, resulting in substantial cost savings and increased safety. Project accomplishments were
numerous and included (1) collecting representative O&M and design data for reactor critical
systems and establish high-fidelity data framework to address the highest-ticket O&M cost drivers
in advanced reactors; (2) developing and demonstrating a digital‐twin approach for reactor health
monitoring; and (3) engaging regulators, academia, and industry through targeted outreach to
accelerate the adoption of digital-twin technology.

Accomplishments and Objectives

This award allowed the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), in collaboration with GE
Research (GER) and GE Hitachi Nuclear Energy (GEH), to demonstrate a number of key
objectives under the ARPA-E GEMINA program. The project aimed to develop transformational
digital twin (DT) technology for advanced nuclear systems, specifically targeting the BWRX-300
small modular reactor, to substantially reduce operations and maintenance (O&M) costs through
predictive health monitoring and model-based fault detection. The focus of the project was on
three broad objectives:

 1. Collect representative O&M and design data for reactor critical systems and develop high-
 fidelity virtual models to address the highest-ticket O&M cost drivers in advanced reactors.
 2. Develop and demonstrate a high-fidelity approach to digital twin (DT) technology for
 reactor health monitoring.
 3. Engage regulators, academia, and industry through targeted outreach to accelerate the
 adoption of digital twin technology.

The high-fidelity computational model of the feedwater system employed the Structure-Based
Resolution of Turbulence (STRUCT) model, a hybrid turbulence strategy that enables targeted
resolution within selected flow regions by locally identifying topological flow structures. The
hybrid model balances accuracy and efficiency by capturing essential features relevant to fatigue
and thermal-hydraulic performance, making it ideal for high-fidelity digital twin applications. This
is possible because very high-frequency data have a negligible impact on the system’s overall
behavior. Localized, high-frequency temperature variations are effectively dampened by the
thermal inertia of the system. The model was validated against the OECD/NEA and Manchester
elbow-tee benchmarks and showed excellent agreement with experimental data. The methodology
and results have been published in NURETH-19, NURETH-20, and Numerical Heat Transfer, Part
B: Fundamentals (2023).

The high-fidelity computational model of the chimney employed the Volume of Fluid (VOF)
model, an interface capturing model that utilizes a volume fraction marker to resolve the interfaces
of the two-phase flow. This was applied for both the bubbles in the low power, bubbly flow and
for the droplets in the high power, mist flow conditions. Through extensive testing of the model
parameters, the efficiency and accuracy of the model was tested to determine the optimal spatial
and temporal discretization. This threshold was found by varying the discretization to find the
resolution of sufficient structures necessary to obtain adequate flow distribution predictions
compared to the validation data from the TOPFLOW facility. The methodology and results with
the VOF model applied to the chimney region have been published in NURETH-20. Additionally,
the results generated with the VOF model were used to help select bubble size distribution models
that were applicable to the given conditions. These were then incorporated into the Log-Normal
Method of Moments (LogMoM) which could then be used to improve the Eulerian-Eulerian
modeling capabilities for use in predicting the distribution within the chimney with lower
computational cost. These results were published in ICNMMF5 and NUTHOS-14.

Project Activities

This project focused on developing transformational digital twins for the BWRX-300 small
modular reactor to drive down O&M costs through predictive health monitoring and model-based
fault detection. The proposed digital twins fundamentally address mechanical and thermal fatigue
failure modes which drive O&M activities well beyond the selected components in BWRX-300,
and are extendable to all advanced reactors (ARs) where a flowing fluid is present. Two digital
twins were constructed and demonstrated: the feedwater DT translates detailed stress histories into
cumulative-damage models (CDMs) and leverages a Gaussian Process regression model to predict
crack initiation and growth at critical locations. The chimney DT combines fast Eulerian–Eulerian
multiphase CFD with experimental data to train surrogate models that rapidly predict moisture
carryover and steam carry-under performance. A modular probabilistic framework based on
Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) was developed to support health monitoring and maintenance
scheduling for small modular reactors. Throughout the project, the team engaged regulators and
the wider community through a dedicated U.S. NRC workshop, panels at ANS and CFD4NRS
conferences, and dissemination via technical papers. A GE Research patent was filed and recorded
on iEDISON. The demonstrated capabilities have shown applicability to all AR concepts being
developed in the United States and show promise to provide commercial to the nuclear industry.

The project was extended by approximately 6 months to encompass sufficient time to advance the
high-fidelity modelling approach of multiphase flow in high void fraction flow.
