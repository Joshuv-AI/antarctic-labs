Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, and Google Scholar for studies published up to August 2025 using the terms “transplant need”, “organ failure incidence”, “projections”, “kidney”, “liver”, “heart”, “lung”, “India”, and “low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)”. There are registry-based waitlist analyses from high-income countries. These approaches depend on comprehensive national disease registries and longitudinal health system data. The robust disease, waitlist, and solid organ transplantation (SOT) registries are largely unavailable in LMICs. In India, future transplant coverage and need across organs has not previously been quantified using population-based disease burden and demographic projections.

Added value of this study

This study provides the first population-based projections of SOT need for India across kidney, liver, heart, and lung. We combined GBD incidence estimates with evidence-based eligibility multipliers derived by international registries and Indian clinical data. We estimated organ-specific transplant need using transparent, reproducible assumptions. We modelled alternative supply scenarios (flat supply, pre-COVID-19 trend, moderate reform (8–10%/year growth), and aspirational expansion (15%/year growth). Thus we quantified the unmet need and possible coverage in future with different scenarios. We further explored the marked geographic concentration of SOT activity, highlighting inequities in access. Our analysis uses publicly available global data sources to estimate SOT in settings where comprehensive registries are lacking.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings show that even modest growth in transplant capacity may be insufficient to meet the projected population-level SOT need in India. Hence, health policies for disease prevention remain a key driver of future demand along with expanding transplant infrastructure.

Introduction

Solid organ transplantation (SOT) is the definitive treatment for end-stage organ failure, yet access to SOT remains profoundly unequal in low- and low middle-income countries (LIC/LMICs).1 India stands third in the world in terms of SOT crude numbers.2 However, this figure doesnot truly reflect the SOT requirements, as the nation encompasses nearly one-fifth of the world's population. Also, it holds a substantial share of global non-communicable disease burden in current era.3 In high/upper middle income countries (HICs/UMIC), transplant need can be routinely estimated using disease registries, waiting list data, and longitudinal cohorts. In India there is lack of population-level estimates of end-stage organ failure or transplant-eligible disease. National reporting systems document transplant activity but have no data for assessing unmet need. Hence, there are no national capacity forecasts. This evidence gap is common across LIC/LMICs and is one of the fundamental barriers to equitable expansion of SOT programs. With lack of comprehensive registries, estimation of SOT need requires alternative approaches in Indian context. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study provides standardised, internally consistent estimates of organ-specific disease incidence across countries. Although GBD does not define transplant eligibility, internationally accepted proportions of patients who meet listing criteria are well characterised in registry-based settings. Applying such eligibility assumptions to population-level disease incidence offers a pragmatic means of estimating transplant need where direct measurement is not feasible.

Herein, we combined GBD 2021 disease incidence estimates with Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME)4 population projections, Global Observatory on Donation and Transplantation (GODT),5 and National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation (NOTTO)6 data to estimate current and future transplant need for kidney, liver, heart, and lung transplantation in India. We utilised evidence-informed eligibility proportions derived from international registries and calibrated to Indian clinical practice. We compared future transplant need against projected supply under alternative transplant programs growth scenarios. We further examine subnational variation to characterise geographic concentration of transplant activity. To the best of our knowledge and literature search, this analysis provides the first population-level transplant coverage and future need and supply mismatch of transplantation services in India. Our approach of estimating the gap can be applied to other LIC/LMICs with similar robust national data constraints.

Methods

We conducted this population based epidemiological modelling study to estimate current and projected transplant need in India. Our approach integrated three data sources: (1) GBD Study 20217 for disease incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs); (2) Population projections for India to 2040 were obtained from the IHME,8 which provides probabilistic forecasts accounting for uncertainty in fertility, mortality, migration, and educational attainment; and (3) GODT for actual transplant supply. We investigated following SOT: kidney, liver, heart, and lung which collectively represent over 95% of global transplant activity. The study period spanned 1990 to 2021 for historical burden analysis, 2010 to 2021 for need-supply gap quantification, and 2022 to 2040 for forward projections. All data were accessed on 1 September 2024 and is publicly available: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17110105 our report complies with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) recommendations for reporting population-level health estimates.

We extracted age-standardised rates (ASR, per 100,000) for metrics of incidence, mortality, and DALY through 1990–2021 for the following diseases: chronic kidney disease; cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma; heart failure and ischaemic heart disease; and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) combined with interstitial lung disease (ILD) from GBD database. GBD provides estimates with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) derived from Bayesian meta-regression incorporating multiple data sources including disease registries, health surveys, and claims data. For India, GBD synthesised data from the National Family Health Survey, Sample Registration System, and state-level disease surveillance systems. We used IHME data for India's population denominators (1990–2021) and projections (2022–2040). We obtained annual transplant counts from the GODT, a World Health Organisation-endorsed collaborative registry compiling national transplant statistics worldwide. For India, GODT data originate from the NOTTO, which aggregates reports from state organ and tissue transplant organisations. We extracted kidney, liver, heart, and lung transplant counts for 2010–2021. In addition, subnational data by state and union territory were obtained from recent NOTTO's 2024 report.

Because GBD reports disease incidence but not transplant eligibility, we applied organ-specific eligibility multipliers (θ) to convert incident disease burden into estimated transplant need. These multipliers represent the proportion of incident severe disease cases that would plausibly meet internationally accepted transplant listing criteria under conditions of full access and absence of resource constraints. Transplant need was defined as the annual number of medically suitable candidates. Multipliers were derived from: 1) International transplant registry data of United States Renal Data System (USRDS),9 European Renal Association-European Dialysis and Transplant Association (ERA-EDTA),10 Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant Registry (ANZDATA),11 United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS),12 International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT),13 which report ratios of transplant-eligible to incident disease populations; 2) Published epidemiological studies documenting the proportion of incident disease cases meeting organ-specific listing criteria (example: Milan criteria14 for hepatocellular carcinoma, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III-IV for heart failure15); and (3) calibration against available Indian anchor reports, including dialysis initiation rates,16 cirrhosis admissions,17 and heart failure registries.18 Each multiplier was treated as an organ-level constant rather than age- or sex-specific, because transplant eligibility is determined primarily by clinical severity, disease progression despite optimal medical management, and absence of absolute contraindications. We did not apply age- or sex-stratified eligibility multipliers as these would imply precision beyond available evidence. For kidney transplantation (θ = 5%; range 3–8%), GBD incident CKD encompasses all disease stages. In established registries, approximately 30–50% of incident stage 5 CKD patients are medically suitable for transplantation, falling to 5–10% when the broader stage 4–5 population is considered. A conservative θ = 5% was selected, directly supported by India's observed transplant-to-dialysis ratio of 4.5–5% (approximately 200,000 annual dialysis initiations against 9000–10,000 kidney transplants nationally).19 For liver transplantation (θ = 2%; range 1–4%), GBD incident cirrhosis and HCC includes compensated and early-stage disease. International cohort data show 1–4% of incident CLD cases progressing to transplantation annually in HIC. Milan criteria select approximately 2–3% of incident HCC cases. A θ = 2% was applied to represent the subset developing refractory decompensation, acute-on-chronic liver failure, or Milan-eligible HCC. This is consistent with NOTTO data showing fewer than 5000 liver transplants annually against India's large cirrhosis burden.20 For heart transplantation (θ = 1%; range 0.3–2%), the GBD category combines heart failure and ischaemic heart disease across all severity stages. ISHLT and UNOS data indicate that fewer than 2% of patients with advanced heart failure (NYHA class III-IV, ACC/AHA stage D, INTERMACS profiles 1–3) progress to transplant listing annually even in well-resourced systems, with 0.5–1.5% meeting listing criteria after accounting for age, comorbidities, and alternative therapies. A θ = 1% was selected to capture patients with refractory symptoms, progressive haemodynamic compromise, or inotrope dependence, consistent with available Indian heart failure registry data. For lung transplantation (θ = 0.3%; range 0.1–0.8%), GBD incident COPD and ILD encompasses all disease severities. International evidence shows fewer than 1% of severe COPD and ILD cases meet lung transplant listing criteria, with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis eligibility estimated at 0.5–0.8% and COPD eligibility lower at 0.1–0.2% due to advanced age and smoking-related comorbidities. A θ = 0.3% was applied, compatible with India's national lung transplant volumes of 100–150 procedures annually against its large COPD and ILD population. The detailed rationale is provided in Supplementary content. For each organ and year, annual transplant need was calculated as: Need = (GBD incidence rate × Population) × θ.

We defined coverage as the proportion of estimated need met by actual transplant supply: Coverage (%) = (Supply/Need) × 100. For historical analysis (2010–2021), supply data from GODT were compared directly with estimated need for each year. To examine future trajectories, we modelled four supply scenarios from 2022 to 2040: (1) Flat scenario: supply maintained at 2021 absolute levels with no growth; (2) Pre-COVID trend scenario: Compound annual growth rate (CAGR) calculated from GODT absolute supply data, using organ-specific reference periods reflecting stable established program growth: kidney 6.8% (2016–2018), liver 15.2% (2013–2021), heart 5.0% (2015–2021), and lung 6.0% (2017–2021). Reference periods were selected to represent each program's mature expansion phase, excluding years with anomalous volumes due to early stage or external disruption. (3) Moderate reform scenario: 8–10% annual growth. A scenario with gradual expansion of transplant programs through policy interventions. The interventions including deceased donation programs expansion, improved organ allocation systems, and increased transplant center capacity; (4) Aspirational scenario: 15% annual growth, achieving Spain-level deceased donation rates (approximately 40 pmp) by 2040 through transformative reforms. Scenario parameters were informed by international benchmarks: Spain averaged 7.2% annual growth from 1990 to 2010, Turkey achieved 12% during rapid expansion (2002–2017), and Brazil sustained 9% growth following legislative reforms.21 We report CAGR22 of need and supply between 2021 and 2040: CAGR = (Value2040/Value2021)1/19–1. We emphasise that all four supply scenarios are counterfactual planning benchmarks rather than forecasts or predictions. They represent plausible supply trajectories under different policy investment assumptions. They inform the scale of intervention required to achieve meaningful coverage improvements and not to predict which trajectory India will follow.

We assessed uncertainty across three domains: epidemiological estimates, demographic projections, and eligibility multipliers. First, GBD 2021 reports 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) for incidence derived from Bayesian meta-regression. These UIs were extracted and propagated through need calculations by applying the same multipliers to upper and lower incidence estimates. Second, IHME reference scenario (1590.6 million by 2040) was compared against faster-growth (1573.2 million, −1.1%) and slower-growth (1643.5 million, +3.3%) variants, considering plausible fertility, mortality, and migration trajectories. Third, eligibility multipliers are reported with predefined ranges based on international evidence: kidney 3–8%, liver 1–4%, heart 0.3–2%, lung 0.1–0.8%.

Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 iterations was used to propagate GBD incidence uncertainty through need calculations, sampling incidence from log-normal distributions calibrated to reported means and 95% uncertainty intervals, with eligibility multipliers fixed at their central values. The uncertainty bands generated in Fig. 4 therefore reflect GBD epidemiological incidence uncertainty only. Demographic uncertainty, derived from discrete IHME population scenarios, contributes an additional ± 1–3% to need estimates and is reported separately. Eligibility multiplier uncertainty which is the dominant source of variance is presented separately in Table S1 and was not incorporated into the Monte Carlo bands to ensure each source of uncertainty is presented clearly and distinctly.

To assess geographic variation (state-wise) in transplant access, we obtained state-level and union territory-level transplant counts from NOTTO's 202423 report.

Statistical analysis

All analyses are descriptive and scenario-based in this study. Historical trends were visualised using line plots with 95% uncertainty bands. Future projections were presented as fan charts showing central estimates with uncertainty envelopes. Coverage was expressed as percentages and compared across organs and scenarios. Subnational data were visualised using geojson file. All data processing was conducted in Google Colab Python 3.11 (pandas 2.1, numpy 1.26). Visualisations were created using matplotlib 3.8 and seaborn 0.13. Monte Carlo simulations for uncertainty analysis were implemented using scipy.stats 1.11. Code and processed datasets for reproducibility and research are available as open source: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18092629.

No ethics approval was required as all analyses used de-identified, publicly available data.

Subnational analysis of NOTTO 2024 data revealed profound geographic disparities in transplant access (Fig. 5). Five states: Tamil Nadu, Delhi NCR, Kerala, Maharashtra, and Telangana accounted for 79.6% of all kidney transplants, 87.4% of liver transplants, 87.0% of heart transplants, and 96.1% of lung transplants, despite representing only 29.3% of India's population. When adjusted for population, Kerala achieved the highest kidney transplant rate at 31.1 pmp, followed by Delhi NCR (75.3 pmp) and Tamil Nadu (16.9 pmp). In contrast, seventeen states and union territories reported fewer than 50 kidney transplants annually despite populations exceeding 10 million, yielding rates below 3 pmp. Uttar Pradesh performed only 447 kidney transplants (1.9 pmp), Bihar performed 31 (0.2 pmp), and several north-eastern states reported zero transplants. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that absolute transplant need estimates varied substantially depending on the eligibility multiplier applied (Table S1). For kidney transplantation, applying the lower-bound multiplier (θ = 3%) yielded a 2040 need estimate of 86,233, while the upper-bound multiplier (θ = 8%) yielded 229,444, which is a 2.8-fold range. For liver transplantation, the corresponding estimates ranged from 105,324 (θ = 1%) to 421,297 (θ = 4%), a 4.2-fold range. Heart transplant need ranged from 28,713 (θ = 0.3%) to 191,422 (θ = 2%), a 7.0-fold range, and lung transplant need ranged from 4285 (θ = 0.1%) to 34,282 (θ = 0.8%), an 8.4-fold range. Despite this wide range in absolute need estimates, transplant coverage under the moderate reform scenario (8–10% annual supply growth) remained critically low across the full multiplier range. For kidney transplantation, 2040 coverage ranged from 15.3% (upper-bound need, θ = 8%) to 38.9% (lower-bound need, θ = 3%). For liver transplantation, coverage ranged from 2.1% (θ = 4%) to 8.7% (θ = 1%). For heart transplantation, coverage ranged from 0.3% (θ = 2%) to 2.1% (θ = 0.3%), and for lung transplantation from 1.5% (θ = 0.8%) to 12.3% (θ = 0.1%). Across all organs and across the full plausible range of eligibility assumptions, moderate reform supply growth was insufficient to achieve coverage exceeding 40% for kidney or 13% for any other organ by 2040 (Table S1).

In additional sensitivity analysis to validate our incidence-based kidney transplant need estimate and address the potential underestimation from excluding the prevalent backlog of eligible patients; we performed a three-approach KRT-based sensitivity analysis (Table S2). In all three approaches, the supply-need gap was consistent.

Discussion

This report provides comprehensive estimates of current and projections of SOT need and supply in India across kidney, liver, heart, and lung. We modelled four plausible pragmatic scenarios: flat supply, pre-COVID-19 trend continuation, moderate reform (8–10% annual growth), and aspirational targets (15% growth). This allowed policymakers to assess implications of different investment trajectories. In our report, the transplant need is likely to increase substantially over the coming decades. Across all supply scenarios, a large proportion of individuals who could benefit from transplantation are unlikely to receive it. This gap is already apparent in current practice24 and is projected to widen if capacity expansion does not keep pace with demographic change. A central finding of this study is the role of population growth and ageing in shaping future transplant demand. Even when organ-specific disease incidence is held constant, absolute numbers of individuals progressing to end-stage organ failure rise steadily over time. This distinguishes transplantation from many other non-communicable disease interventions, where effective primary prevention can substantially reduce future burden.25 Although improvements in chronic disease management may delay progression, demographic momentum means that demand for transplantation is likely to remain high for decades. Additionally, our supply scenarios model aggregate transplant growth without distinguishing living from deceased donor pathways, each of which carries distinct infrastructure requirements, ceiling effects, and policy regulations. Currently, approximately 80% of India's transplants derive from living donors, and the deceased donor rate is grossly low compared to HIC.26 The aspirational scenario implicitly demands a fundamental compositional shift toward deceased donation at scale, requiring transformative investment in ICU donor identification protocols, organ retrieval and transport infrastructure, transplant coordinator workforce, and equitably distributed centre capacity. Each component has distinct growth constraints and timelines that uniform percentage growth assumptions cannot fully capture.

The most realistic scenario for India is moderate reform which is benchmarked to emerging economies like Turkey,27 Latin American nations28 like Brazil and Argentina. This scenario represents achievable progress with sustained political will and infrastructure investment. However, even this scenario achieves only 32.6% kidney coverage and 6.9% liver coverage by 2040. This underscores the magnitude of system transformation required. The aspirational scenario, targeting Spain-level performance (46 pmp),29 would be able to meet acceptable coverage in two decades. However, this would require India to undergo massive transformation. India's current transplant numbers comes from living donation (80% of all SOT), and nation's deceased donor rate is 0.65 per million population in 2021.30 This is 70-fold lower than Spain (46 pmp) and 58-fold lower than the United States (38 pmp).The efforts would require boosting nationwide donation coordination networks, public awareness campaigns, hospital-based transplant coordinators, and deceased donor ICU protocols.31 All these scenario analyses provide realistic benchmarks for national planning while highlighting the extraordinary scale of required investment. In our report, even aspirational growth in transplant activity might fail to fill the gap. Transplant expansion rates 2–10 times higher than India's historical performance are needed.

The persistence of a wide gap between need and access reflects constraints that extend beyond organ availability alone. In absolute numbers, the majority of transplants are concentrated in a handful of states: Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Maharashtra, Kerala, and Delhi NCR while many states reported negligible activity.32 This uneven concentration of transplant activity in a small number of states highlights broader system-level barriers.33 These include uneven distribution of specialised centres, workforce limitations, intensive care capacity, and long-term follow-up infrastructure. Successful state-level programmes, such as Tamil Nadu's deceased donor network and Telangana's Jeevandan initiative,34 demonstrate that high performance is achievable when supported by political will, infrastructure, and public engagement. Replicating such models, while investing in underperforming regions, should be central to national policy. We emphasise that state-level transplant counts from NOTTO reflect the location of the performing centre rather than the recipient's state of residence. In India, patients routinely travel across state boundaries to access transplant services, particularly from states with limited infrastructure to states with major centres. The observed geographic concentration therefore characterises the distribution of transplant infrastructure rather than a direct measure of access for state residents, and should be interpreted accordingly.

Another notable finding of this study is that estimated liver transplant need exceeds kidney transplant need in India throughout the study period. This differs from patterns reported in HIC/UMIC, where kidney transplantation dominates waitlist registries. This finding reflects the exceptionally high burden of CLD in India. ASR-Incidence of CLD approximately four-fold higher than kidney disease rates in GBD 2021. Despite applying a more conservative eligibility multiplier to liver disease (θ = 2%) than to kidney disease (θ = 5%), the epidemiological denominator for liver disease is substantially larger. Additionally, in HIC/UMIC, well-developed CKD registries and dialysis infrastructure render kidney disease burden highly visible compared to Indian settings.

Furthermore, there are sex-specific disparities in our report. ASR incidences of organ specific diseases were consistently higher in males than females. In 2021, male-female ratio of ASR incidence across kidney (1.03), liver (1.18), heart (1.52) and lung (0.98) showed almost equal ratio. These ratios remained stable in projections through 2040 under the flat supply scenario (Table S2). However, actual transplant supply showed disproportionately lower female representation. According to NOTTO 202335 sex-disaggregated data, females accounted for only 36.8% of kidney, 30.4% of liver, 23.5% of heart, and 47.2% of lung transplant recipients. For kidney, liver, and heart transplants, this represents substantial underrepresentation of women relative to their disease burden (which would predict approximately 49%, 46%, and 40% female recipients, respectively, based on incidence ratios adjusted for baseline population sex distribution). Time-series sex-disaggregated supply data were not available from GODT. So, we cannot assess whether these disparities have widened or narrowed over time. In published studies36 from India, persistent barriers to female access have been identified including delayed referral, financial constraints, and sociocultural factors. We emphasise that, the observed underrepresentation of women among transplant recipients relative to their estimated disease burden requires cautious interpretation. Multiple factors beyond differential access may contribute, including higher competing mortality, later referral to tertiary care, and sex-specific differences in disease phenotype and progression.

Our findings should be interpreted considering a few limitations. All estimates in this report are derived from a single modelling framework of GBD 2021. We acknowledge that GBD estimates and WHO Global Health Estimates can slightly diverge for India. These discrepancies arise from differences in methodology, as GBD uses Bayesian meta-regression integrating multiple data sources, while WHO estimates rely more heavily on national vital registration systems that have documented limitations in coverage and cause-of-death attribution in India.37 Our projections assume stable age-specific disease incidence and do not model future changes in risk factor prevalence or disease trajectory. Hence, there could be an underestimation of future transplant need, particularly for liver and kidney transplantation. Metabolic syndrome affects an estimated 25–40% of urban Indians and is rising rapidly across socioeconomic strata. Diabetic nephropathy is an increasingly dominant driver of CKD progression to ESKD.38 For liver transplantation, MASH is projected to become the leading aetiology as HBV and HCV burden decline.39 Hence our current projections should be interpreted as conservative lower-bound estimates of future transplant need. If metabolic risk factor trends continue on their current trajectory, actual liver and kidney transplant need in 2040 may substantially exceed the figures reported in our report.

Furthermore, an inherent limitation is the eligibility multipliers. Observed listing rates in HIC registries are themselves constrained by donor scarcity and system capacity, not purely by medical eligibility. Our need estimates reflect medically eligible patients in settings with constrained access rather than the full pool of patients benefiting from transplantation under ideal conditions. This circularity is unavoidable in the absence of India-specific disease staging data. Additionally, we acknowledge that the heart and lung eligibility multipliers carry greater inherent uncertainty than those for kidney and liver. For heart transplantation, the θ = 1% multiplier was derived from ISHLT and UNOS registries describing listing rates among patients with advanced heart failure (NYHA class III-IV or ACC/AHA stage D) and is calibrated to severe end of the disease spectrum. While GBD estimates combines heart failure with all stage ischaemic heart disease, our multiplier already accounts for the fact that the overwhelming majority of IHD patients never reach transplant candidacy. For lung transplantation, COPD is the second most common cause of death in India by WHO estimates, and will constitutes a large share of our denominator. However, even applying an extremely conservative multiplier to this large COPD and ILD denominator, actual lung transplant supply in India is still inadequate compared to the estimated need. The wide sensitivity ranges in Table S1 (heart: 0.3–2%; lung: 0.1–0.8%) were reported to capture this uncertainty, which showed persistent supply-need gap holds robustly across the entire range. Notably, the eligibility multipliers showed a 2.8-fold–8.4-fold range in absolute need across organs compared with only ± 1–3% variation attributable to demographic projection uncertainty. It is therefore important to interpret our absolute need figures with appropriate caution. However, our report shows persistent supply-need gap across the full uncertainty range. Future analyses incorporating India-specific disease staging data and transplant registry development would substantially reduce this uncertainty.

In brief, this report is relevant for India and other LIC/LMICs undergoing similar epidemiological and demographic transitions. The results highlight a widening gap between supply and demand of organs. There is a clear and urgent need to intensify transplant services, with important implications for health-system planning and equity.

In conclusion, in India, demographic ageing, epidemiological transition, and improved survival from chronic disease will substantially expand the population living with end-stage organ failure. However, the proportion of patients with access to SOT will not align with growing need. Our projections indicate that demand for SOT will increase substantially over the coming decades. Even with moderate reforms to increase current capacity, the gap exists. Continued transplant capacity expansion driven largely by private, urban-centred services is unlikely to close this gap and may deepen existing inequities. To lessen this gap, the health policy should be focussed on both disease prevention strategies and transplant and organ donation programme expansion with equitable access.
