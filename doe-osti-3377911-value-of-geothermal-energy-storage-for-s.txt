Executive Summary
This report presents the results of a study examining the value potential for geothermal energy
storage (GES), a long-duration energy storage resource that stores thermal and/or geomechanical
energy in the subsurface.1 GES could benefit the overall U.S. power system by temporally
shifting electricity generation (supply-side) or meeting building heating and cooling load
(demand-side). This report analyzes supply-side and demand-side opportunities independently
because of differences in applications and models. Currently there is significant uncertainty
about the development costs for GES, with only a limited number of demonstration plants for
electric energy storage and building heating and cooling storage developments.

In this report, we estimate the value of supply-side and demand-side GES to the bulk power
system in the contiguous United States. Because of the significant uncertainty about GES
development costs, this analysis does not consider GES deployment costs but instead focuses on
the value of GES to the U.S. electricity system. The estimated values of GES provide reference
points for economically competitive commercial cost targets. Supply-side GES is modeled as
part of an enhanced geothermal system (EGS) generation plant in NLR’s Regional Energy
Deployment System (ReEDS™) capacity expansion model (Avraam et al. 2025). In contrast to
conventional geothermal plants, which generate constant power, EGS plants have unique
features that may allow for in-reservoir energy storage for flexible generation. Demand-side GES
for heating and cooling, including seasonal hot and cold storage and short-duration heat pump
storage, is incorporated into a price-taker model using Cambium electricity marginal cost
projections.2 To establish an upper bound for the value of GES, analysis focused on favorable
scenarios for storage with high generation from zero marginal cost energy resources. High
penetrations of variable renewable energy generation can increase hourly electricity price
variability, which increases the value of temporal energy arbitrage for storage technologies like
GES.

Demand-Side Key Takeaways
On the demand side using reservoir thermal energy storage (RTES) as the form of GES, we
found that both seasonal cold storage and hot storage for direct heating and cooling of office
buildings result in grid cost savings in most buildings (Figure ES-2). Buildings in colder climates
see greater benefits from both hot and cold storage because of greater use of free cooling in the
winter to charge the cold storage and greater need for winter heating from the hot storage. Short-
duration heat pump storage showed more modest reductions of up to 21% in electricity costs,
with the highest savings in locations with high cooling loads and high solar generation. The grid
cost savings in analysis differed from the total energy savings as GES with grid-responsive
operations shifted heating and cooling end-use electricity consumption to lower cost periods.4

1 Introduction
Energy storage is a grid resource that provides reliability and grid-balancing services. A key
characteristic of a storage technology is its discharge duration, which is the number of hours that
it can discharge energy at its rated capacity. As more variable generation technologies, such as
wind turbines and solar photovoltaics (PV), connect to the grid, storage technologies with longer
discharge durations can balance weekly and seasonal variations in electricity generation and
demand. Although lithium-ion battery storage systems are suitable for addressing diurnal needs
(i.e., intraday energy shifts), costs limit the financial viability of longer discharge durations
(Witter et al. 2025). Pumped hydropower plants can achieve longer discharge durations, but their
deployment is limited by site availability, with technical potential in the contiguous United States
concentrated in the West (Rosenlieb et al. 2022).

One promising form of long-duration energy storage is geothermal energy storage (GES), which
stores thermal and/or geomechanical energy in the Earth’s subsurface. GES could be a cost-
effective option for both long-duration (10–100 hours) or seasonal (≥100 hours) storage for
supply-side electric generation and demand-side thermal applications (Ricks et al. 2022; Witter
et al. 2025).

GES for demand-side applications has a proven history in district heating and cooling systems in
Europe in the form of aquifer thermal energy storage (ATES) (Fleuchaus et al. 2018). In the
United States, the strategic use of GES for heating and cooling could reduce electric grid costs,
both by reducing the overall energy used to meet heating and cooling loads and by shifting the
time of electricity usage to reduce the impact on the grid. The stored thermal energy can be used
for direct heating, direct cooling, water-source heat pump efficiency improvements, or, at higher
temperatures, electricity production.

In contrast, supply-side applications of GES are in earlier stages of commercialization. The Puna
Geothermal Venture facility in Hawai‘i has been operating flexibly to provide ancillary services
to the grid since 2012, but this plant adjusts its generation level by bypassing its turbines to dump
excess heat directly into condensers (Nordquist et al. 2013). Therefore, this plant cannot store
energy to perform energy arbitrage or to provide firm capacity to the power system. However,
more recent demonstration projects have focused on developing GES technologies. Fervo
Energy, a geothermal developer focused on EGS technology, received U.S. Department of
Energy funding in 2021 to demonstrate its proposed FervoFlex geothermal hybrid storage plant,
which would store hot, pressurized fluids to enable flexible generation (ARPA-E 2021). In 2024,
Sage Geosystems, a geothermal developer focused on pressure geothermal for power generation
and energy storage, signed an agreement with Meta to build a 150-MW geopressured geothermal
system that would pump fluid into the subsurface to store energy as heat and pressure for flexible
generation (Meta 2024).

1.1 Study Objective and Scope
In this report, we estimated the potential value of GES to the electric power system in the
contiguous United States. To estimate the maximum value and given the lack of GES capital cost
information, we assumed the GES systems had no additional costs. For demand-side
technologies we focused on potential grid cost savings, and for supply-side technologies we
focused on the additional revenue that can be achieved with GES with different characteristics

 1

(Witter et al. 2025). The additional revenue and savings bound additional development and
operating costs that could be justified for commercial operations of these systems.

We performed independent analyses of GES benefits between supply-side technologies, such as
EGS plants with in-reservoir energy storage (IRES) that enables flexible dispatch, and demand-
side technologies, such as reservoir thermal energy storage (RTES) for heating and cooling.5 On
the supply side, we examined the power system benefits, or grid value, of hybrid geothermal
plants with long-duration GES relative to an inflexible enhanced geothermal systems (EGS)
generation plant using the Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS) capacity expansion
model. We focus on GES paired with EGS, rather than conventional geothermal, because EGS
plants typically have low-permeability formations surrounding their engineered reservoirs that
allow for low leakage from IRES. For demand-side GES, we examined the potential for RTES
for heating and cooling to reduce demand and grid costs using both long- and short-term storage
solutions using a price-taker approach.

We also examined how the potential value of demand-side GES to the U.S. power system varies
with storage characteristics and locations. This analysis can be used to inform the design and
deployment of GES systems.

This analysis endeavored to establish reasonable upper bounds for the value of GES to the
electricity system. For this reason, we focused on future electricity systems scenarios that are
favorable for storage technologies with high shares of variable renewable energy, which can
increase the value of temporal energy arbitrage by increasing hourly electricity price variability.

Although the scope of this study was limited to a few specific technology designs, they were
modeled in a general fashion that enables insights to be applied to other GES technologies. The
supply-side analysis focused on IRES applications, but the results are transferable to other
designs with adjustments for operating characteristics. For the demand-side analysis, we focused
primarily on RTES and tank storage, but the results can be more broadly applied to borehole
thermal energy storage (BTES).

1.2 Proposed Geothermal Energy Storage Designs
GES offers key features that address challenges faced by other storage technologies. GES can be
highly modular and suitable for providing both grid-connected storage as well as addressing end-
use heating, cooling, and process heat demand. When scaled, GES benefits from the square-cube
law, with larger developments reducing thermal self-discharge losses. The subsurface can be
used for thermal and geomechanical storage in multiple ways, including boreholes, reservoirs, or
aquifers, and the thermal energy stored can then provide direct heating or cooling and improve
heat pump efficiency.

1.2.1 Supply-Side Underground Thermal Energy Storage

1.2.1.1 In-Reservoir Energy Storage (IRES)
EGS plants have unique features that allow for IRES. In contrast to conventional geothermal
plants, which generate constant power, EGS with IRES allows for flexible geothermal
generation. Geomechanical energy is stored by pressurizing the reservoir with accumulated hot
geothermal fluid via either increased injection pumping power into the reservoir or throttled
output flow from the wells. A flow diagram for potential IRES operation is shown in Figure 1.
EGS is typically developed in low permeability formations, which can prevent the pressurized
fluid from leaking out of the engineered geothermal reservoir, as demonstrated at the Fenton Hill
U.S. Department of Energy EGS test site (Ricks et al. 2022). The stored energy can then be
discharged later in the form of hot working fluid used to generate additional electricity (Ricks et
al. 2022). The proposed FervoFlex geothermal hybrid storage plant is an example of this design
in early commercialization (ARPA-E 2021). This technology is still in development and requires
commercial-scale demonstration to confirm its viability, including further investigation of the
ability to store energy without further propagating cracks in the formation.

1.2.1.2 Other Storage and Flexibility Mechanisms
Storing electricity in the subsurface is possible using other technologies beyond IRES, but these
technologies would not be able to directly utilize the characteristics of the geothermal field for
storage. Carnot batteries are electric power storage devices that store energy in the form of heat
and have a broader range of proposed designs grouped into three categories: pumped thermal
energy storage, liquid air energy storage (phase change storage), and Lamm-Honigmann storage
(thermochemical energy storage) (Liang et al. 2022). Of these electric power serving designs,
only demonstration plants for pumped thermal energy storage and liquid air energy storage have
been built (McTigue 2019).

 3

On-site storage for electricity generated by geothermal has also been proposed using on-site
battery storage or geomechanical storage in the case of designs developed by Sage Geosystems.
These energy storage designs could be explored in future work.

Finally, some geothermal plants can operate flexibly to provide ancillary services and curtail
their own generation. Puna’s flexible operation was designed to go down to 58% of full load via
turbine bypass, with fixed ramp rates of 2 MW/min to provide spinning reserves (Nordquist et al.
2013). The Geysers plant in California had two demonstration units that were about to ramp
down to zero net power production, with near-constant brine flow rates (Dobson et al. 2020).
Although these features do not allow for storage, they do allow for flexible dispatch of the
geothermal resource.

1.2.2 Demand-Side Geothermal Energy Storage
Researchers have investigated multiple types of thermal energy storage for demand-side heating
and cooling applications with discharge duration timescales ranging from hours to seasons.
Thermal sources include thermal load shifting (inherent in a fifth-generation district heating and
cooling [5G] system but not a grid-responsive option) and electrically powered end uses, such as
heat pumps that could operate with grid-responsive storage. Storing direct-use heat sources
where production is poorly aligned with demand, such as solar thermal or waste heat (not
investigated herein) is another identified application for thermal energy storage. Thermal energy
storage options for heating and cooling systems were detailed in (Sadeghi et al. 2024), including
water tank, pit, borehole, and aquifer thermal energy storage. Note that water tank storage may
or may not be located underground.

1.2.2.1 Reservoir Thermal Energy Storage (RTES) and Aquifer Thermal Energy
 Storage (ATES)
Both RTES and ATES involve storing thermal energy in the subsurface in natural formations
using water or brine as the working fluid. Unlike EGS with IRES discussed earlier, RTES and
ATES require high permeability of the subsurface. These two storage types are typically
differentiated based on depth and water potability, with ATES using shallower reservoirs
containing potable water and RTES accessing deeper reservoirs of non-potable water. This type
of storage has nearly unlimited capacity and thus tends to be used for seasonal applications.
Although research is ongoing for RTES and ATES as high-temperature storage options for
electricity generation, in this section, we focus on RTES and ATES for hot and cold storage for
space conditioning, thus operating at more moderate temperatures below 100°C. More than
2,800 ATES installations exist worldwide, with the majority being low-temperature (<25°C)
storage in shallow reservoirs for heating and cooling applications (Fleuchaus et al. 2018).

Pepin at al. (2021) published example analyses of RTES for cooling, and Zhang et al. (2024) and
Oh et al. (2025) documented an RTES data center cooling project that leveraged dry or free
cooling in the winter to charge the seasonal cold storage. Stober et al. (2023) and Burns et al.
(2020) investigated seasonal heat storage. Heat can come from multiple sources, including solar
thermal water heating in the summer, as investigated by Burns et al. (2020) or from air-source
heat pumps (ASHPs). When ASHPs are used, heat is pulled from the ambient air and
concentrated into a hot water stream that can then be stored. Cold storage may see higher thermal
recovery factors (higher efficiencies) than hot storage because cold storage may be closer to the
original ground temperature (which will vary based on location and depth). For seasonal storage,
after years of operation to reach steady-state results, cold storage can have over 90% thermal
recovery efficiency (Pepin et al. 2021), whereas hot storage reports around 60%–80% thermal
recovery efficiency (Stober et al. 2023; Burns et al. 2020). This difference may be due to starting
reservoir temperature or charging/discharging process. However, simulations of RTES for power
generation have predicted much higher efficiencies with sufficient pre-charging (Sharan et al.
2021). High-efficiency heat recovery relies on low groundwater flow and adequate pre-charging,
among other factors, in the targeted reservoir. For a conservative approach, this study will use
the lower thermal recovery efficiencies reported in studies focused on heating and cooling rather
than power generation.
RTES is a technology with seasonal storage capabilities with a large capacity, with a median
capacity of 1,454 megajoules per square meter (MJ/m2) from an analysis by Pepin et al. (2021),
but it requires a suitable location for development. Developable sites are limited by the

 6

combination of appropriate subsurface characteristics and whether a resource can be developed
at an economically competitive cost. In contrast to the sites suitable for hydrothermal power
production, where naturally occurring permeability and heat in the subsurface is required,
reservoirs for RTES are more plentiful, with the main requirement of sufficient permeability in
the subsurface (a layer of high permeability bracketed by layers of low permeability). As an
example, a permeability criteria of greater than 100 millidarcy was used in Akindipe et al.
(2024), and a range of 250–500 millidarcy was used in Holstenkamp et al. (2017). Identifying
feasible reservoirs requires additional evaluation, and sites for RTES possess additional geologic
and access constraints required for their development compared to sites for shallow storage, such
as BTES.

1.2.2.2 Pit Thermal Energy Storage
Pit thermal energy storage can provide storage for durations ranging from long (hours) to
seasonal (months) with a more finite cap than RTES. In pit storage systems, heat is stored in
water or other working fluids in a large pit in the ground, typically with an insulated cover.

1.2.2.3 Water Tanks
Water tanks provide relatively short-term energy storage compared to the other options
discussed, with a discharge duration of only a few hours. A well-known example of district heat
installation is Drake Landing in Canada, which uses short-term energy storage in water tanks
paired with BTES for long-duration storage (Sibbitt et al. 2012). Although a water tank’s
capacity is quite limited, its cost is low compared to other options. Water tanks may be located
above or below ground level.

2 Methodology
In this section, we outline the methods used in this study, beginning with our modeling
approaches and the specific supply- and demand-side GES technologies analyzed.

Although multiple potential storage technologies were reviewed in the introduction, only a
subset of the GES technologies are used in the analysis. A high-level overview of the
methodology we used for the supply- and demand-side analyses is outlined in Figure 3, with the
specific models and technologies considered for each analysis listed.

2.1 Supply-Side Methodology
2.1.1 Capacity Expansion Modeling
Because new supply-side GES technologies are expected to change the design and operation of
future power systems, we expanded the ReEDS capacity expansion model (Avraam et al. 2025)
to include a representation of a geothermal power plant with GES. While the range of GES
parameters used in this was selected based on expected characteristics of IRES, this storage
representation is general enough to represent a range of GES technologies in the future. ReEDS
optimizes the investment and operation of the bulk power system in the continental United
States, projecting generation, storage, and transmission from present to 2050. ReEDS can
therefore provide insight into how novel technologies, such as GES, could impact the cost and
operation of future electricity systems.
Pumped storage hydropower, lithium-ion batteries, and hydrogen storage technologies are all
included in ReEDS, as well as solar PV hybrids with batteries. The representation of storage in
ReEDS is limited by the time-series aggregation methods used to achieve a computationally
tractable model size for a continental-scale power system. This study used 33 representative days
selected using hierarchical agglomerative clustering with Ward linkage of daily solar and land-
based wind potential as well as demand. These clusters were mapped to actual representative
days, and these representative days were weighted by the actual days they represented to
estimate power system operations costs, as illustrated in Figure 4.c of Brown et al. (2025). In
addition to these representative days, 9 extreme days capturing the peak demand and minimum

 8

land-based wind and solar generation potential days in each of the 3 interconnections in the
contiguous United States were modeled. We modeled long-duration energy storage using
interday linkages between these 42 days to capture seasonal patterns in the state of charge (Chen
et al. 2025). This study also used aggregation of hourly time series, such as variable renewable
energy (VRE) potential and demand, to 4-hour time steps. For short-duration storage
technologies, 4-hour time steps may underrepresent their value and therefore result in reduced
capacity investment. For this analysis, a representative hybrid EGS power plant with GES was
developed in ReEDS (discussed in detail in Section 2.1.2). ReEDS captures two ways for new
storage technologies to add value to future electricity systems: energy arbitrage and resource
adequacy services.
Storage technologies can perform energy arbitrage by storing energy when electricity prices are
low and discharging energy when electricity prices are high. For instance, a storage technology
could charge at midday—when excess low-cost solar energy generation would otherwise be
curtailed—and discharge in the evening, when electricity demand and prices increase. By
shifting electricity generation from high- to low-cost times, storage technologies can decrease
electricity costs and thus add value to the electricity system.
Storage technologies can also add value to the electricity system by contributing to resource
adequacy. To ensure that the electric system can supply enough power during unexpected
outages, ReEDS enforces a seasonal firm capacity constraint. This constraint requires sufficient
firm generation, storage, and transmission capacity to meet peak demand plus a planning reserve
margin in each of the 11 transmission regions in the contiguous United States. The firm capacity
that each technology can provide during peak and net peak hours is the nameplate capacity of the
technology multiplied by its capacity credit. Note that technologies do not have to constantly
generate at their firm capacity level, but this firm capacity represents the expected generation
from a technology during peak and net peak load hours. Storage capacity credits for different
storage discharge durations are calculated based on their ability to meet peak and net peak
demand. Storage technologies then receive capacity credits based on their storage discharge
duration. For example, if the storage discharge duration required to receive full capacity credit is
10 hours, a storage technology with a 5-hour duration would receive 50% capacity credit.
Capacity credits cannot exceed 100%, so storage with a duration above 10 hours would have the
same firm capacity contribution as storage with a 10-hour duration.
Hybrid generation and storage plants receive a combined capacity credit for the generation and
storage portions of their capacity. Hybrid storage allows the plant to provide higher firm capacity
than generation alone can provide, but a hybrid plant’s ability to provide this firm capacity
depends on its storage discharge duration. Consequently, the storage portion of the hybrid plant
capacity credit is calculated based on storage discharge duration analogously to standalone
storage systems. For instance, if a 10-hour storage discharge duration is required to receive full
capacity credit, a hybrid storage technology with 20% of the plant’s generation capacity would
increase the firm capacity by 10% for 5 hours of storage discharge duration and 20% for 10
hours of storage discharge duration.

Geothermal power plants with GES were modeled in ReEDS analogously to other hybrid storage
technologies, such as PV with batteries. For geothermal hybrid generation and storage plants,

 • Storage discharge duration: the number of hours that the geothermal hybrid plant could

 10

 • Grid charging efficiency: the share of energy that is delivered to storage during grid-
 based charging, which is applied in addition to round-trip efficiency losses. This
 characteristic is only applicable when grid charging of GES is enabled.

In this analysis, we have explored a range of these parameters based on literature estimates for
hybrid EGS plants with IRES. This GES technology stores energy as pressure by injecting more
geothermal fluid into the subsurface than is allowed to flow out to generate power. Charging
IRES requires decreasing EGS output fluid flow to build up pressure in the reservoir, therefore
decreasing the typically constant EGS generation. Grid charging IRES requires using surface
pumps to add extra fluid to the reservoir to increase pressure. Simultaneous grid charging and
plant charging are allowed within the storage power capacity limits. Note that while literature
has explored the use of IRES up to 1,000 hours (Ricks et al. 2022), it is more likely that another
GES technology such as RTES would be better suited to that discharge duration. We examined
the grid cost savings from GES changes based on four design parameters: (1) length of storage
discharge duration, (2) storage round-trip efficiency, (3) storage capacity ratio, and (4) grid
charging efficiency. These sensitivity analyses address the following research questions about:
 • Storage duration sensitivity: How does varying storage discharge duration from 5 to
 1,000 hours impact hybrid plant value?
 • Round-trip efficiency sensitivity: How does varying storage round-trip efficiency from
 60% to 90% impact hybrid plant value?
 • Storage capacity ratio sensitivity: How does varying the storage capacity ratio between
 10% and 20% impact hybrid plant value?
 • Grid charging sensitivity: How does varying grid charging efficiency from 50% to 100%
 impact hybrid plant value?

Although storage discharge duration varies across all sensitivity analyses, we used baseline
values of 80% round-trip efficiency, 20% storage capacity ratio, and no grid charging unless
otherwise stated by the sensitivity.

2.1.3 GES Valuation Metric
The traditional method for evaluating the economic potential of a technology in a capacity
expansion model like ReEDS models the additions and system effects of the technology using
scenarios to represent cost and performance characteristics of the examined technology. GES
designs are in early stages of development with a range of proposed operating parameters and
significantly greater unknowns in terms of technology cost outlooks. These limits create a
significant barrier to a conventional capacity expansion modeling analysis process.

For prospective technologies to be economical investments, their development and operating
costs must balance against the expected revenue for services provided over an economic lifetime.
An EGS plant with GES is expected to include additional components compared to an EGS plant
without storage and is thus anticipated to have a cost premium. GES could provide value by
enabling energy arbitrage and increasing the firm capacity of the plant, thus allowing a more
expensive plant to remain economically competitive.

 11

Because of the uncertainty around GES costs, we instead quantified the added value of GES to
the electricity system, defined as the additional revenue for an EGS plant with GES compared to
one without, leaving the costs the same whether the EGS plant has GES or not. This can be done
by only considering a consensus range of technical performance of the new technology (GES)
and not its cost. We first consider a baseline EGS system without GES, from which we can
calculate the reduction in capital cost necessary for this system to be deployed on the grid. We
then compare this to an EGS system with GES added at no cost. The GES increases the
capabilities of the EGS system and decreases the cost reduction necessary for competitive
deployment. The difference in these cost reductions is the added economic value that GES
provides. This approach allowed us to establish an economically viable upper bound for supply-
side GES costs and helped us understand how different GES characteristics, such as discharge
duration and round-trip efficiency, impacted its value to the electricity system.

To measure the added value of GES, we prescribed a small fraction of EGS plant capacity. In
baseline scenarios these EGS plants did not include GES, and in other scenarios these plants
included GES with a range of characteristics. To avoid investing in deep EGS generation or
competing geothermal technologies prior to the deep EGS prescription, we used conservative
geothermal cost projections from the Annual Technology Baseline (NREL, 2023). These
scenarios were constructed so that the generation and capacity across scenarios were identical,
with a shift in operations small enough that it did not significantly alter the equilibrium of the
model. This allows us to attribute any changes in added value for GES hybrids to their
characteristics rather than broader shifts in operations of available resources.

In our analysis of supply-side GES, we compared the reduced cost when prescribing deep EGS

difference in reduced cost the added value of GES. This added value represents the additional
revenues from grid services, such as energy reserves and renewable portfolio standard

justifiable cost premiums for a geothermal hybrid system relative to a conventional design. We
quantified this added value by prescribing 1 kW of EGS in 2050 for a widely available EGS
resource class with and without GES. The investments and operations across all cases were
functionally identical.

2.1.4 Modeled Scenarios
To establish an upper bound for the added value of GES, we assessed GES’ added value in a
ReEDS scenario that is favorable for storage technologies. We considered a ReEDS scenario
with high electric demand growth with a 2.8% compound annual growth rate using load profiles
developed during the National Transmission Planning Study (U.S. Department of Energy 2024).
This scenario also included high VRE deployment, which can increase the value of temporal
energy arbitrage for storage technologies like GES by increasing hourly electricity price
variability. Therefore, we included a constraint that limited national electricity sector greenhouse
gas emissions to zero from 2035 onwards. We further increased the favorability of storage in this
scenario by excluding nascent technologies, such as carbon capture and sequestration, direct air
capture, and biomass with carbon capture and sequestration, as well as nuclear small modular
reactors. Because conservative geothermal cost projections from the Annual Technology
Baseline (NREL 2023) were used to quantify the reduced cost of EGS prescriptions, EGS was
only built when prescribed in 2050.

We modeled hydrogen storage to provide a high-cost alternative to GES for long-duration
storage in the power system. This included endogenous operation production, storage, and
consumption for the electric sector.

In this scenario, the capacity and generation mix transitioned to include high proportions of VRE
with significant storage additions from 2035 onward, as shown in

2.2 Demand-Side Methodology
2.2.1 Heating and Cooling Price-Taker Modeling
In contrast to supply-side GES, demand-side GES installation would likely be based on its
impact on operating costs for heating and cooling individual buildings. For this reason, our
demand-side analysis used a price-taker model to optimize dispatch, given different locations,
scenarios, and storage discharge durations, to see what net revenue is possible. Operating GES
against marginal energy prices is an effective approach for valuing the economic benefits of grid-
responsive storage operations. The timing and magnitude of these marginal electricity prices
shift in response to the interaction of investments, available resources, and demand. Cambium is
a grid planning tool that provides hourly marginal electricity costs, operations, and emissions
data for possible future U.S. power systems (Gagnon et al. 2024). Cambium datasets are
generated using select scenarios from ReEDS Standard Scenarios, combining ReEDS long-term
capacity expansion modeling results with PLEXOS production cost modeling. In contrast to
ReEDS, PLEXOS only considers operations, allowing for increased temporal resolution and
inclusion of additional operating constraints such as unit commitment (Energy Exemplar 2019).
The Cambium dataset of hourly cost and operational data for a range of electricity systems was
used to model an individual building’s net revenue (or profit) from using energy storage
technologies. Cambium’s marginal end-use costs were generated from hourly PLEXOS
electricity dispatch simulations for future electricity systems designed with ReEDS. Therefore,
these prices represented consistent assumptions with our supply-side analysis. However,
Cambium’s estimated prices were available at an hourly temporal resolution, which matches the

 14

building heating and cooling demand data we used. The change in costs between scenarios with
and without GES represents the grid cost savings from GES deployment, which could be passed
on to electricity consumers through lowered electricity rates.
We assessed the benefits to the grid of adding GES for heating and cooling storage with
simulations using hourly building loads. We show our general workflow in Figure 7 and further
describe it in the following methodology.

2.2.1.1 Hourly Building Loads
The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) simulates hourly building load profiles for
commercial and residential buildings across the United States in ComStock™ and ResStock™,
respectively, and provides the results online for research purposes (National Renewable Energy
Laboratory 2023, 2024). This analysis focused on “large” office buildings sampled from
ComStock 2023, Release 2 (CaraDonna et al. 2023).6 Large offices were chosen both to align
with previous subsurface thermal energy storage analysis (Pepin et al. 2021) and because they
provide sufficiently large loads to warrant using GES systems. Other building types and districts
could be studied in future work.

For each location, all the large office buildings available in ComStock were sampled for the

We regressed the hourly building energy loads (electricity and natural gas) that were used to
meet heating and cooling loads from actual meteorological year (AMY) 2018 to AMY 2012; this
is necessary because current ComStock releases do not include AMY 2012 data, but the
Cambium datasets have VRE potential and load data specific to AMY 2012. We performed this
regression using a combination of random-forest regression and neural networks trained on
ComStock data and corresponding hourly meteorological files. Random-forest regression is a
powerful tool that can accurately capture the behavior of a nonlinear function. A limitation of
random-forest-based approaches is they can only represent interactions that are consistent with
the observations in their training data. This becomes an issue for days when temperatures are
colder or hotter than the original training data. To address this limitation, our method used a
neural network to represent heating and cooling demand during these periods. We validated this
method by comparing regression results for converting ResStock building load data from AMY
2018 to AMY 2012 against published ResStock building load data for AMY 2012. The R2
coefficient of determination between regressed and actual 2012 data was above 0.9 for 77% of
the 47 tested buildings in the validation process.

At the time of this analysis, ComStock did not provide ideal thermal loads as outputs from the
building models, so thermal loads were estimated based on known electrical loads. The energy
usage was converted to thermal loads based on reported average coefficients of performance
(COP) for each building’s heating or cooling system, or standard values if the building’s average
COP was not provided.7 The weather files for 2012 for each studied location were also used in
simulations requiring ambient air temperature for component performance.

2.2.2 Demand-Side Designs: Geothermal Energy Storage for Heating and
 Cooling
In this section, we discuss the representation of demand-side GES designs in the price-taker
model. Three different designs are considered: cold storage in a reservoir, hot storage in a
reservoir, and short-term storage for heat pumps. A high-level schematic of the flow of heat and
electricity is shown in Figure 8 for the three designs. The hot and cold storage designs are similar
in that they both use energy to charge a reservoir when it is efficient (based on ambient air
temperature) and then release that charged reservoir at a later time to provide heating or cooling
directly to buildings. The heat pump storage option does not attempt to provide heating or
cooling directly via a heat exchanger but instead attempts to increase the efficiency of the
geothermal heat pump (GHP) at critical times of high electricity price. The methodology for
these three designs is described in detail below.

2.2.2.1 Cold Storage Methodology
For the cold and hot storage models, we assumed that RTES stored heated or cooled fluid in the
subsurface for seasonal use. We assumed the reservoir was 500 m below the surface. A cap was
applied on storage capacity to prevent unstable system charging, though a real RTES system
would have storage capacities far in excess of capacities that are likely to be required (e.g.
systems have been modeled in excess of 4,000 hours of storage (Sharan et al. 2021)). A more
critical limit for an RTES system is the charging and discharging power. For this study, it was
limited based on the flow rate through a single well.

RTES and ATES systems can use two wells in a doublet or one mono-well to access the
reservoir (Fleuchaus et al. 2018). We assumed the RTES system would use a horizontal well
doublet, with one well for injection and one for production when charging (as has been assumed
in previous studies (Pepin et al. 2021; Burns et al. 2020)). The RTES system reversed the flow
direction in the two wells for discharging. Using a 7-inch, lined borehole for the wells and
limiting the flow rate to 10–50 kilograms per second (kg/s), we used the Geothermal Electricity
Technology Evaluation Model (GETEM) to estimate pumping power needed to production and
injection wells, considering depth, flow rates, and temperature (Prilliman 2023). We ran cases
with low, medium, and high flow rates in GETEM and used a spline interpolation function to
estimate the pumping power in each simulation for flow rates over time.

 17

For cold storage, water was cooled to 5°C, stored in the cold reservoir, extracted for direct
cooling (being sufficiently cold to handle most latent and sensible cooling loads), and then
returned to the warm reservoir at 15°C. This temperature range was analogous to the range of 4–
14°C used by (Pepin et al. 2021). We assumed the thermal recovery efficiency for the cold
storage was 90%, based on the literature review in Section 1.2.2.1.

Charging the cold storage was accomplished with either free cooling or with the original cooling
technology, depending on the ambient air temperature and the grid electricity cost. Free cooling
involves pumping water through heat exchangers exposed to ambient air and using fans to
encourage heat transfer. This is a low-energy method for providing chilled water to cool
buildings and is the preferred charging method as it requires the least electrical energy input.
When ambient air temperatures were at least 5°C below the cold reservoir charging temperature,
the model used free cooling. The electrical loads for this method were low as they only included
the surface water pumping and fan loads, which were accounted for in the model.

If the ambient temperature was low but not low enough for free cooling, the model allowed for
charging the storage by using the original cooling equipment and assuming that a higher COP at
the time of charging would be achieved than the equivalent baseline equipment COP at the time
of discharging. This is a consequence of using the cooling equipment to charge below standard
design temperatures. It was assumed that high-efficiency cooling would be used when the
ambient temperature was at least 2°C below the cold storage temperature and the COP was 5.

Charging and discharging decisions were set based on a simple rule-based control scheme using
electricity pricing thresholds. Even when ambient air temperature was appropriate and there was
space for additional storage, charging only occurred when the marginal electricity cost was
below a set limit of the 30th percentile of marginal electricity prices seen throughout one year in
that location. The choice of percentile limit was set through an iterative process. Similarly,
discharging of the cold storage only occurred when there was sufficient cooling demand and the
marginal electricity cost was above the 30th percentile of prices. The dispatch prioritized using
cold storage when possible, but if the load was too low to warrant running the RTES system or
the cooling load exceeded RTES system capacity, the original cooling source was used as a
backup. The price limits that decided when to charge and discharge the storage system varied
between locations to allow for maximum grid-cost impact.

Finally, since the simulation was only run for one year, it was important to make sure the system
started and ended with the same storage capacity so that there was no net change in stored
energy. This was solved by iterating the annual simulation until it reached a steady state, where
the final storage amount was fed back into the next iteration as the starting storage amount. This
iteration process also removed the impact of specifying the initial storage capacity.

2.2.2.2 Hot Storage Methodology
The hot RTES methodology, used for direct heating, mirrors the cold storage methodology as
previously described. The warm reservoir was charged at 55°C and the cold reservoir at 25°C.
The round-trip thermal recovery efficiency for the warm reservoir was 70%, based on the
literature review in Section 1.2.2.1.

 18

While multiple thermal sources are possible for charging the hot storage, an ASHP is used in this
analysis to provide the heat to charge the reservoir. Unlike cold storage, warm storage is not able
to take advantage of low-energy charging (like free cooling for cold storage) because outdoor air
temperatures are rarely sufficiently warm enough (above 130°F). Instead, the warm storage was
charged via an ASHP, and the ambient air temperature used to charge the warm storage was
20°C to ensure a high COP for charging, based on an ASHP COP curve from (Ballerini et al.
2022). In locations with low ambient air temperatures, a waste heat source could provide the heat
needed to charge warm storage. Our calculations accounted for the electricity to operate the
ASHP, along with the pumping energy for the RTES system (based on GETEM).

Charging and discharging decisions were again set based on a simple rule-based control scheme
using electricity pricing thresholds. The hot storage was discharged to meet heating loads when
the electricity pricing was above a set threshold. If the stored heat could not meet the heating
load, an ASHP was used with a COP based on the ambient air temperature. If the flow rate was
below the minimum threshold for the RTES system, the ASHP was used to meet all of the
heating load, and the RTES system was not used.

2.2.2.3 Heat Pump Storage Methodology
For systems that met heating and cooling loads with a water-source heat pump, the use of
thermal storage was implemented differently. Stored heated or cooled water, no longer used
directly to meet thermal loads, was instead fed into the water-source heat pump as a thermal
source or sink. We used storage to modulate the temperature of the water entering the heat pump
to try to improve the heat pump COP. Figure 9 shows an example of how the heat pump COP for
heating and cooling changes with inlet water temperature.

 Figure 9. Commercial water-source heat pump COP curve (ClimateMaster 2023)
Heat pump storage could make use of either seasonal RTES or short-duration storage, located on
the surface and thus reducing pumping energy needed to access the storage. While seasonal

 19

RTES to improve heat pump operation was investigated (and initial results will be presented in
the Results section), it was not found to reduce electric grid load. Therefore, the methodology
below instead focuses on short-term heat pump storage, as shown in Figure 8.

Short-term heat pump storage (on the order of a few hours) may use shallow pit storage or
above-ground water tanks. In this setup, the storage would be easy to access quickly and does not
create a large parasitic load (from pumping fluid to and from the subsurface). These types of
storage systems are much cheaper to install than RTES but may require a larger surface footprint.
Generally, with this system, we aimed to charge and discharge a storage reservoir on a daily
cycle. The model targeted discharging during times of peak grid prices, which tend to last around
4 hours (based on an analysis of Cambium datasets) to increase heat pump COP.

For the baseline option in this model, we assumed a GHP to meet building heating and cooling
loads with the help of GHEDesigner, a standard tool to size and simulate ground heat exchangers
for GHPs (Mitchell et al. 2023; Spitler et al. 2023). We calculated the monthly water inlet
temperature (assumed to come from a geothermal borehole field) based on monthly average inlet
temperatures for simulations run in GHEDesigner for each sample location. Based on the inlet
water temperature, we used a commercial water-source heat pump performance curve to estimate
the COP (ClimateMaster 2023). The storage system can then modify the inlet water temperatures
going to the heat pump, resulting in improved COPs for short periods of time.

We assumed that the storage tank was 500 cubic meters (m3), roughly double the size of the
short-term water storage tank at Drake Landing (Sibbitt et al. 2012) and large enough to feed the
water-source heat pump for approximately 5 hours, depending on a building’s thermal load. We
set the thermal round-trip efficiency for the storage tank at 90%, assuming a well-insulated tank
and short storage times. As a simplifying assumption, if the storage sat idle for more than 36
hours, we zeroed out the storage, rather than accounting for thermal losses over time. In future
models, we suggest using a stratified tank model and accounting for heat loss over time. We also
accounted for electricity used to pump the fluid to and from the tank in the model.

When cooling was needed, the model charged the storage with water at 5°C (for a resultant COP
of 5.7); when heating was needed, it charged the storage with water at 26°C (for a resultant COP
of 5.3). These values were chosen to provide high-efficiency operation of the GHP, without
requiring too much energy input to charge the storage. It used an ASHP to charge the energy
storage to avoid impacting the subsurface temperatures and allow for potentially higher charging
capacities without impacting the normal heating and cooling operating simultaneously to the
GHP. With the ASHP in use, the efficiency of the charging process depended on current ambient
air temperature.

For our dispatch method for short-term storage, we used a rule-based heuristic model with
limited foresight. First, each day was planned out with every hour assigned a goal to charge,
discharge, or do nothing. The hours when prices rose above the discharging limit (up to 4 hours
per day) were assigned to discharge. The time when prices fell below the charging limit (up to 5
hours per day) were assigned to charge if there were highly priced times occurring in the next 36
hours.

 20

Next, the model worked through each hour in the year in sequence, considering the space
available in the storage tanks; the current thermal demands of the building; the ambient air
temperature; and the goal of charge, discharge, or do nothing. The system charged and
discharged as applicable and recorded the electrical energy usage for each hour, along with the
COP.

The price threshold for charging and discharging the system varied by location based on the local
pricing structure, but generally, the charging point was held around $0/megawatt-hour (MWh)
and the discharging limit was held slightly higher. Thus, when the grid cost was free at $0/MWh,
the system would try to charge the storage, and when the grid cost was above $0/MWh, it would
discharge.

We then compared the baseline COP, electrical energy usage, and grid cost to the new system

2.2.4 Demand-Side Scenarios
In this section, we describe the scenarios used for demand-side analysis. We consider the value
of GES under different marginal electricity cost scenarios, described in Section 2.2.4.1, and in
different locations, described in Section 2.2.4.2.

2.2.4.1 Marginal Electricity Cost Scenarios
Because demand-side GES is already used in European district heating and cooling systems, we
looked at both a near-term scenario and a more future-focused scenario, assessing both in 2050.
We examined demand-side GES revenue under two of the future electricity system scenarios,
defined in the Cambium 2023 dataset as the “Mid-Case” scenario and the “100%
Decarbonization by 2035” scenario, which was the basis for our high-VRE scenario. Details on
these scenarios can be found in Gagnon et al. ( 2024). Hereafter, we refer to these scenarios as
“mid-case” and “high-renewables”.

For the mid-case scenario, we included central estimates for fuel prices, technology costs, and
demand growth, as well as current electricity sector policies as of September 2023 (Gagnon et al.
2024). We did not include any nascent technologies in this scenario, such as natural gas, coal,
and bioenergy with carbon capture and storage, hydrogen combustion turbines, nuclear small
modular reactors, floating offshore wind turbines, or EGS. For this scenario, by 2050, we
assumed wind and solar power contributed ~70% of the produced electricity, with natural gas,
conventional nuclear, and hydropower generating most of the remaining ~30%.

For the high-renewables scenario, we included the same central input estimates and policies as
the mid-case scenario but also included nascent technologies and a constraint that limited
national electricity sector greenhouse gas emissions to zero from 2035 onward. Wind and solar
power generated 71% of electricity in 2050 in this scenario, with conventional nuclear power,
natural gas with carbon capture and sequestration and hydropower generating most of the
remaining electricity.

3.1 Supply-Side Results
In all sensitivities, hybrid GES increased geothermal plant value. With storage, EGS plants
captured higher capacity payments and shifted generation away from periods of low energy
prices when wind and solar would otherwise be curtailed.

3.1.1 Storage Duration Sensitivity
Our results showed that storage discharge duration was the strongest driver of GES added value.
At 5 hours of storage discharge duration, geothermal hybrid GES added a regional median of
$230/kW of value to the plant (Figure 10). This storage value doubled when duration increased
to long-duration storage time ranges, with a regional median added value of $470/kW at 20 hours
of duration. This added value could make EGS plants economically viable in a greater number of
regions. With the 2050 advanced overnight capital cost projection for deep-flash EGS at
$2,583/kW (NREL 2023), hybrid GES could provide 9%–18% of the value needed to cover EGS
capital costs. Added value from longer storage discharge durations increased, with 1,000 hours
of storage discharge duration at $700/kW or 27% over EGS overnight capital costs, but the rate
of improvement declined. Using development cost shares for a binary cycle plant, where surface
costs represent a higher fraction of development cost in GETEM, surface plant costs represented
64% of the overnight capital cost. Applied to our benchmark capital cost, the 20% oversizing of
the turbine could represent an additional $330/kW, which is below the median added value for
EGS at storage discharge durations exceeding 10 hours in our analysis.

determines how much additional surface equipment, including power block and grid connection

capacity at 1 kW while altering the share of a plant’s capacity that can operate above baseload.
Because changing the storage capacity ratio changes both the GES power and energy capacity

3.2 Demand-Side Results
3.2.1 Cold Storage Results
In the results presented here, we have focused on using RTES for direct cooling to displace the
use of chillers or cooling towers for cooling of a large office building. The results can be
generalized to other buildings with large cooling loads, and other forms of thermal storage.

The well doublet is designed herein to provide on the order of 1 MWth of cooling (while
maintaining low pumping losses), which is more than most single buildings need, resulting in
small building loads being scaled up to represent multiple buildings. The limiting factor in the
RTES cooling systems investigated here is not cooling power but rather charging up sufficient
storage at times of low price and low temperature. Thus, tuning the price limit for charging such
that sufficient storage is built up at low price times is key to optimal operation of the system. If
storage is depleted before the end of the cooling season, the building will use its original cooling
system to meet the remainder of the cooling loads.

 30

The baseline for comparison is the electricity required to meet the cooling load using the baseline
system average COP, pulled from the ComStock data for each building with its given baseline
HVAC system.

3.2.1.1 Compiled Results for Selected Cities
First, we show overall results compiled for the selected cities before sharing detailed single city
results. In Figure 16 and Figure 17, we plot the range of outcomes seen by every large office
building in each city (based on ComStock data). We note the locations in these figures by state
and climate zone, corresponding to Table 1.

In some buildings, annual electricity usage increased to meet cooling loads (as compared to a
baseline case, denoted with a ratio greater than one), but in the majority of buildings in all
locations, electricity usage decreased. Generally, in colder regions (Colorado, Illinois, and
Vermont) electrical loads decreased more than in warmer regions (Oregon and Tennessee), likely
because of free cooling that could charge the GES in winter. Even in cold regions, office
buildings still tended to have significant cooling needs. For electricity ratios, the two scenarios
saw only small changes in results for each location.

3.2.2 Hot Storage Results
In this section, we detail the analysis results for hot water storage in an RTES system for direct
heating. Depending on heat pump COPs and storage efficiency, hot water storage may not result
in a reduction in energy usage. Accordingly, the effectiveness of a hot storage system depends on
charging at low price times and discharging at high price times to have a positive impact on the
electrical grid.

Although natural gas is a common heat source for buildings, the baseline system for this
comparison was an ASHP so that the grid cost of the energy used could be directly compared
with the storage system. The baseline electrical loads were calculated based on the building
thermal loads and the ambient air temperature over time, applying an ASHP COP curve (based
on Ballerini et al. (2022)). Fan and ventilation loads within the building were not considered in
either the baseline or the proposed case with storage.

For the results shown, we assumed that ambient air temperature was the heat source used by the
ASHP to charge the storage system, but waste heat streams could also provide a more consistent
heat source to charge the reservoir with a heat pump. The potential to use low-grade waste heat

 34

would allow more areas the opportunity to use hot storage, even if the local ambient air
temperature was not sufficiently warm for high-efficiency heat pump use.

3.2.3 Heat Pump Storage Results
We investigated heat pump storage for both seasonal RTES and short-duration storage. Initial
analysis for seasonal RTES for improved heat pump operation did not show benefits to the
electric grid. The resulting change in COP was relatively small, and the reduction in heat pump
electricity usage did not balance out the electrical load required to pump the fluid up from the
reservoir. Because pumping loads are highly dependent on subsurface factors such as depth,
productivity/injectivity indices, permeability, and flow rates, a different RTES system with
different parameters could have a lower parasitic load from pumping, making this type of storage
worthwhile. Alternatively, a BTES system may be able to provide an intermediate storage
amount (between RTES and short-term storage), and with either constant output temperature or
slight seasonal storage could perform better by having lower pumping loads. A seasonal RTES
system may be preferred if the heat pumps needed to generate a large change in the temperature
of the working fluid or if a reservoir was already used as the thermal source, and the only change
was to the timing or use of the injection and production wells.

We focus the remainder of the heat pump storage results on short-term storage for use with
water-source heat pump systems. The results appear distinctly different because these storage
systems did not attempt to lower energy usage overall, but rather only reduce grid cost. Although
we did not investigate installation costs, the capital cost for such a short-term storage system
would likely be much lower than for the seasonal storage systems discussed in previous sections,
and thus, smaller changes in grid cost could still be worthwhile.

 38

For the heat pump storage cases, we assumed the baseline heating and cooling system was a
GHP attached to a borehole field for ground heat exchange (without a storage system). The
storage is then used to improve the COP of the baseline GHP system for short periods of time.

solar irradiance. Given this limited analysis, we found that locations with high cooling loads
(relative to heating loads) and high generation from solar energy were more likely to reduce grid
cost with short-term storage using heat pumps.

4 Conclusions
We found significant potential value for GES as both a supply- and demand-side storage solution
in the United States. This work quantified the potential demand-side revenue and supply-side
added value of GES to the electric power system in different locations for different scenarios.
These metrics provided benchmarks for economically justifiable cost premiums that could serve
as cost targets for GES developers.

On the supply side, we quantified the added value of hybrid GES in an EGS plant in a future
power system under a favorable scenario for storage to establish the upper limits of its added
value. With 5 hours of storage discharge duration and 20% of the capacity of the EGS plant,
GES added a regional median of $230/kW of EGS capacity. These savings more than doubled
for a hybrid EGS plant with long-duration energy storage of 20 hours to $470/kW of EGS
capacity. Under advanced capital cost projections for 2050, hybrid GES could provide 9%–18%
of the value needed to cover deep EGS flash plant capital costs (without accounting for the
additional cost of GES). This added value could make a greater number of EGS plants
economically viable investments.

We also examined how storage characteristics (including discharge duration, storage capacity
ratio, round-trip efficiency, and grid charging) impacted the potential value of supply-side GES.
We found that storage discharge duration was the key determinant of hybrid GES added value.
Short- and long-duration storage allowed hybrid EGS plants to provide more resource adequacy
services. Storage capacity ratio had a secondary impact on resource adequacy provision, and
reducing the storage capacity ratio from 20% to 10% lowered the GES added value by $89/kW
of GES capacity for 5-hour duration storage. Although round-trip efficiency had a secondary
effect on added value, its impact was most pronounced in enabling more energy arbitrage
opportunities for seasonal-duration GES. Grid charging efficiency determined whether the ability
to charge GES with grid electricity increased its added value. At 50% grid charging efficiency,
the model only used EGS-based charging, but at 100% efficiency, grid charging increased the
added value of 5-hour duration GES by 19%. Thus, hybrid GES developers can prioritize long-
duration (20- to 100-hour) storage and high-efficiency grid charging over high storage capacity
ratio or round-trip efficiency to increase the added value of their plant to the electricity system.

On the demand side, we found that using RTES with seasonal cold and hot storage for direct
heating and cooling of buildings both resulted in grid cost savings in most buildings. Regions
with colder climates had greater benefits because of greater utilization of free cooling for cold
storage and a larger need for winter heating from hot storage. For example, the median grid cost
savings for an office building in Chicago was 54% for hot storage and 57% for cold storage.
Cold storage also reduced annual electricity usage, but hot storage resulted in little change in
electricity usage, with some cases increasing.

By evaluating the use of heat pumps with short-duration storage, we focused on opportunities for
charging and discharging for a few hours at a time within about a day. Electricity usage
increased in all locations while grid costs decreased. The reductions in grid cost were smaller
than those with RTES (1%–21%), but the upfront cost to implement short-duration storage is
expected to be lower than for seasonal GES. The biggest reductions in grid cost were in

 47

summertime and in locations with high cooling loads and large amounts of solar energy
generation.

In this analysis, we focused on GES value, so site-specific suitability and development costs
were not considered. For the subsurface, our analysis leveraged prior work, which indicated
locations where suitable subsurface properties would be available, for power (using EGS
resource data) and demand-side selecting cities examined in FLXenabler. Additional analysis is
necessary to determine if GES is possible in each of these locations.

Although our analysis focused on IRES and RTES, similar results could be drawn from the use
of BTES or other seasonal storage options if aligned with the highlighted operating
characteristics. This analysis provided an important first step in characterizing the economic
benefits of the technology, and future work could implement more detailed technology-specific
analysis, including additional operating characteristics, controls, and costs.
