Executive Summary
LANSCE has, over its history, developed user facility specific language to describe the beam patterns and
formats delivered to its users; in several cases, different terminology is used to describe similar phenomena.
While LANSCE nomenclature is efficient in the context of individual user station operation, the LAMP
project will adopt a generally consistent, user-facility-agnostic nomenclature based on current standard
usage in accelerator physics. This approach reduces the risk of developing duplicate, conflicting or
insufficient requirements during requirements generation and flowdown. Also, use of standard
nomenclature lessens the chance of miscommunication with key vendors and collaborators.
This Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) formalizes agreement between the LAMP Project and the
Accelerator Operations and Technology (AOT) division to adopt the standardized nomenclature to describe
beam formats and temporal structure when generating and assessing LAMP performance requirements.
This document also serves as a reference for the terminology used in formulating and formalizing
performance requirements for LAMP, and for mapping LANSCE terminology into LAMP’s descriptions.

1 Introduction
1.1 Project Background

The Los Alamos Neutron Science Center (LANSCE) is located at Technical Area 53 (TA-53) at Los
Alamos National Laboratory in Los Alamos, New Mexico. LANSCE is driven by an 800 mega electron
volt (MeV) proton accelerator that delivered first beam in 1972. The LANSCE accelerator is unique in that
it accelerates both H- (to full energy of 800 MeV) and H+ ions (up to 100 MeV currently but has accelerated
high-power H+ beam to 800 MeV in the past) and supports five separate experimental areas that operate
simultaneously with each having different timing and beam current requirements. Many of the LANSCE
accelerator front end components date back to its original commissioning in 1972, including the ion sources,
Cockcroft-Walton (CW) generators, and the Drift Tube LINAC (DTL) which accelerates the beam up to
100 MeV.

The purpose of the LANSCE Accelerator Modernization Project (LAMP) is to replace and modernize
multiple accelerator systems and components to improve beam delivery, reliability and availability. LAMP
modernization will improve system maintainability and prepare the facility for operation through 2050. The
scope of modernization currently includes the LANSCE front end (up to 100 MeV). This scope includes
modernizing the two ion sources, replacing the two CW generators with a modern Radio Frequency
Quadrupole (RFQ), and replacing the DTL which is exhibiting severe degradation and has significantly
exceeded its expected lifespan. Modernization of accelerator systems beyond the 100 MeV beam energy
are not within the currently approved scope of LAMP, however, end-to-end modeling through the entire
accelerator including the Proton Storage Ring (PSR) will need to be performed to verify the project’s
performance requirements for all five experimental areas.

LAMP will be executed as a congressional line-item project under DOE Order 413.3b [1] for the National
Nuclear Security Agency (NNSA). The design will follow the Program Requirements Document (PRD) for
LAMP [2] and other requirements that flow down from the PRD.

1.2 Nomenclature of LANSCE vs. LAMP

Over the course of its history, LANSCE has evolved terminology specific to its user facilities. While
efficient in terms of describing the beam delivery requirements to a particular user facility, the terminology
is not wholly consistent with common practice in the field of accelerator physics, and can result in multiple
terms being used to describe the same phenomena. This document provides a standardized set of
descriptions the LAMP project will use to define its beam delivery requirements, that is also more in line
with common practice.

LAMP and AOT division, responsible for operating the LANSCE accelerator complex, agree to use the
nomenclature described below when formulating and assessing LAMP’s performance requirements. This
nomenclature will also be used when interacting with external entities, e.g. collaborators at other national
laboratories, industrial vendors and partners, etc.

2 General Structure of Beam from an RF Accelerator
An RF-driven particle accelerator, such as the LANSCE drift-tube linac (DTL) and cavity-coupled linac
(CCL), accelerate particles using a sinusoidally time-varying field. Particles can only be effectively
accelerated within well-defined phases of that field.

The region in longitudinal phase space over which particles can be stably accelerated is referred to as a
“bucket.” In this context, it is often approximated by a range of phases, or window, with respect to the RF
zero-crossings. Particles within this region are said to be “in the bucket” and are stably accelerated. The
collection of particles within a single bucket is generally referred to as a microbunch, or simply, bunch.
Generally, the duration of a bunch is much shorter than an RF period.
Figure 1 illustrates these concepts.

Figure 1: illustration of bucket location on an RF waveform.

Not every bucket must have charge within it. A bucket without charge (or which is supposed to not have
charge in it) is referred to as an “empty” bucket. In many applications, only selected buckets are populated
with charge. A series of populated buckets, or a regular pattern of populated and unpopulated buckets, is
referred to as a bunch-train. Generally, most bunch trains are intended to have the same charge in every
bucket. Buckets at the leading and trailing edges of the bunch-train, however, may for various reasons have
less charge. The “flattop” of a bunch-train is the region of the bunch-train over which the charge per bucket
is uniform. Figure 2 illustrates this.

Figure 2: A bunch-train with a 10-bucket flattop, a leading edge consisting of 2 buckets, and a trailing edge consisting of 3
buckets.

3 Nomenclature and Definitions
3.1 General terms and typical nomenclature

Microbunch (preferred), μbunch, single bunch, bunch: A single bunch of charge, within a single RF
period and typically much shorter in duration than a single RF period.
Bunch-train: A series of microbunches, spaced at the RF period (or other regular interval). Duration can
be specified by either number of microbunches (e.g. 19-bunch train) or time (e.g. a 270-ns bunch-train).
The shortest possible bunch-train is a single microbunch. Historically referred to as “mini-pulses” in the
context of the PSR, and “frames” in the context of pRad.
Beam macropulse, macropulse: The longest duration over which a bunch-train can be generated. For a
pulsed machine such as LANSCE, the macropulse is typically somewhat shorter than the duration of the
RF pulse delivered to an accelerating structure.
Macropulse structure: The pattern of bunch trains (e.g. bunch-train duration, and spacing between bunch
trains) within a beam macropulse.

3.2 Charge and current

Microbunch charge and current
When describing microbunch beam parameters, charge is the preferred unit of measurement.
  • Microbunch charge: The charge contained within a single microbunch, e.g. a 140-pC WNR
 microbunch.
  • Microbunch current: two interpretations; presentations, publications, etc. should state which is
 being used.
 o Interpretation 1 (preferred): charge within a single bucket, divided by 4.96 ns. This is the
 current of a bunch-train consisting of a single bunch (see below).
 o Interpretation 2: The peak current within the microbunch’s time distribution. (This is not
 frequently used at LANSCE, and the performance of LANSCE user facilities typically do
 not depend on this parameter.)

Bunch-train charge and current
 • Charge: The total charge contained within a single bunch-train, including the leading and trailing
 edges.
 • Current: The current as determined by the charge contained within a bucket in bunch-train flattop,
 divided by the spacing between the buckets. Alternately, the charge contained within the bunch-
 train flattop, averaged over the duration of the bunch-train flattop. (Both the charge within, and
 duration of, leading and falling edges is not considered.)
 • Example 1: If a pRad “frame” bunch-train has 48 pC / bunch, then the bunch-train current is (48
          pC / bunch) ∙ (1 / 201.25 MHz) = 9.66 mA.
 • Example 2: If, in Figure 2, bunches within the flattop contain 48 pC, and the RF frequency is
 201.25 MHz, the bunch-train current is again 9.66 mA. The charge within the flattop is
 (10 ∙ 48 pC) = 480 pC. The charge in the leading edge is (⅓ + ⅔) ∙ (48 pC) = 48 pC. The charge
 in the trailing edge is (¼ + ½ + ¾) ∙ (48 pC) = 72 pC. So the bunch-train charge is
          48 pC + 480 pC + 72 pC = 600 pC.

Macropulse average current
 • The average current delivered over an entire beam macropulse
 • Example 1: Beam is delivered to the PSR as 290-ns bunch-trains (or “mini-pulses”) with 70 ns
 gaps between bunch trains, repeated throughout the macropulse. The bunch-train current is 10
 mA. Then the macropulse average current is (290 ns) (10 mA) / (290 ns + 70 ns) = 8.05 mA.
 • Example 2: The charge in a single WNR microbunch is 140 pC. WNR microbunches are
 delivered at a rate of one microbunch every 1.8 μs during a 650-μs beam macropulse. So, 360
 micropulses are delivered to WNR per macropulse. The macropulse average current is then
                  (140 pC / microbunch) (360 microbunches) / (650 μs) = 77.5 μA.

Time-average current
 • The average current delivered over many macropulses, including the time between macropulses.
 A typical averaging time would be 1 second.
 • Example 1: The macropulse average current delivered to the PSR is 8.05 mA. Beam
 macropulses are typically 650 μs in duration, and are delivered at a rate of 20 Hz. So the time-
 average current delivered to the PSR is (8.05 mA) (650 μs) (20 /sec) = 104 μA.
 • Example 2: The typical WNR microbunch charge is 140 pC. WNR bunches are delivered at a
 rate of 1 every 1.8 μs during a 650 μs macropulse, so 360 micropulses are delivered per
 macropulse. WNR receives 100 macropulses per second. So the average WNR current is
 (140 pC / microbunch) (360 microbunches / macropulse) (100 macropulses / sec) = 5 μA.

Dark current

 • Beam current where there should, nominally, be no current, for example between two bunch
 trains, or between WNR microbunches. Dark current can be the result of, for instance, mistuned
 or malfunctioning beam choppers that do not stop 100% of the beam current when operating.

Satellite bunch, Sidelobes

 • A type of dark current specific to WNR, referring to charge in buckets adjacent or near-adjacent
 to the filled bucket intended for delivery to WNR. These are typically the result of the bunch
 capture and formation process within the RFQ, but can also result from improper injection of the
 beam from the RFQ into the DTL, or into the CCL from the DTL.

3.3 Emittance and Twiss Parameters

The preferred units of transverse beam emittance for the LAMP project shall be µm (microns); generally,
normalized values are preferred. The canonical “π” factor shall not be included in front of an emittance
value.

The preferred units of longitudinal beam emittance for the LAMP project shall be ps-MeV; generally,
unnormalized values are preferred.

The assumptions surrounding an emittance value should be specifically articulated, for example,
normalized vs. unnormalized; RMS vs 90%, etc.

The preferred formatting for denoting emittance shall include a superscript indicating whether the
emittance is normalized or unnormalized, and subscript notations indicating the plane and type of
emittance calculation or measurement performed.

3.4 LANSCE-specific terminology for reference

Frame – a bunch-train delivered to pRad. Durations can vary but typically consists of ~100-ns bunch
trains.

Mini-pulse – a bunch-train delivered to the proton storage ring. A typical mini-pulse duration is 290 ns,
repeated every 360 ns throughout the duration of the beam macropulse.

Beam gate – a specific pattern of bunch trains within a beam macropulse, designed to tailor beam
delivery to a specific user facility. In general, beam gate names are not intuitively connected to the
facility, e.g. LANSCE’s “MPEG” beam gate is used to deliver beam to the WNR facility.

4 Conclusions
This document provides a reference as to the nomenclature to be used used to describe beam formats and
parameters by the LAMP project, and cross-reference to typical usage at LANSCE.
