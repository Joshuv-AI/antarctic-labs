Gut microbiota, immune cell traits, and major adverse cardiac events
Authors: Jia Liu, Junxi Liao, Chong Liu, Qiaoyu Zhou, Ting Lu, Kai Xu, Lixiong Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As one of the primary composite endpoints in cardiovascular clinical research, major adverse cardiac events (MACE) are associated with poor clinical outcomes in patients with cardiovascular diseases. Gut microbiota and immune cells are increasingly recognized to be associated with MACE. However, the precise mechanisms remain unclear. We retrospectively analyzed the global burden of ischemic heart disease (IHD) and ischemic stroke (IS), 2 major contributors to the overall burden of MACE, by extracting data from the Global Burden of Disease database, and two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) using the FinnGen R9 I9_CHD dataset as the outcome to investigate the genetically predicted associations between gut microbiota, immune cell traits, and major coronary heart disease events. In 2021, global IHD prevalence reached 254.3 million cases, a 1.4% increase from 1990, whereas IS prevalence declined by 39.6% over the same period. Results of Mendelian randomization analysis provided evidence supporting genetically predicted associations between 21 gut microbiota, 31 immune cells, and major coronary heart disease event. Sensitivity tests confirmed our findings. Additionally, mediation analysis suggested that the association between gut microbiota and major coronary heart disease event may be partly mediated through immune cell traits. Our study provides both an updated epidemiological profile of IHD/IS and genetic evidence linking gut microbiota and immune cell traits to coronary events, with immune cells potentially acting as mediators. These findings provide a foundation for future mechanistic studies and may facilitate the identification of candidate therapeutic targets, although further experimental validation is required.

1. Introduction

Major adverse cardiac events (MACE) are often defined as primary endpoints in randomized clinical trials related to cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including cardiovascular endpoints or all-cause death, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), nonfatal stroke, and revascularization.[1,2] MACE is associated with emergency surgery and advanced cardiac disease leading to high mortality and morbidity rates.[3] Approximately 25.7% of patients suffered from CVDs experience adverse events during their hospital stay.[4] However, there is no well-established target for preventing MACE. Therefore, identifying potential preventive mechanisms and therapeutic targets is crucial.

Microbial cell populations reach their highest density in the intestinal compartment, forming a complex microbial community known as the gut microbiota.[5] Disruptions in gut microbiota composition and function have been associated with a variety of diseases, and increasing evidence suggests that gut microbiota is associated with several CVDs, such as acute myocardial infarction (AMI)[6] and stroke.[7,8] However, whether the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in MACE is still unclear. Therefore, our study aimed to explore the possible association between them and to further explore specific mechanisms involved.

In healthy hosts, the gut microbiota has been implicated in the regulation of cardiac function through multiple homeostatic mechanisms, including immune system development, gut barrier protection, and modulation of blood levels of bioactive metabolites.[9] The gut microbiota also interacts extensively with the immune system from an early stage.[10] The immune response–gut microbiota interaction is implicated in various human diseases, including intestinal bowel diseases, autoimmune diseases, and cancer.[11,12] Accumulating evidence suggests that immune cells exert anti-inflammatory effects and promote tissue repair in MI, atherosclerosis, and abdominal aortic aneurysm.[13] Moreover, recent Mendelian randomization (MR) studies have investigated the relationships between gut microbiota and CVDs, including cardiomyopathies, and explored inflammatory cytokines as potential mediators.[14] However, these studies mainly focused on specific CVDs or a limited number of inflammatory cytokines. Whether immune cell traits, which represent a broader spectrum of the host immune response, mediate the associations between gut microbiota and MACE remains largely unknown.

Given the substantial global burden of MACE, understanding its epidemiological characteristics is essential for placing subsequent genetic analyses into an appropriate public health context. Because no unified global burden of disease (GBD) category or large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) directly corresponds to the broad clinical definition of MACE, different components of MACE were analyzed according to the characteristics of the available datasets. Specifically, ischemic heart disease (IHD) and ischemic stroke (IS), 2 major contributors to the burden of MACE, were included in the GBD analysis to characterize the global epidemiological burden. For the MR analyses, the outcome was defined according to the FinnGen R9 I9_CHD dataset (major coronary heart disease event), which was selected because robust GWAS summary statistics were available for this clinically relevant endpoint.

In the present study, we characterized the global burden of IHD and IS using data from the GBD database. Then, MR analyses were performed to explore the genetically predicted associations between gut microbiota, immune cells, and major coronary heart disease event. Furthermore, mediation MR analyses were conducted to evaluate whether immune cell traits may partially mediate these associations. Finally, bidirectional MR analyses were performed to examine whether genetic liability to major coronary heart disease event influences gut microbiota and immune cell traits. Together, the GBD and MR analyses provide complementary epidemiological and genetic perspectives for understanding the gut–immune–cardiovascular axis underlying clinically significant coronary events.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Global burden of IHD and IS analysis

To characterize the epidemiological burden of MACE, IHD and IS were analyzed because they represent 2 major cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases contributing substantially to the overall burden of MACE. From the GBD database 2021, we extracted data on IHD and IS, including global estimates of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). To obtain sex-specific data, we extracted data by selecting the terms of “Male,” “Female,” and “Both” for gender. Furthermore, age-standardized rates and age-specific rates were extracted for subsequent analyses. These data provide a detailed overview of the burden and change of IHD and IS from 1990 to 2021.

2.2. Exposure data

Approximately 22 million single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) related to immune cell traits were extracted from GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/; GCST0001391–GCST0002121), which included 731 immune cell phenotypes and 3757 European participants. These immunophenotypes comprised absolute cell (AC) counts (n = 118), median fluorescence intensities reflecting surface antigen levels (n = 389), morphological parameters (n = 32), and relative cell counts (n = 192).[15]

The latest summary statistics of the gut microbiota are publicly available in the GWAS Catalog (GCST90032172–GCST90032644) and were applied to 2801 microbial taxa and 7,967,866 human genetic variants from 5959 genotyped individuals, including 11 phyla, 19 classes, 24 orders, 62 families, 146 genera, and 209 species.[16]

2.3. Outcome data

The outcome GWAS summary statistics were obtained from the FinnGen R9 release (https://storage.googleapis.com/finngen-public-data-r9/summary_stats/finngen_R9_I9_CHD.gz). The outcome corresponded to the I9_CHD endpoint (major coronary heart disease event), as defined in the FinnGen study, and served as the outcome for all MR analyses. This endpoint was selected because high-quality GWAS summary statistics were available for clinically significant coronary events.

2.4. Instrumental variable selection

The study flowchart is presented in Figure 1. Immune cells and gut microbiota served as the exposures, whereas major coronary heart disease event served as the outcomes. The significance level of instrumental variables for each immune cell and gut microbiota was set to 1 × 10−5. Linkage disequilibrium between SNPs was assessed using the clumping process (R2 < 0.001 and clumping distance = 10,000 kb). Alleles were aligned to the human genome reference sequence. Ambiguous and duplicate SNPs were removed. Finally, SNPs with heterogeneity and pleiotropy were excluded after matching the outcomes (Tables S1 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content 1).

The flowchart of two-sample and two-step Mendelian randomization evaluating the effects of immune cell traits in mediating the effect of gut microbiota on major coronary heart disease event. GWAS = Genome-wide association study, MACE = major adverse cardiac events, SNPs = single nucleotide polymorphisms.

2.5. Statistical analysis

To evaluate the genetically predicted associations between 731 immune cell traits, 473 gut microbiota, and major coronary heart disease event, two-sample MR analyses were performed, including inverse variance weighting (IVW) method,[17] weighted median-based method,[18] mode-based methods method,[19] MR-Egger regression method,[20] and simple mode method.[21]

Gut microbiota and immune cell traits that showed significant genetically predicted associations with major coronary heart disease event were subsequently included in the mediation analyses. Mediation MR analyses were performed to determine whether immune cell traits may partially mediate the associations between gut microbiota and major coronary heart disease event.

Heterogeneity among instrumental variables was assessed using Cochran Q statistic, with P < .05 indicating significant heterogeneity. In addition, we used scatter plots to show that the results were not affected by outliers and funnel plots to demonstrate the robustness of the correlation and absence of heterogeneity. All analyses were performed using the R 3.5.3 software (http://www.r-project.org/).

Collectively, these findings highlight the substantial global burden of ischemic CVDs, particularly IHD and IS, which together represent major contributors to the overall burden of MACE. These epidemiological findings provide the public health context for the subsequent genetic analyses. We therefore performed MR analyses to investigate the genetically predicted associations between gut microbiota, immune cell traits, and major coronary heart disease event (FinnGen I9_CHD), aiming to explore potential immune-related pathways underlying clinically significant coronary events.

3.2. Genetically predicted associations of gut microbiota and immune cell traits with major coronary heart disease event

Two-sample MR analysis was used to investigate the genetically predicted associations of gut microbiota and immune cell traits with major coronary heart disease event. We found that 21 gut microbiotas were strongly associated with outcome, among which 14 gut microbiota showed genetically predicted protective associations, such as Actinomycetales (95% CI = 0.852–0.997, P = .042), and 7 gut microbiotas showed genetically predicted risk associations, including Desulfovibrionales (95% CI = 1.001–1.121, P = .047; Fig. 4, Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 3).

MR results of IVW method revealed that 31 immune cell traits were strongly associated with major coronary heart disease event, including 17 traits showing protective associations, such as Transitional %B cell (95% CI = 0.926–0.993, P = .019), and 14 traits showing risk associations, including IgD-CD24-%B cells (95% CI = 1.009–1.046, P = .004; Fig. 5, Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 4).

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of these findings. Specifically, the MR-Egger intercept test and the MR-PRESSO global test provided no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy. Scatter plots, funnel plots, and heterogeneity analyses are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

3.3. Reverse MR analyses of major coronary heart disease event with gut microbiota and immune cell traits

To further evaluate the possibility of reverse associations, reverse two-sample MR analyses were performed using major coronary heart disease event as the exposure. No significant genetically predicted associations were observed between major coronary heart disease event and the 21 gut microbiota (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 5).

Next, we evaluated the genetically predicted associations between major coronary heart disease event and the 31 immune cell traits identified in the forward MR analyses. We found that 2 immune cell traits showed significant associations, including naïve CD24 on IgD + CD24 + (B cell panel) and CD27 on IgD − CD38br (B cell panel). Using the IVW method, the odds ratio for naïve CD24 on IgD + CD24 + (B-cell panel) was 0.874 (95% CI = 0.793–0.964, P = .007; Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 5), while the odds ratio for CD27 on IgD − CD38br (B-cell panel) was 0.897 (95% CI = 0.807–0.997, P = .044; Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 5). Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of these findings, and the funnel plots suggested no substantial heterogeneity or directional bias.

3.4. Mediation analysis

Using two-sample MR, we evaluated the genetically predicted associations between gut microbiota and immune cell traits. We identified consistent genetically predicted associations between specific gut microbiota and immune cell traits that were also associated with major coronary heart disease event (STEP 3A in Fig. 1; Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 6), suggesting that certain immune cell traits may partially mediate the associations between gut microbial taxa and major coronary heart disease event. No evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was detected based on the MR-Egger intercept test or the MR-PRESSO global test (Table S7, Supplemental Digital Content 7).

To explore the potential mechanisms underlying these associations, mediation MR analyses were performed. Four potential mediation pathways were identified (Table S8, Supplemental Digital Content 8). Specifically, Activated Treg AC (β = −0.009) and Activated and resting Treg AC (β = −0.012) showed significant positive mediation effects in the association between UBA1375 sp002305795 abundance in stool and major coronary heart disease event. In contrast, these 2 immune cell traits showed significant negative mediation effects in the association between Prevotella massilia sp000437675 abundance in stool and major coronary heart disease event.

4. Discussion

MACE represent a composite clinical endpoint that reflects the occurrence of severe cardiovascular events and remains a major contributor to morbidity and mortality worldwide. In the present study, we characterized the global burden of IHD and IS, which together represent the major ischemic cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases contributing to the overall burden of MACE, using data from the GBD 2021 study. Globally, IHD accounted for 254.3 million prevalent cases, while IS accounted for 69.9 million. Although age-standardized mortality rates and DALY rates decreased substantially for both diseases, with reductions of 31.6 and 28.8% for IHD and 39.6 and 34.9% for IS, respectively, the burden of ischemic CVDs remains substantial, indicating persistent challenges for healthcare systems worldwide.

The GBD analysis established the epidemiological context of ischemic CVDs, whereas the subsequent MR analyses explored the genetically predicted associations between gut microbiota, immune cell traits, and major coronary heart disease event. These complementary analyses link the population-level burden of ischemic CVDs with potential biological pathways, integrating epidemiological observations with genetically informed evidence. Together, they provide a more comprehensive understanding of ischemic CVDs from both public health and biological perspectives. Emerging evidence has confirmed that the gut microbiota is closely related to the development and prognosis of diverse coronary artery disease (CAD) subtypes and has a high predictive value for CVDs.[6] A clinical study based on multitopic analyses was conducted in a Chinese population, which included 161 patients with CAD and 40 healthy patients, reported that the gut microbiota changed significantly with CAD severity.[22] In another study on AMI, Han et al[23] revealed remarkable differences in the gut microbiota among different subgroups of patients with AMI. However, although increasing evidence supports a role for the gut microbiota in CVDs, the associations between specific gut microbiota and major coronary heart disease event, particularly the potential immune-mediated pathways underlying these associations, remain incompletely understood.

In the present study, MR analyses identified genetically predicted associations between 21 gut microbiota and major coronary heart disease event, providing additional evidence that specific gut microbial taxa may be associated with the genetic susceptibility to clinically significant coronary events. Specifically, Actinomycetales, Bacteroides A plebeius, Bacteroides intestinalis A, Bacteroides sp002160055, CAG-345 sp000433315, Jiangellaceae, Prevotellamassilia sp000437675, RUG013 sp001486445, Syntrophorhabdaceae, UBA1375 sp002305795, UBA6382, UBA6398, UBA7182 sp002491115, and UBA7182 were associated with a decreased risk of major coronary heart disease event, while Desulfovibrionales, Enterobacteriaceae, Faecalibacterium sp002160895, Johnsonella ignava, Lachnospira rogosae, Ruminococcus D and Saccharofermentanaceae were associated with an increased risk. Compared with previous MR studies that mainly investigated individual CVDs or inflammatory cytokines, our study further incorporated a comprehensive panel of immune cell traits into mediation MR analyses, providing additional insight into the potential immune-related pathways linking gut microbial taxa with major coronary heart disease event. Nevertheless, these genetically informed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of direct biological mechanisms, and further experimental studies are required to validate the underlying pathways.

The immune system plays a complex role in CAD. Neutrophils, macrophages, B lymphocytes, T lymphocytes, and natural killer T cells play key roles in the immune response through proatherogenic and antiatherogenic signaling pathways.[24] Recent evidence suggests a role for neutrophils in activating reparative processes, and a high peripheral neutrophil count is directly related to the degree of atherosclerosis in coronary arteries.[25,26] In addition, antigen-presenting cells, including dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells, contribute to the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic lesions by regulating immune activation and antigen presentation.[27] Given the pivotal role of immune regulation in CAD, we further investigated the genetically predicted associations between immune cell traits and major coronary heart disease event. We identified 31 immune cell traits associated with major coronary heart disease event, including 17 traits associated with lower genetic susceptibility and 14 traits associated with higher genetic susceptibility. These findings further support the involvement of host immune responses in the pathogenesis of clinically significant coronary events.

Accumulating evidence indicates that the gut microbiota and the immune system are closely interconnected and jointly regulate cardiovascular homeostasis. Dysregulation of the gut microbiota may lead to alterations in the immune system. The interaction between the gut microbiota and the immune system plays a significant role in maintaining homeostasis, and abnormalities in this interaction can lead to particular pathogenic processes in the disease.[23] Sugawara et al reported that patients taking probiotics to improve gut microbiota deterioration had greater natural killer cell activity and increased lymphocyte counts following hepatectomy than those not taking probiotics, thus reducing infectious complications.[28] For patients with coronavirus disease 2019, probiotics can prevent the exacerbation of pneumonia by maintaining beneficial gut microbiota.[29] Impaired gut microbiota–immune interactions have also been implicated in inflammatory bowel disease,[30] Rheumatoid arthritis,[31] hypertension,[32] and atherosclerosis.[33] Collectively, these studies provide biological plausibility for a mechanistic link between gut microbiota, immune regulation, and CVD, although the precise pathways remain incompletely understood.

Building upon these previous observations, we further explored whether immune cell traits may partially mediate the genetically predicted associations between gut microbial taxa and major coronary heart disease event. Liu et al[34] showed that CAD microbiota activated intestinal and systemic Th17 responses and decreased Treg cell distribution. Therefore, we aimed to determine whether the microbiota affected major coronary heart disease event through the immune system. We observed a significant impact of immune cell traits on the gut microbiota, and mediation analysis indicated that some cells mediated the association between the gut microbiota and major coronary heart disease event. For different gut microbiota, the same immune cell traits likely played different roles in major coronary heart disease event, such as CD33dim HLA DR+, CD11b + AC, and activated Treg AC. These 2 immune cells inhibit the protective effect of Prevotella massilia sp000437675 in major coronary heart disease event while promoting the risk of Ruminococcus D. Tony et al[35] found that depletion of the gut microbiota may affect the composition of the immune system, particularly within the myeloid cell compartment, leading to impaired cardiac repair and increased mortality following MI. Microbiota-derived metabolite propionic acid increased regulatory T-cell numbers and augmented atherosclerotic lesion severity in the HFD-fed ApoE−/− mice.[36] Together, these findings provide biological support for the genetically predicted associations observed in our MR analyses. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms require further experimental validation.

By integrating conventional MR and mediation MR analyses, we further explored the potential role of immune cell traits in linking gut microbiota with major coronary heart disease event. Our findings suggest that specific gut microbiota may be associated with major coronary heart disease event through genetically predicted immune-related pathways. For example, Ruminococcus D abundance in stool was associated with an increased genetic susceptibility to major coronary heart disease event, with Activated Treg AC and Activated & CD33dim HLA DR + CD11b + AC identified as potential mediators, each accounting for approximately 4.1% of the observed association. Conversely, these immune cell traits partially attenuated the protective association between Prevotella massilia sp000437675 abundance in stool and major coronary heart disease event by approximately 12.3 and 15.4%, respectively. Although these mediation effects were modest, they suggest that immune regulation may represent one potential biological pathway linking gut microbiota with major coronary heart disease event.

In conclusion, by integrating epidemiological evidence from the GBD study with genetically informed evidence from MR analyses, our study provides complementary insights into the burden of ischemic CVDs and the genetically predicted associations among gut microbiota, immune cell traits, and major coronary heart disease event. Furthermore, mediation MR analyses suggest that specific immune cell traits may partially mediate these associations, providing additional insight into potential immune-related pathways. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the GWAS datasets were derived predominantly from individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Second, although MR reduces confounding and reverse causation, the statistical power for some analyses may have been limited because of the available genetic instruments. Third, despite the large-scale datasets included in this study, some gut microbiota and immune cell traits were unavailable in the current GWAS resources, and additional biomarkers may be identified as more comprehensive datasets become available. Finally, our findings provide genetically informed evidence supporting potential biological associations rather than direct evidence of causal mechanisms or clinical efficacy. Therefore, further experimental, longitudinal, and interventional studies are warranted to validate the underlying mechanisms and determine their clinical relevance.
