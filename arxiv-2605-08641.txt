Introduction

The study of
q
-expansions
of the form

x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{i}}{q^{i}},\quad(d_{i})\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{\infty}

where the
base

q\in(1,2]
is a given real number, goes back to the seminal works of Rényi
[
28
]
and Parry
[
27
]
.
Since then a large body of results has revealed deep connections with number theory, Diophantine approximation, symbolic dynamics, ergodic theory, and fractal geometry (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
14
,
2
,
23
,
5
,
6
,
33
]
).
It is well known that
x
admits a
q
-expansion if and only if
x
lies in the compact interval
[0,\frac{1}{q-1}]

[
28
]
, and that in case
q\in(1,2)
(in Lebesgue’s sense) almost every
x
has
2^{\aleph_{0}}
different
q
-expansions
[
30
]
.
Furthermore, for any prescribed
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{\aleph_{0},2^{\aleph_{0}}\right\}
there exist
2^{\aleph_{0}}
bases
q\in(1,2)
in which
x=1
has exactly
k
different
q
-expansions
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
The theory has subsequently been generalized to multiple-base representations
[
26
,
16
,
20
]
, leading to a much richer symbolic structure.

Hochman and Shmerkin
[
10
]
propose a pioneering fractal-geometric condition for determining whether a measure is pointwise normal in base
n
.
This condition is
C^{1}
-stable and generalizes relevant results from integer bases to the more general setting of Pisot bases
\beta>1
.
Furthermore, Huang and Wang
[
11
]
proved that for two distinct non-integer bases
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>1
, the corresponding Rényi–Parry measures coincide if and only if
\beta_{1}
is a root of
x^{2}-qx-p=0
with
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\leq q
, and moreover
\beta_{2}=\beta_{1}+1
.

Let
T_{\beta}:[0,1)\to[0,1)
be the
\beta
-transformation
T_{\beta}(x)=\{\beta x\}
.
Rényi developed a canonical-digits method: for each finite canonical word
\omega=\omega_{1}\cdots\omega_{n}
he defines the corresponding cylinder
\mathscr{E}(\omega)\subset(0,1)
and proves that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the family

\{\mathscr{E}(\omega):|\omega|=n\}
is pairwise disjoint and forms a cover of
(0,1)
.
This cylinder decomposition yields two-sided estimates for the measures of multiple preimages, providing uniform upper and lower bounds for
\mu(T_{\beta}^{-n}E)
.
Invoking a Dunford–Miller type limit theorem, Rényi deduces the existence of a
T_{\beta}
-invariant measure
\nu
which is equivalent to Lebesgue (
\nu\ll\lambda
), together with the almost everywhere convergence of Birkhoff averages:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g\!\left(T_{\beta}^{k}x\right)\longrightarrow\int g\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\int g\,h_{\beta}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda,

where
h_{\beta}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}
.
Finally, by an application of
Knopp’s theorem
, the system
(T_{\beta},\nu)
is ergodic.
Parry showed that, for each
\beta>1
, the unique
T_{\beta}
-invariant measure admits an explicit step density:

h_{\beta}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta^{-n}\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,\,T_{\beta}^{\,n}(1))}(x).

In the random setting, Dajani and de Vries
[
3
,
4
]
introduce a probabilistic switching
between the greedy and the lazy
\beta
-maps to form the skew product
K_{\beta}
.
They prove that the topological entropy satisfies

h_{\mathrm{top}}(K_{\beta})=\log\!\big(1+\lfloor\beta\rfloor\big)
and that there
is a unique measure of maximal entropy
\nu_{\beta}
.
In parallel, working on the space
BV
of functions of bounded variation, they establish Lasota–Yorke type estimates and, via Cesàro averaging, construct and uniquely identify an absolutely continuous invariant probability measure that is equivalent to the Lebesgue measure.
Equivalently, its density
f\in BV
is a fixed point of the corresponding Frobenius–Perron operator:
\mathcal{P}f=f
, and the associated measure solves the affine fixed-point equation

\mu\;=\;p\,\mu\circ T_{\beta}^{-1}\;+\;(1-p)\,\mu\circ S_{\beta}^{-1},

which is consistent with the invariance of the skew product.
These results give a systematic description of the measure-theoretic structure and the coding features of random
\beta
-expansions.

Recently, following the work
[
16
]
, the papers
[
12
]
,
[
15
]
and
[
21
]
focused on
double-base expansions
or
Q
-expansions
of the form

x=\pi_{Q}(c):=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{i}}{q_{c_{1}}\cdots q_{c_{i}}},\quad c=(c_{i})\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{\infty},

associated with a
double-base

Q=(q_{0},q_{1})\in(1,\infty)^{2}
.
We have
\pi_{Q}(c)\in I_{Q}:=[0,\frac{1}{q_{1}-1}]
for all
c\in\{0,1\}^{\infty}
.
It turned out that the numbers

\ell_{Q}:=\frac{q_{1}}{q_{0}(q_{1}-1)}-1\quad\text{and}\quad r_{Q}:=\frac{q_{0}}{q_{1}}

play an important role, and they have expansions if and only if
q_{0}+q_{1}\geq q_{0}q_{1}
.
Henceforth always assuming this condition, it was proved in
[
12
]
that all three sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:=\left\{Q\in(1,\infty)^{2}:\text{both }\ell_{Q}\text{ and }r_{Q}\text{ have unique $Q$-expansions}\right\},

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{U}}\ \text{ denotes the topological closure of }\mathcal{U},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:=\left\{Q\in(1,\infty)^{2}:\text{both }\ell_{Q}\text{ and }r_{Q}\text{ have unique doubly infinite $Q$-expansions}\right\}

have Hausdorff dimension two.
(The set
\mathcal{V}
is always closed,
\mathcal{U}\subsetneqq\overline{\mathcal{U}}\subsetneqq\mathcal{V}
, and the three sets reduce to the usual ones in the equal-base case
q_{0}=q_{1}
.)
Based on this work, the paper
[
15
]
provided a complete description of the topological relationships and properties among the following variants of
\mathcal{U}
,
\overline{\mathcal{U}}
and
\mathcal{V}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{Q}:=\left\{x\in I_{Q}:x\text{ has a unique $Q$-expansion}\right\},

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{Q}\ \text{ denotes the topological closure of }\mathcal{U}_{Q},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{Q}:=\left\{x\in I_{Q}:x\text{ has at most one doubly infinite $Q$-expansion}\right\}.

Here too,
\mathcal{V}_{Q}
is always closed, and
\mathcal{U}_{Q}\subset\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{Q}\subset\mathcal{V}_{Q}
, but the inclusions are not always strict: four different cases may occur according to the value of
Q
.
Furthermore, the points in
\mathcal{V}_{Q}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{Q}
may have 2, 3 or
\aleph_{0}
distinct expansions (in the equal base case we never have three expansions). The paper
[
21
]
gave a explicit formula for the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{U}_{Q}
and showed the continuity of this formula as a function of
Q
.

Henceforth we always assume that

(1.1)

Q=(q_{0},q_{1})\in(1,\infty)^{2}\quad\text{with}\quad q_{0}+q_{1}\geq q_{0}q_{1}.

Then every number
x\in I_{Q}
has at least one
Q
-expansion
, namely the
greedy
Q
-expansion

b(x,Q)=(b_{i})=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)
.
It is defined recursively by the greedy algorithm, choosing in each step the largest possible digit, so that it is the lexicographically largest expansion of
x
.
The greedy greedy map
G(x):I_{Q}\mapsto I_{Q}
, defined by the formula

(1.2)

G(x)=\begin{cases}G_{0}(x)=q_{0}x,&x\in I_{0}:=[0,\frac{1}{q_{1}})\\
G_{1}(x)=q_{1}x-1,&x\in I_{1}:=[\frac{1}{q_{1}},\frac{1}{q_{1}-1}]\end{cases}

generates the greedy expansion in the sense that

b_{n}=j\Longleftrightarrow G^{n-1}(x)\in I_{j},\quad j=0,1.

Similarly, each
x\in I_{Q}
has a lexicographically smallest expansion
l(x,Q)=(l_{i})=(l_{1},l_{2},\ldots)
, also called the
lazy
Q
-expansion
of
x
.
It is generated by the lazy map
L(x):I_{Q}\mapsto I_{Q}
, defined by the formula

(1.3)

L(x)=\begin{cases}L_{0}(x)=q_{0}x,&x\in J_{0}:=[0,\frac{1}{q_{0}(q_{1}-1)}]\\
L_{1}(x)=q_{1}x-1,&x\in J_{1}:=(\frac{1}{q_{0}(q_{1}-1)},\frac{1}{q_{1}-1}]\end{cases}

in the sense that

l_{n}=j\Longleftrightarrow L^{n-1}(x)\in J_{j},\quad j=0,1.

The following theorem establishes the existence of absolutely continuous invariant probability measures for
G
and

L
.
We recall that a dynamical system
(X,\Sigma,\mu,T)
is called
exact
if the set

\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{T^{-n}(A)\ :\ A\in\Sigma\right\}

is trivial, i.e., it contains only sets of measure zero and their complements.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
Q=(q_{0},q_{1})\in(1,\infty)^{2}
with
q_{0}+q_{1}\geq q_{0}q_{1}
.

(i)

There exist unique probability measures
\mu_{g,Q}
and
\mu_{l,Q}
on
I_{Q}
that are invariant under
G
and
L
, respectively.

(ii)

They are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
m
, and and their density functions
h_{g,Q}
and
h_{l,Q}
are bounded. Moreover,
h_{g,Q}
is non-increasing, while
h_{l,Q}
is non-decreasing.

(iii)

Finally, the dynamical systems
(I_{Q},\Sigma_{I_{Q}},\mu_{g,Q},G)
and
(I_{Q},\Sigma_{I_{Q}},\mu_{l,Q},L)
are exact.

We recall the classical hierarchy of statistical properties:

\text{exact}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{strong mixing}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{ergodic};

see, e.g., the discussion in
[
22
]
.
Therefore, by Theorem
1.1
, the systems

(I_{Q},\Sigma_{I_{Q}},\mu_{g,Q},G)\quad\text{and}\quad(I_{Q},\Sigma_{I_{Q}},\mu_{l,Q},L)

are strong mixing and ergodic.

Next we determine the supports of the measures
\mu_{g,Q}
and
\mu_{l,Q}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
Q=(q_{0},q_{1})\in(1,\infty)^{2}
with
q_{0}+q_{1}\geq q_{0}q_{1}
.

(i)

The invariant measure
\mu_{g,Q}
is equivalent to the Lebesgue measure
m
on the interval
[0,r_{Q})
, and vanishes outside it.

(ii)

Similarly, the invariant measure
\mu_{l,Q}
is equivalent to the Lebesgue measure
m
on the interval
\left(\ell_{Q},\frac{1}{q_{1}-1}\right]
, and vanishes outside it.

Next we give an explicit expression for the invariant density functions.
Let us introduce the jump functions

(1.4)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{g,Q}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q_{1}^{s(n)}q_{0}^{n-s(n)}}1_{[0,G^{n}(r_{Q})}(x)

and

(1.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{l,Q}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q_{0}^{n-t(n)}q_{1}^{t(n)}}1_{(L^{n}(\ell_{Q}),\frac{1}{q_{1}-1}]}(x),

where
s(n)
denotes the sum of the first
n
digits of the greedy expansion of
r_{Q}
, and
t(n)
denotes the sum of the first
n
digits of the lazy expansion of
\ell_{Q}
.
The following result states that the invariant densities
h_{g,Q}
and
h_{l,Q}
are obtained from the functions (
1.4
) and (
1.5
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that
Q=(q_{0},q_{1})\in(1,\infty)^{2}
with
q_{0}+q_{1}\geq q_{0}q_{1}
.
Then the invariant densities
h_{g,Q}
and
h_{l,Q}
are proportional to
\tilde{h}_{g,Q}
and
\tilde{h}_{l,Q}
, respectively.

In the single base case, the univoque set
\mathcal{U}_{Q}
is Lebesgue null set
[
4
]
.
The same phenomenon persists in the double base setting:

Theorem 1.4
.

If
Q=(q_{0},q_{1})\in(1,\infty)^{2}
with
q_{0}+q_{1}>q_{0}q_{1}
, then
\mathcal{U}_{Q}
is a Lebesgue null set.

The following theorem is an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.4
.

Theorem 1.5
.

If
Q=(q_{0},q_{1})\in(1,\infty)^{2}
with
q_{0}+q_{1}>q_{0}q_{1}
, then almost every
x\in I_{Q}
has a continuum of
Q
-expansions.

Our work is based on the important papers of Lasota and Yorke
[
19
,
18
]
.
We introduce the Frobenius–Perron transfer operators associated with the greedy and lazy maps in the double-base
setting, denoted by
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{L}
.
Our approach to the existence of absolutely continuous invariant probability measures is based on a linear conjugacy which rescales the state space
I_{Q}=[0,1/(q_{1}-1)]
to
[0,1]
.
After conjugation, the resulting maps are piecewise
C^{2}
expanding on
[0,1]
,
so that classical results of Lasota–Yorke yield invariant densities.
Pushing these measures forward through the conjugacy produces invariant probability measures for
G
and
L
on
I_{Q}
, which are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. Moreover, by invariance of exactness under measurable
conjugacy, the corresponding dynamical systems are exact.
In the spirit of Parry
[
27
]
, we further construct explicit step representations of the associated (non-normalized) invariant densities, after normalization these give
the probability densities of the two absolutely continuous invariant probability measures.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

Our proofs are based on a classical theorem of Lasota and Yorke
[
19
]
that we recall for the convenience of the reader.

Let
T
be a doubly measurable transformation of the unit interval
[0,1]
into itself, satisfying the following conditions:

(a)

there is a partition
0=a_{0}<\cdots<a_{N}=1
such that for each integer
k=1,\ldots,N
the restriction of
T
to the interval
[a_{k-1},a_{k})
is continuous and convex,

(b)

T_{a_{k-1}}=0
and
T^{\prime}_{a_{k-1}}>0
for
k=1,\ldots,N
, and

(c)

T^{\prime}(0)>1
.

Let
P
denote the Frobenius–Perron operator associated with
T
, defined by the formula

\int_{A}Pf\,dx=\int_{T^{-1}(A)}f\,dx

for all
f\in L^{1}([0,1])
.
Note that
P
is linear, preserves the integral, and is contractive:
\left\lVert Pf\right\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{1}}
for all
f\in L^{1}([0,1])
.

Under these assumptions the following theorem holds:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
19
]
, Theorem 4)
.

(i)

There exists a unique normalized absolutely continuous measure
\mu_{g}
that is invariant under
T
.

(ii)

The system
\left([0,1],\mu_{g},T\right)
is exact, and the density
g=d\mu_{g}/dx
is bounded and non-increasing.

(iii)

Moreover,
\left\lVert P^{n}f-g\right\rVert_{1}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for every nonnegative function
f\in L^{1}([0,1])
of integral one (with respect to the Lebesgue measure).

Remark 2.2
.

It follows from Theorem
2.1
and the continuity of
P
that the density function is a fixed point of
P
:

Pg=P(\lim_{n}P^{n}f)=\lim_{n}P^{n+1}f=g.

Conversely, if
Ph=h
for some nonnegative function
h\in L^{1}([0,1])
with
\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{L^{1}}>0
, then
h
is a constant multiple of
g
because

g=\lim_{n}P^{n}\left(\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{L^{1}}^{-1}h\right)=\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{L^{1}}^{-1}\lim_{n}P^{n}h=\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{L^{1}}^{-1}\lim_{n}h=\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{L^{1}}^{-1}h.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let us introduce the linear bijection
\phi:[0,1]\to I_{Q}
given by the formula
\phi(u):=u/(q_{1}-1)
, and the conjugate map

\widetilde{G}:=\phi^{-1}\circ G\circ\phi:[0,1]\to[0,1].

A direct computation gives

\widetilde{G}(u)=\begin{cases}q_{0}u,&u\in[0,a_{1}),\\[2.84526pt]
q_{1}u-(q_{1}-1),&u\in[a_{1},1],\end{cases}

where
a_{1}=(q_{1}-1)/q_{1}
.

Take the partition
0=a_{0}<a_{1}<a_{2}=1
. For each integer
k=1,2
the restriction of
\widetilde{G}
to the interval
[a_{k-1},a_{k})
is linear, hence continuous and convex.
Moreover,

\widetilde{G}(a_{0})=\widetilde{G}(0)=0,\qquad\widetilde{G}(a_{1})=q_{1}a_{1}-(q_{1}-1)=0,

and

\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(a_{0})=q_{0}>1,\qquad\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(a_{1})=q_{1}>0.

By Theorem
2.1
there exists a
\widetilde{G}
-invariant probability
measure
\mu_{\widetilde{G}}
on
[0,1]
which is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure
m_{1}
, and the system
([0,1],\Sigma_{[0,1]},\mu_{\widetilde{G}},\widetilde{G})
is exact.

Let
m
be the Lebesgue measure on
I_{Q}
and
d\mu_{\widetilde{G}}:=\widetilde{f}(u)\,dm_{1}
.
The formula

\mu_{g,Q}(E):=\mu_{\widetilde{G}}(\phi^{-1}E),\quad E\in\Sigma_{[0,1]}

defines a probability measure on
I_{Q}
because
\mu_{g,Q}(I_{Q})=\mu_{\widetilde{G}}([0,1])=1
.
Furthermore,
\mu_{g,Q}
is
G
-invariant because

\displaystyle\mu_{g,Q}(G^{-1}E)

\displaystyle=\mu_{\widetilde{G}}\bigl(\phi^{-1}(G^{-1}E)\bigr)=\mu_{\widetilde{G}}\bigl((G\circ\phi)^{-1}(E)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mu_{\widetilde{G}}\bigl((\phi\circ\widetilde{G})^{-1}(E)\bigr)=\mu_{\widetilde{G}}\bigl(\widetilde{G}^{-1}(\phi^{-1}E)\bigr)=\mu_{\widetilde{G}}(\phi^{-1}E)=\mu_{g,Q}(E)

for all
E\in\Sigma_{[0,1]}
, where we used the conjugacy relation
G\circ\phi=\phi\circ\widetilde{G}
and the

\widetilde{G}
-invariance of
\mu_{\widetilde{G}}
.
By the change of variable
x=u/(q_{1}-1)
, we have

\mu_{g,Q}(E)=\mu_{\widetilde{G}}(\phi^{-1}E)=\int_{\phi^{-1}E}\widetilde{f}(u)\,dm_{1}(u)=\int_{E}(q_{1}-1)\widetilde{f}\bigl((q_{1}-1)x\bigr)\,dm(x).

This shows that
\mu_{g,Q}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
I_{Q}
.

Since exactness is invariant under measure-theoretical isomorphisms
[
29
]
, we conclude that

([0,1],\Sigma_{[0,1]},\mu_{\widetilde{G}},\widetilde{G})\ \text{is exact}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(I_{Q},\Sigma_{I_{Q}},\mu_{g,Q},G)\ \text{is exact}.

Therefore
(I_{Q},\Sigma_{I_{Q}},\mu_{g,Q},G)
is exact.
This completes the proof for
G
.
The argument for
L
is analogous.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

The proof of Theorem 1.1 is not specific to the double-base setting: it remains valid for all regular alphabet-base systems in the terminology of
[
16
]
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We consider only the case of the greedy map; the proof for the lazy map is analogous.
For the proof first we need a special partition of
I_{Q}
into a family of pairwise disjoint intervals.
We recall that

I_{0}=\left[0,\frac{1}{q_{1}}\right),\quad I_{1}=\left[\frac{1}{q_{1}},\frac{1}{q_{1}-1}\right],\quad G^{-1}_{0}(x)=\frac{x}{q_{0}}\quad\text{and}\quad G^{-1}_{1}(x)=\frac{x+1}{q_{1}}.

Starting with
I_{0}
and
I_{1}
, we define recursively the intervals
I_{\mathbf{w}}
for all words

\mathbf{w}=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
of length
n\geq 2
by the formula

I_{\mathbf{w}}:=G^{-1}_{w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}}(I_{w_{n}})\cap I_{w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}}.

Here
G^{-1}_{w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}}(x)=G^{-1}_{w_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ G^{-1}_{w_{n-1}}(x)
.
For example,
I_{00}=[0,\frac{1}{q_{0}q_{1}})
and
I_{01}=[\frac{1}{q_{0}q_{1}},\frac{1}{q_{1}})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For each
n=1,2,\ldots,
the intervals
I_{\mathbf{w}}
for words of length
n
form a disjoint partition of
I_{Q}
.

Proof.

First we show that the intervals
I_{\mathbf{w}}
and
I_{\mathbf{v}}
for two different words
w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
and
v_{1}\cdots v_{n}
of length
n
are disjoint.

This clearly holds for
n=1
by a direct inspection of
I_{0}
and
I_{1}
.
Proceeding by induction, let
n\geq 2
, and assume that the property is true for
n-1
.

If
w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}\neq v_{1}\cdots v_{n-1}
, then
I_{w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}}
and
I_{v_{1}\cdots v_{n-1}}
are disjoint by the induction hypothesis.
Since
I_{\mathbf{w}}\subset I_{w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}}
and
I_{\mathbf{v}}\subset I_{v_{1}\cdots v_{n-1}}
by construction, then the intervals
I_{\mathbf{w}}
and
I_{\mathbf{v}}
are also disjoint.

If
w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}=v_{1}\cdots v_{n-1}
, then
w_{n}\neq v_{n}
.
Assume on the contrary that there exists a point
x\in I_{\mathbf{w}}\cap I_{\mathbf{v}}
.
Then we have

x\in G^{-1}_{w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}}(I_{1})\cap G^{-1}_{w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}}(I_{0})

by the definition of
I_{\mathbf{w}}
and
I_{\mathbf{v}}
.
This implies that
G_{w_{n-1}\cdots w_{1}}(x)\in I_{0}\cap I_{1}
, contradicting the disjointness of
I_{0}
and
I_{1}
.

We show that

I_{Q}=\cup_{\mathbf{w}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}I_{\mathbf{w}}

for every
n
.
This holds for

G^{n}(x)=\theta_{n}(b(x))
.
Therefore a straightforward application of Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem yields that

(5.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\theta_{j}(b(x))=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}G^{j}(x)=\int_{I_{Q}}xh_{g,Q}(x)\,dx

for
\mu_{g,Q}
-almost every
x\in I_{Q}
, and hence for Lebesgue almost every
x\in[0,r_{Q}]
(we apply Theorem
1.2
(i) here).

In order to show that (
5.1
) holds in fact for almost every
x\in I_{Q}
, let us denote by
N_{0}
the set of numbers
x\in[0,r_{Q}]
for which (
5.1
) does not hold.
We already know that
N_{0}
is a Lebesgue null set.
Since the map
H
of Lemma
5.2
is affine, the set

N:=\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}H^{k}(N_{0})\right)\bigcup\left\{\frac{1}{q_{1}-1}\right\}

is also a Lebesgue null set.

If
x\in I_{Q}\setminus N
, then by Lemma
5.2
there exists a
k\geq 0
such that
x\in H^{k}\left([0,r_{Q}]\right)
, and then
G^{k}x\in[0,r_{Q}]\setminus N_{0}
(we recall that
G\circ H
is the identity map).
Since the Cesàro convergence is not affected by the addition of a finite number of elements to a sequence, we conclude that
x
also satisfies (
5.1
).

Similarly to the relations (
5.1
), by symmetry we also have

(5.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\theta_{j}(l(x))=\int_{I_{Q}}xh_{l,Q}(x)\,dx

for Lebesgue almost every
x\in I_{Q}
, where
l(x)
denotes the lazy expansion of
x
.

Assume for a moment that the integrals in (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) are different.
Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\theta_{j}(b(x))\neq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\theta_{j}(l(x))

for almost every
x\in I_{Q}
, so that almost every
x\in I_{Q}
has different greedy and lazy expansions.
Consequently, the set of numbers having unique expansions is a Lebesgue null set.

The difference of the integrals in (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) follows from the inequalities

\displaystyle\int_{I_{Q}}xh_{g,Q}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle<(q_{1}-1)\Bigl(\int_{I_{Q}}x\,dx\Bigr)\Bigl(\int_{I_{Q}}h_{g,Q}(x)\,dx\Bigr)=\frac{1}{2(q_{1}-1)}

and

\displaystyle\int_{I_{Q}}xh_{l,Q}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle>(q_{1}-1)\Bigl(\int_{I_{Q}}x\,dx\Bigr)\Bigl(\int_{I_{Q}}h_{l,Q}(x)\,dx\Bigr)=\frac{1}{2(q_{1}-1)},

The corresponding weak inequalities follow from Lemma
5.1
because the identity function and
h_{l,Q}
are both non-decreasing, while
h_{g,Q}
is non-increasing on
I_{Q}
, and because non of the three functions is constant.
Indeed, the monotonicity statements follow from Theorem
1.1
, and the functions
h_{l,Q}
and
h_{g,Q}
are not constant because they have nonzero integral, and they vanish on non-empty intervals by Theorem
1.2
.
∎

Finally, we deduce Theorem
1.5
from Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

For convenience, we henceforth denote the lazy and greedy maps by
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
, respectively.
We also define
T_{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}=T_{w_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ T_{w_{1}}
for
w_{1}\ldots w_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
n=1,2,\ldots.

Observe that if
(w_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is an expansion of a number
x\in I_{Q}
, then
(w_{n+i})=w_{n+1}w_{n+2}\cdots
is an expansion of
T_{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}(x)
for each
n\geq 1
.

Since
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
are nonsingular, preimages of null sets are again null sets.
Since
\mathcal{U}_{Q}
has measure zero by Theorem
1.4
, the set

\mathcal{W}_{Q}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{x\in I_{Q}:\,T_{w_{1}\cdots w_{n}}(x)\in\mathcal{U}_{Q}\text{ for some }w_{1}\ldots w_{n}\in\left\{0,1\right\}\right\}

is a null set.

Applying Sidorov’s bifurcation argument
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
we obtain that every number
x\in I_{Q}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{Q}
has
2^{\aleph_{0}}
expansions.
See also Dajani and de Vries
[
4
]
for more details on Sidorov’s reasoning.
∎
