Abstract/Executive Summary

This project combined experimental algal cultures with predictive quantum calculations to
evaluate system-level CO2 uptake and conversion efficiency of cellulose-producing
Nannochloropsis sp. algae. Recently, Nannochloropsis sp. has garnered attention as a novel
host organism for converting low-cost CO2 produced from cellulosic ethanol fermentations into
algal lipids for biodiesel production and microcrystalline cellulose as a high-value co-product.
As depicted in the figure below, this project (1) optimized Nannochloropsis salina (N. salina)
cultures on effluent gas produced directly from cellulosic ethanol fermentation, (2)
characterized the fermentation products, quantify cellulose production, and calculate CO2
uptake efficiency with predictive quantum calculations, (3) conducted a life cycle and techno-
economic analysis of the proposed integration, and (4) provided training opportunities to
students attending UC Riverside.

Introduction and Motivation of the Project

 Cellulose is a highly valuable biopolymer and renewable chemical building block that is
most readily useful when hydrolyzed by fungal enzymes to fermentable glucose. The
crystalline portion of cellulose can be further processed into microcrystalline cellulose, which
is valuable as a texturizer, emulsifier, bulking agent, or textile precursor. Although its isolation
and utilization in the cellulosic ethanol industry are challenged by the recalcitrance of
lignocellulosic biomass to enzymatic breakdown, it is and will be one of the most important
molecules for supporting a future bio-economy and bio-industry. Its presence in both
lignocellulosic biomass and algal biomass presents an exciting synergistic opportunity to
investigate a combined approach of performing cellulosic ethanol and algal cellulose
production in tandem in this project.
 In conventional practices, carbon capture technologies (such as those using membranes,
chemicals, or adsorption) have focused primarily on capturing CO2 for storage and
transportation as a commodity. The production costs of both pure and high-pressure CO2 for
industrial purposes are not trivial and require expensive capture devices, intricate planning of
supply chain logistics, market-sensitive off-take agreements, and management of fluctuating
commodity pricing structures to be viable. Instead, we propose that the highly pure CO2
effluent from ethanol fermentation could be used as a direct feed for algal cultures. Algal
cultures are typically sparged with a 1-10% v/v CO2-enriched air stream into the
photobioreactor (PBR), whereas the CO2 concentrations of the effluent of ethanol
concentrations can also reach up to 39% of the headspace of a bioreactor.3 Furthermore, the
moist CO2 effluent from ethanol fermentation would not need to be dried or concentrated when
directly fed to algal cultures, thus completely eliminating the costly step of exogenous CO2
capture, drying, and storage.
 Although the concept of capturing CO2 from ethanol fermentations to support algal
production is not new, the further application of this concept towards producing cellulosic algae
from cellulosic ethanol production presents additional synergies and potential GHG savings
compared to corn ethanol fermentations. This is because lignocellulosic biomass also contains
lignin, an energy-rich heteropolymer that is 30% more energy-dense than whole biomass.
Lignin is generated as a solid byproduct that can fully provide the heating requirements to
support both cellulosic ethanol production as well as algal cellulose production. As a biogenic
material, its combustion for heat and energy is carbon neutral, and its direct combustion in a
process boiler eliminates additional waste generation during cellulosic ethanol production.
Furthermore, the CO2 emitted from lignin combustion could also be fed to algal production to
further supplement CO2 inputs and reduce the overall CI of the combined process.

 Our model predicts an algae biomass production of 815 kg/hour. N. salina contains ~32%
lipids and ~10% micro-crystalline cellulose (MCC), resulting in algal lipid production of 260
kg/hour and MCC production of 81 kg/hour. By culturing algae from ethanol fermentation
effluent, we can potentially sequester up to 50% of total CO2 produced from fermentation in
the form of algae.
 Finally, to complement the experiments and techno-economic analysis, a series of large-
scale, predictive quantum calculations was carried out to understand both the CO2 uptake
mechanisms and the production of microcrystalline cellulose. To probe the mechanisms
responsible for the production of microcrystalline cellulose, large-scale calculations beyond
the capability of DFT are required. Specifically, this project harnessed a massively-parallelized
density functional tight binding (DFTB)7-12 computational approach, which the PI’s group has
used for both ground- and electronic-excited states.13-24 One of the successful results of one of
those projects was a working implementation of DFTB on massively-parallelized graphical
processing units (GPUs) or other hardware accelerators25 at UC Riverside (described further
below). Specifically, the GPU-enhanced RT-TDDFTB code is parallelized with GPUs,
enabling the routine exploration of large systems (typically ~5,000 atoms or more). The
propagation of the one-electron density matrix in this hybrid CPU/GPU scheme allows a
computational speedup of two orders of magnitude with respect to traditional processors, which
was used to explore the large microcrystalline cellulose production processes in this project.

 7

While classical molecular dynamics (MD) can handle hundreds of thousands of atoms, it
cannot provide a first-principles-based description of large, complex systems at the predictive
quantum level.26-28 At the other extreme, conventional DFT methods can probe the true
quantum nature of chemical systems; however, these methods cannot tackle the large sizes
relevant to the large microcrystalline cellulose production processes in this project. As such,
the DFTB formalism (coupled with our in-house GPU capabilities for enhanced speed) used in
this project provided a viable approach for probing microcrystalline cellulose production
mechanisms at a quantum mechanical level of detail.
 To carry out these large-scale calculations, representative geometries for cellulose were
obtained via a series of DFTB-based molecular dynamics (MD) calculations. A short energy
minimization of steepest descent steps was first applied, subsequently followed by a simulated
annealing cooling step. Finally, an NVT MD equilibration was carried out with a small
integration step with our GPU-enhanced, DFTB-based MD approach for calculating large
systems. With these configurations, a series of DFTB calculations was carried out to obtain
configurational-averaged initial conditions to probe the cellulose production mechanisms.
 We carried out additional in-depth performance tests that compare our CPU vs GPU calculations
(Fig. 6). We found that the optimal configuration appears to be 4 GPU nodes, which shows impressive
performance compared to CPUs.

 These examples of extremely large/complex systems are, to the best of our knowledge, the
first time that an entire explicitly-solvated lignin system has been treated at a quantum-based
MD level of detail. This extremely large-scale calculation demonstrates the efficiency of our
heterogenous computing approach and further emphasizes the importance of DFTB-based
dynamics for probing these large biochemical systems. Specifically, while it is common to
carry out a quantum-mechanical optimization of biological structures initially obtained from
classical MD or molecular docking methods, we show that this conventional strategy can miss
important conformations, and the use of efficient and accurate dynamical approaches, such as
the CPU+GPU-enhanced DFTB approach used here, can be essential. Moving forward, we
anticipate that this heterogenous CPU+GPU computational capability can also be used for other
complex lignin-based systems that require long-time quantum-based dynamics, such as
