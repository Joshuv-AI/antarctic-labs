Demographic characteristics and associated conditions in enteropathogenic Escherichia coli positive patients
Authors: Heather Lusby, Stacy G. Beal, Kenneth H. Rand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMany studies have examined the epidemiology and risk factors of Enteropathogenic Escherichia coli (EPEC); however, few have focused on the adult population. This study aimed to determine if EPEC was associated with certain patient populations or characteristics. We reviewed 792 hospitalized adults and children with symptomatic diarrhea who tested positive for EPEC on the BioFire Gastrointestinal (GI) Pathogen Panel and compared with symptomatic patients with no targets detected on the same panel as controls.ResultsIn the adult group the EPEC positive actual length of stay (LOS) was 13.5 days CI 95% [4.6,22.2] vs. 9.3 days CI 95% [7.9,10.5] (p = 0.14) for the control group, while in the pediatric population, both expected and actual LOS were actually longer in the EPEC negative patients than the EPEC positive patients (p ≤ 0.01 for both). In adults, 31.4% of EPEC positive patients had a past medical history of autoimmune disease, compared to 19.2% (p = 0.03) in the control group, after controlling for multiple variable testing.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this association between EPEC and autoimmune disease has not been previously reported and could be mediated by gut dysbiosis, suggesting avenues for follow up studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13099-026-00864-y.

Introduction

Enteropathogenic Escherichia coli (EPEC) is one of the most important gastrointestinal pathogens worldwide, mainly as a cause of infantile diarrhea [1]. The newer generation of multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) panels has led to the ability of more rapid detection of enteric pathogens. In many institutions, these have largely replaced, or are used in conjunction with, stool cultures [1, 2]. Consequently, EPEC has been detected more frequently than previously described in the adult population [3].

In adults, there is an overall decreased risk of acute EPEC infection, and adults have rarely been described to experience acute episodes [4] However, EPEC can cause acute diarrheal illness in both cancer and post-transplant adult patients [3, 5, 6], as well as volunteers given sufficiently large infectious doses [7]. As a cause of diarrhea, EPEC among other diarrheagenic E. coli infections, has been associated with hematologic malignancy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [8]. In addition, higher bacterial burdens were found in patients with solid tumors, hematologic malignancies and stem cell transplants compared with the levels in healthy controls [9]. Stone et al. [10] found a higher incidence of EPEC after transplantation than prior to transplantation among pediatric patients.

To our knowledge, there has not been a review of conditions associated with EPEC positivity among both children and adults, although positivity rates have previously been described on multiplex PCR panels [2, 3, 11]. Our aim was to describe potential associated conditions or risk factors to further elucidate the pathogenesis of EPEC in all populations.

Materials and methods

This is a single center, case control study based on a retrospective chart review of hospitalized patients from January 1st, 2016 through August 31st, 2019. Both EPEC positive patients and EPEC negative (i.e. no targets detected) controls were symptomatic with diarrheal illness. Symptomatic diarrhea was defined as ≥3 loose bowel movements in less than or equal to 24 h.

All patients had a FilmArray BioFire PCR gastrointestinal (GI) multiplex panel performed during the hospitalization. The EPEC group was defined as patients who were EPEC positive including coinfections with other pathogens. If there was a repeat panel that was positive for EPEC during the same hospitalization, the repeat result was excluded. Patients with diarrhea who had no targets detected by the GI panel were considered the control group. Each EPEC positive patient was matched to 3 controls based upon age (within 5 years) and gender. Past medical history was extracted using ICD9 and ICD10 billing codes as described below in more detail. Fisher’s Exact Test was used for categorical variables. The T-test was used to compute p values for population means with normal distributions. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to correct for false discovery when testing multiple variables in this case N = 7 as described belowand was applied independently for both the pediatric and adult populations. Specifically, the ICD 9 and 10 coded past medical history was assigned to one of the following seven categories: Coronary artery disease (CAD), Diabetes mellitus, Chronic kidney disease (CKD), Autoimmune disease, Human Immunosuppressive virus infection (HIV), History of solid organ or bone marrow transplantation, History of chronic diarrhea, specifically any history of irritable bowel disease, Crohn’s disease, Ulcerative colitis, Celiac disease, Lactose intolerance, Chronic pancreatitis, and Unspecified chronic diarrhea. Chart reviews were performed by trained informatics staff in the UF Health Clinical and Translational Science Institute using electronic search of the Epic Systems, Verona, WI medical record based on ICD codes and natural language processing of clinical notes. Chart reviews were also performed by one of us (KHR) specifically for the EPEC and Control patients identified as having Autoimmune disease, confirming the categorization if the patient had an autoimmune disease listed in their highlighted problem list, ANA ≥ 1:320 or other abnormal autoantibodies, (excluding thyroid and type 1 diabetes), Positive Rheumatoid Factor both performed at ARUP laboratories, Salt Lake City, UT or repeated characterization in clinic notes as having a history of an autoimmune disease. Specific ICD 9 and 10 codes are shown in supplemental Table 1.

Expected and actual length of stay were provided by Vizient Inc, Irving, TX for each patient as part of UF Health Shands Hospital membership.

Results

A total of 792 patients, including 539 adults and 253 children, were included in the analysis. Among the adults (≥ 18), there were 137 in the EPEC group and 402 in the matched control group. A total of 253 children were reviewed with 73 in the EPEC group and 180 in the matched control group. Patient characteristics for the adults and children, are shown in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. For the adult population, the average age was 54.8 ± 18.2 years in the EPEC group and 54.8 ± 18.3 years in control group (P = 0.99), and the average ages for the pediatric population were 4.9 ± 5.2 years in the EPEC positive patients and 6.2 ± 6.5 years in the control (P = 0.13). Males accounted for 69.9% in the pediatric population and 49.0% of the adults (p < 0.0035). Co-infection was observed in 24.8% of EPEC positive adults and 57.5% in children (P < 0.001). Thus, EPEC was the sole pathogen detected in 75.2% of adults, but only 42.5% of children (P < 0.001).

The frequency of co-morbidities for each patient population is shown in Table 3 (adult) and Table 4 (children). In adults, autoimmune disease was observed in 31.4% of the EPEC group and 19.2% of the control (p = 0.03, after correction for testing multiple variables). When charts of the adult EPEC and Control populations with autoimmune disease were reviewed by one of the authors (KHR), there were 15/137 (10.9%) of EPEC positive patients vs. 14/402 (3.5%) of Control patients had a history of (or active) autoimmune disease, p = 0.000823, equivalent to p = 0.00576 after multiple variable testing, N = 7. (Please refer to Supplemental Table 2 for a list of the specific autoimmune diseases in both groups.) In the pediatric population, there was no statistical difference in comorbidities between the EPEC and the control groups.

Length of stay was examined in each of the populations. In adults, the actual length of stay for the EPEC group was 13.5 days CI 95% [4.6, 22.2] and 9.3 days CI 95% [7.9, 10.5] (p = 0.14) in the control group. The expected length of stay was 5.5 days CI 95% [4.4,6.6] for the EPEC group and 5.2 CI 95% [4.7,5.7] (p = 0.54) for the control. In the pediatric population, the actual length of stay in the EPEC group was 12.7 CI 95% [4.1, 12.3] and was 25.8 CI 95% [17.8, 33.8] (p < 0.01) in the control group; the expected length of stay in the EPEC group was 4.7 CI 95% [3.0, 6.4] and 11.9 CI 95% [5.1, 18.7] for the control group (p < 0.01).

Discussion

Autoimmune disease was more common in patients with EPEC compared to an age- and gender-matched control population (p = 0.03 after correction for false discovery when testing multiple variables). Although the 31.4% association of autoimmune disease with EPEC by algorithmic chart review was higher than that of the author’s review, both were statistically significant to the same degree after correcting for multiple hypothesis testing. Since the algorithmic methodology is computer code based, it has the advantage of being inherently unbiased applying equally to both the EPEC and control groups.

To our knowledge, this association has not been previously reported and raises questions about the underlying biological explanation. Numerous studies have shown an association between gut dysbiosis and a variety of inflammatory and/or autoimmune diseases [12–17]. For example, Vaahtovuo et al. [18], showed that patients with early Rheumatoid Arthritis had significant alterations in their intestinal microbiota compared with fibromyalgia control patients, while Zhang et al. [19] found the abnormal microbiome associated with RA was partially normalized during treatment. Li et al. [20] found a disordered microbiome associated with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) that correlated with disease activity and was distinct from the alterations in rheumatoid arthritis. Likewise, gut dysbiosis has been associated with type 1 diabetes [15, 21, 22] as well as autoimmune hepatitis [23]. The normal microbiome exhibits a high level of colonization resistance through a number of mechanisms, such as production of bacteriocins with antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activities, production of short chain fatty acids e.g. butyric acid, that can inhibit pathogen growth and virulence genes, competition for nutrients needed by pathogens, competition for binding sites, strengthening the gut surface by production of innate anti-inflammatory immune cytokines, strengthening tight junctions, and maintaining the functionality of the mucous layer [15, 16, 24]. Thus, gut dysbiosis associated with autoimmune diseases may impact colonization resistance against pathogens, thereby increasing the susceptibility of patients to infection and/or colonization by EPEC.

In our pediatric population EPEC positivity was more likely associated with co-infection at 57.5% vs. 24.8% in the adult population, p < 0.001. In contrast to the adult male: female ratio of ≈ 1:1, there was a male predominance of 69.9% in our pediatric EPEC positive patients. Atypical strains of EPEC (aEPEC) have been shown to have a higher frequency in the pediatric male population, although the reasons are unknown [12].

The length of stay In the adult population for the EPEC group was 4 days longer than the control group, although this was not statistically significant. Expected LOS as a proxy for overall severity of illness on admission was similar between the 2 adult groups and was within 6 h of each other. The expected LOS is based upon benchmark data developed by Vizient using proprietary methodology that includes all ICD coded patient elements at admission. While a trend towards a longer actual length of stay in the adult EPEC group could suggest that the EPEC may have contributed to a delay in discharge timing, we cannot assume cause and effect. The adult EPEC group was also more likely to have EPEC as the sole pathogen (75.2%) compared with 42.47% in the EPEC pediatric population (P < 0.0001).

In the pediatric population the length of stay in the EPEC group was actually shorter than in the Control group, 12.7 days CI 95% [4.1,12.3] vs. 25.8 days, CI 95% [17.8,33.8] (p < 0.01), which seems counter intuitive. Patients in the control group less than 12 months of age, and in particular newborns had lengths of stays that were sometimes greater than 100 days that may have contributed to this difference. Table 5 illustrates the difference between the LOS in pediatric population less than 12 months and between the ages of 1–17 months. The LOS observed in the control group ≤ 12 months was 50.1 days compared with 14.4 days in the EPEC group (p = 0.07). Prior studies have also used > 21 days to define prolonged hospitalizations and including these prolonged hospitalizations could have affected the average LOS in both groups [25, 26]. Correcting for prolonged hospitalizations, the mean expected LOS in the control was 3.8 and in the EPEC group 3.3 (p = 0.20). The actual LOS in the control group at 5.5 and EPEC group at 4.0 (p = 0.06) was not statistically significant, although there was still a trend towards a longer LOS in the control. The difference in LOS also seemed to be less apparent in the ages between 1 and 17 years. The expected LOS in the control group was statistically longer than in the EPEC group at an average 4.7 days CI 95% [3.0,6.4] vs. 11.9 CI 95% [5.1,18.7] in the control group (p < 0.01). These findings may suggest that these patients were sicker overall independent of EPEC.

This study had several limitations. It was performed at a single academic center and past medical history was collected based on natural language processing and chart review for ICD 9 and ICD 10 codes. ICD codes were provided by professional trained coders and were likely to be consistent within a given institution, but there could be wide variations between institutions. Additionally, data for medications were not collected, and it is possible that medications used to treat autoimmune conditions could affect a patient’s susceptibility to disease caused by EPEC.

Conclusions

We found a significantly higher rate of EPEC positivity among patients with autoimmune conditions compared with age and gender matched controls in the adult population. This observation might be related to gut dysbiosis associated with autoimmune disease and suggests further studies should be pursued to confirm and extend these findings.
