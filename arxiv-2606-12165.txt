Pricing mobility services under decision-dependent demand uncertainty: a carsharing case
Jiali Deng, Giovanni Pantuso

Abstract
The problem of pricing mobility services has attracted significant attention. In most studies, uncertain demand is modeled as an exogenous random variable with known distribution. This assumption overlooks the likely effect of prices on user adoption decisions. To address this dependency, we formulate the pricing problem as a stochastic program with decision-dependent demand uncertainty. Specifically, we make the non-standard assumption that the probability distribution of demand depends on pricing decisions. We show that the problem can be written as a mixed-integer linear program whose size is exponential in the input parameters. To find exact numerical solutions we specialize the L-shaped method for stochastic programs with decision-dependent uncertainty. In particular, we devise efficient separation routines by proving closed-form primal and dual solutions to the involved subproblems. In addition, we develop problem-specific valid inequalities and cut-sharing mechanisms which significantly improve convergence. We show that the method outperforms by far a commercial solver used to solve the monolithic formulation. Furthermore, in a case study based on a real-world carsharing system, we show that incorporating decision-dependent uncertainty improves expected profits by 8.39 % 8.39\% compared to a benchmark that considers deterministic price-elastic demand, and by 8.53 % 8.53\% compared to a benchmark that considers exogenous random demand, on average. In addition, we evaluate the performance of preventive pricing and relocation decisions under two vehicle allocation policies. The results suggest that a controlled allocation of vehicles to customers can improve service rates while only marginally affecting profits.

Introduction

Shared mobility has become an important component of modern urban transportation systems. However, offering flexible on-demand mobility in a profitable manner poses significant management challenges. A central element of complexity is the necessity of making operational decisions without complete knowledge of near-future demand.
Consequently, vehicle relocation has been widely studied as an instrument to prevent or ameliorate spatial mismatches between demand and supply, particularly in the carsharing domain, see, e.g.,
27
,
17
,
29
,
18
,
23
. Relocations are, however, inherently expensive and prone to inefficiency
(
14
)
. For this reason, pricing-based demand management is gaining popularity. Real-world examples include, among others, the German shared mobility company
Share Now

(
16
)
, which uses real-time price adjustments based on updated demand information, and the software company
Zoba

(
36
)
, which updates service prices in a regular interval (e.g., every 30 minutes) given real-time supply and demand information.

In the context of one-way carsharing services, the question of how to effectively differentiate service prices by location and/or time has gained popularity.
The optimization literature has offered models and methods where prices act either as a substitute
(
9
,
28
,
31
,
4
)
or a supplement
(
8
,
32
,
15
,
20
)
to vehicle rebalancing efforts. Accordingly,
carsharing system operators
(CSOs) use the pricing levers to mitigate the geographical or/and temporal demand-supply mismatch and, consequently, improve service rate and revenue potential. Hence, the relationship between prices and demand becomes an important component of the models.

In the available shared mobility research, the effect of prices on rental demand is typically modeled via their effects on demand location or volume as well as on individual mode choice.
21
assumed that price incentives would induce users to return bikes to stations other than the one closest to their destination.
25
extended the effect of prices on user’s station choice to both pick-up and drop-off bicycle stations. In a one-way carsharing pricing problem studied by
28
, similar price-induced demand location shifts were also considered on an individual user basis. Apart from this effect,
28
modeled the total demand generated from each station as a reverse logistic function of pick-up prices.
9
assumed that carsharing demand decreases linearly with price at a constant rate.
Likewise,
31
approximated the demand as a linear function of price with constant elasticity.
32
adopted an exponential function to capture the nonlinear relationship between demand and price. The authors truncate the demand function at a given price threshold to prevent the price from rising indefinitely.
8
modeled the behaviour of individual carsharing customers through a Logit model where travel time and price are accounted for as factors in the utility function. The portion of carsharing demand between two traffic zones was then determined by mode choice of individual customers. A similar approach was used by
4
. The authors focus on the partition of the service region as well as zone-to-zone prices. In the above studies, carsharing demand is a deterministic function of price. Hence, while the effect of prices on demand is accounted for, the stochastic nature of demand is not accurately modeled.

When uncertain demand is taken into account, it is usually modeled as an exogenous random variable. In vehicle relocation problems, both
17
and
14
assumed the demand for carsharing to be known only in distribution.
17
used chance constraints to guarantee satisfaction of a given proportion of demand.
14
formulated a two-stage mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) model with finite demand scenarios generated from a joint distribution of one-way and round-trip demand rentals.
14
also conducted experiments on the case that one-way rental demand was endogenously determined by prices, where empirical demand distributions were generated from historical data with breaking rental price points. Nevertheless, the stochastic optimization model proposed by
14
does not include pricing decisions.

7
formulated a long-term fleet sizing and pricing problem as a two-stage stochastic program, where short-term vehicle relocations represent second-stage decisions. Demand was jointly determined by an exogenous random base-values (with given distribution) and a constant demand elasticity.
20
proposed a two-stage stochastic program where first-stage decisions are pricing and relocation decisions while second-stage decisions model rental decisions. Uncertainty of demand is accounted for by means of the randomness in the model of customer behavior (e.g., response to prices). A potential drawback of their approach is the necessity to individually include customers in the optimization model, i.e., demand is not aggregated.

In the account given above, it emerges that pricing decisions (i) are often made before accurately knowing customer demand and (ii) influence adoption decision and hence rental demand.
Stochastic optimization naturally became a reference decision-support methodology, see, e.g.,
14
,
7
,
20
.
However, the existing literature typically models carsharing demand as an
exogenous
random variable, that is, independent of the CSO’s decisions. The dependency of random demand on prices remains, to a large extent, unexplored. Part of the explanation behind this lack of studies is the sparsity of general methodology for stochastic optimization with
endogenous
(or
decision-dependent
) uncertainty. While applications can be found, such as
33
for power generation expansion,
12
for medical evacuation,
10
for asset–liability management, general-purpose methodology is somewhat sparse. To the best of our knowledge, we propose the first application in the domain of shared mobility.

In this paper, we address the problem of pricing mobility services accounting for
endogenous
demand uncertainty.
The problem is studied in the context of a one-way station-based carsharing system where rental prices may depend on the origin and/or destination of the rental. We assume that prices have an impact on rental demand. In particular, we assume that the probability distribution of rental demand is fully specified by the pricing decisions made.
Hence, the CSO seeks to optimally set carsharing rental prices between different locations, and operate the necessary relocations, in order to maximize the conditional (on the price) expectation of rental profits in a near-future interval of time. Carsharing and, more generally, shared mobility services have developed into a variety of practical operating models. We do not aim to provide the most accurate model for a specific business case or for specific assumptions on customers behavior. In contrast, we provide a recipe that can be easily adapted to various specifications of the service.

The contributions and main findings of this paper can be summarized as follows.

•

We propose a
two-stage mixed-integer stochastic program with decision-dependent uncertainty
to model pricing and relocation decisions in a one-way carsharing service, where the random demand follows a price-dependent probability distribution.

•

To address the exponential size of the model, we propose an exact solution method which specializes a variant of the L-shaped method for problems with decision-dependent uncertainty developed by
19
. In particular, (i) we derive distribution-specific optimality cuts, (ii) we prove closed-form primal and dual solutions to the second-stage problems; this guarantees efficient separation routines, (iii) we propose effective problem-specific valid inequalities and, finally, (iv) we devise cut-sharing mechanisms among different versions of the second-stage problem.

•

In extensive computational experiments, we demonstrate the scalability of the proposed method. In particular, the method could solve more than twice as many instances to optimality compared to a commercial solver.

•

Finally, in a case study based on a carsharing system in Copenhagen, Denmark, we show and discuss the effects of accounting for decision-dependent uncertainty. Taking into account decision-dependent demand uncertainty increased expected profits by between
7.04\%
and
10.82\%
compared to a benchmark with deterministic price-elastic demand, and by between
3.01\%
and
13.54\%
compared to a benchmark with decision-independent stochastic demand.

In the remainder of this paper, we first give a detailed problem description and a general formulation of the carsharing pricing and relocation problem with endogenous demand uncertainty in
Section

2
.
In

Section

4

Section

5
, through a real-world case study based on a carsharing system operated in Copenhagen, Denmark, we discuss the practical applicability of our proposed model and the effects of accounting for decision-dependent uncertainty. Finally, we draw conclusions and make final remarks in
Section

6
.

Problem

Given a target interval of time (e.g.,
17:00-18:00
), the CSO needs to determine (i) the rental prices to apply during that interval of time and (ii) the relocations to perform before the beginning of (hence in preparation for) that interval of time.

Let
\mathcal{G}:=(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{A})
denote the carsharing network with
\mathcal{I}
being the set of carsharing stations and
\mathcal{A}:=\{(i,j)|i,j\in\mathcal{I},i\not=j\}
.
We assume the set
\mathcal{I}
is partitioned into zones (e.g., geographical districts of the city). This is without loss of generality, since it is always possible to define one zone for each station. Let
\mathcal{Z}
denote the set of zones and
\mathcal{I}^{z}\subset\mathcal{I}
the set of stations in zone
z
. For each pair of zones, the CSO may select a rental price from a discrete set of prices
\mathcal{L}
. Hence, pricing decisions are modeled by binary variables
x:=(x_{z_{1},z_{2},l})_{z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z},l\in\mathcal{L}}
, with
x_{z_{1},z_{2},l}
taking value
1
if price
l
is applied for rentals that originate in
z_{1}
and terminate in zone
z_{2}
,
0
otherwise. Zone-based rental prices may represent e.g., pick-up or drop-off fees as employed in a number of real-world services. For instance,
Free2move
charges a drop-off fee for trips ending in designated zones of the city, and
Zity
in Milan imposes both drop-off and pick-up fees for trips ending and starting in different zones, see
5
and
35
respectively.
The specification of the rental fee is problem specific and does not have an impact on the derivation of the model. We will provide an example in our experiments, see,
Section

4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the set of shared vehicles. Relocation decisions are modeled by binary variables
s:=(s_{vi})_{v\in\mathcal{V},i\in\mathcal{I}}
where
s_{vi}
takes value
1
if vehicle
v
is made available at station
i
,
0
otherwise. Relocating vehicle
v
from its current station to station
i
generates a cost
C_{vi}
.

The demand within the target interval of time is uncertain at the time pricing and relocation decisions are made. In addition, the probability distribution of demand is influenced by pricing decisions, hence
endogenous
to the problem. Given a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, we model the demand for carsharing rides during the target interval of time as a random variable
\boldsymbol{\xi}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{|\mathcal{A}|}_{\geq 0}
. Hence, element
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{ij}
represents the random demand on arc
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
.
In particular, we assume that there exist
K
potential customers in the system (e.g., all the users registered to the service). Hence, we define
\Omega:=\{0,1\}^{K}
and we set
\mathcal{F}
to be the power set of
\Omega
. This means that each random event
\omega
is a binary vector of length
K
that represents whether or not each customer requests to rent a vehicle in the target period.
Given this setup, for
\omega\in\Omega
, the realization of the
(i,j)
-th random variable is defined as

\xi_{ij}(\omega)=\sum_{k|i(k)=i,j(k)=j}\omega_{k}\qquad\forall i,j\in\mathcal{A}

where
i(k)\in\mathcal{I}
and
j(k)\in\mathcal{I}
represent the origin and destination stations of customer
k
, respectively. Then
\xi(\omega):=(\xi_{ij}(\omega))_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}
.

Figure

1
provides an example of the realization assuming only one origin-destination pair.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu_{x})
.

We let the probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, denoted
\mu_{x}
, be fully determined by pricing decisions
x
.
In particular, for each potential customer
k
, we assume the probability of using carsharing conditional on the price decision
x
is known, denoted by
P(\omega_{k}=1|x)
. This probability can be obtained, for example, using discrete choice models such as the Logit model. Then, the probability measure
\mu_{x}
can be defined for each
\omega\in\Omega
as

\displaystyle\mu_{x}(\{\omega\})=\prod_{k|\omega_{k}=1}P(\omega_{k}=1|x)\prod_{k|\omega_{k}=0}(1-P(\omega_{k}=1|x))

(1)

Finally, with these elements in place, we define the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{|\mathcal{A}|}_{\geq 0}
conditional on
x
as
P_{x}
. Given an integer vector
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{|\mathcal{A}|}_{\geq 0}
, this is defined as

\displaystyle P_{x}(\boldsymbol{\xi}=N)=\mu_{x}\left(\left\{\omega\in\Omega|\xi(\omega)=N\right\}\right)

(2)

Also in this case, the specific way in which
P(\omega_{k}=1|x)
for each
k
is obtained depends on the particular case and on the assumptions made regarding customer behavior. This does not have an impact on the general model proposed. A particular specification of
\mu_{x}(\{\omega\})
is described in
Section

4.1
and
Appendix
D
.

We model the decision problem as a stochastic program. Here, pricing and relocation decisions are made prior to knowing the realization of demand and in preparation for that. Once decisions have been made, rental demand in the target time interval arrives at random according to the conditional distribution
P_{x}
. Then, rental profit
Q(x,s,\xi)
is obtained as a consequence of pricing and relocation decisions and the demand materialized (i.e.,
\xi
).
In
Section

2.1
we model
Q(x,s,\xi)
as an optimization problem, hence obtaining a two-stage stochastic program.
The carsharing pricing and relocation problem can be modeled as the following two-stage stochastic program with decision-dependent uncertainty.

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{vi}s_{vi}+\mathbb{E}_{P_{x}}\big[Q(x,s,\xi)\big]

(3a)

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}x_{z_{1},z_{2},l}=1

\displaystyle\forall z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z},\forall l\in\mathcal{L}

(3b)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}s_{vi}=1

\displaystyle\forall v\in\mathcal{V}

(3c)

\displaystyle x_{z_{1},z_{2},l}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z},\forall l\in\mathcal{L}

(3d)

\displaystyle s_{vi}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall v\in\mathcal{V},\forall i\in\mathcal{I}

(3e)

In problem (
3
), the objective function consists of a negative term corresponding to relocation costs and the conditional expectation of second-stage profits.
Constraints (
3b
) state that exactly one price level must be assigned to each pair of zones. Constraints (
3c
) state that each shared vehicle should be made available at exactly one carsharing station
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Constraints (
3d
) and (
3e
) define the domain of the decisions variables. Problem (
3
) allows for various definitions of the second-stage profit
Q(x,s,\xi)
depending on the specifics and operating strategies of the carsharing system. We provide one such definition – and remark on possible extensions of it – in

2.1
Expected revenue

To model expected revenue conditional on pricing decisions we assume that the CSO adopts a “
demand pooling
” strategy, see, e.g.,
17
,
34
,
13
. This means that, to rent a vehicle during a given interval of time, users must send requests before a given deadline. After the deadline, the CSO matches supply and demand, hereby fulfilling or rejecting rental requests.

This entails that, periodically, the operator is faced with the following problem:
Given a set of rental requests, decide which ones to accept
.
There are obviously many policies an operator may adopt. The most straightforward is to let profits drive the decisions. For simplicity, we present this policy in
Section

2.1.1
and show that it can be implemented by means of a MILP problem.

However, an operator might wish to adopt different policies. For example, a
fixed assignment
of vehicles to requests based on, e.g., characteristics of the request such as the destination station or the type of customer. This might provide the operator additional instruments to control, e.g., the levels of service, the overall accessibility of the service, the future distribution of the fleet and which zones get access to the service.
For example, the operator may prioritize the allocation of vehicles to customers traveling to specific destinations – that are not necessarily the most profitable – in order to ensure more homogeneous access to the service across the operating area.

We stress that, while the implemented policy presented in
Section

2.1.1
is, perhaps, simplified in some real-life features (e.g., they assume each vehicle is rented at most once during the target time interval), it results in a reasonable proxy of expected profits.
To illustrate the performance of the method on more involved assignment policies, in
Appendix
A
we provide a version of
Q(x,s,\xi)
where the assignment of vehicles to customers follows a fixed policy based on the customers destinations. In particular, we assume, as in
26
, that the operator bounds the number of vehicles directed to each destination by the share of demand to that destination.

2.1.1
A profit-driven vehicle allocation policy

Given a first-stage solution
(x,s)
and a demand realization
\xi
of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, we formulate the recourse problem of allocating supply to demand with the goal of maximizing profits as a MILP. We show that
Q(x,s,\xi)
becomes a special transportation problem with independent sources and mutually-exclusive sinks.

Let
P_{ijl}
denote the rental price for picking up a vehicle at station
i
and returning it at station
j
when price level
\mathcal{L}
is applied.
Define a mapping
\zeta:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}
such that
\zeta(i)
identifies the zone to which station
i
belongs. Given a feasible first-stage solution
x
, the price for renting a vehicle at station
i
and returning it at station
j
is
\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}P_{ijl}x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l}
.
Note that
\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l}=1
since
x
is feasible. We define
\mathcal{J}_{i}:=\{j\in\mathcal{I}:(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}\}
as the set of destination stations connected to a source station
i\in\mathcal{I}
. We make
|\mathcal{L}|
copies of each destination station
j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}
. These represent the sinks connected to source station
i
. We then define variables
r_{ijl}
to represent the flow of vehicles from source
i
to sink
(j,l)
, that is, the number of vehicles serving demand on arc
(i,j)
at price level
l
, see
Figure

2
.

(j,l)

.

With this notation in place we can formulate the recourse problem as

\displaystyle Q(x,s,\xi)=\max

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}P_{ijl}r_{ijl}

(4a)

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle r_{ijl}\leq\xi_{ij}x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l},

\displaystyle\forall(i,j)\in\mathcal{A},\forall l\in\mathcal{L}

(4b)

\displaystyle\sum_{(j,l)\in\mathcal{J}_{i}\times\mathcal{L}}r_{ijl}\leq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi},

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathcal{I}

(4c)

\displaystyle r_{ijl}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

\displaystyle\forall(i,j)\in\mathcal{A},\forall l\in\mathcal{L}

(4d)

Constraints (
4b
) state that no vehicles will be sent to sink
(j,l)
if
x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l}=0
, and we can send up to the demand of customer
j
(i.e.,
\xi_{ij}
), otherwise. Observe that, if
x
is feasible for (
3
), only one of the
(j,l)
sinks will have positive demand for each

. Hence, for each
(i,j)
only one of these

variables can take a positive value. Constraint (

.

In

) to include a proportional assignment of vehicles depending on the destination of the requests.

) will be referred to as the

will be referred to as the

3
Exact solution method

We propose an exact decomposition method that accounts for decision-dependent demand uncertainty.

The method instantiates and extends the L-shaped method for stochastic programs with decision-dependent uncertainty (henceforth,
DD-LS
) developed by
19
. The
DD-LS
method is applicable to problems where the space of feasible solutions is partitioned into a finite collection of subsets. To each subset there corresponds a specific probability distribution of the random parameters. The method consists of adding distribution-specific optimality and feasibility cuts. The specificity of the cuts is as follows: given a probability distribution, the corresponding optimality and feasibility cuts are effective only in the subregion which enforces that distribution, and redundant otherwise.

We adopt a number of problem-specific instantiations and extensions of the method.

•

In
19
the authors test the method on a problem where the feasible region is partitioned into
polyhedra
. We show how the method can be implemented on a problem where the feasible region has a
combinatorial
structure and is partitioned into a collection of finite-dimensional subsets.

•

We develop problem-specific optimality cuts. We show that these cuts can be separated efficiently by exploiting the specific structure of both the RPWoPA and RPWPA problems. In particular, we develop closed-form primal and dual solutions, which enable efficient separation routines.

•

We develop effective problem-specific valid inequalities that provide non-trivial upper bounds for the over-estimator of the conditional expected revenue. These are crucial to an efficient implementation of the method.

•

We provide cut-sharing mechanisms. These allow us to reuse the cuts developed for the problem with RPWoPA when solving the problem with RPWPA. These cuts significantly improve the performance of the method when solving the problem with RPWPA.

We start by observing that the feasible region of problem (
3
) can be seen as the union of disjoint subregions. All solutions in a given subregion determine the same distribution
P_{x}
. Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of feasible
(x,s)
solution, that is

\mathcal{C}=\left\{(x,s)\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{Z}|\times|\mathcal{Z}|\times|\mathcal{L}|}\times\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times|\mathcal{I}|}:\eqref{constr: price_set}-\eqref{constr: veh_relocation}\right\}

Let then
\mathcal{X}=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{Z}|\times|\mathcal{Z}|\times|\mathcal{L}|}:\eqref{constr: price_set}\}
identify the set of all feasible pricing solutions.
Observe that
\mathcal{X}
is finite and countable. Let
\mathcal{D}:=\{1,2,\cdots,|\mathcal{X}|\}
index the solution in
\mathcal{X}
, that is
x_{d}
,
d=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{X}|
. For each
d\in\mathcal{D}
, we obtain a subset
\mathcal{C}_{d}=\left\{(x_{d},s)\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\subset\mathcal{C}
.
In words, the subset
\mathcal{C}_{d}
contains all
(x,s)
solutions having component
x=x_{d}
. Observe that

•

\mathcal{C}_{d_{1}}\cap\mathcal{C}_{d_{2}}=\emptyset,\forall d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathcal{D},d_{1}\neq d_{2}
.

•

\cup_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{C}_{d}=\mathcal{C}
.

Observe further that the distribution of demand
\boldsymbol{\xi}
depends only on
x
, see
Section

2

\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert
distributions, one for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Let such distributions be denoted as
P_{d}
.
Denote also
\text{1}_{d}:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
the characteristic function of
\mathcal{C}_{d}
, that is
\text{1}_{d}(x,s)=1
if

,

otherwise.
Then we can reformulate problem (
3
) as

\displaystyle\max_{(x,s)\in\mathcal{C}}\bigg\{-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{vi}s_{vi}+\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\text{1}_{d}(x,s)\mathbb{E}_{P_{d}}\big[Q(x,s,\xi)\big]\bigg\}

(5)

Let
Q(x,s)=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\text{1}_{d}(x,s)\mathbb{E}_{P_{d}}\big[Q(x,s,\xi)\big]
.
Observe that each
P_{d}
, for
d\in\mathcal{D}
, is a distribution on
\mathbb{Z}^{|\mathcal{A}|}_{\geq 0}
and is thus naturally discrete. Therefore, we can let
\{\xi^{nd}\}_{n=1}^{N_{d}}
be the support of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
under distribution

and let
\pi^{nd}

. Then

Q(x,s)=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\text{1}_{d}(x,s)\sum_{n=1}^{N_{d}}\pi^{nd}Q(x,s,\xi^{nd})

(6)

In practical applications, the support of
P_{d}
,

may still be very large. In that case, as in our experiments in
Section

4
, one can use approximations which rely on a smaller set of realizations.

Let
\phi
be a non-negative variable. We can then reformulate problem (
5
) as follows.

\displaystyle\max_{(x,s)\in\mathcal{C},\phi\geq 0}\left\{-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{vi}s_{vi}+\phi|s.t.~\phi\leq Q(x,s)\right\}

(MP)

The method relaxes constraints
\phi\leq Q(x,s)
and re-builds them iteratively.
That is, at each iteration
k=0,1,\ldots
the method solves problem (
RMP
)

\displaystyle\max_{(x,s)\in\mathcal{C},\phi\geq 0}\big\{-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{vi}s_{vi}+\phi|s.t.~\phi\leq o(x,s),o\in\mathcal{O}^{k}\big\}

(RMP)

where
\mathcal{O}^{k}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{O}
is a set of distribution-specific optimality cuts

.
The solution procedure is described in
Algorithm

1
.
The main difference with the classical L-Shaped method is that, prior to generating cuts, the algorithm identifies the probability distribution enforced by

(see,
5
). The value of

can then be computed as

since only

takes value one in (
6
).
Hence, the algorithm generates distribution-specific cuts that are effective only under the distribution they were generated for. If no such cut is identified, the algorithm terminates.

The form of the optimality cuts depends on the characteristics of

and, therefore, on whether we use the RPWoPA or the RPWPA. In
Section

3.1
we derive optimality cuts for the RPWoPA and show how to separate them efficiently. In particular, we show that, even if the problem is a MILP, we can generate exact optimality cuts using linear programming duality. In
Section

3.2
we derive optimality cuts for the RPWPA. These cuts can be adopted every time
Q(x,s,\xi)
is an integer programming problem. For both the RPWoPA and RPWPA there exists a finite set

of optimality cuts. These finitely many optimality cuts ensure that the algorithm converges finitely. Finally, in
Section

we introduce a number of valid inequalities which significantly aid convergence. In particular, we show that the cuts developed for the RPWoPA can be used as valid inequalities when solving the problem with RPWPA.

3.3
Valid inequalities

We devise a set of valid inequalities that provide non-trivial upper bounds on
\phi
within each and across different subsets
\mathcal{C}_{d}
.

The first set of valid inequalities is distribution-specific. It is constructed by assuming that the operator has unlimited supply and is able to fulfill all materialized demand. The valid inequality is as follows

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N_{d}}\pi^{nd}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l}P_{ijl}\right)\xi^{nd}_{ij}+U(2|\mathcal{Z}|-\sum_{z_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}}\sum_{z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}}x_{z_{1},z_{2},l(z_{1},z_{2},d)}),

\displaystyle\forall d\in\mathcal{D}

(VI-1)

The first term computes the expected revenue under distribution
d
assuming demand is fully satisfied at the price determined by
x
decisions.
The last term makes the cut redundant for distributions other than
d
. Valid inequality (
VI-1
) applies to both RPWoPA and RPWPA formulations.

The second set of valid inequalities is also distribution-specific. It is constructed by assuming that the operator receives unlimited demand and is able to entirely allocate supply. For the RPWoPA the valid inequality is as follows

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N_{d}}\pi^{nd}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\max_{j:(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}\{P_{i,j,l(\zeta(i),\zeta(j),d)}\}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi}+U(2|\mathcal{Z}|-\sum_{z_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}}\sum_{z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}}x_{z_{1},z_{2},l(z_{1},z_{2},d)}),

\displaystyle\forall d\in\mathcal{D}

(VI-2-RPWoPA)

Here, the first term on the right-hand side computes the expected revenue obtained under distribution
d
assuming all vehicles at a given station
i
are assigned to the destination for which the largest price is asked for. For the RPWPA the valid inequality is as follows

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N_{d}}\pi^{nd}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}P_{i,j,l(\zeta(i),\zeta(j),d)}(\theta_{ij}^{nd}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi}+1)+U(2|\mathcal{Z}|-\sum_{z_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}}\sum_{z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}}x_{z_{1},z_{2},l(z_{1},z_{2},d)}),

\displaystyle\forall d\in\mathcal{D}

(VI-2-RPWPA)

In this case, the first term on the right-hand side computes the expected revenue obtained under distribution
d
assuming all vehicles are rented out.

The third set of valid inequalities is distribution-independent and applies to both the RPWoPA and RPWPA. It is constructed by exploiting concavity of the recourse problem (or its LP relaxation in the RPWPA case).
Assume, for now, we use the RPWoPA formulation. Observe
Q(x,s,\xi)
is concave in
\xi
(see e.g.,
2
).
Given a pair
(x,s)
, using Jensen’s inequality we can state that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P_{x}}\left[Q(x,s,\boldsymbol{\xi})\right]

\displaystyle\leq Q(x,s,\mathbb{E}_{P_{x}}[\boldsymbol{\xi}])

\displaystyle\leq\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}Q(x,s,\mathbb{E}_{P_{d}}[\boldsymbol{\xi}])

\displaystyle\leq\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}P_{ijl}x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l}\mathbb{E}_{P_{d}}[\xi_{ij}]

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}P_{ijl}x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l}\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{d}}[\xi_{ij}]

where in (

, as in (
VI-1
).
Therefore, in the RPWoPA case we can use valid inequality

\displaystyle\phi\leq\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}P_{ijl}x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l}\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{d}}[\xi_{ij}]

(VI-3)

When using the RPWPA formulation,

forms an ILP and concavity does not necessarily hold. However, concavity holds for the linear programming relaxation of the problem.
This entails that we can replace

in the derivation above. Hence, (

In addition, for the RPWPA problem, we can support cuts (

In particular, at a certain iteration

\displaystyle\begin{split}\phi\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{d_{t}}}\pi^{nd_{t}}\bigg\{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\bigg[\rho_{ijl}^{A,tn}\xi_{ij}^{nd_{t}}x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l}+\rho_{i}^{B,tn}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi}+\rho^{C,tn}_{ijl}(\theta_{ij}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi}+1)\bigg]\bigg\}\\

Numerical tests on the solution algorithm

In this section, we report on extensive experiments on the proposed solution algorithm. We run such experiments on artificially-generated instances of size comparable to real-world instances of the problem. The goal of this section is to provide empirical evidence on the performance of the algorithm.

We run experiments on both versions of the problem, i.e., with and without proportional allocation decisions, hence forming the RPWPA and RPWoPA version of the second-stage problem.
In particular, we compare our method (i.e.,

) against the commercial solver Gurobi 11.0.1 (henceforth

5.3
Value of incorporating decision-dependent uncertainty

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed model incorporating decision-dependent uncertainty, we compare its solutions to those obtained when decision-dependent uncertainty is suppressed.

In particular, we compare the proposed model to two benchmarks. The first benchmark preserves demand elasticity but suppresses the uncertainty. That is, the operator acknowledges that demand reacts to price changes. However, they assume that the demand response to prices is fully known, i.e., deterministic.
The second benchmark preserves demand uncertainty, but suppresses the dependence of this to prices. That is, the operator accounts for the stochasticity of demand. However, it assumes that the demand distribution is fully known and independent of prices. This entails solving an ordinary stochastic program with decision-independent distribution.

The models corresponding to the two benchmarks are constructed as follows.

1.

Deterministic elastic demand benchmark

This benchmark is constructed by assuming deterministic price elasticity of demand. This is a common assumption in the carsharing pricing literature, see, e.g.,
9
,
28
,
32
. In particular, we assume that demand is a deterministic function
\bar{\xi}(x):\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{L}}\to\mathbb{N}^{|\mathcal{A}|\times|\mathcal{L}|}
defined as

\bar{\xi}_{ijl}(x)=\left\lceil\sum_{k|\omega_{k}=1,i(k)=i,j(k)=j}P(\omega_{k}=1|x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l}=1)-0.5\right\rceil,~~\forall(i,j)\in\mathcal{A},\forall l\in\mathcal{L}

That is, for a given price level
l
, the expected demand associated with each arc
(i,j)
is obtained by rounding the expected demand to the nearest integer.
Pricing decisions are then made using the following deterministic MILP

\displaystyle\max\left\{-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{vi}s_{vi}+Q(x,s,\bar{\xi}(x))|s.t.~\eqref{constr: price_set}-\eqref{constr: veh_relocation},{(x,s)\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{Z}|\times|\mathcal{Z}|\times|\mathcal{L}|}\times\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times|\mathcal{I}|}}\right\}

(ELD-model)

Note that the relationship between
x
and the probability distribution is hereby suppressed as
\bar{\xi}(x)
is a singleton for all
x
.

2.

Ordinary stochastic program benchmark

The second benchmark is set up by simply assuming exogenous (i.e., decision-independent) uncertain demand. In this case, the random demand follows a predefined distribution. In particular, the exogenous distribution is chosen as the distribution
\bar{d}\in\mathcal{D}
enforced by the average price level.
The corresponding ordinary stochastic program can be formulated as

\displaystyle\max\left\{-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{vi}s_{vi}+\mathbb{E}_{P_{\bar{d}}}[Q(x,s,\xi)]|s.t.~\eqref{constr: price_set}-\eqref{constr: veh_relocation},(x,s)\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{Z}|\times|\mathcal{Z}|\times|\mathcal{L}|}\times\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times|\mathcal{I}|}\right\}

(OSP-model)

Let
(x^{E},s^{E})
be an optimal solution to (
ELD-model
). To evaluate the performance of
(x^{E},s^{E})
, we need to evaluate it in the actual context where demand is uncertain and decision-dependent. To do so, we first identify the demand distribution enforced by solution
x^{E}
, denoted by
d^{E}
. The performance of the solution
(x^{E},s^{E})
can be measured as the expected profit with respect to distribution
P_{d^{E}}
. We denote this expected profit as the
Expected Return of the Elastic Demand Solution
(
EELD
), which is computed as

EELD=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{vi}{s}^{E}_{vi}+\mathbb{E}_{P_{d^{E}}}[Q(x^{E},s^{E},\xi)]

This value can now be compared to the expected profit delivered by problem (
B.1
), say
EDDU
, which accounts for decision-dependent uncertainty when making pricing and relocation decisions.
We denote the profit increase

\Delta_{EELD}=100\%\times\frac{EDDU-EELD}{EELD}

In a similar way, let
(x^{SP},s^{SP})
be an optimal solution to (
OSP-model
) and let
P_{d^{SP}}
be the distribution enforced by
x^{SP}
. The expected profit of solution
(x^{SP},s^{SP})
is denoted the
Expected Return of the Stochastic Programming Solution
(
EOSP
), and is computed as

EOSP=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{vi}{s}^{SP}_{vi}+\mathbb{E}_{P_{d^{SP}}}[Q({x}^{SP},{s}^{SP},\xi)]

The profit increase obtained by accounting for decision-dependent uncertainty compared to
EOSP
is obtained as

\Delta_{EOSP}=100\%\times\frac{EDDU-EOSP}{EOSP}

In
Table

we report
EDDU
,
EELS
and
EOSP
for the instances solved. The results demonstrate that accounting for decision-dependent demand uncertainty generates a significant increase in expected profits. In particular, accounting for decision-dependent stochastic demand increases profits by
8.39\%
, on average, compared to using decision-dependent deterministic demand (i.e., deterministic price-elastic demand) and by
8.53\%
, on average, compared to using decision-independent stochastic demand. The improvement is more pronounced when customers are more sensitive to prices. These results substantiate the effectiveness of accounting for decision-dependent demand uncertainty.

Concluding remarks

In this paper we studied the problem of deciding carsharing pricing and relocations. We advanced the available literature by proposing mathematical programming formulations that account for decision-dependent demand uncertainty. This allows decision makers to account for the impact of pricing decisions on the distribution of random demand. The problem is formulated as a two-stage stochastic program with decision-dependent uncertainty. The formulation is general and can be adapted to various specifications of the carsharing service. We illustrate two such specifications, one purely profit-driven, the second aiming to distribute vehicles proportionally to materialized demand at different arcs.

To solve the problem, we designed a tailored L-shaped method which incorporates distribution-specific cuts and devised a number of problem-specific improvements. Particularly, we show that for the two configurations of the system considered, we can solve subproblems in closed form, gaining significant efficiency.

We performed numerical experiments on synthetic instances of size comparable to real-world cases. The computational results illustrate that the tailored L-shaped method outperforms by far an off-the-shelf solver employed to solve a monolithic linearized version of the model. Particularly, our method solved the majority of the instances to optimality within one hour, while the solver failed to deliver feasible solutions for the majority of the instances.

Furthermore, the practical applicability of the proposed model is validated through a case study inspired by a carsharing system operated in Copenhagen, Denmark. Our results illustrate that accounting for decision-dependent demand distributions generates significant increases in expected profits, compared to suppressing decision-dependent uncertainty.
Furthermore, we show that additional control over vehicle allocation policies may improve service rates with only marginal effects on profits.

The results of our study open avenues for future research.
In our problem we assume that the endogenous demand for carsharing depends only on price. In practice, demand may also be influenced by other factors, such as supply (in this case, the availability of shared vehicles).
This extension would likely require only minor adjustments to the model provided and to the solution algorithm, in particular to the optimality cuts. However, this would require accurate models of the relationship between supply and demand. More in general, we expect that further insights can be gained by incorporating more advanced models of human preferences with respect to transportation services.
Furthermore, it is likely that decisions made for a given interval of time have an impact on decisions and demand realizations beyond the target interval of time. A multistage model would serve better to capture the sequential nature of the decision process applied in reality. However, this would likewise pose methodological challenges as it would require the solution to multistage stochastic programs with decision-dependent uncertainty.

Finally, while this work was performed with a carsharing application in mind, we believe a similar modeling and solution strategy can be adopted to modeling and solving other transportation pricing and (re)location problems where the distribution of demand (or other random parameters) is influenced by decisions. Hence, we believe this work can help the transportation science community expand the richness of decision-making models and the range of tractable transportation problems solved.

Appendix A
A proportional vehicle allocation strategy

In this section we introduce a variant of the recourse problem which models a policy that allocates vehicles to customers requests in a proportional manner, see, e.g.,
26
.
The allocation problem can be formulated as a MILP by extending model (
4
).

A.1
Problem formulation

Let
\theta_{ij}
denote the fraction of the demand of rentals from station
i
directed to station
j
, that is

\theta_{ij}=\frac{\xi_{ij}}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}:k\not=i}\xi_{ik}}

In order to allocate vehicles proportionally to the demand, the recourse problem (
4
) requires the addition of the following constraints

\displaystyle r_{ijl}\leq\theta_{ij}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi}+1,\forall j\in\mathcal{J}_{i},\forall l\in\mathcal{L}

(A.1)

The resulting recourse problem is therefore

Q(x,s,\xi)=\max_{r_{ijl}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\left\{\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}P_{ijl}r_{ijl}|\eqref{constr_tp1},\eqref{constr_tp2},\eqref{constr: prop_assign}\right\}

(A.2)

Constraints (
A.1
) ensure that supply on arc
(i,j)
is allocated proportionally to the demand. Observe that, since
r_{ij}
is integer, the right-hand side
\theta_{ij}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi}+1
ensures that demand can be satisfied up to
\lceil\theta_{ij}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi}\rceil
when the quota
\theta_{ij}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi}
is fractional. The right-hand side of constraints (
A.1
) leaves the redundancy of allocating one more vehicle to destination
j
when the proportional quota associated with arc
(i,j)
is already an integer. This redundancy prevents the insufficient allocation of all available vehicles. Nevertheless, the number of allocated vehicles is still bounded by materialized demand as stated in constraints (
4b
). The resulting recourse problem is a MILP problem.

A.2
Closed-form solutions

We observe that problem (
A.2
) is separable by
i\in\mathcal{I}
. That is, for each origin station
i\in\mathcal{I}
, we define
Q^{i}(x,s,\xi)
as

\displaystyle Q^{i}(x,s,\xi)=\max

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}P_{ijl}r_{ijl}

(A.3a)

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle r_{ijl}\leq\xi_{ij}x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l},

\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathcal{J}_{i},\forall l\in\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle~~~(\rho^{A}_{ijl})

(A.3b)

\displaystyle\sum_{(j,l)\in\mathcal{J}_{i}\times\mathcal{L}}r_{ijl}\leq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi},

\displaystyle~~~(\rho^{B}_{i})

(A.3c)

\displaystyle r_{ijl}\leq\theta_{ij}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{vi}+1,

\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathcal{J}_{i},\forall l\in\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle~~~(\rho^{C}_{ijl})

(A.3d)

\displaystyle r_{ijl}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathcal{J}_{i},\forall l\in\mathcal{L}

(A.3e)

where
\rho^{A}_{ijl}
,
\rho^{B}_{i}
and
\rho^{C}_{ijl}
are dual variables associated with the corresponding primal constraints.
Problem (
A.3
) is a MILP. We now show that numerical solutions of
Q(x^{k},s^{k},\xi^{nd_{k}})
are available in closed form.

Proposition

A.1
provides closed form solution to problem
Q^{i}(x^{k},s^{k},\xi^{nd_{k}})
for the case when supply is larger than or equal to demand. Likewise,
Proposition

A.3
addresses the case when supply is not sufficient to cover the entire demand.
In the former case, the solution is found simply by allocating the entire supply. In the latter case demand is fulfilled in non-increasing order of the revenue.

Proposition A.1

Let
(x,s)
be a feasible solution to (
RMP
) and
\xi
a realization of demand.
Given an index
\hat{i}\in\mathcal{I}
, assume that

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}s_{v\hat{i}}\geq\sum_{(\hat{i},j)\in\mathcal{A}}\xi_{\hat{i}j}

Then the optimal solution to
Q^{\hat{i}}(x,s,\xi)
, denoted
\hat{r}:=(\hat{r}_{jl})_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\hat{i}},l\in\mathcal{L}}
, is

\hat{r}_{jl}=\xi_{\hat{i}j}x_{\zeta(i),\zeta(j),l}\qquad\forall j\in\mathcal{J}_{\hat{i}},\forall l\in\mathcal{L}

Proof A.2

Proof of

Appendix C
A flow-based MILP formulation for zone division

We report the MILP model for the partition of the carsharing stations into zones. The model was proposed in
24
. Let
\hat{\mathcal{G}}=(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{E})
represent the one-way carsharing system network, where
\mathcal{I}
represents set of nodes composed by stations, and
\mathcal{E}
denotes the edge set containing bi-directional arcs
(i,j)
and
(j,i)
for each pair of nodes. We further let
\mathcal{I}^{-}(i):\{(j,i)\in\mathcal{E}\}
and
\mathcal{I}^{+}(i):\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}\}
.
Let
\theta_{i}
denote the demand density of station
i\in\mathcal{I}
(i.e., the percentage of customers available in the unit containing station
i
). Let
\overline{\theta}
denote the target demand density within each zone, which is calculated by
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\theta_{i}/|\mathcal{Z}|
. Let
d_{ij}
denote the distance between station
i
and
j
.
We define the imbalance level of the demand distribution, denoted
\beta
, as the largest deviation of the demand density from the target density
\bar{\theta}
.
The
\beta
value is set to
10\%
in our experiments.
We introduce binary variable
y_{ij}
, which takes value
1
if station
i\in\mathcal{I}
is assigned to the zone (centered at) station
j\in\mathcal{I}
,
0
otherwise. A set of flow variable
f_{ik}^{j}
is introduced to indicate the amount of flow originating at zone
j
that is sent across link
(i,k)
. The flow-based MILP formulation for zone division is formulated as below.

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}d_{ij}^{2}y_{ij}

(C.1a)

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}y_{ij}=1

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathcal{I}

(C.1b)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}y_{jj}=|\mathcal{Z}|

(C.1c)

\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathcal{I}

(C.1d)

\displaystyle y_{ij}\leq y_{jj}

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathcal{I},\forall j\in\mathcal{I}

(C.1e)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{-}(i)}f^{j}_{ki}-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{+}(i)}f^{j}_{ik}=y_{ij},

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\{j\},\forall j\in\mathcal{I}

(C.1f)

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\{j\},\forall j\in\mathcal{I}

(C.1g)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{-}(j)}f^{j}_{kj}=0,

\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathcal{I}

(C.1h)

\displaystyle f^{j}_{ik}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\forall(i,k)\in\mathcal{E},\forall j\in\mathcal{I}

(C.1i)

\displaystyle y_{ij}\in\{0,1\},

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathcal{I},\forall j\in\mathcal{I}

(C.1j)
