Beyond the Independence Assumption: Finite-Sample Guarantees for Deep Q-Learning under τ \tau -Mixing
Leon Halgryn, Sophie Langer, Janusz M. Meylahn, E. Moritz Hahn

Abstract
Finite-sample analyses of deep Q-learning typically treat replayed data as independent, even though it is sampled from temporally dependent state-action trajectories. We study the Deep Q-networks (DQN) algorithm under explicit dependence by modelling the minibatches used for updating the network as τ \tau -mixing. We show that this assumption holds under certain dependence conditions on the underlying trajectories and the mechanism used to sample minibatches. Building on this observation, we extend statistical analyses of DQN with fully connected ReLU architectures to dependent data. We formulate each update as a nonparametric regression problem with τ \tau -mixing observations and derive finite-sample risk bounds under this dependence structure. Our results show that temporal dependence leads to a degradation in the statistical rate by inducing an additional dimensionality penalty in the rate exponent, reflecting the reduced effective sample size of τ \tau -mixing data. Moreover, we derive the sample complexity of DQN under τ \tau -mixing from these risk bounds. Finally, we empirically demonstrate on standard Gymnasium environments that the independence assumption is systematically violated and that replay sampling yields approximately exponentially decaying correlations, supporting our theoretical framework.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) addresses sequential decision-making problems in which data are generated through interaction with an environment. In value-based methods such as deep Q-learning, this amounts to learning the action-value (Q-)function from data using function approximation. Modern approaches rely on deep neural networks, enabling applications in high-dimensional settings. This paradigm has achieved remarkable empirical success, from Atari games with Deep Q-Networks (DQN)
(
27
)
to large-scale game-playing systems such as AlphaZero
(
33
)
, AlphaStar
(
39
)
, and MuZero
(
32
)
.

From the theoretical perspective, RL is well understood in the tabular setting, where sharp finite-sample guarantees are available (
(
2
;
15
;
37
;
13
)
), and extends to structured function approximation such as linear models (
(
16
)
). However, analyzing nonlinear function approximation, especially deep neural networks, remains challenging. A common approach in recent analyses of deep Q-learning (e.g.,
10
) is to view each update as a supervised learning problem, where the Q-function is estimated from sample transitions. This reduction enables the use of classical empirical process techniques, but typically relies on the assumption that the training data are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.).

In practice, however, data are generated sequentially through agent-environment interaction and stored in a replay buffer, inducing temporal dependence. As a result, the i.i.d. assumption abstracts away the sequential nature of data collection in RL and creates a mismatch between theoretical guarantees and practical implications. This raises the question:

Do the same theoretical guarantees hold under temporal dependence?

To address this, we model the state-action trajectory in the replay buffer as weakly dependent (either
\beta
- or
\tau
-mixing) and show that, under appropriate sampling schemes, the induced minibatches are
\tau
-mixing.
We establish statistical convergence rates for fitted Q-iteration with deep neural networks under temporally dependent data, showing that
\tau
-mixing dependence degrades the rate through a reduced effective sample size. Building on
10
, we extend their supervised learning framework to this dependent setting and quantify the resulting loss in statistical efficiency.
We focus on fully connected feedforward neural networks, which include sparse architectures as a special case. We consider Bellman operators for which, for the relevant class of Q-functions, the backup
\mathcal{T}Q
admits a compositional or low-intrinsic-dimensional representation, or a combination of both. The low-dimensional structure may occur either at the input level, through dependence on a small subset of state-action coordinates, or internally, through low-dimensional intermediate representations in the composition. This is motivated by the structure of many RL problems: rewards often depend on a small number of task-relevant variables, transition dynamics frequently factor into local or modular mechanisms, and the reachable state-action space may concentrate near a low-dimensional latent manifold. Since the Bellman backup combines the reward, transition kernel, and continuation value, such environmental structure can be inherited by
\mathcal{T}Q
.
Empirically, we observe across several standard Gymnasium environments
(
36
)
that RL trajectories and replay buffer data violate the independence assumption, while sampled minibatches exhibit approximately exponential decay of correlations, supporting our theoretical framework.
To summarize, our contributions are as follows:

•

We extend finite-sample guarantees for DQN to dependent data and fully connected ReLU networks under structured Bellman operators that are compositional, low-dimensional, or both. We show that temporal dependence degrades statistical rates via an additional dimensionality penalty in the rate exponent, and derive the corresponding sample complexity under
\tau
-mixing.

•

We show that if the underlying state-action process is exponentially
\beta
- or
\tau
-mixing, then the sampled minibatches are exponentially
\tau
-mixing provided an order-preserving, duplicate-free sampler is used.
Moreover, we show why the standard DQN sampling scheme (uniform with replacement) is problematic and introduce a contiguous block sampler that provides some control over the rate of decay of correlations in the minibatches.

•

We design an estimator for analyzing weak dependence and use this in experiments to (i) show that the i.i.d. assumption is violated for standard Gymnasium environments
(
36
)
, and (ii) empirically assess our assumption on the minibatch dependence.

Theoretical framework

2.1
Markov decision processes, Bellman updates, and DQN

We consider a discounted Markov decision process (MDP)
\mathfrak{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma\rangle,

where
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are the state and action spaces,
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the transition kernel,
R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathbb{R})
is the reward distribution, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor. At each time step
t
, the agent observes a reward
R_{t}\sim R(\cdot\mid S_{t},A_{t})
and transitions to a next state
S_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid S_{t},A_{t})
. We assume rewards are uniformly bounded, i.e.,
|R_{t}|\leq R_{\max}
almost surely for some
R_{\max}>0
.

A policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
assigns to each state a distribution over actions and induces the action-value function

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}\,\Big|\,S_{0}=s,A_{0}=a,A_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S_{t})\Big]=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\big[V^{\pi}(S^{\prime})\big],

where

\displaystyle r(s,a):=\mathbb{E}_{R\sim R(\cdot\mid s,a)}[R],\qquad\text{and}\qquad V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}\big[Q^{\pi}(s,A)\big].

Since rewards are bounded by
R_{\mathrm{max}}
, the
Q
-functions satisfy
|Q^{\pi}(s,a)|\leq\nicefrac{{R_{\mathrm{max}}}}{{(1-\gamma)}}=:Q_{\mathrm{max}}
, for any policy
\pi
.

Our goal is to approximate the optimal
Q
-function
Q^{*}
, which is the unique fixed point of the Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}
defined as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}Q)(s,a):=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\Big[\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q(S^{\prime},a^{\prime})\Big],

(2.1)

i.e.,
Q^{*}=\mathcal{T}Q^{*}
.
Computing
\mathcal{T}Q
exactly is infeasible in large or continuous action spaces, or when the underlying transition dynamics are unknown as in model-free RL. This motivates the use of parametric function classes to approximate action-value functions. In this work, we approximate
Q^{*}
using fully connected feedforward neural networks defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
denote the number of hidden layers and let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},\dots,d_{M+1})
be the layer widths, where
d_{0}
and
d_{M+1}
are the input and output dimensions, respectively. A fully connected neural network
f:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{M+1}}
is defined as

\displaystyle f(x)=W_{M+1}\,\sigma\Big(W_{M}\,\sigma\big(\cdots\sigma(W_{1}x+b_{1})\cdots+b_{M}\big)\Big),

(2.2)

where
W_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}\times d_{\ell-1}}
and
b_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}}
are the weights and biases of layer
\ell
, and
\sigma(u)=\max\{u,0\}
is the ReLU activation applied componentwise. We denote the maximal width of the network by
d_{\max}=\max_{\ell\in[M]}d_{\ell}
.
For
B>0
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(M,d_{\max},B):=\Big\{f\text{ of the form \eqref{network}}:\max_{\ell\in[L+1]}\|W_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\leq B,\;\max_{\ell\in[L]}\|b_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\leq B\Big\}.

(2.3)

We now describe how such function approximators are trained in the DQN algorithm summarized in Algorithm
1
. At environment time
t
, the agent observes a transition
(S_{t},A_{t},R_{t},S_{t}^{\prime})
, which is stored in a replay buffer
\mathcal{M}=\{(S_{m},A_{m},R_{m},S_{m}^{\prime}):m=1,\dots,|\mathcal{M}|\}.

Here,
S_{m}^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid S_{m},A_{m})
denotes the next state and
R_{m}\sim R(\cdot\mid S_{m},A_{m})
the corresponding reward.

At training time, DQN samples a minibatch
\{(S_{i},A_{i},R_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}
of size
n
from the replay buffer to update the network parameters
\theta
. The regression targets are defined as

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle:=R_{i}+\gamma\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{\theta^{*}}(S_{i}^{\prime},a).

(2.4)

Here
Q_{\theta^{*}}
denotes a target network whose parameters are periodically synchronized with those of the main network
Q_{\theta}
. The target network mitigates instability due to the moving-target nature of TD learning (see, e.g.,
10
). The main network is updated by minimizing the squared loss

\displaystyle L(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[Y_{i}-Q_{\theta}(S_{i},A_{i})\Big]^{2}.\qquad

(2.5)

To analyze this update, one can view it as a supervised learning problem. Let
X_{i}:=(S_{i},A_{i})
and fix a function
Q:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, which above corresponds to the target network
Q_{\theta^{*}}
. With the regression targets in (
2.4
)
we obtain, for
X_{i}=x
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x]=(\mathcal{T}Q)(x),

so that the DQN update induces the regression model

\displaystyle Y_{i}=(\mathcal{T}Q)(X_{i})+\xi_{i},

(2.6)

where
\xi_{i}:=Y_{i}-(\mathcal{T}Q)(X_{i})
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}\mid X_{i}]=0
. Since
Q
-values are bounded by
Q_{\max}
, the targets satisfy
|Y_{i}|\leq Q_{\max}
. Consequently,
|\xi_{i}|\leq 2Q_{\max},

and in particular
\xi_{i}
is sub-Gaussian with parameter proportional to
Q_{\max}
(e.g., by Hoeffding’s lemma
(
25
, Lemma 2.6)
).

This perspective underlies existing statistical analyses of deep Q-learning such as in
10
, which study the generalization error of such regression problems. A common assumption in these analyses is that the samples
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d., enabling the use of classical empirical process techniques. In practice, however, the data are generated sequentially through agent-environment interaction and stored in a replay buffer, inducing temporal dependence. This raises the central question of this work: how does such dependence affect the statistical guarantees of deep Q-learning?

2.2
Weak dependence

We model the replay-buffer data as a strictly stationary weakly dependent
state-action trajectory
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
generated under a fixed
behavioral policy. This relaxes the i.i.d. sampling assumption used in prior
work such as
10
, while accounting for temporal dependence due to
sequential data collection. Let

\mathcal{A}_{t}:=\sigma(Z_{s}:s\leq t)
. We say that
(Z_{t})
is exponentially

\beta
-mixing if, for some
C_{\beta},c_{\beta}>0
,

\displaystyle\beta_{Z}(k)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{A\in\sigma(Z_{s}:s\geq t+k)}\big|\mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{A}_{t})-\mathbb{P}(A)\big|\right]\leq C_{\beta}e^{-c_{\beta}k},\qquad k\geq 1.

(2.7)

We also use the
\tau
-mixing coefficient of
8
.
For a
\mathcal{Z}
-valued random variable
V
on a metric space

(\mathcal{Z},d_{\mathcal{Z}})
and a sub-
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{A}
, define

\displaystyle\tau(\mathcal{A},V):=\Bigg\|\sup_{h\in\Lambda_{1}(\mathcal{Z})}\Big|\int h\,d\mu_{V\mid\mathcal{A}}-\int h\,d\mu_{V}\Big|\Bigg\|_{1},

(2.8)

where
\Lambda_{1}(\mathcal{Z})
is the class of real-valued
1
-Lipschitz
functions,
\mu_{V\mid\mathcal{A}}
is a conditional law of
V
given

\mathcal{A}
, and
\mu_{V}
is its marginal law. This is equivalent to the
coupling formulation of
26
.
For a stationary process, set

\displaystyle\tau_{Z}(k):=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\tau(\mathcal{A}_{t},Z_{t+k}),\qquad k\geq 1.

(2.9)

The process is exponentially
\tau
-mixing if

\tau_{Z}(k)\leq C_{\tau}e^{-c_{\tau}k}
for some
C_{\tau},c_{\tau}>0
. The

\tau
-mixing condition is weaker than several classical mixing notions; in particular
\psi
-mixing
\Rightarrow

\phi
-mixing
\Rightarrow

\beta
-mixing
\Rightarrow

\alpha
-mixing
\Rightarrow

\tau
-mixing
(
3
and

23
). Moreover, on bounded
metric spaces, exponential
\beta
-mixing implies exponential

\tau
-mixing with the same exponential rate and a modified prefactor (see
Lemma
C.4
).
For a finite sequence
Y_{1:n}
, define

\mathcal{F}_{r}^{Y}:=\sigma(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r}),\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{Y}^{n}(\ell):=\sup_{1\leq r\leq n-\ell}\tau(\mathcal{F}_{r}^{Y},Y_{r+\ell}),\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq\ell\leq n-1.

Proposition 2.2

(Replay sampling preserves exponential weak dependence)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be strictly stationary with values in

(\mathcal{Z},d_{\mathcal{Z}})
, and denote the replay buffer by

\mathcal{M}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{M}),\quad\text{with}\quad Z_{m}:=X_{t_{0}+m-1}.

Let
Y_{1:n}
be the minibatch obtained from
\mathcal{M}
by any order-preserving,
duplicate-free sampler, independent of
(X_{t})
.

Then for every
1\leq\ell\leq n-1
,
\tau_{Y}^{n}(\ell)\leq\tau_{X}(\ell)
.
Consequently:

(i)

If
(X_{t})
is exponentially
\tau
-mixing,

\tau_{X}(k)\leq C_{\tau}e^{-c_{\tau}k}\implies\tau_{Y}^{n}(\ell)\leq C_{\tau}e^{-c_{\tau}\ell}.

(ii)

If
(\mathcal{Z},d_{\mathcal{Z}})
has finite diameter

D_{\mathcal{Z}}<\infty
and
(X_{t})
is exponentially
\beta
-mixing,

\beta_{X}(k)\leq C_{\beta}e^{-c_{\beta}k}\implies\tau_{Y}^{n}(\ell)\leq C_{\beta\tau}D_{\mathcal{Z}}C_{\beta}e^{-c_{\beta}\ell}.

Proof.

We defer the proof to Appendix
B
.
∎

Proposition
2.2
shows that replay sampling preserves the temporal weak-dependence structure of the underlying trajectory: because order-preserving, duplicate-free sampling cannot decrease temporal separation between samples, minibatch dependencies decay at least as fast as those of the original process. Consequently, exponentially mixing trajectories induce exponentially mixing replay minibatches with the same decay rate.

Corollary 2.3

(Common replay samplers)
.

The conclusions of Proposition
2.2

hold for:
(i) uniform sampling without replacement from the replay buffer, provided sampled indices are sorted after sampling, and
(ii) contiguous block sampling.

Proof.

We show in Appendix
B
that both sampler fit into the framework of Proposition
2.2
. The claim then follows immediately.

3
Theoretical results

In the following, we obtain an upper bound for
\|Q^{\pi_{K}}-Q^{*}\|_{1,\mu}
, where
Q^{\pi_{K}}
is the action-value function corresponding to policy
\pi_{K}
and

is the reference distribution. To derive non-trivial results, we need to impose further assumptions on the Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}
describing our regression function in (

Definition 3.1

(Hölder smooth functions)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
. A ball of radius
H
of
\beta
-Hölder smooth functions on
\mathcal{D}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{d}(\mathcal{D},\beta,H)\!=\!\Bigl\{f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}\,\Big|

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{\alpha}:\|\mathbf{\alpha}\|_{1}<\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\|\partial^{\mathbf{\alpha}}f\|_{\infty}+\hskip-7.11317pt\sum_{\mathbf{\alpha}:\|\mathbf{\alpha}\|_{1}=\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{D}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|\partial^{\mathbf{\alpha}}f(x)-\partial^{\mathbf{\alpha}}f(y)|}{\|x-y\|_{\infty}^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}\leq H\Bigr\}.

We impose an additional structural assumption on the Bellman operator that extends the hierarchical compositional model considered in

Definition 3.2

(see Definition 4.1 in

.

A function

is called a
compositional model of level

\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with dimension vector
\mathbf{D}=(D_{0},\dots,D_{\ell})
, where
D_{0}=D
and
D_{\ell}=1
and

\mathcal{P}=\{(d_{ij},s_{ij})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}\colon i=0,\dots,\ell,\ j=1,\dots,D_{i}\},

if there exist functions

g_{i}\colon[-C,C]^{D_{i}}\to[-C,C]^{D_{i+1}}
,
i=0,\dots,\ell
, for some

,
such that
g_{0}=\text{id}
(the identity function) and

\displaystyle f=g_{\ell}\circ g_{\ell-1}\circ\dots\circ g_{0},

(3.1)

where
g_{i}
is a vector-valued function with components

g_{i}=(g_{i1},\dots,g_{iD_{i+1}})^{\top}

satisfying the following conditions.

(C)

(
Coordinate sparsity
) There exists a subset

such that

, where

(S)

(
Smoothness
) It holds that

g_{ij}\in\mathcal{C}_{|S_{ij}|}([-C,C]^{|S_{ij}|},s_{ij},C).

(M)

(
Low Minkowski dimension
) The set

.

The corresponding class of compositional models is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\ell,\mathcal{P},\mathbf{D}):=\{f\text{ of the form }\eqref{comp_main}\}.

(3.2)

Our formulation further allows the intermediate representations themselves to be supported on sets of low Minkowski dimension, thereby capturing additional geometric low-dimensional structure.

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}:=\mathcal{F}(M,d_{\max},Q_{\max}),\qquad\text{and }\qquad\mathcal{G}_{0}:=\mathcal{G}(\ell,\mathcal{P},\mathbf{D}).

(3.3)

(see Definition

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{f:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}\;\big|\;f(\cdot,a)\in\mathcal{F}_{0}\text{ for every }a\in\mathcal{A}\bigr\},\text{ and}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{g:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}\;\big|\;g(\cdot,a)\in\mathcal{G}_{0}\text{ for every }a\in\mathcal{A}\bigr\}.

(3.5)

Assumption 3.3
.

We impose the following conditions:

(A1)

Compactness:
the state space is
\mathcal{S}=[0,1]^{d}
and the action space

is finite, so that
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}

(A2)

Exponential mixing:
the sampled minibatch

forms a strictly stationary, exponentially

-mixing process, i.e., there exists constants

\tau_{X}(k)\leq C_{\tau}e^{-c_{1}k},\qquad k\geq 1,

(A3)

the regression noise in (

(A4)

Bellman completeness:
the target function

in (

), and the function class

(A5)

there exists a finite constant
\phi_{\mu,\sigma}<\infty

The compositional Hölder structure in (A4) reflects the hierarchical nature of value functions in high-dimensional problems and is widely used to model functions that can be efficiently approximated by deep neural networks (see

The lower dimension intrinsic dimensionality is further motivated as, in many RL problems, the effective state dynamics and rewards are governed by a small number of underlying degrees of freedom, so that the Bellman target typically varies along a low-dimensional manifold embedded in the high-dimensional state space. The closure condition in (A4) ensures that the Bellman operator preserves this structure and is standard in analyses of deep Q-learning (see
10

remains in
\mathcal{G}
over each DQN update.
The concentrability condition in (A5) is common in the offline and batch RL literature; see e.g,
10
;
1
;
6

4
Experiments

We empirically examine whether the dependence assumptions used in our analysis are reflected in data generated by DQN. We study two sources of dependence: state-action trajectories generated by fixed trained policies, and the minibatches supplied to the DQN regression step during training. Experiments are conducted on
CartPole-v1
,
FrozenLake-v1
, and
LunarLander-v3
; implementation details, hyperparameters, and estimator construction are deferred to Appendix
D
.

For both trajectories and minibatches, we estimate the finite-history Wasserstein proxy
for
\tau
-dependence described in Appendix
D.1.2
. This statistic
compares a local empirical law
of future observations, conditioned on a short observed history, to the corresponding empirical marginal law.
It should therefore be interpreted as a computable finite-sample proxy for temporal dependence, rather than as a direct estimate of the population
\tau
-mixing coefficient.
In the trajectory experiments, the lag
k

is ordinary environment-time lag under a fixed greedy policy. In the minibatch experiments, the lag
k
is the order in which samples are returned by the replay sampler within a single minibatch.

Figure

summarizes the results. The trajectory estimates in the top row show that temporal dependence decays in all three environments, but remains non-negligible at short lags. The decay is relatively fast for

Moreover, the estimated dependence decays approximately exponentially as a function of minibatch order. 5
Conclusion & limitations

This work extends the analysis of
10
to DQN with replay buffer sampling under temporally dependent trajectories. Rather than assuming i.i.d. state-action samples, we model the replay trajectory as a strictly stationary
\beta
-mixing (more generally,
\tau
-mixing) process, thereby capturing the temporal dependence induced by sequential environment interactions. We show that this dependence persists in the replay minibatches and degrades the statistical rate polynomially, effectively doubling the intrinsic dimensionality penalty relative to the i.i.d. setting.

While this model more closely reflects practical DQN training, it still relies on stationarity through the ‘all-policy’ concentrability in Assumption
3.3
(A5). In practice, replay trajectories are generated under evolving behavior policies, inducing distributional drifts in addition to temporal dependence. Extending the analysis to such non-stationary setting would therefore require controlling both dependence and policy-induced distribution shifts. In this direction,
42
show in offline RL that the standard ‘all-policy’ concentrability can be replaced by a weaker ‘single-policy’ notion, suggesting that non-stationarity may be controlled through the local discrepancy induced by individual DQN updates rather than the cumulative drift over the full training trajectory. Extending our analysis to this setting is an interesting direction for future work. Finally, extending this analysis to the multi-agent setting such as the minimax DQN algorithm
(
10
)
is another interesting direction for future work.

,

\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{f}(\tilde{X}_{i})-f_{0}(\tilde{X}_{i}))^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\right].

Using the preceding
4B\delta
bound twice, once for
\tilde{X}_{i}
and
once for
X_{i}
, gives

\displaystyle\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{k^{*}}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})\,\middle|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\right]+8B\delta.

Taking expectations and bounding the random index by the supremum over the
cover yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{j\in[N_{\delta}]}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})\right|\right]+8B\delta.

(B.9)

It remains to control the empirical norm term. By the ERM property, for any

f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\hat{f}(X_{i}))^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-f(X_{i}))^{2}.

Substituting
Y_{i}=f_{0}(X_{i})+\xi_{i}
and expanding gives

\displaystyle\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}\leq\|f-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}(\hat{f}(X_{i})-f(X_{i})).

(B.10)

Taking expectations, then taking the infimum over
f\in\mathcal{F}
in the first term and the supremum in the second, yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}\leq\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\|f-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}(\hat{f}(X_{i})-f(X_{i}))\right]\right|.

(B.11)

Since
X_{i}\sim\mu
for every
i
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|f-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}=\mathbb{\|}f-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}.

(B.12)

Using this with the definition of
\mathfrak{C}_{n}(\mathcal{F},\delta)
yields

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{f}-f_{0}\rVert_{\mu}^{2}\leq\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\|f-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}+\mathfrak{C}_{n}(\mathcal{F},\delta).

(B.13)

Combining (
B.9
) with (
B.11
), (
B.12
), and (
B.13
) proves the first claim. The uniform version
follows by taking the supremum over
f_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
and using the
definition of
\omega(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})
.
∎

B.3.1
Stochastic error bound

To control
\mathfrak{C}_{n}(\mathcal{F},\delta)
and
\mathfrak{D}_{n}(\mathcal{F},\delta)
, we use the following results.

Lemma B.5

(Stochastic covariance bound)
.

Suppose Assumption
B.3
holds. Then there exist
constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{n}(\mathcal{F},\delta)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\kappa\delta+C_{2}\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\left[C_{3}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+\sqrt{\delta}+C_{4}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}}\right].

Proof.

Since
\xi_{i}
are centered random variables and
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}\mid X_{i}]=0
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}\cdot g(X_{i})\mid X_{i}]=g(X_{i})\cdot\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}\mid X_{i}]=0

for any bounded, measurable, and deterministic function
g
. For a fixed
f\in\mathcal{F}
this then implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot f(X_{i})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot f_{0}(X_{i})\right]=0,

(B.14)

since both,
f
and
f_{0}
, are measurable, bounded, and deterministic.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot\left[\hat{f}(X_{i})-f(X_{i})\right]\right\}=\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot\left[\hat{f}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})\right]\right\}.

(B.15)

Note that, since
\xi_{i}
is sub-Gaussian, it is also sub-exponential and its moments are bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\xi_{i}\right|^{m}\right]\leq\left(c\sqrt{m}\right)^{m},

(B.16)

for some constant
c>0
(see Proposition 2.6.1.(ii) of
38
).
Furthermore,
\lVert f\rVert_{\infty},\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{\infty}\leq B\;\forall f\in\mathcal{F}
,
\lVert X_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
, and since
X_{i}
forms an exponentially
\tau
-mixing sequence, we can adapt Proposition 2 of
23
to bound the RHS of (
B.15
). Let

C_{\delta}=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}\}
be a fixed

\delta
-cover of
\mathcal{F}
in
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. Since

\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists a random index

k^{*}=k^{*}(\hat{f})\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]
such that

\|\hat{f}-f_{k^{*}}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta.

The only adaptation of Proposition 2 of
23
needed is to account for the sub-Gaussian parameter
\kappa
of the noise
\xi
. Notice that, in their notation, we have an output dimension
\mu_{q+1}=1
, and so we do not have the double summation as in their proof; moreover, they denote the noise term by
\epsilon
, correpsonding to our
\xi
.

First, we apply the triangle inequality to the RHS of (
B.15
), which gives

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot\left[\hat{f}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})\right]\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot\left[\hat{f}(X_{i})-f_{k^{*}}(X_{i})\right]\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot\Big[f_{k^{*}}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})\Big]\right\}\right|,

(B.17)

where
f_{k^{*}}
satisfies
\lVert\hat{f}-f_{k^{*}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\delta
.
For the first term on the RHS of (
B.3.1
) we use the triangle inequality together with the bound
\lVert\hat{f}-f_{k^{*}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\delta
and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot\left[\hat{f}(X_{i})-f_{k^{*}}(X_{i})\right]\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\xi_{i}\right|\cdot\left|\hat{f}(X_{i})-f_{k^{*}}(X_{i})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\xi_{i}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\cdot n\cdot\delta\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}]}.

Since
\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{S}\mathcal{G}(\kappa)
, Lemma
B.13
implies
\mathrm{Var}(\xi_{i})=\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{i}^{2}\right]\leq\kappa^{2}
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot\left[\hat{f}(X_{i})-f_{k^{*}}(X_{i})\right]\right\}\right|\leq 2\,\kappa\cdot\delta.

(B.18)

This is the sub-Gaussian analogue of (23) in the proof of Proposition 2 of
23
.

(24) in
23
(in our notation and setting) shows that:

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\xi_{i}\hat{f}(X_{i})\right]\right\}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot\left[\hat{f}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})\right]\right\}\right|

Proposition 2 of
23
is used to bound the LHS of the equality above, and hence it also holds for the RHS.
Therefore, we can now directly apply Proposition 2 of
23
to bound the RHS of (
B.15
), after accounting for the sub-Gaussian parameter
\kappa
in the second term of (
B.15
). This then yields:

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\bigl[\hat{f}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})\bigr]\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\,\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\left\{\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n_{\rm eff}}}+\sqrt{\delta}+\sqrt{2\,\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}}\right\}+2\kappa\cdot\delta.

(B.19)

where
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is the effective sample size induced by
\tau
-mixing and
C>0
is a constant depending on
B
, the Lipschitz constant
L_{f}
, and the constant
c>0
in (
B.16
) (see Appendix
E.2
for a general discussion on effective sample sizes for
\tau
-mixing sequences).

Under exponential
\tau
-mixing, Lemma
B.14
implies
\exists\,C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}>0
such that, for sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{n}{\log n}\implies\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\leq\sqrt{C^{\prime\prime}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}.

It then follows from (
B.15
) and (
B.19
) that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\left[\hat{f}(X_{i})-f(X_{i})\right]\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\,\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sqrt{C^{\prime\prime}}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log{\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}+\sqrt{\delta}+\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\hat{f}-f_{0}\rVert\right]}\right)+2\kappa\cdot\delta.

Absorbing all absolute constants into
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}>0
we conclude that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\cdot\left[\hat{f}(X_{i})-f(X_{i})\right]\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq C_{1}\,\kappa\cdot\delta+C_{2}\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\left[C_{3}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+\sqrt{\delta}+C_{4}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\hat{f}-f_{0}\rVert_{n}^{2}\right]}\right],

where we take

\displaystyle C_{1}=2,\quad C_{2}=2C,\quad C_{3}=\sqrt{C^{\prime\prime}},\quad C_{4}=\sqrt{2}.

Finally, we take a supremum over all
f\in\mathcal{F}
on both sides of the last inequality to complete the proof.
∎

Lemma B.6

(Squared-differences bound)
.

Suppose Assumption
B.3
holds. Then there exists

C>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\mathfrak{D}_{n}(\mathcal{F},\delta)\leq CB\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\,\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\left(\delta+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Recall the definition of
h_{j}
as in (
B.5
)

\displaystyle h_{j}(x,y)=\left[f_{j}(y)-f_{0}(y)\right]^{2}-\left[f_{j}(x)-f_{0}(x)\right]^{2},

where

f_{j}\in C_{\delta}
is in the minimal
\delta
-cover of
\mathcal{F}
with all functions being
L_{f}
-Lipschitz and
f_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
is
H_{f}
-Hölder with order
\beta_{f}
by Assumption
B.3
.
Lemma
B.16
then implies that
h_{j}
is
L_{h}
-Lipschitz for any
j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]
, where we define

\displaystyle L_{h}

\displaystyle:=B\cdot\left(L_{f}+H_{f}\cdot\max\left\{1,D_{\mathcal{X}}^{\beta_{f}-1}\right\}\right).

(B.20)

Here
\mathcal{X}
is compact and hence its diameter satisfies
D_{\mathcal{X}}<\infty
.
By construction of the ghost sample, we have that
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
and
\{\tilde{X}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
are independent
\tau
-mixing sequences, with

\displaystyle\tau_{X}(k)=\tau_{\tilde{X}}(k)\leq c_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k)\quad\forall k\geq 1.

Lemma
B.17
implies that the paired process
\{Z_{i}:=(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
forms a
\tau
-mixing sequence with

\displaystyle\tau_{Z}(k)

\displaystyle\leq\tau_{X}(k)+\tau_{\tilde{X}}(k)

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k)\quad\forall k\geq 1.

Lemma
B.19
then implies that the process

\displaystyle\{h_{j}(Z_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}=\{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}

is
\tau
-mixing for any
j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]
with coefficients given by

\displaystyle\tau_{h}(k)

\displaystyle\leq L_{h}\tau_{Z}(k)

\displaystyle=L_{h}\cdot 2c_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k)

\displaystyle=\tilde{c}_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k),

(B.21)

where we write
\tilde{c}_{0}=2L_{h}c_{0}
.

We will now use the concentration inequality in
23
.
First, we note that
h_{j}
is centered and absolutely bounded (satisfying the first two conditions in Lemma 1 of
[
23
]
). Since
\lVert f_{j}\rVert_{\infty}\leq B
and
\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{\infty}\leq B
, we have

\displaystyle|h_{j}(x,y)|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(f_{j}(y)-f_{0}(y)\right)^{2}-\left(f_{j}(x)-f_{0}(x)\right)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|f_{j}(y)-f_{0}(y)\right|^{2}+\left|f_{j}(x)-f_{0}(x)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4B^{2}+4B^{2}=8B^{2}.

Hoeffding’s Lemma then implies that
h_{j}
is sub-Gaussian (see e.g.,
25
[Lemma 2.6]),

\displaystyle h_{j}\sim\mathcal{S}\mathcal{G}(8B^{2}).

Lemma
B.13
then ensures that the variance of
h_{j}
is bounded (satisfying the third condition of Lemma 1 of
[
23
]
) by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(h_{j})=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma\times\sigma}\left[h_{j}^{2}\right]\leq 64B^{4}.

This verifies that our sequence
\{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
satisfies the conditions in Lemma 1 of
23
.
We now refine the variance bound following (C.50) of
10
. Since
X_{i}
and
\tilde{X}_{i}
are independent with the same marginal law
\mu
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})]

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Var}\left[\left(f_{j}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f_{j}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})\right)^{4}\right]

(B.22)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\Upsilon^{2},

(B.23)

where

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{2}:=\max\left(16B^{4}\cdot\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(h)}},\;8B^{2}\cdot\max_{j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]}\lVert f_{j}-f_{0}\rVert_{\mu}^{2}\right),

(B.24)

and

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(h)}=\max\left\{1\leq m\leq n:\tau_{h}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\right\rfloor\right)\leq\frac{4B^{2}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\}

(B.25)

is the effective sample size of
\{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
.
Recall that

\displaystyle\tau_{X}(k)\leq c_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k),\quad\tau_{h}(k)\leq\tilde{c}_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k).

Therefore, we have by Corollary
B.15
that
n_{\mathrm{eff}}\asymp n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(h)}
and hence we do not distinguish between the two effective sample sizes
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(h)}
and simply write
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
throughout the remainder of this proof.
By the definition of
\Upsilon
,

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{2}\geq 16B^{4}\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}},

and hence

\displaystyle\Upsilon\geq 4B^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}.

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})}{\Upsilon}\right|\leq\frac{8B^{2}}{\Upsilon}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}},\qquad\forall i\in[n],\;\forall j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}].

(B.26)

Using again
|f_{j}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})|\leq 2B
we obtain

\displaystyle\left(f_{j}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})\right)^{4}\leq 4B^{2}\left(f_{j}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})\right)^{2},

and therefore by the variance bound in (
B.22
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\!\left(h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 4B^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(f_{j}(X_{i})-f_{0}(X_{i})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=8B^{2}\lVert f_{j}-f_{0}\rVert_{\mu}^{2}.

Since, by definition,

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{2}\geq 8B^{2}\max_{j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]}\lVert f_{j}-f_{0}\rVert_{\mu}^{2}\geq 8B^{2}\lVert f_{j}-f_{0}\rVert_{\mu}^{2},

it follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})}{\Upsilon}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{Var}\!\left(h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})\right)}{\Upsilon^{2}}\leq 1,\qquad\forall j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}].

(B.27)

Furthermore, we note that the normalized random variables
h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})/\Upsilon
are still sub-Gaussian. Therefore all conditions of Lemma 1 of
23
are satisfied by the normalized process.
Next, we define

\displaystyle T:=\underset{j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]}{\sup}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})}{\Upsilon}\right|.

(B.28)

We now apply Lemma 1 of
23
together with a union bound to obtain, for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T\geq t)\leq 2\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\exp\left(-\frac{t}{\phi(n)}\right),

where we define

\displaystyle\phi(n):=\frac{n\cdot\left[13\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}+21\,\Gamma_{n}\right]}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}},\qquad\Gamma_{n}:=C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}},

(B.29)

where
C_{1}:=2
.
Since
T
is non-negative, we can integrate over this bound in probability to obtain a bound in expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(T)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(T\geq t)dt\leq\theta+\int_{\theta}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(T\geq t)dt

\displaystyle=\theta+2\,\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\cdot\phi(n)\cdot\exp\left(-\,\frac{\theta}{\phi(n)}\right)=:g(\theta)

(B.30)

for any
\theta\geq 0
.
Noticing that
g(\theta)
is convex in
\theta
, we can minimize this upper bound by solving for
\theta
in

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\theta)=0,

which yields

\displaystyle\theta=\phi(n)\cdot\log(2\,\mathcal{N}_{\delta}).

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T]

\displaystyle\leq\phi(n)\log(2\mathcal{N}_{\delta})+2\,\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\phi(n)\exp\left(-\log(2\mathcal{N}_{\delta})\right)

\displaystyle=\phi(n)\log(2\mathcal{N}_{\delta})+\phi(n).

(B.31)

Since
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\geq 3\geq 2
, we have
\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\geq\log 2
, and hence

\displaystyle\phi(n)\leq\frac{1}{\log 2}\,\phi(n)\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}.

Also, since
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\log(2\mathcal{N}_{\delta})=\log 2+\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\leq 2\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}.

Substituting these two bounds into (
B.3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T]\leq\left(2+\frac{1}{\log 2}\right)\phi(n)\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}.

(B.32)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]}{\sup}\left|\frac{\Upsilon}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})}{\Upsilon}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Upsilon\left(2+\frac{1}{\log 2}\right)\frac{\phi(n)}{n}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}.

(B.33)

Since
\{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
is exponentially
\tau
-mixing with coefficients given in (
B.3.1
), and since
\Upsilon
is a constant, the normalized process
\{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})/\Upsilon\}_{i\in[n]}
is also exponentially
\tau
-mixing. Therefore, Lemma
B.14
implies that there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq C_{2}\frac{n}{\log n}.

Equivalently, there exists a constant
C_{3}>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}\leq C_{3}\frac{\log n}{n}.

Substituting this bound into the definition of
\phi(n)
in (
B.29
), we obtain

\displaystyle\phi(n)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\left[13\sqrt{C_{3}}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+21\,C_{1}\sqrt{C_{3}}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}}\right]

\displaystyle=n\cdot\left[C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+C_{5}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}}\right],

where we set
C_{4}=13\sqrt{C_{3}},C_{5}=21\,C_{1}\sqrt{C_{3}}
. Substituting this into (
B.33
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]}{\sup}\left|\frac{\Upsilon}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})}{\Upsilon}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Upsilon C_{6}\left[C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+C_{5}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}}\right]\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\Upsilon C_{6}\left[C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+C_{5}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}\right].

where
C_{6}=2+\frac{1}{\log 2}
.
Now, since
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\geq 3
we have
\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\geq 1
and therefore there exists a constant
C_{7}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]}{\sup}\left|\frac{\Upsilon}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})}{\Upsilon}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Upsilon C_{6}C_{4}C_{7}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=C_{8}\cdot\Upsilon\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta},

(B.34)

where we take
C_{8}:=C_{4}C_{6}C_{7}
.

Following
10
we note that, by the definition of
\Upsilon
in (
B.24
) we have that

\displaystyle\Upsilon

\displaystyle\leq\max\left(4B^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}},\sqrt{8}B\underset{j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]}{\max}\lVert f_{j}-f_{0}\rVert_{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\max\left(4B^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}},\sqrt{8}B\left(\delta+\lVert\hat{f}-f_{0}\rVert_{\mu}\right)\right).

We need only consider the case where
\Upsilon\leq\sqrt{8}B\left(\delta+\lVert\hat{f}-f_{0}\rVert_{\mu}\right)
. This is because, when using the case
\Upsilon\leq 4\,B^{2}\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}}{{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}}
we obtain a smaller upper bound in the subsequent theorem and hence the bound obtained from
\Upsilon\leq\sqrt{8}B\left(\delta+\lVert\hat{f}-f_{0}\rVert_{\mu}\right)
holds in both cases. Substituting this into (
B.3.1
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{j\in[\mathcal{N}_{\delta}]}{\sup}\left|\frac{\Upsilon}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{h_{j}(X_{i},\tilde{X}_{i})}{\Upsilon}\right|\right]\leq C_{9}B\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\cdot\left(\delta+\lVert\hat{f}-f_{0}\rVert_{\mu}\right),

where
C_{9}:=\sqrt{8}C_{8}
.
Setting
C:=C_{9}
completes the proof.
∎

Combining Proposition
B.4
with Lemmas
B.5
and
B.6
yields the following bound.

Proposition B.7

(Generic risk bound under dependent covariates)
.

Suppose Assumption
B.3
holds and

\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\geq 3
. Then there exist constants

C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}>0
, independent of
n
,
\delta
, and

\mathcal{N}_{\delta}
, such that, for every
\delta>0
and all sufficiently
large

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,\omega(\mathcal{F},\{f_{0}\})+C_{2}B^{2}\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}

\displaystyle+C_{3}\max\left\{B\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+C_{4}(\kappa+B)\delta.

Consequently, if
f_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{f_{0}\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,\omega(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})+C_{2}B^{2}\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}

\displaystyle+C_{3}\max\left\{B\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+C_{4}(\kappa+B)\delta.

Proof.

Fix
\delta>0
. Since

\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\geq 3
, we have
\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\geq 1
. We first derive an
auxiliary bound for the expected empirical error.

By the ERM inequality obtained in the proof of

B.4
,

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}\leq\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\|f-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}+\mathfrak{C}_{n}(\mathcal{F},\delta).

Since
X_{i}\sim\mu
for every
i
,

\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}\|f-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}=\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}=\omega(\mathcal{F},\{f_{0}\}).

Applying Lemma
B.5
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\omega(\mathcal{F},\{f_{0}\})+C_{1}\kappa\delta

\displaystyle+C_{2}\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\left[C_{3}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+\sqrt{\delta}+C_{4}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}}\right].

Set

\displaystyle a^{2}\leq\frac{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}{\epsilon}b^{2}+(1+\epsilon)c,\qquad\epsilon\in(0,1],

(B.35)

and setting
\epsilon=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\omega(\mathcal{F},\{f_{0}\})+2C_{1}\kappa\delta+C_{2}^{2}C_{4}^{2}\frac{\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{n}

\displaystyle\quad+2C_{2}C_{3}\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+2C_{2}\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\delta}.

(B.36)

Next, we use the population–empirical comparison from the proof of

B.4
, namely

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}+\mathfrak{D}_{n}(\mathcal{F},\delta)+8B\delta.

By Lemma
B.6
, there exists
C_{5}>0

such that

\mathfrak{D}_{n}(\mathcal{F},\delta)\leq C_{5}B\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\left(\delta+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}}\right).

Combining this with (
B.3.1
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\omega(\mathcal{F},\{f_{0}\})+2C_{1}\kappa\delta+C_{2}^{2}C_{4}^{2}\frac{\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{n}+2C_{2}C_{3}\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+8B\delta

\displaystyle+2C_{2}\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\delta}+C_{5}B\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\,\delta+C_{5}B\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}}.

Now set

a:=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}},\qquad b:=\frac{C_{5}}{2}B\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta},

and let

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle:=2\omega(\mathcal{F},\{f_{0}\})+2C_{1}\kappa\delta+C_{2}^{2}C_{4}^{2}\frac{\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{n}+2C_{2}C_{3}\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}

\displaystyle+2C_{2}\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\delta}+C_{5}B\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+8B\delta.

Then the preceding inequality is again of the form
a^{2}\leq 2ab+c
. Applying
(
B.35
) with
\epsilon=1
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\omega(\mathcal{F},\{f_{0}\})+C_{5}^{2}B^{2}\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+2C_{2}^{2}C_{4}^{2}\frac{\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{n}+4C_{2}C_{3}\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}

\displaystyle+4C_{2}\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\delta}+2C_{5}B\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+4C_{1}\kappa\delta+16B\delta.

For all sufficiently large
n
,

2C_{2}^{2}C_{4}^{2}\frac{\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{n}+4C_{2}C_{3}\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\leq\left(2C_{2}^{2}C_{4}^{2}+4C_{2}C_{3}\right)B^{2}\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}.

4C_{2}\frac{\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\delta}+2C_{5}B\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\leq(4C_{2}+2C_{5})\max\left\{B\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}.

Substituting these three bounds gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\,\omega(\mathcal{F},\{f_{0}\})+\left(C_{5}^{2}+2C_{2}^{2}C_{4}^{2}+4C_{2}C_{3}\right)B^{2}\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}

\displaystyle+(4C_{2}+2C_{5})\max\left\{B\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+\max\{4C_{1},16\}(\kappa+B)\delta.

Finally, we relabel constants as:

\displaystyle C_{1}^{\prime}=4,\quad C_{2}^{\prime}=C_{5}^{2}+2C_{2}^{2}C_{4}^{2}+4C_{2}C_{3},\quad C_{3}^{\prime}=4C_{2}+2C_{5},\quad C_{4}^{\prime}=\max\{4C_{1},16\}.

This then yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{\prime}\omega(\mathcal{F},\{f_{0}\})+C_{2}^{\prime}B^{2}\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}

\displaystyle+C_{3}^{\prime}\max\left\{B\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}+C_{4}^{\prime}(\kappa+B)\delta,

which proves the first claim.

The uniform statement follows by taking the supremum over

f_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
. The constants in the stochastic bounds are uniform over

\mathcal{G}
under Assumption
B.3
, and

\sup_{f_{0}\in\mathcal{G}}\omega(\mathcal{F},\{f_{0}\})=\omega(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary B.8

(Dominant-term simplification)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
B.7
,
suppose in addition that
0<\delta\leq 1
. Then there exist constants

C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq C_{1}\max\left\{\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta},\,\delta\right\}+C_{2}\,\omega(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}).

(B.37)

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle T_{1}:=\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta},\qquad T_{2}:=\max\left\{\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta},\qquad T_{3}:=\delta.

By Proposition
B.4
, there exist constants
C_{1},\dots,C_{4}>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n

\displaystyle\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq C_{1}T_{1}+C_{2}T_{2}+C_{3}T_{3}+C_{4}\,\omega(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}).

We claim that
T_{2}\leq\max\{T_{1},T_{3}\}
for all
n\geq 3
whenever
0<\delta\leq 1
.

If
0<\delta\leq 1/\log n
, then

\displaystyle T_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta},

so

\displaystyle T_{2}^{2}=\frac{\delta}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}=\frac{1}{\log n}\,T_{1}T_{3}\leq\frac{1}{\log n}\max\{T_{1},T_{3}\}^{2}.

Hence

\displaystyle T_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}\max\{T_{1},T_{3}\}\leq\max\{T_{1},T_{3}\},

since
\log n\geq 1
for
n\geq 3
.

If
1/\log n\leq\delta\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle T_{2}=\delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta},

so

\displaystyle T_{2}^{2}=\delta^{2}\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}=\delta\,T_{1}T_{3}\leq T_{1}T_{3}\leq\max\{T_{1},T_{3}\}^{2}.

Thus again
T_{2}\leq\max\{T_{1},T_{3}\}
.

Therefore, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle C_{1}T_{1}+C_{2}T_{2}+C_{3}T_{3}\leq(C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{3})\max\{T_{1},T_{3}\}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq(C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{3})\max\{T_{1},T_{3}\}+C_{4}\,\omega(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}).

Hence (
B.37
) holds with

\displaystyle C_{1}^{\prime}:=C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{3},\qquad C_{2}^{\prime}:=C_{4}.

Finally,

\displaystyle T_{1}\geq T_{3}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\geq\delta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\geq\sqrt{\frac{n\delta}{\log n}},

and similarly,

T_{1}\leq T_{3}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n\delta}{\log n}}.

This proves the result.
∎

defined as in (
2.3

Proposition B.9

be defined as in (

Theorem B.11

(Convergence rate for ReLU network ERMs)
.

Suppose Assumption
B.3
holds. Let

f_{0}\in\mathcal{G}(\ell,\mathcal{P},\mathbf{D})
and set

(d^{*},s^{*})\in\arg\max_{(d,s)\in\mathcal{P}}d/s.

Let
\hat{f}
be an empirical risk minimizer over the class

\mathcal{F}\!\left(c\ell\big(L+L\sqrt{\log L/\log N}\big),\;c\max_{i}D_{i}N,\;N\right),

with sufficiently large constant
c=c(\ell,\mathbf{D})>0

defined as in (
B.2
). Choose the network size such that

NL\asymp\left(\frac{n}{\log^{3}n}\right)^{\frac{d^{*}}{4(d^{*}+s^{*})}}.

Then, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}\lesssim\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}\right)^{\frac{s^{*}}{d^{*}+s^{*}}}.

Proof.

By Corollary
B.8
, for every

0<\delta\leq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq C_{1}\max\left\{\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta},\,\delta\right\}+C_{2}\omega(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}).

Choose

\delta=(NL)^{-4s^{*}/d^{*}}.

By Proposition
B.10
,

\log\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\lesssim(NL)^{2}\log(NL).

Therefore,

\frac{\log n}{n}\log^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\lesssim\frac{\log n}{n}(NL)^{4}\log^{2}(NL).

Moreover, by Proposition
B.9
,

\omega(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})\lesssim(NL)^{-4s^{*}/d^{*}}.

Substituting these bounds gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\left\{\frac{\log n}{n}(NL)^{4}\log^{2}(NL),\,(NL)^{-4s^{*}/d^{*}}\right\}+(NL)^{-4s^{*}/d^{*}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\left\{\frac{\log n}{n}(NL)^{4}\log^{2}(NL),\,(NL)^{-4s^{*}/d^{*}}\right\}.

Now choose

NL\asymp\left(\frac{n}{\log^{3}n}\right)^{\frac{d^{*}}{4(d^{*}+s^{*})}}.

For this choice,
\log(NL)\asymp\log n
, and the two leading terms are balanced:

\frac{\log n}{n}(NL)^{4}\log^{2}(NL)\asymp(NL)^{-4s^{*}/d^{*}}\asymp\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}\right)^{\frac{s^{*}}{d^{*}+s^{*}}}.

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f_{0}\|_{\mu}^{2}\lesssim\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}\right)^{\frac{s^{*}}{d^{*}+s^{*}}},

∎

B.4
Application to the Q-learning framework and proof of the main result

In this subsection, we translate Theorem
B.11
into the DQN one-step result by identifying the regression target with the Bellman update. For a fixed previous network
Q
, recall that the Bellman response is

\displaystyle Y_{i}=R_{i}+\gamma\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q(S_{i}^{\prime},a)

with covariate
X_{i}=(S_{i},A_{i})
. Then the regression function is

\displaystyle f_{0}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|S_{i}=s,A_{i}=a]=(TQ)(s,a).

Therefore we apply Theorem
B.11
with
f_{0}=TQ
and
X_{i}=(S_{i},A_{i})
.

Theorem B.12

(One-step Bellman regression under dependent replay data)
.

Suppose Assumption
B.3
holds for the state-action process

X_{t}=(S_{t},A_{t}),

and let
Q\in\mathcal{F}
be fixed. Define

Y_{t}=R_{t}+\gamma\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q(S_{t+1},a),

and assume that the Bellman target satisfies

\mathcal{T}Q\in\mathcal{G}(\ell,\mathcal{P},\mathbf{D}).

Let
\widehat{Q}
be an empirical risk minimizer over

\mathcal{F}\!\left(c\ell\big(L+L\sqrt{\log L/\log N}\big),c\max_{i}D_{i}N,N\right)

defined by

\widehat{Q}\in\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(f(S_{t},A_{t})-Y_{t}\right)^{2}.

Choose

NL\asymp\left(\frac{n}{\log^{3}n}\right)^{\frac{d^{*}}{4(d^{*}+s^{*})}},\qquad(d^{*},s^{*})\in\arg\max_{(d,s)\in\mathcal{P}}d/s.

Then, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{Q}-\mathcal{T}Q\|_{\sigma}^{2}\lesssim|\mathcal{A}|\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}\right)^{\frac{s^{*}}{d^{*}+s^{*}}}.

Equivalently,

\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{Q}-\mathcal{T}Q\|_{\sigma}\lesssim|\mathcal{A}|^{1/2}\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}\right)^{\frac{s^{*}}{2(d^{*}+s^{*})}}.

Proof.

By assumption,
TQ\in\mathcal{G}(\ell,\mathcal{P},\mathbf{D})
. Moreover, since
the state-action process is strictly stationary and satisfies the mixing conditions
in Assumption
B.3
, the induced regression sample

\bigl((S_{t},A_{t}),Y_{t}\bigr)_{t=1}^{n}

satisfies the conditions of Theorem
B.11
. It remains only to track the action dependence. Use
the action-wise representation

Q(s,a)=f_{a}(s),\qquad f_{a}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}.

By assumption,

\mathcal{F}=\left\{Q:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{\mathcal{}}{A}\to\mathbb{R}:Q(s,a)=f_{a}(s),\ f_{a}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}\right\}.

Hence

\log\mathcal{N}\left(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\right)\leq|\mathcal{A}|\log\mathcal{N}\left(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F}_{0},\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\right).

Applying Theorem
B.11
with this product entropy bound
therefore yields

\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{Q}-\mathcal{T}Q\|^{2}_{\sigma}\lesssim|\mathcal{A}|\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}\right)^{\frac{s^{*}}{d^{*}+s^{*}}}.

Taking square roots gives

\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{Q}-\mathcal{T}Q\|_{\sigma}\lesssim|\mathcal{A}|^{1/2}\left(\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}\right)^{\frac{s^{*}}{2(d^{*}+s^{*})}}.

∎

Finally, we apply the error-propagation bound from Lemma
B.2
together with the bound from Theorem
B.12
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|Q^{*}-Q^{\pi_{K}}\|_{1,\mu}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{2\phi_{\sigma}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}|\mathcal{A}|\max_{k\in[K]}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{Q}_{k}-\mathcal{T}\widehat{Q}_{k-1}\|_{\sigma}+\frac{4\gamma^{K+1}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}R_{\max}

3.4
.

B.6
Auxilary results

Lemma B.13

(Sub-Gaussian random variables have bounded variance)
.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{S}\mathcal{G}(\kappa)
be a centered sub-Gaussian random variable with parameter
\kappa>0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\leq\kappa^{2}.

Proof.

By the moment generating function (mgf) characterization of sub-Gaussian random variables (see e.g.,
21
), we have that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda X)]\leq\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}\right).

Differentiating the RHS of the mgf above twice with respect to
\lambda
and setting
\lambda=0
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\leq\kappa^{2}.

∎

Lemma B.14

(Effective sample size for exponential
\tau
-mixing sequences)
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an absolutely bounded sequence
\left|X_{i}\right|\leq M
with
\tau
-mixing coefficients satisfying

\displaystyle\tau(k)\leq C_{0}\exp(-ck),\quad k\geq 1,

for some constants
C_{0},c>0
. Define the effective sample size

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}:=\max\left\{1\leq m\leq n:\tau\Big(\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\Big\rfloor\Big)\leq\frac{C_{1}}{m}\lor\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\},

for fixed constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, which corresponds to the number of approximately independent blocks
B=\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\rfloor
. Then there exist constants

0<c_{-}\leq c_{+}<\infty
, depending only on
C_{0},C_{1},C_{2},c
,
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle c_{-}\frac{n}{\log n}\leq n_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq c_{+}\frac{n}{\log n}.

Proof.

We first establish an upper bound.

Let
1\leq m\leq n
and set

\displaystyle B=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\right\rfloor,

so that

\displaystyle\tau(B)\leq\frac{C_{1}}{m}\quad\text{or}\quad\tau(B)\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}.

Since
\lfloor x\rfloor\geq x-1
and by the exponential decay, we have

\displaystyle\tau(B)\leq C_{0}\exp(-cB)\leq C_{0}\exp\left(-c\cdot\frac{n}{m-1}\right)=C_{0}\exp(c)\exp\left(\frac{-cn}{m}\right)

Case I:

Suppose

\displaystyle C_{0}\exp(c)\exp(-cn/m)\leq\frac{C_{1}}{m}.

Taking logarithms yields

\displaystyle-c\frac{n}{m}+\log(C_{0}\exp(c))\leq\log C_{1}-\log m

\displaystyle\iff cn\geq m(\log m+K),

where
K:=\log(C_{0}\exp(c)/C_{1})
.
Note that

\displaystyle\forall\alpha>0\;\exists\,n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\;s.t.\;\alpha\frac{n}{\log n}\leq n\;\forall n\geq n_{0}.

We now set

\displaystyle m=\alpha\frac{n}{\log n},\quad\alpha>0,

and note that
m\lesssim n
.
Then

\displaystyle\log m=\log n-\log\log n+\log\alpha.

Substituting into the inequality gives

\displaystyle cn\geq\alpha\frac{n}{\log n}\left(\log n-\log\log n+\log\alpha+K\right).

Dividing by
n
,

\displaystyle c\geq\alpha\left(1-\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}+\frac{\log\alpha+K}{\log n}\right).

Now assume that
\alpha\leq\sqrt{n}\exp(-K)
, then for sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}+\frac{\log\alpha+K}{\log n}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Hence, for large
n
,

\displaystyle c\geq\frac{\alpha}{2},

which implies
\alpha\leq 2c
. Therefore,

\displaystyle m\leq 2c\frac{n}{\log n}

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Case II:

Suppose instead

\displaystyle C_{0}\exp(c)\exp(-cn/m)\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}.

Taking logarithms gives

\displaystyle cn\geq\frac{m}{2}\log m+K^{\prime}m,

where
K^{\prime}:=\log(C_{0}\exp(c)/C_{2})
.

Substituting
m=\alpha n/\log n
and proceeding exactly as in Case I above yields, for sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle c\geq\frac{\alpha}{4},

and hence
\alpha\leq 4c
. Therefore,

\displaystyle m\leq 4c\frac{n}{\log n}

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Combining the two branches yields

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq c_{+}\frac{n}{\log n}

for some constant
c_{+}>0
.

We now establish a lower bound.
From Lemma 1 of
23
, we have that

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq\min\left\{\frac{n}{2}\cdot c\cdot\left[1\vee\log\left(\frac{C_{0}\cdot c\cdot n}{M}\right)\right]^{-1},n\right\}.

Note that,
\forall\alpha>0\;\exists\,n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\;s.t.\;\forall\,n\geq n_{0}

\displaystyle\log(\alpha n)\geq 1.

Therefore, we assume
n
is sufficiently large such that

\displaystyle 1\vee\log\left(\frac{C_{0}\cdot c\cdot n}{M}\right)=\log\left(\frac{C_{0}\cdot c\cdot n}{M}\right).

Now consider,

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{cn}{2\log\left(\frac{C_{0}cn}{M}\right)}=\frac{cn}{2\left[\log n+K\right]},

where we define
K:=\log\left(\frac{C_{0}c}{M}\right)
. Now, notice that
\forall K>0\;\exists\,C^{\prime}>0\;s.t.\;C^{\prime}\log n\geq\log n+K
. We therefore have that

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{cn}{2\left[\log n+K\right]}\geq\frac{cn}{2C^{\prime}\log n}.

Taking
c_{-}=\frac{c}{2C^{\prime}}
we conclude that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq c_{-}\frac{n}{\log n},

thereby establishing the lower bound.

∎

Lemma B.16
.

Lemma B.19
.

Appendix C
Dependence analysis

In this section, we characterize the induced dependence structure of replay buffers and minibatches. This serves as theoretical justification for our assumption of exponentially
\tau
-mixing minibatches.

Since replay buffers and minibatches are finite sequences, we introduce truncated mixing coefficients.
Let

\displaystyle W_{1:N}:=(W_{1},\dots,W_{N})

be a finite sequence of random variables of length
N
, and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}:=\sigma(W_{1},\dots,W_{t}),\qquad 1\leq t\leq N.

For
1\leq k\leq N-1
, define the truncated mixing coefficients

\displaystyle\tau_{W}^{N}(k):=\sup_{1\leq t\leq N-k}\tau(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W},W_{t+k}),

(C.1)

\displaystyle\beta_{W}^{N}(k):=\sup_{1\leq t\leq N-k}\beta(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W},W_{t+k}).

(C.2)

where
\beta(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\tau(\cdot,\cdot)
are defined in (
2.7
) and (
2.8
).
To streamline the presentation, let
\zeta
denote either
\tau
or
\beta
. Then

\displaystyle\zeta_{W}^{N}(k):=\sup_{1\leq t\leq N-k}\zeta(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W},W_{t+k}).

(C.3)

We say that
W_{1:N}
has exponentially decaying truncated
\zeta
-mixing coefficients if there exist constants
c_{0},c_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\zeta_{W}^{N}(k)\leq c_{0}e^{-c_{1}k},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N-1.

We also note that, when
W_{1:N}
is a contiguous subsequence of an infinite process, these truncated coefficients are the finite-sample analogues of the usual one-sided mixing coefficients.

We first state an obvious result that is crucial in all proofs in this section: that
\zeta
-mixing coefficients are monotone with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra.

Lemma C.1

(Monotonicity in the
\sigma
-algebra)
.

Let
X
be a random variable, and let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be
\sigma
-algebras such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}.

Then, for
\zeta\in\{\tau,\beta\}
,

\displaystyle\zeta(\mathcal{A},X)\leq\zeta(\mathcal{B},X).

Proof.

The claim is immediate from the definitions of
\tau
and
\beta
: enlarging the
\sigma
-algebra can only increase the amount of dependence measured between the past and
X
.
∎

C.1
Dependence strucure of the replay buffer

We first show that a replay buffer inherits exponential mixing from the underlying process.

Lemma C.2

(Exponentially
\zeta
-mixing replay buffer)
.

Let
t_{0}\geq 1
, and suppose the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is exponentially
\zeta
-mixing in the sense that

\displaystyle\zeta_{X}(k):=\sup_{t\geq 1}\zeta(\mathcal{A}_{t},X_{t+k})\leq c_{0}e^{-c_{1}k},\qquad k\geq 1,

where
\mathcal{A}_{t}:=\sigma(X_{s}:s\leq t)
. Define the replay buffer of size
M\in\mathbb{N}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{M}),\qquad Z_{m}:=X_{t_{0}+m-1},\quad m=1,2,\dots,M.

(C.4)

Then
\mathcal{M}
is also exponentially
\zeta
-mixing, with

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}(k)\leq\zeta_{X}(k)\leq c_{0}e^{-c_{1}k},\qquad 1\leq k\leq M-1.

Proof.

For
1\leq t\leq M
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathcal{M}}:=\sigma(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{t}).

Since
(Z_{m})_{m=1}^{M}
is a contiguous subsequence of
(X_{t})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathcal{M}}=\sigma(X_{t_{0}},\dots,X_{t_{0}+t-1})\subseteq\sigma(X_{s}:s\leq t_{0}+t-1)=\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}+t-1}.

Therefore, for every
1\leq t\leq M-k
,

\displaystyle\zeta(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathcal{M}},Z_{t+k})

\displaystyle=\zeta(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathcal{M}},X_{t_{0}+t+k-1})

\displaystyle\leq\zeta(\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}+t-1},X_{t_{0}+t+k-1})

\displaystyle\leq\zeta_{X}(k),

where the first inequality follows from Lemma
C.1
. Taking the supremum over
1\leq t\leq M-k
gives

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}(k)\leq\zeta_{X}(k),\qquad 1\leq k\leq M-1.

The exponential bound follows immediately.
∎

This shows that a replay buffer does not create additional long-range dependence: it inherits the same exponential dependence decay as the underlying process, up to truncation at length
M
.

C.2
From
\beta
-mixing to
\tau
-mixing

In this section, we show that if the underlying process is exponentially
\beta
-mixing, then it is also exponentially

-mixing.

Lemma C.3

(Bounded-metric
\beta

\tau

.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})
be a bounded Polish metric space with

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a
\mathcal{X}

\tau_{X}(k)\leq C_{\beta\tau}D_{\mathcal{X}}\,\beta_{X}(k),

where
C_{\beta\tau}
is a universal constant depending only on the

\beta
and total variation.

Fix
t
and set

\mathcal{A}_{t}:=\sigma(X_{s}:s\leq t),\qquad\mathcal{B}_{t,k}:=\sigma(X_{s}:s\geq t+k).

Let
Z
be an
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{B}_{t,k}

\sup_{h\in\Lambda_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left|\int h\,d\mu_{Z\mid\mathcal{A}_{t}}-\int h\,d\mu_{Z}\right|

\displaystyle\tau_{Z}(k)

\displaystyle\leq 2\,D_{\mathcal{X}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{B}\left|\mu_{Z\mid\mathcal{A}_{t}}(B)-\mu_{Z}(B)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,D_{\mathcal{X}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}_{t,k}}\left|\mathbb{P}(A\mid\mathcal{A}_{t})-\mathbb{P}(A)\right|\right].

Taking the supremum over

\tau_{X}(k)\leq C_{\beta\tau}\,D_{\mathcal{X}}\,\beta_{X}(k).

With our total variation convention we have
B_{\beta\tau}=2
.
∎

Lemma C.4

(Exponential

.

Let
(\mathcal{Z},d_{\mathcal{Z}})
be a bounded Polish metric space with

Let
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be an exponentially

-mixing
\mathcal{Z}
-valued process satisfying

Then
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is exponentially
\tau
-mixing. More
precisely, for every
k\geq 1
,

\tau_{Z}(k):=\sup_{t\geq 1}\tau\bigl(\sigma(Z_{s}:s\leq t),Z_{t+k}\bigr)\leq C_{\beta\tau}D_{\mathcal{Z}}C_{\beta}e^{-c_{\beta}k},

where
C_{\beta\tau}
is a universal constant depending only on the
normalization of
\beta
and total variation. In particular, the
exponential rate is unchanged, and only the prefactor changes.

Proof.

By the bounded-metric comparison between
\beta
- and
\tau
-mixing
coefficients in Lemma
C.3
, for any sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
and any

\mathcal{Z}
-valued random variable
V
,

\tau(\mathcal{A},V)\leq C_{\beta\tau}D_{\mathcal{Z}}\,\beta(\mathcal{A},V).

Applying this inequality with

\mathcal{A}=\sigma(Z_{s}:s\leq t),\qquad V=Z_{t+k},

gives, for every
t\geq 1
and
k\geq 1
,

\tau\bigl(\sigma(Z_{s}:s\leq t),Z_{t+k}\bigr)\leq C_{\beta\tau}D_{\mathcal{Z}}\,\beta\bigl(\sigma(Z_{s}:s\leq t),Z_{t+k}\bigr).

Taking the supremum over
t\geq 1
yields

\tau_{Z}(k)\leq C_{\beta\tau}D_{\mathcal{Z}}\,\beta_{Z}(k).

Using the assumed exponential
\beta
-mixing bound,

\beta_{Z}(k)\leq C_{\beta}e^{-c_{\beta}k},

we obtain

\tau_{Z}(k)\leq C_{\beta\tau}D_{\mathcal{Z}}C_{\beta}e^{-c_{\beta}k},\qquad k\geq 1.

This proves the claim.
∎

C.3
Dependence structure of minibatches

We now analyze the dependence structure of minibatches under different sampling schemes. From Lemma
C.4
we know that if the underlying sequence is exponentially
\beta
-mixing, then it is also exponentially
\tau
-mixing with new constants depending on the bounded metric comparison.

Therefore, to streamline the presentation, we proceed under the assumption that the underlying process is exponentially
\tau
-mixing with

\displaystyle\tau_{X}(k)\leq c_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k),\quad k\geq 1.

In the case where the underlying trajectory is exponentially
\beta
-mixing as in Lemma
C.4
, the above holds with

\displaystyle c_{0}=C_{\beta\tau}D_{\mathcal{Z}}C_{\beta},\qquad c_{1}=c_{\beta}.

C.3.1
Uniform sampling

We first consider the standard sampling scheme used in DQN: uniform sampling with replacement.

At training time, we sample a minibatch from the replay buffer. Let

\displaystyle I_{1},\dots,I_{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}\{1,\dots,M\},

independently of
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{M})
, and let

\displaystyle I_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq I_{(n)}

be the corresponding order statistics. For any realized ordered index set

\displaystyle 1\leq i_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq i_{(n)}\leq M,

we define the ordered minibatch by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}),\qquad Y_{r}:=Z_{i_{(r)}},\quad r=1,\dots,n.

With

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{r}:=\sigma(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r}),

we define, for
1\leq k\leq n-1
,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n}(k):=\sup_{1\leq r\leq n-k}\tau(\mathcal{Y}_{r},Y_{r+k}).

The first result explains why sampling with replacement is problematic for
\tau
-mixing: repeated indices occur with positive probability, so the lag in minibatch order need not correspond to any positive lag in the replay buffer.

Proposition C.5

(Within-minibatch dependence bound for a realized sampled minibatch)
.

With the sampling introduced above
define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{(i)}=(Y_{1}^{(i)},\dots,Y_{n}^{(i)}),\qquad Y_{r}^{(i)}:=Z_{i_{(r)}},\quad r=1,\dots,n.

Write

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{(i)}:=\sigma(Y_{1}^{(i)},\dots,Y_{r}^{(i)}).

and define the lag-zero truncated coefficient

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}(k):=\sup_{1\leq t\leq M-k}\tau(\sigma(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{t}),Z_{t+k}),\qquad 0\leq k\leq M-1.

Then, for every
1\leq r<s\leq n
,

\displaystyle\tau(\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{(i)},Y_{s}^{(i)})\leq\bar{\tau}_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}\bigl(i_{(s)}-i_{(r)}\bigr).

Consequently, for
1\leq\ell\leq n-1
,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{Y}^{(i)}}^{n}(\ell)\leq\sup_{1\leq r\leq n-\ell}\bar{\tau}_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}\bigl(i_{(r+\ell)}-i_{(r)}\bigr).

If, in addition,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}(k)\leq c_{0}e^{-c_{1}k},\qquad 1\leq k\leq M-1,

then

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{Y}^{(i)}}^{n}(\ell)\leq\max\Biggl\{\bar{\tau}_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}(0),\;c_{0}\sup_{1\leq r\leq n-\ell}\exp\bigl(-c_{1}(i_{(r+\ell)}-i_{(r)})\bigr)\Biggr\}.

Proof.

Fix
1\leq r<s\leq n
. By construction,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{(i)}=\sigma(Z_{i_{(1)}},\dots,Z_{i_{(r)}}).

Since

\displaystyle i_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq i_{(r)},

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{(i)}\subseteq\sigma(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{i_{(r)}}).

Therefore, by Lemma
C.1
,

\displaystyle\tau(\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{(i)},Y_{s}^{(i)})

\displaystyle=\tau(\mathcal{Y}_{r}^{(i)},Z_{i_{(s)}})

\displaystyle\leq\tau\bigl(\sigma(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{i_{(r)}}),Z_{i_{(s)}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{\tau}_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}\bigl(i_{(s)}-i_{(r)}\bigr).

This proves the first claim.

Taking the supremum over
1\leq r\leq n-\ell
yields

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{Y}^{(i)}}^{n}(\ell)\leq\sup_{1\leq r\leq n-\ell}\bar{\tau}_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}\bigl(i_{(r+\ell)}-i_{(r)}\bigr).

Finally, if

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}(k)\leq c_{0}e^{-c_{1}k},\qquad 1\leq k\leq M-1,

then for each
r
,

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}\bigl(i_{(r+\ell)}-i_{(r)}\bigr)\leq\max\Bigl\{\bar{\tau}_{\mathcal{M}}^{M}(0),\;c_{0}e^{-c_{1}(i_{(r+\ell)}-i_{(r)})}\Bigr\},

which gives the stated bound.
∎

This shows that uniform sampling with replacement, which is standard in replay-based RL algorithms, is problematic since it does not guarantee exponentially mixing minibatches. In the next corollary, we show that this problem can be overcome by sampling uniformly
without
replacement.

Remark C.6
.

If the sampling is
without
replacement, then the conditions of Proposition
2.2
are satisfied. Specifically, the indices satisfy
I_{1}<I_{2}<\dots<I_{n}
, and hence Proposition
2.2
implies that the sampled minibatches are exponentially
\tau
-mixing.

C.4
Contiguous block sampler

We introduce a new sampler inspired by the blocking ideas of
41
and show in the following proposition and corollary that if the underlying sequence is exponentially
\tau
-mixing, this sampler is guaranteed to produce exponentially
\tau
-mixing minibatches.

Given the buffer indices
\{1,\dots,M\}
, this sampler constructs a number of contiguous blocks of length
b
, each separated by a gap length
a
, and then concatenates the blocks to form the minibatch.

Proposition C.7

(Contiguous block sampling)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be an exponentially
\tau
-mixing sequence with

\displaystyle\tau_{X}(k)\leq c_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k),\qquad\forall k\geq 1,

where

\displaystyle\tau_{X}(k):=\sup_{t\geq 1}\tau(\mathcal{A}_{t},X_{t+k}),\qquad\mathcal{A}_{t}:=\sigma(X_{s}:s\leq t).

Fix a starting point
t_{0}\geq 1
, sample size
n\geq 3
, block length
1\leq b\leq n
, and gap length
0\leq a\leq b-2
. Write

\displaystyle q:=\lfloor n/b\rfloor,\qquad 0\leq r<b,

to denote the number of full blocks of length
b
. Then
n=qb+r
.
For
1\leq i\leq q
, define the starting index of the
i
-th full block by

\displaystyle s_{i}:=t_{0}+(i-1)(a+b),

and define the
i
-th full block by

\displaystyle B_{i}:=(X_{s_{i}},X_{s_{i}+1},\dots,X_{s_{i}+b-1}).

If
r\geq 1
, define the starting index of the remainder block by

\displaystyle s_{q+1}:=t_{0}+q(a+b),

and define the remainder block by

\displaystyle B_{q+1}:=(X_{s_{q+1}},X_{s_{q+1}+1},\dots,X_{s_{q+1}+r-1}).

Let
\mathcal{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
be the concatenation of the blocks

\displaystyle B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{q}

and, if
r\geq 1
, the remainder block
B_{q+1}
. Then

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n}(k)\leq\tau_{X}(k)\leq c_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k),\qquad 1\leq k\leq n-1.

Proof.

For
1\leq j\leq n
, condition on the sampled indices and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{j}:=\sigma(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{j}).

Let

\displaystyle u_{1}<u_{2}<\cdots<u_{n}

denote the ordered time indices corresponding to the concatenated sample
\mathcal{Y}
, so that

\displaystyle Y_{j}=X_{u_{j}},\qquad 1\leq j\leq n.

Since the blocks are contiguous and are concatenated in temporal order, the sequence
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
is strictly increasing. Moreover, consecutive sampled indices satisfy

\displaystyle u_{j+1}-u_{j}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }Y_{j}\text{ and }Y_{j+1}\text{ belong to the same block},\\
a+1,&\text{if }Y_{j}\text{ and }Y_{j+1}\text{ belong to consecutive blocks}.\end{cases}

In particular,

\displaystyle u_{j+1}-u_{j}\geq 1,\qquad 1\leq j\leq n-1.

Hence, for every
1\leq j\leq n-k
,

\displaystyle u_{j+k}-u_{j}\geq k.

This shows that contiguous block sampling preserves the order of the underlying process and guarantees that minibatch lag
k
corresponds to at least
k
time steps in the original sequence. Proposition
2.2
then implies that the sampled minibatches are exponentially
\tau
-mixing.

∎

The following corollary shows that this also holds when sampling from a finite contiguous subsequence of the original process, which corresponds to sampling from a replay buffer.

Corollary C.8

(Contiguous block sampling from finite subsequence)
.

Consider the same setting as in Proposition
C.7
, except suppose now that the minibatches are drawn from the finite replay buffer
\mathcal{M}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{M})
as in (
C.4
). Write

\displaystyle q:=\lfloor n/b\rfloor,\qquad 0\leq r<b

to denote the number of full blocks of length
b
. Then
n=qb+r
.
For
1\leq i\leq q
, define the starting index of the
i
-th full block in the replay buffer by

\displaystyle s_{i}:=1+(i-1)(a+b),

and define the
i
-th full block by

\displaystyle B_{i}:=(Z_{s_{i}},Z_{s_{i}+1},\dots,Z_{s_{i}+b-1}).

Assume that all selected block indices lie inside the replay buffer. Equivalently, assume

\displaystyle(q-1)(a+b)+b\leq M

when
r=0
, and

\displaystyle q(a+b)+r\leq M

when
r\geq 1
.

As in Proposition
C.7
let
\mathcal{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
be the concatenation of the blocks.
and, if
r\geq 1
, the remainder block
B_{q+1}
. Then

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n}(k)\leq c_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k),\qquad 1\leq k\leq n-1.

Proof.

The proof proceeds exactly as in the proof of Proposition
C.7
to show that the sampler is
order-preserving
and
duplicate-free
. Therefore, Proposition
2.2
implies that the minibatches are exponentially
\tau
-mixing and the coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n}(k)\leq c_{0}e^{-c_{1}k},\qquad 1\leq k\leq n-1.

∎

Remark C.9

(Circular wraparound)
.

The same conclusion remains valid for a circular wraparound version of the sampler, in which blocks that extend past the end of the replay buffer continue from the beginning of the buffer, provided that the sampled buffer indices are all distinct and the resulting sampled observations are reordered in increasing buffer index order before the truncated mixing coefficients are evaluated. Under this ordering, the same proof applies, since the sampled indices
u_{1}<\cdots<u_{n}
again satisfy

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{j}\subseteq\sigma(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{u_{j}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad u_{j+k}-u_{j}\geq k.

Therefore, the same exponential bound

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n}(k)\leq c_{0}e^{-c_{1}k},\qquad 1\leq k\leq n-1,

continues to hold.

Remark C.10

(Decay control)
.

Our contiguous block sampler provides some control over the decay in dependence of the subsampled sequence; larger gap lengths
a
and smaller block sizes
b
results in faster decay.

Appendix D
Experimental Details.

We study temporal dependence in two objects produced by DQN training: (i) state–action trajectories generated by a fixed trained policy, and (ii) minibatches sampled from the replay buffer during the course of the DQN algorithm. In both cases, our goal is not to estimate the exact population
\tau
-mixing coefficient directly, but rather a finite-sample, finite-memory proxy that is computable from observed samples and is closely aligned with the definition of
\tau
-dependence through the
1
-Wasserstein distance. Here, we describe the estimator used in our experiments, and provide details about reproducibility.

D.1
Estimator details

We describe the construction of our estimator used in the experiments.

D.1.1
Relationship to
1
-Wasserstein distance

By the Kantorovich-Rubinstein theorem, the
1
-Wasserstein distance admits the dual representation (see e.g., Theorem 2 of
20
)

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\phi(\xi)\mu(d\xi)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\phi(\xi^{\prime})\nu(d\xi^{\prime})\right\},

where the supremum is taken over all
1
-Lipschitz functions. Comparing this to the definition of the
\tau
-mixing coefficient in (
2.8
) we immediately see that

\displaystyle\tau(\mathcal{A},X)=\lVert W_{1}(\mu_{X|\mathcal{A}},\mu_{X})\rVert_{1}=\mathbb{E}\left[W_{1}(\mu_{X|\mathcal{A}},\mu_{X})\right].

That is, the
\tau
-mixing coefficient
\tau(\mathcal{A},X)
is precisely the expected
1
-Wasserstein distance between the marginal law
\mu_{X}
and the conditional law
\mu_{X|\mathcal{A}}
.

In this way, we can also relate the lag-
k

\tau
-mixing coefficient to the expected
1
-Wasserstein distance. Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued stochastic process and let

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{t})

be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the past up to time
t
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\tau_{X}(k)=\underset{t\geq 1}{\sup}\;\mathbb{E}\left[W_{1}\left(\mathcal{L}(X_{t+k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}),\mathcal{L}(X_{t+k})\right)\right],

(D.1)

where
\mathcal{L}(X_{t+k})
denotes the marginal law of
X
and
\mathcal{L}(X_{t+k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})
denotes a conditional law given
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

D.1.2
A finite-window
\tau
-mixing proxy

Consider the relationship in (
D.1
).
Since conditioning on the full past is computationally infeasible in our setting, our estimation procedure replaces
\mathcal{F}_{t}
by a finite history window of length
m
. This yields the truncated target

\tau_{X}^{m}(k):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[W_{1}\!\left(\mathcal{L}(X_{t+m-1+k}\mid\sigma(X_{t},\dots,X_{t+m-1})),\mathcal{L}(X_{t+m-1+k})\right)\right].

(D.2)

Notice that, since

\displaystyle\sigma(X_{t},\dots,X_{t+m-1})\subseteq\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{t+m-1}),\qquad\forall\,t\geq 1,

it follows from Lemma
C.1
that, for any
m\geq 0

\displaystyle\tau_{X}^{m}(k)\leq\tau_{X}(k),\quad k\geq 1.

(D.3)

The estimator in our implementation should therefore be interpreted as an estimator for
\tau_{X}^{m}(k)
, rather than as a direct estimator of the full
\tau_{X}(k)
.

D.1.3
Construction of the estimator

Suppose we observe a sample

\displaystyle x_{1:T}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{T}),\qquad x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Fix a lag
k\geq 1
and history length
m\geq 1
. Define

\displaystyle N_{k}:=T-m-k+1.

If
N_{k}\leq 0
, then there are not enough observations to form a length-
m
conditioning window and a
k
-step-ahead target, so no estimate is produced for that lag; in our implementation, we set the estimate to zero in this case.

For each
i=1,\dots,N_{k}
, define the history vector

Z_{i}:=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}&x_{i+1}&\dots&x_{i+m-1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{md},

(D.4)

and the corresponding future target

Y_{i}:=x_{i+m-1+k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(D.5)

Thus,
(Z_{i},Y_{i})
is the sample used to approximate the map

\displaystyle z\mapsto\mathcal{L}(X_{t+m-1+k}\mid X_{t},\dots,X_{t+m-1}=z).

Global empirical law.

The unconditional law of the future variable is approximated by the empirical measure

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot):=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\delta_{Y_{i}}.

(D.6)

Local conditional empirical law via
k
-nearest neighbors.

To estimate the conditional law given a history
Z_{i}
, we use a
k
-nearest-neighbor procedure in the space of history windows. Let
r
denote the number of nearest neighbors used in the estimation. Using Euclidean distance on
\mathbb{R}^{md}
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{i}\subset\{1,\dots,N_{k}\}

be the set of indices corresponding to the
r
nearest neighbors of
Z_{i}
.

The resulting local empirical measure is

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot|Z_{i}):=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\delta_{Y_{j}}

(D.7)

Optimal transport discrepancy.

For each
i
, the estimator compares
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot\mid Z_{i})
using the
1
-Wasserstein distance with Euclidean ground metric on the target space:

\widehat{W}_{i,k}:=W_{1}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot\mid Z_{i}),\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot)\right).

(D.8)

Because both measures are discrete and uniformly weighted, this can be written as the optimal transport problem

\widehat{W}_{i,k}=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(a_{r},a_{N_{k}})}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{k}}\pi_{\alpha\beta}\,\bigl\|Y_{j_{\alpha}}-Y_{\beta}\bigr\|_{2},

(D.9)

where
\mathcal{N}_{i}=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{r}\}
,

\displaystyle a_{r}=\left(\frac{1}{r},\dots,\frac{1}{r}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{r},\qquad a_{N_{k}}=\left(\frac{1}{N_{k}},\dots,\frac{1}{N_{k}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{k}},

and
\Pi(a_{r},a_{N_{k}})
denotes the set of nonnegative couplings with these marginals. In the default configuration of our experiments, this quantity is computed using Earth Mover’s Distance via
ot.emd2
from the python POT library
[
11
,
12
]
.

Observed dependence score.

Finally, we replace the supremum over
t
with a finite-window maximum to obtain our final estimate

\widehat{\tau}_{X,\mathrm{obs}}^{m}(k):=\max_{1\leq i\leq N_{k}}\widehat{W}_{i,k}.

(D.10)

This is the raw empirical dependence score at lag
k
.

D.1.4
Permutation centering

Even under weak or no dependence, the quantity in (
D.10
) need not be exactly zero in finite samples, because it compares a local empirical distribution based on
r
points to a global empirical distribution based on
N_{k}
points. To reduce this finite-sample bias, we subtract a permutation baseline.

For each permutation replicate
b=1,\dots,B
, draw a random permutation

\displaystyle\sigma_{b}\in\mathfrak{S}_{N_{k}}

and define permuted targets

\displaystyle Y^{(b)}_{j}:=Y_{\sigma_{b}(j)},\qquad j=1,\dots,N_{k}.

The neighbor sets are
not
recomputed after permutation; instead, the same neighborhoods determined by the history vectors
(Z_{i})
are reused.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{i}
denote the index set used in the permutation step. Then the permuted local law is

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{(b)}(\cdot\mid Z_{i}):=\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{i}|}\sum_{j\in\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{i}}\delta_{Y^{(b)}_{j}},\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{(b)}_{k}(\cdot):=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\delta_{Y^{(b)}_{j}}.

(D.11)

The corresponding transport discrepancy is

\widehat{W}^{(b)}_{i,k}:=W_{1}\!\left(\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{(b)}_{k}(\cdot\mid Z_{i}),\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{(b)}_{k}(\cdot)\right),

(D.12)

and the permutation baseline is

\widehat{\tau}_{X,\mathrm{perm}}^{m}(k):=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N_{k}}\widehat{W}^{(b)}_{i,k}\right).

(D.13)

The final estimator used in our experiments is

\widehat{\tau}^{m}_{X}(k):=\max\!\left\{\widehat{\tau}_{X,\mathrm{obs}}^{m}(k)-\widehat{\tau}_{X,\mathrm{perm}}^{m}(k),\,0\right\},\quad 1\leq k\leq K,

(D.14)

where
K
is the maximum lag to test for, and is specified by the user.
Thus,
\widehat{\tau}(k)
is a nonnegative, bias-corrected dependence score: it is large when nearby histories in
Z
-space induce future targets whose empirical law differs substantially from the unconditional law, and it is close to zero when this effect disappears after permutation centering.

Finally, we remark that when

\displaystyle N_{k}-r\leq 0,

then all the
N_{k}
points are included in the
r
nearest neighbors, and hence

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot|Z_{i})=\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot),

and consequently,

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{X,\mathrm{obs}}^{m}(k)=0,\qquad k\geq T-m+1-r.

(D.15)

In our experiments, we use the scikit-learn library
[
30
,
4
]
to implement the KNN estimation with the parameter
leave_one_out=True
. This means that the point itself is not considered when determining its neighboring set, resulting in one fewer available observations for estimation. In this case, we have that

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{X,\mathrm{obs}}^{m}(k)=0,\qquad k\geq T-m-r.

(D.16)

D.1.5
Experimental protocol and objects estimated

We apply the estimator in (
D.14
) to two types of sequences produced by DQN training: trajectories generated by a fixed trained policy, and minibatches sampled from the replay buffer during optimization.

Environments and DQN architecture.

We consider three Gymnasium environments
[
36
]
:
CartPole-v1
,
FrozenLake-v1
, and
LunarLander-v3
. In each case, the DQN agent uses a fully connected ReLU network with two hidden layers of width
256
. The network maps states to action values and has architecture

\mathbf{d}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{S})&256&256&|\mathcal{A}|\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, one forward pass produces
Q
-values for all actions. This is the standard DQN parameterization and avoids the alternative state–action architecture

\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{S})\times|\mathcal{A}|&256&256&1\end{bmatrix},

which would require one forward pass per action.

State–action representation.

Each observation used by the dependence estimator is a state–action vector. Continuous states are represented by their raw observation vectors, while discrete states are represented by one-hot vectors. The selected action is appended as an additional coordinate. Thus, the sequence analyzed by the estimator has the form

X_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}S_{t}\\
A_{t}\end{bmatrix}.

The same representation is used for trajectory-level and minibatch-level estimates.

Trajectory dependence.

To estimate dependence in the underlying RL process, we first train a DQN agent in the given environment. After training, we fix the learned policy and generate rollouts using the greedy action-selection rule, with no exploration. For each rollout, we collect the resulting state–action sequence

(X_{t})_{t=1}^{T},\qquad X_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}S_{t}\\
A_{t}\end{bmatrix},

and apply the estimator in (
D.14
) to obtain

\widehat{\tau}(k),\qquad 1\leq k\leq K.

For trajectory-level estimates, the lag
k
is ordinary environment-time lag along the rollout generated by the fixed greedy policy.

Minibatch dependence.

To estimate dependence in the data used by the DQN regression step, we log every minibatch sampled from the replay buffer during training, before the corresponding optimizer step is performed. For a minibatch of size
B_{\mathrm{mb}}
, the logged matrix is

\mathcal{B}_{u}=\begin{bmatrix}X_{1}\\
X_{2}\\
\vdots\\
X_{B_{\mathrm{mb}}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{B_{\mathrm{mb}}\times(d+1)},

where
u
indexes the gradient update. The ordering of the rows is the order in which samples are returned by the sampler. We then apply the estimator in (
D.14
) separately to each logged minibatch.

For minibatch-level estimates, the lag
k
is therefore lag in the sampled minibatch order. It is not environment-time lag, and it is not lag across gradient updates. These estimates measure the residual dependence among the samples entering a single DQN update.

D.1.6
Contiguous block replay sampler

The minibatches used during training are sampled using a contiguous block sampler inspired by the blocking technique of
41
. Rather than drawing all samples independently from the replay buffer, the sampler draws several contiguous blocks of replay-buffer indices. Each block has length
b
, and successive blocks are separated by a gap of length
a
. The sampled blocks are then concatenated to form the minibatch.

D.1.7
Aggregation and visualization

Both trajectory-level and minibatch-level experiments produce a collection of estimated curves

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}^{(1)}(k),\dots,\widehat{\tau}^{(M)}(k),\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

where
M
is the number of rollouts or the number of logged minibatches. The plotting script averages these estimates across replications and reports bands equal to
\pm 2
standard errors:

\overline{\tau}(k)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{\tau}^{(m)}(k),\qquad\mathrm{SE}(k)=\frac{\mathrm{sd}(\widehat{\tau}^{(1)}(k),\dots,\widehat{\tau}^{(M)}(k))}{\sqrt{M}}.

The randomness in the estimates come from several sources: (i) randomness in the underlying environment, (ii) empirical estimation of conditional and unconditional expectations, and (iii) possible randomness induced by initializations.
The figures therefore summarize how rapidly the empirical dependence score decays with lag for each experimental condition.

D.1.8
Summary

In summary, our estimator replaces the inaccessible conditional law in the definition of
\tau
-mixing by a
k
-nearest-neighbor empirical conditional distribution over length-
m
histories, measures its discrepancy from the unconditional empirical law using optimal transport, and subtracts a permutation baseline to reduce finite-sample bias. The resulting statistic is a practical, Wasserstein-based dependence proxy that can be applied uniformly to RL trajectories and replay-buffer minibatches.

Appendix E
Additional Theoretical Details.

E.1
Ghost sample.

We construct the ghost sample
\{\tilde{X}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
on an enlarged probability space, such that:

1.

\tilde{X}_{i}\overset{d}{=}X_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
,

2.

the ghost sequence
\{\tilde{X}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
is independent of the training sample
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
,

3.

\{\tilde{X}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
is stationary and exponentially
\tau
-mixing with the same coefficients as
(X_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, i.e.,
\tau_{\tilde{X}}(k)=\tau_{X}(k)
for all
k\geq 1
.

This construction allows us to write population norms as expectations over the ghost sample while preserving the dependence structure of the original process. This can be formally justified using the coupling arguments for
\tau
-mixing sequences introduced by
7
.

In classical symmetrization arguments, the ghost sample is typically assumed to be i.i.d. and independent of the training data. However, in our setting, assuming an i.i.d. ghost sample would imply that it arises from a fundamentally different generative mechanism than the exponentially
\tau
-mixing training sample. Consequently, computing expectations with respect to such a ghost sample would not correspond to the same population object as the training sample. Instead, we construct the ghost sample to follow the same generative mechanism as the training data, preserving the
\tau
-mixing dependence structure. This ensures that expectations over the ghost sample remain meaningful and directly comparable to the training sample.

E.2
Effective Sample Size.

The effective sample size
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
quantifies the number of nearly independent observations present in a
\tau
-mixing sequence of length
n
. We provide a general definition below, which is taken from Lemma 1 of
23
.

Definition E.1

(Effective sample size for

\tau
-mixing)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a strictly stationary
\tau
-mixing process satisfying
\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{2}\leq B
,
\mathbb{E}[\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}]=0
, and
\mathbb{E}[\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
, then the effective sample size is given by

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}:=\max\left\{1\leq m\leq n\ \mid\tau\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\right\rfloor\right)\leq\frac{B}{m}\vee\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{m}}\right\}.

(E.1)

Moreover, for an exponentially
\tau
-mixing process with
\tau_{X}(k)\leq c_{0}\exp(-c_{1}k)
, the effective sample size satisfies

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq\min\left\{\frac{n}{2}\cdot c_{1}\cdot\left[1\vee\log\left(\frac{c_{0}\cdot c_{1}\cdot n}{B}\right)\right]^{-1},n\right\}.

Heuristically, one can partition the sequence into contiguous blocks of length
m
such that blocks separated by
m
steps are approximately independent;
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is then roughly the number of such blocks.

This heuristic can be formalized using the block coupling construction of
7
for
\tau
-mixing sequences.
For a block length
m
, one can construct a coupled block sequence with the same marginal distributions as the original blocks such that each block of length
m
is independent of the others, up to a coupling error bounded by
\tau(m)
.
Letting
B=\lfloor n/m\rfloor
denote the number of blocks, the total coupling error is at most
B\cdot\tau(m)
.
By the definition in (
E.1
),
m
is chosen so that this cumulative coupling error is negligible relative to the statistical fluctuations of interest: either of order
1/B
for mean-type bounds (
\tau(m)\lesssim 1/m
) or of order
1/\sqrt{B}
for variance-type bounds (
\tau(m)\lesssim 1/\sqrt{m}
).
Hence, the effective sample size can be interpreted as

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx B\asymp\frac{n}{m},

the number of nearly independent blocks.

Lemma
B.14
shows that under exponential
\tau
-mixing,
\tau(k)\lesssim\exp(-ck)
, the corresponding effective sample size satisfies

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{eff}}\asymp\frac{n}{\log n},

justifying the choice of block length
m\asymp\log n
.
